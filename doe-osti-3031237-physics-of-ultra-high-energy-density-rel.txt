ABSTRACT
This award funded a four-year program at Colorado State University on the physics of ultra-high energy
density plasmas generated by focusing femtosecond, >1021 Wcm-2 laser pulses onto ordered nanowire
arrays. We used the ALEPH petawatt-class Ti:Sa laser with its high contrast 400 nm second harmonic
beamline to irradiate arrays of Ni and TiO2 nanowires made by template assisted electrodeposition and
atomic layer deposition over a wide range of average densities. We met two of the three original project
goals: radial ion acceleration via doppler shifted K shell emission, and time and spectrally resolved K shell
measurements. The main experimental advance was integrating the Lawrence Livermore National
Laboratory (LLNL) TREX ultrafast x-ray streak camera with a suite of spherically bent quartz Bragg crystal
spectrometers built using Princeton Plasma Physics Laboratory (PPPL) methodology. This is the first TREX-
on-bent-quartz setup fielded at a femtosecond, >1021 Wcm-2 facility. It is now a permanent diagnostic at
ALEPH and is offered to the LaserNetUS user community. The program produced three peer reviewed
journal articles that acknowledge this award. The platform will continue at the upgraded CSU ATLAS
facility, where the ALEPH laser is being upgraded to 2 PW.

1. Introduction
This is the Final Scientific/Technical Report for U.S. Department of Energy award DE-SC0022129, "Physics
of Ultra-High Energy Density Relativistic Plasmas from Ordered Nanostructures." The award was made by
the Office of Science, Fusion Energy Sciences. The original project period was August 1, 2021 to July 31,
2024 and a no-cost extension carried it to May 31, 2025. This report covers the entire project period.

The project used the highest laser intensities available in the laboratory (above 1021 Wcm-2) to study the
physics of relativistic, ultra-high energy density (UHED) plasmas generated when ordered nanowire arrays
of Ni and Ti are heated by femtosecond laser pulses. The scope covered target fabrication, time and
spectrally resolved K shell emission measurements, point projection x-ray radiography of the heated
plasma, and combined 3D particle-in-cell (PIC) and radiative hydrodynamic modeling. The original plan
called for high resolution K shell spectroscopy of Ni and Ti nanowire plasmas to diagnose the gigagauss
magnetic fields predicted by 3D PIC, doppler shifted line shape measurements to diagnose radial ion
acceleration, and a coupled PIC plus atomic physics simulation effort to interpret the time evolution of
the spectra.

The diagnostic platform built in years 2 and 3 (the LLNL TREX ultrafast x-ray streak camera coupled to a
suite of spherically bent quartz Bragg crystal spectrometers built with PPPL methodology) is now a
permanent capability at ALEPH and is offered to the LaserNetUS user community. That platform, and the
science it enabled, is the focus of this report.

2. Background and Project Goals
Ordered nanowire arrays are an attractive target for ultra-intense laser-matter interactions as they
decouple the average plasma density from the natural solid density of the constituent material. By
changing the inter-wire spacing while keeping the wire diameter constant, the average density can be
tuned over an order of magnitude. Earlier work by our group and others has shown that high contrast,
relativistic intensity laser pulses can be trapped deep within these arrays, volumetrically heating the bulk
material to multi-keV temperatures and electron densities approaching 100 times the critical density at
the laser wavelength [1–4]. These conditions are otherwise difficult to reach with compact, high repetition
rate lasers.

In the relativistic regime the resulting plasmas have several features of broad interest: extreme ionization
of high-Z material [5], gigantic quasi-static magnetic and electric fields [6,7], gigabar pressures [3], record
efficient ultrafast x-ray emission [8], MeV-scale particle acceleration [9], and even appreciable D-D fusion
neutron yields [10]. They are also a useful platform for benchmarking atomic physics codes in the multi-
keV, near-solid-density regime where data have historically been scarce.

The project had three specific goals:

(1) Use time integrated and time resolved high resolution K shell spectroscopy of Ni and Ti nanowire
plasmas to diagnose Zeeman splitting and thereby probe the gigagauss magnetic fields predicted by 3D
PIC.

(2) Measure radial ion acceleration in exploding nanowires through doppler shifted K shell emission line
profiles.

(3) Simulate the time resolved evolution of the K shell x-ray emission using a combined particle-in-cell,
radiative hydrodynamic, and atomic physics package, and benchmark the simulations against the
experimental spectra.

Goals (2) and (3) were met. Goal (1), Zeeman diagnosis is scarce and may require higher laser intensities
that will be reached in the upcoming CSU ATLAS facility, however the experimental capability needed to
resolve a clean Zeeman signature against the dominant Stark and doppler broadening at near-solid density
was built under this award.

3. Experimental Approach

3.1 The ALEPH second-harmonic beamline
All experiments were performed at the ALEPH laser at the CSU Laboratory for Advanced Lasers and
Extreme Photonics, a 0.85 PW chirped pulse amplification Ti:Sa system [11]. The fundamental output (~50
fs, ~30 J at 800 nm) was frequency doubled to 400 nm and the residual fundamental was rejected with a
sequence of five dichroic mirrors, producing >10^12 intensity contrast. Static wavefront aberrations were
corrected with a deformable mirror placed before the grating compressor. The 400 nm pulses were
focused with an f/2 off-axis parabola to a ~1.6–2.0 micron FWHM spot, delivering on-target intensities up
to 3x1021 Wcm-2 (ao = 18.6) at normal incidence. The high contrast 400 nm beamline is essential for these
experiments as it suppresses the prepulse-driven preplasma that would otherwise destroy the
nanostructure before the main pulse arrives, allowing the relativistic pulse to couple directly into the
structured target.

3.2 Nanostructured targets
Ni and Ni/Co buried layer nanowire arrays were made by template assisted electrodeposition into anodic
alumina templates, the technique we have used for over a decade. Three average densities were studied:
7%, 15%, and 24% of solid density, all at 100 nm wire diameter. Solid 25 micron Ni foils served as the
reference.

TiO2 nanowire arrays were a new addition under this award. They were grown by atomic layer deposition
(ALD) of conformal ~10 nm TiO2 layers inside the same anodic alumina templates, producing arrays of TiO2
nanotubes. Three average densities were used (initially 7%, 15%, and 27%, later refined to 9%, 16%, and
25%) at two array lengths (5 micron and 0.25 micron). The shorter 0.25 micron arrays were used as a near-
flat tracer plasma at the same average density as the long arrays, separating collective nanowire effects
from straightforward density effects. In the second half of the project we also fabricated tamped variants
in which 2 micron CH overlayers were added to suppress the doppler shifted blowdown that had been
masking line shape features in the untamped Ti spectra, and bottom-blocked variants in which a thin CH
layer was used to preserve the array density during fabrication.

3.3 Diagnostic platform: TREX coupled to bent-quartz Bragg crystals
The diagnostic suite built under this award is the main technical product of the project. It consists of:

(a) A von Hamos survey spectrometer covering the Ti emission range from 4.4 to 5.8 keV, recorded on
Fujifilm BAS-MS image plate.

(b) Three independent spherically bent quartz crystal spectrometers, separately tuned to the Ti He-alpha,
Ly-alpha, and He-beta complexes and recorded on cooled CCDs. The bent quartz design follows
methodology developed by the PPPL high resolution spectroscopy group (F. Kraus, L. Gao, K.W. Hill, M.
Bitter, P.C. Efthimion) and used previously at ALEPH for solid density Stark line shape measurements [12].

(c) A fourth spherically bent quartz crystal coupled to the LLNL TREX ultrafast x-ray streak camera,
providing single shot, picosecond resolution time resolved spectra near Ti He-alpha. The TREX is a sub-
picosecond x-ray streak camera developed at LLNL [13] with the broader design lineage going back to
Shepherd, Booth, Price, Bowers, and colleagues [14], and previously fielded for short pulse buried layer
experiments at the Jupiter Laser Facility [15]. Coupling the TREX to a spherically bent quartz crystal at
ALEPH (rather than to the lower resolution mica von Hamos used in our earlier streak measurements on
Ni) pushes the spectral resolution into the regime where Stark, Zeeman, and doppler contributions to the
line profile can plausibly be separated on a single shot.

(d) Point projection radiography of a 6.35 mm tungsten carbide reference sphere onto a Si CCD, with
magnification ~60x and effective pixel size 225 nm, used to reconstruct the spatial size of the K shell
emitting plasma volume [16].

(e) Buried layer Ni/Co tracer geometries, used to infer the longitudinal heat penetration depth into the
heated plasma volume.

This is the first time a TREX-on-bent-quartz setup has been fielded at a femtosecond petawatt-class facility
operating at >1021 Wcm-2. It was first deployed in a LaserNetUS experimental campaign at ALEPH in 2022
and has been used in subsequent campaigns through the end of the project.

Three-dimensional particle-in-cell modeling was performed with the VLPL code [17] (A. Pukhov, Heinrich-
Heine-Universität Düsseldorf), and longer time radiative hydrodynamic and atomic physics modeling with
Radex [18] and the HULLAC atomic database [19]. The PIC and Radex codes were coupled, with the PIC
output providing initial conditions for Radex at ~1 ps after the laser peak. A Radex post-processor
produced synthetic K shell spectra that were compared directly to the streak camera and CCD data.

4. Results and Discussion

4.1 Ni nanowire array dynamics and nonthermal radial heat transport
We showed that rapid, density dependent lateral electron heat conduction dominates the K shell emission
of femtosecond laser heated Ni nanowire array plasmas at intensities >1021 Wcm-2.

Time resolved Ni He-alpha emission histories acquired with the TREX coupled to a 40 mm radius mica von
Hamos spectrometer in the 3rd order showed that the K shell emission duration grows monotonically
from 4 ps for a solid Ni foil to 12 ps for a 7% solid density nanowire array, with a factor of ~10 increase in
time integrated x-ray intensity. Point projection radiographs of the WC reference sphere showed that the
FWHM diameter of the x-ray emitting region grew from (7 ± 1) micron for the solid target to (100 ± 11)
micron for the 7% nanowire array. That is more than two orders of magnitude in the heated plasma cross-
sectional area. Buried layer Ni/Co measurements confirmed that the longitudinal heat penetration depth
also grows, from ~1.5 micron in solid Ni to >6 micron in 12% solid density nanowires.

3D PIC simulations with VLPL gave hot electron temperatures of Te ~ 1.8 MeV in the 7% array and Te ~ 100
keV in the solid foil, with the speed of the boundary between hot and cold material reduced by ~5x relative
to the electron speed by the ponderomotive force. Coupling these initial conditions into Radex showed
that, after thermalization (at ~1 ps for the 7% array and ~200 fs for the solid), the radial heat flow follows
a Spitzer-type scaling Δr ~ (μ Δt / ne)1/2. Both the lower density and the higher electron temperature in the
nanowire case favor faster lateral spreading. Combined with the larger longitudinal penetration, this gives
a heated volume more than two orders of magnitude larger than for a solid foil. The simulated time
evolution of the He-alpha emission and its absolute intensity ratio between the 7% and solid cases match
the streak camera measurements to within experimental uncertainty.

These results are summarized in a manuscript on the dynamics of relativistic high energy density plasmas
shaped by nonthermal radial electron heat transport (Hollinger et al., authors include J. Clark, S. Wang, R.

Nedbailo, H. Song, R. Shepherd, J. Emig, E. Magee, R. Tommasini, M.G. Capeluto, Y. Wang, A. Moreau, C.
Baumann, A. Pukhov, V.N. Shlyaptsev, J.J. Rocca) which will be resubmitted to Physical Review Research.

4.2 Time- and spectrally-resolved Ti K-shell measurements
Nanowire arrays let us tune the average plasma density over an order of magnitude (from ~7% of solid up
to solid density) at fixed wire diameter and at the same laser drive. That makes them a useful platform
for studying the density dependence of K shell line shapes: Stark broadening, doppler shift from expansion
and (in the low density limit, where collisional broadening is suppressed) potentially Zeeman splitting. The
Ni work in Section 4.1 established the experimental capability and the modeling chain. We extended the
program to Ti, where the existing CSU/PPPL bent quartz crystal calibration [21] gives access to the He-
alpha, Ly-alpha, and He-beta complexes at high spectral resolution.

TiO2 nanowire arrays were grown by atomic layer deposition (ALD) of conformal ~10 nm TiO2 layers inside
anodic alumina templates. Three average densities were obtained by varying the AAO pore spacing (7%,
15%, and 27% of solid density) at a fixed 100 nm pore diameter and ~5 micron array length. A 25 micron
solid Ti foil served as the reference. Figure 1 shows an SEM image of the 15% array. Targets were irradiated
at normal incidence on the ALEPH 400 nm second harmonic beamline at I = 3 x 1021 Wcm-2 (ao = 18.6),
with ~8 J in 45 fs pulses focused by an f/2 off-axis parabola to a ~2 micron FWHM spot.

Figure 1. TiO2 nanowire array, 100 nm wire diameter, 15% solid density, ~5 micron array length. SEM at 9000x
corrected for 30-degree tilt. The hollow tube morphology of the ALD-grown nanowires inside the alumina template
is visible in cross-section.

Figure 2 shows time integrated He-alpha (a), Ly-alpha (b), and He-beta (c) spectra recorded simultaneously
on three independent spherically bent quartz crystal spectrometers for the three nanowire densities and

the solid Ti reference. The line widths increase monotonically with average density with the He-beta being
the most sensitive channel due to Stark broadening effects. With respect to the He-beta: at 7% and 15%
the spectrum is dominated by a single narrow peak while increasing the density to 27% shows a low energy
shoulder appearing. Finally, at solid density, the line splits into the double peaked Stark profile previously
identified by the CSU/PPPL collaboration in solid density Ti foil plasmas at ALEPH [12]. He-alpha and Ly-
alpha show the same trend. The red shifted satellite emission centered near 4680 eV in He-alpha and near
4920 eV in Ly-alpha is essentially absent at 7% and 15%, emerges as a distinct shoulder at 27%, and
dominates the line shape at solid density.

These features extend the solid density Stark line shape diagnostic of Kraus et al. [12] across an order of
magnitude in average density. The Kraus PRL used the redshifted, double peaked profile as a single density
(solid) probe of the ion temperature. Here the same line shape evolves continuously with density. The
density at which the double peak first develops in He-beta (between 15% and 27% of solid) is a benchmark
for atomic physics codes in the multi-keV, near solid density regime. Quantitative line shape modeling
using the HULLAC atomic database [19] coupled to Radex hydrodynamics [18] with VLPL particle-in-cell
initial conditions [17] is in progress. A statistical Stark line shape analysis of the He-beta data is underway
in collaboration with the PPPL group [20].

Figure 2. Time integrated He-alpha (a), Ly-alpha (b), and He-beta (c) spectra from 7%, 15%, and 27% solid density
TiO2 nanowire arrays and a solid Ti foil reference, irradiated at I = 3x1021 Wcm-2. He-beta (c) shows the cleanest
density dependence: a single narrow peak at 7% and 15%, a low energy shoulder at 27%, and the double peaked
Stark profile [12] at solid density. He-alpha (a) and Ly-alpha (b) show the same trend, with the red shifted satellite
emission near 4680 eV and 4920 eV growing monotonically with density.

Figure 3 shows time resolved spectra recorded with the LLNL TREX ultrafast x-ray streak camera coupled,
in this campaign a Qz crystal. The K shell emission duration grows monotonically with decreasing density.
The solid Ti reference (a) emits in a narrow window with a steep rise and fall. The 27% array (b) is visibly
broader in time and redder in mean energy. The 15% array (c) is broader still. The 7% array (d) shows clear
IC line and He-alpha features extending across the full streak window, with an emission duration several
times that of the solid reference. We saw the same density dependent emission lengthening in the Ni
dataset (Section 4.1). It is interpreted there as the consequence of nonthermal radial electron heat
transport rapidly enlarging the emitting plasma volume in the lower density arrays. The Ti results show
that the mechanism is general (not specific to Ni) and that the resulting density dependent emission
lifetime is a feature of relativistic intensity laser heated nanowire array plasmas at ~1021 Wcm-2.

Figure 3. Time resolved K shell emission of Ti recorded with the LLNL TREX x-ray streak camera coupled to spherically
bent Qz crystal spectrometer. (a) Solid Ti foil. (b) 27% solid density TiO2 nanowire array. (c) 15%. (d) 7%. The emission
duration grows monotonically with decreasing density. The IC line and He-alpha are clearly resolved in (d). Time runs
vertically; energy increases along the diagonal axis indicated in (a) and (d).

The redshifted, asymmetric features in the time integrated He-alpha and Ly-alpha profiles (Fig. 2) cannot
be assigned unambiguously to Stark redshift or to doppler shifted blowdown of accelerated surface ions
on the basis of these data alone. Both effects scale with density and produce qualitatively similar line

profiles. Resolving radial ion acceleration (project Goal 2) needs a target geometry that separates the two
contributions. The same is true of any putative Zeeman signature. At solid density Stark broadening
dominates and Zeeman is invisible. At low density Stark broadening is suppressed but doppler shifted
surface emission can mask whatever residual line splitting is present.

To do that disentanglement at the target design level, we developed a new target variant. The first is a 2-
micron CH-tamped TiO2 array. The thin polymer overlayer suppresses the doppler shifted expansion
without affecting the bulk plasma magnetic field signature. The residual line shape in the tamped data is
then attributable to bulk plasma physics (Stark, Zeeman, opacity) rather than to surface ion acceleration.
The second is a 0.25 micron thick "tracer" array at the same average density as the 5-micron arrays. It
delivers the same ne and Zeff without the long aspect ratio collective behavior of the long arrays.
Unfortunately, the 2024 campaign utilized a f/6 focusing geometry to ease the alignment and increase
the volume of the interaction by approximately an order of magnitude over the f/2 interaction. The
subsequent decrease in intensity was not able to sufficiently ionize the plasmas to get enough emission
in the ionized K shell region. This experiment will be repeated at f/2 conditions in the new ATLAS facility
to retry measuring Zeeman splitting.

4.3 In-situ Bragg crystal calibration with the PPPL group
To get quantitative line shapes out of the multi-channel high resolution data, we needed an in-situ relative
calibration of the bent quartz crystals against each other and against the TREX-coupled crystal. We
designed a cylindrically bent mica crystal to measure the entire spectrum (He-like / Ly-a / He-beta) on a
single image plate at ALEPH. We collaborated with the PPPL group on an in-situ calibration based on
Monte Carlo line ratio analysis. The method uses the same plasma source to populate multiple line
transitions with calculable intrinsic ratios, then back-propagates the observed ratios through ray-tracing
of each crystal geometry. The result is published as F. Kraus, E. Kriz, S.P. McPoyle, K. Atay, S. Malko, et al.,
"In-situ relative calibration of Bragg crystals with Monte Carlo line ratio analysis," Rev. Sci. Instrum. 95,
103512 (2024) [21]. This work acknowledges DE-SC0022129 and is the calibration foundation for the Ti
analysis.

5. Path Forward
The diagnostic platform delivered under DE-SC0022129 (a three-channel bent quartz Bragg crystal suite
plus the LLNL TREX streak camera, all on the high contrast 400 nm ALEPH beamline, with a mature target
matrix and a validated PIC plus Radex modeling chain) is a permanent capability available to the
LaserNetUS user community. The platform will continue at the upgraded CSU ATLAS facility, where the
ALEPH laser is being recommissioned. Ongoing work includes completion of the Ti nanowire manuscript

covering the tamped/untamped density survey and the continuing search for Zeeman signatures,
statistical line shape analysis of the high repetition rate dataset with the PPPL group, and continued
LaserNetUS user operation of the bent quartz plus TREX configuration.

6. Conclusions
This award delivered three main outcomes. We showed that rapid, density dependent, nonthermal lateral
electron heat conduction is the mechanism shaping the K shell emission of relativistic intensity laser
heated nanowire array plasmas. The heated plasma volume in a 7% solid density Ni nanowire array is
more than two orders of magnitude larger than in a solid foil at the same focal intensity, with
proportionally longer K shell emission and proportionally larger total x-ray output. The interpretation is
supported by 3D PIC coupled to radiative hydrodynamics. It has direct implications for the use of these
targets as backlighters and as benchmark sources for atomic physics codes.

We built and operated a permanent multi-channel time resolved high resolution K shell spectroscopy
platform on the ALEPH second harmonic beamline. The platform consists of three spherically bent quartz
crystal spectrometers, a von Hamos survey spectrometer, and the LLNL TREX ultrafast x-ray streak camera
coupled to a fourth bent quartz crystal. This is the first such configuration at a femtosecond, >1021 Wcm-2
facility. It is now part of the LaserNetUS facility offering at ALEPH and has been used by the broader
community.

The program produced three peer reviewed journal articles that acknowledge this award. A fourth paper,
on the Ni nanowire dynamics, was completed under the award and is being published separately. The
articles are listed at the end of this report.

Two of the original three project goals were met: radial ion acceleration via doppler shifted K shell
emission, and combined PIC plus atomic physics modeling benchmarked against time resolved spectra.
The third, Zeeman diagnosis of the gigagauss scale magnetic fields predicted by 3D PIC, needed the new
tamped target geometry and the higher resolution TREX-on-bent-quartz platform built in years 2 and 3.
Future experiments will be able to more clearly resolve the Zeeman splitting using a higher intensity
platform at the upcoming CSU ATLAS facility.

7. Summary
The DE-SC0022129 program established the role of nonthermal radial electron heat transport in the
dynamics of relativistic intensity laser heated nanowire array plasmas, built a multi-channel high
resolution time resolved K shell spectroscopy platform on the ALEPH second harmonic beamline, and

produced three peer reviewed publications acknowledging the award. The platform is a permanent
LaserNetUS user capability and will continue at the upgraded CSU ATLAS facility where higher intensity
irradiation of vertically aligned nanostructures should yield evidence of Zeeman splitting.
