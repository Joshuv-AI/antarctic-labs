Re‐Purposing Sapropterin (Kuvan) for ACTA2‐Related Multisystemic Smooth Muscle Dysfunction Syndrome: A Translational Mechanistic and First‐In‐Human Therapeutic Report
Authors: Moran Hausman‐Kedem, Noa Bielopolski, Vijai Krishnan, Shane Wald‐Altman, Shelly I Shrian, Omer Bar‐Yosef, Livia Kapusta, Claire Shamber, Patricia L Musolino, Miguel Weil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMultisystemic smooth muscle dysfunction syndrome (MSMDS) is an ultra‐rare, ACTA2‐related disorder characterized by severe cerebrovascular disease, aortic aneurysms, and smooth muscle dysfunction. Using molecular dynamics simulations and in silico drug screening, we identified that sapropterin dihydrochloride (Kuvan) is a candidate capable of restoring p.R179H mutation disrupted actin polymerization. Patient‐derived fibroblasts demonstrated improved actin organization with Kuvan treatment. Off‐label treatment in a single pediatric patient with ACTA2‐MSMDS resulted in observed cerebrovascular stabilization and clinical improvement on longitudinal follow‐up. These findings provide first‐in‐human n‐of‐1 translational evidence supporting sapropterin as a potential disease‐modifying therapy for ACTA2‐related vasculopathies, while gene‐directed therapies are awaited. Further studies are needed to establish a robust therapeutic effect and to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the observed cerebrovascular benefit.Keywords: ACTA2, cerebrovascular disease, pediatric stroke, sapropterin, smooth muscle dysfunction

Introduction

The ACTA2 gene encodes an isoform of α‐actin specific to smooth muscle cells (SMCs), which plays a crucial role in scaffolding the contractile apparatus within the vasculature and hollow viscera. Missense variants in ACTA2 disrupt actin polymerization, leading to intimal proliferation, vessel stenosis, loss of SMC contractility, and weakening of the internal elastic lamina [1]. Perturbation in ACTA2 has been implicated in familial thoracic aortic aneurysm and dissection (TAAD), and abdominal aortic aneurysm and dissection (AAAD). The ACTA2 R179H (Arg179His) variant and other variants affecting codon 179 are associated with multisystem smooth muscle dysfunction syndrome (MSMDS, OMIM #613834), an ultra‐rare, smooth muscle myopathy. MSMDS exhibits a diverse phenotypic spectrum, including congenital mydriasis, patent ductus arteriosus (PDA), thoracic aortic aneurysms, pulmonary arterial hypertension, hypotonic bladder, and intestinal hypoperistalsis [1, 2]. Patients have distinct cerebrovascular features, including progressive dilatation of the proximal internal carotid artery (ICA) and occlusive disease of the distal segments, associated with a high risk of early‐onset stroke. Additionally, small vessel involvement results in white matter degeneration [3]. Aortic dissections typically develop in the second decade of life, often necessitating surgical repair. Life expectancy is markedly reduced, and no disease‐modifying therapies are currently available [4]. At the molecular level, ACTA2 R179 mutations impair SMC function by disruption of actin filament assembly, alteration of intracellular calcium homeostasis, and increased endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) uncoupling, as well as transcriptional dysregulation of synthetic and contractile genes and altered cellular metabolism, including increased glycolytic flux and impaired mitochondrial respiration, reduced nitric oxide signaling, increased oxidative stress, and impaired vascular autoregulation [5, 6].

To identify potential therapeutic strategies for ACTA2‐MSMDS, we undertook a translational pipeline integrating computational, cellular, imaging, and clinical findings. This included computational molecular dynamics simulations to characterize the structural consequences of the R179H mutation and identify a druggable conformational defect, followed by high‐throughput in silico docking of FDA‐approved compounds to identify candidates capable of stabilizing mutant ACTA2. This integrative approach converged on sapropterin dihydrochloride (Kuvan), a BH4 analogue approved for phenylketonuria, as the leading candidate. Then, in vitro validation of candidates in patient‐derived fibroblasts was performed, followed by an off‐label therapeutic trial in a single pediatric patient.

Here, we report first‐in‐human, n‐of‐1 evidence supporting sapropterin dihydrochloride (Kuvan) as a candidate disease‐modifying agent in ACTA2‐related cerebrovascular disease, providing a mechanistic and clinical rationale for further investigation.

Methods

Molecular Dynamics Simulations and In Silico Drug Screening

Molecular dynamics simulations of ACTA2 protein chains were conducted using a computational platform (Desmond, Schrödinger LLC, New York, NY, 2025). First, homology modeling of mutant R179H ACTA2 from the wild‐type crystal structure (PDB 3B5U: 3B5U, https://www.rcsb.org/) was performed and shown to create a mutant‐specific pocket/gap at the actin monomer interface, associated with abnormal actin behavior and impaired polymerization. Time‐dependent simulations were performed to compare wild‐type and mutant conformations [7]. Subsequently, integrative predictive computational analysis and structural biology models were implemented to perform in silico drug screening via high‐throughput docking algorithms, simulating the binding of potential drugs that could restore the structure and function of ACTA2 mutants (Glide, Schrödinger LLC, New York, NY, 2025). Thousands of FDA‐approved compounds were screened iteratively, prioritized by a multi‐parameter ranking process, including docking score, predicted restoration of mutant dynamics, clinical safety, known adverse‐effect profile, feasible dosage regimen, route of elimination, and expected biodistribution/tissue reach. Sapropterin (Kuvan) emerged as the top‐ranked compound across these combined criteria. It is a registered drug with known therapeutic pediatric doses and a well‐established safety profile. In the model, sapropterin was predicted not only to fill the structural gap and stabilize inter‐monomer interactions, but also to reduce the conformational shift between the G‐actin and F‐actin states, thereby supporting restoration of more normal actin behavior.

Fibroblast Assays

To further study Kuvan potential efficacy, fibroblasts were derived from skin biopsies from four females (ages 13–21 years) with genetically confirmed ACTA2 R179H mutations participating in the MGH genetic vasculopathies biomarkers and natural history study. Study protocols were approved by the MGH Institutional Review Board (IRB). Written informed consent was obtained from parents/guardian, assent was obtained from participants as per IRB protocol guidance. Fibroblasts were grown on glass coverslips and fixed in 3.7% methanol‐free formaldehyde in PBS for 15 min. F‐actin staining was performed by permeabilizing the cells with 0.1% Triton X‐100 for 15 min, followed by washing, and staining with Alexa Fluor‐conjugated phalloidin (Thermo Fisher, Cat. No. A12379) at a final concentration of 33 nM in PBS containing 1% BSA. Coverslips were mounted in ProLong Glass Antifade Mountant and imaged using confocal microscopy. Protocol followed manufacturer's protocol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Pub. No. MAN0001777). Actin polymerization state was assessed using a G‐/F‐actin in vivo assay kit (Cytoskeleton Inc., Cat. No. BK037). Briefly, fibroblast cell pellets were lysed in the provided stabilization buffer, followed by ultracentrifugation to separate F‐actin (pellet) and G‐actin (supernatant) fractions. Equal volumes were analyzed by SDS‐PAGE and Western blotting using anti‐actin antibody, and band intensities were quantified to calculate the G‐/F‐actin ratio, per kit instructions.

Off‐Label‐Use Treatment Protocol

Sapropterin dihydrochloride (Kuvan) was administered off‐label following informed parental consent. The drug was given orally once daily as a soluble tablet dissolved in water, at an initial dose of 10 mg/kg/day with escalation to 20 mg/kg/day after 2 weeks based on tolerability. Safety evaluation included daily blood pressure (BP) monitoring, phenylalanine levels at baseline, 4‐, 12‐, and 18‐months following treatment initiation.

Clinical and Neuroimaging Monitoring

The cerebrovascular disease and its evolution were evaluated prior to treatment and every 3 months during the treatment using the following methods: Transcranial Doppler (TCD) Dolphin 4D (Viasonix, Israel) measurements were performed to assess the velocity of bilateral middle cerebral arteries (MCA), anterior cerebral artery (ACA) and basilar artery (systolic, diastolic, and mean). All TCD measurements throughout the monitoring period were performed by the same experienced operator, using the same ultrasound platform, to minimize inter‐operator variability. Pulsatility index (PI) was calculated as a marker of distal cerebrovascular resistance. MRI/TOF‐ MRA of the brain and cervical vessels was performed without sedation using Siemens Medical Solutions scanners (3.0 T Magnetom Vida) to assess stenosis or ectasia of blood vessels. All MRA studies were performed on the same 3T scanner using identical 3D time‐of‐flight MRA acquisition parameters throughout the monitoring period. Luminal diameters of supraclinoid and petrous ICA were measured on coronal MIP reconstructions from TOF‐MRA by a board‐certified pediatric neuroradiologist (S.I.S.) blinded to the clinical timeline, and the ratio between supraclinoid and petrous ICA was calculated as previously described [4]. Echocardiography with measurements of the ascending aorta Z scores was performed every 3 months. Patient‐reported outcomes before and during treatment were assessed every 3 months at clinical visits based on parental reports and included appetite, weight gain, constipation, abdominal pain episodes, endurance of physical activity and exercise intolerance. Health‐related quality of life was assessed using the Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory 4.0 Generic Core Scales, 15‐Item Short Form (PedsQL SF‐15) at baseline, 12 months, and 18 months after treatment initiation.

Results

Molecular dynamic simulations of the mutation (Figure 1A,B) confirmed structural disruption of actin polymerization as previously published [6]. Sapropterin dihydrochloride (Kuvan) demonstrated a high predicted binding score on the in silico modeling and was predicted to fill the structural gap created by the mutation and stabilize inter‐monomer interactions, as well as reduce the conformational shift between the G‐actin and F‐actin states, thereby supporting restoration of more normal actin behavior. Sapropterin (Kuvan) emerged as the top‐ranked compound across these combined criteria and had a favorable risk–benefit ratio given its known pharmacokinetics and tolerance in long‐term use in pediatric populations and its potential effects on vascular biology.

We therefore proceeded to test Kuvan on ACTA2 R179H patient‐derived fibroblasts. Treatment of mutant fibroblasts with Kuvan was associated with increased actin polymerization, reflected by a reduced G/F‐actin ratio (Figure 1C, top) and improved F‐actin organization on phalloidin staining (Figure 1C, bottom).

Based on these results, off‐label use therapy with Kuvan was initiated in a 6‐year‐old girl with R179H ACTA2‐related MSMDS who exhibited progressive cerebrovascular disease. She was born at term with aortic coarctation and a patent ductus arteriosus (PDA), both surgically repaired in the neonatal period. Persistent mydriasis was observed postoperatively, prompting neuroimaging. Brain MRI revealed acute bilateral watershed arterial ischemic infarcts together with characteristic ACTA2 R179H structural brain malformations and cerebrovascular pattern (Figure 2). During infancy, she developed pulmonary hypertension, gastrointestinal dysmotility with severe constipation, failure to gain weight, and marked fatigue. Serial neuroimaging demonstrated progressive diffuse white matter disease and evolving steno‐occlusive vasculopathy, with bilateral narrowing of the supraclinoid ICAs and proximal ICA dilatation (Figure 2).

Sapropterin dihydrochloride (Kuvan) treatment was initiated at 10 mg/kg/day and escalated to 20 mg/kg/day in the absence of adverse effects at the lower dose. No treatment‐related adverse effects were observed. Tolerability was excellent, with no nausea, vomiting, or other gastrointestinal symptoms attributable to the drug.

With treatment, both subjective and objective improvements were observed; clinical status improved across gastrointestinal symptoms, endurance of physical activity, appetite, and fatigue. Reported improvements included appetite, weight gain, reduced constipation and abdominal pain episodes (suggesting improved intestinal motility), as well as enhanced exercise tolerance and endurance of physical activity reported 3 months after treatment initiation. PedsQL Physical Health scores improved markedly (20 → 45 → 65 at baseline, 12, and 18 months; +225% from baseline, exceeding clinically meaningful change), whereas Psychosocial scores declined from 59.7 at baseline to 51.38 at 12 months and 48.6 at 18 months following treatment, resulting in an overall decrease in Total Score (45 → 31.7).

Cerebrovascular status improved during treatment, with serial TCD demonstrating progressive reduction in mean flow velocities over 18 months, reflecting reduced distal resistance (Figure 3A). Serial brain and cervical TOF‐MRA demonstrated increased vessel calibers and significant improvement in the left supraclinoid‐to‐petrous ICA ratio (p = 0.0007) (Figure 3A,B). BP and phenylalanine levels remained within normal limits throughout treatment. Ascending aortic diameters remained stable (Z‐score 2.5 at baseline, 2.27 at 12 months, 2.23 at 18 months following treatment onset). No treatment‐related adverse effects were observed.

Discussion

The rarity and lethality of MSMDS, combined with progressive cerebrovascular injury, underscore the urgent need for innovative therapeutic approaches. Given the long timelines, narrow inclusion criteria, and costs of developing gene‐targeted therapies, drug repurposing represents a pragmatic, potentially beneficial interim strategy. This report provides the first cellular and an n‐of‐1 clinical proof‐of‐concept that sapropterin dihydrochloride (Kuvan), an FDA‐approved therapy for phenylketonuria, may target upstream underlying vascular dysfunction in ACTA2 R179H‐MSMDS, potentially stabilizing disease progression and modifying the natural history of this ultra‐rare and uniformly lethal disease. Although these findings must be interpreted with caution given the use of fibroblasts and the single‐patient, uncontrolled design, they provide preliminary evidence that sapropterin may be repurposed as a potential therapy for ACTA2‐related smooth muscle dysfunction.

The natural history of ACTA2 R179H cerebrovascular disease is progressive stenosis, with no documented spontaneous improvement in ICA caliber ratios or TCD velocities in prior longitudinal cohorts [3, 4]. The observed stabilization and improvement in ICA ratios in our patient over 18 months are unlikely to reflect the expected disease course, strengthening a potential treatment effect.

Direct sapropterin‐actin binding has not been previously reported, and our computational data provide novel, first evidence for this interaction. Our cellular model shows a significant improvement in actin cytoskeletal polymerization, and the human treatment supports the positive effect. However, the mechanistic effect requires biochemical validation, as, in addition to direct structural binding interaction of Kuvan to the structural pocket, alternative mechanisms may contribute to the observed vascular effects. Sapropterin is a critical cofactor for endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), and restoration of eNOS coupling is a well‐established BH4‐mediated effect, known to be disrupted in ACTA2 R179H vasculopathy [8, 9]. This could independently improve vascular tone, with downstream effects on oxidative stress, and may serve as a complementary mechanism.

Emerging evidence further suggests that BH4 may influence pathways involved in mitochondrial maintenance and biogenesis [10]. Improved SMC contractility and function have been reported in models of ACTA2 deficiency by both reduction of reactive oxygen species with antioxidants and boosting mitochondrial respiration with nicotinamide riboside [11, 12]. Future comprehensive molecular and functional assays will be needed to distinguish the relative contribution of direct cytoskeletal stabilization from eNOS‐mediated vascular rescue and understand the mechanisms underlying the observed effect of sapropterin on SMC actin phenotype rescue.

Several other therapeutic targets have been proposed for ACTA2‐related vasculopathy. Platelet‐derived growth factor (PDGFR‐β) inhibitors (such as imatinib or crenolanib) may reduce downstream vascular remodeling by suppressing smooth muscle cell proliferation, migration, and phenotypic switching, based on the established role of PDGF signaling in SMC proliferation and intimal hyperplasia [13, 14]. Notably, these agents act indirectly and do not repair the primary ACTA2 R179H structural defect. Similarly, the HSF1/HMG‐CoA reductase axis is of interest, as ACTA2 pathogenic variants have been linked to HSF1 activation, increased HMG‐CoA reductase activity, and maladaptive smooth muscle modulation [15]. HSF1 pathway activation has been proposed as a molecular chaperone strategy to stabilize mutant cytoskeletal proteins, while statins (HMG‐CoA reductase inhibitors) may reduce vascular inflammation and favorably modulate eNOS activity [15]. However, no HSF1‐targeted compound is currently approved for clinical use, and both approaches likewise represent downstream rather than corrective strategies. Thus, although these alternative pathways target biologically relevant downstream remodeling mechanisms, sapropterin was prioritized because it was predicted to directly fit the mutation‐created pocket, act as a molecular glue, and stabilize the mutant actin fiber itself. Beyond direct actin stabilization, sapropterin's additional advantages include its established pediatric safety profile, oral bioavailability, and blood–brain barrier penetration. Furthermore, its potential secondary mechanisms, including the restoration of eNOS coupling, regulation of eNOS activity, and modulation of cellular redox balance, provide a dual mechanistic rationale that makes sapropterin the most immediately actionable candidate for off‐label use protocols and future prospective trials.

Treatment with Kuvan in our patient led to improvements consistent across multiple domains: vascular imaging as measured by ICA ratios and TCD velocities, clinical symptoms including gastrointestinal, exercise tolerance, appetite, and physical health PedsQL scores, and cellular assays, supporting biological plausibility. The decline in psychosocial quality‐of‐life scores may reflect age‐related psychosocial stressors, which are related to the non‐reversible white matter disease and were less striking at a young age, underscoring the complexity of outcome measurement in progressive pediatric neurogenetic, neurovascular syndromes. Importantly, the drug is not expected to reverse established pathology but to slow further large‐vessel deterioration.

This study has limitations: cellular analyses were performed in patient‐derived fibroblasts, which do not fully recapitulate vascular smooth muscle cell physiology. However, their highly conserved cytoskeletal machinery allows for assessment of actin filament assembly and stability. Therefore, they provide a tractable system to assess mutation‐associated cytoskeletal defects and pharmacologic response. Future studies employing iPSC‐derived vascular smooth muscle cells or organoid models to directly interrogate sapropterin's effects in the relevant cellular context are needed. In addition, while we observe consistent stabilization in cellular phenotypes and clinical features, distinguishing direct actin stabilization from other mechanisms, including eNOS‐mediated vascular rescue, deserves further investigation, and the precise molecular basis underlying these effects remains to be fully defined. Other limitations include the single‐patient off‐label use design, limiting generalizability, and a lack of formal systematic neuropsychological assessment incorporated into the monitoring protocol. Despite these limitations, our study provides a cellular and clinical proof of concept supporting sapropterin as a potential therapeutic strategy in ACTA2‐related MSMDS. By integrating computational modeling, patient‐derived cellular assays, and longitudinal clinical monitoring, our findings highlight a translational approach to targeting smooth muscle dysfunction in this disease, demonstrating functional rescue of actin polymerization and stabilization of cerebrovascular disease over 18 months of therapy. These results provide a compelling justification that drug repurposing can accelerate therapeutic discovery in ultra‐rare vasculopathies and offer a rationale for further evaluation of sapropterin in pre‐clinical research studies and larger, multicenter studies of ACTA2‐associated vasculopathies.
