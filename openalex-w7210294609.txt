Opening the Door for the New Era of Managing Sepsis-induced Coagulopathy
Kenta KONDO1), Makoto HIKI1), Toshiaki IBA1, 2), Yutaka KONDO1)
1)Department of Emergency and Disaster Medicine, Juntendo University Graduate School of Medicine, Tokyo, Japan

Sepsis-associated disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) is a dynamic thromboinflammatory syndrome characterized
by dysregulated coagulation, impaired fibrinolysis, platelet and leukocyte activation, endothelial injury, and microvascular
thrombosis, ultimately leading to organ dysfunction and increased mortality. Recent advances in the understanding of its pathophysiology have shifted the concept of DIC from an irreversible end-stage complication to a potentially reversible condition
when recognized and treated early. The updated 2025 International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH) definition
emphasizes disease progression from an early, often asymptomatic phase to overt DIC, supporting stage-specific diagnosis and
intervention. In particular, recognition of sepsis-induced coagulopathy (SIC) as a precursor to overt DIC provides a valuable
therapeutic window before irreversible organ damage develops. Current therapeutic strategies include heparins, physiological
anticoagulants, and recombinant thrombomodulin, although their clinical benefits remain inconsistent because of patient heterogeneity and bleeding complications. Emerging approaches, including factor XI inhibitors, neutrophil elastase inhibitors, targeted
fibrinolytic therapies, and nanotechnology-based drug delivery systems, aim to preserve antithrombotic efficacy while minimizing bleeding risk. Future management of sepsis-associated DIC will depend on earlier diagnosis, accurate disease staging,
risk stratification, and personalized therapeutic strategies tailored to distinct coagulation phenotypes, thereby opening a new
era in improving outcomes for patients with septic coagulopathy.

Thromboinflammation in sepsis-associated DIC
The pathophysiology of sepsis-associated disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) is highly
intricate, involving multiple overlapping mechanisms
that disrupt hemostatic balance1). While activation
of the coagulation cascade is a hallmark feature,
additional factors such as impaired fibrinolysis,
excessive platelet and leukocyte activation, damageassociated molecular patterns (DAMPs), and neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) release, and widespread endothelial dysfunction contribute significantly to disease progression. Fibrinolysis is commonly
suppressed following the temporal activation due

to elevated levels of plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1) and thrombin-activatable fibrinolysis inhibitor (TAFI), reducing clot breakdown2).
Platelets and leukocytes, beyond their fundamental
roles, exhibit proinflammatory and procoagulant
functions. Activated platelets express P-selectin
and toll-like receptors (TLRs), enhancing leukocyte
recruitment and complement activation3). Simultaneously, endothelial cells lose their protective glycocalyx layer in response to systemic inflammation,
exposing tissue factor (TF) and von Willebrand
factor, which drive thrombin generation and promote
platelet aggregation. This culminates in a hypercoagulable state, marked by microvascular throm-

A paradigm shift in DIC diagnosis
DIC has traditionally been regarded as a critical
and largely untreatable complication of various
underlying diseases, including sepsis, trauma, and
malignancy. It was commonly viewed as a decompensated end-stage process in which therapeutic
interventions had minimal impact, and clinical
management was often limited to supportive care7).
However, this perception is shifting. Recognizing
that early identification and intervention may alter
the clinical course of DIC, the International Society
on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH) Scientific
and Standardization Committee released an updated
definition and diagnostic criteria for DIC in 20258).

This new framework redefines DIC as “an acquired,
life-threatening intravascular disorder characterized by systemic coagulation activation, dysregulated fibrinolysis, and endothelial injury, resulting
in microthrombosis. DIC arises from various underlying etiologies and progresses from a potentially
asymptomatic early phase to an advanced phase
with hemorrhage and/or organ dysfunction.” Importantly, the ISTH now acknowledges that DIC evolves
progressively—from a preclinical or early phase
with subtle biomarker changes to an overt stage
associated with severe symptoms. Furthermore,
the pathophysiology of the early phase is considerably different based on the underlying diseases.
Based on this understanding, the ISTH proposes
tailored diagnostic criteria for both early and overt
phases of DIC. The recognition of sepsis-induced
coagulopathy (SIC) as a precursor to overt DIC
has provided a valuable therapeutic window for
early intervention. Recently, a simplified SIC-2
score based solely on platelet count and prothrombin
time has been proposed to facilitate earlier and
more practical identification of sepsis-associated
coagulopathy without relying on organ dysfunction
assessment. Such simplified approaches may enhance
timely patient selection for future anticoagulant

iological anticoagulants, such as protein C and antithrombin, have been known to have dual antithrombotic and anti-inflammatory properties, and
were expected to be beneficial for treating severe
sepsis. However, the results of the phase 3 trials
were disappointing, and further studies examining
the effects of these anticoagulants on septic DIC
have not been done12). Recombinant thrombomodulin is another anticoagulant that is currently used
for DIC in Japan. It has shown potential in modulating both coagulation and inflammation in sepsis.
However, its global acceptance remains limited due
to inconclusive results in large international trials,
emphasizing the need for further studies to identify
responsive patient subgroups13). It is noteworthy
that although all the clinical studies using anticoagulants tended to show a beneficial effect, bleeding
side effects cancel out their efficacy. Therefore, it
will be difficult to succeed in clinical trials unless
this obstacle is overcome. Beyond conventional
anticoagulant therapies, a neutrophil elastase inhibitor (sivelestat) is expected to prevent tissue damage
from activated neutrophils, especially to endothelial
cells, and mitigate the progression of septic DIC14).
However, a prospective randomized trial is still
lacking. Factor XI (FXI) inhibitors are emerging
anticoagulant agents designed to prevent thrombosis by targeting the intrinsic coagulation pathway.
Their unique mechanism offers the potential to
reduce clot formation with a lower risk of bleeding
compared to traditional anticoagulants. Animal
models have demonstrated that FXI deficiency or
inhibition can improve survival and reduce coagulopathy in sepsis-induced DIC scenarios15). While
FXI inhibitors have shown promise in reducing
thrombotic events with minimal bleeding, there is
currently a lack of clinical trials specifically evaluating their efficacy in DIC. Other than anticoagulant therapies, fibrinolytic therapy can be expected,
especially for patients with a fibrinolysis-suppressive type of DIC. The nanogel spheres loaded with
tPA are under development16). There are some other
advancements that represent promising directions
in DIC therapy, focusing on targeted interventions
that address both coagulation abnormalities and
inflammatory processes. While some of them are
still under investigation, they highlight the ongoing
efforts to improve outcomes for patients with sepsisassociated DIC. Overall, managing sepsis-induced

Conclusion
Sepsis-associated DIC should no longer be viewed
as an inevitable terminal complication but as a
dynamic and potentially modifiable thromboinflammatory syndrome. Advances in the understanding
of coagulation, endothelial biology, and innate immunity have created new opportunities for earlier
diagnosis and more precise intervention. The
updated ISTH definition, together with simplified
diagnostic approaches such as SIC-2, may facilitate
identification of patients during the therapeutic
window when anticoagulant strategies are most
likely to be effective. This transition from treating
overt DIC to identifying and targeting early thromboinflammation represents a fundamental paradigm
shift in sepsis-associated DIC17). The integration of
earlier diagnosis, biomarker-guided patient selection, and safer precision therapeutics may ultimately transform the management of sepsis-associated DIC. By shifting the focus from overt DIC to
the early thromboinflammatory phase, emerging
diagnostic strategies and precision therapeutics
may finally open the door to a new era in the
management of sepsis-induced coagulopathy.
