Plastome evolution in Aurantioideae and plastome-based classification of Citrus

Eranga Pawani Witharana, Myat Htoo San, Nadeeka U Jayawardana, Nobuhiro Kotoda, Masashi Yamamoto, Yukio Nagano

ABSTRACT
Plastomes are widely used in plant systematics and also retain signals of structural evolution. In this study, we investigated plastome evolution in the subfamily Aurantioideae of Rutaceae, focusing on structural variation at the LSC–IR boundary regions, putative pseudogenization of plastid genes, plastome-based classification of Citrus, and divergence-time patterns. Comparative analyses revealed substantial structural variation around the rpl22 and rps19 regions at the LSC–IR boundaries and repeated pseudogenization of plastid genes such as rpl22, ycf68, and orf56. Additional lineage-specific sequence disruptions were detected in cemA, ycf15, rps4, and ycf1. Phylogenetic analyses of complete plastomes from 56 Citrus accessions resolved eight major plastome clusters within Citrus. Divergence-time analysis indicated two comparable Asia–Africa splits within Aurantioideae, whereas the divergence between Australian and Asian Citrus lineages was more recent. These results show that plastome evolution in Aurantioideae combines phylogenetically informative sequence variation with recurrent local structural change and provides useful information for classification and evolutionary interpretation in this economically important plant group.

Introduction Plastomes have become widely used resources in plant systematics because they can be assembled efficiently from genome-skimming data and provide abundant phylogenetic information for tracing plastome lineages [ 1 , 2 ]. Their relatively conserved organization, uniparental inheritance, and moderate genome size make plastomes particularly useful for resolving relationships among closely related taxa [ 1 ]. At the same time, complete plastome sequences offer more than substitution-based phylogenetic signal alone and provide additional insight into genome evolution, including structural rearrangements [ 3 ]. The subfamily Aurantioideae of the family Rutaceae provides an excellent system for exploring such plastome evolution. This group includes Citrus and its relatives, which are of major agricultural and economic importance [ 4 , 5 ]. Some members of Aurantioideae, including bael ( Aegle marmelos ), wood apple ( Limonia acidissima ), and curry tree ( Murraya koenigii ), have long histories of cultivation and human use, making their evolutionary relationships relevant not only to systematics but also to germplasm management, breeding, and the interpretation of crop origins [ 6 – 8 ]. Within this subfamily, Citrus is especially challenging because its taxonomy has been complicated by extensive morphological variation and a long history of hybridization [ 5 , 7 ]. Previous studies have shown that phylogenetic relationships in Aurantioideae are not always consistent across genomic compartments [ 4 , 9 ]. Trees based on plastomes may differ from those based on nuclear data, reflecting differences in inheritance and evolutionary processes. Whereas plastome DNA traces a single lineage, nuclear genomes may retain signals of incomplete lineage sorting, introgression, and hybrid origin [ 9 ]. Despite these limitations, plastome data remain highly informative because they provide a coherent framework for tracing maternal lineages, and complete plastome sequences are particularly useful for examining diversity within taxonomically complex groups such as Citrus [ 10 , 11 ]. In addition to phylogenetic reconstruction, plastome data are valuable for understanding structural aspects of genome evolution. Although plastomes in land plants are often regarded as structurally conserved, comparative studies have revealed lineage-specific rearrangements and variation around junctions between genomic regions, including the boundaries between the large single-copy region (LSC) and the inverted repeat regions (IRs) [ 3 ]. These architectural features can complement sequence-based phylogenies and may help clarify patterns that are otherwise difficult to interpret. In Aurantioideae, such information is especially relevant because both deep divergences among major lineages and more recent differentiation within Citrus are important for understanding the evolutionary history of the group [ 11 , 12 ]. Gene pseudogenization represents another aspect of plastome evolution that can occur within an otherwise conserved genomic framework [ 13 ]. Pseudogenes are gene-derived sequences that have lost their original coding capacity; in comparative plastome analyses, putative pseudogenes are generally identified by disruptions such as frameshift mutations, premature termination codons, loss of recognizable start codons, or substantial deletions [ 14 ]. Such changes have been documented across diverse plant lineages and may accumulate under relaxed selective constraints or, in some cases, following functional replacement by a nuclear-encoded copy [ 14 , 15 ]. Pseudogenization can also accompany structural changes, including shifts in IR boundaries that generate truncated gene copies, linking variation in gene integrity with plastome architecture. However, sequence disruption alone does not establish the underlying evolutionary mechanism or demonstrate loss of biological function, particularly for putative coding regions whose functional status remains uncertain [ 16 ]. In Aurantioideae, examining these disruptions alongside boundary variation and plastome phylogeny provides an opportunity to distinguish shared patterns from lineage-specific changes within a taxonomically complex group. Accordingly, comparative assessment of gene integrity can complement structural and phylogenetic analyses without implying experimental validation of gene function. Here, we investigate plastome conservation and localized variation in Aurantioideae within a phylogenetic and temporal framework. This study builds on previously generated sequencing resources and plastome analyses presented in our earlier bioRxiv preprint [ 17 ], complementing our peer-reviewed studies of plastome–nuclear phylogenetic discordance [ 9 ] and a large plastome inversion [ 3 ]. We first characterize plastome organization and gene content across the subfamily, then examine structural variation at the LSC–IR boundaries and recurrent patterns of putative gene pseudogenization. We also investigate additional lineage-specific coding-sequence disruptions affecting cemA , ycf15 , rps4 , and ycf1 . Subsequently, we use whole-plastome phylogenetic analysis to assess maternal-lineage relationships within Citrus and estimate divergence times among major Aurantioideae lineages, with particular attention to Asia–Africa disjunctions. Together, these analyses reveal broadly conserved plastome organization alongside localized structural and coding-sequence variation, while providing a phylogenetic and temporal context for understanding plastome diversification within the subfamily. Materials and methods Plant materials, plastome dataset, assembly, and annotation The present study investigated plastome evolution within the subfamily Aurantioideae of the family Rutaceae. A total of 103 plastome accessions were analyzed, comprising 88 Aurantioideae accessions and 15 outgroup taxa. The dataset included 29 accessions for which sequencing data had been generated in our previous studies [ 3 , 9 ], 15 accessions represented by publicly available raw sequencing datasets, and 59 accessions represented by publicly available complete plastome sequences. Thus, no new sequencing data were generated specifically for the present study. A complete list of the taxa, data sources, and corresponding sequence accession numbers is provided in S1 Table . Different subsets of this dataset were used for specific analyses. Pseudogenization patterns were examined in 45 Aurantioideae taxa together with 14 additional Rutaceae accessions for comparative purposes. Plastome-based classification within Citrus was conducted using 56 Citrus accessions. Divergence-time estimation focused primarily on Aurantioideae and included four additional Rutaceae accessions, with Ailanthus altissima designated as the outgroup. For the 29 accessions generated in our previous studies [ 3 , 9 ], DNA originated from the same extraction batches used in those studies. DNA quality was evaluated by electrophoresis on a 1% agarose gel, and DNA concentration was determined using a DNA BR Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Sequencing libraries were prepared from total genomic DNA by Novogene (Singapore) using the NEBNext Ultra DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs, USA) and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate 150-bp paired-end reads. In addition, raw sequencing data for 15 accessions were obtained from public databases. Accession numbers and data sources for all raw sequencing datasets are provided in S1 Table . Raw reads used for plastome assembly were preprocessed using fastp v0.23.2 with default settings [ 18 ]. Summary statistics for the filtered reads, including the number of read pairs, total filtered bases, average read length, Q20 and Q30 percentages, and GC content, were calculated using a custom Python script ( S2 Table ). De novo plastome assembly and annotation were performed following the procedures described in our previous study [ 9 ]. Plastomes were assembled from the 44 raw-read datasets using GetOrganelle v1.7.5 [ 19 ] with the parameters -R 15, -k 21,45,65,85,105, and -F embplant_pt. Assembly quality was evaluated by mapping the quality-filtered reads back to the corresponding assembled plastomes using Bowtie2 v2.4.4 [ 20 ]. The resulting alignments were processed using SAMtools v1.14 [ 21 ] and visually inspected in Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) to assess read coverage and assembly consistency. Qualimap v2.1.1 [ 22 ] was used to obtain quantitative mapping statistics, including the percentage of reads mapped to the assembled plastome, mean sequencing coverage, and mean mapping quality ( S2 Table ). Plastome annotation was performed using GeSeq v2.03 [ 23 ], with protein-coding sequences predicted using Chloë v0.1.0, tRNA genes identified using tRNAscan-SE v2.0.7 [ 24 ], and rRNA genes identified using HMMER. Following automated annotation, all plastome annotations were manually inspected and corrected where necessary to ensure annotation accuracy and consistency. Final circular plastome maps were generated using OGDRAW v1.3.1 [ 25 ]. Accession numbers for the source raw sequencing reads and previously deposited plastome sequences, together with the corresponding data sources and information on plant materials processed by our group, are provided in S1 Table . The 15 plastomes assembled de novo from additional public raw-read datasets and the re-annotated plastome files derived from 59 public complete plastome sequences, manually curated where necessary, are available in the Supplementary Dataset deposited in Zenodo ( https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.22743534 ). Comparative plastome sequence and LSC–IR boundary analyses To assess sequence conservation and divergence among representative Aurantioideae plastomes, whole-plastome sequence comparisons were performed using mVISTA [ 26 ] with the Shuffle-LAGAN alignment mode [ 27 ], with the Citrus sinensis plastome used as the reference. Representative taxa included Aegle marmelos , Atalantia bilocularis , A. ceylanica , A. roxburghiana , Citropsis gabunensis , Clausena anisata , Citrus glauca , Limonia acidissima , Glycosmis citrifolia , Naringi crenulata , Murraya caloxylon , Micromelum minutum , Murraya koenigii , Murraya paniculata , Paramignya lobata , Swinglea glutinosa , and Triphasia trifolia . The resulting alignments were examined to evaluate patterns of sequence conservation and divergence across the plastomes. To further investigate structural variation at plastome junctions, boundary regions were manually examined and compared among representative Aurantioideae plastomes, with particular attention to the junctions between the LSC and the IRs. The analysis focused especially on the LSC–IR boundary regions containing rpl22 and rps19 . Structural differences, including gene fragmentation, pseudogenization, and local gene loss, were identified manually by comparing gene order, gene orientation, and junction positions among the annotated plastomes. Whole-plastome alignment and phylogenetic analyses Whole-plastome alignment and phylogenetic analyses were performed as in the previous study [ 9 ]. Complete plastome sequences were aligned using HomBlocks (v1.0) [ 28 ] with default settings. The best-fit evolutionary model was identified using ModelTest-NG (v0.1.7) [ 29 ]. Maximum-likelihood (ML) trees were constructed using RAxML (v8.2.12) [ 30 ] with 1,000 bootstrap replicates. Bayesian inference (BI) trees were generated using BEAST (v1.10.4) [ 31 ] with Markov chain Monte Carlo sampling for 10,000,000 generations, sampling every 10,000 generations and discarding the first 10% as burn-in. ML and BI trees were visualized using Dendroscope (v3.8.8) and FigTree (v1.4.4), respectively. In the present study, this framework was applied to the Citrus plastome dataset and the Aurantioideae-centered datasets used for comparative analyses. Identification and phylogenetic analysis of pseudogenes Protein sequences were extracted from annotation files using genbank_to (v0.42) [ 32 ]. Orthologous protein groups were identified using OrthoFinder (v2.5.4) [ 33 ], and amino acid sequences were aligned using MAFFT (v7.515) [ 34 ]. The resulting alignments were inspected in MEGA11 (v11.0.13) [ 35 ] for comparative analysis. Pseudogenes were identified on the basis of frameshift-causing insertions or deletions, premature stop codons, or loss of recognizable start codons, with comparisons made against intact homologous sequences. This strategy was applied to analyses of rpl22 , ycf68 , orf56 , cemA , ycf15 , rps4 , and ycf1 . Where raw sequencing reads were available, candidate pseudogenization events were further validated by visual inspection of read alignments in IGV. Read coverage across the affected regions was examined to confirm that the sequence variants responsible for pseudogenization were supported by the underlying raw reads rather than resulting from assembly or annotation errors. To assess whether similar pseudogenization states corresponded to particular phylogenetic branches, pseudogenization patterns were mapped onto an Aurantioideae-centered plastome phylogeny that included 14 additional Rutaceae accessions for comparison. We focused on the distribution of repeated pseudogenization involving rpl22 , ycf68 , and orf56 , and separately examined lineage-specific events involving cemA , ycf15 , rps4 , and ycf1 . The phylogenetic interpretation of these events was based on the whole-plastome trees described above. Divergence-time estimation Divergence times were estimated using MCMCtree in PAML v4.10.10 [ 36 ] with the approximate-likelihood method [ 37 ]. The ML topology inferred from the plastome alignment using IQ-TREE v2.3.6 [ 38 ] was fixed for molecular dating and rooted with Ailanthus altissima as the outgroup. The analysis included 47 taxa and a 77,661-bp nucleotide alignment. Branch lengths, gradient vectors, and the Hessian matrix required for the approximate-likelihood calculation were estimated using BASEML under the HKY85 + Γ model with five discrete gamma rate categories and subsequently used in MCMCtree [ 37 , 39 , 40 ]. Four temporal constraints were applied following published fossil assessments and divergence estimates. The root separating A. altissima from the sampled Rutaceae was assigned a secondary soft bound of 100–117 Ma [B (1.00, 1.17)] [ 41 ]. Fossil-based soft minimum constraints were assigned to stem Zanthoxylum at 47.8 Ma [L (0.478)] [ 41 ], the stem Clausena + Glycosmis lineage at 27.23 Ma [L (0.2723)] [ 42 ], and stem Citrus at 11.6 Ma [L (0.116)] [ 43 ]. A prior-only analysis (usedata = 0) was first performed to evaluate the effective time priors. Posterior dating was then conducted using the approximate likelihood (usedata = 2) under an independent-rates relaxed clock (clock = 2) with a birth–death-sampling prior (BDparas = 1 1 0.1 m), rgene_gamma = 2 20 1, and sigma2_gamma = 1 10 1. Two independent MCMC chains were run, each with 200,000 burn-in iterations followed by 20,000 retained samples collected every 100 iterations. Convergence was assessed using rank-normalized R-hat and bulk and tail effective sample sizes [ 44 ]. Calibration sensitivity was assessed by replacing the 47.8-Ma minimum-age constraint for Zanthoxylum with a 66-Ma minimum-age constraint based on the fossil Rutaspermum biornatum [ 41 ], while keeping all other analytical settings unchanged. Use of generative AI ChatGPT ( https://chat.openai.com/ ) was used to rewrite the manuscript into more appropriate English and proofread the English text. Subsequently, the authors verified the correctness of the revisions. Results Aurantioideae plastomes were highly conserved with localized genomic and sequence variation A total of 103 plastome accessions were analyzed, comprising 88 Aurantioideae accessions and 15 outgroup taxa ( S3 Table ). Among the Aurantioideae accessions, plastome size ranged from 158,779 bp in Naringi crenulata to 161,172 bp in Clausena excavata . Overall GC content varied by only 0.39 percentage points, ranging from 38.28% in C. excavata to 38.67% in Clausena harmandiana and Clausena lansium . Gene content was also highly uniform across the subfamily. All 88 Aurantioideae plastomes contained 30 unique tRNA genes and four unique rRNA genes. Most accessions (85 of 88) contained 83 unique protein-coding genes, corresponding to a total of 117 unique genes per plastome ( S3 Table ). Whole-plastome sequence conservation was further evaluated among representative Aurantioideae taxa using mVISTA, with Citrus sinensis as the reference plastome ( S1 Fig ). The comparison included representatives from multiple genera across the subfamily, including Aegle , Atalantia , Citropsis , Clausena , Citrus , Glycosmis , Micromelum , Murraya , Naringi , Paramignya , Swinglea , and Triphasia . Coding regions were generally more conserved than non-coding regions, whereas greater sequence divergence was observed mainly in intergenic and other non-coding regions ( S1 Fig ). The inverted-repeat regions, including the regions containing the rRNA genes, were highly conserved across the sampled taxa. In contrast, localized reductions in sequence similarity were evident at several positions within the large single-copy and small single-copy regions. Despite these localized differences, the overall alignment profiles were highly similar among the representative plastomes ( S1 Fig ). Discussion The present study provides an integrated assessment of plastome evolution and diversification within Aurantioideae through comparative plastome analyses, evaluation of pseudogenization patterns, phylogenetic inference, and divergence-time estimation. Aurantioideae plastomes were highly conserved in overall organization and gene content, with localized variation concentrated at LSC–IR boundaries and in genes exhibiting putative pseudogenization. This combination of broad conservation and localized change provides a framework for examining structural and coding-sequence evolution alongside phylogenetic relationships. Some of these plastome-based analyses were initially reported in our earlier preprint [ 17 ] but had not previously been developed in peer-reviewed form. The conserved organization and largely uniform gene content observed among Aurantioideae plastomes indicate that substantial lineage diversification has occurred without extensive changes in their overall genomic architecture. Nevertheless, this conservation does not imply structural or functional uniformity at individual loci. Comparative analyses revealed boundary-associated modifications and disruptive sequence changes that were not apparent from overall plastome size or gene counts alone. Consequently, general plastome characterization and detailed comparisons of individual regions provide complementary perspectives on plastome evolution within the subfamily. Variation at the LSC–IR boundaries was particularly evident around rpl22 and rps19 , including fragmentation, putative pseudogenization, and local gene loss. Similar boundary-associated changes have been reported in other angiosperm lineages. In Acanthaceae, variation at the LSC–IR junction has been associated with pseudogenization of rps19 and, in some taxa, rpl22 [ 45 ], whereas Urticeae exhibits repeated boundary shifts involving these genes and occasional gene loss [ 46 ]. Comparable, although more limited, variation around rps19 has also been documented in Prunus [ 47 ]. These observations show that local structural variation can occur within otherwise conserved plastomes. The repeated occurrence of boundary modifications across lineages suggests that plastome junctions are evolutionarily labile regions. Boundary architecture may therefore provide informative evolutionary characters, although recurrent changes should be considered when interpreting their distribution across a phylogeny. The distribution of putative pseudogenization events further indicates that localized plastome evolution in Aurantioideae has involved changes at multiple loci. Recurrent sequence disruptions affecting rpl22 , ycf68 , and orf56 suggest that coding-sequence degeneration has occurred through different mutational routes. Comparable patterns have been reported elsewhere in angiosperms. For example, infA has been independently lost or pseudogenized multiple times, in several cases following functional transfer to the nucleus [ 48 ], while repeated plastome loss of rpl22 has been associated with nuclear functional replacement [ 49 , 50 ]. By contrast, ycf68 appears to be nonfunctional or weakly constrained in many angiosperms [ 51 ], emphasizing the need for caution when interpreting sequence disruptions in genes of uncertain functional status. The patterns detected here may therefore reflect several evolutionary processes, including relaxed selective constraint, lineage-specific structural changes, or, for some genes, functional replacement outside the plastome. However, these mechanisms cannot be distinguished using plastome sequences alone. Recurrent disruptions also caution against treating individual putative pseudogenization events as definitive phylogenetic characters. In addition to these more broadly distributed patterns, several lineage-specific sequence disruptions warrant further investigation. In Afraegle paniculata , the putative pseudogenization of cemA [ 52 ] raises questions about whether its function has been retained or compensated through another mechanism. Possible explanations include compensation involving the nuclear-encoded DLDG1 [ 53 ] or functional transfer of cemA to the nucleus followed by acquisition of a plastid-targeting transit peptide. Neither possibility, however, can be established from the present data. The putative pseudogenization of ycf15 in Aegle marmelos is best interpreted primarily as a lineage-specific sequence feature because the functional status of this gene remains uncertain in many angiosperms [ 16 ]. The disruption observed in rps4 in Citrus sinensis is also noteworthy because the sweet orange plastome has been widely used as a reference in citrus plastome studies, yet this feature was not explicitly discussed in the original plastome report [ 54 ]. In Atalantia kwangtungensis , we recovered sequence-level evidence consistent with the previously reported putative pseudogenization of accD [ 55 ] and additionally identified disruptive changes in ycf1 . The accD gene encodes a subunit of acetyl-CoA carboxylase [ 56 , 57 ], whereas ycf1 has been proposed to encode a component of the plastid protein translocon [ 58 ]. Because co-evolution between accD and ycf1 has previously been suggested [ 58 ], the occurrence of disruptive changes in both genes within the same lineage may warrant further functional investigation. Their co-occurrence alone, however, does not demonstrate a functional relationship or a shared mechanism of degeneration. Collectively, the lineage-specific changes identified here provide sequence-level evidence of putative pseudogenization. Raw-read support for the underlying mutations strengthens confidence in the sequence observations but does not establish loss of gene function. Transcriptomic, nuclear-genome, and functional analyses will be required to determine whether these changes are associated with functional loss, compensation, or gene transfer. Alongside the localized structural and coding-sequence changes identified here, the whole-plastome analysis of 56 accessions recovered eight major clusters within the sampled Citrus assemblage: Pummelo, Papeda, Ichang Papeda/Yuzu, Mandarin, Poncirus, Fortunella, Australian, and Citron. Clymenia polyandra was sister to the sampled Australian species, with this assemblage sister to the Citron cluster. Its position within the true citrus group agrees with the expanded circumscription of Citrus supported by Bayer et al. (2009) [ 12 ]. Although the name Clymenia polyandra is retained here following the NCBI taxonomy database, Plants of the World Online (POWO) recognizes it as a synonym of Citrus polyandra ; its inclusion within Citrus therefore accords with established phylogenetic and taxonomic treatments. Several relationships corroborate earlier plastome studies. Our previous matK analysis of 93 accessions identified three principal groups corresponding to citron, pummelo, and mandarin, with Poncirus and Fortunella outside these groups [ 10 ]. Subsequently, Carbonell-Caballero et al. (2015), analyzing 34 plastomes, associated citron with Australian citrus, pummelo with C. micrantha , and mandarins with Ichang papeda, while recognizing Poncirus and Fortunella as distinct lineages [ 11 ]. Our tree similarly places the Citron cluster sister to the Australian assemblage, recovers sister Pummelo and Papeda clusters, and associates Ichang Papeda/Yuzu with the broader mandarin-related assemblage, while retaining distinct Poncirus and Fortunella clusters. The eight-cluster scheme therefore describes finer subdivisions within several previously recognized broader relationships. At the accession level, the placement of C. sinensis , C. aurantium , C. × paradisi , and C. limon within the Pummelo cluster, the association of C. aurantifolia with C. micrantha , and the inclusion of C. limonia within the Mandarin cluster are consistent with the maternal affinities reported by Carbonell-Caballero et al. (2015) [ 11 ]. These relationships illustrate how cultivated accessions with different taxonomic identities can share closely related plastomes, reflecting their maternal ancestry. Relative to our earlier matK analysis, which emphasized citron, pummelo, and mandarin groups [ 10 ], the present tree provides a more differentiated description of the sampled plastome lineages. However, Jena et al. (2009) also recovered eight major clusters using PCR-RFLP data, so the cluster count itself should not be regarded as novel [ 59 ]. Our contribution is to reconstruct and characterize these relationships using complete plastome sequences, extending sequence coverage beyond selected markers. Oueslati et al. (2016), by comparison, developed diagnostic SNP markers from eight plastome regions to identify established Aurantioideae clades [ 8 ]. Their marker-based identification and our phylogenetic reconstruction serve related purposes, but their clades span multiple taxonomic levels and are not directly equivalent to our eight clusters. The occurrence of cultivated hybrids within different plastome clusters also illustrates why these groups should be interpreted as maternal lineages rather than species boundaries. Nuclear genomic analyses have demonstrated extensive hybridization and admixture in Citrus , explaining how accessions sharing a plastome lineage can have different nuclear ancestries [ 5 ]. Our phylogeny therefore provides a framework for interpreting the structural variation, putative pseudogenization, and divergence times examined here, while nuclear evidence remains essential for reconstructing the broader reticulate history of Citrus . The divergence-time analysis extends this phylogenetic framework by placing major lineage separations within a temporal context. The estimated separations between Citropsis and Naringi and between Afraegle and Aegle , approximately 19.5 and 18.1 Ma, respectively, fall within the early Miocene. These broadly comparable estimates suggest a temporal parallel between two lineages exhibiting present-day Asia–Africa disjunctions, although similar point estimates do not establish synchronous geographic isolation or a shared cause. Progressive Himalayan–Tibetan uplift and associated changes in atmospheric circulation, monsoon dynamics, and habitat distribution provide a possible environmental context for changes in the ranges of their Asian ancestors [ 60 ]. However, these prolonged geological processes cannot be directly linked to the inferred separations, and dispersal, range fragmentation, and regional extinction remain alternative explanations for the observed distributions. The younger divergences involving Australian Citrus may instead be considered in the context of changing dispersal opportunities between Southeast Asia and Australasia. Increased Miocene floristic exchange between the Sunda and Sahul regions provides a plausible regional framework but does not establish the route or timing of Citrus migration into Australia [ 61 ]. Wu et al. (2018) previously inferred a late Miocene radiation of Asian Citrus , followed by an early Pliocene radiation of Australian limes [ 5 ]. The present study offers a complementary plastome-based assessment within a broader Aurantioideae framework. However, the deeper separations examined here are not equivalent to diversification within the Australian clade and therefore do not directly confirm the previously reported radiation age. These findings highlight temporal variation among geographically differentiated lineages within Aurantioideae. Their biogeographic interpretation remains conditional on age uncertainty and the distinction between plastome divergence and geographic separation. Ancestral-range reconstruction and independent nuclear evidence will be needed to evaluate the processes underlying these distributions. Overall, Aurantioideae plastomes combine broadly conserved organization and gene content with localized boundary modifications and lineage-specific sequence degeneration. Complete plastome sequences also provide a detailed framework for interpreting maternal-lineage relationships within Citrus and estimating the timing of major lineage divergences. Integrating these perspectives extends the contribution of plastome data beyond sequence-based phylogenetic inference to the study of structural evolution, putative pseudogenization, and lineage diversification within Aurantioideae.
