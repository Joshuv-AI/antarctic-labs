Alaska Village Electric
Cooperative (AVEC), the community of Noatak, and regional partners to design, procure, and
install a 280kW solar photovoltaic array. The system is complemented by a Battery Energy
Storage System (BESS) rated at 495 kWh with a 500kW inverter.

A local Independent Power Producer (IPP)—the Tribe in Noatak—was established in March 2022
as part of NAB’s Economic Development program. The IPP executed a Power Purchase
Agreement (PPA) with AVEC, creating a mechanism for project revenues. The project also
included upgrades to powerhouse controls and switchgear, which enable diesel-off operation
and reliable integration of renewable power into Noatak’s grid.

The project followed earlier initiatives such as a small solar installation at the water treatment
plant, LED streetlight retrofits, and bulk fuel studies. These efforts laid the groundwork for
Noatak’s transition from pilots to a full renewable microgrid.

Since commissioning, the project has produced 255,000 kWh of solar power, displaced nearly
301 hours of diesel runtime, delivered 230,179 kWh to AVEC, and generated $ 123,938.04 in
net income after expenses. For families in Noatak, this means fewer fuel deliveries by air, more
stability in household electricity bills, and a stronger foundation for a locally managed energy
future.

2. Background
The NAB is a home-rule government created in 1986 that includes eleven communities in
Northwest Alaska, among them Noatak. In partnership with the NANA Regional Corporation,
NAB has worked for more than a decade to reduce reliance on imported diesel and to advance
clean energy solutions across the region.

Noatak (Nautaaq) is a traditional Iñupiaq community located about 70 miles north of the Arctic
Circle. The village has a population of about 580 and is only accessible by air or water. In recent
years, changes in river conditions have eliminated barge access, which means all fuel must now
be flown in. This has driven up costs and made Noatak one of the most expensive places in
Alaska for electricity and fuel. For AVEC, which manages 60 diesel power plants across Alaska,
Noatak is the single most expensive location to generate and provide power for the community.
For local residents, this translates into high utility bills on top of already steep living costs.

The community depends on diesel generation, supported by three diesel GenSet units operated
by the AVEC. Even with efficient operations, reliance on flown-in fuel leaves Noatak exposed to
supply risks and high costs. Bulk fuel storage is distributed among several local entities, but
some facilities are aging and face risks from shoreline erosion. The harsh Arctic climate, with
long winters and more than 15,000 heating degree days annually, adds further strain.

For years, community leaders have pushed for renewable energy as part of a larger effort to
make life more affordable and to strengthen local control over energy decisions. That effort has
unfolded in steps. In 2013, Noatak installed an 11kW solar array at the water treatment plant,
which continues to operate today. Two years later, the Borough retrofitted 22 streetlights with
LEDs, saving an estimated $2.4 million and more than 900,000 gallons of diesel over 25 years.

Future projects aim to make energy more affordable and resilient at every level. A community-
wide residential LED upgrade is being explored, along with improvements to the water
treatment plant, which is threatened by unstable ground. A new fuel line from the airport to
AVEC’s tanks would ease the logistical burden of the final leg in delivering flown-in fuel to the
powerplant for power generation. Discussions with the Northwest Inupiat Housing Authority
have also emphasized the importance of building efficiency into new homes.

These projects align with the community’s broader vision of reducing reliance on flown-in diesel
while strengthening local resilience. The work, however, expands past just adding renewable
generation. It is also about making homes and public systems more efficient, ensuring heating
remains dependable through long winters, and giving operators the knowledge and support to
manage these systems into the future. Each step forward eases the strain of high fuel costs on
families and helps make life in Noatak more sustainable.

3. Project Objectives
The project’s overarching goals were to reduce diesel use, stabilize energy costs, and strengthen
Tribal energy security while sustaining renewable generation project momentum in Noatak. It
also tested approaches for Tribal-led IPPs that could be scaled across the NAB and other rural
Alaska communities. By following earlier efforts such as smaller solar installations and energy
efficiency upgrades, this project represented the next step in ongoing clean energy vision.

The specific objectives of the project were:

Solar and battery system construction
The project sought to deliver a solar PV and battery storage system for Noatak, designed to
operate reliably in Arctic conditions while offsetting a meaningful share of diesel use.

Advanced controls integration
Incorporate microgrid control technology to enable diesel-off operation and efficient dispatch of
renewable energy.

IPP and Governance Structure
Administer workforce training and establish an IPP, ensuring that revenues remain in the
community and new jobs are tied to the operation of the system.

Replicability and regional scaling
Demonstrate that both the technical design and the administrative structure for the IPP used in
Shungnak could be repeated in Noatak under similar PPA terms with AVEC. This objective
supported NAB’s broader plan to expand Tribal IPPs and create a Renewable Energy
Performance Optimization Program (REPOP) to assist future projects.

4. Description of Activities Performed
4.1. Solar and Battery System Planning and Construction
The project installed a 280kW solar photovoltaic array paired with a 495kWh / 500kW Battery
Energy Storage System. The work included site preparation, procurement, installation of panels
and inverters, and integration with the local grid. The solar arrays were constructed in two
orientations, designed to extend periods of consistent energy production rather than
concentrate output into shorter peaks. Associated upgrades to powerhouse switchgear were
also completed, allowing automated diesel-off operation and more reliable use of renewable

4.2. Established an IPP to deliver energy to AVEC and create a revenue source

NAB established systems to manage IPP revenues and expenses on behalf of the Tribe which
included developing a clear invoicing process with AVEC for renewable power sales, ensuring
that monthly meter readings and billing were verified and submitted on time. NAB also
assumed responsibility for paying battery station service charges directly to avoid delays or
confusion, while maintaining Tribal utility accounts to preserve eligibility for PCE rates. In
addition, procedures were put in place for reimbursing the Tribe for operator wages and other
expenses, ensuring that IPP revenues supported ongoing operations.

The solar arrays in Noatak were constructed in two orientations to stagger peak output and
extend the duration of usable solar generation. Monitoring shows this design smooths daily
production, with one array capturing earlier sunlight and the other extending output later in
the day. This approach better matches community load profiles and maximizes usable solar
under Arctic daylight conditions as well as reduces impacts to the grid from instantaneous
power drop-offs associated with cloud cover of the sun.

Performance of the Noatak renewable energy project has not met initial projections, falling
short due to a combination of technical integration issues and an urgent environmental threat.
The project was initially expected to offset 10–15% of Noatak’s annual energy needs, but in
Fiscal Year 2024, the displacement was only about 6%. While this still represents meaningful
progress in reducing reliance on diesel, the performance gap highlights unanticipated technical
challenges. Specifically, although AVEC’s diesel powerplant was upgraded to facilitate the
integration of intermittent renewables, the high penetration of solar PV and the ability to
operate in a diesels-off mode created unforeseen operational issues. During high solar
generation periods, the diesel engines would rapidly cool to the ambient Arctic air
temperature. AVEC determined that repeatedly cycling the gensets on and off at these low
temperatures would severely compromise the long-term reliability of the diesel engines,
starters, and power plant breakers.

In response to this risk, AVEC implemented strict temperature and runtime requirements that
must be met to safely achieve diesels-off operation. To satisfy these new requirements and still
maximize PV production, the project now requires the installation of additional infrastructure
to heat the gensets. Funding for this necessary upgrade has been secured, and once completed,
these modifications are expected to allow the system to meet its original projected PV
production goals.

Adding to these technical hurdles, a more recent and urgent barrier has emerged: the power
plant is now imminently threatened by riverbank erosion. AVEC has responded by flying in and
installing an emergency generator at a secure, separate location. The utility's current focus has
shifted to the complex and time-sensitive task of relocating the entire power plant, which has
necessarily postponed work on the renewable integration issue. Given these combined
factors—the necessary operational safeguards and the emergency power plant relocation—it is
understandable that the overall solar displacement of diesel fuel did not meet original

Noatak represents the second successful example of a Tribal IPP in the region, following the
Shungnak project. This effort proved that the model is replicable, with DOE funding essential to
its success. The PPA with AVEC was nearly identical to that of Shungnak, which simplified
negotiations and built on positive working relationships among the Tribe, NAB, AVEC, NANA,
DeerStone, Ageto, and other contractors.

Technically, the project also benefited from lessons learned in Shungnak. The reuse of an Ageto
microgrid controller and standardized battery inverter and control systems enabled faster
implementation with tighter control over design, budget, and schedule.

Noatak has advanced the broader regional effort to establish Tribal IPPs across the Northwest
Arctic Borough. NAB, NANA, and their partners are now applying the lessons learned to develop
the Renewable Energy Performance Optimization Program (REPOP), a structure designed to
provide long-term technical and financial support for Tribal IPPs across the region. Noatak is a
local success and demonstrates that Tribal IPPs can be scaled technically and institutionally to
reduce diesel use, generate revenues, and strengthen Tribal energy sovereignty in rural Alaska.

5.1 Recommendations

We recommend continued support for Tribal IPP creation
The Noatak project has shown that Tribal Independent Power Producers can be successfully
structured and operated in partnership with regional entities like AVEC. Replicating this model in

other rural communities leveraging previous experience can build on standardized agreements
that save time and lower costs.

We recommend expanded community training and education
Both the fiber line damage and the response to communications issues are evidence that
technical knowledge cannot remain concentrated in a few operators. Training should extend to
community members and contractors whose work may affect renewable infrastructure,
reducing instances of accidental damage.

We recommend ongoing performance monitoring and data analysis
Solar production in Noatak has been lower than expected, which underscores the importance of
long-term tracking. Consistent monitoring and ongoing support for technical troubleshooting
can help refine design assumptions, validate modeling tools, and ensure that operating
strategies match real conditions.

We recommend continued investment in building local expertise
The creation of a local operator position was a direct benefit of this project and an important
step toward regional self-reliance. Expanding hands-on training, peer-to-peer learning, and
professional development opportunities will ensure that advanced renewable systems can be
sustained over time.

6. Lessons Learned

The Noatak project reduced diesel use and proved the IPP model, but several challenges
provided useful lessons.

One complication involved the communications
infrastructure. After construction, a fiber line connecting
the solar array to the microgrid was accidentally damaged
by an excavator. The issue was repaired by a third-party
contractor, but it displayed how vulnerable specialized
equipment can be to routine activities. Community
education should reach beyond the operators who maintain
the system to include anyone whose work might affect
renewable energy infrastructure. Timely repair procedures

The project also faced an unforeseen communications outage between the BESS and the AVEC
generator plant. When the Advanced Remote Controller lost contact with the system, the
controller assumed the plant was offline. The BESS charged to full capacity, and without
regulation the inverter began opening and closing its AC/DC breaker to prevent overcharging.
This produced repeated DC overvoltage trips until communication was restored, and the system
stabilized. The outage highlighted the need for redundant communications links and clear
procedures for restoring service.

These challenges showed that keeping the system reliable depends as much on people as on
equipment. Quick resolution required steady communication between the operator and the
utility, along with outside technical support when needed. Just as important, the lessons from
these incidents need to be written down and carried forward so that training can build on real
experience and future projects start from a stronger foundation.

On a more encouraging note, the process of creating the IPP in Noatak was made easier by
experience gained in Shungnak and other communities in the NAB umbrella. The agreement
with AVEC came together more quickly, and the overall structure was already familiar to those
involved. With that being said, aligning all stakeholders around clear procedures for financial
management took deliberate effort, underscoring that governance can be as challenging as the
technical work. Even so, the progress in Noatak shows how each project leaves behind
knowledge that lowers the barriers for the next, making the IPP model more practical to expand
across the region.

We are grateful for the support that made this project possible and for the partnerships that
continue to grow through this work. The knowledge developed and the relationships built will
extend beyond this single installation, contributing to long-term resilience and energy
sovereignty for Noatak and for other communities in the Northwest Arctic Borough.
