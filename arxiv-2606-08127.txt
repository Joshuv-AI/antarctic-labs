Palindrome complexity versus factor complexity
Jeffrey Shallit

Abstract
Let 𝐱 = ( a i ) i ≥ 0 {\bf x}=(a_{i})_{i\geq 0} be an infinite word over a finite alphabet Σ \Sigma . Let ρ ⁡ ( n ) \rho(n) be the factor complexity function for 𝐱 \bf x and Pal ⁡ ( n ) \Pal(n) be the palindrome complexity function for 𝐱 \bf x . We give a new relationship between these two quantities; namely, if 𝐱 \bf x is not ultimately periodic, then lim n → ∞ Pal ⁡ ( n ) ​ log ⁡ ( Pal ⁡ ( n ) + 1 ) ρ ⁡ ( n ) = 0 . \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{{\Pal(n)\log(\Pal(n)+1)}\over{\rho(n)}}=0. Furthermore, we prove that the numerator in this result is essentially optimal.



1
Introduction

Let
{\bf x}=(a_{i})_{i\geq 0}
be an infinite word over a finite alphabet

\Sigma
. The
factor complexity
(aka
subword complexity
) of

\bf x
is defined to be the function
\rho_{\bf x}(n)

mapping
n
to the number of distinct length-
n
blocks
appearing in
\bf x
. The
palindrome complexity

\Pal_{\bf x}(n)

analogously counts the number of distinct length-
n
palindromes
appearing in
\bf x
.

Both factor complexity and palindrome complexity have been extensively
studied by researchers in combinatorics on words.
For factor
complexity, see, for example,
[
13
]
and the
surveys
[
1
,
7
]
. Many classes, such as the
Sturmian words, are known to have linear factor complexity

[
14
]
. In the other direction,
Grillenberger
[
11
]

constructed words with large factor complexity.
For palindrome complexity, see, for example,

[
8
,
2
]
.

Let us look at two examples.

Example 1
.

Let
{\bf t}=01101001\cdots
be the famous Thue-Morse sequence,
the fixed point of the morphism
0\rightarrow 01
,
1\rightarrow 10
.
Then it is known that

\Pal_{\bf t}(n)=\begin{cases}2,&\text{if $n\in\{1,2,3\}$, or $n$ even and $3\cdot 4^{k}<n\leq 4^{k+1}$ for $k\geq 1$;}\\
4,&\text{if $n$ even, $4^{k}<n\leq 3\cdot 4^{k}$ for $k\geq 1$;}\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

See
[
5
, Eq. (4)]
.

It is also known that

3=\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\rho_{\bf t}(n)/n<\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\rho_{\bf t}(n)/n=10/3.

See
[
6
,
12
,
3
]
.
Therefore
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Pal_{\bf t}(n)/\rho_{\bf t}(n)=0
.

Example 2
.

Let
{\bf b}=11011100101110111\cdots
be
the word consisting of the concatenation of the base-
2
representations
of
n=1,2,3,\ldots
. Then clearly

\rho_{\bf b}(n)=2^{n}
, while

\Pal_{\bf b}(n)=\Theta(2^{n/2})
.
Therefore
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Pal_{\bf b}(n)/\rho_{\bf b}(n)=0
.

Examining these two very different examples, at extremes of
complexity, suggests the following:

Conjecture 3
.

If
\bf x
is not ultimately periodic, then

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{{\Pal_{\bf x}(n)}\over{\rho_{\bf x}(n)}}=0.

In June 2024, Jean-Paul Allouche asked me if this was true.
In fact, even more is true. We will prove

Theorem 4
.

If
\bf x
is not ultimately periodic, then

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{{\Pal_{\bf x}(n)\log(\Pal_{\bf x}(n)+1)}\over{\rho_{\bf x}(n)}}=0.

After discussing notation and presenting
some preliminary lemmas, the proof is given in
Section
5
.
Furthermore, it turns
out that the numerator
\Pal_{\bf x}(n)\log(\Pal_{\bf x}(n)+1)

cannot be significantly improved. This
is addressed in Section
6
.

2
Notation

Throughout this paper,
we assume words are defined over a fixed finite alphabet

\Sigma
, with
k=|\Sigma|
. Infinite words are written
in boldface. If
w
is a finite word with
w=a_{0}\cdots a_{n-1}
, then
by
w[i..j]
we mean
a_{i}\cdots a_{j}
, and similarly for infinite words.

All words are indexed starting at position
0
. If

w=xyz
for finite (possibly empty) words
x,y,z,
then

x
is a
prefix
of
w
,

y
is a
factor
of
w
,
and
z
is a
suffix
of
w
.
A finite word
x=x[0..n-1]
has
period

p
,
1\leq p\leq n
,
if
x[i]=x[i+p]
for
0\leq i<n-p
.

The set of all length-
n
factors of an infinite word
\bf x

is denoted by
\Fac_{\bf x}(n)
, and
the factor complexity function

\rho_{\bf x}(n)
is the cardinality of
\Fac_{\bf x}(n)
.

The reversal of a finite word
w
is written
w^{R}
.
A word is a
palindrome
if
w=w^{R}
.
The palindrome complexity function

\Pal_{\bf x}(n)
counts the number of distinct factors of

\bf x
of length
n
that are palindromes.

In the case of both
\Pal_{\bf x}
and
\rho_{\bf x}
, if

\bf x
is clear from the context, we omit it as a subscript.

3
Previous work

There are some previous results that are relevant and put our result in
context.

Theorem 5
.

If
\bf x
is not ultimately periodic, then

\rho_{\bf x}(n)<\rho_{\bf x}(n+1)
for all
n\geq 0
.
In particular,
\rho_{\bf x}(n)
is unbounded.

Proof.

A classic result of Morse and Hedlund
[
13
]
.
∎

Proposition 6
.

We have
\rho_{\bf x}(m+n)\leq\rho_{\bf x}(m)\rho_{\bf x}(n)

for
m,n\geq 0
.

Proof.

Every factor of length
m+n
is a concatenation of a prefix of length
m

and a suffix of length
n
.
∎

Corollary 7
.

The limit
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{{\log\rho_{\bf x}(n)}\over n}

exists and equals
\inf_{n\geq 1}(\log\rho_{\bf x}(n))/n
.

Proof.

Follows from Fekete’s lemma
[
9
]
applied to the subadditive
sequence
(\log\rho_{\bf x}(n))_{n\geq 0}
.
∎

The next two results are
not
used in this paper, but indicate previous
related work.

Theorem 8

(Theorem 12 of
[
2
]
)
.

If
\bf x
is not ultimately periodic, then

\Pal_{\bf x}(n)<{16\over n}\rho_{\bf x}(n+\lfloor n/4\rfloor)

for all
n\geq 1
.

Theorem 9

(Theorem 1.2 of
[
4
]
)
.

Suppose
\bf x
is uniformly recurrent.

(a)

If the set of factors of
\bf x
is not closed under
reversal, then
\Pal_{\bf x}(n)=0
for all sufficiently large

n
.

(b)

If the set of factors of
\bf x
is closed under reversal,
then

\Pal_{\bf x}(n)+\Pal_{\bf x}(n+1)\leq\rho_{\bf x}(n+1)-\rho_{\bf x}(n)+2

for
n\geq 0
.

However, neither Theorem
8
nor Theorem
9
seem strong enough to
prove Theorem
4
.

4
Lemmas

In this section and the next, the goal is to prove Theorem
4
.
We fix an infinite word
\bf x
and
omit it as a subscript on
\Pal
and
\rho
.
The idea of the proof is as follows.
The case where subword complexity is exponentially large
is easy. Otherwise, we show that for each
D\geq 1

and all sufficiently large
n
there is a way of associating,
with each length-
n
palindrome
u
appearing in
\bf x
,
a list
S_{u}
of length-
n
factors of
\bf x
of cardinality

D+1
such that the sets
S_{u}
are ‘almost’ pairwise disjoint—hence
giving ‘roughly’
(D+1)\cdot\Pal(n)
factors of length
n
.
Thus for all these
n
the ratio of ordinary
factors to palindrome factors is roughly
D+1
.

We will need two lemmas.

Lemma 10
.

Let
w
be a palindrome, and
x
a nonempty proper palindromic prefix of
w
.
Then
|w|-|x|
is a period of
w
.

Proof.

This is a standard lemma; see
[
10
, Lemma 2]
, for
example. It has a
1
-line proof: if
x
is a prefix of
w
then

x^{R}=x
is a suffix of
w
, and so
w
has period
|w|-|x|
.
∎

Now we define an “exceptional set”
{\cal E}_{D}(n)
and bound
its cardinality.

Lemma 11
.

Let
D\geq 1
be an integer and
n>2D
.
Define

{\cal E}_{D}(n)=\{v\in\Fac(n)\,:\,v\text{ has a prefix $z$ with $|z|\geq n-2D$
and period $\leq 2D$}\},

and set
E_{D}(n):=|{\cal E}_{D}(n)|
.
Then

(D+1)\Pal(n)\leq\rho(n)+DE_{D}(n)

(1)

and

E_{D}(n)\leq 2D(2D+1)\rho(2D)^{2}.

(2)

Proof.

For each
length-
n
palindrome
u
occurring in
\bf x
, let
i_{u}
be the starting
position of the first occurrence of
u
in
\bf x
,
and define

T(u,d)={\bf x}[i_{u}+d..i_{u}+d+n-1]

for
0\leq d\leq D
. So
T(u,d)
is a length-
n
factor of
\bf x
,
beginning at position
i_{u}+d
.

Claim 1
.

For each fixed
d
,
0\leq d\leq D
, the map

u\rightarrow T(u,d)
is injective.

Proof.

First fix
d
.
We have
u={\bf x}[i_{u}..i_{u}+n-1]

and
T(u,d)={\bf x}[i_{u}+d..i_{u}+d+n-1]
.
The length
n-d
prefix of
T(u,d)
is

{\bf x}[i_{u}+d..i_{u}+n-1]
, which is the
length
n-d
suffix of
u
. Assume
T(u,d)=T(v,d)
.
This implies that the length
n-d
suffix of
u
and
v
coincide.
Since
d\leq D<n/2
,
we have
n-d>n/2
. But every palindrome of length
n

is uniquely determined by a suffix of length
>n/2
. So
u=v
.
Thus the map is injective.
∎

Claim 2
.

Suppose
z=T(u,d)=T(v,e)
for length-
n
palindromic factors

u,v
of
\bf x
, and
0\leq d<e\leq D
. Then

z\in{\cal E}_{D}(n)
.

Proof.

We show that
z\in{\cal E}_{D}(n)
.
Write
u=u_{0}\cdots u_{n-1}
, so that

z=u_{d}\cdots u_{d+n-1}
.
The prefix of
z
of length
n-2d
is then

z^{\prime}=u_{d}\cdots u_{n-d-1}
.
Since
u=u_{0}\cdots u_{d-1}z^{\prime}u_{n-d}\cdots u_{n-1}
,
the word
z^{\prime}
results from removing the prefix and suffix of length

d
from the palindrome
u
. Thus
z^{\prime}
is a palindrome.
So
u_{d+r}=u_{n-d-1-r}
for
0\leq r<n-2d
.
So
z
has a palindromic prefix
w
of length
n-2d
.

By exactly the same argument,
z=T(v,e)
has a palindromic
prefix
y
of length
n-2e
.

Since
d<e
, the word
y
is a nonempty proper
palindromic prefix of
w
. By Lemma
10
, the word
w
has period

|w|-|y|=(n-2d)-(n-2e)=2(e-d)\leq 2D.

Moreover,
|w|=n-2d\geq n-2D
. Hence
z
has a prefix of length at least

n-2D
and period at most
2D
, so
z
lies in the
exceptional set
{\cal E}_{D}(n)
.
∎

Now we are ready to finish the proof of Lemma
2
. For

z\in\Fac(n)
, define

m(z)
to be the number of pairs
(u,d)
with

u
a length-
n
palindromic factor of
\bf x
,

0\leq d\leq D
, and
T(u,d)=z
.

By Claim
1
, for each
d
there is at most one
palindrome
u
with
T(u,d)=z
. So
m(z)\leq D+1

for each
z\in\Fac(n)
.

If
z\not\in{\cal E}_{D}(n)
, then
m(z)\leq 1
, because
Claim
1
rules out
T(u,d)=T(v,d)=z
for
distinct
u,v
, while
T(u,d)=T(v,e)=z
for
distinct
d,e
forces
z\in{\cal E}_{D}(n)
by Claim
2
.

Hence we have

\displaystyle(D+1)\Pal(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{z\in\Fac(n)}m(z)

\displaystyle\leq|\Fac(n)\setminus{\cal E}_{D}(n)|+(D+1)|\Fac(n)\cap{\cal E}_{D}(n)|

\displaystyle=|\Fac(n)|+D|\Fac(n)\cap{\cal E}_{D}(n)|

\displaystyle=\rho(n)+D|\Fac(n)\cap{\cal E}_{D}(n)|

\displaystyle\leq\rho(n)+DE_{D}(n).

It remains to bound
E_{D}(n)
. Suppose
v\in{\cal E}_{D}(n)
.
Then
v
has a prefix
z
of length
N\geq n-2D
and
period
p\leq 2D
. Thus
v
is completely determined by
specifying

•

the period
p
,
1\leq p\leq 2D
, giving
2D
choices;

•

the first
p
letters of
v
, which determine
z
and provide
\rho(2D)

choices;

•

the length
r=n-N
of the remaining suffix of
v
,
where
0\leq r\leq 2D
,
giving
at most
2D+1
choices;

•

the suffix of
v
of length
r
, giving at most
\rho(2D)
choices.

It follows that
E_{D}(n)\leq 2D(2D+1)\rho(2D)^{2}
.
∎

5
Proof of the main result

Proof of Theorem
4
.

Assume that
\bf x
is not ultimately periodic. The goal is
to prove that the ratio

R(n):={{\Pal(n)\log(\Pal(n)+1)}\over{\rho(n)}}

tends to
0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.
Define

h=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{{\log\rho(n)}\over n},

which exists by Proposition
6
.

The easy case is
h>0
. A length-
n
palindrome is completely
determined by its prefix of length
\lceil n/2\rceil
, and
such a prefix is also a factor of
\bf x
.
So
\Pal(n)\leq\rho(\lceil n/2\rceil)
.
Since
\log\rho(n)=hn+o(n)
, we have

\log\rho(\lceil n/2\rceil)=(h/2)n+o(n)
.
If
\Pal(n)=0
, then
R(n)=0
.
Otherwise, for those
n
where
R(n)>0
, we have

\displaystyle\log R(n)

\displaystyle\leq\log\rho(\lceil n/2\rceil)+\log\log(\rho(\lceil n/2\rceil)+1)-\log\rho(n)

\displaystyle=-{h\over 2}n+o(n),

which tends to
-\infty
. Hence
R(n)
tends to
0
.

The harder case is
h=0
.
Suppose, to get a contradiction, that
R(n)
does not tend to
0
.
Then there exist
\varepsilon>0
and an
increasing sequence
(n_{j})_{j\geq 1}
such that

R(n_{j})\geq\varepsilon
for all
j\geq 1
.
Along this particular sequence,
\Pal(n_{j})
must tend to infinity. Indeed, if some infinite subsequence
n_{j_{\ell}}
satisfied
\Pal(n_{j_{\ell}})\leq C
, then

\Pal(n_{j_{\ell}})\log(\Pal(n_{j_{\ell}})+1)\leq C\log(C+1),

while
\rho(n_{j_{\ell}})\to\infty
, since
\bf x
is not ultimately periodic. Hence

R(n_{j_{\ell}})\to 0
,
contradicting
R(n_{j})\geq\varepsilon
. Thus

\Pal(n_{j})\to\infty
.

Fix
A>0
, and set

D_{j}=\left\lfloor A\log(\Pal(n_{j})+1)\right\rfloor.

Since
\Pal(n_{j})\to\infty
, we have
D_{j}\to\infty
. Also, because each length-
n
palindrome is determined by its first
\lceil n/2\rceil
letters,
we have
\Pal(n)\leq\rho(\lceil n/2\rceil)
.
Using
\log\rho(m)=o(m)
, this gives

\log(\Pal(n_{j})+1)=o(n_{j})
.
Consequently
D_{j}=o(n_{j})
,
and in particular
D_{j}<n_{j}/2
for all sufficiently large
j
.

We now apply Lemma
2
.
Since
\log\rho(m)=o(m)
, Eq. (
2
) implies that

\log(E_{D}(n)+1)=o(D)\qquad(D\to\infty),

uniformly in
n
. Substituting
D=D_{j}
, and using

D_{j}\sim A\log(\Pal(n_{j})+1),

we get

\log E_{D_{j}}(n_{j})=o(\log(\Pal(n_{j})+1)).

Thus

E_{D_{j}}(n_{j})=\Pal(n_{j})^{o(1)}=o(\Pal(n_{j})),

because
\Pal(n_{j})\to\infty
. Applying Eq. (
1
) with

n=n_{j}
and
D=D_{j}
, we obtain

\rho(n_{j})\geq(D_{j}+1)\Pal(n_{j})-D_{j}E_{D_{j}}(n_{j})=(1-o(1))D_{j}\Pal(n_{j}).

Since
D_{j}\sim A\log(\Pal(n_{j})+1)
, this gives

\rho(n_{j})\geq(1-o(1))A\Pal(n_{j})\log(\Pal(n_{j})+1).

Equivalently,

\limsup_{j\to\infty}\frac{\Pal(n_{j})\log(\Pal(n_{j})+1)}{\rho(n_{j})}\leq\frac{1}{A}.

Because
A>0
was arbitrary, the right-hand side can be made smaller than
\varepsilon
, contradicting
R(n_{j})\geq\varepsilon
. Therefore
R(n)\to 0
, as claimed.
∎

6
Optimality

It is natural to wonder if the
\Pal(n)\log(\Pal(n))
in the numerator
of the quantity in
Theorem
4
is optimal. We show that it (essentially) is.

Theorem 12
.

Let
f:\mathbb{N}\to[0,\infty)
be a nondecreasing function. The following are
equivalent.

(i)

For every non-ultimately periodic infinite word
\bf x
over a finite
alphabet,

\frac{f(\Pal(n))}{\rho(n)}\to 0.

(ii)

f(t)=O(t\log(t+1))
.

Moreover, if
f(t)/(t\log(t+1))
is unbounded, then there exists a
non-ultimately periodic infinite word
\bf x
for which

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{f(\Pal(n))}{\rho(n)}=+\infty.

Proof.

The implication (ii)
\Rightarrow
(i) follows immediately from
Theorem
4
.
Indeed, by Theorem
4
,

\frac{\Pal_{\bf x}(n)\log(\Pal_{\bf x}(n)+1)}{\rho_{\bf x}(n)}\to 0.

Thus the assertion follows for those
n
with large
\Pal_{\bf x}(n)
; for the
remaining
n
, the numerator is bounded while
\rho_{\bf x}(n)\to\infty
.

We now prove the converse (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii) in a strong form, by
constructing a word
\bf x
with many palindromes.
Assume that

\frac{f(t)}{t\log(t+1)}

is unbounded. We first record a simple consequence. Since
f
is
nondecreasing, the sequence

\frac{f(2^{r})}{2^{r}r}

is also unbounded. To see this, choose an increasing sequence
integers
t_{j}
for which

f(t_{j})/(t_{j}\log(t_{j}+1))\to\infty
, and put

r_{j}=\lceil\log_{2}t_{j}\rceil
. Then
t_{j}\leq 2^{r_{j}}\leq 2t_{j}
, and

r_{j}=O(\log(t_{j}+1)).

Therefore

\frac{f(2^{r_{j}})}{2^{r_{j}}r_{j}}\geq c\,\frac{f(t_{j})}{t_{j}\log(t_{j}+1)}

for sufficiently large
j
and some absolute constant
c>0
,
and hence the right-hand side is unbounded.

We now construct a word
\bf x
over the alphabet

\Sigma=\{0,1,2,3\}

for which

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{f(\Pal_{\bf x}(n))}{\rho_{\bf x}(n)}=+\infty.

For integers
s,r\geq 1
and a word

\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{0}\cdots\varepsilon_{r-1}\in\{1,2\}^{r}
, define

\displaystyle p(s,r,\varepsilon)={}

\displaystyle 0^{s}\varepsilon_{0}0^{s}\varepsilon_{1}\cdots 0^{s}\varepsilon_{r-1}0^{s}\varepsilon_{r-1}0^{s}\varepsilon_{r-2}\cdots 0^{s}\varepsilon_{0}0^{s}.

This word is a palindrome. It has
2r
nonzero letters and
2r+1
blocks
of the form
0^{s}
, so its total length is

n(s,r)=(2r+1)s+2r
.
For fixed
s
and
r
, the
2^{r}
words
p(s,r,\varepsilon)
are
clearly all distinct as

\varepsilon
ranges over
\{1,2\}^{r}
.

We choose parameters recursively. Let
s_{1}=1
and
S_{0}=0
. Suppose that

s_{k}
and
S_{k-1}
have already been chosen. Since

f(2^{r})/(2^{r}r)
is unbounded, we may choose
r_{k}
so large that, with

M_{k}=2^{r_{k}}
, both inequalities

M_{k}\geq S_{k-1}+1

(3)

and

\frac{f(M_{k})}{M_{k}r_{k}}\geq 21k(s_{k}+1)

(4)

hold. Set

n_{k}=n(s_{k},r_{k})=(2r_{k}+1)s_{k}+2r_{k}.

Let
B_{k}
be the concatenation, in any order, of all the
M_{k}
palindromes

p(s_{k},r_{k},\varepsilon),\qquad\varepsilon\in\{1,2\}^{r_{k}},

each followed by the delimiter
3
. Thus

|B_{k}|=M_{k}(n_{k}+1)
.
Define

S_{k}=S_{k-1}+|B_{k}|,\qquad s_{k+1}=n_{k}+1,

and set

{\bf x}=B_{1}B_{2}B_{3}\cdots.

Note that
|B_{1}B_{2}\cdots B_{k}|=S_{k}
.

The word
\bf x
is not ultimately periodic. Indeed,
s_{k}\to\infty
, so

\bf x
contains arbitrarily long blocks of
0
’s. If
\bf x
were ultimately
periodic with period
T
from some point onward, then a sufficiently late
block
0^{T}
would force the periodic tail from that block onward to be
identically
0
. But after every such block there are still later
occurrences of the symbols
1
,
2
, and
3
, a contradiction.

Now fix
k
and examine factors of length
n_{k}
. The block
B_{k}
contains
all
M_{k}
palindromes
p(s_{k},r_{k},\varepsilon)
of length
n_{k}
. Hence

\Pal_{\bf x}(n_{k})\geq M_{k}.

We next bound
\rho_{\bf x}(n_{k})
. A length-
n_{k}
factor starts either
before
B_{k}
, inside
B_{k}
, or after
B_{k}
.
There are exactly
S_{k-1}
starting positions before
B_{k}
, so these
contribute at most
S_{k-1}
distinct factors. There are
|B_{k}|
starting
positions inside
B_{k}
, so these contribute at most

|B_{k}|=M_{k}(n_{k}+1)
distinct factors.
It remains to count factors starting after
B_{k}
. For every
m>k
we have

s_{m}\geq s_{k+1}=n_{k}+1.

Thus, in the tail
B_{k+1}B_{k+2}\cdots
, any two nonzero symbols are
separated by more than
n_{k}
positions: between consecutive nonzero
symbols there is a block of at least
n_{k}+1
zeros. Therefore a factor of
length
n_{k}
starting after
B_{k}
contains at most one nonzero symbol.
Such a factor is either

0^{n_{k}}
or has the form

0^{j}c\,0^{n_{k}-j-1},\qquad 0\leq j<n_{k},\qquad c\in\{1,2,3\}.

There are at most
3n_{k}+1
such words. Consequently,

\rho_{\bf x}(n_{k})\leq S_{k-1}+M_{k}(n_{k}+1)+3n_{k}+1.

By (
3
) we have
S_{k-1}\leq M_{k}
. Since
M_{k},n_{k}\geq 1
, we get

S_{k-1}+M_{k}(n_{k}+1)+3n_{k}+1\leq 7M_{k}n_{k}.

Also we have

n_{k}=(2r_{k}+1)s_{k}+2r_{k}\leq 3r_{k}(s_{k}+1),

and thus

\rho_{\bf x}(n_{k})\leq 21M_{k}r_{k}(s_{k}+1).

Using the fact that
f
is nondecreasing, (
4
),
and the lower bound

\Pal_{\bf x}(n_{k})\geq M_{k}
, we obtain

\frac{f(\Pal_{\bf x}(n_{k}))}{\rho_{\bf x}(n_{k})}\geq\frac{f(M_{k})}{21M_{k}r_{k}(s_{k}+1)}\geq k.

Therefore

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{f(\Pal_{\bf x}(n))}{\rho_{\bf x}(n)}=+\infty.

This completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 13
.

For every unbounded nondecreasing function
g
, there is a
non-ultimately periodic infinite word
\bf x
such that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\Pal_{\bf x}(n)\log(\Pal_{\bf x}(n)+1)g(\Pal_{\bf x}(n))}{\rho_{\bf x}(n)}=+\infty.

Proof.

Apply Theorem
12
to

f(t)=t\log(t+1)g(t).

∎
