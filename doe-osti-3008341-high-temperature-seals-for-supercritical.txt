ABSTRACT

This is the final report for project DE-FE0031924 titled “High-temperature seals for supercritical
carbon-dioxide (sCO2) turbines.” The report provides a summary of the entire project efforts from
October 2020 through December 2024 including the high-temperature commercial dry gas seal
(DGS) tests and thermal modeling of Task 2, as well as the high-temperature, large-diameter seal
design and high-temperature tests of large-diameter seals in Task 3. A key outcome of Task 2 was
the testing completion of specially instrumented commercial DGS in the GE-SwRI Apollo sCO2
compressor (27,000 rpm). Test data from the DGS showed elevated temperatures upwards of 350
o
  F, which are close to the higher operating temperature limit of the DGS. The temperature
measurements provide insight into the expected thermal loads on DGS operating in high-speed
sCO2 compressor and provided test data for validation of an in-house thermal model of the
compressor/seal. Under Task 2.0, this report also presents the development of a steady-state
conjugate heat-transfer model of the DGS operating in the sCO2 compressor – a first of its kind
model for modeling heat transfer of sCO2 in an actual operating compressor. The findings of the
thermal model show a reasonable match between temperature predictions of the model and the
measured temperature data, also pointing out the validity of the approach and assumptions made
in modeling the flows, heat transfer coefficients and windage modeling in the rig. Under Task 3.0,
this report presents the preliminary design of a large-diameter hybrid face seal (14 inch and 26-
inch diameter) for field testing in a land-based GE turbine. The preliminary seal design effort
presented in this report under project DE-FE0031924 builds on the development and successful
laboratory testing for such large diameter hybrid face seal under the prior DE-FE0024007 project.
Key aspects of seal fluid analyses with CFD, mechanical design considerations and assembly
considerations in a land-based turbine are presented. Finally, under Task 3.0, this report also
presents the continued high-temperature testing of the 14-inch diameter hybrid face seal developed
previously under the DE-FE0024007 program. Specifically, test data demonstrating successful
non-contact seal operation and seal effective leakage of 0.001-inch with seal inlet temperatures
above 700 oF are presented in this report. Successful hybrid seal operation in a laboratory
environment for a large diameter (14-inch) seal at temperatures above 700 oF is a major
technological milestone for this technology.

1.0: Accomplishments Summary
 The overall objective of this project was to develop a high-temperature 650oC-capable Dry Gas
Seals (DGS) component technology to TRL5 for application in a 100 MWe supercritical carbon-dioxide
(sCO2) turbine. The proposed component-technology-development effort intended to extend DGS
temperature capability from the present-day 200oC to high-temperature 650oC. The system-level objective
is to enable 0.85% points cycle efficiency and high operational flexibility for a 100 MWe sCO2 power cycle.
Additionally, design insights and high-temperature TRL5 component test data from this effort are expected
to improve DGS reliability for state-of-the-art 10 MWe-scale sCO2 turbine development programs that drive
the turbine technology needed for indirect-fired sCO2-based plants of the future.
 Task 1.0 is Project Management and Planning. The component-technology effort of this project
addressed high-temperature DGS development for two representative sCO2 turbine sizes; namely, the 10
MWe scale representing the state-of-the-art sCO2 axial turbines and the 100 MWe scale representing a
modular plant of the future. At the 10 MWe-scale (i.e., under Task 2.0), the project objective was to perform
a thermal analysis and seal-turbomachinery integration of commercial DGS in sCO2 turbomachinery.
Following this, the team performed TRL5 commercial DGS tests in 10 MWe-scale sCO2 turbomachinery

thermal design tool, and (b) improved reliability of commercial DGS in 10 MWe-scale sCO2 turbine
development programs (e.g., STEP sCO2 Pilot Plant [1, 2]). The project accomplishments under Task 2.0
are described in Section 2.0. At the 100 MWe-scale (i.e., under Task 3.0), the project objective was to design

tests in a high-temperature, rotating Advanced Seals Test Rig [3] were performed to retire the risk of seal
operation at high-temperature. The project accomplishments under Task 3.0 are described in Section 3.0.

Similarly, Figs. 1(e) and 1(f ) show the Apollo sCO2 compressor [6,13–16], where two DGSs were used to
isolate the BRGs from the sCO2 flow. Figure 1(d) shows a picture of a failed DGS with a disintegrated

DGS failure in the SunShot turbine [4, 5], this failure in the Apollo sCO2 compressor led to testing
downtime before being addressed in successful compressor tests [6,13–16] with a new DGS. These DGS
are commercially available components [18,19] and a widely used sealing technology in the oil and gas
compressor industry. However, the reliability of such DGS remains a concern.
 Several root causes can trigger failures such as the ones shown in Figs. 1(c) and 1(d). One such
possible root cause is the thermal runaway of the DGS, where excessive thermal loads during DGS
operation can lead to thermal deformations of the sealing surfaces and failure of the seal. In other words,
improved reliability of the DGS can be achieved if the thermal loads on the DGS are fully known during
the design phase of the turbomachinery. This report presents test data along with thermal model predictions
aimed at predicting and understanding the thermal loads on a DGS in a typical sCO2 turbomachinery.
 Typically, a DGS (Fig. 2) comprises an axial-spring-loaded stationary ring, a rotating ring, and a
secondary seal. During operation, the spring and the differential pressure push the stationary ring toward
 GE Aerospace NOT SENSITIVE – suitable for external release
 6

the spinning rotor. A very thin liquid/gas/supercritical fluid film (typically 2–7 µm thin) between the
stationary ring and the rotating ring generates film pressures due to the presence of aerodynamic spiral
grooves. The “separating force” created by the thin film balances a combination of the closing-pressure
force, the inertial forces, and the frictional loads from the secondary seal. A critical aspect of the DGS
reliability is how well this ultra-thin liquid/gas/supercritical fluid film (and thereby non-contact DGS
operation) is sustained under varying pressure, thermal, and speed conditions. Specifically, excessive
windage heating in the thin film separating the rotating and stationary rings and less than optimal cooling
to remove this generated heat can lead to incorrect thermal system integration of the DGS in the overall
turbomachinery. In other words, the thermal analysis of the seal component cannot be performed in an
isolated setting, but the entire thermal heat balance of the associated turbomachinery needs to be evaluated
to fully understand the thermal environment around the seal, and thereby the seal’s ability to withstand the
thermal loads expected during the turbomachinery transient mission. This article presents a thermal model
for the entire sCO2 compressor and studies the thermal interactions between the DGS and the sCO2
compressor.

 Figure 2 Schematic of a typical DGS

Recently, Rafanelli et al. [20] presented a conjugate heat transfer model of a DGS operating in a dedicated
sCO2 bench test rig at Flowserve. Their work used an ALTAIR-based flow simulator coupled with ANSYS
to analyze and predict thermal loads on a DGS. Their model was validated with the test data from a
dedicated seals test rig. The work of Rafanelli et al. [20] has some similarities but also has important
differences when compared to the work presented in this report. From the standpoint of similarities, the
present work as well as the work of Rafanelli et al. [20] presents conjugate heat transfer analysis of the
DGS. From a differences standpoint, there are two differences. First, the test data presented in this work
are for a DGS operating in the Apollo sCO2 compressor [6,13-16], which allows us to study the thermal
loads expected on a DGS during a transient mission of a compressor compared to a dedicated seals rig in
the work of Rafanelli et al. [20]. Second, the thermal model presented in this article uses an in-house thermal
solver where the fluid advection network as well as the structural heat transfer calculations is based on
ANSYS with an APDL script coupling the fluid and structural conduction/convection solvers. The in-house
thermal model presented in this work is similar to the thermal model presented in the work of Bidkar et al.
[21] for a gas-bearing test rig thermal analysis. The in-house solver uses measured flows and computational
fluid dynamics (CFD) for calculating the local flows in the sCO2 compressor.
 Since a DGS had previously failed once (see Fig. 1(d)) in the Apollo sCO2 compressor [6,13–16],
it was deemed useful to repeat typical sCO2 compressor operation missions with the existing Apollo sCO2

 2.1 Dry Gas Seal Tests on the Supercritical Carbon Dioxide Compressor
 This section is subdivided into three subsections. Section 2.1.1 provides an overview of the sCO2
 compressor, followed by a summary of the compressor modifications (Sec. 2.1.2) and test data for

 2.1.1 Apollo Supercritical Carbon Dioxide Compressor. The Apollo sCO2 compressor
 [6,13–16] cross section is shown in Fig. 1(e). This 4 MWe sCO2 compressor operating nominally
 at 27,000 rpm (suction pressure ∼80 bar, suction temperature 35–50 °C, and outlet pressure ∼250
 bar) was developed jointly by GE and SwRI for testing and demonstrating the aerodynamic and
 rotordynamic performances of a sCO2 compressor. It consists of a single impeller main compressor
 in an opposing configuration with a two-stage simulated recompressor separated by a balance
 piston seal. The recompression closed Brayton cycle for sCO2 [4-6] needs two compressors in the
 compression cycle—a main compressor and a recompressor with a flow split between the two to
 optimize the compression power and effectiveness of the recuperation. For the Apollo sCO2
 compressor [6,13–16], only the main compressor is active (i.e., compressing the CO2) while the
 two stages of the recompressor are simulated disks that are not actively compressing any flow. The
 simulated disks (mimicking rotordynamic inertia of the recompressor impellers) and the axial span
 of the recompressor ensure that the overall machine has rotordynamic characteristics (i.e., critical
 speeds, bearing span, impeller inertias, shaft stiffness) of an opposed main compressor–
 recompressor machine, thereby allowing testing of these mechanical aspects of the sCO2
 compressor [6,13–16] while limiting the aerodynamic compression operation to only the main
 compressor portion of the machine. An electric motor connected via a gearbox on the right side of
 Fig. 1(e) (i.e., the drive-end side) drives the machine. Inlet guide vanes (IGVs) are present upstream
 of the main compressor to provide adjustability of the flow so that the compressor can deliver
 optimal performance for off-design points including hot and cold days where the compressor inlet
 operation near the liquid–vapor dome can cause large swings in the density of the incoming flow.
 Two dry gas seals (DGS and drive-end (DE) DGS) are used to isolate the thrust BRG and two
 journal BRGs from the flow paths. The Apollo sCO2 compressor is connected to a closed sCO2
 loop where the high-pressure output of the main compressor is throttled to a lower pressure and
 passed through a chiller/cooler system to be looped back to the main compressor inlet. Flow that

the recompressor cavity through the recompressor output port. This recompressor output was
bypassed back to the main compressor suction side for this particular machine. The loop and
compressor flow measurement locations for the compressor are discussed by Mortzheim et al. [14].
For the work presented in this report, the drive-end DGS and the housing (see Fig. 1(e)) were

2.1.3 Test Procedure and Dry Gas Seal Test Data
The sCO2 compressor tests were performed over two different days with over 17 steady-state test
points (TP) to generate compressor speed curves. The 17 test points spanned a range of settings for
the IGV position, speed, suction, and discharge operating conditions for the compressor as well as
operating conditions and flows for the DGS. The IGVs shown in Fig. 1(e) (also see Fig. 4 in
Mortzheim et al. [14] for a picture of the IGV hardware) were adjusted when the compressor was
de-pressurized by torquing and locking an externally accessible threaded rod. A mechanism inside
the compressor is used to convert the linear position of the threaded rod (manual linear actuator)
into the angular motion of the IGVs. A correlation curve between the threaded rod axial position
and the IGV angle (established prior to the compressor assembly) was used for setting the various
IGV set points.

2.2 Thermal Model for the Supercritical Carbon Dioxide Compressor
The compressor heat transfer model was set up using an in-house iterative solver similar to the one
described by Bidkar et al. [21] for analyzing a gas-bearing test rig. As shown in Fig. 6, separated
by the vertical dashed line, the in-house solver has two modules—(a) a structural thermal solution
module implemented using ANSYS WORKBENCH, and (b) an advection network solution
module implemented using an ANSYS APDL script.

The first step in setting up the advection network is the fluid flow solution (see the bottom right of
Fig. 6). In this first step, the flow between each connected fluid node on the network is calculated
to assign a mass flow mdot to each arrow. The measured flow passing through the main compressor
was assigned to nodes 1–3. There are several methods available to estimate the leakage flow past
the impeller eye seal (nodes 10–14). These include the bulk flow method [23], a CFD-based method
[24], Martin’s formula [25], or the formulation from Zabriskie and Sternlicht [26], which for
example predicts that about 3% of the flow going past the main compressor (nodes 4–6) recirculates
past the eye impeller seal. Experimentally validated supercritical CO2 flow data past labyrinth seals
are currently not widely available in the literature. In this work, the leakage flow past the impeller
eye seal (nodes 10–14) was assumed to be about 2% of the flow going past the main impeller (nodes
 GE Aerospace NOT SENSITIVE – suitable for external release
 13

4–6). Since the assumed flow is physically away from the region of interest (i.e., the drive-end
DGS), it is expected that the small error introduced from this assumption will not significantly
affect the overall temperature predictions for the drive-end DGS. The measured flow supplied to
the non-drive-end DGS was assigned to nodes 19–21 and the measured flow supplied to the drive-
end DGS was assigned to nodes 64–66. Subtracting the DGS film flows (nodes 31–33 for the non-
drive-end DGS and nodes 78–80 for the drive-end DGS) and the secondary seal flows from the
measured seal supply flows yields the process labyrinth flows (nodes 23–30 for the drive-end DGS
and nodes 69–75 for the non-drive-end DGS). ANSYS CFX was used to calculate the flow past the
fluid nodes tied to the DGS films (e.g., fluid nodes 31, 32, 33, 78, 79, 80). Since the recompressor
discharge flow is measured on node 63, subtracting the drive-end process labyrinth seal flow (nodes
69–75) from this measured recompressor discharge flow yields the balance piston seal leakage,
which was assigned to nodes 16–18. The measured buffer air flow supplied to each of the DGS was
assigned to nodes 87–89, and nodes 41– 43. The measured oil flow was assigned to the respective
oil flow lines.
 For each fluid node tied to a rotating cavity, an associated swirl (fluid tangential motion)
is computed using stator area drag, rotor area windage, and conservation of angular momentum of
the fluid received from its upstream fluid node. Accurate swirl values are needed for estimating
aerodynamic windage heat produced in that cavity. For all rotating cavities, the solver uses GE
proprietary turbulent windage correlations for computing windage, except the DGS film nodes,
where the windage predicted by the GE proprietary turbulent windage correlation was doubled.
Prior to using the GE proprietary turbulent windage correlation with a factor of 2, a different
Couette-flow-based windage model (that assumes a laminar linear velocity profile through the film
separating the stationary and rotating rings) was also attempted. The Couette-flow based windage
model was found to be inadequate in predicting the windage heat in the DGS films. For every fluid
node (rotating cavity or not), the local fluid velocity and fluid properties (density, dynamic
viscosity, conductivity, heat capacity) are used to compute the local Reynolds number and the local
Prandtl number—which are then combined with the GE proprietary correlations to obtain the
Nusselt number and heat transfer coefficients (HTCs).
 The next step is to create the structural thermal model using ANSYS WORKBENCH. An
axisymmetric model of the compressor was used to reduce the computational effort. Temperature-
dependent material properties were assigned to the geometry. The contacts between each of the
components were set up in ANSYS WORKBENCH, and the geometry was meshed with tetrahedral
elements. The surfaces associated with each of the cavity nodes were selected and named. These
surfaces are pointers to apply convection boundary conditions such as HTCs and gas temperatures
(Tgas). For example, fluid node 3 will exchange heat with the dashed (green) and dotted (magenta)
areas shown in Fig. 7(a).
 The iterative scheme is outlined in Fig. 6. The iteration commences (on the left side of Fig.
6) with initial guesses for HTCs and Tgas at each fluid node. Using these, the convection boundary
conditions are applied to the structural solver, which is solved in steady-state to obtain structural
temperature Tmetal and the “heat-exchange” convection reactions for each fluid node. At this point,
the solver hands-off to the advection network module. The fluid node temperatures Tgas and
pressure Pgas are used to update the fluid properties, which are then used to compute HTCs using
GE proprietary correlations. The swirl and windage for each node are also updated using the latest
Tgas and Pgas. For each fluid node (say node X), a conservation of energy constraint is imposed for
the control volume around fluid node X (see the equation on the right side of Fig. 6). ∑minhin
represents the summation of incoming fluid enthalpies from all nodes that are upstream of fluid
node X, windagenode X represents the calculated windage (during the latest iteration) for the cavity
around node X, heat-exchangenode X represents the convection reaction computed by ANSYS in the
previous iteration for the areas tied to fluid node X, and mouthout represents the outgoing enthalpy
for the cavity node X. In this equation, all items are known except hout. After calculating hout, a

 14

new Tgas for the fluid leaving node X is calculated, before proceeding to solve the energy balance
for the next fluid node. Once the Tgas for all fluid nodes is updated, the iterative solver hands the
solution back to the structural side (see Fig. 6) where the next iteration of the structural thermal
solution commences with the recently solved Tgas and HTCs. This process continues until Tmetal and
Tgas converge to within 0.3 °C. Note that natural convection was imposed on the external walls of
the compressor casing.
 The conservation of energy equation described above is applicable to all nodes. However,
for the main compressor nodes 5 and 6, in addition to the windage and heat exchanged with the
surrounding metal, fluid temperature rise also accounts for the inefficiencies of the compression
process (assumed isentropic efficiency as 87%).
 The outcome of this iterative thermal solution is the predicted structural temperatures at
the structural mesh nodes as well as the gas temperatures at all the fluid nodes. Additionally,
aerodynamic windage results, HTCs, and swirl at the fluid nodes are also obtained. As mentioned
earlier, the in-house heat transfer model for the compressor described above uses the various m dot
entering the compressor (CO2 flow, air flow, oil flow) along with their incoming temperatures as
boundary conditions or inputs to the solver. Similarly, this model predicts the temperature of the
fluid leaving the compressor along with structural temperatures for the internal parts of the
compressor.

 Note that the temperature loads such as the ones shown in Fig. 9(a) will lead to thermal
deformations of the seal rotating ring and stationary rings. As an example, the thermal loads shown
in Fig. 9(a), when applied to the rotating ring and the stationary ring result in thermal coning
deformations (i.e., out-of-plane conical deformation of the rings) of the order of 10 µm. For the
five cases TP#1–TP#5 presented here, these net coning (combined rotor and stator coning)
deformations were such that the predicted fluid film (i.e., the axial gap separating the rotating ring
and the stationary ring) had a larger gap at the outer radius (at the high pressure end) and a smaller
gap at the inner radius (on the low pressure end). Note that these predicted deformations are based
on a one-step thermal–mechanical analysis. Specifically, the one-step thermal analysis presented
here assumes the rotating ring and the stationary ring faces to be parallel when calculating the
windage heat, and using these thermal calculations predicts a non parallel film shape due to the
imposed thermal load. A two-way coupled thermal–mechanical solver with multiple iterations that
uses this predicted deformed film shape to recalculate an updated seal force balance, an updated
seal-rotor axial gap, and an updated windage heat is expected to settle on a film shape with net
coning deformations smaller than those predicted with a one-step solution. Nonetheless, the
predicted one-step thermal– mechanical deformations point to a stable converging film shape (i.e.,
converging from a large gap at the higher-pressure outer radius to a smaller axial gap at the low-
pressure inner radius) as against a diverging film shape (smaller axial gap at the high pressure outer
radius), which would have indicated a seal failure due to thermal coning. This is also validated
through the tests of Fig. 5 where the DGS performed successfully (albeit with high operating
temperatures close to its failure limit) without failing during the entire test mission.

temperatures for housing temperatures (Ha, Hb, and Hc), seal temperatures (Ta, Tb, Tc, and Td), and
fluid temperatures (G1, G2, G3, and G4). The model performs relatively well for certain locations
while the prediction accuracy is low for some other locations. Specifically, as shown in Fig. 9(c),

and Td) or the gas temperatures (G1, G2, and G3). Specifically, the model consistently predicts
higher temperatures Hb and Hc than their measurements during the tests. One possible reason for
this discrepancy could be the inadequate conduction of heat from the housing to the neighboring
lower-temperature drive-end bearing. More oil cooling flow on the drive-end bearing or lesser
resistance to the conduction of heat across the various contact surfaces of the assembly might lead
to a better match between the model and the test data. The 1D advection network for the drive-end
DGS (Fig. 7(c)) uses measured seal inlet mass flow (and thereby zero uncertainty in flow estimate)

locations Ta, Tb, Tc, and Td, indicating that the CFD-based film flows and turbulent film windage
using proprietary modeling are performing well in conjunction with the contact interface modeling
of the DGS with its surrounding metal housing. These seal locations are important indicators for
predicting seal failure under thermal loads. Specifically, excess temperatures and thermal gradients
at location Ta measured behind the stationary ring of the seal can drive seal excess deformations
comparable to the film thickness of the DGS, thereby causing seal failure.
 One very important contribution of this work is the development of the predictive in-house
thermal model (steady-state) with sCO2 as the working fluid. It is interesting to note that the
proprietary HTC correlations, windage models typically employed for air as the working fluid did
not need any special tweaking for usage with sCO2 as the working fluid—as far as typical operating
conditions and the corresponding fluid non-dimensional numbers for a typical sCO2 compressor
[6,13–16] are concerned. The 1D thermal axisymmetric model developed here shows a reasonable
match with measured data at discrete structural and fluid locations, thereby pointing to the utility
of this thermal model for performing preliminary and detailed design studies. Of course, the model
developed here can be extended to predict transient thermal behavior and 3D spatially varying (i.e.,
non-axisymmetric) thermal loads if needed. Such extensions remain as future work. The thermal
model developed here is a quick tool for understanding the system interaction of the DGS with the
surrounding compressor hardware. Specifically, this work shows that the lower flow on the drive-
end side led to higher temperatures for the drive-end DGS housing—a result not obvious when
designing this compressor/simulated recompressor without this thermal modeling approach.

2.4 Summary and Conclusions for Task 2.0
This work advances the understanding of thermal loads expected on the DGS when operating in a
4 MWe-scale sCO2 compressor undergoing typical operating missions. Temperature measurements
were presented for a specially instrumented DGS operating on the drive-end of the Apollo sCO2
compressor across multiple test points. This work does not present compressor data analysis (speed
curves, flow comparison with compressor CFD). This work describes the development of an in-

 house thermal modeling tool that uses a 1D thermal advection network (ANSYS APDL) coupled
 with ANSYS thermal to predict fluid and structural temperatures. The in-house tool uses
 proprietary correlations for HTCs, and windage to predict the fluid/structural temperatures with

 predictions of the thermal loads across the entire compressor and allows for studying the system–

 3.1 Design of a high-temperature, large-diamter seal for a representative 100 MWe sCO2 turbine
 100 MWe-scale indirect-fired sCO2 power plant systems offer characteristics of modular, compact,
 highly efficient power cycles [27]. A critical aspect of any such sCO2-based power plant is a 100
 MWe sCO2 axial turbine operating with about 730 oC inlet, 550 oC exit temperatures and 260 bar
 inlet, 70bar exit pressures. In the past decade, GE in partnerships with SwRI, Gas Technology
 Institute (GTI), Baker Hughes (BKR), has been involved in development of sCO2 axial
 turbomachinery in the form of design/testing of the SunShot 10 MWe sCO2 turbine [4,5],
 design/testing of the STEP 10 MWe sCO2 turbine [1,2], design/testing of a 10 MWe sCO2
 compressor [6, 13-16] and conceptual layouts for 50 MWe and 450 MWe sCO2 turbines and
 compressors [10,11] with applications geared towards sCO2-based fossil-based power generation,
 Waste-Heat Recovery (WHR), Concentrated Solar Power (CSP) and Natural-Gas Pipeline
 Compression (NGPC) plants. Echogen EPS100 [28, 29] uses a nominally 7 to 8 MWe radial sCO2
 turbine for waste-heat recovery applications, while sCO2 turbine conceptual designs for 90 MWe
 scale (Siemens) and 550 MWe scale (Doosan Heavy Industries) are reported in previous studies
 [30, 31].
 The sCO2 turbines (and compressors) listed above need low-leakage film-riding DGS for
 shaft-end sealing locations. Since sCO2 turbines operate with closed loop cycles and large exhaust
 pressures (>70bar), any leakage from the turbine/compressor shaft ends to atmosphere needs to be
 recompressed for inserting back in the CO2 loop. Compared to the higher-leakage alternative of
 traditional labyrinth seals, low-leakage film-riding DGS enable lowering of this recompression load
 and avoid significant efficiency losses. As an example, such large diameter DGS can provide a
 0.52% points cycle efficiency benefit over traditional labyrinth seals for a 450 MWe 50% efficient
 sCO2 power cycle as described in Bidkar et al. [32].
 Since large-diameter DGS needed for such sCO2 turbines (and compressors) are not readily
 available from commercial DGS vendors [18,19], the work of Bidkar et al. [32, 33] developed
 prototype DGS (specifically hybrid face seals) at three length scales – 5-inch diameter, 14-inch
 diameter and 24-inch diameter length scales for testing in various laboratory-based seals rigs [3,
 33, 34] at GE and SwRI. However, these seal development efforts of Bidkar et al. [32, 33] were
 confined to prototype articles for laboratory testing, which is less complicated and risky, and
 therefore lower TRL1 compared to designing such hybrid face seals for actual field-operating
 turbines. Developing and testing hybrid face seals in actual fielded large diameter sCO2 turbines is
 the next technology maturation step following the lab-scale seal development in Bidkar et al. [3,
 33, 34].
 However, large-diameter sCO2 turbines operating in field presently do not exist for
 demonstrating the field operation and testing of large-diameter hybrid face seals in shaft-end
 locations. Consequently, a similar-sized (approx. 24-inch diameter) already-fielded GE Steam

settings to reduce the closing force and increase the film thickness followed by a similar (but
smaller) valve adjustment at P#3. After the P#3, the seal film thickness and leakage both settled on
a slight steady increasing trend. After the valve adjustment at P#3, the measured seal effective
clearance was about 0.001 in (0.0254 mm), which is a successful high-temperature laboratory-scale
demonstration during the early stages of technology development for a split-segment, hybrid face
seal. Note that prior to the test shown in Figure 12 (c), several similar nonrotating and rotating tests
were performed on theASTR(with the hardware of Figure 12 (b)) with lower seal upstream
temperatures of 22 oC and 176 oC. These lower temperature data are not shown in this report for
the sake of brevity.
 For a room-temperature analog of the test shown in Figure 12 (c), the measured film
thickness remained unchanged during the entire test as long as the pressure ratio Psupply/Phigh was
maintained constant. This is expected from the film thickness versus Psupply/Phigh ratio relations
discussed in Figure 3 of Bidkar et al. [33]. However, the measured film thickness in Figure 12 (c)
is continuously evolving with inlet temperature even though the pressure ratio Psupply/Phigh is
approximately constant. The main reason for this behavior is that the seal cross section and the
rotor are undergoing slow-timescale thermal deformations that change the shape of the fluid film
(and thereby the seal opening force as well as the operating seal clearance). Beyond the 270-min
mark, the seal measured film thickness shows a slower change because the seal and rotor are
thermally soaked. In other words, the first 270 min represent large thermal deformations and large
seal film clearance changes followed by a more gradual settling of the seal thermal behavior. The
measured leakage (effective clearance) is larger than the small-size seal because of the metallic
piston ring of the intermediate-size seal that is expected to be leakier compared to a polymeric
piston-ring seal used in the small-size seal. The maximum average temperature rise (defined as the
average metal temperature at the seal bearing face minus the seal upstream air temperature) was
about 20 oC. This maximum temperature rise occurs around the 270-min mark when the measured
film thickness is the smallest. Analyzing the transient seal thermal loads and the transient behavior
of the seal film thickness with computational models and validating the thermal model with the
data from Figure 12 (c) remain as future work.

Specifically, the team exercised a thermal-structural model of the 14-inch seal operating in the
ASTR [3] and presented the results to the program manager in Oct 2024. This thermal-structural
model was previously developed under the program DE-FE24007. This thermal model was
tweaked to accept the rig operating mission for the May 31st, 2024 test. The speed, pressurization
and inlet temperature to the seal were input as a function of time to the rig model according to the
May 31st, 2024 test data. The resulting temperatures predicted by the thermal model were applied
on a structural FE model of the seal and the rotor to predict deformations of the seal bearing face.
The predicted thermal deformations were only a fraction of the operating physical film thickness,
leading to the conclusion that the thermal ramp rate seen in the May 31st, 2024 test did not lead to
excessive seal distortion and E-stop seen during the test. The predictions of this model were later
corroborated with the Dec 2024 test described below.
