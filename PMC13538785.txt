A New Highly Concentrated Insulin Aspart AT278 (500 U/mL) Demonstrates Ultra‐Rapid Pharmacokinetic and Pharmacodynamic Properties in Type 2 Diabetes Regardless of BMI
Authors: Eva Svehlikova, Christina Gatschelhofer, Bettina Lackner, Maria Ratzer, Gabriele Fluhr, Anja Stoisser, Gregor Kolaric, David Gerring, Sarah Howell, Jan Jezek, Fiona Lawrence, Thomas R. Pieber
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsTo evaluate the pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, and safety of a novel U500 insulin aspart formulation (AT278 [500 U/mL]; AT278‐U500) compared with standard concentration insulin aspart (InsAsp [100 U/mL]; InsAsp‐U100) and U500 human regular insulin (HumIns [500 IU/mL]; HumIns‐U500).Materials and MethodsThis single‐centre, randomised, double‐blind crossover 12‐h euglycaemic clamp study was conducted in 41 overweight and obese people with type 2 diabetes (BMI 25.0–38.7 kg/m2) receiving a single subcutaneous dose (0.5 U/kg) of AT278‐U500 and InsAsp‐U100. HumIns‐U500 was consecutively studied open label in a 24‐h clamp.ResultsAT278‐U500 exhibited a significantly faster insulin absorption than InsAsp‐U100 and HumIns‐U500 (t Early50%Cmax: 9 min vs. 35 min vs. 55 min), leading to a significantly higher glucose‐lowering effect within the first hour (AUCGIR,0‐60min) compared with both InsAsp‐U100 (treatment ratio 2.02 [95% CI 1.64; 2.50]) and HumIns‐U500 (3.91 [2.89; 5.27]). When divided by median BMI (29.7 kg/m2), AUCGIR,0‐60min was significantly higher with AT278‐U500 in both the low‐BMI and high‐BMI subgroup compared to InsAsp‐U100. Linear regression showed a significant inverse relationship between BMI and AUCGIR,0‐60min for InsAsp‐U100 (slope −0.142, p < 0.0001), whereas AT278‐U500 showed no such relationship. Overall insulin exposure was similar for AT278‐U500 and InsAsp‐U100, while overall glucose‐lowering effect was comparable across all three treatments.ConclusionsAT278‐U500 maintains its ultra‐rapid onset characteristics independent of BMI, representing the first ultra‐rapid U500 option for prandial dosing in insulin‐resistant people with type 2 diabetes requiring high‐dose therapy. Trial Registration: ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05754424

Introduction

The obesity epidemic has grown to affect over 1 billion people worldwide [1], with obesity as a major contributor to the development and progression of type 2 diabetes (T2D). In many obese people with T2D, severe insulin resistance drives total daily insulin requirements beyond 200 units. For this population, concentrated insulins have become an essential therapeutic option [2]. Available at 2‐fold to 5‐fold higher concentrations than standard U100 insulin (100 U/mL), concentrated insulins offer significant clinical advantages by reducing injection volume, number of injections, and injection‐site discomfort, ultimately supporting treatment adherence [3, 4, 5].

Yet, while prandial insulins are available at U200 strength (insulin lispro and URLi) [6, 7], the only approved highly concentrated U500 option is a human regular insulin formulation (HumIns [500 IU/mL]; HumIns‐U500) [8]. However, the 5‐fold concentration substantially prolongs and alters the pharmacokinetic (PK) profile of regular insulin, giving it combined basal–prandial characteristics and a more variable, NPH‐like duration of action [2, 9] that does not meet physiological meal‐related needs. In people with diabetes, obesity is not only the major driver of insulin resistance but also delays subcutaneous insulin absorption through increased subcutaneous fat mass and altered depot structure (including reduced capillary density and blood flow) [10, 11, 12], which slow insulin depot disappearance and impair insulin action [13, 14, 15]. Together, these factors and the large total daily doses typically required in severely insulin‐resistant individuals may further alter HumIns‐U500's action profile. This potentially increases the risk of delayed and nocturnal hypoglycaemia, contributes to weight gain, and necessitates more complex injection schedules and intensive titration [16, 17, 18, 19, 20]—all of which can compromise adherence and make long‐term management particularly challenging.

AT278 (500 U/mL; AT278‐U500) is a new highly concentrated U500 insulin aspart formulation designed to achieve ultra‐rapid action. It combines zinc‐bound hexameric insulin with an absorption accelerator that enhances tissue permeability, a stabilising surfactant, and standard preservatives (phenol and m‐cresol). In a previous euglycaemic clamp study in people with type 1 diabetes, a standard subcutaneous bolus dose of AT278‐U500 demonstrated faster insulin absorption and earlier onset of action compared with insulin aspart in the standard U100 formulation (InsAsp [100 U/mL]; InsAsp‐U100) [21]. However, whether AT278‐U500 maintains its ultra‐rapid characteristics at the higher doses required by obese individuals, who would benefit most from volume reduction of concentrated insulin formulations, has yet to be determined. In the present study, we therefore investigated the pharmacokinetics (PK), pharmacodynamics (PD), and safety of AT278‐U500 compared with InsAsp‐U100 and HumIns‐U500 in overweight and obese people with T2D.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This was a phase 1, single‐dose, randomised, double‐blind, two‐way crossover glucose clamp study with an extension period for an open‐label third comparator, conducted at a single centre (Division of Endocrinology and Diabetology, Department of Internal Medicine, Medical University of Graz, Austria). The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the local health authorities (Eudra CT No. 2022‐001984‐28) and the independent ethics committee of the Medical University of Graz (No. 34‐517 ex 21/22). The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05754424) and conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. All participants provided written informed consent before initiation of any study‐related activities.

Study Population

Eligible participants were men and women aged 18–75 years with T2D for ≥ 6 months, treated with insulin with or without metformin, sulfonylureas, dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 inhibitors, sodium‐glucose co‐transporter‐2 inhibitors, and/or glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists at stable doses for ≥ 3 months before screening. Participants were required to have a body mass index (BMI) of 25–45 kg/m2 and HbA1c ≤ 80 mmol/mol (≤ 9.5%). Individuals were excluded if they had known or suspected hypersensitivity to the study medications or related products, clinically significant concomitant diseases, abnormal values in clinical laboratory, vital signs, and electrocardiogram screening tests, or current treatment with drugs that might interfere with glucose metabolism.

Procedures and Assessments

After a screening visit, eligible participants attended up to three clamp visits separated by 5–21 day washout periods (Figure S1). At the first two clamp visits, participants received a single subcutaneous dose of 0.5 U/kg of AT278‐U500 (Arecor Limited, Little Chesterford, UK) or InsAsp‐U100 (NovoRapid; NovoNordisk, Bagsværd, Denmark) in randomised sequence, injected into a lifted skin fold of the abdominal wall around the umbilicus. HumIns‐U500 (Eli Lilly and Company, Indianapolis, IN, USA) was administered open‐label at the third clamp visit.

Participants temporarily discontinued oral antidiabetic drugs (except metformin and glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists) > 7 days before dosing. Participants using ultralong‐acting and long‐acting insulin analogues were switched to NPH insulin (Insulatard 100 U/mL, 3 mL FlexPen, Novo Nordisk) 72 and 48 h before dosing, respectively. The use of intermediate‐acting and short‐acting insulin analogues was permitted until 3:00 PM and 11:00 PM on the day before dosing, respectively.

At each clamp visit, participants arrived at the study site the evening before dosing, consumed a standardised meal, and started fasting at 10:00 PM. The euglycaemic glucose clamp was initiated at 11:00 PM with an overnight run‐in period. Participants received variable intravenous infusions of insulin (Actrapid [Novo Nordisk] for clamps with AT278‐U500 and InsAsp‐U100; NovoRapid [Novo Nordisk] for clamps with HumIns‐U500) or 20% glucose (Fresenius Kabi, Bad Homburg, Germany) to achieve and maintain plasma glucose (PG) at the target level of 100 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L).

The random allocation sequence was generated using an interactive web response system (https://www.randomizer.at) by a third party not involved in any study activities. Randomisation was performed on the day of first dosing, with participants randomly assigned 1:1 to one of the treatment sequences for crossover comparison of AT278‐U500 and InsAsp‐U100. Both participants and study staff conducting assessments were blinded to the treatment assignment. Unblinded staff, who were not involved in any other study activities, ensured correct treatment allocation, preparation, and administration of study medications. The interactive web response system was programmed with blind‐breaking instructions for use by the investigator in case of emergency. Unblinding was performed after final database lock.

Study medication was administered between 9:00 and 11:00 AM after PG had stabilised for ≥ 1 h with stable insulin infusion and no glucose infusion. Intravenous insulin was gradually decreased after dosing and terminated when PG declined by 5 mg/dL (0.3 mmol/L) from baseline (Figure S2). Variable glucose infusion was then initiated to maintain the target PG level throughout the clamp. The clamps continued for up to 12 h post‐dose AT278‐U500 and InsAsp‐U100, or up to 24 h post‐dose HumIns‐U500, but were terminated earlier if PG was consistently > 160 mg/dL (8.9 mmol/L) without glucose infusion for at least 30 min.

Doses of AT278‐U500 and HumIns‐U500 were prepared gravimetrically using BD Micro‐Fine 0.3 mL syringes with 30‐gauge 8‐mm needles (Becton Dickinson, Heidelberg, Germany). Doses of InsAsp‐U100 were prepared using Omnican 100 syringes with 30‐gauge 8‐mm needles (B. Braun, Melsungen, Germany). A precision balance (CP 25D‐OCE, Sartorius, Göttingen, Germany) with 0.001 mg resolution was used for all dose preparations. Doses were converted from units to weight using the specific density of each study medication and drawn up by trained, unblinded staff. The dose administered was proved by weighing syringes before and after injection.

PD properties were assessed by recording glucose infusion rate (GIR) during the euglycaemic clamp. PG was measured using a Super GL2 glucose analyser (Dr. Müller Gerätebau GmbH, Freital, Germany) at 1‐min intervals until PG decreased by 5 mg/dL (0.3 mmol/L) from baseline (mean of −10, −5, and 0 min pre‐dose). Subsequently, PG analysis and GIR adjustments were performed manually at 1‐ to 30‐min intervals.

PK properties were determined from frequent blood sampling from 20 min before dosing until the end of the clamp period. Serum insulin concentrations were measured using two validated insulin enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs; Mercodia, Uppsala, Sweden): a total serum insulin ELISA (measuring the sum of human insulin and insulin aspart) and a human insulin‐selective ELISA. Insulin aspart concentrations were derived by subtracting the measured human insulin concentration from the total serum insulin concentration at each time point during the AT278‐U500 and InsAsp‐U100 clamps. All PK data were baseline‐corrected. Further details on blood sampling, insulin analytics, and calculations are provided in the Supporting Information (Table S1). To monitor endogenous insulin secretion during the clamps, serum C‐peptide concentrations were measured at predefined intervals using a validated C‐peptide ELISA (Mercodia).

Safety assessments, including adverse events, injection site reactions, hypersensitivity reactions, vital signs, electrocardiograms, and laboratory safety parameters, were performed according to the protocol.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) on an intention‐to‐treat basis including all participants who had completed at least one of the three clamps. No interim analyses were conducted. Data were tested for normality using a Shapiro–Wilk test (5% level of significance).

The primary endpoint was area under the GIR‐time curve from 0 to 60 min (AUCGIR,0–60min). In a previous euglycaemic clamp study with InsAsp‐U100 and faster insulin aspart in people with T2D [22], the standard deviation of log(AUCGIR,0–60min) was 0.68 mg/kg estimated from least square mean treatment ratio (TR) of 1.48 mg/kg (95% confidence interval [CI] 1.24; 1.83), which appeared underestimated compared to a study in people with type 1 diabetes [21]. Sample size was calculated using a one‐sample t‐test for paired differences (5% level of significance, 80% power), with a non‐inferiority margin of −0.163 in log‐space, equivalent to preserving at least 85% of the treatment effect of InsAsp‐U100. Assuming a log‐transformed treatment difference of 0.3 mg/kg (TR 1.35 in the original space) and a standard deviation of 1.0 mg/kg, 39 evaluable participants were required. To ensure 39 evaluable participants, it was planned to randomly assign and expose a maximum of 44 participants.

Insulin data were assessed using baseline‐corrected data, where the baseline insulin concentration (mean of −20, −10, and 0 min) was subtracted from post‐dose concentrations. After baseline correction, any positive values at t = 0 min and all negative values were set to zero. Insulin and PG data were expanded using linear interpolation to generate minute‐resolved data for endpoint calculations, and GIR data were expanded using last observation carried forward. Area under the insulin concentration‐time curve (AUCIns) was calculated using the linear trapezoidal technique and AUCGIR as the area under the step function.

Time to appearance and disappearance were calculated as time from dosing until the first and last time, respectively, that serum insulin aspart concentration was ≥ 38.4 pmol/L (for clamps with AT278‐U500 and InsAsp‐U100) or serum human insulin concentration was ≥ 19.2 pmol/L (for clamp with HumIns‐U500). Time to onset of action was calculated as time from insulin dosing until PG had declined by 5 mg/dL (0.3 mmol/L) from the highest post‐dose PG level.

Endpoints were analysed by using a linear mixed model, with treatment, treatment sequence, and visit as fixed effects and participant as random effect. TR (95% CI) was calculated based on log‐transformed data and back‐transformed to the original scale. The significance level was 2.5% for non‐inferiority and superiority tests for the primary endpoint (one‐sided tests; margins for TR were set at 0.85 and 1.0, respectively, on the original scale). Superiority was tested in a hierarchical manner, conditional on prior demonstration of non‐inferiority.

A sensitivity analysis included only participants who completed euglycaemic clamps with both AT278‐U500 and InsAsp‐U100. Depending on data characteristics, TR (95% CI) was calculated using log‐transformed data or untransformed data. A prespecified subgroup analysis was conducted in participants stratified by median BMI. The slope and p‐value for the association between BMI and the primary endpoint AUCGIR,0–60min were calculated using a linear regression model.

Results

Participants

The screening phase included 69 individuals, of whom 41 were randomised, received at least one study treatment, and were included in analysis (Figure S1). Among these participants, 2 withdrew/discontinued after the first clamp visit due to AEs unrelated to the treatments, while 39 completed both euglycaemic clamps with AT278‐U500 and InsAsp‐U100. Thirty‐six participants completed the additional open‐label comparison with HumIns‐U500.

The study population was white and predominantly male (37 males, 4 females), with a median (min‐max) age of 63 years (42–72). Baseline characteristics included: median body weight 93.1 kg (73.1–122.3), median BMI 29.7 kg/m2 (25–38.7), and median HbA1c of 58 mmol/mol (39–80; 7.5% [5.7–9.5]). The study population had established T2D with a median duration of 18 years (6–32) and a median C‐peptide level of 520 pmol/L (149–1023). All participants were receiving insulin therapy at screening (Table S2), with 93% (38/41) on combination therapy with oral antidiabetic medications and/or injectable glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (Table S3).

AT278‐U500 and InsAsp‐U100 showed comparable offset kinetics, with both formulations reaching 50% of the maximum insulin concentration in the offset phase (t Late50%Cmax) and time to disappearance approximately twice as fast as HumIns‐U500 (Table S5). Overall insulin exposure (AUCIns,0‐tLast) was comparable between AT278‐U500 and InsAsp‐U100 but 1.2‐fold higher for AT278‐U500 vs. HumIns‐U500 (Table S4).

Endogenous insulin secretion was suppressed prior to insulin administration and gradually resumed towards the end of action of the respective insulins, as indicated by C‐peptide concentrations (Figure S3).

Pharmacodynamics

Figure 1C,D show the PD profiles. AT278‐U500 exhibited significantly accelerated insulin action compared with both InsAsp‐U100 and HumIns‐U500 (Table 1). The primary endpoint, AUCGIR,0–60min, was 1.7‐fold higher with AT278‐U500 versus InsAsp‐U100 (Figure 2), establishing both non‐inferiority and superiority (p < 0.0001) based on sensitivity analysis results, and 3.9‐fold higher vs. HumIns‐U500. The higher glucose‐lowering effect observed with AT278‐U500 remained through 2 h post‐dose (AUCGIR,0‐2h) compared with InsAsp‐U100 and through 12 h post‐dose (AUCGIR,0‐12h) compared with HumIns‐U500 (Table S4).

AT278‐U500 and InsAsp‐U100 showed comparable offset dynamics, with both formulations reaching 50% of the maximum GIR in the offset phase (t Late50%GIRmax) approximately twice as fast as HumIns‐U500 (Table S5). Overall glucose‐lowering effect (AUCGIR,0‐tLast) was similar across all three treatments (Table S4).

Subgroup Analysis

In the prespecified subgroup analysis, AT278‐U500 showed consistent early PK/PD responses in both BMI subgroups (BMI < 29.7 kg/m2 and ≥ 29.7 kg/m2), while InsAsp‐U100 exhibited a slower early glucose‐lowering effect in participants with BMI ≥ 29.7 kg/m2 (Figures 3 and S4).

Safety

A total of 54 AEs were reported during the study, of which 4 occurred after administration of AT278‐U500, 5 after InsAsp‐U100, and 8 after HumIns‐U500 (Table S6). The AEs were predominantly mild to moderate in severity and unrelated to the treatments. One serious AE (severe syncope) occurred during the second washout period and was also deemed unrelated to treatment. No injection site reactions were observed.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates that AT278‐U500, a new highly concentrated insulin aspart formulation, exhibits ultra‐rapid early PK/PD properties in overweight and obese people with T2D. Specifically, AT278‐U500 achieved significantly faster insulin absorption compared with standard concentration insulin aspart, InsAsp‐U100, resulting in a doubled early glucose‐lowering effect that was both non‐inferior and superior to InsAsp‐U100, as assessed by the study's primary endpoint AUCGIR,0‐60min. Subgroup analysis indicated that this advantage is maintained across the BMI spectrum studied.

Increasing adiposity has been associated with a delayed and reduced insulin response, driven by changes in subcutaneous tissue composition, impaired insulin depot dynamics, and diminished insulin sensitivity in hepatic and peripheral tissue [13, 23, 24]. Confirming previous findings [25], InsAsp‐U100 in our study exhibited a considerably slower absorption and a blunted PD profile in participants with higher BMI (BMI ≥ 29.7 kg/m2), with AUCGIR,0‐60min showing a negative relationship to BMI. In contrast, AT278‐U500 maintained its fast onset and high early glucose‐lowering effect in the higher BMI subgroup, indicating absorption unaffected by adiposity‐related tissue changes. This advantage was most pronounced during the early post‐dose phase. Beyond 2 h post‐dose, both formulations showed similarly reduced insulin action in participants with higher BMI, likely due to the reduced tissue‐level insulin sensitivity.

The faster PK/PD profile of AT278‐U500 is noteworthy given that its fivefold higher concentration itself opposes rapid absorption. Enhanced hexamer stabilisation and a reduced ratio of diffusion area to amount of insulin in the injection depot typically alter absorption and result in a concentration‐dependent ‘right shift’ in PK/PD that is most pronounced at the U500 level [3, 26]. As previously reported [27, 28, 29], HumIns‐U500 is characterised by a flat and prolonged profile with delayed time‐to‐maximal effect and extended duration of action—a finding our data confirmed. Not all concentrated formulations, however, exhibit this right shift. Unlike HumIns‐U500, the available U200 prandial insulins have demonstrated bioequivalence to their U100 counterparts, likely through an amino‐acid sequence that favours rapid hexamer dissociation, excipient adjustments, or the only modest increase in concentration [26, 30, 31]. To our knowledge, AT278‐U500 is the first U500 prandial insulin formulated to overcome the concentration‐dependent delays in absorption, achieving an ultra‐rapid onset that clearly distinguishes it from the prolonged action profile of HumIns‐U500.

AT278‐U500 contains a hexameric form of insulin aspart and an excipient with a specific binding affinity (logK) for calcium ions, which leads to transient disruption of calcium‐dependent cell adhesion at the injection site via reversible interactions with the calcium‐cadherin complex [32]. This results in increased tissue permeability and consequently faster insulin exposure and enhanced early insulin action compared with InsAsp‐U100. The underlying mechanism for accelerated absorption distinguishes AT278‐U500 from the two ultra‐rapid injectable insulin products currently on the market: faster aspart contains niacinamide, which favours insulin dimerisation at the injection site and additionally enhances local blood flow through vasodilation [33], while URLi contains treprostinil to induce local vasodilation and citrate to increase vascular permeability [34].

The results of the present study are consistent with our previous observations in people with T1D [21]—together demonstrating that AT278‐U500's rapid‐acting characteristics are preserved regardless of diabetes type. In both populations, AT278‐U500 exhibited a distinctive PK/PD profile, characterised by a steep early rise with comparable offset and overall insulin exposure and glucose‐lowering effect to InsAsp‐U100. In the present study, we observed neither a concentration‐dependent right shift nor BMI‐related delayed responses, suggesting that AT278‐U500's excipients effectively counteract the delaying effects of both the higher concentration and increased adiposity.

AT278‐U500's ultra‐rapid, BMI‐independent action profile could offer clinical advantages beyond the convenience of a concentrated formulation, particularly in people with obesity and severe insulin resistance—a population in whom glycaemic targets are often not achieved despite very high insulin doses [35, 36]. Specifically, the more physiological coverage of prandial glucose excursions, combined with the flexibility of meal‐time or post‐meal dosing, may help reduce glycaemic variability, though this remains to be confirmed in a meal study. Given its combination of high concentration and ultra‐rapid kinetics, AT278‐U500 may also facilitate the development of next‐generation miniaturised insulin delivery devices, such as patch pumps and tubeless closed‐loop systems, which have to date been limited by reservoir size and the slow kinetics of existing highly concentrated insulins.

A strength of this study was its randomised, double‐blind, crossover design for the AT278‐U500 versus InsAsp‐U100 comparison. The clamps with HumIns‐U500 were conducted in a non‐randomised, open‐label manner. This was necessary because the markedly different PK/PD profiles between the rapid‐acting insulin aspart formulations and the intermediate‐acting human insulin and the 24‐h clamp duration with HumIns‐U500 does not allow blinding. The euglycaemic clamp technique provided precise, continuous measurement of PK/PD with consistently high clamp quality across all formulations (Figure S5, Table S7). Dosing accuracy was verified and showed no differences between study medications (Table S8), ensuring that observed PK/PD differences reflect true formulation characteristics rather than dosing variability. The inclusion of both standard concentration insulin aspart and the established U500 insulin allowed a direct comparison of AT278‐U500 against relevant clinical options. The prospective BMI stratification allowed systematic evaluation of BMI effects on PK/PD, with the study population representative of those who typically require concentrated insulin formulations in clinical practice.

We cannot exclude that the observed PK/PD advantages of AT278‐U500 may manifest differently in real‐world scenarios compared with the controlled clamp environment. The euglycaemic clamp methodology does not replicate postprandial conditions and meal studies are therefore needed to evaluate the clinical relevance of AT278‐U500's enhanced early action. Additionally, this study did not include comparisons with faster‐acting insulin analogues currently used in clinical practice (e.g., faster aspart U100, URLi U100 and U200), and head‐to‐head studies with these formulations would provide valuable insights to AT278‐U500's relative performance.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that AT278‐U500 maintains its ultra‐rapid onset characteristics independent of BMI in overweight and obese people with T2D. By offering the first ultra‐rapid U500 option for prandial dosing, AT278‐U500 may represent a promising therapeutic advancement for severely insulin‐resistant people with diabetes requiring high‐dose insulin therapy.
