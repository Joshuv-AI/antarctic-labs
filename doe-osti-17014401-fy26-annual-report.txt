Summary
The goal of this project is to develop specific aspects of ultra-high-resolution
microcalorimeter technologies that support IAEA Nuclear Material Laboratory needs and
the goals of SP-1 19/NML-003 "Microcalorimetry Analysis Technique for NML" but are
outside the scope of the SP-1. The focus is on commissioning, assembly, and testing of
the microcalorimeter decay energy spectrometer with superconducting transition-edge
sensors (TESs) and magnetic microcalorimeters (MMCs) in preparation for transfer to the
IAEA Nuclear Material Laboratory.

Task 1: Complete modification and commissioning of the ADR
cryocooler

Initial cooldowns of the adiabatic demagnetization refrigerator (ADR) following its delivery
to LLNL revealed significant thermal performance degradation when the extended
infrared (IR) radiation shields were installed. Consequently, the extended IR shields and
extended vacuum shell were shipped back to LANL, enabling simultaneous refinement
and testing of the IR shield design at LANL while LLNL continued testing the MMC
detectors using the stock IR shields.
Testing resumed in December 2025, following receipt of project funding at LANL. The
addition of multilayer insulation (MLI) to the 3K and 50K IR shields was identified as the
primary factor driving improved thermal performance. In addition, the following
modifications were made to the original design:
  New front panels for the 3K and 50K IR shields were designed and fabricated,
 incorporating deeper, more durable threads for the door panel fasteners.
  A redesigned detector receiver stage with reduced mass was designed and
 fabricated (Figure 1), achieving a mass reduction of 200 g—36% less than the
 original design. This reduction decreases strain on the Kevlar suspension of the
 50 mK stage, mitigating the risk of a thermal contact between the 50 mK, 1 K, and
 3 K stages.
  A new SQUID array bracket was designed and fabricated to relocate the SQUID
 arrays behind the detector receiver, eliminating interference with the removable
 detector modules (Figure 1).
The revised components were transferred to LLNL and installed in the ADR. Figure 2
shows the completed cryostat and associated instrumentation. The removable door
panels on the vacuum shell and 3K/50K shields provide convenient access to the detector
module without requiring the cryostat to be moved to a service stand or the full removal
of the vacuum shell and IR shield.

The 3K and 50 mK stage temperatures reported reflect values recorded after cooldown
from room temperature. The 50 mK base temperature (third column, Table 1) corresponds
to the temperature reached at the 50 mK stage following completion of the magnet
regeneration cycle. Hold time refers to the duration available for measurements at a given
set-point temperature while the ADR operates in regulation mode - here, 80 mK, the
recommended temperature for TES measurements. MMC measurements may be
conducted at alternative temperatures; lower set points yield shorter hold times, while
higher set points extend them.
Overall, the custom IR shields improved base temperatures at both the 3K and 50 mK
stages relative to the stock shields without MLI, though the 50 mK hold time decreased.
This reduction was anticipated, since the stock shield configuration lacked wiring to the
50 mK detector stage and therefore the detector stage carried a lower thermal load. No
substantial performance difference was observed between the fully MLI-covered door
panel configuration and the partially covered, user-accessible configuration.
While operating in regulation mode, the ADR maintained temperature stability (one
standard deviation) consistently below 5 µK at 80 mK and approximately 10 µK at 50 mK.

Previously, detector energy resolution was only adversely affected by temperature
excursions exceeding 50 µK from the set point, confirming that the system's temperature
stability does not compromise detector performance.
At the time of this report, the 50 mK hold time for the new IR shield configuration remains
to be confirmed. Beyond this, the only outstanding component is the front panel of the
magnetic shielding, which is delayed due to long manufacturer lead times; delivery is
expected by the end of September 2026, with installation to follow immediately. Notably,
detector performance remains strong even without the complete magnetic shielding in
place. The ADR is on track and ready for the planned sponsor demonstration scheduled
for November 2026.

Task 2: Complete commissioning of the TES decay energy
spectrometer

Our original plan called for test measurements of the TES module using a comparable
ADR system at LANL, enabling parallel testing of the TES and MMC modules. However,
persistent difficulties with the high-temperature superconducting leads in the LANL ADR
made this approach impractical. As a result, we consolidated all testing efforts at LLNL.
We successfully completed the design and fabrication of the TES module, which
incorporates 8 functioning detectors (Figure 3). The module attaches to the detector
receiver stage via three M5 screws located at its top (see also Figures 1 and 2) and can
be removed using a central M4 screw that pushes against the receiver stage. The module
currently houses 8 DES detectors - 4 Pronghorn-style devices and 4 square devices -
each equipped with three MoAu bilayer TESs. Every TES is biased individually, with bias
voltages controlled by eight precision voltage sources mounted in the instrumentation
rack (Figure 2). To amplify the TES signal, each device connects to a first-stage input
SQUID (Figure 3, right), which in turn feeds into a SQUID array (Figure 1). Each SQUID
array operates under a feedback loop that sends voltage pulses to the data acquisition
system.
The current channel assignments are as follows:
 - Channel 1: Square DES, 1 Si beam length
 - Channel 2: Square DES, 0.5 Si beam length
 - Channel 3: Pronghorn B style
 - Channel 4: Pronghorn B style
 - Channel 5: Pronghorn B style
 - Channel 6: Pronghorn B style
 - Channel 7: Square DES, 0.5 Si beam length

 - Channel 8: Square DES, 0.5 Si beam length

Figure 3: Left photo: TES detector mount with aluminum cover removed. Right photos:
detailed view of a TES detector and the first stage input SQUID. The detector is attached
via BeCu clamps. The Nb clamp can be turned like a record player arm and used to clamp
the absorber to the gold pad on the TES chip.
For the square DES devices, "beam length" refers to the silicon beams connecting the
TES island—which houses the DES and sample—to the thermal bath; longer beams
correspond to lower thermal conductance (G). For the Pronghorn devices, "style" refers
to the thickness of these connecting silicon beams. Device selection was informed by
prior testing that demonstrated good energy resolution and pulse decay times.
Measurements were conducted using 8 absorbers prepared from the ISO 51495-01
sample supplied by the IAEA NML. To ensure uniform distribution of the nuclear material,
the gold foil containing the sample was folded and kneaded with pliers through 20 cycles.
The foil was then divided into 8 parts, each mounted onto one of the 8 detectors using
niobium clamps (Figure 3). The clamp tips were coated with GE varnish (GE 7031), an
insulating adhesive that prevents electrical shorting between the ground and the TES.
Representative pulses and spectra are shown in Figure 4. The clamp-based attachment
method improved pulse rise time to below 0.4 ms—a tenfold improvement over the rise
times typically observed with indium-bonded absorbers. Pulse decay time also improved

by a factor of 2–5 relative to square DES devices with lower G values used in earlier
measurements. However, several square and Pronghorn devices exhibited longer pulse
tailing than would be expected from their initial decay time constants. Further investigation
into this behavior is warranted to determine whether it stems from slow thermalization of
the TES chip, the absorber geometry, or the clamping attachment itself.

Figure 4: Left graph: Example pulse record for each of the 8 channels. Right graphs:
Spectra obtained from the September test measurements of ISO 51495-01. Each
spectrum is measured with a different channel. Included in the plots is the trigger rate
(TR) which is roughly equivalent to the activity of the sample.
The full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the Pu-239/240 peaks ranged from 4 to 8
keV—sufficient to resolve the peaks, but notably broader than the ~2 keV FWHM
previously observed for this absorber and attachment configuration. Sample preparation
is the most likely limiting factor: additional kneading cycles would improve the uniformity
of nuclear material distribution and reduce position-dependent variations in energy
deposition within the absorber. Notably, energy resolution appears to degrade
significantly in absorbers exhibiting higher count rates, potentially due to difficulty
generating a clean noise spectrum uncontaminated by pulse records. We are currently
developing improvements to the data analysis software to address this limitation.
Overall, we are confident that the TES detector module described here constitutes a
functional device suitable for measuring safeguards samples at the IAEA NML. Our
collaborators at NIST plan to deliver an additional 20 square devices and 10 Pronghorn
devices by November 2026, which will serve as spares for the IAEA NML in case a
detector requires replacement.

Task 3: Demonstrate MMC Decay Energy Spectrometer in the Dilu on
Refrigerator

While the ADR was being repaired and commissioned, Lawrence Livermore National
Laboratory (LLNL) tested the Magnetic Microcalorimeter-Decay Energy Spectrometer
(MMC-DES) module in a dilution refrigerator to characterize its performance. The fully
assembled, eight-pixel MMC-DES module is shown in Figure 5. Due to the limited number
of readout cryocables in the dilution refrigerator, four pixels were tested per cooldown. All
eight pixels were tested over two cooldowns and met the success criteria of 5 keV FWHM
energy resolution and 1% uncertainty. The tested detector module was then installed in
the NML-ADR and tested again for demonstration. Detector performance and additional
details are described in Task 4.

Figure 5: The MMC-DES detector module for IAEA NML. Total 8 pixels MMC sensors
are mounted with 8 NML samples via gold wirebonding coupling. Superconducting
readout cables will be replaced with commercial-grade cables being fabricated by Star
Cryoelectronics LLC.

Task 4: Demonstrate MMC Decay Energy Spectrometer in the IAEA-ADR

The 8-pixel MMC detector module previously tested in a dilution refrigerator was installed
in the NML-ADR for final commissioning. The first six pixels were loaded with ISO-51496
samples, and the remaining two pixels were loaded with IDA-51496 samples. Figure 6
shows a screenshot of the Microcalorimetry Online Acquisition and Processing (MOAP)

software developed by LLNL, which independently and simultaneously collects plutonium
decay signal pulses from all eight MMC pixels.

Figure 6: A MOAP software screenshot showing plutonium decay pulses from 8 MMC
pixels.
Collected pulses are analyzed using the same MOAP software to produce energy spectral
histograms. The energy spectra are then analyzed using LLNL Python software to extract
peak counts. This python fitting software will be integrated into MOAP in next months.
Initial results for ²⁴⁰Pu/²³⁹Pu ratios were obtained during two measurement cycles: 3 days
at 80 mK and 3 days at 100 mK. These measurements were used to compare energy
resolution, ADR operation time (holding time), and uncertainty levels. The results are
summarized in Table 2.
Energy resolutions were slightly poorer than that in the dilution refrigerator. This could be
due to increased SQUID pick-up noise or different temperature stability, which is under
investigation. Operation at 100 mK provided longer measurement time and smaller
statistical uncertainties, although the energy resolution was slightly poorer. This indicates
that longer operation time may be preferable for achieving smaller statistical uncertainties
per operation cycle. The superior energy resolution of TES detectors could complement
this result when high resolving power is needed. NML’s gamma-ray spectroscopy
achieves an uncertainty of 0.3%; therefore, an uncertainty below 0.3% is required, with
0.1% as the preferred target. 0.4 % uncertainty was achieved by a single ADR
regeneration cycle at 100 mK measurement by combining 6 pixel data. Pixel 4-A and 4-
B showed significantly higher statistics with higher activity samples, indicating that 0.3 %

or better uncertainty can be achieved if a sample is counted by multiple pixels at high
activity of > 5 Bq.
A demonstration to the NA-241 program office is planned for November 2026, and a final
demonstration to the IAEA will be scheduled afterward. Final commissioning runs for
refinement and preparation for routine operation at NML will be carried out in FY27 under
USSP Task A.318 (LLNL) to establish end-user operating procedures and verify software
functionality.

Task 5. Construction of a High-Resolution Gamma Spectrometer
(HRGS)

The microcal ultra high resolution gamma spectrometer (nicknamed “Edelweiss”) has
been commissioned and is acquiring spectra. The system uses an adiabatic
demagnetization refrigerator (ADR) (Figure 7) to attain temperatures below 100 mK.
Within the ADR, the microcal array is housed with necessary cryogenic readout
electronics (figure 2). The array is made up of 256 transition edge sensor microcal pixels,
each with a tin gamma-ray absorbing element of approximate volume 2 mm by 2 mm by
0.5 mm.
The ADR was commissioned in the first half of the FY. It has been seen to hold the
operating temperature of 90 mK for at least 24 hours. When the hold time runs out, the

system must undergo regeneration using a magnet to magnetize and demagnetize a
paramagnetic salt, a process which takes about 2 hours. The exact hold time is dependent
on voltage bias applied to the array and exact magnet cycle regeneration conditions (such
as magnitude of magnetic field and length of time held at field).

Figure 7: The Edelweiss microcal gamma-ray spectrometer. The ADR cryostat is seen in
the center of the photo on the orange table, with associated valve control motor to the left
and electronics rack to the right.

Figure 8: Transition edge sensor array. Eight clusters of tin absorbers of gamma-ray
pixels are seen in the center, a total of 256. Readout chips surround the pixels, and eight
LEDs are installed to either side of the chips.

The array was installed in mid-FY and initially tested using borrowed electronics. By early
Q4, all room temperature readout electronics for Edelweiss were available, installed, and
tested. Finally, LEDs were installed in the array box, seen in Figure 8. Readout chips
within the array box are sensitive to acquired gamma-ray dose, becoming noisy with
extended dose as charges are believed to be trapped near microfabricated circuitry in the
chips. Shining LEDs on the chips releases the charges and resets the system to normal
operating conditions, a relatively simple and rapid process.
Preliminary spectra of plutonium reference standard CRM 136 produced a spectrum co-
added from 84 gamma microcal pixels, using 3 hours of data from a longer spectrum,
providing 97 eV peak FWHM at 103 keV (0.094% energy resolution) (Figure 9. This
spectrum was obtained with little optimization of measurement conditions or advanced
data processing, and there is much room for improvement in noise conditions.

Figure 9: Magnified regions of spectra from plutonium reference standard CRM136. Peak
full-width at half-maxima attained were 99 eV at 59 keV and 97 eV at 103 keV using the
co-added spectra of 84 pixels
Efforts are underway near the end of FY26 to optimize noise conditions to provide the
highest pixel yield and energy resolution. A total of 256 pixels are available on the array,
but only a subset is typically used to produce the final spectrum, as only the pixel spectra
with desired resolution are usually chosen for co-adding. Optimization of noise will
improve the overall resolution and number of pixel spectra that can be used. This is done
through ensuring all cabling is properly shielded and grounded such that ground loops
are eliminated. Such optimization can be a meticulous process to identify all places in the
system with problematic grounding, but progress is underway. Furthermore, excessive
noise to many pixels can be caused by just a few pixels in which the absorber is poorly
thermally anchored. The problem is solved by identifying the poorly behaved pixels and
removing the absorbers. Several pixels have already been identified and removed, overall
lowering noise. We anticipate finishing noise optimization in FY27 Q1 while waiting for
arrival of shipment crates and full shipment approval.
For shipment plans of the system, plans are in progress. Quotes have been requested
for international shipment crates. The export control team at Los Alamos has been
engaged early for review of hardware components, with no export concerns raised so far.
Software is under review by the Feynman Center for Innovation team at Los Alamos for
appropriate release of this intellectual property.
