EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
The National Energy Technology Laboratory (NETL) has performed a well-to-wheels life cycle
assessment (LCA) of petroleum production and refining for the United States (US), both from
international and domestic oil sources. This analysis largely follows the same methods and
framework established by Cooney et al., which performed a greenhouse gas (GHG) LCA of crude
products for the 2014 data year (Cooney et al., 2017). Results are presented for six major
petroleum products (gasoline, diesel, jet fuel, fuel oil, coke, bunker/residual fuel oil) across each
of the five Petroleum Administration for Defense Districts (PADDs) and at the national US level.
However, there are at least seven other refinery outputs (liquified petroleum gas, refinery fuel
gas, hydrogen, petrochemical feedstocks, asphalt, sulfur) that are modeled but not shown in
this report for brevity. Petroleum product amounts are compared against those reported by US
Energy Information Administration (EIA) for each region. This 2016 update of the NETL
Petroleum Baseline differs from the previous 2014 version in three key ways (Cooney et al.,
2017):
 1. It now covers multiple impact categories for the Tool for Reduction and Assessment of
 Chemicals and Other Environmental Impacts (TRACI), instead of only GHG emissions.
 2. The data year for the mix of crude oils being imported and refined is based on 2016 EIA
 data instead of 2014.
 3. Crude and petroleum product transportation emissions factors are updated
Several publicly available tools are utilized to build out the 2016 NETL Petroleum Baseline,
including the Oil Production Greenhouse gas Emissions Estimator (OPGEE) version 2.0,
Petroleum Refinery Life Cycle Inventory Model (PRELIM) version 1.5, and openLCA. There are
also updates in modeling data that expand both the quantity and detail of data, for example
OPGEE has data on a greater number of oil wells and has more oil field parameters available to
utilize.

1 INTRODUCTION
The National Energy Technology Laboratory (NETL) has carried out a well-to-wheels life cycle
analysis (LCA) for the US petroleum consumption across both domestic and international
sources. In addition, the Petroleum Baseline LCA model has coverage for a wide suite of
different petroleum products, including gasoline, diesel, jet fuel, fuel oil, coke, and bunker fuel.
Bunker fuel is also known as liquid heavy ends (LHE) or residual fuel oil. The scale of the impact
assessment is conducted to encompass each of the five US Petroleum Administration for
Defense Districts (PADDs) and the national level of the US The five PADDs are categorized as 1:
East Coast, 2: Midwest, 3: Gulf coast, 4: Rocky Mountain, and 5 West Coast, as shown in Exhibit
1-1 below.

All the data and tools used in the analysis are publicly available and open source, greatly
increasing the usability and review of the LCA results and methods. Furthermore, specific well
and refinery data are proprietary, which necessitates the use of broader, but representative
data from sources like US EIA to characterize the source, quantity, and quality of crude oil
produced. Most importantly, the Petroleum Baseline model will provide a valuable reference
case with which to compare alternate scenarios in which either different technologies or
policies are deployed.

Changes are made to the existing Petroleum Baseline to update the data and modeling from
2014 to 2016 (Cooney et al., 2017). The key updates to this work are in characterizing the

 6

current assay mix and corresponding changes in production, refining, and transport.
Furthermore, impact results are generated for Tool for Reduction and Assessment of Chemicals
and Other Environmental Impacts (TRACI) 2.1 impact category rather than just greenhouse
gases (GHGs). Past work was modeled in Excel using Energy Information Administration (EIA)
and National Energy Board (NEB) data and results generated using Excel-based tools Oil
Production Greenhouse gas Emissions Estimator (OPGEE) and Petroleum Refinery Life Cycle
Inventory Model (PRELIM). The current approach includes using the newer OPGEE 2.0 and
PRELIM 1.5 versions, updated 2016 EIA data, and the openLCA software tool to compile and
model all the available data generated.

2 METHODS
The existing OPGEE characterized list of possible crude oils produced and consumed in the US
were utilized for the petroleum baseline. Then representative groups of crude oils were
compiled to approximate the necessary PADD assay mixes. Data from the US EIA is used to
guide the selection of imported and domestically produced crude oils based on their source,
quality, and quantity. The life cycle model for petroleum is constructed using open-source
models OPGEE and PRELIM, as was done for the 2014 NETL petroleum baseline. In order to
maintain transparency, proprietary data was avoided in favor of use of public data. Any
modifications from the previously documented methods are discussed below.

Furthermore, secondary and tertiary production, like enhanced oil recovery (EOR), are included
as extraction technologies.

Over 30 parameters are integrated into the calculation of impacts for each oil field, including
production methods, field properties, fluid properties, production and processing practices, and
land use changes. All necessary data from the Oil Climate Index (OCI) are conveniently provided
in the proper layout/format for the OPGEE Input sheet and covers a total of 75 different oil
fields (Wang et al., 2016). Twelve additional oil fields are added that include four different
Canadian oil sand sites taken from existing 2014 Petroleum Baseline data (Cooney et al., 2017).
Where OCI data are missing, OPGEE default values for oil fields are used when the OPGEE
macro is run. Values are also sometimes adjusted using a bulk assessment method and flagged
when they fall outside known literature values. The energy consumption and GHG impacts are
then generated for each stage, the latter of which are further broken down into combustion,
land use, venting, fugitive, and flaring emissions. The functional units used are 1 MJ and 1

 8

 barrel (bbl) of crude oil delivered to refinery, with the MJ unit being based on the lower heating
 value (LHV).

 However, for this update, the Global Warming Potential (GWP) impact results generated by the
 OPGEE model are not used directly. Instead as many of the raw flow inputs as possible are
 extracted and used in conjunction with NETL unit processes in the openLCA model to generate
 a complete environmental impact profile for crude production. The “Run Assessment” Macro
 was modified to pull all the intermediary inventory data and enter them into new worksheets.
 Intermediary data included:

 • Energy use and imports from natural gas, diesel, residual fuel oil and electricity
 (MMBTU/day)
 • Vented gas at every step of crude processing (g/day)
 • Flared and fugitive gas emissions (MMscf/year)
 • Emissions from land use/transformation (g CO2e/MJ of crude)
 • Small source emissions (kg CO2e/bbl of crude)
 • Transport distances (miles)
 It should be noted that energy import values can be negative, for example if a PADD produces
 enough natural gas to export after satisfying energy needs for fuel petroleum transportation,
 production, etc.

2.1.1 Crude
The next step was to examine how much crude was produced/imported from each oil field as
part of the US oil production mix for each PADD (Canada Energy Regulator, 2025a; EIA, 2019c,
2019a). EIA reports data on all petroleum imports, including the country of origin, to which
PADD the crude is imported and processed, the quantity of crude, and the properties of the
crude itself. Domestic crude oil production is also tracked by EIA, which records where it is
extracted, transported, and refined by state or PADD. Therefore, updated 2016 EIA and
Canadian NEB data were used to generate the crude oil mix results for each PADD by combining
the densities of each assay with their respective production/import volumes to get the mass
percentages. However, while the volume of domestic and foreign crude by each mode is being
reported by EIA, distance is not. Therefore, supplementary information from the US Federal
Highway Administration (FHWA) is used to get the tonne-km values necessary for conducting
the LCA (ORNL, 2019).

The 2015 heavy crude value from Canada was aggregated, so it was allocated into conventional
heavy, syncrude, and bitumen using the previously known 2014 import distributions of heavy
crude types into the US (Canada Energy Regulator, 2025b). So, if heavy Canadian crude
imported into US PADD 3 was historically 30% conventional heavy, 24% syncrude, and 47%
bitumen, then those same percentages are used to allocate the reported total of heavy crude.

Domestic transport by mode of crude and refined petroleum products is updated from 2009 to
2016 values. Data from a previous Bureau of Transportation Statistics (BTS) source stopped
reporting after 2009 in the given tonne-km format, but a new BTS data source was found using
the Freight Analysis Framework (FAF) tool from Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) (BTS,
2012; ORNL, 2019). There is EIA data which reports on how many barrels of crude are
transported by each mode and between each PADD and nationally (EIA, 2019b). Therefore, by
cross-referencing the BTS and EIA information, the total distance of crude transported can be
calculated.

There were some conflicts in trying to match the various oil fields between OPGEE and PRELIM
because of what was available in their respective databases. Therefore, the closest assays were

 10

selected first based on location and then by assay property criteria when transitioning the oil
field data from OPGEE to PRELIM.

2.2 PRELIM
PRELIM is a mass and energy-based tool that can be used to analyze the energy use and GHG
emissions related to refinery operations at the process unit level (Abella et al., 2021). The
functional units are set as 1 MJ of product except for crude oil and sulfur, which use 1 kg of
product. It includes data on 149 different crude assays from all over the world and 15 different
refinery configurations across coking and hydrocracking, which range from simpler
hydroskimming to the more complex medium and deep conversions. Several processes are
responsible for the dynamics of the refinery configuration, like the atmospheric distillation
tower, vacuum distillation tower, gas oil hydrocracker, coker, fluid catalytic cracker (FCC), and
resid hydrocracker. Furthermore, custom assays can be created from a mix of the available
crude assays in PRELIM, a feature utilized to create a representative assay mix for each of the
five US PADD regions. PRELIM generates the entire crude assay profile by using a crude
distillation curve across 9 fractions and various petroleum properties, including API gravity,
carbon residue, and sulfur and hydrogen content. In addition, there are over 15 more input
parameters that can be easily modified by the user beyond the assay and refinery
configuration. There are also flue gas treatment and sulfur recovery allocated to units that
produce off gas. Informed parameter choices were made to be consistent with the previous LCA
study, like using hydrogen content allocation because it is a crucial indicator of emissions given
its relationship with carbon and energy intensity (Bredeson et al., 2010; Cooney et al., 2017).

2.2.1 Refinery Gate-to-Consumer
Transportation of finished petroleum products is calculated using a combination of BTS, EIA,
ANL, Census, and geographical data. BTS and EIA have the amount of petroleum product being
transported, however they each have their own set of limitations. BTS reports petroleum
products by state for origin and destination and includes all major modes of transportation
(e.g., pipeline, rail, truck, water), but only gasoline and fuel oils (i.e., diesel) are given as the
available products. EIA has data on all types of petroleum products but is limited to showing
transport only between the PADDs and not within them. Furthermore, all modes are accounted
for except for trucking, which is missing data. Therefore, it is necessary to integrate BTS and EIA
data to have all transport modes, petroleum products, and same level of detail. There was also
a lack of NETL impact data for coke and bunker fuel for combustion and upstream transport, so
emissions data from the GREET model was used (ANL, 2018).

The approach starts with the BTS tonne-km data and extrapolating out the total tonne-km of all
products based on the fact that gasoline and diesel make up 65% of petroleum use by volume
(EIA, 2021). Then the total tonne-km is allocated among each product based on their
transported mass values as reported by EIA and then among each mode of transportation given
the BTS data.

The Census and other location data are used to estimate the transportation distances. The
Census data is used in measuring the distance traveled within a state (i.e., intrastate) based on
its relative size (U.S. Census Bureau, 2010). The distance between states (i.e., interstate) were
calculated using the central latitude and longitude points of each state. To refine this approach,
previously used BTS data was referenced to adjust the calculated values. For example, all the
interstate distances were increased by 27% to account for the fact that travel does not
realistically happen in a straight line between the origin and destination. As for intrastate
transport, post-optimization distances sum up to 25% of a states’ diameters on average.

3 RESULTS & DISCUSSION
Comparing the results from the old 2014 Excel with the 2016 openLCA models (Exhibit
3-1,Exhibit 3-2) there are noticeable differences in the well-to-wheel and well-to-tank
emissions, with results showing a mix of increased and decreased impacts, depending on fuel
and PADD. Well-to-wheel and well-to-tank emissions are down for gasoline across all PADDs,
despite the 1 g CO2e/MJ increase in combustion emissions. There are mixed results for the
other fuels (Exhibit 3-3,Exhibit 3-4). A more in-depth breakdown of the custom assay mix
impacts is provided in APPENDIX C: Detailed GWP Results, where emissions are broken down by
stage, and show how there were generally decreases in crude extraction for all PADDs relative
to 2014, but the reductions are offset by generally higher refining emissions. Well-to-wheel
emissions show a notable increase in emissions for jet fuel, coke, and bunker fuel because of
greater combustion intensities. Several reasons account for the changes between 2014 and
2016, one of which was updated OPGEE data, which showed reduced crude extraction impacts
across multiple oil fields. So even when the share of foreign crude oil increases in 2016, the
overall crude production impact decreases. The various reductions in impacts lead to reduced
well-to-tank emissions that are offset by the generally increased combustion emissions, which
used a mix of more precise NETL and GREET data rather than EPA reported values. Another
reason was using tailored emissions calculations for crude transport instead of a generic
emissions value being used across all PADDs. New data sources for the petroleum product
transport were also used since updates for previously utilized BTS data were discontinued in
2009. Changes made in OPGEE and PRELIM versions also show improved modeling capabilities.

APPENDIX B: LIFE CYCLE IMPACT ASSESSMENT COMPARISONS
The graphs included in this Appendix are to demonstrate the inventory expansion achieved in
this work, allowing a wider range of environmental impacts to be assessed. The graphs are on a
well-to-tank basis to highlight the differences in upstream impacts. Emissions for other impact
categories are very dependent on end-use case, with stationary combustion using pollution
controls will have a very different set of emissions than combustion from vehicle use.
This analysis uses a modified version of TRACI 2.1 method for calculating impact assessment
results (Bare, 2012). TRACI is an impact assessment method developed by the Environmental
Protection Agency’s (EPA) National Risk Management Research Laboratory. TRACI implements
midpoint metrics that describe impacts at some point between the inventory and ultimate
damage to the environment (e.g., chlorofluorocarbons [CFCs] [inventory] depleting the ozone
[midpoint], allowing higher ultraviolet B irradiation, leading to increased rates of human skin
cancer [ultimate damage]). Two impact categories are modified, and three are omitted. The
NETL modifications to TRACI are discussed below and include using updated global warming
potential (GWP) factors from the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) Fifth
Assessment Report (AR5) (IPCC, 2013).
The original version of TRACI was released by EPA in 2002. It was created following a literature
survey of existing impact assessment methods, which determined that no tool existed that
could provide a comprehensive method applicable to the United States. TRACI 2.0 was released
in 2011 and included new characterization factors for human health cancer/non-cancer and
ecotoxicity. These factors are from the USEtox model, which was created as a global consensus
model (Bare, 2012). Further changes have been made in TRACI 2.1 for the impact categories of
acidification, photochemical smog formation, and human health particulates. The changes to
acidification appear to be the adoption of a new reference flow (kg sulfur dioxide [SO2] rather
than moles hydrogen ions [H+]). Smog formation and human health particulates have
undergone more significant updates, and make use of new underlying models (Bare, 2012). This
analysis utilizes the latest factors available in TRACI 2.1, with modified characterization factors
for Global Warming and Human Health Particulates. Toxicity categories from USEtox are not
included because NETL baseline power plants do not account for all toxic environmental
emissions.
The following is a list of the impact categories included in this analysis:
 • Global Warming: Global warming is the average increase in the temperature of the
 Earth’s surface and lower atmosphere. Global warming can occur as a result of increased
 emissions of greenhouse gases (GHGs) (Bare, 2012). Reporting units are g
 CO2-equivalent (CO2e).
 • Acidification: The increased concentration of hydrogen ions in a local environment. This
 can be from the direct addition of acids, or by indirect chemical reactions from the
 addition of substances such as ammonia (Bare, 2012). Reporting units are g
 SO2-equivalent.

 B-1

 • Eutrophication: The “enrichment of an aquatic ecosystem with nutrients (nitrogen,
 phosphorus) that accelerate biological productivity (growth of algae and weeds) and an
 undesirable accumulation of algal biomass” (EPA, 2008). Reporting units are g nitrogen
 (N)-equivalent.
 • Photochemical Smog Formation: Ground-level ozone, formed by the reaction of NOx
 and volatile organic compounds (VOCs) in the presence of sunlight (Bare, 2012).
 Reporting units are g trichlorofluoromethane (CFC-11)-equivalent.
 • Ozone Depletion: The deterioration of ozone within the stratosphere by chemicals such
 as CFCs. Stratospheric ozone provides protection for people, crops, and other plant life
 from radiation (Bare, 2012). Reporting units are kg ozone (O3)-equivalent per MWh.
 • Human Health Particulates: Particulate matter (PM) includes “a mixture of solid
 particles and liquid droplets found in the air” that are smaller than 10 microns in
 diameter (EPA, 2013). These small diameter particles can enter deep inside the lungs
 and cause many serious health problems. Almost all PM health impacts are caused by
 PM 2.5 microns or smaller (PM2.5) (Humbert, 2009). Reporting units are kg PM2.5-
 equivalent per MWh.

Modified Global Warming Potential
GHGs in this analysis are reported on a common mass basis of CO2e using the GWPs of each gas
from IPCC AR5, rather than the IPCC Fourth Assessment Report (AR4) factors used in TRACI 2.1
(IPCC, 2013). The choice to use AR5 is to reflect the latest research and assumes that TRACI will
implement AR5 GWPs in the future. The default GWP used is the 100-year time frame, but in
some cases, results for the 20-year time frame are presented as well. All GHG results in this
analysis are expressed as 100-yr GWPs unless specified otherwise. The GWPs for methane (CH4)
account for climate carbon feedback and CO2 from CH4 oxidation (an appropriate adder for fossil
CH4) (IPCC, 2013). Exhibit B-1 shows the GWPs used for the GHGs that were inventoried.
