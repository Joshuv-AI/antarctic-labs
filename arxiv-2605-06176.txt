Stochastic Optimal Control for Jump Diffusion Models with Singular Drifts Thanks: This work was partially supported by a grant from the IMU-CDC and Simons Foundation
Antoine-Marie Bogso, Edward Fuituh Kameh, Olivier Menoukeu Pamen, Felix Shu

Abstract
. We study a stochastic optimal control problem for jump–diffusion systems whose drift coefficient is piecewise Lipschitz continuous and exhibits threshold-induced discontinuities. Such dynamics naturally arise in applications with intervention policies triggered by safety levels, notably in insurance surplus management with dividend payments and capital injections. These features place the problem outside the scope of classical stochastic maximum principle (SMP) results, which rely on global smoothness assumptions. We establish both necessary and sufficient optimality conditions for this class of control problems. Our approach combines a Sobolev-type representation of the first variation process with smooth approximations and Ekeland’s variational principle. As application, we study an optimal premium adjustment and reserve management policies for an insurance whose surplus is modelled by threshold-based dividend and capital injection policies.

1.
Introduction

Stochastic optimal control problems are fundamental to the modelling and analysis of systems subject to uncertainty, with applications in finance, engineering, and the natural sciences. The aim is to determine a control process that minimizes (or maximizes) an expected cost functional for a system whose dynamics are governed by stochastic differential equations (SDEs). In the classical setting, the controlled system satisfies

dX_{t}^{\alpha}=b(t,X_{t}^{\alpha},\alpha_{t})dt+\sigma(t,X_{t}^{\alpha},\alpha_{t})dB_{t},\qquad X_{0}^{\alpha}=x_{0},

where
B_{t}
is a standard Brownian motion,
\alpha_{t}
is an admissible control, and
b
and
\sigma
denote the drift and diffusion coefficients. The objective is to characterize the optimal control
\hat{\alpha}
minimizing

J(\alpha)=\mathbb{E}\big[\int_{0}^{T}f(t,X_{t}^{\alpha},\alpha_{t})dt+g(X_{T}^{\alpha})\big].

The stochastic maximum principle (SMP), first introduced by Kushner
[
12
]
and further developed in
[
3
,
4
,
8
,
23
,
31
]
, provides necessary (and sometimes sufficient) conditions for optimality via adjoint processes and variational inequalities, under suitable regularity assumptions and convex control sets. Two main approaches exist for solving such problems: the
dynamic programming method
, leading to the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation, and the
SMP or Pontryagin-type approach
. The dynamic programming method, pioneered by Bellman, relies on the Markov property and regularity of the value function, whereas the SMP offers a more flexible framework, especially when jumps or non-smooth coefficients are present.

The classical SMP for diffusion models was first established under strong regularity conditions such as global Lipschitz continuity and boundedness of
b
and
\sigma
(see for example
[
23
,
29
,
31
]
and references therein). These results were later extended to jump-diffusion systems (see for
[
22
]
and references therein).

However, most existing results rely critically on the smoothness (or global Lipschitz continuity) of the drift
b(t,x,\alpha)
in
x
and
\alpha
. This assumption guarantees existence and uniqueness of strong solutions and the differentiability of the state process with respect to control perturbations. In many real-world systems, though, such regularity is too restrictive: dynamics may exhibit discontinuities or abrupt changes, as in hybrid systems, threshold-type financial models, switching diffusions, or controlled processes with regime changes. In these cases, where the drift is only piecewise Lipschitz, classical variational methods fail because the first variation process may not exist in the classical sense. These challenges motivate the development of an SMP under weaker regularity assumptions.

Recent studies have addressed SDEs with discontinuous drifts in the Brownian setting (see, e.g.,
[
10
,
15
,
16
,
18
]
and references therein). More recently, Przemysław and Szölgyenyi
[
26
,
27
]
examined jump-diffusion models with piecewise Lipschitz drifts, motivated by applications in electricity, insurance, and financial markets. Building on this line of research, recent works
[
5
,
17
,
30
]
have established necessary and sufficient SMPs for Brownian-driven systems with irregular drift. These approaches typically combine smooth approximations of the control problem, Sobolev-type representations of the first variation process, and Ekeland’s variational principle to ensure convergence of the corresponding adjoint processes.

In this paper, we study a stochastic optimal control problem for
jump-diffusion systems
whose drift coefficient is
piecewise Lipschitz continuous
. We establish both necessary and sufficient optimality conditions for such systems. The main analytical difficulty arises from the singularity of the drift, which prevents the direct application of classical standard SMP results. Our approach begins with an explicit Sobolev-type representation of the first variation process, followed by the construction of a sequence of approximating problems with smooth coefficients. Using the fact that the law of the state process admits a density with respect to Lebesgue measure, we apply Ekeland’s variational principle to obtain a sequence of adjoint processes and, by passing to the limit, derive an SMP for the original jump-diffusion control problem.

Another key step in our analysis is a convergence result for nonlinear functionals of approximating state processes. In the Brownian additive-noise setting (see, e.g.,
[
5
,
17
]
), Girsanov’s theorem allows one to reduce to expectations involving a Brownian motion, for which explicit Gaussian density estimates are available. Such a reduction is not available in the present jump-diffusion framework. Instead, we exploit the intrinsic regularity of the state process. More precisely, we prove a time-integrated convergence result for compositions of drift approximations with the state process, relying only on uniform moment bounds and a density estimate of order
t^{-1/2}
(see for e.g. Theorem
B.2
). This estimate plays a central role in passing to the limit in the adjoint equations (see Lemma
3.5
) and provides a robust alternative to Girsanov-based arguments under low regularity of the drift.

The main contribution of this paper is the relaxation of the global Lipschitz assumption on the drift to piecewise Lipschitz continuity while retaining a valid stochastic maximum principle for jump-diffusion control systems. This extension broadens the applicability of stochastic control theory to models featuring structural breaks and regime-dependent dynamics.

Threshold-based control policies in insurance and finance often lead to stochastic dynamics with discontinuous yet piecewise Lipschitz drift coefficients. The jump-diffusion surplus model presented in Section
2
provides a concrete illustration of this phenomenon. Such models fall outside the reach of classical stochastic maximum principle results, motivating the theoretical framework developed in this paper.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows.
Section
2
introduces a motivating example and formulates the general control problem.
It also presents regularity results for the jump-diffusion SDE with piecewise Lipschitz drift.
Both necessary and sufficient conditions for optimality are established in Section
3
.
Finally, Section
4
applies the theoretical results to an insurance model, analysing the optimal premium adjustment and reserve management policies for an insurer operating under jump risk.

2.
A motivating example and preliminary results

2.1.
Motivating example and problem formulation

Insurance companies routinely manage large portfolios of liabilities whose values fluctuate due to random claim arrivals and payments. Managing the surplus (the difference between assets and liabilities) requires balancing premium income, capital reserves, and regulatory constraints. This setting naturally leads to a stochastic control problem where the insurer seeks an optimal premium adjustment or reserve policy to maintain financial stability while minimizing risk exposure.

2.1.1.
Motivating example

We consider an insurance company whose liability process (or payment function) at time
t
is denoted by
L_{t}
, representing the total amount of insurance claims minus the premiums received over the interval
[0,t]
. We model the liability using a classical compound Poisson risk model, possibly perturbed by diffusion or jump-diffusion terms. Specifically, the liability of the company at time
t
is described by the following SDE:

-\mathrm{d}L_{t}=(b_{t}+\alpha_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma_{t}\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}+\mathrm{d}\big(\sum_{k=1}^{N_{t}}Z_{k}\big),\quad t\geq 0,\quad L_{0}=l,

(2.1)

where
b_{t}\geq 0
is the liability rate representing the expected liability (or gain) per unit time due to premium loading,
\alpha_{t}
is the premium rate (or premium policy) which acts as the control variable, and
\sigma_{t}>0
is the volatility measuring liability risk. We assume that the insurer is not allowed to invest in risky assets due to regulatory restrictions.

At the initial time
t=0
, the insurer deposits an amount
X_{0}
to cover potential future claims exceeding premiums. Let
X_{t}
denote the insurer’s cash balance at time
t
, which is composed of the initial capital minus net outflows up to time
t
, all accumulated at the compound interest rate. This can be written as

X_{t}=e^{\Delta_{t}}\big(X_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\Delta(s)}\,\mathrm{d}L_{s}\big),\qquad X_{0}=x,

(2.2)

where
\Delta_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\delta_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s
and
x\geq 0
represents the initial reserve. Applying Itô’s formula, the cash balance
X_{t}
satisfies the following SDE:

-\mathrm{d}X_{t}=(\delta_{t}X_{t}+b_{t}+\alpha_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma_{t}\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}+\mathrm{d}\big(\sum_{k=1}^{N_{t}}Z_{k}\big),\qquad t\in[0,T],\quad X_{0}=x,

(2.3)

where
\alpha_{t}
is the control variable. Further, assume that when the surplus exceeds a positive threshold
H
, the insurer may withdraw a fixed amount (dividend or fee), and when the surplus falls below
-H
, the insurer injects a fixed amount (or reduces net outflows). Incorporating this mechanism, the SDE (
2.3
) becomes:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\big(\delta_{t}X_{t}+b_{t}+\alpha_{t}-\beta\,\mathrm{sgn}(X_{t})\mathbf{1}_{\{|X_{t}|>H\}}\big)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma_{t}\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}+\mathrm{d}\big(\sum_{k=1}^{N_{t}}Z_{k}\big),\quad t\in[0,T],\\
\quad X_{0}=x,\end{cases}

where
\beta>0
represents the magnitude of the fixed drain or injection. The above model maintains the surplus within a target corridor, preventing large oscillations, while also accounting for emergency capital infusion or premium adjustments to avoid ruin.
The above equation can be rewritten as:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X^{\alpha}_{t}=

\displaystyle-\big\{\delta_{t}X^{\alpha}_{t}+b_{t}+\alpha_{t}-\beta\,\mathrm{sgn}(X^{\alpha}_{t})\mathbf{1}_{\{|X^{\alpha}_{t}|>H\}}\big\}\mathrm{d}t-\sigma_{t}\mathrm{d}B_{t}-\int_{\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}}zN(\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}t).

(2.4)

We assume that the fund manager’s objective is to determine the optimal rate
\alpha
that minimises the second moment of the fund value. In other words, the goal is to find
\alpha
that keeps
X
as close as possible to zero. More formally, the aim is to identify
\hat{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}
such that

\mathbb{E}\big[\big(X^{\hat{\alpha}}_{T}\big)^{2}\big]=\inf_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}\big[(X^{\alpha}_{T})^{2}\big],

(2.5)

where
\hat{\alpha}
is an optimal control (if it exists).

Although the drift term in equation (
2.4
) is discontinuous at
H
, it can still be seen as a piecewise Lipschitz function. However, the problem of optimal control for jump-diffusion with piecewise Lipschitz coefficients lies outside the scope of existing literature on stochastic optimal control.

2.1.2.
General Problem Formulation

Motivated by the above example, we consider the general controlled jump–diffusion system

X^{\alpha}_{t}=x+\int_{0}^{t}b(X^{\alpha}_{u},\alpha_{u})\mathrm{d}u+\sigma B_{t}+\int_{\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}}\gamma(z)N(\mathrm{d}z,t),

(2.6)

where
b
is a deterministic function and
\sigma
is a constant vector,
B
and
N
are
d
-dimensional Brownian motion and Poisson random measure on a probability space
(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},P)
equipped with the completed filtration
({\mathcal{F}}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
. The control variable
\alpha:[0,T]\times\Omega\mapsto\mathbb{A}\subset\mathbb{R}
is
\mathcal{B}([0,T])\otimes\mathcal{F}
-measurable, and
({\mathcal{F}}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
-adapted.
\mathbb{A}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is a closed convex subset of
\mathbb{R}

The criterion to be optimised over the set of admissible controls
\mathcal{A}
(see Definition
2.2
) has by the following form.

\displaystyle J(\alpha):=\mathbb{E}\big[\int_{0}^{T}f(s,X^{\alpha}_{s},\alpha_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s+g(X^{\alpha}_{T})\big],

(2.7)

where
f
and
g
are function satisfying some conditions.
The optimal control problem is the following

Problem 2.1
.

Find
\hat{\alpha}\in\AA
such that

\displaystyle J(\hat{\alpha})=\sup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}J(\alpha),

(2.8)

.

Definition 2.2
.

The set of admissible controls given in Problem
2.1
is defined as:

\mathcal{A}:=\big\{\alpha:[0,T]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{A},\text{ progressive, \eqref{eqconpb1}}\text{ has a unique strong solution and }\\
\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\alpha(t)|^{4}<M\text{ for some }M>0\big\}.

The following definition corresponds to
[
14
, Definition 2.1]
.

Definition 2.3

(piecewise Lipschitz function)
.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be an interval. A function
f:I\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be
piecewise Lipschitz
if there exist finitely many points
\xi_{1}<\cdots<\xi_{m}\in I
such that
f
is Lipschitz continuous on each of the intervals
(-\infty,\xi_{1})\cap I,\,\,(\xi_{m},\infty)\cap I
and
(\xi_{k},\xi_{k+1}),\quad k=1,\dots,m-1.

We assume the following conditions on
b,f
and
g
.

Assumption 2.4
.

b(x,a)=b_{1}(x,a)+b_{2}(x)+b_{3}(x),

where

•

b_{1}
is globally bounded and
\mathcal{C}^{1}
in its first and second variables, with uniformly bounded derivatives

•

b_{2}
is uniformly bounded and piecewise Lipschitz with
k\in\mathbb{N}
discontinuities in the points
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k}\in\mathbb{R}

•

b_{3}
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
with bounded derivative and satisfies a linear growth condition.

•

f
and
g
are continuously differentiable in their arguments;

•

there exists
C>0
such that

|f(t,x,a)|+|\partial_{x}f(t,x,a)|+|\partial_{a}f(t,x,a)|\leq C(1+|x|^{2}+|a|^{2}),\quad\text{for all }(t,x,a)

and

|g(x)|+|\partial_{x}g(x)|\leq C(1+|x|^{2}).

Note that the controlled SDE in the motivating example,

\mathrm{d}X_{t}^{\alpha}=\big(\delta_{t}X_{t}^{\alpha}+b_{t}+\alpha_{t}-\beta\,\mathrm{sgn}(X_{t}^{\alpha})\mathbf{1}_{\{|X_{t}^{\alpha}|>H\}}\big)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma_{t}\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}+\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}}z\,\tilde{N}(\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}t),

(2.9)

is a particular case of (
2.6
).
In the subsequent section, we provide results on existence, uniqueness, and regularity for the state equation (
2.6
), laying the foundation for establishing Pontryagin-type necessary and sufficient conditions under piecewise Lipschitz drift coefficients.

The main novelty of this work lies in the analysis of stochastic optimal control problems driven by jump–diffusion dynamics with
piecewise Lipschitz
drift coefficients.
Such drift structures naturally arise in practical financial and insurance models involving threshold-type mechanisms, such as dividend distribution, capital injection, or premium adjustment rules.
We develop new sufficient and necessary Pontryagin-type maximum principles under these weaker regularity assumptions, thereby extending the applicability of stochastic control theory to a broader class of discontinuous financial systems.

Proof of the Main results

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Let
\hat{\alpha}
be an optimal control and fix
n\geq 1
.
Under the linear growth assumptions on
f
and
g
, the functional
J_{n}
is bounded from above.
By Lemma
3.4
,
J_{n}
is continuous on
(\mathcal{A},d)
and there exists
\varepsilon_{n}
such that

J(\hat{\alpha})-J_{n}(\hat{\alpha})\leq\varepsilon_{n}\text{ and }J_{n}(\alpha)-J(\alpha)\leq\varepsilon_{n}\quad\text{for all }\alpha\in\mathcal{A}.

Hence,
J_{n}(\hat{\alpha})\leq\inf_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}J_{n}(\alpha)+2\varepsilon_{n}
.
Therefore, by the Ekeland’s variational principle (see
[
6
]
), there exists a control
\hat{\alpha}^{n}\in\mathcal{A}
such that
d(\hat{\alpha},\hat{\alpha}^{n})\leq(2\varepsilon_{n})^{1/2}
and

J_{n}(\hat{\alpha}^{n})\leq J_{n}(\alpha)+(2\varepsilon_{n})^{1/2}d(\hat{\alpha}^{n},\alpha)\quad\text{for all}\quad\alpha\in\mathcal{A}.

Define the perturbed functional

J^{\varepsilon_{n}}_{n}(\alpha):=J_{n}(\alpha)+(2\varepsilon_{n})^{1/2}d(\hat{\alpha}^{n},\alpha),

so that the control
\hat{\alpha}^{n}
is optimal for the problem with performance functional
J^{\varepsilon_{n}}_{n}
.

Let
\beta\in\mathcal{A}
be an arbitrary and fix
\varepsilon>0
. Set
\eta=\eta_{n}:=\beta-\hat{\alpha}^{n}
. By the convexity of
\mathbb{A}
, we have
\hat{\alpha}^{n}+\varepsilon\eta\in\mathcal{A}
.
Thanks to the smoothness of
b_{n}
, the functional
J_{n}
is Gâteau differentiable, with derivative in the direction
\eta
given by

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}J_{n}(\hat{\alpha}^{n}+\varepsilon\eta)_{|_{\varepsilon=0}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\int_{0}^{T}\partial_{x}f(t,X^{n,\hat{\alpha}^{n}}_{t},\hat{\alpha}^{n}_{t})V^{n}_{t}+\partial_{\alpha}f(t,X^{n,\hat{\alpha}^{n}}_{t},\hat{\alpha}^{n}_{t})\eta_{t}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad+\partial_{x}g(X^{n,\hat{\alpha}^{n}}_{T})V^{n}_{T}\big],

where
V_{n}
solves the corresponding linear equation

\mathrm{d}V^{n}(t)=\partial_{x}b_{n}(t,X^{n,\alpha}_{t},\alpha_{t})V^{n}_{t}\mathrm{d}t+\partial_{\alpha}b_{n}(t,X^{n,\alpha}_{t},\alpha_{t})\eta_{t}\mathrm{d}t,\quad V^{n}(0)=0.

Conversely, by the triangle inequality, we obtain

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\big(d(\hat{\alpha}^{n},\alpha+\varepsilon\eta)-d(\hat{\alpha}^{n},\alpha)\big)\leq\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\big[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\eta_{t}|^{4}\big]^{1/4}.

Hence,
J^{\varepsilon_{n}}_{n}
is Gâteau differentiable and the optimality of
\hat{\alpha}^{n}
for
J^{\varepsilon_{n}}_{n}
implies that

\displaystyle 0\leq\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}J^{\varepsilon_{n}}_{n}(\hat{\alpha}^{n}+\varepsilon\eta)_{|_{\varepsilon=0}}=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}J_{n}(\hat{\alpha}^{n}+\varepsilon\eta)_{|_{\varepsilon=0}}+\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}(2\varepsilon_{n})^{1/2}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}d(\hat{\alpha}^{n},\hat{\alpha}^{n}+\varepsilon\eta)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\int_{0}^{T}\partial_{x}f\big(t,X^{n,\hat{\alpha}^{n}}_{t},\hat{\alpha}^{n}_{t}\big)V^{n}_{t}+\partial_{\alpha}f\big(t,X^{n,\hat{\alpha}^{n}}_{t},\hat{\alpha}^{n}_{t}\big)\eta_{t}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\partial_{x}g(X^{n,\hat{\alpha}^{n}}_{T})V^{n}_{T}\big]+\big(2\varepsilon_{n})^{1/2}(E[\sup_{t}|\eta_{t}|^{4}]\big)^{1/4}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\int_{0}^{T}\partial_{\alpha}H_{n}\big(t,X^{n,\hat{\alpha}}_{t},P^{n,\hat{\alpha}^{n}}_{t},\hat{\alpha}^{n}_{t}\big)\eta_{t}\mathrm{d}t\big]+C_{M}\varepsilon_{n}^{1/2},

for a constant
C_{M}>0
depending on the constant
M
(as introduced in the definition of
\mathcal{A}
).
The inequality follows since
\hat{\alpha}^{n}\in\mathcal{A}
, and
H_{n}
denotes the Hamiltonian of the problem corresponding to the drift
b_{n}
given by

H_{n}(t,x,p,a):=f(t,x,a)+\{b_{n}(t,x,a)+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}\gamma(z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z)\}p

and
(P^{n,\hat{\alpha}^{n}},Z^{n,\hat{\alpha}^{n}},R^{n,\hat{\alpha}^{n}}(z))
the adjoint processes satisfying

\mathrm{d}P^{n,\hat{\alpha}^{n}}_{t}=-\partial_{x}H_{n}(t,X^{n,\hat{\alpha}^{n}}_{t},P^{n,\hat{\alpha}^{n}}_{t},\hat{\alpha}^{n}_{t})\mathrm{d}t+Z^{n,\hat{\alpha}^{n}}_{t}\mathrm{d}B_{t}+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}R_{t}^{n,\hat{\alpha}^{n}}(z)\tilde{N}(\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}t).

By standard arguments, we have

C_{M}\varepsilon_{n}^{1/2}+\partial_{\alpha}H_{n}(t,X^{n,\hat{\alpha}^{n}}_{t},P^{n,\hat{\alpha}^{n}}_{t},\hat{\alpha}^{n}_{t})\cdot(\beta-\hat{\alpha}^{n}_{t}))\geq 0\quad\mathbb{P}\otimes\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{-a.s}.

Since
b_{2,n}
does not depend on
\alpha
, this reduces to

C_{M}\varepsilon_{n}^{1/2}+\big\{\partial_{\alpha}f(t,X^{n,\hat{\alpha}^{n}}_{t},\hat{\alpha}^{n}_{t})+\partial_{\alpha}b_{1}\big(t,X^{n,\hat{\alpha}^{n}}_{t},\hat{\alpha}^{n}_{t}\big)P^{n,\hat{\alpha}^{n}}_{t}\big\}\cdot(\beta-\hat{\alpha}^{n}_{t})\geq 0\quad\mathbb{P}\otimes\mathrm{d}t\text{-a.s.}

Taking the limit as
n\rightarrow\infty
, Lemmas
3.3
and
3.5
ensure that
X^{n,\hat{\alpha}^{n}}_{t}\to X_{t}^{\hat{\alpha}}
and
P^{n,\hat{\alpha}^{n}}_{t}\to P^{\hat{\alpha}}_{t}

\mathbb{P}
-a.s. for all
t\in[0,T]
.
Thus, using
\hat{\alpha}^{n}\to\alpha
, we obtain

\big\{\partial_{\alpha}f(t,X^{\hat{\alpha}}_{t},\hat{\alpha}_{t})+\partial_{\alpha}b_{1}\big(t,X^{\hat{\alpha}}_{t},\hat{\alpha}_{t}\big)P^{\hat{\alpha}}_{t}\big\}\cdot(\beta-\hat{\alpha}_{t})\geq 0\quad\mathbb{P}\otimes\mathrm{d}t\text{-a.s.}

This shows (
3.1
), which concludes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem

.

Let
\hat{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}
satisfy (

) and
\alpha^{\prime}
an arbitrary element of

.
We would like to show that

Let

By definition, we have

\displaystyle J_{n}(\hat{\alpha})-J_{n}(\alpha^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\big[\partial_{x}g(X^{n,\hat{\alpha}}_{T})\big\{X^{\hat{\alpha}}_{T}-X^{n,\alpha^{\prime}}_{T}\big\}+\int_{0}^{T}\big\{b_{n}(u,X^{n,\alpha^{\prime}}_{u},\alpha^{\prime}_{u})-b_{n}(u,X_{u}^{n,\hat{\alpha}},\hat{\alpha}_{u})\big\}P^{n,\hat{\alpha}}_{u}\,\mathrm{d}u

where we used the definition of

and the fact that

is concave.
Since

satisfies

it follows by the martingale representation and the Itô’s formula that there is a square integrable progressive process
(P^{\hat{\alpha}}_{n},Z^{\hat{\alpha}}_{n},R_{u}^{n,\hat{\alpha}})
such that

satisfies the equation

\displaystyle P^{n,\hat{\alpha}}_{t}=

\displaystyle\partial_{x}g(X^{n,\hat{\alpha}}_{T})+\int_{t}^{T}\partial_{x}H_{n}(u,X^{n,\hat{\alpha}}_{u},P_{u}^{n,\hat{\alpha}},\hat{\alpha}_{u})\,\mathrm{d}u-\int_{t}^{T}Z_{u}^{n,\hat{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}W_{u}

Since
b_{n}
is smooth, so is

.
Therefore, by the Itô’s formula, we have

\displaystyle P^{n,\hat{\alpha}}_{T}\big\{X_{T}^{n,\hat{\alpha}}-X^{n,\alpha^{\prime}}_{T}\big\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}P^{n,\hat{\alpha}}_{u}\big\{b_{n}(u,X^{n,\hat{\alpha}}_{u},\hat{\alpha}_{u})-b_{n}(u,X^{n,\alpha^{\prime}}_{u},\alpha^{\prime}_{u})\big\}\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{T}\big\{X^{n,\hat{\alpha}}_{u}-X^{n,\alpha^{\prime}}_{u}\big\}\partial_{x}H_{n}(u,X^{n,\hat{\alpha}}_{u},P_{u}^{n,\hat{\alpha}},\hat{\alpha}_{u})\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\big\{X^{n,\hat{\alpha}}_{u}-X^{n,\alpha^{\prime}}_{u}\big\}Z^{n,\hat{\alpha}}_{u}\,\mathrm{d}W_{u}+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}\big\{X^{n,\hat{\alpha}}_{u}-X^{n,\alpha^{\prime}}_{u}\big\}R_{u}^{n,\hat{\alpha}}(z)\tilde{N}(\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}t).

\displaystyle J_{n}(\hat{\alpha})-J_{n}(\alpha^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\big[-\int_{0}^{T}\big\{X^{n,\hat{\alpha}}_{u}-X^{n,\alpha^{\prime}}_{u}\big\}\partial_{x}H_{n}(u,X^{n,\hat{\alpha}}_{u},P_{u}^{n,\hat{\alpha}},\hat{\alpha}_{u})\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\big[\int_{0}^{T}\partial_{\alpha}H_{n}(u,X_{u}^{n,\hat{\alpha}},P_{u}^{n,\hat{\alpha}},\hat{\alpha}_{u})\cdot(\hat{\alpha}_{u}-\alpha^{\prime}_{u})\,\mathrm{d}u\big],

. Substituting this into the expression

\displaystyle J(\hat{\alpha})-J(\alpha^{\prime})

\displaystyle=J(\hat{\alpha})-J_{n}(\hat{\alpha})+J_{n}(\hat{\alpha})-J_{n}(\alpha^{\prime})+J_{n}(\alpha^{\prime})-J(\alpha^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq J(\hat{\alpha})-J_{n}(\hat{\alpha})+\mathbb{E}\big[\int_{0}^{T}\partial_{\alpha}H_{n}(u,X_{u}^{n,\hat{\alpha}},P_{u}^{n,\hat{\alpha}},\hat{\alpha}_{u})\cdot(\hat{\alpha}_{u}-\alpha^{\prime}_{u})\,\mathrm{d}u\big]

\displaystyle\quad+J_{n}(\alpha^{\prime})-J(\alpha^{\prime}).

Since
b_{2,n}
is independent of
\alpha
, we have

Passing to the limit as

3.3
,
3.4
and
3.5
that

\displaystyle J(\hat{\alpha})-J(\alpha^{\prime})\geq\mathbb{E}\big[\int_{0}^{T}\partial_{\alpha}H(u,X^{\hat{\alpha}}_{u},P^{\hat{\alpha}}_{u},\hat{\alpha}_{u})\cdot(\hat{\alpha}_{u}-\alpha^{\prime}_{u})\,\mathrm{d}u\big].

Since
\hat{\alpha}
satisfies (
3.3
), we conclude that
J(\hat{\alpha})\geq J(\alpha^{\prime})
.

5.
Concluding remarks

In this paper, we have established a necessary and sufficient stochastic maximum principle for jump–diffusion systems with a piecewise Lipschitz drift coefficient. The analysis begins with an explicit representation of the first variation process, derived by combining Itô’s formula for jump–diffusions with standard stochastic integral estimates—namely the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy and Kunita inequalities—and the fact that the law of the SDE solution admits a density with respect to the Lebesgue measure.

To handle the discontinuity in the drift, we constructed a sequence of approximating control problems with smooth coefficients. Ekeland’s variational principle was then applied to obtain a sequence of adjoint processes, allowing us to derive the maximum principle in the limit. The framework accommodates drift functions of the form

b(x,a)=b_{1}(x,a)+b_{2}(x)+b_{3}(x),

where the discontinuity arises from
b_{2}
.

The present setting, however, does not encompass the case in which the insurer seeks to optimise a parameter
\beta>0
representing the magnitude of a fixed drain or injection in the model (
2.4
)–(
2.5
). In that context, the drift takes the form

b(x,a)=b_{1}(x)+b_{2}(x)b_{3}(a),

where
b_{2}
is piecewise Lipschitz (and expressible as the difference of two monotone functions), and
b_{1}
is continuously differentiable with bounded derivative and linear growth.

(A.13)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\big|\mathcal{J}_{1,n}\big|^{p}\big]\leq

\displaystyle C(p,M,T)\mathbb{E}\big[\exp\big\{10pM(1+|X_{t}^{n,x}|)\big\}\big]^{1/5}

\displaystyle\times\exp\big\{\frac{t}{5}\int_{\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}}\big(e^{10pM|\gamma(z)|}-1|\big)\nu(\mathrm{d}z)\big\}.

Recall the following from (

Lemma B.1

(An approximation result)
.

Let
G:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a
C^{1}
-diffeomorphism given by (
A.1
) and (
A.2
).
Let
\bar{b}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be given as in (
A.5
), then
\bar{b}
is Lipschitz. Define
b:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
(a.e.) by

b(x):=\frac{\bar{b}(G(x))-\frac{1}{2}G^{\prime\prime}(x)}{G^{\prime}(x)}\qquad\text{for a.e.\ }x\in\mathbb{R}.

(B.1)

Then there exists a sequence
(\bar{b}_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
such that, for every

r\in[1,\infty)
,

\bar{b}_{n}\to\bar{b}\quad\text{in }W^{1,r}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R})\qquad\text{(in particular, }\bar{b}_{n}^{\prime}\to\bar{b}^{\prime}\text{ in }L^{r}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R})\text{)}.

(B.2)

Define, for each
n
and a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

b_{n}(x):=\frac{\bar{b}_{n}(G(x))-\frac{1}{2}G^{\prime\prime}(x)}{G^{\prime}(x)}.

(B.3)

Then for every
r\in[1,\infty)
:

(i)

\bar{b}_{n}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and
b_{n}\in W^{1,r}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
.

(ii)

\bar{b}_{n}\to\bar{b}
Lebesgue-a.e. on
\mathbb{R}
, and
b_{n}\to b
Lebesgue-a.e. on
\mathbb{R}
.

(iii)

b_{n}\to b
in
W^{1,r}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
, i.e.

b_{n}\to b\text{ in }L^{r}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R})\quad\text{and}\quad b_{n}^{\prime}\to b^{\prime}\text{ in }L^{r}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}),

where derivatives are understood as weak derivatives.

Proof.

The Construction of
\bar{b}_{n}
and convergence in
W^{1,r}_{\text{loc}}
follows by standard mollification since
\bar{b}
is Lipschitz. We omit the proof.

From (
B.3
) and (
B.1
),

b_{n}(x)-b(x)=\frac{\bar{b}_{n}(G(x))-\bar{b}(G(x))}{G^{\prime}(x)}\qquad\text{for a.e.\ }x.

Since
\bar{b}_{n}\to\bar{b}
a.e. and
G
is continuous,
\bar{b}_{n}(G(x))\to\bar{b}(G(x))
for a.e.
x
,
and
G^{\prime}(x)\neq 0
, hence
b_{n}(x)\to b(x)
a.e.

Now fix a compact interval
K=[-R,R]
and set
I:=G(K)
(compact).
By (
A.9
) and the change of variables
y=G(x)
(with
dy=G^{\prime}(x)\,dx
),
for any
h\in L^{r}(I)
,

\|h\circ G\|_{L^{r}(K)}^{r}=\int_{K}|h(G(x))|^{r}\,dx=\int_{I}|h(y)|^{r}\,\frac{1}{G^{\prime}(G^{-1}(y))}\,dy\leq\underline{g}^{-1}\|h\|_{L^{r}(I)}^{r}.

(B.4)

Applying (
B.4
) to
h=\bar{b}_{n}-\bar{b}
gives

\|(\bar{b}_{n}-\bar{b})\circ G\|_{L^{r}(K)}\leq C_{K}\,\|\bar{b}_{n}-\bar{b}\|_{L^{r}(I)}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0,

hence
b_{n}\to b
in
L^{r}(K)
(using also
\|(G^{\prime})^{-1}\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}<\infty
).

To control derivatives, note that
\bar{b}_{n}\in C^{\infty}
and
G\in C^{1}
, so

(\bar{b}_{n}\circ G)^{\prime}=(\bar{b}_{n}^{\prime}\circ G)\,G^{\prime}
a.e. on
K
.
Since
G^{\prime\prime}\in L^{\infty}
, we have
G^{\prime}\in W^{1,\infty}
and
\frac{1}{G^{\prime}}\in W^{1,\infty}
,
so the quotient rule holds in
W^{1,r}
and
b_{n}\in W^{1,r}(K)
.
A direct computation in the sense of weak derivatives yields, a.e. on
K
,

b_{n}^{\prime}(x)-b^{\prime}(x)=(\bar{b}_{n}^{\prime}-\bar{b}^{\prime})\big(G(x)\big)-\frac{G^{\prime\prime}(x)}{(G^{\prime}(x))^{2}}\,\big(\bar{b}_{n}-\bar{b}\big)\big(G(x)\big).

(B.5)

Consequently,

\|b_{n}^{\prime}-b^{\prime}\|_{L^{r}(K)}\leq\|(\bar{b}_{n}^{\prime}-\bar{b}^{\prime})\circ G\|_{L^{r}(K)}+\Big\|\frac{G^{\prime\prime}}{(G^{\prime})^{2}}\Big\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}\,\|(\bar{b}_{n}-\bar{b})\circ G\|_{L^{r}(K)}.

Using (
B.4
) with
h=\bar{b}_{n}^{\prime}-\bar{b}^{\prime}
and (
B.2
), both terms
tend to
0
, proving
b_{n}^{\prime}\to b^{\prime}
in
L^{r}(K)
.
Since
K
is arbitrary,
b_{n}\to b
in
W^{1,r}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
.
∎

Theorem B.2
.

Let
Y^{x}
be the solution of the following SDE

\mathrm{d}Y^{x}_{t}=b(Y^{x}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(Y^{x}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}+\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}}\gamma(Y^{x}_{t-},z)\,N(\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}t),\qquad Y^{x}_{0}=x,

where
b,\sigma,\gamma
are globally Lipschitz with linear growth and
\sigma
is uniformly elliptic.
Assume the Poisson random measure has finite activity

\lambda:=\nu(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})<\infty.

Furthermore, assume for each
t>0,Y_{t}^{x}
admits a density

\rho_{t}^{x}(\cdot)
. Then for every
T>0
there exists a constant
C_{T}\geq 0
such that for all
t\in(0,T]
,

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\ \sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\ \rho_{t}^{x}(y)\ \leq\ \frac{C_{T}}{\sqrt{t}}.

(B.6)

Proof.

Consider first the diffusion SDE without jumps

\mathrm{d}Z_{t}=b(Z_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(Z_{t})\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}.

For every
T>0
there exists
A_{T}<\infty
such that its transition density
p(t,u,v)
satisfies (see for e.g.
[
9
,
13
,
20
]
)

\sup_{0<s\leq T}\ \sup_{u,v\in\mathbb{R}}\ p(s,u,v)\ \leq\ \frac{A_{T}}{\sqrt{s}}.

(B.7)

Fix
T>0
and
t\in(0,T]
. Let
N_{t}
be the number of jumps up to time
t
, and let

0<\tau_{1}<\cdots<\tau_{N_{t}}\leq t
be the ordered jump times (with
T_{0}:=0
).
Define the last jump time before
t
by

S_{t}:=\tau_{N_{t}}\quad\text{(with the convention $S_{t}=0$ on $\{N_{t}=0\}$)},\qquad\Delta_{t}:=t-S_{t}\in(0,t].

By definition of
S_{t}
, there are no jumps on
(S_{t},t]
, hence on this interval
Y^{x}
evolves
as the diffusion with coefficients
(b,\sigma)
.
By the strong Markov property at time
S_{t}
(and independence of Brownian increments),
conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{S_{t}}
the terminal value
Y_{t}^{x}
has the same law as the diffusion
started from
Y_{S_{t}}^{x}
run for time
\Delta_{t}
. Therefore its conditional density is

\rho_{t}^{x\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{S_{t}}}(y)=p(\Delta_{t},Y_{S_{t}}^{x},y).

Using (
B.7
) we obtain the conditional
L^{\infty}
bound

\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\rho_{t}^{x\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{S_{t}}}(y)\leq\sup_{u,v\in\mathbb{R}}p(\Delta_{t},u,v)\leq\frac{A_{T}}{\sqrt{\Delta_{t}}}.

Taking expectations yields, for all
x,y
,

\rho_{t}^{x}(y)=\mathbb{E}\Big[\rho_{t}^{x\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{S_{t}}}(y)\Big]\leq A_{T}\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\Delta_{t}^{-1/2}\Big].

(B.8)

It remains to estimate
\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{t}^{-1/2}]
uniformly over
t\in(0,T]
.
Decompose with respect to
N_{t}
. If
N_{t}=0
, then
\Delta_{t}=t
.
If
N_{t}=n\geq 1
, then
\Delta_{t}=t-\tau_{n}
, where
\tau_{n}
is the maximum of
n
i.i.d.
\mathrm{Unif}(0,t)

variables; equivalently
\tau_{n}/t\sim\mathrm{Beta}(n,1)
and

\mathbb{E}[(t-\tau_{n})^{-1/2}\mid N_{t}=n]=\frac{n}{\sqrt{t}}\int_{0}^{1}(1-u)^{-1/2}u^{n-1}\,du=\frac{n}{\sqrt{t}}\,B\!\left(n,\tfrac{1}{2}\right).

Hence

\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{t}^{-1/2}]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}\,\mathbb{P}(N_{t}=0)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(N_{t}=n)\,n\,B\!\left(n,\tfrac{1}{2}\right).

Using
nB(n,\tfrac{1}{2})=\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma(n+1)/\Gamma(n+1/2)\leq C\sqrt{n}
for
n\geq 1
, we obtain

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(N_{t}=n)\,n\,B\!\left(n,\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\leq C\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(N_{t}=n)\sqrt{n}=C\,\mathbb{E}[\sqrt{N_{t}}].

Since
N_{t}\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda t)
and
\sqrt{\cdot}
is concave, Jensen gives

\mathbb{E}[\sqrt{N_{t}}]\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[N_{t}]}=\sqrt{\lambda t}\leq\sqrt{\lambda T}.

Therefore there exists
C_{T}^{\prime}<\infty
such that

\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{t}^{-1/2}]\leq\frac{C_{T}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{t}},\qquad t\in(0,T].

Plugging this into (
B.8
) yields

\rho_{t}^{x}(y)\leq A_{T}\,\frac{C_{T}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{t}}=:\frac{C_{T}}{\sqrt{t}},\qquad t\in(0,T],\ x,y\in\mathbb{R},

and taking suprema in
(x,y)
completes the proof.
∎

Lemma B.3

(A convergence result)
.

Assume:

(L1)

b_{n}\to b
Lebesgue-a.e. on
\mathbb{R}
and there exists
K>0
such that

|b_{n}(x)|+|b(x)|\leq K(1+|x|)\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{R},\ \forall n.

(L2)

For every
t\in(0,T]
,
Y_{t}^{n,x}\rightarrow Y_{t}^{x}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
as
n\to\infty
.

(L3)

Uniform
p
moments:

\sup_{n}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathbb{E}[|Y_{t}^{n,x}|^{p}]<\infty.

(L4)

For every
t\in(0,T]
,
Y_{t}^{x}
admits a density
\rho_{t}^{x}
satisfying

\rho_{t}^{x}(y)\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{t}}\qquad\text{for a.e.\ }y\in\mathbb{R},

(B.9)

with a constant
C
independent of
y
and
t
.

Then

\lim_{n}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\Big[\,|b_{n}(Y_{t}^{n,x})-b(Y_{t}^{n,x})|^{p}\,\Big]\,\mathrm{d}t=0.

Proof.

Define

I_{n}(t):=\mathbb{E}\big[|b_{n}(Y_{t}^{n,x})-b(Y_{t}^{n,x})|^{p}\big],\qquad t\in[0,T].

By the uniform linear growth of
b_{n}
and

C_{1}>0
such that

|b_{n}(y)-b(u)|^{p}\leq C_{p}(1+|y|^{p})\qquad\text{ for all }u\in\mathbb{R},\ \text{ for all }n.

Using the uniform
p

Y^{n,x}
, we obtain

I_{n}(t)\leq C_{1}\Big(1+\sup_{n,t}\mathbb{E}|Y_{t}^{n,x}|^{p}\Big)=:M_{p,T}<\infty,\quad\text{ for all }u\in\mathbb{R},\ \text{ for all }n.

(B.10)

Now, fix
\varepsilon>0
. Choose
\theta\in(0,T]
such that

\int_{0}^{\delta}M\mathrm{d}t\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

(B.11)

Then

\int_{0}^{T}I_{n}(t)\,dt=\int_{0}^{\theta}I_{n}(t)\mathrm{d}t+\int_{\theta}^{T}I_{n}(t)\mathrm{d}t\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\int_{\theta}^{T}I_{n}(t)\mathrm{d}t.

(B.12)

In addition, for
R>0

\displaystyle\sup_{n}\mathbb{E}\big[(1+|Y_{t}^{n,x}|^{p})\mathbf{1}_{\{|Y_{t}^{n,x}|>R\}}\big]\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{n}\mathbb{E}\big[(1+|Y_{t}^{n,x}|^{p})^{2}\big]^{1/2}\sup_{n}\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbf{1}_{\{|Y_{t}^{n,x}|>R\}}\big]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle M_{p,T}\sup_{n}\frac{(\mathbb{E}[|Y^{n,x}_{s}|^{p}])^{1/2}}{R^{p/2}}\leq M_{p,T}\frac{C}{R^{p/2}}

Next, fix
t\in[\theta,T]
and
\eta>0
.
Choose
R>0
such that

\sup_{n}\mathbb{E}\big[(1+|Y_{t}^{n,x}|^{p})\mathbf{1}_{\{|Y_{t}^{n,x}|>R\}}\big]\leq\eta.

(B.13)

Using the linear growth bound,

|b_{n}(y)-b(y)|^{2}\leq C_{1}(1+|y|^{2}),\text{ for all }y\in\mathbb{R}\text{ and }n

we obtain

\displaystyle I_{n}(t)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\!\left[|b_{n}-b|^{2}(Y_{t}^{n,x})\mathbf{1}_{\{|Y_{t}^{n,x}|\leq R\}}\right]+C_{1}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[(1+|Y_{t}^{n,x}|^{2})\mathbf{1}_{\{|Y_{t}^{n,x}|>R\}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\!\left[|b_{n}-b|^{2}(Y_{t}^{n,x})\mathbf{1}_{\{|Y_{t}^{n,x}|\leq R\}}\right]+C_{1}\eta.

(B.14)

Thanks to Egorov’s theorem applied on
[-R,R]
, for every
\delta_{1}>0
, there exists a measurable
F\subset[-R,R]
with
\lambda(F)<\delta_{1}
such that
|\bar{b}_{n}^{\prime}(y)-\bar{b}^{\prime}(y)|
converges uniformly to
0
on the set
F^{C}_{R}=[-R,R]\backslash F
. Note that
F
can be chosen closed since
\lambda
is regular.

Hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[|b_{n}-b|^{2}(Y_{t}^{n,x})\mathbf{1}_{\{|Y_{t}^{n,x}|\leq R\}}\big]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[|b_{n}-b|^{2}(Y_{t}^{n,x})\mathbf{1}_{\{Y_{t}^{n,x}\in F^{C}_{R}\}}\big]+\mathbb{E}\big[|b_{n}-b|^{2}(Y_{t}^{n,x})\mathbf{1}_{\{Y_{t}^{n,x}\in F\}}\big]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{F^{c}_{R}}|b_{n}(y)-b(y)|^{2}\rho^{n}_{t}(y)\mathrm{d}y+C_{1}\mathbb{E}\big[(1+|Y_{t}^{n,x}|^{2})\mathbf{1}_{\{Y_{t}^{n,x}\in F\}}\big]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{u\in F^{c}_{R}}|b_{n}(u)-b(u)|^{2}+C_{1}\mathbb{E}\big[(1+R^{p})\mathbf{1}_{\{Y_{t}^{n,x}\in F\}}\big],

(B.15)

where in the last inequality we have use the facts that
\rho_{s}^{n}(y)
is a density i.e.

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho_{s}^{n}(y)\mathrm{d}y=1

and
F\subset[-R,R]
.

Recall that
Y^{n,x}_{s}
converges to
Y^{x}_{s}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
Therefore
Y^{n,x}_{s}
converges to
Y^{x}_{s}
in probability and thus in distribution and since
F
may be chosen closed, Portmanteau-Alexandrov Theorem gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[(1+R^{p})\mathbf{1}_{\{Y_{t}^{n,x}\in F\}}\big]=

\displaystyle(1+R^{p})\mathbb{P}(Y_{t}^{n,x}\in F)\leq(1+R^{p})\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(Y_{t}^{n,x}\in F)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1+R^{p})\mathbb{P}(Y_{t}^{x}\in F)=(1+R^{p})\int_{F}\rho_{t}^{x}(y)\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{C(1+R^{p})}{\sqrt{\theta}}\lambda(F)\leq\frac{C(1+R^{p})}{\sqrt{\theta}}\delta_{1}.

(B.16)

where we have used the density bound (
B.9
) in the last equality. Combining (
B.14
)–(
B
) and letting

n\to\infty
yields

\limsup_{n\to\infty}I_{n}(t)\leq C_{1}\eta+C_{1}(1+R^{2})\frac{C}{\sqrt{\theta}}\delta_{1}.

First choose
\delta_{1}>0
such that

C_{1}(1+R^{2})\frac{C}{\sqrt{\theta}}\delta_{1}\leq\eta
, and then let
\eta\downarrow 0
.
This proves

I_{n}(t)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0\qquad\text{for every }t\in[\theta,T].

(B.17)

By (
B.10
),
0\leq I_{n}(t)\leq M
for all
t\in[\theta,T]
.
Since
I_{n}(t)\to 0
pointwise on
[\theta,T]
, the dominated convergence theorem yields

\int_{\theta}^{T}I_{n}(t)\mathrm{d}t\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

Combining with (
B.12
) proves the claim.
