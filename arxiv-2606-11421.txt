Abstract
We prove that optimally weighted second-order least squares (SLS) and the degree-two generalized polynomial maximization method (PMM) are the same population estimating equation for linear regression with conditionally homoskedastic non-Gaussian errors: they choose the same optimal linear combination of the first two centered residual moments, solve one population normal system, share one influence function, and attain the common asymptotic variance c 2 ​ g 2 / N c_{2}g_{2}/N —the ordinary-least-squares slope-variance factor c 2 c_{2} scaled by the PMM variance-reduction coefficient g 2 = 1 − γ 3 2 / ( 2 + γ 4 ) g_{2}=1-\gamma_{3}^{2}/(2+\gamma_{4}) (with γ 3 , γ 4 \gamma_{3},\gamma_{4} the error skewness and excess kurtosis). Feasible plug-in implementations are therefore first-order equivalent, with only higher-order finite-sample differences. The identity is sharp: under heteroskedasticity the unconditional PMM body and the conditional SLS weighting separate, costing efficiency for symmetric errors and consistency for asymmetric errors. Beyond degree two, PMM holds an efficiency reserve that SLS cannot reach within its second-moment span. For symmetric platykurtic errors SLS collapses to ordinary least squares for the slope, while degree-three PMM exploits kurtosis information outside the SLS moment span through a closed-form coefficient g 3 g_{3} ; for canonical asymmetric laws this reserve is 30 30 – 50 % 50\% within the degree-three polynomial moment class. The Lean 4 development machine-checks the degree-specific algebraic core—the closed forms for g 2 g_{2} and g 3 g_{3} , the g 2 ≤ 1 g_{2}\leq 1 result, the design cancellations, and the symmetric collapse—while the general monotonicity g S + 1 ≤ g S ≤ 1 g_{S+1}\leq g_{S}\leq 1 is proved analytically by nesting. A Monte Carlo study illustrates the equivalence, the reserve, and the heteroskedastic boundary at finite samples.

1
Introduction

Efficient estimation of regression parameters under non-Gaussian errors occupies a productive middle ground between two extremes. At one pole, fully parametric maximum likelihood attains the Cramér–Rao bound but requires the error density to be known up to its parameters and is fragile under misspecification. At the other pole, ordinary least squares (OLS) asks nothing of the error law beyond a finite second moment, and pays for that generality by discarding every higher-order feature of the data: under a skewed or heavy-tailed error it is consistent but inefficient. A family of
semiparametric
methods sits between these poles, trading a complete distributional model for the more modest commitment of a finite collection of moment conditions. Within this family one finds Huber’s
M
-estimators
[
9
]
, which bound the influence of outliers through a fixed score function; Hansen’s generalized method of moments (GMM)
[
6
]
, which optimally weights a user-specified set of moment equations; the method of L-moments
[
8
]
, which reparameterizes distributions through linear combinations of order statistics; and the higher-order statistics program
[
12
]
, which marshals third- and fourth-order moments to characterize departures from normality. Each method asks how much efficiency one can recover from the data’s higher-order structure while remaining agnostic about its precise distributional form.

Two strands of this literature converge, by very different routes, on the same answer for the regression problem—the same efficient linear combination of the first two centered residual moments, and hence the same asymptotic slope variance—and it is their unrecognized coincidence that motivates this paper.

The first strand is the polynomial maximization method (PMM), developed by Kunchenko and the Cherkasy school
[
11
]
and brought to regression estimation in published work
[
18
,
19
,
17
]
. PMM lifts estimation out of the raw-signal domain into the space of moments: a parameter is estimated by maximizing a polynomial estimating function in powers of the residual,
\{\xi,\xi^{2},\dots,\xi^{S}\}
, with coefficients chosen to minimize the asymptotic variance. The optimal coefficients solve a linear normal system
F_{S}h=b
, in which
F_{S}
is the centered correlation matrix of the polynomial basis and
b
is the vector of derivatives of the basis means with respect to the parameter. The hallmark of the method is a closed-form variance-reduction coefficient. At degree two, the coefficient defined later in (
4

g_{2}

1.80

\mathrm{Gamma}(2,1)

The second strand is second-order least squares (SLS), introduced by Wang for measurement-error models
[

[

, and analyzed for efficiency by Kim and Ma

That the two methods agree so closely is not in dispute; what has been missing is an account of

The traditions nevertheless speak different dialects—one of stochastic polynomials and centered correlation matrices, the other of weighted residual vectors and conditional moment matrices—and the concrete identity between the Kunchenko degree-two body and the SLS weighting operator has not, to our knowledge, been derived as a theorem. Two questions follow immediately. Are PMM at degree two and optimally weighted SLS the same population estimating equation, or merely close in simulations? And if PMM is genuinely a wider construction, indexed by a polynomial degree
S

We answer both. In one sentence: degree-two PMM equals optimally weighted SLS under the homoskedastic baseline model, while higher-degree PMM gains efficiency in score directions that lie outside the second-order moment span SLS is built on. Our contributions are the following.

Background

This section fixes notation and recalls the two estimation principles the paper unifies. Throughout,
\xi
denotes a scalar observation whose distribution depends on a parameter
\theta
, and for the regression instances of Section
3
we write
y_{i}=\mu_{i}(\theta)+e_{i}
with
\mu_{i}(\theta)=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}x_{i}
and zero-mean errors

of variance

\gamma_{3}=\mu_{3}/\sigma^{3}

2.1
The polynomial maximization method

The polynomial maximization method (PMM) of
11
estimates
\theta
by projecting the score onto a finite stochastic polynomial rather than onto the exact likelihood. Given a system of linearly independent basis functions
\{\varphi_{1}(\xi),\dots,\varphi_{S}(\xi)\}
—most commonly the power basis
\varphi_{i}(\xi)=\xi^{i}
—one forms the degree-
S
stochastic polynomial

\eta_{S}(\xi;\theta)\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{S}h_{i}(\theta)\,\bigl\{\varphi_{i}(\xi)-\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\varphi_{i}(\xi)]\bigr\},

(1)

and chooses the coefficients
h_{i}
to maximize the rate at which the sample analogue of
\eta_{S}
responds to
\theta
relative to its variance. The construction is moment-based and semiparametric: only the moments
\Psi_{i}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\varphi_{i}(\xi)]
up to order
S
(equivalently, the cumulants up to order
2S
) enter, so the full error density is never required.

The optimal coefficient vector
h=(h_{1},\dots,h_{S})^{\!\top}
solves the linear normal system

F_{S}(\theta)\,h\;=\;b(\theta),\qquad F_{ij}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\!\bigl[\varphi_{i}\varphi_{j}\bigr]-\Psi_{i}\Psi_{j},\qquad b_{i}(\theta)=-\,\frac{\partial\Psi_{i}(\theta)}{\partial\theta},

(2)

where
F_{S}
is the centered correlation matrix of the basis—a Gram-type matrix whose quadratic form returns the variance of any polynomial (
1
) and whose determinant
\Delta_{S}=\det F_{S}
measures the “volume” of the polynomial body. Non-degeneracy,
\Delta_{S}>0
, guarantees existence and uniqueness of
h
; whether the degree-
S
estimator
strictly
improves on the linear (
S=1
) one is a separate question, settled by whether the higher basis powers carry score information orthogonal to
\{\varphi_{1}\}
(Theorem
2
). For a Gaussian error, for instance, the body is non-degenerate yet
g_{2}=1
and no gain accrues. The resulting estimator
\hat{\theta}_{S}
is consistent and asymptotically normal, with asymptotic variance

\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\theta}_{S})\;\approx\;\frac{g_{S}(\theta)}{N\,\bigl(b^{\!\top}F_{S}^{-1}b\bigr)_{S=1}},\qquad g_{S}\;=\;\frac{\bigl(b^{\!\top}F_{1}^{-1}b\bigr)}{\bigl(b^{\!\top}F_{S}^{-1}b\bigr)}\;\leq\;1,

(3)

so that
g_{S}
is the factor by which degree
S
reduces variance relative to the linear baseline. Because the bases are nested,
\{\varphi_{1}\}\subset\{\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}\}\subset\cdots
, the ratio is monotone,
g_{S+1}\leq g_{S}\leq 1
: enlarging the basis can never raise the asymptotic variance.

For
S=2
on a location/regression parameter the coefficient admits the closed form

g_{2}\;=\;1-\frac{\gamma_{3}^{2}}{2+\gamma_{4}},

(4)

which equals
1
for Gaussian errors (
\gamma_{3}=\gamma_{4}=0
, no gain) and drops below
1
exactly when
\gamma_{3}\neq 0
; the fourth moment scales the gain but does not by itself create one at degree two
[
18
]
. For
\chi^{2}_{3}
errors this gives a relative efficiency of
1.80
over least squares; for
\mathrm{Gamma}(2,1)
errors the efficiency is
1.67
. Extending the description beyond
S=2
requires controlling the otherwise infinite moment chain; PMM does so by
perforation of the cumulant description

[
11
]
, retaining the cumulants that carry information and zeroing the rest, a principled truncation that avoids the bias of naive statistical linearization. The device closes the moment chain at the chosen degree
S
—the basis uses no moment above order
2S
—and is not a distributional assumption: for a symmetric error the odd cumulants are
identically
zero, a property of the law used in §
4
without loss, whereas the general degree-three coefficient of §
4.3
drops nothing and retains all of
\gamma_{3},\dots,\gamma_{6}
. The degree-
3
coefficient on a symmetric error class is defined in (
24
); this is the lever exploited in Section
4
.

2.2
Second-order least squares

Second-order least squares (SLS), introduced by
16
and developed by
15
, augments the least-squares criterion with a second-moment residual. For each observation one stacks a first- and a second-order residual,

\rho_{i}(\theta)\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}y_{i}-\mu_{i}(\theta)\\[2.0pt]
y_{i}^{2}-\mu_{i}(\theta)^{2}-\sigma^{2}\end{pmatrix},

(5)

and minimizes the weighted quadratic form

\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{SLS}}\;=\;\arg\min_{\theta}\;\sum_{i=1}^{N}\rho_{i}(\theta)^{\!\top}W_{i}\,\rho_{i}(\theta),\qquad W_{i}\;=\;\bigl\{\mathbb{E}[\rho_{i}\rho_{i}^{\!\top}\mid x_{i}]\bigr\}^{-1},

(6)

the second weight
W_{i}
being the criterion-optimal choice that attains the smallest asymptotic variance within the class
[
15
,
10
]
. The channel through which non-Gaussian shape sharpens the slope is the skewness
\mu_{3}=\kappa_{3}
: it is the off-diagonal of the centered moment matrix
F_{2}=\mathbb{E}[m\,m^{\!\top}]
of
m=(e,\,e^{2}-\sigma^{2})
, and it determines whether the optimal weighting draws on the squared-residual equation (the conditional matrix
\mathbb{E}[\rho_{i}\rho_{i}^{\!\top}\mid x_{i}]
is the design-coupled reparameterization
T_{i}F_{2}T_{i}^{\!\top}
of Section
3
, with off-diagonal
2\mu_{i}\sigma^{2}+\mu_{3}
). When
\gamma_{3}=0
the body
F_{2}
is diagonal, the optimal combination places no weight on the squared residual, and the slope estimator collapses to ordinary least squares.
15
and
10
document the efficiency gain under asymmetric errors; their simulation studies report variance reductions of roughly
50\%
in small samples (
N<100
) relative to least squares—numerically the same regime captured by
g_{2}
in (
4
).

A subtlety in the second residual matters for what follows. The efficient slope information lives in the form
\rho_{2,i}=y_{i}^{2}-\mu_{i}^{2}-\sigma^{2}=2\mu_{i}e_{i}+e_{i}^{2}-\sigma^{2}
, whose expected Jacobian in
\theta
is
-2\mu_{i}(1,x_{i})\neq 0
. The superficially equivalent centered form
e_{i}^{2}-\sigma^{2}
has zero expected Jacobian for the slope and is finite-sample degenerate, a point that becomes load-bearing in the equivalence theorem of Section
3
.

2.3
Related estimators

PMM and SLS sit inside a broader family of methods that exploit moment information without committing to a full likelihood. Hansen’s generalized method of moments (GMM)
[
6
]
minimizes a quadratic form in a set of moment conditions under a weight matrix; with the conditions
\{e,xe,e^{2}-\sigma^{2},x(e^{2}-\sigma^{2})\}
and the two-step optimal weight, GMM attains, asymptotically and under correct specification of these moments with a non-singular weight, the same second-order efficiency as optimally weighted SLS, and the SLS criterion (
6
) can be read as a structured, per-observation-weighted instance of it. Quadratic inference functions
[
14
]
are another neighboring tradition: they also optimize a quadratic form generated by basis components, but for correlated-data estimating equations rather than the residual-power degree axis studied here. Huber’s
M
-estimators
[
9
]
instead bound the influence of outliers through a fixed
\psi
-function, trading a small efficiency loss at the model for robustness against contamination. Hosking’s L-moments
[
8
]
reparameterize a distribution through linear combinations of order statistics, giving low-variance shape summaries that are mainly descriptive rather than tuned to a regression slope. Robustness and descriptive parsimony, rather than slope efficiency under a known moment structure, are the aims of these last two, so they complement rather than compete with the estimators compared here. The higher-order-statistics tradition
[
12
]
shares PMM’s premise that cumulants beyond the second carry usable information, but works chiefly in the spectral (polyspectral) domain.

Against this backdrop the two protagonists differ from fixed-weight methods in one structural respect that the rest of the paper develops. An
M
-estimator fixes its
\psi
-function a priori, whereas efficient GMM, PMM (through the centered correlation matrix
F_{S}
in (
2
)), and SLS (through
W_{i}
in (
6
)) all read the optimal weighting from the estimated moment structure of the data; what distinguishes PMM and SLS from generic GMM is that this weighting is generated intrinsically from the polynomial body rather than chosen for a user-supplied condition set. PMM, however, is not confined to second order: the degree parameter
S
in (
3
) is a genuine extra dimension, whereas SLS is fixed at the second moment by construction. This contrast—identical at
S=2
(the same estimating equation, influence function, and asymptotic variance), divergent for
S\geq 3
—is the subject of Section
3
. Both methods are, in the language of optimal estimating functions
[
5
,
3
,
4
,
7
]
and under correct specification of the chosen moments with a non-singular moment covariance, Godambe-optimal within their moment span—each selects the minimum-variance combination of the chosen moment functions, equivalently the efficient GMM / optimal-instrument choice under conditional moment restrictions
[
2
,
13
]
. This paper does not claim novelty for the projection fact that such a minimum-variance combination is unique; that is the classical optimal estimating-function result. Read in that light, the unification of Section
3
states that PMM
2
and SLS are the
same concrete realization
of this frontier for the span
\{e,e^{2}-\sigma^{2}\}
: the Kunchenko body
F_{2}
becomes the SLS weighting operator after the
T_{i}
change of variables, the
T_{i}
factors cancel in the slope score, and the model-implied second residual explains the otherwise easy-to-miss Jacobian. What the paper adds is this bridge between the Cherkasy-school PMM construction and the SLS literature, and—through the higher-degree reserve of Section
4
and its machine-checked degree-specific closed forms—a quantification of the efficiency that lies strictly above that shared second-order frontier.

3
Second-Order Least Squares as Degree-2 PMM

This section establishes the first of the paper’s two structural results: at degree two the polynomial maximization method (PMM) and second-order least squares (SLS) are not merely empirically close estimators but, at the population level and hence asymptotically, the same estimator—obtained as the optimal linear combination of one and the same pair of moment functions. (The feasible plug-in implementations then differ only at order
O(1/n)
, as quantified in §
5.3
and §
6.4
.) We work in the linear regression model

y_{i}\;=\;x_{i}^{\!\top}\beta\;+\;e_{i},\qquad\mathbb{E}[e_{i}\mid x_{i}]=0,\qquad i=1,\dots,N,

(7)

with i.i.d. errors
e_{i}
, regression mean
\mu_{i}(\beta)=x_{i}^{\!\top}\beta
, variance
\sigma^{2}=\mathbb{E}[e_{i}^{2}]
, and standardized cumulants
\gamma_{3}=\kappa_{3}/\sigma^{3}
(skewness) and
\gamma_{4}=\kappa_{4}/\sigma^{4}
(excess kurtosis). We impose the following regularity. The error has a finite fourth moment, so that the centered correlation matrix
F_{2}
in (
9
) is well defined and the common asymptotic variance (
14
) is finite; this is all the population identity of Theorem
1
requires. A finite eighth moment is imposed only for the
feasible
weight—it makes the sampling variance of the estimated body entries
\hat{\kappa}_{3},\hat{\mu}_{4}
finite and underwrites the uniform convergence behind the plug-in. The body is non-degenerate,
\det F_{2}>0
. The design
x_{i}
has a finite second moment with
\mathbb{E}[x_{i}x_{i}^{\!\top}]
nonsingular. The standard i.i.d. M-estimation regularity conditions (a compact parameter set, an interior true value, and a uniform law of large numbers for the score and its Jacobian) hold. Throughout we standardize
\sigma=1
unless noted, with general
\sigma
restored by homogeneity. Write
c_{2}
for the OLS slope asymptotic variance factor, i.e. the per-observation asymptotic variance of the ordinary least squares estimator of a slope coordinate (
c_{2}=\sigma^{2}/\mathrm{Var}(x)
); this is distinct from the error variance
\sigma^{2}
. All limits are as
N\to\infty
.

3.1
The shared estimating space

Both estimators are driven by the same two centered moment functions of the error,

m(e)\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}(e)\\[2.0pt]
m_{2}(e)\end{pmatrix}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}e\\[2.0pt]

(8)

For a candidate combination

\mathbb{E}[\partial_{e}m_{2}]\end{pmatrix}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}1\\[2.0pt]
0\end{pmatrix},

(10)

(SLS

Proof

Proof.

Step 1 (both reduce to a quadratic program in
\mathcal{M}

\sum_{i}x_{i}\,a^{\!\top}m(e_{i})=0

3.4

The factorization

A Higher-Degree Efficiency Reserve

4.4
Why SLS structurally cannot reach the reserve

The reason SLS forgoes the gain of Theorem
3
is not a tuning choice but a property of its moment vector. Recall the SLS residual
\rho_{i}=(y_{i}-\mu_{i},\,y_{i}^{2}-\mu_{i}^{2}-\sigma^{2})^{\top}

[
16
,
15
]
: it spans the data through powers up to
e^{2}
only. The slope estimating equation is therefore a linear combination of the first two moment conditions, and its sensitivity to symmetric kurtosis is mediated entirely by the third error moment.

Proposition 2

(Symmetric-kurtosis blindness of SLS)
.

For symmetric error the SLS slope score is proportional to
\mu_{3}=\mathbb{E}[e^{3}]
. Under symmetry
\mu_{3}=0
, so the SLS slope estimating equation reduces to the ordinary least-squares normal equation and carries no information from
\gamma_{4}
or any even cumulant beyond
\sigma^{2}
.

Proof.

The optimally weighted SLS slope first-order condition is
\sum_{i}(\partial_{\beta}\rho_{i})^{\top}W_{i}\rho_{i}=0
with
W_{i}=\{\mathbb{E}[\rho_{i}\rho_{i}^{\top}\mid x_{i}]\}^{-1}
and Jacobian
\partial_{\beta}\rho_{i}=-x_{i}(1,2\mu_{i})^{\top}
. By the
T_{i}
-cancellation of Theorem
1
, (
17
), this condition reduces to
\sum_{i}x_{i}\,(F_{2}^{-1}b)^{\top}m(e_{i})=0
, the degree-two PMM equation with optimal combination
a=F_{2}^{-1}b
. The off-diagonal of the body
F_{2}=\big(\begin{smallmatrix}\sigma^{2}&\kappa_{3}\\
\kappa_{3}&\kappa_{4}+2\sigma^{4}\end{smallmatrix}\big)
is the third cumulant
\kappa_{3}=\mu_{3}
; under symmetry
\mu_{3}=0
, so
F_{2}
is diagonal and
a=F_{2}^{-1}b=(1/\sigma^{2},\,0)^{\top}
. The optimal combination thus places
zero weight on the squared residual
, and the slope condition collapses to
\sum_{i}x_{i}e_{i}=0
, the OLS normal equation. The conditional cross-moment is not the mechanism:
\mathbb{E}[\rho_{i1}\rho_{i2}\mid x_{i}]=2\mu_{i}\sigma^{2}+\mu_{3}
does
not
vanish at
\mu_{3}=0
, so
W_{i}
is
not
block-diagonal; rather the optimal weight annihilates
\rho_{2}
in the slope direction,
W_{i}^{\star}\partial_{\beta}\rho_{i}\propto T_{i}^{-\top}F_{2}^{-1}b=(1/\sigma^{2},\,0)^{\top}
, whose second component is zero. No even cumulant
\gamma_{4},\gamma_{6},\dots
enters, because the centered basis
m=(e,\,e^{2}-\sigma^{2})
contains no monomial above
e^{2}
. Hence the SLS slope variance equals the OLS variance,
g_{2}=1
, independently of
\gamma_{4}
.
∎

Proposition
2
sharpens “structurally cannot reach” into a precise statement about the span of the estimating functions. SLS optimizes a quadratic form in the two-vector
\rho=(e,\,e^{2}-\sigma^{2})
; the symmetric-kurtosis direction that PMM exploits is
\xi^{3}-\text{proj}_{\{\xi\}}\xi^{3}
, an element of
\mathcal{H}_{3}\setminus\mathcal{H}_{2}
. Because
\mathcal{H}_{2}=\operatorname{span}\{e,\,e^{2}-\sigma^{2}\}
is exactly the SLS moment span, this direction is
L^{2}
-orthogonal to everything SLS can form. The reserve is not merely unused by SLS; it lies outside the linear space SLS is defined on. Raising SLS to read it would require appending a cubic residual
e^{3}-\mu_{3}
— which is precisely the degree-three PMM extension, not second-order least squares. In this sense the reserve is the formal boundary of the SLS construction.

4.5
Numerical and formal confirmation

The two predictions of this section are confirmed by the controlled Monte Carlo study of §
5
and by the formal development; we summarize the evidence here and report it in full there. On symmetric platykurtic errors—centered uniform with
\gamma_{4}=-1.2
,
\gamma_{6}=6.857
, so
6+9\gamma_{4}+\gamma_{6}=2.057>0
and
g_{3}=0.300
by (
24
), an asymptotic relative efficiency
1/g_{3}=3.33
within the degree-three polynomial moment class—the second-order estimators SLS and GMM sit at the OLS baseline for the slope while degree-three PMM climbs toward the closed-form ceiling (Table
5
), in agreement with Proposition
2
; under the Gaussian control no estimator gains or loses at any degree (Table
7
). The degree-specific algebraic core of both coefficients—the closed forms (
4
) and (
24
), the
g_{2}\leq 1
result, the design cancellations, and the symmetric reduction—is machine-checked in Lean 4 (§
3
and the code supplement), while the general
g_{S}
monotonicity is the analytic nesting theorem. Together with Theorem
1
, this establishes the asymmetry that motivates the unification: SLS is the degree-two specialization of generalized PMM, and outside that degree the polynomial method holds a strict, quantifiable efficiency reserve that SLS is structurally unable to claim.

5
Monte Carlo Evidence

The two main results of the preceding sections make sharp, testable predictions. Theorem
1
asserts that under asymmetric errors the second-order least squares (SLS) estimator and the second-degree polynomial maximization estimator (PMM2) are not merely comparable but
asymptotically the same statistic
: a single optimal linear combination of the moment functions
\{e,\,e^{2}-\sigma^{2}\}
, sharing one influence function and one asymptotic variance
c_{2}\,g_{2}/N
. Theorem
3
asserts a structural asymmetry between the two methods: under symmetric platykurtic errors the degree-2 channel is empty for both (
g_{2}=1
), yet the degree-3 channel that PMM accesses is informative (
g_{3}<1
), whereas SLS, confined to moment functions up to
e^{2}
, carries no symmetric-kurtosis information for the slope. We now confront both predictions with a controlled Monte Carlo study.

5.1
Design

The data-generating process is the linear regression

y_{i}=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}x_{i}+e_{i},\qquad\beta_{0}=2,\ \beta_{1}=1.5,\ x_{i}\sim\mathrm{U}(0,5),

(28)

with the slope
\beta_{1}
as estimand. Errors are centered to mean zero and drawn from four distributions chosen to populate the two regimes of the theory. The
asymmetric
regime, which activates the degree-2 reserve, uses
\chi^{2}(3)
(
\gamma_{3}=\sqrt{8/3}\approx 1.63
,
\gamma_{4}=4
, so
g_{2}=0.556
and a theoretical relative efficiency
1/g_{2}=1.80
) and
\mathrm{Gamma}(2,1)
(
\gamma_{3}=\sqrt{2}\approx 1.41
,
\gamma_{4}=3
, so
g_{2}=0.600
and
1/g_{2}=1.67
). The
symmetric platykurtic
regime, which activates only the degree-3 reserve, uses
\mathrm{U}(a,b)
(
\gamma_{3}=0
,
\gamma_{4}=-1.2
); evaluating (
24
) gives
g_{3}=0.30
, an efficiency ceiling of
1/g_{3}=3.33
. A Gaussian error serves as the null control, where no polynomial reserve exists at any degree.

We compare five estimators of
\beta_{1}
.
OLS
is the baseline against which all relative efficiencies are measured.
PMM2
and
PMM3
are the degree-2 and degree-3 polynomial maximization estimators implemented with the public
EstemPMM
package on CRAN
[
20
]
.
SLS
is the feasible-optimal Wang–Leblanc estimator
[
16
,
15
]

\sum_{i}\rho_{i}^{\top}W_{i}\,\rho_{i}
with
\rho_{i}=(y_{i}-\mu_{i},\,y_{i}^{2}-\mu_{i}^{2}-\sigma^{2})
and the per-observation optimal weight
W_{i}=\{\mathrm{E}[\rho\rho^{\top}\mid x_{i}]\}^{-1}

, whose expected Jacobian with respect to the slope is

; the algebraically tempting centered form

has zero expected Jacobian for the slope and is finite-sample degenerate, contributing no slope information (cf. the key remark following Theorem

[
6

on the four moment conditions

The regression cells use

5.2
Feasible implementation of the optimal weight

Both estimators require the body
F_{2}
, which by Theorem
1
is the single object that drives either of them, so the feasible plug-in is shared. We form it from ordinary least squares. First, fit OLS to obtain
\hat{\beta}^{(0)}
and residuals
\hat{e}_{i}=y_{i}-x_{i}^{\!\top}\hat{\beta}^{(0)}
, and estimate
\hat{\sigma}^{2}=\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\hat{e}_{i}^{2}
together with the third and fourth central moments
\hat{\kappa}_{3}=\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\hat{e}_{i}^{3}
and
\hat{\mu}_{4}=\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\hat{e}_{i}^{4}
, giving
\widehat{F}_{2}=\big(\begin{smallmatrix}\hat{\sigma}^{2}&\hat{\kappa}_{3}\\
\hat{\kappa}_{3}&\hat{\mu}_{4}-\hat{\sigma}^{4}\end{smallmatrix}\big)
. Second, with
\hat{\mu}_{i}=x_{i}^{\!\top}\hat{\beta}^{(0)}
and
\widehat{T}_{i}=\big(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
2\hat{\mu}_{i}&1\end{smallmatrix}\big)
, set
\widehat{W}_{i}=\widehat{T}_{i}^{-\top}\widehat{F}_{2}^{-1}\widehat{T}_{i}^{-1}
and solve the weighted score
\sum_{i}\dot{\rho}_{i}^{\!\top}\widehat{W}_{i}\,\rho_{i}(\beta)=0
by Newton’s method from
\hat{\beta}^{(0)}
; we re-estimate the moments at the updated
\hat{\beta}
and iterate to convergence, though a single update from the
\sqrt{n}
-consistent OLS start is already first-order efficient. Two structural points govern finite-sample behavior. The slope estimating equation
\sum_{i}x_{i}\,a^{\!\top}m(e_{i})=0
is invariant to a positive rescaling of
a=\widehat{F}_{2}^{-1}b
, so only the
direction
of
a
matters; with
b=(1,0)^{\!\top}
that direction is fixed by
a_{2}/a_{1}=-\hat{\kappa}_{3}/(\hat{\mu}_{4}-\hat{\sigma}^{4})
, and the common factor
1/\det\widehat{F}_{2}
(which carries
\hat{\sigma}^{2}
) cancels. The slope therefore depends on the body only through
\hat{\kappa}_{3},\hat{\mu}_{4}
, and
\hat{\sigma}^{2}
, all
\sqrt{n}
-consistent, which leave
\widehat{F}_{2}
well conditioned down to
n=50

\hat{m}_{5},\hat{m}_{6}
and is the source of the finite-sample cost quantified in §
6.4

\widehat{F}_{2}
, any residual finite-sample gap between them is attributable to the solver alone, not to a different weighting.

6
Discussion

6.1
PMM as a degree-graded family that contains SLS

Theorems
1
and
3
place second-order least squares and the polynomial maximization method on a single axis indexed by the polynomial degree
S
. The estimating family is generated by the basis
\{\xi,\dots,\xi^{S}\}
, and the optimal coefficient vector solves the Kunchenko normal system
F_{S}\,h=b
, where
F_{S}=\Psi_{ij}-\Psi_{i}\Psi_{j}
is the centered correlation matrix of the basis and
b
collects the derivatives of the basis means with respect to the parameter. The construction is monotone in
S
: appending a higher power enlarges the column space of admissible estimating functions, so the attained variance ratio
g_{S}
cannot increase,
g_{S+1}\leq g_{S}\leq 1
. This nesting is what turns “which estimator?” into “at which degree?”.

Read along this axis, SLS is the
S=2
instance. The SLS criterion
\sum_{i}\rho_{i}^{\top}W_{i}\rho_{i}
with
\rho_{i}=(y_{i}-\mu_{i},\;y_{i}^{2}-\mu_{i}^{2}-\sigma^{2})
and optimal weight
W_{i}=\{\mathbb{E}[\rho\rho^{\top}\mid x_{i}]\}^{-1}
is, after the change of variables to the centered basis, the same optimal linear combination of the two moment functions
\{e,\,e^{2}-\sigma^{2}\}
that the degree-two normal system returns;
F_{2}

is
the SLS weighting operator, the two influence functions coincide, and both deliver asymptotic variance
c_{2}\,g_{2}/N
with
g_{2}
defined in (
4
). Hence
\text{SLS}=\text{generalized-PMM}\big|_{S=2}
. The relation is strict where it should be: because SLS already attains the optimal degree-two weighting, it dominates a generalized method of moments estimator built on the
same
two moment conditions whenever that GMM estimator uses a sub-optimal (e.g. identity or first-step) weight, and it equals the optimal-weight GMM at convergence. The ordering
\text{PMM}_{S}\succeq\text{SLS}=\text{PMM}_{2}\succeq\text{GMM}_{\leq 2}
is therefore not three competing methods but one family read at one degree with one weighting rule. The simulations make the
S=2
collapse visible: on
\chi^{2}(3)
and
\mathrm{Gamma}(2,1)
errors the realized slope efficiencies of PMM2, SLS and optimal GMM agree to within Monte Carlo noise, while the per-dataset correlation
\mathrm{cor}(\widehat{\beta}_{\text{PMM2}},\widehat{\beta}_{\text{SLS}})
rises from
0.996
at
n{=}100
to
0.9997
at
n{=}1000
and the paired difference contracts toward zero (
\sim\!5\times 10^{-5}
). The reference relative efficiencies (
1.80
and
1.667
) are the asymptotic slope efficiencies as well as the location ones: the
\mu(x)
-coupling of the second-moment residual cancels identically (Corollary
1
), so the slope attains
\mathrm{RE}=1/g_{2}
design-invariantly, the feasible finite-sample values converging to it as
n
grows. What the simulations establish is the equivalence—the fingerprints of two algebraically identical estimators, not of two methods that merely happen to perform alike.

6.2
The higher-degree efficiency reserve

What the unification exposes, beyond a re-derivation, is the headroom above
S=2
. Theorem
3
isolates a regime that SLS cannot enter by construction. For symmetric errors
\gamma_{3}=0
, so
g_{2}=1
and the degree-two gain vanishes; the optimal SLS slope combination drops the squared residual (its weight is proportional to
\mu_{3}=0
), and SLS collapses to ordinary least squares for the slope. Yet platykurtosis
\gamma_{4}<0
keeps the degree-three coefficient in (
24
) below one, so PMM3 strictly improves on OLS exactly where SLS does not. Because SLS reads only moments up to
e^{2}
, it carries no symmetric-kurtosis information for the slope; the reserve is not an efficiency that SLS leaves on the table for want of a better weight, but a regime structurally outside its estimating span. The simulations confirm the separation cleanly: on uniform (platykurtic,
\gamma_{4}=-1.2
) errors SLS and GMM sit at OLS efficiency (
\approx 1.0
) while PMM3 reaches a relative efficiency of
3.16
at
n{=}500
, in line with the closed-form prediction
g_{3}^{-1}=10/3\approx 3.33
for this law; under the Gaussian control all estimators return relative efficiency
\approx 0.98
, so the higher degree imposes no penalty when there is nothing to extract. The reserve is reached by
perforation of the cumulant description
: the degree-three system keeps the cumulants that carry slope information and zeroes the rest, closing the otherwise non-terminating moment chain without naive statistical linearization.

6.3
Orthogonal axes of extension

The degree axis is one of several along which the same normal-system core can be moved, and it is worth stating where the present unification sits so that the contribution is not misread as an implementation exercise. The basis in
F_{S}\,h=b
need not consist of integer powers, and one may also change the basis family, add regularization, or make the moment body conditional on covariates. These are
orthogonal
extensions: they vary the basis or conditioning structure while holding the degree machinery fixed, whereas this paper varies the degree while holding the integer-power basis fixed. The unification theorem and the higher-degree reserve are therefore statements about the degree axis, not about any particular software implementation or future basis family.

The contribution here is the bridge itself together with the reserve it reveals: a structural theorem relating two estimation traditions and a quantification of the efficiency that lies strictly above their shared degree.

6.4
Limitations

Four boundaries delimit the claims. First, the closed forms are degree-specific:
g_{2}
and
g_{3}
are available in the compact expressions above, but for
S\geq 4
the variance ratio is the solution of a larger normal system and admits no equally tidy formula, so the efficiency ordering for higher degrees is established by the nesting argument rather than by explicit constants. Second, the entire construction is moment-based and presupposes that the cumulants entering
F_{S}
and
b
exist; the apparatus is silent for heavy-tailed laws (
\alpha
-stable, Cauchy, Student-
t
with
\nu\leq 2
) where the relevant moments diverge, and a characteristic-function reformulation may be the more appropriate tool there than a higher polynomial degree. Third,
1/g_{S}
is a
within-polynomial-class
frontier, not a semiparametric efficiency bound over all possible procedures. For bounded-support laws such as the uniform distribution the regular location Fisher information is not the relevant finite constant, and for Gamma-shape laws at or below shape two (including
\chi^{2}(3)
and
\mathrm{Gamma}(2,1)
after centering) the ordinary location Fisher information diverges at the boundary. Adaptive and likelihood-based procedures can therefore target information beyond the finite-degree moment span when their regularity conditions hold
[
1
]
. PMM is positioned here as a transparent, moment-based compromise between OLS and full distributional/adaptive likelihood methods, with
1/g_{3}
describing the attainable degree-three polynomial ceiling rather than the global efficiency frontier.

Fourth, the SLS
=
PMM
2
identity is asymptotic. At finite
n
the two estimators differ at order
O(1/n)
: an independent asymptotic sandwich calculation returns
\mathrm{RE}=1.798
against the theoretical
1.800
, and the simulation study shows
\sqrt{n}\cdot\mathrm{sd}(\widehat{\beta}_{\text{PMM2}}-\widehat{\beta}_{\text{SLS}})\to 0
, so the gap is real but vanishing. A practical corollary surfaced in implementation: the second residual must be written
\rho_{2}=y^{2}-\mu^{2}-\sigma^{2}
, whose expected Jacobian
-2\mu(1,x)
is non-zero; the cosmetically equivalent
e^{2}-\sigma^{2}
form has zero expected Jacobian for the slope and is finite-sample degenerate, a subtlety that the unification makes legible but does not remove.

Fifth, and most consequential for practice, the reserve is an asymptotic guarantee about the
population
coefficients
h=F_{S}^{-1}b
. A feasible estimator must substitute
\hat{h}=\hat{F}_{S}^{-1}\hat{b}
, built from sample moments up to order
2S
whose sampling variance
\operatorname{Var}(\hat{m}_{k})\approx(m_{2k}-m_{k}^{2})/n
climbs steeply with the order
k
. At
S=3
the controlling quantity is the sixth cumulant: on the laws of Table
1
the Monte Carlo standard deviation of
\hat{\gamma}_{6}
is comparable to or larger than
\gamma_{6}
itself for
n\lesssim 500
(for
\chi^{2}(3)
,
\operatorname{sd}(\hat{\gamma}_{6})\approx 80
–
120
against
\gamma_{6}=53
), so the plug-in weights are poorly determined in small samples. A direct feasible-versus-oracle study confirms the cost and probes a fix: for
\chi^{2}(3)
and
\mathrm{Gamma}(2,1)
the feasible degree-three plug-in does not realize its oracle ceiling at moderate
n
, and a uniform Tikhonov ridge
\hat{h}=(\hat{F}_{3}+\lambda I)^{-1}\hat{b}
does
not
rescue it—the ill-conditioning is concentrated in the
\hat{m}_{6}
direction, so a scalar ridge over-penalizes the informative low-order coefficients rather than the noisy one. The realized degree-three efficiency therefore approaches
1/g_{3}
only as
n
grows—the feasible degree-three estimator on the symmetric platykurtic errors of §
5.4
climbs from
2.07
at
n=50
toward its within-class ceiling
1/g_{3}=3.33
, reaching
3.16
at
n=500
(Table
5
)—and a higher polynomial degree, which couples to still higher moments, pays a larger finite-sample penalty: the gain from degree three over degree two is itself an asymptotic quantity. The compact closed forms describe the attainable polynomial ceiling, not a small-sample guarantee, and the practical regime for the higher-degree reserve is large samples in which
\hat{m}_{5}
and
\hat{m}_{6}
are well determined.

7
Conclusion

This paper establishes a bridge and a reserve. The bridge (Theorem
1
) identifies second-order least squares with the polynomial maximization method at degree two: the two estimators are the optimal linear combination of the same pair of moment functions, the Kunchenko centered correlation matrix
F_{2}
realizes the SLS weighting, their influence functions coincide, and both attain asymptotic variance
c_{2}\,g_{2}/N
with
g_{2}
defined in (
4
). SLS is thus generalized-PMM
\big|_{S=2}
, and SLS in turn dominates a same-moment GMM estimator with sub-optimal weight; three apparently distinct traditions are one degree-graded family read at a single degree. The reserve (Theorem
3
) is the within-polynomial-class efficiency that lives strictly above that degree: for symmetric platykurtic errors SLS reduces to OLS for the slope while PMM3 gains through the coefficient in (
24
), a regime SLS cannot reach because its estimating span stops at the second moment. The Monte Carlo study supports both: PMM2, SLS and optimal GMM converge to a common efficiency on asymmetric errors (their PMM2–SLS per-dataset correlation rising toward one and their paired difference vanishing), while PMM3 attains relative efficiency up to
3.16
on uniform errors where SLS and GMM stay at OLS, with no loss under the Gaussian control.

Three directions follow.

•

Higher degrees.
The variance ordering is proved for all
S
by nesting but lacks closed forms beyond
S=3
; the constants and the empirical reserve for
S\geq 4
deserve systematic study, including the trade-off between the larger reserve and the cost of estimating higher cumulants.

•

Nonlinear regression.
The unification is stated for linear regression; extending the SLS
=
PMM
2
identity and the degree-three reserve to nonlinear regression (exponential and growth mean functions; cf. the PMM nonlinear-regression study
[
17
]
) would test how far the bridge carries when the Jacobian
b
becomes parameter-dependent.

•

Real-data validation.
The closed-form efficiency predictions invite confrontation with real asymmetric and platykurtic data, where the question is not whether the reserve exists but how much of it survives estimation of the moment structure that defines
F_{S}
—a feasible-versus-oracle comparison that should measure the stability of the estimated cumulants and the sensitivity to tail behavior.
