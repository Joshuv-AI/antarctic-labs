 Data Analysis, Machine Learning, & More
 The Work I’ve Done at LANL through the SULI Program
 Carter Wolf
 At LANL, I have had the privilege and pleasure of working with Dr. Tika Kaﬂe, an optics
and condensed matter physicist at the MagLab in TA-35. Dr. Kaﬂe’s research primarily involves
the Angle Resolved Photoemission Spectroscopy (ARPES) technique of characterizing materials.
More speciﬁcally, Dr. Kaﬂe works with Time Resolved ARPES (Tr-ARPES), which extends this
technique into the time domain. ARPES involves bombarding a sample with a high intensity
beam of polarized and focused light which ejects the sample’s electrons via the photoelectric
eﬀect. Depending on the energy and momentum of the electrons, they will eject from the
sample in a particular direction and land on the analyzer at a speciﬁc point. This allows us to
visualize the electron and band structure of the sample. To extend this technique into the time
domain, the sample is hit by another beam, called the pump beam, which excites the electrons.
By varying the time delay between the pump beam and the photoemission beam, called the
probe beam, we can visualize the electrons in their excited states as well as during the process
of relaxation. This technique gives us an even more valuable insight into how electronic band
structures and the electron dynamics aﬀect certain materials such as superconductors,
graphene, and topological insulators. While I have yet been unable to run a proper Tr-ARPES
system at LANL, I have worked with the data that Dr. Kaﬂe has taken in previous experiments
and processed in certain ways such as plotting, cutting, ﬁtting, and denoising. This data
processing has taken most of my time here so far at LANL.

 While my initial expectation at LANL was to be able to work in the lab doing hands on
laser experiments, this would not be the case. In order to obtain authorization to work
unsupervised in the lab, I was required to fulﬁll the safety requirements for the lab which
included training that took close to two months to complete. As a result, my work in the lab has
unfortunately been limited. During this time, Dr. Kaﬂe assigned me two primary projects: to plot
and analyze ARPES data from his previous experiments and develop a neural network which
could input noisy data from ARPES experiments and train a model which would mitigate or even
completely remove any noise from the data.

 1. Plot Fitting
 Many scientists who work with ARPES data standardly use the program IgorPro to input
raw data, manage large databases, and generate plots. However, Dr. Kaﬂe wants to transition to
pyARPES, a Python module which can theoretically match the capabilities of IgorPro. The

beneﬁt of pyARPES is the added ﬂexibility of running code in a Python notebook which allows
for implementation of added features beyond the limitations of IgorPro. In particular, Dr. Kaﬂe is
interested in adding AI features to ARPES data analysis such as faster and automated graphics,
more accurate characterization of the sample material, and most importantly denoising of
experimental measurements.

 The relevant data looks like one of the two graph types shown in Figure 1. Since the data
ﬁle itself only contains the intensity values, which is displayed via the colored gradient, the
energy and momentum axes need to be input manually based on the speciﬁcations of the
analyzer. By itself, this data cannot be directly ﬁt to a curve. To remedy this, we can take a
particular slice of the data to generate an Energy Density Curve (EDC). This is usually done by
ﬁxing a particular momentum and specifying an energy range to display a relationship between
energy and intensity. In our case, we instead sum over a range of momenta to create a more
accurate and smoother graph. Using this EDC, we can then ﬁt the graph to a particular formula.
In our case, we use a Fermi-Dirac distribution multiplied with a Lorentzian distribution, which is

 2. Machine Learning
 Due to hardware and experimental apparatus limitations, ARPES data can be quite noisy, as
is seen in Figure 1. This results in EDCs that are not entirely accurate and which can signiﬁcantly
aﬀect the parameters we want to solve for using curve ﬁtting. Even small errors in the EDC can
change, for example, the electron temperature to a degree that it resembles that of the
material’s static state. The solution to this problem is denoising. Many denoising techniques
exist which have been successfully applied to ARPES data, but recent papers1,2 have shown that
machine learning techniques have the potential to outperform traditional methods. Dr. Kaﬂe
wants to apply machine learning on his own data to reﬁne measurements and provide more
accuracy.

 For the model’s architecture, I implemented a U-net architecture (see Figure 3) with a total
of 15 training layers. The same model was used with success in a paper by Lehtinen et al¹. with a
similar number of layers. Another paper by T. Takaai and M. Tsutsui² suggests that a diﬀerent
model architecture called Convolutional Auto-Encoder may better preserve the structure of the
data, but that U-net still generally performs slightly better in terms of Mean Square Error. Most
of the time, when a model is training to denoise data, it compares its denoising attempts to a

clean version of the data it is using to train, but in our case no clean data exists to use for
comparison. One solution would be to simulate clean data, but this is not feasible in our case.
What I have done to remedy this issue is implement a training method called Noise2Noise²,
which uses pairs of independent noisy data to essentially pick out similarities and discard
diﬀerences. With enough pairs of data, the Noise2Noise method becomes an eﬀective tool
despite the lack of clean data.

 Figure 3: The general structure of the U-net architecture.

 To train the model, I fed it a total of 28 pairs of data, allocating 20 pairs for training and 8
for validation. The data comes from two independent data acquisition times with similar time
delays for each measurement. By making the delay time consistent between each data set in
the pair, the model should theoretically perform the best, as the signal will be as close as
possible while the noise is still independent. Since the size of the dataset is relatively small, I
supplemented the number of training samples by patching each sample, that is cropping each
data set to a smaller size, making sure the patch location is consistent between pairs. At ﬁrst,
this patching was done randomly, but I found that systematically patching data to ensure each
data point is represented, then training on the patches in a random order provided the best
results. For the training parameters, I varied each one to optimize performance, and found that
a patch size of 64x64, 2 training sets (4 total patches) per batch, and 200 batches per epoch
worked the best. I varied the number of epochs to compare, the best results of which are
shown below in Figure 4. During some tests, I tried upwards of 50 epochs, but this result was
very overﬁt and contained many artifacts, so I limited myself to 10 at the maximum.

 By taking EDCs of data shown in Figure 1 at each time delay and plotting them with
respect to their time delay, a plot like that in Figure 5 can be created. We would also like to
denoise this type of plot, but a diﬀerent training protocol is required. Instead of training based
on pairs, the initial idea was to selectively train the background towards zero. The hypothesis
was that by then running the background and signal through the model that it would remove
noise. However, as seen in Figure 6, this was not the case. Instead, I believe that the model is
predicting the background noise for the data. What can be done then is to subtract this
predicted background noise from the signal and then train a second model to denoise the

background subtracted data using the same Noise2Noise training method. A potential problem
with this method is that we currently only have six data sets of this type, so the model may be
much more prone to overﬁtting than one with ﬁfty six data sets.

 Figure 6: Time dependent EDC graphs before and after being run through a model which attempts to eliminate
  background. Seemingly, the model almost completely destroys the signal. The top two graphs show data from
 YMn6Sn4Ge2 while the bottom two show NaCd4As3. Note that the x axis should properly be labeled “time delay”

 In the near future, I plan to improve these models in a couple of diﬀerent ways. First, the
time dependent EDC denoising can be improved as mentioned previously by implementing a
second model which can denoise background subtracted data. Additionally, it will be
worthwhile to compare the results from denoising with machine learning to more traditional
denoising methods such as Gaussian Denoising or Principal Component Analysis. One of our
biggest issues is the small amount of data, so it may very well be the case that traditional
methods will perform better than the neural network until we obtain more data. Once Dr. Kaﬂe
and I are satisﬁed with the level of denoising the model can deliver, I plan to return to work on
the plot ﬁtting of EDCs.
