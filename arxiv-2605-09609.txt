Minimal Filling Architectures of Polynomial Neural Networks: Counterexamples, Frontier Search, and Defects
Kevin Dao and Jose Israel Rodriguez

Abstract
We provide a counterexample to the minimal unimodal conjecture for polynomial neural networks (PNNs) with power activation functions. Fixing the input and output widths, the conjecture states that any minimal filling architecture has unimodal widths for the hidden layers. We found a counterexample via a frontier search and certified it using recursive dimension bounds and symbolic computation. Notably, several subarchitectures of this example exhibit large defect, in contrast with the predominantly small-defect behavior observed in prior examples.

The network’s

It is immediate that

Definition 1.2

The image of

One goal of neuroalgebraic geometry is to characterize when the neurovariety and ambient space dimension agree.

Conjecture 1.6
.

Fix

\mathbf{d}=(d_{0},d_{1},\dots,d_{0}pt)

The motivation for the conjecture is that it conforms with “conventional wisdom”. In machine learning, the commonly held belief is that the network expands its capacity for feature representation to capture intricate patterns, and then narrows to refine these features for predictions.
One reason to have believed this conjecture is that it holds in the shallow setting, where it follows from the Alexander–Hirschowitz theorem via the classical secant-Veronese dimension count.

Search Algorithm

Our algorithm for finding MFAs exploits Lemma
2
and the definition of minimality. The pseudocode is provided in Algorithm
1
. To compute the dimension of the neurovariety, we use
finite field computations.
The difficulty in finding MFAs is due to the size of the search space.

To efficiently
find MFAs
(d_{0},\mathbf{a},d_{0}pt)
, we employ a frontier search over
\mathbf{a}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{0pt-1})\in[m,n]^{0pt-1}
.
We first prune the search space using naive dimension bounds, e.g.,

\dim\left(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}\right)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{0pt}d_{i}d_{i-1}-\sum_{i=1}^{0pt-1}d_{i}
.
If there were any previous searches already performed, then we prune the search space using the results. Then, we repeatedly test
(d_{0},\mathbf{a},d_{0}pt)
from among the remaining possibilities for filling architectures
(d_{0},\mathbf{a},d_{0}pt)
:

•

If
(d_{0},\mathbf{a},d_{0}pt)
is filling, we prune all
\mathbf{a}^{\prime}>\mathbf{a}
.

•

If
(d_{0},\mathbf{a},d_{0}pt)
is not filling, we prune all
\mathbf{a}^{\prime}<\mathbf{a}
.

Eventually this terminates to a finite set of candidates. We verify each candidate by computing the dimension of its subarchitectures.
If verification suggests a candidate is not minimal, then we adjust our
m
and
n
and run the search again. Utilizing previous search history to inform subsequent searches allowed us to quickly prune the search space.

4.2
Defectivity: losing expected expressive power

In studying the counterexample, we observed that its subarchitectures had large “defectivity”, a notion we recall next. Defectivity (for fixed activation degree
r
) is a measure of an architecture’s loss in expressivity which cannot be attributed to expected symmetries.
Presently, there does not appear to be a systematic understanding of which architectures are defective. This motivates the definition.

Definition 4.1
.

If
\mathbf{d}=(d_{0},\dots,d_{0}pt)
, then the
expected dimension
of the neurovariety
\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}
, denoted
\expdim\left(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}\right)
, equals the minimum
of the dimension of the ambient space and

\sum_{i=1}^{0pt}d_{i}d_{i-1}-\sum_{i=1}^{0pt-1}d_{i}
. The
defect
of the neurovariety is defined to be the difference
\expdim\left(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}\right)-\dim\left(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}\right)
.

\diamond

The defects of some architectures in Table

These appear to be among the first nontrivial examples of architectures exhibiting large defects.
One can manufacture arbitrarily large defect by making one of the hidden layers have width one while allowing the remaining layers to be very wide.
This motivates the study of
maximal non-filling architectures
and their defect.

It is well known that polynomial activations present a trade-off: low-degree polynomials can limit expressivity, whereas higher-degree polynomials often lead to numerical instability
[
15
]
and gradient explosion
[
5
]
. Efforts to address these issues are already present in the literature
[
7
]
. One natural response is to pair low-degree polynomial activations with architectures that are
a priori
highly expressive; in this context, understanding MFAs for power activations may inform architecture selection.

4.3
Next steps and new directions

Our results concern a classical feedforward PNN model with power activation.
An important generalization is to allow broader polynomial activation families, for example activations expressed in orthogonal polynomial bases such as Chebyshev polynomials.
A complementary direction is to investigate the polynomial networks (
\Pi
-nets) in
[
3
]
using tools from neuroalgebraic geometry, to obtain a geometric characterization of their expressivity.

Another next step is to compare training dynamics on MFAs versus maximal non-filling architectures.
Several works have already investigated optimization landscapes and function-space geometry for convolutional neural networks
[
9
]
and PNNs
[
1
]
through a neuroalgebraic geometry lens. But a direct comparison between MFA and maximal non-filling architectures is yet to be studied.
