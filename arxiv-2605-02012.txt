Shifted asymmetric Laplace mixtures of experts
Sphiwe B. Skhosana, Hien Duy Nguyen

Abstract
Mixtures of experts (MoE) models provide a flexible framework for modelling heterogeneity in data for regression and model-based clustering and classification. MoE models for regression are typically based on the Gaussian assumption for the expert distributions. To robustify the MoE framework with respect to data exhibiting skewness, heavy tails and outliers, we propose a robust non-normal MoE model using the shifted asymmetric Laplace (SAL) distribution. The proposed SALMoE model overcomes the limitations of the Gaussian MoE model when the observed data are asymmetric and heavy-tailed. Through a combination of the minorization-maximization (MM) algorithm with the classical Expectation-Maximization (EM), we develop a dedicated hybrid EM-MM algorithm to estimate the parameters of the SALMoE model. The EM-MM algorithm is shown to yield a nondecreasing observed log-likelihood. A simulation study demonstrates the robustness and practical utility of the proposed model. Finally, the SALMoE model is applied to two real-world economic datasets. The R code used to fit the SALMoE model using the EM-MM algorithm is available through the link: https://github.com/SALMoE_EM-MM .

Introduction

Finite mixtures of regression (FMR) models, first introduced by
37
, are based on the assumption that the data, which consist of the response variable
y\in\mathbb{R}
and a covariate vector
\mathbf{x}=(1,x_{1},\dots,x_{p})\in\mathbb{R}^{p+1}
including an intercept, are generated from, say
K
, unknown regression models. Moreover, the data are governed by a component indicator variable
Z
indicating which regression model generated which data point. The variable
Z
has distribution
\mathbb{P}(Z=k)=\pi_{k}
, for
k=1,2,\dots,K
, where the
\pi_{k}
’s are the non-negative mixing probabilities satisfying
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}=1
. The variable
Z
is typically unobserved or latent. Thus, given the set of covariates
\mathbf{x}
, the conditional density of the response variable
y
is defined as

\displaystyle f(y|\mathbf{x};\bm{\vartheta})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}f_{k}(y|\mathbf{x};\bm{\theta}_{k})

(1)

where
\bm{\vartheta}=(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\dots,\pi_{K};\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2},\dots,\bm{\theta}_{K})
is the vector of model parameters, with
\bm{\theta}_{k}
being the parameter vector of the
k^{th}
component conditional distribution.

Although flexible compared to a single regression model, model (
1
) is still limited by the assumption that the mixing probabilities are constants independent of any covariate information. Mixtures of Experts (MoE) models, first introduced by
19
, increase the flexibility of FMRs by allowing the mixing probabilities to be functions of a set of
q
covariates,
\mathbf{t}=(1,t_{1},\dots,t_{q})\in\mathbb{R}^{q+1}
, such that

\displaystyle f(y|\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t};\bm{\vartheta})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}(\mathbf{t}|\bm{\eta})f_{k}(y|\mathbf{x};\bm{\theta}_{k})

(2)

In the MoE model (
2
), the mixing probabilities
\pi_{k}(\mathbf{t}|\bm{\eta})
, known as gating functions, are typically modelled using the multinomial logistic model as

\displaystyle\pi_{k}(\mathbf{t}|\bm{\eta})=\mathbb{P}(Z=k|\mathbf{t})=\frac{\exp(\mathbf{t}^{\top}\bm{\eta}_{k})}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\exp(\mathbf{t}^{\top}\bm{\eta}_{\ell})}

(3)

where
\bm{\eta}=(\bm{\eta}_{1},\bm{\eta}_{2},\dots,\bm{\eta}_{K})
, with
\bm{\eta}_{k}=(\eta_{k0},\eta_{k1},\dots,\eta_{kq})
, is a vector of the parameters for the gating functions and
\bm{\eta}_{K}=\mathbf{0}
for identifiability. The vector of the model parameters is
\bm{\vartheta}=(\bm{\eta};\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2},\dots,\bm{\theta}_{K})
.

In the MoE model (
2
), the conditional densities
f_{k}(y|\mathbf{x};\bm{\theta}_{k})
are known as experts because different component regression models can model data in different regions of the input space. Thus, each component regression model is an expert at making predictions in its own region and the gating function assigns each component to the region where it has the most predictive power.

Due to their flexibility, MoE models have been used in a wide variety of applications including, among others, the analysis of rank data
15
, network data
16
, time-series data
46
;
6
;
14
;
34
, heavy-tailed data
32
;
8
;
28
, censored data
27
, asymmetric data
9
and functional data
43
;
7
. Detailed comprehensive reviews of the theory and application of MoE models are given in
45
,
25
and recently
31
.

For simplicity and mathematical tractability, MoE models are typically based on Gaussian experts. That is, each conditional density function
f_{k}(y|\mathbf{x};\bm{\theta}_{k})
is assumed to be Gaussian. However, in practice, data exhibit asymmetric behaviour, heavy tails and are often contaminated with outliers or atypical observations. In such cases, the use of the Gaussian distribution is inappropriate and may have a negative impact on the fit of the MoE model. This is because the Gaussian distribution is symmetric and sensitive to outliers.

The inadequacy of the Gaussian distribution for MoE modelling when the data are asymmetric, heavy-tailed and/or contaminated by outliers has been previously considered by many authors. For symmetric heavy-tailed data in the presence of outliers,
32
proposed Laplace MoE (LMoE) models in which each conditional distribution is assumed to follow a Laplace distribution;
8
proposed TMoE models in which each conditional distribution is assumed to follow a Student-t distribution;
27
proposed scale mixture of Gaussian MoE (SMGMoE) models in which each conditional distribution is assumed to follow a scale mixture of Gaussian distributions. The SMGMoE model includes the LMoE and TMoE as special cases. Another useful special case of the SMGMoE model is the contaminated Gaussian MoE model in which each conditional distribution is assumed to follow a contaminated Gaussian distribution. See
24
for a formal definition of the model and its non-parametric extension. The case of fitting MoE models for asymmetric heavy-tailed data with outliers has received limited attention. To the best of our knowledge, the only paper that investigates this case is
9
, who proposed skew-t MoE (STMoE) models in which each conditional distribution follows the skew-t distribution. However, the STMoE model is less tractable: its Expectation-step in the EM algorithm is intractable and estimation is computationally expensive.

In this paper, we propose shifted asymmetric Laplace MoE (SALMoE) models in which each conditional distribution is assumed to follow the shifted asymmetric Laplace (SAL) distribution (see Section
2.2.2
for more details). The SALMoE model provides a mathematically tractable and computationally straightforward MoE model.
12
introduced the finite mixture of SAL distributions for model-based clustering and classification of non-Gaussian (asymmetric and heavy-tailed with outliers) data. The authors demonstrated that this approach performs favourably compared to the finite mixture of Gaussian distributions.
42
proposed to use the finite mixture of SAL distributions for image segmentation in order to achieve robustness against noise and outliers in the images.
29
proposed finite mixtures of contaminated SAL distributions which extend the model proposed in
12
to allow for the automatic detection of outliers and consequently the quantification of the proportion of outliers present in the data. Note, however, that the models proposed in
12
and
29
assume that both the mixing probabilities and component distributions are independent of any covariate information. Recently,
35
proposed finite mixtures of linear SAL regression models which extend the model proposed by
29
to the regression context. However, the authors assumed that the mixing proportions are independent of any covariate information. The proposed SALMoE model considers the case in which both the mixing proportions and the component distributions are functions of a set of covariates.
Compared to the skew-t expert distributions used in
9
, the SAL expert distributions have the advantage of being mathematically tractable and computationally straightforward as shown in
12
.

Inference for MoE models can be conducted under the frequentist framework using maximum likelihood estimation (MLE).
21
derived the MLEs of the MoE model using the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm
10
.
30
proposed to use the Expectation-Conditional-Maximization (ECM) algorithm
26
to obtain the parameter estimates of the MoE model.
15
used a hybrid between the EM algorithm and the Minorization-Maximization (MM) algorithm
23
to derive the MLEs.
32
used an MM algorithm to derive the MLEs for their proposed LMoE model.

Inference for MoE models under the Bayesian framework is performed using Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods (see
36
) and variational inference methods (see
3
).

In this paper, we make use of frequentist methods. In particular, we will make use of a hybrid EM-MM algorithm to derive the parameter estimates of the proposed SALMoE model. The EM-MM algorithm replaces the computationally intensive iterative reweighted least squares (IRLS) step in the traditional EM algorithm for estimating the gating parameters
\bm{\eta}
with a one-step MM update similar to
15
. We show that the one-step MM update ensures a nondecreasing observed log-likelihood at each iteration of the EM-MM algorithm.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows. In Section
2.2
, we define the proposed SALMoE model. In Section
2.3
, we derive the MLEs via the EM-MM algorithm. In Section
2.4
, we discuss model selection for the proposed SALMoE model. In Sections
2.5
and
2.6
, we show how the model can be used for prediction and model-based clustering. In Section
3
, we demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed methods through an extensive simulation study. In Section
4
, we demonstrate the practical usefulness of the proposed methods on real data and then we conclude the paper and provide directions for future studies in Section
5
.

2.1
Notation and standing conventions

All random variables are taken to be defined on a common probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. Densities are with respect to Lebesgue measure, except for the latent component indicators, which are discrete. We write
\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\vartheta}}
,
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\vartheta}}
and
\mathbb{V}_{\bm{\vartheta}}
when the dependence on the model parameter
\bm{\vartheta}
is being emphasised, and we write
\mathbb{R}^{+}=(0,\infty)
. The covariate vectors
\mathbf{x}=(1,x_{1},\dots,x_{p})\in\mathbb{R}^{p+1}
and
\mathbf{t}=(1,t_{1},\dots,t_{q})\in\mathbb{R}^{q+1}
include intercept terms, and the corresponding supports are denoted by
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{T}
. For matrix notation,
\mathbf{A}^{\top}
denotes transpose,
\mathbf{I}_{r}
is the
r\times r
identity matrix,
\mathbf{1}
is a vector of ones of the required length,
\operatorname{diag}(\cdot)
denotes a diagonal matrix, and
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product. The symbols
\preceq
and
\succeq
denote the Loewner order. The full parameter vector of the SALMoE model is
\bm{\vartheta}=(\bm{\eta};\bm{\theta}_{1},\dots,\bm{\theta}_{K})
, where
\bm{\theta}_{k}=(\alpha_{k},\sigma_{k},\bm{\beta}_{k})
,
\sigma_{k}>0
, and the multinomial logit gating parameters satisfy the baseline constraint
\bm{\eta}_{K}=\mathbf{0}
. Thus the natural parameter space is a subset of

\Theta=\Theta_{\bm{\eta}}\times\prod_{k=1}^{K}\left(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{p+1}\right).

When compactness is needed for the convergence and information criteria statements, we work on the compact subset

\displaystyle\Theta_{c}=\{\bm{\vartheta}\in\Theta:

\displaystyle\bm{\eta}_{K}=\mathbf{0},\;\sigma_{\min}\leq\sigma_{k}\leq\sigma_{\max},\;|\alpha_{k}|\leq A,\;\|\bm{\beta}_{k}\|\leq B,\;\|\bm{\eta}_{k}\|\leq E,\;

\displaystyle k=1,\dots,K\}.

for fixed constants
0<\sigma_{\min}<\sigma_{\max}<\infty
and
A,B,E<\infty
, where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the Euclidean norm. When identifiability is invoked, the ordering and irreducibility restrictions are imposed separately.

2.2
The shifted asymmetric Laplace mixture of experts

In this section, we define the proposed SALMoE model. The proposed model is based on the stochastic representation of the SAL distribution. The latter relies on the generalised inverse Gaussian (GIG) distribution. We first recall the GIG distribution followed by the SAL distribution and its stochastic representation. Finally, we describe the hierarchical representation of the SAL distribution and use it to derive the proposed SALMoE model.

2.2.1
The generalised inverse Gaussian distribution

A random variable
W\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
is said to follow a GIG distribution if it has density

h(w)=\frac{(a/b)^{\nu/2}}{2K_{\nu}(\sqrt{ab})}w^{\nu-1}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\left(bw^{-1}+aw\right)\right\}

(4)

where
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
are the parameters. Here
K_{\nu}(\cdot)
is the modified Bessel function of the third kind with index
\nu
. One useful representation of
K_{\nu}(z)
, for
z>0
, is given by

K_{\nu}(z)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{\nu-1}\exp\left\{-\frac{z}{2}\left(s+s^{-1}\right)\right\}ds.

2.2.2
The shifted asymmetric Laplace distribution

The SAL distribution has demonstrated excellent performance in modelling asymmetric data
12
. Moreover, due to its relationship to the GIG distribution, the SAL distribution is mathematically tractable and parametric estimation is computationally straightforward. The probability density function of a random variable
y\in\mathbb{R}
that follows the SAL distribution is

g(y|\alpha,\sigma,\mu)=\frac{2\exp\{\alpha(y-\mu)/\sigma\}}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma}}\left[\frac{(y-\mu)^{2}/\sigma}{2+\alpha^{2}/\sigma}\right]^{\nu/2}K_{\nu}(u)

(5)

where
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
is the skewness parameter,
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
is the scale parameter,
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
is the location (shift) parameter,
\nu=1/2
and

u=\sqrt{(2+\alpha^{2}/\sigma)(y-\mu)^{2}/\sigma}

According to
22
, the SAL distribution can be represented as follows. Let
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma)
, and let
V\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a random variable, independent of
Z
, that follows an exponential distribution with mean
1
, that is
V\sim\text{Exp}(1)
, then

Y=\mu+\alpha V+Z\sqrt{V}

(6)

follows the SAL distribution with density function (
5
). By the conditional-density identity, the conditional distribution of
V
given
Y=y
is

f(v|Y=y)=\frac{f_{Y|V}(y|v)f_{V}(v)}{f_{Y}(y)}

(7)

where
Y|V=v\sim\mathcal{N}\{\mu+\alpha v,v\sigma\}
and
V\sim\text{Exp}(1)
. As given in
12
for the multivariate case, the expression for (
7
) in the univariate case is as follows

f(v|Y=y)=\frac{v^{\nu-1}}{2}\left[\frac{(y-\mu)^{2}/\sigma}{2+\alpha^{2}/\sigma}\right]^{-\nu/2}\frac{\exp\{-\frac{1}{2v}(y-\mu)^{2}/\sigma-\frac{v}{2}(2+\alpha^{2}/\sigma)\}}{K_{\nu}(u)}

(8)

With
a=2+\alpha^{2}/\sigma
and
b=(y-\mu)^{2}/\sigma
in (
8
), the resulting density function can then be seen to be a GIG density function in (
4
). This result will be very useful in the implementation of the estimation procedure for the proposed SALMoE model.

2.2.3
The proposed model

The SALMoE model is a regression mixture-of-experts extension of finite mixtures of SAL distributions first introduced by
12
for model-based classification and clustering of multivariate non-Gaussian data. For univariate non-Gaussian data, the probability density function of a
K
component mixture of SAL distributions is

f(y|\bm{\vartheta})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}g(y|\alpha_{k},\sigma_{k},\mu_{k})

(9)

where
\bm{\vartheta}=((\pi_{1},\bm{\theta}_{1}),(\pi_{2},\bm{\theta}_{2}),\dots,(\pi_{K},\bm{\theta}_{K}))
, with
\bm{\theta}_{k}=(\alpha_{k},\sigma_{k},\mu_{k})
, is a vector of all the model parameters and the
k^{th}
component density is given by (
5
).

29
proposed mixtures of contaminated SAL distributions for model-based clustering of asymmetric data in the presence of outliers. However, the authors assumed that the mixing proportions and the component distributions are independent of any covariate information.
35
extended the model proposed by
29
to the regression case by assuming that each component distribution is a function of a set of random covariates. However, the model assumes that the mixing proportions are constant and hence not influenced by any covariate information.

The proposed SALMoE is an MoE model with SAL distributed experts and gating functions obtained by modelling the mixing probabilities as functions of the covariates using the multinomial logistic model in (
3
). For the covariates
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{t}
defined in the standing conventions, the conditional density function of
y
is

f(y|\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t};\bm{\vartheta})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}(\mathbf{t}|\bm{\eta})g(y|\alpha_{k},\sigma_{k},\mu_{k}(\mathbf{x};\bm{\beta}_{k}))

(10)

having parameter vector
\bm{\vartheta}=(\bm{\eta};\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2},\dots,\bm{\theta}_{K})
, where
\bm{\theta}_{k}=(\alpha_{k},\sigma_{k},\bm{\beta}_{k})
is the vector of model parameters for the
k^{th}
expert and
\mu_{k}(\mathbf{x};\bm{\beta}_{k})=\mathbf{x}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{k}
, with
\bm{\beta}_{k}=(\beta_{k0},\beta_{k1},\dots,\beta_{kp})
the vector of the regression parameters for the
k^{th}
expert component.

2.2.4
Identifiability of the SALMoE model

The identifiability of an MoE model or mixture model, in general, is important in order to conduct meaningful and accurate inference.
44
and
18
established the identifiability (up to label switching) of mixture models and mixtures of regressions, respectively.

20
established the identifiability of ordered, initialized and irreducible MoE models. In the SALMoE model, initialized means that the gating functions have the multinomial logistic form in (
3
) with the baseline constraint
\bm{\eta}_{K}=\mathbf{0}
; ordered means that an ordering relation is imposed on the expert parameters
\bm{\theta}_{k}
such that
\bm{\theta}_{1}\prec\bm{\theta}_{2}\prec\dots\prec\bm{\theta}_{K}
; and irreducible means that if
k\neq j
, then at least one of
\alpha_{k}\neq\alpha_{j}
,
\sigma_{k}\neq\sigma_{j}
or
\bm{\beta}_{k}\neq\bm{\beta}_{j}
holds. The following non-degeneracy condition is the SAL analogue of the condition used in Lemma 2 of
20
.

Condition 1

(Non-degeneracy condition)
.

For any
2K
distinct triplets
(\alpha_{r},\sigma_{r},\bm{\beta}_{r})
,
r=1,2,\dots,2K
, with
\sigma_{r}>0
, the functions

(y,\mathbf{x})\mapsto g(y|\alpha_{r},\sigma_{r},\mu_{r}(\mathbf{x};\bm{\beta}_{r}))

are linearly independent on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{X}
.

Proposition 1
.

For a fixed number of components
K
, any irreducible, ordered and initialized SALMoE model satisfying Condition
1
is identifiable up to label switching. Under the imposed ordering, the model is identifiable.

Proof.

Consider two
K
-component SALMoE representations that give the same conditional density for all
(y,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{T}
. The multinomial logistic gating functions with the baseline constraint
\bm{\eta}_{K}=\mathbf{0}
satisfy the initialized gating condition in
20
. Irreducibility excludes duplicate expert components, and Condition
1
gives the required non-degeneracy of the expert family. Lemma 2 of
20
therefore implies that the two representations have the same gating and expert parameters after a common permutation of the component labels. The ordering constraint removes this remaining permutation ambiguity, giving identifiability under the imposed ordering.
∎

2.3
Estimation procedure for the SALMoE model

Consider a random sample
\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{t}_{i},y_{i}):i=1,2,\dots,n\}
generated from the model (
10
). The corresponding observed-data log-likelihood is

\ell(\bm{\vartheta})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}(\mathbf{t}_{i}|\bm{\eta})g(y_{i}|\alpha_{k},\sigma_{k},\mu_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{i};\bm{\beta}_{k}))\right\}.

(11)

The estimation of the parameter
\bm{\vartheta}
is performed by making use of a hybrid algorithm that combines the Minorization-Maximization (MM) algorithm
23
with the classical Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm
10
, which we call the EM-MM algorithm. Towards that end, for each data point
(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{t}_{i},y_{i})
, we introduce the unobserved component indicator variable
\mathbf{z}_{i}=(z_{i1},z_{i2},\dots,z_{iK})
, where
z_{ik}=1
if the data point
(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{t}_{i},y_{i})
belongs to the
k^{th}
component or
0
otherwise and the latent variable
v_{i}
which results in the complete-data
\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{t}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{i},v_{i},y_{i}):i=1,2,\dots,n\}
.

Now, we can obtain the hierarchical representation for the SALMoE model using the stochastic representation (
6
) as

\displaystyle Y_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{i},v_{i},z_{ik}=1

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{k}+v_{i}\alpha_{k},v_{i}\sigma_{k}\}

\displaystyle v_{i}|z_{ik}=1

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\text{Exp}(1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{i}|\mathbf{t}_{i}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\text{Mult}(1;\pi_{1}(\mathbf{t}_{i}|\bm{\eta}),\dots,\pi_{K}(\mathbf{t}_{i}|\bm{\eta}))

where
\text{Mult}(1;\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{m})
denotes the multinomial distribution with a single trial,
m
categories each having success probabilities
\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\dots,\phi_{m}
.

Based on the above, the corresponding complete-data log-likelihood is

\ell_{c}(\bm{\vartheta})=\ell_{c}(\bm{\eta})+\ell_{c}(\bm{\theta}),

(12)

where

\displaystyle\ell_{c}(\bm{\eta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}z_{ik}\log\{\pi_{k}(\mathbf{t}_{i}|\bm{\eta})\},

(13)

\displaystyle\ell_{c}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}z_{ik}\log\{f(y_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{i},v_{i},z_{ik}=1)\times f(v_{i}|z_{ik}=1)\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}z_{ik}\log\left[\mathcal{N}\{y_{i}|\mu_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{i};\bm{\beta}_{k})+v_{i}\alpha_{k},v_{i}\sigma_{k}\}\times\exp(-v_{i})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}z_{ik}\left[-\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma_{k}v_{i})-\frac{\{y_{i}-\mu_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{i};\bm{\beta}_{k})-v_{i}\alpha_{k}\}^{2}}{2\sigma_{k}v_{i}}-v_{i}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}z_{ik}\left[\begin{aligned} &-\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma_{k}v_{i})-\frac{\{y_{i}-\mu_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{i};\bm{\beta}_{k})\}^{2}}{2\sigma_{k}v_{i}}\\
&+\frac{\alpha_{k}\{y_{i}-\mu_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{i};\bm{\beta}_{k})\}}{\sigma_{k}}-\frac{v_{i}\alpha^{2}_{k}}{2\sigma_{k}}-v_{i}\end{aligned}\right].

(14)

The EM-MM algorithm begins with an appropriately chosen initial parameter vector
\bm{\vartheta}^{(0)}
and then alternates between the Expectation (E-) step, Maximization (M-) step and MM-step until convergence. In the E-step of the EM algorithm, we calculate the conditional expected value of the complete-data log-likelihood
\ell_{c}(\bm{\vartheta})
, denoted by
Q(\bm{\vartheta}|\bm{\vartheta}^{(old)})
, given the observed data
\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{t}_{i},y_{i}):i=1,2,\dots,n\}
and parameter estimate
\bm{\vartheta}^{(old)}
. In the M-step, we maximize
Q(\bm{\vartheta}|\bm{\vartheta}^{(old)})
with respect to
\bm{\vartheta}
.

E-step

In the E-step of the EM algorithm, we obtain the conditional expectation of
\ell_{c}(\bm{\vartheta})
as

Q(\bm{\vartheta}|\bm{\vartheta}^{(old)})=Q(\bm{\eta}|\bm{\eta}^{(old)})+Q(\bm{\theta}|\bm{\theta}^{(old)}),

(15)

where

\displaystyle Q(\bm{\eta}|\bm{\eta}^{(old)})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma^{(new)}_{ik}\log\{\pi_{k}(\mathbf{t}_{i}|\bm{\eta})\},

\displaystyle Q(\bm{\theta}|\bm{\theta}^{(old)})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma^{(new)}_{ik}\left[\begin{aligned} &-\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma_{k})-\frac{1}{2}v^{(new)}_{ik,1}-\frac{v^{(new)}_{ik,2}\{y_{i}-\mu_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{i};\bm{\beta}_{k})\}^{2}}{2\sigma_{k}}\\
&+\frac{\alpha_{k}\{y_{i}-\mu_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{i};\bm{\beta}_{k})\}}{\sigma_{k}}-\frac{\alpha^{2}_{k}}{2\sigma_{k}}v^{(new)}_{ik,3}-v^{(new)}_{ik,3}\end{aligned}\right].

(16)

where

\displaystyle\gamma^{(new)}_{ik}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\vartheta}^{(old)}}\left\{z_{ik}|\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{t}_{i},y_{i}\right\}

(17)

\displaystyle v^{(new)}_{ik,1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\vartheta}^{(old)}}\left\{\log v_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{t}_{i},y_{i},z_{ik}=1\right\}

(18)

\displaystyle v^{(new)}_{ik,2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\vartheta}^{(old)}}\left\{1/v_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{t}_{i},y_{i},z_{ik}=1\right\}

(19)

\displaystyle v^{(new)}_{ik,3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\vartheta}^{(old)}}\left\{v_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{t}_{i},y_{i},z_{ik}=1\right\}

(20)

The conditional expectation (
17
) is the well-known responsibility that the
k^{th}
expert component takes for explaining the
i^{th}
data point. This is given by the posterior probability

\displaystyle\gamma^{(new)}_{ik}=\frac{\pi_{k}(\mathbf{t}_{i}|\bm{\eta}^{(old)})g(y_{i}|\alpha^{(old)}_{k},\sigma^{(old)}_{k},\mu_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{i};\bm{\beta}^{(old)}_{k}))}{f(y_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{t}_{i};\bm{\vartheta}^{(old)})}

(21)

Notice that the conditional expectation
v^{(new)}_{ik,1}
(
18
) is a constant in the maximisation of
Q(\bm{\vartheta}\mid\bm{\vartheta}^{(old)})
with respect to
\bm{\vartheta}
. Therefore, we do not have to calculate it since it is not relevant for the purpose of obtaining the estimate of
\bm{\vartheta}
. However, it is important to note that it has a closed-form expression. This was also noted in
12
. As in
12
, the conditional expectations
v^{(new)}_{ik,2}
and
v^{(new)}_{ik,3}
are defined as follows

\displaystyle v^{(new)}_{ik,2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{a^{(old)}_{k}/b^{(old)}_{ik}}R_{\nu}\left(\sqrt{a^{(old)}_{k}b^{(old)}_{ik}}\right)-\frac{2\nu}{b^{(old)}_{ik}}

(22)

\displaystyle v^{(new)}_{ik,3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{b^{(old)}_{ik}/a^{(old)}_{k}}R_{\nu}\left(\sqrt{a^{(old)}_{k}b^{(old)}_{ik}}\right)

(23)

where
\nu=1/2
,
a^{(old)}_{k}=2+(\alpha^{(old)}_{k})^{2}/\sigma^{(old)}_{k}
,
b^{(old)}_{ik}=\{y_{i}-\mu_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{i};\bm{\beta}^{(old)}_{k})\}^{2}/\sigma^{(old)}_{k}
, for
i=1,2,\dots,n
and
k=1,2,\dots,K
, and
R_{\nu}(z)=K_{\nu+1}(z)/K_{\nu}(z)
. These expressions are used when
b^{(old)}_{ik}>0
; in numerical implementations, exact zero residuals are replaced by a small positive tolerance.

M-step

In the M-step of the EM-MM algorithm, we update the model parameters
\bm{\theta}^{(old)}
by maximizing
Q(\bm{\theta}|\bm{\theta}^{(old)})
with respect to
\bm{\beta}_{k}
,
\alpha_{k}
and
\sigma_{k}
, for
k=1,2,\dots,K
, to obtain the respective updating equations as

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}^{(new)}_{k}

\displaystyle=\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma^{(new)}_{ik}v^{(new)}_{ik,2}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}^{\top}_{i}-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma^{(new)}_{ik}\mathbf{x}_{i}\left(\sum^{n}_{j=1}\gamma_{jk}^{(new)}\mathbf{x}^{\top}_{j}\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\gamma^{(new)}_{jk}v^{(new)}_{jk,3}}\right]^{-1}

\displaystyle\quad\times\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma^{(new)}_{ik}v^{(new)}_{ik,2}\mathbf{x}_{i}y_{i}-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma^{(new)}_{ik}\mathbf{x}_{i}\left(\sum^{n}_{j=1}\gamma_{jk}^{(new)}y_{j}\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\gamma^{(new)}_{jk}v^{(new)}_{jk,3}}\right],

(24)

\displaystyle\alpha^{(new)}_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma^{(new)}_{ik}\left(y_{i}-\mathbf{x}^{\top}_{i}\bm{\beta}^{(new)}_{k}\right)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma^{(new)}_{ik}v^{(new)}_{ik,3}},

(25)

\displaystyle\sigma^{(new)}_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum^{n}_{i=1}\gamma^{(new)}_{ik}v^{(new)}_{ik,2}\left(y_{i}-\mathbf{x}^{\top}_{i}\bm{\beta}^{(new)}_{k}\right)^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma^{(new)}_{ik}}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{2\alpha^{(new)}_{k}\sum^{n}_{i=1}\gamma^{(new)}_{ik}\left(y_{i}-\mathbf{x}^{\top}_{i}\bm{\beta}^{(new)}_{k}\right)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma^{(new)}_{ik}}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\alpha^{2(new)}_{k}\sum^{n}_{i=1}\gamma^{(new)}_{ik}v^{(new)}_{ik,3}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma^{(new)}_{ik}}.

(26)

MM-step

In the MM-step, we maximise
Q(\bm{\eta}\mid\bm{\eta}^{(old)})
with
respect to the gating parameters
\bm{\eta}=(\bm{\eta}_{1},\dots,\bm{\eta}_{K})

under the identifiability constraint
\bm{\eta}_{K}=\mathbf{0}
.
Writing
\tilde{\bm{\eta}}=(\bm{\eta}_{1}^{\top},\dots,\bm{\eta}_{K-1}^{\top})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{(K-1)(q+1)}

for the free parameters, we consider the concave objective function

Q(\tilde{\bm{\eta}}\mid\bm{\eta}^{(old)})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{ik}^{(new)}\log\{\pi_{k}(\mathbf{t}_{i}\mid\bm{\eta})\},

(27)

where
\pi_{k}(\mathbf{t}_{i}\mid\bm{\eta})
is defined in
(
3
) with
\bm{\eta}_{K}=\mathbf{0}
. Note that,
in the present M-step, the weights
\gamma_{ik}^{(new)}
are fixed,
coming from the E-step.

Let
\mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(q+1)}
be the design matrix
with
i
th row
\mathbf{t}_{i}^{\top}
,
\mathbf{E}^{(old)}=[\bm{\eta}_{1}^{(old)},\dots,\bm{\eta}_{K-1}^{(old)}]\in\mathbb{R}^{(q+1)\times(K-1)}

and
\mathbf{G}^{(old)}=\mathbf{T}^{\top}(\bm{\Gamma}-\bm{\Pi}^{(old)})\in\mathbb{R}^{(q+1)\times(K-1)}
,
where
\bm{\Pi}^{(old)}=[\mathbf{p}_{1}(\tilde{\bm{\eta}}^{(old)})^{\top};\dots;\mathbf{p}_{n}(\tilde{\bm{\eta}}^{(old)})^{\top}]
, whose
i^{th}
row is
\mathbf{p}_{i}(\tilde{\bm{\eta}}^{(old)})^{\top}
, with
\mathbf{p}_{i}(\tilde{\bm{\eta}})=(\pi_{1}(\mathbf{t}_{i}\mid\bm{\eta}),\dots,\pi_{K-1}(\mathbf{t}_{i}\mid\bm{\eta}))^{\top}
, and
\bm{\Gamma}=[\bm{\gamma}_{1}^{\top};\dots;\bm{\gamma}_{n}^{\top}]
with
\bm{\gamma}_{i}=(\gamma_{i1}^{(new)},\dots,\gamma_{i,K-1}^{(new)})^{\top}
being the corresponding responsibilities.

The MM update equation is

\mathbf{E}^{(new)}=\mathbf{E}^{(old)}+2(\mathbf{T}^{\top}\mathbf{T})^{-1}\mathbf{G}^{(old)}(\mathbf{I}_{K-1}+\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}).

(28)

The derivation of the MM update equation (
28
) can be found in Appendix
A.1
.

Let
\mathcal{M}(\tilde{\bm{\eta}}\mid\tilde{\bm{\eta}}^{(old)})
be the minoriser of
Q(\tilde{\bm{\eta}}\mid\bm{\eta}^{(old)})
, then by construction,

Q(\tilde{\bm{\eta}}^{(new)}\mid\bm{\eta}^{(old)})\geq\mathcal{M}(\tilde{\bm{\eta}}^{(new)}\mid\tilde{\bm{\eta}}^{(old)})\geq\mathcal{M}(\tilde{\bm{\eta}}^{(old)}\mid\tilde{\bm{\eta}}^{(old)})=Q(\tilde{\bm{\eta}}^{(old)}\mid\bm{\eta}^{(old)}),

so a single update (
28
) increases (or leaves unchanged)
the gating contribution to the
Q
-function. In the overall EM–MM
hybrid algorithm, we perform one update for M-step 1 (the
expert parameters), and then one update for the MM-step given
above (the gating parameters). Since each of these two updates is
chosen not to decrease the relevant part of
Q(\cdot\mid\cdot)
with
the E-step weights fixed, the combined outer iteration is a valid
generalised EM step, and hence yields a nondecreasing sequence of
observed-data log-likelihood values.

The above EM-MM steps are repeated until convergence.

2.4
Model Selection

In practice, prior to estimating the SALMoE model, we need to choose
K
, the number of expert components. Data-driven procedures are usually employed for the purpose of choosing the optimal value of
K
. A popular data-driven procedure for choosing
K
is by using the likelihood-based information criteria (IC) (see Chapter 7 of
13
). In this paper, we propose to use the Bayesian information criterion (BIC)
39
,

\text{BIC}(K)=-2\times\ell(\hat{\bm{\vartheta}})+\log(n)\times\text{df}(K)

(29)

and the integrated classification likelihood (ICL)
2
,

\text{ICL}(K)=-2\times\ell_{\mathrm{cl}}(\hat{\bm{\vartheta}})+\log(n)\times\text{df}(K),

(30)

where
\ell(\hat{\bm{\vartheta}})
is the value of the observed-data log-likelihood at convergence of the EM-MM algorithm and
\ell_{\mathrm{cl}}(\hat{\bm{\vartheta}})
denotes the classification log-likelihood evaluated at the MAP allocations. Specifically, let
\hat{\gamma}_{ik}
be the fitted posterior responsibility in (
17
) evaluated at
\hat{\bm{\vartheta}}
. Then

\hat{z}_{ik}^{\mathrm{MAP}}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }k=\min\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{1\leq j\leq K}\hat{\gamma}_{ij},\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(31)

and

\ell_{\mathrm{cl}}(\hat{\bm{\vartheta}})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{z}_{ik}^{\mathrm{MAP}}\log\left\{\pi_{k}(\mathbf{t}_{i}\mid\hat{\bm{\eta}})g(y_{i}\mid\hat{\alpha}_{k},\hat{\sigma}_{k},\mu_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{i};\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{k}))\right\}.

The use of
\min
in (
31
) gives a deterministic rule for resolving ties. Also,
\text{df}(K)
is the number of estimated parameters in the fitted model. For the SALMoE,
\text{df}(K)=K(p+q+4)-q-1
.

In addition to the BIC and ICL, we use the PanIC framework of
33
, which yields consistent order selection under easily verifiable regularity conditions. In the likelihood setting, the PanIC is defined as

\text{PanIC}(K)=-2\ell(\hat{\bm{\vartheta}}_{K})+2nP_{K,n},

(32)

where

P_{K,n}=\alpha\text{df}(K)\frac{\log_{+}^{(\beta)}(n)}{\sqrt{n}},\qquad\alpha>0,

is a penalty with

\log_{+}^{(\beta)}(n)=\underbrace{\log_{+}\circ\log_{+}\circ\cdots\circ\log_{+}}_{\beta\ \text{times}}(n),\qquad\beta\in\mathbb{N},

and
\log_{+}(x)=\max\{1,\log(x)\}
.

Note that the PanIC requires a choice of the values of the constants
\alpha
and
\beta
. As in
33
, our choice of
\alpha
is coupled with the BIC. Thus, we choose
\alpha=\alpha(\beta,\nu)>0
which is a value of
\alpha
that makes the PanIC with order
\beta
to be equal to the BIC when the sample size is
\nu
, defined as:

\alpha(\beta,\nu)=\frac{\log(\nu)}{2\sqrt{\nu}\log_{+}^{(\beta)}(\nu)}

(33)

For more details about the implementation of the PanIC, see our numerical experiments in Section
3.4
.

For more details about the PanIC for the proposed SALMoE model, see Appendix
A.3
.

The optimal value of
K
is the one that corresponds to the minimum values of the
\text{BIC}(K)
,
\text{ICL}(K)
or
\text{PanIC}(K)
over a range of values of
K\in\{1,2,\dots,K_{max}\}
, where
K_{max}
is the largest value of
K
considered.

2.5
Prediction using the SALMoE model

In this section, we obtain the predictive distribution of the SALMoE model which can be used to make predictions about the response variable
y
given new values of the covariates
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t})
. We also present some useful statistical measures to assess the predictive performance of the SALMoE.

2.5.1
Predictive distribution of the SALMoE

The predictive distribution is a probability distribution of the response variable
y
, which is obtained by substituting the MLE
\hat{\bm{\vartheta}}
into (
2
) to give

\displaystyle f(y|\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t};\hat{\bm{\vartheta}})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}(\mathbf{t}|\hat{\bm{\eta}})f_{k}(y|\mathbf{x};\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{k})

(34)

From
8
;
9
, the mean of the predictive distribution (
34
) is given by

\mu_{\hat{\bm{\vartheta}}}=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\bm{\vartheta}}}[Y|\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}]=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}(\mathbf{t}|\hat{\bm{\eta}})\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\bm{\vartheta}}}[Y|Z=k,\mathbf{x}].

(35)

The corresponding variance is

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{\hat{\bm{\vartheta}}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{V}_{\hat{\bm{\vartheta}}}[Y|\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}(\mathbf{t}|\hat{\bm{\eta}})\left[(\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\bm{\vartheta}}}[Y|Z=k,\mathbf{x}])^{2}+\mathbb{V}_{\hat{\bm{\vartheta}}}(Y|Z=k,\mathbf{x})\right]-\mu^{2}_{\hat{\bm{\vartheta}}}.

(36)

where
\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\bm{\vartheta}}}[Y|Z=k,\mathbf{x}]
and
\mathbb{V}_{\hat{\bm{\vartheta}}}[Y|Z=k,\mathbf{x}]
are the mean and variance, respectively, of the
k^{th}
expert component defined as follows for the SALMoE model

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\bm{\vartheta}}}[Y|Z=k,\mathbf{x}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{\top}\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{k}+\hat{\alpha}_{k}

(37)

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{\hat{\bm{\vartheta}}}[Y|Z=k,\mathbf{x}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}^{2}_{k}+\hat{\sigma}_{k}

(38)

Substituting (
37
) and (
38
) into (
35
) and (
36
), respectively, we obtain the mean and variance of the SALMoE model.

For given values of the covariates
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{t}
, the predicted value of the response variable
y
, denoted
\hat{y}
, is given by the mean of the SALMoE model
8
, that is
\hat{y}=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\bm{\vartheta}}}[Y|\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}]
.

However, from a probabilistic perspective, a predictive distribution is typically desirable because it expresses our uncertainty about the predicted value of the response variable
y
for each of the values of the covariates
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{t}

4
.

Consider a random sample
\{(y_{i},\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{t}_{i}):i=1,2,\dots,n\}
. To quantify the uncertainty of our predictions, we will use the following approximate
95\%
pointwise predictive interval given by

\hat{y}_{i}\pm 2\times\text{sd}_{\hat{\bm{\vartheta}}}(y_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{t}_{i})\quad\text{for }i=1,2,\dots,n

(39)

where
\text{sd}_{\hat{\bm{\vartheta}}}(y_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{t}_{i})=\sqrt{\mathbb{V}_{\hat{\bm{\vartheta}}}[y_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{t}_{i}]}
, for
i=1,2,\dots,n
, is the pointwise standard deviation of the predictive distribution of the SALMoE model.

2.6
Model-based clustering using the SALMoE model

The SALMoE model can be used to obtain a partition of an observed dataset
\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{t}_{i},y_{i}):i=1,2,\dots,n\}
into
K
clusters. Each cluster is associated with a given expert component. Given
\hat{\bm{\vartheta}}
obtained at convergence of the EM-MM algorithm, the posterior probability

\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}_{ik}=\frac{\hat{\pi}_{k}(\mathbf{t}_{i}|\hat{\bm{\eta}})g(y_{i}|\hat{\alpha}_{k},\hat{\sigma}_{k},\mu_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{i};\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{k}))}{f(y_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{t}_{i};\hat{\bm{\vartheta}})}

(40)

provides a soft allocation of the data point
(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{t}_{i},y_{i})
into the
k^{th}
cluster. The corresponding hard allocation is the MAP allocation
\hat{z}_{ik}^{\mathrm{MAP}}
defined in (
31
), which estimates the latent component indicator variable
z_{ik}
.

3
Simulation study

In this section, we perform an extensive numerical study:

1.

to evaluate the performance of the proposed EM-MM algorithm for fitting the proposed SALMoE model;

2.

to demonstrate the robustness of the SALMoE model compared to the GMoE model.

3.

to identify the most suitable information criteria for choosing the number of expert components for the SALMoE model; and

4.

to evaluate the clustering capability of the SALMoE using the approach in Section
2.6
.

Initialization

The parameters of the SALMoE model,
\bm{\vartheta}
, are initialized using a similar approach outlined in
8
;
9
. The data are randomly partitioned into
K
groups and a GMoE model with
K
components is fitted to the data. We use the estimates of the component standard deviations to initialize the scale parameters
\sigma_{k}
, for
k=1,2,\dots,K
. The skewness parameters
\alpha_{k}
, for
k=1,2,\dots,K
, are initialized similarly to the skewness parameters of the STMoE model of
9
. This process is repeated for 30 times and the parameter estimates that produce the largest likelihood value are used to initialize the algorithm.

Stopping rule

To assess convergence, we use the relative log-likelihood increment

\frac{\ell(\bm{\vartheta}^{(new)})-\ell(\bm{\vartheta}^{(old)})}{|\ell(\bm{\vartheta}^{(old)})|}.

(41)

Convergence is declared whenever (
41
) is less than a prespecified threshold
\epsilon
. In our simulations and real data analysis, we set
\epsilon=10^{-5}
.

Robustness performance

. The results of the experiment are given in Figure

5
Conclusion

This paper proposes a new mixture of experts model for non-Gaussian data (i.e. skewed and possibly heavy-tailed data with outliers) using the shifted asymmetric Laplace distribution for the expert distributions. The model is termed the SALMoE model. To fit the model, we develop a hybrid algorithm combining the classical Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm and the minorization-maximization (MM) algorithm for estimating the parameters of the gating functions. A theoretical analysis of the convergence of the hybrid EM-MM algorithm was conducted. An intensive simulation study showed the robustness of the proposed SALMoE model under different scenarios involving skewed and heavy-tailed data. To choose the number of expert components for the SALMoE model, we considered the BIC, ICL and PanIC. Based on a numerical study that considered a variety of scenarios, the three approaches provided comparable results. To demonstrate the practical usefulness of the proposed methods, we applied them to two real datasets.

In this paper, we assumed that the gating functions and the component regression functions are parametric functions of the covariates. However, in practice, the parametric assumption may not hold. A natural future extension of this work is to consider nonparametric gating functions and component regression functions. Another possible extension of the current work is to consider the multivariate response variable case.

A.1
Derivation of the MM update for the gating parameters

For
h=1,\dots,K-1
, a standard calculation gives

\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{\eta}_{h}}Q(\tilde{\bm{\eta}}\mid\bm{\eta}^{(old)})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\gamma_{ih}^{(new)}-\pi_{h}(\mathbf{t}_{i}\mid\bm{\eta})\right\}\mathbf{t}_{i}.

(44)

Stacking the blocks yields the score vector

\bm{g}(\tilde{\bm{\eta}})=\frac{\partial}{\partial\tilde{\bm{\eta}}}Q(\tilde{\bm{\eta}}\mid\bm{\eta}^{(old)})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\bm{\gamma}_{i}-\mathbf{p}_{i}(\tilde{\bm{\eta}})\right\}\otimes\mathbf{t}_{i}.

(45)

Moreover, the Hessian has the Kronecker form

\mathbf{H}(\tilde{\bm{\eta}})=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\tilde{\bm{\eta}}\,\partial\tilde{\bm{\eta}}^{\top}}Q(\tilde{\bm{\eta}}\mid\bm{\eta}^{(old)})=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\mathbf{A}_{\mathbf{p}_{i}(\tilde{\bm{\eta}})}-\mathbf{p}_{i}(\tilde{\bm{\eta}})\mathbf{p}_{i}(\tilde{\bm{\eta}})^{\top}\right\}\otimes(\mathbf{t}_{i}\mathbf{t}_{i}^{\top}),

(46)

where
\mathbf{A}_{\mathbf{p}}=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{p})
.

Let
\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{K-1}
be the vector of ones
and define the symmetric matrix

\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{I}_{K-1}-\frac{1}{K}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}.

(47)

The multinomial logistic regression bound of
5

implies that, for every
\mathbf{p}\in(0,1)^{K-1}
arising from a

K
-category multinomial logit model with a baseline category,

\mathbf{A}_{\mathbf{p}}-\mathbf{p}\mathbf{p}^{\top}\preceq\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{C},

(48)

in the Loewner ordering. Combining (
46
) and (
48
)
and using the property that
\mathbf{M}\preceq\mathbf{N}
and
\mathbf{S}\succeq\mathbf{0}

imply
\mathbf{M}\otimes\mathbf{S}\preceq\mathbf{N}\otimes\mathbf{S}
,
we obtain the global Hessian bound

\mathbf{H}(\tilde{\bm{\eta}})\succeq\mathbf{B}\quad\text{for all }\tilde{\bm{\eta}},\qquad\mathbf{B}=-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{C}\otimes(\mathbf{T}^{\top}\mathbf{T}).

(49)

Assuming
\mathbf{T}^{\top}\mathbf{T}
is nonsingular (full column
rank gating design),
\mathbf{B}
is negative definite.

We now construct a proper MM step around the current gating iterate

\tilde{\bm{\eta}}^{(old)}
. By a second-order Taylor expansion,
for each
\tilde{\bm{\eta}}
there exists
a\in(0,1)
such
that

\displaystyle Q(\tilde{\bm{\eta}}\mid\bm{\eta}^{(old)})

\displaystyle=Q(\tilde{\bm{\eta}}^{(old)}\mid\bm{\eta}^{(old)})+(\tilde{\bm{\eta}}-\tilde{\bm{\eta}}^{(old)})^{\top}\bm{g}(\tilde{\bm{\eta}}^{(old)})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{\bm{\eta}}-\tilde{\bm{\eta}}^{(old)})^{\top}\mathbf{H}\!\left(\tilde{\bm{\eta}}^{(old)}+a(\tilde{\bm{\eta}}-\tilde{\bm{\eta}}^{(old)})\right)(\tilde{\bm{\eta}}-\tilde{\bm{\eta}}^{(old)}).

(50)

Applying the global bound (
49
) inside (
50
)
yields the quadratic minoriser

\displaystyle Q(\tilde{\bm{\eta}}\mid\bm{\eta}^{(old)})\geq\mathcal{M}(\tilde{\bm{\eta}}\mid\tilde{\bm{\eta}}^{(old)})=Q(\tilde{\bm{\eta}}^{(old)}\mid\bm{\eta}^{(old)})+(\tilde{\bm{\eta}}-\tilde{\bm{\eta}}^{(old)})^{\top}\bm{g}(\tilde{\bm{\eta}}^{(old)})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{\bm{\eta}}-\tilde{\bm{\eta}}^{(old)})^{\top}\mathbf{B}(\tilde{\bm{\eta}}-\tilde{\bm{\eta}}^{(old)}),

with equality at
\tilde{\bm{\eta}}=\tilde{\bm{\eta}}^{(old)}
.
Since
\mathbf{B}
is negative definite,
\mathcal{M}(\cdot\mid\tilde{\bm{\eta}}^{(old)})

is strictly concave and has the unique maximiser

\tilde{\bm{\eta}}^{(new)}=\arg\max_{\tilde{\bm{\eta}}}\,\mathcal{M}(\tilde{\bm{\eta}}\mid\tilde{\bm{\eta}}^{(old)})=\tilde{\bm{\eta}}^{(old)}-\mathbf{B}^{-1}\bm{g}(\tilde{\bm{\eta}}^{(old)}).

(51)

Equivalently, using
\mathbf{B}^{-1}=-2\,\mathbf{C}^{-1}\otimes(\mathbf{T}^{\top}\mathbf{T})^{-1}
,

\tilde{\bm{\eta}}^{(new)}=\tilde{\bm{\eta}}^{(old)}+2\left\{\mathbf{C}^{-1}\otimes(\mathbf{T}^{\top}\mathbf{T})^{-1}\right\}\bm{g}(\tilde{\bm{\eta}}^{(old)}).

(52)

A convenient closed form for
\mathbf{C}^{-1}
is

\mathbf{C}^{-1}=\left(\mathbf{I}_{K-1}-\frac{1}{K}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\right)^{-1}=\mathbf{I}_{K-1}+\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top},

(53)

which follows by direct multiplication. Writing the expression (
52
) in matrix form gives rise to the one-step update equation (
28
) for
\bm{\eta}
.

A.2
Convergence of the EM-MM algorithm

Let
\ell(\bm{\vartheta})
denote the observed-data log-likelihood, and set
f(\bm{\vartheta})=-\ell(\bm{\vartheta})
,
where
\bm{\vartheta}=(\bm{\theta},\bm{\eta})

with
\bm{\theta}=\{(\bm{\beta}_{k},\alpha_{k},\sigma_{k})\}_{k=1}^{K}

and
\bm{\vartheta}\in\Theta
, with
\Theta
as in the standing conventions.
We say that
\bm{\vartheta}^{\star}
is a stationary point of the minimisation problem for
f
on
\Theta
if the directional derivative
f^{\prime}(\bm{\vartheta}^{\star};\mathbf{d})
is nonnegative for every feasible direction
\mathbf{d}
at
\bm{\vartheta}^{\star}
.

At the current iterate
\bm{\vartheta}^{(m)}
, the E-step
defines the standard EM surrogate

Q(\bm{\vartheta}\mid\bm{\vartheta}^{(m)})=Q_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{\vartheta}^{(m)})+Q_{\bm{\eta}}(\bm{\eta}\mid\bm{\vartheta}^{(m)}),

where
Q_{\bm{\theta}}
and
Q_{\bm{\eta}}
are the expert and gating contributions, respectively. By the usual EM Jensen argument (see
38
),
there exists an upper-bound function

u_{m}(\bm{\vartheta})=-Q(\bm{\vartheta}\mid\bm{\vartheta}^{(m)})+\text{const}(\bm{\vartheta}^{(m)})

(54)

such that
f(\bm{\vartheta})\leq u_{m}(\bm{\vartheta})

for all
\bm{\vartheta}\in\Theta
and
f(\bm{\vartheta}^{(m)})=u_{m}(\bm{\vartheta}^{(m)})
.
Thus, any update that decreases
u_{m}
yields a valid MM (more precisely,
GEM) descent step for
f
.

Let
\mathcal{M}_{m}(\bm{\eta})=\mathcal{M}(\bm{\eta}\mid\bm{\eta}^{(m)})
denote the quadratic minoriser of
Q_{\bm{\eta}}(\bm{\eta}\mid\bm{\vartheta}^{(m)})
obtained in Appendix
A.1
. Then, for all
\bm{\eta}\in\Theta_{\bm{\eta}}
,

\mathcal{M}_{m}(\bm{\eta})\leq Q_{\bm{\eta}}(\bm{\eta}\mid\bm{\vartheta}^{(m)}),

with equality and first-order agreement at
\bm{\eta}=\bm{\eta}^{(m)}
. Since replacing
-Q_{\bm{\eta}}
by
-\mathcal{M}_{m}
gives an upper bound for the gating part of
f
, define

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{m}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\eta})

\displaystyle=u_{m}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\eta})+\left\{Q_{\bm{\eta}}(\bm{\eta}\mid\bm{\vartheta}^{(m)})-\mathcal{M}_{m}(\bm{\eta})\right\}

\displaystyle=-Q_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{\vartheta}^{(m)})-\mathcal{M}_{m}(\bm{\eta})+\text{const}(\bm{\vartheta}^{(m)}).

(55)

Because
Q_{\bm{\eta}}(\bm{\eta}\mid\bm{\vartheta}^{(m)})-\mathcal{M}_{m}(\bm{\eta})\geq 0
, we have
\tilde{u}_{m}(\bm{\vartheta})\geq u_{m}(\bm{\vartheta})\geq f(\bm{\vartheta})
for all
\bm{\vartheta}\in\Theta
. Moreover, by the tightness of the EM surrogate and the minoriser,

\tilde{u}_{m}(\bm{\vartheta}^{(m)})=f(\bm{\vartheta}^{(m)})
.

The EM-MM update can now be written as the minimisation of the separable upper bound (
55
). In particular,

\bm{\theta}^{(m+1)}\in\arg\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta_{\bm{\theta}}}\{-Q_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{\vartheta}^{(m)})\},

(56)

and

\bm{\eta}^{(m+1)}\in\arg\min_{\bm{\eta}\in\Theta_{\bm{\eta}}}\{-\mathcal{M}_{m}(\bm{\eta})\}.

(57)

The first update is exactly M-step 1. The second update is equivalent to maximising the quadratic minoriser
\mathcal{M}_{m}
and therefore gives the one-step update (
51
). Consequently,

f(\bm{\vartheta}^{(m+1)})\leq\tilde{u}_{m}(\bm{\vartheta}^{(m+1)})\leq\tilde{u}_{m}(\bm{\vartheta}^{(m)})=f(\bm{\vartheta}^{(m)}),

or equivalently,
\ell(\bm{\vartheta}^{(m)})
is nondecreasing.

We next record the standard conditions under which the above monotone MM scheme has stationary limit points. The upper bound (
55
) satisfies the usual tightness, global upper-bound, first-order agreement and continuity conditions used in the BSUM framework of
38
. Indeed, tightness and the global upper-bound property follow from the construction above; first-order agreement follows from the first-order agreement of the EM surrogate together with the first-order tightness of
\mathcal{M}_{m}
at
\bm{\eta}^{(m)}
; and continuity follows from the continuity of the E-step expectations and the quadratic form of
\mathcal{M}_{m}
.

To apply the convergence results of
38
, we assume the usual additional conditions: (i) the level set
\{\bm{\vartheta}\in\Theta_{c}:f(\bm{\vartheta})\leq f(\bm{\vartheta}^{(0)})\}
is compact in the compact parameter set
\Theta_{c}
defined in the standing conventions, (ii)
f
is regular on this level set in the sense of
38
, and (iii) the relevant block subproblems have unique minimisers, or ties are resolved by a closed deterministic rule. The regularity assumption is not automatic for nonsmooth nonconvex objectives, so we impose it explicitly. It is nevertheless mild in the present setting because
f
is directionally differentiable and piecewise smooth, with possible kinks arising only on residual-zero hyperplanes. Under this regularity assumption, coordinate-wise stationarity of the block updates implies stationarity in the directional sense defined above. Uniqueness holds for the gating block whenever
\mathbf{T}^{\top}\mathbf{T}
is nonsingular, since then the quadratic bound in M-step 2 is strictly convex, and holds for the expert block under the corresponding weighted nonsingularity conditions in M-step 1.

Under these conditions, the BSUM convergence results of
38
imply that every limit point of the outer EM-MM iterates
\{\bm{\vartheta}^{(m)}\}
is a stationary point of the observed-data maximum likelihood problem. Equivalently, if
S^{\star}
denotes the set of stationary points of
f
, then

\lim_{m\to\infty}d(\bm{\vartheta}^{(m)},S^{\star})=0.

A.3
Some details about the implementation of the PanIC

In the likelihood setting, the PanIC can be applied by taking the per-observation loss to be the negative log-likelihood, and using a penalty that separates models by a slowly diverging factor. A particularly simple choice is the Sin–White information criterion (SWIC)
40
. The SWIC penalty is defined as follows

P_{K,n}=\alpha\text{df}(K)\frac{\log_{+}^{(\beta)}(n)}{\sqrt{n}},\qquad\alpha>0,

where

\log_{+}^{(\beta)}(n)=\underbrace{\log_{+}\circ\log_{+}\circ\cdots\circ\log_{+}}_{\beta\ \text{times}}(n),\qquad\beta\in\mathbb{N},

with
\log_{+}(x)=\max\{1,\log(x)\}
.

The corresponding SWIC (on the
-2\ell
scale) is

\displaystyle\text{SWIC}_{\beta}(K)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\ell(\hat{\bm{\vartheta}}_{K})+2nP_{K,n}

(58)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\ell(\hat{\bm{\vartheta}}_{K})+2\alpha\text{df}(K)\sqrt{n}\log_{+}^{(\beta)}(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{PanIC}(K).

Thus, the SWIC is a member of the class of PanICs. With the calibration in (
33
), the penalty term equals
\text{df}(K)\log(\nu)
when
n=\nu
, so the criterion is on the same scale as the BIC at the reference sample size.

Note that the PanIC theory requires, for each fixed
K
, that the parameter space is compact and that the per-observation loss is Carathéodory and Lipschitz in the parameters with an
L^{2}
envelope. In our setting, these conditions are verified on the compact parameter set
\Theta_{c}
defined in the standing conventions, together with mild moment conditions on
(Y,\mathbf{X},\mathbf{T})
. Under these conditions, the SAL expert contribution is piecewise smooth in the regression parameters (the only kinks occur on residual-zero hyperplanes through absolute-value terms), the gating contribution is smooth, and hence the negative log-likelihood is Lipschitz in
\bm{\vartheta}
with a square-integrable Lipschitz envelope. Therefore the assumptions of
33
apply, and the resulting SWIC selector is consistent for the parsimonious value of
K
under the usual order-selection condition that the minimal expected risk is attained at some finite
K^{\star}
and does not improve for larger
K
. Establishing analogous consistency statements for the BIC and ICL in the present SALMoE setting would require separate regularity and identifiability arguments for these criteria, and is beyond the scope of this paper.
