A new Chandra look at the globular cluster NGC 6540 and its peculiar X-ray flaring source
A. Sacchi, S. Mereghetti, R. Di Stefano, J. A. Irwin, M. Rigoselli, A. De Luca, N. Sims

Abstract
We report the results of a deep ( ≈ 65 \approx 65 ks) Chandra observation of the globular cluster NGC 6540, obtained to investigate the nature of the peculiar X-ray source 3XMM J180608.9–274553. This source was previously observed with XMM–Newton to exhibit a short ( ≈ 300 \approx 300 s) and intense X-ray flare whose luminosity and duration are inconsistent with both typical type I X-ray bursts from low mass X-ray binaries and stellar flares. Our new data show the presence of three faint X-ray sources near the position of the flare seen by XMM–Newton , only one of which was detected in a previous, much shorter Chandra observation. Based on the properties of these sources, localized at sub-arcsecond precision, and of their optical counterparts, we discuss their possible nature and association with 3XMM J180608.9–274553. We also discuss some scenarios to explain the X-ray flare, such as microlensing-induced amplification, black hole flaring activity analogous to that observed from Sgr A ∗ . Our results place new constraints on the nature of this unusual transient and highlight the power of high-resolution X-ray observations for disentangling rare phenomena in the dense stellar environments of globular clusters.

3.1
Refined astrometry

We corrected the astrometry of the merged dataset by cross-matching the
Chandra
point sources against a higher-precision catalog. We used the 2 micron all sky survey catalog
(2MASS,
Skrutskie et al. 2006
, the same used as reference for the Hubble Space Telescope (HST) astrometry in
Mereghetti et al. 2018
)
. To this end, we employed five
Chandra
sources. After this correction, we identified the centroid of the
Chandra
sources by fitting a 2D Gaussian profile (mimicking the PSF), with free amplitude and location, and scale factor fixed at the PSF size at the approximate location of the source. The derived positions are given in Table
2
.
The errors on these positions are dominated by the uncertainty on the astrometry (
1\sigma
error of
0.075^{\prime\prime}
on R.A. and
0.099^{\prime\prime}
on Dec.), which is larger than the statistical errors derived from the Gaussian fits.

3.2
Spectral properties

The limited photon statistics collected for the three sources in the
J1806
region does not allow meaningful spectral modeling. Hence, we characterize their spectral properties using hardness ratios. We define the hardness ratio as
\mathrm{HR}=(H-S)/(H+S)
, where
S
and
H
are the net counts measured in the soft (0.5–2 keV) and hard (2–7 keV) energy bands, respectively. Given the low-count regime, uncertainties were computed using the Bayesian Estimation of Hardness Ratios code
(
Park et al. 2006
, BEHR)
, which properly accounts for Poisson statistics and background subtraction.

The three sources display different spectral properties. Source C exhibits a hard spectrum with
\mathrm{HR}=0.5^{+0.5}_{-0.5}
. This value indicates that nearly all detected counts are concentrated in the hard band. Sources A and B show comparatively softer emission, sharing the same hardness ratios of
\mathrm{HR}=-0.2^{+0.6}_{-0.8}
.

We estimated the source fluxes by converting their count rates assuming power law spectra, with column density of
N_{\textup{H}}=2.64\times 10^{21}
cm
-2
, corresponding to the Galactic value in that direction
(
HI4PI Collaboration et al. 2016
)
. To reflect the different HR values, the power law photon index was fixed at 2 for sources A and B, while a value of 1.5 was assumed for source C. Additionally, to ease the comparison with the results presented in
Mereghetti et al. 2018
, we estimated the fluxes in the
0.5-10
keV band employing the same powerlaw model they adopted to fit the quiescence of
J1806
(
N_{\textup{H}}=6\times 10^{21}
cm
-2
and
\Gamma=2.5
). For all three sources, the fluxes inferred with the two methods have the same numerical values, as assuming a more absorbed and steeper spectrum is balanced by considering a larger energy band.

Source A is the brightest one, with
F_{0.5-7\,\mathrm{keV}}\approx 1.5\times 10^{-14}
erg cm
-2
s
-1
, corresponding to
L_{X}\approx 3\times 10^{31}\penalty\ d^{2}_{\rm{4kpc}}
erg s
-1
. Source B is the faintest one and is detected only in the August 2024 observation, with a flux
F_{0.5-7\,\mathrm{keV}}\approx 8.6\times 10^{-15}
erg cm
-2
s
-1
. In the previous observations the source is not detected and the
3\sigma
upper limits on its flux, obtained merging the two 2023 observations, is
F_{0.5-7\,\mathrm{keV}}<1.6\times 10^{-15}
erg cm
-2
s
-1
, corresponding to a variability of a factor of
\approx 5
. Source C has a flux of
F_{0.5-7\,\mathrm{keV}}\approx 1.0\times 10^{-14}
erg cm
-2
s
-1
. The non-detection of sources B and C in the short 2008 observation is consistent with their fluxes observed in 2023-2024.

3.3
Timing properties

The small number of counts detected from sources A, B, and C does not allow a detailed study of their possible variability.
No statistically significant long-term variations were seen in sources A and C by comparing their fluxes measured in the different observations. We searched for variability on short timescales in the longest observation (2024) by comparing the distributions of time of arrivals of the three sources with that expected for a constant flux using a Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) test.
The resulting probabilities that the flux is constant were 0.11, 0.19, and 0.24 for sources A, B, and C, respectively.

3.4
Position of
J1806

To assess the exact position of the flaring source
J1806
we reanalysed the
XMM–Newton
data using a maximum likelihood (ML) method as described in
Rigoselli & Mereghetti 2018
, in order to properly take into account the presence of sources N and S.
We based our analysis on the data of the EPIC-MOS cameras in the 0.5–5 keV energy range, as the EPIC-pn data presented a gap between CCDs just in correspondence of source N.

We first performed a ML fit using the whole observation to simultaneously derive the positions of sources
J1806
, N, and S in instrumental coordinates. These were then registered using the
Chandra
positions of sources N and S given in Table
2
. We finally applied the ML fit to the image corresponding only to the time interval of the flare to obtain the position of
J1806
: R.A. = 271.5379, Dec. =
-27.7653
(1
\sigma
error of 0.75” on R.A. and 0.77” on Dec.). Applying the same analysis procedure to different energy ranges and to the EPIC-pn data, we obtained consistent results.

4
Discussion

Figure
3
shows the positions of sources A, B, and C plotted on the optical HST image of the central part of the globular cluster
NGC 6540
. We also indicate in the figure the error region of
J1806
derived above (sect.
3.4
), as well as the position of source A as previously reported in
Mereghetti et al. 2018
.
The improved location accuracy of our new
Chandra
data reduces the number of possible optical counterparts of source A, which, considering its position and brightness, is the most likely origin of the 2005 flare seen by
XMM–Newton
.
Its flux measured with
Chandra
is lower than the quiescent level seen with
XMM–Newton
, implying long-term variability by a factor
\sim 7\pm 2
. However, the quiescent flux of
J1806
was probably contaminated by the contributions of sources B and C, which could not be resolved with the angular resolution of
XMM–Newton
. Taking this possibility into account, no strong claims for long-term variability can be made.

Independent of which of the
Chandra
sources is the quiescent counterpart of
J1806
, the properties of the 2005 flare defy an easy classification. Its remarkably symmetric time profile, with virtually identical rise and decay times (see Fig.
4
), is reminiscent of variability caused by gravitational lensing. We explored this possibility,
examining in particular the case of self-lensing in a binary system containing a compact object.

Using a ML method to properly account for the small number of counts in each bin, we fitted the flare light curve with the magnification model expected in the approximation of a point-like source and lens.
The best fit parameters derived with the microlensing model are
\tau_{e}=R_{E}/v=906
s and
u_{0}
=0.012, where
R_{E}
is the Einstein radius,
v
is the lens transverse velocity with respect to the Earth-source direction, and
u_{0}
is the impact parameter (see, e.g.,
Paczynski 1986
;
Gould 1992
).
However, the best fit (red line in Fig.
4
) does not reproduce well the observed profile, which is instead much better described by a Gaussian curve (blue line).

Beyond the goodness of fit issue is the challenge of creating a physical model within which binary self-lensing can produce an event with the observed properties. The above best-fit parameters imply orbital periods longer than 200 d for a neutron star lens (or more than 100 d if the lens is a black hole of 10
M_{\odot}
).
Would such a wide binary survive in the dense central region of a globular cluster? Only a subset of those consistent with the light curve fit are “hard” binaries, with orbital velocities larger than the ambient stellar velocities. Hard binaries are less likely to be torn apart by interactions with other stellar systems in the dense environment near the cluster center, but developing consistent physical models for such systems is even more challenging.
Furthermore, the high required system inclination, extremely low chance probability of observing at the right orbital phase, and possible hints of spectral variability between flare and quiescence, lead us to discard the binary self-lensing hypothesis.

The symmetry of the rise and fall times of the X-ray flare observed by
XMM-Newton
is unusual,
as most X-ray flares observed in a galaxy have a fast-rise/exponential decay (FRED) shape, indicative
of some sort of fast energy injection describing the rise in luminosity and slower thermodynamic
cooling describing the decline in luminosity. Very few flare mechanisms can lead to a symmetric
profile other than the lensing model described above. However, symmetric X-ray flares are sometimes
seen from the supermassive black hole Sgr A
⋆
(
Porquet et al. 2008
;
Neilsen et al. 2013
;

Haggard et al. 2019
). At first glance, it appears possible that the X-ray flare seen in NGC 6540
could be a scaled-down version of the symmetric flares seen in Sgr A
⋆
but from an
intermediate-mass black hole (IMBH) in NGC 6540. The energy output from representative flares of Sgr
A
⋆
(
0.7-3.3\times 10^{39}
erg;
Haggard et al. 2019
) is about 700-3000 times greater than the
total energy output of the J1806 flare, which would imply an IMBH mass of a few tens of thousands of
solar masses, a reasonable value for an expected IMBH in a globular cluster. In addition, the fitted
power law X-ray index of the spectra of the quiescent and flare emission of Sgr A
⋆
of

\Gamma=3.0\pm 0.2
and
2.0\pm 0.2
, respectively (
Haggard et al. 2019
), are remarkably similar
to the quiescent and flare emission power law values of J1806 of
\Gamma=2.5\pm 0.5
and

1.7\pm 0.2
, respectively (
Mereghetti et al. 2018
).

However, there are two major inconsistencies with an IMBH interpretation for the J1806 flare. The most striking feature is that J1806 is 7
′′
(0.14 pc) from the dynamical center of
NGC 6540
. Although the cluster is rather heavily obscured and somewhat ill-defined in shape, it seems unlikely that the error in the position center could be as large as 7
′′
, and dynamical friction would very quickly cause a 10,000 M
⊙
black hole to sink to the dynamical center of the cluster. Also, if the emission mechanism causing the
\sim
1 hour flares seen in Sgr A
⋆
(interpreted as magnetic recombination near the innermost stable circular orbit (ISCO) of the black hole) is scaled down by mass would imply a flare duration of a 10,000 M
⊙
black hole of only
\sim
15 seconds, far shorter than the observed
\sim
5 minute duration of J1806’s flare. Although a flare emanating from material at a larger radius (
\sim
100 gravitational radii) in the disk of a 10,000 M
⊙
black hole could have an orbital period of five minutes, such a flare would cool radiatively (instead of adiabatic expansion and rapid synchrotron radiation as is the case for Sgr A
⋆
) and therefore exhibit a FRED profile. Thus, an explanation involving a Sgr A
⋆
-like flare from an IMBH in NGC 6540 has significant issues.

The fact that source A has two neighbors within 4
′′
is surprising given that there are only
nine sources within 1
′
of the center of the cluster. Two of the nine sources are likely
foreground/background sources for the 1
′
region considered (
Lehmer et al. 2012
), leaving
\sim
7
sources associated with the cluster itself. We performed 100,000 simulations of randomly placing seven
sources in a 1
′
region according to a King profile with a core radius of 1.8
′′

(appropriate for NGC 6540;
Harris 1996
). We found that only
\sim
1% of the simulations contained
such a grouping of three sources located at a distance of at least three core radii from the cluster
center. Although this could simply be fortuitous, the grouping of two highly variable sources in such
close proximity to a seemingly unique X-ray flaring source is intriguing.

5
Conclusions

In this work, we have presented a re-analysis of the X-ray emission from
NGC 6540
, exploiting both archival
XMM–Newton
data and
\approx 65
ks of new
Chandra
observations. The superior angular resolution of
Chandra
is critical here: it resolves what appeared as a single
XMM–Newton
source into three distinct sources (A, B, and C), s the most likely counterpart of the 2005 flare, and demonstrates that the quiescent
XMM–Newton
flux was likely the result of blended emission from the newly resolved sources. The origin of the remarkably symmetric X-ray flare, however, remains unidentified. A binary self-lensing event is disfavoured by the unphysical orbital parameters, low
a priori
probability it requires, as well as by the hints of spectral variability of the flare with respect to the quiescent emission. We considered an IMBH interpretation, however this is inconsistent with the source’s offset from the dynamical centre of the cluster and the expected flare timescale. Despite this lack of a clear physical explanation, our work represents a significant step forward in characterising the X-ray source population of
NGC 6540
, disentangling blended emission that was previously unresolvable, and placing meaningful constraints on the nature of one of the most unusual X-ray flares observed in a Galactic globular cluster. This ongoing puzzle calls for dedicated theoretical modelling efforts to identify viable physical mechanisms capable of reproducing the observed phenomenology.
