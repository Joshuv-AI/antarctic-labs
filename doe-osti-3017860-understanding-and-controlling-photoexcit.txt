Executive summary

Predicting the outcome of a photochemical reaction is complicated by factors outside the scope of tradition theoretical
chemistry. On such small scales, fluctuations of the environment render outcomes stochastic, nonadiabatic dynamics
blur the separation between nuclear and electronic motion, and ultrafast relaxation renders typical assumptions of
equilibrium inappropriate. This project aimed to elucidate and control nonadiabatic molecular dynamics in condensed
phases. We developed accurate, efficient simulation tools to study irreversible molecular processes computational
across a range of scales. We applied these methodological advances to study electron transfer under exotic conditions
where spin and topology opened new pathways for reactivity. We also brought these ideas to bare on light harvesting
systems, where energy and charge transport are coupled, and where long lifetimes can be deleterious to function. These
advances helped to establish general principles for chemical efficiency and guide the design of nanoscale energy
materials, molecular machines, and related technologies.

PROJECT GOALS

The major goals of this project concern the study of nonadiabatic dynamics in complex, condensed phase
environments. Specifically, we aim to develop a perspective and accompanying set of tools to predict the efficiencies,
yields and mechanisms of photoinduced reactions. Progress in this area is historically complicated by the lack of
formal and numerical tools capable of describing small, nonequilibrium and quantum systems generally, and
especially when there exists a hierarchy of time and energy scales characterizing their dynamics. The specific goals
below address both of these points by synergistically building numerical tools to study concrete chemical systems,
and using those tools to benchmark and hypothesis test simplifying theories.

The first goal was to develop effective models for condensed phase quantum dynamics that are accurate and provide
fundamental insight into diverse sets of problems. Our effort here was aimed at making physically motivated
simplifications to reduce complex systems into simple representations, and to propose equations of motion that
tractable and physically consistent thermodynamic constraints. Simple representations are deduced and derived by
considering natural locality and energetic scales. Effective equations of motion employ such compact representations
and unite them with descriptions of additional environmental degrees of freedom. The result is a compromise between
exact quantum mechanical evolution with explicit wavepacket propagation or electron correlation for some degrees
of freedom with quantum master equation models for the environment that obey weak coupling and Markovian
approximations consistently.

The second specific goal was to develop importance sampling algorithms for nonadiabatic and nonequilibrium
processes. These will was used synergistically with the effective models for condensed phase quantum dynamics
developed in the first goal. The basis for these algorithms is transition path sampling, which has been widely used in
simulations of thermal reactions in condensed phases to provided assumption free calculations of rate constants, and
importantly to admit systematic understanding of the mechanisms of reactions. Their use away from equilibrium has
been recently pioneered, and their extension into fundamental quantum dynamical processes has been limited. This is
because the initial algorithms required detailed balance dynamics, which is not satisfied away from equilibrium or in
many existing approximation to quantum dynamics. We aimed to develop generalizations of transition path sampling
algorithms for studying rare nonadiabatic dynamics like those associated with rare relaxation pathways during
photoisomerization or improbable energy transfer routes in couple chromophores. These were based on stochastic
unravellings of quantum master equations.

The third goal was to develop a formalism to distill the simulations results and experimental observations and enable
general theoretical statements to be made. This effort will employ the formalism of stochastic thermodynamics, in
which thermodynamic variables are endowed with a stochastic dynamics, and quantum information, in which
thermodynamic like bounds are derived to constrain the processes of individual quantum systems. Ultimate goals of
this line of inquiry are explicit equations for the prediction of optimal yields or rates of photochemical reactions, or
response expressions for quantum systems away from equilibrium.

ACCOMPLISHMENTS

Over the course of this grant, we made significant progress on each of the goals. Additionally, we found unexpected
opportunities to apply our newly developed techniques to questions in spin and electron transfer, as well as to help
understand nonphotochemical quenching in naturally occurring light harvesting complexes. Below we detail our
progress in three areas 1) the development of quantum path sampling algorithms, 2) the study of spin and electron
transfer in exotic domains and 3) the study of energy and charge transfer in light harvesting systems.

1. Development of quantum path sampling algorithms
We have recently developed a means of studying rare reactive pathways in open quantum systems using Transition
Path Theory and ensembles of quantum jump trajectories. Specifically, we have taken a hybrid description of a
molecule interacting with its extended environment undergoing a nonadiabatic transition. We treat the most strongly
electronically coupled modes explicitly with basis set techniques, and embed these into a description of the remaining
environment employing weak coupling quantum master equations. Using quantum master equations of the Lindblad

form allows us to unravel these equations into a stochastic hopping process between eigenstates, which is thus
amenable for application of the theory of transition paths of Markov models with suitable generalizations to allow for
a description of initial dephasing processes. We have studied this approach with a minimal model of a conical
intersection embedded in a Markovian environment. We are able to tease out the dominant pathways and rates of
thermally activated processes, as well as the relaxation pathways and photoyields following vertical excitation. We
find that the diabatic coupling strength and thereby the geometry of the conical intersection affects the electronic
character of the transition state, as defined through a generalization of a committor function for a thermal barrier
crossing event. Similarly, the amount of diabatic coupling changes the mechanism of relaxation following a vertical
excitation. Relaxation in models with small diabatic coupling proceed through pathways dominated by pure dephasing,
while those with large diabatic coupling proceed through pathways limited by dissipation. The perspective we have
developed here for the nonadiabatic dynamics of open quantum systems generalizes classical notions of reactive paths
to fundamentally quantum mechanical processes. The manuscript reporting these initial advances was published in
Journal of Chemical Physics [1].
We subsequently introduced a general definition of a quantum committor to clarify reaction mechanisms and enable
control in processes where coherence plays a central role. This framework extends the concept of a transition state to
quantum superpositions and quantifies how interference influences reaction progress. It applies to any linear quantum
master equation with metastability and absorbing reactant and product states. Using this approach, we analyze
coherence-dependent transition states in a polaritonic system and optimize the initial state of a conical intersection
model to achieve near-unity product yields. Beyond its practical utility, the quantum committor offers a conceptual
framework for understanding reactions where classical intuition breaks down. This work was published in Journal of
Chemical Physics [2].
We applied this formalism to attempt to elucidate the origin of rate suppression in so-called vibrational strong coupling
systems. Vibrational strong coupling refers to the thermal chemistry accompanying the coupling of a ground state
reactive mode of a molecule, to light, affected by light-matter coupling in a cavity. By combining transition path
theory with quantum master equations, we have explored the dynamics of a minimal model representing a proton
transfer process in a dissipative solvent with Markovian characteristics, as well as a photon mode exhibiting strong
coupling. Through our investigation, we have identified the primary pathways governing the thermally driven reaction
and have observed a rate suppression phenomenon when the photon cavity mode resonantly couples with the reactive
vibrational mode. Furthermore, we have conducted a thorough analysis of the transition eigenstates, leading us to
conclude that the observed rate suppression arises due to a significant mismatch between the reactant and product
polaritonic wavefunctions. This quantum mechanical effect, which we present in this study, is distinct from previous
classical attempts to explain the emergence of rate suppression. Our findings provide novel insights into the interplay
between strong light-matter coupling and ground state chemistry, shedding light on a unique mechanism that
influences reaction rates in these systems. This work was published in Journal of Physical Chemistry letters [3].
The developments in quantum transition path sampling were facilitated by more abstract theoretical work, including
or application of quantum resource theories to photoisomerization quantum yields published in Physical Review A [4]
and the development of a nonequilibrium response theory for open quantum systems published in Physical Review
Research [5].
2. Theory of spin and electron transfer in exotic environments
We have studied photo‐induced electron transfer processes in complex environments employ suitably adapted
quantum master equations. First we studied a phenomena known as chirality induced spin selectivity. Specifically, we
proposed a mechanism by which spin‐polarization can be generated dynamically in chiral molecular systems
undergoing photoinduced electron transfer. The proposed mechanism explains how spin‐polarization emerges in
systems where charge transport is dominated by incoherent hopping, mediated by spin‐orbit and electronic exchange
couplings through an intermediate charge transfer state. We derive a simple expression for the spin polarization that
predicts a non‐monotonic temperature dependence, consistent with recent experiments, and a maximum spin‐
polarization that is independent of the magnitude of the spin‐orbit coupling. We validated this theory using
approximate quantum master equations and the numerically exact hierarchical equations of motion. This work was
published in Nano Letters [6]. In an article published in the Journal of Chemical Physics [7], we elaborated on a
minimal model of spin selective recombination, elucidating under what regimes of diabatic coupling a CISS effect is
expected. Specifically, we developed the spin-selective reaction operators used to examine the recombination

reactions of donor-bridge-acceptor molecules, where electron transfer occurs through the involvement of chirality and
spin-orbit coupling. In these reactions, the recombination process typically exhibits selectivity only for spin-coherence
between singlet and triplet states, rather than spin polarization in general. Interestingly, we have discovered that spin
polarization selectivity arises specifically in electron transfer processes mediated by hopping.

We brought this new understanding to spin orbit coupling mediated electron transfer theory to bare on organic
photosensitizers. Triplet excited-state generation is central to the function of many photosensitizers, yet most high-
efficiency systems rely on transition metals or heavy atoms, limiting their broader applicability. We showed that the
high triplet quantum yield of a prototypical boron dipyrromethene–anthracene (BD–An) donor–acceptor dyad arises
from a subtle interplay of competing quantum effects that can only be captured through accurate treatment of both
inner- and outer-sphere reorganization energies. An ab initio–derived model quantitatively reproduces experimental
spectra, triplet yields, excited-state kinetics, and triplet lifetimes. We found that rapid triplet formation proceeds
predominantly through higher-lying triplet states via spin–orbit–coupled charge transfer and El-Sayed–rule–breaking
intersystem crossing, rather than through direct spin–orbit–mediated transfer to the lowest triplet state. In addition,
our calculations reveal that nuclear tunneling, electronic-state recrossing, and electronic polarizability collectively
govern the rate of nonproductive ground-state recombination. These results clarify the quantum-mechanical origins
of efficient triplet formation in the BD–An system and established a predictive simulation framework for metal-free
photochemical systems more broadly. This work was published in Chemical Science [8].

Finally, to have made a recent breakthrough in topological catalysis. In an article in Nano Letters [9] we reported the
development of a microscopic theory to explain recent observations of enhanced electron transfer kinetics at twisted
bilayer electrodes. In particular, it has recently been observed that the rate of outer-sphere electron transfer from a
metal ion in aqueous solution into an electrode made of bilayer graphene, depends sensitively on the twist of the two
bilayers relative to their Bernal stacking configuration. An enhancement of the rate of nearly two orders of magnitude
is observed at the first magic angle of twisted bilayer graphene. Using a combination of molecular simulation and
dielectric continuum theory, we have discovered that the origin of this enhancement is primarily due to the stabilization
of the charge transfer transition state due to increased screening at the magic angle. By mapping the twist angle to the
metallicity of the electrode, we are able to employ the theory of nonlocal electrostatics, and constant potential
molecular dynamics simulations, to deduce that the reorganization energy accompanying electrode transfer depends
strongly on the density of states at the Fermi energy of the electrode, and thus the twist angle of the bilayers

3. Energy and charge transfer in light harvesting systems

We have made significant progress learning how to decode nonadiabatic dynamics through a new experimental
technique, two‐dimensional electronic‐vibrational spectroscopy (2DEV). 2DEV spectroscopy has been recently
developed by my colleague at Berkeley, Graham Fleming, and has emerged as an experimental technique that can
directly observe the correlated motion of electronic and nuclear degrees‐of‐freedom. This has shown great promise in
uncovering mechanisms of photoisomerization and energy transfer dynamics. In a pair of recent publications at the
Journal of Chemical Physics [10,11], we studied the role of vibronic coupling mechanisms in a model energy transfer
system through simulations of 2DEV spectroscopy. We considered two forms of nonadiabatic dynamics, through the
consideration of energetic electron‐phonon coupling and additionally Hertzberg‐Teller coupling. In simulations of the
2DEV spectra we found a means of uncovering the impact of vibronic coupling on energy transfer dynamics. We
expanded these generic insights into a novel understanding the mechanistic function of a natural light‐harvesting
system, light‐harvesting complex II (LHCII), where vibronic signatures were suggested to be involved in energy
transfer. In this comparison, we directly assign the vibronic coupling mechanism in LHCII as arising from Herzberg‐
Teller activity and show how this coupling modulates the energy transfer dynamics in this photosynthetic system.

We developed a novel method to treat the correlated dynamics accompanying energy and charge transfer in an open
quantum system. We adapted the hierarchical equations of motion approach, which admits a nonperturbative treatment
of energy transfer dynamics, to efficiently capture charge transfer dynamics accompanying large system-bath coupling
strengths. While the hierarchical equations of motion approach can in principle be used in such a case, doing so when
the system Hilbert space is large is numerically intractable. Applying a Nakajima-Zwanzig approach on the auxiliary
variables within the hierarchy, we developed a hybrid algorithm that separates out those bath degrees of freedom that
while non-Markovian, are not strongly coupled to the electronic variables, from those that are so strongly coupled that

they can be treated perturbatively with a quantum master equation. After benchmarking the approximation on small
systems where numerical exact approaches are available, we studied the charge transfer quenching of excitations in a
natural light harvesting complex. We were able to uncover the role of the exciton energy funnel on the quenching
efficiency. This work was published in Journal of Chemical Physics [12].
The combined energy and charge transport in Photosystem II (PSII) was also studied in order to identify the free-
energetic design principles governing its energy transfer network by combining a structure-based rate matrix with
time-resolved free energy analysis following defined initial excitations. By decomposing the ensemble dynamics into
enthalpic and entropic contributions, we show that their interplay differs across protein subunits, enabling each to
serve a distinct functional role. This division of labor supports efficient trapping in the reaction center (RC), effective
nonphotochemical quenching (NPQ) at the periphery, and resilient energy trapping in the alternate-monomer RC when
the primary RC is closed. Moreover, we demonstrated that entropy is a dynamically tunable component of the PSII
free energy landscape, regulated through LHCII binding. Together, these results provided a thermodynamic
framework for understanding natural photosynthetic regulation and suggest guiding principles for the design of
advanced solar energy harvesting systems. This work was published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of
Sciences [13].

In related work, we have studied the role of electron transfer in photosynthetic systems, such as in energy harvesting
in reaction centers and in protective non‐photochemical quenching in antenna complexes. Initially in collaboration
with my colleague at Berkeley, Graham Fleming, we studied how quenching processes are dynamically controlled
using phenomenological rate equations. To do so, we developed a formal response theory for actinic light conditions
on photosynthetically active organisms, predicting non‐photochemical quenching responses that agreed well with
experiments. This initial phenomological work was published in the Journal of Chemical Physics [14]. We continued
our studies in collaboration with Graham Fleming's group into the molecular origin of photo-protection in light
harvesting systems. Efficiently balancing photochemistry and photoprotection is crucial for survival and productivity
of photosynthetic organisms in the rapidly fluctuating light levels found in natural environments. The ability to respond
quickly to sudden changes in light level is clearly advantageous. In the alga Nannochloropsis oceanica we observed
an ability to respond rapidly to sudden increases in light level which occur soon after a previous high light exposure.
This ability implies a kind of memory. In a follow up study to our initial efforts, we explored the xanthophyll cycle in
N. oceanica as a photoprotective memory system. By combining snapshot fluorescence lifetime measurements with a
biochemistry-based quantitative model we showed that both short-term and medium-term “memory” arises from the
xanthophyll cycle. In addition, the model enabled us to characterize the relative quenching abilities of the three
xanthophyll cycle components. Given the ubiquity of the xanthophyll cycle in photosynthetic organisms, we expect
the model developed will be of utility in improving our understanding of vascular plant photoprotection with important
implications for crop productivity. This work was published in Nature Communications [15]. Related applications of
robustness to error processing was studied in the context of low energy circuits in a paper in Physical Reviews
Research [16]

TRAINING AND PROFESSIONAL DEVELOPMENT
The project supported the effort of three Ph.D. students and one postdoctoral scholars. All trainees were mentored in
the fields of Physical Chemistry, giving them multi-disciplinary skills that will help them in the workplace. The
mixture of simulation and theory found in this project also exposed all trainees to a broad range of skillsets to give
them a deep appreciation for the field. Each met weekly with the PI to discuss research goals and career ambitions,
while also presenting in front of the research group quarterly to develop presentation skills. Professional development
included conference and workshop attendance, as well as career mentoring and counseling from the PI.
