 Public Executive Summary
 The AMPERE project developed a new class of electrode materials that dramatically
 improve fusion device performance and longevity. By using Volumetrically Complex
 Materials (VCMs)—advanced porous metal foams optimized via plasma-material
 interaction science—the project achieved up to 85% reduction in sputtering erosion under
 fusion-relevant plasma conditions, far surpassing the goal of 40% reduction. This means
 these novel electrodes produce far fewer impurities and debris in the plasma, addressing a
 key challenge in fusion reactors by allowing greater plasma efficiency and power output
 due to the reduction of power losses due to unwanted interactions with wall-borne
 impurities.
 The research has advanced understanding in the field of plasma-material interactions
 (PMI). It demonstrated how VCM electrodes allow partial plasma infusion into the material,
 effectively trapping sputtered particles and redistributing heat/current loads. This led to
 significantly improved technical performance, such as a >33× decrease in destructive arc
 frequency and the ability to withstand higher voltages before arcing. In turn, these
 electrodes can last much longer than conventional solid metal components, improving the
 economic feasibility of fusion devices. For example, analysis suggests VCM-based
 electrodes could increase electrode service life by 5× (from ~500 to ~2500 MA·hr/m²
 throughput for steady operation) and potentially cut electrode replacement costs from on
 the order of $1M per year to under $50k per year. Thus, this project has shown VCMs have
 multiple fusion systems benefits including longer material life, reduction of catastrophic
 arcing events, and improved plasma purity and power output.
 Beyond fusion energy, the benefits of the AMPERE-developed materials are broad. The
 ability of VCMs to survive extreme plasma and thermal environments with minimal erosion
 is valuable in aerospace propulsion, high‐power electrical systems, and manufacturing
 processes that face similar conditions. By improving material longevity and performance,
 VCMs can improve both life and performance.

 Accomplishments and Objectives
 Objectives/Goals: The AMPERE project’s primary objective was to develop novel fusion
 electrode materials that meet the lifetime and performance requirements of low-cost
 fusion energy systems. The approach combined advances in plasma–materials
 interactions (PMI) with advanced manufacturing of reticulated metal foams to create
 Volumetrically Complex Materials (VCMs) for electrodes. This new class of material was
 designed to persistently minimize electrode erosion and impurity generation, thereby
 improving fusion plasma performance, and extending component life. Quantitatively, the
 goal was to demonstrate at least a 40% reduction in normalized sputter yield compared to
 state-of-the-art solid materials under fusion-relevant plasma conditions, a goal that was
 far exceeded by demonstrating up to 85% reduction. Achieving this supports the ARPA-E
 GAMOW program mission by enabling more commercially viable fusion, via improved
 electrode longevity, reduced downtime, and device performance.
 Approach: To reach these goals, AMPERE employed a cross-cutting R&D approach:
 • Design & Modeling: We defined a broad design space for VCM geometries (pore
 sizes, ligament dimensions, etc.) and developed theoretical PMI models (hybrid
 fluid-kinetic simulations) to predict foam performance. This included introducing
 the plasma infusion parameter (ξ = pore diameter / Debye length) to characterize the
 level to which plasma penetrates a porous material, which guided design choices.
 • Manufacturing: We leveraged advanced manufacturing to fabricate porous
 electrode samples with fusion-relevant materials (primarily tungsten) and optimized
 architectures.
 • Experimental Testing was performed via multiple plasma material interaction (PMI)
 test facility with diagnostics (quartz crystal microbalances for erosion, optical
 emission spectroscopy, etc.). Iterative experiments under varying plasma fluxes and
 energies were used to measure sputter yields, secondary electron emission, and arc
 behavior on both flat and VCM samples.
 • Model Validation & Design: Experimental data was fed back into the models to
 refine simulations and designs. These models were used to design promising VCM
 designs and collaborated with manufacturers to produce final electrode prototypes
 meeting the design criteria.
 • High-Power Demonstration: Finally, we tested the optimized tungsten-based VCM
 electrodes in a fusion-relevant plasma environment (UCLA’s Large Plasma Device,
 simulating steady ~2 MW/m² heat flux and pulsed up to 20 GW/m²) to validate
 performance at scale.
 Deliverables: The project’s deliverables were: (1) the design and experimental validation of
 high-performance fusion electrode materials (VCMs) that achieve the targeted sputter
 reduction in high-power plasma conditions, and (2) a path-to-demonstration plan with a
 fusion customer, outlining steps to integrate these materials into a real fusion device. Both

 Major Accomplishments: The AMPERE project succeeded on all fronts, meeting or
 exceeding every technical milestone. Key accomplishments include:
 • Sputter Yield Reduction: Demonstrated that VCM electrodes can reduce effective
 sputtering erosion by 75–85% compared to equivalent flat material, far above the
 40% reduction goal. This substantial and persistent sputter yield reduction validates
 the VCM approach for dramatically improving electrode lifetime.
 • Plasma Infusion: We experimentally confirmed the plasma infusion effect in porous
 media and used multi-scale/multi-physics modeling to verify the PMI phenomena
 responsible for plasma infusion in VCMs. By varying foam pore sizes and plasma
 conditions, we observed transitions between plasma-facing regimes (plasma
 mostly outside the VCM) and plasma-infused regimes (plasma penetrating into
 pores). In infused cases, sputtered particles are more effectively trapped and
 redeposited within the material. These results, backed by modeling and a Physical
 Review Letter publication, established plasma infusion as a key mechanism behind
 VCM performance.
 • High-Power Arc Resistance: In high-power plasma tests, custom-designed tungsten
 VCM electrodes outperformed solid tungsten by a wide margin. The VCM could be
 biased to >600 V in the pulsed plasma before arc discharge, whereas a flat tungsten
 comparator would arc at ~400 V. Moreover, the arc frequency with the VCM was only
 0.26% of plasma pulses, vs 8.73% for the flat electrode—a 33× reduction in arcing
 incidence. When arcs did occur on the VCM, they were less destructive: high-speed
 imaging showed arc spots moving across the porous surface, avoiding concentrated
 damage, and optical spectroscopy recorded significantly less sputtered W vapor
 during arcs for the VCM. These results indicate much improved tolerance to
 transient high-stress events in fusion systems.

 Project Activities
 This project set out to develop and validate Volumetrically Complex Materials
 (VCMs)—advanced porous metal electrodes—as a solution for the short lifetimes and
 high erosion of fusion plasma-facing components. The core hypothesis was that a
 porous, multi-phase metal structure could trap sputtered particles and dissipate heat,
 dramatically reducing plasma-induced erosion (targeting ≥40% less sputter erosion
 than a solid electrode) and thereby extending electrode life. In essence, the VCM’s tiny
 pores and internal surfaces would act as built-in “sinks” to re-capture material ejected
 by plasma, preventing loss and minimizing contamination.
 The project combined simulation-driven design, advanced manufacturing of tungsten
 foam electrodes, and multi-stage plasma testing (from lab-scale to reactor-scale) to
 validate the VCM concept.
 VCM electrodes met and exceeded the performance goals. Lab experiments showed
 roughly 50–80% lower sputtering erosion for VCMs compared to equivalent flat metal
 electrodes (surpassing the 40% reduction target). In a fusion-scale plasma test, a
 tungsten VCM sample lost 5–6× less mass than a solid tungsten plate (with no
 structural damage to the VCM)—dramatic proof of greatly enhanced durability and
 lifespan.
 No formal award modifications were required. In Year 1 the team broadened the
 material approach to include additively manufactured lattice VCMs (an expansion that
 did not change the project objectives). All milestones were completed on schedule,
 with only minor adjustments for test scheduling.
 This project successfully demonstrated that VCM technology can drastically reduce
 plasma-material erosion and improve component longevity in fusion environments,
 validating the original hypothesis. All objectives were achieved on time and on scope,
 with the VCM approach now proven for potential adoption by our fusion industry
 partners.
