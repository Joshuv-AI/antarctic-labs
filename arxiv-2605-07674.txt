Abstract
Regulatory audits of AI systems increasingly rely on differential privacy (DP) to protect training data and model internals. We study audit design when the audited developer can strategically respond to the privacy-constrained audit interface. We formalize privacy-constrained auditing as a bilevel Stackelberg game, in which an auditor commits to a query policy and DP budget allocation across harm dimensions, and a strategic developer reallocates mitigation efforts in response. We introduce the welfare-weighted under-detection gap B w B_{w} , the welfare-weighted true residual harm the audit fails to detect at the developer’s strategic best response, and prove that naive DP auditing (uniform or harm-proportional allocation) induces a strictly larger B w B_{w} than any non-strategic mitigation baseline whenever effective detectability is heterogeneous, the welfare weights are not comonotone with detectability, and the developer’s optimum is interior. We characterize the optimal auditor allocation as a four-factor balance of welfare weight, audit miss-probability, detectability elasticity, and mitigation-cost curvature, and provide a single-level reformulation of the bilevel problem via the developer’s KKT system. We propose Strategic Private Audit Design (SPAD), a projected-gradient algorithm with hypergradients computed through the developer’s best response. We specify a synthetic evaluation protocol across harm dimensions, developer behaviors, and privacy budgets under quadratic mitigation costs and exponential residual-harm reduction; preliminary experiments show that SPAD reduces the welfare-weighted under-detection gap B w B_{w} by 5–14% under uniform welfare and 20–44% under non-uniform welfare across the tested privacy-budget regimes, beating welfare-proportional allocation by 13–20% in the non-uniform-welfare case. Keywords: differential privacy, auditing, strategic response, Stackelberg games, mechanism design

Introduction

Differential privacy is standard for protecting individuals during audits of machine learning systems. From fairness audits assessing algorithmic bias to safety evaluations probing model robustness, privacy-preserving mechanisms ensure auditors cannot infer sensitive information about data points or models. However, this protection has a subtle cost. Allocating privacy budgets across harm dimensions creates
uneven observability
: some dimensions become harder to detect due to variations in query frequency, privacy budget, or detection sensitivity. This asymmetry protects privacy but creates strategic incentives for the audited system.

Consider a strategic developer operating the audited system, facing queries about performance failures across dimensions, like false positive rates on subgroups or safety lapses in corner cases. We use
developer
for this strategic agent, whether an ML platform, model provider, or in-house team. If the developer anticipates some dimensions will be noisier, less frequently queried, or assigned smaller privacy budgets, it has an incentive to reallocate mitigation efforts away from low-detectability regions and concentrate harms there. The privacy mechanism, meant to protect privacy during audits, creates an opportunity for the developer to hide failures in less observable areas. This tension between privacy as protection and audit evasion defines the core problem we address. We ask: if an auditor knows the developer will behave strategically in response to the audit interface, how should the auditor design queries and privacy budgets to minimize true undetected harm?

Strategic responses to evaluations have been studied in the context of classifier games and strategic classification
(
13
;
8
)
, where agents learn to manipulate their features when they know they will be evaluated. Audit games have been modeled as bilevel competitions between an auditor who chooses what to inspect and an adversary who reallocates defensive resources in response
(
2
)
. Privacy auditing has simultaneously grown into a mature empirical field, with demonstrated attacks on trained models showing that information leakage is detectable and closely tracked by theoretical differential privacy (DP) bounds
(
30
;
23
)
. However, no prior work jointly models the critical combination of three structural elements: (a) DP composition and the auditor’s allocation of privacy budgets across multiple harm dimensions; (b) the auditor’s commitment to a fixed query policy and privacy allocation, which the strategic developer can observe before choosing its own actions; and (c) the strategic developer’s best-response reallocation of mitigation effort, subject to a cost budget and aiming to minimize audit exposure. The gap is structural. Privacy-auditing papers design audits without modeling the strategic response to the audit interface itself. Strategic-classification papers model agent responses but assume fully observed features, not privacy-noised observations. Audit-game papers do not incorporate DP constraints at all and thus do not capture how detectability heterogeneity created by privacy noise changes incentives. The triple intersection—DP-constrained observation, auditor commitment, and strategic developer response—remains unexplored and is the setting of this study.

We formalize this gap as a Stackelberg game between an auditor and a developer. The auditor first commits to a query policy
\pi
(specifying the probability of querying each harm dimension) and a privacy budget allocation
\varepsilon=(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{d})
across
d
dimensions, subject to a total privacy budget constraint and standard composition rules. A strategically rational developer, anticipating this commitment and knowing which dimensions are noisier and less queried, solves its own optimization problem: it reallocates mitigation efforts to minimize expected audit exposure (weighted by detectability) plus mitigation cost, thereby concentrating residual harm in dimensions where the effective detectability
\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j})
is lowest. We introduce the
welfare-weighted under-detection gap
,

B_{w}(\pi,\varepsilon)=\sum_{j}w_{j}\,\bigl(1-\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j})\bigr)\,g_{j}(h_{j},m^{*}_{j}(\pi,\varepsilon)),

This measures the welfare-weighted true residual harm that the audit fails to detect.
B_{w}
is non-negative under DP, and the substantive question is whether
strategic
reallocation by the developer makes
B_{w}
strictly larger than under a non-strategic baseline mitigation. When the developer concentrates residual harm in low-detectability regions, the audit’s miss-probability
1-\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j})
constructively couples with the inflated
g_{j}
to enlarge
B_{w}
.

Our contributions are threefold.
First

\alpha_{j}

Third

2
Model

We formalize privacy-constrained auditing as a bilevel game in which an auditor allocates queries and privacy budgets across harm dimensions, anticipating a strategic developer who reallocates mitigation efforts in response to the resulting audit interface.

2.1
Setup: harm space and welfare

We model a system that can cause harm across
d
dimensions. Let
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
index these dimensions, which may represent demographic subgroups, failure modes, or other sensitive attributes. Let
h\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
denote the baseline harm vector, where
h_{j}
is the latent harm in dimension
j
in the absence of any mitigation. Let
w\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
be a welfare weight vector, where
w_{j}
reflects the social importance of reducing harm in dimension
j
.

The auditor’s welfare-relevant objective is to minimize the sum of welfare-weighted residual harms:
L(h^{\mathrm{res}})=\sum_{j=1}^{d}w_{j}h^{\mathrm{res}}_{j}
, where
h^{\mathrm{res}}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
is the residual harm vector after the developer’s mitigation. Vector
w
represents the priorities of an external auditor or regulator, not the developer’s objectives. We treat the auditor as a
normative
welfare planner: the framework asks how a regulator who genuinely cares about welfare-weighted harm
should
design audits, not how any specific real-world regulator currently behaves; the latter is a positive question we leave to follow-up work and discuss as a misuse pathway in Appendix
D
.

2.2
Auditor: query and privacy-budget allocation

The auditor’s action consists of two choices: a query policy
\pi\in\Delta^{d}
specifying the distribution of audit queries over the
d
harm dimensions (
\pi_{j}
is the probability of querying dimension
j
), and a privacy budget allocation
\varepsilon=(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{d})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
, where
\varepsilon_{j}
is the DP budget for queries to dimension
j
. These choices are subject to

\sum_{j=1}^{d}\varepsilon_{j}\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tot}},\quad\pi\in\Delta^{d},\quad\varepsilon_{j}\geq 0.

For each query to dimension
j
, the audit interface returns a noisy observation
y_{j}=M_{j}(h^{\mathrm{res}}_{j};\varepsilon_{j})
, where
M_{j}
is a differentially private mechanism, and whose accuracy improves with
\varepsilon_{j}
. We abstract its behavior via a detectability function.

Assumption 2.1
.

For each dimension
j
there exists a detectability function
\alpha_{j}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to[0,1]
that is strictly increasing and continuously differentiable, with
\alpha_{j}(0)=0
and
\lim_{\varepsilon\to\infty}\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon)=1
. The function
\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j})
captures the effective sensitivity of the audit outcome in dimension
j
to true residual harm.

The detectability function is a reduced-form encoding of how the privacy–utility tradeoff translates into observability, calibrated at a fixed reference operating point
h^{\mathrm{res},0}_{j}
(Appendix
B
gives explicit constructions for the Gaussian, Laplace, and randomised-response mechanisms). Holding
\alpha_{j}
as a function of
\varepsilon_{j}
alone — rather than the full
\tilde{\alpha}_{j}(\varepsilon_{j},h^{\mathrm{res}}_{j})
— is a modelling abstraction that preserves Theorem
3.2
’s qualitative conclusion but changes quantitative outcomes; the full coupling is treated as future work. In the local DP setting,
\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j})
relates to the asymptotic detection rate of private estimators
(
9
;
4
)
; in the global DP regime, it depends on composition bounds
(
1
;
3
)
.

2.3
Developer: mitigation under a cost budget

The developer chooses a mitigation allocation
m\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
, where
m_{j}
is the effort directed at reducing harm in dimension
j
, subject to a total cost budget
B
:

m_{j}\geq 0\text{ for all }j,\qquad C(m)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}c_{j}(m_{j})\leq B,

with
c_{j}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
the cost function for mitigation in dimension
j
. Residual harm is determined jointly by baseline harm and mitigation:
h^{\mathrm{res}}_{j}\equiv h^{\mathrm{res}}_{j}(m_{j}):=g_{j}(h_{j},m_{j})
, with
g_{j}
decreasing in its second argument and
g_{j}(h_{j},0)=h_{j}
(we suppress the fixed argument
h_{j}
and write
h^{\mathrm{res}}_{j}
or
h^{\mathrm{res}}_{j}(m_{j})
interchangeably). Standard examples include
h^{\mathrm{res}}_{j}=h_{j}\exp(-\beta_{j}m_{j})
or
h^{\mathrm{res}}_{j}=\max\{h_{j}-\gamma_{j}m_{j},0\}
.

Assumption 2.2
.

Each cost function
c_{j}
is strictly increasing, strictly convex, and coercive (
c_{j}(m_{j})\to\infty
as
m_{j}\to\infty
) with
c_{j}(0)=0
. The residual harm function
g_{j}(\cdot,m_{j})
is strictly convex in
m_{j}
for each fixed
h_{j}>0
.

The coercivity of
c_{j}
rules out cost functions with bounded growth (it holds for power-law
c_{j}(m_{j})=m_{j}^{p}/p
with
p>1
and is needed for compact sublevel sets in Lemma
3.1
). The strict convexity of
g_{j}(h_{j},\cdot)
implies a diminishing marginal effectiveness of the mitigation effort, and the strict convexity of
c_{j}
captures an increasing marginal cost.

2.4
Developer’s objective: minimize audit exposure plus cost

The central asymmetry is that the developer does not minimize welfare-weighted residual harm; it minimizes expected audit exposure. The harm detected in dimension
j
is

\mathrm{DetHarm}_{j}(\pi,\varepsilon,m)=\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j})h^{\mathrm{res}}_{j}(m_{j})
.
The developer solves

m^{*}(\pi,\varepsilon)\in\arg\min_{m\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}:C(m)\leq B}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j})g_{j}(h_{j},m_{j})+C(m).

(2.1)

This best response minimizes the developer’s exposure—detected harm plus mitigation cost—not welfare-weighted true residual harm. This misalignment generates strategic blind spots.

2.5
Auditor’s objective: bilevel optimization

The auditor anticipates the developer’s response and solves the bilevel problem

\min_{\pi\in\Delta^{d},\,\varepsilon\geq 0:\sum_{j}\varepsilon_{j}\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}}B_{w}(\pi,\varepsilon),\quad B_{w}:=\sum_{j}w_{j}(1-\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j}))\,g_{j}(h_{j},m^{*}(\pi,\varepsilon)_{j}),

(2.2)

the welfare-weighted under-detection gap (Definition
2.5
). We retain
\mathrm{TRH}(\pi,\varepsilon):=\sum_{j}w_{j}g_{j}(h_{j},m^{*}_{j})
as a diagnostic and as the basis for Proposition
4.3
;
B_{w}\leq\mathrm{TRH}
pointwise. The Stackelberg structure parallels strategic classification
(
13
;
8
)
and security games
(
2
;
17
;
28
)
.

2.6
Detected harm and the blind-spot gap

Definition 2.3

(Detected harm)
.

\mathrm{DH}(\pi,\varepsilon,m)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j})g_{j}(h_{j},m_{j}).

Definition 2.4

(True residual harm)
.

\mathrm{TRH}(\pi,\varepsilon)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}w_{j}g_{j}\bigl(h_{j},m^{*}(\pi,\varepsilon)_{j}\bigr).

Definition 2.5

(Welfare-weighted under-detection gap)
.

B_{w}(\pi,\varepsilon)\;=\;\sum_{j=1}^{d}w_{j}\,\bigl(1-\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j})\bigr)\,g_{j}\bigl(h_{j},m^{*}(\pi,\varepsilon)_{j}\bigr).

B_{w}
is the welfare-weighted true residual harm that the audit fails to detect, with
w_{j}
giving the welfare weight and
1-\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j})
being the audit miss probability.
B_{w}\geq 0
under DP, with equality only when the audit coverage is perfect on every dimension with positive residual harm; the substantive question (Theorem
3.2
) is whether
strategic
reallocation makes
B_{w}
strictly larger than under a non-strategic baseline.

2.7
A worked example with three dimensions

Consider
d=3
harm dimensions with
h=(1,1,1)
, heterogeneous welfare weights
w=(3,1,1)
,
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}=3
,
\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j})=1-\exp(-\varepsilon_{j})
, smooth residual harm
g_{j}(h_{j},m_{j})=h_{j}\exp(-m_{j})
,
c_{j}(m_{j})=m_{j}^{2}/2
, and developer cost budget
B=1.5
. The numerics were computed using an exact KKT solver (script in the replication package).

Under the naïve uniform allocation (
\pi_{j}=1/3
,
\varepsilon_{j}=1
),
\alpha_{j}(1)\approx 0.632
on every dimension; the developer’s interior best response is
m^{*}_{j}\approx 0.177
uniformly, with
h^{\mathrm{res}}_{j}\approx 0.838
,
\mathrm{DH}\approx 0.530
,
\mathrm{TRH}\approx 4.191
, and
B_{w}\approx 3.308
. Under a welfare-aware policy
\pi=(0.6,0.2,0.2)
,
\varepsilon=(2.4,0.3,0.3)

\alpha=(0.909,0.259,0.259)

m^{*}\approx(0.375,0.049,0.049)

h^{\mathrm{res}}\approx(0.687,0.952,0.952)
: residual harm shifts toward the low-detectability dimensions, but those have small welfare weights; therefore,
\mathrm{TRH}
drops to

and

to
\approx 2.742
(
\sim

3
Strategic Blind Spots Under Naïve Auditing

This section establishes the core negative result: under privacy-constrained auditing, non-strategic (uniform or harm-proportional) audit policies create systematic gaps between the detected and true residual harm. The key insight is that DP induces uneven observability across dimensions, which strategically rational developers exploit by migrating residual harm toward low-detectability regions.

3.1
Developer best response under fixed audit policy

Fix an audit policy
(\pi,\varepsilon)
. Given the lower-level problem (
2.1
), the developer’s objective is convex on a convex feasible region. We have:

Lemma 3.1

(Existence and uniqueness of best response)
.

Suppose Assumptions
2.1
–
2.2
hold and that
c_{j}
are strictly convex and
g_{j}(h_{j},\cdot)
strictly convex in
m_{j}
. For any fixed
(\pi,\varepsilon)
with
\pi\in\Delta^{d}
and
\varepsilon\geq 0
, the developer’s lower-level problem admits a unique best-response mitigation allocation
m^{*}(\pi,\varepsilon)
.

Proof sketch.

The objective in (
2.1
) is a sum of strictly convex per-dimension terms (
\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j})g_{j}(h_{j},\cdot)+c_{j}(\cdot)
) over the convex feasible set
\{m\geq 0:C(m)\leq B\}
. Strict convexity provides at most one minimizer. Existence follows because
c_{j}(m_{j})\to\infty
as
m_{j}\to\infty
(strict convexity of
c_{j}
with
c_{j}(0)=0
implies coercivity); thus, the sublevel sets of the objective are compact in the feasible set. Hence,
m^{*}(\pi,\varepsilon)
exists and is unique.
∎

At an interior optimum where
m^{*}_{j}>0
, the first-order condition for dimension
j
is

\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j})\left|\frac{\partial g_{j}}{\partial m_{j}}\right|=(1+\lambda)\,c^{\prime}_{j}(m_{j}),

(3.1)

where
\lambda\geq 0
is the multiplier on the cost budget constraint
C(m)\leq B
. The marginal cost of mitigation in dimension
j
has two components: the soft cost
c^{\prime}_{j}(m_{j})
from the additive
C(m)
term in the developer’s objective and the shadow price
\lambda c^{\prime}_{j}(m_{j})
of the hard budget; together, they give
(1+\lambda)\,c^{\prime}_{j}(m_{j})
. The developer equalizes the marginal reduction in detectability-weighted exposure per unit (soft + shadow) cost across active dimensions; dimensions with high
\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j})
require more mitigation effort.

3.2
The mechanism of blind spots

Consider two dimensions,
j,k
with equal welfare and baseline harm but different detectability:
\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j})>\pi_{k}\alpha_{k}(\varepsilon_{k})
. By (
3.1
), the developer chooses
m^{*}_{j}>m^{*}_{k}
, that is, more mitigation in the high-detectability dimension. This produces unequal residual harm,
h^{\mathrm{res}}_{k}>h^{\mathrm{res}}_{j}
, indicating that the low-detectability dimension retains larger residual harm.

The auditor detects harm
\mathrm{DH}(\pi,\varepsilon,m^{*})=\sum_{\ell}\pi_{\ell}\alpha_{\ell}(\varepsilon_{\ell})h^{\mathrm{res}}_{\ell}
by weighting residuals by detectability; thus, the term for dimension
k
is the product of large residual harm and small detectability weight. In contrast, the welfare-relevant
\mathrm{TRH}(\pi,\varepsilon)=\sum_{\ell}w_{\ell}h^{\mathrm{res}}_{\ell}
weights by welfare, not detectability. Misalignment produces a blind spot.

Theorem 1: naïve DP auditing induces a positive blind-spot gap

3.4
What drives the gap: detectability, cost, and budget

Three factors determine the magnitude of the strategic gap.
Detectability heterogeneity:
if
\delta_{\ell}:=\pi_{\ell}\alpha_{\ell}(\varepsilon_{\ell})
is constant, the strategic and non-strategic responses coincide and the gap collapses; larger heterogeneity widens it.
Cost-curvature heterogeneity:
cheaper mitigation on high-detectability dimensions amplifies reallocation.
Budget tightness:
the gap scales with
relative
heterogeneity
\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}/\pi_{k}\alpha_{k}
, not absolute
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}
.

Corollary: harm-proportional allocation is not optimal

Corollary 3.3

(Harm-proportional allocation is suboptimal)
.

–
2.2

with

.

The perturbation
\Delta\varepsilon
with
+1

4
Optimal Private Audit Design

We now characterize the
optimal
audit design, which is the allocation that minimizes the welfare-weighted under-detection gap
B_{w}
when the developer responds rationally. We show three results: (i) the optimal design is fundamentally non-proportional to baseline harm (Theorem
4.1
); (ii) the bilevel problem admits a tractable single-level reformulation via the developer’s KKT conditions (Theorem
4.2
); and (iii) we provide an algorithm, Strategic Private Audit Design (SPAD).

4.1
Theorem 2: optimal allocation is non-harm-proportional

Theorem 4.1

(Non-proportionality of optimal allocation)
.

Let
(\pi^{\star},\varepsilon^{\star})
minimize
B_{w}(\pi,\varepsilon)
over the feasible set
\{\pi\in\Delta^{d},\varepsilon\geq 0,\sum_{j}\varepsilon_{j}\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}\}
under Assumptions
2.1
–
2.2
. Then
(\pi^{\star},\varepsilon^{\star})
is not generically harm-proportional: there exist parameter profiles such that

\pi^{\star}_{j}\neq\frac{h_{j}}{\sum_{k}h_{k}}\quad\text{and}\quad\varepsilon^{\star}_{j}\neq\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}h_{j}}{\sum_{k}h_{k}}.

Proof sketch.

The gradient of
B_{w}
has a
direct
term in
(\pi,\varepsilon)
through the audit-coverage factor
1-\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j})
and an
indirect
term through
m^{*}(\pi,\varepsilon)
. Combining the auditor’s first-order conditions with the implicit differentiation of the developer’s FOC (
3.1
) yields a four-factor optimality condition balancing the welfare weight
w_{j}
, audit miss-probability
1-\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j})
, detectability elasticity

4.2
Theorem 3: bilevel reduction via KKT substitution

Theorem 4.2

(Single-level reduction via KKT substitution)
.

Suppose Assumptions
2.1
–
2.2
hold, with

and
g_{j}
twice continuously differentiable and the developer’s lower-level problem strictly convex with a unique interior optimum. Then, the auditor’s bilevel problem
(

)
admits an equivalent single-level reformulation obtained by substituting the developer’s KKT conditions:

\displaystyle\min_{\pi,\varepsilon,m,\mu}\

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}w_{j}\bigl(1-\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j})\bigr)g_{j}(h_{j},m_{j})

(4.1)

s.t.

\displaystyle\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j})\tfrac{\partial g_{j}}{\partial m_{j}}+(1+\mu)\,c^{\prime}_{j}(m_{j})=0\quad\forall j,\quad\text{($\mu$ is the cost-budget multiplier, $=\lambda$ in \eqref{eq:foc-dev}),}

(4.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{j}c_{j}(m_{j})\leq B,\quad m_{j}\geq 0,

(4.3)

\displaystyle\mu\geq 0,\quad\mu\!\left(B-\sum_{j}c_{j}(m_{j})\right)=0,

(4.4)

\displaystyle\pi\in\Delta^{d},\ \varepsilon\geq 0,\ \sum_{j}\varepsilon_{j}\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}.

(4.5)

This is a mathematical program with complementarity constraints (MPCC). Under MPEC-LICQ
and
strict complementarity at the optimum—both generic in
(h,w,\alpha,c,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tot}},B)
but not automatic—it reduces locally to a smooth NLP whose KKT points are S-stationary points of the MPCC. We assume strict complementarity at the optimum throughout; if it fails (e.g., the developer’s cost budget is slack at
m^{*}
, so
\mu=0
and complementarity is degenerate), gradient methods may converge to W-stationary points that are not KKT points of the original bilevel problem. We discuss this regime in §
6
.

Proof sketch.

The developer’s KKT conditions are necessary and sufficient based on strict convexity (Lemma
3.1
); imposing them yields an equivalent MPCC. The MPEC-LICQ and generic strict complementarity arguments are deferred to Appendix
A
.
∎

Computational consequence.

The single-level reformulation enables standard gradient methods.
\nabla B_{w}
admits an

Random restarts mitigate spurious local optima; penalty relaxation or NLP solvers (IPOPT, SNOPT) are alternatives if MPEC-LICQ is in doubt.

B_{w}(\pi,\varepsilon)=\sum_{\ell}w_{\ell}(1-\pi_{\ell}\alpha_{\ell}(\varepsilon_{\ell}))\,g_{\ell}(h_{\ell},m^{*}_{\ell})
the per-coordinate derivative decomposes into a direct and an indirect term:

\frac{\partial B_{w}}{\partial\varepsilon_{\ell}}=\underbrace{-w_{\ell}\pi_{\ell}\,\alpha^{\prime}_{\ell}(\varepsilon_{\ell})\,g_{\ell}(h_{\ell},m^{*}_{\ell})}_{\text{direct (coverage)}}\;+\;\underbrace{w_{\ell}(1-\pi_{\ell}\alpha_{\ell}(\varepsilon_{\ell}))\,\frac{\partial g_{\ell}}{\partial m_{\ell}}\,\frac{\partial m^{*}_{\ell}}{\partial\varepsilon_{\ell}}}_{\text{indirect (developer response)}}.

Both terms are strictly negative (direct:
\alpha^{\prime}_{\ell}>0
and
g_{\ell}>0
; indirect:
\partial g_{\ell}/\partial m_{\ell}<0
and
\partial m^{*}_{\ell}/\partial\varepsilon_{\ell}>0
), so the directional derivative

\tfrac{d}{d\tau}B_{w}\bigr|_{\tau=0^{+}}=\frac{\partial B_{w}}{\partial\varepsilon_{j^{*}}}-\frac{\partial B_{w}}{\partial\varepsilon_{k^{*}}}

is strictly negative whenever
\alpha^{\prime}_{j^{*}}(\varepsilon^{\mathrm{hp}}_{j^{*}})>\alpha^{\prime}_{k^{*}}(\varepsilon^{\mathrm{hp}}_{k^{*}})
(generic heterogeneity): the magnitude of
\partial B_{w}/\partial\varepsilon_{j^{*}}
exceeds that of
\partial B_{w}/\partial\varepsilon_{k^{*}}
in both terms, since
\alpha^{\prime}_{j^{*}}
scales the direct term, and the indirect term inherits the same ordering through
\partial m^{*}_{j^{*}}/\partial\varepsilon_{j^{*}}
. Hence
(\pi^{\mathrm{hp}},\varepsilon^{\mathrm{hp}})
is not a local minimizer of
B_{w}
.

A.3
Proof of Theorem
4.1

The gradient of
B_{w}
has direct and indirect components,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial B_{w}}{\partial\varepsilon_{j}}

\displaystyle=-w_{j}\pi_{j}\alpha^{\prime}_{j}(\varepsilon_{j})\,g_{j}+w_{j}(1-\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j}))\,\frac{\partial g_{j}}{\partial m_{j}}\,\frac{\partial m^{\star}_{j}}{\partial\varepsilon_{j}},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial B_{w}}{\partial\pi_{j}}

\displaystyle=-w_{j}\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j})\,g_{j}+w_{j}(1-\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j}))\,\frac{\partial g_{j}}{\partial m_{j}}\,\frac{\partial m^{\star}_{j}}{\partial\pi_{j}}.

Implicit differentiation of the developer’s FOC (
3.1
) at an interior optimum (active set locally constant, cost-budget multiplier
\lambda
varying smoothly) gives

\frac{\partial m^{\star}_{j}}{\partial\varepsilon_{j}}=\frac{\pi_{j}\,\alpha^{\prime}_{j}(\varepsilon_{j})\,|\partial g_{j}/\partial m_{j}|}{D_{j}(m^{\star}_{j},\lambda)},\qquad\frac{\partial m^{\star}_{j}}{\partial\pi_{j}}=\frac{\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j})\,|\partial g_{j}/\partial m_{j}|}{D_{j}(m^{\star}_{j},\lambda)},

where
D_{j}(m^{\star}_{j},\lambda):=\pi_{j}\,\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j})\,|\partial^{2}g_{j}/\partial m_{j}^{2}|(m^{\star}_{j})+(1+\lambda)\,c^{\prime\prime}_{j}(m^{\star}_{j})>0
by strict convexity of
g_{j}
and
c_{j}
(Assumption
2.2
). Substituting and using
\partial g_{j}/\partial m_{j}=-|\partial g_{j}/\partial m_{j}|
, the auditor’s
\varepsilon
-stationarity condition becomes

w_{j}\,\pi_{j}\,\alpha^{\prime}_{j}(\varepsilon_{j})\,\biggl[\,g_{j}(h_{j},m^{\star}_{j})\;+\;(1-\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j}))\,\frac{|\partial g_{j}/\partial m_{j}|^{2}}{D_{j}(m^{\star}_{j},\lambda)}\,\biggr]\;=\;\lambda_{\varepsilon}\quad\forall j\in\mathrm{active}.

This is a four-factor balance — welfare weight
w_{j}
, query rate
\pi_{j}
, detectability elasticity
\alpha^{\prime}_{j}(\varepsilon_{j})
, and the bracketed effective-coverage–cost term combining residual harm, miss-probability
1-\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j})
, marginal mitigation effectiveness
|\partial g_{j}/\partial m_{j}|
, and the curvature-aggregate
D_{j}
. (Setting
\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}\equiv 0
recovers the three-factor TRH stationarity reported in earlier drafts.) Harm-proportional allocation generically violates this condition: it equates
\varepsilon_{j}\propto h_{j}
across dimensions, which the four-factor balance demands only on a measure-zero set of parameter profiles. Counter-example:
d=2
,
h=(1,1)
,
w=(1,1)
,
\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j})=1-e^{-\kappa_{j}\varepsilon_{j}}
with
\kappa_{1}=2,\kappa_{2}=1
,
c_{j}(m_{j})=m_{j}^{2}/2
,
g_{j}(h_{j},m_{j})=h_{j}e^{-m_{j}}
(smooth, strictly convex; cf. Appendix
B
). Harm-proportional gives
\varepsilon=(1,1)
; numerically solving the four-factor stationarity above yields
\varepsilon^{\star}_{1}>\varepsilon^{\star}_{2}
(the auditor pours additional budget into the higher-elasticity dimension, where both the direct and indirect
B_{w}
effects are larger), so harm-proportional is not optimal.

A.4
Proof of Theorem
4.2

The developer’s lower-level problem is strictly convex (Lemma
3.1
), so its KKT conditions—stationarity (
4.2
), primal feasibility (
4.3
), and complementary slackness (
4.4
)—are necessary and sufficient for
m=m^{*}(\pi,\varepsilon)
. Substituting them into the auditor’s problem yields (
4.1
), which is equivalent to (
2.2
). The complementarity
\mu(B-\sum_{j}c_{j}(m_{j}))=0
makes the reformulation an MPCC, which generically violates the MFCQ. MPEC-LICQ holds at points where the active set of
m_{j}\geq 0
and the cost-budget complementarity are regular; at such points strong stationarity of the MPCC coincides with KKT of the local NLP obtained by fixing the active set (cf.
20
). Strict complementarity at the optimum is generic in
(h,w,\alpha,c,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tot}},B)
; it fails only on a finite union of algebraic varieties, which has Lebesgue measure zero.

A.5
Proof of Proposition
4.3
(
d=2
lower bound)

Fix Assumptions
2.1
–
2.2
with
d=2
and assume
c^{\prime}_{k}(B/d)<h_{k}
for
k\in\{1,2\}
, so
\varepsilon^{\dagger}
is well-defined. The proof has three steps: (i) any feasible audit allocation has at least one dimension with low effective detectability; (ii) the developer’s mitigation in that dimension vanishes as
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}\to 0
; and (iii) the auditor minimizes the resulting TRH by leaving the smaller-
w_{k}h_{k}
dimension unmitigated.

Step (i): Tight-budget allocation has a low-
\varepsilon
dimension.
For any feasible
(\pi,\varepsilon)
with
\sum_{j}\varepsilon_{j}\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}
,
\min_{k}\varepsilon_{k}\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}/2<\varepsilon^{\dagger}
. Pick
k_{0}
achieving this minimum; by Assumption
2.1
,
\alpha_{k_{0}}(\varepsilon_{k_{0}})<\alpha_{k_{0}}(\varepsilon^{\dagger})=c^{\prime}_{k_{0}}(B/d)/h_{k_{0}}
, so the effective detectability
\delta_{k_{0}}=\pi_{k_{0}}\alpha_{k_{0}}(\varepsilon_{k_{0}})<\pi_{k_{0}}c^{\prime}_{k_{0}}(B/d)/h_{k_{0}}\leq c^{\prime}_{k_{0}}(B/d)/h_{k_{0}}
.

Step (ii): Mitigation vanishes in the low-
\delta
dimension.
For
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}
in the regime
0<\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}<2\,\varepsilon^{\dagger}
but bounded away from zero (so the developer’s interior FOC applies; we approach the limit
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}\to 0
from above), the developer’s FOC (
3.1
) at
m^{*}_{k_{0}}
yields
\delta_{k_{0}}\,|\partial g_{k_{0}}/\partial m_{k_{0}}|(m^{*}_{k_{0}})=(1+\lambda)\,c^{\prime}_{k_{0}}(m^{*}_{k_{0}})
. Since
|\partial g_{k_{0}}/\partial m_{k_{0}}|(0)\leq h_{k_{0}}
(mitigation cannot generate negative residual harm) and
|\partial g_{k_{0}}/\partial m_{k_{0}}|
is monotone non-increasing in
m
, we have
\delta_{k_{0}}\,h_{k_{0}}\geq(1+\lambda)\,c^{\prime}_{k_{0}}(m^{*}_{k_{0}})\geq c^{\prime}_{k_{0}}(m^{*}_{k_{0}})
. Combining with Step (i):

c^{\prime}_{k_{0}}(m^{*}_{k_{0}})\;\leq\;\delta_{k_{0}}\,h_{k_{0}}\;<\;c^{\prime}_{k_{0}}(B/d).

Strict monotonicity of
c^{\prime}_{k_{0}}
(Assumption
2.2
) gives
m^{*}_{k_{0}}<B/d
. As
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}\to 0
,
\varepsilon_{k_{0}}\to 0
and
\delta_{k_{0}}\to 0
(since
\alpha_{k_{0}}(0)=0
), so
c^{\prime}_{k_{0}}(m^{*}_{k_{0}})\to 0
and
m^{*}_{k_{0}}\to 0
(using
c^{\prime}_{k_{0}}(0)=0
). Hence
g_{k_{0}}(h_{k_{0}},m^{*}_{k_{0}})\to h_{k_{0}}
.

Step (iii): Auditor’s optimal lower bound.
The auditor selects
(\pi,\varepsilon)
to minimize TRH. By Step (ii), at least one dimension
k_{0}
has
g_{k_{0}}(h_{k_{0}},m^{*}_{k_{0}})\to h_{k_{0}}
, contributing
w_{k_{0}}h_{k_{0}}-o(1)
to TRH. The auditor minimizes this contribution by concentrating the audit budget on the larger-
w_{k}h_{k}
dimension, leaving the smaller-
w_{k}h_{k}
dimension as
k_{0}
. Therefore:

\mathrm{TRH}(\pi,\varepsilon)\;\geq\;\min(w_{1}h_{1},w_{2}h_{2})\;-\;o(1)\quad\text{as }\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}\to 0,

with the
o(1)
term vanishing uniformly over feasible
(\pi,\varepsilon)
by the uniform convergence
m^{*}_{k_{0}}\to 0
established in Step (ii). This proves the proposition.

A.6
Note on Conjecture
4.4
(
d\geq 3
)

The general-
d
extension requires a uniform bound on how much effective detectability
\sum_{j}\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j})
can be concentrated into a single dimension
k_{0}
under the budget constraint
\sum_{j}\varepsilon_{j}\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}
. A natural sufficient condition is the concavity of

under Assumption
2.1
alone.

Appendix B
Concrete DP mechanism instantiations

The main text abstracts the differentially private observation channel via the detectability function
\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j})
, which, by Assumption
2.1
maps
\mathbb{R}_{+}\to[0,1]
with
\alpha_{j}(0)=0
and
\lim\alpha_{j}=1
. For canonical DP mechanisms, the natural detection-probability function depends jointly on the privacy budget and residual harm:
\tilde{\alpha}_{j}(\varepsilon_{j},h^{\mathrm{res}}_{j})
. We treat
\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j})
as the
reduced-form local sensitivity
of
\tilde{\alpha}_{j}
at a fixed reference operating point
h^{\mathrm{res},0}_{j}
— typically the prior expected residual harm
\mathbb{E}[g_{j}(h_{j},m_{j}^{\mathrm{ns}})]
under a budget-exhausting non-strategic baseline. The displayed
\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j})
below should be read as
\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j}):=\mathrm{rescale}\bigl(\tilde{\alpha}_{j}(\varepsilon_{j},h^{\mathrm{res},0}_{j})\bigr)
, where the affine rescaling subtracts the test-level baseline and renormalizes to satisfy Assumption
2.1
exactly. The approximation error from holding
h^{\mathrm{res}}_{j}
at
h^{\mathrm{res},0}_{j}
instead of the equilibrium
g_{j}(h_{j},m^{*}_{j})
scales as
O(|h^{\mathrm{res}}_{j}-h^{\mathrm{res},0}_{j}|\,\partial\tilde{\alpha}_{j}/\partial h^{\mathrm{res}}_{j})
, which is not pointwise small in our ablations (see “Implications” below); the reduced-form is a modelling choice rather than a small perturbation.

Gaussian mechanism (approximate DP).

For a query with
L_{2}
-sensitivity
\Delta_{j}
, the Gaussian mechanism adds noise
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{j}^{2}I)
with
\sigma_{j}=\Delta_{j}\sqrt{2\ln(1.25/\delta)}/\varepsilon_{j}
for
(\varepsilon_{j},\delta)
-DP. Under a one-sided
z
-test of
H_{0}:h^{\mathrm{res}}_{j}=0
versus
H_{1}:h^{\mathrm{res}}_{j}>0
at level
a
,

\tilde{\alpha}_{j}(\varepsilon_{j},h^{\mathrm{res}}_{j})\;=\;\Phi\!\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{j}\,h^{\mathrm{res}}_{j}}{\Delta_{j}\sqrt{2\ln(1.25/\delta)}}-z_{1-a}\right).

At the reference
h^{\mathrm{res},0}_{j}
, the local reduced-form
\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j}):=(\tilde{\alpha}_{j}(\varepsilon_{j},h^{\mathrm{res},0}_{j})-a)/(1-a)
satisfies
\alpha_{j}(0)=0
and
\lim_{\varepsilon_{j}\to\infty}\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j})=1
as Assumption
2.1
requires. Under Rényi DP with order
\alpha
, one substitutes the tighter noise calibration from the RDP accountant
(
21
)
.

Laplace mechanism (pure DP).

For sensitivity
\Delta_{j}
, the Laplace mechanism adds
\mathrm{Lap}(\Delta_{j}/\varepsilon_{j})
. For a one-sided threshold test against null
c
with
h^{\mathrm{res}}_{j}>c
,

\tilde{\alpha}_{j}(\varepsilon_{j},h^{\mathrm{res}}_{j})\;=\;1-\tfrac{1}{2}\exp\!\bigl(-\varepsilon_{j}(h^{\mathrm{res}}_{j}-c)/\Delta_{j}\bigr).

At reference
h^{\mathrm{res},0}_{j}
, the rescaled
\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j}):=2\,\tilde{\alpha}_{j}(\varepsilon_{j},h^{\mathrm{res},0}_{j})-1
satisfies Assumption
2.1
.

Randomised response (local DP).

Under local DP
(
9
)
, each individual answers truthfully with a probability
e^{\varepsilon_{j}}/(e^{\varepsilon_{j}}+1)
. Aggregating
n
responses, the CLT signal-to-noise ratio gives

\tilde{\alpha}_{j}(\varepsilon_{j},h^{\mathrm{res}}_{j})\;\approx\;\Phi\!\left(\sqrt{n}\,h^{\mathrm{res}}_{j}\,\tanh(\varepsilon_{j}/2)-z_{1-a}\right),

strictly increasing in both arguments. The same affine rescaling at the reference yields a reduced-form
\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j})
satisfying Assumption
2.1
. The exponential family
\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j})=1-\exp(-\kappa_{j}\varepsilon_{j})
used in our synthetic evaluation (§
5
) approximates the Gaussian instantiation at moderate
\varepsilon_{j}
via
\kappa_{j}=h^{\mathrm{res},0}_{j}/\sigma_{j}(\varepsilon_{j}=1)
, calibrating the reduced form to a fixed operating point.

Implications for the bilevel analysis.

Treating
\alpha_{j}
as a function of
\varepsilon_{j}
alone is a modelling abstraction; the canonical DP mechanisms above produce
\tilde{\alpha}_{j}(\varepsilon_{j},h^{\mathrm{res}}_{j})
, and across our ablations, the equilibrium
h^{\mathrm{res}}_{j}
varies by
O(1)
relative to a fixed reference (median deviation
\approx 1.07
, script:
scripts/verify_local_approx.py
). We adopt the reduced-form
\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j})
for tractability: the developer’s FOC (
3.1
) and the auditor’s stationarity conditions hold exactly, and Theorem
3.2
’s qualitative conclusion (welfare-detectability misalignment
\Rightarrow
strategic blind spots) is preserved. Generalizing to
\tilde{\alpha}_{j}(\varepsilon_{j},h^{\mathrm{res}}_{j})
replaces the FOC’s
\delta_{j}|\partial g_{j}/\partial m_{j}|
term with
\pi_{j}(\partial\tilde{\alpha}_{j}/\partial h^{\mathrm{res}}_{j})g_{j}+\pi_{j}\tilde{\alpha}_{j}|\partial g_{j}/\partial m_{j}|
, complicating the four-factor stationarity of Theorem
4.1
and changing the quantitative SPAD allocation. The full coupling is future work.

Appendix C
Full experiment setup

This appendix instantiates the synthetic evaluation protocol of §
5
with all design choices fixed, enabling an independent reproduction.

Simulation environment.

All experiments used Python 3.12 with NumPy and SciPy (SLSQP for the developer’s lower-level problem). The hypergradients were computed using forward finite differences with a step size of
10^{-3}
through the developer best response. Each configuration runs with 50 independent seeds drawn from a fixed seed sequence. Code and seeds will be released at acceptance.

Parameterisation.

Harm-space dimensionality
d\in\{5,10,20\}
. Baseline harm
h
is drawn either sparsely (component-wise
\mathrm{Beta}(0.5,2)
) or densely (component-wise
\mathrm{Uniform}[0.5,1.5]
), with the resulting vector normalized to
\|h\|_{1}=d
. Detectability uses the exponential family
\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j})=1-\exp(-\kappa_{j}\varepsilon_{j})
with
\kappa_{j}\sim\mathrm{Uniform}[0.1,2.0]
in heterogeneous regimes and
\kappa_{j}=1.0
in homogeneous controls. Mitigation costs come from a quadratic family
c_{j}(m)=m^{2}/2
and a power-law family
c_{j}(m)=m^{p}/p
with
p\in\{1.5,3\}
. Developer cost budget
B\in\{0.5d,d,2d\}
. Total DP budget
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}\in\{0.1,0.5,1,2,5\}
, spanning tight to loose regimes. Welfare weights
w
are drawn either uniformly (
w_{j}=1/d
) or non-uniformly (
w\sim\mathrm{Dirichlet}(\alpha=0.5)
).

Developer types.

Fully strategic (FS):
solves the lower-level program (
2.1
) to KKT optimality via CVXPY/CLARABEL with gradient tolerance
10^{-6}
. This is the model assumed by SPAD.
Boundedly rational (BR):
runs
K=50
projected-gradient steps from a uniform initialisation
m^{(0)}=(B/d)\mathbf{1}
, with step size
\eta=0.05
and Armijo backtracking; represents a developer with limited optimisation capacity.
Non-strategic (NS):
sets
m_{j}\propto w_{j}h_{j}
, ignoring the audit interface.

Auditor baselines.

Uniform (UNIF):

\pi_{j}=1/d
,
\varepsilon_{j}=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}/d
.
Harm-proportional (HP):

\pi_{j}\propto h_{j}
,
\varepsilon_{j}\propto h_{j}
, normalised.
Welfare-proportional (WP):
analogous with
w_{j}
.
Uncertainty-focused (UF):

\varepsilon_{j}
proportional to the prior standard deviation of
h_{j}
across seeds.
Oracle (ORC):

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}=\infty
, used only as an upper-bound reference; not feasible.
SPAD:
Algorithm
1
with
\eta=0.1\cdot\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}
(decayed by
0.95
every 20 outer iterations),
\delta=10^{-4}
,
T_{\max}=200
, and 5 random restarts (best
B_{w}
reported).

Convergence and projection.

Convergence criterion
\|\nabla B_{w}^{(t)}\|\leq\delta
or
T\geq T_{\max}
. Inner-problem solver is warm-started from the previous outer iterate. Each hypergradient evaluation requires
2d
forward finite-difference perturbations, each re-solving the developer’s lower-level problem. Projection onto
\Delta^{d}
uses the standard simplex projection; projection onto the
\varepsilon
feasible set
\{\varepsilon\geq 0,\sum_{j}\varepsilon_{j}\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}\}
clamps to non-negativity then rescales when the budget is violated.

Metrics.

Welfare-weighted under-detection gap
B_{w}(\pi,\varepsilon)=\sum_{j}w_{j}(1-\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}(\varepsilon_{j}))g_{j}(h_{j},m^{*}_{j})
(the auditor’s objective), reported in absolute terms and as the relative ratio
B_{w}/\mathrm{TRH}
; we also track detected harm
\mathrm{DH}(\pi,\varepsilon,m^{*})
and true residual harm
\mathrm{TRH}(\pi,\varepsilon)
as diagnostics. SPAD efficiency gain is reported as
(B_{w,\mathrm{UNIF}}-B_{w,\mathrm{SPAD}})/B_{w,\mathrm{UNIF}}\times 100\%
. Developer-type degradation:
\Delta B_{w}
when SPAD is optimized assuming FS, but the realized developer is BR.

Statistical methodology.

All metrics are reported as mean
\pm
one standard deviation across 50 seeds, with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals (10,000 resamples). Pairwise comparisons (SPAD versus each baseline) use two-sided paired
t
-tests with the normality assumption checked by Shapiro–Wilk; if normality is rejected, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test is used. Effect sizes are reported as Cohen’s
d
. Multiple-comparison correction across the five baseline comparisons per configuration uses the Holm–Bonferroni procedure.

Ablation design.

Six axes are varied independently with the remaining parameters fixed at the default values
(d=10,\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}=1,\ \mathrm{FS\ developer},\ \mathrm{quadratic\ cost})
.
A1 — Privacy budget:

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}\in\{0.1,0.5,1,2,5\}
.
A2 — Dimensionality:

d\in\{5,10,20\}
.
A3 — Detectability heterogeneity:

\kappa
uniform vs. spread.
A4 — Developer type:
FS vs. BR vs. NS.
A5 — Harm concentration:
sparse vs. dense
h
.
A6 — Cost curvature:

p\in\{1.5,2,3\}
.

Compute.

Each configuration runs in less than 2 min on a single CPU core. The full ablation (six axes
\times
50 seeds, with three values per axis on average) requires approximately 8 CPU-h. No GPU is required.

Appendix D
Broader Impacts

This work is motivated by the growing deployment of differential privacy in AI governance contexts, including fairness audits under data-protection regulation and safety evaluations of high-risk AI systems. The primary positive impact is analytical: by demonstrating that a naïve DP audit design can systematically miss harm concentrated in low-detectability dimensions, the paper provides a principled basis for regulatory bodies to demand strategic audit design. SPAD could be adopted by independent audit firms and compliance teams as a practical tool for allocating limited audit budgets across regulatory harm dimensions (e.g., demographic subgroups, failure modes, and protected attributes).

We identify two potential negative societal impacts that warrant discussion.
First, adversarial use by auditors.
An auditor with strategic interests—for example, an industry self-regulator with commercial ties to the developer—could misuse SPAD to design audits that minimize regulatory exposure rather than true harm. The framework’s assumption that the auditor’s welfare weights
w
reflect genuine social priorities is critical; if
w
is manipulated, the SPAD optimum can be gamed by the auditor rather than by the developer. Safeguards such as independent certification of
w
and public disclosure of audit-design parameters are warranted in practice.

Second, strategic complexity as a barrier to adoption.
The SPAD algorithm requires knowledge of the developer’s cost structure
(c_{j},g_{j})
and mitigation budget
B
to solve the bilevel problem. Regulators with limited technical capacity may find the framework inaccessible relative to simpler heuristic policies. We recommend that future work develop simplified audit-design rules, for example, threshold-based budget allocation, that preserve most of the blind-spot reduction achievable by SPAD without requiring full developer-type knowledge.

This study did not involve human subjects, personal data, or systems deployed in production; the synthetic evaluation protocol used only simulated harm spaces.
