Performance of cardiovascular disease risk prediction tools in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease
Authors: Samaneh Salimian, Nathaniel M Hawkins, Joseph Emil Amegadzie, Mohsen Sadatsafavi, Ricky D Turgeon
Published: 2026

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a chronic, progressive lung disease that affects approximately 10% of adults worldwide.1 Cardiovascular disease (CVD) accounts for one third of deaths in patients with COPD and is arguably the most preventable cause of mortality.2 COPD and CVD share biological and environmental risk factors, including aging, male sex, occupational exposures, smoking, and air pollution.1,3 Traditional risk factors for CVD also cluster in patients with COPD, including dyslipidemia, hypertension, and diabetes.4 Finally, COPD is itself an independent risk factor for CVD, through pathophysiologic mechanisms including inflammation and direct heart–lung interactions.5

In North America and many other regions, use of lipid-lowering medications and other interventions to reduce the risk of CVD is guided by an individual’s predicted CVD risk based on multivariable risk equations.6-8 American guidelines recommend the use of the pooled cohort equations (PCEs) to assess the 10-year risk of a first hard atherosclerotic CVD event,9 while Canadian guidelines recommend the global Framingham Risk Score (FRS) to estimate overall CVD risk over the same period.10 More recently, the American Heart Association introduced the Predicting Risk of Cardiovascular Disease EVENTs (PREVENT) model,11 which is likely to replace the PCEs.

None of these scoring tools includes COPD (or airway diseases in general) as an independent risk factor. Given the increased risk of CVD in patients with COPD, these risk equations developed for the general population may underestimate the risk of CVD among individuals with COPD, leading to underuse of effective therapies to prevent CVD in these patients. This was recently observed in a representative sample of primary-care patients with COPD in the United Kingdom, particularly younger individuals, whose 10-year CVD risk was underestimated by the widely-used QRISK3 score.12 These findings raise concerns about the validity of similar risk assessment tools in patients with COPD.

The main objective of the current study was to evaluate the performance of 3 CVD risk prediction equations—PREVENT, PCEs, and FRS—in patients with COPD and without previous CVD, focusing on validation rather than model development to establish benchmark performance and inform the need for model refinement or redevelopment in this population.

Methods

This study adhered to the transparent reporting of a multivariable prediction model for individual prognosis or diagnosis (TRIPOD) guidelines.13 A completed checklist is provided in Supplementary.

Study design and data sources

We conducted a subgroup external validation study of 3 CVD risk prediction equations, PREVENT, PCEs, and FRS, in patients with COPD identified from 5 longitudinal, community-based epidemiologic North American cohort studies: Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC);14 Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA);15 Jackson Heart Study (JHS);16 Cardiovascular Health Study (CHS);17 and the Framingham Heart Study (FHS) Offspring, OMNI 1, Third Generation (GEN3), OMNI 2, and New Offspring Spouse (NOS) cohorts.18 These cohort studies contributed to the development of the 3 risk prediction models.9-11 Some of the visit data from these cohorts, which were also part of the original model developments, were included in this validation (Tables S1 and S2). There were no major differences in the setting, time horizon, outcome, or predictors between those used in the current study and those in the original risk equation model studies, except for the COPD eligibility criteria specific to this study.

Access to these datasets was granted by BioLINCC, a repository of anonymized datasets from National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute-supported clinical trials and epidemiologic studies. The current study was approved by the Clinical Ethics Review Board at the University of British Columbia, Vancouver, BC, Canada (no. H23-02955).

Patient population

Inclusion and exclusion criteria were aligned with those of the original models, which were broadly similar to one another, having been developed for comparable general populations and benchmarked against one another.

COPD was defined according to the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) guidelines as persistent airflow obstruction based on a post-bronchodilator forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1)/forced vital capacity ratio of < 0.70.3 Symptoms or a clinical or self-reported diagnosis of COPD were not included in the definition due to either incomplete or non-standardized definitions across the cohorts.

We included all patients ≥ 40 years of age from the 5 cohorts who met the definition of COPD based on baseline or subsequent visits with pulmonary function assessment, selecting only study visits that occurred early enough in the calendar timeline to allow a full 10 years of observed CVD follow-up. Accordingly, data from the following visits were included to identify the presence of COPD: visits 1 and 2 for ARIC, visit 4 for MESA, visit one for JHS, visits 3 and 5 to 8 for FHS-OSP, visits one to 3 for OMNI1, and visit one for GEN3, OMNI2, NOS, and CHS (see Figure S1 and Table S1 in the Supplementary). The earliest visit with a COPD diagnosis was used when multiple visits from a cohort were screened for inclusion.

Patients with pre-existing CVD were excluded to align with the intended use of risk prediction tools for primary CVD prevention. Consistent with the development of these CVD clinical prediction models intended for use in patients without prior CVD, we excluded all patients with a baseline history of myocardial infarction, heart failure, and stroke. Each component of CVD was defined according to the specific cohort definitions. Table S7 in the Supplementary details the protocol definitions, and Table S8 describes the methods of ascertainment. Overall, 794 (11.1%) patients were excluded due to prevalent CVD (see Figure S1). Coronary heart disease, without history of myocardial infarction, at baseline was not specifically excluded, as there was no consistent definition of recording coronary heart disease across all cohorts, with some definitions including angina and coronary insufficiency.

Individuals with extreme values for certain clinical variables (see Table S3 in the Supplementary) and those over age 79 (or over 74 for the FRS evaluation) were excluded to align with the population criteria used in the development of the original models.9-11

Outcomes

We assessed the 10-year incidence of total CVD, with the outcome definition matching each of the 3 risk equations being validated. PREVENT defines total CVD as a composite of fatal and non-fatal atherosclerotic CVD (myocardial infarction, fatal coronary heart disease, and stroke [ischemic or hemorrhagic]), and heart failure events. The PCEs define a composite of fatal and non-fatal atherosclerotic CVD; FRS uses a composite of coronary heart disease (coronary death, myocardial infarction, coronary insufficiency, and angina), cerebrovascular events (including ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, and transient ischemic attack), peripheral artery disease (intermittent claudication), and heart failure. Coronary insufficiency, angina, and peripheral artery disease were not included in the atherosclerotic CVD definition for FRS validation in the full COPD cohort due to incomplete ascertainment in most datasets. However, in a sub-analysis using only FHS-derived COPD data, these outcomes were included to validate the FRS model.

Predictors

Age, sex, systolic blood pressure, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, diabetes, current smoking, and use of antihypertensive medications were common across the 3 models. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and statin use were unique to the PREVENT model, whereas race was only included in the PCEs model.

All predictors were collected on the same visit as the spirometry assessment. Following the methodology of the PREVENT study, eGFR in all datasets was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) 2021 creatinine-based equation (Table S4),19 with standardized (in ARIC, and JHS) or calibrated serum creatinine measurements (in MESA, FHS, and CHS, by 5% reduction in creatinine as calibration factor).11

Sample size

The feasibility of model validation and adequacy of sample size were assessed using a simulation-based approach.20 Repeated samples were drawn from a pilot data set (similar in characteristics to the study sample) across varying sample sizes to estimate the expected standard error (SE) of the calibration slope for each prediction model.20 The anticipated SE for the 3 models was ≤ 0.10, corresponding to acceptable precision in the clinically relevant (<20%) risk range.

Missing data

Missing values were present in 1.1% of the predictors in the PREVENT evaluation cohort, 11.7% in the PCEs when race was included, and 1.2% for FRS evaluation cohort. The final cohort of included patients had complete data for all outcomes. When a recent visit value for a predictor was available, the last observation was carried forward. Otherwise, multiple imputation was performed using 5 datasets and 20 iterations per dataset with results combined using Rubin’s rule.21

Statistical analysis

The observed 10-year CVD risk was estimated using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and visualized using Kaplan–Meier plots. For PREVENT, the observed 10-year cumulative incidence was derived using a competing risks framework, consistent with the model’s development, which accounted for non-CVD death.

Model performance was assessed using 3 key metrics: discrimination (the model’s ability to differentiate between individuals who developed CVD and those who did not), calibration (agreement between predicted risks and observed outcomes), and clinical utility.22 The model discrimination was assessed using the time-dependent area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), which accounts for right-censoring and was estimated using the inverse probability of censoring weighting approach.22,23 The model calibration was assessed in 2 ways. Mean calibration was evaluated by the ratio of the observed to the average estimated risk (O/E ratio) in the entire cohort and within subgroups stratified by sex and age. Moderate calibration was assessed using smoothed calibration curves of predicted risks from the original model to predicted risks from a secondary Cox model that served as a proxy for the unobserved event rate among patients censored before 10 years.22,24 To summarize the deviation of the calibration curve from the identity line (perfect calibration), we reported the Integrated Calibration Index, which measures the mean absolute difference between smoothed observed proportions and predicted probabilities, and the E50 and E90 statistics, as median and the 90th percentile absolute differences between observed and predicted probabilities.22,24 To complement smoothed curves, we also compared average predicted with observed event probability across deciles of risk, providing a visualization less sensitive to skewed risk distributions.

Finally, we evaluated the clinical utility of the prediction model using decision curve analysis.25 It assesses whether the model improves clinical decision-making by quantifying its net benefit, which reflects the balance between true positives and false positives, adjusted for the potential harm of unnecessary treatment.25 Net benefit is expressed as the proportion of patients who benefit from model-guided decisions, minus a weighted proportion of false positives (unnecessary treatment), where the weight corresponds to the threshold probability (calculated as threshold/[1 − threshold] or odds being the number of false positives that are worth one true positive).25-27 Net benefit depends on the threshold probability, the level of predicted risk at and above which a clinician would choose to intervene.25-27 By comparing net benefit across different threshold probabilities, decision curve analysis demonstrates whether the model offers greater clinical value than default strategies, such as treating all patients or none. A higher net benefit, at a selected threshold, indicates improved clinical decision-making by identifying more patients who truly benefit from treatment while minimizing overtreatment.25-27 We plotted net benefit across a range of clinically relevant thresholds for each model to reflect different treatment scenarios and patient preferences. For interpretations, we focused on thresholds of 7.5% and 20% based on relevant lipid management guidelines, reflecting the balance of treatment risks and benefits.28,29 Traditional metrics of uncertainty such as confidence intervals are not directly relevant to measures of clinical utility.30 Instead, we calculated the expected value of perfect information (EVPI), the expected loss of net benefit due to uncertainty, for the 2 thresholds of interest.31

The prediction phase was conducted independently of the actual outcomes, or without evaluating the specific values of the predictors to ensure an unbiased evaluation of the model’s performance.

We performed additional sensitivity analyses. Model performance was assessed across COPD severity categories defined by FEV1 impairment according to GOLD 2024 guidelines. Severe and very severe categories were combined due to the limited number of patients with FEV1 < 50% predicted. Percent-predicted values for FEV1 were derived using the race-neutral 2022 Global Lung Function Initiative reference equations,32 implemented through the rspiro package in R.33

We performed a supplementary analysis to evaluate the impact of competing non-CVD death on predictive performance of the PCEs and FRS.

Cohort creation and the extraction of predictors and outcomes were performed using SAS (V.9.4, SAS Institute). Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.2 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing) with relevant packages such as MICE for missing data imputation, survival for survival analysis, cmprsk for competing risk, and timeROC for evaluating discrimination performance.

All 3 models were miscalibrated in patients with COPD (Figure 3, Table 2). PREVENT systematically underestimated CVD risk (O/E ratio 1.25; 95% CI, 1.2, 1.3), whereas both PCEs (0.73; 95% CI, 0.67, 0.80) and FRS (0.67; 95% CI, 0.62, 0.72) overestimated CVD risk. The corresponding differences in 10 years absolute risk were 2.4% underestimation (11.7% vs 9.3%) for PREVENT, and overestimations of 3.1% (8.8% vs 11.9%), and 6.1% (12.3% vs 18.4%) for PCEs and FHS, respectively.

Calibration plots of predicted 10-year risk from the original models (x-axis) vs observed risk in the COPD data for PREVENT, PCEs, and FRS. The secondary Cox models are used as a proxy for unobserved censored data in this calibration plots. Predictions from the original models were transformed using the log(−log) function to linearize their relationship with the survival outcome. These transformed values were then entered as predictors in Cox models, fitted with restricted cubic splines. For PREVENT, the secondary Cox model was replaced with Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard model to account for competing risks. The violet density curves represent the distribution of predicted risks; the y-axis is omitted for clarity, as the density reflects relative concentration.

PREVENT tended to underestimate CVD risk in younger (≤65 years) COPD patients and slightly overestimated the risk in older adults (Table 3), whereas the PCEs and FRS overestimated CVD risk in all age and sex subgroups. Figures S2-S4 provide the decile-based calibration plots for the overall population and subgroups stratified by sex and age.

A similar consistent trend of miscalibration was observed across all 3 models when stratified by COPD severity. Differences between COPD severity categories were small in each model, with overlapping confidence intervals. Confidence intervals were particularly wide in the severe COPD group, reflecting the limited number of patients in this category (Table 3 and Figures S5-S7).

Applying decision curve analysis, all 3 models provided net benefit across the range of guideline-recommend risk threshold for starting lipid-lowering therapy (7.5%-20%) compared to treat-all and treat-none strategies (Figure 4). Clinical utility was greater at lower (eg, 7.5%) than at higher (eg, 20%) risk thresholds when compared with default strategies, except for FRS, which remained unchanged, and slightly greater for the PCEs compared to PREVENT and FRS: respectively PCEs 0.02 vs 0.006; PREVENT 0.01 vs 0.008; FRS 0.01 vs 0.01 (Table 2). At higher risk thresholds, PCEs and FRS had negative clinical utility (at and above 24% and 32%, respectively) (Figure 4). There was little uncertainty around the superiority of these models above the default strategies at risk thresholds of interest, except for PCEs at 20% risk threshold (EVPIs were zero for all 3 models at both 7.5% and 20% threshold, except for PCEs at 20% threshold which had an EVPI of 1.23 × 10−5 in true positive units).

Decision curves for PREVENT, PCEs, and FRS models in the COPD cohort. Solid black line: net benefit, solid red: treat-all, dashed red: treat-none, dashed green lines: selected thresholds (7.5% and 20%). The x-axis represents the threshold probability, the minimum probability at which clinical decisions are made. The y-axis represents the net benefit, which combines the true positive rate and the false positive rate, adjusted for the threshold probability. The maximum net benefit corresponds to the treat-all strategy and reflects the cumulative incidence of the outcome. The higher the curve relative to best default strategy (larger vertical gap or separation between the model and best default strategy) at specific threshold in the decision curve analysis, the greater the net benefit at that threshold probability. The best strategy is to treat-none if the risk threshold exceeds the outcome’s cumulative incidence or treat-all if it falls below. As an example, in PREVENT, a net benefit of 0.06 at 7.5% threshold probability means identifying 6 patients per 100, all of whom will develop CVD (true positives relative to false positives), compared to a default strategy of treating all or none. While the absolute net benefit of the model decreases as the threshold increases, as expected in decision curve analyses (from 0.06 at 7.5% to 0.01 at 20%), the relative improvement over the best default strategy remains similar, indicating consistent added value of the model across clinically relevant decision thresholds. In PREVENT, miscalibration in the form of risk underestimation did not make the model clinically harmful49 (ie, a lower net benefit compared with default strategies), possibly due to its AUC of 0.76. However, in PCEs and FRS, which overestimated risk, the net benefit dropped below the treat-none strategy for risk thresholds of 24% and higher in PCEs, and 32% and higher in FRS, making them clinically harmful (ie, a lower net benefit compared with treat-none) at these thresholds.

Accounting for competing non-CVD death in PCEs and FHS models, modestly reduced the observed absolute risks in older participants, leading to slight shifts in calibration and O/E ratios. However, discrimination (AUROC) remained unchanged (see Table S9 and Figure S8).

In the sensitivity analysis using FHS-derived COPD data, the performance of the FRS model remained largely unchanged, except for minor differences in clinical utility (Tables S10 and S11; Figure S9). Specifically, clinical utility was higher at lower thresholds compared with when the full COPD dataset was used, and lower at higher thresholds (respectively, 2 vs 1 true positive per 100 individuals and 0.2 vs 1 true positive per 100 individuals).

Discussion

We examined the 3 models commonly used to estimate 10-year CVD risk in North America specifically in patients with COPD. Discriminatory performance of the models ranged from poor to good, with PREVENT demonstrating the highest discrimination. However, all models exhibited varying degrees of miscalibration. PREVENT underestimated risk by approximately 30%, whereas the PCEs and FRS overestimated risk to a similar extent. These discrepancies varied by age and sex, with a more pronounced underestimation in younger adults with PREVENT and overestimation in older adults with PCEs. Despite these discrepancies, all 3 models demonstrated clinical utility.

Cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of potentially modifiable morbidity and mortality in patients with COPD.2,34,35 Patients with COPD are 2- to 5-fold more likely to be diagnosed with CVD compared with those without COPD.36 COPD and CVD have a syndemic relationship, clustering through shared biological, social and environmental risk factors and bidirectional pathophysiologic interactions.37 Both are chronic inflammatory disorders in which ‘spillover’ of inflammatory mediators from the lungs into the systemic circulation likely accelerates atherosclerosis and increases hypercoagulability.38,39 All of the major CVD risk factors also cluster in patients with COPD, including smoking, hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, obesity, and physical inactivity.4,40 The relative proportion of disease that could be prevented by elimination of traditional risk factors (ie, the population-attributable risk) vs the disease burden related to COPD per se (ie, the residual risk) is uncertain. Reduced FEV1 and COPD are both independent predictors of CVD incidence, prevalence, and associated mortality in numerous population studies.36,41 For example, in a recent retrospective cohort study of 5.8 million Canadians without CVD, COPD independently increased the risk of major CVD events by 25%.42 However, observational associations are consistently and significantly attenuated in studies with more comprehensive multivariable adjustment, emphasizing the dominance of common risk factors in CVD morbidity and mortality.

The high prevalence of CVD risk factors in patients with COPD defined by chart review or administrative data is well established. However, in a cross-sectional study of over 30 000 patients with COPD in Canadian primary care, the major CV risk factors when defined using objective measures including laboratory results were more common than in age-matched controls, including hypertension (52%), dyslipidemia (62%), diabetes (25%), obesity (41%), and smoking (41%).4 More than half of patients (54%) had a high Framingham risk score. Treatment and control of risk factors has very rarely been assessed. In the Canadian cohort, guideline-recommended targets were only achieved in 61% for hypertension, 47% for dyslipidemia, 57% for diabetes, 11% for obesity, and 12% for smoking. Simple CVD therapies including angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors and statins were notably underused.

Prevention of CVD is founded on obtaining an accurate prognosis from risk prediction models to guide personalized shared decision-making and risk factor reduction using safe, effective, low-cost treatments. It is important to assess model performance when applied in different populations to the original model development, as significant under- or overestimation of risk may lead to incorrect treatment decisions. Growing concerns have emerged regarding the generalizability of CVD prediction models in clinical practice when applied to patients with COPD.43

Validated CV risk prediction models can support personalized risk stratification in patients with COPD. By identifying individuals at highest risk, these tools may guide preventive interventions, optimize clinical decision-making, and improve long-term outcomes. Integration into electronic health records, combined with clinician education and decision support, could facilitate practical implementation in routine care across diverse healthcare settings. Before these models can be integrated into clinical practice, their performance needs to be improved. In a large UK primary care cohort, the QRISK3 score underestimated the observed 10-year CVD risk by 52% overall, with even greater underestimation (82%) in patients younger than 65 years.12 Extending this study, we found the North American guideline-recommended risk models exhibited miscalibration in patients with COPD.

Using the same dataset of individuals with COPD, PREVENT underestimated risk, whereas PCEs and FRS overestimated risk, potentially leading to under- or over treatment, respectively. This is consistent with prior studies showing overprediction by PCEs and FRS in the United States and Canadian general population44-46 However, the magnitude of overestimation was less substantial in our cohort, potentially attributable to the elevated baseline CV risk among patients with COPD. These discrepancies in model performance likely reflect differences between our cohort and the original model development cohorts including variations in the distribution of CVD predictors and the strength of their associations with outcomes.47 For example, patients with COPD were older predominantly male, with more frequent hypertension and smoking exposure. Although the models systematically misestimated risk in relative terms, the absolute difference between predicted and observed 10-year risk was modest. Nonetheless, our findings underscore the need to recalibrate or update risk prediction models for COPD populations to ensure clinical applicability and accuracy.

All 3 models exhibited clinical utility across the range of guideline-recommended treatment thresholds. In practice, a treat-all strategy avoids missed high-risk patients but leads to overtreatment (increases false positives), while a treat-none strategy prevents unnecessary therapy but leaves high-risk patients unprotected (increases false negatives). Decision curve analysis quantifies the net benefit of prediction models by balancing these extremes, with clinical utility demonstrated when a model outperforms both approaches across relevant thresholds. The relative differences in performance between the higher (20%) and lower (7.5%) thresholds were generally consistent across models, with the exception of the PCEs. At the lower threshold, using the PCEs instead of no risk stratification, would correctly identify 2 additional individuals per 100 who would go on to develop CVD, compared with one additional true positive per 100 using the PREVENT or FRS models. At the higher threshold, the incremental benefit of the PCEs was smaller (0.6 additional true positives per 100), while the PREVENT and FRS each maintained an improvement of one true positive per 100 individuals. As in other CV prevention contexts, in other populations, shared decision making between patients and providers remains essential, particularly at lower treatment thresholds where the balance between benefit and burden is more nuanced. Nonetheless, even small improvements in net benefit are valuable, particularly when model inputs are routinely collected, accessible, affordable, and widely implemented in clinical practice.26 Given the observed level of miscalibration, revising these models is likely to improve their clinical utility.

There are several potential strategies to improve CVD risk prediction in patients with COPD. First, models could be updated to incorporate COPD as a predictor, as recently performed in the update from QRISK3 to QRISK4.48 This would partially account for risk associated with COPD per se such as inflammation, which is not captured by other available variables. Second, more granular information could be incorporated on shared risk factors, such as estimation of smoking pack-years rather than categorization as a current smoker or non-smoker. Third, predictors specific to COPD could be incorporated, such as spirometric and inflammatory biomarkers, as well as other novel biomarkers. Additional investigations are typically selected, costly, and lack generalizability for EMR-based models. However, information such as coronary artery calcium scores derived from routinely performed chest computed tomography imaging could potentially be incorporated when available. Finally, models should include additional, clinically relevant CVD outcomes, including HF (as included in PREVENT and FRS but not PCEs) and arrhythmias to capture the full CVD risk burden of COPD.

Several limitations merit consideration. Although we used similar cohorts to the original risk model development to enhance reproducibility, the visits were not identically aligned due to the spirometry schedules. According to GOLD, a diagnosis of COPD requires respiratory symptoms in addition to airflow obstruction.3 This clinical diagnosis was not possible to define due to non-standardized and incomplete respiratory symptom reporting, missing data, and a consequent significant reduction in sample size (Table S6). Incident event rates were modest, likely reflecting the general population derivation, large proportion of patient with mild COPD (approximately 70%), and strict event adjudication as opposed to chart or self-reported CVD.

In conclusion, despite some evidence of miscalibration, the clinical utility of these models, and the decisions based on them, can still outperform default strategies such as “treat-all” or “treat-none” at clinically relevant thresholds. Nonetheless, improving their calibration could enhance prediction accuracy and further increase their net benefit and reliability in guiding clinical decision-making.
