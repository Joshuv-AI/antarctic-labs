Abstract
Diabetes mellitus is one of the fastest-growing global health emergencies and represents a substantial burden on
healthcare systems worldwide. Among its metabolic complications, diabetic neuropathy is highly prevalent and may
cause loss of nerve function, increasing the risk of plantar overload and diabetic foot ulcers. To address this challenge,
NeuroSense was developed as a wearable system designed to monitor plantar pressure and provide real-time
vibrotactile feedback when excessive pressure is detected in predefined areas of the foot. The present study aimed to
evaluate the usability and safety of the device, ensuring that its use does not cause adverse effects. This pilot
feasibility study included four healthy participants who used the system during two consecutive periods of 2.5 hours
while performing their usual activities. Following the intervention, two questionnaires were administered to assess
participants’ perceived ability to modify their gait pattern in response to vibrotactile feedback and their overall
experience with the system. The System Usability Scale was used as a quantitative measure of volunteers’
impressions. The questionnaire 1, which assessed the level of gait pattern redefinition, yielded an average score of
80.63, demonstrating a fair level of effectiveness of the prototype in inducing changes in user behavior. Questionnaire
2, which evaluated the usability of the system, yielded a mean SUS score of 83.13, corresponding to an excellent
usability rating. Overall, the findings support the feasibility and favorable usability of the NeuroSense system in
healthy participants, although further studies with larger and clinical populations are warranted.
Keywords: Diabetes Mellitus; Diabetic Neuropathies; Self-Help Devices.

1. Introduction
In 2021, the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) concluded that Diabetes Mellitus is among the fastest-growing
global health emergencies of the century, accounting for approximately 11.5% of total healthcare investments worldwide
(Magliano et al., 2021). Among the metabolic complications caused by hyperglycemia, diabetic neuropathy (DN) has a high
prevalence among patients (Albers & Pop-Busui, 2014; Fowler, 2008; Hicks & Selvin, 2019). Characterized by peripheral
nerve dysfunction, distal symmetric polyneuropathy (DSP) is the most frequent form of DN, leading to gradual loss of nerve
function, especially in distal limbs, and, in more severe cases, resulting in ulcers and amputations (Boulton et al., 2005; Hicks
et al., 2016; Strand et al., 2024).
Ulcers are skin erosions identified by the loss of epithelial tissue that progresses to deeper layers of the skin, exposing
them to infectious agents. In the case of DN, DFUs (Diabetic Foot Ulcers) are caused by neurological, vascular, and
subcutaneous factors linked to disturbances in the metabolism of carbohydrates, fats, and proteins in the diabetic body (Reiber
et al., 1998; Deng et al., 2023). DFUs are strongly associated with the physical difficulties faced by patients due to the loss of
sensation in distal limbs; they are unable to regulate their posture and maintain balance, resulting in impaired mobility and high
risks of falling (Rinkel et al., 2019; Timar et al., 2016). Mental difficulties are also reported, including higher rates of
socialization challenges, anxiety, depression, pain, suicidal behavior, and economic difficulties due to treatment expenses and
routine adaptations resulting from DFUs (Al-Smadi et al., 2025). Furthermore, this group presents higher mortality rates,

approximately 44% within a five-year period (Sekhar et al., 2015; Moulik et al., 2003; Hoban et al., 2015; Raghav et al., 2018;
Chammas et al., 2016; Chin et al., 2024). Another aggravating factor is DFU recurrence, which affects 6.2% to 41.4% of
patients after healing (Sun et al., 2024). Determinants associated with poor healing include advanced target organ disease and
the inability to walk independently (Prompers et al., 2008).
The tendency for wounds to worsen and lead to amputation can be avoided with appropriate treatment according to
their severity. Typically, debridement, dressings and local care, offloading, vascular assessment, treatment of active infections,
and glycemic control are the set of actions taken to treat DFUs. Additionally, alternatives such as oxygen-based therapies, stem
cell therapy, electrical therapies and negative pressure therapies are being explored, focusing on wound healing (Everett &
Mathioudakis, 2018; Lim et al., 2017).
Despite the variety of existing treatments, there is still a discrepancy between the outcomes desired by patients and
healthcare professionals and reality. Less complex treatment measures capable of reducing the risk of infection and the need
for hospitalization are still in high demand by patients dealing with the high recurrence of DFUs and who constantly require
clinical interventions. In this sense, wearable technologies stand out for their potential to automate part of the treatment and,
consequently, increase adherence to self-care (Miller et al., 2014; Armstrong et al., 2017; Castro-Martins et al., 2024; Kosaji et
al., 2025).
To address this challenge, NeuroSense has been developed. It is a wearable system designed to monitor plantar
pressure and provide real-time vibrotactile feedback when excessive pressure is detected in predefined areas of the foot. The
system consists of three main components: a sensing unit, a control unit, and an alert unit. The sensing unit comprises a tactile
insole with nine pressure sensors distributed across key plantar regions and an electronic module that wirelessly transmits
pressure data to a smartphone. The control unit, implemented through a smartphone application, allows the configuration of
pressure thresholds and stimulation parameters, receives and processes sensor data, and stores the collected information for
subsequent visualization and analysis.
When pressure in a predefined plantar region exceeds the established threshold, the system identifies a pressure peak
and sends a command to the alert unit, a wearable vibrostimulator positioned on the user's waist. The device then delivers a
programmed vibratory stimulus to the skin, providing sensory feedback intended to prompt the user to modify their gait and
redistribute plantar pressure. In this way, NeuroSense is designed to support the protection of vulnerable plantar areas by
promoting gait adaptation in response to excessive mechanical loading, with potential applications in the prevention of plantar
injuries and in the management of existing lesions.
This study aimed to evaluate the usability and safety of the device, ensuring that its use does not cause adverse effects.
Therefore, it represents a preliminary evaluation of the system. Following this initial phase, the device will be tested in patients
presenting symptoms compatible with diabetic neuropathy and its complications, in order to determine whether NeuroSense
can effectively fulfill its intended purposes

2. Methodology
This study is characterized as a pilot feasibility study with an interventional and prospective design. It was conducted
to evaluate the proper functioning of the NeuroSense system, identify potential technical limitations or failures among its
different components, and assess the safety and tolerability of its use in healthy volunteers. The evaluation included the
assessment of potential foot injuries or discomfort associated with the tactile insole, the comfort and stability of the
vibrostimulator when attached to the user’s skin, the adequate perception and tolerability of the vibratory stimulus, and the
absence of any other adverse effects. In addition, the study aimed to determine whether participants could appropriately

Participants were followed prospectively throughout the experimental protocol and underwent two consecutive
periods of 2.5 hours each, corresponding to the different stages of system use.
The experimental group was selected using a convenience sampling approach, with participants recruited through the
dissemination of an invitation document describing the study and its selection process. Four participants were included
according to the following eligibility criteria: being 25 years of age or older, having no diagnosis of diabetic neuropathy, and
presenting no symptoms compatible with diabetic foot syndrome. The absence of diabetic neuropathy was assessed using the
Brazilian version of the Michigan Neuropathy Screening Instrument (MNSI-Brazil), a validated screening tool for the
identification of diabetic peripheral neuropathy.
The experimental protocol was designed to evaluate the user experience with the NeuroSense system across two main
aspects: the participant’s ability to adjust their gait pattern in response to vibratory feedback to protect a specific plantar region
from excessive pressure, and the overall usability of the system.
A mixed-methods study, combining qualitative and quantitative approaches (Gil, 2026; Risemberg et al., 2026a), was
conducted, using a Likert scale to convert qualitative data into quantitative data (Risemberg et al., 2026b) and concepts of
simple descriptive statistics with: data classes, absolute frequency values (counts), and mean scores values, in text (Shitsuka et
al., 2014; Vieira, 2021).
The System Usability Scale (SUS) was used as a quantitative tool to assess the participants’ experience. The SUS was
selected because it is a simple, practical, and objective instrument that can be easily administered to participants and is
appropriate for evaluating the usability of technological systems. The instrument consists of ten statements rated on a fivepoint Likert scale, ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”), providing an overall measure of the user’s
perceived experience with the system. To minimize response bias, odd-numbered items are positively worded, whereas evennumbered items are negatively worded.
As this study involved human participants, the research protocol was submitted to and approved by the Research
Ethics Committee for Human Research - CAAE: 78770924.1.0000.5152.

3. Results and Discussion
The device was tested on participants under the supervision of the research team. To ensure comfort during its usage,
the insole was placed inside a closed shoe belonging to the user, and the vibrotactile stimulation module was attached to the
user’s waist. Volunteers were instructed to perform walking activities and to remain standing at rest, allowing the recording of
the mean plantar pressure levels across different regions of the foot in the absence of vibratory stimulation. Subsequently, they
were instructed to perform the same activities while intentionally controlling the pressure applied to a specific plantar site,
which was randomly selected to simulate the presence of a plantar lesion. This was achieved by adopting different gait patterns
aimed at reducing the mechanical load on the selected region.
The pressure values recorded during this stage were used to characterize the expected pressure distribution patterns
across different regions of the insole and to calibrate the system. These measurements also served to establish the pressure
thresholds required to trigger the vibratory stimulus in response to excessive loading at the selected plantar site.
Following the calibration procedure, each participant was instructed to use NeuroSense for two consecutive periods of
2.5 hours each, while performing usual daily activities, including walking, sitting, and standing up. At the end of each period,
the participant completed questionnaires addressing specific aspects of the device experience. The first questionnaire assessed

The usability of the NeuroSense system was quantitatively assessed using the System Usability Scale (SUS). The
instrument consists of 10 statements rated on a five-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly
agree”). The SUS scoring procedure accounts for the alternating positive and negative wording of the items. For the oddnumbered items (1, 3, 5, 7, and 9), one point is subtracted from the original response score, whereas for the even-numbered
items (2, 4, 6, 8, and 10), the response score is subtracted from 5. Thus, each item contributes a value ranging from 0 to 4, with
higher values consistently representing more favorable evaluations of the system.
The adjusted scores for the ten items are then summed, resulting in a value ranging from 0 to 40, and multiplied by 2.5
to obtain the final SUS score, which ranges from 0 to 100. The resulting score represents a global measure of perceived
usability, with higher scores indicating better perceived experience.
For interpretation, the numerical SUS score was classified according to the acceptability ranges, letter grades, and
adjective ratings proposed by Bangor et al. (2009). These classifications were developed from a large database of SUS
evaluations and were intended to facilitate the interpretation of the otherwise abstract 0 to 100 score. In this framework, scores
above approximately 85 are associated with an “Excellent” adjective rating and an A+ grade, scores around 80 and 85 with
“Excellent”/A, 74 and 80 with “Good”/B, and 68 and 74 with “Good”/C; lower scores correspond to marginal or unacceptable
usability.

The questionnaire 1, which assessed the level of gait pattern redefinition based on vibratory stimulation, yielded an
average score of 80.63. According to the classification by Bangor et al. (2009), this score indicates an acceptable usability,
demonstrating a fair level of effectiveness of the prototype in inducing changes in user behavior.
The second questionnaire, which evaluated the usability, obtained an average score of 83.13, placing the prototype in
the excellent usability range, according to the same reference. When analyzing the usability of the NeuroSense system through
the interpretation of Questionnaire 2, it can be observed that, among the positively worded items, the question related to the
ease of installing the wearable system presented the highest mean score, 4.75/5, indicating that the proper positioning of the
device components was perceived as particularly simple by the participants. The questions related to the level of integration of

Furthermore, the confidence reported by the participants during device use indicates that its operation was perceived
as intuitive and that the learning curve required for autonomous use of the equipment is probably not extensive. A finding that
supports this interpretation is the mean response score for Question 7, which was 4.00/5 and assessed the participants’
perception that most people could quickly learn to use the system. This result suggests that NeuroSense presents an accessible
operational interface, which is an important characteristic for a device intended to be used repeatedly in daily routines.
In addition, the negatively worded items generally presented low mean scores, which also represents a favorable
result. Question 6, which assessed the presence of inconsistencies in the system, presented the lowest mean score, 1.25/5. This
result indicates that the functionalities proposed for the device were perceived by users during the tests and were consistent
with the participants’ expectations regarding how the proposed system should operate to achieve the primary objective of the
study.
Similarly, Questions 2 and 8 presented mean scores of 1.50/5, suggesting that participants did not consider it
necessary to pay excessive attention during installation nor did they perceive the system as excessively complex.
Despite these predominantly positive results, Question 4 was the negatively worded item with the highest mean score
(2.75/5), corresponding to the statement “I felt I would need help from a technical person to be able to use the NeuroSense
system.” This result deserves attention because it indicates a perceived need for technical support when initially using the
device.
In comparison, Question 10, which assessed the need to learn many things before using the system, presented a mean
score of 1.75/5, reinforcing that the main difficulty does not appear to be related to the amount of knowledge required to
operate the device, but potentially to specific aspects of its use that may require initial guidance or technical assistance.

4. Conclusion
Overall, the findings indicate that NeuroSense has significant potential to modify gait patterns while also offering a
favorable usability profile, with participants reporting positive perceptions regarding its installation, integration of components,
ease of learning, and confidence during use. These findings are further supported by the low perception of inconsistencies and
complexity, indicating the system could potentially be incorporated into routine use without requiring extensive learning.
Despite the overall positive assessment, the results also identified a potential limitation related to the need for initial
technical assistance. This need for additional support may imply an indispensable role for a healthcare professional in
introducing and explaining the system’s basic functionalities, which may not be fully understood if the device were obtained
independently by the user. In the context of commercialization or implementation within healthcare services, this requirement
could increase the costs and operational complexity associated with deploying the system.
However, these findings do not demonstrate that NeuroSense has an effect on the prevention or reduction of diabetic
foot ulcers. This potential remains to be clinically validated in a subsequent study involving a larger and more robust sample,
specifically including individuals with diabetic neuropathy, who represent the target population most likely to benefit from the
proposed intervention. Such a study will be necessary to determine whether the observed usability and functional
characteristics of the system translate into meaningful clinical outcomes.
