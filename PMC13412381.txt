Pediatricians’ perceptions of children with disabilities and their health care
Authors: Carolyn C Foster, William Howard Burr, Chloe Somberg, Stefanie G Ames, Lucas Bruton, Elizabeth J Bleed, Garey Noritz, Dennis Z Kuo, Amy Houtrow
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionBecause existing data are limited, we conducted a nationally representative survey to better understand American pediatricians’ views on caring for children with disabilities.MethodsThe American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) 2023 Periodic Survey asked members about their attitudes and perceptions in the care of children with disabilities (33.0% response rate, data weighted to account for nonresponse bias). Descriptive and bivariate statistics were performed; multivariable logistic regressions examined responses adjusting for demographic characteristics.ResultsAmong 463 pediatrician-respondents, 98% strongly/somewhat agreed their practice welcomed patients with disability, but 81% strongly/somewhat agreed that the health care system often treats patients with disability unfairly. While 86% of pediatricians strongly agreed that understanding the needs of patients with disability was valuable to them, only 27% were very confident in their ability to provide the same quality of care to patients with disability as those without disability. Pediatricians who were the most confident in their ability to provide the same quality of care to patients with disability were more than twice as likely to welcome them into their practice.ConclusionResults indicate needed improvements in pediatric training and the health care system to reduce bias against disability and improve pediatricians’ confidence in caring for children with disability.Keywords: children with disabilities, pediatrics, health policy, medical education, ableism

Introduction

One in 4 Americans have a disability, a number that has grown to include more than 70 million American adults.1,2 For the first time, in 2023, the National Institutes of Health (NIH) began to designate people with disabilities as a population with health disparities.1 Occurring over 30 years after passage of the Americans with Disabilities Act, this official designation recognizes that Americans with disabilities face individual and systematic barriers to accessing adequate health care, including preventive services.3-6 In a similar acknowledgement, the US Department of Health and Human Services included disability-specific metrics in its Healthy People 2030 objectives. These metrics include outcomes in health care, health status, employment, and community participation across the lifespan.5

To date, much of the work focused on health care disparities for those with disability has been conducted among adults. Structural biases and personal attitudes both contribute to disability-related health care disparities.4,6-15 Iezzoni et al16 published a 2019 survey analysis on perceptions of people with disability and their health care from physicians caring for adult patients. In the 2019 survey, many adult care providers endorsed concerning attitudes toward people with disability, with only approximately half of participating physicians strongly agreeing they welcome people with disability into their practice; 41% of participating physicians endorsed feeling very confident in their ability to provide the same quality of care for people with disability.16 Focus groups of physicians who participated in a subsequent study shed light into this finding by citing structural barriers, inadequate reimbursement, and lack of knowledge and skill as contributing to their reluctance to provide care for adults with disabilities.12

The increasing population of Americans with disability includes the pediatric population, which has grown to approximately 3 to 4 million children and youth; 1 estimate suggests as many as 1 in 8 children has a disability.17-20 Analyses of children with Medicaid and commercial insurance found that children with disabilities—like adult patients—were less likely than their peers to receive adequate preventive care, acute care, and chronic disease management.17,18 Recent qualitative work among caregivers of children with medical complexity describes caregiver perceptions of lack of clinician knowledge regarding disability and bias within the health care system.21 However, to our knowledge, it remains unknown whether physicians specializing in the care of children have different attitudes toward pediatric patients with disabilities compared with physicians who care for adult patients with disabilities, who often have different patterns of disabilities and may receive care across different health care systems.4,19 However these studies were small or not focused on pediatrician perspectives. Therefore, we conducted a nationally representative survey to further understand American pediatricians’ attitudes and perceptions in the care of children with disabilities.

Data and methods

Survey design and administration

The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) is the largest professional association of pediatricians in the United States, made up of approximately 67 000 board-certified members.22 The Periodic Survey, a 40-year AAP program, is a nationally representative survey of randomly selected, nonretired, US-based AAP members. The 6-page 2023 Periodic Survey included questions adapted from a prior survey of physicians caring for adults with disability, with the lead author's (Iezzoni) permission.16 Adaptation of the survey for pediatric clinician respondents was conducted with subject matter experts on childhood disability as well as parent advocates. Once modified and approved by the AAP Institutional Review Board, the survey was pilot-tested and then sent to 1996 randomly sampled pediatricians beginning in May 2023 (33% response rate, n = 658). The initial mailing included the paper survey instrument and was followed at approximately 5-week intervals by additional requests to nonrespondents, alternating between 3 contact methods: 6 electronic mailings with a link to the survey, 1 postcard reminder, and 3 additional full paper surveys. The first mailing included a $2 bill as a token incentive.

Outcome and predictor variables

Following the item structure from the 2019 Iezzoni et al survey,16 pediatricians were asked 5 questions on their perceptions of children with disability and their care. In advance of these questions, the survey provided a definition of disability, which read, “disability means the patient is usually or always limited in their abilities to do the same things children of the same age typically are able to do, such as walking, taking care of oneself, communicating, etc.” This was a broader definition than that used in the prior 2019 study of adult care providers, which used a narrower definition encompassing only “significant chronic difficulties.” While this difference precludes direct comparisons in most cases, our consensus was that this definition better reflects the varied experiences of children with disabilities across different ages and stages of development. Then, respondents were asked the degree to which they agreed or disagreed with 3 items: “Understanding the needs of my patients with disability is valuable to me as a physician,” “Patients with disability are often treated unfairly in the health care system,” and “I welcome patients with disability into my practice.” Response choices were “strongly disagree,” “somewhat disagree,” “somewhat agree,” and “strongly agree.” As with the prior adult survey,14 responses were dichotomized during analysis and “strongly agree” was compared with the other responses. Pediatricians were then asked to assess their level of confidence in their ability to provide the same quality of care to patients with disability as to those without disability; response choices were “not at all confident,” “not very confident,” “somewhat confident,” and “very confident.” Similarly, responses were also dichotomized for analysis, with “very confident” compared with the other responses. Finally, respondents were asked about the comparable quality of life of children with significant disability: “In general, compared to patients without disability, do you believe the overall quality of life of patients with significant disability is?” Answer choices were “a lot worse,” “a little worse,” “the same,” “a little better,” and “a lot better.” Responses were dichotomized with “a lot worse” and “a little worse” compared with the other responses, mirroring the adult survey.16

The survey also collected personal and practice characteristics including respondent age, race and ethnicity, gender, practice area (urban, suburban, rural), practice setting (solo/2-physician practice, pediatric group practice, multispecialty group practice, medical school or parent university, hospital/clinic/other), primary specialty (general pediatrics, pediatric subspecialty), practice role (full- or part-owner, non-owner), percentage of patients with public insurance (≥50% patients on public insurance, <50% patients on public insurance) and typical monthly patient volume as well as typical monthly volume of patients who have disability (<10%, ≥10% of caseload are patients with disability, dichotomized at the median). Given the small number of racial and ethnic minority respondents, race and ethnicity categories were dichotomized for analysis into those underrepresented in medicine (URM) (ie, Black or African American, Hispanic or Latino/a, multiracial, and other) or non-URM (ie, Asian and White).23

Statistical analyses

All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY). We restricted the analytic sample to post-trainee pediatricians who reported providing direct patient care to children 1 year of age and older, yielding an analytic sample of 463 respondents. We assessed nonresponse bias by comparing respondents with the target sample using key demographic variables available in the AAP membership database (age, gender, geographic region). One-sample proportion tests compared the 2 groups by gender and region, and a t-test compared the 2 groups by age. Respondents differed from the target sample by being older, on average, than the sample (49 vs 45 years; P < .001) and more likely to be men (37% male respondents vs 33% men in the sample; P = .047). To address this nonresponse bias, data were weighted by age and gender.

Descriptive statistics examined personal and practice characteristics. Chi-square tests assessed the associations between respondents’ perceptions about care for patients with disability and personal and practice characteristics. To replicate the prior research,16 we used multivariable analysis to examine the outcome, “I welcome patients with disability into my practice” (strongly agree vs somewhat agree, somewhat disagree, strongly disagree). Similar to the original survey, we fit 3 separate multivariable logistic regression models: model 1 included personal and practice characteristics (described above); model 2 included personal and practice characteristics and 3 variables representing pediatricians’ perceptions of children with disabilities and their care—(1) understanding patients with disability is valuable to me as a physician, (2) patients with disability are treated unfairly in the health system, and (3) quality of life for patients with disability; and model 3 included personal and practice characteristics, pediatrician perceptions of children with disabilities and their care, and confidence in providing the same quality of care for children with disabilities.16 P values < .05 were considered significant in multivariable models.

Limitations

We acknowledge several important limitations of this study. All data are self-reported and pediatricians’ perceptions may not directly coincide with their care decisions for patients with disability. Questions about the quality of care provided are particularly susceptible to social desirability bias. Interpretation of the results is further limited by lack of triangulation with families’ own perspectives. Our analyses included only post-trainee AAP members who reported providing direct patient care to children 1 year of age and older, so our findings may not be generalizable to future pediatricians or those focused exclusively on the care of neonates and infants. Adaptation of questions for the pediatric population limits direct comparison to the prior adult health care survey. Further, the response rate was 33%, leading to nonresponse (selection) bias toward those choosing to participate who may have differing opinions than nonresponders. However, to mitigate this bias, we weighted the data so that the age and gender demographic characteristics of our respondents mirrored those of the target sample and board-certified pediatricians nationally.24 Our cross-sectional survey design precludes inferences about causality and the study was not designed to evaluate disparities in actual care delivered or reasons for the reported responses.

Results

Respondent personal and practice characteristics

Table 1 presents the personal and practice characteristics of participating respondents, two-thirds of whom were female and two-thirds practiced primary care. Respondents were distributed across a range of pediatric practice types and locations, with nearly 8 in 10 being employees (not owners) of their practice. Pediatricians reported providing care for a median of 200 patients per month, but most cared for relatively few children with disability, with pediatricians reporting caring for a median of 5 patients per month with gross motor disability, 5 patients per month with fine motor disability, and 10 patients per month with communication disability.

Pediatricians’ perceptions of care and quality of life for children with significant disabilities

Figure 1 shows the distribution of nondichotomized responses to the questions regarding pediatricians’ perceptions of care and confidence in caring for children with disabilities. Most respondents strongly agreed (86%) or somewhat agreed (12%) that understanding the needs of their patients with disabilities was valuable to them as physicians, while a similar number strongly agreed (76%) or somewhat agreed (22%) their practice welcomed patients with disabilities. The distribution of agreement was more varied among pediatricians’ perceptions of whether patients with disability were “often treated unfairly in the healthcare system” (27% strongly agreed, 54% somewhat agreed). Approximately 9 in 10 respondents endorsed some level of confidence (27% very confident, 62% somewhat confident) in their ability to provide the same quality of care to patients with and without disability. One-third (33%) of pediatricians reported that the quality of life of patients with significant disability was “a lot worse” and over half (56%) said it was “a little worse” than children without significant disability, followed by 12% “the same” and less than 1% for “a little better” and “a lot better.”

Multivariable analysis: perceptions of “welcoming patients with disability into practice”

Overall, three-quarters (76%) of respondents “strongly agreed” that their practice welcomes patients with disability. Table 3 presents the results of 3 multivariable logistic regression models. In all models, pediatricians from racial and ethnic groups that are underrepresented in medicine had significantly higher adjusted odds ratios (aORs) compared with non-URM pediatricians for “strong agreement” that their practice was welcoming of patients with disability. Those who cared for more children with disability as a percentage of their patient caseload were more likely to strongly agree that their practice was welcoming to patients with disability when adjusting for the covariates included in the first 2 models. Model 2 showed significantly higher aORs of respondent practices welcoming patients with disability for pediatricians who “strongly agreed” (vs somewhat agree, somewhat disagree, strongly disagree) that (1) understanding the needs of patients with a disability was valuable (aOR: 5.50; 95% CI: 2.92-10.36), (2) patients with disability are treated unfairly within the health care system (aOR: 2.04; 95% CI: 1.10-3.77), and (3) the quality of life for patients with significant disability was not worse than that of persons without disability (aOR: 2.74; 95%CI: 1.05-7.13). Model 3 found similar associations between welcoming patients with disability and (1) understanding the needs of patients with disability and (2) patients with disability are treated unfairly. The association with perceptions of quality of life for patients with disability was not significant in model 3, nor was the percentage of patient caseload with disability, but the association with pediatricians who were very confident in providing the same level of care for patients with disability was statistically significant (aOR: 2.72; 95% CI: 1.36-5.44).

Discussion

In our nationally representative survey, most pediatrician respondents reported that their practice welcomed patients with disability but that the health care system often treats patients with disability unfairly. Most also reported understanding the needs of patients with disability was valuable to them as physicians, but were less confident in their ability to provide the same quality of care to patients with disability compared with those without disability. In this sample, the majority of respondents reported that quality of life for children with disability is worse than children without disability.

Three-quarters of pediatricians strongly agreed their practice welcomed patients with disability, which compares favorably with findings among adult care providers from a prior study (57%).16 Numerous factors may play a role in welcoming patients with disability into a pediatric practice. One potential factor is the common practice for general pediatricians to accept existing patients’ siblings into their practices, which may, in general, facilitate a broader practice of welcoming new patients. In addition, children with medical complexity, who often have disability, account for a disproportionate number of hospitalizations, thereby increasing the exposure to children with disability during pediatric residency and subspeciality training in these settings. Previous research demonstrated that pediatricians who care for more children with disability, as a percentage of their patient caseload, report feeling more prepared to care for children with disability and are more likely to prescribe therapies and aides for this group (Kirby et al., unpublished data, 2026). Notably, in our multivariable models, pediatricians identifying as from racial or ethnic groups underrepresented in medicine were more likely to strongly agree that they welcome patients with disability into their practice. While it is unknown from this survey what drives the difference in these clinicians’ responses, their own experiences with interpersonal and/or structural racial or ethnic bias may shape their care perspectives on caring for children with disabilities.

Another factor that may negatively influence welcoming children with disability into a practice is inadequate payment. Children with disabilities are much more to likely to live in poverty and receive coverage for their health care through Medicaid than their nondisabled peers.7,25 Thus, insufficient payment to cover the cost of caring for patients who may require more time to serve may, in part, present a challenge for pediatricians who would like to care for more children with disability, but who work in underresourced practices or health systems.26

In our study, pediatrician responses regarding perceptions toward disability also seem to be connected to welcoming children with disabilities into their practice. Pediatricians who reported positive perceptions of quality of life for children with disabilities and agreed that understanding the needs of children with disabilities is clinically valuable were more likely to strongly agree they welcome patients with disability in their practice. However, being more likely to welcome patients with disability might increase pediatrician exposure to this group of patients, which may positively influence perceptions of their quality of life and improve understanding of their needs. This study found that 88% of pediatricians perceive patients who have a significant disability have a worse quality of life than those without a disability. Educational programs aimed at recognizing and addressing implicit and explicit biases have the potential to lead to improved health care access for children with disabilities through multiple pathways. As our study shows, improved pediatrician attitudes toward disability are associated with increased reports of welcoming children with disability, and increased welcoming of these patients may improve pediatricians’ attitudes toward living with disability as a result of more exposure to this patient group. Further, these factors may be mutually reinforcing. Reducing bias among health care workers, including by clinic or hospital staff in addition to pediatricians, is critical to reducing health disparities for those with disabilities. It is also important to note, however, that most pediatricians, both nationally and in our analytic sample, are employees and might not feel empowered to decide which patients are accepted into their practice or whether their practice is welcoming to patients with disability.

Next, pediatricians’ concerns about confidence in care may play a role in their willingness to welcome children with disability into their practice. While more than three-quarters of pediatricians endorsed that they were at least somewhat confident in their ability to provide the same quality of care to patients with disability, this was approximately comparable to adult care providers, despite the fact that adult care providers are much more likely to interface with the 1 in 4 adult patients with disabilities.2,16 Confidence in providing care for patients with disability within pediatrics was higher in male, middle-aged, and specialty pediatric clinicians. Confidence was lowest among the youngest age group, which might be due to their limited time in practice and less exposure to children with disabilities. Most pediatricians in our study reported caring for a small number of patients with disability. To improve care provision, pediatricians need increased exposure to this patient population in medical education and training. This finding has been echoed in pediatric residency training in comprehensive care competency,27,28 demonstrating the need to enhance training to support pediatricians in the care of children with disabilities.29

Enhanced education and training around disability at the trainee and practitioner level may take time to show effects in the workforce, but continued work to create patient- and family-centered education regarding children with disabilities, particularly in partnership with family partners, may be a path to improving provider confidence and transforming perspectives on disability.30-33 The Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act calls for the development of a model disability curriculum and core competencies for all medical trainees.34 However, a recent analysis has shown that only a small proportion of pediatric specialties and subspecialties in the recently updated Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) milestones were related to the care of children with disabilities.35 Expanding teaching opportunities on ableism across trainee levels may be achievable using a flipped classroom model, panel discussions with adult patients and parents, and case-based discussions, along with the creation of disability-specific competencies for pediatric trainees.36-38

It is worth noting that older clinicians in our sample tended to endorse higher levels of confidence than younger clinicians, suggesting that skills may be acquired over time; however, the proportion was still relatively low, suggesting the need for extended training for younger and more experienced clinicians. Further research will need to clarify the training components needed to improve pediatricians’ confidence and skill in childhood disability. For example, pediatricians need knowledge about conditions causing disability, such as awareness of the cerebral palsy diagnostic criteria and equipment needs, as well as skills to assess and treat, such as eliciting spasticity on a muscular skeletal exam to confirm cerebral palsy. This work should include understanding how pediatricians’ perceptions differ between more and less visible disabilities and how improved training can reduce interpersonal bias based on these perceptions. Expanded understanding of needed improvements across disability-related clinical knowledge and skills when building competency requirements will be critical to ensure that pediatricians care for children with disabilities with the same confidence as they do for other children. Continued tracking of pediatricians’ attitudes regarding their care of children with disabilities will allow for longitudinal assessments of these improvement efforts.

Structural ableism, the “structural ableism is a system of historical and contemporary policies, institutions, and societal norms and practices that devalue and disadvantage people who are disabled, neurodivergent, chronically ill, mad, and/or living with mental illness and privilege people who are positioned as able-bodied and able-minded,”12 has been shown to reduce attainment in education, employment, and wealth across the disabled population compared with the non-disabled population and may play a systemic role in the health outcomes of children with disability.11,12,39 Most pediatricians in our study either strongly (27%) or somewhat (54%) agreed that the health care system treated patients with disability unfairly, which is a higher percentage than reported by adult care practitioners (18% and 57%, respectively).16 Recognition of health care system biases by most respondents is an important finding as it reflects their recognition of the challenges families face and demonstrates that further work is needed to ensure children and their families face a just and nondiscriminatory health care system.

In addition to clinician training, health systems can address ableism through human resource efforts and improvements in the built environment. Specifically, health systems can and should consider addressing ableism through educational training of all health care workers, including frontline staff that check in or room patients, and workflow analyses that identify structures and processes that impede care for patients with disabilities.40,41 Additionally, while hospitals must be legally compliant with laws that provide basic requirements for public spaces,42-44 prior research has shown a lack of essential equipment (eg, adaptive exam table, non-infant bathroom changing tables) necessary to accommodate care within the medical space and/or design of spaces to meet the actual volume of patients visiting a health care space (eg, number of disability parking spaces).13,45-47 In total, health systems should review their policies, accommodation procedures, and built environment with those with lived experience to identify gaps that contribute to inequities in access and quality of care.

Collaboration between pediatrician groups and family-led organizations to advocate for just and inclusive policies across society will also be important to addressing these structural barriers. However, health systems can do more now, by incorporating people with disabilities and their families into health care system design and human resources training and expectations to ensure that every interaction, from the time of check-in at a clinic front desk to rooming and postoperative care, considers the individual accommodation needs and dignity of the patient regardless of their disability status.

Conclusion

In a nationally representative survey of 463 practicing pediatricians, the majority of pediatricians report welcoming children with disabilities into their practice. The results of the survey show areas for improvement in pediatric medical education and the health system to increase access to pediatric care for children, reduce bias against disability, and improve pediatricians’ confidence in caring for children with disability. Further research is needed to understand the roles of structural ableism, reimbursement, and health policy on pediatricians’ attitudes toward caring for children with disabilities.
