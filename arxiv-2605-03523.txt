Free sets, thin sets and rainbows for barriers
Lorenzo Carlucci, Oriola Gjetaj

Abstract
. We formulate and prove the generalizations of Friedman’s free set and thin set theorems and of the rainbow Ramsey theorem to colorings of barriers. We analyze the strength of these theorems from the point of view of computability theory proving some upper and lower bounds on the complexity of solutions for computable instances and some uniform computable reductions. We obtain as corollaries some proof-theoretical results on the logical strength of the theorems, in the spirit of reverse mathematics.



1.
Introduction and motivation

Many variants or consequences of Ramsey’s theorem have been extensively investigated in computability, proof theory and reverse mathematics. Among these, the free set theorem, the thin set theorem and the rainbow Ramsey theorem have attracted significant interest in recent decades (see, e.g.,
[
6
,
25
,
19
,
7
,
15
]
).

The free set theorem (
\mathsf{FS}^{n}
) states that, for every coloring of the
n
-subsets of the natural numbers in unboundedly many colors, there exists an infinite set
H\subseteq\mathbf{N}
such that for all
n
-subsets
s
of
H
, the color of
s
is either not in
H
or else is in
s
itself. Such a set is called
free
for the coloring. The thin set theorem, denoted
\mathsf{TS}^{n}_{\omega}
(or just
\mathsf{TS}^{n}
) is a weak variant of the free set theorem asserting that for any coloring of the
n
-subsets of
\mathbf{N}
there is an infinite set
H\subseteq\mathbf{N}
such that the
n
-subsets of
H
avoid at least one color. Such a set is called
thin
for the coloring.
Both the free set theorem and the thin set theorem are consequences of Ramsey’s theorem. Cholak et al.
[
6
]
started the study of the logico-computational content of these theorems. Contrary to Ramsey’s theorem, for any
n\geq 3
, none of these principles for colorings of
n
-subsets codes the halting set. This surprising result was established by Wang
[
25
]
.

The rainbow Ramsey theorem (
\mathsf{RRT}^{n}_{k}
) is an “anti-Ramsey” principle that guarantees, for any coloring of the
n
-subsets of the natural numbers such that no color is used more than
k
times, the existence of an infinite set
H\subseteq\mathbf{N}
whose
n
-subsets are assigned distinct colors (i.e., the coloring is injective on the
n
-subsets). Such a set is called a
rainbow
for the coloring. Galvin
[
13
]
observed that the rainbow Ramsey theorem follows from the classical Ramsey’s theorem. This theorem is studied by Csima and Mileti
[
10
]
and Wang
[
25
]
from the perspective of computability and reverse mathematics. The rainbow Ramsey theorem for pairs (
\mathsf{RRT}^{2}_{2}
) is strictly weaker than Ramsey’s theorem for pairs (
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
) over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
and for no finite dimension
n
and no bound
k
the rainbow Ramsey theorem for
k
-bounded colorings of
n
-sets (
\mathsf{RRT}^{n}_{k}
) does code the halting set
[
25
]
.

In joint work with Le Houérou and Patey
[
2
]
we generalized these principles to colorings of objects of unbounded dimensions, in particular to colorings of sets
s\subseteq\mathbf{N}
such that
|s|=\min(s)+1
. These sets are known as
exactly large sets
or
\omega
-size
sets in logic literature (see
[
4
,
3
,
16
]
).

Ramsey’s theorem for colorings of exactly large sets is computationally and proof-theoretically stronger than the usual Ramsey theorem for fixed finite dimensions (
\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{k}
) and even stronger than Ramsey’s theorem for all finite dimensions (
\forall n\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{k}
). From the point of view of computability, the theorem corresponds to (closure under) the
\omega
-th Turing jump (see
[
8
]
and
[
4
]
).
Generalizations to exactly large sets of the free set, thin set and rainbow Ramsey theorems were studied in
[
2
]
. The free set and thin set theorems for exactly
\omega
-large sets turn out to be significantly stronger than their versions for sets of fixed finite size. In particular, they can code the
\omega
-th Turing jump and imply the system
\mathsf{ACA}_{0}^{+}
. By contrast, the rainbow Ramsey theorem for exactly
\omega
-large sets satisfies cone avoidance and therefore does
not
code the first Turing jump.

The family of exactly large sets forms a barrier in the sense of Nash-Williams better quasi-ordering theory and is known as the Schreier barrier in Ramsey theory and Banach space theory literature (see, e.g.,
[
24
]
). Indeed, Nash-Williams proved that Ramsey’s theorem holds for arbitrary barriers. The computational strength of Ramsey’s theorem for barriers is investigated in
[
8
]
. It is thus quite natural to inquire into the generalization of the free set, thin set and rainbow Ramsey theorems to general barriers.

In the present paper we formulate and prove these theorems and establish some bounds on the computational complexity of their solutions. All principles
\mathsf{P}
studied in this paper have the form

\forall X\,(I(X)\to\exists Y\,S(X,Y))
, where
I
and
S
are arithmetical formulas. We call such statements
\forall\exists
-principles. An
X
satisfying
I
is called an
instance
of
\mathsf{P}
, and a
Y
satisfying
S(X,Y)
is called a
solution
to
\mathsf{P}
for
X
. Our results are naturally stated in terms of strong uniform computable reductions, known as strong Weihrauch reductions (see
[
12
]
).
For
\forall\exists
-principles
\mathsf{P}
and
\mathsf{Q}
, we say that
\mathsf{Q}
is strongly Weihrauch reducible to
\mathsf{P}
,
written
\mathsf{Q}\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}\mathsf{P}
, if there are Turing functionals
\Phi
and
\Psi
such that for every instance
X
of

\mathsf{Q}
,
\Phi(X)
is an instance of
\mathsf{P}
, and every solution
\hat{Y}
to
\Phi(X)
yields a solution
\Psi(\hat{Y})
to
\mathsf{Q}
for
X
.
We occasionally also mention results in reverse mathematics. We refer to
[
12
]
for background and definitions.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we introduce barriers and Nash-Williams’ Ramsey theorem for barriers. In Section
3
we introduce and prove the free set, thin set and rainbow Ramsey theorems for barriers. In Section
4
we prove some lower bounds on their solutions.

2.
Ramsey theory on barriers

We start by fixing some notation. We use
\mathbf{N}
to denote the set of natural numbers and
\mathbf{N}^{+}
to denote the set of positive integers. We use uppercase letters
X,Y,Z
to denote subsets of
\mathbf{N}
which may be finite or infinite. We use lowercase letters

to denote finite subsets of
\mathbf{N}
.
If

we denote by

(respectively

) the set of finite (respectively, infinite) subsets of
X
. For

we denote by

the set of subsets of

Given

. This is stronger than

, which denotes set-theoretic inclusion as usual. The irreflexive versions of the previous relations are denoted by

and

By

. We write

then
F|X=\{s\in F\,:\,s\subseteq X\}
. Thus,

is an abbreviation for

. Let

(the

.

We now introduce the basic definitions concerning barriers. Barriers are combinatorial objects first introduced by Nash-Williams
[
18
]
to define better quasi orderings.

Definition

(Barriers)
.

A set

if the following conditions are met:

(1)

(Base)
\bigcup B=X
;

(2)

(Sperner property) For all
s,t\in B
,
s\not\subset t
;

(3)

(Density) For all
Y\in[X]^{\omega}
there exists an

s\sqsubset Y
.

The set

is called the base of
B
and is denoted by

. The simplest examples of barriers are the families
[\mathbf{N}]^{n}
for

. A trivial barrier often refers to the barrier of singletons,
[\mathbf{N}]^{1}=\{\{n\}:n\in\mathbf{N}\}
.

The family
\{s\subset\mathbf{N}:|s|=\min(s)+1\}
is a barrier, known as the Schreier barrier or as the barrier of exactly
\omega
-large sets (also called
\omega
-size sets). For
X\subseteq\mathbf{N}
the family
\{s\subset X:|s|=\min(s)+1\}
is known as the Schreier barrier on
X

[
24
]
.

Marcone
[
17
]
started the study of barriers in the context of reverse mathematics. Among other things he showed that

proves that if
B
is a barrier then

exists as a set and

is isomorphic to a barrier

with

.

A fundamental fact, proved by Pouzet
[
20

, is that the lexicographic order on barriers is always a well-ordering. Given a barrier
B\subseteq[\mathbf{N}]^{<\omega}
and

, let

if

for the least

such that

B
is a barrier we denote its order type by

[
1
]
characterized the order types of barriers, and proved that they are either of the form
\omega^{n}
for some

, or
\omega^{\alpha}\cdot k
for some

and

. The Schreier barrier is an example of a barrier of order type
\omega^{\omega}
.

If
B
is a barrier on
X\in[\mathbf{N}]^{\omega}
, then for any infinite subset

, the set
B|Y=\{s\in B~|~s\subseteq Y\}
forms a barrier on

, which we call a sub-barrier of

.
Additionally, if
B
is a non-trivial barrier on
X
and

then

is a barrier on

.
Note that

is not in general a sub-barrier of

.

Proposition 1
.

Let

be a barrier on

. Then

is a barrier if and only if there exists

.

Proof.

See the proof of Proposition 2.9 in

. Note that both directions between

3.
Free sets, thin sets and rainbows for barriers

In this section we introduce and prove the natural generalizations of the free set, thin set and rainbow Ramsey theorems to arbitrary barriers.

Definition

(Barrier Free Set Theorem)
.

Fix a barrier
B
on
X\in[\mathbf{N}]^{\omega}
. For every
f:B\to\mathbf{N}
there exists a barrier
B^{\prime}\subseteq B
such that for all
s\in B^{\prime}
if
f(s)\in b(B^{\prime})
then
f(s)\in s
. We say that
B^{\prime}
is free for
f
. If
B^{\prime}=B|Y
for some
Y\in[X]^{\omega}
, we call
Y
a free set for
f
.
We denote this principle by
\mathsf{FS}^{B}
.

Definition

(Barrier Thin Set Theorem)
.

Fix a barrier
B
on
X\in[\mathbf{N}]^{\omega}
. For every
f:B\to\mathbf{N}
there exists a barrier
B^{\prime}\subseteq B
such that the image of
f
restricted to
B^{\prime}
is different from
\mathbf{N}
. We say that
B^{\prime}
is thin for
f
. If
B^{\prime}=B|Y
for some
Y\in[X]^{\omega}
, we call
Y
a thin set for
f
.
We denote this principle by
\mathsf{TS}^{B}
.

Let
k\in\mathbf{N}
. A coloring
f:X\to Y
is called
k
-bounded if for
all
x\in Y

|f^{-1}(x)|\leq k
.

Definition

(Barrier Rainbow Ramsey Theorem)
.

Fix a barrier
B
on
X\in[\mathbf{N}]^{\omega}
and let
k\in\mathbf{N}
.
For every
k
-bounded
f:B\to\mathbf{N}
, there exists a barrier
B^{\prime}\subseteq B
such that
f
is injective on
B^{\prime}
. We say that
B^{\prime}
is a rainbow for
f
. If
B^{\prime}=B|Y
for some
Y\in[X]^{\omega}
, we call
Y
a rainbow for
f
. We denote this principle by
\mathsf{RRT}^{B}_{k}
.

The proofs of the thin set and rainbow Ramsey theorems for barriers are straightforward generalizations of the proofs for the finite dimensional version of these theorems, see sections
3.2
and
3.3
. This observation was indeed the initial motivation for the present project. The case of the free set theorem for barriers is more interesting. We treat this case in the next subsection.

3.1.
Barrier free set theorem

We prove the barrier free set theorem from the barrier Ramsey theorem by generalizing the proof for the Schreier barrier versions of these theorems from
[
2
]
.
Interestingly, some operations that
prima facie
looked specific to exactly
\omega
-large sets naturally generalize to arbitrary barriers. Let
X\in[\mathbf{N}]^{\omega}
and let
S_{X}
be the Schreier barrier on
X
. The following facts are easy to check.

(1) If
s\in S_{X}
and
k\in X
and
k<\min(s)

then
\{k\}\cup\{s_{0},s_{1},\dots,s_{k-1}\}\in S_{X}
.

(2) If
s=\{s_{0},\dots,s_{s_{0}}\}\in S_{X}
and
k\in X
and
s_{i}<k<s_{i+1}
for some
i\in[0,s_{0}-1]
then
\{s_{0},s_{1},\dots,s_{i},k,s_{i+1},\dots s_{s_{0}-1}\}\in S_{X}
.

We jointly generalize the above two properties to general barriers in the following lemma.

Lemma 4
.

Let
B
be a barrier with base
X\in[\mathbf{N}]^{\omega}
and let
s=\{s_{0},\ldots,s_{n}\}\in B
with
s_{0}<\ldots<s_{n}
. Fix
i\in[-1,n-1]
(with
s_{-1}=-1
) and let
k\in(s_{i},s_{i+1})
such that
k\in X
.
Then there exists a (unique) element of
B
of the form

\{s_{0},\ldots,s_{i},k,s_{i+1},\dots,s_{j}\}

with
j<n
. We call such an element the
k
-variant of
s
and denote it by
s[k]
.

Proof.

Such an element must exist by the Density and Sperner properties by considering the infinite set

\{s_{0},\dots,s_{i}\}\cup\{k\}\cup\{s_{i+1},\dots,s_{n}\}\cup X/s_{n}.

Necessarily the desired initial segment must stop not earlier than
\{s_{0},\dots,s_{i}\}\cup\{k\}
(otherwise it would be
a subset of
s
) and not later than
\{s_{0},\dots,s_{i}\}\cup\{k\}\cup\{s_{i+1},\dots,s_{n-1}\}
(otherwise
s
would be a subset of it).

∎

Note that, whenever defined,
s[k]<_{\mathrm{lex}}s
. If
s\in B
and
k\in X
is such that
s_{n-1}<k<s_{n}
, then
(s_{0},\dots,s_{n-1},k)\in B
. For
k<\min(s)
the notion of
k
-variant is closely related with the definition of the relation
\lhd
on a barrier that is a key step in the definition of a better quasi order. For
s,t\in[\mathbf{N}]^{<\omega}
the relation
s\lhd t
is defined to hold if there exists a
u\in[\mathbf{N}]^{<\omega}
such that
s\sqsubseteq u
and
t\sqsubseteq u[1,|u|]
. If
s
is the
k
-variant of

for some
k<\min(t)
then
s\lhd t
holds. If
s
and
t
are members of a barrier and
s\lhd t
, then the reverse implication also holds;
s
is the
k
-variant of
t
for some
k<\min(t)
.

We next observe that barriers are preserved under translations. We use the following notation. If
X=\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots\}\subseteq\mathbf{N}
we define
X^{+}=\{x_{i}+1:i\in\mathbf{N}\}
.

Lemma 5
.

Let
B
be a barrier with base
X\in[\mathbf{N}]^{\omega}
. Let

B^{+}=\{s^{+}\cup\{m\}:s\in B\text{ and }m\in X^{+}/(\max(s)+1)\}

where
s^{+}=\{x+1:x\in s\}
.
B^{+}
is barrier with base
X^{+}
.

Proof.

We verify that
B^{+}
satisfies the Base, Sperner and Density properties:

(Base) We need to show that the base of
B^{+}
is infinite and is equal to
X^{+}
. Notice that every element of
s^{\prime}\in B^{+}
is either
s_{i}+1
with
s_{i}\in X
, or the last coordinate
m\in X^{+}
. Thus,
b(B^{+})\subseteq X^{+}
. Conversely, let
x\in X^{+}
. Then
x=x_{k}+1
for some
x_{k}\in X
. Since
X
is the base of
B
there is some
s\in B
such that
x_{k}\in s
. Let
m\in X^{+}/\max(s^{+})
. Then
s^{+}\cup\{m\}\in B^{+}
and contains
x_{k}+1
.

(Sperner property) For all
s^{\prime},t^{\prime}\in B^{+}
such that
s^{\prime}\neq t^{\prime}
,
s^{\prime}\not\subseteq t^{\prime}
. Let
s^{\prime}=s^{+}\cup\{a\}
and

t^{\prime}=t^{+}\cup\{b\}
for some
s,t\in B
,
a>\max(s)+1
and
b>\max(t)+1
and such that
s^{\prime}\neq t^{\prime}
. Obviously if

s^{+}=t^{+}
then
a\neq b
and therefore
s^{+}\cup\{a\}\not\subseteq t^{+}\cup\{b\}
. So suppose that
s^{+}\neq t^{+}
, i.e.

s\neq t
. By the Sperner Property on
B
we have
s\not\subseteq t
. Suppose
s^{+}\cup\{a\}\subseteq t^{+}\cup\{b\}
.
If
s^{+}\cup\{a\}\subseteq t^{+}
then
s\subseteq t
, which is impossible.

(Density) We want to show that for all
Y\in[X^{+}]^{\omega}
there exists an
s^{\prime}\in B^{+}
such that
s^{\prime}\sqsubset Y
.
By definition we have that
Y=Z^{+}
for some
Z\in[X]^{\omega}
. By the density of
B
on
X

there exists an
s\in B
such that
s\sqsubset Z
. Thus
s^{+}\sqsubset Z^{+}
. Let
z
be the first
element of
Z
larger than
\max(s^{+})
. Then
s^{+}\cup\{z\}\sqsubset Z^{+}
and
s^{+}\cup\{z\}\in B^{+}
.
∎

4.
Lower bounds

The argument used by Jockusch to establish the seminal result that
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
omits
\Sigma^{0}_{2}
-solutions (Theorem 3.1 in
[
14
]
) turned out to be extremely versatile. It stands out as the model whose variations allowed to establish a large number of similar omission results for variants of Ramsey’s theorem. Among those are the increasing polarized Ramsey theorem, Hindman’s finite sums theorem, the free set theorem, the thin set theorem and the rainbow Ramsey theorem. The fertility of Jockusch’s construction is not limited to the lower levels of the arithmetical hierarchy. Indeed, Clote
[
8
]
showed how the scheme of Jockusch’s proof can be lifted to obtain weak anti-basis results with respect to the hyperarithmetical hierarchy. In particular Clote used this idea to obtain lower bounds for the barrier Ramsey theorem
\mathsf{RT}^{B}_{2}
as a function of the order-type of the barrier
B

as shown in Theorem
3
above.

It is thus natural to inquire whether the same scheme of argument applies to the barrier versions of other variants of Ramsey’s theorem for which Jockusch’s argument works in the finite case. In this section we verify that this is the case for the barrier thin set theorem and for the barrier rainbow Ramsey theorem.

is defined on
B_{2}*B_{a}
.

Now let
X\subseteq\mathbf{N}
be an infinite
H_{a}
-computable subset of
b(B_{a})
with characteristic function

.

From Theorem
14
we can deduce the following.
Let
D_{e}
denote the first
2e+2

X
.
Then there exists a
s\in B_{a}
such that
\max(D_{e})<\min(s)
and for all

, we have

g(a,e,x,s)=\varphi_{e}^{H_{a}}(x).

We say that
s

is correct for

D_{e}
.

By choice of

and since
X
is infinite there are exactly
2e+2
numbers
x\leq\max(D_{e})<\min(s)

such that
g(a,e,x,s)=1
(since
s
is correct for
D_{e}
). Since at most
2e
elements
are claimed at stage
s
before substage

, the construction at substage
e

s

claims the least unclaimed numbers

By the minimality of

We briefly observe how some non-provability corollaries follow from the omitting theorems proved in the previous sections. Let
\alpha
be a notation for a computable ordinal. Let
\Pi^{0}_{\alpha}
-
\mathsf{CA}_{0}
be the system axiomatized by

for an ordinal

5.
Conclusions and future work

We formulated and proved natural generalizations of the free set theorem, the thin set theorem and the rainbow Ramsey theorem to colorings of barriers and we initiated the study of their effective and logical strength.

We showed that the barrier free set theorem, the barrier thin set theorem and the barrier rainbow Ramsey theorem are consequences of the barrier Ramsey theorem. Moreover, the barrier thin set theorem and the barrier rainbow Ramsey theorem (for
2
-bounded colorings) are reducible to the barrier free set theorem as is the case in the fixed-dimension versions.

We then showed that for each infinite computable ordinal
\alpha
, the free set, thin set and rainbow Ramsey theorems for barriers of order type
\omega^{\alpha}
have computable instances with no solution computable in
\emptyset^{(\alpha)}
. This was previously known only for
\alpha=\omega
(see
[
2
]
). It remains to investigate whether computable instances of the free set, thin set or rainbow Ramsey theorems can code transfinite jumps. We plan to address this issue in future work.
