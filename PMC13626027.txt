Brain signal variability during rest as a neural mechanism underlying cognitive reserve
Authors: Annabell Coors, Yaakov Stern, Christian Habeck
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Resting-state brain signal variability has been found to vary with age and cognitive function. Neural flexibility has been suggested as a neural mechanism underlying cognitive reserve (CR), a construct that describes better than expected cognition given brain status. We examined the associations between age, resting-state brain signal variability, and CR.Method:Analysis was based on resting-state functional neuroimaging data from 470 participants (ages 20–80) from the Reference Ability Neural Network and the CR studies. Brain signal variability was quantified as the log-transformed standard deviation of the time-varying blood-oxygen-level-dependent signal per region. We then derived variability pattern scores related to age, perceptual speed, episodic memory, fluid reasoning, and vocabulary using Scaled Subprofile Modelling principal component analysis. To formally test whether the cognition-related patterns fulfill the requirements for CR, we examined whether each pattern explained additional variance in cognition beyond brain structural measures, age, sex, education. We checked the robustness of our findings using permutation tests. Finally, we determined which brain regions significantly contributed to each pattern.Results:Brain variability patterns emerged across all four cognitive domains and showed robust associations with CR in the domains of episodic memory, fluid reasoning and vocabulary, explaining between 10.5%−18.3% of variance in cognitive performance. The cognition-related variability patterns inversely mirrored age-related variability patterns in some brain regions.Conclusions:BOLD signal variability plays a role in aging and underlies CR in the domains of episodic memory, fluid reasoning, and vocabulary.Keywords: Resting-state fMRI, Scaled subprofile modelling principal, component analysis, Noise, Cognition, Resilience, Aging

Introduction

Intra-individual variability in brain activity, as measured by the deviation of a brain signal over time, has often been overlooked or considered “noise”. However, previous studies suggest that it represents an informative dimension that reflects the brain’s functional integrity and flexibility and is not captured by the mean-based signals (Garrett et al., 2013, 2010; Grady and Garrett, 2014; Waschke et al., 2021). While greater intra-individual variability in brain activity may imply greater flexibility due to more exploration of available functional network configurations (Faisal et al., 2008; Grady and Garrett, 2014; Mcintosh et al., 2010; Padmanabhan and Urban, 2010), it has also been found that temporal variability is lower during task performance than during rest, which may allow for better signal transmission (He, 2011; Ponce-Alvarez et al., 2015). Further, intra-individual variability in brain activity of selected brain regions during rest has been suggested as a marker of various diseases, including functional neurological disorders (Schneider et al., 2024) and Alzheimer’s disease (Scarapicchia et al., 2018). However, brain activity during rest has been consistently shown to be influenced not only by neuronal signals but also by neurovascular signals (for a good overview see Tsvetanov et al. (2021b)). Still, it could be demonstrated that interindividual differences in brain signal variability persist even after controlling for cardiovascular factors in most (Garrett et al., 2021, 2017; Millar et al., 2020) but not all studies (Tsvetanov et al., 2021a); emphasizing the need to control for cardiovascular factors when analyzing resting-state fMRI data.

Additionally, intra-individual variability in brain activity may be one potential neural implementation of cognitive reserve (CR). CR is defined as “a property of the brain that allows for cognitive performance that is better than expected given the degree of life-course related brain changes and brain injury or disease“ (Stern et al., 2023, p. 101). Thus, CR is a concept that aims to explain the disjuncture between the level of structural brain aging/ brain pathology and cognitive performance (Stern et al., 2023). It is assumed that individuals with higher CR have neural networks with greater capacity and/ or efficiency and/or are better able to compensate for brain pathology by recruiting alternate brain networks (Stern and Barulli, 2019), consistent with the plastic age-related neural changes reviewed by Greenwood (2007). Many life exposures and IQ have been shown to be associated with CR and have therefore been used as sociobehavioral proxy measures of CR (Stern et al., 2020). However, the neural mechanisms underlying CR require further research (Stern et al., 2023). To test for the presence of a true neural mechanism of CR, the CR-test proposed by the “Reserve and Resilience” Collaboratory (https://reserveandresilience.com/) should be used. This test states that a neural mechanism of CR explains variance in cognitive performance beyond the variance explained by brain status and/or moderates the brain status-cognition relationship (Stern et al., 2023, 2020). To test whether intra-individual variability in brain activity reflects a true neural mechanism of CR, we employed a two-step analytical approach: first extracting variability patterns related to the cognitive domain scores, and then we tested whether the extracted variability patterns can explain variance in cognition over and above the variance explained by brain status. This analytical approach is grounded in the Reserve and Resilience framework (Stern et al., 2023), but it permits testing of main effects only. With brain status we refer to a measure that captures both age-related variation and other inter-individual differences in brain structure, including subtle pathological differences. It should consist of continuous measures that include both trait and state effects. Intra-individual variability in brain activity has not yet been examined as a neural implementation of cognitive reserve. However, the association between age and brain signal variability and the relevance of brain signal variability for cognitive performance have already been examined.

Regarding the association between age and brain signal variability, previous findings suggest a complex, brain region-specific pattern across the lifespan. While the overall trend seems to be that variability increases from childhood to young adulthood (McIntosh et al., 2008) and then decreases from young adulthood to older age (Garrett et al., 2010), there seem to be strong brain regional differences. Some brain regions - particularly within the salience network, superior frontal gyrus, inferior temporal gyrus, and cerebellum – have been found to show increased variability in older adulthood (Garrett et al., 2011, 2010; Kielar et al., 2016), while decreased variability in older adulthood has been reported for regions belonging to the default mode network (e.g., angular gyrus, middle temporal gyrus, and superior medial gyrus) and areas relevant to motor control and sensorimotor processing (e.g., precentral and postcentral gyrus, Rolandic operculum, and supplementary motor area) (Garrett et al., 2011, 2010; Nomi et al., 2017).

Studies on BOLD signal variability and cognitive performance suggest an association, particularly in cognitive domains such as perceptual speed, various aspects of memory, and fluid reasoning – although findings have not been entirely consistent (Burzynska et al., 2015; Garrett et al., 2021, 2011; Good et al., 2020; Grady et al., 2023; Millar et al., 2020). When it comes to vocabulary performance, three recent studies in adults found that brain signal variability during rest was not associated cross-sectionally with vocabulary (Burzynska et al., 2015; Garrett et al., 2021; Grady et al., 2023). However, one study found that greater decline in brain signal variability over 2.5 years was associated with a more pronounced decline in vocabulary performance (Garrett et al., 2021).

This project had two aims: the first aim of this project was to characterize associations between age and variability in brain activity during rest, as measured by rs-fMRI. Our second aim was to investigate whether brain signal variability during rest represents a neural implementation of CR. We hypothesized age-related differences in brain signal variability, expecting some regions to show increased and others decreased intra-individual variability in older age. In addition, based on the findings from previous studies (Burzynska et al., 2015; Garrett et al., 2021, 2011; Good et al., 2020; Grady et al., 2023; Millar et al., 2020), we hypothesized that greater brain signal variability represents a neural implementation of CR in the domains of perceptual speed, episodic memory, and fluid reasoning, but not in the domain of vocabulary performance in cross-sectional analyses. To account for possible cardiovascular health confounds, a cardiovascular risk score was regressed out of the brain signal variability data before analyses.

Methods

Participants

This project was conducted using data from the Reference Ability Neural Network (RANN) and the Cognitive Reserve (CR) studies (Habeck et al., 2016; Stern, 2009; Stern et al., 2014). These two studies, which are ongoing at Columbia University, include only participants who are free of psychiatric or medical conditions that could affect cognition. The study procedures were reviewed and approved by Human Research Protection Office and IRB, Columbia University (IRB number: AAAI2752(M02Y16); effective date: 04/21/2026). All participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All procedures were carried out in compliance with the recommendations of the International Council for Harmonisation Good Clinical Practice standards (ICH-GCP). The cognitive assessment was on average 13.5 days earlier than the MRI examination. Resting-state fMRI data were available for 489 participants, with information on educational level available for all but three participants. At least one cognitive domain score could be calculated for 471 of these 486 participants with resting-state and complete covariate data. Of these 471 participants, we were able to determine brain status for all but one participant, leading to a final sample size of 470 participants.

Cognition

Cognitive performance was evaluated across four domains: perceptual speed, episodic memory, fluid reasoning, and vocabulary. Each domain was assessed with three cognitive tasks per domain during the neuropsychological examination. In addition, participants from the RANN study completed three computerized tasks per cognitive domain within the MRI scanner but only behavioral data on task performance were used for the current project. As part of the neuropsychological examination, perceptual speed was assessed using the WAIS-III Digit-symbol task, the Stroop Color and Word Test (Golden, 1975), and the trail-making test A. In the MRI scanner, RANN participants performed the three perceptual speed tasks Digit Symbol, Letter Comparison, and Pattern Comparison tasks (Salthouse and Babcock, 1991). To assess episodic memory performance outside the scanner, three measures from the Selective Reminding Test (SRT) were taken: long-term storage, continuous long-term retrieval, and number of words remembered in the last retrieval (Buschke and Fuld, 1974). Additionally, memory performance in the three in-scanner tasks Logical Memory, Word Order Recognition, and Paired Associates tasks was measured. To assess fluid reasoning performance, the neuropsychological examination included the Matrix Reasoning and the Letter-number Sequencing task of the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale III (WAIS-III) as well as the Block Design test. Within the scanner, we measured fluid reasoning with the Paper Folding, Matrix Reasoning and Letter Sets (Ekstrom et al., 1976) tasks. Lastly, vocabulary performance was measured with the WAIS-III Vocabulary test, the Wechsler Test of Adult Reading (Wechsler, 2001), and the American National Adult Reading Test (Grober and Sliwinski, 1991) during the neuropsychological examination, and with the Synonyms (Salthouse, 1993), Antonyms (Salthouse, 1993), and Picture Naming tasks (Salthouse, 1998) inside the scanner.

The assignment of cognitive tasks to cognitive domains was previously done using factor analysis, and we kept the same factor structure to be consistent with previous work. We then extracted each domain score using confirmatory factor analysis for all participants who had performance data available on at least two tasks in that domain (lavaan package in R (Rosseel, 2012)). Missing values were handled using full information maximum likelihood.

Characterization of brain status

As single global brain structural measures (e.g., total gray matter volume, or mean cortical thickness) only provide a limited estimate of brain status and may also explain little variance in cognitive performance, we performed principal component (PC) analysis on regional cortical thickness, regional cortical and subcortical volume measures, and intracranial volume measures. To achieve this, we used T1-weighted magnetization-prepared rapid gradient echo (MPRAGE) scans that were acquired in a Tesla Philips Achieva MRI scanner with a standard quadrature head coil using the following parameters: TE/TR of 3/6.5 ms, flip angle of 8°, in-plane resolution of 256×256, field of view of 25.4 × 25.4 cm, and 165–180 slices in axial direction with slice-thickness/gap of 1/0 mm. The FreeSurfer 5.1 analysis package was then used to reconstruct the structural T1 scan of each subject and to parcel the cortex into 68 ROIs according to the Desikan-Killiany cortical atlas. We then visually inspected the cortical parcellation and repeated the reconstruction in case of visual discrepancies until the results were satisfactory. To calculate the mean cortical thickness for each of the 68 ROIs, we determined the distance between the gray/white matter surface and the gray/CSF surface at each point across the cortical mesh. We then additionally obtained the volume of the 68 cortical ROIs. To obtain the left and right volumes of the subcortical gray-matter structures brain stem, hippocampus, amygdala, nucleus caudatus, thalamus, putamen, and pallidum, we used the automatically segmented brain volume atlas (ASEG) (Fischl, 2012). We then performed PCA and retained 38 PCs that had an eigenvalue bigger than one. To evaluate the performance of the brain structure-based PCs, we quantified how much variance they explained in the cognitive domain scores. Specifically, these PCs accounted for 24.0% of the variance in perceptual speed, 19.2% in episodic memory, 25.6% in fluid reasoning, and 24.3% in vocabulary performance.

Variability in brain activity during rest

Rs-fMRI data were acquired in 3 Tesla Philips Achieva Magnet scanners using the following parameters: TE/TR = 20/2000 ms, flip angle = 72°, in-plane resolution = 112 × 112 voxels, slice thickness/gap = 3/0 mm, 37 axial slices, and a field of view of 240 mm. Since we were interested in the variability in brain activity during rest, we first pre-processed the rs-fMRI data using a native spaced method developed in-house (Razlighi et al., 2014). In summary, we used FSL’s slice-timer tool for slice-timing correction and FSL’s mcflirt tool to register all volumes to a reference image, which was created by registering all volumes to the middle volume and averaging them using 6 df, 256 bins mutual information, and Sinc interpolation. Frame-wise displacement (FWD) was calculated using the six motion parameters and the root mean square difference (RMSD) of the BOLD percentage signal in the consecutive volumes for each participant, according to Power et al (2012). RMSD was computed on the motion-corrected volumes before temporal filtering and we set the threshold of our RMSD to 0.3%, so that contaminated volumes could be detected and then replaced with new volumes generated by linear interpolation of adjacent volumes. The motion-corrected data were then filtered with a band-pass filter (cutoff frequencies of 0.01 and 0.09 Hz) using FSL’s fslmaths–bptf tool (Jenkinson et al., 2002). In a last step, the motion-corrected, scrubbed, and temporally filtered volumes were residualized by regressing out the FWD, RMSD, lateral ventricular, and the left and right hemisphere white matter signals (Birn et al., 2006).

To derive BOLD-based brain variability patterns, we chose the Scaled Subprofile Modelling principal component analysis (SSM-PCA), which is a multivariate technique with a long history of successful application and development in the Columbia group (Habeck, 2010; Habeck and Stern, 2010; Moeller et al., 1987). The SSM-PCA approach is based on the idea that functionally connected regions are activated together and that there are sources of variation that are shared by multiple brain regions. Thus, it performs PCA for dimensionality reduction and identifies several components that best capture the variability of regional brain activity. It can be used to extract brain variability patterns that maximally correlate with the outcome of interest. In our study, the outcomes of interest were age and cognitive domain scores for perceptual speed, episodic memory, fluid reasoning, and vocabulary. Thus, we applied the SSM-PCA method five times in total. We extracted an age-related brain variability score to compare if our associations are in line with previous findings (Garrett et al., 2011, 2010; Kielar et al., 2016; McIntosh et al., 2008; Nomi et al., 2017). However, the primary focus of our analyses was on the four cognitive domain-specific variability scores.

To quantify the degree of variability in brain activity over time (1), we first parcellated the brain into 264 functionally defined ROIs according to the Power atlas (Power et al., 2011), which provides a functionally derived parcellation suitable for summarizing regional rs-fMRI time-series data. For each participant, the standard deviation (SD) of the BOLD signal was calculated across the rs-fMRI scanning period within each of these 264 ROIs separately. Since the distributions of the temporal variability values were skewed, we log-transformed the SD values (the subject × region of interest (ROI) data matrix) also in line with the original version of the (SSM) technique which assumed multiplicative variability and positive definite signal values. However, this method requires that there are no missing values for any ROI, so we first excluded all ROIs with missing values, resulting in a reduction from 264 to 223 ROIs. We then used age and 4 cognitive domain scores to derive 5 patterns in total. For the derivation of each pattern, we performed PCA in the first step and retained all PCs with an eigenvalue greater than one (assuming a total variance normalization of N), resulting in Npc selected components. This first step was common to all endpoints. (2) For the selected Npc components, we performed Npc brain-behavioural regression analyses with components scores of the PC-sets, 1, 1:2, 1:3, …, 1:Npc as independent variables, and computed the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) criterion for each regression model to select the number of PCs that gave the best model fit. The PC-set with the lowest AIC-value was selected as optimal. (3) We then used this optimal number of PCs and entered their pattern scores as independent variables into a linear regression model, with the outcome of interest serving as the dependent variable. Regression of the outcome of interest onto the selected PC-set yielded the final set of regression coefficients. (4) In the next step, we constructed a composite pattern – a weighted linear combination of the PCs using the regression weights from step (3) – that maximally correlates with the outcome of interest. This weighted linear combination defines a single brain variability pattern associated with that outcome. (5) We then determined the extent to which each person in the sample expresses the brain variability pattern as a pattern score, by computing the inner product between that person’s variability maps and the derived brain-variability patterns. This results in 5 brain variability pattern scores: one for age, and 4 for the cognitive scores. (6) We then calculated four separate regression models to perform the formal CR test, using the following equation: cognition ~ variability score + age + sex + education + 38 brain structural PC scores. We extracted the T-statistic (=beta/SE) for the variability score. We then performed a permutation test by randomly permuting the cognitive outcome and repeated steps 2–6 for 5,000 times. The significance level was estimated based on the location of the point estimate for the T-statistic in the null histogram generated by the permutation procedure.

To understand which ROIs are significantly contributing to the brain variability pattern, we created 1,000 random resamples (drawn with replacement) and determined the robustness of the pattern by calculating the bootstrap z-value for each ROI loading: [z(loading) = original point estimate / standard deviation of the differences between the original point estimate and each bootstrap estimate]. The z-value roughly follows a standard normal distribution, so the z-value can be used to filter for those regions that are robustly associated with the cognitive outcomes (filtering for a z-value > 1.96 equals selecting those ROIs that are significantly associated at a p-value level of 0.05). To obtain the neuroanatomical labels of the significant ROIs, we mapped the coordinates of the regions from the Power atlas to the regions in the AAL atlas. We then visualized the brain regions with robust contributions to the pattern using BrainNet Viewer with the surface template “BrainMesh_CH2withCerebellum.nv” (Xia et al., 2013). If a brain region showed up more than once in a variability pattern with |z|>1.96 and had the same loading sign, we selected the node with the largest magnitude of the z-value. We excluded those regions for which no AAL label could be assigned and arranged the brain regions by AAL label and direction for the visualization and presentation in the table.

For example, a brain signal variability pattern might indicate that higher brain signal variability in brain region A and lower brain signal variability in brain region B is associated with memory performance; i.e., the loading for region A is large and positive, while the loading for region B is large, but negative. An individual who shows high brain signal variability in brain region A and low brain signal variability in brain region B would therefore express this brain variability pattern more strongly (and have a higher brain variability pattern score) than another individual showing high brain signal variability in both brain regions.

Characterization of cardiovascular risk

To characterize cardiovascular risk for each person, we constructed a composite score averaging the following self-reported risk factors: diabetes, hypertension, history of heart disease, and smoking. For diabetes and hypertension, we also distinguished between treated and untreated status, coding no diabetes/hypertension as 0, treated diabetes/hypertension as 0.5, and untreated diabetes/hypertension as 1. We coded history of heart disease as 1 if any of the following questions were answered yes: myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, other heart disease, peripheral vascular disease; otherwise, we coded it as 0. For smoking, we coded it as 1 if any of the following questions were answered yes: cigarette ever, cigarette now, cigar ever, cigar now, pipe ever, pipe now; otherwise, we coded it as 0. The cardiovascular risk score was computed as the mean across available risk factors, such that scores ranged from 0 (no risk factors present) to 1 (all risk factors present at maximum severity). For some participants we had data from two time points available and then selected the time point with the most complete data. We calculated a cardiovascular risk score if at least one risk factor was available, with the mean computed across non-missing values.

Statistical analyses

We first regressed the effects of the cardiovascular risk score out from the BOLD signal variability data, yielding residualized variability data. All SSM-PCA analyses used this residualized variability data to minimize cardiovascular confounds.

To assess the associations between age and brain signal variability, we first tested whether we could successfully derive a brain pattern for age. Next, we investigated whether we could successfully derive brain variability patterns for the four cognitive domain scores perceptual speed, episodic memory, fluid reasoning, and vocabulary. We then performed four separate regression models to test if brain signal variability underlies domain-specific CR using the following formula: cognition ~ variability score + age + sex +education + 38 brain structural PC scores. We extracted the T-statistic for the variability score and compared it to the distribution of T-values from the permutation test.

We performed all statistical analyses in R Studio (version 2022.12.0, R-base version 4.2.2) (RStudio Team, 2019).

Age and brain signal variability

The optimal number of PCs included in the age-related brain variability pattern was 55. We could successfully derive an age-related variability pattern. The brain-region-specific analysis revealed a complex pattern of dissociable neuronal changes (Fig. 1A and 1B). Specifically, older age was associated with higher brain signal variability across multiple distributed neural systems, including (1) subcortical and medial temporal structures (bilateral thalamus, left parahippocampal cortex), (2) primary motor regions (right precentral gyrus), and (3) auditory and visual processing regions (left Heschl’s gyrus, and occipital cortex, including the cuneus). In contrast, lower brain signal variability was observed in older adults in (1) medial default-mode network regions (cingulate cortex and precuneus) and in (2) somatosensory regions (left postcentral gyrus and paracentral lobule). Fronto-parietal regions showed heterogeneous age effects. Increased brain signal variability was observed in parietal association cortex and selected prefrontal regions, including the angular gyrus and supramarginal gyri, lateral and medial superior frontal cortex, whereas decreased brain signal variability was evident in ventromedial orbitofrontal and superior parietal regions. The left and right insula exhibited a divergent pattern with higher variability in the left division and lower variability in the right division in older adults.

Brain signal variability and cognitive reserve

We successfully extracted brain variability patterns across cognitive domains using SSM-PCA. The optimal model retained 3 PCs for perceptual speed, 21 PCs for memory, 18 PCs for fluid reasoning, and 11 PCs for vocabulary. The permutation test assessing unique contributions of the variability score to the cognitive outcome was, however, only significant for memory (p=0.042), fluid reasoning (p=0.011), and vocabulary (p=0.027), but not for perceptual speed (p= 0.376).

Fig. 2 displays robust ROI loadings in the CR-related brain variability patterns in the domains of episodic memory (Panel A), fluid reasoning (Panel B), and vocabulary (Panel C). All three domains showed regionally distributed loadings. When plotting all ROIs identified across the three cognitive domains in a single figure (Fig. 3), we identified one brain region (also same x, y, z coordinates) that was common to all three patterns: the left postcentral gyrus, in which higher variability was associated with better cognitive performance. When we checked for brain regions common to at least two domains and that showed the same directionality, we additionally identified the following regions: left middle cingulate cortex, bilateral inferior frontal gyrus (orbital part), left insula, right precuneus, left paracentral lobule, left superior frontal gyrus, and left supplementary motor area.

The portion of variance in the cognitive domains accounted for by the domain-specific brain variability pattern scores was 6.6% for perceptual speed, 14.5% for episodic memory, 18.3% for fluid reasoning, and 10.5% for vocabulary.

Discussion

In this project we first analyzed whether the variability in BOLD signal during rest differs between adults aged 20 to 80 years, and whether it represents a neural implementation of CR. We found region-specific differences in how age relates to brain signal variability, in line with our hypothesis. For the associations with CR in different domains, we hypothesized that brain signal variability would be related to perceptual speed, episodic memory performance, and fluid reasoning, but not to vocabulary. However, the results suggest that brain signal variability is related to CR in the domains of episodic memory, fluid reasoning, and vocabulary.

We found robust regional differences in how age relates to brain signal variability. Age-related increases in BOLD signal variability were observed across distributed systems: subcortical structures, primary motor regions, sensory processing areas, and fronto-parietal association regions. Some of these findings align with prior rs-fMRI studies reporting an age-related increase in BOLD signal variability in superior frontal gyrus and thalamus (Burzynska et al., 2015; Garrett et al., 2011, 2010; Kielar et al., 2016). In contrast, regions showing lower BOLD signal variability with age included default-mode network regions, somatosensory regions, and ventromedial orbitofrontal and superior parietal regions. There is again some overlap between our study and previous fMRI studies regarding brain regions where older age was associated with lower variability, for example our findings on the default mode network (e.g., precuneus and cingulum) are in line with findings from previous studies (Garrett et al., 2011, 2010).

Since greater brain signal variability goes along with more exploration of available functional network configurations, higher brain signal variability may imply greater flexibility of the brain (Faisal et al., 2008; Grady and Garrett, 2014; Mcintosh et al., 2010; Padmanabhan and Urban, 2010). This could lead to more optimal responses to environmental demands (Faisal et al., 2008; Grady and Garrett, 2014). However, it has also been suggested that lower BOLD signal variability allows for better signal transmission (He, 2011; Ponce-Alvarez et al., 2015). Thus, there may be an optimal level of brain signal variability, and once variability is higher than this optimum, variability may be detrimental, leading to reduced signal transmission and lower performance (McDonnell and Abbott, 2009). The optimum level may vary depending on the function of a brain region (i.e., sensory signal transmission versus association areas). To better understand the observed age-related increases in brain signal variability, its associations with behavioural outcomes are important.

We successfully derived cognition-related brain variability patterns and then demonstrated that these patterns explain variance in cognitive performance over and above brain structural measures, age, sex, and education. This provides empirical evidence that brain signal variability is a potential neural mechanism underlying CR. Our findings align with prior work demonstrating the importance of brain signal variability for cognitive performance (Burzynska et al., 2015; Garrett et al., 2011). We hypothesized cross-sectional associations between brain signal variability and CR in the domains of perceptual speed, episodic memory, and fluid reasoning. Our results confirmed associations in the domains of episodic memory and fluid reasoning, whereas associations with perceptual speed were not robust. Some prior studies also did not find any associations with perceptual speed, and it has been suggested that perceptual speed may rely more on stereotyped responses that require less cognitive flexibility than memory and fluid reasoning (Burzynska et al., 2015). Notably, we also observed an association with CR in the domain of vocabulary performance, which we had not expected based on prior cross-sectional studies that found no link between brain signal variability and vocabulary performance (Burzynska et al., 2015; Garrett et al., 2021; Grady et al., 2023). Still, our finding reconciles with longitudinal evidence that a steeper decline in brain signal variability over time has been found to predict stronger vocabulary decline (Garrett et al., 2021).

Our visualization revealed across the cognitive domains of episodic memory, fluid reasoning and vocabulary a distributed brain activation pattern. Importantly, this dissociation suggests that the functional role of neural variability is not uniform across the cortex but shows regional specificity: optimal levels of brain signal variability seem to vary by functional system. Such a pattern is consistent with the notion that optimal brain function reflects a balance between stability and flexibility, rather than uniformly high or low variability (Garrett et al., 2011).

Furthermore, when we compared the ROIs between the cognition-related and the age-related brain variability patterns, we found that those regions in which higher variability was beneficial for cognitive performance partly overlap with those regions in which variability was lower in older age and vice versa. For example, within a sensorimotor–parietal cluster, older age was associated with lower variability in the left postcentral gyrus and the left paracentral lobule but higher variability in the left supramarginal gyrus and right angular gyrus. The cognition-related pattern showed the opposite arrangement: higher expression of the cognition-related variability pattern was associated with higher variability in the left postcentral gyrus and the left paracentral lobule but lower variability in the left supramarginal gyrus and right angular gyrus. This inverse relationship suggests that the regional profile associated with high cognitive performance represents in some brain regions a reversal of the age-typical pattern. Since the cognition-related variability patterns for episodic memory, fluid reasoning, and vocabulary fulfilled the CR tests, BOLD signal variability may reflect one adaptive neural mechanism in older adults with high CR to sustain cognitive performance despite age-related brain changes.

There is already some evidence that the level of brain signal variability is indeed influenced by structural brain properties, as a study of 28 healthy adults (mean age at baseline: 72 years) showed that changes in cortical thickness partly accounted for age-related changes in brain signal variability (Pur et al., 2019). Further, greater white matter integrity, as measured by global fractional anisotropy, has been associated with greater resting-state brain signal variability after accounting for age in a study including 66 healthy adults aged 60 to 80 years (Burzynska et al., 2015). However, not all brain regions show an inverse pattern, e.g. in some brain regions BOLD signal variability was higher in older adults and associated with greater CR.

We chose rs-fMRI as it may be more applicable to general neuropsychological functioning than data from task-related activation studies, which are specific to the tasks studied and their in-scanner performance. In addition, rs-fMRI and task-related signal amplitude fluctuations have been shown to be linearly related across subjects, which suggests common underlying neuronal and physiological mechanisms (Kannurpatti et al., 2012). We created a cardiovascular risk factor based on externally measured vascular risk factors and regressed its effects out from the BOLD signal variability data, yielding residualized variability data. However, ideally, we would have controlled for cardiovascular factors by checking the BOLD response to hypercapnia (Garrett et al., 2017). Previous research indicates that brain signal variability is a relatively robust marker (Garrett et al., 2017), but we cannot fully exclude the possibility that our results are affected by differences in cardiovascular health. Thus, for a genuine confirmation of brain-variability’s functional role, additional work is needed to further disentangle neuronal from vascular contributions to rs-fMRI brain signal variability. One promising approach is to compare variability measures from rs-fMRI and task-based fMRI within the same dataset.

Another limitation of our study is the cross-sectional nature of the dataset, which precludes causal inference and limits the conclusions that can be drawn regarding CR. Longitudinal evidence, tracking changes in brain signal variability and cognition over time, would provide the optimal test of the CR hypothesis. One factor that limits the generalizability of the results is that our sample was on average highly educated, which likely limits the range of CR levels.

We envision that future studies could leverage brain signal variability in the left postcentral gyrus as an individual-level parameter indicative of CR. For example, subject-specific brain signal variability scores specific to these regions could be incorporated into normative models or used as continuous biomarkers to stratify individuals along a CR continuum. Such an approach would require validation across larger, more diverse samples and longitudinal designs, but could ultimately offer a neuroimaging-based tool to quantify CR in both research and clinical contexts.

Conclusions

Brain signal variability represents one possible neural mechanism underlying CR in the domains of episodic memory, fluid reasoning, and vocabulary, i.e., contributing to cognitive performance beyond brain structural features and demographic covariates. Interestingly, the cognition-related variability patterns for episodic memory, fluid reasoning, and vocabulary partly inversely mirrored age-related differences in variability within a subset of brain regions, pointing towards a potential compensatory mechanism in individuals with high CR.
