Project Summary
Orbia Fluor and Energy Materials (formerly Koura) has successfully developed a new class of fluorinated
electrolyte solvents for lithium-ion batteries. In collaboration with Silatronix and Argonne National
Laboratory, the team synthesized and screened over 20 novel fluorinated compounds, optimizing
formulations that significantly enhance battery performance across critical metrics such as thermal
stability, fast-charging capability, and cycling life at extreme temperatures. Electrolytes with fluorinated
molecules developed in this program demonstrated superior performance in 2 Ah pouch cells, achieving
over 1000 fast-charge cycles with minimal capacity fade, outperforming conventional carbonate-based
electrolytes. Mechanistic studies revealed that the fluorinated electrolytes promote a stable solid
electrolyte interphase at the anode and reduce cathode metal dissolution, contributing to improved long-
term stability. These advancements mark a significant step toward safer, more efficient batteries for
applications ranging from grid storage to defense systems to electric vehicles.

1. Executive Summary
Li-ion batteries are being deployed in a wide range of applications of national importance ranging from
energy storage systems to augment our electricity grid and support AI data center power demand, to
enabling mission-critical military applications, and to powering electric vehicles. The performance
requirements for these applications vary significantly – some require fast charge capability, other need to
operate over a wide operating temperature range – all without compromising safety. The electrolyte
largely dictates these attributes of Li-ion battery performance. Historically, Li-ion battery electrolytes
have been based on carbonate solvents. However, there are alternative fluorinated solvents that can help
alleviate some of these challenges. The literature is rife with examples of fluorinated solvents showing
promise for improved cell performance. However, these examples have yet to demonstrate the full
potential of fluorinated materials to enable previously-inaccessible levels of cell performance and
stability. To achieve a wide range of ambitious performance targets, fluorinated molecules and associated
electrolyte formulations must be systematically optimized and the underlying structure-function
relationships that drive performance benefits must be unveiled through mechanistic studies. As a leading
supplier of fluorinated materials, Mexichem Fluor Inc., (called Koura during the project and now called
Orbia Fluor and Energy Materials) is in a unique position to invent new fluorinated molecules and also
develop electrolyte formulations that can not only meet performance targets, but also cost targets.
Objectives & Approach
The primary objectives of this project were to develop electrolyte formulations based on fluorinated
molecules that improve the performance of state-of-the-art Li-ion cells across multiple dimensions:
thermal stability, low temperature and high temperature cycle life, and fast-charge cycling. A multi-
disciplinary team led by Koura and comprising Silatronix (with significant experience in electrolyte
development, which was acquired by Koura) and Argonne National Laboratory carried out a
comprehensive evaluation of novel fluorinated materials synthesized by Koura, resulting in optimized
electrolyte formulations that can simultaneously meet multiple performance requirements. The key
activities for each organization are shown below.
 Koura
 • Synthesis of fluorinated solvents at small scale initially, and at larger scale (~ 1kg) for promising materials
 • Assessment of properties
 • Delivery of fluorinated materials to Silatronix for electrolyte formulation and testing
 • Use data from testing at Silatronix and Argonne to refine fluorinated material compositions

 Silatronix® (a Koura business)
 • Evaluate the electrochemical, physical, and Li+ solvation properties of the new fluorinated materials
 • Formulate electrolytes with fluorinated solvents for testing at Silatronix and at Argonne
 • Evaluate electrolytes in small pouch cells (~ 230 mAh commercially sourced)
 • Investigate the fundamental mechanism of electrolyte behavior to inform electrolyte development

 Argonne
 • Develop anode and cathode design for pouch cells for testing within this program
 • Conduct performance testing of select fluorinated electrolytes initially in ~30 - 200 mAh cells
 • Fabricate 2 Ah cells and use them for development testing of Koura electrolytes
 • Deliver up to 12 2 Ah cells to DOE testing lab for independent verification of electrolyte performance

The development process began with material synthesis and screening, where over 20 fluorinated
molecules (Koura Development Compounds – KDC) were synthesized and evaluated (Table 1).
Screening criteria included physical properties such as melting and boiling points, viscosity, dielectric
constant, as well as electrochemical stability and safety indicators like flammability and self-
extinguishing time. Down selection was based on manufacturing viability, electrochemical stability, and
similarity to established battery solvents. Subsequent electrolyte formulation testing and optimization

KDC-403 was shown to outperform commercial electrolyte components such as methyl propionate (MP)
and 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether (TTE) in both fast charge and high-

Mechanistic Insights
Mechanistic investigations employed techniques such as SEM, XPS, and NMR to elucidate the role of
fluorinated solvents in solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) formation and transition metal dissolution. These
studies revealed that KDC-403 functions as a diluent, establishing a localized high concentration
electrolyte (LHCE) environment (see page 134). This contributes to the formation of a thinner, more
stable SEI (see page 66), reduces manganese dissolution (see page 132), and enhances cycling stability. It
was also found that a higher degree of fluorination in the solvent structure correlates with improved
electrochemical stability and overall performance.

Scale-Up
During this project, Argonne National Laboratory designed and fabricated NMC811/graphite deliverable
cells. These cells were filled with the top two fluorinated electrolyte formulations developed in this
project, as well as a control baseline electrolyte. Following formation, the cells were handed over to a
different team within Argonne for independent validation (on behalf of DOE) of performance: 0°C
cycling and 4C fast-charge cycling.
The final phase involved scaling up the synthesis of KDC-403 to 200g batches with high purity, and
testing KDC-403 electrolytes in large-format cells. Performance validation was carried out in 2 Ah pouch
cells fabricated at both the University of Michigan (results shown in Figure 3) and Argonne National
Laboratory (deliverable cells under test now). In our testing of cells produced at U. Michigan, in large-
format 2 Ah pouch cells, both 20% KDC-403 and KDC-403 LHCE electrolytes achieved excellent fast
charge cycling stability, with the KDC-403 LHCE exhibiting less than 5% capacity fade over 1000 cycles
and 20% KDC-403 showing less than 8% capacity fade. In contrast, control electrolytes demonstrated
significantly greater capacity fade under identical conditions (Figure 3).
Conclusion
In summary, the project successfully developed and validated a new class of fluorinated electrolyte
solvents, with KDC-403 standing out as the lead candidate. These solvents enable lithium-ion batteries
to operate safely and efficiently across a wide temperature range, offering exceptional fast charge and
cycling stability. The results highlight the value of fluorination in electrolyte materials for improving
stability under extreme operating conditions and demonstrate the success of OF&EM’s approach in
integrating molecular design, comprehensive screening, and mechanistic understanding to advance
battery electrolyte technology. The program resulted in the development of electrolyte formulations based
on KDC-403 that can alleviate multiple issues with today’s state-of-the-art Li-ion cell performance.

2. Major Goals and Objectives of the Project
The objective of the project was to develop and validate Li-ion batteries (LIB) with novel fluorinated
electrolyte solvents capable of electrochemical stability up to 4.5V with a temperature window from
approximately -40°C to approximately 40°C, safety/abuse tolerance, ≥80% capacity retention (≥1000
cycles at ~C/3 at ~0°C) compared to standard conditions; and ≥70% capacity retention (≥1000 cycles at
~4C charge at ~30°C) compared to standard test conditions with reduced gas generation.
To accomplish this goal, we proposed a three-phase research project to develop and optimize Li-ion
battery electrolytes with fluorinated electrolytes. In this project, Koura’s capabilities in the synthesis of
advanced fluorinated materials were augmented by capabilities and expertise of two sub-contractors:
Silatronix® and Argonne National Laboratory.
The proposed research was organized into three budget periods.

 • Budget Period 1 (10/31/2021 – 12/31/2022): Design, Synthesis, and Materials Characterization of
 New Fluorinated Electrolyte Materials.
 • Budget Period 2 (01/01/2023 – 12/31/2023): Development of Fluorinated Electrolyte
 Formulations for Specific Extreme Operating Conditions.
 • Budget Period 3 (01/01/2024 – 06/30/2025): Validation of Extreme Condition Performance in 2
 Ah Pouch Cells
There was a go/no-go decision point and a milestone at the end of each of the three budget periods:
 1. Feasibility of performance demonstrated through physical property characterization (end of BP1):
 ▪ Synthesize and supply 1-3 novel solvents suitable for formulating LIB electrolytes that
 demonstrate electrochemical stability up to 4.5V, and freezing point < -40°C, and/or
 boiling point > 70°C.
 2. Feasibility of performance demonstrated through pouch cell performance testing (end of BP2):
 ▪ Demonstrate improved 0°C cycling (minimum 10% vs baseline) after > 300 cycles.
 3. Demonstrate achievement of technical targets in 2 Ah cells (end of BP3):
 ▪ Demonstrate 80% capacity retention (1000 cycles at C/3 at 0°C) compared to standard
 test conditions.
 ▪ Demonstrate 70% capacity retention (1000 cycles at 4C charge at 30°C) with reduced gas
 generation compared to standard test conditions.

were selected for screening based on their measured properties and structural features which included
linear and branched ethers, dioxolanes, esters, methyl esters and cyclic carbonates. Some of the factors
considered during the down selection process included manufacturing viability, electrochemical stability,
boiling point, and similarity to existing materials used in battery electrolytes. The ten ‘Round 1’ down

selected molecules are hereafter referred to as KDC-301, KDC-303, KDC-401, KDC-402, KDC-403,
KDC-502, KDC-508, KDC-701Z, KDC-702, and KDC-713. Methods were developed at OF&EM for
safety testing including self-extinguishing time and flame propagation of the Round 1 materials, and

                    Quarter 2: 01/01/22-03/31/22
The Round 1 materials were delivered to OF&EM’s U.S. Madison Technology Center (MTC, formerly
Silatronix, site of all electrochemical testing) from OF&EM’s U.K. Technology Center (formerly Koura,
site of all chemical synthesis and scale-up development). All materials were dried over molecular sieves
to <20 ppm moisture as measured by Karl Fisher. Flash point and LiPF6 solubility was measured at MTC
for all Round 1 materials (Table 3). Density, viscosity, dielectric constant and melting point were
measured at the OF&EM U.K. site before shipping (Table 3). Fundamental oxidative (vs platinum
working electrode) and reductive (vs glassy carbon working electrolyte) stability of the Round 1 materials
were analyzed by cyclic voltammetry and linear sweep voltammetry (Figure 8, Figure 9, Figure 10). The
dielectric functional group had the greatest impact on oxidative stability, with the Round 1 materials
showing oxidative stability in the order of linear ethers (701Z, 702) < cyclic ethers (KDC-401, KDC-402,
KDC-403) < ether nitrile (KDC-713) = esters (KDC-502, 508) = carbonates (KDC-301, KDC-303,
control). All materials had good reductive stability, defined as passivation on the second CV cycle.
After the fundamental electrochemical voltammetry testing, a voltage hold screening test was performed
in 230 mAh 4.3V single crystal NMC811/Gr pouch cells. The initial test looked at 5% KDC-701Z, 5%
KDC-402, and 3% OS3 compared to a control electrolyte and 5% FEC. The results showed 5% 402 had
significant parasitic current (i.e., increased cumulative capacity) during the voltage hold which resulted in
decreased post-hold capacity and increased gas generation. This result demonstrated that the 402 material
undergoes significant undesirable side reactions in the NMC811/Gr cells. The other materials (5% 701Z,
5% FEC, and 3% OS3®) showed similar parasitic currents relative to the control indicating these
materials do not undergo significant degradation and were promising for further testing. OF&EM also
initiated synthesis of Round 2 materials for testing which included fluorinated nitriles, beta-ketoesters,
malonates, carbonates and butyrolactones.
                    Quarter 3: 04/01/23-06/30/22
After synthesis development to achieve products at >99% purity, a further four molecules were shipped to
Silatronix for initial screening tests. These new molecules include KDC304, a fluorinated cyclic
carbonate, KDC201, a fluorinated malonate and KDC901 and KDC902, fluorinated nitriles. Synthesis
work was also performed towards a gamma-butyrolactone molecule and a beta-ketoester molecule which
were unsuccessful, and efforts were paused to focus on other materials. Safety measurements were made
of KDC-301, KDC-303, KDC-401, KDC-402, KDC-403, KDC-502, KDC-508, KDC-701Z, and KDC-
702 (Round 1 materials) including self-extinguishing time, flame propagation, and differential scanning
calorimetry (DSC) (Table 5 and Table 6). All KDC materials had improved safety characteristics in self-
extinguishing time compared to commercial carbonates PC and DMC, with either shorter self-
extinguishing times or, remarkably, a nonsustainable flame (low flammability). The materials with
nonsustainable flames were KDC-701Z, KDC-702, KDC-508, KDC-301, and KDC-303. Additionally,

materials with flames lasting less than one second were KDC-713, KDC-401, and KDC-403. By contrast,
DMC had a self-extinguishing time of 171 seconds and PC lasted 32 seconds. DSC of electrolytes
formulated with KDC materials in contact with charged cathode powder (NMC811) were compared to a
control electrolyte, and the onset temperature (higher is safer), peak heating rate (lower is safer), and total

KDC materials (301, 303, 401, 403, 502, 508, 702) were tested at 5% in 230 mAh NMC811/Gr pouches
at 60 ˚C with a 1-week voltage hold at 4.3V. No KDC material improved cumulative capacity during the
voltage hold compared to the control, and KDC-508 increased cumulative capacity and showed
significant consumption from the electrolyte in post-voltage hold electrolyte analysis by NMR. This result
demonstrated that the 508 material undergoes significant undesirable side reactions in the NMC811/Gr
cells while 301, 303, 401, 403, 502, and 702 are all relatively stable at high voltage and temperature. In
fact, KDC-301 and KDC-303 had slightly lower cumulative capacity relative to the control suggesting
slightly improved voltage/thermal stability of these materials. A 1-week voltage hold at 4.3V and 60 ˚C in

elevated cumulative capacity compared to the control (Figure 48) and post-test electrolyte extraction
revealed significant (>10%) consumption of 701Z (Figure 51). These results show that while 5% 701Z
shows similar voltage stability to the control, 20% 701Z is unstable during voltage hold and leads to
increased parasitic current and electrolyte consumption.
                    Quarter 4: 07/01/22-09/30/22
The Round 1 KDC materials were blended as 40% into electrolytes and conductivity and viscosity were
measured from -10 ˚C to 60 ˚C (Table 4). No KDC materials had significantly improved viscosity or
conductivity over the carbonate control, however KDC-403 showed that same viscosities as the control
and only slightly lower conductivities. The KDC material with the next best viscosities and conductivities
was KDC-701Z. The compatibility of KDC-301 with graphite anodes was also assessed and compared to
propylene carbonate (PC). PC is known to co-intercalate with lithium ions between graphite layers, and
cause exfoliation of the graphite sheets, and KDC-301 has structural similarities to PC. The coin cell
results showed that an electrolyte with 30% PC + 70% EMC could not cycle, while an electrolyte with
30% 301 + 70% EMC cycled without problem. The combination of 20% KDC-301 and 24% PC
(remainder EMC) also resulted in graphite exfoliation. Post-test analysis of select cells from the Q3
voltage hold test was performed. Surface analysis indicated that 20% KDC-701Z became incorporated
into the anode and cathode surface layers during voltage hold and may lead to the formation of a thinner
SEI layer on the anode, while the cathode surface layer thickness was not impacted. The KDC materials
were screened through the first low-temperature (-20 ˚C) rate test in coin cells with a 4.2V PC NMC811
cathode and artificial graphite anode (sourced from Argonne National Laboratory’s electrode library).
The test showed significant variability between cell replicates, but overall KDC-701Z, KDC-702, and
KDC-301 showed poor performance compared to the control at the highest 2C discharge rate while all
other materials were similar to the control (Figure 11). Post-test impedance KDC-713 and KDC-502 had
slightly lower impedance than the control cells (Figure 12).

These four KDC materials were added to the electrolyte at 20 v% and evaluated in a wide temperature

Three different characterization tests were used to gain an understanding of the thermal stability of the
Round 1 materials: flame propagation, self-extinguishing time (SET), and differential scanning
calorimetry (DSC). For measuring flame propagation, a Thermoglass braided rope was soaked with 700
µL of the material and ignited. The time for the flame to propagate 10 cm was recorded, and flame
propagation was reported through flame propagation velocity (mm/s) and flame propagation time (s/g).
Results from our measurements are shown in Table 5. Several materials did not produce a sustainable
flame when ignited (NSF), including 701Z, 508, 301, and 303, which indicates these materials have the
highest thermal stability by this metric. The fastest flame propagation velocities were evinced by 402,
403, and 502, while 702, 713, and 401 all had similar, slow flame propagation velocities.
SET was measured by adding 150 µL of the material under test to a coin cell casing and applying a torch
directly to material for ignition. The results of this measurement are summarized in Table 5. As with the
flame propagation test, 701Z, 508, 301, and 303 did not have a sustainable flame. Additionally, 702 had a
non-sustainable flame with the SET test but not the flame propagation, indicating that this material will
burn with a wick, but the bulk material will not burn. For the materials with sustainable flames, 713, 401,
and 403 self-extinguished in less than a second, while 502 and 402 burned for 20-30 seconds.

Overall, the flame propagation and SET measurements showed that the 701Z, 508, 301, and 303 have
superior thermal stability due to an inability to sustain a flame. The 700 series (701Z, 702, 713) and 300
series (301, 303) all showed either non-sustainable flames or low flame propagation velocities and fast
self-extinguishing times, suggesting that fluorinated ether and carbonate functionalities in general show
lower flammability. However, the low flammability of 508 also indicates that the ester structure can be

DSC was used to evaluate the thermal properties of each material as an electrolyte in contact with a
charged cathode material. Each material was formulated into an electrolyte with the general composition
EC/EMC/KDC 24/56/20 w/w + 1 M LiPF6. A control electrolyte of EC/EMC 3/7 + 1 M LiPF6 was also
tested. Coin cells were built with a graphite anode and 4.2V NMC811 cathode, 2325 Celgard separator,
and 50 µL of electrolyte. The cells underwent one charge and discharge at C/10 followed by charging to
4/2V at C/10. Four to five cells per unique electrolyte formulation were disassembled in an argon-
atmosphere glovebox, the charged cathodes were rinsed in DMC, and dried overnight in vacuo. After
drying, the cathode active material was carefully scraped from the current collectors, combined from the
4-5 replicate cells, and homogenized to a fine powder. In stainless-steel DSC pans were added 5 µL of the
charged cathode powders and 2 µL of the same electrolyte formulations used to cycle the cells. The pans
were hermetically closed with gold-coated copper seals and stainless-steel lids, removed from the
glovebox, and placed in the DSC instrument. The pans were heated under nitrogen flow to 350 °C at a
rate of 2 °C/min. Three samples were run per electrolyte formulation. Three metrics are quantified from
the DSC thermal response: onset temperature, peak heating rate, and total heat evolved. The onset
temperature is taken as the temperature where the exothermic response begins. The peak heating rate is
the maximum heat flow value that occurs (maximum peak height). The total heat evolved is the total
integral of the exotherm. The results for each KDC electrolyte are shown in Table 5.

 3.c.4..1. Compatibility of Graphite with KDC-301

Figure 17. Charge/discharge curves for the first cycles for control (black, EC/EMC 3/7 + 1 M LiPF 6), PC (blue, PC/EMC 3/7 +
1 M LiPF6), 301 (red, 301/EMC 3/7 + 1 M LiPF6) and 301/PC (purple, 301/PC/EMC 20/24/56 + 1 M LiPF6) in coin cells with

The compatibility of 301 with graphite was investigated during Q4. The motivation behind this study
came from the similarity between the structure of 301 and propylene carbonate (PC). PC is known to
exfoliate graphite when present at high concentrations in an electrolyte5 and requires special
considerations when formulating the electrolyte for optimal performance; the goal of this study was to
understand if 301 would require the same consideration. Towards this end, a series of coin cells were
made and cycled with the following electrolytes (all solvent ratios by weight): Control (EC/EMC 3/7 + 1
M LiPF6), PC (PC/EMC 3/7 + 1 M LiPF6), 301 (301/EMC 3/7 + 1 M LiPF6), and PC/301 (301/PC/EMC
20/24/56 + 1 M LiPF6). The coin cells used 4.2 V NMC811 cathodes and artificial graphite anodes
sourced from Argonne National Laboratory’s Electrode Library (A-C020A and A-A016). They were
cycled 2.5-4.2 V at C/10 and 30 °C.
Figure 17 shows a representative first charge and discharge curve for each electrolyte. The electrolyte
with PC did not cycle well. The cell capacity reached ~5.5 mAh (compared to 3.2 mAh for the control)
and the voltage did not increase steadily, indicating significant irreversible capacity from electrochemical
degradation. The electrolyte with 301 (red trace) showed similar cycling characteristics as the control, and
therefore does not undergo the same degradation as the PC electrolyte. The 301/PC electrolyte cycling
was similar to the PC electrolyte, indicating that the PC degradation dominated the electrochemical
behavior when both 301 and PC were combined.
Overall, these data showed that 301 can cycle stably against graphite anodes and does not have the same
issues of compatibility that PC demonstrates.

In summary, a new fluorinated malonate, 201, was tested in NMC811/Gr pouch cells and compared as an
electrolyte additive to VC. dQ/dV of the first charge shows that 201 reacts at a slightly lower voltage but
in the same voltage range as VC and EC reduce to form the anode SEI, though the feature relating to EC
and/or VC reduction is not impacted by the addition of 201. Furthermore, the presence of VC reduces the
dQ/dV feature relating to 201 reduction. Thus, the dQ/dV indicates that the addition of VC impacts 201
reactivity, but the addition of 201 does not impact VC or EC reduction. Unlike VC, the addition of 1%
201 also increases the charge capacity on the first charge, decreasing the first cycle efficiency. SEM of
the anodes after formation show that the addition of VC and 201 independently change the morphology of
the SEI compared to an SEI formed in a control electrolyte with just EC, EMC, and DEC carbonates with
LiPF6. Altogether, these data show that 201 is an electrochemically reactive material that does not impact
the anode in the same way as VC, as evidenced by the impact on charge capacity and anode surface
morphology. However, like VC, when added at a high enough concentration (1%), 201 can form a

Page 42 of 166

comprehensive surface layer on graphite. Also like VC, when 201 is present in a high enough
concentration, it will also react on the cathode. When VC and 201 are combined, the reactivity of 201 is
slightly minimized and both materials combine to form a surface layer.
In another study, we performed a mechanistic investigation to try and understand the behaviors
underlying the performance benefits of KDC-403. We started with the hypothesis that the benefits are
related to chemical and morphological effects at the interfaces of the electrodes and the electrolyte.
Specifically, the focusing was on understanding 1) any effects of 403 on the electrode surfaces after
formation (could provide insight into whether 403 impacts the initial SEI formation, which can have long-
lasting impacts on cell performance), as well as 2) whether the nature of the interfaces after long-term
aging can be tied back to performance by analyzing the electrode surfaces after over 300 cycles at high
temperature.
The formation procedure was performed at 45˚C. Cells were charged at C/50 to 4.0V followed by
degassing, charging at C/10 to 4.3V, and discharging at C/10 to 3.75V. A second full cycle was
performed at C/20 and a third and final cycle at C/5. The pouches were 4.3V SC NMC811/Gr pouches
(~2.2 mAh/cm2, with an anode/cathode ratio of 1.04) sourced from LiFun. Prior to performing the
formation test, the pouches were dried for two days under vacuum.

A voltage hold method was utilized for rapid screening of fluorinated materials electrochemically. A
method validation test was performed with a control electrolyte, 5 v% 701Z, 5 v% 402, 5 v%
fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC) and 3 v% of the Silatronix® organosilicon battery material OS3®. In this
test, 230 mAh SC NMC811/Gr pouch cells sourced from an external vendor were filled with each
electrolyte. The cells underwent a formation step, followed by degassing. The cells were then charged to
4.3 V (100% state of charge) and held at that voltage for 7 days at 60 °C. The current required to maintain
4.3 V was measured over time, giving as a result the capacity lost due to parasitic reactions in the cell.
Larger capacity values are associated with greater interaction with the cathode. The cell volume was
measured using the Archimedes method before and after the voltage hold, to give the evolved gas volume.

Page 46 of 166

After the buoyancy measurement, all cells were cycled for 11 cycles at 30 °C to test the post-hold cycling
capacity.
Figure 39 shows the voltage hold capacity for the samples tested. 5% 402 showed significantly (5x)
higher voltage hold capacity than any other electrolyte. In this test, the capacity measures the cumulative
parasitic current required to hold the cell at voltage; in general, higher parasitic current (i.e., higher
capacity during the hold) indicates undesired reactions with the cathode. While the rest of the electrolytes
differed only slightly from the control, the control had overall the lowest voltage hold capacity, followed
by 5% FEC, then 5% 701Z. These data suggest that the electrolyte with 5% 402 shows significant
parasitic side reactions with the cathode, while 5% 701Z and 3% OS3® do so only to a minor extent.

between the pristine and both samples after the high temperature voltage hold (due to the deposition of
decomposition species on the cathode surface), there was no difference in signal between the control and
20% 701Z. These results indicate that there was no difference in surface layer thickness on the cathode
with the addition of 20% 701Z.
On the anode, due to the greater accumulation of surface decomposition there not always a clear substrate
signal that can be used as a marker for surface layer thickness, so depth profiling was performed with
high energy argon ions. XPS was collected at intervals of increasing sputter time. As the surface layer
was removed, lithiated graphite peaks grew in intensity, and the percent composition of a lithiated
graphite peak was calculated as a function of sputter time (Figure 55). The sputtering time in s is equated
to equivalent SiO2 thickness in nm (the thickness of SiO2 that would be sputtered away in an equivalent
amount of time) based on the reported rate by the XPS. These results represent an approximation of the
anode solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) layer thickness, while the actual thickness of the SEI depends on

Page 56 of 166

the exact chemical composition and its sputter rate, which was not determined in this investigation. The
depth profile of the lithiated graphite peak in Figure 55a shows the peak signal appeared at a shorter
sputter time for 20% 701Z compared to the control. Figure 55b shows that the sputter time to the
appearance of this substrate peak was 40% lower for 20% 701Z than control, indicating that the SEI layer
for 20% 701Z was significantly thinner than the control for these samples. These results were congruent
with the SEM anode images in Figure 53 where the control appeared to have more significant surface layer
decomposition.

We investigated the structure-function relationship of KDC-403 by testing structural analogs of 403 in
cells. 403 is a fluorinated dioxolane species, and two other dioxolane molecules were tested, one with no
fluorine (“non-fluoro 403”) and one that is fluorinated but with less fluorine than 403 (“less fluoro 403”).
In general, fluorination can increase oxidative stability and lower flammability, while decreasing
reduction stability and increasing viscosity. This study was carried out to find out if these general trends
held true for this dioxolane family and to map out the exact relationship between fluorination and stability
in NMC811/Gr pouch cells. Figure 126 shows the first cycle dQ/dV in 4.3V SC NMC811/Gr multilayer
pouch cells at 45°C with a baseline control electrolyte (EC/DEC/EMC 1/1/1 v/v + 1v% VC + 1 M LiPF6)
as well as electrolytes with 20v% 403, non-fluoro 403, and less-fluoro 403. The dQ/dv traces of the 403
and less-fluoro 403 electrolytes show no difference from the control, while the non-fluoro 403 has
additional peaks and significant shifts in the electrode intercalation features, including a small new peak
at 2V, a moderate new peak at 3V, and significantly higher peaks at 3.9V and 4.1V with non-fluoro 403
compared to the other electrolytes. These data show that non-fluoro 403 is electrochemically unstable
during the first cycle. The greater peaks at high voltages (3.9V, 4.1V) suggest that at least part of the
instability is due to oxidative reactions.

Overall, the surface analyses after formation and HT cycling show small impacts of electrolyte on

 3.c.6. Manganese dissolution meta-analysis for multiple cycling tests
Transition metal dissolution and deposition on anodes is a common feature of cell aging in NMC-based
batteries. Aging within the cell can lead to transition metal (TM) release from the cathode. Deposition of
transition metals on the anode has been associated with capacity loss, impedance increase, and lithium
loss. In Q12, the relationship between transition metal dissolution and capacity loss under the different
aging conditions tested in this program was studied. The ability of electrolyte composition to mitigate

 3.c.7. Fundamental mechanism investigation: Solvation Study
Previous studies have used NMR analysis of electrolytes to study the solvation structure around lithium
salts.25 We analyzed a series of 21 different electrolyte formulations to understand how the solvation
environment changes as a function of salt concentration and KDC-403 concentration in the electrolyte.
Previous data on the solubility of LiPF6 in pure KDC-403 (Q2 report) showed that 403 could not dissolve
0.1 M LiPF6; in this study, we investigated the solvation properties of 403 as a component in electrolytes
with carbonate solvents. For all NMR analyses, a standard sample of 1% trimethylsilane (TMS) in CDCl 3
was run shortly before the sample of interest. The TMS peak was calibrated to 0 ppm and the samples of
interested were referenced absolutely in MestreNova to the calibrated TMS standard. Figure 152a shows
overlaid 13C-NMR spectra for the carbonate baseline series of electrolytes: EC/EMC 3/7 w/w with no salt
added, 0.25 M, 0.5 M, 0.75 M, 1 M, 1.25 M, 1.5 M, 1.75 M, and 2 M LiPF6 added. The two peaks shown
in Figure 152a for each spectrum are the EC carbonyl carbon (left peak) and the EMC carbonyl carbon
(right peak). As the concentration of LiPF6 in the carbonate solution is increased, both EC and EMC
peaks change position and move to higher chemical shifts (downfield in the NMR spectrum). These
changes in chemical shift are reflective of the average change in local environment around each carbonate
species: for the electrolytes containing a lower ratio of carbonate to LiPF6, a greater fraction of the
carbonate molecules in solution become involved in the lithium solvation shell. The effects of salt
concentration on EC and EMC carbon peak chemical shifts are shown in Figure 152b, with the relative
change in chemical shift (normalized for each species to the chemical shift without salt added) greater for
EC than EMC for all electrolytes. These data show that as the concentration of LiPF6 in the electrolyte
increases and more carbonate solvent molecules become involved in solvating the salt ions, EC
preferentially interacts with LiPF6 over EMC.

In summary, this NMR study of solvation provided key insights into the effect of 403 on the solvation
environments of the carbonate solvents, the lithium ion, the PF6 ion, and 403 itself. The data show that
adding 403 results in an apparent increase in salt concentration for the carbonates and both salt ions. The
403 itself has less participation in solvating LiPF6 than the EC or EMC carbonates. These findings are
consistent with KDC-403 acting in the electrolyte as a diluent which causes a localized high concentration
electrolyte (LHCE),26 whereby the lesser solvating capability of 403 induces greater solvation of LiPF6
with carbonates as well as greater ion pairing within the electrolyte, even while the overall concentration
of LiPF6 (moles per liter of liquid) remains constant. LHCEs have been reported in the literature to
provide significant performance benefits in cells with lithium metal anodes as well as in high voltage, fast
charge, and low temperature test conditions,27 and therefore the benefits observed and reported in the Q7-
Q9 reports with KDC-403 (improved cycling stability during high temperature cycling, fast charge, low
temperature cycling, etc.) are likely related to its action to turn the electrolyte into a localized high
concentration electrolyte.

4. Significant results / key outcomes
• Identified three new fluorinated diluents (KDC-403, 705, and 706) – screened more than 20. These
 diluents can enable local high concentration electrolytes (LHCEs) to have a wide range of application
 benefits and are enabled by fluorinated diluents.

• Developed electrolyte formulations based on KDC-403 that can enable fast-charge (confirmed via 2
 Ah cell testing), enhance 0°C cycling and 45°C cycling.

• Demonstrated that 403-based LHCEs have significant advantages over traditional diluents (BTFE and
 TTE) in graphite/NMC811 cells.

• Post-test analysis of active materials harvested after long duration testing showed that KDC-403
 results in thinner SEI on graphite anode and reduce Mn dissolution from NMC cathodes.

• Argonne designed and fabricated 2 Ah NMC811 / graphite 2 Ah cells as program deliverables. 12
 cells were submitted to DOE for independent testing of the electrolytes developed in this project.

• Independent testing of these formulations underway at Argonne in 2 Ah cells.

• Orbia is continuing to build on the results from this project with development of electrolyte
 formulations with fluorinated components for a wide range of applications.
