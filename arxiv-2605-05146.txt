1.
Introduction

Let
\omega=(\omega_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be the Walsh–Paley system on
\mathbb{I}=[0,1)
, and let
S_{n}f
denote the
n
th partial sum of the
Walsh–Fourier series of
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{I})
. The classical Walsh
analogue of the Fejér–Lebesgue theorem states that the full arithmetic
means

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}S_{n}f

converge almost everywhere to
f
for every
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{I})
; see
Fine
[
2
]
and Schipp–Wade–Simon–Pál
[
9
]
. A
natural refinement, going back to Zalcwasser’s work for the trigonometric
system
[
10
]
, asks how sparse a subsequence of partial sums may
be while the corresponding arithmetic means still reconstruct every
integrable function almost everywhere. For trigonometric Fourier series, Gát

[
4
]
proved that the arithmetic means of the partial sums taken
along a lacunary subsequence converge almost everywhere for every integrable
function. On the other hand, in
[
5
]
he answered a question posed
by Zalcwasser
[
10
]
by constructing a subsequence and an
integrable function for which the arithmetic means diverge almost everywhere
along this subsequence. We also refer the reader to recent papers
[
7
,
8
]
on the divergence of Fourier series along subsequences.

For a strictly increasing sequence of positive integers
a=(a(n))_{n\geq 1}
,
define

(1.1)

\sigma_{N}f:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}S_{a(n)}f,\qquad N\in\mathbb{N}.

If
a
is lacunary, then the almost everywhere convergence
\sigma_{N}f\to f

for all
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{I})
was proved by Gát
[
3
]
. Later,
Gát showed in
[
6
]
that the same conclusion remains valid
whenever

(1.2)

a(n+1)\geq\left(1+\frac{1}{n^{\delta}}\right)a(n)\qquad(n\in\mathbb{N})

for some
0<\delta<1/2
.

The purpose of this note is to extend the conclusion of
[
6
]
from the range
0<\delta<1/2
to the full range
0<\delta<1
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{I})
, and let
a=(a(n))_{n\geq 1}
be
an increasing sequence of positive integers such that

(1.3)

a(n+1)\geq\left(1+\frac{1}{n^{\delta}}\right)a(n)\qquad(n\in\mathbb{N})

for some
0<\delta<1
. Then

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}S_{a(n)}f(x)=f(x)\qquad\text{for
almost every }x\in\mathbb{I}.

The proof follows the general strategy of
[
6
]
, but with a
different decomposition of the index set. Instead of grouping the averages
in intervals of length
N^{2\delta}
, we work on dyadic shells
2^{m}\leq N<2^{m+1}
and split each shell into residue classes modulo
R_{m}\asymp 2^{m\delta}
. The growth assumption then recovers lacunarity inside each
residue class, while the prefactor
R_{m}/2^{m}
leads to a summable series
\sum_{m}2^{-m(1-\delta)}
, which is precisely why the argument works for
every
\delta<1
.

2.
Walsh preliminaries and a kernel decomposition

We follow the notation of
[
6
,
9
]
. Write

\mathbb{I}=[0,1),\qquad x=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}x_{j}2^{-j-1},\qquad n=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}n_{j}2^{j},

where
x_{j},n_{j}\in\{0,1\}
. For dyadic rationals we choose the terminating
expansion. Dyadic addition is defined by

x\dotplus y:=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\lvert x_{j}-y_{j}\rvert 2^{-j-1}.

For
x\in\mathbb{I}
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
, let

I_{m}(x):=\{y\in\mathbb{I}:y_{0}=x_{0},\dots,y_{m-1}=x_{m-1}\},\qquad I_{m}:=I_{m}(0).

Let
\mathcal{A}_{m}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the dyadic
intervals of rank
m
, and let
\mathbb{E}_{m}
be the corresponding
conditional expectation.

The Rademacher functions are
r_{j}(x)=(-1)^{x_{j}}
, and the Walsh–Paley
functions are

\omega_{n}(x):=\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}r_{j}(x)^{n_{j}}.

The Dirichlet and Fejér kernels are given by

D_{n}:=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\omega_{k},\qquad K_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}D_{k}.

For
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{I})
, the
n
th partial sum is

S_{n}f(y):=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\widehat{f}(k)\omega_{k}(y)=\int_{\mathbb{I}}f(x\dotplus y)D_{n}(x)\,dx.

We also use the dyadic convolution

(f*g)(y):=\int_{\mathbb{I}}f(x\dotplus y)g(x)\,dx.

It is well known that
S_{2^{m}}f=\mathbb{E}_{m}f
.

For
n\geq 1
, set

|n|:=\max\{j\in\mathbb{N}:n_{j}\neq 0\},\qquad m(n):=\min\{j\in\mathbb{N}:n_{j}=1\},

and for
j\geq 0
define the upper truncation

n^{(j)}:=\sum_{k=j}^{\infty}n_{k}2^{k}.

For an
L^{2}
function
h
, let

\spec(h):=\{k\in\mathbb{N}:\widehat{h}(k)\neq 0\}

denote its Walsh spectrum.

For
n\geq 1
and
0\leq i<|n|
, define

(2.1)

\lambda_{n,i}:=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }m(n)\leq i<|n|\text{ and }n_{i}=0,\\
0,&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

and

(2.2)

d_{n,i}:=\lambda_{n,i}\,\omega_{n}\bigl(D_{2^{i}}-D_{2^{i+1}}\bigr).

The next proposition gives the decomposition needed below.

Proposition 2.1
.

For every
n\geq 1
,

(2.3)

D_{n}=D_{2^{|n|+1}}-\omega_{n}D_{2^{m(n)}}+\sum_{i=0}^{|n|-1}d_{n,i}.

Consequently,

(2.4)

S_{n}f=\mathbb{E}_{|n|+1}f-\omega_{n}\mathbb{E}_{m(n)}(f\omega_{n})+\widetilde{S}_{n}f,\qquad\widetilde{S}_{n}f:=\sum_{i=0}^{|n|-1}f*d_{n,i}.

Proof.

A standard identity for the Walsh Dirichlet kernel is

(2.5)

D_{n}=\omega_{n}\sum_{i=0}^{|n|}n_{i}\bigl(D_{2^{i+1}}-D_{2^{i}}\bigr).

Since
n_{i}=0
for
i<m(n)
,
n_{|n|}=1
, and
n_{i}=1-\lambda_{n,i}
for
m(n)\leq i<|n|
, we obtain

\sum_{i=0}^{|n|}n_{i}\bigl(D_{2^{i+1}}-D_{2^{i}}\bigr)=\sum_{i=m(n)}^{|n|}\bigl(D_{2^{i+1}}-D_{2^{i}}\bigr)-\sum_{i=0}^{|n|-1}\lambda_{n,i}\bigl(D_{2^{i+1}}-D_{2^{i}}\bigr).

The first sum telescopes to
D_{2^{|n|+1}}-D_{2^{m(n)}}
. Multiplying by
\omega_{n}
and using that
\omega_{n}\equiv 1
on the support of
D_{2^{|n|+1}}
, namely on
I_{|n|+1}
, gives

D_{n}=D_{2^{|n|+1}}-\omega_{n}D_{2^{m(n)}}+\sum_{i=0}^{|n|-1}\lambda_{n,i}\,\omega_{n}\bigl(D_{2^{i}}-D_{2^{i+1}}\bigr),

which is exactly (
2.3
).

Convolving (
2.3
) with
f
yields

S_{n}f=S_{2^{|n|+1}}f-(f*(\omega_{n}D_{2^{m(n)}}))+\sum_{i=0}^{|n|-1}f*d_{n,i}.

Now
S_{2^{|n|+1}}f=\mathbb{E}_{|n|+1}f
, and a standard Walsh character calculation shows that

(f*(\omega_{n}D_{2^{m(n)}}))(y)=\omega_{n}(y)\,\mathbb{E}_{m(n)}(f\omega_{n})(y).

Hence (
2.4
) follows.
∎

For the sequence
a=(a(n))_{n\geq 1}
, put
A(n):=|a(n)|
and define

(2.6)

\displaystyle\sigma_{N}^{0}f

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{A(n)+1}f,

(2.7)

\displaystyle\rho_{N}f

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\omega_{a(n)}\mathbb{E}_{m(a(n))}(f\omega_{a(n)}),

(2.8)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\sigma}_{N}f

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\widetilde{S}_{a(n)}f.

Then (
2.4
) becomes

(2.9)

\sigma_{N}f=\sigma_{N}^{0}f-\rho_{N}f+\widetilde{\sigma}_{N}f.

We use the dyadic maximal operator

f^{*}(x):=\sup_{m\geq 0}\mathbb{E}_{m}\lvert f\rvert(x).

Since
\lvert\mathbb{E}_{m}g\rvert\leq\mathbb{E}_{m}\lvert g\rvert
, we have

(2.10)

\sup_{N\geq 1}\lvert\sigma_{N}^{0}f\rvert+\sup_{N\geq 1}\lvert\rho_{N}f\rvert\leq 2f^{*}.

We shall also use the classical weak-type estimate for the dyadic maximal
function (see, for instance,
[
9
]
)

(2.11)

\lvert\{x\in\mathbb{I}:f^{*}(x)>\lambda\}\rvert\leq\frac{C}{\lambda}\lVert f\rVert_{1}\qquad(\lambda>0).

3.
Frequency blocks

For
n\geq 1
and
0\leq i<|n|
, define the block

(3.1)

B(n,i):=\bigl\{n^{(i+1)}+2^{i}+r:0\leq r<2^{i}\bigr\}.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
n\geq 1
and
0\leq i<|n|
.

(a)

If
\lambda_{n,i}=0
, then
d_{n,i}=0
.

(b)

If
\lambda_{n,i}=1
, then

(3.2)

d_{n,i}=-\omega_{n^{(i+1)}+2^{i}}D_{2^{i}}=-\sum_{r=0}^{2^{i}-1}\omega_{n^{(i+1)}+2^{i}+r}.

In particular,

(3.3)

\spec(d_{n,i})=B(n,i)\subset[2^{|n|},2^{|n|+1}).

(c)

If
0\leq i<j<|n|
and
\lambda_{n,i}=\lambda_{n,j}=1
, then

(3.4)

\max B(n,i)<\min B(n,j).

Hence the nonzero blocks
B(n,i)
are pairwise disjoint.

Proof.

Part (a) is immediate from (
2.2
). If
\lambda_{n,i}=1
, then
n_{i}=0
and
n-n^{(i+1)}<2^{i}
. Hence
\omega_{n-n^{(i+1)}}\equiv 1
on the
support of both
D_{2^{i}}
and
D_{2^{i+1}}
, so

d_{n,i}=\omega_{n^{(i+1)}}(D_{2^{i}}-D_{2^{i+1}}).

Using
D_{2^{i+1}}=D_{2^{i}}+\omega_{2^{i}}D_{2^{i}}
, we get

d_{n,i}=-\omega_{n^{(i+1)}}\omega_{2^{i}}D_{2^{i}}=-\omega_{n^{(i+1)}+2^{i}}D_{2^{i}},

which is (
3.2
). Expanding
D_{2^{i}}=\sum_{r=0}^{2^{i}-1}\omega_{r}

yields (
3.3
).

For part (c), assume
i<j
and
\lambda_{n,i}=\lambda_{n,j}=1
. Since
n_{j}=0
,

n^{(i+1)}=n^{(j+1)}+\sum_{k=i+1}^{j-1}n_{k}2^{k}\leq n^{(j+1)}+\sum_{k=i+1}^{j-1}2^{k}=n^{(j+1)}+2^{j}-2^{i+1}.

Therefore

\max B(n,i)=n^{(i+1)}+2^{i+1}-1\leq n^{(j+1)}+2^{j}-1<n^{(j+1)}+2^{j}=\min B(n,j),

as claimed.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
n\geq 1
and
0\leq i<|n|
. If
\phi
is
\mathcal{A}_{i}
-measurable and
h\in L^{2}(\mathbb{I})
satisfies
\spec(h)\subset B(n,i)
, then

\spec(\phi h)\subset B(n,i).

Proof.

Every
\mathcal{A}_{i}
-measurable function is a Walsh polynomial with
spectrum contained in
[0,2^{i})
. Since multiplication of Walsh characters
corresponds to dyadic addition of the indices, multiplying by
\phi
can
only change the lowest
i
binary digits. The elements of
B(n,i)
have the
form
n^{(i+1)}+2^{i}+r
with
0\leq r<2^{i}
, so changing the lowest
i
digits
leaves the set
B(n,i)
invariant. Hence
\spec(\phi h)\subset B(n,i)
.
∎

For
\lambda>0
, define the stopping time

(3.5)

\nu_{\lambda}(x):=\inf\bigl\{m\in\mathbb{N}:\mathbb{E}_{m}(\lvert f\rvert)(x)>\lambda\bigr\},\qquad\inf\emptyset:=+\infty.

Since
\{\nu_{\lambda}>i\}=\bigcap_{m=0}^{i}\{\mathbb{E}_{m}(\lvert f\rvert)\leq\lambda\}\in\mathcal{A}_{i}
, the next lemma follows from
lemmas

3.1
and
3.2
.

Lemma 3.3
.

For
n\geq 1
, set

(3.6)

X_{n}^{(\lambda)}:=\sum_{i=0}^{|n|-1}\mathbf{1}_{\{\nu_{\lambda}>i\}}\,(f*d_{n,i}).

Then

(3.7)

\spec\bigl(X_{n}^{(\lambda)}\bigr)\subset[2^{|n|},2^{|n|+1}),

and the summands in (
3.6
) are pairwise orthogonal in
L^{2}(\mathbb{I})
. In particular,

(3.8)

\lVert X_{n}^{(\lambda)}\rVert_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i=0}^{|n|-1}\lVert\mathbf{1}_{\{\nu_{\lambda}>i\}}\,(f*d_{n,i})\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Moreover,
X_{n}^{(\lambda)}
is
\mathcal{A}_{|n|+1}
-measurable and
\mathbb{E}_{|n|}X_{n}^{(\lambda)}=0
.

Proof.

For each
i
, the function
f*d_{n,i}
has spectrum contained in
\spec(d_{n,i})\subset B(n,i)
by (
3.3
). Since
\mathbf{1}_{\{\nu_{\lambda}>i\}}
is
\mathcal{A}_{i}
-measurable,
lemma

3.2
gives

\spec\bigl(\mathbf{1}_{\{\nu_{\lambda}>i\}}(f*d_{n,i})\bigr)\subset B(n,i)\subset[2^{|n|},2^{|n|+1}).

By
lemma

3.1
(c), the nonzero blocks
B(n,i)
are pairwise
disjoint, which yields both (
3.7
) and (
3.8
). The
final assertion is immediate because a Walsh polynomial with frequencies in
[2^{|n|},2^{|n|+1})
is
\mathcal{A}_{|n|+1}
-measurable and has zero
\mathcal{A}_{|n|}
-conditional expectation.
∎

The next estimate is the key input from Gát’s paper
[
3
]
.

Lemma 3.4

(Stopped square estimate)
.

Let
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{I})
,
n\geq 1
, and
\lambda>0
. Then

(3.9)

\sum_{i=0}^{|n|-1}\lVert\mathbf{1}_{\{\nu_{\lambda}>i\}}\,(f*d_{n,i})\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq C\lambda\lVert f\rVert_{1}.

Lemma 3.5

(Doob’s
L^{2}
maximal inequality, see
[
1
]
, Ch. 5)
.

Let
(M_{t},\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t=0}^{L}
be an
L^{2}
-martingale on
a probability space. Then

\Bigl\|\max_{0\leq t\leq L}\lvert M_{t}\rvert\Bigr\|_{2}^{2}\leq 4\lVert M_{L}\rVert_{2}^{2}.

4.
A stopped maximal estimate

Throughout this section,
a=(a(n))_{n\geq 1}
satisfies (
1.3
). For
fixed
\lambda>0
, define

(4.1)

\displaystyle X_{n}

\displaystyle:=X_{a(n)}^{(\lambda)}=\sum_{i=0}^{A(n)-1}\mathbf{1}_{\{\nu_{\lambda}>i\}}\,(f*d_{a(n),i}),

(4.2)

\displaystyle T_{N}^{(\lambda)}f

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}X_{n},\qquad T_{\lambda}^{*}f:=\sup_{N\geq 1}\lvert T_{N}^{(\lambda)}f\rvert.

By
lemmas

3.3
and
3.4
,

(4.3)

\lVert X_{n}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq C\lambda\lVert f\rVert_{1}\qquad(n\geq 1).

The next lemma extracts lacunarity inside a dyadic shell.

Lemma 4.1
.

Fix
m\geq 0
, and let

\kappa_{\delta}:=2^{1+\delta}\log 2+2,\qquad R_{m}:=\bigl\lceil\kappa_{\delta}2^{m\delta}\bigr\rceil.

If
\beta\geq 1
and
\beta+R_{m}\leq 2^{m+1}
, then

(4.4)

a(\beta+R_{m})\geq 2a(\beta).

Consequently, for each residue class
b\in\{0,1,\dots,R_{m}-1\}
, the
subsequence

a(b+jR_{m}),\qquad j\geq 1,

is lacunary with ratio at least
2
as long as
b+jR_{m}\leq 2^{m+1}
. In
particular, the integers

A(b+jR_{m})=|a(b+jR_{m})|

are then strictly increasing.

Proof.

If
\beta+R_{m}\leq 2^{m+1}
, then by (
1.3
),

a(\beta+R_{m})\geq\prod_{t=\beta}^{\beta+R_{m}-1}\left(1+\frac{1}{t^{\delta}}\right)a(\beta)\geq\left(1+2^{-(m+1)\delta}\right)^{R_{m}}a(\beta).

Since
0<2^{-(m+1)\delta}\leq 1
, we have
\log(1+u)\geq u/2
for
0<u\leq 1
.
Hence

R_{m}\log\left(1+2^{-(m+1)\delta}\right)\geq\kappa_{\delta}2^{m\delta}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\,2^{-(m+1)\delta}=\kappa_{\delta}2^{-1-\delta}\geq\log 2.

Therefore

\left(1+2^{-(m+1)\delta}\right)^{R_{m}}\geq 2,

which proves (
4.4
). The last assertion follows because
a(\beta+R_{m})\geq 2a(\beta)
implies
|a(\beta+R_{m})|\geq|a(\beta)|+1
.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Assume (
1.3
) with
0<\delta<1
. Then, for
every
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{I})
and every
\lambda>0
,

(4.5)

\lvert\{x\in\mathbb{I}:T_{\lambda}^{*}f(x)>\lambda\}\rvert\leq\frac{C_{\delta}}{\lambda}\lVert f\rVert_{1}.

Proof.

Fix
m\geq 0
and
N
with
2^{m}\leq N<2^{m+1}
. Since

T_{N}^{(\lambda)}f=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{2^{m}}X_{n}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=2^{m}+1}^{N}X_{n},

we have

(4.6)

\lvert T_{N}^{(\lambda)}f\rvert^{2}\leq\frac{2}{2^{2m}}\lvert\sum_{n=1}^{2^{m}}X_{n}\rvert^{2}+\frac{2}{2^{2m}}\lvert\sum_{n=2^{m}+1}^{N}X_{n}\rvert^{2}=:A_{m}+B_{m,N}.

We estimate the two terms separately.

Estimate of
A_{m}
.
For
b=0,1,\dots,R_{m}-1
, set

J_{m,b}:=\bigl\{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:b+jR_{m}\in\{1,2,\dots,2^{m}\}\bigr\}.

Then

\sum_{n=1}^{2^{m}}X_{n}=\sum_{b=0}^{R_{m}-1}\sum_{j\in J_{m,b}}X_{b+jR_{m}}.

By Cauchy–Schwarz,

A_{m}\leq C\frac{R_{m}}{2^{2m}}\sum_{b=0}^{R_{m}-1}\lvert\sum_{j\in J_{m,b}}X_{b+jR_{m}}\rvert^{2}.

For fixed
b
, the spectra of the functions
X_{b+jR_{m}}
lie in disjoint dyadic shells
\left[2^{A\left(b+jR_{m}\right)},2^{A\left(b+jR_{m}\right)+1}\right)
, because
A\left(b+jR_{m}\right)
is strictly increasing by Lemma 4.1. Taking
L^{1}
-norms and using Parseval,
lemmas

3.3
,
3.4
and
4.1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\lVert A_{m}\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{R_{m}}{2^{2m}}\sum_{b=0}^{R_{m}-1}\Bigl\|\sum_{j\in J_{m,b}}X_{b+jR_{m}}\Bigr\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=C\frac{R_{m}}{2^{2m}}\sum_{b=0}^{R_{m}-1}\sum_{j\in J_{m,b}}\lVert X_{b+jR_{m}}\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{R_{m}}{2^{2m}}\sum_{b=0}^{R_{m}-1}\sum_{j\in J_{m,b}}\lambda\lVert f\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{R_{m}}{2^{m}}\lambda\lVert f\rVert_{1}.

Since
R_{m}\lesssim 2^{m\delta}
,

(4.7)

\lVert A_{m}\rVert_{1}\leq C2^{-m(1-\delta)}\lambda\lVert f\rVert_{1}.

Estimate of the tail term.
Set

B_{m}^{*}:=\sup_{2^{m}\leq N<2^{m+1}}B_{m,N}.

For each fixed residue class
b\in\{0,1,\dots,R_{m}-1\}
, list the indices in
the shell
\{2^{m}+1,\dots,2^{m+1}-1\}
that are congruent to
b\pmod{R_{m}}
in
increasing order:

n_{b,1}<n_{b,2}<\cdots<n_{b,L_{m,b}}.

By construction,
n_{b,s+1}-n_{b,s}=R_{m}
, and therefore
lemma

4.1
implies that the integers

k_{b,s}:=A(n_{b,s})=|a(n_{b,s})|

are strictly increasing. For
1\leq t\leq L_{m,b}
, define

M_{b,t}:=\sum_{s=1}^{t}X_{n_{b,s}},\qquad M_{b,0}:=0.

For
2^{m}\leq N<2^{m+1}
, there is an integer
t_{b}(N)\in\{0,\dots,L_{m,b}\}

such that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq s\leq L_{m,b}\\
n_{b,s}\leq N\end{subarray}}X_{n_{b,s}}=M_{b,t_{b}(N)}.

Hence

\sum_{n=2^{m}+1}^{N}X_{n}=\sum_{b=0}^{R_{m}-1}M_{b,t_{b}(N)},

and another application of Cauchy–Schwarz yields

B_{m}^{*}\leq C\frac{R_{m}}{2^{2m}}\sum_{b=0}^{R_{m}-1}\sup_{0\leq t\leq L_{m,b}}\lvert M_{b,t}\rvert^{2}.

Fix
b
. By
lemma

3.3
, each increment
X_{n_{b,s}}
has
spectrum contained in
[2^{k_{b,s}},2^{k_{b,s}+1})
, so in particular

X_{n_{b,s}}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{A}_{k_{b,s}+1})\ominus L^{2}(\mathcal{A}_{k_{b,s}}).

Since
k_{b,s+1}\geq k_{b,s}+1
, the filtration defined by

\mathcal{F}_{b,0}:=\{\emptyset,\mathbb{I}\},\qquad\mathcal{F}_{b,t}:=\mathcal{A}_{k_{b,t}+1}\qquad(1\leq t\leq L_{m,b})

is increasing,
M_{b,t}
is
\mathcal{F}_{b,t}
-measurable, and

\mathbb{E}\bigl(X_{n_{b,t+1}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{b,t}\bigr)=0.

Thus
(M_{b,t},\mathcal{F}_{b,t})_{t=0}^{L_{m,b}}
is an
L^{2}
-martingale.
By Doob’s inequality,

\Bigl\|\sup_{0\leq t\leq L_{m,b}}\lvert M_{b,t}\rvert\Bigr\|_{2}^{2}\leq 4\lVert M_{b,L_{m,b}}\rVert_{2}^{2}.

The increments are pairwise orthogonal because their spectra lie in disjoint
dyadic shells, so

\lVert M_{b,L_{m,b}}\rVert_{2}^{2}=\sum_{s=1}^{L_{m,b}}\lVert X_{n_{b,s}}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq CL_{m,b}\lambda\lVert f\rVert_{1}

by (
4.3
). Therefore,

\displaystyle\lVert B_{m}^{*}\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{R_{m}}{2^{2m}}\sum_{b=0}^{R_{m}-1}\Bigl\|\sup_{0\leq t\leq L_{m,b}}\lvert M_{b,t}\rvert\Bigr\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{R_{m}}{2^{2m}}\sum_{b=0}^{R_{m}-1}\lVert M_{b,L_{m,b}}\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{R_{m}}{2^{2m}}\sum_{b=0}^{R_{m}-1}L_{m,b}\lambda\lVert f\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{R_{m}}{2^{m}}\lambda\lVert f\rVert_{1}.

Hence

(4.8)

\lVert B_{m}^{*}\rVert_{1}\leq C2^{-m(1-\delta)}\lambda\lVert f\rVert_{1}.

Finally, (
4.6
) implies

\{T_{\lambda}^{*}f>\lambda\}\subset\bigcup_{m=0}^{\infty}\{A_{m}+B_{m}^{*}>\lambda^{2}\}.

By Chebyshev’s inequality and (
4.7
)–(
4.8
),

\displaystyle\lvert\{T_{\lambda}^{*}f>\lambda\}\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\bigl(\lVert A_{m}\rVert_{1}+\lVert B_{m}^{*}\rVert_{1}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\lambda}\lVert f\rVert_{1}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}2^{-m(1-\delta)}\leq\frac{C_{\delta}}{\lambda}\lVert f\rVert_{1},

because
0<\delta<1
. This proves (
4.5
).
∎

5.
Proof of the main theorem

Define the maximal operators

\widetilde{M}f:=\sup_{N\geq 1}\lvert\widetilde{\sigma}_{N}f\rvert,\qquad Mf:=\sup_{N\geq 1}\lvert\sigma_{N}f\rvert.

By (
2.9
) and (
2.10
),

(5.1)

Mf\leq 2f^{*}+\widetilde{M}f.

We first show that
\widetilde{M}
is of weak type
(1,1)
. Fix
\lambda>0
, and let
T_{N}^{(\lambda)}f
be defined by (
4.2
). On the
set
\{\nu_{\lambda}=\infty\}
, we have
\mathbf{1}_{\{\nu_{\lambda}>i\}}=1
for
every
i
, hence

T_{N}^{(\lambda)}f=\widetilde{\sigma}_{N}f.

Therefore

\{\widetilde{M}f>2\lambda\}\subset\{\nu_{\lambda}<\infty\}\cup\{T_{\lambda}^{*}f>2\lambda\}.

Using (
2.11
) and
lemma

4.2
, we obtain

(5.2)

\lvert\{\widetilde{M}f>2\lambda\}\rvert\leq\frac{C}{\lambda}\lVert f\rVert_{1}.

Combining (
5.1
), (
2.11
), and (
5.2
), we conclude that

(5.3)

\lvert\{Mf>4\lambda\}\rvert\leq\frac{C}{\lambda}\lVert f\rVert_{1}.

Thus the maximal operator associated with the averages (
1.1
)
is of weak type
(1,1)
.

We now deduce almost everywhere convergence by a standard density argument.
Let
\mathcal{P}
be the set of Walsh polynomials. If
P\in\mathcal{P}
,
then there exists
L\in\mathbb{N}
such that
S_{m}P=P
for every
m\geq L
.
Since
a(n)\to\infty
, it follows that

(5.4)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\sigma_{N}P(x)=P(x)\qquad\text{for every }x\in\mathbb{I}.

Now let
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{I})
, and choose
P_{j}\in\mathcal{P}
with
\lVert f-P_{j}\rVert_{1}\to 0
. For each
j
and for almost every
x\in\mathbb{I}
,

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\lvert\sigma_{N}f(x)-f(x)\rvert\leq M(f-P_{j})(x)+\lvert f(x)-P_{j}(x)\rvert,

because
\sigma_{N}P_{j}(x)\to P_{j}(x)
by (
5.4
). Hence, for every

\varepsilon>0
,

\Bigl\{x:\limsup_{N\to\infty}\lvert\sigma_{N}f(x)-f(x)\rvert>2\varepsilon\Bigr\}\subset\{M(f-P_{j})>\varepsilon\}\cup\{\lvert f-P_{j}\rvert>\varepsilon\}.

By (
5.3
) and Chebyshev’s inequality,

\lvert\{M(f-P_{j})>\varepsilon\}\rvert+\lvert\{\lvert f-P_{j}\rvert>\varepsilon\}\rvert\leq\frac{C}{\varepsilon}\lVert f-P_{j}\rVert_{1}.

Letting
j\to\infty
gives

\lvert\Bigl\{x:\limsup_{N\to\infty}\lvert\sigma_{N}f(x)-f(x)\rvert>2\varepsilon\Bigr\}\rvert=0\qquad(\varepsilon>0),

which proves

\sigma_{N}f(x)\to f(x)\qquad\text{for almost every }x\in\mathbb{I}.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is complete.

Remark 5.1
.

The argument does not reach the endpoint
\delta=1
. Indeed, in the shell
2^{m}\leq N<2^{m+1}
one needs a step length
R_{m}\asymp 2^{m\delta}
to recover
lacunarity along residue classes. When
\delta=1
, this gives
R_{m}\asymp 2^{m}
, so the factor
R_{m}/2^{m}
in the estimates of
A_{m}
and
B_{m}^{*}
no longer
decays. In particular, the quadratic subsequence
a(n)=n^{2}
lies just beyond
the present method.
