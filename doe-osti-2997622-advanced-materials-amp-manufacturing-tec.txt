Executive Summary

Investigations were conducted in fiscal years (FY) 2023 and 2024 to gather relevant data sets
addressing challenges related to qualifying 316H stainless steel (SS) for use in future nuclear
reactors. This work was a collaborative effort involving researchers from Idaho National
Laboratory (INL), Argonne National Laboratory (ANL), and Oak Ridge National Laboratory
(ORNL). Key outcomes included:
 • Development of process-structure-property data sets to better understand the
 relationships between manufacturing processes, material structure, and performance
 characteristics.
 • Establishment of an in-situ monitoring system to link these various data sets together.
 • Detailed characterization of the raw material feedstock to further strengthen the
 understanding of process-structure and process-property relationships.
Building on this foundation, in FY25 there was interest in exploring the behavior of additively
manufactured 316H SS under different test conditions to support the overall material
qualification process. Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL) was tasked with providing
specimen samples to the collaborating labs, who then conducted a round-robin study examining
factors like selective heat treatment, low-cycle fatigue (LCF), and tensile-creep (T-C) properties.
Additionally, high-temperature differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) was performed by LANL
to better understand how the material's thermal characteristics change as it is heated up to the
melting point. This provided a more comprehensive understanding of the material's behavior.
The combined results from these investigations could potentially be used to support the
inclusion of 316H stainless steel in Section III, Division 5 of the relevant codes and standards,
allowing its use in future nuclear reactor applications.

Objective

The current ASME Code has only approved six materials for nuclear construction at elevated
temperatures. This qualification process has proven to be extensive, requiring a substantial data
set for modelers and engineers to reference during the design and selection stages.
Additionally, extensive testing and modeling has been necessary to qualify components and
certify the manufacturing equipment. To address these challenges, the AMMT program aimed to
integrate the development of advanced materials and advanced manufacturing. The program's
approach was based on the required applications, design specifications, functional
requirements, and property needs, while providing an expedited evaluation and qualification
pathway for insertion into WR.

In FY25, the focus was on optimizing the process and post-process treatment for qualifying
laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) 316H SS. The initial task for LANL was to perform DSC scans
between room temperature and 1100°C. This was done to identify the temperatures at which
microstructural changes occur. Multiple heating and cooling cycles at a rate of 20°C per minute
were conducted to characterize the changes between the as-received material and subsequent
thermal processing. The second task centered on producing relevant scaled witness specimens
for campaign testing of the LPBF 316H SS across other work packages. Specifically, the
witness specimens would be machined into LCF and T-C specimens based on the drawings
and geometries specified by INL.

The characterization of LCF behavior is of paramount importance for 316H austenitic SS, a
material commonly utilized in nuclear and power generation applications that experience cyclic
thermal and mechanical loading. LCF testing enables the determination of the materials fatigue
life, defined as the number of cycles to failure under prescribed stress or strain amplitudes. This
fatigue life data is crucial for the development of accurate life prediction models to ensure the
reliability and safe operation of 316H components. Furthermore, LCF testing provides insights
into the underlying damage mechanisms, such as the initiation and propagation of fatigue
cracks, which are influenced by the evolving microstructure of the material under cyclic
deformation. Detailed analysis of the microstructural changes, including dislocation dynamics,
slip band formation, and phase transformations, allows for the correlation of the material's
mechanical response to its fundamental deformation and failure modes. This comprehensive
understanding gained from LCF testing is essential for the optimization of 316H alloy
compositions and manufacturing processes to enhance the fatigue resistance and service life of
critical high-temperature components.

Due to funding constraints, the scope for FY25 was significantly reduced to these two tasks.
The LANL results and conclusions can be found in the subsequent sections of this report.

 316H Fabrication
LANL fabricated 316H SS specimens as shown in Figure 1 to support both DSC analysis and
the provision of witness specimens for collaborating research sites. Among the collaborators,
only INL requested these specimens for further testing in the upcoming FY25. The powder and
witness specimen geometries were specified by Michael McMurtrey to ensure consistency
across the various characterization and testing activities. Table 1 displays the chemical
composition of the 316H SS feedstock powder used in this build. The powder exhibited an
apparent density of 4.40 g/cc, a BET surface area of 0.052 m²/g, a Hall flow rate of 14 seconds
per 50 grams, a skeletal density of 8.038 g/cc, and a tap density of 5.0 g/cc. These powder
characteristics are critical in determining the flowability, packing, and melting behavior during
the LPBF process, which directly impact the final part quality and performance. The LCF and T-
C specimen drawings are displayed in Figures 2 and 3.

Discoloration along the Z-height was observed in previous years due to powder oxidation and
high energy density. Therefore, two different parameter sets were used for the LPBF of 316H
SS in FY25. The application of high energy density during LPBF can significantly influence the
microstructural evolution and properties of the fabricated components. The rapid melting and
solidification associated with high energy input promotes the formation of a refined,
predominantly austenitic microstructure, which can exhibit enhanced mechanical characteristics,
such as increased strength and hardness [1]. However, high thermal gradients and localized
heating can also lead to the development of substantial residual stresses within the 316H parts,
potentially contributing to the formation of cracks and distortion. Furthermore, the rapid thermal
cycling can result in the precipitation of secondary phases, such as carbides and sigma phase,
as well as the emergence of complex solidification structures, like cellular or dendritic
morphologies. These microstructural changes can, in turn, impact the corrosion resistance and
thermal stability of the 316H components [1]. Concurrently, the repeated use and recycling of
the 316H powder in the LPBF process can lead to oxidation and contamination of the powder
feedstock, adversely affecting its flowability and chemical composition. The presence of oxide
inclusions and impurities in the final 316H parts can degrade their mechanical properties and
corrosion performance.

Therefore, careful optimization of the LPBF process parameters, along with effective powder
management and post-processing treatments, is crucial and was addressed to mitigate the
adverse effects of high energy density and powder oxidation. Doing so ensured the reliable
performance of 316H stainless steel components fabricated by additive manufacturing.
The two parameter sets used in this study provided full density parts. The specific parameter
sets and specimen geometries are displayed in Table 2. All samples will be serialized for
tracking purposes. The heat treatment and machining of the specimens will be performed at

 316H DSC
The application DSC is a valuable tool for the thermal analysis of 316H austenitic SS. In a DSC
experiment, a sample of 316H SS and an inert reference material are subjected to a controlled
temperature program, typically involving heating and cooling cycles. As the 316H specimen
undergoes phase transformations or other thermal events, the energy required to maintain the
temperature difference between the sample and reference is recorded.
The DSC thermogram of 316H SS can provide critical information about the material's thermal
behavior. For example, the DSC signal can reveal the temperatures at which phase transitions,
such as the austenite-to-ferrite transformation, occur. The magnitude of the DSC peaks

corresponds to the enthalpy changes associated with these phase transformations, which are
influenced by the chemical composition and microstructure of the 316H alloy [2].
Furthermore, DSC can be used to study the precipitation and dissolution of secondary phases in
316H SS, such as carbides and intermetallic compounds. The formation and decomposition of
these phases during thermal processing can have a significant impact on the mechanical
properties and corrosion resistance of the material. By analyzing the DSC data, researchers can
optimize the heat treatment procedures to achieve the desired microstructure and performance
characteristics of 316H stainless steel.
The versatility of DSC also allows for the determination of other thermodynamic properties of
316H, such as specific heat capacity and thermal stability. These parameters are crucial for
modeling the thermal behavior of 316H components in high-temperature applications, such as
those found in nuclear power plants and nuclear reactors. Overall, the application of differential
scanning calorimetry provides a comprehensive understanding of the thermal behavior and
phase transformations in 316H SSs, enabling the optimization of material processing and the
prediction of in-service performance [2].

Two specimens were tested using a PerkinElmer STA 8000. The specimens were 5.5mm in
diameter and 5mm in length. Both were cycled three times at 20C/min from room temperature to
1100C to characterize the microstructural changes. The results of both specimens were the
same though it was unsure if the PerkinElmer instrument had an oxygen leak during testing as a
leak was identified soon after the scans were complete. Therefore, there may be slight
discrepancies in the curves. It was difficult identifying any clear peaks so only temperature
ranges were obtained. Those results are displayed in Figures 5 and 6.

2. Around 700-800°C: There is a large endothermic peak, which likely corresponds to the
  dissolution or re-solution of the carbide phases back into the austenitic matrix.

3. Above 900°C: There is a small endothermic peak, which may be associated with a phase
  transformation, such as the onset of recrystallization or recovery of the austenitic
  microstructure.

The overall shape and features of the DSC plots suggest that 316H undergoes various phase
 transformations and microstructural changes, primarily related to the precipitation and
 dissolution of carbide phases, as the temperature increases. It would have been beneficial to
 perform isothermal holding to possibly make the peaks more dominant and easily identifiable.
 Transformation events should be confirmed through transmission electron microscopy (TEM).

 Conclusion
The investigations conducted in FY23 and FY24 established the foundation of process-
 structure-property data sets to better understand the relationships between manufacturing
 processes, material structure, and performance characteristics of 316H. This laid the
 groundwork for FY 2025, which focused on using optimizing processing parameter sets to
 provide witness specimens to INL for further exploration in qualifying LPBF 316H. Additionally,
 DSC analysis revealed several key phase transformations in 316H, including the precipitation
 and dissolution of carbide phases, as well as potential recrystallization or recovery of the
 austenitic microstructure at higher temperatures. These thermal events are crucial for
 understanding the material's behavior and optimizing the heat treatment for the best final
 material and performance in the application needed. The adverse effects of high energy
 density and powder oxidation were addressed and mitigated in FY25 ensuring reliable
 performances for LCF and T-C tests that will hopefully be shared with LANL in the future.
 combined results from these investigations could potentially be used to support the inclusion
 of 316H stainless steel in Section III, Division 5 of the relevant codes and standards, allowing
 its use in future nuclear reactor applications. This was the final year of this work package. No
 further work is projected at this time.
