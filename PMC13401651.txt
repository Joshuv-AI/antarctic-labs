Development and prospective validation of a machine learning model for risk stratification of drug-induced liver injury using real-world clinical data
Authors: Ngan Thi Tran, Tung My Pham, Mai Thi Quynh Ngo, Anh Van Tran, Thu Thi Kim Ninh, Long Duc Nguyen, Dung Van Hoang, Phuong Thi Thu Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Drug-induced liver injury (DILI) is difficult to diagnose and manage in routine care because it lacks pathognomonic biomarkers and is often recognized only after clinically meaningful injury has occurred. Existing computational approaches are largely drug-centric and do not routinely incorporate patient-level clinical data available in electronic health records (EHRs).Objectives:To develop and temporally validate a machine learning model for episode-level risk stratification of Roussel Uclaf Causality Assessment Method (RUCAM)-defined DILI using routinely available baseline clinical data.Design:This was an observational cohort study conducted at Hai Phong International Hospital using linked EHR, laboratory, and pharmacy data. The final labeled cohort was partitioned chronologically at the patient level into a retrospective development cohort (2019–2023) and a temporally subsequent prospective validation cohort (2024–2025).Methods:Eligible drug-exposure episodes with complete baseline liver biochemistry and key exposure covariates were included. Analysis-ready episodes were monitored for biochemical liver injury triggers, and trigger-positive episodes underwent clinical review and RUCAM adjudication. DILI was defined as RUCAM ⩾6. Predictors were limited to baseline demographics, comorbidities, laboratory values, drug-exposure features, and FDA DILIrank 2.0 metadata. Candidate models included logistic regression, elastic-net logistic regression, random forest, ExtraTrees, XGBoost, and LightGBM.Results:Among 5095 eligible episodes from 3579 patients, 2786 episodes from 2712 patients were analysis-ready after exclusions. The final labeled cohort comprised 274 DILI-positive and 2512 non-DILI episodes. The prospective validation cohort included 828 episodes, of which 108 (13.0%) were DILI-positive. Tree-based ensemble models outperformed regression-based models. Logistic regression achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.777 and an area under the precision–recall curve (PR-AUC) of 0.349, whereas the final LightGBM model achieved an AUROC of 0.965 (95% CI 0.942–0.983), a PR-AUC of 0.903 (95% CI 0.856–0.943), and a Brier score of 0.034.Conclusion:A prospectively validated machine learning model using routinely collected baseline clinical data showed excellent performance for DILI risk stratification and may strengthen hospital pharmacovigilance.

Introduction

Drug-induced liver injury (DILI) constitutes a formidable challenge in clinical hepatology and pharmaceutical development, manifesting as a spectrum of pathologies ranging from transient enzymatic elevations to fulminant hepatic failure.1,2 Despite a relatively low incidence in the general population—estimated at approximately 14–19 cases per 100,000 inhabitants.3 –5 Furthermore, hepatotoxicity acts as a primary driver of attrition during drug development and precipitates the withdrawal of approved pharmaceuticals from the post-marketing landscape, carrying profound implications for patient safety and healthcare economics. 6

Pathophysiologically, DILI is typically dichotomized into intrinsic and idiosyncratic mechanisms. 7 Intrinsic hepatotoxicity, exemplified by acetaminophen, is dose-dependent and predictable. Conversely, idiosyncratic DILI (iDILI) is unpredictable, generally dose-independent, and driven by a complex interplay of host factors, metabolic activation, and adaptive immune responses. 8 While the mechanisms of intrinsic toxicity are well-characterized, iDILI remains elusive, partially because it is largely irreproducible in standard preclinical animal models; seminal studies have demonstrated a concordance of less than 55% between animal toxicology and human hepatotoxicity outcomes. 9

In the clinical setting, the diagnosis of DILI remains fundamentally one of exclusion, complicated by the lack of pathognomonic biomarkers.10,11 The Roussel Uclaf Causality Assessment Method (RUCAM), established by Danan and Benichou in 1993, serves as the standard psychometric tool for adjudicating causality, though its application can be hindered by inter-observer variability. 12 Prognostic assessment relies heavily on “Hy’s Law,” initially observed by Hyman Zimmerman and formalized by Temple, which posits that hepatocellular injury accompanied by jaundice implies a case-fatality rate of at least 10%.13,14

To address these limitations, computational toxicology has increasingly integrated in silico modeling. Pioneering approaches, such as Quantitative Structure-Activity Relationship (QSAR) models, have utilized physicochemical properties—specifically lipophilicity and daily dose—to predict hepatotoxic potential, notably establishing the “rule-of-two” as a significant risk factor.15,16 Furthermore, mechanistic modeling platforms like DILIsym have attempted to integrate pharmacokinetics and systems biology to simulate liver injury pathways. 17 These tools are valuable for preclinical hazard identification and regulatory risk characterization. However, they do not directly address the need for patient-level prediction at the point of care, where clinicians must integrate host factors, baseline laboratory data, and real-world drug exposure.18,19

Current clinical practice lacks decision-support systems capable of synthesizing routine clinical parameters—such as demographics, medication history, and longitudinal biochemical profiles—to predict DILI onset at the earliest sign of laboratory abnormality. Despite advances in understanding DILI mechanisms, significant gaps remain in prediction and diagnosis. 20 Traditional biomarkers like alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) often rise only after hepatocellular injury has already occurred, limiting their utility for early prevention. 21 Furthermore, while real-world data from electronic health records (EHRs) offer a rich source of clinical information, their integration into predictive models remains limited, 19 with most current AI approaches focusing predominantly on chemical structure rather than host clinical factors. 18 This study aims to develop and validate a machine learning algorithm utilizing retrospective clinical data to predict early-stage DILI. By moving beyond static structural alerts and leveraging dynamic clinical variables, this model seeks to assist clinicians in rapid causality assessment and intervention, thereby mitigating the risk of progression to severe hepatic outcomes.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study was designed as an observational cohort study with a retrospective model development phase and a temporally subsequent prospective testing phase, conducted at Hai Phong International Hospital, a tertiary academic hospital with an established pharmacovigilance workflow.

The development phase included eligible drug-exposure episodes from January 1, 2019 to December 31, 2023. The prospective testing phase included newly observed episodes from January 1, 2024 to December 31, 2025, during which the final model developed in the earlier cohort was evaluated without using these later data for model fitting, hyperparameter tuning, threshold determination, or recalibration.

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Hai Phong International Hospital, Hai Phong, Viet Nam (Approval Number 835/IRB-ĐKQT). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee for both the retrospective and prospective phases because the study used routinely collected clinical data, posed minimal risk to participants, and involved no direct patient contact or intervention by the investigators. The 2024–2025 phase was conducted under the hospital’s approved pharmacovigilance and clinical research procedures.

The reporting of this clinical prediction model study followed the TRIPOD+AI reporting guideline.29,30

Data sources and cohort construction

Data sources

Data were extracted from three routinely maintained hospital information systems:

Electronic medical records, which provided demographic characteristics, admission-related information, inpatient location and ICU status, and structured diagnostic data

Electronic medical records, which provided demographic characteristics, admission-related information, inpatient location and ICU status, and structured diagnostic data

Laboratory information systems, which provided serial biochemical and hematological test results together with laboratory reference ranges

Laboratory information systems, which provided serial biochemical and hematological test results together with laboratory reference ranges

Pharmacy information systems, which provided prescription records, drug administration data, dosage information, and treatment duration

Pharmacy information systems, which provided prescription records, drug administration data, dosage information, and treatment duration

The three datasets were linked using unique patient identifiers and episode-level timestamps. Data extraction, linkage, cleaning, and variable construction were performed using prespecified scripts to ensure reproducibility.

Unit of analysis and episode definition

The unit of analysis was the admission-anchored, hospitalization-based drug-exposure episode. Each unique hospital admission identifier defined one episode, even if the patient had a previous or subsequent admission close in time. Transfers between wards or clinical services within the same admission were not treated as new episodes. If a patient was discharged and then readmitted, the readmission was treated as a new episode regardless of the interval between admissions. Multiple prescriptions or administrations occurring within the same admission were aggregated into the same episode and were not split by drug-specific interruption thresholds. Time zero (t0) was defined as the first prescription or administration date within that admission. The episode ended at hospital discharge or at the last documented medication exposure date within the same admission, whichever was later within the available record. Baseline predictors were restricted to information available at or before t0, and follow-up for biochemical liver injury triggers was conducted from t0 until discharge or the end of available laboratory follow-up. Cohort entry was based on hospital admission, medication exposure, and data availability, not on prior clinical suspicion of DILI. The final modeling cohort was hospitalization-based; therefore, no separate outpatient episode boundary rule was applied in the primary analysis.

Cohort assembly

The source population consisted of all eligible drug-exposure episodes identified from the hospital EHR during the study period. Episodes were included if they had sufficient data to support baseline characterization and longitudinal follow-up, including baseline liver biochemistry and essential drug-exposure covariates.

Episodes were excluded during data preparation if baseline liver biochemistry measurements were unavailable or if key drug-exposure covariates required for analysis were missing. These exclusions were applied before outcome labeling. The remaining analysis-ready episodes constituted the modeling cohort. Because baseline liver biochemistry was a core predictor domain and part of the intended clinical-use population, episodes without any eligible baseline liver biochemistry were not imputed into the primary analysis. The potential selection effect of this exclusion was evaluated by comparing included episodes with episodes excluded for missing baseline liver biochemistry.

Follow-up and identification of potential liver injury

After cohort assembly, episodes were followed longitudinally using serial laboratory data to identify potential liver injury events. Potential liver injury was screened using predefined biochemical criteria adapted from commonly used DILI surveillance thresholds:

- ALT ⩾5 × the upper limit of normal (ULN)

- ALT ⩾5 × the upper limit of normal (ULN)

- alkaline phosphatase (ALP) ⩾2 × ULN

- alkaline phosphatase (ALP) ⩾2 × ULN

- ALT ⩾3 × ULN together with total bilirubin ⩾2 × ULN

- ALT ⩾3 × ULN together with total bilirubin ⩾2 × ULN

ULN values were defined according to the hospital laboratory reference ranges in use at the time of testing.

This laboratory screening step was used to identify episodes requiring formal clinical review for possible DILI outcome adjudication. It was not used as a criterion for cohort entry or predictor construction.

Clinical review and outcome adjudication

Episodes meeting biochemical screening criteria underwent structured clinical review within the hospital pharmacovigilance workflow. Treating physicians first assessed whether the laboratory abnormalities represented clinically meaningful liver injury and reviewed major alternative explanations, including but not limited to acute viral hepatitis, ischemic hepatitis, biliary obstruction, sepsis-associated liver dysfunction, and progression of underlying liver disease.

Causality assessment was then performed using the RUCAM. RUCAM scoring was conducted by clinical pharmacists trained in DILI assessment and reviewed in conjunction with the treating physician. Standard RUCAM domains were considered, including temporal relationship to exposure, course after drug withdrawal, risk factors, concomitant drugs, exclusion of non-drug-related causes, prior evidence of hepatotoxicity, and response to re-exposure when applicable. Adjudicators necessarily had access to the clinical, laboratory, medication, and chronology information required to apply RUCAM, but they were not provided with model predictions, model-derived risk scores, feature-importance results, or any outputs from the machine learning pipeline.

When disagreement or uncertainty arose, the final binary classification was determined by consensus between the clinical pharmacist and the treating physician. The consensus review considered the initial RUCAM category together with chronology, competing etiologies, and multi-drug exposure. Final consensus classifications were recorded in the pharmacovigilance documentation system and then transferred to the study database. Thus, descriptive initial RUCAM categories recorded during screening were not used mechanically as model predictors, and borderline cases could be reclassified during consensus review when competing etiologies or chronology made DILI less likely.

Episodes that did not develop laboratory triggers during follow-up were classified as non-DILI for prediction modeling purposes. Therefore, the final labeled cohort consisted of all analysis-ready drug-exposure episodes, with DILI status determined through trigger-based surveillance and adjudication rather than preselection of only clinically suspected cases.

Outcome ascertainment and definition

Outcome definition

The primary outcome was DILI at the episode level.

For episodes undergoing causality assessment, outcomes were defined as follows:

- episodes with a final consensus RUCAM total score ⩾6, corresponding to probable or highly probable DILI after adjudication, were classified as DILI-positive

- episodes with a final consensus RUCAM total score ⩾6, corresponding to probable or highly probable DILI after adjudication, were classified as DILI-positive

- episodes with a final consensus RUCAM total score <6, including trigger-positive episodes downgraded during consensus review because of competing etiologies or chronology, were classified as non-DILI

- episodes with a final consensus RUCAM total score <6, including trigger-positive episodes downgraded during consensus review because of competing etiologies or chronology, were classified as non-DILI

For episodes without any biochemical liver injury trigger during follow-up, the outcome was classified as non-DILI.

RUCAM was used exclusively for outcome adjudication. No RUCAM component, intermediate domain score, initial RUCAM category, final consensus decision, injury phenotype, R ratio at injury onset, injury window, or variable derived from the clinical review process was included as a model predictor.

Predictor variables and preprocessing

Predictor variables

Candidate predictors were restricted to variables available at or before the index time point (t0), defined as the start of the drug-exposure episode. Variables generated after t0, including follow-up laboratory changes, maximum post-baseline laboratory values, treatment modifications after episode entry, liver injury trigger variables, RUCAM components, initial or final RUCAM categories, injury phenotype, R ratio at injury onset, injury window, dechallenge information, and any information derived during clinical review or outcome adjudication, were excluded a priori to reduce temporal leakage.

Demographic variables

Demographic predictors included age at t0 (years), sex, and body mass index (kg/m²).

Clinical context

Clinical context variables at baseline included ICU admission status at or before t0 and admission-related hospitalization context. Because the final modeling cohort was admission-anchored and hospitalization-based, care-setting heterogeneity from separate outpatient episode definitions was not part of the primary analysis. If length of stay was used for descriptive purposes, total length of stay was not used as a model predictor; only information accrued up to t0 was eligible for predictor construction.

Pre-existing comorbidities

Comorbid conditions recorded before t0 were identified from structured diagnostic data and summarized as both individual binary indicators and a cumulative burden measure. These included hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, history of pulmonary embolism, alcohol-related risk status, chronic liver disease, and the total number of recorded comorbid conditions.

Baseline laboratory variables

Baseline laboratory variables were constructed before outcome ascertainment using a prespecified extraction algorithm. For each marker, the baseline value was defined as the closest eligible laboratory result obtained before t0. Same-day values were accepted only when they represented baseline assessment at episode entry; if time-of-day information indicated that a laboratory value was obtained after drug administration, that value was not eligible for feature construction. When repeated measurements were available before t0, the closest pre-t0 measurement was selected; post-t0 maxima, minima, slopes, deltas, or trigger-defining values were not used. The temporal relationship between baseline liver biochemistry and t0 was audited for the key predictors ALT, AST, and gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) and is reported in Supplemental Table 1. For each laboratory variable, a corresponding missingness indicator was generated and retained during feature quality control. Laboratory results obtained after t0 were not used as model inputs.

Temporal leakage control and repeated-measurement audit

Temporal leakage control was implemented at three stages. First, t0 was frozen before outcome ascertainment and before model development. Second, the feature-construction scripts retained only baseline values meeting the prespecified timing rule and excluded all post-t0 laboratory evolution, liver injury triggers, RUCAM domains, RUCAM categories, dechallenge information, and clinical-review outputs. Third, the analysis-ready dataset was audited to confirm that baseline liver biochemistry values used for modeling preceded or coincided with episode entry and that repeated post-baseline measurements within the same episode were not selected as predictors.

Drug-exposure variables

Drug-related predictors were summarized at the episode level using only information available up to t0. These included the number of unique drugs present at episode entry, number of concomitant medications, baseline exposure duration up to t0 where applicable, maximum prescribed daily dose at baseline, route of administration, and overall treatment complexity. Drug-exposure descriptors summarized the medication regimen at episode entry and were not derived from RUCAM suspected drug attribution.

Because multiple drugs could be present within a single episode, a prespecified, outcome-independent ranking algorithm was used to assign episode-level drug-risk descriptors. First, all drugs present at or before t0 were mapped to FDA DILIrank 2.0 when possible. Second, the episode-level highest-risk exposed drug was selected using the ordered concern categories Most-DILI-concern > Less-DILI-concern > Ambiguous-DILI-concern > No-DILI-concern > unmapped. Third, if more than one drug shared the same highest concern category, the drug with the highest FDA-derived DILI severity score was selected. If a tie remained, the earliest drug recorded at t0 was used only as a deterministic tie-breaker. The same ranking algorithm was used across the primary model and feature-restricted/sensitivity analyses; it was not modified according to observed outcome status or RUCAM adjudication.

External drug-risk metadata

Drug-specific hepatotoxicity metadata were derived from the FDA DILIrank 2.0 resource. 22 Drugs were categorized into four regulatory concern groups: Most-DILI-concern, Less-DILI-concern, No-DILI-concern, and Ambiguous-DILI-concern. For analysis, this variable was treated as an ordinal baseline descriptor, with higher categories indicating greater prior regulatory concern for DILI.

In addition, the FDA-derived DILI severity score (range 0–8), reflecting increasing severity of reported liver injury signals in humans, was incorporated as a drug-level baseline risk descriptor when available.

These external drug-risk variables were used as pre-existing metadata and not as outcome-derived features.

Handling of missing data

Continuous variables with missing values were imputed using median values calculated from the development cohort only. Binary missingness indicators were created for baseline laboratory variables and retained as separate candidate predictors.

No information collected after t0 was used for imputation. Imputation parameters derived from the development cohort were applied unchanged to the temporal and prospective validation cohorts.

Dataset partitioning and temporal validation strategy

To preserve temporal validity and reflect real-world deployment, the analysis-ready cohort was partitioned chronologically into two non-overlapping datasets:

- Development cohort: episodes with t0 from January 1, 2019 to December 31, 2023

- Development cohort: episodes with t0 from January 1, 2019 to December 31, 2023

- Prospective locked validation cohort: episodes with t0 from January 1, 2024 to December 31, 2025

- Prospective locked validation cohort: episodes with t0 from January 1, 2024 to December 31, 2025

Prospective testing procedure

After completion of model development in the 2019–2023 cohort, the final modeling pipeline was locked, including the predictor set, preprocessing steps, imputation strategy, model hyperparameters, probability calibration procedure, and decision threshold. From January 1, 2024 to December 31, 2025, newly occurring eligible hospitalization-based drug-exposure episodes were enrolled consecutively using the same cohort definitions and data-extraction rules as in the development phase. Baseline predictors were derived at episode entry, while outcome ascertainment followed the same pharmacovigilance workflow, including biochemical trigger surveillance, clinical review, and RUCAM-based adjudication. Predictions for the 2024–2025 cohort were generated using the locked model without retraining, recalibration, or threshold modification.

Model development, calibration, and evaluation

Model development

A penalized logistic regression model was developed as a baseline comparator. The primary machine learning model used gradient-boosted decision trees implemented with the Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) framework.

All model development procedures were conducted exclusively within the development cohort. Internal validation within the development cohort was performed using fivefold GroupKFold cross-validation at the patient level, ensuring that episodes from the same patient were not split across training and validation folds. Hyperparameter tuning was performed exclusively within the development cohort.

Only predictors defined before or at t0 were eligible for model training. Variables reflecting events after t0, variables generated during DILI adjudication, initial or final RUCAM category variables, injury window, R ratio at injury onset, and post-baseline laboratory evolution were excluded a priori.

Probability calibration and threshold selection

Predicted probabilities from the primary model were calibrated using isotonic regression based on out-of-fold predictions from the development cohort. A single operating threshold was then selected in the development cohort according to the prespecified decision rule and applied unchanged to the temporal retrospective and prospective validation cohorts.

Model evaluation

Final model performance was evaluated in the prospective temporal test cohort (2024–2025).

Discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) and the area under the precision–recall curve (AUPRC or PR-AUC). Threshold-dependent performance measures included sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and F1-score when applicable. Calibration was assessed using calibration plots and summary calibration metrics, including the Brier score. Uncertainty was quantified using nonparametric bootstrap resampling to derive 95% confidence intervals.

To improve interpretability, feature contributions were examined using model-based importance measures and permutation-based analyses. Selected correctly classified and misclassified cases were reviewed to relate model outputs to clinically interpretable patient characteristics, baseline laboratory profiles, and drug-exposure patterns.

Quality control and software

Quality control

Quality control procedures were implemented throughout data extraction, cohort assembly, variable construction, and model evaluation. These included verification of patient-level separation across cohorts, consistency checks for laboratory units and reference ranges, review of implausible predictor values, audit of episode linkage across hospital information systems, confirmation that no outcome-derived variables were included in the predictor set, and review of repeated laboratory measurements to confirm that post-t0 values were not selected as baseline predictors.

We additionally performed a data-processing audit to verify completeness of baseline liver biochemistry in the final modeling cohort, to document patient-level separation between the development and temporal validation cohorts, and to quantify the timing of key baseline liver biochemistry measurements relative to t0. The final analysis-ready table retained the extracted baseline values used for modeling; marker-specific laboratory timestamps were used only for auditing and were not used as predictors.

Software

All analyses were performed using Python version 3.9 (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA). Data processing and statistical analyses used standard scientific computing libraries, including pandas, NumPy, scikit-learn, and LightGBM.

Results

Study cohort and dataset construction

As shown in Figure 1, 5095 eligible drug-exposure episodes from 3579 patients were initially identified from the EHR. During data preparation, 2308 episodes were excluded, including 2215 due to missing baseline liver biochemistry and 93 due to missing drug-exposure covariates. The remaining 2786 episodes from 2712 patients constituted the analysis-ready cohort. All analysis-ready episodes were followed for biochemical liver injury triggers. Trigger-positive episodes underwent clinical review and RUCAM-based adjudication within the hospital pharmacovigilance workflow. The final labeled cohort comprised 274 DILI-positive episodes and 2512 non-DILI episodes. For model development and validation, the final labeled cohort was partitioned chronologically at the patient level, with no patient overlap between cohorts. Episodes from 2019 to 2023 were assigned to the development cohort, and episodes from 2024 to 2025 formed the prospective validation cohort. The development cohort included 1958 episodes, of which 166 were DILI-positive, and 1792 were non-DILI, while the prospective validation cohort included 828 episodes, of which 108 were DILI-positive, and 720 were non-DILI.

Study cohort derivation and dataset splitting.

A marker-specific timing audit was performed for key baseline liver biochemistry predictors. Baseline ALT, AST, and GGT measurements were obtained before or on the same day as t0 in all audited records, with no post-t0 measurements used for feature construction. The median interval before t0 was 1 day for ALT (IQR 0–39), 0 days for AST (IQR 0–25), and 0 days for GGT (IQR 0–18). Measurements obtained strictly before t0 accounted for 89.1% of ALT, 93.6% of AST, and 97.9% of GGT values, while same-day measurements accounted for the remainder. No ALT, AST, or GGT value occurred after t0 (Supplemental Table 1).

To assess potential selection bias from exclusions due to missing baseline liver biochemistry, we compared included episodes with those excluded for this reason. Most baseline characteristics showed negligible or small imbalance. The largest differences were observed for hypertension and length of hospital stay, with higher hypertension prevalence and longer hospitalization among included episodes. These findings suggest that the analysis-ready cohort may represent patients with greater clinical complexity or more intensive laboratory monitoring, whereas less monitored episodes were more likely to be excluded. Accordingly, the model should be interpreted as applying to drug-exposure episodes with available baseline liver biochemistry rather than to all medication exposures in the source population (Supplemental Table 2).

To assess the consistency of RUCAM-based outcome adjudication, an adjudication consistency audit was performed. Two clinical pharmacists independently assessed RUCAM classification, followed by consensus review when required. Agreement for binary DILI classification using the predefined final consensus threshold of RUCAM ⩾6 was 87.5%, with a Cohen’s kappa of 0.73, indicating substantial agreement. Agreement on the full initial RUCAM category was 87.5%, with a weighted kappa of 0.68. The median absolute difference in RUCAM total score was 1 point (IQR 0–2). Consensus adjudication was required in 115 episodes (4.13%), mainly because of uncertainty regarding chronology, competing etiologies, or multi-drug exposure. Some borderline episodes initially classified as possible or probable were assigned to the non-DILI group after consensus review when competing etiologies or chronology made drug causality less likely. All disagreements were resolved after consensus review (Supplementary Table 3).

Baseline characteristics of the study population

Table 1 summarizes baseline demographic, anthropometric, clinical, laboratory, and drug-exposure characteristics, together with descriptive outcome-adjudication descriptors that were not used as model predictors. Compared with non-DILI episodes, DILI episodes occurred in older patients, were less frequent in males, and were more often associated with hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and pulmonary embolism. Baseline total bilirubin was slightly higher in the DILI group. Drug-exposure patterns were generally similar between groups, although the descriptive injury window was substantially longer in DILI episodes, and drugs classified as Most-DILI-concern were more common. The hepatocellular pattern was the predominant phenotype, particularly among DILI episodes. As expected, RUCAM scores were higher in the DILI group; however, the RUCAM categories shown in Table 1 are initial descriptive categories recorded during clinical review and should be interpreted together with final consensus adjudication.

Factors associated with DILI

Table 2 presents crude and adjusted logistic regression analyses of factors associated with DILI. In crude analyses, female sex, older age, chronic liver disease, dyslipidemia, number of unique drugs, number of prescription records, and severity class of the suspected drug were associated with DILI. After multivariable adjustment, older age remained independently associated with higher odds of DILI, while chronic liver disease showed an inverse association. This inverse association was considered most likely to reflect coding, monitoring, prescribing, and adjudication effects rather than a protective biological effect. Among baseline laboratory variables, AST and ALP remained statistically significant after adjustment, whereas ALT and GGT were no longer significant in the fully adjusted model. Alcohol risk was also associated with higher adjusted odds, although the estimate was imprecise, as reflected by the wide confidence interval. Among drug exposure-related variables, the severity class of the suspected drug remained significantly associated with DILI, whereas the number of unique drugs and prescription records did not remain statistically significant after adjustment.

Predictive model performance

As shown in Table 3, tree-based ensemble models consistently outperformed regression-based models on the held-out temporal test set. LightGBM achieved the highest discrimination, with an AUROC of 0.965 and a PR-AUC of 0.903, while maintaining good overall probability accuracy, with a Brier score of 0.034. XGBoost and random forest also showed strong discrimination, but neither exceeded the final LightGBM model in overall validation performance. At the prespecified sensitivity-oriented operating point selected in the development/calibration cohort and applied unchanged to the temporal test cohort, LightGBM achieved a sensitivity of 0.944, specificity of 0.796, PPV of 0.410, and NPV of 0.990, corresponding to 30.1 alerts per 100 episodes. In contrast, the regression-based models showed substantially lower discrimination, with AUROC values below 0.780 and PR-AUC values below 0.360, while requiring a higher alert burden to achieve high sensitivity.

Calibration across probability ranges and clinical subgroups

To assess calibration beyond the global calibration plot, we further evaluated calibration across predicted-risk ranges and clinically relevant subgroups (Supplemental Table 5). Overall, the final LightGBM model showed close agreement between observed and predicted risk in the temporal test cohort, with an observed event rate of 0.130, a mean predicted risk of 0.119, an observed-to-expected ratio of 1.09, and a Brier score of 0.034. Across predicted-risk ranges, observed event rates increased from 0.012 in the <0.01 predicted-risk stratum to 0.803 in the ⩾0.20 stratum, supporting clinically meaningful risk ordering. Calibration was strongest in the highest-risk stratum, where observed and predicted risks were closely aligned (0.803 vs 0.815; O/E ratio 0.99). In lower-risk strata, the model tended to underestimate observed risk, although the absolute event rates remained low.

Model interpretation and feature importance

Figure 3 indicates that baseline liver biochemistry variables contributed most strongly to model performance. In the permutation-importance analysis on the test set, baseline GGT showed the largest decrease in AP after permutation, followed by ALT, total bilirubin, and AST. Chronic liver disease and baseline ALP had smaller contributions, while the remaining variables showed limited influence. The split-gain analysis showed a similar ranking pattern, with ALT, bilirubin, GGT, and AST among the most influential features during model training. Overall, the results suggest that liver-related laboratory parameters were the main drivers of prediction, whereas clinical and drug-related variables provided more modest additional contribution.

As shown in Figure 4, the final LightGBM model consistently outperformed both reference strategies in decision curve analysis, providing greater net benefit than “treat all” and “treat none” across the evaluated threshold spectrum. Incremental value of non-laboratory predictors

Because LightGBM was selected as the final model and feature-importance analysis showed that baseline liver biochemistry dominated model performance, we conducted an additional ablation analysis to quantify the incremental value of non-laboratory predictors within the LightGBM framework. Feature-restricted LightGBM models were trained and evaluated on the same temporal test cohort (Supplemental Table 4). The laboratory-only model already achieved strong discrimination, with an AUROC of 0.972 and a PR-AUC of 0.900, confirming that baseline liver biochemistry accounted for the largest share of predictive signal. Models based only on non-laboratory variables performed substantially worse, including the clinical non-laboratory model (AUROC 0.680; PR-AUC 0.288), the drug/DILIrank-only model (AUROC 0.610; PR-AUC 0.182), and the combined non-laboratory model (AUROC 0.733; PR-AUC 0.292). However, adding non-laboratory variables to laboratory predictors modestly improved PR-AUC from 0.900 to 0.916 and reduced the Brier score from 0.042 to 0.034. The full model achieved a PR-AUC of 0.917 and a Brier score of 0.034, with similar AUROC but better probability accuracy than the laboratory-only model. These findings indicate that baseline liver biochemistry was the dominant predictive domain, while non-laboratory variables provided modest incremental value, particularly in precision–recall performance and calibration.

Discussion

In this temporally validated EHR cohort, tree-based ensemble models outperformed regression-based approaches, and the final LightGBM model showed strong discrimination on the prospectively assessed, temporally separated test set (AUROC 0.965, 95% CI 0.942–0.983; AP 0.903, 95% CI 0.856–0.943). Calibration also showed acceptable agreement between predicted and observed risk on grouped assessment. These metrics are particularly relevant because DILI was relatively infrequent in routine care (test prevalence 0.13), making precision–recall performance directly relevant to expected alert burden and case capture.23 –25 Model interpretation was also clinically plausible, with baseline liver biochemistry emerging as the dominant group of predictors and platelet count showing a smaller, secondary contribution. In practice, such a model may support early risk stratification within pharmacovigilance workflows by helping prioritize higher-risk episodes for pharmacist or hepatology review. However, model outputs should be interpreted as decision-support information rather than definitive diagnostic evidence, and decisions regarding drug discontinuation or dechallenge should remain grounded in comprehensive clinical evaluation, including alternative etiologies, temporal relationships, and clinician judgment.10,11

Previous work in this field has largely followed two directions. Drug-centered approaches, such as QSAR models and rule-based hepatotoxicity signals, are mainly intended for drug development or regulatory risk characterization rather than patient-level bedside prediction.15,22 Mechanistic platforms such as DILIsym and quantitative systems pharmacology provide explanatory value but are not designed for real-time prediction using routine EHR variables. 17 Within clinical prediction, most published models have focused on tuberculosis-related DILI, where recent transaminase values, dynamic changes, and regimen-specific exposures are often the most prominent predictors.26 –28 By contrast, the present study provides a general DILI episode-level model embedded in a real-world pharmacovigilance adjudication workflow, evaluated in a prospectively assessed, temporally separated validation cohort and reported using AP and calibration in addition to AUROC.29,30 This is relevant because EHR-based adverse-event prediction models often report more modest discrimination and frequently lack robust temporal validation. 25

Recent DILI prediction studies have increasingly incorporated biological and immune-susceptibility information. Plasma proteomics has been investigated for predicting anti-tuberculosis DILI, suggesting that pretreatment protein signatures may identify susceptible patients before overt injury. In parallel, machine learning analyses have reported that prior drug allergies are associated with worse outcomes in iDILI, supporting the role of immune-mediated host susceptibility. These studies are complementary to our EHR-based model. Whereas proteomic and allergy-based approaches may improve biological specificity and prognostic stratification, our model was designed for immediate implementation using routinely available baseline laboratory, clinical, and drug-exposure data. Future models should evaluate whether adding structured drug-allergy history and omics-derived biomarkers can further improve DILI risk prediction beyond conventional EHR variables.31,32

Several design choices strengthen the translational relevance of these findings. First, temporal separation at the patient level reduces leakage from repeated episodes and better approximates real-world distribution shift over time. 30 Second, reporting precision–recall performance is appropriate for an imbalanced endpoint because PR curves provide a more informative view of expected positive predictive value behavior when negative episodes predominate.23,24 Third, calibration was assessed using a post hoc holdout procedure within the development cohort, which is clinically relevant because implementation decisions are generally threshold-based rather than rank-based. 33 Fourth, combining permutation importance on the temporally separated test set with split-gain analysis during training offers two complementary perspectives on model behavior and helps reduce over-interpretation of training artifacts.10,34 Finally, because the outcome definition was based on RUCAM, the model estimates the probability of RUCAM-defined DILI within the institutional clinical workflow rather than a biomarker-confirmed mechanistic subtype of liver injury.10,13

It is unsurprising that baseline ALT, AST, bilirubin, and GGT were among the leading predictors, as baseline liver biochemistry likely reflects pre-existing hepatic stress, cholestatic burden, or reduced physiologic reserve that may lower tolerance to hepatotoxic insults.10,13 Among these variables, baseline GGT was the most distinctive signal. In our previous study, baseline GGT was already independently associated with DILI risk in a multivariable logistic regression model and contributed to a clinically usable prediction model with an AUROC of 0.883 (95% CI 0.868–0.897), supporting its value as an early baseline biomarker before prescribing. 35 The present study extends that earlier observation within a machine learning framework: baseline GGT ranked highest on permutation importance in the temporally separated test set and remained among the leading variables in split-gain analysis during training. From a machine learning perspective, this pattern suggests that GGT contributed not only to model fitting but also to out-of-sample prediction on unseen data. Clinically, this is plausible because DILI still lacks a specific diagnostic biomarker and continues to rely on conventional liver tests interpreted in the context of drug exposure and exclusion of competing causes. 10 GGT has also been linked to glutathione turnover and oxidative stress, which may make it a broader indicator of hepatobiliary vulnerability than transaminases alone. 36

Baseline total bilirubin was also highly ranked and is mechanistically relevant in DILI. The clinical importance of bilirubin is underscored by Hy’s law, while the EASL guideline notes that conjugated bilirubin is more directly informative than unconjugated bilirubin for impaired hepatocellular excretory function. 10 In this context, the prominence of baseline total bilirubin in the model may indicate limited hepatobiliary reserve or subclinical excretory vulnerability before overt DILI becomes clinically apparent. This interpretation is biologically plausible because bilirubin handling depends on coordinated hepatobiliary transport processes, and disruption of canalicular transport is central to drug-related bilirubin retention and clinically meaningful liver injury. 37 Bilirubin also has antioxidant and signaling properties, suggesting that it may represent more than a passive marker of established liver damage. 38 However, this signal should be interpreted cautiously because the present model used total rather than fractionated bilirubin, and mild unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia may also reflect conditions such as hemolysis or Gilbert syndrome.

Baseline platelet count still appeared to contribute to the model, although its importance was clearly lower than that of the major liver biochemistry variables. In Figure 3, platelet count showed a visible contribution in split-gain analysis during training, whereas its permutation importance on the test set was smaller but remained above zero. This pattern suggests that platelet count may provide incremental information within the multivariable prediction structure, possibly by refining risk stratification in combination with liver biochemistry and drug-related variables, rather than acting as a dominant standalone predictor. From a biological perspective, platelet count is a recognized surrogate marker of portal hypertension and impaired hepatic reserve, 39 and it is incorporated into widely used noninvasive fibrosis indices such as FIB-4 because it can capture aspects of chronic liver disease severity that may not be fully represented in structured EHR coding. 40 This may be relevant in the present setting, as host factors and underlying liver disease are known to influence the severity and clinical course of DILI.20,41 At the same time, these findings should be interpreted conservatively. The present data do not support direct inference regarding the predictive role of composite indices such as the GGT-to-platelet ratio.42 –44 The most defensible interpretation is that platelet count contributes modest additional information and remains secondary to the dominant contribution of core liver biochemistry variables.8,45

The contribution of DILIrank-derived variables is also clinically relevant because they incorporate prior human evidence on hepatotoxic potential into a structured feature set that can be combined with patient vulnerability markers.22,46 FDA DILIrank-derived variables represent external prior knowledge on drug hepatotoxicity rather than patient-outcome-derived information. Their use as baseline descriptors is therefore appropriate for risk stratification, particularly in multi-drug episodes where the model aims to summarize regimen-level hepatotoxicity burden rather than assign causality to a single drug. This provides a pragmatic bridge between drug-level hepatotoxicity knowledge bases and patient-level prediction. It may also help explain why tree-based ensemble models outperformed baseline regression-based approaches, as ensemble methods can accommodate nonlinear effects and higher-order interactions more flexibly than conventional regression models.33,34 Nonetheless, the model should not be interpreted as establishing causality for any individual feature; rather, it captures a risk signature that is broadly consistent with current understanding of iDILI as a multifactorial process involving host susceptibility, drug properties, and clinical context.8,17

The inverse association observed for coded chronic liver disease should not be interpreted as evidence that chronic liver disease protects against DILI. In this EHR cohort, the chronic liver disease variable may have captured stable historical diagnoses, prior abnormal liver tests, or patients already under closer hepatobiliary surveillance rather than active decompensated liver disease. Patients with known chronic liver disease may also have been monitored more frequently, prescribed hepatotoxic drugs more cautiously, or adjudicated more conservatively because alternative hepatic etiologies were more apparent. This pattern is therefore most plausibly explained by coding, selection, monitoring, prescribing, and adjudication effects, and it supports treating chronic liver disease as a contextual risk-stratification variable rather than as a causal protective factor.

This study has several strengths: it used a sizeable real-world episode cohort assembled from linked electronic medical record, laboratory information system, and pharmacy databases; outcome adjudication was embedded within an established pharmacovigilance workflow using RUCAM; model evaluation was performed in a prospectively assessed, chronologically separated validation cohort from 2024 to 2025; performance reporting included AUROC, AP, and calibration assessment; and model interpretation was supported by both permutation importance on the test set and split-gain analysis during training. Because liver biochemistry was the dominant predictor group, we specifically audited the timing of key baseline laboratory variables relative to t0. This audit showed that ALT, AST, and GGT were selected before or on the same day as t0, with no post-t0 measurements included. These findings support the temporal validity of the baseline feature-construction process and reduce concern that model performance was driven by follow-up laboratory information. Nevertheless, the model should be interpreted as a pragmatic episode-level risk-stratification tool for pharmacovigilance prioritization rather than as definitive evidence of mechanistic pre-injury prediction.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center study, and generalizability may be limited by differences in prescribing patterns, laboratory reference ranges, coding practices, and monitoring workflows across institutions. Second, a substantial number of episodes were excluded because of missing baseline liver biochemistry, which may have introduced selection bias toward patients undergoing more intensive testing; the model should therefore be applied to settings where baseline liver biochemistry is available rather than to all medication exposures. Third, although RUCAM is widely used, it has known inter-rater variability and depends on the completeness of alternative-cause evaluation. RUCAM adjudicators could not be blinded to the clinical, laboratory, medication, and chronology information required for RUCAM scoring, although they were not provided with model predictions or model-derived outputs. Potential incorporation bias may therefore remain, particularly because baseline liver biochemistry and drug exposure are also clinically relevant to DILI adjudication; accordingly, the model predicts RUCAM-defined DILI probability within the institutional workflow rather than a biomarker-confirmed mechanistic endpoint. Fourth, the model was intentionally restricted to baseline variables available at episode start and therefore does not incorporate dynamic post-exposure trajectories that may further improve short-term detection. Fifth, descriptive variables such as injury window, R ratio at injury onset, liver injury phenotype, and RUCAM categories were not used as predictors but still depend on follow-up and adjudication processes. Finally, although the validation cohort was prospectively assessed and temporally separated, external multicenter validation remains necessary before broader implementation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we developed and validated an EHR-based machine learning model for predicting DILI at the drug-exposure episode level using routinely available baseline clinical variables. Among the candidate models evaluated, LightGBM showed the best overall performance and demonstrated strong discrimination with acceptable grouped calibration in a prospectively assessed, temporally separated validation cohort. Baseline liver biochemistry, particularly GGT and bilirubin, together with drug-related hepatotoxicity priors, were central to model performance, while platelet count provided a more modest additional contribution. These findings suggest that integrating host-related clinical features with drug-related risk information may support earlier risk stratification within pharmacovigilance workflows. Future studies should focus on external multicenter validation and prospective evaluation of clinical utility and workflow impact.
