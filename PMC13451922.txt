Flood-season surface water-groundwater interactions across distinct geomorphic units in a Yellow River Great Bend tributary basin
Authors: Yinlong Wang, Ruizhong Gao, Debin Jia, Xixi Wang, Tingxi Liu, Xiaomin Liu, Shiming Bai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySurface water-groundwater interactions are complex in the Great Bend region of the Yellow River. Clarifying water exchange and source differences among geomorphic units is important for understanding regional hydrological processes and improving water resources management. In this study, the Wulanmulun River Basin, a typical tributary of the Yellow River, was selected as the study area. A total of 90 water samples were collected, including 25 river water samples, 43 groundwater samples, and 22 precipitation samples. Gibbs diagrams, multivariate statistical analysis, PMF, and MixSIAR models were used to investigate the hydrochemical and isotopic characteristics, source contributions, and transformation relationships of surface water and groundwater. The results showed that, under the combined effects of rock weathering dissolution and evaporation concentration, the hydrochemical types of surface water and groundwater in the study area were dominated by Cl-Na and HCO3-Ca types, respectively. The source contributions of hydrochemical components differed between surface water and groundwater. Groundwater was mainly controlled by geological processes (41.1%), followed by agricultural input (31.3%); surface water was also dominated by geological processes (36.1%), but the influence of domestic input was enhanced (34.1%). In the hilly regions, groundwater discharge mainly recharged surface water, with an average contribution rate of 32.4%. In the plain and built-up areas, surface water infiltration mainly recharged groundwater, with average contribution rates of 34.2% and 25.0%, respectively. These results enhance understanding of surface water-groundwater transformation in the Yellow River’s “Great Bend” and support scientific management and efficient utilization of basin water resources.Keywords: stable isotopes, hydrogeochemistry, MixSIAR model, water cycle, Yellow River’s Great Bend

Introduction

Water resources play a key role in maintaining the stability of natural ecosystems and supporting the sustainable development of socio-economic systems. Water resources hold a particularly strategic importance in the Yellow River Basin, where water scarcity coexists with increasing development demands.1 The interactions and transformation processes between different water sources within a watershed, as the fundamental unit of the hydrological cycle, are vital for achieving sustainable water management and scientific resource allocation.2 This is especially true in regions undergoing rapid urbanization and intensive agricultural development, where surface water and groundwater interactions exhibit dynamic complexity and spatial-temporal heterogeneity, posing greater challenges for sustainable water use and protection.3,4

As a major water-supply source in northern China, the Yellow River is crucial for safeguarding national ecological security and promoting regional socio-economic development. However, different regions within its basin are confronted with multiple water and environmental challenges, including groundwater overexploitation, water pollution, flood hazards, and water shortages.5,6 In particular, the Inner Mongolia reach of the Yellow River’s “Great Bend,” situated in the river’s northernmost segment, is characterized by pronounced water scarcity and a vulnerable ecological environment.7,8 To address such challenges, existing studies have predominantly examined the origins of regional water resources, variations in water quality, and the ecological evolution of watershed environments.9,10,11 However, investigations into the interaction and transformation mechanisms between surface-subsurface water within representative basins of the Yellow River’s “Great Bend” remain insufficiently explored. Therefore, accurately identifying and evaluating the recharge dynamics and transformation processes between surface-subsurface water within these basins is critical for sound regional water resources management.

Over the past few years, the application of advanced methods and technologies has significantly enhanced our ability to characterize interactions and transformation processes between groundwater and surface water. Widely adopted methodologies comprise stable and radioactive isotopic tracing, hydrochemical characterization, and numerical modeling.12,13 Among these, δ2H and δ18O isotopes have proven especially effective due to their pronounced spatial and temporal variability, allowing researchers to capture key hydrological processes such as evaporation, precipitation, and recharge. Hydrochemical indicators also provide important insights into water origins, flow paths, and geochemical transformations through their interactions with surrounding environmental media. While each method offers valuable information, they also come with inherent limitations, uncertainties, and scale dependencies. As such, adopting integrated, multi-method, and multi-scale approaches is essential for effectively capturing the complex and dynamic interactions between groundwater and surface water. As an example, Zabala et al. employed stable-isotope methods to investigate spring-summer spatiotemporal variability in precipitation, evaporation, and groundwater-surface water exchange in the Del Azul Creek Basin, thereby improving the hydrological conceptual model; however, they did not quantify inter-source water fluxes.14 Isotopes such as δ18O and δ2H have been extensively applied to identify groundwater recharge sources, assess the effects of evaporation on salinity, and quantify vertical exchange fluxes between rivers and aquifers.15,16,17,18 Statistical approaches with Piper diagrams, Gibbs plots, and ion ratio analysis have been used to examine the spatiotemporal variability of hydrochemical characteristics and the associated formation mechanisms in surface water and groundwater systems.19 In parallel, MixSIAR has been used to interpret δ18O and δ2H patterns, demonstrating that atmospheric precipitation is the dominant water source in many regions and enabling quantification of the relative contributions from different water bodies.20 Furthermore, the joint use of hydrochemical datasets, land-use information, and MixSIAR, together with multi-isotope fingerprinting, has been shown to effectively determine the dominant sources of nitrate pollution and to quantify their relative contributions.21

Located in the Inner Mongolia reach of the Yellow River’s “Great Bend,” the Wulanmulun River functions as an important tributary, playing a critical role in maintaining regional ecological integrity and promoting the sustainable use of water resources. Over recent years, changes in urban development and patterns of resource exploitation in the Wulanmulun River Basin may have influenced the natural movement and storage of both surface water and groundwater, thereby modifying the magnitude and characteristics of surface water-groundwater interactions. This has led to potential variations in hydrological characteristics of water bodies in different geographic locations during the water cycle. Currently, studies addressing surface water-groundwater interactions and conversion processes within this basin remain scarce, and the underlying mechanisms have not yet been comprehensively elucidated. To address this gap, the present research combines hydrochemical characterization, stable isotope analyses, and a Bayesian mixing approach to examine hydrological and geochemical processes, as well as the interactions and transformation dynamics between surface and subsurface waters, in different zones (i.e., hilly regions, plain region, and built-up area) of the Wulanmulun River Basin. This study seeks to provide a scientific foundation for the optimization of water resource allocation and management, with the ultimate goal of supporting high-quality development across the Yellow River Basin. Specifically, the objectives are to: (1) characterize the hydrochemical features and water-rock interactions across different regions of the basin, (2) elucidate the hydrogeochemical processes governing the formation of surface water and groundwater across different zones, and (3) quantify surface-groundwater transformation processes in different regions.

Results

Hydrochemical characteristics of water bodies in different subregions

The study area is the Wulanmulun River Basin (Figure 1). The hydrochemical compositions of groundwater and surface water in different zones of the Wulanmulun River Basin showed obvious zonal differences (Figure 2). The mean pH of groundwater was 8.82, indicating weak alkalinity overall, and the mean total dissolved solids (TDS) was 500.5 mg/L. Both pH and TDS were highest in the plain region and lowest in the built-up area. The mean pH of surface water was 9.25, indicating overall alkalinity, and the mean TDS was 837 mg/L. Both parameters showed a spatial pattern of built-up area > plain region > hilly regions. In the hilly regions, no significant differences were observed in any indicators between groundwater and surface water, and TDS was generally low, indicating that the hydrochemical compositions of the two water bodies were relatively similar in this area, possibly influenced by a low-mineralization recharge background.

In contrast, differences between groundwater and surface water were more pronounced in the plain region and built-up area, with significant differences mainly concentrated in K+, Na+, Cl−, NO3−, SO42−, and TDS. Among them, K+, Na+, Cl−, SO42−, and TDS were generally higher in surface water, whereas NO3− was relatively enriched in groundwater. Particularly in the built-up area, surface water TDS increased markedly, while groundwater TDS remained relatively low, showing strong intergroup differentiation. This suggests that groundwater may be affected by relatively low-mineralization recharge or dilution caused by river water infiltration. Overall, the groundwater-surface water hydrochemical relationships in the Wulanmulun River Basin showed obvious differences. The hilly regions exhibited hydrochemical consistency under a low-mineralization background, whereas the plain region and built-up area showed stronger water differentiation, indicating that processes such as hydraulic connectivity and solute migration pathways between groundwater and surface water may differ among different geomorphic units.

Hydrochemical types of water bodies in different subregions

Hydrochemical typology of waters varies across subregions of the Wulanmulun River Basin (Figure 3). Most groundwater samples fall within subregion 1 of the Piper diagram, predominantly classified as HCO3–Ca type. This is especially true for groundwater in the hilly and plain regions, suggesting that carbonate mineral dissolution significantly influences groundwater chemistry. In contrast, most surface water samples cluster in subregion 2 of the diagram, primarily exhibiting a Cl–Na type. In the hilly and plain region, several surface-water samples also exhibit a shift toward the HCO3‑Ca type, suggesting that the hydrochemical composition of these river reaches may be affected by groundwater discharge (baseflow input) or by mixing between river water and groundwater. The hydrogeological setting and stratigraphic profile of the basin provide additional context for interpreting these interactions (Figure 4). Groundwater is primarily characterized by Ca2+, Na+, and HCO3− as the dominant ions, whereas surface water shows relatively higher levels of Na+, HCO3−, and Cl−. This contrast indicates that the two water bodies are governed by different prevailing processes and may exhibit potential mixing relationships.

Mineral saturation indices of water bodies in different subregions

Saturation index (SI) values were applied to simulate the dissolution and precipitation status of five common minerals, including anhydrite (CaSO4), calcite (CaCO3), dolomite (CaMg(CO3)2), gypsum (CaSO4·H2O), and halite (NaCl) (Figure 5). SI values between −0.2 and 0.2 indicate near-equilibrium between the mineral and water. SI values less than −0.2 suggest undersaturation, where the mineral tends to dissolve, while values greater than 0.2 indicate supersaturation and a tendency for precipitation.22 As shown in Figure 5A, the SI of calcite (CaCO3) and dolomite (CaMg(CO3)2) in surface water are both greater than 0.2, indicative of a supersaturated state for these minerals. Dolomite exhibits a higher SI compared with calcite, suggesting a stronger tendency for Ca2+, Mg2+, CO32−, and HCO3− to precipitate. In contrast, CaSO4, CaSO4·H2O, and NaCl exhibit SI values well below −0.2, indicating that these minerals remain undersaturated and continue to dissolve. Similarly, groundwater in the basin also shows supersaturation with respect to CaCO3 and CaMg(CO3)2, with SI values in the hilly regions markedly exceeding those observed in the plain and built-up areas (Figure 5B). In the hilly regions, anhydrite and gypsum show a transition from undersaturated to supersaturated states, while in the plain and built-up areas, these minerals remain undersaturated and continue to dissolve. Halite remains strongly undersaturated across all regions, and its SI values are also higher in the hilly regions than in the other two zones.

Isotopic characteristics of water bodies in different subregions

Statistical analysis of isotopic data indicates that groundwater δ2H ranges from −85.62‰ to −47.15‰ and δ18O from −12.06‰ to −7.49‰, respectively, with the highest values observed in the plain region, followed by the hilly regions and then the built-up area. For surface water, δ2H varies from −57.06‰ to 10.28‰, and δ18O ranges from −9.79‰ to 3.70‰. The strongest isotopic enrichment occurs in the built-up area, followed by the plain and hilly regions (Table 1). Overall, the δ18O and δ2H contents in the basin’s water are greater in surface water than in groundwater, and there are significant spatial differences in isotopic composition across different subregions.

Discussion

Sources and controlling factors of major ions

Gibbs diagrams can be used to evaluate the relative influences of precipitation input, evaporation concentration, and rock weathering on the hydrochemical composition of natural waters. By comparing TDS with Na/(Na + Ca) values (Figure 6A) and Cl−/(Cl− + HCO3−) values (Figure 6B), the controlling factors and formation mechanisms of natural water chemistry can be identified. As presented in Figure 7 surface water and groundwater samples in the basin were generally distributed within the rock weathering dominance zone, indicating that water–rock interaction was the main controlling process for the formation of hydrochemical compositions in the study area.23 Notably, some surface water samples from the built-up area showed a tendency to shift toward the evaporation end-member.

The ionic origins were further investigated on the basis of relationships among Ca2+/Na+, Mg2+/Na+, and HCO3−/Na+ in surface water and groundwater, thereby revealing the evolutionary characteristics of hydrochemical components among different water bodies24 (Figures 7A and 7B).Groundwater sampling points are primarily concentrated between the silicate and carbonate rock zones, which further indicates that the hydrochemical composition of groundwater is primarily influenced by both factors. In contrast, surface water samples fall between the silicate and evaporite zones, with those from the built-up area shifting notably toward the evaporite zone. This suggests that surface water in the hilly and plain regions is influenced by both evaporite and silicate weathering, while surface water from the built-up area exhibited a more pronounced evaporite dissolution signal. Together with the higher TDS and major ion concentrations in surface water from the built-up area, evaporation concentration may have further promoted the accumulation of dissolved ions.

The milliequivalent concentration ratios were further used to identify the main controlling factors affecting hydrochemical characteristics.25The (Na+ + K+)/Cl− diagram is widely applied to identify the dominant sources of Na+ (Figure 8A). Most surface-water and groundwater samples from the basin plot below the y = x equilibrium line. Among these, groundwater samples and some plain region surface water samples are positioned close to the y = x line, indicating that Na+ is mainly derived from halite dissolution. In contrast, surface water samples from the plain and built-up areas deviate from the y = x balance line, suggesting that their ionic compositions may be influenced by external inputs. This deviation may be related to inputs from agricultural irrigation return flow, domestic sewage, and urban runoff, indicating that human activities can affect the relative composition of Na+ and Cl− by altering solute input pathways. The relationship between (Ca2+ + Mg2+) and (HCO3− + SO42−) shows that most water samples are distributed near and above the y = x line, indicating that carbonate weathering and sulfate mineral dissolution may be important sources of major ions in the water bodies (Figure 8B). Further analysis based on the relationships of Ca2+/HCO3− (Figure 8C) and Ca2+/SO42− (Figure 8D) shows that the water samples do not fall completely along the y = x line, suggesting that the variation in major ions cannot be fully explained by the dissolution of carbonate or sulfate minerals alone. Comparatively, groundwater samples are closer to the y = x line, showing more evident water-rock interaction characteristics, whereas surface water samples are more scattered. In particular, some surface water samples from the plain region and built-up area exhibit relative enrichment of HCO3− or SO42−, suggesting that their hydrochemical compositions may be jointly influenced by multiple processes. Among these, the relative enrichment of SO42− may be related not only to evaporite or sulfate mineral dissolution, but also to the combined influence of domestic sewage, agricultural drainage, and local industrial inputs.

Cation exchange can be identified by examining the relationship between (Ca2+ + Mg2+ - HCO3− - SO42−)/(Na+ + K+ - Cl−) and the chloro-alkaline indices (CAIs).26 Most groundwater and surface water samples from the study area cluster near the y = -x line exhibiting a negative correlation, indicating that cation exchange exerts a measurable influence on water-chemistry formation (Figure 8E). The CAI is further used to determine the direction of ion exchange (Figure 8F). Negative values of CAI-I and CAI-II predominate in the two water bodies, indicating that Na+ and K+ in the water are exchanged with Ca2+ and Mg2+ on mineral and soil surfaces. In the plain region, where irrigation is intensive or groundwater runoff is sluggish, irrigation return flow and evaporation concentration can increase water salinity and further enhance cation exchange processes, thereby altering the relative proportions of Na+, Ca2+, and Mg2+.27

Moreover, the NO3−/Na+ and Cl−/Na+ relationship can be used to diagnose anthropogenic influences (Figure 8G). Most groundwater samples in the study area show a clear shift toward the anthropogenic end-member, reflecting significant impacts from human activities such as agricultural fertilization and urban wastewater discharge. In contrast, surface water samples tend to move toward the evaporite end-member, highlighting the dominant influence of evaporation processes on surface water chemistry. Specifically, agricultural fertilization and manure application can increase NO3− input, while irrigation return flow can promote the migration of soluble ions, such as NO3− and Cl−, into shallow groundwater. Domestic sewage and urban drainage may contribute ions such as Cl− and Na+. Therefore, the influence of human activities on hydrochemistry is not reflected by a single pollution input but rather by the combined effects of external solute input, infiltration/return-flow migration, salt accumulation, and coupled water-rock interaction.28,29,30

Pearson correlation analysis was used to reveal the linear relationships among hydrochemical parameters and to further infer their possible sources, similarities, and differences (Figure 9).The correlations among hydrochemical elements are jointly influenced by their sources and external environmental factors. When elements share a common origin or are subject to similar environmental processes, they tend to exhibit strong statistical correlations (p < 0.05).

(A) Surface water and (B) groundwater.

There is a significant correlation among K+, Na+, Cl−, and SO42− in surface water within the basin; similarly, a significant correlation is observed among Ca2+, Mg2+, and HCO3− in groundwater, indicating that the above ions share common sources and migration pathways within their respective water bodies. Surface water δ2H and δ18O values exhibit significant positive relationships with TDS, Na+, Cl−, and SO42−, suggesting that evaporation serves as the primary controlling process for surface water evolution. During evaporation, lighter isotopes preferentially enter the vapor phase, while the remaining water becomes progressively enriched in δ2H and δ18O, causing the isotopic composition to shift toward the heavier end. Simultaneously, evaporation reduces water volume, leading to the relative accumulation of dissolved salts, which drives the overall increase in TDS and major ion concentrations. Groundwater δ2H values are significantly and positively correlated with Ca2+ and Mg2+, indicating that the groundwater is affected by the combined influence of evaporation-driven concentration and carbonate rock weathering. If the recharge water experiences evaporation before infiltration, δ2H will be relatively enriched in heavier isotopes. After infiltration, the water reacts with carbonate minerals such as calcite and dolomite along the flow path, releasing Ca2+ and Mg2+, which increases water hardness. Therefore, in recharge paths with stronger evaporation or longer residence times, δ2H, Ca2+, and Mg2+ may increase simultaneously. This co-variation also suggests that groundwater may have different sources of recharge.

Source apportionment of surface and groundwater chemical components

The positive matrix factorization (PMF) model was employed to quantify the contributions of chemical constituents in water bodies from the Wulanmulun River Basin. The signal-to-noise ratio (S/N) values of the input variables for surface water and groundwater were generally close to 9.00, indicating that the selected indicators had relatively strong signal intensities. When three factors were selected for both surface water and groundwater, all 20 base runs of the model converged, and no obvious abnormal fluctuation was observed in the Q values, suggesting that the model had certain operational stability under different initial conditions. Approximately 89.05% and 83.14% of the residuals for surface water and groundwater, respectively, fell within the range of −3 to +3, and the residuals were generally concentrated near zero without obvious unidirectional systematic bias. This indicates that the model had acceptable reconstruction ability for the major hydrochemical compositions of the two water types.

The source apportionment of groundwater hydrochemistry is shown in Figures 10A–10C. Factor 1 is dominated by NO3−, with a contribution rate of 91.08%. Elevated NO3− concentrations in shallow groundwater are linked to the application of fertilizers and pesticides during agricultural practices.31 Expansion of cultivated land and policies promoting soil fertility have increased fertilizer application, making it an important driver of groundwater NO3− levels. Large amounts of fertilizers and pesticides infiltrate through the soil, leading to groundwater contamination.32 Thus, factor 1 is attributed to agricultural inputs. Factor 2 is dominated by Na+, Cl−, and SO42−, with respective contributions of 58.68%, 54.60%, and 62.48%. Specifically, Na+ typically originates from the weathering and dissolution of NaCl, as well as from agricultural irrigation and domestic wastewater discharge33; Cl− is often associated with industrial and domestic wastewater; SO42− may derive from the dissolution of evaporite minerals and be present in manure or industrial effluents.34 Therefore, factor 2 reflects the combined influence of anthropogenic inputs and evaporite dissolution and can be attributed to a domestic input-evaporite dissolution mixed factor. Factor 3 is dominated by Ca2+, Mg2+, K+, and HCO3−, with respective contributions of 52.33%, 58.70%, 63.52%, and 65.47%. Ca2+, Mg2+, and HCO3− are the principal products of carbonate weathering and dissolution, consistent with the dominant hydrochemical types observed within the study region. During water-rock interaction processes, silicate mineral weathering can also contribute considerable amounts of K+, Ca2+, Mg2+, and HCO3− to the groundwater system.35,36 Therefore, factor 3 represents geogenic processes, specifically water-rock interactions involving carbonate and silicate mineral weathering. The relative contributions of each factor to groundwater chemistry are as follows: geogenic processes (41.1%) > agricultural inputs (31.3%) > domestic input-evaporite dissolution mixed factor (27.6%).

The hydrochemical sources of surface water are illustrated in Figures 10D–10F. Factor 1 is dominated by Ca2+, Mg2+, and NO3−, with contribution rates of 59.98%, 41.07%, and 83.52%, respectively. In agricultural regions, while Ca2+ and Mg2+ primarily derive from natural processes, such as carbonate rock weathering, they can also be attributed to lime application and irrigation return flows. The NO3− concentration primarily reflects diffuse agricultural pollution, such as fertilizer application, manure use, and farmland runoff. Thus, factor 1 can be interpreted as an agricultural input-dominated mixed factor. NO3− mainly reflects agricultural non-point source input, whereas Ca2+ and Mg2+ are jointly influenced by agricultural activities and water-rock interactions, such as carbonate weathering. Factor 2 is characterized by K+ (43.87%), Na+ (61.43%), Cl− (83.43%), and SO42− (57.75%), suggesting strong anthropogenic influence. These ions are commonly associated with domestic sewage, industrial effluents, and irrigation return flows. Consequently, factor 2 is attributed to domestic/anthropogenic inputs. Factor 3 is dominated by HCO3− (73.55%), which is primarily released through the weathering of carbonate and silicate rocks, indicating typical geogenic water-rock interactions. Therefore, factor 3 represents geogenic processes. The relative contributions of each source to surface water chemistry are as follows: geogenic processes (36.1%) > domestic/anthropogenic inputs (34.1%) > agricultural input-dominated mixed factor (29.8%).

In summary, groundwater is primarily influenced by geological processes due to its slower flow and prolonged interaction with rocks, while surface water is more sensitive to anthropogenic inputs, such as domestic sewage and industrial effluents. Accordingly, the contributions of geological processes (36.1%) and human inputs (34.1%) to surface water are roughly comparable.

Spatiotemporal interactions between surface and groundwater

The relationship between hydrogen and oxygen isotopes is commonly applied to determine water sources and hydraulic connectivity (Figure 11). The slopes of the groundwater line (GL) and river water line (RL) are 5.84 and 5.34, respectively—both lower than those of the global meteoric water line (GMWL) and local meteoric water line (LMWL). This suggests that evaporation influences both water types within the study area, although the magnitude varies between them. Consistent results were also documented by Yue et al. for the Yellow River Basin.37 A large proportion of the surface water samples fall below the GMWL and LMWL, indicating that evaporation has led to isotopic enrichment in surface water, which has also undergone mixing with tributary and groundwater.38 The slope and intercept of the GL are lower than those of the GMWL and LMWL but higher than those of the RL. Water samples occur on both sides of the LMWL, indicating that groundwater recharge is jointly influenced by regional precipitation and surface water inputs.39,40 A subset of surface-water and groundwater samples exhibits overlapping isotopic ranges, suggesting a degree of hydraulic connectivity between the two water bodies.41

Relationships among δ2H and δ18O in precipitation, groundwater, and surface water

Based on the relationships of deuterium excess (d-excess) (Figure 12A) and local condition excess (lc-excess) (Figure 12B) with δ18O in basin waters, it can be observed that, except for groundwater in the hilly regions of the basin, d-excess and lc-excess exhibit trends opposite to that of δ18O and are negatively correlated. This suggests that evaporation affects both water types, which is generally consistent with the above results. In particular, groundwater in the plain and built-up areas shows a stronger decline in d-excess and lc-excess with increasing δ18O, indicating that evaporation has a significant influence on groundwater discharge to surface water in these zones.

Significant spatial variation in δ18O and d-excess values is observed among water bodies across different subregions in the basin (Figure 13). The δ18O of groundwater in the hilly regions is more depleted than that in the plain region, suggesting that the groundwater is more strongly influenced by precipitation recharge. During its flow toward the plain region, groundwater mixes with surface water that has been affected by evaporation, resulting in a spatial gradient in δ18O from more depleted to more enriched values (Figure 13A). Surface-water δ18O is markedly enriched in the built-up area and exhibits a clear spatial trend of increasing enrichment from northwest to southeast, reflecting hydrological processes along the flow path that are influenced by evaporation-mixing effects (Figure 13C). Xia et al. reported similar results in the urbanized Qingbaijiang River Basin, suggesting that this enrichment phenomenon was related to water source transformation, evaporation, and the expansion of urban impervious surfaces.42 Most groundwater samples display d-excess values greater than 10‰, suggesting that evaporation has a relatively minor influence on groundwater. In contrast, the majority of surface water samples have d-excess values below 10‰, indicating that surface water is strongly affected by evaporation43 (Figures 13B and 13D). Collectively, these lines of evidence point to a degree of hydraulic linkage and frequent exchange between surface water and groundwater.44

The relationships between δ18O and geographic variables were analyzed to investigate their influence on isotope composition (Figure 14). In the built-up area, δ18O values in groundwater show a positive correlation with elevation (R2 = 0.46), possibly indicating that groundwater recharge is not solely derived from high-altitude precipitation but may also be influenced by anthropogenic factors such as surface water infiltration. In contrast, groundwater in the hilly and plain regions exhibits only weak correlations with elevation. Surface water samples show minimal correlation overall, indicating that altitude has limited influence on precipitation isotopes in the study area. In high-altitude, large-scale basins, δ18O commonly shows pronounced continental and latitudinal effects.40 However, the study basin has a relatively small spatial scale and limited elevation gradient and no significant correlations were observed between δ18O and longitude or latitude, indicating that longitude and latitude exert limited control on δ18O in the study area.

Exchange between groundwater and surface water in different subregions

Hydrogen and oxygen isotope data were integrated into the Bayesian mixing model (MixSIAR) to quantify surface water-groundwater mixing, thereby providing a detailed assessment of their exchange patterns. Following the hydrogen and oxygen isotope mass balance approach, a Bayesian mixing model was applied. The model incorporated the mean and standard deviation of precipitation, surface-water, and groundwater δ2H and δ18O values to quantify the proportional contribution of each source to different water bodies (Figure 15).

Interactions and transformation relationships among water bodies across different subregions

In the hilly region of the basin, five cross-sections were selected, and recharge proportions were determined based on δ2H and δ18O measurements from groundwater, surface-water, and local precipitation samples. Groundwater discharge contributes 22.3%–44.2% to surface water, with a mean value of 32.4%, whereas atmospheric precipitation accounts for an average recharge contribution of 67.6%. In the plain and built-up areas, groundwater is persistently recharged by surface water through infiltration. Recharge proportions were quantified using δ2H and δ18O values from groundwater, surface-water, and local precipitation samples. In the plain region, seven cross-sections were selected, and the contribution of surface water to groundwater ranges from 32.4% to 37.9%, with an average of 34.2%, while the average recharge contribution from atmospheric precipitation is 65.8%. In the built-up area, two cross-sections were selected, and the contribution of surface water to groundwater ranges from 24.3% to 25.7%, with an average of 25.0%, whereas the average recharge contribution from atmospheric precipitation is 75.0%. Previous research has indicated that in highly urbanized areas, vegetation loss and decreased soil water storage capacity can modify evaporation processes45; an increase in urban land surface area is associated with a 50% increase in total river runoff, accompanied by an approximate 50% reduction in both groundwater infiltration and actual evaporation rates.46 Consequently, the contribution of surface water to groundwater in the built-up area was lower than that in the plain region. It should be noted that only two surface water-groundwater comparison profiles were established in the built-up area, and the sample representativeness remains somewhat limited. Therefore, the assessment of differences in recharge contributions in the built-up area should be regarded as a preliminary understanding based on limited samples. Future studies should establish additional sampling profiles to further verify these differences.

Main findings

By integrating isotope-based tracing methods, hydrochemical analysis, and the MixSIAR model, this research investigates interactions and transformations between surface water and groundwater in the Wulanmulun River Basin. This comprehensive approach effectively identifies the origins of various water bodies and quantitatively characterizes the exchange processes and proportions between surface and groundwater. The results yield important insights into regional hydrological processes and provide a scientific foundation for water-resource management at the basin scale. The main conclusions are as follows.

(1)The hydrochemical characteristics of groundwater and surface water are primarily controlled by a combination of rock weathering and evaporative concentration. Groundwater is mainly of the HCO3–Ca type, whereas surface water is dominated by the Cl–Na type.

Limitations of this study

This study still has some limitations. First, water samples were collected mainly from July to August 2024, and therefore, primarily reflect the hydrochemical and hydrogen-oxygen isotopic characteristics of surface water and groundwater under flood-season conditions. They cannot fully represent the hydrological cycling processes throughout the entire year. In semi-arid regions, dry-season evaporation concentration, spring freeze-thaw recharge, irrigation return-flow infiltration, and interannual precipitation variability may all affect the hydraulic connection and isotopic compositions of surface water and groundwater. Therefore, the interpretations of water transformation, recharge sources, and source apportionment in this study should be regarded as a stage-specific understanding within the sampling period. Second, the identification of human activity impacts in this study was mainly based on hydrochemical indicators, hydrogen-oxygen isotopic characteristics, and ionic ratio relationships. Quantitative data strictly matched with the sampling period, such as land-use types, irrigation intensity, coal-mining disturbance, urban drainage, groundwater levels, and river discharge, are still lacking. Therefore, this study did not independently quantify the relative contributions of different types of human activities, but instead focused on discussing their potential influence mechanisms from the perspectives of hydrochemical responses and process-based evidence. Further supplementation of these data would help distinguish the relative influences of agricultural irrigation, urban drainage, and industrial/mining activities on ions such as NO3−, Cl−, and SO42−, and further constrain the coupling relationships among external inputs, hydrodynamic disturbances, and water-rock interactions. Future studies should integrate multi-seasonal sampling, land-use dynamics, irrigation water use, groundwater levels, river discharge, and long-term meteorological data to further quantify the relative contributions of natural processes and human activities to the hydrochemical evolution of surface water and groundwater. Nevertheless, based on samples collected from July to August 2024, this study still provides effective evidence for identifying flood-season surface water-groundwater connections, evaporation effects, and potential human activity disturbances in a semi-arid basin of the Great Bend region of the Yellow River. These findings can provide a reference for subsequent multi-seasonal monitoring and basin water resources management.

Method details

Study area description

The Wulanmulun River Basin (39°19′57″N–39°51′35″N, 109°10′09″E−109°44′02″E) is situated in the northeastern Ordos Plateau and forms a key part of the “Great Bend” of the Yellow River in Inner Mongolia. The basin spans an area of around 1,952 km2 and primarily spans three administrative regions: Dongsheng, Kangbashi, and Ejin Horo Banner in Ordos City The topography of the basin is dominated by loess hills and ravines, with elevation gradually decreasing from northwest to southeast, ranging from 1,166 m to 1,575 m above sea level. The landscape features typical undulating terrain. Based on elevation, the basin is categorized into three geomorphic zones: hilly regions, plain region, and built-up area (Figure 1). The basin is characterized by a semi-arid climate, with mean annual precipitation of 396.8 mm, average annual evaporation of 1,753.8 mm, and a mean temperature of approximately 7.2°C. The hydrological regime shows strong seasonal variability, with 60%–90% of the annual precipitation concentrated from June to September. The basin is also one of the primary source areas of coarse sediment in the Yellow River.

The hydrogeological map of the study area is shown in Figure 4. The northern and eastern parts are dominated by erosional structural ridges and hilly terrain, where precipitation mostly forms surface runoff along gullies and the proportion of groundwater runoff is relatively small. The southwestern part is characterized by a gently undulating structural denudation high plain, where groundwater runoff accounts for a relatively larger proportion. Overall, surface water and groundwater in the study area converge from the surrounding areas toward the Wulanmulun River. Groundwater mainly occurs in porous–fissured aquifers. In the southeastern Jurassic coal-bearing strata, groundwater mainly occurs as fissure water; however, due to the compact lithology and poorly developed fissures, the water abundance is relatively weak. In the remaining areas, the aquifers are mainly composed of sandstones, glutenites, and pebbly sandstones of the Lower Cretaceous Zhidan Group, and groundwater mainly occurs as pore water. The alluvial deposits in the Wulanmulun River valley have relatively well-developed pores, which are favorable for precipitation infiltration and recharge. Their water abundance is mainly controlled by the thickness, particle composition, and sorting degree of the alluvial deposits. The strata in the study area are gently inclined and dip slightly westward, with no large-scale faults developed; therefore, the conditions for concentrated groundwater accumulation are relatively weak. In the northern and eastern hilly regions, groundwater is affected by gully incision and mostly discharges toward gullies along the aquifer floor.

Sample collection and measurement

Given the interactions among different water bodies across various geomorphic zones of the basin, water samples were collected from representative sites covering upstream, midstream, and downstream segments, as well as across the hilly regions, plain region, and built-up area (Figure 1). Sampling was conducted between July and August 2024, during which a total of 90 samples were obtained, comprising 25 river water, 43 groundwater, and 22 atmospheric precipitation. A total of 14 precipitation sampling sites were established, including 5 in the hilly regions, 8 in the plain region, and 1 in the built-up area. Rainwater was collected using self-made collectors during precipitation events exceeding 2 mm within a 48-h period. Groundwater samples were obtained from 37 monitoring points, the sampling depth ranged from 2.6 to 30.3 m, comprising 16 in the hilly regions, 16 in the plain region, and 5 in the built-up area. Surface water was sampled at 23 cross-sections, with 2 in the hilly regions, 12 in the plain region, and 9 in the built-up area.

Sample collection was conducted in accordance with the national standard procedure specified in the Technical Guidelines for Water Quality Sampling (HJ 494–2009). In situ measurements of pH, TDS, and other basic parameters were performed using a portable multiparameter water-quality meter. All samples were filtered through 0.22 μm membranes. Major cations were quantified by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS), whereas major anions were determined by ion chromatography. Concentrations of carbonate (CO32−) and bicarbonate (HCO3−) were measured using acid–base titration. The detection limits for potassium (K+), calcium (Ca2+), sodium (Na+), and magnesium (Mg2+) were 0.05 mg/L, 0.02 mg/L, 0.12 mg/L, and 0.003 mg/L, respectively. The detection limits for chloride (Cl−), nitrate (NO3−), and sulfate (SO42−) were 0.007 mg/L, 0.016 mg/L, and 0.018 mg/L, respectively. Analytical accuracy for cation and anion measurements was assessed using ion-balance error, and only samples with charge-balance errors < ±5% were accepted. Stable hydrogen and oxygen isotopes were measured using a liquid water isotope analyzer (LMIA-V2,DLT-100).
