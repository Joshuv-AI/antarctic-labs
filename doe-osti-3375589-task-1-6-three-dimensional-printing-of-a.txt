Task 1.6 – Three-Dimensional Printing of All-Solid-State Lithium Batteries
(Jianchao Ye, Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory)

Please update these sections as needed for FY25.

Objectives. The project has two primary objectives: (1) down select three-dimensional (3D) printing and
post-processing approaches for solid-state electrolyte (SSE) / cathode integration, and (2) understand battery
failure mechanisms via ex situ and in situ characterization.
Impact. The adoption of a thin separator layer, thick cathode structure, and metallic lithium anode will lead to
electric vehicle batteries with > 350 Wh/kg energy density for increased mileage per charge. The higher ionic
conductivity with suppression of lithium dendrite growth will allow high critical current densities for
fast-charging applications. The improved electrode/electrolyte contact will increase battery cycle life for
long-term service.
Approach. The technical approaches include advanced manufacturing based on 3D printing and related
techniques, ex situ / in situ characterizations, and battery testing. Direct-ink writing (DIW) and projection
micro stereolithography 3D-printing techniques will be employed to fabricate thin-film SSEs (< 100 m),
gradient SSEs, and 3D interfaces for battery performance evaluation. Three approaches, including sintering-
free, hybrid, and co-sintering, will be investigated. The knowledge obtained from these approaches is
transferable and complementary to each technique.
Out-Year Goals. The long-term vision of the team is to 3D-print all components of the all-solid-state lithium
battery (ASSLB) to facilitate the scale-up of ASSLB manufacturing. In this project, the team will tackle the
issues emerging from integrating solid electrolyte (SE) with electrodes. The project goal is to demonstrate a
successful 3D-printing approach to integrate SSE into electrodes and show reasonable capacity retention
(> 80%) after 300 cycles at current density ≥ 1 mA/cm2.
Collaborations. The team will work closely with a computational partner (Task 3.6 led by B. Wood) to better
understand battery failure mechanisms and design new battery architectures and chemistries for
performance improvement.

 Milestones
 1. Successful printing of a 3D porous separator for 250Wh/kg gravimetric energy density design, with
 satisfying porosity and mechanical/thermal stabilities. (Q1 FY 2025; Completed)
 2. Achieving conductivity > 10-4 S/cm @RT, good stability with Li anode and LFP or NMC cathode for
 the polymer electrolyte (Q2 FY 2025; On schedule)
 3. Successful printing of a 3D porous separator to further increase the gravimetric energy density to 300
 Wh/kg, with improved chemical/mechanical/thermal stabilities. (Q3 FY 2025; On schedule)
 4. Integrate the polymer electrolyte into the printed porous separator while maintaining > 10-4 S/cm @RT
 conductivity (Q4 FY2025; on schedule)

 Task 1.6 – Ye, LLNL

 Progress Report

3D porous membrane (3DM) designing and printing. The team designed a 3D interdigitated battery architecture
based on a cross-sectional schematic shown in Figure 1a. With a set of assumptions in the materials and porosity
of polymer electrolyte infilled cathode channels, 3D membrane separator, and anode channels, as well as the
packaging materials, the targeting specific energy density of 250 Wh/kg in a single stack pouch cell
configuration can be achieved with 200 µm cathode channel width, 50 µm separator wall thickness, 40 µm
anode channel width, and 250 µm total 3D membrane thickness (Figure 1b). The 3DMs were printed using
projection micro stereolithography (PµSL, Figure 1c). The used HDDA resin is composed of 37.5wt% HDDA,
37.5wt% TEGDME, and 25wt% PEG400, as well as small amount of photoinitiators and absorbers. The
theoretical porosity can be up to 60% but in reality, is lower due to the monomer diffusion and multiple light
exposures in the layer-by-layer printing. Figure 1d shows a 10mm x 10mm x 0.2mm 3DM printed by PµSL.
Fine adjustments of 3DM designs were conducted to achieve desired geometries. Three examples were
demonstrated in Figure 1e.

Figure 1. 3D porous membrane printing. (a) Schematic of a 3D interdigitated battery. (b) Schematic of the projection micro stereolithography
(PµSL) technology. (c) Computer design of a 3D membrane model. (d) A printed 3DM (10mm x 10mm) with designed cathode channel width
of 185 µm, separator thickness of 50 µm, and anode channel width of 55 µm. Total designed thickness is 260 µm. (e) zoomed in top-view
and cross-sectional-view images for three designs. Images were taken with 3DMs immersed in isopropanol alcohol.

For the refilling of cathode and anode, the printed 3DMs require post treatments after printing, including
cleaning, solvent exchange, and/or drying. The team evaluated the geometrical stability of 3DMs under solvent
exchange, drying, and thermal treatment. Figure 2 shows measured geometries under IPA, water, and after
drying. Compared with the designed membrane size of 10mm x 10mm, the 3DM exhibited 1.4% linear
shrinkage under IPA, and 8.6% under water. Relatively large shrinkage under water is related to the high
porosity and large contact angle between cured HDDA and water. It is important to note that no cracks were
developed during the solvent exchange from IPA to water, which allows the infilling of water-based slurries
while the sample is wet. In addition, the 3DMs survived during the drying process, with overall shrinkage of
10.3%, slightly larger than the shrinkage under DI water. The small difference in the geometry change makes
it possible to cast and dry water-based electrode slurries for full battery integration.

We employed a method to infill the 3DMs with electrode slurry, utilizing a stainless-steel (SS) mesh as a
replacement of the aluminum current collector. This modification avoided the Al corrosion issue and facilitated
uniform solvent evaporation, thereby minimizing drying-induced stress that could lead to deformities in the
membrane. The LFP-HEC slurry was first cast onto the SS mesh. While the membrane remained saturated with
water, it was positioned on top of the electrode slurry. A layer of Celgard membrane and an additional SS mesh
were placed on top to secure the assembly. The sandwiched assembly was placed inside a vacuum chamber and
pumped down for 5 minutes to evaporate the water. This method enabled the membrane to dry successfully
without deformation and cracking, eliminating the need for a complex drying step such as freeze drying or
supercritical drying. Figure 3 shows the backside and cross section of a LFP infilled 3DM with SS mesh current
collector. Note that the voids observed in the cathode channel may be due to the insufficient amount of slurry
used for casting and the evaporation of water solvent. Improvement in the refilling of cathode will be carried
out in the near future work.

To utilize lithium metal anode, one method is to melt and infiltrate Li into the anode channel. A necessary step
prior the melt infiltration is to understand the thermal stability of the porous HDDA 3DM. The air dried 3DM
was placed in an ambient-air-filled oven at testing temperatures for 1h. They were then removed and cooled
down for imaging, as shown in Figure 4. Compared with the sample size right after air drying (8.94mm x
8.92mm), there is 1.1%, 1.7%, and 2.2% shrinkage after thermal treatment at 150°C, 180°C, and 200°C,
respectively. No apparent damage was observed at temperatures near the melting point of metallic lithium
(180.5°C). The team will conduct melt infiltration of lithium into the anode channels and report the results in
the future.
