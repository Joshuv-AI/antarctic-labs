Preface
This document summarizes risks to the U.S. Department of War (DOW) mission from the
worldwide adoption of advanced energy technologies. Although advanced energy technologies
offer many benefits, they also introduce new risks that warrant mitigation. At the request of
DOW, the advanced energy technologies included in this document range from batteries/energy
storage, photovoltaics, wind, geothermal, synthetic aviation fuels, and hydrogen, to
marine/hydrokinetic power. Advanced nuclear energy technologies are discussed only in
Appendix F due to the nascent nature of the technology (i.e., lower technology readiness and
deployment levels). Risks are projected for the next 10–20 years, and mitigations are identified.
Installation and operational energy risks are addressed, but trade-offs, such as when increasing
installation energy resilience might increase operational energy risks, are not evaluated.

This report does not address the benefits of advanced energy technology adoption and therefore
does not consider net gains associated with adopting these technologies, nor does it consider the
relative risks of DOW maintaining reliance on legacy energy sources versus advanced energy
technologies. This report also does not mention mitigations that are not specifically tied to
advanced energy technology risks (e.g., energy efficiency measures). This study did not consider
classified material or classified DOW mission areas. China (hereafter referring specifically to the
government, not the people) is referenced in this document due to its predominance in advanced
energy technology markets, but similar considerations could apply to other nations.

Risks are often broken down into three elements: hazards and threats, vulnerabilities, and
consequences or impacts. Hazards and threats, often used interchangeably, refer to conditions or
events that cause damage, with hazards typically encompassing disruptive events outside human
control (e.g., severe winter storms) and threats typically involving human-caused incidents (e.g.,
cyberattacks). Vulnerabilities are weaknesses in infrastructure, systems, or processes that
increase the likelihood and severity of failure when exposed to hazards or threats, which can
damage, disrupt, or destroy assets. Impacts can include national security consequences as well as
economic and other costs affecting multiple stakeholders (Anderson et al. 2019).

Because hazards and vulnerabilities often intersect and are sometimes difficult to isolate
(Applegate et al. 2025), this report focuses on risk drivers and provides a qualitative mapping
and explanation of the key types of interrelated vulnerabilities affecting DOW’s mission either
introduced or exacerbated by the global adoption of advanced energy technologies, highlighting
interconnections and nested consequences. Risk drivers are categorized by those originating in
(1) energy infrastructure itself; (2) the adoption and integration of advanced energy technologies;
and (3) actions of allies, partners, or adversaries. Some of these risks overlap, and mitigation
strategies can sometimes simultaneously reduce more than one risk.

This report does not address certain concepts outlined in the 2023 U.S. Department of Defense
Operational Energy Strategy that are not unique to the adoption of advanced energy
technologies. For example, energy demand reduction and the recommendation for enterprise-
wide visibility on operational energy supply and demand are not unique to the type of energy
generation technology deployed. Such considerations are outside the scope of this analysis.

For a description of the methodology used for this report, please see Appendix J.

Executive Summary
The adoption of advanced energy technologies is happening to varying degrees around the world
and has the potential to enhance energy security and resilience; however, these technologies can
introduce new vulnerabilities even as they mitigate existing ones. This dichotomy highlights that
both action and inaction carry strategic risks in a contested and logistically complex operating
environment. Regardless of U.S. civilian or military adoption of such technologies, key allies
and adversaries are on adoption paths that will impact the U.S. military at the tactical,
operational, and strategic level. The global adoption of advanced energy technologies presents
the potential for both positive and negative consequences for U.S. Department of War (DOW)
(also referred to as the U.S. Department of Defense) missions in the next 10–20 years.
Throughout its history, DOW has successfully navigated global shifts in technology: from
mechanization and aviation to space, cyber, and unmanned systems. Each technological
revolution introduced new risks, and advanced energy technologies are no different. Energy
experts note that energy is a critical weapon of war, serving as a key power projection capability
in modern defense strategies (Yergin 1991). In future conflicts, the ability to secure and manage
energy resources may determine outcomes related to victory.

Although many of these risks are routinely managed by public power providers and other
stakeholders under normal conditions, such mitigation strategies might be much less reliable in a
contested environment. The complexities of modern energy systems can become liabilities
during conflicts, where durability, simplicity, and resilience are more critical. Relying too
heavily on systems that perform well under stable conditions could foster a false sense of
security and obscure system vulnerabilities under duress. DOW adoption of advanced energy
technologies will require modernizing logistics and acquisitions as well as careful budget
planning for initial investment costs. See Table A-1 in Appendix A for an overview of how the
risk drivers identified in this report map to potential DOW vulnerabilities and consequences from
the adoption of advanced energy assets worldwide.

Three categories of risk drivers are presented in this analysis: infrastructure-related, adoption-
related, and response-related. Each risk type can generate consequences for DOW, including
power disruptions, suboptimal DOW mission performance and operational effectiveness, higher
costs and shortages for both legacy and advanced energy technologies, and loss of U.S. influence
and strategic deterrent value. Specific impacts could include tactical impacts, operational
impacts, and strategic impacts, as summarized in the following.

Tactical Impacts
 • From infrastructure-related risk drivers: Power disruptions due to local grid failures
 or undersea cable sabotage could hinder mission-critical operations, delay tactical-level
 missions, or compromise readiness in contested environments.
 • From adoption-related risk drivers: Inconsistent, premature, or delayed adoption of
 energy technologies across units could reduce energy reliability, leading to uneven power
 supply and potential mission delays, particularly if less effective or unvetted technologies
 are used during high-demand operational periods.

 vii

 • From response-related risk drivers: Targeted physical or cyberattacks on energy
 infrastructure could disrupt energy access during combat or crisis scenarios, delaying
 mission-critical operations and affecting tactical decision-making and readiness.

Operational Impacts
 • From infrastructure-related risk drivers: The increasing integration of decentralized
 energy resources could complicate grid management across DOW facilities. During high-
 demand periods (e.g., operational surges), vulnerabilities introduced by a proliferation of
 nongovernmental power producers or power supplies dependent on weather conditions
 could force reliance on backup systems, potentially undermining sustained operations.
 • From adoption-related risk drivers: Advanced technologies such as batteries and solar
 power introduce new logistical challenges, including transportation, storage, and safety
 procedures. These requirements could reduce responsiveness, especially in extreme
 environments or during fast-paced deployments.
 • From response-related risk drivers: China’s dominance in energy technology markets
 and its involvement in foreign energy infrastructure could limit U.S. allies or DOW
 assets’ access to critical materials, components, or energy generation technologies,
 complicating operational planning and sustainment.

Strategic Impacts
 • From infrastructure-related risk drivers: Increased DOW dependence on local grids,
 both at overseas installations and within the contiguous United States, could result in
 power disruptions if local grid infrastructure is either inadequate for demand growth or
 compromised by adversaries. Disruptions in the form of power outages, degraded quality
 of power supplied, or insufficient generation could, in turn, undermine DOW’s ability to
 project power globally, particularly during surge operations or crises. The military’s
 reliance on civilian energy infrastructure makes public-private coordination and
 resilience planning increasingly essential.
 • From adoption-related risk drivers: Managing both legacy systems and newly adopted
 energy technologies could stretch DOW resources and budgets, potentially complicating
 the seamless integration of energy systems across the force and hindering energy
 resilience and readiness at scale.
 • From response-related risk drivers: China’s control over certain advanced energy
 technology markets and supply chains could undermine U.S. leadership in international
 energy governance and security cooperation. This could erode diplomatic leverage,
 access, and alliance reliability, degrading overall deterrent value.

Mitigation Strategies
To reduce these risks, DOW can build upon current mitigation efforts. Interviewees advocated
for considering both installation and operational energy when designing mitigation strategies to
help develop cross-domain solutions as energy technologies evolve.

Energy resource and technology planning: Use artificial intelligence to manage generation
resources, including planning, permitting, operations, reliability, resilience, and smart grids.
Install bidirectional charging equipment so that EVs can provide backup power to facilities.

 viii

Cybersecurity: Incorporate cyber-informed engineering into acquisitions. Expand cyber
exercises and penetration testing for energy technologies set for integration into DOW
installations. Perform cybersecurity risk assessments on distributed energy resources. Use virtual
environments to test vulnerabilities with partners and explore mitigation strategies. Eliminate
external control board physical access points, encrypt data and network communications, use
code signatures for firmware updates, and incorporate Transport Layer Security (TLS).

Supply chain resilience: Use integrated supply chain models that consider competition with the
private sector and other nations to identify key vulnerabilities and improve stockpiling plans.
Collaborate with stakeholders to manage supply chain risks. Employ long-term contracts to
encourage onshoring or friendshoring of mining, refining, manufacturing, or material recapture.
Work with industry to improve software and firmware supply chain transparency.

Workforce development: Create a formal talent pipeline for workers skilled in advanced energy
technologies. Expand interdisciplinary training for information technology and operational
technology professionals. Ensure workforce development plans cater to mission and technology-
specific needs, such as deployability.

Exercises and simulations: Expand black-start exercises to encompass more interdependent
entities and longer outage durations. Expand use of exercises to account for compounding natural
hazards and cyberthreats to create granular impact predictions and inform contingency planning.

Investments in commercialized technology: Leverage proven technologies, including
geothermal systems, hybrid tactical vehicles, solar plus storage, and microgrids, working with
commercial providers to adapt them to defense energy needs.

Operational testing and pilot programs: Broadly share lessons learned from pilot programs
and prototype testing across services.

Research into emerging technologies: Dedicate funding to encourage faster advancement of
promising and potentially game-changing emerging technologies (e.g. optical/laser and radio
frequency power beaming, quiet generator alternatives, and point-of-need fuel production).

Partnerships and working groups: Establish partnerships and working groups to leverage
domestic and foreign expertise on emerging technologies with military applications and to build
capacity and improve interoperability in key regions, such as the Indo-Pacific.

Common standards: Establish common energy technology standards (domestically and with
partners) to promote interoperability, safety, and smoother adoption.

Budget planning: Integrate the adoption of advanced energy systems into budget planning,
using cost-saving approaches such as energy savings performance contracts (ESPCs).

Repurposing infrastructure: Reuse legacy assets to reduce capital investment.
Decommissioned missile silos and similar infrastructure can be converted into energy storage
facilities using technologies such as gravity batteries, hydrogen, or compressed air, extending
their utility and supporting distributed resilience goals.

1 Expanding Energy Generation Technologies
The U.S. Department of War (DOW) shares foundational interdependencies with civilian
industries such as the aviation, shipping, and automotive sectors that are adopting advanced
energy technologies 1 for reasons ranging from increasing energy resilience and reducing long-
term costs to pressure from shareholders, customers, and legislative requirements. This civilian
shift to advanced energy technologies will impact DOW missions in several ways in the next 10–
20 years. DOW, well known as the largest single energy consumer in the U.S. federal
government (U.S. CRS 2019), will most likely be competing with the civilian sector for energy
supplies.

Moreover, the demand for energy, both in military and non-military sectors, is projected to
increase in the next 25 years (EIA 2023). U.S. electricity demand is estimated to increase by
almost 16% by 2029 (Wilson, Zimmerman, and Gramlich 2024) and 78% by 2050 from 2023
levels (Batra et al. 2025), in part due to the rapid increase in demand driven by artificial
intelligence (AI) and data centers. For example, a U.S. Department of Energy (DOE)-funded
analysis projects a compound annual growth rate in data center electricity consumption of 13%
to 27% between 2023 and 2028 (Shehabi et al. 2024). Military energy use has increased from
approximately 1 gallon of fuel per soldier per day during World War II, to approximately 4
gallons during the 1990–1991 Persian Gulf War, to 16 gallons by 2006 in Iraq and Afghanistan
(Crowley et al. 2007), and 22 gallons in 2011 (ASA IE&E 2011). Future demand is expected to
grow as DOW adopts energy-intensive technologies such as AI, employs more dispersed
operational strategies, and develops directed energy weapons (Jafari et al. 2024; Simulcik,
Villalobos, and Bazilian 2025). Meeting this surging demand while ensuring energy security and
resilience will likely depend on the timely expansion and deployment of energy generation and
storage technologies. Without proactive efforts, both military and civilian sectors risk facing
energy shortfalls.

Like any large-scale change, the adoption of advanced energy technologies introduces new risks.
Many of these risks are mitigated during normal operations but could become more challenging
during conflicts or large-scale combat operations. Mapping interconnected risks and identifying
mitigation strategies should help position DOW to reduce these risks and chart a path forward.

2 Drivers of Infrastructure Risk
As countries around the world adopt advanced energy technologies to increase energy production
and enhance resilience, adopters must consider risks to new infrastructure. Risks to and from
electricity infrastructure have always existed, and advanced energy technologies can amplify
some vulnerabilities and introduce novel risks.

2.1 Expanded Electrification
The increasing electrification of assets from vehicles to building heating systems is happening
around the globe at varying rates, potentially exacerbating existing vulnerabilities, such as a
dependence on local grids, including in overseas locations. The electrification of energy systems
is also occurring in tandem with infrastructure digitalization, creating vulnerabilities from
cyberattacks and electromagnetic pulses. The electrification and digitization of the grid also
intensify existing risks tied to the reliance on Global Positioning System (GPS) for precise time
synchronization for grid management. These unencrypted, publicly accessible civil signals are
susceptible to jamming and spoofing. Without proper mitigation, these threats have the potential
to render large portions of interconnected energy infrastructure inoperable, leaving DOW
equipment and installations reliant solely on backup power not dependent on electrified
infrastructure and potentially creating significant resupply and operational continuity challenges.
Moreover, damage to local transmission infrastructure (e.g., weather-induced or human-caused)
carries higher consequences with electrification and can disrupt power deliveries to U.S. forces
regardless of mutual use or local agreements regarding priority service. For example, winter
storm Uri knocked out power across the southern United States in 2021, leaving some military
installations without power for several days and others realizing how poorly prepared they were
to secure enough diesel fuel to keep backup generators running (U.S. Congress 2022).

Outside of the continental United States, reliance on external power grids can cause more acute
vulnerabilities, as local grids in host nations might not be configured to handle the increased load
associated with a surge in U.S. troop presence. Such demand-side considerations for DOW are
already present in non-conflict conditions, including in Guam. The U.S. Navy, which accounts
for approximately 20% of Guam’s utility power sales, voluntarily switches to standby generators
during periods of peak demand to help prevent power outages (Scarpitti and Voss 2024; Pacific
Daily News 2024).

While such challenges can often be managed during peacetime through coordination and
advanced planning, the situation could be more precarious in a contested environment, when
adversaries may target local energy infrastructure, logistical routes may be disrupted, and host-
nation priorities may shift, all of which can magnify the operational risks associated with
electrification. Additionally, the increased digitization of energy systems is lowering the barrier
for adversaries to operate in operational technology (OT) environments that were once the
domain of specialized engineers, a trend compounded by the use of AI-driven adversarial
techniques that further reduce the expertise required to disrupt military energy systems.

With many warfighting missions originating from within the continental United States, U.S.
domestic power grid reliability is directly linked to the ability to project power. Infrastructure
component shortages, increasing load demand, and inadequate transmission infrastructure might
pose a risk to the U.S. power grid’s ability to support national security objectives. For example:

 2

 • Critical components such as large power transformers (essential for efficiently delivering
 power over long distances, converting voltage levels, and ensuring reliable and safe grid
 operation) are in short supply in the United States, leaving the grid vulnerable when
 transformers are damaged by intentional attacks or environmental factors. Many
 transformers, particularly distribution transformers, have already exceeded their design
 life expectancy (McKenna et al. 2024). Transformer supply lead times, especially those
 of large power transformers, have nearly tripled in the past several years, increasing from
 50 weeks in 2021 to an average of 120 weeks in 2024 (Chopra and Boucher 2024).
 Further, the United States is heavily reliant on foreign-manufactured large power
 transformers, with more than 80% of demand being met by imports (DOE EERE 2022).
 • Increasing electricity demand, driven by factors such as the proliferation of data centers,
 is straining the grid’s capacity. Data center load growth has tripled in the past decade and
 is projected to again double or triple by 2028, potentially overtaking DOW electricity
 demand and diminishing DOW’s influence on grid requirements (Shehabi et al. 2024).

 • Studies project that intraregional transmission needs will increase 20% by 2035 to
 accommodate moderate load growth and more with high load growth. Current plans
 might not ensure enough generation and transmission infrastructure to meet demand
 (DOE 2023). Regulatory challenges, environmental concerns, and local opposition have
 hindered transmission build-out that would support load growth (McLaughlin 2025).
 Moreover, U.S. grid operators cite complexity and a lack of software vendors as
 impediments to upgrading transmission lines and carrying capacity, potentially delaying
 improvements by years (McLaughlin 2025).

Dependence on local utility grids accentuates a vulnerability associated with nonuniformity
across utilities in critical mission prioritization, in part because countries, states, and localities
have distinct energy policies, regulatory frameworks, and market dynamics that influence how
power providers allocate resources. For example, states with more aggressive advanced energy
adoption mandates may focus more on transitioning away from legacy energy sources, whereas
others might prioritize traditional power generation methods to support critical infrastructure
(REN21 2023). Additionally, most advanced economies’ utility distribution systems (including
in the United States) use aging infrastructure that will require significant improvement for larger
energy demands (IEA 2023). The reserve capacity for potential military surge needs could differ
across utilities and regions as well. This patchwork of policies and priorities can lead to
inconsistencies in how critical missions are supported across different regions, impeding DOW’s
ability to anticipate risks and ensure energy resilience. Moreover, in the United States, some
distribution providers and cooperatives are not required to follow Federal Energy Regulatory
Commission (FERC) Critical Infrastructure Protection (CIP) standards for cybersecurity, so
cybersecurity investments compete against other investments that support reliability, enhance
performance, or lower consumer rates. Partnering with local utilities at home and abroad could
help clarify these entities’ challenges and improve cybersecurity.

Electrification further blurs the traditional divide between installation energy and operational
energy. Maintaining strict separation between the two can create artificial silos that hinder
integrated energy strategies. For instance, a microgrid on a military base could potentially serve

2.2 Control System Communication Requirements
Advanced energy systems depend on reliable and regular communications between digital
control systems and distributed energy assets to balance load, maintain voltage, and manage peak
load. These digital systems and communications from a rapidly increasing number of distributed
energy generation assets expose energy systems to new cyber threats, which could shut down
power provision or create uneven power supply to DOW installations. This type of vulnerability
allows adversaries to target energy infrastructure remotely, complicating attribution and lowering
the threshold for disruptive action. In addition, asset owners and operators (e.g., utilities,
individual “prosumers,” and private energy generation companies) vary in their cybersecurity
maturity, and nonuniform cybersecurity by these stakeholders could allow cyberattacks to disrupt
grid reliability. Effective cybersecurity measures can be cost-prohibitive for some installations,
and there are few established standards or incentives for stakeholders to prioritize cybersecurity
over other investments, such as reliability or performance. Insufficient information sharing on
cyber threats, vulnerabilities, and mitigation strategies remains a challenge (DOE EERE 2020).
Certain adversaries and state-sponsored groups exhibit patterns of cyber-targeting critical
advanced energy supply chains and infrastructure, and cyber incidents targeting digital
components and communications of all energy systems are expected to increase in both
sophistication and frequency (DOJ 2024; DOE EERE 2020). These risks could be intensified by
reliance on products from companies headquartered in adversarial countries and further
amplified by the involvement of multiple parties from construction to decommissioning,
complicating efforts to maintain effective cybersecurity practices throughout the supply chain
and system life cycle (DOE EERE 2020).

Moreover, the management systems used to maximize the benefits of advanced energy
technologies require advanced monitoring and control. For example, management systems for
offshore wind plants rely on satellite communications for remote monitoring and control,
creating vulnerabilities to cyberattacks. The U.S. government assessed that Russia launched
cyberattacks on commercial satellite communication networks as part of Russia’s invasion of
Ukraine on Feb. 24, 2022 (Blinken 2022). This attack disrupted a major German energy
company’s access to more than 5,800 wind turbines (CyberPeace Institute 2022).

The integration of distributed energy resources (DERs) also involves the collection and
transmission of data related to energy usage and generation, creating potential privacy concerns
for DOW. Adversaries could exploit these data to assess vulnerabilities or predict energy
consumption patterns of defense-critical infrastructure. Quantum computing advancements
anticipated in the next two decades could magnify these cyber vulnerabilities and add uncertainty
around legacy cybersecurity best practices, although research on post-quantum cybersecurity
protocols is underway (KPMG Global Organization 2025; Torkington 2024).

Requiring high procurement cybersecurity standards, testing the cybersecurity of energy assets,
and maintaining non-digitized backup generation for critical loads would help mitigate this risk.

2.3 Footprint and Detectability
Energy infrastructure has long been a target during conflicts, and adopting advanced energy
technology will not shift this paradigm. Although DERs have advantages when it comes to point-
of-need generation and resilience, other characteristics can make them more vulnerable to
detection and targeting, particularly in contested environments. For example, the larger physical
footprint of distributed systems makes them more easily discovered, observed, or identified by
adversaries, potentially limiting their applications in sensitive or high-risk areas.

Undersea cables bring offshore energy production to consumers and help balance supply and
demand across regional power grids. Damage to undersea cables (whether from accidents or
intentional interference) is not uncommon and can be repaired in a matter of weeks or months in
peacetime (Leadvent Group 2025). An average downtime of 40 days is estimated for intra-array
cables and 60 days for export cables (Arikan 2025). Although undersea cables used to transmit
electricity are typically located within 50 kilometers from shore (closer than communication
cables [McCoy et al. 2024]), they remain vulnerable to sabotage or accidental damage, especially
during conflicts, as they are frequently uncrewed and relatively accessible.

Especially overseas, DOW might rely on power coming from offshore wind resources reliant on
such cables. For example, in Germany, offshore wind energy accounted for 5.9% of the
country’s total electricity generation in 2024. With expansion targets set by German law,
predictions indicate that offshore wind generation could more than double by 2035 (Deutsche
WindGuard 2025). Offshore wind power has expanded off the coasts of the United Kingdom,
Germany, Vietnam, and China, and wind plants in the Atlantic serve U.S. power markets.

DOW may want to consider obfuscation/camouflage and plan for a certain share of DERs to be
inoperable during a conflict.

2.4 Variability of Some Advanced Energy Assets
The integration of some advanced energy technologies introduces variability in power
generation, requiring energy storage to meet demand during low generation. Although
distributed generation increases resilience, it has necessitated more complex planning systems
for electric utilities. Photovoltaic (PV) solar generation variability is evident over a 24-hour
period, as solar output fluctuates with the time of day and weather conditions (Saxena et al.
2024). Prolonged overcast conditions or unexpected PV system failures can result in periods
when backup from the power grid or energy storage assets is needed to meet electricity demand.
While these challenges can typically be managed during peacetime through careful planning and
reserves, the situation becomes more critical in a contested or wartime scenario. In such
environments, the unpredictability of energy generation could be exacerbated by disruptions to
supply chains, cyberattacks, or intentional damage to infrastructure. Naturally inconsistent and
unpredictable wind patterns make wind energy especially uncertain (Saxena et al. 2024),
complicating fixed generation planning and raising concerns about system reliability (Saxena et
al. 2024).

2.5 Distributed Nature of Advanced Energy Assets
The increasing role of DERs, such as those operated by private solar power plant owners and
other nontraditional power producers, makes grid management more challenging (Rickerson,

 5

Gillis, and Bulkeley 2024). Although DERs can enhance the resilience of defense-critical
infrastructure by decentralizing power generation, the proliferation of non-utility-owned devices
(including by homeowners) has made electric sector reliability increasingly dependent on assets
sourced from companies with links to potentially hostile foreign entities. Moreover, the growing
number of interconnected devices (and the increasing presence of digital, often unregulated
connections) introduces new vectors for cyber intrusion, especially when those connections are
poorly secured or lack consistent oversight. The energy sector is accelerating the convergence of
OT and information technology (IT), allowing adversaries to apply well-established IT attack
strategies to infiltrate protected OT networks. Cyberattacks could degrade power quality and
provision to DOW installations, disrupting mission readiness and forcing reliance on limited-
duration backup systems. Beyond direct military bases, such attacks could also target the
Defense Industrial Base, cutting off power to critical nodes, such as munitions plants, aircraft
manufacturers, and key petroleum-based fuel pipelines. Interruptions to these assets would not
only slow production and resupply but could cascade across logistics and sustainment operations,
undermining the broader defense posture. DOW advocacy for cybersecurity minimums for local
grid providers could improve resistance to cyberattacks.

DERs are often connected through inverters. If these devices are not properly managed or
controlled, they can create issues with power factor correction, harmonics, and phase imbalances.
These factors could degrade the quality of the power supplied to defense infrastructure and could
require sophisticated grid management solutions to maintain reliability. DER integration into the
grid requires more advanced management systems to efficiently balance supply and demand
(Horowitz et al. 2019), but many utility providers are not yet fully equipped to integrate DERs in
a way that optimally supports critical national security and defense missions, particularly during
emergencies when grid stability is most crucial. The rapid proliferation of DERs can outpace
planned grid reinforcement, creating a trade-off between enabling local energy resilience and the
technical strain on underprepared utility infrastructure. Working with local power providers is
key to transparency for DOW.

2.6 Radar Disruption
Wind turbines’ large size and rotating blades reflect electromagnetic waves, creating interference
that reduces radar sensitivity and hinders its ability to accurately track targets (DOE n.d.;
WTRIMWG 2025). Radar systems can detect fast-moving blades as objects, complicating the
identification and tracking of airborne targets and disrupting critical weather forecasting
(WTRIMWG 2025). Radars can be tuned to reduce the detection of “noise” caused by wind
turbines, but the false positives that wind turbines create could complicate DOW’s detection of
small, low-flying drones and increase the likelihood of missed or misidentified targets (DOE
2024). The potential for maritime radar interference will increase as offshore wind plants
proliferate (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine 2022). In 2024, Sweden
canceled plans for 13 offshore wind plants in the Baltic Sea, in part citing concerns that the
turbines’ interference with sensors and radars would hinder the detection of submarines and
potential adversary attacks (Bryant 2024). Working with foreign partners and frequent updates of
methodologies to filter out such interference can mitigate this risk.

3 Drivers of Adoption Risk
Drivers of adoption-related risks occur due to the adoption of advanced energy technologies.

3.1 Breadth of Available Technologies
The sheer volume of available technologies and the uncertainty surrounding global adoption and
infrastructure build-out present challenges for DOW to prioritize investments and select
technologies for different applications and environments, increasing the complexity of defense
acquisition processes and possibly leading to the adoption of subpar technologies. With different
units adopting different solutions for tactical, operational, and strategic energy needs, DOW
might struggle to streamline the vast number of potential energy solutions. Spreading capital
investment too thin could divert resources away from improving or maintaining existing energy
infrastructure, complicating strategic decision-making in energy resource allocation (Simulcik,
Villalobos, and Bazilian 2025). Additionally, without an explicit plan for deployment to areas
with appropriate infrastructure, the acquisitions system risks acquiring legacy assets with
extended lifespans that might become increasingly difficult or costly to maintain. For example,
in 2024, DOW acquired approximately 17,600 internal combustion engine vehicles and 2,900
electric vehicles (EVs) (U.S. GSA 2024), and these vehicles will require ongoing maintenance
and parts throughout their lifespans. In 2023, the average federally owned military vehicle was
12.4 years old (U.S. GSA 2024).

An integrated DOW cross-service approach to working with government laboratories and
commercial developers to determine which emerging technologies best suit DOW needs should
improve clarity on technology choices.

3.2 New Logistics Requirements
Advanced energy technologies present risks and uncertainties related to weight, size, safety, and
ease of assembly and disassembly. Advanced energy systems have varied sets of logistics
demands, and the military adoption of these technologies will require updating protocols,
including servicing, storage, transportation, and disposal (Lutton 2024). DOW could face higher
costs and implementation delays while updating regulations and possibly safety or performance
shortfalls if new technologies are adopted before logistics norms are fully adjusted.

Expanding battery adoption requires planning for transportation, charging, lifetimes, safety, and
end-of-life management. New, end-of-life, and damaged, defective, or recalled batteries require
distinct handling procedures, with end-of-life and damaged batteries requiring more stringent
handling due to their potential hazards, such as leaking harmful electrolyte or fire risks from
thermal runaway events. Heavier batteries require extra measures to prevent thermal events
during transportation (Khabur 2025). For military applications, batteries might need to remain in
storage for extended periods and still be ready for immediate deployment when needed, and
safety risks associated with such storage need to be considered. Moreover, DOW batteries are
sometimes used in more extreme environments than commercial applications, reducing battery
lifetimes and introducing uncertainty into safety protocols. Current battery technology remains
relatively heavy compared to conventional diesel fuel, and EVs with lithium-ion (Li-ion)
batteries tend to be 30% heavier than gas-powered vehicles, requiring adjustments in heavy-lift
plans for deployment (Center for Transportation Research n.d.; NLR 2024b).

 7

Li-ion batteries require different safety and fire-suppression protocols than petroleum fuels.
While gasoline is a flammable liquid, Li-ion batteries, which are labeled as Class 9 hazardous
materials by the U.S. Department of Transportation, pose other safety risks such as chemical
leakage or even explosions under certain conditions (FAA 2016). Gasoline tank fires can be
extinguished with foam-based systems, but Li-ion fires need significant amounts of water to
prevent thermal runaway (U.S. Antarctic Program 2022). If thermal runaway occurs,
extinguishing the resulting fire with common ABC fire extinguishers would be difficult and
could release toxic gases (NLR 2023b; Thomas Edison Electric 2025). Li-ion batteries are
generally safer when transported at a lower state of charge, and the International Civil Aviation
Organization mandates that Li-ion batteries be transported at or below a 30% state of charge
(PRBA 2015), suggesting that DOW will need to determine the acceptable risk of a potential
flare-up event versus the risk of arriving in a combat zone with a lower state of charge.

Hydrogen has a relatively high energy density by mass that could make it suitable for heavy-duty
vehicles, ships, and aircraft, and such applications are being explored. Storing hydrogen requires
much larger volumes than legacy fuels to achieve comparable energy output (Bhuiyan and
Siddique 2025). Hydrogen is stored, compressed, and dispensed at high pressures, so
infrastructure components such as compression, storage, and purification systems must be
integrated (DOE HFTO 2025a). Hydrogen fuels also have different hazard properties than
petroleum fuels (e.g., a greater flammability range and faster combustion), which impact siting
and storage (Hydrogen Tools 2025). Hydrogen’s high flammability poses a risk of accidental
fires or explosions due to its low ignition energy and propensity to leak, although safer hydrogen
storage technologies are being developed to mitigate these risks (Balcerzak, Lau, and Heubner
2024; Bhuiyan and Siddique 2025). In a military setting, the site selection for storage must
consider proximity to munitions, other fuel storage areas, housing, and training facilities.

Allowing sufficient time and budget for full operational testing and broad intra-DOW sharing of
lessons learned would mitigate potential safety risks.

3.3 Changing Workforce Needs
The integration and maintenance of expanded energy technologies necessitate specialized
advanced training for DOW employees or contractors responsible for managing these systems.
Frequent reassignment of active-duty personnel often results in staff exposure to a wide range of
systems, fostering generalists rather than specialists. This can limit the development of deep
expertise in any one area, potentially impairing the proper operations and maintenance of
advanced energy technologies and leading to unforeseen costs and potential equipment
breakdowns. The increased variety of advanced energy technologies compounds this problem,
potentially creating gaps in institutional knowledge and slowing the effective deployment and
maintenance of expanded energy technologies across installations. Changes in defense contracts
create challenges for retaining skilled workers within defense supply chains, and once these
workers leave, they become difficult and expensive to rehire and retrain or replace (DOD 2022).

Competition for skilled talent related to advanced energy technologies is expected to intensify in
the coming years, suggesting worker shortages and increased labor costs for DOW energy needs.
For example, in the United States, there is a projected shortage of 1 million electricians by 2030.
An underdeveloped solar training pipeline and the absence of recognized apprenticeship
programs has left 97% of PV companies struggling to find qualified workers, and labor costs

 8

increased 25% in 2023, with further labor market tightening expected as the PV market expands
(Huckstep and Dempster 2024). Access to experienced cybersecurity professionals with energy
expertise to defend advanced energy systems could pose the greatest challenge, and the DOD
Cyber Workforce Strategy 2023–2027 flagged the shortage of skilled professionals as a
continuing risk to operational readiness and national security. In 2024, there were 500,000
unfilled vacancies for experienced cybersecurity personnel in the United States, while DOW
reported a shortfall of 28,000 military and civilian cyber professionals (House Committee on
Homeland Security 2024; Pomerleau 2024).

A holistic workforce acquisition and development plan for DOW energy systems could mitigate
this risk.

3.4 Performance Under Extreme Conditions
Just as diesel fuel can gel in cold temperatures, the performance of some advanced energy
technologies can degrade under different weather conditions (e.g., batteries in extreme cold and
PV panels in extreme heat), which can impact DOW costs, planning, and readiness. Moreover,
having different systems that perform variably across different environments complicates
logistics, as it may require additional spare parts, specialized maintenance knowledge, or
redundant systems to ensure reliability. This increases the logistical burden on deployed forces,
reduces standardization across units, and can strain sustainment operations in contested or
resource-limited environments. DOW missions often require operations in harsher conditions
than the commercial sector, so its energy technologies must reliably perform under extreme
temperatures and withstand shock and vibration (FCAB 2021). While the Army’s Cold Regions
Research and Engineering Laboratory tests some technologies, one interviewee specializing in
Arctic issues noted that DOW typically tests technologies in moderate climates, leaving greater
uncertainty around choosing advanced energy technologies for extreme environments. For
example, extreme cold temperatures can impair battery performance and lifetime (Walsh et al.
2024). Studies have shown that battery EV range can drop by more than 40% in typical cold
conditions (around 20°F), while internal combustion engine vehicles experience an approximate
10% range drop in the cold (Walsh et al. 2024). Li-ion batteries with carbonaceous anodes, the
dominant type used in vehicles, are generally known for their poor performance at sub-zero (oC)
temperatures, including the inability to be charged quickly. In hot climates (such as in Arizona
and coastal/saltwater environments), Li-ion batteries used in EVs and stationary storage
experience accelerated degradation, leading to more frequent replacements (Pesaran,
Santhanagopalan, and Kim 2013). Further, certain components of solar PV systems can be less
durable when exposed to extreme heat, humidity, or hail; and solar panels in regions prone to
heatwaves can experience efficiency declines (Masterson 2022).

Operational testing in extreme physical conditions and allowing for higher rates of inoperability
in operational plans would help mitigate this risk.

3.5 Power Density
Some advanced energy technologies have lower power densities than legacy alternatives and
require more surface area or volume to produce an equivalent amount of power (Van Zalk and
Behrens 2018). The lower power density of certain advanced energy technologies may require
larger physical footprints to meet operational energy demands, potentially limiting their

 9

suitability for forward-deployed, mobile, or space-constrained installations. This could hinder
DOW’s ability to rapidly deploy or sustain forces in contested or austere environments where
space, concealment, and mobility are critical. For example, power density is a key shortcoming
of Li-ion batteries, which provide significantly less energy per unit of weight and volume than
conventional fuels (Shaffer 2024). A typical battery that is the same size as a gallon of gas (0.134
cubic feet) can store only 15.3% of the gallon of gas’s energy when used for transportation
(AFDC 2024). This will require more frequent vehicle downtime for charging (as compared to
previous refueling intervals) and changes to transportation plans to account for the added weight
of the energy systems needed by forward-deployed units. Investing in research and development
to improve power density would mitigate this risk over the longer term.

3.6 Nascent Infrastructure and Capacity
Like any new technology, advanced energy infrastructure and manufacturing capacity are
expanding along with adoption. Infrastructure and supply development for some advanced
energy technologies is still inadequate for forecasted demand, creating insufficient infrastructure,
escalating costs, and increasing supply chain disruption risks for the large-scale DOW
commitment to these technologies. Examples include the following:

 • Hydrogen fuel is not yet widely adopted in the commercial sector, so finding refueling or
 maintenance parts during deployment would be challenging. The small U.S. hydrogen
 market and underdeveloped customer support may dampen adoption despite hydrogen’s
 technical promise.
 • Although there is significant overcapacity in Li-ion battery cell manufacturing at the
 global scale, U.S. capacity is still emerging. According to projections by Argonne
 National Laboratory, U.S. Li-ion battery cell manufacturing capacity will reach 210
 gigawatt hours (GWh) in 2025, supplying roughly 30–50% of North American demand.
 Domestic manufacturing capacity for battery cell components (e.g., anodes, cathodes,
 electrolyte, foils, and separators) is even more nascent, with insufficient planned North
 American component production to fully supply announced battery cell production
 (Gohlke et al. 2024). DOW could face escalating costs and supply chain disruptions,
 impacting the mission.
 • The domestic production capacity of synthetic aviation fuel (SAF) (also known as
 sustainable aviation fuel) does not currently meet domestic demand (Rosales Calderon et
 al. 2024a, 2024b) or potential military demand. Although the domestic SAF supply chain
 is growing, unclear capacity growth contributes to market uncertainty (Rosales Calderon
 et al. 2024a, 2024b). DOW could face fuel shortages if SAF becomes a requirement. For
 more details on SAF, see Appendix G.

3.7 Evolving Standards
Gaps in standards are inherent risks in any major technological transition, and the shift to
advanced energy systems is no exception, creating potential safety, security, and cost risks for
DOW. If they are not addressed, these gaps could lead to incidents (e.g., fires or cyber
intrusions) that degrade mission readiness and have even more severe consequences during
conflict. Moreover, unless military logistics considerations are integrated into standards early on,
manufacturers might produce advanced energy technologies that are unrealistic for use during
deployments, limiting resilience and energy security benefits.

 10

Without timely, consistent standards, advanced technologies like hydrogen fuel systems and
interconnected energy networks such as microgrids could introduce vulnerabilities before
appropriate safeguards are in place. UFC 3-520-05 outlines the technical requirements for the use
of Li-ion-based batteries that are stored or charged inside facilities, but it does not address
requirements for smaller Li-ion batteries (e.g., in phones and vehicles). Current standards from
the Society of Automotive Engineers, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), and the
U.S. Department of Transportation have been established for commercial or consumer
applications for some types of batteries, but safety standards are lacking for battery chemistries
that are in development or that are not yet widely commercialized, such as solid-state batteries
(Beers et al. 2024). Even as standards are established, there can be delays in adoption (Fire and
Life Safety Policy Institute 2018), and some stakeholders might adopt bespoke standards or
interfaces that are not widely recognized (Sanders and Holderness 2021). The California Public
Utilities Commission proposed new standards for the operations and maintenance of battery
storage facilities (CPUC 2025) only after improper storage and fire suppression was linked to a
consequential facility fire (Pehling 2025). Frequent standards updates through collaboration with
civilian partners and broad DOW compliance campaigns could counter this risk.

Recent events, such as the 2020 SolarWinds Orion supply chain compromise (CISA 2021), have
highlighted inadequate standards for supply chain visibility, which increase uncertainty. The
absence of standardized commercial practices for digital bills of materials and software bills of
materials hampers DOW’s ability to effectively track suppliers and mitigate risks across critical
supply chains (Defense Business Board 2025). Moreover, DOW’s governance structure and
supply chain risk management processes face significant limitations in enforcing effective data
management and integration, undermining DOW’s ability to achieve comprehensive supply
chain visibility (Defense Business Board 2025). Working with vendors to improve transparency
and increasing rigor in supply chain management would mitigate this risk.

3.8 Shrinking Legacy Fuel Markets
Although advanced energy technologies are projected to act as a stabilizing factor in the global
energy market writ large (Su et al. 2025a), their adoption is projected to introduce volatility in
fossil fuels markets (IRENA 2024). The International Energy Agency (IEA) predicts a
significant decline in global oil demand, dropping from around 100 million barrels per day to 77
million barrels per day, with a 75% reduction forecast by 2050 (IEA 2024c). But industry experts
project a more moderate decline of 10–20% by 2040 (Fraser 2025). If the military’s share of the
legacy fuel markets grows, such volatility could become increasingly impactful, exacerbating
cost swings and the uncertainty of DOW’s ability to find supplies in all deployment locations.
More robust stockpiles and long-term contracts could help insulate DOW from price swings in
the market.

3.9 Gradual Adoption
In the next 20 years, the global adoption of advanced energy technologies might vary greatly
across geography and time, requiring the simultaneous use of both legacy and newer energy
technologies. Some legacy assets might still be required to ensure power capacity and reliability
in areas where advanced fuel supplies are not yet readily available or where environmental
factors such as extreme temperatures reduce the performance of some advanced energy
technologies. DOW will likely see higher direct costs from maintaining or upgrading legacy

 11

infrastructure while expanding advanced energy assets, including replacement parts and fueling
infrastructure. The initial expense of a resilient energy system can slow the adoption timeline,
with procurement categorized into time-phased pieces or structured by funding types (e.g.,
financed and appropriated portions), which introduces complexities and delays into deployment
timelines. These segmented approaches can make system integration more difficult.

Ensuring the parallel functionality of legacy and new systems will likely require hybrid solutions
to bridge gaps, especially in remote or harsh environments where new energy supplies might be
variable (e.g., during winter in polar regions where EV battery performance is less optimal or
where solar energy is insufficient). Hybrid arrangements will still require new support systems,
including EV charging infrastructure, cybersecurity support, and battery disposal (Tegler 2025).
Beyond the added cost of maintaining multiple supply lines, these systems introduce greater
maintenance complexity. Support personnel will need expertise not only in internal combustion
engines, but also in electronics and energy storage that may require additional training and
resources. Delays in adjusting DOW plans for bridging gaps could result in power shortfalls.

If the adoption of advanced energy technologies in some operational theaters moves more
quickly than DOW’s standard timelines for equipment lifespans, legacy equipment and
infrastructure could become stranded assets that become economically unviable before their
anticipated lifespan is over due to changes in the market, technology, or regulations. For
example, heavy machinery and handling equipment staged at military airfields worldwide have a
useful life of 5,000 to 12,000 hours depending on the equipment type and maintenance
(Louisiana CAT 2023), and internal combustion engine vehicles are likely to be functional for
decades, according to an interviewee. Maintaining battlefield strength would then incur higher-
than-anticipated costs, and contingency planning for such cases will be crucial for DOW to
prepare for potential budgetary needs. Moreover, stranded assets could become unexpected costs
or liabilities for DOW. Depending on their location, DOW may be responsible for proper
disposal. Additionally, as seen with U.S. military equipment left behind in Afghanistan and Iraq,
adversaries could repurpose even nonlethal assets for hostile or disruptive purposes.

4 Drivers of Response Risk
Drivers of response risk are actions by adversarial and partner countries, firms, and individuals,
potentially increasing costs or introducing vulnerabilities to DOW energy assets and operations.
More broadly, adversarial actions can undermine U.S. leadership and challenge its strategic
partnerships. Some single actions can lead to a variety of negative consequences, while multiple
actions can compound a given consequence.

4.1 Adversary Activity in the Commercial Market
China’s proactive research and commercial competition in advanced energy markets and supply
chains, especially in the Li-ion battery, inverter, and PV markets, have resulted in a dominant
global market position for China-based firms, bringing risks for DOW in the following areas.

4.1.1 Supply Chains
The United States, and therefore DOW, is dependent on Chinese-based firms for advanced
energy supplies including critical materials, components, and products, introducing longer lead
times for acquisition and maintenance, cost uncertainty, and vulnerabilities to disruption due to
conflicts, extreme weather, or trade factors. Moreover, China’s dominant market influence
impacts the availability and cost of innovative technologies for DOW. China currently dominates
the midstream supply chains (i.e., from material refining to component production) for both
crystalline silicon solar PV and Li-ion batteries. In 2023, more than 90% of global polysilicon
production for PV and 99% of the spherical graphite used in most Li-ion anodes was
manufactured in China (Solar Media Limited/PV-Tech Research 2025; IEA 2024b). China is
also home to the world’s top 10 suppliers of PV manufacturing equipment (IEA 2024c).
Appendix H provides additional details on China’s dominance in the Li-ion battery supply chain.
Additionally, Chinese companies have employed tactics to limit U.S. efforts to build a
competitively viable domestic mining and production sector. For example, when the United
States aimed to tap into its domestic lithium reserves, Chinese refiners, which control roughly
two-thirds of global lithium processing, drove prices down by around 80% using strategies such
as dumping and overproduction. The U.S. Defense Industrial Base is not a sufficiently large
consumer of critical minerals to sustain an independent, parallel supply chain outside of China
(Select Committee on the Strategic Competition Between the United States and the Chinese
Communist Party 2025). Alternative suppliers are few, and production in U.S. partner countries
is often directed locally before exporting to the United States (Dollar et al. 2023). Working with
other stakeholders to invest in domestic production, from mining to components, would help
counter this risk.

Beyond component dependence, mission owners must also grapple with cybersecurity risks
embedded in a complex supply chain ecosystem, including nondefense, third-party-owned
devices, where blurred responsibility for incident response complicates risk management and
mission assurance. For instance, it is difficult to determine who is responsible for cybersecurity
incident response if nation-state adversaries compromise residential rooftop solar inverters,
which may be aggregated by a third-party overseas company providing grid stability services
through a power purchase agreement with a local utility. Improving cybersecurity in DOW and
power provider networks could mitigate this risk.

 13

The increase in the global adoption of technologies such as solar, wind, and batteries has further
spiked the demand for shipping, and the limited U.S. share (around 1%) of the world’s
shipbuilding industry puts the United States at a strategic disadvantage, subtly boosting China’s
influence at a grand strategy level (Brooks 2025). Advocating for support to the U.S.
shipbuilding industry could help address this risk.

4.1.2 Access To Foreign Energy Grids
China’s leadership in advanced energy development and manufacturing has enabled it to access
foreign energy grids by supplying low-cost equipment and infrastructure. This creates potential
cyber and influence risks for DOW operations that rely on local power systems while also
providing a publicly accepted justification for Chinese presence near U.S. activities that may
conceal covert intentions. Since 2013, Chinese companies have completed 369 overseas power
projects through the Belt and Road Initiative, totaling 156 GW of capacity (Thomton et al. 2025).
The State Grid Corporation of China has secured stakes in regional power grids, such as 40%
ownership of the National Grid Corporation of the Philippines (Morales, Jerome, and Lopez
2025).

At DOW’s overseas military bases across 55 countries and territories (Nicastro and Tilghman
2024), the risks associated with relying on civilian infrastructure are compounded by limited
U.S. authority and control over energy technology components and infrastructure. Third-party
providers could introduce vulnerabilities, particularly in regions where foreign influence can
extend to critical military infrastructure. As industry leaders and low-cost bidders, Chinese firms
are increasingly winning solar energy contracts across Pacific Island nations (Xinhua 2024). In
Europe, Chinese firms are likely to gain greater access to Germany’s power grid if proposed
legislation to improve solar energy integration is passed, creating new opportunities for Chinese
solar inverter manufacturers (Wehrmann 2025). Moreover, many wind turbines in Germany use
Chinese-made parts, raising concerns about potential foreign influence on this technology
(Wehrmann 2025). This pervasive access for monitoring, control, and software management
further exacerbates the cyber risk arising from electrification because energy assets, whether
manufactured domestically or in friendly nations, pose a cyber risk if they contain embedded
electronics sourced from potential adversaries or require maintenance directly from original
equipment manufacturers. DOW microgrids with high cybersecurity standards, point-of-need
generation, and inside-the-fence backup power would help mitigate this risk.

4.1.3 Access Near DOW Facilities and Operations
Having successfully taken the commercial lead in advanced energy technology manufacturing
and development, China-based companies are often a top choice of U.S. firms and communities
looking to develop advanced energy inputs or infrastructure, allowing China to expand its
presence in U.S. territory, possibly near DOW facilities or operations, and creating the potential
for Chinese surveillance. For instance, in 2024, Chinese PV manufacturer JinkoSolar donated
solar modules to Tribes in Montana and South Dakota (Fischer 2025)—states that host
intercontinental ballistic missile fields and one of the U.S. Air Force’s two B-1B Lancer wings.
In addition, an anticipated shortage of offshore wind turbine installation vessels by the late 2020s
(WindEurope 2025c) could create an opportunity for China to gain access to strategically
important regions worldwide. As of 2022, China operated 84 wind turbine installation vessels,
accounting for roughly 45% of the global fleet of wind turbine installation vessels and 90% of

 14

those under construction (Webster 2024). Working with local authorities and international
partners will be key to preventing surveillance opportunities.

4.1.4 U.S. Influence
More generally, adversaries could leverage their contributions to advanced energy technologies
and manufacturing to undermine U.S. influence, potentially degrading DOW interests, such as in
area/port access, basing, and overflight. By promoting itself as a leader in energy manufacturing,
adoption, and research and development, China could challenge U.S. leadership in the energy
technology space (Russell 2024; Geall and Ely 2018). For example, China’s status as a leader in
advanced energy technologies could give China more influence in international forums and
bilateral engagements, such as at a United Nations-organized meeting in April 2025 when
Chinese President Xi Jinping and European Commission President von der Leyen joined other
global leaders (absent U.S. representation) in reaffirming international commitment to climate
action (Millan, Ainger, and Iglesias 2025). U.S. engagement would need to offer these countries
more than Chinese energy investment does, and DOW may want to work with other U.S. federal
partners to determine tailored mitigation opportunities by country. Contingency planning and
deepening partnerships with foreign partners could also help mitigate this risk.

4.2 Competition for Innovative Technologies
Research and development into advanced energy technologies continues around the world, and
adversaries could gain the upper hand on the battlefield and secure a strategic lead for years to
come if they are the first to deploy new technologies at scale, especially potentially disruptive
technologies, such as power beaming, point-of-need marine and hydrokinetic energy (i.e., a
power source that is harnessed from the natural movement of water, including waves, tides, and
river and ocean currents), solid-state batteries, point-of-need hydrogen production, and small
modular (i.e., nuclear) reactors. A historical parallel can be seen in the early 20th-century shift
from coal to oil-powered naval fleets, which gave nations like the United Kingdom and the
United States a decisive operational advantage over coal-dependent rivals (Yergin 1991).
China’s leadership in research and development and in translating energy innovations to
commercialization, in part due to its control over supply chains and access to critical minerals
and its established and interconnected manufacturing infrastructure, positions the country as the
United States’ primary competition for innovative energy technologies. For example, China
plans to develop radio frequency power-beaming capabilities in space to capture and transmit
solar energy from orbiting PV stations to the ground, unlocking power that is less variable than
ground-based solar (Tong 2025).

China also has several ocean energy installations along its coast, despite the limited coastal
energy available for exploitation, and wave power installations are currently undergoing sea
trials (Qing 2023). The South China Sea, particularly the waters surrounding Taiwan, holds
significant tidal current energy potential (Wan, Feng, and Zhang 2023). If China is first to deploy
marine and hydrokinetic energy at scale, this advancement could strengthen the country’s control
over critical maritime areas, such as the South China Sea (and subsequently its control over ports
and supply routes), enhance its surveillance capabilities, and potentially disrupt the activities of
other nations. Front-runner status in distributed large-scale tidal power assets could translate to
advantages in maritime areas with high tidal and wave capacity outside the Indo-Pacific, such as
the Persian Gulf, the Black Sea, the Gulf of Oman, the Bay of Biscay, and the French Atlantic.

 15

DOW investments in promising emerging technologies will be key to maintaining a competitive
position on the battlefield.

4.3 Faster Adoption Abroad
Broader adoption of advanced energy technologies abroad could hamper the availability of
DOW-compatible legacy fuel infrastructure (e.g., gas stations being widely replaced by EV
charging stations) or capacity (e.g., aviation fuel tank reserves being replaced by 100% biofuel
reserves) during deployments in friendly or hostile destinations. One study estimates that the
number of gas stations in Europe will decrease by 45% by 2050 (Consultancy.eu 2024). Some
interviewees suggested that a reduction in petroleum-based refueling infrastructure across
Europe could impede a land force’s ability to rapidly respond to conflict. Adjusting acquisitions
to regional conditions or increasing owned energy provisions could reduce this risk.

Faster adoption could also result in the establishment of infrastructure or standards that DOW
must adapt to, rather than hold a leading role in choosing the best fit for DOW purposes, and
U.S. military concerns, such as cyber or other vulnerabilities, might not be prioritized. For
example, the rapid development of offshore wind plants could lead to unforeseen radar
interference affecting military or civilian maritime operations nearby (National Academies of
Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine 2022), despite permitting processes intended to prevent
such impacts. Moreover, the rapid proliferation of offshore wind plants or solar installations in
key geopolitical regions could lead to unintended security risks if critical technologies are
sourced from countries with competing interests to the United States or those that have less
rigorous cybersecurity standards.

In the European Union, DOW must accommodate the Alternative Fuels Infrastructure Regulation
for standardizing charging infrastructure within member states (European Commission n.d.-a.).
This regulation designates the Type 2 (Mennekes) connector as the standard charging connector
for Europe, whereas the U.S. commonly uses the J1772 (Type 1) connector, which works with
most non-Tesla EVs and Tesla vehicles using an adapter (Besen Group 2024). New U.S. Tesla
models are compatible with Type 2 connectors (Ndungu 2024), but most existing U.S. EVs
equipped with the J1772 plug would require adapters for European deployments (Ellmore 2024).

Participating in international standards working groups may help integrate DOW mission
priorities into decision-making.

4.4 Adversarial Exploitation of Distributed Infrastructure
Adversaries might exploit advanced energy technologies (such as solar PV, wind farms, or
space-based systems) to camouflage or reduce the detectability of their own sensitive military
assets (including weapons, drones, and communication equipment). The increasing prevalence of
large-scale solar and wind installations, particularly in remote or contested regions, presents
opportunities for adversaries to hide critical infrastructure or surveillance equipment. For
example, solar PV could be used to conceal weaponry, making them more challenging to detect
through conventional intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance methods. In addition, on-site
generation assets like solar PV or wind can mask power demand signatures that would otherwise
be visible on the broader grid, complicating efforts to identify unusual consumption patterns that
might indicate covert operations. Similarly, the vast expanses of wind plants, often located in

 16

areas with low civilian populations, could serve as effective covers for missile launchers or
command and control facilities, leveraging the cluttered radar signatures generated by the
turbines. Offshore wind assets could conceal sensors, creating a more reliable monitoring
network for adversaries. Innovative updates to U.S. detection capabilities should help counter
this risk.

4.5 Increased Global Demand and Competition for Resources
Individuals and government entities across the globe are adopting advanced energy technologies,
increasing demand for advanced energy inputs. The U.S. Energy Information Administration
(EIA) estimates that within the next two decades, global wind energy generation will double or
triple, and solar energy generation will increase to two to four times their 2024 levels (EIA
2023). Some policies abroad (e.g., the European Union’s Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism
(CBAM), which imposes carbon price requirements on imports of key goods) contribute to the
surge in demand for advanced energy technologies (Burgess 2024).

Without cost-saving technological change, competition for advanced energy assets will increase
market prices for all consumers, including DOW, possibly leading to supply shortages for
consumers who are less able or willing to pay higher prices. Such competitive pressures are
likely to impact entire supply chains, from critical material mining to finished products. For
example, expected increases in deep-sea mining due to increasing critical mineral demand,
driven in part by advanced energy technology proliferation, will likely impact U.S. Navy
operations and maritime norms. To help offset these pressures, DOW can invest in research and
development of cost-effective, resource-efficient technologies that reduce dependence on scarce
or strategically contested materials.

Associated supply shortages and rising prices will impact DOW even more for those materials
that are also critical to other defense applications. For instance, antimony is used to improve the
efficiency of perovskite solar cells (Guo et al. 2023), and it also plays a critical role in military
applications such as flame-retardant materials, armor-piercing ammunition, and explosives (New
Age Metals Inc. 2025). Working with partners to increase the domestic production of key
materials and components could be key to maintaining supply, although higher costs are harder
to mitigate.

5 Uncertainties
Several uncertainties could unfold within the 20-year time horizon used for this report that could
amplify or reduce the risks outlined. Risks that are not evident today could become more
apparent as the adoption of advanced energy technologies accelerates and as more nations rely
on these assets for both civilian and defense energy needs.

5.1 Technological Innovations
Some technological innovations could facilitate the deployability of advanced energy
technologies, creating opportunities and risks. For example, radio frequency or optical power
beaming and the potential to move bulk power generation to space could enable DOW operations
worldwide but also shift DOW’s strategic focus toward defending space-based and aerial
systems. The United Kingdom is considering uncrewed space assets for power delivery, which
could alter the future landscape of military operations (U.K. Ministry of Defence 2023). The
adoption of terrestrial or space-based power beaming would likely benefit militaries with
relatively more electrified assets.

Market-driven innovations will likely continue to reduce the cost and amount of materials
required for advanced energy systems. For example, innovative batteries and motors for EVs are
being developed to avoid rare earth materials, reduce size and weight, and increase power
density. Although this example aligns with DOW goals, innovation driven predominantly by
commercial rather than military needs and interests could result in solutions not fully suitable for
military applications. The gaps might not be clear without testing, failures, and modifications.

5.2 Changes in Supply Chain
Efforts to secure increasing supplies of advanced energy inputs could have other impacts. For
example, deep-sea mining for lithium and other critical minerals could impact the broader
security environment and therefore DOW priorities. Supply chain uncertainty also stems from
the complex interactions between advanced energy supply chains and technological
developments. For example, increasing demand for n-type solar cells has prompted a higher
demand for silver due to these cells’ typically higher silver content (Zuboy et al. 2023). In other
instances, material availability directly influences technological trends. For example, National
Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) experts note that the amount of cobalt in nickel-manganese
cobalt-oxide Li-ion battery cathodes has significantly dropped during the past decade in part to
help reduce cobalt-related supply chain issues.

5.3 Other Unknowns
New technologies often introduce unforeseen vulnerabilities that become apparent only after
widespread adoption. For example, only after the widespread implementation of nuclear power
did risks become clear: early assumptions held that technological advancements would enable
safe storage or reprocessing of nuclear waste, but finding acceptable long-term storage solutions
proved more complex than anticipated. Additionally, the risks of long-term health impacts from
radiation exposure became more apparent after the 1986 Chernobyl and the 2011 Fukushima
disasters. On a smaller scale, after the early adoption of energy-efficient light-emitting diode
(LED) lighting, the U.S. Air Force issued a temporary moratorium on purchasing LED lighting
until reliability issues were resolved, at which time the moratorium was lifted.

6 Risk Mitigation Strategies
A number of mitigation options could help reduce vulnerabilities or potential consequences
associated with the identified risk drivers. DOW is already pursuing many of these mitigations,
but additional measures would enable DOW to increase resilience during times of conflict. The
following mitigations could be integrated across DOW, prioritized within strategic initiatives,
and informed by lessons learned from current DOW efforts. Where feasible, these efforts could
be replicated and incorporated into the planning and priorities of DOW leadership.

6.1 Energy Resource and Technology Planning
Although DERs can enhance grid reliability and resilience, the shift toward advanced energy
technologies for installation-based energy requires additional engineering planning to manage
daily resource supply. Traditional methods of long-term and short-term energy planning as well
as real-time operations must evolve to address uncertainties surrounding resource availability
and variability. The introduction of both variable resources and alternative generation resources
increases system complexities, largely controlled by software. AI might be one tool to better
manage generation resources. For example, DOE has identified opportunities for AI to inform
grid management, including planning, permitting, operations, reliability, and resilience (Benes,
Porterfield, and Yang 2024). AI support might involve power grid models, permitting assistance,
and smart grids. Installing cyber-secure bidirectional charging equipment so that EVs can
provide backup power to facilities would enhance resilience. For mission-based mobile energy
technology planning and more advanced capabilities that are still under development, strategies
to select suitable options and manage technologies could include custom cost-benefit analyses to
identify trade-offs. For example, a cost-benefit analysis could show whether the lighter weight of
lithium-sulfur batteries, which are not yet widely available, are worth the initial added cost for
deployment in unmanned aerial vehicles.

6.2 Cybersecurity
Cyber defense can mitigate risks across the life cycle of digital energy technologies, from
procurement, to maintenance, and end of life. For example, implementing cyber-aware practices
in procurement processes (e.g., requiring a software bill of materials) can improve supply chain
transparency. Employing cyber best practices for all systems would include (1) using a virtual
private network for remote connections, (2) employing Transport Layer Security (TLS) for
internal networks to encrypt communications between critical assets, (3) deploying a certificate
authority for message authentication and secure communications between site equipment, and (4)
integrating intrusion detection and protection tools for better alerts on potential threats (Sanghvi
et. al. 2021). These measures could collectively improve the security posture of DER systems,
reducing the likelihood and impact of cyber threats to DOW’s energy infrastructure.

Developing common cyber standards is essential for integrating advanced energy technologies
into defense operations while safeguarding against adversarial influence on supply chains. For
instance, adhering to the National Institute of Standards and Technology’s Special Publication
800 series and the General Services Administration’s as well as Naval Facilities Engineering
Systems Command’s EV supply equipment guidance when acquiring electrified systems, such as
EVs or electric unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs), helps mitigate cyber vulnerabilities.
Implementing a unified cybersecurity framework early in the development process and

 19

throughout the charging infrastructure can bolster DOW’s operational system resilience, and
standardization helps ensure that the infrastructure remains defensible against potential
cyberattacks (Markel and Sanghvi 2022).

FERC, in partnership with the North American Electric Reliability Corporation, is also working
to enhance supply chain security standards for the power grid, particularly focusing on
vulnerabilities in low-impact bulk electric power cyber systems and vendor remote access
(FERC 2024; FERC 2025). This work provides a potential model for DOW to consider when
coordinating with external utilities or adapting relevant standards for mission-critical systems.

Additional mitigations for enhancing cyber resilience in energy systems could include:

 • Performing cybersecurity risk assessments on DERs: for example, NLR’s Distributed
 Energy Resource Cybersecurity Framework (DER-CF) tool evaluates the cybersecurity
 posture of federal sites that employ distributed energy systems or plan to implement
 DERs for daily operations. The DER-CF helps organizations and federal sites harden
 their systems against risks by evaluating DER cybersecurity postures and providing
 customized recommendations for improvement (NLR 2023a)
 • Collaborating with the newly formed Energy Threat Analysis Center, a DOE-led public-
 private partnership that convenes experts from the federal government and the U.S.
 energy sector, to share information regarding threats to energy systems (DOE CESER
 n.d.)
 • Using virtual environments, such as the NLR Advanced Research on Integrated Energy
 Systems (ARIES) Cyber Range, for high-fidelity, at-scale models of a more integrated
 and interconnected grid to test vulnerabilities, assess shared risks, and explore mitigation
 strategies in realistic, simulated conditions
 • Eliminating external control board physical access points from EV supply equipment,
 encrypting data at rest and network communications, using code signatures for firmware
 updates, and incorporating TLS (Hodge et al. 2019)
 • Performing penetration testing for emerging energy technologies set for integration into
 DOW installations (NLR 2024c)
 • Incorporating cyber-informed engineering as a requirement for acquisitions, both within
 traditional acquisition frameworks and through Other Transaction Authorities for cutting-
 edge technologies (OUSD A&S 2023).

6.3 Supply Chain Resilience
Ensuring upstream, midstream, and downstream supply will be key to any DOW energy supply
chain resilience effort. The 2022 revelation that a subcontractor for the F-35 program had
sourced cobalt (for use in the aircraft’s pump magnets) from China (Clark 2022) highlights how
a lack of supply chain visibility can permeate DOW programs. Expanding supply chain
resilience could be achieved by:

 • Conducting reviews on key military equipment and assets to determine where supply
 chain transparency is lacking, from raw materials to transport (Helton 2021)
 • Using integrated supply chain models that consider competition with the private sector
 and other nations to focus on key vulnerabilities and improve stockpiling plans

 20

 • Collaborating with stakeholders and utilities to proactively manage supply chain risks
 • Employing long-term contracts to encourage onshoring or friendshoring of mining,
 refining, manufacturing, or recycling
 • Working with industry to improve software and firmware supply chain transparency.

6.4 Workforce Development
More structured approaches to hiring and skills development could help DOW deploy and
maintain advanced energy technologies. Simply hiring more contractors would likely be
insufficient, given that some missions, such as cyber, require experts who are able to deploy.
Efforts could include:

 • Creating a formal talent pipeline, including structured recruitment and training programs
 in collaboration with educational institutions, ensuring a steady flow of skilled workers
 • Expanding interdisciplinary training for IT and OT professionals, including cross-
 disciplinary education and certifications
 • Adaptable workforce development plans that cater to mission and technology-specific
 needs (President’s National Infrastructure Advisory Council 2021). For example, to
 mitigate the risk of losing institutional cyber knowledge due to personnel turnover,
 identify cyber talent requirements that demand deployment-ready members, and those
 that are better suited for contracted personnel.

6.5 Exercises and Simulations
Expanding the scope and depth of DOW’s simulations and exercises could help address the risks
outlined throughout this analysis. By simulating the behavior of advanced energy technologies
under stress (i.e., considering weather extremes, different physical environments, interoperability
challenges, and logistics requirements), DOW can identify vulnerabilities and refine energy
resilience strategies. These exercises also help assess the readiness of personnel and systems,
ensuring that the military can maintain operations in diverse, high-pressure scenarios.
Congressionally mandated energy resilience readiness exercises (e.g., black-start exercises)
could be expanded in duration (currently limited to 8 hours or less, which does not account for
factors such as shift changes, changing mission-essential functions, or the nonlinear value of lost
load), could involve more non-direct mission-related entities (such as childcare centers and base
housing), and could include off-base communities that support mission-critical military
personnel in line with the Office of the Undersecretary of War for Research and Engineering
energy resilience recommendations (DOD 2024).

Additionally, power-hardware-in-the-loop testing and modeling systems allow for the physical
replication of extreme grid disruptions such as power loss, frequency manipulation, and other
anomalies that could arise from natural hazards or cyberattacks. These systems provide a realistic
testing environment for simulating how advanced energy grids might behave under stress,
allowing DOW to evaluate vulnerabilities that could impact military readiness. Moreover,
simulations could model how outages affect military personnel, assets, and operations, as well as
civilian infrastructure and stakeholders in theater. Finally, simulations can explore various
restoration scenarios to understand the trade-offs between different energy restoration
approaches.

 21

Simulations must consider traditional threats (such as adversary capabilities and logistical
challenges) in concert with cyberattacks and factors such as DOW hardware capabilities, backup
power sources, and the complex interdependencies among these components. Simulating the
consequences of compounding natural hazards and cyberattacks could help DOW understand
how grid failures could occur in tandem with other catastrophic events, potentially exacerbating
their impacts. The resulting granular predictions could allow military planners to make informed
energy resilience decisions. For example, a simulation might predict the number of military
sorties impacted by power outages in a specific geographic region, thereby offering vital insights
into operational impacts and the need for contingency plans.

6.6 Investments in Commercialized Technology
Investments in existing advanced energy technologies could expand energy production, improve
resilience, and reveal the best options for military applications. Expanded microgrid investments
could enhance resilience and reduce dependence on external energy infrastructure, particularly in
outage-prone or remote locations. To enable microgrid advantages, DOW could update its
backup power requirements to specify the use of parallel-capable diesel generators (i.e., those
equipped with controls and switchgear that enable integration and power sharing within
microgrids). Any backup energy system that depends on fuel deliveries should also consider
what constitutes an optimal level of on-site fuel storage, since emergency conditions may disrupt
resupply. In contrast, energy sources such as geothermal, solar, wind, and advanced nuclear
power could offer more resilient alternatives in contested or logistically constrained
environments.

Preparing to invest in promising technologies as they are commercialized would provide the
advantages of increasing DOW’s energy security while minimizing the risks of technology
failure during the development phase. Potential funding sources for such investments include the
Office of Strategic Capital, the Defense Innovation Unit, the Strategic Capabilities Office, the
Army Futures Command, and the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA),
among others. Appendix E provides a list of promising new technologies, and Appendix I details
some related NLR resources. Hydrogen and small modular (nuclear) reactors have strong
potential for point-of-need generation capabilities.

Enhanced geothermal systems (EGS) are less variable than PV and wind resources while also
reducing footprints and detectability. EGS use hydraulic fracturing approaches developed for oil
and gas to create reservoirs, expanding possible development beyond existing natural geothermal
conditions (NLR 2025d). Although EGS systems have high up-front costs, costs are decreasing
and are expected to follow a trend similar to costs for PV technologies (Kah and Kleinberg
2025). Technological advancements and reductions in project costs could enable geothermal
energy to meet up to 15% of global electricity demand growth by 2050 (IEA 2024a). On a
smaller scale, the installation of geothermal heat pumps to heat individual buildings, combined
with building envelope improvements, could reduce the need for additional generation capacity,
energy storage, and transmission infrastructure compared to other energy solutions (DOE 2023).

6.7 Operational Testing and Pilot Programs
While laboratory research provides increasing capabilities to simulate environmental conditions,
DOW field-testing support and participation could achieve measurable performance indicators of

 22

advanced technologies in challenging environments, such as the Arctic. Field-testing of
advanced energy technologies in various terrains and simulated contexts (e.g., contested
environments, extreme temperature environments, and austere conditions necessitating
infrequent system maintenance) can provide valuable insights into performance and reliability.
Battery technologies, in particular, benefit from testing across a range of conditions for lifetime,
charging speed, and the ability to hold an idle charge. Broad sharing of pilot program lessons
learned could inform future decision-making.

6.8 Research Into Emerging Technologies
For technologies that are far from commercialization, backing research into advanced energy
technologies that address DOW’s specific power, mobility, and security needs could speed
progress to the commercialization phase. For example, one interviewee cited quiet alternatives to
diesel generators as critical in field operations. Point-of-need generation capabilities, too, are an
important area for further research, to avoid some constraints on fuel transportation, reduce risks
related to response times, and ensure power for mobile operations. For example, the Defense
Innovation Unit is supporting research for SAF (DIU 2023a) and hydrogen (DIU 2024).

Research into advanced battery chemistries and designs could expand mobility and power
capabilities. Lighter-weight and higher-energy-density batteries, such as lithium-sulfur (Li-S) or
solid-state lithium batteries with lithium anodes, could improve capabilities for UAVs, high-
altitude pseudo-satellites, autonomous underwater vehicles, and heavy road vehicles (Dorfler et
al. 2020). Researchers are continuing to improve the capacity, performance, and cost-
competitiveness of these advanced battery options, with commercialization expected by 2030 for
Li-S, sodium-ion batteries, and silicon and silicon-carbon anodes, according to NLR experts.

DOW might want to partner with others to fund research of mutual interest. For example,
because tidal power commercialization is expected to take at least another decade due to high
costs (Cart 2023), DOW might partner with allied leaders in this area, including the United
Kingdom, France, Canada, Australia, Portugal, and South Korea. South Korea’s 254-megawatt
(MW) facility is the largest to date.

To counter the risks related to cyber and kinetic attacks and natural disasters, more research
could be developed to address energy infrastructure vulnerabilities supporting critical missions
or geographies. One existing effort at NLR identifies tools for informing cybersecurity solutions
for emerging energy technologies and evolving threats. Continued growth in this research area
could yield mitigations that are applicable in the defense space as DOW technology adoption
evolves (DOE CESER 2024).

Actions to mitigate the risks of adversaries leapfrogging U.S. advancements in technologies that
are critical to DOW operations include implementing robust intellectual property protection
strategies where possible, ensuring secure development practices, applying stringent
cybersecurity measures, and facilitating international cooperation to safeguard proprietary
technologies from theft or unauthorized access.

6.9 Partnerships and Working Groups
DOW can mitigate some risks by expanding relationships with international partners, local
utilities, and communities surrounding DOW installations. DOW could also leverage working
groups. For example, DOW could participate in the Alaska Hydrogen Working Group, which is
an informal group of stakeholders who are interested in following developments related to
hydrogen technologies and in providing inputs related to the proposed use and future deployment
in the state of Alaska (UAF n.d.). DOW’s involvement could raise certain DOW-specific
hydrogen energy needs to broaden commercial hydrogen development efforts.

Expanding international partnerships beyond interoperability to the development of new
technologies could help keep DOW, allies, and partners ahead of adversaries. For example,
DOW might want to partner with the United Kingdom, France, Canada, and Portugal to explore
defense applications of wave and tidal energy advancements. In 2023, the global installed
capacity of tidal energy was 600 MW; leading projects are located in the United Kingdom,
France, and Canada. China and India are also starting pilot tidal projects.

Additionally, multiple interviewees pointed to the strategic advantage in building and
maintaining relationships with other nations, including through building infrastructure, to
increase the likelihood of access to ports and materials and to influence energy infrastructure
development in those countries, which is a strategy China has successfully used in countries such
as the Philippines. Stronger partnerships with local utilities could better integrate DOW energy
needs and resilience into regional generation reserves and interregional transfers, which could
enhance access to capacity from less impacted regions during disruptions (Monken et al. 2024).
In addition, DOW can adopt a more proactive stance in working with electricity industry partners
to expedite and prioritize transmission investments, such as hardening, redundancy, and capacity
expansion (Monken et al. 2024). New strategies, including funding mechanisms, might be
needed to incorporate DOW energy needs into regulatory and planning processes at the state,
regional, and federal levels while aligning DOW energy procurement with broader grid
resilience efforts (Monken et al. 2024). Partnerships with local communities can also inform
DOW strategies to better maintain functional advanced energy infrastructure, especially in
extreme and remote environments, such as Alaska, according to an Arctic researcher. Local
expertise on unique risks and possible solutions could inform the testing of advanced
technologies and help in the upkeep of remote infrastructure. Technology-specific partnerships
with national laboratories and private companies could inform innovation and suitability.

6.10 Common Standards
Developing common standards within DOW, the United States, and the international community
early in the global adoption timeline could facilitate interoperability and smoother integration of
advanced energy technologies into defense operations while also helping to protect supply chains
from adversarial influence. For example, adherence to international standards makes the inability
of U.S. military aircraft to refuel due to higher SAF concentrations in countries adopting higher
blends unlikely, because existing standards ensure compatibility with conventional jet fuel and
legacy aircraft. International standards for SAF ASTM D7566 (ASTM 2022b) and aviation fuel
ASTM D1655 (ASTM 2022a) ensure that SAF is consistent with the necessary chemical
composition and properties for safe use in aircraft engines and fueling equipment (IATA n.d.).
Raising awareness of standards can be critical to preparing for operations abroad. DOW can

 24

promote shared technical standards through foreign military sales, joint exercises, and
international basing (Parthemore and Nagl 2010). When DOW standards are lacking, DOW can
follow industry best practices where feasible (e.g., by protecting Li-ion batteries from physical
damage during transportation and storage and over-/under- temperature/voltage with a battery
management system). Standards or commercial best practices are available from UL, the Society
of Automotive Engineers, the U.S. Department of Transportation, EPA, and the National Fire
Protection Association. Creating standards for advanced energy inputs in larger systems and
platforms could help DOW streamline—for example, by encouraging vendors to use specific
battery types that could be used in a variety of systems.

6.11 Budget Planning
Although advanced energy technologies such as utility-scale solar and onshore wind are
projected to bring cost savings over traditional fuels and nuclear energy in the long term (Lazard
2024), DOW budget planning should consider costs related to maintaining legacy fuel
infrastructure as well as building and maintaining advanced energy assets. Gradually integrating
new technologies allows for implementing controlled transitions, testing innovations in real-
world settings, and ensuring that they meet the military’s requirements before full-scale
adoption. Due to the rapid evolution of technologies, DOW might want to exercise extra caution
before entering multi-decadal contracts, power purchase agreements, and leases. It would be
wise to evaluate whether the market will evolve, or technology might become obsolete (i.e.,
superseded by superior new technology) during the contract period. Budgets could include a
shorter replacement period for such systems, or contracts could include early termination terms
advantageous to the government, or, in the case of energy performance contracts, the contract
requirements could put the risk of change onto the other party. Budgets should also plan for
charging infrastructure, cybersecurity, local electricity prices, and requirements for battery
disposal or recycling (Tegler 2025).

6.12 Repurposing Infrastructure
Repurposing legacy infrastructure could present a cost-effective way to integrate advanced
energy technologies (EPA 2020). Out-of-service generation assets can be repurposed for biogas,
biomass, or other advanced energy projects, reducing installation costs by $45 to $113 per
kilowatt hour (EPA 2020). Biogas can be transported via existing pipelines to facilities where the
gas can be used directly or processed into natural gas (Raythatha and Olsen 2024). Additionally,
oil and gas wells can be used to harness geothermal energy through retrofitting inactive wells or
co-production on active wells (DOE GTO 2025). Natural gas pipelines could be repurposed for
low-blend levels of hydrogen in natural gas (Kass 2023), but they would likely need to be
modified to prevent leakage (Wood 2024).

Black-start Black-start capability refers to the ability of a power system or specific
capability generating units to restart and restore power without relying on an
 external power grid. From an inertial perspective, black start involves
 initiating the system recovery in the absence of grid-supplied
 frequency and voltage references, which are normally stabilized by the
 inertia of synchronized rotating machines (for more information on
 inertia, see Gracia et al. [2019]). In conventional systems, the inertia
 provided by large synchronous generators helps maintain grid stability
 during fluctuations. During black-start situations, this inertial support
 is initially absent, requiring black-start units to independently establish
 the system frequency and voltage. These units must quickly form a
 stable reference (i.e., act as grid-forming sources), often using internal
 control systems or stored kinetic energy, to energize the transmission
 lines and sequentially bring additional generators online, progressively
 rebuilding the system inertia and ensuring grid stability (Gracia et al.
 2019).

Black-start exercise Also known as an energy resilience readiness exercise, black-start
 exercises are real-world installation energy exercises that are designed
 to determine the ability of an installation’s backup power systems to
 start independently, transfer the load, and carry the load until energy
 from outside the installation is restored (DOD 2025).

Critical material “Any nonfuel mineral, element, substance, or material that the U.S.
 Secretary of Energy determines (1) has a high risk of supply chain
 disruption and (2) serves an essential function in one or more energy
 technologies, including technologies that produce, transmit, store, and
 conserve energy” (DOE 2025).

Energy resilience The ability to prepare for and adapt to changing conditions and recover
 rapidly from operational disruptions. Resilience includes the ability to
 withstand and recover from deliberate attacks, accidents, or naturally
 occurring threats or incidents (Hotchkiss and Dane 2019).

Energy security Maintaining the uninterrupted availability of energy sources at an
 affordable price while minimizing the negative consequences from
 energy use, including efficiency, environmental, social, and
 geopolitical factors (NLR 2024d).

Term Definition

Levelized cost of The levelized cost of energy, also called the levelized energy cost, is a
energy cost of generating energy (usually electricity) for a particular system. It
 is an economic assessment of the cost of the energy-generating system,
 including all the costs over its lifetime: initial investment, operations
 and maintenance, fuel, and capital. The levelized cost of energy is used
 for investment planning and to compare different methods of electricity
 generation on a consistent basis.

Marine/hydrokinetic A power source that is harnessed from the natural movement of water,
energy including waves, tides, and river and ocean currents.

Operational energy 10 U.S.C. 2924 defines operational energy as the “energy required for
 training, moving, and sustaining military forces and weapons platforms
 for military operations. The term includes energy used by tactical
 power systems and generators and weapons platforms” (10 U.S.C.
 2924). Operational energy does not include the energy consumed by
 facilities on permanent U.S. Department of Energy installations and
 does not include the fuel consumed by nontactical vehicles.

Optical power The technology to send and receive power wirelessly, using lasers to
beaming potentially provide energy to remote installations and areas, unmanned
 aerial vehicles, and other applications.

Point of need In distribution operations, point of need is a physical location
 designated by the commander at the tactical level where force
 employment, emplacement, or commodity consumption occurs (U.S.
 Joint Chiefs of Staff 2019, Joint Publication 4).

Reliability The ability of the system to withstand instability, uncontrolled events,
 cascading failures, or the unanticipated loss of system components.

Resource adequacy The ability of a power system to supply enough electricity at the right
 locations to keep the lights on during all hours of the year.

Appendix C. USINDOPACOM
U.S. Indo-Pacific Command’s (USINDOPACOM’s) distinctive geographic and operational
characteristics shape the risk drivers outlined in this analysis in ways that differ from other
regions, highlighting vulnerabilities surrounding local energy sources (see Section 4.1.3) as well
as operational and logistical challenges (see Sections 3.2 and 3.5) that could degrade readiness.
In particular, the increasing dependence on China for components of, or investments in, local
energy infrastructure (see Sections 4.1.1 and 4.1.2) leave this area vulnerable to local autonomy
over energy resources via increasing foreign influence or potential compromise of the energy
infrastructure.

The primary distinguishing features of this region are the large distances and many islands.
Islands face unique energy challenges, including heavy dependence on imported fossil fuels and
workforce limitations, especially the shortage of trained technicians required to maintain new
advanced energy assets (NLR 2024a). Additional challenges include the technical difficulties of
integrating variable energy generation into small grids, aging generation assets, long lead times
for the delivery of specialized equipment, and frequent typhoons (NLR 2024a). For example,
some Pacific power utilities have experienced challenges when dispatching solar assets due to
inadequate energy storage. The energy infrastructure in this region is particularly vulnerable to
severe storms, and the intensity of tropical cyclones is increasing over time (Klotzbach et al.
2022). This exacerbates the need for more backup power and a greater variety of power sources
for U.S. bases to ensure uninterrupted power in a region that is critical to the forward posturing
of U.S. forces. In Guam, for example, the U.S. Navy accounted for more than 20% of the Guam
Power Authority’s energy sales in 2023 (Scarpitti and Voss 2024).

When considering small modular reactors as an energy option, sociocultural views might have an
impact on acceptability. For example, historical experiences with nuclear weapons, including
Hiroshima, Nagasaki, the Fukushima disaster, and mid-20th century nuclear testing in the South
Pacific, have contributed to widespread regional aversion to nuclear technologies. This sentiment
is reflected in the 1985 South Pacific Nuclear Free Zone Treaty, in which signatory nations
pledged not to develop or test nuclear weapons (South Pacific Bureau for Economic Co-
operation 1987). Although the treaty does not explicitly prohibit nuclear energy generation, the
agreement underscores a strong cultural commitment to keeping nuclear activities out of local
communities.

C.1 Strategic Leadership
Strategic competitors, particularly China, are using energy investments to expand their
geopolitical influence in the USINDOPACOM region, where control over or cooperation with
island nations is key to overcoming geographic challenges. China’s dominance in solar, wind,
battery, inverter, and large power transformer technologies and supply chains has made Indo-
Pacific nations increasingly reliant on China-supplied infrastructure. Figure C-1 illustrates the
growing share of electricity production from solar and wind among countries in
USINDOPACOM’s area of responsibility. For example, the Cook Islands’ recent embrace of
Chinese infrastructure investment, diplomatic engagement, and strategic alignment highlights
how China is leveraging advanced energy technologies as one aspect of a broader strategy to
expand its influence in the USINDOPACOM region. A 2025 agreement between the two
countries encourages joint business cooperation, including photovoltaic (PV), wind, tidal power,

 62

energy transmission networks, and the exploration of deep-sea mineral resources, among other
sectors (Kumar 2025). A U.S. effort to replicate China’s Belt and Road Initiative energy
infrastructure investments abroad would likely require billions of U.S. dollars in aid and decades
for implementation. As of 2025, China’s Belt and Road Initiative is estimated to have a total cost
of $1.308 trillion since 2013 for construction and investments across 150 countries, with $40
billion in 2024 and $44 billion in the first half of 2025 on energy engagement (representing 35%
of China’s international efforts in 2025) (Nedopil 2025). The G7-led Partnership for Global
Infrastructure and Investment (PGII) announced in 2022 was designed to finance $600 billion in
infrastructure, including energy infrastructure, for low- to middle-income countries by 2027
(Brownstein 2023) as an effort to build global influence in response to China’s efforts (Losos
and Fetter 2022). In addition, a western supply chain of energy assets would be necessary to
reduce the risk of Chinese influence and control of software or hardware (Sutherland and Sutter
2024).

C.2 Local Energy Vulnerabilities
Key East Asian allies, such as South Korea and Japan, have high energy consumption rates and a
significant reliance on imported hydrocarbons (ITA 2021). The Philippines relies on imported
fossil fuels for more than half its consumption (Prétat et al. 2024), leaving the country vulnerable
to supply disruptions. This dependence has acted as a catalyst for the accelerated adoption of
advanced energy technologies by these countries, with Japan investing ¥15 trillion ($107.5
billion) in their hydrogen infrastructure and launching multiple large-scale battery energy storage
systems, including a 69.6-MWh sodium-sulfur project (Ravichandran 2023). South Korea, which
holds more than 30% of the Asia-Pacific’s battery energy storage system market, aims to reach

 63

25 gigawatts (GW)/127 GWh in storage capacity by 2036, while the Philippines is developing
one of the world’s largest battery energy storage system portfolios, at 1,000 MW, and has
secured more than $13 billion in foreign investment for advanced energy technology and storage
projects (Ravichandran 2023).

But even as these countries adopt advanced energy technologies, their strategies introduce new
forms of dependence. More than 85% of battery energy storage systems expected to come online
globally in 2024 will use lithium-iron-phosphate chemistries, for which China controls nearly the
entire supply chain—including raw material processing and cell manufacturing (Hughes 2025).
As a result, more than 75% of global battery deployments outside China still rely on Chinese
supply chains (Hughes 2025), underscoring that energy security challenges persist. Such reliance
on Chinese-sourced battery components could introduce additional cyber risks to the energy
infrastructure of host nations and (by extension) to the U.S. installations operating within those
countries.

China’s dominance of several aspects of the advanced energy technology market has left island
nations increasingly dependent on China for these technologies and for supporting grid
infrastructure upgrades. A number of U.S. allies are incorporating Chinese-built assets, such as
solar PV systems and transformers, to bolster their energy resilience. For instance, Chinese-built
solar technology filled the electricity gap left when Japan shut down its nuclear plants in the
wake of the Fukushima nuclear disaster in 2011 (Coca 2024).

In another example, the Philippines is increasingly dependent on China for grid updates. China
owns 40% of the National Grid Corporation of the Philippines (Lema and Flores 2024), and the
imminent depletion of the Malampaya gas source in the South China Sea, which supplies 20% of
Luzon’s (the largest and most populous island in the Philippines) electricity (Malampaya 2024),
will intensify dependence on imports. The large share of power grid and advanced energy
components provided by China exacerbates uncertainty surrounding cyber vulnerabilities.

In a contested environment, geographic constraints on energy transmission methods (e.g., limited
overland infrastructure and the vulnerabilities of undersea cables to, for example, China’s newly
unveiled deep-sea cable cutter [Chen 2025]) make local energy unlikely as a dependable DOW
energy source.

C.3 Operational and Logistics Considerations
The vast distances between landmasses and from the U.S. mainland in the Pacific, combined
with the need to disperse assets to counter Chinese surveillance, reconnaissance, and targeting
capabilities (Simulcik, Villalobos, and Bazilian 2025), underscore the need for power-dense,
logistically flexible energy technologies to support USINDOPACOM operations. Lithium-ion
batteries, though widely used in civilian sectors, bring added logistics considerations. Recall
from Section 3.5 of this report that they offer only a fraction (15.3%) of the energy per volume
compared to gasoline equivalents (AFDC 2024; Shaffer 2024). Similarly, solar and wind energy
typically require significantly more surface area to generate comparable power (Van Zalk and
Behrens 2018), making them less practical as stand-alone power sources for rapid deployment in
space-constrained or contested environments.

 64

DOW purchases nearly 50% of its fuel overseas from foreign partners (DOD 2023). Increased
DOW reliance on advanced energy technologies (such as batteries) that are heavier and not as
easy to store or transport over long distances compared to legacy fuels could pose risks in the
vast USINDOPACOM region, where logistics are an ever-present challenge.

Interoperability will face challenges in USINDOPACOM because, despite strong alliances and
partnerships, there is no North Atlantic Treaty Organization-equivalent shared security pact that
includes a majority of countries in the region. USINDOPACOM works with a wide range of
allies and partners, each with their own standards and regulations. This variation can complicate
the integration of energy technologies because each country might have different requirements
and systems, and each country adopts advanced technologies at different rates. A forum with
USINDOPACOM partners to discuss common standards and regulations (including cyber) could
replicate NATO’s standardized agreement process to improve interoperability, including the
Quadrilateral Security Dialogue countries of Australia, India, and Japan, plus other key regional
partners (e.g., South Korea). In addition, DOW staff might require specialized training to
understand the advanced energy assets incorporated into the region’s varied energy infrastructure
and how these assets can interfere with sensors. This added complexity could further stretch
existing capabilities, exacerbating the risks of operational inefficiency.

Global market forces will also create a more muddied operating theater. Deep-sea mining in
USINDOPACOM, partly driven by increased demand for critical materials needed in advanced
energy technologies, could give Chinese ships another reason to operate in contested waters. For
example, in February 2025, China and the Cook Islands signed an agreement to collaborate on
research and the potential extraction of critical minerals from the Cook Islands’ seabed. This
agreement highlights China’s continued focus on the South Pacific while also offering Pacific
Island nations more opportunities for international partnerships. Advances in deep-sea mining
are significant, as they could help establish important precedents in terms of norms and legal
frameworks for undersea commercial activities. These developments also have military
implications, particularly concerning the potential “dual use” nature of certain seabed exploration
activities. Deep-sea mining can be leveraged as both a tool and a result of foreign policy, thereby
carrying broader implications for national security (Baruah 2025).

C.4 Mitigations of Particular Importance to USINDOPACOM
The operational and logistical challenges associated with both legacy and advanced energy
technologies underscore the strategic importance of maturing and deploying emerging solutions
that are not yet operational but are uniquely suited to overcoming the region’s geographic and
logistical energy challenges. For example, DOW’s heavy reliance on liquid petroleum-based
fuels transported by vulnerable (mostly commercial) ships (Carmel 2023) could be mitigated by
adopting advanced energy technologies, such as geothermal, marine and hydrokinetic energy,
hydrogen fuel cells, or even power beaming. In particular, tidal and wave energy could become a
point-of-need generation alternative to expensive diesel generation for islands (Schuler and
Dupont 2025), offering a resilient and locally sourced solution for remote or distributed
locations. Local energy vulnerabilities in USINDOPACOM add urgency to developing point-of-
need energy generation. Research and development efforts in this vein, such as the National
Laboratory of the Rockies’ Distributed Flexible Fuel Production trailer, which produces natural
gas from carbon dioxide, hydrogen, and methane-producing organisms at forward operating
bases (Harrison and Dowe 2025), could provide solutions if continued and operationally tested.

 65

At 16 feet long and 100 cubic feet, the trailer and control room that house this capability are
compact enough to fit in the back of a C-130J, demonstrating the promise of mobile and low-
footprint energy production capabilities.

Prepositioning assets could also be considered, possibly in partnership with local authorities. For
example, a geothermal plant could be built and maintained in a small community, ready to
provide power to a potential forward operating base in the future. Hydrogen and other fuels
might also be suitable for prepositioning.

Appendix D. EUCOM
Europe’s geography and geopolitical landscape make certain risks more pronounced than others.
Europe is adopting advanced energy technologies at a higher rate compared to other continents
(see Figure D-1), with nearly half of the European Union’s (E.U.’s) electricity now generated
from advanced energy technologies such as wind, solar, hydropower, bioenergy, geothermal, and
ocean energy technologies (Eurostat 2025b). The continent lacks significant domestic reserves of
legacy fuels, such as oil and natural gas, making Europe heavily reliant on foreign energy
suppliers. The unique nature of Europe’s economic and political integration through institutions
such as the E.U. and NATO creates a more interconnected energy ecosystem, resulting in
opportunities and risks shared across borders. This has implications for European resilience and
U.S. military operations in the U.S. European Command (EUCOM) theater.

D.1 Strategic Leadership
The changing global energy landscape is reshaping the foundation of geopolitical influence, with
advanced energy systems increasingly viewed not only as alternative forms of energy but also as
instruments of power projection and strategic alignment. In the European theater, as legacy fuel
markets shrink and nations accelerate their shift toward advanced energy technologies, a new set
of dependencies is emerging. Although Europe has made significant progress in reducing its
reliance on fossil fuels from Russia and the Middle East, the continent now faces increasing
exposure to another form of strategic vulnerability: dependence on China for critical materials,
technologies, and manufacturing supply chains that underpin the adoption of advanced energy
technologies. China’s control of installed components creates cyber vulnerabilities (CISA 2022),
and an embargo of inputs to advanced energy assets could degrade Europe’s grid and undermine
economic growth. While China may not currently use this dependence to overtly coerce
European actors, the deep integration of Chinese materials and technologies into Europe’s
energy ecosystem creates a latent influence that can shape European policy decisions over time,

 67

encouraging European decision makers to carefully weigh the risks of antagonizing China on
unrelated geopolitical issues. Dependence on Chinese manufacturing could also hinder Europe’s
technological development and defense readiness in the future.

D.2 Local Energy Vulnerabilities
Europe’s dependence on developing countries for fossil fuels and China’s dominance of
advanced energy technology markets are particularly impactful for U.S. Department of War
(DOW) missions that depend on local power provision in the EUCOM theater. Historically
dependent on Russia for natural gas and petroleum, the International Association of Oil and Gas
Producers estimates that E.U. countries meet only 40% of E.U. natural gas demand through
domestic production (IOGP Europe 2025), and Europe stores less than one-quarter of its annual
consumption to help meet winter demand (Bousso 2025). In a contested environment in the early
spring, remaining storage could be exhausted quickly. Additionally, the Organization of the
Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC) reports that the European countries in the Organisation
for Economic Co-operation and Development held only 11.12 billion barrels in proven crude oil
reserves in 2023, just 0.72% of the global total (OPEC 2025). Following the onset of the Russia-
Ukraine war, Europe developed a broad strategy to reduce reliance on Russian gas, phasing out
Russian petroleum by 2027. But increased liquefied natural gas imports from countries such as
the United States, Qatar, and Norway have introduced challenges, including price volatility and
competition from China, which prioritizes liquefied natural gas imports (Abnett 2025).

In 2024, almost half of the E.U.’s net electricity generation came from advanced energy
technologies (see Figure D-2), with solar surpassing coal as the fastest-growing power source
(Eurostat 2025b; Rosslowe and Petrovich 2025).

supply chain risks for the European nations where DOW operates. The Chinese origins of many
components also add uncertainty to wide-scale cyber vulnerabilities across Europe.

Moreover, during conflict, gas delivery disruptions could compound with weather events to
disrupt Europe’s power ecosystem, which, in turn, can affect DOW installations that rely on this
power. For example, in early 2025, a four-month period of unseasonably low wind resulted in
significantly reduced output from wind turbines, forcing Germany to heavily depend on its
legacy power plants to meet demand (Farhat and Wertz 2025), but this low-wind period did not

D.3 Operational and Logistical Challenges
The rapid adoption of advanced energy technologies in Europe could strain DOW readiness in
times of surge energy needs, especially as infrastructure shifts away from legacy systems (see
Section 4.3), such as gas stations in favor of electric vehicle (EV) charging (Consultancy.eu
2024). Fossil fuel generation fell by 75 TWh (9%) from 2023, reaching its lowest level in more
than 40 years (Rosslowe and Petrovich 2025), signaling shrinking infrastructure tied to these
industries.

Moreover, many NATO militaries, some with commitments to achieve net zero by 2050, have
more aggressive advanced energy technology adoption plans than DOW, potentially leading to
disagreements regarding interoperability and strategic planning for operational readiness
(Griffiths, Hammock, and Tate 2025). This could challenge a unified approach to efforts such as
the 2023 NATO Climate Change and Security Impact Assessment’s emphasis on the need to
research alternative fuels for NATO warships—or choosing among focusing on securing the
carbon-based liquid fuel supply chain, investing in electrifying the force while relying on
Europe’s increasing wind and solar sectors, or prioritizing the development of nuclear
capabilities (Griffiths, Hammock, and Tate 2025).

The British Army’s approach to battlefield electrification, which outlines research and
development plans through 2035 and supports the 2021 Ministry of Defense’s Climate Change
and Sustainability Strategic Approach, emphasizes the need for U.K. defense to be a fast
follower of commercial-sector advancements. An example that defense experts cite as a model
are hydrogen generators, which were used to power the 2024 Isle of Wight music festival and
which are capable of scaling to meet expeditionary or semifixed military operations, such as a
divisional headquarters or a patrol base (Goodall 2025).

In the northern EUCOM theater, risks to operational readiness and logistical challenges would be
more acutely felt in Arctic operations, where extreme weather challenges could impact advanced
energy technologies like batteries (Pechko 2025). In addition, the region’s critical mineral

 71

reserves could draw adversaries to new seafloor mining efforts, thereby increasing their presence
and complicating the operational environment.

D.4 Mitigations of Particular Importance to EUCOM
With these challenges in mind, DOW could work with European partners to ensure energy
infrastructure modernization and hardening could improve resilience in times of crisis or war.
The Russia-Ukraine war demonstrated the vulnerability of transmission lines to drone attacks
(Kyiv Post 2024) and exposed Ukraine’s aging transmission infrastructure as a weakness
(Dreves, Fall, and McCan 2023).

Moreover, hydrogen fuel cell EVs offer notable advantages in the EUCOM theater: the smaller
battery size can reduce the need for rare earth minerals by 8–16 times compared to conventional
battery EVs (Goodall 2025). This reduction in rare earth metal usage not only reduces Europe’s
strategic vulnerability to supply disruptions but also decreases overall system weight and
enhances operational range due to the fuel cell EVs’ self-power generation capabilities (Goodall
2025).

Appendix E. New Technologies on the Horizon
A number of technologies currently under development might address some of the U.S.
Department of War’s (DOW’s) specific needs, including reliable power that is resistant to
disruptions, energy generation with reduced visibility and detectability, and/or portable and
easily transported and installed power technologies, including for use in remote locations.

These innovations are at varied stages of commercialization and availability and are primarily
driven by cost considerations. Strategic DOW investments in these emerging technologies could
help to accelerate the time to advancement and commercialization for broader applications.

E.1 Advanced Batteries
Advanced battery chemistries and designs can provide batteries with lighter weights and
improved performance, including higher energy densities (greater energy storage per unit volume
or weight), faster charging, longer life cycles (functional lifetimes), and improved safety
compared to standard lithium-ion (Li-ion) batteries. Some of these advanced chemistries and
designs could offer a more reliable supply of batteries and reduced costs by using alternatives to
critical minerals, including lithium and graphite. These battery advancements include solid-state
batteries (SSBs), lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries, sodium-ion (Na-ion) batteries, vanadium redox
flow batteries, lithium-titanate (LTO) batteries, and silicon (Si) anodes. Some battery options are
commercially available, though most are still being optimized for capacity, performance, and
cost-competitiveness. According to National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) experts, these are
expected to be commercially available by 2030 or 2035.

Some batteries are well suited for electric vehicle (EV) applications because of their reduced
weight combined with their increased energy storage and faster charging, properties that are well
suited for improved mobility. A leading example is Li-S, which uses abundant sulfur as the
cathode material instead of critical minerals (Benveniste et al. 2018); it has been identified as
useful for unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs), high-altitude pseudo-satellites, autonomous
underwater vehicles, and heavy road vehicles (Dorfler et al. 2020).

Relatively heavier-weight advanced batteries might be more suitable for stationary storage while
offering improved performance, including Na-ion, redox flow, and the commercially available
LTO.

E.2 Enhanced and Advanced Geothermal
Next-generation geothermal technologies offer more consistent energy generation and less
detectability than other distributed energy resources (DERs), such as solar photovoltaics (PV)
and wind. While conventional geothermal (hydrothermal) baseload is available continuously,
conventional geothermal systems rely on naturally occurring hydrothermal resources (hot water
and steam in porous rock). Enhanced geothermal systems (EGS) can be deployed in a much
broader range of geologic settings where high temperatures exist but without the preexisting
natural reservoir conditions. EGS use technologies applied to hydraulic fracturing to inject fluid
underground to create fractures and improve rock permeability, allowing the fluid to flow within
the rock fractures, after which the heated fluid is collected for energy generation (NLR 2025d,
DOE EERE 2023). EGS can also include chemicals and additives in addition to physical
stimulation to improve permeability (NLR 2025d) to further enhance accessibility to geothermal
energy.

Closed-loop geothermal (CLG) systems are emerging technologies that are transitioning from
pilot-scale demonstrations to early commercial deployments, according to an NLR expert. They
represent a novel approach to geothermal energy because they do not rely on fluid exchange with
the subsurface. Instead, a sealed loop circulates a working fluid through deep boreholes,
absorbing heat from surrounding rock and transferring that heat to the surface for use in power
generation or direct heating. One of the challenges associated with this technology is the low
heat transfer efficiency.

Geothermal heat pumps (GHPs) (also known as ground source heat pumps) are a proven,
commercial, and widely available technology used for space heating, cooling, and sometimes
water heating. They use the relatively constant temperatures just below the Earth’s surface to
provide efficient thermal energy for residential, commercial, and institutional buildings.

While CLG systems and GHPs both use a closed-loop circulation of fluid, they serve different
purposes. CLG systems circulate fluid through deep, hot rock formations for electricity
generation or direct heating. GHPs use shallow ground temperatures near the surface for space
heating and cooling in buildings (NLR 2025a). CLGs and GHPs can increase energy efficiency
and cut costs but are primarily suitable for smaller scale (i.e., building-level) applications (DOE
2023a). CLGs and GHPs make geothermal a possible option in more areas than when relying on
natural geothermal features, yet they do not require the reservoir stimulation needed with EGS,
reducing costs and water demand (NLR 2025a); however, according to an interviewee, this
approach may require higher temperatures compared to EGS, limiting applicability to areas with
specific natural geologic characteristics.

EGS could be commercialized by 2030, with a projected national average cost of $60–$70 per
MWh by 2030, and potentially $40–$50 per MWh in select regions (DOE EERE 2023). The U.S.
Department of Energy (DOE) Enhanced Geothermal Shot is aiming to reduce costs to $45 per
MWh by 2035. According to an NLR expert, several CLG systems have been demonstrated
globally.

Risks associated with inaccurate resource characterization and site identification are lower for
next-generation geothermal than traditional geothermal (DOE EERE 2023). Enduring risks from
EGS include induced seismic activity, although a DOE mitigation protocol reduces and manages

 75

this risk; water quality degradation through chemical use in the injection fluid; and overall water
resource availability. According to an NLR expert, technical challenges can include creating
sufficient area for heat transfer in the surrounding rock. Overall, the high initial investment in
project development could present the greatest hurdle in using advanced geothermal
technologies.

E.3 Power Beaming
Optical and radio frequency power-beaming technologies enable the wireless transfer of power
to remote areas and allow for power generation to occur in different areas from power use.
Electricity is generated at one point, such as using solar PV panels or a generator, and then
transmitted using laser beams to the site where the electricity will be used. The transmission can
occur through air as land-to-land and land-to-air (to UAVs), through space as satellite-to-satellite
and space-to-ground (radio frequency), or through fiber-optic cables (including powering
tethered UAVs).

Advantages of the technology include reducing some supply chain complexities by removing the
need to mobilize energy generation equipment, such as solar PV infrastructure, or transport fuels.
Power beaming offers more reliable energy availability by transmitting power to areas that might
be less suitable for traditional DERs, including solar PV. This technology can be adapted for
directed energy weapons, similar to how nuclear technology serves both civilian and military
purposes. As advancements are made in directed energy weapon systems, progress in these areas
could accelerate developments in power-beaming technologies and vice versa, potentially
enhancing both military capabilities and broader energy applications.

According to NLR experts, the use of high-energy laser beams creates a safety risk, and the lack
of extensive field-testing creates uncertainty regarding field performance. Adversarial threats
include possible power interception and cybersecurity threats.

Although laser and radio frequency power conversion technologies are commercially available,
other technological challenges need to be resolved before wireless power beaming can be
commercialized. These hurdles include system integration issues and thermal dissipation at the
receiving point as a limitation on the amount of power that can be transferred. For any space-
based power, the logistics and costs to dispatch solar PV in space are substantial.

For space applications, NLR has developed advanced III-V PV cells optimized for harsh space
environments (NLR 2023c). These cells are the standard in space-based PV due to their high
efficiency and proven stability in radiation-rich and thermally extreme conditions. NLR also
researches materials for PV receivers for space and terrestrial power beaming.

Projected commercialization timelines for optical power beaming vary based on application,
ranging from short-distance, low-power, land-based applications; to long-distance, higher-power,
land-based applications; to space applications. According to NLR experts, the technological and
manufacturing challenges of land-based systems could likely be overcome in 1 year if prioritized
with intensive funding and effort.

E.4 Marine Hydrokinetic Energy
Marine energy includes energy generation from wave, tide, and current kinetic movement in
oceans, coastal areas, estuaries, and rivers (NLR 2025c). Marine energy resources (wave, tides,
currents) are more predictable and less variable than other advanced energy resources, such as
wind and solar. This technology offers resilient, point-of-need electricity generation for islands
and coastal areas, with the potential to provide 60% of the total domestic U.S. electricity needs
(NLR 2025c). By producing electricity locally rather than relying on centralized generation
assets located hundreds of miles away, marine energy can increase energy resilience. For
example, Oregon’s coastal communities, where there are several U.S. Army National Guard
bases, currently depend on electricity generated by inland hydroelectric dams hundreds of miles
away. A large-scale wildfire or earthquake could disrupt transmission lines, potentially leaving
those coastal regions without power for extended periods. Marine energy could mitigate such
risks by enabling coastal self-sufficiency. Although the broad range of different marine energy
technologies are still being developed, the deployment of demonstration and full-scale projects,
as well as the capacity, in the E.U. exceeds that in the United States. As of 2025, the combined
ocean energy capacity in the E.U. totaled 106 GWh, with capacity expected to significantly
expand by 2030 (Schuler and Dupont 2025). Although Europe continues to have the most
installed wave-energy devices and the greatest capacity, cumulative installations in the rest of the
world have, for the first time since 2015, marginally surpassed Europe’s.

For example, in July 2022, CalWave Power Technologies Inc. retrieved its xWave wave energy
pilot device after a successful 10-month deployment off the coast of San Diego, California. The
device, which remained operational for 99% of its deployment, survived two extreme storms,
marking a critical milestone in proving wave energy’s commercial viability (DOE WPTO 2023).
A U.S. Navy wave energy test site located offshore from a Marine base in Hawaii tests marine
energy technologies and connects to a land-based grid using underwater cables (SOEST 2025).

In addition to other domestic deployments and demonstration projects, U.S.-headquartered
company ORPC has developed river energy generation systems, deploying the RivGen Power
System in a remote community in Alaska (ORPC 2025a) and testing a Modular RivGen Power
System, including one in Maine in partnership with Sandia National Laboratories (ORPC 2025b;
DOE WPTO 2024).

Environmental risks from marine energy equipment and operation may present challenges or
delays due to regulatory processes and requirements (Copping 2024), From a technological and
investment perspective, marine energy deployment is still emerging, particularly in the United
States, presenting uncertainties.

E.5 Ocean Thermal Energy Conversion
Ocean Thermal Energy Conversion (OTEC) technology generates electricity by using the
difference in temperature between warm surface and cold deep ocean layers, typically requiring a
20°C difference (IRENA 2014, EIA 2023). Warm seawater is pumped through an evaporator
containing fluid, and the vaporized fluid (either seawater or another fluid) drives a turbine while
cold seawater condenses the vapor back to liquid. OTEC technology can be used for continuous
electricity generation as well as cooling and desalination.

 77

The technology is at demonstration scale, but high capital and operational costs and operational
inexperience remain challenges (IRENA 2014). In part, high costs are driven by material needs
(e.g., pipes) and the corrosive impacts of saltwater. Regions for the most effective deployment of
the technology are tropical, 20 degrees north and south, given the requirement for warm surface
water (Xiao and Gulfam 2023). Optimal OTEC deployment also requires deep water access,
which may be considerable distances from shore in some tropical regions.

In the United States, a 105-kilowatt (kW) demonstration facility is operational offshore of
Hawaii; it was installed in 2015 and delivers electricity to the local grid (EIA 2023). The EU is
developing and testing OTEC systems, including a demonstration project in the Canary Islands
launched in 2024 (PLOCAN 2024). Additional recent demonstration projects include a 20-kW
floating system and 50-kW systems, both deployed in China in 2023 (Su et al. 2025b). With
OTEC systems still at demonstration scale and facing cost and optimal region limitations, the
timeline for commercial-scale deployment is uncertain.

E.6 Deployable Wind Energy
Deployable wind turbines offer improved mobility and faster deployment compared to traditional
wind turbines, with targeted applications for defense and disaster response (Summerville et al.
2022; NLR 2025a). These deployable systems range from small systems that are less than 3 kW
and transported by backpacks or small trailers, to 10- to 20-kW systems that are transported by
trailers or containers, to larger systems that are up to 100 kW to support larger electrical loads
(Summerville et al. 2022). Setup times range from minutes to hours for smaller systems, to hours
or days for larger systems. Midsize to large systems can be transported in 20-ft and 40-ft
standard shipping containers.

These systems do not require permanent foundations and instead use custom trailers or shipping
containers in combination with outriggers and anchors to provide safety and stability in all wind
conditions (Summerville at al. 2022).

These deployable wind systems are often paired with solar and battery energy storage to form a
microgrid, offering reliable, on-site power for communications, water filtration, heat, lights, and
medical equipment. Power systems powered by wind and solar energy help reduce reliance on
liquid fuels for diesel generators that must be regularly delivered to the facility (McDermott-
Murphy 2022).

Deployable wind energy systems are currently being developed by U.S.-headquartered
companies, such as a 12-kW portable wind turbine by Uprise Energy and the 30-kW Deployable
Advanced Renewable Power System by Bergey Windpower Company (Uprise Energy n.d.;
Bergey Windpower Company n.d.-a). Bergey Windpower offers a 10-kW wind turbine with a
height of 23 feet that is capable of being connected to the grid and is certified by the Small Wind
Certification Council (Bergey Windpower Company n.d.-b).

Global competitors offer a range of commercialized options. Canadian-based Aurea
Technologies markets the 40-W Shine Turbine system, which operates within an 8- to 28-mile-
per-hour wind speed range and can be packed in a backpack (Shine Turbine 2025). Ryse Energy,
headquartered in the United Arab Emirates, markets several micro wind turbines that provide 90
amp-hours per day given average wind speeds of 12 miles per hour and weigh 13 pounds (Ryse

 78

Energy 2025). Chinese-based companies also market deployable wind turbines, such as
Zonhan’s options, which range from 2 kW to 10 kW (Zonhan 2024).

E.7 Tactical Hydrogen
Hydrogen generators can provide tactical point-of-need energy and are already being tested for
DOW applications. Hydrogen can serve as an alternative to diesel generators, serving as self-
sufficient power sources and reducing concerns related to diesel resupply as well as detectability
(e.g., reducing heat, smoke, and noise) (Espinosa 2025; Kauffman 2025; Pasquini 2025).
Multiple innovations and demonstration projects reflect progress towards tactical hydrogen
systems for military applications.

 • A Hydrogen at the Tactical Edge of Contested Logistics (HyTEC) system is an
 electrolysis-based system using electricity from other sources to produce point-of-use
 hydrogen for expeditionary applications including small generators and unmanned
 aviation equipment (DIU 2025). As of 2025, this system is being tested at Marine Corps
 Base Hawaii and Marine Corps Air Station Yuma.
 • A portable Hydrogen Small Unit Power (H-SUP) system prototype has been developed
 by the U.S. Naval Research Laboratory to function as a fuel cell electric generator able to
 support remote field operations where low detectability is critical (Pasquini 2025). The
 fuel cell has previously been successfully demonstrated for unmanned vehicle
 applications in the Naval Air Warfare Center Aircraft Division’s H2 Stalker Program,
 with power-to-weight advantages.
 • U.S. manufacturer NovaSpark Energy Corp. offers designs for hydrogen generators as
 closed-loop systems that can be transported on military trailers and that function in low-
 humidity desert environments or Indo-Pacific islands (Kauffman 2025).

E.8 Advanced Fuel Cell Technology
Advanced fuel cell technologies offer improved efficiencies for electricity generation based on
chemical conversion to electricity compared to traditional combustion-based conversion (DOE
HFTO n.d.-a.). Researchers continue to address technological challenges to improve the cost,
performance, and durability of fuel cells both for mobile and stationary power applications (DOE
HFTO n.d.-a, NLR 2025b).

One notable advancement area is hydrogen, which can be used to generate electricity for
stationary applications, with one key advantage being its ability to be stored for long periods.
When paired with salt cavern storage and fuel cell electricity generation, hydrogen can be
competitive with today’s natural gas combined-cycle technologies (Hunter et al. 2021).

An advanced fuel cell on the verge of commercialization includes Japan-based Honda’s Next
Generation Fuel Cell Module planned for 2027 (Honda 2025), which is slated to deliver three
times the power density of previous models (Tripathi 2025). The fuel cell features a compact
design, measuring 28.7 by 27.5 by 22.8 inches; and the device operates effectively across a wide
range of conditions, with temperatures ranging from -30°C (-22°F) to +60°C (140°F) and at
altitudes up to 11,500 feet. Additionally, in 2026, Honda is scheduled to begin production on its
fuel cell power generator (Honda 2025), which is designed as a reliable backup power source for
large-scale facilities, with a unit capacity up to 250 kW of power and multiple connected units
providing 1,000 kW or more (Tripathi 2025).

 79

Some emerging research offers the potential for innovative options for expanded applications.
An advanced proton exchange membrane fuel cell for application in underwater EVs
(autonomous underwater vehicles) is at the prototype stage, developed by German researchers, it
uses artificial gills to overcome pressure challenges for this oxygen-based technology (Lea 2025;
Merckelbach and Georgopanos 2025).

Appendix F. Small Modular Reactors
Small modular reactors (SMRs) are an emerging technology that will offer an advanced option
for energy generation as well as heat generation, hydrogen production, and desalination for
potable water (IAEA 2024; INL 2025). These small nuclear reactors can generate up to
approximately 300 MW(e) (or 7.2 million kWh) per day (European Commission, n.d.-a, n.d.-b).
SMRs could potentially mitigate some risks related to the variability and suitability of other
distributed energy resources (DERs), such as solar power that is subject to variability based on
geographic location or weather conditions (IAEA 2024). SMRs have the potential to be used at
sites alongside DERs to improve the overall reliability and resilience of the energy system.
Additionally, the smaller footprint of SMRs could reduce vulnerabilities to physical threats.

The regulatory approval process impacts commercialization and deployment timelines. In 2023,
the U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) certified the first U.S.-produced SMR design, a
50-MW(e) capacity power module developed by U.S. company NuScale (Office of Nuclear
Energy 2023). Overall, the NRC certification of the NuScale SMR was a multi-year process,
beginning with an application in 2018 (Office of Nuclear Energy 2023). Additionally, the
International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) establishes standards and coordinates across
nations to facilitate safe nuclear energy development (IAEA 2024). Further, costs associated with
SMR technologies could slow commercial deployment: cost concerns by utility customers were a
key factor contributing to the cancellation of a multiple reactor 462 MW SMR project with
NuScale and the Utah Associated Municipal Power Systems (Gardner and Mishra 2023).

Because this technology has not yet been deployed in the United States, there are uncertainties
about its performance, its safety, and the regulatory process, according to a National Laboratory
of the Rockies expert. Reactors at the 20-MW scale are not yet in use, and uncertainty about
deployment introduces a risk for planning, investments, and preparedness. For example, the
required energy generation might not be achieved in field use, creating disruptions and delays.
There are also risks related to moving toward commercialization too quickly to allow for a
thorough assessment of possible technological issues and safety risks, including understanding
vulnerabilities to physical attacks, whether passive safety mechanisms work, and the volume of
waste produced. Scaling to commercial production and deployment too quickly could potentially
introduce adverse safety impacts. An insufficiently trained workforce to install, manage, and
operate these systems likely poses a greater risk to the timeline and effectiveness of SMR
adoption compared to technologies such as solar photovoltaics (PV), which have been
commercially available for decades.

Technical challenges include inefficiencies in transforming the heat produced by SMRs to
electricity, resulting in a potential need for substantial volumes of water to provide cooling after
steam exits the turbine, although some systems avoid water use by using other coolant
approaches (e.g., open gas cycles). If cooling demand is not reduced, deployment could be
limited in low-water locations.

Potential radioactivity after an accident or kinetic strike is a risk that is unique to nuclear energy,
and physical and cyber threats are possible, although installing SMRs inside an installation fence
line could reduce some physical risks.

 81

Moreover, supply chain vulnerabilities are pronounced, with the United States importing 99% of
the uranium concentrate used in existing nuclear generators in 2023 (EIA 2025a). In 2023,
leading foreign sources included (as percentages of total deliveries) Canada (27%), Australia
(22%), Kazakhstan (22%), Russia (12%), and Uzbekistan (10%). The United States also imports
enriched uranium, as domestic enrichment capacity provided for only 30% of uranium fuel needs
in 2023, with more than 20% of these imports being sourced from Russia as of the same year
(Roy and Dareen 2024). Although the United States adopted a ban on uranium products from
Russia in 2024, companies can apply for a waiver through 2027 (EIA 2025a). U.S. production of
uranium concentrate increased in 2024, at five facilities in Texas and Wyoming, and total
employment in the U.S. uranium production industry increased 49% from 2023 to 2024 (EIA
2025b). A U.S. White House executive order in March 2025 announced the prioritization of
domestic critical mineral access, production, and processing, including uranium (The White
House 2025), which could also encourage more domestic production. Further, many SMR
designs would use “high-assay low-enriched uranium,” which is not yet widely produced (Office
of Nuclear Energy 2024). The United States and allies would need to substantially increase
uranium concentrate production and enrichment capacity to avoid supply chain vulnerabilities
for the expanded demand that widespread SMR deployment would introduce.

Competition to lead the industry could exacerbate supply chain risks, given that China and
Russia lead in SMR deployment. Supply chain vulnerabilities will be especially acute if China
accelerates commercialization and comes to dominate the supply chain for SMRs as it has for
lithium-ion batteries and solar PV. Although radioactive waste generation by SMRs has not been
thoroughly researched, one study reported that SMRs produce a greater volume of radioactive
waste than traditional larger-scale reactors, by a factor of 2 to 30, creating a need for waste
handling and disposal (Shwartz 2022). In addition, the potential exportation of SMR
technologies presents a nuclear proliferation risk.

Russia’s two 35-MW(e) floating SMRs have been operational since 2020, and China’s 200-
MW(e) capacity demonstration project has been operational since 2023. Argentina, Canada,
South Korea, and the United Kingdom are planning or developing SMRs. Denmark, South
Korea, Russia, and China are developing floating SMR designs.

U.S.-based Holtec International is developing a 300-MW SMR (IAEA 2024); it is working with
the state of Utah to develop a training facility for skilled workers in Utah, and it is planning to
produce and deploy these reactors for deployment in Utah and other western states (Kaufman
2025). Oak Ridge National Laboratory developed SMR modeling capabilities, collaborated with
NuScale to assess its technologies (NuScale 2023), and began construction on a demonstration
project in Tennessee in 2024 (Kairos Power’s Hermes low-power demonstration reactor) (IAEA
2024).

Appendix G. Synthetic Aviation Fuel
Increasing global use of synthetic aviation fuel (SAF) (also known as sustainable aviation fuel)
could lead to higher fuel costs for the U.S. Department of War (DOW) and could pose supply
chain shortfalls if SAF becomes required for DOW.

SAF, after blending with petroleum diesel, is identical to conventional petroleum diesel, as
ensured by the international standard ASTM qualification process. SAF functions as a drop-in
replacement fuel for blending with conventional jet fuel (Jet A or Jet A1 kerosene) (IATA n.d.)
and can be produced from nonpetroleum-based biomass feedstocks (e.g., waste fats, oils, and
greases, as well as agricultural and woody biomass and waste) using multiple processes (AFDC
n.d.). Although multiple production feedstocks and methods have been approved to meet
international standards (Rosales Calderon 2024a), only one is currently commercialized (Rosales
Calderon 2024b).

With the exception of some older or experimental aircraft and engines not designed to run on
conventional jet fuels, adherence to international standards ensures that SAF blends are
compatible with existing aircraft engines and fueling equipment. International standard ASTM
D7566 defines the technical requirements for SAF, including the eligible feedstocks and
pathways to produce SAF (ASTM 2022b), and includes the requirements of ASTM D1655 for
conventional jet fuels (ASTM 2022a). When SAF is blended with conventional jet fuel, SAF
must meet a list of requirements in D7566 which are designed to guarantee that SAF is identical
to the conventional jet fuels which meet D1655 petroleum jet fuel standards. SAF blends
meeting these requirements are released to the fungible fuel distribution system as jet fuel, with
no designation that it contains a SAF component. This is the case whether it is Jet A, Jet A-1, JP-
8, or JP-5. Because of these standards, different types of SAF must undergo costly and complex
certification to ensure safety, performance, and compliance with technical standards and
airworthiness requirements, as well as maintain manufacturer warranties. Aircraft and engine
manufacturers, the regulatory authorities (e.g., FAA in US and EASA in EU) of the aircraft’s
country of registration, and standards organizations (e.g., ASTM international) are all included in
the approval process (ASTM 2025). One interviewee expressed concerns that some European
military aircraft may not achieve SAF certification in time to keep pace with the civilian sector’s
transition to these fuels due to distinct national military certification regimes, the variety of
international aircraft/engine origins, and the lack of a strong regulatory mandate equivalent to the
EU's civilian targets. Furthermore, some stakeholders have expressed uncertainty regarding SAF
performance under conditions (such as high altitudes or rapid maneuvers) that military aircraft
may encounter at the extremes of their operating capability which have not yet been fully tested
and certified.

In addition, the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) established a global
framework for the regulation of international alignment around SAF (ICAO 2018; ICAO n.d.-b.).
Regardless of nations potentially adopting higher-blend targets, those blends would need to meet
international requirements and approval.

Boeing approved the use of SAF in military aircraft with military fuels such as JP-8/NATO F-34
at blends of up to 50% (Boeing 2024). In 2025, Lockheed Martin approved the use of synthetic
aviation turbine fuels in the F-35 Lightening II at blends of up to 50% based on “comprehensive

 83

technical and strategic analysis to ensure that synthetic aviation turbine fuel meets the strict
performance and reliability standards required for the F-35’s complex, high-demand missions”
(Lockheed Martin 2025). Also in 2025, Norway tested the F-35 stealth fighter using 40%
SAF/biofuels (Hoyle 2025).

Researchers are working to achieve blends of SAF up to 100% with the necessary properties to
ensure engine performance and safety. One possibility involves mixing two types of SAF with
different properties, including one with aromatics from nonpetroleum sources. National
Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) experts estimate achieving 100% SAF could take 10 years with
sufficient research and development funding and an internationally collaborative effort. New
progress includes efforts by NLR in collaboration with the Massachusetts Institute of
Technology in developing SAF that contains aromatics using plant lignin (Ringle 2025). The use
of 100% drop-in SAF probably presents little technical risk to DOW, because DOW involvement
in setting SAF requirements and blends at ASTM could prevent changes that risk fuel
incompatibility with the current aircraft fleet.

However, DOW may face higher costs for refueling where SAF blends are mandatory because
SAF costs two to three times more than conventional aviation fuel (Parolini 2025, Primrose
2025). Demand (and therefore prices) will rise in part due to E.U. mandates for aviation fuel
suppliers to provide minimum SAF blends to E.U. airports starting at 2% in 2025 and increasing
to 70% in 2050, and United Kingdom mandates for a 2% SAF minimum in 2025 increasing to
22% in 2040 (EASA 2025). E.U. subsidies for airlines purchasing SAF will also encourage use
(Abnett 2025b). As of June 2025, 17 additional countries have declared or proposed targets or
mandates (including Japan, South Korea, and Turkey), or are otherwise developing policies
(including China, the Philippines, Saudi Arabia) (Williams and Keane 2025).

Because domestic SAF production capacity does not meet current demand (Rosales Calderon
2024b), any requirement for DOW to use SAF could pose a supply risk. In 2023, imported SAF
met nearly half the 26 million gallons per year of domestic SAF consumption. (For comparison,
air carriers consumed almost 20 billion gallons of jet fuel in 2023 [DOT BTS 2025]). In 2021,
the United States announced a target of 3 billion gallons per year of domestically produced SAF
by 2030 (EASA 2025). Although six facilities in the United States were producing SAF using the
only biomass-based production process as of 2024, additional facilities producing an alternative
to petroleum-based diesel, known as renewable diesel, could be converted to produce SAF.
Further, other facilities are planned or under construction, including a facility in Washington
state that produces 50 million gallons per year of SAF (SkyNRG n.d.).

SAF can also be produced without biomass using electricity, hydrogen, and carbon dioxide
(ICAO n.d.-a.). Known as e-SAF, this fuel is produced using an ASTM-approved process and
can be used as a drop-in fuel in blends up to 50% (ICAO n.d.-a.), and work is underway toward
making e-SAF commercially available in regions such as the E.U. (SkyPower 2025), which have
limited biomass capacity relative to the United States. This aviation fuel is subject to similar
risks and regulatory measures as biomass-based SAF, although there is no biomass feedstock
limitation. Costs for e-SAF are estimated at 10 times greater than conventional fuel (Primrose
2025), but an NLR expert estimates that e-fuels and e-SAF will become more economically
feasible in 20 years. The E.U. requires suppliers to include 1.2% of aviation fuel as e-SAF
starting in 2030, gradually increasing to 35% in 2050.

 84

In the future, SAF may be developed that is compatible only with limited aircraft. If realized,
“non-drop-in” SAF presents risks of requiring a segregated fuel supply for different aircraft.

Appendix J. Methodology
This study employed a multi-method qualitative research approach. The methodology integrated
expert interviews, internal stakeholder engagement, and an extensive review of written sources.

J.1 Internal NLR Input
The research process began in November and December 2024 with two structured internal
National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) brainstorming sessions to identify specific
infrastructure, response, and transition risks over the coming two decades. These sessions
included NLR staff involved in defense-related projects as well as employees with prior military
service. The objective was to identify initial risk themes and refine the key research questions
that would be explored in subsequent phases.

J.2 External Expert Interviews
A core component of the research was a series of semi-structured interviews conducted in early
2025 with a total of 30 experts. To encourage candor in responses, interviewees were assured
that their responses would remain non-attributed.

Initial interviews were conducted with five senior NLR experts with relevant subject matter
expertise in energy systems, resilience, defense applications, or international energy policy.
Following these internal interviews, 25 external interviews were conducted with experts from
government and non-governmental sectors, identified jointly by the then-Office of the Secretary
of Defense-Policy’s Office of Arctic and Global Resilience and NLR. Most of these experts had
backgrounds in U.S. Department of War (DOW) or closely affiliated agencies. The full list of
DOW offices represented is listed in the acknowledgments section of this report. Organizations
represented outside of DOW included independent policy research organizations, academic
security centers, and think tanks focused on defense, energy, and geopolitical strategy, as well as
major energy technology firms and utility companies involved in energy infrastructure
development, advanced energy systems, and defense-related energy applications.

Interviews were guided by a standardized set of open-ended questions designed to explore
multiple dimensions of risk to the DOW mission from global advanced energy technology
adoption, sometimes referred to as the energy transition. Topics covered included geopolitical
shifts, allied and adversarial actions, evolving energy infrastructure, interoperability challenges,
and region-specific vulnerabilities. Questions asked were as follows:

 • Underrated Risks: What are the most important risks to the DOW mission from the
 global energy transition that are not receiving sufficient attention?
 • Technological Adoption Risks: How will the development and deployment of new
 energy technologies over the next 10–20 years present risks to DOW interests and
 missions?
 • Allied Nation Risks: What actions by allied partners related to new energy technologies
 could pose risks to DOW operations?
 • Non-Allied Nation Risks: What actions by non-allied nations in the energy space may
 present strategic or operational risks?
 • Infrastructure-Specific Risks: What evolving renewable energy infrastructure risks
 should receive more attention from the DOW?

 95

 • Long-Term Impact: Which infrastructure-related risks are expected to be most
 impactful to the DOW’s mission over the next two decades, and why?
 • Regional Focus – USINDOPACOM: What risks are likely to be especially acute in the
 INDOPACOM region?
 • Regional Focus – EUCOM: What risks are likely to be particularly significant in the
 EUCOM region?
 • Mitigation Strategies: What operations, activities, or investments could mitigate the
 risks discussed?
 • Additional Input: What other thoughts do you have about the energy transition’s
 potential impacts on DOW missions?
Interview data were synthesized thematically to identify recurring patterns, regional distinctions,
and gaps in current strategic awareness.

J.3 Literature Review
A review of written sources was conducted to reveal additional risk aspects and to contextualize
and expand upon information gathered during the first two phases of the project. The literature
review covered:

 • Official DOW Strategic Documents: including the 2023 Department of Defense
 Operational Energy Strategy, Department of Defense Lithium-ion Battery Strategy 2023–
 2030, 2023 Joint Risk Analysis Methodology, 2024 Arctic Strategy, and 2022 Annual
 Report: Air Force Operational Energy.
 • Prior NLR Studies: relevant internal and published work conducted by NLR on energy
 security, resilience, and defense applications.
 • Peer-Reviewed Academic Literature: research articles addressing advanced energy
 technologies, military energy systems, geopolitical risk, and technology adoption.
 • Think Tank Analyses: reports from leading national security and energy policy think
 tanks offering independent perspectives on emerging risks.
 • Open-Source News Reports: credible media sources to track recent developments in
 energy technology, international policy shifts, and military exercises relevant to energy
 infrastructure.
Together, these methods allowed for a robust triangulation of insights, combining practitioner
expertise, institutional knowledge, and documented strategic priorities to inform the final
analysis. Drafting was completed in September 2025.
