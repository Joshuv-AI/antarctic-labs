Pole Dynamics, Linearization, and Perturbations of the Satsuma–Mimura Equation
Ling-Juan Yan, Xing-Biao Hu, Ya-Jie Liu, Ying-Nan Zhang

Abstract
This paper investigates the pole dynamics and perturbation theory of algebraic soliton solutions associated with the Satsuma–Mimura (SM) equation. First, we give a qualitative analysis of the pole system associated with algebraic soliton solutions, thereby completing a point left open in [ 24 ] . We then examine three perturbations of the SM equation. The u x u_{x} perturbation preserves exact linearizability and leads to an explicit shifted algebraic soliton solution. The u x ​ x u_{xx} perturbation can be reduced to the unperturbed SM equation by a scaling transformation, which yields the corresponding pole asymptotics and soliton profile. For the genuinely nontrivial H ​ u x ​ x Hu_{xx} perturbation, we derive the first-order perturbation equations and present a short-time numerical simulation based on an explicit Euler discretization. These results clarify how algebraic solitons of the SM equation respond to different perturbative mechanisms.

2
Properties of the pole systems for the Satsuma–Mimura and Mikhailov–Novikov equations

In
[
24
]
, we derived the general polynomial solution of the linearized SM equation:

f_{N}(x,t)=\sum_{j=0}^{N}\Bigl(c_{j}\sum_{m+2n=j}\frac{x^{m}(\delta t)^{n}}{m!n!}\Bigr).

(6)

We also obtained the asymptotic expression for the zeros of
f
when
\delta=-\frac{1}{2}
as
t\to+\infty
, namely

x_{j}(t)\sim\alpha_{j}\sqrt{t}-c_{N-1},

(7)

where
\alpha_{j}
are the zeros of the
N
-th degree probabilists’ Hermite polynomial

H^{\mathrm{Prob}}_{N}(x)=N!\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor\frac{N}{2}\rfloor}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{j!(N-2j)!}\frac{x^{N-2j}}{2^{j}}.

(8)

The corresponding general polynomial solution of the MN equation is

f_{N}(x,t)=\sum_{j=0}^{N}\Bigl(c_{j}\sum_{m+2n=j}\frac{x^{m}(i\delta t)^{n}}{m!n!}\Bigr).

(9)

The following scaling observation gives the corresponding statement for a general value of
\delta

Proposition 1
.

If
x_{j}(t),\ (j=1,2,\cdots,N)
are the zeros of the polynomial
\sum_{j=0}^{N}c_{j}\sum_{m+2n=j}\frac{x^{m}(-\frac{1}{2}t)^{n}}{m!n!}
, then
\sqrt{-2\delta}\,x_{j}(t),\ (j=1,2,\cdots,N)
are the zeros of the polynomial:

(-2\delta)^{\frac{N}{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{N}\frac{c_{j}}{(-2\delta)^{j/2}}\sum_{m+2n=j}\frac{x^{m}(\delta t)^{n}}{m!n!}.

(10)

Using this proposition, we obtain the asymptotic representation of the zeros for both the MN and SM equations for a general
\delta
as
t\to+\infty
.

Theorem 1
.

For the SM equation (
1
) (
\delta<0
), the pole system
\{x_{j}(t)\}

(j=1,2,\dots,N)
of the
N
-algebraic soliton solution
u
constructed from the polynomial solution
f
of (
5
) satisfies

x_{j}(t)\sim\sqrt{-2\delta}\,\alpha_{j}\sqrt{t}+i\,\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathrm{Im}\,x_{j}(0)}{N},\quad t\to+\infty,

(11)

where
\alpha_{j}
are the zeros of
H^{\mathrm{Prob}}_{N}(x)
.

For the SM equation (
1
) (
\delta>0
),
\{x_{j}(t)\}
satisfies

x_{j}(t)\sim i\sqrt{2\delta}\,\alpha_{j}\sqrt{t}+i\,\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathrm{Im}\,x_{j}(0)}{N},\quad t\to+\infty.

(12)

For the MN equation (
2
) (
\delta<0
),
\{x_{j}(t)\}
satisfies

x_{j}(t)\sim e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}}\sqrt{-2\delta}\,\alpha_{j}\sqrt{t}+i\,\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathrm{Im}\,x_{j}(0)}{N},\quad t\to+\infty.

(13)

For the MN equation (
2
) (
\delta>0
),
\{x_{j}(t)\}
satisfies

x_{j}(t)\sim e^{i\frac{3\pi}{4}}\sqrt{2\delta}\,\alpha_{j}\sqrt{t}+i\,\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathrm{Im}\,x_{j}(0)}{N},\quad t\to+\infty.

(14)

We next complete the analysis left open in
[
24
]
by studying the pole system directly from a qualitative perspective.
It is enough to analyze the pole system of the SM equation for
\delta<0
:

\frac{dx_{j}}{dt}=-2\delta\sum_{k\neq j}\frac{1}{x_{j}-x_{k}}.

(15)

The other cases can be reduced to this one by rotation:

•

For the SM equation with
\delta>0
, set
y_{j}=e^{-i\frac{\pi}{2}}x_{j}
; then
\frac{dy_{j}}{dt}=2\delta\sum_{k\neq j}\frac{1}{y_{j}-y_{k}}
.

•

For the pole system of the MN equation
\frac{dx_{j}}{dt}=-2i\delta\sum_{k\neq j}\frac{1}{x_{j}-x_{k}}
with
\delta>0
, set
y_{j}=e^{-i\frac{3\pi}{4}}x_{j}
; then
\frac{dy_{j}}{dt}=2\delta\sum_{k\neq j}\frac{1}{y_{j}-y_{k}}
.

•

For
\frac{dx_{j}}{dt}=-2i\delta\sum_{k\neq j}\frac{1}{x_{j}-x_{k}}
with
\delta<0
, set
y_{j}=e^{-i\frac{\pi}{4}}x_{j}
; then
\frac{dy_{j}}{dt}=-2\delta\sum_{k\neq j}\frac{1}{y_{j}-y_{k}}
.

Throughout this qualitative discussion we restrict attention to the non-coalescing case. If pole coalescence occurs, the pole system ceases to provide the appropriate description and the underlying linear equation should be analyzed directly.

Lemma 1
.

The sum of the poles is conserved:

\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}(0).

(16)

Proof.

Summing (
15
) over
j=1,\dots,N
gives
\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{dx_{j}(t)}{dt}=0
, which proves the assertion.
∎

Lemma 2
.

At any
t_{0}>0
, let
x_{l}
be the pole with the smallest imaginary part and
x_{k}
the pole with the largest imaginary part. Then

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d\mathrm{Im}\,x_{l}}{dt}\right|_{t=t_{0}}

\displaystyle\geq 0,

(17)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d\mathrm{Im}\,x_{k}}{dt}\right|_{t=t_{0}}

\displaystyle\leq 0.

(18)

Thus the lowest pole cannot move downward, while the highest pole cannot move upward.

Proof.

From (
15
),

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d\mathrm{Im}\,x_{l}}{dt}\right|_{t=t_{0}}

\displaystyle=\left.-2\delta\sum_{j\neq l}\frac{\mathrm{Im}\,x_{j}-\mathrm{Im}\,x_{l}}{|x_{l}-x_{j}|^{2}}\right|_{t=t_{0}}\geq 0,

(19)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d\mathrm{Im}\,x_{k}}{dt}\right|_{t=t_{0}}

\displaystyle=\left.-2\delta\sum_{j\neq k}\frac{\mathrm{Im}\,x_{j}-\mathrm{Im}\,x_{k}}{|x_{k}-x_{j}|^{2}}\right|_{t=t_{0}}\leq 0.

(20)

This proves the claim.
∎

Lemma 3
.

At time
t
, label the poles by increasing real part as
x_{1}(t),x_{2}(t),\dots,x_{N}(t)
(equal real parts are allowed; the assignment of indices to specific poles may vary with time). Let
d_{j}=|\mathrm{Re}\,x_{j+1}(0)-\mathrm{Re}\,x_{j}(0)|
. Then

|\mathrm{Re}\,(x_{j+1}(t)-x_{j}(t))|\leq\sqrt{-8\delta(N-1)t+d_{j}^{2}}.

(21)

Proof.

From the equations for
x_{j}
and
x_{j+1}
,

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathrm{Re}\,x_{j+1}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle\leq-2\delta(N-1)\frac{1}{\mathrm{Re}\,(x_{j+1}(t)-x_{j}(t))},

(22)

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathrm{Re}\,x_{j}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle\geq 2\delta(N-1)\frac{1}{\mathrm{Re}\,(x_{j+1}(t)-x_{j}(t))}.

(23)

Therefore,

\frac{d(\mathrm{Re}\,x_{j+1}(t)-\mathrm{Re}\,x_{j}(t))}{dt}\leq-4\delta(N-1)\frac{1}{\mathrm{Re}\,(x_{j+1}(t)-x_{j}(t))}.

(24)

Integrating with respect to
t
gives the stated bound.
∎

Theorem 2
.

Let
L=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathrm{Im}\,x_{j}(0)
. Then

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\mathrm{Im}\,x_{j}(t)=L,\quad j=1,\dots,N.

(25)

Proof.

Set
y(t)=\max\{|\mathrm{Im}\,x_{j}(t)-\mathrm{Im}\,x_{k}(t)|:j,k=1,\dots,N\}
and
d=\max\{d_{j}:j=1,\dots,N-1\}
.
By Lemma
2
,
y(t)
is non-increasing. Then

\displaystyle\frac{dy(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\delta\,y(t)}{{y(0)}^{2}+\bigl((N-1)\max\{\mathrm{Re}\,(x_{j+1}(t)-x_{j}(t))\}\bigr)^{2}}

(26)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\delta\,y(t)}{{y(0)}^{2}+(N-1)^{2}\bigl(d^{2}-8\delta(N-1)t\bigr)}.

Since
y(t)\geq 0
, integration gives
\lim_{t\to+\infty}\ln y(t)=-\infty
, hence
\lim_{t\to+\infty}y(t)=0
.
∎

Theorem 3
.

For
x_{1}
and
x_{N}
(defined as in Lemma
3
),

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}\mathrm{Re}\,x_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=-\infty,

(27)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}\mathrm{Re}\,x_{N}(t)

\displaystyle=+\infty.

(28)

Proof.

Let
y(0)=\max\{|\mathrm{Im}\,x_{j}(0)-\mathrm{Im}\,x_{k}(0)|\}
and
c=\frac{{y(0)}^{2}}{|\mathrm{Re}\,x_{N}(0)-\mathrm{Re}\,x_{1}(0)|}
.
Set
x(t)=\mathrm{Re}\,x_{N}(t)-\mathrm{Re}\,x_{1}(t)
. Then

\frac{dx(t)}{dt}\geq-2\delta\frac{x(t)}{{y(0)}^{2}+{x(t)}^{2}}\geq-2\delta\frac{1}{c+x(t)},

(29)

which yields
x(t)\geq\sqrt{-4\delta t+(c+x(0))^{2}}-c
.
If either
x_{1}(t)
or
x_{N}(t)
remained bounded, Lemma
1
would be contradicted. Hence the limits hold.
∎

For the SM equation (
1
) with
\delta<0
, Theorem
2
implies that as
t\to+\infty
all poles converge to a horizontal line, while Theorem
3
shows that the leftmost and rightmost poles tend to
-\infty
and
+\infty
, respectively. Rotating back gives the pole behavior in the remaining three cases:

•

For the SM equation with
\delta>0
, the poles approach the vertical line
x=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathrm{Re}\,x_{j}(0)
.

•

For the MN equation with
\delta>0
, the poles approach the line
y=-x+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}(\mathrm{Re}\,x_{j}(0)+\mathrm{Im}\,x_{j}(0))
.

•

For the MN equation with
\delta<0
, the poles approach the line
y=x+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}(\mathrm{Im}\,x_{j}(0)-\mathrm{Re}\,x_{j}(0))
.

In all cases, the poles on the two sides of the limiting line move to infinity along that line; hence the pole set is unbounded.

These pole dynamics are reflected directly in the corresponding algebraic soliton solutions. For the SM equation (
1
) (
\delta<0
), the algebraic soliton solution
u
constructed from the polynomial
f_{N}
evolves as
t\to+\infty
into
N
waves of equal amplitude, moving symmetrically about the origin.

3
Review of methods for perturbed solitons

Before turning to particular perturbations of the SM equation, we recall two standard perturbative approaches for soliton equations.

Keener and McLaughlin
[
9
]
developed a Green-function method for soliton perturbations. For a nonlinear equation

\partial_{t}u+K(u)=\epsilon f(u),\quad 0\leq\epsilon\leq 1,

(30)

where
K
is a nonlinear operator. One assumes

u\simeq u_{0}+\epsilon u_{1}+\cdots,

(31)

with
u_{0}
satisfying

\partial_{t}u_{0}+K(u_{0})=0.

(32)

Then
u_{1}
satisfies

(L(u_{0}))u_{1}\equiv\partial_{t}u_{1}+(\delta K(u_{0}))u_{1}=f(u_{0}),\quad u_{1}|_{t=0}=0,

(33)

where
(\delta K(u_{0}))u_{1}=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{K(u_{0}+\epsilon u_{1})-K(u_{0})}{\epsilon}
. A Green function can then be used to invert
L(u_{0})
. Define the linear operator
\hat{G}(t,t^{\prime})
from a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
to itself by

\displaystyle(L(u_{0}))\hat{G}\equiv\partial_{t}\hat{G}+(\delta K(u_{0}))\hat{G}=0,\quad 0<t^{\prime}<t,

(34)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to t^{\prime}}\hat{G}(t,t^{\prime})=I.

Let
G(x,t|x^{\prime},t^{\prime})
be the kernel of
\hat{G}(t,t^{\prime})
. Then
u_{1}
can be written as

u_{1}(x,t)=\int_{0}^{t}\hat{G}(t,t^{\prime})f(u_{0}(\cdot,t^{\prime}))\,dt^{\prime}=\int_{0}^{t}\bigl(G(x,t|\cdot,t^{\prime}),f(u_{0}(\cdot,t^{\prime}))\bigr)\,dt^{\prime},

(35)

where
(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the
L^{2}
inner product. To keep
u_{1}
bounded over time scales
O(1/\epsilon)
, i.e., for fixed
\tau
,

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\epsilon u_{1}(\cdot,\tau/\epsilon)=0,

(36)

one usually allows the parameters of
u_{0}
to depend on
\epsilon t
. Write
u_{0}(x,t,\epsilon t)
; then
u_{1}
satisfies

\displaystyle(L(u_{0}))u_{1}=F(u_{0}),

(37)

\displaystyle F(u_{0})=f(u_{0})-\frac{1}{\epsilon}\bigl(\partial_{t}u_{0}+K(u_{0})\bigr),

and

u_{1}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\bigl(\hat{G}(t,t^{\prime})F(u_{0}(t^{\prime}))\bigr)(x)\,dt^{\prime}.

(38)

The problem is therefore reduced to choosing the slow evolution of the parameters so that (
36
) holds.

In this approach, an explicit expression for
\hat{G}
is needed. Setting
\tilde{K}(u_{0})=\delta K(u_{0})
and
\tilde{K}^{\prime}
by
(f,\tilde{K}g)=-(\tilde{K}^{\prime}f,g)
, for inverse-scattering-solvable equations satisfying
\tilde{K}^{\prime}(u_{0})=\tilde{K}(u_{0})

[
9
,
8
]
, one splits
\hat{G}(t,t^{\prime})=\hat{G}_{d}(t,t^{\prime})+\hat{G}_{c}(t,t^{\prime})
, where
\hat{G}_{d}
projects onto the discrete subspace spanned by the soliton parameters. For an
N
-soliton solution
u_{0}
with
2N
free parameters
\{p_{j}\}
, the set

\Bigl\{\frac{\partial u_{0}}{\partial p_{j}},\ j=1,\dots,2N\Bigr\}

(39)

spans this discrete subspace. The kernel of
\hat{G}_{d}(t,t^{\prime})
then has the form

G_{d}(x,t|x^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\sum_{j=1}^{2N}A_{j}(x,t)\frac{\partial u_{0}}{\partial p_{j}}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}).

(40)

For large
t^{\prime}
, the dependence of
u_{0}
on
x^{\prime}
and
t^{\prime}
is through combinations
x^{\prime}-c_{j}t^{\prime}
, so for large
t
,
u_{1}
behaves roughly as
\iint\mathcal{F}(x^{\prime}-c_{j}t^{\prime})\,dx^{\prime}\,dt^{\prime}
. To avoid secular growth, one requires
\int\mathcal{F}(x^{\prime}-c_{j}t^{\prime})\,dx^{\prime}=0
, i.e.,
F(u_{0})
to be orthogonal to the discrete subspace (
39
). This gives

\Bigl(\frac{\partial u_{0}}{\partial p_{k}},f(u_{0})-\sum_{j=1}^{2N}\frac{\partial p_{j}}{\partial\tau}\frac{\partial u_{0}}{\partial p_{j}}\Bigr)=0,\quad k=1,\dots,2N,

(41)

with
\tau=\epsilon t
.

For cases in which
\tilde{K}^{\prime}(u_{0})\neq\tilde{K}(u_{0})
, Tanaka
[
23
,
13
]
introduced a more direct method. Continuing to consider (
30
), assume

u(x,t)=u_{0}(x,t,t_{1})+\epsilon u_{1}(x,t,t_{1})+\cdots,\quad t_{1}=\epsilon t,

(42)

with
u_{0}
satisfying
\partial_{t}u_{0}+K(u_{0})=0
. Define
L(u_{0})=\partial_{t}+\delta K(u_{0})
; then

(L(u_{0}))u_{1}=f(u_{0})-\frac{\partial u_{0}}{\partial t_{1}}.

(43)

Let
L^{*}=\partial_{t}+(\delta K(u_{0}))^{\prime}
be the adjoint operator. If a function
g
satisfies

L^{*}(g)=0,

(44)

then combining with the equation for
u_{1}
gives

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Bigl(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}gu_{1}\,dx\Bigr)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g\cdot\Bigl(f(u_{0})-\frac{\partial u_{0}}{\partial t_{1}}\Bigr)\,dx.

(45)

When
u_{0}
is a soliton solution and
g
depends on
u_{0}
, the right-hand side becomes time-independent for large
t
. If it were nonzero,
\int gu_{1}dx
would grow linearly in
t
, which would be inconsistent with a regular perturbation expansion. Hence one imposes

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g\cdot\Bigl(f(u_{0})-\frac{\partial u_{0}}{\partial t_{1}}\Bigr)\,dx=0.

(46)

For an
N
-soliton solution, if
2N
independent solutions
g
of (
44
) can be found, they determine the slow evolution of the
2N
parameters of
u_{0}
with respect to
t_{1}
.

4
Perturbations of the Satsuma–Mimura equation

In the approaches recalled above, the crucial step is to obtain
L^{*}(u_{0})
and solve

(L^{*}(u_{0}))g=0.

(47)

For the SM equation (
1
), a direct computation gives

L^{*}(u_{0})=\partial_{t}-\delta\bigl(-\partial_{x}^{2}-(Hu_{0})\partial_{x}+H(u_{0}\partial_{x})\bigr),

(48)

so the equation for
g
is

g_{t}=\delta\bigl(-g_{xx}-g_{x}Hu_{0}+H(u_{0}g_{x})\bigr).

(49)

Solving (
49
) directly is difficult. In the following three subsections, we instead combine the linearized equation with direct numerical computation to examine perturbations of the form
u_{x}
,
u_{xx}
, and
Hu_{xx}
.

4.1
The
u_{x}
perturbation

Consider the perturbed equation

u_{t}=\delta(u_{xx}-(uHu)_{x})+\epsilon u_{x}.

(50)

Using the transformation
u=i\bigl(\ln\frac{f^{*}}{f}\bigr)_{x}
, we obtain the linearized equation

f_{t}=\delta f_{xx}+\epsilon f_{x}.

(51)

Let
f=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\bigl(x-x_{j}(t)-\epsilon x_{1,j}(t)\bigr)
. The pole system becomes

\frac{dx_{j}}{dt}+\epsilon\frac{dx_{1,j}}{dt}=-2\delta\sum_{k\neq j}\frac{1}{x_{j}-x_{k}+\epsilon(x_{1,j}-x_{1,k})}-\epsilon.

(52)

Since the
x_{j}
satisfy the unperturbed pole system

\frac{dx_{j}}{dt}=-2\delta\sum_{k\neq j}\frac{1}{x_{j}-x_{k}},

(53)

and
x_{1,j}(0)=0
, we obtain
x_{1,j}(t)=-t
, hence
f=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\bigl(x-x_{j}(t)+\epsilon t\bigr)
.

Equivalently, the change of variables
t=t^{\prime},\ x=x^{\prime}-\epsilon t^{\prime}
transforms
f_{t}-\epsilon f_{x}=\delta f_{xx}
into
f_{t^{\prime}}=\delta f_{x^{\prime}x^{\prime}}
. The solution of the latter equation is
\prod_{j=1}^{N}(x^{\prime}-x_{j}(t^{\prime}))
, so the solution of the former is
\prod_{j=1}^{N}(x+\epsilon t-x_{j}(t))
.

The corresponding algebraic soliton solution is

u(x,t)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{2\,\mathrm{Im}\,x_{j}(t)}{(x-\mathrm{Re}\,x_{j}(t)+\epsilon t)^{2}+(\mathrm{Im}\,x_{j}(t))^{2}},

(54)

where
x_{j}(t)
are the poles of the unperturbed system.

4.2
The
u_{xx}
perturbation

We next consider

u_{t}=(\delta+\epsilon)u_{xx}-\delta(uHu)_{x}.

(55)

Set
x=kx^{\prime}
with
k=\frac{\delta+\epsilon}{\delta}
; the equation becomes

u_{t}=\frac{\delta^{2}}{\delta+\epsilon}\bigl(u_{x^{\prime}x^{\prime}}-(uHu)_{x^{\prime}}\bigr).

(56)

By Proposition
10
, the zeros
x^{\prime}_{j}(t)
of (
56
) have the asymptotic form

x^{\prime}_{j}(t)\sim\sqrt{-2\frac{\delta^{2}}{\delta+\epsilon}}\,\alpha_{j}\sqrt{t}+i\,\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathrm{Im}\,x^{\prime}_{j}(0)}{N}.

(57)

Therefore the poles of (
55
) satisfy

\begin{split}x_{j}(t)&\sim\frac{\delta+\epsilon}{\delta}\Bigl(\sqrt{-2\frac{\delta^{2}}{\delta+\epsilon}}\,\alpha_{j}\sqrt{t}+i\,\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathrm{Im}\,x^{\prime}_{j}(0)}{N}\Bigr)\\
&\sim\sqrt{\frac{\delta+\epsilon}{\delta}}\,\sqrt{-2\delta}\,\alpha_{j}\sqrt{t}+i\,\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathrm{Im}\,x_{j}(0)}{N}\\
&\sim\Bigl(1+\frac{\epsilon}{2\delta}\Bigr)\sqrt{-2\delta}\,\alpha_{j}\sqrt{t}+i\,\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathrm{Im}\,x_{j}(0)}{N}.\end{split}

(58)

For large
t
, the algebraic soliton solution behaves like

u(x,t)\sim\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{2\,\mathrm{Im}\,x_{j}(t)}{\bigl(x-(1+\frac{\epsilon}{2\delta})\mathrm{Re}\,x_{j}(t)\bigr)^{2}+(\mathrm{Im}\,x_{j}(t))^{2}}.

(59)

A more precise description, avoiding reliance on the asymptotic formula alone, is as follows. Consider the linearized SM equation. Suppose its initial condition is
f(x,0)=\prod_{j=1}^{N}(x-x_{j}(0))
. If we modify this initial condition to

f(x,0)=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\left(x-\sqrt{\frac{\delta}{\delta+\epsilon}}\,x_{j}(0)\right),

(60)

then let
\tilde{x}_{j}(t)
(
j=1,\dots,N
) be the poles of the SM equation obtained from this modified initial condition. The poles
x^{\prime}_{j}(t)
of equation (
56
) are then given by

5
Conclusion

We have analyzed the pole dynamics of the Satsuma–Mimura equation through the associated pole system and then used this structure to study three perturbations of the SM equation. For the
u_{x}
perturbation, exact linearizability is preserved by a simple shift transformation, which gives an explicit algebraic soliton solution. For the
u_{xx}
perturbation, a scaling transformation reduces the perturbed equation to the unperturbed SM equation and yields the asymptotic pole locations and soliton profile. For the non-linearizable
Hu_{xx}
perturbation, we derived the first-order perturbation equations and carried out a short-time simulation using an explicit Euler scheme. These results show how algebraic solitons respond to different perturbative mechanisms and provide a basis for further analytical and numerical work.
