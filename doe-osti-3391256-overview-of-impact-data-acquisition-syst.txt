 ABSTRACT
 This report documents the development of the data acquisition system (DAS)
and data reduction methodologies for the Irradiated Material Property
Accelerated Characterization Test (IMPACT) experiment at the Advanced Test
Reactor (ATR). The IMPACT experiment was designed to enable in-pile
measurement of thermal conductivity in metallic nuclear fuels, specifically U-
10Zr, using an instrumented thermal conductivity probe.
 The DAS supports both passive temperature monitoring and active thermal
interrogation of the probe through controlled alternating current (AC) and direct
current (DC) excitation. Significant modifications to laboratory-scale systems
were required to accommodate the higher resistance paths associated with the in-
pile application. Custom electronics and relay-controlled measurement
sequencing were developed to enable the measurement capability and sufficient
power delivery to the sensing region.
 A reduced-order, axisymmetric thermal model based on the thermal
quadrupoles method is presented to support data interpretation. This model
enables efficient evaluation of transient heat transfer behavior and facilitates
solution of the inverse problem required to extract thermal properties from
measured signals. Multiple boundary condition formulations are discussed to
address varying experimental timescales and geometries.
 Additionally, machine learning (ML) techniques are introduced to support
data reduction and improve confidence in inverse solutions. Convolutional neural
networks are applied to identify the presence of gas gaps and other evolving
geometric features that significantly impact thermal response during irradiation.
These efforts contribute to the broader integration of digital twin (DT)
frameworks and real-time modeling capabilities within the Advanced Fuels
Campaign (AFC).

 1. INTRODUCTION
 The Irradiated Material Property Accelerated Characterization Test (IMPACT) began in fiscal year
2022 as a Laboratory Directed Research and Development (LDRD) project. The original proposal scope
of the project was to perform a first-of-a-kind instrumented drop-in experiment in the Advanced Test
Reactor (ATR). This new type of irradiation experiment would require new penetrations in ATR’s top
head closure plate so as to allow for easy routing of the instrumentation leads out of the reactor. At the
time of the project’s initiation, the new top head closure plate was being fabricated but had not yet been
installed. The proposed experiment would utilize a thermal conductivity sensor previously developed by
the Advanced Sensors and Instrumentation (ASI) program to measure the thermal conductivity of metallic
fuel U10Zr. The thermal conductivity of U10Zr evolves as a function of burnup, due to the fission-gas-
induced porosity that occurs. For early burnup, the thermal conductivity degrades with porosity; however,
for sodium-bonded designs (sodium inside of the cladding), the thermal conductivity increases when the
porosity reaches the point that the pores interconnect and thus allows sodium to infiltrate the fuel. This
process has never been measured in-pile. Post-irradiation examination has provided much of the current
understanding. The original scope of the LDRD project included advanced modeling and machine
learning (ML) techniques to improve data analysis and interpretation. Recent advancements in ML and
artificial intelligence (AI) have exacerbated the need for more data that can be provided to these models.
 The irradiation experiment had reached mid-assembly by the end of the LDRD project budget and
timeline (end of fiscal year 2024). At the beginning of fiscal year 2025, the project was adopted by the
Advanced Fuels Campaign (AFC) and the ASI program, who jointly funded the remaining scope so as to
complete the irradiation experiment. AFC funded the remaining reactor experiment hardware and
assembly activities, while the ASI funding focused on the data acquisition system (DAS) and sensor-
related activities. This report documents the ASI-funded activities that were conducted to establish the
DAS capability to read all instrumentation, thereby enabling it to perform active interrogation of the
thermal conductivity sensor. As part of this work, additional scope was included for integrating ML
techniques to carry out reduction of the active measurement data, as will be briefly discussed later in this
report.
 1.1. Design Overview
 Though a more detailed description of the experiment design is provided by Downey et al. [1], an
overview will be given here so as to support discussion in the context of the instrumentation integration
and data acquisition. The experiment was designed for ATR’s B3 position, which is classified as a “small
B position” and is located just north of the Southeast lobe of the reactor [2]. The experiment contains four
experiment capsules, three of which are fueled. The fourth capsule contains a stainless-steel reference
material. The three fueled specimens correspond to a traditional “sodium-bonded” fuel rod design, an
annular fuel sodium-free rodlet, and a slotted fuel rodlet. These capsules were vertically stacked in the
experiment and then welded together by the bulkhead at the top of each capsule.
 A thermal conductivity probe (discussed in detail in the following sections) was located at the
centerline of the fuel specimens inside each capsule. Each fuel sample had a hole drilled into its center to
accommodate the sensor. The fuel was then contained inside a cladding tube welded to the capsule
bulkhead. A thin sodium layer, which melts during irradiation, afforded a good thermal connection
between the cladding and a larger inner-capsule housing. The inner capsule had slots on its outer radius to
act as a conduit for the sensors pertaining to the capsules located lower in the test train. Two of the slots
held thermocouples for measuring boundary condition temperatures: (1) a single-point thermocouple for

 1

monitoring the sodium-bonded capsule, and (2) a multipoint (3 points) thermocouple covering a
measurement location on each of the three fueled capsules. The irradiation temperature was largely set by
the heat generation rate in the fuel based on the fission and the thickness of the gas gap between the inner
capsule and the outer capsule.

 1.2. Thermal Conductivity Probe Design
 The thermal conductivity probe is a custom mineral-insulated metal-sheathed probe capable of high-
temperature operation and high radiation resistance. For deployment in the IMPACT experiment, the
conductors were selected as type N thermoelements, the sheath as niobium, and the insulation as
magnesium oxide. The probe had four conductors (two type N+ and two type N-), which transitioned to
two wires in the measurement region of the probe. At this transition point, the positive leads were welded
together, as were the negative leads. Finally, at the tip of the probe, representing the end of the
measurement region, the positive and negative leads are welded together. Figure 2 shows a diagram of the
probe and how it is utilized in the conceptual interrogation method.
 Two thermal property measurement methodologies were envisioned for application in the IMPACT
experiment. The passive measurement technique utilizes the thermal conductivity probe as a regular type-
N thermocouple, in conjunction with a boundary condition thermocouple. The thermal conductivity of the
fuel can be calculated directly by knowing the heat generation rate and these temperature measurements.
 The active thermal property measurement technique is based on a modified line heat source method.
Previous work has established that the probe can be accurately modeled using an equivalent analytic heat
transfer model that can be simplified to an axisymmetric geometry with little loss of fidelity as compared
to a 3D finite element model. The following section discusses in detail the thermal model used in this
study.
 The probe functions by constantly exciting the leads via a small alternating current (AC); while
meanwhile, a large direct current (DC) is applied to generate heat in the probe. The AC is used as a
temperature monitor while the system is heating, since the thermocouple cannot be read while a voltage is
being applied. The resistance of the thermoelements changes as a function of temperature; therefore, a
measurement of the AC voltage is representative of the temperature. During the heating phase, the DC is
measured and the DC voltages at the voltage tap locations are recorded. This enables calculation of the
power deposited in the two-wire section. After the heating is turned off, the probe slowly cools back to
the steady-state condition it was at prior to the heating. During this cooldown period, the AC signal is still
recorded. At the same time, the thermocouple is read, but through a low-pass filter. This low-pass filter
removes the AC signal, allowing the voltage generated via the Seebeck effect to be read by a
thermocouple reader. This provides simultaneous AC voltage and thermocouple readings during the
cooldown, from which a calibration curve is produced to evaluate the temperature during the heat up.

 2. THERMAL MODEL
 This section details the thermal models used for evaluating thermal properties from the experimental
data. Presented is an analytic model based on an axisymmetric, transient, thermal quadrupoles solution.
An equivalent model was established to approximate the probe behavior as an axisymmetric solution.
Three reasonable boundary conditions are presented for the modeling scenario, along with a brief
discussion on how to apply each of them.

 3. DATA ACQUISITION OVERVIEW
 The DAS shown in Figure 2 was representative of the system used to interrogate the thermal
conductivity probe in a laboratory setting; however, for in-pile applications, significant changes were
required to accommodate the longer lead lengths, as well as the impedance they entail. Additionally, the
system shown in Figure 2 is only used for “active” measurement. When not performing active
measurement, the probe behaves as a traditional thermocouple by providing a centerline temperature
reading. A diagram of this system is given in Figure 4.

Figure 4. Diagram of the data acquisition control system for the IMPACT experiment.

 11

 The DAS is programmed to control a series of relays to power supplies, enabling automatic switching
between passive and active measurements at prescribed intervals. When the active measurement system is
engaged, the system performs the active measurement sequentially, sensor by sensor, per a user-defined
pre-record time, heating time, and cooling time. The user can also define the output of both the AC and
DC for the measurement and heating, respectively.

 This interrogation system required significant development of custom electronics to enable this in-
pile application. This is primarily due to the long lead lengths required for implementation. Additionally,
type N thermocouple wire was used up until the point where the instrumentation exited the top head
closure plate of the ATR vessel. At that location, a cold junction is made to copper conductors, and this
temperature is monitored via a platinum resistance temperature detector. Furthermore, it is necessary to
use mineral-insulated cable until the leads are a few feet above the top of the active fuel prior to
transitioning to fiber-glass-insulated conductors. As a result, a large resistance is created between the
electronics and the measurement section of the thermal conductivity probe. The first problem encountered
is that significant power is deposited throughout the lead lengths. As a result, the voltage is increased to
compensate for this so as to maintain the same level of power generated in the measurement section. This
increased voltage causes the common-mode voltage between the voltage taps on the probe to exceed what
is acceptable for most conventional instrumentation. To mitigate this problem, a custom signal
conditioner was designed and fabricated to handle this higher input voltage. A custom signal generator
was also required in order to mix a high-voltage DC and AC signal, and is also capable of generating a
high current signal. Rendered images of these custom electronics are shown in Figure 5, along with the
as-built DAS cabinet prior to installation at ATR.

 4. MACHINE LEARNING, DIGITAL TWINS, AND ARTIFICIAL
 INTELLIGENCE
 As discussed in the Introduction section, this project included digital twin (DT) and ML facets going
back all the way to the proposal stage of the project, including some pre-irradiation testing of certain
models [3]. As the project has progressed, the roles of ML, DTs, and AI have been adapted and expanded
in some areas. The AFC has significantly expanded this scope as regards the IMPACT experiment. AFC
efforts have established links from the Nuclear Data Management and Analysis System to Idaho National
Laboratory’s high-performance computing resources. This has enabled the use of Nuclear Energy
Advanced Modeling and Simulation (NEAMS) tools to perform real-time modeling of experiment
conditions obtained from in situ instrumentation and reactor conditions. This has been setting the
framework for future experiments in which feedback from the real-time modeling can provide an input to
experiment to influence irradiation conditions, essentially “closing the loop” on modeling and
experimental conditions. It is easy to imagine many applications in which an AI layer interprets modeling
results prior to providing feedback to the experiment, thereby adding a much more nuanced and complex
control system informed by both live instrumentation and state-of-the-art NEAMS modeling.
 The ASI program’s ML efforts in the context of the IMPACT experiment have focused on
instrumentation performance and measurement uncertainty. This effort has specifically been focused on
the inverse heat transfer problem in processing the data collected via active measurements from the
thermal conductivity probe. In general, inverse problems can be challenging in terms of proving that the
measurement has sensitivity to the properties being measured. Typically, this is done by calculating
sensitivities from an analytic or finite element model to demonstrate there is sensitivity to the unknown
parameter within the measurement range. For problems encompassing multiple unknown parameters, the
solution must be shown to be unique. This is typically done by demonstrating that the thermal response is
governed by the different unknown parameters at different timescales. Those dimensions or properties
that are located closer to the heat source will influence the thermal response earlier on during a transient
response, and those further away will influence it later on. Having multiple unknown parameters that
significantly impact the thermal response at a given timescale can make it hard to demonstrate a unique
solution for the values determined. This time response is largely governed by the Fourier number(s) of the
thermal system. For multilayered models, each layer will have its own Fourier number, given by:

 𝑎𝑎 𝑡𝑡

where 𝑎𝑎𝑖𝑖 is the thermal diffusivity of the layer and 𝐿𝐿𝑖𝑖 is the length scale for the layer. The squared
dependence on the layer length scale in Equation 38 makes the time response more sensitive to geometric
parameters than to thermal parameters. This presents a unique problem for experiments involving
changing geometry. Specifically, the challenge presents itself in the fuel layer in the IMPACT geometry.
Metallic fuel expands significantly under irradiation. Thus, metallic fuel rod designs include significant
space radially and axially so the fuel can expand into the extra space as the rod is irradiated. This is the
reason for the free space within the rodlet, shown in Figure 1. As the fuel burnup progresses, this fuel will
swell and close the radial gaps between the fuel and the cladding. This necessitates a significant change in
the appropriate thermal model, as the helium gas gap can significantly affect thermal transport from the
fuel.
 Identifying the presence of this higher thermal resistance layer has been the focus of the ML efforts
conducted under the ASI program in regard to IMPACT. A training dataset has been generated using the
analytic models presented. From this training dataset, a convolution neural network was established to
identify whether the gas gap should be modeled as being present in the data reduction process of the
inverse problem. This work is ongoing and the results obtained via the training dataset are being

 5. SUMMARY
 This work describes the design, implementation, and modeling framework for the data acquisition and
data reduction systems supporting the IMPACT irradiation experiment. The primary objective is to enable
in-pile measurement of thermal conductivity in metallic fuels by using an actively interrogated thermal
conductivity probe. A DAS was developed to accommodate the unique challenges associated with
deploying this instrument in-core. The system enables automated switching between passive
thermocouple measurements and active heating cycles, all while compensating for signal degradation and
power losses stemming from long instrumentation leads and high-resistance paths. Custom electronics
were implemented to ensure accurate, repeatable probe operation.
 To interpret the measured data, an analytic thermal model was developed based on an equivalent
axisymmetric representation of the probe and surrounding materials. The thermal quadrupoles approach
allows efficient simulation of transient heat transfer and provides a practical framework for solving the
inverse problem required to determine material thermal properties. Multiple boundary condition
treatments were evaluated to balance model fidelity and computational efficiency.
 ML methods were incorporated to enhance the data reduction process, particularly in terms of
addressing uncertainties associated with evolving fuel geometry and thermal contact conditions. These
approaches support identification of key features (e.g., gas gaps) that influence thermal response and
solution uniqueness. These efforts are ongoing and will continue throughout the current fiscal year.
Overall, the integration of advanced instrumentation, physics-based modeling, and ML establishes a
foundation for high-fidelity, real-time characterization of irradiated materials. This capability will support
future efforts directed toward closed-loop experimentation and DT-enabled control of irradiation
conditions.
