Subclinical cholestasis is a hallmark of gut dysbiosis causing resistance to cancer immunotherapy
Authors: Anne-Laure Mallard de La Varende, Ai-Ling Tian, Simon Thomas, Imran Lahmar, Meriem Messaoudene, Sijing Li, Omar Motiño, Hortense Guillaume-dit-Taunière, Valerio Iebba, Yoan Hurtado, Thao-Nguyen Pham, Cassandra Thélémaque, Miguel Araujo-Voces, Deborah Suissa, Pierre Ly, Ella Reich, Giacomo Vitali, Bryan Thierry Arlunno, Sylvere Durand, Fanny Aprahamian, Marion Leduc, Sabrina Forveille, Oliver Kepp, Carlos de la Calle-Fabregat, Angela Schippers, Norbert Wagner, Pierre-Edouard Fournier, Kenya Honda, Federica Marmorino, Chiara Cremolini, Saman Maleki Vareki, John G Lenehan, Aurélien Marabelle, François-Xavier Danlos, Margaux Deligne, Caroline Truntzer, François Ghiringhelli, Anne H Ree, Paula A Bousquet, Sebastian Meltzer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryGut dysbiosis compromises cancer immunosurveillance by downregulating ileal mucosal addressin cell adhesion molecule 1 (MAdCAM-1), but the metabolic landscape associated with gut dysbiosis remains elusive. Here, we show that antibiotics (ABX) or ABX-associated Enterocloster species lead to the loss of secondary bile acids (BAs) including deoxycholic acid (DCA) and the accumulation of tauro-conjugated primary BAs (tauro-chenodeoxycholic acid [TCDCA] and tauro-β-muricholic acid [T-βMCA]) from the alternative pathway in the plasma of patients and mice. Fecal microbial transplantation (FMT), the ileum-specific farnesoid X receptor (FXR) agonist fexaramine, or glycodeoxycholic acid (GDCA) compensated dysbiosis-associated BA abnormalities and circumvent primary resistance to PD-1 blockade. GDCA curtailed ABX-induced MAdCAM-1 downregulation and T cell exhaustion in tumors. Subclinical cholestasis defined by elevation of γ-glutamyl transferase (γGT) correlated with increased TCDCA and decreased soluble MAdCAM-1 in plasma and predicted poor survival in multivariate analyses in six cohorts of patients who received immunotherapy. Hence, subclinical cholestasis accompanies gut dysbiosis, paving the way to immunoresistance.Keywords: cancer, PD-1, immune checkpoint blockade, bile acids, mucosal addressin cell adhesion molecule 1, MAdCAM-1, antibiotics, gut microbiota, microbiome, immunotherapy

Introduction

Among metabolic pathways, the lipid metabolism has emerged as a key determinant of T cell responses, with extracellular lipid uptake contributing to their activation status, differentiation, and adaptation to environmental stress.1,2,3 Bile acids (BAs) are abundant cholesterol-derived metabolites essential for lipid absorption. Primary BAs such as cholic acid (CA) and chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA) are synthesized in the liver from cholesterol via the classical pathway under the action of hepatic enzymes (CYP7A1 and CYP8B1) and conjugated with taurine, glycine or other amino acids and then excreted into the small intestine.1 Under physiological conditions, the liver-specific classical pathway is dominant, while the alternative (or acidic) pathway dependent on the enzymes CYP27A1 and CYP7B1 is marginal and not liver specific.1 BA composition is determined by not only the activity of hepatic microsomal enzymes but also that of microbial enzymes in the small and large intestines.2 Bacterial bile salt hydrolases (BSHs) mediate the deconjugation of BAs, whereas bacterial hydroxysteroid dehydrogenases (HSDHs) mediate oxidation and epimerization of the 3-, 7-, and 12-hydroxy groups of BA. Liver-derived BAs are further modified by the gut microbiota to generate intestinal secondary BAs, such as CA-derived deoxycholic acid (DCA), CDCA-derived lithocholic acid (LCA), and ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA).1 In mice, but not humans, an additional C6 hydroxylation of CDCA (by murine Cyp2c70) gives rise to muricholic acids (MCAs) and gut microbiota-modified derivatives.1 In germ-free mice, the absence of microbiota leads to an increase in the BA pool and farnesoid X receptor (FXR) antagonists, including tauro-βMCA (T-βMCA).1

These hepatic and intestinal BAs along with their conjugated or hydroxylated derivatives profoundly influence T cell functions. Intestinal BAs regulate Th17 and peripheral Foxp3+ regulatory T cell differentiation directly or indirectly through dendritic cells.3,4,5,6,7 Liver cancers exhibit elevated levels of BAs in their parenchyma, presumably as a result of deregulated BA synthesis or defects in BA export.8 A recent report unveiled the immunosuppressive effect of primary conjugated and secondary BAs accumulating in hepatocellular carcinomas on T cell responses and cancer immunosurveillance during PD-1 blockade,9 echoing a pioneering study showing the inhibition of hepatic natural killer T (NKT) cell chemotaxis by secondary BAs during liver carcinogenesis.10 Beyond their local effects on the gut-liver axis, BAs may exert systemic immunomodulation.11,12,13 Given the critical role of intestinal commensalism in the metabolic transformation of hepatic BAs, dysbiosis, which is a prolonged deviation of the taxonomic composition of the gut microbiota,14 may affect both local and systemic adaptive T cell functions.15,16,17 Carcinogenesis18 and cancer treatments, co-morbidities, and comedications may lead to dysbiosis.19,20 Typically, cessation of antibiotics (ABX) induces the relative dominance of the genus Enterocloster, associated with immunosuppression and resistance to PD-1 blockade.21 This immunosuppressive effect of Enterocloster species involves the downregulation of mucosal vascular addressin cell adhesion molecule 1 (MAdCAM-1), expressed on high endothelial venules of the ileal lamina propria and gut lymphoid organs. Specifically, Enterocloster clostridioformis reshuffles the composition of BAs.21 However, deviations in BA metabolism and their links with gut dysbiosis and immune dysfunction in cancer patients during immunotherapy remain poorly characterized and were the focus of the present study.

Results

Gut dysbiosis-associated shifts in BA metabolism

ABX alter the BA composition, but their effects in mice bearing extraintestinal tumors have not been comprehensively studied.22 Mass spectrometry-based targeted metabolomics was performed on ileal and colonic contents, liver tissue, and plasma from C57BL/6 mice inoculated with subcutaneous MCA205 fibrosarcoma. Mice received a broad-spectrum ABX cocktail (ampicillin, colistin, and streptomycin) orally for two weeks prior to analysis (Figures 1A and S1A–S1C) in two geographically distinct animal facilities (France and Canada) but harbored similar plasma BA profiles at baseline (Figures S1A and S1B). ABX caused significant perturbations in BA metabolism (Figures 1B and S1D; Table S1). At both sites, ABX increased the plasma BA pool (Figures S1E and S1F) and drastically decreased secondary BA plasma concentrations, including DCA (Figure 1C, upper; Figure S1G, left), UDCA, ω-MCA, hyo-DCA (HDCA), as well as their conjugated forms (tauro-DCA [TDCA]) (Figure 1C, lower; Figures S1D and S1G, right), 7-ketoDCA, and nor-UDCA (Figures 1B and S1D). In plasma, the levels of primary unconjugated BAs (CA and CDCA) dropped (Figures 1B and S1D), while the concentrations of conjugated primary BAs (tauro-α-β-ω-muricholic acid [T-α-β-ω-MCA], tauro-cholic acid [TCA], and tauro-chenodeoxycholic acid [TCDCA]) rose with ABX, together with the tauro-conjugated UDCA (TUDCA) for about 2 weeks (Figures 1D, S1D, and S1H). In the liver, ABX caused a significant reduction in α-MCA, ω-MCA, TDCA, and tauro-lithocholic acid (TLCA), as well as a rise in β-MCA, its tauro-conjugated derivative (T-α-β-ω-MCA), and TUDCA (Figure 1B). Similarly, in the colon and to a lesser extent in the ileum, ABX depleted most BAs, such as DCA, glyco-DCA (GDCA), UDCA, and primary BA, but increased tauro-conjugated primary BAs, T-α-β-ω-MCA, TCA, TCDCA, and TUDCA (Figure 1B). To establish a causal link between gut dysbiosis and BA perturbations, we subjected ABX-pretreated mice to oral gavage with fecal microbial transplantation (FMT) from four cancer patients responding to immunotherapy in the ONCOBIOTICS cohort to revert dysbiosis (Figures S1I–S1K). Each of the four FMTs prevented the loss of DCA, HDCA, 7-ketoDCA, nor-UDCA (Figures S1I and S1J) and unconjugated primary BAs (CDCA, CA) after ABX treatment, while blunting the recirculation of tauro-conjugated primary BAs, T-α-β-ω-MCA, TCDCA, and TCA (Figures S1I and S1K).

Next, we replaced ABX by iterative gavages with the ABX-associated Enterocloster clostridioformis strain 141.21 In such conditions, we generated a “gut dysbiosis” as revealed by 16S rRNA targeted sequencing, unveiling a profound shift in the stool taxonomic composition, characterized by the enrichment of strains from the Enterocloster genus (Figures S2A and S2B), increased alpha diversity (Figure S2C), and significant alterations in the dynamics of beta diversity (Figure S2D), along with a relative overrepresentation of Eggerthellaceae and Clostridiaceae family members, coinciding with the depletion of immunogenic Alistipes spp. (Figure S2E) over 4 days after gavaging with E. clostridioformis. In these conditions, E. clostridioformis tended to mimic some of the effects of broad-spectrum ABX on BA composition, inducing the accumulation of β-MCA and its derivates, such as HDCA (Figures S3A and S3B), as well as T-α-β-ω-MCA and TUDCA in the plasma of both naive (Figure 1E) and tumor-bearing mice treated with anti-PD1 antibodies (Abs) (Figures S3A, S3C, and S3D). Importantly, such tauro-conjugated primary BAs reached the tumor microenvironment (TME), as indicated by their increased relative abundance among all BAs (Figures 1F and 1G).

We next analyzed the transcriptional profile of BA rate-limiting enzymes in the liver by quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) in animals receiving ABX, followed by 4 days of recolonization with or without two gavages of E. clostridioformis in naive mice. The expression of the enzymes of the classical pathway, Cyp7a1 and Cyp8b1, was drastically decreased by ABX and did not recover after ABX cessation (Figures S3E and S3F). However, ABX +/− E. clostridioformis tended to increase the expression of enzymes of the alternative pathway, Cyp27a1 and Cyp7b1 (Figure S3G), in line with the increase in CDCA derivatives, such as TCDCA, β-MCA, and T-α-β-ω-MCA after oral gavage with E. clostridioformis or ABX. Of note, the mouse-specific enzyme converting CDCA into α-MCA (Cyp2c70) was upregulated in the liver post oral gavage with ABX and E. clostridioformis (Figure S3H). The activity of bile acid-CoA:amino acid N-acyltransferase (Baat) enzyme involved in the glyco- or tauro-conjugation tended to increase following ABX and E. clostridioformis in the liver and the ileum, albeit at low levels (Figure S3I). We confirmed the downregulation of the enzymes of the BA classical pathway and the upregulation of Cyp2c70 using bulk RNA sequencing of cell-sorted hepatocytes as well as the upregulation of Baat gene product in ileal enterocytes (albeit at very low expression levels) (Figures S3J–S3M). Gut dysbiosis increased Vdr transcription in ileal enterocytes but not that of other BA receptors in hepatocytes, cholangiocytes, or enterocytes (Figures S3N–S3P). Of note, the sodium/BA co-transporter family member Slc10a1, which is involved in the uptake of BA for the enterohepatic cycle, was not significantly affected by ABX (Figures S3Q–S3S).

Altogether, ABX caused a metabolic shift in the BA composition, typically featured by an abnormal accumulation of primary BAs derived from CDCA, such as the tauro-conjugated primary BAs (TCDCA), and β-MCA and its tauro-conjugated derivative (T-α-β-ω-MCA), as well as the loss of intestinal and plasma secondary BAs derived from CA, i.e., DCA and G/TDCA. These ABX-induced metabolic shifts in the BA composition were prevented by FMT and partially mimicked by intestinal colonization with E. clostridioformis.

GDCA circumvents resistance to PD-1 blockade during dysbiosis

To draw causal links between gut dysbiosis-associated BA abnormalities and resistance to immunotherapy, we attempted to compensate dysbiotic tumor-bearing mice with oral delivery of secondary BAs during PD-1 blockade. C57BL/6 mice were orally gavaged with E. clostridioformis, then inoculated with MCA205 sarcoma cells or RET melanoma cells, and treated five days later intraperitoneally (i.p.) with α-PD-1 monoclonal antibody (mAb) alone or together with anti-CTLA4 Abs. At immunotherapy start, mice were fed with selected secondary BAs (GDCA, LCA, and UDCA) at a physiological concentration four times every other day (Figure 2A). Tumor growth retardation induced by immunotherapy was markedly inhibited by E. clostridioformis (Figure 2B left, p = 0.023) and restored by GDCA (Figure 2B right, p = 0.021) but neither by UDCA nor by LCA (Figure 2C). Similarly, the Ret transgenic melanoma model responded to the combination of α-CTLA-4 + α-PD-1 mAbs, but the gut colonization with E. clostridioformis tended to compromise the efficacy of immunotherapy (Figure 2D, p = 0.06), unless GDCA or UDCA (but not LCA) was co-administered (Figure 2D, p = 0.002; Figure 2E left, p = 0.009; right, p = 0.044). Even in the context of full-fledged gut dysbiosis provoked by ABX followed by two E. clostridioformis oral gavages, other secondary BAs from the classical pathway, TDCA and DCA, could control the progression of MCA205 sarcomas to some extent (Figure 2F, p = 0.035 with TDCA; p = 0.065 with DCA on day 13).

To investigate the pharmacokinetics of these exogenously delivered BA, we performed longitudinal mass spectrometry-based metabolomics of plasma and liver on day 7, 24 h after the 3rd administration, or on day 12, 4 days after the 4th oral administration of BA. Oral gavage with GDCA and TDCA (but not with LCA or UDCA) increased or tended to increase the recirculation of GDCA and/or DCA but decreased that of TCDCA (Figure 2G; Figures S4A and S4B; Table S1). Oral LCA and/or UDCA (but not GDCA) increased the plasma concentrations of LCA and UDCA as well as those of MDCA and microbial-CA or TLCA, TUDCA, and tauro-HDCA (THDCA) (Figure 2G; Figures S4A and S4B; Table S1). In the liver, GDCA decreased α-MCA, while UDCA increased T/G-UDCA, T/G-HDCA, and TLCA (Figure S4C). The direct comparison of the BA metabolic profile of animals gavaged with GDCA versus LCA confirmed the dominance of GDCA in the plasma at day 7 in the former group, while the LCA-fed group exhibited not only a dominance of LCA and TLCA but also an ABX-like profile (TCDCA, THDCA, and TUDCA) in the liver and plasma (Figures 2G and 2H; Figure S4C). Tauro-conjugated primary BAs, TCDCA and TCA, were dampened by GDCA (Figure 2G, left) and UDCA, respectively (Figure 2G, right) in the plasma.

To better understand the kinetics of orally fed BA and their downstream derivatives, we analyzed the bile salt hydrolase/aminoacyltransferase (bsh) sequences and activity of our E. clostridioformis strain 141 that are required to deconjugate acyl-bound amines from the sterol core of the primary BAs.23 Bsh sequencing of our strain revealed that its enzyme is more closely related to Penicillin V acylases, a group of N-terminal nucleophile hydrolases that are similar to classical cholylglycine hydrolases. This is somewhat in contrast to many other Enterocloster species, which contain classical enzymes (Figure S4D). Activity analysis of the E. clostridioformis strain 141 showed that it has a limited capacity to hydrolyze tauro-conjugated BAs, such as TCDCA or T-β-MCA, compared with other Enterocloster species but drastically hydrolyzes GDCA (Figures S4E and S4F), in accordance with the dysbiosis-associated BA abnormalities. Moreover, using 16S rRNA targeted sequencing, we did not observe major differences in the fecal taxonomic composition across BA recipient groups (Figures S5A–S5C). GDCA and TDCA triggered similar microbial shifts (Figures S5A and S5B), and GDCA significantly induced a mild relative overrepresentation of immunogenic Bifidobacterium pseudolongum24,25 and Erysipelatotrichaceae family members26 (Figures S5C and S5D; Table S2).

We conclude that oral GDCA supplementation of dysbiotic mice causes a rise in GDCA and DCA levels and a drop in TCDCA level in the plasma while decreasing α-MCA level in the liver, culminating in the restoration of sensitivity to PD-1 blockade, though without correcting dysbiosis.

Inhibitory effects of experimental cholestasis on PD-1 blockade

Attempting to draw causal links between the accumulation of tauro-conjugated primary BAs and resistance to immunotherapy, we used two procedures. First, we conducted a surgical bile duct ligation (BDL) (versus sham surgery) in mice transplanted with MCA205 fibrosarcoma three days before (Figure 3A), which resulted in cholestatic liver inflammation and injury (Figure 3B) associated with a brisk rise in plasma Tα-β-ω MCA and TCA (Figure 3C; Table S1), paralleled by a drastic drop in CA and DCA (Figure 3D; Table S1). Consequently, BDL culminated in complete cancer resistance to PD-1 blockade (Figure 3E). Secondly, using fexaramine, an ileum-selective FXR agonist (Figure 3F), we markedly rescued the efficacy of α-PD-1 Ab in ABX-treated tumor bearers (Figure 3G), restoring T cell functions in tumor-resident lymphocytes (Figures 3H and S6A) and decreasing plasma Tα-β-ω-MCA compared with ABX alone treatment (p = 0.015 without vs. p = 0.422 with fexaramine on day 7) and TCA (p = 0.001 without vs. p = 0.422 with fexaramine on day 7; Figure 3I; Table S1), albeit with interindividual variability (Figure S6B). Indeed, fexaramine induced the ileal and colonic rise in the FXR antagonist Tα-β-ω-MCA,27 potentially for a subsequent elimination (Figure S6C) but did not act on ABX-induced liver expression of Baat, the enzyme inducing tauro-conjugation of primary BAs (Figure S6D). In parallel, fexaramine tended to increase UDCA (p = 0.052) and DCA (p = 0.054) (Figures 3J and S6B; Table S1) as well as other secondary BAs (Figure S6E). Driven by a recent report showing that TCDCA can promote exhaustion of T lymphocytes infiltrating liver cancers12 and by the capacity of fexaramine to restrain LAG3 expression on CD8+ tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) (Figure 3H, right), we assessed whether cholestasis and dysbiosis-associated BAs could directly affect T cell functions in vitro. Indeed, we confirmed that TCDCA and T-β-MCA (but not all the other BAs) markedly reduced the viability of T cell receptor (TCR)-engaged splenic CD3+ T cells, while GDCA maintained the proliferation of CD8+ T cells (Figures 3K, S6F, and S6G). Hence, mechanical cholestasis or gut dysbiosis could lead to plasma accumulation of tauro-conjugated primary BAs, including the strong T-β-MCA FXR antagonist, culminating in T cell dysfunction and resistance to cancer immunotherapy that the ileal FXR agonist fexaramine could antagonize.

Gut BAs reach the subcutaneous TME to reprogram local immunity

Natural and orally fed BAs reached distal tissues by day 12, as revealed by tumor metabolomics (Figures 1F and 1G and 4A–4D; Table S1). Indeed, oral gavage with GDCA increased GDCA tumoral levels only in one-third of the animals (Figure 4A, p = 0.144), while that of UDCA induced a significant rise in LCA and UDCA (Figures 4B and 4C, p = 0.004 and 0.03, respectively). This might be explained by the capacity of E. clostridioformis strain 141 to metabolize GDCA into DCA in the intestines (Figures S4E and S4F). Of note, oral GDCA could prevent the tumoral accumulation of Tα-β-ω-MCA observed with E. clostridioformis (Figure 4D, p = 0.025).

Flow cytometric analyses of TILs and myeloid cells unveiled an E. clostridioformis-induced rise in the LAG3 exhaustion marker in CD4+ T cells (Figure 4E, p = 0.01; Figure S6A), most specifically in effector memory (TEM) CD4+ T cells (Figures 4F and 4G, p = 0.006). Conversely, GDCA significantly prevented this dysbiosis-associated T cell exhaustion, dampening LAG3 in CD4+ (Figure 4E, p = 0.035) and in TEM CD4+ T cells (Figures 4F and 4G, p = 0.013). We confirmed these findings using single-cell RNA sequencing of tumor-infiltrating CD45+ cells on day 11 post oral gavage with GDCA, DCA, or LCA (Figures 4H–4K; Figure S7). Single-cell-resolved levels of gene expression of the TME were determined after isolating 18,669 and 14,842 cells from the tumor beds and tumor-draining lymph nodes (tdLNs), respectively, across all experimental conditions. We identified 20 clusters in tumors (including 6 T cell clusters) and 19 clusters in tdLNs (including 12 T cell clusters) (Figures S7A–S7C). Focusing on GDCA versus corn oil (CO) control-imprinted TME, we did not find a quantitative impact of GDCA on the distribution of T cells within clusters (Figures S7B and S7C), but rather observed significant variations in gene expression levels in the proliferating CD4+ and/or CD8+ T cell subset in the tumor beds and in the naive/memory CD8+ T cell cluster in the tdLNs (Figure S7D, lower and upper panels). This naive/memory CD8+ T cell subset from GDCA-imprinted tdLNs was characterized by a type 1 IFN signature (Stat1, Ifit3, Ifi27l2a, Gimap7, Gbp2, and B2m) associated with the upregulation of the prototypic translation machinery (including many Rps and Rpl ribosomal proteins) and the cytoskeleton remodeling (Actb), with a downregulation of the mitochondrial oxidative metabolism (Lgals1, mt-Cytb, mt-Co-1/2/3, and mt-Atp6) (Figure S7D, upper panel). This signature has been described during the T cell clonal expansion phase, notably followed by inhibition of the translation of those transcripts in CD8+ T effectors,23 what we indeed observed in the TME (Figure S7D, lower panel). The cluster of proliferating CD4+ and/or CD8+ T cells in the GDCA-imprinted TME (defined by markers featuring in Table S3, including Nkg7, Cxcr6, Skap1, Ablim1, Sh2d2a, MKi67, Cdk1, and Top2a) that may contain progenitor exhausted T cells (Tpex), downregulated Ptpn22,28 Gzmb29 (Figure S7D, lower panel), Tox, Havcr2, Pdcd1, and Lag3 (Figures 4H–4K) gene transcripts, while upregulating the AP1 family member Fos, Il21r, Il1r2, Cd40, and Cd70 gene products (Figure S7D, lower panel), suggesting retardation in the T cell terminally differentiation status. Interestingly, one of the top gene transcripts upregulated by GDCA on this proliferating CD4+ and/or CD8+ T cell subset is the creatine transporter Slc6a8, critical for TCR-mediated activation of the mTORC1 signaling relevant in cancer immunosurveillance (Figure S7D, lower panel).30,31 The proliferating CD4+ and/or CD8+ T cells of the “GDCA tumors” also had a myeloid chemoattracting secretory profile (Figure S7D, lower panel). Intriguingly, the dominant CX3CR1 monocytic/macrophage cluster within the myeloid compartment of “GDCA tumors” was characterized by the expression of the nonsense mediated mRNA decay (NMD), a cellular quality control pathway degrading mutant transcripts, potentially involved in tumor antigenicity and immunoediting32 (Figure S7E). In the “DCA-imprinted TME”, the CD8+ cytotoxic T cell cluster was characterized by the functions associated with proficient antitumor responses, including the upregulation of Bhlhe40, Trbv2, Ccr7, Irf8, Il21r, and Sema4c33 (Table S3). The “GDCA imprinted” gene profiles contrasted with that observed with LCA-gavaged mice where NOS2+ and/or CX3CR1+ macrophages lost transcription of their antigen-processing machinery (H2-K1, H2-D1, H2-Ab1, Ctss, Ccl5, and Ifitm3) (Table S3). Of note, “LCA imprinted” tdLN-derived naive/memory CD8+ T cells upregulated Adamts17, a sheddase blunting the CD122 signaling in CD8+ T cells34 (Table S3). Moreover, the gene expression fingerprint of LCA TIL revealed a profound downregulation of the type 1 and 2 IFN responses compared with the negative control-imprinted TME (Figure S7F; Table S3). Altogether, these results showed that BAs can act at distal sites to reprogram the TME and tdLNs, influencing the outcome of tumor-specific immune responses.

Because dysbiosis facilitates the exodus of terminally differentiated and exhausted α4β7-expressing CD4+ Tregs and their emigration to the tdLNs by downregulating ileal MAdCAM-1 in high endothelial venules,21 we wondered whether BAs might downregulate MAdCAM-1 expression, thereby favoring the emigration of enterotropic Tregs to tdLNs. For this, transformed endothelial sinusoidal cells (TSECs) were cultured in the presence of physiological concentrations of an array of commercially available primary and secondary, tauro- and glyco-conjugated BAs, followed by the quantification of Madcam1 mRNA by RT-qPCR.21 As reported, LCA downregulated Madcam1 expression.21 In sharp contrast, DCA and its derivatives (GDCA, HDCA, and UDCA) upregulated Madcam1. Conversely, most tauro-conjugated BAs failed to affect Madcam1 expression (Figure 4L). CRISPR-Cas9-based knockout of 3 potential BA receptors (Fxr, Vdr, and Tgr5) in TSECs led to the conclusion that Fxr/Nr1h4 is essential for the GDCA-induced upregulation of Madcam1 (Figure 4M). In vivo, GDCA efficiently increased Madcam1 mRNA in the ileum from ABX-treated mice, validating the in vitro results (Figure 4N). Next, we injected carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE) into the mesenteric lymph nodes (mLNs) and then monitored the emigration of CFSE-labeled cells to distant sites at 24 h in tumor-bearing mice fed with E. clostridioformis under PD-1 blockade (Figure 4O). Oral administration of GDCA decreased the migration of CFSE+ α4β7+ CD4+ T cells (and more specifically α4β7+ CD4+ Foxp3+ CD25+ Treg cells) (Figure 4P) but not that of α4β7− CD4+ Treg to tdLNs (Figure 4Q), compared with E. clostridioformis + LCA, albeit non-significantly compared with the E. clostridioformis group without BA gavage.

Finally, we compared the capacity of GDCA to restore sensitivity to PD-1 blockade in MCA205 tumor-bearing Madcam1-proficient mice versus deficient mice in settings of gut dysbiosis (as detailed above, Figure 2F). GDCA lost its adjuvant properties in the Madcam1−/− mice (Figures 4R and 4S) but not in Tgr5/Gpbar −/− animals (Figure S8A).

Incidentally, to exclude possible direct effects of BAs on cancer cells, we analyzed the expression patterns of the five BA receptors in MCA205 fibrosarcoma by RT-qPCR. In comparison with ileal mucosa, MCA205 sarcoma tissues expressed low levels of Fxr/Nr1h4, Vdr, and Tgr5/Gpbar1 gene transcripts (Figure S8B). At physiological concentrations, BAs failed to kill HT29 cells, which readily succumbed to oxaliplatin or mitoxanthrone (Figure S8C). CRISPR-Cas9-mediated knockout of Fxr/Nr1h4 or Tgr5/Gpbar1 failed to affect tumor outgrowth, but the Fxr/Nr1h4 knockout sensitized MCA205 fibrosarcomas to α-PD-1 blockade (Figures S8D and S8E, p = 0.001).

In summary, oral GDCA reached tumor bed and prevented the exhaustion of TIL effector memory lymphocytes. It is, at least in part, a consequence of the MAdCAM-1-dependent blockade of tumor homing of immunosuppressive enterotropic Tregs.

Subclinical cholestasis in cancer patients treated with immune checkpoint blockade

To investigate the clinical significance of these findings, we analyzed the effects of ABX on mass spectrometry-detectable circulating BAs in a prospective cohort (ONCOBIOTICS cohort [NCT04567446]), which included 133 patients with lung, kidney, or bladder cancer treated with immune checkpoint blockade (ICB)-based first-line regimens.35 ABX exposure prior to plasma sampling significantly decreased the plasma concentration of UDCA (Figure 5A, p = 0.021 compared with no ABX exposure), while increasing TCDCA (Figure 5A, p = 0.038 compared with no ABX exposure). These ABX effects were significant within a 30-day time window (Figures 5B–5D) and led to a disruption of the normal pattern of correlations between unconjugated primary and secondary BAs (Figure 5E). In the 61 patients with paired plasma metabolomics and stool metagenomics data, S-score (reflecting gut dysbiosis when closest to 0)36 showed significant, albeit weak, positive correlations with DCA (Figure 5F, left) and negative correlations with total tauro-conjugated BAs (Figure 5F, right).

To dissect the patterns of circulating BAs in the ONCOBIOTICS cohort of cancer patients, we performed an unsupervised hierarchical clustering of the plasma concentrations of BAs, monitored before or within the first 42 days of PD-1/PD-L1 blockade in 111 patients. Three clusters of patients could be clearly identified (Figure 6A; Table S4). Cluster 1 was characterized by the relative dominance of unconjugated primary and secondary BAs (Figure 6B). In cluster 2, patients harbored higher levels of glyco-conjugated primary BAs than those of the others (Figure 6C), while cluster 3 was typified by the accumulation of tauro-conjugated primary and secondary BAs, leading to a ratio between glyco-/tauro-conjugated BAs of <3, as well as by elevated plasma levels of the inflammatory marker calprotectin (S100A9) (Figures 6D and 6E). A similar clustering pattern was found in metastatic melanoma (Figures S9A–S9C) and NSCLC patients (Figures S9D–S9F) in Canada (as described by Routy et al.37 and Duttagupta et al.38; Table S4). Univariate Cox regression analysis of the overall survival (OS) of ONCOBIOTICS patients revealed that cluster 3 tends to be associated with dismal outcome (Figure 6F; Table S4). Notably, these 3 clusters exhibited different immune profiles. Cluster 2 was enriched in naive CD8+ T cells, while cluster 3 showed a relative scarcity of total and memory CD27+ and CD127+ CD4+ T cells (Figures S10A and S10B).

Given the limited effect size of ONCOBIOTICS and the demonstration of a potential clinical relevance of subclinical cholestasis1 brought up in rare retrospective studies in renal cell carcinoma patients treated with nivolumab,39,40 we determined whether the routine biomarkers of cholestasis, which are gamma-glutamyl transferase (γGT) and alkaline phosphatases (ALP), might correlate with immunotherapy resistance across six independent and pan-cancer cohorts gathering 599 patients: ONCOBIOTICS (N = 111),35 PREMIS ( N = 185),41 G2R (N = 100), MEDITREME (N = 54),42 METIMMOX (N = 38),43 and AtezoTRIBE (N = 111) amenable to PD-1/PD-L1 and/or CTLA-4 blockade, alone or combined with other therapeutic modalities (Figure 7A; Table S5). An elevation of γGT (defined as twice the upper limit of normal [ULN]: ≥45 U/L for males and ≥35 U/L for females) was observed at baseline in 25.7% ± 3.9% (mean ± SEM) of patients across these cohorts (Figure 7A; Tables S5). Among the 561 patients across the five cohorts, those above this threshold at immunotherapy initiation exhibited reduced progression-free survival (PFS) and/or OS in univariate analyses (Table S5; Figures 7B–7G; Figure S11A), except the AtezoTRIBE arm “no ICB” (Figure 7F). The Cox regression multivariate analyses adjusting for liver metastases and other prognostic factors (but not for co-medications or autoimmune hepatitis) confirmed that γGT was an independent negative prognostic parameter for PFS in ONCOBIOTICS (p = 0.024, Table S6), G2R (p = 0.015, Table S6), and MEDITREME (p = 0.013, Table S6), as well as for OS in PREMIS (p = 0.014, Table S6), MEDITREME (p = 0.048, Table S6), and AtezoTRIBE (p = 0.039, Table S6).

Similar results were obtained for elevated ALPs (ALP ≥ 1 ULN) that were associated with a dismal prognosis in univariate analyses (Figures S11B–S11D for ONCOBIOTICS, METIMMOX, and MEDITREME) and in multivariate analyses, for PFS and OS in ONCOBIOTICS (p = 0.002 and 0.042, respectively; Table S6) and for OS in MEDITREME (Table S6, p = 0.017). Immunotherapy tended to decrease γGT concentrations in patients with PFS of ≥6 months, while the levels remained ≥2 ULN in patients who progressed within 6 months after ICB initiation (Figures 7H and S11E). This trend was not observed for ALP levels (Figures S11F and S11G), although patients with elevated ALP at the start of immunotherapy had significantly reduced PFS in both ONCOBIOTICS and METIMMOX cohorts (Figures S11B and S11C). Moreover, circulating liver enzymes (γGT, ALP, and transaminases) and soluble MAdCAM-1 (sMAdCAM-1) at baseline were positively and negatively correlated with tauro-conjugated BAs, respectively, in all patients from the pooled ONCOBIOTICS, as well as in the individual FMT-LUMINATE, MIMIC, and AtezoTRIBE cohorts (Figure 7I). The increased prevalence of immunosuppressive SIG1 bacteria (as defined by the S-score and Toposcore36) in patients presenting elevated γGT or tauro-conjugated BAs at baseline is in line with the observation that γGT levels are higher in patients after ABX uptake (Figures 8A–8C; Figure S11H). Moreover, γGT levels correlated with blood markers of inflammation (neutrophils) and gut permeability (soluble ST2) (Figure 8D). In accordance with our previous report21 and Figure 7I, sMAdCAM-1 and γGT baseline plasma levels exhibited a negative correlation in all three cohorts pooled together and PREMIS (Figure 8E, p = 0.008 and 0.004, respectively). Altogether, subclinical cholestasis defined by γGT can be considered as a proxy of gut dysbiosis.

Finally, we addressed whether FMT might avoid the accumulation of tauro-conjugated BAs associated with gut dysbiosis and resistance to immunotherapy in cancer patients. Using a linear mixed-effect model approach to predict trajectory slope, we analyzed the evolution of each plasma BA pre- and post-FMT in responders versus non-responders from the MIMIC and FMT-LUMINATE trials, using three time points of plasma metabolomics.37,38,44 Notably, tauro-CDCA (TCDCA) followed an ascending slope in non-responders but a stable or descending slope in responders (Figure 8F, left). Concomitantly, the levels of TCDCA were increased in the stool of the non-responder patients (Figure 8F, right; p = 0.009). We validated this observation in the TACITO trial randomizing metastatic renal cell carcinoma (mRCC) patients into FMT versus placebo, in addition to the combination of tyrosine kinase inhibitor and ICB, showing a 30% increase in patients without progression at 12 months in the FMT arm.45 Plasma levels of TCDCA increased in the first week of therapy in the placebo but not in the FMT arm (Figure 8G, p = 0.03), and a descending slope of TCDCA and TCA was observed in the FMT group over the first year of follow-up, but not in placebo (Figures 8H and 8I; Figure S11I).

Altogether, FMT can be considered as a therapy preventing dysbiosis-associated accumulation of tauro-conjugated BAs over the course of the disease and/or treatment.

Discussion

This study links gut dysbiosis with abnormalities in BA recirculation to the tumor, compromising tumor immunosurveillance and hence causing resistance to ICB in murine malignancies distant from the enterohepatic cycle. In particular, the abnormal synthesis or recirculation of TCDCA or Tα-β-ω-MCA (FXR antagonists) and the loss of secondary BAs of the classic pathway (DCA, an FXR agonist, and GDCA/TDCA derivatives) act in concert to compromise cancer immunosurveillance during ABX uptake or E. clostridioformis-enforced gut dysbiosis.

Our data extend previous findings using BA oral gavage in mice19,21 and others demonstrating the aberrant activity of the BAAT enzyme in liver cancers culminating in TCDCA synthesis and exhaustion of TILs.9 In mice, GDCA (and its derivative DCA, an FXR agonist), FMT, or the ileal FXR agonist fexaramine could dampen plasma levels of TCDCA and/or Tα-β-ω-MCA, notoriously FXR antagonists, while surgical BDL did the contrary, phenocopying the effects of ABX. Accordingly, fexaramine and GDCA supplementation of dysbiotic mice prevented CD4+ effector T cell and Tpex exhaustion (characterized by Lag-3, Tox, and Tim-3 expression) in TILs. Hence, the balance between FXR agonists and antagonists in the ileum might regulate immunosurveillance of tumors located at distal sites from the intestines.

The prognostic value of baseline γGT levels had long been underestimated until Winter et al. reported their independent negative impact on OS.46 Our results across six independent cohorts of cancer patients corroborated this original study, suggesting that γGT elevations may be added to the routine panels that identify patients unlikely to respond to immunotherapy, presumably as a consequence of a disabled gut-liver axis. Therapeutic strategies (such as UDCA47 or obeticholic acid1) to curtail liver cholestasis aim at increasing BA flow, reducing BA reabsorption from the intestine, shutting down BA synthesis in the liver, and/or triggering intestinal FGF15/FGFR-βKlotho signaling. However, in our hands, UDCA inconsistently circumvented primary resistance to α-PD-1 mAb induced by ABX/E. clostridioformis. In contrast, oral GDCA supplementation restored immunotherapy responses in dysbiotic mice. A proof-of-concept phase 1 trial showed that GDCA-containing capsules repressed primary BA synthesis without impacting glucose and energy metabolism.48 FMT represents another option for dysbiotic cancer patients. Indeed, subclinical cholestasis correlated with low Toposcore or ABX uptake, downregulation of sMAdCAM-1, and systemic inflammation in our study, in accordance with previous reports suggesting that intestinal microbiota plays an important role in the pathophysiology of cholestatic liver disease.49,50 Pioneering studies have evaluated the interceptive function of probiotics, such as Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG, in cholestatic liver diseases.51,52 Probiotics could decrease the T-β-MCA FXR antagonist, unleashing liver FXR activity. Similarly, gut microbiota not only regulates secondary BA metabolism but also inhibits BA synthesis in the liver by alleviating FXR inhibition in the ileum.53 Therefore, restoring a healthy intestinal commensalism may avoid cancer-associated subclinical cholestasis to facilitate adaptive anticancer immune responses. Prospective clinical trials may stratify cancer patients based on various proxies of gut dysbiosis including subclinical cholestasis to predict primary resistance to cancer immunotherapy.

Limitations of the study

In murine models, Enterocloster spp.-induced gut dysbiosis was not stricto sensu mimicking that mediated by ABX. The use of stools from ABX-treated patients for a transfer into avatar mouse models would have been an additional line of evidence that gut dysbiosis is clinically significant to dampen ICB efficacy. Demonstration of a causal link between GDCA compensatory effects on dysbiosis and endothelial MAdCAM-1 function would have required endothelium-restricted Nr1h4/Fxr gene deletion. Therefore, this study cannot exclude alternative BA sensing routes. Multivariable analyses testing the prognostic value of γGT in cancer patients took into account only ABX and liver metastases but failed to consider other confounding factors such as hepatic autoimmune reactions,46 alcohol consumption, several co-medications, or the socioeconomical status. Prospective studies including these extrinsic factors should confirm these findings.

Study participant details

Data were derived from eight prospective clinical trials, comprising both observational and interventional design.

1.ONCOBIOTICS◦ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT04567446◦Type: Observational design◦Description: ONCOBIOTICS is a multicenter observational study conducted across 14 sites in France and Canada. It investigates the impact of the microbiome on clinical outcomes in patients with advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), renal cell carcinoma (RCC), and bladder cancer (BC) treated with anti-PD-(L)1 therapies, either alone or combined with chemotherapy or tyrosine kinase inhibitors. Patients were followed according to standard care until disease progression, intolerable toxicity, or up to two years of immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) treatment. Detailed eligibility criteria, baseline data, and laboratory tests including baseline gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), are provided in the trial protocol and recorded via electronic case report forms. All participants provided written informed consent. Blood and stool samples were collected at baseline. Available samples were used in this analysis, with availability varying based on patient consent, sample pairing, volume constraints, and site-level data reporting. Blood samples were used for metabolomics analysis, specifically for TDCA quantification in the MVA (multivariate analysis), following Alves Costa Silva et al., 2024.54 Stool samples were used to calculate the Toposcore via metagenomic analysis, following the methodology described in Derosa et al., Cell, 2024.36

2.MEDITREME◦ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT03202758◦Type: Interventional design◦Description: Clinical data were obtained from the phase II clinical trial NCT03202758, which enrolled 54 patients with histologically confirmed metastatic colorectal cancer harboring a KRAS mutation. All patients received a combination treatment consisting of Durvalumab (750 mg every 2 weeks), Tremelimumab (75 mg every 4 weeks), and FOLFOX chemotherapy. Eligible patients had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0 or 1. All participants provided written informed consent and any locally required authorizations prior to undergoing protocol-related procedures, including screening assessments. 3.METIMMOX◦ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT03388190◦Type: Interventional design◦Decription: Clinical data were obtained from the NCT03388190 study, a multicenter, open-label, randomized phase II trial evaluating first-line treatment in patients with microsatellite stable (MSS)/mismatch repair-proficient (pMMR) metastatic colorectal cancer. Patients were randomized to receive either the oxaliplatin-based standard-of-care Nordic FLOX regimen (control arm) or a sequential treatment with Nordic FLOX followed by nivolumab (experimental arm). The primary objective was to determine whether the experimental arm improved progression-free survival (PFS), with secondary endpoints including safety, tolerability, and quality of life. The trial was approved and conducted as per Norwegian legislation, and all participants provided written informed consent. Eligibility criteria, baseline characteristics, and concomitant medications were documented in eCRFs, with full criteria available in the trial protocol on ClinicalTrials.gov. For the purpose of this study, we used de-identified clinical data from this trial, focusing particularly on the ALP variable for Kaplan–Meier survival analyses.

Experimental model

Mice

All animal experiments were performed in compliance with French and European laws and regulations or the Canadian Council on Animal Care guidelines. The institutional animal ethics committee (Direction départementale de la protection des populations (DDPP) du Val-de-Marne ) and the French Ministère de la Recherche approved all procedures in this study (permission number: 2023-024-42156 (all mice experiments except written otherwise) and 2024_010_48794 (FMT experiments) or #46833–202401111607908 v6 (BDL experiment)). Experiments were performed in accordance with Government and institutional guidelines and regulations. Seven-weeks old female C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Harlan or Envigo (France). Canadian animal studies were approved by the Institutional Animal Care Committee (CIPA) (Ethics numbers: C18025BRs). Murine experiments were conducted using seven-week-old female C57BL/6 mice, obtained from Charles River. All mouse experiments were performed at the animal facility at the GRCC or at CHUM in Montreal (Figure 1 except D; Figures S1A and S1C) except for the bile duct ligation performed at the Center de Recherche des Cordeliers. The animals were housed in SPF conditions with a 12 hour-day/night rhythm and naive at baseline. Before every experiment, we randomized the mice to avoid bias. If not otherwise indicated, mice were treated with a broad-spectrum ABX solution containing ampicillin (1mg/mL), streptomycin (5 mg/mL), and colistin (1 mg/mL) (Sigma-Aldrich) (ABX) added to sterile drinking water. In accordance with our institutional ethics committee guidelines, the maximal permitted tumor burden was 1500 mm3 corresponding to 300 mm2 (calculation was L x l). Tumor sizes were measured and controlled three times a week and when the tumor size exceeded 250 mm2, mice were euthanized immediately to avoid reaching the permitted size of 300 mm2. Mice were also controlled by technicians every day. Predefined humane endpoints included tumor ulceration or body weight loss greater than 20% relative to baseline. If either endpoint was reached, mice were euthanized within 24 h.

Madcam1−/− (B6.129-Madcam1tm1.2Nwag/J) mice were a kind gift from A. Schippers (University Hospital Aachen, Aachen, Germany).

Method details

Blood sample collection for ONCOBIOTICS, MIMic and FMT-LUMINATE

Blood samples were drawn from patients. Plasma was collected after centrifugation of lithium heparin tubes without EDTA and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) were separated using a density gradient medium. Blood samples were used for the detection of bile acids by UHPLC-MS.

Stool samples collection for ONCOBIOTICS

Stool samples were self-collected by the participants at home according to the IHMS_SOP 004 (https://human-microbiome.org/index.php?id=Sop&num=004). Immediately after the collection, they were stored at −20°C and transported on dry ice. Upon arriving at the biobank of the recruitment center (between 4 and 24 h later), they were stored at -80°C in plastic tubes. Two aliquots of the same sample were used for metaproteomic and metagenomic analyses. All the samples were processed according to International Human Microbiome Standards (IHMS) guidelines (SOP 03 V1).

DNA was extracted from an aliquot of the same stool samples used for metaproteomic analysis using the IHMS SOP 07 V2 H and sequenced with an Ion proton sequencer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The sequencing was performed at a single site (MetaGenoPolis). Reads were cleaned using Alien Trimmer (version 0.4.0) to (1) remove resilient sequencing adapters and (2) trim low-quality nucleotides at the 3′ side using a quality cutoff of 20 and a length cutoff of 45 bp. Cleaned reads were subsequently filtered from human and other possible food contaminant DNA using human genome (GRCh38-p13), with an identity score threshold of 95%. For each metagenome we profiled the taxonomic composition with MetaPhlAn-4.55 For alpha diversity, we computed the per-sample Richness and Shannon indexes56 using the vegan R package.57 Differences between groups were assessed using the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test. For beta-diversity, we computed the between-samples Bray-Curtis dissimilarities using the implementation available in the Vegan R package 51. Differential abundance analysis on taxonomic profiles between groups were performed using the LefSE software.

Human and mouse plasma sample preparation and bile acid detection by UHPLC-MS

For each patient, 50 μL of human plasma was mixed with 500 μL of ice-cold extraction mixture (methanol/water, 9/1 spiked with a cocktail of internal standards) and centrifuged (15 min at 10,000g, 4°C). Regarding murine plasma, 25 μL of biological sample was mixed with 250 μL of the same ice-cold extraction mixture and treated as described above. Mice plasma was harvested at the same time for each time point. Supernatants were collected and several fractions were split to be analyzed by different liquid and gaz chromatographies coupled with mass spectrometers (LC-MS and GC-MS).58 For human samples, 150 μL of supernatant (50 μL for murine) were transferred to glass vials and evaporated to be used for GC-MS. Then, 50 μL of methoxyamine (20 mg/mL in pyridine) was added on dried extracts, which were stored at room temperature in the dark, overnight. The day after, 80 μL of N-Methyl-N- trimethylsilyl-trifluoroacetamide were added, and the final derivatization was made at 40°C for 30 min. Samples were then transferred into vials and directly injected into the GC-MS system – 7890A gas chromatograph coupled with a triple quadrupole 7000C (Agilent Technologies) – equipped with a high sensitivity electronic impact source (EI) operating in positive mode. Others aliquots (polyamines, bile acids and short chain fatty acids) were analyzed by LC-MS 1290 UHPLC (Ultra-High Performance Liquid Chromatography) coupled to a QQQ 6470 (Agilent Technologies). Biliary acids and polyamines were analyzed by MRM analysis.54 Small organic acids and ketones were analyzed by MRM analysis in both positive and negative polarity, alternatively, gas temperature was set to 300°C with a gas flow of 12 L/min. Capillary voltage was set to 4 kV in positive and 5 kV in negative polarity mode. Ten (10) μL of sample were injected on a Zorbax Eclipse XDB- column (P/N 981758-902, 100 mm × 2.1 mm particle size 1.8 μm 1200 bars) from Agilent technologies, protected by a guard column (XDB-C18; 5 mm × 2.1 mm particle size 1.8 μm) and heated at 50°C by a Pelletier oven. Gradient mobile phase consisted of water with 0.1% of formic acid (A) and 0.1% of formic acid in acetonitrile (B) freshly made. Flow rate was set to 0.7 mL/min, and gradient as follow: initial condition was 80% phase A and 20% phase B for 3 min, changing to 45% phase B over 4 min before rinsing phase and equilibrium before next injection. Peak detection and integration of the analytes were performed using the Agilent Mass Hunter quantitative software (B.07.01).

Pseudo-targeted analysis by UHPLC-HRAM (Ultra-High Performance Liquid Chromatography – High Resolution and Accurate Mass) was performed on a U3000 (Dionex)/Orbitrap q-Exactive (Thermo) coupling, previously described in Durand et al. 202159 and Abdelatif et al.60 All targeted treated data were merged and cleaned with a dedicated R package (version 4.0) (https://github.com/kroemerlab/GRMeta).

sST2, sMAdcAM-1 and S100A9 quantification in patients

sST2, sMAdCAM-1 and S100A9 were quantified in patient’s plasma samples with the Bio-Plex 200 systems (Bio-Rad) and sMAdCAM-1/ST2/S100A9 kit from R&D Systems (Human Luminex Discovery Assay LXSAHM).

Slope-prediction method

Responders and non-responders were compared using a longitudinal slope estimation approach. For each metabolite, individual longitudinal slopes were estimated using Best Linear Unbiased Predictors (BLUP) derived from linear mixed-effects models fitted to repeated measurements over time. Estimated slopes were subsequently compared between responders and non-responders to evaluate differences in metabolite trajectories. The significance of temporal changes within responders and non-responders was assessed from the estimated time effect in each group. (Related to Figure 8F, left).

Clinical cox models

Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated using Prism 10 (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA) and the statistical difference between groups was compared using the Log rank test. Multivariate analysis and hazard ratio evaluation were performed using the Cox proportional hazard regression model.

Patients clustering based on circulating bile acids composition

Unsupervised hierarchical clustering was performed to identify groups of patients with similar circulating bile acid profiles. A matrix containing bile acid percentages or log2foldchange values for each patient was used as input. Clustering analysis was conducted in R using the “pheatmap” package (version 1.0.13). Hierarchical clustering was applied to both patients and bile acid variables, or only for bile acid variables. Pairwise distances between patients were calculated using the Euclidean distance metric. Clusters were generated using Ward’s minimum variance method (Ward.D2 linkage), which minimizes the total within-cluster variance at each step of the agglomerative clustering process. No data scaling or normalization was applied prior to clustering. The resulting dendrograms and heatmaps were generated using the “pheatmap” function, with patient annotations displayed according to the available clinical metadata. The clustering output was used to define patient subgroups exhibiting distinct circulating bile acid signatures. (related to Figure 6A; Figures S9A, S9D, and S11I).

In FMT experiments, mice received 3 days of ABX before undergoing FMT (from 4 different cancer patients) the next day by oral gavage. Statistical analysis of tumor growth

Longitudinal tumor growth data were analyzed using the online tool “TumGrowth” available at https://kroemerlab.shinyapps.io/TumGrowth/, which is an open-access platform specifically developed for the statistical analysis of experimental tumor growth curves. Tumor volume measurements collected over time were modeled using linear mixed-effects models, allowing incorporation of repeated measurements within individual animals and inter-animal variability. Tumor growth kinetics were fitted using a two-phase piecewise linear regression model (linear-linear model). When appropriate, the breakpoint between growth phases was estimated automatically by the software. Treatment group and time were included as fixed effects, while individual animals were modeled as random effects. Model adequacy was assessed using the diagnostic plots provided by the application. Differences in tumor growth between treatment groups were assessed by pairwise comparisons of the estimated growth slopes derived from the mixed-effects model. Statistical significance was evaluated using Wald tests, and p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons when appropriate. A two-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RNA-seq data generation and analysis

RNA-seq libraries were generated in five replicates for each condition and sequenced in paired-end mode with a read length of 100 bp. Raw sequencing reads were processed with fastp (v0.23.2)61 to trim adapters and remove low-quality bases (Phred score <30), and, when necessary, downsampled using seqkit (v2.5.1)62 to obtain comparable read depths across samples (20–60 million high-quality reads per sample). Fastq files were subsequently assessed with FastQC (v0.12.1)63 and MultiQC (v1.16),64 and pseudoaligned to the GRCm38 transcriptome reference with kallisto (v0.50.1)65 using default parameters. Genes with an absolute expression of less than 20 reads across all samples were filtered out.

Differential expression analysis was performed with DESeq266(v1.40.2). Logarithm fold changes were modified by the lfcShrink algorithm with the “Ashr” option. Differentially expressed gene (DEG) cutoff was defined at an FDR less than 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change (log2FC) value greater than 0.5 in pairwise comparisons between experimental conditions. For visualization purposes, variance stabilizing transformation (VST) values and transcripts per million (TPM) were obtained with DESeq2 functions and used where indicated in the figures.

Single cell RNA-sequencing for tdLN and tumors

BD Rhapsody experiments were performed following manufacturer’s (BD Biosciences) protocol. Prior to experiment, tumor cells were enriched using mouse CD45 microbeads from Miltenyi (130-052-30) following the Miltenyi’s protocol. Two independent cartridges were used to capture 77,821 cells (tumor) and 76,529 cells (lymph nodes). Experimental conditions were pooled prior to analysis using the BD™ Mouse Single Cell Sample Multiplexing Kit (Cat No: 633793), with 9 and 11 sample barcodes per cartridge, respectively. Libraries were processed according to the BD Rhapsody™ Whole Transcriptome Analysis (WTA) Amplification Kit (Cat No: 633801) and the BD Rhapsody™ Whole Transcriptome Analysis (WTA) Reagent Kit (Cat No: 665915). A total of 1,415 million reads and 2,068 million reads were sequenced for the tumor and lymph node libraries, respectively.

Alignment and pre-processing of single-cell RNA sequencing data

Transcriptomic FASTQ files were processed using the standard Rhapsody analysis pipeline (BD Biosciences), following the manufacturer’s recommendations. For read quality control, recursive substitution error correction (RSEC) and distribution-based error correction (DBEC) were applied; these manufacturer-developed algorithms correct for PCR and sequencing errors. A cell was defined as a singlet if the minimum read count associated with a single sample tag exceeded a threshold of 75%. A cell was classified as a multiplet if it exceeded this threshold for more than one sample tag. Cells that did not meet the threshold were labeled as undetermined. Both multiplets and undetermined cells were excluded from downstream analysis. Samples were demultiplexed using the Sample Tag Call matrix generated by the Seven Bridges BD Rhapsody alignment pipeline, and metadata were manually assigned to each tag.

Analysis of single cell RNA-sequencing

Filtered gene-expression matrices were subjected to processing using Seurat R Package (v 5.4.0). Quality control measures were implemented to remove debris, damaged cells and doublets (min_genes_per_cell = 600; max_genes_per_cell = 5000; max_%mitochondrial_reads = 20; max_%ribosomal_reads = 30). After filtering, each dataset was normalized using Seurat’s log-normalization method, and the top 3,000 highly variable genes were identified. The data were then scaled while regressing out technical sources of variation, including mitochondrial and ribosomal content and total UMI counts. Principal component analysis (PCA) was subsequently performed using the selected variable genes, and the first 30 principal components were retained for downstream analyses. UMAP dimensionality reduction was generated based on these 30 components, followed by neighbor identification and clustering using the Louvain algorithm (resolution = 0.5). Differential expression analysis was performed using FindAllMarkers to identify cluster-specific upregulated genes, which were used for manual annotation of each cell population. For T cells, FindSubCluster (resolution = 0.1, Louvain algorithm) was applied to distinguish CD4 and CD8 subsets. Statistical comparisons were performed using Wilcoxon tests with FDR correction.

For the differential expression analysis of total CD8 lymphocytes in tumor single-cell data, all clusters containing CD8 lymphocytes were merged into a single cluster and filtered to remove cells containing Cd4 reads (Cd3e > 0 & Cd8a >0 & Cd4 = = 0). Gene expression was extracted using the FetchData function from Seurat. Pairwise statistical comparisons were performed between experimental conditions using Wilcoxon tests with FDR correction, comparing each bile acid-treated group against the EC control. Results were visualized as violin plots overlaid with boxplots and individual data points using ggplot2.

GSEA was performed using the fgsea R package (v1.32.4) on ranked gene lists derived from differential expression results. Genes were ranked by average log2 fold change, with a small random noise term (mean = 0, sd = 1 × 10−6) added to break ties. Genes with non-finite log2 fold change values were excluded prior to ranking. Pathways from the msigdb_v2024.1.Mm_GMTs collection were used as the reference gene sets, with a minimum gene set size of 15 and a maximum of 500. GSEA was run with eps = 0.0 and 100,000 permutations. Pathways with an adjusted p-value <0.05 were considered significant, and results were visualized as dot plots displaying the top 50 pathways ranked by absolute normalized enrichment score (|NES|).

Ileal, colonic, liver, tumor tissues preparation and bile acid detection by UHPLC-MS

Ileal, colonic, liver, tumor mouse tissues. Approximately 30 mg of collected tissue was weighed and placed in a 2-mL homogenizer tube with ceramic beads (Precellys, Bertin Technologies, France) with 1 mL of ice-cold extraction mixture (9:1 methanol–water, -20°C, with labeled 13C-glycocholic acid as internal standard). To facilitate the extraction of endogenous metabolites, samples were then completely homogenized (3 cycles of 20 s/2380g; Precellys 24, Bertin Technologies, Montigny-le-Bretonneux, France) and centrifuged (10 min at 15,000g, 4°C). To detect bile acids, 300 mL of supernatant was collected and treated following a previously described protocol.58 Bile acids were analyzed by LC-MRM (Multiple Reaction Monitoring) with a 1290 UHPLC (Ultra-High Performance Liquid Chromatography, Agilent Technologies) coupled to a mass spectrometer 6470 TQ (Triple Quadrupole, Agilent Technologies), as previously described.59 Targeted data were cleaned with a R (version 4.0) dedicated package (https://github.com/kroemerlab/GRMeta).

Mouse clustering based on bile acids composition

Metabolomic profile analysis and visualization were performed in R (version ≥4.4), with data normalization and heatmap organization adapted to the biological objective of each figure using the “pheatmap” package (version 1.0.13). For multi-compartment analyses (plasma, liver, ileum, and colon; related to Figures 1B and S3A), raw metabolite intensities were normalized using row-wise Z score scaling and hierarchically clustered were done across rows using Ward’s minimum variance method (ward.D2). Pairwise group comparisons were performed using two-sided non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Statistical significance was represented as lateral row annotations using discretized p-value categories (≥0.05, <0.05, <0.01, and <0.001). Assessment of the impact of antibiotic treatment in Figure S1D was based on an initial selection of metabolites significantly altered relative to the Water group (Wilcoxon test, p ≤ 0.05). Selected metabolites were subsequently normalized using row-wise Z score scaling and hierarchically clustered across rows using Ward’s minimum variance method (ward.D2). Bile acids were highlighted in red, and p-value categories from the Water versus ABX comparison were displayed as lateral annotations. Unsupervised exploration of the bile acid composition across the Water, ABX, and FMT groups (related to Figure S1I) or with Fexaramine treatment (related to Figure S6B) was performed after inverse hyperbolic sine (asinh) transformation of metabolite intensities and removal of zero-variance variables. Hierarchical clustering was applied to both metabolites and samples using Euclidean distance and complete linkage (ward.D2). In Figure S6B, lateral annotation of p-value classes derived from a two-sided Wilcoxon test between the two groups. (related to Figures 1B; S1D and S1I, and S3A, S6B).

PCR amplification and TIDE analysis

Genomic DNA was isolated from transduced TSECs or MCA205 fibrosarcoma cell clones using the PureLink™ Genomic DNA Mini Kit (catalog no. K182002, Invitrogen). PCR products were purified with the PureLink™ PCR Micro Kit (catalog no. DNA editing efficiency was evaluated by uploading sequencing chromatograms to the Tracking of Indels by DEcomposition (TIDE) online platform (https://tide.nki.nl/)67 to calculate indel percentages. Enterocloster spp. treatment with BA

All three bacteria were incubated with 500 μM of either TCDCA, TBMCA, or GDCA. Each bacteria was grown in 2 mL of RCM medium overnight at 37 C. Each overnight culture’s OD600 was measured and for each strain, the OD was standardized to 0.05 in 1 mL of RCM. Samples were prepared in a 24 well plate containing 1 mL of RCM and 500 μM of one of the bile acids. The 24 well plates were incubated at 37 C for 24 h. After 24 h, each sample’s endpoint OD600 was measured and 300 μL of each culture was incubated in ice-cold methanol, vortexed briefly, and then sat on the benchtop overnight. The methanol extracts were then centrifuged and the supernatant was frozen at -80 C until it was run on the LC-MS/MS. Sterile RCM was used as a control and also received the bile acid treatment(s). Samples used for LC-MS/MS were prepared in a 96 well plate. Each well contained a 50 μL aliquot of each extracted culture and 50 μL of 50% methanol/phenol red (2.5 μg/mL). Injection blanks were prepared as 100% methanol and were the first well in each row. Quality control wells were located in column 12 of each row and contained 10 μL from each non-injection blank well in each row.

Genome assembly and BSH identification of strain Enterocloster clostridioformis 141

For the Enterocloster clostridioformis strain 141, genomic sequences underwent rigorous quality control by Trimmomatic (version 0.27) before being assembled using MEGAHIT68 (version 1.2.9). Protein-coding sequences were predicted by Prodigal (version 2.6.1) from the resulting contigs. To identify bile salt hydrolase (bsh) genes, a BLASTp search was performed against the predicted protein sequences using known BSH sequences as queries.

Distribution and diversity analysis of BSH in Enterocloster genomes

To investigate the distribution of BSH isoforms across the Enterocloster genus, a total of 151 E. clostridioformis and 158 E. bolteae genomes were retrieved from the NCBI database (2026.03.01). Potential bsh gene sequences were initially identified using Bakta69 (version 1.6.1) genome annotation and subsequently filtered based on protein length, retaining sequences between 300 and 400 amino acids to ensure functional integrity. Representative BSH sequences were then clustered using MMseqs2 with a 95% identity threshold to analyze the diversity and prevalence of specific isoforms within each species. For phylogenetic reconstruction, multiple sequence alignment of the identified BSH sequences was performed using MUSCLE within the MEGA (version 11), followed by the construction of a Maximum Likelihood (ML) tree to delineate the evolutionary relationships between these BSH isoforms.

Cancer cell viability after BA in vitro treatment

HT29 cells were purchased and grown as per ATCC. HT29 cells were left untreated or were exposed to BA (LCA, CDCA, TDCA, GDCA, UDCA, DCA) at different dosages as indicated for 48 h. Mitoxantrone (1 and 3 μM; Sigma-Aldrich; catalog No. M6545) and oxaliplatine (250 and 500 μM; Accord), known ICD inducers, were the positive control.70 Cells were treated with the bile acids for 48 h, then stained with Hoechst (33342; H3570, Thermofisher; 1/5000) + propidium iodide (PI; P4864-10mL; Sigma; 1/1000) for 30 min at 37°C before acquisition with an ImageXpress XL automated microscope (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA, US), equipped with a 20× PlanApo objective (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) with a minimum of 6 images per well. Nuclei were segmented using Hoechst fluorescence, and cell parameters (nuclear area, Hoechst and PI fluorescence intensities) were measured. Cell data were cleaned using a gating based on nuclear area and Hoechst intensity to separate “cells”, “healthy cells” and “debris”, and a threshold was determined on PI intensity to identify PI+ cells. Data were then summarized to a cell count and a percentage of PI cells by image. A one way unpaired Mann-Whitney U-test was performed.

DNA extraction and 16S rRNA targeted sequencing of mouse stools

Preparation and sequencing of mouse fecal samples were performed at IHU Méditerranée Infection, Marseille, France. Briefly, DNA was extracted using two protocols. The first protocol consisted of physical and chemical lysis, using glass powder and proteinase K, respectively, then processing using the Macherey-Nagel DNA Tissue Extraction Kit.71 The second protocol was identical to the first protocol, with the addition of glycoprotein lysis and deglycosylation steps.72 The resulting DNA was sequenced,73,74 targeting the V3–V4 regions of the 16S rRNA gene.

Bioinformatics, statistics and reproducibility of 16S rRNA targeted sequencing data

Microbial species which did not have a biological meaning (environmental, rumen, extra-mammals, food, etc.) were excluded from raw data. In addition, a strengthened prevalence cutoff of 5% was employed in order to enrobust the differential analysis (DA) thus resulting in 172 species that were considered for subsequent statistical analyses. Measurements of α diversity (within sample diversity) such as Richness, Shannon and Inverse Simpson indexes, were calculated at species level using the SciKit-learn package v1.0.1, starting from raw reads counts. For beta-diversity, data matrices were first transformed with pseudocount and centered-log-ratio (CLR), then normalized and standardized using QuantileTransformer and StandardScaler methods from Sci-Kit learn package v1.0.1. Normalization using the output_distribution = ‘normal’ option transforms each variable to a Gaussian-like shaped distribution, whilst the standardization results in each normalized variable having a mean of zero and variance of one (10.1080/2162402 × .2025.2484880). Exploratory analysis of β-diversity (between sample diversity) was calculated using the Bray-Curtis measure of dissimilarity and represented in principle Coordinate Analyses (PCoA), along with methods to compare groups of multivariate sample units (analysis of similarities - ANOSIM, permutational multivariate analysis of variance - PERMANOVA) to assess significance in data points clustering. ANOSIM and PERMANOVA were automatically calculated after 999 permutations, implemented with custom scripts (Python v3.8.2, Seaborn v0.11.2, SciKit-learn v1.0.1) (10.1080/2162402 × .2023.2237354). We implemented Partial Least Square Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) and the subsequent Variable Importance Plot (VIP) as a supervised analysis, wherein the VIP values (equal or higher than 1, default settings) are used to identify the most discriminant bacterial species among the cohorts. Mann–Whitney U test and Kruskal–Wallis test were employed to assess significance for pairwise or multiple comparisons, respectively, considering a p value ≤ 0.05 as significant. Clustermaps of microbial abundances were generated with Euclidean distance and Ward linkage algorithms by means of custom scripts (Python v3.8.2, Seaborn v0.11.2, SciKit-learn v1.0.1). Where clearly stated, all p values were corrected for multiple hypothesis testing using a two-stage Benjamini-Hochberg FDR at 10% (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ejca.2023.112948).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Overall survival and progression-free survival analyses of patients were performed using the Kaplan-Meier estimator, and Cox regression in uni-or multivariate analyses (Table S4) and the patient groups were compared using the log rank (Mantel-Cox) test. In in vitro and in vivo experiments, unless otherwise stated, statistical significance was determined by non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test (two unpaired groups), Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test (two groups of paired samples), Kruskal-Wallis H test (more than two groups), correcting the statistical significance for multiple comparisons and FDR using the two-stage step-up method of Benjamini and Hochberg using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA) and the statistical environment R (http://www.R-project.org/). For tumor growth analysis, we used Tumor Growth available at https://kroemerlab.shinyapps.io/TumGrowth/. p-value and FDR p-value adjusted <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All values are mean ± (SD) or (SEM) as stated in the legends. The number of independent biological replicates for each experiment and the sample size of each experimental group or condition are provided in the figure legends.
