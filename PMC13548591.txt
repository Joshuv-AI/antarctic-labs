Chinese version of the labour ward job satisfaction scale for midwives: translation, cultural adaptation and psychometric validation
Authors: Jing Li, Yuxuan Zeng, Xueyan Wei, Zhiqiang Cheng, Baozhen Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMidwives’ job satisfaction is essential to guarantee maternal and infant delivery safety and improve the quality of midwifery services. Along with the transformation of childbirth models and the rapid development of midwifery specialty, there is a lack of clinically applicable assessment instruments with rigorous psychometric validation tailored to domestic labour ward settings in China, which impedes accurate evaluation of current job satisfaction among labour ward midwives.ObjectiveThis study aimed to translate and conduct cross-cultural adaptation of the English translation of the original Spanish scale version Labour Ward Job Satisfaction Scale for Midwives (LWJSS-M), and evaluate the psychometric properties of its Chinese version.MethodsThis cross-sectional methodological study was conducted from March to May 2026 across 23 hospitals in Jiangxi and Hubei Provinces, China. A total of 456 midwives were recruited via convenience sampling for questionnaire investigation. Following Brislin’s translation guidelines, the English translation of the original Spanish scale Labour Ward Job Satisfaction Scale for Midwives (LWJSS-M) was translated into Chinese through forward translation, back-translation and cross-cultural adaptation procedures. Subsequently, psychometric properties of the Chinese version were tested. Construct validity was examined by exploratory factor analysis (EFA, Group A, n = 228) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA, Group B, n = 228). Convergent validity, discriminant validity, internal consistency, split-half reliability and test-retest reliability were assessed in the present study.ResultsThe Chinese version of the Labour Ward Job Satisfaction Scale for Midwives contained 37 items distributed across 5 dimensions. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) extracted five factors, accounting for a cumulative explained variance of 60.072%. The overall Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale was 0.936, and the Cronbach’s α values for each dimension ranged from 0.889 to 0.932; the split-half reliability was 0.791, and the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC, test-retest reliability) was 0.957. The scale-level content validity index (S-CVI) reached 0.993. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) yielded satisfactory model fit indices: CMIN/DF = 1.107, CFI = 0.986, TLI = 0.985, RMSEA = 0.022, SRMR = 0.042.ConclusionThe Chinese version of the Labour Ward Job Satisfaction Scale for Midwives has sound psychometric properties. This scale can effectively measure job satisfaction among Chinese labour ward midwives and serves as a reliable instrument for nursing administrators to conduct relevant assessments.Keywords: Midwifery, Job satisfaction, Reliability, Validity, Scale

Background

Midwives constitute the core workforce for safeguarding maternal and neonatal delivery safety and delivering high-quality pregnancy care services, and play a pivotal role in optimizing the maternal and child health service system and fostering the corresponding professional workforce [1]. As professionals specializing in maternal and infant health, midwives provide continuous care throughout the antenatal, perinatal and postnatal periods. Their core clinical responsibilities include facilitating women’s positive childbirth experience and implementing woman-centered midwifery care. Beyond clinical duties, they undertake diverse additional missions: offering emotional support and psychological empowerment for childbearing women, delivering health promotion and education, and advocating for women’s reproductive health rights [2].

The multifaceted roles of midwives expose them to unique occupational challenges alongside professional rewards. These challenges markedly affect their job satisfaction and career progression, as well as the quality and safety of clinical care they provide. Midwives’ job satisfaction directly correlates with their burnout prevalence, turnover intention and long-term career trajectories, and exerts a crucial impact on maternal and infant care quality and safety. According to data released by the World Health Organization (WHO) [3], a global shortage of 900 000 midwives exists worldwide, yet midwives can deliver approximately 90% of essential sexual, reproductive, maternal, newborn and adolescent health interventions. Accordingly, understanding midwives’ job satisfaction is critical to cultivating a workplace environment that improves staff engagement, work motivation and occupational fulfillment. Existing evidence suggests that low job satisfaction is a major driver of increased burnout and turnover intention among midwives [4].

Job satisfaction comprehensively reflects individuals’ affective responses, emotional experiences and attitudes toward their occupation; it is a core employee attitudinal variable derived from subjective evaluations of multiple work-related domains, including job content, salary and benefits, promotion prospects, interpersonal relationships and career development [5]. For midwives, job satisfaction is inherently closely linked to their perceptions of working environments, coworker rapport and personal advancement opportunities [6]. Within midwifery practice, job satisfaction is reflected in positive collegial collaboration, opportunities to build meaningful therapeutic bonds with childbearing women, clinical autonomy and proficient midwifery skills [7]. Meanwhile, midwives’ job satisfaction is influenced by fluctuating birth rates, excessive workload, chronically stressful working conditions, staffing shortages and inflexible shift scheduling [8].China’s midwifery workforce faces distinctive domestic challenges beyond the global shortage of midwifery staff. Successive adjustments to national fertility policies have reshaped obstetric service demands: aggregate hospital delivery volumes have trended downward, yet the share of high-risk, complex deliveries has expanded rapidly, driven by delayed childbearing and wider adoption of assisted reproductive technologies. This dual occupational burden consists of reduced total birth numbers paired with elevated clinical complexity of individual cases, and it creates chronically stressful labour ward environments, inadequate staffing allocation, and inflexible shift scheduling, all of which significantly erode midwives’ job satisfaction. Therefore, rigorously validated scientific assessment tools are urgently needed to support the evaluation of midwives’ occupational experience.

At present, two dedicated scales exist to measure midwives’ job satisfaction; however, neither covers specific elements of intrapartum practice, namely the Midwifery Process Questionnaire (MPQ) [9] and the Iranian Midwives Job Satisfaction Inventory (MJSI) [10]. Although both scales contain general domains concerning midwives’ professional perceptions and satisfaction, they lack items related to labour and childbirth care, making them inappropriate for job-satisfaction assessment targeting labour ward midwives.

Pérez-Castejón et [11] originally developed the Labour Ward Job Satisfaction Scale for Midwives (LWJSS-M) in Spanish for Spanish clinical settings in 2025, and the published paper included an official English translation of the original Spanish scale as an attachment. This authorized English translated version was adopted as the source instrument for our Chinese cross-cultural adaptation, with an overall McDonald’s ω of 0.937, indicating satisfactory reliability and validity. The five dimensions of the original scale cover core work scenarios for labour ward midwives globally, encompassing clinical delivery skills, interdisciplinary teamwork, intra-professional communication among midwives, prenatal education services, and workplace resources and working environments. These core work modules align closely with the daily clinical responsibilities of midwives in China. Consistent with this dimensional framework, the domestic midwifery service system also adopts a childbirth-centred care model, featuring collaborative work between midwives, obstetricians and anaesthesiologists, standardized prenatal education for parturient families, and widespread workplace strains stemming from insufficient staffing and limited delivery beds. None of the five dimensions contradict domestic clinical practices, indicating that this five-factor structure is a priori suitable for cross-cultural transfer to the Chinese context.Following formal permission from the original scale authors, the English LWJSS-M was cross-culturally adapted into Chinese and subjected to psychometric testing in the present study. The resulting Chinese version serves as a context-specific instrument to measure job satisfaction for Chinese labour ward midwives. Given the lack of a universally applicable domestic assessment tool, this study aimed to translate, cross-culturally adapt and psychometrically validate the LWJSS-M, so as to establish a reliable instrument for evaluating job satisfaction of labour ward midwives in China.

Methods

Study design and participants

A cross-sectional design was adopted. This cross-sectional study was conducted from March to May 2026 in 23 tertiary hospitals in Jiangxi and Hubei Provinces, China, and eligible midwives were recruited via convenience sampling.

Inclusion criteria: (1) holding a valid Chinese Nurse Practicing Certificate and Maternal and Infant Health Qualification Certificate; (2) full-time employment as a labour ward midwife with no less than one year of working experience; (3) voluntary participation with signed written informed consent.

Exclusion criteria: (1) midwives on further education leave, sick leave, or maternity leave during the investigation period; (2) non-permanent staff, including student nurses and visiting midwives.

The sample size was determined based on the empirical rule for exploratory factor analysis (EFA), which recommends 5–10 participants per scale item [12]. Given that the LWJSS-M contains 37 items, the preliminary required sample size ranged from 185 to 370. Considering that confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) generally requires a minimum sample of 200 participants, a total of 456 midwives were finally enrolled. This sample size satisfied the statistical requirements for both EFA and CFA and ensured the scientific rigor and reliability of the study results.

Instruments

Demographic questionnaire

The questionnaire was developed based on systematic literature review and expert panel discussion. The baseline demographic information collected included gender, age, educational level, marital status, position, working experience, monthly night-shift frequency, employment method, monthly income, hospital type, and the maximum number of delivery beds that each midwife can care for.

English version of the Labour Ward Job Satisfaction Scale for Midwives (LWJSS-M)

Originally developed in Spanish by Pérez-Castejón et [11] in 2025, the LWJSS-M is a specialized instrument for assessing job satisfaction among labour ward midwives.The English version used in the present study is the official English translation of the original Spanish scale provided by the original authors, which served as the sole source version for our translation and cultural adaptation work.It consists of 37 items distributed across five dimensions: professional skills for childbirth support (Items 1–17), well-functioning multidisciplinary teamwork (Items 18–25), working conditions and determinants of job satisfaction (Items 32–37),intradisciplinary relationships and collaboration (Items 26–29), and satisfaction with antenatal education (Items 30–31). All items are scored on a 4-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly dissatisfied) to 4 (strongly satisfied). The total score of the scale ranges from 37 to 148, with higher scores reflecting higher levels of job satisfaction. The original scale achieved an overall McDonald’s ω of 0.937, confirming excellent reliability and validity.

Procedures

Translation and cross-cultural adaptation

In October 2025, the research team obtained formal permission from the original scale developer Marta Pérez-Castejón via email. Following the Brislin translation model [13] and guidelines for cross-cultural adaptation of research instruments [14], forward and back-translation procedures were implemented to ensure conceptual, semantic and content equivalence of the Chinese version.

First, two Chinese native midwifery specialists proficient in English independently translated the original English scale into Chinese versions A1 and A2; one was an associate professor with overseas study experience, and the other a doctoral nursing student who passed the College English Test Band 6. A postgraduate student majoring in midwifery synthesized A1 and A2 into a consolidated Chinese draft A3. Afterwards, one nursing English instructor and another nursing postgraduate independently back-translated A3 into two English versions (B1 and B2), which were further integrated into version B3 by the research team to amend semantic errors arising from forward translation.Subsequently, six midwifery experts and two psychology experts (all at associate senior professional title or above with ≥ 10 years of working experience) evaluated and revised the scale via the Delphi expert consultation method, and all experts participated voluntarily. The Chinese draft was revised in accordance with expert feedback and cross-checked against the original scale to guarantee consistency in concept, semantics and content. Several items were adjusted to adapt to China’s cultural and linguistic context and domestic midwifery practice. The revised Chinese scale together with the consolidated back-translated English draft was sent to the original author for verification.Prior to formal data collection, a pilot test was conducted among 30 midwives to examine questionnaire acceptability and completion time. The questionnaire took approximately 8–10 min to finish. Items reported ambiguous by participants were further modified, yielding the finalized Chinese version of the Labour Ward Job Satisfaction Scale for Midwives.

Data collection

Data collection was conducted via the Wenjuanxing online platform between March and May 2026. First, the research team communicated with the nursing directors of all 23 participating hospitals to explain the research aims and reach cooperation agreements. Prior to questionnaire distribution, we prepared a unified written screening checklist with objective judgment rules for all inclusion and exclusion criteria, which was distributed to every nursing department head. A centralized online training meeting was then organized to elaborate each screening rule in detail, so all hospitals adopted identical selection standards. All nursing directors followed this unified checklist to identify eligible midwives before sharing the electronic questionnaire link.Strict anonymity protection measures were implemented to avoid social desirability bias: the questionnaire did not collect names, employee IDs or any other identifiable personal information. After reading the electronic informed consent and giving voluntary agreement, participants filled out the survey. All questions were set as required fields to reduce missing data. Several quality control rules were applied to filter invalid questionnaires: each IP address was limited to one submission only; forms with straight-lining responses or a completion duration shorter than 8 min were eliminated. A total of 461 questionnaires were sent out, among which 456 valid copies were retained for analysis, yielding an effective response rate of 98.9%.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0 and AMOS 29.0 software. Categorical variables were described as frequencies (n) and percentages (%), while continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (±s). A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Item analysis

Item analysis was conducted using the critical ratio method and correlation coefficient analysis. For the critical ratio method: total scale scores of all questionnaires were sorted in ascending order; the top 27% and bottom 27% of participants were categorized into the high-score and low-score groups, respectively. An independent-samples t-test was performed between the two groups. Items with a critical ratio (CR) > 3 and statistically significant difference (P < 0.05) were retained to guarantee satisfactory discrimination of the scale [15]. For the correlation coefficient method: Pearson correlation coefficients (r) between each individual item score and the total scale score (with the target item excluded) were calculated to examine item homogeneity. Items with r ≤ 0.4 were eliminated [16].

Reliability analysis

Reliability testing of the Chinese LWJSS-M was performed to evaluate its internal consistency and temporal stability. Internal consistency was estimated by Cronbach’s α coefficients for the overall scale and each dimension; a Cronbach’s α > 0.7 indicated acceptable internal consistency [17]. Split-half reliability was calculated by splitting items into odd-numbered and even-numbered subsets; split-half reliability above 0.7 was regarded as acceptable [18]. Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was adopted to assess test-retest reliability (temporal stability). Thirty randomly selected participants completed the scale again after a two-week interval, and ICC > 0.7 was defined as good test-retest reliability.

Validity analysis

Content validity

Content validity was assessed via the Delphi method. Six midwifery specialists and two psychology experts were invited to rate the relevance of each item on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (irrelevant) to 4 (highly relevant). The item-level content validity index (I-CVI) and scale-level content validity index (S-CVI) were calculated. I-CVI ≥ 0.78 and S-CVI ≥ 0.90 were considered acceptable thresholds [19].

Construct validity

Construct validity was examined using exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). A total of 456 midwives were randomly allocated into Group A (n = 228) for EFA and Group B (n = 228) for CFA. For Group A, principal component analysis with varimax orthogonal rotation was performed to explore the underlying factor structure and verify its consistency with the preset dimensional framework. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) test and Bartlett’s test of sphericity were used to check the suitability for factor extraction; factor analysis was feasible when KMO>0.6 and Bartlett’s test reached statistical significance (P < 0.05) [20]. Common factors with eigenvalues>1 were retained, and items with factor loading<0.4 or obvious cross-loading were eliminated. A cumulative explained variance>60% together with item factor loading>0.5 indicated favorable construct validity [21].For Group B, CFA was conducted to verify the factor structure derived from EFA, with extracted common factors set as latent variables and individual items as observed variables; the structural equation model was established using AMOS. Model fit was judged by the following criteria: CMIN/DF ≤ 3, RMSEA<0.08, SRMR<0.08, CFI>0.9 and TLI>0.9 represented adequate model fit. Convergent validity was evaluated based on average variance extracted (AVE) and composite reliability (CR); AVE ≥ 0.5 and CR ≥ 0.7 were regarded as satisfactory convergent validity [20]. Discriminant validity was confirmed when the square root of each dimension’s AVE exceeded the correlation coefficient between paired dimensions [22].

Ethical approval

All participants provided written informed consent after full explanation of study objectives. Participants retained the right to withdraw from the survey at any time without penalty, and all questionnaires were completed anonymously. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (Approval No. IIT[2026] Clinical Ethics Review No.203).

Translation and revision of the scale

The Chinese version of the Labour Ward Job Satisfaction Scale for Midwives was translated strictly in accordance with the Brislin translation model via forward translation, back-translation and cross-cultural adaptation, followed by revision and refinement based on consultations with six midwifery experts and two psychology specialists. The finalized scale consisted of five dimensions and 37 items. Results of the Delphi consultation demonstrated a 100% expert response rate, an expert authority coefficient of 0.968, and a Kendall’s coordination coefficient of 0.222.Five items were linguistically revised to fit China’s cultural context and domestic clinical practice, with detailed modifications listed below: Item 4 was revised from “Satisfaction with the rate of natural births in my healthcare area”to “Satisfaction with the natural birth rate in my department”;Item 13 was revised from “Satisfaction with the atmosphere of respect for women’s choices in childbirth” to “Satisfaction with the provision of a climate for maternal delivery choices”;Item 23 was revised from “Satisfaction with the support I receive from my line managers” to “My satisfaction with the support I received from the chief obstetrician nurse”;Item 31 was revised from “Satisfaction with the antenatal education received by partners prior to the onset of labour” to “Satisfaction with prenatal education for maternal immediate family members before delivery initiation”;Item 32 was revised from “Satisfaction with the midwife-to-woman ratio in my department” to “Satisfaction with the midwife-to-delivery-bed ratio in the department”.The modified scale achieved better cultural compatibility and readability. All 30 midwives participating in the pilot test reported that all items were easy to comprehend.

Item analysis

Item analysis based on the critical ratio method and correlation analysis was performed using data from 456 participants to screen item quality. The critical ratio (CR) values of all items ranged from 8.88 to 17.23, with statistically significant differences between the high-score and low-score groups (P < 0.001). Pearson correlation coefficients between each item and the scale total score (excluding the corresponding item) varied from 0.407 to 0.643 (P < 0.001). Since all items satisfied the retention criteria of CR > 3 and r > 0.4, none of the items were eliminated and all 37 items were retained in the formal scale.

Reliability analysis

The overall Cronbach’sαcoefficient of the Chinese LWJSS-M was 0.936, and the Cronbach’sαvalues for each dimension ranged from 0.889 to 0.932, indicating excellent internal consistency and favorable item homogeneity for both the whole scale and its subdomains (Table 2). The split-half reliability of the total scale was 0.791. The total test-retest ICC was 0.957, with dimensional ICC values ranging between 0.829 and 0.940, which verified satisfactory temporal stability and reproducibility of the scale.

Validity analysis

Content validity

Six midwifery experts and two psychology experts were recruited to evaluate content validity. The item-level content validity index (I-CVI) ranged from 0.875 to 1, and the scale-level content validity index (S-CVI) was 0.993. All I-CVI values exceeded 0.78 and the S-CVI was higher than 0.8, suggesting satisfactory content validity of the scale.

Construct validity

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA)

EFA was performed on Group A randomly sampled from all participants (n = 228). The feasibility of factor analysis was verified beforehand. The KMO index was 0.924, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity reached statistical significance (X2=4650.123, P < 0.001), supporting the appropriateness of EFA. Five factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 were extracted via principal component analysis, collectively explaining 60.072% of the total variance. All items had factor loadings above 0.4 without obvious cross-loadings (Table 3), demonstrating strong correlations between individual items and their corresponding latent factors.

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA)

CFA was conducted on Group B (n = 228) using the maximum likelihood estimation method (Fig. 1). Following the classic psychometric criteria of Fornell and Larcker (1981) [23], convergent validity is still regarded as acceptable if the CR of a latent construct is greater than 0.7, even when its AVE marginally fails to reach 0.5. Dimension 1 achieved a CR of 0.941, and all standardized factor loadings of its items were above 0.6. Taken together, these indicators confirm acceptable convergent validity for Dimension A. Discussion

Following rigorous translation and cross-cultural adaptation, the Labour Ward Job Satisfaction Scale for Midwives was successfully adapted into its Chinese version in the present study. Psychometric findings verified favorable psychometric properties of the Chinese scale, which can serve as a reliable instrument to measure job satisfaction among Chinese labour ward midwives and provide evidence for nursing administrators to identify workplace problems and develop targeted management strategies.

Existing studies have verified that the MPQ [9] and MSJI [10] are specialized scales for measuring midwives’ job satisfaction. Compared with the LWJSS-M, the MPQ does not contain an independent dimension of working conditions, while the MSJI lacks the dimension of professional skills in supporting childbirth. Working conditions including staffing allocation, salary and remuneration are core factors shaping midwives’ occupational experience and satisfaction. As the LWJSS-M covers both of these two critical domains simultaneously, it exhibits greater comprehensiveness and pertinence when evaluating job satisfaction among labour ward midwives in China.

The Chinese scale was developed in strict accordance with the Brislin translation framework for cross-cultural adaptation [24]. To guarantee conceptual, semantic and content equivalence with the original scale, the drafted Chinese version was submitted to the original author for proofreading so as to secure translation accuracy. During cultural adaptation, five items were revised in line with domestic midwifery practice and Chinese cultural context. For instance, the original wording referring to “healthcare area” was modified to “department”, and “line managers” was replaced with “chief obstetric nurse” consistent with China’s hospital administrative system and conventional job titles; “partners of pregnant women” was adjusted to “immediate family members of parturients” to fit the unique childbirth companionship culture in China. In the original Spanish clinical setting, pregnant women are mostly accompanied only by their spouses to receive prenatal education [25]; by contrast, parents and other close relatives routinely accompany parturients throughout labour and antenatal education in Chinese obstetric wards [26]. The original expression “midwife-to-woman ratio” was revised to “midwife-to-delivery-bed ratio”, which conforms to the national standardized workload evaluation criteria for Chinese hospitals. Spanish labour wards evaluate staffing burden based on the number of women receiving care, while Chinese tertiary obstetric departments uniformly take delivery beds as the core statistical indicator for staffing allocation assessment [27].

Reliability is an essential indicator reflecting the internal consistency and temporal stability of a measurement scale [12]. The overall Cronbach’s α of the Chinese LWJSS-M was 0.932–0.936 across all dimensions, demonstrating excellent item homogeneity. Consistent with the original Spanish scale (total McDonald’s ω = 0.937, dimension ω = 0.804–0.933), the Chinese version achieved comparable internal consistency. Additionally, the split-half reliability was 0.791 and the overall test-retest ICC was 0.957 (dimension ICC: 0.829–0.940), confirming robust temporal stability.Collectively, the Chinese LWJSS-M possesses satisfactory reliability.

Validity refers to the accuracy of measurement relative to its intended construct.Content validity was evaluated via the Delphi method, and the content validity index (CVI) was calculated. The original Spanish scale reported item-level CVI (I-CVI) ranging from 0.83 to 1. In the current study, the I-CVI ranged from 0.875 to 1, and the scale-level CVI (S-CVI) reached 0.993, which demonstrated satisfactory content validity of the Chinese version.

Construct validity was examined to verify the rationality of the scale’s theoretical framework. The EFA yielded a KMO value of 0.924; five factors with eigenvalues above 1 were extracted, accounting for 60.072% of the total cumulative variance, and factor loadings for all items exceeded 0.4. These findings indicated that the factorial structure of the Chinese version was highly consistent with the original scale and could adequately reflect the latent construct of labour ward midwives’ job satisfaction.The CFA produced desirable model fit indices: CMIN/DF = 1.107, CFI = 0.986, and TLI = 0.985, all meeting the criteria for good model fit and further supporting the robustness and rationality of the five-factor structure.In terms of convergent validity, although the AVE of Dimension 1 was slightly below the threshold of 0.5, its composite reliability reached 0.941, and all standardized item loadings of this dimension exceeded 0.6. In accordance with classic psychometric standards proposed by Fornell and Larcker [23], latent constructs with CR>0.7 are still regarded as having acceptable convergent validity even if AVE marginally fails to reach 0.5.while the AVE values of the remaining dimensions all exceeded 0.5, and their CR values ranged from 0.897 to 0.941. These results indicated acceptable convergent validity according to psychometric criteria. For discriminant validity, the square root of the AVE of each dimension was greater than the correlation coefficients between paired dimensions, which reflected adequate discriminant power across all latent factors.

Limitations of the present study

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, participants were enrolled via convenience sampling exclusively from Jiangxi and Hubei Provinces in central China, with no recruitment of labour ward midwives from other geographic regions, which may restrict the nationwide generalizability of this scale. Future research could recruit midwives from diverse administrative regions and hospitals at different tiers to further verify its applicability across China. Second, all data relied on self-reported questionnaires, which is susceptible to reporting bias. To improve the robustness of job satisfaction assessment, multi-source evaluation is recommended in follow-up investigations, including supervisor ratings, parturient feedback and behavioural observation to complement self-reported data and mitigate potential measurement bias.Third, although unified screening criteria were fully explained and centralized training was delivered to all nursing directors in advance, the questionnaires were entirely distributed by these administrators, which inevitably introduced gatekeeper bias and selection bias.We acknowledge that failing to implement IRT constitutes a methodological limitation of this study.

Conclusions

The present study verified that the Chinese version of the Labour Ward Job Satisfaction Scale for Midwives has satisfactory reliability, content validity, construct validity and convergent validity, which can be used as a reliable instrument to assess job satisfaction among labour ward midwives. Accordingly, this scale enables nursing administrators to conduct objective evaluation, formulate targeted intervention strategies, improve midwives’ job satisfaction and guarantee the quality of maternal and infant care.
