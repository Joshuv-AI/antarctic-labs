The Unsampled Truth: Psychometrics in SLMs Measure Prompt Artifacts, Not Psychological Constructs
Nils Schwager, Christoph Hau, Simon Münker, Achim Rettinger

Abstract
When prompting SLMs for psychometric assessments, researchers assume the outputs reflect semantic reasoning. We evaluate this premise across 13 open-weights models (0.6B to 14B parameters) using a prompt variation framework that separates semantic signals from prompt artifacts. By systematically varying personas, instructions, items, and option symbols, we find that artifactual variance frequently overpowers the semantic signal. In these cases, models predominantly reflect prompt compliance rather than simulated psychological traits. While these findings limit SLM utility in psychometrics, our framework provides a diagnostic tool to identify destructive artifacts and isolate semantic understanding for future frontier-model research.

Introduction

When researchers prompt an LLM to take a personality test, they are operating under a fragile premise. The current methodology involves feeding models specific personas and standardized inventories to extract simulated human attitudes and behaviors
Jiang et al. 2024
;
Lee et al. 2025
;
Zheng et al. 2025
. However, this paradigm rests on a critical assumption: that the model’s predicted output is primarily a reflection of the injected persona and the semantic meaning of the survey item.

Ongoing research questions whether LLMs can accurately predict human response behavior

;

;

;

;

However, core elements including the task instruction remain critically under-researched. More importantly, the field lacks a unified experimental design that evaluates prompt components (instruction, option symbols, item & persona) simultaneously. Because prior studies have not quantified the impact of variations of these components when their semantic meaning is held constant, the true signal-to-artifact ratios of LLM psychometrics remain unknown. Our work makes two primary contributions to the evaluation and critique of SLM psychometrics:

A Variance Partitioning Framework:

We introduce a diagnostic pipeline to decompose measurement error in SLM-based survey experiments. By constructing prompt variations while preserving semantics, we compute the Average Pairwise Wasserstein Distance (APWD) and apply distance-based PERMANOVA to isolate the psychological signal from artifactual variance.

Artifact Dominance in SLM Psychometrics:

Applying this framework across 13 open-weights models (0.6B to 14B parameters), we systematically quantify the artifact dominance that raises concerns about operational reliability of SLMs for psychological assessment. We demonstrate that small models are predominantly governed by prompt variants, remaining deaf to semantic variations. Crucially, scaling to 14B parameters fails to resolve this brittleness. Because these artifacts frequently eclipse the semantic signal, we conclude that in many settings, prompt artifacts account for a substantial proportion of explained variance, complicating the interpretation of latent psychological traits.

2.3.1
Logit-Based Distribution Extraction

We apply a standard softmax function (
t=1
) over the full vocabulary to extract the absolute probability mass of the target symbols and compute the joint probability for multi-token variants as the product of their sequential token probabilities. Treating the output as a distribution captures the model’s underlying uncertainty across the trait spectrum, avoiding the information loss caused by greedy text generation.

2.3.2
Total and Marginal Variance (APWD)

Because Likert responses are ordinal, standard probability metrics (e.g., Jensen-Shannon divergence) fail to penalize the ordinal magnitude of an error. Therefore, we quantify distributional shifts using the 1-Wasserstein distance (
W_{1}
), which calculates the minimum transport cost required to transform one distribution into another.

4
Conclusion & Future Work

We developed the Prompt Variant Matrix and distance-based variance decomposition and find that prompt-induced variance remains substantial across tested open-weight models up to 14B parameters, limiting the reliability of psychometric interpretations under current prompting paradigms. One possible explanation is that instruction tuning introduces strong formatting priors, while Transformer decoding may not be well-suited for stable single-token psychometric prediction
Wang et al. 2024b
. While this dominance raises serious concerns about the reliability of SLMs for psychometric use under current prompting paradigms, our framework offers two paths forward. First, it serves as a diagnostic tool; researchers can utilize this design to identify specific prompt variants that steer a model heavily away from its distribution. Second, it provides a mechanism to isolate artifactual variance from semantic impact. This isolation opens the layer of assessing a model’s latent semantic understanding, potentially explaining why prior literature has struggled to establish robust construct validity.

To determine if these vulnerabilities persist at the frontier limit, future research could apply this framework to significantly larger architectures (e.g., >70B parameters). Finally, because the artifactual noise might be exacerbated by lacking trait representation in the baseline Nemotron personas, future studies must test whether injecting deeper, highly explicit psychological anchors can overpower this artifactual variance.

Limitations

While our Prompt Variant Matrix and distance-based variance decomposition provide a robust diagnostic framework for quantifying measurement error, this study operates within several methodological boundaries:

Non-Exhaustive Parameter Space

The structural axes of the Prompt Variant Matrix represent a targeted, not exhaustive, sample of potential prompts. We bounded our experimental design to instruction constraints, option symbols, and paraphrasing. We did not evaluate variations in structural placement (e.g., shifting the baseline persona from the system prompt to the user prompt) or ordinal directionality (e.g., reversing the order of answer options), which prior literature
Zeng et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024a
suggests could induce further distributional shifts.

Sensitivity to Variant Selection & Trait Density

The magnitude of the measured variance is dependent on our specific variant configurations. While we anchored our prompts in established published methodologies to ensure ecological validity, the framework remains sensitive to variants that diverge too sharply. Furthermore, the baseline Nemotron personas may lack the latent trait density necessary to measurably anchor complex Big Five or SD3 dimensions. This weak initial trait representation could exacerbate the severe artifact dominance we observed.

Restriction to Small-Scale Open Models

While the observed artifact dominance restricts the operational use of SLMs in psychometric environments, we intentionally restricted our evaluation to open-weights architectures to ensure transparent, reproducible access to the raw logit distributions required for the Wasserstein distance calculations. Consequently, we cannot definitively conclude whether closed-weights, frontier-scale models (e.g., ChatGPT, Claude) successfully overcome this prompt compliance, or if extreme parameter scaling merely obfuscates the underlying structural brittleness.

Absence of Human Baselines

We evaluated structural instability purely
in silico
. It is a well-documented phenomenon in classical psychometrics
Krosnick and Alwin 1987
;
Bogner and Landrock 2016
that human subjects also exhibit response-order bias when item wording or scale formats change. Because we did not benchmark against a parallel human control group, we cannot precisely quantify the divergence between expected human methodological variance and the total semantic displacement observed in the SLMs.

Exclusion of Interaction Effects

Our PERMANOVA analysis establishes a conservative lower bound for artifact-induced measurement error. While complex interaction effects between prompt components undoubtedly exist, our data reveal that individual SLMs possess highly idiosyncratic and overfitted structural signatures. Attempting to model more generalized interaction effects may overcomplicate the analysis and obscure the fundamental finding: that the effects of these artifacts are already sufficient to overpower the semantic signal.

Ethical Considerations

The primary ethical consideration arising from our findings relates to how researchers and practitioners interpret SLM-generated survey responses. Our results demonstrate that researchers, especially those employing naive prompting approaches, must be highly cautious when using SLMs for psychometrics. Because small-scale models exhibit strong sensitivity with respect to their prompting, treating their outputs as valid psychological measurements risks propagating artifactual noise as human-like reasoning.

Deployment Risks

If researchers do not isolate this artifact dominance, the field risks establishing invalid literature built on prompt compliance rather than simulated psychology. Beyond academic contexts, the operational deployment of these models in applied settings, such as synthetic user testing, algorithmic profiling, or automated screening, poses significant real-world risks. Relying on psychological predictions that can be drastically altered simply by changing an option symbol from a letter to a numeral makes any downstream decision inherently arbitrary and potentially harmful.

Reproducibility Constraints

Finally, our findings underscore the necessity of methodological transparency. To prevent the misinterpretation of artifacts as simulated traits, researchers must publicly release their exact prompt templates and parameters. Without this transparency, validating the true capabilities of SLMs in computational social science remains highly unreliable.
