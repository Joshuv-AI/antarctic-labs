Predominance of autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia in a nephrology department
Authors: Manuel Laslandes, Claire Leman, Charles Ronsin, Simon Ville, Catherine Ternisien, Antoine Babuty, Nicolas Drillaud, Christophe Masset
Published: 2026

Introduction

Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) is a rare immunologic side effect of unfractionated heparin and low-molecular-weight heparin, occurring in approximately 0.065% of hospitalizations and associated with a mortality rate of 10% to 15% within 30 days [1,2]. It typically presents within 5 to 14 days of heparin initiation and is associated with a paradoxical prothrombotic state through immunoglobulin G antibodies directed against complexes of platelet factor 4 (PF4) and heparin, which activate platelets [3]. Nephrology patients are at risk due to heparin use for renal replacement therapy (RRT) [4], especially those newly initiated on hemodialysis in the acute care setting [5]. Autoimmune HIT (aHIT), characterized by heparin-independent platelet activation, persistent thrombocytopenia, and severe outcomes, has been described [6]. Autoimmune diseases might be associated with HIT, without distinguishing the classic from autoimmune forms of HIT [7,8]. The objective of this work was to describe the potential association of immune-mediated nephropathies with HIT and notably aHIT in an academic nephrology department.

Short Methods

We retrospectively queried our hospital’s hemostasis laboratory database for all HIT assays requested by our department between January 1, 2013, and December 31, 2024. Anti-PF4/heparin antibodies were detected by chemiluminescence immunoassay (AcuStar, Werfen) or an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), and functional assays were a serotonin release assay (SRA) or an in-house platelet aggregation test. HIT was confirmed by positivity of both immunologic and functional assays. Data on clinical characteristics, heparin exposure, and outcomes were extracted from anonymized records. Patients were followed for up to 6 months after diagnosis. Detailed methods are provided in Supplementary Methods.

Five of the 7 patients had concomitant immune-mediated kidney diseases: 2 had anti–glomerular basement membrane (anti-GBM) disease (patients 1 and 2), 2 had scleroderma renal crisis (SRC; patients 3 and 4), and 1 had phospholipase A2 receptor (PLA2R)–associated membranous nephropathy (patient 5). During the same period, 14 patients were diagnosed with anti-GBM disease and 9 with SRC at our center, corresponding to a HIT prevalence of 14.3% and 22.2%, respectively, in these subgroups.

Patients were classified according to the presence of heparin-independent platelet activation (ie, positive functional assay under buffer control), a defining feature of aHIT [6]. SRA was performed in 5 patients. Three (patients 1, 2, and 3) exhibited heparin-independent platelet activation and were therefore identified as “patients with aHIT phenotype” (Figure A). The two patients with anti-GBM disease both exhibited this profile. In the 2 patients who did not undergo SRA (patients 4 and 7), in-house platelet aggregation testing was positive only in the presence of heparin, consistent with classic HIT. Patients with and without an aHIT phenotype had similar 4T scores and platelet nadirs (Figure B). However, platelet recovery was markedly delayed in patients with aHIT: median time to platelet count >100 G/L following heparin discontinuation was 19 days (IQR, 9-52 days) vs 7 days (IQR, 3-13 days) in classic HIT (Figure B). Clinical outcomes were severe: 6 patients required RRT and 4 died within 6 months. Death occurred in the context of treatment limitation for severe COVID-19 (patient 2), progressive scleroderma (patient 3), septic shock with disseminated intravascular coagulation (patient 4), and refractory cardiogenic shock due to aortic stenosis (patient 6).

Discussion

Despite frequent heparin exposure in nephrology wards, HIT remained rare in our department (0.079%), consistent with previous reports [1]. Strikingly, 5 of 7 diagnosed patients had immune-mediated kidney disease, including 2 with anti-GBM disease, representing 14% of all anti-GBM disease cases diagnosed during the study period. A prior series similarly reported an overrepresentation of anti-GBM disease or antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody–associated vasculitis among HIT cases [9], although no antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody–associated vasculitis was observed in our cohort.

Importantly, 3 patients demonstrated heparin-independent platelet activation, consistent with an aHIT. All had immune-mediated kidney disease, including both anti-GBM disease cases. Several features supported this classification. First, patients 1 and 2 were exposed to low-dose heparin, either as anticoagulation for dialysis circuits or as a catheter lock solution, corresponding to the “heparin flush HIT” form [6,10]. Moreover, patients 2 and 3 displayed markedly elevated anti-PF4/heparin antibody titers, a common characteristic of aHIT [11]. Even higher titers may have been underestimated in patients 1 and 2 due to therapeutic plasma exchanges performed as part of anti-GBM disease treatment. Finally, platelet recovery to >100 G/L required 9 to 52 days in these 3 patients, consistent with the “persisting HIT” phenotype [6].

Although limited by small samples, these findings suggest that patients with immune-mediated kidney diseases may be more susceptible to HIT and that a subset may develop the autoimmune form. Several mechanisms could explain this association. Infectious triggers, predisposing to aHIT [6] and frequently reported in patients with immune-mediated nephropathies [12], may initiate a shared immune response. In addition, extensive renal and immune cell injury could increase circulating nucleic acids, which have been implicated in anti-PF4/heparin autoantibodies formation [13]. On the other hand, both anti-GBM disease [14] and SRC [15] are associated with platelet activation, and thus PF4 release. High levels of positively charged PF4 combined with negatively charged nucleic acids may create conditions favoring PF4 immunization and heparin-independent platelet activation. Another potential explanation for the overrepresentation of aHIT in our cohort is that our hemostasis laboratory systematically assesses for heparin-independent platelet activation in all cases of confirmed HIT, whereas this is not routinely performed in all laboratories. In this regard, some authors have reported that approximately half of the sera tested positive in a functional HIT assay were capable of activating platelets in the absence of heparin, suggesting that the aHIT phenotype may be more common than previously recognized [16].

The poor outcomes observed (RRT in 6 of 7 patients and death in 4 of 7 within 6 months) should be interpreted with caution as they could have been influenced by the patient’s overall clinical condition and severity of kidney disease. Notably, 4 patients were older than 70 years, and acute RRT is required in up to 78% of patients with anti-GBM disease at presentation [17], while mortality in SRC can reach 36% at 1 year [18]. Although thrombotic events and thrombocytopenia may have increased clinical complexity, the contribution of HIT to poor outcomes remains difficult to delineate.

This study has limitations. Its retrospective design may have introduced bias, and immunosuppressive therapies could have influenced delayed platelet recovery in patients with aHIT. Patients with aHIT typically show strongly positive ELISA results [6]. In this study, most patients were tested by chemiluminescence rather than ELISA, potentially affecting antibody quantification [19]. Finally, the small sample size precludes any definitive conclusions regarding a potential association between some kidney diseases and aHIT.

Conclusion

In this single-center series, HIT was rare but frequently associated with immune-mediated kidney disease, and nearly half of the cases exhibited features of aHIT. Pending larger prospective studies, nephrologists should be aware of that potential association and manage such cases with caution.
