Introduction to Stochastic Differential Equations for Generative Machine Learning: A Variational Perspective
Ole Winther ole.winther@bio.ku.dk, Paul Jeha pauje@dtu.dk, Sander Dieleman sedielem@deepmind.com, Andriy Mnih andriy@deepmind.com, Manfred Opper manfred.opper@tu-berlin.de, Andrea Dittadi andrea.dittadi@gmail.com

Abstract
The use of ordinary and stochastic differential equations has led to substantial progress in generative machine learning with applications to, for example, image, video and biomolecule generation. This paper provides a self-contained and informal introduction to the differential equations, the probabilistic framework for using them in generative modeling and the Fokker–Planck equation that governs the temporal evolution of the marginal distribution of the stochastic variables of the differential equations. The variational lower bound on the log-likelihood (the evidence lower bound, ELBO) is derived and used as a general starting point for a discussion of diffusion models, score matching, and flow matching. All of these approaches may be viewed as specific parameterizations of the most general variational approach. A one-dimensional density modeling problem is used as a simple example to compare different parameterizations.

Introduction

In recent years, stochastic processes have played a fundamental role in generative machine learning, as exemplified by neural ordinary differential equations (Neural ODEs)
(
Chen et al. 2018
)
, score matching
(
Song & Ermon 2019
;
Song et al. 2020a
;
Song et al. 2020b
;
Song et al. 2021
;
Song et al. 2023
)
, diffusion models
(
Sohl-Dickstein et al. 2015
;
Ho et al. 2020
;
Kingma et al. 2021
)
, flow matching
(
Lipman et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2022
;
Tong et al. 2023b
)
and stochastic interpolants
(
Albergo et al. 2023
)
. These methodologies have led to applied breakthroughs in, for example, image and video generation and bio-molecule structure prediction and design
(
Ho et al. 2020
;
Ho et al. 2022
;
Hoogeboom et al. 2022
)
. It is noteworthy that older work, such as
Archambeau et al. 2007
;
Opper & Sanguinetti 2007
, developed a similar methodology with applications in the time series setting rather than in generative modeling.

Most publications assume a relatively advanced level understanding of stochastic processes and probabilistic methods. The exposition typically starts from a statement of the generative model as a stochastic process written in terms of an ordinary or stochastic differential equation (ODE or SDE) with fundamental concepts, such as the Fokker-Planck equation, being applied without being formally derived. In addition, recent advances often present different perspectives on similar concepts, making it challenging to understand connections between them without a strong background in the basic methodology. On the other end of the spectrum, many good primers have appeared online that have strong intuitive explanations starting from simulations and heuristic objective functions, but lack connection to the probabilistic foundation.

This has motivated us to provide a self-contained introduction to the fundamentals of these methods and present methods from the machine learning literature in a unified way. In some instances, our variational perspective may differ slightly from the prevailing paradigm, with the goal of offering a fresh view on popular methods.

The approach we take here is to focus on
how to
derive the results appearing in the machine learning literature with less focus on formality and mathematical rigor. Specifically, the master Fokker–Planck equation that governs the temporal evolution of the marginals distribution of the stochastic variables and the reverse-time SDE are often presented as fundamental results.
The exposition is sought to be self-contained with minimal need for specialized background knowledge for example neither Itô calculus nor the Girsanov theorem will be used directly. Rather we will appeal to using Taylor expansions or start from finite differences equation and show without proving that the continuous formulation follows from taking the infinitesimal limit. The necessary assumptions (such as existence of derivatives to different orders or integrands vanishing at integration limits) will be introduced where they occur in the derivation rather than upfront, to avoid overloading the reader.

This paper is not intended as a review. Our aim is to provide a key to unlock the literature at the intersection of machine learning and stochastic processes. As a result, many recent methodological developments, such as more computationally efficient sampling techniques and constraints applied to stochastic processes to endow models with specific properties, are not covered. It is assumed that the reader has good understanding of calculus and probability and thus the paper will not be the ideal starting point for all readers.

The main contributions of this paper are:

•

A self-contained presentation of probabilistic methodologies, including the derivation of the Fokker–Planck equation starting from the definition of the ordinary and stochastic differential equations (ODEs and SDEs), consolidating this content in one place rather than being scattered across textbooks, stochastic process literature, and appendices in machine learning papers. The next three sections are dedicated to this: ODEs and SDEs (Section
2
), generative models (Section
3
) and the Fokker-Planck equation (Section
4
).

•

A unified derivation of objectives used in generative modeling with ODEs and SDEs, including diffusion, score and flow matching, all presented within a general variational framework. Three sections are dedicated to this: training objective (Section
5
), parameterization of the SDE objective (Section
6
) and diffusion, score and flow matching (Section
7
).

•

A methodology for constructing analytically tractable processes that extend beyond those with Gaussian marginals. The motivation for pushing these limits is inspire researchers to go beyond how these methods are used today described in Section
6
.

Sections
8
and
9
contain a simple one-dimensional density modeling example comparison and the discussion.

2
Ordinary and stochastic differential equations (ODEs and SDEs)

We begin by introducing the fundamentals of differential equations. We will consider a stochastic variable
\mathbf{x}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
indexed by a continuous
time
parameter
t\in[0,1]
.
The evolution of
\mathbf{x}_{t}
is described in terms of infinitesimal changes, expressed using the differential notation
\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{t}
, which can be seen as the limit of
\Delta\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{x}_{t+\Delta t}-\mathbf{x}_{t}
for
\Delta t\to 0
.
This limit is not formally defined on its own, but serves as an intuitive and compact representation of local dynamics. A rigorous understanding can be achieved using an integral formulation.

In this work, we will focus on Markov processes, where the system’s infinitesimal evolution
\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{t}
depends on the current time
t
and the past through the current state
\mathbf{x}_{t}
only.
As a starting point, we consider the deterministic case, where the system evolves according to an
ordinary differential equation
(ODE):
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)
, where the
drift

\mathbf{f}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
specifies the instantaneous rate of change of the state. We will write the ODE as

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)\mathrm{d}t\ .\mbox{\hskip 133.72786pt(ODE)}

(1)

This is an informal notation, not rigorously defined in the infinitesimal limit, but it allows us to treat the deterministic and stochastic versions in a unified way.
To model systems influenced by random fluctuations, we extend this formulation to
stochastic differential equations
(SDEs), which include an additional stochastic term:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{t}\mbox{\hskip 71.13188pt(SDE)}

(2)

Here, the
diffusion coefficient

\bm{\sigma}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}
determines how noise enters and influences each component of the state, and
\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{t}
denotes the infinitesimal stochastic increment of an
m
-dimensional
Wiener process
(or Brownian motion). There are no restrictions on the choice of
\bm{\sigma}
other than in one specific case (Section
5.2
), we will require that
\bm{\sigma}\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{T}}
is full rank.

A Wiener process
\mathbf{W}_{t}
is a continuous-time stochastic process such that (1)
\mathbf{W}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
almost surely (i.e., with probability 1); (2) its increments are distributed as
\mathbf{W}_{t+\Delta t}-\mathbf{W}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\Delta t\,\mathbf{I})
for
\Delta t>0
and (3) is independent of past values
\{\mathbf{W}_{s}:s<t\}
; (4) its sample paths are almost surely continuous in
t
.
The term
\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{t}
is thus a symbolic representation of an increment of the Wiener process over an infinitesimal time interval. It introduces a source of noise that is independent of the current system state. This independence can be defined as
p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{t})=p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})p(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{t})
, where
p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
is the
marginal distribution of
\mathbf{x}_{t}
at time
t
. This leads to the so-called Itô SDE that we study in this paper. The alternative but equivalent Stratonovich formulation with dependent noise is sometimes more convenient
(
Gardiner 2009
;
Øksendal 2013
)
.

In practice, solutions to both ODEs and SDEs are often approximated using time discretization methods. Typically, time is discretized by partitioning the interval
[0,1]
into
T
subintervals of length
\Delta t=\nicefrac{{1}}{{T}}
. For stochastic systems, the most basic and widely used approach is the
Euler–Maruyama
scheme, a stochastic analogue of the classical Euler method. The evolution of the process is approximated by

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{t+\Delta t}\approx\mathbf{x}_{t}+\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)\;\Delta t+\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)\;\Delta\mathbf{W}_{t}\ ,

(3)

where
\Delta\mathbf{W}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Delta t\,\mathbf{I})
.
This method provides a foundation for both numerical simulations and theoretical analysis of stochastic dynamics.
As
T\to\infty
, the discrete approximation converges to the marginal distribution and under some conditions to the full distribution of the continuous-time Wiener process
(
Platen 1999
)
, and
\Delta t,\Delta\mathbf{W}_{t}
are replaced by the differentials
\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{t}
.

3
Generative differential equations

In this paper we will only consider differential equations for generative modeling.
We assume an observed (continuous or discrete) random variable
\mathbf{y}
with an unknown true distribution
q(\mathbf{y})
, and our goal is to learn a model distribution
p(\mathbf{y})
that approximates
q(\mathbf{y})
. Here we follow the convention from the infinite data limit derivation of the score and flow matching methods to use
q(\mathbf{y})
for the data distribution, see
Appendix

F
. Additionally, we aim to efficiently generate new samples
\mathbf{y}\sim p(\mathbf{y})
.
The model specification consists of

(i)

a latent process
\{\mathbf{x}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, described by the ODE (
1
) or the SDE (
2
), which starts from

(ii)

a simple
prior
distribution
p_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
at
t=0
, and

(iii)

a distribution
p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_{1})
conditioned on
\mathbf{x}_{1}
, typically called
likelihood function
.

Using a latent process to model
p(\mathbf{y})
is a powerful construction for flexible modeling. The latent process in general will provide a smooth non-linear transformation of the latent variable and thus is more expressive than the one-shot variational autoencoder approach of using a likelihood function
p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})
with a simple prior distribution
p(\mathbf{x})
.
To learn the generative model, we will assume access to a training set of
n
independent and identically distributed (iid) samples
\{\mathbf{y}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{y}^{(n)}\}
from the data distribution
q(\mathbf{y})
.

Formally, the prior and the latent process induce a distribution
p(\mathbf{X})
over the continuous-time paths
\mathbf{X}=\{\mathbf{x}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}
. We note that the joint distribution
p(\mathbf{X})=p(\mathbf{x}_{t_{0}},\mathbf{x}_{t_{1}},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{t_{T}})
only exists for discretizations of the process with a finite number of steps
0=t_{0}<\ldots<t_{T}=1
, and is not well-defined in the
T\to\infty
limit.
Nevertheless, we will adopt this informal perspective as it makes the derivations more manageable and helps build an intuitive understanding. The
likelihood
(the probability of the data point
\mathbf{y}
under the model)
can now formally be written as a marginalization over the latent paths:

p(\mathbf{y})=\int p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_{1})p(\mathbf{X})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}=\int p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_{1})p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{1}\ ,

(4)

which shows that we can compute the (marginal) likelihood if we have access to the marginal distribution at
t=1
.

Computing the likelihood is in general analytically intractable, and (bridge, nested and tempered) Monte Carlo estimation is computationally expensive. The variational approach presented in
Section

5
provides a computationally efficient stochastic estimate of a lower bound to the log-likelihood, making it attractive for large-scale machine learning. The log-likelihood lower bound is commonly referred to as the evidence lower bound (ELBO).

Both the exact (
Eq.

4
) and approximate (
Section

5
) computation of the log-likelihood require integration over path distributions (path integrals). The path integral derivation is given in
Section

E.1
. Alternatively, the marginal
p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1})
in (
4
) can be obtained as the solution of a differential equation for the marginal
p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
, involving the time derivative
\frac{\mathrm{d}p_{t}(x_{t})}{\mathrm{d}t}
. Integrating this equation, we will then express
p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1})
in terms of an initial density and a standard integral over time. We give this derivation in
Section

5
. It uses the fundamental and central Fokker–Planck equation, which governs the
partial
time derivative of the marginal distribution:
\frac{\partial p_{t}(x_{t})}{\partial t}
. It is discussed in
Section

4
and derived in
Appendix

A
.

4
The Fokker–Planck equation

The
Fokker–Planck equation
(FPE) is a partial differential equation (PDE) that describes the temporal evolution of the probability density
p_{t}(\mathbf{x})
of a state
\mathbf{x}
in a stochastic or deterministic dynamical system.
A key feature of the FPE is that it provides a unified description of how these densities evolve, regardless of the specific ODE or SDE. Notably, different types of dynamics—SDEs, ODEs, and reverse-time SDEs—can all give rise to the same set of marginal distributions
p_{t}(\mathbf{x})
,
t\in[0,1]
.
This equivalence underlies several modern generative modeling techniques. For example, diffusion models are typically formulated as learning to reverse an SDE that adds noise to data. Similarly, turning an SDE into an ODE and vice versa without changing the marginals
p_{t}(\mathbf{x})
enables deterministic sampling from a diffusion model and stochastic sampling from a deterministic flow model.

In this section, we introduce the Fokker–Planck equations for the differential equations in
Section

2
and use it to define different processes with the same marginal densities
p_{t}
.
The FPE for the SDE (
2
) is:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\sum\nolimits_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\left[f_{i}(\mathbf{x},t)p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right]+\frac{1}{2}\sum\nolimits_{i,j}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}\left[\left[\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{x},t)\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{T}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right]_{ij}p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right]

(5)

\displaystyle=-\nabla\cdot\left[\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right]+\frac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot\left(\nabla\cdot\left[\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x},t)p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right]\right)\ ,

(6)

where in the last line we defined the positive semi-definite diffusion matrix
\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x},t)\coloneqq\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{x},t)\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{x},t)^{\mathrm{T}}
and used standard notation for divergences of vector and matrix fields.
1
1

1

Vector and matrix fields simply means vectors and matrices that are functions of
\mathbf{x}
. The divergence of a vector field
\mathbf{v}
is defined as
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}\coloneqq\sum_{i}\frac{\partial v_{i}}{\partial x_{i}}
. For a matrix field
\mathbf{A}
, we denote by
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{A}
the vector field whose
i
-th element is the divergence of the
i
-th row of
\mathbf{A}
, i.e.,
(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{A})_{i}\coloneqq\nabla\cdot\mathbf{A}_{i}=\sum_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}A_{ij}
.

Notice that the drift
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)
of the SDE corresponds to a first-order derivative, and the diffusion
\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{x},t)
to a second-order derivative.
For a deterministic ODE, the FPE reduces to the
Liouville
or
continuity
equation, as the diffusion term vanishes:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\nabla\cdot\left[\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right]\ .

(7)

In the ODE setting, the drift is often referred to as the flow field, as it determines how probability mass is transported through state space.

We derive the Fokker–Planck equation using the
Kramers–Moyal expansion
in
Appendix

A
. The derivation starts from the definition of the partial derivative and writes it as a Taylor expansion in the (jump) moments of the
transition kernel

p_{t+\Delta t}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{x})
. For the Wiener process, it turns out that only the two first jump moments will contribute, leading to the drift and diffusion coefficient terms in the Fokker–Planck equation. An alternative equivalent derivation based upon Itô’s lemma is also possible
(
Risken 1996
)
.

4.1
ODEs and SDEs with the same time-marginals

Consider the ODE (
1
) with initial condition
\mathbf{x}_{0}\sim p_{0}
, and let
p_{t}
denote the marginal density of
\mathbf{x}_{t}
, satisfying the continuity equation (
7
).
Here we will show that we can rewrite
Eq.

7
in a form that is similar to the Fokker–Planck equation (
6
), which corresponds to an SDE. That will allow us to define an SDE with an arbitrary diffusion coefficient that, starting from
p_{0}
, induces the same time-marginals
p_{t}
as the ODE.

From ODE to SDE.

We can rewrite the diffusion term in the Fokker–Planck equation (
6
) as a drift term:

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\left(\nabla\cdot\left[\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x},t)p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot\Bigg[p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\underbrace{\frac{1}{p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\nabla\cdot\left[\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x},t)p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right]}_{\mathrm{(*)}}\Bigg]

(8)

where
\mathrm{(*)}
plays the role of a drift.
For any sufficiently regular
\bm{\sigma}
, we can add and subtract the same term to the r.h.s. of the continuity equation (
7
) to obtain a Fokker–Planck equation for an SDE:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\nabla\cdot\left[\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)\;p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right]

(9)

\displaystyle=-\nabla\cdot\Bigg[\left(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)+\frac{1}{2\,p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\nabla\cdot\left[\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x},t)p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right]\right)\;p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\Bigg]+\frac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot\left(\nabla\cdot\left[\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x},t)p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right]\right)\ .

(10)

The two equivalent equations (
9
,
10
) correspond to the following ODE and SDE, respectively:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)\,\mathrm{d}t

(ODE)

(11)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{t}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)+\frac{1}{2\,p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\nabla\cdot\left[\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x},t)p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right]\right)\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{t}

(SDE)

(12)

Since these two equations are derived from the same Fokker–Planck equation, they will have the same
p_{t}
solution.
The effect of the introduced diffusion
\bm{\sigma}
is exactly canceled by the additional term in the drift.
So we can conclude that,
given an initial distribution
\mathbf{x}_{0}\sim p_{0}
and an ODE (
11
), we can define an SDE (
12
) with arbitrary diffusion coefficient
\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)
that has the same time-marginals
p_{t}
as the ODE
. In
Appendix

C
, we further discuss the relationship between the ODE and SDE as continuity equations for probability.

Reverse-time SDEs.

In diffusion models, we will use a diffusion process that is initialized to a data point
\mathbf{x}_{1}\approx\mathbf{y}
at
t=1
and runs backwards in time, gradually adding more noise. We will therefore need to extend the above analysis to the reverse-time case.

We have just shown how to manipulate the Fokker–Planck equation to turn an ODE into an SDE with arbitrary stochasticity without changing the solution for the time-marginals
p_{t}(\mathbf{x})
. The ODE can be solved forward and backward in time so we can use the results above to define a reverse-time SDE for the reverse-time solution to the ODE. Writing the reverse-time ODE in terms of the reverse clock
\tau
that goes from 0 to 1 when
t
goes from 1 to 0,
\tau\coloneqq 1-t
, we can introduce an SDE for clock
\tau
that has the same marginals as the ODE. To formalize this, we write the original ODE
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)
in terms of clock
\tau
renaming
\mathbf{x}_{t}
to
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{\tau}\coloneq\mathbf{x}_{1-\tau}=\mathbf{x}_{t}
, which yields
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{\tau}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}=\mathbf{f}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{\tau},\tau)
. Since
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}=-\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}
, the
\tau
clock ODE has drift
\mathbf{f}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x},\tau)=-\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},1-\tau)
. We can now trivially write down the reverse-time SDE for the clock
\tau
that has the same marginals as the ODE:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{\tau}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{f}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{\tau},\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau\

(RT ODE,
\tau
clock)

(13)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{\tau}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{f}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{\tau},\tau)+\frac{1}{2\;p_{t}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{\tau})}\nabla\cdot[\mathbf{D}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{\tau},\tau)\,p_{t}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{\tau})]\right)\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{\tau},\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}^{\prime}_{\tau}\hskip 20.00003pt

(RT SDE,
\tau
clock)

(14)

with
\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x},\tau)=\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{x},1-\tau)
and for simplicity we have kept
t
in the marginal as a shorthand for
p_{t}(\mathbf{x})=p_{1-\tau}(\mathbf{x})
.

While this SDE is properly defined as running forward in the
\tau
clock, it is common in the literature to re-express it in terms of the original time variable
t
, effectively describing the process backward in time. Here we provide a derivation sketch, while a detailed derivation is given in
Appendix

B
.

The Wiener process
\mathbf{W}^{\prime}_{\tau}
introduced above starts from
\mathbf{W}^{\prime}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
and has independent increments according to the
\tau
clock. This is a standard Wiener process running backward in time according to the
t
clock, and can be defined in terms of the standard (forward) Wiener process as
\mathbf{W}^{\prime}_{\tau}\coloneqq\mathbf{W}_{1-\tau}-\mathbf{W}_{1}
. We now change the variables in the reverse-time SDE above back to the
t
clock and write

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{t}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)-\frac{1}{2\;p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})}\nabla\cdot\left[\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x},t)\;p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\right]\right)\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)\,\overleftarrow{\mathrm{d}}\mathbf{W}_{t}\qquad\qquad\hskip 10.00002pt\text{(RT SDE, $t$ clock)}

(15)

where we used the substitutions
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{\tau}\to\mathbf{x}_{t}
,
\mathrm{d}\tau\to-\mathrm{d}t
,
\mathbf{f}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{\tau},\tau)\to-\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)
and
\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{\tau},\tau)\to\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)
. The left arrow on the stochastic differential denotes a so-called backward Itô integral; see
Appendix

B
for further details.

We have now written the reverse-time SDE with marginals
p_{t}(\mathbf{x})
in terms of the standard
t
clock. Its structure mirrors the standard SDE with the reverse-time Wiener process replacing the standard Wiener process and a changed sign in the drift correcting the diffusion term. That is, when running the SDE in reverse we need to reverse the contribution to the drift countering the diffusion.

An even stronger result, due to

Anderson 1982
, says that when the forward and backward SDEs have the same time marginals
and
the same exact diffusion coefficient
\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{x},t)
,
the path distributions
—not just the marginals—
in both directions are also identical
.

Summary.

We have shown that, for any
\bm{\sigma}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that the SDEs have solutions,
the ODE (
11
), SDE (
12
), and reverse-time SDE (
15
) have the same time-marginals
p_{t}(\mathbf{x})
. In the diffusion models literature, the ODE (
11
) is typically called the
probability flow ODE
(PF ODE) for the SDE (
12
). In the remainder of the paper when we discuss more than one of these three models, we will denote ODE drift by
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)
and the forward and reverse time with arrows:

\displaystyle\overrightarrow{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)+\frac{1}{2\,p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\nabla\cdot\left[\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x},t)p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right]

(16)

\displaystyle\overleftarrow{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)-\frac{1}{2\,p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\nabla\cdot\left[\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x},t)p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right]=\overrightarrow{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)-\frac{1}{p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\nabla\cdot\left[\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x},t)p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right]\ .

(17)

The divergence term is typically rewritten as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\nabla\cdot\left[\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x},t)p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right]=\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x},t)+\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x},t)\;\nabla\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x})

(18)

to highlight the
Stein score

\nabla\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x})
, which plays a central role in the score matching framework discussed in
Section

7.2
.

In the literature, diffusion matrices are typically scaled identity matrices, i.e.,
\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{x},t)=\sigma(\mathbf{x},t)\,\mathbf{I}
. In addition,
\sigma(\mathbf{x},t)
is typically chosen to be constant in space and is denoted as
\sigma_{t}
.
With these two assumptions, the drifts of the SDE and reverse SDE can be simplified as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)\pm\frac{1}{2\,p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\nabla\cdot\left[\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x},t)p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)\pm\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x},t)+\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x},t)\nabla\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right)

(19)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)\pm\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{t}^{2}\;\nabla\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\qquad\quad\mathrm{for}\ \,\sigma(\mathbf{x},t)=\sigma_{t}\ .

(20)

5
Maximum likelihood training with ODEs and SDEs

In the machine learning approach, the ODE/SDE and the likelihood function introduced in
Section

3
are written in terms of a parameter vector
\bm{\theta}
. We can use neural networks to parameterize the drift
\mathbf{f}_{\bm{\theta}}
and diffusion coefficient
\bm{\sigma}_{\bm{\theta}}
that govern the latent process
\{\mathbf{x}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
described by the ODE (
1
) or SDE (
2
). The likelihood function
p_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_{1})
, mapping the terminal latent
\mathbf{x}_{1}
to the observed variable
\mathbf{y}
, may also have parameter dependencies.

The objective is to perform
maximum likelihood optimization
of the model using a training set
\{\mathbf{y}^{(m)}\}_{m=1}^{n}
. We want to use a gradient ascent method to maximize the log-likelihood
\sum_{m=1}^{n}\log p_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{y}^{(m)})
, where
p_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{y})=\int p_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_{1})p_{1,\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{1}
is obtained from
Eq.

4
by adding the parameter dependencies.
This marginal likelihood
p_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{y})
is typically intractable, i.e., it is not available in closed form and difficult to estimate using numerical methods. Furthermore, even if we could obtain samples from the posterior
p_{1,\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})
, computing the log-likelihood gradient would still require that we can compute gradients of
\log p_{1,\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})
:

\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log p_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{y})=\mathbb{E}_{p_{1,\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})}\big[\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log p_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_{1})+\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log p_{1,\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\big]\ .

(21)

In the following, we will describe how to make gradient computations tractable. We omit the parameter dependence to keep the notation as simple as possible. Specifically, we introduce a variational posterior
q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})
(with parameters
\bm{\phi}
) that approximates the true posterior
p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})\propto p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_{1})p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1})
. Note that the prior distribution for
\mathbf{x}_{1}
,
p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1})
, typically will be a complicated distribution.
In contrast, the posterior
p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})
is a much narrower distribution because conditioning on a data point will limit the support to only the part of latent space associated with the data point and its slight variations. We can therefore hope that choosing
q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})
as a simple distribution with support on the relevant part of latent space will be a good approximation to the true posterior. Furthermore, as discussed in the following, if
q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})
can be evaluated in closed form and efficiently sampled from, then we get a tractable lower bound on the log-likelihood. We discuss how to construct good variational distributions in
Section

6
and the variational distribution used in diffusion, score and flow matching in
Section

7
.

A standard lower bound on the log-likelihood, the
Evidence Lower BOund
(ELBO) is obtained from the following rewrite of the marginal log-likelihood:

\displaystyle\log p(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})}\left[\log p(\mathbf{y})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})}\left[\underbrace{\log\frac{p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_{1})p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1})}{p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})}}_{\log p(\mathbf{y})}+\underbrace{\log\frac{q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})}{q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})}}_{=0}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})}\left[\log p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_{1})\right]-\mathbb{E}_{q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})}\left[\log\frac{q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})}{p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1})}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})}\left[\log\frac{q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})}{p(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})}\right]

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})}\left[\log p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_{1})\right]-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})\|p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}))}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ELBO}}^{\mathrm{ODE}}(\mathbf{y})}+\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})\|p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y}))}_{\text{ELBO gap ($\geq 0$)}}\ ,

(22)

where
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q(\mathbf{x})\|p(\mathbf{x}))\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{x})}\left[\log\frac{q(\mathbf{x})}{p(\mathbf{x})}\right]
is the
Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence
, which is non-negative. We denote the ELBO as
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ELBO}}^{\mathrm{ODE}}
for reasons that will become clearer in
Section

5.1
.
The non-negative ELBO gap is a measure of the mismatch between the true and approximate posterior distributions: it is zero if and only if
q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})=p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})
, and in that case the ELBO coincides with the log-likelihood.

The first term in the ELBO is often referred to as the
reconstruction term
since it involves “encoding”
\mathbf{y}
into a latent variable
\mathbf{x}_{1}
and then “reconstructing”
\mathbf{y}
with a probabilistic model. Assuming that
q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})
is easy to sample from and that
\log p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_{1})
can be evaluated, the reconstruction term can be easily estimated by Monte Carlo sampling.
For the second term,
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})\|p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}))=\mathbb{E}_{q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})}\left[\log q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})-\log p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\right]
, we can choose
q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})
such that it is easy to evaluate and sample from. However,
\log p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1})
is not available except in trivial cases. This is typically the main obstacle to estimating the ELBO, and the following sections develop two strategies that overcome this obstacle.

Roadmap.

In
Sections

5.1
and
5.2
, we consider two approaches to address the issue of estimating the second term in the ELBO,
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})\|p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}))
. This depends on how we define the latent process: (1) If the latent process is governed by an ODE,
\log p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1})
can be computed exactly by a change-of-variable formula, but this requires solving an ODE. Plugging this expression into the ELBO produces a
simulation-based
training objective. (2) For a latent process described by an SDE, directly evaluating
\log p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1})
is not feasible in general. Instead, we will derive a bound on the second term in the ELBO that can be estimated without solving any differential equations, thus enabling efficient
simulation-free
training.
Finally, in
Section

5.3
, we discuss the role of the likelihood function
p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_{1})
and the variational distribution
q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})
, which define the link between the observable data
\mathbf{y}
and the final-time latent variable
\mathbf{x}_{1}
.

5.1
Latent ODE

If the latent process
\{\mathbf{x}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}
satisfies an ODE,

\log p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1})
can be computed exactly, as we show next.
For the ODE
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)
, the
total
time-derivative of the density (i.e., along a path) is:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}p_{t}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial\mathbf{x}}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle\!\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq:Fokker Planck ODE})}}{{=}}-\nabla\cdot\left[\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right]+\frac{\partial p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial\mathbf{x}}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=-p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)-\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)\cdot\frac{\partial p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial\mathbf{x}}+\frac{\partial p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial\mathbf{x}}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=-p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)+\frac{\partial p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial\mathbf{x}}\cdot\underbrace{\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}}{\mathrm{d}t}-\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)\right)}_{=0\ \mathrm{(ODE)}}=-p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)\ ,

which can be rearranged into
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x})=-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)
. This equation can be combined with the state equation (
1
) into an ODE in
d+1
variables:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}\\
\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)\\
-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)\end{bmatrix}

(23)

Let
\bm{\phi}_{s}^{t}
denote the
flow map
, defined such that
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bm{\phi}_{s}^{t}(\mathbf{x}_{s})=\mathbf{f}(\bm{\phi}_{s}^{t}(\mathbf{x}_{s}),t)
and
\bm{\phi}_{t}^{t}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}
. Intuitively, this maps
\mathbf{x}_{s}
to
\mathbf{x}_{t}
along the ODE solution initialized at
\mathbf{x}_{s}
at time
s
.
Integrating
Eq.

23
on
[s,t]
, we obtain the following change of variable formula:

\displaystyle\log p_{t}(\bm{\phi}^{t}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s}))=\log p_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s})-\int_{s}^{t}\nabla\cdot\mathbf{f}(\bm{\phi}_{s}^{\tau}(\mathbf{x}_{s}),\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau\ ,

(24)

where
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t)
is shorthand for

\left(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\cdot\mathbf{f}\right)(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t)
, i.e., the spatial divergence evaluated at
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
.
In
Appendix

C
, we give an interpretation of this result.

To compute
\log p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1})
for a given terminal value
\mathbf{x}_{1}
, we integrate the system of ODEs in
Eq.

23
backwards from
t=1
to
t=0
, obtaining
\mathbf{x}_{0}=\bm{\phi}_{1}^{0}(\mathbf{x}_{1})
and
\log p_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
, and computing
\int_{0}^{1}\nabla\cdot\mathbf{f}(\bm{\phi}_{1}^{t}(\mathbf{x}_{1}),t)\,\mathrm{d}t
in the process. Substituting these quantities into
Eq.

24
gives
\log p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1})
. The ELBO (
22
) then takes the form:

\displaystyle\log p(\mathbf{y})\geq\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ELBO}}^{\mathrm{ODE}}(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})}\left[\log p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_{1})\right]-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})\|p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}))

(25)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})}\left[\log p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_{1})-\log q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})+\log p_{0}(\bm{\phi}_{1}^{0}(\mathbf{x}_{1}))-\int_{0}^{1}\nabla\cdot\mathbf{f}(\bm{\phi}_{1}^{t}(\mathbf{x}_{1}),t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right]\ .

This objective serves as the basis for training. Because its evaluation requires solving the augmented ODE in
Eq.

23
, it is often referred to as a
simulation-based
objective in the generative modeling literature.
In certain cases, this objective coincides with the exact log-likelihood: for example, as we discuss in
Section

5.3
, when the observation
\mathbf{y}
is continuous and
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}_{1}
, the bound becomes tight.
Generation also requires simulation, where samples are obtained by integrating the forward dynamics in
Eq.

1
from prior to data
(
Grathwohl et al. 2018
;
Chen et al. 2018
)
. At this stage, both training and inference depend on numerical ODE solvers, but in
Section

we will discuss
simulation-free
training.

5.2
Latent SDE

If the latent process follows an SDE,
p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1})=\int p(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{x}_{0})p(\mathbf{x}_{0})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{0}
cannot be computed directly via a change of variables, and is therefore intractable in most cases. As a result, the KL divergence
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})\|p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}))
in the ELBO (
22
) is also intractable.
We will address this by deriving an upper bound to
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})\|p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}))
which consists in a time-averaged mean-squared error that can be efficiently estimated, as seen in diffusion models and flow matching (see
Section

7
). This will result in a tractable, though generally looser, log-likelihood bound
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ELBO}}^{\mathrm{SDE}}(\mathbf{y})\leq\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ELBO}}^{\mathrm{ODE}}(\mathbf{y})\leq\log p(\mathbf{y})
. Consequently, unlike the ODE approach in
Section

5.1
, the SDE formulation enables training without explicitly solving differential equations.

We assume the latent process is governed by the SDE

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\overrightarrow{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x},t)\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{x},t)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}

(26)

and has marginals
p_{t}(\mathbf{x})
. We define a variational latent process:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\overrightarrow{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{x},t)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}

(27)

that is conditional on a data point
\mathbf{y}
and has marginals
q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})
.
We then rewrite the KL divergence as:

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})\,\|\,p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}))=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{y})\,\|\,p_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0}))+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})\,\|\,p_{t}(\mathbf{x}))\,\mathrm{d}t\ .

(28)

The KL derivative can be written in terms of the partial derivatives of the two densities (see
Section

D.1
):

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})\,\|\,p_{t}(\mathbf{x}))

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\partial q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})}{\partial t}\log\frac{q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})}{p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}-\int\frac{q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})}{p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\frac{\partial p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial t}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\ .

(29)

Although
p_{t}(\mathbf{x})
and
q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})
come from SDEs, we will now work with their associated probability-flow ODE vector fields
\mathbf{f}
and
\mathbf{g}
since they satisfy simple continuity equations. These ODEs have the same time-marginals
p_{t},q_{t}
as the SDEs, and the relationship between their drifts is discussed in
Section

4.1
(in particular, see
Eq.

16
).
Plugging in these continuity equations, we obtain the following expression (proof in
Section

D.1
):

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})\,\|\,p_{t}(\mathbf{x}))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})}\big[(\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)-\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t))\cdot(\nabla\log q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})-\nabla\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x}))\big]\ .

(30)

Using
Eq.

17
,
we can also define a
reverse-time
SDE with drift
\overleftarrow{\mathbf{f}}
and same diffusion
\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{x},t)
as the generative SDE above, that has the same time-marginals
p_{t}
. Similarly, we define
\overleftarrow{\mathbf{g}}
from
\mathbf{g}
, using again the same diffusion
\bm{\sigma}
.
In
Section

D.2
, we integrate (
30
) to show that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})\,\|\,p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\big)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{f}},\overrightarrow{\mathbf{g}},\mathbf{y})-\mathcal{D}(\overleftarrow{\mathbf{f}},\overleftarrow{\mathbf{g}},\mathbf{y})\ ,

(31)

where we assumed that
\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x},t)=\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{x},t)\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{T}}(\mathbf{x},t)
is invertible, and we defined

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{g},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})}\left[\left(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)-\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\,\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x},t)^{-1}\,\left(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)-\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)\right)\right]\mathrm{d}t

(32)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})}\left[\frac{\left\|\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)-\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)\right\|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x},t)}\right]\mathrm{d}t\ .

(33)

The last step follows from assuming an isotropic diffusion coefficient, i.e.,
\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x},t)=\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x},t)\,\mathbf{I}
, and it results in the usual MSE loss we find in diffusion models and flow matching.

Using the decomposition derived above, the KL divergence at
t=1
admits the exact representation:

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})\|p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}))=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{y})\|p_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0}))+\mathcal{D}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{f}},\overrightarrow{\mathbf{g}},\mathbf{y})-\mathcal{D}(\overleftarrow{\mathbf{f}},\overleftarrow{\mathbf{g}},\mathbf{y})\ .

(34)

Substituting this into (
22
) yields a full decomposition of the marginal log-likelihood:

\displaystyle\log p(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})}\left[\log p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_{1})\right]-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{y})\|p_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0}))-\mathcal{D}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{f}},\overrightarrow{\mathbf{g}},\mathbf{y})

(35)

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathcal{D}(\overleftarrow{\mathbf{f}},\overleftarrow{\mathbf{g}},\mathbf{y})+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})\|p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y}))\ .

The first four terms on the r.h.s. correspond to the ELBO associated with the probability-flow ODE formulation (the ODE ELBO), while the final term is the usual variational gap (see
Eq.

22
).

The reverse-time discrepancy
\mathcal{D}(\overleftarrow{\mathbf{f}},\overleftarrow{\mathbf{g}},\mathbf{y})
is generally not available in closed form, since the reverse-time drifts depend on the (typically unknown) score of the generative model,
\nabla\log p_{t}
. However,
\mathcal{D}(\overleftarrow{\mathbf{f}},\overleftarrow{\mathbf{g}},\mathbf{y})
is always nonnegative because the quadratic form is weighted by
\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x},t)^{-1}
, which is positive definite.
Consequently, discarding the reverse discrepancy yields a valid (possibly looser) lower bound that remains tractable.
We therefore define the SDE-based ELBO as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ELBO}}^{\mathrm{SDE}}(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})}\left[\log p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_{1})\right]}_{\text{Reconstruction}}-\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{y})\|p_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0}))}_{\text{Prior}}-\underbrace{\mathcal{D}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{f}},\overrightarrow{\mathbf{g}},\mathbf{y})}_{\text{Diffusion}}\ .

(36)

With this notation, the decomposition (
35
) can be written compactly as

\displaystyle\log p(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ELBO}}^{\mathrm{SDE}}(\mathbf{y})+\mathcal{D}(\overleftarrow{\mathbf{f}},\overleftarrow{\mathbf{g}},\mathbf{y})}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ELBO}}^{\mathrm{ODE}}(\mathbf{y})}+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})\|p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y}))\ ,

(37)

which makes explicit that
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ELBO}}^{\mathrm{SDE}}(\mathbf{y})\leq\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ELBO}}^{\mathrm{ODE}}(\mathbf{y})\leq\log p(\mathbf{y})
.

Interpretation of the
\mathcal{D}
functionals as path-space KL divergences.

Above, we decomposed the KL divergence
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_{1}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})\,\|\,p_{1}(\mathbf{x})\big)
into three tractable terms using only the continuity/Fokker–Planck equations and algebraic manipulations.
An equivalent, more classical derivation starts from standard identities for KL divergences between path measures (that is, the laws on continuous trajectories induced by the generative and variational SDEs). This yields two path-space KL terms, corresponding to forward and reverse processes. Evaluating these terms with Girsanov’s theorem gives precisely the functionals
\mathcal{D}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{f}},\overrightarrow{\mathbf{g}},\mathbf{y})
and
\mathcal{D}(\overleftarrow{\mathbf{f}},\overleftarrow{\mathbf{g}},\mathbf{y})
obtained above, and hence exactly the decomposition in
Eq.

35
. This enriches the interpretation of our objective: the terms obtained by integrating the KL derivative are in fact information-theoretic quantities measuring discrepancies between trajectory laws. We present the more direct derivation in the main text because it uses only the Fokker–Planck equation and requires less background. In
Appendix

E
, we give the standard path-space derivation in detail, including an informal derivation of Girsanov’s theorem.

Simulation-free ELBO estimation.

An important property of the diffusion term is that it requires an expectation over
q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})
. For a general choice of variational drift function

to be a specific distribution (e.g., a Gaussian, or the transformation of a simple distribution through a normalizing flow) and derive the drift function corresponding to that choice of marginal. With this direct access to marginal samples, we can estimate the diffusion term by Monte Carlo sampling, drawing
t

[0,1]
, denoted

\int_{0}^{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}t=\mathbb{E}_{t\in\mathcal{U}[0,1]}\left[\ldots\right]
. This means that we now have a

objective to learn the model. Generation, on the other hand, of course still requires simulation of the SDE e.g. with the Euler–Maruyama discretization.

5.3
Mapping between latents and observables

In
Sections

5.1
and
5.2
, we have established two approaches for estimating the ELBO—evaluating
\log p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1})
exactly via the ODE change-of-variables, or bounding the marginal KL by a tractable path KL, which results in a looser bound. In this section, we turn to a complementary issue: how the latent process interfaces with data through the likelihood function
p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_{1})
and the variational posterior
q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})
.
While the design of these distributions is in principle orthogonal to whether the latent dynamics follow an ODE or an SDE, there are subtle dependencies. In the ODE case, we require
q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})
to be easy to evaluate and sample from. In the SDE case, this requirement extends to all intermediate marginals
q_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{y})
, and these marginals must be compatible with the variational SDE (
108
) with drift
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)
.

The variational posterior
q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})
should be chosen with two properties in mind: tractability of sampling and density evaluation, and, ideally, closeness to the true posterior. If one were able to set
q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})=p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})\propto p_{1}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_{1})p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1})
, then the ELBO gap in
Eq.

22
would vanish and the ELBO would coincide with the log-likelihood.
2
2

2

In the SDE approach, the corresponding statement holds at the path level: if
q(\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{y})=p(\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{y})
, then the SDE ELBO coincides with the ODE ELBO.

In the deterministic case,
p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_{1})=\delta(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{1})
, this condition is automatically satisfied: the true posterior
p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})\propto\delta(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{1})p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1})
is concentrated at
\mathbf{x}_{1}=\mathbf{y}
, and the variational posterior can be defined as the same Dirac distribution:
q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})\coloneqq p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})=\delta(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{1})
.
3
3

3

To show this more rigorously, we integrate the true posterior against a suitable test function
\phi
and apply Bayes’ theorem:

\int\phi(\mathbf{x}_{1})p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{1}=\frac{\int\phi(\mathbf{x}_{1})p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_{1})p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{1}}{\int p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{1})p_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{1})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{1}}=\frac{\phi(\mathbf{y})p_{1}(\mathbf{y})}{p_{1}(\mathbf{y})}=\phi(\mathbf{y})

which holds for all
\phi
and therefore implies
p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})=\delta(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{1})
.

For latent ODE models this implies that the marginal log-likelihood
\log p(\mathbf{y})
is in fact computable exactly. This construction underlies continuous normalizing flows
(
Chen et al. 2018
)
, where the ODE dynamics transform a simple prior at
t=0
into the observed data at
t=1
. In the SDE setting, the situation is more delicate: even if the final-time posterior is matched, there typically remains a gap due to mismatch between the true and variational posterior path distributions (
q(\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{y})\neq p(\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{y})
).

6
Parameterization of the SDE model

In this section, we discuss how to parameterize the SDE model in a way that ensures maximum flexibility while at the same time keeping the objective simulation-free, that is, being able to draw samples from the variational marginal distribution
q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})
at any time
t
without simulating the variational SDE. We explore marginals defined as normalizing flows and mixtures of normalizing flows and derive their corresponding ODEs and SDEs. We discuss likelihood functions for continuous and ordinal data and how the variational distribution can be chosen to match the likelihood function. In
Section

7
, we connect the results of this section to the diffusion literature. We present a numerical example in
Section

8
.

Generative drift and the diffusion coefficient.

The SDE generative model is defined by the drift
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x},t)
and the diffusion coefficient
\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{x},t)
, both of which can be directly parameterized by neural networks.

Variational drift.

In the following, we will work with ODE and forward-/reverse-time SDE variational drifts and denote these by
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)
,
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)
and
\overleftarrow{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)
, respectively. The definition of the variational drift
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)
requires more care because we need to define it such that the marginal distribution
q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})
(that is the solution to the associated Fokker–Planck equation) admits computationally efficient sampling so that we get the benefit from
the simulation-free ELBO as outlined in
Algorithm

2
. We will describe two routes to achieving this: start with the desired parameterization of the marginal and then derive the corresponding drift (this section), or use a Gaussian Markov chain that has Gaussian probability paths
(
Ho et al. 2020
;
Kingma et al. 2021
;
Nielsen et al. 2023
;
Song et al. 2020b
)
(
Section

7
). Below we will consider the marginal
q_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{y})
being defined as a normalizing flow and a mixture of normalizing flows, and derive the corresponding explicit expression for the variational ODE drift
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)
. Once we have expressions for both the marginal and the ODE drift, we can use the result in
Eq.

12
to write the SDE drift as

\overrightarrow{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)=\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)+\frac{1}{2\,q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})}\nabla\cdot\left[\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x},t)q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})\right]\ .

(38)

Drift from marginal defined as normalizing flow.

We consider the marginal distribution
p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
induced by a flow
\mathbf{F}_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
that is continuously differentiable in both variables
(
Bartosh et al. 2024
)
. Specifically, we will use this to construct the variational
q_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{y})
from a flow
\mathbf{G}_{t}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{y})
.

Given a random variable
\bm{\epsilon}
with density
p(\bm{\epsilon})
, we let
\mathbf{x}_{t}\coloneqq\mathbf{F}_{t}(\bm{\epsilon})
and assume
\mathbf{F}_{t}
is invertible for all
t
, such that
\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{F}_{t}(\mathbf{F}_{t}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_{t}))
.
Here,
\bm{\epsilon}
can be thought of as a
Lagrangian label
in fluid dynamics, i.e., a unique identifier assigned to a particle to track its movement over time.
The explicit expression for the marginal is given by the change-of-variable formula:

p_{t}(\mathbf{x})=\left|\det\frac{\partial\mathbf{F}^{-1}_{t}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial\mathbf{x}}\right|p\left(\mathbf{F}^{-1}_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right)\ .

(39)

The derivation will be valid for general flows and base measures
p(\bm{\epsilon})
. A concrete example that is relevant for practical applications is a linear flow with independent components:
\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{F}_{t}(\bm{\epsilon})=\bm{\alpha}(t)+\beta(t)\bm{\epsilon}
, with
p(\bm{\epsilon})
a standard Gaussian. The inverse is
\bm{\epsilon}=\mathbf{F}^{-1}_{t}(\mathbf{x})=(\mathbf{x}-\bm{\alpha}(t))/\beta(t)
. Below we generalize the derivation to a mixture of normalizing flows:
p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})=\sum_{k}\pi_{k}p_{k,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
with fixed mixture coefficients
\pi_{k}
.

Now we define the velocity given by the flow
\mathbf{F}_{t}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)\coloneqq\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbf{F}_{t}(\bm{\epsilon})\quad\text{with}\quad\bm{\epsilon}=\mathbf{F}_{t}^{-1}(\mathbf{x})\ .

(40)

Then, for each label
\bm{\epsilon}
, using the definition
\mathbf{x}_{t}\coloneqq\mathbf{F}_{t}(\bm{\epsilon})
we have

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbf{x}_{t}=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbf{F}_{t}(\bm{\epsilon})=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)
,
i.e.,
\mathbf{x}_{t}
solves the ODE with velocity
\mathbf{f}
and initial condition
\mathbf{x}_{0}=\mathbf{F}_{0}(\bm{\epsilon})
. This shows that, given a base density
p(\bm{\epsilon})
and a flow
\mathbf{F}_{t}
that transports it to the desired marginals
p_{t}
(
Eq.

39
), we can derive the velocity of the associated ODE with
Eq.

40
.
For the linear transformation:
\mathbf{F}_{t}(\bm{\epsilon})=\bm{\alpha}(t)+\beta(t)\bm{\epsilon}
, the ODE drift becomes
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)=\frac{\partial\bm{\alpha}(t)}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\beta(t)}{\partial t}\frac{\mathbf{x}_{t}-\bm{\alpha}(t)}{\beta(t)}
.

Drift from marginal defined as a mixture.

For the mixture model
p_{t}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{k}\pi_{k}p_{k,t}(\mathbf{x})
, each marginal
p_{k,t}(\mathbf{x})
is defined by a normalizing flow
\mathbf{F}_{k,t}(\mathbf{x})
with corresponding drift
\mathbf{f}_{k}(\mathbf{x},t)
obtained from (
40
):
\mathbf{f}_{k}(\mathbf{x},t)=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{F}_{k,t}(\bm{\epsilon})}{\mathrm{d}t}\right|_{\bm{\epsilon}=\mathbf{F}^{-1}_{k,t}(\mathbf{x})}
. To derive the drift for the mixture we use the continuity equation for the mixture and for each component:

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}p_{t}(\mathbf{x})=\sum\nolimits_{k}\pi_{k}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}p_{k,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})=-\sum\nolimits_{k}\pi_{k}\nabla\cdot\left[\mathbf{f}_{k}(\mathbf{x},t)p_{k,t}(\mathbf{x})\right]=-\nabla\cdot\bigg[\underbrace{\sum\nolimits_{k}\pi_{k}\mathbf{f}_{k}(\mathbf{x},t)p_{k,t}(\mathbf{x})}_{\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)\,p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\bigg]\ .

(41)

This leads to

\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)=\sum\nolimits_{k}r_{k}(\mathbf{x},t)\mathbf{f}_{k}(\mathbf{x},t)

(42)

with the “responsibility” for component
k
given input
\mathbf{x}
:

r_{k}(\mathbf{x},t)=\frac{\pi_{k}p_{k,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})}{\sum_{k^{\prime}}\pi_{k^{\prime}}p_{k^{\prime},t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})}\ .

(43)

This result can also be generalized to time-dependent mixture coefficients and the SDE setting
(
Brigo 2002
;
Peluchetti 2021
;
Skreta et al. 2025
)
.

Variational drift example.

We will now use the methodology developed above to the standard choice of the variational distribution, a Gaussian:
q_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{y})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\bm{\alpha}(\mathbf{y},t),\beta^{2}(y,t)\mathbf{I})
. The ODE drift becomes
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y},t)=\frac{\partial\bm{\alpha}(\mathbf{y},t)}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\beta(\mathbf{y},t)}{\partial t}\frac{\mathbf{x}_{t}-\bm{\alpha}(\mathbf{y},t)}{\beta(\mathbf{y},t)}
and the score
\nabla\log q_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{y})=-\frac{\mathbf{x}_{t}-\bm{\alpha}(\mathbf{y},t)}{\beta^{2}(\mathbf{y},t)}
. From (
38
), setting
\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x},t)=\sigma^{2}_{t}\mathbf{I}
, we get

\overrightarrow{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)=\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)+\frac{\sigma^{2}_{t}}{2}\nabla\log q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})=\frac{\partial\bm{\alpha}(\mathbf{y},t)}{\partial t}+\left(\frac{\partial\beta(\mathbf{y},t)}{\partial t}-\frac{\sigma^{2}_{t}}{2\beta(\mathbf{y},t)}\right)\frac{\mathbf{x}-\bm{\alpha}(\mathbf{y},t)}{\beta(\mathbf{y},t)}\ .

(44)

We will investigate this drift function for a simple numerical example in Section
8
.
The extension to the mixture with marginals
q_{k,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{y})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\bm{\alpha}_{k}(\mathbf{y},t),\beta_{k}(\mathbf{y},t)\mathbf{I})
leads to the ODE drift (
42
) and a simple responsibility-weighted score function:
\nabla\log q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})=\sum_{k}r_{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)\nabla\log q_{k,t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})
and thus the same form for the SDE drift:
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)=\sum_{k}r_{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)\overrightarrow{\mathbf{g}}_{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)
.

Likelihood function examples.

We will consider likelihood functions for continuous and ordinal data. For continuous data, it is common to use a Gaussian with a simple covariance:

p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x},\delta^{2}\mathbf{I})\ .

(45)

For ordinal data, e.g., 8-bit pixels
\mathbf{y}\in[0,1,\ldots,255]^{d}
, we can take independent dimensions and build a discrete normalized distribution by the following construction
(
Kingma et al. 2021
)
:

p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\mathcal{N}(y_{i}|x_{i},\delta^{2})}{\sum_{y^{\prime}}\mathcal{N}(y^{\prime}|x_{i},\delta^{2})}\ .

(46)

With the choice
q_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{y})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\bm{\alpha}(\mathbf{y},t),\beta^{2}(t)\mathbf{I})
, we can easily calculate the prior and reconstruction terms in the SDE ELBO (
36
).

We will often match the variational distribution at
t=1
with the likelihood function, setting
\bm{\alpha}(\mathbf{y},1)=\mathbf{y}
and
\beta(1)=\delta

(
Kingma et al. 2021
)
, where
\delta
is set to a small fixed value or is a learned parameter.
We will consider an explicit example in the context of flow matching in
Section

7.3
.

7
Diffusion, score and flow matching

Diffusion
(
Sohl-Dickstein et al. 2015
;
Ho et al. 2020
;
Kingma et al. 2021
)
, score
(
Vincent 2011
;
Song et al. 2020b
)
and flow matching
(
Lipman et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2022
)
are a set of closely related and extremely popular latent process generative models that can be well-understood in terms of the variational framework described in this paper. One may even say that your favorite score and flow matching objectives are secretly ELBOs. In this section, we will introduce each method and show how each method’s learning objective can be identified as a special case of the SDE ELBO (
36
).

7.1
Diffusion models

The denoising diffusion probabilistic model (DDPM,
Ho et al. 2020
) derives its name from being defined in terms of two processes: the diffusion, a reverse-time process initialized in the data that gradually adds noise so that at
t=0
most of the information about the data is removed, and the denoising, a forward-time process that starting from pure noise gradually transforms the latent such that at
t=1
one ideally gets samples from the training set distribution. The diffusion and denoising processes are readily identified with the variational and generative processes described above.
The original diffusion formulation is defined in terms of a finite number of stochastic layers, that is,
\Delta t
is finite, but it has a well-defined SDE limit
(
Kingma et al. 2021
;
Song et al. 2021
)
. We will give a brief derivation of this using the variational diffusion models (VDM) framework
(
Kingma et al. 2021
)
, which is mathematically equivalent to the DDPM.

Variational diffusion model (VDM)

In the VDM, the variational marginal is selected to be Gaussian:

q_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{y})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{t}|a_{t}\mathbf{y},b_{t}\mathbf{I})\ .

(47)

that is a special case of the more general Gaussian setting from the previous section with
\bm{\alpha}(\mathbf{y},t)=a_{t}\mathbf{y}
and
\beta(\mathbf{y},t)=b_{t}
.
Furthermore, a finite step size
reverse-time
Markov process
q(\mathbf{x}_{s}|\mathbf{x}_{t})
,
s<t
is
selected
that preserves the form of the marginal, that is, the marginal
q_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s}|\mathbf{y})=\int q(\mathbf{x}_{s}|\mathbf{x}_{t})q_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{x}_{t}
is given by
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{s}|a_{s}\mathbf{y},b^{2}_{s}\mathbf{I})
. The explicit form of the Markov process is

q(\mathbf{x}_{s}|\mathbf{x}_{t})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{s}|a_{s|t}\mathbf{x}_{t},b_{s|t}^{2}\mathbf{I})\ ,\qquad a_{s|t}=\frac{a_{s}}{a_{t}}\quad\mathrm{and}\quad b_{s|t}^{2}=b^{2}_{s}-a^{2}_{s|t}b_{t}^{2}\ .

(48)

This reverse-time Markov process is only defined when the signal-to-noise ratio
\SNR(t)\coloneqq\frac{a^{2}_{t}}{b^{2}_{t}}
is a strictly increasing function with
t
. This can be achieved with a learned (monotonic) function or a fixed function such as linear in
\log\SNR(t)=t\,\log\SNR(1)+(1-t)\,\log\SNR(0)
. As a further restriction, a so-called variance-preserving process is often used, such that
a^{2}_{t}+b^{2}_{t}=1
.
Song et al. 2020b
discusses various alternatives in the same class. We discuss the choice of denoising process after taking the SDE limit of the variational process.

SDE limit.

We can convert the finite step process to a difference equation and then take the
\Delta t\to 0
limit like we did in the ELBO derivation in
Section

E.1
. We write
q(\mathbf{x}_{s}|\mathbf{x}_{t})
as a reverse-time finite step size process:

\Delta\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{x}_{s}-\mathbf{x}_{t}=a_{s|t}\mathbf{x}_{t}+b_{s|t}\bm{\epsilon}-\mathbf{x}_{t}=\underbrace{\frac{a_{s|t}-1}{\Delta t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}_{\overleftarrow{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},t)}\Delta t+\underbrace{\frac{b_{s|t}}{\sqrt{|\Delta t|}}}_{\sigma_{t}}\overleftarrow{\Delta}\mathbf{W}^{\prime}_{t}

(49)

with
\Delta t=s-t
,
\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\epsilon}|\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
and
\overleftarrow{\Delta}\mathbf{W}^{\prime}_{t}
being the finite step reverse-time Wiener process with variance
\Delta t
. We can derive the drift and diffusion coefficient in the limit:

\displaystyle\overleftarrow{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle=\frac{a_{s|t}-1}{\Delta t}\mathbf{x}_{t}=\frac{a_{s}-a_{t}}{\Delta t}\frac{\mathbf{x}_{t}}{a_{t}}\to\frac{\partial\log a_{t}}{\partial t}\mathbf{x}_{t}

(50)

\displaystyle\sigma_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{b^{2}_{s|t}}{|\Delta t|}=\frac{\SNR(t)-\SNR(s)}{|\Delta t|}\frac{b_{s}^{2}}{\SNR(t)}\to b_{t}^{2}\frac{\partial\log\SNR(t)}{\partial t}\ .

(51)

We can use the result from Section
4.1
:
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)=\overleftarrow{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)+\sigma_{t}^{2}\nabla\log q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})
to derive the forward SDE drift

\overrightarrow{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)=\frac{\partial\log a_{t}}{\partial t}\mathbf{x}_{t}+\sigma_{t}^{2}\nabla\log q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})=\frac{\partial\log a_{t}}{\partial t}\mathbf{x}_{t}-\sigma_{t}^{2}\frac{\mathbf{x}-a_{t}\mathbf{y}}{b^{2}_{t}}\ .

(52)

This can be compared to the more general result using the linear normalizing flow (
44
).

Parameterizing generative drift as a signal prediction network.

Finally, it is preferred to parameterize the generative drift function
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x},t)
in terms of a signal or noise prediction neural network. For example, one may define the drift as
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x},t)\coloneq\overrightarrow{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},\widehat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x},t),t)
with
\widehat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x},t)
being the signal prediction neural network and
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)
being the variational drift function. For the Gaussian variational used in the VDM this leads to a simplified expressions for the diffusion term in the SDE ELBO (
36
) with a signal from latent objective:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{f}},\overrightarrow{\mathbf{g}},\mathbf{y})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial\SNR(t)}{\partial t}\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})}\left[||\mathbf{y}-\widehat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x},t)||^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t

(53)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\SNR(0)}^{\SNR(1)}\left.\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})}\left[||\mathbf{y}-\widehat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x},t||^{2}\right]\right|_{t=t(\SNR)}\mathrm{d}\SNR\ ,

where in the second equality we have made a change of integration variable to
\SNR
and
t(\SNR)
is the inverse function of
\SNR(t)
.

7.2
Score matching

Score matching
(
Vincent 2011
;
Song et al. 2020b
)
derives its name from the objective of fitting
\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x},t)
, an approximation to the unconditional score function
\nabla\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x})
, to the conditional score function
\nabla\log q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})
. In this section, we will show that the score matching objective can be obtained from the diffusion term in the SDE ELBO for a specific choice of parameterization of the generative and variational processes. The reconstruction and prior terms in the ELBO can be ignored as these only depend upon the variational process which is fixed in the score matching framework. The score matching framework is thus optimizing the ELBO with respect to the generative process for a given variational distribution. This relationship between the SDE ELBO and the score matching has been pointed out in the literature before, e.g. by
Song et al. 2021
. The original derivation of score matching takes a different route based upon an infinite dataset argument. It is presented in
Appendix

F
.

Score matching uses the same reverse-time variational SDE as used in the diffusion model and again we can use
Section

4.1
to reverse the direction of time:

\overrightarrow{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)=\overleftarrow{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},t)+\nabla\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x},t)+\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x},t)\nabla\log q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})\ .

(54)

Likewise, we can write the generative drift as:
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x},t)=\overleftarrow{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x},t)+\nabla\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x},t)+\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x},t)\nabla\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x})
. In order to get to a tractable score matching objective, we select
\overleftarrow{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x},t)=\overleftarrow{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},t)
and replace the unknown score function with a learnable score function
\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x},t)
:

\overrightarrow{\mathbf{f}}_{\mathrm{score}}(\mathbf{x},t)=\overleftarrow{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},t)+\nabla\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x},t)+\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x},t)\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x},t)\ .

(55)

Since the score function only approximates the true drift, the transformation between the different drifts are only approximate as well.
Inserting the score matching parameterization for the generative and variational SDE drifts equations (
55
) and (
54
) into the SDE ELBO diffusion term leads to the score matching objective:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{Score}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})}\left[\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x},t)\left\|\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x},t)-\nabla\log q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})\right\|^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t\ .

(56)

This is exactly the score-matching loss used in the diffusion models literature
(
Song et al. 2020b
)
, which is a weighted average of denoising score matching objectives
(
Vincent 2011
)
over different noise levels. In score matching, the variational distribution is chosen in the same way as in diffusion models.

It can be shown, see Appendix
F
, that in the infinite-data limit the optimal
\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x},t)
is the score function of the unconditional marginal distribution:
\mathbf{s}^{*}(x,t)=\nabla\log q_{t}(\mathbf{x})
, where
q_{t}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{y})}[q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})]
. Since the data distribution
q(\mathbf{y})
is not available, it is replaced by an average over the training set in the objective. The name score matching thus is derived from the objective of matching the data conditional score function
\nabla\log q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})
to a function that approximates the unconditional score function
\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x},t)\approx\nabla\log q_{t}(\mathbf{x})
.

7.3
Flow matching

In flow matching
(
Lipman et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2022
)
we use the same setup as in score matching, but instead of introducing the score as our objective we will use the flow (or velocity) which is another name for the ODE drift
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)
. From Section
4.1
, we can formally write
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)=\left(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)+\overleftarrow{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)\right)/2
. If we restrict the reverse-time drift to be data-independent we can rewrite the relationship between the drift terms as:

\overrightarrow{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)=2\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)-\overleftarrow{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},t)\ .

(57)

Similarly, we may choose to define the generative SDE drift in terms of a flow function
\mathbf{v}(x,t)
:

\overrightarrow{\mathbf{f}}_{\mathrm{flow}}(\mathbf{x},t)=2\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x},t)-\overleftarrow{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},t)\ .

(58)

Inserting these parameterizations into the diffusion term leads to the flow matching objective:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{Flow}}

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})}\left[\frac{\left\|\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x},t)-\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)\right\|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x},t)}\right]\mathrm{d}t\ .

(59)

In flow matching, samples of
\mathbf{x}_{t}
are often obtained as
\mathbf{x}_{t}=t\mathbf{y}+(1-t)\bm{\epsilon}
with
\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\epsilon}|\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
or equivalently
q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}|t\mathbf{y},(1-t)^{2}\mathbf{I})
. This type of interpolation can be generalized to other settings, see for example
Albergo et al. 2023
;
Tong et al. 2023a
. We can get further insight about the flow matching objective, by using the result from
Section

6
to write the variational drift in a particular simple form:

\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y},t)=\frac{\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{t}}{1-t}=\frac{y-t\mathbf{y}-(1-t)\bm{\epsilon}}{1-t}=\mathbf{y}-\bm{\epsilon}\ .

(60)

We can conclude from this that the flow matching objective’s target for the drift
\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)=\mathbf{v}(t\mathbf{y}+(1-t)\bm{\epsilon},t)
is
\mathbf{y}-\bm{\epsilon}
with
\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
.

The diffusion loss derived above is all we need as an objective because the prior and reconstruction terms have no tunable parameters when we use the stochastic interpolation variational. Nonetheless, it is still instructive to write the prior and reconstruction terms to get the full ELBO picture. We will generalize the interpolation slightly to
x_{t}=ty+\beta_{t}\bm{\epsilon}
with
\beta_{t}=(1-t)\beta_{0}+t\beta_{1}
. The prior term is
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{y})\|p_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0}))=-\frac{d}{2}(\log\beta_{0}^{2}+1-\beta_{0}^{2})
for a standard Gaussian prior. The reconstruction term is
\mathbb{E}_{q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})}\left[\log p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_{1})\right]=-\frac{d}{2}(\log 2\pi\delta^{2}+\frac{\beta_{1}^{2}}{\delta^{2}})
for the Gaussian likelihood
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_{1},\delta^{2}\mathbf{I})
. We observe that reconstruction term diverges in the
\delta\to 0
(noise-free) limit. The ELBO is thus ill-defined in that case. Optimizing
\beta_{0}
and
\beta_{1}
might lead to a higher ELBO than the flow matching defaults
(
Zheng et al. 2023
)
. Optimizing the flow matching objective with respect to the diffusion coefficient
\sigma(\mathbf{x},t)
will lead to the trivial
\sigma(\mathbf{x},t)\to\infty
solution. This is directly connected to the ELBO gap diverging in this limit (results not shown).

As shown in Appendix
F
, analogous to the score matching result, in the infinite data limit, the optimal flow corresponds to a marginalized drift:

\mathbf{v}^{*}(\mathbf{x},t)=\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}[\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)]

with
q_{t}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})=q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})q(\mathbf{y})/q_{t}(\mathbf{x})
.
Flow matching may, like score matching be used either as a generative ODE using the learned flow function
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)=\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x},t)
or as an SDE with drift
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x},t)=2\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x},t)-\overleftarrow{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},t)
.

To sum up, score and flow matching may be viewed as slightly restricted versions of the general variational SDE approach because the variational is defined as a “from data to noise” diffusion (reverse-time SDE). The score and flow matching approaches have the advantage that learned generative model can be used both in an ODE and SDE formulation. This result is approximate because it hinges on a derivation of the objective which is only strictly valid in the infinite-data limit.

9
Discussion

This paper has provided a self-contained and informal methodological introduction to generative models based on ordinary and stochastic differential equations. The differential equations and the Fokker–Planck equation governing the temporal evolution of the marginal distribution of the latent variables have been presented and derived. The variational approach has been used to derive lower bounds on the log likelihood for the different models. This ELBO (evidence lower bound) serves as a tractable objective function for learning the parameters of the generative differential equation specified in terms of the drift and diffusion coefficient. Recently proposed popular machine learning methods for generative modeling with differential equations (neural ordinary differential equations, diffusion, score and flow matching models) have been presented in the context of the general variational approach.

The purpose of this paper is to present the relevant methodology in one place. Various practical considerations necessary for practical use, such as neural architectures for efficient inference and good performance, will have to be found in the recent more application-focused literature.

The application of generative modeling in machine learning has reached a very mature stage in a relatively short time with very impressive results in diverse areas ranging from image and video to molecules. Interestingly, the setting used in machine learning is most often that of a simple (conditional) prior followed by a latent path and then generation. The typical setting in statistics, where the differential equation describes a latent process for a
time series
, is rarely considered in the machine learning setting. This stems from the fact that construction of a suitable variational latent process is much more difficult than in the “one observation” case. Going beyond the one observation scenario is thus a largely unexplored research topic that could have implications for further use of variational techniques for modeling time series
(
Bartosh et al. 2025
)
.

Appendix A
The Kramers–Moyal expansion and the Fokker–Planck equation

In this appendix we show (i) a general Taylor series expansion expression for the partial time derivative of the marginal density that (ii) for the SDE will only consist of the first and second order term. The Fokker–Planck equation holds for any continuous-time stochastic process as long as there are no jumps, i.e., discrete changes of the stochastic variables
(
Risken 1996
;
Øksendal 2013
;
Gardiner 2009
)
. Markov jump processes
(
Gardiner 2009
)
provide a tool to deal with jumps in the process, but this is beyond the scope of this paper.

A.1
Kramers–Moyal

The Fokker–Planck equation is a special case of a more general equation, the Kramers–Moyal expansion, that describes the evolution of the density
p_{t}(\mathbf{x})
over time in any stochastic process. In this section, we will derive the Kramers–Moyal expansion, and in
Section

A.2
we will show that for Wiener (Brownian) stochastic processes it has only two terms and reduces to the Fokker–Planck equation.

The derivation of the Kramers–Moyal expansion starts from the definition of the partial derivative:

\frac{\partial p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial t}=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{1}{\Delta t}\left(p_{t+\Delta t}(\mathbf{x})-p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right)\ ,

(61)

where we denote the finite time difference by
\Delta t
and take its limit to zero.
The time-shifted distribution
p_{t+\Delta t}(\mathbf{x})
can be expressed as a marginalization over the distribution of
\mathbf{x}
at time
t
:

p_{t+\Delta t}(\mathbf{x})=\int p_{t+\Delta t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{x}^{\prime})p_{t}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\ ,

(62)

where we will leave the “transition kernel”
p_{t+\Delta t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
unspecified for now. In Section
A.2
we consider stochastic differential equations that can be discretized to give a Gaussian transition kernel.
Now, for any smooth analytic function
h(\mathbf{x})
such that
h(\mathbf{x})\frac{\partial p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial t}
is integrable, we can use
Eqs.

61
and
62
to get:

\displaystyle\int h(\mathbf{x})\frac{\partial p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial t}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq:def_pde})}}{{=}}\int_{\mathbf{x}}h(\mathbf{x})\left[\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{1}{\Delta t}\left(p_{t+\Delta t}(\mathbf{x})-p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right)\right]\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

(63)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq:marg})}}{{=}}\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{1}{\Delta t}\left(\int_{\mathbf{x}}h(\mathbf{x})\int_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}p_{t+\Delta t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{x}^{\prime})p_{t}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}-\int_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}h(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})p_{t}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{1}{\Delta t}\bigg(\int_{\mathbf{x}}h(\mathbf{x})\int_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}p_{t+\Delta t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{x}^{\prime})p_{t}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}h(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})p_{t}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\underbrace{\int_{\mathbf{x}}p_{t+\Delta t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}}_{=1}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{1}{\Delta t}\int_{\mathbf{x}}\int_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}p_{t+\Delta t}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})p_{t}(\mathbf{x})(h(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})-h(\mathbf{x}))\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\ ,

where in the last step we have swapped names of the integration variables
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
.
Applying a Taylor expansion of
h(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
around
\mathbf{x}
,
we can rewrite
Eq.

63
as follows:

\displaystyle\int h(\mathbf{x})\frac{\partial p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial t}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{1}{\Delta t}\int_{\mathbf{x}}\int_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}p_{t+\Delta t}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})p_{t}(\mathbf{x})(h(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})-h(\mathbf{x}))\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

(64)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{1}{\Delta t}\int_{\mathbf{x}}\int_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}p_{t+\Delta t}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}=1}^{d}\frac{(x^{\prime}_{i_{1}}-x_{i_{1}})\cdots(x^{\prime}_{i_{n}}-x_{i_{n}})}{n!}

\displaystyle\mbox{\hskip 213.39566pt}\times\underbrace{\frac{\partial^{n}h(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{i_{1}}\cdots\partial x_{i_{n}}}}_{=h^{(n)}_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}}(\mathbf{x})}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\int p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}=1}^{d}D^{(n)}_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}}(\mathbf{x},t)\;h^{(n)}_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\ ,

where we defined the
Kramers–Moyal coefficients

D^{(n)}_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}}(\mathbf{x},t)
for
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
(also called jump moments) as:

\displaystyle D^{(n)}_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}}(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{n!}\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{1}{\Delta t}\int(x^{\prime}_{i_{1}}-x_{i_{1}})\cdots(x^{\prime}_{i_{n}}-x_{i_{n}})p_{t+\Delta t}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}

(65)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{1}{\Delta t}\mathbb{E}[\Delta x_{t,i_{1}}\cdots\Delta x_{t,i_{n}}|\mathbf{x}_{t}]\ ,

(66)

where the conditional expectation of the increment
\Delta\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{x}_{t+\Delta t}-\mathbf{x}_{t}
is over the distribution
p_{t}(\Delta\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{t})
.

With integration by parts, we shift the derivatives from
h(\mathbf{x})
to
p_{t}(\mathbf{x})D^{(n)}_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}}(\mathbf{x},t)
. To show how this works consider the first order term:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int p_{t}(\mathbf{x})D^{(1)}_{i}(\mathbf{x},t)\frac{\partial h(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{i}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\underbrace{\left[p_{t}(\mathbf{x})D^{(1)}_{i}(\mathbf{x},t)h(\mathbf{x})\right]_{-\infty}^{+\infty}}_{=0\,\mathrm{by\,assumption}}-\int h(\mathbf{x})\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\left[D^{(1)}_{i}(\mathbf{x},t)p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right]\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

(67)

The assumption of the first term being zero is based upon the following argument: the density
p_{t}(\mathbf{x})
must necessarily vanish at
\pm\infty
because it integrates to 1 and the jump moment
D^{(1)}_{i}(\mathbf{x},t)
and
h(\mathbf{x})
(that we choose) are both well-behaved so that they together will not grow faster than
p_{t}(\mathbf{x})
goes to zero.
For the
n
-th order term we apply this
n
times to arrive at:

\int h(\mathbf{x})\frac{\partial p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial t}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\int h(\mathbf{x})\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}}\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i_{1}}\cdots\partial x_{i_{n}}}\left[D^{(n)}_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}}(\mathbf{x},t)p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right]\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\ .

(68)

Since this has to hold for any
h
, we get the Kramers–Moyal expansion:

\frac{\partial p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial t}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}}\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{i_{1}}\cdots\partial x_{i_{n}}}\left[D^{(n)}_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}}(\mathbf{x},t)p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right]\ ,

(69)

which is an infinite sum that either truncates at
n=2
or has infinitely many nonzero terms
(
Pawula 1967
;
Risken 1996
, Pawula’s theorem;)
.

Comparing this equation with the Fokker–Planck equation (
6
) we see that only including the two first orders gives the Fokker–Planck equation and we identify the drift coefficient
f_{i}(\mathbf{x},t)=D^{(1)}_{i}(\mathbf{x},t)
and the diffusion coefficient
\sum_{k}\sigma_{ik}(\mathbf{x},t)\sigma_{jk}(\mathbf{x},t)/2=D_{ij}^{(2)}(\mathbf{x},t)
with the two first drift moments. In the next section we will show how SDEs are connected to the Fokker–Planck equation.

A.2
Derivation of Fokker–Planck for the SDE

We can derive the Fokker–Planck equation for the SDE starting from the Kramers–Moyal expansion Equation (
69
) and show that only the two first jump moments will be non-zero in the infinitesimal limit.

We can calculate the jump moments Equation (
66
) for the SDE by noting that the conditional for the increment
p(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{x}_{t},t)
used in the definition of the jump moment is Gaussian for the SDE because we use the Wiener process:

p_{t}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{x})=\mathcal{N}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)\mathrm{d}t,\;\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{x},t)\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{T}}(\mathbf{x},t)\mathrm{d}t)\ .

(70)

Using the definition can immediately write down the two first jump moments:
D^{(1)}_{i}(\mathbf{x},t)=f_{i}(\mathbf{x},t)
and
D^{(2)}_{ij}(x,t)=\sum_{k}\sigma_{ik}(\mathbf{x},t)\sigma_{jk}(\mathbf{x},t)/2
. To show that the higher moments disappear we will calculate

\mathbb{E}[dx_{t,i_{1}}\cdots dx_{t,i_{n}}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}]
which is just a non-central moment of a Gaussian with mean
m_{i}=f_{i}(x,t)\mathrm{d}t
and covariance
v_{ij}=\sum_{k}\sigma_{ik}(\mathbf{x},t)\sigma_{jk}(\mathbf{x},t)\mathrm{d}t
. Since we are only interested in the scaling property in the infinitesimal limit, we can simplify to counting powers of mean
m
and variance
v
components. The non-central moment is a sum where in our case only one term dominates as
dt\to 0
:

\displaystyle n\text{ odd}:

\displaystyle\qquad\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{d}x_{t,i_{1}}\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{t,i_{n}}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}]

\displaystyle=mv^{\frac{n-1}{2}}+o(mv^{\frac{n-1}{2}})=O(\mathrm{d}t\cdot(\mathrm{d}t)^{\frac{n-1}{2}})=O((\mathrm{d}t)^{\frac{n+1}{2}})

(71)

\displaystyle n\text{ even}:

\displaystyle\qquad\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{d}x_{t,i_{1}}\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{t,i_{n}}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}]

\displaystyle=v^{\frac{n}{2}}+o(v^{\frac{n}{2}})=O((\mathrm{d}t)^{\frac{n}{2}})

or in a single expression using the ceiling function:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{d}x_{t,i_{1}}\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{t,i_{n}}|\mathbf{x}_{t}]

\displaystyle=O((\mathrm{d}t)^{\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil})\ .

(72)

Now when taking the limit in the definition of the jump moment we divide by
\mathrm{d}t
, so any term with
n>2
will vanish, since it will be
O((\mathrm{d}t)^{\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil-1})
where the exponent is
\geq 1
. So the SDE recovers the Fokker–Planck Equation (
6
) with
D^{(1)}_{i}(\mathbf{x},t)=f_{i}(\mathbf{x},t)
and
D^{(2)}_{ij}(\mathbf{x},t)=\sum_{k}\sigma_{ik}(\mathbf{x},t)\sigma_{jk}(\mathbf{x},t)/2
.

Appendix B
Reverse-time SDEs

Here we give the derivation of the reverse-time SDE using the forward clock
t
stated in
Section

4.1

While this can be made rigorous (see, e.g.,
Kunita 2019
),
we will present here a heuristic derivation by leveraging the view of SDEs as limits of discrete-time processes.

We start by introducing the
reverse-time Wiener process

\mathbf{W}^{\prime}_{t}\coloneqq\mathbf{W}_{1-t}-\mathbf{W}_{1}
. Because this construction simply reverses the original trajectory and shifts it so that it starts at 0, this is a standard Wiener process when viewed backward in time.
We also need to define
\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{x},t)\coloneqq\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x},1-t)
and

\displaystyle\overleftarrow{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle\coloneqq-\overrightarrow{\mathbf{f}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x},1-t)

(73)

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{f}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x},1-t)-\frac{1}{2\;p^{\prime}_{1-t}(\mathbf{x})}\nabla\cdot\left[\mathbf{D}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x},1-t)\;p^{\prime}_{1-t}(\mathbf{x})\right]

(74)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)-\frac{1}{2\;p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\nabla\cdot\left[\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x},t)\;p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right]\ .

(75)

Now, note that the precise meaning of
Eq.

14
is given by the integral equation:

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{\tau}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{0}+\int_{0}^{\tau}\overrightarrow{\mathbf{f}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{s},s)\,ds+\int_{0}^{\tau}\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{s},s)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{s}\ .

(76)

Consider its discretization with steps
\{\nicefrac{{i}}{{T}}\}_{i=0}^{\mathrm{T}}
and rewrite it in reverse-time by reindexing with
j=T-i
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{\frac{k}{T}}=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{1-\frac{k}{T}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{0}+\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=0}^{T-k-1}\overrightarrow{\mathbf{f}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{\frac{i}{T}},\nicefrac{{i}}{{T}})+\sum_{i=0}^{T-k-1}\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{\frac{i}{T}},\nicefrac{{i}}{{T}})\,(\mathbf{W}_{\frac{i+1}{T}}-\mathbf{W}_{\frac{i}{T}})

(77)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{x}_{1}-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=0}^{T-k-1}\overleftarrow{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x}_{1-\frac{i}{T}},1-\nicefrac{{i}}{{T}})+\sum_{i=0}^{T-k-1}\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_{1-\frac{i}{T}},1-\nicefrac{{i}}{{T}})\,(\mathbf{W}^{\prime}_{1-\frac{i+1}{T}}-\mathbf{W}^{\prime}_{1-\frac{i}{T}})

(78)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{x}_{1}-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{j=k+1}^{\mathrm{T}}\overleftarrow{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x}_{\frac{j}{T}},\nicefrac{{j}}{{T}})-\sum_{j=k+1}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_{\frac{j}{T}},\nicefrac{{j}}{{T}})\,(\mathbf{W}^{\prime}_{\frac{j}{T}}-\mathbf{W}^{\prime}_{\frac{j-1}{T}})\ .

(79)

Crucially, the terms in the two sums use the value on the
right
of each interval, unlike in the usual forward SDE with the reverse-time clock
\tau
(
Eq.

14
). While for the deterministic term this is equivalent in the limit, in the stochastic term it is not.
In the limit, we get the integral equation:

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{x}_{1}-\int_{t}^{1}\overleftarrow{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x}_{s},s)\,\mathrm{d}s-\int_{t}^{1}\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_{s},s)\,\overleftarrow{\mathrm{d}}\mathbf{W}^{\prime}_{s}

(80)

where
\overleftarrow{\mathrm{d}}
indicates a
backward Itô integral
, in the sense explained above.
The reverse-time SDE is typically written in the literature in its differential form

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{t}=\overleftarrow{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)\,\overleftarrow{\mathrm{d}}\mathbf{W}^{\prime}_{t}\qquad\qquad\text{(RT SDE F-clock)}

(81)

Anderson 1982
showed the stronger result that, when the forward and backward SDEs have the same time marginals
and
the same exact diffusion coefficient
\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{x},t)
,
the path distributions
—not just the marginals—
in both directions are also identical
.

B.1
Negative semi-definite diffusion matrices

When rewriting the FPE for
Eq.

14
—which is originally in the reverse-time clock
\tau
—in terms of the
forward
time variable
t
, we obtain a diffusion term with a
negative sign
or, equivalently, a standard diffusion term with a negative semi-definite matrix (
-\mathbf{D}
):

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial t}=-\frac{\partial p^{\prime}_{\tau}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial\tau}

\displaystyle=-\left(-\nabla\cdot\left[\overrightarrow{\mathbf{f}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x},\tau)\;p^{\prime}_{\tau}(\mathbf{x})\right]+\frac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot\left(\nabla\cdot\left[\mathbf{D}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x},\tau)p^{\prime}_{\tau}(\mathbf{x})\right]\right)\right)

(82)

\displaystyle=-\nabla\cdot\left[\overleftarrow{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x},t)\;p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right]-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot\left(\nabla\cdot\left[\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x},t)p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right]\right)\ .

(83)

This reveals a fundamental symmetry between forward- and reverse-time SDEs. Starting from the continuity equation of an ODE, adding a positive semi-definite diffusion term and adjusting the drift to preserve marginals
p_{t}
results in a valid FPE corresponding to a forward-time SDE. In contrast, introducing a
negative
semi-definite diffusion term and similarly adjusting the drift leads to a PDE that is not a forward-time FPE. However, under time reversal, this PDE becomes the FPE associated with a reverse-time diffusion process.

Appendix C
The ODE and SDE are continuity equations preserving probability mass

Interpretation of the log density derivative.

The integral in
Eq.

24
accounts for the change of log density along a solution of the ODE.
Intuitively,
the divergence of
\mathbf{f}
measures the instantaneous rate of volume change under the flow
, or in other words, it describes how space contracts or dilates over time along the flow,
4
4

4

Note that
\mathbf{f}
could still expand the volume along some directions and contract it along others. For example, if the divergence is positive, not all paths will necessarily move away from each other. Divergence merely describes the net effect.
as we briefly show next.
Consider a small volume
\mathcal{V}(t)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
evolved by the velocity field
\mathbf{f}
, let
\partial\mathcal{V}(t)
be its boundary and
\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x},t)
the outward-pointing unit normal vector to the boundary.
Using (a) the Leibniz integral rule and (b) the divergence theorem gives:

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left|\mathcal{V}(t)\right|=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathcal{V}(t)}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\text{a})}}{{=}}\int_{\partial\mathcal{V}(t)}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)\cdot\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x},t)\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{x})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\text{b})}}{{=}}\int_{\mathcal{V}(t)}\nabla\cdot\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\ .

(84)

If the volume is small enough that
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)
is approximately constant, then
\int_{\mathcal{V}(t)}\nabla\cdot\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\approx\left|\mathcal{V}(t)\right|\nabla\cdot\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)
, and we have our result relating the divergence to local volume expansion:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\log\left|\mathcal{V}(t)\right|\approx\nabla\cdot\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)\ .

(85)

In addition, combining this with
Eq.

23
, we obtain
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\log\left|\mathcal{V}(t)\right|\approx-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x})
, suggesting that local volume and probability density are inversely proportional along the ODE flow. This is formalized below, where we show that the probability mass within a region transformed by the flow remains constant.

Probability conservation in the ODE.

Given a volume
\mathcal{V}(t^{*})\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
at some time
t^{*}
and an ODE
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)
, we can define the time-dependent volume
\mathcal{V}(t)
as the set of all points
\mathbf{x}_{t}
obtained at time
t
by evolving each point in
\mathcal{V}(t^{*})
under the flow of the ODE (i.e., the image of
\mathcal{V}(t^{*})
under the flow map
\phi_{t^{*}}^{t}
from
t^{*}
to
t
).
Then, the probability associated with this evolving volume remains constant over time, i.e.,
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}P(\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{V}(t),t)=0
.

To show this, we start from the ODE continuity equation:

\frac{\partial p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial t}=-\sum_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}[f_{i}(\mathbf{x},t)p(\mathbf{x},t)]=-\nabla\cdot[\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)p_{t}(\mathbf{x})]\ .

(86)

The total derivative of the probability is expressed through the Leibniz integral rule:

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathcal{V}(t)}p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\int_{\mathcal{V}(t)}\frac{\partial p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial t}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}+\int_{\partial\mathcal{V}(t)}p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}}{\mathrm{d}t}\cdot\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x},t)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{x})\ ,

(87)

where
\partial\mathcal{V}(t)
is the boundary of
\mathcal{V}(t)
,
\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x},t)
is the outward-pointing unit normal vector to the boundary and
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}}{\mathrm{d}t}\cdot\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x},t)
is the normal component of the velocity field on the boundary, describing how the boundary itself is moving. We can now use the divergence theorem
\int_{\mathcal{V}}\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\int_{\partial\mathcal{V}}\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{x})
to rewrite the surface term in the expression above as a volume integral:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathcal{V}(t)}p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{V}(t)}\left(\frac{\partial p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}}{\mathrm{d}t}p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right]\right)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

(88)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{V}(t)}\underbrace{\left(\frac{\partial p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\left[\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right]\right)}_{=0\,\mathrm{(Liouville)}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=0\ .

This fundamental result is a consequence of the Liouville equation being a continuity equation for a conserved quantity, the probability, see for example
Villani et al. 2009
. Over time the probability density can change but the continuity equation ensures that the total probability is conserved. The Fokker–Planck equation generalizes probability conservation to the stochastic setting.

Probability conservation in the SDE.

We cannot apply the same analysis as for the ODE because
\mathcal{V}(t)
is not defined in the stochastic case. Instead, we will answer the methodological question: Is there an effective deterministic velocity field
\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x},t)
such that a volume evolving under this field conserves the probability mass of the SDE marginal
p_{t}(\mathbf{x})
.

To answer this question, we apply the Leibniz integral rule (
87
) to a volume
\mathcal{V}(t)
moving with an unknown velocity field
\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x},t)
while requiring total probability conservation:

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathcal{V}(t)}p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\int_{\mathcal{V}(t)}\frac{\partial p_{t}}{\partial t}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}+\int_{\partial\mathcal{V}(t)}p_{t}\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{n}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{S}=0\ .

(89)

Using the Fokker-Planck equation and the divergence theorem, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathcal{V}(t)}p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\mathcal{V}(t)}\left(-\overrightarrow{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x},t)p_{t}(\mathbf{x})+\frac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot[\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x},t)p_{t}(\mathbf{x})]+\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x},t)p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right)\cdot\mathbf{n}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{S}\ .

(90)

For this to be zero for any volume, the integrand must be zero. Solving for
\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x},t)
, we recover the drift of an ODE with the same marginals as the SDE:

\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x},t)=-\frac{1}{p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\frac{\partial p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial t}=\overrightarrow{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x},t)-\frac{1}{2p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\nabla\cdot[\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x},t)p_{t}(\mathbf{x})]=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)\ .

(91)

This result shows that the Fokker–Planck is a continuity equation for the probability in the same sense as the Liouville equation. This should not surprise us because we already know that we can define both ODEs and SDEs with the same temporal evolution of the marginal.

Appendix D
Derivations for latent SDE

D.1
Derivative of KL divergence

We will use the following shorthand and general notation in the following:
\mathbf{f}_{t}=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)
,
\mathbf{g}_{t}=\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},t)
,
p_{t}=p_{t}(\mathbf{x})
and
q_{t}=q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})
.
Let
p_{t},q_{t}
be time-varying densities that solve the continuity equation with the vector fields
\mathbf{f}_{t},\mathbf{g}_{t}
, respectively, i.e.:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p_{t}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\nabla\cdot(p_{t}\,\mathbf{f}_{t})

\displaystyle\frac{\partial q_{t}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\nabla\cdot(q_{t}\,\mathbf{g}_{t})

and rewrite the KL derivative in terms of the partial time derivatives of the two densities:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{t}\|p_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int q_{t}\log\frac{q_{t}}{p_{t}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left[q_{t}\,\log\frac{q_{t}}{p_{t}}\right]\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\partial q_{t}}{\partial t}\log\frac{q_{t}}{p_{t}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}+\int q_{t}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\log q_{t}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}-\int q_{t}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\log p_{t}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\partial q_{t}}{\partial t}\log\frac{q_{t}}{p_{t}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}+\cancel{\int\frac{\partial q_{t}}{\partial t}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}}-\int\frac{q_{t}}{p_{t}}\frac{\partial p_{t}}{\partial t}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\ .

Now we can plug in the continuity equations to obtain:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{t}\|p_{t})

\displaystyle=-\int\nabla\cdot(q_{t}\mathbf{g}_{t})\log\frac{q_{t}}{p_{t}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}+\int\frac{q_{t}}{p_{t}}\nabla\cdot(p_{t}\mathbf{f}_{t})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\ .

Integrating the first term by parts and using the divergence theorem:

\displaystyle\int\nabla\cdot(q_{t}\mathbf{g}_{t})\log\frac{q_{t}}{p_{t}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\cancel{\oint_{\partial\Omega}\log\frac{q_{t}}{p_{t}}\,(q_{t}\,\mathbf{g}_{t}\cdot\mathbf{n})\;\mathrm{d}S}-\int q_{t}\,\mathbf{g}_{t}\cdot\nabla\log\frac{q_{t}}{p_{t}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\ ,

where the boundary term can be understood as a flux for
\Omega
defined as a ball with radius that tends to
\infty
, and under standard assumptions (e.g., densities decaying fast enough) it vanishes.

Analogously for the second term (once again with vanishing boundary term since the densities decay fast enough), we have:

\displaystyle\int\frac{q_{t}}{p_{t}}\nabla\cdot(p_{t}\,\mathbf{f}_{t})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=-\int p_{t}\,\mathbf{f}_{t}\cdot\nabla\frac{q_{t}}{p_{t}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=-\int q_{t}\,\mathbf{f}_{t}\cdot\nabla\log\frac{q_{t}}{p_{t}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

(92)

and we conclude:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{t}\|p_{t})

\displaystyle=\int q_{t}\,\mathbf{g}_{t}\cdot\nabla\log\frac{q_{t}}{p_{t}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}-\int q_{t}\,\mathbf{f}_{t}\cdot\nabla\log\frac{q_{t}}{p_{t}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}}\big[(\mathbf{g}_{t}-\mathbf{f}_{t})\cdot\nabla\log\frac{q_{t}}{p_{t}}\big]\ .

(93)

Going back to our case, the result above is:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{t}\|p_{t})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})}\big[(\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)-\mathbf{f}_{t}(\mathbf{x},t))\cdot\nabla\log\frac{q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})}{p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\big]\ .

(94)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_{t}\,\|\,p_{t}\big)

we can rewrite that as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_{t}\,\|\,p_{t}\big)\,\mathrm{d}t

Appendix E
SDE ELBO via KL divergence between path measures

Here we provide an alternative derivation of the SDE ELBO, i.e., the ELBO arising from having a latent process that follows an SDE. As discussed, in this case
p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1})=\int p(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{x}_{0})p(\mathbf{x}_{0})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{0}
cannot be computed directly via a change of variables and is intractable in most cases. The KL divergence

in the ELBO (
22

In
Section

5.2

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{y})\|p_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}))

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ELBO}}^{\mathrm{SDE}}(\mathbf{y})\leq\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ELBO}}^{\mathrm{ODE}}(\mathbf{y})\leq\log p(\mathbf{y})
.
Here, we will derive exactly the same bound by using properties of KL divergences over path measures.

Path distributions for generative and variational models.

We start by specifying the path distribution
p(\mathbf{X})
of the generative model over

\mathbf{X}=\{\mathbf{x}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}

\mathbf{x}_{1}

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{t}\ ,\qquad\mathbf{x}_{0}\sim p_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\ .

(107)

Similarly, the variational distribution
q(\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{y})
is defined through a second SDE:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y},t)\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{t}\ ,\qquad\mathbf{x}_{0}\sim q_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{y})

(108)

that has a data-dependent drift
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y},t)
and shares the diffusion coefficient with the generative SDE. The latter is a necessary condition to get a finite KL divergence in

(
Archambeau et al. 2007

Nielsen et al. 2023

Särkkä & Solin 2019
)
.

3

(111)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{g},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})}\left[\left(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)-\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x},t)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)-\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)\right)\right]\mathrm{d}t\ .

(112)

The diffusion coefficient is often chosen to be a scalar function
\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{x},t)=\sigma(\mathbf{x},t)\mathbf{I}
. Then,
\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x},t)=\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{x},t)\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{T}}(\mathbf{x},t)=\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x},t)\mathbf{I}
and the diffusion term simplifies to:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{g},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})}\left[\frac{\left\|\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)-\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)\right\|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x},t)}\right]\mathrm{d}t\ .

(113)

E.1
Informal derivation of the path KL divergence

The KL divergence between path measures in (
110
), necessary to compute the ELBO for the SDE, can be directly derived in continuous time using the Girsanov theorem
(
Huang et al. 2021
;
Song et al. 2021
;
Särkkä & Solin 2019
)
. Here we will instead derive it using the Euler–Maruyama discretization of the SDE starting from finite
\Delta t
and taking the infinitesimal limit, as also done, e.g., by
Archambeau et al. 2007
and
Bartosh et al. 2024
.
6
6

6

Other works
(
Ho et al. 2020
;
Kingma et al. 2021
;
Nielsen et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
employ a different discretization that has the same continuous-time limit—see, for example,
Song et al. 2020b
for a discussion.

This discretization approach is close in spirit to an informal derivation of the Girsanov theorem
(
Särkkä & Solin 2019
)
.

We consider the two discrete-time processes with
T
steps and
T+1

t
-values:
corresponding to the generative model
p
and the inference model
q
:

\displaystyle p:\mbox{\hskip 14.22636pt}\Delta\mathbf{x}_{l}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{x}_{t_{l+1}}-\mathbf{x}_{t_{l}}=\mathbf{f}_{l}\Delta t+\bm{\sigma}_{l}\Delta\mathbf{W}_{l}

(114)

\displaystyle q:\mbox{\hskip 14.22636pt}\Delta\mathbf{x}_{l}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{x}_{t_{l+1}}-\mathbf{x}_{t_{l}}=\mathbf{g}_{l}\Delta t+\bm{\sigma}_{l}\Delta\mathbf{W}^{\prime}_{l}\ ,

(115)

where
\Delta\mathbf{W}_{l}
and
\Delta\mathbf{W}^{\prime}_{l}
are zero-mean Gaussians with variance
\Delta t=\nicefrac{{1}}{{T}}
, and we use the shorthand
\mathbf{f}_{l}=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t_{l}},t_{l})
and similarly for
\mathbf{g}_{l}
,
\bm{\sigma}_{l}
and
\mathbf{D}_{l}=\bm{\sigma}_{l}\bm{\sigma}_{l}^{\mathrm{T}}
.
Assuming
t_{l}=l\,\Delta t
, these two processes can be seen as discrete-time analogues of the inference and generative SDEs considered earlier.
The joint distributions over
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}_{t_{0}},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{t_{T}}
are:

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{X})

\displaystyle=p_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\prod_{l=0}^{T-1}p_{t_{l}}(\mathbf{x}_{t_{l+1}}|\mathbf{x}_{t_{l}})=p_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\prod_{l=0}^{T-1}\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{t_{l+1}}|\mathbf{x}_{t_{l}}+\mathbf{f}_{l}\Delta t,\bm{\sigma}_{l}\bm{\sigma}_{l}^{\mathrm{T}}\Delta t)

(116)

\displaystyle q(\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=q_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{y})\prod_{l=0}^{T-1}q_{t_{l}}(\mathbf{x}_{t_{l+1}}|\mathbf{x}_{t_{l}},\mathbf{y})=q_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{y})\prod_{l=0}^{T-1}\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{t_{l+1}}|\mathbf{x}_{t_{l}}+\mathbf{g}_{l}\Delta t,\bm{\sigma}_{l}\bm{\sigma}_{l}^{\mathrm{T}}\Delta t)\ ,

(117)

where we have left the prior distributions unspecified for now. We plug in these distributions into the ELBO Equation (
110
).
The KL divergence is the expectation with respect to
q(\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{y})
of the following log-likelihood ratio:

\displaystyle\log\frac{p(\mathbf{X})}{q(\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{y})}=\log\frac{p_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0})}{q_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{y})}-\frac{1}{2\Delta t}\sum_{l=0}^{T-1}

\displaystyle\left\{(\Delta\mathbf{x}_{l}-\mathbf{f}_{l}\Delta t)^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{D}^{-1}_{l}(\Delta\mathbf{x}_{l}-\mathbf{f}_{l}\Delta t)-\right.

(118)

\displaystyle\,\,\left.(\Delta\mathbf{x}_{l}-\mathbf{g}_{l}\Delta t)^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{D}^{-1}_{l}(\Delta\mathbf{x}_{l}-\mathbf{g}_{l}\Delta t)\right\}\ .

We can rewrite the summation term in a form that makes it more suitable for taking expectation and for the
T\to\infty
limit:

\displaystyle\underbrace{-\frac{\Delta t}{2}\sum_{l=0}^{T-1}(\mathbf{f}_{l}-\mathbf{g}_{l})^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{D}^{-1}_{l}(\mathbf{f}_{l}-\mathbf{g}_{l})}_{\text{(i)}}+\underbrace{\sum_{l=0}^{T-1}(\mathbf{f}_{l}-\mathbf{g}_{l})^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{D}^{-1}_{l}(\Delta\mathbf{x}_{l}-\mathbf{g}_{l}\Delta t)}_{\text{(ii)}}\ .

(119)

The expectation of (ii) with respect to
q(\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{y})
is zero:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{y})}\left[\sum_{l=0}^{T-1}(\mathbf{f}_{l}-\mathbf{g}_{l})^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{D}^{-1}_{l}(\Delta\mathbf{x}_{l}-\mathbf{g}_{l}\Delta t)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}_{q_{l}}\Bigg[(\mathbf{f}_{l}-\mathbf{g}_{l})^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{D}^{-1}_{l}\ \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q_{l+1|l}}\left[\mathbf{x}_{l+1}-\mathbf{x}_{l}-\mathbf{g}_{l}\Delta t\right]}_{=0}\Bigg]\ ,

(120)

where we used the shorthand
q_{l}=q_{t_{l}}(\mathbf{x}_{t_{l}}|\mathbf{y})
and
q_{l+1|l}=q_{t_{l+1}}(\mathbf{x}_{t_{l+1}}|\mathbf{x}_{t_{l}},\mathbf{y})
.
We can then take the limit for
T\to\infty
of the expectation of (i):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{y})}

\displaystyle\left[-\frac{\Delta t}{2}\sum_{l=0}^{T-1}(\mathbf{f}_{l}-\mathbf{g}_{l})^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{D}^{-1}_{l}(\mathbf{f}_{l}-\mathbf{g}_{l})\right]=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}_{q_{l}}\left[(\mathbf{f}_{l}-\mathbf{g}_{l})^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{D}^{-1}_{l}(\mathbf{f}_{l}-\mathbf{g}_{l})\right]\Delta t

(121)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{T\to\infty}\underbrace{-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})}\left[(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)-\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t))^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{D}^{-1}_{l}(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)-\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t))\right]dt}_{\mathrm{Diffusion}}\ .

We have now calculated the diffusion term in the ELBO (
110
). The remaining term appearing in the log-likelihood ratio (
118
) is the prior term:

\mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{y})}\left[\log\frac{p_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0})}{q_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{y})}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{q_{0}(\mathbf{x}|y)}\left[\log\frac{p_{0}(\mathbf{x})}{q_{0}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})}\right]=\underbrace{-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{0}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y}),p_{0}(\mathbf{x}))}_{\mathrm{Prior}}\ .

(122)

We can now write the ELBO for the SDE (
110
) in the continuous limit as stated in
Eq.

36
.

Appendix F
Connection to standard derivations of score and flow matching

In this appendix, we elaborate on the derivation of the score and flow matching objectives presented in
Section

7.2
and draw connections to alternative derivations of the same objectives found in the literature.

F.1
Score matching

Here we will show that the optimal score function is indeed the marginal score, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}^{*}(\mathbf{x},t)=\argmin_{\mathbf{s}}\mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{y})}\left[\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{Score}}\right]=\nabla\log q_{t}(\mathbf{x})\ ,

(123)

where
q(\mathbf{y})
denotes the (unknown) data distribution. To derive this result, we average Equation
Eq.

56
over
q(\mathbf{y})
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{y})}\left[\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{Score}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})q(\mathbf{y})}\left[\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x},t)\left(||\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x},t)||^{2}-2\mathbf{s}^{\mathrm{T}}(\mathbf{x},t)\nabla\log q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})+||\nabla\log q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})||^{2}\right)\right]\mathrm{d}t\ .

(124)

We consider each term in turn. The first term does not depend on
\mathbf{y}
, thus:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})q(\mathbf{y})}\left[\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x},t)||\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x},t)||^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\left[\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x},t)||s(\mathbf{x},t)||^{2}\right]\ .

The second term can be rewritten as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})q(\mathbf{y})}\left[\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x},t)\mathbf{s}^{\mathrm{T}}(\mathbf{x},t)\nabla\log q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\left[\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x},t)\mathbf{s}^{\mathrm{T}}(\mathbf{x},t)\ \mathbb{E}_{q_{t}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}\left[\nabla\log q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\left[\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x},t)\mathbf{s}^{\mathrm{T}}(\mathbf{x},t)\ \nabla\log q_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right]\ ,

where we used Bayes
q_{t}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})=\frac{q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})q(\mathbf{x})}{q(\mathbf{y})}
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}\left[\nabla\log q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}\left[\nabla\log q_{t}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})\right]+\nabla\log q_{t}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\int\nabla q_{t}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{y}+\nabla\log q_{t}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\nabla\underbrace{\int q_{t}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{y}}_{=1}+\nabla\log q_{t}(\mathbf{x})

to write the expression in terms of the marginal score
\nabla\log q_{t}(\mathbf{x})
.

Eq.

124
can therefore be rewritten as:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{y})}\left[\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{Score}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\left[\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x},t)\left(||\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x},t)||^{2}-2\mathbf{s}^{\mathrm{T}}(\mathbf{x},t)\nabla\log q_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right)\right]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})q(\mathbf{y})}\left[\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x},t)||\nabla\log q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})||^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\left[\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x},t)\left(\left\|\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x},t)-\nabla\log q_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right\|^{2}-||\nabla\log q_{t}(\mathbf{x})||^{2}\right)\right]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})q(\mathbf{y})}\left[\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x},t)||\nabla\log q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})||^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\left[\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x},t)\left(\left\|\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x},t)-\nabla\log q_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right\|^{2}\right)\right]\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{const.}\ ,

where we completed the square in the first integral and in the last expression arrived at the sought objective with the constant collecting terms independent of
\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x},t)
.

This shows that the expectation of the diffusion loss
Eq.

56
over the data distribution defines a least squares objective with optimum
\mathbf{s}^{*}(\mathbf{x},t)=\nabla\log q_{t}(\mathbf{x})
. So in the asymptotic limit of infinite data, the optimization of the diffusion term leads to learning of the score
\nabla\log q_{t}(\mathbf{x})
.

In the diffusion models literature (see, e.g.,
Song et al. 2020b
), it is common to derive the score-matching formulation by proceeding in the reverse order. Typically, one starts from a
marginal
score matching objective like the following:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\left[\lambda(\mathbf{x},t)\left\|\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x},t)-\nabla\log q_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right\|^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t\ ,

which is a weighted average over time, with (almost) arbitrary weights
\lambda(\mathbf{x},t)
. While this objective is intractable, as it requires access to the true marginal score, it has the same optimum
\mathbf{s}^{*}(\mathbf{x},t)
as the expectation of
Eq.

56
, which can be optimized in practice. However, unless
\lambda(\mathbf{x},t)
is exactly
\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x},t)
, as we found above, this is not linked with maximum likelihood (this connection was discussed, e.g., by
Song et al. 2021
,
Huang et al. 2021
, and
Kingma et al. 2021
).

F.2
Flow matching

To connect to the flow matching literature
(
Lipman et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2022
)
, we calculate the expectation of the expression above in the same way as done for the score objective:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{y})}\left[\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{Flow}}\right]=

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\left[\frac{\left\|\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x},t)-\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},t)\right\|^{2}-||\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},t)||^{2}}{\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x},t)}\right]\mathrm{d}t\ +

(125)

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})q(\mathbf{y})}\left[\frac{||\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)||^{2}}{\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x},t)}\right]\mathrm{d}t\ ,

(126)

where we have introduced the “marginalized” drift:

\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},t)=\frac{1}{q_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\int\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})q(\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}\ .

(127)

So in the limit of infinite data, we get a least squares objective with optimum
\mathbf{v}^{*}(\mathbf{x},t)=\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},t)
.

Connection between conditional and marginal Liouville equations.

We can get further insight about this drift by relating the conditional Liouville equation:
\frac{\partial q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})}{\partial t}=-\nabla\cdot\left[\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})\right]
to the Liouville equation for the marginal
\frac{\partial q_{t}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial t}=-\nabla\cdot\left[\tilde{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},t)q_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right]
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}q_{t}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})q(\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}=\int\frac{\partial q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})}{\partial t}q(\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}=-\int\nabla\cdot\left[\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})\right]q(\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}

(128)

\displaystyle=-\nabla\cdot\underbrace{\int\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},t)q_{t}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})q(\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}}_{\tilde{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},t)q_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\ .

This indeed confirms that the drift
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},t)
Equation (
127
) is the drift
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},t)
in the Liouville equation for the marginal
q_{t}(\mathbf{x})
.
