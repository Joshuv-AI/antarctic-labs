Broadening the Assessment of Antimicrobial Value: Insights from Two Evaluations of Aztreonam–Avibactam in Spain
Authors: Sonia Luque, Luis Martínez-Martínez, Ricard Ferrer, Julián Torre-Cisneros, Carlota Moya-Alarcón, Alfonso de Lossada, Marta Maroto-Diaz, James Dennis, Edward Broughton, Maria Gheorghe
Published: 2026

Commentary

The challenge of determining the full value of antimicrobials is a persistent problem while antimicrobial resistance (AMR) continues to rise [1]. Traditional health technology assessment (HTA) frameworks capture patient‑level benefits but commonly overlook population‑level benefits, such as slowed resistance development and reduced transmission [2]. Along with high research and development costs and limited returns on investment for new antimicrobials, [3] this has led to under-investment in antimicrobial research and development. Quantifying the additional value of antimicrobials is essential for informing policy decisions, such as incentives to support the research and development of new antimicrobials.

Two cost-effectiveness analyses of aztreonam–avibactam (ATM-AVI) in Spain [4, 5] have been published and provide the opportunity to compare how modelling choices influence the valuation of antimicrobials. Both models used the same data sources for many inputs and reported similar outcomes; however, they reported considerably different estimates of value [4, 5]. Therefore, we aim to discuss the key differences in the modelling approaches employed, how these differences affect outcomes, and the role of both models in quantifying value for different stakeholders.

Aguilar et al. [5] report a cost-effectiveness model (CEM) approach, herein referred to as the ‘conventional CEM’, where cost-effectiveness is assessed by comparing the incremental costs and health benefits [quality-adjusted life years (QALYs)], that are accrued to an individual against a willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold, in line with conventional HTA methodologies. This approach overlooks the population-level effects of antimicrobials on resistance development and the transmission of infections.

In contrast to the conventional CEM, Ferrer et al. [4] have published a dynamic disease transmission model embedded into the STEDI (spectrum, transmission, enablement, diversity, insurance) value framework for antimicrobials focused on the transmission, diversity and enablement (TED) elements, to estimate both patient- and population-level healthcare system benefits of antimicrobials, herein referred to as the ‘TED model’. The dynamic transmission model, most frequently used to assess vaccinations, enables the model to capture the epidemiological impact of ATM-AVI in reducing the transmission of metallo-β-lactamase-producing Enterobacterales infections, and the value of increasing treatment diversity to slow resistance development to currently available treatments. Furthermore, the TED model extrapolates the indirect effects of population-level benefits of resistance and efficacy on antimicrobials used prophylactically to prevent infections in surgical procedures to estimate enablement value. Whilst both modelling approaches showed ATM-AVI to be cost-effective in the Spanish setting, the TED model demonstrated ATM-AVI to result in cost savings of €940.49 per QALY gained (dominant) [4] compared with an incremental cost–utility ratio (ICUR) of €3116 per QALY gained in the conventional CEM. Both ICURs are considered cost-effective at the WTP threshold in Spain of €25,000/QALY. This difference in ICUR was primarily driven by a considerably higher estimate of incremental QALYs (2.8 vs. 0.6). The TED model has a higher estimate of clinical benefit through several different assumptions. Firstly, modelling ATM-AVI as an additional treatment, as opposed to a head-to-head comparison with the comparator arms (conventional CEM), reflects the improved treatment effectiveness of the overall treatment arsenal, where adding a new antimicrobial reduces the resistance to existing treatments through increasing the diversity of treatment options. Secondly, compared with the comparator, the dynamic nature of the model captures the treatment arm’s improved effectiveness, driven by slower resistance gain over the modelled period, resulting in fewer infections in the population. This contrasts with the conventional CEM, which considers no changes of resistance over time (i.e. a static assumption). Finally, the lower resistance enables the safe use of antimicrobials prophylactically to perform medical procedures (i.e. the enablement component of the model), which is beyond the scope of the conventional CEM.

The conventional CEM demonstrated a marginal increase in overall costs of €1876 per patient treated with ATM-AVI [5], while the TED model showed a cost saving of €2666 per patient treated. This difference was largely attributed to fewer infections and improved survival, leading to lower costs [4]. In addition to the typical outcomes produced by a cost-effectiveness analysis, the TED model showed that, for every treated patient with ATM-AVI, there are 1.17 fewer infections and 0.27 lives could be saved. Assessing the population-level outcomes allows for the estimation of overall net monetary benefit (NMB) to the healthcare system (€1.22 billion) [4]. NMB summarises the monetary value of a treatment taking into account the WTP per QALY gained and the incremental costs, calculated as: (incremental benefit × WTP) – incremental cost. The conventional CEM supports decision-makers by demonstrating whether the clinical and economic benefits from a patient-level perspective justify the costs. As a required analysis for many HTA bodies, it allows comparisons of health outcomes across different disease areas. This methodology has been widely accepted and studied and therefore provides greater confidence. Capturing the population-level value considered in the TED modelling approach through the transmission, diversity and enablement components of the STEDI framework, enumerates the full economic value of an antimicrobial to the healthcare system. The capability to incorporate the dynamics of resistance within a disease transmission model enables the estimation of both patient- and population-level benefits unique to antimicrobials that conventional static models fail to capture. This estimation of value is vital to support policy decisions, including the design of incentives to support research and development of new antimicrobials. In 2022, the United Kingdom tested an innovative subscription-style reimbursement model to incentivise investment in new antimicrobials. As part of this pilot, two antimicrobials were assessed considering STEDI value in its HTA approach [6]. Following this pilot, the estimation of STEDI value in terms of QALYs was replaced with a more pragmatic approach where additional value elements were captured via a qualitative point-based scoring system [6]. However, the quantification of value in the pilot scheme played a significant role in ensuring the subscription programme acts as an effective incentive, reflecting the value of the products developed, and is affordable to the National Health Service. In Spain, this conversation is timely as the 2025–2027 Spanish National Action Plan against AMR (PRAN) [7] recognises the need to consider the broader value of antimicrobials; however, it is not yet clear how this will be integrated into policy and reimbursement decisions.

As with most modelling approaches, the TED model also has some limitations. First, as the approach demonstrated in the TED model goes beyond the current method-centric approach of most HTA bodies, HTA bodies may need to develop expertise or collaborate with government agencies and research organisations that already have such experience, for example, the Joint Committee on Vaccination and Immunisation in the UK or the World Health Organization globally. Second, this type of model requires robust epidemiological parameterisation. Limited publicly available surveillance data, particularly for emerging resistance strains, meant that the current example relied on unpublished data and expert opinion, introducing uncertainty. Likewise, transmission models frequently require the estimation of transmission parameters by empirical calibration, and it is often inappropriate to perform probabilistic sensitivity analysis, which is usually called for in HTA methods [8].

An approach that models the dynamics of AMR, such as the STEDI (here TED elements), may include additional uncertainty due to data limitations and require additional expertise to appraise. However, quantifying the additional value of novel antimicrobials to society should be the goal of HTAs whose raison d’être is to maximise societal health benefits within given constraints. Continued methodological research, including value-of-information analyses to prioritise data collection, and improvements to surveillance systems should be prioritised. HTA bodies could play an important role in combatting the ever-increasing threat of AMR by adapting assessment frameworks to quantify the full value that antimicrobials provide to healthcare systems, within the context of limited data. Such changes may establish a framework for dialogue to develop proposals aimed at encouraging the much needed investment in the research and development of new antimicrobials by sending clear signals that companies will be appropriately compensated for the broad value of novel antimicrobial products brought to market.

In summary, the selection of the modelling framework substantially influences the estimated health and economic value of ATM-AVI in Spain. While both the conventional CEM and TED models support the cost-effectiveness of ATM-AVI and represent methodologically valid approaches, they provide different information to decision makers with different limitations. The conventional CEM provides a conservative, minimum-estimate baseline that satisfies current HTA requirements. The TED model incorporates dynamic transmission and resistance elements, enabling quantification of both patient-level and population-level benefits, including reductions in AMR and infection transmission, to inform broader policy and incentive decisions, at the cost of increased complexity and uncertainty. These results highlight the importance of continuing to develop advanced modelling approaches for HTA for antimicrobials and highlight the need for policymakers to integrate broader value components when assessing novel agents.
