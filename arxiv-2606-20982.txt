. 1.
Introduction

A primary question in arithmetic dynamics concerns pre-periodic points of a polynomial map
f:\mathbb{Q}\to\mathbb{Q}
.
Let
f^{(n)}
denote the
n
-th iterate of
f
under composition.
We say that a point
p
is
periodic of order n
if
f^{(n)}(p)=p
and if
f^{(i)}(p)\neq p
for
0<i<n
.
A point is
pre-periodic
if there exists a non-negative integer
m
such that the point
f^{(m)}(p)
is periodic.

It was conjectured in
[
4
]
that if
f
is quadratic, then it has no rational periodic points of order
n>3
.
This is also known as Poonen’s conjecture, due to the progress made in
[
9
]
.
There is evidence for the
6
-cycle case in
[
4
]
.
However, it was also shown in
[
2
]
that if, instead,
f:\mathbb{P}^{1}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
is a degree two rational map, there are several infinite families of maps and rational periodic points of order
6
.

The collection of rational maps and points
(f,p_{1},...,p_{n})
that exhibit a prescribed dynamic structure naturally forms a moduli space.
The study of these moduli spaces is motivated by the Uniform Boundedness Conjecture posed by Morton and Silverman
[
8
]
.
The conjecture asserts that for every
D\geq 1,N\geq 1
, and
d\geq 2
, there is a constant
C(D,N,d)
such that for all number fields
K/\mathbb{Q}
, satisfying
[K:\mathbb{Q}]\leq D
and all endomorphisms of
\mathbb{P}^{N}(K)
of degree
d
, the number of pre-periodic points of
f
is bounded above by a constant,
C(D,N,d)
.
Even in the simplest case,
(D,N,d)=(1,1,2)
, the answer is not known.

Although this conjecture is rooted in number theory and arithmetic dynamics, we can understand it from the perspective of geometry.
These moduli spaces, denoted as
\mathcal{M}_{d}^{N}[\mathcal{P}]
where
d
is the degree of the rational maps
f
,
N
is the dimension of the projective space, and
\mathcal{P}
is the prescribed dynamic structure, which were constructed in
[
3
]
, naturally arise from this problem.

Silverman and Doyle’s conjecture
[
3
, Conjecture 10.6]
asserts that if
N\geq 1,d\geq 1
, then there is a constant
C(N,d)
such that for all unweighted pre-periodic portraits
\mathcal{P}
with more points than
C(N,d)
and
\mathcal{M}_{d}^{N}[\mathcal{P}]\neq\emptyset
, the variety
\mathcal{M}_{d}^{N}[\mathcal{P}]\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{C}
is an irreducible variety of general type.
The moduli space structure identifies rational tuples on
\mathcal{M}_{d}^{N}[\mathcal{P}]
with rational points exhibiting the portrait structure.
Since varieties of general type are conjectured to have sparse rational points, a general type moduli space would indicate that the rational points exhibiting the portrait structure are not Zariski dense.

Suppose that
\mathcal{P}
is a prescribed pre-periodic structure on six points and
f
is a degree two rational map on
\mathbb{P}^{1}
over
\mathbb{Q}
.
Then the moduli space of degree two rational maps that exhibit the prescribed structure can be realized as a surface in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
.
Blanc, Canci, and Elkies showed in
[
2
]
that if
\mathcal{P}_{6}
is a
6
-cycle,
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{6}]
is a surface of general type.
They also showed that if
\mathcal{P}_{5}
is a
5
-cycle, then
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{5}]
is a rational surface.

In this article, we study the case where
\mathcal{P}
has at most
6
points and decomposes as a union of cycles and
f
is a rational map of degree two on
\mathbb{P}^{1}
.
The remaining cases where
\mathcal{P}
is a cycle are investigated in
[
2
]
.
Milnor proved in
[
7
]
that the moduli space
\mathrm{Rat}_{2}^{1}\mathbin{/\!/}\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{P}^{1})\cong\mathbb{C}^{2}
where the coordinates are given by the symmetric polynomials on the multipliers of the fixed points of
f
.
Silverman later showed that
\mathrm{Rat}_{d}^{1}\mathbin{/\!/}\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{P}^{1})
exists as a geometric quotient over
\mathbb{Z}
, which uses the coefficients of
f
for the coordinates
[
10
]
.
It was shown that
[
2
,
6
]
that the surfaces
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{n}]
are geometrically irreducible for
n>1
and that they are all rational for
1\leq n\leq 5
.
The six point case is the first instance that the moduli spaces are not rational.
However, six points is not enough to guarantee that the moduli space is general type; there are many six-point portraits for which the moduli spaces yield other values for the Kodaira dimension.
If the Silverman and Doyle conjecture is true, we must consider more points.

If
n
is a positive integer, then each partition
n=i_{1}+\cdots i_{r}
determines a pre-periodic structure
\mathcal{P}_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{r}}
on
n
points, which is the union of
i_{1},...,i_{r}
-cycles.
Note that every pre-periodic structure on
n
points arises from a partition of
n
.
The strategy to classify these surfaces is to embed each one as a surface in
\mathbb{A}^{3}
.
From there, we either explicitly resolve the singularities, use sub-pre-periodic structures to express it as a covering space, or embed it into the space of six ordered points on
\mathbb{P}^{1}
.
We will not be focusing on the
3,4,5,6
-cycle, as those were dealt with in
[
2
]
.
In section 3 we show that all pre-periodic structure on
3,4,5
points yield moduli spaces which are rational surfaces or empty.
In section 4, we construct different models for the moduli spaces that we will use to classify spaces in the
n=6
case.
In section 5, we prove the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{5,1},\mathcal{P}_{4,1,1},\mathcal{P}_{4,2},\mathcal{P}_{3,3},\mathcal{P}_{3,2,1},\mathcal{P}_{3,1,1,1}
be pre-periodic portraits on six points and let
\kappa
denote the Kodaira dimension.
Then,

•

\kappa=-\infty
for
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{3,1,1,1}]
,

•

\kappa=0
for
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{4,2}]
,

•

\kappa=1
for
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{3,2,1}],\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{3,3}],\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{4,1,1}]
,

•

\kappa=2
for
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{5,1}]
.

We use this and the results of section 5 to show:

Corollary 1.2
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{P}_{6}
or
\mathcal{P}_{5,1}
are subportraits of
\mathcal{P}
.
If
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}]\neq\emptyset
, then it is a surface of general type.

Silverman and Doyle conjecture that there exists an
N
such that for every portrait with more than
N
points,
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}]
is either empty or general type.
Theorem 1.1 shows that
n=6
is not sufficient.
If we assume that the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{k}]
is general type for all sufficiently large
k
, then we have the following:

Corollary 1.3
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{k}]
is general type for all
k\geq 6
.
Let
\mathcal{P}
be a pre-periodic portrait with
N
points where
N\geq 59
.
Then,
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}]
is general type.

2.
Preliminaries

We make precise some definitions from the introduction and work over
\mathbb{C}
.

Definition 2.1
.

An
unweighted pre-periodic portrait
is a pair

\mathcal{P}=(\mathcal{V},\Psi)

such that:

•

\mathcal{V}
is a finite set

•

\Psi:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}
is a function.

An
(unweighted pre-periodic) subportrait
of
\mathcal{P}
is a portrait
(\mathcal{W},\widehat{\Psi})
such that
\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
and
\Psi|_{\mathcal{W}}=\widehat{\Psi}
.
Since we are only considering unweighted pre-periodic portraits, we will refer to them as portraits.

We now describe the structure of these moduli spaces.
Let
\mathrm{Rat}_{d}^{1}
denote the variety that parametrizes the endomorphisms of degree
d
on
\mathbb{P}^{1}
.
Each endomorphism of
\mathbb{P}^{1}
can be expressed as
f([U:V])=[f_{1}([U:V]):f_{2}([U:V])]
, where,

\displaystyle f_{1}([U:V])

\displaystyle=a_{0}U^{d}+a_{1}U^{d-1}V+\cdots+a_{d-1}UV^{d-1}+a_{d}V^{d},

\displaystyle f_{2}([U:V])

\displaystyle=a_{d+1}U^{d}+a_{d+2}U^{d-1}V+\cdots+a_{2d}UV^{d-1}+a_{2d+1}V^{d}.

Taking the ordering
[a_{0}:\cdots:a_{2d+1}]
, we see that
\mathrm{Rat}_{d}^{1}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2d+1}
.
It is an open subvariety of dimension
2d+1
, given by
\mathrm{Res}(f)\neq 0
, which is a polynomial in the coefficients,
[
5
]
.
These maps behave well under conjugation.
That is, given
\varphi\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{P}^{1})
, the map
\varphi^{-1}f\varphi
has the same degree and the same preperiodic structure of
f
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
n\geq 1
be an integer and
\mathcal{P}
be a portrait on
n
points.
Then, the space of degree
d
rational maps on
\mathbb{P}^{1}
that exhibit the portrait structure is given by

\mathrm{End}_{d}^{1}[\mathcal{P}]=\left\{(f,p_{1},\dots p_{n})\mid f(p_{i})=p_{\Psi(i)}\text{ and }p_{i}\neq p_{j}\text{ for }i\neq j\right\}\subset\mathrm{Rat}_{d}^{1}\times(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{n}

The action by
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{P}^{1})
on
\mathrm{End}_{d}^{1}[\mathcal{P}]
is given by

\varphi\ast(f,p_{1},...,p_{n})=(\varphi^{-1}f\varphi,\varphi^{-1}(p_{1}),...,\varphi^{-1}(p_{n}))

and the moduli space of degree
d
rational maps on
\mathbb{P}^{1}
is given by
\mathcal{M}_{d}^{1}[\mathcal{P}]:=\mathrm{End}_{d}^{1}[\mathcal{P}]\mathbin{/\!/}\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{P}^{1})
and exists as a geometric quotient scheme
[
3
]
.

We will be focusing on degree two rational maps so we write
[a_{0}:a_{1}:a_{2}:a_{3}:a_{4}:a_{5}]\in\mathbb{P}^{5}
for the map
f([U:V])=[a_{0}U^{2}+a_{1}UV+a_{2}V^{2}:a_{3}U^{2}+a_{4}UV+a_{5}V^{2}]
.
The open subvariety,
\mathrm{Rat}_{2}^{1}\subset\mathbb{P}^{5}
, is given by

0\neq a_{2}^{2}a_{3}^{2}+a_{0}^{2}a_{5}^{2}-2a_{0}a_{2}a_{3}a_{5}-a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}a_{4}-a_{0}a_{1}a_{4}a_{5}+a_{0}a_{2}a_{4}^{2}+a_{1}^{2}a_{3}a_{5}.

We make a convention about the ordering of a tuple
(f,p_{1},...,p_{i+j})\in\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{i,j}]
.
The points
p_{1},...,p_{i}
form the
i
-cycle and
p_{i+1},...,p_{i+j}
form the
j
-cycle.
We follow a similar convention for portraits with more indices.
We also make a note of how many cycles a degree two map
f:\mathbb{P}^{1}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
has.
We write
f^{(n)}([U:V])=[f_{1}^{(n)}([U:V]):f_{2}^{(n)}([U:V])]
where the polynomials
f_{1}^{(n)},f_{2}^{(n)}
are given by the recursion

\displaystyle f_{1}^{(n)}([U:V])

\displaystyle=f_{1}^{(n-1)}([f_{1}([U:V]):f_{2}([U:V])]),

\displaystyle f_{2}^{(n)}([U:V])

\displaystyle=f_{2}^{(n-1)}([f_{1}([U:V]):f_{2}([U:V])]).

Let
\Phi_{n,f}([U:V])=Vf_{2}^{(n)}([U:V])-Uf_{1}^{(n)}([U:V])
.
If
p=[u:v]\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
is a root of this polynomial, then
f^{(n)}(p)=p
.
It is not necessarily true that
p
is periodic of order
n
.
To fix this issue, we consider the
n
-th dynatomic polynomial.

Definition 2.3
.

The
n
-th dynatomic polynomial for
f
is given by

\Phi_{n,f}^{*}([U:V])=\prod\limits_{k\mid n}\left(\Phi_{k,f}([U:V])\right)^{\mu(n/k)}.

If
p=[u:v]
is a point of period
n
, then it is a root of the
n
-th dynatomic polynomial.
The degree of the
n
-th dynatomic polynomial tells us how many points of period
n
to expect.
Thus,
f
can have at most three fixed points, one
2
-cycle, two
3
-cycles, three
4
-cycles, and six
5
-cycles.
The discriminant of
\Phi_{n,f}^{*}([U:V])
encodes whenever any of the points of period
n
has multiplicity greater than
1
.
In general,
p
is not a root of a
k
-th dynatomic polynomial for
1\leq k<n
.
An example is provided by Manes in
[
6
]
with
[U:V]\longmapsto[-U^{2}+V^{2}:UV]
with
p=[1:0]
.

Since we will often require a specific choice of coordinates on a blow-up, we fix some notation here.
The blow up of
\mathbb{A}^{n}
at the origin,
\Bl_{0}\mathbb{A}^{n}\subset\mathbb{A}^{n}\times\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
, can be expressed with coordinates
(x_{1},...,x_{n})\times[X_{0}:\cdots X_{n-1}]
defined by
x_{i}X_{j}=x_{j}X_{i}
for
1\leq i,j\leq n
.
If we want to blow up a point
y\in A^{n}
that is not the origin, we apply a linear transformation
(x_{1}-y_{1},...,x_{n}-y_{n})
to shift it to the origin, proceed as above, and apply the inverse linear transformation.
If
V\subset\mathbb{A}^{n}
is a singular subvariety, then the charts
X_{i}=1
let us identify the infinitely near singularities.
We will show that the moduli spaces can be realized as singular surfaces in
\mathbb{A}^{3}
in the
6
point case.
These moduli spaces will often require iterated blow ups and we use the product notation to identify singularities.

For example, consider the quintic curve ,
C:\{y^{2}=x^{5}\}\subset\mathbb{A}^{2}
.
It has a singularity at
(0,0)
.
If we blow up the singularity, the strict transform
C^{\prime}
is still singular.
Using
(x,y)\times[U:V]\in\mathbb{A}^{2}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
for our coordinates, then in the
U=1
chart, our strict transform is given by
C^{\prime}:\{V^{2}=x^{3}\}
, a cuspidal cubic, which has a double point at
(x,V)=(0,0)
.
We have that
C
has an infinitely near singularity at
(0,0)\times[1:0]
.
To resolve this singularity, we deal with the affine chart with coordinates
(x,V)\in\mathbb{A}^{2}
and continue the blow up process as before.

3.
Portraits With Less Than Six Points

In this section we show that if
\mathcal{P}
is a portrait with
3,4
, or
5
points,
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}]
is a rational surface.
The proof for each moduli space is done by a case-by-case analysis.
We do, however, take advantage of the action by
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{P}^{1})
and take three of the points to be
[0:1],[1:0]
, and
[1:1]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a portrait on three points.
Then,
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}]
is isomorphic to an open subset of
\mathbb{A}^{2}
.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{P}
is a portrait on
3
points, that is not a cycle, then
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}_{2,1}
or
\mathcal{P}_{1,1,1}
.

(i)

We embed
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{2,1}]
in
\mathrm{Rat}_{2}^{1}\subset\mathbb{P}^{5}
.
It is given by the maps
f
which satisfy

f([0:1])=[1:0],f([1:0])=[0:1]
, and
f([1:1])=[1:1]
.
These equations then show that
f
must be of the form

f([U:V])=[a_{1}UV+a_{2}V^{2}:a_{3}U^{2}+(a_{1}+a_{2}-a_{3})UV].

That is, it is an open subset of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, with coordinates
[a_{1}:a_{2}:a_{3}]
.
The corresponding resultant of
f
is given by
-a_{3}a_{2}(a_{1}-a_{3})(a_{1}+a_{2})
, so that
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{2,1}]
is the complement of three lines in
\mathbb{A}^{2}
.

(ii)

We embed
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{1,1,1}]
in
\mathrm{Rat}_{2}^{1}\subset\mathbb{P}^{5}
.
It is given by the maps
f
which fix
[0:1],[1:0]
, and
[1:1]
.
These equations then show that
f
must be of the form

f([U:V])=[a_{0}U^{2}+a_{1}UV:a_{4}UV+(a_{0}+a_{1}-a_{4})V^{2}].

It is an open subset of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
with coordinates
[a_{0}:a_{1}:a_{4}]
.
The corresponding resultant of
f
is
a_{0}(a_{1}+a_{0})(a_{0}-a_{4})(a_{0}+a_{1}-a_{4})
.
Thus,
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{1,1,1}]
is also the complement of three lines in
\mathbb{A}^{2}
.

∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a portrait on four points.
Then, either
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}]
is empty or isomorphic to an open subset of
\mathbb{A}^{2}
.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{P}
is a portrait on four points, that is not a cycle, then
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}_{3,1},\mathcal{P}_{2,2},\mathcal{P}_{2,1,1},\mathcal{P}_{1,1,1,1}
.

(i)

If
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}_{2,2}
or
\mathcal{P}_{1,1,1,1}
, then
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}]=\emptyset
.

(ii)

We embed
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{3,1}]\subset\mathrm{Rat}_{2}^{1}\times\mathbb{A}^{1}\subset\mathbb{P}^{5}\times\mathbb{A}^{1}
.
It is given by
(f,x)
where

f([0:1])=[1:0],f([1:0])=[x:1],f([x:1])=[0:1]
, and
f([1:1])=[1:1]
.
So, the map
f
corresponds to the endomorphism

f([U:V])=[a_{3}xU^{2}+a_{1}UV-(a_{3}x^{3}+a_{1}x)V^{2}:a_{3}U^{2}-(a_{3}x^{3}+a_{1}x-a_{3}x-a_{1}+a_{3})UV].

It is an open subset of
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{A}^{1}
, with coordinates
([a_{1}:a_{3}],x)
.
The resultant of
f
is given by
a_{3}x(x-1)(x^{2}-x+1)(a_{3}x^{2}+a_{1})(a_{3}x^{2}+a_{3}x+a_{1})(a_{3}x^{2}+a_{3}x+a_{1}-a_{3})
, so that
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{3,1}]
is the complement of six lines in
\mathbb{A}^{2}
, when taking the affine chart
a_{3}=1
.

(iii)

We embed
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{2,1,1}]\subset\mathrm{Rat}_{2}^{1}\times\mathbb{A}^{1}\subset\mathbb{P}^{5}\times\mathbb{A}^{1}
.
It is given by the pair
(f,x)
, where
f([1:1])=[x:1],f([x:1])=[1:1]
and
[0:1],[1:0]
are fixed.
Then, the map
f
can be expressed as

f([U:V])=[a_{0}(x+1)U^{2}+(a_{0}x^{2}+a_{0}x-a_{4}x+a_{0})UV:a_{4}(x+1)UV-x(a_{0}+a_{4})V^{2}].

It is an open subset of
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{A}^{1}
, with coordinates
([a_{0}:a_{4}],x)
.
The resultant of
f
is given by
-a_{0}x(a_{0}+a_{4})(x+1)^{2}(a_{4}x^{2}-a_{0}x)
.
Thus,
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{2,1,1}]
is the complement of four lines in
\mathbb{A}^{2}
, when taking the affine chart
a_{0}=1
.

∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a portrait on five points.
Then, either
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}]
is empty or isomorphic to an open subset of
\mathbb{A}^{2}
.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{P}
is a portrait on five points, that is not a cycle, then
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}_{4,1},\mathcal{P}_{3,2},\mathcal{P}_{3,1,1},\mathcal{P}_{2,1,1,1},\mathcal{P}_{2,2,1},\mathcal{P}_{1,1,1,1,1}
.

(i)

If
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}_{2,2,1}
or
\mathcal{P}_{1,1,1,1,1}
, then
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}]=\emptyset
.

(ii)

We embed
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{4,1}]\subset\mathrm{Rat}_{2}^{1}\times\mathbb{A}^{2}\subset\mathbb{P}^{5}\times\mathbb{A}^{2}
.
The points are given by
(f,x,y)
where
f
cycles
[0:1],[1:0],[x:1],[y:1]
and fixes
[1:1]
.
The map
f
can then be expressed as

f([U:V])=[a_{0}U^{2}+a_{1}UV+a_{2}V^{2}:a_{3}U^{2}+a_{4}UV]

where

\displaystyle a_{0}

\displaystyle=x(xy^{2}-xy+x-y),

\displaystyle a_{1}

\displaystyle=-x(xy^{3}+x^{2}-2xy-y^{2}+y),

\displaystyle a_{2}

\displaystyle=xy(xy^{2}+x^{2}-3xy+y),

\displaystyle a_{3}

\displaystyle=xy^{2}-xy+x-y,

\displaystyle a_{4}

\displaystyle=x^{3}y-2x^{2}y^{2}-x^{3}+x^{2}y+xy^{2}+x^{2}-xy-x-y,

and the resultant of
f
is
x^{2}y(x-y)(x-2y)(x-1)(y-1)(xy-x+1)(x^{2}-xy-x+1)(xy^{2}-xy+x-y)(xy^{2}+x^{2}-3xy+y)
so that it is isomorphic to an open affine subset of
\mathbb{A}^{2}
.

(iii)

We embed
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{3,2}]\subset\mathrm{Rat}_{2}^{1}\times\mathbb{A}^{2}\subset\mathbb{P}^{5}\times\mathbb{A}^{2}
.
The points are given by
(f,x,y)
where
f
cycles
[0:1],[1:0],[1:1]
and swaps
[x:1],[y:1]
.
Then, the map
f
can be expressed as

f([U:V])=[a_{0}U^{2}+a_{1}UV+a_{2}V^{2}:a_{0}U^{2}+a_{4}UV]

where

\displaystyle a_{0}

\displaystyle=xy(x+y+1),

\displaystyle a_{1}

\displaystyle=-xy(xy+x+y+1),

\displaystyle a_{2}

\displaystyle=x^{2}y^{2},

\displaystyle a_{4}

\displaystyle=-(x^{2}y^{3}+x^{2}y^{2}+xy^{2}+xy+x+y+1),

(iv)

We embed

\displaystyle a_{0}

\displaystyle=x^{2}y^{2}-x^{2}y+x^{2}-xy^{2}-xy+y^{2},

\displaystyle a_{1}

\displaystyle=x^{3}y-x^{3}-x^{2}y^{3}+xy^{3}+xy-y^{2},

\displaystyle a_{4}

\displaystyle=x^{3}y-x^{2}y^{2}-x^{2}+x+y^{3}-y^{2},

\displaystyle a_{5}

\displaystyle=-xy(x^{2}-xy+y^{2}-x-y+1),

.

(v)

We embed
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{2,1,1,1}]\subset\mathrm{Rat}_{2}^{1}\times\mathbb{A}^{2}\subset\mathbb{P}^{5}\times\mathbb{A}^{2}
.

∎

Thus, we have shown that for all portraits on

.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a portrait on

Then, either

4.
Different Models of the Moduli Spaces

We now consider the case when
\mathcal{P}
is a portrait on
6
points.
In order to classify these moduli spaces, we take advantage of different models.
For six points, there are a total of eleven portraits.
Recall that we are not considering the case where
\mathcal{P}
is a cycle.
We classify the remaining ten moduli spaces by first showing that four of them yield empty moduli spaces.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be one of the following portraits,
\mathcal{P}_{2,2,2},\mathcal{P}_{2,2,1,1},\mathcal{P}_{2,1,1,1,1},\mathcal{P}_{1,1,1,1,1,1}
.
Then
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}]=\emptyset
.

Proof.

We know that there are no degree two rational maps with more than three fixed points or more than one
2
-cycle.
Therefore,
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}]=\emptyset
.
∎

Before we construct a model of these moduli spaces, we establish some notation.
Each moduli space,
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}]
, must satisfy open conditions, namely, all the points must be distinct and
\mathrm{Res}(f)\neq 0
.
Let
S
be the closure of
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}]
in
\mathbb{A}^{2}
for the
3,4,5
point case.
The indices of the surface

correspond to which portrait we are considering and use a bar to denote the projective closure.
For example, we write
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{4,1}]\subset S_{4,1}=\mathbb{A}^{2}
and
\bar{S}_{4,1}=\mathbb{P}^{2}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}_{5,1},\mathcal{P}_{4,2},\mathcal{P}_{4,1,1},\mathcal{P}_{3,3},\mathcal{P}_{3,2,1},\mathcal{P}_{3,1,1,1}
.
Then, each
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}]
is isomorphic to an open subset of a hypersurface in
\mathbb{A}^{3}
.

Proof.

Recall that after the
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{P}^{1})
action, the points in
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}]
are given by
(f,[0:1],[1:0],[1:1],[x:1],[y:1],[z:1])
with the condition that
f(p_{i})=p_{\Psi(i)}
.
This condition is not linear in the points but is linear in the coefficients of
f
.

We build the matrix equation
M\vec{a}=0
where
\vec{a}=[a_{0}:a_{1}:a_{2}:a_{3}:a_{4}:a_{5}]^{T}
,
a_{i}
are the coefficients of
f
from definition 2.2, and
M\vec{a}
is derived from
f(p_{i})=p_{\Psi(i)}
.
To illustrate, the following matrix equation is for
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{5,1}]

\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0&0&0&-1\\
1&0&0&-1&0&0\\
1&1&1&-x&-x&-x\\
x^{2}&x&1&-x^{2}y&-xy&-y\\
y^{2}&y&1&0&0&0\\
z^{2}&z&1&-z^{3}&-z^{2}&-z\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a_{0}\\
a_{1}\\
a_{2}\\
a_{3}\\
a_{4}\\
a_{5}\\
\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
0\\
0\\
0\\
0\\
\end{pmatrix}.

If this matrix is full rank, then we only have the trivial solution,
\vec{a}=\vec{0}
.
The zeros of
\det{(M)}
determine when the matrix drops in rank, which also defines a sextic surface in
\mathbb{A}^{3}
.
For each portrait
\mathcal{P}
, let
\mathcal{S}
be the corresponding (possibly reducible) closed surface in
\mathbb{A}^{3}
.

Any tuple
(f,[0:1],[1:0],[1:1],[x:1],[y:1],[z:1])\in\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}]
will always satisfy the determinant equation.
However, it is not necessarily true that every point on
\mathcal{S}
corresponds to a tuple in
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}]
.
We require that none of the points in the tuple are the same; this guarantees that the points in the cycle do not collide.
We also require that the resultant of
f
be nonzero so that the degree of
f
is still two, where the coefficients of
f
are determined by
x,y,z
.
These are open conditions in
\mathbb{A}^{3}
, so that
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}]
is an open subset
\mathcal{S}
.
Following the convention above, let
S
be the closure of
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}]
in
\mathbb{A}^{3}
, which is an irreducible component of
\mathcal{S}
.
This is equivalent to removing the planar condition(s) from
\mathcal{S}
.
Following the procedure, we have that

•

\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{5,1}],\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{3,3}]
are isomorphic to open subsets of a sextic surface in
\mathbb{A}^{3}
.

•

\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{4,2}],\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{4,1,1}],\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{3,2,1}]
are isomorphic to open subsets of a quintic surface in
\mathbb{A}^{3}
.

•

\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{3,1,1,1}]
is isomorphic to an open subset of a cubic surface in
\mathbb{A}^{3}
.

∎

This is not the only model for these moduli spaces.
We can also exhibit them as covering spaces via projection maps.
The projection maps are useful because they arise naturally from subportraits.
It is always possible to remove a subportrait condition; it is possible to recover some of the information after removing a subportrait condition.
That is, there is an explicit description to find the points in the fiber.
The fibers of the projection map depends heavily on how many distinct fixed points/cycles a degree two rational map can have.

Lemma 4.3
.

Consider the portrait
\mathcal{P}_{n,1}
, where
n\geq 5
.
Then, there is a map from

, which is generically
3
-to-

are defined by the cycle conditions shown in
[
2
]
, and the branch locus is given by the discriminant of

Proof.

Suppose that

By
[
2
]
, we have that
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{n}]\subset\mathbb{A}^{n-3}

S_{n}
.
We also have that the fixed point equation defines a hypersurface,
H\subset\mathbb{A}^{n-2}
.
Then, the surface
S_{n,1}=H\cap S_{n}\subset\mathbb{A}^{n-3}
, where we are considering
S_{n}
in
\mathbb{A}^{n-2}
via
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-3})\hookrightarrow(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-3},0)

Let
(f,[0:1],[1:0],[1:1],p_{4},\dots,p_{n},\tilde{p})\in\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{n,1}]

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{n,1}]

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{n}]

\displaystyle(f,[0:1],[1:0],[1:1],p_{4},\dots,p_{n},\tilde{p})

\displaystyle\longmapsto(f,[0:1],[1:0],[1:1],p_{4},\dots,p_{n})

.

This projection map is ramified along a curve in
\mathbb{A}^{n-2}
and has a corresponding branch locus in
\mathbb{A}^{n-3}
.
Since any degree two rational map has three fixed points, counting multiplicity, we have that this is generically a
3

whose fibers have less than three elements.

are given in terms of

∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Consider the portrait
\mathcal{P}_{n,m}
, with

and

.
Then, there is a natural projection map

\Phi_{n,f}^{*}([U:1])
.

Proof.

We follow a similar format but now, we must keep track of how many distinct
m
-cycles a rational map of degree two can have.
Since
n\geq 5
, we can solve for the coefficients of
f
in terms of the
n
-cycle.
Thus,
S_{n}\subset\mathbb{A}^{n-3}
.
We consider
S_{n}\subset\mathbb{A}^{n+m-3}
via the standard inclusion.
Then, each condition from the
m
-cycle defines a hypersurface.
Let
H_{i}
be the hypersurface derived from
f(p_{i})=p_{\Psi(i)}
for
n<i\leq n+m
.
Then, we have
m

\mathbb{A}^{n+m-3}
.
Intersecting
m

, we have the surface

.

Then, the map

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{n,m}]

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{n}]

\displaystyle(f,[0:1],[1:0],[1:1],p_{4},\dots,p_{n+m})

\displaystyle\longmapsto(f,[0:1],[1:0],[1:1],p_{4},\dots,p_{n})

is the restriction of the projection map

(x_{1},\dots,x_{n+m-3})\longmapsto(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-3})

In the fixed point case, we had three possible choices because there were three solutions, counting multiplicity, to the fixed point equation.
In the
m
-cycle case, it is possible to have more than one
m
-cycle.
If
f
only has one
m
-cycle, then up to reordering, we have
m
different elements in the fiber.
If
f
has
k

m
-cycles, then we have

This map is ramified when the cycle degenerates.
The discriminant of
\Phi_{m,f}^{*}([U:1])
identifies when the solutions to

collide.
It also defines a hypersurface in
\mathbb{A}^{n-3}

Thus, intersecting the hypersurface defined by the discriminant of
\Phi_{n,f}^{*}([U:1])

∎

5.
Classifying the Surfaces

We now start to classify the surfaces for portraits with six points.
Using the tools of birational geometry, we can resolve the singularities if we realize it as a surface in
\mathbb{A}^{3}
, use the projection maps and the branch loci, or find a new model in the space of six ordered points,

Lemma 5.1
.

The surface
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{3,1,1,1}]
is rational.
Further,
S_{3,1,1,1}
is a degree three weak del Pezzo surface.

Proof.

We use the model for this surface in
\mathbb{A}^{3}
.
The surface

Taking the projective closure, with coordinates

\overline{S}_{3,1,1,1}
only has the following double points

\displaystyle p_{1}

\displaystyle=[1:0:0:0],

\displaystyle p_{2}

\displaystyle=[0:1:0:0],

\displaystyle p_{3}

\displaystyle=[0:0:1:0].

Denoting by

We see that

\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{3,1,1,1}]

-\infty

∎

Lemma 5.2

\displaystyle=(4\widetilde{H}^{2}+E_{8}^{2}+E_{9}^{2}+E_{10}^{2}+E_{11}^{2}+E_{12}^{2})(6\widetilde{H}-2E_{8}-2E_{9}-2E_{10}-2E_{11}-2E_{12})

\displaystyle=24\widetilde{H}^{3}-2E_{8}^{3}-2E_{9}^{3}-2E_{10}^{3}-2E_{11}^{3}-2E_{12}^{3}

\displaystyle=14.

Note that
K_{\overline{S}_{5,1}}^{\prime}
is nef as it is base-point free.
Hence,
\overline{S}_{5,1}^{\prime}
is general type and
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{5,1}]
has Kodaira dimension
2
.
∎

We now have that the following is proved.

.

-\infty

•

\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{4,2}]
has Kodaira dimension
0

•

\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{3,2,1}],\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{3,3}]
, and
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{4,1,1}]
have Kodaira dimension
1

\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{5,1}]

.

Corollary 5.9
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a portrait.
Suppose that
\mathcal{P}_{6}
or
\mathcal{P}_{5,1}
are subportraits.
If
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}]\neq\emptyset
, then it is a surface of general type.

Using Riemann-Hurwitz, we have that the canonical divisor is still the sum of a big and effective divisor.

Corollary 5.10
.

Suppose that the surface
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{k}]
is general type for
k\geq 6
.
Let
\mathcal{P}
be a pre-periodic portrait with
N
points where
N\geq 59
.
Then, the surface
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}]
is general type.

Proof.

Note that the only partitions of
N\geq 59
that we consider have a bound on the number of
1,2,3,4,5
that can appear.
Any degree two rational map on
\mathbb{P}^{1}
has at most three fixed points, one
2
-cycle, two
3
-cycles, three
4
-cycles, and six
5
-cycles.
Thus, any partition of
N
that yields a nonempty moduli space is guaranteed to have
k\geq 6
appear in its partition.
Hence, there is a map,
p:\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}]\to\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{k}]
for
k\geq 6
.
Thus, the canonical divisor of
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{N}]
,
p^{*}(K_{\mathcal{M}_{2}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{k}]})+\mathcal{R}
, is the sum of a big and effective divisor, so that the moduli space is general type.
