ABSTRACT
A common method of determining elastic material properties is utilizing an ultrasonic pulse receiver.
This is a non-destructive test (NDT) causing no plastic deformation of the material. It requires a
pulse generator, oscilloscope, and two transducers to measure the sound velocity of a material. Both
longitudinal and shear sound velocities may be obtained by utilizing the appropriate transducer.
Acoustic nondestructive testing methods are often used in manufacturing certification processes
and for material defect detection in industry. These methods can also be utilized to directly measure
the elastic moduli of a material without damaging the material. Overall, it is a low-cost experiment
with minimal preparation, and is easy to use.

1. INTRODUCTION

A common method of determining elastic material properties is utilizing an ultrasonic pulse receiver.
This is a non-destructive test (NDT) causing no plastic deformation of the material. It requires a
pulse generator, oscilloscope, and two transducers to measure the sound velocity of a material. Both
longitudinal and shear sound velocities may be obtained by utilizing the appropriate transducer.
The pulse generator generates a square wave electric pulse which excites the transducer, creating an
ultrasonic wave in the sample material. The ultrasonic wave is either reflected echoing back to the
transducer (pulse-echo configuration) figure 1-1, or a second transducer is placed on the opposing
side of the sample to record the ultrasonic wave (through-transmission method) figure 1-2. [18]
The through-transmission method was chosen for this set of experiments.By placing the transducers
flush against each other with no sample, just some couplant gel, in between the transducers, the
reference offset may be measured. Then the ultrasonic wave is measured by the transducer after
traveling through the sample. By subtracting the reference offset, the travel duration of the ultrasonic
wave may be determined by matching distinct pulse characteristics or determining a root mean
square difference between the measured pulse and an artificially generated pulse profile. This
duration divided by the thickness of the sample results in the sound velocity of the material. Both
longitudinal sound velocity and shear sound velocity were determined for each sample material.
Combining these values with the measured density of the sample materials, the elastic moduli can
be calculated according to the following equations.

2. BACKGROUND

The speed of sound is defined as the rate at which vibrational waves propagate through a medium.
While the term "speed of sound" is often associated with the propagation of sound waves in air,
perceived by human ears as audible sound, it is important to recognize that sound also travels
through liquids, solids, and complex systems such as granular materials. In solids, sound can
propagate in various modes, including compressive (longitudinal) waves and shear (transverse)
waves, each characterized by distinct motion relative to the direction of wave propagation.

2.1. Compression Waves

Compression waves, commonly referred to as longitudinal waves, consist of alternating compressive
and rarefactive (decompressive) phases that travel parallel to the direction of loading. These waves
can manifest as either planar or spherical waves, depending on the nature of the source. In the
context of this paper, we will focus on planar, longitudinal waves in solids. To visualize this
phenomenon, consider an elastic material modeled as a cubic array of spheres interconnected by
springs. When a force is applied normally, such as striking the face of the cube with a hand,
the compressive wave travels through the medium as the springs are sequentially compressed in
a plane. This compression propagates from the plane of impact to the opposite side of the cube.
Upon reaching the far side, the springs decompress, returning to their original lengths, thereby
completing the wave cycle.

2.2. Shear Waves

Shear waves, or transverse waves, propagate through a material via a sideways shearing motion.
The transfer of energy occurs as the material’s atomic structure responds to the applied shear
force. Continuing with the cubic model of balls and springs, imagine striking the edge of the
cube downward rather than applying force normally. In this scenario, the first plane of spheres
moves downward, creating tension in the springs connecting it to the adjacent plane of spheres.
As the downward force is transmitted to the subsequent planes, the initial planes return to their
original positions. The speed at which shear waves travel through a material is influenced by the
material’s shear modulus and density, which govern its ability to resist deformation under shear
stress. It should be noted that shear waves are polarized therefore require the transducers to be
aligned properly to accurately measure the arrival time of the wave.

 4. ANALYSIS THEORY

 4.1. Analysis theory overview

 The two most important and complex functions of pulse receiver NDT sound speed measurements,
 the pulse finder and the estimated pulse generator, attempt to answer the question of "when did
 the wave arrive?" While it may seem a simple solution, the methods of answering this question
 are debated in the NDT community. One method is simply using the maximum or minimum of
 the pulse as the time of arrival. This method may cause discrepancies in the samples of varying
 thickness due to pulses deteriorating over travel distances, and as a result widening in duration.
 Another issue arises if the pulse is overly dampened resulting in flat topped, or clipped, peaks and
 valleys. Dampening of the signal should only be implemented to reduce the noise of the signal,
 but not to the point of deteriorating the sharpness and clarity of the pulse. Another method of
 determining the time of arrival of the pulse is by simply measuring where the signal deviates
 from the baseline by a certain threshold. This method is highly dependent upon the noise of the
 signal therefore sensitive to the coupling between the transducers and the sample. As a result,
 the uncertainty increases dramatically due to user errors. An additional method of determining
 time of arrival of the pulse is by marking the point at which the pulse crosses the x-axis after
 achieving a maximum or a minimum. This method works well for both longitudinal and shear
 wave measurements as well as pulse-echo mode and through transmission mode, since the pulses
 for each may vary in profile. The pulse may increase or decrease at the beginning depending upon
 which mode and transducer are used. A method that is applicable for all pulse profiles and reduces
 sensitivity of coupling between the transducer and sample is the the weighted pulse finding method
 described in the subsequent sections of this chapter.

5. DENSITY

The densities of the samples in this work were measured using a Mettler Toledo Density kit .

 Figure 5-1. Mettler Toledo Density kit.

 𝐴
                                       𝜌=       (𝜌0 − 𝜌 𝐿 ) + 𝜌 𝐿                                    (5.1)
 𝐴−𝐵
Where A is the weight of the sample in air, B is the weight of the sample in the auxiliary liquid, 𝜌
is the density of the sample, 𝜌0 is the density of the air, and 𝜌 𝐿 is the density of the auxiliary liquid.
This method requires knowledge of the temperature of the auxiliary liquid(water), the temperature
of the air, and the pressure of the air. Ideally, two thermometers should be used, one in the air,
one in the water, and a barometer in the laboratory. Common uncertainty values for this method
of determining density are in the range of + − 5 𝑚𝑘𝑔3 [23] This method of determining density comes
from Archimedes’ principle which states, "Any object, totally or partially immersed in a fluid or
liquid, is buoyed up by a force equal to the weight of the fluid displaced by the object." [17] When
using the Mettle Toledo Density kit, it is best practice to use a cylindrical sample with a diameter
of 1.5 inches. That way, it fits in the pan and basket well while exerting the greatest force upon the
balance, giving a higher tolerance to the density reading.
