Preoperative brain injury and neurodevelopmental outcomes in syndromic and non-syndromic children with complex congenital heart disease: a single-centre cohort study

Chiara Paolin, Alice Pozza, Claudio Ancona, Laura Mezzalira, Paola Cogo, Giovanni Di Salvo, Massimo Padalino, Fabio Scattolin, Vladimiro Vida, Stefano Sartori, Renzo Manara

Abstract

Children with complex congenital heart disease (CHD) are at high risk of brain lesions both before and after cardiac surgery. Preoperative brain injury reflects foetal and perinatal brain development and represents an independent predictor of neurodevelopmental outcome. This study aimed to characterize preoperative brain lesions and their correlation with clinical course and neurodevelopmental outcomes in children with complex CHD. The secondary objective was to compare findings between syndromic and non-syndromic patients, as preoperative brain injury in syndromic children remains underreported. A single-centre cohort study was performed at the University of Padova, Italy, involving children with complex CHD who underwent preoperative brain MRI between January 2020 and October 2024. Patients were categorized based on the presence or absence of a known or suspected genetic syndrome. Neurological and neurodevelopmental evaluations were conducted pre- and postoperatively, and at 6- and 18-months follow-up. Among 54 enrolled patients, 21 (39%) had a suspected or confirmed genetic syndrome. Preoperative brain lesions were present in 24 children (44%), including 16/33 (48%) non-syndromic and 8/21 (38%) syndromic patients (OR 0.65 [0.21–1.94], p = 0.5708). Arterial ischemic stroke was the most common lesion (20%), followed by subdural haemorrhage (11%), cerebral sinovenous thrombosis (9%), and white matter injury (6%). Despite similar lesion rates, syndromic children showed more complicated perinatal histories and greater neurodevelopmental impairment, particularly in motor and language domains. 
Conclusion: Syndromic children with CHD do not have higher rates of preoperative brain injury but remain at greater neurodevelopmental risk. Further studies are warranted to confirm these findings.

Introduction

Congenital heart disease (CHD) is the most common birth defect, affecting 1 in every 8000 children. Over the past years, advances in diagnostic tools and cardiac surgery techniques have enabled more than 97% of children born with CHD to reach adulthood. Besides survival, much interest has been conveyed to functional concerns, as adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes are increasingly recognized in children affected with CHD [–]. Overall, CHD encompasses a broad spectrum of diseases, from simple lesions to more complex defects, the latter requiring open heart surgery within the first year of life and presenting an increased risk of adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes [–].

Across classification of heart defects, isolated or non-syndromic CHD defines lesions in which the heart is the only affected structure, whereas syndromic CHD refers to the presence of additional developmental defects [].

Recent literature has been focusing on the incidence and significance of brain lesions in children with complex CHD, both pre- and postoperatively.

Preoperative brain lesions reflect foetal/perinatal brain growth and susceptibility to damage, and represent an independent risk factor for neurodevelopmental outcome []. White matter injury (WMI), Arterial ischemic stroke (AIS) and microhaemorrhages are the most common lesions in preoperative scans and their presence has been largely described [, –]. However, syndromic children have been excluded from most of the literature assessing neurodevelopmental outcomes in complex CHD.

The primary aim of the current study was to characterize preoperative brain lesions in a cohort of syndromic and non-syndromic children with complex CHD, and to examine their correlation with clinical course and neurodevelopmental outcome. A secondary objective was to compare neurodevelopmental outcomes between syndromic and non-syndromic children.

Material and methods

Study design

This single-centre cohort study was performed at the Department of Women’s and Children’s Health, University of Padova, Italy, between January 2020 and October 2024.

Study population

Patients were considered eligible for the study if they were diagnosed with complex CHD requiring cardiac surgery within the first year of life and underwent a brain MRI study before cardiac surgery. Exclusion criteria were severe hemodynamic instability or severe hepatic or renal insufficiency requiring intensive care before surgery. Patients were subdivided according to presence or absence of known/suspected genetic syndrome (i.e. Di George or Down syndrome). A genetic syndrome was considered suspected in the presence of multiple dysmorphic features in patients with inconclusive or ongoing genetic testing.

Patient characteristics as well as surgical and medical data were extracted from the electronic clinical records of our institution and collected in a Research Electronic Data Capture database. Clinical data extracted from medical records included: prenatal (maternal conditions, prenatal diagnosis), perinatal (type of delivery, APGAR score), demographic (sex, anthropometrics, CHD type), presurgical (time to surgery/MRI, preoperative interventions), surgical (cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) details) and neurological (neurological and neurodevelopmental evaluations) factors.

All children underwent preoperative brain MRI on the same 1.5 T scanner (Philips Achieva, Best, the Netherlands). MRI protocol included T1, T2, diffusion and T2* weighted imaging, phase contrast MR-venography and Time-of Flight (TOF) Magnetic Resonance Angiography.

Lesion type, number and location were recorded on preoperative brain MRIs according to the European Association Brain in Congenital Heart Disease Consortium []. Postoperative brain MRI was performed whenever clinically required and included scans from the above-mentioned protocol.

Clinical, cardiological and neurodevelopmental evaluations were performed before and after surgery and afterwards at 6 and 18 months of age. Cardiological evaluation consisted of comprehensive structural echocardiography, performed by two paediatric cardiologists (A.P., G.DS.) using Vivid E95ultrasound system (General Electric Healthcare, Horten, Norway). Neurodevelopmental evaluations were performed by two paediatric neurologists (C.P. and S.S.) using Hammersmith Neonatal and Infant Neurologic Evaluation and Griffiths III (2nd edition) scales as a reference.

The study was approved by the local hospital Research Board, University of Padova (Protocol ID 3142/AO/14), and parental written informed consent was obtained for included patients. All data were collected while maintaining confidentiality and were anonymized for statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

The continuous variables are displayed as mean ± standard deviation, if normally distributed, or as median and interquartile range (IQR), if not normally distributed. The normality distribution was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The binomial and ordinal qualitative variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. In univariate analyses, chi-square test of proportions for categorical variables was used to assess differences. When expected frequencies were low, Fisher’s exact test was preferred. Univariate analyses were not adjusted for multiple comparisons.

To evaluate the mechanism of missing data across variables, Little’s Missing Completely at Random (MCAR) test was performed. A p-value > 0.05 was considered indicative of data missing completely at random (MCAR). Complete-case analyses were applied for subgroup comparisons.

To account for baseline imbalances between groups (such as intrauterine growth retardation (IUGR), birth weight, and delivery mode), adjusted comparisons for primary neurodevelopmental outcomes at follow-up (language and motor delay) were performed using stratified Mantel–Haenszel tests, reporting common Odds Ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI).

All statistical analyses were performed with SPSS software version 25.0 (International Business Machines Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA).

Brain MRI findings

Preoperative brain MRI findings are reported in Table . Median age at brain MRI was 11 (IQR 6–66) days and surgery was performed 6 (IQR 2–15) days after brain MRI. At least one brain lesion was detected in 24/54 (44%) children, 16/33 non-syndromic and 8/21 syndromic (OR 0.65 [0.21–1.94], p = 0.5708). More than one brain lesion was found in 5 (9%). The most frequent lesion was AIS, seen in 11 (20%) children. All these lesions but one had high DWI signal and low ADC values (one patient had two ischemic lesions, one of them with low DWI signal/high ADC values). The main artery involved was the middle cerebral artery. Cerebral sinovenous thrombosis (CSVT) was diagnosed in 5 (9%) children, WMI in 3 (6%), haemorrhages were intraventricular in 2 (4%), intraparenchymal in 2 (4%) and subdural in 6 (11%). In addition, increased subarachnoid spaces were observed in 11/54 (20%) children.

In our univariate analysis, although a higher proportion of infants with preoperative brain injury were born before 37 weeks compared with those without injury (25% vs. 10%), the difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.2783).

Neurological and neuropsychological outcome

Characteristics of non-syndromic and syndromic children who underwent preoperative and follow-up neurological evaluations are reported in Table .

Preoperative neurological evaluation was available in 44/54 children (81%; 28 males; 28 non-syndromic and 16 syndromic). Axial hypotonia was diagnosed in 12/28 (42%) non-syndromic and 12/16 (75%) syndromic children (OR 4.00 [1.03–15.53], p = 0.0600). Psychomotor delay was evident in 4/16 (25%) syndromic and in 0/33 non-syndromic children (OR 20.52 [1.03–410.70], p = 0.0134). Two non-syndromic children suffered from preoperative seizures: one child in neonatal period and his brain MRI showed two ischemic lesions; one child suffered from acute provoked seizures during a preoperative sepsis episode and his brain MRI showed bilateral transverse CSVT. Both did not develop epilepsy at the following neurological evaluations. One syndromic child suffered from preoperative symptomatic seizures due to cardioembolic stroke. One syndromic child diagnosed with tricuspid atresia showed early signs of severe motor impairment (tetraparesis) on preoperative neurological evaluation, and his brain MRI showed both grade III IVH and CSVT. Genetic testing was inconclusive.

At least one follow-up neurological evaluation was available for 35/54 children (65%; 24 were non-syndromic and 11 syndromic, 31/35 underwent preoperative evaluation).

Ten (42%) non-syndromic and nine (82%) syndromic children showed a language delay (OR 6.30 [1.11–35.67], p = 0.0354). Five (20%) non-syndromic and eight (73%) syndromic showed a motor delay (OR 10.13 [1.94–52.90], p = 0.0069). Four (17%) non-syndromic children showed neuropsychiatric symptoms (e.g. hyperactivity). Five (45%) syndromic children suffered from postoperative seizures: two were diagnosed with focal symptomatic epilepsy after a postoperative ischemic stroke, one has a developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (SALL4 gene mutation) and one child had only one isolated seizure in the postoperative period.

Stratified Mantel–Haenszel analyses confirmed that syndromic status remained significantly associated with both language delay and motor delay at follow-up after adjusting for baseline confounders (gestational age < 37 weeks, IUGR, birth weight < 2500 g, caesarean delivery).

A sensitivity analysis was performed excluding the 7 patients with unconfirmed genetic syndrome (n = 14 confirmed syndromic vs. n = 33 non-syndromic). The analysis yielded consistent results: the prevalence of preoperative brain MRI lesions remained comparable between groups (36% vs 48%; OR 0.59 [0.16–2.18], p = 0.528), whereas confirmed syndromic children maintained significantly higher rates of motor delay (75% vs 20%; OR 12.00 [1.83–78.68], p = 0.009) and language delay (88% vs 42%; OR 9.33 [1.02–85.71], p = 0.041).

No statistically significant association was observed between the presence of preoperative brain MRI lesions and subsequent motor or language delay. Specifically, motor and/or language delay was identified in 7 out of 14 patients (50.0%) with preoperative brain injury compared to 13 out of 21 patients (61.9%) with normal preoperative MRI scans (OR = 0.62, [0.16–2.42], p = 0.501). Subgroup analysis stratified by genetic status further confirmed this lack of association.

Regarding missing data, Little's MCAR test was performed across all clinical variables with missing values (birth head circumference, preoperative neurological evaluation, postoperative MRI and follow-up neurological assessment). The test was consistent with data being missing completely at random (χ2 = 14.82, df = 11, p = 0.191), suggesting that missingness was completely random and is unlikely to have introduced systematic bias.

Discussion

Main findings: syndromic vs non-syndromic cohort

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to compare preoperative brain lesions and neurodevelopmental outcomes between syndromic and non-syndromic children affected by CHD.

Most existing literature has focused on non-syndromic patients, primarily to minimise confounding factors related to underlying genetic conditions. However, we believe that including syndromic children is of significant clinical relevance, particularly because clinical and surgical decision-making often occurs before genetic test results become available.

Preoperative brain injury is an established independent predictor of adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes, reflecting cerebral vulnerability prior to cardiac surgery. In our cohort, no significant differences were observed between syndromic and non-syndromic patients in the number, type or severity of preoperative brain lesions, including white matter injury, arterial ischemic stroke, haemorrhage and cerebral sinovenous thrombosis. Only one prior study included patients with known or suspected chromosomal disorders and reported minimal association between chromosomal abnormalities and brain MRI findings [].

Nevertheless, our subcohort of syndromic children experienced a more complex perinatal course characterized by lower birth weights, higher rates of IUGR and caesarean delivery. In addition, their clinical spectrum was further compounded by a higher severity of cardiac diagnoses. Notably, a high prevalence of twin pregnancies was observed in our cohort, especially among the syndromic children. While the increased risk of CHD in multiple gestations has been extensively described, to our knowledge no study had previously reported the prevalence of twin pregnancies among syndromic children with CHD []. The overall higher rate of caesarean sections in our cohort of syndromic infants can likely be attributed to the elevated prevalence of prenatal risk factors or to physician/hospital policy for elective surgical delivery in the presence of recognized dysmorphisms, extracardiac anomalies, or severe complex heart lesions on prenatal screening. Moreover, compared to non-syndromic peers, syndromic children showed higher rates of axial hypotonia and psychomotor delay on preoperative neurological evaluations, indicating a global impairment independent of macrostructural MRI lesions. Besides, preoperative axial hypotonia likely reflects a combination of prolonged hospital admission, bedridden status, and reduced environmental stimulation typical of infants awaiting cardiac surgery, alongside intrinsic genetic vulnerabilities in the syndromic subgroup.

Moreover, during post-surgical follow-up, the syndromic subgroup continued to display higher rates of language delay, motor delay and seizures or epilepsy compared to non-syndromic peers with comparable rates of preoperative brain lesions. While neurodevelopmental outcomes in CHD children are inherently multifactorial, reflecting foetal, perinatal, perioperative and surgical variables, this disparity highlights an underlying vulnerability in the syndromic cohort.

Furthermore, we found no direct correlation between preoperative brain lesions and poor neurodevelopmental outcomes, in either the syndromic or non-syndromic subcohort. This result further confirms that neurodevelopmental outcome is multifactorial and may be driven by microstructural or functional network alterations rather than overt macrostructural brain damage, as supported by recent literature [].

Preoperative brain lesions and risk factors

When considering the whole cohort, preoperative brain MRI revealed at least one structural lesion (WMI, ischemia, haemorrhage, CSVT) in 44% of all children. This prevalence aligns with previously published data, particularly considering that subdural hematomas were included in the total count [, –]. The most frequent lesion in our cohort was AIS, observed in 20% of all children, consistent with previously reported data [, , ]. The main affected artery was the middle cerebral artery, mirroring findings from the European Association Brain in Congenital Heart Disease Consortium, which reported prevalence of middle cerebral artery involvement in preoperative AIS, in contrast to postoperative strokes, which predominantly affect basal ganglia/thalamus region []. CSVT occurred in 9% of our cohort and the transverse sinus was most commonly affected, as previously reported []. Conversely, in our cohort the prevalence of WMI in preoperative MRI was 6%, low if compared to extensively reported data [, ].

Although not statistically significant, a higher prevalence of enlarged subarachnoid spaces was observed in the syndromic population, consistent with previous reports in children with genetic disorders [, ].

Several risk factors for preoperative brain injury detectable on MRI have been identified in the literature, including gestational age, 5-min APGAR score, preoperative cyanosis, specific heart defects (CoA, SVP), BAS, low cardiac output syndrome, altered maternal–fetal environment, male sex, older age at surgery and induced vaginal delivery [, , , ]. In the present study, we explored these variables alongside other potential risk factors. However, no statistically significant association was observed, probably due to low sample size. Nonetheless, a trend towards lower gestational age and lower preoperative peripheral oxygen saturation in children with preoperative brain injury was noted.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, it was conducted at a single tertiary centre, which is a referral institution for patients with complex CHD, therefore findings may not be generalizable to the broader CHD population. The modest sample size restricted our ability to perform subgroup analyses and limited statistical power for certain secondary endpoints.

Furthermore, a potential selection bias exists, as critically ill infants with severe hemodynamic instability or life-threatening conditions were systematically excluded from our analyses. These critically ill infants are potentially at the highest risk for both pre- and postoperative brain injury and adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes. Consequently, our findings should be interpreted with caution when generalized to the broader CHD population.

Unconventionally, premature infants were included in our sample. Although preterm birth is an established independent risk factor for both brain injury and adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes, prematurity was not associated with a higher risk of these conditions in our univariate analyses. Nonetheless, it remains a potential confounder and our findings should be interpreted with caution.

Moreover, we acknowledge methodological limitations regarding the classification of the syndromic group. Diagnostic misclassification could have occurred considering that 7/21 syndromic patients had a suspected, rather than genetically confirmed, syndrome. However, a sensitivity analysis excluding unconfirmed cases yielded consistent results: the prevalence of preoperative brain MRI lesions remained comparable between groups, while confirmed syndromic children maintained significantly higher rates of motor and language delays. Additionally, substantial genetic heterogeneity was present within the syndromic group, encompassing conditions with distinct neurodevelopmental trajectories and disease-specific risks for cognitive, motor, and language impairments. While grouping these different genetic conditions was necessary due to sample size considerations, future multicentre studies with larger cohorts are needed to isolate the relative contributions of specific genetic aetiologies versus structural brain injury on long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes.

Regarding neurodevelopmental assessments, neurologic evaluations were conducted using standardized scales, such as Hammersmith Neonatal and Infant Neurological Examination scales, as a reference, but only qualitative data were collected []. Implementing quantitative developmental scales preoperatively and during follow up would provide a more precise stratification of neurodevelopmental risk and track multi-domain developmental progress more objectively [, ]. Another limitation in assessing the language domain was that the study did not account for whether the participants' families were monolingual or bilingual.

Regarding brain MRI examinations, the study lacks quantitative brain MRI metrics, which have been utilized in recent literature to detect subtle structural alterations []. A limited number of postoperative brain MRI scans were performed, as post-surgical neuroimaging was obtained solely for clinical indications rather than systematically.

Regarding statistical limitations, univariate analyses were not corrected for multiple testing; thus, secondary findings must be interpreted as exploratory. Furthermore, statistical power limitations in this modest cohort precluded multivariable adjusted models, meaning that residual confounding cannot be entirely ruled out, and our results should be validated in larger prospective multicentre cohorts.

Conclusions

Neurodevelopmental outcome is one of the main concerns in children with CHD. Preoperative brain lesions reflect foetal and perinatal brain growth and susceptibility to damage and represent an independent risk factor for neurodevelopmental outcome.

We found a high prevalence of preoperative brain injuries in our cohort of children with CHD, mainly ischemic lesions, as already reported in literature. The key and novel finding of our study is that syndromic children with CHD do not show increased rates of preoperative brain injury compared to non-syndromic children, although they remain a population at higher neurodevelopmental risk. We believe that the present study adds new insights on preoperative risk factors for neurodevelopmental outcome in children with CHD, especially in those with genetic syndromes, that have been so far excluded from most of the literature. Further studies are needed to validate our results.
