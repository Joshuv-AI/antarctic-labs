Objectives and Scope
This project aimed to develop a unified, multi-physics computational framework to study
stellar mergers and their observable consequences. The work focused on connecting binary
interactions, hydrodynamic merger simulations, stellar evolution, nucleosynthesis, and
radiation transport into a single predictive pipeline.

Major Scientific Results
Over the course of the award, we demonstrated that stellar mergers provide a unifying
explanation for a wide range of astrophysical phenomena. Analytical and numerical studies
showed that Betelgeuse can be explained as a post-main-sequence merger product
(Chatzopoulos et al. 2020; Shiber et al. 2024]. Three-dimensional hydrodynamic
simulations using OCTO-TIGER revealed asymmetric mass ejection (~0.6 solar masses] and
angular momentum redistribution, which sustain rapid rotation in post-merger stars. These
simulations were coupled to long-term stellar evolution models using MESA.

In parallel, we modeled white dwarf mergers leading to R Coronae Borealis stars,
demonstrating the dependence of surface abundances on helium-burning shell temperature
and metallicity (Crawford et al. 2020]. This work provided a physical explanation for
observed chemical anomalies.

We also developed SuperLite, an open-source Monte Carlo radiation transport code capable
of producing synthetic spectra for supernovae and other transients (Wagle et al. 2023]. This
enables direct comparison between theoretical models and observations, completing the
end-to-end pipeline from merger physics to observables.

Software and Computational Advances
A major deliverable of this project is the SuperLite radiation transport code, which has been
released publicly at https://github.com/gururajw/superlite. The project leveraged high-
performance computing resources for large-scale 3D hydrodynamic simulations and multi¬
parameter stellar evolution studies.

Workforce Development and Training
This project supported the training of postdoctoral researchers Gururaj A. Wagle and Sagiv
Shiber, and graduate student Bradley Munson. These researchers contributed to
hydrodynamic simulations, stellar evolution modeling, and radiation transport code
development.
