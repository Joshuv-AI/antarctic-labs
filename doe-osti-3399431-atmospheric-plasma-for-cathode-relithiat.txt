 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 The Parties are working together to utilize a one-step atmospheric microplasma reaction for the
 elimination of electrolyte degradation products and the relithiation of end-of-life cathode
 materials. This project aims to streamline the direct recycling process of battery components by
 reducing the required steps and time.

 1

 CRADA benefit to DOE, Participant, and US Taxpayer:

 • Enhances the laboratory’s core competencies

 The CRADA demonstrated the use of an atmospheric microplasma process to recycle end-of-life
 lithium-ion battery cathode materials. These materials contain critical elements, and their
 recovery and reuse reduce U.S. dependence on difficult-to-source and geopolitically constrained
 supply chains.

 The work strengthened DOE laboratory core competencies by validating a novel recycling
 approach that had not previously been demonstrated at this level, thereby reducing technical risk
 and expanding the laboratory’s expertise in advanced battery recycling and materials processing.

 The private-sector participant, Storagenergy Technologies, has expressed interest in licensing the
 technology developed under this CRADA for potential commercialization. If deployed, the
 technology could contribute to domestic manufacturing capacity, improve U.S. competitiveness
 in battery materials, and support the creation of high-skilled technical jobs, providing a direct
 benefit to U.S. taxpayers through improved supply-chain resilience and return on federal
 research investment.

 Summary of Research Results:

 Task 1: Run small (~100’s of mg) amount of aged cathode powder through plasma

 1.1: Determine the change in morphology (scanning electron microscopy (SEM)) and CEI (x-ray
 photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS)) with various plasma conditions.

 1.2: Test relithiation of cathode particles by plasma treating spray relithiated material (x-ray
 diffraction (XRD) and XPS).

 Task 1 Result:

 Task 1 involved increasing the throughput of the plasma system. At the project’s inception, the
 plasma system was able to process 15 mg/hr of end-of-life (EoL) cathode material. By project
 end, the material throughput had increased from 15 mg/hr to 1050 mg/hr continuous throughput.
 This was achieved through the design and fabrication of a custom auger-based powder
 introduction system and a doubling of the number of reaction vessels.

 The non-thermal atmospheric plasma system works by physically sputtering the surface of the
 EoL cathode particles with argon ions (Ar⁺) to remove the cathode electrolyte interphase (CEI)
 (Figure 1a, 1b). The physical removal of the CEI removes challenges associated with chemical
 treatment and allows for incredibly fast processing times, estimated to be less than 250
 milliseconds based on gas flow velocity. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis of the
 chemical speciation of the plasma-processed EoL cathode material (Figure 1c) shows a slight
 compositional change and an overall reduction in the CEI, evidenced by the metal–oxygen bonds

 masked by the CEI before plasma treatment.

 Task 2 Result:

 Task 2 focuses on the scale up of the microplasma system. Scaling up of the system required that
 both the EoL powder introduction system and the microplasma reactor array be doubled in
 volume. The system uses a simple auger to feed EoL powder into the reactors from an argon
 (Ar)-purged hopper. At inception, the system had one auger feed that could feed approximately
 15 mg/hr (Figure 2a). After redesign and fabrication, the system now has a dual auger feed that
 can supply the reactors at a rate of approx. 1500 mg/hr (Figure 2b).

 3

 Reaching the target material throughput also required the partial scale up of the reaction vessels
 themselves. At the beginning of the project, the system contained one reaction vessel capable of
 processing approx. 10–20 mg/hr (Figure 2c). After a complete redesign of the RF matching
 network and a modest electrode redesign, the plasma reactors are now capable of processing
 1050 mg/hr of EoL cathode material (Figure 2d).

 After the completion of the reactor and powder feed scale up, a bulk plasma treatment was run
 on cathode material from a commercial vendor. The treatment included an N-methyl-2-
 pyrrolidone (NMP) soak of shredded cathode electrodes, filtration and separation, and vacuum
 drying of the powders. The powder was then loaded into a hopper containing Ar gas and fed into
 the reactor at approx. 1000 mg/hr. The plasma power was set to 175 W, which corresponds to
 approximately 5 W/cm³ power density. The Ar flow rate was 175 standard cubic centimeters per
 minute (sccm), and the electrode spacing was 30 cm. This plasma-treated material was sent to
 Argonne National Laboratory for pouch cell fabrication on November 24, 2025.

 Task 3: Cathode up cycling of aged cathode powders

 3.1: Combine the existing plasma cathode synthesis at the Contractor’s laboratory by
 cointroduction of aged cathode particles (post CEI removal) with more desirable chemistries.

 Task 3 Results:

 Cathode synthesis using the atmospheric plasma is currently accomplished at NLR by first
 dissolving metal acetate hydrates, lithium hydroxide, and chelating agents into deionized (DI)
 water and vaporizing this solution into small droplets to be introduced into the plasma reactor.
 Depending on the power source and reactor geometry, fully functional cathode particles can be
 formed, or they may require an additional heating (calcination) step. For this project, EoL
 cathode materials were co-introduced into the reaction vessel with the synthesis precursor with
 the intent of creating a core–shell particle. These core–shell particles could possibly increase the
 value-added (upcycle) of the process by creating particles that demonstrate higher storage
 capacity.

 Unfortunately, attempts at adding newly synthesized cathode material to the EoL cathode
 particles were unsuccessful in the machine’s current geometry. While newly synthesized material
 can be created in some geometries, the current geometry requires a 30–60 minute heating step
 that lies outside of the scope of the current project. Attempts to upcycle the EoL material did not
 lead to newly formed cathode material on the EoL cathode particles, but seemingly the formation
 of a nickel metal shell. In the future, reactor geometries that can form finely crystalline nickel
 manganese cobalt (NMC)-like cathode material without the need for additional heating should be
 used in these attempts.

 Task 4: Cathode prep and characterization

 4.1: Determine optimal washing procedure to prepare aged cathodes for plasma treatment at
 Storagenergy’s laboratory.

 4.2: Establish spray coating process for applying relithiating agent onto the surface of aged
 cathodes pre plasma treatment. This process involves mixing the particles with a lithium
 precursor and spraying the mixture in air to dry. It coats the cathode particles with a lithium
 containing precursor that is then plasma treated at the Contractor’s facilities to relithiate the
 particle.

 4.3: Establish spray coating process for applying relithiating agent onto the surface of aged
 cathodes post plasma treatment. Cathodes particles will be plasma treated first to remove CEI
 and then spray coated with lithium precursor for an additional plasma treatment. Compare tasks
    4.2 and 4.3.

 5

 Task 4 Result:

 Task 4 involved the development of a delamination and cathode washing procedure to prepare
 the EoL cathode material for plasma treatment. Several methods of retrieval were tested, and one
 method was ultimately chosen for both the delamination and washing. Delamination and
 retrieval were accomplished by ball milling shredded cathode electrodes in NMP solvent at 500
 rpm at 60 °C overnight. The solid material was then filtered using a 1000 × 1000 µm mesh and
    then a 100 × 100 µm mesh.

 Tasks 4.2 and 4.3 involved the pre-lithiation of the EoL cathode material with the hope that
 lithium deposited on the particles would have enough energy to diffuse into the EoL particles.
 The pre-lithiation was attempted using several methods, with the final process being a rotary
 evaporator (rotovap)-based process. This process has shown promise in its ability to uniformly
 coat cathode particles in a thin film of various chemical makeup. The lithium-coated particles
 were then passed through the plasma reactor and analyzed electrochemically and via XPS.
 Unfortunately, XPS suggests that lithium hydroxide used to pre-lithiate the particles did not fully
 decompose, and lithium did not seem to diffuse into the cathode particles. As above, reactor
 geometries with higher plasma power densities could prove promising, supplying more energy
 and allowing lithium to diffuse partially.

 Task 5: Fabricate coin and pouch cells

 5.1: Fabricate coin and single layer pouch cells to determine electrochemical performance of
 pristine and plasma treated/spray relithiated cathodes.

 Task 5 Results:

 In order to determine the effectiveness of the plasma treatment, half coin cells were fabricated to
 determine changes in resistance and discharge capacity. Coin cells were fabricated with plasma-
 treated and as-received EoL cathode material. Cells were cycled between 2.9–4.3 V at room
 temperature in Gen-2 electrolyte. The overall cell resistance comparison between the plasma-
 treated and the EoL cathode material can be seen in Figure 3a. The substantial reduction in the
 plasma-treated material can be attributed to the removal of at least some of the CEI, as evidenced
 in XPS (Figure 1c). This can partially explain the increase in discharge capacity seen in the
 plasma-treated material. Half coin cells cycled at one-tenth of the C-rate (C/10) show an increase
 from 110 mAh/g to 145 mAh/g for the plasma-treated material. The 145 mAh/g value
 approaches the discharge capacity of other pristine NMC 532 cathodes cycled in this voltage
 range.
