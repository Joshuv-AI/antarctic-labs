ABSTRACT
Requirements specific to hydrogen rail transportation and its unique components are currently not
well represented in safety codes and standards. Notably, one of the most important components of
the system, the hydrogen storage vessel, may be subject to entirely different shock and vibration
conditions compared to hydrogen tanks on road vehicles. This report focuses on how composite
hydrogen tanks are tested for performance and how this may relate to these tanks being used on rail
systems. The shock and vibration testing procedures that are relevant for hydrogen composite tanks
used for rail applications have been identified, as well as some general testing requirements for
hydrogen tanks and composite tanks.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
The safety and performance of onboard hydrogen fuel storage technologies, particularly type IV
composite tanks, have emerged as critical areas of research. Sandia National Laboratories was
tasked with identifying the available testing protocols and requirements for type IV composite tanks
for hydrogen storage in rail applications, with a specific focus on relevant shock and vibration
testing requirements. This report provides background on composite tanks, materials, and their
general testing, as well as potential rail-specific testing for type IV tanks.

There are important inherent differences between composite tanks, such as those typically used for
on-road hydrogen fuel cell vehicles, and steel fuel tanks, such as those used to store diesel, which
have been used for rail and other applications. Steel tanks are ductile and can absorb significant
impact energy, while composite tanks are lightweight, resistant to corrosion, resilient under high
pressure conditions, and cost effective. Notably, composite tanks respond differently to static,
harmonic, and dynamic loads which are prevalent in rail environments, so understanding these
interactions with loads and rail-specific shock and vibration conditions is important to curating
appropriate testing protocols to ensure safe and reliable tank design. Type IV composite tanks,
constructed from carbon fiber reinforced polymer, face specific failure modes compared to metals
including fiber-matrix shearing, matrix cracking, debonding, fiber rupture, liner collapse, liner
degradation, and oxidation which can be exacerbated by the unique stresses experienced during rail
operations. Optimizing the manufacturing of these vessels, including implementation of fiber-
prestressing techniques, and carefully controlling variables such as winding, cycle time, temperature,
cure rate, and porosity of the overwrap, is crucial to minimizing these failures. Additionally, rail
design approaches can be implemented to provide dampening, stiffness, or mass, which may
preserve the integrity and life of the tank and other on-board rail equipment.

This report outlines testing requirements provided by relevant codes and standards that are specific
to hydrogen vessels, composite tanks, and rail. Different tests required for composite materials and
tanks include visual inspections, pressure testing, non-destructive testing, and destructive testing,
which may be part of the post-manufacturing testing protocol. Standards published by the
International Organization for Standardization (ISO) and the Compressed Gas Association (CGA)
provide essential guidelines for periodic inspection, testing, and maintenance of composite tanks
used in hydrogen applications. For example, ISO 11623 provides a sequential procedure for gaseous
and liquid composite tank testing. ISO 11119-3, ISO 11515, Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code
(BPVC) Section X, and CGA C-1 also provide requirements for various types of composite tank
tests which should be considered for any application, including rail.

Specific to shock and vibration, this report summarizes a testing protocol tailored for type IV
composite tanks in rail environments based on standards from the International Electrotechnical
Commission (IEC). IEC 63341-2 and IEC 61373, when combined, can be utilized as a testing
procedure to assess the integrity of hydrogen storage systems under operational rail conditions. This
protocol is useful for ensuring that components such as type IV composite tanks meet minimum
reliability requirements for rail use.

Future research should focus on refining testing methodologies to better reflect realistic rail
conditions and enhancing the understanding of long-term composite material behavior under rail-
specific loading conditions. These advancements can support and further the safe adoption of
hydrogen as a locomotive fuel.

1. INTRODUCTION
This report discussed pertinent national and international codes and standards requirements and
testing processes from engineering and science literature for testing type IV tanks for hydrogen use
on rail vehicles. Previous work published by the Department of Transportation (DOT) Federal
Railroad Administration (FRA) from 2021 provided a standards gap analysis to identify primary
aspects of current rail codes that may require revision for hydrogen use. The study identified gaps in
codes and standards related to hydrogen’s unique physical properties and how revisions may be
necessary to address such phenomenon as metal embrittlement, invisible flames, and hydrogen’s
temperature effect on tanks and flowrate, including other considerations such as tank location on
the railcar for optimal venting and designing hydrogen systems to withstand the vibration and shock
experienced in rail environments [1]. This work builds on this previous study and identifies existing
rail-specific shock and vibration conditions and testing requirements for type IV hydrogen vessels.

1.1. Background
Better performance characterization of all on-board hydrogen components, including the safety of
fuel storage technologies, is important as rail investors, suppliers, equipment designers, and owners
begin to explore hydrogen as a fuel. Many different tanks can be used to store hydrogen on
locomotives and multiple unit trainsets (MUs), but due to their low weight, integrity under high
pressure conditions, cost effectiveness, corrosion resistance, and popular use in the automotive
sector [2, 3], type IV composite tanks are being considered for on-board fuel storage for rail
applications. However, unlike steel tanks which are ductile, undergo plastic deformation, and can
absorb higher levels of impact energy before failure, composites are more brittle and can generally
absorb less before laminate failure [4]. Compared to steel tanks, composite tanks do not fail as a
result of macroscopic crack propagation, which is a process relatively easy to detect [5]. Instead,
damage in composite laminates is more complex than with metallic tanks and depends on the
composite material, thickness, ply-stacking sequence, and the loading direction (tension or
compression). The strength and lifetime of composite tanks can be difficult to predict, even using
finite element analysis (FEA) and modeling.
Designing tanks to address these drawbacks has been a strong area of focus in the composite
research space. Additionally, since hydrogen freight and passenger rail transportation is still in its
nascency, studying the effects of rail environment shock- and vibration-induced failure for storage
vessels has emerged as an important step in ensuring safety compliance.

1.2. Objectives
The objectives of this project were to explore the application of type IV composite tanks for
hydrogen storage on trains specifically by:

 • Providing background on the design and characteristics of these tanks, and explaining the
 differences between composite and steel tanks
 • Presenting the types of loads to which composite tanks are subjected
 • Identifying unique rail-specific shock and vibration conditions which need to be considered
 when selecting components such as fuel tanks
 • Presenting the mechanisms for failure for this type of tank according to scientific literature
 and providing methods for minimizing or preventing these failures

 • Summarizing current relevant composite sample and tank testing approaches
 • Summarizing safety codes and standards testing requirements for these types of tanks,
 considering hydrogen-, composite tank- and rail-specific standards
 • Providing relevant shock and vibration testing requirements for type IV tanks which may be
 used in hydrogen rail systems.
The material presented in this report represents a confluence of findings from scientific and
engineering literature, codes and standards, and current websites. This project specifically focused
on identifying testing requirements for type IV composite tanks for hydrogen on-board storage on
rail, with an emphasis on identifying shock and vibration testing protocols. Other gases such as
compressed natural gas (CNG) or liquid fuel storage such as diesel were not considered. The testing
protocols for other types of tanks beside type IV composite were not considered. Other equipment
such as the temperature and pressure relief device (TPRD), vent stack, fuel cell, batteries, electrical
system, valve assembly, or other components were not considered. Other aspects of performance
compliance such as requirements for railcar design, crashworthiness, equipment quality control, etc.
were outside the scope of this report.

2. TYPES OF LOADS AND MODES OF FAILURE FOR TYPE IV TANKS
Composite tanks are comprised of a matrix, typically made of polymers, metals, or ceramics, and
reinforcement, which can be made of fibers, or particulate fillers [6]. Type IV composite tanks are
typically made of carbon fiber impregnated in epoxy thermoset resin, typically called a carbon fiber
reinforced polymer (CFRP). The matrix and reinforcement are spun around a thermoplastic tank
liner and cured in an oven. The liner provides a low-permeability barrier for the gas stored inside it,
while the carbon fiber matrix provides strength and protection for the tank [7].
Much manufacturing effort is focused on minimizing residual stresses in composite materials, which
are stresses that can lead to long-term material degradation or loss of strength. Residual stresses are
not created by errant external forces such as puncture or collision. Instead, they are inherent in
fiber-reinforced composite materials and are part of the degradation of the material which causes
deformation or failures over time [8].

2.1. Types of Composite Tank Loads
Composite tanks can be subjected to many types of loads, including:
 1. Static loads, which are characterized by steady pressure or force [9, 10].
 2. Harmonic loads or excitation, which have a predictable pattern or frequency, such as a
 periodic stress or force caused by rotation. These may be single frequency or multi-
 frequency stresses [11, 10].
 3. Dynamic loads, which are characterized by varying and random forces or anything that
 excites and amplifies the natural frequency of a structure. This type of load has an irregular
 and unpredictable pattern, such as shock due to road roughness, gusts, or turbulence. In the
 literature, these may be described by a probability distribution such as the time-dependent
 Power Spectral Density (PSD) which defines the intensity (magnitude) and variability of the
 stress or vibration [12, 10].
Some attention has been given in the literature to characterizing the types of dynamic loads that
trains may be subject to under normal operating conditions, especially since this can be informative
for developing testing protocol and estimating component lifetime. Some sources have emphasized
the importance of categorizing vibrational loads as Gaussian or non-Gaussian since composite
materials tend to accelerate crack propagation under a random load with complex amplitude.
Experiments have shown that while Gaussian random vibration loads create minimal vibration-
induced fatigue damage of composite materials, non-Gaussian vibrational loads can significantly
affect material integrity and result in lower life. Non-Gaussian loads are categorized as either sub- or
super-Gaussian vibrational loads where kurtosis is less than or greater than 3, respectively [13, 14].
Fan et al. performed experiments on unidirectional and cross-ply 1 mm thick cantilevered composite
swatches subjected to vibrational loads between 5 and 95 Hz [14]. When Gaussian (kurtosis = 3)
and non-Gaussian (kurtosis = 5 or 7) loads on these swatches were compared, vibrational failure
occurred more readily under non-Gaussian vibration loads compared to Gaussian loads. This work,
performed on composite layer swatches rather than on whole composite tanks, highlights the need
for more experiments to better characterize the types of shocks, vibrations, stresses and/or loads
that composite hydrogen tanks mounted on railcars may endure, to ensure safe fuel tank design and
better predict tank performance.

2.2. Rail-Specific Shock and Vibration Conditions
Railcars are subject to unique shock and vibration conditions that on-road vehicles may not
experience, so to properly design hydrogen tanks for these environments, it is helpful to better
understand their routine and anomalous stresses. Vibration is a repetitive stress or load that railcars
and their components may experience, while shock is a type of stress or load which is typically
isolated and a higher magnitude event than vibration. Examples of conditions where more
significant loading stresses occur include railroad travel crossings, switches, car coupling, and in-train
forces such as slack action, which is the free movement between moving cars before motion is
transmitted from one car to another [15]. Importantly, the magnitude and frequency of these
associated shocks and vibrations are highly dependent on the route, speed, and weight of the rolling
stock. Additionally, the degree of stress may vary based on location, for example, because the car's
structure dampens vibrations, the mid-section of the railcar experiences less acceleration than the
ends located directly over the wheel trucks. Also, passenger trains generally experience less stressful
loads compared to freight trains which undergo more coupling and carry heavy freight. While
standard operations involve shocks of roughly 2g, railcars are subject to much higher extremes
during specific events. For instance, long-haul cross-country transit can produce shocks of 22g due
to track irregularities, high speed segments, and slack action, nearly rivaling the 26g experienced
during an 8 mph coupling event. Additionally, the loading and unloading process when on the
transport platform represents one of the most severe stresses to which railcars are subjected [16].
Though liquid and gaseous hydrogen have been transported by truck for decades, rail transport has
not been as widely adopted, so it is important to consider the effect of hydrogen embrittlement of
any steel structural components in a rail environment, since this may compromise the mechanical
integrity of these parts and exacerbate the failure effects of shock and vibration [16].

2.3. Modes of Failure
All the types of loads described in Section 2.4 can lead to different failures in composite materials.
There are several typical categories of failure depending on which components of the composite
have separated or broken [4, 10]:
1. Fiber-matrix shearing or interply delamination is one of the most common modes of failure
 and is associated with low velocity impact to the material. These types of failures can be due to
 in-plane shearing which can result in separation occurring in the direction of the carbon fiber.
 This can result in layers of the composite fiber delaminating from one another. This type of
 failure is not necessarily catastrophic; however, extensive damage of this kind may lead to
 breakage.
2. Matrix cracking or intraply matrix failure is also a common mode of failure for composite
 materials, and, like interply delamination, is associated with low impact to the material. This type
 of failure is associated with cracks forming within the polymer, ceramic, or metal matrix, within
 a single laminate layer, usually in the same direction as the composite fiber. Like the shearing or
 delamination described above, this type of failure is not necessarily catastrophic, however,
 extensive damage of this kind may lead to breakage.
3. Fiber breakage or rupture are more commonly associated with higher velocity impact strikes
 to the surface and involve the splitting of reinforcement fibers. Typically, if one fiber fractures,
 then those surrounding it begin to fail, leading to a coalescence of breaks [5].

 14

 Back-face splitting is a type of fiber breakage which may occur when a composite laminate is
 impacted and compressed on the front face, causing the back face to experience tensile stress
 and possible breakage. Depending on the extent, this type of failure can be materially
 catastrophic, however this greatly depends on the design, manufacturing, and wrapping process
 [17].
4. Debonding is also commonly associated with higher velocity impact strikes for inner liners that
 are bounded to the composite overwrap. It usually involves the matrix and reinforcement
 overwrap decoupling from the inner liner or other material it is designed to protect [18].
 Depending on the extent, this type of failure can be materially catastrophic.
Fiber fracture or breakage is typically more catastrophic than matrix failure, though matrix failure
can contribute to and promote fiber failure [19]. Tensile preloading, discussed in Section 2.4 below,
may be a significant contributor to composite weakness or failure and should be carefully controlled
during the manufacturing process.
Other types of failure may involve the liner’s interaction with its composite overwrap. Potential
drawbacks to type IV tanks include the possibility of collapse, plastic deformation, or blistering
during decompression, which may involve liner shrinking or debonding at the liner-composite
interface. Additionally, since the CFRP overwrap responds to thermal and mechanical stresses
differently than the thermoplastic liner, this may lead to different deformation behavior between the
two materials. Some high-density polyethylene liners have an inner resin layer that hydrogen may
diffuse into, which can cause deterioration of the liner due to cycles of decompression [19]. This
may be why hydrogen tanks under static working pressure conditions suffer the greatest risk of
leakage from the head of the tank, since a metal polar boss is interfacing with a thermoplastic liner
and a composite overwrap, increasing the likelihood of separation between different materials [19].
Additional causes of failure for type IV tanks can also include degradation of the CFRP caused by
heat, moisture, salt, and/or ultraviolet radiation exposure over time [20]. These types of failures can
be minimized by designing appropriate on-board shielding or protection for the tank in the location
where it is mounted on the railcar.

2.4. Potential Methods for Minimizing Failure in Composite Tanks
Tank defects and potential points of failure are best prevented during the manufacturing process.
Optimization and consistency of the production process are linked to longer life and higher tank
reliability, and for this reason manufacturers invest in carefully evaluating different manufacturing
variables.
During the composite tank manufacturing process, the matrix and reinforcement may be subject to
both tensile and compressive stresses, sometimes called preloading. Compressive stresses, often
delivered by curing the tank under pressure, are believed to enhance the mechanical properties of
the composite material by preventing cracks from spreading throughout the matrix, while tensile
stresses may be detrimental because they can cause distortion, dimensional instability, and matrix
cracking [21, 4]. This is one of the reasons tensile fiber prestressing during the manufacturing
process may be advantageous since such prestressing can reduce material failure which can minimize
the residual stress that causes moisture absorption, shrinkage of fibers, or thermal expansion defects,
without adding bulk [22]. Other approaches which may aid in preventing these same defects include
optimizing cure cycle time, temperature, and cooling rate [23] as well as carbon fiber orientation [24].
During manufacturing, controlling porosity of the laminate to under 1% has been shown to reduce
interlaminar shear and vessel bursting. This is done by having consistent filament winding and a

 15

homogeneous matrix impregnation bath [25]. Also, electron beam curing can reduce stress
concentration by promoting polymerization and crosslinking at room temperature [26]. Any metals
in direct or indirect contact with hydrogen should be chosen carefully. For example, nickel-plated or
stainless steel bosses have been used for their resilience to hydrogen embrittlement [27]. Another
important consideration is the potential for galvanic corrosion of metal hardware if it is exposed to a
combination of moisture and carbon fiber. In this situation, carbon fibers may act as a cathode,
resulting in corrosion and pitting of the metal, which is acting as an anode [28].
Additionally, several design approaches for equipping hydrogen storage on railcars can be taken to
prevent tank shock and vibration under normal use. To isolate railcars from ground-induced
stresses, spring or elastomer isolators can be employed, or additional damping, stiffness, or mass can
be added to the railcar. Also, regularly assessing and correcting wheel flat spots, which contribute to
slippage and transfer of shock and vibration through the car, can help protect the tank and other
components [15].

3. TYPES OF TESTING
This section outlines the types of testing composite materials and type IV tanks to evaluate
performance, though these tests may not be explicitly required for compliance with existing
regulations, codes, and standards.

3.1. Composite Material Sample Testing
Composite sample materials undergo a different testing protocol compared to equipment such as
composite tanks or vessels. CFRP material swatches or coupons are fatigue tested by first forming
tabs, notches, or through-holes in sample materials. These purposely created “risk points” in the
material are areas which experience significant stress concentration under testing conditions. After
stress is applied, damage to the material starts at the risk point, the first crack occurring at the
maximum stress position, which typically expands with load time until the material fails. Stages of
the fracture process involve sequential intralaminar, followed by interlaminar damage [10].
Specifically, for composite materials, the stages of induced damage during the destructive testing
process are [14, 29] :
 1. Intralaminar matrix cracking
 2. Saturation of matrix cracks, known as the characteristic damage state (CDS)
 3. Cracking coupling-interfacial debonding and delamination
 4. Fiber breaking, fracture, and failure
To create these four stages of damage, in-plane tensile testing is the most common test performed
on composite materials. This often involves putting a tabbed composite hoop or sample under
progressive degrees of tension using a metal split disk or other tensioner device, while failure at the
tabbed or notched risk point is examined as the test proceeds. Other types of sample testing include
compression, shear, fatigue via static load testing, and high strain rate testing. Another composite
test is Compression After Impact (CAI) where a material suffers a controlled impact via a drop, then
is subjected to a compressive load, and tested to determine its residual strength [30, 31].
These testing processes are valuable for informing composite tank design since it can influence
choices of materials, winding patterns, matrix and reinforcement thicknesses, cure times, and other
manufacturing choices.

3.2. Composite Tank Testing
There are many different types of composite tank testing approaches discussed in the composite
literature, from defect prediction to damage detection techniques. Many of these testing approaches
focus on post-manufacturing assessment of the integrity of the tank.
Inspections of composite tanks can involve:

 1. Visual inspection of cracks, delamination, or fiber damage

 2. Hydraulic pressure testing is typically used for design qualification and as an initial proof test
 by the manufacturer. For vessels where the liner is not bonded to the composite, the tank is
 pneumatically pressurized to seat the liner, which ensures adequate contact between the liner
 and the overwrap, then the air is bled out and replaced with high-pressure water [32]. For
 large scale bulk transport vessels such as tube trailers, this test may be difficult to perform

 17

 because of the mass of water required. Drying of the interior of the tank is performed after
 the test to ensure removal of water.

 3. Pneumatic pressure testing, involving pressurizing a vessel and checking that it can retain
 static pressure over time. Sometimes this involves holding the pressurized vessel under
 water and checking for bubbles, which can signify leaks. The International Association for
 Hydrogen Safety (HySafe) states that 70 MPa type IV tanks should have a safety factor (the
 burst pressure divided by the working pressure) of 2.25 [25]. Requirements such as these are
 important to consider for static load (pressure) testing while the tank is in service.

 4. Non-destructive testing for detection of damage that may not be visible, including [33, 34]:

 a. Ultrasonic testing (UT), the most popular contact non-destructive testing method,
 typically used in aerospace for composites up to 50 mm thick. This approach commonly
 employs water-coupled ultrasonic waves to find internal flaws, cracks, variations, voids,
 and delamination.

 b. Radiographic testing (RT), also called radiography, radioscopy, tomography, or
 laminography, is a noncontact, non-destructive testing method that uses X-rays or
 gamma rays to detect voids and porosity, inclusions, cracks, resin-to-fiber ratio, fiber
 distribution, or anomalies such as wrinkles, folds, or weld lines.

 c. Acoustic emission testing (AET), a non-destructive technique that detects acoustic
 (sound) waves released by a composite material under stress. This enables the detection
 of damage such as cracks, delamination, corrosion, and fiber breakage. The American
 Society for Testing Materials has a standard ASTM E1067-07 which provides protocols
 for acoustic emissions tests for fiberglass reinforced plastic resin vessels [35].

 d. Infrared thermography (IRT) detects variations in heat transfer to help identify
 anomalies in the material. While this approach enables the detection of the onset of
 damage and its progression, it has limited depth detection and involves exposure to
 halogen or flash lamps [10].

Destructive testing is required and performed annually by manufacturers on a subset of tanks
produced [32, 36]. It involves invasive techniques to detect and locate structural tank damages. This
type of testing can also be performed on swatches or samples of materials prior to manufacturing.
If performed on tanks, some of these destructive tests include placing a tank under vibrational stress
while empty [37] or when filled with water [38].

A drawback to these types of inspections for composite tanks is that the detection of damage can
depend on the inspector and the equipment used, and the damage type, size, and shape, is currently
not evaluated consistently or quantitatively [39]. This inconsistency makes predicting the lifetime of
tanks difficult. Tank lifetime is usually assessed through testing and modeling approaches, though it
is challenging to accurately model the effect of realistic, variable amplitude loads, such as those in
rail environments, on material fatigue and there is no set standard for modeling composite lifetime
[39].

4. STANDARDS REVIEW AND REQUIREMENTS FOR HYDROGEN
 TANK OR COMPOSITE TANK TESTING
There are numerous safety regulations, codes, and standards relating to rail components as well as
pressurized or fuel-containing composite vessels. The stakeholders that may depend on or benefit
from this guidance are broad in scope and may include composite tank manufacturers, railcar
manufacturers, railroads, municipalities, authorities having jurisdiction, federal government agencies,
and regulators. Some organizations that provide test standards for composite materials, which may
not necessarily encompass composite tanks, include the International Organization for
Standardization (ISO), the American Society for Testing and Materials (ASTM), European Standards
(EN), Japanese Industrial Standards (JIS), Suppliers of Advanced Composite Materials Association
(SACMA), and Composites Research Advisory Group (CRAG) [30]. This section presents a non-
comprehensive overview of some design and testing requirements provided by common regulation
organizations such as the International Organization for Standardization, the Compressed Gas
Association, the Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code, Canadian Standards Association (CSA) and the
United Nations Global Technical Regulation (UN GTR).

4.1. Standards Relevant to Hydrogen Storage, Vessel Testing, and
 Recertification
The following CGA and ISO standards provide guidance and requirements on hydrogen storage
vessel damage assessment and testing for recertification and reuse. This guidance is potentially
helpful in developing procedures for identifying and assessing the severity of damage that may be
caused by routine onboard rail shock and vibration. These assessments can be used to recertify,
repair, or decommission a tank.

SAE J2579: Standard for Fuel Systems in Fuel Cell and Other Hydrogen Vehicles
SAE J2579 was last updated in 2023 and is a performance-based standard for on-road, non-rail
vehicles [40]. Though it focuses on hydrogen fuel systems, the storage and handling requirements in
this standard can be useful consideration for rail applications. This standard requires temperature
management of the tank, ensuring the composite materials are not exposed to greater than 85°C
temperatures. Additionally, requirements for leak tightness and testing are specified, including
pneumatic cycling protocol performed at -40°C and 85°C, chemical exposure, and destructive
tolerance testing. These tests qualify the tank for 15 years usually, after which they must be
destroyed. SAE J2579 was revised in 2023 to include important modifications appropriate for heavy
duty vehicles including tank mounting, installation requirements, and localized fire test methods.
Such updates for heavy duty vehicles may help inform requirements for fuel systems on locomotives
and MUs.

CGA H-5: Bulk Hydrogen Supply Systems
Most recently published in 2020 by the Compressed Gas Association, CGA H-5 provides minimum
requirements for liquid or gaseous bulk hydrogen supply systems operating at pressures up to 15,000
psi (103.4 MPa) [41]. This standard is specific to hydrogen, but not specific to composite vessels. It
primarily focuses on siting, layout, and setback distance requirements for bulk storage as well as
equipment selection criteria for tanks, piping, valves, and venting systems.

 19

This standard’s requirements are general in that they emphasize reliance on the manufacturer for
information on lifetime assessment, and that re-certification must be performed by a qualified
person. This standard requires that tanks be installed according to manufacturer’s specifications and
states that a composite tank’s lifetime is determined by the number of maximum pressure cycles,
which is determined and specified by the manufacturer. Tanks must be recertified by a recertifying
entity and annual maintenance must be performed by a qualified representative. Maintenance
involves inspection for physical damage, leak tightness, grounding system integrity, and other safety
related features. Ordinarily, shear wave angle beam ultrasonic inspection is used to inspect tank
walls. This standard states that inspections should determine if there are longitudinal flaws in excess
of 5% of the wall thickness or 3 mm (whichever is less), though this requirement may be more
appropriate for metal, not composite tanks. As discussed in Section 2 of this report, composite
tanks do not fail in the same way as metal tanks and therefore could benefit from composite-specific
tests.

CGA C-6.2: Standard for Visual Inspection and Requalification of Fiber Reinforced High
Pressure Cylinders
The latest edition of CGA C 6-2, published in 2019, is specific to composite high pressure cylinders
and provides guidance for visual inspection and hydrostatic testing [42]. Frequently cited alongside
CGA C-1, this standard provides procedures, including preparation for testing and guidance on how
to identify and characterize specific exterior damage due to abrasions, cuts, impacts, structural
defects, chemical exposure, heat, or fire. This standard provides visual guidance for identifying the
type of damage and its severity from level 1 (acceptable), level 2 (requiring further inspection or
repair), and level 3 (requiring decommissioning). This standard also provides a procedure for
interior visual inspection, including removal of the valve and performing a hydrostatic test.
Additionally, this standard provides the test pressure requirements for hydrostatic requalification
testing, citing CGA C-1 for the hydrostatic test methods which may be used.

ISO 11623: Gas Cylinders – Composite Cylinders and Tubes – Periodic Inspection and
Testing
Last updated in 2023 by the International Organization for Standardization, ISO 11623 provides
requirements for the periodic inspection and testing of composite compressed, liquified, or
dissolved pressurized gas cylinders with volumes between 0.5 and 3,000 L, including type IV tanks
[43]. This standard may be applied to cylinders less than 0.5 L when authorized by the
manufacturer. This standard shares some similarities to CGA C-6.2 described above, specifically in
terms of visual inspection criteria and volumetric expansion limits.
Though this document is not hydrogen-specific, it does apply to gaseous hydrogen composite
vessels. This standard provides these requirements which should be followed sequentially:
 • Timelines and procedures for periodic inspection and testing
 • Procedures for identifying a cylinder
 • Preparation requirements for inspection
 • Requirements for external visual inspection, including preparation procedures and
 identification of damage and necessary repairs

 20

 • Requirements for internal visual inspection, including procedures for safe removal of the
 valve, internal visual inspection and cleaning, internal damage assessment, and neck
 inspection.
 • Performing a pressure test, such as filling the cylinder with water, pressurizing it with gas, or
 performing a non-destructive test. Non-destructive testing is permitted only if the Authority
 Having Jurisdiction (AHJ) and the manufacturer allow it.
 • Performing a leak test, such as pressurized cylinder immersion under water or applying liquid
 soap to check for bubbles.
 • Inspecting the valve, which should be performed in accordance with ISO 22434 [44]
 • Last steps including drying, cleaning, painting, re-valving, weighing, and marking
There are several important requirements from ISO 11623 that may apply to gaseous hydrogen
composite tanks. In Annex C of the standard, it states that periodic testing of composite tanks used
for hydrogen should be conducted every 5-10 years based on the type of composite cylinder.
This standard provides other important general requirements for composite tanks. For example,
composite tanks must be inspected and tested to continue to be in operation. The expiration date
depends on the last test date, or other indications such as the manufacturer’s expiration date. Using
the remaining contents of the cylinder even if the tank is past the periodic inspection date is allowed.
Tanks must be clearly marked with the contents and the design life of the tank. Tanks can be
repaired with a compatible resin system by anyone competent, so long as limited damage has
occurred, and as long as tanks are pressure tested after repair. The standard also provides helpful
tank acceptance/rejection criteria for damage assessment.
This ISO standard does not explicitly require an ultrasound test, instead only requiring external and
internal visual inspection, valve inspection, pressure testing, and leak testing. There does not appear
to be a formal process for determining tank lifetime from any of the above tests or using any other
methodology. Though this standard focuses on tank testing, it refers to design and manufacturing
requirements for composite tanks which are provided by ISO 11119-1, 11119-2, 11119-3, 11119-4,
or 11515 [45, 46, 47, 48, 36].

CGA C-1: Methods for Pressure Testing Compressed Gas Cylinders and Tubes
CGA C-1 was last published in 2022 by the Compressed Gas Association and provides water jacket
method, direct expansion method, and proof pressure method pressure testing requirements for
compressed gas cylinders [49]. This standard is referenced by the Canadian Standards Association
CSA B341 as a protocol for pressure testing and requalifying composite tanks [50]. CGA C-1 is not
specific to hydrogen and only covers methods for testing gas-containing vessels, so while this
standard does apply to gaseous hydrogen, it does not apply to liquid hydrogen cylinders and vessels.
The water jacket method and direct expansion method involve pressurizing a vessel with a liquid,
while the proof pressure method involves pressurizing with a gas.
This standard presents the most common method employed in the U.S. and Canada for testing high
pressure cylinders, which is the water jacket method. It involves taking a pressurized cylinder filled
with liquid and surrounding it with a test jacket filled with water. The cylinder is under pressure to
induce expansion, which is measured by the amount of water displaced due to the expansion. This
measurement is performed both when the cylinder is under pressure and after the pressure is

 21

released. The direct expansion method involves measuring the liquid required to reach the cylinder’s
test pressure, then releasing the pressure in the vessel and measuring the liquid released. This
approach determines the elastic expansion of a cylinder. The proof pressure method involves
checking for leaks, bulges, and any visible defects while the cylinder is pressurized to its test
pressure. These tests could be considered for gaseous supply composite tanks with the
understanding that the maximum expansion limits for the water jacket method and direct expansion
method may be different for composite tanks compared to metal tanks.

4.2. Testing Requirements Relevant to Prototyping and Manufacturing
The following standards are used primarily by manufacturers to test tank performance under a
variety of real-world routine use and anomalous abuse conditions. Though this testing is important
to tank manufacturers, they may also be relevant to hydrogen locomotive manufacturers so that they
may perform their own independent testing.

UN GTR 13: Hydrogen and Fuel Cell Vehicles
United Nations (UN) Global Technical Regulation (GTR) UN GTR 13 was last updated in 2013
with the aim of creating requirements such that hydrogen and fuel cell vehicles exhibit the same
safety level as gasoline-based internal combustion engine vehicles [51]. This standard provides
safety-related requirements for hydrogen and fuel cell vehicles which are 4536 kg or less, to protect
from and prevent fire, explosion, electric shock, among other hazards. This 4536 kg weight upper
limit is associated Gross Vehicle Mass (GVM) which includes the vehicle, cargo, and passengers, but
not trailers.
Though this standard encompasses many components of hydrogen and fuel cell vehicles, they
provide very specific procedures and specifications for post-manufacturing hydrogen storage system
tests including hydraulic burst, static pressure, ambient pressure and ambient temperature cycling,
extreme temperature and pressure cycling, proof pressure, drop/impact, surface damage, chemical
exposure, gas permeation, localized gas leak, localized/engulfing fire, and other tests. In addition to
these test procedures, this standard provides pre- and post- testing, instrumentation and sensor
placement, mounting, burner placement, and other detailed requirements. This standard is similar to
ISO 11119-3 and 11515 in that they cover some of the same post-manufacturing tests, however, UN
GTR 13 generally features more rigorous, system-level requirements, such as for cycle life and abuse
tolerance compared to the more general guidance provided in the ISO standards. This standard
includes vibration testing requirements for temperature and pressure relief devices (TPRDs), check
valves, and shut-off valves, but not for tanks.

ISO 11119-3: Gas cylinders - Design, construction and testing of refillable composite gas
cylinders and tubes - Part 3: fully wrapped fibre reinforced composite gas cylinders and
tubes up to 450 L with non-load-sharing metallic or non-metallic liners or without liners
ISO 11119-3:2020 was last published in 2020 and provides minimum requirements for the post-
manufacturing material, design, construction and workmanship, manufacturing processes,
examination, and testing of type III, type IV, and type V compressed or liquefied gas pressure
vessels, having capacities up to 450 L [47]. This regulation is not specific to gaseous or liquid
hydrogen but instead provides requirements for any compressed or liquified gas storage within type
IV or V composite vessels, cylinders, or tubes.

 22

This standard focuses on design requirements and inspection/testing post-manufacturing, providing
guidelines for testing tank quality and resilience. Suppliers have the burden of ensuring that
materials are of uniform and consistent quality, the correct properties, satisfactory quality, and that
each batch is inspected. This standard emphasizes the importance of high vessel reliability under
sustained and cyclic loading. For new designs/prototypes, tanks must undergo the following tests:
hydraulic proof pressure test or hydraulic elastic expansion test, cylinder burst test, ambient
temperature cycle test, environmental cycle test, flaw test, drop/impact test, high velocity impact
(large caliber bullet gunfire) test, torque test, leak test, pneumatic test, resin shear strength test, and
glass transition test. A cylinder can be used for multiple tests, and for many of these tests the
cylinder must be subjected to multiple pressure cycles. One common test procedure is the
environmental cycle test, which involves pressure cycling at high temperature and high humidity,
pressure cycling at low temperature, followed by a leak test and burst test. Other tests include the
drop/impact test where two cylinders are each filled with a specific amount of water and dropped
on a surface where the surface and angle at which it is dropped is based on cylinder capacity. After
being dropped, one cylinder is subjected to a burst test and the other subjected to an ambient cycling
test.
This standard requires producing test documentation for the liner, composite overwrap, and
composite cylinder, including determination of the vessel’s test pressure, minimum design burst
pressure, and design life in years. Additionally, minimum fiber stress ratio (the fiber stress at design
minimum burst pressure divided by the fiber stress at 2/3 test pressure) needs to be established.

ISO 11515: Gas cylinders - Refillable composite reinforced tubes of water capacity between
450 L and 3000 L - Design, construction and testing
If larger tank capacities will be used, ISO 11515 should be applied instead of ISO 11119-3. Last
published in 2022, this standard appears to be similar in scope and requirements compared to ISO
11119-3. It covers type IV fully-wrapped composite tanks with capacities between 450 L and 3,000
L for use with compressed or liquefied gases up to 1600 bar and temperatures between -40°C and
65°C [36]. Due to the large size of gas cylinders covered by this standard, it may be used for
guidance on tube trailer or bulk hydrogen storage. Like ISO 11119-3, though this standard is not
hydrogen-specific, it should be considered for composite tank testing, especially if larger tanks, such
as those between 450 and 3,000 L, will be used, as may be likely for railcars. This standard specifies
requirements for liner materials and prototype tests such as hydraulic burst tests, ambient cycle tests,
temperature cycling, and fire resistance, similar to the tests specified in ISO 11119-3 for smaller
vessels.

BPVC Section X: Fiber-Reinforced Plastic Pressure Vessels
The Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code (BPVC) Section X was last published in 2025 by the American
Society of Mechanical Engineers (ASME) and it provides production, processing, fabrication,
inspection and testing methods requirements for fiber-reinforced plastic pressure vessels that
operate at pressures greater than 15 psig in conformance with a manufacturer's design report [32].
This standard is similar in scope to ISO 11119-3 and ISO 11515 in that it outlines many
requirements for vessel prototype and design testing. There are three classes of plastic vessel design
covered in the standard. Class I and Class III vessels are qualified through the destructive testing of
a prototype while Class II vessels are required to abide by mandatory design rules and

 23

nondestructive testing. It should be noted that these class numbers are different from CSA B342’s
“Class 2” vessels described in Section 4. Type IV composite tanks may qualify under Class II, since
this category includes vessels designed for compressed gases and liquids.
This standard provides extensive fabrication requirements, along with qualification and quality
control tests. Requirements include ensuring and inspecting correct fiber weight, material selection,
speed of winding consistency, cure conditions, uniformity of heating, and other requirements.
These properties should be assessed during and after manufacturing. This standard provides
minimum requirements for materials of construction, methods of fabrication, end closure,
connections, and attachments to vessels. Additionally, this standard provides requirements for
prototype vessels including fabrication procedures, requalification/reexamination requirements due
to design changes, rules for acceptance testing and inspection, and stamping and marking
procedures. Though this is a general pressure vessel standard and is intended for fixed (rather than
mobile) installations, type IV tanks are included, and this standard’s requirements should therefore
be considered for composite tank design and testing.

Canadian Standards
Canadian Standards Association (CSA) B341 and B342 were each most recently published in 2025
and specifically apply to the transport of dangerous goods within pressurized containers [50, 52].
CSA B341, entitled, “UN pressure receptacles and multiple-element gas containers for the
transportation of dangerous goods,” outlines requirements for the design, construction, initial
inspection, marking, periodic inspection, testing, and repair of United Nations (UN) pressure
receptacles and multiple-element gas containers. This standard defers to ISO 11623 for composite
tank inspection and recertification [43]. This standard also defers to ISO 11119-1, ISO 11119-2,
ISO 11119-3, and CGA C-1 for testing of composite tanks [45, 46, 47, 49]. Appendix A within CSA
B341 includes information for registering design types and procedures for keeping records. This
standard is commonly used as guidance regarding how pressurized gas vessels, including hydrogen
composite tanks, must be designed and maintained to be legally used in Canada.
CSA B342, entitled, “Selection and use of UN pressure receptacles, multiple-element gas containers,
and other pressure receptacles for the transport of dangerous goods, Class 2” applies specifically to
Class 2 gases and sets the requirements for the selection, use, handling, and filling of UN pressure
receptacles. Unlike BPVC Section X’s classifications, Class 2 for CSA B342 refers to the container,
its pressure rating, and its use in storing potentially dangerous gases being transported. This
standard focuses on which vessels are authorized for use and how they can be operated or filled,
specifically by providing guidance on filling requirements, vehicle or trailer mounting, and
requalification periods. Neither CSA B341 nor B342 are specific to hydrogen vessels or composite
tanks, however their requirements should be useful and considered when designing hydrogen tanks
for rail transport.

5. PERFORMING SHOCK AND VIBRATION TESTING ON TANKS OR
 COMPONENTS FOR RAIL ENVIRONMENTS: STANDARDS
 REQUIREMENTS
There is one standard that provides guidance for shock and vibration testing for land vehicles and
four main standards that are currently most applicable for developing shock and vibration testing
protocol for tanks intended for rail use. The land vehicle shock and vibration testing standard is
ISO 19887-1 and provides many tests for hydrogen fuel system components. The Code of Federal
Regulations’ 49 CFR 178.75 specifies an impact, static, and dynamic tests for assemblies of
composite tanks in rail use. IEC 63341-2 provides an overview of the testing process requirements
specific to hydrogen systems and IEC 61372 provides detailed requirements for how rail shock and
vibration tests should be conducted for on-board equipment. Together, the requirements in these
documents in this section can be combined to provide a shock and vibration testing protocol for
type IV hydrogen tanks onboard rolling stock.

5.1. Non-tank component testing
ISO 19887-1: Gaseous Hydrogen — Fuel system components for hydrogen-fuelled vehicles
— Part 1: Land vehicles
Though specific to components rather than tanks for light-duty and heavy-duty land vehicles, ISO
19887-1, most recently published in 2024, may be a useful standard for performance-based testing
and safety requirements for fuel system components that can help inform requirements for rail
applications [53]. This standard focuses on hydrogen system components such as flow valves,
pressure regulators, relief valves, receptacles, filters, fittings, connections, and other fuel
components, but not the tank. For this reason, it is a useful compliment and comparison to the IEC
standards below to inform potential tank testing protocols. It outlines requirements for fuel
components to ensure durability, reliability, and safety for gaseous hydrogen. Tests encompassed by
the standard include assessments of component characteristics and performance, such as hydrostatic
strength, leakage, excess torque resistance, continuous operation, corrosion resistance, ultraviolet
resistance, automotive fluid exposure, atmospheric exposure, abnormal electrical voltages, non-
metallic hydrogen compatibility, stress corrosion and cracking resistance, and resistance to
operational shock and vibration.

ISO 19887-1’s Clause 5 outlines a vibration resistance test which is required for a variety of
components such as some valves, assemblies, and indicators to ensure component integrity, gas leak-
tightness, and/or hydrostatic leak tightness. The vibration test discussed in this standard is not as
comprehensive as those in IEC 61373 since ISO 19887-1 prescribes one 30 minute vibration test,
but does not specifically provide a functional random vibration test, simulated long life vibration
test, or shock test, as does IEC 61373 (as discussed below). The test requirements specify that the
equipment being tested should be subjected to a broad spectrum of frequencies to find the resonant
frequency (if possible), and the component vibration must be conducted in each direction:
longitudinally, transversely, and vertically.

5.2. Tank-specific testing
49 CFR 178.75: Specifications for MEGCs
Most recently updated in 2024, 49 CFR 178.75 is a U.S. Code of Federal Regulations (CFR) which
provides requirements of design, construction, and materials integrity for Multiple-Element Gas
 25

Containers (MEGCs) [54]. MEGCs are defined by 49 CFR 171.8 as a manifold-interconnected
assembly of UN seamless steel or composite cylinders, tubes, or bundles of cylinders that are
assembled in a framework or support structure [55]. This regulation is relevant since railcars
utilizing gaseous hydrogen may have such assemblies of multiple identically-designed composite
tanks mounted in a frame with a manifold on-board.
Section (i) of 49 CFR 178.75 specifies an initial inspection and test for MEGCs before they are put
into service for the first time including checks of design characteristics, visual exterior inspection,
and a pressure test of the tank, manifold, and service equipment separately and after assembly.
Notably, subsection (i)(4) of CFR 178.75 specifies an impact test of no less than 4g the maximum
permissible gross mass of the fully loaded MEGC. This impact test uses a prototype meant to
absorb forces similar to railcar coupling. The direction of the test should be typical of the
mechanical shocks experienced in rail transportation and tank pressure must be monitored before,
during, and after the impact. Though considered separate standards, 49 CFR 178.75(i)(4) and CSA
B625 Annex C [56] feature some harmonized standards for tank impact testing for rail
environments.
Section (h) of 49 CFR 178.75 specifies a static and dynamic forces test for MEGC supports,
frameworks, lifting, and tie-downs to ensure that MEGCs as a whole are designed and constructed
to protect against effects of lateral, longitudinal, and overturning events. The static and dynamic test
requires equipment to be proven to withstand four force tests: 2g in the direction of travel, 1g
horizontally at right angles to the direction of travel, 1g vertically upward, and 2g vertically
downward within a specific frequency range.
IEC 63341-2: Railway applications – Hydrogen and fuel cell systems for rolling stock – Part
2: Hydrogen fuel system
Most recently published in 2025, IEC 63341-2 is an international standard last published in 2025
written by the International Electrotechnical Commission which provides requirements for the
Compressed Hydrogen Storage Systems (CHSS) used to supply fuel cells and power for rail
applications [57]. Currently, this standard applies only to gaseous hydrogen storage, not liquid
hydrogen. This standard applies to all railcar types from light duty to heavy-duty locomotives. Light
duty rolling stock generally refers to vehicles with smaller tank sizes and less frequent refueling
cycles, such as light rail vehicles, tramways, or streetcars. Conversely, heavy duty locomotives can
refer to vehicles with larger fuel capacity, designed for high-capacity freight or passenger rail service.
This standard provides three key sequential steps in performing shock and vibration testing for
gaseous hydrogen systems including the tank, valves, sensors, instrumentation, and piping, with
some important requirements:

 1. Electrical, fluidic, control, and monitoring tests must be conducted to prove the system is
 fully operational before shock and vibration tests are started.

 2. Shock and vibration tests should be performed in accordance with IEC 61373:2010:
 “Railway applications - Rolling stock equipment - Shock and vibration tests” while following
 these requirements:

 a. These tests must be conducted on the complete hydrogen fuel cell and any
 subassemblies.

 26

 b. The compressed hydrogen storage system (CHSS) must be filled with inert gas such as
 helium or nitrogen or a blend of these at the minimum operation pressure for the high
 pressure section (HP MnOP). The HP MnOP is a value which is agreed upon by the
 user and manufacturer and is the minimal pressure the CHSS can be operated in normal
 conditions.

 c. Automatic valves should be kept open, unless otherwise stated (see below). Manual
 valves, such as those at the container or sub-assembly level, may be kept closed to allow
 for leak testing.

 d. During the Functional Random Vibration Test described in the following section
 describing IEC 61373, equipment such as automatic valves, pressure sensors, and
 temperature sensors should be tested for functionality during or after the test, as agreed
 to between the manufacturer and the tester ahead of time. When performing the
 Functional Random Vibration Test, automatic valves should be periodically closed and
 opened.

 e. During Long-life Testing and half-sinusoidal Shock Tests described in the section below,
 the hydrogen systems equipment does not need to be functional.

 3. Electrical, fluidic, control, and monitoring tests must be repeated to prove the system is
 functioning within acceptable limits.

During these tests, some components can be substituted by representative masses, which are objects
similar in mass and volume, in order to reduce the overall test cost. In these situations, a sample of
the original component must be present during the test.

A critical aspect of this three-step process is the shock and vibration testing which is performed with
the Functional Random Vibration Test, Simulated Long-life Random Vibration Test, and the Shock
Test, which are described in IEC 61373 below.

IEC 61373: Railway Applications – Rolling Stock Equipment – Shock and Vibration Tests
The International Electrotechnical Commission most recently published IEC 61373 in 2025, which
is an international standard that outlines the requirements for railway vehicle equipment shock and
vibration testing [58]. These requirements are not specific to hydrogen or composites, but does
include any pneumatic, electrical, and electronic equipment/components on railcar tanks that may
be mounted on the axels, bogies, or the train body.

This standard specifies Category 1 as equipment which is body mounted, and Class A as cubicles,
subassemblies, equipment, or components mounted directly on or under the train car body. Though
there are a few different categories and classes specified in the standard, the tests pertaining to
Category 1 and Class A appear to most directly apply to hydrogen fuel tanks on railcars. Their
corresponding recommended shock and vibration tests are summarized below.

Clause 8 in this standard outlines the requirements for a Functional Random Vibration Test,
which involves demonstrating that the equipment under test is capable of handling typical train
operating conditions. While the threshold requirements for this test should be determined between

 27

the manufacturer and the end user before testing, this standard provides guidance on how these
vibration tests should be performed. Section 8 of this standard provides tables which specify the
acceleration the test equipment should be subjected to at five frequencies: 2, 5, 10, 75, and 150 Hz.
The tests are performed by vibrating (shaking) the equipment in one direction per test, either
longitudinally, transversely, or vertically, which means at least 15 unique tests are performed, one for
each of five frequencies and three different directions. The degree of acceleration the equipment is
subjected to in each of these directions depends on the direction, for example, equipment
undergoing vertical vibration must be subjected to higher accelerations, and therefore more stress,
than when the equipment is undergoing vibration testing in the longitudinal or transverse direction.
The Functional Random Vibration Test is not considered a full performance evaluation.

Clause 9 in this standard outlines the Simulated Long-life Random Vibration Test, and although
it is not necessary to demonstrate full equipment functionality under these conditions, this test
assesses the mechanical integrity of equipment at increased service levels. Similar to the Functional
Random Vibration Test above, the Simulated Long-life Random Vibration Test requirements
specify the acceleration the test equipment should be subjected to at five frequencies: 2, 5, 10, 75,
and 150 Hz, which must be conducted for each direction: longitudinally, transversely, or vertically,
resulting in at least 15 unique tests. As with the Functional Random Vibration Test, equipment
undergoing vertical vibration tests is subject to higher required accelerations than equipment
experiencing longitudinal or transverse vibration tests. Additionally, since these tests simulate
extended use, the required accelerations are higher than those for the Functional Random Vibration
Test. For the Simulated Long-life Random Vibration Test, it is not necessary to demonstrate that
the equipment can operate during this test, but it is necessary to show that there is no visual
deformation, and that mechanical integrity has been retained. The Simulated Long-life Random
Vibration Test can be conducted for 5 and/or 100 hours, where the required accelerations for the
100 hour test are considerably lower than for the 5 hour test.

Clause 10 in this standard outlines the Shock Testing requirements, which simulate rare service
events. This portion of the standard outlines the requirements for 18 shock pulses to the test
equipment, three in one direction and three in the opposite direction, for each of the three axes.
The standard provides limits, tolerances, and durations for these pulses. For Category A, Class 1
equipment, depending on the direction, peak accelerations of 30-50 m/s2 are required for a duration
of 30 msec for compliance with this test. These accelerations are much higher than those required
for the Functional Random Vibration Test and the Simulated Long-life Random Vibration Test. For
the Shock Test, it is not necessary to demonstrate that the equipment can operate during the test.

This standard provides some other specific requirements, such that tests should be longer than 10
minutes, and that measured accelerations should have a probability density function which is
approximately Gaussian with a crest factor (the ratio of the peak to the root mean square value
[RMS]) of at least 2.5.

These three tests, combined with pre- and post- electrical, fluidic, control, and monitoring tests
complete the standard’s required shock and vibration testing protocol for hydrogen components for
rail environment use. This testing process helps assess the functionality of these components within
acceptable limits for rail applications.

6. CONCLUSION
As the North American rail industry considers hydrogen as a fuel, the importance of the safety and
performance of onboard fuel storage technologies has emerged as an area of focus. This report,
prepared by Sandia National Laboratories, identified shock and vibration testing protocols and
requirements for type IV composite tanks used for hydrogen storage in rail applications. The
primary objectives of this project included providing background information on the design and
characteristics of these tanks, highlighting the differences between composite and steel tanks, and
outlining the specific testing requirements necessary for ensuring their reliability in rail
environments.

Type IV composite tanks, made from carbon fiber reinforced polymers, offer several advantages
over traditional steel tanks, including reduced weight, enhanced corrosion resistance, and better
performance under high-pressure conditions. However, understanding their inherent differences is
essential since composite tanks exhibit unique responses to various stresses and loading conditions.
This report details the types of loads that composite tanks encounter, including static, harmonic, and
dynamic forces, which are particularly relevant in rail transport scenarios.

Rail-specific shock and vibration conditions should be considered when selecting components such
as fuel tanks. These conditions can lead to specific failure mechanisms in composite tanks, including
fiber-matrix shearing, matrix cracking, debonding, fiber rupture, liner collapse, liner degradation, and
material oxidation. These failures can be minimized or prevented by optimizing manufacturing
processes via techniques such as fiber-prestressing and careful control of variables like winding
patterns, cycle time, temperature, cure rates, and overwrap porosity.

Various testing methods for composite samples and tanks, including visual inspections, pressure
testing, non-destructive testing, and destructive testing, are outlined as part of the post-
manufacturing protocol. Relevant safety codes and standards that govern the design, construction,
and testing of composite tanks were provided. ISO 11623 provides an important sequential
procedure for gaseous and liquid composite tank testing, while ISO 11119-3, ISO 11515, Boiler and
Pressure Vessel Code Section X, and CGA C-1 provide requirements for various types of composite
tank tests. These standards are important in ensuring compliance with hydrogen-, composite tank-,
and rail-specific requirements.

Shock and vibration testing requirements for type IV composite tanks in rail applications were
ascertained by reviewing standards and can be derived by combining standards from the
International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC), particularly IEC 63341-2 and IEC 61373, which
together provide a three step process for assessing the integrity of hydrogen storage systems under
operational shock and vibration rail conditions.

Ongoing work may be needed between stakeholders in the space, for example, tank manufacturers,
regulators, rail owners, and municipalities, to determine if current composite tank tests are
appropriate for rail applications. Ongoing research to refine testing methodologies to accurately
reflect the complexities of rail environments would be beneficial toward ensuring that hydrogen
tanks and other associated components are used and maintained reliably and safely in rail
environments. Additional research into the long-term behavior of composite materials under rail-
specific stresses is useful for advancing the safe integration of hydrogen as a locomotive fuel.
