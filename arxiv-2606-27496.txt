Abstract
. We introduce and study ( 2 , 3 ) (2,3) -palintropic algebras, a class of commutative algebras defined by the identity ( x 3 ) 2 − ( x 2 ) 3 = 0 (x^{3})^{2}-(x^{2})^{3}=0 . This specific relation is the simplest generator of the 2 2 -dimensional space of minimal-degree evanescent identities in degree 6 6 , and encompasses several well-studied structures, including Jordan and medial algebras. The primary motivation for investigating these algebras lies in their trivial Peirce polynomials, which removes a priori restrictions on the spectrum of the multiplication operator associated with an idempotent. In this paper, we review and further develop the theory of Peirce operators, Peirce polynomials, and second-order linearizations. We demonstrate that despite the triviality of the Peirce polynomial, any idempotent c c admits well-behaved, explicit fusion rules for multiplication between its λ \lambda -Peirce spaces for λ ≠ 1 2 \lambda\neq\tfrac{1}{2} . Furthermore, we prove that multiplication by such an idempotent always constitutes an algebra homomorphism. Finally, we present concrete examples of ( 2 , 3 ) (2,3) -palintropic algebras and provide applications of these algebraic structures to commutative polynomial maps.



1.
Introduction and motivations

The usual approach to studying a class of algebras defined by a polynomial identity
P=0
is to use the Peirce decomposition and corresponding fusion rules associated with an idempotent in an algebra satisfying
P
. These arise from linearization of the defining identity
P
. This approach has been used successfully to study and classify associative algebras, power associative algebras, Jordan algebras, pseudocomposition algebras, Bernstein genetic algebras, Hsiang algebras
[
8
]
, and many other classes of algebras satisfying identities of low degrees. In
[
22
]
, the case of a general univariate identity (in one formal variable) was considered with the goal of identifying spectral properties of idempotents in algebras satisfying an identity
P
that depend only on the specific identity
P
.

Linearization yields the
Peirce operator
,
\rho(P;q)
, a
\mathbb{k}
-linear map from the
\mathbb{k}
-algebra of univariate nonassociative commutative polynomials in
x
(identities) to the polynomial ring
\mathbb{k}[q]
(see Definition
2.4
). The algebraic set

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathbb{k}}(P)=\{\alpha\in\mathbb{k}:\rho(P;\alpha)=0\}

constitutes the
abstract Peirce spectrum
over
\mathbb{k}
of the identity
P
(see Definition
2.17
). The Peirce spectrum of any idempotent
c
contained in a commutative
\mathbb{k}
-algebra satisfying
P
is contained in the abstract Peirce spectrum of
P
, although the containment can be proper. In this sense the abstract Peirce spectrum of
P
provides a priori constraints on the Peirce spectra of idempotents in commutative algebras satisfying
P
.

However, as was observed in
[
18
, p. 230]
, it can occur that
\rho(P;q)
is identically zero. In this case the abstract Peirce spectrum is all of
\mathbb{k}
,
\sigma_{\mathbb{k}}(P)=\mathbb{k}
. In
[
22
]
, an identity was called
degenerated
if its Peirce polynomial
\rho(P;q)
vanishes identically. The same notion was studied by Varro
[
25
]
in a study of baric algebras, where such identities were called
evanescent
, and this terminology is adopted here.

Varro
[
25
, Proposition
30
]
(see Lemma
3.4
) showed the set
\mathcal{E}\subset\mathbb{k}(\{x\})
of evanescent identities is an ideal and so must be the kernel of a homomorphism to some algebra.
Lemma
2.12
shows that the polynomial ring
\mathbb{k}[q]
with the product

f\star g=q(f(\tfrac{1}{2})g+g(\tfrac{1}{2})f)

is an (infinite-dimensional) medial algebra in the sense defined in
[
13
,
23
]
and recalled below and Theorem
2.13
reinterprets the Peirce operator as a homomorphism from
\mathbb{k}(\{x\})
to
(\mathbb{k}[q],\star)
.

By their definition, evanescent identities yield no constraints on the values of Peirce eigenvalues of idempotents in algebras satisfying
P
, so such an identity can be said to be
spectrally unconstrained
.
For evanescent identities, the standard approach using the Peirce decomposition encounters difficulties, and it is necessary to revisit the basic methodology. One of the main points of what follows is nonetheless, the Peirce spectrum of a commutative algebra satisfying an evanescent identity
P
must satisfy multiplicative fusion rules determined by the second-order linearization of the identity.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{k}
be the vector space of degree
k
homogeneous evanescent identities. Varro proved
[
25
]
that the smallest degree
k
for which
\mathcal{E}_{k}
is nonzero is

and
\mathcal{E}_{6}
has dimension
2
and is spanned by identities
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
defined in (
2
) and (
67
) (Proposition
5.1
). The identity
E_{1}
is the main focus of this article in part because it is in some sense the simplest univariate evanescent identity. As is explained next, the
1
-dimensional subspace of
\mathcal{E}_{6}
spanned by

is distinguished by properties of the second-order linearization of
P

Fusion rules for Peirce eigenspaces of an idempotent in an algebra satisfying
P
are controlled by the second order linearization of
P
. The second-order Peirce polynomial
\mathfrak{D}(P;q,r,s)\in\mathbb{k}[q,r,s]
is defined from the second order linearization in a manner analogous to how the Peirce operator
\rho(P;q)
is defined from the first order linearization (see Definition
4.2
). If a commutative
\mathbb{k}
-algebra
\mathbb{A}
satifies
P
and
\alpha,\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{k}
are eigenvalues of an idempotent

such that the product of
\lambda
and
\mu

\alpha

\alpha,\lambda,\mu
must be roots of
\mathfrak{D}(P;q,r,s)
. The
abstract fusion rules
of

are given by the algebraic set

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}_{\mathbb{k}}(P)

\displaystyle=\{(\alpha,\lambda,\mu)\in\sigma_{\mathbb{k}}(P)^{3}:\mathfrak{D}(P;\alpha,\lambda,\mu)=0\}.

(See Definition
4.7
.) By definition, the fusion rules associated with any idempotent
c
in a particular algebra satisfying
P
are constrained by the abstract fusion rules of
P
in a manner exactly analogous to how the abstract Peirce spectrum of
P
constrains the Peirce spectrum of
c
.

It can happen that

has the
second-order evanescence property

\mathscr{F}_{\mathbb{k}}(P)=\mathbb{k}^{3}
. Example

gives an example of a homogeneous degree
11

that is both evanescent and satisfies

. However, it turns out that no homogeneous degree
6

\mathscr{F}_{\mathbb{k}}(P)
is a proper subset of
\mathbb{k}^{3}
for all such

. Moreover, in this case

is always at most quadratic in
\alpha
and it is linear in
\alpha
exactly when

is a multiple of

(Proposition

and to motivating their study from other perspectives with the goal of better understanding how abstract fusion rules constrain behavior even for evanescent identities.

A general nonassociative commutative monomial in an indeterminate
x
is denoted by

, in which the
monomial power

\alpha
is a formal symbol that indicates the abstract (non-planar) binary rooted tree corresponding with the monomial

and coding the manner of parenthesizing the products appearing in

of

and

for

The subspace of evanescent identities is surprisingly large. In the Peirce polynomial

of the monomial

the coefficient of

is the number of leaves of height

In particular no monomial is evanescent, but the difference of monomials is evanescent if the corresponding trees have the property that the number of leaves of a given height in either tree is the same for all heights. This observation yields perhaps the simplest examples of evanescent identities,

(1)

(x^{\alpha})^{\beta}-(x^{\beta})^{\alpha}=0,

for monomial powers
\alpha
and

of degree at least

(this is a special case of Proposition

). A commutative algebra
\mathbb{A}
is

-palintropic
if it satisfies (
1

, an algebra
\mathbb{A}
is
(p,q)
-
palintropic
if any

satisfies

(x^{p})^{q}=(x^{q})^{p}
.
The simplest homogeneous evanescent identity is the previously mentioned degree
6
identity

(2)

E_{1}:=(x^{3})^{2}-(x^{2})^{3}=0,

that is the particular case
(p,q)=(2,3)
.
The medial algebras studied in
[
13
,
23
]
satisfy

, and this is another motivation for studying
(2,3)
-palintropic algebras.

Next the methodology of the Peirce decomposition is recalled.
Here
\mathbb{A}=(\mathbb{A},\cdot)
denotes a commutative (not necessarily associative) algebra over a field
\mathbb{k}
of characteristic not
2
with multiplication
\cdot
. The assumption
\operatorname{char}\mathbb{k}\neq 2
is supposed implicitly throughout the paper because
\tfrac{1}{2}
appears constantly.
Although
\mathbb{A}
is allowed to be infinite-dimensional, when it is finite-dimensional its dimension will be denoted
\dim\mathbb{A}=n
.
Throughout the paper, when the algebra product
\cdot
is clear from context, the notation indicating it is omitted and the product of elements
x,y\in\mathbb{A}
is indicated by juxtaposition,
xy
, instead of
x\cdot y
. Notation such as
\cdot
indicating explicitly the product is used when it is helpful to distinguish various different products under consideration.
The operator
L(a):\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{A}
of multiplication by
a
is defined by
L(a)x=ax
.
An element
c\in\mathbb{A}
is an idempotent if
c^{2}=c
. The set of nonzero idempotents of
\mathbb{A}
is denoted
\Idm(\mathbb{A})
.

If
\mathbb{A}
is finite-dimensional, for
a\in\mathbb{A}
the set roots of
\det(L(a)-\lambda\operatorname{Id})=0
in an algebraic closure
\bar{\mathbb{k}}
of
\mathbb{k}
is denoted
\sigma(a)
and the
Peirce spectrum
of the algebra
\mathbb{A}
is defined to be

\sigma(\mathbb{A})=\bigcup_{c\in\Idm(\mathbb{A})}\sigma(c).

As the existence of idempotents depends on the arithmetic properties of the underlying field, the spectrum
\sigma(\mathbb{A})
is sensitive to the underlying field. For
c\in\Idm(\mathbb{A})
, the eigenspace
\mathbb{A}_{c}(\lambda)=\ker(L(c)-\lambda\operatorname{Id})
is called the
Peirce subspace
associated with
\lambda\in\sigma(c)
.

If a
\mathbb{k}
-algebra is given by an identity
P
, then the Peirce spectrum of
\mathbb{A}
is contained in the abstract Peirce spectrum of
P
,

\sigma(\mathbb{A})\subset\sigma_{\mathbb{k}}(P),

so that the identity imposes a priori restrictions on the Peirce spectrum
\sigma(\mathbb{A})
provided
P
is not evanescent.

Because the degree of the Peirce polynomial
\rho(P;q)
of a homogeneous identity
P
is less than or equal to
\deg P-1
(Corollary
2.3
), if the Peirce polynomial
\rho(P;q)
is nonzero, the cardinality of the abstract Peirce spectrum of
P
is at most
\deg P-1
. In particular, when a commutative algebra satisfies an identity
P
that is not evanescent, its Peirce spectrum is finite, with cardinality bounded by
\deg P-1
, although in general it can be still smaller (the concept of
Peirce complexity
introduced in
[
7
]
makes precise the possible gap). This is in sharp contrast to the situation for evanescent identities, for which the cardinality of abstract Peirce spectrum is unbounded.

Practically, the abstract Peirce spectrum is extracted from the defining algebraic identity by linearization followed by specialization to an algebra idempotent. Precisely, replacing
x
in the linearization of
P(x)
by an idempotent
c
and simplifying the resulting expression yields the Peirce polynomial evaluated at
q=L(c)
; see Proposition
2.15
for the precise statement.
Example
1.2
illustrates this process in one of the simplest cases, that of power associative algebras.

Example 1.2
.

An algebra
\mathbb{A}
is power-associative if the subalgebra generated by any element
x
is associative.
By
[
1
, Theorem 1]
, if
\operatorname{char}\mathbb{k}
is prime to 30 then a commutative algebra
\mathbb{A}
is power-associative if and only if it satisfies the degree
4
identity

(3)

A(x):=x^{4}-x^{2}x^{2}=0.

In this case, linearization of (
3
) yields

(4)

2L(x)^{3}+L(x)L(x^{2})+L(x^{3})-4L(x^{2})L(x)=0,

and conversely, (
4
) implies (
3
).
For

equal to an idempotent
c\in\Idm(\mathbb{A})
, (
4

(5)

(2L(c)-1)(L(c)-1)L(c)=2L(c)^{3}-3L(c)^{2}+L(c)=0.

The polynomial in

appearing in (

\rho(A;q)=2q^{3}-3q^{2}+q,

is the Peirce polynomial of the identity

\sigma_{\mathbb{k}}(A)=\{0,\tfrac{1}{2},1\}
.
An associative algebra provides a simple example of a power associative algebra for which the Peirce spectrurm
\sigma(c)
of any nonzero idempotent is contained properly in the abstract Peirce spectrum, for
\tfrac{1}{2}
is not in the spectrum of an idempotent of an associative algebra.

If

is moreover diagonalizable, then there holds the corresponding Peirce decomposition

Utilizing the second linearization of (

describing the containments of products of Peirce eigenspaces,

(6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{c}(\lambda)\mathbb{A}_{c}(\lambda)\subset\mathbb{A}_{c}(\lambda),

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{\lambda}(c)\mathbb{A}_{c}(\tfrac{1}{2})\subset\mathbb{A}_{c}(\tfrac{1}{2}),

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{c}(\tfrac{1}{2})\mathbb{A}_{c}(\tfrac{1}{2})\subset\mathbb{A}_{0}(c)\oplus\mathbb{A}_{1}(c).

for
\lambda\in\{0,1\}
(see
[

.)
∎

The study of algebras satisfying irreducible identities of degree greater than
4
is somewhat limited, in part because of the complexity of the Peirce decomposition methodology, although baric algebras and train algebras are some well known exceptions. One example is the algebras satisfying the degree

In this case the eigenvalue

In practice, a focus on low degree identities hides the phenomenon of evanescence, as the lowest degree of a homogeneous univariate evanescent identity is
6

A different motivation for studying
(2,3)
-palintropic algebras is an analogy relating associative and medial algebras in which
(2,3)
-palintropic algebras are analogues of power associative algebras.

In a commutative algebra the associator
[x,y,z]=(xy)z-x(yz)
measures the failure of equality of the products corresponding with different labelings of the abstract binary rooted tree corresponding with the third principal power. Similarly, the mediator of Definition
1.3
measures the failure of equality of the products corresponding with different labelings of the abstract binary rooted tree corresponding with the monomial
(x^{2})^{2}
.

Definition 1.3
.

Let
\mathbb{A}
be a commutative
\mathbb{k}
-algebra (possibly infinite-dimensional). The
mediator
is the
\mathbb{k}
-multilinear map from
\otimes^{4}\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{A}
defined for

by

(8)

\displaystyle[x,y,z,w]:=(xy)(zw)-(xz)(yw).

The vanishing of the associator defines the class of associative algebras. The vanishing of the mediator yields the following class of algebras. A commutative algebra
\mathbb{A}
is
medial

[
13
,
23
]
if satisfies the identity

(9)

[x,y,z,w]=0.

Consider evaluating the associator or mediator on monomials in
x
in a commutative algebra. The lowest degree case for which the associator of various monomials in
x
is not automatically zero is
[x,x,x^{2}]
, which equals
(x^{2})^{2}-x^{4}
and whose vanishing is equivalent to power associativity if the characteristic is prime to
30
. Similarly, the lowest degree case for which the mediator of monomials in
x
is not automatically zero is
[x,x,x^{2},x^{2}]
, which equals
(x^{2})^{3}-(x^{3})^{2}
and whose vanishing characterizes the
(2,3)
-palintropic algebras. Thus treating the associator and mediator in formally the same way yields a parallelism between associativity and mediality and between power associativity and
(2,3)
-palintropicity.

A further connection is seen by considering unital commutative algebras. Theorem 5.5 in
[
23
]
states that the category of special medial algebras is isomorphic to the category of unital calibrated associative algebras.
A bit more prosaically, evaluating the mediator on a unit

shows that
[x,y,z,e]=[x,y,z]
. In particular, unital medial algebras are associative. Proposition

shows that, analogously, unital
(2,3)
-palintropic are power associative.

The preceding discussion shows several senses in which
(2,3)
-palintropic algebras are a natural generalization of power associative algebras. Consideration of examples further confirms this perspective.

A commutative algebra
\mathbb{A}

Define two subclasses of medial algebras, the first of which is a subclass of the other. An algebra is

\rho(2x^{5}-3(x^{2})^{2}x+x^{3}x^{2},q)=4q^{4}-8q^{3}+5q^{2}-q=q(q-1)(2q-1)^{2}.

By Proposition
2.15
, this implies that the Peirce spectrum of any commutative algebra
\mathbb{A}
satisfying (
7
) is contained in
\{0,\tfrac{1}{2},1\}
, a property basic in the study of Jordan and power-associative algebras which both satisfy (
7
).
Note, however, that (
38
) shows that the Peirce polynomial of the Osborn identity has a multiple root
\tfrac{1}{2}
. It follows from
[
22
]
that, in this situation, the fusion rules need not coincide with those of Jordan algebras.

Here the abstract Peirce spectrum has been defined only to be the algebraic set comprising the roots of the Peirce polynomial, without taking into account their multiplicities. The Peirce polynomial of the Osborn identity illustrates that it is necessary to take into account the extra information given by multiplicities. This could be incorporated by defining the abstract Peirce spectrum as a scheme, but as the issue of multiplicity is not explored further here, this has not been developed here.
∎

3.
Evanescent identities

Definition 3.1
.

A nonassociative polynomial
P\in\mathbb{k}(\{x\})
is
evanescent
if
\rho(P;q)
is identically zero.

The idea of evanescence originates in the study of Peirce polynomials in
[
22
]
, where such polynomials were called
degenerated
. The terminology
evanescent
is taken from
[
25
]
.

The goal in what follows is to make a detailed analysis of certain specific evanescent polynomials, without pursuing the general theory or addressing broader natural questions, such as the classification of all evanescent polynomials; see, however,
[
25
]
for some results in this direction.

Lemma 3.2
.

If
P\in\mathbb{k}(\{x\})
is evanescent, then
P(1)=0
.

Proof.

By Corollary
2.9
,
P(1)=\rho(P;\tfrac{1}{2})=0
, the last equality by evanescence of
P
.
∎

Example 3.3
.

Lemma
3.2
means that the base field
\mathbb{k}
, viewed as a commutative algebra, satisfies the identity
P=0
for any homogeneous evanescent

(
[

, Proposition

)

\mathcal{E}:=\ker\rho\subset\mathbb{k}(\{x\})

This follows from (

and that, by Lemma

, for

there holds

.
∎

.

&=(q^{2}-q)\left(Q_{1}(1)\Delta(P_{2},R_{3},q)+Q_{2}(1)\Delta(P_{3},R_{1},q)+Q_{3}(1)\Delta(P_{1},R_{2},q)\right.\\

where

,

, and

is evanescent if the evaluations

The formula (

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \rho(&\Tri(P,Q,R);q)\\

A special feature of

is that it admits the composition of univariate nonassociative polynomials

defined by

.

Lemma 3.12
.

For any
P,Q\in\mathbb{k}(\{x\})
,

(48)

\displaystyle\rho(P\circ Q;q)=\rho(P;q)\rho(Q;q).

Proof.

Because
P
is a linear combination of nonassociative monomials

and

is
\mathbb{k}
-linear, it suffices to check the claim for

, namely that

\displaystyle\rho(Q(x)^{\alpha};q)=\rho(x^{\alpha};q)\rho(Q;q)

holds for any
x^{\alpha}
and
Q\in\mathbb{k}(\{x\})
. This can be shown by induction on the degree as follows. The claim is valid for
x^{\alpha}=x
because
\rho(x;q)=1
. Suppose that (
49
) is valid for all nonassociative powers
x^{\beta}
of degree less or equal
\weight(x^{\beta})=p\geq 1
and suppose that
\weight(x^{\alpha})=p+1
. Then
x^{\alpha}=x^{\beta}x^{\gamma}
, where
\weight(x^{\beta})\leq p
and
\weight(x^{\gamma})\leq p
, hence by the inductive hypothesis,
\rho(Q(x)^{\beta});q)=\rho(x^{\beta};q)\rho(Q;q)
and
\rho(Q(x)^{\gamma});q)=\rho(x^{\gamma};q)\rho(Q;q)
, and so, by (
23
) and (

),

(50)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \rho(Q(x)^{\alpha};q)&=\rho(Q(x)^{\beta}Q(x)^{\gamma};q)\\
&=q\left(\rho(Q(x)^{\beta};q)+\rho(Q(x)^{\gamma};q)\right)\\
&=q\left(\rho(x^{\beta};q)\rho(Q;q)+\rho(x^{\gamma};q)\rho(Q;q)\right)\\
&=q\left(\rho(x^{\beta};q)+\rho(x^{\gamma};q)\right)\rho(Q;q)\\
&=\rho(x^{\alpha};q)\rho(Q;q)\end{aligned}

which shows (
49
) by induction.
∎

Proposition 3.13
.

For any
P,Q\in\mathbb{k}(\{x\})
,

is evanescent.

Proof.

By Lemma

,
\rho(P\circ Q;q)=\rho(P;q)\rho(Q;q)=\rho(Q\circ P;q)
.
∎

(Palintropic evanescence)

by the special case of Proposition

and

[

.

.

and

for any nonassociative power

By Lemma

,
\rho(P(x^{\alpha}),q)=\rho(P,q)\rho(x^{\alpha},q)=\rho(P(x)^{\alpha};q)

.

4.
The second order Peirce operator and fusion rules

The second-order Peirce operator defined in
[
22
]
arises from evaluating the second linearization of a nonassociative univariate polynomial on elements of a commutative algebra one of which is an idempotent. It can be interpreted as providing fusion rules for eigenvectors of multipliction by this idempotent. Theorem
4.1
defines the second-order Peirce operator on
\mathbb{k}(\{x\})
via a recursive definition as in the definition in Lemma
2.2
of the first-order Peirce operator. The analogue for the second-order Peirce operator of the formula (
20
) is the formula (
52
), which shows how to calculate the second-order Peirce operator of a nonassociative monomial directly from the associated binary rooted tree.

For vertices
l_{1}
and
l_{2}
of an abstract binary rooted tree the
nearest common ancestor

\operatorname{NCA}(l_{1},l_{2})
is the root of the minimal subtree of
T
that contains both
l_{1}
and
l_{2}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

There is a unique
\mathbb{k}
-linear map
\mathfrak{D}:\mathbb{k}(\{x\})\times\mathbb{k}^{3}\to\mathbb{k}[q,r,s]
satisfying

(51)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathrm{(i)}\,\,&\mathfrak{D}(x;q,r,s)=0,\\
\mathrm{(ii)}\,\,&\mathfrak{D}(x^{\beta}x^{\gamma};q,r,s)=q(\mathfrak{D}(x^{\beta};q,r,s)+\mathfrak{D}(x^{\gamma};q,r,s))\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad+\rho(x^{\beta};r)\rho(x^{\gamma};s)+\rho(x^{\beta};s)\rho(x^{\gamma};r),\end{aligned}

for monomials
x^{\beta},x^{\gamma}\in\mathbb{k}(\{x\})
. Moreover, for the monomial
x^{\alpha}\in\mathbb{k}(\{x\})
associated with the abstract binary rooted tree
T
having set of leaves
L(T)
,
\mathfrak{D}(x^{\alpha};q,r,s)
equals the sum over all unordered pairs of distinct leaves,

(52)

\mathfrak{D}(x^{\alpha};q,r_{1},r_{2})=\sum_{\{l_{1},l_{2}\}\subseteq L(T)}q^{d(l_{1},l_{2})}\left(r_{1}^{\height(l_{1}|l_{2})-1}r_{2}^{\height(l_{2}|l_{1})-1}+r_{2}^{\height(l_{1}|l_{2})-1}r_{1}^{\height(l_{2}|l_{1})-1}\right),

where
d(l_{1},l_{2})=\height(\operatorname{NCA}(l_{1},l_{2}))
denotes the height of the nearest common ancestor
\operatorname{NCA}(l_{1},l_{2})
and
\height(l_{1}|l_{2}):=\height(l_{1})-d(l_{1},l_{2})
is the height of
l_{1}
relative to
l_{2}
.

Observe that the dependence on the indeterminate
q
is qualitatively different from the dependence on
r
or
s
.

Proof.

The map
\mathfrak{D}
is defined by extending (
52
)
\mathbb{k}
-linearly to
\mathbb{k}(\{x\})
. It suffices to check that
\mathfrak{D}
so defined satisfies (
51
). This is shown by induction on the degree of the binary tree
T
. In the proof it is convenient to write
\mathfrak{D}(x^{\alpha})
in place of
\mathfrak{D}(x^{\alpha};q,r,s)
and to write tree for abstract binary rooted tree.

If
T
is the trivial tree representing the monomial
x
then the leaf set contains only
x
, so the set of unordered pairs of distinct leaves is empty and the sum in (
52
) and the initial condition
\mathfrak{D}(x)=0
is satisfied.

Suppose that
T
has height at least one and suppose the claim holds for all subtrees of degree lower than that of
T
. Then
T
can be written in a unique way as the graft,
T=A\vee B
, of two subtrees
A
and
B
each of degree lower than
T
. Denote by
\binom{L(T)}{2}
the set of unordered pairs of leaves of
T
. The set of leaves of
T
is the disjoint union of the sets of leaves of its subtrees:
L(T)=L(A)\cup L(B)
. Consequently,

(53)

\displaystyle\binom{L(T)}{2}=\binom{L(A)}{2}\cup\binom{L(B)}{2}\cup\Big\{\{l,r\}\mid l\in L(A),r\in L(B)\Big\}.

Let
x^{\alpha}
and
x^{\beta}
be the monomials corresponding with
A
and
B
so that the monomial corresponding with
T
is
x^{\alpha}x^{\beta}
. The inductive hypothesis implies that (
52
) holds for each of
x^{\alpha}
and
x^{\beta}
. It will be used to calculate the right-hand side of the recursive formula (
51
) and this will be shown to equal (
52
) for
T
.

(i) Consider pairs of leaves contained in the subtree
A
,
\{l_{1},l_{2}\}\subseteq L(A)
. The root of
A
has height
1
relative to the root of
T
, so the height of the nearest common ancestor of
l_{1},l_{2}
relative to the root of
T
is one more than its height relative to the root of
A
,
d_{T}(l_{1},l_{2})=d_{A}(l_{1},l_{2})+1
. Similarly, the height in
T
of a leaf of
A
is one more than its height in
A
,
\height_{T}(v)=\height_{A}(v)+1
. Consequently,

(54)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \height_{T}(l_{1}|l_{2})-1&=\height_{T}(l_{1})-d_{T}(l_{1},l_{2})-1=(\height_{A}(l_{1})+1)-(d_{A}(l_{1},l_{2})+1)-1\\
&=\height_{A}(l_{1})-d_{A}(l_{1},l_{2})-1=\height_{A}(l_{1}|l_{2})-1,\end{aligned}

so the relative height of
l_{1}
with respect to
l_{2}
is the same in
A
as in
T
, and the same is true with
l_{2}
and
l_{1}
interchanged.
Therefore, the relative path distances from the local ancestor to the leaves remain invariant.
Multiplying by
q
yields
q^{d_{A}(l_{1},l_{2})}
to
q^{d_{A}(l_{1},l_{2})+1}=q^{d_{T}(l_{1},l_{2})}
.
By the inductive hypothesis and (
54
)

(55)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} q\mathfrak{D}&(x^{\alpha};q,r_{1},r_{2})\\
&=q\sum_{\{l_{1},l_{2}\}\subseteq L(A)}q^{d_{A}(l_{1},l_{2})}\big(r_{1}^{\height_{A}(l_{1}|l_{2})-1}r_{2}^{\height_{A}(l_{2}|l_{1})-1}+r_{2}^{\height_{A}(l_{1}|l_{2})-1}r_{1}^{\height_{A}(l_{2}|l_{1})-1}\big)\\
&=\sum_{\{l_{1},l_{2}\}\subseteq L(T)}q^{d_{T}(l_{1},l_{2})}\left(r_{1}^{\height_{T}(l_{1}|l_{2})-1}r_{2}^{\height_{T}(l_{2}|l_{1})-1}+r_{2}^{\height_{T}(l_{1}|l_{2})-1}r_{1}^{\height_{T}(l_{2}|l_{1})-1}\right).\end{aligned}

The same argument establishes the parallel formula with
B
and
\beta
in place of
A
and
\alpha
.

(ii) Consider pairs
\{l_{1},l_{2}\}
such that
l_{1}\in L(A)
and
l_{2}\in L(B)
. Because these leaves belong to separate subtrees, their nearest common ancestor is the root of
T
, so that
d_{T}(l_{1},l_{2})=0
and
q^{d_{T}(l_{1},l_{2})}=1
. The heights of
l_{1}
and
l_{2}
in
A
and
B
are one less than their heights in
T
, so that
\height_{A}(l_{1})=\height_{T}(l_{1})-1
and
\height_{B}(l_{2})=\height_{T}(l_{2})-1
. Consequently
\height_{T}(l_{1}|l_{2})=\height_{T}(l_{1})-d_{T}(l_{1},l_{2})=\height_{A}(l_{1})+1
and similarly
\height_{T}(l_{2}|l_{1})=\height_{B}(l_{2})+1
.
By the formula (
20
) for the Peirce operator
\rho
,

\displaystyle\rho(A;r)\rho(B;s)

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum_{l_{1}\in L(A)}r^{\height_{A}(l_{1})}\Big)\Big(\sum_{l_{2}\in L(B)}s^{\height_{B}(l_{2})}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l_{1}\in L(A),l_{2}\in L(B)}r^{\height_{T}(l_{1}|l_{2})-1}s^{\height_{T}(l_{2}|l_{1})-1}.

Symmetrizing this in
r
and
s
yields

(56)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \rho(A;r)&\rho(B;s)+\rho(A;s)\rho(B;r)\\
&=\sum_{l_{1}\in L(A),l_{2}\in L(B)}q^{d_{T}(l_{1},l_{2})}\Big(r^{\height_{T}(l_{1}|l_{2})-1}s^{\height_{T}(l_{2}|l_{1})-1}+s^{\height_{T}(l_{1}|l_{2})-1}r^{\height_{T}(l_{2}|l_{1})-1}\Big).\end{aligned}

In conjunction with (
53
), summing (
55
) and its parallel with
B
in place of
A
with (
56
) yields that
q(\mathfrak{D}(x^{\beta};q,r,s)+\mathfrak{D}(x^{\gamma};q,r,s))+\rho(x^{\beta};r)\rho(x^{\gamma};s)+\rho(x^{\beta};s)\rho(x^{\gamma};r)
equals the sum on the left-hand side of (
52
). This completes the inductive step and shows the claim.
∎

Definition 4.2
.

\mathfrak{D}(P;q,r,s)
is the
second order Peirce operator
of
P\in\mathbb{k}(\{x\})
.

Corollary 4.3
.

\deg_{q}\mathfrak{D}(P;q,r,s)\leq\deg_{x}(P)-2
.

Proof.

This follows from (
52
) because the height of the nearest common ancestor of two leaves in an abstract binary rooted tree is at most
2
less than the number of leaves of the tree (which equals the degree of the corresponding monomial) .
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

For all
P,Q\in\mathbb{k}(\{x\})
there holds

(57)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathfrak{D}(PQ;q,r,s)&=q(Q(1)\mathfrak{D}(P;q,r,s)+P(1)\mathfrak{D}(Q;q,r,s))\\
&+\rho(P;r)\rho(Q;s)+\rho(P;s)\rho(Q;r)\end{aligned}

Proof.

This follows from (
51
) by
\mathbb{k}
-linearity of the second order Peirce operator.
∎

9.
Application to commuting and integrable polynomial maps

The main point of this section is that commutative algebras satisfying evanescent identities of a certain kind yield examples of integrable polynomial mappings in the sense defined in
[
26
]
.

Definition 9.1
.

A polynomial map of degree at least one
p:\mathbb{k}^{n}\to\mathbb{k}^{n}
is
integrable
if there exists a polynomial map
q:\mathbb{k}^{n}\to\mathbb{k}^{n}
of degree at least one such that:

•

p\circ q=q\circ p
;

•

the sets of iterates of
p
and
q
are disjoint.

Integrable maps play an important role in the theory of dynamical systems, as they exhibit an unusual degree of symmetry. For
n=1
, integrable polynomials were completely classified by Julia, Fatou, and Ritt in the early 1920s: any such map is linearly conjugate either to
z^{n}
or to a Chebyshev polynomial; see
[
3
]
for this and references. There is an analogous construction in higher dimensions, related to Lie algebras via an action of an affine Weyl group
[
14
,
2
]
; for further information and references, see also
[
21
]
.

Given a commutative
\mathbb{k}
-algebra and a basis
\mathscr{B}=\{e_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
of
\mathbb{A}
, each
P\in\mathbb{k}(\{x\})
determines a polynomial map
\Phi(P):\mathbb{k}^{n}\to\mathbb{k}^{n}
whose components
\Phi(P)(t)^{i}
in the basis
\mathscr{B}
are given by

(127)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Phi(P)(t)^{i}e_{i}=P(\sum_{i=1}^{n}t^{i}e_{i})

for
(t^{1},\dots,t^{n})\in\mathbb{k}^{n}
. Although
\Phi(P)
depends on the choice of basis
\mathscr{B}
, its equivalence class under conjugation by an element of
GL(n,\mathbb{k})
does not, and it is generally convenient to omit notation indicating dependence on
\mathscr{B}
.

By Proposition
3.13
, for any
P,Q\in\mathbb{k}(\{x\})
the identity
P\circ Q-Q\circ P
is evanescent. If
\mathbb{A}
is a commutative
\mathbb{k}
-algebra satisfying
P\circ Q-Q\circ P
then polynomial maps
\Phi(P)
and
\Phi(Q)
of
\mathbb{k}^{n}
induced by
P
and
Q
and the choice of basis
\mathscr{B}
commute in the sense that

(128)

\displaystyle\Phi(P)\circ\Phi(Q)=\Phi(Q)\circ\Phi(P).

However, it is not immediate that the sets of iterates of
\Phi(P)
and
\Phi(Q)
be disjoint, so it need not be the case that they are integrable polynomial mappings. On the other hand, for the simplest case where
P(x)=x^{\alpha}
and
Q(x)=x^{\beta}
are nonassociative monomials of relatively prime degrees, it is not hard to show that the resulting polynomials are integrable, and this motivates focusing on that particular case in what follows.

This means that a commutative algebra satisfying
P\circ Q-Q\circ P
and such that iterates of
\Phi(P)
and
\Phi(Q)
are disjoint can be viewed as a kind of
quantization
of the integrable pair
\Phi(P)
and
\Phi(Q)
. What is not obvious is what the disjoint iterates condition means at the level of
P
and
Q
. Alternatively, an evanescent identity plus some condition generalizing the disjointness of the iterates of
\Phi(P)
and
\Phi(Q)
can be viewed as a kind of
integrable nonassociative algebra
.

The remainder of this section focuses on the case of
(\alpha,\beta)
-palintropic algebras for abstract binary rooted trees
\alpha
and
\beta
. In this case the nonassociative polynomials
P
and
Q
are the monomial power maps
P(x)=x^{\alpha}
and
Q(x)=x^{\beta}
and there are written
\Phi_{\alpha}=\Phi(P)
and
\Phi_{\beta}=\Phi(Q)
(for some choices of
\mathbb{A}
and basis
\mathscr{B}
). (In the case of the principal powers
P(x)=x^{p}
and
Q(x)=x^{p}
, there are written
\Phi_{p}=\Phi(P)
and
\Phi_{q}=\Phi(Q)
.) By assumption these satisfy
\Phi_{\alpha}\circ\Phi_{\beta}=\Phi_{\beta}\circ\Phi_{\alpha}
.
Proposition
9.2
shows that these polynomial maps are integrable provided the degrees of
\alpha
and
\beta
are relatively prime.

Recall that
|\alpha|
means
\deg(x^{\alpha})
.

Proposition 9.2

Let

and

A homogeneous quadratic polynomial map determines a commutative algebra whose idempotents are the fixed points of the map. Polarization associates with a homogeneous quadratic polynomial mapping

(133)

W_{2}(x,y,z)=(x^{2}-2yz-4z^{2},y^{2}-2x^{2}+4yz+4z^{2},z^{2}):\mathbb{k}^{3}\to\mathbb{k}^{3}

By (
131
), this gives the multiplication
\ast
on
\mathbb{k}^{3}
defined explicitly by

(134)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &(x_{1},y_{1},z_{1})\ast(x_{2},y_{2},z_{2})=\\
&=(x_{1}x_{2}-y_{1}z_{2}-y_{2}z_{1}-4z_{1}z_{2},y_{1}y_{2}-2x_{1}x_{2}+2y_{1}z_{2}+2y_{2}z_{1}+4z_{1}z_{2},z_{1}z_{2})\end{aligned}

The idempotents of the algebra
\mathbb{B}:=(\mathbb{k}^{3},\ast)
are exactly the fixed points of (
133
).
Let
(x,y,z)
be a fixed point of (
133
), then
z^{2}=z
. If
z=0
then
x^{2}=x
and
y^{2}-2x^{2}=y
, implying 4 fixed points

(135)

I_{1}=\{(0,0,0),\,(0,1,0),\,(1,-1,0),\,(1,2,0)\}.

Similarly, if
z=1
then
x^{2}-2y-4-x=0
and
y^{2}-2x^{2}+3y+4=0
, summing up obtain
(x-y)(x+y+1)=0
, thus implying 4 more fixed points

(136)

I_{2}=\{(1,-2,1),\,(-2,1,1),\,(-1,-1,1),\,(4,4,1)\}.

The idempotents of
\mathbb{B}
are
\Idm(\mathbb{B})=I_{1}\cup I_{2}
. A further calculation yields the Peirce spectrum for each idempotent (in one case there must be supposed
\sqrt{-1}\in\mathbb{k}
):

(137)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\sigma(0,0,0)=\{0,0,0\},&&\sigma(0,1,0)=\{0,0,1\},\\
&\sigma(1,-1,0)=\{-1,0,1\},&&\sigma(1,2,0)=\{0,1,2\},\\
&\sigma(1,-2,1)=\{-1,1,2\},&&\sigma(-1,-1,1)=\{1,\sqrt{-1},-\sqrt{-1}\},\\
&\sigma(-2,1,1)=\{-1,1,2\},&&\sigma(4,4,1)=\{1,2,8\}.\end{aligned}

Since there are exactly
2^{3}=8
idempotents and
\tfrac{1}{2}
is absent in the Peirce spectrum,
\mathbb{B}
is a generic algebra according to the definition in
[
22
]
. This gives an example where 1) all idempotents can be calculated explicitly and they furthermore have integer coordinates and 2) the Peirce spectrum can be determined explicitly and consists only of (Gaussian) integers.
∎
