Towards a safer future: a qualitative exploration of strategies for preventing medical errors in nursing practice
Authors: Nastaran Heydarikhayat, Nezar Ghanbarzehi, Masoume Moqaddam, Fatemeh Darban
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMedical errors remain a significant and preventable cause of patient injury and death, presenting a continuous challenge to safety within hospitals worldwide. Understanding the origins and preventative strategies for medical errors has become a renewed priority for numerous healthcare systems worldwide in an effort to enhance patient safety.MethodsIn this descriptive phenomenological study, 12 multidisciplinary healthcare staff, nurse managers, and faculty members were selected through purposive sampling from three hospitals and one university-based center affiliated with a medical university in southeastern Iran. Data collection was done through semi-structured in-depth individual interviews with participants. All interviews were analyzed by the seven steps of Colaizzi’s method in MAXQDA version 10.Results“The unfinished architecture of safe care: reflection on prevention” emerged as the main theme of the study. Six subthemes and 29 categories were extracted from data analysis. Six subthemes were identified: “Excellence in Care”, “Empowerment: the cornerstone of patient safety”, “Professional Supervision: a vigilant eye for patient safety”, “Managerial Enhancement”, “Supportive Chains”, and “Cultural Responsiveness”.ConclusionsParticipants perceived multidimensional strategies for error prevention. Although some of the strategies are already recognized, in settings with limited resources, workforce, and infrastructure, they may still require sustained effort, repetition, and ongoing revision. The proposed strategies encompass consideration of contextual factors, care delivery, infrastructural readiness, and management and planning practices —along with certain preventive changes and innovations— which may contribute to reducing medical errors in nursing practice. Background

Medical errors represent a major contributor to preventable morbidity and mortality across healthcare systems worldwide, posing a persistent challenge to hospital safety [1, 2]. It is estimated that one in ten patients experiences harm during healthcare delivery, with unsafe care accounting for over three million deaths annually worldwide [3]. Findings indicates that between 3% and 17% of patients admitted to health care facilities sustain unintentional injuries during hospitalization, with an estimated 30–70% of such incidents being preventable [3, 4]. Higher incidence rates have been consistently reported in low- and middle-income (LMIC) countries [5]. Substandard healthcare quality now represents a more substantial barrier to mortality reduction than inadequate access. Of deaths from conditions amenable to medical intervention, 60% are attributable to poor-quality care, while the remaining deaths result from non-utilization of health services [6]. LMIC nations carry a substantial and disproportionate share of global morbidity and mortality attributable to hospital-based adverse events. Approximately 134 million adverse events result from unsafe care annually in healthcare facilities across LMIC countries [7, 8]. The tangible economic and personal burdens of medical errors are directly incurred by healthcare systems, individual patients, and their families [9]. These include the delivery of supplementary care by such centers, extended hospitalizations, elevated health care expenditures, and diminished patient satisfaction [3]. In addition, medical errors profoundly compromise confidence in health care system performance, weakening patient trust and subsequently reducing the propensity to pursue essential medical treatment [10].

Although the delivery of quality patient care remains a fundamental objective of healthcare, providers encounter numerous challenges in ensuring patient safety within today’s increasingly complex environment. These challenges are particularly pronounced in nursing practice, where escalating workload demands and the growing complexity of patient care further complicate the maintenance of safety [1, 11]. Despite years of local, national, and international efforts to reduce medical error, these endeavors have yet to achieve a substantial reduction in medical error rates [5, 10, 12]. A lack of alignment among leadership style, cultural orientation, and actionable strategy by healthcare’s senior leaders has prevented system-wide progress on medical errors and patient safety [9].

On a global scale, patient safety has become an increasingly urgent concern, particularly due to the potential financial savings achievable through stronger safety practices and the reduction of errors [5]. Medical errors are inherently complex, as identifying consistent causal factors remains challenging; moreover, even when such factors are discerned, implementing reliable and sustainable solutions to mitigate recurrence proves equally difficult [13]. Understanding the scope, origins, and preventative strategies for medical errors has become a renewed priority for numerous healthcare systems worldwide in an effort to enhance patient safety [2]. Therefore, this descriptive phenomenological study aimed to explore and describe the lived experiences of multidisciplinary healthcare staff regarding medical error prevention in nursing practice.

Research question

What are the lived experiences of multidisciplinary healthcare staff, including nurses, midwives, physicians, nurse managers, and faculty members, regarding strategies for preventing medical errors in nursing practice?

Materials and methods

Research design

This is a qualitative study with descriptive phenomenological design that was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Iranshahr university of Medical Sciences (Approval Code: IR.IRSHUMS.REC.1398.012). In October 2025, permission to take samples was again issued by the Ethics Committee of the Vice President for Research and Technology. The study was carried out between October 12, 2025, and February 14, 2026. Phenomenology aims to construct a rich description of a phenomenon’s essential structures by rigorously examining the first-person perspectives of individuals who have lived through it [14]. Unlike content analysis—which reduces textual data to frequency-based, predefined categories—or qualitative descriptive designs—which remain largely a theoretical and surface-level—phenomenology penetrates the prereflective, embodied, and temporal dimensions of human experience [15, 16].

Study setting

Data were collected from three hospitals and one university-based center affiliated with a medical university in southeastern Iran. This province, with a population exceeding 2.77 million, shares borders with South Khorasan Province and Afghanistan to the north, Pakistan and Afghanistan to the east, the Oman Sea to the south, and the provinces of Kerman and Hormozgan to the west. The population comprises two main ethnic groups—the Baloch and the Sistani. The Baloch speak the Balochi dialect and follow the Sunni branch of Islam. Sistan and Balochistan ranks as the poorest among Iran’s 31 provinces. Educational deprivation in the province is well-documented, with media reports emphasizing insufficient school infrastructure. The shortage of healthcare services and treatment facilities in the province has also drawn media coverage [17].

Participants and data collection

In this study, 12 participants were selected by using purposive sampling. The study included a multidisciplinary group of healthcare providers, comprising nurses at various levels (staff nurses, clinical supervisors, and nurse managers), nursing and midwifery faculty members, a physician, and midwives. Participants were purposively recruited from three hospitals and one university-based center affiliated with a medical university in southeastern Iran to ensure they possessed relevant and varied experiences related to the phenomenon under investigation [18]. Purposive sampling allows for the deliberate selection of participants who can offer thick and varied data, a practice closely aligned with qualitative research principles [19].

Inclusion criteria comprised: (1) registered nurses with at least one year of direct patient care experience in a hospital setting; (2) nursing managers and supervisors with a minimum of three years in a supervisory or leadership role; (3) nursing and midwifery faculty members affiliated with a medical university; (4) physicians with at least one year of clinical experience. All participants were required to voluntarily provide written informed consent before participating in the study. Exclusion criteria were transfer to another city or university during the study period. To capture diversity of perspectives, purposive sampling was used to seek variation in demographics (e.g., age, gender, and marital status), socioeconomic factors (income), and professional attributes (job role and experience). Data were gathered from 12 participants via in-depth, semi-structured interviews conducted between October 2025 and February 2026 at three hospitals affiliated with a medical university in southeastern Iran.

Two authors (NH and NGh), both nursing faculty with qualitative research expertise, conducted the face-to-face and telephone interviews. Following an explanation of the study’s purpose and the acquisition of both oral and written informed consent, participants were recruited via phone or workplace visits and subsequently selected their preferred interview time and location. Access to participants was secured through flexible scheduling at their workplaces across different shifts. The process involved multiple points of contact, including repeated visits and phone calls, to coordinate around their clinical responsibilities. Given the participants’ work schedules and availability, 7 interviews were conducted face-to-face in a private room at the participants’ workplaces, while the remaining 5 interviews were conducted via telephone. No one other than the interviewer and participant was present during the interviews to ensure confidentiality and to allow participants to speak freely. All interviews were audio-recorded with participants’ permission. No repeat interviews were conducted.

The interview guide was developed based on the study aims and a review of the literature. It was pilot tested with two nurses who were not included in the main study. This process helped refine the wording, sequencing, and clarity of questions to ensure they were open-ended and aligned with phenomenological inquiry. Some questions were revised following the pilot interview.

During the interviews, several key questions were asked: “Could you please describe a situation where you successfully prevented a potential medical error? What did you do, and what enabled you to act?” “Based on your experiences, what do you think would help nurses like you to prevent errors more effectively? Please describe what you believe is needed.” “Please describe the strategies that can be implemented to minimize clinical errors in healthcare facilities”. The interview was then continued with probing questions, such as “Please explain more.” and “Can you provide an example?.” Further details regarding the interview questions are provided in the interview guide.

Each interview was audio-recorded after all participants signed an informed consent form. The duration of interviews ranged between 40 and 52 min, depending on participants’ responses. Field notes were taken immediately after each interview to document contextual details, non-verbal cues, reflections on the interview process, and the researcher’s initial impressions. These notes were used to supplement the transcripts and inform subsequent interviews and analysis. To further enhance reflexivity, the principal investigator maintained a reflexive journal throughout the study to document personal assumptions, biases, and evolving interpretations. Regular debriefing sessions were held within the research team to critically examine how the researchers’ professional backgrounds might shape data collection, analysis, and interpretation. Bracketing was practiced during data analysis, whereby the researchers set aside their preconceptions and remained open to participants’ perspectives, in line with Colaizzi’s phenomenological method.

Data analysis

Interview data were analyzed promptly, within 24 h of collection, employing Colaizzi’s phenomenological method. The data management and analysis was implemented by MAXQDA version 10 software. Colaizzi’s phenomenological method follows a structured, seven-step analytical procedure. This process begins with researchers immersing themselves in verbatim transcripts to gain familiarity with the data. It then proceeds through the identification of significant statements, the formulation of meanings, and the clustering of these into emergent themes. The analysis culminates in the development of an exhaustive description of the phenomenon, which is often validated by returning the findings to participants for verification [20].

The research team consisted of the principal investigator and three co-authors with expertise in qualitative research. The transcripts were independently coded by two team members. Discrepancies in coding were discussed in team meetings until consensus was reached. In cases where agreement could not be achieved initially, a third team member was consulted to mediate and finalize the coding decisions. After each interview, transcripts were coded and analyzed iteratively. The research team (comprising the principal investigator and two co-authors) held regular debriefing sessions to review emerging codes, themes, and categories. The research team collectively confirmed that saturation had been achieved when no new codes or subthemes emerged from three consecutive interviews (interviews 10–12), and existing themes were sufficiently rich and well-supported by participant quotations.

Data saturation was achieved after the interview number nine, but three additional interviews were conducted for further assurance. It means no new themes or codes emerged from subsequent interviews, indicating comprehensive exploration of the research subject. In addition, 3 consecutive interviews yielding no novel codes/ themes and final interviews demonstrated code redundancy, suggesting saturation. In qualitative research, data collection typically continues until saturation is achieved, which ensures the comprehensiveness and depth of analysis [21, 22]. Data saturation serves as a pivotal quality marker, guiding researchers in determining whether data and analytical processes are sufficiently robust or whether further sampling and analysis are warranted [22]. In this study, saturation was evaluated through concurrent data collection and analysis, following established qualitative research practices.

For member checking, transcripts were returned to all participants for review and confirmation of accuracy. After the thematic analysis was complete, a summary of the findings was also shared with participants to ensure that the interpretations accurately reflected their lived experiences. Any feedback received was considered and incorporated where appropriate. The member checking process contributed to the identification of the themes of religious care and error reporting channels. This study was conducted in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines.

Rigor of the study

The rigor of this qualitative study was ensured by applying Lincoln and Guba’s four criteria: credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability [23]. To enhance credibility and transferability, participants were purposively selected to maximize diversity. Member checking was conducted by returning five interview transcripts to participants for validation of data extraction. Additionally, two external experts with doctoral qualifications from X University of Medical Sciences independently audited the data analysis process. The authors also provided detailed descriptions of the research methodology and analytical procedures to further support the study’s transferability.

Ethical considerations

The research proposal was approved by the Institutional Review Board and Research Ethics Committee of Iranshahr University of Medical Sciences (Approval Code: IR.IRSHUMS.REC.1398.012). In October 2025, permission to take samples was again issued by the Ethics Committee of the Vice President for Research and Technology. Required permissions were obtained from all associated centers affiliated with the medical university. The study objectives were clearly communicated to participants, and informed consent was obtained from each individual prior to participation. Participation in the study was entirely voluntary, with participants informed of their right to withdraw at any point without consequence.

Theme: The unfinished architecture of safe care: reflection on prevention

The concept of ‘unfinished architecture’ acknowledges that clinical safety systems are inherently dynamic and perpetually evolving. Although numerous strategies exist to ensure safe care and prevent errors, ongoing innovations, technological advancements, and contextual shifts continuously give rise to new vulnerabilities. Patient safety, therefore, cannot be regarded as a finalized endeavor. Rather, it demands sustained critical reflection, rigorous gap analysis, and the development and implementation of adaptive strategies to address emerging risks.

This theme embodies the comprehensive suite of care-related, educational, and managerial strategies, as well as cultural considerations essential for mitigating medical errors. Some of the proposed strategies are currently in practice; however, they require proper implementation or modification. This theme encompasses six subthemes pertaining to, consisting of “Excellence in care”, “Empowerment: the Cornerstone of Patient Safety”, “Professional supervision: a vigilant eye for patient safety”, “Managerial enhancement”, “Supportive chain”, “Cultural responsiveness”.

Subtheme 1: excellence in care

This theme pertains to fundamental principles that underpin care, the omission of which may lead to irreparable adverse outcomes. These principles are intrinsically linked to conscientious care, management, and professional, ethical, and work-conscience standards- all of which are essential for delivering the quality of service that care recipients deserve. There are five categories in this subthemes, including “Adherence to professional principles of care”, “Alignment with global standards”, “Upholding ethical and professional commitment”, “Integration of technology and innovation”, “and Professional vigilance in care”.

1–1: Adherence to professional principles of care

This category represents the most fundamental concepts of care, which are sometimes easily overlooked—such as hand hygiene or the presence of long or artificial nails, both of which can facilitate infection transmission to patients.

Participant #1, Faculty Member, stated:Some nurses wear artificial nails and fail to perform proper hand hygiene during patient care. In some cases, gloves are changed to circumvent handwashing. Occasionally, the same gloves remain in use for extended periods, coming into contact with multiple surfaces.

Some nurses wear artificial nails and fail to perform proper hand hygiene during patient care. In some cases, gloves are changed to circumvent handwashing. Occasionally, the same gloves remain in use for extended periods, coming into contact with multiple surfaces.

2 − 1: Alignment with global standards

Most participants stated that the chronic mismatch between patient and nurse staffing levels is a persistent issue and a risk factor. Interventions that establish an appropriate ratio can influence the incidence of medical errors. Participant #4, Nurse Manager, stated:Staff shortages contribute to increased workload pressure, which in turn diminishes nurses’ concentration on primary tasks. The proposed solution—recruiting additional nursing staff and distributing them equitably according to the difficulty of each unit’s working conditions—could potentially reduce error rates.

Staff shortages contribute to increased workload pressure, which in turn diminishes nurses’ concentration on primary tasks. The proposed solution—recruiting additional nursing staff and distributing them equitably according to the difficulty of each unit’s working conditions—could potentially reduce error rates.

3 − 1: Upholding ethical and professional commitment

This category underscores that ethical and professional dedication, coupled with a strong work conscience, constitutes a critical factor in preventing errors. This implies that all staff members provide the highest quality of care to patients regardless of challenging working conditions or fatigue. Participant #8, Clinical Supervisor, stated:Having a strong work conscience is crucial. One night, a patient requiring a surgical consultation was admitted to the emergency department. Unfortunately, the surgeon did not visit the patient until the following morning, resulting in a seven-and-a-half-hour delay.

4 − 1: Integration of technology and innovation

The use of advanced technologies in equipment, laboratory testing, and other areas by healthcare providers can significantly reduce the occurrence of errors. Keeping pace with contemporary innovations is a key factor in mitigating medical errors. Participant #2, Clinical Nurse, explained:The implementation of smart hospital beds would trigger an alarm if a patient attempts to get up unsafely, thereby alerting the nurse and preventing potential falls. However, as long as outdated, conventional beds remain in use, the risk of patient falls will likely remain high.

5 − 1: Professional vigilance in care

Vigilance and sustained professional attentiveness among caregivers are fundamental principles in error prevention. Certain incidents—such as patient falls occurring despite raised bed rails—highlight the need for a form of watchfulness coupled with proactive skepticism to prevent safety events. Participant #9, Clinical Supervisor, said:Instances have occurred in which, despite providing education to patients’ companions and raising bed rails, patients have fallen from the foot of the bed—an area that lacks railing. These incidents have particularly occurred during night hours when both the patient and their companion were asleep, often resulting in head trauma.

Subtheme 2. Empowerment: the cornerstone of patient safety

Empowerment is recognized as a fundamental strategy for patient safety and for the success of health institutions. Empowerment is defined as the organizational frameworks that shape working conditions and processes, enabling staff to perform effectively, exercise decision-making authority, and assume accountability for their actions. Empowerment in this subtheme is conceptualized as a dynamic process that integrates education, critical thinking, and sound clinical decision-making. This is achieved through bedside learning, systematic reflection on errors, and heightened sensitivity to real-world clinical situations—all of which contribute to the professional growth and safety competency of healthcare providers. Raising awareness of legal consequences, analyzing hospital-based medical errors by critical thinking, and conducting targeted workshops are examples of measures that can prevent safety incidents in healthcare settings. There are six categories in this subthemes, including “Staying up-to-date”, “Revising the continuing training”, “Experiential learning and critical reflection”, “Educational bridge to the patient’s bed”, “Legal awareness”, and “Reforming academic education”.

1–2: Staying up-to-date

Maintaining and continually updating knowledge and skills related to patient care, disease management, pharmacology, and diagnostic and therapeutic procedures is essential for preventing medical errors in healthcare settings. Participant #7, Nurse Executive, stated:How can medical errors be prevented if nursing staff knowledge does not keep pace with the expanding range of diagnostic, therapeutic, and pharmacological advancements?

How can medical errors be prevented if nursing staff knowledge does not keep pace with the expanding range of diagnostic, therapeutic, and pharmacological advancements?

2–2: Revising the continuing training

This category indicates that continuing education has not yet achieved its intended objectives. The training provided is often either of insufficient quality or delivered in a manner that fails to effectively engage learners—particularly after long and exhausting work shifts. The timing, quality, delivery methods, and overall appeal of current continuing education programs have not succeeded in attracting genuine participation. Many participants attend solely to obtain the requisite certificate, whereas intrinsic motivation and learning goals are rarely a priority. Participant #9, Clinical Supervisor, stated:Workshops should be scheduled at appropriate times and locations, accompanied by adequate refreshments, and facilitated by instructors who are both expert and highly knowledgeable—avoiding a one-way, lecture-based approach limited to reading slides. When learners are tired or hungry, even the most skilled instructor cannot achieve meaningful impact. Effective workshops require sufficient investment; however, adequate resources are not currently allocated for their proper implementation in this setting.

Workshops should be scheduled at appropriate times and locations, accompanied by adequate refreshments, and facilitated by instructors who are both expert and highly knowledgeable—avoiding a one-way, lecture-based approach limited to reading slides. When learners are tired or hungry, even the most skilled instructor cannot achieve meaningful impact. Effective workshops require sufficient investment; however, adequate resources are not currently allocated for their proper implementation in this setting.

3 − 2: Experiential learning and critical reflection

This category highlights the integration of experiential learning and critical thinking. Educational initiatives—particularly those incorporating Root Cause Analysis (RCA) of serious errors—enable staff to engage in reflective practice, identify systemic vulnerabilities, and develop preventive strategies. This process not only reduces error recurrence but also empowers healthcare providers by enhancing their clinical reasoning, situational awareness, and decision-making confidence. Participant #3, Specialist Nurse said:Cases involving unforeseen errors that result in severe patient harm or death require structured discussion within the framework of medical staff debriefings. Systematic analysis of contributing factors, along with the development of explicit prevention strategies, directly influences the reduction of recurrent adverse events.

Cases involving unforeseen errors that result in severe patient harm or death require structured discussion within the framework of medical staff debriefings. Systematic analysis of contributing factors, along with the development of explicit prevention strategies, directly influences the reduction of recurrent adverse events.

4 − 2. Educational bridge to the patient’s bed

Physician engagement and accountability play a crucial role in fostering the scientific growth of nurses at the patient’s bedside. Although education can occur in various settings, the communication of critical insights and new findings by physicians during patient visits inherently enhances the learning of the clinical team and serves as a significant factor in reducing adverse safety events. Participant #4, Hospital Administrator, said:This represents a significant gap, particularly observable among the new generation of physicians. Many practitioners fulfill their routine duties and depart without providing any educational input or assuming any personal responsibility for discussing cases with colleagues. Additionally, nursing staff often demonstrate limited initiative in this regard.

Additionally, nursing staff often demonstrate limited initiative in this regard.

5 − 2: Legal awareness

Providing legal education and fostering legal literacy among all caregivers—particularly novice personnel—can serve as a barrier against errors. Awareness of the consequences associated with cases of error, negligence, and malpractice can act as a deterrent to clinical errors. Participant #6, Faculty Member of Nursing, said:Educational workshops that critically analyze actual case outcomes—including errors and mortality—and detail the legal ramifications of clinical errors serve to raise legal awareness and enhance staff sensitivity to the repercussions of negligence and oversight.

Educational workshops that critically analyze actual case outcomes—including errors and mortality—and detail the legal ramifications of clinical errors serve to raise legal awareness and enhance staff sensitivity to the repercussions of negligence and oversight.

6 − 2. Reforming academic education

This category underscores the indispensable role of clinical faculty in preparing, sensitizing, and effectively educating students within the clinical environment. Continuous and sustained supervision of student performance not only promotes superior bedside learning but also mitigates errors arising from inadequate supervision. Participant #2, Clinical Nurse, said:In academic medical centers, preventing student errors requires the direct presence of the supervising instructor during clinical procedures. This necessitates bedside observation, rather than having the instructor positioned remotely at the nursing station while students perform patient care tasks.

Subtheme 3. Professional supervision: a vigilant eye for patient safety

This subtheme underscores the importance of comprehensive, multi-level supervision throughout the patient’s journey, from admission to post-discharge. Critical thinking, thorough information analysis, and identification of at-risk patients are key measures essential for preventing medical errors. There are three categories in this subthemes, including “Prioritizing critical logic over paperwork”, “Identifying subtle systematic errors”, and “Preventing uninformed compassionate care”.

1–3: Prioritizing critical logic over paperwork

Effective patient care fundamentally requires providers to engage in a systematic process of assessment, critical thinking, and deep reflection, culminating in sound clinical reasoning to guide care decisions. However, what is currently observed is the prioritization of administrative documentation over critical thinking and logical analysis. Assessment tools are merely “checked off” without subsequent analysis of their results, leading to a failure to extract and comprehend their clinical significance. Participant #10, Clinical Supervisor, stated:

Some nurses complete the Morse Fall Scale assessment merely as a checkbox exercise, assigning scores without a practical understanding of their clinical meaning or the ability to identify patients who are truly at high risk for falling.

Some nurses complete the Morse Fall Scale assessment merely as a checkbox exercise, assigning scores without a practical understanding of their clinical meaning or the ability to identify patients who are truly at high risk for falling.

Also, participant #1, Faculty Member of Nursing, said:

The Morse Scale is often documented as a routine administrative task rather than being actively applied in clinical practice. Furthermore, while it assesses fall risk, it does not offer specific preventive strategies.

The Morse Scale is often documented as a routine administrative task rather than being actively applied in clinical practice. Furthermore, while it assesses fall risk, it does not offer specific preventive strategies.

2–3: Identifying subtle systematic errors

This category refers to errors arising during order transcription, medication charting, or the use of medical equipment such as infusion pumps. If left unaddressed, such errors are prone to recurrence and can systematically affect a large number of patients. Periodic equipment evaluation, the presence of a clinical engineer in the unit to assess device functionality, and the re-verification of physician orders can help prevent systematic clinical errors. Participant #5, Nurse, stated:

If an infusion pump malfunctions, the patient may receive medication via infusion over several consecutive shifts without the nursing staff detecting the issue. One reported incident involved this exact problem; the pump’s defect was eventually identified, and the patient’s medication was administered without it until the pump could be repaired.

3–3: Preventing uninformed compassionate care

This category addresses situations in which well-intentioned but unqualified individuals—such as service staff or patient companions—intervene in the care process without proper knowledge. A critical example is the unauthorized adjustment of infusion rates for cardiac or vasoactive medications. This underscores the essential need for vigilant nursing supervision to promptly identify and prevent such safety-compromising events. Participant #6, Faculty Member of Nursing, stated:

An incident occurred during clinical practice in which a patient’s companion rapidly increased the nitroglycerin infusion rate, believing the drip was too slow. Following this event, it has been consistently emphasized to all companions that such cardiac medications require precise titration and that unauthorized adjustment may result in dangerous hypotension and serious adverse outcomes for the patient.

Subtheme 4. Managerial enhancement

This subtheme addresses compensatory strategies for error prevention, especially in under-resourced settings that do not yet meet international standards. The effectiveness of these strategies depends on managerial enhancement, which involves revising current practices, optimizing resources, implementing systemic changes, and fostering continuous improvement. It encompasses seven distinct categories including “Error prevention supervisor”, “Color codes still matter”, “Restructuring error reporting systems”, “Standardization of processes and protocols”, “Eliminating cascaded delegation”, “Professional meritocracy”, and “Anticipating and planning for crises”.

1–4. Error prevention supervisor

Despite extensive efforts over many years, medical errors remain significantly underreported. It seems that in each shift, alongside the clinical and educational supervisors, an error prevention supervisor must also be present. Busy shifts, crises, critical times such as shift changes or medication administration hours, and night hours are high-risk periods where the supervision of a dedicated supervisor could prevent errors from occurring. Participant #6, Faculty Member of Nursing, stated:Given the high frequency of reported medical errors, healthcare facilities should consider appointing a dedicated, expert supervisor for error prevention. The alarming mortality statistics and the leading causes of medical errors underscore the necessity of such a role.

2–4: Color codes still matter

Even with established hemovigilance protocols and mandatory double-checks, fatal transfusion errors persist in some settings- particularly during overcrowded and chaotic shifts. This category indicates that greater use of color-coding could still significantly reduce medical errors. The implementation of standardized, universally defined color labels for blood types and Rh factors would help prevent errors, especially during simultaneous transfusions or in high-stress environments such as operating rooms. Furthermore, a global standardization of distinct, fixed color schemes should be assigned to each drug category, while individual drug names within the same category are differentiated by color variations on the label. The category-specific color should remain uniform across all vials to ensure consistent identification, and high-alert drugs should be re-evaluated. Currently, variations in packaging colors across different pharmaceutical companies can increase the risk of error. Participant #8, Clinical Supervisor, stated:

A blood group misidentification and the simultaneous transfusion of two patients resulted in fatal outcomes. In one instance, the patient’s death was not attributable to the initial trauma but rather to the administration of an incompatible blood transfusion.

3–4: Restructuring error reporting systems

Current error reporting systems have not been fully effective. Reporting forms are often not completed accurately or in a timely manner, and alternative channels are not entirely efficient. Many healthcare staff may avoid using designated reporting boxes due to concerns about confidentiality and the risk of being identified by colleagues. Therefore, the implementation of mobile-based reporting systems emerges as a feasible strategy to enhance error reporting. Participant #2, Nurse, said:

A voluntary reporting system through a dedicated digital channel—where reports are reviewed without access to the reporter’s identifying information—could address this concern. Traditional physical drop boxes are also ineffective, as staff may fear being observed.

4–4: Standardization of processes and protocols

This category refers to the systematic development and implementation of care procedures based on validated scientific evidence rather than experiential habits. Standardization ensures uniformity of practice among healthcare professionals, adherence to evidence-based guidelines, and the capacity to continuous evaluation and quality improvement. By reducing variability in clinical decision-making, standardized protocols enhance patient safety, decrease the likelihood of errors, and provide a consistent framework for achieving reliable clinical outcomes. 5 − 4: Eliminating cascaded delegation

This category addresses the emergence of a hierarchical care structure within certain wards, comprising nurses, nursing assistants, and service personnel, wherein each group exhibits a tendency to delegate tasks to subordinates. This delegation often results in critical responsibilities being assigned to individuals who lack the requisite authority or possess limited clinical knowledge. Consequently, nursing staff devote a disproportionate amount of time to administrative documentation and reporting, leading to reduced bedside presence and direct patient engagement. Participant #12, physician, stated:Some nurses delegate medication administration and even certain educational tasks to nursing assistants. Many of these assistants possess only basic knowledge of the drug’s name and primary indication, while remaining largely uninformed about its potential adverse effects, drug interactions, and the specific monitoring requirements.

6 − 4: Professional meritocracy

The reduction of medical errors is closely linked to the presence of competent managers—specifically, those selected on the basis of meritocratic principles and possessing a deep understanding of the clinical context. The ascendancy of managerial meritocracy within healthcare settings is a prerequisite for achieving significant reductions in medical errors. Conversely, tribalism, cronyism, and unprofessional management practices severely compromise patient safety. Participant #3, Specialist Nurse, stated:

Ineffective management is a direct precursor to medical errors, primarily because professional meritocracy is neglected, resulting in the selection of administrators based on criteria other than competence and capability. For instance, if a head nurse possesses the requisite skills to identify staff deficiencies, to strategically plan for error prevention, and to effectively mentor novice personnel, a clear reduction in the incidence of clinical errors can be anticipated.

7 − 4: Anticipating and planning for crises

Anticipating and preparing for crises represents a core principle of error mitigation. Healthcare settings are continually confronted with unforeseen crises—such as pandemics and mass casualty incidents. Seasonal variations and specific calendar events that may coincide with increased incident rates should be incorporated into shift planning and personnel management during crisis periods. Participant #2, Nurse, stated:

During periods of high hospital admission—such as influenza or cholera outbreaks—workload planning must ensure that pressures are not concentrated solely on one department. Medical staff should be strategically redeployed from other hospital sectors to prevent staff fatigue, illness, and subsequent medical errors.

Subtheme 5. Supportive chain

This subtheme demonstrates the positive impact of supporting healthcare providers on the prevention of medical errors. This framework encompasses a top-down support structure, specifically: the support provided by the Ministry of Health and Medical Education to healthcare organizations, and the subsequent support extended by these organizations to their healthcare providers. This subtheme comprises three categories including “Organizational caring of frontiers”, “Justice, an unseen concept in medical errors”, and “The magnetism of clinical settings”.

1–5: Organizational caring of frontiers

While the primary function of healthcare personnel is care delivery, they themselves, in their most vulnerable state, are the initial recipients of organizational support—support that must be adequately provided to ensure effective performance. Delayed salary payments, compensation disproportionate to economic conditions and inflation, and mandatory overtime work—all of which contribute to psychological distress—can directly impair the concentration of care providers, thereby increasing the likelihood of medical errors.

Participant #4, Nurse Manager, stated:

Delayed salary disbursements affecting contracted hospital personnel—many of whom are young married professionals who depend on timely compensation—can create considerable psychological and professional strain, which may ultimately increase the likelihood of medical errors due to impaired concentration.

2–5: Justice, an unseen concept in clinical errors

3–5: The magnetism of clinical settings

A primary threat to clinical workforce sustainability is the loss of skilled nurses to administrative positions, as they increasingly leave bedside care due to burnout, inadequate compensation, and systemic lack of support. Mitigating this crisis requires a strategic focus on retention through equitable pay, reduced workloads, psychological support, and clear clinical career advancement pathways. Subtheme 6. Cultural responsiveness

This subtheme underscores the influence of cultural and ethnic factors on error occurrence, whether direct or indirect—a dimension that has likely been underrecognized. Five principal categories emerged from this subtheme: “Comprehensive awareness of the cultural context”, “Non-ethnic management”, “Observance of cultural rights”, “Maintaining the chain of interaction”, and “Religion-oriented caring”.

1–6: Comprehensive awareness of the cultural context

A deep understanding of the cultural context of both patients and the healthcare team is a critical component of patient safety, as it helps prevent medical errors by reducing communication and interpretive gaps. This awareness enables providers to comprehend culture-specific beliefs, taboos, language preferences, and decision-making patterns. For example, understanding varied expressions of pain, health-related beliefs, and religious sensitivities can help prevent misdiagnosis, treatment non-adherence, or refusal of informed consent. Participant #10, Clinical Supervisor, said:

A simple yet compelling example of such cultural difference is the gendered nature of names. A name commonly associated with one gender in a given culture may be predominantly associated with the opposite gender in another. Unfamiliarity with this distinction can contribute to clinical errors—such as mismatching patient records, incorrectly administering gender-specific treatments, or causing significant distress and erosion of patient trust.

Discussion

This phenomenological study explored the experiences of multidisciplinary healthcare staff, nurse managers, and faculty members of medical university regarding strategies to prevent medical errors in clinical settings. From the lived experiences of participants, six subthemes and one overarching emerged, each representing distinct strategies for medical errors prevention.

Achieving excellence in care emerged as a core strategy for preventing medical errors in clinical settings. This subtheme encompasses adherence to evidence-based standards, alignment with global standards, and the integration of technological innovation into daily practice. Evidence indicates that poor-quality care remains a significant contributor to adverse patient outcomes, and despite ongoing efforts, the consistent delivery of high-quality, safe care continues to be challenging [24, 25]. A survey conducted among anesthesiologists in India identified a high prevalence of suboptimal practices with potential consequences for patient safety [24]. Clinical practice guidelines serve as foundational tools for implementing evidence-based medicine, thereby improving patient outcomes and enhancing safety [26]. In this study, adherence to principles of care was identified by participants as a mechanism for error reduction, reflecting the broader concept of care effectiveness—defined as the delivery of health services grounded in scientific knowledge and tailored to patients’ clinical needs [27]. Participants in the present study also highlighted the importance of adequate nurse-to-patient and physician-to-patient ratios, as well as staying current with technological advancements, as key factors in reducing errors. These elements were conceptualized as alignment with global standards. However, in many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), under-resourced healthcare facilities, limited access to essential medicines, equipment, and diagnostic tools impede the delivery of safe and effective care. A critical shortage of healthcare professionals further exacerbates the situation, resulting in high patient-to-provider ratios and overburdened staff, which increases the risk of burnout, fatigue, and ultimately, medical errors [27]. Additionally, suboptimal technology—stemming from insufficient training, limited interoperability, and technical malfunctions—can further compound the risk of errors [10].

The second subtheme of the present study refers to empowerment: the cornerstone of safety. Empowerment, at both organizational and individual levels, is believed to enhance nurses’ capacity to deliver safe and effective care. Structural empowerment specifically refers to institutional practices that facilitate staff access to resources, information, support, and professional development opportunities. Structural empowerment has been identified as a crucial component in efforts to enhance healthcare quality [28, 29]. Empowerment, as conceptualized in this study, extends beyond traditional definitions of education or skill acquisition. It represents a holistic and dynamic process through which healthcare professionals develop the capacity to think critically, act autonomously, and respond effectively to the complexities of clinical practice. This subtheme captures the multidimensional nature of empowerment, encompassing not only the acquisition of knowledge and technical skills but also the cultivation of reflective judgment, legal awareness, and the ability to translate learning into bedside practice. Nurse leaders, preceptors, and healthcare administrators should prioritize nurse empowerment through workshops, continuous training, and improvements in working conditions. Greater emphasis on empowerment is essential, as enhancing care quality not only improves patient outcomes but also strengthens the professional image and societal standing of nursing [30]. The study by Sánchez and Corvalan also demonstrated that patient safety education must be integrated as a core component of health professions training and ongoing professional development [10]. Ongoing education for health care professionals in patient safety, effective communication, teamwork, and error management is essential [10]. In addition, insights and lessons derived from incident reports must be disseminated throughout the healthcare organization. Incident-based learnings must inform the revision of policies, the enhancement of care delivery processes, and the overall improvement of patient safety and care quality. These resulting systemic changes should then be communicated across the entire organization [7]. Instruction in clinical reasoning and decision-making can mitigate cognitive biases. Additionally, ensuring that practitioners remain proficient in electronic health tools—including awareness of system updates and modifications—is critical [10]. Cognitive biases—systematic patterns of thinking that result in erroneous judgments and premature decisions—can significantly influence how clinicians gather, interpret, and synthesize clinical information, thereby contributing to diagnostic and treatment errors [10, 31].

The third subtheme, professional supervision, encompasses a broad spectrum of oversight activities, including direct nursing vigilance, monitoring of clinical staff and equipment performance, and identification of systemic or individual practices that may inadvertently contribute to risk. Supervision in nursing is recognized as a fundamental component of healthcare delivery, exerting a substantial influence on both the quality of patient care and subsequent health outcomes [32]. Evidence indicates that inadequate supervision is a significant contributor to increased rates of medical errors [10]. Evidence indicates that clinical supervision contributes most effectively to patient safety and care quality when its educational and supportive roles are distinguished from its managerial and evaluative functions. Among the various supervision models, narrative-based supervision is especially valuable, as it is tailored for post-training clinicians and offers a means to examine the complexity of clinical reasoning while fostering a supportive environment in which practitioners can respectfully challenge each other’s authority. For supervision to be successful, it should be professionally guided and learner-centered, rather than externally imposed or institution-driven. It is therefore proposed that regular clinical supervision be established as a professional requirement to fulfill the quality and safety objectives [33].

The subtheme of managerial enhancement identified in the present study reflects deliberate attention and corrective action directed at practices that are either inadequately performed or in need of significant revision. While patient safety principles must be consistently adopted and operationalized by nurses in clinical practice, their application is foundational to improving care quality and preventing medical errors [3]. Management has been identified as a factor in promoting patient safety culture and error reporting [9, 34]. Furthermore, Adams’s study linked successful outcomes to four key leadership practices: regular direct observation, cultivating teamwork, ensuring psychological safety, and promoting open communication. Positive leadership approaches enhanced outcomes, independent of the specific safety intervention employed. Strategic leaders hold the core responsibility for cultivating a safety culture that systematically supports both organizational learning and innovation [9].

The findings of the present study suggest that, alongside the educational and clinical supervisor, a dedicated patient safety supervisor should be present during each shift. Adams’s study identified the Chief Quality Officer as responsible for leading organizational efforts to improve quality and patient safety by shaping strategic priorities, fostering a supportive culture, and implementing evidence-based practices aligned with leadership direction to reduce patient harm [9]. The next issue necessitating management and intervention in this study is that using mobile-based reporting channels, where the individual’s identity remains anonymous, is an effective measure for error reporting. Kumah et al.‘s study identified the incident reporting form as a commonly employed tool for error reporting [7]. Conversely, the present study indicates that minimizing bureaucratic processes and implementing mobile-based reporting systems lead to a higher frequency of error submissions, especially in anonymous virtual platforms. An interventional study by Khajouei et al.’s revealed that introducing an electronic format for reporting errors led to a significant reduction in occupational stress among nursing staff, while increase in the frequency of error reporting [35].

The literature, including a review by Silvana et al., shows that mobile applications used for education, checklists, and incident reporting are effective tools for improving patient safety [36]. However, these applications are typically complex and institution-based. In contrast, our study participants proposed a simpler, more accessible alternative: anonymous reporting via messaging channels. This low-technology, anonymous approach emphasizes ease of use and minimal bureaucracy, thereby potentially increasing reporting frequency. Our study thus extends the existing literature by highlighting that simpler, anonymous reporting channels may offer a practical and feasible strategy.

Based on the findings of the present study, another error prevention strategy involves eliminating cascaded delegation, in which critical nursing responsibilities are assigned to personnel lacking the requisite skills or knowledge. The study by Alhassan et al. showed that delegation in nursing is accompanied by numerous challenges. In nursing, delegation challenges are closely linked to supervision, as shortages in staffing and resources restrict both the pool of available delegates and the capacity of supervisors to monitor and support them effectively. Personnel deficits hinder the efficient assignment of tasks, while skill gaps—particularly among unskilled workers—further compromise the competent execution of delegated duties. These interrelated issues ultimately undermine care quality and patient safety [32]. The study by Alhassan et al. identified delegation challenges in nursing—including staffing shortages, skill gaps, and inadequate supervision—that compromise patient safety in low-resource settings. Our study aligns with these findings, as participants similarly reported that critical tasks are often delegated to unqualified staff due to workload pressures and insufficient staffing. However, our study extends the literature by introducing the concept of “cascaded delegation,” where tasks are progressively delegated down multiple hierarchical levels, and by providing contextual insights into how cultural and organizational factors in Iranian hospitals further complicate delegation practices. Thus, while Alhassan et al. offer a valuable foundation, our study adds depth by revealing the layered nature of delegation and its systemic vulnerabilities in a new cultural context.

The fifth subtheme, “Supportive Chain,” underscores the critical role of organizational, financial, and collegial support in reducing medical errors. Participants in the present study emphasized that attention to employees’ comprehensive needs, sustained presence in the clinical environment, and organizational justice were identified as effective factors in error reduction. These findings align with the broader literature, which indicates that nurses’ adherence to patient safety principles is influenced by individual, systemic, and environmental factors [13]. Human error is an inherent feature of healthcare delivery, particularly in demanding clinical environments marked by sleep deprivation, high patient throughput, and rapid workflows [10]. A key dimension of support identified by participants was financial and policy-related. Assessing satisfaction with national and hospital policies was considered essential for understanding frontline healthcare workers’ quality of life. Participants further highlighted that salary satisfaction reinforces the benefits of such policies, underscoring the need for financial incentives alongside effective governance. To safeguard professionals’ well-being during public health emergencies, policymakers must prioritize both policy efficacy and financial support [37]. Another critical aspect raised by participants was the impact of staffing shortages on workload and professional burnout. Staffing shortages precipitate excessive workloads and professional burnout [10]. Increased nursing workloads are associated with disengagement, turnover intentions, and burnout. Implementing workload reduction strategies is therefore critical to support nurse well-being and maintain care quality [38]. Nursing retention is influenced by the degree to which organizational culture and working conditions align with practitioners’ individual and occupational expectations. A sense of professional commitment and continuity emerges when collegial interactions are characterized by mutual support, trust, and an atmosphere that promotes a sense of safety and belonging [39]. Finally, participants emphasized the importance of peer support following adverse events. Encouraging peer support among clinicians fosters effective communication about medical errors, which in turn promotes professional autonomy and enhances overall patient safety [1]. Our study contributes to the literature by showing that ‘retention’ in this context must be redefined. It is not just about keeping nurses within the organization, but about keeping them in clinical practice. This requires systemic interventions that address the root causes of the ‘push’ factors, such as workload and compensation, and also address the ‘pull’ factors by making clinical roles more professionally rewarding and sustainable.

The sixth subtheme, “Cultural responsiveness,” underscores the critical role of integrating cultural, religious, and ethnic factors into care delivery as a strategy for error reduction. A systematic review by Suen et al. emphasized that patients from minority ethnic backgrounds, those with low socioeconomic status, and individuals with limited health literacy face an elevated risk of medication errors. The study highlights that increasing awareness of these disparities among healthcare staff is essential for developing targeted interventions aimed at reducing inequalities in patient experiences and medication safety outcomes [40]. A key finding of Adams’s study was the emphasis on ongoing, clear communication as essential for reducing medical errors. Constructive communication and teamwork were identified as factors that enhance patient safety and reduce errors [9]. However, language barriers in healthcare contribute to miscommunication between clinicians and patients, diminishing satisfaction for both parties and compromising care quality and patient safety [41]. Communication errors encompass failures in exchanges between healthcare professionals and patients, as well as disruptions in inter-professional communication, which can result in misunderstandings and incorrect clinical decisions [10].

The present study underscores that failure to consider patients’ religious and cultural practices, along with their associated rituals, in clinical care can contribute to the occurrence of medical errors. A systematic review by Chauhan et al. (2020) found that ethnic minority patients face higher rates of adverse drug events and dosing errors, with language barriers, cultural beliefs, and interpreter use contributing to increased safety risks [42]. While our findings align with these observations, our study extends the literature in several key ways. First, we provide a more nuanced exploration of specific religious practices—including fasting, prayer, ritual purity, and hijab—and their direct impact on patient safety, moving beyond the general category of beliefs about illness. Second, participants proposed practical, context-sensitive strategies such as same-gender nursing, culturally adapted communication, and collaboration with religious leaders—offering actionable solutions for culturally complex settings.

Limitations

While this phenomenological study provides rich, contextual insights into healthcare professionals’ lived experiences of error prevention, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the findings are context‑specific to southeastern Iran, which may limit transferability to other settings; however, thick descriptions of the study context and participants have been provided to enable readers to assess applicability to their own settings. Second, the sample size was relatively small (n = 12), consistent with phenomenological research, where sample size is determined by data saturation rather than statistical representativeness. Nevertheless, this limits the generalizability of the findings. Third, the inclusion of participants from diverse professional roles (nurses, physicians, midwives, nurse managers, and faculty members) provided a breadth of perspectives but may have introduced heterogeneity that limits the depth of analysis for any single group. Fourth, recall bias may have affected participants’ accounts, as they were asked to describe past experiences and events. Fifth, social desirability bias may have influenced participants’ responses regarding error reporting and prevention strategies; to minimize this, confidentiality was assured, and participants were reminded that there were no right or wrong answers. Sixth, the use of both face‑to‑face and telephone interviews may have introduced variability in the richness and depth of data collected. However, steps were taken to ensure consistency in the interview process across both modalities. Finally, despite efforts to practice bracketing and reflexivity, the researchers’ professional backgrounds and preconceptions may have influenced data collection and interpretation.

Conclusions

The perceived strategies for medical error prevention, as reported by participants in this study, encompass attention to multiple interconnected dimensions. Consideration of contextual factors, care delivery, infrastructural readiness, and management and planning practices may contribute to reducing medical errors in nursing practice. Although some of the strategies proposed by participants are already recognized, in settings with limited resources, workforce, and infrastructure, they may still require sustained effort, repetition, and ongoing revision. These include standardizing care protocols, updating individual competencies and technological infrastructure, implementing effective crisis management, and retaining skilled personnel through organizational support and justice. Furthermore, attention to the cultural and religious backgrounds of care recipients during care delivery may reduce the likelihood of errors that could otherwise arise along the care pathway. Based on participants’ suggestions, future research and policy could consider innovations in the design and packaging of medications, equipment, and blood products—such as standardized color‑coding—as potential measures to reduce errors in high‑stress environments. While these participant‑perceived recommendations require further evaluation, they may offer a helpful basis for designing context‑sensitive interventions in culturally diverse and resource‑constrained settings. Overall, this study may contribute to a better understanding of patient safety, with possible implications for practice, management, and policy in under‑resourced healthcare environments.
