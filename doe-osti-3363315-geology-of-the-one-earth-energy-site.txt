EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
The One Earth Energy site is one of two sites in the Illinois Storage Corridor (ISC) project. The
objectives of the ISC project is to accelerate commercial deployment of carbon capture utilization
and storage at two individual sites and receive approvals for Underground Injection Control (UIC)
Class VI permits for construction at each site. At the One Earth Energy site, an extensive data
collection program was undertaken, which included the drilling of a test well (One Earth Energy
#1 [OEE #1]), four 2D seismic lines, and a small 3D seismic survey.
The OEE #1 well was drilled in 2022 and acquired extensive core, log, and testing data to
characterize the subsurface geology of the site. Coring was focused on the storage interval, the
Mt. Simon Sandstone, and the confining interval, the Eau Claire Formation. The core and log data
were used to evaluate the sedimentology and sequence stratigraphy, as well as to develop the
conceptual geologic model. This report includes the geological summaries of the Mt. Simon
Sandstone and the Eau Claire Formation. The extensive analysis of the log data is included in the
petrophysical section, showing ranges of porosity, estimated pore size, and the mineral content of
selected zones in the well. The separate petrographic technical report entitled “Petrographic and
Advanced Geologic Characterization Report on One Earth Energy #1 (API# 1211325373)”, report
number DOE-UIUC-0031892-04, details thin section point-counting analysis that includes
mineralogical and pore space analysis, including grain size analysis, annotated thin section
photomicrographs, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) with energy dispersive X-ray
spectroscopy (EDS), and statistics of grain size analysis on Mt. Simon thin sections from OEE #1.
The final OEE #1 well data to be included in this geology report is the routine core analysis of
both whole core plugs and rotary sidewall core plugs.
In addition to the OEE #1 well, four 2D seismic lines and a small 3D survey were acquired as part
of the overall subsurface geological characterization. This geology report references the seismic
interpretation report, entitled “One Earth Energy Site Seismic Interpretation Task 5.0”, report
number DOE-UIUC-0031892-07. This report details the stratigraphic and structural interpretation
of the 2D and 3D seismic data acquired at the One Earth Energy site. The 2D seismic data was
acquired in 2019 and 2021, and the 3D survey was acquired in 2022. The objectives of the seismic
programs were to contribute to the subsurface characterization of the Mt. Simon-Eau Claire
Storage Complex by evaluating the continuity of potential storage reservoirs and containment
intervals across the project area, and to determine if any geologic features are present that would
increase containment risk to the proposed carbon storage project.

INTRODUCTION
This report summarizes the geology interpreted from data acquired at the One Earth Energy site.
The primary data collection came from the drilling of the One Earth Energy #1 well. The well was
drilled to a total depth of 7,105’ MD in the Precambrian crystalline basement. A full suite of
standard geophysical logs were run, including Fullbore Microimaging (FMI) from 400’ MD to TD
at 7,105’ MD. Approximately 10 feet of core was recovered from the Ironton-Galesville
Sandstone, 59 feet of core was recovered from the Eau Claire Formation, and 183 feet of core was
recovered from the Mt. Simon Sandstone. 116 one-inch diameter rotary sidewall cores were
acquired from the St. Peter Sandstone, the Franconia Dolomite, the Ironton-Galesville Sandstone,
the Eau Claire Formation, and the Mt. Simon Sandstone. Additional data utilized in the subsurface
geological characterization included four 2D seismic lines totaling xx linear miles. Three of the
2D lines were acquired for the ISC project; the fourth line had already been acquired previously.
Finally, a small 3D seismic survey was acquired, with an area of approximately 7.5 square miles.

Geologic Setting of the Illinois Basin
The One Earth CCS project is sited within the Illinois Basin, an intracratonic basin that formed
during the early Paleozoic. The Illinois Basin (Figure 1) is bordered by a series of prominent
structures (Kolata and Nelson, 1990), including the Kankakee Arch to the northeast, Cincinnati
Arch at the east, Mississippi River Arch at the northwest, Ozark Dome to the southwest, and
Nashville Dome to the southeast. The New Madrid Rift System, including the Rough Creek
Graben and Reelfoot Rift, extends into the southernmost part of the Basin.

 1

Figure 1. Regional map of the central United States showing selected structural features within the Illinois Basin and
the major structural features that surround it (Finley, 2005).

The arches and domes that surround the Illinois Basin are structural features that contributed to
the basin’s evolution and extent. The Ozark Dome is a long-lived uplift cored by low density
granites and rhyolites (Nelson, 2010). The Kankakee Arch formed in Ordovician time, and the
Mississippi River Arch formed somewhat later after Early Pennsylvanian time (Nelson, 2010).
The Cincinnati Arch is a long-lived high with uplift episodes in both the Devonian and the
Carboniferous periods (“Cincinnati Arch”, 2017) The Nashville dome is considered a southward
geologic extension of the Cincinnati Arch (“Nashville Dome”, 2017). The Pascola Arch is “partly
responsible for closing off the southern end of the Illinois Basin.” (Nelson, 2010) and probably
developed in Late Pennsylvanian and Permian time (Nelson, 2010). These structural features have
contributed to the shape and extent of the Illinois Basin.
Precambrian crystalline rocks form the basement of the Illinois Basin. The oldest sedimentary
rocks in the Basin are the Middle Cambrian Mt. Simon Sandstone, which contains the injection

 2

zone for this project, and the Argenta sandstone, a stratigraphic unit that underlies the Mt. Simon
in the central part of the basin (Freiburg et al., 2015). The Mt. Simon system was deposited over
much of the current North American midcontinent in depositional environments including fluvial,
eolian, and marginal marine (Freiburg et al., 2014; Reesink et al., 2020). Precambrian topography
exerted substantial control over both the thickness and lithology of Mt. Simon deposits, including
shedding the feldspars that helped create high porosity arkosic zones.
The Mt. Simon Sandstone is conformably overlain by the Eau Claire Formation, which is the
primary confining zone for the Mt. Simon Storage Complex. The Eau Claire is the basal unit of
the Knox Group. The Knox Group is a thick succession of mostly marine, dominantly carbonate
(limestone and dolomite) formations with secondary siltstone, shale, and sandstone components
(Figure 2). The Knox Group was deposited across a large swath of the proto-North American
continent during the Cambrian and Ordovician (Fritz et al., 2012). Thick, dense dolomites within
the Knox (e.g., Oneota and Shakopee formations) are secondary confining zones that occur
between the Mt. Simon Storage Complex and the St. Peter Sandstone which is the lowermost
USDW (LUSDW).

Post-Knox deposits of the Ancell, Platteville, and Galena Groups (mostly carbonate formations)
are overlain by the Maquoketa Group, a primarily shaly-silty sequence that contain impermeable
strata serving as secondary confining zones to the storage site. After Maquoketa deposition, a thick
succession of Silurian through Pennsylvanian rocks (dominantly carbonates and shales, with lesser
amounts of sandstone) were deposited in the central Illinois area. These units include the
Devonian-Mississippian New Albany Shale Group that is an additional Tertiary confining zone at
the One Earth CCS storage site. The shallowest bedrock units in the One Earth CCS area are
Pennsylvanian in age that are covered by a few hundred feet of much younger, unconsolidated
sediments deposited during the multiple episodes of Quaternary glaciation.

INTRODUCTION
Regional Geological Setting
The Mt. Simon Sandstone represents one of the oldest sedimentary units in the Illinois Basin,
deposited during the Early to Middle Cambrian (Freiburg et al., 2014; Delpomdor, 2021, 2022b).
Regional isopach mapping indicates the Mt. Simon thickens northward and eastward, with
maximum thicknesses exceeding 2,400 feet in east central Illinois (Figure 3a). This distribution
pattern reflects syn-depositional subsidence controlled by early rift architecture and Precambrian
basement topography, with the primary depocenter located in northern and east-central Illinois
along the rift axis (Leetaru and McBride, 2009; Freiburg et al., 2014).
The Mt. Simon overlies the Argenta Formation, the oldest sedimentary unit in Illinois, which rests
unconformably on Precambrian crystalline basement of the Eastern Granite-Rhyolite Province
(1.4–1.1 Ga) (Van Schmus et al., 1996). The Argenta is thought to represent initial syn-rift
sedimentation in fluvial to alluvial environments adjacent to basement highs (Delpomdor, 2022b).
The Mt. Simon is overlain by the Upper Cambrian Eau Claire Formation, a regional confining unit
marking a major marine transgression (Figure 3b). Early Cambrian zircons identified in the
Argenta and Lower Mt. Simon indicate Early Cambrian-age volcanism and a culminating phase
of rifting in the basin (Freiburg et al., 2020).
The depositional evolution of the Argenta–Mt. Simon succession reflects the interplay between
active tectonic subsidence, basement topography, and sediment supply. Depositional processes
were controlled by eroded paleo-uplift features (monadnocks) on the Precambrian crystalline
basement, which generated differential tectonic movements across the proto-Illinois Basin
(Delpomdor, 2022b). Coarse-grained sediment supply from basement sources initially filled
incised valleys and paleohighs during Argenta deposition. With decreasing tectonic subsidence
and expansion of the drainage basin, basement paleo-highs were progressively buried, eventually
drowned by fluvio-deltaic sediments in the Upper Mt. Simon to overlying Eau Claire Formation
(Delpomdor, 2022b).
The Upper Cambrian Eau Claire Formation is a regional confining unit marking a major marine
transgression that established fully marine conditions across the Illinois Basin (Figure 3). Regional
studies document an overall transgressive depositional trend from nonmarine to marine
environments through the Mt. Simon succession, with two internal sequences of fluvial to fluvio-
eolian environments evident before the major Sauk transgression (Freiburg et al., 2014).

Stratigraphic Framework at OEE #1
At the One Earth Energy Well #1 site (OEE #1), the Argenta–Mt. Simon succession extends from
approximately 6,918’ MD at the Precambrian basement contact to 4,455’ MD at the base of the
Elmhurst Sandstone, representing 2,463 feet of section. Based on wireline log character and
regional correlations, the succession is subdivided into five units (Figure 4): Precambrian
Basement (6,918’ MD), Argenta Formation (6,469’–6,918’ MD; 449 ft thick), Arkose Zone
(6,262’–6,469’ MD; 207 ft thick), Lower Mt. Simon Sandstone (5,969’–6,262’ MD; 293 ft thick),
Middle Mt. Simon Sandstone (5,181–5,969’ MD; 788 ft thick), and Upper Mt. Simon Sandstone
(4,455’–5,181’ MD; 726 ft thick).
The Arkose Zone is a stratigraphic unit characterized by elevated feldspar content, high porosity.
Its stratigraphic affinity—whether representing uppermost Argenta or basal Mt. Simon—remains
a subject of interpretation discussed in Section 4.2.
Study Objectives and Scope
This preliminary report integrates sedimentological, petrographic, mineralogical, and
petrophysical datasets to characterize the Mt. Simon–Argenta succession at OEE #1. Primary
objectives include: (1) documenting lithological characteristics and depositional facies from core
descriptions; (2) characterizing petrographic composition and diagenetic evolution; (3) assessing
mineralogical variability through X-ray diffraction analysis; (4) evaluating petrophysical
properties from wireline logs; and (5) proposing preliminary depositional models.
Core recovery was limited to 183.3 feet near the basal interval (6,523.1’–6,339.8’ MD),
representing approximately 7.4% of the total succession (Figure 4). Petrographic analysis utilized
158 thin sections, with detailed point-counting on 58 Mt. Simon samples (see “Petrographic and
Advanced Geologic Characterization Report on One Earth Energy #1 (API# 1211325373)”, DOE-

entire interval, while X-ray diffraction analysis provided mineralogical characterization on
selected samples. All interpretations presented herein are explicitly preliminary and subject to

METHODOLOGY
Core Description
Whole core was described following standard sedimentological procedures, documenting
lithology, grain size (Wentworth scale), sorting, color, sedimentary structures, bounding surfaces,
and trace fossil occurrences (Delpomdor, 2022a). Sedimentary structures were characterized with

 8

attention to bedding geometry, cross-stratification style, and soft-sediment deformation. This
preliminary core logging forms the primary basis for initial interpretation of the depositional
environments.
Petrographic Analysis
Thin sections were prepared from rotary sidewall cores and whole core samples. For details, see
“Petrographic and Advanced Geologic Characterization Report on One Earth Energy #1 (API#
1211325373)”, DOE-UIUC-0031892-04. All sections were digitized using a Zeiss Axioscan under
10× objective with plain polarized light (PPL), cross-polarized light (XPL), and circular polarized
light (CPL). Point counting analysis employed a Petrog automated stage with 400 points for
mineralogy and 100 points for texture. Cathodoluminescence imaging distinguished authigenic
from detrital phases.
Grain size distribution and sorting were determined through digital image analysis. Rock
classification follows Dott (1964) and Folk (1980), wherein sandstones with <15% clay matrix are
arenites, 15–50% are wackes, and >50% are mudstones. Compositional classification uses ternary
diagrams based on quartz, feldspar, and lithic proportions. Porosity was quantified using point-
counting and digital phase analysis with blue epoxy-impregnation. Porosity types were categorized
as primary intergranular, secondary dissolution, and microporosity.
X-ray Diffraction Mineralogy
XRD analyses were completed at Schlumberger TerraTek Laboratory and the Illinois State
Geological Survey. Samples were prepared as powdered whole-rock mounts and oriented clay
separates. Analyses provided semi-quantitative data on bulk mineralogy and clay mineral
assemblages for selected samples.
Petrophysical Analysis
Petrophysical log data include gamma ray, total porosity, and calculated permeability across the
Argenta–Mt. Simon interval (Figure 4). Gamma ray response provides lithological discrimination
and clay content estimation. Porosity logs allow continuous profiling, calibrated to core
measurements. Permeability was calculated using empirical relationships (Juhasz, 1979). Log data
characterizes broad stratigraphic trends and unit-level reservoir properties.

RESULTS
Argenta Formation (6,469’–6,918’ MD)
Sedimentology
The lower 54 feet of recovered core (6,523.1’–6,469’ MD) represents the uppermost Argenta
Formation. The interval consists predominantly of brown-red to pale brown-red, very coarse-
grained to pebbly sandstone with subordinate coarse- to medium-grained beds (Figure 5a). Local
bleaching produces whitish to pale tan coloration. Sorting ranges from poor to moderate. Grain
composition is dominated by subrounded to subangular quartz with minor feldspar; granules and
pebbles are predominantly quartz. Cement consists of hematitic clay matrix in unbleached zones
and variable silica and clay in bleached intervals.
Sedimentary structures include massive bedding in very coarse intervals, planar parallel
lamination, and both low-angle (5–10°) and higher-angle (10–25°) planar and trough cross-
bedding. Normal grading appears in thin intervals of alternating pebbly and coarse-grained
sandstone. Rare features include climbed ripples, wavy lamination, and isolated "floating"

 9

granules. Erosive contacts dominate at both unit bases and tops. The vertical succession shows
multiple fining-upward sequences (1–3 feet thick) interrupted by erosive surfaces reintroducing
coarse material. Liesegang banding occurs in bleached zones, indicating post-depositional fluid
migration. Variable cementation intensity suggests complex diagenetic fluid flow.
The Argenta Formation is interpreted as a high-energy braided fluvial system with coarse bedload
transport. The dominance of very coarse to pebbly sandstone, poor sorting, abundant erosive
contacts, and prevalence of cross-bedding are diagnostic of braided channel and bar facies. The
lack of significant mudstone suggests proximal setting with limited floodplain preservation.
Provenance from Precambrian basement highlands is indicated by coarse grain size and arkosic
mineralogy.
Petrography
Petrographically, the Argenta Formation is dominated by quartz arenite, with grain composition
reflecting variable feldspar content and clay cementation. Quartz grains are angular to subangular
and poorly sorted, ranging from fine to very coarse sizes (Figure 6a). Framework mineralogy is
predominantly quartz and clay minerals—both detrital and authigenic—with minimal to moderate
feldspar content. XRD confirms quartz dominance (38–99 wt.%, averaging 90%), with K-feldspar
content averaging 9%. A single arkosic interval sample contains 58% K-feldspar reflecting
temporal heterogeneity. Clay minerals include illite (<1–3%, mean <1%), with subordinate
chlorite and kaolinite identified petrographically. Hematite occurs as both cement and grain
coatings, imparting characteristic red-brown coloration to unbleached intervals. Toward the top of
the Argenta, detrital clay content decreases and feldspar becomes slightly more abundant,
suggesting a compositional transition toward the overlying Arkose Zone. The quartz-rich
composition with heterogeneous feldspar distribution and clay cementation reflects basement-
proximal provenance with variable weathering intensity during deposition.
Petrophysics
Wireline log data show the Argenta exhibits variable gamma ray response (50–100 API), reflecting
heterogeneous lithology with interbedded sandstone and clay-rich intervals. Total porosity
averages 10.0% (range 5.9–15.2%), with calculated permeability averaging 7.6 mD (range 0.04–
47 mD). The low permeability reflects clay cementation, poor sorting, and hematite precipitation.
The heterogeneity indicates interbedded channel sandstones and lower-permeability muddy
intervals.

Arkose Zone (6,262’–6,469’ MD)
Sedimentology
The upper 129 feet of core (6,469’–6,339.8’ MD) is dominated by pale brown-red to brown-red,
medium- to coarse-grained arkosic sandstone with local very coarse intervals (Figure 5b). Color
varies with subordinate greenish-gray intervals associated with muddy zones. Sorting is moderate
to moderately well developed, notably better than the underlying Argenta. Grain composition
includes abundant feldspar (15–30%) in addition to quartz.
Within this section, the sandstone commonly displays cross-bedding with dip angles ranging from
5° to 25°. Widespread green-to-red mud clasts and pebbles (1–2 inches in diameter) are interpreted
as rip-up clasts derived from eroded, semi-consolidated muddy substrates. Isolated intervals
exhibiting near-vertical, burrow-like bioturbation—tentatively identified as Skolithos (Delpomdor,
2022a)—provide limited evidence of brackish to marine influence. Heterolithic facies with flaser
and lenticular bedding appear in several places, indicating tidal influence, and a single thin bed
contains symmetrical ripples consistent with wave action. Soft-sediment deformation features,
including water escape and flame structures, are present in multiple locations, consistent with rapid
deposition. A ~1 ft section displays upward-steepening lamination transitioning into high-angle
cross-bedding (~25°), consistent with wind-driven dune architecture and subaerial exposure.
The dominant features—cross-bedded arkosic sandstone, erosive contacts, massive bedding, and
abundant rip-up clasts—are consistent with fluvial channel deposition and reworking of adjacent
muddy substrates, likely from overbank or interdistributary areas. Moderate sorting and high
feldspar content suggest a proximal, basement-derived sediment source. Episodic indicators of
marine influence, including trace-fossil bioturbation and heterolithic tidal facies, point to brief
incursions during transgressive pulses. Localized evidence of aeolian processes and subaerial
exposure further supports intermittent shifts in depositional energy and base level. Collectively,
the facies assemblage supports interpretation of a fluvial-dominated coastal plain system with tidal
and aeolian overprints, reflecting dynamic environmental transitions during Early Cambrian sea-
level fluctuations.
Petrography
Petrographically, the zone is classified as subarkose to arkose, distinguished by elevated feldspar
content and a coarse-grained texture. Detrital K-feldspar comprises approximately 15–30% of the
framework grains—substantially higher than in overlying Mt. Simon units—while quartz remains
dominant at 50–65% (Figure 6b). XRD confirms the arkosic composition, with quartz ranging
from 34–99 wt.% (mean 77%), K-feldspar averaging 22% and negligible plagioclase feldspar
(<0.3%). The wide compositional range reflects vertical heterogeneity, with cleaner quartz-rich
intervals interbedded with feldspathic zones. Lithic fragments, including granitic and metamorphic
clasts, are more abundant than in overlying formations, indicating proximal source areas and
minimal chemical weathering.
Grain size ranges from medium to coarse sand, with localized very coarse intervals. Sorting is poor
to moderate, and grains are typically subangular to subrounded. Compaction varies from moderate
to significant across the interval. Authigenic quartz cement is present but volumetrically minor.
Hematitic clay matrix is prominent in unbleached intervals, whereas bleached zones are dominated
by clay minerals. XRD analysis indicates clay content ranges from 2–7 wt.% (mean 4%), with
illite as the dominant clay mineral (0.2–4.1 wt.%, mean 1.0%), followed by chlorite (0.2–1.0 wt.%,
mean 0.6%) and kaolinite (0.1–2.3 wt.%, mean 0.7%). Feldspar grains exhibit variable degrees of
alteration, contributing to secondary porosity through partial dissolution.

 13

Porosity is primarily intergranular in cleaner, better-sorted intervals, while secondary porosity
from feldspar dissolution contributes modestly to overall pore space. The high K-feldspar content
explains the elevated gamma ray response (60–100 API) observed in wireline logs, as potassium-
40 radioactivity from K-feldspar dominates over clay-related radioactivity. The arkosic
composition and mineralogical characteristics support a depositional interpretation involving
proximal sediment sources, likely derived from granitic basement rocks with limited transport
distance or chemical weathering.
Petrophysics
The Arkose Zone exhibits exceptional reservoir quality with the highest porosity and permeability
of the entire succession (Figure 4). Total porosity averages 14.8% (range 3.6–20.9%), substantially
higher than the underlying Argenta Formation. Calculated permeability averages 213 mD (range
0.002–900 mD), nearly 30 times higher than the Argenta. Gamma ray values are moderate to
elevated (60–100 API), reflecting increased K-feldspar and variable clay content.
Heterogeneity within the zone is notable, with porosity varying by ~17 % and permeability
spanning three orders of magnitude. This variability reflects interbedded clean sandstone and
muddy heterolithic facies. The exceptional reservoir quality aligns with regional observations from
the IDBP, where the arkosic-rich interval of the Lower Mt. Simon exhibited the highest porosity
(27%) of the stratigraphic succession (Freiburg et al., 2014).
Lower Mt. Simon Sandstone (5,969’–6,262’ MD)
Petrographic analysis of rotary sidewall cores reveals variable composition ranging from quartz
arenite to subarkose. Feldspar content (5–20%) is elevated relative to Middle and Upper Mt.
Simon. Quartz dominates at 55–70%. Grain size ranges from medium to very coarse sand,
representing the coarsest Mt. Simon interval. Sorting is poor to moderate, and grains are
subangular to subrounded. Authigenic quartz cement is more abundant than in overlying units,
ranging from minor to moderate volumes. Clay minerals include illite and mixed-layer clays as
pore-filling cement and grain coatings. Feldspar alteration to clay minerals (kaolinite, illite) is
common. Compaction is moderate to significant with long and concavo-convex grain contacts.
Primary porosity is very limited. The dominant porosity type is oversized porosity from total grain
dissolution—complete removal of framework grains leaving pores significantly larger than
surrounding grains (Figure 6c). Critically, oversized porosity provides good conductivity and
permeability despite moderate total porosity values.
Wireline logs show moderate gamma ray (40–70 API), reflecting moderate feldspar and sparse
clay content. Porosity averages 9.3% with calculated permeability averaging 17.5 mD. The
moderate values reflect trade-offs between diagenetic cementation and secondary porosity
development.
The Lower Mt. Simon likely reflects more proximal conditions with coarser, less well-sorted
sediment. Increased feldspar and lithics indicate less weathering and shorter transport, consistent
with basement-proximal alluvial or fluvial deposition.
Middle Mt. Simon Sandstone (5,181’–5,969’ MD)
Petrographic analysis reveals the Middle Mt. Simon is classified entirely as quartz arenite,
representing the most mineralogically mature interval. Detrital quartz comprises 65–75% with
feldspar typically <5%. Lithic fragments include granitic clasts serving as sites of secondary
porosity development (Figure 6d). Grain size ranges from fine to medium sand, coarser and less
well sorted than the Upper Mt. Simon. Grains are subrounded to subangular.

 14

A distinctive characteristic is high abundance of partial dissolution porosity (Figure 6d). Partial
dissolution is particularly prevalent within lithic granitic clasts, where feldspar components have
been selectively dissolved while leaving intergranular quartz and oxide frameworks. The
interconnected dissolution porosity contributes to effective permeability. Authigenic quartz is
present but volumetrically minor. Clay minerals are sparse. X-ray diffraction shows quartz
dominance (70–85%) with minimal feldspar (<5%) and low clay content, confirming high
mineralogical maturity.
The Middle Mt. Simon records enhanced chemical diagenesis with extensive feldspar dissolution.
The mechanism likely involves interaction with acidic formation waters during burial. Granitic
lithic clasts acted as susceptible substrates for dissolution, as feldspar is more reactive than quartz.
The quartz-rich composition provides framework strength limiting compaction.
Wireline logs show the lowest gamma ray of the succession (20–50 API), consistent with quartz
arenite composition and minimal clay. Porosity averages 9.9% with calculated permeability
averaging 21.9 mD. The moderate porosity despite increased diagenesis reflects secondary
dissolution porosity offsetting compaction and cementation effects.
The Middle Mt. Simon's quartz arenite composition (>95% quartz) and improved sorting indicate
substantial sediment reworking and increased transport distance or recycling. While direct
sedimentological observations are unavailable due to lack of core, the mineralogical maturity
combined with regional analogues suggests deposition in high-energy fluvial to coastal
environments, potentially including eolian reworking.
Upper Mt. Simon Sandstone (4,455’–5,181’ MD)
The Upper Mt. Simon is classified as quartz arenite to subarkose. Detrital mineralogy consists of
59–75% quartz, 5–15% feldspar (predominantly K-feldspar), and trace lithics. Quartz grains are
subrounded to well-rounded. Feldspar shows variable alteration from fresh to partially dissolved.
Clay content is generally low (<10%). Grain size ranges from very fine to medium sand, with most
samples fine- to lower medium-grained. Sorting is moderate to moderately well developed,
superior to underlying units. Grain contacts range from point contacts in high-porosity samples to
tangential and long contacts in more compacted intervals. Some samples exhibit bimodal grain
size distributions.
Quartz overgrowth cement is common, particularly toward the top, though typically <10% by
volume (Figure 6e). Overgrowths are identified by dust rims and cathodoluminescence imaging.
Feldspar overgrowths occur on some K-feldspar grains. Clay minerals (illite, chlorite) occur as
grain coatings and pore linings. Compaction is mild to moderate with preservation of primary
porosity. X-ray diffraction confirms quartz dominance (60–75%) with moderate K-feldspar (5–
15%). Clay content is low with illite as the dominant clay mineral. Minor phases include hematite
and carbonate cements.
The Upper Mt. Simon exhibits high porosity and permeability (Figure 4). Wireline logs show low
to moderate gamma ray (30–60 API), reflecting subarkose composition with modest feldspar
content. Porosity averages 11.4% (locally exceeding 15%) with calculated permeability averaging
41.4 mD (locally >100 mD). Porosity is predominantly primary intergranular with interconnected
pore throats providing good permeability. Some secondary dissolution porosity occurs through
partial feldspar dissolution.

 15

The Upper Mt. Simon's fine grain size, high textural maturity, and occasionally subarkosic
composition permits multiple depositional interpretations. The dominant quartz arenite intervals
could reflect either: (1) shallow marine reworking where waves and currents winnowed feldspars
and fines, (2) eolian processing in coastal or inland dune fields where wind abrasion produced
mature sands, or (3) extensive fluvial recycling through multiple transport cycles. The occasional
preservation of feldspar (producing subarkosic intervals) suggests episodic rapid sediment influx
that bypassed normal weathering processes, possibly from nearby basement sources during floods
or progradation of alluvial fans into the depositional system. Without core, distinguishing between
shallow marine, eolian, and fluvial origins remains speculative, though the upward transition to
the marine Eau Claire Formation indicates that marine influence eventually dominated, whether
or not it was present throughout Upper Mt. Simon deposition.
Diagenetic Trends
The Mt. Simon–Argenta succession exhibits systematic diagenetic trends controlling reservoir
quality distribution. Diagenetic intensity generally increases downward with more advanced
compaction, quartz cementation, and clay authigenesis in lower units. Key diagenetic processes
include mechanical compaction, quartz and feldspar overgrowth cementation, clay mineral
transformation (smectite to illite), chemical compaction through pressure solution, and feldspar
dissolution creating secondary porosity.
Secondary porosity development through feldspar dissolution is most extensive in the Middle Mt.
Simon (partial dissolution within granitic lithic fragments) and Lower Mt. Simon (oversized pores
from total grain dissolution) (Figure 6). The timing and mechanism of dissolution likely involve
interaction with organic acids or meteoric waters, though precise constraints are lacking.
Reservoir quality distribution reflects the balance between porosity-destructive (compaction,
cementation) and porosity-constructive (dissolution) processes. The Upper Mt. Simon exhibits the
highest quality of Mt. Simon proper due to limited diagenetic modification preserving primary
porosity. The Arkose Zone exhibits the highest overall reservoir quality of the entire succession,
with exceptional porosity and permeability reflecting compositional and textural characteristics
combined with moderate diagenetic modification. The Argenta Formation shows the lowest
reservoir quality due to heterogeneity, clay cementation, poor sorting, and hematite precipitation.

DISCUSSION
Depositional Environments
Argenta Formation
The Argenta Formation at OEE #1 likely represents deposition in a high-energy braided fluvial
system draining Precambrian basement highlands. Sediment was derived from weathering of
granitic and granodioritic basement, producing arkosic detritus with minimal chemical weathering.
The coarse grain size, poor sorting, and abundance of erosive surfaces indicate high-gradient,
bedload-dominated transport in migrating braided channels.
Depositional facies include channel thalweg deposits (very coarse to pebbly sandstone with
massive bedding), bar facies (planar and trough cross-bedded coarse sandstone representing
transverse and linguoid bar migration), and upper bar and channel margin facies (medium to coarse
sandstone with low-angle cross-stratification). The lack of significant mudstone suggests limited
floodplain development or preferential preservation of channel facies. The red-brown coloration
and hematitic cement indicate oxidizing conditions typical of continental systems.

 16

The Argenta Formation regionally is interpreted as proximal alluvial fan to braided fluvial deposits
associated with basement-derived sediment shed from highlands during Early Cambrian rifting
and subsidence (Delpomdor, 2022b). At OEE #1, the Argenta represents the initial phase of basin
filling with high sediment supply and limited accommodation.
Arkose Zone
The stratigraphic assignment of the Arkose Zone at OEE #1 remains uncertain, with evidence
supporting affinities to the Argenta and Mt. Simon formations. Support for Argenta assignment
includes the tentative identification of marine trace fossils and the presence of heterolithic facies
with flaser and lenticular bedding—features commonly associated with marine-influenced
Argenta strata (Freiburg et al., 2014; Delpomdor, 2021, 2022b). Regional studies, such as those
from the Illinois Basin Decatur Project (IBDP) located 47 mi from the OEE well (Figure 3a),
document Skolithos burrows in the Argenta Formation and note their absence in the overlying Mt.
Simon (Freiburg et al., 2014). Regionally, the arkosic zone (IDBP and T.R. McMillen) are
assigned to the Lower Mt. Simon and are generally interpreted as continental environments that
lack marine indicators (Freiburg et al., 2014).
Conversely, several lines of evidence support a Mt. Simon assignment. The arkosic mineralogy is
consistent with the basal Mt. Simon regionally, and the dominant sedimentological features at OEE
#1—medium-grained cross-bedding, erosive contacts, and abundant mud clasts—align with
fluvial channel deposition. Additionally, the presence of aeolian facies suggests wind reworking
within a broader continental setting. The marine evidence may only reflect brief marine incursions
superimposed on an otherwise continental system. A definitive assignment will require more
detailed ichnological, geochemical, and regional stratigraphic analysis.
Mt. Simon Sandstone
The Mt. Simon Sandstone (Lower, Middle, Upper) was not cored at OEE #1, limiting direct
sedimentological interpretation. Petrographic textures and mineralogy suggest predominantly
continental deposition. The Lower Mt. Simon, with coarser grains and elevated feldspar, likely
reflects proximal fluvial environments with active basement-derived sediment input. The Middle
Mt. Simon, composed of well-sorted quartz arenite and exhibiting very low gamma ray readings,
may represent eolian dune or sand sheet deposits, consistent with regional analogs from the Illinois
Basin. The Upper Mt. Simon shows moderate sorting and mineralogical maturity, suggesting
continued eolian to fluvial deposition, with possible marine influence near the transition to the Eau
Claire Formation. These interpretations remain preliminary and speculative, relying on
petrographic character and regional stratigraphic correlation in the absence of core-based
sedimentological data.
4.2 Diagenesis and Reservoir Quality Controls
Diagenetic patterns at OEE #1 reflect the interplay between mineralogical composition, burial
history, and fluid-rock interactions. The Arkose Zone exhibits exceptional reservoir quality (14.8%
porosity, 213 mD permeability) despite elevated gamma ray values (60–100 API). XRD analysis
demonstrates that K-feldspar content (1–60 wt.%, mean 22%) drives the elevated gamma response
rather than clay content (2–7 wt.%, mean 4%), as potassium-40 radioactivity from feldspar
dominates over clay-related signatures. Feldspar dissolution has created secondary porosity while
preserving primary intergranular pore networks, explaining the zone's exceptional permeability.
Secondary porosity development through feldspar dissolution is most extensive in the Middle Mt.
Simon, where partial dissolution within granitic lithic fragments creates interconnected pore
networks, and in the Lower Mt. Simon, where total grain dissolution produces oversized pores that

 17

enhance permeability despite moderate porosity values. The Upper Mt. Simon preserves primary
intergranular porosity due to limited diagenetic modification and low feldspar content (5–15% by
XRD). In contrast, the Argenta Formation exhibits the lowest reservoir quality (10.0% porosity,
7.6 mD permeability) due to heterogeneous clay cementation, poor sorting, and hematite
precipitation.
These patterns are consistent with observations from the IBDP, where the arkosic interval similarly
exhibited the highest porosity (27%) of the stratigraphic succession (Freiburg et al., 2014).
Regionally, reservoir quality distribution reflects the balance between porosity-destructive
processes (mechanical compaction, quartz cementation, clay authigenesis) and porosity-
constructive processes (feldspar dissolution). The feldspar-rich zones paradoxically yield superior
reservoir quality because dissolution creates secondary porosity and enhances pore connectivity
more effectively than mechanical processes can reduce it.

CONCLUSIONS
This preliminary assessment characterizes the Mt. Simon–Argenta succession at OEE #1 based on
183.3 feet of core (7.4% recovery) supplemented by petrographic, mineralogical, and
petrophysical data from the 2,463-foot interval.
Key findings include:
 1. The Argenta Formation (6,469’–6,918’ MD) represents high-energy braided fluvial deposition
 with very coarse to pebbly sandstone, poor sorting, and abundant erosive surfaces. Porosity
 averages 10.0% with permeability averaging 7.6 mD.
 2. The Arkose Zone (6,262’–6,469’ MD) contains medium- to coarse-grained arkosic sandstone
 with 15–30% feldspar content. Limited marine indicators occur within predominantly continental
 siliciclastic sandstones.
 3. The Mt. Simon Sandstone proper (4,455’–6,262’ MD) transitions from coarse, feldspar-bearing
 sandstone (Lower) through quartz arenite with extensive dissolution features (Middle) to
 mineralogically mature, moderately sorted sandstone (Upper).
 4. Diagenetic modification includes mechanical compaction, quartz cementation, feldspar
 dissolution creating secondary porosity, and clay mineral authigenesis.
 5. The stratigraphic assignment of the Arkose Zone remains unresolved—marine indicators suggest
 Argenta affinity while arkosic composition and fluvial architecture support Lower Mt. Simon
 assignment.
Interpretations are preliminary given limited core coverage, absence of complete sedimentological
observation through most intervals, and lack of biostratigraphic control. Depositional environment
interpretations for uncored Mt. Simon intervals rely on petrographic textures and regional
analogues rather than direct observation. The mixed continental-marine signals in the Arkose Zone
require additional analysis to resolve stratigraphic position. All findings should be considered
tentative pending additional data acquisition and integration with regional stratigraphic
frameworks.

CHAPTER 2 SEDIMENTOLOGY AND SEQUENCE STRATIGRAPHY OF
THE EAU CLAIRE FORMATION
INTRODUCTION
The Eau Claire Formation, a prominent Upper Cambrian stratigraphic unit (Figure 7), is widely
recognized across the Midwestern United States and plays a crucial role in understanding the
tectonostratigraphic evolution of the Illinois Basin. This formation is characterized by a diverse
assemblage of siliciclastic and carbonate lithologies, indicative of dynamic depositional conditions
during a major marine transgression across the North American craton.
Regionally extensive, the Eau Claire Formation is documented in Illinois, Indiana, Wisconsin,
Michigan (Cohee, 1948a), Minnesota (Stauffer and Thiel, 1941), and Iowa (Steinhilber et al.,
1961). Its thickness varies considerably across the basin, reflecting both depositional processes
and subsidence patterns. At its type section in Eau Claire County, Wisconsin, it measures
approximately 100 feet (Cohee, 1948b). Moving southward into western Illinois, the formation
thickens to around 300 feet, increasing further to 700 feet in southwestern Indiana (Kolata, 2010).
However, in southwest Illinois in Perry County, there is a small area where the formation rapidly
thins to zero thickness, likely due to the presence of a preserved Precambrian basement high that
was never eroded flat. In far southern Illinois, it exceeds 1,200 feet and reaches a maximum
thickness of approximately 3,000 feet in the Rough Creek Graben of western Kentucky (Kolata,
2010). This progressive southward thickening reflects enhanced subsidence and greater
accommodation space toward the axis of the Illinois Basin, particularly near structural lows such
as the Rough Creek Graben.

 19

Figure 7. Stratigraphic column of the Cambro-Ordovician Succession in Illinois and Indiana (Lasemi, Y., and Askari
Z., 2022), modified from Kolata, 2005 and from Thompson et. al., 2016.

Lithologically, the Eau Claire Formation comprises a heterogeneous mix of fine- to medium-
grained sandstone, siltstone, shale, and interbedded dolomite. These lithologies record deposition
in a shallow marine to tidally influenced shelf environment, reflecting a complex interplay between
siliciclastic influx, carbonate production, and relative sea-level changes (Schwalb, 1982; Harris et

 20

al., 1995). Sedimentary structures such as ripple marks, cross-bedding, herringbone structures, and
widespread bioturbation provide further evidence of variable energy conditions, with periodic
storm influence. The presence of trace fossils such as Planolites, Skolithos, and Cruziana supports
interpretations of benthic colonization in environments ranging from intertidal flats to lower
shoreface and offshore transition zones.
Stratigraphically, the Eau Claire Formation lies conformably atop the Mt. Simon Sandstone, a
thick, regionally extensive sandstone unit that marks the onset of Cambrian sedimentation
following the Great Unconformity. In some areas, where the Mt. Simon is absent or thin, the Eau
Claire directly overlies the Precambrian crystalline basement. The Eau Claire is overlain by the
Galesville Sandstone in the central to northern Illinois Basin and by the Davis Member of the
Franconia Formation to the south (Willman et al., 1975).
Within northern Illinois, the Eau Claire Formation is subdivided into three distinct members, listed
from base to top (Figure 7): the Elmhurst Sandstone Member, characterized by fine- to medium-
grained, moderately sorted quartz arenites; the Lombard Dolomite Member, a carbonate-rich
interval with interbedded shales; and the Proviso Siltstone Member, composed primarily of fine-
grained siliciclastics and indicative of a more distal, offshore depositional environment.
Tectonically and sedimentologically, the Eau Claire Formation represents a key interval within the
Sauk transgressive sequence (Sloss, 1963; Kolata, 2010). It captures the transition from
dominantly fluvial to shallow marine sandstone systems of the Mt. Simon Sandstone to more
widespread marine sedimentation associated with the advancing Cambrian seas. The progressive
marine flooding across the Laurentian margin resulted in the deposition of fine-grained siliciclastic
and carbonate sediments, signaling a shift from high-energy nearshore to lower-energy shallow
offshore environments.
A key aspect is that the Eau Claire Formation also plays a critical role in subsurface carbon
sequestration efforts across the Midwest. It serves as an effective regional seal over the Mt. Simon
Sandstone, which is one of the primary targets for deep saline aquifer CO₂ storage due to its high
porosity and permeability. The fine-grained nature and lateral continuity of the Eau Claire's shales
and carbonates contribute to its sealing capacity, making it a key component in long-term geologic
carbon storage strategies.
In sum, the Eau Claire Formation not only records a significant phase in early Paleozoic
sedimentation but also provides crucial insights into basin evolution, Cambrian
paleoenvironments, and the stratigraphic framework necessary for understanding both resource
potential and environmental applications within the Illinois Basin and beyond.

METHODOLOGY
Due to the lack of surface exposure of the Eau Claire Formation within the OEE study area, this
research is based on lithostratigraphic and sedimentological analyses derived from multiple
subsurface data sources, including borehole geophysical logs, core samples, thin section analyses,
and drillers’ diaries. A total of 56.6 feet of 4-inch diameter core was available from the OEE #1
well (Figure 8), covering depths from 4,366.6’ to 4,310’ MD (, Figure 10). Gamma ray, bulk
density, and neutron porosity logs were acquired across the entire thickness of the Eau Claire
Formation in the OEE #1 well, from 4,440’ to 3,930’ MD. Lithological interpretation was
primarily conducted using core, supported by the geophysical logs. Sedimentological features
(e.g., grain size, sedimentary structures, fossil content) were documented directly from the core.

 21

These observations were then correlated with log signatures to establish a preliminary lithological
model based on geophysical data. This model was subsequently used to interpret lithology at
intervals where core was not available.
A total of 12 thin sections were studied from this well (Figure 11), prepared from both whole core
and sidewall core samples at multiple depths. These thin sections were examined under a
petrographic microscope to refine lithological interpretations and to provide additional insight into
the depositional environment inferred from the geophysical logs.
In addition to the OEE #1 well (UWI: 121132537300), cores from the lower Eau Claire Formation
were analyzed from several key wells across the central Illinois Basin (Figure 8), including:
 • The FutureGen well in Morgan County (UWI: 121372213200)
 • Three wells from the Illinois Basin – Decatur Project in Macon County (CCS1 (UWI:
           121152341500), VW1 (UWI: 121152346000), and VW2 (UWI: 121152355200))
 • The Covert well in Henderson County (UWI: 120710031300)

RESULTS
Core descriptions
The recovered 4-inch diameter core, extracted from a depth interval of 4,310’ to 4,366.6’ MD,
exhibits a remarkably well-preserved stratigraphic sequence dominated by fine-grained, highly
fissile, and finely laminated black shale (Figure 10). At the base of the core, beginning at 4,366.6’
MD, the sequence begins with an interpreted glauconitic storm bed containing abundant shell
fragments and displaying well-defined crossbedding, characteristic of episodic high-energy
depositional events—likely related to storm reworking in a shallow marine shelf setting. This

 24

glauconitic interval continues up to 4,365.5’ MD, above which the lithology transitions sharply
into organic-rich, laminated black shale (Figure 10).

Figure 10. Core samples recovered from the OEE #1 well at various depths.

The overlying shale is extremely fissile and consistently laminated, suggesting deposition under
persistently low-energy, anoxic to dysoxic marine conditions, ideal for the preservation of organic
matter. Throughout the shale interval, occasional thin, lenticular storm deposits with glauconite
grains are interbedded, pointing to intermittent distal storm influences or turbidity currents that
disturbed the otherwise quiescent depositional regime. The shale remains compositionally

 25

consistent throughout most of the core, reflecting a relatively stable depositional setting with
limited siliciclastic influx. The black shale interval contains ductile clay and organic material that
is ideal for a confining unit of an underlying energy storage horizon.
A notable feature is observed at 4,312’ MD (Figure 10), where a 1-foot thick interval of black
shale displays a distinct oily texture, although lacking a strong hydrocarbon odor. This suggests
localized zones of bitumen staining or early-stage hydrocarbon maturation, likely driven by
elevated thermal maturity within this part of the formation.
Overall, this section of the formation represents a classic transgressive systems tract, with a basal
condensed section overlain by organic-rich marine shale, an ideal configuration for source rock
development. Its preservation potential, coupled with local indications of hydrocarbon staining,
underscores its significance for both basin analysis and petroleum system evaluation. Future
studies, including TOC profiling, and sequence stratigraphic correlation, could help further define
the source rock potential and regional depositional controls of this interval.

core is available from depths of 4,310’ to 4,362’ MD, and thin section analysis was critical in
refining facies interpretations and correlating them with geophysical logs, especially in intervals
lacking core.

Log Interpretation
We interpreted the geophysical logs to infer the sedimentological characteristics of the OEE #1
well in intervals where core data is not available (Figure 9). We utilized gamma ray, neutron and
density porosity to infer the lithology based on the pattern we observed where core is available
along with the geophysical log.
As shown in Figure 10, core data from depths of 4,310’ to 4,260’ MD reveal highly organic black
shale interbedded with occasional storm deposits of siltstone and skeletal grains. By correlating
these core observations with geophysical logs from multiple wells, including OEE #1, VW1,
 28

FutureGen, and UPH3, we developed a lithological model. This model was then applied to
interpret the Eau Claire Formation in intervals where only geophysical log data are available
(Figure 9). Based on geophysical log interpretation, the following stratigraphic framework is
proposed:
 • 4,430–4,408’ MD: This lower interval of the Eau Claire Formation is interpreted as a heterolithic
 assemblage of sandstone, siltstone, and shale, likely representing a tidal flat or tidal channel
 environment.
 • 4,408–4,380’ MD: Despite high gamma ray readings suggesting a shaly composition, the density
 and neutron porosity logs indicate a sandy lithology. This interval is interpreted as feldspathic
 sandstone deposited in an upper shoreface setting.
 • 4,380–4,310’ MD: The lithology transitions to black shale deposited in an offshore or restricted
 circulation setting.
 • 4,310–4,260’ MD: Confirmed by core observation, this interval consists of black shale with
 intermittent storm-generated beds of siltstone and skeletal debris.
 • 4,260–4,150’ MD: Predominantly dolomitic shale, with a notable increase in sandstone and
 siltstone content between 4,200’ and 4,160’ MD.
 • 4,150–4,050’ MD: Characterized by heterolithic bedding of shale, siltstone, and sandstone,
 indicative of the lower portion of the upper shoreface.
 • 4,050’ MD to top of Eau Claire Formation: The interval up to the overlying Galesville
 Sandstone is primarily composed of sandstone and siltstone with minimal shale content,
 representing a higher energy upper shoreface environment. Occasional, thin carbonate layers are
 also interpreted, including a distinct 6-foot carbonate layer from 4,010’ to 4,016’ MD.

Depositional Model and Paleoenvironmental Evolution
Based on the core and geophysical log data from the OEE #1 well, a depositional model was
developed for various depths, as previously described. This model provides valuable insights into
the paleoenvironmental evolution of the area. Three distinct transgressive-regressive cycles can be
clearly identified in the OEE #1 core, as illustrated in Figure 9. These cycles form the basis for
interpreting the depositional evolution of the Eau Claire Formation as follows:
The Eau Claire Formation represents a significant episode in the tectonostratigraphic development
of the Illinois Basin during the Late Cambrian, reflecting a complex interplay of marine
transgression, sediment supply, and subsidence. The formation records a transition from the high-
energy, fluvial to marginal marine environments of the underlying Mt. Simon Sandstone to a
dominantly marine depositional regime characterized by low-energy black shales, tempestites, and
dolomitic intervals. This transition corresponds to the advance of the Sauk Sea across the craton,
resulting in the progressive flooding of the Laurentian margin. The widespread presence of
glauconite, skeletal carbonates, and finely laminated shales indicates deposition in a storm-
dominated shelf setting with significant periods of sediment starvation and organic matter
preservation. The shelf platform is shallow and as a result, minor fluctuations in sea level and
sediment input result in a complexly interbedded stratigraphic interval.
Stratigraphically, the Eau Claire Formation exhibits three well-defined transgressive-regressive
(T-R) cycles in the OEE #1 core, each capturing shifts from shallow to deeper marine conditions
and back. The Eau Claire Formation begins with tidally influenced sandstones/shales and evolve
upward into organic-rich shales and offshore carbonates before shallowing again into shoreface

 29

deposits. This completes the first cycle. The second cycle follows the same pattern as the first,
beginning with lower shoreface deposits, transitioning into offshore deposits during the
transgressive phase, and returning to upper shoreface deposits during regression. The third cycle
follows a similar transgressive pattern but exhibits a shallower regressive phase, culminating in
upper shoreface deposits with minimal shale content, indicating a higher-energy depositional
environment compared to earlier cycles. This uppermost interval is capped by a regional
unconformity or correlative conformity that separates the Eau Claire Formation from the overlying
Galesville Sandstone.
 The cyclic pattern in the Eau Claire Formation highlights the sensitivity of the basin to relative
sea-level changes, likely driven by a combination of eustatic fluctuations and regional tectonic
subsidence. The formation’s southward thickening—from ~100 feet in Wisconsin to over 3,000
feet in western Kentucky—further reflects increased accommodation space toward the basin axis
and areas of structural depression such as the Rough Creek Graben. The sequential evolution of
the Eau Claire Formation is shown in the figure below (Figure 12).

Conclusions
The Eau Claire Formation offers a record of Late Cambrian sedimentation and paleoenvironmental
evolution within the Illinois Basin. Integrating core descriptions, thin section analyses, and
geophysical log interpretation from the OEE #1 well and regional datasets, this study delineates a
complex stratigraphic framework characterized by three distinct transgressive-regressive cycles.
The alternating presence of black shale, tempestites, and dolomitic shale layers suggests a dynamic

 32

depositional system dominated by offshore sedimentation punctuated by episodic high-energy
storm events. The recurring presence of glauconite across multiple intervals reinforces the
interpretation of slow sedimentation rates under marine transgressive conditions, aligning with
widespread cratonic flooding during the Sauk Sequence.
Beyond its stratigraphic and paleoenvironmental significance, the Eau Claire Formation holds
considerable applied importance in subsurface resource management. Its laterally extensive, fine-
grained shales act as an effective regional seal above the Mt. Simon Sandstone, making it a key
component in carbon capture and storage (CCS) initiatives across the Midwest. Evidence of
localized hydrocarbon staining suggests that portions of the Eau Claire may also have functioned
as a source rock under suitable thermal conditions. These features highlight further geochemical
and thermal maturity analysis to evaluate the formation's role in early petroleum systems within
the Illinois Basin. The formation’s complex facies architecture and heterogeneity underscore the
need for high-resolution, integrated subsurface analysis in evaluating seal integrity, reservoir
continuity, and stratigraphic trapping mechanisms. As such, the Eau Claire Formation stands as
both a valuable archive of Cambrian sea-level and tectonic processes, and a critical asset in future
energy transition strategies.

CHAPTER 3 PETROPHYSICAL EVALUATION OF THE EAU CLAIRE
FORMATION AND MT. SIMON SANDSTONE
Petrophysical analysis of wireline data is one of the primary methods to characterize the reservoir
properties of the formations evaluated for CO2 injection and their associated confining zones. A
full suite of modern well logs including a standard triple combo (Gamma ray [GR], resistivity,
neutron-density porosity), spectral Gamma ray (SGR), photoelectric (Pe), Nuclear Magnetic
Resonance (NMR), and Elemental Capture Spectroscopy (ECS) logs were acquired at the OEE #1
well used for petrophysical interpretations of the Mt. Simon Sandstone storage complex and its
confining zone, Eau Claire Formation. Lithologic and mineralogy properties of the Mt. Simon and
Eau Claire Formation were assessed using petrophysical cross plots of Pe against bulk Density
(RHOZ). The average of NPHI and density porosity (DPHI) were determined to calculate the total
porosity of the intervals. Cross plots of thorium-potassium derived from the SGR log were used to
identify clay mineral type and to distinguish shale and clay-rich intervals from the pure sandstone,
limestone, and dolomite. The NMR logs were used to assess the presence of the clay mineral and
fine grain sediments and determine the bound water and free water in the pore spaces quantitively.

INJECTION ZONES:
Arkose
The Arkose unit consists of fine to medium- grained feldspar sandstone with massive and large
cross-stratification. The total thickness of the Arkose unit is 207 ft, containing porous and
permeable sandstone intervals. The natural GR log shows a range of 40 to 150 API for the entire
interval (Figure 13). The potassium contents of SGR log have a range of 2 to 18%, which is an
indication of feldspar rich sandstone, The crossplot of Pe against RHOZ shows a high range of
porosity from 7 to 24% (Figure 14). The top of the Arkose is identified by the presence of feldspar
sandstone with a high GR range and can be differentiated from Lower Mt. Simon intervals with
low GR values. The crossplot of thorium vs potassium shows that most of the clay contents are
mica (Figure 15) which most likely generated as a result of weathering of the feldspars.

SEISMIC INTERPRETATION OF 2D AND 3D SEISMIC DATA
SUMMARY
The following is a brief summary of a separate technical report “One Earth Energy Site Seismic
Interpretation Task 5.0”, report number DOE-UIUC-0031892-07. Please see this report for the
detailed seismic interpretation results.
The seismic interpretation report referenced above details the stratigraphic and structural
interpretation of the 2D and 3D seismic data acquired near Gibson City, Illinois. 2D seismic data
was acquired in 2019 and 2021, and the 3D survey was acquired in 2022. The objectives of the
seismic programs were to contribute to the subsurface characterization of the Mt. Simon-Eau
Claire Storage Complex by evaluating the continuity of potential storage reservoirs and
containment intervals across the project area, and to determine if any geologic features are present
that would increase containment risk to the proposed carbon storage project.
The seismic data confirms the stratigraphic continuity of the Mt. Simon Sandstone Arkose Zone
storage interval and the Eau Claire Formation containment interval across the project area. The
seismic data also indicates that there are faults that transect the Mt. Simon Arkose Zone Sandstone
storage reservoir within the modeled CO2 plume. However, the seismic data also shows that there
are no faults within the modeled CO2 plume that transect the containment interval Eau Claire
Formation. Within the area of the CO2 plume, all of the faults that transect the Mt. Simon Arkose
Zone Sandstone storage reservoir die out in the Lower Mt. Simon Formation and do not reach the
overlying Eau Claire containment formation. A small 3D survey acquired around the OEE #1
characterization well confirmed these findings.
