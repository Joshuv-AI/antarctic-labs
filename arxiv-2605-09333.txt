Integral elements of Okubo algebra and the E8-lattice
Daniele Corradetti

Abstract
. In this work we study the interplay between the Coxeter-Dickson E 8 E_{8} -order, the para-octonions, and the real Okubo algebra. We prove that the Coxeter-Dickson order remains closed for the para-octonionic product, so that one recovers a genuine ℤ \mathbb{Z} -integral system with underlying lattice E 8 E_{8} . Intriguingly, the Okubo product behaves in a different and more arithmetic way: it forces ℚ ⁡ ( 3 ) \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3}) -coefficients and does not preserve the same ℤ \mathbb{Z} -order. After a diagonal 2 2 -adic scaling we obtain a closed ℤ ⁡ [ 3 ] \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{3}] -order, whose direct metric shadow is a 2 2 -primary conductor sublattice of E 8 E_{8} , not E 8 E_{8} itself. The lattice E 8 E_{8} is recovered only by 2 2 -adic saturation, equivalently by gluing, and this recovery is metric-arithmetic rather than multiplicative.



introduction and motivations

Integral numbers in division algebras are a classical and fascinating topic at
the
interplay
of algebra, arithmetic, and exceptional geometry. Already
in the XIX century, Gauss
[
Gau
]
and Hurwitz
[
Hu19
]
extended the notion
of integers to the complex numbers and the quaternions, identifying the Gaussian
integers and the Hurwitz quaternions as natural integral structures. The culmination
of this classical line of research is the
Coxeter-Dickson octonions

\mathbb{O}_{E_{8}}

[
Co46
,
Di23
]
, the maximal order in the algebra of the
octonions
\mathbb{O}
, which is isometric to the exceptional lattice
E_{8}
.
This octonionic background is also close to modern exceptional geometry and to
the systematic study of octonionic Rosenfeld spaces

[
MCCAI23
]
.

The landscape of eight-dimensional real division algebras, however, extends well
beyond the octonions. As a consequence of the Generalised Hurwitz Theorem

[
El18
]
, among the eight-dimensional composition algebras over

\mathbb{R}
there are three non-isomorphic division cases: the
octonions

\mathbb{O}
(unital), the
para-octonions

p\mathbb{O}
(para-unital), and
the
real Okubo algebra

\mathcal{O}
(non-unital). While octonions have a
unit element and para-octonions have a para-unit, the Okubo algebra merely contains

idempotent
elements, and its automorphism group is
\mathrm{SU}(3)
rather
than
\mathrm{G}_{2}
. A synoptic comparison of these algebras is given in
Table
3
.
This non-unital direction has recently appeared in the study of Okubo projective
geometry, Okubo Spin groups, minimal Cayley-plane realisations, collineation
groups of octonionic planes, and possible physical uses of the Okubo algebra

[
CZ22
,
CMZ24
,
CMZ25
,
MCZ25
]
.

The question of integral elements for
p\mathbb{O}
and
\mathcal{O}
has, to
our knowledge, not been systematically separated from the classical unital case.
The definition due to Dickson
[
Di23
]
and refined by Johnson

[
Jo13
,
Jo17
,
Jo18
]
requires the presence of a unit element, and therefore
does not directly apply to non-unital algebras such as
p\mathbb{O}
and

\mathcal{O}
. In this work we isolate the part of Johnson’s definition that
survives for isotopes: closure under the relevant product, integrality of norm
and trace, and the relation with a discrete lattice or module.
For complementary viewpoints on eight-dimensional composition algebras, the
Cayley plane, and their recovery from geometric algebra, see also

[
Cor26
,
Cor24
]
.

The para-octonionic case gives a clean result. Since the Coxeter-Dickson order

\mathbb{O}_{E_{8}}
is closed under octonionic conjugation and multiplication,
it is also closed under
x\bullet y=\overline{x}\cdot\overline{y}
; hence the
same
E_{8}
lattice supports a
\mathbb{Z}
-integral para-octonionic structure.
This is a perfect analogy with the octonionic case.

The Okubo case is subtler and, to our knowledge, this is the first time that its
integral relation with the Coxeter-Dickson order is made completely explicit.
The Petersson product
x*y=\tau(\overline{x})\cdot\tau^{2}(\overline{y})

involves an automorphism whose matrix has coefficients in

\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3})
, and already on basic integral elements this introduces
coefficients outside
\mathbb{Z}
. The exact computation shows that a diagonal

2
-adic scaling produces an order over
R=\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{3}]
, but its direct
rank-eight shadow is not
E_{8}
. Rather, it is a conductor sublattice inside

E_{8}
of index
2^{12}
and determinant
2^{24}
.

It is worth noting that this does not make the title literal in a naive sense.
The Okubo integral structure does not form the
E_{8}
lattice over

\mathbb{Z}
. Instead, it gives rise to a
2
-primary sublattice from which

E_{8}
can be recovered by saturation or gluing. This distinction is the main
arithmetic point of the work.

The paper is organised as follows. In Sec. 1 we review the classical notion of
integral elements over division algebras following Dickson and Johnson. In
Sec. 2 we review the three algebras
\mathbb{O}
,
p\mathbb{O}
, and

\mathcal{O}
and their interplays. In Sec. 3 we prove the para-octonionic
closure theorem, exhibit the Okubo
\sqrt{3}
-obstruction, and construct the
scaled Okubo order over
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{3}]
. In Sec. 4 we describe the
Okubo-compatible conductor sublattice and recover
E_{8}
by saturation and
gluing. In Sec. 5 we replace continuous orbit claims by arithmetic stabilizers
of the relevant order. Finally, in Sec. 6 we discuss our findings and outline
future directions.

1.
what is an integer

Modern research on integral numbers started in the XIX century with
Gauss, Kronecker and Frege, culminating in the works of Dedekind,
Peano
[
De88
,
Pe89
]
on the axiomatization of natural and integer
numbers. On the other hand, attempts to generalise the notion of integers
in the realm of division algebras such as
\mathbb{C}
complex numbers
and
\mathbb{H}
quaternions were done by Gauss
[
Gau
]
, Hamilton
and Hurwitz
[
Hu19
]
. From that chain of research the definition
that settled in the scientific community was that of Leonard Dickson
in 1923
[
Di23
, p. 141-142]
which we briefly review here. Given
a composition algebra
\mathbb{A}
, i.e. an algebra with a norm
n

such that
n\left(x\cdot y\right)=n\left(x\right)n\left(y\right)
,
endowed with an involution
x\longrightarrow\overline{x}
called

conjugation
, then a set
I
of
integral elements
of

\mathbb{A}
is a set that is closed under addition and multiplication
that contains the element
1
and such that for every element
x\in I

we have

(1.1)

\displaystyle tr\left(x\right)=x+\overline{x}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{Z},

(1.2)

\displaystyle n\left(x\right)=x\overline{x}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{Z}.

Although this definition is broad and widely accepted, it has two
notable shortcomings. First, it can easily yield an infinite number
of integral elements over Hurwitz division algebras, encompassing
the real numbers
\mathbb{R}
real numbers, the
\mathbb{C}
complex
numbers, the
\mathbb{H}
quaternions and the
\mathbb{O}
octonions.
For example, for any natural number
m\in\mathbb{N}
, the subring

\mathbb{Z}\left[\sqrt{-m}\right]
is a set of integral elements
of
\mathbb{C}
. Second, its specificity -requiring the presence
of the unit element- automatically excludes integral systems from
non-unital algebras, such as para-octonions
p\mathbb{O}
and Okubo
algebra
\mathcal{O}
.

The first issue with Dickson’s definition was addressed by Johnson

[
Jo13
,
Jo17
,
Jo18
]
. Inspired by the work of Coxeter
[
Co46
]
,
Johnson proposed that integral systems should inherently relate to
lattices and, by extension, to crystallographic root systems. He then
arrived to the following definition

Definition 1
.

A
basic system of integral
elements
over a real algebra is given by a set of elements such that:

(1)

The trace and the norm are integers. Specifically,

\displaystyle tr\left(x\right)

\displaystyle=x+\overline{x}\in\mathbb{Z},

\displaystyle n\left(x\right)

\displaystyle=x\overline{x}\in\mathbb{Z},

and where the conjugation is given by an involution of the algebra.

(2)

The elements form a subring of the algebra, closed under multiplication,
and comprising a set of invertible unit elements.

(3)

The elements span a two-, four-, or eight-dimensional lattice embedded
in
\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C},\mathbb{H}
or
\mathbb{O}
.

1.1.
Complex integral sets

In order to appreciate the algebraic richness of the octonionic case,
it is instructive to begin with the simpler complex setting. The complex
plane
\mathbb{C}
admits two classical
crystallographic integral
sets
, each corresponding to a different sublattice of the Gaussian
plane.

The
Gaussian integers

\mathbb{Z}[i]=\left\{a+bi:a,b\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}

form a ring under standard complex multiplication, with norm
n(a+bi)=a^{2}+b^{2}\in\mathbb{Z}

and trace
tr(a+bi)=2a\in\mathbb{Z}
. The four invertible unit elements
are
\left\{+1,-1,+i,-i\right\}
, forming the cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}_{4}
.
Their lattice is isometric to
C_{2}
, the square lattice.

The
Eisenstein integers

\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
, where
\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}=\frac{-1+i\sqrt{3}}{2}
,
constitute a second integral system. Here the norm is
n(a+b\omega)=a^{2}-ab+b^{2}\in\mathbb{Z}

and the trace is
tr(a+b\omega)=2a-b\in\mathbb{Z}
. The six unit
elements are
\left\{\pm 1,\pm\omega,\pm\omega^{2}\right\}
, forming
the cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}_{6}
, and their lattice is the triangular
lattice
A_{2}
.

.

It is worth noting that
\sqrt{3}

\omega

is not merely a numerical coincidence: it reflects the underlying
A_{2}

\sqrt{3}
arithmetic will reappear
in the Okubo construction, although over the real quadratic ring

\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{3}]
rather than over the Eisenstein integers.

1.2.
Quaternionic integral sets

Moving to dimension four, the quaternions
\mathbb{H}
admit three
crystallographic integral sets, reflecting a richer algebraic structure.

The
Hamilton integers

\mathbb{H}_{2C_{2}}
consist of all
quaternions
a_{0}+a_{1}\mathrm{i}+a_{2}\mathrm{j}+a_{3}\mathrm{k}

with
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Their eight unit elements

\left\{\pm 1,\pm\mathrm{i},\pm\mathrm{j},\pm\mathrm{k}\right\}

form the quaternion group
Q_{8}
, and the lattice is
C_{2}\oplus C_{2}
.

The
Hurwitz integers

\mathbb{H}_{D_{4}}
are the integral
closure of
\mathbb{H}_{2C_{2}}
in
\mathbb{H}
: they consist of
all quaternions of the form
a_{0}+a_{1}\mathrm{i}+a_{2}\mathrm{j}+a_{3}\mathrm{k}

where either all
a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
or all
a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}+\frac{1}{2}
.
They possess 24 unit elements forming the
binary tetrahedral
group

2T\cong\mathrm{SL}(2,3)
, whose underlying lattice is
D_{4}
.
The 24 unit elements correspond to the vertices of the 24-cell, the
unique regular polytope in dimension four which is its own dual. In
analogy to the octonionic case, the Hurwitz integers
enjoy

the property of being the maximal order in
\mathbb{H}
over
\mathbb{Z}
.

The
Hybrid integers

\mathbb{H}_{2A_{2}}
are generated by
combining two Eisenstein systems within
\mathbb{H}
and possess 12
unit elements. Their lattice is
A_{2}\oplus A_{2}
.

Remark 3
.

The Hurwitz integers
\mathbb{H}_{D_{4}}

are, up to isomorphism, the unique maximal order in
\mathbb{H}
over

\mathbb{Z}
. This maximality is the quaternionic analogue of the
Coxeter-Dickson octonions being the maximal order in
\mathbb{O}
,
a theme that recurs, with important modifications, in the Okubo setting
where the coefficient ring must be extended from
\mathbb{Z}
to
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{3}]
.

1.3.
Octonionic integral sets

The octonionic case is considerably richer than its lower-dimensional
counterparts. In analogy to the quaternionic case, there are multiple
crystallographic integral sets in
\mathbb{O}
, nested by inclusion
and distinguished by the number of unit elements they contain.

The
Cayley-Graves integers

\mathbb{O}_{C_{8}}
are the simplest
octonionic integral set, consisting of octonions with all-integer coefficients
in the standard basis
\left\{1,\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k},\mathrm{l},\mathrm{il},\mathrm{jl},\mathrm{kl}\right\}
.
They possess 16 unit elements forming the Moufang loop
M_{16}(Q_{8})
,
and their lattice is
C_{8}
.

The
Compounded Eisenstein integers

\mathbb{O}_{4A_{2}}
are
obtained by embedding four copies of the Eisenstein integers into
\mathbb{O}

using compatible quaternionic subalgebras. They possess 24 unit elements
forming the loop
M_{24}(4A_{2})
, and their lattice is
A_{2}\oplus A_{2}\oplus A_{2}\oplus A_{2}
.

The
Coupled Hurwitz integers

\mathbb{O}_{2D_{4}}
are obtained
by coupling two copies of the Hurwitz quaternionic integers in a compatible
manner. They possess 48 unit elements forming the loop
M_{48}(2D_{4})
,
and their lattice is
D_{4}\oplus D_{4}
.

Finally, the maximal integral set is the
Coxeter-Dickson octonions

\mathbb{O}_{E_{8}}
, to which we now turn. As we will see, this
is the unique octonionic integral system whose lattice is the exceptional

E_{8}
, and it is the one against which the para-octonionic and Okubo
products will be tested.

For our purposes, the integral set called Coxeter-Dickson octonions,
and denoted here as
\mathbb{O}_{E_{8}}

2.1.
The algebra of octonions

The algebra of octonions
\mathbb{O}
is the only division Hurwitz
algebra with a dimension of eight. We define the composition algebra
of octonion
\left(\mathbb{O},\cdot,n\right)
as the eight dimensional
real vector space with basis
\left\{\text{e}_{0}=1,\text{e}_{1},...,\text{e}_{7}\right\}

with a bilinear product encoded through the
Fano plane
and
explained in Fig.
1.2
. The resulting algebra is
non-associative, non-commutative, but alternative and thus flexible.
Given an element
x\in\mathbb{O}
with decomposition

(2.1)

x=x_{0}+\mathrel{{\mathop{\sum}\limits}}x_{k}\text{e}_{k},

the norm
n
is the obvious Euclidean one defined by

(2.2)

n\left(x\right)=x_{0}^{2}+x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}+x_{4}^{2}+x_{5}^{2}+x_{6}^{2}+x_{7}^{2},

for which the conjugation results

(2.3)

\overline{x}=x_{0}-\mathrel{{\mathop{\sum}\limits}}x_{k}i_{k},

and therefore

(2.4)

n\left(x\right)=\overline{x}\cdot x,

as it happens for every Hurwitz algebra. Then a look at (
2.2
)
shows that
n\left(x\right)=0
if and only if
x=0
and thus the
inverse of a non-zero element of the octonions is easily found as

(2.5)

x^{-1}=\frac{\overline{x}}{n\left(x\right)}.

Also, from (
2.4
) we have that the octonionic
inner product is given by

(2.6)

\displaystyle\left\langle x,y\right\rangle

\displaystyle=x\overline{y}+y\overline{x},

so that
\left\langle x,x\right\rangle=2n\left(x\right)
.

2.2.
The algebra of para-octonions

In unital composition algebras, as noted earlier, there exists a canonical
involution, an order-two antihomomorphism known as
conjugation
.
This can be defined using the orthogonal projection of the unit element
as

(2.7)

x\mapsto\overline{x}=\left\langle x,1\right\rangle 1-x.

This canonical involution has the distinctive property of being an
antihomomorphism with respect to the product, i.e.,
\overline{x\cdot y}=\overline{y}\cdot\overline{x},

and the basic property with the norm of
x\cdot\overline{x}=n\left(x\right)1
.

Given the order-two antihomomorphism of the conjugation over the Hurwitz
algebra of octonions
\left(\mathbb{O},\cdot,n\right)
we can now
obtain a para-Hurwitz algebra defining a new product

(2.8)

x\bullet y=\overline{x}\cdot\overline{y},

for every
x,y\in\mathbb{O}
. The new algebra
\left(\mathbb{O},\bullet,n\right)

is again a composition algebra, in fact a para-Hurwitz algebra, called
para-octonions and denoted with
p\mathbb{O}
. As expected the algebra
does not have a unit but only a para-unit, i.e.
1\in p\mathbb{O}

such that
1\bullet x=\overline{x}.
Finally, it is worth noting
that also the algebra of para-octonions is a division algebra since
if

(2.9)

x\bullet y=\overline{x}\cdot\overline{y}=0,

then either
\overline{x}
or
\overline{y}
are zero and thus implying
that either
x
or
y
are zero.

2.3.
The Okubo algebra

Let us now focus on the order-three automorphism
\tau
over the
octonions
\mathbb{O}
. Given by the following map

(2.10)

\begin{array}[]{cc}\tau\left(\text{e}_{k}\right)&=\text{e}_{k},k=0,1,3,7\\
\tau\left(\text{e}_{2}\right)&=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\text{e}_{2}-\sqrt{3}\text{e}_{5}\right),\\
\tau\left(\text{e}_{5}\right)&=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\text{e}_{5}+\sqrt{3}\text{e}_{2}\right),\\
\tau\left(\text{e}_{4}\right)&=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\text{e}_{4}-\sqrt{3}\text{e}_{6}\right),\\
\tau\left(\text{e}_{6}\right)&=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\text{e}_{6}+\sqrt{3}\text{e}_{4}\right),\end{array}

This definition extends to an order-three automorphism of the octonion
algebra; for arithmetic questions we regard its matrix over

K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3})
. It is interesting to note that in the octonions
there are two real two-planes, generated by
\left\{\text{e}_{2},\text{e}_{5}\right\}

and
\left\{\text{e}_{4},\text{e}_{6}\right\}
, on which the automorphism

\tau
acts as a rotation by
2\pi/3
, i.e. with the same matrix as
multiplication by the primitive cubic root

\omega=-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\mathrm{i}
.

Given the order-three automorphism
\tau
over the Hurwitz algebra
of octonions
\left(\mathbb{O},\cdot,n\right)
we can now obtain
a Petersson algebra defining a new product

(2.11)

x*y=\tau\left(\overline{x}\right)\cdot\tau^{2}\left(\overline{y}\right),

for every
x,y\in\mathbb{O}
. The new algebra
\left(\mathbb{O},*,n\right)

is again a composition algebra, called the Okubo algebra
\mathcal{O}
.
The algebra does not have a unit nor a paraunit but has idempotent
elements. Again, it is worth noting that also the Okubo algebra is
a division algebra since if

(2.12)

x*y=\tau\left(\overline{x}\right)\cdot\tau^{2}\left(\overline{y}\right)=0,

then either
\tau\left(\overline{x}\right)
or
\tau^{2}\left(\overline{y}\right)

are zero and since
\tau
is an homomorphism, either
x
or
y

are zero.

Unlike the case of the para-octonions it is worth presenting here
an independent way of realising the Okubo algebra. In fact, the following
realisation was the one independently found by Okubo ten years after
Petersson. Following
[
Ok78
]
and
[
EM90
]
,
we define the real Okubo Algebra
\mathcal{O}
as the set of three
by three Hermitian traceless matrices over the complex numbers
\mathbb{C}

with the following bilinear product

(2.13)

x*y=\mu\cdot xy+\overline{\mu}\cdot yx-\frac{1}{3}\text{Tr}\left(xy\right)I_{3},

where
\mu=\nicefrac{{1}}{{6}}\left(3+\text{i}\sqrt{3}\right)
and the
juxtaposition is the ordinary associative product between matrices.
It is worth noting that (
2.13
) can be seen as a modification
of the Jordanian product. Indeed, setting
\mu=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
and
neglecting the last term, we retrieve the usual Jordan product over
Hermitian traceless matrices, i.e.

(2.14)

x\circ y=\frac{1}{2}xy+\frac{1}{2}yx.

Nevertheless, Hermitian traceless matrices are not closed under such
product, thus requiring the additional term
-\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}\text{Tr}\left(xy\right)

for the closure of the algebra. Indeed, setting in (
2.13
)

\text{Im}\mu=0
, one retrieves from the traceless part of the exceptional
Jordan algebra
\mathfrak{J}_{3}\left(\mathbb{C}\right)
, whose derivation
Lie algebra is
\mathfrak{su}\left(3\right)
.

Analyzing (
2.13
), it becomes evident that the resulting
algebra is neither unital, associative, nor alternative. Nonetheless,

\mathcal{O}
is a
flexible
algebra, i.e.

(2.15)

x*\left(y*x\right)=\left(x*y\right)*x,

which will turn out to be an even more useful property than alternativity
in the definition of the projective plane. Even though the Okubo algebra
is not unital, it does have idempotents, i.e.
e*e=e
, such as

(2.16)

e=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}2&0&0\\
0&-1&0\\
0&0&-1\end{array}\right),

that together with

(2.17)

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\text{e}_{1}=\sqrt{3}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&1&0\\
1&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{array}\right),&&\text{e}_{2}=\sqrt{3}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&0&1\\
0&0&0\\
1&0&0\end{array}\right),\\
\text{e}_{3}=\sqrt{3}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&0&0\\
0&0&1\\
0&1&0\end{array}\right),&&\text{e}_{4}=\sqrt{3}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&0&0\\
0&-1&0\\
0&0&0\end{array}\right),\\
\text{e}_{5}=\sqrt{3}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&-i&0\\
i&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{array}\right),&&\text{e}_{6}=\sqrt{3}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&0&-i\\
0&0&0\\
i&0&0\end{array}\right),\\
\text{e}_{7}=\sqrt{3}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&0&0\\
0&0&-i\\
0&i&0\end{array}\right),\end{array}

form a basis for
\mathcal{O}
that has real dimension
8
. It is
worth noting that the choice of the idempotent
e
as in (
2.16
)
does not yield to any loss of generality for the subsequent development
of our work since all idempotents form a single orbit under the full
automorphism group
\mathrm{SU}(3)
(see
[
El15
, Thm. 20]
);
the cyclic subgroup
\langle\tau\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}_{3}
alone does
not suffice to connect all idempotents. The choice of this special
basis is useful for the matrix realization, but its orthogonality with respect
to the norm in (
2.18
) is a separate Gram-matrix check and will not
be used as an arithmetic input below. Through a special bijective map between
Okubo algebra and octonions the elements of the basis

\left\{e,\text{e}_{1},...,\text{e}_{7}\right\}
correspond to the octonionic
ones previously defined.

In this realisation a direct way of defining the quadratic form
n

over Okubo algebra, is through the following

(2.18)

n\left(x\right)=\frac{1}{6}\text{Tr}\left(x^{2}\right),

for every
x\in\mathcal{O}
. It is straightforward to see that the

norm
has signature
(8,0)
and is a composition norm over
the Okubo algebra; the associated symmetric bilinear form
\langle x,y\rangle

is associative in the sense
\langle x\cdot z,y\rangle=\langle x,z\cdot y\rangle
.

2.4.
Okubo algebra, octonions and para-octonions

An important feature of the Okubo algebra
\mathcal{O}
is its interplay
with the algebra of octonions
\mathbb{O}
. Indeed, octonions and
the Okubo algebra are linked one another in such a way that we can
easily pass from one to the other simply changing the definition of
the bilinear product over the vector space of the algebra. Let us
consider the Kaplansky’s trick we introduced earlier and let us define
a new product over the Okubo algebra
\mathcal{O}
as

(2.19)

x\cdot y=\left(e*x\right)*\left(y*e\right),

where
x,y\in\mathcal{O}
and
e
is an idempotent of
\mathcal{O}
.
Given that
e*e=e
and
n\left(e\right)=1
, the element
e
acts
as a left and right identity, i.e.

(2.20)

\displaystyle x\cdot e

\displaystyle=e*x*e=n\left(e\right)x=x,

(2.21)

\displaystyle e\cdot x

\displaystyle=e*x*e=n\left(e\right)x=x.

Moreover, since Okubo algebra is a composition algebra, the same norm

n
enjoys the following relation

(2.22)

n\left(x\cdot y\right)=n\left(\left(e*x\right)*\left(y*e\right)\right)=n\left(x\right)n\left(y\right),

which means that
\left(\mathcal{O},\cdot,n\right)
is a unital composition
algebra of real dimension
8
. Since it is also a division algebra,
then it must be isomorphic to that of octonions
\mathbb{O}
as noted
by Okubo himself
[
Ok78
,
Ok78c
]
. On the other hand,
al already noticed, if we consider the order three automorphism of
the octonions in (
2.10
), the Okubo algebra is
then realised as a Petersson algebra from the octonions setting

(2.23)

\displaystyle x*y

\displaystyle=\tau\left(\overline{x}\right)\cdot\tau^{2}\left(\overline{y}\right).

Note that (
2.10
) is formulated assuming the knowledge
of the octonionic product. Reading the same maps as Okubic maps we
then have the notable relation, i.e.

(2.24)

\displaystyle\overline{x}

\displaystyle=\left(\left(x*e\right)*e\right)*e,

(2.25)

\displaystyle\tau\left(x\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\left(\left(x*e\right)*e\right)*e\right)*e,

so that, in fact, the two maps are linked one another, i.e.

(2.26)

\displaystyle\tau\left(x\right)

\displaystyle=\overline{x}*e

(2.27)

\displaystyle\overline{x}

\displaystyle=\tau\left(e*x\right).

While these maps are intertwined, it is important to highlight their
distinct impacts on the algebra’s structure. While
\tau
is an automorphism
for both Okubo algebra
\mathcal{O}
and octonions
\mathbb{O}
,

\overline{x}
do not respect the algebraic structure of the Okubo
algebra
\mathcal{O}
, since it is not an automorphism nor an anti-automorphism
with respect to the Okubo product, while it is an anti-homomorphism
over octonions
\mathbb{O}
.

The scenario with para-octonions,
p\mathbb{O}
is more straightforward.
By definition, para-octonions are obtainable from octonions
\mathbb{O}

through

(2.28)

x\bullet y=\overline{x}\cdot\overline{y},

while, on the other hand, octonions
\mathbb{O}
are obtainable from
para-octonions
p\mathbb{O}
through the aid of the para-unit
1\in p\mathbb{O}
,
such that

(2.29)

\displaystyle x\cdot y

\displaystyle=\left(1\bullet x\right)\bullet\left(y\bullet 1\right)

(2.30)

\displaystyle=\overline{x}\bullet\overline{y}=x\cdot y.

The new algebra
\left(p\mathbb{O},\cdot,n\right)
is again an eight-dimensional
composition algebra which is also unital and division and thus, for
Hurwitz theorem, isomorphic to that of octonions
\mathbb{O}
. Moreover,
since
\tau\left(\overline{x}\right)=\overline{\tau\left(x\right)}
,
we also have that the Okubic algebra is obtainable from the para-Hurwitz
algebra with the introduction of a Petersson-like product, i.e.

(2.31)

x*y=\tau\left(x\right)\bullet\tau^{2}\left(y\right).

3.
the
\text{E}_{8}
-lattice and integral sets
of composition algebras

If we want to define integral elements for para-octonions or for the Okubo
algebra, we must modify the definition provided by Johnson in
(
1
). Upon closer inspection, Johnson’s definition
cannot be transported unchanged to non-unital algebras for two reasons. First,
the role played by the unit in a Hurwitz order has to be replaced either by the
paraunit, in the para-Hurwitz case, or by a chosen idempotent, in the Okubo
case. Second, in the Okubo case the trace used below is not the classical
Hurwitz trace
x+\overline{x}
, but a trace functional relative to an idempotent.

To adapt the trace condition we choose a nonzero idempotent element. In the
para-octonion algebra the paraunit
\mathbf{1}
is a distinguished idempotent,
although it is not the only nonzero one. A direct calculation shows that every
element of the form

(3.1)

x=-\frac{1}{2}+v,\qquad v\in\mathrm{Im}(\mathbb{O}),\quad\|v\|^{2}=\frac{3}{4},

also satisfies
x\bullet x=x
, giving a continuous sphere of idempotents. In
the present construction we choose the paraunit
\mathbf{1}
, which belongs to
the Coxeter-Dickson order and is compatible with the classical trace functional.
For the Okubo algebra, as noted already, the nonzero idempotents form a single
orbit under the full automorphism group
\mathrm{SU}(3)
, so we choose the
idempotent
e
of the form (
2.16
) as a reference point.

We are now ready to adjust Johnson’s definition in a form that separates the
ordinary lattice case from the arithmetic-order case.

Definition 5
.

Let
A
be a real composition algebra with
product
\circ
, norm
n
, and a chosen nonzero idempotent
e
. Let

R
be either
\mathbb{Z}
or
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{3}]
. An
arithmetic
system of integral elements relative to
e
is an
R
-module
\Lambda_{R}

such that:

(1)

\Lambda_{R}
is closed under the product
\circ
;

(2)

the norm and the relative trace

\operatorname{tr}_{e}(x)=\langle x,e\rangle

and the composition norm
n(x)
lie in
R
for every
x\in\Lambda_{R}
;

(3)

when
R=\mathbb{Z}
, the underlying additive group spans a discrete
Euclidean lattice. When
R=\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{3}]
, the object is an

R
-order; a rank-eight Euclidean lattice must be extracted separately as a

\mathbb{Z}
-submodule, called here a metric shadow.

Theorem 6

(Para-octonionic integral structure)
.

Let
\mathscr{O}=\mathbb{O}_{E_{8}}
be the Coxeter-Dickson octonion order.
Then
\mathscr{O}
is closed under the para-octonionic product

x\bullet y=\overline{x}\cdot\overline{y}
. Consequently it is a

\mathbb{Z}
-integral system for
p\mathbb{O}
relative to the paraunit

\mathbf{1}
, and its underlying Euclidean lattice remains
E_{8}
.

Proof.

Let
x,y\in\mathscr{O}
. The Coxeter-Dickson order is stable under octonionic
conjugation, hence
\overline{x},\overline{y}\in\mathscr{O}
. Since it is also
closed under the octonionic product, we have

(3.2)

x\bullet y=\overline{x}\cdot\overline{y}\in\mathscr{O}.

The norm is the same Euclidean norm as in the octonionic case, and therefore

(3.3)

n(x\bullet y)=n(\overline{x}\cdot\overline{y})=n(x)n(y).

Finally, the relative trace with respect to the paraunit agrees with the usual
trace functional on the Coxeter-Dickson order, and so it is integer-valued.
∎

Theorem 7

(Okubo
\sqrt{3}
-obstruction)
.

Let
*
be the Petersson product

(3.4)

x*y=\tau(\overline{x})\cdot\tau^{2}(\overline{y})

associated with the automorphism (
2.10
). Then the
Coxeter-Dickson order
\mathbb{O}_{E_{8}}
is not closed under
*
as a

\mathbb{Z}
-module.

Proof.

It is enough to spot one product of integral basis elements which does not have
integral coordinates in the Coxeter-Dickson basis

(3.5)

b_{0}=1,\quad b_{1}=\mathrm{i},\quad b_{2}=\mathrm{j},\quad b_{3}=\mathrm{k},\quad b_{4}=\mathrm{h},\quad b_{5}=\mathrm{i}\mathrm{h},\quad b_{6}=\mathrm{j}\mathrm{h},\quad b_{7}=\mathrm{k}\mathrm{h}.

The exact calculation gives

\displaystyle b_{0}*b_{2}={}

\displaystyle-\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{3}\,b_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{3}\,b_{1}+\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{3}\right)b_{2}

(3.6)

\displaystyle-\sqrt{3}\,b_{5}-\sqrt{3}\,b_{6}-\sqrt{3}\,b_{7}.

In particular the coefficient of
b_{0}
is
-\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{3}
, and is not
an integer. Hence
\mathbb{O}_{E_{8}}*\mathbb{O}_{E_{8}}
is not contained in

\mathbb{O}_{E_{8}}
as a
\mathbb{Z}
-module.
∎

Remark 8
.

The preceding theorem is the reason for separating the Okubo order from the
ordinary Euclidean lattice
E_{8}
. The correct coefficient field for the
Petersson product is
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3})
, and the natural arithmetic ring
is
R=\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{3}]
, which is the ring of integers of
K
. Nevertheless
an
R
-module of rank
8
is not the same object as a rank-eight Euclidean
lattice over
\mathbb{Z}
.

In analogy to the octonionic case, the next question is whether a different
basis can restore integrality. Quite surprisingly, the obstruction is exactly
absorbed by a diagonal
2
-adic scaling.

Theorem 9

(The scaled Okubo
R
-order)
.

Let
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3})
and
R=\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{3}]
. Define

(3.7)

D=\operatorname{diag}(2,2,2,2,4,4,4,4)

and set

(3.8)

u_{i}=D_{i}b_{i}\qquad(0\leq i\leq 7).

Then

(3.9)

\mathcal{O}_{0}=Ru_{0}+\cdots+Ru_{7}

is closed under the Okubo product. Moreover the relative trace, the composition
norm, and the bilinear form are
R
-valued on the basis
u_{0},\ldots,u_{7}
.

Proof.

Write

(3.10)

b_{i}*b_{j}=\sum_{k=0}^{7}c_{ij}^{k}b_{k},\qquad c_{ij}^{k}\in K.

The exact structure constants in the Coxeter-Dickson basis have denominators
only in
\{1,2,4\}
. With the scaling (
3.7
) one has

(3.11)

u_{i}*u_{j}=\sum_{k=0}^{7}\frac{D_{i}D_{j}}{D_{k}}c_{ij}^{k}u_{k}.

The coefficients

(3.12)

m_{ij}^{k}=\frac{D_{i}D_{j}}{D_{k}}c_{ij}^{k}

were computed exactly and all belong to
R
. Therefore

\mathcal{O}_{0}
is closed by
R
-bilinearity. The same exact verification gives

\operatorname{tr}_{e}(u_{i})\in R
,
n(u_{i})\in R
,

\langle u_{i},u_{j}\rangle\in R
, and

\operatorname{tr}_{e}(u_{i}*u_{j})\in R
for all
i,j
.
∎

Remark 10
.

The scaling (
3.7
) is not an arbitrary normalization. Among
all diagonal rescalings
u_{i}=2^{a_{i}}b_{i}
with
a_{i}\in\{0,1,2\}
, the vector

(3.13)

(a_{0},\ldots,a_{7})=(1,1,1,1,2,2,2,2)

is the unique componentwise minimal solution for
R
-integrality of the Okubo
structure constants.

4.
The Okubo conductor sublattice of
E_{8}

Theorem
9
gives an arithmetic order over

R=\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{3}]
. In order to compare it with the classical

E_{8}
lattice, however, we must pass from the
R
-module to a genuine

\mathbb{Z}
-lattice. The most direct choice is the
\mathbb{Z}
-span of the
same scaled basis. This gives rise not to
E_{8}
itself, but to a conductor
sublattice inside it.

Definition 11
.

The
direct Okubo metric shadow
is the
rank-eight
\mathbb{Z}
-module

(4.1)

L_{\mathrm{Ok}}=\mathbb{Z}u_{0}+\cdots+\mathbb{Z}u_{7}.

Equivalently, in the Coxeter-Dickson coordinates,

(4.2)

L_{\mathrm{Ok}}=D\Lambda,

where
\Lambda=\mathbb{O}_{E_{8}}
and
D
is the diagonal matrix
(
3.7
).

Theorem 12

(The Okubo conductor sublattice)
.

The lattice
L_{\mathrm{Ok}}
is an even positive definite sublattice of

\Lambda\cong E_{8}
with

(4.3)

[\Lambda:L_{\mathrm{Ok}}]=2^{12}

and

(4.4)

\det(L_{\mathrm{Ok}})=2^{24}.

Moreover

(4.5)

\min(L_{\mathrm{Ok}})=8,

so
L_{\mathrm{Ok}}
has no roots of norm
2
.

Proof.

Since
u_{i}=D_{i}b_{i}
, the change-of-basis matrix from
\Lambda
to

L_{\mathrm{Ok}}
is
D
. Hence

(4.6)

[\Lambda:L_{\mathrm{Ok}}]=|\det D|=2^{4}4^{4}=2^{12}.

The lattice
\Lambda
is unimodular, therefore

(4.7)

\det(L_{\mathrm{Ok}})=[\Lambda:L_{\mathrm{Ok}}]^{2}\det(\Lambda)=2^{24}.

Furthermore

(4.8)

4\Lambda\subset L_{\mathrm{Ok}}\subset 2\Lambda\subset\Lambda.

Thus every nonzero
x\in L_{\mathrm{Ok}}
can be written as
x=2y
with

y\in\Lambda
, and since the minimum of
E_{8}
is
2
, one has

(4.9)

\langle x,x\rangle=4\langle y,y\rangle\geq 8.

The vector
2b_{0}\in L_{\mathrm{Ok}}
has norm
8
, and so
(
4.5
) follows.
∎

Remark 13
.

It is of paramount importance to notice the following point. The direct Okubo
shadow is compatible with the Okubo
R
-order, but it is not
E_{8}
. Indeed it
has determinant
2^{24}
and no roots, whereas
E_{8}
is unimodular and has

240
roots of norm
2
.

The previous theorem explains in which sense the title of this work has to be
read. The Okubo product does not give the same integral
E_{8}
order. Rather,
it gives a
2
-primary conductor sublattice from which
E_{8}
can be retrieved
by saturation or by gluing.

Theorem 14

(Recovering
E_{8}
by saturation and gluing)
.

The
2
-adic saturation of
L_{\mathrm{Ok}}
inside

\Lambda\otimes\mathbb{Q}
is

(4.10)

\operatorname{Sat}_{2}(L_{\mathrm{Ok}})=\Lambda\cong E_{8}.

Equivalently,
\Lambda
is the even unimodular overlattice obtained from

L_{\mathrm{Ok}}
by gluing along the maximal isotropic subgroup

(4.11)

H=\Lambda/L_{\mathrm{Ok}}\subset A_{L_{\mathrm{Ok}}}=L_{\mathrm{Ok}}^{*}/L_{\mathrm{Ok}}.

This recovered lattice is not closed under the Okubo product.

Proof.

The Smith invariants of the inclusion
L_{\mathrm{Ok}}\subset\Lambda
are

(4.12)

(2,2,2,2,4,4,4,4).

Removing the
2
-primary part gives

(4.13)

(1,1,1,1,1,1,1,1),

and hence the
2
-saturation is exactly
\Lambda
.

For the gluing description, the discriminant group of
L_{\mathrm{Ok}}
has
order

(4.14)

|A_{L_{\mathrm{Ok}}}|=\det(L_{\mathrm{Ok}})=2^{24}.

The quotient

(4.15)

H=\Lambda/L_{\mathrm{Ok}}\simeq(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{4}\oplus(\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z})^{4}

has order
2^{12}
. The exact check of the discriminant quadratic form gives

q(h)=0
for all
h\in H
, so
H
is isotropic. Since

|H|^{2}=|A_{L_{\mathrm{Ok}}}|
, it is maximal isotropic, and the corresponding
overlattice is even and unimodular. It is generated by
L_{\mathrm{Ok}}
and the
classes of
b_{0},\ldots,b_{7}
, hence it is
\Lambda
.

Finally, the last statement follows from the same obstruction as in
(
3
): the saturated lattice contains
b_{0}
and

b_{2}
, but
b_{0}*b_{2}
has coefficient
-\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{3}
on
b_{0}
.
Thus the saturation recovers
E_{8}
metrically, not as an Okubo integral order.
∎

Remark 15
.

The natural restriction of scalars of
\mathcal{O}_{0}
has rank
16
over

\mathbb{Z}
, with basis
u_{i},\sqrt{3}u_{i}
. The trace form

\operatorname{Tr}_{K/\mathbb{Q}}\langle x,y\rangle
is positive definite and
even in the exact computation, but its minimum is
16
and it does not give an
immediate
E_{8}\oplus E_{8}
decomposition. This indicates that the rank-eight
shadow and the rank-sixteen trace lattice encode different arithmetic aspects
of the Okubo order.

5.
Arithmetic stabilizers and finite orbits

In analogy with the octonionic case, it is natural to study the orbit structure
of integral elements. However, one must distinguish continuous automorphism
groups from the finite arithmetic groups that preserve a chosen order. The
compact Lie group
\mathrm{G}_{2}
acts on the real octonion algebra, but it does
not preserve the Coxeter-Dickson order in general. Likewise, the compact group

\mathrm{SU}(3)
acts on the real Okubo algebra, but it does not automatically
preserve the scaled order
\mathcal{O}_{0}
or the conductor lattice

L_{\mathrm{Ok}}
.

5.1.
The octonionic and para-octonionic stabilizer

For the octonionic and para-octonionic orders the relevant finite group is not
the whole of
\mathrm{G}_{2}
, but the arithmetic stabilizer

(5.1)

\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{O},\mathbb{O}_{E_{8}})=\{g\in\mathrm{G}_{2}:g(\mathbb{O}_{E_{8}})=\mathbb{O}_{E_{8}}\}.

This is the group that can act on the 240 norm-one elements of the
Coxeter-Dickson order. The same stabilizer is relevant for the para-octonionic
product, because the para product is defined from octonionic conjugation and
multiplication, both of which preserve the Coxeter-Dickson order.

5.2.
The Okubo arithmetic stabilizer

For the Okubo algebra, the corresponding object is

(5.2)

\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{O},\mathcal{O}_{0})=\{g\in\mathrm{SU}(3):g(\mathcal{O}_{0})=\mathcal{O}_{0}\},

where
\mathcal{O}_{0}
is the scaled
R
-order of
Theorem
9
. If the direct shadow is selected, one may
further restrict to

(5.3)

\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{O},L_{\mathrm{Ok}})=\{g\in\mathrm{SU}(3):g(L_{\mathrm{Ok}})=L_{\mathrm{Ok}}\}.

Only these arithmetic stabilizers act on finite sets of integral elements.
Consequently, any statement about orbits of roots or integral elements must be
formulated with respect to such a discrete stabilizer, not with respect to the
full compact continuous group
\mathrm{SU}(3)
.

Remark 16
.

The automorphism
\tau
in (
2.10
) gives a natural candidate
for a generator of a discrete subgroup, but it fails the arithmetic test for the
scaled order. In coordinates relative to
u_{0},\ldots,u_{7}
, the image of

u_{2}
has a coefficient
-\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{3}
on
u_{0}
for
\tau
, and

\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{3}
for
\tau^{2}
. Thus
\tau
and
\tau^{2}
are Okubo
automorphisms over
K
, but not stabilizers of
\mathcal{O}_{0}
.

Remark 17
.

A first exact search among signed permutations preserving the two conductor
blocks
\{0,1,2,3\}
and
\{4,5,6,7\}
tests
147456
candidates. Only four of
them preserve the metric, and only the identity preserves also the Okubo product
in this search class. This does not determine the full arithmetic automorphism
group, but it confirms that nontrivial finite orbits should not be inferred from

\mathrm{SU}(3)
alone.

6.
Conclusions and Future Developments

In this work we separated three layers that must not be conflated: the classical
Euclidean lattice
E_{8}
, the Coxeter-Dickson octonionic order over

\mathbb{Z}
, and the Okubo arithmetic order naturally forced into

\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{3}]
. The para-octonionic result is unconditional and mirrors
the octonionic case: the Coxeter-Dickson order is stable under

x\bullet y=\overline{x}\cdot\overline{y}
, and therefore gives a

\mathbb{Z}
-integral system for
p\mathbb{O}
with underlying lattice
E_{8}
.

For the Okubo algebra the situation is more mesmerizing and surprising. The
Petersson product defined by the order-three automorphism
\tau
introduces
coefficients in
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3})
, and the Coxeter-Dickson order is not
closed as a
\mathbb{Z}
-module. Nevertheless, after the diagonal scaling

D=\operatorname{diag}(2,2,2,2,4,4,4,4)
, one obtains a closed

\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{3}]
-order
\mathcal{O}_{0}
. Its direct metric shadow is not

E_{8}
, but the conductor sublattice
L_{\mathrm{Ok}}
of index
2^{12}
and
determinant
2^{24}
inside
E_{8}
.

The lattice
E_{8}
is then recovered in a precise but weaker sense: it is the

2
-adic saturation of
L_{\mathrm{Ok}}
, equivalently the even unimodular
overlattice obtained by gluing along
H=E_{8}/L_{\mathrm{Ok}}
. This recovery is
metric-arithmetic rather than multiplicative, because the saturated lattice is
again not closed under the Okubo product. This is the exact sense in which the
Okubo construction is related to the
E_{8}
lattice.

It would be definitely interesting to analyze whether larger multi-generator
overorders of
\mathcal{O}_{0}
give rise to finer conductor lattices, and
whether the full arithmetic stabilizer of the Okubo order contains nontrivial
elements outside the signed block-permutation class tested here. In a
forthcoming work, it would also be natural to relate these conductor lattices to
the polytope structure of the Gosset polytope and to the arithmetic of the
Okubo idempotent orbit.
