Modelling chronic stress as an excitatory-inhibitory perturbation in recurrent working-memory networks
Mauricio A. Diaz, Manuela A. Beyer, Janina Hesse

Abstract
Stress is an adaptive response coordinated by neural and physiological systems. While acute stress can enhance survival, chronic stress drives structural brain changes, cognitive dysfunction, and increased psychiatric risk. At the cellular level, chronic stress shifts the excitatory-inhibitory (E/I) balance of prefrontal pyramidal neurons toward inhibitory dominance, yet the mechanisms underlying these alterations are still unknown. We here investigate possible mechanisms causing inhibitory dominance using recurrent neuronal networks trained on a working memory task. Chronic stress is modelled as a modulation in synaptic strength or neuronal activity, systematically comparing eight candidate operators against three experimentally motivated signatures of stress-induced prefrontal dysfunction: inhibitory dominance, excitatory hypofunction, and impaired task performance. These signatures are all recovered by a single stress mechanism, stronger inhibitory-to-excitatory synapses. Contrasting naive networks with resilient networks trained under the stress mechanism, we find that resilience training not only preserves task performance under stress, but also confines the network to the same dynamical subspace and energetic regime with and without stress. This resilience comes at a cost: resilient networks generalise less well when the task requires longer memory than seen during training, indicating that resilient networks find a specialised solution tuned to the trained regime. This trade-off between resilience and generalization performance persists across stress magnitude and network size, offering a computational analogue of the shift toward rigid, habit-like behaviour reported in animal following chronic stress.

Introduction

Chronic stress affects individuals across multiple scales of neural organisation, impairing circuit dynamics, structural integrity, cognitive and affective function, as well as behaviour
[
15
,
21
]
. The mechanisms underlying these detrimental effects, and the causal relations between them, are not yet completely understood. Computational models of neural circuits that incorporate biologically grounded constraints offer a principled framework to systematically isolate and compare candidate stress mechanisms.

The literature shows that chronic stress impairs cognitive and affective functions that depend on the prefrontal cortex (PFC) and hippocampus. Prolonged stress exposure weakens working memory, cognitive flexibility, declarative memory, and spatial navigation, while promoting rigid, habit-driven behavioural strategies
[
2
,
26
,
10
,
11
,
14
]
. At the cellular and circuit levels, these functional changes are accompanied by structural remodelling of prefrontal pyramidal neurons, including dendritic retraction, debranching, and spine loss
[
33
]
, as well as by dysregulated corticolimbic and hypothalamic circuits involved in threat processing, emotional learning, and stress-hormone control
[
15
,
9
,
5
]
.

A central candidate mechanism for this stress-induced reorganisation is disruption of excitatory/inhibitory (E/I) balance within prefrontal circuits. E/I balance refers to the functional equilibrium between excitatory glutamatergic and inhibitory GABAergic signalling, which is essential for stable yet flexible information processing
[
47
]
. In the PFC, this balance supports working memory, decision-making, planning, error monitoring, and emotional regulation, and its disruption is implicated in several neuropsychiatric conditions, including major depressive disorder and anxiety disorders
[
27
,
18
,
38
]
. In the context of chronic stress, converging evidence indicates that prolonged exposure shifts medial PFC circuitry toward reduced excitatory output or inhibitory dominance, thereby weakening prefrontal control over cognition and behaviour.

Experimental studies support the E/I balance as possible stress mechanism but also reveal that stress-induced E/I disruption does not follow a single uniform mechanism. In adult male rats, chronic stress increased inhibition in the infralimbic mPFC by enhancing presynaptic GABA release and increasing GABAergic contacts onto pyramidal neurons, an effect associated with selective loss of glucocorticoid receptors in parvalbumin (PV) interneurons
[
35
]
. In adult mice, chronic unpredictable stress increased the inhibitory/excitatory ratio in layer 5/6 pyramidal neurons across the mPFC through subregion-specific synaptic changes: inhibitory drive increased in the prelimbic cortex, whereas excitatory drive decreased in the infralimbic cortex
[
43
]
. Whether prefrontal hypofunction reflects elevated inhibitory drive, weakened excitation, disinhibition of specific output pathways, or some combination of these mechanisms, and how such shifts propagate through local PV-mediated inhibition and long-range PFC projections, remain open questions
[
35
,
58
,
30
,
15
,
36
,
30
]
. Stress-induced prefrontal dysfunction likely reflects a family of circuit perturbations rather than a single causal pathway, motivating modelling frameworks that can systematically disentangle these mechanisms.

We here use recurrent neuronal networks (RNNs) trained on a working memory task to elucidate possible mechanisms underlying these observations. Computational models offer fully observable and controllable systems in which candidate mechanisms can be isolated, combined, and selectively ablated. Previous work using spiking circuit models, mean-field analyses, and recurrent networks has shown that E/I balance shapes evidence accumulation, attractor stability, and competition between neural representations, and that inhibition contributes actively to persistent activity and competitive dynamics rather than acting purely as a suppressive force
[
28
,
42
]
. Task-trained recurrent networks have also become a useful framework for studying working-memory computation, including the maintenance and manipulation of information through persistent activity, short-term synaptic mechanisms, dynamic coding, structured population manifolds, and biologically grounded constraints such as Dale’s principle
[
48
,
20
,
32
,
8
,
40
]
. Recent work has begun connecting stress and neuromodulation to recurrent computation
[
12
,
52
]
, and related studies of noise injection and weight perturbation have examined regularisation and robustness in RNNs
[
29
,
55
,
45
]
. However, existing work has not systematically tested which biologically motivated E/I perturbations best reproduce experimental signatures of stress-induced prefrontal dysfunction.

Here, we constrain all-to-all connected RNNs by Dale’s law, such that all synaptic connections arising from one neuron are either excitatory or inhibitory. Previous studies have
shown that this constraint shapes network dynamics: analyses of the spectral properties
of the recurrent weight matrix and of network function indicate that
excitatory-inhibitory networks suppress oscillatory dynamics and respond faster to
stimuli than their unconstrained counterparts
[
22
,
4
]
. We train the networks on a working-memory task, in which the network has to compare two stimuli shown with a variable delay between both,
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
, and report the higher stimulus
[
48
,
56
,
57
]
, see Fig.
1
. Working-memory maintenance offers a natural test bed, because it depends on recurrent dynamics shaped by the balance between excitation and inhibition
[
54
,
7
]
.

We model chronic stress in these networks as a circuit-level perturbation, with the aim to reproduce the shift in the E/I ratio toward inhibitory dominance, experimentally observed in prefrontal pyramidal neurons during stress-induced prefrontal dysfunction, as introduced above. We formalise stress as an operator
S
that selectively enhances synaptic connectivity or neuronal activity, and systematically compare different candidate mechanisms.

Networks trained solely on the working memory task, called
naive networks
in the following, are highly sensitive to the stress operators. To enhance robustness, we introduced a
resilience training
motivated by the observation that animals are most resilient against stress when subjected to a moderate amount of stress during development (stress inoculation,
[
24
]
): we trained networks on the memory task while simultaneously employing the stress operator. These networks maintained task performance under stress, and we call them, following the stress-resilience literature
[
37
]
,
resilient networks
. Note that, from a network-theoretical point of view, our resilient networks might be seen, due to their lack of plasticity after training, rather as robust than resilient to the stress operator.

We find that selectively enhancing inhibitory synaptic strength onto excitatory neurons is the best fitting stress candidate mechanism, and that resilience comes at a cost: Resilient networks generalise less well to delays longer than those used during training (out-of-distribution delays), while naive networks retain stronger delay generalisation even under stress. This suggest that resilience training promotes a more specialised solution tuned to the regime experienced during training.

Stress operators and target signatures.

As outlined in the introduction, chronic stress disrupts E/I balance in prefrontal circuits, with converging evidence from rodent studies indicating a shift toward inhibitory dominance and weakened excitatory output of pyramidal neurons
[
35
,
43
,
38
]
. Inhibitory dominance can result from different mechanisms, increased inhibitory synaptic strength or neuronal activity, or decreased excitatory synaptic strength or neuronal activity. We implement these alternatives as stress operators
S
into our networks.

We consider two mechanistically distinct ways of modelling chronic stress and how it reshapes excitatory-inhibitory dynamics, giving rise to eight stress operators. We perturb the
network with a stress operator
S
either by rescaling recurrent synaptic weights, or by rescaling the gain on neural activity. In both cases a single magnitude
\delta
sets the size of the perturbation. On average the operator multiplies its target element by
1+\delta
, namely
\mathbb{E}[S[X]]=(1+\delta)\,X
,
so
\delta
reads directly as a fractional change (
\delta=0.1
is a
10\%
increase,

\delta=-0.1
a
10\%
decrease), while a fixed relative dispersion

\sigma_{\mathrm{stress}}
adds heterogeneity across synapses or neurons.
On the synaptic side we target two pathways: inhibition onto excitatory cells
(
S^{\uparrow}\!\left[W_{I\to E}\right]
, rescaling
W_{EI}
) and recurrent excitation (
S^{\downarrow}\!\left[W_{E\to E}\right]
, rescaling
W_{EE}
),
benchmarked against non-selective controls that rescale all recurrent synapses up or down
(
S^{\uparrow}\!\left[W\right]
,
S^{\downarrow}\!\left[W\right]
). On the activity side we apply
a gain on
each population’s output: inhibitory (
S^{\uparrow}\!\left[r_{I}\right]
) or excitatory (
S^{\downarrow}\!\left[r_{E}\right]
) gain, against
non-selective controls applied to the whole network (
S^{\uparrow}\!\left[r\right]
,
S^{\downarrow}\!\left[r\right]
). The selective
operators isolate the contribution of a specific pathway or cell class, whereas the
non-selective controls quantify what follows simply from a global change in drive.

We evaluate the network under each candidate stress operator
S
against three minimal, experimentally motivated signatures focused on the excitatory population of the circuit (Table
1

h_{E\to E}/|h_{I\to E}|
; (ii) excitatory hypofunction, quantified by

and
h_{E\to E}
; and (iii) impaired task performance. A successful stress mechanism modelled as a perturbation to the network should reproduce all three signatures when applied, while the unstressed network remains within baseline ranges.

2.2
Delay-generalisation trade-off analysis

We speculated that the required specialization of the network under resilience training should become weaker, and hence generalization stronger, when the task was made easier either by reducing the applied stress level, or by a larger network size. Yet, we did not find evidence for such relations. To understand how resilience training mediates the trade-off between stimulus-delay generalization and stress performance introduced above, we introduced two manipulations into the training regime. First, we varied the maximum stress magnitude applied during stress training,
\delta_{\max}

3
Discussion

Using Dale-constrained recurrent networks trained on a delayed parametric working-memory task, we modelled chronic stress as an external, circuit-level perturbation. We first asked which perturbation best reproduces the experimentally reported signatures of stress-induced prefrontal dysfunction. Across eight candidate stress operators, selectively increasing inhibitory-to-excitatory synapses,
S^{\uparrow}[W_{I\to E}]
, stood out as the only individual
mechanism that jointly produced inhibitory dominance, excitatory hypofunction, and impaired performance, fitting experimental reports.
Treating stress as an external operator reflects that it is fundamentally a bodily response which cooperates with neural circuits rather than a property of those circuits alone
[
15
,
33
]
. This is a simplification that separates the circuit-level consequences of a stress-induced perturbation from the processes that generate it. Our finding that one stress mechanism reproduced both inhibitory dominance and excitatory hypofunction shows that different observations on inhibitory dominance in prefrontal pyramidal cells – elevated PV interneuron activity
[
38
]
, more
GABAergic contacts onto pyramidal cells
[
35
]
, and subregion-specific
increases in inhibition and decreases in excitation
[
43
]
– might well form a causal cascade with excitatory hypofunction emerging as a
downstream consequence rather than an imposed alteration, instead of acting in parallel.

We then studied how networks adapt to the stress operator via resilience training. Comparing networks trained with and without applying the stress operator, naive and resilient, revealed that
resilience training preserves task performance under stress but introduces a
delay-generalisation trade-off. Furthermore, resilient networks show decreased network density and reciprocity, and absorb the stress operator while
preserving their geometric dynamics and energetic structure observed without stress. Reduced density indicates that resilience training is associated with a more selective pattern of effective recurrent connectivity. Lower weighted reciprocity further suggests a shift away from symmetric bidirectional interactions toward a more directional recurrent information flow.
These observations are consistent with the broader picture emerging from the present study, in which resilience training preserve performance under stress operators, but exhibit reduced delay generalisation outside the training distribution.

Resilient networks concentrate their activity and energy in a compact region near baseline. This compact organisation is what protects them, but it is also the dynamical side of their specialisation: a network tuned tightly to its trained regime handles the stress operator it was trained on, yet copes poorly with the longer, untrained delays, which is where the generalisation cost appears.
Notably, energetic stability was not part of the training objective; resilient networks were optimised only to perform under the perturbation, yet they preserved their baseline metabolic regime as a byproduct, suggesting that energetic organisation and dynamical robustness are linked in these solutions. As a behavioural hypothesis, the trade-off offers a new reading of stress-induced rigidity: in chronically stressed animals
[
11
,
14
]
, the habit-like solution that preserves performance on familiar tasks may be the very thing that weakens generalisation to novel ones, recasting inflexibility as the cost of resilience rather than a general deficit, even without acute stress.

Together, the findings suggest that resilience training is accompanied by a reorganization of recurrent connectivity toward architectures specialized for maintaining function under chronic stress-like conditions, potentially at the expense of computational flexibility and generalization capacity.

This study illustrates the value of training biologically constrained recurrent networks
to study stress
[
48
,
56
]
. By imposing a minimal mechanistic
constraint, here an E/I network obeying Dale’s principle, we can ask how stress affects
that specific component while retaining complete observation of the system’s activity,
drives, and energetics, a level of access unavailable
in vivo
. Because the network performs a
cognitive function, working memory, the effect of stress operators can be related directly to
behaviourally relevant computation. More broadly, the approach opens experimental
directions for understanding the circuit and cellular mechanisms of stress: it generates
qualitative, testable signatures, such as the asymmetric drive pattern above, and may
reveal mechanisms that can in turn be built into biologically inspired artificial systems
to account for stress in a principled way.

Limitations.

A strength of the RNN approach, and equally its main caveat, is that we control every part of the system: its architecture and temporal activity (the biology), the data it is
trained on (the environment), and the rule by which it learns (the learning mechanism)

[
50
,
13
]
. This control is what makes the learned
mechanisms interpretable, but it also means that apparently minor design choices can
change both performance and the solution the network adopts
[
25
]
. Our
results should therefore be read as properties of a deliberately simplified model of
cognition, one that, unlike a behaving animal, carries no experiential history beyond its
training
[
31
]
. The model also enforces a strict Dale’s principle, so that
each neuron either excites or inhibits; while a widely used first-order approximation,
real neurons engage in co-transmission and neuromodulation, releasing multiple
transmitters and recruiting parallel fast and slow signalling pathways

[
51
]
, so biological circuits carry greater chemical and functional
complexity than captured here. Because our study explicitly models biological alterations observed under chronic stress, our networks are not applicable to study acute stress.

Future directions.

Several extensions follow directly. We treated stress as an external operator; modelling
how such a perturbation arises from internal plasticity mechanisms, as the network’s own response to stressful
stimuli, would close the loop between the stressor, the bodily response, and the circuit
change. We also addressed resilience only in the sense of maintained function under
stress; its complementary sense, rapid recovery of function after a stress-induced
decline, maps onto online adaptation and recovery dynamics that our static-perturbation
setting does not capture. Each operator was applied independently, whereas stress in the
organism is a coordinated set of processes acting across molecular, endocrine, and circuit
scales
[
23
]
; combinations of operators may correspond to distinct physiological processes, and characterising how they
interact is a promising direction. Finally, we characterised the delay-generalisation
trade-off in a single categorical-decision task; whether it generalises across task
structures, in particular to memories supported by continuous attractors, remains open.
Addressing these would extend the present framework further toward biologically inspired models of
stress and stress inoculation.

4
Methods

4.1
Network model

We follow the formulation of
48
, using a rate-based recurrent neural network of excitatory and inhibitory populations. Unless denoted otherwise, network models simulate 200 neurons, 80% of which where treated as excitatory, the remaining 20% as inhibitory. Each neuron is described by an internal state (a membrane-potential analogue)
x_{P}(t)
, where
P\in{E,I}
denotes the population. Its activity is the instantaneous firing rate
r_{P}(t)=\phi(x_{P}(t))
, where
\phi(\cdot)
is the rectified linear unit (ReLU),
\phi(x)=\max(0,x)
. The network evolves in discrete time by a first-order update, with the state of a neuron in population
P
at time
t+1
given by

x_{P}(t+1)=(1-\alpha)\,x_{P}(t)+\alpha\,h_{P}(t)+\alpha\,s_{P}(t)+\sqrt{2\alpha}\,\sigma_{\xi}\,\xi_{P}(t),

(1)

where
h_{P}(t)
is the recurrent synaptic drive,
s_{P}(t)=W_{\mathrm{in},P},u(t)
is the input drive to population
P
,
\alpha=\Delta t/\tau
sets the integration timescale, and
\xi_{P}(t)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
is independent Gaussian noise of amplitude
\sigma_{\xi}=0.02
. Simulations used a time step
\Delta t=20
ms and a membrane time constant
\tau=100
ms.

The recurrent synaptic drive collects excitatory and inhibitory contributions,

\displaystyle h_{E}(t)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{W_{EE}\,r_{E}(t)}_{h_{E\to E}(t)}+\underbrace{W_{EI}\,r_{I}(t)}_{h_{I\to E}(t)},

(2)

\displaystyle h_{I}(t)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{W_{IE}\,r_{E}(t)}_{h_{E\to I}(t)}+\underbrace{W_{II}\,r_{I}(t)}_{h_{I\to I}(t)},

(3)

where
r_{E}(t)
and
r_{I}(t)
are the population activity vectors and
W_{PQ}
is the recurrent connectivity from population
Q
to population
P
. While the input drive projects the task to both populations, the output reads only from the excitatory population,
y(t)=W_{\mathrm{out}},r_{E}(t)
. This mirrors the organisation of cortical circuits: thalamic afferents target both excitatory and inhibitory neurons, whereas long-range output to downstream areas is carried predominantly by excitatory pyramidal cells, with inhibitory interneurons acting mainly within local circuitry
[
17
]
.

Following
48
, recurrent weights were initialised as positive magnitudes and assigned excitatory or inhibitory signs according to Dale’s law
[
49
,
51
]
, i.e. positive sign for
W_{EE}
and
W_{IE}
, and negative sign for
W_{EI}
and
W_{II}
. Using fully connected recurrent networks, excitatory and inhibitory presynaptic weights were sampled from Gamma distributions (
k=2
) with means
\mu_{E}=1
and
\mu_{I}=N_{E}/N_{I}
, respectively. Self-connections were removed, and the signed recurrent matrix was rescaled to a spectral radius of
1.5
. Input and output weights were drawn from rectified Gaussian distributions with scale
1/\sqrt{N}
, with non-negativity maintained during training via a ReLU parameterisation. The initial state
x_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,0.1^{2})
was optimised jointly with the synaptic parameters.

4.2
Delayed parametric comparison task implementation

The delayed parametric comparison task adapts the parametric working-memory paradigm in which prefrontal delay activity encodes remembered stimulus values
[
44
,
3
]
. Each network received three input channels and produced two output channels. The input channels encoded fixation, the first scalar stimulus
S_{1}
, and the second scalar stimulus
S_{2}
, respectively. The two output channels encoded the choices
S_{1}>S_{2}
and

S_{2}>S_{1}
. Trials used a time-step of
\Delta t=20
ms.

Each trial lasted 2000 ms, corresponding to
T=100
simulation steps. Trials
began with a 400 ms fixation epoch. The first stimulus was then presented for
250 ms and the second stimulus for 250 ms. The delay between
S_{1}
and
S_{2}

was sampled uniformly from 400–900 ms. The decision epoch began immediately after the second stimulus and continued until the end of the trial.

Stimulus amplitudes were sampled from the interval
S_{1},S_{2}\in[0.2,1.2]
. Trial difficulty was controlled by the signed difference
\Delta=S_{2}-S_{1}
. For training, difference magnitudes were sampled from
|\Delta|\in\{0,0.032,0.064,0.128,0.256,0.512\}
. For non-zero differences, the sign was chosen randomly, so trials included both

S_{1}>S_{2}
and
S_{2}>S_{1}
comparisons. For a given sampled
\Delta
,
S_{1}
was
drawn uniformly from the range that allowed
S_{2}=S_{1}+\Delta
to remain within
the stimulus bounds. The effective difference was then recorded as the realised
difference
S_{2}-S_{1}
. The fixation channel was active from trial onset until the decision epoch and a tonic baseline input of
0.2
was added to all input channels throughout the trial. Input noise was added independently across time points and input channels, sampled log-uniformly from
\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}\in[0.01,0.05]
and rectified to be non-negative.

Targets are zero during the trial time span except only during the decision period. If
S_{1}>S_{2}
, the target was
[1,0]
; if
S_{2}>S_{1}
, the target was
[0,1]
. For exact ties
(
S_{1}=S_{2}
), the target was
[0.5,0.5]
. The training mask assigned full loss
weight during fixation and decision periods. The intervening stimulus and delay
periods were assigned a reduced mask weight of
w_{\mathrm{stim}}=0.01
, so that training emphasised fixation maintenance and final choice accuracy while
only weakly constraining network output during stimulus presentation and memory
maintenance.

4.3
Training objective

Networks were trained by minimising the mean-squared error (MSE) between the readout

y(t)=W_{\text{out}}\,r_{E}(t)
and the target signal
\hat{y}(t)
, weighted by a
per-timestep mask
\lambda_{m}(t)\geq 0
:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{task}}(\theta)=\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T}m_{t}\left\lVert y_{t}-\hat{y}_{t}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}m_{t}+\epsilon}+\lambda_{r}\frac{1}{TN}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{N}r_{ti}^{2}.

(4)

where
\epsilon=10^{-6}
was used for numerical stability and

\lambda_{r}=10^{-3}
.
\theta=\{W_{\mathrm{rec}},W_{\mathrm{in}},W_{\mathrm{out}}\}
are the trainable
parameters and
\lambda_{r}=10^{-3}
is the weight on the firing-rate regularisation term,
which penalises high neural activity across both populations. The mask
\lambda_{m}(t)

takes the value
1
during the fixation and decision periods and
0.01
during the
stimulus and delay periods. This soft weighting discourages premature decisions during evidence
accumulation without excluding those time-steps entirely from the loss.

4.4
Stress mechanisms

We model chronic stress as a random operator
S
acting on a target
X
, either a
recurrent weight submatrix
W_{P}
or a population activity vector
r_{P}
, by multiplying it
element-wise by random factors,

S[X]=m\odot X,\qquad m_{k}=\exp(\eta+\tau Z_{k}),\quad Z_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\ \text{i.i.d.},

(5)

drawing one factor
m_{k}
independently for each weight or neuron in the target. The
magnitude
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
and relative dispersion
\sigma_{\mathrm{stress}}\geq 0

(fixed at
\sigma_{\mathrm{stress}}=0.2
) enter through

\tau=\sqrt{\log\!\big(1+\sigma_{\mathrm{stress}}^{2}\big)},\qquad\eta=\log(1+\delta)-\tfrac{1}{2}\tau^{2}.

(6)

The
-\tfrac{1}{2}\tau^{2}
term centres the factors so that, on average,
S
rescales its
target by a single number. Since
\mathbb{E}[\exp(\eta+\tau Z)]=\exp(\eta+\tfrac{1}{2}\tau^{2})

for
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
,

\mathbb{E}[m_{k}]=1+\delta,\qquad\mathrm{CV}[m_{k}]=\sqrt{e^{\tau^{2}}-1}=\sigma_{\mathrm{stress}},

(7)

so
\delta
sets the average change and
\sigma_{\mathrm{stress}}
its spread, separately,
and

\mathbb{E}\big[S[X]\big]=(1+\delta)\,X.

(8)

We write
S^{\uparrow}
for upscaling (
\delta>0
) and
S^{\downarrow}
for the matched
operator with
\delta
negated, and state the magnitude where needed (for example

S^{\uparrow}[W_{EI}]
with
\delta=0.25
). The baseline network is recovered at

\delta=0
,
\sigma_{\mathrm{stress}}=0
, where
m\equiv 1
and
S[X]=X
.

Synaptic weight scaling.

The operator acts on a recurrent weight submatrix,
S^{\uparrow,\downarrow}[W_{P}]
,

\widetilde{W}_{P,ij}=S_{ij}\,W_{P,ij},\qquad S_{ij}=\exp(\eta+\tau Z_{ij}),\quad Z_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\ \text{i.i.d.},

(9)

where
S_{ij}
is the factor for synapse
(i,j)
, drawn independently, so that

\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{W}_{P,ij}]=(1+\delta)\,W_{P,ij}
. The population-specific
operators select the inhibitory-to-excitatory and recurrent excitatory pathways,
S^{\uparrow}\!\left[W_{I\to E}\right]

acting on
W_{EI}
and
S^{\downarrow}\!\left[W_{E\to E}\right]
on
W_{EE}
, while the non-selective controls apply the
operator to the full recurrent matrix
W_{\mathrm{rec}}
, with
S^{\uparrow}\!\left[W\right]
at
\delta>0
and

S^{\downarrow}\!\left[W\right]
at
\delta<0
.

Activity-gain scaling.

The operator instead acts on a population’s output,
S^{\uparrow,\downarrow}[r_{P}]
, giving
an effective rate

r_{P}^{\mathrm{eff}}(t)=g_{P}\odot r_{P}(t),\qquad g_{P,j}=\exp(\eta+\tau Z_{j}),\quad Z_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\ \text{i.i.d.},

(10)

with one factor
g_{P,j}
per neuron, so that

\mathbb{E}[r_{P,j}^{\mathrm{eff}}(t)]=(1+\delta)\,r_{P,j}(t)
. Because the gain
multiplies a neuron’s output, it scales that neuron’s contribution equally across all of
its postsynaptic targets. The population-specific operators act on the inhibitory and
excitatory populations,
S^{\uparrow}\!\left[r_{I}\right]
on
r_{I}
and
S^{\downarrow}\!\left[r_{E}\right]
on
r_{E}
, while the non-selective
controls act on all neurons
r_{E\cup I}
, with
S^{\uparrow}\!\left[r\right]
at
\delta>0
and
S^{\downarrow}\!\left[r\right]
at

\delta<0
.

4.5
Stress training

We compare two training regimes. Under standard training the recurrent weights are left
unperturbed. Under stress training, each trial draws its own stress instance, with
magnitude
\delta\sim\mathcal{U}[0,\delta_{T}]
, held fixed throughout that trial’s
forward pass. The stress operator
S
is then applied to its target according to the
mechanism under study, perturbing either the weights in
\theta
or the population
activity
r(t)
, and the task loss is computed on the output of the resulting perturbed
forward pass. Because the perturbation is an element-wise product by sampled factors that
are held fixed, it is differentiable in the underlying quantities, and gradients flow back
to
\theta
whether the operator acts on weights or on activity. Drawing a new stress
instance each trial, the network learns to solve the task robustly across perturbations of
magnitude up to
\delta_{T}
, rather than under a single unperturbed configuration.

4.6
Systematic stress mechanism comparison

We evaluated all eight operators on both naive and resilient networks at three magnitudes

\delta\in\{0,0.5,1.0\}
(negated for downscaling protocols): the unstressed baseline,
the maximum training magnitude
\delta_{T}
, and the maximum tested magnitude
\delta_{\max}
.
For each network and magnitude we ran
100
task trials, each under
100
independent stress
realisations, recording per trial the population rates
\bar{r}_{E},\bar{r}_{I}
(mean activity
per population, time-averaged); the absolute drive components
h_{E\to E}
,
|h_{I\to E}|
,

h_{E\to I}
,
|h_{I\to I}|
(time-averaged absolute population-summed input from each
source); the drive ratio
E/|I|
(computed per time step, then time-averaged); and three
performance measures, accuracy (fraction of the
100
realisations classified correctly),
readout MSE, and decision confidence (the mean winner-minus-runner-up output margin over the
decision window).

Metrics were aggregated in three steps: per trial, dynamic quantities were averaged over
time and accuracy over the
100
realisations; per seed, we took the median over the
100

trials; and each seed was then expressed as a signed percentage change from its own
unperturbed baseline,

\Delta\%_{m,s}(\delta^{\prime})=100\times\frac{\widetilde{m}_{s}(\delta^{\prime})-\widetilde{m}_{s}(\delta_{0})}{\bigl|\,\widetilde{m}_{s}(\delta_{0})\,\bigr|},

(11)

where
\widetilde{m}_{s}(\delta)
is the median of metric
m
for seed
s
at magnitude

\delta
and
\delta_{0}=0,\sigma_{stress}=0
(unstressed). We report the median and interquartile range of

\{\Delta\%_{m,s}\}_{s=1}^{N}
across
N=50
networks, initialised with different seeds, preferred over mean and standard
deviation for robustness to outlier networks at this seed count. Each metric is evaluated at

\delta^{\prime}=\delta_{T}
and
\delta^{\prime}=\delta_{\max}
and shown in the diagonally split cells of
Fig.
3
(upper-left
\delta_{T}
, lower-right
\delta_{\max}
), placing
within-range robustness and beyond-range generalisation side by side.

4.7
Recurrent activity PCA analysis

For each network, PCA was fit to the baseline population activity collected from a fixed set of 5000 trials. The first three baseline PCs were used only for visualisation in Figure
5
. Activity from each stress condition was then projected onto the same baseline PCs, allowing baseline and stressed trajectories to be compared in a common coordinate system within each network.

We then selected the smallest number of PCs needed to explain at least
90\%
of baseline variance,

k=\min\left\{k^{\prime}:\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k^{\prime}}\lambda_{i}}{\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}}\geq 0.90\right\},

(12)

where
\lambda_{i}
are the baseline PCA eigenvalues. These k PCs defined the baseline subspace and were kept fixed for all stress conditions. For each stress level
\delta
, activity was centered using the baseline mean,
\tilde{X}^{\delta}=X^{\delta}-\mu
, and its covariance
C^{\delta}
was computed. The subspace preservation index was defined as the fraction of stressed-condition variance captured by the baseline subspace:

\rho(\delta)=\frac{\operatorname{tr}\left(PC^{\delta}P^{\top}\right)}{\operatorname{tr}\left(C^{\delta}\right)},

(13)

where
P\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times N}
contains the top k baseline PCs. Values close to 1 indicate that stressed activity largely remained in the same subspace as baseline activity, whereas lower values indicate that activity moved into dimensions that were not dominant at baseline. By construction,
\rho(0)\geq 0.90
.

Using this same baseline PCA subspace, baseline and stressed activity were projected onto the selected baseline PCs. For each trial and time point, we computed the Euclidean distance between the stressed and baseline projected activity vectors from the same trial. These distances were then averaged across trials and time points to obtain one displacement value for each seed, network, and stress condition.

All cross-group and within-group comparisons in Section
2.3
use Mann–Whitney
U
tests, and variance comparisons use Brown–Forsythe tests; full test statistics are reported in Table
3
.

4.8
Metabolic energy analysis

We computed a per-timestep metabolic energy from recurrent activity and
synaptic transmission costs following
[
1
]
, evaluated on the
same 5000 shared trials used for the recurrent activity analyses. For each
seed, network type, and stress condition, we ran the network forward under
perturbation strength
\delta
and computed energy using the effective weights
induced by that condition.

For each neuron
i
, the synaptic input cost was

s_{i}(t)=\sum_{j}|W_{ij}|\,r_{j}(t)+\sum_{k}|W^{\mathrm{in}}_{ik}|\,u_{k}(t),

(14)

where
W_{ij}
and
W^{\mathrm{in}}_{ik}
are the effective recurrent and
input weights,
r_{j}(t)
is the presynaptic firing rate, and
u_{k}(t)
is the
external input. Under stressed conditions,
W_{ij}
includes the stress
perturbation applied during the forward pass.

Population-level activity and synaptic costs were decomposed by target
population (
N
total units):

\mathcal{R}_{E}(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in E}r_{i}(t),\qquad\mathcal{R}_{I}(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in I}r_{i}(t),

(15)

\mathcal{S}_{E}(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in E}s_{i}(t),\qquad\mathcal{S}_{I}(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in I}s_{i}(t).

(16)

Total metabolic energy was then

E(t)=\alpha_{1}\bigl(\mathcal{R}_{E}(t)+\mathcal{R}_{I}(t)\bigr)+\alpha_{2}\bigl(\mathcal{S}_{E}(t)+\mathcal{S}_{I}(t)\bigr),

(17)

with
\alpha_{1}=1/3
and
\alpha_{2}=2/3
. For each seed and condition,

E(t)
was averaged across trials and time, yielding one
scalar energy measure per seed.

To compare stress-induced changes while controlling for baseline differences,
we computed the per-seed relative energy deviation between baseline (BL) and stressed with magnitude
\delta

D_{E}^{\mathrm{seed}}(\delta)=\frac{\left|E^{\mathrm{seed}}_{\delta}-E^{\mathrm{seed}}_{\mathrm{BL}}\right|}{E^{\mathrm{seed}}_{\mathrm{BL}}},

(18)

and, for an individual component
\mathcal{Q}
(e.g.,
\mathcal{S}_{E}
,

\mathcal{S}_{I}
), the per-seed relative drop

\Delta_{\mathcal{Q}}^{\mathrm{seed}}(\delta)=\frac{\mathcal{Q}^{\mathrm{seed}}_{\mathrm{BL}}-\mathcal{Q}^{\mathrm{seed}}_{\delta}}{\mathcal{Q}^{\mathrm{seed}}_{\mathrm{BL}}},

(19)

where positive values indicate a stress-induced reduction and negative values
an increase.

4.9
Delay-generalisation tradeoff analysis

We evaluated whether training for stress resilience altered temporal generalisation on the parametric working-memory task. During training, delays were sampled from
400
–
900~\mathrm{ms}
. We therefore defined delays in this interval as within-distribution delays, and delays longer than
900~\mathrm{ms}
as long out-of-distribution delays, denoted OOD-high. For each trained network, accuracy was averaged separately over within-distribution and OOD-high delays. Accuracy was computed from
2000
trials per delay condition, excluding zero-coherence trials. A trial was counted as correct when the sign of the mean decision variable during the decision period matched the sign of the stimulus difference.

We analysed two generalisation tradeoff experiments. In the training stress magnitude experiment, Resilient networks were trained with maximum stress magnitude

\delta_{\max}\in\{0.25,0.5,0.75,1.0,1.5,2.0\}
.
For each value of
\delta_{\max}
,
50
independently trained networks were evaluated for each network type, Naïve and Resilient. In the network size experiment, we tested
N\in\{50,100,200,400,500\}
, with the excitatory population fixed at
N_{\mathrm{exc}}=0.8N
, rounded to the nearest integer. Again, for each network size,
50
independently trained networks were evaluated for each network type. All networks were evaluated under two test conditions: no stress, and acute test-time stress using
S^{\uparrow}\!\left[W_{I\to E}\right]
at
\delta=0.25
.

For each experiment, delay region, and evaluation condition, we quantified the group difference as

\Delta=\bar{a}_{\mathrm{Naive}}-\bar{a}_{\mathrm{Resilient}},

where
a
denotes accuracy. Thus,
\Delta>0
indicates better performance in Naïve networks, whereas
\Delta<0
indicates better performance in Resilient networks. Because Naïve and Resilient networks were trained from independent random initialisations and training seeds, we treated the two network classes as independent replicate groups rather than paired observations. Confidence intervals for
\Delta
were therefore computed using the independent-groups standard error,

SE_{\Delta}=\sqrt{\frac{s^{2}_{\mathrm{Naive}}}{n_{\mathrm{Naive}}}+\frac{s^{2}_{\mathrm{Resilient}}}{n_{\mathrm{Resilient}}}}.

(20)

For the primary statistical analysis, we treated network type as an independent between-network factor and fit ordinary least-squares models with heteroscedasticity-consistent HC3 standard errors. The main effect of network type was tested with
\mathrm{accuracy}\sim\mathrm{network\ type}+\mathrm{parameter}
,
where the swept parameter was
\delta_{\max}
in the training stress magnitude experiment and
N
in the network size experiment. To test whether the Naïve–Resilient performance gap changed across each sweep, we fit the interaction model
\mathrm{accuracy}\sim\mathrm{network\ type}\times\mathrm{parameter}
.
The interaction term tested whether the generalisation tradeoff varied with stress-training magnitude or network capacity. As a direct descriptive comparison, we also computed Welch two-sample tests comparing Naïve and Resilient accuracies while allowing unequal variances between groups.

4.10
Network density and reciprocity measures

Graph-theoretical analyses were performed on trained recurrent connectivity matrices,

W_{\mathrm{rec}}
, interpreted as weighted, directed, and signed graphs according to the convention

W_{\mathrm{rec}}[\mathrm{post},\mathrm{pre}].

Density was computed from the binary directed support graph

A_{ij}=\mathbf{1}\left(|W_{ij}|>0\right),\qquad i\neq j,

as

D=\frac{\sum_{i\neq j}A_{ij}}{N(N-1)}.

Weighted directed reciprocity was computed from the absolute recurrent connectivity matrix,

W_{ij}^{\mathrm{abs}}=|W_{ij}|,

with self-connections excluded, as

R_{w}=\frac{\sum_{i\neq j}\min\left(W_{ij}^{\mathrm{abs}},W_{ji}^{\mathrm{abs}}\right)}{\sum_{i\neq j}W_{ij}^{\mathrm{abs}}}.
