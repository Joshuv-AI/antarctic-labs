 SUMMARY

 The FY2025 Light Water Reactor Sustainability (LWRS) Program Materials Research Pathway
Technical Program Plan represents a strategic effort by the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) to support
the continued reliable performance of light water reactors (LWRs) well beyond their initially intended
operational life spans. With LWRs providing a significant portion of the nation’s carbon-free electricity
generation, addressing challenges associated with aging materials and infrastructure is essential for both
energy security and sustainability. The report highlights critical advancements in understanding material
degradation mechanisms, developing innovative mitigation strategies, and exploring next-generation
technologies to improve reactor efficiency, durability, and safety.

 Key achievements include examining embrittlement trends in reactor pressure vessel (RPV) materials
under high neutron fluences, understanding irradiation-assisted stress corrosion cracking (IASCC) in
irradiated stainless steels, and assessing the environmental fatigue performance of additively
manufactured (AM) alloys. The report also delves into advanced non-destructive evaluation (NDE)
technologies for concrete shielding and electrical cable systems, as well as testing cutting-edge weld
repair techniques for helium-containing irradiated materials. Multidisciplinary collaborations among
national laboratories, utilities, research institutions, and regulatory bodies have enabled robust
experimental data, validated predictive modeling tools, and supported solution-driven approaches for
ensuring long-term reactor sustainability.

 The findings not only address existing reactor challenges but also pave the way for future innovation
in nuclear materials research. This work directly supports life-extension programs, cost-effective
maintenance strategies, and regulatory decisions, enhancing the overall safety, reliability, and
sustainability of nuclear power as a cornerstone of the domestic energy mix.

 FY2025 Light Water Reactor Sustainability Program
 Materials Research Pathway Technical Program Plan
 1. Introduction
 Light water reactors (LWRs) form the backbone of the global nuclear energy infrastructure, providing
reliable, low-carbon electricity for decades. However, as the fleet of LWRs approaches and exceeds
design lifetimes originally targeted at 40 years, the challenges of aging materials and infrastructure
become increasingly prominent. Extending reactor operations to 60 years, 80 years, or beyond demands
cutting-edge research into the mechanisms of material degradation, advanced repair techniques, and
predictive tools to assess safety margins over extended time spans. The FY2025 LWRS Materials
Research Pathway Technical Program Plan reflects the ongoing commitment of the U.S. government, led
by the Department of Energy (DOE) Office of Nuclear Energy, to address these challenges through
coordinated multidisciplinary research aimed at identifying and overcoming barriers to long-term reactor
sustainability.
 The report presents key areas of focus, including advanced studies into reactor metals, concrete
biological shields, electrical cabling, and welding techniques—all of which are impacted by prolonged
environmental exposure, irradiation, mechanical stresses, and aging. Specific efforts such as evaluating
the mechanisms behind irradiation-assisted stress corrosion cracking (IASCC), studying the radiation-
induced volumetric expansion (RIVE) in concrete aggregates, and assessing environmentally assisted
fatigue (EAF) in additively manufactured components highlight some of the milestones discussed in this
report. These initiatives are shaped by experimental data, modeling insights, and the integration of
international research findings to refine standards and enable proactive maintenance strategies.
 By examining degradation phenomena through innovative technologies—such as machine learning
for cable condition monitoring and proton irradiation as a surrogate for neutron experiments—the
program opens new avenues for diagnosing, mitigating, and preventing material failures in nuclear
reactors. The introduction of highly engineered materials like oxide dispersion-strengthened (ODS) alloys
also underscores progress in mitigating stress corrosion and cracking, safeguarding reactor integrity
against extreme irradiation environments. The LWRS Program serves as a model for leveraging
collaborative efforts among national laboratories, industry stakeholders, utilities, and regulatory agencies
to secure the future of nuclear energy as a vital and sustainable energy source.

 2. Reactor Metals
 2.1 High-Fluence Effects on RPV Steels
2.1.1 Objective
 The project in FY25 aims to characterize the effects of high neutron fluence on materials harvested
from the surveillance capsule A-60 of the Palisades Nuclear Generating Station (PNGS) reactor vessel
[1]. The primary focus is to evaluate embrittlement trends in key reactor pressure vessel (RPV) materials,
including weld and base metals, after exposure to a fluence equivalent to more than 150 reactor years.
This data will support the development and validation of embrittlement trend correlations (ETCs) for
predicting long-term material performance in nuclear reactor components.
2.1.2 Approach
 The capsule A-60, which contained weld, base metal, and standard reference material (SRM)
specimens, was retrieved and analyzed through a series of mechanical and microstructural tests. Testing
included:
 1. Charpy Impact Testing: Understanding the transition temperature shifts and drop in upper-shelf
 energy (USE).
 2. Chemical Analysis: Studying the compositions of nickel, copper, and other radiation-sensitive
 elements.
 3. Tensile Testing: Evaluating radiation-induced hardening and changes in tensile properties.
 4. Microstructural Studies: Recommended as future tasks to better understand fracture toughness
 and irradiation effects at the atomic level (e.g., Atom Probe Tomography).
 These analyses provide insight into the material behavior under high fluence conditions and enable
comparisons to existing databases and predictions of ETC models.
2.1.3 Accomplishments
 The research successfully characterized the highly irradiated materials from the A-60 capsule:
 • High Radiation Embrittlement: Weld metal exhibited a significant Charpy T41J transition
 temperature shift of 243°C (Figure 1a), and base metal showed a shift of 165°C (Figure 1b),
 indicating a very high degree of embrittlement.

 1. Enhanced Predictive Modeling: The study provides benchmark data for refining both current

 2. Implications for Long-Term Operation (LTO): The findings suggest that mitigation strategies

 materials. The study validates the potential for addressing pressurized thermal shock (PTS) and
 other safety concerns in aging reactors.
 3. License Extensions: The insights gained from the A-60 capsule analysis support regulatory
 measures and decisions regarding license renewals and long-term reactor sustainability.
 4. Material Testing Standards: The results contribute to the ongoing development of material
 surveillance programs, specifically for high-fluence environments, and inform future material
 testing protocols.

 2.2 Mechanisms of Irradiation-Assisted Stress Corrosion Cracking
 (IASCC)
2.2.1 Objective
 This project investigates the irradiation-induced deformation and fracture behavior of 304L stainless
steel recovered from the baffle assembly of the San Onofre Nuclear Generating Station (SONGS) [2]. The
primary objective is to examine the mechanical performance, fracture mechanisms, and microstructural
changes caused by neutron irradiation at damage levels up to 19.1 displacements per atom (dpa). The goal
is to understand the influence of test temperature and irradiation on material degradation, with a focus on
intergranular (IG) fracture and its suppression at elevated temperatures.
2.2.2 Approach
 Multifaceted approaches were adopted in this study:
 1. Specimen Selection and Preparation:
 o Specimens were harvested from compact tension (CT) samples of irradiated SONGS
 baffle plates and formers. Cuboids (8×8×12 mm) were cut to preserve pristine material
 conditions.
 o Tensile test specimens (subsize "SS-Tiny" geometry) were machined, and additional
 slices were reserved for microstructural and chemical analyses, including helium and
 hydrogen measurements.

 4

 2. Testing and Analysis:
 o Tensile Testing: Conducted at room temperature (RT), 100°C, and 320°C at a strain rate
                   of 10⁻³ s⁻¹.
 o Digital Image Correlation (DIC): Non-contact strain measurements were used to study
 strain localization and deformation patterns under different conditions.
 o Fractography & Microstructural Study: Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and
 electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) were employed to analyze fracture surfaces and
 pre/post-deformation microstructure.
 3. Irradiation Dose and Microstructure: The effect of irradiation-induced phase transformations
 (e.g., bcc-phase formation via strain-induced martensite) at ~9.62 dpa was studied.
2.2.3 Accomplishments
 Main findings of this study include:
 1. Temperature Sensitivity of Fracture Behavior:
 o At RT, IG brittle fracture dominated, with no significant necking observed (Figure 2).
 o Increasing the temperature to 100°C and 320°C suppressed IG fracture entirely, resulting
 in ductile failure dominated by microvoid coalescence.
 o At 9.62 dpa, RT tensile tests revealed unusual features, including moving deformation
 bands indicative of strain-induced martensitic transformations.

Figure 2 General view of the fractured specimen. (a, b) Appearance of specimen halves, (c) view of the fracture area at 70° tilt,
and (d) gauge view at 70° tilt. 5C2A-2-15 tensile specimen (RT test/10−3 s−1).

 5

 2. Fracture Complexity at Room Temperature:
 o Fractographic analysis showed dominant IG fracture with significant secondary cracks,
 minimal inclusions, and evidence of slip lines at grain boundaries (GBs). Minor
 transgranular (TG) fracture and shallow dimples were also present.
 o Elevated temperatures promoted full recovery of ductile fracture behavior and suppressed
 susceptibility to irradiation-induced embrittlement.
 3. Impact of Irradiation on Microstructure:
 o EBSD mapping showed high fractions of strain-induced bcc-phase (25-30% in the gauge
 region and >60% near the fracture line) in RT specimens, suggesting increased instability
 in 304L steel under irradiation.
 o Irradiation-induced defect-free channels and dislocation pileups at GBs were observed,
 highlighting the deterioration of GB integrity.
 4. Strain Rate Effects:
 o Reducing the strain rate led to a dramatic drop in ductility during RT tests, suggesting a
 strong dependency of fracture on deformation kinetics.
2.2.4 Impact on the nuclear industry
 The study has strong implications for the nuclear industry:
 1. Material Degradation Insights:
 o The discovery of IG brittle fracture at RT, even in a traditionally ductile material like
 304L stainless steel, has significant implications for reactor component integrity during
 long-term operation in light-water reactors (LWRs).
 o The findings highlight the risk of unexpected material failure under ambient conditions
 for irradiated components, necessitating improved material screening and lifecycle
 predictions.
 2. Improved Long-Term Reliability Models:
 o By linking microstructural changes (e.g., bcc-phase formation) and embrittlement
 mechanisms (e.g., IG cracking), these results provide key data for refining predictive
 models of irradiation-induced degradation.
 3. Operational Safety:
 o The suppression of IG fracture at elevated temperatures underscores the importance of
 maintaining in-service operating conditions that minimize brittle fracture risks.
 o These results are critical for assessing the lifetime extension feasibility of reactor
 internals, particularly in aging plants.
 4. Future Research Directions:
 o The findings call for further exploration of the relationship between irradiation dose,
 strain rate, and phase transformations to enhance material performance predictions in
 severe neutron-flux environments.

 2.3 Crack Initiation in Metal Alloys
2.3.1 Objective
 The project aims to investigate the impact of dissolved oxygen (DO) and other service conditions on
the initiation of stress corrosion cracking (SCC) in austenitic stainless steels (304L and 316L) under
pressurized water reactor (PWR) primary water conditions [3]. The research focuses on understanding the
key environmental and mechanical factors contributing to SCC initiation in cold-forged stainless steels,
which are commonly used in nuclear power plants for core and auxiliary components. Recent SCC
incidents in French nuclear power plants have raised safety and operational concerns, prompting the need
to examine the role of DO concentrations in crack formation and its interaction with other environmental
factors. The findings aim to improve inspection, maintenance strategies, and the design of SCC-resistant
materials.
2.3.2 Approach
 The study utilized a multi-step experimental and characterization plan. Stainless steel specimens with
cold-forging levels of 10%, 20%, 30%, and 40% were prepared to introduce different levels of strain and
produce hardness levels reported in SCC-affected cases. Experimental tests were conducted in 300°C
PWR primary water under different DO concentrations: 2 ppm, 50 ppb, and hydrogenated water (30 cc/kg
H2), using blunt notch compact tension (BNCT) specimens to simulate complex strain paths. In situ
monitoring of crack initiation was performed via direct current potential drop (DCPD), complemented by
SEM and EBSD analyses of microstructural changes at various stages of testing. To better replicate the
loading scenarios encountered in PWR conditions, periodic partial unloading (PPU) was introduced to
simulate stress cycling.
2.3.3 Accomplishments
 The study found that dissolved oxygen directly influenced the SCC initiation behavior of cold-forged
304L specimens. After approximately 4,000 hours of testing, SEM analysis revealed small cracks, with
more pronounced cracking in 2 ppm DO water compared to 50 ppb DO water as shown in Figure 3. The
cracks were primarily IG but included TG features as well, and the density of cracks increased with the
level of cold forging. Slip bands observed at grain boundaries in less cold-worked specimens (10% and
20% forging) indicated localized dislocation activity leading to crack initiation. Reducing the DO
concentration to 50 ppb significantly lowered the crack density and slowed cracking processes, suggesting
the role of protective oxide layers in reducing the electrochemical potential (ECP) and SCC susceptibility.
Hydrogenated water conditions with 30 cc/kg H2 showed no crack initiation during 400 hours of exposure
under constant load, underscoring the effectiveness of hydrogenated environments in mitigating SCC.
 Introducing PPU for stress cycling allowed observations of its influence on SCC initiation, though no
major crack growth was detected up to 6,000 hours of testing. Cold forging to higher levels (30% and
40%) increased mechanical hardness and strength but did not significantly accelerate SCC initiation under
constant load conditions.

2.3.4 Impact on the nuclear industry
 The findings of this study carry implications for the operational safety and long-term performance of
nuclear reactors. The ability to identify and quantify the role of DO in SCC initiation provides critical
insights into mitigating SCC in PWR auxiliary and primary systems. By demonstrating that lower DO
concentrations and hydrogen environments limit SCC susceptibility, these results can inform best
practices in water chemistry control and inspection regimes. Furthermore, the study provides valuable
service-relevant data that can guide inspection intervals and maintenance planning for stainless steel
components, particularly in high-stress or cold-forged conditions. Insights into the interaction of DO,
stress cycling, and metallurgical properties will help refine predictive models for SCC in nuclear reactor
materials, supporting license renewals and long-term sustainability programs for aging reactors.

 2.4 Environmental Fatigue
2.4.1 Objective
 The project investigates the performance of additively manufactured (AM) 316L stainless steel in
LWR environments, focusing on SCC and environmentally assisted fatigue (EAF) behavior [4]. This
research supports the qualification and certification of AM components for nuclear applications,
addressing their long-term mechanical and corrosion performance under PWR conditions. The goal is to
assess the potential of AM technologies to produce high-quality, cost-effective components that meet the
rigorous safety standards of the nuclear industry, enabling their deployment in aging reactors and
innovative reactor designs.
2.4.2 Approach
 The study utilized AM 316L stainless steel components fabricated using laser powder bed fusion
(LPBF) methods, with geometries mimicking complex configurations in nuclear applications, such as

 8

tubing and flanges. Samples were produced by both the Argonne National Laboratory (ANL) using the
Renishaw AM400 system, and by the industry (Westinghouse Electric Company) using an EOS M290
system. Testing focused on key mechanical properties and performance metrics, including SCC crack
initiation, SCC crack growth rate (CGR), and EAF behavior under simulated LWR conditions.
 Porosity and microstructural characteristics were evaluated through synchrotron-based X-ray
tomography at ANL’s Advanced Photon Source (APS). For mechanical performance testing, CT
specimens were examined for SCC in an autoclave simulating PWR conditions, while fatigue tests were
performed in both air and simulated LWR environments. Environmental correction factors (Fen) were
calculated to assess the influence of PWR water on fatigue lifetimes.
2.4.3 Accomplishments
 The study demonstrated that AM 316L stainless steel exhibits comparable SCC and EAF performance
to conventionally manufactured alloys under the tested conditions. The porosity of AM materials,
measured at 0.06% for ANL-produced tubing and 0.09% for industry-printed flanges, was minimal and
consistent along the sampled regions. Crack growth rate tests in PWR water revealed no significant
differences between AM-produced and wrought materials, with SCC resistance observed in both.
 Fatigue testing indicated that AM 316L materials had slightly reduced fatigue lifetimes compared to
wrought stainless steels, particularly in PWR water as shown in Figure 4. While a solution annealing (SA)
post-print heat treatment altered cyclic stress behavior, it did not significantly enhance fatigue
performance. Additionally, environmental correction factors Fen for AM 316L were calculated to be
approximately 3, aligning with values for conventionally produced stainless steel.

 SCC initiation tests revealed that the "as-printed" surface geometry (e.g., unsupported notches) led to
faster cracking compared to machined surfaces. This highlights the importance of geometry and post-print
surface finishing in minimizing crack susceptibility. Results emphasize the need for further evaluation of
AM components’ near-net-shape surface finishes in future studies.
2.4.4 Impact on the nuclear industry
 The findings support the adoption of AM 316L stainless steel for nuclear applications by showing its
mechanical integrity under simulated reactor conditions. The use of AM technologies has the potential to
produce custom-designed and cost-effective components, addressing challenges associated with long-term
reactor operations and the production of complex replacement parts. This research also provides critical
performance data that can influence regulatory guidelines and accelerate the acceptance of advanced
manufacturing technologies in the nuclear industry.
 By demonstrating that AM alloys perform similarly to conventional materials regarding SCC and
EAF, the study alleviates concerns about the variability and anisotropy inherent in AM processes.
However, the study also underscores the need for optimizing geometry, surface finishing, and post-print
heat treatments to ensure consistent reliability. These findings will help refine manufacturing standards
and guide future development of AM components tailored for demanding LWR environments.

 2.5 Post-Irradiation Examination of Ginna Baffle Bolts
2.5.1 Objective
 The project investigates radiation-induced degradation mechanisms in baffle-former bolts (BFBs)
retrieved from a high fluence Westinghouse PWR [5]. The goal is to characterize microstructural
evolution, assess mechanical property degradation, and understand the behavior of transmutation gases
and nanocavity formation along the length of the bolts. These insights aim to inform predictive models for
radiation-induced swelling, segregation, precipitation, and susceptibility to IASCC, ultimately supporting
the long-term operation and sustainability of aging reactors.
2.5.2 Approach
 Two high fluence BFBs were harvested in 2016 and subjected to extensive inspections and
experimental characterizations. The bolts were examined for their microstructural evolution across
sections with different fluences, including their bolt head (CS), mid-shank (MS), and thread ends (BS). A
suite of advanced tools, including transmission electron microscopy (TEM), atom probe tomography
(APT), and time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS), was utilized to quantify
gradients in cavity density and size, transmuted gas concentrations, and isotopic distributions. TEM
imaging provided cavity characterizations based on size and density, while ToF-SIMS captured elemental
and isotopic compositions, including hydrogen isotopes (protium and deuterium), and spatial imaging of
structural distributions. Specific challenges related to detecting inert gases and minimizing analytical
artifacts were addressed through refining sample preparation techniques and exploring cryogenic
transfers.
2.5.3 Accomplishments

cavities (average radius: ~1.9 nm) but a higher number density, while the thread and shank sections
developed larger cavities (up to ~5 nm) but lower overall density as shown in Figure 5. These gradients
were attributed to differences in temperature, strain, neutron fluence, and hydrogen accumulation along
the bolts. ToF-SIMS confirmed the presence of hydrogen isotopes and highlighted higher concentrations
of protium and deuterium compared to unirradiated samples. APT further validated the isotopic findings,

 2.6 Mitigation of IASCC
2.6.1 Objective
 The project investigates strategies to mitigate IASCC in stainless steel alloys used in nuclear reactor
components [6]. Building on decades of mechanistic insights into IASCC behavior, the study pivots
toward engineered microstructures tailored to resist IASCC. Three innovative materials—oxide-
dispersion-strengthened (ODS) 304, ultrafine-grained (UFG) 304, and additively manufactured and hot
isostatically pressed (AM-HIP) 316 stainless steels—were evaluated for their ability to resist stress
corrosion cracking. The goal is to advance materials science for components subjected to irradiation,
tensile stresses, and high-temperature water in reactor environments, supporting the durability and safety
of LWRs.
2.6.2 Approach
 Proton irradiation was conducted to mimic the radiation damage that occurs in nuclear reactors,
targeting these novel microstructures. Specimens were irradiated to 5 displacement per atom (dpa) at high
temperatures (360°C), creating a hardened layer on the surface while leaving the material bulk
unirradiated. The mechanical behavior of irradiated layers was analyzed using micro-indentation
techniques, enabling the determination of hardness-to-yield strength correlations at elevated temperatures.
 IASCC susceptibility was tested using incremental four-point bend (4PB) tests in simulated PWR
conditions at 320°C and 2000 psi, with water chemistry closely resembling reactor environments. Finite
element analysis (FEA) models were developed to calculate surface stresses on proton-irradiated samples,
allowing accurate surface stress determination during bending. Crack initiation thresholds were identified
relative to the fraction of irradiated yield strength. Complementary techniques like SEM and TEM were
utilized to evaluate grain boundary oxidation, crack microstructure, and thermodynamic stabilization of
dispersoids.
2.6.3 Accomplishments
 The study demonstrated the ability to quantify IASCC susceptibility for various engineered
microstructures under high-temperature and radiation conditions. It confirmed that wrought 304 stainless
steel samples cracked at 60% of their irradiated yield strength under bending stress conditions (see Figure
6 ), consistent with prior literature. In contrast, the ODS 304 sample showed no cracking even under
higher stress conditions, suggesting substantial resistance to IASCC due to oxide dispersoids stabilizing
the microstructure. TEM characterization revealed supersaturated solid solutions of yttrium and oxygen
atoms incorporated into the steel matrix during mechanical alloying, leading to nanometric oxide
dispersions. Grain refinement achieved through oxide dispersion reduced grain size by 93% and enhanced
hardness by 46%, further improving resistance to IASCC and mechanical properties under irradiation.

 12

 Figure 6 IASCC crack progression with increasing stress in wrought stainless steel

 Micro-indentation experiments demonstrated that hardness decreased from room temperature to high-
temperature conditions (320–340°C) in a manner consistent with yield strength reductions. Proton-
irradiated materials exhibited performance closely aligned with neutron-irradiated reference materials,
validating the relevance of proton irradiation for accelerated IASCC studies. The breakthrough of
advanced synthesis and processing methods for ODS materials establishes a framework for manufacturing
IASCC-resistant alloys with enhanced mechanical robustness.
2.6.4 Impact on the nuclear industry
 The findings have significant implications for improving the reliability and longevity of reactor
components. By demonstrating that the ODS 304 microstructure resists IASCC far better than traditional
wrought materials, the study paves the way for incorporating advanced alloys into reactor designs. This
could mitigate long-term degradation concerns, improve safety margins, and reduce maintenance costs in
aging nuclear infrastructure. The use of proton irradiation as a surrogate for neutron-based testing also
represents a cost-effective and expedited method to screen materials for stress corrosion cracking
resistance, accelerating the deployment of novel materials.
 The insights gained around grain refinement, oxide dispersion strengthening, and mechanical
behavior under irradiation allow for optimizing material selection for next-generation reactors. These
advancements contribute to efforts aimed at extending the operating life of existing reactors while
ensuring performance under increasingly demanding irradiation environments. The study underlines the
importance of integrating material innovation into frameworks that support regulatory acceptance and the
transition toward sustainable nuclear energy.

 3. Concrete
 3.1 NDE of Concrete and Civil Structures
3.1.1 Objective
 The objective of the project was to develop ultrasonic nondestructive evaluation (NDE) techniques
for assessing tensile stress in steel rebar within reinforced concrete structures, specifically leveraging the
acoustoelastic effect [7]. This effort aimed to enable field-deployable, non-invasive diagnostics to
measure embedded rebar stress in nuclear infrastructure without requiring destructive sampling. The
study addresses a critical need for accurate and non-invasive stress monitoring in aging structures,
particularly in high-radiation environments like nuclear power plants.
3.1.2 Approach
 The project implemented a series of tensile tests on steel rebar samples to understand the relationship
between ultrasonic wave velocity and tensile stress using the acoustoelastic effect. Experimental efforts
included the use of #6 and #8 Grade 60 rebars, a customized ultrasonic monitoring setup employing 5
MHz contact transducers, and a digital signal processing algorithm. The team integrated advanced
techniques such as coda wave interferometry and stretching algorithms to process ultrasonic signals and
detect subtle variations in wave velocity caused by stress. The testing also incorporated synchronized
measuring devices to monitor mechanical load, temperature, and ultrasonic signals during both loading
and unloading cycles.
3.1.3 Accomplishments
 The study successfully demonstrated a strong, repeatable linear correlation between ultrasonic wave
velocity and tensile stress in rebar, under both loading and unloading conditions as shown in Figure 7.
The findings were consistent across various test scenarios with differences in loading rates and peak stress
levels, confirming the robustness and repeatability of the acoustoelastic response. Additionally, the
research validated the importance of temperature compensation, identifying thermal effects on ultrasonic
measurements during elastic loading and unloading. These efforts provided critical empirical support for
extending ultrasonic NDE techniques to in situ applications in nuclear infrastructure.

 3.2 Irradiated Concrete
3.2.1 Objective
 The project's objective was to investigate the effects of neutron flux on irradiation-induced volumetric
expansion (RIVE) in concrete aggregates and minerals, which is a critical factor in the long-term
structural integrity of reactor shielding [8]. The study aimed to provide a fundamental understanding of
how flux and time-dependent phenomena such as defect formation, annealing, and irradiation creep
influence RIVE. Additionally, the project explored the potential role of gamma radiation in the damage
and healing processes and used ion irradiation as a surrogate method to examine the interplay between
neutron and gamma irradiation effects.
3.2.2 Approach
 The researchers conducted a comprehensive literature review of neutron flux effects on common
concrete aggregates (e.g., quartz, feldspar, basalt, mica) and their associated radiation damage
mechanisms, including RIVE. The study introduced ion irradiation as a surrogate to simulate neutron and
gamma interactions, leveraging computational methods such as SRIM (Stopping and Range of Ions in
Matter) to model energy loss profiles and predict displacement damage. Key mechanisms such as point
defect recombination, defect migration, cascade-induced annealing, and irradiation creep were analyzed
to understand time- and flux-dependent recovery processes. Experimental and theoretical modeling was
carried out to validate these hypotheses, supplemented by ion irradiation studies of quartz under varying
stopping power conditions.
3.2.3 Accomplishments
 The study demonstrated the significant flux dependence of RIVE, revealing that higher neutron fluxes
result in greater volumetric expansion due to limited recovery time for defect annealing. The research
identified quartz as exhibiting the highest RIVE (up to 15-18%), driven by crystalline-to-amorphous
transformations. It also highlighted the mitigating role of low-flux irradiation in reducing net swelling by
allowing defect recombination and structural relaxation. Gamma radiation was found to contribute to both
damage and defect healing, particularly through hydrogen-assisted passivation mechanisms. A
computational investigation using ion irradiation provided preliminary insights into the relative
contributions of nuclear and electronic stopping mechanisms to RIVE as shown in Figure 8. These results
offer a deeper understanding of neutron-gamma flux interactions and their impact on aggregate and
concrete behavior over reactor lifetimes.

 The findings of this study significantly enhance the understanding of long-term concrete degradation
in light-water reactor shielding and other nuclear facilities. By elucidating the flux-dependent
mechanisms of RIVE, the research provides a solid scientific basis for refining predictive models of
concrete degradation at extended reactor lifetimes, which can lead to more accurate assessments of
structural integrity and improved life-extension strategies. The project's insights into ion irradiation as a

radiation effects, which can guide the development of more radiation-resistant materials. This work is

 3.3 Cross Pathway PRA Concrete Bio Shield
3.3.1 Objective
 The objective of this project is to evaluate the reliability and structural performance of aging nuclear
infrastructure, specifically the concrete biological shield (CBS) and RPV supports, when subjected to

properties and structural integrity. This study aims to provide insights necessary for life-extension

 4. Cabling
 4.1 Cable Aging Degradation and Gap Analysis
4.1.1 Objective
 The objective of this research is to understand the roles of material type, history, and operating
environment on cable system degradation; understand accelerated testing limitations; and support partners
in modeling activities, surveillance, and testing criteria. The motivation for the work comes from the need
to address aging management of hundreds of miles of power, control, instrumentation, and other cable
types found in nuclear reactors. Local adverse environments of elevated temperature, moisture, and
radiation contribute to degradation of cables that can lead to circuit failures, unplanned outages, and
safety concerns. Cable aging management is key for continued use of cables, originally envisioned for a
40-year service life, for operation to 60 years, 80 years, or beyond. Safety-related Class 1E
environmentally qualified (EQ) cable lives of 40 years have been extended to 60 years and 80 years in
license renewal through re-analysis of data from the initial 40-year qualification. However, the
conservatisms and margin in the historic qualification processes are finite and extension of the qualified
status of EQ cables will eventually require utilization of a different strategy than re-analysis, such as re-
qualification or establishment of condition-based qualification. Meanwhile, as a requirement of license
renewal, the reliability of non-EQ cables in long term operation must be maintained through a cable aging
management program that involves assessment of adverse cable environments, periodic testing of
representative cables, and replacement of aging cables as needed.
4.1.2 Approach
 This research involves testing and characterizing both naturally aged nuclear electrical cables and
cables subjected to accelerated aging to better understand cable material changes from aging and the
implications of those changes for long-term cable system performance. Predictive understanding of
degradation behavior is sought to enable informed cable aging management including direction and
interpretation of cable inspections and optimized repair and replacement decisions. Capabilities at Pacific
Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL) established for electrical cable accelerated aging, testing,
characterization, and modeling, established by LWRS and other programs, are applied to near-, mid-, and
long-term needs of the light water reactor community to support reliable and cost-effective cable use.
Research is performed in cooperation and collaboration with partners from the Nuclear Regulatory
Commission, the Electric Power Research Institute, nuclear cable service providers, cable manufacturers,
utilities, universities, and other stakeholders to elucidate cable material aging behavior and enable tools
for effective and efficient monitoring of cable health.
4.1.3 Accomplishments
 The recent move by the EPA to ban decabromodiphenyl ether (decaBDE), the flame retardant used in
the ubiquitous RSCC Firewall® III cross-linked polyethylene (XLPE) nuclear cable insulation, spurred
concern in the nuclear community regarding the equivalency of the new XLPE formulation RSCC
developed to existing decaBDE version for safety-related cables that are required to demonstrate
significant thermal and gamma radiation resistance. In FY25, this investigation directedly compared
thermal and gamma radiation aging effects (see Figure 11) in insulated conductors containing the two
XLPE formulations using cables provided by RSCC for the purpose [11]. Statistical differences in aging
behavior were observed between the two formulations in terms of color change, chemical bonding, and
mass change. Antioxidant behavior differences were observed in the as-received formulations, but
differences went away with aging. No statistical differences between the two formulations were observed
in mechanical performance loss due to aging. These observations are consistent with the fact that the
decaBDE-containing XLPE and the decaBDE-free XLPE materials have different chemical compositions
and do not support the expectation of differences in performance of the safety-related functions of cable
constructed from the two formulations.

 • Antioxidants were effectively protecting the material from thermal degradation, but less

 4.2 NDE of Cable and Cable Insulation
4.2.1 Objective
 Reliable performance for electrical cables in NPPs is extremely important, not just for delivery of
generated power and communication with sensors and controls, but also for activation of safety systems
in the event of a plant emergency. With hundreds of miles of power, control, instrumentation, and other
cable types typically installed, replacing all the cables in an NPP would be a significant and likely cost-
prohibitive undertaking. Degradation of the cable jacket, electrical insulation, and other cable components
is a key issue that is likely to affect the ability of the currently installed cables to operate safely and
reliably in the period of extended operation to license periods of 60 and 80 years. The development of
NDE techniques and models that could assist in determining the current degradation state of cables to
inform repair and replace decisions would be of significant interest. The ability to determine material and
electrical properties of cable jackets and insulation without disturbing the cables or connections is
essential.
 The objectives of this task include the development and validation of new NDE technologies for
monitoring the condition of cable insulation. This task delivered an R&D plan in 2012 for sensor
development to monitor cable performance. An initial step in the R&D plan was to examine the key
issues and available technologies. Completed research since then included an assessment of key aging
indicators for tracking cable health through NDE techniques. Continued research involved the
development of a physics-based model for NDE signal response of compromised or degraded cables. This
includes included techniques for both global (long-length) cable NDE techniques, such as frequency-
domain reflectometry (FDR), and local techniques, such as interdigital capacitance (IDC) spectroscopy.
 Cable NDE has long been performed by (a) taking the cable out of service, (b) de-terminating one or
both ends of the cable for test, (c) performing one or more tests to indicate if the cable can continue to
perform its target function, (d) if the cable fails, either additional tests will be ordered, or the cable will be
repaired or replaced. (e) the cable ends must be re-terminated and the system returned to service. Modern
field programable array electronic systems can be made to withstand nuclear containment environments
and can perform several cable tests as online measurements.
4.2.2 Approach
 In FY25, the concept of online cable monitoring was extended by demonstrating a 7-channel
multiplexing field programable gate array (FPGA) instrument suitable for online reflectometry-based
cable monitoring. The approach leveraged PNNL experience with mm-wave airport security monitoring,
and the FY24 work on an inductive clamshell coupler to transfer test signals without damaging the
instrument. A Zilinx FPGA Radio Frequency System on Chip (RF SOC) development kit was
programmed to acquire and process the data for both FDR and spread spectrum time domain
reflectometry (SSTDR) of cable systems. The system architecture is shown in Figure 13 below. The

and signatures were compared to laboratory instrument cable tests. The development and test were

 4.3 Cross Pathway NDE Online Monitoring
4.3.1 Objective
 The objective of this task is to advance technologies for online monitoring of nuclear cable systems
by leveraging the cable NDE expertise and capabilities, including the Accelerated Real-Time
Environmental Nodal Assessment (ARENA) Cable Test Bed, at PNNL with the advanced data analysis
expertise at Idaho National Laboratory (INL). Recent research has demonstrated the potential for online
techniques such as SSTDR to provide information regarding the location and extent of cable degradation
due to a local adverse thermal environment while the cable is connected to and powering a motor.
Advanced indicators such as the local impedance change observable through reflectometry inform cable
condition when correlated with an understanding of material aging from offline and destructive

techniques such as tensile elongation at break. Successful use of continuous digital signals from online
NPP systems to assess and predict the health of aging systems in long term operation is anticipated to

powerful artificial intelligence tools guided by physical insight and operating experience to develop
strategies for safe and efficient operation of nuclear cable systems.
4.3.2 Approach
 Frequency domain and spread spectrum time domain reflectometry have been demonstrated to
effectively locate cable damage well before cable failure. The signals, however, are difficult to interpret.
This is an ideal application for machine learning (ML) to automate and assist analysts in interpreting test
signatures. ML is a broad analytical category.
 In FY 2023, unshielded, low voltage cable samples with a range of induced defects relevant to

set of over 500 test cases (with instrument parameters and induced cable anomalies - thermal aging, line-
to-line faults, and water exposure) using a laboratory SSTDR, a commercial SSTDR, and a laboratory
FDR was performed. Discriminative ML was used to distinguish cable anomalies associated with
potential degraded performance from non-destructive in situ cable monitoring using both supervised
unsupervised methods
 In FY 2024, a larger data set of over 2000 test cases (with instrument parameters and induced cable
anomalies - thermal aging, line-to-line faults, and water exposure) was generated using a laboratory
SSTDR, a commercial SSTDR, and a laboratory FDR to interrogate shielded low voltage cable in the
ARENA Test Bed. Tests included measurements on unenergized and energized cable plus testing using
the novel PNNL inductive clamshell coupler. The feasibility of ML for test methods applied to low
voltage cables was verified.
 The FY25 work extended the low-voltage work to medium voltage cable, example data is plotted in
Figure 14. As with the FY24 effort, two teams were tasked to analyze time and frequency domain
reflectometry to distinguish between good and bad cables. One team applied an unsupervised ML
approach. The other applied a supervised approach that included extending the training data from

when using training data from the earlier work (different voltage range plus shorter or longer cable

 5. Mitigating Strategies
 5.1 Weld Repair Techniques
5.1.1 Objective
 The primary objective of this research was to evaluate and advance welding methods for repairing
irradiated Ni-base Alloy 182 in nuclear reactor components [14]. With extended reactor service lifetimes,
materials in highly irradiated zones accumulate significant helium concentrations, which induce
intergranular cracking during welding. Aiming to address this challenge, the project focused on refining
welding parameters and understanding microstructural behavior to develop guidelines for repair
techniques that mitigate helium-induced cracking (HeIC), even in materials with helium levels as high as
20–30 appm. This initiative plays a critical role in ensuring the long-term operability and reliability of
nuclear power plants.
5.1.2 Approach
 The approach leveraged advanced welding technologies, including auxiliary beam stress improved
laser welding (ABSI-LW), which introduces localized compressive stresses to counteract cracking
mechanisms triggered by helium bubble formation. Multiple experimental campaigns were conducted,
utilizing irradiated Ni Alloy 182 samples with targeted helium concentrations from 10 to 30 appm. The
weld samples were prepared using varied heat inputs, welding techniques, and auxiliary stresses to isolate
and characterize conditions that minimize HeIC. Microstructural analyses, including optical imaging and
SEM, were performed to evaluate crack distribution, grain boundary degradation (GBD), and helium
entrapment in the weld zones. Reliability improvements were validated through statistical comparisons
across conventional and advanced welding techniques.
5.1.3 Accomplishments
 The accomplishments of this project are illustrated in Figure 15 and Figure 16. Refined laser welding
parameters, including reduced power during the first weld pass and supplemental autogenous passes,
successfully mitigated weld toe cracking in samples with 10 appm helium concentration, as observed in
prior campaigns. Figure 15 highlights this improved weld quality with reduced instances of HeIC,
although defects from insufficient shielding gas persisted. Additionally, SEM analyses, such as those
shown in Figure 16, revealed that ABSI-LW significantly reduces grain boundary degradation compared
to conventional methods. Samples welded with ABSI exhibited fewer microscopic cracks, with average
crack lengths decreasing from 10.2 μm to 7.1 μm, and GBD counts reduced by over 70%. These results
demonstrate the effectiveness of ABSI in enhancing weld integrity for irradiated materials containing
helium.

5.1.4 Impact on the nuclear industry
 The impact of this research on the nuclear industry is significant. By providing validated repair
techniques and refined welding protocols, the project addresses key challenges in maintaining and

 6. Integrated Research Activities
 6.1 Materials Harvesting Management and Coordination
6.1.1 Objective
 The objective of this study is to investigate material degradation mechanisms in reactor-grade
concrete and stainless steel welds from Ringhals Unit 2, a decommissioned nuclear reactor in Sweden
[15]. The project aims to characterize the properties of unirradiated concrete cores and harvested stainless
steel welds to establish baseline data and understand long-term material performance under operational
reactor conditions. This research provides critical insights that enhance predictive models for radiation
damage, improve safety margins, and inform strategies for the sustainable operation and eventual
decommissioning of nuclear power plants.
6.1.2 Approach
 The approach combines advanced experimental techniques and international collaboration to achieve
comprehensive material characterization. Unirradiated concrete cores extracted from the CBS of Ringhals
Unit 2 were analyzed using X-ray computed tomography (XCT), micro X-ray fluorescence (XRF),
petrographic analysis, X-ray diffraction (XRD), and powder XRF to determine chemical composition and
mineralogy. Core samples were prepared as slices and aggregates to establish baseline properties of
silicate-rich aggregates. Stainless steel welds, primarily composed of stainless steel 316, were harvested
from the surge line and relevant reactor piping systems. Collaborative studies among Oak Ridge National
Laboratory (ORNL), NRC, and other stakeholders were conducted to investigate microstructural changes,
thermal aging, and environmentally assisted fatigue in these welds.
6.1.3 Accomplishments
 Key accomplishments include the detailed characterization of two unirradiated concrete cores and the
successful initiation of harvesting of a stainless steel weld for degradation studies. Figure 17 shows the
unirradiated concrete cores analyzed using XCT, which revealed significant heterogeneities including
distinct aggregate-matrix boundaries and internal void distributions. Thin sections and extracted
aggregates were further studied using micro XRF and petrography. The analysis identified quartz- and
feldspar-dominant aggregates with inclusions of pyrite, garnet, and biotite, establishing valuable baseline
data for RIVE studies. In parallel, the SL1W stainless steel (see Figure 18) weld will be harvested for
long-term thermal and mechanical property testing. This weld represents a critical system component,
enabling studies of environmental fatigue, crack initiation, and thermal embrittlement. These efforts
contribute directly to advancing material testing methodologies and understanding reactor-material
interactions.

 6.2 Materials Degradation Assessment for Operation Beyond 80
 Years
6.2.1 Objective
 The objective of this project was to investigate and compile insights into the synergistic degradation
modes affecting reinforced nuclear concrete, specifically focusing on the combined effects of irradiation,
alkali-silica reaction (ASR), and corrosion [16]. The report aimed to update existing knowledge gaps
highlighted in the Expanded Materials Degradation Analysis (EMDA) from 2014 and to assess how these
modes operate in tandem under nuclear power plant conditions. With the long-term operation of LWRs
being critical for sustainable energy production, the study emphasizes the importance of understanding
the complex, coupled mechanisms involved in material aging to ensure the safety and durability of critical
structures such as the CBS and containment buildings.
6.2.2 Approach
 The approach involved an extensive review of global research findings, including experimental data
from laboratories, nuclear energy programs, and real-world observations from nuclear power plants. Key
degradation mechanisms such as irradiation-induced changes in concrete aggregates, irradiation-assisted
ASR, and irradiation-assisted corrosion (IAC) were analyzed independently and in combination. To
bridge the knowledge gaps, the study reviewed experimental techniques such as ion and gamma

 30

irradiation experiments, advanced microscopy, and spectroscopy to quantify chemical, structural, and
mechanical damage at multiple scales. It also identified the need for coupling structural and chemical
models to predict long-term performance under combined exposures of neutron flux, gamma doses, high
moisture, and elevated temperatures.
6.2.3 Accomplishments
 The study achieved significant advancements in understanding the degradation phenomena caused by
synergistic effects. One key accomplishment was distinguishing the interaction between irradiation-
induced aggregate dissolution and ASR. Experiments revealed enhanced dissolution rates of irradiated
mineral aggregates, such as quartz and albite, with the dissolution rate increasing by factors of 15 to 700
depending on mineral type. This phenomenon is critical because it demonstrates how radiation
significantly accelerates the initial chemical reactions that lead to ASR gel formation and subsequent
concrete damage. Additionally, Figure 19 highlights the strong correlation between irradiation-induced
density changes in aggregates and increased reactivity, quantifying how irradiation affects concrete’s
microstructural stability. Another key finding was elucidating the role of gamma irradiation on IAC. Data
showed that gamma rays accelerate corrosion rates in embedded steel, driven by carbonation and
radiolytic changes in the pore structure of the cement matrix, with rates increasing up to an order of
magnitude in some cases. The study also identified discrepancies in the understanding of radiolysis
effects under varying environmental conditions such as aerobic versus anaerobic corrosion environments,
calling for further research.

 7. Summary
 The FY2025 LWRS Program Materials Research Pathway Technical Program Plan is a testament to
the power of science and innovation in overcoming complex material degradation issues that challenge
the safety and operability of light water reactors (LWRs). Through targeted research and holistic
strategies, the program has addressed key areas such as reactor metals embrittlement, concrete
degradation under neutron flux, cable system aging, and helium-induced cracking in irradiated welds. The
findings demonstrate measurable progress in understanding the effects of irradiation, stress,
environmental factors, and aging mechanisms, thereby reducing uncertainties and enabling more accurate
predictions of reactor component behavior under prolonged operational conditions.
 Significant strides have been made in developing advanced non-destructive evaluation (NDE)
methods, facilitating real-time monitoring of cable systems and structural components without requiring
invasive sampling. The integration of intelligent machine learning models to interpret reflectometry
signals further enhances the capability to proactively identify degradation before critical failures occur.
Similarly, initiatives such as leveraging proton irradiation to rapidly assess the susceptibility of materials
to stress corrosion cracking have accelerated the development and validation of IASCC-resistant alloys.
These breakthroughs demonstrate the value of innovation and multidisciplinary collaboration in achieving
program goals, aligning advances in materials science with industry needs and regulatory expectations.
 Looking forward, the LWRS Program continues to provide critical data and methodologies that
directly contribute to extending the operational lifetimes of reactors safely and economically. By
addressing barriers to long-term operation—including aging infrastructure, regulatory compliance
concerns, and cost-effective maintenance—the program ensures that nuclear power remains a cornerstone
of the energy mix. Moreover, the lessons learned and protocols developed are paving the way for future
generations of reactors and advanced energy systems to operate under demanding conditions while
delivering sustainable energy to meet growing domestic demand. In conclusion, the LWRS Program is
not only securing the present and future of nuclear energy but also contributing to broader goals of carbon
reduction, energy security, and innovation in sustainable technology.
