Executive Summary
The Mobility Energy Productivity (MEP) metric, developed by the National Renewable Energy
Laboratory, offers a comprehensive performance measure for transportation systems. It
integrates time, cost, and energy-related metrics to assess accessibility and mobility. The
practical application of the MEP metric has been demonstrated in various case studies, such as
evaluating the introduction of new transit lines, the adoption of energy-efficient transportation
modes like autonomous vehicles and micromobility, and improvements in fuel efficiency across
different transportation modes. These case studies provide valuable insights into the impacts of
these changes on mobility. This study illustrates how the MEP metric has been integrated with
the Delaware Department of Transportation’s statewide travel demand model data to support
transportation project evaluations.

In this study, the MEP tool was used to assess the mobility and accessibility provided by driving,
transit, biking, and walking in Delaware, both under baseline conditions and across various
alternative scenarios. The baseline MEP calculation for 2020 shows that driving dominates
accessibility and mobility in the state, as expected, while transit offers relatively lower
accessibility—often less than biking and walking in many areas. A county-level analysis reveals
that transit contributes a larger share of accessibility in New Castle County compared to Kent
and Sussex counties. This finding suggests a need for improvements in transit service and
coverage across the state to boost transit mobility and accessibility, especially in these regions.
Several alternative scenarios, including the 2030, Churchman, and Old Orchard scenarios, were
evaluated to understand the effects of various changes on mobility and accessibility in the region.

In the 2030 scenario, which assessed the impact of changes in network speeds, opportunities, and
job availability, the statewide MEP score decreased by 3.28% compared to baseline conditions.
New Castle County experienced the most significant drop, while Kent and Sussex counties saw
an increase in MEP scores. This suggests that, without infrastructure or service improvements,
mobility and accessibility in the state are likely to decline by 2030. The Churchman and Old
Orchard scenarios focused on localized network changes. The Churchman scenario included the
addition of a new network link and a transit service line, while the Old Orchard scenario focused
on speed improvements on certain links. These changes resulted in localized MEP score
improvements of 1.89% and 0.028%, respectively. Given the modest nature of these proposed
changes, the increases in MEP scores align with expectations, further demonstrating the MEP
tool’s capacity to capture localized changes and its potential for evaluating the accessibility
benefits of local infrastructure projects.

In conclusion, the MEP tool provides a robust framework for assessing the complex interactions
between mobility, energy consumption, and productivity within transportation systems. It
supports evidence-based decision-making and empowers stakeholders to develop better
transportation infrastructure. The findings from this project highlight the effectiveness of MEP in
evaluating the impact of various transportation projects across Delaware.

1 Introduction
Transportation agencies and urban planners continuously seek ways to enhance mobility, reduce
congestion, and improve access to opportunities. Understanding how infrastructure investments
and policy changes influence mobility is crucial for developing efficient transportation networks.
Traditionally, mobility assessments have focused on travel time and cost; however, growing
concerns over greenhouse gas emissions highlight the need to incorporate energy efficiency into
these evaluations. To address this, the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL)
developed the Mobility Energy Productivity (MEP) metric—an innovative tool designed to
quantify and evaluate mobility and accessibility by considering travel cost, time, and energy
consumption across different modes. Additionally, MEP can be used to assess the potential
impacts of future transportation scenarios.

This study applies the MEP metric to Delaware’s transportation network, focusing on evaluating
energy-efficient mobility and accessibility while integrating these results into the Delaware
Department of Transportation’s (DelDOT’s) travel demand modeling (TDM) efforts. Baseline
accessibility scores are first calculated for different travel modes across the state, with
distinctions at the county level. The impacts of several scenarios are then assessed:

 1. 2030 scenario: Incorporates network and land use changes from DelDOT’s travel
 demand models.
 2. Churchman scenario: Involves a local road extension and the addition of a transit line.
 3. Old Orchard scenario: Includes link-level infrastructure improvements.
This study demonstrates how the MEP metric can be leveraged to evaluate the impacts of various
transportation enhancements and support DelDOT’s TDM efforts in advancing mobility and
accessibility across Delaware.

2 MEP Methodology
2.1 Overview
MEP quantifies a region’s effectiveness in providing access to opportunities, evaluating how
well a location is connected while considering the energy, cost, and time required to reach jobs
and opportunities such as food, health care, education, recreation, and retail. A location with a
MEP score of zero signifies no access—either due to the absence of a transportation network or
because no opportunities can be reached within a reasonable travel time (typically up to 40
minutes). The MEP score for a location represents the total access provided by all transportation
modes to all opportunity types. This score can be further broken down to show access by a
specific mode to all opportunities, by all modes to a particular type of opportunity, or by a
specific mode to a specific type of opportunity. These breakdowns are illustrated in Figure 1 for
a sample region. A lower MEP score for one mode compared to another indicates that it provides
less access to opportunities and jobs when accounting for energy, time, and cost. Likewise, a
lower MEP score for a specific type of opportunity (e.g., medical facilities) compared to another
(e.g., jobs) for a given mode suggests lower accessibility to that opportunity type.

 Figure 1. Breakdown of MEP scores by mode and activity for a sample region

2.2 Methods and Calculation
A fundamental step in calculating a MEP score is generating isochrones for each location within
a study region. An isochrone—similar to a watershed—is a polygon that connects the farthest
points a traveler can reach from a given origin for a specific mode within a defined travel time.
Constructing an isochrone requires data on travel patterns across the transportation network. For
driving, biking, and walking, a road network dataset is essential, ideally with average travel time
or speed data for each link. For transit, route information, stops, and schedules are necessary to
generate isochrones accurately. This ensures a realistic assessment of accessibility. By default,
MEP generates isochrones for four travel time thresholds: 10, 20, 30, and 40 minutes. These
thresholds can be easily adjusted to meet specific analysis needs. For each location, mode, and

 2

travel time threshold, MEP applies Dijkstra’s algorithm to construct a minimum spanning tree of
accessible locations. Figure 2 illustrates 20-minute walking and 10-minute biking isochrones for
a traveler starting at a location in Wilmington, Delaware. Land use data, including points of
interest and buildings, are sourced from OpenStreetMap (OSM) (OpenStreetMap 2025).

Figure 2. An example of a 20-minute walking isochrone (green) and a 10-minute biking isochrone
 (blue) originating from a location in Wilmington, Delaware
Isochrones are then intersected with land use and employment data to assess the accessibility of
various opportunities within a region. In Figure 2, the opportunity space is represented using
point-of-interest and building data from OSM. Ideally, the land use layer should capture a
comprehensive range of opportunities, including health care, food, retail, education, and
recreation—categories that serve as the default in MEP but can be customized as needed. The
total number of opportunities within each isochrone is aggregated and normalized. The flexibility
of these categories depends on the detail available in the land use data, but they must align with
regional trip purpose data for either the general population or specific traveler groups.

MEP accounts for the varying frequencies of different trip purposes. For example, while access
to health care is critical, visits to medical facilities are far less frequent than trips to grocery
stores or restaurants. Normalizing trip frequencies prevents more common activities, such as
food-related trips, from disproportionately influencing MEP scores. Trip purpose data are
typically sourced from travel surveys, such as the National Household Travel Survey (NHTS) or

 3

regional studies. In this study, trip frequencies were based on data from the NHTS (Federal
Highway Administration 2017).

The final step in calculating MEP involves adjusting the total accessible opportunities based on
the energy, cost, and time required to reach them. This process begins by determining the
average energy consumption and cost per passenger mile traveled (PMT). For a standard MEP
calculation, these values are obtained from the Transportation Energy Data Book (Davis and
Boundy 2021). Specifically, driving and transit energy consumption are estimated at 0.9
kWh/PMT and 0.65 kWh/PMT, respectively (Davis and Boundy 2021). The corresponding costs
are assumed to be $0.48/PMT for driving and $0.86/PMT for transit (AAA 2023; Federal Transit
Administration 2016). Biking and walking are considered to have no energy or monetary cost.
These mode-specific factors are incorporated into the MEP calculation to adjust opportunity
accessibility based on the associated energy use, time investment, and financial cost. The MEP

3.1.1 Baseline Scenario
The baseline scenario represents the existing level of access to opportunities in the study region,
using data from the year 2020. MEP scores were calculated for four travel modes: drive, transit,
bike, and walk. These scores were assessed for the entire state and also disaggregated by the
three counties within the state. The transit MEP calculation in the baseline scenario considered
service provided by five existing transit agencies in the region: (1) inter-county bus, (2) Kent-
Sussex bus, (3) Newcastle bus, (4) shuttle bus, and (5) rail transit. The results from this baseline
scenario serve as a reference point for evaluating the impacts of alternative scenarios (discussed
next).

3.1.2 2030 Scenario
While the baseline scenario represents mobility and accessibility conditions in 2020, the 2030
scenario was designed to reflect changes in network infrastructure, travel patterns, and land use.
The updated inputs were derived from DelDOT’s travel demand model projections for 2030. In
this scenario, network speeds were adjusted to reflect projected travel conditions in 2030. Land
use and job availability were also updated based on DelDOT’s forecasts. Transit operations were
modified to reflect the expected changes from these network updates, though the number of
transit agencies and their routes/stops remained the same. However, the population was held
constant between the baseline (2020) and 2030 scenarios.

3.1.3 Churchman Scenario
The Churchman scenario builds upon the baseline scenario with two key modifications, as
illustrated in Figure 3. First, a new road link was added to complete Delaware Park Drive, which
was previously discontinuous. This new link connects Delaware Park Kirkwood Lot to an
intersection at Delaware Park Drive and Delaware Park Boulevard, highlighted in orange at the

 6

top right of Figure 3. The link is accessible to all travel modes, including driving, transit, biking,
and walking.

 Figure 3. Churchman scenario with added road link and a transit line
Second, a new transit service, called the Churchman transit line, was introduced in this scenario.
The route and stops are depicted in Figure 3, where blue polygons represent transit routes and
red dots indicate proposed stops. The transit line operates at an average speed of 19.6 mph,
including stops (the average speed of existing bus lines in Delaware). It has a 30-minute
headway and runs in both clockwise and counterclockwise directions. The service operates daily
from 6 a.m. to 11 p.m. in both directions. The improvements in MEP scores from this scenario
were analyzed at both the statewide level and locally, given that the changes primarily affected a
specific area.

3.1.4 Old Orchard Scenario
The Old Orchard scenario incorporates localized network and infrastructure improvements.
Some road links were improved by increasing their speeds by 5 mph, while a slight realignment
of a link was made to conform to DelDOT’s intersection improvement plans. Figure 4 illustrates
these modifications, where blue links indicate speed improvements and yellow and green links

evaluated both at the statewide level and within the affected local area.

3.2 Assembling Data
The DelDOT team provided multiple datasets—primarily derived from their travel demand
model inputs, projections, and outputs—to the NREL team for this project. Additionally,
assumptions for both the baseline and alternative scenarios were finalized through discussions
between the NREL and DelDOT teams.

3.2.1 Network Data
DelDOT provided network data for both the 2020 (baseline) and 2030 scenarios. The Churchman
and Old Orchard scenarios were based on the 2020 and 2030 network, respectively, with
modifications as discussed in Section 3.1. The overall link representation remained consistent
between 2020 and 2030, covering 266,402 links and 21,291 miles within the state boundary.
However, speed variations were observed, with many links in 2030 having lower speeds than in
2020, likely reflecting increased congestion in DelDOT’s travel demand model projections. A
snapshot of the network is shown in Figure 5.

3.2.2 Transit Data
Transit MEP calculation relies on a standard data format for public transportation schedules and
associated geographic information called the GTFS (MobilityData 2025). A GTFS feed for a
transit service consists of several .txt files describing the schedule, routes, stops, and other
necessary information about the transit service. DelDOT provided data for routes, stops, links,
and schedules from their travel demand models for all five transit lines in the state: inter-county
bus, Kent-Sussex bus, Newcastle bus, shuttle bus, and rail transit. These DelDOT-provided
datasets were processed alongside the statewide network data (discussed above) to generate
standard GTFS files. As a result, five unique GTFS files—each corresponding to a transit
service—were generated for the baseline (2020) scenario.

For the 2030 scenario, the network layer for the year 2030 was used, while other transit data
(e.g., routes, stops, schedules) remained the same. This resulted in another set of five GTFS files
for the 2030 scenario. For the Churchman scenario, where a new transit service is proposed, a
new GTFS file was generated. Thus, transit MEP in the Churchman scenario consisted of five
existing transit lines plus the newly proposed Churchman transit line. For the Old Orchard
scenario, which does not include any changes to transit services, the existing transit line GTFS
files were used but updated for the year 2030, as the scenario is designed for that year.

3.2.3 Opportunity and Employment Data
Opportunity and employment data were obtained from the DelDOT statewide TDM for 2020
(baseline) and 2030. The data were available at the geographic resolution of transportation
analysis zones. MEP calculations consider five opportunity types—school/daycare/religious,
shopping/errands, medical, meals, and recreational—along with jobs. The DelDOT-provided
data were mapped to MEP opportunity categories for MEP calculation.

Additionally, MEP allows for considering either the raw number of opportunities (e.g., one
hospital) or the number of jobs associated with that opportunity (e.g., 100 jobs for that hospital).
Depending on whether the raw opportunity count or associated jobs is used, the application of
the relational vector in MEP calculation varies, as discussed later. However, because the
DelDOT-provided data included the total number of jobs in each opportunity category, the MEP
calculations considered the count of jobs in each opportunity type as well as total jobs. The count
of jobs in each opportunity type and total jobs in both the baseline (2020) and 2030 scenarios are
presented in Figure 7.

To maintain consistency with the network data used in the Churchman and Old Orchard
scenarios, these scenarios respectively used the land use data for the years 2020 and 2030.

3.2.4 Other Inputs
Population: The MEP scores, calculated at the square-kilometer pixel level, are aggregated
using the pixel-level population to obtain regionwide MEP scores—state level or county level in
this case. For this purpose, statewide population data were sourced from the U.S. Census Bureau
(2025) and apportioned to the square-kilometer pixels.

Cost: As discussed in Section 2.2, MEP calculation requires mode-specific cost inputs. For this
purpose, the default cost values presented in the MEP methodology paper (Hou et al. 2019),
sourced from different references, were used. Drive and transit costs were assumed to be $0.48
and $0.86 per PMT (AAA 2023; Federal Transit Administration 2015), respectively, while bike
and walk modes were assumed to have zero energy use and cost.

Energy intensity: Similarly, as MEP is an energy-accounted accessibility score, mode-specific
energy intensities are required for its calculation. As discussed in Hou et al. (2019), drive and
transit energy consumption were assumed to be 0.9 and 0.65 kWh/PMT (Davis and Boundy
2021), respectively, with zero energy consumption for bike and walk modes.

Activity engagement frequency: To account for the varying frequencies of visiting different
types of opportunities (e.g., visiting a hospital is less common than visiting a grocery store),
MEP adjusts for the frequency of trips to different purposes. Engagement frequencies were
sourced from the NHTS (Federal Highway Administration 2017), which shows the engagement
frequencies for school/daycare, medical, shopping/errands, recreational, meals, and jobs as 3.1%,
1.5%, 19%, 8.4%, 6.7%, and 16.6%, respectively. Because DelDOT provided land use data
consisting of the number of jobs belonging to each opportunity type, instead of raw opportunity
counts, these frequencies were adjusted according to the number of jobs associated with each
opportunity type.

 11

Activity relational vector: MEP calculation also accounts for the differences in availability
between different types of opportunities and jobs. For example, the number of jobs in a region is
generally much higher than the number of medical facilities or grocery stores. These differences
are accounted for using the activity relational vector, which adjusts for the unequal availability of
different opportunity types. However, because this analysis uses the number of jobs for each
opportunity type instead of raw opportunity counts, the activity relational vector is set to 1 for
each opportunity type.

3.3.1 Baseline Scenario

4 Dockerization of MEP
Deploying the MEP metric tool in a scalable and efficient way is essential for transportation
agencies seeking data-driven insights into mobility, energy, and accessibility. To facilitate
seamless integration and operational efficiency, the NREL team created a containerized version
of the MEP tool for easy deployment, reproducibility, and cross-platform compatibility. This
approach ensured that the MEP tool can be leveraged without concerns about software
dependencies, system variations, or installation complexities. By encapsulating the tool within
Docker containers, DelDOT was able to streamline its analytical workflows and enable on-
premises deployments to support scenario evaluation and transportation planning efforts. The
DelDOT MEP Docker container facilitates robust analysis of bike, walk, drive, and transit modes
by leveraging geospatial data, land use parameters, and transit schedules within a
PostgreSQL/PostGIS database. This document provides an overview of:

 • The Docker setup
 • Data requirements
 • Step-by-step instructions to execute MEP calculations.
These instructions are designed to help DelDOT analysts evaluate mobility performance and
support informed transportation planning decisions.

4.1 Shared Folder Structure
The dataset and scripts required for these analyses via Docker are shared with the DelDOT team
through a Box shared folder. The folder includes the following five components:

 • 1_Raw Dataset: Contains raw files provided by DelDOT, including network, land use,
 and scenario data.
 • 2_Input_Processing Code: Includes scripts used to convert the raw datasets into a format
 compatible with the MEP tool. This includes processing network data, land use files, and
 GTFS transit feeds.
 • 3_Processed Dataset: Houses the cleaned and processed datasets that are ready for MEP
 analysis via Docker.
 • 4_Docker Container: The folder structure consists of several key directories and files:
 o Data: The location to read/write data used in MEP calculations (e.g., networks,
 land use data). Some preprocessed data are provided here to help start testing the
 MEP Docker container.
 ̶ data/config.yml: This is the configuration file where you will set all the
 parameters required for a MEP run, with more details provided later.
 ̶ data/db_connect.R: This script demonstrates how to connect and
 read/write data to the database created by our Docker container.
 ̶ data/compute_mep_local.R: This script is an option to compute the final
 step of MEP locally if you only need to change parameters in the
 config.yml and not other data.
 ̶ data/local_functions.R: This script is a collection of utility functions
 provided for functions in data/db_connect.R and
 data/compute_mep_local.R.

 24

 o Scripts: The files in the scripts folder support various MEP processing steps.
 Below is a brief description of each:
 ̶ compute_isochrone.py: Generates isochrones for accessibility analysis.
 ̶ configuration.py: Allows users to set parameters or make scenario-specific
 modifications.
 ̶ generate_bbox_for_city_delaware.py: Creates bounding box coordinates
 for the study region in Delaware.
 ̶ mep_utils2.R and mep2.R: R scripts used to compute MEP scores.
 ̶ recount_opp.py: Counts opportunities (e.g., jobs, services) within
 generated isochrones.
 ̶ upload_custom_network.py: Uploads a routable transportation network
 and its attributes to the database.
 While it may be technically possible to run some of these scripts outside the container,
 doing so is generally not recommended, as the container ensures a controlled and
 reproducible environment for all dependencies.

 o gtfs: The location of transit data for transit MEP runs (includes <gtfs_name>.zip
 and <city_name>.osm.pbf files). In the current folder, the NREL team has
 included “gtfs-deldot-transit-mep20.zip” and “delaware-latest-filtered.osm.pbf”
 files that are to be used for transit runs.
 o setup-db.sql: This file specifies how to create a database needed for MEP runs.
 This file is included in the main zip folder.
 o install_R_dependencies.R and requirements.txt: These are the R and Python

4.3 Configuration (config.yml) File Information
The config.yml file allows users to update default assumptions, such as MEP parameters and
county boundaries. It includes parameters such as:

 • Scenario: A unique name (string) for a MEP run. This must meet the requirements of
 a valid PostGres Schema name, such as using underscores in place of spaces, and
 beginning with a letter (e.g., my_test_scenario123). Each MEP scenario can have one
 mode of each type (drive, bike, walk, and transit). You should create a different scenario
 for each set of changing assumptions in the network, land use, employment, or transit
 schedules.
 • Modes: A vector of the travel mode names you would like to run for this scenario, such
 as ["drive","bike","walk","transit"]. You can change this depending on the mode you are
 running the analysis for.
 • Degrees: Resolution of grid cells for MEP in degrees latitude/longitude. We recommend
 this to be kept at 0.01, which is approximately 1 km2. For instance, if the users want to
 change the grid cell to a 0.5-km2 pixel, use 0.00707 as the value, as shown in Table 4.
 However, note that smaller values increase computing time.
 Table 4. Approximate Degree Value To Create Different Pixel Sizes

 Approximate Degree Value MEP Pixel Size (km2)
 0.01 1
                   0.00707                               0.5
 0.005 0.25
                   0.00316                               0.1

 • db_*: Database credentials. Keep "mep" for db_name and db_user. We recommend
 choosing your own password for db_pass, which you will also need to specify when
 initializing the Docker container for the first time. You may need to update the port
 number (e.g., 5433) if your host computer has PostgreSQL or another service utilizing the
 default port of 5432. db_host, db_name, db_user, and db_driv remain unchanged.
 • mode_energy: The energy use by mode (kWh/PMT). This vector must match the order
 and length of modes (e.g., if drive is first in modes, the drive energy use must be first
 in mode_energy). If the energy of a mode is zero, enter 0.0 to ensure all inputs are
 numeric. These parameters can be changed depending on the user’s practical desire of the
 use of kWh/PMT by mode.
 • mode_cost: The cost by mode (USD/PMT). This vector must match the order and length
 of modes (e.g., if drive is first in modes, the drive cost must be first in mode_cost). If the

 26

 cost of a mode is zero, enter 0.0 to ensure all inputs are numeric. These parameters can be
 changed depending on the user’s practical desire of the use of USD/PMT by mode.
 • city_bound_path: The filepath for a boundary shapefile to be used for MEP analysis. For
 example, if a user plans to perform analysis at the county level, a county-level shapefile
 can be provided as input.
 • Activities: The activity categories considered in MEP. It is recommended to keep this as
 is.
 • N_star: The baseline total opportunities for reference category (meals by default) in the
 opportunity space. If you want to localize the opportunity normalization, pick a reference
 category and sum all available opportunities of that type and use here.
 • N_j: The total opportunities for all categories. This vector must match the order and
 length of activities. If you want to localize the opportunity normalization, sum all
 available opportunities by type and use here (this includes the reference category).
 • freq: A vector for the frequency of engagements by opportunity type. This vector must
 match the order and length of activities. By default, this is derived from NHTS (e.g., 3.1
 means 3.1% of trips were by said activity type). Exclude trips that do not include the
 types of included MEP activities. This vector does not need to sum to 100.
 • otp_*: These are parameters for OpenTripPlanner settings used to calculate transit MEP.
 These are recommended to be left as is.

5 Conclusion
The MEP metric is a valuable tool for assessing the quality of accessibility and mobility over
time and space, particularly in understanding how energy-efficient access is influenced by
technological advancements, transportation policies, infrastructure investments, and modal shifts.
This project aimed to demonstrate how the MEP metric can leverage data from DelDOT’s TDM
to evaluate the impacts of various transportation projects and scenarios across Delaware. The
findings highlight MEP’s potential to support future project evaluations and decision-making by
incorporating energy-efficient accessibility into transportation planning.

Using the MEP tool, this project assessed baseline MEP scores for Delaware across different
travel modes, further disaggregated by county. The results indicate that the drive mode has the
highest accessibility score both statewide and at the county level. Bicycling offers higher
accessibility than transit across the state and within counties, while walking surpasses transit in
Kent and Sussex counties. Overall, these findings highlight that mobility and accessibility in
Delaware are predominantly supported by the drive mode, while transit services are relatively
limited—even ranking below biking and walking in many areas.

Additionally, the project analyzed alternative scenarios to assess potential accessibility and
mobility changes driven by several projections or infrastructure improvements in the state. In the
2030 scenario, the analysis considered the impact of transportation performance changes
(notably congestion) alongside growth in businesses, opportunities, and jobs. The results indicate
an overall accessibility score decline of 3.28%, though Kent and Sussex counties experienced
increases, largely due to a significant drop in MEP scores in New Castle County. In the
Churchman and Old Orchard scenarios, the project examined the effects of local network
improvements on MEP scores. The results show that such improvements positively impact
accessibility in the affected regions, demonstrating MEP’s capability to capture localized effects
and reinforcing its utility in project evaluations.

With the dockerization of the MEP code and extensive support from the NREL team, the
DelDOT team successfully tested the MEP tool. This enabled DelDOT to run MEP for both
baseline and alternative scenarios, extending beyond the analyses presented in this report. The
successful transfer of MEP operational knowledge ensures that DelDOT can now independently
run the tool to estimate MEP for any scenario of interest, strengthening its integration into their
TDM efforts and project evaluations.

One of the major challenges encountered in this project was developing a robust method to
account for biking and walking accessibility in the absence of detailed TDM for these modes. To
address this, the project utilized DelDOT’s TDM network data with certain assumptions
regarding mode eligibility, biking/walking speeds, and other parameters, based on discussions
between DelDOT and the NREL team. Additionally, all scenarios maintained a constant
population due to the unavailability of population projections for 2030, which may have
influenced the statewide and county-level MEP scores. Because pixel-level MEP scores are
weighted by population to derive regional MEP scores, this assumption may affect the accuracy
of long-term projections. Further, the lack of reliable public GTFS feeds for transit services in
the state necessitated processing GTFS data using DelDOT’s TDM results, which introduced
additional assumptions. Despite these challenges, the study underscores the potential of MEP as

 37

a critical tool for evaluating transportation projects, and ensuring energy-efficient accessibility
remains a key consideration in future planning efforts.
