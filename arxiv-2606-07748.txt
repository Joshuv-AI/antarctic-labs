Introduction

This note focuses on a new digraph parameter, called partial order decomposition number.
Recall that a
partial order

\leq
on a set
S
is a transitive, reflexive, and anti-symmetric relation. We will abuse terminology and say that a digraph
P=(V,A)
which is acyclic, and furthermore satisfies that when
(u,v)\in A(P)
and
(v,w)\in A(P)
then
(u,w)\in A(P)
, is a
partial order
. Thus, we are interpreting partial orders as transitive acyclic digraphs. Such digraphs are also called
comparability digraphs
.

Let
D
be a digraph. A
partial order decomposition
of
D
is a collection
P_{1},\ldots,P_{k}
of partial orders on
V(D)
such that

A(D)=A(P_{1})\cup\cdots\cup A(P_{k})
.
The
partial order decomposition number
of a digraph
D
, denoted by
pod(D)
, is the minimum integer
k
such that
D
admits a partial order decomposition with
k
parts.

The interest of the parameter
pod
lies in its relation to the dichromatic number and the clique number of digraphs.

Before defining these two notions, let us recall their classic undirected counterpart.
Given an undirected graph
G
, we denote by
\omega(G)
the size of a maximum clique of
G
. A
k
-colouring
is a map
f:V(G)\to\{1,\ldots,k\}
such that for all
xy\in E(G)
, we have
f(x)\neq f(y)
. The
chromatic number
of
G
, denoted by
\chi(G)
, is the minimum
k
such that
G
is
k
-colourable (i.e. admits a
k
-colouring).

Digraphs in this paper have no parallel edges or loops. The existence of an arc from
a
to
b
in a digraph
D
is denoted
a\to_{D}b
. We drop the subscript when the digraph is clear from context.
For a digraph
D
, a
k
-dicolouring
is a map
f:V(D)\to C
such that
|C|=k
for all
i\in C
, the vertices coloured
i
induce an acyclic digraph. The
dichromatic number
of
D
, denoted
\vec{\chi}(D)
, is the minimum
k
such that
D
has a
k
-dicolouring. It was first introduced by Neumann-Lara in
[
14
]
.

Extending the notion of clique number to digraphs is slightly more cumbersome, as it requires introducing backedge graphs. Given a digraph
D
and a total ordering
<
on
V(D)
, the
backedge graph of
D
with respect to
<
, denoted
B(D,<)
(or simply
B
when
D
and
<
are clear from context), is the undirected graph with
V(B)=V(D)
and edge set
E(B)=\{vw:v<w,\ w\to_{D}v\}
. We say that a graph
G
is a backedge graph of a digraph
D
if there is an ordering
<_{G}
of
V(D)
such that
B(D,<_{G})
is isomorphic to
G
. Note that
B(D,<)
comes naturally equipped with the ordering
<
, and we will often use it as the ordered graph
(B(D,<),<)
.

The following observation shows that the dichromatic number of a digraph is completely determined by the backedge graphs of the digraph.
It is straightforward that every independent set of
B(D,<)
induces an acyclic subdigraph of
D
. As a consequence, we have that
\vec{\chi}(D)\leqslant\chi(B(D,<))
.
Conversely, by taking an ordering built from a
\vec{\chi}(D)
-dicolouring,
that is taking colour classes one after the other, and ordering each colour class in a topological ordering, we get that:

\vec{\chi}(D)=\min\{\chi(B(D,<)):\,<\text{ is a total ordering of }V(D)\}.

This naturally leads to the following notion of clique number for a digraph, introduced in
[
1
]
.
The
clique number of a digraph
D
, denoted
\vec{\omega}(D)
, is the minimum clique number of any backedge graph of
D
. That is,

\vec{\omega}(D)=\min\{\omega(B(D,<)):\,<\text{ is a total ordering of }V(D)\}

A total ordering
<
of
V(D)
is an
\vec{\omega}
-ordering
of
D
if
\omega(B(D,<))=\vec{\omega}(D)
.

More generally, see Section 3 of
[
2
]
for a discussion of digraph parameters defined from graph parameters via backedge graphs.

The following theorem is the main reason why the parameter
pod
is useful in our setting.

Theorem 1
.

For every digraph
D
, we have
\vec{\chi}(D)\leqslant\vec{\omega}(D)^{pod(D)}
.

A natural application of the above inequality is to
\vec{\chi}
-bounded classes of digraphs. Let us first recall the corresponding undirected notion.
It is well known that there exist graphs
G
with
\omega(G)=2
and
\chi(G)
arbitrarily large. Thus, it is natural to study graph classes for which chromatic number is controlled by clique number.
More specifically, the following question has been widely studied: for which graph classes
\mathcal{C}
does there exist a function
f
such that for all
G\in\mathcal{C}
, we have
\chi(G)\leqslant f(\omega(G))
? Such graph classes are called
\chi
-bounded
(see
[
16
]
for a survey on this topic). In the case where
f
is a polynomial, they are called
polynomially
\chi
-bounded
, and there exist
\chi
-bounded classes which are not polynomially
\chi
-bounded
[
6
]
.

This notion extends naturally to digraphs, with
\vec{\chi}
and
\vec{\omega}
playing the roles of
\chi
and
\omega
. Let
\mathcal{C}
be a class of digraphs. We say
\mathcal{C}
is
\vec{\chi}
-bounded
if there exists a function
f
such that for every digraph
D\in\mathcal{C}
, we have
\vec{\chi}(D)\leq f(\vec{\omega}(D))
.
As before, if
f
can be taken to be a polynomial, then we say that
\mathcal{C}
is
polynomially
\vec{\chi}
-bounded.

As an immediate corollary of Theorem
1
, we get that digraph classes with bounded
pod
are polynomially
\vec{\chi}
-bounded.

Corollary 2
.

Let
m
be a positive integer, and let
\mathcal{C}
be a class of digraphs such that

pod(D)\leqslant m
for every
D\in\mathcal{C}
. Then
\mathcal{C}
is polynomially
\vec{\chi}
-bounded.
More precisely, for every
D\in\mathcal{C}
, we have
\vec{\chi}(D)\leqslant\vec{\omega}(D)^{m}
.

We use this to make progress on a conjecture of Aubian, Charbit, Lopes and the first author
[
1
]
. The notion of substitution is standard, and will be recalled in Section
3
.
Given a class of digraphs
\mathcal{C}
we define
\mathcal{C}^{subst}
to be the
closure of
\mathcal{C}
under substitution
.
A
tournament
is an orientation of a complete graph.

Conjecture 3

(
[
1
]
)
.

If a class of tournaments
\mathcal{C}

is polynomially
\vec{\chi}
-bounded, then so is
\mathcal{C}^{subst}
.

This conjecture is motivated from the graph version, which says that the graph analogy for substitution preserves polynomial
\chi
-boundness
[
8
]
.
We prove Conjecture
3
for the class of tournaments which have bounded dichromatic number:

Theorem 4
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a class of tournaments such that each tournament in
\mathcal{C}
has dichromatic number at most
k
. Then for each
T\in\mathcal{C}^{subst}
, we have
\vec{\chi}(T)\leq(\vec{\omega}(T))^{2\lceil\log_{2}(k)\rceil+1}
. In particular, the class
\mathcal{C}^{subst}
is polynomially
\vec{\chi}
-bounded.

It is natural to ask whether bounded
pod
characterizes polynomially
\vec{\chi}
-bounded classes of tournaments.
We exhibit a class of tournaments with unbounded
pod
, namely crossing tournaments. In a follow-up paper, some of the authors of this note show that this class is nevertheless polynomially
\vec{\chi}
-bounded, thereby showing that bounded
pod
does not characterize polynomially
\vec{\chi}
-bounded classes of tournaments.

We also prove that a subclass of poset tournaments (defined in Section
5
), namely the class of poset tournaments with bounded dimension is
\vec{\chi}
-bounded.

We then show how to derive lower bounds on the clique number of tournaments from the inequality
\vec{\chi}\leq\vec{\omega}^{pod}
. We illustrate this method by showing that a particular family of tournaments, denoted
(B_{k})_{k\geq 0}
and introduced in
Section

6
, has clique number at least polynomial in
|V(B_{k})|
. This leads to a positive answer to the following conjecture.

Conjecture 5

(Gutowski and Rams,
[
10
]
)
.

There exists an explicit family of tournaments
(T_{n})_{n\geq 1}
such that
|V(T_{n})|=\Theta(n)
and
\vec{\omega}(T_{n})=\Omega(n^{1/100})

The structure of the paper is as follows. We prove the inequality
\vec{\chi}\leq\vec{\omega}^{pod}
in Section
2
. Corollary
2
and Theorem
4
are proved in Section
3
, and the result on crossing tournaments and poset tournaments are proved respectively in Section
4
and Section
5
, and the lower bounds on the clique number of tournaments are discussed in Section
6
. Finally, we end the paper with Section
7
, which contains two additional short results on
pod
and a few open questions on tournaments and
pod
. The two results show that acyclic digraphs can have arbitrarily large
pod
, and that tournaments with bounded
pod
have bounded domination number.

2
Proof that
\vec{\chi}\leq\vec{\omega}^{\,pod}

A graph
G
is a
comparability graph
if it admits a transitive orientation.
The next lemma follows from the well-known fact that the intersection of two partial orders is a partial order. We include a proof for completeness:

Lemma 6
.

Let
D
be a partial order digraph. Then every backedge graph of
D
is a comparability graph.

Proof.

Let
<
be a total order on
V(D)
, and let
B
be the corresponding backedge graph.
We orient each edge of
B
as in
D
, that is, if
x<y
and
xy\in E(B)
, we orient the edge
xy
of
B
as
y\to x
.
We claim that this is a transitive orientation of
B
.
Indeed, suppose that
a\to b
and
b\to c
are oriented edges of
B
.
Since the orientation of
B
is inherited from
D
, we have
a\to b
and
b\to c
in
D
.
Moreover, because these edges belong to the backedge graph, both arcs are backward with respect to
<
, so
c<b<a
.
Since
D
is transitive, it follows that
a\to c
in
D
.
As
c<a
, we have that
ac\in E(B)
, and is oriented as
a\to c
.
Thus the chosen orientation of
B
is transitive and
B
is a comparability graph.
∎

This is useful because comparability graphs are perfect.

Theorem 7

(
[
4
]
)
.

Comparability graphs are perfect: if
G
is a comparability graph, then every induced subgraph
H
of
G
satisfies
\chi(H)=\omega(H)
.

We are now ready to prove the main result on
pod
, that is the equation linking it with
\vec{\chi}
and
\vec{\omega}
.

Theorem
1
.

For every digraph
D
, we have
\vec{\chi}(D)\leqslant\vec{\omega}(D)^{pod(D)}
.

Proof.

Let
D
be a digraph. Set
m=pod(D)
and let
P_{1},\ldots,P_{m}
be a partial order decomposition of
D
.
Let
<
be an
\vec{\omega}
-ordering of
D
and
B=B(D,<)
. So
\omega(B)=\vec{\omega}(D)
. For
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, let
B_{i}=B(P_{i},<)
. By Lemma
6
, each
B_{i}
is a comparability graph, and is thus perfect. Moreover, since

A(D)=A(P_{1})\cup\cdots\cup A(P_{m})
, we have

E(B)=E(B_{1})\cup\cdots\cup E(B_{m})
.

Now, there exist proper colourings
f_{i}:V(B)\to\{1,\ldots,\omega(B_{i})\}
of each
B_{i}
.
Since
\omega(B_{i})\leqslant\omega(B)
for every
i
, the colouring

f:V(B)\to\{1,\ldots,\omega(B)\}^{m}
defined by
f(v)=(f_{1}(v),\dots,f_{m}(v))

is a proper colouring of
B
using at most
\omega(B)^{m}=\vec{\omega}(D)^{m}
colours.
Now, since the chromatic number of a backedge graph is always at most the dichromatic number of the digraph, we have
\vec{\chi}(D)\leqslant\chi(B)\leqslant\vec{\omega}(D)^{m}
, as desired.
∎

3
Polynomially
\vec{\chi}
-bounded classes and substitution

The goal of this section is to prove Theorem
4
.
Let us first give necessary definitions.

Given a digraph
D
, and
X,Y\subseteq V(D)
, we say
X\Rightarrow Y
if, for every
x\in X
and
y\in Y
, we have
xy\in A(D)
. If
X
or
Y
is a singleton, we may drop the brackets to simplify the notation.

Given two digraphs
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
with disjoint vertex sets, and a vertex

u\in V(D_{1})
, we say that a digraph
D
is obtained by
substituting
D_{2}
for
u
in
D_{1}
if the following hold:

•

V(D)=(V(D_{1})\setminus\{u\})\cup V(D_{2})
,

•

D[V(D_{1})\setminus\{u\}]=D_{1}\setminus u
,

•

D[V(D_{2})]=D_{2}
,

•

for all
v\in V(D_{1})\setminus\{u\}
if
v\to_{D_{1}}u
(resp.
u\to_{D_{1}}v
, resp.
u
and
v
are non-adjacent in
D_{1}
), then
v\Rightarrow V(D_{2})
(resp.
V(D_{2})\Rightarrow v
, resp. there are no arcs between
v
and
V(D_{2})
) in
D
.

We first prove that the class of tournaments with
pod
at most
m
is closed under substitution.

Lemma 8
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a class of tournaments such that every
T\in\mathcal{C}
satisfies
pod(T)\leqslant m
.
Then every tournament
T\in\mathcal{C}^{subst}
also satisfies
pod(T)\leqslant m
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that if
T
and
T^{\prime}
are both tournaments such that
pod(T)\leq m
and
pod(T^{\prime})\leq m
, then the same holds for all substitutions of
T^{\prime}
for a vertex of
T
.

Consider a substitution of
T^{\prime}
for a vertex
v
of
T
and let the resulting tournament be
T_{v\rightsquigarrow T^{\prime}}
. Let
P_{1},\dots,P_{m}
and
P^{\prime}_{1},\dots,P^{\prime}_{m}
be partial order decompositions of respectively
T
and
T^{\prime}
. We set
Q_{i}
to be the spanning subdigraph of
T_{v\rightsquigarrow T^{\prime}}
obtained from substituting
P^{\prime}_{i}
for vertex
v
in
P_{i}
. More formally:

\displaystyle A(Q_{i})={}

\displaystyle A(P^{\prime}_{i})\cup\{e\in A(P_{i})\mid v\notin e\}\cup\{(x,y):x\in V(T^{\prime}),\ (v,y)\in A(P_{i})\}\cup\{(y,x):x\in V(T^{\prime}),\ (y,v)\in A(P_{i})\}.

Clearly
A(T_{v\rightsquigarrow T^{\prime}})=A(Q_{1})\cup\dots\cup A(Q_{m})
. It remains to show that each
Q_{i}
is transitive. Let
a,b,c\in V(T_{v\rightsquigarrow T^{\prime}})
be three vertices such that
a\rightarrow_{T_{v\rightsquigarrow T^{\prime}}}b
and
b\rightarrow_{T_{v\rightsquigarrow T^{\prime}}}c
. If
a,b,c\in V(T^{\prime})
, then
(a,b),(b,c)\in A(P^{\prime}_{i})
, and
P^{\prime}_{i}
is transitive, so
(a,c)\in A(P^{\prime}_{i})\subseteq A(Q_{i})
. If
|\{a,b,c\}\cap V(T^{\prime})|\leq 1
, then the arcs corresponding to
(a,b)
and
(b,c)
in
P_{i}
guarantee the existence of
(a,c)
because
P_{i}
is transitive.

Finally, observe that if
|\{a,b,c\}\cap V(T^{\prime})|=2
then
b\in V(T^{\prime})
by definition of the substitution. Indeed, we have
x\Rightarrow V(T^{\prime})
for every
x\in V(T_{v\rightsquigarrow T^{\prime}})\setminus V(T^{\prime})
. Then, exactly one of
a
and
c
is in
V(T^{\prime})
and in either case we have
a\to_{T_{v\rightsquigarrow T^{\prime}}}c
by definition of the substitution.
∎

A particular special case of tournaments with bounded
pod
are tournaments with bounded
\vec{\chi}
. In particular, every digraph with a bipartite underlying undirected graph has
pod
at most 2, which can be used iteratively to show the following.

Lemma 9
.

Let
T
be a tournament with dichromatic number
k
and colour classes
V_{1},\dots,V_{k}
arising from a
k
-dicolouring of
T
. For each
i
, let
A(V_{i})
denote the set of arcs of
T
with both endpoints in
V_{i}
. Then
T\backslash\{A(V_{1}),\dots,A(V_{k})\}
decomposes (disjointly) into at most
2\lceil\log_{2}(k)\rceil
comparability digraphs.
In particular,

pod(T\backslash\{A(V_{1}),\dots,A(V_{k})\})\leq 2\lceil\log_{2}(k)\rceil
.

Proof.

Throughout the proof let
V=V(T)
for clarity. Let
f(k)
be the smallest number such that every tournament with dichromatic number
k
and colour classes
V_{1},\dots,V_{k}
allows
T\backslash(A(V_{1})\cup\dots\cup A(V_{k}))
to be decomposed into at most
f(k)
comparability digraphs. We aim to show that
f
exists, and that
f(k)\leq 2\lceil\log_{2}(k)\rceil
for
k\geq 1
.

We proceed by strong induction on
k
. It suffices to consider the cases when
k
is a power of
2
. The case
k=1
is straightforward as
V_{1}=V
, and so
T\setminus A(V_{1})
has no edges.

Now let
k=2^{t}
for some
t\geq 1
. Let
m=2^{t-1}
. Let
T_{\alpha}=T[V_{1}\cup\dots\cup V_{m}]
and
T_{\beta}=T[V_{m+1}\cup\dots\cup V_{k}]
be the subtournaments of
T
induced by
V_{1}\cup\dots\cup V_{m}
and
V_{m+1}\cup\dots\cup V_{k}
. respectively. We apply strong induction to
T_{\alpha}
and
T_{\beta}
to decompose
T_{\alpha}\setminus(A(V_{1})\cup\dots\cup A(V_{m}))
and
T_{\beta}\setminus(A(V_{m+1})\cup\dots\cup A(V_{k}))
into
2\lceil\log_{2}m\rceil=2(t-1)
comparability digraphs each.
Since the disjoint union of two comparability digraphs is a comparability digraph, we may partition the arcs of
(A(T_{\alpha})\cup A(T_{\beta}))\setminus(A(V_{1})\cup\dots\cup A(V_{k}))

into
2(t-1)
comparability digraphs
D_{\alpha}^{1}\cup D_{\beta}^{1},\dots,D_{\alpha}^{2(t-1)}\cup D_{\beta}^{2(t-1)}
, where
D_{\alpha}^{1},\dots,D_{\alpha}^{2(t-1)}
and
D_{\beta}^{1},\dots,D_{\beta}^{2(t-1)}
are obtained by induction hypothesis on
T_{\alpha}
and
T_{\beta}
, respectively.

Now we create two more comparability digraphs: One containing all arcs from
V(T_{\alpha})
to
V(T_{\beta})
, and one containing all arcs from
V(T_{\beta})
to
V(T_{\alpha})
. Note that every vertex is a source or a sink with respect to each of these sets, so these are indeed comparability digraphs. This yields the desired decomposition using
2t
comparability digraphs.
∎

Corollary 10
.

Let
T
be a tournament with dichromatic number
k
. Then
T
may be decomposed (disjointly) into at most
2\lceil\log_{2}(k)\rceil+1
comparability digraphs. In particular,
pod(T)\leq 2\lceil\log_{2}(k)\rceil+1
.

Proof.

By Lemma
9
, if
V_{1},\dots,V_{k}
are colour classes of a
k
-colouring of
T
, then
A(T)\backslash(A(V_{1})\cup\dots\cup A(V_{k}))
may be decomposed into at most
2\lceil\log_{2}(k)\rceil
comparability digraphs. Then the result follows by noting that
(V(T),A(V_{1})\cup\dots\cup A(V_{k}))
, as a disjoint union of acyclic tournaments, is a comparability digraph as well. ∎

With these lemmas in hand, it is straightforward to prove the main result of this section.

Theorem
4
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a class of tournaments such that each tournament in
\mathcal{C}
has dichromatic number at most
k
. Then for each
T\in\mathcal{C}^{subst}
, we have
\vec{\chi}(T)\leq(\vec{\omega}(T))^{2\lceil\log_{2}(k)\rceil+1}
. In particular, the class
\mathcal{C}^{subst}
is polynomially
\vec{\chi}
-bounded.

Proof.

By Corollary
10
, each tournament
T\in\mathcal{C}
satisfies
pod(T)\leq 2\lceil\log_{2}(k)\rceil+1
. By Lemma
8
, each tournament
T\in\mathcal{C}^{subst}
also satisfies
pod(T)\leq 2\lceil\log_{2}(k)\rceil+1
. Then, by Corollary
2
, the result follows.
∎

By modifying the above lemmas and theorems to the case where
\mathcal{C}
is a polynomially
\vec{\chi}
-bounded class of tournaments, say with
\vec{\chi}(T)\leq(\vec{\omega}(T))^{r}
for each
T\in\mathcal{C}
, it is possible to show that then
\mathcal{C}^{subst}
is
\vec{\chi}
-bounded by

\vec{\omega}(T)^{2\lceil r\log_{2}\vec{\omega}(T)\rceil+1},

improving the single-exponential bound of
[
1
]
to a “quasi-polynomial” one. However, it appears that a different technique may be required to prove (or disprove) that substitution fully preserves polynomial
\vec{\chi}
-boundedness in tournaments.

4
Crossing tournaments do not have bounded pod

In the previous section, we showed that digraphs with bounded
pod
are polynomially
\vec{\chi}
-bounded, and that whenever a class
\mathcal{C}
of tournaments has bounded
pod
, then
\mathcal{C}^{subst}
is polynomially
\vec{\chi}
-bounded. It is therefore natural to ask whether the converse of these two statements holds. In this section, we show that it does not: there exist acyclic digraphs with arbitrarily large
pod
, and there is a class of tournaments, namely crossing tournaments, with unbounded
pod
. In a future paper, some of the authors show that crossing tournaments are nevertheless polynomially
\vec{\chi}
-bounded, completing the proof that the converse of Theorem
4
is false.

We now turn our attention to crossing tournaments. Let us first recall some definitions about interval graphs.

An
interval system

\mathcal{I}
is a set of open intervals in
\mathbb{R}
such that no two elements of
\mathcal{I}
share an endpoint. For an interval
I\in\mathcal{I}
, we denote its left endpoint by
L(I)
and its right endpoint by
R(I)
. The interval system
\mathcal{I}
is naturally equipped with two (not necessarily distinct) total orders:
<_{L}
, the ordering by left endpoint, and
<_{R}
, the ordering by right endpoint. There is also a natural partial order
\leq_{\mathcal{I}}
defined by
I<_{\mathcal{I}}J
if and only if
R(I)<L(J)
; that is, if and only if
I
and
J
are disjoint and
I
lies to the left of
J
in
\mathbb{R}
.

The
interval graph

G_{\mathcal{I}}
of an interval system
\mathcal{I}
is the ordered graph with vertex set
\mathcal{I}
, ordered by
<_{L}
, in which two intervals
I,J\in\mathcal{I}
with
I<_{L}J
are adjacent if and only if
R(I)>L(J)
.

A tournament
T
is a
crossing tournament
if there exists an interval system
\mathcal{I}
and a total ordering
<
of
V(T)
such that the ordered graph
(B(T,<),<)
is isomorphic to the ordered interval graph
(G_{\mathcal{I}},<_{L})
. Crossing tournaments were introduced in
[
15
]
as tournaments with unbounded
\vec{\omega}
.

Let us now prove that “universal” crossing tournaments have unbounded
pod
.

We define the following notation.
Intuitively, this corresponds to using closed intervals with additional tie-breaking rules to order their endpoints.

Definition 11
.

For positive integers
1\leq i<j\leq n
, let

\tilde{[}i,j\tilde{]}=\left(i-\frac{1}{j+2},j+\frac{1}{i+2}\right).

Let
\mathcal{I}_{n}
be the interval system consisting of intervals
\{\tilde{[}i,j\tilde{]}:1\leq i<j\leq n\}
. Let
B_{n}
be the ordered interval graph of
\mathcal{I}_{n}
, and let
T_{n}
be the tournament with backedge graph
B_{n}
.

Thus, the vertices of
B_{n}
are the intervals
\tilde{[}i,j\tilde{]}\in\mathcal{I}_{n}
, whose left endpoints are increasing in lexicographic order on the pair
(i,j)
.

It is easy to check that two intervals
I_{1}=\tilde{[}i_{1},j_{1}\tilde{]}
and
I_{2}=\tilde{[}i_{2},j_{2}\tilde{]}
with
L(I_{1})<L(I_{2})
are adjacent in
B_{n}
if and only if
i_{1}\leq i_{2}\leq j_{1}
. Thus, these are exactly the cases in which pairs of intervals in
T_{n}
have an arc
I_{2}\rightarrow_{T_{n}}I_{1}
, and in all other cases we have an arc
I_{1}\rightarrow_{T_{n}}I_{2}
.

The proof of the theorem below is adapted from a similar one of Gyárfás, Marits, and Tóth
[
11
]
, proving that in the undirected setting, interval graphs cannot be written as a union of a bounded number of comparability graphs.

We need the following definitions. Given a digraph
D
, its
underlying (undirected) graph

UG(D)
is defined as the graph with
V(UG(D))=V(D)
and
vw\in E(UG(D))
if and only if
(v,w)\in A(D)
or
(w,v)\in A(D)
. Given a digraph
D=(V,A)
, the
line digraph

\overrightarrow{L}(D)
has vertex set
A
and arc set
\{(u,v)(v,w):u,v,w\in V,(u,v),(v,w)\in A\}
. Thus, whenever two arcs of
D
meet head-to-tail, there is an arc between them in the natural direction in
\overrightarrow{L}(D)
. We will use the following auxiliary result on line digraphs.

Theorem 12

(Erdős and Hajnal
[
9
]
, Harner and Entringer
[
12
]
)
.

Given a digraph
D
, we have

\chi(UG(\overrightarrow{L}(D)))\geq\log_{2}(\chi(UG(D)))

where for
D
a digraph,
UG(D)
is the underlying undirected graph.

Theorem 13
.

For every positive integer
k^{\prime}
, there is a crossing tournament
T
with
pod(T)\geq k^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Let
T_{n}
be as in Definition
11
with
n\geq 2^{2^{k^{\prime}}}
.

Let
P_{1},\dots,P_{m}
be a partial order decomposition of
T_{n}
. Let
\phi:A(T_{n})\rightarrow[m]
map each arc
r
of
T_{n}
to the least
i
such that
r\in A(P_{i})
. Thus the sets
\phi^{-1}(i)
for
i\in[m]
partition
A(T_{n})
.

For each quadruple of positive integers
1\leq a<b<c<d\leq n
, note that we have
\tilde{[}a,b\tilde{]}\leftarrow\tilde{[}b,c\tilde{]}
,
\tilde{[}b,c\tilde{]}\leftarrow\tilde{[}c,d\tilde{]}
, and
\tilde{[}a,b\tilde{]}\rightarrow\tilde{[}c,d\tilde{]}
in
A(T_{n})
, so these intervals form a cyclic triangle. It follows that for each such integer quadruple, we have
\phi(\tilde{[}b,c\tilde{]}\tilde{[}a,b\tilde{]})\neq\phi(\tilde{[}c,d\tilde{]}\tilde{[}b,c\tilde{]})
. Thus, letting
G_{n}
be the graph with
V(G_{n})=\{(a,b,c):1\leq a<b<c\leq n\}
and edge set
\{(a,b,c)(b,c,d):1\leq a<b<c<d\leq n\}
, we have that the graph
G_{n}
is
m
-colourable, by giving vertex
(a,b,c)
the colour
\phi(\tilde{[}b,c\tilde{]}\tilde{[}a,b\tilde{]})
, which is a proper colouring by the previous observation.

Now, consider the transitive tournament
TT_{n}
with vertices
[n]
such that for each
i<j
we have
i\rightarrow_{TT_{n}}j
. Then
\overrightarrow{L}(TT_{n})
has vertices consisting of ordered pairs
(i,j)
with
i<j
with arcs
\{(i,j)(j,k):1\leq i<j<k\leq n\}
. Moreover,
\overrightarrow{L}(\overrightarrow{L}(TT_{n}))
is isomorphic to a digraph with vertex set
\{(i,j,k):1\leq i<j<k\leq n\}
and arc set
\{(i,j,k)(j,k,l):1\leq i<j<k<l\leq n\}
. In particular
UG(\overrightarrow{L}(\overrightarrow{L}(TT_{n}))
is isomorphic to
G_{n}
defined above. But then, by Theorem
12
, we have

m\geq\chi(G_{n})=\chi(UG(\overrightarrow{L}(\overrightarrow{L}(TT_{n}))))\geq\log_{2}(\chi(UG(\overrightarrow{L}(TT_{n}))\geq\log_{2}(\log_{2}(\chi(UG(TT_{n}))))=\log_{2}(\log_{2}(n))\geq k^{\prime}

Since our initial partial order decomposition was arbitrary, we have
pod(T_{n})\geq k^{\prime}
.∎

5
Poset tournaments

We now turn our attention to poset tournaments.
A tournament
T
is a
poset tournament
if there exist a total ordering
<
of
V(T)
such that, for all
u<v<w
, if
u\to_{T}v
and
v\to_{T}w
, then
u\to_{T}w
. In other words, the forward edges of
T
for
<
form a poset. In this case, it follows that
B(T,>)
is a comparability graph.

Poset tournaments were first introduced in
[
7
]
, where they are conjectured to be precisely the tournaments
H
such that
H
-free tournaments have bounded domination number.

We propose the following conjecture:

Conjecture 14
.

The class of poset tournaments is
\vec{\chi}
-bounded.

We now recall the standard notion of (Dushnik–Miller) dimension of a poset. A
linear extension
of a poset
P
is a total ordering
<
of
V(P)
such that whenever
u<_{P}v
, we also have
u<v
. Given two posets
P_{1},P_{2}
on the same vertex set, their intersection, denoted
P_{1}\cap P_{2}
, is the poset
P
where
u<_{P}v
if
u<_{P_{1}}v
and
u<_{P_{2}}v
. (This also matches taking the intersection of the arc sets of corresponding oriented graphs).
The
dimension
of a poset
P
, denoted
\dim(P)
, is the minimum integer
d
such that there exist
d
linear extensions
<_{1},\dots,<_{d}
of
P
whose intersection is
P
, that is, such that for all distinct
u,v\in V(P)
, we have
u<_{P}v
if and only if
u<_{i}v
for every
i\in\{1,\dots,d\}
.

A
witness
for a poset tournament
T
is a pair
(P,<)
, where
P
is a poset on
V(T)
and
<
is a linear extension of
P
, such that for all
u,v\in V(T)
with
u<v
, we have
v\to_{T}u
if and only if
u<_{P}v
.

The
dimension
of a poset tournament
T
is the minimum value of
\dim(P)
over all witnesses
(P,<)
for
T
.

As a partial result on conjecture
14
, we prove that the class of poset tournaments with bounded dimension is
\vec{\chi}
-bounded.

Lemma 15
.

Let
T
be a poset tournament. Then
pod(T)\leqslant\dim(T)+1
.

Proof.

Let
(P_{0},<_{0})
be a witness for
T
such that
\dim(P_{0})=\dim(T)=d
. By definition of the dimension, there are linear extensions
<_{1},...,<_{d}
of
P_{0}
such that
P_{0}={}<_{1}\cap\cdots\cap<_{d}
. For each
1\leqslant i\leqslant d
, we define the poset
P_{i}={}>_{0}\cap<_{i}
. We show that
E(T)=E(P_{0})\cup E(P_{1})\cup\cdots\cup E(P_{d})
.

First, we show that
E(T)\subseteq E(P_{0})\cup E(P_{1})\cup\cdots\cup E(P_{d})
. Let
u<_{0}v
be two vertices of
T
.
If
u\to_{T}v
, then
u<_{P_{0}}v
and
u\to_{P_{0}}v
.
Otherwise,
v\to_{T}u
, which means that
u
and
v
are incomparable in
P_{0}
. This implies that there are
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant d
such that
u<_{i}v
and
v<_{j}u
, hence
v\to_{P_{j}}u
.

Conversely, we show that
E(P_{0})\cup E(P_{1})\cup\cdots\cup E(P_{d})\subseteq E(T)
.
If
u\to_{P_{0}}v
, then
u<_{P_{0}}v
, and
u<_{0}v
since
<_{0}
is a linear extension of
P_{0}
, which means that
u\to_{T}v
.
Now, if
u\to_{P_{i}}v
for some
1\leqslant i\leqslant d
, then
u>_{0}v
and
u<_{i}v
. This implies that
u
and
v
are not comparable in
P_{0}
, which in turn means that
u\to_{T}v
.
∎

Corollary 16
.

For every integer
k
, the class of poset tournaments with dimension at most
k
is
\vec{\chi}
-bounded.

6
Using partial order decompositions to lower bound the clique number of tournaments

The inequality
\vec{\chi}\leq\vec{\omega}^{pod}
can be rewritten as
\vec{\omega}\geq\vec{\chi}^{1/pod}
, and thus yields lower bounds on the clique number of tournaments when
\vec{\chi}
and
pod
are known. Let us illustrate this with two examples.

Let
B_{0}
be the one-vertex tournament, and for
k\geq 1
, let
B_{k}
be the tournament obtained from three disjoint copies
A
,
B
, and
C
of
B_{k-1}
by adding all possible arcs from
A
to
B
, from
B
to
C
, and from
C
to
A
, i.e.
A\Rightarrow B,B\Rightarrow C,C\Rightarrow A
.
Computing the clique number, or even a decent approximation of the clique number of
B_{k}
appears to be quite difficult, whereas obtaining lower bounds on the dichromatic number and upper bounds on the partial order decomposition number is much easier, yielding a useful lower bound on its clique number. We now explain how to bound both the dichromatic number and the partial order decomposition number of
B_{k}
.

Let
TT_{k}
be the transitive tournament on
k
vertices, and let
\vec{C}_{3}
be the directed triangle. Set
\mathcal{B}=\{TT_{1},TT_{2},\vec{C}_{3}\}^{subst}
. Observe that
B_{k}\in\mathcal{B}
for every
k
. Since
TT_{1}
,
TT_{2}
, and
\vec{C}_{3}
all have
pod
at most
3
, Lemma
8
implies that
pod(B_{k})\leqslant 3
for every
k
. Hence
\vec{\omega}(B_{k})\geq\vec{\chi}(B_{k})^{1/3}
.

Now, let us prove that
\vec{\chi}(B_{k})\geq\frac{3}{2}\,\vec{\chi}(B_{k-1})
. In any dicolouring of
B_{k}
, each colour can appear in at most two of the three copies of
B_{k-1}
, since otherwise choosing one vertex of that colour in each copy yields a monochromatic directed triangle. Since each copy of
B_{k-1}
requires at least
\vec{\chi}(B_{k-1})
colours, it follows that
\vec{\chi}(B_{k})\geq\frac{3}{2}\,\vec{\chi}(B_{k-1})
. By induction, we obtain
\vec{\chi}(B_{k})\geq(3/2)^{k}
. Altogether we get the following.

Theorem 17
.

For every integer
k
,
\vec{\omega}(B_{k})\geq(3/2)^{k/3}
.

Since
|V(B_{k})|=3^{k}
, Theorem
17
gives
\vec{\omega}(B_{k})\geq|V(B_{k})|^{1/8.13}
and thus a positive answer to Conjecture
5
as announced in the introduction.

Next, we introduce a construction due to Nassar and Yuster
[
13
]
, and show how to bound its clique number from below. For
t\in\mathbb{N}
, the tournament
G_{t}
has vertex set
[t]\times[t]
, and we have
(i,j)\rightarrow(k,l)
if and only if:

•

i\leq k
and
j<l
; or

•

i<k
and
j>l
;

•

i>k
and
j=l
.

From this definition, it is immediate that
pod(G_{t})\leq 3
, since each of the bullet points above is a partial order. In Lemma 3.1 (and the proof of Theorem 1.6) of
[
13
]
, it is shown that no backedge graph of
G_{t}
has an independent set of size
2t
. (In fact, the proof of Theorem 1.6 explicitly shows that every backedge graph of
G_{t}
has a clique of size at least
|V(G_{t})|^{1/8}/3
and can be adapted to give a similar bound to ours. However, using
pod
yields a much shorter proof.) Consequently, we have
\vec{\chi}(G_{t})\geq t^{2}/(2t)=t/2
. Since
pod(G_{t})\leq 3
, we conclude the following.

Theorem 18
.

For every
t\geq 1
, we have
\vec{\omega}(G_{t})\geq\vec{\chi}(G_{t})^{1/3}=(t/2)^{1/3}\geq|V(G_{t})|^{1/6}/2.

This construction gives another answer to Conjecture
5
. We are not aware of an explicit construction achieving a better exponent than
1/6
.

A related question is the following.

Question 19
.

Let
f_{\vec{\omega}}(n)
denote the largest possible value of the clique number of an
n
-vertex tournament. Can we determine
f_{\vec{\omega}}(n)
at least asymptotically?

At an earlier stage, we were led to study the families
(B_{k})_{k\geq 0}
and
(G_{t})_{t\geq 0}
as possible extremal families for estimating
f_{\vec{\omega}}(n)
. We later realized, however, that a construction of Alon, Pach, and Solymosi
[
3
, Theorem 1.3]
gives stronger bounds on
f_{\vec{\omega}}(n)
, placing it between
n^{1/3}/\log^{2}n
and
en^{1/2}
. Nevertheless, the families
(B_{k})_{k\geq 0}
and
(G_{t})_{t\geq 0}
remain natural explicit families in this context, and it is still conceivable that the true values of
\vec{\omega}(B_{k})
and
\vec{\omega}(G_{t})
are much larger than the lower bound obtained in Theorems
17
and
18
. Note also that the construction of Alon, Pach, and Solymosi relies on probabilistic tools, and therefore does not answer the conjecture of Gutowski and Rams.

7
Miscellaneous results and open questions

We start this section with two more results on the partial order decomposition number of digraphs and tournaments. We first prove that acyclic digraphs can have arbitrarily large
pod
.

Theorem 20
.

For every positive integer
k
, there exists an acyclic digraph
D
such that
pod(D)\geq k
.

Proof.

Let
G
be a triangle-free graph with chromatic number
2^{k}
, and let
D
be an acyclic orientation of
D
. Then given a partial order decomposition
D=D_{1}\cup\dots\cup D_{m}
, colour each arc
a\in A(D)
with the smallest
i
such that
a\in D_{i}
. Note that two arcs forming a directed three-vertex path must receive different colours since
UG(D)
is triangle-free. Thus, this colouring is in fact a proper colouring of
UG(\overrightarrow{L}(D))
. But this implies by Theorem
12
that
m\geq\chi(UG(\overrightarrow{L}(D)))\geq\log_{2}(\chi(UG(D)))=\log_{2}(2^{k})=k
. Thus, every partial order decomposition of
D
requires at least
k
partial orders, so
pod(D)\geq k
.
∎

Let
T
be a tournament. A
dominating set
of
T
is a set of vertices
S
such that
V(T)=S\cup N^{+}(S)
, and the
domination number
dom(T)
of
T
is the size of a smallest dominating set of
T
. We prove that a tournament with bounded
pod
also has bounded domination number.

Theorem 21
.

There is a function
f
such that, for every tournament
T
,
dom(T)\leq f(pod(T))
.

Proof.

The Erdős-Sands-Sauer-Woodrow-Bousquet-Lochet-Thomassé Theorem
[
5
]
states that a
k
-arc coloured tournament has a set
S
of at most
g(k)
vertices such that, for every
v\in V(T)
, there is a monochromatic directed path from
S
to
v
. By definition of
pod
, a tournament has an arc covering by
pod(T)
posets
P_{1},\dots,P_{\ell}
. We colour each arc
(u,v)
of
T
by the nonempty subset
X\subseteq[\ell]
such that
(u,v)\in A(P_{i})
for every
i\in X
. Each colour class induces a poset as an intersection of posets.
We deduce a
(2^{pod(T)}-1)
-arc colouring where each colour class is a poset, implying that
S
is a dominating set.
∎
