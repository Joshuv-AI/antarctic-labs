Discrimination and depressive symptoms: the role of psychological resilience across racial and ethnic groups
Authors: Yi Wang, Yifan Lou, Huei-Wern Shen, Man Guo, Megan Gilster, Markus Schafer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to identify distinct typologies of discrimination experiences and their association with depressive symptoms among older adults across racial/ethnic groups and the potential moderating role of psychological resilience across racial/ethnic backgrounds.MethodsData from 11,832 respondents (65% non-Hispanic White, 18% non-Hispanic Black, 13% Hispanic, and 4% Other) in the 2016/2018 Health and Retirement Study Psychosocial Leave-Behind Questionnaire were analyzed. Six discrimination types (e.g., disrespect, poor service in restaurants, perceived as unintelligent) and a 12-item resilience index covering domains such as perseverance despite major setbacks, adaptability to change, and recognition of inner strengths were assessed.ResultsLatent class analysis identified three distinct typologies of discrimination experiences: (C1) Low discrimination—low across all experiences, (C2) Microaggressions—elevated probabilities of disrespect, poor service at restaurants, and being perceived as unintelligent, and (C3) Pervasive/threatening discrimination—high across all experiences, including being feared and harassed. Regression models showed that for non-Hispanic Whites, both C2 and C3 were associated with higher depressive symptoms; for non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic individuals, only C2 was linked with more depressive symptoms; for the Other category, only C3 was related to more depressive symptoms. Higher resilience was associated with fewer depressive symptoms across all groups but moderated the effects of C2 and C3 for non-Hispanic Whites and only C2 for Hispanic individuals.DiscussionFindings highlight discrimination as a mental health risk factor for older Americans and suggest that psychological resilience may mitigate its impact, contingent on experience type and racial/ethnic background. Implications for policy and practice are discussed.Keywords: Minority aging, Bias, Mental health

Discrimination is a well-documented risk factor for a wide range of mental health issues, including depression, psychological distress, and anxiety (Moody, 2022; Mouzon et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2024; Wang & Narcisse, 2025). This is particularly true among older adults from racial and ethnic minoritized groups. Racial and ethnic minoritized groups, including Black, Hispanic, Asian, and Native American populations, often experience discrimination in various forms, such as interpersonal prejudice, systemic inequities, hate speech/incidents, and microaggressions (Hernández & Villodas, 2020; Lewis et al., 2015; Li et al., 2024; White et al., 2020). These experiences are not only frequent but also cumulative, compounding over the life course and contributing to heightened vulnerability to mental health challenges. Studies have shown that compared to their White counterparts, Black Americans report higher levels of perceived discrimination, which is associated with elevated depressive symptoms and lower psychological well-being (Qin et al., 2020; Williams, Lawrence, & Davis, 2019). Similarly, Hispanic and Asian individuals frequently encounter discrimination tied to language barriers, cultural or ethnic stereotypes, or immigration status, further exacerbating risks for depression and anxiety (Andrade et al., 2021; Gao & Liu, 2021).

As an important psychological resource, resilience reflects individuals’ healthy psychological profiles, characterized by positive coping, active adaptation, and effective stress regulation, and plays a pivotal role in managing adversity (Masten et al., 2023). Conceptually rich and multifaceted, resilience encompasses cognitive, emotional, and behavioral strategies that enable individuals to respond adaptively to stressors, including those stemming from discriminatory experiences (Southwick et al., 2014). Rather than succumbing to defeat or pessimism, resilient older adults reframe the negative experiences, draw on inner strengths, and leverage external resources to manage stress associated with life challenges, thereby enhancing their mental health and overall well-being (Masten et al., 2021; Southwick et al., 2014). Psychological resilience, in this study, is examined as the mental capability to navigate adversity through positive adaptation that protects health and well-being (Taylor et al., 2024; Taylor & Carr, 2021), aligning with previous research that also draws on data from the Health and Retirement Study (Du et al., 2025; Manning et al., 2016). Empirical literature further supports the protective role of psychological resilience in later life, showing that resilient older adults demonstrate greater emotional regulation, reduced susceptibility to stress-related disorders, and enhanced coping with adversity (Ribeiro-Gonçalves et al., 2023; Vannini et al., 2021; Zapater-Fajarí et al., 2021).

Although empirical studies suggest that resilience can play a critical role in protecting mental health in the face of discrimination, evidence also indicates that its effectiveness may vary across racial and ethnic groups (Cook et al., 2023). While research specifically examining the role of psychological resilience in health outcomes across diverse racial groups remains limited, broader resilience literature offers useful insights. For instance, Black individuals with high levels of resilience, often bolstered by culturally grounded resources, may experience less psychological distress or fewer depressive symptoms despite frequent discrimination experiences (Anderson et al., 2023; Neblett, 2023). In contrast, resilience among Hispanic individuals may be influenced by familism—a cultural value emphasizing family cohesion, obligation, and support—which can serve as a protective factor against the mental health toll of discrimination (Campos et al., 2014; Curci et al., 2023). However, less is known about how resilience operates among Asian or Native American populations, where cultural stigmas around mental health (e.g., in Asian American communities) or historical disenfranchisement (e.g., among Native American communities) may complicate coping mechanisms (Hartmann et al., 2019; Yang & Woo, 2025). Given that resilience among racial and ethnic minoritized groups may be shaped by cultural factors, such as collectivism, spiritual beliefs, or community support, these influences can play a unique role in combating discrimination. That said, the efficacy of resilience may vary across racial and ethnic groups due to differences in cultural values, historical contexts, and access to resources.

Despite the rich literature, gaps remain in understanding discrimination’s multidimensional nature and protective factors such as psychological resilience that may reduce its adverse mental health impact. Prior studies often treat discrimination as a unidimensional construct, overlooking the co-occurrence of various aspects of discrimination experiences (e.g., disrespect, poor service, and threats) that may jointly shape mental health. Moreover, individuals may experience qualitatively different patterns of discrimination that are not adequately captured by variable-centered approaches. By focusing on average effects, such approaches obscure meaningful heterogeneity in how discrimination is experienced across individuals and groups. In contrast, a person-centered approach, such as latent class analysis (LCA), allows for the identification of conceptually distinct profiles of discrimination experiences, capturing both the co-occurrence of multiple dimensions and the diversity of exposure patterns. However, there is limited research probing how different discrimination experiences, ranging from subtle microaggressions to overt threats, cluster together to form meaningful typologies across race and ethnicity and how they might be linked differentially to later-life mental health outcomes. Second, the moderating role of psychological resilience in mitigating discrimination’s negative effects on mental health outcomes remains underexplored.

Guiding theories

This study is guided by two theoretical frameworks: the Minority Stress Theory and the Stress Process Theory. The Minority Stress Theory (Meyer, 2003) provides a critical lens for understanding how exposure to discrimination, conceptualized as a chronic, identity-related stressor, negatively influences mental health among racial and ethnic minoritized groups. The theory posits that minoritized individuals experience unique and persistent stressors that arise from their marginalized social status, including prejudice, microaggressions, stigma, and systemic inequities. These stressors operate both externally (e.g., overt discrimination, institutional bias) and internally (e.g., expectations of rejection, internalized stigma), cumulatively contributing to adverse psychological outcomes such as depression and anxiety. Minority Stress Theory further emphasizes that these experiences are not uniform but are shaped by intersecting identities and sociocultural contexts, making the nature and intensity of minority stress multifaceted and dynamic across groups. Informed by this perspective, the current study aligns with the application of Minority Stress Theory to older adults from diverse racial and ethnic backgrounds by identifying distinct typologies of discrimination experiences (e.g., microaggressions versus pervasive and threatening discrimination) and examining their associations with depressive symptoms. This approach acknowledges that discrimination is not a singular construct but a constellation of experiences that vary in frequency and severity, each with potentially different psychological implications.

The Stress Process Theory (Pearlin, 2010; Pearlin & Schooler, 1978) offers a robust framework for not only understanding how discrimination, as a primary stressor, contributes to mental health disparities but also highlighting the importance of coping resources in mitigating the negative mental health consequences of such stress. According to the theory, stressors like discrimination trigger a cascade of physiological and psychological responses that can erode mental health, particularly when they are chronic or compounded by secondary stressors, such as acculturation challenges, language barriers, and social exclusion (Aneshensel, 1992; Jackson et al., 2003), which are more frequently experienced by racial and ethnic minorities. Moreover, the harmful effects of discrimination may be influenced by various levels of coping resources. According to the theory, a lack of coping resources, for instance, psychological resilience, can compromise one’s ability to combat the adverse health effects of such stressors (Aneshensel, 2015). These coping resources shape how older adults appraise discrimination-related stress, their adaptation, and well-being. Being in the racial/ethnic minoritized group may intensify exposure to stressors (discrimination) and restrict access to psychosocial coping resources (resilience) that could otherwise ameliorate the adverse mental health outcomes. Thus, coping resources are thought to moderate the deleterious effects of discrimination experiences on mental health outcomes.

This interplay between discrimination and coping resources underscores the critical role of psychological resilience in mitigating stress. As a key coping resource, psychological resilience is susceptible to discrimination experiences. Chronic exposure to discriminatory treatment can erode an individual’s psychological resilience over time, diminishing their capacity to recover from stress and maintain emotional balance (Grey et al., 2024; Munawar et al., 2025; Wang, Zhang, et al., 2025). For older adults, this erosion is particularly consequential, as aging often coincides with additional life stressors such as health decline, loss of loved ones, or social isolation. When resilience is compromised, the protective buffer it provides against stress weakens, leaving individuals more vulnerable to the psychological toll of discrimination. In this way, discrimination not only acts as a stressor but also undermines the very resources needed to cope with it—creating a feedback loop that exacerbates mental health disparities, especially among racially and ethnically marginalized older populations.

Informed by this perspective, the current study examines the moderating role of psychological resilience as a potential protective factor that may buffer against the harmful effects of discrimination. This integration underscores how psychological resilience, shaped by individual and cultural resources, may mitigate the mental health burden imposed by stressors such as discrimination experiences across diverse racial and ethnic groups.

The present study

To date, few studies have examined the mental health consequences of the different forms of discrimination among diverse aging populations, and even fewer have explored the potential protective factors that may moderate these effects. Guided by the Minority Stress Theory and the Stress Process Theory, this study seeks to address these gaps by investigating how different types of discrimination experiences relate to depressive symptoms and whether psychological resilience mitigates these associations across racially and ethnically diverse aging populations. Specifically, the study aims to (1) identify distinct typologies of discrimination experiences among older adults across racial and ethnic groups, (2) examine their associations with depressive symptoms, and (3) explore the moderating role of psychological resilience across racial/ethnic backgrounds. Beyond reaffirming that discrimination is harmful for mental health, this study advances the literature by (a) using a person-centered latent class approach to identify subgroups of older adults who experience distinct patterns of multi-dimensional discrimination, instead of the common approach of treating discrimination as unidimensional and additive, and (b) testing whether psychological resilience buffers specific discrimination profiles differently across racial/ethnic groups. By elucidating these dynamics, this study contributes to a more nuanced understanding of which patterned discrimination experiences are most strongly linked to depressive symptoms within each racial/ethnic group. The findings may inform targeted interventions aimed at promoting mental health equity for racially and ethnically diverse older adults experiencing discrimination.

Methods

Data and sample

The study uses data from the Health and Retirement Study (HRS), a nationally representative survey of U.S. adults aged 50 and older, along with their spouse or partner, conducted by the Institute for Social Research at the University of Michigan. The HRS examines various aspects of aging, including socioeconomic status, health and mental health, and household structures. Once recruited, respondents are followed biannually, and the sample is refreshed every six years with a younger cohort to ensure sample representativeness (Sonnega et al., 2014).

The present study draws on data from the 2016 to 2018 HRS waves for the following practical and theoretical reasons. The 2016 and 2018 waves represent the most recent pair of waves with replenished samples. More importantly, the 2016/18 data represent the most recent pre-COVID-19 data. Arguably, the COVID-19 pandemic substantially disrupted discrimination experiences (e.g., via heightened racial/ethnic tensions, health-related biases, and social isolation), resilience processes, and mental health trajectories in later life. Using pre-pandemic data allow us to establish a cleaner benchmark for associations among discrimination typologies, perceived resilience, and depressive symptoms, unconfounded by these COVID-related disruptions. The key variables, such as discrimination experiences and psychological resilience, were drawn from the HRS Psychological Leave-Behind Questionnaire (LBQ), a self-report mail-in psychosocial survey. In each wave, approximately half of the participants received the LBQ upon the completion of the in-person core survey, with the other half completing it in the subsequent wave. The eligible LBQ respondents from the 2016 wave were stacked with those from the adjacent wave year, 2018, to create a full sample. The final analytic sample consists of 11,832 participants who provided valid responses to all the key variables.

Measures

Depressive symptoms (dependent variable)

The Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D) was used to assess depressive symptoms, with higher scores indicating greater severity. CES-D included eight items to indicate symptoms such as sadness, loneliness, and restless sleep. Two items were reverse-coded to ensure higher scores indicate more depressive symptoms across all items. Responses were then averaged with a range of 0–8. The Cronbach’s alpha of the scale in our sample is 0.80.

Discrimination experiences (independent variable)

Discrimination experiences were measured based on self-reports to the following six items: (1) being treated with less courtesy or respect, (2) receiving poorer service at restaurants or stores, (3) people acting as if they think the respondent is not smart, (4) people acting as if they are afraid of the respondent, (5) being threatened or harassed, and (6) receiving poorer service from doctors or hospitals. All responses were binary (yes/no).

Psychological resilience (moderator)

Following the validated instrument and coding scheme in previous research (Du et al., 2025; Taylor et al., 2024), psychological resilience was measured by a 12-item index, Simplified Resilience Score (Manning et al., 2016) assessing five domains, including perseverance despite setbacks, adaptability to change, sense of purpose in life, recognition of inner strengths, and realization that some experiences must be faced alone (existential loneliness). Qualitative work suggests comparability (i.e., common attributes and themes in resilience aptitudes, identities, and processes) across gender and race in later life (Manning & Bouchard, 2020; Taylor et al., 2024). Responses to each item were measured on either a 7-point or 6-point Likert scale. Five items were reverse-coded so that higher scores indicated greater resilience across items. Each item was first standardized to a 0–1 range to account for differences in response scales, and the standardized values were summed to create a total score ranging from 1.93 to 12. The Cronbach’s alpha of the scale in our sample is 0.85.

Race and ethnicity (sample stratifier)

HRS respondents were asked to report their race (White/Caucasian, Black/African American, or Other [includes American Indian, Alaska Native, Asian, Native Hawaiian, and Pacific Islander] and whether they were of Hispanic ethnicity. Following previous research (Wang, Lou, et al., 2025), a combined race/ethnicity variable was created with four categories: non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, and non-Hispanic Other. Because the ‘Other’ category aggregates multiple distinct racial groups in the public HRS race variable, it is heterogeneous; however, we retained it to avoid erasing these respondents entirely, while interpreting findings for this group as exploratory and emphasizing the need for subgroup-disaggregated research in the future. Race/ethnicity was used to stratify the sample for subgroup analyses.

Control variables

We included a set of sociodemographic and health covariates that may confound the relationship between discrimination and depressive symptoms. Sociodemographic variables include age (in years), gender (1 = female), marital status (1 = married), years of education, household income in the previous calendar year, and immigration background (1 = foreign-born). Household income, cleaned and calculated by RAND, represents the combined total of respondents’ and spouses’ earnings, pensions and annuities, Supplemental Security Income (SSI) and Social Security Disability, Social Security Retirement, unemployment and workers’ compensation, other government transfers, household capital income, and other income sources. Health variables include self-reported health (1 = poor to 5 = excellent), number of difficulties in activities of daily living (ADLs; range 0–5; including bathing, dressing, eating, getting in/out of bed, and walking across a room), number of difficulties in instrumental activities of daily living (IADLs; range 0–5; including using the phone, managing money, taking medications, shopping for groceries, and preparing hot meals), and number of chronic conditions (range 0–8: hypertension, diabetes, cancer, chronic lung disease, heart disease or heart attack, stroke, emotional, nervous or psychiatric problems, and arthritis or rheumatism) that may influence quality of life and mental well-being.

Data analysis

All analyses were conducted using a sample stratified by race/ethnicity, recognizing systematic differences in discrimination experiences and daily living across groups. Data analyses were performed in Stata. Within each racial/ethnic subsample, LCA was conducted using the gsem package to identify typologies of discrimination experiences based on six dichotomous indicators. The optimal number of classes was determined using multiple fit indices, such as Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and entropy, as well as interpretability. Lower AIC and BIC values indicated better model fit, whereas higher entropy reflected better classification quality (Nylund et al., 2007). After determining the number of classes, each respondent was assigned to a class based on maximum likelihood estimation. Then, separate multivariate linear regressions were estimated within each subsample (non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, and Other) to examine the relationship between discrimination typologies and depressive symptoms as well as the moderating effect of resilience. Moderation was tested using an interaction term between class membership and resilience score. There was no missing data for the LCA indicators. A small proportion of missing values in covariates (1.6%) was addressed by listwise deletion in the regression analysis.

Last, ad hoc power analyses were conducted to assess the ability to detect interaction effects between latent discrimination class membership and psychological resilience on mental health outcomes. In addition, to evaluate whether the moderation analyses satisfied the assumption of adequate common support, diagnostic procedures were conducted following the recommendations by Hainmueller and colleagues (Hainmueller et al., 2019), using the interflex package in Stata. These analyses were conducted to reduce potential extrapolation beyond the observed data range and to assess the robustness of the estimated interaction effects.

Results

Sample characteristics

Table 1 presents descriptive statistics for the study sample (N = 11,832), consisting of 65% non-Hispanic White, 18% non-Hispanic Black, 13% Hispanic, and 4% Other participants (including Asian, Native American, and other minoritized groups). Significant group differences were observed across all sociodemographic (e.g., age, gender, education, and household income) and health-related variables (e.g., self-rated health, number of chronic conditions, ADLs, and IADLs). Among all the racial/ethnic groups, non-Hispanic White participants were the oldest, had the highest education levels and household income, and reported the best self-rated health, the fewest ADLs and IADL limitations, the highest resilience scores, and the fewest depressive symptoms. In contrast, non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic participants had lower levels of education, household income, and perceived health status. Participants in the “Other” group were the youngest and reported the fewest chronic conditions, yet had the lowest resilience and relatively high depressive symptoms. Immigration status also varied substantially, with a majority of Hispanic (61%) and Other (42%) participants being immigrants, compared with only 4% of Whites and 5% of Blacks.

Latent classes of discrimination experiences

LCA identified three distinctive patterns of discrimination experiences across all racial/ethnic groups (Table 2). Class 1 (Low Discrimination), the largest group across race/ethnicity, was characterized by very low probabilities (<.010) on all six discrimination items, representing individuals who reported few or no discrimination experiences. Class 2 (Microaggressions) was defined by high probabilities of interpersonal disrespect (.80–.82) and moderate but significantly elevated probabilities of being perceived as less intelligent (.55–.78) and receiving poorer service in restaurants or stores (.35–.53) compared with the Low Discrimination class. This pattern reflects individuals experiencing frequent microinsults and microinvalidations but not overt discrimination, such as threats, harassment, or systemic mistreatment in institutional settings. Class 3 (Pervasive and Threatening Discrimination) exhibited high probabilities across all [Class 2] items as well as the most overt and threatening experiences, such as being treated as though others were afraid of you, being threatened or harassed, or being discriminated against in healthcare settings. Model fit statistics for the two- to five-class LCA solutions across all subsamples are reported in Supplementary Table S1.

While Class 1 was the most common for all groups, its prevalence varied starkly. It encompassed the vast majority of Non-Hispanic White (72%) and Hispanic (74%) older adults. In contrast, Non-Hispanic Black and Other race participants were disproportionately concentrated in the more severe classes. Only 63% of Non-Hispanic Black and 59% of Other race participants were in the “low discrimination” class (C1), with correspondingly higher proportions experiencing C2 (30% and 35%, respectively) and Class 3 (7% and 6%). In other words, Non-Hispanic Black and Other participants were more likely than Whites to belong to the microaggression and pervasive and threatening discrimination classes, highlighting persistent racial and ethnic disparities in both the frequency and intensity of discriminatory experiences. Notably, Hispanic and Other participants reported particularly high probabilities of receiving poorer medical care.

Regression results

Table 3 and Figure 1 present the moderation effects of psychological resilience on the association between discrimination experiences and depressive symptoms across racial and ethnic groups. Overall, the main effects showed that greater exposure to discrimination, whether in the form of microaggressions (Class 2) or pervasive and threatening discrimination (Class 3), was significantly associated with higher depressive symptoms. Specifically, microaggressions were linked to more depressive symptoms among non-Hispanic White (b = 1.40, p < .001), Black (b = 0.79, p < .05), and Hispanic (b = 1.61, p < .001) participants, whereas pervasive and threatening discrimination was significantly related to higher depressive symptoms among Whites (b = 2.02, p <.001) and those in the Other category (b = 2.17, p < .05).

Higher psychological resilience was consistently related to fewer depressive symptoms across all groups (p < .001). However, its moderating effects varied. Among Whites, resilience significantly buffered the negative effects of both microaggressions (C2 × resilience: b = −0.14, p < .001) and pervasive and threatening discrimination (C3 × resilience: b = −0.19, p < .001) on depressive symptoms. Among Hispanics, the buffering effect was evident for microaggressions only (C2 × resilience: b = −0.14, p < .05), while for Black and Other participants, no significant moderation effects were observed.

Post hoc power analyses were conducted to check if the small sample sizes for certain groups (e.g., “Other” respondents in C3) are statistically powered to detect significant interactions with plausible effect sizes. Assuming a conventional alpha of .05 and 80% power, detecting a small interaction effect (Cohen’s f2 = 0.02) would require at least ∼130 participants per group. While the total C3 sample (n = 530) meets this threshold overall, the smallest group (“Other,” n = 31) is underpowered to detect small to medium interaction effects. Post-hoc sensitivity analyses indicate that the study is well-powered to detect small-to-medium interaction effects across the three larger groups, but very small effects within the smallest group may remain undetectable. These considerations informed the cautious interpretation of non-significant interaction effects in the results.

Following the diagnostic steps recommended by Hainmueller and colleagues (2019) for multiplicative interaction models, diagnostic plots (Supplementary Figure S1) indicated limited common support at the lower tail of the resilience distribution, particularly for resilience scores below 4, where relatively few observations were available for estimating the marginal effects. Sensitivity analyses using linear spline specifications for resilience in the interaction terms (Supplementary Table S3) suggested that the protective association between psychological resilience and depressive symptoms was not uniform across the resilience distribution. Specifically, the significant buffering effects primarily emerged within the observed common-support region (resilience ≥4), whereas interaction terms in the low-resilience region (<4) were unstable and non-significant. This pattern suggests that estimates in the extreme lower tail of resilience may rely on extrapolation. An additional sensitivity analysis was conducted by re-estimating the models after restricting the sample to the observed common-support region (resilience scores ≥4, representing approximately 96.4% of observations) (Supplementary Table S4). The restricted-sample analyses yielded substantively identical conclusions regarding both the main effects of discrimination classes and the moderating role of resilience. Overall, these diagnostic and sensitivity analyses support the robustness of the primary findings, as the substantive moderation effects remained evident within the well-supported resilience range (4–12), which included the vast majority of the sample. Taken together, the results suggest that the buffering role of resilience was robust within the observed data-support region and was not driven solely by sparse observations at the extreme low tail of the resilience distribution.

Discussion

This study utilized LCA to identify distinct patterns of discrimination experiences and examined the moderating role of psychological resilience in the link between these experiences and depressive symptoms among a nationally representative sample of U.S. adults. Our findings advance research on discrimination and later-life mental health by moving beyond the common practice of treating a unidimensional exposure (e.g., a single summed or averaged frequency score) and instead adopting a person-centered approach that captures the multidimensional patterning of discriminatory encounters. Using LCA, we identified three qualitatively distinct typologies, underscoring the heterogeneity of discriminatory experiences. The majority of individuals (C1) report minimal exposure to discrimination (59%–74%), suggesting that not all individuals encounter frequent or severe discriminatory events. In contrast, the microaggressions group (C2) highlights the prevalence of subtle, everyday slights that affect a substantial minority (23%–35%). The pervasive and threatening discrimination group (C3), though smaller (3%–7%), experiences discrimination across multiple domains, reflecting a more severe and chronic stressor. These typologies align with prior research indicating that discrimination varies in form and intensity (Sue et al., 2007). Moreover, the distinct discrimination typologies vary across racial/ethnic groups, with non-Hispanic White individuals predominantly experiencing milder or no discrimination, in contrast to racial/ethnic minorities who reported a spectrum from minimal to pervasive and threatening forms. These results align with prior research documenting that racial and ethnic minoritized groups reported more exposure to discrimination compared to Whites (Williams, Lawrence, Davis, et al., 2019). Also consistent with prior research, which highlighted the pervasive nature of discrimination experienced by minoritized groups, microaggressions, subtle, often ambiguous forms of bias, were prevalent across all groups. However, experiences of pervasive and threatening discrimination (e.g., being harassed or denied fair medical treatment) occurred more frequently among minoritized populations (Sue et al., 2007; Williams et al., 2019). The elevated prevalence of poor medical service or treatment reported by Hispanic and Other groups may reflect additional barriers, such as language challenges, limited health literacy, or implicit and explicit bias in clinical interactions (Gee & Ford, 2011; Lauderdale et al., 2006). These disparities highlight the need for culturally and linguistically responsive healthcare environments for older adults from diverse backgrounds.

This study also examined how these patterned experiences relate to depressive symptoms within racial/ethnic groups. Regression results confirmed that discrimination is a significant risk factor for depressive symptoms, consistent with the Stress Process Model’s assertion that stressors exacerbate mental health challenges. However, the specificity of its effect varies by racial/ethnic group and discrimination class. For White participants, both microaggressions (C2) and pervasive and threatening discrimination (C3) are linked to increased depressive symptoms, perhaps because such experiences violate expectations of social privilege and belonging. In contrast, for Black and Hispanic individuals, only microaggressions (C2) are associated with higher depressive symptoms, whereas pervasive and threatening discrimination (C3) shows no significant effect. This finding may reflect Minority Stress Theory’s premise that marginalized groups develop adaptive coping strategies due to chronic exposure to discrimination, potentially mitigating the mental health impact of severe experiences (Meyer, 2003). Also, for Black and Hispanic individuals, the toll of microaggressions may be more psychologically salient than overt discrimination, possibly due to their frequent and subtle nature, which can erode psychological well-being over time. Moreover, members of historically marginalized groups, such as Black and Hispanic individuals, may interpret overt discrimination as systemic rather than personal, thereby attributing it to systemic biases rather than internalizing it personally, which could explain the lack of association with depression in these groups for Class 3 experiences. In contrast, the “Other” category, potentially encompassing diverse subgroups like Asian, Native American, or multiracial individuals, showed the most pronounced depressive response to pervasive and threatening discrimination (C3). These findings must be interpreted cautiously because they rest on several assumptions about the composition of this heterogeneous 4% subsample. Asian Americans frequently confront “model minority” stereotypes, language barriers, and subtle microaggressions that differ markedly from the historical trauma, sovereignty issues, and overt exclusion experienced by Native American respondents (Hartmann et al., 2019; Yang & Woo, 2025). Without knowing the exact proportions of each subgroup within the HRS “Other” category, we cannot determine whether the elevated C3–depression link is driven primarily by one population, another, or their combination. The observed pattern therefore serves as an important signal that severe discrimination may be especially detrimental among these understudied older adults, but the precise mechanisms and effect sizes remain unknown until larger, disaggregated datasets become available. This limitation underscores a core contribution of the present study: even a small, aggregated “Other” group reveals discrimination patterns that would have remained invisible if the analyses had been restricted to the three largest racial/ethnic categories, illustrating the importance of subgroup-specific research for developing more tailored program and practice recommendations.

These regression findings help clarify heterogeneity in the observed psychological associations: not all discriminatory experience clusters “carry” the same mental health risk for depressive symptoms across groups. However, and importantly, these results should not be interpreted as suggesting discrimination is inconsequential for minoritized populations. Extensive prior research documents the profound physiological wear-and-tear (often described as “weathering”) associated with chronic discrimination, particularly among Black Americans and other minoritized groups (Erving et al., 2021; Thorpe et al., 2016; Williams, Lawrence, & Davis, 2019).

Psychological resilience emerged as a significant protective factor against depressive symptoms, consistent with evidence that resilience mitigates stress-related outcomes (Da Silva-Sauer et al., 2021). Yet, its buffering role was not uniform, contingent on both the type of discrimination exposure and racial/ethnic context: for White individuals, resilience buffers the impact of both microaggressions (C2) and pervasive and threatening discrimination (C3), by reducing depressive symptoms; and for Hispanic individuals, resilience attenuates depressive symptoms only in the microaggressions group (C2), not in the pervasive and threatening discrimination group (C3). Notably, resilience did not serve as a buffer for Black or Other groups, suggesting that individual-level psychological resilience may be insufficient against entrenched structural discrimination in these populations. For example, the same positive self-talk or cognitive reframing skills that might help a White older adult cope with a rude encounter in a store may do little to protect a Black older adult’s mental health from the cumulative impact of a lifetime of such experiences. This disparity may stem from cultural differences in the conceptualization of resilience or the cumulative burden of racism, in which systemic interventions might be more effective than personal coping strategies (Clark et al., 2022; Utsey et al., 2007). For Black and Other minoritized older adults, resilience might manifest collectively (e.g., through racial identity, faith, or community engagement) rather than as an individual psychological trait, which may not be fully captured by the measure used. For instance, the cultural practice among some older Asian adults of enduring hardship through quiet forbearance and focusing on family success can be a source of strength. Therefore, Black and Other individuals may rely on community-based or culturally specific resilience strategies that are more effective against everyday slights and systemic discrimination (Jacob et al., 2023; Wang, Zhang, et al., 2025). The non-significant interaction effects between pervasive and threatening discrimination (C3) and psychological resilience among Non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, and Other respondents should be interpreted with caution. Given the relatively small number of individuals in this class across these groups, the analyses were underpowered to detect moderation effects of plausible magnitude; thus, these null findings are best viewed as inconclusive rather than indicative of the absence of a buffering effect. Assuming sufficient statistical power, the non-significant findings may reflect the possibility that pervasive and threatening discrimination limits the observable protective effects of individual psychological resilience. This observation underscores the potential limitations of individual-level coping and highlights the importance of structural interventions to address systemic inequities.

These results have implications for practice and policy. While individual-level resilience interventions remain valuable, the differential buffering effects of psychological resilience observed, strong across both microaggressions (C2) and pervasive/threatening discrimination (C3) for non-Hispanic Whites, partial (C2 only) for Hispanics, and absent for Black and Other participants, highlight the limitations of relying primarily on personal coping strategies against entrenched structural stressors. Mindfulness-based interventions, increasingly embraced in social sciences for their potential to buffer discrimination-related depressive symptoms (Shallcross & Spruill, 2018; Watson-Singleton et al., 2019), warrant targeted adaptation and testing in older adults.

To address the lack of buffering against pervasive/threatening discrimination (C3) in minoritized groups, where individual resilience appears insufficient, policy should prioritize upstream structural interventions that reduce exposure to severe, multi-domain discrimination at its source. These include mandating routine audits and accountability mechanisms within healthcare systems to eliminate poorer treatment or service (as observed elevated in Hispanic/Other participants) through ensuring equitable geriatric care delivery (e.g., culturally/linguistically responsive mental wellbeing assessments and mental health integration in primary care for older adults). Innovative organizational strategies could involve embedding discrimination typologies and resilience screening into routine geriatric assessments to enable tailored referrals: connecting microaggression-exposed groups to mindfulness programs, while linking pervasive/threatening cases to structural advocacy resources (e.g., community-legal partnerships or anti-racism coalitions in aging services).

Ultimately, broader equity-focused policies should promote intersectional approaches that simultaneously tackle racism and ageism in later-life mental health. Examples include training providers to monitor discrimination-related depressive symptoms via standardized screening and funding programs that integrate community-led resilience-building (e.g., older adults’ storytelling circles rooted in cultural pride for Black, Asian, Native American participants or familism-enhanced support groups for Hispanics) with policy advocacy training to empower older adults as agents of systemic change.

Several limitations should be noted. The measures of discrimination and resilience were self-reported, potentially subject to recall bias, and may not capture the full range of lived experiences. Additionally, the small sample size and the heterogeneity of the “Other” category limit generalizability and interpretability. The HRS measured race by collapsing multiple populations into a single “Other” category (e.g., Asian, American Indian/Alaska Native, and Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander), preventing subgroup-specific inference; thus, results for this category should be interpreted as generative for future disaggregated analysis. Third, although cross-sectional study design was well suited to the present study’s focus on between-person differences in discrimination patterning and resilience’s conditional role at a single point in time, future research should adopt longitudinal designs to examine within-person changes in the dynamics of discrimination experiences, resilience, and mental health over time, especially across a period that included the onset of the global COVID-19 pandemic, which would introduced substantial exogenous disruptions in longitudinal trajectories. Last, examining specific cultural or contextual factors, such as spirituality, community solidarity, and familism, that shape resilience across racial/ethnic groups could help better understand group differences and further elucidate the observed differences.

Despite the limitations, this study extends the literature in several ways. First, the identification of three distinct discrimination classes, rather than relying on frequency alone, reveals qualitative differences in exposure patterns. Second, the differential moderation findings highlight group-specific protective effects of resilience and contribute to the current knowledge body, as prior studies on resilience and discrimination have rarely stratified moderation tests across multiple racial/ethnic groups. Third, the inclusion of the “Other” category (4% of the analytic sample) addresses a gap in representation. By retaining this heterogeneous group rather than dropping it, the analysis gives voice to populations that are routinely erased in aging research and reveals discrimination–mental health patterns that would otherwise remain unknown. At the same time, every interpretive statement about “Other” respondent is assumption-based, given the distinct discrimination, identity, and political realities faced by its constituent subgroups (e.g., Asian Americans vs Native Americans). This approach highlights the need for future disaggregated analyses when larger samples or targeted oversamples become available to better specify which subpopulations are most affected and under what conditions.

In conclusion, this study identified distinct patterns of everyday discrimination among older adults from four major racial/ethnic groups and examined the moderating role of psychological resilience in the association between discrimination and depressive symptoms. These findings, interpreted through the lens of the Stress Process Model and the Minority Stress Theory, offer critical insights into the complex interplay between discrimination, resilience, and mental health outcomes across different racial/ethnic groups. The findings underscore the need for tailored, equity-focused interventions to combat discrimination’s toll on psychological well-being for a diverse aging population in the United States.
