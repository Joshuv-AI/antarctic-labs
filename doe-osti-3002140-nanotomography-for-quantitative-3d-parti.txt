 ABSTRACT

Particulates are ubiquitous across fuel cycle operations and carry critical information about particle
formation, processing, and potential proliferation-related activities. Traditional analytical techniques,
including micro-Raman spectroscopy and standard electron microscopy, are often limited in spatial
resolution or dimensionality, particularly when used to examine metallic or submicron-scale features.
Understanding particle morphology, phase distribution, and internal porosity is essential for constraining
formation conditions, thermodynamic environments, and material transport behavior.

In this report, we demonstrate the application of plasma focused ion beam nanotomography to reconstruct
micron-scale particulates at nanoscale resolution. Using high-resolution backscattered electron imaging
and Avizo software, we obtained 3D reconstructions that enabled quantitative analysis of particle
morphology, phase composition, and internal voids. Representative examples include a Ta particle with a
large central void and a composite particle with embedded tetrahedral crystalline structures. These
reconstructions reveal structural and compositional details that are inaccessible through conventional 2D
imaging.

The results demonstrate that nanotomography provides both qualitative and quantitative insights into
particle formation and behavior. Using nanotomography, porosity and phase distributions can be
quantified to inform models of particle density, transport, and solidification conditions. Beyond technical
insights, the workflow developed here establishes a transferable capability for analyzing heterogeneous
particles and has potential applications in bulk materials studies via x-ray computed tomography or other
volumetric imaging modalities.

Ongoing efforts are focused on optimizing the workflow to process multiple particles simultaneously,
increasing throughput and statistical robustness. Overall, this work illustrates the power of
nanotomography as a tool for connecting particulate morphology to formation mechanisms, composition,
and transport, thereby strengthening analytical capabilities for nuclear forensics, fuel cycle analysis, and
related scientific investigations.

 1. BACKGROUND

Particulates are pervasive across fuel cycle operations, and particulate analysis can provide valuable
insights into conversion-, production-, and proliferation-related activities. For isotopically unperturbed
particles, analysis must rely on chemical and physical characterization—often requiring micron- or
submicron-scale spatial resolution. These properties are governed by the conditions under which the
particles formed, placing constraints on the thermodynamic environment at their origin. Understanding
the stability ranges of specific chemical phases can significantly narrow the parameter space for
simulations that aim to reconstruct formation conditions. Building this foundational knowledge not only
clarifies formation mechanisms but also strengthens attribution efforts, even as operational and
environmental contexts become increasingly complex. Metallic particulates present unique challenges.
Although micro-Raman spectroscopy is widely used for micron-scale chemical analysis, it is ineffective
for characterizing submicron features and metallic phases. In such cases, characterization beyond mass
spectrometry is typically limited to electron microscopy and related techniques.

In this report, we demonstrate the use of nanotomography to analyze collected particulates. We outline a
complete workflow that enables particle down-selection and present representative reconstructed
particles. This approach addresses a key limitation of traditional electron microscopy, which provides
only 2D snapshots and lacks the ability to capture the full 3D morphology of particulates.
Nanotomography, by contrast, enables volumetric reconstruction, making it particularly valuable for

analyzing heterogeneous particles. It enables quantification of the volume fractions of all relevant phases
and assessment of internal void content. Together, these insights provide critical constraints on particle
formation mechanisms and transport behavior.

 2. PARTICLE RECONSTRUCTION: GENERAL DESCRIPTION

2.1 GENERAL CONCEPT

The general concept of particle or material tomography is consistent across applications: a series of 2D
images are collected at regular thickness or rotation intervals throughout the entire particle or substrate,
and these images are used to reconstruct a 3D volume. Variations in image contrast arise from differences
in composition, crystallographic orientation, or density, and these variations enable features to be tracked
across successive slices. The achievable spatial resolution is governed by both the imaging method and
slice thickness; finer steps and higher-resolution imaging yield more precise reconstructions. In this
report, we targeted nanoscale resolution for micron-scale particulates by combining electron microscopy
imaging with focused ion beam (FIB) milling. This approach leverages the precise, layer-by-layer
removal of material afforded by the ion beam together with high-resolution electron imaging of each
newly exposed surface to generate detailed 3D reconstructions of particulate structures. Because the
underlying principles of tomography are general, expertise developed for particulate analysis is readily
transferable to bulk materials—for example, through the use of x-ray computed tomography for larger
sample volumes.

2.2 FOCUSED ION BEAM MILLING

For the work presented herein, we primarily employed a plasma-FIB using Xe ions, recently acquired
through an institutional capital equipment procurement. This system also supports an O ion source that is
optimized for milling carbon-based materials and may be leveraged in future studies involving organic or
polymeric matrices. The plasma-FIB system provides state-of-the-art software that is readily integrated
into nanotomography workflows. Using built-in automation, nanoscale slices (typically, 50 nm) are
removed sequentially across either a portion or the entirety of a probed particle. Compared to
conventional Ga-FIB systems, the plasma-FIB Xe source enables faster milling rates, larger accessible
volumes, and reduced ion implantation artifacts (Ga and Al are wholly incompatible, making Ga-FIB
unusable for Al-bearing materials), making it well suited for tomographic studies of micron-scale
particulates.

After each milling step, high-resolution backscattered electron (BSE) images are collected with a built-in
scanning electron microscope (SEM). In these images, contrast arises from atomic number differences,
with higher-Z phases appearing brighter. The resulting image stack is then transferred to a reconstruction
workstation, where the voxel size is defined by the SEM imaging parameters and the milling step size. To
enable more definitive phase identification, backscatter-derived brightness variations can be correlated
with compositional information from energy-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy or with crystallographic
information by performing electron diffraction on locally thinned regions of the particle.

As with all FIB-based approaches, some limitations must be considered, including potential artifacts (e.g.,
curtaining), redeposition of milled material, charging of nonconductive samples, and sample damage from
prolonged ion exposure. These effects can typically be mitigated through optimized milling parameters,
protective coatings, or post-processing of image data.

2.3 NANOTOMOGRAPHY

Several commercial software packages are available for particle reconstruction. Within this laboratory
directed research and development project, both DragonFly and Avizo were evaluated, and Avizo
(Thermo Fisher Scientific) was ultimately selected because of its stability, documentation, and broad
adoption in tomography workflows.

The reconstruction process begins with importing a stack of image slices (typically in .tiff format) into
Avizo, where they are treated as a single object within the “Project” workroom. The built-in FIB Stack
Wizard guides preparation of the dataset by cropping the field of view to include the particle(s) and
fiducial marks (see the large “X” mark in the image within Figure 1), performing slice-to-slice alignment
(usually via an automatic least squares method), cropping again to include only the particle(s) of interest,
and applying optional shear corrections. Each step generates a new image stack, enabling iterative
refinement if adjustments are needed. To reduce graininess and facilitate segmentation, a median filter is
often applied at this stage. Figure 1 shows an example of this workflow in the Avizo program.

Segmentation is carried out in the “Segmentation” workroom by assigning voxels to different materials
that correspond to distinct phases. The most common approach uses threshold-based segmentation, in
which minimum and maximum brightness values are selected to isolate regions of interest. Thresholds
can be refined with built-in tools for shrinking/growing selections, smoothing boundaries, filling holes, or
manually drawing regions. As each segmented phase is added to a material label, overlaps with existing
phases can be excluded to ensure accurate partitioning of the volume.

For relatively simple particle morphologies, this workflow provides reliable results. However, complex
morphologies or phases with similar brightness values often require more advanced segmentation
strategies. Avizo includes additional methods such as watershed segmentation and AI-assisted
segmentation; the latter allows users to train a model to recognize specific phases or morphologies. This
process can significantly improve phase separation in challenging datasets.

2.4 PARTICLE IDENTIFICATION

One of the key challenges in high-value particulate analysis is accurately identifying which particulates
are truly of interest. To address this issue, we have integrated downstream single-particle characterization
capabilities with automated particle analysis (APA). APA enables rapid, high-throughput screening of
more than 20,000 particles per day by scanning substrates for user-defined features and capturing low-
resolution morphological and compositional data for each detected particle. Crucially, the spatial
coordinates of each particle are recorded, enabling precise relocation of particles identified as high-value
(e.g., particles with the target size, morphology, or elemental composition). These selected particles are
then subjected to nanotomography for detailed 3D characterization.

 3. PARTICLE RECONSTRUCTION: APPLICATION TO PARTICULATES

Having established the requirements for particle nanotomography, we now present two examples of
reconstructed particles. Figure 2 shows BSE images from various time stamps of a particle primarily
composed of Ta. Visually, the particle appears uniform, but a large central void is evident in its interior.
Using Avizo and the methods outlined in Section 2, we reconstruct this particle. The particle
reconstruction clearly shows a large central void (purple region) embedded within the particle. Based on
these results, the particle’s porosity can be estimated to be approximately 13% of the total particle
volume. Consequently, the total density of the particle is substantially lower than that of a fully dense
particle, and this characteristic would have a significant influence on particle transport properties. This
example demonstrates that quantitative volumetric information can be extracted from a reconstructed
particle, directly informing particle collection and transport models.

However, the particle appears slightly egg-shaped, and this feature is inconsistent with the 2D images
collected before FIB milling. This distortion arises because no consistent fiducial mark was used to aid in
reconstruction. In all subsequent examples, an “X” mark was placed next to the particle or particle string
to improve alignment accuracy.

Figure 3 shows a particle composed of Ta and steel. Again, several slices are shown from the SEM
images. Although SEM images alone provide qualitative insights, only reconstruction reveals the true 3D
morphology. The particle reconstruction shows a region crystallized in tetrahedral structures embedded
within an otherwise spherical particle. This observation suggests that the tetrahedral regions solidified
within a molten matrix, with cooling conditions sufficient to nucleate and grow microscopic structures.
The volume fraction is determined to be approximately 25% of the total sphere volume. This information
is used to constrain particle density, thereby informing particle transport modeling. These examples
highlight the utility of nanotomography in understanding particle formation conditions and transport
properties.

3.1 MULTIPARTICLE SECTIONING

One of the key barriers to implementing advanced particulate characterization is the significant costs in
terms of time and cost required to extract meaningful information. Although particulate analysis can yield
unrivaled insights, the effort needed to analyze particles individually often renders such techniques
impractical for routine use.

To address this challenge, we developed a method for particle relocation and simultaneous sectioning.
Using a micromanipulator tip or microgripper (Figure 4), strings of particles can be relocated as a unit.
This configuration enables simultaneous sectioning, significantly reducing analysis time and cost per
particle; the maximum benefit is proportional to the number of particles in the string.

Figure 4 demonstrates this capability using a string of 316L steel particles. The particles were relocated
with a microgripper, sectioned together, and imaged simultaneously for aggregate reconstruction. This
approach enables extraction of particle volume, composition, and other metrics at a fraction of the
traditional cost. In our trials, we achieved up to a sevenfold increase in throughput.

being prepared for publication.

 4. CONCLUSIONS

In this work, we demonstrated the application of plasma-FIB nanotomography to reconstruct micron-scale
particulates at nanoscale resolution. Using the Avizo software, we obtained 3D reconstructions that
enabled quantitative analysis of particle morphology, internal porosity, and phase distribution. The first
example highlighted a Ta particle with a significant central void, illustrating how porosity can
substantially reduce particle density and affect transport behavior. The second example revealed

tetrahedral crystalline structures embedded within a spherical particle, showing how 3D reconstruction
provides insights into particle formation conditions that cannot be obtained from 2D SEM images alone.

These results underscore the utility of nanotomography in linking particle structure to key physical
properties, including density and transport characteristics. More broadly, this capability addresses a
central challenge in particulate analysis across the fuel cycle: extracting constraints on formation
conditions and thermodynamic environments from chemical and physical features, even in isotopically
unperturbed particles. By enabling the quantification of phase distributions and internal void content,
nanotomography provides a powerful complement to established chemical methods, offering insights that
are directly relevant to attribution.

Current efforts are focused on scaling the workflow to multiple particles simultaneously, which will
further enhance throughput and statistical confidence. Overall, this work demonstrates that quantitative
3D reconstructions not only advance fundamental understanding of particle formation mechanisms but
also strengthen the analytical tool kit available for nuclear forensics in increasingly complex operational
and environmental contexts.
