Improving laboratory workforce efficiency using AI-assisted digital cytology within an HPV-based cervical screening programme: A model-based evaluation for the NHS Cervical Screening Programmes
Authors: Allan Wilson, Alison Cropper, Yixuan Ma, Elisabeth J Adams, Sue Mehew, Margaret Morgan, Nichole Villeneuve, Caron Mitchell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesWe aimed to estimate the time required to review and report cervical cytology slides within a Human Papillomavirus (HPV)-primary cervical cancer screening programme, where cytology is used for triage following a positive HPV test, comparing Artificial Intelligence (AI)-assisted digital cytology (Genius) with manual microscopy.DesignA decision-tree model of the flow of slides through the National Health Service (NHS) England cervical screening cytology laboratory workflow, parameterised using published evidence and expert input.SettingCytology laboratories within the NHS Cervical Screening Programme in England and Scotland.ResultsScreening and reporting 479,125 cytology slides annually in England was estimated to require 31,842 staff hours with AI-assisted digital cytology versus 103,151 hours with manual microscopy. The mean time per slide review and reporting was 4.0 vs 12.9 minutes, respectively, corresponding to a potential 69% increase in overall productivity.ConclusionsAI-assisted digital cytology can support more resilient cervical screening programmes in the UK and other countries by reducing staff time required for slide review and reporting. These findings may inform policymakers considering strategies to mitigate workforce shortages.

Introduction

Cervical cancer is a largely preventable malignancy, yet it remains the fourth most common cancer in women worldwide, with around 660,000 new cases and 350,000 deaths reported in 2022.1 In the UK, around 3,300 new cervical cancer cases are diagnosed annually.2 The primary aim of cervical screening is to prevent cervical cancer by identifying and treating high-grade cervical precancer (CIN2+) before progression to invasive disease.3

The NHS Cervical Screening Programmes use a high-risk HPV (HR-HPV) primary screening approach. Cervical samples are first tested for HPV, and samples with a positive HPV result are then processed for cytology.4 During cytology, slides are reviewed to detect any cellular abnormalities, which result in women being referred for colposcopy.

Routine invitations are offered to women and people with a cervix aged 25–64. From July 2025, NHS England moved to a 5-year recall interval for those with a negative HPV test for all ages, instead of the previous 3-year interval.5 6 In England, ~3 million HPV tests are done annually, resulting in an estimated ~<500,000 cytology tests (based on the reported HPV-positivity rates7). Over time, widespread HPV vaccination should continue to result in reduced HPV positivity,8 less subsequent cytology demand in younger cohorts, and fewer cervical precancers.9

Delivering HPV-based screening at scale depends on timely cytology triage and reporting by a skilled laboratory workforce. However, high cytologist vacancy rates, and an ageing workforce are straining service capacity. In England, Scotland and Wales, 14% of Cytoscreener, Biomedical Scientist, and Pathologist positions involved in cytology screening were unfilled in 2024 (60/426).10 This insufficient workforce risks an increasing backlog in reporting slides, delayed results and compromises service delivery.

AI-assisted digital cytology can improve laboratory productivity by reducing the time required to review and report slides. The Genius Digital Diagnostics System is an AI-enabled platform that supports (rather than replaces) human screening by prioritising areas of interest for review, with final interpretation and reporting remaining human-led.11 Genuis uses digitised liquid-based cytology slides and employs volumetric imaging technology designed to generate high-resolution, in-focus images from multiple focal planes. It has equivalent performance (sensitivity and specificity) to standard manual microscopy,11 12 and hence no other impact on cases diagnosed or onward care is anticipated.

This study evaluates the potential impact of introducing Genius on the laboratory workforce time required for cytology screening compared with manual microscopy in the UK, using a decision-tree model of the NHS Cervical Screening Programme laboratory workflow in England as an example informed by published sources and expert input.

Methods

Model overview

A decision tree model was developed to represent the cytology screening workflow in laboratories in England over a 1-year period, and was built to follow the National Health Service Cervical Screening Programme (NHSCSP) guidelines.13 14 The model was implemented in Microsoft Excel and simulates 479,125 HPV-positive samples requiring cytology triage annually within the NHSCSP (estimated from the total number of women tested for HPV annually × percentage of women with positive HR-HPV requiring cytology screening; Table 1).

Two pathways were modelled: (i) manual liquid-based cytology (current standard of care) and (ii) AI-assisted digital cytology using Genius. The model assumes that the overall workflow structure and diagnostic performance are equivalent between pathways, with differences limited to time required for slide screening and review.

Model structure

The model structure reflects current NHSCSP laboratory practice: all slides undergo primary screening; abnormal slides are reviewed by a first checker (senior Biomedical Scientist), and negative slides by rapid review. A subset with discrepant results may be reviewed by a second checker. Slides with confirmed or suspected abnormalities are sent for final consultant reporting (Figure 1). Time and labour are included for slide movement through these stages; pre-analytical steps (including digitally scanning slides) were excluded.

Model inputs and data sources

Model input parameters included screening volumes, probabilities of abnormality and pathway transitions across workflow stages, staff time per activity for manual and digital pathways, operational constraints (eg., working patterns and screening limits), and process-related times such as slide movement within the laboratory (Table 1). Data for the base case and uncertainty analysis were obtained from published sources and several NHS Trust laboratories, including a service evaluation. Consultants from four laboratories (named co-authors; University Hospitals of Derby and Burton NHS Trust laboratory; North Bristol NHS Trust laboratory; University Hospital Monklands Laboratory; Health Services Laboratories) participated in workshops to validate model structure, key inputs and assumptions.

Outcomes and analysis

Model outcomes were annual staff time (hours) required for screening and reporting. Most inputs were varied in an uncertainty analysis to assess their impact on outcomes and explore key assumptions (Table 1: high and low values), compared to in the base case. A scenario was run assuming a different workflow in Scotland, keeping all other inputs the same as in the base case (online supplemental figure S1).

Patient and public involvement

None.

Results

Screening and reporting 479,125 cytology slides annually would require 31,842 staff hours with Genius, versus 103,151 hours using manual methods (Figure 2, Table 2). The average time to review and report per slide (including negative and abnormal) was 4.0 minutes (Genius) and 12.9 minutes (manual microscopy).

Discussion

Principal findings

Implementing Genius could significantly reduce the time required for staff to screen and report slides, thereby improving workforce productivity and alleviating chronic staffing shortages. This is the first study in the UK to compare AI-assisted digital cytology using Genius with manual microscopy screening workflows. These results are similar to those from studies from the United States and Germany, which also found that Genius reduces the average time needed to screen a cytology slide by ~50%.12 15

Strengths and weaknesses of the study

This study has several strengths and limitations. For this paper to reflect best-practice standards and generalisable results, the model workflow was based on published guidelines.13 14 This gives a transparent decision-tree model that reflects the NHS cervical screening laboratory workflow. It was populated with evidence from the literature and informed and validated by expert input, from a range of laboratories across England and Scotland. Additionally, to explore potential differences in workflow, an alternative workflow in Scotland was modelled in a scenario; this had a marginal impact on results, assuming all other inputs were fixed.

A limitation of the study is that there could be differences in workflow, laboratory organisation, staffing mix and utilisation, and quality assurance practices across laboratories in the UK that were not captured in the model structure or inputs. This may change the magnitude of the impact of implementing Genius per laboratory. This was explored by running uncertainty analyses in which the input parameters were changed and the effect on the results considered, and identified time-to-screen and time-to-report assumptions as the main drivers of results. Therefore, if laboratories had a very different base case time per step, or implemented Genius differently, there may be an impact on results.

Another limitation is that the model focused on staff time for slide review and reporting and did not include wider implementation requirements (eg., slide scanning, IT infrastructure, training, validation, and quality management), nor did it model potential changes in diagnostic performance, abnormal cytology rates, referral patterns, or downstream workload for colposcopy and histopathology. Given that Genius has equivalent performance as manual microscopy,11 12 no impact on onward referral to colposcopy is anticipated.

In addition, a proportion of slides may be unsuitable for digital/AI-assisted analysis and require manual review, which may be expected to reduce overall efficiency gains. This is likely to be low based on the co-authors’ experience (<1%–4%) and a recent study by Harinath et al reported that 2.3% of slides were unreadable using Genius,16 which may impact slightly on the results. Prospective real-world evaluations are needed to confirm time savings in NHS laboratories and to assess broader system impacts, including costs and downstream service capacity.

The implications of the study for clinicians and policymakers

NHS England mandates a 14-day turnaround time from sample collection to receipt of results for 98% of samples as a key performance indicator for the NHSCSP.17 While some laboratories met the target, on average, only 89% of eligible individuals aged 25–64 across England received screening results within 2-weeks (April 2023–March 2024).7 Implementing AI-assisted digital cytology could improve efficiency in the screening pathway, as cytology is the main bottleneck in the process.7

This barrier to efficient reviewing and reporting of samples is largely driven by cytology workforce shortages, which remain a persistent challenge. The vacancy rate is expected to rise to 28% within the next 5 years due to an ageing workforce.10 Pressures on the available workforce now and in the future increase the burden on service capacity, which is likely to cause delays in reporting, results, and overall service quality. In this context, new digital technology such as Genius could potentially help mitigate against workforce vacancies and the associated costs.

However, it is unclear if the demand for cytology will remain constant over time. It is anticipated that with a lower HPV positivity rate in coming years due to the impact of vaccination in younger cohorts,8 there may be fewer samples processed for cytology annually. Alongside potentially reduced demand from fewer HPV-positive samples, there may be an increased number of screening tests done, as HPV self-sampling for under-screened women is rolled out nationally.18 One of the goals of self-sampling is to reach more women who typically do not participate in screening. While the specifics for self-sampling in England, Scotland and Wales are being determined, it is likely that this population will be asked to provide a sample initially for HPV screening, and those with a positive HPV result will be asked to then attend a healthcare provider to have a clinician taken cervical sample for cytology. These additional samples to the overall screening programme may add additional pressure to cytology services; switching to AI-assisted cytology could mitigate the increase in samples through greater efficiency. Empirical evidence from any increased uptake can be used to validate and quantify this hypothesis once self-sampling is fully implemented.

Conclusions

The results from this model-based evaluation suggest that introducing AI-assisted digital cytology such as Genius, into the NHS cervical screening cytology workflow in England, could substantially reduce the laboratory staff time required to review and report cytology slides (31,842 vs 103,151 hours annually for 479,125 slides). These findings offer early evidence to support policy decisions and suggest that AI-assisted digital cytology could help mitigate workforce shortages, improve turnaround times and strengthen service resilience.

However, the magnitude of benefit will depend on local workflows and implementation factors. The model did not capture the potential impact on the wider implementation of this technology such as supporting service standardisation and training, and other benefits, that have been highlighted in a recent paper.19 Prospective real-world studies are needed to confirm time savings in NHS laboratories and to evaluate downstream impacts (eg., reduced backlog of slides), including future changes in the number of slides to be read, driven by HPV vaccination and self-sampling.
