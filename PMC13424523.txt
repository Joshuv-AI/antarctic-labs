Relationship between non-O blood groups and microvascular risk in type 2 diabetes
Authors: Fabian O. Lurquin, Elise L. Petit, Philippe Oriot, Sylvie A. Ahn, Michel P. Hermans
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionABO groups impact macroangiopathy risk in T2D. It is unknown whether such modulation extends to microvascular complications.MethodSingle-centre cross-sectional study including 1,006 T2D patients.ResultsPrevalence of overall microangiopathy was 51%. No significant differences (ANOVA) were observed between ABO groups in terms of age, sex, diabetes duration, BMI, hypertension, chronic kidney disease or diabetic foot. Insulin sensitivity and the hyperbolic HOMA product [BxS] were lower among non-O groups (-20% and -14%, respectively; p 0.001 and 0.013), while HbA1c and lifetime hyperglycaemia exposure were higher (+5% and +25%, respectively; p 0.006 and 0.002). Overall microangiopathy was more prevalent among non-O patients (+23%; p 0.001), as was neuropathy (+44%; p 0.005). Differences across ABO groups were significant for overall microangiopathy (p 0.009) and neuropathy (p 0.027) (ANOVA). In multivariate analysis, group A (vs O) was associated with overall microangiopathy (OR=1.62; 95%CI: 1.18–2.22; p 0.003) after adjustment for diabetes duration, HbA1c, smoking, remnant-C and hypertension.ConclusionT2D patients of blood group A have increased frequency of overall microangiopathy, regardless of other risk factors. Group O subjects exhibit better glucose homeostasis, with better insulin sensitivity and β-cell function. The ABO system therefore impacts both glucose homeostasis and microvascular susceptibility in T2D.

Introduction

Non-O blood groups have been linked to incident vascular complications, such as coronary artery disease (CAD), cerebrovascular disease (CeVD), venous thromboembolism, and peripheral artery disease (PAD) among non-diabetic subjects and type 2 diabetes (T2D) patients, while giving reduced macrovascular risk to patients with O blood group. 1

Despite the abundant literature on the association between ABO blood group, macrovascular disease and incident T2D,1–3 there have been few or conflicting studies on whether or not such an association exists for microvascular disease in T2D.4–10 Neither has there been any studies on the relationship between blood types and the risk of microangiopathy in type 1 diabetes or in secondary diabetes. The pathophysiology of microvascular damage from chronic hyperglycaemia is distinct of macrovascular damage, both in terms of risk factors and mechanisms. This is mainly due to the lack of media and adventitia in microvessels, consequently immune to atherosclerosis irrespective of diabetes type. On the other hand, all vessels share common damaging mechanisms, notably inflammatory-induced processes. An association was reported between ABO gene rs2073823 polymorphism A/A, a minor allele on intron 6, with reduced microvascular complications (diabetic retinopathy (DR) and/or diabetic neuropathy (DN)) in T2D. 11 Moreover, given the reported associations between ABO blood groups and retinal structural parameters (e.g. macular and peripapillary retinal nerve fibres layer thicknesses), one may assume that DR susceptibility may vary according to ABO groups. 12 Cell adhesion molecules (e.g. selectins), markers of microvascular damage in T2D, 13 are linked to ABO locus.14,15 Finally, experimental and clinical data suggest that endothelial glycocalyx injury and von Willebrand factor (vWF)-mediated microthrombosis, may vary depending on ABO blood type.

The aims of the study were to document among T2D patients whether: (i) modulation of vascular risk by ABO blood types extends to microvascular disease; (ii) such an association correlates with known drivers of microangiopathy; and (iii) microvascular risk modulation by ABO groups is global or target organs damage (TOD)-specific, namely DR, DN, diabetic kidney disease (DKD), and diabetic foot (DF).

Patients & methods

This cross-sectional study reviewed electronic medical records of 1006 T2D adults, including 12.5% sub-Saharan Africans (SSA), followed at an academic centre, whose last visit took place between Sept 1st 2022 and Sept 1st 2025. The following were recorded: age, gender, diabetes duration (DD), smoking, weight, height, body mass index (BMI), waistline, hypertension, and blood pressure (BP). Hypertension was defined as systolic/diastolic BP ≥140 and/or/BP ≥90 mmHg, and/or ongoing antihypertensive medication(s). Current therapies included BP-, glucose-lowering therapies (GLT), aspirin, and lipid-modifying drugs (LMD). Main ABO groups O, A, B and AB, total cholesterol (C), HDL-C, TG; LDL-C (Friedewald’s formula), remnant cholesterol (RC; non-HDL-C minus LDL-C) were determined by routine methods, as were HbA1c, fasting glucose and insulin, creatininaemia, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) and albuminuria/creatininuria ratio (ACR). The hyperglycaemia index was computed as the smoothed average of HbA1c >6.5% x diabetes duration (%.yrs).

β-cell function and insulin sensitivity were determined by HOMA (https://www.dtu.ox.ac.uk/), from triplicate fasting glucose and specific insulin obtained after overnight fast and discontinuation of long-acting GLTs for at least two half-lives. Values of insulin secretion ([B]; normal 100%) were plotted as a function of insulin sensitivity ([S]; normal 100%), defining a hyperbolic product area [B×S] (unit: %2; normal: 100% or 104 %2), representing the true, underlying β-cell function. Loss over time of hyperbolic product ([BxS] loss rate [%.year-1]) was obtained by dividing (100%-[BxS]) by participant’s age at HOMA modelling.16,17

Overall microangiopathy was defined by the presence of one or more of DR, DN, DKD, and DF. Previously described methods were used to define DR and DN. 17 DF was defined as past/current neuroarthropathic or neuroischaemic foot ± ulcer(s). Albuminuria was considered for urine ACR (albumin [mg/dL]/creatinine [g/dL]) >30 mg/g. Chronic kidney disease (CKD) included DKD and non-DKD, both defined as decreased estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2), albuminuria or both. Only KD occurring in the course of long-standing hyperglycaemia, not predating T2D, was considered as DKD. Overall macroangiopathy was defined by the presence of one or more of CAD, CeVD and PAD. 18

The study was performed in agreement with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice. The study protocol was approved by the Hospital-Academic Biomedical Ethics Committee (B403/2022/08NOV/416).

Statistical methods

Analyses were performed using STATA (StataCorp. Stata Statistical Software: Release 18. College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC; 2023). Descriptive results are presented as mean ±1 standard deviation (SD), median [interquartile range; IQR], or proportion (%). To assess differences between groups, mean comparison tests were conducted using Student’s t test or ANOVA when more than 2 groups were compared, and Fisher’s exact test for differences in proportions. Multivariate logistic regressions estimated the association between patients’ phenotypes based on ABO blood groups and microvascular disease.

Results

Study population

Mean age was 71 13 years; diabetes duration 20 11 years; and men represented 62%. Current glycaemic control was suboptimal (mean HbA1c 59 10 mmol.mol-1), with mean hyperglycaemia index 33 19 %.yrs (median [perc 25-75] 23 [8-48] %.yrs). Albuminuria and CKD prevalence were 34% and 45%, respectively. Mean insulin sensitivity was markedly decreased, almost by half, with 48% of patients on insulin. Seventy-two percent were on metformin, 32% on sulfonylurea/glinides, 40% on incretin-based therapies (IBT), and 17% on sodium-glucose transporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i). Mean RC was just below the upper limit of normal (30 mg/dL). Most patients (84%) were taking LMD(s) (statins 80%; ezetimibe 37%; and/or fibrates 16%) and 41% took aspirin as antiplatelet agent.

Overall macroangiopathy was 36% (Table 1). Macrovascular TOD prevalence was CAD (24%), CeVD (9%), and PAD (9%). Patients’ characteristics according to overall microangiopathy

Patients with overall microangiopathy (Micro[+]) differed significantly in terms of age (older by a mean 7 yrs in Micro[+]), DD (longer by a mean 8.5 yrs in Micro[+]), lesser residual β-cell function (-12%), increased loss of β-cell function (+8%), and hyperglycaemia index (+84% in Micro[+]) than patients without microangiopathy (Micro[-]). HbA1c was also higher (+0.4%) in Micro[+], who were more likely past/current smokers (+25%) and hypertensive (+11%). Micro[+] patients had significantly more RC (+11%) (not shown).

ABO distribution was analysed according to overall microangiopathy. Group O was markedly less frequent (-20.9%) among Micro[+] (38% vs 48% in Micro[-]; p 0.001). Conversely, group A was much more frequent (+20.6%) among Micro[+] (41% vs 34% in Micro[-]; p 0.010). Group B was 16% in Micro[+] vs 13% in Micro[-] (p 0.235), and group AB was 4.9% (Micro[+]) vs 4.9 (Micro[-] (p 0.968) (Figure 2). ABO distribution according to overall microangiopathy among SSA patients showed that group O was underrepresented (-32.1%) among Micro[+] (44% vs 65%; p 0.0229), while group A was non-significantly more frequent among Micro[+] (+42% vs Micro[-]).

Patients’ phenotypes according to ABO groups: O vs. non-O

There were no significant differences between O and non-O patients, nor across all blood groups, for age, gender, DD, BMI, hypertension, [BxS] loss rate, metformin-sulfonylurea/glinides-IBT or aspirin intakes, hsCRP, eGFR, and CKD. There were significant differences between O and non-O groups and/or between all blood groups as regards SSA patients’ repartition, higher in O and B groups. Smoking was more frequent among group A patients, whereas group AB patients were less likely smokers. Waistline was higher among non-O patients (mean +2 cm). SGLT2i and statin intakes were significantly higher among non-O patients. Age at the time of HOMA testing was similar across blood groups. Insulin sensitivity and hyperbolic product [BxS] were lower among non-O patients, relatively by 20% and 14%, respectively. Both current HbA1c and hyperglycaemia index were higher among non-O patients, relatively by 5% and 25%, respectively. More non-O patients received insulin (+19%). RC was higher (+8%) among non-O patients. Overall macroangiopathy was lower in O patients (-21%) (Table 1).

Patients’ phenotypes across ABO groups

Overall microangiopathy prevalence was lowest among O patients (45.1%), and highest among A patients (56.1%), followed by B patients (55.9%) and AB (51%). Compared to O group patients, microangiopathy was more prevalent by a relative 24%, 24% and 9% among A, B and AB patients, respectively, corresponding to a relative higher prevalence of overall microangiopathy of 23% among non-O patients.

Compared to O group patients, DR was more prevalent by a relative 29%, 8% and 18% among A, B and AB patients, respectively, corresponding to a prevalence of overall DR higher by 22% among non-O patients. Compared to O patients, DN was more prevalent, by a relative 49%, 30% and 71%, among A, B and AB patients, respectively, corresponding to higher prevalence of overall DN by 46% among non-O patients. Compared to O patients, DF was more prevalent by a relative 29% and 69% among B and AB patients, respectively, and lower in A group patients by 4%, corresponding to a higher prevalence of overall DF by 10% among non-O patients (Table 1 and Figure 1).

Discussion

The main findings of this study are threefold. Firstly, modulation of vascular risk by ABO types also applies to microvascular disease, since group A was over-represented and group O under-represented among patients with microangiopathy. Secondly, HbA1c, diabetes duration, hypertension and RC, all standard/emerging microangiopathy drivers, did not explain the unbalanced representation of blood types among microangiopathic patients. Thirdly, insulin resistance was more severe among non-O groups patients. Patients with microangiopathy had more lifelong hyperglycaemia, from increased duration of diabetes, lesser residual β-cell function, and faster β-cell function loss, with group O (43% of patients) showing better indices than non-O groups.

Group A subjects express the A antigen, a terminal N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc) residue added in α(1,3)-linkage to the H-antigen backbone by ABO glycosyltransferase. 20 This surface oligosaccharide is found not only on erythrocytes but also on vascular endothelial cells and present on various plasma glycoproteins including von Willebrand factor (vWF).21,22 ABO variation, particularly A alleles, is associated with higher circulating levels of VWF and lower levels of soluble E-selectin and P-selectin, the latter possibly reflecting slower cleavage or shedding of adhesion molecules carrying A antigen from endotheliocytes, suggesting a pro-adhesive and pro-thrombotic endothelial microenvironment in group A.15,23

Through its effects on glycosyltransferase activity and glycan structures on endothelial cell surfaces and on VWF, the A antigen may interact with the oxidative and inflammatory pathways described by Brownlee which potentiate microangiopathic damage in diabetes, providing a putative link for the higher burden of microvascular complications among group A patients with T2D. 24

The endothelial glycocalyx and the ABO antigen system share a common structural foundation. The endothelial glycocalyx consists of glycosaminoglycan (GAG)-rich structures on microvessels. Its susceptibility to hyperglycaemia-induced oxidative damage is considered a central mechanistic event in diabetic microangiopathy.24–27 ABO-dependent differences in endothelial glycation may thus modulate glycocalyx integrity.

Oggianu et al. demonstrated in T1D and T2D that high-molecular-weight vWF multimers and vWF-bound carbonyls — a marker of oxidative modification — were higher among subjects with micro- and macro-angiopathic complications, despite similar vWF-protease ADAMTS-13 activity. 28 Both the amount and quality of vWF (e.g. oxidative modification by reactive oxygen species (ROS) during hyperglycaemia) - drive its prothrombotic effect on microvasculature. Among T1D from the EURODIAB prospective complications study, higher vWF was associated with incident neuropathy after adjustment for HbA1c and DD. 29

Oxidation of vWF at the A2 domain impairs cleavage by ADAMTS-13, allowing accumulation of larger, adhesive multimers that favour platelet tethering and microthrombi in small vessels. ABO antigen A carried on N-linked glycan chains of vWF confers resistance to ADAMTS-13 proteolysis compared with H antigen of group O patients’ vWF, resulting in higher vWF among non-O individuals.22,30 In hyperglycaemia, ROS-driven oxidation of vWF inhibits ADAMTS-13-cleavage, and vWF level in group A patients is raised, with possible adverse microvascular impact. vWF is considered a biomarker of diabetic microangiopathy at target-organ level, with a link to neuropathy. 31

vWF predicted deterioration in peroneal motor and sural sensory nerve conduction velocities in diabetes, irrespective of glycaemic control and diabetes type. 32 Higher vWF were reported in T2D patients with neuropathic foot ulceration (236.3 ± 20.3% vs 130.4 ± 11.8% in controls; p<0.001), extending the vWF–DN link to its severest form, i.e. DF. 33 The mechanistic pathway linking vWF to DF may involve microvascular insufficiency of vasa nervorum, vWF levels favouring platelet adhesion and microthrombi in endoneurial microvessels. 31 This is supported by skin biopsies demonstrating increased vWF-stained vessel density among patients with DN.34,35 A pro-vWF, pro-thrombotic endothelial phenotype favours microvascular nerve ischaemia and may explain why individuals with constitutively high-vWF (especially non-O, particularly group A) might be at increased DN risk.

Higher vWF is also a predictor of DKD and a marker of generalised endothelial damage in diabetes, with levels rising across albuminuria and DR, further supporting its role as pan-microvascular injury biomarker.19,36 That group A represents a novel susceptibility driver to diabetic microangiopathy, amplifying endothelial dysfunction induced by hyperglycaemia carries clinical implications. As blood type is known for many patients, one may consider adding blood group A to standard drivers/risk factors of small vessels disease, thereby helping to further address the gap in understanding residual microvascular risk.

This study presents limitations inherent to its cross-sectional design, which does not allow attributing for causal direction to the associations observed. However, the fixed nature of blood groups de facto rules out reverse causality. We did not measure vWF nor hemostatic and thrombotic markers, such as factor VIII or platelet activation markers, as this is not part of routine monitoring for T2D. Our population was predominantly Caucasian, with a subgroup of Africans whose findings were consistent with those of the overall cohort, excluding bias related to the ethnic composition of the study sample. It would be worth evaluating whether microvascular risk associated with non-O blood groups applies to other populations, such as Asians and Latinos, and whether heightened vascular risk of non-O groups extends to non-diabetic microangiopathies.

In conclusion, T2D patients of blood group A have an increased frequency of microangiopathy, including DN, regardless of standard microvascular risk factors. Group O subjects exhibit less altered glucose homeostasis than non-O subjects, due to better insulin sensitivity and β-cell function. These findings suggest that both glucose homeostasis and microvascular susceptibility are modulated by the ABO system in T2D, with A blood group associated with more prevalent DN. Microvascular risk of non-O groups is noteworthy, because of their preponderance across most ethnicities. ABO group may represent a novel marker/driver of microvascular risk, to be accounted for when assessing diabetes-related target organ damage.
