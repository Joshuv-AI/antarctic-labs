Riemann invariant-based alternative WENO scheme for a two-layer thin film model
Biswarup Biswas, Rahul Barthwal, Rakesh Kumar

Abstract
In this article, we develop a multi-dimensional two-layer thin film model extending the thin film model proposed in [ 9 ] . The model considered in [ 9 ] considered a very specific Marangoni scale by choosing Marangoni numbers in both layers to be 1 1 . We relax this condition here and prove that the obtained system possesses a full set of Riemann invariants. Based on these findings, we develop a Riemann Invariant-based Local Characteristic Decomposition WENO (RI-WENO) method for the two-layer thin film model in one and two dimensions. The method is built upon a specially designed variable transformation constructed from the derived Riemann invariants of the system. This transformation partially diagonalizes the governing equations and yields a sparse structure in the transformed eigenvector matrices. As a result, the proposed RI-WENO framework significantly reduces the computational cost of the standard Local Characteristic Decomposition WENO approach while retaining its strong capability to suppress spurious oscillations. Numerical experiments, including new benchmark test cases, demonstrate that the RI-WENO method achieves an effective balance between accuracy and computational efficiency, making it a promising and practical choice for solving the two-layer thin film model.

Introduction

The study of thin-film equations constitutes a rich and intricate research area, especially when the flow dynamics are affected by soluble or insoluble substances that induce variations in the surface and interfacial tension. These flows appear in many technical and environmental applications, including coating technologies, thin-film solar cells, and surfactant replacement therapies (see, e.g.,
[
15
,
26
,
28
]

[

,
18

27

and references cited therein. Surface and interfacial tension variations may arise due to temperature gradients or due to the presence of solute particles such as surfactants and anti-surfactants.

Mathematically, thin-film equations are typically formulated as fourth-order degenerate hyperbolic–parabolic equations, with degeneracy occurring as the film height vanishes. Even in the scalar case, the analysis of these equations and the design of robust, accurate, and high-order numerical schemes for the full lubrication models remain highly challenging. In suitable asymptotic regimes, however, the large-scale lubrication dynamics can be reduced to a first-order hyperbolic subsystem; see, e.g.,
[
9
,
24
,
14
,
7
]
. From this perspective, thin-film flows have also been extensively studied within the framework of hyperbolic balance laws, particularly in the context of gravity-driven films, inclined-plane flows, and dynamics induced by surfactants or solute particles; see, e.g.,
[
9
,
24
,
14
,
6
,
10
,
5
,
29
]
. Nevertheless, stable and physically consistent numerical discretizations for the underlying hyperbolic subsystems remain relatively scarce, even though they seem crucial for the reliable numerical approximation of the full model.

Recently, Barthwal & Rohde
[
9
]
proposed a two-layer thin film flow lubrication model, which governs the dynamics of two immiscible fluids under the influence of surface and interfacial tension induced by the presence of anti-surfactant particles. The full model turns out to be a coupled fourth-order system. However, they were able to obtain a first-order hyperbolic system from this lubrication model, which possesses a complete entropy structure and for which Riemann solutions can be constructed. Their first order system takes the following form.

Among high-order methods for problems with discontinuities, Weighted Essentially Non-Oscillatory (WENO) schemes have become one of the most widely used approaches, particularly for hyperbolic conservation laws. The WENO methodology was first introduced in the finite volume framework in
[
25
]
and later extended to the finite difference setting in
[
19
]

[

]

[

]
, and WENO-AO
[

,

,

While WENO schemes perform remarkably well for scalar problems, their extension to systems of equations may lead to spurious oscillations
[
19
,
23
,
1
,
2
]
. A common strategy to mitigate these oscillations is to perform reconstruction in the characteristic space
[
19
]
. However, this approach necessitates the computation of eigenvectors, thereby increasing the computational cost.
To alleviate this issue, several hybrid strategies have been developed, wherein characteristic-wise reconstruction is employed near discontinuities, while component-wise reconstruction is used in smooth regions to reduce computational overhead
[
23
,
1
,
33
]
. More recently, methods based on local characteristic decomposition using Riemann invariants have been proposed, demonstrating significant computational savings without compromising accuracy
[
32
,
31
]
.

Motivated by these developments, we exploit the Riemann-invariant structure of the governing system (
2
) to design an alternative WENO reconstruction in Riemann-invariant variables. Although the system admits a set of Riemann invariants that forms a coordinate system, recovering the physical variables through the inverse transformation requires solving a nonlinear quartic equation, which increases the computational cost of the scheme. To avoid this difficulty, we instead introduce a modified set of Riemann invariants that yields a sparser characteristic decomposition. This choice not only reduces the computational expense but also improves the accuracy of the numerical method. Moreover, we introduce new benchmark test cases in both one and two space dimensions for the system (
2
). In particular, we investigate the effect of two distinct Marangoni numbers on the flow. Moreover, under the assumption that the corresponding concentration gradient vectors are aligned with the line
y=x

x
- and
y

The rest of the article is structured as follows. In section 2, we formulate the multi-dimensional two-layer thin film model and obtain the governing system (

3.1
Preliminaries

In this section, we recall some foundational facts on hyperbolicity and
Riemann invariants for systems of conservation laws, following

[
31
,
16
,
30
]
.

Consider a system of conservation laws of
m
components in one space dimension,

\mathbf{u}_{t}+\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{u})_{x}=\mathbf{0},

(21)

where
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{m})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
are the
conserved variables and

\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{u})=(f_{1}(\mathbf{u}),f_{2}(\mathbf{u}),\ldots,f_{m}(\mathbf{u}))^{T}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
are the flux functions.
For smooth solutions, the system (
21
) can be rewritten in the
quasilinear form

\mathbf{u}_{t}+\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{u})\,\mathbf{u}_{x}=\mathbf{0},

(22)

where
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{u})=\dfrac{\partial\mathbf{f}}{\partial\mathbf{u}}

is the Jacobian matrix.

Definition 3.1

(Hyperbolicity in 1D)
.

The quasilinear system (
22
) is
hyperbolic
if the Jacobian
matrix
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{u})
is diagonalizable for all
\mathbf{u}
, that is, there exists an invertible matrix
\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{u})
such that

\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{u})\,\bm{\Lambda}(\mathbf{u})\,\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{u})^{-1},

where

\bm{\Lambda}(\mathbf{u})=\operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{u}),\,\lambda_{2}(\mathbf{u}),\ldots,\,\lambda_{m}(\mathbf{u})\bigr)

is the diagonal matrix of real eigenvalues,

\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{u})=\bigl(\mathbf{r}_{1}(\mathbf{u}),\,\mathbf{r}_{2}(\mathbf{u}),\,\ldots,\,\mathbf{r}_{m}(\mathbf{u})\bigr)

is the right eigenmatrix whose columns are the right eigenvectors, and

\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{u})^{-1}=\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{u})=\bigl(\mathbf{l}_{1}(\mathbf{u})^{T},\,\mathbf{l}_{2}(\mathbf{u})^{T},\,\ldots,\,\mathbf{l}_{m}(\mathbf{u})^{T}\bigr)^{T}

is the left eigenmatrix whose rows are the left eigenvectors.

The one-dimensional notion extends naturally to multiple spatial dimensions.

Definition 3.2

(Hyperbolicity in multiple dimensions)
.

A system of conservation laws in
d
spatial dimensions,

\mathbf{u}_{t}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbf{f}_{i}(\mathbf{u})_{x_{i}}=\mathbf{0},

is called
hyperbolic
if, for every unit vector

\mathbf{n}=(n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{d})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the associated
one-dimensional problem

\mathbf{u}_{t}+\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}\,\mathbf{f}_{i}(\mathbf{u})\Bigr)_{x}=\mathbf{0}

is hyperbolic in the sense of Definition
3.1
.

3.2
Change of Variables and Transformed Jacobian

We next consider variable transformations derived from the eigen-structure of
the Jacobian
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{u})
.
Such transformations play a fundamental role in the Local Characteristic
Decomposition (LCD) procedure described in Section
4
.

Let
\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{u}):\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a
differentiable and invertible transformation defined on the state space

\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
The system (
21
) can then be rewritten in the new variables

\mathbf{V}
as

\mathbf{V}_{t}+\tilde{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{V})\,\mathbf{V}_{x}=\mathbf{0},

(23)

where the transformed Jacobian matrix is

\tilde{\mathbf{A}}=\frac{\partial\mathbf{V}}{\partial\mathbf{u}}\,\mathbf{A}\,\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial\mathbf{V}}.

(24)

Proposition 3.1

(Change of variables)
.

If the transformation
\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{u})
is differentiable and
invertible, then the transformed Jacobian
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
has the same
eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}
as the original Jacobian
\mathbf{A}
.
Moreover, the right and left eigenmatrices transform according to

\tilde{\mathbf{R}}=\frac{\partial\mathbf{V}}{\partial\mathbf{u}}\,\mathbf{R},\qquad\tilde{\mathbf{L}}=\mathbf{L}\,\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial\mathbf{V}}.

(25)

3.3
Riemann Invariants

The structure of the transformed Jacobian
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
depends
critically on how the new variables relate to the characteristic fields of the
system.
This relationship is made precise through the notion of a Riemann invariant.

Definition 3.3

(Riemann invariant)
.

A scalar function
w:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}
is called a

k
-Riemann invariant
of the hyperbolic system (
21
) if it
satisfies

\nabla_{\mathbf{u}}\,w(\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{r}_{k}(\mathbf{u})=0,\qquad\forall\,\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U},

(26)

where
\mathbf{r}_{k}(\mathbf{u})
is the
k
-th right eigenvector of

\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{u})
.

The following result
[
32
]
, which is central to the design of characteristic-based
numerical schemes, shows that choosing Riemann invariants as the transformation
variables introduce zeros into the transformed Jacobian.

Proposition 3.2

(Sparsity of the transformed Jacobian)
.

Let
\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{u})=(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{m})^{T}
be a differentiable
and invertible transformation, and let
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
be the transformed
Jacobian defined in (
24
).
If the component
v_{i}(\mathbf{u})
is a
k
-Riemann invariant, then the

(i,k)
-th entry of
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
vanishes:

\tilde{A}_{ik}=0.

The following remark identifies the strongest possible consequence of
Proposition
3.2
: a coordinate system composed entirely of
Riemann invariants completely diagonalizes the system.

Remark 3.3

(Full diagonalization via Riemann invariants)
.

Suppose there exists an invertible transformation

\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{u})=(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{m})^{T}
such that, for each
index
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, the component
v_{i}
is a
k
-Riemann
invariant for every
k\neq i
.
Then, by Proposition
3.2
, every off-diagonal entry of

\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
vanishes, and the transformed Jacobian reduces to

\tilde{\mathbf{A}}=\operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{u}),\,\lambda_{2}(\mathbf{u}),\ldots,\,\lambda_{m}(\mathbf{u})\bigr).

In this coordinate system, the quasilinear system (
23
)
decouples completely into
m
independent scalar advection equations,
each governed by a single characteristic speed
\lambda_{i}
.

Such a global coordinate system of Riemann invariants always exists for
hyperbolic systems of two equations
[
16
]
.
For systems of three or more equations, it exists only in special cases;
in general, only local (i.e. in a neighbourhood of a point in state space)
coordinates of this type can be guaranteed, and only under genuine nonlinearity
or linear degeneracy conditions on the characteristic fields

[
16
,
30
]
.
When such a coordinate system is available, it is the natural setting for
the construction of Riemann solvers and high-order characteristic-based
schemes.

3.4
Hyperbolicity of the system (
20
)

In what follows, we prove that the two-dimensional system (
20
) is a hyperbolic system of conservation laws in the sense of Definition
3.2
. Let
\mathbf{n}:=(n_{1},n_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a unit normal vector and let
\mathbf{t}:=(t_{1},t_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a unit tangent vector such that
\{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{t}\}
form an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Then we can decompose the concentration gradient vectors
\mathbf{b}
and
\mathbf{q}
as a linear combination of these vectors. Precisely, we can express

\mathbf{b}=(\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{n})\mathbf{n}+(\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{t})\mathbf{t},\qquad\qquad\mathbf{q}=(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{n})\mathbf{n}+(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{t})\mathbf{t}

Assuming that the solutions of the system (
20
) depend only on the direction
x=\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{n}
, we have

\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}:=\mathbf{n}\partial_{x}.

Therefore, one can rewrite the system (
20
) as the one-dimensional directional system

\displaystyle f_{t}+\partial_{x}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}f^{2}b\right)

\displaystyle=0,

(27)

\displaystyle b_{t}+\partial_{x}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}f(b^{2}+\eta^{2})\right)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\eta_{t}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle g_{t}+\partial_{x}\left(\alpha fbg+\frac{\beta}{2}g^{2}q\right)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle q_{t}+\partial_{x}\left(\frac{\beta}{2}g(q^{2}+\zeta^{2})+\alpha f(bq+\eta\zeta)\right)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\zeta_{t}

\displaystyle=0,

where
b=\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{n}
,
q=\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{n}
,
\eta=\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{t}
and
\zeta=\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{t}
.

Hence, in the variables
\mathbf{u_{n}}=(f,b,\eta,g,q,\zeta)^{T}
, the system (
27
) can be written in a compact form as

\mathbf{u}_{t}+\mathbf{F_{n}(u)}_{x}=0

where

\mathbf{F_{n}(u)}=\left(\dfrac{\alpha}{2}f^{2}b,~\dfrac{\alpha}{2}f(b^{2}+\eta^{2}),~0,~\alpha fgb+\dfrac{\beta}{2}g^{2}q,~\dfrac{\beta}{2}g(q^{2}+\zeta^{2})+\alpha f(bq+\eta\zeta),~0\right)^{\top}.

with the flux Jacobian

\mathbf{A}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{u})=\frac{\partial\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{u})}{\partial\mathbf{u}}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha fb&\dfrac{\alpha}{2}f^{2}&0&0&0&0\\[1.99997pt]
\dfrac{\alpha}{2}(b^{2}+\eta^{2})&\alpha fb&\alpha f\eta&0&0&0\\[1.99997pt]
0&0&0&0&0&0\\[1.99997pt]
\alpha gb&\alpha fg&0&\alpha fb+\beta gq&\dfrac{\beta}{2}g^{2}&0\\[1.99997pt]
\alpha(bq+\eta\zeta)&\alpha fq&\alpha f\zeta&\dfrac{\beta}{2}(q^{2}+\zeta^{2})&\alpha fb+\beta gq&\beta g\zeta\\[1.99997pt]
0&0&0&0&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

We note that the Jacobian
\mathbf{A}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{u})
is a
6\times 6
matrix, corresponding to the six variables
(f,b,\eta,g,q,\zeta).
In particular, the tangential components
\eta
and
\zeta
satisfy

\eta_{t}=0,\qquad\zeta_{t}=0,

and therefore give rise to two zero characteristic speeds. Thus, the full directional system has six eigenvalues, which include four nontrivial characteristic speeds and two zeros.

To identify the four nonzero eigenvalues, observe that the
4\times 4
block is

\mathbf{A}_{4\times 4}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha fb&\dfrac{\alpha}{2}f^{2}&0&0\\[1.99997pt]
\dfrac{\alpha}{2}(b^{2}+\eta^{2})&\alpha fb&0&0\\[1.99997pt]
\alpha gb&\alpha fg&\alpha fb+\beta gq&\dfrac{\beta}{2}g^{2}\\[1.99997pt]
\alpha(bq+\eta\zeta)&\alpha fq&\dfrac{\beta}{2}(q^{2}+\zeta^{2})&\alpha fb+\beta gq\end{pmatrix},

which is a lower block triangular matrix. Hence, its eigenvalues are those of the two diagonal
2\times 2
blocks.

The eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{u})
are

\displaystyle\tilde{\lambda}_{1,2}=\alpha fb\pm\frac{\alpha f}{2}\,|\mathbf{b}|,\quad\tilde{\lambda}_{3,4}=\sigma\pm\frac{\beta g}{2}\,|\mathbf{q}|,\quad\tilde{\lambda}_{5,6}=0,

(28)

where

\displaystyle|\mathbf{b}|

\displaystyle=\sqrt{b^{2}+\eta^{2}},\qquad|\mathbf{q}|=\sqrt{q^{2}+\zeta^{2}}.

The corresponding right eigenvectors are given by

\displaystyle{\mathbf{\tilde{r}_{1}}}(\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-f\,\Delta_{-}\\
|\mathbf{b}|\,\Delta_{-}\\
0\\
-\alpha fg\!\left[\delta_{-}(|\mathbf{b}|-b)-\frac{\beta g}{2}\bigl(|\mathbf{b}|q-(bq+\eta\zeta)\bigr)\right]\\
\alpha f\!\left[\frac{\beta g}{2}|\mathbf{q}|^{2}(|\mathbf{b}|-b)-\delta_{-}\bigl(|\mathbf{b}|q-(bq+\eta\zeta)\bigr)\right]\\
0\end{pmatrix},\quad{\mathbf{\tilde{r}_{2}}}(\mathbf{u})=\begin{pmatrix}f\,\Delta_{+}\\
|\mathbf{b}|\,\Delta_{+}\\
0\\
-\alpha fg\!\left[\delta_{+}(|\mathbf{b}|+b)-\frac{\beta g}{2}\bigl(|\mathbf{b}|q+(bq+\eta\zeta)\bigr)\right]\\
\alpha f\!\left[\frac{\beta g}{2}|\mathbf{q}|^{2}(|\mathbf{b}|+b)-\delta_{+}\bigl(|\mathbf{b}|q+(bq+\eta\zeta)\bigr)\right]\\
0\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle{\mathbf{\tilde{r}_{3}}}(\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
0\\
-\dfrac{g}{|\mathbf{q}|}\\
1\\
0\end{pmatrix},\qquad{\mathbf{\tilde{r}_{4}}}(\mathbf{u})=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
0\\
\dfrac{g}{|\mathbf{q}|}\\
1\\
0\end{pmatrix},\qquad{\mathbf{\tilde{r}_{5}}}(\mathbf{u})=\begin{pmatrix}2f\eta\,\Delta_{0}\\
-4b\eta\,\Delta_{0}\\
(3b^{2}-\eta^{2})\,\Delta_{0}\\
\dfrac{\alpha fg}{2}\Bigl(4\alpha b^{2}\eta f+\beta g(2b\eta q+(3b^{2}+\eta^{2})\zeta)\Bigr)\\
\alpha f\Bigl(\sigma(2b\eta q-(3b^{2}+\eta^{2})\zeta)-\beta bg\eta|\mathbf{q}|^{2}\Bigr)\\
0\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle{\mathbf{\tilde{r}_{6}}}(\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
0\\
2\beta^{2}g^{3}\zeta\\
-4\beta g\zeta\,\sigma\\
4\sigma^{2}-\beta^{2}g^{2}|\mathbf{q}|^{2}\end{pmatrix},

where

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle=\alpha fb+\beta gq,

\displaystyle\delta_{\pm}

\displaystyle=\beta gq\pm\frac{\alpha f}{2}\,|\mathbf{b}|,

\displaystyle\Delta_{\pm}

\displaystyle=\delta_{\pm}^{2}-\frac{\beta^{2}g^{2}}{4}\,|\mathbf{q}|^{2},

\displaystyle\Delta_{0}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}-\frac{\beta^{2}g^{2}}{4}\,|\mathbf{q}|^{2}.

Thus, for every unit vector
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the projected one-dimensional system has six real eigenvalues. Moreover, for a fixed direction
\mathbf{n}
, the repeated eigenvalue
\tilde{\lambda}_{5,6}=0
has geometric multiplicity
2
provided

\Delta_{0}\neq 0,\qquad f>0,\qquad\mathbf{b}\neq 0,\qquad 3b^{2}\neq\eta^{2}.

On the other hand, a direct computation shows that the four eigenvectors
\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_{i}
,
i\in\{1,2,3,4\}
, are linearly independent provided

f>0,\qquad g>0,\qquad\Delta_{\pm}\neq 0,\qquad|\mathbf{q}|\neq 0,\qquad|\mathbf{b}|\neq 0.

Hence, for each fixed unit vector
\mathbf{n}
, the set of eigenvectors
\{\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{6}
is linearly independent whenever

f>0,\qquad g>0,\qquad|\mathbf{b}|\neq 0,\qquad|\mathbf{q}|\neq 0,\qquad\Delta_{\pm}\neq 0,\qquad\Delta_{0}\neq 0,\qquad 3b^{2}\neq\eta^{2}.

Therefore, for every fixed direction
\mathbf{n}
, the projected system is diagonalizable under the above nondegeneracy conditions. Consequently, the two-dimensional system (
20
) is hyperbolic in the sense of Definition
3.2
at every state for which the directional matrix
A_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{u})
is diagonalizable for all unit vectors
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

3.5
Hyperbolic structure of the system (
20
) with zero tangential concentration gradients

We now consider the special case in which the tangential components of the concentration gradients vanish. Recall that, in the directional system (
27
), these components satisfy

\eta_{t}=0,\qquad\zeta_{t}=0.

Hence, if the initial data satisfy

\eta(\cdot,0)=0,\qquad\zeta(\cdot,0)=0,

then

\eta(\cdot,t)=0,\qquad\zeta(\cdot,t)=0

for all
t>0
. Therefore, the manifold

\{\eta=\zeta=0\}

is invariant under the projected dynamics. On this invariant subclass of solutions, the directional system reduces to a
4\times 4
system of conservation laws involving only the normal variables
(f,b,g,q)
, and the hyperbolic structure becomes considerably simpler.

More precisely, restricting (
27
) to the invariant manifold
\{\eta=\zeta=0\}
, we obtain

\mathbf{u}_{t}+\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{u})_{x}=0,

(29)

where

\mathbf{u}=(f,b,g,q)^{\top},\qquad\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{u})=\left(\dfrac{\alpha}{2}f^{2}b,\;\dfrac{\alpha}{2}fb^{2},\;\alpha fgb+\dfrac{\beta}{2}g^{2}q,\;\dfrac{\beta}{2}gq^{2}+\alpha fbq\right)^{\top}.

The corresponding flux Jacobian is

\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{u})=\frac{\partial\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{u})}{\partial\mathbf{u}}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha fb&\dfrac{\alpha}{2}f^{2}&0&0\\[3.00003pt]
\dfrac{\alpha}{2}b^{2}&\alpha fb&0&0\\[3.00003pt]
\alpha gb&\alpha fg&\alpha fb+\beta gq&\dfrac{\beta}{2}g^{2}\\[3.00003pt]
\alpha bq&\alpha fq&\dfrac{\beta}{2}q^{2}&\alpha fb+\beta gq\end{pmatrix}.

In what follows, we consider the system (
20
) along the invariant manifold
\{\eta=\zeta=0\}
and thus consider (
29
) as our main system. Moreover, we consider the state space as

\mathcal{U}=\left\{\mathbf{u}=(f,b,g,q)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}\;:\;f>0,\quad b\neq 0,\quad g>0,\quad q>0,\quad\alpha fb<\beta gq,\quad 2\alpha fb+\beta gq>0\right\}.

The positivity conditions
f>0
and
g>0
select the physically relevant regime, while the additional inequalities

\alpha fb<\beta gq,\qquad 2\alpha fb+\beta gq>0

ensures the strict hyperbolicity of the system (
29
). We take the assumption
q>0
to ensure the existence of an explicit Riemann invariant defined in
\mathcal{U}
; see (
33
) below.

The eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}=\lambda_{k}(\mathbf{u})
of
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{u})
can be
deduced from (
28
) by setting
\eta=\zeta=0
and can be ordered for
\mathbf{u}=(f,b,g,q)^{\top}\in\mathcal{U}
based on the sign of
b
. In particular, for
b>0
we have the following set of eigenvalues

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{u})=\dfrac{\alpha fb}{2}<~\lambda_{2}(\mathbf{u})=\dfrac{3\alpha fb}{2}<~\lambda_{3}(\mathbf{u})=\alpha fb+\dfrac{\beta gq}{2}<~\lambda_{4}(\mathbf{u})=\alpha fb+\dfrac{3\beta gq}{2}.

(30)

along with the associated right eigenvectors
\mathbf{r_{k}}=\mathbf{r_{k}}(\mathbf{u})
, which reduces to

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\mathbf{r_{1}}(\mathbf{u})=\left(-\frac{f}{b},1,0,0\right)^{\top},&&\!\!\!\!\!\!\displaystyle\mathbf{r_{2}}(\mathbf{u})=\left(\frac{\alpha fb-3\beta gq}{4\alpha bq},\frac{\alpha fb-3\beta gq}{4\alpha fq},\frac{g}{q},1\right)^{\top},\\[12.91663pt]
\displaystyle\mathbf{r_{3}}(\mathbf{u})=\left(0,0,-\frac{g}{q},1\right)^{\top},&&\!\!\!\!\!\!\displaystyle\mathbf{r_{4}}(\mathbf{u})=\left(0,0,\frac{g}{q},1\right)^{\top}.\end{array}

(31)

Similarly, for
b<0
, we have the following ordering of eigenvalues for
\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{u})=\dfrac{3\alpha fb}{2}<~\lambda_{2}(\mathbf{u})=\dfrac{\alpha fb}{2}<~\lambda_{3}(\mathbf{u})=\alpha fb+\dfrac{\beta gq}{2}<~\lambda_{4}(\mathbf{u})=\alpha fb+\dfrac{3\beta gq}{2}.

(32)

The eigenvectors are linearly independent in
\mathcal{U}
. This implies the hyperbolicity of the reduced system (
29
) in
\mathcal{U}
.

v_{1}=\frac{b}{f},\quad v_{2}=fb,\quad v_{3}=\frac{q}{g},\quad v_{4}=gq.

It is worth noting that, for the system (
1
), the entropy–entropy flux pairs derived in
[
9
]
were constructed using the fact that the Riemann invariants form a coordinate system. In particular, the diagonal structure of the system was exploited to identify a broad class of entropy functions; see
[
9
]
for more details. A similar approach can also be employed in the present setting to derive explicit entropy/entropy-flux pairs, including a strictly convex entropy. We provide one such strictly convex entropy of the hyperbolic system (
29
) without proof. The entropy of the hyperbolic system (
29
) takes the form

E(f,b,g,q)=\dfrac{1}{|fb|}+\dfrac{1}{\alpha fb+\beta gq}

Consequently, the local well-posedness of classical solutions for the Cauchy problem for the system (
29
) can be established using the classical theory of hyperbolic balance laws. However, in order to keep the focus of the present article on the computational aspects, we do not pursue the entropy structure and associated nonlinear waves of (
29
) in detail and instead refer the interested readers to
[
9
]
for a more intensive analysis.

4
Numerical Methodology

We are interested in developing a high-order finite difference WENO scheme for the hyperbolic system (
29
). We adopt the alternative WENO (AWENO) formulation proposed in
[
31
]
, which uses a Riemann invariant-based local characteristic decomposition (RI-LCD) to achieve high-order accuracy and non-oscillatory properties with a more computationally efficient implementation compared to the classical WENO schemes.

We start by considering the one-dimensional system (
29
). We discretize the computational domain with a uniform grid
I_{j}=[x_{j-1/2},x_{j+1/2}]
with grid size
\Delta x
. Then a semi-discrete finite difference scheme for (
29
) can be written as

\frac{d}{dt}\mathbf{u}_{j}+\frac{1}{\Delta x}\left(\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{j+1/2}-\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{j-1/2}\right)=\mathbf{0},

(37)

where
\mathbf{u}_{j}
approximates
\mathbf{u}(t,x_{j})
and
\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{j+1/2}
is the numerical flux at the cell interface
x_{j+1/2}
and
k
-th order accurate in the sense that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Delta x}\left(\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{j+1/2}-\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{j-1/2}\right)=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{u}(x))_{x}|_{x_{j}}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta x^{k}).

4.1
Alternative WENO Formulations

Unlike classical finite difference WENO schemes, which reconstruct split fluxes, the AWENO scheme operates on the point values of conserved variables. The numerical flux
\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{j+1/2}
is computed via the following steps
[
20
,
3
]
:

1.

Preparation:
Obtain ghost point values via boundary conditions. Compute the flux function values
\mathbf{F}_{j}=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{u}_{j})
for all grid points.

2.

WENO Interpolation:
Apply the WENO interpolation to the conserved variables
\mathbf{u}
component-wise to obtain high-order reconstructed values
\mathbf{u}^{\pm}_{j+1/2}
at the interface to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{-}_{j+1/2}

\displaystyle=\text{WENO}(\mathbf{u}_{j-r+1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{j+r-1}),

(38)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{+}_{j+1/2}

\displaystyle=\text{WENO}(\mathbf{u}_{j+r},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{j-r+2}).

(39)

3.

Flux Assembly:
The final numerical flux is the sum of a low-order flux and a high-order correction:

\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{j+1/2}=\hat{\mathbf{F}}^{\text{low}}_{j+1/2}+\hat{\mathbf{F}}^{\text{cor}}_{j+1/2}.

(40)

Here,
\hat{\mathbf{F}}^{\text{low}}_{j+1/2}=\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{u}^{-}_{j+1/2},\mathbf{u}^{+}_{j+1/2})
is computed using an approximate Riemann solver (e.g., HLL flux). The high-order correction term
\hat{\mathbf{F}}^{\text{cor}}_{j+1/2}
is computed directly from the flux values
\mathbf{F}_{j}
.

The high-order flux correction terms
\hat{\mathbf{F}}^{\text{cor}}_{j+1/2}
ensure the scheme achieves the desired order of accuracy
k=2r-1
. The coefficients for various orders can be found in
[
3
]
. In this work, we will use the following correction terms for the 5th order scheme, which corresponds to
r=3
, and given by

\hat{\mathbf{F}}^{\text{cor},3}_{j+1/2}=\frac{19}{3840}(\mathbf{F}_{j-2}+\mathbf{F}_{j+3})-\frac{137}{3840}(\mathbf{F}_{j-1}+\mathbf{F}_{j+2})+\frac{59}{1920}(\mathbf{F}_{j}+\mathbf{F}_{j+1}).

(41)

If we apply WENO interpolation to the conservative variable
\mathbf{u}
, the resulting scheme is called the
Componentwise WENO
(CP-WENO) scheme. This is the simplest approach but can lead to spurious oscillations near discontinuities which we will demonstrate in the numerical results section. We will now present two more approaches that are designed to mitigate these oscillations by reconstructing in the characteristic space.

4.2
Local Characteristic Decomposition

In the LCD (local characteristic decomposition) approach, the characteristic decomposition is performed locally at each interface
x_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
. We first compute the Jacobian matrix of the flux function and evaluate it at the interface using locally averaged states. In our implementation, we use simple arithmetic averaging. In particular,

\mathbf{u}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{\mathbf{u}_{j}+\mathbf{u}_{j+1}}{2}.

Using this interface state, the Jacobian is diagonalized as

\mathbf{A}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{u}_{j+\frac{1}{2}})=\mathbf{R}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\,\Lambda_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\,\mathbf{L}_{j+\frac{1}{2}},

where
\mathbf{R}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
and
\mathbf{L}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
denote the right and left eigenvector matrices, respectively, and
\Lambda_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
is the diagonal matrix of eigenvalues.

The conservative variables in the reconstruction stencil are then projected onto the local characteristic space:

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{k}=\mathbf{L}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{u}_{k},\qquad k=j-r+1,\ldots,j+r.

(42)

The WENO interpolation is then carried out component-wise on the characteristic variables as

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}^{-}_{j+1/2}

\displaystyle=\text{WENO}(\mathbf{w}_{j-r+1},\dots,\mathbf{w}_{j+r-1}),

(43)

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}^{+}_{j+1/2}

\displaystyle=\text{WENO}(\mathbf{w}_{j+r},\dots,\mathbf{w}_{j-r+2}).

(44)

These are finally transformed back to physical space via

\mathbf{u}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{\pm}=\mathbf{R}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{w}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{\pm}.

4.3
Riemann Invariant based Local Characteristic Decomposition

The LCD approach removes spurious oscillations from the solution but is computationally expensive due to the need for several matrix-vector products in (
42
) and (
43
). To mitigate this cost, we can use the Riemann invariant-based LCD (RI-LCD) approach. Instead of projecting onto the local characteristic space, we perform the WENO interpolation directly on the Riemann invariants defined in (
35
). This allows us to achieve a sparse structure in the transformed eigenvector matrices, which significantly reduces the computational cost while still effectively suppressing oscillations. The following steps summarize the RI-LCD procedure:

1.

Variable Transformation:
Transform the conservative variables
\mathbf{u}_{j}
to the Riemann invariant variables
\mathbf{V}_{j}
using the transformation defined in (
35
).

2.

WENO Interpolation:
Apply the WENO interpolation to the Riemann invariant variables
\mathbf{V}
using the LCD approach presented in Section
4.2
with the transformed eigenvector matrices defined in (
36
) to obtain
\mathbf{V}^{\pm}_{j+1/2}
.

3.

Inverse Transformation:
Transform the reconstructed Riemann invariant values
\mathbf{V}^{\pm}_{j+1/2}
back to conservative variables using the inverse of the transformation defined in (
35
).

4.4
Time Integration

For time integration of the semi-discrete scheme, we use the following third-order strong stability preserving Runge-Kutta (SSP-RK3) method
[
17
]
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}^{n}+\Delta t\,L(\mathbf{u}^{n}),

(45a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{4}\mathbf{u}^{n}+\frac{1}{4}\mathbf{u}^{(1)}+\frac{1}{4}\Delta t\,L(\mathbf{u}^{(1)}),

(45b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{n+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\mathbf{u}^{n}+\frac{2}{3}\mathbf{u}^{(2)}+\frac{2}{3}\Delta t\,L(\mathbf{u}^{(2)}).

(45c)

where
L(\mathbf{u})
represents the spatial discretization operator defined by the right-hand side

\displaystyle L(\mathbf{u}_{j})=-\frac{1}{\Delta x}\left(\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{j+1/2}-\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{j-1/2}\right).

Time step
\Delta t
is chosen according to the CFL condition

\displaystyle\Delta t\leq\text{CFL}\cdot\frac{\Delta x}{\max_{j}|\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{u}_{j})|}.

In one exception, for the accuracy verification test problem, we use
\Delta t=\Delta x^{\frac{5}{3}}

to ensure that the temporal error does not dominate the spatial error, allowing us to accurately measure the convergence rates of the schemes.

4.5
Two dimensional Extension

The proposed schemes can be extended to two dimensions using a dimension-by-dimension approach. Consider the two-dimensional system

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{t}+\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{u})_{x}+\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{u})_{y}=0.

We can discretize the spatial domain with uniform grid cells
I_{i,j}=[x_{i-1/2},x_{i+1/2}]\times[y_{j-1/2},y_{j+1/2}]
with grid sizes
\Delta x
and
\Delta y
. The semi-discrete scheme can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathbf{u}_{i,j}+\frac{1}{\Delta x}\left(\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{i+1/2,j}-\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{i-1/2,j}\right)+\frac{1}{\Delta y}\left(\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{i,j+1/2}-\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{i,j-1/2}\right)=\mathbf{0},

where
\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{i+1/2,j}
and
\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{i,j+1/2}
are the numerical fluxes in the
x
and
y
directions, respectively. The numerical fluxes can be computed using the same AWENO procedure as described in the one-dimensional case, applied separately in each spatial direction. The time integration can be performed using the same SSP-RK3 method as in the one-dimensional case (
45
), with

\displaystyle L(\mathbf{u}_{i,j})=-\frac{1}{\Delta x}\left(\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{i+1/2,j}-\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{i-1/2,j}\right)-\frac{1}{\Delta y}\left(\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{i,j+1/2}-\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{i,j-1/2}\right),

and the time step
\Delta t
chosen as

\displaystyle\Delta t\leq\text{CFL}\cdot\frac{1}{\frac{\lambda_{\max}^{x}}{\Delta x}+\frac{\lambda_{\max}^{y}}{\Delta y}},

where
\lambda_{\max}^{x}
and
\lambda_{\max}^{y}
are the maximum wave speeds in the
x
and
y
directions, respectively.

0.4

Two-dimensional test cases

As pointed out in section

, we are interested in developing solutions for the system (
20
) along a normal direction. In particular, one can take the normal vector as
\mathbf{n}=(1/\sqrt{2},1/\sqrt{2})
. In other words, the concentration gradient affects are along

x
- and
y

20
) can be rewritten as a simplified two-dimensional hyperbolic system of the form

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial t}+\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\dfrac{\alpha}{2}f^{2}b\right)+\dfrac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\dfrac{\alpha}{2}f^{2}b\right)

\displaystyle=0,\vskip 5.69046pt

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial b}{\partial t}+\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\dfrac{\alpha}{2}fb^{2}\right)+\dfrac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\dfrac{\alpha}{2}fb^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=0,\vskip 5.69046pt

(Smooth circular perturbation test)

In this test case, we consider a smooth circular perturbation problem for the two-dimensional system on the domain

To construct a smooth interface, we introduce the profile function

\phi(r)=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\tanh\left(2(2-r)\right)\right).

The initial condition is prescribed as

\displaystyle f(x,y,0)

\displaystyle=1.0,

\displaystyle b(x,y,0)

\displaystyle=0.5+0.1\,\phi(r),

\displaystyle g(x,y,0)

\displaystyle=2.0,

\displaystyle q(x,y,0)

\displaystyle=0.5+0.2\,\phi(r).

b
and

and

is chosen larger than that in

The plots of the primitive variables show that the concentration-gradient profiles generate a Marangoni-driven flow. As a result, the initially constant film heights evolve into nonlinear wave patterns. More precisely, the localized Marangoni forcing redistributes the fluid layers and transports mass throughout the computational domain. Consequently, even though the film heights are initially constant, the solution develops alternating regions of larger and smaller height.

6
Conclusions and future outlook

In this article, we developed a multidimensional two-layer thin-film model extending the model proposed by Barthwal and Rohde
[
9
]
. We analyzed its hyperbolicity and Riemann invariant structure. The resulting system admits a Riemann invariant structure in multidimensions along a prescribed spatial direction, which enables the construction of a Riemann invariant-based alternative WENO finite difference scheme. This scheme is computationally cheaper while remaining highly efficient compared with its LCD-based counterparts.

A key aspect of the proposed approach is the careful selection of Riemann invariants so as to reduce the computational cost. Although the system admits a full set of Riemann invariants that can be used as a coordinate system, the corresponding inversion process requires an iterative solver, thereby increasing the computational expense. Therefore, a suitable choice of Riemann invariants, leading to a sparser eigenstructure, is essential for designing more efficient high-order numerical methods.

Since the model possesses an entropy structure and the film heights must remain positive in thin-film flows, a natural next step is to develop a provably positivity-preserving and entropy-stable numerical scheme. Finally, in the present work, we considered only the reduced first-order model and neglected capillarity and diffusivity effects. Another important direction for future work is therefore to analyze the full lubrication model and develop robust numerical schemes for it.
