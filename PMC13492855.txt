Improving knowledge of iron deficiency anemia through a nurse-led educational program: A Quasi-experimental study among adolescent girls
Authors: Sharlet Dsouza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:In India, iron deficiency anemia remains common among adolescent girls, with low awareness persisting despite school-based programs. In this context, we conducted a study to assess their knowledge and evaluate the effectiveness of a structured health promotion program on iron deficiency anemia among adolescent girls in a school.MATERIALS AND METHODS:The study was conducted among 136 girls aged 12–15 years across 7th, 8th, and 9th standard students selected without randomization. Participants attended a one-hour interactive health education session developed by the researchers, using video aids and lectures. Pre- and post-test assessments measured knowledge gains using a structured, validated questionnaire developed by the researchers. Outcomes were compared within participants using their own pre-intervention scores as the comparator, consistent with a quasi-experimental single-cohort design. All eligible students who were present on the day of the intervention and assessments were included. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, paired t-tests, Cohen’s d, and multivariable linear regression to explore predictors of improvement.RESULTS:Among 7th standard students, mean scores increased from 6.23 to 13.34 (P < 0.001), followed by 8th standard (6.96 to 12.90, P < 0.001) and the 9th standard (7.82 to 10.45, P < 0.001) using R software. Multivariable regression further showed that lower income and younger grade level were associated with higher post-test gains.CONCLUSION:Targeted, interactive school-based interventions can play a critical role in addressing persistent knowledge gaps on iron deficiency anemia among adolescent girls. In this study, a single nurse-led educational session produced significant improvements in knowledge, with the largest gains observed in younger students and those from lower-income households. These findings underscore the importance of tailoring health promotion content to the age, baseline understanding, and socioeconomic background of the learners. By leveraging their clinical expertise, community presence, and communication skills, nurses are uniquely positioned to deliver such context-specific interventions, bridging knowledge gaps early and fostering healthier behaviors that can help reduce the long-term burden of anemia.

Introduction

Anemia remains a massive global health challenge, striking an alarming 1.92 billion people in 2021, affecting 1 in 4 people.[1] While prevalence rates have dipped slightly since 1990, the sheer number of cases has surged, fueled by population growth. The progress we’ve made is being overshadowed by the growing scale of the problem, particularly among women, pregnant individuals, young girls, and children under 5, who bear the heaviest burden, with a glaring disparity in reproductive-age populations.[23] Among those aged 15–49, anemia affects 33.7% of women compared to just 11.3% of men, highlighting a severe gender gap.[4]

The study also found that among all affected demographics, children and adolescent girls have shown some of the least improvement in reducing anemia over the years, despite global efforts.[56] While iron deficiency remains the primary cause of anemia, the Global Burden of Disease study has identified 37 underlying causes, including malaria, chronic kidney disease, and various tropical infections.[3]

Iron deficiency anemia (IDA) arises from diverse pathophysiological mechanisms, including increased physiological demand, inadequate dietary intake, impaired gastrointestinal absorption, chronic blood loss, and, less commonly, disruptions in iron transport. In mild and even some moderate cases, IDA may remain asymptomatic. Pallor is the most common clinical sign, but it can be subtle and is often missed in individuals with melanin-rich or darker skin tones.[78] Many children may quietly experience symptoms such as low appetite, fatigue, irritability, dizziness, or reduced tolerance to physical activity. These signs are easily overlooked or misattributed to other causes. In more severe anemia, the body’s compensatory mechanisms begin to fail.[910] Children may present with rapid heartbeat, shortness of breath during routine activity, excessive sweating, and delayed capillary refill. What is particularly concerning is that if anemia begins early in life and remains uncorrected, it can interfere with brain development.[11] Long-standing or severe IDA has been linked to cognitive and neurodevelopmental deficits that may not fully reverse, even after treatment.[912]

A study conducted in Karnataka found that adolescent girls, particularly those who had not experienced pregnancy or motherhood, had very low awareness of anemia. Even as school-based programs provided iron and folic acid supplementation and perhaps even occasional nutrition talks, these initiatives were not sufficient to add or retain knowledge or change behavior.[13] These results show that screening and health education around anemia often become a priority only during pregnancy, leaving many adolescent girls vulnerable to undiagnosed and untreated nutritional deficiencies.[14] Public health strategies frequently center on improving maternal and infant outcomes, which, while essential, can unintentionally sideline those who have not yet entered the reproductive phase. By focusing predominantly on women as future mothers, systems may overlook the broader health needs of adolescent girls.[1516]

A 2011 report from the World Health Organisation also identified that information dissemination to teenagers was effectively done through community engagement, social media messaging, etc.[1718] In India, where, despite system-wide efforts, IDA among adolescent girls remains a pressing concern. The challenge is compounded by a significant vegetarian population and persistent socioeconomic barriers.[1719] Given the persistent low awareness of IDA among adolescent girls and the critical role of early intervention in preventing long-term health consequences, this study was designed to evaluate the effectiveness of a targeted, school-based educational approach. Specifically, the aim was to assess the demographic profile and baseline knowledge of adolescent schoolgirls regarding IDA, and to determine the impact of a single nurse-led, structured educational session on improving their knowledge.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This was a quasi-experimental, school-based study conducted in a rural school between December 16, 2023, and February 28, 2024. The aim was to evaluate the effectiveness of a well-structured, validated educational module on IDA among adolescent girls. From a pedagogical perspective, the module was intended to improve knowledge of the definition, causes, signs and symptoms, complications, diagnosis, management, and prevention of IDA, thereby equipping students with foundational health knowledge to support healthier behaviors. From a research perspective, the study assessed the effectiveness of this module in producing measurable knowledge gains.

Study participants and sampling

Participants were girls aged 12–15 years from the 7th, 8th, and 9th standards. Eligibility criteria included enrollment in the selected school and informed parental consent with student assent. A total of 136 students who were present on the intervention day completed both pre- and post-tests.

Data collection tools and technique

The intervention consisted of a single one-hour, nurse-led health promotion session delivered separately to each academic standard during regular school hours to ensure age-appropriate engagement. The session followed a structured module that covered the definition, causes, signs and symptoms, complications, diagnosis, management, and prevention of IDA.

The teaching was supported by multiple instructional aids, including PowerPoint slides, short educational video clips, and charts and posters. The lecture was delivered in an interactive style, with guided questioning and discussion to reinforce learning and sustain student attention. To enhance comprehension and retention, the nurse-facilitator employed strategies such as repetition of key points, simplification of terms, and participatory prompts. The use of a validated module ensured fidelity and consistency across sessions.

Each participant completed a pre-test immediately before the session and a post-test seven days later using the same validated structured questionnaire, allowing for direct measurement of knowledge improvement in a real-world classroom context.

Variables and Data Sources: Two tools were used to collect data: (1) a demographic proforma and (2) a structured knowledge questionnaire on IDA. The demographic proforma was designed by the research team to capture key sociodemographic and dietary factors that may influence the risk of anemia. The demographic proforma captured key sociodemographic details, dietary habits, and relevant health history associated with anemia risk. All these factors reflect known sociodemographic and lifestyle influences on IDA identified in previous studies of adolescent health. This was validated by 10 experts including pediatricians, Medical doctors, nursing faculty, and nutritionists/dietitians.[20] The 20-item multiple-choice questionnaire assessed knowledge across six key domains related to IDA, with a total possible score of 0–20, and is available to other researchers upon request. Based on a pilot study with 15 students aged 12–15 years, the structured knowledge questionnaire demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.90, indicating high reliability.[21] The primary outcome was the change in knowledge score regarding IDA, measured through the questionnaires administered before (pre-test) and after (post-test) the educational intervention. Predictor variables included pre-test score, including age (in years), academic standard, type of family, parental occupation, monthly household income, and dietary habits.

Bias and Study Size: We used a single-group pretest–posttest quasi-experimental design in a rural school setting. There was no separate control group or it is also known as one group pretest–posttest design.[22] Each participant served as their own control, with the pre-intervention knowledge score as the comparator for the post-intervention score measured seven days later. To minimize selection bias, all eligible students present on the day of intervention and assessment were included.[23] The sample size was based on school enrollment and availability. All eligible adolescent girls aged 12–15 years in classes VII to IX who were present on the intervention day were invited to participate. A total of 136 students consented and completed both pre- and post-tests.

Statistical methods

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics: categorical variables were reported as frequencies (n) and percentages (%), while continuous variables were reported as mean (standard deviation) for normally distributed data or median (interquartile range) for skewed data. The Shapiro–Wilk test assessed normality. Pre- and post-test score comparisons were performed using paired t-tests (for normally distributed data) or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (for non-normal data). To quantify the magnitude of change within each academic standard, Cohen’s d was calculated for the pre- to post-test score differences. Effect sizes are interpreted as small (0.2), medium (0.5), and large (0.8).

To control for potential confounding and evaluate predictors of post-test scores, multivariable linear regression modeling was conducted. The model included pre-test scores and key sociodemographic variables (age, academic standard, occupation, income, family type), along with interaction terms (e.g., pre-test * academic standard) to assess effect modification across subgroups. Subgroup analyses were performed by academic standards to examine stratified intervention effects. Interaction plots were generated to visually assess differences in post-test scores across pre-test values and academic standards. All analyses were conducted using R (v 4.5.1) software. (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Missing data and sensitivity analyses

There were no instances of loss to follow-up due to the cross-sectional nature of the assessment and the short time interval between pre- and post-intervention. Sensitivity analysis was performed by introducing this interaction term to examine potential effect modification by academic level. However, no additional model diagnostics were used to formally assess model robustness.

Ethical consideration

Ethical Considerations: The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee. Informed consent was secured from parents or guardians, and written assent was obtained from all participating students. This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.

Results

Of the 136 adolescent girls enrolled and available at the school during the study, all completed both pre- and post-test assessments. Participants were distributed as follows: 47 in 7th standard, 51 in 8th, and 38 in 9th standard.

Table 1 reveals the key demographic and socioeconomic patterns of the participants. The majority of students in all standards belonged to Hindu (48%) and Muslim (43%) communities, with a relatively smaller proportion identifying as Christian (9%). Most participants were from nuclear families, with this trend especially pronounced in the 7th standard (33.9%). Notably, the proportion of parents without formal education was highest among 9th standard students (64.3%), while a greater share of parents in the 7th and 8th standards had attained secondary or higher education. Employment among parents was nearly universal, though slightly lower in the 9th standard (26.5%). Income distribution varied, with a higher proportion of 9th standard students (68.8%) reporting household income below ₹5000 compared to the younger cohorts. Hospital admission was most common in the 8th standard students (36.2%), while a history of blood transfusion was rare across all groups. The majority had received iron or deworming supplementation. Median age aligned with academic level: 12, 13, and 14 years for 7th, 8th, and 9th standards, respectively. Pre-test and post-test scores improved across all groups, with the highest post-test median in the 7th and 8th standards[13] and relatively lower improvement observed in the 9th standard (median 11).

Supplementary table reports the improvements in knowledge scores related to IDA observed across all three academic standards following the educational intervention. In the 7th standard, the mean pre-test score was 6.23 (±3.64), which increased substantially to 13.34 (±3.67) post-intervention, yielding a mean difference of 7.11 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 6.04 to 8.18, P < 0.001). Similarly, the 8th standard showed an increase from a mean pre-test score of 6.96 (±3.65) to 12.90 (±3.61), with a mean difference of 5.94 (95% CI: 4.92 to 6.96, P < 0.001). In the 9th standard, the pre-test score was higher at baseline (7.82 ± 3.52), but the post-test improvement was comparatively modest (10.45 ± 3.06), with a mean difference of 2.63 (95% CI: 1.54 to 3.72, P < 0.001). Despite these variations, all groups exhibited a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 1.64).

Discussion

The pre-intervention assessment revealed low baseline knowledge of IDA across all academic standards. The mean pre-test scores were 6.23 ± 3.64 for 7th standard, 6.96 ± 3.65 for 8th, and 7.82 ± 3.52 for 9th, indicating that, despite minor differences, knowledge was consistently limited. These results align with findings of the study discussed earlier in this paper as well as of findings from Indonesia, Ethiopia, Bangladesh and more.[13242526]

An interesting finding specific to the studied samples is the high rate of meal skipping (40.4% in 7th standard), low red meat consumption (25% in 9th standard), and junk food intake (37.9% in 7th and 35.6% in 9th) reflect dietary behaviors that may predispose these girls to anemia, supporting the assumption that vulnerable demographics may also be at higher risk of IDA, despite this study focusing on knowledge outcomes rather than direct hemoglobin measurement.

National Health Survey five showed that 67.1% of children and 59.1% of adolescent girls in India are affected by anemic, and the country has implemented the Adolescent Girls’ Anaemia Control Programme, which focuses on IFA distribution, deworming, dietary diversification, and fortified food consumption across all life stages.[27] However, our findings suggest that these initiatives alone are insufficient to promote meaningful understanding or behavioral change.

In contrast, the structured health promotion module used in this study had a significant impact on improving knowledge among the cohort. The knowledge gains were statistically significant across all standards, with the largest mean improvement seen in the 7th standard. All three groups showed a large effect size, confirming the strong educational impact of the intervention.

These findings reinforce the critical role nurses play in developing and administering structured health education within school settings to address knowledge-based interventions. As frontline health educators, nurses are well-positioned to assess knowledge gaps, develop tailored content, and engage adolescent learners through interactive methods.[28] The unique combination of clinical experience, community engagement, and extensive communication skills enables the nurse to step into health promotion mode in myriad settings.

Our findings also reaffirm the value of delivering structured, age-appropriate education using interactive tools such as video-assisted lectures. However, the comparatively smaller gain in the oldest group, despite higher baseline knowledge, indicates that older adolescents may be less responsive to a single-session intervention. This may be due to competing academic demands, lower motivation, or cognitive overload. For this group, more engaging and age-specific strategies such as gamified learning tools, peer-led activities, or social media-based approaches may be needed to enhance retention and impact.[29]

To explore what shaped the effectiveness of the health promotion program, we conducted a multivariable linear regression analysis. This allowed us to examine how various factors including academic grade, household income, family type, and parental occupation were associated with post-test knowledge scores. We also included interaction terms to see whether a student’s initial knowledge influenced how much they benefited from the intervention.

Household income showed a meaningful association with learning gains. Students from families earning less than ₹5000 per month had significantly higher post-test scores than those from higher-income households. This suggests that students from economically disadvantaged backgrounds gain from such attempts.

Another important result was the significant interaction between pre-test scores and academic grade. An interesting outcome observed in the 9th class cohort, was that those who started off with higher pre-test scores ended up gaining more after the intervention. The interaction term showed a beta of 0.52 with a P value of 0.021. It reaffirms that baseline knowledge about the topic helps in absorption and retention of new knowledge. The multivariable analysis showed that both academic level and socioeconomic status influenced how students responded to the program. These findings point to the importance of tailoring health education approaches to the needs and contexts of learners. Students’ starting points should be taken into account when designing educational content.

Limitations and recommendations

The scope of the study is narrowed as it focuses solely on knowledge improvement without measuring clinical outcomes such as hemoglobin levels or tracking long-term behavioral changes. It is also being conducted in a single school using a non-random sample, which limits the generalizability of its findings. Furthermore, the one-time intervention and short follow-up period restrict the ability to assess how well the knowledge is being retained or applied over time.

Despite these limitations, the study is achieving full participation with no loss to follow-up, which strengthens its internal validity. It is using a structured, researcher-developed health promotion module tailored specifically for adolescent girls and is applying robust statistical methods, including multivariable analysis and effect size estimation. Conducted in a real-world school setting, the intervention is demonstrating feasibility, contextual relevance, and strong impact, particularly among students from low-income backgrounds.

Conclusion

IDA is a multifaceted public health challenge with widespread and long-term effects, particularly for adolescent girls whose nutritional needs fluctuate with maturation and growth. Left unaddressed, it can lead to reduced physical capacity, impaired academic performance, and long-term cognitive and developmental deficits. Despite national and global initiatives, knowledge gaps persist among adolescents, especially those who have not yet entered the reproductive phase, leaving them vulnerable to undiagnosed and untreated anemia.

This study set out to evaluate the impact of a structured, nurse-led, school-based health promotion program on improving knowledge of IDA among adolescent girls. The intervention was designed as a validated and reliable educational module, tailored to address age-specific needs and rooted in evidence-based content. By assessing knowledge both before and after the program, we were able to measure its direct impact, demonstrating significant improvements across all targeted domains of knowledge. The findings confirm that such interventions not only meet their immediate aim of enhancing knowledge but also highlight the broader role of nurses as frontline health educators in school health systems.
