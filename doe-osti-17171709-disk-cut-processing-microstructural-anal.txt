INTRODUCTION
Hole saw sectioning is the conventional approach to extracting samples from larger assemblies.
However, the process introduces significant damage to the regions surrounding the cut due to both the
high strains and heat generation from the process [1,2]. The high strain and temperatures, in turn, induce
changes to the microstructure that includes an increase in dislocation content, phase changes, and grain
and precipitate size changes (e.g. refinement and growth) [1-3]. Femtosecond laser machining is a
comparatively new technique that leverages high intensity (>1 W), short pulses (~100s fs) to remove
material [4]. Laser pulse interaction with the material includes highly localized ionization and subsequent
ejection of material due to the resulting Coulomb explosion. This process is near or below the
thermalization time of the host lattice and, for many materials with low conductivity, results in a damage-
free surface region. Damage to the host lattice can be incurred by the high pressures or shockwaves
produced in the material [5]. For materials with comparatively higher thermal conductivity (i.e. metals),
localized damage near the cut surface of up to a few micrometers has been observed in addition to surface
chemistry modification if not performed in vacuum [6-7]. In this study, we evaluate the damage from
both hole saw and femtosecond laser cuts on various materials. Scanning electron microscopy with both
back-scatter imaging and electron backscatter diffraction maps is leveraged to resolve changes to the
microstructure below the cut surface in addition to the peak-to-peak surface roughness.

EXPERIMENTAL METHODS
Plates of various materials including Al and Ti (hereafter “Al/Ti”) and Ti and Ta (hereafter “Ti/Ta”) were
furnished with various processing techniques used to section disks from the plates. Disks from each plate
were acquired using a hole saw to cut a disk from the Al or Ta side into the Ti material as indicated by a
chamfered edge visible on cuts. A femtosecond laser was used to section disks from plates with varying
process parameters. A subset was selected for analysis and included 2 and 3 mm kerf samples from the
Al/Ti and 2.5 mm and 3 mm kerf samples from the Ti/Ta.

All disk specimens were sectioned into quarters using an Allied TechCut high speed abrasive cut-off
wheel with water cooling to reduce process heat and minimize aerosolization of debris. Specimens were
mounted into a two part epoxy mixture and left to cure overnight. Specimens were polished using
successively finer grits of SiC papers from 320 to 1200 on a Struers Tegramin-25. Specimens were then
polished using successively finer diamond suspensions with particle sizes from 3 µm to 1 µm. Most
specimens were subject to a final polish of 0.05 µm alumina solution until a mirror finish was achieved.
However, Al-containing specimens (Al/Ti disks) were vibratory polished using a Buehler Vibromet 2 for
105 minutes. All specimens were cleaned using a micro-organic soap and de-ionized water to remove
any polishing suspension on the surface. Specimens subject to vibratory polishing were wiped with a
cotton ball soaked in acetone, followed by isopropanol and methanol.

All specimens were evaluated on a ThermoFisher Scientific Apreo scanning electron microscope (SEM)
operated at 30 kV. The use of a non-conductive epoxy mount required the use of a low vacuum (10 Pa)
mode on the SEM alongside the use of backscatter electron (BSE) imaging for nearly all micrographs
acquired. For BSE imaging of the sample without any simultaneous spectroscopic measurements, an
annular backscatter (ABS) detector was used to image the surface. Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy
(EDS) was collected using an EDAX Octane detector. Electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) maps
were collected using an Oxford Symmetry detector. Grain orientation assignment was made using built-
in routines in the Oxford Aztec software. Those points with Kikuchi patterns that do not produce a fit
above the default threshold are assigned a zero value and appear as black in the inverse pole figure maps.

MICROSTRUCTURAL CHARACTERIZATION
Figure 1 provides a comparison of the microstructure of the Al/Ti and Ti/Ta plates furnished for the
study. Combined inverse pole figure (IPF) and band contrast (BC) maps were collected at the interior of
the disks, far from the processed surface to ensure minimal influence from the processing on the
microstructure. It is important to note, the interface used hereafter refers to the interface between two
metals in an assembly. The phrase “cut surface” will be used to refer to the metal interface which was
processed with a hole saw or laser and faces the ambient environment. There are several qualitative
observations that can be derived from Fig. 1 and include:

 • The texture of Al is stronger than that of the other materials where there appears to be a mostly
 random grain orientation.

 • The Ti material used in the assembly with the Al plate is microstructurally different than that with
 Ta.

 • The Ta plate has the largest grain size (50-150 µm) followed by Al (20-60 µm) and the two Ti
 materials.

 • Grain morphology in all specimens appears equiaxed with some slight elongation of larger grains
 in the Ti specimen in the assembly with Al.

 • Ti/Al sample shows a bimodal grain size distribution with notable in-grain misorientation (color
 gradient within a grain) in larger grains.

In contrast, the EBSD IPF maps show the cut edge near surface region has no obvious grains with the
scan parameters selected. Moreover, the grain morphology appears to qualitatively differ from that
derived at first glance from the BSE image. That is, grains appear to have distorted towards the interface
in the BSE image while they appear to distort away from the interface in the EBSD IPF maps. Both
images provide only part of the picture.

The amount of “damage” to the microstructure from the processing method can manifest in several forms
and should be defined, qualitatively, to provide a fair comparison across techniques and conditions.
These include: 1) Dislocation generation, 2) morphological changes to grain size (i.e. refinement or
growth) and shape, 3) crystallographic orientation changes. BSE imaging is capable of resolving changes
to grain size and shape but provides limited information on the other factors. Leveraging EBSD to
generate maps provides additional and complimentary information to BSE imaging albeit at a significant
time investment. For example, for grain sizes significantly below the spot size of the system grain
orientations cannot be identified and will appear as zero solutions (black color). When combined with a
BSE image, the data enables the derivation of a correct damage affected zone beneath the surface. This is
shown in the high magnification EBSD map of the interface (Fig. 2) where the BSE image has been
layered on top of the IPF map to accurately resolve the cut surface.

The majority of analysis performed in this report will be derived from qualitative assessments of
combined EBSD IPF, BC, and BSE images. Yellow dashed lines in Fig. 2 provide a qualitative boundary
from the cut surface to the end of the process-affected region. Several factors are integrated into the
assessment with most of those noted previously. No additional analysis is incorporated into the
evaluations (i.e. definition of quantitative threshold for in-grain misorientation and subsequent data
extraction). For the Al damage depth, a boundary beyond which the zero solution or poorly indexed spots
saturates is used to define the extent of the process-affected zone. For Ti, a transition from grains
distorted by the process to those unaffected is visible from the change in grain morphology (shape and
orientation).

MICROSTRUCTURAL CHANGES FROM PROCESSING

oxygen and carbon enrichment at the interface occurred but is limited to ~25 µm from the cut edge. A red
ellipse is annotated on the O map to indicate oxidation at the interface. Ti/Ta EDS maps (Fig. 6) show no
significant changes to the interface after laser processing.

SURFACE ROUGHNESS EVALUATION
Surface roughness of the cuts was evaluated using BSE images located near the edge of the samples.
Figs. 9 and 10 compare the surface features of Al/Ti and Ti/Ta plates sectioned using both hole saw and
laser processing. All images have been tilt-corrected (linear correction) but did not have any higher order
corrections applied to preserve the structure induced by processing.

For Al in the Al/Ti (Fig. 9), the hole saw cutting resulted in the formation of a rough surface with cracks
and burrs/islands that appear with material flow during this process. The laser processing resulted in a
smoother surface with fewer features including the formation of wings or cantilevers of materials
resulting from the beam undercutting the surface. As shown previously in Figs. 5 and 6, oxide from the
laser ablation process is apparent on all laser cut surfaces. The surface height difference between the 2
mm kerf and 3 mm kerf samples is similar and more pronounced than the hole saw cutting, despite having
a smoother surface finish. For the Ti, the surfaces show significantly less surface features and roughness
than the Al. Beam undercutting is visible in the 3 mm kerf specimen, but no new or notable features
distinct from the Al are visible in the Ti.

For the Ta in the Ti/Ta (Fig. 10), the hole saw cutting resulted in a similar surfaces features as observed in
the Al material with a greater extent. Sample burrs are also visible in the hole saw cut Ta specimen (right
edge of the sample). Laser ablation with the 2.5 mm kerf and 3 mm kerf conditions both resulted in a
smooth surface similar to that observed in the Ti with some changes to the curvature of the surface. For
the Ti sample, the surface structure is similar to that observed in the Al/Ti material with smooth surfaces
and little surface features. Additionally, the 3 mm kerf condition resulted in a larger variation in surface
height than the other conditions.

CONCLUSIONS
Several specimens sectioned from Al/Ti and Ti/Ta plates were examined using electron microscopy
techniques. Significant changes were observed at the interface in hole saw cut samples for the Al/Ti
including significant distortion of the mating Ti material. Laser processing resulted in separation of and
oxidation of the materials at the interface of the Al/Ti specimens. In contrast, significant less changes
were observed for both hole saw cuts and laser ablation for the Ti/Ta specimens. Across all processing
conditions, hole saw cuts consistently induced more damage to the microstructure than laser ablation at
either condition examined. Microstructural damage for materials varied with Al exhibiting the greatest
damage, followed by Ta and Ti. Surface roughness values, taken as the difference between the highest
and lowest point on the sample within a 0.75 mm window, showed that the laser cutting resulted in a
rougher surface in nearly all materials except for Ta for the surfaces evaluated.
