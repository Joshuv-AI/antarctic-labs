LE - PAVD: Learning-Enhanced Physics-Aware Vehicle Dynamics for High-Speed Autonomous Navigation
Musabbir Ahmed Arrafi, Malik Ali, Nicholas M. Stiffler, Krishna Bhavithavya Kidambi

Abstract
Accurate modeling of nonlinear vehicle dynamics is essential for high-speed autonomous racing, where controllers operate at the handling limits. Model-based methods are interpretable but rely on simplifying assumptions, while purely learned models capture nonlinearities yet often lack physical consistency and generalization. We propose LE - PAVD (Learning-Enhanced Physics-Aware Vehicle Dynamics), a hybrid model that integrates physics priors with learned components. Our architecture adds four components: load-sensitive Pacejka tire forces, longitudinal load transfer, lateral tire-force effects, and rate-limited actuator inputs. Trained end-to-end on simulation and real-world telemetry, LE - PAVD enforces physical consistency while improving state prediction accuracy. On an unseen track, LE - PAVD reduces average displacement error ( ADE ) by 16.1 16.1 %, final displacement error ( FDE ) by 20.6 20.6 %, and lowers yaw-rate root mean squared error ( RMSE ) by 91.3 91.3 % versus a deep dynamics baseline, while using 21.6 21.6 % fewer FLOPs and achieving ∼ \sim 1.50 1.50 × \times faster inference. In closed-loop simulations, LE - PAVD consistently outperforms the baseline by achieving faster lap times by 17.4 17.4 % on a training track and 9.5 9.5 % on a test track, without any track boundary violations. Overall, LE-PAVD offers a compact, physics-grounded dynamics backbone that improves predictive fidelity and closed-loop performance while reducing inference cost.



I
Introduction

Autonomous racing is a high-speed testbed for advances in control, planning, and vehicle dynamics.
[
15
,
20
]
.
Unlike conventional autonomous driving, racing vehicles operate under extreme conditions, including sharp cornering, rapid acceleration, and high-G braking, where maneuvers press against limits of tire grip, actuator response, and vehicle stability
[
12
]
.
Accurate, robust, and efficient dynamics modeling is essential for predictive control, trajectory optimization, and real-time decision-making
[
18
]
.
Beyond racing itself, insights gained here directly transfer to broader autonomous driving applications where reliability and safety margins are critical.

Traditional vehicle models (single-track and double-track) are interpretable but rely on simplifying assumptions such as constant stiffness, quasi-static loads, or instantaneous actuation.
These assumptions break down in high-slip and transient regimes, and identifying parameters requires costly instrumentation
[
16
]
.
By contrast, deep learning models capture nonlinearities directly from telemetry
[
19
,
3
]
, but often lack interpretability, generalization, and safety guarantees.
Such models can yield physically infeasible predictions that destabilize closed-loop control
[
9
]
.
This persistent trade-off motivates hybrid models that combine physical consistency with learning-based flexibility.

In this work, we propose the Learning-Enhanced Physics-Aware Vehicle Dynamics (LE
-
PAVD) model.
LE
-
PAVD integrates physics priors with neural components by explicitly modeling
(i) load-sensitive Pacejka tire forces,
(ii) longitudinal load transfer,
(iii) lateral tire-force variation, and
(iv) rate-limited actuator inputs.

Trained end-to-end on telemetry from simulation and real-world, LE
-
PAVD enforces physical consistency while delivering accurate, lightweight predictions.
We evaluate LE
-
PAVD on simulated and full-scale racetrack data in open-loop prediction.
On real-world telemetry, LE
-
PAVD reduces trajectory prediction error by about
20\%
while using fewer computational resources.

We also show that adding physical structure yields larger closed-loop gains with a shallower network, even under identical controllers. Together, these results support LE-PAVD as a practical, physics-aware foundation for faster, safer, and more resource-efficient autonomous racing.

II
Related Work

Estimating vehicle dynamics for autonomous racing has been studied extensively across both scaled and full-scale platforms.
Existing approaches fall into three families:
(i) classical physics-based models,
(ii) data-driven neural models, and
(iii) hybrid or physics-informed learning architectures.
We emphasize two primary baselines-DDM
[
4
]
and FTHD
[
5
]
-as closest to our setting; contrasts below clarify where LE
-
PAVD extends them.

II-A

Physics-Based Vehicle Dynamics Models

Classical dynamics models use Newtonian mechanics to describe system behavior.
Kinematic bicycle models are widely adopted for planning due to simplicity but neglect inertial and frictional effects
[
6
]
.
Dynamic single- and two-track models incorporate lateral/longitudinal forces, inertial coupling, and tire slip dynamics
[
23
]
.
Multi-DoF models capture suspension and load transfer but require extensive parameter identification and computation
[
25
]
.
High-fidelity Pacejka-based formulations improve tire modeling but demand calibration and instrumentation
[
16
]
.
Most physics-based models assume static parameters and omit actuator delays or suspension transients, limiting accuracy under near-limit racing transients
[
17
]
.
These models provide interpretability but struggle with near-limit transients (load sensitivity, damping, delay) that LE
-
PAVD embeds directly into training and inference.

II-B

Data-Driven and Deep Learning Models

To bypass parameter identification, supervised learning approximates vehicle dynamics from telemetry
[
2
]
.
Neural models capture nonlinear effects and often outperform classical models in-distribution
[
19
,
21
]
; e.g.,
[
21
]
pairs a differentiable UKF (unscented Kalman filter) with a learned model and online friction estimation for aggressive maneuvers.
However, black-box models extrapolate poorly and provide limited physical insight.
End-to-end policy learning approaches such as DeepRacing
[
22
]
achieve strong performance but do not explicitly learn vehicle dynamics models and are therefore not suitable as dynamics backbones for model-based control. Recent work explores Gaussian Process RL with collision avoidance
[
14
]
and digital-twin contextual RL
[
8
]
, but interpretability remains limited.
LE
-
PAVD retains learning capacity while constraining parameters and forces with physics priors to improve generalization and control-readiness.

II-C

Hybrid Learning Models

Hybrid frameworks embed physical priors within neural architectures to combine interpretability and expressiveness.
The Deep Dynamics Model (DDM)
[
4
]
uses a Physics-Constrained Neural Network to estimate Pacejka coefficients and drivetrain resistances within a single-track model.
While effective, it assumes fixed tire parameters and neglects load-dependent tire forces, nominal tire-force variation, suspension effects, and actuator delays.
These omissions limit its ability to capture the nonlinear dynamics critical for aggressive maneuvers in autonomous racing.
The Fine-Tuning Hybrid Dynamics (FTHD) model
[
5
]
augments a static physics model with a residual PINN and incorporates an EKF layer for denoising, enhancing robustness under limited data.
However, it adopts a sequential training approach rather than end-to-end optimization and treats the physics model as fixed during learning.
Moreover, it omits key dynamic effects such as load transfer, suspension damping, and actuator latency, limiting its fidelity in high-slip and transient conditions.
Residual learning strategies augment calibrated single-track models with Gaussian-process residuals inside MPC
[
13
]
.
Broader approaches include PINNs for slip-induced dynamics
[
10
]
and adaptive Koopman operators with acceleration-based constraints and online adaptation
[
24
]
.
LE
-
PAVD extends the hybrid line by
explicitly modeling
load sensitivity and longitudinal load transfer, while treating suspension effects as transient nominal tire-force variation and actuator inputs as rate-limited commands. Feasible parameter ranges are enforced through the physics guard layer during end-to-end training.

In summary, Physics-based models offer interpretability but oversimplify transient effects.
Purely neural approaches capture nonlinearities but fail to generalize safely.
Hybrid methods highlight the value of embedding priors but still omit load sensitivity, suspension dynamics, and actuator delays.
These gaps motivate the need for a more expressive, physics-aware dynamics formulation, which we formalize next in Sec.
III
.

III
Problem Statement

We formalize the challenge of accurately modeling the dynamics of an autonomous racecar for real-time prediction and control.
Realistic vehicle modeling is critical for downstream tasks such as model predictive control (MPC), trajectory tracking, and reinforcement learning-based decision making.

III-B

Motivation for Model Improvement

Despite progress in vehicle dynamics modeling, current approaches including the DPM
[
9
]
, DDM
[
4
]
and FTHD
[
5
]
face fundamental limitations that hinder real-time control and long-horizon prediction tasks.
DPM
[
9
]
relies on empirical coefficients that lack physical interpretability and adaptability. It does not inherently account for dynamic phenomena like load transfer or actuator delay-key factors in high-speed or aggressive driving.
While prior models like DDM
[
4
]
and FTHD
[
5
]
introduced physics-guided learning, they omit key transient dynamics such as load-sensitive tire forces, suspension smoothing, and actuation delay-phenomena that dominate under limit-handling conditions.
This motivates a more expressive model architecture, which we now formalize.

Our LE
-
PAVD model directly addresses these limitations through a tightly integrated architecture that blends interpretable physics with deep learning (See Fig.
2
).
Unlike previous models, LE
-
PAVD performs end-to-end learning of physically meaningful parameters within an explicit dynamics model, allowing it to adaptively capture nonlinearities in high-performance driving.
It embeds physics directly into the network structure, modeling load-sensitive tire forces, transient nominal tire-force variation via first-order dynamics, and rate-limited actuator inputs as native components of the system.
The learned dynamics are integrated using a custom Euler scheme that incorporates time-varying actuator and suspension effects, enabling stable multi-step rollout under transient conditions.

Training minimizes one-step state prediction error subject to bounded physical parameter constraints, while model performance is evaluated using root-mean-square error (RMSE), average displacement error (ADE), and final displacement error (FDE).
The model also demonstrates real-time inference capability, enabling seamless deployment in closed-loop systems, including model predictive control (MPC) and deep reinforcement learning (DRL) pipelines.

In this work, we limit our primary comparison to DDM
[
4
]
, as FTHD
[
5
]
is neither evaluated for real-time deployment nor designed for control integration, and lacks support for critical dynamic effects modeled in LE
-
PAVD.
LE
-
PAVD work focuses on delivering significant improvements over DDM by offering greater robustness, long-horizon consistency, and integration-readiness for control-critical autonomous driving tasks.

III-C

Physics-Informed Learning Objective

The model is trained by minimizing the prediction error between the estimated and observed future states
\mathbf{X}_{t+1}
, using only the current state and control input, while enforcing the learned dynamics through the physics-based differential equations
[
4
]
. The learning objective is defined as:

\hat{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{u}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\left\lVert\mathbf{X}_{t+1}^{\text{pred}}-\mathbf{X}_{t+1}^{\text{obs}}\right\rVert^{2}\quad\text{s.t.}~\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\min}\leq\boldsymbol{\Phi}\leq\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\max}

(4)

which includes learning the tire and drive train coefficients using a physics-informed neural network (PINN) that incorporates a
Physics Guard Layer
(See Fig.
2
) to ensure all the predicted parameters stay within the physically meaningful bounds.
This physics guard layer constrains the learned physical parameters via clamping within empirically defined bounds (See Table
III
), ensuring physical plausibility during both training and inference.

LE
-
PAVD architecture for the real-world dataset
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{D}}{}
adopts a recurrent, physics-aware neural architecture designed to encode temporal context for nonlinear vehicle dynamics with high temporal fidelity and physical interpretability.
The network begins with a five-layer gated recurrent unit (GRU) comprising 144 hidden units, which encodes temporal correlations between state and control histories over a 12-step prediction horizon.

The GRU does not introduce additional physical states (e.g., suspension
deflection), but instead learns a causal temporal mapping from commanded
and feedback inputs to applied inputs, resulting in smooth, rate-limited
actuation behavior.
The GRU output is flattened and passed through two fully connected layers of 184 neurons each, followed by Mish activation and Batch Normalization to promote smooth gradients and numerical stability during training.
A final
guard layer
projects the latent representation into 17 bounded physical parameters using a Sigmoid activation, ensuring that all learned coefficients remain within physically meaningful limits (e.g., tire stiffness, damping, and inertia bounds).
Training is performed end-to-end using the Adam optimizer with a starting learning rate of
10^{-3}
, a batch size of 128, cosine warm-up learning-rate scheduling, and a mean-squared-error (MSE) loss objective.
This configuration achieves an effective balance between dynamic accuracy and computational efficiency, enabling sub-millisecond inference while preserving generalization across both simulated and real driving domains.

IV-B

Enhanced Physics-Based Vehicle Dynamics

The physics component of LE
-
PAVD is governed by a set of interpretable differential equations derived from classical vehicle dynamics.
These equations form the backbone of the simulation, enabling accurate prediction of a vehicle’s motion under diverse operational conditions.
Our LE
-
PAVD integrates nonlinear tire behavior, dynamic load transfer, and suspension effects, which are solved through forward Euler integration. The key modified dynamics modeled include:

IV-B
1
Tire Slip Angle Calculations

The slip angles
\alpha_{f}{}
and
\alpha_{r}{}
quantify the angular difference between the orientation of the tire and its actual direction of travel, which is crucial for lateral tire force generation. They are computed as:

\displaystyle\alpha_{f}

\displaystyle=\delta-\arctan\!\left(\frac{l_{f}\,\omega+v_{y}}{|v_{x}|}\right)+S_{hf},

(5)

\displaystyle\alpha_{r}

\displaystyle=\arctan\!\left(\frac{l_{r}\,\omega-v_{y}}{|v_{x}|}\right)+S_{hr}.

(6)

This formulation accounts for combined vehicle motion due to yaw and lateral dynamics, resulting in a more accurate slip estimation.

IV-B
2
Load Transfer Effects

During acceleration and braking, the vertical load on each axle shifts due to inertia and suspension geometry. We model longitudinal load transfer in the bicycle model via static equilibrium of vertical forces and pitch moments about the CG.

The longitudinal force–induced acceleration is modeled as,

\displaystyle a_{x}^{(*)}\;\triangleq\;\frac{F_{rx}}{m}

(7)

where the longitudinal force
F_{rx}
on the rear wheels (RWD) is given as

\displaystyle F_{rx}=(C_{m1}-C_{m2}\,v_{x})\,\text{T}-C_{r0}-C_{r2}\,v_{x}^{2},

(8)

i.e., the engine/brake minus resistive terms, consistent with our implementation.
Solving yields

\displaystyle F_{fz}

\displaystyle=\frac{mg\,l_{r}-h_{cg}\,m\,a_{x}^{(*)}}{l_{f}+l_{r}},

(9)

\displaystyle F_{rz}

\displaystyle=\frac{mg\,l_{f}+h_{cg}\,m\,a_{x}^{(*)}}{l_{f}+l_{r}}.

(10)

Equations (
9
)–(
10
) increase front-axle load under braking (
a_{x}^{(*)}\!<0
) and rear-axle load under acceleration (
a_{x}^{(*)}\!>0
), improving realism of the tire forces.
In the single-track abstraction, lateral (left–right) transfer cancels at the axle level and is not considered in this work.
We also enforce non-negativity via a small clamp in code to avoid numerical issues at very low loads.

IV-B
3
Lateral Tire Forces (Pacejka Model)

The lateral tire forces are modeled using a load-scaled Pacejka formulation with curvature:

\displaystyle F_{fy}=\Big[S_{vf}+D_{f}\sin\!\big(C_{f}\psi_{f}\big)\Big]\frac{F_{fz}}{F_{z0}}

(11)

\displaystyle F_{ry}=\Big[S_{vr}+D_{r}\sin\!\big(C_{r}\psi_{r}\big)\Big]\frac{F_{rz}}{F_{z0}}

(12)

where

\displaystyle\psi_{f}=\arctan\!\left[B_{f}\alpha_{f}-E_{f}\!\left(B_{f}\alpha_{f}-\arctan(B_{f}\alpha_{f})\right)\right],

(13)

\displaystyle\psi_{r}=\arctan\!\left[B_{r}\alpha_{r}-E_{r}\!\left(B_{r}\alpha_{r}-\arctan(B_{r}\alpha_{r})\right)\right].

(14)

Here
F_{z0}
is the nominal vertical load. The scaling
(F_{z}/F_{z0}{})
makes lateral forces load-dependent.
Combined slip effects (e.g., longitudinal-lateral coupling) are omitted in our formulation due to the planar simplification of the single-track model.
Similarly, lateral load transfer is not modeled, as the single-track abstraction aggregates left and right tires at each axle, causing lateral forces to cancel at the axle level.
These simplifications enable efficient learning and inference, while still capturing the dominant nonlinearities relevant for aggressive driving.

IV-B
4
Rate-Limited Actuator Inputs

In real systems, steering and throttle actuators exhibit finite response and cannot change instantaneously.
Rather than enforcing explicit slew-rate limits as

\tilde{\mathbf{U}}=\sat\!\big(\mathbf{U}_{t-1}+\Delta\mathbf{U}^{\mathrm{cmd}}_{t}\big),

(15)

LE
-
PAVD learns this rate-limited behavior by processing both feedback and commanded control signals
through its recurrent GRU layer:

\tilde{\mathbf{U}}=g_{\theta}\!\big([\mathbf{U}^{\mathrm{cmd}}_{t-k:t},\,\mathbf{U}^{\mathrm{fb}}_{t-k:t}]\big),

(16)

where
g_{\theta}(\cdot)
denotes the learned temporal mapping of actuator dynamics.
This formulation captures finite-rate actuator effects without hand-tuned saturation or lag parameters,
preserving full differentiability for end-to-end learning.
The continuous-time dynamics are discretized with forward Euler at step
\Delta t
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{X}_{t}+\Delta t\,\dot{\mathbf{X}}_{t},

(17)

where
\tilde{\mathbf{U}}
are the applied, rate-limited inputs from Sec.
IV-B4
.
This discretization keeps single-step rollouts consistent with the physics and
stabilizes multi-step prediction.

V
Results and Evaluation

This section presents a comparative analysis between DDM and LE
-
PAVD. The evaluation focuses on parameter efficiency, predictive accuracy, and real-time feasibility.

V-A

Evaluation Methods

To evaluate the open-loop predictive performance of the LE
-
PAVD and DDM models, we conducted experiments on two distinct datasets: a simulated dataset
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{D}}{}

and a real-world dataset
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{D}}{}
. To produce valid comparisons we trained both models on the same train subset and tested on the same unseen test subset for both simulated and real scenarios.

The real-world dataset
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{D}}{}
was collected from a full-scale autonomous Indy racecar
[
11
]
.
Vehicle states were recorded at 25Hz using an Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) running at 100Hz, which fused sensor data from dual RTK-GNSS receivers and onboard inertial measurement units (IMUs).
The training subset,
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{D}}^{\text{train}}{}
, consists of 62419 samples recorded over three laps at the Putnam Park Road Course in Indiana, while the completely unseen test subset,
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{D}}^{\text{test}}{}
, includes 56602 samples collected over two laps from the Las Vegas Motor Speedway.

The simulated dataset
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{D}}{}
was generated using a 1:43 scale autonomous racecar simulator
[
7
]
.
This data was sampled at 50Hz using a pure-pursuit controller to follow a predefined raceline across two tracks. Track 1 (ETHZ) was used to generate the training data
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{D}}^{\text{train}}{}
, while Track 2 (ETHZMobil) was solely used for the test set
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{D}}^{\text{test}}{}
.
Model performance was first evaluated using one-step prediction metrics. Specifically, we computed the RMSE and the maximum error (
\epsilon_{\max}
) for three key vehicle states:
[v_{x},v_{y},\omega]^{\top}
.

Beyond single-step predictions, we assessed each model’s ability to forecast multi-step future states by simulating their respective dynamics using learned parameters.
From these rollouts, we computed ADE, FDE, consistent with trajectory forecasting benchmarks such as those described in
[
1
]
, using a 300 ms prediction horizon for simulated data and 600 ms for real-world rollouts.
We also compared neural model architecture complexity in terms of the number of model-parameters, floating point operations (FLOPs-in millions), and average inference time to evaluate computational efficiency.

All rollouts use the
applied
inputs
\tilde{\mathbf{U}}{}
produced by the learned GRU mapping in Sec.
IV-B4
; no hand-tuned rates or bounds are used.
While explicit rate-limiting equations can be integrated in future work, the current architecture captures actuator smoothness through its recurrent temporal structure.
All experiments were performed on a workstation equipped with an Intel i9-14000k processor (24 cores, 32 threads, 5.70 GHz), an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4070 SUPER GPU, and 64 GB of DDR5 memory.

VI
Conclusion

We presented LE
-
PAVD, a physics-aware neural dynamics model that integrates
structured vehicle dynamics with learning to improve prediction and control
performance near the handling limits of autonomous racing. By incorporating
load-sensitive Pacejka tire forces, longitudinal load transfer, suspension-aware
tire-load effects, and rate-limited actuator inputs, LE
-
PAVD enforces
physical consistency while remaining computationally efficient.
Across both simulation and real-world data, LE-PAVD matches or
improves open-loop prediction accuracy relative to a deep dynamics baseline
while substantially reducing model complexity and inference cost.
When deployed as the internal dynamics model within an identical closed-loop
framework, LE-PAVD achieves faster lap times and improved raceline adherence on unseen tracks, while maintaining zero constraint violations.
These results demonstrate that enriching physical structure within a compact learned model yields measurable closed-loop performance gains beyond architectural changes alone.
