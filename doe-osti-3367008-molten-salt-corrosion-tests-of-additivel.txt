 ABSTRACT
 Molten salt reactors (MSRs) have drawn considerable interest due to
their favorable safety features, high thermal efficiency, and compatibility
with different fuel cycles. Yet, the success of MSRs hinges critically on
the performance of structural materials to be used in these aggressive
molten salt environments, where corrosion and material compatibility
remain primary challenges to long-term reliability. Additively
manufactured (AM) nuclear structural materials prompt the use of novel
geometries and compositions to enhance material performance and reduce
costs of constructing MSRs. The rapid solidification conditions inherent to
AM processing impart distinctive microstructural features, including
cellular sub-structures, dislocation densities, residual stress, and oxide
inclusions, which can influence material performance in MSR
components. While the mechanical properties of AM stainless steels have
been widely studied, their corrosion behavior, particularly in molten salt
environments, has received far less attention. Addressing these needs, the
Advanced Materials and Manufacturing Technologies (AMMT) program
provides a framework for systematically evaluating how unique
microstructures produced by AM processes influence the performance of
these materials in these demanding environments and for developing
reproducible testing workflows that can support future code qualification
efforts and standards development. Bridging this knowledge gap is
essential for assessing the viability of AM alloys in MSRs and informing
qualification strategies.
 A further challenge is the absence of standardized protocols for molten
salt corrosion testing. Accordingly, this report provides an account of the
corrosion evaluation of AM 316H stainless steel in NaCl2-MgCl2 molten
salt at 550 °C, with exposure times of 100 and 500 hours. It documents the
experimental procedures implemented under the AMMT program,
including salt preparation, exposure protocols, and post-test
characterization methods, to establish reproducibility and transparency.
Importantly, the study examines AM 316H samples in the as-fabricated
condition, directly reflecting the surface state most relevant to engineering
applications, and compares their behavior to machine-cut surfaces.
Overall, preliminary evaluations have noted that surface conditions (e.g.
morphology, contamination, etc.) have a noticeable impact on the

 v

corrosion resiliency. The impact of the corrosion is difficult to detect at
100h, unless, in the case of AM 316H, the specimen surface is
decontaminated. After 500 h, as-fabricated surfaces of AM and wrought
316H display evidence of general versus preferential corrosion attack,
respectively. Both AM and wrought 316H machine-cut surfaces exhibit a
continuous Cr depletion zone, evident of general corrosion. While the
estimated extent of corrosion appears within the same order of magnitude
regardless of the surface condition, it is apparent that more predictable
behavior is observed on machine-cut surfaces. Nonetheless, further
investigation is necessary to fully elucidate the corrosion mechanism
under these conditions.

 1. INTRODUCTION
 Recent U.S. federal policy has reaffirmed nuclear energy as a strategic priority for national
security, energy independence, and industrial competitiveness. In May 2025, a suite of Executive
Orders, including “Reinvigorating the Nuclear Industrial Base”, was issued to accelerate
advanced nuclear deployment, expand materials testing capabilities, and strengthen domestic
supply chains (Reinvigorating the Nuclear Industrial Base 2025). These initiatives highlight the
national importance of resolving materials challenges to meet the accelerated timelines
envisioned for advanced nuclear reactors deployment. In parallel, the Department of Energy’s
Advanced Materials and Manufacturing Technologies (AMMT) program has been pursuing this
objective by establishing the scientific basis and qualification pathways for additively
manufactured (AM) alloys in advanced energy applications. AM nuclear structural materials
prompt novel geometries and compositions to enhance material performance and reduce costs of
constructing advanced nuclear reactor concepts (Jokisaari et al. 2024). Furthermore, the
solidification conditions inherent to AM processing impart distinctive microstructural features,
including cellular sub-structures, dislocation densities, residual stress, and oxide inclusions,
which could be employed for nuclear applications (Wang et al. 2018; Lodhi et al. 2019). The
AMMT program provides the framework for systematically evaluating how unique
microstructures produced by AM processes influence the performance of materials in demanding
environments, and for developing reproducible testing workflows that can support future code
qualification efforts and standards development.
 Among the advanced reactor concepts under active development, molten salt reactors
(MSRs) have drawn considerable interest due to their favorable safety features, high thermal
efficiency, and compatibility with different fuel cycles. Yet, the success of MSRs hinges
critically on the performance of structural materials to be used in these aggressive molten salt
environments, where corrosion and compatibility remain the primary challenges to long-term
reliability (Roper et al. 2022). Understanding materials degradation under these aggressive
conditions is therefore central to advancing MSR technology from design to deployment. Type
316H stainless steel serves as a critical reference material in the evaluation of candidate materials
for molten salt systems due to its qualification in ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code,
Section III, Division 5 for high temperature applications. This code status provides immediate
relevance to reactor design and licensing, making 316H stainless steel the benchmark against
which new materials and processing routes can be assessed.
 While the mechanical properties of AM stainless steels have been widely studied, their
behavior in molten salt environments still needs more effort. Bridging this knowledge gap is
essential for assessing the viability of AM alloys in MSRs and informing qualification strategies.
A further challenge is the absence of standardized protocols for molten salt corrosion testing. In
contrast to aqueous or gaseous corrosion environments, where ASTM standards guide specimen
preparation, exposure conditions, and evaluation methods, no such consensus procedures exist
for high-temperature molten salts. This lack of standardization has resulted in variability across
laboratories, hindering reproducibility and comparability. Establishing reproducible workflows is
therefore an explicit goal of the AMMT program, ensuring that corrosion data can be interpreted
consistently and adopted broadly.
 Accordingly, this report provides an account of the corrosion evaluation of AM 316H
stainless steel in NaCl2-MgCl2 molten salt at 550 °C, with exposures of 100 and 500 hours. It

 17

documents the experimental procedures implemented under the AMMT program, including salt
preparation, exposure protocols, and post-test characterization methods, to establish
reproducibility and transparency. Importantly, the study examines AM 316H samples in the as-
fabricated condition, directly reflecting the surface state most relevant to engineering
applications, and compares their behavior to machine-cut surfaces. Whereas many previous
studies have focused on highly polished specimens to enable fundamental mechanistic analysis,
the present work emphasizes conditions closer to practical service, thereby providing data of
direct relevance for application scale assessment. The results are presented in terms of surface
degradation, and compositional changes along the damaged surface, highlighting similarities and
differences between AM and wrought materials. Together, these findings deliver a baseline
dataset of AM 316H in chloride salt environments and demonstrate the testing approach adopted,
providing a foundation for subsequent studies on AM alloys in advanced reactor systems.
 2. EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN
 2.1. Materials and Chemical Composition
 Additively manufactured (AM) 316H stainless steel was produced using a laser powder bed
fusion (LPBF) process and provided by Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL). For
comparison, wrought 316H stainless steel was included as benchmark reference material. The
chemical composition, as determined by LECO Corporation metallurgical analysis and
inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) for AM material, are listed in Table 1,
along with the standard composition range for wrought 316H as specified by ASTM standards.
 While the overall composition of AM material falls broadly within the specification range of
316H, several points are noteworthy. First, the carbon content of the AM feedstock powder lies
within the ASTM specification (0.04 - 0.10 wt.%), but near the lower bound at 0.043 wt.%. After
LPBF processing, however the as-fabricated AM material exhibited a reduced carbon level of
0.032 wt.%, which falls slightly below the specification range. This variation suggests partial
carbon loss during the AM process, a phenomenon that could influence microstructural stability
and its properties, particularly at higher temperatures. Additionally, unlike carbon, oxygen (O) is
not specified in the ASTM standard for 316H but becomes a significant factor in AM due to the
higher surface area of the powder feedstock and its sensitivity to environmental pickup during
handling and processing. O was measured at 0.048 wt.% in the AM powder and increased further
to 0.056% in the as-fabricated AM material, reflecting incorporation during the LPBF process.
This elevated O content is expected to promote the formation of oxide inclusions, a characteristic
feature of AM alloys that can strongly influence mechanical properties and corrosion behavior.
Minor variations in chromium (Cr), nickel (Ni), molybdenum (Mo), and residual elements were
also observed between the AM and wrought materials, but still within the ASTM specifications.

 2.2. Sample Preparation and Geometry
 Rectangular coupons of 25 × 12 × 7 mm were sectioned from the AM and wrought 316H
materials for corrosion testing using a Buehler IsoMet low speed saw with a diamond wafering
blade. Coupons were cut with an as-fabricated surface preserved on one face and machine cut
surfaces on the remaining sides, thereby maintaining consistency with the AM sample
preparation strategy. These comparisons were chosen to reflect the conditions most relevant for
engineering applications, since AM components may often be deployed in the as-fabricated state,
while machining is also required for certain geometries or tolerances. After machining, samples
were ultrasonically cleaned in ethanol to remove any residual debris from sectioning, followed
by drying in air. In addition, some AM 316H specimens were decontaminated following an
adopted standard procedure using an acid bath, which is elaborated upon elsewhere (Copeland-
Johnson et al. 2024). A summary of the experimental conditions is shown in Table 2.

 2.3. Pre-Exposure Microstructural Characterization
 Representative coupons of the AM 316H material were selected for detailed microstructural
examination prior to corrosion testing. These samples were mounted and polished to a mirror
finish to resolve micro-scale features using a JEOL IT800HL scanning electron microscope
(SEM), located at the INL Research Innovation Center. Initial preparation employed colloidal
silica as the final polishing step. However, this approach was found to introduce ambiguity, as
sub-micron features in the microstructure were difficult to distinguish from possible silica
residues. To avoid misinterpretation, the procedure was refined to use a diamond suspension for
the final polishing stage, which provided clear resolution of intrinsic microstructural features.
Microstructural characterization was carried out using SEM across multiple length scales, from
melt pool morphology at low magnification to sub-micron cellular structures and oxide
inclusions at higher magnification. Electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) was used to examine
grain morphology, crystallographic orientation, and texture. Energy dispersive spectroscopy

 19

(EDS) was used to confirm the chemical nature of inclusions observed within AM
microstructure.
 The comprehensive characterization was conducted specifically for the AM material, as the
microstructure of wrought 316H stainless steel is well established in the literature and was not
repeated here. The observations provide essential baseline context for interpreting the corrosion
response of the AM specimens, where microstructure-environment interactions are expected to
strongly influence degradation mechanisms.
 2.4. Static Corrosion Testing
 Preliminary evaluations of corrosion resilience of AM and wrought 316H were conducted
through static corrosion testing in purified NaCl-MgCl2 eutectic salt. NaCl and MgCl2 salts were
procured from Sigma-Aldrich (anhydrous beads, 99.99% trace metals basis). A detailed
description of the salt purification process is documented in Copeland-Johnson et al (Copeland-
Johnson et al. 2024). Static corrosion tests were conducted within an argon atmosphere glovebox
maintained at < 1 ppm O and < 1 ppm H2O atmosphere. All components were cleaned outside
the glovebox using deionized H2O and CH3CH2OH, followed by air drying and vacuum oven
drying at 120°C for 1 hour. The alumina crucibles were also pre-cleaned using a 2% HNO3
solution and were then baked in a box furnace located within the glovebox at 700°C for 4 h. The
salts were held within the alumina crucibles with a submerged BN sample holder to orient the
samples and allow for removal from the salt while still hot. Additional details on the design of
the BN sample holders are documented in Copeland-Johnson, et al (Copeland-Johnson et al.
2024). Specimen duplicates were then corroded in the salt bath at 550 °C for 100 and 500 h.
 2.5. Post Exposure Characterization
 Following the static molten salt tests, specimens were examined to assess both the surface
degradation and compositional changes induced by molten salt exposure. Laser scanning
confocal microscopy (LSCM) was initially employed with the aim of capturing surface
degradation morphology across representative samples. However, the technique did not provide
sufficient resolution or contrast to reliably distinguish corrosion damage and therefore was not
pursued further for the full sample set. The primary analysis relied on cross-sectional analysis of
the samples using SEM combined with EDS. To evaluate corrosion damage, coupons were
mounted horizontally, such that both the as-fabricated surfaces and the machine cut surface were
exposed in cross-sectional view. After mounting, the cross-sectional surfaces were ground and
polished to obtain a surface suitable for SEM and EDS analysis. This approach allowed direct
examination of the corrosion damage beneath each surface condition without the need to
physically section through the sample after testing. SEM imaging was used to observe the
corrosion damage and penetration and to observe morphological differences across as-fabricated
and machine-cut surfaces. EDS mapping was carried out to identify compositional changes,
including Cr depletion, Ni enrichment, and some other elemental changes that are possible using
SEM EDS.
 3. RESULTS
 3.1. Microstructural characterization of AM 316H (Pre-corrosion)
 To establish the as-fabricated baseline, representative AM 316H samples were examined
using SEM in backscattered electron (BSE) mode over a wide range of magnifications,
complemented by low magnification EBSD map (Figures 1 and 4). Imaging was performed on
diamond-polished surfaces to resolve sub-micron features without polishing artifacts. At low

 20

magnification (Figure 1a), the overall build structure is visible. The direction of the laser scan
can be seen from right to left, and overlapping melt pools give rise to a layered appearance. At
higher magnification (Figure 1b – c), the microstructure is dominated by elongated, columnar
grains that follow the build direction. Melt pool boundaries are also apparent in BSE contrast.
Moving further in magnification (Figure 1d – e), a fine cellular solidification structure becomes
visible inside the grains. The cell walls appear brighter in BSE, which suggests the segregation
of alloying elements on the cell boundaries. At even higher magnification (Figure 1f – g), small
dark features appear throughout the structure. Importantly, these inclusions are not randomly
distributed but are most often found decorating the cellular walls. These preferential locations at
these boundaries suggest a close link between solidification dynamics, solute redistribution, and
inclusion formation. This raises the question of how such inclusions might influence properties
that are sensitive to cell-wall chemistry and is worth exploring further. The EBSD map (Figure
1h) confirms the columnar grain morphology observed in BSE and shows the crystallographic
texture typical of LPBF 316H. Overall, the microstructure of AM 316H shows a hierarchy of
features, including melt pools, columnar grains, cellular substructures, and inclusions
concentrated at cell boundaries.

pool structure and hatch direction from LPBF process. (b-c) BSE images highlighting columanar grains. (d-e) Higher
magnification BSE images resolving the cellular substructure within grains, with bright contrast along cell walls. (f-g) High

confirming the columnar grain morphology and crystallographic orientation distribution.

 To further investigate the inclusions observed along cellular boundaries in Figure 1, higher
magnification imaging was carried out at multiple magnifications (Figure 2). These images
reveal that the inclusions are not uniform in size but instead exhibit a bimodal distribution. A
fine population, typically on the order of 20 – 50 nm, is distributed densely along cellular walls,
while a coarser population, extending several hundred nanometers, appear less frequently but is
clearly distinguishable. The concentration of inclusions at cellular walls suggests that the
formation is closely tied to solute partitioning during solidification fronts providing favorable
nucleation sites. The presence of a bimodal size distribution suggests that both nucleation-limited
fine inclusions and growth-controlled larger inclusions are present, reflecting variation in local
thermal and chemical conditions during solidification.
 The presence of inclusions aligned with the cellular network is important not only because
they introduce chemically distinct regions, but also because they may differ structurally from the
surrounding metal matrix. Many oxide or silicate type inclusions are expected to be at least
partially amorphous, in contrast to a crystalline austenitic matrix, creating interfaces with distinct
physical and chemical properties. Such interfaces can act as preferential sites for localized

 21

corrosion initiation or for changes in dissolution kinetics under exposure to a corrosive
environment. While the present work is focused on corrosion behavior at the engineering scale,
these observations highlight a potential mechanistic pathway that merits further study in the
future.

Figure 2. BSE images of AM 316H at progressively higher magnifications showing inclusions along cellular boundaries. The
inclusions exhibit a bimodal size distribution, with a fine population (20 - 50 nm), and coarser population (up to several hundred
nanometers) present a lower density.

 To understand the chemical composition of the inclusions identified in Figures 1 and 2, EDS
analysis was carried out on a representative region containing multiple inclusions (Figure 3).
The compositional maps demonstrate a consistent chemical partitioning between inclusions and
the surrounding austenitic matrix. O Kα and Si Kα signals are strongly enriched within the
inclusions. In contrast, both the Fe Kα and Ni Kα intensities are suppressed in the inclusion
regions, indicating the depletion of these elements relatively to bulk alloy. Cr Kα distribution
remains largely uniform, with very little distinction between inclusions and the adjacent matrix.
These results suggest that inclusions formed during solidification are oxide-silicates in nature,
with O and incorporation balanced by local depletion of metallic elements (Lodhi et al. 2022).
The absence of strong Cr depletion is noticeable, since Cr is a key element governing corrosion
response.

 To complement the compositional mapping shown in Figure 3, standardless EDS point scans
were performed on representative inclusion and the surrounding matrix (Figure 4). The SEM
image highlights both fine inclusions and coarser one, with spot 1 located on the coarser
inclusion and spot 2 located in the adjacent matrix. The EDS results show that the inclusion is
strongly enriched in O (27.7 ± 6 wt. %) and silicon (16.7 ± 6.3 wt. %), consistent with the oxide-
silicate phase. In contrast the matrix composition at spot 2 contains only 3.4 ± 6.7 wt.% O and
1.4 ± 8.2 wt.% Si, with Fe, Ni and Cr contents that are consistent with the expected ranges for
316H stainless steel as specified in ASTM standards. Although the absolute quantification of O
by EDS is subject to uncertainty, the clear enrichment of O and Si strongly suggest the inclusions
are oxide-silicate phase.

 The pre-exposure characterization of AM 316H established the hierarchical microstructure of
the LPBF produced alloy, including weld pool structures, cellular structures and inclusions.
These features are a direct consequence of the rapid solidification conditions in the LPBF
process, and they differ fundamentally from the microstructure of wrought or as-fabricated
316H, which solidify closer to equilibrium. This distinction is important, as it suggests that AM
alloys may respond to corrosive environments in ways that differ from their conventionally
processed counterpart. Accordingly, the following section examines how these microstructural
differences, together with surface condition, shape the corrosion response in molten chloride
salts.

 3.2. Post-corrosion
 Preliminary characterization of corrosion damage was structured around two key
comparisons: (i) AM versus wrought 316H, to bench mark the AM against a reference alloy, and
(ii) as-fabricated versus machine-cut surfaces, to capture the role of surface condition.

 After 100h of corrosion, the extent of attack appears minimal in both as-fabricated AM and
wrought 316H, corresponding to little distinguishability between the two systems in terms of
resiliency, Figure 5. Interestingly, when the as-fabricated surface of AM 316H is
decontaminated, intragranular depletion of Cr is observed, indicative of preferential corrosion
pathways along grain boundaries intersecting with the surface, Figure 5C. It is evident that
decontamination of the surface has a notable impact on the corrosion mechanism within such a
short time frame. More notable behavior is observed when comparing the machine cut surfaces.
Wrought 316H exhibits a uniform Cr depletion zone less than 1 µm from the alloy surface,
indicative of general corrosion, while minimal attack is observed in its AM counterpart, Figure
5D and E.

 After 500h of corrosion, Figure 5, the impact of the molten salt on the resiliency of the as-
fabricated AM surface is more apparent, exhibiting a distinguishable Cr depletion zone spanning
about 2 µm from the surface and featuring voids, evident of a more severe general corrosion
mechanism than that observed after 100 h. Wrought 316H also exhibits porous Cr depletion
zones spanning a similar length scale as that observed in AM but isolated to specific regions,
suggesting that wrought 316H experienced preferential corrosion attack. Upon comparison of the
machine-cut surfaces, Figure 6, the trend of a distinctive continuous Cr depletion zone is
observed in wrought 316H, extending about 2 µm from the surface. The machine-cut AM
surface showed a distinguishable continuous Cr depletion zone less than 1 µm from the surface,
featuring voids. In both cases, the behavior observed is evident of general corrosion attack.

 4. DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS
 Overall, the surface conditions (e.g. morphology, contamination, etc.) have a noticeable
impact on the corrosion resiliency of 316H. The impact of the corrosion proved difficult to detect
at 100 h, unless, in the case of AM 316, the specimen surface is decontaminated. After 500 h, as-
fabricated surfaces of AM and wrought 316H display evidence of general versus preferential
corrosion attack, respectively. Both AM and wrought 316H machine-cut surfaces exhibit a
continuous Cr depletion zone, evident of general corrosion. While the estimated extent of
corrosion appears within the same order of magnitude regardless of the surface condition, it is
apparent that more predictable behavior is observed on machine-cut surfaces. This observation is
to be expected given that machine-cutting the surfaces corresponds to a more consistent surface
morphology compared to that after fabrication. As a result, corrosion kinetics at the specimen
surface are more likely to follow a consistent mechanism given the more uniform surface (Sato
1987). Nonetheless, further investigation is necessary to fully elucidate the corrosion mechanism
under these conditions.

 5. PUBLICATIONS AND HARVEST STRATEGY
 Preliminary results acquired from this investigation, including the sample preparation
techniques, corrosion testing practices, and SEM microstructural characterization will prompt
follow-up studies in FY 26, the details of which will be culminated into a peer-reviewed
manuscript, tentatively titled “Standard practices for static corrosion testing of additively
manufactured SS316H.” Activities will include repeating static corrosion studies over longer
time scales, up to 1000 h, with an expanded number of replicates to both verify reproducibility
and extend the results to a more operationally relevant time scale. In addition, investigators will
revisit LSCM to acquire more quantitative information on the evolution of the surface
morphology of as-fabricated and machine cut surfaces after corrosion for 1000 h. The longer
length of time should allow for the instrument to generate datasets mapping the entire specimen,
corresponding to sample volumes at the more relevant macroscale, strengthening conclusions
about corrosion resiliency. Overall, this manuscript will culminate progress to develop a
systemic framework for assessment of the corrosion resiliency of AM structural materials in
extreme environments through reproducible testing workflows. The investigators aim for this
publication to be available FY 26 – 27 and will serve as a critical asset to the AMMT mission of
supporting future code qualification efforts and standards development for evaluating
environmental effects of AM materials for nuclear applications.
