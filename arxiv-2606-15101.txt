Optimizing Wigner Negativity in Scattering Processes Using Energetic Cost Functions
Kian Hwee Lim, Kiarn T. Laverick, Sahil Sardar Jafar, Samyak P. Prasad, Maria Maffei, Alexia Auffèves

Abstract
Wigner negativities (WNs) are key signatures of non-Gaussian bosonic states and essential resources for quantum technologies. We study their generation in the scattering of coherent pulses by a two-level atom coupled to a one-dimensional reservoir, a unitary and energy-preserving platform. Optimization in this multimode setting is hindered by the complexity of evaluating Wigner functions. We overcome this challenge by introducing energetic cost functions that identify output modes most likely to host large negativities. First using incoherent energy and then isolating a genuinely non-Gaussian contribution, we demonstrate a strong correlation between these quantities and WNs. This correlation extends beyond short, intense pulses to encompass pulses of finite energy, where photons are scattered while the two-level atom is driven. Focusing on the energy-efficiency of the process, we show that maximally efficient generation takes place for less than one input photon, on average, spectrally mode-matched with the atom.



Introduction.
—Wigner negativities (WNs) signal the non-Gaussian character of bosonic states
Kenfack and Życzkowski 2004
;
Albarelli et al. 2018
. Beyond their fundamental interest, they constitute key resources for quantum metrology and sensing
Adesso et al. 2009
;
Genoni et al. 2009
;
Tan and Jeong 2019
;
Rahman et al. 2025
, as well as for continuous-variable quantum computing
Kenfack and Yczkowski 2004
;
Mari and Eisert 2012
;
Veitch et al. 2013
;
Lvovsky et al. 2020
. Generating WNs from initially Gaussian states requires nonlinear processes, which can arise, for instance, from quantum measurements
Genoni et al. 2010
;
Wang and Zhou 2025
or from scattering off a quantum emitter—the simplest example being a two-level atom (TLA) coupled to a one-dimensional (1D) reservoir of bosonic modes
He et al. 2013
.
Scattering processes involving such “1D atoms” are particularly appealing because they can be treated as unitary and intrinsically lossless processes
Maffei et al. 2022
;
Prasad et al. 2026
. As a result, they are deterministic and energy-preserving. These schemes rely on state-of-the-art platforms in quantum photonics
Scarpelli et al. 2019
;
Siampour et al. 2017
and circuit cavity quantum electrodynamics (cQED)
Blais et al. 2021
, where they already enable the implementation of quantum interfaces
Javadi et al. 2018
;
Sipahigil et al. 2016
, the generation of resource states
Hu et al. 2008
;
Hoi et al. 2012
;
Javadi et al. 2015
, and the realization of quantum gates
Joshi et al. 2021
;
Krastanov et al. 2022
;
Pettersson et al. 2026
. While classical
\pi
-pulses based on short and intense input pulses are natural candidates to generate WNs through the spontaneous emission of single photon states, recent studies have focused on drives of finite intensities in the steady-state scattering regime
Quijandría et al. 2018
;
Strandberg et al. 2019
;
Kleinbeck et al. 2023
. They have demonstrated the emergence of WNs in the resonance fluorescence of a 1D atom, with typical negativities on the order of
10\%
that of a single photon’s negativity.

In this Letter, we propose strategies to optimize WNs in the scattering of a coherent pulse by a 1D atom. Our study encompasses finite energy pulses allowing for incoherent scattering to take place while the TLA is driven. Here optimizations are particularly challenging because the scattered field is inherently multimode: consequently, they must be performed not only over the input pulse, but also over the output modes in which the WN is detected
Brecht et al. 2015
;
Loudon 2000
. In addition, evaluating WNs requires prior computation of the Wigner function over the full phase space of the detected mode, making it impractical as a cost function for direct optimization. To overcome this difficulty, we exploit the energy-preserving nature of scattering processes and instead introduce optimization strategies based on energetic cost functions.

The central idea of our approach is to identify within the energy of the scattered field the components which do not contribute to WN. By maximizing the remnant contribution over all possible output modes, we can single out those most likely to yield large negativities. We implement this optimization first using the energy of field fluctuations—or incoherent energy
Laverick et al. 2026
—and then refine the analysis by isolating in the incoherent energy a specific contribution we attribute to the non-Gaussian (NG) character of the field
Jafar et al.
.

By repeating this procedure as a function of the input pulse parameters, we observe a strong correlation between the maxima of the WN and those of the chosen energetic contribution. While this correlation is limited to short and intense (classical) input pulses when using the incoherent energy, it extends to longer and less intense pulses when the NG contribution is isolated. These results demonstrate that energetic cost functions provide an efficient route to identifying input pulses that maximize WNs, while providing new insights into the key mechanisms yielding the generation of NG states under energy constraints. In particular, comparing the obtained WN to the input pulse energy, we show that WN is generated most efficiently when the pulse contains less than one photon on average, and is spectrally mode-matched with the TLA. We show that in this regime, the scattering effectively implements a SNAP-like gate
Heeres et al. 2015
on
the incident coherent pulse, whose vacuum component undergoes a
\pi
phase shift.

System and model—
We consider a TLA coupled to a 1D reservoir of electromagnetic modes labelled by their frequency
\omega

1

\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{\rm Q}+\hat{H}_{\rm F}+\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}

\hat{H}_{\rm Q}=\omega_{{q}}\hat{\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{\sigma}

\hat{H}_{\rm F}=\int{\rm d}\omega\,\omega\hat{b}^{\dagger}(\omega)\hat{b}(\omega)

[\hat{b}(t),\hat{b}^{\dagger}(t^{\prime})]=\delta(t-t^{\prime})

\hat{H}_{Q}+\hat{H}_{F}

In this work, we consider coherent input pulses
|{\alpha_{\text{in}}}\rangle=e^{\alpha\hat{b}_{u}^{\dagger}-\alpha^{*}\hat{b}_{u}}|{\text{vac}}\rangle

\alpha

|{\text{vac}}\rangle

\hat{b}_{u}=\int{\rm d}t\,u(t)e^{i\omega_{q}t}\hat{b}(t)

u(t)=(\pi\Delta^{2})^{-1/4}{\rm exp}[-(t-t_{0})^{2}/2\Delta^{2}]

|\alpha|^{2}
being the mean number of photons in the pulse and

\gamma\Delta\ll|\alpha|^{2}

\gamma\Delta\gg|\alpha|^{2}

The stimulated regime provides a clear temporal separation between the coherent excitation of the TLA, and the spontaneous release of a single mode field. In full generality however, incoherent scattering takes place while the TLA is driven. In this case a few to several output modes are populated, while only one mode is detected and characterized. This detected mode is defined by the normalized filter function
v(t)
and the related mode operator
\hat{b}_{v}=\int{\rm d}t\,v(t)\hat{b}(t)

Kiilerich and Mølmer 2020

Kiilerich and Mølmer 2020

Energetic approach.
—
From the scenario above, it is clear that a direct optimization of the WN as a function of the scattering parameters
(\alpha,\Delta,v(t))
is highly impractical, calling for alternative strategies. Here we build on the simple intuition that the mean field energy of the scattered pulse does not contribute to the WN. This invites to search for WNs in the “incoherent modes”,
i.e.
, the modes whose energy mostly consists in fluctuations.

Conclusion.
—In this paper we analyze the creation and optimization of Wigner negativity by a two-level atom driven with a coherent Gaussian pulse through energetic figures of merit. Using the simplest figure of merit, the incoherent energy, we identified the temporal mode that contained the most incoherent energy, which when there were only a few output temporal modes, yielded near optimal negativity. However, when with a larger number of output temporal modes, we found that the incoherent energy performs poorly; instead one must maximize another quantity, the non-Gaussian energy. In identifying the output mode that maximized this energy, we found significant improvement in the Wigner negativity as compared with the incoherent energy.

While these energetic quantities provide good, simple figures of merit for the generation of Wigner negativity, there are still many open questions. Firstly, it would prove prudent to apply these energetic quantities beyond the simplest emitter scenario to, say, three-level atoms, real atoms, non-linear crystals and other types of quantum emitters. Not only would this enable a broader assessment of the viability of these energetic measures but it would also allow us to observe more exotic scenarios and test the robustness of the energetic decomposition,
e.g.
, cases where there is not a clear parameter regime where the non-Gaussian energy is maximized, with the squeezing and mixing energies being almost negligible. Furthermore, it would be useful to further investigate the non-Gaussian energy from a more fundamental perspective, in particular, showing that even for mixed states that it is a valid quantifier of non-Gaussianity, or even finding an analytic connection between the non-Gaussian energy and the Wigner negativity. Finally, it would be useful to put these energetic figures of merit to practice experimentally.

Appendix A
Computing the Wigner Function of the detected mode

The Wigner Function of a temporal mode
\hat{b}_{v}
of the output field can be computed following the method proposed in Ref.
Kiilerich and Mølmer 2020
. For completeness, we present the method here. First, define the composite system
\rho_{sv}(t)
of the TLA and an auxiliary bosonic mode,
i.e.
, the “virtual cavity” mode, with annihilation operator
\hat{a}_{v}
, with
\rho_{sv}(0)=\outerproduct{g}{g}_{s}\otimes\outerproduct{\text{vac}}{\text{vac}}_{v}
.
Thereafter, we define

g_{v}(t)=-v^{*}(t)/\sqrt{\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}t^{\prime}\,|v(t^{\prime})|^{2}}
,

\hat{H}_{sv}(t)=\sqrt{\gamma}(g_{v}(t)\hat{a}_{v}^{\dagger}\hat{\sigma}-g^{*}_{v}(t)\hat{\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{v})/2i
,
\hat{c}_{sv}(t)=\sqrt{\gamma}\hat{\sigma}+g_{v}^{*}(t)\hat{a}_{v}
,
\hat{H}_{d}(t)=i(\alpha^{*}u^{*}(t)\hat{c}_{sv}(t)-\alpha u(t)\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{sv}(t))
, and solve the following Lindblad master equation

\dot{\rho}_{sv}=-i[\hat{H}_{sv}(t)+\hat{H}_{d}(t),\rho_{sv}]+\mathcal{D}[\hat{c}_{sv}(t)]\rho_{sv},

(5)

where
{\cal D}[\hat{A}]\bullet=\hat{A}\bullet\hat{A}^{\dagger}-\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\{\hat{A}^{\dagger}\hat{A},\bullet\}
. As shown in Ref.
Kiilerich and Mølmer 2020
, the reduced state of the virtual cavity
\hat{a}_{v}
in the long time limit,
\rho_{v}(\infty)=\text{Tr}_{s}[\rho_{sv}(t=\infty)]
, equals the state of the temporal mode
\hat{b}_{v}
. With the quantum state of the mode at hand, one can simply compute the Wigner function via

W(\alpha)=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\int d^{2}\lambda e^{\lambda^{*}\alpha-\lambda\alpha^{*}}\text{Tr}\left[e^{\lambda\hat{a}_{v}-\lambda^{*}\hat{a}_{v}}\rho_{v}(\infty)\right]\,.

(6)

Appendix B
Energy of an output temporal mode
\hat{b}_{v}

The energy
E_{v}
of a temporal mode
\hat{b}_{v}
can be written as
E_{v}=\omega_{v}\langle\hat{b}_{v}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{v}\rangle
, where
\omega_{v}=\matrixelement{\text{vac}}{\hat{b}_v \hat{H}_{\rm F} \hat{b}^\dagger_v}{\text{vac}}
is the effective frequency of the mode
\hat{b}_{v}
.
Since the temporal profile of our input pulse
u(t)
is real-valued, the incoherent energy kernel
K^{\mathcal{I}}(t,t^{\prime})
is also real-valued, the eigenfunctions
w_{i}(t)
are also real-valued. Since the Fourier transform of a real-valued function is symmetric in
\omega
-space, for each
(\alpha,\Delta)
when we choose
v_{\text{opt}}(t)=w_{\rm inc}(t)
, we have
\omega_{v_{\text{opt}}}=\omega_{q}
. The same thing occurs for
v_{\text{opt}}=w_{\rm ng}(t)
, as long as we restrict ourselves to real-valued linear combinations. Hence, we can simply measure the energy of both the input pulse and the output temporal mode in units of
\omega_{q}
, justifying the treatment of
|\alpha|^{2}
,
\langle\hat{b}_{v}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{v}\rangle
, and other photon-number related quantities as energies in this manuscript. More details can be found in the SM
ref
(See S.2).

Appendix C
Relation between
K^{\mathcal{I}}(t,t^{\prime})
and the incoherent energy

The total energy
E_{v}
of an output mode
\hat{b}_{v}
can be written as

\langle{\hat{b}_{v}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{v}}\rangle=\iint{\rm d}t{\rm d}t^{\prime}\,v^{*}(t)K(t,t^{\prime})v(t^{\prime})

(7)

where
K(t,t^{\prime}):=\langle\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\text{out}}(t)\hat{b}_{\text{out}}(t^{\prime})\rangle
is the usual
Gardiner and Zoller 2004
;
Kiilerich and Mølmer 2020
quantum optical total
energy kernel. Defining the Heisenberg operator
\delta\hat{b}_{\text{out}}(t)=\hat{b}_{\text{out}}(t)-\langle\hat{b}_{\text{out}}(t)\rangle
, we see that
K^{\mathcal{I}}(t,t^{\prime})
is related to
K(t,t^{\prime})
by
K^{\mathcal{I}}(t,t^{\prime})=K(t,t^{\prime})-\langle\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\text{out}}(t)\rangle\langle\hat{b}_{\text{out}}(t^{\prime})\rangle
. Hence, the incoherent energy
E_{\cal I}=\langle{\delta\hat{b}_{v}^{\dagger}\delta\hat{b}_{v}}\rangle
of an output mode
\hat{b}_{v}
can be written as

\langle{\delta\hat{b}_{v}^{\dagger}\delta\hat{b}_{v}}\rangle=\iint{\rm d}t{\rm d}t^{\prime}\,v^{*}(t)K^{\mathcal{I}}(t,t^{\prime})v(t^{\prime}).

(8)

We note that
\langle{\delta\hat{b}_{v}^{\dagger}\delta\hat{b}_{v}}\rangle
takes the largest value when
v(t)
is the eigenfunction of
K^{\mathcal{I}}(t,t^{\prime})
with the largest eigenvalue. Furthermore, because
K^{\mathcal{I}}(t,t^{\prime})=\langle{\hat{\sigma}^{\dagger}(t)\hat{\sigma}(t^{\prime})}\rangle-\langle{\hat{\sigma}^{\dagger}(t)}\rangle\langle{\hat{\sigma}(t^{\prime})}\rangle
, the largest eigenvalue
\delta n_{0}
of
K^{\mathcal{I}}(t,t^{\prime})
is less than
1
.

Appendix E
Fine structure of the incoherent energy

We split the incoherent energy in mode
v
in the following way:

E^{v}_{\cal I}=E^{v}_{\cal S}+E^{v}_{\cal M}+E^{v}_{\cal NG}\,.

(9)

E^{v}_{\cal S}=\left(\sqrt{\langle\delta{X_{\phi}^{2}}\rangle}-\sqrt{\langle\delta{Y_{\phi}^{2}}\rangle}\right)^{2}
, with
X_{\phi}:=(\hat{b}_{v}e^{-i\phi}+\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{v}e^{i\phi})/\sqrt{2}
,
Y_{\phi}:=X_{\phi+\pi/2}
, where
\phi
is chosen such that
\langle\delta{X_{\phi}^{2}}\rangle
is maximal.
E^{v}_{\cal S}
captures an energetic contribution due squeezing.
E_{\cal M}=n_{th}
, with
n_{th}
the number of thermal photons in a thermal state that has the same von Neumann entropy as the state of the mode.
E_{\cal M}

captures an energetic contribution due to the mixedness of the field. The remnant contribution to
E_{\cal I}
,

E^{v}_{\cal NG}=\sqrt{\left(\langle{\delta\hat{b}_{v}^{\dagger}\delta\hat{b}_{v}}\rangle+\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\right)^{2}-|\langle{\delta\hat{b}_{v}^{2}}\rangle|^{2}}-\left(n_{th}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\,.

(10)

is an energetic contribution of non-Gaussian effects, like WN.

Finite rank approximation of
K^{\mathcal{I}}(t,t^{\prime})

For numerical simulations, it is helpful
to perform a finite rank approximation of
K^{\mathcal{I}}(t,t^{\prime})
by setting
to zero the eigenvalues which are less than some
\epsilon
that we can freely
choose. This is mathematically justified since
K^{\mathcal{I}}(t,t^{\prime})
is compact
Conway 2007
. This gives us

\displaystyle K^{\mathcal{I}}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\,\delta n_{j}w_{j}(t)w^{*}_{j}(t^{\prime})

(S.1)

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\,\delta n_{j}w_{j}(t)w^{*}_{j}(t^{\prime})+0\times\sum_{j=m}\,w_{j}(t)w^{*}_{j}(t^{\prime})

(S.2)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\delta n_{j}w_{j}(t)w_{j}^{*}(t^{\prime})+0\times w_{\perp}(t)w_{\perp}^{*}(t^{\prime}),

(S.3)

where the second line follows from the finite rank approximation and the third line by defining

w_{\perp}(t)=\sum_{j\geq m}\frac{\langle\hat{b}_{w_{j}}^{\dagger}\rangle}{\sqrt{\sum_{l\geq m}|\langle\hat{b}_{l}\rangle|^{2}}}w_{j}(t).

(S.4)

Under these assumptions, we will show, momentarily, that
\langle\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{w_{\perp}}\hat{b}_{w_{\perp}}\rangle=\sum_{j\geq m}|\langle\hat{b}_{w_{j}}\rangle|^{2}
, meaning that the output field in the temporal mode
\hat{b}_{w_{\perp}}
is a
coherent state that captures all the coherent energy not in the first
m
modes.
Eq. (
S.2
) can be made
as precise as we wish by choosing
\epsilon
such that the total number of
output photons is approximately equal to the total number of input photons up to some tolerance
f(\epsilon)
,
that is, as long as
|\langle\hat{b}_{w_{\perp}}\rangle|^{2}+\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}|\langle\hat{b}_{w_{j}}\rangle|^{2}+\langle\delta\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{w_{j}}\delta\hat{b}_{w_{j}}\rangle=|\alpha|^{2}-f(\epsilon)\approx|\alpha|^{2}
.

We now show that the orthogonal mode, under the finite rank approximation, captures a coherent field. Let us define the temporal mode
\hat{b}_{z}=\int{\rm d}tz(t)\hat{b}_{\text{out}}(t)
, where
z(t)=\langle{\hat{b}_{\rm out}^{\dagger}(t)}\rangle/|\beta|
, and
|\beta|^{2}:=\int{\rm d}t\,|\langle{\hat{b}_{\rm out}^{\dagger}(t)}\rangle|^{2}
.
Applying a displacement of
|\beta|
on mode
\hat{b}_{z}
, we find
D_{z}(|{\beta}|)\hat{b}_{z}D^{\dagger}_{z}(|{\beta}|)=\hat{b}_{z}-|\beta|=\hat{b}_{z}-\langle b_{z}\rangle
, where
D_{z}(\lambda)=e^{\lambda\hat{b}_{z}^{\dagger}-\lambda^{*}\hat{b}_{z}}
. By defining the displaced field state
|{\text{Field}_{D}}\rangle=D^{\dagger}_{z}(|\beta|)|{\text{Field}}\rangle
, we see that the output energy kernel for this state is equivalent to the incoherent energy kernel,
i.e.,

\displaystyle\matrixelement{\text{Field}_D}{\hat{b}_\text{out}^\dagger(t) \hat{b}_\text{out}(t^\prime)}{\text{Field}_D}

\displaystyle=\matrixelement{\text{Field}}{\delta\hat{b}_\text{out}^\dagger(t) \delta\hat{b}_\text{out}(t^\prime)}{\text{Field}}

(S.5)

\displaystyle=K^{\mathcal{I}}(t,t^{\prime})=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\delta n_{j}w_{j}(t)w_{j}^{*}(t^{\prime})\,.

(S.6)

Since we have assumed that all modes with
j\geq m
have zero incoherent energy, this means that these modes in the displaced field state have zero total energy as well. As such, they must be in the vacuum state,
i.e.
,
|{{\rm Field}_{D}}\rangle=|{\Psi}\rangle_{w_{0},w_{1},\dots,w_{m-1}}\otimes|{\rm vac}\rangle_{w_{m},w_{m+1},\dots}\equiv|{\Psi}\rangle_{w_{0},w_{1},\dots,w_{m-1}}\otimes|{\rm vac}\rangle_{w_{\perp}}
.

Finally, by defining
\langle f,g\rangle=\int dt\,f^{*}(t)g(t)
, we can decompose the
z(t)
mode into the natural orthogonal basis
\{w_{j}(t)\}_{j}
as
z(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\langle w_{j},z\rangle w_{j}(t)+\langle w_{\perp},z\rangle w_{\perp}(t)
,
which allows us to divide the displacement operator as

D_{z}(\lambda)=\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}D_{w_{j}}(\lambda\langle\beta,w_{j}\rangle)D_{w_{\perp}}(\lambda\langle\beta,w_{\perp}\rangle)\,.

(S.7)

Thus, undoing the displacement on the field yields,

\displaystyle|{\text{Field}}\rangle

\displaystyle=D_{z}(|\beta|)|{\text{Field}_{D}}\rangle

(S.8)

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}D_{w_{j}}(\langle\hat{b}_{w_{j}}\rangle)|{\Psi}\rangle_{w_{1},w_{2},\dots w_{m-1}}\right)\otimes\left(D_{w_{\perp}}(\langle\hat{b}_{w_{\perp}}\rangle)|{0}\rangle\right)\,,

(S.9)

where
D_{w_{\perp}}(\langle\hat{b}_{w_{\perp}}\rangle)|{\text{vac}}\rangle
is a coherent state with
|\langle\hat{b}_{w_{\perp}}\rangle|^{2}
number of photons.

In the main text, we considered the case where
K^{\mathcal{I}}(t,t^{\prime})\approx\delta n_{0}w_{0}(t)w_{0}^{*}(t^{\prime})+0\times w_{\perp}(t)w^{*}_{\perp}(t^{\prime})
, where we can construct
w_{\perp}(t)
using the
formula above or equivalently by simply doing
w_{\perp}(t)\propto\langle\hat{b}_{\text{out}}^{\dagger}(t)\rangle-\langle\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{w_{0}}\rangle w_{0}(t)
, where the
normalization constant can be found by enforcing

. Following the above calculations, this means that

|{\text{Field}}\rangle\approx|\psi\rangle_{w_{0}}\otimes|\alpha^{\prime}\rangle_{w_{\perp}}

(S.10)

where

is a coherent state with

Energy of the scattered modes

In this section we show that the energy of both the input and output modes are directly proportional to their number operators, with the constant of proportionality being the characteristic frequency of the TLA,
\omega_{q}
.

First, let us begin with the input mode. The energy of the input field is determined via the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\rm F,in}=\int{\rm d}\omega\,\omega\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{i}(\omega)\hat{b}_{i}(\omega)
, where
\hat{b}_{i}(\omega)
denotes the annihilation operator in the Heisenberg picture before the input light pulse has
interacted with the TLA. The input field annihilation operator, as standard in input-output theory
Gardiner and Collett 1985
, is defined as
\hat{b}_{\text{in}}(t)=\int\frac{dt}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-i\omega(t-t_{i})}\hat{b}_{i}(\omega)
. Also, recall that the mode of the input pulse is defined via
\hat{b}_{u}=\int{\rm d}t\,u(t)e^{i\omega_{q}t}\hat{b}(t)
. Note, here it is not necessary to assume the Gaussian form of
u(t)
we used in the main text, instead, any real-valued
u(t)
will suffice. The energy of the input state
|{\alpha_{\text{in}}}\rangle=e^{\alpha^{*}\hat{b}_{u}-\alpha\hat{b}_{u}^{\dagger}}|{\text{vac}}\rangle
is

\displaystyle\matrixelement{\alpha_\text{in}}{H_{\rm F, in}}{\alpha_\text{in}}

\displaystyle=\int d\omega\,\iint\frac{{\rm d}t{\rm d}t^{\prime}}{2\pi}\omega e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}\matrixelement{\alpha_\text{in}}{\hat{b}^\dagger_\text{in}(t)\hat{b}_\text{in}(t^\prime)}{\alpha_\text{in}}

(S.11)

\displaystyle=|\alpha|^{2}\int d\omega\,\omega\iint\frac{{\rm d}t{\rm d}t^{\prime}}{2\pi}u^{*}(t)u(t^{\prime})e^{-i(\omega-\omega_{q})(t-t^{\prime})}

(S.12)

\displaystyle=|\alpha|^{2}\int d\omega\,\omega|\tilde{u}(\omega-\omega_{q})|^{2}

(S.13)

\displaystyle=\omega_{q}|\alpha|^{2}

(S.14)

\displaystyle=\omega_{q}\langle{\hat{b}_{u}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{u}}\rangle\,.

(S.15)

where,
\tilde{u}(\omega)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int{\rm d}tu(t)e^{-i\omega t}
and we have used the fact that the Fourier transform of a real-valued function is symmetric.

Now, let us consider the output modes. The energy of the output field is determined via the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\rm F,out}=\int{\rm d}\omega\,\omega\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{f}(\omega)\hat{b}_{f}(\omega)
, where
\hat{b}_{f}(\omega)
denotes the annihilation operator in the Heisenberg picture after the light pulse has finished interacting with the TLA. The output field annihilation operator is defined as
\hat{b}_{\text{out}}(t)=\int\frac{dt}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-i\omega(t-t_{f})}\hat{b}_{f}(\omega)
. Let
\{w_{k}(t)\}_{k}
be the eigenfunctions of
K^{\mathcal{I}}(t,t^{\prime})
, and consider the temporal mode functions
\{e^{i\omega_{q}t}w_{k}(t)\}_{k}
which form an orthonormal basis,
i.e.
,
\int{\rm d}tw_{k}^{*}(t)w_{\ell}(t)=\delta_{k\ell}
. Note,
\{w_{k}(t)\}_{k}
are real-valued as the kernel is both real-valued and symmetric. The full output field after the interaction with the TLA can be written as

|{\text{Field}}\rangle=\sum_{n_{1},n_{2},\dots}c_{n_{1},n_{2},\dots}|{n_{1}}\rangle_{w_{1}}\otimes|{n_{2}}\rangle_{w_{2}}\dots\,,

(S.16)

where
|{n}\rangle_{w_{k}}=\frac{(\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{w_{k}})^{n}}{\sqrt{n}}|{\text{vac}}\rangle
and
\hat{b}_{w_{k}}=\int{\rm d}tw_{k}(t)\hat{b}_{\text{out}}(t)
. The total energy of the output field is

\displaystyle\matrixelement{\text{Field}}{\hat{H}_{\rm F, out}}{\text{Field}}

\displaystyle=\int{\rm d}\omega\,\omega\langle{\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{f}(\omega)\hat{b}_{f}(\omega)}\rangle

(S.17)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,\ell}\int{\rm d}\omega\,\iint\frac{{\rm d}t{\rm d}t^{\prime}}{2\pi}e^{-i(\omega-\omega_{q})(t-t^{\prime})}w_{k}(t)w^{*}_{\ell}(t^{\prime})\langle{\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{w_{\ell}}\hat{b}_{w_{k}}}\rangle

(S.18)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,\ell}\Omega_{k\ell}\langle{\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{w_{k}}\hat{b}_{w_{\ell}}}\rangle,

(S.19)

where
\Omega_{k\ell}=\int{\rm d}\omega\,\omega\tilde{w}_{k}(\omega-\omega_{q})\tilde{w}^{*}_{\ell}(\omega-\omega_{q})
.

Simplifying
\Omega_{k\ell}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Omega_{k\ell}

\displaystyle=\int{\rm d}\omega\,\omega\tilde{w}_{k}(\omega-\omega_{q})\tilde{w}^{*}_{\ell}(\omega-\omega_{q})

(S.20)

\displaystyle=\omega_{q}\int{\rm d}\nu\,\tilde{w}_{k}(\nu)\tilde{w}^{*}_{\ell}(\nu)+\int{\rm d}\nu\,\omega\tilde{w}_{k}(\nu)\tilde{w}^{*}_{\ell}(\nu)

(S.21)

\displaystyle=\omega_{q}\delta_{k\ell}+\int{\rm d}\omega\,\omega\left[\underbrace{\frac{\tilde{w}_{k}(\nu)\tilde{w}_{\ell}^{*}(\nu)+\tilde{w}_{k}^{*}(\nu)\tilde{w}_{\ell}(\nu)}{2}}_{\text{Even function in }\nu}+i\frac{\tilde{w}_{k}(\nu)\tilde{w}_{\ell}^{*}(\nu)-\tilde{w}_{k}^{*}(\nu)\tilde{w}_{\ell}(\nu)}{2i}\right]

(S.22)

\displaystyle=\omega_{q}\delta_{k\ell}+\int{\rm d}\omega\,\omega\left[\frac{\tilde{w}_{k}(\nu)\tilde{w}_{\ell}^{*}(\nu)-\tilde{w}_{k}^{*}(\nu)\tilde{w}_{\ell}(\nu)}{2}\right]

(S.23)

where, in the second line, we have defined
\nu=\omega-\omega_{q}
and used the fact that
\tilde{w}_{k}(\omega)=\tilde{w}_{k}^{*}(-\omega)
, since
w_{k}(t)
are real. Using the above result, we can check that

\displaystyle\sum_{k\neq\ell}\Omega_{k\ell}\langle{\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{w_{k}}\hat{b}_{w_{\ell}}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{k<\ell}(\Omega_{k\ell}+\Omega_{\ell k})\langle{\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{w_{k}}\hat{b}_{w_{\ell}}}\rangle

\displaystyle=0.

(S.24)

As such, the energy of the output mode is given by

\matrixelement{\text{Field}}{\hat{H}_{\rm F, out}}{\text{Field}}=\omega_{q}\sum_{k}\langle\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{w_{k}}\hat{b}_{w_{k}}\rangle\,.

(S.25)

Importantly, we can see that each mode
\hat{b}_{w_{k}}
contributes to the total energy by their photonic occupation, with each mode having the same proportionality being
\omega_{q}
. We do consider linear combinations of these mode functions to construct
w_{\rm ng}(t)=e^{i\omega_{q}t}\sum_{k}c_{k}w_{k}(t)
, the above analysis also holds provided the coefficients
c_{k}
are real, which is the case in the main text.

Details of numerical optimization of
E_{\mathcal{N}\mathcal{G}}

In the main text, it was mentioned that for each
(\alpha,\Delta)
of the input pulse, we used the functions
w_{i}(t)
which are the eigenvalues of
K^{\mathcal{I}}
to construct a new mode

v_{\alpha,\Delta}(t)=\sum_{j}c_{j}w_{j}(t)

(S.26)

where
c_{i}
are chosen such that
v_{\alpha,\Delta}(t)
is square normalised.

Obtaining a displaced single photon state in the output temporal mode

In the main text, it was mentioned that when the input pulse
u(t)=(\pi\Delta^{2})^{-1/4}{\rm exp}[-(t-t_{0})^{2}/2\Delta^{2}]
approximates a finite-width, finite-intensity
(2k+1)\pi
-pulse, where
k\in{\mathbb{Z}^{+}}
, if we were to pick
v_{\alpha,\Delta}(t)=w_{0}(t)
, we would see a single photon state in the temporal mode
\hat{b}_{w_{0}}
with high fidelity.

The displacement in the single photon state can be easily understood by first computing

\displaystyle\langle\hat{b}_{w_{0}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\,w_{0}(t)\langle\hat{b}_{\text{out}}(t)\rangle

(S.27)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\,w_{0}(t)\left(\langle\hat{b}_{\text{in}}(t)\rangle+\sqrt{\gamma}\langle\sigma(t)\rangle\right)

(S.28)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\,w_{0}(t)\frac{(2k+1)\sqrt{\pi}}{2\sqrt{2}\Delta}e^{-\frac{(t-t_{0})^{2}}{2\Delta^{2}}}+\sqrt{\gamma}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\,w_{0}(t)\langle\sigma(t)\rangle\,,

(S.29)

where in the last line, we used the fact that the input state performs a
(2k+1)\pi
pulse on the TLA,
i.e.
, that
\int{\rm d}t\alpha u(t)=(2k+1)\frac{\pi}{2}
for
k\in{\mathbb{Z}^{+}}
. In the limit of
\Delta\ll\gamma
and
\alpha\gg 1
the Gaussian envelope approximates a Dirac
\delta
-function, yielding
\langle\hat{b}_{w_{0}}\rangle\approx\frac{(2k+1)\pi}{2}w(t_{0})
. This is approximates an ideal
(2k+1)\pi
-pulse. The output temporal mode for the single photon scattered from such a pulse is well-known to be
w_{0}(t)=\Theta(t-t_{0})e^{-\gamma t/2}
, where
\Theta(t-t_{0})
is the Heaviside step function with
\Theta(t_{0})=0
.
An illustration of
the above for a
\pi
-pulse with
\Delta=0.01
is shown in Figure
S2
. Since we are using continuous functions as output temporal mode functions, the Heaviside step function is replaced with a straight line interpolation such that
w(t_{0})=1/2
, and we can expect a displacement of
\pi/4\approx 0.785
. Here we only see a displacement of
0.762
, which we can attribute to finiteness of the pulse.
