Decoding full-length factor VIII through the structural and functional lens of its B domain
Authors: Samhitha Urs Ramaraje Urs, Jean-Luc Pellequer, Jean-Marie Teulon, Boxue Ma, Deniz Ugurlar, Simone Gasper, Anna Pepanian, Jens Müller, Diana Imhof, Sneha Singh, Johannes Oldenburg, Arijit Biswas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•The B domain of FVIII stabilizes it by shielding crucial domains, preventing early activation and inhibitor binding.•B-domain glycosylation and cleavage are key to FVIII conformational stability, aiding in proper folding and secretion.

Introduction

Factor VIII (FVIII) is a large glycoprotein that acts as a nonenzymatic cofactor of activated FIX (FIXa) leading to proteolytic activation of FX. An incapacity of FVIII activity causes the coagulation defect referred to as hemophilia A (HA) affecting 1 in 5000 male newborns.1, 2, 3 FVIII is synthesized as a single-chain protein of ∼300 kDa by liver sinusoidal endothelial cells.4, 5, 6 The mature protein FVIII has the domain structure A1-a1-A2-a2-B-a3-C1-C2 and circulates as a heterodimer consisting of the heavy chain HCA1-A2-B and the light chain LCA3-C1-C2 in the blood. Its canonical structure bears a threefold symmetry under divalent metal binding (Cu2+ and Ca2+), which stabilizes thrombin-cleaved domains after activation. B domain, the largest and heavily glycosylated part (1000 amino acids), cleaved from FVIII during activation, is functionally dispensable for cofactor activity but is involved in FVIII secretion.7, 8, 9, 10, 11 Although recombinant FVIII (rFVIII) products often lack or partially include this domain (ie, B-domain–deleted FVIII [BDD-FVIII]), some FVIII variants retaining parts of the B domain have been shown to enhance synthesis and secretion efficiency.12 The uncharacterized B domain poses a key challenge in developing FVIII-based therapies, requiring a deeper understanding of FVIII biology to mimic the native protein, enhance tolerance, extend half-life, and clarify its lifecycle. Many of these challenges could be addressed by resolving the full structure of native FVIII, including the B domain.13, 14, 15, 16, 17 The intrinsic disorder and uniqueness of the B domain have made it challenging for traditional biophysical techniques and modeling approaches to resolve its structure. To address these knowledge gaps, we used an integrated approach that combines computational models of both full-length FVIII (FL-FVIII) and the FL-FVIII–von Willebrand factor (FL-VWF) complex, along with atomic force microscopy (AFM) and cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM) data from rFL-FVIII and plasma-derived FL-FVIII (pdFL-FVIII) proteins.18, 19, 20, 21 This approach allowed us to explore the structural and functional characteristics of the B domain, including its roles in intracellular processing, interactions with inhibitory antibodies, and binding partners such as VWF.

Materials and methods

Generating a physically realistic FL-FVIII computational structural model

The FL-FVIII computational model was constructed using the AlphaFold2 prediction for coagulation FVIII (AF-FL-FVIII; UniProt ID: P00451) as the primary reference.22, 23, 24, 25 To represent the glycosylation of FL-FVIII, 19 N-linked glycans were incorporated into the AF-FL-FVIII model after experimentally verified glycosylation patterns reported in previous studies,26, 27, 28 using the Glycam Server, with 14 located within the B domain and 5 distributed across the A and C domains. Figure 1A illustrates the glycosylated model and the sites schematically. The glycosylated model was cleaved at the primary PACE/Furin cleavage site (R1667) to mimic native conditions and was named the “Gly/FC-FL-FVIII” model. Additionally, the nonglycosylated model, named “NGly/FC-FL-FVIII” (nonglycosylated FC-FL-FVIII) was retained to observe the effect of glycosylation. Models were subjected to an all-atom molecular dynamics simulation for >300 nanoseconds using the YASARA structure suite (supplemental Methods, model simulation).29, 30, 31 Subsequently, a comparative detailed structural analysis, including root mean square deviation (RMSD) and root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), the radius of gyration (Rg), hydrogen bonds (H-bonds), and total potential energy, were analyzed for the simulation-equilibrated models. The simulation results in this study only represent data from single simulations for both models. Further replicas will need to confirm original molecular dynamics simulations results, avoid false-positive results, and identify any errors in the software.

Assembling the computational FL-FVIII-VWF complex structural model

The D′-D3 domains from the cryo-EM structure (Protein Data Bank [PDB] ID: 7KWO)32 were superimposed onto the simulation-equilibrated Gly/FC-FL-FVIII model, to create the Gly/FC(1×)-FL-FVIII-VWF-D′-D3 complex model (furin cleaved at R1667). Additionally, a second furin cleavage at R1332 in addition to R1667 was introduced in this model to generate an alternative complex, the Gly/FC(2×)-FL-FVIII-VWF-D′-D3 complex model. Further minimization, remodeling, and refinement of all the models were performed using COOT,33 MIFit, PyMOL,34 and XPLOR version 3.8,35 and model quality was analyzed with MolProbity (supplemental Methods, model refinement).20,33, 34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39 Final coordinates can be found in the Model Archive (https://www.modelarchive.org/) with accession identifiers ma-ooxos (Gly/FC-FL-FVIII model) and ma-8ueqd (Gly/FC[1×]-FL-FVIII-D′-D3 complex model).

Acquiring AFM topographs and validating the computational models by fitting AFM topographs using DockAFM

Highly purified rFL-FVIII and rBDD-FVIII samples (supplemental Methods, purification of FVIII) were deposited on mica (Bruker, Santa Barbara) and imaged under in-air and in-liquid conditions (50 μL of water). AFM images were obtained on a multimode 8-nanoscope V-controller (Bruker, Santa Barbara, CA). Triangular ScanAsyst-Fluid probes (k = 0.7 N/m; F0 = 150 kHz; L = 70 μm; tip radius: 20 nm; BrukerAFMProbes, Camarillo, CA) were used. PeakForceTapping was used in a semi-automated mode with default parameters (ramp: 150 nm) except for the gains (manually adjusted). Peak-2 fractionated pdFL-FVIII-VWF complex (supplemental Figure 1) was imaged on a DriveAFM microscope (Nanosurf AG) with WM0.6AuD cantilever (k = 0.6 N/m; F0 = 350 kHz; L = 35 μm; tip radius: 10 nm). AFM topographs were processed in Gwyddion40 (plane fitting, line correction), destriped,41 and Laplacian-filtered.42,43 A comparative DockAFM44,45 analysis used the rFL-FVIII, rBDD-FVIII, and pdFL-FVIII-VWF complex topographs as stationary objects and their respective refined structural models as moving objects, with a 1283 grid (4.9 Å, XY; 4 Å, Z), baseline threshold of 30 Å, forbidden layer of 25 Å, favorable layer of 40 Å, and 6° rotation step, with solutions ranked by pseudoenergy scores at a given XYZ offset in the grid.44, 45, 46, 47, 48

In addition, cryo-EM attempts were also performed to visualize the overall architecture of FL-FVIII and its complexes, aiming to capture the spatial organization of the B domain relative to the core, detailed in the supplemental Methods.

Analyzing the computational models to elucidate the functional roles of the B domain

Electrostatic analysis was conducted with Adaptive Poisson-Boltzmann Solver (PyMOL)34,48 to evaluate columbic potential differences between Gly/FC-FL-FVIII and BDD-FVIII, providing insights into FL-FVIII’s structural features and the functional roles of the B domain. Complementary solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) analysis (calculated using MSMS49; threshold of 1 Å) quantified solvent-exposed regions of both models, with and without the B domain and glycans. Residue-specific and total SASA values were then compared to assess their influence on inhibitory antibody binding, molecular interactions, and hemophilic mutations (listed categorically).

Results

Detailed structural characteristics and effect of glycosylation observed in the refined computational models

The Gly/FC-FL-FVIII model exhibited minimal RMSD, RMSF, and Rg fluctuations (0.5-1 Å) during the production phase of simulation, with an average of 1950 H-bonds, indicating robust intramolecular interactions promoting a stable structural conformation (supplemental Figure 3). The NGly/FC-FL-FVIII model remained unequilibrated even after 350 nanoseconds. The Gly/FC-FL-FVIII model maintained the pseudo-threefold symmetry of the A1, A2, and A3 domains and the coplanar orientation of the C1 and C2 domains.20 Visually, the Gly/FC-FL-FVIII model exhibited a compact, stable structure (X: 187.5 Å and Y: 178.9 Å), with the B domain wrapped around the central globular core, unlike the NGly/FC-FL-FVIII model (X: 185.7 Å and Y: 130 Å), in which the B domain unfolded into a tail-like appendage. Because the source AF-FL-FVIII model lacked furin cleavage, it displayed structural instability and significant divergence from the final simulation equilibrated model (Figure 1A). The B domain interacted specifically with each domain in HC and LC. The HC(A1-A2)–B-domain and LC(A3-C1-C2)–B-domain interfaces (DIs) displayed 29 and 11 primary H-bonds, respectively. The A1-B region has 3 DIs covering the first thrombin cleavage site (R391). A2-B has 4 DIs covering the second site (R759). The a3-A3-B region has 4, C1-B has 2, and C2-B has 2 DIs, covering the third thrombin cleavage site (R1708; supplemental Table 2; Figure 1B). The furin-cleaved (R1667) FL-FVIII-VWF-D′-D3 complex showed DIs between the VWF D′-D3 domains and FVIII’s B, a3, A3, C1, and C2 domains. Overall, 11 H-bonds were observed between the B and D′ domains (R1650-S6801, R1650-R6782, and T1651-R6782; Figure 2A; supplemental Table 3). These interactions disappeared when half of the B domain was removed at the secondary furin cleavage (R1332) in the FL-FVIII-VWF-D′-D3 complex model (Figure 2B). Further validation of these detailed structure models revealed MolProbity scores of 1.65 (Gly/FC-FL-FVIII) and 1.57 (FL-FVIII-VWF-D′-D3 complex). Stereochemical analysis exhibited RMS bond and angles of 0.0147° and 1.83° (Gly/FC-FL-FVIII) and 0.0146° and 1.86° (FL-FVIII-VWF-D′-D3 complex). With lower clash scores and fewer Ramachandran outliers, both models demonstrated superior quality compared with the source AF-FL-FVIII model (supplemental Figure 2; supplemental Table 4).

AFM and cryo-EM reconstructions reveal the sparse distribution of the B domain around the core, akin to the detailed structural models

AFM and cryo-EM analyses provided complementary insights into FVIII architecture, both revealing the sparse and flexible distribution of the B domain around the core, consistent with the computational models (Figure 3A). Due to the low resolution (∼7-8 Å), the cryo-EM reconstructions are now provided as supplemental Figure 4 for reference. AFM analysis revealed rFL-FVIII and rBDD-FVIII as globular, whereas pdFL-FVIII-VWF had elongated VWF tails with a globular FVIII structure (Figure 3B). rFL-FVIII topographs displayed a height and width of 6 to 7 nm and 30 to 40 nm, respectively, whereas rBDD-FVIII displayed a height and width of 4 to 5 nm and 20 to 30 nm, respectively (Figure 3B). The pdFL-FVIII-VWF complex had a globular height of 6 to 7 nm, a tail height of 3 to 4 nm, with a width of 80 to 90 nm (Figure 4B). The DockAFM result of the Gly/FC-FL-FVIII model to FL-FVIII topographs showed the lowest docking energy (DE) of −48.10 kcal/mol with −14.93, −11.84, 2.07 displacement (Figure 4A). Similarly, the rBDD-FVIII topograph displayed the lowest DE of −48.40 kcal/mol (3 CDZ), with 2.99, 24.46, and 5.27 displacement (Figure 4B). The FL-FVIII-VWF-D′-D3 complex model also revealed DE of −63.60 kcal/mol with −53.07, −142.77, and −1.49 displacement (Figure 4C; supplemental Figure 5). Compact docking results displaying the lowest DE and minimal displacement validated the quality of the models.

Electrostatics and SASA analyses highlight the B domain’s crucial role in defining molecular interaction surfaces for FVIII

The Coulombic values for Gly/FC-FL-FVIII, (HC(A1-A2) mean: 0.96 kcal/mol/e; LC(A3-C1-C2) mean: 0.37 kcal/mol/e) were significantly different than those of the BDD-FVIII structure (PDB ID: 3CDZ; HC(A1-A2), mean: −1.91 kcal/mol/e; LC(A3-C1-C2) mean: −0.26 kcal/mol/e) highlighting the impact of the B domain on overall electrostatics (supplemental Figure 5). Comparative SASA analysis showed reduced epitope accessibility for most inhibitory antibodies in the Gly/FC-FL-FVIII model, primarily attributed to the B domain and its glycosylation.

Inhibitory antibodies

Structural alignment of the Gly/FC-FL-FVIII model with inhibitory antibody complexes revealed that the B domain hinders the binding site epitopes (supplemental Figure 10). Any reduction in the FVIII solvent accessibility due to the presence of the B domain will provoke a steric hindrance preventing inhibitors from reaching FVIII epitopes. The 4KI5 and 4XZU anti-C2 (G99 and 3E6 Fab HC, LC) structure shows that B-domain residues (listed in Table 1) overlap with the G99 and 3E6 Fab HC, LC interaction site on the C2 domain, reducing their SASA by −9.54 Å2 and −3.32 Å2, respectively, in Gly/FC-FL-FVIII (Figure 5A). Discussion

The B domain wraps the central core of the FVIII protein

Our AFM investigations showed that both rFL-FVIII and rBDD-FVIII adopt globular conformations, whereas pdFL-FVIII-VWF displays globular structures with tail-like extensions. The observed height and width differences (2-3 nm) between rFL-FVIII and rBDD-FVIII indicate that the B domain contributes substantially to FVIII architecture. In contrast to earlier reports that described the B domain as forming independent peripheral appendages, our AFM imaging indicates that the B domain instead wraps around the core, rather than projecting as a separate structure (Figure 3A-B). In air, FL-FVIII showed sparse regions surrounding a dense center, supporting a globular organization with the B domain encircling the core (Figures 3A and 4A-B).47,77 AFM dockings frequently revealed the C1 and C2 domains oriented toward the negatively charged mica surface, resembling physiological interactions with phospholipid membranes (supplemental Figure 5). In the pdFL-FVIII-VWF complex, globular features matched unbound FVIII, whereas the tail-like regions likely corresponded to associated VWF multimers (Figure 4C). Multimer analysis confirmed that peak 2, representing the FVIII-VWF complex, contained dimeric to hexameric VWF species (Figure 4; supplemental Figure 1). Cryo-EM reconstructions were hindered by conformational disorder and low resolution, precluding definitive B-domain modeling. Although early transmission electron microscopy studies proposed a rod-like B domain in porcine FVIII, our cryo-EM data revealed structural diversity in rFL-FVIII and improved, but still heterogeneous, conformations in pdFL-FVIII (supplemental Figure 4B). Rigid body fitting indicated a dispersed B domain enveloping the core domains (supplemental Figure 4A-B). Collectively, these findings support that the FL-FVIII is a globular structure with the B domain wrapping around the core domains, only exposing the C domains that bind to membranes and VWF, hinting to its shielding role.

Critical roles of glycosylation and furin cleavage in the B domain in maintaining the structure of FL-FVIII

The B domain, rich in complex-type and high-mannose N-linked oligosaccharides, is known to play essential roles in FVIII intracellular processing and secretion. In our Gly/FC-FL-FVIII models, B-domain glycosylation markedly stabilized FVIII with a compact globular architecture with lower RMSD, reduced Rg, and more H-bonds, and suppressed RMSF fluctuations in B-domain regions (residues 1000-1600, 1800-2000) compared with the NGly/FC-FL-FVIII (supplemental Figure 3). This stabilization is consistent with glycosylation raising the free energy of the unfolded state, thereby preventing aggregation and enhancing folded-state stability.78 Several O-linked glycans (S1478, T1483, and T1491) are predicted to enhance hydration and confer flexibility to B-domain loops, supporting a dynamically wrapped conformation rather than a rigid, fixed structure.79,80 Although key N-glycosylation sites (N1829, N2137, N2150) facilitate interactions with endoplasmic reticulum chaperones (calnexin, calreticulin) and sorting receptors (LMAN1, MCFD2) to ensure proper folding and secretion of FVIII, the absence of these glycans in BDD-FVIII reduces LMAN1-MCFD2 binding, highlighting the B domain’s role in efficient cargo recognition. Beyond intracellular processing, B-domain glycans mediate clearance through high-affinity interactions with lipoprotein receptor–related protein 1 (Kd ≈ 20nM), ASGPR (Kd ≈ 2nM),28,81, 82, 83, 84, 85 as well as lectin and scavenger receptors SIGLEC5 (Kd ≈ 50nM), CLEC4M (≈ 25nM), STAB2, SCARA1, SCARA5, and MGL.86 These interactions underline the dual role of B-domain glycosylation in FVIII stability, clearance, and half-life regulation. This concept aligns with the design of extended-half-life FVIII products such as rurioctocog alfa pegol (Adynovate), in which targeted B-domain modifications and PEGylation reduce receptor-mediated uptake, thereby enhancing plasma persistence.87 Furthermore, our data identify glycosylation as the primary stabilizing factor, because removal of glycans leads to elevated RMSD values and domain drift even when furin cleavage is retained. The combined presence of glycosylation and furin cleavage in our Gly/FC-FL-FVIII, Gly/FC(1x)-FL-FVIII-D′-D3, and Gly/FC(2x)-FL-FVIII-D′-D3 complex models demonstrates their synergistic role in relieving structural strain and promoting B-domain unwrapping during Golgi processing, thereby facilitating VWF interaction and maintaining conformational stability. We strongly support the fact that furin cleavage complements B-domain glycosylation by enhancing secretion efficiency and overall structural stability (Figure 2B).

How the B domain shields the remainder of FVIII

Our model differs from the source AF-FL-FVIII model because in the source model, the B domain hinders access to key functional regions in the C domains. However, the Gly/FC-FL-FVIII model maintains the pseudo-threefold symmetry in the A domains and allows for unobstructed access to the functionally important regions within the C domains. This enables direct interactions with critical binding partners such as VWF, thrombin, and phosphatidylserine surfaces, preserving FVIII’s functional integrity. Additionally, we infer the shielding mechanism of the B domain based on the steric hindrance and physical overlap. Reduction in the SASA occurs either via overlapping of the B-domain residues with the binding epitopes or by indirectly influencing the accessibility of the surrounding regions near the epitopes. Furthermore, electrostatics analysis of our models indicates that although the B domain slightly redistributes surface potentials on the C domains,88 the conserved basic clefts centered on R2182 and R2339 retain their positive charge, maintaining equivalent phosphatidylserine-binding interfaces (supplemental Figure 5).7,63 Such modulation may fine-tune FVIII orientation within the tenase complex, reducing nonspecific activation or immune exposure.

Inhibitors

Comparative SASA studies in our models demonstrate that the B domain plays a significant shielding role by limiting the accessibility of key immunogenic epitopes. Specifically, decreased solvent exposure of the R503-I527 region in the FL-FVIII model, which interacts with FIXa and lipoprotein receptor–related protein 1 and has been reported as highly immunogenic,51, 52, 53, 54, 55, 56,58, 59, 60,89 suggests that the B domain contributes to reducing inhibitor binding and premature activation by thrombin. In the context of anti-C2 inhibitors, our data show that B-domain residues directly overlap antibody binding sites, potentially mitigating their neutralizing effects on FL-FVIII (Figure 5A). Similarly, anti-C1 antibodies (ET3i/2A9, NB2E9, 8TY1, NB33, NBG12) exhibited reduced accessibility to membrane-interactive sites in the presence of the B domain (Table 1; Figure 5A). These observations align with previous reports indicating that VWF binding shields the C1 and C2 domains from recognition and uptake by immunocompetent cells.90 Together with the B domain, this interaction provides a cooperative “dual shielding” effect that limits epitope exposure and mitigates FVIII immunogenicity. Supporting this, FVIII variants, engineered to form ultrastable complexes with VWF, show significantly reduced anti-FVIII immune responses in vivo.91,92 Clinically, these structural insights are consistent with reduced inhibitor development among patients receiving FL-FVIII products and highlight design strategies for next-generation FVIII therapeutics with improved immunological tolerability.93,94

Shielding from interacting partners

In our FL-FVIII model, the B domain partially covers the A2 domain, blocking access to thrombin/FXa cleavage sites. Previous studies have shown that BDD-FVIII variants exhibit reduced activity in 1-stage clotting assays (one-stage clotting and chromogenic substrate assay ≈ 0.7) compared with FL-FVIII79,86 suggesting a slower thrombin-mediated activation and in support of this, the SASA variations between the Gly/FC-FL-FVIII model and the BDD-FVIII structure suggest that the B domain shields thrombin cleavage sites (R391, R759, R1708), and thrombin-binding regions in A1 and A3 domains (Figure 5B). The first cleavage site (R759), with its notably reduced accessibility compared with the other 2 sites, might be instrumental in preventing premature activation, thus affecting FVIII activation kinetics and proteolytic accessibility (Figure 5B). This kinetic regulation may underlie the assay discrepancies between BDD-FVIII and FL-FVIII, highlighting the B domain’s functional significance beyond structural stabilization.13,60,62,95,96 Additionally, furin-mediated cleavage plays a crucial role in B-domain release, which is essential for VWF binding. Previous studies have shown that the primary furin cleavage unlocks the region in which VWF-D′-D3 is known to bind.32 Similarly, our FL-FVIII-VWF-D′-D3 complex model showed that the primary furin cleavage (R1667) exposes binding regions by removing certain B-domain residues that would otherwise sterically interfere with VWF-D′ binding. The secondary cleavage (R1332) partially unwraps the B domain, allowing efficient and stable VWF binding (Figure 2B).9,29, 30, 31,64,95 In combination, SASA analyses indicate that the B domain shields thrombin sites, yet FL-FVIII activates faster than BDD-FVIII, likely due to glycosylation and furin processing that optimize thrombin engagement. Thus, the B domain serves as a regulatory shield, preventing premature activation while ensuring efficient cleavage.

Mutations

Changes in SASA quantify how mutations alter residue exposure and folding stability or binding propensity. An increase in SASA at normally buried sites can signal local destabilization or misfolding, whereas reduced SASA near functional interfaces may impair partner recognition. Several mutations affecting thrombin cleavage, VWF binding, and tyrosine sulfation were observed to be buried within the B domain (supplemental Figure 7A; Table 2).68,69 Notably, p.G1456E may disrupt folding due to glycine’s role in maintaining flexibility, whereas p.L1182V and p.F1194V at the B-A1 interface increase SASA and introduce steric clashes that weaken interdomain cohesion and secretion efficiency (Table 2). Mutations on or near glycosylation sites (p.M798T) may further hasten FVIII degradation. Missense mutations located at its interface with the A1 domain appear to disrupt critical interactions that maintain FVIII structural integrity. The mutations p.L1182V and p.F1194V were identified at the B domain–A1 DI, disrupt H-bonding, and introduce steric clashes that could destabilize the DI (supplemental Figure 7B; Table 2). This disruption likely impairs proper alignment and structural cohesion between the B and A1 domains, potentially compromising FVIII function. Similarly, the p.G1437Q introduces steric clashes with surrounding residues in the B domain, which could hinder the local structural flexibility necessary for FVIII stability. Our FL-FVIII model shows an overall reduction in SASA for inhibitor epitopes and various mutations (irrespective of the type of mutational pathogenicity) in the presence of the B domain; however, this effect can vary and is subjective. In some cases, the mutated epitope, together with the B domain, may form a neoepitope that could provoke a stronger inhibitory response.3,70,97,98 Collectively, the SASA analyses, and mapping of pathogenic variants offers a structural framework to interpret genotype-phenotype correlations and supports the relevance of the B domain in maintaining functional and immunological properties of FVIII. Although most B-domain missense variants are benign or of uncertain significance, their clustering near interdomain junctions and glycosylation motifs suggests modulatory effects on folding, secretion, or epitope presentation rather than direct impairment of coagulant activity. Thus, although B-domain mutations seldom cause severe HA independently, our model indicates they may modulate FVIII expression or influence inhibitor risk under specific conditions.

Together, our findings indicate that the B domain functions as a dynamic regulator and its glycosylated envelope protects FVIII from premature activation and immune recognition, whereas furin-mediated unwrapping enables timely interaction with VWF and thrombin. This dual modulation likely underlies the functional differences between FL- and BDD-FVIII, positioning the B domain as a key coordinator of FVIII activation, trafficking, and immune tolerance.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study highlights the critical role of the B domain in maintaining the structural integrity and functional stability of FL-FVIII. By shielding key domains and cleavage sites, it prevents premature activation and inhibitor binding, whereas glycosylation promotes folding and stability. These findings advance understanding of FVIII maturation and immune recognition, providing a foundation for engineering B-domain modifications that enhance stability and tolerance. This work also demonstrates the value of artificial intelligence–based modeling, such as AlphaFold2, in accelerating structural-functional analysis and guiding the rational design of improved HA therapeutics.
