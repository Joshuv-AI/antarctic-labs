Introduction
Each year, Brookhaven National Laboratory (BNL) is required to provide a report of its completed
Laboratory Directed Research and Development Program (LDRD) projects to the Department of
Energy (DOE) Office of Scientific and Technical Information in accordance with DOE Order
413.2C Chg1 (MinChg) dated August 2, 2018. This summary report provides an overview of all
the LDRD projects funded at Brookhaven that concluded in FY 2024 in satisfaction of that
requirement. The fiscal year in which each project was funded initially is indicated by the first
two digits of the LDRD project number.

The investments that BNL makes in its LDRD program support the Laboratory’s strategic goals
and priority programs as well as new areas of research and competencies at the Laboratory that are
consistent with the Lab’s vision and mission. Specifically, BNL’s LDRD Program funds projects
to advance science and technology and develop scientific tools that provide remarkable insights
into our physical and biological world in support of DOE’s mission. This discretionary R&D
tool stimulates the scientific-technological community by advancing the frontiers of
science, technology, and engineering; enabling agile responses to energy, environmental,
and national security challenges; and developing tomorrow’s scientific and technical workforce.

This Program Activities Report represents the future of BNL science and technology; it is an
impressive body of exploratory work that investigates many scientific and technical directions in
support of the DOE and BNL missions.

 21-013
 Development of an Integrated Multi-scale Bioimaging Capability
 Qun Liu, Liguo Wang, Xianghui, Yong Chu, Yang Yang, Tim Paape, Yuewei Lin, and Jun Liu

PURPOSE:
One fundamental question underlying all living organisms is the need to understand their
hierarchical organizations and physical changes with the necessary spatial and temporal
resolutions under physiological or pathological conditions. This is a multi-scale challenge
requiring imaging from sub-nanometers to micrometers in a cellular context. While individual
imaging techniques are available, there is a critical need to integrate them into a workflow
capability. Our objective is to develop an integrated multi-disciplinary and multi-scale bioimaging
capability at Brookhaven National Laboratory (BNL). The capability expands BNL’s existing
facility operation program in bioimaging and positions BNL in a leadership position in bioimaging
research. The capability also addresses the grand challenges of the Department of Energy (DOE)
science programs for national bioenergy sustainability and security.

One of the major challenges in bioscience is achieving a comprehensive understanding of
hierarchical living systems and their physical changes, with the necessary spatial and temporal
resolution under physiological or pathological conditions. Addressing this challenge requires
multiple disciplinary efforts in technology development for visualization of biological processes
ranging from atoms, molecules, organelles, and cells to tissues with spatial resolutions from sub-
nanometers to micrometers in a cellular context. However, due to the highly heterogeneous,
dynamic, and adaptive nature of living organisms, understanding such complexity is challenging
mainly due to a lack of multi-scale imaging capabilities that can visualize transient biological
processes under the cellular context and physiological conditions. Multi-scale imaging is
recognized by the DOE Office of Biological and Environmental Research (BER) as a major grand
challenge.

We are developing a bioimaging capability integrating three different, but highly complementary
imaging techniques (light, X-ray, and electron imaging techniques) to quantitatively explore
cellular structures and dynamics at multiple scales in a cellular and physiological context. We are
also developing machine-learning-based computational methods for imaging data segmentation
and aggregation for visualization of biological significance.

The project started on April 1, 2021. During our previous reporting cycles, we
• Implemented a three-dimensional (3D) live-cell imaging system
• Implemented a telescope for cell labeling, targeting, and centering at the Full Field X-ray
 Imaging (FXI) beamline
• Tested cryogenic X-ray tomography and X-ray fluorescence imaging at the FXI and
 Submicron Resolution X-ray Spectroscopy (SRX) beamlines at National Synchrotron Light
 Source II (NSLS-II)
• Developed a preliminary U-Net transformer for cell segmentation
• Developed a cell tracking system using a distance-based algorithm

• Obtained $1.5M from DOE BER for purchase of a cryogenic focused ion beam scanning
 electron microscope to extend the capability of the Laboratory for BioMolecular Structure
 (LBMS)
• Assisted in the design of the Quantitative Cellular Tomography beamline as one of the first
 two National Synchrotron Light Source II Experimental Tools III (known as NEXT-III)
 beamlines.
• Organized an NSLS-II users’ meeting workshop on Bioimaging
• Received two DOE BER Biopreparedness Research Virtual Environment (known as BRaVE)
 grants to study host-pathogen interactions.

During the current reporting cycle, we continued the development of both computation and
experiments for live cell imaging, X-ray imaging, and cryo-electron electron tomography (cryo-
ET). On the experimental side, we published one paper titled “Correlative single-cell hard X-ray
computed tomography and X-ray fluorescence imaging” in the journal Communications Biology
(2024). This work was highlighted as one of Brookhaven's Top 10 Discoveries of 2024 with a
media report on Pioneering the cellular frontier. The paper is also highlighted on the DOE/BER
website “Multimodal X-Ray Imaging Technique for Single Cells.”

On the computational side, the team further developed and optimized the deep learning model,
SwinCell, for cellular segmentation in 3D with state-of-the-art performance. The manuscript
describing the method entitled “SwinCell: a transformer-based framework for dense 3D cellular
segmentation” was recently accepted for publication in the journal Communications Biology. We
also extended the SwinCell model for 3D particle picking for cryo-ET data analysis. The SwinCell
model may be used as a foundation model for multiscale and multimodal imaging data integration
and modeling.

In addition to the technical developments, the preliminary data from the project contributed to the
preparation and submission of a BER FY25 bioimaging proposal “Multiscale and multimodal in
situ 3D bioimaging for resilient bioenergy and environment.” The project also contributed to the
development of the cryo-ET workflow and the successful renewal of the LBMS operation proposal
from 2024 to 2028, supported by BER.

 21-029
 Bridging the Gap between Scientific Simulations and Experiments with Cycle-
 consistent Generative Models
 Yihui Ren and Meifeng Lin

PURPOSE:
Many large-scale physics experiments, such as ATLAS at the Large Hadron Collider, the Deep
Underground Neutrino Experiment (DUNE), and sPHENIX at the Relativistic Heavy Ion
Collider, rely on accurate simulations to inform data analysis and derive results. Any systematic
biases in the simulations can propagate through the analysis steps and result in systematic
uncertainties. These biases may be detected and corrected using heuristics in the conventional
analysis workflow. However, they may become intractable when machine learning (ML) and
artificial intelligence (AI) methodologies are applied, which tend to train on the simulations and
infer on real data. Our goal is to develop a physics- informed ML framework that can bridge the
gap between simulations and experiments. Potentially, this can be realized by applying
generative adversarial networks (GAN) in an innovative way. We seek to construct a GANs-
based cycle-consistent cross-domain (known as GC3D) simulation augmentation framework to
improve scientific simulations so they will better represent reality. We also will investigate ways
to encode relevant physics conservation laws and symmetries into the framework while applying
effective high-performance computing (HPC) techniques for scalability.

This novel application of cross-domain GANs represents a paradigm shift from using GANs as
surrogate models to reduce and remove systematic biases with real data knowledge. Unlike
traditional uses of GANs that generate data from a random noise vector, cycle-consistency GAN
(cGAN) models use two GANs to form a closed cycle. Each GAN learns a mapping between
two domains in the opposite direction and discriminates the generated examples from the other
GAN. There are two main advantages: data efficiency and no annotation. In earlier work, the
cGAN only gets trained on roughly 1,000 of Monet’s paintings to be able to transfer an unseen
photo. The scenes of a photo and a painting do not need to be matched, which is the crux for
mapping simulations to experimental data as every experiment outcome is unique. However,
retaining the physics meaning during the transformation between simulation and experiment
data is the key challenge of this work.

Our multidisciplinary project team consists of experts in ML (Yihui Ren), HPC (Meifeng Lin),
experimental nuclear physics (Jin Huang), and high-energy physics (Brett Viren and Haiwang
Yu). We will focus on two major physics experiments, DUNE and sPHENIX. Brett Viren is the
author of Wire-Cell, a liquid argon time projection chamber (LArTPC) simulation package,
which will be used to generate several fidelity tiers of simulation data (https://lar.bnl.gov/wire-
cell/). Jin Huang developed the sPHENIX test beam, data acquisition pipeline, and heavy-ion
TPC simulator. The project also supports two early career researchers, Yi Huang and Dmitrii
Torbunov.

Since the publication of our breakthrough UNet Vision Transformer cycle-consistent GAN
(known as UVCGAN) work at the 2024 Winter Conference on Applications of Computer

 5

Vision, we have successfully applied this approach to LArTPC detector simulation data,
demonstrating its effectiveness in critical high energy physics experiments, such as DUNE. Our
method leverages modern Unpaired Image-to-Image (known as UI2I) translation techniques,
designed to perform image translations across different domains in a fully unsupervised manner.
This approach addresses the domain shift problem commonly encountered in LArTPC detector
research, where the goal is to translate between two differently distributed LArTPC datasets
consistently. By mapping real-world data to the simulated data domain, our method helps reduce
performance degradation in downstream algorithms caused by domain shifts. During this
period, we revised a manuscript, which was subsequently published in Machine Learning:
Science and Technology in March 2024 [1].

Beyond the DUNE application, we also applied our method to background subtraction in
nuclear physics experiments, specifically the SPHENIX experiment. Here, domain A consists
of particle jets with background separation (simulation data), while domain B combines jets
and background, mimicking experimental data. Our method proved effective in extracting jets
from the combined measurements, yielding promising preliminary results. We presented this
work at the 2025 Relativistic Heavy Ion Collider/Alternating Gradient Synchrotron Annual
Users’ Meeting [2].

Additionally, we have explored a more advanced generative model, known as probabilistic
diffusion, which is widely used in image generation. We have evaluated its potential as an AI
surrogate model for full-event simulation. Compared to traditional methods, the approach is
faster and more efficient. Furthermore, when compared to other generative models, e.g.,
GANs, the diffusion model demonstrated more stable behavior and fewer errors in regions
where training data were sparse. We published these findings in Physics Review C in
September 2024 [3].

                                        LDRD 21-045S
 The Study of the Nucleon Structure
 Haiyan Gao

PURPOSE:
This project consists of two studies concerning the structure of the nucleon, and they include a
precision measurement of the proton charge radius and the tomography of nucleon structure in
three-dimensional (3D) momentum space, allowing for access to Quantum Chromodynamics
(QCD) dynamics and partonic orbital angular momentum contributions to the proton spin. The
question of the proton spin was identified as one of the three science questions of the Electron- Ion
Collider (EIC) by the 2018 National Academy of Sciences report assessing the science of the EIC.
The proton charge radius was also highlighted in both the 2015 and 2023 Nuclear Science
Advisory Committee Long Range Plans for Nuclear Science. The proton radius project involves
computation of two-loop radiative corrections (RC) relevant for the new Upgraded High Precision
Measurement of the Proton Charge Radius (called PRad-II) experiment at Jefferson Lab. The
objective is to derive theoretical formulas that can be incorporated into the data analysis pipeline
to obtain the proton radius at the next precision frontier. Successful completion of this project
would help to resolve tensions between different methods of determination of the proton radius,
and to address the question of whether there is new physics that can explain the proton charge
radius between muonic and electronic systems, if such a difference exists robustly. For the 3D
tomography, the purpose is to study RC effects for the physical process used at both Jefferson Lab
and the future EIC using state-of-the-art approaches.

The approach to the proton radius calculation involves several methods. There is a new standard
for the computation of multiloop diagrams based on differential equations. However, this method
has not been extended to processes with real photon emission, which are crucial for the PRad-II
experiment. We seek to extend this approach to processes with real photon emission. As a
complement, we continue the traditional line of investigation that used semi-analytic intermediate
representation to obtain the values of radiative corrections numerically.

The 3D imaging of the nucleon structure in momentum space can be accessed via the Semi-
Inclusive Deep Inelastic Scattering (SIDIS) process. However, to correctly extract such structural
information, one needs to account for higher-order quantum electrodynamic (QED) effects, such
as real photon emissions and virtual loops. Photon emission would change the energy of the lepton
or hadron arms and affect the kinematics of such processes. Therefore, one needs to estimate and
correct for such loss due to QED higher order effects, also known as the RC, to correctly extract
the structure function. Two methods, a traditional approach that computed the lowest order
analytical QED RC, and the factorized method, which simultaneously treats QED and QCD effects
on the same footing, are employed in our studies to investigate theoretical uncertainties for such
corrections.

For the study related to the proton charge radius, in work completed in previous years, we
identified the expressions that need to be calculated and identified the terms that contain the
infrared (IR) divergences. We wrote numerical code in Python and Mathematica that handles the
relevant integrals with photon mass regularization. This led us to the most significant challenge
that needs to be overcome for numeric computation of the radiative corrections – finding an
 7

expression that is free from IR singularities. We obtained partial results in this direction for two
out of three process types, namely for the double Bethe Heitler process and for the process with
single real emission together with a single virtual loop.

For the SIDIS RC work, we implemented both RC approaches. In this fiscal year, we obtained
results and completed the comparisons between these two approaches using three typical Jefferson
Lab kinematics and three typical EIC kinematics. We found the RC effects vary from 3% to 6%,
depending on different kinematics. The results were submitted to Physical Review C. We are still
working on addressing the comments, which require substantial studies.
In the course of our RC-related work, we also carried out dedicated research for calculations of
Feynman perturbative loop integrals based on developing a new method in the context of
mathematical physics. Notably, we analyzed the singularities of the loop integrals by making use
of the so-called Mayer–Vietoris spectral sequence, where we also gave a complete classification
of the singularity development mechanisms. The novelty in our calculations can be considered an
approach, which is used to carry out an asymptotic analysis that employs the Feynman momentum
representation, where the Feynman parameter representation is usually employed in the literature.
We performed computations of some example diagrams in the case of arbitrary complex masses
and QED. Further development of this entire framework may potentially lead to exact calculations
of one-loop and two-loop Feynman diagrams, as well as other next-to-leading and higher-order
diagrams, in studies of radiative corrections for upcoming lepton–hadron scattering experiments,
including the PRad-II experiment.

In the final year of this project, we:
• Developed code in MacAulay2 and other specialized software to compute symbolically the
 differential equations for the processes with real emission. While developing this code, we
 encountered the following issues: the set of generating equations is quite large in typical
 amplitudes (>20 entries), which necessitates study of the ways to automate the generation of
 these equations; and the differential equations that we obtain must be understood as a
 generalization of the classical Ore-Sato equations. This means that the coefficients of these
 equations should be expressed based on the appropriate symmetry of the coefficients of these
 equations. This led us to a systematic study of the use of GIT in the MacAulay2 package, which
 is rudimentary presently. We hope to improve the package in the future.
• Found an IR finite expression for double virtual processes. We achieved this in an ad hoc
 manner for the on shell two-loop diagram. We are working on a preprint that summarizes this
 study. However, full understanding of IR cancellation should be approached based on the
 theory of a generalized Ore-Sato series (or Gamma series), and this is work in progress, as it
 touches upon deep properties of regular systems, derived categories, GIT, quantum groups,
 and other central topics in the theory of special functions.
• Put together these partial results for final expression that can be used by experimentalists in
 their data analysis. There is a gap between what we achieved and what can be immediately
 used by the experimentalists for their data analysis, which aims at extracting the proton radius
 from electron-proton scattering data. More work is necessary to bridge this gap.
• Addressed all referees’ comments and published the paper on the SIDIS RC studies in the
 European Physical Journal A.

 22-034
Coherent X-ray Detection of Dynamics Associated with Topological Phases in Quantum Matter
 Xiaoqian M. Chen, Mark P. M. Dean, Andrei Fluerasu, and Lutz Wiegart

PURPOSE:
Realizing topological quantum states in real materials is challenging because the underlying
interactions that compete with topological orders often stabilize a system to form simpler non-
topological, quasi-classical phases, such as antiferromagnetism. This project utilizes synchrotron
coherent X-rays to study quantum dynamics in the proximate Kitaev topological quantum spin
liquid Na2IrO3 and compares it to thermodynamics in the quasi-classical magnet Sr2IrO4 with the
aim of developing strategies to stabilize topological phases. In the process, we will develop a low-
temperature, low-noise sample environment for coherent resonant hard X-ray detection of
quantum-originated dynamics at the Coherent Hard X-ray (CHX) beamline at National
Synchrotron Light Source II (NSLS-II). The technical capabilities we develop align with Lab and
national interest in Quantum Information Science and Technology. Implementation of new
methods to measure quantum dynamics is identified as one of the priority research opportunities
in the Office of Basic Energy Sciences Roundtable on Opportunities for Basic Research for Next-
Generation Quantum Systems.

Quantum spin liquids (QSL) are a topological state of matter with strongly interacting spins, but
instead of forming long-range magnetic orders, the spins form liquid-like long-range entangled
degenerate ground states. Consequently, spin dynamics at zero temperature governs their physics.
The Kitaev QSL is a type of QSL predicted to form in honeycomb lattice materials, such as
Na2IrO3. Recently, signatures proximate to Kitaev QSL behavior were detected in the magnetic
excitation spectrum of Na2IrO3. This phase is, however, overrun by an antiferromagnetic (AFM)
phase at low temperature (~ 14K) due to other competing exchange interactions unfavorable to
QSLs. While the exact relations are yet to be uncovered, it is expected that a small change in the
competing exchange interactions can dramatically modify the magnetic spin dynamics. Studying
dynamics in the proximate QSL phase, therefore, will not only uncover the physics of QSLs but
also can suggest how to suppress the undesired interactions to achieve a true QSL.

This project leverages a newly available high-flux coherent hard X-ray instrument to demonstrate
that we can distinguish between topological and classical orders with spatial and temporal
sensitivity. Coherent X-ray scattering from lattice, electronic, and magnetic textures in materials
results in complex interference (speckle) patterns. Such patterns encode the complexity of phase
textures and, through their time variation, provide direct insight into their motion and stability. The
technique of tracking the time variation of the speckle pattern is called X-ray photon correlation
spectroscopy (XPCS). We use XPCS to study dynamics associated with the crossover between the
proximate Kitaev QSL phase and AFM phase with the aim of developing strategies to stabilize
topological phases. We will compare two iridates Sr2IrO4 and Na2IrO3, both of which are spin-orbit
coupled Mott insulators with AFM ground states. However, while Na2IrO3 reaches its AFM ground
state via a proximate Kitaev QSL phase, Sr2IrO4 directly orders as a quasi-two-dimensional
Heisenberg magnet with a quasi-classical AFM ground state. The coherent X-ray illumination on
spin ordering in iridates creates speckle patterns under corresponding magnetic Bragg peaks and
diffuse scattering. These speckle patterns are unique to the exact real space magnetic spin textures,
and their critical dynamics close to the phase transitions can reflect the nature of the spin
 9

interactions. We will perform XPCS measurements
at the iridium (Ir) L resonance edge to enhance the magnetic signal. In addition, a displacement
measuring interferometer will be implemented to monitor sample stability. Interferometric data
will be taken simultaneously with the XPCS data to rule out extrinsic dynamical signals from the
sample environment.

During years 1 and 2, we purchased equipment to support XPCS measurements at the CHX
beamline at NSLS-II. We have further performed XPCS experiments at the CHX beamline on the
proposed materials Sr2IrO4 and Na2IrO3 at the Ir L3 edge.

 22-050
Trapping Noble Gases in Silicate Nanocages for Medical Isotopes, Nuclear Energy, and Nuclear
 Nonproliferation
  Vanessa A. Sanders, J. Anibal Boscoboinik, Alejandro Sonzogni, and Elizabeth McCutchan

PURPOSE:
Separations of chemicals, including gases, are energy intensive processes affecting numerous
applications from catalysis to nuclear energy. Immobilizing single atoms of noble gases on a
surface is fundamental as it allows their detailed study in confinement using surface science
characterization methods. Aside from fundamentals, trapping noble gases at 300 K and higher
temperatures can have important implications in various areas related to the use and/or control of
radiological inert gases. This work focuses on the development of single-layer two-dimensional
crystalline nanoporous silicates that can selectively trap noble gases at room temperature, and aid

 Chongai Kuang, Nathan Urban, and Shawn Serbin

PURPOSE:
The technical objective of the project is to develop a novel, adaptable, and scalable computational
Model-Data Experimentation (ModEx) Framework to advance earth system model predictability,
accelerate the model-integration of observations, and carry out optimal observing system design.
This Framework will intelligently guide the strategic design and deployment of earth observing
systems for surface-atmosphere observations, thereby: advancing the objectives of Brookhaven
National Laboratory’s (BNL) Environmental Science and Technologies Department (EE) to
improve earth system model predictability by constraining model parametric uncertainty with
observations; aligning with BNL’s Discovery Science by Human-AI-Facility Integration priority
area; responding to anticipated funding calls from the Office of Biological and Environmental
Research Artificial Intelligence for Earth System Prediction initiative; and advancing BNL cross-
directorate research synergy across EE and the Computing and Data Sciences Directorate.

Optimizing the deployment of earth observing systems is a critical, but unrealized priority. Many
observing systems are expensive, requiring years of careful planning before a system design and
location are identified. While it is critical to maximize the scientific value of the data, it is
exceedingly rare for models to guide observing system design. Resources are often misallocated,
and critical insights are not identified during the lifetime of the deployment - this leads to missed
opportunities to improve and/or modify the observing system while data is being collected. To
address these challenges, our ModEx Framework uses an iterative approach to improve model
predictability by linking the following five components:
• Model Simulation: The Department of Energy Exascale Earth System Model (E3SM) land
 model (ELM) was chosen as our starting point. ELM simulations were performed to propagate
 the parametric uncertainty to model output uncertainty. ELM simulates essential variables
 associated with carbon fluxes and the energy budget, which play pivotal roles in studying
 biosphere-atmosphere exchange.
• Statistical Emulation: Due to the computational cost of Earth System Models, conducting tens
 of thousands of simulations required for Uncertainty Quantification is often unaffordable. To
 address this, a statistical emulator was developed as a computationally efficient surrogate for
 the ELM, allowing for significant savings in computational resources.
• Global Sensitivity Analysis (GSA): We performed GSA before model calibration to identify a
 subset of physical parameters that exert the most substantial influence on model outputs. By
 reducing the number of parameters, this step makes Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)
 sampling more efficient and simplifies the interpretation of parameter uncertainty reduction.
• Model Calibration: Given the observational dataset introduced, Bayesian inference was
 employed using an MCMC sampling algorithm, which explores parameter space via a random
 walk, converging to high-probability parameter settings that minimize discrepancies between
 model simulations and observational data. The fast emulator of ELM was embedded inside the
 MCMC algorithm to produce posterior distributions of model parameters conditional on land-
 atmosphere observational data.

 13

•  Model Prediction: We sampled the parameters from the posterior distribution after calibration
 and applied them on the Gaussian process (GP) emulators, which is the fast surrogate of ELM
 to get predictions at the same site/variable as well as new sites/variables.
Our team includes postdoctoral research associates Zhongjing Jiang and Tamanna Subba and a
graduate student, Jessica Gasparik, who assist in the development and implementation of this
ModEx Framework.

In years 1 and 2 of the project, we successfully tuned ELM on the EE computing cluster on which
all large ensemble simulations were performed, finished model-output processing, completed
training of the ELM GP emulators, and performed GSA and model calibration. In year 3, we used
the calibrated model emulator to perform an optimal experimental design. We computed the “value
of information” by estimating the amount of parameter uncertainty reduction that could be
obtained from new observations at a hypothetical new site. The results reveal that:
• Posterior parameter distributions vary significantly across different sites and observables
 within the same plant functional type
• Parameters calibrated at one site can enhance predictive accuracy at other sites, although site
 heterogeneity may sometimes outweigh parametric uncertainty
• Calibration for one variable can also improve predictability for other variables, maximizing
 predictive capabilities with limited observation.
We have successfully demonstrated the ModEx Framework, providing a powerful approach for
reducing parametric uncertainty in earth system models.

Important forward-looking considerations from this project include:
• Site heterogeneity: We acknowledge the potential influence of environmental factors at
 different site locations. Variations in weather and soil conditions across sites may significantly
 impact the findings. If site heterogeneity proves to be more influential than initially anticipated,
 it will be imperative to address parametric uncertainty and delve deeper into diagnosing
 uncertainties associated with model driver data, such as meteorological conditions and
 weather.
• Model structure uncertainty: This project focuses only on parametric uncertainty, and neglects
 addressing model structural uncertainty. Because model structural uncertainty may involve
 numerous simplifications and different processes, designing controlled experiments is more
 complex than dealing with parametric uncertainty.
• Spatial scale: While this project has been conducted at a site-specific level, future endeavors
 should aim to extend this research to regional and global scales. This expansion is important
 for capturing spatial variability, assessing broader impacts, and identifying patterns and trends.
• Earth system domain: While this project utilized the ELM as the model testbed, the Framework
 developed in this study can be adapted to other earth system model components such as the
 E3SM atmospheric component. Due to the greater system complexity of atmospheric models,
 implementing the ModEx Framework will require substantially more computational resources
 (and more data).

Accurately simulating the atmospheric aerosol-cloud-precipitation system is important to weather
forecasting. However, many microphysical processes, such as aerosol-cloud-turbulence
interactions which are crucial to weather forecasting, are poorly understood and represented in
atmospheric models, such as in the well-known large eddy simulations, hindering further progress
in numerical weather and energy forecasting. The need to better simulate those microphysical
processes and understand their effect on weather at various scales calls for a cross-cutting particle-
resolved direct numerical simulation (PR-DNS) model. Despite its importance, considerable
challenges exist due to its high computational cost, especially in a large domain size. The
challenges are mainly driven by solving multiphysical partial differential equations (PDE) and the
large number of particles to be tracked. In this project, we aim to address the computational
bottlenecks of the PR-DNS models, with the goal of using PR-DNS as part of the digital twin of
the planned cloud chamber facility to study the fundamental processes involving clouds, droplets,
and precipitation.

The original Brookhaven National Laboratory PR-DNS code did not run on the graphics
processing unit (GPU) accelerators and did not scale well on central processing units (CPU) either.
In this project, we will improve the performance and scalability of the PDE solvers used in PR-
DNS through two research thrusts: Thrust 1, the effective use of large-scale heterogeneous high
performance computing (HPC) systems, where issues related to performance, scalability, and
portability of the existing algorithms and software will be addressed; and Thrust 2, the
investigation and development of novel Machine Learning (ML)-based PDE solvers to further
speed up PR-DNS simulations.

In FY22, for the HPC acceleration, we focused on understanding the performance bottlenecks of
PR-DNS through performance profiling and did the initial porting to NVIDIA GPU accelerators
using the CUDA application programming interface. On the ML side, we used the numerical PR-
DNS solver to collect a large amount of training data for ML-based solvers. We also did initial
evaluation of various ML-based solvers and chose Fourier Neural Operators (FNO) as our main
target.

high repetition rate. Laser ion sources can produce pulsed beams of fully ionized lithium ions at
very high peak currents that are two orders of magnitude higher than those of typical heavy ion
sources. On the other hand, the average beam power is limited because the available repetition rate
of beam production is currently on the order of 1 Hz. A liquid lithium target system will be
established to overcome the limitation of repetition rate to realize a high- power lithium
accelerator. The high-power lithium accelerator will make it possible to develop a compact clean
neutron source. The new neutron source offers an advantage of a forward-directed neutron beam
because of high center of mass velocity with lithium bombardment to hydrogen. The directionality
enhances neutron flux and reduction of background neutrons. Although this idea has been proposed
before, its application was limited due to the low intensity of conventional lithium ion sources.
Our proposed neutron source, utilizing the high-power lithium accelerator, will be the first machine
suitable for imaging, such as diagnostics of the structure of metal and water in concrete used for
civil construction, and detecting nuclear and explosive materials for border protection. This project
is aligned with the Laboratory’s mission as it targets development of a state-of-the-art heavy ion
accelerator for innovative applications.

The repetition rate limitation stems from the use of solid laser targets because the laser creates
craters. To get a fresh surface, either the laser beam or the target needs to be moved. In addition,
an enormously large surface area is needed for long-term operation. These limit the repetition rate
and the total yield of lithium ions and the application of laser ion sources. To dramatically increase
the repetition rate, we propose the use of a liquid lithium target, which has never been done. The
surface deformation by the laser is recovered by itself and the same spot can be irradiated. Thus,
it is expected the repetition rate could increase by orders of magnitudes.

A liquid lithium target will be created by melting lithium in a container or crucible because the
simple configuration is expected to be preferable for operation. Meanwhile, surface waves will be
induced by laser irradiation due to the rapid expansion of the produced plasma. The surface vertical
oscillation can affect the spot size of the focused laser beam and plasma production. We will try
to mitigate the vibrations by placing shielding structures in or on the liquid or by using
electromagnetic forces. On the other hand, the number of fully ionized lithium ions produced, the
shot-to-shot stability, the target consumption, and the achievable repetition rate depend on the laser
irradiation conditions. The condition includes laser energy, laser spot size, and the distance around
the laser waist (Rayleigh length.) We will optimize the condition by measuring the charge-state
distribution in the produced plasma with an ion analyzer that will be constructed. Finally, we plan
to position a heated structure over the crucible to protect the chamber from lithium vapor
accumulation and the resulting degradation of the vacuum chamber. This will also help reduce the
radiative cooling from the lithium.

This was the second year of the project. First, the ion analyzer was constructed based on the design
developed during the first year. It was tested using copper (Cu) ions generated by laser

promising potential for high repetition rate operation.

 Proton Structure, From Partons to Strong Fields
 Yacine Mehtar-Tani

PURPOSE:
The Electron-Ion Collider (EIC) aims to address two fundamental goals in nuclear physics:
mapping the three-dimensional (3D) structure of the nucleon and probing gluon saturation at small
values of Bjorken-x, where classical gluon fields dominate and the traditional partonic description
breaks down. Bridging these two regimes remains a significant theoretical challenge. We carried
out the first theoretical study of the 3D gluon distribution and its quantum structure across the full
x-range, within the frameworks of deep inelastic scattering (DIS), deeply virtual Compton
scattering (DVCS), and jet production in both electron-ion and heavy-ion collisions. By computing
one-loop quantum corrections, our work addresses long-standing numerical instabilities in the
evolution of dipole operators at small x, laying the foundation for a unified, all-x framework for
nucleon and nuclear structure. The results of this high-risk, high-reward project have the potential
to seed a new subfield at Brookhaven National Laboratory and advance key scientific objectives
of the EIC.

Our understanding of proton structure at high energy relies on two complementary frameworks:
the parton model at short distances and the wee gluon picture of dense classical fields at small x,
which underpins gluon saturation. However, the transition between these regimes remains unclear,
complicating the interpretation of EIC data. A key symptom of this gap is the numerical instability
in small-x evolution equations beyond leading order, a long-standing issue only partially addressed
by ad hoc solutions. To tackle this, we employ background field methods to construct new gauge-
invariant gluon operators that can be accessed in DIS and DVCS and compute their next-to-leading
order corrections — establishing a foundation for an all-orders, precision description of hadronic
structure. We also investigate the interplay between collinear QCD (Quantum Chromodynamics)
evolution and quantum small-x dynamics, particularly in single-jet and dijet production in hot and
cold nuclear matter. Our team brings together leading experts in the field: Raju Venugopalan,
Renaud Boussarie, Alfred Mueller, and Yoshitaka Hatta.

In the first year of the project, Y. Mehtar-Tani and R. Boussarie developed a new high-energy
framework for exclusive processes that retains Feynman-x dependence in both the hard scattering
and hadronic matrix elements. This approach led to a compact, factorized master formula and
successfully reproduced known one-loop results. Complementary work by collaborators
uncovered anomaly poles in Compton scattering and advanced next-to-leading order (NLO)
calculations within the Color Glass Condensate (CGC) framework, providing deeper insight into
gluon saturation and Sudakov effects at small x. In the past fiscal year, Y. Mehtar-Tani and R.
Boussarie published their results in JHEP 10 (2024) 056, presenting the factorized master formula
as a tool to probe the Bjorken limit at leading twist and gluon saturation, simultaneously.

In a major advance toward the EIC physics program, P. Caucal, F. Salazar, B. Schenke, T. Stebel,
and R. Venugopalan performed the first full NLO calculation of the cross-section for back-to-back
dijets in electron-ion collisions (Phys. Rev. Lett. 132, 081902, 2024). This work quantifies the role
of gluon saturation relative to other effects across a range of parameters — including nuclear size,
energy, and transverse momentum imbalance — providing a clear and robust signature for the
discovery of gluon saturation at the EIC.

In parallel, Y. Hatta and collaborators uncovered novel quantum features of partons at small x. They
demonstrated that the helicity and orbital angular momentum of quarks and gluons are maximally
entangled in the CGC framework (Phys. Lett. B 859, 139134, 2024). They further analyzed gluon

related work, Y. Hatta and F. Yuan investigated angular correlations in semi-inclusive diffractive

During the past fiscal year, Y. Mehtar-Tani and R. Boussarie also computed the one-loop correction

Lipatov-Altarelli-Parisi (known as DGLAP) evolution equation at high momentum transfer. This
result provides strong confirmation that the new formalism can smoothly connect to established
frameworks within high-energy QCD. The main technical challenge ahead is to obtain a closed-form
expression of the newly derived equation suitable for numerical simulations and EIC
phenomenology. Additionally, they obtained a rigorous derivation of the collinear- improved BK
equation, which does not suffer from the numerical instabilities present in previous formulations.
This work will be released soon in the form of two publications.

the context of jet production in hot QCD matter, focusing on the interplay between collinear
evolution and small-x dynamics. In collaboration with V. Vaidya, B. Singh, and F. Ringer, he derived
a new factorization theorem within the framework of effective field theory, where interactions with
the QCD medium are encoded in Wilson-line operators akin to dipole operators in the gluon
saturation regime. This work has been presented in two articles: arXiv:2409.05957 [hep- ph] and
arXiv:2504.00101 [hep-ph], submitted to Physics Letters B and the Journal of High Energy Physics,
respectively. In a related effort, Y. Mehtar-Tani studied the QCD evolution of jets as they propagate
through the background field of hot QCD matter and derived novel nonlinear evolution equations
that describe the energy loss of collinear partons. This work was published in JHEP 04 (2025) 163.
These theoretical developments may shed new light on the interplay between small-x dynamics and
collinear QCD physics at the EIC, paving the way for unified theoretical tools to describe high-
energy QCD dynamics in dense environments.

 R&D for PIONEER: a Next-generation Rare Pion Decay Experiment
 Vladimir Tishchenko

PURPOSE:
The goal of this project is to design, fabricate, and characterize five-dimensional (5D) (3D position
+ time + energy) thin (120 to 200 μm) self-supporting sensor prototypes for the novel Active pion
stopping Target (ATAR) with tracking capabilities, based on Low Gain Avalanche Diode (LGAD)
technology. These sensors are key to enabling the next-generation rare pion decay experiment,
called PIONEER. PIONEER aims to measure the charged pion branching ratio to positrons vs.
muons, with a precision fifteen times greater than the current experimental result. The target is a
0.01% level of precision – matching he Standard Model (SM) prediction at one part in 10,000.
This would constitute the most precise test of lepton flavor universality (LFU) to date. Given
existing tensions between SM predictions and experimental results that hint at LFU violation,
PIONEER has the potential to probe new physics through sensitivity to quantum effects of
hypothetical particles with masses up to the peta electron volt scale. Besides testing LFU,
PIONEER will explore physics beyond the SM by searching for exotic pion decay channels and
testing the Cabibbo–Kobayashi–Maskawa (known as CKM) matrix unitarity, directly addressing
the so-called Cabibbo Angle Anomaly.

An active pion stopping target is essential to distinguish rare “signal” decays from the far more
common decay chain, and to accurately determine the detector acceptance ratio for these decay
modes at the 0.01% level. A target composed of stacked, thin (∼120 μm) LGAD-based silicon strip
sensors is expected to deliver the required functionality. While LGAD sensors have demonstrated
excellent timing and spatial resolution in high-energy physics applications, their energy
measurement performance remains less explored. This project seeks to fill that gap. Standard
silicon sensors (without internal gain) provide excellent energy resolution but require ultra-low-
noise preamplifiers, which would need to be developed. This project will evaluate the energy-
resolution potential of LGADs and lay the foundation for the ATAR subsystem critical to the
success of PIONEER.

LGAD technology has matured into a well-established solution offering internal charge
amplification and exceptional timing and spatial resolution. Leveraging Brookhaven National
Laboratory’s (BNL) in-house expertise in LGAD technology, this project will explore the energy
measurement capabilities of LGAD sensors – a critical requirement for the PIONEER experiment.
The scope of this work encompasses four key components:
• Sensor design, supported by simulations to optimize sensor geometry and performance
• Sensor fabrication, to produce thin, self-supporting LGAD-based prototypes
• Sensor characterization, through a comprehensive set of tests
• Physics simulations, aimed at quantifying the performance requirements for sensor parameters.
This effort is carried out in collaboration with G. Giacomini in the Instrumentation Department, a
specialist in LGAD sensor technology, and X. Qian and C. Zhao in the Physics Department, who
contribute expertise in detector simulations and physics analysis.

 38

Using a common mask set, three 200 μm-thick 4 inch-diameter wafers and five 120 μm-thick 3
inch-diameter wafers were processed. The 4-inch wafers can be handled using the existing
equipment at BNL, only if their thickness is larger than 200 μm. To process thinner wafers using
the same equipment, we opted for smaller 3 inch diameter wafers to fabricate 120 μm-thick sensors.
Unfortunately, all of the 120 μm-thick wafers broke at various fabrication steps, with no obvious
cause or consistent pattern. This was the first attempt to process wafers of this thickness using the
equipment at BNL, and the outcome confirmed our concern about their fragility. These results
underscore the need for further R&D to develop appropriate handling methods for ultra- thin
wafers. In contrast, all 200 μm-thick wafers were successfully processed, resulting in two types of
devices: double-sided strip detectors and capacitively coupled (AC-LGADs). The fabricated
devices include:
• Rectangular devices (1 cm or 2 cm-long), with strip pitch of 200 μm, produced in both double-
 sided strip and AC-LGAD formats. Each device contains five strips to limit the total area and
 maximize fabrication yield.
• Square devices (0.5 cm), with varying strip pitches, in both double-sided strip and AC-LGAD
 configurations, designed to study pitch-dependent performance.
• Single channel devices with both direct current (DC) and alternating current (AC) coupling.
The back side of the wafer features p+ DC-coupled strips, oriented either parallel to the n-side
strips in the rectangular devices or orthogonal in the square devices. The front side (n-side),
required a more complex processing sequence, involving six lithography steps and three implants.
On the p-side, a simpler process was used, involving four lithography steps and one implant.
Characterization of the fabricated devices was carried out in multiple steps:
• On-wafer testing prior to dicing, to assess production quality by measuring the current-voltage
 characteristics using standard laboratory equipment
• Post-dicing measurement of individual devices, performed using the same setup as in the on-
 wafer testing to confirm consistency and detect any handling-induced defects
• Beta source testing, after mounting devices on dedicated test boards, to study charge collection
 efficiency and timing response
• Laser-based measurements, to investigate spatial and temporal resolution
• Beam tests at a proton accelerator, to evaluate detector response to highly ionizing particles
 and simulate realistic experimental conditions.
The lab measurements confirmed the good quality of the produced devices and validated the
success of the fabrication process. However, the accelerator tests revealed significant effects in the
LGAD devices, including signal saturation and a pronounced dependence of signal amplitude on
the track angle of highly ionizing particles. These effects introduce serious challenges for particle
identification and charge reconstruction for the PIONEER experiment. Further R&D will be
necessary to evaluate the feasibility of using LGAD-based ATAR systems in PIONEER and to
explore potential design or operational mitigations.

 23-021
 Utilizing AI/ML and Automation Systems to Inform and Optimize Isotope Separations
 Jasmine Hatcher-Lamarre, Carlos Soto, and Dohyun Kim

PURPOSE:
Separation processes are essential to isotope production but remain labor-intensive and largely
dependent on literature-driven, trial-and-error methods. This project began with an ambitious goal:
to apply artificial intelligence/machine learning (AI/ML) to transform how chemical separations
are understood and designed, particularly for transition metals and lanthanides. While the long-
term vision includes predictive modeling and AI-guided separation strategies, initial efforts
focused on two fronts: extracting relevant chemical data from the literature using AI/ML and
translating current scientific expertise into automated separation workflows. Though these efforts
remain distinct for now, future integration will support more efficient, scalable, and continuous
operations. Building on this progress, a pre-proposal was submitted and encouraged for further
funding; a full proposal has since been developed and is currently under review. This work lays a
strong foundation for innovation, aligns with Department of Energy (DOE) priorities, and
reinforces Brookhaven National Laboratory’s (BNL) leadership in radioisotope research and
production.

Chemical separations are fundamental to isotope production. If the desired isotope cannot be
effectively isolated from the target material and byproducts, it cannot be used. However, the
development of new separation strategies is often slowed by the manual effort required to identify
and assess methods from the scientific literature. To address this, we explored the use of Natural
Language Processing (NLP) to extract relevant chemical data, with the goal of eventually
identifying patterns related to the coordination and separation of transition metals and lanthanides.
While challenges remain in extracting consistent and structured data at scale, this work provided
valuable insight into the limitations of current literature formats and NLP tools.

In parallel, we began translating traditional bench-scale separation strategies into automated
workflows using a custom-built, in-house system. Although the AI and automation efforts are not
yet fully integrated, they represent complementary paths toward accelerating and scaling isotope
separations. Together, they lay important groundwork for future progress in data-driven,
automated radiochemical processing.

Machine Learning: An ML technique known as Named Entity Recognition (known as NER) was
employed to extract relevant information from individual scientific articles. To train the language
model, chemistry experts manually annotated journal articles with specific labels corresponding to
key features, such as target element, separation strategy, ligand type, and reaction conditions.
These annotated examples were used to teach the model how to identify and classify similar
information in unstructured text. After training, the model was tested on a separate set of articles
to evaluate its accuracy in recognizing and extracting these features. To make the tool accessible,
an interface was developed that allows users to upload a PDF document and automatically extract
relevant chemical information. The extracted data can then be structured in a standardized format,
creating a searchable and reusable knowledge base that can ultimately support automated
separation workflows. This represents an important step toward integrating literature-derived
insights into scalable, AI-driven isotope production processes.
 40

Automation: Our approach to automation is grounded in a detailed understanding of each step in
isotope production. By identifying which stages are sensitive to parameters, such as time, pressure,
and flow rate — and how these factors impact product yield and purity — we can develop targeted
strategies for automating separation processes. At BNL, we have extensive experience with
automated synthesis systems for radioisotopes. Leveraging this expertise, we are actively
exploring the automation of chemical separations. As part of this effort, we developed a compact,
partially three-dimensional-printed prototype capable of autonomously operating multiple small
chromatography columns or sequentially processing solutions through a single column. This
device features 10 servo motors that control two five-gang stopcocks and two peristaltic pumps. It
is run through a Maestro controller program via USB, enabling users to define motor positions and
pump timings without requiring advanced programming knowledge. The software automatically
converts these settings into code that is uploaded to the device, allowing for fully automated
operation. The system offers precise, customizable, and repeatable control over each step of a
chromatography run.

In a proof-of-concept study, we automated the established separation of nickel from cobalt using
a 2 mL butyl diglycol acetate (known as BDGA) column to process a mock target containing
100 mg of nickel and 1 mg of cobalt. While this separation typically takes six hours under gravity,
the automated system completed it in just 20 minutes without compromising cobalt yield or purity.
Preloaded with solutions, the device executed the separation as it would be done manually,
collecting load, wash, and elution fractions separately for purity analysis.

During this project, a returning summer student worked on adapting the chemistry to process larger
quantities (500 mg and above). Through that work, we identified the need for a second column to
effectively handle increased material loads. In response, we developed a second, more intricate
system designed specifically for scale-up. This advanced prototype replaced manual stopcocks
with solenoid valves for directing flow, enabling more precise and flexible multi- column
operations. Like the first system, it also runs on the Maestro controller, which simplifies the
conversion of analog actions, such as opening or closing valves and starting or stopping pumps
into reusable, script-based workflows. This ease of programmability allows researchers to
automate complex procedures without requiring coding expertise, making the system both
powerful and accessible.

Together, these efforts demonstrate a practical and forward-looking approach to modernizing
isotope production. By combining AI-assisted data extraction with customizable, scalable
automation systems, we are laying the foundation for a more efficient, data-driven workflow that
reduces manual labor and accelerates the development of new separation strategies. Although
challenges remain, particularly in structuring complex chemical data from the literature, our
progress highlights the potential of integrating machine learning and automation to transform how
radiochemical processes are designed and executed. This work strengthens BNL’s position as a
leader in innovative isotope production and supports DOE priorities for advancing U.S. capabilities
in radioisotope research and supply.

 23-026
 Entangling Multiplexed Atomic Quantum Sensors
 Julián Martínez-Rincón

PURPOSE:
In this project, we worked towards building a multiplexed atomic magnetometer to study the nature
of distributed atomic sensing. We targeted to use two sets of up to 49 laser beams to generate up
to 49 Electro-Magnetically Induced Transparency (EIT) spectral lines in a grid of 7x7 atomic
clouds within a single rubidium vapor cell. These frequency lines create shifts with respect to each
other when an external field is applied, so we use them to estimate a control (known) B-field (also
called a magnetic field) created by a magnetic solenoid. By creating this array of magnetometers,
we will study the advantage of having such a classical network as a comparison to using just one
sensor. As a long-term goal, we will study applications of the technique, and further improvement
by generating quantum entanglement among nodes in this network to evaluate a quantum
advantage.

The advantages of using quantum networks of sensors are still to be demonstrated. For example,
if a network/array of m sensors is used in parallel, the overall sensitivity of a measurement should
improve by up to √m. If, in addition to this, quantum entanglement is distributed among all sensors
in the network, an extra improvement of √m is expected. This latter scaling factor has not been
demonstrated for large number of sensors. We will pursue two main goals: demonstrating the
capabilities of having an array of up to 49 magnetometers with an improved sensitivity of up to
seven-fold and understanding how such an advantage could be further extended using quantum
entanglement.

During this project, we built the experimental setup and partially addressed the first of the two
goals. We used a two-laser system to create an EIT response in the atomic cloud. One strong laser
beam (control beam with Rabi frequency Ω𝑐𝑐𝑐𝑐) was used to create an EIT response for a probe beam
(Ω𝑝𝑝𝑝𝑝) in a cloud of rubidium atoms. The transmission of the probe beam presented a dispersive
response in frequency around the two-photon resonance. When an external B-field was applied,
this spectral line split into three different lines, with separation in frequency given by ±(2.8 ×
1010 Hz/T) Bz [PRA 82, 033807, 2010], where Bz is the component of the field along the laser
field propagation. Measuring these spectral shifts offers high-sensitive readout of the external B
field.

As part of the project, we built the laser system capable of delivering two phase-locked beams, 6.8
GHz detuned. The design can provide up to ~100 mW on each beam, which then can be used to
split each to up to 49 independently controlled beams for the array. We demonstrated EIT in the
atomic system, showing the expected dependence upon control and probe laser powers. We
evaluated the B-field sensitivity when operating one pair of beams (one control and one probe),
i.e., one sensor operation, with sub-nanotesla (nT) sensitivities. We also demonstrated a 3x3
multiplexed configuration, with sub-nT sensitivity in each node of the array.

 42

There were many technical improvements performed along the way to achieving these results.
These include:
• We characterized and optimized linear vs. circular polarization states for the probe and control
 fields. Linear polarization offers more spectral shifted peaks, so we chose it over circularly
 polarized light.
• We were able to reach sub-nT resolution for B-field sensing in a single readout shot due to
 high fidelity (>99%) fittings to the spectral shifts. We improved on noise reduction and
 robustness of the experiment to external environmental changes.
• We evaluated and analyzed the noise advantages of such a grid using differential
 measurements.

In summary, this project created the infrastructure and capabilities of an atomic-based quantum
sensing experiment, the only existing one at Brookhaven National Laboratory. Magnetic field
sensing was chosen since the rubidium vapor cells, used in the Quantum Information Science and
Technology lab as quantum memories, are also the core of the most sensitive B-field sensor known
today. These infrastructure and capabilities will allow us to explore applications and future
improvements.

 23-028
 Nonlinear On-Chip Waveform Processing for Detector ASICs
 Soumyajit Mandal

PURPOSE:
Modern high energy and nuclear physics experiments generate massive (and steadily increasing)
amounts of real-time data, much of which cannot be acquired and processed online and is thus
discarded using a variety of hardware triggering schemes. This situation is not optimal since the
triggering logic is based on established signal models and may discard unexpected signals (some
of which may have led to new scientific discoveries). As a result, there is a growing trend towards
trigger-less or “streaming” data acquisition (DAQ) schemes in which the hardware triggers are
replaced by online feature extraction and machine learning algorithms. Hardware- level feature
extraction from detected signals (also known as waveform processing) is an essential component
of such streaming DAQ architectures, since it allows real-time data rates to be greatly reduced
while preserving the signal’s information content. However, implementing these algorithms in
hardware is challenging due to the limited power and area budget of the front-end application
specific integrated circuits (ASIC) used for signal conditioning and digitization of the detected
signals. This project addresses this important issue by exploring computationally efficient yet high
performance alternatives to established methods for real-time on-chip waveform processing.

The project explored the architecture of novel smart front-end ASICs that incorporate energy
efficient on-chip circuitry for real-time feature extraction and machine learning. Extraction of high
level features from the input waveform is essentially a data fitting problem that can be solved using
function minimization, i.e., by numerically minimizing a cost function. Trained artificial neural
networks (ANN) are powerful algorithms for general purpose function minimization and are thus
of interest for real-time waveform processing. Thus, one goal of the project is to explore
hyperparameter optimization methods to reduce the relatively high computational complexity of
ANNs while maintaining inferencing accuracy, including quantization of weights, i.e., storing
them as low resolution (4-8 bit) fixed point integers; network pruning to reduce computing and
memory requirements; and quantization-aware training. Another goal is to compare ANNs with
alternative algorithms, such as matched filters, nonlinear least-squares, and nonlinear recursive
filters.

The project approach was focused on evaluating several feature extraction algorithms by using
classical estimation theory to derive fundamental accuracy limits and theoretical performance
bounds on noisy data; comparing the performance of the chosen algorithms against these bounds;
and implementing the best algorithms on-chip using high-level synthesis (HLS) tools. The use of
HLS provided to be beneficial for reducing ASIC design times and allowing the resulting smart
front-end chips to be easily modified and reused for different applications.

Collaborators in the Instrumentation Department include Grzegorz Deptuch, Prashansa Mukim,
Nicholas St. John, and Dominik Gorni (ASIC design), Piotr Maj (testing), and Abdul Rumaiz
(resistive random access memory (RRAM)-based ANNs). Others from Brookhaven National
Laboratory include Yihui Ren and Shubha Kharel from Computing and Data Sciences (hardware-
aware optimization of ANN hyperparameters) and Chang-Yong Nam and Nikhil Tiwale from the
Center for Functional Nanomaterials (RRAM-based energy-efficient

 44

ANNs). External collaborators include Kanad Basu from the University of Texas and Emre Salman
from Stony Brook University (models of ANN aging, high reliability ANN design).

As an initial step, the estimation error of several feature extraction algorithms on noisy data was
computed and compared with theoretical limits derived from the Cramer-Rao lower bound (CRLB)
for the problem. This work showed that the algorithms have similar performance on simple tasks,
such as amplitude and time of arrival estimation, and several approach the CRLB. Thus, the choice
of “best” algorithm is largely determined by the performance metrics of the on- chip
implementation, such as layout area and power consumption.

A custom semi-automated HLS design flow was developed for rapidly implementing the proposed
feature extraction algorithms on resource-constrained front-end ASICs. The flow begins by
converting a high-level description of the algorithm in MATLAB or Simulink to fixed- point
arithmetic, while using adaptive bit-width minimization to reduce resource usage while
maintaining numerical accuracy. Next, a synthesizable hardware description language project is
generated from the fixed-point code. A standard digital ASIC flow is finally used for logic
synthesis, place and route, and timing analysis. The novel design flow described above was used
for ASIC designs in two different Complementary metal–oxide–semiconductor (CMOS)
fabrication processes (130 nm and 65 nm) using open source and industry standard CAD tools.
The implementation results showed that ANNs provide the best tradeoff between inferencing
accuracy and hardware complexity.

The flow described above was used to implement a prototype smart front-end ASIC with on-chip
ANNs for feature extraction. The chip, which has a layout area of 5 mm x 5 mm in a 65 nm CMOS
process, contains an array of 23 detection channels. Each channel contains a programmable low
noise analog front-end; a low power high speed analog-to-digital converter (ADC); a
programmable digital signal processor (DSP) for filtering, decimation, baseline suppression, and
waveform alignment; and a multilayer perceptron (MLP) for performing regression and/or
classification tasks. The MLP employs stochastic rounding of the outputs of the multiply-
accumulate units to allow in-system training even when the ANN weights have relatively low
resolution to minimize on-chip area and power. The chip also includes several configuration,
readout, and testability options that can be externally programmed via a standard two wire serial
inter-integrated circuit (known as I2C) interface. The ASIC has been fabricated and is currently
being tested. Initial results have confirmed the proper functionality of the DSP.

The potential energy efficiency advantages of replacing clocked ANNs with asynchronous spiking
neural networks (SNN) were also studied. SNNs are particularly attractive for front-end ASICs
since they eliminate the need for high speed ADCs within the signal path. The results of this study
were used to write a funding proposal to the Office of High Energy Physics.

Overall, a smart multi-channel readout ASIC containing on-chip DSP, an ANN for real-time
feature extraction, and a test board for the ASIC were designed. The work was presented to the
community in two conference papers and a journal paper (that is being finalized).

 Radiolytically Generated Spin-Correlated Radical Pairs for Molecular QIS
 Tomoyasu Mani, Matthew Bird, and David Grills

PURPOSE:
Molecular qubits in quantum information science (QIS) offer the potential to operate at
significantly higher temperatures than current solid-state qubits, thereby overcoming the “tyranny
of low temperature” that limits many QIS platforms. In this project, we focus on a distinctive class
of molecular qubits: spin-correlated radical pairs (SCRP) formed within electron donor/acceptor
(D/A) systems. Upon photoinduced electron transfer from D to A, a pair of spatially separated
radical spins (D●⁺ and A●⁻) is generated. These radical pairs are inherently spin-entangled qubit
pairs, distinguishing them from other single-spin qubit systems and offering unique opportunities
for quantum applications. SCRPs are particularly promising due to their synthetic tunability, well-
defined molecular structures when connected by a chemical bridge, optical addressability,
magnetic sensitivity, and the ability to be prepared in an initial pure, entangled spin state. These
attributes position SCRPs as strong candidates for advancing QIS, particularly in the development
of magneto-optical quantum sensors. Their magnetic field sensitivity enables potential
applications, such as resolution-enhanced optical imaging, magnetometry, and molecular logic
operations.

However, practical implementation requires a deeper understanding of spin coherence in these
systems. While some molecular parameters, such as hyperfine interactions and g-factor anisotropy,
are known to influence coherence and magnetic sensitivity, strategies to control these parameters
through rational chemical design remain largely unexplored.

This project pursues two central scientific objectives:
• To establish pulse radiolysis as a novel experimental platform for studying quantum coherence
 in SCRPs. We aim to demonstrate time-resolved magnetic field effects and magnetically
 affected reaction yield (MARY) spectroscopy at the Laser Electron Accelerator Facility
 (LEAF) at Brookhaven National Laboratory’s (BNL) Accelerator Center for Energy Research
 (ACER).
• To elucidate how molecular structure governs spin coherence and magnetic response in
 SCRPs, ultimately defining design principles for molecular qubits.

By combining pulse radiolysis with spin-sensitive detection, this work will provide a powerful
new approach for probing coherence dynamics and exploring a broad chemical space relevant to
molecular quantum technologies. The overarching goal is to establish a new research program that
combines pulse radiolysis with QIS, emphasizing the rational design of molecular systems to
optimize performance and uncover key parameters that govern spin coherence and magnetic
sensitivity. This effort leverages BNL’s unique expertise and infrastructure to conduct time-
resolved studies of molecular radical pairs specifically engineered for quantum sensing and control
applications.

In conventional studies, SCRPs are typically generated via photoexcitation followed by electron
transfer from short-lived singlet excited states, a process limited by the nanosecond lifetimes of
these states. This short timescale often constrains the types of molecules that can be used and may

limit access to key spin dynamics. In contrast, our project leverages pulse radiolysis
combined with magnetically modulated optical detection, a powerful and distinctive method for
generating spatially separated SCRPs in a spin-selective manner, independent of excited-state
lifetimes. Using this approach, we probe SCRP spin dynamics by measuring magnetically affected
yields of recombination fluorescence from singlet states (S₁A*) and/or phosphorescence from
triplet states (T₁A*). By decoupling SCRP generation from the photophysical constraints of short-
lived excited states, pulse radiolysis dramatically expands the accessible molecular design space.
This enables us to harness the full synthetic tunability of donor–bridge–acceptor molecules to
optimize coherence properties and magnetic sensitivity, key parameters for advancing molecular
qubits and quantum sensing applications.

We have successfully installed an electromagnet on the B-line of the LEAF and implemented two
experimental geometries in which the electron beam and probe light are aligned either
perpendicular or parallel. These configurations now allow us to perform time-resolved magnetic
field effects (known as TR-MFE) and MARY spectroscopy at LEAF. Initial experiments
demonstrated clear magnetic field effects on the formation of triplet excited states (T₁A*). We also
observed magnetic field effects on recombination fluorescence; however, the magnitude of these
effects was initially smaller than expected, likely due to lower-than-anticipated yields of
recombination fluorescence under pulse radiolysis conditions. To overcome this limitation, we
enhanced the detection sensitivity by modifying and optimizing the optical components in the
LEAF detection setup. As a result, we can observe pronounced quantum beats in the recombination
fluorescence of radiolytically generated SCRPs, validating the effectiveness of our approach.
Combined with the transient absorption detection, we can probe the magnetic modulation of
SCRPs through both singlet and triplet channels, providing a solid foundation for further
investigation of spin dynamics. In parallel, computational chemistry studies highlighted significant
solvent effects on SCRP formation. Additional improvements to the experimental design are
ongoing.

We synthesized a series of donor–acceptor molecules for experimental studies and electron
paramagnetic resonance characterization at the University of Connecticut. Characterization of
chemically generated radical species is underway. Complementary computational studies are
evaluating their energetic and spin properties. Some results from these efforts have been published.

•   J. Am. Chem. Soc. 2025, 147, 9, 7187–7190 (https://doi.org/10.1021/jacs.4c16922).

LEAF, creating powerful tools for probing spin dynamics in molecular systems. These
advancements open compelling opportunities that align with the research priorities within the

 Hybrid Digital-Analog Quantum Algorithms

PURPOSE:
This project aims at combining the notions of discrete (digital) and continuous (analog) quantum
processing to design and analyze new and more efficient quantum protocols that can enhance and
expand the applications of current and emerging quantum devices. We start by focusing on the
problem of generating, controlling, and measuring highly entangled quantum states, and plan to
expand this program to carry out a systematic study of the possibilities and advantages of the
hybrid approach with the goal of designing and analyzing new quantum algorithms and testing
them on near-term programmable quantum devices. The work is a collaborative effort between
theory and experiment, and the results are expected to catalyze follow-up programs for
investigating hybrid analog/digital quantum devices.

Recent technological advances in the design and control of quantum devices have enabled the
possibility of combining analog and digital elements on the same device, thus opening the door to
potential new hybrid algorithms that take advantage of both gate-based and time-evolved elements.
It is known that with only discrete local digital elements, it is impossible to prepare highly
entangled states in finite depth. We examine novel approaches that may lead to preparing complex
entangled states in finite depth. From a theoretical perspective, we take advantage of long-range
interactions that appear naturally in neutral/cold atoms and ion trap architectures, and employ the

was designed which could mount several thin foils to be used as material samples. Each could be
remotely moved into the path of the beam, or the entire assembly removed from the beam path to
provide a beam-energy reference measurement. In this way, by scanning across beam energies,
many measurements could be made in rapid succession.

acquisition system) were purchased and characterized with a standard alpha particle source. Once
the performance of the system was found to be satisfactory, a test run was performed at the BTVG

 24-071-8
 Locating the Critical End Point on the QCD Phase Diagram
 Peter Petreczky

PURPOSE:
The goal of this project is to develop an innovative method to locate the critical point on the
Quantum Chromodynamics (QCD) phase diagram using a lattice QCD approach that combines
the technique of Taylor expansion of the pressure in chemical potential and simulations at the
imaginary chemical potential. This research is important for the Relativistic Heavy Ion Collider
beam energy program that aims at exploration of the QCD phase diagram at non-zero baryon
density.

Lattice QCD is the only first principle theoretical approach to study the QCD phase diagram. It
was successful in calculating the equation of state and the crossover nature of the transition at zero
net baryon density. These calculations by large were led by the Brookhaven National Laboratory
Nuclear Theory Group. Unfortunately, direct lattice QCD calculations are not possible at non-zero
net baryon density due to the infamous sign problem. To circumvent this problem, it was proposed
to use a Taylor expansion of the QCD pressure in the baryon chemical potential.

We investigated a new idea to study the QCD phase diagram at non-zero baryon chemical potential
on the lattice that relies on calculating the Taylor expansion coefficients of the QCD pressure at
non-zero values at non-zero imaginary chemical potential, instead of zero chemical potential as is
usually done. There is no sign problem in lattice QCD simulations at non-zero imaginary chemical
potential. Combining the information from the Taylor expansion and imaginary chemical potential,
one can map out much better the detailed dependence of thermodynamic quantities on the baryon
chemical potential and study the corresponding singularities that would indicate the existence of
the critical end point on the QCD phase diagram.

We performed lattice QCD calculations at temperature T=108 MeV where previous calculations
indicated the existence of the critical end point. The calculations were performed for two different
volumes corresponding to 203x10 and 323x10 lattices using the Summit supercomputer at Oak
Ridge National Laboratory. We did not find significant volume dependence of the results. Using
the Padè approximation at multiple values of the imaginary chemical potential, we mapped out the
dependence of the QCD pressure in the complex plane of the baryon chemical potential. We have
found that the dependence on the QCD pressure on chemical potential agrees well with the
predictions of a hadron resonance gas, which has no singularities in the chemical potential. Our
results thus indicate that there is no critical point at temperatures around 108 MeV. The results are
now being prepared for publication in a refereed journal.

The Schwinger model (QED in one spatial and time dimension) shares many properties with QCD,
including confinement, vacuum chiral condensate, and chiral anomaly. Because of this, it
represents an important step towards understanding the dynamics of high energy hadron
interactions. The real-time dynamics of the Schwinger model, including the production of jets, can
be studied using quantum simulations.

The main challenge in implementing these simulations on quantum hardware is that the resulting
lattice Hamiltonian (obtained via staggered fermion formulation and the Jordan-Wigner
transformation) contains a term with all-to-all qubit connectivity. This term is proportional to the
square of the coupling constant g and as such does not present a problem at zero (or very small)
value of the coupling, but at a finite g, it brings noise to it and severely limits the depth of the
circuits that can be implemented. This is the reason that the Principal Investigators (PI), together
with Cameron Cogburn (Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute [RPI]) and Sebastian Groeninger (Stony
Brook University), decided to investigate a more efficient formulation of Trotterization, and to
study possible truncation schemes to reduce the noise and to facilitate the quantum simulations of
the Schwinger model on quantum hardware.

The PIs implemented the lattice formulation of the Schwinger model in a circuit, employing
Kogut-Susskind staggered fermions and Jordan-Wigner transformation. Furthermore, they
represented the gauge field through electric flux operators by using the Gauss law. The resulting
code was used both on classical hardware, and on RPI’s IBM Quantum System One quantum
computer.

Quantum error mitigation methods were employed to reduce the noise, and the limit to the circuit
depth on IBM Quantum System One was established, corresponding to about five lattice steps in
time evolution. At zero coupling, the noise was small, and a much longer time evolution was
possible. The PIs investigated different reformulations of Trotterization. This resulted in modest
but meaningful improvements in the achievable circuit depth.

The most promising direction that emerged from these studies is the truncation of the evolution
kernel that is enabled by the screening of electric flux by the fermion-antifermion pairs that are
produced. As a result, only a few nearby qubits appear entangled, and one can truncate the terms
corresponding to all-to-all qubit connectivity.

lattice formulation in terms of the XYZ spin chain. The PIs, together with Cameron Cogburn and
Sebastian Grieninger, have begun studies of baryon interactions in this approach.

• A novel truncation scheme for gauge theories, enabling their real-time simulations on IBM

• Circuit development for quantum simulations of QCD2 at a large number of colors, utilizing

The objective of this project is to simulate the performance of direct laser acceleration of electrons
using a radially polarized long-wave infrared (LWIR) laser. This process uses the longitudinal
electric field produced at the focus of a radially polarized laser to accelerate electrons without
requiring a medium, such as a plasma, to perform the acceleration. The wavelength scaling of the
direct laser acceleration process suggests that using an LWIR laser, with a wavelength ten times
longer than the near-infrared (NIR) lasers typically used, can result in much higher acceleration
efficiency and beam quality. Our goal is to quantify this improvement to show the degree to which
LWIR lasers outperform NIR lasers and determine the viability of current LWIR lasers for an
experimental demonstration of efficient direct laser acceleration. This result supports future
proposals for experimentally demonstrating the creation of high power LWIR radially polarized
beams and utilizing them for electron acceleration.

Lasers have been studied as a method for accelerating electrons for the past few decades, due to
the much higher potential acceleration gradient compared to conventional radio frequency
accelerators. The main pathway towards laser-driven accelerators has been plasma-based
accelerators, using the laser to create a bubble in a plasma and using the electric field generated by
the plasma to accelerate electrons. However, it is also possible to accelerate electrons directly with
the electric field of the laser, avoiding the complicated dynamics resulting from the plasma. This
requires a special polarization state, a radially polarized laser, with the direction of the electric
field changing as a function of spatial position, unlike conventional linearly or circularly polarized
light. Due to the relatively fragile nature of the necessary polarization optics for generating radially
polarized light, efforts in direct laser acceleration have been hampered by the low power of typical
radially polarized laser sources. Carbon dioxide (CO2) lasers, since the gaseous gain medium is
polarization insensitive, potentially allow the radial polarization to be generated at low power,
where damage is not a concern, before subsequently amplifying to high power. Thus, they offer a
potential pathway forward to direct laser acceleration at practically useful levels. Furthermore,
theoretical studies of direct laser acceleration have demonstrated that the acceleration mechanism
is insensitive to wavelength, as long as the focal size and pulse length are scaled with the
wavelength. Since CO2 lasers operate in the LWIR regime, with a wavelength around an order of
magnitude higher than conventional NIR lasers, the focal size and pulse length for the same peak
acceleration performance are an order of magnitude larger as well. This increased size can
potentially allow for much more efficient electron beam coupling, and thus, much higher
acceleration efficiency.

In this project, we focus on characterizing the acceleration performance of a hypothetical radially
polarized LWIR laser. First, we aim to compare the acceleration efficiency of an LWIR laser to
that of an NIR laser with the same pulse energy and equivalent focal sizes and pulse lengths. Since
theory indicates that the LWIR laser should have far superior efficiency, our goal was to quantify
this more precisely for a wide range of laser powers, ranging from terawatts to petawatts. Second,
we aim to study the potential acceleration performance of the current Accelerator Test Facility

 106

(ATF) terawatt CO2 laser. Currently, the ATF CO2 laser is capable of producing a few terawatts of
peak power at a pulse duration of 2 ps. We simulate the interaction of this laser with the ATF
electron beam and characterize the beam quality and energy gain to determine the viability of an
experiment conducted with the available equipment.

These studies are all done using Astra, a particle tracking code that simulates the interaction of an
electron beam with electromagnetic fields, including the space charge forces from the beam’s self-
interaction. As there is no plasma involved in the direct laser acceleration process, the laser fields
can be incorporated into the simulation using the analytical expression of a radially polarized
beam, included natively as a module in Astra. We vary the laser and electron beam parameters to
capture the dependence of acceleration performance on wavelength for a wide range of settings.

We conducted all the simulation studies listed above, both the comparison of NIR to LWIR
acceleration performance and the simulated experiment with the ATF facilities. In the first part of
the project, we found that the LWIR laser typically achieves between five to ten times higher

electron beam sizes from 2 microns to 10 microns, and that it can produce up to hundreds of MeV

scaled spot size and pulse length of the LWIR laser causes the NIR laser to fail to produce any

typical photoinjector beam quality.

We have demonstrated that LWIR lasers are the ideal candidate for direct laser acceleration,
substantially outperforming equivalent NIR lasers, and that the ATF laser is sufficient for a record-
breaking demonstration of direct laser acceleration. Advances in LWIR laser technology have been
estimated to allow for hundreds of terawatts of peak power, compared to the few terawatts
available. At those powers, we have shown that direct laser acceleration with LWIR lasers can
produce >100 MeV energy gain, allowing it to compete with linacs and wakefield accelerators,
with a more compact footprint than the former and potentially better beam quality than the latter.

analysis was carried out based on the published data for the conductor. A test bench was developed
for Voltage-Current (V-I) characterization of HTSC cables in liquid nitrogen. A LabVIEW

similar and other HTSC-based projects. A high-level road map was prepared for pursuing HTSC-
