ABSTRACT
Development of a capability to measure trace gas contaminants released from pulsed power
electrodes would immediately impact Sandia pulsed power research. These releases occur during
ultra-fast heating of metal electrodes during pulsed power discharges and can lead to substantial
power losses through plasma formation. Detection of contaminants is a formidable challenge due to
the need for in-situ spatially and temporally resolved measurements of trace gases in the extreme
environment of ultra-fast heated metal surfaces. We investigate the feasibility of laser diagnostics for
detecting contaminants, including H-atom, OH, and H2O. Laser-induced fluorescence and
photofragmentation fluorescence showed significant plasma emission interferences and did not yield
any detectable H-atom, OH, or H2O. Our newly developed H-atom detection using femtosecond
degenerate four-wave mixing suppressed interferences and enabled detection of H-atoms. A few
shots showed large signals in the near-surface region of metal foils, suggesting the formation of a
wave of H-atoms from the metal.

1. FOIL/WIRE PULSED DISCHARGE APPARATUS
To develop laser diagnostics for detecting contaminants released from rapidly heated metal
electrodes, we constructed a model wire/foil discharge apparatus that was based on a system
previously developed by Mark Johnston and co-workers. The new system was adapted to facilitate
optical access for laser diagnostics. This pulser apparatus involves rapidly heating metal surfaces
through a fast, capacitive discharge and was previously used to investigate plasma formation from
rapidly heated fine wires. Figure 1, Figure 2, and Figure 3 respectively show a schematic diagram,
CAD drawing, and photos of the experimental setup. The electrical circuit, including capacitors,
charge relays, and spark-gap switch, is submerged in transformer oil to ensure electrical isolation.
Three capacitors are charged in parallel to positive high voltage (20-60kV) and discharged through a
triggered Maxwell spark-gap switch to ground (< 2ns jitter). The wire setup is mounted inside a 6-
port cross vacuum chamber with a turbo pump that provides a base pressure in the range of 1x10-5
Torr, which is typical for pulsed-power devices like Z. This system provides heating rates of the
order 1x1010 C/sec, as shown for an aluminum wire in Figure 4. These heating rates are consistent
with those predicted for Z. This apparatus has previously been used as a plasma source with plasma
densities of 1015-1017 cm-3 and 1-10eV temperatures. For this LDRD, we are interested in the early
resistive heating phase of the wire and subsequent gas desorption, which occurs on timescales of
~100-200 ns depending on wire material and thicknesses. Figure 4 shows the ~160 ns resistive
heating phase for a 100 um diameter aluminum wire. The rate of current rise was ~60A/ns. As seen
from the thermodynamical calculation (ThD), which takes into account the experimental current,
the resistivity of the metal and its heat capacity, both as functions of temperature, the metal rapidly
passes through the melting point (663 C) and boiling point (2470 C) during this initial heating phase.

Coupling of the wire heating apparatus with single-shot laser measurements presents significant
challenges, including spatiotemporal overlapping of the laser probes with the near-field region of the
wire as it undergoes rapid desorption and vaporization, as well as suppression of potential
interferences. The probe volume is positioned a millimeter or less from a metal wire or foil to detect
desorbed species in the near field where number densities are expected to be highest as desorbed
gases expand radially away from the wire. Although recent spectral measurements on the Z machine
have identified hydrogen as the primary plasma constituent [1], density estimates in the pre-plasma
phase have large uncertainties due to lack of available information. As a risk mitigation strategy to
improve the probability of detecting desorbed species, we took advantage of recent efforts to adapt
the rapid heating system to metal foil targets. The foil provides an alternative system with longer
detection pathlengths and larger number densities of desorbed species than occur in the single wire
configuration. Figure 5 shows the foil being rapidly heated along with a current trace. The foil
mounting configuration evolved from initial tests in which it was attached to the electrode rod by
copper tape to a clamping mechanism that used a clamp containing ridges that provided improved
electrical contact between the electrode and foil.

2. TWO-PHOTON LASER-INDUCED FLUORESCENCE OF ATOMIC
 HYDROGEN
Laser-induced fluorescence (LIF) is a highly sensitive detection technique that is used for spatially
and temporally resolved imaging of atomic and molecular species whose electronically excited states
are accessible by laser excitation with subsequent fluorescence emission. Our first efforts to
implement LIF imaging near a rapidly heated metal foil in the pulsed discharge apparatus focused on
detection of atomic hydrogen. The first electronic transition from the ground state of the hydrogen
atom requires a photon of 10.2 eV. This large energy spacing of the electronic states precludes
single-photon LIF excitation of ground-state hydrogen atoms, which requires VUV light that is
difficult to generate and is absorbed by air and vacuum chamber windows. Instead, we use an
alternative LIF scheme of two-photon excitation (TP-LIF) in which the combined energy of two
photons spans the energy gap of the H-atom n=1®3 transition, as shown in the energy level
diagram in Figure 6. The resulting fluorescence emission from the Ha (n=3®2) transition is
detected at a visible wavelength of 656 nm. Two-photon laser-induced fluorescence is the most
widely adopted strategy for H-atom LIF detection and has a long history in combustion research.[2-
9]
Two-photon excitation could be accomplished using excitation by nanosecond (ns), picosecond (ps),
or femtosecond (fs) pulsed lasers. While early work used ns pulsed lasers, more recent availability of
commercial ultrafast lasers has expanded their use in TP-LIF measurements. For some applications,
ultrafast pumped variants of TP-LIF detection of H-atom have advantages over ns excitation.[5, 6,
8] For example, the high peak intensity of ps and fs duration excitation pulses can dramatically
reduce the propensity for photolytic production and detection of excess H during the laser-sample
interaction in flames.[5, 9] While we performed some initial H-atom LIF tests outside of the pulsed
discharge chamber using a fs laser (see discussion in following paragraphs), our main feasibility
studies of TP-LIF H-atom measurements in pulsed discharges used a 10-ns pulsed tunable dye laser,
which provided the best possibility for single-shot H-atom detection. The laser was tuned to the H-
atom n=1®3 transition at 205.1 nm, which was generated by frequency tripling the fundamental
output of a dye laser at 615 nm. We detected the H-atom LIF emission with a gated intensified CCD
camera (Andor Technologies) that was positioned orthogonal to the laser beam propagation
direction and fitted with a 58-mm focal length, f/1.2 Noct Nikkor camera lens and a bandpass
interference filter that transmitted the Ha transition (Dl=7 nm) and blocked out-of-band optical
emission.
To test the H-atom LIF excitation-detection setup, we first performed measurements in a premixed
H2-air flame with an equivalence ratio of 1.4 that was stabilized on a 1.25 mm diameter tube outside
of the pulsed discharge vacuum chamber, as shown in Figure 6. For these measurements, the laser
beam traversed the premixed flame cone and was focused on the axis of the burner. The H-atom
LIF image in the figure shows two regions of strong signal on either side of the flame cone. The LIF
signal intensity peaks near the flame front and then decays away at larger radial locations as the laser
beam traverses the combustion products that eventually mix with the surrounding room air. The
peak H-atom number density at the flame front is predicted using Chemkin calculations [10] to be
approximately 3x1017 cm-3. The dark region in the middle of the flame cone corresponds to the
location of the H2-air reactant mixture upstream of the flame front.
In addition to using the ns dye laser, we also tested a femtosecond laser for H-atom LIF. The very
large bandwidth of a femtosecond laser is problematic for single-photon LIF excitation because of
the greatly reduced spectral overlap with the LIF transition. However, excitation of two-photon

transitions with a femtosecond laser doesn’t suffer the same inefficiencies due to photon pairing. In
photon pairing, photons with energies less than the two-photon transition can pair with photons
with energies that are greater than the two-photon transition, as illustrated in the diagram of Figure
7. Photon pairing enables off-resonance photons to contribute to excitation of the laser-pumped
transition. We measured H-atom LIF excitation spectra using both the fs and ns lasers by tuning the
laser wavelength while recording the LIF signal in the flame. The resulting excitation spectra are
shown in Figure 7. The significantly broader width of the femtosecond laser excitation spectrum is
the result of the laser’s inherent broad bandwidth, and it demonstrates the utility of photon-pairing
in two-photon excitation of H-atom LIF.
We also used the fs-laser excitation of H-atom LIF in the flame to evaluate the turnoff time of the
gated image intensifier on the camera. The turnoff time is important for knowing how effectively the
intensifier can be used to gate out unwanted optical emission interference. By scanning the time
delay of the image intensifier gate relative to the fs-laser pulses and recording the H-atom LIF signal
at each time delay, we mapped out the edge of the intensifier gate. The time resolution of such a
measurement is very high because we rely on the rapid rise of H-atom LIF signal, and the rate of rise
is determined by the approximately 110-fs duration of the excitation laser pulse. The resulting switch
time of the image intensifier was determined to be 3.3 ns, as show in Figure 8.

After optimizing the LIF imaging detection setup in the flame, we transitioned to feasibility studies
of H-atom LIF imaging in the vacuum chamber during a high-voltage pulsed discharge through a
foil using the ns pulsed dye laser. This transition involved routing the laser beam through a fused
silica window on the chamber and focusing the beam as close as possible to the foil surface without
creating interference from light scattering from the foil. The burner for the flame experiments was
removed, and the camera was positioned closer to the chamber window such that the focal plane of
the camera coincided with the position of the laser beam. The timing of the laser pulse and gating of
the image intensifier were adjusted using digital delay generators such that the LIF detection could
be performed at different time delays relative to the high-voltage discharge. Despite numerous
attempts at detecting H-atom LIF signal during a pulsed discharge with different metal foils, we
were unable to detect any signal. The dominant impediment was that optical emission interference
overwhelmed the detection system. To minimize the interference, we tried using two different Ha
bandpass filters with bandpasses of 7 nm and 1 nm in front of the camera. As shown in Figure 9,
the 1 nm bandpass significantly reduced the interference but it was still overwhelming, indicating
that the main interference is likely Ha emission during the discharge. In fact, for the images in Figure

3. LASER-INDUCED FLUORESCENCE OF HYDROXYL RADICAL
The results of the previous section demonstrate that strong optical emission precludes the use of
two-photon LIF imaging for detecting low levels of atomic hydrogen near a rapidly heated metal
foil. Next, we investigated the feasibility of LIF imaging of the hydroxyl radical (OH), which is
another potentially important highly reactive molecule that could be produced by dissociation of
H2O contaminants on metal electrodes. LIF detection of OH is widely used in combustion and
plasma research and is readily accessible using single-photon UV excitation.

The OH-LIF experimental setup was similar to that of H-atom LIF with the substitution of a
different laser and camera. The OH-LIF excitation/detection scheme is depicted in the energy-level
diagram in Figure 12. The OH molecules were excited by a 10-ns pulsed dye laser tuned to the Q1(6)
rotational transition in the A–X (1,0) band at 283.011 nm. This wavelength was produced using
second harmonic generation of a dye laser at 564 nm (Lambda-Physik Scanmate), pumped by the
second harmonic of a 10 Hz Nd:YAG laser (Spectra-Physics GCR-150). The laser’s fundamental
wavelength was monitored using a wavemeter (HighFinesse WS7). The resulting fluorescence from
the A–X (1,1) and A–X (0,0) bands was detected using UV-sensitive Gen-II intensified CCD camera
(Andor USB iStar) fitted with a band pass filter centered at 310 nm (Δλ=10 nm) and a UG-11
colored glass filter. Emission from the A–X (0,0) band results from the ground vibrational level of
the OH A-state being populated by vibrational energy transfer (VET) from the laser-excited v=1
state.

Similar to the H-atom TP-LIF measurements, we first tested OH-LIF excitation and detection
outside of the pulsed discharge chamber using a premixed H2/air jet flame with an equivalence ratio
of 1.4, as shown in Figure 13. The j=6 rotational level is near the peak of the rotational population
distribution at flame temperatures. The OH-LIF image on the far right of the figure shows the very
strong signals that are generated in this flame, which has predicted peak OH number densities on
the order of 4x1016 cm-3. The dark conical region at the center bottom of the OH-LIF image is the
cold H2/air reactant mixture upstream of the flame front.

After optimizing the OH-LIF imaging in the flame, we removed the burner and repositioned the
laser beam and camera for OH-LIF measurements inside the vacuum chamber during a pulsed
discharge through a metal foil, as shown in Figure 14. To minimize plasma emission interference, we
gated the image intensifier on the camera for only a 20 ns period bracketing the laser pulse. The UV
detection wavelength for OH-LIF signals reduced the optical emission interference during the
pulsed discharge by orders of magnitude relative to that of H-atom LIF detection at 656 nm. As a
result, we did not need to use any neutral density filters to attenuate the emission to prevent
saturation of the detector. The image on the right side of Figure 14 shows the signal that was
recorded with the laser positioned near the face of a 4 mm x 25 mm 304 stainless-steel foil with a
thickness of 25 um. The OH-LIF laser excitation pulse timing was 230 ns before the peak current,
which was monitored using the current probe that was integrated into the pulsed discharge
apparatus. The blue arrow in the figure indicates the laser beam position and direction of
propagation. This image does not show any discernable signal from OH. The only observable laser-
generated signal is two spots, one on each side of the foil where stray laser light scattered off the foil.
Although the optical emission interference from the discharge is substantially less than that of the
H-atom LIF measurements in the previous section, there is significant interference in the upper and
lower portions of the foil, with the largest interference occurring where the foil is clamped to the
upper electrode. After attempting OH-LIF measurements at a range of different time delays relative

4. DETECTION OF H2O BY LASER-INDUCED
 PHOTOFRAGMENTATION FLUORESCENCE
Water is another potentially important contaminant species that could desorb from an electrode
surface. It remains unclear the extent to which water would desorb from an ultra-rapidly heated
electrode and remain as H2O molecules in the vapor phase, as opposed to dissociating to form
fragments such as H, O, H2, OH. Temporally and spatially resolved laser diagnostics for gas-phase
H2O detection can be challenging because H2O does not have strong laser-induced fluorescence
transitions. Spontaneous Raman scattering is one approach that has been used extensively to
measure water vapor, for example in combustion research [11, 12], but the detection sensitivity
limits its use to systems in which H2O is a major species. To address this need for water vapor
measurements in the near-surface region of an electrode during a discharge, we investigated the use
of laser-induced photofragmentation fluorescence (LIPF), a diagnostic technique that was recently
developed at Sandia’s Combustion Research Facility for sensitive gas-phase water vapor detection.
The LIPF technique involves laser excitation of the target molecule to an excited state that
subsequently dissociates to form excited-state photofragments. As these photofragments decay to
lower energy states, they can emit fluorescence that can be detected. LIPF has previously been used
for detection of molecules such as CH3 that have pre-dissociative electronically excited states and
therefore can’t be directly detected by LIF.[13] Livingston and Kliewer [14] recently showed that fs
pulses at 244 nm readily excite H2O molecules by a doubly resonant 2+2 scheme through the D
excited state of H2O to a neutral Rydberg state, which undergoes rapid dissociation producing
excited H (n=2 to n=9) and OH(A) that then fluoresce. Examples of the resulting LIPF emission
spectra for excited-state H and OH are shown in Figure 15.

resulting from laser-induced photofragmentation of H2O.

The H2O LIPF technique thus provides a choice of detection schemes because photofragmentation
produces emission from both H and OH molecules. Our initial LIF imaging studies showed that
detection of Ha emission at 656 nm in the near-surface region of a foil during an electrical discharge
is overwhelmed by interference from background Ha emission (see Section 2 above). It may be
possible to detect emission from other H-atom transitions shown in Figure 15, such as 4®2 or
5®2, with less interference than the Ha transition. However, we expect that detection of OH(A-X)
band emission in the UV is more promising for reducing interferences. This expectation is in part
based on our experience with feasibility studies that we performed on OH-LIF measurements in the
pulsed discharge apparatus. As a result, we performed our studies on H2O LIPF measurements
using the same ICCD camera detection system that we used for OH-LIF feasibility studies in the
previous section. A narrow bandpass interference filter centered near 310 nm and UG-11 colored
glass filter were mounted in front of the camera lens to transmit the OH-LIF signal and block
interference. To excite the LIPF signal, we used 110 fs laser pulses with the laser wavelength tuned
to 244.3 nm.
A convenient aspect of the H2O LIPF technique is that it is straightforward to perform in-situ
optimization of the alignment, focusing, and laser tuning simply by filling the vacuum chamber of
the pulser apparatus with a known pressure of water vapor. Figure 16 shows the H2O LIPF
experimental setup and the resulting calibration signal from a known concentration of water vapor.
In this case, we evacuated the vacuum chamber and then leaked in water vapor to fill the chamber
with 3.7 Torr of H2O. The water was evaporated from a test tube filled with water that had been put
through freeze-pump-thaw cycles to remove contaminants. The LIPF image on the right side of
Figure 16 shows that the fs laser was well focused to a line, and the emission from the line generated
a well-focused image on the ICCD camera.

Once the H2O LIPF signal was established and the focus and laser timing were synchronized to the
HV pulser, we performed a series of tests to search for H2O LIPF signal during a pulsed discharge.
The laser was focused to a line that was positioned a few hundred microns away from a 304
stainless-steel foil, as shown in Figure 17. Prior to the pulsed discharge, the foil was dosed with
water by exposing it to 2-5 Torr of water vapor, and then the vacuum chamber was evacuated prior
to the high-voltage discharge. Examples of single-shot LIPF measurements are shown in Figure 18.
The image on the top of the figure was acquired using a stainless-steel foil with a thickness of 25 um
at 430 ns before the peak current of the discharge. The position of the horizontal path of the
focused laser beam is indicated by the light blue arrow in the figure. Note that unlike the H-atom
LIF measurements, the neutral density filters were not needed since the background was orders of
magnitude lower for the OH spectral bandpass near 310 nm. An image intensifier gate width of
40 ns bracketing the LIPF laser pulse was used to help block optical emission interference from the
discharge. Nevertheless, for the 25 um foil there was significant emission interference from the foil
itself, and no discernable H2O LIPF signal was detected above the background emission. The
bottom panel of Figure 18 shows results from a 50 um stainless-steel foil at both 430 ns before peak
current and at peak current, which exhibit significantly less emission interference than for the 25 um
foil. At the 430 ns timing, the optical emission from the discharge is negligible, whereas at peak
current there is some background emission observed but fortunately it mostly occurs in the lower
portion of the image at a distance from the laser probe region. An extensive series of tests was
performed in an effort to detect H2O LIPF signal using different delay times, stainless-steel foil
thicknesses of 25 um and 50 um with widths of 4 mm and 10 mm, and discharge voltages of 20 kV
and 30 kV. In addition to stainless-steel, we tested aluminum foils that were roughened or anodized,
which should increase water adsorption during the water vapor dosing. However, none of these tests
showed a detectable H2O LIPF signal.

By combining the lack of detectable H2O LIPF signal with an evaluation of the detection limits, we
can provide an estimate of the upper bounds of H2O vapor desorbing from the foils during a
discharge. To this end, we performed a series of measurements to determine the H2O detection limit
of this LIPF setup. We measured the H2O LIPF signal from OH fluorescence with the vacuum
chamber filled with incrementally larger number densities of water vapor. The left side of Figure 19
shows the LIPF signal as a function of H2O number density for two different intensifier gate widths
of 40 ns and 640 ns. The shorter gate of 40 ns was used for the measurements in the pulsed
discharge to gate out background emission interference. While this short gate width was good for
blocking interference, it did attenuate the signal because at these low pressures the decay time for
the OH fluorescence is considerably longer than 40 ns. For comparison, we included the longer gate
of 640 ns to show what LIPF signal would be obtained if the intensifier gate was sufficiently wide to
capture the entire decay of the OH fluorescence emission. The right side of Figure 19 shows the
signal-to-noise ratio of the LIPF signal as a function of the H2O number density. Again, the results
are shown for both 40 and 640 ns intensifier gate widths. In this plot, we define a detection limit as
a signal-to-noise ratio equal to 5. Based on this definition, the H2O LPF signal detection limits for
40 ns and 640 ns intensifier gate widths are 1.0x1016 cm-3 and 2.3x1015 cm-3, respectively. We
conclude that the H2O number density desorbing from the foil under the tested conditions is likely
below 1.0x1016 cm-3.

5. DEGENERATE FOUR-WAVE MIXING OF ATOMIC HYDROGEN
The results of the previous sections demonstrate that two-dimensional imaging using laser-induced
fluorescence or photofragmentation fluorescence techniques are challenging to perform in extreme
environments of pulsed discharges due to optical emission from the discharge creating significant
interferences. While strategies can be developed to help mitigate these interferences, they are
difficult to avoid. An alternative approach is to use coherent wave-mixing techniques that provide
sensitive species-specific single-point measurements. In coherent approaches, the signal emerges
from the probe volume as a coherent beam and can be detected far away from the experimental
apparatus. This feature enables suppression of interferences from extremely bright incoherent
backgrounds such as plasma optical emission as well as electromagnetic interferences. Additional
blocking of incoherent backgrounds can be achieved using spatial pinhole filters. Coherent wave-
mixing techniques are thus well-suited for the challenges of these environments and have been
pursued using our femtosecond laser system. In this project, we developed and demonstrated the
use of femtosecond degenerate four-wave-mixing (DFWM) for sensitive detection of atomic
hydrogen, and much of the following description of the diagnostic development is published in Ref.
[15].
In a previous study of femtosecond four-wave mixing detection, Stauffer et al [16] demonstrated fs-
DFWM detection of molecular NO. In this scheme, fs pulses at 450 nm are two-photon resonant
with the A to X transition of NO. A frequency-shifted probe beam at 398 nm induced the DFWM
signal generation. In that experiment, the probe beam was demonstrated in both fs and ps regimes.
Because the probe beam was shifted from the signal, a collinear arrangement was used and a spectral
filter was used to isolate the signal on the detector. The clear advantage of such a scheme is that the
fs driving fields are faster than collision timescales even at pressures above 1 bar, enabling
collisional-quenching-free acquisition of signals.
We extend the idea of fs-DFWM to the detection of atomic hydrogen. Our aim is to create an
alternative method that overcomes the collisional quenching uncertainties associated with LIF-based
approaches as well as creates a coherent laser-like signal beam which can be spatially separated from
bright incoherent backgrounds. The experimental strategy is outlined in Figure 20. A Ti:Sapphire
based fs regenerative amplifier (Spectra-Physics Solstice Ace) produces 110 fs pulses that drive an
automated OPA (Topas-Prime Plus) with mixing to the deep UV. Figure 20c shows the energy level
diagram for the scheme developed in this work. Two photons near 243 nm promote nascent H-
atoms to the n=2 excited state (1S to 2S transition), and a degenerate probe beam scatters from the
electronic coherence to generate the DFWM signal. The laser beam is split into three different
paths, two pump beams and a probe beam. The mirrors are configured to align the separate beams
through a single spherical lens such that the beams are spatially overlapped at the focus of the lens.
The combination of the two pump beams drives a coherence in the hydrogen atoms, and the probe
beam scatters off this coherence to produce a coherent signal beam at the same wavelength of
243 nm. A folded BOXCARS like phase matching arrangement was used with a beam arrangement
on the L1 lens that is shown in Figure 20a, and all beam polarizations were set to vertical prior to
mixing. This phase-matching arrangement allowed the signal beam to be spatially isolated from
both pump and probe beams. To increase the spatial isolation of the signal from scattering of the
incoming light, a 50 µm pinhole was placed in front of the photomultiplier tube (PMT, Hamamatsu).
A photodiode (Thorlabs Det10A) monitored a weak back-reflection from the focusing lens allowing
shot-to-shot tracking of laser intensity fluctuations. The output of the PMT and photodiode were
recorded on an oscilloscope. Time-of-flight delay stages (New Focus 8310, Newport XMS 100)
were used for precise control of the pulse timing. By scanning the delay lines, we can temporally

To achieve temporal overlap of the femtosecond laser pulses requires very precise alignment and
timing because these ultrafast laser pulses are on the order of 30 um long. We developed a method
for using laser-induced photofragmentation fluorescence (LIPF) signal from water vapor (see
Section 4 above) as a convenient method to achieve and monitor pulse timing. An intensified
charge-coupled device (ICCD) camera (Andor Technology iStar) was placed orthogonally to the
laser beam propagation direction and collected the emitted OH(A) fragmentation fluorescence.
Figure 21 shows an image that was recorded using LIPF of the water vapor in room air to visualize
the overlap of the crossing beams. Figure 22a displays an image of the H2O LIPF emission
collected from the H2/CH4/air flame. The laser beam propagation through the conical flame is
readily seen with the strongest signals being generated by water vapor that is produced in the flame.
Because this photofragmentation fluorescence signal is highly nonlinear, a bright emission spot
occurs at the beam crossing when the pulse timing is overlapped between each pair of laser beams.
This is demonstrated in Figure 22b, where the background has been subtracted by first recording the
signal when the pulses are not temporally overlapped. The fs-LIPF signal was also used during

 To assess the capability of fs-DFWM to quantitatively determine the concentration of H-atom,
radial scans of the flame were performed and compared to a Chemkin [10] simulation of the species
concentration profiles. The experimental data were corrected for fluctuations in laser power by
dividing the measured intensity with the cube of the photodiode signal intensity. The peak-
normalized fs-DFWM signal profiles are compared for a range of laser power to the simulated H-
atom concentration profile (squared for direct comparison) in Figure 23. The effect of spatial
averaging caused by the finite length of the probe volume (~1 mm estimated by the geometric
overlap of the laser beam waists) is taken into account by simulating the axisymmetric H-atom
profile and convolving it with a 1D Gaussian kernel (FWHM=1 mm) that’s oriented tangential to
the curved flame front. The axisymmetric profile is simulated by rotating the 1D H-atom
concentration profile from the Chemkin calculation about the burner axis to emulate a 2D radial
cross section of the flame cone with the same radius of curvature as the actual flame at the
measurement location. Comparison of the profiles in Figure 23 demonstrates that at powers up to
about 20 µJ / pulse the profiles are quite reproducible and overlap well with the simulated
concentration profile. The simulation does not take into account entrainment of room air at the
outer edge of the flame, which explains the drop-off of H-atom signal beyond 4 mm radial distance.
However, for the profile taken at 27 µJ / pulse, the measured profile is highly distorted and begins
to resemble the species profile for H2O. At these elevated laser powers, we know that the fs pulse
photolyzes H2O to form H-atom based on the ICCD fluorescent emission signals. Thus, it is most
likely that photofragmentation fluorescence becomes a dominant interference at high laser fluence.
Below this power threshold, a cubed dependence on laser power was verified, indicating that
ionization is not significantly diminishing the H-atom PFWM signal.
One clear benefit of the current approach is the ability to time-resolve the coherent response. When
all three pulses are overlapped at time zero, a very large non-resonant signal dwarfs the response
from H-atom. In addition to the non-resonant signal, resonant fs-DFWM from the water C/D state
is likely. However, these states are short-lived, less than 2 ps [14], and the H-atom coherence
persists for many 10’s of ps, allowing simple isolation of the desired signal from both non-resonant
background and resonant interference from the water bands. A probe delay scan is presented in
Figure 24 with the probe volume placed at the maximum H-atom signal within the flame front. A
coherence decay constant of 42 ps was determined from a global fit to the delay scans. This value is
in excellent agreement with the coherence decay rate of 38 ps for H-atom n=1->2 transition
measured with two-color resonant six wave mixing spectroscopy.[17] It should be noted however
that our delay stage was limited in this setup to a maximum delay of 60 ps, limiting the dynamic
range of this exponential fit. Further, there is evidence of shallow coherent oscillations, or
coherence beats, on the exponential decay envelope, but this behavior could not be definitively
captured because of signal-to-noise constraints. Global fitting indicated a beat period of 7.8 ps,
which did not correlate to any obvious atomic transition spacings. Thus, we can only speculate that
a Stark splitting effect or Rabi oscillation may be minimally modulating the signal.

measurement of H-atom concentrations. The laser-like signal enables measurements with extremely
bright background luminosity as it may be spatially filtered and propagated over long distances.

pulse as the H-atom coherence lifetime is significantly longer than that of the nearby H2O C/D

approximately three meters away from the discharge chamber. The ability to place the detector at a

narrow wave of contaminants that desorb from the foil and propagate away from the surface. The

6. CONCLUSIONS
We have investigated the feasibility of several laser diagnostics for sensitive detection of
contaminants released from ultra-rapidly heated metal foils in a model pulsed discharge apparatus.
We investigated two-photon laser-induced fluorescence of atomic hydrogen, single-photon laser-
induced fluorescence of the hydroxyl radical, laser-induced photofragmentation of water vapor, and
we developed a technique for femtosecond degenerate four-wave mixing detection of atomic
hydrogen. The LIF detection of H-atom and OH had limited detection sensitivity due to large
optical emission interferences from the discharge. The H2O LIPF measurements used a 40 ns
detector gate time to help suppress interferences, resulting in a detection limit (SNR~5) of
approximately 1x1016 cm-3. This density represents an upper bound on the water vapor in the
contaminants released from the heated stainless-steel. To mitigate these interference challenges of
LIF and LIPF techniques, we developed a coherent detection approach for H-atoms using
femtosecond DFWM. This approach yielded H-atom signals in a coherent beam that could be
detected with a sensor positioned meters away from the discharge apparatus and enabled spatial
filtering of optical interferences with a pinhole in front of the detector. The results showed rare
events of very large signal. The example presented in this report indicates a peak H-atom density of
8.6x1014 cm-3 with an uncertainty on the order of ±1x1014 cm-3. The observation of these rare events
with exceedingly large H-atom signals suggests that the desorption of contaminants from ultra-
rapidly heated electrodes may occur in a narrow wave that propagates outward from the electrode
surface. The impact of these results is twofold: They inform considerations for implementing laser
diagnostics in larger pulsed power systems. In addition, the H-atom fs-DFWM measurements
provide the first measurements of atomic hydrogen in the near electrode region during the resistive
heating phase of the discharge, which helps inform future simulations of contaminant desorption.
