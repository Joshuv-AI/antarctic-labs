Activity-cliff awareness enables robust graph learning for molecular property prediction
Authors: Chao Cui, Xiaorui Su, Zaixi Zhang, Alejandro Velez-Arce, Jianmin Wang, Xiangcheng Shi, Yanbin Zhang, Jie Wu, Yu Zong Chen, Marinka Zitnik, Wanxiang Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurately predicting molecular activity is hindered by activity cliffs (ACs), which are sharp potency changes between highly similar compounds that distort the smoothness assumed by modern quantitative structure–activity relationship (SAR) models and graph neural networks (GNNs). Here we introduce AC awareness (ACA), an inductive bias that reshapes GNN latent spaces to account for these discontinuities. Implemented through an ACA loss combining regression with soft-margin triplet contrastive learning, the method dynamically mines high-value activity-cliff triplets during training and corrects inconsistent neighborhoods in latent space. This process yields progressively fewer cliff violations, more coherent activity gradients, and reduced label incoherence across diverse chemical spaces. Evaluated on 52 datasets spanning low-sample narrow-scaffold series, large mixed-scaffold benchmarks, matched-pair cliff classification, and absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion and toxicity (ADMET) property prediction, ACA consistently improves predictive accuracy over strong extended-connectivity fingerprint (ECFP) and GNN baselines. The approach generalizes across multiple GNN backbones and remains robust under fixed hyperparameters. These results suggest that ACA provides a principled strategy for enhancing molecular property prediction by aligning latent representations with the nonadditive behavior underlying ACs.Subject terms: Computer science, Drug screening, Cheminformatics

Introduction

Molecular activity prediction is an essential part of drug discovery to identify, refine, and virtually screen compounds. It helps researchers evaluate the potential biological activity of chemical compounds and select lead candidates for development1. Ligand-based approaches use deep learning to build predictive quantitative structure–activity relationship (SAR) models from datasets of molecular properties1,2. These models are designed to capture the complex relationships between molecular structures and their biological activities, utilizing a variety of molecular representations, such as graphs3–5, molecular descriptors6–9, images10–13, and fingerprints14–16. Geometric deep learning and graph neural networks (GNNs), which can directly model the structural and relational information of molecular graphs, have been successfully used for molecular activity prediction17. Despite their potential, GNNs can underperform compared to other methods18–24 and can perform worse than advanced molecular fingerprint techniques19. This drop in model performance can be in part attributed to the presence of activity cliffs (ACs)19,21,22.

ACs occur when structurally similar molecules exhibit drastically different biological activities, creating discontinuities in the chemical space that are difficult for models to capture accurately25. In most studies19,26–29, “structurally similar” is defined using fingerprint-based Tanimoto/Jaccard similarity or scaffold-based criteria such as Bemis–Murcko frameworks. However, such empirical cutoffs (e.g., Tanimoto ≥ 0.9) capture only limited aspects of similarity, and practically relevant cliffs may also arise from subtle within-scaffold modifications or side-chain rearrangements that are not detected by these rules. Importantly, ACs also reflect the well-established phenomenon of SAR non-additivity. In nonadditive regions, small structural changes can yield disproportionately large activity shifts—behavior repeatedly documented through double-transformation cycle (DTC) analyses and related studies30–33. Such regions violate assumptions made by classical additive SAR models, such as the Hansch and Free–Wilson approaches34,35, and they correspond closely to the most pronounced ACs. This poses particular difficulties for GNNs: their latent spaces often embed structurally similar molecules near one another, leading to erroneous predictions when their activities diverge markedly. Addressing ACs is therefore central to improving the robustness and reliability of molecular activity prediction19. While cliff prediction can also be formulated using matched molecular pairs (MMPs)36, quantitative SAR and ML models generally exhibit low sensitivity when both molecules are unseen during training21. Developing model inductive biases that directly enhance AC sensitivity thus represents a promising strategy for advancing molecular machine learning.

Here, we introduce AC awareness (ACA), an inductive bias for GNN models designed to improve molecular activity prediction. Our approach optimizes the latent feature space to address discontinuities in SAR25, avoiding fragmented feature distributions37. This is achieved by integrating an ACA loss that couples a standard regression loss with a triplet soft-margin (TSM) objective. The TSM component penalizes violations in the relative distances between an anchor and its neighbors in AC triplets (ACTs), ensuring that the latent geometry correctly reflects potency differences. Two parameters, cliff lower (cl) and cliff upper (cu), define the activity difference window used to generate high-value ACTs (HV-ACTs), allowing the model to develop explicit awareness of the most informative and ambiguous regions of SAR space. By explicitly optimizing HV-ACTs, ACA loss reshapes the latent space to push cliff-forming neighbors apart while keeping potency-consistent analogs close, thereby encoding structural, semantic, and functional nuances that standard regression losses cannot capture. This design directly targets the underlying mechanism of ACs—nonadditive and abrupt SAR discontinuities—providing a task-aligned inductive bias that enhances model expressiveness without altering the network architecture.

The proposed ACA loss demonstrates three key advantages. First, it provides a consistent mechanism for correcting latent-space inconsistencies: across architectures, the number of HV-ACTs remains largely unchanged when using regression loss alone, whereas ACA loss steadily reduces HV-ACTs as the latent representation becomes progressively aligned with activity differences. Second, this correction leads to substantially enhanced label coherence in the learned feature space. Representative case studies spanning low-sample narrow-scaffold (LSSNS) BRAF38 and high-sample mixed-scaffold (HSSMS) JAK119 datasets both show clearer and more continuous activity gradients under ACA, and systematic analyses across all 30 HSSMS benchmarks confirm that this behavior is dataset-agnostic and robust to scaffold diversity. Third, at the task level, ACA improves predictive performance across four major benchmark categories—including LSSNS regression, HSSMS regression, MMP cliff classification, and ADMET delta prediction—demonstrating that the same inductive bias naturally extends to broader property-cliff phenomena beyond potency alone. Together, these results show that ACA loss provides a general, architecture-independent mechanism for enhancing GNN sensitivity to discontinuous SAR, offering a principled pathway toward more reliable and chemically meaningful molecular activity prediction.

Results

Equipping graph neural network models with ACA

The concept of ACA was introduced to address the issues of ACs in GNNs used for molecular activity prediction. Here, the ACA of a model is the ability to distinguish between structurally similar molecules with different activities in the learned latent space. The infusion of ACA is achieved by minimizing the innovative ACA loss function, which balances regression loss (e.g., MAE) with TSM loss, controlled by a tunable hyperparameter α. Figure 1a provides an overview of the proposed ACA loss and its implementation in the GNN-based ACA network (ACANET) models. The TSM loss penalizes the model when the relative distances between anchor (A), positive (P), and negative (N) compounds in the latent space deviate from their corresponding distances in the activity space. This penalty ensures that the model learns more informative features by aligning the latent space more accurately with the true activity relationships.

Overview of the proposed ACA loss and the GNN-based ACANET for molecular activity prediction.

a ACANET training and ACA loss. During the training of ACANET, the ACA loss is calculated for each batch. The model dynamically mines activity cliff triplets (ACTs) in real time. This on-the-fly scheme avoids precomputing all triplets and allows the model to adaptively focus on informative cliff cases. b Cliff cut-off scenarios for on-the-fly ACT mining. The two hyperparameters, cliff lower (cl) and cliff upper (cu), define the activity-difference window used to construct label-conditioned triplets. All triplets that satisfy these label conditions form the set M, referred to as conditional ACTs. Among them, only those whose triplet soft-margin loss is strictly positive (i.e., they violate the desired ordering in latent space) are used for optimization; this subset is denoted as M′, the mined high-value (HV) ACTs. Thus, M controls the quantity of candidate triplets, while M′ reflects the effective triplets that provide a learning signal in each batch. c Soft margin triplets in ACA loss. Instead of using a fixed constant margin, each triplet adopts a margin derived from the ground-truth activity differences of the involved compounds. This adaptive margin enables ACANET to better handle continuous-valued activity labels and improve its discrimination between molecules with subtle or large activity differences.

During the training of ACANET, the conditional ACTs are first mined using two cliff cut-offs cl and cu—applied to the activity labels y, then only the high-value ACTs (conditional ACTs with their triplet loss values greater than zero) are used for the TSM loss calculation in each batch (Fig. 1b). Each triplet in the TSM loss has a margin calculated from the ground truth labels rather than a fixed margin for all triplets (Fig. 1c). These soft margins allow the model to handle continuous activity labels more effectively, improving its ability to differentiate between compounds with similar and different activities. Overall, the number of mined HV-ACTs reflects the degree of ACs in the current stage of learned molecular representations. If the model is equipped with ACA during training, the number of mined HV-ACTs would be decreased gradually with the increase in training epochs. This is because the training objective of minimizing the TSM loss will reduce the number of mined HV-ACTs, helping the model focus on predicting latent feature space for more challenging compounds.

Overview of datasets and tasks

We evaluated ACA and the ACANET model across 52 datasets, grouped into four tasks (Fig. 2b), to address the challenges posed by ACs in molecular property prediction. These tasks represent distinct scenarios encountered in drug design. The first task focuses on predicting molecular activities within LSSNS (Supplementary Table 1) datasets, which include compounds with conserved scaffolds, often developed as derivatives or analogs. These nine datasets are representative of fragment-to-lead39,40 and hit-to-lead41 drug design, where available data is limited. In this regime, researchers work with structurally similar compounds that share a core structure, aiming to optimize properties while maintaining or improving biological activity. However, ACs complicate quantitative SAR analysis, making accurate molecular activity prediction more difficult. The second task focuses on the HSSMS datasets (Supplementary Table 2). These 30 datasets contain substantially larger numbers of compounds and span a broad range of scaffolds and chemical space19, providing a setting for evaluating activity-prediction models across heterogeneous chemotypes. In contrast to the narrow-scaffold LSSNS datasets—typically used in classical quantitative SAR analysis within a single lead series—the HSSMS datasets better reflect machine-learning–based prediction across wider chemical space, where models are often applied to virtual screening tasks that involve prioritizing compounds from multiple scaffold families1. This distinction enables us to assess how well ACANET captures activity-cliff phenomena under both localized (LSSNS) and chemically diverse (HSSMS) conditions. The third task is classifying ACs using three MMP datasets. These datasets consist of pairs of structurally similar compounds with considerable differences in biological activity21,42–44. This classification task is crucial in the lead optimization phase of drug discovery, as it helps develop structural alerts and avoid modifications that may result in substantial drops in activity. We extended ACA to this classification task and tested its efficacy on these MMP datasets (Supplementary Table 3).

The 10 ADMET datasets (Supplementary Table 4) were included to evaluate the broader applicability of our method beyond activity prediction.

ACA captures activity cliffs in GNN models

Figure 3a, b presents a comparison between a GNN model trained without (left) and with (right) ACA. To illustrate the challenge posed by ACs, consider an ACT consisting of an A, a P, and a N. Although A and P are much closer in activity than A and N, A and N are more structurally similar, exemplifying the AC issue. Without the ACA, the model learns representations where the distance between A and P is much greater than between A and N in the latent space. This occurs because A and N exhibit high structural similarity despite their differing activities. Consequently, the model struggles with accurate training and prediction outcomes, facing challenges in effectively learning within the latent space due to AC issues. Conversely, with ACA, the model addresses this challenge by optimizing the latent vectors, ensuring that A is closer to P and further from N. In other words, the ACA loss function encourages the model to learn a latent space where the relative distances between compounds correspond to their activity relationships. Overall, the model with ACA integrates metric learning in the latent space while minimizing regression error. In contrast, a model without ACA focuses solely on regression loss and fails to perform well in the presence of ACs.

To validate the effectiveness of the ACA, we conducted a study using a medium-sized PPARδ dataset from the diverse HSSMS collection. Initially, we used the fixed ACA loss parameters (α = 1, cu = 1, cl = 1) to test the differences in training behavior, validation, and test performance between models with ACA (MAE loss + TSM loss) and without ACA (MAE loss only) (Fig. 3c). The results show that AC-aware models tend to converge faster (training MAE loss), although the final MAE loss is not clearly different between the two models. However, the optimization of HV-ACTs during training varied greatly. In the model without ACA, the number of HV-ACTs remained almost constant throughout training. In contrast, the model with ACA showed a gradual decrease in HV-ACTs during training, indicating that the model was refining the latent space to overcome the challenges posed by ACs. Remarkably, the validation and test RMSE results further highlighted the utility of ACA. When generalized to external validation and test sets, AC-aware models consistently achieved notably lower RMSE values than those without ACA, demonstrating the substantial benefits of incorporating ACA into the training process. The ACANET model uses principal neighborhood aggregation (PNA) as its default backbone, but we also tested it with three other GNN backbones: graph convolutional network (GCN), graph attention network (GAT), and graph isomorphism network (GIN). The results, shown in Fig. 4, indicate that across all tested GNN backbones, models equipped with ACA exhibit a gradual decrease in HV-ACTs during training, which also generalizes to the validation and test sets. Performance improvements were observed across the board, with test RMSE reductions of 5.81%, 5.48%, 4.63%, and 6.62% for the PNA, GAT, GCN, and GIN models, respectively, compared to models without ACA. These findings strongly support the effectiveness of the ACA in enhancing the predictive performance of the model by effectively handling the challenge of ACs, regardless of the GNN model backbone used. Two additional HSSMS datasets showed the same trends (Supplementary Fig. 19). To further rule out confounding from hyperparameter tuning, we trained all models under a unified configuration across the full set of 30 HSSMS datasets, keeping all ACA-related hyperparameters fixed (cu = 1, cl = 1, α = 1). Across all backbones (GAT, GCN, GIN, and PNA), ACA-aware models achieved lower test RMSEs on the vast majority of datasets (Supplementary Fig. 24), confirming that the benefits of ACA are robust, reproducible, and not contingent on model-specific tuning.

Effects of ACA loss hyperparameters

While the above analyses confirm the effectiveness of the ACA, the influence of hyperparameters in the ACA loss requires further investigation. We first conducted two ablation experiments to evaluate the impact of the ACA factor α and cliff parameters (cu and cl) in the ACA loss (with MAE as regression loss) on the ACANET model (PNA backbone) performance. We also explore the improvements of AC awareness on activity prediction performance under different regression losses.

In the first ablation study (Fig. 5a), we varied the cliff parameters while keeping the awareness factor α fixed at 1.0 to evaluate their effect on the number of mined conditional ACTs (M) and the RMSE performance of ACANET. The value of M is jointly determined by the label distribution and the chosen cliff parameters, and each dataset exhibits a dataset-specific upper limit on how many conditional ACTs can be mined under feasible (cl, cu) combinations (Supplementary Fig. 1). When M = 0 (visualized as purple dots in Fig. 5a), the triplet component of the ACA loss becomes inactive, and the overall loss reduces to the MAE regression loss alone, resulting in a notable degradation of model performance. In contrast, when M > 0, meaning at least some ACTs are mined during training, the model performance improves markedly. Although both the quality and quantity of ACTs influence performance, the RMSE displays a strong negative correlation with M across datasets (Supplementary Fig. 2), suggesting that the quantity of mined ACTs is a dominant factor in driving performance gains. This ablation confirms that properly choosing the cliff cut-off parameters is essential for enabling effective ACT mining, strengthening cliff awareness, and ultimately enhancing predictive accuracy.

In the second ablation study (Fig. 5b), we examined the impact of the ACA factor α on model performance while keeping the cliff parameters fixed. We varied α from 0.00 (no cliff awareness) to 1.00. As α increased, the number of mined high-value ACTs (M) decreased steadily during training, indicating that the model placed progressively stronger emphasis on activity-cliff distinctions. When α = 0, M remained nearly constant across epochs, consistent with the behavior expected from a model trained without ACA. Validation RMSE consistently decreased as α increased, demonstrating that a stronger ACA component generally leads to better predictive accuracy. However, α also controls the balance between the standard regression loss and the triplet contrastive component. Setting α too high can overweight the triplet loss, causing the model to overfit hard triplets and ultimately degrade overall performance. These results highlight that careful tuning of both the cliff parameters and the awareness factor α is important for achieving effective activity-cliff awareness. When properly selected, these hyperparameters enable the model to capture meaningful activity cliff structure in the data and substantially improve molecular activity prediction.

We evaluated the impact of the ACA on training ACANET models with different regression loss functions, specifically focusing on PNA-based architectures. The cross-validation performance on the LSSNS datasets and the optimized parameters (α, cl, cu) are provided in Supplementary Table 7. Our findings indicate that incorporating the ACA significantly enhances model performance, regardless of whether MAE or MSE is used as the regression loss function. In baseline activity prediction tasks on the LSSNS datasets, MAE outperforms MSE. However, after integrating the ACA, the improvements with MSE are particularly noteworthy. Across the nine LSSNS datasets, models using MAE as the baseline with ACA demonstrated an average improvement of 7.16%, while those using MSE showed a more substantial average improvement of 17.23%. The pronounced improvement observed with MSE can be attributed to its sensitivity to large errors, as MSE disproportionately penalizes large errors associated with ACs. By incorporating the ACA, the model becomes better at detecting and addressing these cliffs, leading to a reduction in the large errors that MSE would typically amplify. This results in greater performance gains when using MSE compared to MAE. Nevertheless, despite the considerable benefits MSE derives from ACA in mitigating large errors, models using MAE in combination with ACA ultimately achieve the best overall performance, with consistent improvements over strong traditional baselines and much larger gains relative to previous deep-learning methods (Supplementary Table 7). The linear treatment of errors by MAE, combined with the robustness introduced by ACA, provides a more balanced and effective approach for molecular activity prediction across the datasets.

ACA improves activity coherence in the latent space

In molecular representation learning, optimizing the latent space can sometimes fail to capture shared molecular features, leading to fragmented feature distributions37. This fragmentation becomes particularly problematic in regression tasks involving ACs, where structurally similar molecules may be mapped to nearby regions in the latent space despite having markedly different activity labels. This misalignment leads to incoherent label distributions and diminished model performance. The issue is especially evident in graph-based molecular representations, where topological information, while useful for capturing structural similarities, can complicate the distinction between ACs. For example, in the first activity-cliff triplet (Triplet-1) shown in Fig. 6, a high potency anchor (A1, CHEMBL3661239) and a positive compound (P1, CHEMBL3665861) with IC50 values of 56.5 nM and 19 nM against BRAF, respectively, are expected to be closely positioned in the latent space (Fig. 6a). However, in the GNN model without ACA, the distance between A1 and P1 was not shorter than the distance between A1 and the negative compound (N1, CHEMBL3661211), which has a similar structure but markedly reduced bioactivity (IC50 of 1357 nM). Similarly, in the second activity-cliff triplet (Triplet-2), the distance between a lower-potency anchor (A2, CHEMBL3661215, IC50 of 842 nM) and a positive compound (P2, CHEMBL3661206, IC50 of 809.6 nM) was not closer than that between A2 and the negative compound (N2, CHEMBL3665862, IC50 of 23.4 nM), which exhibits higher bioactivity despite its structural similarity to A2. These examples highlight the model’s inability to properly handle ACs without ACA, leading to suboptimal latent space distributions (Fig. 6b). In contrast, with ACA, the model successfully pushes N1 and N2 further away from their respective anchors (A1 and A2) and positive compounds (P1 and P2), refining the latent space to better handle ACs (Fig. 6c). These observations underscore the importance of ACA in guiding the model to prioritize bioactivity differences over structural similarities when mapping compounds in the latent space. By incorporating ACA, the model becomes more effective at distinguishing structurally similar compounds with divergent bioactivities, thereby optimizing the latent-space distribution, which in turn improves the accuracy of activity predictions (Fig. 7 and Supplementary Tables 7, 8).

We computed the label incoherence index (LII) for the BRAF dataset before and after model learning to analyze the coherence of activity label distributions in the original chemical space and the learned latent space. We also visualized the label distributions in the original chemical space and the latent space learned by models with and without ACA (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Figs. 22, 23). The label incoherence index measures the degree of activity label disparity among neighboring molecules; a lower LII indicates higher consistency between molecular distances and their activity labels. Our findings show that, compared to the original chemical space, the LII decreased in the latent spaces learned by the models. Notably, the latent space learned with ACA exhibited a markedly lower LII (0.199) than that without ACA (0.290), indicating higher coherence, with a performance improvement of 31.4%. Consistently, across all HSSMS datasets, ACA achieves lower LII than both the extended connectivity fingerprint (ECFP) structure baseline and GNNs trained without ACA (Fig. 8), and PCA projections of the HSSMS training and test sets show much smoother and more coherent activity gradients under ACA (Figs. 9 and 10). This enhancement translated into average performance improvements of 7.54% and 21.6% over baseline models using MAE and MSE regression losses, respectively. This improvement is also evident in the qualitative analysis of label color distribution, where the space with ACA shows greater label continuity (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Figs. 22, 23). In conclusion, our results demonstrate that the incorporation of ACA substantially improves label coherence in the latent space, highlighting its crucial role in enhancing the model’s ability to distinguish bioactivity differences effectively.

AC-informed ACANET outperforms other methods on HSSMS

We evaluated the performance of ACANET with ACA loss on the 30 HSSMS benchmark datasets for molecular activity prediction. In the benchmark study by van Tilborg et al.19, various models were assessed, including graph-based GAT, GCN, AFP, and MPNN; SMILES string-based Transformer, CNN, and LSTM; and fingerprint ECFP-based MLP, KNN, GBM, RF, and SVM. Among these, ECFP-based conventional ML models, particularly the SVM, achieved state-of-the-art (SOTA) performance. We compared the performance of these algorithms and ACANET on the overall test set RMSEall and the cliff-specific test set RMSEcliff of the HSSMS datasets using RMSE as the evaluation metric. The cliff parameters and ACA factor α were optimized using a linear search under nested five-fold cross-validation (see Supplementary Fig. 3), as described in the “Methods” section.

These results showed that ACANET consistently scored much lower RMSE values on both the overall test set and the cliff-specific test set (Fig. 7). ACANET models outperform molecular fingerprint-based models on 70%–76% of the HSSMS benchmark datasets. The performance of ACANET can be attributed to the equipment of ACA, as evidenced by the impact of the factor α on the model’s performance across various datasets (Supplementary Fig. 5). The model with ACA successfully reduced the cross-validation RMSE across all 30 HSSMS datasets, achieving an average performance improvement of 6.59% across all datasets (Supplementary Table 8). On RMSEall, ACANET outperforms all of the fingerprint ECFP-based ML approaches significantly. In particular, ACANET outperformed the SOTA ECFP-based SVM (Supplementary Fig. 6) by notable margins in both RMSEall (0.646 vs. 0.671) and RMSEcliff (0.711 vs. 0.742) across all 30 datasets. In terms of individual datasets, ACANET outperformed the SOTA SVM for 23 and 21 of the 30 datasets in RMSEall and RMSEcliff, respectively. These results suggest that ACANET with ACA loss is a robust approach for predicting the activity of both overall and cliff-specific compounds in the HSSMS datasets. We further analyzed the impact of the ACA factor α on the performance of a more challenging dataset, CLK4, and an easier dataset, 5-HT1A19. We observed that a larger α value contributed to better performance on CLK4, whereas for 5-HT1A, a larger α tended to result in overfitting (Supplementary Fig. 7). This occurs because a larger α emphasizes the ACA loss, penalizing the model for not correctly predicting the ACs. In an easier dataset like 5-HT1A, ACs may not play as crucial a role in overall bioactivity prediction. Thus, a larger α may force the model to overly focus on ACs at the expense of overall performance. Therefore, the tunable α parameter helps the model adapt to different situations and ensures robustness across various datasets. We also compared the AC-informed ACANET with traditional quantitative SAR methods, including a scaffold-based Free-Wilson model35 and a descriptor-based Partial Least Squares (PLS) model46 (see Supplementary Note 2). ACANET consistently and substantially outperforms both methods across targets and metrics.

ACA can help classify activity cliffs

We extended the concept of ACA to classification tasks for predicting AC and non-AC pairs (Supplementary Table 3). Unlike absolute regression activity prediction in quantitative SAR models, MMP-based AC classification aims to determine whether a pair of similar compounds forms an AC21,42–44. In contrast to regression tasks, ACA in classification is derived from the contrastive learning of paired molecules in latent space by pushing the molecules of AC pairs further apart and bringing the molecules of non-AC pairs closer together (as detailed in the Methods section). Similar to the ACA loss in regression, the final ACA loss in classification is a combination of classification loss and contrast loss.

We compared our method on three benchmark MMP datasets for AC classification against fingerprint-based ML models (MLP, LightGBM, XGBoost, and RF) and the AC-agnostic ACGCN model. The results show that the AC-informed ACGCN model significantly outperforms existing competitive fingerprint-based ML models in AC classification20,21, and also significantly outperforms the AC-agnostic ACGCN43 model across all evaluation metrics on all three benchmark datasets. Specifically, the AC-informed model improves the MCC of the AC-agnostic model from 0.373 to 0.462 on the Melanocortin receptor 4 dataset, from 0.451 to 0.550 on the Mu opioid receptor dataset, and from 0.487 to 0.510 on the Thrombin dataset (Supplementary Table 9). The results show that the ACA concept can be further extended to AC classification in MMP datasets, demonstrating its versatility and robustness in different predictive modeling tasks within cheminformatics.

ACA improves ADMET property prediction

To explore ACA beyond activity prediction, we evaluated ACANET for the prediction of relative changes across ten ADMET properties (Supplementary Table 4). An established approach, DeepDelta45, predicts ADMET property differences by directly training on molecular pairs and their property differences, outperforming both ECFP-based RF and LightGBM models. Another widely used approach, ChemProp47, trains a GNN model using the absolute ADMET property values. Given our method’s significant improvements in predicting absolute activity values, we trained ACANET on absolute property values and inferred the relative changes (delta values) from the predicted absolute property values. We then compared ACANET’s delta values with existing methods.

Similar to activity prediction, we found that the ACA loss hyperparameters cl, cu, and α are crucial for model performance in ADMET property prediction. This is evidenced in the Caco-2 Permeability dataset (Supplementary Fig. 4), underscoring the effectiveness of ACA. Using the same data split and performance measurement method, ACANET demonstrated comparable performance to ChemProp47 and DeepDelta45 on the three absorption datasets, despite the more challenging task of predicting absolute ADMET property values. Moreover, ACANET achieved the lowest or statistically comparable cross-validation RMSE across all seven DMET datasets: two distribution datasets, one excretion dataset, three metabolism datasets, and one toxicity dataset, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 8.

These results demonstrate that directly training on and predicting absolute property values to infer property differences can match or exceed the performance of state-of-the-art methods that train and predict directly on property differences. This success is attributed to the ACA in ACANET, which enables the model to learn relative molecular differences in the latent space while training on absolute values. This approach provides a principled approach compared to models trained on relative differences. Our results indicate that ACA is effective across molecular property predictions and shows competitive performance in indirectly predicting property differences.

Discussion

ACs and molecular property cliffs (PCs) remain among the most persistent obstacles in quantitative SAR modeling19,21,24,44,48. They arise when compounds that appear structurally or functionally related exhibit sharp potency differences that violate the smoothness assumptions underlying classical quantitative SAR and modern GNNs. In practice, “structurally similar” is typically defined by fingerprint Tanimoto/Jaccard scores or scaffold-based rules19,26, yet many ACs occur outside these thresholds and often involve only subtle scaffold-preserving modifications. Such cases reflect the long-recognized phenomenon of SAR nonadditivity, where small substituent changes produce disproportionate potency shifts30–33. These discontinuities are precisely the regions where regression-optimized latent spaces struggle: structurally close yet functionally divergent compounds collapse together, and activity-consistent analogs remain unnecessarily far apart.

To address these limitations, we introduced ACA, a principled inductive bias that explicitly encodes the structure–activity discontinuities characteristic of ACs. Implemented through the ACA loss, the method jointly optimizes the task objective and a triplet-based contrastive geometry. HV-ACTs expose latent-space inconsistencies by pairing an anchor with an activity-similar analog and an activity-different neighbor. The ACA loss reshapes the latent geometry by increasing separation between cliff-forming neighbors while preserving continuity among potency-aligned analogs, providing a mechanistic correction for misleading structural similarity. Because the cliff window (cl, cu) is defined purely by observed activity differences, the model acquires functional awareness rather than a structural bias, avoiding the limitations of fixed similarity thresholds.

A central finding of this study is that standard regression training leaves HV-ACT inconsistencies essentially unchanged—indicating that these challenging regions of SAR space receive little corrective signal. By contrast, ACA steadily reduces HV-ACT violations during learning, demonstrating that the latent space is progressively repaired. This behavior is observed in chemically narrow LSSNS series (e.g., BRAF) and in mixed-scaffold HSSMS datasets (e.g., JAK1), indicating that cliff-aware optimization improves label coherence regardless of scaffold diversity. Complementary analyses using label-incoherence indices further show that AC-aware latent spaces form smoother and more biologically plausible activity manifolds than their AC-agnostic counterparts.

Importantly, the benefits of ACA are not tied to a particular architecture: across four distinct GNN backbones, ACA loss consistently reduces HV-ACTs and improves external performance under matched hyperparameters. Moreover, under a single shared configuration applied to all 30 HSSMS datasets, AC-aware models outperform their non-ACA variants on most targets, demonstrating that the inductive bias is intrinsic rather than architecture- or tuning-dependent. Together, these findings establish cliff-aware contrastive geometry as a broadly applicable mechanism for mitigating discontinuous SAR, rather than as an optimizer- or backbone-specific artifact.

Finally, ACA extends beyond activity prediction. Its formulation naturally generalizes to other property-cliff phenomena—as demonstrated by improvements on MMP cliff classification and ADMET delta prediction—suggesting that the same inductive bias can address discontinuities across diverse endpoints. Future directions include exploring cliff-awareness in multitask settings, generative models, and protein–ligand affinity prediction, as well as investigating property-specific cliff definitions for endpoints such as toxicity, drug-likeness, and selectivity.

Overall, ACA provides a principled and architecture-agnostic strategy for improving molecular representation learning by aligning latent-space geometry with the nonadditive behavior that defines activity cliffs. These results underscore the importance of explicitly modeling structure–activity discontinuities as a general mechanism for enhancing the reliability and interpretability of molecular machine learning.

Methods

Molecular property prediction datasets

We consider a total of 52 datasets, including 9 LSSNS activity regression datasets, 30 HSSMS activity regression datasets, 3 MMP activity cliff classification datasets, and 10 ADMET delta regression datasets (Fig. 2).

The 30 HSSMS benchmark datasets (Supplementary Table 1) were curated by van Tilborg et al.19 using data from ChEMBL49. It serves as a benchmark for exposing the limitations of molecular machine learning with ACs. The HSSMS consists of 30 mixed-scaffold datasets totaling 48,707 molecules. The HSSMS datasets cover a wider range of sample sizes (from 615 to 3657 compounds), scaffold diversity, bioassay types, and target families. Each dataset of HSSMS has been split into train and test sets, and the AC compounds are labeled with a cliff flag for evaluation purposes19. Molecules within the HSSMS are categorized as either “cliff molecules” or not. Specifically, the structural similarity of ACs in HSSMS is determined by three string-based methods: substructure similarity, scaffold similarity, and SMILES similarity. A pair of molecules is considered structurally similar if any of these similarities is greater than 0.9. The activity difference criterion is defined as a 10-fold difference in potency. A molecule is classified as a cliff molecule if it can find at least one molecule in the dataset that satisfies both the structural similarity criterion and has a potency difference greater than 10-fold. To ensure both similar structural and label distributions between the training and test sets, molecules are first spectrally clustered based on their ECFP fingerprints. Within each cluster, an 80/20 split is performed using stratified sampling based on the cliff tag for training and testing, respectively.

The 9 LSSNS datasets (Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1a) contain small molecules with relatively conserved scaffold (similar structures) but varying activity labels, with sample sizes ranging from less than 50 to over 100 compounds (3 sets are less than 50 compounds, three sets are in 50–100, 3 sets are greater than 100 compounds). The LSSNS datasets were collected from fragment-to-lead medicinal chemistry publications in 201940 and the ChEMBL49 database. These datasets represent a real-world scenario of a series of conservative compounds developed by a single laboratory or institution (Supplementary Table 2), and we use them to test the ACA loss in the low-sample-size and narrow scaffold regimes.

The 3 MMP benchmark datasets (Supplementary Table 3) were curated by Park et al.43 for AC classification, focusing on the targets thrombin, Mu opioid receptor, and melanocortin receptor 4. The binary AC labels of MMPs are defined as MMP-cliff if ∆pKi ≥ 2, and MMP-nonCliff (non-AC) if ∆pKi ≤ 1.

The 10 ADMET benchmark datasets (Supplementary Table 4) were collected and preprocessed by Fralish et al.45 for delta prediction of molecular ADMET properties. These datasets include 3 for absorption properties (solubility, Caco2 permeability, and free solvation), 2 for distribution properties (fraction unbound in brain, volume of distribution at steady state), 1 for an excretion property (renal clearance), 3 for metabolism properties (microsomal clearance, hepatic clearance, and half-life), and 1 for a hemolytic toxicity property. The structures were sanitized, and the property labels were log-transformed except for the free solvation dataset45.

Activity cliffs in chemical and latent spaces

Molecular ACs are formed by pairs of structurally similar compounds that are active against the same bio-target but have large differences in potency19,27. Importantly, two aspects complicate the consistent assessment of ACs in computational and medicinal chemistry: the definition of “similarity” and the quantification of “large” potency differences27.

Conventionally, ECFPs are used to measure structural Tanimoto similarity between compounds in chemical space26,51. However, similarity can also be assessed in latent space through molecular representations learned by GNN models using graph convolutions. Ideally, unlike the original chemical space, the presence of ACs should be significantly reduced in the latent space of molecular representations. This is because the molecular representation in the latent space should align with the activity labels of the molecules, as illustrated in Fig. 3a, allowing for better predictions. Therefore, optimizing the similarity between compounds in the latent space is a crucial step in addressing the molecular AC challenges in deep learning. Nevertheless, ECFPs suffer from inherent limitations. For example, the use of manually defined Tanimoto thresholds may overlook many compound pairs that contain valuable quantitative SAR information. And other commonly used strategies, such as scaffold-based comparison and SMILES similarity, are also unable to fully capture all relevant types of structural similarity (Supplementary Fig. 21). Moreover, since these traditional methods remain fixed throughout the entire training process, they cannot distinguish between the most challenging AC samples for the current quantitative SAR model and those that have already been well learned, which may lead to overfitting. By contrast, GNNs can capture structural information dynamically through neighborhood aggregation. As a result, latent-space distances derived from GNN representations have been widely adopted as a measure of molecular structural similarity, including in MMP analysis43. In this study, we employed two approaches to determine structural similarity: (1) directly using the latent-space distance of GNNs, and (2) introducing an additional gating mechanism that incorporates three traditional structural similarity measures (see Supplementary Note 1). In the latter case, compounds are considered structurally similar only if both the latent distance and the traditional similarity measures meet the similarity criterion.

Furthermore, the magnitude of the bioactivity difference that defines an AC is subjective and can vary depending on the target and the assay used for measuring activity. Different studies may have different thresholds, such as a 10-fold19 or 100-fold27 difference (i.e., reported Ki, IC50, or EC50 values) in bioactivity to define AC pairs. However, determining ACs requires careful consideration of dataset characteristics, such as the chemical series being studied and the distribution of bioactivity values. In this study, we do not adopt a single fixed cutoff to define ACs. Instead, we introduce two tunable hyperparameters, cl and cu, which specify the activity-difference range for identifying label-conditioned ACTs. The total number of such triplets is denoted as M. This approach allows for more flexibility in defining ACs and can be tailored to the specific dataset being analyzed. More details about the ACTs mining by cliff parameters can be found in the section on online mining of the ACTs.

Online mining of the activity cliff triplets

In this study, we employed online mining of ACTs for model contrastive learning in the latent space. Online ACT mining offers computational efficiency and scalability compared to offline mining, as it dynamically selects ACTs in each training batch based on the model’s current state52. During the mining process, we identify a positive compound and a negative compound for each anchor compound to form an ACT. The positive compound has an activity value close to the anchor, while the negative compound exhibits significantly different activity. To facilitate real-time conditional ACT sampling in each batch, we introduce cl and cu as cliff cut-off hyperparameters (Fig. 1b). By comparing the activity difference between compounds and the anchor, we categorize compounds as positive examples if the difference is within the cl threshold and as negative examples if the difference exceeds the cu threshold. When cl is equal to cu, the triplets are obtained using a single “cliff” hyperparameter, maximizing the number of ACTs acquired. It is important to note that cl should be smaller than cu, and leaving a suitable margin ensures the obtained triplets are reasonable.

To obtain HV-ACTs in each batch, only an ACT with its triplet loss greater than zero is counted (Fig. 1b). In this setting, anchor–Negative (A-N) pairs are typically close enough in the latent space, that is, structurally similar, but at the same time differ greatly in activity, representing ACs that need to be pushed apart, whereas anchor–Positive (A-P) pairs are functionally consistent and should be pulled closer. This ensures that the selected triplets are informative and contribute effectively to the model’s learning. The number of mined HV-ACTs (denoted as M′) reflects the degree of ACs in the current stage of molecular representations. If the model is already AC-aware during training, the number of mined HV triplets should gradually decrease as training progresses. This reduction occurs because the ACA of the model optimizes the latent feature space to reduce prediction errors. Gradually decreasing the number of mined HV-ACTs helps the model focus on refining the latent feature space for compounds that are more challenging to predict accurately.

In addition to the latent-only ACA strategy, we also implemented an ACA variant with conventional structure-based gating. In the original ACA procedure, candidate ACTs are generated according to activity-difference thresholds. For each anchor molecule, molecules with activity differences below the lower cliff threshold are assigned as positives, whereas molecules with activity differences above the upper cliff threshold are assigned as negatives. These candidate triplets are further evaluated using the current GNN latent-space distances, allowing high-value ACTs to be dynamically mined during training and used to optimize the ACA loss. In the gated ACA variant, we introduced an additional fixed filtering step based on conventional string-based structural similarity measures. For each candidate, ACT, fingerprint-, scaffold-, and SMILES-based similarities were calculated between the anchor and the putative cliff molecule. A candidate ACT was retained only if the corresponding structural similarity satisfied the predefined threshold; otherwise, it was removed before ACA-loss computation (Supplementary Fig. 10, and Supplementary Note 1). We compared latent-only ACA, gated ACA, and the non-ACA baseline across the 30 HSSMS datasets. Both ACA variants outperformed the non-ACA baseline, whereas latent-only ACA achieved the best overall performance (Supplementary Fig. 11). We further varied the structural threshold from 0.9 to 0.3 and found that relaxing this constraint increased the number of mined triplets and improved performance (Supplementary Fig. 12), indicating that strict fixed structural gates may discard informative HV-ACTs required for learning discontinuous SAR patterns.

Activity cliff awareness loss function

Overall definition of ACA loss in regression task: The ACA loss LACA is a combination of the regression loss (e.g., mean absolute error MAE loss) and the triplet contrastive loss (Fig. 1a). Specifically, in each batch, the MAE loss LMAE was computed as the mean absolute difference between the predicted and actual activity values for the compounds in the batch. The triplet contrastive loss LTSM was then computed based on HV-ACTs of compounds within the batch, where a triplet consists of an anchor compound, a positive compound with similar activity (controlled by parameter cl), and a negative compound with dissimilar activity (controlled by parameter cu). The triplet contrastive loss encourages the model to learn representations that distinguish between the positive and negative compounds while also maintaining proximity between the query and positive compounds. ACANET model architecture and training

We investigated four widely-used GNN backbones in the ACANET: GCN53, GIN54, GAT55, and PNA56. PNA was selected as the default backbone due to its reported superior performance in related tasks56. The message passing settings for these convolutional layers were tailored to each backbone’s architecture. For the GCN backbone, a mean aggregation approach was used to combine information from neighboring nodes. In the GAT backbone, a multi-head attention mechanism was employed, where outputs from multiple attention heads were averaged to form the final node representation. For the GIN layers, a trainable parameter ϵ was used to adjust the weight of the central node’s feature during aggregation, allowing for flexibility in balancing the influence of neighboring nodes. In the PNA backbone, multiple aggregators (“mean”, “min”, “max”, “sum”, “std”) and scalers (“identity”, “amplification”, “attenuation”) were employed to extract node features from various neighborhoods. All these GNN convolutional layers were implemented using the PyTorch Geometric57 library, ensuring efficient computation and integration within the ACANET model.

The inputs to ACANET are small molecules with graph representations (Fig. 1a), which are featurized using the 39 node features and 10 edge features (see Supplementary Table 5). The graph convolutional layers are with channel sizes of (64, 128, 256, 512). The dense layers followed a pyramid-shaped structure with 256, 128, and 32 neurons, respectively, allowing the network to learn complex representations in the early layers and progressively reduce the dimensionality towards the output layer. The default ACANET configuration did not include dropout, while Batch Normalization was applied after each graph convolutional layer to stabilize the learning process. A Global Max Pooling (GMP) layer was employed to retain the most important features from the graph, selecting the maximum value in each feature map channel (Fig. 1a).

The outputs of ACANET included the predicted activity labels and the latent vectors derived from the GMP layer, which were used for the distance calculation of ACTs as they encapsulate the most relevant information from the molecular graph. For training ACANET, a learning rate of 10−4 was fixed, and the batch size was set to 128 for all datasets. The batch size is particularly important in ACANET, as a larger batch size enables more effective sampling of HV-ACTs. An early stopping mechanism was implemented to select the best model based on the ACA loss value calculated from the monitor dataset, ensuring optimal model performance.

ACANET with and without ACA across different GNNs

To verify the concept of ACA and test the effect of ACA loss, we analyzed ACANET with and without ACA across four different backbones (GCN, GIN, GAT, and PNA). A medium-sized dataset PPARδ (n = 1125) from the HSSMS collection, was used, and it was split into training, validation, and test sets in a proportion of 6:2:2. The ACANET models with ACA (MAE loss + TSM loss) and without ACA (MAE loss only) used the exact same hyperparameters except for the loss function. For the hyperparameters in the ACA loss, the awareness factor α and cliff parameters were all set to 1, following the default settings. We recorded the training history of MAE loss, the number of mined HV-ACTs (M') during training, and the RMSE performance of the validation and test sets over all training epochs. To improve the robustness of the results, we repeated the data splits 10 times with different random seeds, resulting in 10 repetitions of the PoC experiments on these different splits. The final results for PNA-based ACANET are shown in Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 9, with shaded areas representing the standard deviation from 10 repetitions with different random seeds in the data splits. The impact of ACA across the four different GNN backbones is illustrated in Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 19, which shows the number of mined HV-ACTs (M') for the training, validation, and test sets during training, as well as the final test RMSE performance. The best model was selected based on the validation set RMSE (Supplementary Table 6). We also mitigated hyperparameter confounding by training each backbone on the full HSSMS with a single fixed setup: all ACA hyperparameters were kept at default (cu = 1, cl = 1, α = 1). Under this shared configuration, we compared ACA and non-ACA on all 30 HSSMS datasets. Supplementary Fig. 24 shows per-dataset test-error deltas, with ACA lowering RMSE on most datasets.

Ablation studies on the ACA loss

Ablation studies were conducted to investigate the impact of the ACA factor α and cliff hyperparameters in the ACA loss on PNA-based ACANET performance using the BRAF dataset from LSSNS with a stratified five-fold cross-validation repeated at least three times (Fig. 5).

In the first study, the cl and cu parameters were varied while keeping α fixed at 1. The range of values for cl and cu was 0 to 4 with a step size of 0.1, ensuring that cl was less than or equal to cu. This study evaluated the effect of these changes on the number of mined label-based conditional ACTs (M) and the RMSE performance of ACANET. The validation RMSE was averaged from five-fold cross-validation. The cliff parameters control the number of mined label-based conditional ACTs, which are crucial for predicting molecular activity. The evaluation on the other four LSSNS datasets (PLK1, IDO1, USP7, and RIP2) is shown in Supplementary Fig. 2.

The second study focused on the ACA factor α in the ACA loss. Here, the cl and cu parameters were fixed, and α was varied from 0.00 (no ACA) to 0.01, 0.05, 0.10, 0.20, 0.50, and 1.00. This study evaluated the historical performance of ACANET over at least 800 training epochs under different α values using the BRAF dataset from LSSNS with five-fold cross-validation repeated at least three times. The results provide insights into the impact of the ACA factor α on ACANET performance and can guide the selection of appropriate values for this parameter in future applications.

Label incoherence in chemical and latent spaces

We conducted both qualitative and quantitative analyses on a narrow scaffold BRAF dataset to examine the discontinuity (or cliff index) of compound activity distribution in the original chemical space and the latent space learned by our model.

Qualitative analysis: In the original chemical space, we computed ECFP4 fingerprints (1024-bit) to measure the Tanimoto distance between molecules and used TMAP58 to visualize the chemical space in 2D (Fig. 6a). For the latent space, we performed PCA to obtain 2D embeddings using the learned 512-dimensional feature vectors from the model, both with and without ACA-awareness (Fig. 6b, c). Benchmarking ACANET on LSSNS datasets

To evaluate the effectiveness of AC-awareness on molecular activity prediction, we conducted a benchmark study on the LSSNS collection using both MAE and MSE as the regression loss (Supplementary Table 7). We trained two versions of the same model: one with ACA loss and the other without. For the model with the ACA loss, we tuned the cliff parameters and the ACA factor a. Initially, we fixed the awareness factor a at its default value of 0.1 and used a “T-shape” grid search strategy to find the optimal values of cl and cu. Specifically, we kept the cliff lower fixed at 0.1 and varied the cliff upper from 0.5 to 4.0 with a step size of 0.5. Then, we used the best cliff upper value and varied the cliff lower from 0.1 to the best cliff upper value in 8 steps. After determining the optimal cliff lower and cliff upper parameters, we tuned a by performing stratified five-fold cross-validation on the training set with a range of [0.0, 0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0]. The best a value was chosen based on cross-validation performance. In contrast, for the model without ACA loss, we set a to 0.0. All models were trained for 800 epochs using the same batch size (128) and learning rate (10−4), and the best parameters of cl, cu, and a were used in the model with ACA. We selected the best RMSE value on the validation set of a stratified five-fold cross-validation and repeated the process ten times to ensure the stability of the results. We reported the average RMSE and the standard deviation performance for each model. To assess the significance of the performance improvement of the model with ACA loss, we used paired t-tests. The levels of statistical significance are indicated as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001. Results not meeting these thresholds are denoted as “n.s.” for not significant.

Benchmarking ACANET on HSSMS datasets

We assess the performance of ACANET on the HSSMS benchmark datasets. These datasets serve as a representative set of mixed-scaffold datasets for exposing the limitations of molecular machine learning with ACs19.To compare the benchmark results with those obtained using off-the-shelf machine learning models for molecular activity prediction, we compared ACA-equipped ACANET with the following models: the graph-based GAT, GCN, AFP, and MPNN methods (without ACA); the SMILES-based Transformer, CNN, and LSTM; and ECFP4 fingerprint-based MLP, KNN, GBM, RF, and SVM. The statistical significance of the performance difference between ACANET with ACA and the off-the-shelf models was determined using the paired t-test with a significance level of 0.05. We further compared ACA with two classical quantitative SAR baselines, namely a scaffold-based Free–Wilson model and a descriptor-based PLS regressor (Supplementary Note 2). The Free–Wilson comparison was restricted to the dominant Bemis–Murcko scaffold subset, consistent with its single-scaffold assumption, whereas PLS was evaluated on the full HSSMS datasets using standard molecular descriptors. ACA consistently outperformed both baselines, supporting its advantage over both classical SAR and modern machine learning approaches. Additionally, we performed a paired t-test to assess the statistical significance of the performance improvement of ACANET with ACA compared to ACANET without ACA.

To train ACANET on all datasets, we used a batch size of 128 and a learning rate of 10−4. For hyperparameter optimization in ACA loss, we followed these steps: First, we fixed the awareness factor a at its default value of 0.1 and used a “T-shape” grid search strategy to find the optimal values of cliff lower and cliff upper (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Specifically, we kept the cliff lower fixed at 0.1 and varied the cliff upper from 0.5 to 4.0 with a step size of 0.5. Then, we used the best cliff upper value and varied the cliff lower from 0.1 to the best cliff upper value in 8 steps. After determining the optimal cliff lower and cliff upper parameters, we tuned a by performing stratified five-fold cross-validation on the training set with a range of [0., 0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1.] (Supplementary Fig. 3b). The best a value was chosen based on training set cross-validation performance (Supplementary Fig. 5), the training set cross-validation performance of ACANET model with and without ACA is shown in Supplementary Table 8. We set all other parameters in ACA loss, such as squared (squared = False) and p-norm (p = 2), to their default values. During the final training stage of ACANET, we employed early stopping based on the validation set in a nested stratified five-fold cross-validation to select the best model. To predict on the external test set, we averaged the predictions from the five sub-models from the nested cross-validation. The performance was evaluated using the RMSE metric on the overall test set (RMSEall) and cliff-specific test set (RMSEcliff) as used by van Tilborg et al.19. The results are shown in Supplementary Fig. 6. We adopted an as-fair-as-possible strategy by conducting hyperparameter searches within comparable ranges to enable fair comparisons with other baselines (Supplementary Table 10).

To further assess the generalizability of ACA, we evaluated ACANET under more stringent data-splitting strategies on the HSSMS collection (see Supplementary Note 3). First, in a Bemis–Murcko scaffold–held-out split (20% scaffolds assigned to the test set), ACA improved performance over the non-ACA baseline on all 30 targets. Second, we constructed an even more challenging independent test split by removing any molecule whose fingerprint, scaffold, or SMILES similarity to any training compound exceeded 0.90, producing a cliff-rich test set (mean 76.8% cliffs). Under this stringent setting, ACA achieved lower RMSE on 28 of 30 targets, demonstrating that ACA generalizes to unseen cliffs and remains beneficial even when all relevant cliffs occur exclusively at test time.

Benchmarking ACANET on ADMET datasets

To further evaluate our method for predicting other molecular properties such as ADMET, we tested ACANET on the 10 ADMET benchmark datasets (Supplementary Table 4) for property delta prediction. As molecular property delta (the property difference between two molecules) prediction can assess model performance on “property cliffs”, we compared the model performance with established methods such as ECFP-based RF and LightGBM ML models, and the GNN-based Chem Prop and Deep Delta models for ADMET property differences prediction. To predict the delta values using our method, we trained the ACANET model on the absolute ADMET properties and inferred the property differences on the test set by predicting the absolute values. During training, the ACA loss hyperparameters cl, cu, and α were optimized using the same strategy as HSSMS benchmarking. An example of hyperparameter search for the Caco-2 Permeability dataset is shown in Supplementary Fig. 4. Using the same data split method and delta prediction evaluation as the established methods, the final model performance of delta RMSE from the 5 × 10-fold cross-validation results is shown in Supplementary Fig. 8.

Extending the ACA for activity cliff classification

In addition to quantitative SAR, predicting pairs of AC molecules is another important challenge related to AC. This task involves classifying whether a pair of similar compounds forms an AC or not6,36,43. In this section, we extend ACA to AC classification in matched molecular pairs.

An AC pair can be represented by an MMP, where the two molecules share the same core structure but have different substituents. Following Park et al.43 and Tamura et al.44, molecule pairs were decomposed using the algorithm by Hussain et al.59, with each fragment treated as a subgraph. The representation of the molecule is the concatenation of its core and substituent representations encoded by GNN. Most deep learning methods currently use cross-entropy loss for AC prediction, without explicitly emphasizing the substituents that contain rich SAR knowledge. To develop ACA for classification tasks, these AC pairs have already been pre-provided and do not need to be constructed in the batch. We use a simple yet effective contrastive learning strategy to push the features of ACs apart and bring the features of non-ACs closer.
