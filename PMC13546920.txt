Routine Dysmenorrhea Screening in Adolescents: Recommendations for Timely Diagnosis and Treatment of Endometriosis
Authors: Sarah Simko, Janet Cruz, Mallory Stuparich
Published: 2026

Kiara is a 12-year-old girl with dysmenorrhea. She has had severe cramping during her period since menarche. During her periods, she has a hard time focusing at school, occasionally staying home due to the pain. She learns to self-manage her pain with nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and heating pads. After persistent pain for years, she is started on oral contraceptive pills (OCPs), but the pain worsens. She presents to the emergency room on multiple occasions for her pain without relief. When she is 18 years old, she is diagnosed with appendicitis and endometriotic lesions are noted at the time of the appendectomy. She is referred to an obstetrician-gynecologist and she is trialed on a levonorgestrel intrauterine device, a subdermal implant, and then a gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonist with minimal improvement in her symptoms. She now reports chronic pelvic pain in addition to her menstrual cramping, as well as dyschezia, dyspareunia, and dysuria.

At 21 years of age, she is diagnosed with stage IV endometriosis and undergoes a laparoscopic ablation for endometriosis by an obstetrician/gynecologist. Over the next 5 years, she undergoes 2 additional ablation procedures for endometriosis and her left ovary is removed due to the presence of a left endometrioma. She now presents at age 26 with chronic pelvic pain and multiple concurrent pain disorders, seeking other options for addressing her pain.

Kiara's case reflects a pattern seen in many patients with endometriosis: early symptoms that were initially missed and later classified as “normal period cramping,” leading to delayed treatment and diagnosis. Although management with NSAIDs and OCPs or other hormonal methods is a recommended initial treatment option for dysmenorrhea, her management was not reassessed when her pain persisted, leading to delayed referral, delayed diagnosis, and escalation of disease severity.

Her case demonstrates the importance of screening all adolescents with female reproductive organs for dysmenorrhea and earlier recognition of clinical features that should raise concern for secondary causes of dysmenorrhea. Early evaluation by providers with an awareness of and comfort in diagnosing endometriosis in adolescents and subspecialty referral to a pediatric and adolescent gynecologist, adolescent medicine specialist, or gynecologist with experience in the treatment of endometriosis is key for patients who do not improve with medical management or have clinical features suggestive of endometriosis. In this article, we aimed to discuss how practicing physicians can best assess, treat, and refer adolescent patients with dysmenorrhea and to briefly review options to manage symptoms as patients wait for a referral.

Pediatric clinicians are often the first—and sometimes only—healthcare providers adolescents see during the onset of menstruation. Despite this opportunity, menstrual health is inconsistently addressed in pediatric practice. A survey in 2020 of primary care pediatricians in the US reported that 33% did not discuss menstruation with postmenarchal patients.1 In a review of electronic health records of adolescents aged 12-21 years, <5% of family physicians and 10% of pediatricians obtained history regarding the patient's last menstrual period, length of cycle, or presence of associated symptoms.2 This screening is key because menstrual stigmatization and menstrual disparities in our society are still widely prevalent. For example, 1 in 4 teens do not have adequate access to menstrual products and many lack access to menstrual education.3

The stigmatization surrounding menstruation can make it difficult for adolescents to bring up these topics during their visits. Discussing menstruation can be embarrassing for adolescents to initiate, and many lack adequate information on what is normal. In a survey of menstruating students between ages 13 and 19, 74% of students had questions about their periods and 57% reported that periods were not openly discussed in schools.4 Many teens feel unprepared for menstruation and report negative attitudes surrounding their menstrual cycle.4 Routine, provider-initiated discussions about menstruation could normalize these conversations and identify concerning symptoms with hopes of reducing delays in care.

Menstrual stigma, as well as normalization of pain and dismissal from healthcare providers, can contribute to delays in the diagnosis and treatment of pelvic pain conditions.5 Primary dysmenorrhea is defined as painful menstruation in the absence of any underlying pelvic pathology that is generally characterized by cramping pain before or during a period.6 Secondary dysmenorrhea is painful menses secondary to a medical condition, such as endometriosis, fibroids, pelvic inflammatory disease, pelvic masses, congenital abnormalities of the uterus, vaginismus, or other pelvic floor disorders.6 Approximately 70% of adolescents report discomfort associated with menstruation with ≤20%-40% missing school due to menstrual pain.7 Menstrual pain has also been associated with decreased academic performance, sports participation, and socialization with peers.8 Many teens with persistent and severe pain may have secondary dysmenorrhea from endometriosis. Approximately 67% of adolescents with dysmenorrhea unresponsive to hormonal therapies and NSAIDs are diagnosed with endometriosis at the time of diagnostic laparoscopy.6

Endometriosis affects approximately 10%-15% of women of reproductive age and 70% of women with chronic pelvic pain.9 On average, patients experience a delay of approximately 7 years between symptom onset and diagnosis, but early screening and referral as well as prompt treatment could help to mitigate pain and prevent disease progression. Longer delays in diagnosis have been identified in Hispanic and Black patients, as well as patients with higher body mass index and older age.10 Additionally, Black patients are less likely to be diagnosed with endometriosis compared with White patients, but further clarity on whether there is a lower prevalence of endometriosis among Black women is needed.11 Implicit biases and discrimination can influence diagnostic and management strategies that can impact time to care for patients.11,12 Increased delays in care for patients with endometriosis have been shown to be associated with increased rates of infertility, comorbid pain conditions, and increased costs associated with healthcare utilization.13 In a retrospective database study, patients with a longer diagnostic delay were noted to have higher rates of infertility and 2 times the endometriosis-related healthcare costs compared with those in the shorter diagnostic delay group.13 Women with a delayed diagnosis of endometriosis have also been found to require increased cycles of artificial reproductive technology to achieve a pregnancy and have lower birth rates.14 Additionally, studies show that patients with endometriosis have higher rates of premature menopause, which could put them at risk of long-term health consequences, such as osteoporosis and cardiovascular disease.15, 16, 17

Moreover, the impacts of dysmenorrhea and chronic pelvic pain, as well as the chronic dismissal of symptoms that patients often experience, can severely impact patients’ quality of life and mental health. In a review of patients with endometriosis <25 years of age, those with endometriosis had lower quality-of-life and mental health scores compared with adolescents without endometriosis.18 Endometriosis has been associated with increased rates of depression and anxiety.13,19 Many patients with chronic pelvic pain and endometriosis feel dismissed by the healthcare system, feeling a sense of validation when they finally receive a diagnosis.20,21 Quality of life can be significantly improved with treatment of endometriosis; therefore, helping patients to access specialized care earlier is essential.22

Finally, as our understanding of central sensitization expands, there are increasing concerns regarding the correlation of dysmenorrhea with hyperalgesia. Central sensitization is the abnormal augmentation of pain by mechanisms within the central nervous system due to increased responsiveness of neurons to sensory input, leading to increased pain signals.23 Multiple studies have found that women with dysmenorrhea had greater sensitivity to pain compared with those without dysmenorrhea.23,24 A prospective study found that patients with dysmenorrhea are more likely to develop chronic pain disorders.25 Although further research is required to evaluate whether early treatment of dysmenorrhea can decrease these risks, screening for dysmenorrhea may allow us to identify patients who are at risk of comorbid pain conditions.

Both the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) and the American Academy of Pediatrics recommend asking adolescents about their menstrual cycle at every preventive care visit.26,27 This provides an opportunity to assess for a variety of menstrual concerns, including abnormal uterine bleeding, dysmenorrhea, primary amenorrhea, and access to menstrual products, among others. Integrating a menstrual history into the review of systems or into a pre-appointment questionnaire can be a time-efficient method to obtain this information. If a patient reports menstrual pain, asking the patient how the pain affects their day-to-day activities can help to elucidate the severity of the pain. Although more time intensive, validated questionnaires can be a useful tool to help identify patients who require treatment or referral. A recent systematic review evaluated the available instruments to identify menstrual complaints in adolescents; the Period ImPact and Pain Assessment and the Adolescent Menstrual Bleeding Questionnaire were identified as the most suitable because they were validated in adolescent populations and took into account the impact of these symptoms on teen's quality of life.29 The Period ImPact and Pain Assessment is a 5-question survey that was validated in a large cohort of >1000 adolescents that assists with differentiating between mild period pain and severe dysmenorrhea.28 Emphasizing a gynecologic history in the teen visit and learning how to assess the severity of patients' symptoms could help to identify patients with secondary dysmenorrhea sooner and help patients with primary dysmenorrhea gain access to treatment.

Although the majority of adolescents with dysmenorrhea have primary dysmenorrhea, symptoms that are more suggestive of secondary dysmenorrhea include severe dysmenorrhea immediately after menarche or progressively worsening symptoms, abnormal uterine bleeding, pain outside of menses, dysuria, dyschezia, dyspareunia, or a family history of endometriosis.6 A study of 304 patients aged <22 years with biopsy-confirmed endometriosis showed that the most common presenting symptoms are progressive dysmenorrhea, heavy menstrual bleeding, and functional impairment, with 55% missing school or other activities due to the pain.30 If secondary dysmenorrhea is suspected, a workup should be initiated with a physical examination and pelvic ultrasound examination. Bimanual pelvic examination findings may indicate signs of endometriosis and help to rule out other potential etiologies, such as pelvic masses and reproductive tract abnormalities; however, a normal examination does not rule out endometriosis. In adolescent patients, shared decision-making should be emphasized, given that these examinations may be emotionally distressing for patients, and early-stage endometriosis is unlikely to be identified on physical examination.11,31,32 A speculum examination can often be omitted. Similarly, if a transvaginal ultrasound examination is not tolerable for the patient, an abdominal ultrasound examination can be performed to assess for adnexal masses and other structural sources of pain.11 Testing for sexually transmitted infections should also be considered based on the clinical history. A negative ultrasound examination does not rule out endometriosis, and if there is suspicion for secondary dysmenorrhea, a referral should be offered, so that the risks and benefits of medical vs surgical management can be discussed. Recent ACOG guidelines recommend the clinical diagnosis of endometriosis in patients who present symptoms and/or physical examination findings that are suggestive of endometriosis.11 Additionally, the ACOG recommends empiric treatment of primary dysmenorrhea with OCPs; however, adolescents with persistent pain despite treatment for 3-6 months have a higher risk of endometriosis and should be referred to a specialist.33 For patients with suspected endometriosis who are awaiting referral, suppressive hormonal therapy, such as continuous OCPs, progesterone agents, or a levonorgestrel intrauterine device can help to mitigate symptoms.6

For patients who desire to proceed with surgical management for diagnosis and treatment, identifying physicians with experience managing endometriosis and chronic pelvic pain is beneficial to the patient. Given the complexity and range of disease extent of endometriosis, not all obstetrician-gynecologists feel equipped or adequately trained to surgically manage endometriosis or coordinate an interdisciplinary care team for patients who develop comorbid pain conditions. Surgical management options for treating endometriosis include laparoscopic ablation of endometriotic lesions vs laparoscopic excision of endometriosis. Hysterectomy with or without oophorectomy is often considered in adults; however, they should be rarely considered in an adolescent population with ovarian-sparing methods, such as ovarian cystectomy, being the preferred management option for endometriomas.32 Laparoscopic ablation of endometriosis involves looking in the abdomen and burning the surface of superficial lesions, whereas an excision of endometriosis involves dissecting around the lesions with removal and pathological evaluation. In adolescents with superficial, early-stage disease, there are limited studies evaluating long-term outcomes comparing symptom and lesion recurrence for those who undergo laparoscopic ablation vs excision; however, short-term symptom outcomes are similar.32 For patients with deep endometriosis with later stage disease, evidence suggests that excision of endometriosis followed by menstrual suppression can improve dysmenorrhea symptoms and decrease the risk of recurrence.34,35 Importantly, although surgical management can provide diagnosis and treatment for patients, pain symptoms can persist and endometriosis can recur; therefore, follow-up care and consideration for postoperative hormonal suppression for these patients is important.35

Kiara underwent an excision surgery for endometriosis with significant improvement in her pain, and she was placed on menstrual suppression following the surgery. She was referred to a pelvic floor physical therapist, urogynecologist, gastroenterologist, and pain management specialist to provide her with a multidisciplinary approach to her pain. Although chronic pelvic pain can be a lifelong battle for some, screening for and identifying teens with dysmenorrhea can help patients like Kiara gain access to earlier diagnosis and treatment to help avoid years of poor quality of life and to help provide community and support.

Pediatric clinicians play a critical role in identifying adolescents with dysmenorrhea who require further evaluation. Treating the pain of these adolescents will take a multidisciplinary effort. Addressing menstrual pain early, reassessing treatment response, and resisting normalization of pelvic pain are essential steps toward improving long-term outcomes for patients like Kiara.
