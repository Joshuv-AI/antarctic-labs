Efficient and real-time polyp segmentation: An empirical evaluation of lightweight Mamba integration strategies

Si Chen, Mohd Shafry Mohd Rahim, Hiew Moi Sim, Yee Yong Pang, Pei Kian Teo, Ei Lin Ong, Wei Jer Lim

ABSTRACT
Accurate and efficient polyp segmentation is essential for computer-aided colorectal cancer screening, yet achieving a favorable balance among segmentation accuracy, computational efficiency, and cross-dataset generalization remains challenging. Recent studies suggest that Mamba-based state-space modeling can enhance long-range dependency modeling with relatively low complexity, but how lightweight Mamba integration strategies affect dense prediction frameworks for polyp segmentation remains insufficiently understood. In this study, we present LiteMamba-Seg, a lightweight polyp segmentation framework built on a U-Net-like encoder–decoder architecture with a ResNet34 backbone, and conduct an empirical evaluation of different lightweight Mamba integration strategies. Specifically, we investigate bottleneck-only integration as well as alternative second-stage placements at an encoder-side intermediate stage and a decoder-side reconstruction stage. Experimental results on four public benchmark datasets show that LiteMamba-Seg achieves competitive segmentation performance while maintaining a lightweight design and real-time model-level inference, with 26.81 M parameters, 13.89 GFLOPs, and 167.73 FPS. Further ablation, controlled placement, multi-seed, robustness, boundary, calibration, and false-positive analyses indicate that the effect of Mamba integration depends on its placement within the network. In particular, decoder-side reconstruction-stage integration yields more favorable quantitative and qualitative results than encoder-side intermediate placement across the evaluated benchmarks. These findings suggest that, for lightweight polyp segmentation frameworks, Mamba integration should be considered jointly with placement strategy rather than simply increasing the number of sequence modeling blocks.

Introduction Colorectal cancer remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide. Since most colorectal cancers arise from precancerous adenomatous polyps, accurate detection and delineation of polyps during colonoscopy are essential for early intervention and improved clinical outcomes. In recent years, computer-aided detection systems have shown substantial clinical potential in assisting endoscopists during routine examinations and improving lesion detection rates [ 1 , 2 ] Among related tasks, polyp segmentation is particularly important because it provides pixel-level localization of lesion extent, which is valuable for lesion boundary assessment, size estimation, lesion monitoring, and subsequent clinical decision-making [ 3 ]. Despite substantial progress, automatic polyp segmentation remains challenging in real-world colonoscopy scenarios. Polyps vary considerably in size, shape, texture, and appearance, and difficult cases often involve small lesions, low contrast against surrounding mucosa, irregular morphology, or blurry boundaries [ 4 ]. In addition, specular highlights, motion blur, intestinal contents, and illumination variation can further obscure lesion regions and degrade prediction quality [ 5 ]. These challenges become more pronounced when models are evaluated on unseen datasets collected under different imaging conditions, where domain shift often leads to a clear drop in segmentation performance [ 6 ]. Therefore, an effective polyp segmentation framework should not only achieve high accuracy on in-domain distributions, but also maintain reliable performance across different domains. Existing polyp segmentation frameworks have achieved encouraging progress, yet balancing segmentation accuracy, computational efficiency, and cross-domain robustness remains a persistent challenge. In practical deployment, a segmentation framework should be not only accurate, but also lightweight, efficient, and reliable under unseen imaging conditions. In the broader deep learning literature, lightweight architecture design has been widely explored to improve deployment efficiency, as exemplified by models such as MobileNetV3 [ 7 ] and GhostNet [ 8 ]. More recently, selective state space models (SSMs), especially Mamba and its medical variants, have emerged as an efficient alternative for long-range dependency modeling, and recent review studies have highlighted their growing role in medical image analysis [ 9 ]. Representative frameworks such as U-Mamba [ 10 ] further show how state-space modeling can be incorporated into biomedical dense prediction pipelines. Built on these developments, subsequent lightweight Mamba-based designs have continued to explore parameter-efficient segmentation mechanisms [ 11 – 13 ]. However, how such state-space capability should be integrated into a lightweight hierarchical pipeline remains insufficiently studied for polyp segmentation. In particular, the effect of lightweight Mamba block placement, especially whether such modules are more effective at encoder stages or decoder reconstruction stages, has not been sufficiently investigated. Motivated by these considerations, we develop LiteMamba-Seg, a lightweight segmentation framework for efficient polyp segmentation with an emphasis on cross-dataset evaluation. Built on a U-Net-like encoder–decoder architecture with a ResNet34 backbone, LiteMamba-Seg introduces Mamba-based contextual modeling in a selective rather than densely stacked manner, aiming to enhance semantic representation while keeping additional complexity limited. More importantly, instead of treating Mamba as a plug-in component that can be inserted arbitrarily, we investigate how different lightweight Mamba integration strategies affect segmentation performance, computational efficiency, and cross-dataset generalization. This framework serves not only as an efficient segmentation model, but also as a practical basis for studying how lightweight contextual modeling should be integrated for dense prediction. The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows: We develop LiteMamba-Seg, a lightweight segmentation framework for efficient polyp segmentation. We conduct an empirical evaluation of lightweight Mamba integration strategies and analyze how different component choices and placement schemes affect segmentation behavior. We validate the proposed framework on four public benchmark datasets and demonstrate competitive performance under both standard in-domain and cross-dataset evaluation settings. We conduct detailed analyses on computational efficiency, component behavior, Mamba placement, multi-seed stability, input perturbation robustness, boundary quality, calibration, and false-positive behavior to examine the practicality and reproducibility of the proposed design. Related work Recent advances in polyp segmentation can be broadly categorized into CNN-based architectures, Transformer-based models, and, more recently, Mamba-enhanced designs. In this section, we briefly review these representative directions and highlight the remaining research gap that motivates the present study. CNN-based polyp segmentation Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have long been widely used in medical image segmentation because of their strong local representation ability and favorable computational efficiency. Classical encoder–decoder architectures such as U-Net [ 14 ] and UNet++ [ 15 ] established an important foundation for biomedical dense prediction by combining hierarchical feature extraction with multi-scale skip connections. In polyp segmentation, CNN-based frameworks such as PraNet [ 16 ] and ACSNet [ 17 ] further improved lesion delineation through reverse attention mechanisms and multi-scale contextual aggregation. To support practical deployment, recent lightweight architectures such as LightCF-Net [ 18 ] have also explored how to preserve segmentation accuracy while reducing computational cost for real-time colonoscopy applications. However, although CNNs are effective at modeling local textures and short-range spatial patterns, capturing broader contextual dependencies may require deeper architectures or additional contextual modeling mechanisms. Transformer-based segmentation To address the limitations of restricted receptive fields, Transformer-based methods have been introduced into medical image segmentation [ 19 ]. By using self-attention to model long-range spatial dependencies, these methods provide an effective mechanism for global contextual modeling beyond conventional convolutional networks [ 20 ]. Representative hybrid frameworks such as TransUNet [ 21 ] and TransFuse [ 22 ] showed that Transformer-enhanced feature modeling can complement convolutional representations in dense prediction tasks. For polyp segmentation, methods such as Polyp-PVT [ 23 ] further demonstrated the potential of Transformer-based feature encoding for lesion analysis. More recent hybrid designs have continued to explore richer feature fusion strategies. For example, DECTNet [ 24 ] combines a convolution-based encoder with a Swin Transformer encoder for hierarchical representation learning, while DSCANet [ 25 ] adopts a dual-encoder framework with spatial cross-attention for polyp segmentation. These studies further highlight the value of combining local feature extraction with stronger contextual modeling. However, Transformer-based and dual-stream attention architectures can introduce considerable computational and memory overhead, particularly when processing high-resolution images. This motivates continued investigation of more computationally efficient approaches for polyp segmentation. Mamba-based segmentation More recently, selective SSMs, especially Mamba and its visual variants, have emerged as an efficient alternative for long-range sequence modeling [ 26 ]. Optimal transport-based undersampling preserves data distributions under class imbalance using Wasserstein distance [ 27 ], while lightweight Mamba variants emphasize efficiency and deployability [ 12 , 28 ]. Related efforts have also explored combining state-space modeling with additional spatial modules to better fuse global dependencies and local representations, as exemplified by SA-UMamba [ 29 ]. Recent Mamba-enhanced studies on polyp segmentation have explored different architectural strategies. For example, ProMamba [ 30 ] investigates prompt-based feature enhancement, BAMPolyp [ 13 ] introduces a bi-axial Mamba bottleneck for gastrointestinal polyp segmentation, and PolyMamba [ 31 ] incorporates spatial-prior guidance and high-frequency enhancement to better adapt to complex lesion morphology. These studies suggest that Mamba-based state-space modeling can complement convolutional feature extraction for polyp segmentation. However, many existing designs focus primarily on proposing new Mamba-enhanced architectures, while comparatively less attention has been given to how the placement and integration strategy of lightweight Mamba blocks affect segmentation performance within hierarchical 2D encoder–decoder networks. Therefore, the influence of lightweight Mamba integration strategy and placement remains insufficiently understood in polyp segmentation. Methodology This section describes the LiteMamba-Seg framework in detail. We first introduce the overall network architecture, followed by the ResNet34-based hierarchical encoder, the lightweight Mamba-based context modeling block, and the decoder for progressive feature reconstruction. Finally, the efficiency-oriented design is described to explain how LiteMamba-Seg maintains a lightweight computational profile. For this purpose, LiteMamba-Seg adopts a pre-trained ResNet34 [ 32 ] as the backbone, since it offers a favorable balance between representational capacity and computational efficiency, providing a favorable balance between representation capacity and computational efficiency. Their channel dimensions are {64, 64, 128, 256, 512}, reflecting the gradual expansion of feature capacity along the encoder hierarchy. These feature maps provide complementary visual information across different abstraction levels. Specifically, shallow features ( F 1 , F 2 ) retain fine-grained texture, edge, and boundary cues, whereas deeper features ( F 3 , F 4 , F 5 ) encode increasingly compact semantic representations that provide progressively more abstract semantic representations for subsequent segmentation. An important advantage of ResNet34 lies in its residual learning mechanism [ 32 ], which facilitates optimization stability and enables effective hierarchical representation learning. For polyp segmentation, this property is particularly beneficial because polyps often exhibit weak boundaries, irregular morphology, and subtle intensity variations relative to surrounding healthy mucosal tissues. Therefore, efficient and discriminative local feature extraction remains a critical prerequisite before introducing lightweight global context modeling. In LiteMamba-Seg, the encoder is not intended to perform heavy global reasoning by itself. Instead, it provides a strong and computationally efficient convolutional feature backbone, upon which the subsequent Unified Mamba Blocks further enhance long-range contextual perception at the deepest bottleneck and the early decoder reconstruction stage. Decoder design The decoder of LiteMamba-Seg progressively reconstructs high-resolution segmentation predictions from the encoded feature representations. To keep the overall framework lightweight, a simple decoder topology is adopted instead of heavy multi-branch aggregation or densely stacked refinement modules. This design follows the general encoder–decoder principle of U-Net-like architectures, in which high-level semantic features are gradually upsampled and fused with the corresponding encoder features through skip connections [ 14 ]. Starting from the bottleneck output, the decoder performs spatial recovery through four successive reconstruction stages. Let D 4 , D 3 , D 2 and D 1 denote the decoder features from deep to shallow stages. In the first decoder stage, the bottleneck feature is first upsampled, concatenated with the corresponding encoder feature F 4 , and then refined by the decoder convolution block: D 4 = ϕ 4 ( Concat ( Up ( B ) , F 4 ) ) (10) Where B denotes the bottleneck output, Up ( · ) denotes transposed convolution upsampling, Concat ( · ) denotes channel-wise concatenation, and ϕ 4 ( · ) denotes the convolutional refinement block at the D 4 stage. Transposed convolution was adopted to provide learnable spatial upsampling while preserving the standard stage-wise decoder structure and alignment with the corresponding skip features. In the main architecture, the fused D 4 feature is further processed by a Unified Mamba Block after the convolutional refinement at the first decoder stage. For the controlled ablation study, four additional insertion points were considered within the D 4 stage: before upsampling, before skip fusion, after fusion, and after convolutional refinement. These variants were introduced solely to isolate placement effects and are not part of the final LiteMamba-Seg architecture. The remaining decoder stages follow the same progressive reconstruction pattern: D i = ϕ i ( Concat ( Up ( D i + 1 ) , F i ) ) , i ∈ { 1 , 2 , 3 } (11) Accordingly, the D 3 stage fuses the upsampled D 4 feature with F 3 , the D 2 stage fuses the upsampled D 3 feature with F 2 , and the D 1 stage fuses the upsampled D 2 feature with F 1 . Through this hierarchical recovery process, coarse semantic features from deep layers are gradually transformed into dense pixel-level representations. Because the decoder produces hierarchical feature representations at multiple reconstruction stages, LiteMamba-Seg adopts a deep supervision strategy during training [ 33 ]. Specifically, lightweight prediction heads are applied to D 1 , D 2 , D 3 , and D 4 , generating four supervised predictions in total: one main high-resolution output and three auxiliary outputs. All outputs are upsampled to the input resolution for supervision during training, whereas only the highest-resolution prediction is retained during inference. This progressive reconstruction strategy enables LiteMamba-Seg to combine hierarchical feature fusion with lightweight decoding while preserving a simple overall decoder structure. Efficiency-oriented design A central design objective of LiteMamba-Seg is to introduce contextual modeling without substantially increasing computational cost. In practical colonoscopy scenarios, computational efficiency and inference speed are important considerations for practical deployment. Therefore, computational efficiency is considered as part of the architectural design rather than only as a post-hoc evaluation metric. The lightweight design of LiteMamba-Seg is supported by three main choices. First, the framework adopts ResNet34 as the encoder backbone, which provides hierarchical convolutional feature extraction with moderate complexity [ 32 ]. Second, the decoder follows a simple reconstruction topology based on transposed convolutions, skip fusion, and local convolutional refinement, avoiding heavy multi-branch aggregation or densely stacked refinement modules [ 14 ]. Third, contextual modeling is introduced through selective Mamba integration rather than dense self-attention, so that longer-range dependency modeling can be incorporated with relatively limited additional overhead [ 11 ]. Compared with Transformer-based global modeling, whose self-attention mechanism scales quadratically with sequence length [ 20 ], the Mamba operator provides a more efficient alternative for sequence-based contextual modeling [ 11 ]. This property is especially relevant for dense prediction tasks such as medical image segmentation, where flattening high-resolution feature maps can lead to very long token sequences. In LiteMamba-Seg, the Unified Mamba Block is therefore inserted only at selected stages of the network rather than throughout the entire architecture. Through these design choices, LiteMamba-Seg combines hierarchical convolutional representation learning with lightweight contextual modeling while keeping the overall framework compact. The actual trade-off between segmentation performance and computational efficiency is further examined in the experimental section. Experimental results and analysis To evaluate LiteMamba-Seg from multiple perspectives, this chapter presents experiments covering the experimental setup, benchmark segmentation performance, computational efficiency, ablation analysis, multi-seed stability, robustness to input perturbations, prediction reliability, and qualitative results. We first describe the datasets, evaluation metrics, and implementation details, and then compare LiteMamba-Seg with representative segmentation methods under both in-domain and cross-dataset settings. This is followed by computational profiling and a series of ablation studies examining component contributions and Mamba placement strategies. Additional analyses are conducted to assess reproducibility across random seeds, robustness to input perturbations, boundary quality, calibration, and lesion-level false-positive behavior. Finally, qualitative examples are provided to complement the quantitative findings. Experimental setup To evaluate the segmentation performance, computational efficiency, reproducibility, and robustness of LiteMamba-Seg, experiments were conducted on four publicly available polyp segmentation datasets. This section describes the datasets, evaluation metrics, training protocol, and implementation settings used throughout the experiments. Datasets. Following the benchmark partition widely adopted in PraNet-style polyp segmentation studies [ 16 ], experiments were conducted on four public datasets: Kvasir-SEG [ 34 ], CVC-ClinicDB [ 35 ], CVC-ColonDB [ 36 ], and ETIS-LaribPolypDB [ 37 ]. The original development pool contained 1,450 image-mask pairs, including 900 images from Kvasir-SEG and 550 images from CVC-ClinicDB. To enable model selection without using the predefined test subsets, the 1,450 development samples were first sorted by image identifier to establish a deterministic initial ordering and then randomly shuffled using a fixed split seed of 42 before applying a 90:10 training-validation split. This resulted in 1,305 image-mask pairs for model optimization and 145 pairs for internal validation. The resulting split was fixed before any data augmentation and remained unchanged across all model configurations and multi-seed runs. Only the training subset was subjected to stochastic data augmentation, whereas the validation subset underwent deterministic resizing and normalization. For evaluation, the predefined test subsets contained 100 images from Kvasir-SEG, 62 images from CVC-ClinicDB, 380 images from CVC-ColonDB, and 196 images from ETIS-LaribPolypDB. The Kvasir-SEG and CVC-ClinicDB test subsets were disjoint from the development pool at the image level and were reserved for final in-domain evaluation. CVC-ColonDB and ETIS-LaribPolypDB were used for cross-dataset evaluation without fine-tuning. None of the test subsets were used for model training, gradient updates, checkpoint selection, or hyperparameter tuning. Exact training and validation image identifiers are provided in the accompanying code repository to facilitate reproducibility. All datasets used in this study are publicly available and de-identified. No new human participants were recruited, and the authors did not have access to any identifiable patient information. Therefore, no additional institutional ethics approval or informed consent was required for this secondary analysis of publicly available data. Evaluation metrics. Segmentation performance was evaluated using four standard pixel-level metrics: Dice coefficient, Intersection over Union (IoU), Recall, and Precision. Dice and IoU quantify the overlap between predicted masks and ground-truth annotations, while Recall and Precision characterize foreground sensitivity and positive predictive performance, respectively. For each test image, the raw model output was first resized to the original image resolution using bilinear interpolation with align_corners = False, converted to a probability map using the sigmoid function, and binarized using a fixed threshold of 0.5. The corresponding ground-truth mask was also binarized using a threshold of 0.5. Dice, IoU, Recall, and Precision were computed independently for each image and then macro-averaged over all images in each test dataset. A smoothing constant of 1 × 1 0 − 5 was applied to the Dice and IoU calculations to avoid undefined zero-denominator cases. Precision and Recall used explicit zero-denominator handling rather than smoothing. When no foreground pixels were predicted for an image containing foreground, Precision was defined as 0; when both prediction and ground truth contained no foreground, Precision and Recall were defined as 1. No empty ground-truth masks were present in the evaluated test sets, so the latter case did not affect the reported results. No connected-component filtering, morphological processing, or other post-processing was applied. To complement overlap-based evaluation, boundary quality was assessed using Boundary IoU and the 95th-percentile Hausdorff distance (HD95). Prediction calibration was evaluated using expected calibration error (ECE) with 15 equally spaced confidence bins, while lesion-level false-positive behavior was measured as the average number of false-positive connected components per image (FP/image). These additional metrics were computed from the clean-image predictions. Robustness to image degradation was further evaluated using five perturbations applied independently to the test images: Gaussian blur with a radius of 2.0, JPEG compression with a quality factor of 50, brightness enhancement by a factor of 1.2, contrast reduction by a factor of 0.8, and low-resolution degradation. For the low-resolution condition, each image was downsampled to 176 × 176 and subsequently resized back to its original spatial resolution using bilinear interpolation before the standard 35 × 352 preprocessing. No retraining or fine-tuning was performed for any perturbed condition. Computational efficiency was characterized using the number of trainable parameters (Params), floating-point operations (GFLOPs), model-level inference latency, and frames per second (FPS). Implementation details. All experiments were implemented in PyTorch [ 38 ]. Input colonoscopy images and corresponding masks were uniformly resized to 352 × 352. The encoder was initialized with ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-34 weights unless otherwise specified in a controlled experiment. LiteMamba-Seg was optimized using AdamW [ 39 ] with an initial learning rate of 1 × 1 0 − 4 and a weight decay of 1 × 1 0 − 4 . A cosine annealing learning-rate scheduler [ 40 ] was applied over 100 training epochs with T m a x = 1 0 0 a minimum learning rate of 1 × 1 0 − 6 . The batch size was set to 16, and mixed-precision training was enabled. To improve training diversity and reduce overfitting, online data augmentation was applied using the Albumentations library [ 41 ], including random horizontal and vertical flipping, random 90-degree rotations, shift-scale-rotate transformations, elastic and grid distortions, intensity perturbations, blur-based augmentation, and coarse dropout. Coarse dropout was applied to the input image while preserving the corresponding segmentation mask. Only the training subset was subjected to stochastic augmentation, whereas the internal validation subset and test datasets underwent deterministic resizing and ImageNet normalization. The overall optimization objective combined StructureLoss with Boundary IoU loss and deep supervision from four prediction outputs, consisting of one main output and three auxiliary outputs. The main output was supervised using StructureLoss together with a weighted Boundary IoU loss, while the auxiliary outputs were supervised using StructureLoss with weights of 0.3, 0.2, and 0.1, respectively. Boundary IoU loss was computed independently for each image and averaged across the mini-batch, with the boundary-loss computation performed in FP32 to improve numerical stability under mixed-precision training. The best checkpoint was selected according to the highest macro-averaged Dice score on the fixed internal validation subset. For the multi-seed analysis, the final LiteMamba-Seg configuration was independently trained using five random seeds: 42, 3407, 2025, 666, and 1234. All training and inference experiments were conducted on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3080 Ti GPU with 12 GB VRAM under a CUDA-enabled PyTorch environment. Model-level inference speed was measured using a batch size of 1 with 100 warm-up iterations followed by 300 timed iterations. Ablation study To better understand how the final LiteMamba-Seg configuration is formed, we conduct a series of ablation experiments focusing on three aspects: preliminary component analysis, cross-stage Mamba placement, and controlled placement within the D 4 decoder stage. All compared configurations follow the same training-validation split and evaluation protocol. The purpose of these experiments is to examine how individual design choices affect segmentation performance and to determine whether the effectiveness of Mamba integration depends on where the sequence-modeling block is introduced. Conclusion In this study, we presented LiteMamba-Seg, a lightweight segmentation framework for polyp segmentation, and empirically evaluated how selected Mamba integration strategies influence segmentation performance under both in-domain and cross-dataset settings. Rather than increasing architectural complexity through extensive sequence modeling, the proposed framework focused on lightweight integration guided by placement strategy within a U-Net-like encoder–decoder architecture. The experimental results show that bottleneck Mamba integration improves segmentation performance across the evaluated benchmarks, while the subsequent placement analysis indicates that the effect of an additional Mamba block varies with its integration stage. Introducing an additional Mamba block at the encoder-side Layer3 stage does not consistently improve performance, whereas decoder-side D 4 integration provides better quantitative performance than the Layer3 alternative across the evaluated benchmarks, with the qualitative examples showing a similar trend. The controlled D 4 placement experiments further show that post-convolution integration performs better than the other examined positions under closely matched model capacity. LiteMamba-Seg achieves a favorable balance between segmentation accuracy and model-level inference efficiency while maintaining competitive performance across the evaluated benchmark datasets. The multi-seed experiments further demonstrate reproducible training behavior, while the perturbation, boundary, calibration, and false-positive analyses provide additional evidence of stable and reliable segmentation performance under the evaluated conditions. Although LiteMamba-Seg does not outperform all compared methods on every benchmark, the overall findings indicate that selective Mamba integration can be effective when combined with an appropriate placement strategy. Future work may further investigate the mechanisms underlying placement-dependent behavior through effective receptive field and frequency-domain analyses, examine the influence of backbone initialization including training from scratch, explore additional lightweight state-space integration strategies, and evaluate the framework across broader medical image segmentation tasks and acquisition conditions.
