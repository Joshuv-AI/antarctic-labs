ABSTRACT
Accurate detection, discrimination, and characterization of high-altitude hypersonic events using
infrasonic monitoring are critical to planetary defense and global strategic surveillance. This report
synthesizes recent advances achieved through rigorous analysis of infrasonic signatures from natural
meteoroids, emphasizing shallow entry-angle meteoroids as essentially proxies for artificial hypersonic
systems. Meteoroids naturally encompass diverse velocities, trajectories, altitudes, and fragmentation
behaviors, enabling systematic validation of empirical period–yield relationships, waveform
morphology classifiers, and trajectory-induced back-azimuth deviation models. Integration of adaptive
array-processing enhancements within Cardinal software further extends infrasonic detection
sensitivity and signal classification reliability. Collectively these advances, based solely on infrasonic
signatures or limited optical data, offer robust methodologies for distinguishing natural from artificial
hypersonic sources, significantly reducing event geolocation uncertainties and refining source-function
determination. The outcomes detailed herein lay foundational groundwork for improved global
hypersonic event-surveillance frameworks, supporting improved security preparedness and informing
strategic monitoring and defense policies.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Robust infrasonic characterization of atmospheric hypersonic events directly supports planetary-
defense strategies and global-security objectives by facilitating accurate discrimination between natural
meteoroids and artificial hypersonic vehicles. This report comprehensively reviews recent
accomplishments achieved through the systematic analysis of infrasonic signals from naturally
occurring bolides and carefully monitored sample-return capsule re-entries. Shallow entry-angle
meteoroids, including rare “earthgrazers,” uniquely populate a diverse parameter space, encompassing
entry velocity, altitude, trajectory geometry, and fragmentation processes, that closely mirrors
conditions encountered by hypersonic systems. Exploiting these natural analogs has enabled
substantial methodological advancements: refined empirical relationships linking infrasonic period
and event energy, detailed waveform morphology classification distinguishing continuous cylindrical
shocks from discrete spherical shocks, and quantification of trajectory-induced back-azimuth
deviations via the newly developed geometric model. Moreover, significant improvements in adaptive
infrasonic array processing through enhanced Cardinal software markedly strengthen detection and
event classification under challenging noise conditions. These technical and methodological
achievements provide a scientifically rigorous framework that promises significant improvements in
infrasonic monitoring accuracy, geolocation precision, and source discrimination reliability, directly
contributing to strategic hypersonic-vehicle surveillance and security preparedness.

1. PROJECT OVERVIEW
Hypersonic platforms such as boost‑glide vehicles [1, 2] travel at extreme speed, maneuver at high
altitude, and give decision makers very little reaction time [3]. Because foreign test flights are
infrequent, short‑lived, and often poorly instrumented, they provide scant physical data for
independent assessment. A shallow‑entry meteoroid or asteroid, on the other hand, occupies a
comparable aerodynamic regime yet occurs in the open atmosphere and produces global acoustic
signatures [1, 4-6]. Misidentifying one phenomenon for the other could lead to costly or even
catastrophic decisions.

This three‑year Laboratory Directed Research and Development (LDRD) effort used meteoroids,
including Earth-grazing and shallow entry-angle events, as empirical surrogates for artificial hypersonic
objects. By utilizing global International Monitoring System (IMS) records, dense regional infrasound
deployments, space‑based optical data, and targeted case studies, the project delivered new tools for
acoustic detection, geolocation, and source‑type discrimination.

Main findings, methodologies, and detailed analyses have been disseminated through five Sandia
technical reports [7-13] and 10 peer‑reviewed publications [14-22]. These publications detail advances
in signal processing, physical modeling of shock propagation, empirical energy–yield relations,
automated optical fragmentation analysis, and multi‑sensor data fusion. The present report
summarizes those advances for sponsors, policymakers, and technical stakeholders; full
methodological specifics remain in the cited literature. A summary of project accomplishments is given
in Section 8.

2. INTRODUCTION AND MOTIVATION

2.1. Scientific Motivation and Strategic Importance
The reliable identification, characterization, and discrimination of elongated-trajectory, high-altitude
atmospheric entries, both natural and artificial, represent a critical priority within global monitoring
efforts, planetary defense frameworks, and strategic surveillance initiatives [23, 25-28]. Such events
typically involve meteoroids, asteroids, spacecraft, orbital debris, or re-entry vehicles traversing
significant horizontal distances at hypersonic velocities. The combination of their speed and trajectory
presents substantial observational and analytical challenges due to complex shockwave propagation
dynamics, altitude-dependent energy deposition, and ambiguity in infrasonic signal interpretation.

The differentiation of natural elongated-trajectory events (meteoroids and asteroids) from artificial
sources (spacecraft, re-entry vehicles, orbital debris, and other types of engineered vehicles) remains
particularly critical because misclassification could potentially trigger disproportionate defensive
responses or lead to uncertainties in atmospheric event monitoring systems. The 2013 Chelyabinsk
event, which entered at a shallow angle and delivered almost half a megaton of trinitrotoluene (TNT)
equivalent energy (1 kt = 4.184·10¹² J) without prior warning, illustrates the scale of such a risk
[29-32]. The event was accompanied by loud sonic booms and a strong blast wave, which caused
numerous injuries and significant damage to the infrastructure [31]. For comparison, the energy
deposited by the Chelyabinsk superbolide was nearly 30 times greater than that of Little Boy, a nuclear
bomb that detonated over Hiroshima (15 kt of TNT equivalent) in 1945.

2.2. Limitations of Artificial Benchmarks
Laboratory tests cannot reproduce multi‑hundred‑kilometer flight paths at high Mach numbers. In
principle, well-characterized atmospheric-entry vehicles, particularly those associated with controlled
space-exploration missions, can provide invaluable calibration benchmarks due to their accurately
known initial conditions (mass, aerodynamic profile, entry velocity, and trajectory). Yet, despite their
utility such controlled artificial re-entry events remain exceedingly rare. Since the Apollo era, only five
interplanetary Sample Return Capsule (SRC) re-entries (Genesis [33], Stardust [34], Hayabusa [35],
Hayabusa2 [36], and the Origins, Spectral Interpretation, Resource Identification, Security, Regolith
Explorer (OSIRIS-REx)) have provided seismo-acoustic observational opportunities [6, 17, 37]. This
inherent scarcity severely constrains the ability to develop and globally validate robust empirical
geolocation methodologies, energy-deposition modeling, and signal-characterization algorithms.

Furthermore, other engineered hypersonic objects, such as orbital-debris re-entries, are not numerous
enough to provide a statistically meaningful sample and typically have poorly constrained parameters,
unpredictable entry conditions, and infrequent observational opportunities, compounding
uncertainties and limiting empirical analyses [7]. Hypersonic glide tests by foreign states are sporadic,
instrumented sparsely, frequently classified, and in most cases not accessible to direct active
characterization [1, 3, 8, 38]. Consequently, this critical data gap demands a complementary approach
that can offer statistically significant observational datasets spanning extensive parameter spaces
necessary for rigorous methodological calibration. In this context, naturally occurring meteoroids
provide indispensable empirical analogues, while infrasound monitoring represents an inexpensive,
passive, and reliable surveillance and characterization technology.

2.3. Natural Objects as High-Altitude Hypersonic Proxies
Hypersonic vehicles such as boost-glide bodies, scramjets, and spacecraft re-entry capsules share
critical aerodynamic and physical characteristics with naturally occurring meteoroids [1, 4, 15, 23, 38,
39]. By the definition accepted by the International Astronomical Union (IAU), an asteroid is a rocky,
icy, or metallic solar-orbiting object one meter or larger in diameter, whereas a meteoroid is a natural
fragment smaller than one meter, extending down to the sub-millimeter scale. When a meteoroid
enters Earth's atmosphere, the luminous phenomenon produced is termed a meteor. If the meteor
brightness exceeds that of Venus (magnitude –4), it is classified as a fireball or a bolide [40].
Exceptionally energetic and bright bolides are commonly referred to as superbolides. Fragments that
survive passage through the atmosphere and reach the Earth's surface are called meteorites. These
natural phenomena offer ideal observational analogues for engineered elongated-trajectory
atmospheric entries.

Extraterrestrial objects routinely enter Earth’s atmosphere at velocities between 11.2 and 72 km/s
[40], corresponding to Mach numbers of ~35–270. Here, the Mach number (M) is the dimensionless
ratio of an object’s velocity (v) to the local speed of sound (va) in the surrounding medium, M=v/va,
thereby indicating whether the motion is subsonic (M<1), transonic (M~1), supersonic (1<M<5), or
hypersonic (M≥5) [41]. Because the object enters the continuum flow regime at transitional Knudsen
numbers [4, 39], these high-velocity interactions generate strong shockwaves, which decay into low-
frequency sound waves, i.e., infrasound, and can be detected by microbarometers on the ground [42]
or aloft [43], independent of daylight or cloud cover.

Infrasound sensing, as a passive surveillance technology, has a notable advantage because it can detect
shockwave signatures resulting from objects traveling through Earth's atmosphere at supersonic and
hypersonic velocities, even in cases where the object itself remains optically invisible or undetectable
otherwise (e.g., beyond horizon). This advantage is possible because the shockwave formation occurs
whenever an object exceeds the local speed of sound in a fluid medium, such as the Earth’s
atmosphere, generating acoustic signals detectable by infrasonic sensors.

2.4. Project Objectives and Approach
Motivated by the strategic imperative of improving event discrimination, this three-year LDRD effort
systematically leveraged naturally occurring meteoroids, particularly shallow entry-angle and Earth-
grazing events, as empirical proxies to study elongated-trajectory atmospheric shock phenomena. This
project focused on infrasonic signal analysis as a primary detection modality, supported by optical
observations from the National Aeronautics and Space Administration’s (NASA’s) Jet Propulsion
Laboratory's Center for Near-Earth Object Studies (CNEOS) and space-based observations, including
U.S. Government sensors (USG) and the Geostationary Lightning Mapper (GLM) onboard National
Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) Geostationary Operational Environmental
Satellites (GOES).

Within this project, an ambitious multi-modal geophysical observational campaign was conceived for
the OSIRIS-REx sample-return capsule re-entry on 24 September 2023. Initially designed as part of
this research effort, the OSIRIS-REx observational campaign subsequently evolved into a significant,
independent investigation involving collaboration between Sandia and multiple national laboratories,
universities, and federal agencies [6]. This successful campaign deployed more than 400 geophysical
instruments (infrasonic microphones, broadband seismometers, fiber-optic distributed acoustic
sensing (DAS) arrays, high-rate Global Positioning System (GPS) receivers, and balloon-borne

 14

microphones) across Nevada and Utah. The OSIRIS-REx SRC re-entry provided unprecedented high-
resolution data documenting Mach-cone evolution, acoustic propagation, shockwave characteristics,
and atmospheric attenuation processes, delivering critical validation of theoretical and empirical
models for hypersonic-to-subsonic transitions and acoustic-seismic coupling [37].

Overall, the project has produced substantial methodological advances and significant contributions,
systematically documented in peer-reviewed articles, Sandia technical reports, and open-source
software packages. These advancements include, but are not limited to, refined empirical infrasound
period–yield relationships [19], automated optical fragmentation characterization methods [18],
trajectory geometry modeling (BIBEX-M)[16], adaptive array processing (Cardinal) [21], and
comprehensive natural entry (regional meteors and global bolides) and artificial re-entry datasets [7, 8,
14]. Detailed analyses performed as part of this project, such as studies of the 2008 Tajikistan bolide
(Silber, 2024b), regional analyses of bolide events (Wilson et al., 2025), analysis of the 2020 Earth-
grazing fireball [44], and the 2023 Australian bolide [45], have improved capabilities to detect,
characterize, geolocate, and discriminate elongated-trajectory high-altitude atmospheric entries.

This report synthesizes the major outcomes and broader implications of this extensive research effort,
demonstrating the indispensable value of naturally occurring meteoroids as empirical proxies. The
methodologies, analytical frameworks, and observational data developed during this project advance
the capability for robust global atmospheric monitoring, planetary defense preparedness, and strategic
surveillance, directly supporting informed decision-making and reducing the risks associated with
event misclassification. We note, however, that the accomplishments summarized here represent
only selected highlights of the extensive work completed throughout this three-year LDRD
project. Readers are encouraged to consult the cited publications and technical reports for a complete
and detailed account.

3. SHOCK GENERATION AND ENERGY DEPOSITION

3.1. Physical Mechanisms of Shock Formation and Energy Deposition
When a meteoroid or asteroid enters Earth's atmosphere at hypersonic velocities, strong shockwaves
are generated due to rapid aerodynamic heating, material ablation, and kinetic energy deposition along
its trajectory [4, 46]. Two primary shock-generation mechanisms characterize bolide entries: (1)
cylindrical or line-source shocks produced by continuous aerodynamic ablation, and (2) quasi-
spherical shocks resulting from discrete, instantaneous fragmentation events [15, 40, 47]. Continuous
ablation occurs when kinetic energy is relatively steadily deposited along an extended path, generating
ballistic or radially propagating cylindrical shockwaves (Figure 1a). In contrast, discrete fragmentation
occurs abruptly, rapidly releasing a significant fraction of the object's kinetic energy and producing
quasi-spherical, point-source shockwaves. Real meteoroid entries typically exhibit intermediate or
combined scenarios, involving strong continuous ablation (Figure 1b), ongoing (Figure 1c) or
successive (Figure 1d) fragmentation episodes, or singular catastrophic airbursts (Figure 2e) [23, 32].

line‑source radiation is generated. (c) Strong ablation or ongoing fragmentation generates a spray
of small fragments and a broadened, quasi‑cylindrical disturbance. (d) Multiple discrete
fragmentation episodes along the path create successive localized shock fronts superimposed on

3.2. Differences Between Natural Meteoroids and Artificial Entry Objects
Artificial atmospheric entry objects (e.g., spacecraft, reentry and launch vehicles, orbital debris, and
other engineered hypersonic platforms) exhibit several significant differences compared to naturally
occurring meteoroids. Unlike meteoroids, engineered platforms generally enter Earth's atmosphere at
lower velocities, typically ranging from about 7 to 12 km/s (Mach ~20–40). For instance, NASA's
Space Shuttle typically re-entered Earth's atmosphere at speeds near 7.7 km/s, substantially lower than
typical meteoroid entry speeds (11.2–72 km/s). This reduced velocity results in correspondingly lower
peak heating rates and different energy deposition characteristics.

 17

Unlike naturally occurring meteoroids, whose size, density, chemical composition, and trajectory are
mostly unknown beforehand, spacecraft such as sample return capsules (e.g., Stardust, Hayabusa,
OSIRIS-REx) have carefully documented mass, aerodynamic shape, and trajectory characteristics. In
contrast, reentering space debris, such as spent rocket upper stages, defunct satellites, or discarded
payload components, often have unknown mass distributions, compositions, and structural
properties, complicating predictive modeling and characterization. Thus, studying meteoroids
becomes even more critical because they provide empirical benchmarks across a statistically
meaningful parameter space for evaluating methods of estimating object size, structural integrity,
fragmentation altitude, and energy deposition profiles from acoustic signals alone.

Despite their differences, both artificial objects and meteoroids generate acoustic signatures in the
lower transitional and continuum flow regimes [4], fundamentally linked by two primary shock-
generation mechanisms: continuous cylindrical line-source shocks, and quasi-spherical point-source
shocks. These shared shock-generation mechanisms yield acoustic signals that can be analyzed using
similar physical and empirical modeling approaches. Thus, studies of meteoroids can significantly
inform methodologies applicable to engineered hypersonic entries, making naturally occurring events
indispensable empirical benchmarks.

Moreover, artificial objects, though engineered for stability and controlled descent, may still undergo
unplanned fragmentation events. The catastrophic breakup of NASA’s Space Shuttle Columbia in
2003 exemplified unexpected fragmentation during atmospheric entry, generating complex quasi-
spherical shockwaves recorded by infrasonic and seismic stations [53]. Similarly, recent events such as
the SpaceX Starship flight termination in 2023 emphasize the critical importance of accurately
quantifying energy-deposition dynamics, fragmentation mechanisms, and subsequent forensic event
reconstruction [54]. In such cases, infrasonic measurements provide essential data, facilitating
independent verification of trajectory, breakup sequence, and aerodynamic loads that optical or
telemetry instruments may not fully capture.

Even in carefully orchestrated missions, unanticipated instrumentation or telemetry failures can
impede planned trajectory reconstruction. Notably, during the OSIRIS-REx sample return capsule re-
entry campaign, precise in-flight trajectory reconstruction encountered instrumentation challenges,
limiting the effectiveness of planned measurements [55]. In such instances, infrasonic observations
become critical for recovering trajectory details and energy deposition characteristics, illustrating the
importance of acoustic observations as complementary or sometimes even primary data sources for
atmospheric re-entry characterization [6, 37, 56]. Artificial hypersonic platforms, in general, introduce
additional complexity, characterized by deliberate maneuvering and shallow flight trajectories, which
can result in varying acoustic signatures. Maneuvering can influence shockwave generation and energy
deposition patterns, thereby complicating infrasonic detection, characterization, and source
localization.

Meteoroid observations therefore offer indispensable analogs, providing empirical datasets to refine
methodologies critical for acoustic source characterization, reliable event discrimination, and effective
planetary defense and strategic monitoring capabilities.

3.3. Observational Validation Through Bolide Case Studies
Significant empirical advancements in understanding bolide shockwave morphology, fragmentation
dynamics, and energy deposition mechanisms were achieved in this project through detailed

 18

observational analyses. Case studies of notable bolide events provided robust validation of theoretical
models, demonstrating practical applications for infrasonic and optical analyses and their effectiveness
for event characterization.

The 23 July 2008 Tajikistan bolide was rigorously investigated as part of this work [23]. Infrasound
signals recorded at global-scale distances (>1500 km) from two IMS (see Section 4.1.1) stations
allowed independent and robust determination of the primary fragmentation altitude (~35 km) and
indicated a quasi-spherical blast wave resulting from the object's abrupt fragmentation. This global
infrasonic analysis provided key empirical validation for theoretical models of bolide energy deposition
and shock propagation. Such capability to reliably characterize fragmentation altitude and shock
morphology from sparse infrasonic data alone emphasizes the value of acoustic measurements in high-
altitude atmospheric event characterization.

Complementing global-scale observations, using dense regional infrasonic arrays Wilson, et al. [20]
analyzed two well-documented bolide events over Arizona (2016) and Arkansas (2019). This regional
network enabled detailed resolution of both continuous cylindrical shocks produced by sustained
ablation and discrete spherical shocks generated from fragmentation. By systematically analyzing
amplitude, period, and waveform morphology at individual stations, Wilson, et al. [20] refined
estimates of fragmentation altitude, improved bolide geolocation, and provided essential observational
constraints for acoustic propagation modeling.

The Australian bolide event of 20 May 2023 offered an additional valuable observational scenario,
showcasing the integration of infrasonic and optical observations for robust event characterization
[45]. A small asteroid entered Earth's atmosphere over Queensland, undergoing catastrophic
fragmentation at an altitude of ~29 km and releasing an energy equivalent to ~7.2 kt TNT (as reported
by CNEOS). Infrasonic signals from this bolide were recorded by six distant IMS stations, some as
far away as 6,000 km, yielding a consistent energy estimate that independently corroborated the
CNEOS-reported optical observations. The Australian bolide illustrates the complementary value of
infrasonic and optical measurements, providing critical cross-validation for fragmentation altitude,
energy-deposition characteristics, and bolide event-discrimination methodologies.

A rare category of fireballs, known as Earth-grazing meteoroids or “earthgrazers,” was also
investigated [44]. Earthgrazers enter the atmosphere at extremely shallow entry angles, sometimes
traversing horizontally before returning to space [57]. One such event occurred over Northern Europe
on 22 September 2020, generating distinct low-frequency infrasonic signals detected by three stations
of the Royal Netherlands Meteorological Institute (KNMI). Despite its extremely high-altitude
trajectory (~100 km), the earthgrazer produced measurable infrasonic signatures, providing unique
observational evidence for high-altitude shock generation, propagation, and energy-deposition
dynamics. Earthgrazers are of particular scientific and practical interest due to their direct aerodynamic
analogy to artificial hypersonic glide vehicles, making these observations valuable for refining
infrasonic signal interpretation and high-altitude source characterization methods.

3.4. Optical and Infrasonic Energy Deposition Characterization
A robust understanding of the energy deposition processes of elongated-trajectory atmospheric
entries, particularly meteoroids, requires accurate characterization of fragmentation and ablation
dynamics. Within this project, two key methodological advancements, one optical and one infrasonic,
were developed and validated: the Bolide Lightcurve Analysis and Discrimination Explorer (BLADE)

 19

[18], and refined multiparametric infrasound energy–yield relationships [19]. Together, these
methodologies improve the reliability of event characterization, fragmentation identification, and
source energy estimation.

BLADE, developed by Silber and Sawal [18], provides an automated and systematic approach to
classifying bolide fragmentation events based on optical radiometric data from CNEOS. BLADE
objectively identifies discrete fragmentation episodes within optical brightness curves, distinguishing
them from continuous ablation signatures. By systematically categorizing bolide light curves into
fragmentation-driven and non-fragmentation energy deposition profiles, BLADE supplies
independent, optically derived constraints on fragmentation altitude, timing, and relative energy
release, thereby directly informing infrasonic interpretation. This optical-infrasonic integration
substantially reduces ambiguity in the identification and interpretation of acoustic signatures arising
from different fragmentation modes, significantly enhancing the accuracy of infrasonic source
characterization.

Leveraging BLADE-derived fragmentation characteristics, Silber, et al. [19] further developed and
refined empirical infrasound period–yield relationships by explicitly incorporating parameters such as
peak brightness altitude, entry geometry, and light curve classification (derived through BLADE).
Prior energy–yield models typically relied on simplified assumptions derived from nuclear explosion
data [58] or limited bolide observations [59, 60]. By explicitly integrating event-specific parameters and
fragmentation modes derived optically from BLADE into infrasonic period–yield analyses, the
accuracy and reliability of energy deposition estimates from infrasonic measurements alone were
improved. These refined multiparametric relationships provide robust estimates of source energy and
facilitate a more comprehensive insight into shockwave morphology directly linked to the physical
fragmentation processes occurring along the trajectory.

The combination of automated optical fragmentation classification (BLADE) and multiparametric
infrasonic energy estimation frameworks thus represents a major step forward in the characterization
of elongated-trajectory atmospheric entries, offering reliable, validated, and empirically robust tools
for event identification and characterization.

4. MULTI-MODAL SENSING AND DATA FUSION
Comprehensive and reliable characterization of elongated-trajectory, high-altitude atmospheric events
depends on strategically leveraging infrasound as well as complementing it with additional
observational modalities [23]. This project strategically leveraged multiple sensing techniques and
instrumentation platforms, including global infrasonic arrays, dense regional infrasound networks,
satellite and ground-based optical sensors, and controlled observational campaigns involving artificial
re-entry objects. These complementary sensing modalities provided robust and diverse empirical
datasets essential for cross-validation, detailed event characterization, and refinement of source
discrimination methodologies.

4.1. Infrasound as a Sensing Modality
Infrasound offers distinct observational advantages due to its capability to detect and characterize
shockwave signatures generated by hypersonic and supersonic atmospheric entries [4, 41], including
distant and visually obscured sources. When objects enter Earth's atmosphere at velocities exceeding
the local speed of sound, strong shockwaves form and propagate as low-frequency acoustic signals
detectable at ranges from hundreds to thousands of kilometers [42]. This acoustic propagation is
largely independent of daylight, weather conditions, and visibility, providing a reliable means of
detecting and characterizing events that may produce negligible radar or optical signatures.

Due to the relatively low propagation speed of sound (~300 m/s), infrasonic signals arrive at stations
minutes to hours after the event occurs. Thus, while not suitable for real-time monitoring, infrasound
serves as an essential modality for post-event detection, source localization, and forensic analysis. This
application includes characterizing natural impulsive events such as bolides [23], volcanic eruptions
[61], and lightning [62], as well as anthropogenic sources including explosions [54, 63], rocket launches
[64], and artillery fire [65]. The practicality of infrasonic sensing is further increased by low
instrumentation costs, continuous year-round operation, and modest station-density requirements.

However, the interpretation of infrasonic signals requires careful consideration of atmospheric
propagation effects. Temperature gradients, atmospheric winds, waveguide formation, and
multipathing can substantially modify signal amplitudes, arrival times, and waveform structures during
propagation from source to receiver [66, 67]. Despite these complexities, source characteristics (e.g.,
fragmentation altitude, energy release profile, and event location) can often be inferred through
advanced waveform processing and acoustic propagation modeling [68]. This forensic analysis of
infrasonic signals aims to leverage acoustic waveforms, particularly when complementary
observational data is scarce or unavailable, to enhance event discrimination, improve characterization
accuracy, and provide robust reconstructions of energy deposition and source dynamics [48, 58].

Within this project, infrasound formed the primary observational foundation, systematically integrated
with complementary optical and satellite measurements, yielding comprehensive multi-modal datasets.
These integrated data provided rigorous validation and calibration for acoustic event characterization
methods, significantly advancing detection, geolocation, and discrimination capabilities for elongated-
trajectory, high-altitude atmospheric events.

4.1.1. Global Infrasound Monitoring and Detection
Initially developed for nuclear test surveillance during the Cold War [14, 58], infrasound sensing was
later institutionalized as a core detection modality within the International Monitoring System (IMS)
operated by the Preparatory Commission for the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty
Organization (CTBTO PrepCom) [69]. The IMS network, designed to detect a 1 kt or greater
atmospheric explosion originating anywhere on the globe, currently consists of 53 certified infrasound
stations (out of 60 planned, Figure 3) [42, 69, 70]. The IMS has documented numerous bolide events,
including those investigated as part of this project, providing valuable empirical datasets for validating
infrasonic geolocation, yield estimation, and event discrimination methods.

Using infrasonic signal processing methodologies and propagation modeling techniques developed
within the scope of this project, notable improvements were achieved in extracting and interpreting
bolide-generated acoustic signals recorded by IMS arrays. These methodological advancements are
demonstrated by detailed analyses of two notable bolide events: (1) the 2008 Tajikistan bolide [23],
and (2) the 2023 Australian bolide [45].

The 2008 Tajikistan bolide was detected at two IMS stations located at distances of 1530 km (I31KZ)
and 2130 km (I46RU). Leveraging waveform processing and propagation modeling techniques
developed in this work, infrasonic signals were successfully analyzed despite challenging atmospheric
conditions, low SNR, and propagation through complex atmospheric ducts and leaky waveguides [71].
These analyses yielded robust estimates of the primary fragmentation altitude (~35 km) and provided
identification of a dominant quasi-spherical shock signature associated with the bolide’s primary
fragmentation event. The Tajikistan case thus represents a significant methodological accomplishment

 22

of this project, demonstrating that global-scale infrasonic observations alone, when combined with
advanced propagation modeling, can reliably characterize high-altitude fragmentation events and
substantially improve bolide source discrimination and acoustic source-function analysis.

The second event, an energetic bolide over Australia on 20 May 2023, was detected by six IMS stations
at distances exceeding ~6,000 km [45]. In this analysis, infrasonic waveforms were processed using
Cardinal, an advanced adaptive array processing framework developed within this project (Ronac
Giannone, et al. [21], discussed in detail in Section 5.4). Cardinal enabled robust signal extraction, even
in challenging low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) conditions. Subsequent propagation modeling
employed a novel post-processing method established during this investigation, effectively
constraining the bolide's primary fragmentation altitude. Additionally, refined infrasonic energy
relationships developed through this project [19] produced yield estimates closely matching
independent energy values derived from U.S. government (USG) sensors, corroborating their
applicability and accuracy in operational scenarios [45]. The infrasonic analyses also showed strong
consistency with optical observations, demonstrating the utility of a multi-modal observational
approach for accurately characterizing the bolide's source function and energy deposition (peer-
reviewed publication in preparation).

4.1.2. Regional and Temporary Infrasound Networks and Detection
Regional infrasonic networks complement global-scale networks by offering increased sensor density
and improved spatial resolution within specific geographic regions. Under desirable observational and
atmospheric conditions, these dense networks can offer improved capacity to resolve multiple
infrasonic arrivals, characterize shockwave morphology, and identify fragmentation events occurring
along elongated atmospheric trajectories. However, interpretation remains challenging due to
propagation complexities, multipathing, wind-induced refraction, and atmospheric attenuation effects
[15].

As part of this project, Wilson, et al. [20] demonstrated the capabilities of regional infrasonic arrays
through detailed analyses of two well-documented bolide events (Arizona in 2016 and Arkansas in
2019). Their work leveraged a dense regional infrasonic network in the south-central United States,
allowing discrimination between cylindrical shocks arising from continuous ablation and discrete
quasi-spherical shocks from fragmentation episodes. These distinctions advanced fragmentation
altitude localization, yield estimation accuracy, and overall infrasonic waveform interpretation
reliability. Regional arrays also play an important role in observing and characterizing rare, high-
altitude meteoroid events. For example, an earthgrazer fireball observed over Europe on 22 September
2020 was detected by regional infrasonic sensors operated by KNMI [44]. Analysis of these signals
provided critical observational evidence for understanding high-altitude shock formation and acoustic
propagation at transitional-flow atmospheric conditions (publication in preparation).

Temporary regional deployments further extend the value of high-density infrasonic arrays [72],
especially for well-anticipated artificial re-entry events such as sample return capsules (SRCs) (for
additional details, see a review by Silber, et al. [17]). A prominent example includes the extensive
observational campaign for the OSIRIS-REx capsule re-entry on 24 September 2023 [6, 17, 37].

Finally, accurate characterization of trajectory-induced infrasonic back-azimuth deviations was
addressed explicitly by Silber [16]. Using detailed geometric modeling, quantitative relationships and
maximum expected azimuthal deviations were derived as a function of bolide entry angle. These

 23

results directly improve regional and global infrasonic geolocation methodologies, significantly
advancing reliability in source location and bolide event characterization.

4.2. Optical and Space-Based Observations
Complementing infrasonic observations, optical and satellite-based monitoring approaches improve
event characterization. NASA’s JPL CNEOS maintains a comprehensive database of fireball events
detected by U.S. government space-based sensors (USG), providing critical event parameters such as
entry location, altitude, velocity, energy deposition, and radiated optical energy. The CNEOS database
serves as an indispensable resource for validating infrasonic-derived parameters and refining empirical
energy-deposition models, due to its detailed optical signature records (e.g., radiometric light curves),
as was exemplified as part of this work [18, 19, 23].

Satellite-based sensors such as the GLM, onboard the NOAA’s GOES satellites, further augment
bolide detection and characterization. GLM's high cadence observations and broad geographic
coverage have proven valuable in providing independent location estimates, improving the accuracy
and confidence in infrasonic-derived geolocations. Wilson, et al. [20], as part of this research effort,
leveraged GLM observations to critically assess infrasonic source localization a bolide event, and
demonstrated that, under appropriate observational conditions, infrasonic measurements can yield
event geolocation results consistent than those derived solely from USG. This finding demonstrates
that infrasonic measurements, when appropriately integrated with complementary observations, can
provide reliable and independent source characterization.

5. METHODOLOGICAL ADVANCES IN INFRASOUND PROCESSING,
 MODELING, AND INTERPRETATION
Infrasonic detection and analysis form the cornerstone of effective characterization for elongated-
trajectory, high-altitude atmospheric entries, providing essential information about source properties,
energy deposition, and fragmentation processes. Within the scope of this project, several notable
advancements were achieved, addressing key analytical challenges and improving the robustness and
reliability of infrasonic event detection and interpretation.

5.1. Infrasound Signal Interpretation: Challenges and Methodological
 Improvements
Bolide-generated infrasonic signals present unique analytical challenges due to their transient nature,
extended spatial trajectories, and complex acoustic emission patterns. A comprehensive review
conducted as part of this project [15] systematically addressed critical considerations specific to bolide
infrasonic signal processing, including methods for signal detection, arrival identification, waveform
interpretation, and accurate source characterization. Bolides produce infrasonic signatures through
two primary shock mechanisms: cylindrical line-source shocks associated with sustained aerodynamic
ablation and quasi-spherical shocks resulting from discrete fragmentation. The interplay between these
shock types, compounded by complex atmospheric propagation conditions, introduces significant
variability in observed signal parameters, particularly periods, amplitudes, and back azimuths. Silber
[15] presented detailed case studies illustrating these challenges and outlined a unified methodological
framework to enhance consistency in bolide infrasound analysis.

5.2. Computational Prediction of Infrasound Arrival Times and Directions
Effective detection and interpretation of infrasound requires reliable prediction of signal arrival times
and directions at monitoring stations. Silber [12] described a detailed computational methodology
designed explicitly to calculate infrasonic signal arrival windows and back-azimuth directions for
impulsive atmospheric events, both stationary and moving. The methodology systematically models
signal propagation through four distinct atmospheric layers: the boundary layer, troposphere,
stratosphere, and thermosphere. For moving events such as bolides or re-entering objects, the model
accounts explicitly for multiple trajectory points, predicting signal arrivals and associated propagation
paths at ground-based sensor locations. By providing signal times and directions of arrival predictions,
this modeling approach reduces the search space and analysis effort required when examining
recorded infrasonic waveforms. Although the original computational Python code is not publicly
available, the published report [10] thoroughly describes the underlying physical principles,
mathematical formulations, and computational strategies, enabling independent implementation and
verification by users.

5.3. Trajectory-Dependent Infrasonic Back-Azimuth Deviations: BIBEX-M
Accurate geolocation of elongated-trajectory bolide events critically depends upon understanding and
quantifying trajectory-induced infrasonic back-azimuth deviations. Silber [16] developed the Bolide
Infrasound Back-Azimuth EXplorer Model (BIBEX-M), explicitly quantifying maximum expected
infrasonic back-azimuth deviations based on bolide entry geometry and trajectory angle. Using detailed
geometric modeling, Silber [16] derived quantitative tables of expected azimuthal deviations (Figure
4), improving infrasonic event localization and interpretation methodologies. This model directly
informs infrasonic array analysis, improving event discrimination accuracy and geolocation reliability,
particularly for shallow entry-angle bolides and analogous engineered hypersonic events.

5.4. Advanced Adaptive Array Processing (Cardinal)
Reliable detection, classification, and characterization of hypersonic atmospheric events, whether
naturally occurring bolides or hypersonic vehicles, critically depend on the ability to extract subtle
infrasonic signals from background noise. Traditional infrasonic array processing techniques, while
effective for strong, impulsive signals, often struggle in scenarios characterized by lower signal-to-
noise ratios (SNR), interference from coherent noise sources, or complex atmospheric propagation
conditions. Cardinal, an established multi-frequency seismoacoustic array-processing software initially
developed to measure signal directionality and coherence via conventional beamforming, has recently
undergone substantial improvement with the integration of an adaptive array algorithm as part of this
project [21]. This novel approach represents a major methodological advancement, significantly
expanding Cardinal’s effectiveness by determining optimal subarray configuration for each frequency
band. The algorithm boosts signal coherence across all frequency bands making it particularly useful
for detecting infrasonic signatures at lower SNR, resolving multiple simultaneous arrivals, and
accurately estimating source azimuths under challenging noise conditions.

Unlike traditional array processing, which relies on fixed sensor configurations and static detection
thresholds, Cardinal's adaptive framework dynamically adjusts array configurations in response to
specific frequency bands and anticipated phase velocities. This approach maximizes directional
sensitivity toward potential event sources while actively suppressing coherent background noise
sources via the implementation of frequency-dependent adaptive detection thresholding. This
framework allows Cardinal to predict the optimal set of parameters to improve signal coherence while
substantially reducing detection ambiguities arising from atmospheric propagation complexities and
variable noise environments.

 26

Cardinal’s adaptive detection thresholding functions by setting dynamic threshold values calibrated to
the instantaneous noise field. This method markedly reduces false detections and significantly
improves detection confidence, especially for infrasonic signals previously obscured within
conventional array-processing approaches. Such advancements improve the reliability of event
identification, particularly important when analyzing signals that exhibit weak amplitudes or subtle
waveform structures.

Validation tests of the improved Cardinal software, using controlled acoustic experiments and real-
world infrasonic signals from documented events, have demonstrated notable improvements in signal
detection, directional accuracy, and classification performance compared to earlier versions of the
software. The updated version consistently improves signal characterization at higher frequencies and
provides more accurate back-azimuth estimates. Figure 5 demonstrates Cardinal’s improved
capabilities on two distinct bolide events captured at separate infrasound arrays.

Beyond natural bolide detection, Cardinal’s adaptive capabilities are particularly relevant to strategic
monitoring contexts, including the surveillance of hypersonic vehicles, spacecraft re-entries, and
surface or low-altitude explosive events. The ability to detect subtle infrasonic signatures under
complex atmospheric and noise conditions greatly increases the reliability of infrasonic surveillance
systems, directly informing strategic decision-making, planetary defense assessments, and hazard
monitoring strategies.

The addition of adaptive array processing methodologies to the existing Cardinal software constitutes
a significant step forward in infrasonic signal processing. By enabling dynamic and optimized detection
configurations, improved noise mitigation, and enhanced directional estimation, Cardinal now
provides a substantially more robust and effective analytical tool. This adaptive improvement
emphasizes substantial progress in infrasonic detection technology, furthering the practical and
scientific interpretation of atmospheric hypersonic phenomena.

6. GROUND TRUTH AND SIGNATURE DATASETS
Accurate characterization and discrimination of elongated-trajectory atmospheric entry events
fundamentally depend on robust observational datasets and empirical benchmarks. The ability to
effectively distinguish between natural meteoroid phenomena and hypersonic vehicles, spacecraft, or
orbital debris re-entries requires extensive observational resources and well-characterized, publicly
available reference data. To address critical observational gaps, this project developed and curated
several datasets, documented in technical reports and peer-reviewed publications. These datasets
include publicly sourced compilations of foreign hypersonic vehicle test events, orbital and artificial
re-entry events, historical bolide infrasound observations, and curated joint optical-infrasonic
observations of regional meteors.

6.1. Publicly Sourced Dataset of Foreign Hypersonic Vehicle Tests
As part of this project, an exploratory data-mining investigation was conducted to compile publicly
available data on foreign hypersonic vehicle flight tests (Sawal & Silber, 2025a). This dataset aggregates
various parameters, including approximate flight speeds, geographic locations, event timing, and
related publicly accessible technical details, providing boundary conditions and ground-truth
information. Given the scarcity of openly documented artificial hypersonic entry events, this dataset
provides an unprecedented open-source reference for validating trajectory models, acoustic
propagation techniques, and event discrimination methods.

6.2. Compiled Dataset of Orbital and Artificial Re-entries
In parallel, the project developed a publicly accessible dataset compiling open-source observations of
orbital debris re-entries, spacecraft returns, and artificial atmospheric entry events (Sawal & Silber,
2025b). This database provides documented re-entry trajectories, times, geographic coverage, and
related empirical parameters critical for validating acoustic and optical propagation modeling
methodologies. The re-entry events documented in this dataset span various entry conditions and
aerodynamic regimes, offering empirical data required for characterizing acoustic signatures of
artificial re-entries. Such comprehensive datasets are essential references for forensic analysis of
unanticipated re-entry events and strategic surveillance efforts.

6.3. Curated Historical Bolide Infrasound Dataset (1960–1972)
A curated historical bolide infrasound dataset covering the period 1960–1972 was produced through
comprehensive curation and formal publication of previously digitized archival infrasonic recordings
from Cold War-era networks (Silber & Whitaker, 2025). This dataset provides critical long-term
observational constraints for evaluating infrasonic signatures, bolide fragmentation mechanisms, and
energy deposition processes across diverse event parameters. The availability of this historically
significant dataset directly supports validation and refinement of empirical infrasonic period–yield
relationships and source characterization methodologies, significantly extending the observational
baseline required for reliable bolide discrimination and event reconstruction.

6.4. Curated Dataset of Regional Meteor Events (Optical–Infrasonic)
To address the critical observational need for robust characterization of high-altitude atmospheric
events, this project produced and curated a unique dataset containing simultaneous optical and
infrasonic observations of 71 regional meteor events recorded between 2006 and 2011 (Silber et al.,
2025a). Currently, this dataset represents the largest and most comprehensive publicly available
compilation of simultaneously recorded optical and acoustic signatures of regional meteor events

 29

worldwide. Each event in the dataset provides detailed infrasonic waveforms paired directly with
optical trajectory data and fragmentation characteristics, offering essential boundary conditions and
empirical benchmarks. This unique integration enables systematic investigations of meteoroid
fragmentation dynamics, ablation, infrasonic waveform morphology, and acoustic-optical
correlations. As a result, this dataset substantially advances methodologies for accurate
characterization and reliable discrimination of elongated-trajectory atmospheric entries, directly
contributing to planetary defense, global atmospheric monitoring, and strategic surveillance
capabilities. The availability of such comprehensive, rigorously documented datasets establishes an
indispensable empirical foundation for refining and validating multi-modal sensing approaches and
infrasonic source characterization methodologies.

7. OUTSTANDING TECHNICAL CHALLENGES AND PATH FORWARD
Despite significant advancements achieved in this project regarding the detection, characterization,
and interpretation of high-altitude elongated-trajectory atmospheric events, several key technical
challenges remain, presenting critical avenues for further research. Addressing these outstanding issues
is essential to further advance the precision, reliability, and operational effectiveness of infrasonic
monitoring capabilities. It is important to note that the challenges and directions described herein
represent only a subset of potential research avenues and do not constitute an exhaustive list.

7.1. Atmospheric Acoustic Propagation Accuracy
A significant remaining challenge is accurately modeling atmospheric acoustic propagation. Current
infrasonic models commonly rely on averaged atmospheric conditions or retrospective numerical
weather re-analysis datasets, insufficiently capturing real-time atmospheric variations in wind profiles,
temperature gradients, and wave ducting structures. These inaccuracies introduce considerable
uncertainty in predictions of infrasonic amplitudes, arrival times, and waveform characteristics,
thereby limiting precise event characterization and localization. Future research should prioritize
integrating real-time atmospheric data from advanced numerical weather prediction models, alongside
supplementary meteorological data from platforms such as radiosondes and lidar systems. Combining
these datasets with refined trajectory modeling approaches, such as BIBEX-M (Silber, 2025b), would
substantially improve source localization accuracy.

7.2. Signal Detection and Noise Mitigation
Although significant progress has been achieved through adaptive array processing techniques,
exemplified by the Cardinal software package (Ronac Giannone et al., 2025), difficulties remain in
detecting and characterizing infrasonic signals embedded within complex ambient noise
environments. Continued development and implementation of advanced signal-processing methods,
potentially incorporating machine-learning approaches trained on extensive, carefully labeled
databases, could improve detection thresholds, facilitate automated identification of overlapping
signals, and enhance acoustic source discrimination.

7.3. Refinement of Empirical Energy Scaling Relations
Empirical infrasonic period–yield relationships have advanced significantly, incorporating event-
specific source parameters (Silber et al., 2025b). Nevertheless, uncertainties persist due to inherent
variability associated with meteoroid composition, internal structure, fragmentation dynamics, and
aerodynamic properties. Continued collection and analysis of empirical data from both natural bolide
events and controlled artificial re-entry campaigns remain critical to further refine and constrain these
energy relations. Furthermore, extending the application of refined infrasonic period–yield
relationships to surface-based explosions and lower-altitude energetic events may increase their
applicability in forensic explosion analysis, strategic surveillance, and treaty verification contexts.
Lastly, amplitude-based energy relations present a complex challenge due to their pronounced
susceptibility to propagation path effects and atmospheric non-linearities. Future research should
rigorously test amplitude-based energy estimation approaches using statistical methodologies
developed during this project.

7.4. Multi-Modal Physical Source Characterization
Accurate inference of physical source properties, including aerodynamic strength, density, structural
composition, and fragmentation characteristics, remains challenging due to limited availability of

 31

simultaneous multi-modal observations. While combined optical-infrasonic analysis provided
important constraints (Silber and Sawal, 2025; Silber et al., 2025b), uncertainties remain significant,
particularly for artificial objects such as orbital debris, spacecraft re-entries, or hypersonic platforms.
Expanded coordinated measurement campaigns incorporating multi-modal sensing platforms
(including optical telescopes, ground-based infrasonic and seismic arrays, airborne sensors, satellite-
based observations, and GPS-based measurements) would produce richer and more comprehensive
datasets, advancing source characterization methodologies.

7.5. Establishment of Unified Multi-Modal Observational Archives
A significant strategic limitation is the current lack of unified, standardized, and curated databases
encompassing optical, infrasonic, seismic, satellite-derived, and meteorological data. Observational
datasets remain fragmented across diverse platforms and institutions, complicating comprehensive
analysis and operational integration (Sawal and Silber, 2025a,b; Silber and Whitaker, 2025). The
development of centralized, quality-controlled archives with standardized metadata, calibrated
measurements, and quantified uncertainties would greatly facilitate advanced data analytics, enable
robust algorithm training and validation, and streamline operational monitoring capabilities.

7.6. Interdisciplinary Collaboration
Addressing these outstanding technical and strategic challenges requires continued interdisciplinary
collaboration integrating atmospheric physics, acoustic propagation modeling, robust signal
processing methodologies, comprehensive data fusion approaches, and advanced computational
techniques. By systematically pursuing these avenues, future research has the potential to expand
infrasonic monitoring performance, reliability of event characterization, and effectiveness of strategic
surveillance and planetary defense frameworks, enabling improved detection and robust
discrimination of high-altitude elongated-trajectory atmospheric events.

 methodology that systematically classifies bolide fragmentation events using radiometric data
 from NASA JPL’s Center for Near-Earth Object Studies (CNEOS), improving source
 characterization and energy deposition analysis (Silber and Sawal, 2025).
  • Empirical infrasonic energy relationships: Developed refined multiparametric period–yield
 relations specifically calibrated for elongated-trajectory bolides, explicitly incorporating
 fragmentation altitude, trajectory geometry, and atmospheric propagation conditions (Silber
      et al., 2025b).

Observational validation through case studies
  • Comprehensive observational analyses of bolide events: Conducted detailed observational
 case studies, including the 2008 Tajikistan bolide, the 2023 Australian superbolide, the 2020
 Earth-grazing event, and two regional bolide events, providing robust empirical validation of
 theoretical shockwave propagation, fragmentation dynamics, and infrasonic energy deposition
 models (Silber, 2024b; Wilson et al., 2025).

Methodological advances and standardized frameworks
  • Trajectory geometry and azimuthal deviation modeling (BIBEX-M): Established a robust
 quantitative model (BIBEX-M) for predicting maximum infrasonic back-azimuth deviations
 as a function of bolide entry angles, significantly enhancing geolocation accuracy and
 confidence in event characterization (Silber, 2025).
  • Standardized infrasonic interpretation and analytical guidelines: Developed explicit,
 standardized infrasonic signal interpretation methodologies, reducing ambiguity, analyst-
 driven variability, and improving reproducibility of infrasonic event characterization (Silber,
     2024a; Silber, 2025a).

Multi-modal sensing, data fusion, and infrasound signature repositories
  • Advanced adaptive infrasound processing techniques (Cardinal): Implemented and
 demonstrated improved infrasonic detection and characterization through adaptive array-
 processing methods (Cardinal), enabling accurate extraction of acoustic signals under
 challenging atmospheric and noise conditions (Ronac Giannone et al., 2025).
  • Artificial vehicle and orbital re-entry event databases: Curated comprehensive publicly sourced
 datasets for foreign hypersonic tests and orbital debris re-entries, providing essential empirical

 33

 benchmarks supporting multi-modal observational analyses, algorithm validation, and cross-
 validation efforts (Sawal and Silber, 2025a,b).
 • Curated historical bolide infrasonic dataset (1960–1972): Digitized, curated, and analyzed
 historical bolide infrasonic records, providing indispensable long-term empirical datasets for
 calibration, validation, and enhanced characterization of elongated-trajectory high-altitude
 atmospheric entries (Silber & Whitaker, 2025).
 • Regional meteor dataset (2006–2011): Curated a detailed regional meteor dataset incorporating
 simultaneous optical and infrasonic observations, facilitating comprehensive comparative
 analyses (Silber et al., 2025a, submitted).

Strategic implications and global monitoring capabilities
 • Integrated strategic monitoring framework: Synthesized methodological and empirical
 advancements into a cohesive operational framework, enhancing reliability in discrimination,
 accuracy in geolocation, and precision in characterization of elongated-trajectory atmospheric
 entries, significantly advancing planetary defense capabilities, global atmospheric monitoring
 effectiveness, and strategic surveillance methodologies.

These accomplishments represent substantial advancements in our understanding, detection, and
characterization of elongated-trajectory high-altitude atmospheric events, providing robust
methodologies, validated empirical models, and comprehensive datasets essential for current and
future planetary defense, global atmospheric monitoring, and strategic event discrimination initiatives.
