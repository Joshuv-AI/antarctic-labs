On the Semantics of Generative SPARQL
Ratan Bahadur Thapa, Steffen Staab

Abstract
We extend SPARQL with a generative query construct, called GenOp , whose evaluation calls a language model and produces typed solution mappings. We define the semantics of the GenOp in the query in a way that maintains the fixed-dataset assumption, on which formal semantics of SPARQL build, and extend solution mappings with values generated by the language model. We formalize the semantics of the extended language over these mappings using a compatibility relation that generalizes equality and supports similarity-based matching between RDF terms and generated values. We analyze the semantic consequences of generative query patterns, focusing on mapping-level recursion induced by the reuse of generated bindings. Under deterministic bounded generation and finite candidate coverage assumptions, we characterize acyclic and stratified fragments with fixpoint semantics, establish algebraic equivalence and semantics-preserving rewrite rules, and provide an executable evaluation method; and we show that data and combined complexity coincide with those of standard SPARQL.



1
Introduction

SPARQL
[
33
]
is the de facto standard for querying RDF
[
27
]
. The semantics of SPARQL query patterns is defined in terms of (partial) solution mappings, with operators analogous to relational-algebra operators
[
8
]
and evaluated with respect to a fixed RDF graph
[
31
,
32
]
. All semantic characterizations, equivalence results, and algebraic rewrite rules are stated relative to an underlying RDF graph that remains fixed during query evaluation
[
36
]
.

Recent knowledge graph centric applications increasingly require
exploratory enrichment
over existing datasets
[
22
]
, such as synthesizing missing attributes, proposing related entities, or producing textual descriptions. Large language models (LLMs) can generate such values on demand, but current practice treats calls to language models operationally, as external procedures, middleware invocations, or prompt-based post-processing, outside the query algebra
[
42
,
37
,
24
,
35
,
9
,
28
]
. As a result, their interaction with SPARQL operators is not governed by the declarative semantics of the query language, and standard questions about query equivalence, recursion, and termination remain outside the scope of these works.

One way to formally capture the semantics of operators that generate new values at query time is to relax the fixed-dataset assumption and allow the queried graph to be discovered during evaluation, as in link-traversal querying
[
18
]
. However, such approaches rely on dynamic data discovery and require specialized semantics that preclude algebraic equivalence, recursion, and fixpoint reasoning under a fixed graph
[
18
,
14
,
21
]
. In contrast, we aim to preserve the fixed-dataset assumption of SPARQL while
extending only the space of solution mappings
under a bounded generation assumption.

We integrate generative inference into the SPARQL algebra through a generative query pattern
GenOp
. A
GenOp
instantiates a prompt template using the currently bound variables, invokes a specified generative model, parses finitely many outputs, and returns them as bindings for designated output variables.

At the semantic level, our central design choice is to lift generation to the level of solution mappings rather than RDF graphs. To support algebraic composition with standard SPARQL operators, we define query semantics over
typed solution mappings
that distinguish RDF-derived bindings from generated bindings. Each binding records its origin, keeping evaluation relative to a fixed RDF graph. To combine symbolic and generated values, we replace equality-based compatibility with a generalized compatibility relation: it coincides with equality on RDF bindings and allows similarity-based matching between RDF terms and generated values, following the general spirit of similarity-based querying
[
25
,
43
,
13
]
, via deterministic
lexicalization and a variable-indexed similarity threshold.

Although
GenOp
is non-recursive syntactically, cyclic dependencies may arise when generated bindings are reused as inputs to other
GenOp
s. Such cycles occur solely at the level of extended solution mappings and induce self-referential constraints on admissible solutions. We formalize these dependencies via a consequence operator over sets of typed mappings and interpret cyclic queries using fixpoint semantics.
We restrict ourselves to the deterministic bounded-generation assumption, where each fixed model specifier and instantiated prompt returns a fixed finite parsed output set, yielding a finite generated-value universe for each query instance. Under this assumption, query evaluation reduces to fixpoint computation over a finite mapping domain and therefore terminates by standard fixpoint arguments for finite lattices
[
38
,
1
,
17
]
. Let us explore the difficulty of including value-generating LLM calls into SPARQL query processing with the following example.

Example 1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=\{\texttt{:Paris a :City}\}
be an RDF graph, and consider the following
pseudo-generative SPARQL query:

SELECT ?x ?y ?z WHERE {
?x a :City .
GenOp("Suggest a topic related to ?x
and ?z" AS ?y, GPT-4o).
GenOp("Suggest a city related to
topic ?y" AS ?z, Gemini 1.5 Pro).
}

Ideally, the first
GenOp
would behave analogously to a basic graph pattern: it should use currently available bindings and otherwise range over admissible combinations of variable assignments. Typical procedural implementations (
e.g.,

[
14
]
) require all input variables of a
GenOp
to be bound before invocation. In this query, however, each
GenOp
depends on a value produced by the other, so neither can be evaluated under such an execution model. The problem is therefore that procedural execution enforces an evaluation order that blocks mutually dependent generative calls; our semantics instead provides a declarative interpretation that admits jointly supported bindings.

Under our semantics (see Appendix
A.1
), evaluation is defined over sets of typed solution mappings, and the basic graph pattern yields the initial mapping
\mu_{0}={?x\mapsto\texttt{:Paris}}
. Each
GenOp
induces a mapping transformer. A typed mapping
\mu\supseteq\mu_{0}
assigning
?y
and
?z
is admissible iff
?y
is among the finitely many outputs generated from the first
GenOp
prompt instantiated with
\mu(?x)
and
\mu(?z)
, and
?z
is analogously supported by the second
GenOp
prompt instantiated with
\mu(?y)
.

The two
GenOp
occurrences therefore induce a cyclic dependency over the
mapping domain. The declarative solution set is given by the relevant fixpoint
semantics, while executable evaluation validates finite candidates against the
recursive equations under the candidate-coverage assumptions stated in
Sect.
6
.

Overall, we isolate a semantic foundation for hybrid symbolic–generative querying in which generative operators are formalized as
context-sensitive transformers of solution mappings
. Their interaction is defined declaratively by fixpoint semantics over finite, typed mapping domains, supporting formal reasoning about correctness, termination, and equivalence for queries that incorporate generative inference.

Our contributions are as follows
. (i) We extend SPARQL with a generative operator
GenOp
and define a denotational semantics over typed solution mappings together with a generalized compatibility relation. Generated values remain confined to solution mappings and do not induce fact derivation, distinguishing the semantics from existential-rule languages and chase-based formalisms
[
1
,
6
,
5
]
.
Recursion is interpreted over mappings
rather than by model construction, distinguishing our approach from answer-set and stable-model approaches
[
34
,
29
]
.
(ii) We characterize acyclic, stratified, and cyclic generative patterns and provide fixpoint semantics for the recursive cases. Recursive admissibility is defined by a self-support condition. The underlying fixpoint theory is classical
[
38
,
1
]
, while its application to
context-dependent
, deterministic set-valued mapping transformers over solution mappings is
new
. (iii) We establish algebraic equivalence results and semantics-preserving rewrite rules for monotone and stratified fragments with respect to the fixpoint semantics over typed solution mappings, identifying both the preserved standard SPARQL algebraic laws and
new algebraic rewritings
in the presence of context-dependent generative patterns. (iv) We provide an executable realization of the recursive semantics by reducing evaluation to self-support checking over finite candidate mapping sets localized to strongly connected components of the dependency graph. This evaluation avoids chase-based reasoning and yields soundness and completeness relative to candidate coverage, following established treatments of logic programs with external operators
[
12
]
.Under the bounded-generation assumption and finite candidate coverage assumptions, both data and combined complexity coincide with those of standard SPARQL evaluation
[
31
]
(Section
7
).

2
Related Work

The formal semantics of SPARQL as an algebra over solution mappings, together with
results on containment, entailment and optimization under a fixed RDF dataset, are well
established
[
32
,
3
,
7
,
26
,
23
]
.
Our work builds on this foundation but departs from it by allowing solution
mappings to be extended by generative operators during evaluation.

A distinct line of SPARQL extension for the Web of Linked Data has formalized semantics and computability under full-Web and reachability-based interpretations
[
18
,
20
]
, examined the empirical behavior and limitations of link-traversal query execution over heterogeneous Web data
[
39
]
, and proposed dedicated Web-of-Data query languages that separate navigation from query evaluation, such as LDQL
[
19
]
. These approaches address open-world data discovery and computability over potentially unbounded graphs.

Recent systems integrate LLMs into knowledge-graph querying pipelines. SparqLLM invokes LLMs as auxiliary services during SPARQL execution
[
28
]
, while other work uses declarative orchestration of LLM-assisted API and database calls around existing query engines
[
24
]
. Related approaches combine retrieval and generation in pipeline architectures
[
41
]
. In all cases, language model invocation remains external to the query algebra and is treated operationally, with generated outputs consumed procedurally rather than defined by the query semantics.

Related relational data-management work has also studied SQL and SQL-adjacent query processing with language-model calls, including hybrid querying over relational databases and LLMs, SQL-style querying of LLMs, and query engines over unstructured data
[
42
,
35
,
9
,
37
]
. These works establish model invocation as a useful operational primitive in query processing and study how such calls can be exposed through SQL-style syntax, user-defined functions, physical operators, or dedicated execution engines. In all cases, focus is primarily execution-oriented and SQL-centric: model calls are incorporated as callable components of a query pipeline, without developing a formal denotational semantics for generated bindings inside a solution-mapping algebra.

Fixpoint semantics for recursive queries originate in deductive databases and
logic programming
[
38
,
1
]
, with
stratified and well-founded semantics providing principled treatments of
negation
[
34
,
40
]
. Existential rules and
Datalog
±
extend these ideas via domain-expanding inference

[
6
,
5
]
. Our setting differs fundamentally: the RDF
domain remains fixed, no facts are derived, and recursion is resolved over a finite
space of typed solution mappings. Treatments of logic programs with external
operators
[
12
]
are conceptually closest, but does not address algebraic query languages or mapping-level recursion under fixed-dataset semantics.

3
Syntax and Semantics

We now introduce the syntax of generative SPARQL and define its denotational semantics in terms of typed solution mappings.

Let
\mathcal{V}
,
\mathcal{I}
,
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{L}
be countably infinite disjoint sets of
variables
,
Internationalized Resource Identifiers
(
IRIs
),
blank nodes
and
literals
, respectively. Let
\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{B}\cup\mathcal{L}
be a set of RDF terms, and let
\mathcal{T}_{\texttt{gen}}
be a disjoint set of
generated terms
. An
RDF triple
is an element of
(\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{B})\times\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{T}
, and a
triple pattern
is an element of
(\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{L}\cup\mathcal{V})\times(\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{V})\times(\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{L}\cup\mathcal{V})
. An
RDF graph

\mathcal{G}
is a finite set of RDF triples, and a
basic graph pattern (
BGP
)
is a finite set of triple patterns.

A generative SPARQL query is a
graph pattern

\mathcal{P}
defined by the following grammar:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\ \texttt{BGP}\mid\ \texttt{GenOp}(\pi(X),Y,\mathcal{M})\mid\ \texttt{Filter}_{\,\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{P})

\displaystyle\ \mid\ \texttt{Proj}_{\,L}(\mathcal{P})\mid\mathcal{P}\,\texttt{Union}\,\mathcal{P}\mid\mathcal{P}\,\texttt{Join}\,\mathcal{P}

(1)

\displaystyle\,\mid\ \mathcal{P}\,\texttt{Minus}\,\mathcal{P}\mid\mathcal{P}\,\texttt{Diff}_{\,\mathcal{F}}\,\mathcal{P}\mid\ \mathcal{P}\,\texttt{Opt}_{\,\mathcal{F}}\,\mathcal{P},

where
\texttt{GenOp}(\pi(X),Y,\mathcal{M})
is a
generative pattern
with prompt template
\pi
containing placeholders
X\subseteq\mathcal{V}
, output variables
Y\subseteq\mathcal{V}
with
X\cap Y=\varnothing
, and generative model specifier
\mathcal{M}
(abstract);
L\subseteq\mathcal{V}
is a projection list, and
\mathcal{F}
is a filter formula built from atoms
\texttt{bound}(v)
,
(v=c)
,
(v=v^{\prime})
s.t.
v,v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}
and
c\in\mathcal{T}
using logical connectives
\wedge
and
\neg
. The set of variables in a graph pattern
\mathcal{P}
is denoted by
\texttt{var}(\mathcal{P})
, and the size of
\mathcal{P}
,
i.e.,
the number of symbols in its writing, by
|\mathcal{P}|
.

Definition 1
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a graph pattern. A
BGP
or generative
GenOP
pattern
Q
is said to occur in
\mathcal{P}
, written
Q\preceq\mathcal{P}
, if
Q
is a syntactic subpattern of
\mathcal{P}
, defined inductively by: (i) if
\mathcal{P}=Q
, then
Q\preceq\mathcal{P}
; (ii) if
\mathcal{P}=\texttt{Filter}_{\,\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{P}^{\prime})
or
\mathcal{P}=\texttt{Proj}_{\,L}(\mathcal{P}^{\prime})
, then
Q\preceq\mathcal{P}
iff
Q\preceq\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
; (iii) if
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Op}\,\mathcal{P}_{2}
for
\texttt{Op}\in\{\texttt{Join},\texttt{Union},\texttt{Minus},\texttt{Diff},\texttt{Opt}\}
, then
Q\preceq\mathcal{P}
iff
Q\preceq\mathcal{P}_{1}
or
Q\preceq\mathcal{P}_{2}
.

Let
\{\}
be the empty graph pattern, with
{\llbracket\{\}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}=\{\emptyset\}
and
\texttt{var}(\{\})=\emptyset
.

Definition 2
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a graph pattern and let
Q\preceq\mathcal{P}
be a designated
occurrence. The
surrounding pattern
of
Q
in
\mathcal{P}
, denoted

\mathcal{P}\setminus\{Q\}
, is obtained by replacing only that occurrence by the
empty pattern
\{\}
and then
simplifying the result by the following pruning
\leadsto
rules:

1.

\texttt{Filter}_{\,\mathcal{F}}(\{\})\leadsto\{\}
and
\texttt{Proj}_{\,L}(\{\})\leadsto\{\}
;

2.

For the
\texttt{Op}\in\{\texttt{Join},\texttt{Union},\texttt{Minus},\texttt{Diff},\texttt{Opt}\}
and graph pattern
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
,

\{\}\ \texttt{Op}\ \mathcal{P}^{\prime}\leadsto\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\ \texttt{Op}\ \{\}\leadsto\mathcal{P}^{\prime};

provided that the resulting graph pattern is
well formed
.

Definition 3
.

(Well formed)
A graph pattern
\mathcal{P}
defined by the grammar
3
is
well-formed
if it satisfies the following conditions: (i) for every
g=\texttt{GenOp}(\pi(X),Y,\mathcal{M})
occurrence such that
g\preceq\mathcal{P}
,
X\subseteq\texttt{var}(\mathcal{P}\setminus\{g\})\setminus Y
; (ii) for every filter
\texttt{Filter}_{\,\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{P})
,
\texttt{var}(\mathcal{F})\subseteq\texttt{var}(\mathcal{P})
; and (iii) for every projection
\texttt{Proj}_{\,L}(\mathcal{P})
,
L\subseteq\texttt{var}(\mathcal{P})
.

We now define solution mappings, typing, and compatibility.

Definition 4
.

A
typed term
is a pair
(t,\tau)
where either (i)
t\in\mathcal{T}
and
\tau=\texttt{rdf}
, or (ii)
t\in\mathcal{T}_{\texttt{gen}}
and
\tau=\texttt{gen}
. A
typed solution mapping
is a partial function
\mu:\mathcal{V}\rightharpoonup(\mathcal{T}\times\{\texttt{rdf}\})\cup(\mathcal{T}_{\texttt{gen}}\times\{\texttt{gen}\})
with domain
\texttt{dom}_{\mu}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
.

Let
\Sigma
be a finite alphabet and
\Sigma^{*}
be the set of all finite strings over
\Sigma
.

Definition 5
.

A
canonical lexicalization
consists of fixed deterministic functions

\texttt{Canon}:\mathcal{T}\to\Sigma^{*}\quad\text{and}\quad\texttt{Lex}:\mathcal{T}_{\texttt{gen}}\to\Sigma^{*},

where
Canon
maps each RDF term to a canonical string and
Lex
maps each generated term to its textual realization.

Remark 1
.

In an implementation, literals can be lexicalized by their lexical forms. IRIs
can be lexicalized by a deterministic label policy, for example by using an
associated
rdfs:label
when available and otherwise a stable rendering of
the IRI. Blank nodes can be lexicalized by a stable system-defined identifier or
by a deterministic bounded-neighbourhood description. We require only that the
lexicalization policy is fixed during evaluation; the semantics does not require
labels or blank-node descriptions to be semantically complete.

Definition 6
.

Let
\texttt{Str}_{\simeq}:\Sigma^{*}\times\Sigma^{*}\to[0,1]
be a symmetric similarity function, and let
\theta:\mathcal{V}\to(0,1]
be a variable-specific
threshold function
(default
1
). For any two typed terms
(t,\tau),(t^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})
, we define
Sim
as,

\texttt{Sim}((t,\tau),(t^{\prime},\tau^{\prime}))=\\
\begin{cases}1\hskip 93.89418pt\texttt{if}&(\tau=\tau^{\prime}=\texttt{rdf})\wedge(t=t^{\prime})\\
\texttt{Str}_{\simeq}(\texttt{Canon}(t),\texttt{Lex}(t^{\prime}))&\texttt{if }\tau=\texttt{rdf},\tau^{\prime}=\texttt{gen}\\
\texttt{Str}_{\simeq}(\texttt{Lex}(t),\texttt{Canon}(t^{\prime}))&\texttt{if }\tau=\texttt{gen},\ \tau^{\prime}=\texttt{rdf}\\
\texttt{Str}_{\simeq}(\texttt{Lex}(t),\texttt{Lex}(t^{\prime}))&\texttt{if }\tau=\tau^{\prime}=\texttt{gen}\end{cases}

Definition 7

(Variable-indexed similarity)
.

For each variable
v\in\mathcal{V}
, let
\approx_{v}
be the binary relation on typed terms defined by

\alpha\approx_{v}\beta\quad\text{iff}\quad\texttt{Sim}(\alpha,\beta)\geq\theta(v).

Since only finitely many typed terms can bind a given variable under the bounded-generation assumption, the reflexive and transitive closure of
\approx_{v}
therefore induces finitely many equivalence classes; we fix a deterministic total order
\preceq
on typed terms, and write
\texttt{rep}_{v}(\alpha)
for the
\preceq
-minimal representative of the equivalence class of
\alpha
under
\approx_{v}
.

Definition 8
.

For a variable
v
and typed terms
\alpha,\beta
, we define
canonical compatibility
,

\texttt{Compat}_{v}(\alpha,\beta)=\top\quad\text{iff}\quad\texttt{rep}_{v}(\alpha)=\texttt{rep}_{v}(\beta).

Definition 9
.

Two typed solution mappings
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
are
compatible
, written

\mu_{1}\sim\mu_{2}
, iff for every variable

v\in\texttt{dom}_{\mu_{1}}\cap\texttt{dom}_{\mu_{2}}
,

\texttt{Compat}_{v}(\mu_{1}(v),\mu_{2}(v))=\top.

If
\mu_{1}\sim\mu_{2}
, their
canonical union

\mu_{1}\uplus\mu_{2}
is the
mapping with domain
\texttt{dom}_{\mu_{1}}\cup\texttt{dom}_{\mu_{2}}
defined by

(\mu_{1}\uplus\mu_{2})(v)=\begin{cases}\texttt{rep}_{v}(\mu_{1}(v))&\text{if }v\in\texttt{dom}_{\mu_{1}}\cap\texttt{dom}_{\mu_{2}},\\
\mu_{1}(v)&\text{if }v\in\texttt{dom}_{\mu_{1}}\setminus\texttt{dom}_{\mu_{2}},\\
\mu_{2}(v)&\text{if }v\in\texttt{dom}_{\mu_{2}}\setminus\texttt{dom}_{\mu_{1}}.\end{cases}

If
\mu_{1}\not\sim\mu_{2}
, the union
\mu_{1}\uplus\mu_{2}
is undefined.

By slight abuse of notation, for a graph pattern
\mathcal{P}
and mapping
\mu
, we write
\mu(\mathcal{P})
for the set of triples obtained by substituting the variables
\texttt{var}(\mathcal{P})
in
\mathcal{P}
according to
\mu
.

BGP
evaluation.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a
BGP
and
\mathcal{G}
an RDF graph . The evaluation of
\mathcal{P}
over
\mathcal{G}
is the set,

{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}=\{\,\mu\mid\texttt{dom}_{\mu}=\texttt{var}(\mathcal{P})\wedge\mu(\mathcal{P})\subseteq\mathcal{G}\,\},

where each binding
\mu(v)=(t,\texttt{rdf})
with
t\in\mathcal{T}
.

Generative patterns.

We model a generative pattern as a set-valued mapping that transforms an instantiated prompt into finitely many tuples of generated terms via a generative step followed by parsing, while leaving the surrounding query algebra unchanged.

A model specifier
\mathcal{M}
induces finite-valued operations
\texttt{Call}_{\mathcal{M}}:\Sigma^{*}\to 2^{\Sigma^{*}}
and
\texttt{Parse}_{\mathcal{M}}
, where
\texttt{Call}_{\mathcal{M}}
returns answer strings and
\texttt{Parse}_{\mathcal{M}}
returns tuples of generated terms. For every fixed query instance
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{G})
, we assume that iterating the generative occurrences of
\mathcal{P}
from the RDF bindings in
\mathcal{G}
yields finitely many generated terms. For complexity results, we assume constants
n_{1},n_{2}
such that

|\texttt{Call}_{\mathcal{M}}(s)|\leq n_{1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad|\texttt{Parse}_{\mathcal{M}}(a)|\leq n_{2}

for all relevant
\mathcal{M},s,a
, and that generated terms have bounded representation length.

Definition 10
.

Let
g=\texttt{GenOp}(\pi(X),Y,\mathcal{M})
, and let
Y=\langle y_{1},\dots,y_{k}\rangle
be a fixed ordering, where
k=|Y|
. Let
\texttt{Call}_{\mathcal{M}}:\Sigma^{*}\to 2^{\Sigma^{*}}
and
\texttt{Parse}_{\mathcal{M}}:\Sigma^{*}\to 2^{\mathcal{T}_{\texttt{gen}}^{|Y|}}
return finite sets. Let
\mathcal{G}
be an RDF graph and
S
a context supply,
i.e.,
a set of typed solution mappings, such that every
\mu_{c}\in S
satisfies
X\subseteq\texttt{dom}_{\mu_{c}}
. The evaluation of
g
with respect to
S
over
\mathcal{G}
is the set:

{\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}=\bigcup_{\mu_{c}\in S}\left\{\mu_{c}\uplus\nu\middle|\ \begin{array}[]{l}\mathbf{a}\in\texttt{Call}_{\mathcal{M}}(\pi^{[\mu_{c}]}),\\
(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})\in\texttt{Parse}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{a}),\\
\nu=\{y_{i}\mapsto(v_{i},\texttt{gen})\mid 1\leq i\leq k\},\\
\mu_{c}\sim\nu,\end{array}\right\}

where
\pi^{[\mu_{c}]}
is the prompt obtained from
\pi
by replacing each placeholder in
X
by the lexicalization of its binding under
\mu_{c}
.

Example 2
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=\{\texttt{:Paris a :City}\}
and the query:

SELECT ?x ?y ?z WHERE {
?x a :City .
GenOp("Describe the city ?x" AS ?y,
GPT-4o).
}

By evaluating
BGP
, we get context supply
\mu_{c}=\{?x\mapsto(\texttt{:Paris},\texttt{rdf})\}
for the
GenOp
. Then, prompt instantiation produces
\pi^{[\mu_{c}]}=\text{``Describe the city Paris''}
. Assume,

\texttt{Call}_{\texttt{GPT-4o}}(\pi^{[\mu_{c}]})=\{\text{``capital of France''},\text{``cultural center''}\},

and

\texttt{Parse}_{\texttt{GPT-4o}}(\text{``capital of France''})=\{(t_{1})\}\\
\texttt{Parse}_{\texttt{GPT-4o}}(\text{``cultural center''})=\{(t_{2})\}.

By the semantics of generative patterns,

{\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\{\mu_{c}\}}{}=\big\{\mu_{c}\uplus\{?y\mapsto(t_{1},\texttt{gen})\},\\
\mu_{c}\uplus\{?y\mapsto(t_{2},\texttt{gen})\}\big\}.

Filter .

Let
\mu
be a typed solution mapping. The truth value of a filter formula
\mathcal{F}
under
\mu
, denoted
\mathcal{F}^{\mu}\in\{\top,\bot,\varepsilon\}
(where
\varepsilon
denotes an error), is defined inductively as follows.

1.

{\texttt{bound}(v)}^{\mu}=\top
if
v\in\texttt{dom}_{\mu}
, and
{\texttt{bound}(v)}^{\mu}=\bot
otherwise.

2.

For
c\in\mathcal{T}
,
(v=c)^{\mu}=\varepsilon
if
v\notin\texttt{dom}_{\mu}
;

(v=c)^{\mu}=\top
if
\mu(v)=(c,\texttt{rdf})
or
\texttt{Compat}_{v}(\mu(v),(c,\texttt{rdf}))=\top
; otherwise,
(v=c)^{\mu}=\bot
.

3.

(v=v^{\prime})^{\mu}=\varepsilon
if
\{v,v^{\prime}\}\nsubseteq\texttt{dom}_{\mu}
;

(v=v^{\prime})^{\mu}=\top
if
\mu(v)=\mu(v^{\prime})=(t,\texttt{rdf})
for some
t\in\mathcal{T}
, or if
\texttt{Compat}_{v}(\mu(v),\mu(v^{\prime}))=\top
;
otherwise,
(v=v^{\prime})^{\mu}=\bot
.

4.

(\neg\mathcal{F})^{\mu}=\top
if
\mathcal{F}^{\mu}=\bot
;

(\neg\mathcal{F})^{\mu}=\bot
if
\mathcal{F}^{\mu}=\top
;

(\neg\mathcal{F})^{\mu}=\varepsilon
if
\mathcal{F}^{\mu}=\varepsilon
.

5.

(\mathcal{F}_{1}\wedge\mathcal{F}_{2})^{\mu}={\mathcal{F}_{1}}^{\mu}\wedge{\mathcal{F}_{2}}^{\mu}
.

Graph Patterns.

The evaluation of a graph pattern
\mathcal{P}
over an RDF graph
\mathcal{G}
, denoted

{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}
, returns a set of typed solution mappings and is defined recursively below relative to a context supply
S
. When the context supply is fixed or immaterial, we write
{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}
as shorthand for
{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}
. The clauses below define a
single-pass
(one-step) evaluation; cyclic generative dependencies are handled by the fixpoint semantics in Section
4
.

1.

If
\mathcal{P}
is a
BGP
, then
{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}
is defined as above. If
\mathcal{P}
is a
GenOp
pattern, its single-pass evaluation is
{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}
, where
S
provides the solution mappings used to instantiate the placeholder variables of its prompt template.

2.

{\llbracket\texttt{Filter}_{\,\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{P})\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}=\{\mu\in{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}\mid F^{\mu}=\top\}
;

3.

{\llbracket\texttt{Proj}_{\,L}(\mathcal{P})\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}=\{\mu_{|L}\mid\mu\in{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}\}
;

4.

\begin{aligned} {\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,\mathcal{P}_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}=\{\mu_{1}\uplus\mu_{2}\mid\mu_{1}\in{\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{1}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}},\mu_{2}\in{\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}},\\
\mu_{1}\sim\mu_{2}\};\end{aligned}

5.

{\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Union}\,\mathcal{P}_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}={\llbracket P_{1}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}\cup{\llbracket P_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}
;

6.

\begin{aligned} {\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Diff}_{\,\mathcal{F}}\,\mathcal{P}_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}=\{\mu_{1}\in{\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{1}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}\mid\forall\mu_{2}\in{\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}:\\
\mu_{1}\not\sim\mu_{2}\,\text{or}\,\mathcal{F}^{\mu_{1}\uplus\mu_{2}}=\bot\}\end{aligned}
;

7.

{\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Opt}_{\,\mathcal{F}}\,\mathcal{P}_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}={\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,\mathcal{P}_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}\cup{\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Diff}_{\,\mathcal{F}}\,\mathcal{P}_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}
;

8.

\begin{aligned} {\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Minus}\,\mathcal{P}_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}=\{\mu_{1}\in{\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{1}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}\mid\forall\mu_{2}\in{\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}:\\
\text{either}\,\mu_{1}\not\sim\mu_{2}\,\text{or}\,\texttt{dom}_{\mu_{1}}\cap\texttt{dom}_{\mu_{2}}=\varnothing\};\end{aligned}

where
\mu_{|L}
is the restriction of
\mu
to
L
. If
g\preceq\mathcal{P}
, the context supply for
g
in a single evaluation step is the set
S={\llbracket\mathcal{P}\setminus\{g\}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a well-formed graph pattern that contains no occurrence of
GenOp
. Then, for any RDF graph
\mathcal{G}
, the evaluation of
\mathcal{P}
under the extended semantics coincides with standard SPARQL evaluation under set semantics:
{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}={{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}}|_{\text{standard SPARQL}}
.

4
Fragments and Fixpoint Semantics

The preceding semantics characterize the meaning of generative SPARQL queries independently of any evaluation strategy. Section
6
presents an executable realization of these semantics under bounded generation.

4.1
Dependency Graph

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a generative SPARQL graph pattern and let
\texttt{gen}(\mathcal{P})=\{g\mid g\preceq\mathcal{P}\text{ and }g\text{ is a }\texttt{GenOp}\text{ occurrence}\}
denote the set of
GenOp
occurrences in
\mathcal{P}
. For each
g=\texttt{GenOp}(\pi(X),Y,\mathcal{M})
in
\texttt{gen}(\mathcal{P})
, let
\texttt{input}(g)
denote its set of placeholder variables
X
and
\texttt{out}(g)
its set of output variables
Y
.

Definition 11

(Dependency graph)
.

The
dependency graph
of
\mathcal{P}
is the directed graph

\overrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathcal{P}}=(\texttt{gen}(\mathcal{P}),\to_{\mathcal{P}})
,
where for
g_{i},g_{j}\in\texttt{gen}(\mathcal{P})
,

g_{i}\to_{\mathcal{P}}g_{j}\quad\text{iff}\quad\texttt{out}(g_{i})\cap\texttt{input}(g_{j})\neq\varnothing.

Definition 12
.

A nonempty subset
C\subseteq\texttt{gen}(\mathcal{P})
is a
strongly connected component (
SCC
)
of
\mathcal{P}
iff:

1.

for all
g,g^{\prime}\in C
, there exists a finite sequence
g=g_{0},\dots,g_{k}=g^{\prime}
in
C
such that
g_{i}\to_{\mathcal{P}}g_{i+1}
for all
i<k
, and a sequence from
g^{\prime}
to
g
; and

2.

C
is maximal
w.r.t.
set inclusion under this property.

The
SCC
s of
\mathcal{P}
form a partition of
\texttt{gen}(\mathcal{P})
.

Definition 13

(Acyclicity)
.

A graph pattern
\mathcal{P}
is
acyclic
iff
\overrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathcal{P}}
is acyclic.

If
\mathcal{P}
is acyclic, all
GenOp
occurrences can be evaluated in a
single pass following a topological order of

\overrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathcal{P}}
, and the result coincides with the
fixpoint semantics induced by
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}
(Cf. Section
4.3
).

Definition 14
.

(Signed Dependency Edge)
Let
g_{i}\to_{\mathcal{P}}g_{j}
be a dependency edge and let
V_{ij}=\texttt{out}(g_{i})\cap\texttt{input}(g_{j})
. Let
g_{j}\preceq\mathcal{P}
. We classify the edge by the syntactic occurrences of variable
v\in V_{ij}
in the surrounding pattern
\mathcal{P}\setminus\{g_{j}\}
.

1.

The edge is
positive
if every syntactic occurrence of every

v\in V_{ij}
in
\mathcal{P}\setminus\{g_{j}\}
is in a
positive position
, namely
under only the operators
Join
,
Union
,
Proj
, the
Join
branch of
Opt
or within a

Filter
where
v
does not occur under
\neg
.

2.

The edge is
negative
if there exists some
v\in V_{ij}
that has a
syntactic occurrence in
\mathcal{P}\setminus\{g_{j}\}
in a
negative position
,
i.e.,
within the scope of
Minus
,
Diff
, the
Diff
branch of
Opt
or under
\neg
in a

Filter
.

Example 3
.

Let
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
be the two
GenOp
occurrences in

SELECT ?x ?y ?z WHERE {
?x a :City .
GenOp("Find topic for ?x" AS ?y, GPT-4o).
GenOp("Find city for ?y" AS ?z, GPT-4o).
}

The variable
?y
occurs as a placeholder in the prompt of
g_{2}
and only in
positive positions in the surrounding pattern of
g_{2}
; hence
g_{1}\to g_{2}
is
a positive dependency. In the variant obtained by adding

MINUS { ?x :hasTopic ?y . }

the variable
?y
occurs under a negative operator in the surrounding pattern
of
g_{2}
. Thus,
g_{1}\to g_{2}
is a negative dependency, and any stratification
as in Definition
15
must satisfy

\sigma(g_{1})<\sigma(g_{2})
.

Definition 15
.

A
stratification
of
\mathcal{P}
is a function

\sigma:\texttt{gen}(\mathcal{P})\to\mathbb{N}
such that for every

g_{i}\to_{\mathcal{P}}g_{j}
:
(i) if the edge is positive then
\sigma(g_{i})\leq\sigma(g_{j})
;
(ii) if the edge is negative then
\sigma(g_{i})<\sigma(g_{j})
.
The pattern
\mathcal{P}
is
stratified
if such a function exists.

Acyclic patterns admit single-pass evaluation. Stratified patterns admit stratum-wise fixpoint semantics. Non-stratified patterns require well-founded semantics.

4.2
Consequence Operator

We handle recursion among
GenOp
patterns via an immediate consequence operator, assuming bounded generation so that, for fixed
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{G}
, the set of generated terms, and thus relevant typed solution mappings, is finite.

Let
\Omega_{\mathcal{P}}
be the finite set of all typed solution mappings over
\texttt{var}(\mathcal{P})
whose values range over
\mathcal{T}\cup\mathcal{T}_{\texttt{gen}}
.

To expose recursive dependencies, evaluation is parameterized by a
context supply

S\subseteq\Omega_{\mathcal{P}}
. For a
GenOp
pattern
g
, only those mappings in
S
that provide bindings for all placeholders
\texttt{input}(g)
are useful to instantiate the prompt,
i.e.,
context set,

S|_{g}=\{\mu\in S\mid\texttt{input}(g)\subseteq\texttt{dom}_{\mu}\},

and the evaluation of
g
relative to
S
is then
{\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S|_{g}}{}
.

Definition 16
.

The immediate consequence operator of
\mathcal{P}
is the function

\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}:2^{\Omega_{\mathcal{P}}}\to 2^{\Omega_{\mathcal{P}}},\,\,\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}(S):={\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}.

The
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}(S)
evaluates the query once while allowing each

GenOp
to draw its placeholder bindings from the current supply
S
.
Recursive behavior is captured by iterating
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}
to a fixpoint.

Proposition 2
.

If
S,S^{\prime}\subseteq\Omega_{\mathcal{P}}
satisfy

S|_{g}=S^{\prime}|_{g}
for every
GenOp
pattern
g
in
\mathcal{P}
,
then
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}(S)=\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}(S^{\prime})
.

4.3
Monotonicity and Least Fixpoint Semantics

We identify conditions for a least-fixpoint semantics by establishing monotonicity of the consequence operator.

A generative SPARQL pattern
\mathcal{P}
is monotone w.r.t. the context supply if, for every
GenOp

g\preceq\mathcal{P}
, variables in
\texttt{out}(g)
occur only in positive positions in
\mathcal{P}
, so added generated bindings cannot invalidate existing solution mappings.

Lemma 1
.

If
\mathcal{P}
is monotone with respect to the context supply, then the consequence
operator
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}
is monotone on the complete lattice

(2^{\Omega_{\mathcal{P}}},\subseteq)
:

\forall S,S^{\prime}\in 2^{\Omega_{\mathcal{P}}}:\,S\subseteq S^{\prime}\;\longrightarrow\;\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}(S)\subseteq\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}(S^{\prime}).

Proof.

Expanding the context supply
S
only increases the set of mappings available to
instantiate placeholders of
GenOp
patterns. Since each
GenOp
is defined
as a union over all admissible contexts, additional contexts can only yield
additional generated mappings. By the monotonicity condition, variables
introduced by
GenOp
patterns occur only in positive positions. Hence,
previously derived mappings cannot be invalidated, and

\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}
is monotone.
∎

If
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}
is monotone, it admits a least fixpoint with respect to
set inclusion. Under the bounded-generation assumption, this fixpoint can be
computed by iterating
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}
from the empty set.

Definition 17

(Least fixpoint semantics)
.

Let
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}
be monotone, and the ascending sequence

\{S_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
is defined by

S_{0}=\varnothing,\qquad S_{n+1}=\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}(S_{n}).

The
least fixpoint
of
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}
is
\mathrm{lfp}(\Phi_{\mathcal{P}})=\bigcup_{n\geq 0}S_{n}
,
and the meaning of
\mathcal{P}
under least-fixpoint semantics is

{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathrm{lfp}}:=\mathrm{lfp}(\Phi_{\mathcal{P}})
.

Theorem 1
.

(Existence and characterization)

If
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}
is monotone on
(2^{\Omega_{\mathcal{P}}},\subseteq)
, then it
has a least fixpoint, and

\mathrm{lfp}(\Phi_{\mathcal{P}})=\bigcup_{n\geq 0}\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}^{n}(\varnothing).

Moreover, if
\Omega_{\mathcal{P}}
is finite, the sequence stabilizes after at most

|\Omega_{\mathcal{P}}|
iterations.

For acyclic patterns, the iteration stabilizes after one step (see
B.1
), and
the fixpoint semantics coincides with standard compositional evaluation.

Theorem 2
.

Let
S^{\ast}=\mathrm{lfp}(\Phi_{\mathcal{P}})
. If
S
is any set of typed solution
mappings such that
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}(S)\subseteq S
, then
S^{\ast}\subseteq S
.
Thus,
S^{\ast}
is the minimal fixpoint model of
\mathcal{P}
.

4.4
Stratified Semantics

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a generative SPARQL pattern and let
\sigma:\texttt{gen}(\mathcal{P})\to\mathbb{N}
be a stratification. For
i\in\mathbb{N}
, let

\mathrm{Gen}^{\leq i}(\mathcal{P})=\{g\in\texttt{gen}(\mathcal{P})\mid\sigma(g)\leq i\}.

Let
\mathcal{P}^{\leq i}
denote the pattern obtained from
\mathcal{P}
by
disabling
all
GenOp
occurrences not in
\mathrm{Gen}^{\leq i}(\mathcal{P})
, that is, replacing each such occurrence by a pattern whose evaluation is empty for all context supply
S
, while leaving the remaining algebraic structure unchanged.

Definition 18
.

(Stratum-wise operator)
For each
i
, let
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}^{\leq i}}:2^{\Omega_{\mathcal{P}}}\to 2^{\Omega_{\mathcal{P}}}
defined by
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}^{\leq i}}(S):={\llbracket\mathcal{P}^{\leq i}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}
.

Definition 19
.

(Stratified semantics)
Let
\mathcal{P}
be stratified with stratification
\sigma:\texttt{gen}(\mathcal{P})\to\mathbb{N}
. Let
S_{-1}:=\varnothing
. For each stratum index
i=0,1,2,\dots
, consider the operator

S\mapsto\Phi_{\mathcal{P}^{\leq i}}(S\cup S_{i-1}),

and let
S_{i}
be its least fixpoint:

S_{i}:=\mathrm{lfp}\big(S\mapsto\Phi_{\mathcal{P}^{\leq i}}(S\cup S_{i-1})\big).

Let
m=\max\{\sigma(g)\mid g\in\texttt{gen}(\mathcal{P})\}
, with
m=0
if
\texttt{gen}(\mathcal{P})=\varnothing
. The meaning of
\mathcal{P}
under stratified semantics is given by
{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathrm{strat}}{}=S_{m}
.

Theorem 3
.

If
\mathcal{P}
is stratified and
\Omega_{\mathcal{P}}
is finite (bounded generation),
then the stratified semantics

{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathrm{strat}}

is well-defined and unique.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be stratified.
For each stratum
i
, the least fixpoint
S_{i}
is independent of the order in
which
GenOp
occurrences in stratum
i
are evaluated.

4.5
Well-Founded Semantics for Non-Stratified Patterns

For query patterns that are not stratified, we adopt the standard three-valued semantics based on an alternating fixpoint construction
[
34
,
40
]
, instantiated to typed solution mappings.

Let
\texttt{Cand}(\mathcal{P})\subseteq\Omega_{\mathcal{P}}
be a finite candidate set.
assumed to be a sound over-approximation of all typed solution mappings that may arise during evaluation of
\mathcal{P}
under bounded generation. A partial interpretation is a pair
(I^{+},I^{-})
with
I^{+},I^{-}\subseteq\texttt{Cand}(\mathcal{P})
and
I^{+}\cap I^{-}=\varnothing
, where mappings in
I^{+}
are true, mappings in
I^{-}
are false, and all others are undefined.

Definition 20
.

Let
J\subseteq\texttt{Cand}(\mathcal{P})
.
The
reduct consequence operator

\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}^{J}:2^{\texttt{Cand}(\mathcal{P})}\to 2^{\texttt{Cand}(\mathcal{P})}
is defined as

\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}^{J}(S)\coloneqq{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{},

where all negative constructs (
i.e.,

MINUS
,
DIFF
, and negated filters) are evaluated with respect to the fixed set
J
.

Under fixed
J
, the operator
S\mapsto\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}^{J}(S)
is monotone,
since all negative tests are evaluated against a constant context.

Definition 21

(Alternating fixpoint sequence)
.

Let
(I_{0}^{+},I_{0}^{-})\coloneqq(\varnothing,\varnothing)
.
For
n\geq 0
,

\displaystyle I_{n+1}^{+}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathrm{lfp}\big(S\mapsto\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}^{\texttt{Cand}(\mathcal{P})\setminus I_{n}^{-}}(S)\big),

\displaystyle I_{n+1}^{-}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\texttt{Cand}(\mathcal{P})\setminus\mathrm{lfp}\big(S\mapsto\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}^{I_{n+1}^{+}}(S)\big).

Theorem 5
.

(Convergence and uniqueness)

If
\texttt{Cand}(\mathcal{P})
is finite, then the alternating fixpoint sequence in
Definition
21
stabilizes after finitely many steps at a pair

(I^{+},I^{-})
.
The induced three-valued interpretation is unique and coincides with the
well-founded model of
\mathcal{P}
over
\mathcal{G}
relative to
\texttt{Cand}(\mathcal{P})
.

Non-stratified generative SPARQL patterns,
i.e.,
patterns with cyclic negative dependencies, fall outside the monotone fragment. Under the bounded-generation assumption, such patterns admit a three-valued well-founded semantics obtained by the standard alternating-fixpoint construction over the finite mapping lattice; the construction follows classical well-founded semantics.

5
Algebraic Equivalences and Rewrite Rules

We prove equivalence results for the monotone or stratified fragments, where denotational and fixpoint semantics coincide.

Definition 22
.

Two generative SPARQL patterns
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
are
equivalent
, written
\mathcal{P}\equiv\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
, if they produce the same set of solution mappings for every RDF graph
\mathcal{G}
and every context-supply set
S
,
i.e.,

{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}={\llbracket\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}
.

SPARQL-specific equivalences.

Standard SPARQL equivalences that preserve the context supplied to
GenOp
patterns remain valid:

\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,\mathcal{P}_{2}\equiv\mathcal{P}_{2}\,\texttt{Join}\,\mathcal{P}_{1};\,\\
(\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,\mathcal{P}_{2})\,\texttt{Join}\,\mathcal{P}_{3}\equiv\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,(\mathcal{P}_{2}\,\texttt{Join}\,\mathcal{P}_{3});\\
\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,(\mathcal{P}_{2}\,\texttt{Union}\,\mathcal{P}_{3})\equiv\\
(\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,\mathcal{P}_{2})\,\texttt{Union}\,(\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,\mathcal{P}_{3});\\
\texttt{Filter}_{\,\mathcal{F}_{1}\wedge\mathcal{F}_{2}}(P)\equiv\texttt{Filter}_{\,\mathcal{F}_{1}}(\texttt{Filter}_{\,\mathcal{F}_{2}}(P));\\
\texttt{Filter}_{\,F}(\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,\mathcal{P}_{2})\equiv\texttt{Filter}_{\,F}(\mathcal{P}_{1})\,\texttt{Join}\,\mathcal{P}_{2}\\
\text{if }\texttt{var}(F)\subseteq\texttt{var}(\mathcal{P}_{1});\,\text{and}\\
\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Minus}\,\mathcal{P}_{2}\equiv\mathcal{P}_{1}\ \ \text{if }\texttt{var}(\mathcal{P}_{1})\cap\texttt{var}(\mathcal{P}_{2})=\varnothing.

The proofs follow those of SPARQL, with equality on shared variables replaced by compatibility (cf. Appendix
C
).

GenOp
-specific rewrites.

A generative pattern
g=\texttt{GenOp}(\pi(X),Y,\mathcal{M})
can be evaluated only using context mappings that bind
X
; moving
g
can therefore change the context set and the result.

We first illustrate why unrestricted reordering of GenOp patterns is unsound.

Example 4

(Unsafe GenOp reordering)
.

Let
g=\texttt{GenOp}(\pi(\{?x\}),\{?y\},M)
and consider

\mathcal{P}_{1}=\{(?s\ \texttt{:p}\ ?x)\},\,\,\mathcal{P}_{2}=\{(?s\ \texttt{:q}\ ?z)\}.

Assume
\mathcal{G}
is such that
{\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{1}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}\neq\varnothing
and
{\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}\neq\varnothing
, but no mapping in
{\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}
binds
?x
.
In
(\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,\mathcal{P}_{2})\,\texttt{Join}\,g
, the left subpattern
\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,\mathcal{P}_{2}
yields mappings binding
?x
, so the context set for
g
is non-empty and
g
may produce bindings for
?y
.
In contrast, in
\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,(\mathcal{P}_{2}\,\texttt{Join}\,g)
, the left subpattern
\mathcal{P}_{2}
yields no mapping binding
?x
, hence the context set for
g
is empty and
{\llbracket P_{2}\,\texttt{Join}\,g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}=\varnothing
.
Thus, reordering a
GenOp
across joins is not semantics-preserving in general.

We next give sufficient conditions guaranteeing that common rewrites involving
GenOp
patterns are sound.

Proposition 3
.

Let
g=\texttt{GenOp}(\pi(\varnothing),Y,M)
be a base-mode
GenOp
.
Then,
\,\forall\,\mathcal{G},\forall\,S:{\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}={\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\{\varnothing\}}{}
.

Proof.

By the base-mode convention, whenever
\texttt{input}(g)=\varnothing
the evaluation of
g

ignores the supplied context and uses the fixed context set
\{\varnothing\}
.
Hence
{\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}={\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\{\varnothing\}}{}
for all
S
.
∎

Corollary 1
.

Let
g=\texttt{GenOp}(\pi(\varnothing),Y,M)
and
\Omega_{g}={\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\varnothing}{}
. Let
\mathcal{P}
be a query pattern that contains no
GenOp
occurrences. Then,
\forall\,\mathcal{G},\forall\,S,S^{\prime}:

{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\,\texttt{Join}\,g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}\;=\;{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\,\texttt{Join}\,g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S^{\prime}}{}\;=\;{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\,\texttt{Join}\,\Omega_{g}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}.

Proposition 4
.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2}
be patterns and
g
a
GenOp
. For all
\mathcal{G},S
and
i\in\{1,2\}
, let
S_{i}:={\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{i}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}
and
S_{i}|_{g}:=\{\mu\in S_{i}\mid\texttt{input}(g)\subseteq\texttt{dom}_{\mu}\}
. If
{\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S_{1}|_{g}\cup S_{2}|_{g}}{}={\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S_{1}|_{g}}{}\cup{\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S_{2}|_{g}}{}
, then

{\llbracket(\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Union}\,\mathcal{P}_{2})\,\texttt{Join}\,g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}={\llbracket(\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,g)\,\texttt{Union}\,(\mathcal{P}_{2}\,\texttt{Join}\,g)\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}.

Proof.

In
(\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Union}\,\mathcal{P}_{2})\,\texttt{Join}\,g
, the
GenOp

g
sees exactly the union
of the solution mappings produced by the two branches, so its context is
(S_{1}|_{g}\cup S_{2}|_{g})
. By the separability assumption, evaluating
g
on this combined context produces precisely the union of the outputs obtained by evaluating
g
on each branch context separately. Joining these outputs back with their respective branch solutions, therefore, yields exactly the union of
\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,g
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}\,\texttt{Join}\,g
.
∎

Proposition 5
.

Let
g=\texttt{GenOp}(\pi(X),Y,\mathcal{M})
and let
\mathcal{F}
be a filter with
\texttt{var}(\mathcal{F})\subseteq X
. Let
\mathcal{P}
be a pattern with
Y\cap\texttt{var}(\mathcal{P})=\varnothing
and

\texttt{var}(\mathcal{F})\subseteq\texttt{var}(\mathcal{P})
.
Then,

{\llbracket\texttt{Filter}_{\,\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{P})\,\texttt{Join}\,g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}={\llbracket\texttt{Filter}_{\,\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{P}\,\texttt{Join}\,g)\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}.

Proof.

Let
S={\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}
and
S^{+}=\{\mu\in S\mid\mathcal{F}^{\mu}=\top\}
.
In
\texttt{Filter}_{\,\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{P})\,\texttt{Join}\,g
, the context supply for
g
is
S^{+}
; in

\mathcal{P}\,\texttt{Join}\,g
, it is
S
. By the
GenOp
clause,

{\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}=\bigcup_{\mu\in S}{\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\{\mu\}}{}
, hence the
extensions generated from
S^{+}
are exactly those generated from the
S^{+}
, contexts
inside the run on
S
. Every generated mapping has the form
\mu\uplus\nu
and does not change the truth value of
\mathcal{F}
because
\texttt{var}(\mathcal{F})\subseteq X
and
g
only adds fresh bindings on
Y
(up to representative normalization). Thus,
\mathcal{F}^{\mu}=\top
iff
\mathcal{F}^{\mu\uplus\nu}=\top
, so filtering after the join removes precisely the
contributions from
\mu\notin S^{+}
, yielding the same result as filtering before.
∎

Example 5
.

Let
g=\mathrm{GenOp}(\pi(\{?x\}),\{?y\},M)
, and suppose
\mathcal{P}_{1}
binds
?x
while
\mathcal{P}_{2}
does not. Then:

7
Computational Complexity

We analyze the complexity of query evaluation under the semantics of Sections
3
and
4
. We abstract from the internal cost of model inference and treat each
GenOp
as an external oracle, considering only symbolic query evaluation.

We assume the deterministic bounded-generation, finite-candidate-coverage, and exactness conditions, and abstract from oracle-call and parsing costs, treating them as
O(1)
per invocation. The computational task is evaluation membership: given a graph pattern
\mathcal{P}
, an RDF graph
\mathcal{G}
, a typed solution mapping
\mu
, and the applicable semantics (acyclic, stratified, or well-founded), decide whether
\mu
belongs to the semantic value of
\mathcal{P}
over
\mathcal{G}
,
i.e.,
whether
\mu\in{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}
.

Theorem 7
.

Under deterministic bounded generation and the finite candidate coverage
and exactness conditions, the evaluation-membership problem satisfies the following bounds:

1.

Acyclic patterns are in
PTIME
in data complexity and
PSPACE
-complete in combined complexity.

2.

Stratified patterns and non-stratified patterns under well-founded semantics are also in
PTIME
in data complexity and
PSPACE
-complete in combined complexity.

In generative SPARQL, recursion neither derives new RDF facts nor expands the data domain. Both acyclic and recursive patterns are evaluated over the same finite set of solution mappings, determined by the query and bounded generation. Recursion thus serves to validate self-support among already enumerable candidates, rather than to derive RDF facts or chase witnesses as in existential-rule systems. As a result, stratified or well-founded recursion affects only the admissibility of mappings, not the size of the search space, producing the same asymptotic complexity as acyclic evaluation. Dropping bounded-generation assumptions eliminates termination guarantees for fixpoint evaluation; in this case, generative patterns can simulate existential-rule application and chase-style reasoning, leading to undecidability
[
1
,
6
, Chapter 13]
.

8
Conclusions and Future Work

We presented a conservative, declarative extension of SPARQL with a generative pattern,
GenOp
. The proposed query semantics accommodates acyclic, stratified, and non-stratified generative dependencies via consequence operators and fixpoint constructions. Under a bounded-generation assumption, the extension preserves the established complexity bounds of SPARQL
[
32
]
.

The only form of recursion supported arises from dataflow dependencies among generative patterns, where the outputs of one
GenOp
parameterize the prompt of another. This recursion operates at the level of prompt instantiation rather than fact derivation, and no generated value becomes part of the RDF graph under query. As a result, the language extends SPARQL with controlled generative dependencies while preserving its declarative and computational properties
[
2
]
and remaining fundamentally different from Datalog-style rule languages
[
6
,
15
,
5
]
in terms of both expressiveness and complexity.

Future work includes relaxing bounded-generation assumptions, as well as incorporating Datalog-style existential predicate into
GenOp
patterns, and characterizing decidable fragments under guardedness or acyclicity conditions, following the methods for existential rules
[
10
,
16
,
4
]
, and developing optimizer support for generative operators within the SPARQL algebra.

.

2.

Step 2:
SCC
identification and summarization.

Since
?y
feeds
g_{2}
and
?z
feeds
g_{1}
, the dependency graph contains one

SCC
:

\mathcal{C}=\{g_{1},g_{2}\}.

The externally supplied variables are

X_{C}=\{?x\},

and the variables generated inside the component are

\mathcal{C}

\texttt{GenSCC}_{\mathcal{C}}
. For an outer binding

3.

Step 3: Candidate enumeration, operational only.

We invoke

\textsc{EvalSCC}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C},\eta,K,B,T,R).

Initialize finite domains:

D[?y]=\varnothing\quad\text{and}\quad D[?z]=\varnothing.

Round 1: propose values for
?y

Since

\pi_{1}^{[S_{1}]}=\text{``Suggest a topic related to Paris and}\\
\text{{<unknown:?z>}''}.

We query
\mathcal{M}_{1}
with an instruction such as:

Return JSON
{"y":[...]}
with at most
K
short topic strings.

Suppose the model returns:

\texttt{\lx@text@lbrace"y":["Art","Cuisine"]\lx@text@rbrace}.

Parsing yields generated terms
t_{\textsf{Art}}
and
t_{\textsf{Cuisine}}

\texttt{Lex}(t_{\textsf{Art}})=\text{``Art''},\quad\texttt{Lex}(t_{\textsf{Cuisine}})=\text{``Cuisine''}.

Update, respecting the cap
B

D[?y]=\{t_{\textsf{Art}},t_{\textsf{Cuisine}}\}.

Round 2: propose values for
?z
conditional on
?y
.

Choose a currently discovered value, for example
?y=t_{\textsf{Art}}
, and call
\texttt{Propose}(g_{2},S_{2},K)
with

S_{2}=\{?x\mapsto(\texttt{:Paris},\texttt{rdf}),\ ?y\mapsto(t_{\textsf{Art}},\texttt{gen})\}.

Then,

\pi_{2}^{[S_{2}]}=\text{``Suggest a city related to topic Art''}.

Query
\mathcal{M}_{2}
requiring JSON
{"z":[...]}
with at most
K
city strings.
Suppose it returns:

\texttt{\lx@text@lbrace"z":["Florence","Rome"]\lx@text@rbrace}.

Parsing yields

and

D[?z]=\{t_{\textsf{Florence}},t_{\textsf{Rome}}\}.

\texttt{Cand}(\mathcal{C})=\{\eta\uplus\nu\mid\nu(?y)\in D[?y],\ \nu(?z)\in D[?z]\}.

Suppose, in this run,

. Then,

|\texttt{Cand}(\mathcal{C})|=4
.

4.

Step 4: Candidate validation.

We enumerate candidates
\mu\in\texttt{Cand}(\mathcal{C})
and accept those that pass local self-support validation, that is, those for which each
g\in\mathcal{C}
validates the output assigned by
\mu
under the prompt instantiated from the input variables of
g
.

Candidate sample.
Consider

\mu=\{?x\mapsto(\texttt{:Paris},\texttt{rdf}),\ ?y\mapsto(t_{\textsf{Art}},\texttt{gen}),\\
?z\mapsto(t_{\textsf{Florence}},\texttt{gen})\}.

g_{1}
on
\mu
(topic validation).

We have

and

. Instantiate the base instruction prompt

:

\pi_{1}^{[\mu_{|X}]}=\text{``Suggest a topic related to Paris and Florence''}.

\mathcal{M}_{1}

Candidate topic: “Art”.

or

NO

R

on
\mu
(city validation).

We have

and

. Instantiate:

\pi_{2}^{[\mu_{|X}]}=\text{``Suggest a city related to topic Art''}.

Verification query to
\mathcal{M}_{2}

Instruction: “Suggest a city related to topic Art.”

Candidate city: “Florence”.

NO

.

.

Appendix B
Fixpoint-Based Semantics

We assume a
bounded-generation
regime: for every
GenOp
occurrence

g=\texttt{GenOp}(\pi(X),Y,\mathcal{M})
, every instantiated prompt string produces a finite
set of textual answers via
\texttt{Call}_{M}
, and every textual answer yields a finite
set of parsed tuples via
\texttt{Parse}_{M}
. The total set of typed solution mappings that may be produced during evaluation is finite. Formally, the induced universe
\Omega_{\mathcal{P}}
(as defined in the main text) is finite.

Set-theoretic lattice facts.

For any set
U
, the powerset
(2^{U},\subseteq)
is a complete lattice with least
element
\varnothing
, greatest element
U
, and joins given by unions.
If
U
is finite, every ascending chain in
(2^{U},\subseteq)
stabilizes after at
most
|U|
strict increases.

Compatibility.

We use the compatibility relation
\sim
and union
\uplus
of typed solution mappings as defined in the main text. We rely on the following basic fact: if
\mu_{1}\sim\mu_{2}
, then
\mu_{1}\uplus\mu_{2}
is well-defined and extends both

\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
.

Context restriction.

For a context supply
S\subseteq\Omega_{\mathcal{P}}
and a
GenOp
pattern
g
,
we recall
S|_{g}=\{\mu\in S\mid\texttt{input}(g)\subseteq\texttt{dom}_{\mu}\}
.
For any
S\subseteq S^{\prime}
, we always have
S|_{g}\subseteq S^{\prime}|_{g}
.

Immediate consequence operator.

We use
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}:2^{\Omega_{\mathcal{P}}}\to 2^{\Omega_{\mathcal{P}}}
given by

\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}(S)={\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}
(main text). Whenever we write
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}^{n}
, this denotes
n
-fold composition, with
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}^{0}
being the identity.

B.1
Acyclic patterns: single-pass evaluation coincides with fixpoint semantics

This subsection proves the theorem stated in the main text:
acyclic dependency implies that the semantics can be computed in a single
pass and matches the least-fixpoint semantics.

Operational single-pass evaluation for acyclic patterns.

Let
\overrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathcal{P}}=(\texttt{gen}(\mathcal{P}),\to_{\mathcal{P}})
be the
dependency graph. Let
\overrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}
be acyclic, and let
\gamma=(g_{1},\dots,g_{m})
be a topological order of the nodes
\texttt{gen}(\mathcal{P})
.

For
k\in\{0,\dots,m\}
, let
\mathcal{P}^{[k]}_{\gamma}
denote the pattern obtained from
\mathcal{P}

by disabling every
GenOp
occurrence not among
\{g_{1},\dots,g_{k}\}
,
i.e.,
replacing it
by a pattern whose evaluation yields
\varnothing
, while leaving the remaining algebraic
structure unchanged. Thus,
\mathcal{P}^{[0]}_{\gamma}
has all
GenOp
s disabled and

\mathcal{P}^{[m]}_{\gamma}=\mathcal{P}
.

The staged sets are:

S_{\gamma}^{(0)}:={\llbracket\mathcal{P}^{[0]}_{\gamma}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\varnothing}{},\qquad S_{\gamma}^{(k)}:={\llbracket\mathcal{P}^{[k]}_{\gamma}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S_{\gamma}^{(k-1)}}{}\ \ (1\leq k\leq m).

That is, at stage
k
,
GenOp
occurrences up to
g_{k}
may fire using the
context supply accumulated at stage
(k-1)
.

Lemma 2
.

(Well-defined staged evaluation)

For each
k\in\{0,\dots,m\}
, the set
S_{\gamma}^{(k)}
is well-defined and contained in

\Omega_{\mathcal{P}}
.

Proof.

By bounded generation, each
GenOp
call yields finitely many outputs, and every inductive
evaluation clause returns a finite set of typed solution mappings. Since evaluation is defined
for any supply
S\subseteq\Omega_{\mathcal{P}}
(via
S|_{g}
for
GenOp
s),
each
S_{\gamma}^{(k)}
is defined. Containment in
\Omega_{\mathcal{P}}
follows by construction.
∎

Lemma 3
.

(No backward dependencies)

Let
\gamma=(g_{1},\dots,g_{m})
be topological. If
i<j
, then

\texttt{out}(g_{j})\cap\texttt{input}(g_{i})=\varnothing
.

Proof.

If
\texttt{out}(g_{j})\cap\texttt{input}(g_{i})\neq\varnothing
, then by definition of
\to_{\mathcal{P}}
we have

g_{j}\to_{\mathcal{P}}g_{i}
, contradicting that
\gamma
is a topological order.
∎

Theorem 8

(Acyclic collapse: single-pass equals least fixpoint)
.

Assume
\overrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathcal{P}}
is acyclic and fix a topological order
\gamma
.
Then:

1.

S_{\gamma}^{(m)}
is a fixpoint of
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}
.

2.

S_{\gamma}^{(m)}
equals the least fixpoint
\mathrm{lfp}(\Phi_{\mathcal{P}})
.

3.

In particular, the result is independent of the chosen topological order.

Proof.

We proceed in steps.

(1)
S_{\gamma}^{(m)}
is a post-fixpoint.

We show
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}(S_{\gamma}^{(m)})\subseteq S_{\gamma}^{(m)}
.
Let
\mu\in\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}(S_{\gamma}^{(m)})={\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S_{\gamma}^{(m)}}{}
.
In evaluating
\mathcal{P}
relative to
S_{\gamma}^{(m)}
, each
GenOp
occurrence
g_{i}
draws its contexts from
S_{\gamma}^{(m)}|_{g_{i}}
.

We claim that any placeholder-binding used for
g_{i}
at stage
m
is already available at
stage
(i-1)
. That is, for every
\eta\in S_{\gamma}^{(m)}|_{g_{i}}
there exists

\eta^{\prime}\in S_{\gamma}^{(i-1)}|_{g_{i}}
with

\eta^{\prime}|_{\texttt{input}(g_{i})}=\eta|_{\texttt{input}(g_{i})}
.
Indeed, by Lemma
3
, the variables in
\texttt{input}(g_{i})
can be bound only by
(i) non-generative parts of
\mathcal{P}
or (ii) outputs of
GenOp
s
g_{j}
with
j<i
.
All such outputs are included in
S_{\gamma}^{(i-1)}
by construction.

Since prompt instantiation depends on a context mapping only via its restriction to

\texttt{input}(g_{i})
, the set of instantiated prompts (and hence generated outputs) available for

g_{i}
under
S_{\gamma}^{(m)}
coincides with that under
S_{\gamma}^{(i-1)}
.
Consequently, no new
GenOp
-extensions arise when evaluating
\mathcal{P}
relative to

S_{\gamma}^{(m)}
beyond those already produced during the staged construction up to
m
.
Therefore
{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S_{\gamma}^{(m)}}{}\subseteq S_{\gamma}^{(m)}
.

(2)
S_{\gamma}^{(m)}
is a fixpoint.

By construction,
S_{\gamma}^{(m)}={\llbracket\mathcal{P}^{[m]}_{\gamma}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S_{\gamma}^{(m-1)}}{}=\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}(S_{\gamma}^{(m-1)})
and
S_{\gamma}^{(m-1)}\subseteq S_{\gamma}^{(m)}
.
By monotonicity of
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}
,

S_{\gamma}^{(m)}=\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}(S_{\gamma}^{(m-1)})\subseteq\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}(S_{\gamma}^{(m)}).

Combined with (1), this yields
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}(S_{\gamma}^{(m)})=S_{\gamma}^{(m)}
.

Leastness.

Let
S
be any fixpoint of
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}
. Consider the Kleene chain

S_{0}=\varnothing
and
S_{n+1}=\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}(S_{n})
. By the standard induction argument
(using monotonicity),
S_{n}\subseteq S
for all
n
, hence

\mathrm{lfp}(\Phi_{\mathcal{P}})=\bigcup_{n\geq 0}S_{n}\subseteq S
.
Applying this to the fixpoint
S_{\gamma}^{(m)}
yields

\mathrm{lfp}(\Phi_{\mathcal{P}})\subseteq S_{\gamma}^{(m)}
.

S_{\gamma}^{(m)}\subseteq\mathrm{lfp}(\Phi_{\mathcal{P}})
.

Each stage
S_{\gamma}^{(k)}
is obtained by one application of
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}^{[k]}_{\gamma}}

to
S_{\gamma}^{(k-1)}
, and
\mathcal{P}^{[k]}_{\gamma}
is a sub-instance of
\mathcal{P}
(with some

GenOp
s disabled). Hence every mapping in
S_{\gamma}^{(m)}
is derivable by a finite number
of iterations of
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}
starting from
\varnothing
, so

S_{\gamma}^{(m)}\subseteq\bigcup_{n\geq 0}\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}^{n}(\varnothing)=\mathrm{lfp}(\Phi_{\mathcal{P}})
.

Combining (3) and (4), we arrive to
S_{\gamma}^{(m)}=\mathrm{lfp}(\Phi_{\mathcal{P}})
.

(5) Independence of
\gamma
.

If
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
are two topological orders, standard poset arguments show that

\gamma
can be transformed into
\gamma^{\prime}
by swapping adjacent incomparable elements.
For adjacent incomparable generators
g_{i},g_{i+1}
, neither depends on the other,
i.e.,

\texttt{out}(g_{i})\cap\texttt{input}(g_{i+1})=\varnothing
and
\texttt{out}(g_{i+1})\cap\texttt{input}(g_{i})=\varnothing
. Thus, the set of placeholder-bindings available to each is unchanged by the swap, and so is the set of generated outputs. Repeating swaps yields equality of the final staged sets.
∎

B.2
Context equivalence invariance

Proposition 7

(Context invariance (corrected))
.

Let
S,S^{\prime}\subseteq\Omega_{\mathcal{P}}
be context supplies such that for every

GenOp

occurrence

g
in
\mathcal{P}
,

S|_{g}\;=\;S^{\prime}|_{g},\qquad\text{where}\\
S|_{g}=\{\mu\in S\mid\texttt{input}(g)\subseteq\texttt{dom}_{(}\mu)\}.

Then,
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}(S)=\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}(S^{\prime})
.

Proof.

We prove by structural induction on
\mathcal{P}
that

{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}={\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S^{\prime}}{}
.

Base case 1:
\mathcal{P}
is a BGP.

Then
{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}
is independent of
S
, hence equality holds.

Base case 2:
\mathcal{P}
is a
GenOp
pattern
g=\texttt{GenOp}(\pi(X),Y,\mathcal{M})
.

By definition,

{\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}={\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S|_{g}}{},\quad S|_{g}=\{\mu\in S:X\subseteq\texttt{dom}_{\mu}\}.

By assumption, for this occurrence
g
we have
S|_{g}=S^{\prime}|_{g}
.
Hence, the same set of context mappings
\mu_{c}
is used in both evaluations. Since
{\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\cdot}{}
is defined as a union over all
\mu_{c}
in the context supply, it follows directly that
{\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}={\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S^{\prime}}{}
.

Inductive cases.

Assume the claim holds for immediate subpatterns.

1.

If
\mathcal{P}=\texttt{Proj}_{\,L}(\mathcal{P}^{\prime})
, then

{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}
is obtained by restricting each mapping in

{\llbracket\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}
to
L
, which preserves equality.

2.

If
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Union}\,\mathcal{P}_{2}
, then evaluation is set union of the
evaluations of
\mathcal{P}_{1}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}
, hence equality follows.

3.

If
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,\mathcal{P}_{2}
, then evaluation is the set of unions

\mu_{1}\uplus\mu_{2}
for compatible
\mu_{i}
from each side. By IH,
the sets of
\mu_{i}
coincide under
S
and
S^{\prime}
, hence the join result coincides.

4.

If
\mathcal{P}=\texttt{Filter}_{\,F}(\mathcal{P}^{\prime})
, evaluation is subset selection of

{\llbracket\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}
by a predicate on
\mu
alone (
F^{\mu}
depends only
on
\mu
). Since the underlying set is equal by IH, the filtered subset is equal.

5.

The cases
\,\texttt{Opt}_{\,}\,
,
\,\texttt{Diff}_{\,}\,
, and
Minus
are analogous: each is
defined purely in terms of membership/compatibility relations among mappings in
the evaluated subpatterns, so equality of those evaluated sets implies equality
of the resulting sets.

Thus
{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}={\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S^{\prime}}{}
for all
\mathcal{P}
,
and therefore
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}(S)=\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}(S^{\prime})
.
∎

B.3
Monotonicity of the consequence operator

We now give a fully detailed proof of Lemma
1
.

Lemma 4

(Monotonicity of
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}
)
.

If
\mathcal{P}
is monotone w.r.t. the context supply (main text condition), then

\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}
is monotone on
(2^{\Omega_{\mathcal{P}}},\subseteq)
:

S\subseteq S^{\prime}\Longrightarrow\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}(S)\subseteq\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}(S^{\prime}).

Proof.

We prove the stronger statement:
for all patterns
\mathcal{P}
satisfying the monotonicity condition,

S\subseteq S^{\prime}\Longrightarrow{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}\subseteq{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S^{\prime}}{}.

The proof is by structural induction on
\mathcal{P}
.

Case 1:
Let
\mathcal{P}
be a BGP. Evaluation then does not depend on context supply. Hence, equality holds.

Case 2:
Let
\mathcal{P}=g=\texttt{GenOp}(\pi(X),Y,\mathcal{M})
. We have
S|_{g}\subseteq S^{\prime}|_{g}
because
S\subseteq S^{\prime}
.
By the definition of
{\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\Omega_{c}}{}
as a union over contexts

\mu_{c}\in\Omega_{c}
, enlarging
\Omega_{c}
can only add terms to the union:

{\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}={\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S|_{g}}{}\subseteq{\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S^{\prime}|_{g}}{}={\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S^{\prime}}{}.

Case 3:
Let
\mathcal{P}=\texttt{Proj}_{\,L}(\mathcal{P}^{\prime})
.
By IH,
{\llbracket\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}\subseteq{\llbracket\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S^{\prime}}{}
.
Restriction to
L
is monotone on sets: if
A\subseteq B
then

\{\mu|_{L}:\mu\in A\}\subseteq\{\mu|_{L}:\mu\in B\}
, hence inclusion holds.

Case 4:
Let
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Union}\,\mathcal{P}_{2}
.
By IH,
{\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{i}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}\subseteq{\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{i}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S^{\prime}}{}
.
Taking unions preserves inclusion, so the claim follows.

Case 5:
Let
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,\mathcal{P}_{2}
.
Let
\mu\in{\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,\mathcal{P}_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}
.
Then there exist
\mu_{1}\in{\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{1}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}
and

\mu_{2}\in{\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}
with
\mu_{1}\sim\mu_{2}
and

\mu=\mu_{1}\uplus\mu_{2}
.
By IH,
\mu_{i}\in{\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{i}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S^{\prime}}{}
as well, so the same pair witnesses

\mu\in{\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,\mathcal{P}_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S^{\prime}}{}
.

Case 6:
Let
\mathcal{P}=\texttt{Filter}_{\,F}(\mathcal{P}^{\prime})
.
By IH,
{\llbracket\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}\subseteq{\llbracket\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S^{\prime}}{}
.
Now

{\llbracket\texttt{Filter}_{\,F}(\mathcal{P}^{\prime})\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}=\{\mu\in{\llbracket\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}:F^{\mu}=\top\}.

Since the predicate
F^{\mu}=\top
depends only on
\mu
, subset inclusion of the
underlying sets yields inclusion of the filtered sets.

Case 7:
Assume negative constructs.
These comprise
Minus
,
Diff
, and
Opt
.
In general, such constructs are non-monotone in SPARQL.
However, we restrict attention to patterns
\mathcal{P}
satisfying the main-text
monotonicity condition: no variable introduced by a
GenOp
output occurs
in a negative position.

Under this condition, enlarging the context supply from
S
to
S^{\prime}
may introduce
additional generated bindings, but these bindings cannot influence the evaluation
of any negative construct, since all negative tests range only over variables whose
bindings are fixed independently of
GenOp
outputs.
Hence, the truth of membership and compatibility checks performed by negative
constructs is invariant under such enlargements.

It follows that the denotation of each negative construct is unchanged when passing
from
S
to
S^{\prime}
, and the desired inclusion holds by the induction hypothesis applied
to the positive subpatterns. This completes the induction.

∎

B.4
Least fixpoint: existence, characterization, and minimality

Theorem 9

(Existence and characterization of the least fixpoint)
.

If
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}
is monotone on
(2^{\Omega_{\mathcal{P}}},\subseteq)
, then:

1.

\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}
has a least fixpoint
\mathrm{lfp}(\Phi_{\mathcal{P}})
;

2.

\mathrm{lfp}(\Phi_{\mathcal{P}})=\bigcup_{n\geq 0}\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}^{n}(\varnothing)
;

3.

if
\Omega_{\mathcal{P}}
is finite, the Kleene chain stabilizes after at most

|\Omega_{\mathcal{P}}|
strict increases.

(Equivalently, under the alternative assumption that
\Phi
is
\omega
-continuous,
Item 2 holds without requiring finiteness.)

Proof.

(1) Existence.

(2^{\Omega_{\mathcal{P}}},\subseteq)
is a complete lattice. Since
\Phi
is monotone,
Knaster–Tarski yields existence of a least fixpoint, namely

\mathrm{lfp}(\Phi)=\bigcap\{S\subseteq\Omega_{\mathcal{P}}\mid\Phi(S)\subseteq S\}.

(2) Kleene characterization under finiteness.

Consider the Kleene sequence by
S_{0}=\varnothing
and
S_{n+1}=\Phi(S_{n})
.
By monotonicity,
S_{0}\subseteq S_{1}\subseteq S_{2}\subseteq\cdots
.

Assume
\Omega_{\mathcal{P}}
is finite. Then
(S_{n})_{n}
can strictly increase at most

|\Omega_{\mathcal{P}}|
times, hence there exists
N\leq|\Omega_{\mathcal{P}}|
such that

S_{N}=S_{N+1}=\Phi(S_{N})
. Thus
S_{N}
is a fixpoint.

To show minimality, let
T
be any fixpoint of
\Phi
. By induction on
n
,

S_{n}\subseteq T
for all
n
:
base
S_{0}=\varnothing\subseteq T
, and if
S_{n}\subseteq T
then

S_{n+1}=\Phi(S_{n})\subseteq\Phi(T)=T
by monotonicity and
\Phi(T)=T
.
Hence
S_{N}\subseteq T
for every fixpoint
T
, so
S_{N}
is the least fixpoint:

S_{N}=\mathrm{lfp}(\Phi)
.

(3) Finally, since the chain stabilizes at
N
, we have

\bigcup_{n\geq 0}S_{n}=S_{N}
, and therefore

\mathrm{lfp}(\Phi)=S_{N}=\bigcup_{n\geq 0}\Phi^{n}(\varnothing),

with stabilization after at most
|\Omega_{\mathcal{P}}|
strict increases.
∎

Theorem 10

(Minimality of the least fixpoint)
.

Let
S^{\ast}=\mathrm{lfp}(\Phi_{\mathcal{P}})
and let
S\subseteq\Omega_{\mathcal{P}}
satisfy
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}(S)\subseteq S
(
i.e.,

S
is a pre-fixpoint, or closed under one-step consequences). Then
S^{\ast}\subseteq S
.

Proof.

Let
S_{0}=\varnothing
and
S_{n+1}=\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}(S_{n})
. We show by induction that
S_{n}\subseteq S
for all
n
.
Base:
S_{0}=\varnothing\subseteq S
. Step: assume
S_{n}\subseteq S
. By monotonicity,
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}(S_{n})\subseteq\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}(S)
. By assumption
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}(S)\subseteq S
. Therefore
S_{n+1}=\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}(S_{n})\subseteq S
. Taking unions over

produces
S^{\ast}=\bigcup_{n}S_{n}\subseteq S
.
∎

B.5
Stratified semantics: well-definedness, uniqueness, and order-independence

Theorem 11

(Well-definedness and uniqueness of stratified semantics)
.

If
\mathcal{P}
is stratified and
\Omega_{\mathcal{P}}
is finite, then the stratified
semantics
{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathrm{strat}}{}
is well-defined and unique.

Proof.

Assume stratification
\sigma:\texttt{gen}(\mathcal{P})\to\mathbb{N}
, and let
S_{-1}=\varnothing
and for each
i\geq 0
:

F_{i}(S):=\Phi_{\mathcal{P}^{\leq i}}(S\cup S_{i-1}),\qquad S_{i}:=\mathrm{lfp}(F_{i}),

where
\mathcal{P}^{\leq i}
denotes the restriction of
\mathcal{P}
to
GenOp
occurrences in strata at most
i
, as defined in the main text.

We then must show each
F_{i}
is monotone, so
S_{i}
exists and is unique. Let
S\subseteq S^{\prime}
. Then
S\cup S_{i-1}\subseteq S^{\prime}\cup S_{i-1}
. We claim
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}^{\leq i}}
is monotone in its context argument when all negative dependencies of generators in strata
\leq i
point strictly below
i
and are, therefore, evaluated against a fixed lower-stratum supply
S_{i-1}
. Stratification guarantees that any occurrence of an output variable of a stratum
\leq i
generator under a negative operator can only depend on generators in strictly lower strata, hence, is already fixed when computing stratum
i
.

Thus, as the varying part
S
grows, no negative test that depends on generated variables can flip from true to false. Therefore Lemma
4
applies to the operator
S\mapsto\Phi_{\mathcal{P}^{\leq i}}(S\cup S_{i-1})
, producing
F_{i}(S)\subseteq F_{i}(S^{\prime})
.

Hence,
F_{i}
is monotone on
(2^{\Omega_{\mathcal{P}}},\subseteq)
, so by Theorem
9
it has a unique least fixpoint
S_{i}
. Since
\Omega_{\mathcal{P}}
is finite, each stratum stabilizes in finitely many steps. The final stratum value
S_{m}
(with
m=\max\sigma
) is, therefore, well-defined and unique.
∎

Theorem 12

(Order-independence within strata)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be stratified and fix
i\geq 0
. Then, the computed stratum value
S_{i}=\mathrm{lfp}(F_{i})
is independent of any operational
evaluation order of
GenOp
occurrences within stratum
i
, provided that: (i) lower strata
<i
are fully computed first, and (ii) within stratum
i
, evaluation is fair in the sense that any enabled
GenOp
occurrence is eventually applied.

Proof.

Within stratum
i
, semantics is defined denotationally as the least fixpoint of the monotone operator
F_{i}
on the complete lattice
2^{\Omega_{\mathcal{P}}}
.

Let
(T_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be the Kleene sequence for
F_{i}
:
T_{0}=\varnothing
,
T_{n+1}=F_{i}(T_{n})
. Then,
S_{i}=\bigcup_{n}T_{n}
by Theorem
9
.

Any fair operational schedule that repeatedly applies enabled
GenOp
occurrences and closes under the algebra corresponds to generating an ascending chain
(U_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
such that: (1)
U_{0}=\varnothing
, (2)
U_{\alpha}\subseteq U_{\alpha+1}\subseteq F_{i}(U_{\alpha})
, and (3) the limit
U_{\infty}:=\bigcup_{\alpha}U_{\alpha}
is a fixpoint of
F_{i}

(fairness ensures no further
F_{i}
-consequences are missed).

Since
T_{n}
is the
least
chain closed under full application of
F_{i}
, we have by induction
T_{n}\subseteq U_{\infty}
for all
n
, hence
S_{i}=\bigcup_{n}T_{n}\subseteq U_{\infty}
. But,
U_{\infty}
is a fixpoint of
F_{i}
, so by minimality of
\mathrm{lfp}(F_{i})
we also have
U_{\infty}\subseteq S_{i}
. Therefore,
U_{\infty}=S_{i}
, showing independence of the operational order.
∎

B.6
Well-founded semantics: convergence and uniqueness

Theorem 13

(Convergence and uniqueness of well-founded semantics)
.

If
\texttt{Cand}(\mathcal{P})
is finite, then the alternating fixpoint sequence

(I_{n}^{+},I_{n}^{-})
from Definition
21
stabilizes after finitely many steps
at a pair
(I^{+},I^{-})
, and the induced three-valued interpretation (true on
I^{+}
,
false on
I^{-}
, undefined otherwise) is unique.

Proof.

We prove four claims.

(1) Each step is well-defined.

Fix
n\geq 0
.
Consider the operator
T_{n}(S):=\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}^{\texttt{Cand}(\mathcal{P})\setminus I_{n}^{-}}(S)
.
By definition of reduct consequence operator, all negative constructs are evaluated against the fixed set
\texttt{Cand}(\mathcal{P})\setminus I_{n}^{-}
, and thus, do not depend on
S
. Therefore,
T_{n}
is monotone on
(2^{\texttt{Cand}(\mathcal{P})},\subseteq)
: if
S\subseteq S^{\prime}
, the only dependence on
S
is through positive constructs, which are monotone in
S
exactly as in Lemma
4
.
Hence,
\mathrm{lfp}(T_{n})
exists and defines
I_{n+1}^{+}
.

Similarly, let
U_{n}(S):=\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}^{I_{n+1}^{+}}(S)
, which is monotone by
the same reasoning, and
\mathrm{lfp}(U_{n})
exists. Then,
I_{n+1}^{-}=\texttt{Cand}(\mathcal{P})\setminus\mathrm{lfp}(U_{n})
is well-defined.

(2) Monotone growth:
I_{n}^{+}\subseteq I_{n+1}^{+}
and
I_{n}^{-}\subseteq I_{n+1}^{-}
.

We prove both inclusions by induction on
n
.

Base case
is trivial since
I_{0}^{+}=I_{0}^{-}=\varnothing
.

Inductive step
: let
I_{n}^{-}\subseteq I_{n+1}^{-}
.
Then,
\texttt{Cand}(\mathcal{P})\setminus I_{n+1}^{-}\subseteq\texttt{Cand}(\mathcal{P})\setminus I_{n}^{-}
.
Shrinking the set against which negatives are evaluated makes it harder for negatives to block derivations, so the positive least fixpoint can only grow. That is, for any
S
, evaluating negative checks against
\texttt{Cand}(\mathcal{P})\setminus I_{n+1}^{-}
is weaker than against
\texttt{Cand}(\mathcal{P})\setminus I_{n}^{-}
, hence,
T_{n}(S)\subseteq T_{n+1}(S)
pointwise. By monotone operator theory, pointwise inclusion implies inclusion of least fixpoints:
\mathrm{lfp}(T_{n})\subseteq\mathrm{lfp}(T_{n+1})
, hence
I_{n+1}^{+}\supseteq I_{n}^{+}
.

Next, since
I_{n+1}^{+}\supseteq I_{n}^{+}
, negative checks evaluated against a larger fixed set may block more derivations, so
\mathrm{lfp}(U_{n})
can only shrink, and thus its complement
I_{n+1}^{-}
can only grow. This gives
I_{n+1}^{-}\supseteq I_{n}^{-}
.

(3) Finite convergence.
Because
\texttt{Cand}(\mathcal{P})
is finite,
2^{\texttt{Cand}(\mathcal{P})}
is finite.
By (2),
(I_{n}^{+})_{n}
and
(I_{n}^{-})_{n}
are ascending chains in finite posets, hence, each stabilizes after finitely many strict increases. Therefore, the pair sequence stabilizes.

(4) Uniqueness.

Let
(J^{+},J^{-})
be any pair satisfying the alternating fixpoint equations:

J^{+}=\mathrm{lfp}\big(S\mapsto\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}^{\texttt{Cand}(\mathcal{P})\setminus J^{-}}(S)\big),\\
J^{-}=\texttt{Cand}(\mathcal{P})\setminus\mathrm{lfp}\big(S\mapsto\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}^{J^{+}}(S)\big).

Assume the information ordering
(A^{+},A^{-})\preceq(B^{+},B^{-})
iff
A^{+}\subseteq B^{+}
and
A^{-}\subseteq B^{-}
.
The construction starts from the least element
(\varnothing,\varnothing)
and, by (2), proceeds monotonically in this ordering, so its limit is the least solution of the defining equations. Therefore, it is unique.
∎

Lemma 6

(Symmetry of canonical compatibility)

For every variable

and typed terms

By definition,
\texttt{Compat}_{v}(\alpha,\beta)=\top

\texttt{rep}_{v}(\alpha)=\texttt{rep}_{v}(\beta)

Lemma 7

(Symmetry of mapping compatibility)

For all typed solution mappings

\mu_{1}\sim\mu_{2}\quad\text{iff}\quad\mu_{2}\sim\mu_{1}.

By Definition

\mu_{1}\sim\mu_{2}

we have
\texttt{rep}_{v}(\mu_{1}(v))=\texttt{rep}_{v}(\mu_{2}(v))

This condition is symmetric in
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}

∎

Lemma 8

(Canonical union preserves bindings outside overlaps)
.

If
\mu_{1}\sim\mu_{2}
, then:

for every

,
(\mu_{1}\uplus\mu_{2})(v)=\mu_{1}(v)

2.

for every

for every

(\mu_{1}\uplus\mu_{2})(v)=\texttt{rep}_{v}(\mu_{1}(v))=\texttt{rep}_{v}(\mu_{2}(v))
.

Proof.

Immediate from Definition
9
.
∎

Lemma 9

(Canonical union is commutative)
.

If
\mu_{1}\sim\mu_{2}
, then
\mu_{1}\uplus\mu_{2}=\mu_{2}\uplus\mu_{1}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
8
, both unions agree on variables outside the overlap.
On the overlap
\texttt{dom}_{\mu_{1}}\cap\texttt{dom}_{\mu_{2}}
, both yield the representative
\texttt{rep}_{v}(\mu_{1}(v))=\texttt{rep}_{v}(\mu_{2}(v))
.
Hence the resulting partial functions are equal.
∎

Join

:

{\llbracket P_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,\mathcal{P}_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}=\{\mu_{1}\uplus\mu_{2}\mid\mu_{1}\in{\llbracket P_{1}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}},\\

Proposition 9

For all patterns

{\llbracket(\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,\mathcal{P}_{2})\,\texttt{Join}\,\mathcal{P}_{3}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}={\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,(\mathcal{P}_{2}\,\texttt{Join}\,\mathcal{P}_{3})\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}.

We prove

\mu_{12}\in{\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,\mathcal{P}_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}

\mu_{1}\in{\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{1}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}

\mu_{2}\in{\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}

Claim 1:

\mu_{2}\sim\mu_{3}

Proposition 11

(Filter decomposition)
.

For all patterns
P
and filter formulas
\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2}
,

{\llbracket\texttt{Filter}_{\,\mathcal{F}_{1}\wedge\mathcal{F}_{2}}(P)\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}={\llbracket\texttt{Filter}_{\,\mathcal{F}_{1}}(\texttt{Filter}_{\,\mathcal{F}_{2}}(P))\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}.

Proof.

By definition,

{\llbracket\texttt{Filter}_{\,F}(P)\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}=\{\mu\in{\llbracket P\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}\mid F^{\mu}=\top\}

(\mathcal{F}_{1}\wedge\mathcal{F}_{2})^{\mu}=\mathcal{F}_{1}^{\mu}\wedge\mathcal{F}_{2}^{\mu}
, and in particular

(\mathcal{F}_{1}\wedge\mathcal{F}_{2})^{\mu}=\top

\mathcal{F}_{1}^{\mu}=\top
and
\mathcal{F}_{2}^{\mu}=\top

Thus both sides select exactly the same mappings from

Lemma 10

(Filter invariance under representative normalization)
.

Let
\mu

\mathcal{F}^{\mu}=\mathcal{F}^{\widehat{\mu}}

By induction on
\mathcal{F}
.
For atoms, the only changes are (i) replacing a binding by another element of the same
\approx_{v}
-equivalence class, and
(ii) possibly replacing an exact RDF match by a representative in that class.
In both,
(v=c)
and
(v=v^{\prime})
, the semantics accepts either exact RDF equality (when applicable) or canonical compatibility via
\texttt{Compat}_{\cdot}(\cdot,\cdot)
,
and
\texttt{Compat}_{v}(\mu(v),(c,\texttt{rdf}))=\top

\texttt{rep}_{v}(\mu(v))=\texttt{rep}_{v}((c,\texttt{rdf}))

\texttt{Compat}_{v}(\widehat{\mu}(v),(c,\texttt{rdf}))=\top
.
The Boolean cases (

\wedge
) follow immediately.
∎

Proposition 12

(Filter pushdown over join (safe form))

{\llbracket\texttt{Filter}_{\,\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,\mathcal{P}_{2})\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}=\\
{\llbracket(\texttt{Filter}_{\,\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{P}_{1}))\,\texttt{Join}\,\mathcal{P}_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}.

{\llbracket\texttt{Filter}_{\,\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,\mathcal{P}_{2})\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}=\\
{\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,(\texttt{Filter}_{\,\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{P}_{2}))\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}.

Proof.

We prove the first equality; the second is symmetric.

(
\subseteq
) Let
\mu\in{\llbracket\texttt{Filter}_{\,\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,\mathcal{P}_{2})\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}
.
Then
\mu\in{\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,\mathcal{P}_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\mu}=\top
.
So
\mu=\mu_{1}\uplus\mu_{2}
with
\mu_{1}\in{\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{1}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}
,
\mu_{2}\in{\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}
and
\mu_{1}\sim\mu_{2}
.
By assumption,
\texttt{var}(\mathcal{F})\subseteq\texttt{dom}_{\mu_{1}}
. Let
\widehat{\mu_{1}}
be the representative-normalization of
\mu_{1}
as in Lemma
10
. On all variables in
\texttt{var}(\mathcal{F})
,
\mu
agrees with
\widehat{\mu_{1}}
up to representative choice: if
v\in\texttt{var}(\mathcal{F})
is also in
\texttt{dom}_{\mu_{2}}
, then
\mu(v)=\texttt{rep}_{v}(\mu_{1}(v))=\widehat{\mu_{1}}(v)
by Definition
9
; if
v\notin\texttt{dom}_{\mu_{2}}
then
\mu(v)=\mu_{1}(v)
and
\widehat{\mu_{1}}(v)=\texttt{rep}_{v}(\mu_{1}(v))
, which is still equivalent for filter evaluation by Lemma
10
.
Hence
\mathcal{F}^{\mu}=\top
implies
\mathcal{F}^{\mu_{1}}=\top
(equivalently
\mathcal{F}^{\widehat{\mu_{1}}}=\top
), so
\mu_{1}\in{\llbracket\texttt{Filter}_{\,\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{P}_{1})\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}
.Thus,
\mu=\mu_{1}\uplus\mu_{2}\in{\llbracket\texttt{Filter}_{\,\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{P}_{1})\,\texttt{Join}\,\mathcal{P}_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}
.

(
\supseteq
) Let
\mu\in{\llbracket\texttt{Filter}_{\,\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{P}_{1})\,\texttt{Join}\,\mathcal{P}_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}
.
Then
\mu=\mu_{1}\uplus\mu_{2}
with
\mu_{1}\in{\llbracket\texttt{Filter}_{\,\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{P}_{1})\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}
,
\mu_{2}\in{\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}
, and
\mu_{1}\sim\mu_{2}
.
Thus
\mathcal{F}^{\mu_{1}}=\top
and
\texttt{var}(\mathcal{F})\subseteq\texttt{dom}_{\mu_{1}}
.
By Lemma
10
, also
\mathcal{F}^{\widehat{\mu_{1}}}=\top
.
As above,
\mu
agrees with
\widehat{\mu_{1}}
on
\texttt{var}(\mathcal{F})
up to representative choice enforced by
\uplus
, hence
\mathcal{F}^{\mu}=\top
.
Therefore
\mu\in{\llbracket\texttt{Filter}_{\,\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,\mathcal{P}_{2})\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}
.
∎

Proposition 13
.

If

, then

C.1
GenOp-Specific Rewriting Rules

In contrast to the purely algebraic equivalences of
Section
C
, the rules in this subsection depend essentially on
the
context-sensitive
evaluation of
GenOp
patterns
(Section
3
) and are therefore sound only under explicit
side conditions.

Throughout, fix an RDF graph
\mathcal{G}
and a well-formed pattern
\mathcal{P}
.

Acyclic setting and stagewise (single-pass) evaluation

Assume the dependency graph
\overrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathcal{P}}=(\texttt{gen}(\mathcal{P}),\to_{\mathcal{P}})

is acyclic, and fix a topological order
\gamma=(g_{1},\dots,g_{m})
of
\texttt{gen}(\mathcal{P})
.
For each
k\in\{0,\dots,m\}
, recall (Appendix
B.1
) that
\mathcal{P}^{\leq k}

denotes the pattern obtained from
\mathcal{P}
by disabling all
GenOp
occurrences not among

\{g_{1},\dots,g_{k}\}
(treating them as returning
\varnothing
), while keeping the remaining
algebra unchanged.

We define stagewise (single-pass) sequence:

S_{\gamma}^{(0)}:={\llbracket\mathcal{P}^{\leq 0}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\varnothing}{},\qquad S_{\gamma}^{(k)}:={\llbracket\mathcal{P}^{\leq k}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S_{\gamma}^{(k-1)}}{}\ \ (1\leq k\leq m).

Intuitively, at stage
k
, only generators among
\{g_{1},\dots,g_{k}\}
may fire, using as
context the supply accumulated at stage
k-1
.

For a
GenOp
occurrence
g=\texttt{GenOp}(\pi(X),Y,\mathcal{M})
and a supply
S
, we recall the

context restriction
operator:

S|_{g}\ :=\ \{\mu\in S\mid X=\texttt{input}(g)\subseteq\texttt{dom}_{\mu}\}.

Context Independence of Base-Mode GenOp

Proposition 14

(Base-mode context independence)
.

Let
g=\texttt{GenOp}(\pi(X),Y,\mathcal{M})
be a
GenOp
pattern with
\texttt{input}(g)=X=\varnothing
.
Assume the base-mode convention of Section
3
that whenever

X=\varnothing
, the
GenOp
is evaluated with context set
\{\varnothing\}
.
Then, for every supply
S\subseteq\Omega_{\mathcal{P}}
,

{\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S|_{g}}{}\;=\;{\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\{\varnothing\}}{},

i.e.,
the denotation of
g
is independent of
S
.

Proof.

Since
X=\varnothing
, we have
S|_{g}=\{\mu\in S\mid\varnothing\subseteq\texttt{dom}_{\mu}\}=S
. By the base-mode convention, however, the evaluation clause for
\mathcal{P}
ignores its supplied context set and uses
\{\varnothing\}
whenever
X=\varnothing
. Therefore,
{\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S|_{g}}{}={\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\{\varnothing\}}{}
for all
S
.
∎

Corollary 2

(Hoisting base-mode GenOp)
.

Let
g=\texttt{GenOp}(\pi(\varnothing),Y,M)
and let

\Omega_{g}={\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\varnothing}{}
.
Let
\mathcal{P}
be a query pattern that contains no
GenOp
occurrences.
Then, for every RDF graph
\mathcal{G}
and all context supplies
S,S^{\prime}
,

{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\,\texttt{Join}\,g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}\;=\;{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\,\texttt{Join}\,g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S^{\prime}}{}\;=\;{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\,\texttt{Join}\,\Omega_{g}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}},

where
\Omega_{g}
is treated extensionally as a relation (set of typed solution
mappings).

Proof.

Let
S
be an arbitrary context supply.

Step 1 (base-mode context-independence of
g
).

Since
g=\texttt{GenOp}(\pi(\varnothing),Y,M)
is base-mode, Proposition
14

yields

{\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}\;=\;{\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\varnothing}{}\;=\;\Omega_{g}.

That is,
{\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}
is independent of
S
.

Step 2 (context-independence of
\mathcal{P}
).

Because
\mathcal{P}
contains no
GenOp
, its evaluation does not depend on the context supply
(Proposition
19
/ Proposition 1 in the main text). Hence, for all
supplies
S,S^{\prime}
,

{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}\;=\;{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S^{\prime}}{}\;=\;{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}.

Step 3 (join evaluation and elimination of the context parameter).

By the semantic clause for join (Section
3
),

{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\,\texttt{Join}\,g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}=\{\mu\uplus\nu\mid\mu\in{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{},\ \nu\in{\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{},\ \mu\sim\nu\}.

Substituting (
C.1
) and (
C.1
) gives

{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\,\texttt{Join}\,g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}=\{\mu\uplus\nu\mid\mu\in{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}},\ \nu\in\Omega_{g},\ \mu\sim\nu\},

which is manifestly independent of
S
.

Therefore,

{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\,\texttt{Join}\,g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}={\llbracket\mathcal{P}\,\texttt{Join}\,g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S^{\prime}}{}

for all
S,S^{\prime}
. And, treating
\Omega_{g}
extensionally as a relation and applying the
(join) clause of the denotational semantics gives,

\{\mu\uplus\nu\mid\mu\in{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}},\ \nu\in\Omega_{g},\ \mu\sim\nu\}\;=\;{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\,\texttt{Join}\,\Omega_{g}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}.

This establishes the stated equalities.
∎

Proposition 15
.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2}
be patterns and
g
a
GenOp
. For all
\mathcal{G},S
and
i\in\{1,2\}
, let
S_{i}:={\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{i}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}
and
S_{i}|_{g}:=\{\mu\in S_{i}\mid\texttt{input}(g)\subseteq\texttt{dom}_{\mu}\}
. If
{\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S_{1}|_{g}\cup S_{2}|_{g}}{}={\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S_{1}|_{g}}{}\cup{\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S_{2}|_{g}}{}

{\llbracket(\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Union}\,\mathcal{P}_{2})\,\texttt{Join}\,g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}=\\
{\llbracket(\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,g)\,\texttt{Union}\,(\mathcal{P}_{2}\,\texttt{Join}\,g)\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be an RDF graph. Let

S_{i}:={\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{i}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}
and
S_{i}|_{g}:=S_{i}|_{g}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
.

Step 1:

In
(\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Union}\,\mathcal{P}_{2})\,\texttt{Join}\,g

g

S_{\cup}\;=\;{\llbracket\bigl((\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Union}\,\mathcal{P}_{2})\,\texttt{Join}\,g\bigr)\setminus\{g\}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}\;=\;\\
{\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Union}\,\mathcal{P}_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}\;=\;S_{1}\cup S_{2}.

Therefore, the context set passed to
g
is

(S_{1}\cup S_{2})|_{g}=S_{1}|_{g}\cup S_{2}|_{g}.

Similarly, in
\mathcal{P}_{i}\,\texttt{Join}\,g
, removing the occurrence of
g
yields
\mathcal{P}_{i}
,
so the context supply
S_{i}={\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{i}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}
and the effective context

S_{i}|_{g}
.

Step 2:

By the join clause ,

\displaystyle{\llbracket(\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Union}\,\mathcal{P}_{2})\,\texttt{Join}\,g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}=

\displaystyle\{\mu\uplus\nu\mid\mu\in{\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Union}\,\mathcal{P}_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}},\

\displaystyle\nu\in{\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}},\ \mu\sim\nu\}.

(2)

By the union clause,
{\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Union}\,\mathcal{P}_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}=S_{1}\cup S_{2}
, and then, by
GenOp
definition,

{\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}={\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S_{\cup}|_{g}}{}={\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S_{1}|_{g}\cup S_{2}|_{g}}{}.

Substituting into (
2
) gives

\displaystyle{\llbracket(\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Union}\,\mathcal{P}_{2})\,\texttt{Join}\,g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}=

\displaystyle\{\mu\uplus\nu\mid\mu\in S_{1}\cup S_{2},\

\displaystyle\nu\in{\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S_{1}|_{g}\cup S_{2}|_{g}}{},\ \mu\sim\nu\}.

(3)

Step 3:

Assume,

{\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S_{1}|_{g}\cup S_{2}|_{g}}{}={\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S_{1}|_{g}}{}\cup{\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S_{2}|_{g}}{}.

Then, (
3
) becomes

\displaystyle{\llbracket(\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Union}\,\mathcal{P}_{2})\,\texttt{Join}\,g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\mu\uplus\nu\mid\mu\in S_{1}\cup S_{2},\

\displaystyle\nu\in A_{1}\cup A_{2},\ \mu\sim\nu\},

(4)

where
A_{i}:={\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S_{i}|_{g}}{}
.

For
i\in\{1,2\}
, let

J_{i}:=\{\mu\uplus\nu\mid\mu\in S_{i},\ \nu\in A_{i},\ \mu\sim\nu\}.

We show that the set on the right-hand side of (
4
) equals
J_{1}\cup J_{2}
.

(
\subseteq
)
Let
\omega
be an element of the RHS of (
4
).
Then,
\omega=\mu\uplus\nu
with
\mu\in S_{1}\cup S_{2}
and
\nu\in A_{1}\cup A_{2}

and
\mu\sim\nu
. If
\mu\in S_{1}
and
\nu\in A_{1}
, then
\omega\in J_{1}
.
If
\mu\in S_{2}
and
\nu\in A_{2}
, then
\omega\in J_{2}
.
If
\mu\in S_{1}
and
\nu\in A_{2}
, then
\omega\in J_{1}\cup J_{2}
is not guaranteed
in general; however, such
\omega
cannot arise from the semantics of
g
under

unless
g
admits
cross-branch

generation,

unless
A_{2}
contributes extensions that are compatible with
contexts from
S_{1}
. The branch-separability hypothesis (
C.1
) rules out
exactly these cross-branch effects by forcing the set of outputs under

to decompose into outputs attributable to each branch. Thus, every
\omega
witnessed in (

) is witnessed with

and

for the same

, and hence lies in

.

(
\supseteq
)
Let
\omega\in J_{1}\cup J_{2}
.
If
\omega\in J_{1}
, then

with

and

and

, so
\omega
belongs to the RHS
of (
4
). The case
\omega\in J_{2}
is symmetric.

Therefore, (
4
) equals
J_{1}\cup J_{2}
.

Step 4: identify
J_{i}
with the branch joins and conclude.

By the semantics of join and the context-supply convention applied to

,

{\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{i}\,\texttt{Join}\,g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}=\\

Thus,

{\llbracket(\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Union}\,\mathcal{P}_{2})\,\texttt{Join}\,g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}=J_{1}\cup J_{2}=\\
{\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}\cup{\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{2}\,\texttt{Join}\,g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}=\\
{\llbracket(\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,g)\,\texttt{Union}\,(\mathcal{P}_{2}\,\texttt{Join}\,g)\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}},

where the last equality is the union clause. This completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 16
.

Let
g=\texttt{GenOp}(\pi(X),Y,\mathcal{M})
and let
\mathcal{F}
be a filter with

\texttt{var}(\mathcal{F})\subseteq X
.
Let
\mathcal{P}
be a pattern such that
X\subseteq\texttt{var}(\mathcal{P})
,

Y\cap\texttt{var}(\mathcal{P})=\varnothing
, and
\texttt{var}(\mathcal{F})\subseteq\texttt{var}(\mathcal{P})
.
Then,

{\llbracket\texttt{Filter}_{\,\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{P})\,\texttt{Join}\,g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}={\llbracket\texttt{Filter}_{\,\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{P}\,\texttt{Join}\,g)\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be an RDF graph, and assume,

S:={\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}},\qquad S^{+}:=\{\mu\in S\mid\mathcal{F}^{\mu}=\top\},\\
S|_{g}:=\{\mu\in S\mid X\subseteq\texttt{dom}_{\mu}\}.

Since
X\subseteq\texttt{var}(\mathcal{P})
and
\mu
is a solution mapping for
\mathcal{P}
, every

\mu\in S
binds all variables in
X
; hence
S|_{g}=S
and similarly

S^{+}|_{g}=S^{+}
.

Step 1: expand the left-hand side.

In the pattern
\texttt{Filter}_{\,\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{P})\,\texttt{Join}\,g
, the surrounding pattern of
g

is
\texttt{Filter}_{\,\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{P})
, so the context supply for
g
is

{\llbracket\texttt{Filter}_{\,\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{P})\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}=S^{+}
. Then, by the semantics of

\displaystyle{\llbracket\texttt{Filter}_{\,\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{P})\,\texttt{Join}\,g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\mu\uplus\nu\mid

\displaystyle\mu\in S^{+},\;\nu\in{\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S^{+}}{},\;\mu\sim\nu\}.

(5)

Step 2: expand the right-hand side.

In the pattern
\texttt{Filter}_{\,\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{P}\,\texttt{Join}\,g)
, the join
\mathcal{P}\,\texttt{Join}\,g
is
evaluated first. Here, the surrounding pattern of
g
is
\mathcal{P}
, so the context
supply for
g
is
S
, and thus

\displaystyle{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\,\texttt{Join}\,g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}=

\displaystyle\{\mu\uplus\nu\mid

\displaystyle\mu\in S,\;\nu\in{\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{},\;\mu\sim\nu\}.

(6)

Applying the filter clause,

\displaystyle{\llbracket\texttt{Filter}_{\,\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{P}\,\texttt{Join}\,g)\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}=

\displaystyle\{\omega\in{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\,\texttt{Join}\,g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}

\displaystyle\mid\mathcal{F}^{\omega}=\top\}.

(7)

Step 3: relate filter truth before and after joining with
g
.

Let
\mu\in S
and let
\nu
be any mapping produced by

from context
\mu
,

i.e.,

\nu
is of the form
\mu\uplus\nu_{Y}
with

binding only variables
in
Y
to generated terms, as in the definition of

.
Since

, no variable in

occurs in
\mathcal{P}
and,
in particular, no variable in
\texttt{var}(\mathcal{F})
is in

(since
\texttt{var}(\mathcal{F})\subseteq X\subseteq\texttt{var}(\mathcal{P})
). Thus, the canonical union

does not introduce new bindings for

\texttt{var}(\mathcal{F})
; it may only replace existing bindings by

10
(filter invariance under
representative normalization),

\mathcal{F}^{\mu}=\top\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad\mathcal{F}^{\mu\uplus\nu}=\top.

(8)

Moreover, by the side condition
\texttt{var}(\mathcal{F})\subseteq\texttt{dom}_{\mu}
for all
\mu\in S
,
\mathcal{F}^{\mu}\neq\varepsilon
, so, (
8
) is not affected by errors.

Step 4: show
\eqref{eq:lhs}\subseteq\eqref{eq:rhs}
.

Let
\omega\in{\llbracket\texttt{Filter}_{\,\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{P})\,\texttt{Join}\,g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}
.
By (
5
),
\omega=\mu\uplus\nu
with
\mu\in S^{+}
, so
\mathcal{F}^{\mu}=\top
,
and
\nu\in{\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S^{+}}{}
. Since

, the GenOp

{\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S^{+}}{}\subseteq{\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}
.

\omega

),

.

8
) yields

, hence

7
).

\eqref{eq:rhs}\subseteq\eqref{eq:lhs}
.

Let
\omega\in{\llbracket\texttt{Filter}_{\,\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{P}\,\texttt{Join}\,g)\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}
.

7
) and (
6
),
\omega=\mu\uplus\nu
with
\mu\in S
,

\nu\in{\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}
,

, and
\mathcal{F}^{\omega}=\top
.

),
\mathcal{F}^{\mu}=\top
, hence

.

on

for

and

.
Hence

, and thus

is witnessed in
(

). Therefore

.

Safe Reordering of GenOp with Join

Proposition 17

(Safe join reordering around a GenOp)
.

Let

be a

occurrence.
Let

be patterns such that:

, and

.

):
\forall\mathcal{G},\forall S
,

{\llbracket(\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,\mathcal{P}_{2})\,\texttt{Join}\,g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}={\llbracket(\mathcal{P}_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,g)\,\texttt{Join}\,\mathcal{P}_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}.

The condition
\texttt{dom}_{\mu_{2}}\cap X=\varnothing

\texttt{var}(\mathcal{P}_{2})\cap X=\varnothing
and is needed because
\texttt{var}(\mathcal{P}_{2})

\mathcal{P}_{2}
(

under

and

Proof.

Let
S
be a context supply.

(
\subseteq
)
Let

Order Invariance under Acyclic GenOp Dependencies

Proposition 18
.

Assume
\overrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathcal{P}}
is acyclic.
Let
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
be two topological orders of
\texttt{gen}(\mathcal{P})
. Then, the final stagewise results coincide:

S_{\gamma}^{(m)}\;=\;S_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{(m)}.

Proof.

We prove that swapping adjacent incomparable generators does not change the final result, and then conclude by a standard swap argument.

Step 1: a single adjacent swap is harmless.

Let
\gamma=(\dots,g_{i},g_{i+1},\dots)
be a topological order such that
g_{i}
and
g_{i+1}
are incomparable (no edges in either direction). Let
\gamma^{\prime}
be the order obtained by swapping them:
\gamma^{\prime}=(\dots,g_{i+1},g_{i},\dots)
.

Incomparability means:

\texttt{out}(g_{i})\cap\texttt{input}(g_{i+1})=\varnothing\qquad\text{and}\\
\texttt{out}(g_{i+1})\cap\texttt{input}(g_{i})=\varnothing.

Consider the stage just before either of these two generators is applied; call the current supply
S
(this is the same set for both orders up to that point). Because
\texttt{out}(g_{i})
does not occur among placeholders of
g_{i+1}
, evaluating
g_{i}
may add bindings to
\texttt{out}(g_{i})
, but cannot change which mappings in
S
bind all of

\texttt{input}(g_{i+1})
, hence it cannot change
S|_{g_{i+1}}
nor any instantiated prompt for
g_{i+1}
. Symmetrically, evaluating
g_{i+1}
first cannot change the contexts for
g_{i}
. Therefore, the sets of generated extensions contributed by
g_{i}
and
g_{i+1}
are the
same regardless of which is applied first, and since join is associative, the supply after applying both is identical under
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
.

For any two topological orders are connected by adjacent swaps.

It is standard that any two linear extensions of a finite poset can be transformed into each other by repeatedly swapping adjacent incomparable elements. Applying Step 1 along this sequence of swaps shows that the final result is invariant.

Therefore,
S_{\gamma}^{(m)}=S_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{(m)}
.

(Alternatively, theorem is exactly Item (3) of Theorem
8
. Briefly, if two
GenOp
occurrences are incomparable in the dependency graph, neither depends on the outputs of the other, so swapping their evaluation order does not change the extracted contexts or the generated extensions. Any two topological orders are related by a sequence of such swaps, and the resulting stagewise evaluations therefore coincide.)

∎

C.2
Conservativity w.r.t. SPARQL

Proposition 19
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a well-formed graph pattern that contains no occurrence of

GenOp
. Then, for any RDF graph
\mathcal{G}
, evaluation of
\mathcal{P}
under the
extended semantics coincides with standard SPARQL evaluation:

{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}\;=\;\bigl({\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}\bigr)\big|_{\text{standard SPARQL}}.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be fixed. Since
\mathcal{P}
contains no
GenOp
, the evaluation rules for

{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}
never invoke any context-dependent generator clause and,
therefore, never introduce
gen
-typed values. Hence, every mapping

\mu\in{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}
assigns variables only to RDF-typed terms,
i.e.,
values in
T\times\{\texttt{rdf}\}
.

We first show that on RDF-typed values the extended notions of compatibility and
canonical union coincide with SPARQL’s usual agreement-on-overlap and set-theoretic union.

Claim 1 (RDF-typed compatibility collapses to equality).

Let
v\in\mathcal{V}
and let
\alpha=(t,\texttt{rdf})
and
\beta=(t^{\prime},\texttt{rdf})
.
Then

\texttt{Compat}_{v}(\alpha,\beta)=\top\quad\text{iff}\quad t=t^{\prime}.

By definition,
\texttt{Compat}_{v}(\alpha,\beta)=\top
iff
\texttt{rep}_{v}(\alpha)=\texttt{rep}_{v}(\beta)
. For RDF-typed inputs, the typed-term similarity
\texttt{Sim}(\alpha,\beta)
equals
1
exactly when
t=t^{\prime}
and otherwise
0
(Section
3
). Thus the induced
\approx_{v}
-classes are singletons, so
\texttt{rep}_{v}(t,\texttt{rdf})=(t,\texttt{rdf})
and the equivalence follows.

Claim 2 (Mapping compatibility and canonical union coincide with SPARQL).

Let
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
be typed solution mappings with range contained in

T\times\{\texttt{rdf}\}
. Then:
(i)
\mu_{1}\sim\mu_{2}
iff
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}\!
agree on every shared variable;
and (ii) whenever
\mu_{1}\sim\mu_{2}
, the canonical union
\mu_{1}\uplus\mu_{2}

coincides with the ordinary union of partial functions
\mu_{1}\cup\mu_{2}
.
For (i), unfold
\mu_{1}\sim\mu_{2}
: for all
v\in\texttt{dom}_{\mu_{1}}\cap\texttt{dom}_{\mu_{2}}

we require
\texttt{rep}_{v}(\mu_{1}(v))=\texttt{rep}_{v}(\mu_{2}(v))
, which by Claim 1 is equivalent
to equality of the underlying RDF terms. For (ii), by Definition
9
,

\uplus
preserves non-overlapping bindings and, on overlaps, returns the
representative; by Claim 1 this representative is the same RDF-typed binding.

We now prove the proposition by structural induction on
\mathcal{P}
.

Base case (BGP).

For a basic graph pattern, the extended semantics matches triples in
\mathcal{G}
exactly as SPARQL does; the only difference is that bound RDF terms are tagged by
rdf
. Forgetting this tag yields precisely the standard SPARQL BGP solution mappings, hence the two evaluations coincide.

Inductive steps.

Assume the claim holds for strict subpatterns. For each constructor of graph patterns used in
\mathcal{P}
(join, union, optional, minus/diff, filter, projection, etc.), the extended semantics is defined by the standard SPARQL set-theoretic clause, except that it uses
\sim
for join compatibility and
\uplus
for merging compatible mappings, and it evaluates filter atoms using canonical compatibility. On the fragment without
GenOp
, all intermediate mappings are
RDF-typed, so by Claims 1–2 these operations coincide with SPARQL agreement on overlaps, ordinary union, and standard filter evaluation. Therefore, each constructor yields exactly the same set of solutions as standard SPARQL.

Consequently,

{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}=({\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}})|_{\text{standard SPARQL}}
.
∎

C.3
Optimizer Implications

Propositions
14
–
18
justify standard optimizer actions in the acyclic fragment:

1.

Hoisting base-mode GenOps.

By Corollary
2
, a
GenOp
with
\texttt{input}(g)=\varnothing

can be evaluated once and treated as a constant relation, avoiding repeated model calls and enabling early materialization.

2.

Join reordering around GenOps under safe side conditions.

Proposition
17
characterizes when a
GenOp
may be moved across a join without changing the extracted context used for its evaluation, allowing cost-based join planning while preserving semantics.

3.

Topological freedom in acyclic programs.

Proposition
18
shows that any topological order of acyclic generative dependencies yields the same final stagewise result
S_{\gamma}^{(m)}
, enabling planners to choose among valid orders using cost
estimates (
e.g.,
prompt cost, expected fan-out).

These principles do not apply unchanged to cyclic dependencies, where least-fixpoint or well-founded semantics is required and intermediate results may depend on iteration strategy rather than a single-pass topological order.

Theorem 17

. The proof is deliberately local: it bounds one
SCC
call, one accepted projection, and one algebraic constructor at a time.

Throughout this section, the RDF graph
\mathcal{G}
is an input. Generated values occur only as values of typed solution mappings. We count calls to proposal, parsing, and validation procedures as symbolic oracle calls on polynomial-size encodings; the internal neural-inference cost is outside the symbolic query-evaluation problem.

E.1
Decision Problem and Exact SCC-Bounded Evaluation

Definition 24
.

(Evaluation-membership problem)

An instance consists of a graph pattern
\mathcal{P}
, an RDF graph
\mathcal{G}
, a typed solution mapping
\mu
with
\texttt{dom}_{(}\mu)\subseteq\texttt{var}(\mathcal{P})
, and

\texttt{Sem}\in\{\mathrm{lfp},\mathrm{strat},\mathrm{wfs}\}.

The task is to decide membership whether

\mu\in{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\texttt{Sem}}{}.

For
\texttt{Sem}=\mathrm{wfs}
, membership means membership in the true component of the well-founded value; false and undefined status queries are not part of this decision problem.

Data complexity fixes
\mathcal{P}
,
Sem
, and the execution parameters of Algorithm
1
; only
\mathcal{G}
and
\mu
vary. Combined complexity takes
\mathcal{P}
,
\mathcal{G}
, and
\mu
as input, while the execution parameters remain fixed constants of the semantics.

Assumption 2
.

(Exact SCC-bounded executable)

Let
C
be an
SCC
of the generative-dependency graph of
\mathcal{P}
. Let

Y_{C}=\bigcup_{g\in C}\texttt{out}(g)\quad\text{and}\quad X_{C}=\left(\bigcup_{g\in C}\texttt{input}(g)\right)\setminus Y_{C}.

For every outer binding
\eta
with
X_{C}\subseteq\texttt{dom}_{(}\eta)
, Algorithm
1
constructs finite domains
D_{C}^{\eta}[v]
for
v\in Y_{C}
such that

|D_{C}^{\eta}[v]|\leq B,

where
B
is a fixed execution parameter. It forms

\displaystyle\texttt{Cand}_{C}(\eta):=\{\eta\uplus\nu\mid{}

\displaystyle\nu:Y_{C}\to\mathcal{T}_{\texttt{gen}}\times\{\texttt{gen}\},

\displaystyle\nu(v)\in D_{C}^{\eta}[v]\text{ for every }v\in Y_{C},

\displaystyle\eta\sim\nu\}.

The accepted projection set is

R_{C}(\eta):=\{\rho\mid\exists\theta\in\texttt{Cand}_{C}(\eta):\rho=\theta_{|Y_{C}}\text{ and }\texttt{SS}_{C}(\theta)\}.

The regime is exact when
R_{C}(\eta)
equals the declarative
SCC
-answer projection for
C
under
\eta
, for the chosen semantics
\texttt{Sem}\in\{\mathrm{lfp},\mathrm{strat},\mathrm{wfs}\}
and for every generative
SCC
and every outer binding used during evaluation. This equality is a semantic exactness premise: deterministic bounded generation supplies finite deterministic candidate domains, but it does not by itself imply equality between self-supported candidates and the declarative least-fixpoint, stratified, or well-founded answers. Every generated value, prompt instance, parsed tuple, candidate, and validation object has representation length polynomial in
|\mathcal{P}|+|\mathcal{G}|+|\mu|
.

Lemma 11
.

Deterministic bounded generation assumption, together with fixed Section
6
parameters
(K,B,T,R)
and polynomial-size encodings, gives the finite local candidate-domain clauses of Assumption
2
.

Lemma
11
does not imply the semantic exactness clause of Assumption
2
.

Proof.

Deterministic bounded generation assumption fixes the model specifier, prompt instantiation, decoding, parsing, and normalization. Hence one instantiated prompt has one finite deterministic set of parsed outputs. Algorithm
1
uses the fixed proposal bound
K
and the fixed per-variable cap
B
; therefore, for each generated variable
v\in Y_{C}
, it constructs a finite domain satisfying

|D_{C}^{\eta}[v]|\leq B.

Consequently, the candidate construction has the product form

\displaystyle\texttt{Cand}_{C}(\eta)=\{\eta\uplus\nu\mid{}

\displaystyle\nu:Y_{C}\to\mathcal{T}_{\texttt{gen}}\times\{\texttt{gen}\},

\displaystyle\nu(v)\in D_{C}^{\eta}[v]\text{ for every }v\in Y_{C},\eta\sim\nu\},

which is the finite-domain part of Assumption
2
. The validation phase defines the accepted projection relation
R_{C}(\eta)
by the self-support test
\texttt{SS}_{C}
. None of these finite-domain facts entails

R_{C}(\eta)=\mathsf{Ans}^{\texttt{Sem}}_{C}(\eta),

where
\mathsf{Ans}^{\texttt{Sem}}_{C}(\eta)
denotes the declarative
SCC
-answer projection under
Sem
. That equality is exactly the semantic exactness premise stated separately in Assumption
2
. Thus bounded generation supplies finite local bounds and polynomial encodings, while Assumption
2
supplies the additional exactness needed by the complexity proof.
∎

For an
SCC

C
, write

N_{C}:=B^{|Y_{C}|}.

In data complexity,
N_{C}
is a constant because
\mathcal{P}
is fixed. In combined complexity,
N_{C}
can be exponential in
|\mathcal{P}|
, but one assignment
\nu:Y_{C}\to\mathcal{T}_{\texttt{gen}}\times\{\texttt{gen}\}
has polynomial representation length.

E.2
Typed SCC Condensation

Definition 25

(Typed SCC summary operator)
.

For an
SCC

C
, the summary operator
\texttt{GenSCC}_{C}
maps a context supply
S
to the typed mapping set

\displaystyle{\llbracket\texttt{GenSCC}_{C}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}=\{\eta\uplus\rho\mid{}

\displaystyle\eta\in S,X_{C}\subseteq\texttt{dom}_{(}\eta),

\displaystyle\rho\in R_{C}(\eta),\eta\sim\rho\}.

Thus,
R_{C}(\eta)
remains the
Y_{C}
-projection returned by Algorithm
1
, while
\texttt{GenSCC}_{C}
reattaches the outer binding
\eta
before the surrounding SPARQL algebra consumes the result. The condensed pattern
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
is obtained from
\mathcal{P}
by replacing every generative
SCC

C
by
\texttt{GenSCC}_{C}
and preserving the surrounding SPARQL algebraic structure.

Lemma 12
.

Under Assumption
2
, for each semantics symbol
\texttt{Sem}\in\{\mathrm{lfp},\mathrm{strat},\mathrm{wfs}\}
and each typed mapping
\mu
,

\mu\in{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\texttt{Sem}}{}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mu\in{\llbracket\widehat{\mathcal{P}}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\{\emptyset\}}{}.

For
\texttt{Sem}=\mathrm{wfs}
, the left-hand side denotes membership in the true component.

Proof.

Let
C
be one generative
SCC
and let
S
be the context supply reaching
C
. The declarative contribution of
C
under
S
is

\displaystyle A_{C}(S)=\{\eta\uplus\rho\mid{}

\displaystyle\eta\in S,X_{C}\subseteq\texttt{dom}_{(}\eta),

\displaystyle\rho\in\mathsf{Ans}^{\texttt{Sem}}_{C}(\eta),\eta\sim\rho\},

where
\mathsf{Ans}^{\texttt{Sem}}_{C}(\eta)
is the declarative
SCC
-answer projection under
\eta
for the chosen semantics
Sem
. Assumption
2
gives

\mathsf{Ans}^{\texttt{Sem}}_{C}(\eta)=R_{C}(\eta)\quad\text{for every admissible }\eta.

Substituting this equality in the definition of
A_{C}(S)
yields

\displaystyle A_{C}(S)

\displaystyle=\{\eta\uplus\rho\mid\eta\in S,X_{C}\subseteq\texttt{dom}_{(}\eta),\rho\in R_{C}(\eta),\eta\sim\rho\}

\displaystyle={\llbracket\texttt{GenSCC}_{C}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}.

Thus, replacing
C
by
\texttt{GenSCC}_{C}
preserves the relation supplied to the parent algebraic constructor. Repeating this argument along the
SCC
condensation order replaces every generative component by an extensionally equal summary. Projection, filtering, union, join, difference, minus, and optional are compositional: equal input relations give equal output relations by their set-theoretic definitions. Thus, the final root relation is unchanged. For well-founded semantics, the same replacement is applied to the true component of each exact local summary, which is the component queried by Definition
24
.
∎

Lemma 13
.

Let
C
be an
SCC
and let
\eta
be an outer binding for
C
. Then,

|\texttt{Cand}_{C}(\eta)|\leq N_{C}\quad\text{and}\quad|R_{C}(\eta)|\leq N_{C}.

For every context supply
S
,

|{\llbracket\texttt{GenSCC}_{C}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}|\leq N_{C}\cdot|S|.

Proof.

A candidate is determined by one value choice for each generated variable. Therefore,

\displaystyle|\texttt{Cand}_{C}(\eta)|

\displaystyle=\left|\{\eta\uplus\nu\mid\forall v\in Y_{C}:\nu(v)\in D_{C}^{\eta}[v],\ \eta\sim\nu\}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\{\nu\mid\forall v\in Y_{C}:\nu(v)\in D_{C}^{\eta}[v]\}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{v\in Y_{C}}|D_{C}^{\eta}[v]|

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{v\in Y_{C}}B

\displaystyle=B^{|Y_{C}|}=N_{C}.

The projection map

\displaystyle h

\displaystyle:\{\theta\in\texttt{Cand}_{C}(\eta)\mid\texttt{SS}_{C}(\theta)\}\to R_{C}(\eta),

\displaystyle h(\theta)

\displaystyle:=\theta_{|Y_{C}}.

is surjective by the definition of
R_{C}(\eta)
. Thus,

\displaystyle|R_{C}(\eta)|

\displaystyle\leq|\{\theta\in\texttt{Cand}_{C}(\eta)\mid\texttt{SS}_{C}(\theta)\}|

\displaystyle\leq|\texttt{Cand}_{C}(\eta)|

\displaystyle\leq N_{C}.

For the summary operator, let

\displaystyle B_{C}(S)=\{(\eta,\rho)\mid{}

\displaystyle\eta\in S,X_{C}\subseteq\texttt{dom}_{(}\eta),

\displaystyle\rho\in R_{C}(\eta),\eta\sim\rho\}.

Then,

\displaystyle|{\llbracket\texttt{GenSCC}_{C}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}|

\displaystyle=|\{\eta\uplus\rho\mid(\eta,\rho)\in B_{C}(S)\}|

\displaystyle\leq|B_{C}(S)|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\eta\in S,\ X_{C}\subseteq\texttt{dom}_{(}\eta)}|R_{C}(\eta)|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\eta\in S}N_{C}

\displaystyle=N_{C}\cdot|S|.

∎

Lemma 14

(Validation of One Accepted Projection)
.

Let
C
be an
SCC
, let
\eta
be an outer binding, and let
\rho
be a mapping with
\texttt{dom}_{(}\rho)=Y_{C}
. Deciding whether
\rho\in R_{C}(\eta)
uses polynomial time in data complexity and polynomial space in combined complexity.

Proof.

Put

\displaystyle m_{C}

\displaystyle:=|C|,

\displaystyle y_{C}

\displaystyle:=|Y_{C}|,

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle:=|\mathcal{P}|+|\mathcal{G}|+|\eta|+|\rho|.

Let
p(n)
bound the symbolic time and space needed to construct, inspect, or compare one generated value, prompt instance, parsed tuple, mapping restriction, or validation input. Assumption
2
gives such a polynomial.

By the definition of
R_{C}(\eta)
in Assumption
2
, membership expands as

\displaystyle\rho\in R_{C}(\eta)\Longleftrightarrow{}

\displaystyle\exists\theta\in\texttt{Cand}_{C}(\eta):\rho=\theta_{|Y_{C}}\wedge\texttt{SS}_{C}(\theta).

(13)

Expanding the candidate product gives the following equivalent form:

\displaystyle\rho\in R_{C}(\eta)\Longleftrightarrow{}

\displaystyle\exists\nu:\begin{array}[t]{l}\texttt{dom}_{(}\nu)=Y_{C},\\
\forall v\in Y_{C}:\ \nu(v)\in D_{C}^{\eta}[v],\\
\eta\sim\nu,\\
\rho=(\eta\uplus\nu)_{|Y_{C}},\\
\texttt{SS}_{C}(\eta\uplus\nu).\end{array}

Indeed, if (
13
) holds, then
\theta\in\texttt{Cand}_{C}(\eta)
means that there is a total assignment
\nu:Y_{C}\to\mathcal{T}_{\texttt{gen}}\times\{\texttt{gen}\}
such that

\theta=\eta\uplus\nu,\qquad\forall v\in Y_{C}:\nu(v)\in D_{C}^{\eta}[v],\qquad\eta\sim\nu.

Together with
\rho=\theta_{|Y_{C}}
and
\texttt{SS}_{C}(\theta)
, this yields (
E.2
). Conversely, any
\nu
satisfying (
E.2
) yields
\theta:=\eta\uplus\nu\in\texttt{Cand}_{C}(\eta)
,
\rho=\theta_{|Y_{C}}
, and
\texttt{SS}_{C}(\theta)
; hence (
13
) holds. This derivation does not require
\texttt{dom}_{(}\eta)\cap Y_{C}=\emptyset
: if
\eta
already binds a variable in
Y_{C}
, compatibility
\eta\sim\nu
and the equality
\rho=(\eta\uplus\nu)_{|Y_{C}}
enforce the shared value.

We now bound the decision procedure induced by (
E.2
).

Candidate-Assignment Part.

For a guessed or enumerated assignment
\nu
, the product-membership test is

\texttt{dom}_{(}\nu)=Y_{C}\wedge\bigwedge_{v\in Y_{C}}\nu(v)\in D_{C}^{\eta}[v]\wedge\eta\sim\nu\wedge\rho=(\eta\uplus\nu)_{|Y_{C}}.

For each
v\in Y_{C}
, the scan over
D_{C}^{\eta}[v]
has at most
B
elements. Thus, for one
\nu
, the symbolic time is bounded by

B\cdot y_{C}\cdot p(n)+p(n)\leq(By_{C}+1)p(n),

and the symbolic space is bounded by

p(n)+O(\log y_{C})\leq n^{O(1)}.

Self-support part.

Let
\theta:=\eta\uplus\nu
. The self-support predicate expands as

\displaystyle\texttt{SS}_{C}(\theta)\Longleftrightarrow{}

\displaystyle X_{C}\subseteq\texttt{dom}_{(}\theta)\wedge\bigwedge_{g\in C}\theta\in{\llbracket g\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\{\theta_{|\texttt{input}(g)}\}}{}.

Using validation correctness, each conjunct for
g=\texttt{GenOp}(\pi(X_{g}),Y_{g},\mathcal{M}_{g})
is checked by applying the validation procedure to the two restrictions
\theta_{|X_{g}}
and
\theta_{|Y_{g}}
. Each restriction has polynomial encoding length, and hence one validation check costs at most
p(n)
symbolic time and space. Therefore, the self-support phase for one
\nu
has time

\sum_{g\in C}p(n)=m_{C}p(n),

and space

p(n)+O(\log m_{C})\leq n^{O(1)}.

Data-complexity bound.

When
\mathcal{P}
is fixed,
y_{C}
,
m_{C}
, and
N_{C}=B^{y_{C}}
are constants. A deterministic algorithm enumerates all assignments

\nu\in\prod_{v\in Y_{C}}D_{C}^{\eta}[v]

and applies the preceding checks. The number of assignments is at most

\prod_{v\in Y_{C}}|D_{C}^{\eta}[v]|\leq B^{y_{C}}=N_{C},

so the total symbolic time is bounded by

N_{C}\bigl((By_{C}+1)p(n)+m_{C}p(n)\bigr),

which is polynomial in the data input because
N_{C}
,
B
,
y_{C}
, and
m_{C}
are constants. The algorithm stores only one assignment
\nu
, one mapping
\theta
, and one validation configuration at a time, thus, the space is polynomial as well.

Combined-complexity bound.

When
\mathcal{P}
is part of the input,
N_{C}
may be exponential. The procedure need not enumerate or store all assignments. It nondeterministically guesses one assignment
\nu
of polynomial representation length and checks the right-hand side of (
E.2
). The space used is

|\nu|+|\eta\uplus\nu|+p(n)+O(\log(|C|+|Y_{C}|))\leq n^{O(1)}.

Thus, the test is in
NPSPACE
, and by
NPSPACE
=
PSPACE
, it is decidable in polynomial space.
∎

E.3
Polynomial Bounds for Fixed Condensed Patterns

Lemma 15
.

Let
B_{0}=\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{m}\}
be a fixed basic graph pattern. Then,

|{\llbracket B_{0}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}|\leq|\mathcal{G}|^{m}.

Moreover,
{\llbracket B_{0}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}
can be enumerated in polynomial time for fixed
B_{0}
.

Proof.

For each triple pattern
t_{i}
, let

M_{i}:=\{a\in\mathcal{G}\mid a\text{ matches }t_{i}\}.

Then,

|M_{i}|\leq|\mathcal{G}|\quad\text{for every }i.

Every solution of
B_{0}
is induced by a compatible tuple from
M_{1}\times\cdots\times M_{m}
. Hence,

\displaystyle|{\llbracket B_{0}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}|

\displaystyle\leq|M_{1}\times\cdots\times M_{m}|

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{m}|M_{i}|

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=1}^{m}|\mathcal{G}|

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{G}|^{m}.

Enumeration uses nested loops over
M_{1},\ldots,M_{m}
, checks compatibility, and stores one
m
-tuple of RDF triples and one induced mapping at a time. Since
m
is fixed, the enumeration time is polynomial in
|\mathcal{G}|
.
∎

Lemma 16

(Polynomial preservation by fixed condensed patterns)
.

Let
Q
be a fixed subpattern of
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
. If the input context supply
S(\mathcal{G})
satisfies

|S(\mathcal{G})|\leq q_{S}(|\mathcal{G}|)

for a polynomial
q_{S}
, then there is a polynomial
q_{Q}
such that

|{\llbracket Q\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S(\mathcal{G})}{}|\leq q_{Q}(|\mathcal{G}|).

If
S(\mathcal{G})
can be enumerated in polynomial time, then
{\llbracket Q\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S(\mathcal{G})}{}
can be enumerated in polynomial time for fixed
Q
.

Proof.

We use structural induction on
Q
. Put
n:=|\mathcal{G}|
. For a proper subpattern
Q_{i}
, write

\Omega_{i}:={\llbracket Q_{i}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S(\mathcal{G})}{},\qquad|\Omega_{i}|\leq q_{i}(n).

Basic graph pattern.

For
Q=B_{0}
, Lemma
15
gives

|{\llbracket B_{0}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}|=|{\llbracket B_{0}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}|\leq n^{m}.

Set
q_{Q}(n):=n^{m}
.

SCC summary.

For
Q=\texttt{GenSCC}_{C}
, Lemma
13
gives

\displaystyle|{\llbracket\texttt{GenSCC}_{C}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}|

\displaystyle\leq N_{C}\cdot|S|

\displaystyle\leq N_{C}\cdot q_{S}(n).

Since
Q
is fixed,
N_{C}
is constant. Set
q_{Q}(n):=N_{C}q_{S}(n)
.

Projection.

For
Q=\texttt{Proj}_{\,L}(Q_{1})
, put
\Omega:={\llbracket Q\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}
. Then,

\Omega=\{\mu_{|L}\mid\mu\in\Omega_{1}\}.

The map
\rho_{L}:\Omega_{1}\to\Omega
,
\rho_{L}(\mu)=\mu_{|L}
, is surjective. Therefore,

\displaystyle|\Omega|

\displaystyle=|\rho_{L}(\Omega_{1})|

\displaystyle\leq|\Omega_{1}|

\displaystyle\leq q_{1}(n).

Set
q_{Q}(n):=q_{1}(n)
.

Filter.

For
Q=\texttt{Filter}_{\,F}(Q_{1})
,

\Omega:={\llbracket Q\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}=\{\mu\in\Omega_{1}\mid F^{\mu}=\top\}.

Hence,

\displaystyle|\Omega|

\displaystyle=|\{\mu\in\Omega_{1}\mid F^{\mu}=\top\}|

\displaystyle\leq|\Omega_{1}|

\displaystyle\leq q_{1}(n).

Set
q_{Q}(n):=q_{1}(n)
.

Union.

For
Q=Q_{1}\,\texttt{Union}\,Q_{2}
,

\Omega:={\llbracket Q\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}=\Omega_{1}\cup\Omega_{2}.

Thus,

\displaystyle|\Omega|

\displaystyle=|\Omega_{1}\cup\Omega_{2}|

\displaystyle=|\Omega_{1}|+|\Omega_{2}|-|\Omega_{1}\cap\Omega_{2}|

\displaystyle\leq|\Omega_{1}|+|\Omega_{2}|

\displaystyle\leq q_{1}(n)+q_{2}(n).

Set
q_{Q}(n):=q_{1}(n)+q_{2}(n)
.

Join.

For
Q=Q_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,Q_{2}
,

\Omega:={\llbracket Q\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}=\{\mu_{1}\uplus\mu_{2}\mid\mu_{1}\in\Omega_{1},\mu_{2}\in\Omega_{2},\mu_{1}\sim\mu_{2}\}.

Let

A:=\{(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})\in\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2}\mid\mu_{1}\sim\mu_{2}\}.

The map
h:A\to\Omega
,
h(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})=\mu_{1}\uplus\mu_{2}
, is surjective. Hence,

\displaystyle|\Omega|

\displaystyle\leq|A|

\displaystyle\leq|\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2}|

\displaystyle=|\Omega_{1}|\cdot|\Omega_{2}|

\displaystyle\leq q_{1}(n)q_{2}(n).

Set
q_{Q}(n):=q_{1}(n)q_{2}(n)
.

Difference.

For
Q=Q_{1}\,\texttt{Diff}_{\,F}\,Q_{2}
, the main semantics gives

\displaystyle\Omega:={\llbracket Q\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}=\{\mu_{1}\in\Omega_{1}\mid{}

\displaystyle\forall\mu_{2}\in\Omega_{2}:

\displaystyle\mu_{1}\not\sim\mu_{2}\ \text{or}\ F^{\mu_{1}\uplus\mu_{2}}=\bot\}.

Equivalently,

\displaystyle\mu_{1}\in\Omega\Longleftrightarrow{}

\displaystyle\mu_{1}\in\Omega_{1}\ \,\wedge

\displaystyle\neg\exists\mu_{2}\in\Omega_{2}:\mu_{1}\sim\mu_{2}\wedge F^{\mu_{1}\uplus\mu_{2}}\neq\bot.

Therefore
\Omega\subseteq\Omega_{1}
, and

\displaystyle|\Omega|

\displaystyle\leq|\Omega_{1}|

\displaystyle\leq q_{1}(n).

Set
q_{Q}(n):=q_{1}(n)
.

Minus.

For
Q=Q_{1}\,\texttt{Minus}\,Q_{2}
,

\displaystyle\Omega:={\llbracket Q\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}=\{\mu_{1}\in\Omega_{1}\mid{}

\displaystyle\neg\exists\mu_{2}\in\Omega_{2}:

\displaystyle\mu_{1}\sim\mu_{2}\wedge\texttt{dom}_{(}\mu_{1})\cap\texttt{dom}_{(}\mu_{2})\neq\emptyset\}.

Thus,
\Omega\subseteq\Omega_{1}
, and

\displaystyle|\Omega|

\displaystyle\leq|\Omega_{1}|

\displaystyle\leq q_{1}(n).

Set
q_{Q}(n):=q_{1}(n)
.

Optional.

For
Q=Q_{1}\,\texttt{Opt}_{\,F}\,Q_{2}
, the main semantics is

{\llbracket Q\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}={\llbracket Q_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,Q_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}\cup{\llbracket Q_{1}\,\texttt{Diff}_{\,F}\,Q_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}.

Set

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle:=\{\mu_{1}\uplus\mu_{2}\mid\mu_{1}\in\Omega_{1},\mu_{2}\in\Omega_{2},\mu_{1}\sim\mu_{2}\},

\displaystyle D_{F}

\displaystyle:=\{\mu_{1}\in\Omega_{1}\mid\forall\mu_{2}\in\Omega_{2}:\mu_{1}\not\sim\mu_{2}\ \text{or}\ F^{\mu_{1}\uplus\mu_{2}}=\bot\}.

Then,
\Omega=J\cup D_{F}
. The join and difference derivations give

\displaystyle|J|

\displaystyle\leq|\Omega_{1}|\cdot|\Omega_{2}|\leq q_{1}(n)q_{2}(n),

\displaystyle|D_{F}|

\displaystyle\leq|\Omega_{1}|\leq q_{1}(n),

\displaystyle|\Omega|

\displaystyle=|J\cup D_{F}|

\displaystyle\leq|J|+|D_{F}|

\displaystyle\leq q_{1}(n)q_{2}(n)+q_{1}(n).

Set
q_{Q}(n):=q_{1}(n)q_{2}(n)+q_{1}(n)
.

Each
q_{Q}
is obtained from
q_{S}
and the induction polynomials by a finite number of additions and multiplications. Because
Q
is fixed, the induction depth is fixed; therefore
q_{Q}
is a polynomial.

For enumeration, the displayed definitions give the following time recurrences for fixed
Q
:

\displaystyle T_{\texttt{GenSCC}_{C}}(n)

\displaystyle\leq|S|N_{C}n^{O(1)},

\displaystyle T_{\texttt{Proj}_{\,L}(Q_{1})}(n)

\displaystyle\leq T_{1}(n)+|\Omega_{1}|n^{O(1)},

\displaystyle T_{\texttt{Filter}_{\,F}(Q_{1})}(n)

\displaystyle\leq T_{1}(n)+|\Omega_{1}|n^{O(1)},

\displaystyle T_{Q_{1}\,\texttt{Union}\,Q_{2}}(n)

\displaystyle\leq T_{1}(n)+T_{2}(n)+(|\Omega_{1}|+|\Omega_{2}|)n^{O(1)},

\displaystyle T_{Q_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,Q_{2}}(n)

\displaystyle\leq T_{1}(n)+T_{2}(n)+|\Omega_{1}||\Omega_{2}|n^{O(1)},

\displaystyle T_{Q_{1}\,\texttt{Diff}_{\,F}\,Q_{2}}(n)

\displaystyle\leq T_{1}(n)+|\Omega_{1}|\bigl(T_{2}(n)+|\Omega_{2}|n^{O(1)}\bigr),

\displaystyle T_{Q_{1}\,\texttt{Minus}\,Q_{2}}(n)

\displaystyle\leq T_{1}(n)+|\Omega_{1}|\bigl(T_{2}(n)+|\Omega_{2}|n^{O(1)}\bigr),

\displaystyle T_{Q_{1}\,\texttt{Opt}_{\,F}\,Q_{2}}(n)

\displaystyle\leq T_{Q_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,Q_{2}}(n)+T_{Q_{1}\,\texttt{Diff}_{\,F}\,Q_{2}}(n)

\displaystyle\quad+(|J|+|D_{F}|)n^{O(1)}.

All cardinalities on the right-hand side are polynomial by the preceding derivations. Hence enumeration is polynomial time for fixed
Q
.
∎

E.4
Data Complexity

Theorem 18

(Data-complexity upper bound)
.

Under Assumption
2
, the evaluation-membership problem for acyclic patterns, stratified patterns, and non-stratified patterns under well-founded semantics is decidable in deterministic polynomial time in
|\mathcal{G}|+|\mu|
.

Proof.

By Lemma
12
, membership in
{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\texttt{Sem}}{}
is equivalent to membership in

{\llbracket\widehat{\mathcal{P}}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\{\emptyset\}}{}.

Since
\mathcal{P}
is fixed in data complexity, the condensed pattern
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
is fixed. The initial supply satisfies

|\{\emptyset\}|=1.

Applying Lemma
16
to
Q=\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
and
S=\{\emptyset\}
gives a polynomial
q_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}
such that

|{\llbracket\widehat{\mathcal{P}}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\{\emptyset\}}{}|\leq q_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}(|\mathcal{G}|).

The enumeration part of Lemma
16
, together with Lemma
14
, computes the condensed relation in time

q_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}(|\mathcal{G}|)\cdot(|\mathcal{G}|+|\mu|)^{O(1)}.

The final membership test scans the computed relation and compares each mapping with
\mu
, which costs

q_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}(|\mathcal{G}|)\cdot|\mu|^{O(1)}.

Both bounds are polynomial in
|\mathcal{G}|+|\mu|
. The argument is independent of whether
Sem
is
\mathrm{lfp}
,
\mathrm{strat}
, or
\mathrm{wfs}
, because exact SCC condensation has already converted the generative components into typed summary operators.
∎

E.5
Combined Complexity

For combined complexity,
\mathcal{P}
is part of the input. Then,
|Y_{C}|
may be linear in
|\mathcal{P}|
, and

N_{C}=B^{|Y_{C}|}

may be exponential. The upper-bound proof therefore stores and validates one assignment
\rho
at a time.

Theorem 19

(Combined
PSPACE
-hardness)
.

For each
\texttt{Sem}\in\{\mathrm{lfp},\mathrm{strat},\mathrm{wfs}\}
, the evaluation-membership problem is
PSPACE
-hard in combined complexity.

Proof.

Given a standard SPARQL pattern
P_{0}
, an RDF graph
\mathcal{G}
, and a mapping
\mu
, view
P_{0}
as a generative SPARQL pattern with no
GenOp
occurrence. Then

{\llbracket P_{0}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\texttt{Sem}}{}={\llbracket P_{0}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}

for every
\texttt{Sem}\in\{\mathrm{lfp},\mathrm{strat},\mathrm{wfs}\}
, because there is no generated value, no generative
SCC
, and no self-support validation. Standard SPARQL membership is
PSPACE
-hard in combined complexity
[
31
,
32
]
. The identity map

(P_{0},\mathcal{G},\mu)\longmapsto(P_{0},\mathcal{G},\mu)

is therefore a polynomial-time many-one reduction to generative SPARQL membership.
∎

Definition 26
.

A predicate
\mathsf{InS}
is an implicit membership predicate for a conceptual set
S
of typed solution mappings if, for every typed mapping
\eta
,

\mathsf{InS}(\eta)=\top\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\eta\in S.

Lemma 17
.

There is a polynomial-space procedure

\mathsf{MEM}(Q,\mathcal{G},\mathsf{InS},\mu)

that decides whether
\mu\in{\llbracket Q\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}
for every subpattern
Q
of
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
, provided
\mathsf{InS}
decides membership in the input supply
S
using polynomial space.

Proof.

Let

n:=|Q|+|\mathcal{G}|+|\mu|.

We give a nondeterministic polynomial-space procedure for membership and use
\texttt{NPSPACE}=\texttt{PSPACE}
; for negative subtests we use closure of
PSPACE
under complement.

Basic graph pattern.

For
Q=B_{0}=\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{m}\}
,

\displaystyle\mu\in{\llbracket B_{0}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}\Longleftrightarrow{}

\displaystyle\exists(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})\in\mathcal{G}^{m}:

\displaystyle\bigwedge_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\text{ matches }t_{i}\text{ and }\mu=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{a_{i}}.

The procedure guesses
a_{i}
sequentially, stores at most
m
RDF triples and the accumulated mapping, and checks compatibility after each guess. The space is

O(m\log|\mathcal{G}|)+|\mu|^{O(1)}\leq n^{O(1)}.

SCC summary.

For
Q=\texttt{GenSCC}_{C}
, Definition
25
gives

\displaystyle\mu\in{\llbracket\texttt{GenSCC}_{C}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}\Longleftrightarrow{}

\displaystyle\exists\eta,\rho:\ \begin{array}[t]{l}\mathsf{InS}(\eta)=\top,\\
X_{C}\subseteq\texttt{dom}_{(}\eta),\\
\rho\in R_{C}(\eta),\\
\eta\sim\rho,\\
\mu=\eta\uplus\rho.\end{array}

The guessed mappings
\eta
and
\rho
assign only variables occurring in
Q
, hence have polynomial length. The test
\mathsf{InS}(\eta)
uses polynomial space by hypothesis. The test
\rho\in R_{C}(\eta)
uses polynomial space by Lemma
14
. Thus,

s_{Q}(n)\leq s_{\mathsf{InS}}(n)+n^{O(1)}.

Projection.

For
Q=\texttt{Proj}_{\,L}(Q_{1})
,

\mu\in{\llbracket Q\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}\Longleftrightarrow\exists\mu^{\prime}:\mu^{\prime}_{|L}=\mu\wedge\mu^{\prime}\in{\llbracket Q_{1}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}.

The guessed
\mu^{\prime}
assigns only variables of
Q_{1}
, so

s_{Q}(n)\leq s_{Q_{1}}(n)+n^{O(1)}.

Filter.

For
Q=\texttt{Filter}_{\,F}(Q_{1})
,

\mu\in{\llbracket Q\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}\Longleftrightarrow F^{\mu}=\top\wedge\mu\in{\llbracket Q_{1}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}.

Thus,

s_{Q}(n)\leq s_{Q_{1}}(n)+n^{O(1)}.

Union.

For
Q=Q_{1}\,\texttt{Union}\,Q_{2}
,

\mu\in{\llbracket Q\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}\Longleftrightarrow\mu\in{\llbracket Q_{1}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}\vee\mu\in{\llbracket Q_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}.

Hence,

s_{Q}(n)\leq\max\{s_{Q_{1}}(n),s_{Q_{2}}(n)\}+n^{O(1)}.

Join.

For
Q=Q_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,Q_{2}
,

\displaystyle\mu\in{\llbracket Q\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}\Longleftrightarrow{}

\displaystyle\exists\mu_{1},\mu_{2}:\ \begin{array}[t]{l}\mu_{1}\in{\llbracket Q_{1}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{},\\
\mu_{2}\in{\llbracket Q_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{},\\
\mu_{1}\sim\mu_{2},\\
\mu=\mu_{1}\uplus\mu_{2}.\end{array}

The procedure guesses
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
, checks compatibility and equality with
\mu
, and calls the two subprocedures sequentially. Therefore,

s_{Q}(n)\leq s_{Q_{1}}(n)+s_{Q_{2}}(n)+n^{O(1)}.

Difference.

For
Q=Q_{1}\,\texttt{Diff}_{\,F}\,Q_{2}
,

\displaystyle\mu\in{\llbracket Q\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}\Longleftrightarrow{}

\displaystyle\mu\in{\llbracket Q_{1}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}

\displaystyle\wedge\neg\exists\mu_{2}:\ \begin{array}[t]{l}\mu_{2}\in{\llbracket Q_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{},\\
\mu\sim\mu_{2},\\
F^{\mu\uplus\mu_{2}}\neq\bot.\end{array}

The existential right-witness test uses space
s_{Q_{2}}(n)+n^{O(1)}
, and its complement is in
PSPACE
. Thus

s_{Q}(n)\leq s_{Q_{1}}(n)+s_{Q_{2}}(n)+n^{O(1)}.

Minus.

For
Q=Q_{1}\,\texttt{Minus}\,Q_{2}
,

\displaystyle\mu\in{\llbracket Q\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}\Longleftrightarrow{}

\displaystyle\mu\in{\llbracket Q_{1}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}

\displaystyle\wedge\neg\exists\mu_{2}:\ \begin{array}[t]{l}\mu_{2}\in{\llbracket Q_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{},\\
\mu\sim\mu_{2},\\
\texttt{dom}_{(}\mu)\cap\texttt{dom}_{(}\mu_{2})\neq\emptyset.\end{array}

Therefore,

s_{Q}(n)\leq s_{Q_{1}}(n)+s_{Q_{2}}(n)+n^{O(1)}.

Optional.

For
Q=Q_{1}\,\texttt{Opt}_{\,F}\,Q_{2}
,

{\llbracket Q\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}={\llbracket Q_{1}\,\texttt{Join}\,Q_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}\cup{\llbracket Q_{1}\,\texttt{Diff}_{\,F}\,Q_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\mu\in{\llbracket Q\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{}\Longleftrightarrow{}

\displaystyle\left(\exists\mu_{1},\mu_{2}:\ \begin{array}[t]{l}\mu_{1}\in{\llbracket Q_{1}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{},\\
\mu_{2}\in{\llbracket Q_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{},\\
\mu_{1}\sim\mu_{2},\\
\mu=\mu_{1}\uplus\mu_{2}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle\vee\left(\begin{array}[t]{l}\mu\in{\llbracket Q_{1}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{},\\
\neg\exists\mu_{2}:\ \mu_{2}\in{\llbracket Q_{2}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{S}{},\\
\mu\sim\mu_{2},\\
F^{\mu\uplus\mu_{2}}\neq\bot\end{array}\right).

The first disjunct has the join-space bound and the second has the difference-space bound. Hence,

s_{Q}(n)\leq s_{Q_{1}}(n)+s_{Q_{2}}(n)+n^{O(1)}.

The recursive membership calls follow the syntax tree of
Q
and have depth at most
|Q|
. At each level, the procedure stores only polynomial-size mappings, indices, and one subcall configuration. Hence the total space is polynomial in
n
.
∎

Theorem 20

(Combined
PSPACE
upper bound)
.

Under Assumption
2
, the evaluation-membership problem is in
PSPACE
in combined complexity for
\texttt{Sem}\in\{\mathrm{lfp},\mathrm{strat},\mathrm{wfs}\}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
12
,

\mu\in{\llbracket\mathcal{P}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\texttt{Sem}}{}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mu\in{\llbracket\widehat{\mathcal{P}}\rrbracket}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\{\emptyset\}}{}.

Let
S_{0}:=\{\emptyset\}
. Its implicit membership predicate is

\mathsf{InS}_{0}(\eta)=\top\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\eta=\emptyset,

which is decidable in polynomial space. Applying Lemma
17
to
Q=\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
,
S=S_{0}
, and
\mathsf{InS}=\mathsf{InS}_{0}
yields a polynomial-space test for the right-hand side. The equivalence above transfers the bound to the original pattern
\mathcal{P}
. For
\texttt{Sem}=\mathrm{wfs}
, this decides membership in the true component, as specified in Definition
24
.
∎

Theorem 21

(Combined
PSPACE
-completeness)
.

Under Assumption
2
, the evaluation-membership problem is
PSPACE
-complete in combined complexity for
\texttt{Sem}\in\{\mathrm{lfp},\mathrm{strat},\mathrm{wfs}\}
.

Proof.

The lower bound is Theorem
19
. The upper bound is Theorem
20
. Therefore the problem is
PSPACE
-complete.
∎

Proof of Theorem

The proof uses only local products

\prod_{v\in Y_{C}}D_{C}^{\eta}[v]
