The MSI-20 Score Provides Reproducible Mortality Risk Stratification in Spinal Infection: A Multicenter Registry Study and External Validation
Authors: Sara Lener, Raphael Gmeiner, Anto Abramovic, Anna Koller, Christoph Orban, Sebastian Hartmann, Alexandra Grob, Victor Staartjes, Ann Kathrin Joerger, Carolin Albrecht, Bernhard Meyer, Felipa Lange, Andreas Kramer, Florian Ringel, Christopher Marvin Jesse, Ralph T Schär, Christoph Bettag, Veit Rohde, Insa K Janssen, Karl Schaller, Julia Onken, Aminaa Sanchin, Peter Vajkoczy, Imran Shah, Rebecca Sutherland, Andreas K Demetriades, Claudius Thomé
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveSpinal infection (SI) is associated with substantial morbidity and mortality, and optimal treatment strategies remain debated, particularly in medically fragile patients. The Mortality in Spinal Infection (MSI-20) score was developed as the first dedicated prognostic tool for mortality risk estimation, but external validation in large cohorts has been lacking. This study aimed to externally validate the MSI-20 in a large multicenter registry cohort and assess its performance, clinical thresholds, and generalizability. MethodsThis retrospective multi-institutional registry study included 1,122 adult patients with clinically, radiologically, and laboratory-confirmed SI treated at 6 tertiary referral centers between 2010 and 2023. The multicenter registry design enabled robust analysis of this relatively rare outcome. MSI-20 scores were calculated according to the original definition. Predictive performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis with 95% confidence intervals. Mortality across score strata and center-wise performance were analyzed, with exploratory assessment of additional baseline predictors. ResultsMean age was 66.4±13.1 years, and overall mortality was 18.3%. The MSI-20 demonstrated fair discriminative ability (area under the curve [AUC], 0.68; 95% confidence interval [CI], 64–72). The optimal ROC threshold was 3.5; a cutoff ≥4 yielded sensitivity 0.49 and specificity 0.74 (F1 score, 0.38). Mortality increased progressively, approaching 50% (47.4%) at scores ≥9. Performance was consistent across centers (AUC, 0.60–0.75). Exploratory univariable analyses did not identify additional baseline variables with consistent strong associations beyond the MSI-20 components. ConclusionThis large multicenter registry study provides the first external validation of the MSI-20, confirming reproducibility and generalizability. The score demonstrated fair discrimination and a clear risk gradient across increasing score categories. Given its simplicity and high negative predictive value, the MSI-20 may serve as an adjunctive tool to support risk communication, multidisciplinary discussion, patient counseling, and preoperative optimization in spinal infection.Keywords: Spinal infection, Mortality, Score validation, Risk Stratificatio, MSI-20, Spondylodiscitis

INTRODUCTION

Spinal infection (SI) represents a severe and potentially lifethreatening condition involving the vertebral column and adjacent soft tissues [1,2]. The disease may arise through hematogenous spread from distant foci, by direct extension from adjacent structures, or as a complication of spinal surgery or invasive procedures [3]. Despite advances in diagnostic imaging, microbiological techniques, and antimicrobial therapy, SI remains associated with substantial morbidity and mortality, representing a significant challenge for clinicians across multiple specialties. The incidence of SI has increased substantially over the past decades. Population-based studies from Denmark demonstrated an increase from 2.2 cases per 100,000 persons in 1995 to 5.8 cases per 100,000 persons by 2008 [4]. More recent data from Germany and France demonstrate an even more pronounced trend. In Germany, the population-adjusted incidence increased by 104%, from 5.4 to 11.0 cases per 100,000 individuals between 2005 and 2015 [5]. In France, the incidence increased from 6.1 to 11.3 per 100,000 between 2010 and 2019 [6]. Furthermore, mortality rates in SI remain substantial despite treatment advances. Hospital mortality rates of 2%–20% have been consistently reported across industrialized countries [7,8]. German national data revealed a 347% increase in deaths attributable to spondylodiscitis between 2005 and 2021, with in-hospital mortality rising from 3.4% to 7.5% over that period of time [5].

However, the optimal treatment strategy for SI remains controversial. Early and radical surgery is often advocated to achieve rapid source control and prevent complications of immobilization, in order that the operative risk may outweigh the benefit in medically fragile patients [7-10]. Identifying those who can safely undergo surgery versus those that are at unacceptably high risk of perioperative mortality remains a central challenge in clinical decision-making.

To address this gap, we developed the Mortality in Spinal Infection (MSI-20) score [9], the first dedicated prognostic tool for SI. Derived from a single-center cohort of 252 patients, the MSI-20 integrates 8 variables (body mass index, American Society of Anesthesiologists [ASA] physical status classification, sepsis, age-adjusted Charlson Comorbidity Index, renal failure, hepatopathy, neurological deficit, and C-reactive protein [CRP] at diagnosis) into a cumulative 20-point score (Table 1). These parameters were chosen as they represent independent predictors of mortality. They capture complementary dimensions of host vulnerability, including global comorbidity, organ dysfunction, systemic inflammatory response, and neurological com-promise. Weighted according to their relative risk, they were combined in the 20-point score. The study by Lener et al. [9] demonstrated a strong correlation between increasing MSI-20 and mortality, with a cutoff of ≥10 points identifying patients at markedly increased risk.

While the implementation study established the conceptual validity of MSI-20, the present multicentric, multi-institutional registry study therefore aimed to externally validate the MSI-20 score in a large, heterogeneous cohort of patients treated for SI at 6 tertiary referral centers. The use of a multi-institutional registry is particularly critical in this context, as it enables the investigation of relatively rare outcomes such as mortality in SI by aggregating sufficiently large and diverse patient populations. Primary objectives were to assess the discriminatory performance of the MSI-20 for predicting mortality. We hypothesized that the MSI-20 would demonstrate reproducible predictive ability and provide a practical framework for mortality risk assessment and clinical risk stratification in SI.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Study Design and Setting

This was a retrospective, multicentric registry study conducted across 6 tertiary referral centers specializing in spinal surgery. Institutional databases were screened for SI cases treated between January 2010 and December 2023. None of the patients included in the original MSI-20 derivation cohort were included in the present validation dataset.

This study was approved by the ethics committees of Munich and Bern (approval numbers: 2023-204-S-SR and 2023-00461, respectively). The principal investigators at the participating institutions in Mainz, Berlin, Göttingen, and Geneva confirmed that, under the institutional regulations applicable to the retrospective data used in this study, separate ethics committee approval was not required for the analysis of anonymized clinical data. Only anonymized data were transferred for pooled analysis, and no directly identifiable patient information was shared with the coordinating center. The requirement for informed consent was waived or was not required under the applicable regulations of each participating institution.

2. Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Adult patients (≥18 years) were eligible if they met the following criteria: (1) Clinically, radiologically, and laboratory-confirmed SI (spondylodiscitis, vertebral osteomyelitis, or spinal epidural abscess). (2) Complete datasets for MSI-20 computation: body mass index (kg/m2) [11,12], ASA physical status classification [8], septic patient state [13,14], age-adjusted Charlson Comorbidity Index [15], renal insufficiency [13,14,16,17], hepatopathy [18,19], muscle force [20], CRP at diagnosis [21,22]. (3) Documented survival or death within 90 days of diagnosis. The primary outcome was 90-day all-cause mortality, defined as death from any cause within 90 days of diagnosis. Patients with incomplete variables, nonspinal infections, or pediatric patients were excluded.

3. Data Collection and MSI-20 Calculation

Demographic, clinical, and laboratory variables were extracted from electronic records according to Good Clinical Practice standards. Each patient’s MSI-20 score was calculated exactly as defined in the original publication, summing the weighted points for the 8 parameters (maximum, 20) (Table 1).

4. Statistical Analysis

All analyses were performed using R ver. 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Austria). Continuous variables are presented as means±standard deviations, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Predictive performance of the MSI-20 score was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, with the area under the curve (AUC) reported together with bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals (1,000 resamples). The optimal binary cutoff was determined using the closest-to-(0,1) criterion. Based on this cutoff, a confusion matrix was constructed to derive sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), accuracy, and F1-score, each with corresponding confidence intervals.

Mortality across MSI-20 strata was explored by grouping scores into bins of 2 points and visualized with 95% binomial confidence intervals to illustrate the stepwise increase in mortality risk. To assess inter-center variability, model performance metrics were additionally calculated separately for each participating center, including ROC analysis and confusion-matrix–derived measures using the predefined cutoff of ≥4 points.

In addition to the predefined MSI-20 variables, exploratory analyses were conducted to evaluate whether other baseline clinical characteristics were associated with mortality. Candidate variables included age, sex, treatment modality, infection etiology, infection localization, and the presence of epidural or paraspinal abscess. These analyses were performed using univariable logistic regression and were considered hypothesisgenerating.

RESULTS

1. Cohort Characteristics

Of 1,347 screened cases, 1,122 patients met the inclusion criteria. The mean age was 66.4±13.1 years, and 62% of patients were male. The mean MSI-20 score was 4.8±2.9, with an over all mortality rate of 18.3%.

2. Predictive Performance

The MSI-20 demonstrated fair discriminative ability, with an AUC of 0.68 (95% CI, 64–72) (Fig. 1). The ROC-derived optimal threshold corresponded to a score of 3.5. Patients were dichotomized into low- and high-risk groups using a cutoff of ≥4 points to improve clinical interpretability. Using this cutoff, sensitivity was 0.49 (42–56) and specificity 0.74 (71–77). Additional performance metrics included an accuracy of 0.69 (67–72), PPV of 0.30 (25–34), NPV of 0.87 (84–89), and an F1 score of 0.38 (33–42), indicating moderate overall classification performance (Table 2).

3. Score-Mortality Relationship

Mortality increased progressively across MSI-20 categories (Table 3). Patients with scores of 0–2 points had a mortality rate of 6.2%, compared with 47.4% in those with scores of 9–11 points. Because patient numbers in these highest MSI-20 categories were limited, mortality estimates in these extreme-risk strata should be interpreted cautiously. Center-wise analyses demonstrated comparable performance of the MSI-20 across institutions. In larger centers, AUC values predominantly ranged between 0.60 and 0.75, with no evidence of systematic performance differences, supporting the generalizability of the model.

4. Exploratory Predictor Analysis

Exploratory univariable analyses did not identify additional baseline variables with consistent strong associations beyond the MSI-20 components. Increasing age was significantly associated with higher mortality rates (p<0.001), while secondary infection etiology and conservative treatment were also associated with mortality (p<0.05). No significant associations were observed for sex, infection localization, or the presence of epidural or paraspinal abscess. Overall, no additional variable demonstrated a consistent association beyond the MSI-20 score in these exploratory analyses.

DISCUSSION

This study represents the first large multicentric external validation of the MSI-20 score, demonstrating its reproducibility and generalizability across diverse clinical settings. In addition to confirming its prognostic performance, we identify a clinically meaningful dual-threshold pattern, with a practical riskstratification cutoff and an upper extreme-risk zone, while exploratory analyses did not reveal additional baseline parameters with consistent incremental prognostic value beyond the MSI-20 components.

This multicentric external validation demonstrates that the MSI-20 score retains meaningful prognostic utility for mortality prediction in SI across a large and heterogeneous cohort. The score showed fair yet consistent discriminative performance (AUC, 0.68), closely aligning with the original derivation study9 and confirming the underlying concept of physiologically driven mortality risk stratification. Importantly, performance remained stable across centers, underscoring the reproducibility and external validity of the model and supporting its use as a pragmatic clinical tool in routine preoperative assessment.

Consistent with the original hypothesis, mortality increased progressively with rising MSI-20 values, confirming a clear doseresponse relationship. Patients with low scores (0–2 points) exhibited a mortality of only 7.2%, whereas mortality approached 50% (47.4%) at scores ≥9, highlighting the extreme-risk tail of the distribution. This observation emphasizes that the MSI-20 should be interpreted as a continuous risk gradient rather than a single dichotomous threshold. At the same time, the ROCderived cutoff of ≥4 points represents the point of maximal diagnostic discrimination and therefore provides a practical boundary for clinical risk stratification.

The model’s high NPV (87%) further supports its clinical relevance, as it allows reliable identification of patients at low mortality risk who may represent appropriate candidates for early surgical intervention after multidisciplinary assessment. This characteristic may be particularly valuable in multidisciplinary decision-making, where objective risk estimation is crucial for balancing operative risk against the consequences of delayed source control. The MSI-20 demonstrated fair discriminative performance and a high NPV, supporting its use as an adjunctive risk-stratification tool. However, the moderate sensitivity and low PPV indicate that the score should not be used as a definitive prognostic instrument for individual patients. Future studies may investigate whether dynamic clinical variables, microbiological characteristics, biomarker trajectories, and treatment-related factors improve prognostic accuracy beyond the baseline MSI-20 score.

1. Comparison With the SITE Score

Since the introduction of the MSI-20, the Spinal Infection Treatment Evaluation (SITE) score [23] has been proposed as an alternative decision-support tool incorporating both clinical and radiological variables. In its external validation, Quiceno et al. [24] reported an AUC of 0.66, which is comparable to the performance observed in the present study. However, the conceptual focus of the 2 scores differs substantially. While the MSI-20 quantifies systemic physiological vulnerability and mortality risk, the SITE score primarily addresses anatomical and radiological features to guide treatment strategy. Previous reports have also highlighted limitations of the SITE score, including inconsistent prediction of treatment allocation [24] and reduced interobserver reliability, particularly among nonspine specialists [25]. These differences suggest that the 2 tools may be complementary rather than interchangeable, with the MSI-20 providing a dedicated assessment of systemic risk. In clinical practice, the MSI-20 and SITE score may be used complementarily. The MSI-20 primarily captures systemic vulnerability, comorbidity burden, organ dysfunction, and inflammatory response, whereas the SITE score integrates anatomical and radiological features relevant to treatment strategy. A patient with a high SITE score but low MSI-20 may represent a candidate for timely surgical source control, whereas a patient with a high MSI-20 may require intensified monitoring, preoperative optimization, and multidisciplinary discussion before treatment decisions are finalized. Thus, MSI-20 should be understood as an adjunctive systemic risk tool rather than a replacement for anatomical or radiological decision-support systems.

2. Clinical Implications

The MSI-20 integrates key determinants of outcome in SI, including comorbidity burden, systemic inflammatory response, and organ dysfunction. Its weighting of renal impairment, septic state, and physiological reserve reflects well-established predictors of mortality in critically ill patients [8,13,16,17,19]. The current validation demonstrates that these parameters collectively provide a reproducible framework for mortality risk estimation across diverse clinical settings. Exploratory analyses did not identify additional baseline variables with consistent or clinically meaningful incremental prognostic value beyond the MSI-20 components. Although age and treatment modality were associated with mortality, these associations likely reflect case-mix differences rather than independent physiological determinants. These findings suggest that the principal domains of vulnerability captured by the MSI-20 remain the dominant drivers of mortality risk in SI, while future studies may explore whether additional dynamic or treatment-related factors improve prediction in selected subgroups. Together, these findings reinforce the dual interpretative framework of the MSI-20. A cutoff of ≥4 points enables clinically meaningful risk stratification, whereas scores in the upper range (approximately ≥9 points) may identify an exploratory extreme-risk zone characterized by markedly increased mortality. Although mortality increased substantially in the highest MSI-20 categories, the small number of patients in these strata limits the precision of these estimates. Therefore, the upper score range should be interpreted as an exploratory extreme-risk zone rather than a definitive treatment threshold. From a practical perspective, the MSI-20 may support clinical decision-making in 3 domains: (1) identification of lower-risk patients, (2) risk communication and shared decision-making in medically complex patients, and (3) risk stratification for future interventional trials. High MSI-20 scores should not be interpreted as surgical contraindications. Rather, they identify patients in whom intensified systemic optimization, careful perioperative planning, and multidisciplinary decision-making are particularly important. Rather, than replacing clinical judgment, the score provides an objective framework to contextualize physiological vulnerability.

3. The Role of Early Surgery

Our results are consistent with the treatment paradigm proposed in the original MSI-20 study [9], suggesting that patients with moderate scores may benefit from early surgical intervention despite substantial comorbidity. However, patients in the highest score range may require initial medical stabilization or, in selected cases, conservative management. These considerations align with emerging evidence supporting early surgical treatment in SI. A recent meta-analysis by Thavarajasingam et al. [10] demonstrated a 39% reduction in mortality and lower treatment failure rates with early surgery, while Kernich et al. [26] reported markedly improved survival compared with nonsurgical management. Within this context, the MSI-20 provides an objective framework to support shared decision-making and to balance operative risk against potential benefits in medically complex patients.

4. Strengths and Limitations

The strengths of this study include its large sample size, multicentric design, and rigorous statistical methodology with bootstrapped confidence intervals, all of which enhance the generalizability of the findings. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The retrospective design introduces potential confounding. Treatment protocols were not standardized across participating centers, and surgical indications, antimicrobial regimens, perioperative care, and postoperative management may have differed between institutions. Patients with incomplete MSI-20 variables were excluded because calculation of the cumulative score requires complete information on all predefined components. This may have introduced selection bias, particularly if missingness was associated with disease severity or center-specific documentation practices. Mortality was analyzed as an all-cause endpoint, and postoperative or long-term functional outcomes were not assessed. The moderate sensitivity and low PPV reflect the multifactorial nature of mortality in SI and the relatively low event rate. Because the present study focused on validation of discriminative performance and clinical risk stratification, formal calibration analyses were not performed. Although calibration is an important aspect of prediction model validation, the MSI-20 was originally conceived as a pragmatic risk-stratification score rather than a probability-based prediction model. Future prospective studies should evaluate calibration characteristics, including calibration plots, calibration slope/intercept, and Brier score. Also, future refinements may incorporate dynamic biomarkers, pathogen-specific factors, or treatment-related variables to improve prediction in the highest-risk subgroup.

CONCLUSION

This multicenter external validation confirms that the MSI-20 provides reproducible mortality risk stratification in SI using routinely available baseline clinical parameters. The score demonstrated fair discrimination and a clear risk gradient across increasing score categories. Given its simplicity and high NPV, the MSI-20 may serve as an adjunctive tool to support risk communication, multidisciplinary discussion, patient counseling, and preoperative optimization. It should not be interpreted as a stand-alone instrument for determining treatment strategy or surgical contraindications, but rather as one component of comprehensive clinical decision-making.
