Purpose: The relationship between patient-reported severity of septic shock and popular clinical risk scores, including the
quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) and the National Early Warning Score 2 (NEWS 2), is not well under­
stood in Intensive Care Unit (ICU) patients; this exploratory study investigates these associations.
Methods: A cross-sectional questionnaire-based study was conducted among 433 adult ICU patients with septic shock at a
tertiary-care hospital from March to November 2025. The demographic data, qSOFA and NEWS 2 scores, and severity of
symptoms as reported by the patients were all collected. The statistical methods used for the analysis included Pearson correla­
tion, independent-samples t-tests, one-way ANOVA, and multiple linear regression.
Results: qSOFA was positively correlated with NEWS 2 (r = 0.382) and patient-reported symptom severity (r = 0.417), while
NEWS 2 was correlated with patient-reported symptom severity (r = 0.356; all p < 0.001). Men scored higher on qSOFA and
NEWS 2, whereas women reported higher symptom severity (p < 0.001). Higher qSOFA and NEWS 2 scores were observed in
older patients, whereas higher patient-reported symptom severity was observed in younger patients (p < 0.01). Multiple regres­
sion analysis indicated that qSOFA, NEWS 2, female gender, younger age, and chronic disease were independently associated
with patient-reported symptom severity (p ≤ 0.02).
Conclusions: These results indicate exploratory associations between clinical risk scores and patient-reported symptom burden
in ICU patients with septic shock; findings should not be interpreted as predictive or causal.

Introduction
Sepsis is a serious condition that occurs when
the body's defence mechanisms react to an infec­
tion. If this reaction goes out of control, it can lead
to septic shock, which contributes to high global
morbidity and mortality, especially in low and middleincome countries1, 2). Some studies indicate that
critically ill patients who are overweight or moder­
ately obese have slightly lower adjusted mortality
rates than those with a normal weight. In contrast,
morbidly obese and underweight patients do not
show this difference3).
In intensive care units (ICUs), clinical risk scores
such as Sequential Organ Failure Assessment
(SOFA), Acute Physiology and Chronic Health
Evaluation (APACHE), and Modified Early Warning
Score (MEWS) are routinely applied for systemic
classification of the patients based on their risk,
management decision-taking, and outcome predic­
tion4-6). These tools are primarily based on objec­
tive physiological and laboratory parameters and
are well established in critical care practice.
Nevertheless, patient-reported symptom severity
is not commonly included in a standard ICU evalu­
ation. Critical care research evidence indicates reli­
ance mostly on physiological and surrogate outcomes,
and a lack of focus on patient-centred and patientreported outcomes7). Subjective illness burden may
manifest as fatigue, dyspnea, dizziness, confusion,
and generalised discomfort and not be entirely
reflected by physiological scoring systems alone8, 9).
Understanding the relationship between clinical
risk scores and patient-reported symptoms could
provide additional insight into the burden of illness
in ICU patients with septic shock.
Thus, examining the relationships between patientreported symptom severity and clinical risk scores,
without suggesting prediction or clinical outcome
evaluation, could help explain the relationship
between objective measures of illness severity and
subjective symptom experiences in this critically ill
group. A viable means of investigating these asso­
ciations in ICU patients with septic shock is through
survey-based methods.
Study novelty and research gap
Even though clinical risk scores, including the quick
Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA)

2

and the National Early Warning Score 2 (NEWS2),
have been extensively utilised to determine illness
severity and identify high-risk patients, little
research has examined their applicability to patientreported symptom burden in ICU patients with
septic shock. Moreover, the patient-reported level
of symptom severity is infrequently included in
risk assessment, even though it can provide early,
subjective indicators of clinical deterioration.
Our study, which concurrently measures qSOFA,
NEWS 2, and patient-reported symptom severity,
provides initial findings on the correlations among
these variables in the ICU with septic shock. The
patient-reported symptom severity scale used in
this study has not undergone formal psychometric
validation; therefore, this study aims to explore
associations rather than establish definitive clinical
relationships. This study provides an exploratory
view of the symptom burden in critically ill patients
by integrating objective clinical scoring with subjec­
tive patient-reported outcomes, which may guide
further prospective studies of symptom evaluation
and clinical measures.
Study objectives
In this study, the relationships between qSOFA,
NEWS 2, and patient-reported symptom severity
among ICU patients with septic shock are to be
analysed. Also, it explores possible exploratory rela­
tionships between symptom burden and these clin­
ical risk scores, without making predictions or clin­
ical-outcome assertions, to provide exploratory
insight into patient-reported symptom burden in
ICU patients.
Material and Methods
The relationship between patient-reported symp­
tom severity and clinical risk scores among ICU
patients with septic shock was analysed using a
cross-sectional questionnaire study. The research
was conducted in the intensive care unit of a
tertiary care hospital from March 2025 to November
2025. The data were collected through a structured
questionnaire that encompassed demographic char­
acteristics, clinical parameters, validated risk scores,
and patient-reported symptom severity.
Researchers who received the proper training
contacted patients who met the trial criteria or
their legally authorised representatives to inform

them about the study's aims and methods and to
obtain written consent before the onset of data
collection. The study participants' anonymity was
guaranteed, and they were informed of their with­
drawal right, which could be exercised at any time
without adverse consequences for their medical
treatment. The aforementioned data management
practice led to the use of standard methods for
collecting information, a reduction in participants'
irritation, and an increase in data reliability.
Recruitment and sample
As no data were available on the prevalence of
symptom burden in ICU patients with septic shock
at their local hospitals, a conservative value of 0.5
was used to calculate the sample size. This p-value
was plugged into the sample size calculation
formula, along with 95% confidence interval (Z =
1.96) and a 0.05 margin of error, to yield the
required sample size10). The sampling method in
the study was influenced by logistical limitations,
so patients with septic shock in the ICU were
recruited through convenience sampling. Conse­
quently, the sample might not be accurately repre­
sentative of the normal demographic representa­
tion of the patients with septic shock in the ICU,
who, in many cases, are old and have many comorbid
conditions.
All of the patients in the intensive care unit who
were recently diagnosed with septic shock were
invited to participate in the study. Of the patients
screened, 433 met the eligibility criteria, signed the
informed consent form, and were analysed as part
of the study. The use of convenience sampling
enabled the recruitment of participants in the crit­
ical care environment quickly. Convenience sampling
may have resulted in a younger, healthier ICU
cohort compared with the typical septic shock
population, limiting generalizability.
Eligibility criteria
The research was conducted on adults who were
18 years old and older and were diagnosed with
septic shock and admitted to the ICU. The inclu­
sion criteria required complete clinical data for
these patients and that they be able to participate
either directly or through a surrogate respondent
when clinically suitable for symptom assessment.
If a patient had incomplete medical records, was

admitted for non-septic shock, or could not give
consent and had no legally authorised representa­
tive, that patient was excluded. Furthermore, patients
with advance directives, instructions that limit active
management, were discharged before the researchers
finished data collection and were therefore excluded.
Instruments and procedures
Data collection was performed using a structured
questionnaire comprising three main parts: demo­
graphic and clinical characteristics, clinical risk scores,
and patient-reported symptom severity. Demo­
graphic data comprised age, sex and relevant clin­
ical information retrieved from medical charts.
The quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment
(qSOFA) score was proposed by Seymour et al. in
2016 in conjunction with the Third International
Consensus Definitions for Sepsis and Septic Shock
(Sepsis-3). It is a bedside screening tool that has
been simplified and developed for quickly identi­
fying patients with suspected infection who are at
risk of adverse outcomes outside the ICU. The
qSOFA score is based on three clinical criteria:
systolic blood pressure ≤ 100 mmHg, respiratory
rate ≥ 22 breaths per minute, and altered mental
status (Glasgow Coma Scale score <15). Each of
the three criteria is assigned 1 point, and a total
score between 0 and 3 is possible. A qSOFA score
of two or more is deemed a sign of heightened risk
of adverse outcomes, such as organ failure and
death. Because of its straightforwardness and reli­
ance on readily accessible clinical measures, qSOFA
is widely used in emergent and intensive medical
care to quickly classify risk among septic patients11).
The qSOFA score was used in the original English
version in this study, without modification. Even
though qSOFA was initially developed for early
risk stratification in the absence of the ICU, included
in this exploratory study to examine its association
with patient-reported symptom burden in ICU
patients, recognizing that it is not validated for ICU
populations.
The National Early Warning Score 2 (NEWS 2)
was created by the Royal College of Physicians
(RCP) in the United Kingdom, and the revised
edition was released in 2017. The NEWS 2 was
designed to detect the clinical deterioration of
acutely ill patients in hospitals at an early stage and
includes patients suffering from sepsis and septic

shock. The scoring consists of six daily-measured
physiological factors: respiratory rate, oxygen satu­
ration, systolic blood pressure, heart rate, tempera­
ture, and consciousness level measured by the
AVPU scale, plus a plus for the use of extra oxygen.
Each parameter is evaluated on a scale of 0 to 3
based on the allowed limits, and the total score is
calculated from 0 to 20. A NEWS 2 score of ≥ 5
indicates that an immediate clinical evaluation is
required, and a score of ≥ 7 indicates a very highrisk patient who should receive care first12). The
NEWS 2 system was used in this study in its offi­
cial English version.
The researchers utilised the patient-reported
symptom severity scale in the study to system­
atically evaluate subjective symptoms commonly
observed in patients with septic shock in the ICU.
Participants were asked to rate the intensity of
seven symptoms: fatigue or weakness, dizziness or
lightheadedness, shortness of breath, pounding or
rapid heartbeat, faintness or low blood pressure,
confusion or poor concentration, and nausea or
vomiting, all occurring within the last 24 hours.
These seven items were selected based on a review
of previous ICU symptom studies and clinician
consensus on the most common and clinically rele­
vant symptoms experienced by patients with septic
shock. All symptoms were rated using a five-point
scale with 0 (none) to 4 (very severe), and a Total
Symptom Severity Score was obtained (0 to 28),
where higher scores indicated higher symptom
burden. The overall scores were also divided into
four groups: 0-7 (mild), 8-14 (moderate), 15-21
(severe), and 22-28 (very severe). This scale
enabled a systematic examination of patient-re­
ported symptoms and their association with clinical
risk scores. This is a locally developed patientreported symptom severity scale. It has not been
subject to formal validation, such as reliability (e.g.
Cronbach's alpha) and construct validity tests, nor
has it been applicable to external populations.
Consequently, the findings from this scale must be
treated as exploratory associations rather than
conclusive indicators of symptom severity.
Patients completed the questionnaires themselves
or with assistance from trained research staff,
depending on their condition. The completed ques­
tionnaires were verified and stored in a safe place
for later analysis.

Statistical plan
The data analysis was performed using IBM
SPSS Statistics version 26. For categorical vari­
ables, descriptive statistics such as frequencies and
percentages were utilised, while continuous vari­
ables were presented as means ± standard devia­
tions. Associations between qSOFA, NEWS 2, and
PRSS scores were evaluated by Pearson correla­
tion analysis. Gender differences were compared
using independent-samples t-tests, and one-way
ANOVA was used to investigate differences among
age groups, reporting partial eta-squared (η2) as a
measure of effect size. Multiple linear regression
was used to identify factors associated with PRSS,
with the independent variables being qSOFA,
NEWS 2, age, gender, chronic disease, and smoking
status. A two-tailed approach was used for all tests,
and p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically
significant. By utilising this method, it was possible
to evaluate the relationships between clinical risk
scores and patient-reported symptom severity, to
test for significant differences between groups, and
to identify the main predictors of symptom burden
in ICU patients with septic shock. Multicollinearity
among independent variables was assessed using
the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), and all values
were below the commonly accepted threshold, indi­
cating no significant multicollinearity.
Ethical standards
The study was conducted in accordance with the
basic ethical principles for human research. The
institutional review board of Lewisham and Green­
wich NHS Trust hospital (IRB/LGNT/2026/EA0147) granted ethical clearance prior to the study.
Participation was entirely voluntary, and written
informed consent was obtained from all partici­
pants or their legally authorised representatives.
All gathered data were made anonymous to
guarantee privacy and kept in a secure place with
limited access. The participants were told that
their refusal to take part or leave would not affect
their current treatment. The missing or incomplete
data were handled so that the validity of the conclu­
sions remained intact.

Discussion
This study aimed to examine the associations
between pre-existing clinical risk scales (qSOFA
and NEWS 2) and patient-reported symptom
severity in ICU patients with septic shock. These
findings are descriptive and exploratory; they
should not be interpreted as predictive of clinical
outcomes. Such associations cannot be taken as a
signal of prognostic or predictive value in the ICU
environment. In the current study, qSOFA and
NEWS showed a moderate positive correlation,
indicating that patients who scored higher on one
tool tended to score higher on the other. This is in
accordance with earlier literature, which suggests
that both qSOFA and NEWS tend to show higher
values in patients with more severe clinical presen­

tations and often demonstrate parallel patterns13).
In our research, patient-reported symptom severity
was moderately associated with high qSOFA scores.
This is in line with previous studies on acute decom­
pensated heart failure that indicate an increase in
the level of symptoms with an increase in the
qSOFA score to more serious symptoms like
dyspnea14). Similarly, higher NEWS scores were
associated with greater patient-reported symptom
severity, thus supporting previous studies showing
that they were associated with higher symptom
burden15).
Concerning the differences between genders, the
males' scores on qSOFA and NEWS were higher,
which corroborates the findings of previous ICU
and ED studies that indicated male patients had
higher qSOFA and NEWS scores than females,
consistent with previous ICU studies16, 17). On the

other hand, female participants showed much
greater severity of the symptoms than the male
ones; this is consistent with previous findings indi­
cating that women tend to report higher levels of
symptom-related distress, especially pain, fatigue,
and nausea18).
Age was associated with differences in clinical
severity scores and symptom perception. The older
participants showed higher qSOFA and slightly
higher NEWS scores, consistent with previous
research demonstrating that older participants had
higher qSOFA and slightly higher NEWS scores,
consistent with previous findings19, 20). On the
contrary, both patient-reported symptom severity
and age were inversely related, consistent with
previous findings that older people tend to report
fewer and less severe symptoms across several
areas and other domains21).
The multiple regression analysis results revealed
the variables related to patient-reported severity
of the symptoms, corroborating prior studies
linking clinical severity, sex, and age to symptom
burden13, 15, 18, 21). Chronic disease was also associated
with higher patient-reported symptom severity,
which is in line with past studies that have shown
that patients with chronic conditions suffer from a
multitude of severe and bothersome symptoms
that impair daily activities and the overall quality
of life22). Smokers reported slightly lower symptom
severity, though causality cannot be inferred, which
is in line with earlier research that indicated the
possibility of under-reporting or delayed recogni­
tion of symptoms in smokers23).
Overall, these findings demonstrate significant
associations between clinical severity scores, demo­
graphic characteristics, comorbidities, and patientreported symptom severity. Given the cross-sec­
tional design, these relationships should be inter­
preted as associative rather than causal.

with septic shock was relatively young, which may
have been a convenience sample and also a result
of the nature of the tertiary-care ICU. This skew in
the population can restrict externalising the results
to the general population of septically shocked, crit­
ically ill patients. The qSOFA score was originally
developed for early risk stratification outside the
ICU setting. In this study, qSOFA was applied
solely to ICU patients for exploratory association
analysis; therefore, the observed relationships should
not be interpreted as evidence of predictive validity
in critically ill populations. The patient-reported
symptom severity scale (PRSS) is a locally devel­
oped instrument that has not undergone formal
psychometric validation, including reliability testing
and construct validity assessment. As such, the
PRSS should be considered an exploratory measure,
and findings on symptom severity should be inter­
preted with caution. In addition, alterations in the
patient's consciousness, the use of a proxy, and
errors in symptom recall may affect symptom
reporting in seriously ill patients.
Future research should employ prospective, longi­
tudinal, and multicentre designs to examine temporal
relationships between clinical risk scores, patientreported symptoms, and subsequent clinical outcomes.
Formal validation of symptom-based instruments,
including psychometric testing, is necessary to
enhance reliability and applicability across diverse
ICU populations.

identified. Firstly, the cross-sectional, questionnairebased method does not allow one to infer causes. It
does not allow for establishing the time correlation
between high scores and the severity of symptoms

In conclusion, the current study established that
qSOFA and NEWS 2 are both correlated with the
intensity of patient-reported symptoms in ICU
patients with septic shock. The differences between
genders and age groups emphasise the need to
consider patient-reported symptom burden and

symptom burden and clinical severity among ICU
