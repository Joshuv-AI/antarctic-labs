Registries in Spine – What Have We Learned So Far, and What Will the Future Bring?
Authors: Paul Gerdhem, Bjorn Knutsson, Olof Wolf, Victor E. Staartjes, Adrian Elmi-Terander
Published: 2026

INTRODUCTION

In this thematic issue of Neurospine, several registry-based papers are presented. We would like to share some of our experience in registry-based research over the past 3 decades.

The earliest records of illness and treatment were simple descriptions—observations captured to remember individual patients and their outcomes. Over time, these evolved into more structured documentation, enabling not only individual follow-up but also the systematic collection of data across groups of patients [1]. The increasing use of large-scale data will further strengthen the power of this transformation, with national registries playing a leading role.

THE EMERGENCE OF NATIONAL REGISTRIES

The Swedish Spine Registry (Swespine) was initiated as a local registry at Lund University Hospital, Sweden, in the early 1990s. The key to a successful national implementation was an increasing interest in monitoring and improving patient outcome by spinal surgeons. Through the Swedish Society of Spinal Surgeons, the registry was implemented nationally in the late 1990s. Initially focused on lumbar conditions, the registry expanded in 2006 to include degenerative conditions in the cervical and thoracic spine, as well as deformity, infection, fracture and metastasis.

Annual meetings have been held to discuss registration issues, including both local registry administrative personnel and surgeons, as well as the steering board of Swespine. The original software for the Swespine registry was also provided to other interested national spinal societies at cost, but modernization has in recent years led to the use of commercially available software platforms. Coverage of spinal units has been more than 90% and 1-year completeness of patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) around 75% for many years. High coverage and completeness have also been driven by governmental demands and financing, in line with an increased governmental interest.

Similar implementation stories are valid for other registries of Northern European countries, such as the Norwegian and Danish spine registries, and the recently introduced Finnish Spine registry [2-4]. In Asia, the Japanese Spine and Spinal Cord registry has recently been established [5].

The Swedish Fracture Registry was initiated at Sahlgrenska University Hospital in 2011 to collect data on extremity and pelvic fractures, both surgically and non-surgically treated [6,7]. In 2015, the collection of data on vertebral fractures was transferred from Swespine into this registry, also enabling collection of data on non-surgically treated patients [8].

Beyond these examples, numerous other health registries have been established, enabling both national and international comparisons of treatment strategies and outcomes.

FROM OBSERVATIONAL DATA TO INTERVENTIONAL RESEARCH

One of the most important developments in recent years has been the emergence of registry-based randomized controlled trials (rRCTs), the first successful trial being conducted in the field of interventional cardiology [9,10]. By embedding randomization within existing registry infrastructures, these studies combine the internal validity of randomized trials with the external validity of a registry. This enables large-scale studies at relatively low cost. The Swedish Fracture Register’s digital registration was found to be an excellent tool to bring rRCTs into orthopaedics [8]. The Fracture Registry platform is used to notify the registering physician of eligibility, and randomization can then be performed after patient consent [11-13]. Recently, 3 large rRCTs have completed enrollment, and are now in different stages of completion.

CHALLENGES IN DATA COLLECTION AND VALIDITY

Despite their strengths, registries face important methodological challenges. Early experience from the Swedish Spine Registry demonstrated that extensive data collection directly from surgeons was associated with incomplete reporting. Collecting fewer, well-defined variables resulted in improved compliance. Data validity is generally good to excellent [14,15]. However, complications and reoperations reported by surgeons are consistently underreported. To address this limitation, alternative methods are increasingly used, particularly linkage to other registries.

THE ROLE OF DATA LINKAGE

The ability to link data across registries and administrative databases is one of the most powerful features of modern registry-based research. In many Northern European countries, this is facilitated by the national population register and the use of personal identification numbers. These enable longitudinal follow-up and comprehensive capture of outcomes, including complications and mortality. Balancing accessibility and protection of personal data remains a central challenge.

OUTCOME MEASUREMENT IN SPINE SURGERY

Common disease-specific questionnaires for spinal disorders include the Oswestry Disability Index and the Neck Disability Index. For fractures, the Short Musculoskeletal Function Assessment is used. The EQ-5D is used as a generic quality-of-life instrument for both spinal disorders and fractures.

EXPANDING THE SCOPE OF REGISTRIES

Traditionally, many registries have focused on surgical interventions. However, the inclusion of nonsurgical treatments is essential for a comprehensive understanding of patient care pathways. Without such data, it is difficult to evaluate treatment effectiveness or to compare surgical and nonsurgical strategies.

Harmonization of data collection across registries is another important goal. Standardized definitions and outcome measures facilitate comparisons and enable data pooling at national and international levels.

THE FUTURE OF REGISTRIES

Looking ahead, registries are likely to play an increasingly central role in personalized medicine. The dialogue support tool developed by Swespine, which is a clinical outcome prediction model based on national data, may be used to support decision making [16,17]. Ongoing work tries to improve predictive capabilities [18]. Concepts such as “digital twins” or individualized predictive models may allow clinicians to simulate treatment outcomes for individual patients based on large-scale registry data. This could improve decision making and tailor treatments to patientspecific characteristics. An improved prediction may require both greater data granularity and more patients. International collaborations may therefore be important, although limitations may include legal, cultural and health care differences.

Other ways of increasing data completeness and accuracy include automated export from medical records of the variables of interest. Such efforts are ongoing, and the Norwegian Spine Registry will soon launch a working module.

EFFICACY VERSUS EFFECTIVENESS

Registries are particularly well-suited to study treatment effectiveness in real-world settings, complementing randomized trials that primarily assess efficacy under controlled conditions. Together, these approaches provide a more complete understanding of treatment outcomes and allow more pragmatic studies depicting spine surgery on a national level with epidemiologically accurate coverage.

Due to recent changes in the European Medical Device Regulation, all companies and healthcare providers must demonstrate the safety and performance of their implants. Spine and fracture registries are inexpensive and reliable, making them a potential source for collecting these outcomes.

SUMMARY

Our registries have been started by a few pioneers, surgeons with an interest in surveying treatment and PROMs, successively leading to national implementation, which was driven by surgeons. After some years, governmental interest has increased, realizing the value of systematically collecting data on treatment and outcome. We believe that a successful start of a registry must come from the profession, and at some time point include a specialty society, and preferably stakeholders such as patient representatives and health care funders, and all must have a genuine interest in surveying treatments and outcomes. Systems that avoid duplicate and redundant registration will definitely improve compliance.

Registries have become an integral part of modern healthcare, enabling systematic evaluation of treatments and their outcomes in routine clinical practice. The inclusion of patient-reported outcomes has broadened the perspective on treatment success, while registry-based trials have accelerated the generation of high-quality evidence. Meanwhile, reduced governmental funding threatens the sustainability of many registries, necessitating an ongoing balance between the extent of data collection and the maintenance of data quality. Ultimately, registries have moved clinical research closer to everyday practice—and may continue to shape the way we generate and apply evidence in the years to come.
