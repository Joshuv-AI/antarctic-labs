Current landscape of pediatric optic neuritis management in India: A clinical practice survey
Authors: Adeline S Lasrado, Ramesh Kekunnaya, Snehal Ganatra, Virender Sachdeva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To understand the practice patterns in the management of pediatric optic neuritis in India and detect the gaps in evaluation and management.Methods:An online survey-based study questionnaire was shared among ophthalmologists in the last quarter of 2024. The questionnaire included questions pertaining to the clinical experience, evaluation, and management of optic neuritis in children.Results:Seventy ophthalmologists across India participated in the survey. Of these, 45% had a formal neuro-ophthalmology training and 42% had more than 5 years of experience in managing pediatric optic neuritis. Most respondents managed under less than five cases every month. Participants with under 5 years’ clinical experience reported higher rates of myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein-associated disease and post-infectious ON followed by neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder, multiple sclerosis, and chronic relapsing inflammatory optic neuritis. Visual fields (50%) and optical coherence tomography (45%) were frequently requested ocular investigations. Magnetic resonance imaging brain and spine with contrast and fat-suppressed sequence was recommended by 23% of respondents, serological workup in 91%, and cerebrospinal fluid analysis in 17%. For treatment, intravenous methylprednisolone (IVMP) was used by 94% for 3 days (45%) or 5 days (53%). 34% ophthalmologists advised IVMP dosing at 30 mg/kg. In recurrent cases, those with less than 5 years’ experience more often repeated neuroimaging (P = 0.050) and initiated IVMP irrespective of vision (P = 0.046).Conclusions:This survey highlights the variability in diagnostic testing and management practices of pediatric optic neuritis among ophthalmologists in India underscoring the need for developing, guiding, and following standardized practice guidelines.

Optic neuritis is characterized by an acute to subacute decrease in vision, dyschromatopsia, and pain on eye movements. While about 50% of the cases can be postinfectious/idiopathic, acute demyelinating pediatric optic neuritis constitutes about 50% cases in a child[12] and can be associated with neuroinflammatory syndromes which include multiple sclerosis (MS), acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM), neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD), or myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG)-associated optic neuritis,[345] which can lead to poor visual outcomes, and an increased risk of recurrence if not intervened timely. While in adults, the disease clinical profile, approach, and management are extensively studied, it is relatively uncommon in children with a less defined clinical approach and management strategies.[267] Reports suggest that optic neuritis in children differs between western population and India in terms of higher rate of bilaterality, worse outcomes, and a lower rate of development of MS in India.[89] Pediatric optic neuritis is also different from the adults in terms of bilaterality, painless profound vision loss, and optic disc swelling in comparison to adult optic neuritis.[1011]

Despite the insights from studies like those conducted by Pediatric Eye Disease Investigator Group;[12] three Indian retrospective studies by Khadse et al.,[13] Ambika et al.,[3] and Singh D et al.;[14] and one prospective study by Kowsalya A et al.,[15] the data on pediatric optic neuritis management in India are limited. Consequently, gaps persist in the development of effective tailored management guidelines for optic neuritis in children of the Indian population.

Currently, the approach to diagnosis and management varies between institutions and practitioners in India, underscoring the need for standardized national guidelines for management of pediatric optic neuritis in India.

The purpose of this study was to understand the experience and practice patterns among Indian ophthalmologists on the burden of the disease and treatment strategies of pediatric optic neuritis in India.

Methods

A cross-sectional, Internet-based survey was performed using Google forms, which were sent to ophthalmologists and neurologists in the last quarter of 2024 to evaluate the self-reported practice patterns of ophthalmologists in management of pediatric optic neuritis. The survey link was distributed among ophthalmologists practicing in India through professional networks and alumni groups. It was shared via WhatsApp with reminders sent at 1- and 3-months follow-ups. Eligible participants included ophthalmologists practicing in medical colleges, academic institutional settings, and even private institutes. The study was approved by the ethics committee of our institute and adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Data were anonymized for analysis. This survey questionnaire was self-developed containing 32 questions pertaining to respondents’ area of practice, clinical experience, their evaluation, and management in dealing with different types of pediatric optic neuritis to characterize clinical practice patterns [e-Supplement Table 1]. Participation was voluntary, and no personal identifiable information was collected. Responses to individual questions were not mandatory. However, questionnaires with five or more unanswered questions were excluded from final analysis.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analysis for count and percentage was performed using Microsoft Excel v16.93 (Microsoft Inc, Richmond, USA). Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Differences in the practice patterns between respondents with less than 5 years and more than 5 years of clinical experience, among respondents with formal neuro-ophthalmology training versus other respondents, were evaluated using Fisher Exact Test (extended) and Pearson’s Chi-square test using the online statistical calculator (VassarStats.net). A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Distribution of respondents

Seventy respondents participated in the survey. The only neurologist response was excluded due to single representation, and five responses were excluded due to incomplete responses, i.e., responses with five or more unanswered questions (n = 5) were excluded from the analysis. Among the remaining participants, 45% were certified neuro-ophthalmologists and 42% were pediatric ophthalmologists with interest in neuro-ophthalmology. 23% had <3 years of experience, 3 to 5 years in 34%, 5–10 years in 17%, and >10 years of clinical experience in 25% [Table 1].

Among the respondents, 84% reported seeing fewer than five patients per month in their practice. 80% of respondents managed pediatric optic neuritis cases independently, while 19% referred cases to neuro-ophthalmologists.

Etiology and diagnostic workup

Etiological diagnosis of postvaccination and MOG-associated disease (MOGAD) was most common, followed by NMOSD, MS, and chronic relapsing inflammatory optic neuritis (CRION). At presentation, 32% respondents advised a combination of visual fields, visual evoked potential (VEP), and optical coherence tomography (OCT) of the peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) and macula ganglion cell analysis (mGCL), and 25% respondents advised only visual fields, OCT of RNFL and mGCL.

Neuroimaging and serological workup

All respondents advised neuroimaging. However, only 23% respondents advised for magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of brain and orbit with contrast and fat suppression along with MRI whole spine screening, 34% respondents advised MRI brain and orbit with contrast with fat suppression sequence, 20% obtained MRI brain and orbit with contrast (no emphasis on fat suppression), and 20% respondents did not request for orbital cuts [Fig. 2].

Neuroimaging protocol requested at initial episode and repeat episode by participants in our survey

Ninety-one percent respondents advised serological workup. Interestingly, 38% respondents ordered serology irrespective of imaging findings and 27% advised serological workup only if MRI features were suggestive of NMOSD or MOG type and 10% if MRI features were suggestive of any neuroinflammatory syndrome and/or recurrent optic neuritis. 9% of respondents left it for neurologists to decide on the serology testing.

Among all participants, 24/64 (38%) participants preferred the specific serological work up and the testing method they advised. 50% advised NMOSD and MOG antibody testing irrespective of the method, 25% recommended NMOSD and MOG via cell-based assay, and 21% advised ANA (anti-nuclear antibody) profile in addition to NMOSD and MOG serology test. In cases of double seronegative pediatric optic neuritis, workup for systemic autoimmune disease was advised by 54% of the respondents.

CSF analysis was not routinely recommended by 83% of respondents. Among those who advised CSF analysis, 53% respondents advised CSF analysis in children showing atypical features and 21% in children with MRI features of MS.

Management of the first episode

Ninety four percent of these patients were treated with intravenous methylprednisolone (IVMP) followed by tapering oral steroids, 75% respondents treated confirmed cases irrespective of best corrected vision at presentation, and 14% respondents in patients with vision < 20/100 in both eyes. The duration of IVMP therapy was 3 days in 45% and for 5 days in 53%. 63% respondents administered the dosage of 15 mg/kg body weight, and 34% administered the dose of 30 mg/kg body weight. Following pulse IVMP, the patients were started tapering oral steroids. 48% respondents initially started their patients steroids at a dosage of 1 mg/kg for 2 weeks and then tapered based on serological results, 34% prescribed oral steroids on a weekly tapering basis, and 15% respondents gave tapering oral steroids for 11 days and stopped.

Systemic neuroinflammatory cases

In NMOSD, 33% of respondents maintained patients on long-term low-dose oral steroids, 20% prescribed azathioprine, 17% recommended rituximab, and 10% respondents referred the patients to a neurologist. For MOGAD pediatric optic neuritis, 31% of respondents maintained their patients on slow taper of low-dose oral steroids, 25% on Azathioprine, 17% on Rituximab, and 13% referred patients to a neurologist. In MS-associated optic neuritis, 33% respondents used low-dose oral steroids, 28% used Interferon beta, and 23% referred patients to neurologists. For CRION, 44% recommended low-dose oral steroids with a prolonged taper and 21% recommended Mycophenolate Mofetil [Table 1].

The total duration of immunosuppressive agents in pediatric optic neuritis was guided as per the neurologist in 59% of the respondents, 15% continued for a minimum of 6 months, 13% for up to 1 year, and 9% used based on the patient profile and a recurrence free period of at least 3 years.

Recurrent pediatric optic neuritis

In case of recurrent pediatric optic neuritis, among the 24 respondents, 63% repeated neuroimaging, while 29% repeated imaging only if contrast was not initially performed. MRI brain and orbits with contrast was advised in 54% and 42% additionally recommended whole spine screening [Fig. 2].

77% referred these patients to a neurologist, 11% of respondents switched their patients to other immunosuppressive agents, commonly to rituximab, and 7% continued the same immunosuppressive agents with augmented dose.

Regarding the follow-up, 48% of the respondents followed up every 3 to 6 months for the first year, 25% in every 2 months, 14% based on patient symptoms, and 12% respondents referred the patients to a pediatric neurologist. At each follow-up visit, 41% respondents performed visual field and OCT RNFL and MGCL, 20% obtained only visual fields, and 14% performed only OCT.

Tables 2 and 3 show that <5 years experienced, and neuro-ophthalmology-trained ophthalmologists favored structured protocol-based management, while senior and nontrained clinicians used more individualized or referral-based approaches (referral to neurologists).

Discussion

This online survey assessed the diagnosis and management practices for pediatric optic neuritis among Indian ophthalmologists with neuro-ophthalmology training or interest. Most respondents (75%) had less than 10 years’ clinical experience in neuro-ophthalmology; 84% saw fewer than five pediatric optic neuritis cases per month. This highlights that pediatric optic neuritis in India is relatively uncommon. Interestingly, 80% of them were managing these cases independently. The study also compared clinicians with ‘less than 5 years’ versus ‘more than five years clinical experience’ and with formal neuro-ophthalmology training versus others with interest in the subject to identify factors influencing the management of pediatric optic neuritis.

In pediatric optic neuritis, MS-related optic neuritis is less frequent in India and Asia as compared to the western countries.[316] Postinfectious and postvaccination optic neuritis are common etiologies, along with 50% of the cases being MOG optic neuritis.[4517] Notably, over 25% of pediatric optic neuritis cases were associated with postvaccination, postinfectious (16%), and MOGAD (15%) groups, which is similar to other studies in the literature.[231819]

In a retrospective study by Ambika et al.,[3] 48% of children were able to perform visual field testing. In the prospective study by Kowsalya A et al.[15] among the 25% of studied eyes that could complete the test, generalized visual field constriction was observed in 11% and centrocecal scotoma in 7.4%. Visual field testing might be limited by the age, presenting visual acuity, and availability of the devices; however, this is considered an important baseline investigation for optic neuritis to document baseline visual function.

NMOSD optic neuritis leads to greater, uniform RNFL loss (30–40 mm across quadrants) with/without pronounced microcystic changes at the macula unlike MS optic neuritis which shows predominantly temporal RNFL thinning.[202122] OCT is a potential biomarker to predict disease progression, prognosis, and recurrence by assessing neuronal injury, especially macular ganglion cell loss and retinal nerve fiber layer thinning. This is particularly valuable in children where visual field testing can be challenging, and unreliable. However, this may not be mandatory at presentation but may be indicated if there is suspicion of another nondemyelinating etiology. Follow-up OCT changes start around 3–4 weeks later and are definitely indicated from there.

Visual evoked potential (VEP) is a noninvasive test that is sensitive to detect silent optic neuritis in initial episodes showing prolonged P100 latency and decreased amplitude with MS who have no prior optic nerve involvement.[23] Wilejto M et al.[24] reported 88% sensitivity of VEP for diagnosing optic neuritis in children. Interestingly, in our survey, 8% of the respondents relied solely on VEP. But it risks missing early or recurrent optic neuritis, lacks structural evidence of the lesion, and cannot distinguish between active and inactive disease or distinguish between different demyelinating etiologies.

Ideally, a combination of clinical examination with visual fields, VEP, and OCT should be used to monitor disease progression and prognosis, as advised by 32% of practitioners in our study. However, 27% respondents did not obtain visual fields at presentation, possibly due to difficulty of the test in younger children, poor vision, or cooperation. However, whenever possible, this combined approach enhances diagnostic accuracy, number of sites affected, and disease management.

MRI white matter lesions are important objective markers of active lesions in optic neuritis. Contrast-enhanced orbital MRI can show thickening, and hyperintensities within the optic nerve that show enhancement after contrast injection. MRI brain and spine MRI, including FLAIR sequences, help identify associated neuroinflammatory syndromes.[2526] Our survey showed a variability of the protocol ordered among respondents. 23% requested MRI brain, orbit imaging with contrast and fat-suppressed sequences along with MRI spine imaging which is essential for accurate diagnosis and subclassifying the type of neuroinflammatory syndrome.[27] 20% of the respondents did not request orbital cuts or fat-suppressed sequences which are crucial to delineate the extent of optic nerve involvement, and detect associated perineuritis. Inadequate imaging and inadequate attention to the length of the optic nerve involvement may deny understanding the underlying etiology. This might contribute to a lower reported incidence of neuroinflammatory syndromes in India compared to Western countries.[34]

AQP4 antibodies evaluation is critical due to direct astrocyte damage and neuronal necrosis leading to secondary demyelination which affects the overall management of the patients.[52829] In contrast, MOGAD has better visual prognosis but high risk of recurrence.[5] Our survey showed 90% respondents recommended serology testing, 38% respondents regardless of MRI findings, 27% with atypical imaging features, and 10% for recurrent cases. Nearly half advised AQP4 and MOG antibody evaluation irrespective of the testing method, while 25% requested only for cell-based assays which are more specific and 21% additionally requested for ANA profile. The method of serological testing affects the sensitivity and specificity of the results. The cell-based assays are more sensitive.[19] Bilateral optic nerve involvement with ADEM was associated with MOG antibodies in about 50% of cases.[19] Longitudinal follow-up of MOG antibody titers guides treatment duration and predict recurrence.[193031] Limited availability of live cell-based assay In India remains a challenge. About 50% of the respondents also test for other autoimmune disease in double seronegative optic neuritis in children. Sudhakar P et al.[27] recommended additional testing for other autoimmune diseases in children with poor recovery and recurrent optic neuritis to minimize underdiagnosis and optimize treatment.

CSF analysis is not routinely recommended for all pediatric optic neuritis. It is reserved for children with atypical presentations, poorly resolving optic neuritis, or suspected MS. Only 17% of the respondents in our survey advised CSF analysis as a primary investigation. Given the low incidence of MS in Indian children and the invasive nature of lumbar puncture, we can limit CSF analysis to children with optic neuritis having features suggestive of MS and in recurrent, poorly resolved cases. However, due to a higher incidence of postinfectious etiology in pediatric optic neuritis, only a few authors recommended CSF analysis in all patients.[17]

Currently, no clinical trials have studied the management of pediatric optic neuritis. The management is based on adult treatment guidelines extrapolated from the ONTT study and emerging evidence for different neuroinflammatory disorders in adults. ONTT recommended treatment with IVMP for 3–5 days at 15–30 mg/kg/day to hasten visual recovery.[32] Initial high-dose IMVP therapy hastens recovery, helping resume their studies, and thus positively impacts the wellbeing of the children and caregivers.[33]

In our survey, 94% of respondents treated pediatric optic neuritis with IVMP. Among them, 75% respondents administered pulse steroids to all patients regardless of their presenting vision. Treatment duration varied with 45% giving IVMP for 3 days and 53% opted for 5 days. While nearly two-thirds of the respondents advised IVMP in a moderate dose of 15 mg/kg body weight, nearly one-third of them administered it at a dose of 30 mg/kg body weight, suggesting possible undertreatment. Theoretically, it might be associated with an increased risk of recurrence; however, this needs to be established with future prospective studies. Based on the current literature, initial treatment with IVMP of 30 mg/kg body weight for 5 days is the recommended practice pattern in all children presenting with optic neuritis.

It is widely accepted that a prolonged taper will be useful in patients with NMOSD/MOG disease. However, Jayakody H et al.[34] compared the duration of steroid treatment of 2 weeks versus more than 2 weeks and found no difference in the relapse rate, side effects, or final visual outcome in the two treatment groups. In our study, oral steroids tapering practices after IVMP have been inconsistent with nearly half of the respondents prescribing 1 mg/kg for 2 weeks followed by slow tapering based on a positive serology for NMOSD/MOG; one-third of the respondents taper the steroids on a weekly basis, and 15% administer for 11 days irrespective of imaging or serology findings. Developing uniform tapering protocols based on prospective collaborative research will enhance treatment consistency and efficacy in pediatric optic neuritis.

The choice of immunosuppressive therapy to prevent recurrent disease depends on the underlying neuroinflammatory syndrome. Chitnis T et al.[35] observed that in pediatric MS, Fingolimod is superior to β-Interferon but with more adverse effects, while Vollmer BL et al. and Krysko KM et al. observed that Rituximab was superior in efficacy in MS compared to Natalizumab and Dimethyl fumarate.[36] Hacohen et al. studied the treatment approach for MOGAD in children where they administered 30 mg/kg/day of intravenous steroids followed by slow oral tapering steroids. They reported that while children are more prone for relapses and recurrences, serology titers can help in guiding the duration of immunotherapy for good clinical outcomes.[3738] Hacohen et al.[38] and Averseng-Peaureaux et al. reported excellent outcomes in atypical MOG optic neuritis with intravenous immunoglobulin.[39] Newer monoclonal antibodies like Eculizumab, Satralizumab, and Tocilizumab have shown promise in NMOSD in adults, but their safety and efficacy in pediatric NMOSD need to be evaluated.[33]

Most respondents (54%) preferred neurologist opinion to decide on the type and duration of immunosuppressants, while the rest tailored immunosuppressants themselves. About 15% continued the immunosuppressants for 6 months, 13% for 1 year, and 9% individualized according to the patient profile.

Pediatric optic neuritis is ideally comanaged with a pediatric neurologist due to the disease heterogeneity and the need for personalized treatment. However, due to limited availability of pediatric neurologists, and to avoid delay in starting the treatment, it is prudent to educate neuro-ophthalmologists in this regard. Current literature suggests that one-third of children with pediatric optic neuritis patients have persistence of visual field defects, highlighting the importance of starting early immunosuppression.[39]

Relapsing and recurrent optic neuritis can be managed with long-term oral steroids, IVIG, Rituximab, Mycophenolate Mofetil, and Azathioprine.[38] In children with recurrent and refractory optic neuritis, plasmapheresis as the second-line acute therapy has been considered safe and efficacious.[4041]

For recurrent or poorly resolved pediatric optic neuritis, 63% of the respondents repeated neuroimaging, with 29% repeating in all recurrent pediatric optic neuritis regardless of clinical profile and 10% only if prior imaging did not include contrast. As per the existing literature, the distinct imaging patterns may be noticed on imaging on first or subsequent attacks.[4243] Early diagnosis of recurrent optic neuritis can be missed in a routine contrast-enhanced T1 protocol and can be detected in contrast-enhanced fat-suppressed orbital imaging T1 sequences and T2 fluid-attenuated inversion recovery imaging.[44] Most (59%) requested MRI brain and orbit with contrast, while 45% also included spine MRI. As per the existing literature, the recommended imaging protocol for recurrent optic neuritis includes brain and orbit MRI with contrast and fat-suppressed sequences, plus spine MRI.[1726]

As expected, most respondents obtained repeat serological testing for recurrent cases, especially if prior reports were negative and referred them to pediatric neurologists. However, some adjusted the immunosuppressive regimen and/or increased the dose of the prior immune-suppressive agent.

Studies by Benson LA et al.[29] and Gorman MP et al.[45] reported that the risk of recurrence is highest within the first two years after an initial demyelinating event of MS and increases further over the next 5 years.

Follow-up intervals among respondents varied with nearly half opting to follow up every 3–6 months for the first year, 25% within 2 months, 14% based on patient symptoms, and 12% referring directly to neurologists. This survey suggests that the most common practice was three monthly follow-up in the first year and subsequent visits based on clinical course using both visual fields and OCT to detect recurrences and contralateral eye involvement. In our survey, 41% advised visual fields and optical coherence tomography, while 20% only visual fields during follow-up. This could be due to the heterogenous group of people with difference in experience.

The limitations of the current study include the fact that although pediatric neurologists were contacted, only one neurologist responded. Therefore, this study reflects the responses primarily from ophthalmologists. Further, only 44% were trained in neuro-ophthalmology, which influenced the variability in the practice patterns. Additionally, this survey captured recollection of the respondent’s clinical practice rather than data obtained from the review of clinical records.

Conclusion

Despite these limitations, this survey highlights variability in the diagnosis, management, and follow-up practices among Indian ophthalmologists. There is a need to standardize evidence-based guidelines tailored to the Indian pediatric population, supported by multidisciplinary collaboration and prospective research to ensure correct workup and optimal management and prevent recurrences.
