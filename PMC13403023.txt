Inter‐Group Agreement in Chest Radiographic Quality Assessment: Comparing Final‐Year Students and Experienced Radiographers Across Three Quality Assessment Domains During the COVID‐19 Pandemic
Authors: Peter O'Reilly, Dania Abu Awwad, Ernest Ekpo, Warren Reed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionReproducible image quality assessment (QA) by radiographers is vital to minimise inter‐observer variation and reduce unnecessary repeat exposures. This study compared the consistency of chest X‐ray (CXR) image quality assessments between final‐year student radiographers and experienced radiographers across three distinct QA domains (positioning, sharpness and visibility) to observe skill consistency across different pathology categories.MethodsThis retrospective observational study analysed 70 de‐identified CXRs acquired between May and September 2021 from patients presenting with symptoms of pulmonary infection during the COVID‐19 pandemic and retrieved from the PACS archive of a tertiary teaching hospital in Sydney, Australia. Two experienced radiologists independently reviewed each case and discussed their interpretations to reach a consensus classification based on the available clinical information.ResultsNo significant differences were found between groups in any domain across the different pathology categories (p > 0.05). Bland–Altman analysis showed minimal mean variability, with students rating marginally lower and narrow 95% limits of agreement across all domains. Overall ICC values indicated good‐to‐excellent reproducibility (ICC = 0.90, 95% CI 0.85–0.94). These single‐institution findings should be interpreted within the dataset's context.ConclusionFinal‐year students and experienced radiographers applied QA criteria consistently, demonstrating robust skills across different pathology categories. The small negative bias observed may reflect a more cautious interpretive approach rather than meaningful disagreement. These findings indicate that final‐year radiography students demonstrate agreement with experienced radiographers in the application of structured image‐quality criteria under controlled assessment conditions.Keywords: chest X‐ray, COVID‐19, CXR, image quality assessment, inter‐rater reliability, radiation exposure, radiographer training, reproducibility

Introduction

Image quality is fundamental to both professional practice and patient safety [1]. Superior image clarity prevents unnecessary repeat exposures, directly minimising patient radiation dose in accordance with the ALARA principle [2]. Furthermore, image quality is a routine but critical part of everyday radiographic practice. It directly affects whether an image is accepted, repeated or used for diagnosis, making it central to both patient safety and clinical decision‐making [1, 2]. It encapsulates the essential responsibility of the radiographer to consistently produce diagnostic images that support accurate and timely interpretation.

Quality assurance (QA) in radiography can be conceptualised across three interrelated domains of positioning accuracy, exposure/sharpness and anatomical visibility, reflecting both the technical quality criteria established by the European Guidelines on Quality Criteria for Diagnostic Radiographic Images [3, 4] and the professional capabilities defined by the Medical Radiation Practice Board of Australia (MRPBA) [5].

More recently, artificial intelligence (AI) tools have started to influence chest radiography workflows, particularly in image post‐processing and automated quality metrics. Techniques such as noise reduction, bone suppression and image enhancement can improve visual clarity and, in some cases, diagnostic confidence. However, these tools do not replace good acquisition technique. Poor positioning or inadequate exposure at the time of imaging still cannot be fully corrected afterwards, which reinforces the importance of the radiographer's role at the point of capture [6, 7, 8].

Observer variability in radiographic image quality assessment emerged as a concern during the COVID‐19 pandemic when CXRs, including those acquired via mobile x‐ray units, became critical for rapid diagnosis [9, 10, 11]. Technical adequacy depends not only on equipment but also on the radiographer's judgement in patient positioning and exposure technique [4]. Although objective national standards are in place [5], contextual pressures such as patient acuity, infection‐control constraints and fatigue can introduce subjectivity. Consequently, radiographers may unconsciously rate image quality differently depending on the visibility or severity of pathology [12, 13].

While structured QA frameworks exist [3, 4], few studies have directly examined final‐year students' ability to undertake image quality assessment using these criteria. Benchmarking final‐year students against experienced practitioners provides insight into how well students apply structured QA criteria, offering one indicator of their developing professional capability. While students gain significant technical knowledge during their training, their ability to apply subjective quality criteria consistently alongside experienced staff is an important indicator of their preparation for autonomous clinical practice. Addressing this knowledge gap may provide data to establish how knowledge gained in an academic setting translates into clinical practice and the influence that clinical experience has on quality assessment standardisation. Therefore, this study aims to compare image quality assessments of final‐year radiography students and experienced radiographers (minimum 5 years post‐qualification) in their application of structured chest QA criteria. It also aims to explore whether visible pathology systematically influences technical image quality evaluation. Findings from this work have potential applicability beyond COVID‐19. Previous respiratory outbreaks, including SARS and MERS, also involved pulmonary changes detectable on chest radiographs [14], and chest radiography has played an important role in diagnosis, monitoring and follow‐up [7, 9, 11]. The likelihood of future respiratory epidemics [15] highlights the need for radiographers and new graduates to maintain strong image‐quality assessment skills in both routine and high‐pressure clinical contexts [16].

Materials and Methods

Image Selection

This retrospective observational study analysed 70 de‐identified CXRs acquired between May and September 2021 and retrieved from the PACS archive of a tertiary teaching hospital in Sydney, Australia. Thirteen experienced radiographers and 12 final‐year students rated the images using a 10‐item, six‐point Likert QA scale. Inter‐group differences, mean limits of agreement and reproducibility were evaluated using Mann–Whitney U tests, Bland–Altman plots and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC).

Images with gross technical errors were excluded because these were likely to be readily identified by both groups and could artificially inflate agreement. The analysis therefore focused on more subtle variations in image quality. This approach reflects true clinical practice, where the decision to accept or repeat an image often rests on subtle quality differences rather than obvious failures. The final dataset comprised 30 typical COVID‐19, 20 indeterminate, 10 alternative pathology and 10 normal cases. The sample included both departmental and mobile acquisitions.

Study Population Demographics

Twenty‐five participants were recruited: 13 experienced radiographers (mean experience = 9.6 ± 4.2 years, minimum 5 years post‐qualification) and 12 final‐year student radiographers enrolled in an MRPBA‐accredited four‐year bachelor programme. Students had completed all required clinical placements (approximately 1600–2200 h) in alignment with national education provider frameworks active during the 2020 cohort period [17, 18, 19]. Between November 2022 and June 2023, the 25 participants independently rated the same 70 CXRs, producing 1750 ratings: 910 from experienced radiographers and 840 from students.

Image Evaluation Procedure

Participants independently reviewed the 70 de‐identified CXRs using 6MP diagnostic monitors in the Medical Image Optimisation and Perception Group (MIOPeG) laboratory or equivalent clinical reporting environments. They rated each image using the 10‐item QA scale and classified the images using the Royal Australian and New Zealand College of Radiologists (RANZCR) COVID‐19 reporting template to provide realistic clinical context for the quality assessment task and establish if the presence of pathology confounds participants' image quality rating. This diagnostic classification was not the focus of the analysis.

Definitions and Criteria

The 10 QA items, adapted from the European Guidelines on Quality Criteria for Diagnostic Radiographic Images [3], were rated on a six‐point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree to 6 = Strongly Agree) (Table 1). The items were grouped into three domains: Positioning accuracy (Q1–Q4), Exposure/Sharpness (Q5–Q7) and Anatomical Visibility (Q8–Q10).

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were first calculated for each QA domain and diagnostic group. To compare student and radiographer ratings, Mann–Whitney U tests were performed for each domain (Normal, COVID‐19, Indeterminate and Alternative Pathology) as the data were ordinal and non‐normally distributed. Each test compared the distributions of the two groups for (a) composite domain scores, (b) individual QA items and (c) overall mean quality scores.

To further assess inter‐group agreement and potential differences, Bland–Altman analyses were conducted comparing mean QA scores per image between students and radiographers. These analyses illustrated mean potential differences and 95% limits of agreement.

The total spread of disagreement between students and experienced radiographers as shown in Table 5 was small across all areas. For positioning, the total disagreement span took up only 19% of the scale. For sharpness and visibility, it took up 26% and 25% of the scale. This shows that even when the two groups did not give the exact same score, their ratings stayed clustered close together within a small fraction of the total measurement scale [20, 21].

Finally, intra‐class correlation coefficients (ICC) were calculated using a two‐way random‐effects model with absolute agreement to quantify the reproducibility of image quality ratings between groups, both within each QA domain and across all domains combined. ICC values were interpreted according to standard thresholds: < 0.5 = poor, 0.5–0.75 = moderate, 0.75–0.9 = good, > 0.9 = excellent reliability. The overall ICC was 0.90, indicating good‐to‐excellent reliability.

All analyses were two‐tailed, with a significance level of p < 0.05. All data visualisations (box plots and Bland–Altman plots) were generated using SPSS statistics software (Version 30.0; IBM Corp) [22]. Graphical outputs were visually inspected for outliers and proportional potential difference.

Agreement Analysis (Bland–Altman and ICC)

Bland–Altman analyses revealed small and negative mean variability values, indicating that students tended to rate image quality marginally lower than radiographers (Table 4). The 95% limits of agreement (LoA) were narrow (typically within ±0.6 points on the 6‐point scale), demonstrating high concordance. ICC results indicated good‐to‐excellent inter‐group reliability across the QA domains.

Discussion

The central contribution of this study is the empirical evidence that final‐year students are already applying image quality criteria in a way that closely matches practising radiographers. While clinical experience is often viewed as the primary driver of diagnostic judgement, our results suggest that final‐year students can apply structured image‐quality criteria consistently following their undergraduate education and clinical training. This high level of inter‐group agreement across all three QA domains and various pathology types suggests that final‐year students can apply structured quality criteria in a manner broadly aligned with experienced radiographers under controlled assessment conditions. In clinical practice, consistent clinical judgement is critical when deciding whether an image is of sufficient quality or will require repeating. This has direct implications for patient dose and workflow efficiency.

The slight negative bias observed in student scores where they rated images marginally lower than their experienced counterparts further highlights the study's significance regarding workforce readiness. This tendency may reflect a more cautious approach among final‐year students. However, the reasons for the slightly lower ratings were not directly investigated.

For the healthcare system, this consistency suggests that final‐year students possess the technical and evaluative foundations needed to contribute to quality‐focused imaging practice. Although this study did not assess real‐time accept or repeat decisions, the observed agreement indicates that students can apply structured QA criteria in a way that is broadly comparable to experienced radiographers in a controlled setting.

Moreover, the lack of significant differences between groups across different pathologies, including COVID‐19 and alternative conditions, suggests that the presence of pathology does not confound image quality assessment of experienced and student radiographers. It also highlights the stability of quality ratings across different pathology categories under controlled assessment conditions. This study supports the value of standardised QA frameworks in promoting consistent image‐quality assessment within the profession. Further, the study provides preliminary evidence that students demonstrated a level of agreement with experienced radiographers that reflects strong application of structured QA criteria, though this should not be interpreted as equivalence in broader clinical evaluative accuracy, which was beyond the scope of this study.

Limitations

While the high ICC values suggest good‐to‐excellent reproducibility, these limitations mean findings might not perfectly reflect performance in every clinical setting. In a fast‐paced department, factors such as fatigue, high patient volume and acute operational environments could widen the gap between a student's cautious approach and an experienced radiographer's seasoned efficiency. Beyond these operational pressures, the generalisability of these results is restricted by the small, single‐institution sample size and a pathology‐weighted dataset predominantly featuring pulmonary infections. Consequently, evaluating participants under simulated clinical conditions may overlook how the vastly asymmetrical experience levels between veteran radiographers and the student cohort manifest in daily clinical practice.

Conclusion

In summary, final‐year radiography students assessed chest X‐ray image quality in a manner that demonstrated good to excellent agreement with experienced radiographers across positioning, sharpness and visibility domains, regardless of underlying pathology. The minor tendency for students to assign slightly lower scores appears to reflect caution rather than meaningful disagreement. These findings provide evidence that, under controlled structured assessment conditions, final‐year students demonstrate a level of preparedness to apply professional QA criteria comparably to experienced practitioners. Such agreement supports the value of standardised image‐quality frameworks in radiography education and clinical practice, as they promote consistency in how image‐quality criteria are interpreted. While this study did not evaluate clinical repeat‐rate outcomes, consistent application of QA criteria is an important component of radiation‐safety practice.
