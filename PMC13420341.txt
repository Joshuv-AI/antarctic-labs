Arthroscopic Technique for Posterior Labral Repair in the Beach‐Chair Position
Authors: Saad M AlQahtani, Victor A Pacheco, Adeeba A AlBadran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPosterior labral repair is commonly performed in the lateral decubitus position; however, comparable outcomes have been reported using the beach‐chair position. Limited visualization of the posterior‐inferior glenoid remains a perceived disadvantage of the beach‐chair setup. This Technical Note describes a reproducible arthroscopic technique for posterior labral repair performed in the beach‐chair position using strategic portal placement, dynamic portal switching, and controlled anterior translation of the humeral head to optimize visualization. A posterolateral viewing portal enhances exposure of the posterior‐inferior labrum, while anterior and posterior portals are alternated as working portals to facilitate suture passage and anchor insertion. Fixation is performed from inferior to superior to restore anatomic labral contour and capsulo‐labral tension. This technique preserves the ergonomic and practical advantages of the beach‐chair position while maintaining established principles of arthroscopic posterior stabilization.

Posterior shoulder instability represents only 10% of shoulder instability cases 1 yet remains a clinically significant cause of pain and dysfunction, particularly in contact and overhead athletes. 2 Subtle clinical presentation often delays diagnosis, with risk factors including repetitive microtrauma, bony abnormalities, and capsular laxity. Rehabilitation aimed at strengthening dynamic stabilizers is the first‐line treatment, but persistent symptoms frequently necessitate surgery. 1 Arthroscopic repair, now favored over open approaches, has traditionally been performed in the lateral decubitus position using posterior portals, although this setup presents traction‐related risks and difficulties with arm manipulation.

Beach‐chair positioning, initially described as an alternative to lateral decubitus, is now well established for a variety of arthroscopic procedures. It offers simplified setup, improved arm handling, and safe access to most pathology. Although once questioned for inferior glenoid visualization, recent reports confirm that posterior labral repair can be performed effectively with proper portal placement and technique. 3 The purpose of this Technical Note is to describe a reproducible, stepwise approach for arthroscopic posterior labral repair in the beach‐chair position, providing good visualization of the posterior labrum and capsule with reliable access for posterior glenohumeral stabilization.

Step 1: Instrumentation and Implant Verification

Although seemingly obvious, a formal preincision verification of instruments and implants is essential for reproducible results. The availability, sterility, and proper function of all equipment and implants should be confirmed before skin incision. Particular attention is given to instruments required for arthroscopic shoulder stabilization, including cannulas (appropriate sizes and quantities), suture anchors (sizes and quantities) with their corresponding drills and sleeves, a power drill with a fully charged battery, mallet, suture passer, and complete shoulder arthroscopy instrument set. Implant packaging integrity and expiration dates must also be verified.

Step 2: Anesthesia and Beach‐Chair Positioning

General anesthesia combined with an interscalene block is routinely used. The patient is positioned in the beach‐chair position on a surgical table that hinges at the hip section to elevate the backrest. Proper and stable alignment of the head, neck, and trunk is ensured while maintaining the scapula free of mechanical obstruction (Figure 1, Video 1). A padded, waterproof wedge is placed to prevent patient slippage. Head and lateral supports, together with a padded face mask, are used to maintain centered, stable alignment of the head and trunk on the surgical table (Figure 1).

After careful positioning of the patient in beach chair, the procedure is initiated through the posterior portal. A systematic diagnostic Arthroscopy is performed allowing identification of the tears within the glenohumeral joint. Using spinal needle, the anterior and posterolateral portals are established under direct visualization to optimize access to the joint. After cannula placement in all three portals, the arthroscope is switched to the posterolateral portal. Anterior translation of the humeral head increases the working space and improves visualization of the inferior posterior labrum. The next step is labral preparation, beginning with debridement followed by elevation and final tissue revitalization. Adequate labral preparation is critical to optimize repair quality and enhance healing. Therefore, sufficient time should be dedicated to this step. The next step is the labral repair, which begins with suture passage through the labral tissue using a curved suture passer to perform a knotless technique. In this technique, all steps are performed with a posterolateral view. The posterior and anterior portals serve as the working portals, a key pearl that facilitates posterior labral repair, as shown in the video. As shown in the video and illustration, the suture is passed as a loop, allowing the free ends to be passed through it to improve tissue fixation. All necessary sutures, usually two or three, should be passed through the tissue before final fixation is a key pearl of this technique in the beach‐chair position. Each suture will be loaded in a single anchor, so the number of sutures corresponds to the number of anchors to be used. Another pearl is to use alternating suture colors, which allows easy identification of the most distal and most proximal sutures, ensuring proper suture management and organization. From the posterior portal view, we can confirm proper passage of all sutures through the labrum. The arthroscope is then switched to the anterior portal for optimal visualization of anchor placement, while the posterolateral portal is used to insert the anchors. The suture is loaded into the knotless anchor, and the hole is drilled using the appropriate instrument. Another pearl is to insert a blunt K wire before removing the drill sleeve to prevent losing the hole, as shown in the video. This also helps avoid redrilling and confirms by tactile feedback that the bone is adequate for anchor placement, as shown in the video. The desired tension is applied, and the anchor is finally inserted. Finally, the suture is cut using an arthroscopic cutter. Now, the final posterior labral repair and the position of posterior and posterolateral cannulas can be visualized. The repair can be visualized through both the anterior and posterolateral portals. The operative arm is supported with a padded arm holder to allow controlled positioning and movement during arthroscopy; alternatively, the arm may be left free, which requires a second assistant. Sequential compression devices are applied to both lower extremities for deep venous thrombosis prophylaxis, and a forced‐air warming system is used throughout the procedure to prevent hypothermia (Figure 1).

Step 3: Diagnostic Arthroscopy and Portal Establishment (Figure 2)

A systematic diagnostic arthroscopy is performed to evaluate the glenohumeral joint and confirm the presence of a posterior labral tear (Figure 3). The anterior portal is established through the rotator interval, and a 7‐mm cannula (Twist‐In Cannula, Arthrex, Naples, FL) is inserted. Under direct visualization from the posterior portal, a posterolateral portal is created while the humeral head is gently translated anteriorly (Figure 4), followed by insertion of a 7‐mm cannula (Twist‐In Cannula, Arthrex, Naples, FL). Finally, with visualization from the posterolateral portal, an 8‐mm cannula (Twist‐In Cannula, Arthrex, Naples, FL) is inserted into the posterior portal (Video 1). All 3 portals are confirmed to provide safe trajectories, adequate visualization, and sufficient instrument maneuverability throughout the procedure.

Step 4: Labral Preparation: Debridement, Elevation and Revitalization (Video 1)

With posterolateral visualization and the posterior portal used as the working portal, this step is performed in the following sequence:

Debridement: The labral tear is gently debrided using a 4.0‐mm arthroscopic shaver (Arthrex, Naples, FL) with suction turned off to regularize the torn edges while preserving the native labral tissue.

Elevation: Labral elevation begins with mobilization of the posterior labrum from the glenoid using a combination of elevators and dissectors, as needed. Careful release restores labral mobility and facilitates anatomic repositioning.

Revitalization: It is performed by lightly debriding the glenoid rim to promote a bleeding bony bed. Adequate revitalization promotes biologic healing and enhances secure labral fixation. Before proceeding with suture passage and anchor placement, confirm that the labrum is fully mobilized and the glenoid surface is adequately prepared (Table 2).

Step 5: Suture Passage

Continue with the arthroscope in the posterolateral portal. The anterior and posterior portals are used as working portals. The suture passer is introduced through the posterior portal, while the anterior portal is used for suture management (Video 1). Size No. 2 Sutures (FiberWire, Arthrex, Naples, FL) are passed in a loop configuration to achieve secure tissue capture and minimize the risk of labral cut‐through (Figures 5 and 6). Alternating suture colors are recommended to facilitate organization and identification (Video 1). All sutures should be passed sequentially and completed before anchor insertion.

High‐strength braided polyethylene suture (blue) is loaded into the guide suture (black) in a loop configuration. Arthroscopy is performed using a 30° arthroscope.

Step 6: Anchor Insertion

For knotless repair of the posterior labral tear, the arthroscope is switched to the anterior portal to optimize visualization of the posterior labrum, and the posterolateral portal is used as the working portal (Figure 7). Each suture tape is retrieved individually and loaded into a separate 2.9‐mm knotless anchor (PushLock; Arthrex, Naples, FL). A pilot hole is drilled, followed by insertion of a blunt guidewire to maintain the trajectory after sleeve removal. The anchor is partially inserted, the suture is tensioned to achieve anatomic labral reduction, and the anchor is then fully seated. Excess suture is trimmed arthroscopically (Video 1). This sequence is repeated for each anchor, beginning distally and progressing proximally, to secure the posterior labral repair from inferior to superior.

Postoperative Rehabilitation

The shoulder is immobilized for 4 weeks. Active motion of the elbow, wrist, and hand is allowed immediately without restriction. Early periscapular muscle activation is initiated during the second postoperative week to promote scapulothoracic control while minimizing stress on the repair. Progressive passive shoulder range of motion is introduced after 4 weeks under supervised physical therapy. 4 Posterior capsular stretching and posteriorly directed loading are strictly avoided for the first 3 months to allow adequate healing of the repaired labrum.

DISCUSSION

This stepwise technique shows that arthroscopic posterior labral repair can be performed safely and effectively in the beach‐chair position. 1 , 3 Although lateral decubitus has traditionally been favored due to perceived advantages in inferior glenoid visualization and use of a two‐portal technique, 5 , 6 clinical outcomes are not significantly different between positions. Thus, patient positioning should be guided by surgeon experience and the ability to perform a reproducible technique.

A limitation of the beach‐chair position is suboptimal visualization of the inferior posterior labrum when using a fixed viewing portal. This Technical Note shows that this challenge can be reliably overcome through deliberate portal placement and systematic switching of viewing and working portals (Tables 2 and 3). Specifically, a posterolateral viewing portal combined with controlled anterior translation of the humeral head consistently improves exposure of the posterior‐inferior glenoid while allowing both anterior and posterior portals to function effectively as working portals.

Advantages and Disadvantages of the Procedure

In addition to technical feasibility, the beach‐chair position offers practical advantages, including simplified operating room set up, unrestricted arm manipulation, and ease of conversion to open procedures or combined techniques if necessary. The use of combined general anesthesia and interscalene block ensures reliable analgesia and hemodynamic stability, supporting safe and efficient execution of the procedure. 7

In summary, this Technical Note describes a controlled, stepwise, and reproducible approach to arthroscopic posterior labral repair in the beach‐chair position. By emphasizing visualization strategies, portal versatility, and procedural sequencing, this technique maintains the principles of anatomic restoration and reliable fixation while expanding the practical applicability of the beach‐chair position for posterior shoulder instability.
