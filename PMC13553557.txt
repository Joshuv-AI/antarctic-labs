Assessment of local provider engagement of an in-home collaborative model for remote genetic services
Authors: Daniel Chavez-Yenter, Kevin Oeffinger, Brian Egleston, Elisabeth McCarty Wood, Sarah Howe, Sarah Brown, Janice Christiansen, Cara Cacioppo, Michelle Weinberg, Elena Elkin, Linda Fleisher, Rajia Mim, Santina Hernandez, Demetrios Ofidis, Dominique Fetzer, Tara O. Henderson, Angela Bradbury
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeClinical genetic testing demand has increased in the era of precision medicine. However, the availability of cancer genetic services remains limited in the United States, prompting a rise in telehealth delivery. Through our Telegenetics program, we seek to promote genetic service delivery across the United States, and here, we assess engagement through registrations with local providers using a service delivery model.MethodsThis report describes the Penn Telegenetics Program experience across 4 research and clinical activities using an in-home collaborative local provider model. We report the clinical procedures and our initial experience implementing this model across these 4 research and clinical activities in the Penn Telegenetics Program. We present descriptive findings of the registrations and, in cases of refusals, the rationale provided.ResultsFrom 2018 to 2025, 473 providers (89.4%) successfully registered. Providers were predominantly MD/DO licensed (85.0%). Family medicine was the most frequent specialty (54.3%), followed by internal medicine (20.9%) and clinical oncology (11.8%). Most providers were in suburban locations by zip codes (56.2%); 20.1% were in rural zip codes. Only 56 providers declined collaboration (10.6%); the most common reasons reported were preferring local genetic services (22.5%) and not being comfortable as the ordering provider (22.5%).ConclusionOur data demonstrate that most local providers are willing to collaborate with a centralized telegenetics program. Still, subsequent engagement from local providers in follow-up questions or other referrals remains a missed opportunity to improve telegenetics access. Refining procedures to increase collaborative care and engagement may provide opportunities to increase access to cancer genetic testing.

Introduction

Given the exponential growth of genetic testing services and the limited availability of genetic counselors (GCs) in many areas, there has been an increased interest in providing clinical genetic services via telehealth.1, 2, 3 Following the COVID-19 pandemic, telehealth services have been more accepted and used by the public.4,5 While many genetic programs offer remote services within their state, broader provision of services is limited by state licensure requirements and limited reimbursement for genetic counseling and telehealth services. Additionally, successful implementation of genetic testing includes changes to screening and risk reduction based on test results, which often requires collaboration with local primary care providers (PCPs).6

The Penn Telegenetics Program was founded in 2012 to expand genetic services to community practices without access to GCs.7 Initially, remote services were provided on-site in collaboration with specific practices and providers.8 As the program expanded, we sought to provide services nationally to patients who meet clinical guidelines for genetic testing and have limited access to genetic services. Further, we sought to allow patients to self-refer to the program. To achieve this expanded access, we developed the in-home collaborative local provider (LP) model.9 This delivery model addresses licensure requirements (the GC and physician must be licensed in the state in which the patient resides), provides care in the home, and allows patients across the United States—regardless of location or proximity to a participating clinic—to access remote telegenetic services with their PCP/LP.9 These activities are considered clinical and not research activities, as they are facilitating clinical genetic testing in collaboration with a certified GC. Other commercial programs develop a network of providers contracted with the company or program to provide a clinical provider in the state, but these providers often have no established relationship with the patient. We developed our approach to both meet licensure requirements for genetic testing and to provide a direct clinical handoff to the LP who has an established patient-provider relationship. While the primary goal of this model was to facilitate clinical testing for individual patients, we hypothesized that this process might provide opportunities for LPs to engage with our team to answer clinical questions and to consider genetic testing for other patients in their practice.

This collaborative LP delivery model for remote genetic services has been used across multiple research studies and clinical activities that provide remote cancer genetic services across the United States through the Penn Telegenetics Program.9, 10, 11 In this brief report, we describe the clinical procedures and our initial experience implementing the collaborative LP model across 4 research and clinical activities in the Penn Telegenetics Program.

Methods

Our procedures for provider collaboration and engagement have been informed by the ENGAGE9 Study PCP Advisory Board but are applied across all Penn Telegenetics clinical and research activities where patients self-refer. The ENGaging and Activating Survivors in Genetic Services (ENGAGE) study evaluates whether offering remote centralized telegenetics services can increase the uptake of testing compared with local usual care options among childhood cancer survivors.9 The Telegenetics Patient Access Program was a pre-COVID clinical program that offered remote genetic services to patients meeting clinical criteria for genetic testing, with genetic counseling provided at no cost. eReach1 and eReach2 are 4-arm randomized noninferiority studies testing the effectiveness of replacing traditional counseling visits with a digital alternative (eg, self-directed web-based patient intervention) in patients who meet clinical criteria for cancer genetic testing based on advanced or metastatic cancer (eReach1) or personal and family history (eReach2).10,11 See Supplemental Table 1 for more detail about each individual study. In all these programs, the genetic testing is real-world clinical genetic testing billed to the patient’s insurance, consistent with clinical care. Similarly, the counseling provided by the GCs is considered clinical care.

Patients enrolling in each of the 4 research studies and clinical activities are requested to provide the name and contact information for their local LP. The research team contacts the office to confirm the mutual patient and then faxes a registration packet, which includes an informational flyer and the provider registration form (which can be completed by office staff). The 1-page flyer describes the Telegenetics Program, what we are asking of LPs, and how they can contact the team for any questions. The registration form requests the provider’s name, provider type (MD, DO, NP, PA, etc.), provider specialty, practice contact information and address, gender, and practice type. Registration is considered part of clinical care and not direct engagement in research, as these procedures could be used if we were providing clinical billable services.

If the registration form is not returned, the research team contacts the provider’s office to confirm receipt and remind the office that testing cannot proceed without the provider registration. If providers decline, the Penn Telegenetics physician attempts to contact the LP directly to discuss the value of testing and address any concerns. Reasons for declining registration, as reported by the office staff or provider, are recorded. If a provider declines or cannot be reached, patients are contacted to see if they would like to provide a second LP.

Patients complete pretest counseling according to individual study procedures (eg, some patients are randomized to a self-directed digital alternative instead of meeting with a GC). Consistent with clinical care, the GC coordinates with the patient to select the appropriate testing and to order the clinical testing billed to insurance, including the local provider on the test requisition with the GC. Genetic test results are shared with the patient by the Penn GC by phone, Penn-approved videoconference (the software for which varied over the years), or digital research interventions according to study protocols. In the eReach studies, all patients have the option to speak directly with a GC, even if randomized to a digital visit. The Penn Telegenetics Program GCs (5-6 on average, with a total of 13 across the time period) all adhere to the licensure laws of all 50 states. This typically includes having at least 2 GCs licensed in each required state, although this has varied over time depending on program needs and evolving licensure requirements and costs. The GC’s clinical pretest counseling visit note, disclosure visit note, and test results are faxed to the LP.

Registration attempts and successful registrations are recorded in the Telegenetics Program clinical database because they are considered clinical activities and not research activities. Categorical clinical data include provider type, specialty, practice location, gender (self-reported from the registration form), year registered, and practice type. Provider race and ethnicity are not collected because they are not needed for clinical purposes. Reasons for declining registration and testing are recorded when available for quality improvement.

For the purposes of this report, categorical data are presented descriptively. χ2 tests were used to test across categorical data, and generalized estimating equation models were used for repeated measures by study. Qualitative reasons for refusal were open-coded by 2 investigators (AUTHOR 1, AUTHOR 17) for analysis. The University of Pennsylvania institutional review board determined that these data and analysis do not meet the definition of research and qualify as a quality or performance improvement initiative.

Results

Across the research and clinical activities employing the collaborative LP model (2018-2025), 529 providers were contacted, with 473 providers (89.4%) successfully registered (Table 1). Providers were predominantly MD/DO licensed (85.0%). The most LPs were registered between 2022 and 2024 (381 collective registrations, 80.5%), following timelines and recruitment of various clinical activities. Most LPs came from group practices (51.0%) or multispecialty practices (22.8%). Family medicine was the most frequent specialty (54.3%), followed by internal medicine (20.9%) and oncology (11.8%). Most providers were in suburban locations by zip codes (56.2%), and 20.1% were in rural areas. Only 4 states–Nebraska, Oregon, South Dakota, and Hawaii–did not have any registered providers. Significant differences across studies in provider characteristics were noted in practice setting location (P = .005), provider specialty (P < .001), type of practice (P < . 001), and region setting (P < .001). Provider specialties varied by study, reflecting the populations recruited. Although most LPs were willing to register and collaborate with the program to facilitate genetic testing for their patients, 10.6% of providers declined registration, resulting in their patients not receiving genetic testing through our program (Table 2). The most frequent reasons for declining registration were the provider’s preference for local genetic testing services (22.5%) and not being comfortable being on the genetic testing requisition form (22.5%). Some providers reported that they needed to see or speak with the patient before testing (14.1%). In some cases, patients reported that they had spoken with their LP and decided not to pursue genetic testing (14.1%). Discussion

These data demonstrate that the large majority of LPs contacted by our program were willing to register and provide access to genetic testing for their patients. The success might be due to some LPs’ interest in testing for their patients but low comfort or training in genetics.12, 13, 14 Willingness may also have been related to the low burden for local practices.13 Future work is planned to understand LPs’ motivations, perceptions, and experiences engaging with the Penn Telegenetics Program and genetics services more broadly.

We encountered a subset of LPs that declined to collaborate with the Telegenetics Program. Some providers refused because of a preference for using local genetic testing services, which is understandable. In this setting, there may still have been a benefit in making the patient aware of their risk and motivating a discussion with their provider, although we do not know if these patients ultimately completed testing. Some reported not being comfortable being on the test requisition. It may be useful to develop materials for the patient to share with the LPs when discussing the testing process. This may prompt mutual informed decision-making between patient and provider, greater willingness for providers to collaborate, and subsequent testing.

We have not received follow-up contact from LPs, nor have registered providers referred other patients, which represent potential missed opportunities. We had hoped that providers would see the service as being of low burden for them and of benefit for their individual patients. It could be that providers felt comfortable modifying medical care based on the genetic test results, or it may not be impacting care at all. Unfortunately, we do not know at this time how providers used the results or the GC notes that we sent.15 Several studies are collecting longitudinal behaviors, which could provide insights when these data become available. Limited referrals and engagement could be due to time constraints in busy local practices,16,17 limited interest in genetic testing,18 lack of training in genetic medicine,19 identification of ideal candidates for genetic testing,13,14,20 or comfort with local services as a previously noted reason for declining registration.

While the program has been successful, we believe there are areas for improvement. Future qualitative research will include interviews with providers to understand their perceptions of the program and their perceived barriers to and facilitators of testing uptake. It will also be important to understand their perceived utility of genetic testing and how it impacts their local workflow. Interviews may also provide insights into how we can improve our procedure to better address provider questions and reservations. Connecting with other practice members (eg, nurses or office staff) or providing educational content for providers could be beneficial to increase engagement with LPs to optimize care for patients after testing and facilitate subsequent referrals for other patients in the providers’ practices. Though much of the data presented here are in the context of research studies and may not be entirely appropriate to extrapolate to wider primary practices, we believe that presenting these results in the context of the studies and clinical activities shows its potential for wider distribution of germline genetic testing services using a telehealth model.

These data demonstrate that most LPs contacted by our Telegenetics Program are willing to collaborate with a centralized cancer genetics program to facilitate remote clinical genetic services for their patients. Low posttest engagement with providers could be a missed opportunity for expanding access to genetic services and represents an area for improvement.
