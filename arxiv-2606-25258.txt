Introduction

An incompressible viscoelastic equation does not use the full elastic stress as
an absolute tensor. It uses the stress only modulo pressure: adding an
isotropic tensor
r(C)I
changes the scalar pressure but not the projected
velocity dynamics. The basic object is therefore the pressure-free active
part of the stress, namely the component that survives in vorticity and can
exchange derivatives with the non-diffusive conformation tensor. This quotient
viewpoint is especially rigid in two space dimensions.

Indeed, let
C\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{2}
be a positive conformation tensor and let

T(C)
be a smooth spectral isotropic elastic stress. By the
Cayley–Hamilton reduction,

T(C)=q_{0}(\operatorname{tr}C,\det C)I+q_{1}(\operatorname{tr}C,\det C)C.

Writing
C=aI+Y
, with
a=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}C
and
Y=C^{\circ}
, gives

T(C)=(q_{0}+aq_{1})I+q_{1}Y.

Since
\det C=a^{2}-\frac{1}{2}|Y|^{2}
, the scalar
q_{1}
may be regarded as a
function of
(a,|Y|^{2})
. Thus, after pressure projection, every
two-dimensional spectral isotropic stress has the same active direction:

[T(C)]_{\rm active}=q_{1}(a,|Y|^{2})Y.

The pressure-free anisotropic channel is not a feature of Oldroyd–B or FENE-P;
it is the natural quotient-level form of a two-dimensional isotropic tensorial
stress. The scalar factor carries the constitutive law, while the deviatoric
tensor
Y
carries the only stress direction visible to the incompressible
velocity.

This observation changes the role of model classes in the continuation
problem. The central regularity difficulty in stress-diffusion-free
viscoelastic flow is a derivative imbalance between the viscous velocity and a
non-diffusive conformation tensor. The elastic stress enters the momentum
equation through a divergence, so one derivative of a transported quantity is
fed back into the parabolic velocity equation. A useful criterion should
therefore be formulated at the level of the active pressure class of the
stress, not at the level of a chosen spring law. In two dimensions this active
pressure class is exactly a scalar spectral coefficient times
Y
.

The first result of the paper is the corresponding active-deviatoric
projection principle. For upper-convected stretching, the trace-free equation
has the universal principal term
2aS(u)
. Testing this equation with the
weight
q_{1}/a
cancels the top-order velocity–stress coupling generated by

\operatorname{div}(q_{1}Y)
. Thus the leading continuation mechanism is dictated by the
pressure quotient and by two-dimensional isotropic spectral algebra, rather
than by a special cancellation in a particular constitutive model.

This projection is intrinsically two-dimensional. In three dimensions, the
Cayley–Hamilton representation of a spectral stress generally contains a
quadratic term,

T(C)=q_{0}I+q_{1}C+q_{2}C^{2}.

After pressure projection this leaves, in addition to the
Y
-channel,
the trace-free part of
Y^{2}
. The active stress is therefore no longer a
single scalar multiple of the deviatoric conformation, and the cancellation
below does not extend without further constitutive structure. We regard the
three-dimensional analogue as a separate problem.

The second result is an endpoint estimate for the natural abstraction suggested
by this quotient structure. We call it a spectral-admissible active channel.
For

C=aI+Y,\qquad\eta=|Y|^{2},

the compact-window normal form is

\partial_{t}u+u\cdot\nabla u-\nu\Delta u+\nabla p=\alpha\operatorname{div}(\tau(a,\eta)Y),

D_{t}a+\lambda^{-1}G(a,\eta)=S(u):Y,\qquad D_{t}Y+\lambda^{-1}\mu(a,\eta)Y=2aS(u)+[\nabla u\,Y+Y(\nabla u)^{T}]^{\circ}.

Here
\tau(a,\eta)Y
is the most general active stress allowed by
two-dimensional spectral isotropy after pressure projection. The relaxation
terms
G(a,\eta)
and
\mu(a,\eta)Y
are the corresponding scalar and
deviatoric spectral relaxation channels. The assumptions used below are not a
new polymer ansatz; they isolate the structural properties needed for local
continuation on a compact conformation window: positivity of the active and
deviatoric relaxation coefficients, and monotonicity of the scalar relaxation.
Under these hypotheses the principal cancellation uses the weight

\tau(a,\eta)/a
, and the high-order energy closes with coefficient

1+\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}+\left\lVert\operatorname{Log}C\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}^{2}.

This places the result beyond a scalar-Peterlin generalization and beyond a
pair of separate improvements for Oldroyd–B and FENE-P. Within the
two-dimensional incompressible setting, spectral-admissible active channels are
the canonical pressure-free abstraction of isotropic tensorial stresses coupled
to an upper-convected positive tensor. The result is therefore a unified
continuation theory for two-dimensional spectral isotropic tensor-transport
equations; Oldroyd–B and FENE-P are model cases in which the geometric clocks
can be propagated explicitly. The framework can also be read as a statement
about transported tensor metrics, orientation tensors, or second moment
closures whenever the velocity only sees a spectral isotropic stress through
its divergence.

This pressure-quotient viewpoint is not tied to polymeric terminology. Similar
stress–transport couplings arise in active-matter descriptions, liquid-crystal
and orientational-flow models, and more general systems in which a parabolic or
incompressible conservation law is coupled to a transported tensorial order
parameter. In such settings, the isotropic part of an elastic or active stress
again acts as a pressure mode, while the deviatoric part is the component that
can create vorticity and exchange derivatives with the transported field. Our
results do not assert that all these systems satisfy the spectral-admissible
hypotheses; chiral terms, three-dimensional quadratic channels, gradient
elasticity, or nonspectral stresses may introduce additional active modes.
Rather, the pressure quotient provides a diagnostic principle: before estimating
a coupled tensor system, one should identify which part of the stress is
dynamically active after pressure projection and which part is invisible to the
incompressible velocity. The scalar-Peterlin class

T_{\psi}(C)=\psi(\operatorname{tr}C)C-I,\qquad D_{t}C=\nabla u\,C+C(\nabla u)^{T}+\lambda^{-1}\chi(\operatorname{tr}C)I-\lambda^{-1}\phi(\operatorname{tr}C)C

is the trace-only specialization

\tau=\psi(2a),\qquad\mu=\phi(2a),\qquad G=a\phi(2a)-\chi(2a).

Oldroyd–B and FENE-P are the two main concrete specializations of the general
endpoint theorem.

The word “admissible” is used here in a mathematical continuation sense. The
coefficients are smooth on the compact conformation window under consideration,
the active coefficient is positive, the deviatoric relaxation is coercive, and
the scalar relaxation is monotone in the trace variable. These assumptions are
sufficient for the endpoint estimate. They should not be read as saying that
arbitrary choices of
\tau,\mu,G
, or of
\psi,\phi,\chi
in the
scalar-Peterlin subcase, automatically define a thermodynamically dissipative
polymer model; a free-energy law requires an additional compatibility condition
between the stress coefficient and the relaxation.

The main payoff is a pure velocity-clock criterion. We prove that, for
Oldroyd–B, the velocity gradient alone determines whether a smooth solution
can be continued:

\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert\nabla u(t)\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}\,dt<\infty.

The compact positive-cone spectral window is not assumed. It is propagated by
this clock through a Lagrangian comparison argument. On that window, the
low-order pressure-free energy generates the logarithmic regularity

\operatorname{Log}A\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1+\varepsilon})
, and the high-order cancellation then
restarts the solution. In this specialization the scalar-Peterlin weight is

a^{-1}
, and the general active-deviatoric cancellation becomes the
cancellation between
\alpha\operatorname{div}Y
and
2aS(u)
.

The criticality of this clock is essential. The norm

\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}
is the zero-order endpoint Besov control
that gives the Lipschitz modulus needed to propagate the Lagrangian
conformation geometry. Within the scale
B^{0}_{\infty,q}
, the summability
index
q=1
is the endpoint at which the dyadic
L^{\infty}
information
still sums to a flow-map modulus; weaker zero-order summability no longer gives
this control. Thus the criteria below are endpoint velocity-clock criteria at
the natural critical scale, not high Sobolev continuation criteria rewritten in
Besov notation.

The FENE-P model adds one geometric boundary but not a new top-order coupling.
Its conformation tensor lies in

\mathcal{D}_{b}=\{C\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{2}:\operatorname{tr}C<b\},\qquad b>2,

so the Oldroyd–B positive-cone geometry is supplemented by the finite-
extensibility boundary
\operatorname{tr}C=b
. The scalar barrier

\phi_{b}(C)=-\log(b-\operatorname{tr}C)
is propagated pointwise by a squared endpoint
velocity clock. Thus FENE-P has the same generated logarithmic regularity
mechanism, but its geometric clock is
L^{2}_{t}B^{0}_{\infty,1}
. After the
pressure-free splitting

C=aI+Y
,
T_{b}(C)=(f_{b}a-1)I+f_{b}Y
, the same active-deviatoric cancellation closes
the FENE-P high-order estimate. The logarithmic

L^{2}_{t}H^{1+\varepsilon}_{x}
conformation bound needed in that estimate is again
generated on the compact FENE window.

The incompressible Oldroyd–B system without artificial stress diffusion is

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+u\cdot\nabla u-\nu\Delta u+\nabla p

\displaystyle=\alpha\operatorname{div}(A-I),

(1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}A+u\cdot\nabla A

\displaystyle=\nabla u\,A+A(\nabla u)^{T}-\lambda^{-1}(A-I),

(2)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}u

\displaystyle=0.

(3)

Here
u:\mathbb{T}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
A:\mathbb{T}^{2}\to\mathbb{S}_{++}^{2}
, and

\nu,\alpha,\lambda>0
. The conformation tensor has no spatial diffusion.
The velocity is parabolic, but the force in the vorticity equation contains

\operatorname{curl}\operatorname{div}A
, so derivatives of the stress enter the velocity equation at
the same level at which parabolic smoothing is being used.

The corresponding stress-diffusion-free FENE-P system is

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+u\cdot\nabla u-\nu\Delta u+\nabla p

\displaystyle=\alpha\operatorname{div}T_{b}(C),

(4)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}C+u\cdot\nabla C

\displaystyle=\nabla u\,C+C(\nabla u)^{T}-\lambda^{-1}\bigl(f_{b}(C)C-I\bigr),

(5)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}u

\displaystyle=0,

(6)

where

f_{b}(C)=\frac{b-2}{b-\operatorname{tr}C},\qquad T_{b}(C)=f_{b}(C)C-I.

The Oldroyd–B system is recovered formally on bounded trace windows as the
Hookean limit
b\to\infty
, where
f_{b}(C)\to 1
and

T_{b}(C)\to C-I
. At fixed
b
, however, derivatives of the spring
factor contain powers of
(b-\operatorname{tr}C)^{-1}
, and these coefficients cannot be
read from the Oldroyd–B logarithmic variable alone.

The continuation result is stated in terms of these geometric variables, but
for Oldroyd–B the spectral window is not an additional hypothesis. The
endpoint velocity clock

\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert\nabla u(t)\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}\,dt<\infty

propagates both upper and lower eigenvalue bounds for
A
by a Lagrangian
comparison argument. Once this automatic cone control is separated out,
the low-order pressure-free estimate generates

\operatorname{Log}A\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1+\varepsilon})
, and the high-order estimate then
restarts the solution. The improvement from the purely logarithmic

L^{4}_{t}
closure to an internally generated
L^{2}_{t}
logarithmic channel is
obtained by the pressure-free physical decomposition
A=aI+Y
.
For FENE-P the same positive-cone geometry remains, and the finite-
extensibility boundary is controlled by a scalar barrier. The restoring force
near
\operatorname{tr}C=b
prevents trace-gap collapse under

\nabla u\in L^{2}_{t}B^{0}_{\infty,1}
. On the resulting compact FENE window,
the low-order estimate again generates

\operatorname{Log}C\in L^{2}_{t}H^{1+\varepsilon}_{x}
, and the pressure-free anisotropic energy
controls the remaining differentiability channel.

Main theorem

(Informal form)
.

In two dimensions, every spectral isotropic elastic stress has an active
deviatoric part
q_{1}Y
after pressure projection, and this active part
cancels at top order against upper-convected stretching when the trace-free
equation is tested with weight
q_{1}/a
. More generally, for compact-window
spectral-admissible active channels

\operatorname{div}(\tau(a,|Y|^{2})Y),\qquad D_{t}Y+\lambda^{-1}\mu(a,|Y|^{2})Y=\cdots,

with positive
\tau,\mu
and stable scalar relaxation, this gives a
compact-window endpoint estimate with coefficient

1+\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}+\left\lVert\operatorname{Log}C\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}^{2}.

Consequently, a smooth two-dimensional Oldroyd–B solution continues beyond

T
if

\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert\nabla u(t)\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}\,dt<\infty,

and a smooth FENE-P solution continues beyond
T
if

\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert\nabla u(t)\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}^{2}\,dt<\infty.

In both cases the
L^{2}_{t}H^{1+\varepsilon}_{x}
logarithmic conformation bound is
generated on the propagated compact window rather than assumed as an external
continuation hypothesis.
The different velocity clocks come from window propagation: the Oldroyd–B
positive cone is propagated by the endpoint flow-map clock, while the FENE-P
trace gap has a finite-extensibility restoring force with boundary singularity
exponent one. In the general trace-gap comparison, a restoring singularity

(b-\operatorname{tr}C)^{-\theta}
gives the velocity-clock exponent

(1+\theta)/\theta
.

We keep the traditional notation

This identifies the unique stress component that enters vorticity and the

The second result is the full spectral-admissible compact-window endpoint

\tau=\psi(2a),\qquad\mu=\phi(2a),\qquad G=a\phi(2a)-\chi(2a),

\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert\nabla u(t)\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}\,dt<\infty.

C>0,\qquad\operatorname{tr}C<b.

The lower spectral boundary is propagated by the velocity clock, as in

1.2
What is new in the criterion

The first novelty is structural rather than model-specific. The paper
identifies the pressure quotient of two-dimensional isotropic elastic stress.
In two space dimensions, a smooth spectral isotropic stress has the
pressure-free active form

T(C)=\hbox{pressure mode}+q_{1}(\operatorname{tr}C,\det C)Y,\qquad C=aI+Y,\quad Y=C^{\circ}.

Thus only the deviatoric active stress enters the vorticity equation and the
principal high-order velocity–conformation coupling. The isotropic stress is
not a dangerous elastic force; it is a pressure mode. Consequently the active
channel
q_{1}(a,|Y|^{2})Y
is not a chosen constitutive form but the canonical
object left after incompressibility, isotropy, and two-dimensional
Cayley–Hamilton algebra have been imposed. This turns the anisotropic
cancellation from an Oldroyd–B computation into a projection principle for
spectral two-dimensional tensor flows.

The second novelty is that this canonical active channel has a closed endpoint
energy theory. In variables

C=aI+Y
,
\eta=|Y|^{2}
, these channels have active stress

\tau(a,\eta)Y

and coercive deviatoric relaxation
\mu(a,\eta)Y
, together with a stable
scalar trace relaxation
G(a,\eta)
. The correct weighted energy uses

W(a,\eta)=\frac{\tau(a,\eta)}{a}.

On compact conformation windows this gives a high-order estimate with
coefficient

1+\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}+\left\lVert\operatorname{Log}C\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}^{2}.

The scalar-Peterlin class, and hence Oldroyd–B and FENE-P, are then
trace-only specializations of this pressure-free spectral theory. The point is
not that the paper adds more spring laws to a list; it isolates the active
degree of freedom common to two-dimensional incompressible tensor-transport
systems driven by a spectral isotropic stress.

For the continuation theorems, this structural estimate is combined with a
low-order generation estimate. On a compact conformation window, the low-order
pressure-free energy satisfies a Gronwall inequality controlled only by

1+\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}},

and therefore generates the logarithmic norm required by the high-order
estimate. The final criteria have direct geometric meaning:

\hbox{model-specific flow clock}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\hbox{compact window and generated logarithmic regularity}.

The flow clock is measured by the critical Besov norm

\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}
. This endpoint is part of the result,
not a technical convenience:
B^{0}_{\infty,1}
is the sharp Besov summability
level at which the critical velocity-gradient information controls the
Lipschitz modulus of the flow map. It is the natural analogue of the
Beale–Kato–Majda quantity

\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert\nabla u(t)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\,dt
for the velocity gradient.
Below we write

\mathcal{B}(u)=\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}},

which controls the Lipschitz modulus of the Lagrangian flow at the endpoint
scale. Oldroyd–B uses the
L^{1}_{t}
version of this clock. FENE-P uses the
square of the same clock because the finite-extensibility trace gap has a
boundary restoring force of singularity exponent one.

The proof has four parts. First, the active-deviatoric projection identifies
the unique stress component that survives pressure projection. Second, on a
compact conformation window, the spectral-admissible weighted energy closes
with the endpoint coefficient above; the trace-only scalar-Peterlin estimate is
recovered as a special case. Third, the required compact windows are
propagated by model-specific scalar clocks and the low-order estimate generates
the logarithmic
L^{2}_{t}H^{1+\varepsilon}_{x}
norm. Fourth, the entropy-blind and
fixed-spectrum sequences in Section
7
show that this generated
derivative threshold is sharp: zero-order spectral thermodynamics cannot see
high-frequency rotation of the active deviatoric stress.

Proof architecture.

The proof has a closed four-step structure. First, the spectral stress is
split as

T(C)=(q_{0}+aq_{1})I+q_{1}Y,\qquad C=aI+Y,\qquad Y=C^{\circ}.

The first term is a pressure mode and the active force is
\operatorname{div}(q_{1}Y)
.
Second, for spectral-admissible active channels this active force is paired
with the trace-free equation through the weight
W(a,\eta)=\tau(a,\eta)/a
.
The differentiated
velocity equation contains

-\alpha\int\tau(a,\eta)\,\partial^{\beta}Y:S(\partial^{\beta}u)\,dx,

while the trace-free energy contains the opposite term

\frac{\alpha}{2}\int W(a,\eta)\partial^{\beta}Y:2aS(\partial^{\beta}u)\,dx=\alpha\int\tau(a,\eta)\,\partial^{\beta}Y:S(\partial^{\beta}u)\,dx.

The leading terms cancel before any absorption is used. Third, the compact
window needed for this comparison is propagated by the relevant scalar barrier,
and the low-order pressure-free estimate generates the logarithmic

L^{2}_{t}H^{1+\varepsilon}_{x}
term on that window. Fourth, after the endpoint transport
commutators and the derivative of the weight are estimated, the resulting
high-order coefficient is

1+\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}+\left\lVert\operatorname{Log}C\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}^{2}.

In the Oldroyd–B specialization,
\psi\equiv 1
and
W=a^{-1}
, which gives
the pure velocity-clock criterion in Theorem
6.10
; see also
Proposition
6.9
for the generated
logarithmic channel.

1.3
Relation with Euler and stress-diffusive models

The regularity problem for the two-dimensional Oldroyd–B system
without stress diffusion lies between two regimes that are much better
understood. If stress diffusion is imposed, the missing derivative in the
conformation equation is restored by parabolic smoothing. If the data are
small, perturbative mechanisms can keep the non-diffusive stress from feeding a
large cascade back into the velocity. The present paper treats the difficult
regime in which neither source of control is available: no artificial stress
dissipation and no smallness.

For the two-dimensional Euler equation, vorticity is transported and the
classical obstruction is growth of the Lipschitz norm of the velocity. For
Oldroyd–B without stress diffusion, the vorticity equation contains the extra
forcing
\operatorname{curl}\operatorname{div}A
. The stress is transported and stretched, but it has
no parabolic regularization. Thus the Euler-type vorticity criterion becomes
a coupled velocity–conformation criterion; see Corollary
6.12
.
The endpoint velocity clock prevents Oldroyd–B escape from the positive cone,
while the generated logarithmic channel controls the remaining high-frequency
stress mechanism.

1.4
Related work

The Oldroyd model goes back to Oldroyd’s constitutive theory

[
13
]
. Strong and weak solution theories for differential
viscoelastic models were developed in several directions, including the early
work of Guillope and Saut
[
9
]
and the global weak-solution
framework of Lions and Masmoudi
[
11
]
. Lifespan and
breakdown criteria for related viscoelastic systems were studied by Chemin and
Masmoudi
[
4
]
and by Lei, Masmoudi, and Zhou

[
10
]
. When additional stress diffusion is present,
two-dimensional regularity becomes substantially more tractable; see, for
example, Constantin and Kliegl
[
5
]
, the global
regularity results of Elgindi and Rousset
[
6
]
, and the
regularized model of Barrett and Boyaval
[
1
]
. In the
stress-diffusion-free system, global control is also known in small-data
regimes; a recent example is the work of Tu, Wang, and Wen

[
14
]
. The present paper is complementary to both directions:
it does not add stress diffusion and does not assume smallness. Instead it
states a continuation criterion in variables adapted to the conformation
geometry.

The logarithmic conformation representation was introduced in numerical
rheology by Fattal and Kupferman
[
7
,
8
]

to preserve positivity and improve high-Weissenberg-number computations. The
present use is analytic: the logarithmic chart separates spectral control from
high-frequency concentration. The velocity part of the criterion follows the
spirit of the Beale–Kato–Majda continuation principle

[
2
]
, but the non-diffusive stress equation forces the
additional logarithmic stress diagnostic.

The finitely extensible models considered below originate from the standard
FENE closures for dilute polymeric fluids; see, for example, Bird, Armstrong,
and Hassager
[
3
]
. Mathematical work on FENE dumbbell models has
also emphasized the role of the finite-extension boundary; a representative
global well-posedness result is due to Masmoudi
[
12
]
. The
FENE-P part of the present paper has a different aim. It does not prove a new
global regularity theorem. It shows that the finite-extensibility boundary can
be propagated by the squared endpoint velocity clock and that the same
pressure-free active-deviatoric cancellation closes the high-order continuation
estimate.

1.5
Comparison with direct continuation criteria

Direct Sobolev criteria for the conformation tensor mix two mechanisms:
approach to the boundary of the positive cone and derivative concentration
inside the cone. The logarithmic chart
A=e^{B}
separates these mechanisms.
The endpoint velocity clock propagates the spectral window, while the
generated
L^{2}_{t}H^{1+\varepsilon}_{x}
bound for
\operatorname{Log}A
controls the remaining
high-frequency channel. Entropy and relaxation are still used for physical
admissibility, but the examples in Section
7
show that they
cannot supply this derivative.

The gain is therefore not an interpolation improvement of a direct stress
criterion. It comes from first removing the pressure mode and then cancelling
the principal velocity–anisotropic stress coupling in the physical variables

A=aI+Y
. In FENE-P the same mechanism survives with force

\operatorname{div}(f_{b}Y)
and weight
f_{b}/a
; the additional squared clock enters only
through propagation of the trace-gap barrier.

To make the comparison precise, the present theorem should be read as
complementary to the classical direct-stress criteria rather than as a formal
replacement of them. Chemin and Masmoudi proved a two-dimensional non-blowup
criterion in terms of the stress itself, with a condition of the form

\int_{0}^{T}\bigl(\left\lVert\tau(t)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+|b|\,\left\lVert\tau(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\bigr)\,dt<\infty

for the Oldroyd stress formulation. Lei, Masmoudi, and Zhou later gave a
simpler proof and improved this to a BKM-type condition involving

\tau\in L^{1}(0,T;{\rm BMO})
, together with a low-integrability stress
control such as
L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}
or
L^{2}_{t}L^{2}_{x}
. Those criteria monitor
the directly forced stress tensor. The criterion proved here uses a different
geometric variable and a different quotient: the isotropic stress is first
removed as a pressure mode, the remaining active channel is written in the
trace-free conformation variable
Y
, and the derivative control is generated
for the log-conformation
\operatorname{Log}A
. The new feature is therefore not merely
a different time exponent. It is the direct coupling of the critical velocity
clock to the positive-cone geometry of the conformation tensor, through the
decomposition
A=aI+Y
and the generated bound

\operatorname{Log}A\in L^{2}_{t}H^{1+\varepsilon}_{x}
. In this sense the result replaces a
direct-stress continuation mechanism by a pressure-quotient,
trace-free/log-conformation mechanism.

2
Functional Setting and Continuation Framework

We work on the periodic torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
to avoid boundary effects. The same
local estimates apply on the whole plane with the standard modifications for
decay and low frequencies. The main estimates are stated for integer

s\geq 3
, so the high-order differentiations are classical and the velocity
parabolic estimate controls the required Lipschitz quantities.

Notation.

Constants denoted by
C
may change from line to line. Constants denoted by

C_{K}
may also depend on a fixed spectral window

\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq K
. In the FENE-P part,
K
also denotes a
compact subdomain of

\mathcal{D}_{b}=\{C\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{2}:\operatorname{tr}C<b\};

the constants may then depend on
b
, the lower spectral gap of
C
, and
the upper trace gap
b-\operatorname{tr}C
. To keep the exposition focused on the
geometric mechanism, high-order differentiations are written for integer

s\geq 3
. The fractional case follows from the same paradifferential
estimates in the usual Besov-Sobolev scale.

Definition 2.1

(Strong positive-cone solution)
.

Let
T>0
. A strong positive-cone solution on
[0,T]
is a pair

(u,A)
such that

u\in C([0,T];H^{s}),\qquad A\in C([0,T];H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{S}_{++}^{2})),

u\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{s+1}),\qquad\operatorname{div}u=0,

and (
1
)–(
2
) hold in the classical
Sobolev sense.

Definition 2.2

(Strong FENE-P solution)
.

Let
T>0
. A strong FENE-P solution on
[0,T]
is a pair
(u,C)
such
that

u\in C([0,T];H^{s}),\qquad C\in C([0,T];H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{S}_{++}^{2})),

u\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{s+1}),\qquad\operatorname{div}u=0,\qquad C(t,x)\in\mathcal{D}_{b},

and (
4
)–(
5
) hold in the
classical Sobolev sense.

Proposition 2.3

(Local continuation principle)
.

Let
s\geq 3
,
u_{0}\in H^{s}
,
\operatorname{div}u_{0}=0
, and

A_{0}\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{S}_{++}^{2})
with spectral range contained in

[m_{0},M_{0}]
,
0<m_{0}<M_{0}<\infty
. Then there exists a unique strong
positive-cone solution on a time interval
[0,T_{\rm loc}]
. Moreover, if a
solution on
[0,T_{*})
satisfies

\sup_{t<T_{*}}\left(\left\lVert u(t)\right\rVert_{H^{s}}+\left\lVert A(t)\right\rVert_{H^{s}}+\left\lVert A(t)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+\left\lVert A(t)^{-1}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\right)<\infty,

then it extends as a strong positive-cone solution beyond
T_{*}
.

Proof.

Regularize the system, solve by Picard iteration, and obtain estimates in

H^{s}
for
A
and in
H^{s}\cap L^{2}_{t}H^{s+1}
for
u
. Positivity is
propagated along characteristics by Lemma
4.3
. The lower spectral
bound prevents degeneration of the cone, while the upper spectral and Sobolev
bounds control all nonlinear coefficients.

More explicitly, the regularized estimates give a local existence time

T_{\rm loc}\geq\tau\!\left(\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}+\left\lVert A_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{s}},\left\lVert A_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}},\left\lVert A_{0}^{-1}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\right)>0,

where
\tau
is nonincreasing in its arguments. The same estimate applies
when the construction is restarted from any time
t_{0}<T_{*}
. Under
(
2.3
), these arguments remain bounded uniformly for

t_{0}<T_{*}
. Hence there is a uniform
\tau_{*}>0
such that the solution
restarts on
[t_{0},t_{0}+\tau_{*}]
for every
t_{0}<T_{*}
sufficiently close to

T_{*}
. Choosing
t_{0}>T_{*}-\tau_{*}/2
gives an extension beyond
T_{*}
.
∎

Remark 2.4

(Why logarithmic coordinates appear in the estimates)
.

The local principle is naturally stated in
A
, but the compact-window
estimates are most transparent in
B=\operatorname{Log}A
. Uniform upper and lower
spectral bounds for
A
are equivalent to an
L^{\infty}
bound for
B
.
On such spectral windows, the maps
B\mapsto e^{B}
and
A\mapsto\operatorname{Log}A
are
smooth on Sobolev spaces. Therefore controlling
B
in
H^{s}
is equivalent
to controlling
A
in
H^{s}
, with constants depending only on the spectral
window.

Proposition 2.5

(Local continuation in the FENE-P domain)
.

Let
s\geq 3
,
u_{0}\in H^{s}
,
\operatorname{div}u_{0}=0
, and

C_{0}\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathcal{D}_{b})
. Assume that the range of
C_{0}
is contained
in a compact set
K_{0}\Subset\mathcal{D}_{b}
. Then there exists a unique strong
FENE-P solution on a time interval
[0,T_{\rm loc}]
. Moreover, if a
solution on
[0,T_{*})
satisfies

\sup_{t<T_{*}}\left(\left\lVert u(t)\right\rVert_{H^{s}}+\left\lVert C(t)\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\right)<\infty

and the range of
C(t,\cdot)
remains in a fixed compact set

K\Subset\mathcal{D}_{b}
for all
t<T_{*}
, then the solution extends beyond

T_{*}
as a strong FENE-P solution.

Proof.

On
K\Subset\mathcal{D}_{b}
, the maps

C\mapsto f_{b}(C),\qquad C\mapsto T_{b}(C),\qquad C\mapsto f_{b}(C)C-I

are smooth with bounded derivatives of every finite order. The FENE-P system
therefore has the same quasilinear parabolic-transport structure as the
Oldroyd–B system, with composition constants depending only on
K
,
b
,
and the
H^{s}
size of
C
. Regularization and Picard iteration give a
local existence time bounded below by a nonincreasing function of

\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}+\left\lVert C_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}

and the compact-set constants of
K_{0}
. Restarting the construction at
times
t_{0}<T_{*}
gives the same lower bound with
K
in place of
K_{0}
.
The stated uniform bounds therefore give a uniform restart time and extend the
solution beyond
T_{*}
.
∎

3
Analytic Preliminaries

We collect the analytic estimates used below. They are standard consequences
of Littlewood–Paley theory and Moser calculus, but isolating them makes the
continuation proof transparent.

Lemma 3.1

(Standard Moser and tame calculus)
.

Let
r>1
in two dimensions. Then
H^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
is an algebra and

\left\lVert fg\right\rVert_{H^{r}}\leq C_{r}\left(\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{H^{r}}+\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{H^{r}}\right).

If
F\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
,
F(0)=0
, and

\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq K
, then

\left\lVert F(v)\right\rVert_{H^{r}}\leq C_{F,K,r}\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{H^{r}}.

The same estimates hold componentwise for matrix-valued functions.
Moreover, if
m\geq 2
,
0<\varepsilon<1
, and

f_{1},\ldots,f_{N}
be smooth scalar, vector, or matrix fields on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
. If
at most one factor is measured at order
m
, then

\left\lVert\prod_{j=1}^{N}f_{j}\right\rVert_{H^{m}}\leq C\prod_{j=1}^{N}\left(1+\left\lVert f_{j}\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}\right)\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\lVert f_{j}\right\rVert_{H^{m}}.

The constant depends on
m,\varepsilon,N
and on fixed
L^{\infty}
bounds for
smooth coefficient functions of the
f_{j}
’s.

Proof.

These are standard consequences of the Bony paraproduct decomposition

fg=T_{f}g+T_{g}f+R(f,g)
. Since
r>1=d/2
,
H^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
is an algebra.
The composition bound follows by writing

F(v)=\int_{0}^{1}DF(\theta v)v\,d\theta.

For (
3.1
), put the highest derivative on one factor and
place all remaining factors in
H^{1+\varepsilon}\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}
.
∎

Lemma 3.2

(Endpoint transport estimate)
.

Let
m\geq 2
,
\operatorname{div}u=0
, and
f\in H^{m}
. Then

\left|\sum_{|\gamma|\leq m}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\partial^{\gamma}f\,[\partial^{\gamma},u\cdot\nabla]f\,dx\right|\leq C_{m}\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}.

Proof.

This is the standard commutator estimate in the critical Besov scale. In
Littlewood–Paley variables, low-high interactions are controlled by the
Lipschitz modulus of the low-frequency velocity, while high-low interactions
are summable exactly because the velocity gradient is in
B^{0}_{\infty,1}
.
The divergence-free condition removes the top-order transport contribution.
∎

4
Entropy and Positive-Cone Geometry

Definition 4.1

(Free energy)
.

For a smooth positive-cone solution define

\mathcal{E}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}|u|^{2}\,dx+\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\left(\operatorname{tr}A-\log\det A-2\right)\,dx.

Proposition 4.2

(Entropy identity)
.

Every smooth solution of (
1
)–(
2
)
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}(t)

\displaystyle+\nu\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx+\frac{\alpha}{2\lambda}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\operatorname{tr}(A+A^{-1}-2I)\,dx=0.

Proof.

Testing the momentum equation by
u
gives

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int|u|^{2}\,dx+\nu\int|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx=-\alpha\int(A-I):\nabla u\,dx.

Testing the conformation equation by
I-A^{-1}
gives

\frac{d}{dt}\int(\operatorname{tr}A-\log\det A-2)\,dx=2\int(A-I):\nabla u\,dx-\lambda^{-1}\int\operatorname{tr}(A+A^{-1}-2I)\,dx.

The stretching terms cancel after multiplication by
\alpha/2
.
∎

Lemma 4.3

(Cone propagation)
.

If
A_{0}(x)\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{2}
and

\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert\nabla u(t)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\,dt<\infty
, then

A(t,x)\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{2}
for all
t\leq T
. Moreover, along characteristics,
the extreme eigenvalues obey

\lambda_{\max}A(t)\leq C\left(\lambda_{\max}A_{0}+1\right)\exp\left(2\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert\nabla u(s)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\,ds\right),

and an analogous lower bound holds for
\lambda_{\min}A(t)
.

Proof.

Along a trajectory
X(t)
, the matrix satisfies

\frac{d}{dt}A=\nabla u\,A+A(\nabla u)^{T}-\lambda^{-1}(A-I).

Testing against unit vectors and applying Gronwall gives the upper spectral
bound. Applying the same argument to
A^{-1}
, whose equation is obtained by
differentiating
A^{-1}A=I
, gives the lower bound.
∎

5
Logarithmic Coordinates and Good Unknowns

Let
B=\operatorname{Log}A
. The derivative of the matrix exponential is

d\exp_{B}(C)=\int_{0}^{1}e^{(1-\theta)B}Ce^{\theta B}\,d\theta.

If the spectrum of
B
is contained in
[-K,K]
, then

c_{K}|C|\leq|d\exp_{B}(C)|\leq C_{K}|C|\qquad(C=C^{T}),

and the constants depend only on
K
. Thus the logarithmic coordinate is
not merely a change of notation: it gives an elliptic chart on every compact
spectral subset of the positive cone.

Lemma 5.1

(Sobolev calculus for the exponential chart)
.

Let
s>1
in two dimensions and suppose

\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq K
. Then

\left\lVert e^{B}-I\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\leq C_{K}\left(1+\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\right),

and, for two symmetric fields
B_{1},B_{2}
with the same
L^{\infty}
bound,

\left\lVert e^{B_{1}}-e^{B_{2}}\right\rVert_{H^{s-1}}\leq C_{K}\left(1+\left\lVert B_{1}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}+\left\lVert B_{2}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\right)\left\lVert B_{1}-B_{2}\right\rVert_{H^{s-1}}.

The same estimates hold for
B\mapsto(d\exp_{B})^{-1}
on symmetric matrices.

Proof.

Use the integral representation

e^{B}-I=\int_{0}^{1}e^{\theta B}B\,d\theta

and Lemma
3.1
. The Lipschitz bound follows from

e^{B_{1}}-e^{B_{2}}=\int_{0}^{1}d\exp_{B_{2}+\theta(B_{1}-B_{2})}(B_{1}-B_{2})\,d\theta.

The inverse derivative is controlled by (
5
) and the
smooth dependence of the inverse operator on
B
on compact spectral sets.
∎

In logarithmic variables, the conformation equation can be written as

d\exp_{B}\left(\partial_{t}B+u\cdot\nabla B\right)=\nabla u\,e^{B}+e^{B}(\nabla u)^{T}-\lambda^{-1}(e^{B}-I).

When the spectrum of
B
is bounded,
d\exp_{B}
and its inverse are bounded
on symmetric matrices. Thus the equation is transport-dominated, but the
source is nonlinear in
B
and
\nabla u
.

The naive differentiated equation contains two bad-looking terms. The first
is the standard transport commutator

[\partial^{\gamma},u\cdot\nabla]B
, which is controlled by a critical Besov
norm of
\nabla u
. The second is more specific to the positive cone: when
one differentiates
e^{B}
, the top derivative does not simply equal

e^{B}\partial^{\gamma}B
. It contains non-commuting matrix factors and
lower-order products. The good unknown below subtracts the paralinear part of
this matrix-exponential commutator. Geometrically, it applies the inverse
tangent map of the exponential chart and therefore pulls the differentiated
conformation tensor
\partial^{\gamma}A
back to the logarithmic tangent
space at
B
. This is the point at which the positive-cone geometry enters
the continuation argument.

Definition 5.2

(Logarithmic good unknown)
.

For a multi-index
\gamma
, define

\mathcal{G}_{\gamma}=(d\exp_{B})^{-1}\left(\partial^{\gamma}(e^{B})\right).

By Lemma
5.3
,

\mathcal{G}_{\gamma}=\partial^{\gamma}B+(d\exp_{B})^{-1}\mathcal{C}_{\gamma}(B).

Thus
\mathcal{G}_{\gamma}
is an Alinhac-type correction of

\partial^{\gamma}B
: it is defined intrinsically from the differentiated
conformation tensor and differs from
\partial^{\gamma}B
only by lower-order
matrix-exponential commutators.

Lemma 5.3

(Commutator structure)
.

Let
m\geq 2
,
0<\varepsilon<1
, and assume
\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq K
. Then
for
|\gamma|=m
,

\partial^{\gamma}(e^{B})=d\exp_{B}(\partial^{\gamma}B)+\mathcal{C}_{\gamma}(B),

where

\left\lVert\mathcal{C}_{\gamma}(B)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq C_{K}\left(1+\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}^{2}\right)\left(1+\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{H^{m-1}}\right).

Proof.

Expanding
\partial^{\gamma}(e^{B})
by repeated differentiation of the integral
formula for
d\exp_{B}
, the unique term containing
m
derivatives of
B

is
d\exp_{B}(\partial^{\gamma}B)
. Every other term is a finite product with
at least two positive-order derivatives of
B
and total order at most
m
.
In two dimensions the
H^{1+\varepsilon}
factor controls the low-order products,
while the remaining derivative is placed in
H^{m-1}
. The spectral bound
absorbs the exponential coefficients.
∎

Proposition 5.4

(Good-unknown estimate)
.

Let
s\geq 3
,
0<\varepsilon<1
, and let
(u,B)
be smooth. If
B

has bounded spectrum on
[0,T]
, then for every
2\leq m\leq s
,

\frac{d}{dt}\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{H^{m}}\leq C\left(1+\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}+\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}^{2}\right)\left(1+\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{H^{m}}+\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{m+1}}\right).

Proof.

Write the logarithmic equation as

\partial_{t}B+u\cdot\nabla B=\mathfrak{F}(B,\nabla u),\qquad\mathfrak{F}(B,M)=(d\exp_{B})^{-1}\left(Me^{B}+e^{B}M^{T}-\lambda^{-1}(e^{B}-I)\right).

On a fixed spectral window, Lemma
5.1
and
Lemma
3.1
give the tame bound

\left\lVert\mathfrak{F}(B,\nabla u)\right\rVert_{H^{m}}\leq C_{K}\left(1+\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}^{2}\right)\left(1+\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{H^{m}}+\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{m+1}}\right).

Indeed the top derivative falls either on
\nabla u
, giving

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{m+1}}
, or on one copy of
B
in the exponential
coefficients, giving a term linear in
\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{H^{m}}
; all remaining factors
are placed in
H^{1+\varepsilon}\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}
. Differentiating the
transport equation and pairing with
\partial^{\gamma}B
yields

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left\lVert\partial^{\gamma}B\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\partial^{\gamma}B\,[\partial^{\gamma},u\cdot\nabla]B\,dx+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\partial^{\gamma}B\,\partial^{\gamma}\mathfrak{F}\,dx.

The endpoint transport commutator is bounded by Lemma
3.2
:

\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\partial^{\gamma}B\,[\partial^{\gamma},u\cdot\nabla]B\,dx\right|\leq C\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}.

After summing over
|\gamma|\leq m
, using (
5
), and taking the
square root of the resulting differential inequality, we obtain
(
5.4
). The good unknown
\mathcal{G}_{\gamma}
gives an equivalent
way to phrase the same estimate in the differentiated conformation variable

\partial^{\gamma}A
: by Lemma
5.3
,

\mathcal{G}_{\gamma}-\partial^{\gamma}B
is lower order and is absorbed by the

\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}^{2}(1+\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{H^{m}})
term.
∎

Proposition 6.1

(Pressure-free active-deviatoric projection)

Let

T(QCQ^{T})=QT(C)Q^{T},\qquad Q\in O(2).

For every compact
K\Subset\mathbb{S}_{++}^{2}
, there are smooth scalar functions

q_{0},q_{1}
, depending on
s=\operatorname{tr}C
and
d=\det C
, such that

T(C)=q_{0}(\operatorname{tr}C,\det C)I+q_{1}(\operatorname{tr}C,\det C)C.

Writing
C=aI+Y
,
a=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}C
,
Y=C^{\circ}
, one has

T(C)=(q_{0}+aq_{1})I+q_{1}Y,\qquad T(C)^{\circ}=q_{1}Y.

Consequently, in the incompressible momentum equation, the isotropic part is a
pressure mode and only
\operatorname{div}(q_{1}Y)
enters the vorticity and the top-order
velocity–conformation coupling.
If the conformation equation has upper-convected stretching

D_{t}C=\nabla u\,C+C(\nabla u)^{T}+R(C),

then the principal active coupling cancels when the trace-free equation is
tested with weight
q_{1}/a
.

Proof.

Spectral isotropy implies that
T(C)
and
C
are coaxial. If
C
has
distinct eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
and
T(C)
has corresponding
eigenvalues
t_{1},t_{2}
, define

q_{1}=\frac{t_{1}-t_{2}}{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}},\qquad q_{0}=\frac{\lambda_{1}t_{2}-\lambda_{2}t_{1}}{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}.

The smooth spectral representation theorem, equivalently the smooth
divided-difference lemma for symmetric spectral functions, extends
q_{0},q_{1}

smoothly across the repeated-eigenvalue set. Since symmetric smooth functions
of
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
are smooth functions of
\operatorname{tr}C
and
\det C
on
compact positive-cone windows, the displayed representation follows.

The pressure projection is then algebraic:

\operatorname{div}T(C)=\nabla(q_{0}+aq_{1})+\operatorname{div}(q_{1}Y).

The first term is absorbed into the pressure and has zero curl. Finally, the
trace-free part of the upper-convected stretching is

\{\nabla u\,C+C(\nabla u)^{T}\}^{\circ}=2aS(u)+[\nabla u\,Y+Y(\nabla u)^{T}]^{\circ}.

At derivative order
\beta
, the velocity equation contributes

-\alpha\int q_{1}\,\partial^{\beta}Y:S(\partial^{\beta}u)\,dx,

while testing the trace-free equation by

(\alpha/2)(q_{1}/a)\partial^{\beta}Y
gives

\frac{\alpha}{2}\int\frac{q_{1}}{a}\partial^{\beta}Y:2aS(\partial^{\beta}u)\,dx=\alpha\int q_{1}\,\partial^{\beta}Y:S(\partial^{\beta}u)\,dx.

The principal terms cancel exactly.
∎

We next record the closed endpoint estimate for the full spectral-admissible
compact-window class. This is the model class in which the active-deviatoric
projection above becomes a high-order continuation theorem at the level of the
two-dimensional spectral active channel itself.

Definition 6.2

(Full spectral-admissible compact-window closure)
.

Let
K\Subset\mathbb{S}_{++}^{2}
be a compact conformation window and write

C=aI+Y,\qquad a=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}C,\qquad Y=C^{\circ},\qquad\eta=|Y|^{2}.

A compact-window closure is spectral-admissible on
K
if, in the variables

(a,Y)
, it has the form

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+u\cdot\nabla u-\nu\Delta u+\nabla p

\displaystyle=\alpha\operatorname{div}(\tau(a,\eta)Y),

(7)

\displaystyle D_{t}a+\lambda^{-1}G(a,\eta)

\displaystyle=S(u):Y,

(8)

\displaystyle D_{t}Y+\lambda^{-1}\mu(a,\eta)Y

\displaystyle=2aS(u)+[\nabla u\,Y+Y(\nabla u)^{T}]^{\circ}.

(9)

The coefficients are smooth on the range of
(a,\eta)
generated by
K
.
Moreover, there are constants
a_{0},\tau_{0},\mu_{0},\kappa>0
and
a_{*}
such that

a\geq a_{0},\qquad\tau(a,\eta)\geq\tau_{0},\qquad\mu(a,\eta)\geq\mu_{0},\qquad G(a_{*},0)=0,\qquad\partial_{a}G(a,\eta)\geq\kappa

on this range. These are compact-window continuation assumptions. They do
not by themselves assert a thermodynamic free-energy law for arbitrary

\tau,\mu,G
.

Proposition 6.3

(Full spectral-admissible compact-window endpoint estimate)
.

Let
m\geq 3
,
0<\varepsilon<1
, and let
(u,a,Y)
be a smooth solution of
(
7
)–(
9
) on
[0,T]
, with

C=aI+Y
remaining in
K\Subset\mathbb{S}_{++}^{2}
. Put

W(a,\eta)=\frac{\tau(a,\eta)}{a},\qquad h=a-a_{*}.

Then there exists
\sigma_{0}=\sigma_{0}(K)>0
such that, for every

0<\sigma\leq\sigma_{0}
, the energy

\mathcal{E}_{m}^{\rm spec}=\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}+\sigma\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{4}\sum_{|\beta|\leq m}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}W(a,\eta)|\partial^{\beta}Y|^{2}\,dx

satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}_{m}^{\rm spec}

\displaystyle+c\nu\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{m+1}}^{2}+c\lambda^{-1}\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}+c\sigma\lambda^{-1}\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{K}\left(1+\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}+\left\lVert\operatorname{Log}C\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}^{2}\right)\mathcal{E}_{m}^{\rm spec}.

Proof.

The compact window makes
\mathcal{E}_{m}^{\rm spec}
equivalent to

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}+\sigma\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}+\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}
, with constants
depending on
K
and
\sigma
. It also gives the composition bound

\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}+\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}\leq C_{K}\left(1+\left\lVert\operatorname{Log}C\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}\right).

We first isolate the new coefficient commutators. For

M_{\beta}^{\tau}=\partial^{\beta}(\tau(a,\eta)Y)-\tau(a,\eta)\partial^{\beta}Y,

the tame composition lemma gives, for
|\beta|\leq m
,

\left\lVert M_{\beta}^{\tau}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq C_{K}\left(1+\left\lVert\operatorname{Log}C\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}\right)\left(\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{H^{m}}+\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{H^{m}}\right).

Indeed, for
|\beta|\geq 1
,

\partial^{\beta}\tau=\tau_{a}\partial^{\beta}a+2\tau_{\eta}(Y:\partial^{\beta}Y)+R_{\beta}^{\tau},

where

\left\lVert R_{\beta}^{\tau}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq C_{K}\left\lVert(a,Y)\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}\left\lVert(h,Y)\right\rVert_{H^{m}}.

All remaining terms in
M_{\beta}^{\tau}
contain at least two positive
derivatives and obey the same Moser bound. Therefore, for every
\delta>0
,

\left|\int M_{\beta}^{\tau}:S(\partial^{\beta}u)\,dx\right|\leq\delta\nu\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{m+1}}^{2}+C_{\delta,K}\left(1+\left\lVert\operatorname{Log}C\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}^{2}\right)\mathcal{E}_{m}^{\rm spec}.

The identical estimate holds with
\tau
replaced by
\mu
in

\partial^{\beta}(\mu Y)-\mu\partial^{\beta}Y
, without the viscous absorption.

Apply
\partial^{\beta}
to the velocity equation and test by

\partial^{\beta}u
. The principal active term is

-\alpha\int\tau(a,\eta)\partial^{\beta}Y:S(\partial^{\beta}u)\,dx,

and the difference between
\partial^{\beta}(\tau Y)
and

\tau\partial^{\beta}Y
is controlled by
(
6
). The transport and viscous terms are
controlled as in Lemma
6.7
.

Next apply
\partial^{\beta}
to the
Y
-equation and test by

(\alpha/2)W\partial^{\beta}Y
. The stretching term gives

\frac{\alpha}{2}\int W\partial^{\beta}Y:2aS(\partial^{\beta}u)\,dx=\alpha\int\tau(a,\eta)\partial^{\beta}Y:S(\partial^{\beta}u)\,dx,

which cancels (
6
). The main relaxation term gives

\frac{\alpha}{2\lambda}\int W\mu|\partial^{\beta}Y|^{2}\,dx\geq c_{K}\lambda^{-1}\left\lVert\partial^{\beta}Y\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

The coefficient commutator

\partial^{\beta}(\mu Y)-\mu\partial^{\beta}Y
is controlled by the tame bound
above. Since

D_{t}W=W_{a}D_{t}a+W_{\eta}D_{t}\eta

and

D_{t}\eta=4aY:S(u)+2Y:[\nabla u\,Y+Y(\nabla u)^{T}]^{\circ}-2\lambda^{-1}\mu|Y|^{2},

the compact window gives

\left\lVert D_{t}W\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq C_{K}\left(1+\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\right)\leq C_{K}\left(1+\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}\right).

The weighted transport commutator is then estimated by
Lemma
6.7
, and the remaining stretching commutators
have the same form as in the Oldroyd–B physical estimate.

It remains to treat the scalar equation. For
|\beta|\geq 1
,

\partial^{\beta}G(a,\eta)=G_{a}\partial^{\beta}h+2G_{\eta}(Y:\partial^{\beta}Y)+R_{\beta}^{G},

with

\left\lVert R_{\beta}^{G}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq C_{K}\left\lVert(a,Y)\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}\left\lVert(h,Y)\right\rVert_{H^{m}}.

Testing the differentiated scalar equation by

\sigma\partial^{\beta}h
, the top relaxation quadratic form is

\sigma G_{a}|\partial^{\beta}h|^{2}+2\sigma G_{\eta}\partial^{\beta}h\,(Y:\partial^{\beta}Y)+\frac{\alpha}{2}W\mu|\partial^{\beta}Y|^{2}.

Since
G_{a}\geq\kappa
,
W\mu\geq c_{K}>0
, and
Y
is bounded on
K
, Young’s
inequality shows that this form is bounded below by

c\sigma|\partial^{\beta}h|^{2}+c|\partial^{\beta}Y|^{2}

provided
0<\sigma\leq\sigma_{0}(K)
. At order zero,

G(a,\eta)h=G(a,0)h+\{G(a,\eta)-G(a,0)\}h.

The first term controls
h^{2}
by the same monotonicity in
a
, while the
second is bounded by
C_{K}|Y|^{2}|h|
and is absorbed by the
Y
-relaxation and
the small scalar weight. The source
S(u):Y
is estimated by

C\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{m+1}}+C\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{H^{m}},

followed by Young’s inequality.

Summing the velocity, trace-free, and scalar estimates over

|\beta|\leq m
, choosing
\delta>0
small and then

\sigma\leq\sigma_{0}(K)
, gives (
6.3
).
∎

The scalar-Peterlin theorem is the trace-only specialization of
Proposition
6.3
. We keep the explicit
statement because it is the form used for Oldroyd–B and FENE-P.

Definition 6.4

(Continuation-admissible scalar-Peterlin closure)
.

Let
\mathcal{I}\Subset(0,\infty)
be a compact trace interval. A triple

(\psi,\phi,\chi)
is continuation-admissible on
\mathcal{I}
if the
coefficients are smooth on
\mathcal{I}
and, with

\Psi(a)=\psi(2a),\qquad\Phi(a)=\phi(2a),\qquad G(a)=a\phi(2a)-\chi(2a),

there are constants
a_{0},\Psi_{0},\Phi_{0},\kappa>0
and

a_{*}\in\{a:2a\in\mathcal{I}\}

such that

a\geq a_{0},\qquad\Psi(a)\geq\Psi_{0},\qquad\Phi(a)\geq\Phi_{0},\qquad G(a_{*})=0,\qquad G^{\prime}(a)\geq\kappa

for
2a\in\mathcal{I}
. These are the hypotheses used in the endpoint
continuation estimate.

The thermodynamic status of a general scalar-Peterlin closure is an additional
assumption, not a consequence of the notation. A sufficient compatibility
condition is the existence of a potential
F
with
F^{\prime}=\psi
such that

\bigl(\psi(\operatorname{tr}C)I-C^{-1}\bigr):\bigl(\chi(\operatorname{tr}C)I-\phi(\operatorname{tr}C)C\bigr)\leq 0

for
C
in the compact conformation window. Then

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}(F(\operatorname{tr}C)-\log\det C)\,dx
is dissipated by the relaxation part.
Oldroyd–B and FENE-P satisfy this compatibility, but
Proposition
6.5
only uses the continuation
admissibility stated above.

Proposition 6.5

(Scalar-Peterlin compact-window endpoint estimate)
.

Let
m\geq 3
,
0<\varepsilon<1
, and consider a smooth solution of

\partial_{t}u+u\cdot\nabla u-\nu\Delta u+\nabla p=\alpha\operatorname{div}(\psi(r)C-I),\qquad r=\operatorname{tr}C,

D_{t}C=\nabla u\,C+C(\nabla u)^{T}+\lambda^{-1}\chi(r)I-\lambda^{-1}\phi(r)C,\qquad\operatorname{div}u=0.

Write
C=aI+Y
,
a=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}C
,
Y=C^{\circ}
, and set

\Psi(a)=\psi(2a),\qquad\Phi(a)=\phi(2a),\qquad G(a)=a\phi(2a)-\chi(2a),\qquad W(a)=\frac{\Psi(a)}{a}.

Assume that
C(t,x)
remains in a compact conformation window
K
, that

a\geq a_{0}>0,\qquad\Psi(a)\geq\Psi_{0}>0,\qquad\Phi(a)\geq\Phi_{0}>0

on this window, and that for some
a_{*}
and
\kappa>0
,

G(a_{*})=0,\qquad G^{\prime}(a)\geq\kappa

on the scalar range of
K
. Define

\mathcal{E}_{m}^{\psi}=\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}+\left\lVert a-a_{*}\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{4}\sum_{|\beta|\leq m}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}W(a)|\partial^{\beta}Y|^{2}\,dx.

Then

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}_{m}^{\psi}

\displaystyle+c\nu\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{m+1}}^{2}+c\lambda^{-1}\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}+c\lambda^{-1}\left\lVert a-a_{*}\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}

(10)

\displaystyle\leq C_{K}\left(1+\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}+\left\lVert\operatorname{Log}C\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}^{2}\right)\mathcal{E}_{m}^{\psi}.

Proof.

The pressure-free splitting is

\psi(r)C-I=(\Psi a-1)I+\Psi Y.

Thus the momentum equation becomes

\partial_{t}u+u\cdot\nabla u-\nu\Delta u+\nabla\pi=\alpha\operatorname{div}(\Psi Y).

The scalar and trace-free equations are

D_{t}(a-a_{*})+\lambda^{-1}G(a)=S(u):Y,

D_{t}Y+\lambda^{-1}\Phi Y=2aS(u)+[\nabla u\,Y+Y(\nabla u)^{T}]^{\circ}.

The compact window makes
\mathcal{E}_{m}^{\psi}
equivalent to

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}+\left\lVert a-a_{*}\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}+\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}
.

Apply
\partial^{\beta}
,
|\beta|\leq m
, to the velocity equation and test
by
\partial^{\beta}u
. The principal elastic term is

-\alpha\int\Psi\,\partial^{\beta}Y:S(\partial^{\beta}u)\,dx.

The difference between
\partial^{\beta}(\Psi Y)
and

\Psi\partial^{\beta}Y
is estimated by Moser calculus:

\left\lVert\partial^{\beta}(\Psi Y)-\Psi\partial^{\beta}Y\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq C_{K}(1+\left\lVert\operatorname{Log}C\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}})\bigl(\left\lVert a-a_{*}\right\rVert_{H^{m}}+\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{H^{m}}\bigr).

There is no additive constant: the commutator vanishes for
\beta=0
, and
for
|\beta|>0
every term contains a positive derivative of
a-a_{*}
or

Y
. Young’s inequality bounds the commutator contribution by

\delta\nu\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{m+1}}^{2}+C_{\delta,K}(1+\left\lVert\operatorname{Log}C\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}^{2})\mathcal{E}_{m}^{\psi}.

Next differentiate the
Y
-equation and test by

(\alpha/2)W\partial^{\beta}Y
. The principal stretching term gives

\frac{\alpha}{2}\int W\partial^{\beta}Y:2aS(\partial^{\beta}u)\,dx=\alpha\int\Psi\,\partial^{\beta}Y:S(\partial^{\beta}u)\,dx,

which cancels the principal velocity contribution. The relaxation term gives

\frac{\alpha}{2\lambda}\int W\Phi|\partial^{\beta}Y|^{2}\,dx\geq c\lambda^{-1}\left\lVert\partial^{\beta}Y\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

The material derivative of the weight satisfies

D_{t}W=W^{\prime}(a)\{S(u):Y-\lambda^{-1}G(a)\},

and hence

\left\lVert D_{t}W\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq C_{K}(1+\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}})\leq C_{K}(1+\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}).

The weighted transport commutator is controlled by the endpoint
Coifman–Meyer estimate

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{|\beta|\leq m}\int W\,\partial^{\beta}Y:[\partial^{\beta},u\cdot\nabla]Y\,dx\right|

\displaystyle\leq C_{K}\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}+C_{K}\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{m+1}}\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{H^{m}}.

This is precisely the weighted form of Lemma
6.7
:
the weight is placed in
L^{\infty}
, while the evolution of the weight is
handled by
D_{t}W
. The remaining stretching and relaxation commutators have
the same tame form. Using the compact-window composition bound

\left\lVert a-a_{*}\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}+\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}\leq C_{K}(1+\left\lVert\operatorname{Log}C\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}),

their total contribution is bounded by

\delta\nu\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{m+1}}^{2}+C_{\delta,K}(1+\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}+\left\lVert\operatorname{Log}C\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}^{2})\mathcal{E}_{m}^{\psi}.

It remains to estimate the scalar equation. Put
h=a-a_{*}
and

F(h)=G(a_{*}+h)
. Then
F(0)=0
and
F^{\prime}(h)\geq\kappa
. For
|\beta|\geq 1
,

\partial^{\beta}F(h)=F^{\prime}(h)\partial^{\beta}h+R_{\beta}^{F},

where
R_{\beta}^{F}=0
if
|\beta|=1
, and if
|\beta|\geq 2
,

\left\lVert R_{\beta}^{F}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq C_{K}\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{H^{m}}.

This is the standard scalar Moser remainder: every monomial in
R_{\beta}^{F}

contains at least two positive derivatives of
h
. Therefore the principal
part yields

\int F^{\prime}(h)|\partial^{\beta}h|^{2}\,dx\geq\kappa\left\lVert\partial^{\beta}h\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2},

while the remainder is bounded by

C_{K}(1+\left\lVert\operatorname{Log}C\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}^{2})\mathcal{E}_{m}^{\psi}.

The zero-order contribution satisfies

\int F(h)h\,dx=\int h^{2}\int_{0}^{1}F^{\prime}(\theta h)\,d\theta\,dx\geq\kappa\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Finally, the source
\partial^{\beta}(S(u):Y)
is bounded by

C\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{m+1}}+C\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{H^{m}},

and is treated by Young’s inequality.

Summing the velocity, trace-free, and scalar estimates, choosing
\delta>0

small, and absorbing the viscous terms gives
(
10
).
∎

We now specialize this principle to Oldroyd–B. In that case

\psi=\phi=\chi=1
, so
a_{*}=1
,
G(a)=a-1
, and
W=a^{-1}
.
The key refinement of the Oldroyd–B estimate is therefore that the stress
force should not be estimated directly in logarithmic coordinates. Instead write

A=aI+Y,\qquad a=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}A,\qquad Y=A^{\circ}=A-\frac{1}{2}(\operatorname{tr}A)I.

Then
\operatorname{div}(aI)=\nabla a
is absorbed into the pressure and
(
1
)–(
2
) become

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+u\cdot\nabla u-\nu\Delta u+\nabla\pi

\displaystyle=\alpha\operatorname{div}Y,

(11)

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}+u\cdot\nabla)a+\lambda^{-1}(a-1)

\displaystyle=S(u):Y,

(12)

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}+u\cdot\nabla)Y+\lambda^{-1}Y

\displaystyle=2aS(u)+[\nabla u\,Y+Y(\nabla u)^{T}]^{\circ}.

(13)

Here
S(u)=(\nabla u+\nabla u^{T})/2
. The principal coupling in
(
11
) and (
13
) cancels in the energy below.
At derivative order
\beta
, the top-order velocity contribution is

-\alpha\int\partial^{\beta}Y:S(\partial^{\beta}u)\,dx,

while the top-order anisotropic contribution, after testing the
Y
-equation
by
(\alpha/2)a^{-1}\partial^{\beta}Y
, is

\alpha\int\partial^{\beta}Y:S(\partial^{\beta}u)\,dx.

Thus the leading stress–velocity interaction is removed by an exact identity,
not by a smallness assumption. This pressure-free cancellation is the
mechanism which gives the
L^{2}_{t}H^{1+\varepsilon}_{x}
criterion.

Lemma 6.6

(Propagation of Oldroyd–B spectral windows)
.

Let
(u,A)
be a smooth positive-cone solution of
(
1
)–(
2
) on
[0,T)
, and assume
that

\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert\nabla u(t)\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}\,dt<\infty.

If the initial spectrum of
A_{0}
is contained in
[m_{0},M_{0}]
, with

0<m_{0}\leq M_{0}<\infty
, then there are constants

m_{T},M_{T}
, depending only on
m_{0},M_{0},T,\lambda
and

\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert\nabla u(t)\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}\,dt
, such that

0<m_{T}I\leq A(t,x)\leq M_{T}I\qquad\hbox{for all }(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{2}.

In particular, the compact spectral window required by the high-order physical
energy estimate is propagated by the endpoint velocity clock.

Proof.

Let
X(t;x_{0})
be the Lagrangian flow. Along this trajectory write

\mathcal{A}(t)=A(t,X(t;x_{0}))
. Then

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{A}=\nabla u\,\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}(\nabla u)^{T}-\lambda^{-1}(\mathcal{A}-I).

Let
M(t)
and
m(t)
denote the largest and smallest eigenvalues of

\mathcal{A}(t)
. At times of differentiability of these Lipschitz functions,
using a unit eigenvector for the corresponding eigenvalue gives

\dot{M}(t)\leq 2\left\lVert\nabla u(t)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}M(t)+\lambda^{-1},

and

\dot{m}(t)\geq-\bigl(2\left\lVert\nabla u(t)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+\lambda^{-1}\bigr)m(t).

The same inequalities hold for the upper and lower Dini derivatives. Since the
endpoint embedding gives

\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}},

Gronwall’s inequality gives

M(t)\leq\exp\left(2C\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert\nabla u(\tau)\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}\,d\tau\right)\left(M_{0}+\lambda^{-1}t\right),

and

m(t)\geq m_{0}\exp\left(-\lambda^{-1}t-2C\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert\nabla u(\tau)\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}\,d\tau\right).

Taking the infimum over trajectories for the lower bound and the supremum for
the upper bound proves the claim.
∎

Lemma 6.7

(Endpoint transport and physical commutator bounds)
.

Let
m\geq 3
and
0<\varepsilon<1
. For smooth fields on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
and

\operatorname{div}u=0
,

\left|\sum_{|\beta|\leq m}\int\partial^{\beta}f:[\partial^{\beta},u\cdot\nabla]f\,dx\right|\leq C\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}.

Moreover, if
w\in L^{\infty}
, then

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{|\beta|\leq m}\int w\,\partial^{\beta}f:[\partial^{\beta},u\cdot\nabla]f\,dx\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}

\displaystyle+C\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{m+1}}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{H^{m}}.

Moreover, for
|\beta|\leq m
,

\left\lVert[\partial^{\beta},a]\nabla u\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq C\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{m+1}}+C\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{H^{m}},

and the same estimate holds with
a
replaced by any component of
Y
. In
particular,

\displaystyle\left\lVert[\partial^{\beta},Y]\nabla u\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\left\lVert[\partial^{\beta},\nabla u]Y\right\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{m+1}}+C\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{H^{m}}.

The same right-hand side controls

after subtracting any explicitly displayed top-order term.

Proof.

The first bound is the endpoint transport energy estimate of

. For the weighted bound, decompose the
commutator by Bony’s paraproduct. The low-frequency velocity interactions are
estimated by the endpoint modulus

\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}
, with the coefficient

placed in

L^{\infty}
. In the remaining high-frequency velocity interactions, the
top derivative falls on

corresponding derivative of

in
H^{\varepsilon}\hookrightarrow L^{p}
and the
derivative of

in
H^{1}\hookrightarrow L^{q}
, giving

This is the form used below: after Young’s inequality, the last term is
absorbed by the viscous dissipation at the cost of a coefficient proportional
to
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}^{2}
.
For (

), decompose

[\partial^{\beta},a]\nabla u
by Bony’s paraproduct. In the high-low and
balanced pieces the derivative falling on

is placed in

H^{\varepsilon}\hookrightarrow L^{p}
, while the remaining derivative of

is
placed in

The low-high piece is the endpoint paraproduct controlled by

\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{H^{m}}
. The estimates with

are componentwise identical. Expanding

\partial^{\beta}((\nabla u)Y)
or

\partial^{\beta}(Y(\nabla u)^{T})
and removing the chosen top-order term leaves
exactly the same commutator structure.
∎

Proposition 6.8

(Oldroyd–B active-deviatoric endpoint estimate)
.

Let

,
0<\varepsilon<1
, and suppose that

0<c_{0}I\leq A(t,x)\leq C_{0}I

on
[0,T]
. Define

\mathcal{E}_{m}(t)=\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}+\left\lVert a-1\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{4}\sum_{|\beta|\leq m}\int a^{-1}|\partial^{\beta}Y|^{2}\,dx.

Then

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}_{m}

\displaystyle+c\nu\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{m+1}}^{2}+c\lambda^{-1}\bigl(\left\lVert a-1\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}+\alpha\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq C_{c_{0},C_{0}}\left(1+\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}+\left\lVert\operatorname{Log}A\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}^{2}\right)\mathcal{E}_{m}.

Write
D_{t}=\partial_{t}+u\cdot\nabla
. The compact spectral window gives
uniform upper and lower bounds for
a
and
a^{-1}
, so
\mathcal{E}_{m}
is
equivalent to

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}+\left\lVert a-1\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}+\alpha\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}
.

Apply
\partial^{\beta}
,
|\beta|\leq m
, to
(
11
) and test by
\partial^{\beta}u
. Since
\operatorname{div}u=0
,
the transport term is a commutator and the pressure term vanishes. The elastic
term is

\alpha\int\partial^{\beta}u\cdot\partial^{\beta}\operatorname{div}Y\,dx=-\alpha\int\partial^{\beta}Y:S(\partial^{\beta}u)\,dx.

The remaining velocity terms are bounded by

C\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}-\nu\left\lVert\nabla\partial^{\beta}u\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Next differentiate the anisotropic equation (
13

\partial^{\beta}
with the material derivative,

\displaystyle D_{t}\partial^{\beta}Y+\lambda^{-1}\partial^{\beta}Y

\displaystyle=2aS(\partial^{\beta}u)+2[\partial^{\beta},a]S(u)

\displaystyle+\partial^{\beta}\bigl([\nabla u\,Y+Y(\nabla u)^{T}]^{\circ}\bigr)-[\partial^{\beta},u\cdot\nabla]Y.

Test this identity by
(\alpha/2)a^{-1}\partial^{\beta}Y

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{4}\frac{d}{dt}\int a^{-1}|\partial^{\beta}Y|^{2}\,dx+\frac{\alpha}{2\lambda}\int a^{-1}|\partial^{\beta}Y|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle=\alpha\int\partial^{\beta}Y:S(\partial^{\beta}u)\,dx+\mathcal{R}_{\beta}^{a}+\mathcal{R}_{\beta}^{Y}+\mathcal{R}_{\beta}^{tr}+\mathcal{R}_{\beta}^{w},

\mathcal{R}_{\beta}^{Y}

\partial^{\beta}([\nabla u\,Y+Y(\nabla u)^{T}]^{\circ})

\mathcal{R}_{\beta}^{w}=\frac{\alpha}{4}\int D_{t}(a^{-1})|\partial^{\beta}Y|^{2}\,dx

with the elastic term from the velocity equation:

This is the top-order cancellation identity. It occurs after the isotropic
stress has been removed into the pressure and before the use of Young’s
inequality. Consequently the remaining estimates have to control only
commutators, the time derivative of the weight
a^{-1}
, the scalar equation
for

logarithmic coefficient.

We now bound the remainders. For the coefficient commutator,

6.7

\displaystyle|\mathcal{R}_{\beta}^{a}|

\displaystyle\leq C\left\lVert\partial^{\beta}Y\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\left\lVert[\partial^{\beta},a]S(u)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\delta\nu\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{m+1}}^{2}+C_{\delta}\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}^{2}\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}+C\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}\mathcal{E}_{m}.

The same lemma gives

|\mathcal{R}_{\beta}^{Y}|\leq\delta\nu\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{m+1}}^{2}+C_{\delta}\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}^{2}\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}+C\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}\mathcal{E}_{m}.

The transport commutator is controlled by the weighted transport estimate in

|\mathcal{R}_{\beta}^{tr}|\leq\delta\nu\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{m+1}}^{2}+C_{\delta}\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}^{2}\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}+C_{c_{0},C_{0}}\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}.

Finally, from (

Hence, using the compact spectral window and

It remains to estimate the scalar equation. Applying
\partial^{\beta}
to
(

\partial^{\beta}(a-1)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left\lVert a-1\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}+\lambda^{-1}\left\lVert a-1\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}

\displaystyle+C_{\delta}\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}^{2}\left\lVert a-1\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}+C\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}\mathcal{E}_{m}.

On the compact spectral window, the smooth maps

Choose

∎

7
Sharpness of the Generated Logarithmic Threshold

The endpoint theorem removes the compact spectral window as an independent
Oldroyd–B hypothesis and, through Proposition
6.9
,
generates the logarithmic differentiability needed in the high-order estimate.
The generated threshold is nevertheless a real derivative threshold rather than
a disguised entropy bound. Entropy and relaxation control zeroth-order
functions of the eigenvalues of
A
, and the endpoint velocity clock
propagates pointwise spectral bounds, but zero-order spectral information alone
does not control rapid oscillation of the active deviatoric stress. The next
results record this obstruction first through bounded logarithmic amplitudes
and then through a fixed-spectrum eigenframe oscillation, where every spectral
density is exactly constant while the active deviatoric stress oscillates at
high frequency.

Theorem 7.1

(High-frequency entropy-blind sequence)
.

Let
H\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
be nonzero and trace free, and let

B_{N}(x)=\delta\sin(Nx_{1})H,\qquad A_{N}=e^{B_{N}}.

For each fixed
\delta>0
, the fields
A_{N}
remain in a compact spectral
window independent of
N
, and the entropy density

\operatorname{tr}A_{N}-\log\det A_{N}-2

is bounded uniformly in
N
in every
L^{p}
,
1\leq p<\infty
, while

\left\lVert B_{N}\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}\to\infty\qquad\hbox{as }N\to\infty.

Proof.

The spectrum of
B_{N}
is contained in a fixed compact interval depending only
on
\delta
and
H
. Therefore the eigenvalues of
A_{N}=e^{B_{N}}
remain in
a fixed compact subinterval of
(0,\infty)
, and the entropy density is
uniformly bounded. The
H^{1+\varepsilon}
norm grows like

N^{1+\varepsilon}
.
∎

Remark 7.2

(Interpretation)
.

Theorem
7.1
does not construct a solution. It shows that any
argument using only entropy, relaxation, and positivity cannot control the
high-frequency logarithmic term. The continuation proof therefore needs the
flow-map clock, which both propagates the compact window and generates the
logarithmic differentiability used by the high-order estimate.

Proposition 7.3

(Spectral energies are blind to the active high-frequency channel)
.

Let
T:\mathbb{S}_{++}^{2}\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
be a smooth spectral isotropic stress law. Fix
distinct eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\neq\lambda_{2}
in a compact positive-cone
window and assume that the active coefficient in

satisfies
q_{1}(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2},\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2})\neq 0

such that every zero-order spectral density

and

\left\lVert\operatorname{Log}C_{N}\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}+\left\lVert T(C_{N})^{\circ}\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}\to\infty.

Let

\sin\vartheta&\cos\vartheta\end{pmatrix},

. The eigenvalues of

are

,
\det C_{N}=\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}
,

On the other hand,

\left\lVert\operatorname{Log}C_{N}\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}\gtrsim N^{1+\varepsilon}.

has the same frequency

\left\lVert T(C_{N})^{\circ}\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}\gtrsim N^{1+\varepsilon}.

∎

(Exact active-forcing threshold)

Remark 7.6

(Why the physical energy matters)
.

The logarithmic coordinate prevents loss of positive definiteness and gives an
elliptic chart on the cone. Lemma

logarithmic chart does not by itself add smoothing. A purely

naturally produces a fourth power of

8
The finite-extensibility barrier for FENE-P

We now record the part of the argument which is not present in Oldroyd–B. In
the FENE-P system the conformation tensor must remain in

\mathcal{D}_{b}=\{C\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{2}:\operatorname{tr}C<b\}.

The logarithmic conformation
B=\operatorname{Log}C
still controls the lower spectral
boundary of the positive cone, but it does not control the upper trace
boundary. The missing coordinate is

\phi_{b}(C)=-\log(b-\operatorname{tr}C).

The role of this section is to show that this boundary is propagated by the
squared endpoint velocity clock and that the high-order estimate closes in the
same pressure-free active-deviatoric variables.

Definition 8.1

(Compact FENE window)

\delta>0
such that

c_{0}I\leq C(t,x)\leq c_{1}I,\qquad\operatorname{tr}C(t,x)\leq b-\delta

for all
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{T}^{2}
.

Lemma 8.2

(FENE window recovery)
.

Let
C(x)\in\mathcal{D}_{b}
. If

\left\lVert\operatorname{Log}C\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq M,\qquad\left\lVert\phi_{b}(C)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq M_{b},

then

e^{-M}I\leq C\leq e^{M}I,\qquad\operatorname{tr}C\leq b-e^{-M_{b}}.

In particular the logarithmic field and the barrier field recover a compact
FENE window.

Proof.

The eigenvalues of
C
are exponentials of the eigenvalues of
\operatorname{Log}C
,
which gives the spectral bounds. The barrier bound gives

-\log(b-\operatorname{tr}C)\leq M_{b}
, hence
b-\operatorname{tr}C\geq e^{-M_{b}}
.
∎

Lemma 8.3

(Smooth coordinates on compact FENE windows)
.

Let
s>1
. On every compact FENE window
K\Subset\mathcal{D}_{b}
, the quantities

\left\lVert C\right\rVert_{H^{s}},\qquad\left\lVert\operatorname{Log}C\right\rVert_{H^{s}},\qquad\left\lVert T_{b}(C)\right\rVert_{H^{s}}

are mutually controlled, with constants depending on
K,s,b
. Moreover

\left\lVert f_{b}(C)\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\leq C_{K,s,b}\bigl(1+\left\lVert\phi_{b}(C)\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\bigr).

Proof.

The first statement follows from the Sobolev composition theorem on compact
subsets of
\mathcal{D}_{b}
. For the spring factor,

f_{b}(C)=\frac{b-2}{b-\operatorname{tr}C}=(b-2)e^{\phi_{b}(C)}.

The
L^{\infty}
bound supplied by the compact window and the Moser
composition estimate give the displayed inequality.
∎

The FENE-P free energy is

\mathcal{F}_{b}(C)=-(b-2)\log\left(1-\frac{\operatorname{tr}C}{b}\right)-\log\det C.

It contains both boundaries of
\mathcal{D}_{b}
: the logarithmic determinant sees
loss of positive definiteness, while the first term sees the trace boundary.
This zeroth-order information is important, but it is not the same as the
pointwise barrier propagation and derivative control needed for continuation.

Proposition 8.4

(FENE-P free-energy identity)
.

For smooth FENE-P solutions with
C\in\mathcal{D}_{b}
,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left[\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\mathcal{F}_{b}(C)\,dx\right]+\nu\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2\lambda}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\mathcal{D}_{b}^{\rm rel}(C)\,dx=0,

(14)

where
\mathcal{D}_{b}^{\rm rel}(C)\geq 0
is the FENE-P relaxation dissipation.

Proof.

Multiplying the velocity equation by
u
gives the coupling term

-\alpha\int T_{b}(C):\nabla u\,dx
. The variational derivative of

\mathcal{F}_{b}
is
f_{b}(C)I-C^{-1}
. Pairing this derivative with the
conformation equation gives the stretching contribution

2\int T_{b}(C):\nabla u\,dx
. After multiplication by
\alpha/2
, this
cancels the velocity coupling. The remaining relaxation contribution is
nonnegative by convexity of
\mathcal{F}_{b}
on
\mathcal{D}_{b}
.
∎

The new differential structure is the trace-gap equation. Taking the trace of
(
5
) gives

D_{t}\operatorname{tr}C=2C:\nabla u-\lambda^{-1}\bigl(f_{b}(C)\operatorname{tr}C-2\bigr),\qquad D_{t}=\partial_{t}+u\cdot\nabla.

Writing
g=b-\operatorname{tr}C
, we obtain

D_{t}g=-2C:\nabla u+\lambda^{-1}\left(\frac{b-2}{g}\operatorname{tr}C-2\right).

(15)

Equivalently,

D_{t}\phi_{b}=\frac{2C:\nabla u}{g}-\lambda^{-1}\frac{(b-2)\operatorname{tr}C}{g^{2}}+2\lambda^{-1}\frac{1}{g}.

(16)

The negative
g^{-2}
term is the finite-extensibility restoring force. It
propagates the trace gap under a squared endpoint velocity clock.

Lemma 8.5

(Finite-extensibility clock-exponent law)
.

Let
r=\operatorname{tr}C<b
,
C\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{2}
, and suppose that along a smooth
Lagrangian trajectory

D_{t}r=2C:\nabla u-\lambda^{-1}H(r).

Assume that for some
\theta>0
,
c_{0}>0
, and
c_{1}\geq 0
,

H(r)\geq c_{0}(b-r)^{-\theta}-c_{1}

on the trace range reached by the solution. Put

p_{\theta}=\frac{1+\theta}{\theta}.

If
b-r(0,x)\geq\eta_{0}>0
and

\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert\nabla u(t)\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}^{p_{\theta}}\,dt<\infty,

then there exists
\eta>0
, depending only on the displayed quantities and
the initial trace gap, such that

b-r(t,x)\geq\eta\qquad\hbox{on }[0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{2}.

Proof.

Let
\Phi_{g}=-\log(b-r)
,
z=e^{\Phi_{g}}
, and

L(t)=\left\lVert\nabla u(t)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}
. Since
C>0
and
r<b
,

|C:\nabla u|=|C:S(u)|\leq bL(t)
. Therefore

D_{t}\Phi_{g}=\frac{2C:\nabla u-\lambda^{-1}H(r)}{b-r}\leq C(1+L(t))z-cz^{1+\theta}.

The elementary inequality

Az-cz^{1+\theta}\leq C_{\theta,c}(1+A^{p_{\theta}}),\qquad A,z\geq 0,

follows by maximizing the left-hand side in
z
, with the maximum occurring
at scale
z\sim A^{1/\theta}
. Hence

D_{t}\Phi_{g}\leq C\left(1+L(t)^{p_{\theta}}\right)\leq C\left(1+\left\lVert\nabla u(t)\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}^{p_{\theta}}\right).

Integrating along Lagrangian trajectories gives a uniform upper bound for

\Phi_{g}
, which is equivalent to a positive lower bound for
b-r
.
∎

For FENE-P,

H_{b}(r)=f_{b}(r)r-2=\frac{(b-2)r}{b-r}-2=\frac{b(b-2)}{b-r}-b.

Thus
\theta=1
in Lemma
8.5
, and
the algebraic clock exponent is
p_{\theta}=2
.

Remark 8.6

(Algebraic sharpness of the clock exponent)
.

The exponent
p_{\theta}=(1+\theta)/\theta
is sharp for the comparison
inequality used in Lemma
8.5
. If

p<p_{\theta}
, there is no constant
C_{p}
such that

Az-cz^{1+\theta}\leq C_{p}(1+A^{p})\qquad\hbox{for all }A,z\geq 0.

Indeed, maximizing the left-hand side in
z
gives a value comparable to

A^{(1+\theta)/\theta}
. We use this only as an algebraic sharpness
statement for the boundary ODE comparison, not as a claim of dynamical
optimality for all possible continuation criteria.

Lemma 8.7

(Propagation of compact FENE windows)
.

Let
(u,C)
be a smooth FENE-P solution on
[0,T)
, and assume

\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert\nabla u(t)\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}^{2}\,dt<\infty.

If the initial range of
C_{0}
is contained in a compact subset of

\mathcal{D}_{b}
, then
C(t,\cdot)
remains in a compact FENE window on

[0,T)
.

Proof.

Let
L(t)=\left\lVert\nabla u(t)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}
. Since
C>0
and
\operatorname{tr}C<b
,

|C:S(u)|\leq bL(t)
. From (
16
), with

g=e^{-\phi_{b}}
and
\operatorname{tr}C=b-g
, we get

D_{t}\phi_{b}\leq\left(2bL(t)+b\lambda^{-1}\right)e^{\phi_{b}}-b(b-2)\lambda^{-1}e^{2\phi_{b}}.

Completing the square in
e^{\phi_{b}}
gives

D_{t}\phi_{b}\leq C_{b,\lambda}\bigl(1+L(t)^{2}\bigr).

Taking the supremum along Lagrangian trajectories and using

L(t)\leq C\left\lVert\nabla u(t)\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}
propagates a positive lower bound for

b-\operatorname{tr}C
.

The trace bound gives
C\leq bI
. The smallest eigenvalue
m(t)
satisfies
the Dini inequality

\dot{m}(t)\geq-\left(2L(t)+\lambda^{-1}\left\lVert f_{b}(C(t))\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\right)m(t).

The propagated trace gap bounds
f_{b}(C)
in
L^{\infty}
, and

\int_{0}^{T}L(t)\,dt<\infty
follows from the squared clock. Gronwall’s
inequality gives a positive lower spectral bound. Hence
C
stays in a
compact subset of
\mathcal{D}_{b}
.
∎

We now use physical FENE-P variables

C=aI+Y,\qquad a=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}C,\qquad Y=C^{\circ}.

Since

T_{b}(C)=f_{b}(C)C-I=(f_{b}a-1)I+f_{b}Y,

the isotropic part is absorbed into the pressure. With

f=f_{b}(C)=(b-2)/(b-2a)
, the equations become

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+u\cdot\nabla u-\nu\Delta u+\nabla\pi

\displaystyle=\alpha\operatorname{div}(fY),

(17)

\displaystyle D_{t}a+\lambda^{-1}(af-1)

\displaystyle=S(u):Y,

(18)

\displaystyle D_{t}Y+\lambda^{-1}fY

\displaystyle=2aS(u)+[\nabla u\,Y+Y(\nabla u)^{T}]^{\circ}.

(19)

Lemma 8.8

(FENE-P coefficient commutators)
.

Let
K\Subset\mathcal{D}_{b}
,
m\geq 3
, and
0<\varepsilon<1
. On
K
, for

|\beta|\leq m
,

\left\lVert\partial^{\beta}(fY)-f\partial^{\beta}Y\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\left\lVert[\partial^{\beta},f]Y\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq C_{K}\bigl(1+\left\lVert\operatorname{Log}C\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}\bigr)\bigl(\left\lVert a-1\right\rVert_{H^{m}}+\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{H^{m}}\bigr).

Proof.

On
K
,
f=(b-2)/(b-2a)
,
a
, and
Y
are smooth functions of

\operatorname{Log}C
. The Sobolev composition theorem gives

\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}+\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}+\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}\leq C_{K}\bigl(1+\left\lVert\operatorname{Log}C\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}\bigr).

For positive derivatives, the tame composition estimate also gives

\left\lVert\nabla f\right\rVert_{H^{m-1}}\leq C_{K}\bigl(1+\left\lVert\operatorname{Log}C\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}\bigr)\left\lVert a-1\right\rVert_{H^{m}},

because every positive derivative of
f(a)
contains a positive derivative
of
a
, and
\partial^{\gamma}a=\partial^{\gamma}(a-1)
for

|\gamma|>0
.
Expanding

\partial^{\beta}(fY)-f\partial^{\beta}Y=\sum_{0<\gamma\leq\beta}c_{\beta,\gamma}\partial^{\gamma}f\,\partial^{\beta-\gamma}Y

and applying the tame product estimate gives the first bound: one factor
carries the high norm
\left\lVert a-1\right\rVert_{H^{m}}
or
\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{H^{m}}
, while all
remaining factors are placed in
H^{1+\varepsilon}
or
L^{\infty}
. No additive
constant is produced, since for
\beta=0
the left-hand side vanishes and for

|\beta|>0
each term contains a positive derivative. The commutator

[\partial^{\beta},f]Y
has the same expansion.
∎

Proposition 8.9

(Pressure-free FENE-P endpoint estimate)
.

Let
m\geq 3
,
0<\varepsilon<1
, and let
(u,C)
be a smooth FENE-P solution on

[0,T]
whose conformation tensor remains in a compact FENE window
K
.
Set
C=aI+Y
,
a=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}C
,
Y=C^{\circ}
, and
f=f_{b}(C)
. Define

\mathcal{E}_{m}^{F}(t)=\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}+\left\lVert a-1\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{4}\sum_{|\beta|\leq m}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\frac{f}{a}|\partial^{\beta}Y|^{2}\,dx.

Then

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}_{m}^{F}

\displaystyle+c\nu\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{m+1}}^{2}+c\lambda^{-1}\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{K}\left(1+\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}+\left\lVert\operatorname{Log}C\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}^{2}\right)\mathcal{E}_{m}^{F}.

where
C_{K}
depends on the compact FENE window and the fixed parameters.

Proof.

The compact FENE window makes
\mathcal{E}_{m}^{F}
equivalent to

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}+\left\lVert a-1\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}+\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}
. Apply

\partial^{\beta}
,
|\beta|\leq m
, to (
17
) and test
by
\partial^{\beta}u
. The principal elastic contribution is

-\alpha\int f\,\partial^{\beta}Y:S(\partial^{\beta}u)\,dx.

The difference between
\partial^{\beta}(fY)
and
f\partial^{\beta}Y
is
bounded by Lemma
8.8
and Young’s inequality:

\delta\nu\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{m+1}}^{2}+C_{\delta,K}\bigl(1+\left\lVert\operatorname{Log}C\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}^{2}\bigr)\mathcal{E}_{m}^{F}.

Next differentiate (
19
) and test by

(\alpha/2)(f/a)\partial^{\beta}Y
. The term
2aS(\partial^{\beta}u)

produces

\alpha\int f\,\partial^{\beta}Y:S(\partial^{\beta}u)\,dx,

which cancels the principal elastic term from the velocity equation. The
relaxation term contributes

\frac{\alpha}{2\lambda}\int\frac{f^{2}}{a}|\partial^{\beta}Y|^{2}\,dx,

which controls
\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}
on
K
. The time-dependent weight
satisfies

D_{t}\left(\frac{f}{a}\right)=\left(\frac{f}{a}\right)^{\prime}(a)\left(S(u):Y-\lambda^{-1}(af-1)\right),

and hence

\left|D_{t}\left(\frac{f}{a}\right)\right|\leq C_{K}\left(1+\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\right).

The weighted transport commutator is controlled by
Lemma
6.7
, with
w=f/a
. The coefficient,
stretching, and relaxation commutators are controlled by
Lemmas
8.8
and
6.7
.
Their total contribution is bounded by

\delta\nu\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{m+1}}^{2}+C_{\delta,K}\left(1+\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}+\left\lVert\operatorname{Log}C\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}^{2}\right)\mathcal{E}_{m}^{F}.

Finally, apply
\partial^{\beta}
to (
18
). Since

af(a)-1=0\quad\hbox{at }a=1,\qquad\frac{d}{da}(af(a)-1)=\frac{b(b-2)}{(b-2a)^{2}}>0

on
K
, the relaxation part is coercive for
a-1
, up to lower-order
commutators. The source
\partial^{\beta}(S(u):Y)
is estimated by placing the
top derivative of
u
in the viscous dissipation and all remaining factors in

H^{1+\varepsilon}
or
L^{\infty}
. Thus

\frac{d}{dt}\left\lVert a-1\right\rVert_{H^{m}}^{2}\leq\delta\nu\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{m+1}}^{2}+C_{\delta,K}\left(1+\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}+\left\lVert\operatorname{Log}C\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}^{2}\right)\mathcal{E}_{m}^{F}.

Choosing
\delta
sufficiently small and summing over
|\beta|\leq m

proves (
8.9
).
∎

Theorem 8.10

(Unified geometric continuation criterion)
.

Let
s\geq 3
and
0<\varepsilon<1
.

For Oldroyd–B, let
(u,A)
be a strong positive-cone solution on

[0,T)
. If

\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert\nabla u(t)\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}\,dt<\infty,

then
A
automatically remains in a compact spectral window and the strong
solution continues beyond
T
.

For FENE-P, let
(u,C)
be a strong FENE-P solution on
[0,T)
, so that

C(t,x)\in\mathcal{D}_{b}
. If

\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert\nabla u(t)\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}^{2}\,dt<\infty,

then the FENE-P strong solution continues beyond
T
.

Proof.

The Oldroyd–B statement is Theorem
6.10
. For FENE-P, the
squared endpoint velocity clock propagates a compact FENE window by
Lemma
8.7
. Since
T<\infty
, this squared
clock also gives
\nabla u\in L^{1}(0,T;B^{0}_{\infty,1})
. On the compact FENE window,
Proposition
6.9
gives

\operatorname{Log}C\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1+\varepsilon}).

Proposition
8.9
gives

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}_{s}^{F}\leq C_{K}\left(1+\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}+\left\lVert\operatorname{Log}C\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}^{2}\right)\mathcal{E}_{s}^{F}.

The coefficient is integrable by the squared velocity clock, the finite time
interval, and the generated logarithmic bound. Gronwall’s inequality gives a uniform
H^{s}
bound for

(u,a-1,Y)
. On the compact FENE window, this is equivalent to an
H^{s}

bound for
C
and
T_{b}(C)
. Proposition
2.5

then restarts the solution and extends it beyond
T
.
∎

Remark 8.11

(On time exponents and optimality)
.

The Oldroyd–B part of Theorem
8.10
improves the
purely logarithmic energy exponent from
L^{4}_{t}H^{1+\varepsilon}_{x}
to an internally
generated
L^{2}_{t}H^{1+\varepsilon}_{x}
logarithmic channel. This improvement is tied to the pressure-free
physical unknown
A=aI+Y
and to the cancellation between the elastic force

\alpha\operatorname{div}Y
and the stretching term
2aS(u)
. The statement should not
be read as a converse blow-up theorem: the criteria are sufficient conditions
for continuation and identify the only channels not controlled by the available
energy, cone, and barrier structures. Thus divergence of one listed quantity
is necessary for breakdown within this framework, but it is not asserted to be
sufficient for singularity formation.

For FENE-P the same cancellation survives after the pressure-free splitting

T_{b}(C)=(f_{b}a-1)I+f_{b}Y
. The weight
f_{b}/a
cancels the principal
interaction between
\alpha\operatorname{div}(f_{b}Y)
and
2aS(u)
, while the
finite-extensibility restoring force propagates the trace gap under the
squared endpoint velocity clock. Thus the FENE-P endpoint criterion requires

\nabla u\in L^{2}_{t}B^{0}_{\infty,1}
, while the logarithmic stress
integrability needed in the high-order estimate is generated on the propagated
compact FENE window.

Corollary 8.12

(Endpoint breakdown alternatives)
.

At the first breakdown time of a smooth stress-diffusion-free solution, the
Oldroyd–B model can fail only through the endpoint flow-map channel:

\int_{0}^{T_{*}}\left\lVert\nabla u(t)\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}\,dt=\infty.

For FENE-P, finite-time breakdown forces loss of the squared endpoint velocity
clock:

\int_{0}^{T_{*}}\left\lVert\nabla u(t)\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}^{2}\,dt=\infty.

In particular, positive-cone loss, trace-gap collapse, and logarithmic
high-frequency concentration cannot occur while the relevant velocity clock
stays finite.

Proof.

If none of the listed quantities diverges, then the relevant continuation
criterion in Theorem
8.10
applies and extends the
solution beyond
T_{*}
, a contradiction.
∎

9
Hookean limit and relation between the two criteria

The FENE-P criterion reduces to the Oldroyd–B criterion only in a controlled
limit. Suppose
0<C\leq MI
and
\operatorname{tr}C\leq M
, with
M
independent of

b
. Then

f_{b}(C)=\frac{b-2}{b-\operatorname{tr}C}=1+\frac{\operatorname{tr}C-2}{b}+O_{M}(b^{-2}),

and hence

T_{b}(C)=C-I+\frac{\operatorname{tr}C-2}{b}C+O_{M}(b^{-2}).

Moreover

\phi_{b}(C)=-\log b+O_{M}(b^{-1}),\qquad\nabla\phi_{b}(C)=\frac{\nabla\operatorname{tr}C}{b-\operatorname{tr}C}=O_{M}(b^{-1})\nabla C.

Thus, on bounded trace windows independent of
b
, the FENE-P physical
criterion formally reduces to the Oldroyd–B positive-cone criterion. At fixed

b
, or along sequences for which
\operatorname{tr}C
approaches
b
, this reduction
is invalid at the level of pointwise barrier control. The endpoint theorem
therefore propagates the trace gap before applying the pressure-free high-order
estimate.

Proposition 9.1

(Entropy does not control trace-gap oscillation)
.

Fix
b>2
. There are smooth diagonal fields
C_{N}:\mathbb{T}^{2}\to\mathcal{D}_{b}
whose
eigenvalues stay in a fixed positive interval and whose positive-cone entropy

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\bigl(\operatorname{tr}C_{N}-\log\det C_{N}\bigr)\,dx

is uniformly bounded, while

\left\lVert\phi_{b}(C_{N})\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}\to\infty.

Proof.

Choose

\theta_{N}(x)=\theta_{0}+a_{N}\sin(Nx_{1}),

with
0<\theta_{0}<b
,
a_{N}\to 0
, and

a_{N}N^{1+\varepsilon}\to\infty
. Let

C_{N}(x)=\frac{\theta_{N}(x)}{2}\,I.

For
N
large, the eigenvalues remain in a fixed positive interval and the
positive-cone entropy is uniformly bounded. However

\phi_{b}(C_{N})=-\log(b-\theta_{N}),

whose
H^{1+\varepsilon}
norm grows like
a_{N}N^{1+\varepsilon}
. Thus positive-cone
entropy does not control high-frequency oscillation of the trace-gap barrier.
The FENE-P endpoint criterion above avoids assuming this barrier norm directly;
it uses the pointwise barrier equation to recover the compact window and the
pressure-free physical variables to close the derivative estimate.
∎

10
Conclusion

This paper identifies the pressure-free channel that controls continuation for
two-dimensional stress-diffusion-free viscoelastic models. For every smooth
spectral isotropic stress, the isotropic part is a pressure mode and the active
part is
q_{1}Y
, where
C=aI+Y
. Testing the trace-free equation with the
weight
q_{1}/a
cancels the principal velocity–stress interaction and gives
the compact-window endpoint coefficient

1+\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{B^{0}_{\infty,1}}+\left\lVert\operatorname{Log}C\right\rVert_{H^{1+\varepsilon}}^{2}.

The model consequences are sharp in their geometric clocks. For Oldroyd–B,
the endpoint velocity clock propagates the positive-cone window and generates
the logarithmic
L^{2}_{t}H^{1+\varepsilon}_{x}
channel, giving the pure criterion

\nabla u\in L^{1}_{t}B^{0}_{\infty,1}
. For FENE-P, the same cancellation
survives after the splitting
T_{b}(C)=(f_{b}a-1)I+f_{b}Y
, while the trace-gap
barrier requires the squared clock

\nabla u\in L^{2}_{t}B^{0}_{\infty,1}
.

The obstruction results mark the boundary of this mechanism. Entropy,
relaxation, and fixed spectral densities do not control high-frequency
rotation of the active deviatoric stress. The endpoint theory therefore gives
both a closed continuation loop and a precise reason why a purely spectral or
entropy-based argument cannot replace the generated logarithmic threshold.
