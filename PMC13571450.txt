Rationale and Design of Traditional and Adaptive Calorie Restriction and Time-Restricted Eating Interventions Used in the Dietary Approaches to Longevity and Health (DiAL Health) Pilot Trial
Authors: Samantha F Kennedy, Hannah E Cabre, Hanim E Diktas, Kaja Falkenhain, Katherine E Spring, Courtney M Peterson, Gareth R Dutton, Kimberly L Drews, Stephanie T Broyles, Robert Dubin, Laura Q Rogers, Catherine M Champagne, Dennis T Villareal, Daniel S Hsia, Peter G Jacobs, Jack Rejeski, Jason Fanning, Leanne M Redman, Corby K Martin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundResearch shows that dietary interventions improve biomarkers of health span and aging.ObjectiveThis paper describes the implementation and data collection methods for 4 dietary interventions and a control condition comprising the 5 arms of the Dietary Approaches to Longevity and Health (DiAL Health) Pilot study.MethodsDiAL Health is a 24-wk, randomized controlled pilot trial comparing the feasibility and preliminary efficacy of traditional and adaptive approaches to calorie restriction (CR) and time-restricted eating (TRE) on biomarkers of biological aging and health span in young, healthy adults [age: 25–49 y, body mass index (BMI): 22.0–29.9 kg/m2]. To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to examine the effects of both CR and TRE against a usual diet and will generate preliminary data to inform a larger, longer-term comparative trial. The 2 CR arms of DiAL Health target 25% CR, whereas the 2 TRE arms promote ad libitum food intake within an 8-h daily window. Traditional CR and TRE arms will receive established interventions. The corresponding Adaptive arms will receive Just-in-Time Adaptive Interventions that leverage mobile health technologies to adjust behavior and facilitate adherence with real-time or near-real-time feedback and support. Using a validated mathematical model, weight loss nomograms with measurements from internet-connected smart scales will be used to monitor dietary adherence in the Adaptive CR arm. Feedograms with eating events populated by continuous glucose monitoring (CGM), the Remote Food Photography Method/SmartIntake app, and manual entry by counselors and participants will be used to monitor meal timing adherence in the Adaptive TRE arm. Integration of CGM will support passive, automated detection of meal timing and eating behavior, potentially reducing reliance on self-reported measures.ConclusionsFindings from the DiAL Health Pilot study will inform the design and implementation of a future 5-y clinical trial evaluating personalized dietary strategies to promote longevity and improve health span.This trial was registered at clinicaltrials.gov as NCT05549362.

Introduction

Nutritional interventions are among the most promising non-pharmacological strategies for mitigating the adverse effects of an obesogenic environment on human health span, defined as the portion of life characterized by overall good health and freedom from chronic age-related disease or disability [1]. Although dietary approaches such as calorie restriction (CR) have demonstrated efficacy in improving biomarkers of health span and biological aging in clinical trials [[2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]], adherence to restrictive diets is challenging to maintain for extended periods [2]. Time-restricted eating (TRE), which promotes a daily fasting period of ≥14 h, is an alternative dietary strategy that may provide similar benefits to CR while being easier to follow [[9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14]].

Just-in-Time Adaptive Interventions (JITAIs) are a tailored approach to delivering the right type and amount of support during behavior change, precisely when participants are most receptive or at risk of relapse [15]. These interventions dynamically adapt to changes in internal and external states, making them particularly well-suited for CR interventions that require more intensive support [16]. The personalization and responsiveness of JITAIs can also be enhanced by integrating mobile health (mHealth) technologies, including smartphones, smart scales, wearable trackers, and other internet-connected devices that generate real-time data streams (Figure 1) [17,18]. The Dietary Approaches to Longevity and Health (DiAL Health) Pilot trial aimed to 1) evaluate feasibility and 2) assess the preliminary efficacy of 2 CR and 2 TRE interventions to modulate health span and biomarkers of aging. Feasibility scores are based on self-reported satisfaction, attrition rates relative to the control group, and diet adherence. Preliminary efficacy measures include change in biological age, calculated using Klemera-Doubal’s method [23], and change in health span, assessed using metabolic syndrome score [24,25]. This paper reports the intervention methodology of the DiAL Health Pilot study with particular emphasis on delivery strategies, data collection procedures, and adherence quantification metrics.

Methods

DiAL Health is a 24-wk, 5-arm, parallel-group, randomized controlled pilot trial designed to evaluate and compare the feasibility and preliminary efficacy of CR and TRE interventions delivered through both adaptive and traditional approaches in young, healthy adults. In this issue, a companion protocol manuscript by Redman et al. [26] is also published and provides a comprehensive description of DiAL Health’s trial design, enrollment criteria, and study procedures. In brief, participants will be randomly assigned into 1 of 4 intervention groups shown in Table 1 (i.e., Traditional CR, Adaptive CR, Traditional TRE, and Adaptive TRE) or to an ad libitum control group with no restrictions on food intake or meal timing. Participants randomly assigned to a CR diet will target a 25% reduction in caloric intake, while those randomly assigned to TRE will be counseled to eat within a designated 8-h window. Administrative procedures ensuring counselor preparation and oversight will be implemented to maintain intervention fidelity and internal validity across treatment arms and study sites. Counselors responsible for delivering dietary interventions will undergo training led by 1 Principal Investigator (CKM) and 3 Co-Investigators (GRD, CC, and CMP) prior to study initiation. Regularly scheduled case conferences (e.g., weekly or biweekly) involving intervention teams at Pennington Biomedical Research Center and University of Alabama at Birmingham will also provide ongoing counselor supervision and serve as a forum for discussing cases that require additional guidance.

To minimize attrition, both sites will implement a proactive retention program in which counselors intervene early at the first indication of participant disengagement. Counselors will initiate this program by calling the participant to identify and address potential barriers to attending visits. This step is followed by phone and e-mail outreach from project managers and principal investigators as needed. If the participant remains unreachable, the team will contact the designated emergency contact, and, in rare cases where all prior attempts fail, a certified letter will be sent as a final effort to reestablish communication. All study staff will be trained to recognize early warning signs of disengagement and refer participants promptly. These procedures will be supported by broader engagement strategies, including regular newsletters, monthly check-ins by phone or text, and birthday cards to maintain ongoing connection. Thorough screening procedures will ensure that participants understand the demands of the study from the outset, and they will also be eligible for ≤$500 in prorated compensation upon completion of major study visits at baseline, week 12, and week 24.

All participants enrolled in the DiAL Health study will receive a daily multivitamin and mineral supplement containing ∼1000 IU of vitamin D, along with an oyster shell calcium supplement with 500 mg of elemental calcium (Nature Made Nutritional Products) to ensure nutritional adequacy while undergoing dietary treatment. Counselors will provide ongoing diet-specific nutritional guidance and, at baseline, encourage participants to engage in moderate-intensity physical activity consistent with public health guidelines (e.g., brisk walking for ∼150 min/wk and strength training 2 d/wk) [32].

CR interventions

Traditional CR

The Traditional CR arm of DiAL Health is modeled after the Comprehensive Assessment of Long-Term Effects of Reducing Intake of Energy Phase II (CALERIE 2) trial, which demonstrated that sustained reductions in daily energy intake can improve key biomarkers associated with slowed biological aging and enhanced health span [2,33]. Traditional CR participants will be prescribed a diet corresponding to a 25% reduction in their energy intake, derived from baseline energy requirements for weight maintenance measured using the doubly labeled water method. This diet will begin on day one of the interventions, with no gradual increase in CR, and will be supported by nutritional and behavioral counseling. A structured meal plan featuring 3 meals per day and preplanned snacks will also be provided. Initially, meals will follow a macronutrient distribution of ∼55% carbohydrates, ∼15% proteins, and ∼30% fats, emphasizing lean protein sources and whole grains [34]. As participants progress through the curriculum, they will learn how to adjust this formula to meet their individual needs and mitigate hunger-driven eating behaviors. Additionally, the provided meal plans will include several portion-controlled foods (e.g., frozen entrees, prepackaged single-serving packets of oatmeal, nutrition shakes, fruit) that will be relied upon heavily earlier in the intervention when participants are learning about food preparation and portioning (see Supplemental Material 1 for an example meal plan adhering to a 2000 kcal/d diet). Consistent with CALERIE 2 [2,33], moderate alcohol consumption will be permitted, but the energy (kilocalories) from habitual intake of alcohol will be included in the energy intake target of participants’ meal plans.

The nutrition and behavior change curriculum for CR participants is adapted from CALERIE 2 [2,28], with minor updates to improve health literacy [35,36] and align with the current clinical guidelines for adult weight management [37]. Diet and body weight will be systematically monitored over the 24-wk CR intervention. Consistent with the study protocol (see Redman et al. [26]), all participants will record their food intake for ≥5 consecutive days at 3 time points (baseline, week 12, and week 24) using their choice of paper logs or a commercially available mobile application (e.g., MyFitnessPal), with flexibility in tracking methods encouraged to support sustained adherence. Weekly weights will be obtained by trained clinic staff during in-person counseling sessions, whereas for remote sessions, participants will provide photographic documentation of their scale readings. To ensure accurate and standardized self-monitoring of body weight, Traditional CR participants will be provided with a non-internet-connected home scale at the start of the intervention.

Counselors will manually upload weight data to the Companion App, where they will be plotted on personalized weight loss nomograms (Figure 2). CR adherence will be monitored using nomograms (i.e., weight graphs) by comparing participants’ weekly weights against their expected weight loss trajectories. When deviations occur, the counselor will initiate targeted strategies from the Adherence Toolbox (see Supplemental Table 1 in Supplemental Material 2 for example strategies and “The toolbox approach” section below for a detailed description). Adaptive CR

The Adaptive CR arm of DiAL Health is the JITAI adaptation of Traditional CR. Using the same diet restriction, validated energy balance models [[38], [39], [40], [41]], weight loss nomograms [19,21], counseling session schedule, curriculum, and adherence metrics, the Adaptive CR arm is distinguished only by its integration of mHealth technology to remotely and objectively assess behavior in real time. Following JITAI principles [15,22], these data will be used to identify the current needs of individual participants and systematically personalize toolbox strategy options to address those needs. Importantly, because the adaptive approach enables real-time detection of adherence challenges, participants may also be contacted outside of their scheduled counseling sessions, providing additional timely counselor outreach that is not available in the traditional approach.

Using nomograms generated in the Companion App and a provided BodyTrace smart scale, counselors and Adaptive CR participants will have daily, objective information on participants’ adherence to the CR goal. The Companion App will generate automated alerts when adherence thresholds determined by the weight loss nomogram (red dotted lines in Figure 2) are exceeded. Once alerted, the counselor and participant will work together to select a strategy from the Adherence Toolbox (see Supplemental Table 1 in Supplemental Material 2 for example strategies and “The toolbox approach” section below for a detailed description) that is appropriate for the type and severity of nonadherence observed. Intervention strategies included in this toolbox menu are categorized as either “elective,” which may be used at the discretion of the counselor or participant, or “mandatory,” which will be required when adherence goals are not met and elective strategies prove insufficient. The effectiveness of selected options will be evaluated after a prespecified period, typically 2 to 3 weeks, with more intensive strategies implemented if adherence issues persist.

The Remote Food Photography Method (RFPM) and the SmartIntake smartphone application [42,43] will be used to capture daily dietary intake and characterize eating behaviors in participants assigned to the Adaptive CR intervention. As in the Traditional CR group, dietary intake will be systematically assessed over ≥5 consecutive days at 3 predefined time points (baseline, week 12, and week 24). The SmartIntake application also incorporates Ecological Momentary Assessments [44], which prompt participants in real time to report contextual factors surrounding each eating event.

The Companion App

The Companion App is a secure, US Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)-compliant mHealth platform accessible via both a website and smartphone interface. Developed by Wake Forest University, the app will serve as the central hub for CR intervention delivery [19]. By generating personalized weight loss nomograms, the app will function as an online weight-tracking system to support remote monitoring of dietary adherence; guide the frequency and intensity of treatment support [45,46]; and standardize CR intervention delivery across sites, counselors, and participants [2,[19], [20], [21],28,36]. Administrative access to data stored within the app will also offer a platform for multisite research teams to monitor the study’s overall progress and address systemic challenges collaboratively.

The app’s user experience will be tailored according to group assignment. Participants in the Adaptive CR arm will receive login credentials granting access to their personalized nomogram and a secure messaging portal for between-session communication with counselors. Consistent with the JITAI framework, Adaptive CR participants also receive a BodyTrace scale for daily home weighing. These internet-connected smart devices will automatically transmit weight data to the app and populate the nomograms in real time. In contrast, app access will be restricted for participants in the Traditional CR arm. Although nomograms will be generated for these participants, weights will be entered manually by counselors based on self-reported values or measurements obtained during in-person visits. Updated graphs will then be shared during counseling sessions.

Weight loss nomograms for CR interventions

Weight loss and adherence to CR prescription will be monitored using personalized weight loss nomograms (Figure 2). Each nomogram is generated from individual participant data using a validated mathematical model of energy balance that predicts weight change in response to sustained reductions in calorie intake [19,21,38,40,41]. This weight graphing approach was first implemented in CALERIE 2 [28,29], where it successfully promoted 11.7% CR and 10.4% total body weight loss over 2 y [2]. Since then, more advanced energy balance models have been independently developed and validated [38,40,41,47]. The model used in the present study is one of these validated models and has also been successfully integrated into SmartLoss [19,21], an effective mHealth-based JITAI.

After randomization, CR participants will receive a calorie prescription equal to 75% of their baseline energy requirements. When projected over the 24-wk study period, the nomogram generates a target weight loss trajectory (blue dotted line in Figure 2) and a corresponding “zone of adherence” defined by upper and lower error bounds (red dotted lines in Figure 2). During the first 2 wk, the upper and lower bounds are plotted at +1.3 kg and −1.8 kg, respectively, relative to the weight loss trajectory. These bounds adjust to +1.1 kg and −1.8 kg at 3 mo and expand to +1.7 kg and −2.8 kg at 6 mo [38].

The Toolbox approach

The toolbox approach has successfully been used to address suboptimal adherence in prior behavior-based clinical trials [19,21,28,48,49]. Separate from the preprogrammed counseling session topics, the toolbox provides participants and counselors with a process for selectively initiating evidence-based strategies from a menu in response to each participant’s current needs and challenges. The toolbox approach is grounded in several well-established behavior change theories that shape how the program guides users toward healthier habits [21]. The toolbox aligns with the theory of planned behavior and the theory of reasoned action [50] by helping users form intentions to change through clearly defined goals, supportive messaging, and personalized information that strengthens their sense of control and confidence in their ability to succeed. Principles from social cognitive theory [51] further support the toolbox design by emphasizing self-monitoring, reinforcement, and the development of personal agency, all of which encourage individuals to take an active, confident role in managing their weight.

In the DiAL Health Pilot study, 4 prespecified toolboxes will be used that directly correspond to common obstacles in CR interventions: attendance, adherence, self-monitoring, and behavioral/psychosocial challenges. Each toolbox outlines clear criteria for activation and provides both elective and mandatory strategies to address issues within that category (e.g., motivational interviewing, structured meal plans, behavioral agreements, and alternative self-monitoring methods). Using a guided problem-solving workflow, participants and counselors collaboratively identify the behavior impeding progress (e.g., dietary adherence), select a strategy to address it, and test the chosen solution for 2 to 4 wk. If the problem is resolved, the strategy is continued until adherence is restored. Ineffective strategies are discontinued and replaced with more intensive or directive options (see Supplemental Material 3 for a diagram of this workflow). The variety of prespecified options ensures that counselors remain responsive to fluctuations in individual motivations and barriers to behavior change while promoting intervention fidelity. The result is an approach that creates a less prescriptive and more personalized participant experience while remaining empirically grounded [48,49].

Effective and timely identification of lapses in dietary adherence is critical for achieving and sustaining weight loss in CR interventions, making the Adherence Toolbox an essential component of the DiAL Health Pilot study. The toolbox introduces an intuitive stoplight system that translates complex self-regulation processes into clear, actionable items when nonadherence is detected. Within this framework, participants’ daily weight data determine their status as green, yellow, or red, each reflecting a distinct pattern of dietary adherence and prompting an appropriate intervention response. A green status indicates that a participant’s daily weights fall within the defined adherence zone, signaling that weight loss is progressing as expected following the CR prescription. A yellow status reflects emerging challenges or early recovery from relapse and is triggered when weights fall within 1 lb (0.45 kg) of the upper bound, when reported weights reach a plateau, or when weights exceed the upper limit of the adherence zone but are decreasing at a rate of ≥0.5 lb/wk (0.23 kg/wk). Appropriate mandatory strategies for participants with yellow status include increased counselor contact, targeted reduction in energy-dense foods, or adjustments to caloric distribution across meals to address hunger and reduce unplanned snacking. In contrast, red status denotes significant nonadherence. Participants fall into this category when plotted weights fall outside the adherence zone and are either stable or declining at a rate of ≤0.5 lb/wk (0.23 kg/wk). Red status necessitates more intensive intervention, including structured meal replacements or prescribed portion-controlled foods to restore momentum toward the weight loss trajectory. Embedding this stoplight system within the Adherence Toolbox will allow counselors to quickly identify adherence lapses and deploy appropriate, scalable strategies that match the severity of deviation from expected weight loss. When paired with the BodyTrace scales in the Adaptive CR group, this structured yet flexible monitoring approach aligns with JITAI principles, enabling adaptive, timely support that helps participants course-correct before small lapses develop into extended periods of nonadherence.

Quantifying CR adherence

Percent CR adherence will be quantified and reported as the percentage reduction in energy intake during the intervention relative to baseline intake (i.e., total energy expenditure measured under weight-stable conditions). Energy intake during the intervention will be estimated from an end-of-study measure of energy expenditure adjusted for changes in body composition. Fat mass and fat-free mass changes will be converted to energy equivalents using coefficients of 9.3 kcal/g and 1.1 kcal/g, respectively. TRE interventions

Traditional TRE

Participants assigned to the Traditional TRE group will be instructed to consume all food within an 8-h window early in the day and fast for the remaining 16 h. To accommodate individual sleep schedules, participants self-select a consistent start time for their eating window between 6:00 and 10:00, ensuring that the first meal occurs no later than 4 h after waking. Participants will be encouraged to maintain a regular start time within a 30-min range to support circadian synchrony. The intervention emphasizes early TRE to leverage the likely metabolic benefits of prolonged daily fasting and alignment with circadian rhythms.

Participants will be instructed to adhere to their eating window ≥6 d/wk, corresponding to a mean adherence rate of 86% over the 24-wk intervention. Aside from the following restrictions, counselors will not provide guidance regarding the quantity or type of foods consumed. During fasting periods, participants may only drink water or other noncaloric beverages. Caffeinated beverages should be avoided after 14:00 to minimize disruption of circadian rhythms. Moderate alcohol consumption will be permitted during the eating window or on designated break days.

Participants randomly assigned to the Traditional TRE group will meet with their counselors in person biweekly during their first month of treatment. Subsequent sessions will occur monthly, with the option to attend in person or virtually. All 7 of these meetings will focus on adherence to the prescribed 8-h eating window, with the second session also covering social support. This topic was included because TRE can create unique social challenges, particularly when eating occasions with friends or family occur outside the prescribed eating window. Counseling will therefore provide strategies for navigating social situations while maintaining adherence to the intervention. If a participant’s adherence falls below a mean of 5 d/wk for over 2 wk, additional counseling sessions will be scheduled as frequently as every 2 wk. These supplemental sessions will also be conducted remotely via secure videoconference or phone.

Adherence to the Traditional TRE protocol will be monitored daily through participant self-report using a brief, automated survey administered via Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) [30,31]. Each evening, after their final meal, or the next morning, participants will complete the survey at home, reporting the times they began and ended eating that day, whether they successfully adhered to the 8-h eating window, and any reasons for nonadherence.

Adaptive TRE

The Adaptive TRE intervention is designed to mirror the Traditional TRE eating window duration, adherence targets, counseling schedule, curriculum, and adherence metrics. In contrast to the Traditional TRE approach, the Adaptive TRE intervention will leverage mHealth technology to objectively and remotely assess adherence in near real time. Participants’ daily eating window will be continuously monitored via the feedogram on the meal-timing website (Figure 3), which is accessible on any device with internet service (e.g., computer, smartphone, tablet). This JITAI approach will enable personalized treatment adjustments based on individual behavioral trends. The feedogram will be populated using 4 data sources. First, real-time, date-and time-stamped meal images submitted via the SmartIntake app will automatically plot eating events on the feedogram. Second, eating occasions will be passively inferred from elevated glucose peaks detected in continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) data and auto-populated as eating events on the feedogram. Third, participants may manually enter eating events on the meal-timing website or within the SmartIntake platform when photographs or automated capture is not feasible. Finally, study counselors may add or correct eating events during participant interactions to ensure completeness and accuracy. To manage participant burden, CGM data in the Adaptive TRE group will be captured for 10 d every 1.5 mo. During each CGM collection period, participants will be asked to use SmartIntake to record all eating events for ≥5 of those 10 d. Outside these data collection periods, participants may continue using SmartIntake or manually enter eating events via the self-report feature on the meal-timing website. Together, these complementary data streams provide a comprehensive record of diet and eating behavior.

Analogous to the weight nomogram used in the CR group, the feedogram will be accessible on the meal-timing website, allowing counselors and participants to view a 24-h timeline of eating events relative to each participant’s prescribed eating window (Figure 3). This visualization will help identify patterns in adherence, such as consistent late eating on weekends, which can inform targeted, data-driven intervention strategies. For example, if social events frequently lead to late eating, the counselor may initially recommend strategies to enhance social support. Should adherence not improve within 2 or 3 wk, more intensive strategy options will be deployed.

Meal-timing feedograms for the Adaptive TRE intervention

Participants randomly assigned to the Adaptive TRE intervention will log their daily food intake on a 24-h timeline in a digital feedogram (Figure 3, bottom). Although these visualization tools effectively track and summarize when eating occurs [11], they have traditionally relied solely on self-reported data that is susceptible to misreporting. To overcome this limitation in DiAL Health, feedograms will be hosted on a secure web-based platform (mealtiming.pbrc.edu) that supports both manual entry of eating events by participants and counselors, as well as automated input from CGM and the RFPM/SmartIntake app. Integrating computerized methods to automate data capture and populate the feedogram addresses a major shortcoming of previous TRE research and positions these technologies as promising tools for improving the fidelity and effectiveness of dietary interventions. Nonetheless, reports of these technologies being used in TRE studies are minimal; thus, their feasibility will be evaluated in DiAL Health.

Adherence to the TRE protocol will be visually evident in the feedogram, as indicated by the colors of plotted eating events. If a participant begins eating within 30 min before their scheduled start time, the event will be flagged as yellow, and the window’s end time will be adjusted accordingly. For example, if a participant’s eating window is scheduled from 6:00 to 14:00, and an eating event was captured at 05:45, then the event would be documented on their feedogram in yellow, and the end time of their window would be adjusted to 13:45. If eating begins >30 min early, the event will be marked red (i.e., nonadherent), and the window will be adjusted to begin at the time of the first eating event or at 05:30, whichever falls later. Eating events that began within 30 min after the scheduled start time are marked green, and the window will extend 8 h from that logged event. If eating begins >30 min late, the event will still be marked green if the last recorded eating event for that day does not extend 30 min past the scheduled end time. However, any first meal occurring after 10:00 will automatically be considered nonadherent and marked red. These flags will be visible to Adaptive TRE participants and counselors via the feedogram on the meal-timing website. Counselors will monitor the color status of eating events throughout the week and will reach out to participants to help them address nonadherence, which is indicated on the feedogram by meals logged as red. During these unscheduled contacts, counselors will gather contextual information about the nonadherent eating event, explore barriers that may have contributed to the lapse, and work with participants to identify strategies that support future adherence.

CGM

CGM devices are wearable sensors that measure changes in interstitial glucose concentrations. When plotted over time, the location and amplitude of glucose concentration peaks reflect the timing and magnitude of food intake. Deployment of the Dexcom G6 Commercial CGM System in DiAL Health will be paired with a web-based artificial pancreas monitoring system (iPancreas; Oregon Health & Science University) initially designed for the management of type 1 diabetes [[53], [54], [55]]. This integration will support near real-time acquisition and processing of CGM data to ultimately populate the feedogram on the meal-timing website.

To further delineate this data collection process, CGM devices coupled with a proprietary smartphone application will first capture glucose readings and then wirelessly transmit them to iPancreas software monitoring system. The smartphone application serves primarily as a data transmission tool and, when opened, can only be used to verify the status of the CGM device (i.e., active/inactive and battery level). The application does not display glucose data or provide any feedback about glucose concentrations; therefore, participants will be asked to minimize their interaction with it. Once the data is transmitted to iPancreas, an algorithm developed and validated by Jacobs and Mosquera-Lopez subsequently identifies carbohydrate consumption based on patterns in glucose fluctuations. This algorithm previously had ∼80% accuracy [53,54], though those data were derived from people with type 1 diabetes. Detected glucose peaks will be identified as actual eating events when both the immediate and previously calculated (i.e., within the 6 min prior) probabilities ≥0.86 and no CGM values fall below 70 mg/dL in the preceding 30 min. These data will be uploaded to a secure server, where they will be filtered using Boolean indicators to distinguish valid from invalid eating events. Final timestamps will be displayed and color-coded on the feedogram without further modification.

RFPM

RFPM is a validated [42,43] approach to assess dietary intake in free-living conditions. To complete the assessment, participants photograph their plated and leftover food at each eating or drinking event using a mobile device and the SmartIntake app. SmartIntake guides users through image capture to ensure consistency and completeness, and then securely transfers the photos to a server where trained analysts estimate energy and nutrient intake. This approach improves the precision and reliability of dietary data collection, thereby informing adaptive intervention strategies by delivering insights into food selection and eating behavior. More importantly for the Adaptive TRE group, the SmartIntake app also contributes to the feedogram by providing time-stamped records of submitted eating events. In doing so, RFPM/SmartIntake will complement CGM data by offering a detailed record of the foods consumed at the times reported by CGM. Together, these tools function as a remote dietary monitoring system that is both scalable and minimally burdensome to participants.

Ad libitum control

The control group in the DiAL Health study serves as a comparator for the CR and TRE groups. Participants randomly assigned to this group will continue their habitual eating patterns without any imposed dietary restrictions or guidance regarding meal timing, frequency, or composition. All participants will undergo identical screening and baseline assessments prior to randomization. After randomization, those assigned to the control group will be instructed to maintain their habitual diet entirely ad libitum, with no additional counseling or guidance regarding what or when to eat. Body weight in this group is expected to fluctuate naturally over time. Control participants will maintain regular contact with study investigators and will complete all outcome assessments according to the same schedule as participants in the CR group.

Discussion

This paper presents a comprehensive overview of the design and methodology employed in the DiAL Health Pilot study, which evaluates the feasibility and preliminary efficacy of dietary interventions incorporating both traditional and adaptive approaches to CR and TRE. By detailing the structure and implementation of all 4 intervention arms, this work offers valuable insights for researchers and clinicians seeking to develop or refine strategies that promote participant longevity. The design principles and operational considerations outlined herein may inform the planning and execution of future iterations of the DiAL Health study, as well as guide broader efforts to translate dietary interventions into sustainable, real-world applications.

One objective of this pilot trial is to characterize the feasibility of CR and TRE interventions relative to a usual diet, with particular emphasis on adherence sustainability. We expect that both dietary strategies, when implemented with adaptive support, will demonstrate acceptable and clinically meaningful adherence, thereby supporting their feasibility for long-term evaluation in healthy adults. Based on prior clinical trials [2,56] demonstrating attenuation of achieved CR over time, we expect DiAL Health’s Traditional CR intervention to yield only partial adherence relative to the prescribed 25% target. Alternatively, Adaptive CR is expected to demonstrate improved maintenance of achieved reductions in food intake through real-time adherence monitoring that will inform tailored, context-sensitive behavioral support. We also expect both Traditional and Adaptive TRE interventions to demonstrate measurable reductions in daily eating duration. Specifically, we anticipate that the Adaptive TRE group’s intervention approach and feedogram will likely support more consistent adherence to the eating window over time, yet the passive detection of eating events with CGM used together with other methods (e.g., SmartIntake) will make it more likely that nonadherence will be detected and thus the adherence scores might be lower than Traditional TRE.

The DiAL Health study incorporates several innovative elements to advance dietary intervention research in aging. To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to compare the feasibility and efficacy of both CR and TRE approaches against an ad libitum control using biologically meaningful and clinically validated measures of health span and aging [[23], [24], [25]]. Its most novel contribution, however, lies in its evaluation of Adaptive

TRE supported by passive, objective detection of eating episodes. To our knowledge, DiAL Health is among the first trials to deploy and formally evaluate passively collected meal-timing data as part of an intervention to regulate eating windows. Unlike Traditional TRE studies that rely on and assert the adequacy of self-reported intake, DiAL Health directly examines whether passive meal detection is feasible, accurate, and actionable within real-world behavioral intervention. This represents a meaningful step forward, as self-report bias remains a critical weakness in TRE research, obscuring true adherence. We expect lower but more accurate adherence rates in Adaptive TRE because misreporting is minimized and eating events are detected passively and objectively.

One weakness of this pilot trial is that the study design did not include direct participant involvement in shaping the intervention. Instead, the behavioral framework for the CR arm was informed by established evidence from landmark behavior change programs, including the Diabetes Prevention Program [49], Look AHEAD: Action for Health in Diabetes [48], and CALERIE 2 [28], which collectively provide robust guidance for structured behavioral support within CR paradigms. In contrast, no empirically supported behavior change models currently exist for TRE, and the literature offers no consensus on best practices for supporting adherence to this eating pattern. As part of the DiAL Health Pilot study, diet-specific satisfaction ratings and focus groups will provide participant feedback to guide the design and implementation of interventions for future studies.

Across all intervention arms, adherence outcomes will be evaluated and considered alongside attrition and intervention satisfaction. These indicators will be integrated into a composite feasibility score intended to assess intervention practicality and sustainability rather than to formally compare intervention arms. The resulting adherence profiles will inform intervention refinement and selection for a subsequent 5-y, multisite trial focused on dietary strategies to promote healthy aging.
