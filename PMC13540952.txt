ROS-responsive injectable hydrogel enables controlled release of human adipose tissue–derived extracellular vesicles for multifaceted osteoarthritis therapy
Authors: Yikai Wang, Le Kang, Kaizhe Chen, Yu Jiang, Yi Zheng, Peng Xu, Chuandong Wang, Wenhui Zhu, Dequn Wu, Kai Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic inflammation and elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS) are pivotal drivers of osteoarthritis (OA), demanding integrated, pathology-adaptive strategies. Here, we develop an injectable, ROS-responsive hydrogel for intra-articular delivery of human adipose tissue–derived extracellular vesicles (AT-EVs) to enable multifaceted OA treatment. Unlike conventional cell-derived MSC-EVs, AT-EVs are tissue-derived and can be isolated directly from lipoaspirate under aseptic operating-room conditions, providing a clinically practical EV source that bypasses prolonged cell expansion and multi-step culture conditioning. Small RNA-seq established their OA therapeutic potential. To facilitate intra-articular retention, the AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel is formed via dynamic boronate ester crosslinking between phenylboronic acid–grafted chitosan and epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG), achieving triple functionality: (i) injectable self-healing capacity, (ii) ROS-triggered controlled release, and (iii) synergistic ROS scavenging capacity. In vitro, the hydrogel attenuated oxidative stress, protected chondrocytes, restored matrix homeostasis, and suppressed inflammatory macrophage activation. Integrated small RNA profiling, transcriptomics, and phospho-protein validation consistently implicated PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway modulation as a key mechanism. In a rat OA model, AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG mitigated cartilage degeneration, reduced oxidative damage, and dampened inflammatory macrophage signatures. Collectively, this study provides a clinically practical, ROS-adaptive EV–hydrogel platform with translational potential for OA therapy.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-04838-9.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) represents a progressive whole-joint disorder characterized by irreversible cartilage degradation, synovitis-driven microenvironment alterations, and chronic pain-mediated functional impairment. Current treatment relies on NSAID-based palliative pharmacotherapy or late-stage arthroplasty, both presenting critical limitations: symptom-modifying approaches fail to address underlying pathophysiology, while invasive procedures incur substantial complications and socioeconomic burdens [1, 2]. Critically, no existing strategy effectively arrests early-stage OA progression or prevents extracellular matrix catabolism, highlighting an unmet need for disease-modifying interventions [3].

OA pathogenesis involves a self-sustaining cycle of chronic inflammation and oxidative stress that disrupts cartilage homeostasis and accelerates joint degeneration [4]. Early-stage OA features chondrocyte activation by mechanical/inflammatory stimuli, initially promoting compensatory anabolic activity but concurrently triggering catabolic factor release (MMPs, IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α). These mediators promote extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation, suppresses chondrocyte function via autocrine/paracrine signaling, and propagates synovial inflammation [5]. As OA progresses, catabolism becomes dominant while anabolism weakens, disrupting the metabolic balance and increasing pathological overproduction of pro-inflammatory cytokines and ROS. Critically, early synovitis recruits pro-inflammatory macrophages that drive inflammatory cascades, generating ROS which exacerbate chondrocyte dysfunction and matrix destruction [6]. This establishes a vicious “inflammation-ROS-macrophage activation-chondrocyte damage” cycle causing irreversible joint injury [7, 8]. Disrupting this circuit is therapeutically imperative, yet current biomaterial strategies remain limited by single-mechanism targeting that fails to address OA’s pathological complexity [9]. Thus, advanced platforms enabling multimodal and microenvironment-adaptive intervention are urgently needed.

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) have emerged as promising cell-free biologics for OA because they can deliver a combinatorial repertoire of regulatory RNAs, proteins, and lipids with high biocompatibility and multi-target activity [10]. Compared with cell therapies, EVs reduce concerns related to engraftment, uncontrolled differentiation, and safety management, while retaining much of the paracrine functionality of their parental tissues [11]. To date, most EV-based OA studies have focused on culture-expanded cell sources, particularly mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) such as adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs) or bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs). Although these cell-derived EVs show therapeutic promise, their clinical translation is often hindered by the need for large-scale cell expansion and long production timelines. Prolonged in vitro culture may increase the risk of microbial contamination that can lead to culture loss or batch failure. Quality variability caused by culture conditions and passage-dependent phenotypic changes also remain concerns. In addition, stem cell–based sources may further entail additional ethical and regulatory considerations [12, 13].

Tissue-derived EVs have recently attracted attention as endogenous mediators of organ homeostasis with translational potential [14]. Subcutaneous adipose tissue, an essential energy storage and metabolic regulatory organ, mediates protective functions against glucose intolerance, insulin resistance, and type 2 diabetes risk [15–18]. Accumulating evidence supports the therapeutic applications of adipose tissue extracts. Cell-free subcutaneous adipose tissue extracts exhibit multifunctional therapeutic utility, including anti-apoptotic, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory functions applicable to anti-aging, wound healing, and organ protection [19]. Murine studies further demonstrate that mouse adipose tissue-derived extracellular vesicles (AT-EVs) functionally coordinate whole-body homeostasis and provide endogenous protection against oxidative stress [20]. Despite OA being characterized by chronic inflammation and oxidative stress, the therapeutic potential of AT-EVs in this pathology remains unexplored. To establish clinically relevant evidence, we utilized human AT-EVs isolated directly from clinical specimens, thereby reducing cross-species translation gaps. Critically, our modified methodology enables instant preparation of AT-EVs from liposuction waste during surgery within 2 h, enabling immediate therapeutic use or frozen storage [21]. This approach significantly reduces contamination risks while eliminating the 2–4 week cell expansion required for traditional MSC-EVs production, thereby mitigating the concerns of dedifferentiation and tumorigenicity inherent in stem cell culture. It bypasses the need for GMP-grade labs and costly equipment, enabling cost-effective translation [14]. Importantly, as a tissue-derived EV source integrated with routine surgery, AT-EVs offer a clinically practical route that can simplify manufacturing workflows while supporting scalability. In this study, high-throughput small RNA sequencing confirmed that AT-EVs are enriched with OA-associated miRNAs, demonstrating therapeutic potential. However, their inadequate intra-articular retention severely restricts their application [22].

Recent studies confirm biocompatible hydrogels can prevent rapid joint clearance to enhance drug retention [23]. An ideal EV therapeutic system should have the following properties: i) structural/molecular stability during storage/post-administration and ii) controlled delivery [24, 25]. This is critically relevant given that elevated ROS levels are not only a hallmark of OA pathology, but also compromise EV integrity and bioactivity [26]. Specifically, ROS-induced lipid peroxidation of EV membranes alters surface area, thickness, and permeability, impairing EV morphology and function [27]. These findings underscore the necessity of antioxidant-capable hydrogels for OA applications. Concurrently, ROS can be harnessed to trigger microenvironment-responsive drug release, thereby enhancing local drug concentration and therapeutic efficacy at pathological sites [28]. We engineered AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel by encapsulating AT-EVs within phenylboronic acid-modified chitosan (BA-CS) crosslinked via dynamic boronate ester bonds to epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG) (Scheme 1). EGCG as a natural antioxidant, serves dual roles in the hydrogel: participating in hydrogel crosslinking while providing synergistic antioxidant enhancement [29]. This design confers injectability and self-recovery for minimally invasive delivery, controlled release responsive to pathologically elevated ROS, and synergistic therapy in which EGCG scavenges ROS to support EV stability and bioactivity while collaborating with AT-EVs for integrated treatment. In vitro, the AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel demonstrates multifunctional therapeutic actions against OA pathology through potent antioxidant effects, chondrocyte cytoprotection with metabolic balance restoration, and immunomodulation via attenuation of inflammatory (M1-like) macrophage polarization. In rat OA models, it effectively mitigates OA progression by preventing cartilage degradation, suppressing M1 macrophage polarization, and reducing oxidative stress damage, establishing the AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel as a clinically practical and promising OA therapeutic strategy.

Materials and methods

Preparation of AT-EVs

Human subcutaneous adipose tissue was obtained as medical waste from abdominal liposuction procedures performed in the Department of Plastic and Reconstructive Surgery, Shanghai Ninth People’s Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, Shanghai, China. Tissue was sourced from young, healthy female donors undergoing cosmetic liposuction procedures, with a normal body mass index (BMI, 18.5–24.9 kg/m²). All protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (SH9H-2023-T403-3), with written informed consent obtained from all participants. The adipose tissue was washed with sterile PBS and transferred into 50 mL centrifuge tubes (Corning, USA). Blood and tumescent fluid were removed by centrifugation (1,500 rpm, 5 min, room temperature (RT)). The adipose layer was collected, mixed with an equal volume of PBS, and homogenized using a handheld homogenizer (25,000 rpm, 1 min) until a homogeneous emulsion was obtained. The homogenate was centrifuged (3,000 × g, 15 min, 4 °C) to remove the lipid layer and solid residues, and the EV-enriched fluid was collected. AT-EVs were isolated from the fluid using an established ultrafiltration protocol [30]. Briefly, the fluid was filtered through a 0.22 μm sterile filter (Millipore, USA) to remove residual cells and large debris. The filtrate was then concentrated and washed using a 100 kDa molecular weight cut-off centrifugal ultrafiltration tube (Millipore #UFC910096, Merck, Billerica, MA, USA) by centrifugation (3,000 × g, 20 min, 4 °C). The retentate containing AT-EVs was collected and, further washed with PBS to remove soluble proteins. AT-EVs were quantified via BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, China) and stored at −80 °C for subsequent experiments. EV characterization included nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA; ZetaView, Germany), transmission electron microscopy (TEM; JSM-7001TA, Japan), and Western blot analysis. For Western blot analysis, crude adipose extract (Adipose Tissue) and the AT-EV-enriched fraction (AT-EVs) were compared. Crude adipose extract was collected after PBS washing, homogenization, and low-speed centrifugation of human subcutaneous adipose tissue. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, and probed with antibodies against CD9 (Abcam, UK), TSG101 (Proteintech, USA), GM130 (Abcam, UK), Calnexin (Affinity, USA), ApoA1 (Affinity, USA), and Albumin (Abcam, UK).

miRNA analysis of AT-EVs

Total RNA isolation utilized Trizol reagent with subsequent quality verification via Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometry and Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer electrophoretic integrity assessment. Small RNA libraries were constructed from 1 µg total RNA using NEBNext® kits (E7330S). This involved sequential 3’/5’ adapter ligation, cDNA synthesis, and PCR amplification. PCR products (140–160 bp) were size-selected and purified via agarose gel electrophoresis. Validated libraries underwent 150-bp paired-end sequencing on Illumina NovaSeq 6000. Small RNA-seq and bioinformatics analysis was conducted by OE Biotech.

Raw sequencing data underwent base calling to generate raw reads, followed by stringent quality filtering to yield clean reads. This process removed: 1) low-quality reads, 2) sequences containing 5’ primers or poly(A) tails, 3) reads lacking 3’ adapters or sample barcodes, and 4) fragments outside the 15–41 nt size range. Clean reads were first mapped to the reference genome for length distribution analysis. Subsequent annotation involved sequential alignments using Bowtie against: i) Rfam v10.1 ((http://www.sanger.ac.uk/software/Rfam) to filter structural RNAs (rRNA, tRNA, snRNA, etc.), ii) cDNA sequences and Repbase repeats for transcript degradation and repeat removal, iii) miRBase v22.1 (mirbase.org) for known miRNA identification. Unannotated reads were processed through miRDeep2 for de novo miRNA prediction based on pre-miRNA hairpin structures and miRBase homology, identifying mature and star sequences. miRNA expression was quantified in transcripts per million (TPM). Target prediction employed miRanda with stringent parameters (Score ≥ 150, ΔG ≤ −30 kcal/mol, strict 5’ seed pairing). Predicted targets were cross-referenced with osteoarthritis (OA)-associated pathway genes and literature-curated OA-related miRNAs. Candidate OA-associated miRNAs were prioritized by selecting the top 30 TPM-ranked species overlapping with AT-EVs’ highly expressed miRNAs. Functional enrichment of target genes was performed using hypergeometric testing for GO terms and KEGG pathways (R stats package).

Preparation of BA-CS, BA-CS/EGCG and AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel

3-carboxyphenylboronic acid-modified chitosan (BA-CS) was synthesised as previously described in the literature [31], 3-carboxyphenylboronic acid (3-CPBA), 1-Ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC·HCl), and N-Hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) were dissolved in methanol at molar ratios of CS : 3-CPBA : EDC·HCl : NHS = 1 : 2 : 2.5 : 2.5, activated for 1–2 h at 25 °C. The mixture was slowly added to chitosan (CS) dissolved in acetic acid/water (1% v/v) under stirring. After 24 h reaction, the product was dialyzed against ultrapure water for 72 h (water replaced every 8 h), then lyophilized to yield BA-CS. The chitosan used in this study had a molecular weight of 50–190 kDa and a degree of deacetylation of 90%. The degree of substitution of BA on chitosan was approximately 13–15%, as determined by ^1H NMR analysis.

For the formation BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel: BA-CS was dissolved in PBS (pH 7.4) to form a 1% (w/v) polymer solution. EGCG (60 µM final concentration) was added and vortexed for 10 s. Gelation occurred within 30 s at 37 °C, forming BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel.

For the preparation AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel: AT-EVs (100 µg/mL final concentration) were mixed with BA-CS solution (1% w/v) for 5 min. EGCG (60 µM) was added, followed by immediate vortexing for 10 s. The mixture gelled within 30 s at 37 °C, yielding AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel.

Characterization of BA-CS, BA-CS/EGCG and AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel

BA-CS, BA-CS/EGCG and AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel were characterized by FTIR spectroscopy (Bruker TensorFlow II, Germany) and ¹H-NMR (400 MHz, Bruker AVANCE NEO, Switzerland). The degree of substitution (DS) of BA on chitosan was calculated based on the ^1H NMR spectrum of BA-CS (Fig. 2B). Specifically, the aromatic proton signals of phenylboronic acid appeared at δ 7.4–8.2 ppm, while the characteristic proton peaks of the chitosan backbone were located at δ 3.0–4.0 ppm. The DS was calculated according to the following equation:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned}&\text{Degree of substitution (DS}, \%) =\\& [(\mathrm{I}_\mathrm{aromatic} / 4) / (\mathrm{ICS}\ _\mathrm{backbone} / 6)] \times 100\%\end{aligned}$$\end{document}

where Iaromatic represents the integral area of the aromatic protons from BA, and ICS backbone corresponds to the integral area of the glucosamine ring protons of chitosan. According to the ^1H NMR spectrum shown in Fig. 2B, the integral area ratio between the aromatic protons at δ 7.4–8.2 ppm and the chitosan backbone protons at δ 3.0–4.0 ppm was approximately 0.09:1. Therefore, the calculated BA substitution degree was approximately 13–15%.

The microstructure of BA-CS/EGCG and AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel was observed by SEM (Regulus SU8010, Japan).

Injectability, self-healing, rheological testing, and degradation assay

Injectability was assessed by manually extruding the hydrogel through a 1 mL syringe to write the letters “KOA” on a 10 cm cell culture dish. The picture was captured 30 min after extrusion.

Self-healing characterization: Unmodified BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel and red dye-incorporated BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel were separately extruded into triangular shapes. After contact between the triangle tips, interfacial color diffusion was photographed to characterize self-healing properties. Separately, BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel and red-dye-labeled BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel were extruded onto finger joints, and after contact healing, the fingers were bent to different angles to observe healing firmness.

Rheological behavior was evaluated using the rheometer (Thermo Scientific™ HAAKE™ MARS 60, Germany). Oscillation amplitude sweeps were performed at fixed ω = 1 rad/s and T = 25 °C with strain (γ%) scanning from 0.01% to 1000%. Oscillation frequency sweeps were conducted at fixed γ% = 1% and T = 25 °C with angular frequency (ω) scanning from 0.1 to 100 rad/s.

The self-healing behavior of the hydrogel was evaluated by oscillatory time sweep rheological measurements using alternating low and high strain intervals [32, 33]. The test was conducted at a fixed frequency of ω = 1 rad/s. A low strain of 1% was applied for 50 s to monitor the intact network structure, followed by a high strain of 500% for 50 s to disrupt the hydrogel network. Each complete cycle lasted 100 s, and the measurement was repeated for three consecutive cycles to assess the recovery capability of the hydrogel after repeated structural destruction.

The degradation kinetics of the hydrogel were evaluated in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) under non-enzymatic conditions. Lyophilized dry AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogels with an initial mass of 0.1 g per sample (n = 3 per time point) were weighed to obtain the initial dry mass (W₀). Each sample was immersed in 1 g PBS and incubated on a shaker (37 °C, 50 rpm). At predetermined intervals, hydrogels were extracted from the PBS medium, excess PBS was gently removed by blotting, and the samples were again lyophilized to obtain consistent dry weight. The dry mass at each time point (Wₜ) was recorded. The remaining mass (%) was calculated as: Remaining mass (%) = (Wₜ/W₀) × 100%.

ROS-responsive disassembly/release and intra-articular retention of AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG

ROS-triggered disassembly: To assess ROS responsiveness, 0.5 mL pre-formed AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel was prepared in vials, treated with 10 µL of 30% H₂O₂, and incubated at 25 °C for 48 h. Hydrogel disassembly was evaluated macroscopically by vial inversion and recording flow behavior.

ROS-responsive release profiling: Hydrogels were immersed in 10 mL PBS containing H₂O₂ (0, 0.1, 1, or 10 mM) and incubated on a shaker (37 °C, 50 rpm). At predetermined time points, 1 mL of supernatant was collected and replaced with fresh H₂O₂/PBS. EGCG release was quantified by absorbance at 273.5 nm (spectrophotometer, Thermo Fisher, USA), and AT-EVs release was estimated using a BCA protein assay (Beyotime, China) on the same supernatant. Cumulative release was calculated as the percentage of released EGCG or AT-EVs relative to the initial loading amount.

In vivo intra-articular retention (IVIS): AT-EVs were labeled with DiR and free dye was removed by repeated washing/ultrafiltration. DiR-labeled AT-EVs (DiR–AT-EVs) and DiR–AT-EVs–loaded AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel were administered by intra-articular injection. Animals were allocated into three groups (n = 3 rats per group): (i) WT rats receiving free DiR–AT-EVs, (ii) WT rats receiving DiR–AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel, and (iii) OA rats receiving DiR–AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel. The OA model induction procedure is described in detail in the in vivo study section below. Fluorescence was monitored longitudinally using IVIS at defined time points over 7 days under identical acquisition settings. Fluorescence retention was quantified by drawing ROIs over the injected joints and normalizing signal intensity to the day 1 value for each animal.

Radicals scavenging capacity

The radical scavenging capacity of different samples was evaluated using DPPH, ABTS, •OH, and H₂O₂ scavenging assays. For the DPPH assay, samples were mixed with an equal volume of 0.2 mM DPPH/ethanol, incubated at 37 °C for 5 min, and absorbance was measured at 517 nm. For the ABTS assay, ABTS⁺ stock solution was generated by reacting 5 mM ABTS with 2 mM K₂S₂O₈ for 4 h in the dark at room temperature and then diluted to OD₇₃₄ = 0.8 as the working solution. Samples were mixed with an equal volume of working solution, incubated at 37 °C for 5 min, and absorbance was measured at 734 nm. For •OH scavenging, the working solution contained 9 mM salicylic acid, 9 mM FeSO₄, and 8.8 mM H₂O₂ at a 1:1:1 ratio. Samples were mixed with an equal volume of working solution, incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, and absorbance was measured at 520 nm. For H₂O₂ scavenging, samples were mixed with an equal volume of 9 mM H₂O₂ and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Then, an equal volume of titanium sulfate probe (3% TiOSO₄ in H₂SO₄) was added. After incubation at 37 °C for 5 min, absorbance was measured at 405 nm. Radical scavenging efficiency was calculated as follows: Radical scavenging (%) = (ODcontrol - ODsample)/ODcontrol ×100%.

Chondrocyte isolation and culture

All animal protocols were approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Fudan University Experimental Animal Science Department (2024-HSYY-594). Primary chondrocytes were isolated from postnatal day 3 Sprague-Dawley (SD) rat knee joints under aseptic conditions via enzymatic dissociation: 30-min digestion in 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (37 °C) followed by 4–6 h collagenase II (Worthington, USA) treatment (0.2% in DMEM, 37 °C under gentle agitation). Cell suspensions were filtered through 70-µm strainers (Corning, USA), centrifuged (300 ×g, 5 min), and expanded in complete medium (DMEM +10% FBS +1% P/S) for subsequent experiments.

Cellular internalization of AT-EVs

AT-EVs cellular internalization was detected as previously described [34]. EVs were tracked using lipophilic dye labeling. Purified EVs were labeled with CM-Dil dye (Invitrogen), with excess dye removed by 100-kDa cutoff ultrafiltration (3,000 ×g, 20 min, 4 °C). To exclude fluorescence interference from dye aggregates, a CM-Dil-only non-EV control was prepared using the same labeling and ultrafiltration procedure in the absence of EVs. Chondrocytes were incubated with labeled EVs or the non-EV control for 12 h, followed by PBS washing, 4% PFA fixation, and cytoskeletal/nuclear counterstaining with phalloidin/DAPI (Yeasen, China). Cellular internalization was visualized by confocal microscopy (Nikon A1, Japan).

Cytocompatibility of AT-EVs

Chondrocyte biocompatibility of AT-EVs was assessed via live/dead fluorescence staining and Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assays. Chondrocytes were treated with AT-EVs (0–300 µg/mL) for 24/48 h. For live/dead analysis, cells (1 × 10⁵/well in 12-well plates) underwent Calcein-AM/PI staining (Beyotime, 20 min), with live (green) and dead (red) cells imaged using a fluorescence microscopy (Leica, Germany). CCK-8 assays used 4,000 cells/well in 96-well plates. After treatment, CCK-8 reagent (100 µL, Beyotime) was added for 2 h at 37 °C, with absorbance measured at 450 nm (Thermo Fisher, USA).

Cytocompatibility of AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel

The extract solutions of BA-CS/EGCG and AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG were prepared by incubating 0.1 g of each hydrogel sample in 1 mL serum-free DMEM at 37 °C for 72 h. Cytocompatibility was evaluated through live/dead staining, CCK-8 assay, and Alcian blue staining. Chondrocytes were cultured with complete medium containing BA-CS/EGCG, AT-EVs, or AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG extracts for 24/48 h, with PBS-supplemented complete medium as control. Live/dead and CCK-8 assays were performed following previously described. Extracellular matrix of chondrocytes was evaluated by Alcian blue staining. Cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min and stained with Alcian blue 8GX (pH 2.5, Sigma, USA) for 30 min. Glycosaminoglycan deposition was visualized under inverted microscope (Olympus, Japan).

For hemolysis assay, fresh rat whole blood (heparinized) was centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 10 min to isolate erythrocytes. The pelleted erythrocytes were washed three times with PBS and reconstituted to 5% (v/v) in PBS. Test materials (BA-CS/EGCG, AT-EVs, AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG) were mixed with erythrocyte suspension at 1:1 volume ratio and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Positive (0.1% Triton X-100) and negative (PBS) controls were processed in parallel. After centrifugation (1000 × g, 5 min), supernatant absorbance was measured at 541 nm.

In vitro antioxidant capacity assessment

The extract solutions of BA-CS/EGCG and AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogels used in the in vitro cell assays were prepared by incubating hydrogel samples in serum-free DMEM at a hydrogel-to-medium ratio of 100 mg/mL at 37 °C for 72 h. Unless otherwise stated, the resulting hydrogel extracts were used for subsequent cell treatments, and AT-EVs were applied at a concentration of 100 µg/mL. Chondrocytes were treated with 1 mM H₂O₂ in complete medium containing PBS or test samples, while controls received H₂O₂-free medium. Cell viability was assessed at 24/48 h via live/dead staining and CCK-8 assay as previously described. Intracellular ROS levels were measured using the Reactive Oxygen Species Assay Kit (Beyotime, China) with 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA). After 48 h treatments, cells were PBS-washed, incubated with 5 µM DCFH-DA (37 °C, 20 min), rewashed, and imaged under a fluorescence microscope (Zeiss, Germany). Sulfated glycosaminoglycan (sGAG) deposition was evaluated by Alcian blue staining: Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (20 min), stained with Alcian blue 8GX (pH 2.5, Sigma, USA) for 30 min, and visualized under inverted microscope (Olympus, Japan). Quantification analysis was performed using ImageJ software with threshold-based analysis.

In vitro OA chondrocyte protection assay

Chondrocytes were pretreated with 10 ng/mL IL-1β for 24 h to induce an OA-related inflammatory chondrocyte model. After PBS washing, cells were treated with fresh medium containing corresponding agents for 48 h. Untreated chondrocytes were served as control group. Intracellular ROS levels were measured by DCFH-DA staining as previously described.

Gene and protein expression in OA chondrocytes were analyzed via RT-PCR and Western blot. Total RNA isolated with Trizol underwent reverse transcription (EZBioscience Master Mix). qPCR amplification on a CFX384 system (Bio-Rad, USA) employed SYBR Green chemistry with primers from Table S2, quantifying mRNA via 2-ΔΔCt method with GAPDH normalization. Experiments were repeated three times independently.

Protein extracts in RIPA buffer were resolved by SDS-PAGE, transferred to membranes, and probed overnight (4 °C) with anti-COL2 (Affinity, USA), anti-MMP3 (Proteintech, USA), and anti-MMP13 (Affinity, USA). Secondary antibody incubation proceeded for 1 h at RT. Signals were detected using ECL with β-actin as loading control and were imaged using the Tanon-4600 multifunctional chemiluminescence system (Tanon, China).

Macrophage polarization assay

RAW 264.7 cells were used to investigate hydrogel-mediated immunomodulation in inflammatory microenvironments. 5 × 105 RAW 264.7 cells were seeded in 6-well plates. After adhesion, medium was replaced with LPS-containing medium (1 µg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) supplemented with corresponding agents to induce polarization. Control group was maintained in complete medium without LPS. Following 24-h incubation, cells were harvested for flow cytometry, RT-PCR, and ELISA to quantify M1/M2 macrophage polarization.

Harvested RAW264.7 cells were stained with FITC-conjugated anti-CD86 and anti-CD206 (BioLegend, USA) in PBS for 30 min at 4 °C protected from light. After PBS washing, cells were analyzed on a flow cytometer (CytoFLEX, USA). Gene expression analysis was performed using RT-PCR with primers in Table S1. Cell supernatants were centrifuged (300 ×g, 10 min) and quantified by commercial ELISA kits (R&D Systems) following standard sandwich immunoassay protocol. Briefly, 100 µL samples/standards incubated in coated wells (2 h RT), followed by sequential biotinylated antibody, streptavidin-HRP, and TMB substrate additions. Reactions were terminated after 20-min chromogenic development and absorbance read at 450 nm within 30 min.

RNA-seq assay and OA signaling pathway detection

Total RNA of OA chondrocytes and AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG treated OA chondrocytes was isolated using Trizol, with integrity verified (RIN > 8.0, Bioanalyzer 2100). Libraries prepared with VAHTS V6 kits (OE Biotech) underwent 150-bp paired-end sequencing on Illumina Novaseq 6000. Post-fastp quality control, HISAT2-aligned reads were quantified via HTSeq. DESeq2 identified DEGs (FDR < 0.05, |log₂FC| > 1), with hierarchical clustering in R. Functional enrichment (GO/KEGG, hypergeometric test) and GSEA were performed on significant DEGs (q < 0.05).

Phosphorylation dynamics of PI3K/AKT/mTOR were assessed by Western blot under phosphatase inhibition. Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Roche, Switzerland), with proteins separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes, blocked with 5% BSA, and probed overnight (4 °C) with antibodies against: p-PI3K (Affinity, USA), PI3K (Proteintech, USA), p-AKT (Affinity, USA), AKT (Affinity, USA), p-mTOR (Affinity, USA) and mTOR (Affinity, USA). HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000, 1 h RT) enabled chemiluminescent detection (Tanon-4600, China).

Establishment of OA rat model

All the animal experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Fudan University Experimental Animal Science Department (2024-HSYY-594). Twenty-five male Sprague–Dawley rats (8 weeks old) were randomly assigned to the Sham group (n = 5) or the OA group (n = 20). Following anesthesia with sodium pentobarbital, OA was surgically induced by anterior cruciate ligament transection (ACLT) combined with medial meniscal transection (MMT) as previously described [35]. Sham-operated rats underwent arthrotomy without ligament/meniscus transection. After 1 month of OA development, OA rats were re-randomized into four treatment groups (n = 5 per group) and received weekly intra-articular injections for 4 consecutive weeks: PBS, BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel, AT-EVs, and AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel. Each knee received a total injection volume of 100 µL over the 4-week treatment course, administered as 25 µL per injection once weekly. The hydrogel concentration was 1% (w/v), and AT-EVs were administered at 100 µg/mL. Four weeks after the first injection (i.e., after completion of the 4-week treatment course), rats were euthanized and knee joints were harvested for micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) scanning. Three-dimensional reconstruction and quantitative analyses were performed using Quick Render software (Pingseng Scientific, China).

Knee joints underwent 48 h fixation in 4 °C-cold 4% PFA followed by 4-week decalcification in 10% EDTA (pH 7.4) with daily solution renewal. Tissues were dehydrated through graded ethanol series, xylene-cleared, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned. Histological evaluation employed H&E for cellular morphology, 0.04% toluidine blue (pH 4.0) for proteoglycan visualization, and 0.1% safranin O/0.02% fast green for matrix assessment. Cartilage degeneration was quantified via enhanced Mankin scoring by two blinded evaluators. For immunohistochemistry (IHC), antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0, 95 °C, 20 min) preceded 5% BSA blocking and overnight incubation at 4 °C with anti-collagen II or anti-MMP13 (Abcam, UK). HRP-conjugated secondaries enabled DAB-based detection. Multiplex immunofluorescence after identical antigen retrieval used triple labeling with anti-CD206 (M2), anti-iNOS (M1), and anti-8-OHdG (oxidative damage) antibodies (Abcam, UK), with Alexa Fluor secondaries (1 h RT) and DAPI counterstaining. All sections were imaged on a Zeiss Axio Imager, with quantitative analysis via ImageJ using automated thresholding.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted in GraphPad Prism 9.5.0 with continuous variables presented as mean ± SD. Multi-group comparisons utilized one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc correction for multiple comparisons, while two-group comparisons used unpaired Student’s t-test. Statistical significance was defined as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01.

Results and discussion

Isolation and characterization of AT-EVs

In this study, we acquired abdominal subcutaneous adipose tissue using standard liposuction, a decades-established plastic surgery technique with minimal complication risk. AT-EVs were then isolated directly from this tissue through an optimized ultrafiltration protocol (Fig. 1A) [21]. Unlike conventional MSC-EVs isolation requiring weeks of cell expansion, our approach eliminates in vitro culture needs. This protocol enables direct AT-EV extraction in operating rooms within 2 h, permitting immediate clinical application or cryopreservation for scalable production—demonstrating superior clinical translation potential [14, 21]. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that AT-EVs had a typical EVs morphology with characteristic saucer-shaped or hemispherical structures exhibiting membrane curvature (Fig. 1B) [36]. To assess the reproducibility of AT-EV production, three independent AT-EV batches were quantified by NTA (Fig. 1C). The particle concentrations were highly consistent across batches, yielding an average concentration of (1.97 ± 0.06) × 10¹¹ particles/ml. Consistent with this, all three batches showed nearly identical size-distribution profiles with a peak diameter of 121.3 ± 4.1 nm, confirming excellent batch-to-batch uniformity. Protein content measured by BCA assay was 0.98 ± 0.06 mg/mL across the three batches (Table S1). Combining these values with NTA particle counts resulted in a protein-to-particle ratio of (5.0 ± 0.2) × 10⁻¹⁵ g/particle.

Western blot analysis further confirmed the enrichment of EV-associated marker proteins CD9 and TSG101 in the AT-EV preparation (Fig. 1D). In contrast, the levels of non-EV contaminant markers, including GM130 and Calnexin for intracellular organelle-associated components, Albumin for soluble protein contamination, and ApoA1 for lipoprotein-associated contamination, were markedly reduced in the AT-EV preparation compared with the crude adipose tissue extract (Fig. 1D). These results indicate that the filtration and ultrafiltration-based workflow effectively enriched adipose tissue-derived EVs while substantially reducing major soluble protein, lipoprotein, and intracellular contaminants.

Taken together, the TEM, NTA, BCA, and Western blot results support the successful enrichment of adipose tissue-derived EVs. We therefore refer to the obtained EV-enriched adipose tissue-derived preparation as AT-EVs throughout the manuscript. Nevertheless, as with other size-based EV enrichment methods, trace non-vesicular components cannot be completely excluded, and further purification strategies such as size-exclusion chromatography or density-gradient separation may be considered in future mechanistic studies [30].

Small RNA sequencing reveals OA-associated miRNA enrichment in AT-EVs

Having confirmed the successful isolation of tissue-derived EVs from human subcutaneous adipose tissue, we next profiled their bioactive cargo, as EV cargo composition is a major determinant of downstream biological functions and therapeutic efficacy [37]. Among EV cargo components, miRNAs represent the most extensively studied and demonstrate high evolutionary conservation across species. Prior studies have shown that the therapeutic effects of conventional MSC-EVs in OA are largely mediated by their miRNA cargo [13, 38]. Accordingly, to evaluate the therapeutic potential of AT-EVs for OA, we performed high-throughput small RNA sequencing to systematically characterize the miRNA profile. Given that biological activity is typically driven by a limited number of highly abundant miRNAs, we focused on the top 30 most abundant miRNAs in AT-EVs to capture the predominant cargo while minimizing noise from low-abundance transcripts. We then compared these miRNAs with a curated list of the top 30 OA-associated miRNAs, revealing a substantial overlap between highly expressed AT-EV miRNAs and OA-related miRNAs (Fig. 1E) . The 18 intersecting miRNAs, which were both abundant in AT-EVs and implicated in OA, were subsequently ranked and quantitatively presented in descending order of expression (Fig. 1F) .

To functionally characterize these 18 miRNAs, their target genes were predicted using miRWalk and miRDB, and only the overlapping targets between the two databases were retained for downstream analyses (Fig. 1G). Functional annotation of the target genes of these 18 miRNAs was performed through GO and KEGG pathway analyses to identify enriched correlative terms and signaling pathways. GO enrichment analysis demonstrated significant involvement in “chondrocyte differentiation”, “chondrocyte proliferation”, “cell migration”, “collagen-activated tyrosine kinase receptor signaling”, and “positive regulation of PI3K/Akt signal transduction”. These terms connect directly to OA development and cartilage healing (Fig. 1H) [39, 40]. Consistently, KEGG analysis demonstrated significant enrichment of these miRNA target genes in “PI3K/AKT signaling pathway” and downstream “mTOR signaling pathway” cascades (Fig. 1I). Notably, the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis plays an essential role in chondrocyte survival and matrix homeostasis, whereas dysregulated and persistent activation in OA has been linked to cartilage degeneration, impaired autophagy, and chronic inflammation [41, 42]. Together, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of the predicted targets suggest that AT-EV miRNA cargo is associated with cartilage-related functions and PI3K/AKT–mTOR signaling, providing a rationale for subsequent mechanistic interrogation.

Given the limited RNA yield from EV preparations and the lack of universally accepted endogenous reference miRNAs for normalization in EV qRT-PCR assays, we primarily relied on small RNA sequencing for miRNA quantification and downstream analyses in this study. Notably, definitive quantification of specific miRNAs and validation of miRNA–target interactions will require orthogonal experiments, including qRT-PCR–based miRNA quantification and dual-luciferase reporter assays to confirm direct targeting, which will be pursued in future work [43].

Synthesis and characterization of BA-CS

Chitosan (CS), a natural polysaccharide, offers high biocompatibility and is degradable by lysozyme. Owing to its inherent positive charge, CS may electrostatically interact with negatively charged glycosaminoglycans, such as chondroitin sulfate, within the cartilage matrix. Previous studies have suggested that such charge-based interactions may enhance cartilage matrix association or local retention of positively charged materials and chitosan-containing hydrogels [44]. Therefore, this electrostatic interaction was considered as a design rationale for improving intra-articular retention in our CS-based hydrogel system, although direct cartilage-surface adhesion was not experimentally demonstrated in the present study. The osteoarthritic (OA) joint microenvironment exhibits elevated levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS). To introduce ROS-responsive crosslinking motifs while preserving these advantages, CS was further modified by conjugation with 3-carboxyphenylboronic acid (3-CPBA), yielding phenylboronic acid–grafted chitosan (BA-CS). Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Fig. 2A) confirmed the successful grafting of phenylboronic acid onto chitosan. Compared to pure CS, the grafted product (BA-CS) exhibited characteristic absorption peaks corresponding to amide I (C = O) at 1636 cm⁻¹ and amide II (N–H) at 1543 cm⁻¹. This indicates an amidation reaction occurred between the amino groups of chitosan and the carboxyl groups of 3-CPBA, forming new amide bonds. Furthermore, BA-CS retained characteristic absorption peaks observed in 3-CPBA, notably the aromatic ring C = C stretch at 1604 cm⁻¹ and the B–O vibration at 1367 cm⁻¹. These findings provide additional evidence for the successful introduction of phenylboronic acid groups onto the chitosan backbone.

Nuclear magnetic resonance (1HNMR) spectroscopy (Fig. 2B) further confirmed BA-CS synthesis through characteristic chitosan backbone signals. The multiplet peaks at δ 3.0–4.0 ppm corresponded to -CH-OH, -CH-NH₂, and -CH₂- protons of glucosamine rings, while the minor δ 2.0 ppm signal represented residual acetyl groups (-NHCOCH₃). Critically, aromatic protons at δ 7.0–8.0 ppm from 3-carboxyphenylboronic acid’s phenyl ring provided definitive evidence of successful grafting. Collectively, FTIR and 1HNMR analyses confirm the successful synthesis and functionalization of BA-CS.

Formation and physicochemical properties of the AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel

Epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG) is the primary bioactive catechin in green tea, renowned for its potent antioxidant properties. It exhibits high efficacy in antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-tumor and anti-aging activities in biological systems [29, 45]. AT-EVs are bioactive nanoparticles with lipid bilayer structures that possess inherent therapeutic properties, yet their structural and biomolecular integrity is readily compromised under high ROS conditions [26]. Given the dynamically heightened ROS levels in the OA joint, an ideal delivery system should both preserve EV integrity during oxidative exposure and enable controlled local release. To meet these requirements, BA-CS was crosslinked with EGCG via dynamic boronate ester bonds formed between phenylboronic acid groups and EGCG catechol groups. AT-EVs were homogeneously blended into the BA-CS polymer solution, followed by the addition of EGCG. The resulting AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel was stabilized through dual interactions involving boronate ester bonding and hydrogen bonding. FTIR analysis revealed significant changes in the AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel compared to BA-CS alone (Fig. 2C): intensified absorption in the 1641–1533 cm⁻¹ region supports EGCG incorporation; the B–O stretching vibration shifted from 1367 cm⁻¹ to 1387 cm⁻¹, indicating boronate ester formation; and the broadened -OH stretching band (3670–3110 cm⁻¹) was intensified, consistent with strengthened hydrogen-bonding interactions.

Macroscopically, the AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel exhibited good injectability and shape retention, as demonstrated by smooth extrusion through a 1 mL syringe and the maintenance of “KOA” lettering on culture dishes 30 min after extrusion (Fig. 2D). When differently colored hydrogel fragments were brought into contact, rapid self-healing was observed (Fig. 2E, left). Within 10 s, the fragments fused into an integrated construct, with microscopy showing a seamless interface. The healed hydrogel tolerated repeated bending without fracture (Fig. 2E, right), indicating robust structural recovery and flexibility.

Rheological tests were performed to quantitatively substantiate the mechanical basis underlying injectability and self-recovery. In strain sweep measurements (Fig. 2F), the hydrogel exhibited elastic-dominant, solid-like behavior at low strains with G′ consistently higher than G″. Notably, a clear linear viscoelastic region was observed below ~50% strain (Fig. 2F, bottom), where both moduli remained nearly constant, indicating a stable crosslinked network. With increasing strain, G′ and G″ progressively decreased and approached each other at ~350–400% strain, suggesting the onset of network yielding; at higher strains (≥ 500%), G″ exceeded G′, consistent with a transition toward more liquid-like behavior under large deformation. Frequency sweep measurements performed at 1% strain (within the LVR) showed that G′ remained greater than G″ across 0–100 rad/s, with a modest increase in G′ with frequency, indicating a robust viscoelastic network (Fig. 2G).

The self-healing behavior of the hydrogel was evaluated by alternating strain time sweep measurements (Fig. 2H). At 1% strain, the storage modulus (G′) remained higher than the loss modulus (G″), indicating a stable elastic network. When the strain increased to 500%, both G′ and G″ decreased markedly, suggesting disruption of the hydrogel structure. After returning to 1% strain, G′ rapidly recovered to its initial value and remained higher than G″ over three consecutive cycles, demonstrating the self-healing capability of the hydrogel.

SEM imaging showed that the BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel possessed an interconnected porous microstructure (Figure S1). After EV loading, the AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel maintained a similar porous architecture, with AT-EVs uniformly distributed within the matrix, supporting successful encapsulation without apparent structural damage (Fig. 2I). In PBS, the hydrogel exhibited sustained mass loss over 72 h (Fig. 2J), indicating gradual degradation compatible with prolonged release[46]. To evaluate whether EVs released from the hydrogel retained their cellular internalization capability, EVs released after hydrogel dissolution were labeled with CM-Dil and co-cultured with chondrocytes. Confocal microscopy confirmed intracellular red fluorescence signals within 12 h (Figure S2), indicating successful cellular uptake of released AT-EVs. In parallel, a CM-Dil-only non-EV control processed using the same labeling and washing procedure showed negligible red fluorescence, suggesting that the observed signal was not caused by residual dye aggregates.

ROS-responsive release and prolonged intra-articular retention of AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel

ROS-responsive behavior was evaluated using H₂O₂ to simulate pathological microenvironments. The AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel utilizes boronate ester bonds as dynamic crosslinking points. Under H₂O₂-induced oxidative stress, these bonds undergo cleavage, triggering network disassembly and a gel-to-sol transition (Fig. 2K), demonstrating ROS-responsive drug release characteristics. Given the dynamically fluctuating ROS levels during OA progression, release kinetics were quantified across physiological H₂O₂ gradients (0.1-10mM, Fig. 2L and M)[47]. Results demonstrated concentration-dependent release acceleration: in PBS solution, EGCG showed 91.2% cumulative release at 96 h while AT-EVs released 87.1% at 96 h; under 0.1 mM H₂O₂, EGCG reached 92.3% release at 72 h versus 92.8% for AT-EVs at 72 h; at 1 mM H₂O₂, both achieved > 92% release by 48 h (EGCG 92.0%, AT-EVs 92.8%); and under 10 mM H₂O₂, 93.9% of EGCG and 94.0% of AT-EVs were released within 24 h. Together, the macroscopic gel-to-sol response and the H₂O₂-dependent release profiles confirm ROS-adaptive, concentration-dependent cargo release behavior (Fig. 2K–M).

To assess intra-articular retention in vivo, we used an in vivo imaging system (IVIS) to longitudinally monitor fluorescently labeled AT-EVs. DiR-labeled AT-EVs were injected into WT rat joints either as free EVs or encapsulated within AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel, and hydrogel-encapsulated EVs were additionally evaluated in OA joints. IVIS imaging for 7 days showed rapid signal loss for free DiR-AT-EVs, whereas encapsulation in the AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel markedly prolonged intra-articular fluorescence retention up to 7 days (Fig. 2N). Quantification of relative fluorescence intensity further confirmed significantly higher residual signal in the hydrogel groups compared with free AT-EVs throughout the observation period (Fig. 2O). Notably, AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG in OA joints displayed a slightly faster decline than in WT joints, which is consistent with ROS-responsive network destabilization and accelerated release under a high-ROS microenvironment.

Biocompatibility of AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel

The biocompatibility remains essential for tissue engineering biomaterials [48]. So live/dead staining and CCK-8 assay were conducted using different concentrations of AT-EVs to establish safe application concentrations. Live/dead staining demonstrated sustained chondrocyte viability at 24/48 h with no mortality increase, while concentrations over 100 µg/ml enhanced viable cell numbers (Figs. S3A and B). Consistently, CCK-8 assays confirmed no cytotoxicity at any concentration and revealed significantly enhanced cell viability at concentrations ≥ 100 µg/mL (p < 0.05) (Figure S3C). Based on these results, 100 µg/mL AT-EVs was selected for subsequent experiments.

We next evaluated the cytocompatibility of BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel (without AT-EVs), free AT-EVs, and AT-EVs-loaded hydrogel (AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG). Live/Dead staining and CCK-8 assays demonstrated good biocompatibility in all groups (Figures S4–S5). Notably, both AT-EVs and AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG significantly promoted chondrocyte viability compared with controls (p < 0.05) (Figure S6). Alcian blue staining, which labels proteoglycans in cartilage matrix to assess phenotype maintenance [49], showed no obvious differences among groups, indicating preserved chondrocyte phenotype (Figure S6).

Hemocompatibility was assessed by hemolysis testing to account for potential blood contact during intra-articular injection. All groups exhibited excellent blood compatibility, with hemolysis rates well below the ISO 10993-4 threshold of 5% (Figures S7A and B), and the AT-EVs group showed the lowest hemolytic activity (< 0.5%). In addition, histopathological evaluation of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) from rats treated with AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG revealed no detectable morphological abnormalities or pathological lesions (Figure S8), further supporting the in vivo biosafety of this treatment. Collectively, these results demonstrate the excellent biocompatibility and biosafety of the AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel.

Dual-level anti-ROS performance of AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel: chemical radical scavenging and intracellular antioxidation

Excessive ROS in osteoarthritic (OA) chondrocytes contributes to disease progression by inducing matrix-degrading enzymes, suppressing ECM synthesis, and promoting chondrocyte apoptosis [50]. ROS also directly damages DNA, proteins, and lipids, leading to strand breaks, protein denaturation, and lipid peroxidation that compromises membrane integrity and cellular functions [51]. Importantly, previous study has highlighted that oxidative stress–induced lipid peroxidation can also impair EV membrane structure and bioactivity [27]. AT-EVs as natural lipid-membrane nanoparticles encapsulating bioactive nucleic acids and proteins, are highly vulnerable to elevated ROS levels. Therefore, incorporating antioxidant capacity into our AT-EVs delivery system is essential to simultaneously mitigate ROS-driven OA pathology and protect AT-EVs from oxidative damage. In this platform, EGCG is integrated into the BA-CS network via dynamic boronate ester and hydrogen-bond interactions, while AT-EVs are physically encapsulated. To dissect component-specific antioxidant contributions and potential synergy, we compared BA-CS/EGCG, AT-EVs, and the composite AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG.

Chemical radical scavenging assays demonstrated distinct and complementary antioxidant profiles between BA-CS/EGCG and AT-EVs (Fig. 3A–F). We first evaluated the rapid scavenging capacity against DPPH and ABTS radicals - two standard assays for characterizing antioxidant properties [52]. In the DPPH assay, BA-CS/EGCG and AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG induced a rapid color change from purple to pale yellow (Fig. 3A), a corresponding decrease in absorbance at 517 nm (Figure S9B), and achieved high DPPH scavenging efficiency (approximately ~80%; Fig. 3D), whereas AT-EVs alone showed minimal DPPH scavenging. Similar results were observed in the ABTS assay, where BA-CS/EGCG-containing samples led to marked decolorization (Fig. 3B) with a significant decrease in absorbance at 734 nm (Figure S9C), and ~90% ABTS scavenging (Fig. 3E), again outperforming AT-EVs alone. Beyond conventional DPPH and ABTS radical assessments, we investigated hydroxyl radical (•OH) scavenging - particularly crucial given •OH’s extreme reactivity and capacity to damage DNA, proteins, and cellular membranes [52, 53]. In •OH assays, AT-EVs exhibited substantially higher scavenging activity than BA-CS/EGCG (Fig. 3C, F), with significant absorbance reduction at 520 nm (Figure S9D). Notably, the composite AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG achieved the greatest •OH removal (Fig. 3F), exceeding either component alone, indicating a synergistic advantage in detoxifying biologically relevant ROS. We also evaluated H₂O₂ scavenging efficiency and found all three groups exhibited strong scavenging capacity, with the AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel demonstrating the strongest scavenging rate (70.4%) (Figure S9A). Collectively, these results suggest that BA-CS/EGCG primarily contributes broad-spectrum radical scavenging capacity (DPPH/ABTS), while AT-EVs provide additional efficacy against highly reactive •OH, together enabling a more comprehensive antioxidant profile in the composite.

The DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging capacities are primarily attributed to the BA-CS/EGCG. EGCG’s ortho-dihydroxyphenyl structure provides abundant phenolic hydroxyl groups, while the boronate ester-crosslinked 3D hydrogel network significantly enhances active site accessibility. The dynamic reversibility of these bonds enables sustained release and regeneration of antioxidant moieties, synergistically enhancing free radical scavenging [29]. The •OH and H₂O₂ scavenging capacities of AT-EVs are primarily derive from their intrinsic biochemical properties. The lipid components within the membrane structure of AT-EVs can effectively neutralize •OH through a passive quenching mechanism involving hydrogen abstraction [54, 55]. In addition, previous literature reports that various endogenous tissue-derived EVs, including those from adipose tissue, are enriched with catalase (CAT), suggesting that AT-EVs likely mediate direct catalytic decomposition of H₂O₂ through their internally abundant CAT [20, 56].

Building on these extracellular/chemical assays, we next evaluated whether the composite system could protect chondrocytes under oxidative stress. Chondrocytes were challenged with 1 mM H₂O₂ for 24 h and 48 h to mimic a pathological ROS microenvironment. Live/dead staining showed substantial cell death in the H₂O₂ + PBS group, whereas BA-CS/EGCG or AT-EVs alone provided partial protection; importantly, AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG most effectively preserved cell viability, with markedly increased live cells compared with PBS (Fig. 3G). CCK-8 assays further confirmed that H₂O₂ significantly reduced cell viability, while BA-CS/EGCG, AT-EVs, and especially AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG restored chondrocyte viability at both 24 h and 48 h (Fig. 3J). To directly assess intracellular ROS levels, DCFH-DA staining was performed. H₂O₂ stimulation induced strong DCF fluorescence, indicating pronounced intracellular ROS accumulation, whereas BA-CS/EGCG and AT-EVs each reduced fluorescence intensity. Consistent with the cytoprotection results, AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG produced the greatest reduction in intracellular ROS (Fig. 3H), which was corroborated by quantitative analysis (Fig. 3K). Finally, because maintenance of the chondrocyte phenotype depends on sustained synthesis of cartilage ECM components, particularly proteoglycans [57], Alcian blue staining was used to evaluate proteoglycan deposition under oxidative stress. H₂O₂ markedly diminished Alcian blue staining, whereas AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG most effectively restored proteoglycan deposition compared with BA-CS/EGCG or AT-EVs alone (Fig. 3I), supported by quantification of Alcian blue–positive area (Fig. 3L). Together, these results demonstrate that the AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel not only exhibits complementary and synergistic free radical scavenging capacity in chemical assays, but also effectively suppresses intracellular ROS, rescues chondrocyte viability, and preserves chondrocyte matrix-producing function under H₂O₂-induced oxidative stress.

Therapeutic modulation of chondrocyte function and macrophage polarization in an in vitro inflammatory microenvironment

Chronic inflammation fundamentally drives osteoarthritis (OA) progression, where sustained proinflammatory stimulation disrupts chondrocyte homeostasis. This dysregulation suppresses anabolic activity—marked by reduced COL2 expression—while activating catabolic pathways via matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) overexpression. Together, these shifts drive metabolic imbalance and extracellular matrix degradation [38]. Thus, an in vitro inflammatory model was developed to systematically evaluate the therapeutic potential of the AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel. An OA-related inflammatory chondrocyte was induced by pretreating chondrocytes with 10 ng/mL IL-1β for 24 h, with untreated chondrocytes used as the control group. As demonstrated in Fig. 4A and B, the AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG significantly reduced intracellular ROS generation compared to the PBS group (p < 0.01). RT-PCR analysis demonstrated that AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG treatment effectively restored expression of key cartilage anabolic genes type II collagen (COL2) and aggrecan (ACAN) in IL-1β-injured chondrocytes to near-normal levels, countering OA-related dysfunction (Fig. 4C and D). Concomitantly, the AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG significantly suppressed IL-1β-induced upregulation of catabolic mediators MMP3 and MMP13 (p < 0.01), confirming its capacity to inhibit extracellular matrix degradation (Fig. 4E and F). Western blot analysis further confirmed that AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel treatment upregulated COL2 protein expression while downregulating matrix metalloproteinase MMP3 and MMP13 protein levels in inflammatory chondrocytes, aligning with RT-PCR trends (Fig. 4G). While BA-CS/EGCG, AT-EVs, and AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG all reduced MMP3 and MMP13, AT-EVs alone exerted significantly weaker suppression compared to the composite hydrogel, with AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG showing a greater effect than either component alone (Fig. 4E-G). Collectively, the gene and protein expression changes in OA-related inflammatory chondrocytes demonstrate that AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG preserves chondrocyte function through anabolic/catabolic regulation, establishing its potential as an OA therapeutic strategy. Together, these data indicate that AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG partially restores cartilage matrix homeostasis in IL-1β–challenged chondrocytes by promoting anabolic marker expression while suppressing catabolic mediators.

Macrophages serve as pivotal sources of pro-inflammatory mediators (e.g., IL-1β, TNF-α) post-joint injury. Critically, macrophage activation exhibits a functional spectrum often described as: M1-polarized macrophages amplify inflammation and tissue damage, while M2-polarized macrophages promote tissue repair and inflammation resolution [58, 59]. In osteoarthritis, sustained M1 polarization dominates OA pathology, driving progressive cartilage degradation. To investigate the AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel’s effect on macrophage polarization, LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 macrophages were treated with PBS, BA-CS/EGCG, AT-EVs and AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG. Morphological analysis revealed that LPS-induced macrophages developed the flattened, irregular shape typical of M1 polarization, while the AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG group maintained a more rounded morphology resembling the unstimulated control (Fig. 4H). Flow cytometry quantification demonstrated that LPS potently triggered macrophage differentiation toward the M1 phenotype, elevating CD86⁺ cells by 38.1% versus controls (p < 0.01). Conversely, AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG treatment effectively curbed this shift, reducing the M1 population by 31.9% (p < 0.01). While AT-EVs (21.8% reduction, p < 0.01) and BA-CS/EGCG (10.78% reduction, p < 0.01) also suppressed M1 polarization, their effects were significantly weaker than the AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG (p < 0.01) (Fig. 4I and K). Notably, CD206⁺ M2 macrophages remained virtually undetectable (< 1.2%) in all conditions (Fig. 4J and L), suggesting that AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG primarily suppresses M1-like polarization rather than inducing an overt M2 shift under these conditions. RT-qPCR analysis revealed that AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG significantly downregulated the gene expression of M1 marker iNOS, further indicating the anti-inflammatory capacity (Figure S10). Supernatant analysis revealed LPS dramatically amplified secretion of pro-inflammatory mediators, with IL-6 and TNF-α levels markedly increased in the PBS group (p < 0.01), and AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG treatment suppressed these effector cytokines most effectively among the treatments (p < 0.01) (Fig. 4M and N), demonstrating potent inhibition of M1 macrophage-driven inflammation. These findings indicate that AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG mitigates LPS-induced inflammatory activation predominantly by dampening M1-like polarization and pro-inflammatory cytokine output, rather than inducing overt M2 polarization under the current experimental conditions.

Exploration of potential mechanisms of the AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel by RNA sequencing

To elucidate the therapeutic mechanisms of AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG in osteoarthritis chondrocytes, we performed RNA-seq comparing IL-1β-stimulated OA chondrocytes with OA chondrocytes treated by AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG. The transcriptomic profiling identified 638 differentially expressed genes (DEGs; 253 upregulated and 385 downregulated) meeting stringent criteria (q < 0.05, |log2FC| > 1), as visualized in the volcano plot (Fig. 5A). Hierarchical clustering of expression patterns revealed distinct transcriptional signatures between treatment groups, demonstrating robust modulatory effects (Fig. 5B). To gain deeper mechanistic insights into the affected biological functions, we conducted comprehensive Gene Ontology (GO) analysis and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA). GO analysis highlighted cartilage repair–related programs, including “extracellular matrix organization,” “cartilage development,” and “chondrocyte differentiation,” as well as stress/inflammation-associated terms such as “cellular response to hydrogen peroxide,” “DNA repair,” and “inflammatory response” (Fig. 5C and D). Notably, “negative regulation of PI3K/AKT signal transduction” was significantly enriched (Fig. 5C), which is consistent with our prior miRNA cargo analysis indicating that abundant OA-related miRNAs in AT-EVs potentially converge on the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis (Fig. 1H–I). GSEA further showed significant enrichment of extracellular matrix–associated gene sets in the AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG-treated group, including “extracellular matrix organization,” “positive regulation of collagen biosynthetic process,” and “extracellular matrix structural constituent” (Fig. 5E), supporting enhanced matrix anabolic potential. Collectively, GO and GSEA analyses indicate that AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG treatment is associated with transcriptional programs favoring ECM restoration while attenuating oxidative/inflammatory stress responses, consistent with our in vitro observations.

The PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway regulates fundamental cellular processes, and its aberrant activation under inflammatory stress is closely linked to OA-related catabolic signaling and cellular dysfunction [60]. To further define pathway-level changes, we performed KEGG enrichment analysis, which identified the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway among the significantly enriched pathways (Fig. 5F). Integrating miRNA cargo prediction, transcriptomic enrichment, and pathway analysis collectively supports PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling as a key node modulated by AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG. We next validated pathway activation by Western blot. As shown in Fig. 5G, IL-1β markedly increased PI3K, AKT, and mTOR phosphorylation, whereas AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG treatment substantially reduced these phosphorylation levels, restoring PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling toward baseline.

mTOR is a central negative regulator of autophagy, a fundamental cellular process that maintains energy balance and nutrient homeostasis. Impaired autophagy can elevate intracellular ROS and exacerbate mitochondrial dysfunction, thereby promoting chondrocyte apoptosis [61]. Consistent with this concept, multiple studies have reported that modulating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis can ameliorate osteoarthritis by restoring chondrocyte autophagy and limiting apoptosis [42, 62]. Moreover, mTOR-regulated autophagy is tightly coupled to inflammatory signaling: IL-1β stimulation rapidly activates PI3K/AKT and upregulates mTOR in human OA chondrocytes, mechanistically linking inflammatory cues to autophagy dysregulation [63]. In this context, our transcriptomic and pathway analyses, together with phospho-protein validation, indicate that AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG attenuates IL-1β–induced hyperactivation of PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling and shifts pathway activity toward baseline (i.e., a more physiological range). This normalization provides a plausible mechanistic basis for the observed protective phenotypes, suggesting that the hydrogel may mitigate OA-associated cellular stress by dampening inflammation-associated signaling and facilitating recovery of autophagy-related homeostasis.

Importantly, our current data support pathway-level normalization of PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling as a mechanistic node associated with the observed protective phenotypes. However, the specific upstream EV cargo components and direct molecular interactions responsible for this regulation remain to be defined. AT-EVs as a tissue-derived EV, contain multiple classes of bioactive cargo—including OA-related miRNAs identified by sequencing, as well as proteins and other functional molecules—which may act in a combinatorial and multi-target manner on PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling [14]. Accordingly, it is unlikely that the observed pathway regulation can be attributed to a single miRNA alone. Future studies will integrate multi-omics profiling (e.g., small RNA/proteomics coupled with target validation) with gain-/loss-of-function designs in vitro and in vivo to identify the key cargo components and signaling mediators responsible for PI3K/AKT/mTOR regulation. In parallel, mechanistic validation will include assessment of autophagy flux (e.g., LC3B-II/I conversion) and inflammatory signaling activity (e.g., NF-κB p65 nuclear translocation) to link pathway normalization to functional outcomes [64].

In vivo therapeutic effect of AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG on OA

To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG in osteoarthritis, we established an OA rat model followed by intra-articular administration. The in vivo experiments were divided into the following five groups (each group n = 5): sham-operated controls (Sham), PBS treated OA controls (PBS), BA-CS/EGCG treated OA group (BA-CS/EGCG), AT-EVs treated OA group (AT-EVs), and AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG treated OA group (AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG). Rat osteoarthritis (OA) models were surgically induced, followed by intra-articular injection therapy initiated 4 weeks post-surgery. After weekly treatments for four weeks, joint specimens were harvested for micro-CT analysis and subsequent histological staining (Fig. 6A). Micro-CT reconstructions and quantitative indices revealed characteristic OA changes in PBS controls, including joint space narrowing, osteophyte formation, and subchondral bone microarchitectural deterioration (Fig. 6B). In contrast, AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG treatment most effectively alleviated these structural changes, restoring joint space width to ~87% of sham (Fig. 6C), significantly reducing osteophyte volume (p < 0.01) (Fig. 6D), and improving trabecular bone parameters as reflected by increased BV/TV (Fig. 6E), supporting preservation of joint structural integrity.

In vivo antioxidative and macrophage-modulatory effects

Since oxidative stress is a pathological hallmark and key driver of osteoarthritis, and our in vitro studies demonstrated robust antioxidative capacity of the hydrogel, we next examined whether AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG mitigates oxidative damage in vivo. Because ROS are transient, oxidative stress was assessed using 8-hydroxy-2’-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG), a stable biomarker of oxidative DNA damage. Immunofluorescence staining showed markedly elevated 8-OHdG signals in PBS-treated OA cartilage, whereas BA-CS/EGCG and AT-EVs each reduced oxidative damage. Importantly, AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG produced the strongest suppression, approaching near-baseline levels (Fig. 8A and C), supporting a synergistic in vivo antioxidative effect consistent with the complementary radical-scavenging profiles observed in vitro.

Polarization of synovial macrophages was evaluated by immunofluorescence staining of iNOS (M1-like marker) and CD206 (M2-like marker). The AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG group exhibited significantly fewer iNOS⁺ cells than PBS controls (p < 0.01), indicating potent suppression of inflammatory macrophage activation in vivo (Fig. 8B and D). In contrast, CD206⁺ signals remained low across OA groups (Fig. 8B and E), suggesting that under the current disease stage and readouts, the treatment primarily attenuates M1-like polarization rather than inducing an overt M2-like shift.

Collectively, these in vivo results demonstrate that AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel provides superior chondroprotection by integrating structural preservation (micro-CT), cartilage matrix maintenance (histology/IHC), oxidative damage mitigation (8-OHdG), and suppression of inflammatory macrophage activation (iNOS). Together with the in vitro findings, these results support the feasibility and therapeutic efficacy of the rapidly prepared, clinically practical AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel platform for multifaceted OA treatment.

It should be noted that the main objective of this study was to establish and evaluate an integrated AT-EV-loaded hydrogel platform, rather than to comprehensively dissect the relative contribution of all adipose tissue-derived secretome components. This multiparameter design was intended to address the multifactorial pathogenesis of OA through ROS-responsive release, antioxidant protection, chondroprotection, and macrophage immunomodulation. Nevertheless, because AT-EVs were prepared using a size-based filtration/ultrafiltration workflow, minor contributions from trace co-isolated non-vesicular components cannot be fully excluded. Future mechanistic studies using additional purification strategies, such as size-exclusion chromatography or density-gradient separation, together with EV-depleted fraction controls, may further clarify the relative contributions of EV-associated and non-EV components to the observed therapeutic effects.

Conclusion

This work establishes human adipose tissue–derived extracellular vesicles (AT-EVs) as a clinically relevant EV source for osteoarthritis therapy, with the practical advantage of intraoperative isolation from lipoaspirate under aseptic operating-room conditions. Notably, this tissue-derived, surgery-integrated isolation strategy bypasses prolonged cell expansion and multi-step conditioning required for culture-derived MSC-EVs, thereby reducing manufacturing complexity and potential culture-associated variability. To enhance intra-articular retention and address the ROS-enriched OA microenvironment, we engineered an injectable, ROS-responsive AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel via dynamic boronate ester crosslinking between BA-CS and EGCG. This network enables pathology-adaptive co-delivery of EGCG and AT-EVs, while EGCG serves as both a crosslinker and an antioxidant component. Across in vitro and in vivo studies, AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG conferred robust chondroprotection by mitigating oxidative damage, preserving cartilage matrix homeostasis, suppressing inflammatory macrophage activation, and normalizing PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling toward a physiological range. Collectively, these findings support AT-EVs@BA-CS/EGCG hydrogel as a multifunctional intra-articular strategy and provide a translational framework for tissue-derived EV–based biomaterial therapeutics.
