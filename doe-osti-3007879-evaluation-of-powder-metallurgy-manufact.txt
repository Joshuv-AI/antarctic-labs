Summary:

This project evaluated the feasibility of nanostructured bainitic steel (NBS) for wind turbine gearbox
applications. NBS alloys show superior rolling-contact fatigue and wear performance and require only
minimal heat-treatment steps to form the desirable microstructure. However, despite the single-step heat
treatment, NBS takes longer to form the target microstructure, and the high carbon content of the alloy
requires careful processing to avoid cracking. To address the long heat-treatment time, our ORNL team
used an accelerated alloy design approach combining CALPHAD, machine learning, and data mining to
design new compositions that form nanostructured bainite with seven times faster kinetics. In this WETO
incubator work, we evaluated the processability of ORNL’s highly wear resistant NBS for gearbox
applications. The project had three goals: (i) assess alternative processing techniques for fabricating NBS
for gearbox applications; (ii) assess whether a powder metallurgy approach can fabricate NBS; and (iii)
determine the mechanical properties of powder metallurgy-processed NBS. We demonstrated that powder
metallurgy is a viable route to fabricate NBS and produced a gear prototype, indicating potential for large
scale, near-net shape production. We also explored Powder Metallurgy/Hot Isostatic Pressing (PM/HIP),
a scalable approach to fabricating NBS, with partial success within the available project funds.

Method:

The overall methodology of the project involved following steps:

 1. Lab scale powder atomization: This involved fabrication of powders of NBS alloy developed
 by ORNL. AMAZEMET rePowder ultrasonic atomizer (40 kHz) under Ar atmosphere at ORNL
 was used to produce small batches (60 g) of NBS powder.
 2. Developing Sintering Parameters: A CALPHAD thermodynamic approach was used to identify
 temperature windows to achieve maximum densification. Using the predicted temperature
 windows, and the atomized powders from step 1, sintering trials were conducted to identify
 temperature and time conditions that resulted in densification of powder particles.
 3. Large scale powder production: Once ideal sintering conditions were determined, large scale
 atomization was carried out via industrial partners to produce 100 Kg of NBS powders.
 4. Sintering of large-scale coupons: Optimized sintering conditions from step 2 were used to sinter
 larger coupons which enabled mechanical testing analysis.

Key Findings:

 1. Lab scale powder atomization: Figure 1 shows the obtained powder surface chemistry, powder
 morphology, and powder size distribution. Figure 1a and Figure 1b show that the powders are
 spherical and do not contain any satellites highlighting the efficacy of the ultrasonic atomization
 process. The EDS elemental maps in Figure 1c shows that the main elements, i.e., Fe, Si, Co, and
 Al are homogeneously distributed in the powder particles. Oxygen content in the powder
 (estimated by Inert Gas Fusion ASTM E 1019-18) was 0.011 wt.% indicating minimal oxidation
 during atomization process. The powder size distribution and the cumulative frequency of powder
 particles in Figure 1d shows that the powders have a d90 of 70 μm, d50 of 54 μm, d10 of 37 μm
 respectively.

 4. Sintering of large-scale coupons: Based on the learnings from lab scale sintering trials, sintering
 of large scale coupons (3inch length, 1 inch diameter cylinders) were carried out. Figure 5 shows
 the sintered coupons and the representative X-ray CT images. Representative porosity
 quantification shows that the fine powder lot sinters better, achieving a total density of 92%,
 while the coarse powder lot achieves 88%. Compared to the lab scale atomized powders, it can be
 seen that the density is relatively lower. It is hypothesized that the initial gas porosities in the
 powder reduced the final sintered density of the NBS component.

Following sintering, the sintered coupons were subjected to heat treatment involving austenitization at
1000 ⁰C for 1 hour followed by isothermal heat treatment at 200 ⁰C for 48 hours to produce
nanostructured bainite. Figure 6 shows the microstructure of the samples after heat treatment, and the
corresponding hardness achieved from the sintered coupons. It can be seen that both the microstructure
and hardness achieved via a powder-metallurgy route are comparable to those achieved via traditional
manufacturing approaches, which further indicates that powder metallurgy is a viable route to
manufacture NBS.

Challenges Encountered and lessons learned:

One of the key challenges encountered was the time to get large scale powders, considering the alloy is a
custom alloy. This resulted in delays in mechanical property evaluation like fatigue strength, tensile
strength, and wear of powder metallurgy produced NBS. Furthermore, the produced powder had gas
porosity, which we hypothesize reduced the achievable density in the as-sintered state. Regardless, the
proposed project demonstrated that NBS can be fabricated via powder metallurgy, which opens future
research directions, such as the use of other advanced manufacturing techniques, including binder-jet
additive manufacturing and Powder Metallurgy/Hot Isostatic Pressing (PM/HIP), which can produce
more complicated near-net-shape parts.

Key Accomplishments:

• Demonstrated the feasibility of atomizing pre-alloyed nanostructured bainite powders.
• Demonstrated powder metallurgy as a viable route to fabricate NBS for gearbox applications.
• Achieved sintered components with higher density and hardness than those produced by the
conventional route.
• Fabricated a small-scale gear prototype, demonstrating potential for scalability.
• Filed a provisional patent for the alloy used in this study.
