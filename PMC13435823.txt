Experiences and perceptions of occupational therapy and physiotherapy practice education in research settings
Authors: Amy Lonergan, Miia Rahja, Tamina Levy, Kate Laver
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPractice education is an essential component of occupational therapy programmes in Australia, providing supervised opportunities for students to develop skills across diverse practice settings. Rising student enrolments have intensified pressure on universities to secure sufficient practice education opportunities, prompting exploration of alternative models and emerging settings. The research setting, with supervision provided by occupational therapists working in research roles, presents a promising but under‐utilised option for practice education.ObjectiveThis study aimed to explore the perspectives and experiences of occupational therapy and physiotherapy students, practitioners, and university educators on placements in a research setting.MethodsSemi‐structured interviews (n = 30) were conducted with occupational therapy and physiotherapy students, practitioners with experience working in research‐specific roles, and university educators. The Theoretical Domains Framework guided the development of the interview schedules. Participants were recruited using purposeful and snowball sampling. Data were analysed thematically.Consumer and Community InvolvementThe interview schedule was developed in consultation with research practitioners and university educators.FindingsPractice education is occurring within research settings, though it is not yet common, with generally positive perceptions reported among those involved. Participants viewed these placements as an opportunity to strengthen research confidence, develop research readiness, and generate interest, alongside meeting core placement competencies. For some participants, practice education in a research setting was a new concept. University educators questioned whether all research settings could satisfy accreditation requirements. Seven themes were identified: research confidence, professional responsibility, sparking interest, missed opportunity, accreditation requirements, fear of missing out on client contact, and risks to the research quality.ConclusionUtilisation of the research setting for practice education may strengthen students' understanding of how evidence is generated and translated into practice, helping to address persistent concerns about low research confidence and engagement. Effective implementation will require collaboratively developed models that reflect the needs of all stakeholders. Ongoing trial and evaluation are essential to determine feasibility, educational value, and impact.Keywords: health occupations, occupational therapy, practice education, qualitative research, research placements

INTRODUCTION

Practice education is a mandatory component of occupational therapy training programmes in Australia. Practice education experiences, in the form of placements, offer opportunities for students to learn professional and clinical skills in real‐world settings under the supervision of a qualified practitioner. Placements may be completed in various practice settings with clients with different needs and circumstances across the lifespan and are an essential requirement for developing and assessing competence for practice (World Federation of Occupational Therapists, 2026). Between 2013 and 2022, the number of registered allied health professionals in Australia increased by 67% (from 108,700 to 180,900) (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare, 2024). The associated growth in student enrolments has contributed to a rising demand for practice education placements across all allied health professions, placing pressure on higher education providers to secure suitable learning opportunities (Hamilton et al., 2015). Alternative approaches to placement design and supervision (e.g., off‐site supervisor), new practice education models (e.g., inter‐professional clinics, student‐led clinics, and simulation), and emerging practice settings continue to be explored (Gustafsson et al., 2017).

Workforce data show that registered occupational therapists are primarily working in clinical settings (78.4%), with the remainder working in other roles, including research, education, and management (Australian Health Practitioner Regulation Agency & National Boards, 2022). Occupational therapists are employed in diverse research roles within various settings, including universities, research institutes, and health care. There is potential for practitioners in these research roles to offer supervision to students on placement. As with other practice settings, the aim of a placement in a research setting (or ‘research‐based placement’) is to facilitate the development of essential skills and professional competencies required for accreditation. Students may also benefit from increased exposure to see how research is generated and translated into practice (Dario & Simic, 2021). Despite an expectation that research is used to inform decision‐making and is a core part of professional roles, in practice, health‐care practitioners and students lack confidence with applying research skills and few are actively involved in research (Janssen et al., 2016; Smith et al., 2024). Research exploring occupational therapy students' and academics' perspectives on research‐based education has shown that students value research competence and view it as key to delivering best practice (Helgøy et al., 2020).

A recent scoping review investigating research training for occupational therapy and physiotherapy students found that the best way for students to learn basic research skills was through ‘hands‐on’ participation in research (Helgøy et al., 2022). Further, nursing and allied health studies have found that early exposure to research in practice may increase the potential of future research engagement (Pain et al., 2020), promote a stronger connection between the integration of research into practice (Dario & Simic, 2021; Lee et al., 2023), contribute to positive attitudes towards research (Smith et al., 2024), and encourage research as a potential career pathway (Gastle et al., 2023; Shree Chhetry et al., 2024).

High‐quality studies investigating the feasibility of student placements in research settings are scarce and even more limited for occupational therapy. The only identified occupational therapy example, published in 2010, illustrated how students enrolled at a South African university met their practice education requirements by participating in small‐scale research projects in an aged care setting (Du Toit et al., 2010). A systematic review exploring research placements failed to identify any examples from the field of occupational therapy (Zuchowski et al., 2020), and there is little guidance as to how to include students in the research setting (Schwartz & Smith, 2015). However, a recent study on the time use and activity of Australian occupational therapists and physiotherapists working in research settings found that the tasks commonly undertaken in these research roles could offer relevant learning opportunities to students on placements (Lonergan et al., 2025). Other studies describing cases of research‐based physiotherapy placements in Canada, Australia, and the United Kingdom have shown that students can successfully meet core learning requirements while also developing valuable research skills (Dario & Simic, 2021; Gastle et al., 2023; Minns Lowe et al., 2025; Wojkowski et al., 2017). Across these studies, students reported gaining insight into how research informs practice and, in some cases, this increased their motivation to engage in research in the future. Although Australian evidence remains limited for occupational therapy and the field is still emerging, these early local and international physiotherapy studies suggest that the research setting can offer adequate and relevant professional learning opportunities.

In summary, although early studies show encouraging results, there is still limited understanding of how key stakeholders perceive research‐based placements and how these placements are experienced in practice. This study aimed to address this gap by exploring the perspectives and experiences of occupational therapy and physiotherapy students, practitioners, and university educators on research‐based placements.

METHODS

Design

This qualitative study used semi‐structured interviews to investigate the perspectives of students, qualified practitioners, and university educators from disciplines of occupational therapy and physiotherapy. Given the scarcity of research available on this topic, a qualitative design enabled an in‐depth exploration of participant experiences and perceptions (Creswell & Poth, 2018). Ethical approval for this project was obtained from the Southern Adelaide Clinical Human Research Ethics Committee (216.21) and the Flinders University Human Research Ethics Committee (5018), South Australia, Australia. The consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) guided study reporting (File S1) (Tong et al., 2007).

Setting

Participants in this study were recruited from university institutions and health‐care settings across Australia.

Recruitment

Eligible participants were from one of three categories:

students: occupational therapy or physiotherapy students enrolled at an Australian university;

students: occupational therapy or physiotherapy students enrolled at an Australian university;

practitioners: Allied Health Practitioner Regulation Agency (Ahpra)‐registered occupational therapists and physiotherapists with experience working in a research role; or

practitioners: Allied Health Practitioner Regulation Agency (Ahpra)‐registered occupational therapists and physiotherapists with experience working in a research role; or

university educators: Ahpra‐registered, or equivalent, occupational therapists and physiotherapists affiliated with occupational therapy and physiotherapy university programmes.

university educators: Ahpra‐registered, or equivalent, occupational therapists and physiotherapists affiliated with occupational therapy and physiotherapy university programmes.

This study recruited participants from the disciplines of occupational therapy and physiotherapy because within the Australian health and education context, the two professions share similar accreditation frameworks and practice education models, including supervised block placements, exposure to diverse practice settings, and a focus on the development of entry‐level competencies (Australian Physiotherapy Council, 2025; Occupational Therapy Council of Australia Ltd, 2018). The sample size was informed by the study's qualitative design, exploratory nature, and practical constraints related to time and resources. A target of approximately 30 participants was considered appropriate to generate rich and meaningful data and was consistent with other qualitative studies employing semi‐structured interviews to explore allied health clinician and manager perspectives (Newington et al., 2021; Wenke et al., 2017) and student experiences (Menatnia et al., 2019; Whitehouse, 2017). Recruitment ceased once the research team determined that the data collected were sufficiently rich and relevant to address the research questions (Braun & Clarke, 2006).

Participants were identified using a combination of purposeful and snowball sampling. Recruitment was conducted through multiple channels, including social media platforms (e.g., X), university virtual noticeboards, and targeted email invitations distributed via the authors' professional networks. Individuals interested in participating were invited to contact the lead author directly. The participant information and consent form were provided to those who expressed interest, and informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection.

Data collection

Data were collected using semi‐structured interviews and a sociodemographic questionnaire. All interviews were completed one‐on‐one via video conferencing, which was the most feasible approach in the context of interstate recruitment. Data collected remotely have been found to generate similar themes and topics as in‐person data collection (Roberts et al., 2025).

Interview guide questions and prompts were informed by the 14 domains of the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) (Michie et al., 2005). The TDF provided a useful framework for systematically examining, from multiple perspectives, the factors that may influence student placements in research settings (e.g., students' knowledge of research and university educators' beliefs about consequences). The interview guide questions were tailored to each participant group (e.g., students, practitioners, and university educators). Discussion topics in each interview guide included general questions about experiences with placements to build rapport, as well as questions focussing on perceptions of and experiences with research‐based placements, potential challenges, perceived benefits, and strategies to support implementation. An example of an interview guide is provided in File S2.

The semi‐structured interview methodology allowed the interviewer to respond to ideas raised by the participants and follow up on information not previously considered (Taylor et al., 2015). The interview guide was not pilot tested. Instead, the last author (K. L.), an experienced qualitative researcher, reviewed the first two transcripts for relevance of the content, clarity of the questions, and the effectiveness of the interview technique in eliciting rich data. No modifications to the interview guide were deemed necessary following this review. All interviews were conducted by the lead author (A. L.; female). On commencement of each interview, participants were made aware of the motivation for this research and the characteristics of the lead author and interviewer. Some practitioners and university educators were known to the authors through professional work networks. However, there were no ongoing professional relationships and no perceived conflict of interest. No students were known to the lead author at the time of conducting the interviews.

Data analysis

Demographic data were summarised using descriptive statistics to provide a profile of participants. All interviews were audio recorded using a digital recorder and transcribed verbatim by a professional transcription service. Interview data were analysed thematically using inductive, thematic analysis, guided by Braun and Clarke's (2006) six‐phase approach. This involved (1) becoming familiar with the data, (2) generating codes, (3) searching for initial themes, (4) developing and reviewing themes, (5) refining, defining, and naming themes, and (6) writing the analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006). The first author (A. L.) listened to, read, and re‐read all transcripts (Step 1). Anonymised transcripts were transferred to qualitative research analysis software QSR NVivo (1.7.1) (QSR International Pty Ltd., 2020) for systematic, line‐by‐line coding (Step 2). Codes were assigned to as many topics as possible to capture meaningful aspects of the research question. For example, quotes from the transcripts ‘I do prefer the client interactions’ and ‘students really value the ones [placements] that have the face‐to‐face contact’ were represented by the code ‘direct, client work is a priority’. A reflective memo was used to document thoughts and ideas that arose during this process, which were subsequently discussed with the broader research team. Codes were reviewed and developed into initial themes and revised continuously (Step 3). For example, the codes ‘direct client work is a priority’ and ‘research is desk work’ were considered to represent the same major theme of ‘fear of missing out on client contact’. To manage for individual bias and enrich interpretation, 25% of interview transcripts were independently double coded by co‐authors K. L. and T. L. (Steps 1–3) and reviewed by co‐author M. R. Meetings were held with the authors to discuss data collection, coding, and theme development to define and name key themes and monitor for accuracy and relevance (Steps 4 and 5). These meetings provided opportunities to explore different experiences, perspectives, and assumptions. The topics discussed and decisions made were documented for reflexivity and transparency. Furthermore, individual quotes were selected to show the connection between the themes and data. The written analysis aimed to provide a rich, interpretive understanding of the data in relation to the research question (Step 6).

Positionality statement

This study was led by a PhD candidate (A. L.), a White, cisgender Australian who has professional experience working in traditional clinical roles, practice education, and clinical trial coordination. These roles directly informed and motivated the development of this research to explore the potential for practice education to occur more routinely in research settings. Other members of the research team included two occupational therapists (K. L. and M. R.) and a physiotherapist (T. L.), who all conduct research within clinical and academic settings. K. L., who has extensive research expertise, holds a joint appointment between a university and public health service with a focus on bringing together clinical practice, teaching, and research. T. L. has clinical, teaching, and research experience. One of the researchers (M. R.) was born outside of Australia. Some university educator and practitioner respondents came through the authors' professional networks and were known to the authors. However, there were no professional ongoing relationships.

Participant characteristics

Most student participants represented occupational therapy (n = 9; 82%) and were enrolled at six different universities across South Australia and Victoria (Table 1). Nine students (82%) had undertaken one or more placements at the time of the interview, whereas two students (18%) were awaiting their first placement experience.

Practitioner participants comprised equal numbers of occupational therapists (n = 5) and physiotherapists (n = 5). They had been working in their profession for an average of 20 years (range 5–40 years). The majority of participants (n = 8; 80%) reported previous experience supervising students for undergraduate or postgraduate placements. They were employed primarily within the public health system (n = 6) or university (n = 4). The practitioner participants' most recent research roles included research lead (n = 4, 40%), research fellow (n = 3, 30%), allied health research coordinator (n = 2, 20%), and research practitioner (n = 1, 10%).

University educator participants represented occupational therapists (n = 4; 44%) and physiotherapists (n = 5; 56%). They had been working in the profession for an average of 26 years (range 9–44 years). All participants reported previous experience with practice placements, and five participants had experience working in a role responsible for the coordination of occupational therapy and physiotherapy practice placements (e.g., ‘practicum leader’, ‘placement education coordinator’, and ‘professional practice coordinator’). Two university educators, from the United Kingdom and New Zealand, responded to the invitation on social media and were included because of their experience with the topic and the international relevance of practice placements in research settings.

Research confidence

Students' previous exposure to research coursework activities influenced their understanding of and feelings about research. They anticipated that the tasks involved in a research‐based placement would largely include reading journal articles, statistical analysis, and report writing. These tasks were not viewed favourably. Students were doubtful about their research skills and expressed concerns that they would not be able to meet the demands of a research setting.

I haven't had to do any academic writing, which was my biggest fear. (S08)

I don't think I would, because I don't think I'm the best at research …. So, if I did get put in an [research] area, I would probably be like 10 times more worried. (S03)

Practitioners and university educators shared similar workplace observations of colleagues experiencing low confidence and general discomfort with the idea of participating in research activities. A disconnect between research and practice had been noticed in clinical settings, and the implications of practitioners not engaging in research activity were of concern.

We are working with clinicians who are really scared of research. (E05)

Early and increased exposure to research was proposed as a potential strategy to positively influence perceptions of research, confidence, and future research engagement. Practitioners were hopeful that the negative perceptions of research could be demystified through participation.

And I think there's such a sense of, ‘Oh, I couldn't possibly do it. It's too hard.’ And then people don't start. But if you've had that exposure as an undergrad, and we know, don't we, that what students learn as an undergrad, it tends to be how they practice once qualified anyway. So maybe doing more research as a preregistration does make them do more research once they're qualified. Who knows? (E04)

Students who had undertaken placement in a research setting reported that the practical research exposure changed their initial perceptions of research. The tasks were more varied than anticipated, and they felt able to participate. After placement, they reported feeling more confident with research processes and had a better understanding of how to apply research skills learnt in the university setting to provide client care in traditional clinical roles.

I think learning about the whole, the background of research and how it's conducted. And obviously with uni I have gone through a lot of papers and stuff, but really understanding the context of how those papers are created, it was actually very, very useful …. (S01)

Professional responsibility

University educators and practitioners highlighted the importance of helping students to see how research can be ‘a part of normal clinical practice’ (E09). It was felt that this could be achieved by incorporating placements into research settings. For practitioners, the upskilling of future colleagues in foundational research skills and supporting students to make early connections between research and practice were key motivators for offering a research‐based placement.

… to show them [there is a] priority need to be involved in research, and [practitioners]should be involved in research and evidence‐based practice is core to being an effective OT. (C03)

I think it's really immersing people in the evidence and the processes around research to demystify it in a way and make it accessible for more clinicians to understand that research alongside your clinical roles is really quite possible and quite feasible. (C02)

Students expressed a strong sense of professional responsibility to develop research competence in order to provide best practice in their future work roles.

Because all of this work that's being done, essentially behind the scenes, is what informs our practice. That's, at the end of the day, where our evidence comes from …. (S08)

These sentiments were echoed by other participants who emphasised the strategic importance of research engagement for professional growth and service quality. Promoting readiness for participation in research generation and growing the evidence base of the profession were also highlighted as potential positive outcomes of research‐based placements.

We need to create research to find our place for our professions collectively, and drive quality care forward. And actually, you've got a chance to create new knowledge. And that's a selling point. As well as be a part of creating new knowledge that will provide longevity for services, and the place of allied health, and the profession moving forward. (E01)

I think just being actively involved in the research to improve the field of occupational therapy. That's really big. It's really important. (S07)

Sparking interest

Creating opportunities for students to learn about research roles and generate active interest in research was a key motivator for practitioners to offer a research‐based placement.

… one of the main intentions of the placement was to give the students experience in another sort of placement. So that research academic placement, because if we think about where OTs can go, there's so many different areas where OTs can go, but most of the placements tend to be clinical placements. (C04)

It was anticipated that direct experiences of research activity and roles, outside of the classroom setting, could better foster awareness of and interest in pursuing research opportunities in the future. Practitioners were not confident that students would seek out research opportunities on their own, as students or future practitioners.

So giving them that exposure because we know that's one of the big things to getting people into a research space, having that research exposure is really important. So, I think that's been a benefit for the placement. (C04)

… I hadn't considered that [research] as a possibility, to be a part of something I had hold in such high regard …. (S06)

Students spoke about the various options of placement setting, for example, community, rehabilitation, and mental health, but had limited awareness of research roles and the possibility of research‐based placements. Those who were allocated to a research setting expressed their surprise by this outcome and recalled being the only student allocated to a research setting.

I do think that it was an unexpected placement. (S08)

Students described practice placements as an opportunity to gain insight into different roles and suggested that placement experiences often influence career choices (Table 2). Using placements to provide research exposure was identified as a potential catalyst for students to increase research engagement in future practice and seek out specific research opportunities on graduation.

And I think that bit of exposure might spark a light in someone in thinking, ‘Maybe this is something that I want to head into.’ (S04)

Students acknowledged that although placements occurring in research settings may not appeal to all students, they were confident the option would interest some.

I definitely think it is a hundred percent worth doing and worth pursuing, but I also think that there's that level of anticipating that it's probably not going to be a popular option for everyone. I definitely think that a lot of people are going to want to do the standard clinical placements, but you'll still get those few people that would be really keen on it. (S10)

Missed opportunity

University educators spoke about the ongoing pressure to source suitable practice placements for growing student numbers (Table 2) and the constant search for more supervisors and settings to host students. However, research settings were rarely considered during placement planning. The university educators noted that they had not been approached by researchers to request or support student placements. They also admitted that they had not taken steps to initiate contact with researchers themselves.

Haven't been approached by any researchers for that interest …. That's not really an avenue that I've pursued. (E06)

Some university educators considered the lack of exploration on their behalf could be attributed to the perceived emerging nature and expansion of different research roles.

I feel like the clinical research that's happening has grown. I don't feel like in 10 years ago, that OT were really doing this kind of research or it wasn't as visible to me … I just don't think it'd be on our radar until now. (E02)

Similarly, those practitioners without direct experience supervising a student in a research setting said they had not considered supervising students in their research role and had never been approached to do so (Table 2). A lack of precedent and invitation were described as barriers. One practitioner attributed the absence of students in research settings to longstanding placement practices within their hospital setting whereby student allocations followed established pathways, driven by discipline directors and practice educators. Collectively, they recognised this gap as a missed opportunity for fostering new student placement collaborations.

… I think people just haven't considered it before because there were already those processes in place to take the students in the places that they've always been. (C01)

Accreditation requirements

There were mixed perspectives about the suitability of research settings for practice placements. University educators, including those who had reported experiences of successful research‐based placements, remained cautious about the potential for research settings to provide the required opportunities for students to meet their core competencies, as guided by the accreditation standards. Specifically, they questioned whether sufficient client contact would be offered in the research setting. It was suggested that more information about each individual research setting would be needed before endorsing a research‐based placement.

And if you're doing this idea of a placement and you're being embedded in a team that's not involved in the provision of clinical services, I mean, we're still giving them something of value, but I can see that it would be a shortfall for accreditation. (E09)

Flexible and creative placement design, within the limits of the accreditation requirements, was noted by some university educators as essential to support emerging settings.

… our accrediting body is, what my understanding is, that they are outcomes‐focused, which means they've got various criteria, and we need to demonstrate that we can produce practice, entry‐level, graduate‐ready, physiotherapy clinicians into the workforce. But how we go about achieving that, is open for each university to structure. (E01)

Students and practitioners who had engaged in a research‐based placement reported that the experiences contributed to the development of core skills as outlined in the student placement assessment tool (Table 2). Practitioners provided examples of extensive and considered placement preparation to make the placement both meaningful and relevant.

… we were able to do some work arounds and find some different ways of doing things and even tapping into the clinical team around finding some experiences that would still be meaningful, and support them to demonstrate and practice the skills they needed too in their clinical placement. (C04)

Yet, despite thorough planning, one instance was described in which strong views about the research setting's inability to meet learning needs led to the placement offer being declined.

So, we were all set for this one particular student. And because of the way it was set up, we had to approach her [university] manager about the placement. And her manager went, ‘That's not a real placement …. There's no transferable skills.’ Said no and shut the whole thing down. (E03)

Not all practitioners held the same degree of confidence to plan a placement in their research setting and wanted support from the university to ensure all stakeholder needs would be addressed.

I think a understanding of what the expectation from the uni is, and what the student will be being marked on, I think that would be fundamental if we wanted to select what we would do with the student … we definitely want to make sure that what we're doing is valuable and in line with what the student needs to be learning as dictated from the university. (C10)

Fear of missing out on client contact

Students anticipated that a research‐based placement would involve more indirect, desk‐based work and offer fewer client interactions when compared with more traditional placement settings. Contact with clients was noted to be a strong motivator for their decision to enrol in an occupational therapy or physiotherapy programme. The possibility of missing out on client‐facing opportunities created apprehension about how enjoyable a research‐based placement would be and if placement learning would be relevant to their preferred future work roles.

I think a research one [placement] would be a little bit like, ‘Am I going to learn as much as I would on the floor [i.e. a traditional clinical setting]?’ (S11)

I sort of came into physio wanting to probably be a bit more hands on rather than the research stuff …. (S09)

Students who had participated in a research‐based placement reflected on their initial concerns that there would be a lack of client interactions and the potential for this to negatively impact their ability to address placement competencies. However, their placement design offered adequate client‐facing activity as part of or in combination with research activity.

Initially I was a bit bummed about it, because in my head it would just be all paperwork … but it ended up being more face to face than expected. (S01)

I had a little moment of like, ‘Oh, my gosh.’ It's not traditionally face‐to‐face with the client, so how are you going to meet some of those key competencies … it's so much face‐to‐face that it wasn't as different as I thought it was going to be. I wasn't stuck in a room behind a computer, or anything like that, that the word ‘research’ has hanging over it. (S08)

Practitioners were aware of student preferences for client contact and considered this during placement planning. They emphasised the importance of helping students to draw connections between skills developed while participating in research activities, with skills required for more traditional clinical practice.

So, then I had to switch tack a little bit and talk about what skills they would get in my setting that would translate to the hospitals they'd be working in. (C05)

Risks to the research quality

Designing the placement so that students could make a positive contribution to research activity was identified as a potential strategy to balance time and resource demands and sustain the provision of placements.

That's something that my manager's been aware of. How can we maximize the resource of having students, given the huge resource that goes into supporting the placements? (C03)

One practitioner summarised their thinking around whether a student could be facilitated:

It comes back to the tasks that you've had, so I'd need to do a fair bit of thinking about that and what would the next task be. I'd have to make sure that I had a rather specific thing to achieve in that time and would it be appropriate for a student to do. (C09)

When reflecting on their experiences supervising students in the research setting, practitioners weighed up the commitment of time and resources, with the importance of providing such placements.

… I do remember it [research placement preparation] being quite an important but significant undertaking, because I did need to allocate a fair bit of time to it. But I think because we'd had the clear objectives and goals investing that time, it was well spent because we got the output at the end that we wanted from it. (C09)

Several concluded that placement design meant that the outputs generated by the students were more than if they did not have a student.

I needed some additional support with our data entry. It was a huge thing that I often don't get done for myself. Having that student resource to plug in a couple 100 data set for me while they were stuck at home [remote placement during COVID‐19], was a huge help. (C03)

However, there were some practitioners and university educators who did not feel ready to trust students with research tasks. They identified the potential for errors and a negative impact on research quality as key concerns.

Well, as a researcher, your main focus is the quality of data collection … you want to know that it's done with integrity and it's done with precision accuracy so that you've got valid data …. (E08)

But with this ICU study at the moment, I'm actually doing all the data entry for that. Because it's my baby, I want to know that the data's a hundred percent accurate … I wouldn't trust many people to do it. (C06)

DISCUSSION

This study explored the perceptions and experiences of practice placements in research settings from the perspectives of key stakeholders, namely, students, practitioners, and university educators, from disciplines of occupational therapy and physiotherapy. Firsthand experiences of research‐based placements were described and largely positive. For some, research‐based placements were a completely new concept, not previously considered. The results of this study suggested that opportunities to engage in research, like research‐based placements, can enhance students' research knowledge and confidence. The results also revealed that research‐based placements could support better integration of research into practice and foster awareness of research activity and research careers in health care. Several challenges of these placements were identified, including students' low confidence with research tasks, a general lack of knowledge about research roles, and uncertainty about whether the activity offered on research‐based placements would meet accreditation requirements.

In this study, students reported low confidence in their ability to engage in research‐related activity, despite valuing research skills and aspiring to be evidence‐based practitioners. Many students were hesitant to undertake a research‐based placement because they felt underprepared for the potential tasks, echoing wider findings that physiotherapy and nursing students and clinicians tend to view research as overly complex, too academic, and beyond their capability (Gastle et al., 2023; Janssen et al., 2016; Whitehouse, 2017). Thus, even if opportunities exist to develop desired research skills and confidence, self‐doubt and low confidence may impact students' uptake of opportunities. These perceptions of research being too hard often persist into clinical practice, reducing future research engagement (Janssen et al., 2016). Evidence from a recent scoping review showed that research activities outside of the classroom setting improved occupational therapy and physiotherapy students' understanding of research and strengthened connections between research theory and research in practice (Helgøy et al., 2022). Further, several allied health and nursing studies have reported that after a research‐based placement, students described an improved understanding of research processes and made stronger connections between research theory and practice (Dario & Simic, 2021; Smith et al., 2024; Whitehouse, 2017; Zuchowski et al., 2020). As such, the provision of research‐based placements could be a strategy to address low confidence and hesitancy in engaging with research, build research competence in future practitioners, and mitigate the risks associated with a workforce with low research engagement.

A limited awareness of research activity, research‐specific roles, and career pathways, for example, enrolling in a PhD, was described by students in this study and is consistent with physiotherapy and nursing research (Newington et al., 2021; Whitehouse, 2017). This is not surprising given there are still small numbers of allied health practitioners working in research‐specific roles (Department of Health Disability and Ageing, 2026). Practitioners in this present study identified research‐based placements as a strategy to increase visibility of research and generate interest in moving along the research continuum from consumers of research to roles that would align with research generation. An Australian survey calculating predictive factors of future research interest of allied health professionals found that applied research experience was associated with an increase in the likelihood of future research engagement (Pain et al., 2020). Additionally, multiple studies from the fields of nursing, social work, and physiotherapy confirm the positive impact of exposure to research on placement on future engagement, reporting outcomes, including an increased interest in engaging in research activity on graduation (Kennel et al., 2009; Shree Chhetry et al., 2024; Wojkowski et al., 2017), employment in research teams (Gastle et al., 2023), and a significant increase in student interest in postgraduate research programmes (Menzies et al., 2021). Collectively, these findings suggest that research‐based placements may be an effective strategy for increasing visibility of research opportunities and roles, promoting interest and readiness to participate in future research opportunities, and growing the research capacity of the occupational therapy profession.

The many examples of successful research‐based placements occurring in practice education were an unexpected finding. Extensive placement planning, to identify opportunities for students to learn and demonstrate core competencies, was recognised as essential for the success of these placements. However, university educators anticipated a lack of client contact in these settings and therefore were less confident than practitioners that, even with extensive planning, all research settings would meet accreditation requirements. This perception has also been identified in the literature whereby, prior to their research‐based placement, physiotherapy and nursing students anticipated that activity would not offer as much client contact when compared with a traditional placement and, therefore, would not be as worthwhile (Gastle et al., 2023; Whitehouse, 2017). Yet, post‐research‐based placement, physiotherapy and social work students reported more client contact than expected, sufficient learning opportunities, and an enjoyable experience (Gastle et al., 2023; Lomas et al., 2023). This was also a finding in this present study. A recent exploratory study mapping the tasks of occupational therapists and physiotherapists in research roles to discipline‐specific placement evaluation tools showed how the activity of practitioners in these roles can align readily with student placement competencies (Lonergan et al., 2025). However, the potential for and implications of a lack of client‐facing activity were also highlighted by Lonergan et al. (2025) and in this present study, drawing attention to the importance of preplacement preparation to identify and plan for both opportunities and potential gaps. A collaborative approach to placement design is necessary, whereby university placement coordinators support supervisors to map available activity to placement evaluation tools and proactively address potential learning gaps. Although this work would likely only yield a small number of additional placements, it could enable research‐based placements to be offered as an appropriate, contemporary placement option, alongside more traditional settings.

Strengths and limitations

A strength of this study includes the research design, which enabled rich and detailed insights to be captured to address the study aims. The inclusion of three different stakeholder groups was also a strength. Interviews were conducted with participants with a diverse range of experiences, including many with direct experience with the research‐based placements. Data analysis was undertaken by multiple authors with a range of professional and personal experiences and included collaborative coding and peer debriefing.

Participants for this study were only from metropolitan locations and did not include representation from all Australian states and territories, which is a limitation. Thus, the findings may reflect only the experiences of those who were included and limit application of results to the wider population. Furthermore, whereas practitioners and university educators were represented equally by occupational therapy and physiotherapy disciplines, physiotherapy students were under‐represented. Another limitation is the potential under‐representation of stakeholders in leadership positions who are responsible for approving the inclusion of students in research projects and the inclusion of new placement settings (e.g., principal investigator and head of occupational therapy programme). It is possible that their perspectives may have differed from those captured in this study.

Implications for future research

Placements in research settings offer an alternative setting for students to develop and demonstrate mandatory placement competencies. Placement planning is essential to addressing the needs of key stakeholders as identified in this study. Further studies may focus on a single discipline examination of the utilisation of research settings for placements, according to specific discipline requirements, and consider participant experience with practice education in research settings as a key inclusion criterion. The perspectives of key decision‐makers in leadership positions should be explored. Further trial, evaluation, and reporting of these placements is needed to identify their feasibility, educational value, and impact.

CONCLUSION

This study has provided information about the experiences and perceptions of research‐based placements from the perspectives of occupational therapy and physiotherapy students, practitioners, and university educators. The findings indicate that the research setting offers a contemporary placement option for occupational therapy and physiotherapy programmes in Australia. Placement in this setting could strengthen students' understanding of and engagement with research, build confidence and capacity, and contribute to the development of interested, research‐ready graduates. To address accreditation requirements, support placement supervisors, and promote placement uptake among students, co‐design of placements between practitioners, university educators, and students is necessary. This study should motivate university programmes and occupational therapists to work together to develop the research setting into a standard placement option.
