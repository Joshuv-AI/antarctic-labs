A Comparative Study of Projected and Unprojected Schemes for Micromagnetic Simulations
Changjian Xie

Abstract
In micromagnetic simulations, the constant magnitude of the magnetization can be derived from the continuity equation. Since the time evolution of the magnetization in the continuity equation is perpendicular to the plane determined by the magnetization and the effective field, taking the inner product of both sides of the model with the magnetization shows that the evolution rate of the magnitude of the magnetization is zero, thus keeping the magnitude constant. From this perspective, the equation itself can maintain the constraint of constant magnetization magnitude. We discretized the continuity equation and compared two first-order semi-implicit strategies in time: one is the implicit Gauss-Seidel method, and the other is the semi-implicit Backward Differentiation Formula (BDF) method. We considered the comparison between these two schemes with and without the projection step. The results of micromagnetic simulations show that when the dissipation coefficient is large, the implicit Gauss-Seidel method without the projection step has significant differences from the method with the projection step in both the achieved steady state and domain wall motion. When an appropriate dissipation coefficient is selected, the difference between the two narrows, and both the steady state and domain wall motion can be simulated. For the other method, BDF1, whether the dissipation coefficient is large or small, the results with and without the projection step are quite consistent, and it can effectively simulate the domain wall motion.

Introduction

The temporal evolution of magnetization in ferromagnetic materials is fundamentally governed by the Landau-Lifshitz-Gilbert (LLG) equation
8
,
5
.
Such an equation forms a vector-valued nonlinear system subject to a pointwise constant-magnitude constraint on the magnetization vector. Extensive research on preserving the constraint has been devoted to developing efficient and numerically stable algorithms for micromagnetic simulations, see references
7
,
4
and therein. Although numerous studies have investigated constraint-preserving algorithms for the LLG equation, see
10
,
3
,
9
,
6
, and
1
, such methods are not directly derived from the governing equation itself. Instead, starting from the requirement of norm preservation, they typically introduce nonlinear projection steps, adopt Lagrange multipliers, or embed the constraint indirectly into the discrete formulation.

Starting from the equation, in fact, we should not impose any constraints. We compared two typical methods: one is the Gauss-Seidel projection method, and the other is the semi-implicit projection method based on BDF1. Both types of methods apply a nonlinear projection step at the end, which brings additional difficulties to the mathematical analysis. From the perspective of engineering applications, the extent of the difference between the projection and non-projection methods in numerical simulations has not yet been studied. People usually consider designing numerical methods that satisfy the normalization condition, and it is also particularly important in applications to simply discretize the original continuity equation. In this paper, we present various results of micromagnetic simulations and apply them to domain wall motion. Starting from energy stability, we attempt to prove the energy stability of the non-projection method and simulate the energy curves under various dissipation conditions.

2
The LLG equation

The micromagnetic equation integrates gyromagnetic precession and dissipative relaxation
8
,
2
. Its nondimensionalized model is given by

\displaystyle{\mbox{\boldmath$m$}}_{t}=-{\mbox{\boldmath$m$}}\times{\bm{h}}_{\text{eff}}-\alpha{\mbox{\boldmath$m$}}\times({\mbox{\boldmath$m$}}\times{\bm{h}}_{\text{eff}}),

(1)

where
\partial_{t}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}
is the time derivative for the magnetization and
{\bm{h}}_{\text{eff}}
is the term from the energy variation, say

\bm{h}_{\text{eff}}=-\delta E[\mbox{\boldmath$m$}]/\delta\mbox{\boldmath$m$}
. In this paper, we take the free energy below,

\displaystyle E[\mbox{\boldmath$m$}]=\epsilon\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\mbox{\boldmath$m$}|^{2}\;dx+Q\int_{\Omega}(m_{2}^{2}+m_{3}^{2})\;dx-\int_{\Omega}\mbox{\boldmath$h$}_{s}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$m$}\;dx-\int_{\Omega}\mbox{\boldmath$h$}_{e}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$m$}\;dx,

(here
Q
and
\epsilon
are nondimensionalized parameters) and get the effective field below,

\displaystyle{\bm{h}}_{\text{eff}}=\epsilon\Delta\mbox{\boldmath$m$}-Q(m_{2}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2}+m_{3}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{3})+\mbox{\boldmath$h$}_{s}+\mbox{\boldmath$h$}_{e},

where
\epsilon\Delta\mbox{\boldmath$m$}
is the exchange field,
-Q(m_{2}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2}+m_{3}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{3})
is anisotropy field,
\mbox{\boldmath$h$}_{s}
is the stray field and
\mbox{\boldmath$h$}_{e}
is the applied external field.

This equation is subject to the homogeneous Neumann boundary condition

\frac{\partial{\mbox{\boldmath$m$}}}{\partial{\bm{\nu}}}\Big|_{\partial\Omega}=0,

(2)

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
d=1,2,3
) denotes the bounded spatial domain occupied by the ferromagnetic material, and
\bm{\nu}
represents the unit outward normal vector on the domain boundary
\partial\Omega
. It is noteworthy that this boundary condition is physically consistent for isolated ferromagnetic systems, as it inherently ensures the absence of magnetic surface charge—an essential prerequisite for accurately modeling unperturbed magnetic dynamics.

To achieve a comprehensive understanding of the LLG equation, it is essential to elucidate the physical essence of its key components. The magnetization field
\mbox{\boldmath$m$}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a three-dimensional vector field subject to the pointwise constraint
|\mbox{\boldmath$m$}|=1
—a fundamental characteristic derived from the quantum mechanical alignment of electron spins in ferromagnetic materials. With respect to the right-hand side of
eq.

1
, the first term describes the gyromagnetic precession effect, whereby magnetic moments undergo precessional motion around the effective magnetic field
\bm{h}_{\text{eff}}
. The second term represents dissipative relaxation, where the parameter
\alpha>0
stands for the dimensionless Gilbert damping coefficient, which quantifies the rate of energy transfer from the magnetic subsystem to the lattice structure. Consequently, the model
eq.

1
inherently satisfies the norm-preserving constraint.

Gauss-Seidel projection method

The Gauss-Seidel projection method (GSPM) is given as the following three steps:

Step 1. Implicit Gauss-Seidel:

\displaystyle g_{i}^{n}

\displaystyle=(I-\epsilon\Delta t\Delta_{h})^{-1}(m_{i}^{n}+\Delta tf_{i}^{n}),

(3)

\displaystyle g_{i}^{*}

\displaystyle=(I-\epsilon\Delta t\Delta_{h})^{-1}(m_{i}^{*}+\Delta tf_{i}^{n}),\quad i=1,2,3

(4)

\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}^{*}\\
m_{2}^{*}\\
m_{3}^{*}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}^{n}+(g_{2}^{n}m_{3}^{n}-g_{3}^{n}m_{2}^{n})\\
m_{2}^{n}+(g_{3}^{n}m_{1}^{*}-g_{1}^{*}m_{3}^{n})\\
m_{3}^{n}+(g_{1}^{*}m_{2}^{*}-g_{2}^{*}m_{1}^{*})\end{pmatrix}.

(5)

Step 2. Heat flow without constraints:

\bm{f}^{*}=-Q(m_{2}^{*}\bm{e}_{2}+m_{3}^{*}\bm{e}_{3})+\bm{h}_{s}^{n}+\bm{h}_{e},

m_{3}^{**}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}^{*}+\alpha\Delta t(\epsilon\Delta_{h}m_{1}^{**}+f_{1}^{*})\\
m_{2}^{*}+\alpha\Delta t(\epsilon\Delta_{h}m_{2}^{**}+f_{2}^{*})\\
m_{3}^{*}+\alpha\Delta t(\epsilon\Delta_{h}m_{3}^{**}+f_{3}^{*})\end{pmatrix}.

(7)

Step 3. Projection onto

\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}^{n+1}\\
m_{2}^{n+1}\\
m_{3}^{n+1}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{|m^{**}|}\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}^{**}\\
m_{2}^{**}\\
m_{3}^{**}\end{pmatrix}.

(8)

where
\bm{m}^{*}
denotes the intermediate values of
\bm{m}
. The stray field
\bm{h}_{s}
is computed using the intermediate values
\bm{m}^{*}
in (
11
) and (
12
).

3.2
Semi-implicit projection methods

The BDF1 projection method is given below,

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\tilde{\bm{m}}_{h}^{n+1}-\bm{m}_{h}^{n}}{k}&=-\bm{m}_{h}^{n}\times\left(\epsilon\Delta_{h}\tilde{\bm{m}}_{h}^{n+1}+\bm{f}_{h}^{n}\right)\\
&\quad-\alpha\bm{m}_{h}^{n}\times\left(\bm{m}_{h}^{n}\times\left(\epsilon\Delta_{h}\tilde{\bm{m}}_{h}^{n+1}+\bm{f}_{h}^{n}\right)\right),\\
\bm{m}_{h}^{n+1}&=\frac{\tilde{\bm{m}}_{h}^{n+1}}{\left|\tilde{\bm{m}}_{h}^{n+1}\right|},\end{aligned}\right.

4
Typical methods without projection

4.1
Gauss-Seidel without projection method

The Gauss-Seidel without projection method (GSnoPM) is given as the following three steps:

Step 1. Implicit Gauss-Seidel:

\displaystyle g_{i}^{n}

\displaystyle=(I-\epsilon\Delta t\Delta_{h})^{-1}(m_{i}^{n}+\Delta tf_{i}^{n}),

(9)

\displaystyle g_{i}^{*}

\displaystyle=(I-\epsilon\Delta t\Delta_{h})^{-1}(m_{i}^{*}+\Delta tf_{i}^{n}),\quad i=1,2,3

(10)

\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}^{*}\\
m_{2}^{*}\\
m_{3}^{*}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}^{n}+(g_{2}^{n}m_{3}^{n}-g_{3}^{n}m_{2}^{n})\\
m_{2}^{n}+(g_{3}^{n}m_{1}^{*}-g_{1}^{*}m_{3}^{n})\\
m_{3}^{n}+(g_{1}^{*}m_{2}^{*}-g_{2}^{*}m_{1}^{*})\end{pmatrix}.

(11)

Step 2. Heat flow without constraints:

\bm{f}^{*}=-Q(m_{2}^{*}\bm{e}_{2}+m_{3}^{*}\bm{e}_{3})+\bm{h}_{s}^{n}+\bm{h}_{e},

(12)

\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}^{n+1}\\
m_{2}^{n+1}\\
m_{3}^{n+1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}^{*}+\alpha\Delta t(\epsilon\Delta_{h}m_{1}^{n+1}+f_{1}^{*})\\
m_{2}^{*}+\alpha\Delta t(\epsilon\Delta_{h}m_{2}^{n+1}+f_{2}^{*})\\
m_{3}^{*}+\alpha\Delta t(\epsilon\Delta_{h}m_{3}^{n+1}+f_{3}^{*})\end{pmatrix}.

(13)

where
\bm{m}^{*}
denotes the intermediate values of
\bm{m}
. The stray field
\bm{h}_{s}
is computed using the intermediate values
\bm{m}^{*}
in (
11
) and (
12
).

For brevity, we take
\epsilon=1
and
\mbox{\boldmath$f$}_{h}^{n}=0
and for the step 1, we note that

\displaystyle\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{*}-\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n}}{k}=-\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{s}\times\Delta_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$g$}_{h}^{s},

(14)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$g$}_{h}^{s}=(I-k\Delta_{h})^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{s}
,
s=n,*
. The linear stability analysis can not work. However, we notice that for Gauss-Seidel iteration method,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}1&&\\
-g_{3}^{n}&1&\\
g_{2}^{*}&-g_{1}^{*}&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}^{*}\\
m_{2}^{*}\\
m_{3}^{*}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-g_{3}^{n}&g_{2}^{n}\\
&1&-g_{1}^{*}\\
&&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}^{n}\\
m_{2}^{n}\\
m_{3}^{n}\end{pmatrix}

(15)

If
g
is a constant vector, we can do the spectral analysis for matrix
A
of
\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}=A\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n}
. However, here, the matrix depends on the previous
\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{*}
. The exact stability property of the scheme
eq.

15
is difficult to analyze. Numerical experimentation suggests that the scheme is unconditionally stable.

Noting that

\displaystyle\frac{g_{3}^{n}-m_{3}^{n}}{k}=\Delta_{h}g_{3}^{n},\quad\frac{g_{2}^{n}-m_{2}^{n}}{k}=\Delta_{h}g_{2}^{n},

We can derive that
\|\nabla_{h}g_{3}^{n}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|\nabla_{h}m_{3}^{n}\|_{2}^{2}
and
\|\nabla_{h}g_{2}^{n}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|\nabla_{h}m_{2}^{n}\|_{2}^{2}
. Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{h}g_{1}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|\nabla_{h}m_{1}^{*}\|_{2}^{2},\quad\|\nabla_{h}g_{2}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|\nabla_{h}m_{2}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}.

say, we have
\|\nabla_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$g$}_{h}^{s}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|\nabla_{h}m_{h}^{s}\|_{2}^{2}
.
We take inner product with
\Delta_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$g$}_{h}^{s}
, we get

\displaystyle RHS=(-\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{s}\times\Delta_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$g$}_{h}^{s},\Delta_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$g$}_{h}^{s})=0,

and

\displaystyle LHS=\frac{1}{k}(\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{*}-\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n},\Delta_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$g$}_{h}^{s})=-\frac{1}{k}(\nabla_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{*}-\nabla_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n},\nabla_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$g$}_{h}^{s}),

Thus,
we can prove that

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|\nabla_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n}\|_{2}^{2}.

For step 2, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}-\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{*}}{k}=\alpha\Delta_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}.

We take the inner product with
-\Delta_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}
, and have

\displaystyle LHS

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}(\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}-\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{*},-\Delta_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2k}\left(\|\nabla_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\nabla_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}-\nabla_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}-\|\nabla_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}\right),

and

\displaystyle RHS=-\alpha(\Delta_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1},\Delta_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1})=-\alpha\|\Delta_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}\|_{2}^{2}\leq 0.

We thus have

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|\nabla_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}.

4.2
Semi-implicit without projection method

The BDF1 without projection method is given below,

\displaystyle\frac{{\bm{m}}_{h}^{n+1}-\bm{m}_{h}^{n}}{k}

\displaystyle=-\bm{m}_{h}^{n}\times\left(\epsilon\Delta_{h}{\bm{m}}_{h}^{n+1}+\bm{f}_{h}^{n}\right)-\alpha\bm{m}_{h}^{n}\times\left(\bm{m}_{h}^{n}\times\left(\epsilon\Delta_{h}{\bm{m}}_{h}^{n+1}+\bm{f}_{h}^{n}\right)\right).

(16)

For such a BDF1 method, we can give a stability analysis below, for brevity, we take
\epsilon=1
and
\mbox{\boldmath$f$}_{h}^{n}=0
and take inner product with
-\Delta_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}
, and obtain

\displaystyle LHS

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}(\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}-\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n},-\Delta_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2k}\left(\|\nabla_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\nabla_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}-\nabla_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n}\|_{2}^{2}-\|\nabla_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n}\|_{2}^{2}\right),

and

\displaystyle RHS

\displaystyle=\alpha\left(\Delta_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1},\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n}\times(\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n}\times\Delta_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1})\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha\left(\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n}\times\Delta_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1},\Delta_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}\times\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n}\right)=-\alpha\|\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n}\times\Delta_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}\|_{2}^{2}\leq 0.

We then have

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|\nabla_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n}\|_{2}^{2}.

If
\mbox{\boldmath$f$}^{n}_{h}=-Q(m_{2}^{n}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2}+m_{3}^{n}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{3})+\mbox{\boldmath$h$}_{s}(\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n})+\mbox{\boldmath$h$}_{e}
, we take the inner product for
eq.

16
with
-\epsilon\Delta_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}+\mbox{\boldmath$f$}_{h}^{n}
, and we obtain that

\displaystyle LHS

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}(\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}-\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n},-\epsilon\Delta_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}+\mbox{\boldmath$f$}_{h}^{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2k}\left(\epsilon\|\nabla_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}\|_{2}^{2}+\epsilon\|\nabla_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}-\nabla_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n}\|_{2}^{2}-\epsilon\|\nabla_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n}\|_{2}^{2}+2(\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1},\mbox{\boldmath$f$}_{h}^{n})-2(\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n},\mbox{\boldmath$f$}_{h}^{n})\right),

and

\displaystyle RHS

\displaystyle=\alpha\left(\epsilon\Delta_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}+\mbox{\boldmath$f$}_{h}^{n},\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n}\times(\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n}\times(\epsilon\Delta_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}+\mbox{\boldmath$f$}_{h}^{n}))\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha\left(\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n}\times(\epsilon\Delta_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}+\mbox{\boldmath$f$}_{h}^{n})),(\epsilon\Delta_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}+\mbox{\boldmath$f$}_{h}^{n}))\times\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n}\right)=-\alpha\|\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n}\times(\epsilon\Delta_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}+\mbox{\boldmath$f$}_{h}^{n}))\|_{2}^{2}\leq 0.

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}\|_{2}^{2}+2(\mbox{\boldmath$f$}_{h}^{n},\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1})\leq\|\nabla_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n}\|_{2}^{2}+2(\mbox{\boldmath$f$}_{h}^{n},\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n}).

The BDF1 method without projection is energy stable in the discrete level.

6
Conclusions and discussions

In this paper, we compare two types of methods and investigate the effect of the projection step on the actual results of micromagnetic simulations. The results show that in practical simulations, removing the projection step does not bring significant impact on the simulation effect. Our study also indicates that there are obvious differences in the actual performance of the two types of methods: the GSPM method depends on the dissipation coefficient in the calculation results of both the projected and unprojected steps, and under small dissipation coefficients, the results of the GSPM projected method are consistent with those of the unprojected step. The BDF1 method reported in this paper does not depend on the dissipation coefficient in the calculation results of the projected and unprojected steps, and the results of the BDF1 projected method are consistent with those of the unprojected step.

In this paper, we only carry out applied research on the first-order projected and unprojected methods, and the research results on the projected and unprojected high-order methods can be similarly extended. In addition, mathematically, projecting onto a sphere is a nonlinear operation, which brings essential difficulties to the numerical analysis of the projection method; mathematically, the unprojected method is easier to analyze. This paper suggests that the application effect of the unprojected method will not be much worse than that of the projection method.
