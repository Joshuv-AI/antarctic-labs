Explainable AI framework for skin lesion imaging based cancer detection

Sivarama Prasad Tera, Ravikumar Chinthaginjala, Priya Natha, Asadi Srinivasulu, Fadi Al-Turjman, Faruq Mohammad

ABSTRACT
The classification of skin cancer remains a challenging task because of the subtle visual patterns of benign and malignant lesions. In this work we propose an interpretable ensemble learning framework for dermoscopic skin lesion classification by combining Random Forest, XGBoost and LightGBM using Max-Voting. The proposed system demonstrates good predictive performance with clinical interpretability, achieving 95.94% accuracy on the HAM10000 dataset. To increase transparency we use explainable AI techniques to visualise the image regions and features that are most important for the model’s decisions. We also perform feature selection based on Genetic Algorithm to select the most discriminative descriptors and to reduce the redundancy in the hybrid feature space. The combination of deep features, handcrafted features, ensemble learning and explainability leads to a strong framework for accurate prediction and meaningful interpretation. The experimental results show that the proposed approach outperforms the individual classifiers and provides more explainable clinically relevant explanations that may help to improve the confidence and trust of dermatologists. In summary, the study demonstrates that the combination of ensemble learning and explainable AI can provide an effective and practical direction towards trustworthy skin cancer decision support.

Introduction Among the cancers, skin cancers are one of the most common cancers in the world. Even though melanoma accounts for only a tiny percentage of the total skin cancers, it accounts for nearly all deaths caused by skin cancers [ 1 , 2 ]. Early identification and accurate classification of lesions play a key role in ensuring the best possible outcome for the patients, since the five-year survival rate becomes extremely low once melanoma metastasizes. The clinical diagnosis of different skin cancers, especially differentiation of malignant melanomas and benign nevi, remains a challenge, since there are minor differences in the morphology and also due to the subjectivity involved in visual diagnosis [ 3 ]. The advent of dermoscopy has paved way for high-resolution images revealing structures unseen to the naked eye [ 4 ]. The analysis of the images obtained through dermoscopy needs an expert and can lead to variability in the results obtained by different experts [ 5 ]. Examination and biopsies are considered the gold standards, yet they are tedious, painful, and susceptible to error in early cases. There have been attempts to employ various ML techniques to solve these issues of automated disease detection and diagnosis [ 6 – 10 ] and prove that ML models can successfully assist in automated diagnosis based on dermoscopic images [ 11 ]. However, one major issue preventing the deployment of AI-based diagnostic tools in real-world situations lies in their opacity by nature. According to the GDPR, every algorithmic decision made using patient data needs to be explainable to the user [ 12 ], thus highlighting the legal requirement of transparency in AI-based diagnostic practices. Moreover, the surveys among healthcare practitioners consistently show that they expect to receive detailed information about how machine learning model predictions were derived before incorporating them in practice [ 13 ]. The lack of explanation of ML decision-making process is especially critical for clinicians because the final decision belongs to them anyway, making them less likely to trust models that make diagnoses with black-box approaches. At the same time, it should be noted that advanced AI models can encode important features alongside spurious correlations. Therefore, the lack of model interpretability becomes an issue to be addressed using XAI techniques. In order to address these limitations effectively, in this work, a new explainable ensemble learning methodology is proposed for multi-class skin cancer diagnosis, which integrates cutting-edge machine learning algorithms with explainability solutions. The proposed approach is based on a Max Voting ensemble algorithm incorporating the synergies between Random Forest (RF), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) classifiers to improve classification performance and enhance robustness for various types of skin cancers. The proposed methodology solves the clinically significant problem of multi-class skin lesion classification, including melanoma, basal cell carcinoma, melanocytic nevi, benign keratosis, dermatofibroma, vascular lesions, and actinic keratoses, unlike binary classification models widely used in contemporary research. As part of enhancing the explainability aspect within our framework, several methods of explainable AI (XAI), such as Grad-CAM to help visualize parts of an image that influence the outcome of diagnostic procedures and SHAP method for explaining the significance of particular features, were proposed [ 14 ]. In this way, by introducing several XAI methods, we were able to offer physicians comprehensible visualization regarding regions of images and the importance of features influencing outcomes of medical diagnosis, which consequently enhances their level of confidence when making decisions in their practice. The key highlights of this research include: Introduction of a reliable Max Voting ensemble technique that leverages the complementary capabilities of various machine learning methods to enhance the efficiency of multiclass classification; Implementation of a broad spectrum of explainable AI techniques that deliver intuitive visual and numeric explanations corresponding to dermatologists’ professional experience; Employment of Genetic Algorithm optimization in feature selection to enhance model interpretability while maintaining high diagnostic accuracy. This research responds to the increasing demand for interpretable AI solutions in dermatology and serves as the basis for development of automated skin cancer detectors that can be accepted clinically. Literature review on explainable AI in skin cancer detection AI, ML, and DL have greatly revolutionized various industries through automation, better decision-making, customized experiences, diagnosis improvement, and innovation among others in the past couple of years [ 15 – 25 ]. Specifically, AI, mainly deep neural networks (DNNs), has proven to be quite effective in dermatology through diagnostic capabilities and skin cancer classification, being as accurate or even more accurate than those by expert dermatologists. Lack of interpretability (black boxes nature) of these models poses a significant limitation for their use because clinicians will not be able to understand the working of the system. This explains the necessity for development of interpretable models. Explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) might help in bridging this gap by providing explanations of the processes that led to decisions made by the model. These explanations would be evaluated and trusted by clinicians. Explainable artificial intelligence is summarized by [ 26 ]. Content-based image retrieval (CBIR) systems help with generating prototype explanations through the retrieval of similar cases from the training dataset and assist the clinicians with understanding their diagnostic reasoning [ 28 , 30 ]. Conceptual explanation methods such as Testing with Concept Activation Vectors (TCAV), Concept Whitening allow us to map learned concepts to meaningful clinical concepts and provide more interpretable explanations than attributing raw pixels alone [ 33 ]. Yet, all these explainable AI techniques are inherently limited in scope. Fidelity issues associated with explainable AI arise from the approximation gap, where explanations provided by AI models may be approximate and do not represent the underlying decision-making procedure [ 36 ]. The testing of XAI techniques is still quite weak. Studies that involved non-specialists saw about a 9–20% improvement in accuracy thanks to CBIR-based explanations [ 37 ]. Yet in much larger studies with dermatologists, including the experiment by Tschandl et al., clinicians barely took any note of XAI explanations, which only improved diagnostic accuracy and confidence in about 302 subjects [ 28 ]. This indicates the necessity of conducting robust clinical trials in order to estimate the effectiveness of XAI tools. Additional research has also demonstrated that cognitive biases affect AI explanations. An eye-tracking experiment carried out by Chanda et al. showed the same pattern – AI explanations that mimicked those used by dermatologists improved diagnostic accuracy while showing cognitive biases among humans [ 31 ]. An important aspect is model fidelity, i.e., how good the explanations correspond to the true decision-making process of the model. Explanations such as heatmaps or prototype retrievals are interpreted by clinicians, who are prone to confirmation bias, where clinicians may prefer explanations that confirm preconceptions, regardless of their true accuracy [ 38 ]. Methods such as SmoothGrad, which denoise saliency maps by averaging over multiple noisy input perturbations have shown to improve visualization and reduce artifacts [ 32 ]. Other attribution methods such as Layer-wise Relevance Propagation (LRP) and DeepLIFT provide more stable explanations and have been shown to be particularly valuable for histopathology image analysis [ 34 ]. Recent research has investigated the influence of explanations on clinical decision-making and trust using human-in-the-loop approaches. While these approaches are promising, the evidence is still mixed and there is a need for standardized evaluation protocols. There are several gaps in the existing literature on XAI for skin cancer detection that need to be addressed. One big gap is the lack of integration of multiple XAI methods. Most studies only evaluate one explanation technique and do not integrate complementary techniques that can provide more comprehensive insights. Another major gap is the lack of robust clinical validation. Few large-scale trials have been conducted to evaluate the clinical utility of XAI tools in real-world settings. Finally, there is a lack of standardization in the evaluation of explanation quality. There is no consensus on the metrics to evaluate the effectiveness and trustworthiness of XAI methods. Bias detection and correction remain underexplored, with limited use of XAI techniques to identify and mitigate biases present in the dataset and model. Finally, there have been no large-scale studies of the robustness and generalizability of XAI explanations in different clinical contexts in the literature and discussions on the robustness of explanations between different healthcare settings have been limited. These gaps underscore the importance of more robust and clinically relevant research to facilitate the successful translation of XAI in dermatology. Though XAI is an excellent strategy in increasing the efficacy and reliability of skin cancer diagnoses, the challenge of providing faithfulness of explanation, clinical confirmation, and methodological standardization continues to pose another difficulty that has to be tackled. Trustworthy implementation of AI in dermatology calls for a multi-pronged strategy. This paper uses dermoscopy images sourced only from the HAM10000 database [ 39 ] which comprises of seven important types of lesions namely: Actinic Keratosis, Basal Cell Carcinoma, Benign Keratosis, Dermatofibroma, Melanocytic Nevi, Melanoma, and Vascular Lesions. The dermoscopic images used in this project were sourced from the HAM10000 dataset only. In order to conduct a proper validation of the system, initially the dataset was separated into training and testing subsets, following an 80/20 separation ratio, at the lesion level and before performing any data augmentation. Augmentation was conducted only on the training subset; thus, it can be guaranteed that there are no duplicate images in the test subset. Any duplicate samples contained in the HAM10000 dataset have been filtered out before applying the proposed models. This cannot be achieved at the patient level. In order to prevent any information leakage and ensure an unbiased evaluation, the initial split between the training and test datasets was done before any kind of data augmentation was performed. The training data within each fold were only augmented, whereas never did the test data undergo this process, which ensured that the same image in its augmented form would not appear in the training set. It was decided so due to the necessity of maintaining the independence of the test set, ensuring that the 95.94% accuracy was achieved by a truly generalized model. As the HAM10000 dataset is highly unbalanced, augmentation was utilized in order to improve the representation of the training classes. Firstly, all images were uniformly scaled to a resolution of 224 × 224 pixels so that they would be compatible with the common input dimensions of CNN backbones such as ResNet-50. Secondly, the pixel intensities of each image were standardized to have a mean of zero and a variance of one. Thirdly, a median filter of size 3 × 3 pixels was used to eliminate noise from the sensors while retaining lesion boundaries. Lastly, the RGB values of each image were transformed into HSV values to emphasize pigment features associated with skin lesion distinction. Feature extraction and structuring (Step 2) Starting with the preprocessed images of Step 1, we employed a pipeline for extracting both high-level semantic and low-level texture features needed to effectively distinguish between the types of skin lesions. For that purpose, first we generated deep learning-based features utilizing a ResNet-50 architecture pretrained on the ImageNet data set. Specifically, the final convolutional block (‘conv5_x’) produced an output feature map of size 7 × 7 × 2048 . Afterward, to obtain a 1 × 2048 feature vector from this tensor, global average pooling was performed. In order to make the vector scale-invariant, its elements were normalized with L 2 norm, forming a powerful representation of complicated semantic information in images. In addition to deep learning-based features, handcrafted image descriptors were calculated to provide more information about textures and shapes of images. The Scale-Invariant Feature Transform (SIFT) algorithm was used with the following parameters: number of octaves are 4, contrast threshold is 0.04, edge threshold -is 10. For each image, the best 500 keypoints, measured with response values, were selected, each producing a feature vector with 128 elements. Then, the Bag-of-Visual-Words (BoVW) model was applied to represent each local feature vector in a condensed manner. Namely, K-means clustering ( K = 256) with randomly chosen samples of vectors served as a code-book, and histograms of visual words were formed, making a 256 × 1 vector. Moreover, wavelet texture descriptors at multiple resolutions were extracted using three levels of discrete 2D Haar wavelet transform. From each of the three levels, descriptors based on statistics such as mean, standard deviation, skewness, and kurtosis were computed from each sub-band (LH, HL, HH), resulting in 36 descriptors for texture representation. The process is effective in describing the relevant textures for lesion heterogeneity. Features representing color and shape were also extracted. For color, statistical descriptors comprising mean, standard deviation, and skewness were computed for the first three moments on each channel in the HSV color model, resulting in nine color features. Shape features were obtained by extracting contours using Canny edge detection followed by morphological closing. All the feature sets extracted—namely, the 2048 dimensions deep features, 256 dimensions BoVW histogram, 36 dimensions wavelet texture vectors, 9 dimensions color moments, and 4 dimensions shape descriptors—were joined together to form a consolidated 2353 dimensional feature set per image. Consequently, the whole data set was structured to produce an organized N × 2353 matrix, forming the input for further stages of feature selection and modeling. Such a multi-faceted feature extraction and arrangement approach is essential to ensure an ample, diverse feature set representing skin lesion images, considering both automatic deep features and handcrafted domain features, which is critical for improving skin cancer classification models’ performance. Feature selection and optimization (Step 3) However, feature vectors of high dimensionality may result in redundancy and make overfitting more likely in case of machine learning models [ 42 – 44 ]. To address these issues, reduce redundancy, and improve the generalizability of the model, we opt for using Genetic Algorithms (GAs) in order to carry out feature selection efficiently [ 45 – 47 ]. In the context of the problem at hand, every solution is presented as a chromosome which is encoded as a 2353-bit binary vector since there are 2353 features extracted per image in our case. If the value of a particular gene equals ‘1’, it means that the corresponding feature will be included into the solution; otherwise, the feature is excluded from the solution. Population initialization consists of randomly generating 100 chromosomes; in our case, each bit is activated with a probability of 10%−90%. The fitness function aims at maximizing classification accuracy, while taking the size of the subset into account. More specifically, a lightweight Random Forest (50 trees, maximum depth 10) is used as a fitness criterion and is trained with selected features on 80% of the data and its validation accuracy is estimated by applying the model to the remaining 20%. The penalty factor ( λ = 0.01 ) penalizes the subset according to its percentage of selected features and is added to the final fitness result. Parent selection employs the tournament approach where five competitors form a group, and the one with the highest fitness value is chosen from each group. Moreover, an elitist approach guarantees that the top 5% of the population pass directly to the next generation. Genetic diversity is achieved through the following two key operators: two-point crossover with a probability of 80%, where pieces of genetic information from the parents are swapped in order to generate offspring, and bit-flip mutation with a probability of 2%, whereby certain genes are flipped randomly. By doing so, genetic diversity is retained, thereby allowing for exploration of the solution space. The GA repeats until either a maximum number of 50 iterations have been reached, or convergence occurs when there is no improvement greater than 0.001 in the best fitness score for five successive generations. With the help of evolution, the GA usually selects a minimal set of only 150–200 features with maximum predictability and minimum complexity. This optimized feature set forms the foundation for subsequent model training in Step 4, enabling improved classification performance and computational efficiency. Training and testing of ML models (Step 4) Using the selected set of features based on the output of Step 3, the following step includes training and evaluating a number of machine learning classifiers. The use of multiple classifiers will benefit from their strengths in improving the prediction accuracy. After the balancing of the dataset, the dataset is split into a training set and a testing set, with the ratio of 80% for the training data and 20% for the testing set. Stratification by lesion class is used in the splitting process. In order to develop and tune the models, the training dataset will be split further into five folds for cross-validation using stratification again. Three types of classifiers are explored, including Random Forest, XGBoost, and LightGBM. For each classifier, an exhaustive search of parameters is performed to find out the optimal parameters that yield the best F1-score in five-fold cross-validation. Hyperparameters were fine-tuned for the Random Forest Classifier, which involved adjusting some values to achieve the best results. In particular, we tried different numbers of trees within the random forest ensemble; these values included 100, 300, and 500. Furthermore, we tried different tree depth parameters, namely, None (unlimited depth), as well as specific depths equal to 10, 20, and 30. The minimum number of samples needed in order to consider a node during splitting was also varied; such values included 1, 2, and 4. Moreover, two different criteria for measuring split quality (“gini” and “entropy”) were analyzed in this process. Finally, bootstrapping, which is either on or off, was explored as well. In terms of parameter tuning for XGBoost, there were multiple parameters that required attention. The first one is estimators; here the value was 100, 200, and 300, which refers to the number of boosted trees. The learning rate, which determines how each tree contributes to the overall process, was adjusted between 0.01, 0.1, and 0.2. Maximum depth is a value that regulates model complexity by assigning its values equal to 3, 6, and 9. Subsample ratios of both the training samples and the columns per tree, which can be used to control stochastic sampling, were tuned to values 0.6, 0.8, and 1.0. Lastly, alpha and lambda are parameters that provide regularization in the model by preventing overfitting; their values were chosen as 0, 0.1, and 1; and 1, 1.5, and 2 correspondingly. The main tuning parameters for LightGBM classifier include the number of leaves (31, 61, 127) in a decision tree which determines the model’s capacity to capture complex patterns. Estimator value was selected from three options: 100, 200, and 300 as an optimum number of trees in the final model. The learning rate (0.01, 0.05, 0.1) is used to control the learning speed, while parameters of feature fraction and bagging fraction were varied by setting up their value as either 0.6, 0.8, or 1.0 to determine the number of features and samples that need to be taken into account during each iteration. L1 (0, 0.1, 1) and L2 (0, 0.1, 1) are regularizers applied in order to decrease the chances of overfitting. All preprocessing, feature selection, and training steps should be performed strictly within each training fold to prevent any information leakage. As the main metric, average F1-score among all validation folds can be used to select the best parameter combinations. Once the best configurations have been determined, classifiers should be trained again using all of the available data, after which, performance evaluation can take place using metrics like accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, AUC, etc. In addition to that, confusion matrices will help to find out potential biases and misclassifications. By following this procedure, we make sure that each individual classifier has been optimally fine-tuned, which makes our classification system stronger. Model ensemble and prediction generation (Step 5) In order to take advantage of complementary strengths among the classifiers, we adopt an approach known as Max Voting. Each classifier, namely Random Forest, XGBoost, and LightGBM, will be trained using their respective optimal parameters before generating probability predictions or class labels for every test data point. In Max Voting, the vote tally of class labels predicted by all three classifiers will be tallied for every data point. In the case of discrete voting, one vote will be assigned by each classifier to the class it predicts as output. The class that receives two or three votes will become the final output of the ensemble. On the other hand, if there is a tie between more than two classes, the tie will be broken by calculating the mean of probabilities for each class predicted by the three classifiers. In addition, we also utilize the Soft-Voting technique that allows each classifier to provide class probabilities instead of assigning a vote to a particular class label. We assign weights to classifiers based on their F1-score on the validation set normalized to add up to one: w i = F1 i ∑ j F1 j . (1) During testing, for each class c , the ensemble computes a weighted sum of predicted probabilities p i , c from each classifier i , P c = ∑ i w i p i , c , (2) and choose the class with the maximum P c . As we combine both hard and soft voting strategies, we make use of model diversity in order to decrease possible biases, prevent overfitting, and ensure more robust classifications. The proposed method is always more accurate and obtains higher F1 scores than any of the classifiers alone. Explainability with XAI Methods (Step 6) In order to gain insight into the workings of our model, we employ several cutting-edge XAI tools, each chosen based on its own unique contribution towards feature importance visualization and computation. For starters, we apply Grad-CAM on the output of the deep feature pipeline constructed using the CNN. In other words, we calculate the gradient of the target class score with respect to the feature maps of the last CNN layer (‘conv5_x’) and then average them in order to obtain channel importance weights. These are further used to construct a heatmap, which is scaled up to the original resolution ( 7 × 7 pixels) – the red spots show us the areas of the image with highest importance as perceived by the network. Secondly, we use Integrated Gradients to visualize and quantify pixel importance. This technique is based on approximating the path integral of the gradient function as the input changes from the baseline (black image) to the real image. We take 50 samples from this path and sum their gradient values to generate an attribuion heatmap. The local explanations based on super-pixel manipulation for the tabular feature representation are provided through the LIME framework. First, each sample image undergoes segmentation through the use of the Quick-shift algorithm to extract around 200 super-pixels (kernel size: 4, max distance: 200). The super-pixel values of the input image are then altered by setting its pixel values to zero in 1,000 synthesized images. A linear model fitted to the alterations gives us five super-pixels that have the most significant change for the predictions made by the ensemble classifier. The SHAP framework can also be used for both global and local explanations of the predictions. For our gradient boosting models, the Tree-explainer algorithm allows for calculating Shapley values, which are additive feature attributions. These Shapley values can be aggregated to obtain the importance ranking of each feature in terms of the contribution to the model output and also an explanation vector per prediction. Further validation on consistency is performed through the use of gradient-based methods, such as Saliency Maps, DeepLIFT, and SmoothGrad. Grad-CAM, Integrated Gradients, Saliency Maps, SmoothGrad, and DeepLIFT are utilized for the analysis of the ResNet-50 CNN feature extractor; hence, this represents the analysis of the spatial attention and pixel importance of the feature learning part. In order to explain the prediction of the final feature vector classifier, we used model-agnostic algorithms (SHAP and LIME) that explain the predictions of tree-based classifiers based on selected hybrid feature vectors. Collectively, these approaches provide a detailed heatmap visualization and feature importance analysis, which provides an understanding of the areas of interest both from the broad and granular perspectives along with the importance of individual features in the structured dataset. Using different XAI approaches, robustness, faithfulness, and interpretability of the explanations are ensured. Performance and explainability evaluation (Step 7) To fully evaluate the efficacy of our framework, we carry out both performance assessments for classification and explanation quality assessments. The accuracy of the prediction will be evaluated on the held-out test dataset via the use of several standard classification performance measures. Accuracy represents the overall number of correct decisions, while the precision and recall are determined for every lesion class separately in order to determine the level of performance at minimizing false positives and negatives, respectively. The F1-Score, which denotes the harmonic mean of precision and recall, is used as the main criterion in the selection and cross-validation of models. In addition, ROC AUC for every possible binary one-versus-rest classification will be obtained for measuring the balance between true positive rate (sensitivity) and false positive rate (specificity). To obtain reliable estimates, all values are reported along with 95% confidence intervals, calculated using bootstrap technique based on resampling with replacement 1,000 times, accounting for sampling error. Furthermore, evaluation of the quality of explanations is carried out. In total, there are two important components to assess: firstly, it is the coherence of the generated explanations compared to established standards and, secondly, faithfulness. As for coherence, it represents how much the explanations generated through XAI techniques (Grad-CAM heat maps and SHAP feature ranking) correspond to clinical criteria used in skin lesion analysis, for instance, ABCDE rule. Here, it can be estimated as the percentage of the region considered by the system as having high importance and overlap with the region annotated by the dermatologists. Faithfulness, however, represents the reliability of the explanations in terms of reflecting how model decision-making process changes under specific interventions, such as perturbations. For this purpose, we apply sequential occlusion for the top 10% most important regions. Ethical Statement and Participant Approval: Not applicable. Simulation results and its discussion This section describes the experiments performed in terms of GPU processing on Kaggle and Colab computing platforms, emphasizing the importance of Explainable AI (XAI) for gaining acceptance of deep learning models by clinicians for detecting skin cancers. Although deep neural networks are highly accurate in diagnosing diseases, they suffer from opacity, making it difficult to achieve regulatory and clinical trust. Interpretation is particularly necessary in dermatology to confirm that AI-derived diagnoses agree with the clinically important ABCDE criteria used to diagnose melanomas. Perturbation-based, gradient-based, and model-intrinsic are three classes of XAI that have been considered in this study. Perturbation techniques include LIME, whereas gradient-based include Grad-CAM, Integrated Gradients, and saliency maps, and DeepLIFT and SmoothGrad belong to the category of intrinsic techniques. Each type of technique brings its own advantages; perturbation techniques help gain intuition about a model independently of how it works, while gradient-based techniques focus on identifying important image patches related to malignancies. Namely, the Grad-CAM approach relies on the ability of the CNN models that make up our ensemble to output a gradient at the level of the last convolutional layer. In other words, this allows us to generate a spatially localized heatmap highlighting those parts of the dermoscopic images that contribute to the models’ decision in discriminating malignant from benign skin lesions based on the HAM10000 dataset. Deep Learning Important FeaTures (DeepLIFT) To perform quantitative analysis of contributions of individual input pixels in accordance with explainable AI flowchart illustrated by algorithm 1, we used the DeepLIFT method in Step 6 in order to determine the absolute contribution of each input pixel to the output predictions of the skin cancer classifier. Following completion of Steps 1–5, DeepLIFT attribution values have been computed for individual convolutional layers of the neural network on a set of test images from the HAM10000 skin lesion dataset. Specifically, DeepLIFT compares neuron activation values of each input image relative to a baseline image (black image) and backpropagates these differences in activations in order to estimate contributions of each input pixel. This technique generates consistent and theoretically sound attribution scores that can capture both positive and negative contribution to the class score and thus address the problem of saturation of gradients inherent to other attribution methods. SHAP explanations for skin cancer classification One commonly used interpretability technique is SHAP, which allows analyzing the process of decision-making by machine learning models [ 26 , 48 , 49 ]. By assigning weights to various features of the image, SHAP provides useful information about their importance and influence on predictions. Using SHAP values improves the transparency of artificial intelligence systems’ operations, allowing us to make sure that the classification result is coherent with existing medical knowledge and clinical expectations. Moreover, the classification process, conducted using ML, strongly depends on the feature extraction stage. Features necessary for discriminating between benign and malignant lesions include mostly color and texture features and can be represented as follows: Color features have proven their value in the process of classification. In particular, color histogram represents the color distribution of the lesion in several color spaces (RGB, HSV, etc.), allowing its discrimination according to color differences. Color moments represent the statistical properties of the color distribution of the lesion. It includes the first moment (mean, the average color intensity), the second moment (standard deviation, measuring intensity variation), and the third moment (skewness, representing asymmetry and, thus, potentially malignancy). Textures, in turn, are represented by four GLCM-derived features that measure specific characteristics of the lesion. Specifically, contrast measures intensity differences between adjacent pixels, while correlation represents their mutual dependence. Energy shows the homogeneity of pixel intensities distribution. Homogeneity measures intensity smoothness. These features are provided as inputs for ML classifiers (RF, XGBoost, and LightGBM), predicting the type of the skin lesion. Fig 8 is the SHAP summary plot, which is one of the most critical tools to visualize the contribution of individual features to the ML model’s decision. The SHAP summary plot is a powerful visualization method for feature importance evaluation because in the plot, each dot is a single data point, providing detailed information about feature importance. The y-axis of the SHAP summary plot shows the order of importance of features in terms of their influence on the model’s decisions. For the skin cancer classification model, the Color_Moment_Skewness, GLCM_Contrast, GLCM_Correlation, and GLCM_Energy are the features having significant impact on model predictions by providing information required for discrimination between benign and malignant lesions. The x-axis of the SHAP summary plot shows the SHAP values for individual features reflecting the feature impact on the model’s prediction. The positive SHAP values mean that the feature values lead to higher probability of malignant classification, while the negative SHAP values indicate a probability of benign classification. The magnitude of SHAP values is an indication of how much each feature impacts model’s prediction. Color encoding in the SHAP summary plot is another critical visual aid for understanding the significance of individual features. In the summary plot, each dot represents a particular data point with the colors assigned based on the values of features. Blue color in the dot means that the feature value is low while red means high feature intensity. Fig 8 is the SHAP feature importance of the binary classification layer placed above the multi-class ensemble of the models discussed earlier. Conclusion This work demonstrates that the use of multiple machine learning models, together with a range of Explainable AI approaches, is capable of improving diagnostic accuracy while also meeting the transparency requirements necessary for successful adoption in healthcare settings. In particular, by combining Random Forest, XGBoost, and LightGBM through a Max Voting algorithm, alongside the use of Genetic Algorithms for feature selection, this model performs with near state-of-the-art accuracy on the challenging dermoscopy images. Importantly, the use of seven different approaches that fall under the Explainable AI paradigm, namely spatial methods such as Grad-CAM, pixel-wise explanation methods such as Integrated Gradients and DeepLIFT, smoothed explanations such as SmoothGrad, local explanations through LIME, and global approaches such as SHAP, provides interpretable output. This differs from traditional “black box” solutions, which do not offer clear explanations behind their decisions. Future work will consider practical applications to clinical settings, broader validation studies using various image modalities and diverse patient populations, and hybrid deep learning ensemble solutions to improve both accuracy and transparency.
