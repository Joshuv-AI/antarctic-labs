 Testing Recommendations for Thermal Failure Analysis

 Key Points:

 • Current critical failure temperatures for components do not capture key failure behavior.
 • To capture relevant complex failure behavior, we propose augmenting thermal models with a
 damage accumulation quantity of interest (QOI).
 • Based on best practices for thermal decomposition models, two styles of time-temperature
 experimental schedules are proposed:
 o A linear time-temperature ramp schedule
 o A rapid ramp to constant temperature hold schedule
 • To generate a sufficient calibration and testing data set, we recommend a minimum of 5 time-
 temperature schedules per type should be conducted, with a minimum of 5 repeats per
 schedule.
 o Total of 50 tests:
 ▪ 25 linear time-temperature ramps experiments
 ▪ 25 ramp-to-hold experiments
 • After initial testing, if time and resources allow, model informed testing schedules can be
 generated to efficiently drive down uncertainty.

Historical Context:

Current thermal failure QOIs compare a point temperature/pressure with a critical failure
temperature/pressure. These models are sufficient for many purposes; however, some modes of failure
are more complicated than a single temperature or pressure can capture. Failure modes such as thermal
decomposition or creep have a strong dependence on the history of experienced temperatures (e.g., a
temperature held for a certain period). To capture these types of failures with a critical
temperature/pressure will incur a large band of uncertainty because of the inherent discrepancy of the
QOI to the physics of the problem.

Proposed Method for Identifying Complex Failure:

Minimizing ranges of uncertainty in failure QOIs is vital for making informed engineering decisions. To
improve our decision-making ability, it is necessary to have a QOI representation that captures more
complex failure modes. Capturing the physics of complex time-dependent temperature failure in
explicit detail can be computationally and experimentally expensive. To avoid this blooming of
complication and costs, an empirical QOI component-level failure/damage model may be sufficient to
help identify component-level failure. In this context, failure and damage are abstractions of more
complicated processes that lead to the cessation of normal operating behavior, not a representation of
mechanical damage, such as void formation. Such a model requires minimal increases in computational
cost and experimental burden. The proposed QOI serves as an indicator that something has likely failed,
similar to the previous critical failure temperatures or pressures. This QOI will not replace any existing
failure QOIs. It will be calculated alongside them, allowing for preservation of legacy style QOI
measurements with new context from the new QOI.

The ideal work required to define the new QOI failure model:

 1. Conduct exploratory physical experiments,
 2. Formulate a mathematical failure‐criterion model,
 3. Generate high-quality experimental data for model calibration,
 4. Calibrate the model against that calibration data,

However, time constraints preclude the full implementation of the ideal workflow. Instead, by
leveraging subject matter expert (SME) judgment, several steps can be streamlined. The streamlined
requirements are:

 1. Identify a right-sized family of failure QOIs (Completed).
 2. Generate a right-sized data set to calibrate and test QOI.
 3. Calibrate QOI model against calibration data.

This approach will get a QOI model faster and with less experimental cost; however, in forgoing the
exploratory testing, there is increased risk due to the potential for unexpected material behavior in
untested regions.

QOI Model Summary:

To capture history dependent failure, we propose a failure/damage accumulation QOI model. At low
temperatures virtually no damage builds up, but at high temperature damage accumulates. To capture
this behavior, the exponential family of model forms is chosen, because they can efficiently capture

large changes in behavior over short domains with very few parameters. With only a few parameters to
calibrate, this minimizes the experimental costs.

More details about the mathematical form of the QOI are detailed in the “QOI Model Details” section.

Experimental Data Recommendations:

The following information is the minimum required to calibrate the failure model from experimental
data:

 1. Time-temperature measurements at the target component surface
 2. The time and temperature at which the component fails

Although any time-temperature schedule can be used to construct the exponential failure model, two
conventional time-temperature schedules are commonly used:

 1. A linear time-temperature ramp schedule
 2. A rapid ramp to constant temperature hold schedule

The first schedule is useful because it captures the range of effects that may contribute to failure across
different temperatures. The second is valuable for isolating behavior associated with specific thermal
environments or failure modes.

Because the model contains only two free parameters, the minimum (and not recommended) number
of required distinct time-temperature schedules is three. This number of schedules exposes the failure
criteria to a large amount of risk because of the limited range of histories it sees during training.
Exploring more schedules with more repeats per schedule will drive down the risk and uncertainty,
enabling more precise engineering decision making.

In balancing risk and cost, it is recommended that five different time-temperature schedules be
performed per schedule type (ramp & ramp-to-hold), with an aim to capture conditions spanning the 1,
25, 50, 75, and 99th percentile for each type. Spanning this range is important because it minimizes the
risk that the model must extrapolate behavior beyond tested conditions. Additionally, it provides evenly
spaced data points for reasonable interpolation. Both of these properties will help minimize model
error.

Based on the large variation at time and temperature of failure seen in historical documents, a larger
number (10+) of repeats per time-temperature schedule would be ideal; however, such a schedule,

 3

while decreasing risk, would drive up cost. To have confidence in the statistical representation of the
experimental data, a minimum of five repeats per time-temperature schedule is recommended.

With time and resources after the initial testing, model-informed testing schedules can be suggested to
help drive down the uncertainty more efficiently than by SME judgement alone.

Summary of testing options:

 1. 15 component level tests at across 3 time-temperature schedules (5 replicates): Not
 Recommended
 1. Risk: Very High risk; lack of historical data increases risk that chosen ramp rates
 or temperature holds will not capture key failure behavior
 2. Impact: Lowest cost associated with fewer tests
 2. 50 component level tests at 5 temperature ramp rates and 5 temperature holds (5
 replicates): Recommended
 1. Risk: Low to medium
 2. Impact: Increased data for component teams to drive down uncertainty;
 decreased need for mod/sim to extrapolate data, better interpolation data,
 potentially sufficient data to adapt to unexpected discoveries
 3. 100 component level tests at 5 temperature ramp rates and 5 temperature holds (10
 replicates): Recommended, But Costly
 1. Risk: Low to medium (decreased risk to understanding failure; increased risk due
 to possible schedule delay)
 2. Benefit: increased confidence in spread of experimental data distribution,
 allowing for increased rigor of model calibration approaches.
