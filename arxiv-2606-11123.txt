Overcoming Rank Collapse in Feedback Alignment
Gauthier Boeshertz, Razvan Pascanu, Claudia Clopath

Abstract
Backpropagation is widely viewed as biologically implausible, in part because it requires feedback weights to be the transpose of forward weights for error propagation. Interestingly, when training a network with fixed random feedback weights to circumvent this issue, learning aligns the forward weights with the feedback weights, leading the backpropagated error signal to become an approximation of the standard gradient used by backpropagation. This process, called feedback alignment, occurs in MLPs and very shallow CNNs but does not scale well to deeper architectures. In this work, we first investigated differences between backpropagation and feedback alignment models, trained on CIFAR10, specifically focusing on the effective rank of the signal. We found that the FA error has a considerably lower rank and hence is constrained to a lower-dimensional subspace compared to backpropagation, limiting exploration of the parameter space. Motivated by this observation, we evaluated two mechanisms for increasing the effective dimensionality of FA: Muon, an optimiser that orthogonalises weight updates; and hidden activity normalisation, which promotes activation orthogonality. Across larger architectures and benchmarks, we find that these methods consistently improve over feedback alignment baselines, for example, on CIFAR100 with a Resnet-18, accuracy increases by 9 percentage points. Our results identify low-dimensional gradient dynamics as a key obstacle to scaling feedback alignment and suggest that inducing higher-dimensional update geometry is a promising route toward scaling alternatives to backpropagation.



1
Introduction

Most neural networks are trained using error backpropagation
(
43
)
. This method performs credit assignment by computing the gradient of a loss function for every parameter inside the network. A biological view of this process says that the neurons project an error signal backwards, using synapses that share the same weights as the synapses projecting upwards. Sharing the weight between the forward and backward connections has been one of the main issues with regard to the biological plausibility of backpropagation
(
12
;
30
;
31
)
. However,
(
30
)
has shown that it is possible to train networks with random feedback projections and achieve results similar to backpropagation on simple tasks. They observed that throughout training, the forward weights would align to the random feedback, allowing a meaningful error to be backpropagated, as quantified by the alignment of the gradients with the random feedback and gradients obtained with weight sharing. While the error signal propagated in feedback alignment is not a gradient of the loss, we will refer to it as a gradient to be consistent with prior works.

This observation created a vast literature on making more biological training methods using random feedback projections. Direct feedback alignment
(
35
)
showed that it is possible to directly feed the error signal from the output to any hidden layer using feedback projections of the appropriate size. Building on this idea,
(
17
)
showed that even the error can be replaced by the target itself.

However all of these methods fail to scale to more difficult tasks
(
29
;
52
;
34
;
28
;
4
)
. For example,
(
28
)
reported that convolutional neural networks trained with direct feedback alignment do not perform better than chance on CIFAR100. In general, feedback alignment methods have been found to perform much worse than backpropagation even in shallow convolutional neural networks. The reason behind this difference in performance was attributed to the lack of flexibility of convolutional layers due to weight sharing
(
40
;
28
)
. To mitigate this lack of flexibility, several methods use the signs of the forward weights to set the signs of the feedback
(
29
;
52
;
34
)
, and achieved performance closer to that of backpropagation. Instead of imposing a shared structure, some methods start with random feedback and train the feedback along with the forward weights
(
1
;
27
;
19
)
, and reach performance even closer to backpropagation.

In this work, we focus on feedback alignment networks that do not impose any relation between the forward and feedback weights, nor do we consider adapting the feedback weights. Our objective is not to completely close the gap between backpropagation and feedback alignment. Rather, it is to explain what stops alignment from happening.
Through our attempt of understanding feedback alignment, we provide new perspectives on optimisation techniques that differ from those usually obtained in backpropagation, since the feedback alignment gradients have data-independent terms induced by the fixed feedback matrices. To pursue this objective, we investigated the differences in the geometry of the gradients in networks trained using backpropagation and feedback alignment. We find that when the weights start aligning, there is a marked decrease in the effective rank
(
42
)
of gradients in feedback alignment networks. This means that the gradients will have a few directions that dominate the others, which may not allow the alignment process to continue, since the parameter updates have to explore much smaller subspaces. We therefore try two different methods aimed at increasing the dimensionality of the gradients. When writing dimensionality, we mean the effective dimensionality or rank. First, we used a recently proposed optimiser, Muon
(
5
;
6
;
24
)
. Muon differs from standard SGD with momentum by adding an orthogonalisation step to the momentum update. This means setting every singular value to 1. The second approach is to normalise the activity of the layers using Batch Normalization, as it was shown to increase their dimensionality
(
14
;
13
)
. First, we test these methods on the CIFAR10
(
26
)
dataset and find that they improved the performance of feedback alignment models considerably. Using these methods, the effective dimensionality of the gradients did not exhibit the marked decrease observed before, which supports the claim that feedback alignment requires gradients to have large rank for alignment to happen. We then measured the impact of the two methods when using Resnets
(
20
)
, and Alexnet
(
25
)
on STL-10
(
10
)
, CIFAR100
(
26
)
, Tiny Imagenet
(
44
)
, and again found better performance. While both methods separately bring benefits, using them together increases the accuracy on the three benchmarks.

2
Optimisation with Feedback Alignment

2.1
Backpropagation and Feedback Alignment

Backpropagation computes gradients by traversing the computation graph from the output layer back to the input, propagating error signals layer by layer through the transpose of the forward weights. For a standard MLP network with
L
layers:

a^{\ell}=W^{\ell}h^{\ell-1}+b^{\ell},\qquad h^{\ell}=\phi(a^{\ell})

(1)

where
a^{\ell}
is the pre-activation vector,
h^{\ell}
is the post-activation vector,
W^{\ell}
is the weight matrix,
b^{\ell}
is the bias vector, and
\phi(\cdot)
is the activation function, for layer
\ell=1,\dots,L
. Using the loss computed at the output of the network, backpropagation computes the error for each layer recursively:

\delta^{L}=\frac{\partial E}{\partial a^{L}},\qquad\delta^{l}=\left((W^{l+1})^{\top}\delta^{l+1}\right)\odot\phi^{\prime}(a^{l})\quad\text{for }l=L-1,\dots,1.

(2)

where
\odot
denotes the Hadamard product,
\phi^{\prime}
is the derivative of the activation function. Then the errors are multiplied by the activity of the layer the weights project from to make the gradient:

\frac{\partial E}{\partial W^{\ell}}=\delta^{\ell}(h^{\ell-1})^{\top},\qquad\frac{\partial E}{\partial b^{\ell}}=\delta^{\ell}.

(3)

where we set
h^{0}
to be the input to the network.

The use of the transposed weight in Eq.
2
is the core of the Weight Transport problem. Training a network with random feedback connections replaces the transpose with a weight
B^{\ell}
, usually sampled from the same distribution as the forward weights, and keeping it fixed during training, such that the error computation results in:

\delta^{l}=\left(B^{\ell+1}\delta^{l+1}\right)\odot\phi^{\prime}(a^{l})\quad\text{for }l=L-1,\dots,1.

(4)

Although we wrote the equations for an MLP, the same ideas apply to convolutional neural networks. In convolutional layers, backpropagation still propagates errors backward and forms gradients from error signals and layer activations, but the linear maps are convolutional operators with shared weights across spatial locations rather than dense matrices. Accordingly, the backward pass uses the adjoint convolution operator, and feedback alignment replaces this operator with a fixed random feedback map of compatible shape.

2.2
Optimisation

Once gradients have been computed, their use is determined by the optimiser, which is separate from the choice of learning rule used to obtain them. feedback alignment can therefore use any optimiser that works for backpropagation. We will especially be interested in a recently proposed optimiser, called Muon
(
5
;
6
;
24
)
. This optimiser first received attention because it leads to faster convergence under mild additional computational costs
(
24
)
, and recently because it leads to lower loss for the same compute budget in large language models
(
32
)
. It works similarly to SGD with momentum, where
\beta
is the momentum parameter, but adds an orthogonalisation step:

M_{t}=\beta M_{t-1}+(1-\beta)\frac{\partial E}{\partial W^{\ell}}\quad W_{t+1}=W_{t}^{\ell}-\eta\,\operatorname{Orth}(M_{t}^{\ell}),\quad

(5)

The orthogonalisation function
\operatorname{Orth}
computes the closest orthogonal matrix to its input, which can be written in terms of the SVD of the input,
X=U\Sigma V^{\top}
, then the orthogonalised matrix is given by
\operatorname{Orth}(X)=UV^{\top}.
This operation is usually done with the Newton-Schulz approximation
(
24
)
, which is quicker than computing the singular vectors. An intuitive explanation of the orthogonalisation is given in
(
24
)
, which we rely on for our results. It states that updates in standard optimisers have low rank, and orthogonalising the update will make the lesser explored directions more relevant. As the non-matrix parameters can not have their updates orthogonalised and need to use SGD or AdamW
(
33
)
, we use a version of Muon that scales the updates to have a similar RMS norm to AdamW
(
32
)
, which allows the reuse of the learning rate across parameters trained with AdamW and Muon.

We first explored the effects of feedback alignment in deep networks to understand why it performs substantially worse than backpropagation in deep convolutional neural networks. We trained convolutional neural networks with depth varying from 1 to 4 before the classifier. Each convolutional layer had 64 channels, a 3x3 kernel, and was followed by 2x2 max-pooling; on the CIFAR10
(
26
)
dataset. Using more channels leads to a decrease of the gap between feedback alignment and backpropagation
(
7
)
; however, in sufficiently deep networks, this gap remains large, leading us to focus on the settings in which feedback alignment fails. We use 64 as it strikes a balance between too narrow and too wide networks, and because it usually is the number of channels in the first layer of deep CNNs, for example, Resnets
(
20
)
. We used ReLU activations, as it is the activation used in these deep networks. For the optimiser, we used both SGD with momentum and AdamW
(
33
)
, to show that the results do not depend on what type of momentum is used. The weights of the convolutional and linear layers were initialised using the Kaiming uniform initialisation as in
(
17
)
. We also found that it was the one that worked best for feedback alignment networks, which heavily depended on having the right initialisation. We first measured the effect of depth on accuracy, and then turned to alignment, gradient dimensionality, and feature dimensionality to diagnose the source of the gap (Figure
1
).

Consistent with earlier studies
(
9
;
28
)
, we found that the accuracy of the feedback alignment models decreases with depth (Figure
1
A). To understand this failure of feedback alignment, we turned our attention to networks with 4 convolutional layers. We found that feedback alignment models train much more slowly than backpropagation models (Figure
1
B). Although the feedback alignment models have a tiny jump in accuracy at first, they slowly converge to their maximum. Similarly, the alignment of the forward weights with the feedback weights, measured by the cosine similarity between the flattened weights, slowly increases throughout training (Figure
1
C). However, these small increases in weight alignment do not translate to increases in gradient alignment (Figure
1
D), quantified by the cosine similarity of the gradients obtained using the feedback weights and when using the transpose forward weights as done in backpropagation. Since the gradient alignment is small, the feedback alignment gradient will not follow the true gradient of the error, which explains why the accuracy changes slowly. To explain why feedback alignment gradient alignment does not increase much after an initial bump, we analysed the geometry of the updates of feedback alignment models.

Guided by the intuition that feedback alignment makes optimisation happen in lower-dimensional subspaces, where the forward weights have aligned to the feedback, we first measured the dimensionality of the gradients by computing the effective rank of the gradients (Figure
1
E). The effective rank is a continuous approximation to the rank of the matrix. It is computed by taking the exponentiated entropy of the normalised singular values of the matrix:
\text{erank}(M)=\exp\!\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{r}p_{i}\log p_{i}\right)
, with
p_{i}=\frac{\sigma_{i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{r}\sigma_{j}}
where
\sigma_{i}
are the singular values. We found that the dimensionality of the gradients of feedback alignment networks is always lower than that of similar backpropagation networks (Figure
1
E). We then looked at how the gradients change throughout training. We measured the dimensionality of the trajectory of the gradients (Figure
1
F). At each training step, we computed the gradient on 100 samples, making the highest possible rank 100, and computed the effective rank of the Gram matrix of the stacked gradients flattened into a vector. The Gram matrix is the covariance of the normalised gradient, letting
h_{i}
denote the gradient at timestep i,
G_{ij}=\tilde{h}_{i}^{\top}\tilde{h}_{j},\tilde{h}_{i}=\frac{h_{i}}{\lVert h_{i}\rVert}
.
A lower-rank Gram matrix indicates that the gradients are more aligned with one another. In other words, the gradients span a lower-dimensional subspace, so the parameter updates are confined to fewer directions. In this regime, the training explores only a limited region of parameter space, which may be insufficient to produce meaningful alignment. In agreement with our hypothesis, the dimensionality of the trajectory of feedback alignment networks decreases early, and stays much lower than that of the backpropagation networks (Figure
1
F). Indeed, while the backpropagation trajectory dimensionality almost reaches its maximum rank, 100, the feedback alignment one is stuck under 20, and ends at 12. To better explain this, we looked at the entries next to the diagonal of the Gram matrix, which are the cosine similarity of gradients adjacent in the trajectory. We computed the mean of this value across the whole matrix and found high cosine similarity for the feedback alignment network where it is almost zero for the backpropagation networks (Figure
1
G). The trajectory of the gradients will be higher for backpropagation as it uses updated weights to propagate the error, which may increase the dimensionality when keeping other parts of the gradient equal. feedback alignment by construction can not do this, however, it is not the only reason for the lower trajectory dimensionality, as we show in the next section.

One of the effects of low-dimensional gradients could be that the representations will be low-dimensional too. To measure this, we computed the Gram matrix using the activations that feed into the classifier. We then computed the effective rank of this matrix, as done in
(
21
)
. As expected, the features of the feedback alignment networks are much lower-dimensional than those of backpropagation networks, which may be due to the small subspace spanned by the gradients. While low-dimensional features have been found to be beneficial for generalisation
(
50
;
21
;
2
)
, we argue here that the small feature dimensionality may result from limited exploration of the parameter space, which limits the alignment of the weights.

Together, these results suggest that the poor performance of feedback alignment in deeper convolutional neural networks is not only due to imperfect alignment with backpropagation, but also to the geometry of the updates themselves. In particular, feedback alignment appears to restrict learning to a low-dimensional subspace, producing highly correlated gradients and low-dimensional features that may be insufficient for discriminating between classes.

4
Higher-dimensional updates enable better feedback alignment

The previous section suggests a direct intervention: if the failure mode of feedback alignment is caused by updates that are too low-dimensional, then methods that increase the dimensionality of the updates or of the intermediate representations should improve feedback alignment training. Therefore, we tried different ways of increasing the dimensionality of the updates, using the same setup as in the previous sections.

Using orthogonalised updates

First, we used the Muon optimiser
(
24
)
. We showed above that the effective rank of the feedback alignment gradient, computed using the entropy of the singular values of the gradient matrix, is much lower compared to what it is in backpropagation. Therefore, there may be many directions associated with low singular values that will not be explored. Muon directly acts on this by setting every singular value of the momentum that is not 0 to 1, thereby increasing the effective rank of the update. As highlighted above, feedback alignment only changes how the gradients are computed; we can use any optimiser we want to perform the actual updates. Therefore, we can simply swap SGD or AdamW with Muon. This simple change proved to be quite effective (Figure
2
). The accuracy of the shallow models is a bit better when using Muon than with standard optimisers like AdamW or SGD. More importantly, the accuracy of the feedback alignment models trained using Muon does not decrease with depth (Figure
2
A), in networks with 4 convolutional layers, Muon has 69.5% test accuracy, whereas AdamW has 59.5%. We again looked at the dynamics during training for the 4 convolutional layer model. The accuracy also converges to its maximum quicker (Figure
2
B). The weight alignment is also higher for every layer of the network (Figure
2
C). Importantly, the gradient alignment is also much higher for the models trained with Muon, and have much smoother curves (Figure
2
D). Coming back to the geometry analysis, we found that models trained with Muon have higher-dimensional gradients (Figure
2
E), gradient trajectories (Figure
2
F), and features (Figure
2
G).

Figure 3
:
Using the top-K directions as the opposite of Muon.
Using low-rank SGD results in much worse performance with feedback alignment, whereas the effect is not so large with backpropagation.

Given that an optimiser that increases the dimensionality of the update improves the performance of feedback alignment models. We investigated how an optimiser that does the opposite affects feedback alignment. To do so, we replaced the orthogonalisation function in Muon with the rank r approximation of the momentum matrix using the SVD of the momentum. We used the rank 2 and rank 5 approximation (Figure
3
) and found that while backpropagation models are not so affected, the effect on feedback alignment models is catastrophic.

Taken together, these results show that orthogonalised updates substantially mitigate the main failure mode of feedback alignment by increasing the dimensionality of the optimisation trajectory. This, in turn, improves alignment, accelerates convergence, and largely removes the degradation in performance with depth.

Normalising the activities

A second way to increase the dimensionality of the updates is to act directly on the activities of the network. As the gradient is the dot product of the error signal and the previous activation, increasing the dimensionality of the activation may increase the dimensionality of the gradients. Batch Normalization has been shown to promote more orthogonal hidden representations
(
23
;
14
;
13
)
, suggesting that it may prevent the collapse of the representations onto low-dimensional subspaces. Since our previous results indicate that feedback alignment fails partly because its gradients and features become too low-dimensional, we added Batch Normalization layers after each convolutional layer, using the same experimental setup as above.

The results were very similar to those obtained with Muon (Figure
4
). Batch Normalization substantially reduced the degradation in accuracy with depth (Figure
4
A), accelerated convergence (Figure
4
B), and increased both weight and gradient alignment (Figure
4
C-D). Consistent with our hypothesis, it also increased the dimensionality of the gradients (Figure
4
E), the gradient trajectory (Figure
4
F), and the final features (Figure
4
G). Thus, improving the geometry of the representations can mitigate the main pathologies of feedback alignment, further supporting the view that the poor performance of feedback alignment in deep convolutional neural networks is not only an alignment problem, but also a dimensionality problem.

Together, these results may explain why Batch Normalization and Muon produce similar improvements in feedback alignment: both help maintain a richer set of directions through which alignment and learning can continue. Conversely, forcing updates to be low-rank severely harms FA, confirming that higher-dimensional optimisation is crucial for robust FA training.

Given that Muon and Batch Normalization improved the trainability of feedback alignment networks in our previous setting, we next asked whether these interventions also scaled to harder datasets and more realistic architectures. We trained AlexNet
(
25
)
and ResNet-18
(
20
)
on three image-classification benchmarks of increasing difficulty: STL-10
(
10
)
, CIFAR-100
(
26
)
, and Tiny ImageNet, a 200-class subset of ImageNet rescaled to 64x64
(
44
)
. For each dataset and architecture, we compared standard backpropagation and feedback alignment under four settings: the baseline optimiser, Batch Normalization, Muon, and Muon combined with Batch Normalization (Table
1
).

Consistent with the results from the previous section, baseline feedback alignment performed poorly in deeper models. The degradation was especially severe for ResNet-18, where baseline feedback alignment reached only 14.5% on STL-10, 1.4% on CIFAR-100, and 0.7% on Tiny ImageNet. These results show that the failure mode of feedback alignment becomes more pronounced as both the model and the task become more difficult. In contrast, both Batch Normalization and Muon substantially improved feedback alignment performance across architectures and datasets. On STL-10, for example, AlexNet trained with baseline feedback alignment reached 34.2%, whereas adding Batch Normalization increased performance to 60.5%, Muon to 57.7%, and Muon+Batch Normalization to 67.5%. A similar pattern was observed for ResNet-18, where Muon+Batch Normalization improved performance from 14.5% to 64.2%.

The same trend held on the harder benchmarks. On CIFAR-100, baseline feedback alignment reached 9.9% with AlexNet and 1.4% with ResNet-18. Adding either Batch Normalization or Muon produced gains, and the combination of Muon+Batch Normalization gave the best performance, reaching 38.1% for AlexNet and 46.1% for ResNet-18. On Tiny ImageNet, where the baseline feedback alignment models again performed poorly, Muon+Batch Normalization improved AlexNet from 6.4% to 32.1% and ResNet-18 from 0.7% to 24.3%. Thus, although a large gap remains between feedback alignment and backpropagation on the most difficult settings, the combination of higher-dimensional updates and normalised representations allows feedback alignment to train models that would otherwise fail almost completely.

Interestingly, Muon and Batch Normalization were complementary in the feedback alignment setting. In most cases, each method alone produced a large improvement over baseline feedback alignment, but their combination gave the strongest performance. This suggests that they act through related but non-identical mechanisms. Muon directly modifies the geometry of the parameter update by flattening the spectrum of the momentum, whereas Batch Normalization acts on the forward representations and changes the geometry of the gradients. When combined, they appear to preserve a richer set of learning directions throughout training, making it possible for alignment to emerge even in deeper architectures and harder tasks.

The effect of these methods was more modest and less systematic under backpropagation. Batch Normalization generally improved backpropagation, especially for ResNet-18 and the harder datasets, whereas Muon alone did not consistently improve performance outside of STL-10. This contrast further supports the idea that feedback alignment is especially sensitive to the dimensionality of the optimisation trajectory. Whereas backpropagation already provides informative, high-dimensional gradients, feedback alignment requires additional mechanisms to prevent its updates and representations from collapsing onto a small number of directions.

Overall, these scaling experiments show that the geometric interventions identified in the previous section are not limited to small convolutional networks. Increasing the dimensionality of updates with Muon, increasing the dimensionality of representations with Batch Normalization, and especially combining the two, improves feedback alignment across datasets, architectures, and task difficulty. These results support our observation that feedback alignment needs high-dimensional updates.

6
Discussion

While feedback alignment was surprisingly shown to work in shallow MLP
(
30
)
, the fact that it did not scale well to deeper architectures raised doubts on whether the brain could be functioning on this principle. Indeed, even though convolutional networks are not biologically plausible due to neurons sharing exact weights, although they are inspired by the visual system
(
4
;
39
)
, this weight sharing is what makes optimisation easier with backpropagation. Therefore, it would be desirable that an approximation to backpropagation also performs well with such architectures. In this work, we presented ways of making feedback alignment work in deeper convolutional neural networks. Indeed, using a different optimiser or by normalising the activities, we found that feedback alignment did not have such a steep decrease in accuracy when adding layers as it does when using standard methods. While we did not investigate why alignment happens, as previous work already investigated this question
(
30
;
40
)
, we focused on why it seemingly stops after a period of early alignment, and explained how to allow alignment to continue after this period.

Biological plausibility directly motivates the study of feedback alignment, but the interventions used here should not be interpreted as biologically plausible learning algorithms in their current form. In particular, Muon requires orthogonalising a matrix-valued momentum update, which depends on coordinated information about the spectrum of an entire weight-update matrix rather than on purely synapse-local quantities. Although local plasticity rules such as Oja-like mechanisms can extract principal directions
(
36
;
45
)
, our implementation of Muon should be viewed as a probe of an alternative rule for updates, not as a direct model of synaptic plasticity.

The other mechanism we investigated was normalising the activities, in our case by using Batch Normalization
(
23
)
. Beyond its original motivation as a way of reducing internal covariate shift, Batch Normalization can be understood as helping deep networks preserve high-dimensional representations across layers. In unnormalised deep networks, the activity matrix over a batch can progressively collapse to a low-rank representation
(
21
;
14
)
. By contrast, Batch Normalization counteracts this rank collapse and helps maintain multiple active directions of variation in the hidden activities
(
14
;
13
)
. In addition, Batch Normalization often makes training less sensitive to initialisation, permits the use of larger learning rates, and improves gradient propagation
(
23
;
46
)
. While we did not explicitly control for these factors, the fact that the performance of feedback alignment with either Muon or Batch Normalization is similar in Table
1
supports our claim that the dimensionality of the gradient is an important aspect of feedback alignment. Although standard Batch Normalization is not strictly biologically plausible because it relies on batch-level statistics and global normalisation across examples, normalisation mechanisms are widespread in biological neural systems
(
8
)
. In particular, divisive normalisation and homeostatic regulation
(
51
;
47
)
could provide more local approximations that similarly stabilise activity scales and prevent representational collapse. Transformations of neural activity at the population level have been studied in the visual cortex
(
15
)
, and are thought to be behind its high dimensionality
(
41
;
18
;
49
;
15
)
.

Beyond biological plausibility, feedback alignment may be useful as a distinct optimisation system from standard backpropagation. Because FA uses fixed random feedback, it follows different parameter-space trajectories and can expose how optimisation techniques behave when the learning signal is only approximate. This makes FA a useful testbed for studying whether methods such as normalisation or update orthogonalisation improve learning by better conditioning BP gradients specifically, or by more generally shaping update geometry. Moreover, alternative credit-assignment rules can affect loss-landscape exploration
(
37
)
, for example, feedback alignment could use structured feedback that guides learning to different solutions than what backpropagation would reach. For instance, feedback alignment has been shown to allow better continual learning
(
16
)
.

We also tried adding a local loss at each layer, which aimed at making the activity dimensionality higher. Specifically, we used a loss introduced in
[
11
]
that minimises the covariance of the channels inside a layer; we did not backpropagate the loss of one layer to the previous layers. We followed the formulation used in
[
3
]
:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{decor}}(X)=\frac{1}{D}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{D}\left[\frac{1}{B-1}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\left(x_{bi}-\bar{x}_{i}\right)\left(x_{bj}-\bar{x}_{j}\right)\right]^{2},\bar{x}_{i}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}x_{bi}

(6)

It minimises the off-diagonal components of the covariance of the activity in a given layer for one batch. The goal of this loss is to make the dimensionality higher by reducing the redundancies of the representations inside a layer. It is related to a lower bound approximation to the rank of a matrix introduced in
[
14
]

r(X)=\frac{\operatorname{Tr}(M(X))^{2}}{\|M(X)\|_{F}^{2}},M(X)=\frac{X^{\top}X}{N}
, as minimising the off diagonal components of the covariance will have a similar objective to maximising the ratio of the squared trace to the squared frobenius norm of the covariance. We add the gradient of this loss weighted by
\lambda
to the gradient of the cross-entropy loss used for classification. We optimised the network using SGD and AdamW
[
33
]
.

We ran the same analysis as the one we did on Muon, and the results appear similar (Figure
5
). The accuracy does not decrease much when adding layers (Figure
5
A). When looking at the 4 convolutional layers network, we also see better behavior (Figure
5
B-G). The network trains faster and has a smoother curve (Figure
5
B). The weights align much more (Figure
5
C), and importantly, the gradients are more aligned, and the alignment is more stable (Figure
5
D). The geometry of the updates is also better behaved. The gradients are higher-dimensional (Figure
5
E), and their trajectory is also higher-dimensional (Figure
5
F). The features also exhibit higher dimensionality (Figure
5
G), which is due to the local loss we added. This increase in activity dimensionality allows higher-dimensional gradients, which means the optimisation will not get stuck in the lower-dimensional space that feedback alignment networks get trapped in.

Unfortunately, preliminary experiments seemed to suggest that it would not scale to the larger networks. More careful tuning of the loss might make it scale better.

Appendix C
Interpolating between SGD and Muon

(a)

Figure 6
:
A more balanced singular value spectrum improves performance.
Increasing the exponent in Freon optimiser
[
48
]
, which, until 0.5, flattens the spectrum of the updates, then makes it skew the other way, increases the performance of the feedback alignment networks.

To better understand how the distribution of the singular values of the updates impacts the performance of the network, we used the Freon optimiser
[
48
]
, which transforms the momentum matrix
M
to:
U\,\operatorname{diag}\!\left(\left(\frac{\sigma}{\|M\|_{q}}\right)^{q-1}\right)V^{\top}
, where
U\,\operatorname{diag}\!\left(\sigma\right)V^{\top}
is the SVD of
M
. The exponent
c=1-q/2
controls the transformation of the singular values. With
c=0
, the update is equivalent to SGD, and directions with large singular values dominate the update. As
c
increases, the spectrum of the update becomes progressively flatter, giving more weight to directions that would otherwise contribute very little. Using
c=0.5
corresponds to Muon, where all non-zero singular directions are weighted equally. Using
c>0.5
scales up the smaller singular values and scales down the bigger ones. This interpolation is relevant for our work since
c
controls the dimensionality of the updates, similarly to what we did in Figure
3
. When applied to feedback alignment models, performance improves as the Freon exponent is increased from the SGD regime toward the Muon regime (Figure
6
), supporting the claim that making the update higher-dimensional improves feedback alignment.

Appendix D
Full rank updates by adding noise

(a)

Figure 7
:
Adding noise to the updates does not make the FA models better.
We added Gaussian noise to the parameter updates, matching their norm or 0.1 of their norm. SGD deteriorates with even small noise compared to updates, whereas AdamW is not so affected.

The improvement from Muon and normalisation could be explained simply by increasing the dimensionality of the update, rather than by preserving useful update directions. We tested this by adding an isotropic update-noise control. After each SGD or Adam update, we measured the actual parameter change and added Gaussian noise rescaled to match the norm of that change or 0.1 of the norm:

W_{t+1}=W_{t}+\Delta_{t}+\lambda\frac{\|\Delta_{t}\|_{2}}{\|\xi_{t}\|_{2}}\xi_{t},\qquad\quad\xi_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I).

(7)

Where
\Delta_{t}
is the update obtained using the optimiser,
\lambda
is the scale that we set to 1 or 0.1, and
\xi_{t}
is Gaussian noise.
This produces high-dimensional perturbations with controlled magnitude while leaving the base optimiser and its state unchanged. In contrast to Muon and batch normalisation, adding isotropic update noise did not improve FA performance (Figure
7
). This suggests that higher dimensionality alone is not sufficient: the additional directions must remain aligned with the learning signal. Thus, the benefit of Muon is better interpreted as preserving or reweighting useful update directions, rather than merely injecting arbitrary high-rank motion.
