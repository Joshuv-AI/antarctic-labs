Abstract
This report is a Milestone 2 deliverable for FY2026 under work package MT-26AN020101,
supporting research and qualification activities within the Advanced Materials and Manufacturing
Technologies (AMMT) program. The report provides an update on laser-based additive
manufacturing of steels for nuclear applications conducted collaboratively by Argonne National
Laboratory (ANL) and Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL). Building upon previous
years’ efforts, the FY2026 work focused on continued optimization of additive manufacturing
processes and post-build heat treatments for Alloy 709 (A709), Grade 91 (G91), and Grade 92
(G92), together with comprehensive microstructural characterization and mechanical, creep, and
irradiation testing. The investigations encompass laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) conducted at
ANL and laser powder directed energy deposition (LP-DED) conducted at PNNL, providing
complementary insights into process–structure–property relationships relevant to qualification of
additively manufactured nuclear structural materials.
 • For LPBF A709, creep testing of SSJ3 and ASTM-size specimens demonstrated a strong
 dependence of rupture behavior on post-build condition, with solution-annealed material
 exhibiting shorter rupture lives than as-deposited and precipitation-treated conditions.
 Fractographic analysis associated crack initiation with chemically enriched cellular
 boundaries. Irradiation studies showed that as-deposited A709 exhibited greater resistance
 to irradiation-induced void formation than heat-treated conditions, while all evaluated
 A709 conditions demonstrated improved swelling resistance relative to LPBF 316H.
 Constant-load irradiation further revealed a strong stress dependence of void formation.
 CALPHAD calculations complemented these experimental studies by identifying
 opportunities to tailor alloy chemistry to suppress undesirable σ-phase formation while
 controlling M23C6 carbide precipitation.
 • LPBF investigations of G91 and G92 demonstrated comparable as-deposited
 microstructures but distinct responses to post-build heat treatment. G92 exhibited greater
 carbide and Laves-phase precipitation and consistently higher strength than G91 across the
 investigated temperatures while maintaining comparable ductility, demonstrating the
 influence of alloy chemistry on microstructural evolution and mechanical performance.
 • For LP-DED A709, tensile, creep, fractographic, atom probe tomography (APT), and X-
 ray computed tomography (XCT) investigations established strong relationships among

 1

 deposition-induced microstructures, heat treatment, and mechanical response. The as-
 deposited condition exhibited the highest tensile strength while maintaining substantial
 ductility, whereas combined solution annealing and precipitation treatment (SA+PT)
 provided the most favorable overall strength–ductility and creep response among the
 ruptured heat-treated conditions. APT identified nanoscale Nb- and C-rich MX precipitates
 associated with improved creep behavior, while XCT demonstrated that differences in
 creep performance could not be attributed solely to lack-of-fusion porosity. A neutron-
 irradiation matrix covering 500–600°C and doses of 2 and 10 dpa was also established to
 evaluate irradiation stability.
 • LP-DED G92 studies evaluated as-deposited, tempered, normalized, and normalized-plus-
 tempered conditions. The as-deposited material exhibited the highest strength but limited
 high-temperature ductility, while normalization provided improved property retention. The
 normalized-plus-tempered condition developed a fine dispersion of Nb- and V-rich MX
 carbonitrides considered important for long-term creep resistance, establishing it as a key
 condition for continued creep evaluation.

Collectively, the FY2026 LPBF and LP-DED investigations demonstrate that additive
manufacturing route, alloy chemistry, post-build thermal treatment, precipitation behavior,
process-induced defects, irradiation, and applied stress collectively govern the performance of
A709, G91, and G92. The results establish critical process–structure–property relationships and
identify promising processing and heat-treatment pathways for improving creep performance,
microstructural stability, and irradiation resistance, providing an important technical foundation
for the continued development and qualification of additively manufactured Fe-based structural
materials for advanced nuclear applications.

 Chapter 1: Introduction
Additive Manufacturing (AM) is a process of fabricating objects via a layer-by-layer deposition
from a 3d geometry model into complex near net shape components, thus reducing the need for
post process machining, welding, and brazing[1]. Recently, there has been increasing interest in
evaluating AM for the fabrication of components for energy industries. Of particular interest for
the current project, based on the Advanced Materials and Manufacturing Technologies (AMMT)
roadmap, is the incorporation of established and candidate advanced-reactor structural materials
into AM fabrication routes [2]. These alloys are the austenitic stainless steel: A709, and
ferritic/martensitic steels: Grade 91 and Grade 92 (henceforth referred to as G91 & G92).
Through the DOE-NE Advanced Reactor Technology (ART) down-selection process in prior
years, Alloy 709 (A709) has been designated for sodium-cooled fast reactor (SFR) applications
because of its superior high-temperature performance compared to that of conventional 316H
stainless steel[3]. Grade 92 (G-92) steel represents a third-generation creep-resistant
ferritic/martensitic (F/M) steel with tungsten and boron additions that enhance performance
beyond that of Grade 91 (G-91) steel. Despite the potential for superior creep resistance at
temperatures exceeding 600°C, G92 remains unqualified under nuclear codes with much less
documented AM research [4]. As part of this work, the three alloys were manufactured using laser
powder bed fusion (LPBF) at Argonne National Laboratory and laser powder directed energy
deposition (LP-DED) at Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL).
AM processing introduces rapid solidification and repeated thermal cycling that can alter
segregation, grain morphology, phase formation, and precipitate distributions. Defects and residual
stresses may further influence mechanical behavior. For A709, the stability of the austenitic
microstructure and strengthening precipitates is important, whereas G91 and G92 require
particular attention to martensite formation, tempering, and carbide/carbonitride distributions. As
such, the properties of wrought materials cannot be assumed to transfer directly to AM
components.
The current M2 report summarizes the initial findings of high temperature mechanical behavior,
including tensile and creep properties in LPBF and LPDED, as-built and heat-treated conditions.
The objectives are to determine how processing and heat treatments affect microstructure and
mechanical response, compare the investigated AM conditions with relevant wrought references,
and identify priorities for further development.

Detailed descriptions of the experimental methods are not repeated here, as they have been

 Chapter 2: Literature Review
As mentioned in the previous section, based on the work in earlier FYs, two labs, ANL & PNNL
focused on two main classes of steels as part of the current work package: (i) Austenitic Stainless
Steels, (ii) Ferritic/Martensitic steels. The following section gives a brief overview of the alloys
considered.

2.1 Austenitic Stainless Steels

2.1.1 A709(NF709)
First developed by the British in 1950s, the 20Cr25Ni/Nb stainless steel has been used as a fuel
cladding material for the British Advanced Gas-cooled Reactor (AGR) fleet since 1962[9].
Approximately 90,000 fuel pins were present in each AGR plant, and there were 14 plants
constructed and operated. While the creep strength of 20Cr25Ni/Nb was relatively low, it was
sufficient for its intended application. Based on this, in the 1980s, the Nippon Steel Corporation
modified the base chemical composition by adding B, Mo, and Ti to 20Cr25Ni/Nb in order to
strengthen the creep resistance for ultra-supercritical boilers. The resulting austenitic stainless steel
was trademarked NF 709[7]. The design criteria for developing the composition of NF 709 were
to have a stable austenite devoid of the sigma and other intermetallic phases under long-term
elevated-temperature service conditions, and creep-strengthened by carbonitride M(CN)
precipitated in a stable, fine dispersion. Through a DOE-NE ART material down-selection and
intermediate term testing program, Alloy 709, an advanced austenitic stainless-steel alloy, was
recommended as a Class A structural material for the SFR because of its overall superior structural
strength advantage[10][11]. While extensive work has been performed on A709 in the wrought
form to have it code-qualified, there is no available literature on the additive manufacturing aspect.

2.2 Ferritic/Martensitic Steels

2.2.1 Grade 91
G91 steel was developed as a 2nd generation creep resistant alloy. ORNL modified the composition
by adding small amounts of V and Nb to 9Cr–1Mo to form fine carbo-nitrides[4]. Broadly used in
fossil and nuclear power plants in components operating at temperatures up to ~650°C, it is the
current “workhorse” alloy and the only alloy being studied in the current work package which is
included in ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel (B&PV) Code Section III Division 5. Having a Cr
concentration of 9 wt. % gives very good creep strength while having a minimal increase in the

 11

DBTT. Significant work has been performed recently on the additive manufacturing of G91
steel[7], [8], [9], [10]. Blown powder technique, wire DED, and LPBF based techniques have all
been used to fabricate this alloy to understand the microstructural evolution, mechanical behavior,
corrosion, and in some cases even irradiation behavior[11]. In the case of LPBF sample, as-
deposited additively manufactured G91 steel had a microstructure of lower bainitic regions
surrounded by martensite. The as-deposited additively manufactured material had excellent tensile
mechanical properties with greater strength than the wrought material at room and elevated
temperatures, showing excellent promise for nuclear applications. Retention of strength at 300 and
600 °C for the as-deposited additively manufactured material was attributed to transitional carbides
in the lower bainitic regions.

2.2.2 Grade 92
To improve the creep properties of 2nd generation G91 alloy, G92 steel was developed as a 3rd
generation creep-resistant F/M steel with the addition of tungsten and minute amounts of boron.
While it has been reported to have better creep properties relative to G91, it has not been code
qualified[12], [13]. In practice, the microstructure is made up of tempered martensitic lath structure
which is stabilized by Cr rich M23C6 carbide, intra-lath MX type of Nb and V carbonitrides,
martensite phase transformation induced high dislocation density, and solid solution strengthening
from tungsten[14], [15]. The presence of tungsten in the M23C6 precipitate decreases growth rate
of the precipitate during creep exposure, which in turn increases the stability of the martensitic lath
structure of the steel on creep exposure[12].

3.1.2 ASTM Sized Creep Data
Creep tests were conducted in accordance with ASTM Standard E139-11, “Standard Test Methods
for Conducting Creep, Creep-Rupture, and Stress-Rupture Tests on Metallic Materials.” Once the
furnace reached the target temperature, the specimen was soaked for 1 hour before applying the
load stepwise until full loading was reached within 2 to 5 minutes. A load cell positioned directly
above the dead weight monitored the force applied at each increment, and data was logged using
WinCCS II software. Following specimen fracture, the furnace was air-cooled and the sample
removed along with the test rig. Overall length and minimum diameter were then measured by
carefully rejoining the broken pieces to calculate elongation and reduction of area. Table 1 lists the
conditions initiated in FY25-26. A total of 9 tests were initiated out of which 6 ruptured.

Figure 5 shows the creep curves for all samples tested under different temperature, stress, and
processing conditions. The dashed lines represent wrought samples previously tested as part of the
Code Case program, while the solid lines represent the additively manufactured (AM) samples
investigated in this study. The creep response shows a strong dependence on material condition,
temperature, and applied stress. The SA samples generally exhibit higher creep ductility than the
AD and PT conditions, whereas the latter show longer creep rupture lives under several test
conditions. Several wrought specimens accumulate approximately 35–40% creep strain within
~500–800 h, while some AM conditions maintain less than 1% strain for more than 1000–2000 h.
For example, the AD 650 °C/250 MPa condition remains near 0.25–0.30% strain beyond 2000 h,
while the SA 650 °C/250 MPa condition reaches approximately 0.7–0.8% after ~2500 h. The
differences in creep rate, rupture time, and final ductility indicate that processing and post-build
heat treatment significantly influence creep performance. Further analysis is underway to quantify
these differences and develop Larson–Miller parameter plots for comparison of the long-term
creep and rupture behavior of the AM and wrought materials.

4.2 Ferritic/Martensitic Steels: G92

4.2.1 Tensile Response of LP-DED Grade 92
Testing carried out in FY 2026 completed the N and N+T conditions, thereby completing the tensile
test matrix for LP-DED G-92 across all heat-treatment conditions and both build orientations.
As a martensitic 9Cr steel, the AD strength of G-92 is dominated by untempered martensite formed
during the rapid cooling and cyclic reheating inherent to DED. Accordingly, the AD condition was
by far the strongest, with AD-H and AD-V reaching approximately 1,650 and 1,580 MPa at RT
but fracturing at only 10–16% strain after a short hardening stage—behavior typical of a high-
dislocation-density, carbon-supersaturated matrix. Tempering alone reduced strength to
approximately 900–1,000 MPa through recovery of the martensitic substructure and carbide
precipitation. The N condition retained relatively high strength (N-H ≈ 1,300 MPa) with
continuous hardening to 12–13% strain, consistent with fresh martensite formed on cooling from
the austenitizing temperature, while N+T gave the lowest strength (approximately 600–650 MPa)
but the greatest elongation (14–18%), representing the fully tempered microstructure intended for
service. Horizontal specimens were consistently stronger than vertical ones. This anisotropy was
largest in the AD and N conditions and smallest after N+T, indicating that austenitization and
tempering partially homogenize the deposited microstructure, although residual porosity and lack-
of-fusion features along the build direction likely sustain the remaining orientation dependence.
At elevated temperature (Figure 35b), all conditions showed reduced flow stress and contracted
hardening. The AD specimens remained strongest (approximately 1,250–1,350 MPa) but failed at
only 4–5% strain, indicating pronounced loss of ductility. N-H provided the best combination,
sustaining approximately 1,250 MPa to nearly 15% elongation, whereas the tempered and N+T
conditions reached only 400–700 MPa with 8–11% elongation and exhibited early peak stresses
followed by extended decline. Overall, the completed matrix reveals a clear heat-treatment-
controlled strength–ductility trade-off: AD offers exceptional strength but inadequate ductility and
thermal stability, N+T provides the ductility and stability required for service at the expense of
strength, and the N condition offers the most attractive intermediate balance, particularly in the
horizontal orientation at elevated temperature.

4.2.4 Neutron Irradiation Plan of DED A709
The matrix in Figure 41 has been designed to isolate the combined effects of post-build heat
treatment, irradiation temperature, and displacement damage on the mechanical and
microstructural response of LP-DED A709. Three microstructural states are included because each
presents a fundamentally different starting condition for radiation damage accumulation. The AD
condition retains the fine cellular solidification substructure, high dislocation density, and solute
segregation characteristic of DED, all of which provide a high density of internal sinks for point
defects and may therefore confer intrinsic radiation tolerance. The solution-annealed condition
removes this substructure and provides a comparatively clean, low sink-density austenitic matrix,
serving as a reference against which the benefit of the as-built substructure can be quantified. The
SA+PT condition contains the Nb-rich MX carbide dispersion identified by atom probe
tomography and represents the candidate service microstructure; its behavior under irradiation is
critical, since MX particles may act as beneficial defect sinks but are also potentially susceptible
to irradiation-induced dissolution, coarsening, or ballistic mixing.

Two doses are included to establish the dose dependence of the response. The 2 dpa condition
captures the low-dose regime in which defect cluster and dislocation-loop populations develop and
hardening typically saturates, while the 10 dpa condition probes the higher-dose regime relevant

 58

to extended service, in which void swelling, precipitate instability, and radiation-induced
segregation become significant. Comparing the results for the two doses allows saturation behavior
of hardening and the onset of microstructural instability to be distinguished rather than inferred
from a single dose. The inclusion of both 500 and 600°C for the SA+PT condition addresses the
strong temperature dependence of defect mobility in austenitic steels. The lower temperature
favors retention of small defect clusters and greater irradiation hardening, whereas the higher
temperature promotes defect recombination and annealing but also accelerates thermally assisted
precipitate evolution and cavity nucleation. This pairing therefore identifies whether the candidate
microstructure is more vulnerable to hardening-dominated or swelling-dominated degradation
across the intended operating window.
Following irradiation, post-irradiation tensile testing of the SSJ-2 specimens will quantify
irradiation hardening, loss of strain-hardening capacity, and any reduction in ductility relative to
the unirradiated baseline already established in this program. These measurements directly address
the principal engineering concerns for structural application, namely irradiation-induced
embrittlement and the potential for flow localization. Because the tensile data alone cannot identify
the responsible defect populations, complementary transmission electron microscopy will be
performed to characterize dislocation-loop densities and size distributions, cavity and void
populations, and any changes in precipitate morphology or distribution. Atom probe tomography
will then be used to resolve the nanoscale chemistry that TEM cannot access, specifically
radiation-induced segregation at grain boundaries and prior-layer interfaces, solute redistribution
within the matrix, and the compositional stability of the MX carbides under displacement damage.
The integration of mechanical testing with correlative TEM and APT is essential because the
performance of DED A709 in a radiation environment will be determined by whether the process-
derived microstructural features that provide its favorable creep and tensile behavior remain stable
under irradiation. Establishing these structure–property relationships across a systematic matrix of
heat treatments, temperatures, and doses provides the mechanistic basis required for the eventual
qualification of additively manufactured A709 for nuclear structural service.

 Chapter 5: Comparison between LPBF and LPDED
As noted in the FY-2025 report [8], there are substantial differences between laser powder bed
fusion (LPBF) and laser powder directed energy deposition (LP-DED) processes, particularly in
terms of thermal history and cooling rates. Among these, cooling rate is the most critical parameter
influencing solidification dynamics, segregation behavior, and subsequent phase evolution. LPBF
typically achieves significantly higher cooling rates than LP-DED due to its smaller melt pool
dimensions and finer energy input control. These rapid solidification conditions in LPBF promote
the formation of highly refined microstructures, with pronounced non-equilibrium phases and
cellular/dendritic morphologies. In contrast, LP-DED, with its lower cooling rates and larger melt
pool volumes, tends to produce coarser microstructures with more equilibrium-like phase
distributions. These inherent differences lead to distinct thermokinetic pathways that ultimately
control precipitate stability, defect formation, and dislocation density. The comparative analysis of
LPBF and LP-DED G92 and A709 revealed process-dependent microstructural differences. As-
deposited G92 exhibited mixed ferrite/martensite microstructures, with LPBF showing higher
ferrite content. After tempering, LPBF showed phase-selective precipitation, whereas LP-DED
exhibited more uniform secondary-phase distributions. Normalization largely eliminated these
differences, producing comparable martensitic microstructures. For A709, LPBF exhibited higher
dislocation density, while LP-DED showed evidence of secondary-phase formation. Following
solution annealing, MX precipitates persisted in LPBF but dissolved in LP-DED. Precipitation-
treated and solution-annealed plus precipitation-treated conditions showed broadly similar
precipitate characteristics, demonstrating the significant influence of deposition route and heat
treatment on microstructural evolution in both alloys.

 Chapter 6: Conclusions and Future Work

6.1 Conclusions

This report advances the fundamental understanding of additive manufacturing for nuclear-grade
materials, building upon FY 2025 investigations through comprehensive process–structure–
property correlations for Fe-based alloys. The systematic evaluation of laser powder bed fusion
(LPBF) and laser powder directed energy deposition (LP-DED), supported by mechanical testing,
creep and irradiation studies, fractography, atom probe tomography (APT), X-ray computed
tomography (XCT), and CALPHAD modeling, has provided critical insights for optimizing G91,
G92, and A709 for nuclear structural applications.
 1. LPBF G91 & G92 Ferritic/Martensitic Steel: As-deposited G91 and G92 exhibited
 comparable microstructures, while heat treatment promoted precipitation in both alloys,
 with G92 showing greater carbide and Laves phase formation. G92 demonstrated
 significantly higher strength than G91 across the evaluated temperature range while
 maintaining comparable ductility with only a minimal reduction in elongation. These
 findings demonstrate the improved strength potential of G92 and emphasize the role of
 alloy chemistry and thermal treatment in controlling microstructural evolution and
 mechanical performance.
 2. LPBF A709 Austenitic Stainless-Steel Process-Property Relationships: Creep testing
 of SSJ3 and ASTM-size specimens showed that solution-annealed (SA) A709 had shorter
 rupture lives than as-deposited (AD) and precipitation-treated (PT) conditions, with crack
 initiation associated with Cr-rich and Cr-, Mo-, and C-rich cellular boundaries. Under
 irradiation, AD specimens exhibited greater resistance to void formation than SA and PT
 specimens, while all A709 conditions showed improved swelling resistance relative to
 LPBF 316H. Constant-load irradiation demonstrated a pronounced stress effect, with void
 density increasing exponentially with applied load. CALPHAD simulations further
 indicate that A709 chemistry can be optimized to minimize detrimental σ-phase
 precipitation while controlling M23C6 carbide fractions.
 3. LPDED G92 Ferritic/Martensitic Steel: As-deposited G92 exhibited the highest tensile
 strength (~1,650 MPa) but limited high-temperature ductility, while normalization
 provided the highest property retention. Normalizing and tempering reduced strength but

 64

 produced a fine, coarsening-resistant Nb- and V-rich MX carbonitride dispersion important
 for long-term creep resistance.
 4. LPDED A709 Process–Property Relationships: The AD condition exhibited the highest
 LP-DED tensile strength (~650–850 MPa) while retaining fracture strains above 30%,
 whereas SA produced the greatest elongation (40–45%). The combined SA+PT treatment
 provided the best overall strength–ductility balance and significantly improved creep
 behavior, doubling the SA rupture life (350 vs. 180 h) while increasing creep elongation
 from 9% to 33%. APT identified Nb- and C-rich MX precipitates that promote
 intragranular deformation and delay boundary cavity coalescence. XCT revealed
 comparable lack-of-fusion porosity in SA and SA+PT, confirming that their creep-
 performance differences primarily originate from precipitate and matrix state rather than
 defect population.

This comprehensive investigation demonstrates the technical viability of additive manufacturing
for nuclear-grade Fe-based alloys, with G91 & G92 ferritic/martensitic steels and A709 austenitic
stainless steel achieving mechanical properties that meet or exceed conventional processing
benchmarks. The successful production of complex geometries with tailored microstructures
through controlled thermal histories validates AM as a transformative manufacturing technology
for nuclear applications.

6.2 Future Work

Building upon the current findings, the following integrated research framework in coming years
addresses critical knowledge gaps and establishes the foundation for understanding both the
microstructural evolution and mechanical behavior of additively manufactured A709 and G92.
Phase I: Material Fabrication and High-Temperature Performance
 • Fabricate LPBF A709 and G91/G92 specimens using optimized parameters. Evaluate
 creep, creep-fatigue, and long-term elevated-temperature behavior, as a function of build
 orientation and as-built/heat-treated condition.
 • Benchmark LPBF results against wrought materials and compare tensile, creep, and
 fracture performance.
 • Complete high-temperature creep testing of A709 and G91/G92 as-built and heat-treated
 materials.
 • Benchmark LP-DED results against wrought materials and compare tensile, creep, and
 fracture performance.
Phase II: Microstructure Mechanical Properties Correlations
 • Characterize LPBF A709 and G91/G92 before and after creep and creep fatigue testing
 using advanced characterization techniques (SEM and TEM) and correlate microstructural
 features with mechanical properties.
 • Perform SEM, TEM, and selected APT on LP-DED A709 and G91/G92 before and after
 creep testing; correlate precipitation, segregation, defects, boundaries, and creep damage
 with mechanical performance. Selected APT on LPBF A709 and G92 will also be carried
 out.
Phase III: Irradiation and Microstructure Correlations
 • Provide as-built and heat-treated LPBF G92 for ex-situ ion irradiation to be performed as
 part of the Environmental Effects Technical Area. PIE will be performed as part of the
 Advanced Materials and Manufacturing Technical Area to understand the irradiation
 induced defects. Results will be incorporated in the M3 milestone report: Creep, Creep
 Fatigue, and Ion-irradiation benchmarking of LP-DED and LPBF A709 & G91/G92:
 Performance Mapping and Microstructure–Thermodynamics Linkages.
 • Provide as-built and heat-treated LPBF A709 samples for ex-situ ion irradiation and PIE
 as part of the Environmental Effects Technical Area. The data generated will be included

 (LAIN) Framework Using Ion- and Neutron-Irradiated LPBF Iron-Based Alloys: a case
 study (ANL)”.
 • Provide as-built and heat-treated LP-DED A709 and G-92 samples to ANL, where ex situ
 ion irradiation will be performed as part of the Environmental Effects Technical Area.
 PNNL will perform PIE, as part of the Advanced Materials and Manufacturing Technical
 Area to understand the irradiation induced defects. The results will be incorporated into the

 and LPBF A709 & G91/G92: Performance Mapping and Microstructure Thermodynamics
