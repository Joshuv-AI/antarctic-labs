Semi-discrete convex order and Laguerre tessellation fitting
David P. Bourne, Thomas O. Gallouët, Quentin Mérigot, Andrea Natale

Abstract
. Laguerre tessellations offer an efficient way to parameterize a large class of convex partitions of Euclidean space using only a set of points and scalar weights. For this reason, they have become popular in computational geometry, imaging and numerical analysis, both as a modeling and a discretization tool. In this paper we study the problem of reconstructing a Laguerre tessellation with prescribed cell volumes from the barycenters of its cells. We establish a geometric interpretation of this problem in terms of the set of discrete measures dominated in convex order by an absolutely continuous measure. In particular, we show that the reconstruction problem can be solved approximately by computing a Wasserstein projection onto this set. More generally, our method can also be applied to fit a Laguerre tessellation to an arbitrary set of barycenters. We give a concrete application of this in materials science, of fitting a Laguerre tessellation to an electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) image of a steel.



1.
Introduction

Laguerre tessellations, also known as weighted Voronoi tessellations, power diagrams
[
6
]
, affine partitions
[
1
]
, or regular partitions
[
28
]
, are partitions of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
into convex cells that generalize Voronoi tessellations. Specifically,
given a vector of distinct generators
Y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{N})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
, i.e.,
y_{i}\neq y_{j}
for all
i\neq j
,
and a vector of weights
\phi=(\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the associated Laguerre tessellation is the partition of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
defined by the cells

L_{i}(\phi,Y)\coloneqq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,;\,\langle x,y_{i}\rangle-\phi_{i}\geq\langle x,y_{j}\rangle-\phi_{j}~\forall i\neq j\}.

Setting
\phi_{i}=|y_{i}|^{2}/2
, the definition above yields the classical Voronoi tessellation associated with
Y
.
In general, Laguerre tessellations form a strict subset of the space of all convex
tessellations of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. However, it turns out that for
d\geq 3
all convex
tessellations satisfying some mild geometric requirements (specifically, “simple” tessellations) are always Laguerre
[
5
, Theorem 4]
.

Because of their versatility, Laguerre tessellations have become an established modeling tool in various fields, including biology
[
10
]
, optics
[
31
]
, and materials science
[
11
]
to name a few.
In particular, they are often used to describe the
microstructure of materials such as metals or foams. A common problem in this context
consists in computing a Laguerre tessellation with prescribed geometrical properties coming
from experimental observations
[
29
,
34
,
11
,
23
]
. Motivated by these applications, in this work we will study the following problem:

Question 1
.

Can one recover a set of generators and weights associated with a given Laguerre tessellation provided only the volumes and the barycenters of its cells?

We refer to Figure
1
for an illustration of the problem, and to Section
1.1
for more about the physical motivation. This problem was originally posed in
[
12
]
. In Theorem 4.5 of
[
12
]
, the authors show that a Laguerre tessellation is uniquely determined by the volumes and barycenters of its cells. However, the generators and weights are not unique in general.

The key idea of this work is to characterize the barycenters of Laguerre cells with prescribed volumes in terms of convex order between probability measures; see Section
1.3
.
This establishes a direct connection between the reconstruction problem and semi-discrete optimal transport.
We leverage this connection to propose an algorithm that approximately solves the reconstruction problem. Specifically, our method consists in computing a Wasserstein projection onto the set of measures in convex order with a given absolutely continuous measure. Importantly, the proposed approach can be used more generally on experimental data which does not necessarily arise from a Laguerre tessellation.

Figure 1.
We seek to reconstruct a Laguerre tessellation given its cells’ barycenters (i.e., their centroids) and volumes (computed with respect to a given density; here constant on a star), represented by the disks on the left. The empty dots on the right are the unknown generators of the tessellation.

1.1.
Motivation from materials science.

Question
1
was inspired by
[
12
]
and the image reconstruction problem in
[
34
]
, which is about reconstructing the microstructure of polycrystalline materials from incomplete data, namely, far-field three-dimensional X-ray diffraction data (3DXRD).
Polycrystalline materials, such as steel, are composed of
grains
, which are regions where the atoms form a crystal lattice.
The grains form a tessellation of the material; see Figure
15
(left), where the colors correspond to the grains. The 3DXRD data consists of the volumes and barycenters of the grains, but the grains themselves are unknown. In
[
34
]
the authors reconstruct the grains by finding a Laguerre tessellation that approximately matches the measured volumes and barycenters, using stochastic optimization, where each cell in the Laguerre tessellation represents a grain.
This reconstruction problem has also been studied for example by
[
29
,
36
,
12
]
. We propose a solution using convex optimization and apply it to reconstruct an image of steel (see Section
6.3.2
).

1.2.
Semi-discrete optimal transport

Laguerre tessellations with prescribed cell volumes arise naturally in the theory of optimal transport
[
4
,
32
]
. Let
\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denote the set of probability measures with finite first moments, i.e., such that
\int|x|\mathrm{d}\mu(x)<\infty
. Let
\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be an absolutely continuous measure and define

\Delta_{N}\coloneqq\{Y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{N})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}~~;~~\exists~1\leq i<j\leq N\text{ such that }y_{i}=y_{j}\}.

Given a vector of volumes
v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{N})\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{N}
satisfying
\sum_{i}v_{i}=1
, for any
Y\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}\setminus\Delta_{N}
,
there always exists a vector of weights
\phi^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that

(1)

\rho(L_{i}(\phi^{*},Y))=v_{i},\quad\forall\,i=1,\ldots,N.

Moreover, there is a unique Laguerre tessellation that satisfies this condition (see
[
4
, Theorem 3]
or also
[
32
, Section 4]
).

To see this, consider the following semi-discrete optimal transport problem:

(2)

\max\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle x,y\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y)\;;\;\gamma\in\Gamma(\rho,\nu_{N}(v,Y))\right\},

where
\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
denotes the set of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
with marginals
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
and
\nu_{N}(v,Y)
is the discrete measure supported on
Y
with masses
v
, i.e.,

(3)

\nu_{N}(v,Y)\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_{i}\delta_{y_{i}}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

The term semi-discrete refers here to the fact that one two marginals of the plan
\gamma
is absolutely continuous while the other is discrete. Since
\rho
is absolutely continuous, Brenier’s
Theorem
[
37
, Section 1.3.1]

guarantees that problem (
2
) admits a unique solution
\gamma
, which is characterized as the only transport plan
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying
\gamma=(\mathrm{Id},\nabla u)_{\#}\rho
and

(4)

(\nabla u)_{\#}\rho=\nu_{N}(v,Y),

where
u:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is a convex function known as Brenier potential, and
\#
denotes the push-forward of measures. It can be shown that
u
necessarily takes the form

(5)

u(x)=\max_{i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}}\left\{\langle x,y_{i}\rangle-\phi_{i}\right\},

for an appropriate vector of weights
\phi
; see, e.g.,
[
32
, Proposition 37]
. Consequently,

\nabla u(x)=y_{i}\quad\text{for a.e. }x\in L_{i}(\phi,Y).

By expressing condition (
4
) with this ansatz, one deduces the existence of a vector of weights
\phi^{*}
satisfying (
1
). Choosing
\phi=\phi^{*}
in (
5
) yields a Brenier potential for the transport problem (
2
). Moreover, the plan
\gamma^{*}
defined by

(6)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\gamma^{*}(x,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{L_{i}(\phi^{*},Y)}\varphi(x,y_{i})\,\mathrm{d}\rho(x)\,,

for all
\varphi\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
is the unique solution of problem (
2
).

1.3.
Convex order

Two probability measures
\nu,\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
are said to be in convex order, denoted
\nu\preceq_{C}\rho
, if and only if

(7)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\nu\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\rho

for all convex functions
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. By Strassen’s Theorem
[
38
]
, a convex order
relationship between two measures is equivalent to the existence of a martingale coupling between them (see Lemma
3.2
). This fact has made convex order relations a central tool in economics and finance applications, for example. In particular, common problems in this context are the design of sampling algorithms preserving convex order relations or, conversely, statistical tests to infer convex order relationships from samples. Recently, these problems have been approached using Wasserstein projections: sampling strategies were proposed in
[
2
,
3
]
, while statistical tests for convex order were developed in
[
25
,
26
]
.

The Wasserstein projection of a given probability measure
\nu
onto the set of measures dominated by
\rho
in the convex order
is found by solving

(8)

\inf\{W^{2}_{2}(\mu,\nu)\,;\mu\preceq_{C}\rho\}

where
W_{2}
denotes the 2-Wasserstein distance (see equation (
36
) for the definition). Remarkably, this problem can also be recast as an instance of weak optimal transport
[
22
,
21
]
; this is a class of optimization problems over couplings
\gamma\in\Gamma(\rho,\nu)
as in (
2
), but where the functional minimized is
not linear in
\gamma
.
To be precise, problem (
8
) is equivalent to a barycentric weak optimal transport problem; see for example
[
21
, Proposition 1.1]
or
[
2
, Theorem 2.1]
.
This interpretation has been crucial for the recent development of numerical algorithms to solve projection problems as in (
8
) in the fully discrete setting, where all measures are discrete
[
3
,
20
,
25
]
.
To the best of our knowledge, no numerical strategies for the semi-discrete setting (where
\rho
in equation (
8
) is absolutely continuous and
\nu
is discrete) have been proposed prior to this work.

1.4.
Contributions and structure of the paper

Let
\mathcal{C}_{N}(v,\rho)
be the set of particle positions
B=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{N})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
associated with discrete measures
\nu_{N}(v,B)
that satisfy (
7
),
i.e.,

\mathcal{C}_{N}(v,\rho)\coloneqq\{B=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{N})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}\,;\,\nu_{N}(v,B)\preceq_{C}\rho\},

where
\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a given absolutely continuous probability measure with bounded density with respect to Lebesgue, and as before
v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{N})\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{N}
with
\sum_{i}v_{i}=1
.

Using (
7
) and the definition of convexity one can verify that
\mathcal{C}_{N}(v,\rho)
is a convex set.
Our main contributions concern the structure of this set and its relation to the reconstruction problem stated in Question
1
.
Specifically, we will show that the exposed points of the set
\mathcal{C}_{N}(v,\rho)
are the vectors
B=(b_{i})_{i}
with

b_{i}=\frac{1}{v_{i}}\int_{L_{i}(\phi^{*},Y)}x\,\mathrm{d}\rho(x)\,,

where
Y=(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}\setminus\Delta_{N}
is any vector of generators and
\phi^{*}
is such that condition (
1
) holds. In other words, there exists

Y\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}\setminus\Delta_{N}
and
\phi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that, for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
,
L_{i}(\phi,Y)
has measure
v_{i}
and barycenter
b_{i}
(with respect to
\rho
) if and only if
(b_{1},\ldots,b_{N})
is an exposed point of
\mathcal{C}_{N}(v,\rho)
. This answers an open problem posed in
[
12
]
.
Moreover we show that the extreme points of
\mathcal{C}_{N}(v,\rho)
can be constructed in a similar way but replacing Laguerre tessellations by an inductive type of tessellation introduced in
[
1
]
, which we name hierarchical Laguerre tessellations.

Similar results are already present in the literature in different contexts. In
[
18
]
,
Ekeland and Schachermayer already identified a connection between convex order and optimal transport that relies on interpreting random variables as exposing directions, which is essentially equivalent to our point of view. However, they do not consider specifically the semi-discrete case, which allows for a very explicit characterization of exposed and extreme points.
On the other hand, in the fully-discrete case,
i.e., when also
\rho
is a fixed discrete measure, the set
\mathcal{C}_{N}(v,\rho)
coincides with the so-called
gravity body
introduced by Brieden and Gritzmann
[
15
]
to study balanced clusterings of points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

[
14
]
. In that case,
\mathcal{C}_{N}(v,\rho)
is a polytope and its vertices correspond to the barycenters of a certain class of Laguerre tessellations.
Our results represent the natural extension of such a characterization to the case of an absolutely continuous reference measure
\rho
.

Exploiting our characterization of the set
\mathcal{C}_{N}(v,\rho)
, we provide sufficient conditions that guarantee that, given a collection of barycenters
B
of a Laguerre tessellation, one can recover a Laguerre tessellation whose cell barycenters are arbitrary close to
B
by solving
the
Wasserstein projection problem (
8
).
Finally, we provide two numerical algorithms to
solve this reconstruction problem, or more generally problem (
8
) in the semi-discrete setting,
i.e.,
whenever
\rho
is absolutely continuous and
\nu
is discrete. Importantly, our algorithm can also be applied to solve the
fitting problem
, which corresponds to the case where
B
is not necessarily a collection of barycenters of a Laguerre tessellation.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
we introduce the concept of a hierarchical Laguerre tessellation and describe its relation with classical Laguerre tessellations. In Section
3
we characterize the exposed and extreme points of the set
\mathcal{C}_{N}(v,\rho)
. We also prove in Proposition
3.4
that a hierarchical Laguerre tessellation is uniquely determined by the volumes and barycenters of its cells. This generalizes Theorem 4.5 in
[
12
]
. In Section
4
we study the projection problem onto to
\mathcal{C}_{N}(v,\rho)
, and in Section
5
we describe how one can use it to recover a Laguerre tessellation with prescribed cell volumes from the cell barycenters. Finally, we propose different convex optimization methods to solve the projection problem and show numerical results in Section
6
, including an application in materials science of fitting a Laguerre tessellation to an EBSD image of steel provided by Tata Steel Netherlands.

2.
Hierarchical Laguerre tessellations

In this section we describe the concept of a hierarchical Laguerre tessellation, which has already appeared in the literature for different types of applications
[
1
,
24
]
. Here, we focus on the case where the cell volumes of the tessellations are assigned, and describe the precise relation between hierarchical Laguerre tessellations and classical ones. This will be useful in the next section to characterize the set of discrete measures dominated in convex order by a given measure.

We start by recalling that a convex tessellation of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a list
(P_{1},\ldots,P_{N})
of (possibly empty) closed convex sets
P_{i}
, called cells, with pairwise disjoint interiors such that

\bigcup_{i}{P_{i}}=\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Let
\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a reference probability measure
that is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure.
We will also denote by
\rho
its density with respect to the Lebesgue measure, which we assume to be bounded.
We will be interested in tessellations with fixed cell volumes with respect to
\rho
.
More precisely, given a vector
{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{N})\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{N}
with
\sum_{i}v_{i}=1
, we define the set of convex tessellations with volumes
{v}
as follows:

\mathcal{T}^{N}_{\mathrm{conv}}(v,\rho)\coloneqq\{(P_{i})_{i=1}^{N}\text{ convex tessellation of }\mathbb{R}^{d}\,;\,\rho(P_{i})=v_{i}~\forall i\}\,.

Similarly, we define the set of Laguerre tessellations with volumes
v
as follows:

\mathcal{T}^{N}_{\mathrm{Lag}}(v,\rho)\coloneqq\{(P_{i})_{i=1}^{N}\text{ Laguerre tessellation of }\mathbb{R}^{d}\,;\,\rho(P_{i})=v_{i}~\forall i\}\,.

Clearly
\mathcal{T}^{N}_{\mathrm{Lag}}(v,\rho)\subset\mathcal{T}^{N}_{\mathrm{conv}}(v,\rho)
. However there are convex tessellations that are not Laguerre tessellations. Typical examples in any dimension are binary partitions by hyperplanes (see Figure
4
, right). In
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, however, we have further counterexamples due to stronger geometric constraints implied by the definition of Laguerre tessellations, illustrated in Figure
2
.

Figure 2.
An example of convex tessellation of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
which is not a (hierarchical) Laguerre tessellation. The two-dimensional cells of the tessellation have as boundaries the solid lines. In order for this tessellation to be Laguerre, it should be possible to recover it as the projection of a polyhedron in

\mathbb{R}^{3}
, but this is impossible if the dashed lines do not meet at a single point. In fact if three planes intersect in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, their common intersection is either a line or a single point.

By identifying the convex cells of a given tessellation with the associated characteristic
functions, we can interpret
\mathcal{T}^{N}_{\mathrm{conv}}
and
\mathcal{T}^{N}_{\mathrm{Lag}}
as subsets of
(L^{\infty}(\rho))^{N}
, which we equip with the product weak-* topology.
Using
this
topology, we now characterize the closure of
\mathcal{T}^{N}_{\mathrm{Lag}}(v,\rho)
in terms of hierarchical Laguerre tessellations.

Definition 2.1

(Hierarchical Laguerre Tessellation)
.

A hierarchical Laguerre tessellation is defined recursively as follows:

•

Every Laguerre tessellation is a hierarchical Laguerre tessellation;

•

If
(P_{i})_{1\leq i\leq N}
is a hierarchical Laguerre tessellation with
N
cells and if
(Q_{j})_{1\leq i\leq M}
is a Laguerre tessellation with
M
cells, then for any
i_{0}\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, the tessellation with
N+M-1
cells

P_{1},\ldots,P_{i_{0}-1},P_{i_{0}}\cap Q_{1},P_{i_{0}}\cap Q_{2},\ldots,P_{i_{0}}\cap Q_{M},P_{i_{0}+1},\ldots,P_{N}

is also a hierarchical Laguerre tessellation.

Informally, given a hierarchical Laguerre tessellation
(P_{i})_{i}
, the tessellation obtained by partitioning a given cell
P_{i}
with a Laguerre tessellation is also a hierarchical Laguerre tessellation; see Figure
4
(right).
In particular, any recursive binary partition of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by hyperplanes defines a hierarchical Laguerre tesselation.
As for Laguerre tessellations, we denote the set of hierarchical Laguerre tessellations with
N
cells of prescribed volumes as follows:

\mathcal{T}^{N}_{\mathrm{hLag}}(v,\rho)\coloneqq\{(P_{i})_{i=1}^{N}\text{ hierarchical Laguerre tessellation of }\mathbb{R}^{d}\,;\,\rho(P_{i})=v_{i}~\forall i\}\,.

Proposition 2.2
.

\overline{\mathcal{T}^{N}_{\mathrm{Lag}}(v,\rho)}=\mathcal{T}^{N}_{\mathrm{hLag}}(v,\rho)
.

Proof.

Let us show that
\overline{\mathcal{T}^{N}_{\mathrm{Lag}}(v,\rho)}\subseteq\mathcal{T}^{N}_{\mathrm{hLag}}(v,\rho)
.
Given any sequence of tessellations
((L_{i}^{n})_{i})_{n}\subset\mathcal{T}^{N}_{\mathrm{Lag}}
, let

(Y^{n})_{n}\subset(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}\setminus\Delta_{N}

be the associated sequence of generators, shifted and normalized so that
y^{n}_{1}=0
and
\max_{i\neq k}|y_{i}^{n}-y_{k}^{n}|=1
.
(This is always possible thanks to the invariance properties of Laguerre tessellations with respect to translations and dilations of the generators; see also
[
33
, Proposition 6]
.)
Up to extracting a subsequence (not relabelled), for some
2\leq M\leq N
and
Y^{\infty}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{M}\setminus\Delta_{M}
, and a surjective map
\sigma:\{1,\ldots,N\}\rightarrow\{1,\ldots,M\}
,

y^{n}_{i}\rightarrow y^{\infty}_{\sigma(i)}\,,\quad\forall i=1,\ldots,N.

Moreover,

(9)

\nu_{N}(v,Y^{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_{i}\delta_{y_{i}^{n}}\rightharpoonup\nu_{M}(w,Y^{\infty})=\sum_{j=1}^{M}w_{j}\delta_{y_{i}^{\infty}}\,,\quad w_{j}=\sum_{i\in\sigma^{-1}(j)}v_{i},

where the convergence is understood as weak convergence of measures, i.e., in duality with continuous bounded functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Let
(\gamma^{n})_{n}
be the sequence of optimal transport plans from
\rho
to
\nu_{N}(v,Y^{n})
, obtained by solving problem (
2
). By stability of optimal plans (see, e.g., Theorem 5.20 in
[
41
]
), this converges weakly to the unique optimal transport plan from
\rho
to
\nu_{M}(w,Y^{\infty})
,

\gamma=\sum_{j=1}^{M}{\rho|_{L^{\infty}_{j}}\otimes\delta_{y_{j}^{\infty}}}\,,

where
(L^{\infty}_{j})_{j}
is the Laguerre tessellation with generators
Y^{\infty}
and volumes
{w}
. In particular, denoting

(10)

Q^{n}_{j}\coloneqq\bigcup_{i\in\sigma^{-1}(j)}L_{i}^{n}\,.

then
\rho|_{Q^{n}_{j}}\rightharpoonup\rho|_{L^{\infty}_{j}}
weakly as
n\rightarrow\infty
, from which we can deduce that also the corresponding characteristic
functions converge, i.e.,

\mathbf{1}_{Q^{n}_{j}}\rightarrow\mathbf{1}_{L^{\infty}_{j}}\quad\text{weakly-* in }L^{\infty}(\rho).

If
M=N
, we would be already done, since
(L_{j}^{\infty})_{j}
is a Laguerre tessellation. Otherwise, let
1\leq j\leq M
be any index such that
\tilde{N}\coloneqq|\sigma^{-1}(j)|>1
and define
Q^{n}_{j}
as above.
We shift and normalize the set
\tilde{Y}^{n}=(y_{i}^{n})_{i\in\sigma^{-1}(j)}
so that
y^{n}_{i_{1}}=0
for some
i_{1}\in\sigma^{-1}(j)
and

\max\{|y_{i}^{n}-y_{k}^{n}|\,;\,i,k\in\sigma^{-1}(j),\;i\neq k\}=1
.
Up to extracting a subsequence (not relabelled), for some
2\leq\tilde{M}\leq\tilde{N}
and
\tilde{Y}^{\infty}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\tilde{M}}\setminus\Delta_{\tilde{M}}
, and a surjective map
\sigma:\{1,\ldots,\tilde{N}\}\rightarrow\{1,\ldots,\tilde{M}\}
,

y^{n}_{i}\rightarrow y^{\infty}_{\sigma(i)}\,,\quad\forall i=1,\ldots,\tilde{N}.

We can then proceed as before, with
\rho
replaced by
\rho|_{Q^{n}_{j}}
, for which
\rho|_{Q^{n}_{j}}\rightharpoonup\rho|_{L^{\infty}_{j}}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.
Iterating this argument implies
that
\overline{\mathcal{T}^{N}_{\mathrm{Lag}}}\subseteq\mathcal{T}^{N}_{\mathrm{hLag}}
.

Let us prove that
{\mathcal{T}^{N}_{\mathrm{hLag}}}\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{T}^{N}_{\mathrm{Lag}}}
. Given any hierarchical Laguerre tessellation
(L_{i})_{i}\in\mathcal{T}^{N}_{\mathrm{hLag}}(v,\rho)
, using Lemma 3.5 in
[
1
]
, we can construct a sequence of Laguerre tessellations
((\tilde{L}_{i}^{n})_{i})_{n}

with generators
Y^{n}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}\setminus\Delta_{N}
(but possibly different volumes) such that
\tilde{L}_{i}^{n}\rightarrow L_{i}
locally in the Hausdorff metric. Since
\tilde{L}_{i}^{n}
and
L_{i}
are convex sets, this implies

(11)

\mathbf{1}_{\tilde{L}^{n}_{i}}\rightarrow\mathbf{1}_{L_{i}}\quad\text{weakly-* in }L^{\infty}(\rho);

see
[
9
]
, for example.
In particular, this means that

v_{i}^{n}\coloneqq\rho(\tilde{L}_{i}^{n})\rightarrow v_{i}

for all
i
.
Let
({L}_{i}^{n})_{i}
be the Laguerre tessellation with generators
Y^{n}
, but with weights chosen so that
\rho({L}_{i}^{n})=v_{i}
for all
i
. We now show that, up to the extraction of a subsequence,
{L}^{n}_{i}
also converges towards
L_{i}
for all
i
, which implies that
\mathcal{T}^{N}_{\mathrm{hLag}}\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{T}^{N}_{\mathrm{Lag}}}
.
Proceeding as before, we can assume that, for some
2\leq M\leq N
and
Y^{\infty}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{M}\setminus\Delta_{M}
, and a surjective map
\sigma:\{1,\ldots,N\}\rightarrow\{1,\ldots,M\}
,

y^{n}_{i}\rightarrow y^{\infty}_{\sigma(i)}\,,\quad\forall i=1,\ldots,N.

Denoting
{v}^{n}\coloneqq(v_{i}^{n})_{i}
, and
w
as defined in equation (
9
), we have

\nu_{N}(v^{n},Y^{n})\rightharpoonup\nu_{M}(w,Y^{\infty})\,,

and also

\nu_{N}(v,Y^{n})\rightharpoonup\nu_{M}(w,Y^{\infty}).

Using again the stability of optimal transport maps we deduce that

\mathbf{1}_{Q^{n}_{j}}\rightarrow\mathbf{1}_{L^{\infty}_{j}}\quad\text{weakly-* in }L^{\infty}(\rho),

where
Q^{n}_{j}
is again defined as in (
10
)
and
(L_{j}^{\infty})_{j}
is the Laguerre tessellation with volumes
{w}
and generators
Y^{\infty}
. Moreover, due to (
11
), we also have

L_{j}^{\infty}=\bigcup_{i\in\sigma^{-1}(j)}L_{i}\,.

Iterating the argument as before we obtain the result.
∎

3.
Semi-discrete convex order

In this section we study the set of discrete measures of prescribed masses in convex order with respect to
\rho
. We will give a characterization of this set as a convex body
in

\mathbb{R}^{dN}
, and in terms of measures supported on the cell barycenters of Laguerre and hierarchical Laguerre tessellations.

As before, we fix a vector
v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{N})\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{N}
such that
\sum_{i}v_{i}=1
, and
an absolutely continuous
reference measure
\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with bounded density.
We study the following set:

\mathcal{C}_{N}(v,\rho)\coloneqq\{B\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}~;~\nu_{N}(v,B)\preceq_{C}\rho\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{dN},

where the notation
\preceq_{C}
was defined in equation (
7
) and
we recall that

\nu_{N}(v,B)\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_{i}\delta_{b_{i}}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\,,

for any
B=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{N})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
.
For brevity, we will omit to indicate the dependency of
\mathcal{C}_{N}
on
\rho
and
{v}
when it is clear from the context. It will be convenient to use the weighted
l^{2}
-metric on
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
defined by

\langle Y,Z\rangle_{v}\coloneqq\sum_{i}\langle y_{i},z_{i}\rangle v_{i},

for any
Y=(y_{i})_{i}
and
Z=(z_{i})_{i}
in
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
. Moreover, we will denote
\|Y\|_{v}^{2}\coloneqq\langle Y,Y\rangle_{v}
.

3.1.
Basic properties of
\mathcal{C}_{N}

By definition, a point
B\in\mathcal{C}_{N}
if and only if

\sum_{i}\varphi(b_{i})v_{i}\leq{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\rho}

for any convex function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
In particular,
\mathcal{C}_{N}
is convex.
It is also non-empty because, by Jensen’s inequality,
(\mathrm{bary}(\rho),\ldots,\mathrm{bary}(\rho))\in\mathcal{C}_{N}(v,\rho)
, where

\mathrm{bary}(\rho)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x\,\mathrm{d}\rho(x)\,.

The set
\mathcal{C}_{N}

is also closed, since the functions
\varphi
defining the set are continuous. Moreover
\mathcal{C}_{N}
is bounded, since we can
take
\varphi(x)=|x|
.
Now, if
B\in\mathcal{C}_{N}
,
by taking
\varphi(x)=\pm x
,
we obtain that

(12)

\sum_{i}b_{i}v_{i}=\mathrm{bary}(\rho)\,.

Due to this constraint
\mathcal{C}_{N}
cannot have full dimension, and more precisely
\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{C}_{N})\leq(N-1)d
.
(Recall that the dimension of a convex set is defined to be the dimension of its affine hull.)
Since we are supposing that
\rho
is absolutely continuous, the following equality holds:

Lemma 3.1
.

\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{C}_{N})=(N-1)d
.

Proof.

We argue by induction on
N
.
The statement is trivially true for
N=1
.
Suppose it is also true for a given
N
, and fix a vector of admissible volumes
v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{N+1})
.
Let
\tilde{\rho}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be any density such that
0\leq\tilde{\rho}\leq\rho
and
\tilde{\rho}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=v_{N+1}
.
Let
\tilde{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{N})
.
By Jensen’s inequality, we can construct
B=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{N+1})\in\mathcal{C}_{N+1}(v,\rho)
by setting

b_{N+1}=\mathrm{bary}(\tilde{\rho})\coloneqq\frac{1}{\tilde{\rho}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{\rho}(x),

and taking any
\tilde{B}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{N}){\in\mathcal{C}_{N}(\tilde{v},\rho-\tilde{\rho})}
so that

\nu_{N}(\tilde{{v}},\tilde{B})\preceq_{C}\rho-\tilde{\rho}\,.

Hence we just need to show that
\tilde{\rho}
can be chosen so that

b_{N+1}=\mathrm{bary}(\tilde{\rho})
is an arbitrary point in a set of dimension
d
, i.e.,

\{\mathrm{bary}(\tilde{\rho})\,;\,\tilde{\rho}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\;0\leq\tilde{\rho}\leq\rho,\;\tilde{\rho}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=v_{N+1}\}

has dimension
d
, as
this implies that
\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{C}_{N+1})\geq dN
.
By convexity, it suffices to find
d+1
densities
\{\tilde{\rho}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d+1}
as above
(i.e.,
\tilde{\rho}_{i}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
0\leq\tilde{\rho}_{i}\leq\rho
,
\tilde{\rho}_{i}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=v_{N+1}
)
such that the convex hull of
b_{1},\ldots,b_{d+1}
, with
b_{i}=\mathrm{bary}(\tilde{\rho}_{i})
, has dimension
d
. We can construct them as follows.

First, we define the sets
A_{i}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
inductively by

A_{1}\coloneqq\mathbb{R}^{d}\,,\quad p_{i}\coloneqq\mathrm{bary}(\rho|_{A_{i}})\,,\quad A_{i+1}\coloneqq A_{i}\cap\{x{\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,:\,\langle h_{i},x-p_{i}\rangle>0\}\,,

for
i=1,\ldots,d
,
where
h_{1}=\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
is arbitrary and, for all
i\geq 2
,
h_{i}\in\mathrm{span}(p_{2}-p_{1},\ldots,p_{i}-p_{1})^{\perp}\setminus\{0\}
is also chosen arbitrarily. It is easy to check that
\rho(A_{i})>0
: in fact, if
\rho(A_{i})>0
, then
\rho(A_{i+1})>0
independently of the choice of
h_{i}
, since
p_{i}=\mathrm{bary}(\rho|_{A_{i}})
. (If this were not the case, then necessarily
\rho(\partial A_{i+1})>0
, but this is impossible since
\rho
is absolutely continuous).
Then we define

\tilde{\rho}_{i}\coloneqq\frac{v_{N+1}}{1-\varepsilon_{i}}\left(\rho-\varepsilon_{i}\frac{\rho|_{A_{i}}}{\rho(A_{i})}\right)

where
0<\varepsilon_{i}<\min(\rho(A_{i}),1-v_{N+1})
is a given coefficient. By the bounds on
\varepsilon_{i}
, we have indeed
0\leq\tilde{\rho}_{i}\leq\rho
and moreover
\tilde{\rho}_{i}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=v_{N+1}
.
Now, since
A_{1}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then
b_{1}=p_{1}
and, for all
i\geq 2
,

(13)

{b_{i}-b_{1}=\mathrm{bary}(\tilde{\rho}_{i})-b_{1}=\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon_{i}}(p_{1}-\varepsilon_{i}p_{i})-p_{1}=\frac{\varepsilon_{i}}{\varepsilon_{i}-1}(p_{i}-p_{1}).}

Since
\rho
is absolutely continuous and
A_{i+1}
is convex, then
p_{i+1}=\mathrm{bary}(\rho|_{A_{i+1}})\in A_{i+1}
. By definition of
A_{i+1}
, this means that for all
i\geq 2
,

p_{i+1}-p_{i}\notin\mathrm{span}(p_{2}-p_{1},\ldots,p_{i}-p_{1})\,,

which also implies that
p_{i+1}-p_{1}\notin\mathrm{span}(p_{2}-p_{1},\ldots,p_{i}-p_{1})
.
Hence, by induction and equation (
13
), the vectors
\{b_{2}-b_{1},\ldots,b_{d+1}-b_{1}\}

are linearly independent, and we are done.
∎

3.2.
Strassen’s Theorem and barycenters of Laguerre tessellations

As can already be deduced from the proof of Lemma
3.1
, one can construct points in
\mathcal{C}_{N}
by decomposing the density
\rho
appropriately. In order to describe this procedure, let us first recall the following alternative characterization of convex order, due to Strassen
[
38
]
:

Lemma 3.2

(Strassen’s Theorem)
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then
\mu\preceq_{C}\nu
if and only if there exists a martingale coupling between
\mu
and
\nu
, namely, there exists
a coupling
\theta\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
such that
\mathrm{d}\theta(x,y)=\mathrm{d}\theta_{x}(y)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)
(in the sense of disintegration of measures) and

(14)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}y\,\mathrm{d}\theta_{x}(y)=x\quad\text{ for $\mu$-a.e. }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,.

In our setting
(
\mu=\nu_{N}(v,B)
,
\nu=\rho
),
this means that

B\in\mathcal{C}_{N}(\rho,v)
if and only if there exists
N
densities
(\rho_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
such that

(15)

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\rho_{i}=\rho\,,\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{i}=v_{i}\,,\quad b_{i}=\mathrm{bary}(\rho_{i})\coloneqq\frac{1}{v_{i}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}y\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{i}({y})\,,

for all
i=1,\ldots,N
, or in other words, the points
b_{i}
are the barycenters of the densities
\rho_{i}
.
In the notation of Lemma
3.2
,
\theta_{b_{i}}=\rho_{i}/v_{i}
for all
i
.
Note, in particular, that if
v
and
B

satisfy (
15
), then clearly
\nu_{N}(v,B)\preceq_{C}\rho
by Jensen’s inequality. An illustration of this construction is shown in Figure
3
.

\mathcal{B}^{N}_{\mathrm{Lag}}

Lemma 3.3
.

\overline{\mathcal{B}^{N}_{\mathrm{Lag}}}=\mathcal{B}^{N}_{\mathrm{hLag}}
.

The following proposition states that there exists a one-to-one correspondence between
\mathcal{T}^{N}_{\mathrm{hLag}}
and
\mathcal{B}^{N}_{\mathrm{hLag}}
, generalizing an analogous result for Laguerre tessellations proved in
[

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
B\in\mathcal{B}^{N}_{\mathrm{hLag}}(v,\rho)
. Then there exists a unique collection of probability measures
(\theta_{i})_{i=1}^{N}\subset\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

(16)

\sum_{i}v_{i}\theta_{i}=\rho\,,\quad\mathrm{bary}(\theta_{i})\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x\mathrm{d}\theta_{i}(x)=b_{i}\,,\quad\forall\,i=1,\ldots,N.

In particular, there exists a unique tessellation of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with volumes and barycenters equal to
v
and
B
respectively, which therefore must be a hierarchical Laguerre tessellation.

Proof.

Let
(\tilde{L}_{i})_{i}
be a given hierarchical Laguerre tessellation corresponding to
B
. The existence of a
(\theta_{i})_{i}
as in the statement follows from the fact that we can always take, for
i=1,\ldots,N
,
\theta_{i}=\frac{1}{v_{i}}\rho|_{\tilde{L}_{i}}
. We want to prove that
(\theta_{i})_{i}
is uniquely determined by the conditions (
16
), which implies that
(\tilde{L}_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
is also unique.

Since
B\in\mathcal{B}^{N}_{\mathrm{hLag}}(v,\rho)
, there exists a Laguerre tessellation
(L_{j})_{j=1}^{M}
with generators
Y\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{M}\setminus\Delta_{M}
, with
2\leq M\leq N
, and a surjective map
\sigma:\{1,\ldots,N\}\rightarrow\{1,\ldots,M\}
such that, for all
j=1,\ldots,M
,

\rho(L_{j})=\sum_{i\in\sigma^{-1}(j)}v_{i}\eqqcolon w_{j}\,,\quad\mathrm{bary}_{\rho}(L_{j})=\frac{1}{w_{j}}\sum_{i\in\sigma^{-1}(j)}v_{i}b_{i}\,.

By construction, the coupling
\gamma^{*}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
defined by

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\gamma^{*}(x,y)=\sum_{j=1}^{M}\int_{L_{j}}\varphi(x,y_{j})\,\mathrm{d}\rho(x)\quad\forall\,\varphi\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})

is the unique optimal transport plan from
\rho
to
\nu_{M}(w,Y)
.
Now, let
(\theta_{i})_{i}\subset\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be an arbitrary collection of probability measures satisfying (
16
). We can define a coupling
\gamma\in\Gamma(\rho,\nu_{M}(w,Y))
as follows:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y)=\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{i\in\sigma^{-1}(j)}v_{i}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(x,y_{j})\,\mathrm{d}\theta_{i}(x)\quad\forall\,\varphi\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\,.

In particular, it is easy to check that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle x,y\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y)=\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{i\in\sigma^{-1}(j)}v_{j}\langle b_{i},y_{j}\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle x,y\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\gamma^{*}(x,y)\,,

and therefore by the uniqueness of
\gamma^{*}
, for all
j=1,\ldots,M
,

\sum_{i\in\sigma^{-1}(j)}v_{i}\theta_{i}=\rho|_{L_{j}}\,.

This means that the left-hand side is uniquely determined for all
j
.
But then we can iterate the construction replacing
\rho
with
\rho|_{L_{j}}
, for each
j=1,\ldots,M
. Eventually, this allows us to deduce that each
\theta_{i}
is uniquely determined, and we are done.

3.3.
Support function, exposed and extreme points

In this section we compute the support function of
\mathcal{C}_{N}
, and use this to prove that
\mathcal{B}^{N}_{\mathrm{Lag}}
and
\mathcal{B}^{N}_{\mathrm{hLag}}
are precisely the sets of exposed and extreme points
of
\mathcal{C}_{N}
, respectively.

Define the function
F_{N}:(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as follows:

(17)

F_{N}(Y)\coloneqq\max\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle x,y\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y)~~;~~\gamma\in\Gamma(\rho,\nu_{N}(v,Y))\right\}.

We will also denote the same function by
F_{N}(v,\rho;Y)
when we want to
emphasize the dependence on
{v}
and
\rho
.
Note that (if
\rho
has finite second moments)
F_{N}
is related to the Wasserstein distance
W_{2}(\rho,\nu_{N}(v,Y))
via

F_{N}(Y)=-\frac{1}{2}W_{2}^{2}(\rho,\nu_{N}(v,Y))+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\rho(x)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_{i}|y_{i}|^{2}.

The following proposition states that
F_{N}^{*}
is the indicator function of the convex set
\mathcal{C}_{N}
. Therefore
F_{N}=F_{N}^{**}=\iota_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}^{*}
is the support function of
\mathcal{C}_{N}
by, e.g.,
[
7
, Example 18.3]
.

Proposition 3.5
.

The function
F_{N}
is convex and continuous; it is non-differentiable on
\Delta_{N}
and
C^{1}
on
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}\setminus\Delta_{N}
, and for all
Y\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}\setminus\Delta_{N}
,

\nabla F_{N}(Y)=(\,\mathrm{bary}_{\rho}(L_{i}^{*}(v,Y))\,)_{i}\,,

where
\nabla
denotes the gradient with respect to the
l^{2}
-inner product weighted by
{v}
,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{v}
, and
(L_{i}^{*}(v,Y))_{i}
is the unique Laguerre tessellation with generators
Y
and volumes
v
.
Moreover, for all
B\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
,

(18)

F^{*}_{N}(B)=\iota_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}(B)\coloneqq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\text{if }\nu_{N}(v,B)\preceq_{C}\rho,\\
+\infty&\text{otherwise},\end{array}\right.

where
F^{*}_{N}
is the Fenchel-Legendre transform of
F_{N}
, again with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{v}
. Furthermore, for all
Y\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
, the subgradient of
F_{N}
at
Y
is given by

(19)

\displaystyle\partial F_{N}(Y)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{argmax}\left\{\langle Y,X\rangle_{v}\,;\,X\in\mathcal{C}_{N}\right\}

(20)

\displaystyle=\{B\in\mathcal{C}_{N}~;~F_{N}(Y)=\langle B,Y\rangle_{v}\}\,.

Note that, for
Y\in\Delta_{N}
, we give an explicit expression for an element of
\partial F_{N}(Y)
below in equation (
46
).

Proof.

The first part follows from Proposition 5.1 in
[
27
]
or Theorem 4.2 of
[
12
]
, for example. We give a sketch of the proof for completeness. Given any two vectors
Z,Y\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}\setminus\Delta_{N}
,
the coupling
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
defined by

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y)=\sum_{i}\int_{L_{i}^{*}(v,Y)}\varphi(x,z_{i})\,\mathrm{d}\rho(x),

for any bounded continuous function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, belongs to
\Gamma(\rho,\nu_{N}(Z,v))
and is admissible for the problem defining
F_{N}(Z)
. In addition, the coupling

\gamma^{*}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
defined by

(21)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\gamma^{*}(x,y)=\sum_{i}\int_{L_{i}^{*}(v,Y)}\varphi(x,y_{i})\,\mathrm{d}\rho(x)\quad\forall\;\varphi\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})

is the unique solution of the problem defining
F_{N}(Y)
. In particular, this implies that

F_{N}(Z)\geq F_{N}(Y)+\sum_{i}\int_{L_{i}^{*}(v,Y)}\langle x,z_{i}-y_{i}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\rho(x).

This means that
B(Y)\coloneqq(\mathrm{bary}_{\rho}L_{i}^{*}(v,Y))_{i}\in\partial F_{N}(Y)
, the subgradient of
F
with respect to the metric
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{v}
, and therefore
F
is convex. By stability of optimal transport,
\mathrm{bary}_{\rho}(L_{i}^{*}(v,Y))
is a continuous function of
Y
(see Proposition 5.1 in
[
27
]
), which implies at once that
B(Y)=\nabla F_{N}(Y)
and that
F
is
C^{1}
. On the other hand, by a similar reasoning, one can show that if
Y\in\Delta_{N}
, then
\partial F_{N}(Y)
does not consist of a single vector so
F_{N}
is not differentiable at these points.

For the second part of the statement, observe that, by disintegrating the plan
\gamma
in equation (
17
) with respect to its second marginal, the function
F_{N}
can be equivalently written as follows:

(22)

F_{N}(Y)=\max\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle x,y_{i}\rangle v_{i}\mathrm{d}\theta_{i}(x)~~;~~\theta_{i}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\,,~\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}v_{i}=\rho\right\}\,.

In view of Strassen’s theorem (see in particular equation (
15
)), this is equivalent to

(23)

F_{N}(Y)=\max\{\langle Y,X\rangle_{v}\;;\;X\in\mathcal{C}_{N}\}\,,

which in turn is equivalent to (
18
).

Next we prove (
19
).
Since
F_{N}=\iota_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}^{*}
, by Fenchel duality,

\displaystyle B\in\partial F_{N}(Y)

\displaystyle\iff Y\in\partial\iota_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}(B)

\displaystyle\iff\iota_{C_{N}}(X)\geq\iota_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}(B)+\langle Y,X-B\rangle_{v}\,,~\forall\,X\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}

\displaystyle\iff B\in\mathcal{C}_{N}~\text{ and }~\langle Y,B\rangle_{v}\geq\langle Y,X\rangle_{v}\,,~\forall\,X\in\mathcal{C}_{N}\,,

which proves (
19
). Finally, (
20
) follows from (
19
) and (
23
).

The function
F_{N}
allows us to provide a characterization of the exposed and extreme points of
\mathcal{C}_{N}
. We remark that a similar result but in the continuous setting was established in
[
17
]
, but considering the linear structure on probability measures, which is different from the type of convexity considered in this work.

We recall that the exposed faces of
\mathcal{C}_{N}
are the sets of maximizers of a linear form on
\mathcal{C}_{N}
. Exposed faces are faces, i.e., convex subsets
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{C}_{N}
containing every closed segment in
\mathcal{C}_{N}
whose interior intersects
\mathcal{F}
. Proper (exposed) faces are (exposed) faces that are neither the empty set nor
\mathcal{C}_{N}
. Exposed points are exposed faces containing a single point.

Theorem 3.6

(Exposed points and faces)
.

The following holds:

(24)

\mathcal{C}_{N}=\mathrm{conv}(\overline{\mathcal{B}^{N}_{\mathrm{Lag}}})\,.

Moreover,

1.

\mathcal{B}^{N}_{\mathrm{Lag}}

Theorem 3.9

(Extreme points)
.

\mathcal{B}^{N}_{\mathrm{hLag}}=\overline{\mathcal{B}^{N}_{\mathrm{Lag}}}

.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.3
and Theorem

,

is the closure of the exposed points of

[

, which states that the set of exposed points of a compact convex set is dense in the set of its extreme points.

It remains to prove the converse inclusion. Let

B=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{N})\in\mathcal{B}^{N}_{\mathrm{hLag}}
, and let

(L_{i})_{i=1}^{N}\in\mathcal{T}^{N}_{\mathrm{hLag}}(v,\rho)
be the corresponding hierarchical Laguerre tessellation. Thus

b_{i}=\frac{1}{v_{i}}\int_{L_{i}}x\,\mathrm{d}\rho(x),\qquad\rho(L_{i})=v_{i}.

By Proposition
3.4
, the collection

\bar{\theta}_{i}\coloneqq\frac{1}{v_{i}}\rho|_{L_{i}}
is uniquely characterized by the conditions

(30)

\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_{i}\bar{\theta}_{i}=\rho,\qquad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\theta}_{i}(x)=b_{i}\,.

Suppose now that
B
is not extreme. Then there exist

X=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}),Z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{N})\in\mathcal{C}_{N}
and some

\lambda\in(0,1)

By Strassen’s Theorem, applied in the form of (
15
), there exist probability measures

(\theta_{i}^{X})_{i}
and
(\theta_{i}^{Z})_{i}
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_{i}\theta_{i}^{X}=\rho,\qquad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x\,\mathrm{d}\theta_{i}^{X}(x)=x_{i},

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_{i}\theta_{i}^{Z}=\rho,\qquad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x\,\mathrm{d}\theta_{i}^{Z}(x)=z_{i}.

Define

Then each

30

for all
i
. We now show that this forces

. Since

and all measures involved are nonnegative, and since

\theta_{i}^{X}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\bar{\theta}_{i}

a_{i}^{X}
be the density of
\theta_{i}^{X}

\bar{\theta}_{i}
, so that
v_{i}\theta_{i}^{X}=a_{i}^{X}\,\rho|_{L_{i}}
. Then, using
\sum_{i}v_{i}\theta_{i}^{X}=\rho

\rho=\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}^{X}\,\rho|_{L_{i}}.

Since the cells

form a partition up to a
\rho

coincide with the indicator function of
L_{i}

\rho

. Thus,

This implies that

and similarly that

4.
Semi-discrete Wasserstein projections in convex order

In this section we describe how the
L^{2}
-projection of a vector
X\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
onto
\mathcal{C}_{N}(v,\rho)
can be reinterpreted as a Wasserstein projection of the discrete measure
\nu_{N}(v,X)
onto the set of measures
\mu\preceq_{C}\rho
. While this problem is of independent interest, it will be instrumental to our approach to
reconstructing a Laguerre tessellation from its cell volumes and barycenters, discussed in the following sections.

Let us define the following weighted orthogonal projection

(31)

P_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}(X)\coloneqq\mathrm{argmin}\{\|B-X\|_{v}^{2}~;~B\in\mathcal{C}_{N}\}.

Note that since
\mathcal{C}_{N}
is a bounded, closed and convex set, this problem is well-defined and admits a unique solution.

It will be useful to consider its dual (with respect to the duality expressed by the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{v}
), which
can be written
as follows:

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
X\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
. The following holds:

(32)

\inf_{B{\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}}}\left\{\frac{\|B-X\|^{2}_{v}}{2}+\iota_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}(B)\right\}=-\inf_{Y{\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}}}\left\{\frac{\|Y\|^{2}_{v}}{2}-\langle Y,X\rangle_{v}+F_{N}(v,\rho;Y)\right\}.

Moreover the problems on the left- and right-hand sides admit
unique solutions, denoted
P_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}(X)
and
Y^{*}
respectively, which
satisfy

(33)

Y^{*}=X-P_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}(X)\,.

Proof.

The result is classical since the right-hand side of (
32
) is just the Legendre transform of the sum
Y\mapsto F_{N}(Y)+\|Y\|^{2}_{v}/2
, which is given by the inf-convolution of the Legendre transforms of
F_{N}
and
\|\cdot\|^{2}_{v}/2
. We detail the proof for clarity.

Both problems admit a unique solutions by the strong convexity and lower-semicontinuity of the objective functions. Moreover,
B^{*}=P_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}(X)
is the unique solution of the problem on the left-hand side of (
32
) if and only if

\displaystyle 0\in\partial\left(B\mapsto\frac{\|B-X\|_{v}^{2}}{2}+\iota_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}(B)\right)(B^{*})\quad

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\quad X-B^{*}\in\partial\iota_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}(B^{*})

(34)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\quad B^{*}\in\partial F_{N}(X-B^{*})\,,

since
F_{N}^{*}=\iota_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}
, by Proposition
3.5
. Similarly,

Y^{*}
is the unique solution of the problem on the right-hand side of (
32
) if and only if

(35)

0\in\partial\left(Y\mapsto\frac{\|Y\|^{2}_{v}}{2}-\langle Y,X\rangle_{v}+F_{N}(Y)\right)(Y^{*})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad X-Y^{*}\in\partial F_{N}(Y^{*}).

Then equation (
33
) follows from (
34
) and (
35
), and moreover

B^{*}\in\partial F(Y^{*})
. Equation (
32
) follows since, by Fenchel duality and equation (
33
),

F_{N}(Y^{*})+\iota_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}(B^{*})=\langle B^{*},Y^{*}\rangle_{v}=-\frac{\|B^{*}-X\|^{2}_{v}}{2}-\frac{\|Y^{*}\|^{2}_{v}}{2}+\langle Y^{*},X\rangle_{v}\,.

∎

We now show the equivalence between problem (
31
) and
the
2-Wasserstein projection problem (
8
). In order to state this, let us first recall the definition of the 2-Wasserstein distance
W_{2}
:

(36)

W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x-y|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y)\right\}

for any
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
probability measures with finite second moments, and
where
\Gamma(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the set of probability measures with first and second marginals equal to
\mu
and
\nu
, respectively.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
X\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
. The measure

\nu_{N}(v,P_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}(X))

is the unique solution
of
the projection problem

(37)

\inf\{W^{2}_{2}(\nu,\nu_{N}(v,X))\,;\,\nu\preceq_{C}\rho\}.

Proof.

Existence and uniqueness of the minimizer
\nu^{*}
of (
37
) has been proven in
[
21
]
. In the same work, the authors showed that
there exists a Lipschitz continuous
optimal transport map
T^{*}
from
\nu_{N}(v,X)
to
\nu^{*}
.
This implies that

\nu^{*}=\nu_{N}(v,Y^{*})

for some vector
Y^{*}=(T^{*}(x_{i}))_{i}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
.
Using the fact that

W_{2}^{2}(\nu_{N}(v,Y),\nu_{N}(v,X))\leq\|Y-X\|_{v}^{2},

for any
Y\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
, we obtain

\|Y^{*}-X\|_{v}^{2}=\inf\{W^{2}_{2}(\nu,\nu_{N}(v,X))\,;\,\nu\preceq_{C}\rho\}\leq\inf\{\|Y-X\|_{v}^{2}\,;\,\nu_{N}(v,Y)\preceq_{C}\rho\},

which implies that
Y^{*}=P_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}(X)
.
∎

We conclude this section by remarking that the set
\mathcal{C}_{N}
gives rise to an alternative characterisation of the quantization problem and optimal centroidal Laguerre tessellations.

Remark 4.3

(Connection with optimal quantization)
.

Consider the non-convex optimization problem

(38)

\inf_{X\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}}\left\{-\frac{\|X\|^{2}_{v}}{2}\;;\;X\in\mathcal{C}_{N}\right\}.

Suppose that
X\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}\setminus\Delta_{N}
is a global minimizer. Then
0\in\partial(-\|\cdot\|_{v}^{2}/2+\iota_{\mathcal{C}_{N}})(X)
, and it follows from Proposition
3.5
that
X\in\partial F_{N}(X)
or equivalently
x_{i}=\mathrm{bary}_{\rho}(L_{i}^{*}(v,X))
for all
i
. In other words,
X
generates a centroidal Laguerre tessellation
[
13
,
42
]
. Moreover, it can be shown using Toland duality
[
40
]
that global minimizers of problem (
38
) are global minimizers of the following optimal quantization problem
[
11
,
30
,
35
]
:

(39)

\inf_{Y\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}}W_{2}(\rho,\nu_{N}(v,Y)).

Here we look for the best approximation of the absolutely continuous measure
\rho
by a discrete measure with fixed volumes.
Problem (
38
) provides an alternative formulation of the quantization problem (
39
). For an analogous statement in the fully discrete setting, where
\rho
is discrete, see
[
15
, Theorem 2.4]
.

5.
Laguerre tessellation reconstruction

In Theorem
3.6
, we showed that the vectors in
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}

that
can be obtained as the barycenters of Laguerre tessellations with given volumes,
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{Lag}}^{N}
, are precisely the exposed points of
\mathcal{C}_{N}
.
Moreover, for any
B\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{Lag}}^{N}
, there exists a supporting hyperplane with normal
Y{\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}}\setminus\Delta_{N}
that exposes
B
, i.e., such that

\{B\}=\mathrm{argmax}\{\langle X,Y\rangle_{v}\,;\,X\in\mathcal{C}_{N}\}.

By Proposition
3.5
the vector
Y
is a vector of generators associated with the tessellation with barycenters
B
.

We can then reformulate the Laguerre tessellation reconstruction and fitting problems as follows: can one retrieve an exposed point of
\mathcal{C}_{N}
that is closest to a given point
B\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
and recover a normal vector
Y
that exposes it?
If

B

is already an exposed point, we can regard this as a reconstruction problem (as this is equivalent to Question
1
),
otherwise we regard it as a fitting problem.
In this section we focus on the reconstruction problem, and discuss two possible methods to solve this.

5.1.
Reconstruction via Legendre transform

By Proposition
3.5
, given a point
B\in\mathcal{B}^{N}_{\mathrm{Lag}}
, the generators
X
associated with
B
solve the problem

(40)

\sup_{Y}\,H(Y;B)\,,\quad H(Y;B)\coloneqq\langle Y,B\rangle_{v}-F_{N}(Y)\,.

In
[
12
]
it was proposed to solve the reconstruction problem precisely by computing a solution to this maximization problem.
While this seems to work well in practice (see
[
12
, Section 5.1]
), the function

H(\,\cdot\,;B)
is not strictly concave and it
is also maximized by the constant vector
Y=(y,\ldots,y)\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
for any
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and so maximising
H
is not guaranteed to solve the reconstruction problem. We remark that this issue can be addressed by adding a convex constraint to the problem. Specifically, fixing arbitrary indices
i\neq j
and
\delta>0
, one can restrict the maximization over the set

\{Y\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}\,;\,\langle b_{i}-b_{j},y_{i}-y_{j}\rangle\geq\delta\,\}.

In fact, by the invariance of the barycenters upon dilation of the generators, it is always possible to construct a set of generators
Y
associated with
B
that satisfies this constraint.
On the other hand, by Proposition
3.4
, any maximizer
Y
satisfying the constraint must also satisfy
y_{i}\neq y_{j}
for all
i\neq j
, and is therefore a set of generators associated with the barycenters
B
.

5.2.
Reconstruction via projections in convex order

We describe here a second approach to
solving
the reconstruction problem based on the orthogonal projection on
\mathcal{C}_{N}
discussed in Section
4
, which has the advantage of being unconstrained and strongly convex.

The idea is illustrated in Figure
7
. Given an exposed point
B
of
\mathcal{C}_{N}
, we first perturb it by a dilation, i.e. we consider the point
\lambda B
, with
\lambda>1
. One can check that
\lambda B\notin\mathcal{C}_{N}
and that
B^{*}=P_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}(\lambda B)
is on the relative boundary of
\mathcal{C}_{N}
(see Lemma
5.1
below). By standard properties of the orthogonal projection,
B^{*}
is exposed by the vector
Y^{*}=\lambda B-B^{*}
, and
\|B-B^{*}\|_{v}\leq|\lambda-1|\|B\|_{v}
is small for
\lambda
close to one. Finally, if
Y^{*}\notin\Delta_{N}
, we have found an approximate solution of the reconstruction problem, since then
Y^{*}
is a vector of distinct generators associated with
B^{*}
.

In order to show in which cases the property
Y^{*}\notin\Delta_{N}
is satisfied we will need the following lemma:

B^{*}
Y^{*}
\lambda B
B
Y
\mathcal{C}_{N}(v,\rho)

Figure 7.
In order to approximate
Y
, the direction exposing
B
, we compute
Y^{*}=\lambda B-B^{*}
where
B^{*}=P_{\mathcal{C}_{N}(v,\rho)}(\lambda B)
for
\lambda>1
close to 1. By construction,
B^{*}
maximizes
\langle\cdot,Y^{*}\rangle_{v}
on
\mathcal{C}_{N}
, i.e.,
Y^{*}
exposes
B^{*}
, and
B^{*}\rightarrow B
as
\lambda\rightarrow 1
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
N\geq 2
, and suppose that
B
is in the relative boundary of
\mathcal{C}_{N}
, denoted
\mathrm{rel}\,\partial\mathcal{C}_{N}
. Then, for any
\lambda>1
we also have
P_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}(\lambda B)\in\mathrm{rel}\,\partial\mathcal{C}_{N}
. Moreover,
Y^{*}\coloneqq\lambda B-P_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}(\lambda B)
is not a constant vector in
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
, i.e., there does not exist
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
y_{i}^{*}=y
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
.

Proof.

Since
B\in\mathrm{rel}\,\partial\mathcal{C}_{N}
, there exists a vector
Y\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
, different from the constant vector, such that

(41)

B\in\mathrm{argmax}\{\langle Y,X\rangle\,;\,X\in\mathcal{C}_{N}\}\,.

Furthermore, we can pick
Y
parallel to the affine hull of
\mathcal{C}_{N}
defined by equation (
12
), i.e. such that
\sum_{i}v_{i}y_{i}=0
. Note that the constant vector

\mathrm{bary}(\rho)_{N}\coloneqq(\mathrm{bary}(\rho),\ldots,\mathrm{bary}(\rho))\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}

is in the relative interior of
\mathcal{C}_{N}
, by the third point of Theorem
3.6
. Therefore, because of (
41
), we have

(42)

\langle B,Y\rangle_{v}>\langle\mathrm{bary}(\rho)_{N},Y\rangle_{v}=\sum_{i}v_{i}\langle\mathrm{bary}(\rho),y_{i}\rangle=0\,.

This implies that
\lambda B\notin\mathcal{C}_{N}
and
P_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}(\lambda B)
belongs to the relative boundary of
\mathcal{C}_{N}
.
In fact, since

\langle\lambda B,Y\rangle_{v}>\langle B,Y\rangle_{v}
by equation (
42
),
then
\lambda B
and
\mathcal{C}_{N}
are separated by the supporting hyperplane containing
B
given by

\{X\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}\,;\,\langle X,Y\rangle_{v}=\langle B,Y\rangle_{v}\}\,.

In addition, define
Y^{*}\coloneqq\lambda B-P_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}(\lambda B)
. By the classical characterisation of the orthogonal projection (e.g.
[
7
, Theorem 8.4]
),

\langle Y^{*},\mathrm{bary}(\rho)_{N}-P_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}(\lambda B)\rangle_{v}<0\,,

where the inequality is strict because
\mathrm{bary}(\rho)_{N}
belongs to the relative interior of
\mathcal{C}_{N}
. This means that
Y^{*}
cannot
have
the form
y^{*}_{i}=y
for all
i
, since otherwise this inner product would be zero
by equation (
12
).
∎

As a direct consequence of Theorem
3.10
and Lemma
5.1
, we obtain the following result:

Proposition 5.2
.

Suppose that
B\in\mathcal{B}^{N}_{\mathrm{iLag}}(v,\rho)
.
Then for any
\lambda>1
sufficiently small,
P_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}(\lambda B)\in\mathcal{B}^{N}_{\mathrm{iLag}}(v,\rho)
and moreover
Y^{*}\coloneqq\lambda B-P_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}(\lambda B)\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}\setminus\Delta_{N}
is a vector of generators associated with
P_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}(\lambda B)
.

Proposition
5.2
says that we can recover an irreducible Laguerre tessellation with barycenters arbitrary close to a given
B\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{iLag}}^{N}
via an orthogonal projection.
On the other hand, in the general case where
B\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{Lag}}^{N}
or
B\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{hLag}}^{N}
, we cannot guarantee that
Y^{*}\notin\Delta_{N}
. Even when
B\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{iLag}}^{N}
, Proposition
5.2

does not provide a quantitative estimate on how small
\lambda
should be in order to obtain a set of generators
Y^{*}\notin\Delta_{N}
. However, numerically, the approach seems robust with respect to the choice of
\lambda
, in the sense that it generally provides a set of distinct generators even for
\lambda\gg 1
. Note also that as
\lambda\rightarrow 1
,
B^{*}\rightarrow B
and by stability of optimal transport maps (see, e.g., Theorem 5.20 in
[
41
]
) one can deduce that the Laguerre tessellation associated with
B^{*}
converges to the one associated with
B
(i.e., corresponding cells converge in measure). However it is not straightforward to quantify this convergence in terms of
\|B-B^{*}\|_{v}
.

We conclude the section with two further reinterpretations of the projection
P_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}(\lambda B)
. First, by the convex duality in Lemma
4.1
, if
P_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}(\lambda B)
is an exposed point that does not belong to any face of dimension strictly larger than zero, its exposing normal is

Y=\lambda B-P_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}(\lambda B)\,.

Moreover,
Y

is the unique minimizer for the problem

\inf_{Y}\left\{\frac{\|Y\|^{2}_{v}}{2\lambda}-\langle Y,B\rangle_{v}+\frac{1}{\lambda}F_{N}(v,\rho;Y)\right\}\,.

In other words, by introducing
\lambda>1
, we are weakening the problem’s strong convexity to avoid the trivial solution
Y=0
, which is the unique solution of the problem when
\lambda=1
and
B\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{Lag}}^{N}
. On the other hand, note that this is a strongly convex unconstrained problem, and therefore easier to solve than problem (
40
), which is only convex and constrained. The downside is that here we are only computing an approximate solution. Remarkably, however, in numerical tests the projection approach generally produce good results even for relatively large values of
\lambda
(see Section
6
).

The second interpretation is a consequence of Proposition
4.2
. Specifically,
P_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}(\lambda X)
can be related to the set of particle positions at time
t=\lambda/(\lambda-1)>1
obtained by extending (in a specific way) the
W_{2}
-geodesic connecting
\rho
at time
0
to
\nu_{N}(v,X)
at time
1
. Such an extension was introduced in
[
20
]
under the name of
metric extrapolation
, and is defined via a minimization of a difference of squared Wasserstein distances, as explained in the following remark.

Remark 5.3

(Relation with the metric Wasserstein extrapolation)
.

Let us consider the problem of projecting
\lambda X
onto

\mathcal{C}_{N}
with
\lambda>1
, as in Proposition
5.2
, but with
X\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
arbitrary. By the change of variable
Y=(\lambda-1)Z
, the dual problem in Lemma
4.1
becomes up to constant terms

(43)

\inf_{Z{\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}}}\left\{\lambda(\lambda-1)\frac{\|Z-X\|_{v}^{2}}{2}-(\lambda-1)\frac{W^{2}_{2}(\nu_{N}(v,Z),\rho)}{2}\right\}.

Set
t=\lambda/(\lambda-1)
. Then the infimum in (
43
) is larger than

(44)

\lambda\inf_{\mu{\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}}\left\{\frac{W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu_{N}(v,X))}{2(t-1)}-\frac{W^{2}_{2}(\mu,\rho)}{2t}\ \right\}\,.

In fact,
\|Z-X\|_{v}\geq W_{2}(\nu_{N}(v,Z),\nu_{N}(v,X))
, and we get problem (
44
) by replacing the minimization over
Z
with a minimization over any probability measure
\mu
with finite second moments. This is precisely the metric extrapolation problem, which was introduced in
[
20
]
to extend up to time
t>1
the
W_{2}
-geodesic connecting
\rho
at time
0
to
\nu_{N}(v,X)
at time
1
. It was proven in
[
20
]
that the solution to this problem is unique and
has
the form
\mu^{*}=T_{\#}\nu_{N}(v,X)

where
T
is a continuous map. This implies that problem (
44
) actually coincides with (
43
) and
\mu^{*}=\nu_{N}(v,Z^{*})
with
Z^{*}
solving (
43
).

6.
Numerical methods and tests

In this section we describe two numerical strategies to solve the projection problem considered in Section
4
, and then apply them to the reconstruction and fitting of Laguerre tessellations.

6.1.
Subgradient descent

Let us go back to the dual problem in Lemma
4.1
, i.e.

(45)

\inf_{Y}G_{N}(Y)\,,\quad\text{where}\quad G_{N}(Y)\coloneqq\frac{\|Y\|_{v}^{2}}{2}-\langle Y,X\rangle_{v}+F_{N}(v,\rho;Y)\,.

Note that minimizing
G_{N}
is equivalent to evaluating the proximal operator of
F_{N}
with respect to the weighted Euclidean norm
\|\cdot\|_{v}
; see
[
7
, Definition 25.1]
.

Given any
Y\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
, let
M\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
and
\tilde{Y}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{M}\setminus\Delta_{M}
be such that
y_{i}=\tilde{y}_{\sigma(i)}
for all
i
, for some surjective map
\sigma:\{1,\ldots,N\}\rightarrow\{1,\ldots,M\}
. Define
w=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{M})
by

w_{j}=\sum_{i\in\sigma^{-1}(j)}v_{i}.

Then, from the proof of Theorem
3.6
, one can deduce that

(46)

B(Y)\coloneqq(\mathrm{bary}_{\rho}(L^{*}_{\sigma(i)}(w,\tilde{Y})))_{i}\in\partial F_{N}(Y),

where
\partial F_{N}
denotes the subgradient of
F_{N}
with respect to the metric
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{v}
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Suppose
\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, i.e.

m_{2}^{2}(\rho)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho(x)<+\infty.

Then
F_{N}
is
m_{2}(\rho)
-Lipschitz. Moreover, denoting by
Y^{*}
the unique solution of
(
45
),
\|Y^{*}-X\|_{v}\leq m_{2}(\rho)
and
G_{N}
is
2m_{2}(\rho)
-Lipschitz on the set
\{Y\;;\;\|Y-X\|_{v}\leq m_{2}(\rho)\}
.

Proof.

For any
Y,Z\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
, since
B(Z)\in\partial F_{N}(Z)
,

\displaystyle F_{N}(Z)-F_{N}(Y)

\displaystyle\leq\langle B(Z),Z-Y\rangle_{v}

\displaystyle\leq\|B(Z)\|_{v}\|Z-Y\|_{v}

\displaystyle\leq m_{2}(\rho)\|Z-Y\|_{v}\,,

where the last inequality is due to Jensen’s inequality. Exchanging
Y
and
Z
we obtain that
F_{N}
is
m_{2}(\rho)
-Lipschitz. Moreover, by Lemma
4.1
,

\|Y^{*}-X\|_{v}=\|P_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}(X)\|_{v}\leq m_{2}(\rho)\,,

where the last inequality follows from the definition of convex order and choosing as test function
\varphi:x\rightarrow|x|^{2}
. We conclude by observing that

\partial G_{N}(Y)=Y-X+\partial F_{N}(Y)

and using the triangle inequality.
∎

Given the considerations above, we can formulate a projected subgradient descent method with varying stepsize
\eta_{k}
(see, e.g., Section 3.4.1 in
[
16
]
) as follows: given
Y_{1}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
, for all
k\geq 1
,

(47)

\displaystyle\textbf{Step 1. }

\displaystyle Z_{k}=Y_{k}-\eta_{k}(Y_{k}-X+B(Y_{k}))\,,

\displaystyle\textbf{Step 2. }

\displaystyle Y_{k+1}=X+{\max\left(\frac{\|Z_{k}-X\|_{v}}{m_{2}(\rho)},1\right)}^{-1}(Z_{k}-X)\,.

As a consequence of Lemma
6.1
, the strong convexity of
G_{N}
, and
Theorem 3.9 in
[
16
]
we have that the subgradient method defined by (
47
) converges with sublinear rate with an appropriate choice of step-size. More precisely:

Lemma 6.2
.

Choosing
\eta_{k}=2/(k+1)
, the iterates of algorithm (
47
)
satisfy
for
k\geq 1

G_{N}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{2j}{k(k+1)}Y_{j}\right)-G_{N}(Y^{*})\leq\frac{4m_{2}^{2}(\rho)}{k+1}\,.

6.2.
Frank-Wolfe algorithm

The projection problem

\inf_{B\in\mathcal{C}_{N}}J_{N}(B)\,,\quad J_{N}(B)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\|B-X\|^{2}_{v}\,,

can be solved by applying directly the classical Frank-Wolfe algorithm
[
19
]
.
In our setting, this amounts to the following iterative scheme, which allows us to compute
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}(X)
from a given initial guess
B_{1}\in\mathcal{C}_{N}
: for all
k\geq 1
,

(48)

\displaystyle\text{{Step 1}. }

\displaystyle\text{Compute }C_{k}=B(X-B_{k})\in\partial F_{N}(X-B_{k}),

\displaystyle\text{{Step 2}. }

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}=\arg\min\{\|\lambda B_{k}+(1-\lambda)C_{k}-X\|^{2}_{v}\,;\,\lambda\in[0,1]\},

\displaystyle\text{{Step 3}. }

\displaystyle B_{k+1}=\lambda_{k}B_{k}+(1-\lambda_{k})C_{k}\,.

Note that in the first step
B(\cdot)
is defined as in (
46
).
The expression for
C_{k}
is derived by minimizing over
\mathcal{C}_{N}
the linearization of
J_{N}
about
B_{k}
.
As for the convergence of the algorithm, we start by observing that
\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{C}_{N})\leq 2{m_{2}(\rho)}
. In fact, for any
X_{1},X_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{N}
, by the triangle inequality
and Jensen’s inequality,

\|X_{1}-X_{2}\|_{v}^{2}\leq 2\|X_{1}\|_{v}^{2}+2\|X_{2}\|^{2}_{v}\leq 4m_{2}^{2}(\rho)\,.

Then, as a direct application of Theorem 13.14 in
[
8
]
, we find:

Lemma 6.3
.

The iterates of algorithm (
48
)
satisfy
for
k\geq 2

J_{N}(B_{k})-J_{N}(P_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}(X))\leq\frac{8m_{2}^{2}(\rho)}{k-1}\,.
