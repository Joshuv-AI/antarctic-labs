Changing contexts of death and dying: Influence of the COVID-19 pandemic and voluntary assisted dying legislation on death literacy in Australia
Authors: Sumina Shrestha, Andrea Grindrod, Sora Lee, Kerrie Noonan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic and the introduction of voluntary assisted dying (VAD) legislation in Australia are major societal exposures that altered the social context around death and dying. Although death literacy is a socially embedded concept, there are limited studies assessing how death-related societal events like COVID-19 and VAD legislation influence its development.ObjectiveTo examine associations of the COVID-19 pandemic and VAD legislation introduction with death literacy, and whether these societal exposures functioned as contextual pathways linking social capital, attitudes, and supportive actions around death and dying with death literacy outcomes. This study assessed death literacy as operationalised by the Death Literacy Index.DesignA cross-sectional national survey.MethodsParticipants were recruited through the Online Research Unit from a representative online non-probability panel of 1202 Australian adults, stratified by age, gender, and geographic region. Death literacy was measured using the Revised Death Literacy Index. Multivariate regression examined associations of COVID-19 and VAD legislation with death literacy after adjusting for relevant covariates. Mediation analyses explored whether these societal exposures functioned as indirect contextual pathways linking social factors with death literacy.ResultsOf the 1202 participants, 56.4% were female and 43.6% male. Over 40% (483) reported some form of COVID-related experience, while nearly 12% (139) reported experience related to VAD. Multivariate analysis showed that perceived gain of knowledge during the pandemic (B=0.179) and VAD legislation introduction (B=0.319) were positively associated with death literacy, whereas experiencing COVID-related death had a negative association. Social capital, social attitudes toward death, and supportive actions to carers demonstrated significant positive indirect effects, partially mediated by the pandemic and VAD exposure. Personal attitudes toward death showed negative indirect effects.ConclusionThis study highlights the importance of public health palliative care approaches that promote death literacy, especially during public health crises or policy change. Findings reinforce that death literacy does not develop in isolation but is shaped by social environments that enable or limit the exposure, awareness, and engagement to death, dying, and bereavement.Keywords: death literacy, death education, COVID-19, voluntary assisted dying, end-of-life care, palliative care, public health palliative care, social capital, attitudes to death, social support

Introduction

Death literacy is a key concept within public health palliative care, offering insight into the knowledge, skills, experiences, and social practices that shape how individuals engage with ageing, living with terminal illness, grief and bereavement, and long-term caregiving situations. 1 Death literacy refers to the knowledge, skills, understanding, and capacity that individuals and community have regarding end-of-life, death, and dying. Opportunities to learn about the end of life, death, dying, and bereavement often arise through shared experiences, social engagement, religious beliefs, and cultural norms, thereby shaping the conversations within families and communities.2,3 Major societal events have the potential to substantially alter these social mechanisms.

The COVID-19 pandemic (2020 to 2023) represented one of the most disruptive global events of this century, with widespread social, mental, and physical health impacts.4,5 High incidence of infection along with restrictions to travel, hospital visitation, funeral attendance, and the overall social interaction denied opportunities for families and communities to support one another during illness, death, and bereavement.5–7 At the same time, death and dying also became highly visible in everyday life throughout the world, including Australia, through widespread media coverage, public health messaging, and increased exposure to loss and grief.8–10

Another major societal development in Australia was the introduction of Voluntary Assisted Dying (VAD) legislation. Victoria was the first state to pass VAD legislation (2017), which came into effect in 2019. 11 All Australian states—Western Australia (2019), Tasmania (2021), Queensland (2021), South Australia (2021), and New South Wales (2022) and Australian Capital Territory (2024)—now have VAD legislation.11,12 VAD was not legal in 2023 in ACT and the Northern Territory when this study was carried out.

VAD legislation allows an adult with an advanced terminal illness with associated suffering and limited life expectancy to request medical assistance to end their life voluntarily.13,14 The introduction of VAD in Australia has intensified public conversation around end-of-life decision-making, including ethical and legal aspects of death and dying.11,15,16 Both awareness and procedural understanding of VAD legislation is important for communities to access and utilise service options. 17 Studies have shown that families and carers involved in the VAD processes may experience grief and bereavement in a distinct way. 18

Together, the COVID-19 pandemic and VAD introduction altered the social context surrounding death, dying and bereavement in Australia. However, there is a dearth of studies assessing the influence of such social events on how individuals and communities understand and engage with death-related issues and how these contextual changes translate into death literacy. In this study, we examined how the COVID-19 pandemic and VAD introduction were associated with death literacy of Australians. We further explored whether exposure to these major death-related societal exposures functioned as contextual pathways linking factors such as social capital, social and personal attitudes toward death and dying, and engagement in supportive actions with death literacy outcomes. In this study, death literacy is examined within the domains captured by the Death Literacy Index—Practical knowledge, Experiential knowledge, Factual knowledge, and Community knowledge.1,19

Methods

Study design

This study is the extension of a national cross-sectional survey originally conducted to revise the Death Literacy Index in the context of the post-COVID-19 pandemic. 19 The survey instrument was further adapted to capture participants’ experiences related to the COVID-19 pandemic and introduction of VAD legislation in Australia, enabling an investigation of their potential influence on death literacy among Australians. Detailed description of the study setting, population, and data collection method has been previously published. 19

Study setting and sample

A national representative sample was recruited from Online Research Unit (ORU), 20 comprising over 300,000 Australians and is representative of the Australian population by state and by metro/regional. Adults aged 18 years and older were eligible to participate. A proportionate sample of 1,202 adults was selected, stratified by age, gender, and geographic region, with equal representation across six age groups and state-based quotas to ensure national coverage. This sample size was determined adequate to achieve a 95% confidence level with a 3% confidence interval using the Australian Bureau of Statistics Sample Size Calculator. 21 Participants could opt in or out, provided informed consent, and were offered incentives for completion. Participation was voluntary, with the option to withdraw at any time. Data collection took place in December 2023. The median completion time was 12 minutes. Standard data-quality checks were implemented by the panel provider ORU, including screening for speeding and straight-lining responses.

Detailed description of the participant characteristics has been previously published. 19

Measures

Death literacy, the dependent variable, was assessed using Revised Death Literacy Index (DLI-R), which also includes four domains of the index—practical knowledge, experiential knowledge, factual knowledge and community knowledge. The DLI-R has been validated for use in the Australian population to measure death literacy. 19

COVID-19-related variables included perceived influence of the pandemic on their knowledge of death, dying, and bereavement; experience of losing a close person to COVID-19; organising funeral; and attending funeral during the pandemic. VAD-related variables included perceived influence of the introduction of VAD on participants’ knowledge of death, dying, and bereavement, as well as personal experiences such as caring for someone approved for VAD and knowing someone who has died using VAD or is planning to do so.

Attitudes, actions, and experiences related to death and dying were assessed using multiple scales and the internal reliability of the scales were assessed before analysis. Attitudes were measured from both individual and societal perspectives. The individual attitude scale comprised four items (Cronbach’s α = 0.837), and the community attitude scale included two items (α = 0.791). Planning for participants’ own end of life was measured using 4-item scale (α = 0.803). Supportive actions toward carers were measured using an 11-item scale (α = 0.883), while experiences of being with someone at the time of death were assessed using a 6-item scale (α = 0.804). Social capital was measured with a 4-item scale (α = 0.816). A full list of items and corresponding Cronbach’s alpha values is available in Supplemental File 1 – Table A.

Other end-of-life related variables included living with terminal illness, being a primary carer, participating in community activities/events related to death and dying, and employment or volunteering experience in palliative care or grief support.

Socio-demographic variables included income, marital status, education, employment status, cultural background, religion, years lived in Australia, and belief in afterlife.

The survey includes 94 questions and were presented as 10 grouped questions, many of which used a matrix format with shared instructions and response scales, mainly those relating to experiences. The survey comprised structured, closed-response questions, and several items were brief. All questions used in this study were derived from a previously conducted population-representative Australian survey,1,22 including the DLI-R, which has been validated for use in the Australian population. 19

Data analysis

Descriptive characteristics of the data were presented in frequency and percentage. Scaled mean score of death literacy was provided for each experience related to the COVID-19 pandemic and VAD.

A hierarchical multivariable regression approach was adapted to identify the associations of the pandemic and VAD with death literacy. In the first stage, all non-COVID and non-VAD-related variables showing significance at p-value 0.10 in bivariate analysis were entered into the regression model using a backward elimination method. All categories within each categorical variable were initially examined in the bivariate analyses, and only those showing a significance at p < 0.10 were retained as separate dummy variables for inclusion in the multivariable model. This threshold was used only as a preliminary screening criterion for model building, not as the threshold for statistical significance. 23 Multicollinearity was assessed among all variables included in the regression analysis. In the second stage, COVID and VAD related variables were added to the model to assess their independent associations, adjusting for covariates retained from the first stage. Variables significant at p<0.05 in the final model were interpreted as independently associated with death literacy.

Mediation analysis

Krieger (2001) emphasised the importance of examining pathways in social epidemiology over narrow notion of causation. 24 A mediation framework was used to examine indirect pathways operating within broader societal contexts, rather than to test moderation effects at the individual level. We examined indirect pathways associated with COVID-19 and VAD-related exposure in Australia, linking social capital, social attitude towards discussing death and dying, personal attitude on death and dying conversations, and action to support carers with death literacy. COVID-19 and VAD were conceptualised as broader societal contexts rather than individual-level mediators.

Simple mediation models were assessed for each social factor. For each model, social factors were identified as predictors, death literacy as the criterion variable, and COVID and VAD exposures as the mediator. We utilised Model 4 from the PROCESS macro (version 4.2) for mediation analysis and employed the bias-corrected percentile Bootstrap method with 5,000 resamples, and 95% confidence intervals were calculated. A 95% confidence interval (95% CI) for the estimated path coefficient that excludes 0 suggests that the direct, indirect, or total effect is statistically significant. A mediation effect was considered statistically significant if the indirect effect is statistically significant.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 29.0. Two-tailed tests were conducted, with statistical significance set at p≤0.05.

Results

A total sample of 1202 was used for the analysis. Of the total participants, 56.4% identified as female and 43.6% as male, with no respondents identifying with other gender categories. The youngest age group, 18–24 years, accounted for 8.6% of the sample, while participants aged 80 years or older represented 5.8%. Among the participants, more than 40% (483) mentioned that they had some form of experience related to the COVID-19 pandemic. The death literacy score was higher among participants who reported that the pandemic or VAD introduction had contributed to their death literacy than those who did not. As shown in Table 1, participants with any form of COVID or VAD related experiences had a higher death literacy score than those who had no such experiences. Among those with COVID experiences, those who cared for someone dying with COVID-19 had the highest death literacy, followed by those who organised funeral during the pandemic. Those with any VAD experiences were found to have highest death literacy among all. The scores for Practical knowledge, Experiential knowledge, Factual knowledge and Community knowledge were also significantly higher among those with COVID or VAD-related experiences.

Relationship of end-of-life attitude, experience and action with death literacy

Death literacy was higher in participants living with terminal illness, in primary carers of someone expected to die within 12 months, and in those supporting a primary carer compared to participants without these personal experiences. Participation in death-related community events or activities observed higher death literacy, alongside those with job related experiences, such as health professionals, paid workers, and those volunteering in death and grief support. Belief in the afterlife was also significantly associated with higher levels of death literacy in the bivariate analysis (See Supplemental file 1 - Table B). These findings suggest that death literacy is shaped by a range of personal, social, and contextual influences.

Similarly, participants who reported discussing death and dying in their family or community were more likely to have higher levels death literacy than those who did not. Participants with lower levels of death literacy tended to avoid conversations about death and dying, including conservations with the bereaved, in an attempt to circumvent discomfort for both them and the grieving person. Those who had undertaken end-of-life planning, such as wills, end-of-life wishes, or appointing financial or health decision makers, also had higher levels death literacy. Participants who supported carers by providing physical, emotional or social assistance were more likely to have higher death literacy, as well as those who were present at the time of someone’s death. Perceived availability of social capital was associated with higher levels of death literacy, such as feeling supported before, during, or after the death of someone close. (See Supplemental file 1 - Table C).

Consistent with the finding above, Practical knowledge, Experiential knowledge, Factual knowledge and Community knowledge were higher among those who held a positive attitude towards death and dying, had engaged in any action related to death or dying for themselves or for someone they are caring for, or had professional or volunteer experience in end-of-life care or bereavement care.

Associations of COVID-19 pandemic and VAD-related variables with death literacy

The variables included in the first stage of multivariate regression analysis were Income status (dummy variables – income<40K and Income>80K per annum), marital status (dummy variable – married, Never married and divorced), qualification of Year 12 or more, employment status (dummy variable – employed and not working), ancestry (dummy variable – Chinese and Indian), indigenous status, religion (Catholic, Islam and None), belief in afterlife, years lived in Australia (Dichotomised to 15 years or less and more than 15 years to distinguish more recent migrants from longer-term residents), paid employment experience in end-of-life or grief support, volunteering experience in end-of-life or grief support, current profession as a health or medical practitioner, participation in community events or activities related to death or dying, primary carer of someone in end-of-life care, and all measures related to end-of-life attitudes, experiences and actions mentioned above (see Supplemental file - Tables B–D).

The variables significantly associated with death literacy in this step included the experience of feeling supported around recent death, personal attitudes towards death and dying, social attitudes towards death and dying, action to support carers, social capital, participation in community events or activities related to death or dying, paid employment in end-of-life care or grief support, current health or medical practitioner, employed, non-religious status, never married and income level below AUD 40K (See Supplemental file 1, Table E).

In the second stage, COVID and VAD related variables were added to the model to assess their independent associations, adjusting for relevant covariates that were significant in the first stage (See Supplemental file 1, Table F). The final multivariate model (Table 2) indicated that knowledge contribution of the COVID-19 pandemic (B = 0.121) and VAD (B = 0.213) were positively associated with death literacy. However, knowing someone who died from COVID-19 had a significant negative association (B = -0.365), showing that personal loss from COVID-19 was linked to lower death literacy.

Positive indirect effects were observed for social capital, social attitudes toward death, and actions to support carers, indicating that these social factors were associated with higher death literacy, partly through pandemic-related exposure. The proportion mediated was relatively small, with 16.28% of the association between social capital and death literacy, 16.20% between social attitude toward death and death literacy, and 10.17% of the association between action to support carers and death literacy accounted for by indirect pathways associated with COVID-19 pandemic. As for personal attitudes toward death, a negative indirect effect of the COVID-19 pandemic (β = -.0940, p < .001) was observed. Although a positive attitude toward death had an overall positive total effect on death literacy (β = .1381), the pandemic exposure attenuated this relationship, with a relatively large negative proportion mediated (-68.07%).

Similarly, exposure to the introduction of VAD-related legislation in Australia was associated with indirect pathways linking social factors with higher death literacy. The proportion mediated was relatively small, with VAD exposure accounting for 15.32% of the total effect between social capital and death literacy, 21.37% between social attitude towards discussing death and death literacy, and 9.71% of the association between actions to support carers and death literacy. A negative indirect effect was observed for personal attitude towards death, with a negative proportion mediated of -37.51, indicating opposing indirect pathway (Table 3).

Discussion

This study adds to the understanding of death literacy as a socially embedded concept, suggesting that major societal events may cause disruption to the social processes and alter the broader context in which death-related learning occurs. This study examined how the COVID-19 pandemic and the introduction of VAD legislation were associated with death literacy in Australia. Findings suggest that both the COVID-19 pandemic and VAD introduction were significantly associated with death literacy, through direct associations as well as indirect pathways linking social factors with death literacy.

The COVID-19 pandemic represents one of the most significant global health crises to have an influence on the palliative and end-of-life care sector.6,25 It substantially disrupted established social practices and rituals around caregiving, dying at home, funeral arrangements, and mourning in the community.5,8 Studies reported that the pandemic delayed the referral to hospices and palliative care. 26 Despite the rise of home death during the pandemic, 27 only a small proportion of people were able to care for and say their final goodbyes to a dying family member or friend, reducing the experiential learning about death and dying. 28 However, widespread social media coverage (both positive and negative) and public health messaging during the pandemic also made death highly visible to the general population, contributing to increased public awareness on death and dying as well as fear among people. 10 In such context, this study found that death literacy scores were significantly higher among those who reported acquiring knowledge about end-of-life care or experiencing the death of someone close due to COVID-19, highlighting how pandemic-related exposure shaped death literacy in the communities.

In contrast, knowing someone who died from COVID-19 was negatively associated with death literacy in this study. One possible explanation is that many COVID-19 deaths occurred suddenly and in isolation with restricted visitation and limited funeral attendance. 29 These circumstances may have reduced opportunities for family members and the communities to be involved in caregiving, communicate with health professionals, participate in death and funeral-related decision making, and receive support during bereavement.29,30 An Australian bereavement study reported that two-thirds of its study participants had unmet support needs during the pandemic, 7 highlighting the impact of prolonged and restrictive public health measures endured to contain the COVID-19 infection. 31 This lack of meaningful engagement and support during death, funeral arrangements, and bereavement may have limited opportunities to develop knowledge, skills, and confidence around death-related processes.

Unlike the COVID-19 pandemic, the introduction of VAD legislation generated policy-driven societal exposure that invited increased public discussion through media and health systems and policy debates in Australia.16,17 Community attitudes toward VAD are mainly shaped by beliefs and emotions. 17 Evidence suggests that faith and religion have an impact on attitudes towards VAD, and religious people may experience higher moral distress in comparison to the secular population.32,33 Service providers and policymakers emphasise the complexity of VAD processes and the importance of providing accurate and accessible information for professionals, patients and families, supported by development of resources, policies, and awareness strategies. 34 A Queensland study reported that 33% of community members were aware that VAD was legal and 26% reported they know how to access a VAD service. 14 In this study, higher death literacy was observed among participants who learnt about end-of-life care due to the introduction of the VAD legislation, suggesting that policy-related exposure may also influence death-related learning.

This study also observed the influence of social factors and mechanisms in shaping death literacy across both COVID-19 pandemic and VAD introduction in Australia. Social capital is often viewed as critical to supporting the navigation of death, dying, funeral, and grief. 35 Research has shown that engaged citizens are more likely to access information and community resources, and are thus better able to cope with pandemic-like challenges. 36 However, other studies found that the lack of opportunities and skills to initiate discussion as well as personal discomfort inhibited death and dying conversations.26,37

The findings of this study suggest that the association between social mechanisms—social capital, social attitudes towards discussing death, and actions to support carers—and death literacy may be partly mediated by contextual events like COVID-19 pandemic and the introduction of VAD legislation. In contrast, personal attitudes toward death and dying did not translate into higher death literacy under these contexts. Attitudes toward death are often described in terms of whether individuals are more open to or avoid engaging with death related issues and conversations. 38 Death of a close family member is considered one of the most stressful events in a person’s life.39,40 The negative indirect effect observed in this study suggests that exposure to major societal events such as the pandemic and VAD may introduce uncertainty, distress, or avoidance, limiting the extent to which positive personal attitudes are translated into practical knowledge and engagement.

Further analysis of the same data found that social connections enhance confidence and improve understanding about death, thereby shaping how communities engage, share knowledge, and participate in supportive end-of-life care and other death-related practices. 41 Since death literacy is socially embedded and is mainly developed through shared social experiences, positive personal attitudes toward death may not always translate into higher death literacy within the contexts of social disruption or structural change, as observed in this study. This finding emphasise that death literacy is not just an individual attribute, but is also shaped by social and contextual factors that may influence people’s ability to access, understand and act on end-of-life information and resources within their social context.

During the pandemic, social isolation, movement restriction, and reduced opportunities to care and mourn together as a community may have denied individuals to learn even when personally open to discussing death and dying. 29 Similarly, VAD involves complex legal, medical and social engagement, that may have limited the opportunities for personal beliefs and openness to improve community death literacy. 11 Developing compassionate communities, implementing social prescribing interventions through formal health services and engaging communities to provide informal network-centred end-of-life care may play a critical role in maintaining social connection, improving social attitudes around death, developing social capital, and increasing community capacity, thereby developing death-literate communities.42–44

Limitations

This study assessed death literacy using DLI-R. While the DLI-R provides a structured quantitative assessment, it may not fully capture culture-specific, relational, spiritual, or community-based understandings and practices surrounding death and dying.

The cross-sectional design of this study limits the causal relationships between social factors, including societal exposures, and death literacy. The endogeneity may be present, as reciprocal relationships between social factors and death literacy cannot be ruled out. Mediation analyses should be carefully interpreted as exploratory and theory-generating, as both COVID-19 and VAD legislation were societal events and reflect contextual associations through which social factors related to death literacy.

Further, utilisation of online research panels to collect the data may have introduced selection bias as individuals with greater willingness to talk about death and loss may have been more likely to complete the survey. Stratified sampling was used to minimise the selection bias. The use of self-reported data may have introduced social desirability bias, which was mitigated by informing participants that the survey was anonymous and non-identifiable.

Conclusion

This study contributes to the public health palliative care literature by advancing our understanding of how social factors shape death literacy, and how this happens within the context of major societal events. By assessing the contextual influence of two distinct forms of exposure, the COVID-19 pandemic and the introduction of VAD legislation, this study highlights that death literacy does not develop in isolation but is shaped by social environments that enable or limit the exposure, awareness, and engagement to death, dying, and bereavement. Findings also emphasise the contribution of social capital, shared attitudes, participation in caregiving, and social support networks to develop the knowledge, skills, and confidence of individuals to deal with death-related issues. While findings should be interpreted within the Australian context, they may inform public health palliative care approaches in other settings experiencing similar societal and policy changes related to death and dying. This study reinforce the importance of public health palliative care approaches that promote community conversations around death and dying, strengthen social support systems and community-based end-of-life and bereavement care, especially during public health crises or policy change.

Future research should adopt longitudinal designs to improve our understanding of how major death-related societal level exposure influences death literacy and how it develops over time. In addition, qualitative and mixed-methods research would provide deeper insight into how individuals and communities experience death-related learning during periods of crisis or policy change.
