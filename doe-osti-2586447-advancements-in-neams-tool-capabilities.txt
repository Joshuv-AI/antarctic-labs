 Abstract
 Under the U.S. Department of Energy Office of Nuclear Energy Advanced Modeling and
Simulation (NEAMS) Program, an integrated multiphysics approach is being developed to model
the core bowing phenomena important to liquid metal-cooled fast reactors. Core bowing is an
important passive safety mechanism in liquid metal-cooled fast reactors and involves multiphysics
effects including radiation transport, fluid flow, heat transfer, and mechanical response to
temperature and flux gradients. This report summarizes recent progress on developing a
multiphysics, MOOSE-based workflow to predict core bowing and associated reactivity feedback.
 Last year, thermal fluids and mechanics were coupled on a multi-assembly benchmark problem
based on ABR-1000 design. This year, the reactor physics code Griffin was assessed for readiness
of core bowing calculations. Preliminary integration of Griffin’s ring-heterogeneous model with
thermal fluids and thermal mechanics solvers was performed. Specifically, thermal-mechanics and
reactor physics were coupled for single- and multi-assembly problems, and reactor physics and
subchannel methods were coupled for a single assembly model. Finally, the workflow of all three
physics was preliminarily demonstrated on a single assembly model. Caveats and future
development needed have been identified.
 To supplement the multiphysics demonstration, verification and assessment efforts of thermo-
mechanical capabilities for modeling thermo-mechanical core bowing behavior were continued by
analyzing IAEA Verification Problem 5 which includes radiation swelling and creep. Additionally,
a small core reactor physics benchmark defined by Japan Atomic Energy Agency (JAEA) was
performed to assess neutronics models for estimating reactivity feedback.
 Finally, Fast Flux Test Facility (FFTF) validation test data for core bowing phenomena has
been identified and summarized, with a recommended path forward for validation once this
capability is mature.

1 Introduction
 Under the U.S. Department of Energy Office of Nuclear Energy’s Advanced Modeling and
Simulation (NEAMS) Program [1], an integrated multiphysics approach using the Multiphysics
Object Oriented Simulation Environment (MOOSE) [2] is being developed to model core bowing
phenomena in liquid metal-cooled fast reactors (LFMR). Core bowing is an important passive
safety mechanism in LMFRs. When the core restraint system is properly designed, temperature
and flux gradients influence assemblies in the core to bow into less reactive configurations during
accident scenarios, resulting in negative reactivity feedback. However, core bowing is difficult to
predict due to complex interplay of radiation transport, heat transfer to the duct, and thermo-
mechanical responses of the induced temperature and flux gradients. While some legacy tools are
available to predict core bowing phenomena, there is no integrated capability which directly
predicts both the deformation and the reactivity feedback in high detail. Given that the reactivity
feedback effect is induced by small deformation on the order of millimeters, a high-fidelity physics
reference solution capability is needed to instill confidence in existing techniques, and to predict
bowing response for yet-to-be-built restraint system designs in accident scenarios.
 In FY24, a one-way thermal fluids-structural mechanics coupling demonstration was performed
[3] using MOOSE Subchannel (MOOSE-SCM) [4], Heat Transfer, Solid Mechanics, and Contact
modules on 7-assembly and 19-assembly cores based on ABR-1000 [5] sodium-cooled fast reactor
(SFR) fuel assemblies. That demonstration produced qualitatively correct results for radial bowing
driven by imposed thermal gradients. However, this workflow did not include reactor physics.
 The focus of this year’s work was to begin integrating the reactor physics code Griffin [6] into
the multiphysics workflow and assess what development work is still needed. We focus our
multiphysics demonstration efforts on small ABR-1000-based geometries (single and seven
assembly). We examine the coupling of reactor physics to both fluids as well as structural
mechanics calculations. We also examined the fidelity of thermal fluids model needed for accurate
duct wall temperature prediction. Finally, we demonstrate an initial three-physics workflow
involving thermal fluids, solid mechanics, and reactor physics on a single assembly.
 In addition to these multiphysics assessments using ABR-1000, we continued to perform
verification activities of individual physics for the specific application to core bowing. For the
second year, along with the ART Fast Reactor Program, we participated in the U.S.-Japan Civil
Nuclear Energy Working Group (CNWG) Expert Group on Core Bowing, where we contributed
to a reactor physics benchmark. The findings from this code-to-code comparison activity will be
useful to compare reactor physics models and associated accuracy. We also continued verification
of MOOSE’s Solid Mechanics [7] and Contact Modules [8] by leveraging code benchmark
problems developed by the International Working Group on Fast Reactors (IWGFR) which was
organized and openly published by the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) [9]. To date,
MOOSE’s thermo-mechanical modeling tools have been assessed on increasingly complex

 1

problems for producing core bowing predictions consistent with conventional tools from a
structural mechanics standpoint only. This status report describes additional work on IAEA
Verification Problem 5 (VP5) including assessments of MOOSE capabilities related to irradiation
swelling and creep material behavior.
 Finally, in partnership with the DOE ART Fast Reactor Program, we have identified available
validation data for core bowing phenomena. The identification of this data early in the project
allows us to plan how to validate the tool once it is mature.

 The demonstrations in this report are performed on two core bowing assessment cases derived
from the ABR-1000 design.
2.1 Single Assembly Model
 The ABR-1000 sodium-cooled fast reactor design [5] is shown in Figure 2-1. For the single
assembly case, we extract one inner core fuel assembly from the core. The ternary metal alloy (U-
TRU-10Zr) fuel assembly contains 271 fuel pins arranged in a triangular pitch array. At cold
conditions, the assembly pitch is 16.142 cm, and the total duct height is 477.52 cm (4.7752 m).
The fuel pin outer diameter is 0.755 cm with a cladding thickness of 0.056 cm. A wire wrap with
a diameter of 0.131 cm is placed around the fuel pins. The total pin length and active core height
are 332.7 cm and 81.3 cm, respectively.
 The fuel pin and axial layouts of the fuel assembly are shown in Figure 2-2 and Figure 2-3.
Detailed cold dimensions for this geometry are available in other references; cold condition
dimensions (20 °C) were expanded to hot condition (380 °C) for this analysis prior to performing
bowing calculations. The hot expanded assembly pitch is 16.2471 cm, and the hot height is ~480
cm. The solid mechanics solver models the entire duct domain, including the 35.76 cm region of
inlet nozzle/nosepiece where the assembly attaches to the grid. We note that the reactor physics
domain is a subset of the full duct, as shown in the right of Figure 2-3.

3 MOOSE-Based Multiphysics Workflow
 This chapter describes the MOOSE-based physics applications and how they are coupled
together to model the physics associated with core bowing.
3.1 MOOSE MultiApp Hierarchy
 To predict reactivity feedback due to core bowing, we intend to use several MOOSE-based
tools in a MultiApp hierarchy which allows flexible execution and data transfer. The physics
involved with core bowing includes neutronics (Griffin), heat transfer in both fluid and solid
domains (MOOSE Subchannel and Heat Transfer modules), and thermo-mechanical responses
(MOOSE Solid Mechanics and Contact modules). We will begin with steady state normal
conditions to ensure transfers are correctly working prior to inducing transients.
 Our target MultiApp hierarchy is shown in Figure 3-1. The reactor physics code Griffin serves
as the MainApp, under which there are three MultiApps: Griffin Pin Power Reconstruction, Heat
Conduction (MOOSE Heat Transfer module), and Solid Mechanics. Under Heat Conduction, the
Subchannel (MOOSE SCM) MultiApp calculates the temperature distributions within each
assembly. Separate MOOSE SCM Apps are created for each assembly.

 Figure 3-1. MOOSE MultiApp Hierarchy for Multiphysics Simulation of Core Bowing

 During a coupled calculation, Griffin MainApp transfers neutron flux to Griffin Pin Power
Reconstruction, then the reconstructed pin power is transferred to Subchannel through the Heat
Conduction app. Together, Heat Conduction and its Subchannel subapps calculate the core
temperature distribution and transfer the duct temperature distribution to Solid Mechanics App.

 7

With the duct temperature, Solid Mechanics calculates detailed duct displacements, accounting for
contact with other assemblies, and transfers these displacements back to Griffin for neutronics
calculation with displaced geometry. The data transfer between Griffin Pin Power Reconstruction
and Heat Conduction and between Heat Conduction and Solid Mechanics are performed with the
MOOSE sibling transfer capability such that the data can be sent from a MultiApp to another
MultiApp under the same MainApp directly. Presently, we do not send temperature back to Griffin
from Heat Conduction, but in the future, this data transfer between Heat Conduction and Griffin
MainApp will be added.

3.2 Reactor Physics: Griffin
 The MOOSE-based Griffin reactor physics code can compute the detailed flux and power
distribution on the initial and deformed geometries using one of several models, depicted in Figure
3-2.
 1. Fully heterogeneous model: Pins, coolant, duct, and inter-assembly gap are explicitly
 represented.
 2. Ring-heterogeneous model: Pins and coolant are explicitly represented in an
 “equivalent” hexagonal-ring geometry; duct and inter-assembly gap are explicitly
 represented. (In this work, we use the detailed ring-heterogeneous model which has
 explicit cladding regions on each side of the fuel region.)
 3. Duct-heterogeneous model: Pins and coolant are homogenized; duct and inter-
 assembly gap are explicitly represented. This model also incorporates a distinct region
 of coolant just inside the duct wall.

 Figure 3-2. Radial View of Assembly Modeling Geometries: Fully Heterogeneous (Left),
 Ring-Heterogeneous (Center), and Duct-Heterogeneous (Right)

 These models have advantages and drawbacks. The fully heterogeneous model includes no
geometry approximations (aside from not modeling wire wrap explicitly which does not

 8

neutronically impact the solution) but is computationally intractable for a full core problem due to
the detailed mesh representation. Pin power reconstruction (PPR) is not required for this method
since pins are explicitly modeled. The fully heterogeneous model is also not available in Griffin’s
automated SFR workflow (which includes automated multigroup cross section generation), so
cannot be created without significant effort. Previous work studied the accuracy of the ring-
heterogenous method for nominal (undeformed) geometries [10].
 The ring-heterogeneous model is available in Griffin’s automated SFR workflow and holds the
advantage of separating solid and fluid material zones, which is important because sodium backfill
can be more easily treated. Sodium backfill refers to the filling of liquid sodium into volumes that
increase (or decrease) such that sodium density is conserved, but not sodium mass. This is in
contrast to solid materials, which expand or contract, but do not increase in mass. The accurate
treatment of material densities in neutronics due to deformation is critically important to obtaining
the correct reactivity feedback. The disadvantage of the ring-heterogeneous model is the
approximate fuel and cladding geometry representation, as well as the increased density of mesh
elements compared to a homogenized model which may deform into unphysical shapes as the
calculation progresses.
 The duct-heterogeneous model is also available in Griffin’s automated SFR workflow.
Homogenized assemblies are well-known to be accurate for fast spectrum reactors due to long
mean free paths, and the duct heterogeneous model combines this intra-duct homogenization with
the duct and inter-assembly heterogeneity required for capturing leakage effects. However, the
duct heterogeneous model combines solid and fluid materials inside the duct, and currently, Griffin
has no capability to re-homogenize the solid-fluid mixture to correctly treat their relative densities
upon deformation. Therefore, presently, the duct heterogeneous model cannot be used for this
application. The Griffin development team plans to add the capability to handle mixed fluid-solid
materials in deformed mesh cases. Beneficially, the duct-heterogeneous model has the fewest
number of mesh elements compared to the ring heterogeneous and fully heterogeneous approaches,
it should be the least susceptible to high computational time or bad convergence.
 PPR is required for both ring-heterogeneous and duct-heterogeneous models. PPR is available
in Griffin for both standard and deformed geometries, although assumptions are made for
deformed geometries that require verification. Reconstructed pin powers are passed to the MOOSE
SCM for thermal-hydraulics computations.

3.3 Thermal Fluids: MOOSE SCM Heat Conduction
 Subchannel codes are thermal-hydraulic codes that offer an efficient compromise for the
simulation of a nuclear reactor core, with modeling fidelity between that of Computational Fluid
Dynamics (CFD) and system codes. Although CFD codes offer a robust mechanism to predict core
temperature distribution, they present significant computational cost constraints when considering
whole-core analyses. Further, even at the assembly-level, computational time associated with
 9

iterative design calculations using CFD tools remains substantial. Subchannel analyses use a quasi-
3D model formulation and allow for a finer mesh than system codes without the high
computational cost of CFD. Present methodologies and foreseeable advancements underscore the
relevance of subchannel analysis in the thermal-hydraulics evaluation of SFRs.
 Consequently, the subchannel analysis code MOOSE SCM was selected and utilized for this
application. MOOSE SCM is a MOOSE thermal hydraulic subchannel analysis module that
performs reactor core, single-phase subchannel simulations. Initially developed for water-cooled
reactors with square-lattice arrangements, it has been extended to model liquid-metal fast reactors
with wire-wrapped fuel pins in hexagonal assemblies. MOOSE SCM leverages the MOOSE
framework and PETSc [11] library to solve the subchannel equations using a Newton non-linear
solver. For the hexagonal assemblies with wire-wrapped fuel pins, the friction factors and mixing
parameters are determined with Cheng and Todreas correlations [12]. Since MOOSE SCM models
only a single assembly, multiple assembly modeling requires MOOSE SCM to be coupled to the
MOOSE Heat Transfer module to observe heat transfer between assemblies. Specifically, in the
coupled multi-assembly model, the inter-assembly gap and duct wall are modeled with heat
conduction in the MOOSE Heat Transfer module. The heat flux at the inner surface of the duct is
transferred from Heat Conduction to MOOSE SCM, and the temperature at the inner duct surface
temperature is transferred from MOOSE SCM to Heat Conduction calculation. Picard iterations
are performed between Heat Conduction and MOOSE SCM to achieve a converged thermal-
hydraulics solution. Once convergence is achieved, the duct temperature distribution is transferred
from MOOSE Heat Conduction to Solid Mechanics simulation.

3.4 Thermal Mechanics: MOOSE Solid Mechanics and Contact
 The MOOSE Solid Mechanics Module is a library of tools for simulating continuum mechanics,
which can model the mechanical behavior of solids elements. It can be used in purely mechanical
simulations or be coupled with simulations in multiphysics. Solid Mechanics can simulate both
linear and finite strain mechanics, including elasticity, plasticity, and creep. For high-fidelity
analysis of fast reactor systems, it is important to account for several simulation scenarios,
including linear elastic behavior, thermal expansion, and irradiation material effects, along with
inter-assembly contact due to gap closures as the ducts deform. Beam elements used in low-fidelity
simulations can estimate the overall displacement behavior of the centerlines of beams, and in fast
reactors assembly ducts, but they usually neglect cross-section distortion effects due to expansion,
creep, and contact. The use of solid elements, in contrast to beam element formulation common
for low-fidelity simulation, allows the treatment of radial and azimuthal temperature and
irradiation material behavior over the duct cross sections, rather than taking average or absolute
differences between duct faces or corners. The Contact module is used to treat the contact between
assemblies at the load pad locations, using node-on-face, penalty contact formulation.

 Displacements computed by the MOOSE Solid Mechanics module are transferred to Griffin for
calculation of the reactivity in the deformed state.

4 Assessment of Single Physics Models
 Single physics models for the test cases were assessed prior to coupling multiple physics
applications into the workflow, which introduces complexities related to data transfer and
execution strategy.
4.1 Single Assembly Ring-Heterogeneous and Duct-Heterogeneous Griffin Models
 The use of ring-heterogeneous and duct-heterogeneous models in Griffin for fast reactor
analysis has been scant to date, as fully homogenized assemblies are typically preferred for
modeling of fast spectrum systems. However, for the problem of core bowing, local changes in
gap thickness between fuel assemblies play a prominent role in introducing reactivity feedback.
Additionally, the duct wall temperature is important to predict as it drives the bowing itself.
Therefore, the use of a model with heterogeneous gap and duct wall is likely preferred.
 The following discussion of standalone physics models is provided only as a brief description
of the approaches applied. The single assembly ring-heterogeneous and duct-heterogeneous
models in Griffin were compared to quantify differences in effective multiplication factor (k-eff),
reconstructed pin powers, and computational time. These are presented merely to give readers a
sense of the two modeling approaches. Such comparisons should not be interpreted as meaningful
benchmarks, since both sets of calculations fall outside their intended application range. In
particular, high-leakage problems are unsuitable for verification because the spectrum used for
cross-section generation differs substantially from that of a single-assembly calculation with
vacuum boundary conditions and strong leakage. Instead, these demonstrations were used to
understand approximations and implications on coupling with other physics models.
 Griffin’s automated SFR workflow capability was utilized to automatically generate both sets
of inputs based on the heterogeneous assembly geometry description, which is based on the
MOOSE Reactor Module [13] intrinsic meshing capabilities. For both cases, the DFEM-SN solver
was employed with 2 polar angles, 3 azimuthal angles, and a maximum scattering anisotropy set
to 2. The CMFD acceleration method was implemented with on-the-fly coarse mesh generation.
A 33-energy group structure was automatically generated using the SFR workflow, and vacuum
boundary conditions were applied on all sides (top, bottom, and lateral surfaces). The ring- and
duct-heterogeneous models both utilized Griffin’s pin power reconstruction capabilities to
generate pin-wise powers from a non fully-heterogeneous flux solution. The axial zones in the
Griffin models from bottom to top are the lower shield/reflector (125.16 cm), active core (85.82
cm), sodium plenum (20.06 cm), and void plenum (101.01 cm). The total assembly height is
332.05 cm, with midpoint at 166.025 cm. The active core midpoint is at 168.07 cm.

MOOSE Heat Transfer and MOOSE SCM was demonstrated last year [3] on a seven-assembly
problem and two nineteen-assembly problems based on the ABR-1000 sodium-cooled fast reactor
design. Unlike the simpler single assembly test cases, multi-assembly problems require the

MOOSE Heat Transfer module due to inter-assembly heat transfer. The coupling of MOOSE Heat
Transfer, MOOSE SCM and MOOSE Solid Mechanics was performed as shown inside the dotted

 No Griffin reactor physics calculations were involved. Instead, the power distribution was
directly applied as user input to MOOSE SCM (no reactor physics calculation was involved). The
heat flux at the inner surface of the duct is transferred from MOOSE Heat Transfer to MOOSE
SCM, and the temperature at the inner duct surface temperature is transferred from MOOSE SCM
to MOOSE heat conduction calculation. Picard iterations are performed between MOOSE Heat

is achieved, the duct temperature distribution is transferred to MOOSE Solid Mechanics. The
thermally-driven displacements are then calculated by MOOSE Solid Mechanics and stored as
nodal displacements in variables disp_x, disp_y, and disp_z for the x, y, and z
displacements, respectively. The demonstrations have showcased the capability of coupled
MOOSE SCM, MOOSE Heat Transfer, and MOOSE Solid Mechanics solvers to predict detailed
duct wall temperatures in small core configurations, including modeling heat transfer between
assemblies, and furthermore, the ability to calculate complex deformations based on thermal
gradients.

5.3 Solid Mechanics Feedback to Reactor Physics: Coupling MOOSE Solid
 Mechanics and Griffin
 Finally, the coupling of solid mechanics (MOOSE Solid Mechanics) and reactor physics
(Griffin) was tested. The Solid Mechanics solve computes the duct thermal expansion and
subsequent duct displacements while considering contact with neighboring assemblies. Typical
thermal gradients result in “bowed” assemblies (unique to each assembly’s location) that move
fuel in the core and locally change the gap size between assemblies. Solid materials expand but
preserve mass. The density of liquid coolant changes according to temperature as the coolant fills
in around expanded/contracted solid materials (known as sodium backfill). These material changes
are essential to capture in the reactor physics model, as they significantly impact reactivity
feedback.
 The approach to coupling MOOSE Solid Mechanics and Griffin is illustrated in Figure 5-5.
MOOSE Heat Conduction and SCM are not involved in this calculation. Instead, fixed duct
temperature inputs as described in Section 4.4 are provided to Solid Mechanics to initiate the
bowing simulation.

 Figure 5-5. MultiApp hierarchy for testing coupled workflow with MOOSE Solid Mechanics
 and Griffin

 27

 The Solid Mechanics model only models the duct walls and load pads, omitting the assembly
internals since their contribution to the duct bending stiffness is assumed to be negligent. However,
the Griffin and Subchannel models contain meshed elements and nodes in those regions. To
transfer the displacements, the internals are assumed to move along with the duct walls and take
on the same displacement values, however due to expansion and slight distortion of the duct walls,
interpolated or average values at every axial section are needed. We now discuss two possible
methods for transferring displacements from a duct-only mesh to the reactor physics mesh
covering the duct and assembly internals.
 Mesh Divisions Transfer: One method for transferring the displacements is to use mesh
divisions to partition the hexagonal ducts into sections where the displacements can be
interpolated. For this method, CylindricalGridDivision is used to partition the duct into
slices and average the displacements for the sections with
MeshDivisionFunctorReductionVectorPostprocessor and store them into
AuxVariables spatially with a SpatialUserObjectAux. Then, the average values are
transferred to the Griffin mesh with MultiAppGeneralFieldUserObjectTransfer,
which allows interpolation to the mesh elements in the Griffin mesh which exist within the duct
center region. This method was tested by running an example duct displacement simulation and
transferring the displacement data back onto a separate duct mesh, which had the same dimensions
as the thermal mechanical model. Figure 5-6 shows the results of the averaged centerline
displacements of the Solid Mechanics solution plotted with the averaged centerline displacement
of the model where the displacements were transferred, showing matching displacement behavior.
 Average Displacement Transfer: A simpler method for transferring the displacements is to take
the average disp_x and disp_y values for each axial layer of the duct and transfer it directly
to the duct wall in the Griffin model and use a nearest location transfer to apply the average values
to the duct internal sections. This method uses
MultiAppGeneralFieldNearestLocationTransfer to transfer the displacements
from the Solid Mechanics duct walls to the duct walls in the Griffin model. We note that the duct
walls themselves receive the actual duct displacements from the Solid Mechanics model, whereas
the duct interior (fuel zone) receives these average values. The duct walls must have subdomain
IDs different from the duct interior material so that when the average values are applied, the duct
wall displacements aren’t overwritten by average values. Then, the average displacement value of
the duct walls at every axial level is found with NearestPointLayeredAverage and applied
to the non-duct wall subdomains.

5.3.1. Single Assembly
 A coupled simulation integrating Griffin and Solid Mechanics was conducted for a single
assembly case in which a thermal gradient drives the duct into a restrained bow shape (see Section
4.4). The simulation utilized a MultiApp framework, with Griffin designated as the parent
application. Two child applications were incorporated: Griffin PPR and Solid Mechanics. The
displacement data computed by Solid Mechanics shown in Figure 5-7 was transferred to Griffin.
Griffin employed the ring-heterogeneous model with DFEM-SN(2,3) solver, and the
use_displaced_mesh option was enabled within the TransportSystems block so that
the transport solver computes the solution on the displaced mesh. CMFD acceleration was
employed, as Griffin can reconstruct coarse mesh information using the updated displaced mesh.
 The average displacement-type transfer was used in this example. It is worth noting that the z-
displacement for both the duct interior and exterior was set to zero during this process because the
interior z-displacement should be derived from fuel expansion calculations, not duct expansion.

 29

 Figure 5-7. Duct displacements in x, y, and z directions along with displacement magnitude
 (calculated by Solid Mechanics)

 The coolant regions in the ring-heterogeneous model deform, but in order to treat the fluid
density correctly, Griffin’s new fluid_density and reference_fluid_density
parameters were employed to override mesh changes on density and instead incorporate
temperature feedback. These parameters can be summarized as:
 • fluid_density_reference: the initial fluid density at the reference temperature
 • fluid_density: the current fluid density (potentially at a different temperature).
The ratio of these two parameters determines the density change for the material. If there is no
change in temperature (e.g. for simple problems), then setting these two parameters to the same
value holds the fluid density constant at its reference temperature value. If there is a change in
temperature, fluid_density can be retrieved during the simulation according to a temperature
distribution.
 The upper and lower plenum in the single assembly were modeled as homogenized regions
containing both fluid and solid materials. This is a typical modeling approximation because
detailed geometry information in these zones is usually not available or needed neutronically. In
these homogenized regions, fluid_density and reference_fluid_density were used
to control the fluid density instead of displacements along with the fluid_isotopes parameter
which identifies which isotopes are fluid (e.g., sodium).
 Griffin still requires further development to handle solid isotope densities which are collocated
with fluid isotopes in the same mixture. Currently, solid isotope densities are fixed in these zones,
whereas they should expand based on the mesh displacements. In summary, isotopic densities of
 30

homogenized regions are not treated correctly yet in Griffin following mesh deformation. This
means that the duct-heterogeneous method, which homogenizes the coolant and fuel pins, will not
correctly treat material densities in Griffin at time. For this reason, no duct-heterogeneous results
are shown in this report. These capability improvements are in progress but were not available for
this report.
 Accounting for temperature feedback effects from Doppler broadening in both solid and fluids
further requires temperature tabulation of multigroup cross sections in Griffin’s ISOXML format.
This coupling example did not employ temperature-dependent cross-sections. Temperature
dependent cross sections will be assessed in the future once temperature feedback is incorporated
from the Heat Conduction/SCM module and applied to a more realistic test case with smaller
neutron leakage (to remove impacts due to strong flux dependence of the cross sections)..

5.4 Three-Way Coupled Simulation: Subchannel, Griffin, and Thermo-Mechanics
 Following the coupling demonstration and assessment of individual pairs of physics, we
attempted to couple all physics together (Griffin, MOOSE Heat Transfer/SCM, and MOOSE Solid
Mechanics) on the single assembly problem. The MultiApp hierarchy in this coupled simulation
is shown in Figure 5-13. It is slightly different than the hierarchy shown in Figure 3-1 in two ways.
First, the MOOSE Heat Transfer Module is not invoked since it is only needed for multi-assembly
problems. Instead, power is transferred directly from Griffin PPR to MOOSE SCM. Second, a
 40

special execution sequence of these codes (noted in circled numbers) is required for each iteration
to ensure the data transfers are performed in the expected order, e.g. that fields are updated before
they are used. Notably, sibling transfers (transfers between two child apps) are only executed
before the execution of the child application, so the sibling applications must be executed on a
different schedule to enable their coupling. The requirement of this execution sequence is a current
limitation in MOOSE which can be lifted in the future with additional development of the sibling
transfer capability, which is planned for early FY26.
 At the beginning of the simulation, MOOSE SCM (1) is executed using an initial power
assumption to compute duct wall temperature. Next, Griffin (2) computes the flux and power
distribution without any input from SCM (as we have not yet implemented temperature feedback),
or Solid Mechanics, as no deformation calculation has yet taken place. Third, Griffin Pin Power
Reconstruction (3) computes the pin powers and transfers these to MOOSE SCM. Following this
data transfer, the MOOSE Solid Mechanics (4) calculation is performed using the original SCM
duct wall temperature solution (prior to Griffin feedback) and transfers the displacement back to
the Griffin MainApp (at end of timestep).
 Griffin then receives the displacements, which will be used in the next iteration. Then we
proceed to the next iteration, crucially not resetting the pin-powers sent to MOOSE SCM by Griffin
in the first iteration. MOOSE SCM (1) executes again using the pin powers from the non-deformed
Griffin case, as they have not been computed yet. It is not until iteration 3 that MOOSE SCM
receives the pin powers from the deformed Griffin case. This current execution strategy results in
a lag of data as the pin power distributions used in MOOSE SCM are always from the Griffin
results of last iteration.

6 Thermo-Mechanical Analysis of IAEA Verification Problem 5
  (VP5) Symmetric Sector
 The International Working Group on Fast Reactors (IWGFR) verification and validation report,
which was organized and openly published by the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA)
[9], contain a series of thermo-mechanical computational verification problems (VP) relevant to
core bowing phenomena which specify input temperatures, irradiation damage dose, and contact
phenomena. Past benchmark participants contributed solutions using a variety of approaches, for
VPs ranging from single duct bowing to complex multiple assembly problems. The benchmark
participant solutions can be used to assess the accuracy of MOOSE-based thermo-mechanical
physics solvers for solving core-bowing related problems.
 In past years, we demonstrated good agreement between MOOSE Solid Mechanics simulations
and the IAEA working group participant results for VP1, VP3A, VP3B, VP4, VP7A, and VP7B
for displacement and contact force estimations [3] [16] [17]. The goal is to leverage continually
improved capabilities of the MOOSE thermo-mechanical solvers to solve larger problems (in
terms of DOFs and contact points). This year, we continued assessments with VP5, which is a core
sector undergoing bowing due to irradiation damage dose induced swelling and creep, including
limited free-bow core restraint system and duct-to-duct contact interactions.
 The MOOSE Solid Mechanics simulation results are compared with the IAEA participants’
software results and the NUBOW-3D core bowing code developed at Argonne, which are based
on results from first generation software with beam element formulation to calculate displacement
and contact. The MOOSE Solid Mechanics models use 3D solid elements, with the ducts meshed
as empty, thin-walled hexagonal cross-sections. Comparing MOOSE’s solid model predictions
with beam element predictions presents some challenges. For consistent comparisons of bowing
deflections using solid element models against beam element models, which only specify
centerline displacements, the effective centerline displacement of each duct is found by averaging
all the nodal displacements at each cross-section location along the axis of the 3D ducts. Therefore,
some differences in results are expected since MOOSE considers cross-section distortion and
twisting effects for the nodal displacements, which are captured when averaging the cross-section
displacements.
6.1 VP5 Problem Description
 We continued working on IAEA Verification Problem 5 which began in FY23 [17]. Initial
results showed good agreement with the duct deflections due to irradiation swelling, but neglected
creep behavior, which resulted in contact force estimations which could not be reasonably
compared. Another key item of concern was the meshing of the boundary condition. Previous
modeling for VP3A and VP3B [16] considered the lower boundary of the duct as completely rigid,
while the VP5 model considers a rotational stiffness of 1e8 N/mm which was modeled directly
with a lower nozzle element.

feedback was calculated directly by Griffin (NEAMS).

8 FFTF Test Data Benchmarks to Support Validation of Fast
  Reactor Core Bowing Predictions
 For validation of fast reactor codes that simulate radial core expansion and assembly bowing in
reactor cores with a limited free bow core restraint system and the reactivity feedbacks generated
by such systems, the only available data is from tests performed at the Fast Flux Test Facility
(FFTF). The Passive Safety Testing Program (PST) included a set of tests for characterizing the
system’s reactivity feedbacks and a series of Loss of Flow Without Scram (LOFWOS) tests as part
of an effort to evaluate the system’s safety margins and demonstrate FFTF’s safety features. Both
sets of tests were also performed to support code validation, so key measurements were recorded
and are still available today.
 Benchmark specifications that have been defined for these tests in support of fast reactor code
validation are described in the following subsections along with recommendations for utilizing
those benchmarks [20], [21]. For each test type described below, the different components of radial
core expansion generated during the tests are highlighted.
8.1 Cycle 8A Reactivity Characterization Tests
 The reactivity characterization tests consisted of quasi-static steps where all or some of the core
inlet temperature, flow rate, and power level were changed to induce reactivity feedback responses
[21]. More than 200 such tests, or steps, were performed during Cycle 8A, a few months before
the Cycle 8C LOFWOS tests described in the next section. The conditions for each test were
defined such that a target set of reactivity feedbacks generated significant responses while the other
reactivity feedbacks generated negligible or nearly negligible responses. For example, Type 1 tests
varied power and flow so that fuel temperatures changed significantly, cladding temperatures
changed very little, and coolant and structure temperatures did not change at all. Although each
type of test stimulates responses from multiple reactivity feedbacks, these tests are sometimes
referred to as the individual reactivity feedback tests.
 During these tests, core power was varied between 10% and 100%, core flow between 67% and
100%, and the core inlet temperature between 576 K and 633 K. After the conditions for a specific
test were changed, reactor conditions were held constant for an extended period of time as the
system adjusted to the new steady-state conditions. For each test, the control rod position change
required to maintain the new conditions was measured and converted to a change in reactivity to
determine the net change in reactivity induced by the initial core condition changes. The data that
has been preserved for these tests also includes the calculated changes in reactivity associated with
fuel burnup. Reference [21] defines the boundary conditions and the resulting changes in reactivity
for each of the Cycle 8A reactivity characterization tests.
 There were seven types of Cycle 8A tests:
 • Type 1: Fuel Temperature Tests,

 67

 • Type 2: Constant Average Temperature Tests,
 • Type 3: Constant Outlet Temperature Tests,
 • Type 4: Temperature Coefficient Tests,
 • Type 5: Flow Coefficient Tests,
 • Type 6: Static Loss of Flow Tests, and
 • Type 7: Power Coefficient Tests.
 The different conditions defined for each test meant that the components of the radial core
expansion reactivity feedback that were stimulated varied from test type to test type. In the Type
1 tests, there was no significant reactivity feedback from radial core expansion or assembly bowing
because the coolant temperature profile remained unchained.
 The reactivity feedback due to radial core expansion in the Type 3 tests was dominated by grid
plate expansion because of changing core inlet temperatures. Temperatures at the top of the fuel
and above did not change, so there was no feedback due to expansion of the upper core structures,
and likely minimal assembly bowing, in these tests.
 In the Type 4 tests, the test conditions were defined to induce uniform coolant, cladding, and
structure temperature increases across the height of the core. The radial core expansion reactivity
feedback for these tests was dominated by uniform radial core expansion.
 In the Type 5, Type 6, and Type 7 tests, the core inlet temperature was held constant while
coolant, cladding, and structure temperatures above the core inlet changed. Thus, in these tests the
radial core expansion reactivity feedback included components of expansion at the above core load
pads and assembly bowing, with no component due to grid plate expansion.
 Finally, in the Type 2 tests, the entire coolant temperature profile changed, including at the core
inlet. Thus, for these tests all components of radial core expansion were generated: grid plate
expansion, expansion at the above core load pads, and assembly bowing.
 Along with the definitions of each of the 200+ tests, Reference [21] provides further details of
which reactivity feedbacks were generated during each test type. While the benchmarks for the
LOFWOS tests discussed in the next section are defined for transient progressions to be simulated,
the Cycle 8A tests are defined for analysts to calculate the change in reactivity introduced by each
new set of boundary conditions for the core. There is no data supporting transient progressions
during the tests.
8.2 Loss of Flow Without Scram (LOFWOS) Tests
 While the scope of the Cycle 8A reactivity characterization tests was limited to the reactor core,
the LOFWOS tests performed during Cycle 8C were integral tests encompassing all major plant
systems. The tests were initiated by the three primary pumps being tripped simultaneously with
the plant protection system modified to prevent the control rods from inserting prematurely. The

 68

secondary loop pumps remained fully operational throughout the LOFWOS tests. Additionally,
the fan speeds of the dump heat exchangers (DHX), i.e. sodium-to-air heat exchangers that rejected
heat generated in the system to the environment, were reduced shortly before the primary pumps
tripped. The timing of the DHX fan speed adjustments was intended to reduce how much the core
inlet temperature changed at the start of each test.
 Thirteen LOFWOS were performed. In the first six tests, the primary loop pony motors were
left on to ensure flow rates remained at approximately 9% of the nominal full flow rate after the
primary pumps tripped. In the other seven tests, the pony motors were left off, allowing the system
to transition to natural circulation without any assistance. Initial core power ranged from 10% to
50%, and all tests were initiated at 100% flow.
 Reference [20] is the benchmark specification for LOFWOS Test #13, which was originally
analyzed under an International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) Coordinated Research Project
(CRP). This report provides all necessary information for developing a model of the FFTF core,
primary loops, and secondary loops. Boundary conditions are defined for LOFWOS Test #13 for
the speeds of the three primary loop pumps, mass flow rates of the three secondary loops, and the
sodium outlet temperatures for each DHX. Assemblies have sufficient descriptions although load
pads are not defined, and assemblies utilize irradiation history (given as effective full power days
for each assembly). Previous neutronics modeling work by the ART Fast Reactor Program for
Cycle 8C (LOFWOS) could be leveraged for Cycle 8A core analysis. Additionally, previous
modeling efforts may be available using SAS and/or SAM.
 Following the completion of the IAEA CRP, Argonne, under a CRADA with TerraPower,
developed benchmark specifications for LOFWOS Tests #10-12, which were similar to LOFWOS
Test #13 (all were pump trip to natural circulation tests) but with an initial core power level of
40%, 45%, and 50%. Tests 12 and 13 were initiated from essentially the same conditions. The
benchmark specifications of those tests are provided in Reference [21], the same report that defines
the Cycle 8A tests.
 As stated above, one objective of the LOFWOS tests was to demonstrate the unique safety
features in the FFTF design. This includes the reactivity feedback generated by the limited free
bow core restraint system during off-normal conditions, and the gas expansion modules (GEMs).
The GEMs, which were added to the core after the Cycle 8A tests, were hollow assemblies filled
with gas and capped on top to prevent gas leakage. When the primary pumps were on, the core
inlet pressure compressed the gas level above the active fuel. When the primary pumps were off
and the core inlet pressure decreased, the gas expanded and the level dropped below the active fuel
region. Because they were loaded on the periphery of the core, the gas level dropping when the
primary pumps tripped led to increased radial neutron leakage from the core and a corresponding
negative reactivity insertion. Although many different phenomena affected the gas level within the
GEMs, the FFTF benchmark specification includes a simple formula to correlate gas level to the
core flow rate.

 69

 For each of the LOFWOS test benchmarks, a wide range of test data is available for comparing
against code simulations:
 • Total, fission, and decay heat power,
 • Net reactivity,
 • Mass flow rate in each of the three primary loops,
 • Outlet temperatures for two instrumented assemblies,
 • Primary loop hot leg temperatures,
 • Primary loop cold leg temperatures,
 • Secondary loop hot leg temperatures, and
 • Secondary loop cold leg temperatures.
 Several parameters were not directly measured during the tests. Net reactivity was calculated
during post-test evaluations based on the fission power measurements. Updated calculations of net
reactivity were made during Argonne’s benchmarking efforts utilizing modern neutronics data
libraries. Decay heat was calculated based on fission power measurements using the ANS decay
heat standard. Total power was calculated as the sum of measured fission power and calculated
decay heat.
8.3 Recommendations on Simulation Order for Benchmarking with FFTF Tests
 Because of the integral nature of these tests, predictions for the radial core expansion reactivity
feedback cannot be validated on their own; they must be validated along with predictions for other
reactivity feedbacks. However, how much of the FFTF system that must be modeled differs for
the Cycle 8A tests and the LOFWOS tests. This section presents a recommended approach for
performing benchmark simulations of the FFTF tests in support of code validation, particularly
geared towards validation of the radial expansion modeling approaches in codes like NUBOW-3D
and the novel MOOSE-based approach being developed under NEAMS.
 It is recommended to begin FFTF benchmarking with the Cycle 8A tests. For the most part,
only the core needs to be modeled for the Cycle 8A tests, as long as any reactivity changes due to
control rod driveline expansion, which develops as temperatures in the core outlet plenum evolve,
can be captured with a core only model.
 Neutronics parameters for modeling the Cycle 8C LOFWOS tests were calculated as part of the
IAEA CRP. This includes power distributions, reactivity feedback coefficients, and point kinetics
parameters. These parameters were calculated specifically for LOFWOS Test #13 but were
deemed be acceptable for modeling the other LOFWOS tests [21]. No neutronics calculations were
performed for Cycle 8A due to the unavailability of assembly compositions and burn-up for that
cycle. However, many of the Cycle 8A tests were simulated with SAS4A/SASSYS-1 using the
core model for Cycle 8C with good agreement between the simulation results and measured data

 70

[21]. The only change made to the Cycle 8C core model for modeling the Cycle 8A tests was
deactivating the GEM reactivity feedback.
 Because of the quantity of Cycle 8A tests, it is not necessary to model all 200+ tests. A sampling
of tests from each category is sufficient to identify modeling aspects that require improvement.
Ideally, the selected tests from each type would cover a range of initial conditions. Modeling only
tests with a high power-to-flow ratio, for example, could obscure the fact that the model does not
capture certain phenomena at low power-to-flow ratios.
 There is not a best order to approach modeling the Cycle 8A test types. Ideally, users would
model at least some of each test type before attempting to identify areas of potential modeling
improvement. If test types are modeled individually and improvements made before moving onto
another test type, users risk calibrating their models in a way that will make simulation predictions
worse for other test types, and it may be difficult to identify the causes of those poor predictions.
Rather, users should model some of each test type, compare their results against the measured data,
and then carefully assess how best to improve their model.
 The LOFWOS tests should be modeled after the Cycle 8A tests, either before or after any
modeling improvements are made based on comparisons of simulation predictions and measured
test data. If the Cycle 8A tests were modeled using a core-only model, models of the primary loops,
secondary loops, and gas expansion module reactivity feedback effect must be developed. Because
the four LOFWOS tests for which benchmarks are available are similar, there is no recommended
order for which test or tests to simulate first.

9 Conclusions
 We have presented several studies in this report related to advancing the high-fidelity
simulation of core bowing. First, we assessed and integrated the readiness of the reactor physics
code, Griffin, for inclusion in a multiphysics core-bowing workflow. Single- and seven-assembly
benchmarks based on the ABR-1000 design were used as test cases for coupling physics together.
Griffin’s detailed ring-heterogeneous model was utilized along with pin power reconstruction
capabilities. Griffin was successfully coupled to MOOSE SCM for a single assembly (and
demonstrated for seven assembly case although detailed results were not presented). Griffin was
also successfully coupled to MOOSE Solid Mechanics to take in deformation results and re-
compute reactivity feedback on both the single- and seven-assembly problems. Potential issues
were noted with lack of sodium backfill capability which precludes the use of homogenized
regions in the Griffin model (e.g. duct-heterogeneous model). Griffin’s pin power reconstruction
methodology may need further development to sample flux from the deformed mesh. The three-
way coupling methodology had shortcomings summarized at the end of the previous chapter that
center around an unexpected power distribution computed in Griffin post-deformation which needs
to be further investigated to determine whether the result is physical or a numerical artifact which
should be corrected.
 Standalone physics verification continued with the successful verification of MOOSE Solid
Mechanics module for IAEA VP5, which includes irradiation dose damage effects for creep and
swelling deformation. Additionally, a collaboration with JAEA resulted in code-to-code
verification for Griffin’s modeling of deformed configurations for three small core neutronics
benchmark cases.
 Finally, FFTF data has been summarized in the final chapter for future validation considerations
of core-bowing related phenomena. While the MOOSE-based capabilities are not mature enough
to embark on validation today, the identification of this data as well as continued collaborations
with the ART Fast Reactor Program will ensure that code capabilities are designed to leverage
these benchmarking exercises.
