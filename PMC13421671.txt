Controlled release of CD200 inhibits inflammatory macrophages and chondrocyte catabolism
Authors: Shreedevi Kumar, Folly M Patterson, Tiffany Conklin, Ssu-Chieh J Hsu, Vijaykumar Meli, Kiara Chan, Kyle D Allen, Wendy F Liu, Blanka Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the osteoarthritic joint, activated macrophages release pro-inflammatory factors that promote cartilage degeneration. The CD200:CD200R immune checkpoint pathway is a promising therapeutic target that inhibits classical macrophage activation. We hypothesized that the presentation of CD200 to macrophages would shift their cytokine profile from pro-inflammatory to pro-regenerative, thereby reducing chondrocyte catabolism. CD200 encapsulated in poly(lactide-co-glycolide) microparticles (MPs) were incubated with murine bone marrow-derived macrophages. CD200 MP treatment reduced protein and gene expression of pro-inflammatory mediators in M1 macrophages and increased protein and gene expression of anti-inflammatory mediators in M2 and M1/2 macrophages. Conditioned media from CD200 MP-treated M1 macrophages reduced the expression of catabolic enzyme genes in chondrocytes. This study is the first to demonstrate that delivery of CD200 alters the inflammatory cascade and paracrine signaling to chondrocytes. There is significant potential for the CD200:CD200R inhibitory signaling pathway to be leveraged as an intra-articular treatment of OA.Subject terms: Cell biology, Immunology

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a multifactorial degenerative joint disease characterized by synovial inflammation, articular cartilage degeneration, and subchondral bone thickening. While previously considered a non-inflammatory wear-and-tear disease of cartilage, chronic low-grade inflammation has since been accepted as a key mediator of OA pathogenesis1. Damaged joint tissues release damage-associated molecular patterns that activate the innate inflammatory response of joint-resident and systemic immune cells. In particular, macrophages that make up the intimal lining of the synovium are activated to a pro-inflammatory phenotype by the degradation products of cartilage via pattern recognition receptors such as toll-like receptors (TLRs)2. In turn, activated macrophages secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines, growth factors, and matrix-degrading enzymes, which further promote the breakdown of cartilage, exacerbating disease. Macrophages are a promising therapeutic target for modifying OA disease progression3.

Treatments aimed at curbing inflammatory mechanisms in the joint have been studied for the treatment of OA but have met numerous challenges when implemented in the clinic. The standard of care for OA includes non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and intra-articular injections of corticosteroids, which modestly improve pain and function4 but have undesirable systemic side effects5 or may worsen OA progression6,7. Newer therapeutics such as platelet-rich plasma, hyaluronic acid, and mesenchymal stem cell intra-articular injections lack sufficient high-quality evidence for efficacy and are recommended in limited scenarios5. Clinical studies of monoclonal antibodies against pro-inflammatory mediators such as lutikizumab (anti-interleukin 1α/β; IL-1α/β)8, tocilizumab (anti-IL-6)9, and etanercept (anti-tumor necrosis factor α; TNF-α)10 have demonstrated no or limited improvement in pain or joint structure compared to placebo. This suggests that single-cytokine inhibitors may not sufficiently modulate complex inflammatory responses in OA joints. As such, cell-specific immunomodulatory treatments may be more successful at shifting the cytokine profile in the joint from a pro-inflammatory to pro-regenerative milieu and consequently modifying OA progression.

The CD200:CD200R immune checkpoint pathway is a critical pathway in immune regulation that can be leveraged in modulating inflammation. CD200 is a transmembrane glycoprotein expressed by a broad array of cell types, while CD200R is primarily expressed by myeloid cells, particularly macrophages11. CD200 engagement with CD200R inhibits classical macrophage activation while promoting alternative activation12. CD200 is a checkpoint molecule exploited by hematologic and solid tumor cancer cells to inhibit immune activity, and samalizumab, a humanized anti-CD200 antibody, demonstrated reduced chronic lymphocytic leukemia tumor burden in a clinical trial13. Further, CD200 plays a critical role in resolution of neuroinflammation including from stroke14, spinal cord injury15, and ischemia16. In articular tissues, CD200 expression by tendon stem cells may regulate inflammation caused by rotator cuff tendon injury17, and CD200-expressing fibroblasts have been shown to help resolve inflammation caused by inflammatory arthritis18. Macrophages were also found to transfer mitochondria to dorsal root ganglion neurons innervating inflamed tissues in a CD200R-dependent mechanism19. The critical role that CD200 plays in resolving inflammation and inflammation-related pain suggests the CD200:CD200R pathway is a potential therapeutic target for OA.

The overall goal of this study was to develop a therapeutic strategy for the presentation of CD200 within OA joints. Specifically, we engineered a controlled release system for CD200 to modulate macrophage phenotype and protect cartilage. We hypothesized that the presentation of CD200 to macrophages would shift their cytokine profile from pro-inflammatory to pro-regenerative, thereby reducing chondrocyte catabolism. To test this hypothesis, a soluble form of the extracellular domain of CD200 was loaded into poly(lactide-co-glycolide) microparticles (PLGA MPs) such that the resulting delivery system was (1) in a non-phagocytosable size range of 10–30 µm, to mediate extracellular CD200 release within the synovium20, and (2) able to provide controlled released of CD200 over a sustained period of time (Fig. 1). We then evaluated the effects of CD200 delivery on macrophage functions and paracrine signaling to chondrocytes using in vitro systems. This study marks the first report of CD200 delivery in the context of macrophage-chondrocyte interactions, which may be leveraged for therapeutic strategies for OA.

Design of the CD200-loaded PLGA MPs.

CD200-loaded PLGA MPs are engineered to (1) be non-phagocytosable so that the particles would be trapped in the synovial matrix and released CD200 would bind with its receptor CD200R present on the surface of macrophages and (2) have a controlled release of CD200 over a sustained period of time.

Results

Formulation and characterization of MPs

Fluorescently tagged CD200 was successfully encapsulated within PLGA MPs (fluor-CD200 MPs; Fig. 2a). The fluor-CD200 MPs had a median diameter of 17 µm, with approximately 30% of the particles falling in the phagocytosable range of less than 10 µm, 40% falling in the desired engineering criteria range of 10–30 µm and 30% being greater than 30 µm (Fig. 2b). Blank MPs were also fabricated and used to create a standard curve for encapsulation (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Based on this standard curve, the encapsulation efficiency of the fluor-CD200 MPs was 86.1% ± 8.6%. The release profile showed a high initial burst, corresponding to 44.2% ± 3.0% (7.8 μg ± 0.5 μg from 25 mg of MPs) of the total amount of protein encapsulated after the first day of incubation in the release buffer, followed by a sustained release (Fig. 2c). Overall, 65.6% ± 4.6% (11.6 μg ± 0.8 μg) of the protein initially encapsulated was released from PLGA MPs after 47 days.

Cell viability in the presence of CD200 MPs and blank MPs

The CD200 and Blank MPs were cytocompatible with murine bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) in monolayer, as determined by the MTS assay for metabolic activity, at all concentrations of MPs used in the forthcoming studies (Fig. 3). This was the case whether the BMDMs were unstimulated in an “M0” state or if they were stimulated to an “M1” state with ultrapure lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and interferon γ (IFN-γ).

Functional activity of CD200 following encapsulation in MPs

Functional assays confirmed the CD200 remained bioactive upon encapsulation into and release from MPs. Specifically, we examined the response of classically and alternatively activated macrophages to CD200, CD200 MPs, and Blank MPs based on gene and protein expression for various M1 and M2 markers.

Classical stimulation of the BMDMs with 0.9 ng/mL LPS and 1.5 ng/mL IFN-γ lead to a 5,600% increase in TNF-α secretion compared to M0 Control as measured by an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA; 401.2 ± 25.2 vs. 7.0 ± 2.7 pg/mL; Fig. 4a). Free CD200 (260.2 ± 23.3 pg/mL, P < 0.001) as well as CD200 MPs (317.1 ± 34.0 pg/mL, P < 0.001) comparably decreased TNF-α secretion, suggesting the released CD200 is indeed biologically active. The Blank MPs increased TNF-α further compared to M1 Control (477.8 ± 53.7, P < 0.001), suggesting the PLGA may augment pro-inflammatory signaling in classically stimulated BMDMs. However, free CD200 (8.8 ± 3.0 pg/mL, P > 0.99), CD200 MPs (4.6 ± 1.1 pg/mL, P > 0.99), and Blank MPs (4.5 ± 0.6 pg/mL, P > 0.99) did not significantly alter TNF-α secretion in M0 BMDMs. Similarly, the gene expression of Tnfa by the classically activated macrophages was upregulated 9.2-fold from the M0 condition (Fig. 5a). Upon exposure to CD200 MPs, Tnfa gene expression by M1 BMDMs was downregulated compared to M1 Control (3.1-fold vs. 9.2-fold, P < 0.001) but there were no significant differences in expression when the M1 BMDMs were treated with the free CD200 (8.2-fold, P = 0.86) or Blank MPs (7.5-fold, P = 0.30; Fig. 5a). Free CD200 modestly increased Tnfa gene expression in M0 BMDMs (1.5-fold, P = 0.03).

Classical activation of the BMDMs led to a 5,800% increase in IL-6 secretion relative to the M0 condition (350.9 ± 56.7 vs. 6.0 ± 1.4 pg/mL; Fig. 4b). Free CD200 (67.4 ± 16.5 pg/mL, P < 0.001) and CD200 MPs (265.2 ± 38.8 pg/mL, P < 0.001) respectively decreased this stimulation. Blank MPs also induced a significant increase in IL-6 compared to M1 Control (641.3 ± 59.7 pg/mL, P < 0.001), again suggesting the PLGA alone may induce pro-inflammatory signaling in classically stimulated BMDMs. There was no change in IL-6 secretion in M0 BMDMs following treatment with free CD200 (11.3 ± 4.8 pg/mL, P = 0.99), CD200 MPs (6.2 ± 2.4 pg/mL, P > 0.99), or Blank MPs (5.6 ± 0.8 pg/mL, P > 0.99). Interestingly, the gene expression of Il6 by the BMDMs was decreased by 1.5-fold when stimulated to M1 condition (Fig. 5b), potentially because of the time point of evaluation (12 h post-stimulation). When exposed to the free CD200 or CD200 MPs, there was no change in Il6 gene expression when compared to M1 Control (-2.0-fold, P = 0.70 and -2.3-fold, P = 0.26, respectively). However, the Blank MPs significantly increased Il6 gene expression in M0 BMDMs relative to M0 Control (2.2-fold, P = 0.01) and in M1 BMDMs relative to M1 Control (1.3-fold, P = 0.03).

Stimulating BMDMs with LPS and IFN-γ led to a 20,500% increase in nitric oxide (NO) secretion as measured by the Griess assay (19.4 ± 1.1 vs. 0.09 ± 0.08 µM; Fig. 4c). Free CD200 (12.2 ± 0.9 µM, P < 0.001) and CD200 MPs (12.9 ± 0.5 µM, P < 0.001) both decreased this stimulation. The Blank MPs did not cause any change in NO production from the M1 Control (18.9 ± 0.4 µM, P = 0.38). The gene expression of Nos2 by the BMDMs was increased by 1,349-fold from the M0 condition when classically activated, while treatment with CD200 MPs slightly decreased Nos2 compared to M1 Control, though this difference was not statistically significant (638-fold, P = 0.08; Fig. 5c). Free CD200 and Blank MPs did not significantly alter Nos2 expression in M1 BMDMs (802-fold, P = 0.41 and 1198-fold, P = 0.97, respectively), while Nos2 expression decreased slightly in M0 BMDMs treated with free CD200 (-2.0-fold, P = 0.09) or CD200 MPs (-2.3-fold, P = 0.05).

When BMDMs were classically activated, there was a 43% increase in CC chemokine 2 (CCL2) secretion from BMDMs in M0 condition (3.4 ± 0.2 vs. 2.4 ± 0.2 ng/mL; Fig. 4d). However, the treatment of the M1 BMDMs with free CD200 (3.7 ± 0.1 ng/mL, P = 0.13), CD200 MPs (3.0 ± 0.4 ng/mL, P = 0.06), or Blank MPs (3.3 ± 0.2 ng/mL, P = 0.99) did not lead to any significant differences in protein expression of CCL2. Treating M0 BMDMs with free CD200 increased CCL2 secretion (3.1 ± 0.2 ng/mL, P < 0.001) but treatment with CD200 MPs decreased CCL2 secretion (1.9 ± 0.3 ng/mL, P = 0.01). On the other hand, the gene expression of Ccl2 was downregulated when classically activated BMDMs were treated with the CD200 MPs (1.2-fold vs. 4.8-fold, P < 0.001) or with free CD200 (2.6-fold vs. 4.8-fold, P < 0.001), while there was no significant difference in expression when treated with Blank MPs (3.8-fold, P = 0.13; Fig. 5d). Ccl2 expression was decreased in M0 BMDMs treated with CD200 MPs (-2.3-fold, P < 0.001) or Blank MPs (-1.5-fold, P = 0.004). The gene expression of other pro-inflammatory mediators including Tlr4, Il1b, Ccl3, and Ccl7 did not change in response to treatment in most groups (Supplementary Fig. 1).

When BMDMs were alternatively activated with IL-4 and IL-13 (“M2”), there was an 11.4-fold increase in arginase 1 (Arg1) gene expression compared to the M0 Control (Fig. 6a). Treatment of M2 BMDMs with free CD200 and CD200 MP further increased Arg1 expression, by 124-fold (P < 0.0001) and 927-fold (P < 0.001), respectively (Fig. 6a). There was a slight decrease in Arg1 expression following treatment with Blank MPs in M2 BMDMs (7.2-fold, P = 0.06). Activation with LPS, IL-4, and IL-13 (“M1/2”) increased Arg1 by 14.1-fold. Treatment with CD200 MPs (51.8-fold, P < 0.001) or Blank MPs (37.3-fold, P < 0.001), increased Arg1 expression compared to M1/2 Control, while treatment with free CD200 (8.0-fold, P = 0.02) decreased Arg1 expression. M1 stimulation decreased Arg1 expression by 10.1-fold, and Arg1 was further decreased by treatment with Blank MPs (18.3-fold, P = 0.01) M1/2 stimulation induced a 94% increase in IL-10 secretion from BMDMs compared to M0 Control (127.3 ± 96.0 vs. 64.3 ± 33.7 pg/mL; Fig. 6b). Treatment of M1/2 BMDMs with CD200 MPs further increased IL-10 secretion compared to M1/2 Control (321.8 ± 57.8 pg/mL, P < 0.001). Treatment of M1 and M2 BMDMs with free CD200, CD200 MPs, or Blank MPs did not significantly affect IL-10 concentration compared to their respective control groups (P > 0.76 for all).

Paracrine effects on chondrocytes

To evaluate the paracrine effects of macrophages on chondrocytes, conditioned media studies were performed. Chondrocytes exposed to macrophage-conditioned medium (MCM) from the M1-stimulated BMDMs experienced increases in expression of the catabolic enzymes, matrix metalloproteinase 3 (Mmp3; 38.2-fold; Fig. 7a), 13 (Mmp13; 3.3-fold; Fig. 7b), a family of multidomain extracellular protease enzyme 4 (Adamts4; 1.3-fold; Fig. 7c), and 5 (Adamts5; 3.4-fold; Fig. 7d), confirming that pro-inflammatory macrophages promote cartilage catabolism. Interestingly, when chondrocytes were treated with MCM from M1-stimulated BMDMs that were treated with CD200 MPs, Mmp3 (-6.8-fold, P < 0.001), Mmp13 (-5.9-fold, P < 0.001), Adamts4 (-5.2-fold, P < 0.001), and Adamts5 (-3.0-fold, P < 0.001) were downregulated compared to untreated M1 MCM. Free CD200 MCM decreased Mmp3 (17.2-fold, P < 0.001) and Mmp13 (1.5-fold, P = 0.01) expression, but not Adamts4 (-1.4-fold, P = 0.14) or Adamts5 (3.2-fold, P = 0.97). MCM from M1 BMDMs treated with Blank MPs, however, also significantly decreased Mmp3 (14.6-fold, P < 0.001), Mmp13 (-1.5-fold, P < 0.001), Adamts4 (-2.7-fold, P < 0.001), and Adamts5 (-3.0-fold, P < 0.001) in chondrocytes, in contrast to increased TNF-a and IL-6 secretion measured in the BMDM media (Fig. 4a, b). In chondrocytes treated with free CD200-treated M0 MCM, Mmp3 (11.7-fold, P < 0.001), Mmp13 (3.9-fold, P < 0.001), Adamts4 (2.0-fold, P = 0.04), and Adamts5 (3.5-fold, P < 0.001) expression was increased. This trend was repeated in chondrocytes treated with MCM from CD200 MP-treated BMDMs for Mmp3 (1.6-fold, P = 0.01), Adamts4 (2.0-fold, P = 0.04), and Adamts5 (2.8-fold, P < 0.001), but not Mmp13 (1.5-fold, P = 0.26), though these fold-changes were of a smaller magnitude than those for free CD200-treated M0 MCM. Adamts5 expression was also increased in chondrocytes treated with MCM from Blank MP-treated M0 BMDMs (1.8-fold, P = 0.03) compared to controls. However, Mmp3 (-1.3-fold, P = 0.28), Mmp13 (-1.2-fold, P = 0.83), or Adamts4 (1.3-fold, P = 0.59) exhibited no significant changes. Chondrocytes treated with microparticle-conditioned media without BMDMs showed a 1.7-fold increase in Mmp3 expression (P = 0.04), but no significant change in Mmp13, Adamts4, or Adamts5 (Supplementary Fig. 2; P = 0.11, P = 0.50, and P = 0.74, respectively).

Discussion

The current work highlights a promising immunomodulatory strategy, CD200 encapsulated microparticles, designed for delivery to macrophages in osteoarthritic synovium. The efficacy of intra-articular injection of therapeutic proteins for the treatment of OA is often decreased due to rapid clearance from the joint space and loss of bioactivity21. This issue is currently addressed by frequent injections of high doses of therapeutic proteins, which is associated with unwanted side effects and patient morbidity22. Formulating therapeutic proteins in an injectable drug depot enabling controlled release over a prolonged period of time may address this problem. With this in mind, we aimed not only to test the therapeutic potential of CD200, but to incorporate it into a controlled release system designed for in vivo applications. A key challenge, however, is many therapeutic proteins rapidly lose bioactivity when formulated in controlled release systems23. For example, Anakinra, a modified version of naturally occurring IL-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1RA) designed to counteract inflammation, is a potential disease-modifying osteoarthritis drug (DMOAD)24. However, upon intra-articular injection, it shows a short half-life of approximately 4–6 h and when incorporated within slow-release systems, it loses bioactivity23. In this study, following encapsulation using the water-in-oil-in-water double emulsion-solvent evaporation method, the soluble form of the extracellular domain of CD200 was successfully incorporated into PLGA MPs for controlled release over 47 days. Bioactivity of the released protein was confirmed after 24 h in cell culture, which corresponds to 44% of the loaded protein. Though further work is needed to determine the dosing and duration of CD200 presentation for the treatment of OA, the findings presented here provide a foundation for further optimization of particle size and release kinetics needed for therapeutic CD200 delivery upon intra-articular injection in vivo.

CD200 is widely expressed by cells throughout the body, including in cells in joint tissues which include synovium, articular cartilage, meniscus, tendon, ligament, and bone. More recently, a population of pro-resolving CD200+ fibroblasts in RA synovium was identified, and CD200 expression in human synovium was inversely correlated with RA severity18. Differing numbers of CD200+ cells in RA synovium were also found compared to healthy synovium, though the direction of change depended on the cell population25. On the other hand, Ren et al. noted increased CD200 and CD200R1 expression in OA and RA synovium compared to healthy but did not identify which cell population accounted for these differences26. CD200 is expressed in prehypertrophic and hypertrophic growth plate chondrocytes27,28, but the expression and significance of CD200 in hypertrophic OA articular chondrocytes is unclear, as they originate from a different lineage than growth plate chondrocytes29. To our knowledge, CD200 or CD200R expression has not been quantified in the meniscus, which is composed of fibrocartilage. CD200 plays a role in bone homeostasis, which has implications for therapeutic CD200 delivery in OA30–33. Our overall understanding of CD200:CD200R interactions in both the synovium and other disease contexts supports the rationale for its therapeutic exploration. Given that there is much that is unknown about these interactions in the joint, future studies would need to evaluate bony changes following treatment.

To test the functional activity of the CD200 following encapsulation, murine monocytes derived from bone marrow were differentiated towards a macrophage phenotype using M-CSF and were further polarized towards M1-like or M2-like phenotypes. The CD200 MPs demonstrated the ability to decrease pro-inflammatory mediator production, specifically for TNF-α (Fig. 4a), IL-6 (Fig. 4b), and NO (Fig. 4c) by classically activated BMDMs. The CD200 MPs also led to decreased gene expression in Tnfa (Fig. 5a), but in other cases such as Il6 (Fig. 5b) and Nos2 (Fig. 5c), and Ccl2 (Fig. 5d) there was a discordance in the protein secretion and gene expression. This could be attributed to differences in timing of the protein and gene expressions given the media for ELISAs and RNA for RT-PCR were collected from the same wells. While the media being tested for cytokine secretion has the accumulation of the inflammatory proteins being secreted over the incubation period, gene expression is a snapshot in time at the point in which the cells are lysed. Further investigation into the time course of gene expression will be needed to determine the longitudinal changes in the gene expression levels of the various mediators.

The recombinant extracellular domain of CD200 used here was previously determined to functionally bind and engage with the CD200 receptor34. As a membrane-bound protein, the extracellular domain of CD200 is the ligand for CD200R and is responsible for subsequent immune inhibitory signaling35. In these experiments, a soluble form of CD200 consisting of the extracellular domain was used. Prior work demonstrates that soluble CD200, produced by proteolytic cleavage of the extracellular domain, is functionally active and binds to CD200R36,37. Studies of therapeutic CD200 delivery have largely used a recombinant CD200-Fc fusion protein using the extracellular domain of CD200, including for inflammatory arthritis models18,38,39. Membrane-bound and soluble CD200 both play important roles in signaling and cell function, though comparisons between the two signaling pathways are sparse40, much less how they function in OA. Together, the extent of the data supporting the efficacy of soluble CD200 in modulating immune responses motivated its use in the present experiments as a potential OA therapeutic. Our findings further add evidence that soluble CD200 may be efficacious for OA through macrophage-chondrocyte paracrine interactions.

PLGA has favorable characteristics for use in injectable MPs: easy synthesis, biocompatible degradation products (lactic acid and glycolic acid), and low toxicity41. There are multiple PLGA MP-based therapeutics approved by the FDA, including Zilretta, triamcinolone acetonide encapsulated in PLGA for intra-articular OA treatment42. Though CD200-loaded MPs demonstrated notable anti-inflammatory effects, the Blank MPs appeared to have some biological effects themselves. Blank MPs demonstrated stimulation of TNF-α (Fig. 4a) and IL-6 (Fig. 4b) from classically activated macrophages, suggesting potential exacerbation of immune activation in the presence of LPS and IFN-γ. In M0 BMDMs, the gene expression of Il6 increased (Fig. 5b) and that of Ccl2 decreased (Fig. 5d) with Blank MPs, suggesting a combination of pro- and anti-inflammatory effects. There have been contrasting reports on the immunogenicity of PLGA in the literature. On one hand, some reports suggest PLGA MPs have no apparent immunostimulatory properties and may even be immunosuppressive43,44. In fact, the immunomodulatory properties of PLGA are associated with the physicochemical characteristics of the polymer itself, particularly the molecular weight45. On the other hand, some reports, performed at shorter time periods, show increased IL-1β production through activation of the NALP3 inflammasome, suggesting that PLGA has intrinsic immunogenic properties which may be counterproductive to the goal of mitigating inflammation46. One study reported that macrophages incubated with PLGA can exhibit upregulation in both M1 subtype related surface markers and pro-inflammatory cytokines over the time course of the degradation process of PLGA, indicating the acidic degradation products of PLGA can convert macrophages to the pro-inflammatory polarization state47. In our study, the particles were incubated with the macrophages for 24 h in buffered media, during which time the extent of PLGA degradation, and any associated pH changes, would be minimal48. It is conceivable that other components of the MPs used in the formulation process may be released in this initial period and contribute to immune activation. Further investigation into the source and time course of this response is warranted to ensure the delivery vehicle does not undermine the immunomodulatory effects of CD200 long-term. Here, in the case of CD200 MPs, the effects of the released CD200 overcame any exacerbation of inflammatory signaling attributed to the PLGA MPs, leading to an overall reduced secretion of TNF-α and IL-6.

In addition to reducing pro-inflammatory signaling in M1 macrophages, CD200 enhanced aspects of pro-regenerative, M2-like signaling. This is consistent with previous studies where CD200 tethered to the surface of PLGA MPs and PLGA films promoted an M2 phenotype, thereby reducing the foreign body response to PLGA upon implantation34. In this study, M2 and an M1/2 controls were included in this experimental setup to enable comparisons of gene expression by M1 BMDMs when incubated with CD200 MPs to the pro-regenerative M2 phenotype. While there was no change in Arg1 downregulation in M1 BMDMs treated with CD200 MPs, Arg1 was upregulated in CD200 MP-treated M2 and M1/2 macrophages (Fig. 6a). Interestingly, M1/2 stimulation was required to stimulate IL-10 production, following CD200 MP treatment (Fig. 6b). Together, this suggests that CD200, particularly when encapsulated in PLGA MPs, promotes an anti-inflammatory macrophage profile.

The change in cytokine secretion by pro-inflammatory macrophages induced by CD200 MPs demonstrated potential chondroprotective effects. Specifically, chondrocytes downregulated expression of catabolic enzymes when incubated with MCM from M1 macrophages treated with CD200 MPs (Fig. 6). During the initiation and progression of OA, there is often a stimulation of catabolic enzymes such as MMPs and ADAMTS, which degrade the ECM of cartilage, leading to the release of ECM components such as glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) from cartilage. MMP-13 in particular is considered a major catabolic effector in OA due to its ability to cleave type 2 collagen in articular cartilage, leading to GAG loss as well as collagen degradation49. Interestingly, while treatment of M1 macrophages by CD200 MP led to decreased expression of Mmp3, Mmp13, Adamts4, and Adamts5 in chondrocytes, soluble CD200 treatment was only associated with a decrease in Mmp3 and Mmp13. Additionally, treatment of M1 macrophages with Blank MPs led to decreases in expression of all catabolic enzymes in chondrocytes. MCM from M0 macrophages treated with free CD200, but not CD200 MPs or Blank MPs, likewise increased catabolic enzyme expression in chondrocytes. These findings could be attributed to differential response in the secretome of the macrophages due to the sustained release of CD200 instead of bolus release that produced favorable responses in chondrocytes despite increases in TNF-α (Fig. 4a) and IL-6 (Fig. 4b). This also raised the question of whether or not released PLGA degradation components carried over in the MCM may have had direct effects on the chondrocytes independent of BMDM paracrine signaling. To assess this, chondrocytes were treated with media from Blank MPs incubated in transwells without macrophages. There was a slight increase in Mmp3 expression, but no change in Mmp13, Adamts4, or Adamts5 (Figure. S2) suggesting only a minimal effect of the MPs alone on chondrocytes.

Several limitations of these experiments should be noted. Primary murine BMDMs are a convenient and abundant cell source; however, they may not fully represent the synovial macrophage populations in OA joints. While macrophage phenotype is more complex than a dichotomy between M1 and M2, the focus of this study was on the modulation of secreted factors in BMDMs in vitro using stimulation conditions that mimic inflammatory (M1), healing (M2), and mixed (M1/2) environments. Future studies will consider a broader spectrum of macrophage phenotypes. Despite these limitations, the paracrine interaction between the classically activated BMDMs and chondrocytes observed here was consistent with those observed in the OA joint. As such, these studies provide an indication of the effects of CD200 on inflammatory mechanisms relevant to OA that provide a basis for further study in more advanced in vitro and in vivo models including more in-depth characterization of macrophage phenotype.

In conclusion, this study presents a promising immunomodulatory approach to treating osteoarthritis. CD200 MPs were engineered to be in a non-phagocytosable size range with a sustained release profile of the encapsulated protein within. The results indicate that the CD200 MPs have the potential to reduce the secretion of inflammatory mediators from macrophages with a resultant improvement in chondroprotection of articular chondrocytes. Future studies will test the MP system in an OA animal model wherein the impact of CD200 on the immune profile of the joint can be evaluated. This study marks the first time the delivery of CD200 to alter the inflammatory cascade and paracrine signaling to chondrocytes has been investigated. Given the promising results from the in vitro work, there is significant potential for the CD200:CD200R inhibitory signaling pathway to be leveraged as an intra-articular treatment of OA.

Methods

CD200 MP formulation

PLGA MPs were prepared using a water-in-oil-in-water double emulsion. The soluble form of the extracellular domain of mouse CD200 protein was produced recombinantly using Chinese Hamster Ovary cells as previously reported50. An aqueous solution (W1) of 0.1% CD200 was obtained by diluting 200 µg of CD200 in 200 µL of phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and a control solution (W2) comprised of 200 µL PBS alone. The oil solution (O) of 10% PLGA was obtained by adding 200 mg PLGA (Resomer RG 504 H, acid terminated, lactide:glycolide 50:50, Mw 38,000–54,000; Sigma Aldrich) to 2 mL dimethyl carbonate (Sigma Aldrich) and shaking at room temperature until the solution appeared homogenous, about 2 h. Either W1 or W2 was added to O and vortexed at the highest speed for 15 s to obtain the primary emulsion. 3 mL of 2.5% polyvinyl alcohol (PVA; Mw 30,000–70,000, 87–90% hydrolyzed; Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS was added and the mixture was vortexed at the highest speed for 30 s to obtain the secondary emulsion. Using a positive displacement pipette, the secondary emulsion was added 1 mL at a time to 47 mL of 1% PVA being stirred at 600 RPM. The resulting mixture was covered loosely with aluminum foil and stirred (600 RPM) for 4–6 h at room temperature to evaporate the solvent. The mixture was then centrifuged at 4000 RPM for 20 min; the supernatant was removed, and the MPs were resuspended in PBS and washed two more times. Following the last wash, the supernatant was removed, and the MPs were lyophilized for 48 h and stored at –80 °C until required. The formulation of MPs that encapsulated W1 or W2 will herein be referred to as “CD200 MPs” and “Blank MPs,” respectively.

Characterization of MP size

Particle size is an important parameter for macrophage targeting. Particles smaller than 10 µm are readily phagocytosed by macrophages in the synovium51–53 while particles 10–30 µm in diameter remain trapped in synovial tissue20. Since CD200 binds to CD200R on the macrophage surface, phagocytosis is not desired. The molecular weight and percentage of PLGA used in the formulation process was varied to assess conditions needed to achieve MPs in a non-phagocytosable range that would be trapped in the synovial matrix (10–30 µm).The average size of the MPs was evaluated using an inverted optical EVOS microscope. Images taken were analyzed using ImageJ 1.51j854. The diameter of each particle was determined by using the software to manually trace the particles and histograms were prepared for each formulation.

Determination of encapsulation efficiency

A micro bicinchoninic acid (µBCA) assay was used for to determine CD200 loading efficiency within PLGA MPs. Standards for this assay were prepared using free CD200 added to blank PLGA MPs formulated with the same method as CD200-loaded PLGA MPs, to account for interference from the components of the MP process55. When each CD200 MP batch was prepared, a batch of Blank MPs was also formulated. To determine encapsulation efficiency, 1 mL of 0.5 N sodium hydroxide (NaOH; Fisher Scientific) was added to 5 mg of each particle formulation. To the Blank MPs formulation only, 5 µg of CD200 was added within the mixture of NaOH and particles to have a mixture that contains 100% loading of CD200. Both the CD200 MP and Blank MP formulations were mixed using a bath sonicator for 15 min. The pH of each formulation was adjusted to 7.0. An encapsulation standard curve for each formulation was created by diluting the Blank MPs with PBS to get 80%, 60%, 40% and 20% loading of CD200. The absorbance of both the encapsulation standard curve and the digested CD200 MPs was determined using the µBCA assay. Based on the standard curve from free CD200 addition to Blank MPs (Fig. S3a), the encapsulation efficiency of the fluor-CD200 MPs was determined.

Characterization of CD200 release profile

CD200 protein was tagged with a fluorescent marker prior to encapsulation within PLGA MPs (fluor-CD200). Briefly, Alexa Fluor 488 NHS Ester (Succinimidyl Ester; Thermo Fisher) was added to CD200 in a 10:1 molar ratio in a 10% sodium bicarbonate buffer and incubated at room temperature on a gyromini for 3 h before being washed once in an Amicon™ Ultra-15 Centrifugal Filter Unit with a 10,000 MWCO (EMD Millipore). Fluor-CD200 MPs and Blank MPs were fabricated. To measure the release profile of fluor-CD200, a triplicate of 25 mg of either the fluor-CD200 MPs or Blank MPs were weighed directly into amber tubes. 1.2 mL of PBS was added directly into each tube, vortexed at the highest speed for 10 s to break up the pellet of particles and then placed in a 37 °C shaker. At least 15 replicates of standard curves of the fluor-CD200 were created at the same time and also incubated in a 37 °C shaker. At each timepoint over a 47-day period, the tubes containing the particles, and one set of standard curve tubes were removed from the shaker. Then, 800 µL of PBS was removed carefully from the tubes containing particles without disturbing the pellet of particles and plated in a black 96-well plate. The fluorescence from release buffer removed from each particle type and from the standard curve tubes was read on a spectrophotometer at the same time at excitation 485 nm, emission 528 nm (Supplementary Fig. 3b and 3c). 800 µL of fresh PBS was added back into the tubes containing the particles, ensuring to break up the pellet by vortexing each tube at the highest speed for 10 s before placing back in 37°C shaker. The fluorescence of the release buffer for the particles was converted to mass of CD200 using the standard curve (Supplementary Fig. 3d). The percentage of encapsulated CD200 released at each timepoint was determined using the encapsulation efficiency for each particle type measured by the µBCA assay.

Cell isolation and culture

All animal procedures were approved by the University of Florida Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (protocol #202111442). BMDMs were harvested from the femurs of 9–12-week-old C57BL/6 female mice (Charles River Laboratories). Animals were allowed to acclimate for at least three days after arrival at the facility and were provided food and water ad libitum prior to euthanasia. Mice were euthanized by carbon dioxide asphyxiation followed by cervical dislocation. To isolate and differentiate BMDMs, bone marrow was flushed from the femurs and the cells were treated with ACK lysis buffer (Invitrogen), centrifuged, and resuspended in D-10 media, consisting of Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM; Corning) supplemented with 4.5 g/L glucose, 2 mM L-glutamine, 110 mg/L sodium pyruvate, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, 10% heat-inactivated FBS, and 25 ng/mL recombinant mouse macrophage colony stimulating factor (M-CSF; BioLegend). BMDMs were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 with addition of fresh media and M-CSF on day 3. BMDMs were dissociated using TrypLE Select Enzyme (1X) (Invitrogen) on day 7, and either immediately seeded on the tissue culture plates in fresh D-10 culture media supplemented with M-CSF or frozen and stored in the vapors of liquid nitrogen. Only cells of P0 were utilized in the experiments. BMDMs from one donor were used to assess cell viability while BMDMs from 2 to 3 donors were pooled for subsequent experiments.

Different stimulation conditions were used on the BMDMs to obtain M1, M2, and M1/2 macrophages. For the “no stimulation” M0 condition, 50 µL of D-10 media containing just M-CSF was added. For the M1 condition, ultrapure LPS (InvivoGen) and mouse IFN-γ (R&D Systems) were added in 50 µL to get a final concentration of 0.9 ng/mL LPS and 1.5 ng/mL IFN-γ. For the M2 condition, mouse IL-4 (BioLegend) and mouse IL-13 (BioLegend) were added in 50 µL to get a final concentration of 0.1 ng/mL IL-4 and 0.1 ng/mL IL-13. For the M1/2 condition, ultrapure LPS, mouse IL-4, and mouse IL-13 were added in 50 µL to get a final concentration of 0.9 ng/mL LPS, 0.1 ng/mL IL-4, and 0.1 ng/mL IL-13. IFN-γ was excluded from the M1/2 condition because it attenuates IL-10 and Arg1 pathways56,57.

Our studies used an ultrapure version of LPS derived from E. coli 0111:B4 to stimulate BMDMs to the M1 phenotype. LPS is the principal component of Gram-negative bacteria that activates the innate immune system and is recognized by TLR4 which interacts with three different extracellular proteins: LPS binding protein, CD14, and myeloid differentiation protein 258. Most LPS preparations on the market are contaminated by other bacterial components, such as lipoproteins, thus activating TLR2 as well as TLR4 signaling. However, ultrapure LPS is extracted by successive enzymatic hydrolysis steps and purified by a phenol-triethylamine-deoxycholate extraction protocol which removes contaminating lipoproteins, and therefore it only activates TLR459. Given the importance of TLR4 signaling in the pathogenesis of OA58, the ultrapure LPS enabled us to simulate the most relevant inflammatory signals with the macrophages in vitro.

Primary murine chondrocytes were isolated from 5–7 day old C57BL/6 mice pups following cryo-euthanasia and decapitation. Chondrocytes were obtained by overnight digestion of the femoral heads in 0.2% collagenase II (Worthington Biochemical Corporation) in chondrocyte media containing DMEM supplemented with 4.5 g/L glucose, 2 mM L-glutamine, 110 mg/L sodium pyruvate, and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 with shaking at 50 RPM. Following digestion, the cell mixture was passed through a 40 µm cell strainer, centrifuged, and either immediately seeded on tissue culture plates in fresh chondrocyte media with 10% heat-inactivated FBS or frozen and stored in liquid nitrogen vapor. Only chondrocytes of P0 and P1 were utilized in the experiments. Isolated chondrocytes consisted of a mixed population of articular and growth plate chondrocytes which develop from different progenitors and have distinct phenotypes29. Chondrocytes from 10 to 30 donors were pooled for experiments.

Characterization of cell viability in presence of CD200 MPs and blank MPs

To simulate the interaction of the macrophages with the MPs, a transwell system with permeable polyester membrane inserts was used wherein MPs were sequestered within the transwell inserts and the BMDMs from a single murine donor were seeded in the wells beneath. With a pore size of 0.4 µm, the transwells ensured transport of CD200 released ( ~ 45 kDa = 2.3 nm)60 from the MPs and not the MPs (10–30 µm) themselves. The cytotoxicity of the CD200 and Blank MPs to BMDMs was determined using an MTS assay (CellTiter 96 AQueous One Solution Cell Proliferation Assay; Promega). BMDMs were seeded on 24-well tissue culture plates at 93,750 cells/cm2 with 0.6 mL of D-10 media. After overnight attachment, a transwell containing different concentrations of MPs of each formulation in 150 µL of D-10 media was added to each well for 24 h. Following this incubation, the M0 and M1 stimuli were added directly to the wells for 12 h. At this point, the transwells were removed, and the cells were washed with PBS once before fresh media was added to the wells for incubation for 48 h. The media was refreshed in each well 30 min prior to adding the MTS reagent. In each well, 120 µL of MTS reagent was added to 600 µL of D-10 media, including control wells that did not contain any cells. Three hours after addition of the MTS reagent, the media with the MTS reagent was transferred to a new plate and absorption at 490 nm was measured using a spectrophotometer (Biotek Synergy HT).

Functional activity of CD200 following encapsulation

BMDMs were seeded on 24-well tissue culture plates at 93,750 cells/cm2 with 600 µL of D-10 media. After overnight attachment, a transwell containing MPs of each formulation in 150 µL of D-10 media was added to each well. Based on previous batches of particles, 85% encapsulation efficiency and 45% release of CD200 within 24 h were assumed. Therefore, to get 6 µg of CD200 released into each well (1 µg/30,000 cells, consistent with previous studies) from the loaded MPs within 24 h, 15.6 mg MPs was added to each transwell. 6 µg of free soluble CD200 was added in 150 µL volumes within transwells to control wells.

When delving into M1 expression markers, after 24 h of incubation of particles with the cells, the M0 and M1 stimuli were added directly to the wells for 12 h. After 12 h of stimulation, the supernatants were collected while the quantitative gene expression in the stimulated BMDMs was measured. When measuring M2 expression markers, after 24 h of incubation of particles with the cells, the M0, M1, M2, and M1/2 stimuli were added directly to the wells for 18 h for gene expression or 24 h for protein expression.

Protein expression using ELISAs

The supernatants were analyzed for TNF-α, IL-10, IL-6, and CCL2 secretion by ELISA following the manufacturer’s instructions (BioLegend). NO concentrations in the media were evaluated by using a Griess Reagent kit (ThermoFisher Scientific) and absorption at 528 nm was assessed by spectrophotometer. Absorbance was converted to concentration using a standard curve.

Macrophage-conditioned media (MCM) studies

To determine the effects of CD200 delivery to macrophages on chondrocytes, conditioned media studies were performed. Articular chondrocytes were seeded on 12-well tissue culture plates at 26,315 cells/cm2 with 1 mL of chondrocyte media. Concurrently, macrophages were pretreated with free CD200, CD200 MPs, or Blank MPs in transwells for 24 h and then stimulated to M0 or M1 conditions as described above for 12 h. Following macrophage polarization, supernatants were collected, centrifuged at 1000 RPM for 5 min, and diluted with chondrocyte media at a ratio of 3:1 (MCM:chondrocyte media). Following overnight adherence of the articular chondrocyte to the wells, the media from the articular chondrocytes was replaced with the diluted MCM for 48 h before RNA was extracted from the cells.

To determine if MPs themselves had a direct effect on chondrocytes, chondrocytes were seeded and allowed to adhere as described above. 20.5 mg of Blank MPs were incubated in transwells without BMDMs in D-10 containing 25 ng/mL of M-CSF for 36 h. D-10 and M-CSF without Blank MPs was used as a control. Following incubation, the supernatant was collected, centrifuged, and diluted 3:1 with chondrocyte media. The media from the chondrocytes was replaced with diluted microparticle-conditioned media for 48 h, followed by RNA extraction.

RT-PCR from macrophages and chondrocytes

Quantitative gene expression was conducted in both the stimulated BMDMs and the articular chondrocytes exposed to macrophage- or microparticle-conditioned media. Total RNA was extracted from the cells using RNEasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA samples were reverse transcribed into cDNA according to the manufacturer’s instructions (iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit, Bio-Rad). The samples were held at 25 °C for 5 min, 46 °C for 20 min, and 95 °C for 1 min. Gene expression levels were quantified by Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 6 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Life Technologies). The assay was performed using the Fast SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Life Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The sequences for the primers (Eurofins Genomics) used are listed in Supplementary Table 1. After an initial hold stage at 95 °C for 2 min, cDNA was amplified for 45 cycles at 95 °C for 5 s and 60°C for 30 s, followed by the melt curve stage at 65 °C for 2 min, 65 °C for 5 s, and 95 °C for 15 s. Relative expression levels of target genes were determined using the comparative CT method. For each replicate, target gene expression was normalized to murine Gapdh and calculated by 2-ΔΔCt. M0 Control BMDMs were used for baseline gene expression for the macrophages while murine chondrocytes incubated with macrophage-conditioned media from M0 Control BMDMs were used for baseline gene expression for the chondrocytes. For microparticle-conditioned media-treated chondrocytes, chondrocytes treated with plain D-10 and M-CSF were used for baseline gene expression.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted on GraphPad Prism version 9.4 whereby error bars indicate standard deviation. Normality was assessed with Shapiro-Wilk’s test. Two-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s correction was used to compare groups within stimulation condition for the ELISAs. In the PCR experiments, statistical analysis was performed on the ΔCt values61. The BMDM PCR and MCM-treated chondrocyte PCR were respectively compared using two-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s correction to compare groups within stimulation condition. The microparticle-conditioned media chondrocyte PCR was compared using Welch’s t test. Statistical significance was determined as P < 0.05.
