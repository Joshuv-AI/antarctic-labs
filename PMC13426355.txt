Safety and Efficacy of Oliceridine Combined with Propofol for Painless Gastroscopy: A Single-Center, Randomized, Parallel Controlled Clinical Trial
Authors: Xuping Hu, Chunyuan Yang, Jun Li, Fuping Wang, Hongjiang You, Changchun Chen, Leyu Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo explore the safety and efficacy of the novel G protein-biased μ-opioid receptor agonist oliceridine for painless gastroscopy.MethodsThis study is a single-center randomized controlled clinical trial. One hundred and eighty-eight patients scheduled for painless gastroscopy were randomly assigned to four groups: Group L (oliceridine 10 μg/kg + propofol 2mg/kg), Group M (oliceridine 20 μg/kg + propofol 2mg/kg), Group H (oliceridine 30 μg/kg + propofol 2mg/kg) and Group C (sufentanil 0.1 μg/kg + propofol 2mg/kg). The primary outcome was the incidence of respiratory depression. Secondary outcomes included the incidence of respiratory arrest, bradypnea (respiratory rate<8 bpm) and hypoxemia (pulse oxygen saturation (SpO2) < 90%). Other outcomes included procedure and sedation-related outcomes, perioperative vital signs and adverse effects were recorded.ResultsAmong the 184 patients, 46, 46, 45 and 47 patients were randomly assigned to the L, M, H and C groups, respectively, and completed the trial. The incidence of respiratory depression in Group C (55.32%) and Group H (46.67%) was significantly higher than that in Group L (15.22%) and Group M (17.39%) (P<0.05). The incidence of respiratory arrest was higher in group H (42.22%) and group C (51.06%) compared to group L (13.04%) and group M (15.22%) (P < 0.05). The incidence of bradypnea and hypoxemia in group C (31.90% and 14.89%) was higher than that in group M (6.5% and 0) (P < 0.05). The sedation success rate was significantly lower in Group L than in Group M (71.74% vs 93.48%; P < 0.05). No significant differences were observed in the incidence of postoperative adverse effects.Conclusion10 μg/kg and 20 μg/kg oliceridine is associated with a reduced risk of respiratory depression when co-administered with propofol for painless gastroscopy in adults, and the 20 μg/kg oliceridine exhibits superior sedative efficacy.

Introduction

Gastroscopy is currently a key diagnostic and therapeutic modality for gastrointestinal tract disorders. However, during the procedure, patients often experience significant throat discomfort, nausea, vomiting, choking, and cardiovascular reactions, which may lead to anxiety and reluctance to undergo the examination.1 Painless gastroscopy is characterized by ease of operation, rapid onset of action, and effective sedative and analgesic properties, contributing to its increasing clinical adoption. The procedure itself is brief in duration, typically lasting between 2 and 10 minutes.2 Ideal anesthetic agents for painless gastroscopy should exhibit rapid onset, reliable sedation and analgesia, quick recovery, and a favorable safety profile with few adverse effects.3

Currently, guideline-recommended sedation protocols for painless gastroscopy predominantly consist of propofol combined with fentanyl or sufentanil.4 Although this anesthesia regimen is capable of providing rapid and precise sedation, both propofol and opioids have the potential to cause respiratory depression. The incidence of hypoxemia during painless gastroscopy ranges from 1.8% to 69.0%.5 The combined adverse effects of these drugs may result in serious consequences for the patient. To date, anesthesiologists have been continuously exploring new drug combinations (eg, propofol combined with nalbuphine,6 remimazolam combined with alfentanil7 in pursuit of safer and more effective anesthesia regimens. However, the optimal sedation protocol remains controversial.

Oliceridine is a novel G protein-biased μ-opioid receptor agonist.8 In terms of the mechanism of action, oliceridine exhibits high selectivity for the μ-opioid receptor (its selectivity is 400-fold that of the κ and δ opioid receptors). The coupling efficacy of oliceridine with G proteins is approximately four times that of morphine. However, its capacity to recruit β-arrestin, which is believed to be related to adverse effects of opioid drugs, is only 14% of that of morphine. Therefore, oliceridine exhibits superior analgesic efficacy and fewer side effects. In terms of pharmacokinetics, oliceridine exhibits rapid onset of action (within 2 to 5 minutes) and a short duration of action (half-life: 1.3 to 3.7 hours). Age, weight, gender and other factors do not exert a significant influence on the pharmacokinetics of oliceridine.9,10 Pharmacological experiments have demonstrated that when oliceridine is administered to patients with end-stage kidney disease or mild to moderate liver dysfunction, no dose adjustment is required.11 The characteristics of oliceridine, including rapid onset, short duration of action and fewer adverse reactions, make it suitable for painless gastroscopy procedures.

To date, no studies have directly compared the safety and efficacy of oliceridine versus conventional opioids during painless gastroscopy when co-administrated with propofol. Moreover, no evidence-based dosing regimen has been established for the coadministration of oliceridine and propofol in this procedure. Therefore, we designed a randomized controlled trial in which three different dosage groups of oliceridine and one sufentanil control group were established to compare the anesthetic effects of different drug combinations used in painless gastroscopy.

Materials and Methods

This trial was carried out at the Endoscopy Center of Dazhou Central Hospital from July 2025 to October 2025. Patients scheduled to undergo painless gastroscopy were selected and randomly divided into four groups. The patients were administered an anesthetic regimen consisting of 10 μg/kg, 20 μg/kg, or 30 μg/kg of oliceridine (Nhwa Pharma. Corporation, Jiangsu, China), or 0.1 μg/kg of sufentanil (Humanwell Healthcare, Wuhan, China) combined with 2 mg/kg of propofol (Guorui Pharma. Corporation, Sichuan, China), respectively. All participants signed the informed consent form. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Dazhou Central Hospital in July 2024 and was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2500097246).

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The inclusion criteria are as follows: (1) American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification of I–III; (2) aged 18–60 years old; (3) Body mass index (BMI) ranges from 18.0–25 kg/m2; (4) signed the informed consent form. The exclusion criteria are as follows: (1) patients with severe organ disease: chronic lung disease, coronary artery disease, cerebrovascular diseases, liver or kidney decompensation, etc; (2) those allergic to the drugs used in this study; (3) those with malignant tumors; (4) pregnant or lactating women, or those with a recent pregnancy plan.

Randomization and Blinding

Patients were randomized using a random number sequence. The total sample (N) was stratified into four equal blocks of size N/4; within each block, participants were sequentially assigned to one of three intervention groups or the control group according to pre-specified allocation ratios. A double-blind design was implemented: both the anesthesiologists administering sedation and the participants remained blinded to group assignment. A designated unblinded anesthesiologist prepared and labeled the study drugs (olicerdine and sufentanil) under standardized protocols.

Intervention

Preoperative Preparation

Patients who fulfilled the inclusion criteria were recruited on the day of their painless gastroscopy. The basic information, including age, gender, weight, and BMI of the recruited patients was documented. The Apfel score was employed to evaluate the risk of nausea and vomiting in patients. The Apfel score, which is based on four predictive factors - female gender, history of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) and/or motion sickness, no smoking history, and planned postoperative use of opioids - ranges from 0 to 4. Each of these factors is assigned 1 point, and a higher score indicates a greater risk of PONV.

All patients were required to fast for 8 hours and abstain from fluids for 2 hours prior to gastroscopy. Upon entering the endoscopy room, patients were positioned in the left lateral decubitus position, and intravenous access was established. Patient’s vital signs, including heart rate (HR), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), mean arterial pressure (MAP), respiratory rate (RR), and pulse oxygen saturation (SpO2), were continuously monitored. Oxygen was administered via a facial mask at a flow rate of 5 L/min throughout the procedure and continued until patients achieved full consciousness.

Intraoperative Operation

Different doses of oliceridine (Group L, 10 μg/kg; Group M, 20 μg/kg; Group H, 30 μg/kg) or sufentanil (0.1 μg/kg) was intravenously administered, followed by a slow intravenous injection of propofol (2 mg/kg) after 1 min. The gastroscopy procedure was initiated when the Modified Observer’s Assessment of Alertness/Sedation (MOAA/S) score was ≤ 3 points. If the MOAA/S score was ≥ 4 or if body movement or choking occurred, an additional dose of propofol (0.5 mg/kg) could be administered.

During the procedure, ephedrine was administered intravenously whenever hypotension occurred, defined as a systolic blood pressure (SBP) decrease of 30% or more from the baseline. Urapidil was administered intravenously whenever hypertension occurred, defined as a SBP increase of 20% or more from the baseline. Atropine should be administered when patients experience bradycardia (HR < 50 bpm). If the SpO2 drops below 90% during the operation, jaw-thrusting should be performed. In severe cases, endotracheal intubation may be performed, and mechanical ventilation can be implemented.

Postoperative Monitoring

After the procedure, the patient was transferred to the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) for spontaneous recovery. Vital signs were continuously monitored, and supplemental oxygen was administered until the patient demonstrated unequivocal return of consciousness. Patients were discharged upon meeting all of the following criteria: (1) completion of a minimum 20-minute observation period in the PACU following return of consciousness, (2) Aldrete score ≥9, and (3) absence of acute adverse reactions; discharge was permitted only when accompanied by a responsible adult.

Primary and Secondary Outcome

The primary outcome is the incidence of respiratory depression, which is defined as a respiratory rate lower than 8 breaths per minute, the occurrence of respiratory arrest (lasting more than 15 seconds), or SpO2 < 90%.7 In the event of a decrease in respiratory rate or SpO2, the anesthesiologist must verify the accuracy of these values to confirm that the readings are normal. If the SpO2 drops below 90%, intervention becomes necessary. The specific intervention measures encompass adjusting the body position and elevating the chin. The type and frequency of interventions must be documented.

Specific manifestations of respiratory depression (Respiratory arrest, RR<8 bpm and SpO2 < 90%) were defined and recorded as secondary outcomes.

Other Outcomes

Procedure and sedation-related outcomes, perioperative vital signs and postoperative adverse effects were recorded: (1) operation time—defined as the interval from endoscope insertion to withdrawal; (2) awakening time: measured from endoscope withdrawal to first sustained eye opening with purposeful response; (3) propofol dosing—separately documented for induction and total administration; (4) sedation success rate—defined as successful completion of gastroscopy without requirement for supplemental propofol; (5) incidence of predefined physiological responses: (i) body movement: involuntary limb movement; (ii) choking—cough reflex elicited during endoscope insertion; and (iii) hypotension—defined as a reduction in SBP, DBP, or MAP exceeding 30% from baseline; (6) vital signs: including SBP, DBP, MAP, HR, RR, and SpO2, recorded at four standardized time points: T1 (baseline, upon room entry), T2 (endoscope inserting), T3 (endoscope withdrawal), and T4 (5 minutes post-procedure); and (7) postoperative adverse events—drowsiness, dizziness, PONV, and agitation.

All prespecified observation parameters were systematically assessed and documented prior to PACU discharge.

Sample Size

The sample size was calculated by PASS 2021 software. According to our pilot experiment, the incidence of respiratory depression in each group during painless gastroscopy were as follows: 60% in Group C, 10% in Group L, 25% in Group M, and 25% in Group H. We conducted a power analysis, setting the significance level (α) at 0.05, the expected power (β) at 0.80, and considering an expected 20% dropout rate. Based on these parameters, the calculated sample size was 188 participants, with 47 in each group. The pilot study employed the same procedural protocol as the main trial; however, its sole objective was to assess feasibility and safety, with respiratory depression incidence serving as the main observational index. Therefore, the pilot data were not included in the analysis of the formal trial.

Data Analysis

Quantitative data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. For data with normal distribution, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used, followed by Tukey’s test for post-hoc comparisons between groups; for non-normally distributed data, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied. Repeated-measures data (predominantly hemodynamic parameters) were analyzed using repeated-measures analysis of variance (RM-ANOVA). When Mauchly’s test of sphericity indicated P< 0.05), the Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied to adjust degrees of freedom. Post hoc between-group and within-group multiple comparisons were conducted using the Bonferroni correction. Qualitative data were expressed as counts and percentages, and analyzed using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. A p-value of less than 0.05 is considered statistically significant.

Results

From July 2025 to October 2025, 188 patients were enrolled, with 47 patients allocated to each of the group. Among them, one patient in Group L withdrew informed consent before the operation. In Group M, one patient experienced significant respiratory depression (SpO2 < 90%) during the examination. However, we found that this patient was wearing an extremely tight body-shaping garment, which was deemed to have an impact on the results, and thus this patient was excluded from the group. In Group H, one patient withdrew informed consent before the operation, and one patient was excluded because her operation time exceeded the pre-set threshold. Ultimately, a total of 184 patients successfully completed the study. The study flowchart is shown in Figure 1. Discussion

Painless gastroscopy is a medical procedure that employs pharmacological sedation to enable patients to undergo gastroscopy without awareness.12 Propofol is widely employed in painless gastroscopy owing to its rapid onset of action and short elimination half-life, which collectively facilitate swift recovery and early discharge. However, propofol alone may result in inadequate sedation and increase the risk of adverse effects associated with high-dose propofol. Therefore, opioids are commonly added clinically to provide adjunct analgesia and improve patient tolerance during procedures.6 Conventionally opioids such as sufentanil, remifentanil, fentanyl exert their analgesic effects by binding to μ-opioid receptors. The μ-opioid receptor is a seven-transmembrane G protein-coupled receptor, and its signal transduction primarily occurs through two pathways: G protein-dependent signaling and β-arrestin-mediated signaling. The G protein pathway mediates the analgesic effect of opioids, whereas the β-arrestin pathway is associated with opioid-related adverse events (ORAEs), such as respiratory depression and gastrointestinal disturbances. The anesthesia regimen of propofol combined with opioids is associated with adverse effects such as respiratory depression, nausea and vomiting.13,14

Oliceridine, formerly known as TRCV130, is a novel G protein-biased μ-opioid receptor agonist.15 Oliceridine exhibits approximately fourfold greater G protein coupling efficacy than morphine, while its ability to recruit β-arrestin is only 14% that of morphine.16 Preclinical studies indicate that oliceridine produces potent analgesia with a more favorable safety profile relative to conventional μ-opioid receptor agonists, including lower incidence of respiratory depression and gastrointestinal adverse effects (eg, nausea, vomiting),15,17–19 as well as reduced impairment of cognitive function and orientation.20 The combination of oliceridine and propofol shows promising potential to enhance procedural safety and efficacy in painless gastroscopy.

Opioid-induced respiratory depression (OIRD) is a syndrome characterized by opioid-mediated depression of the respiratory center and upper airway obstruction. OIRD, one of the most serious adverse effects of opioids, may lead to severe complications such as hypoxemia, hypercapnia, and even respiratory or cardiac arrest if not treated promptly.21 In our study, Group C (sufentanil 0.1 μg/kg + propofol 2mg/kg) exhibited the highest incidence of respiratory depression (55.32%), respiratory arrest (51.06%), bradypnea (RR ≤8 bpm; 31.90%), and hypoxemia (SpO2 <90%; 14.89%), all of which were statistically significantly higher than those observed in Groups L (oliceridine 10 μg/kg + propofol 2mg/kg) and Group M (oliceridine 20 μg/kg + propofol 2mg/kg). Oliceridine (10 μg/kg and 20 μg/kg) demonstrated significant superiority over sufentanil in reducing the incidence of respiratory depression. No statistically significant difference in the incidence of respiratory depression was observed between Groups L and M. In contrast, Group H (oliceridine 30 μg/kg + propofol 2 mg/kg) exhibited a significantly higher incidence of respiratory depression than both Groups L and M (46.67% vs 15.22% and 17.39%, respectively; P < 0.05). Regarding the secondary outcome, Group H exhibited a significantly higher incidence of respiratory arrest compared with Groups L and M (42.22% vs 13.04% and 15.22%, respectively; P < 0.05). Ayad et al22 found that the incidence of respiratory depression in patients receiving 0.1 mg, 0.35 mg, and 0.5 mg of oliceridine for postoperative pain management was 3.2%, 13.9%, and 15.1%, respectively. These findings indicate a dose-dependent increase in the risk of respiratory depression with escalating oliceridine doses. This dose-dependent effect may be attributable to progressive saturation of opioid receptors and a reduction of receptor selectivity at higher oliceridine doses.13 Meanwhile, the combination with propofol further increases the incidence of respiratory depression. As a result, both high-dose oliceridine (30 μg/kg) and sufentanil exhibit significant higher incidence of respiratory depression and respiratory arrest, which may limit their use in obese patients, as these individuals are more likely to have obstructive sleep apnea syndrome. The use of high-dose oliceridine or sufentanil combined with propofol for painless gastroscopy in obese may further trigger apnea, leading to severe hypoxemia.

We categorized the types of respiratory depression that occurred in patients. Intriguingly, the overall incidences of respiratory depression and respiratory arrest in Group H and Group C were higher than those in Group L and Group M. However, only Group C had a higher incidence of reduced respiratory rate than Group M. The line chart of respiratory rate further demonstrates that patients in Groups L and M exhibited more rapid recovery of baseline respiratory function compared with those in Group C. Oliceridine-associated respiratory depression during endoscopy predominantly presents as transient respiratory arrest, followed by rapid spontaneous recovery of respiratory function. A study that compared the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of the respiratory effects of olicerdine and morphine in elderly volunteers demonstrated that 0.5 mg of olicerdine exerted no significant impact on respiration. Nevertheless, 2 mg of olicerdine and 8 mg of morphine both led to rapid episodes of respiratory depression. However, when compared to morphine, the recovery rate of olicerdine following respiratory depression was approximately five times faster than that of morphine.19 The favorable safety profile of oliceridine—characterized by a low incidence of respiratory depression and rapid spontaneous recovery of respiratory function following transient suppression—supports its suitability for brief procedures and minimally invasive examinations (eg, painless bronchoscopy, hysteroscopy). A single-center, randomized controlled clinical trial was carried out to assess the clinical efficacy of two anesthesia regimens in patients undergoing gastrointestinal endoscopy. These regimens were remifentanil-etomidate-oliceridine (1mg or 1.5mg) or remifentanil-etomidate-sufentanil (5μg or 7.5μg).23 It was observed that the incidence of respiratory depression in the oliceridine group was 14.1%, which was lower than the incidences in our study (15.2% in group L, 17.4% in group M, and 46.7% in group H). Additionally, the incidence of respiratory depression in the sufentanil control group in their study was 21.8%, which was lower than that in our study (55.3%). Potential explanations for the observed findings include the broader operational definition of respiratory depression adopted in this study, as well as the selection of sedative agents. Notably, remimazolam and etomidate are associated with a significantly lower incidence of respiratory depression relative to propofol.24–26 The ED50 values required to inhibit bronchoscopy response when using oliceridine in combination with propofol are 30.20 μg/kg and 40.47 μg/kg for males and females, respectively. Meanwhile, the ED95 values are 46.49 μg/kg and 57.55 μg/kg, respectively, which are significantly higher than the dosage used for painless gastroscopy. This phenomenon is related to the high irritability associated with fiberoptic bronchoscopy examination. In their study, the incidence of respiratory depression during fiberoptic bronchoscopy was 21.74% among males and 72.73% among females. This disparity may be attributed to the higher dose of oliceridine administered in the female group compared to the male group.27 The proportion of female patients was higher in our present study. However, we have not yet explored whether there are differences in the incidence of respiratory depression between men and women, and this aspect can be further investigated in the future.

No statistically significant differences were observed between Groups L and M for either the primary or secondary outcomes. However, the sedation success rate was significantly lower in Group L than in Group M (71.74% vs 93.48%; P < 0.05). Tang et al28 found that the 90% effective dose (ED90) and 95% effective dose (ED99) of oliceridine combined with propofol for suppressing body movement and cough reflexes during gastroscopy were 22.5 μg/kg and 23.8 μg/kg, respectively. Another study reported that the median effective dose (ED50) and ED95 of oliceridine combined with 0.3 mg/kg remimazolam for inhibiting responses to gastroscopic catheterization were 12.717 μg/kg and 18.818 μg/kg, respectively.29 The moderate-dose oliceridine (20μg/kg) approximated the ED95 reported in two prior studies for repressing the response to endoscopic intubation. Therefore, the lower sedation success rate observed in the low-dose group is likely attributable to subtherapeutic oliceridine dosing. Moreover, no universally accepted or standardized criterion currently exists for defining the optimal timing of endoscope insertion during painless gastroscopy.30 In this study, endoscope insertion was performed when patients achieved a MOAA/S score ≤3, which might have increased the likelihood of unconscious body movement when compared to lower MOAA/S score. Notably, definitions of sedation success vary across studies. For instance, several studies define sedation success operationally as successful completion of the endoscopic procedure.31 We predefined sedation success strictly as uneventful gastroscopy completion without any propofol supplementation, which is a relatively narrow definition. In fact, all patients successfully completed the gastroscopic examination.

In procedure and sedation-related outcomes, the Group L showed the highest incidence of body movement (23.91%) and choking (17.39%), suggesting inadequate sedation. Hypotension is a prevalent phenomenon during painless gastroscopy, potentially resulting from prolonged fasting and intestinal preparation that leads to inadequate fluid volume. The administration of sedatives and analgesics can suppress vascular tension, ultimately causing a reduction in blood pressure. This phenomenon is especially pronounced in elderly patients.32 In our study, the incidence of hypotension was 19.15% in the sufentanil group and 17.39% in Group M, which is consistent with previous research.23,33 Additionally, our study found that the incidence of hypotension in Group L (21.74%) was higher than that in Group M (17.39%) and Group H (17.78%). We speculate that it might be due to the fact that patients in Group L are prone to body movement, which would result in an increase in the dosage of propofol. An increase in the dosage of propofol gives rise to a higher incidence of hypotension. There were no statistically differences in hemodynamics among the groups at the same time point. In this study, we did not observe any significant hemodynamic advantages of oliceridine. A recent study demonstrated that oliceridine pre-treatment significantly reduces the incidence of etomidate-induced muscle tremors during painless gastroscopy.34 In this study, propofol was used as the primary sedative agent, and no cases of muscle tremors were observed. Additionally, no statistically differences were observed among groups in the incidence of postoperative adverse events, including agitation, drowsiness, dizziness, nausea, and vomiting. This may be attributable to the exclusive enrollment of young adult patients, combined with the inherently brief duration of painless endoscopy and the relatively low total sedative doses administered—factors that collectively limit pharmacodynamic burden and preclude the emergence of significant adverse reactions during the peri-procedural period. In the future, more efforts are required to investigated the effect of olicerdine in special populations (such as the elderly, patients with liver and kidney dysfunction, etc.) and painless endoscopic examinations that demand longer procedural sedation (such as painless gastroenterological endoscopy, painless fiberoptic bronchoscopy, etc).

Limitations

This study is a single-center trial with a small sample size, and further large-scale clinical research is still needed for validation. Moreover, the cohort consisted predominantly of young, healthy adults. As a result, the dosing regimens identified as optimal in this study may not be appropriate for older adults, patients with hepatic or renal impairment, or individuals with obesity since these populations are known to exhibit altered pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics.

Conclusion

Overall, the combination of propofol with sufentanil (0.1 μg/kg) or high-dose oliceridine (30 μg/kg) exhibited a higher incidence of respiratory depression, whereas the combination with low-dose oliceridine (10 μg/kg) achieved a lower sedation success rate. Moderate-dose oliceridine (20 μg/kg) co-administered with propofol demonstrated both superior procedural efficacy and an improved safety profile in adults undergoing painless gastroscopy.
