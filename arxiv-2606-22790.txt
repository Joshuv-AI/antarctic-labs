Scaling Audio Models Efficiently: A Joint Study of Compute Constraints and Optimization Behavior
Vyom Agarwal, Mokshda Gangrade, Siddharth Pal, Jerry Wu

Abstract
In this paper, we investigate the tradeoffs between compute allocation and model performance for two speech-processing tasks: Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) and Speech Emotion Recognition (SER). We propose a unified framework that analyzes three fundamental compute dimensions: model size ( N N ), input length ( T T ), and representation resolution ( V V ). Motivated by recent advances in compute optimal scaling for multimodal models, we systematically vary these dimensions to examine their influence on task performance under fixed computational budgets. Our study provides insights into how compute resources can be optimally distributed across model capacity, temporal context, and representational granularity, offering practical guidelines for the design of efficient speech models. Through experiments on LibriSpeech and CREMA-D datasets, we demonstrate non-linear scaling behavior and identify optimal operating points. Our results show that (1) increasing model size yields diminishing returns—scaling Tiny (39M) to Small (244M) reduces WER by 8.22%, whereas Small to Medium (769M) reduces WER by only 2.35%; (2) an optimal audio duration of approximately 4 seconds exists for SER; and (3) reducing encoder token resolution provides an effective mechanism for lowering inference cost, Large-v3 (1540M) with 750 frames requires 2572 GFLOPS whereas with 1500 frames requires 5228 GFLOPS, with less than 3% relative increase in WER. Additionally, LoRA-based adaptation enables efficient finetuning with minimal performance degradation. Code: https://github.com/vyomya/SAME



I
Introduction

Recent advances in large-scale audio models such as
Whisper
[
1
]
, which demonstrates strong robustness
across diverse datasets, and wav2vec 2.0
[
2
]
,
which enables powerful self-supervised representation learning, have
significantly improved performance in speech recognition and audio
understanding tasks
[
11
]
. However, these gains come
with substantial computational costs, limiting their deployment in
resource-constrained settings.

Parameter-efficient finetuning methods such as LoRA
[
3
]

reduce the number of trainable parameters by introducing low-rank
updates, enabling efficient adaptation with reduced compute.

Scaling law studies suggest that performance improves predictably with
increased compute; however, more recent work highlights that imbalanced
allocation across compute dimensions leads to
inefficiencies. In particular,
Inference Compute-Optimal Video
Vision Language Models

[
4
]
show that optimal
inference performance depends on jointly scaling model capacity, input
resolution, and token budget, rather than scaling any single axis in
isolation.

Our work extends this idea to the audio domain by defining analogous
compute axes
(x_{\!N},x_{\!T},x_{\!V})
, studying their interaction in
encoder decoder architectures, and empirically validating
compute-optimal tradeoffs for ASR and SER.

Additionally, we incorporate insights from DAMA (Depth-Aware Model
Adaptation)
[
5
]
, which improves parameter-efficient
finetuning by adapting layers selectively based on their importance,
leading to better compute performance tradeoffs compared to uniform
adaptation strategies.

This paper addresses the following question:
How should
computational resources be optimally allocated across model size

x_{\!N}
, input length
x_{\!T}
, and representation resolution
x_{\!V}
to
maximize performance under a fixed compute budget?

Rather than uniformly scaling all components, we study how targeted
allocation along different compute axes affects performance. We
understand that optimal performance arises from balanced scaling across
axes, rather than maximizing any single dimension
[
4
]
.

Our contributions include:

•

A detailed analysis for optimizing compute performance
tradeoffs in ASR and SER.

•

Systematic exploration of compute axes
x_{\!N}
,
x_{\!T}
,
x_{\!V}
,
with empirical evidence that their relative importance is
task-dependent.

•

Empirical identification of optimal tradeoffs and diminishing
returns.

•

Insights into efficient adaptation using LoRA and DAMA.

II
Problem Formulation

We formulate the problem as a constrained optimization task. The central objective is similar to the Efficient Net optimization problem
[
13
]
:

\max_{x_{\!N},x_{\!T},x_{\!V}}\;\mathrm{Accuracy}(x_{\!N},x_{\!T},x_{\!V})\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\mathrm{FLOPs}(x_{\!N},x_{\!T},x_{\!V})\leq C

(1)

where
C
is the fixed compute budget (in FLOPs) determined by hardware
and latency constraints.

II-A

Objectives

For ASR, we minimize Word Error Rate (WER):

\mathrm{WER}=\frac{S+D+I}{N}

(2)

where
S
,
D
, and
I
denote substitutions, deletions, and
insertions, and
N
is the number of reference words.

For SER, we maximize Unweighted Accuracy (UA%), defined as the average
per-class recall, to account for class imbalance in CREMA-D.

II-B

Compute Axes

•

Model Size
x_{\!N}
:
controls parameter count and model
capacity. FLOPs scale as
\mathrm{FLOPs}\propto L\cdot T^{2}\cdot d
,
where
L
is the number of layers,
T
is the token count, and

d
is the model dimension.

•

Input Length
x_{\!T}
:
number of audio frames / duration,
which directly impacts encoder compute and available temporal
context.

•

Resolution
x_{\!V}
:
number of encoder tokens passed to
the decoder. Reducing
x_{\!V}
lowers cross-attention
FLOPs
\sim\mathcal{O}(T_{\text{dec}}\times T_{\text{enc}})
.

•

Adaptation Axis:
LoRA rank and layer-wise adaptation
strategy (DAMA).

The goal is to identify Pareto-optimal configurations where no
improvement in performance is achievable without increasing compute.

Real-Time Factor (RTF) is used as the deployment constraint metric:

\mathrm{RTF}=\frac{d_{a}}{t_{p}}

(3)

where
d_{a}
is the duration of the audio (in seconds) and
t_{p}
is the
time taken to process the audio (in seconds).
\mathrm{RTF}<1

indicates faster-than-real-time processing and is required for
practical deployment.

III
Methodology

We investigate compute performance tradeoffs across three
complementary axes—model size
x_{\!N}
, input length
x_{\!T}
, and encoder
token resolution
x_{\!V}
. These axes form a structured search space where

x_{\!N}
governs representational capacity but offers the least
flexibility post-deployment;
x_{\!T}
provides a dynamic,
input-dependent compute handle tunable without architectural changes;
and
x_{\!V}
reduces cross-attention cost orthogonally to both other axes.
Critically, these axes interact non-linearly: gains from additional
temporal context are amplified in larger models, while resolution
subsampling becomes increasingly effective as encoder sequences grow.
Jointly varying all three under a fixed FLOPs budget reveals
Pareto-optimal configurations invisible to any single-axis analysis,
providing principled guidance for deploying audio models under
real-world compute constraints. Critically, we find that the relative
priority of these axes differs qualitatively between tasks: for ASR all
three axes are active and the Pareto frontier is smooth, while for SER
the resolution axis is inapplicable and the frontier is sparse,
confirming that compute axis priorities cannot be assumed to transfer
across tasks without empirical validation.

III-A

ASR Pipeline (Whisper)

We finetune Whisper models on LibriSpeech using parameter-efficient
adaptation, leveraging all three compute axes to characterize the full
compute performance tradeoff space for speech recognition.

1.

Model Size
x_{\!N}
:
We evaluate Whisper-Tiny, Small,
Medium, and Large-v3, spanning 39M to 1540M parameters.

2.

Input Length
x_{\!T}
:
We control input length via active
encoder frames: Full (1500 frames) and Reduced (750 frames).

3.

Resolution
x_{\!V}
:
We subsample encoder outputs before
cross-attention using a stride factor
x_{\!V}=1
or
x_{\!V}=2
,
reducing key/value tokens passed to the decoder by

\sim\mathcal{O}(T_{\text{dec}}\times T_{\text{enc}})
.

In the ASR setting, all three axes are fully applicable. Compute is
best allocated by selecting a model size appropriate to the deployment
budget, maximizing input length for temporal context, and applying
resolution subsampling to reduce cross-attention cost, a priority
ordering empirically validated by our Pareto frontier analysis.

III-B

SER Pipeline (wav2vec2)

We finetune wav2vec2-large-robust on CREMA-D for 6-class emotion
classification
[
9
]
. Unlike ASR, the SER pipeline surfaces
important task-specific constraints that alter how each axis operates;
however,
x_{\!V}
is treated as fixed for architectural reasons described
below.

1.

Model Size
x_{\!N}
:
We evaluate wav2vec2-base and
wav2vec2-large-robust, spanning 95M to 317M parameters.

2.

Input Length
x_{\!T}
:
We evaluate audio durations of
2s, 4s, and 6s. Unlike ASR where additional context is
generally beneficial, SER exhibits a non-trivial
optimum, too-short clips truncate prosodic patterns while
excessively long clips introduce speaker-neutral content and
padding artifacts.

3.

Resolution
x_{\!V}
:
No explicit subsampling is applied.
SER relies on pooled frame-level representations where token
reduction is less architecturally natural, and the dominant
compute cost lies in the transformer encoder rather than
cross-attention. We treat
x_{\!V}
as fixed and identify it as a
direction for future work.

The SER pipeline therefore operates primarily over
x_{\!N}
and
x_{\!T}
,
revealing a qualitatively different scaling behavior from ASR. The
Pareto frontier is sparse rather than smooth, suggesting task
complexity saturates at the capacity level of wav2vec2-base and that
compute is better allocated toward adaptation strategy than model scale.

III-C

Optimization Strategies

Full finetuning of large pretrained audio models is computationally
prohibitive and risks catastrophic like forgetting of pretrained acoustic
representations. We therefore adopt two complementary
parameter-efficient strategies that together address both the breadth
and depth of adaptation, and sweep their key hyperparameters to
characterize their individual contributions to the
compute performance tradeoff.

LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation):
LoRA constrains weight updates to
a low-rank factorization, preserving pretrained attention structure
while dramatically reducing trainable parameters. This is well suited
to audio models where pretrained representations should be retained
rather than overwritten. We sweep rank
r\in\{8,16,32,64\}
to
characterize the tradeoff between adaptation capacity and parameter
overhead, with higher ranks enabling richer task-specific updates at
increasing compute cost.

DAMA (Depth-Aware Model Adaptation):
Uniform adaptation
ignores the established asymmetry between transformer layers—lower
layers encode general acoustic features while upper layers capture
abstract, task-specific representations. DAMA exploits this by
selectively unfreezing top encoder layers, concentrating adaptation
capacity where it is most impactful. This is especially critical for
SER, where emotion-discriminative features emerge primarily in upper
layers and LoRA alone proves insufficient. We sweep unfrozen top
encoder layers
\in\{0,4,8\}
to identify the minimum adaptation depth
required for effective domain transfer, directly quantifying the
compute cost of task-specific layer unfreezing.

IV
Experimental Setup

IV-A

Datasets

To systematically evaluate compute performance tradeoffs across audio
understanding tasks, we require benchmarks that are well-established,
diverse in their acoustic demands, and widely used in prior work,
enabling direct comparison against published baselines. We therefore
select one dataset per task, chosen to reflect complementary challenges
in temporal modeling and emotional discriminability.

ASR-LibriSpeech:
To evaluate ASR performance under controlled compute budgets, we benchmark against
LibriSpeech
[
6
]
, the standard reference
dataset for English speech recognition. Following prior work on
Whisper
[
1
]
and efficient ASR adaptation, we
train on
train-clean-100
(100 hours) to reflect a realistic
mid-scale supervised setting, validate on the standard validation set,
and report final results on
test-other
, the more
acoustically challenging split to assess robustness under
distribution shift. All audio is resampled to 16 kHz to match
Whisper’s input requirements.

SER-CREMA-D:
We use CREMA-D
[
7
]
, a
controlled, balanced corpus of 7,442 clips from 91 actors expressing
six discrete emotions, which has served as a standard benchmark in
recent emotion recognition studies. Compared to in-the-wild corpora,
CREMA-D’s controlled recording conditions isolate the effect of compute
axis choices from confounding acoustic variability, making it
well-suited for systematic scaling analysis. We apply an 80/10/10
train/val/test split based on speaker identity to prevent speaker
leakage, a common source of inflated results in prior SER work,
ensuring that reported accuracies reflect generalization to unseen
speakers rather than memorization of speaker-specific vocal
characteristics.

IV-C

Training Configuration

To ensure that observed performance differences reflect the effect of
compute axis choices rather than optimization artifacts, we maintain a
unified training configuration across all experiments. All models are
trained using the AdamW optimizer with a linear learning rate schedule,
a learning rate of
1\times 10^{-5}
, 500 warmup steps, and
4000 total training steps, a budget sufficient for convergence on both
LibriSpeech and CREMA-D at the scales studied, while remaining
comparable to the finetuning protocols reported in prior LoRA-based
speech adaptation work. LoRA for the Whisper ASR model is applied to

q_{\text{proj}}
and
v_{\text{proj}}
across all attention layers
with rank
r=32
,
\alpha=64
, and dropout
=0.05
, following the
configuration shown to generalize well across
modalities
[
3
]
. Training is conducted with gradient
checkpointing enabled to reduce memory overhead, allowing larger model
variants to be finetuned within the same hardware budget without
architectural compromise.

\sim

\sim

2\times
the FLOPs. Increasing input length from 750 to 1500 frames consistently reduces WER by 3–10% relative across all model sizes. Notably, the gain from doubling input length on Whisper-Small (

VI
Conclusion

This work presents a systematic study of compute-performance tradeoffs in large pre-trained audio models across Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) and Speech Emotion Recognition (SER). By jointly varying three compute axes - model size (
xN
), input length (
xT
), and encoder token resolution (
xV
), alongside parameter-efficient adaptation strategies (LoRA and DAMA), we identify optimal Pareto configurations with direct deployment implications.

In conclusion, our central finding is that balanced and targeted allocation of compute across multiple inference axes consistently outperforms strategies that maximize any single dimension in isolation. For automatic speech recognition (ASR), encoder token subsampling along the representation axis (
xV
) emerges as the most efficient inference-time lever, recovering approximately 29% of cross-attention FLOPs while incurring negligible degradation in accuracy. In addition, increasing input length (
xT
) provides a more compute-efficient performance gain than scaling model size (
xN
) in the mid-capacity regime; for example, doubling the context window in Whisper-Small achieves performance comparable to a full model upgrade while requiring less than one-sixth of the additional compute.

For speech emotion recognition (SER), we observe a task-specific optimal operating point at approximately four seconds of audio. Shorter input durations result in loss of critical prosodic context, whereas longer durations introduce padding-induced artifacts that degrade performance. Collectively, these results highlight the importance of jointly optimizing compute allocation across model size, temporal context, and representation resolution rather than relying on single-axis scaling strategies.
Adaptation strategy proves equally critical. LoRA alone collapses SER performance to near random, while depth-aware layer unfreezing (DAMA) recovers meaningful accuracy at identical FLOPs, demonstrating that tasks involving significant domain shift require structural adaptation, not just parameter-efficient overlays [8]. The optimal compute SER configuration achieves a
4.3\times
FLOPs reduction with only 7.7% accuracy degradation, underscoring that efficiency and performance are not fundamentally at odds. Notably, the Pareto frontier topology differs qualitatively between tasks smooth and monotonic for ASR, sparse for SER confirming that compute axis priorities are dependent on task and cannot be transferred without empirical validation.
The three-axis framework (
xN
,
xT
,
xV
) introduced here provides a principled scaffold for compute budget allocation across audio tasks.

VII
Future Work

Joint axis optimization:
Our current study uses a star-sweep
strategy, varying one axis at a time. Future work should conduct
iso-FLOP sweeps jointly over
(x_{\!N},x_{\!T},x_{\!V},\text{LoRA rank})
to
identify the globally optimal configuration at each compute budget,
analogous to the approach in
[
4
]
.

Quantization as a compute axis:
Post-training quantization
(INT8, INT4) is orthogonal to the axes studied here and could unlock
additional compute–performance tradeoffs
[
10
]
.
Combining LoRA with quantization (e.g., QLoRA) is a particularly
promising direction.

Continuous
x_{\!V}
subsampling in SER:
Extending the encoder
token subsampling mechanism from the ASR pipeline to wav2vec2-based SER
could expose additional compute savings without parameter overhead,
completing the three-axis framework for both tasks.
