Executive Summary
This work was carried out as part of the Heliostat Consortium (HelioCon) Field Deployment
subtask with the aim to distill published literature in wind loads on solar photovoltaic (PV)
panels and heliostat facets to develop a reduced-order model for heliostat wind loads. It is
intended to be understood and applied by structural engineers and heliostat designers as inputs to
determine wind loads on heliostats. The derivations and equations applied to estimate wind loads
on heliostats in this document adopt correlations and relationships developed in wind tunnel
experiments and field measurements between atmospheric wind parameters and non-dimensional
wind load coefficients. The report contains four sections with the following conclusions:

 • Similarity in geometric scaling of PV panels and arrays defined in ASCE/SEI 7-22 design
 wind load standards compared with commercial-scale heliostat facets and arrays
 • Comparable statistics of pressure distributions for maximum wind load cases in operating
 and stow positions in wind tunnel experiments and field measurements
 • Analysis of reduced frequencies for assessing dynamic loads as a function of heliostat
 size and wind speed
 • Correlations between atmospheric turbulence parameters and stow wind load coefficients
 developed in the literature of a single heliostat in stow position showed a weak
 dependence of peak load magnitudes on a single heliostat in a full-scale operational field.

1 Introduction
This report provides a literature summary of design wind load standards in American Society of
Civil Engineers (ASCE) and Structural Engineering Institute (SEI), ASCE/SEI 7-22, for solar
photovoltaics (PV) and reconciles analytical and semi-empirical equations to develop a reduced-
order model for heliostat wind loads. The stand-alone report contains four sections that present
scaling parameters, develop equations, and analyze correlations from published literature and
field data to estimate the design wind loads on heliostats. The first section compares the
geometric parameters of ground-mounted PV panels and PV array load multipliers defined in
ASCE/SEI 7-22 wind load standards with heliostat geometric scaling parameters and heliostat
array wind load variations in published wind tunnel experiments. The second section outlines
four critical load cases, or heliostat azimuth-elevation configurations, corresponding to
maximum wind loads for heliostat design and provides a statistical summary of representative
wind pressure distributions reported in wind tunnel experiments and field measurements. The
third section investigates reduced frequencies on full-scale heliostats to estimate ranges of wind
speeds that are relevant to dynamic loads on different heliostat sizes. The fourth section applies
correlations between localized atmospheric boundary layer (ABL) turbulence and non-
dimensional wind load coefficients of a stowed heliostat to full-scale ESDU atmospheric data
and compares with stow load coefficients calculated from field measurement data at Crescent
Dunes heliostat field.

2 PV Array Wind Load Standards and Their Geometric
  Similarity and Applicability to Heliostat Fields
This section compares the scaling parameters of solar PV panels with heliostats. American
Society for Civil Engineers (ASCE) is the nationally adopted standard for wind load calculations
in the United States. ASCE/SEI 7-22 [1] contains minimum design loads and associated criteria
for ground-mounted and rooftop PV panels. Figure 1 shows schematically the geometric scaling
parameters of ground-mounted PV arrays, which are generally similar in magnitude and non-
dimensional scaling to heliostats. Design wind pressures and net pressure coefficients for PV
panels are calculated in ASCE/SEI 7-22 [1] as a function of effective wind area and several
factors. For example, the array edge factor 𝛾𝛾𝐸𝐸 = 1.5 applies to uplift loads for exposed panels
within 1.5𝐿𝐿𝑐𝑐 panel chord lengths from the end of a row at an exposed edge of the array,
whereas 𝛾𝛾𝐸𝐸 = 1.0 applies to uplift and downward forces on all other (interior) panels.

Despite some similarities with PV panels, significant differences of heliostat scaling parameters
include the reduced aspect ratio, larger range of operating elevation (tilt) angles and larger non-
dimensional array spacing. These factors lead to reduced torsional loads on heliostats, and
increased variation of heliostat loads with elevation angle and within an array from the field edge
to the interior, depending on the local field density.

4 Dynamic Loads as a Function of Heliostat Size and
  Wind Speed
The variation of dynamic wind loads with heliostat facet size is investigated using a simplified
reduced frequency approach to summarize the ranges of wind speeds that most effectively
contribute to dynamic loads on different heliostat sizes. Aerodynamic admittance functions
relating the wind load fluctuations on solar panels to the atmospheric wind fluctuations have
shown that a reduced (non-dimensional) frequency 𝑛𝑛 = 𝑓𝑓𝑓𝑓/𝑈𝑈 between 0.1 and 1 effectively
generates aerodynamic loads, such as lift forces [12], drag forces, and hinge moments [6] on a
heliostat facet. Here, 𝑓𝑓 (Hz) is the lowest natural frequency of the facet structure, 𝐿𝐿 (m) is the
panel chord length, and 𝑈𝑈 (m/s) is the mean wind speed at the mid-height of the panel. This
range of reduced frequencies corresponds to turbulent eddies that are approximately the same
length as the panel and an order of magnitude larger. At 𝑛𝑛 > 1, the turbulent eddies that are
smaller in size than the panel are not fully effective in generating aerodynamic loads. At 𝑛𝑛 < 0.1,
the turbulent eddies are at least an order of magnitude larger in size than the panel and act in a
quasi-static manner with the mean wind (i.e., “sway” of the heliostat).

Section 5.3.4.3.6 on ground-mounted fixed-tilt solar panel systems in ASCE/SEI 7-22 [1] defines
the reduced frequency 𝑛𝑛 = 𝑓𝑓𝑓𝑓/𝑈𝑈 in equation 5.38, noting that a practical approach to obtain the
lowest natural frequency 𝑓𝑓 and structural damping (not total damping including aerodynamic
damping) is to conduct field measurements on representative full-scale systems. In lieu of field
measurements, estimates of the natural frequencies can be obtained from computer models of the
system. Table 2 shows the reduced frequency calculated as a function of panel chord length 𝐿𝐿
and mean wind speed 𝑈𝑈 with the lowest natural frequency 𝑓𝑓 = 3 Hz assumed for a heliostat facet
based on field measurements of experimental modal analysis and displacement amplitude spectra
[13] and wind-induced deviation spectra [14] of small- and medium-sized heliostats (14 m2 and
48.5 m2). It is evident for 𝑓𝑓 = 3 Hz that the mean wind speed corresponding to 𝑛𝑛 < 1 increases
with increasing panel chord length, such as from 𝑈𝑈 ≥ 10 m/s for 𝑐𝑐 = 3 m to 𝑈𝑈 ≥ 18 m/s for
𝑐𝑐 = 6 m. While the onset wind speed induces dynamic loads, 𝑓𝑓 varies with heliostat size, and the
peak frequency of near-surface atmospheric wind fluctuations varies with height below 10 m
between 0.01 and 0.1 Hz for the longitudinal 𝑢𝑢-component and between 0.1 and 1 Hz for the
vertical 𝑤𝑤-component [15]. Based on the range of heliostat facet chord lengths, it is therefore
more likely that dynamic effects are induced by vertical wind fluctuations.

5 Wind Load Correlations in Wind Tunnel
  Experiments and Application to Field Data
For a heliostat with a ratio of elevation axis height to chord length 𝐻𝐻/𝑐𝑐 = 0.5, peak wind load
coefficients in stow will vary with heliostat size depending on the relationships in Equations (1)–
(4) in Appendix A as a function of the profiles of turbulence intensities and integral turbulence
length scales in [16], [17], and [18]. “Open country terrain” is typically characterized as 𝑧𝑧0
ranging between 0.01 m (flat area with negligible vegetation) and 0.05 m (area with grassy plains
and isolated obstacles such as trees and buildings). The turbulence intensities in ESDU 85020
and ESDU 74031 in Figure 5 show a similar trend that indicates increasing turbulence intensity
of both longitudinal 𝑢𝑢-component and vertical 𝑤𝑤-component with increasing 𝑧𝑧0 and closer to the
surface. The trends in 𝐿𝐿𝑥𝑥𝑢𝑢 /𝑐𝑐 and 𝐿𝐿𝑥𝑥𝑤𝑤 /𝑐𝑐 vary with height, as ESDU 85020 at 𝑧𝑧0 = 0.03 m shows
that both 𝐿𝐿𝑥𝑥𝑢𝑢 /𝑐𝑐 and 𝐿𝐿𝑥𝑥𝑤𝑤 /𝑐𝑐 increase slightly with increasing height, whereas ESDU 74031 at
𝑧𝑧0 = 0.1 m shows that 𝐿𝐿𝑥𝑥𝑢𝑢 /𝑐𝑐 increases with decreasing height. Similarly, EN1991 1-4 shows that
both 𝐿𝐿𝑥𝑥𝑢𝑢 /𝑐𝑐 and 𝐿𝐿𝑥𝑥𝑤𝑤 /𝑐𝑐 increase with decreasing height and decreasing 𝑧𝑧0 .

Using Equations (1)–(4) in Appendix A, which were developed from wind tunnel experiments,
and the full-scale atmospheric wind data in Figure 5 for ESDU 85020 (turbulence intensities) and
EN 1991 1-4 (integral turbulence length scales), the peak load coefficients in stow position at
𝛼𝛼 = 0° in Figure 6 are derived as a function of heliostat elevation axis height 𝑧𝑧 = 𝐻𝐻 and surface
roughness height 𝑧𝑧0 of the terrain. It should be noted that drag coefficient in stow is several times
smaller than in operation; however, lift forces and hinge moments are comparably large in
magnitude given the maximum wind speeds in stow. The correlations from EN 1991 1-4 data
show that both lift force and hinge moment coefficients increase significantly with decreasing
surface roughness and decreasing height (i.e., for smaller heliostats), due to the increases of 𝐼𝐼𝑤𝑤
and 𝐿𝐿𝑥𝑥𝑤𝑤 /𝑐𝑐. This represents the turbulent eddy size approaching the mirror chord length, such that
vertical wind fluctuations lead to the maximum generation of lift forces at increased distance
from the central horizontal axis of the mirror surface. It should be noted that despite peak wind
load coefficients in stow position increasing with decreasing height for smaller heliostats [19],
the increase in load coefficients will be offset with decreasing wind speeds closer to the surface
for determining ultimate design loads in stow position.

It is evident from regression analysis of Crescent Dunes field measurement data in Figure 8 that
the peak lift force coefficient in stow is weakly dependent on 𝐼𝐼𝑤𝑤 and increases with increasing 𝐼𝐼𝑤𝑤

0.31–0.6 and peak hinge moment coefficients 0.11–0.20 at Crescent Dunes are similar to the
ranges 0.32–0.51 and 0.10–0.22, respectively, predicted by wind tunnel correlations in equations
(2) and (3) in Appendix A applied to full-scale wind data with 𝑧𝑧0 ranging between 0.01 m and
0.05 m [18]. This demonstrates that stow load coefficients derived in wind tunnel experiments
are generally accurate at estimating the peak loads on real-scale heliostats in stow position at the
field edge exposed to ABL turbulent wind conditions in an operational field. Weak dependence
of peak stow load coefficients on turbulence parameters are observed with considerable scatter in
the data. Future work will investigate regression analysis of longer periods of stow and operating
positions and additional wind parameters to reduce scatter and uncertainties of field
measurement data correlations.

Conclusions
This report presented a literature summary of published data and standards on solar PV panel
wind loads and applied semi-empirical correlations relating atmospheric wind turbulence
parameters to heliostat wind loads in a reduced-order model. The outcomes are intended to be
applicable to structural design of heliostats for the determination of wind loads. The following
conclusions were found from the four sections in this standalone report:

 • Geometric scaling parameters of PV panels and arrays defined in ASCE/SEI 7-22 design
 wind load standards were shown to be similar in magnitude and non-dimensional scaling
 ratios to commercial-scale heliostats. Differences were found in the panel/facet aspect
 ratio, range of operating elevation (tilt) angles and non-dimensional array spacing (field
 edge factors) influencing load variations from the field edge to the interior.
 • Mean and RMS pressure coefficient distributions for maximum wind load cases in
 operating and stow positions in wind tunnel experiments were generally consistent but
 smaller in magnitude compared with field measurements.
 • Analysis of reduced frequencies for assessing dynamic loads quantified wind speed
 ranges that contribute to effective generation of wind loads as a function of heliostat size.
 • Correlations between atmospheric turbulence and wind load coefficients developed in the
 literature for heliostats in stow position were applied to full-scale atmospheric wind data
 and showed weak correlation of peak stow loads on a single heliostat in a commercial-
 scale operational field with localized wind turbulence.
