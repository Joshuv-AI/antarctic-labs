A Novel Plant Secondary Metabolite‐Binding Gene Family Required for Full Virulence in Pectobacterium parvum
Authors: Jinhui Wang, Beibei Wu, Jiaxing Shen, Shangqing Zhang, Xingkui Cai, Minna Pirhonen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT Pectobacterium parvum is an emerging phytopathogen causing aerial stem rot of potato. P. parvum has a broad host range, with Chinese cabbage as one of its important hosts. Through multi‐omics analysis, we discovered a previously uncharacterized gene family highly expressed during in planta infection, designated the plant secondary metabolite‐binding (PSMB) family. This family likely originated via horizontal gene transfer and underwent lineage‐specific expansion in P. parvum , forming a four‐paralog pathogenicity island. Structural modelling revealed that PSMB proteins resemble the rhizobial RhiA protein, though their function in phytopathogens has not been established. Biochemical assays demonstrated that the representative member, PSMB1a, binds defensive plant secondary metabolites (PSMs) with high affinity. PSMB1a binds the solanidine with submicromolar affinity (Kd = 0.103 μM) and exhibited approximately 100‐fold higher affinity over salicylic acid (Kd = 9.23 ± 2.35 μM), indicating selectivity for specific PSMs. Functional studies demonstrated that PSMB1a enhances tolerance to defensive PSMs and contributes to virulence. Heterologous expression of PSMB1a in sister species P. polare enhanced PSM tolerance and virulence on Chinese cabbage, but suppressed bacterial proliferation under non‐stress conditions. Conversely, deletion of the entire PSMB pathogenicity island in P. parvum attenuated virulence on potato stems, Chinese cabbage, and radish while increasing in vitro growth, confirming a trade‐off between virulence contribution and basal fitness. These findings identify the PSMB family as a new class of virulence factors in Pectobacterium and reveal a specialized adaptive strategy in P. parvum that involves the binding of defensive PSMs to facilitate infection.Keywords: gene family, Pectobacterium parvum, plant secondary metabolites, protein‐ligand binding, virulence factors

Introduction

Pectobacterium species are phytopathogenic bacteria that cause soft‐rot diseases in a wide range of crops, vegetables, fruits, and ornamental plants worldwide (Charkowski 2018). Their pathogenicity primarily depends on plant cell wall‐degrading enzymes (PCWDEs), which are secreted via the Type II secretion system (T2SS) under the regulation of a quorum‐sensing (QS) network (Charkowski et al. 2012). Additional virulence factors, including other secretion systems (e.g., Type I secretion system [T1SS], Type III secretion system [T3SS]), outer membrane vesicles, and associated effectors, also contribute to disease development (Jonca et al. 2021; Kvitko et al. 2025). The genus comprises 21 validly published species (as of 2024), which exhibit remarkable phenotypic and genomic heterogeneity in host range, disease symptoms, geographical distribution, and genomic architecture (Sawada et al. 2024).

Among these, Pectobacterium parvum is a recently delineated species of particular interest. Initially regarded as an atypical group within Pectobacterium polare (synonym: P. polaris ), P. parvum was later recognized as a distinct species (Pasanen et al. 2020); both were originally classified under Pectobacterium carotovorum (Dees et al. 2017). P. parvum is primarily associated with aerial stem rot of potato ( Solanum tuberosum ) and has been reported in Finland, Poland, the Netherlands, South Korea, the United States and China (Jee et al. 2020; Pasanen et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2022). Recent reports indicate its expanding geographical distribution in northern China (Wu, Lv, et al. 2023; Ma et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2025).

The type strain s0421ᵀ, isolated from a rotten potato stem in Finland, produces only trace amounts of the QS signal molecule 3‐oxo‐C6‐HSL (Pasanen et al. 2013). Despite causing aggressive stem rot, P. parvum exhibits weak virulence on tubers. No natural infection of tubers or underground stems has been reported, and artificial inoculations under field conditions fail to cause blackleg, indicating its specialized adaptation to aboveground stems (Pasanen et al. 2013, 2020; Han et al. 2023). Although potato is its primary host, P. parvum can also infect bok choy ( Brassica rapa var. chinensis), sunflower ( Helianthus annuus ), and radish ( Raphanus sativus ) (Pasanen et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2025). This pathogenic profile differs from that of its sister species, P. polare , which causes potato blackleg and tuber soft rot and has a host range that includes Chinese cabbage ( B. rapa var. pekinensis), lettuce ( Lactuca sativa ), and broccoli ( B. oleracea var. italica) (Dees et al. 2017; Chen et al. 2021; Wu, Lu, et al. 2023; Qin et al. 2024).

Pectobacterium parvum exhibits significant genome reduction compared to closely related species, with gene losses in metabolic pathways (e.g., citrate and phosphonates utilization) and stress responses. Additionally, its genome lacks the Type VI secretion system (T6SS) and the plant‐associated hrp/hrc T3SS (Pasanen et al. 2020; Jee et al. 2022). These gene losses correlate with phenotypic traits such as inability to utilize citrate and failure to elicit a hypersensitive response (HR) in non‐host tobacco plants (Pasanen et al. 2020; Han et al. 2023). The genome reduction is accompanied by a slight decrease in GC content (51% in P. parvum versus 52% in P. polare ), suggesting concurrent acquisition of AT‐rich exogenous DNA (Pasanen et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2022). Despite genome reduction, P. parvum encodes a complete SPI‐1‐like T3SS, likely acquired via horizontal gene transfer (HGT) (Pasanen et al. 2020; Jee et al. 2022). Given the role of the SPI‐1 T3SS in Salmonella enterica persistence within leaf hopper (Dundore‐Arias et al. 2015), its presence in P. parvum suggests a potential adaptation to insect interactions.

Thus, P. parvum represents a pathogenically specialized lineage in which genome reduction has coincided with the acquisition of specific exogenous genes, raising the possibility that horizontally acquired genetic elements contribute directly to its distinctive pathogenic traits. However, the functional roles of these exogenous genes remain largely unknown.

To address this knowledge gap and identify novel virulence factors, we employed a stepwise multi‐omics strategy. We first performed pan‐genome analysis to identify species‐level gene family expansions in P. parvum . Concurrently, we conducted RNA‐Seq during in planta infection to profile genes highly expressed in the host environment. By intersecting these results, we prioritized expanded gene families with elevated in planta expression. Among the candidate genes, we focused on a four‐paralog family showing consistently high expression and characterized its structural features and ligand‐binding properties through molecular docking and biochemical assays. This approach led to the identification of a previously uncharacterized gene family, designated the plant secondary metabolite‐binding (PSMB) family. Focusing on a representative member, PSMB1a, we conducted genetic, biochemical, and pathogenicity assays to elucidate its role in bacterial virulence.

Results

Genomic Characterization and Taxonomic Reclassification

To identify novel virulence factors in P. parvum via multi‐omics analysis, we expanded the genomic resources for this species by sequencing a newly isolated strain and retrieving additional strains previously misclassified in public databases. A new strain, designated YT22221, was isolated in 2022 from a rotten potato stem in Yutian County, Hebei Province, China (Figure S1a). Its identity was confirmed by multilocus sequence analysis (MLSA) and supported by its inability to utilize citrate (Figure S1b,c). The complete genome of YT22221 comprises a 4.73 Mb chromosome and a 3.8 kb plasmid (GCF_024722495.1).

Given the recent taxonomic revision separating P. parvum from P. polare and P. carotovorum , we performed a phylogenetic analysis to resolve misclassifications in NCBI GenBank (Table S1). BLASTn based on four housekeeping genes revealed that four strains originally labelled as P. polare (WBC1, WBC6, WBC9 and WBC11) and one as P. carotovorum (MAFF301879) shared > 97% nucleotide identity with the P. parvum s0421T. Core‐genome MLSA (cgMLSA) placed these strains within the P. parvum clade (Figure 1a), supported by average nucleotide identity (ANI > 99.5%) and in silico DNA–DNA hybridization (isDDH > 95%) values relative to s0421T (Figure S1d). Strains isolated from brassicaceous and solanaceous plants did not cluster by host origin, indicating no host‐specific genetic differentiation within P. parvum (Table S1; Figure 1a).

Virulence in Host Plants

The reclassified WBC strains were originally isolated from Chinese cabbage (Chen et al. 2021), prompting investigation whether Chinese cabbage serves as an important host for P. parvum or P. polare . Pathogenicity assays revealed significant variation among species (Figure 1b). P. carotovorum BY21112 caused the most severe maceration (26.05 ± 4.36 cm2), followed by P. parvum FN20211 (15.67 ± 2.77 cm2) and YT22221 (10.35 ± 4.58 cm2), and P. polonicum BY21311 (10.34 ± 3.14 cm2). P. polare CM22132 and CM22112 exhibited the weakest virulence, with lesion areas of 1.68 ± 0.82 cm2 and 3.61 ± 2.58 cm2, respectively. In potato stems, P. versatile FN20111 caused the most severe maceration (9.69 ± 3.19 cm), followed by P. parvum YT22221 (7.74 ± 3.04 cm), P. brasiliense FR19412 (7.51 ± 3.81 cm), and P. parvum FN20211 (7.28 ± 3.67 cm), with no significant differences among these strains (Figure 1c; Figure S1e). However, P. polonicum BY21311 was significantly less virulent (4.65 ± 3.25 cm, p = 3.4e−4).

Pan‐Genome Characteristics

The pan‐genome of P. parvum comprised 5135 orthologous groups (OGs) and exhibited an open structure based on Heaps' law (α = 0.13), indicating substantial genomic plasticity (Figure 2a,b). Presence–absence variation analysis further resolved the genetic architecture of the species: the core‐genome contained 3395 hard‐core OGs (present in all strains) and 264 soft‐core OGs (present in ≥ 85% but < 100% of strains), whereas the accessory genome consisted of 1008 shell OGs (present in ≥ 10% but < 85% of strains) and 468 cloud OGs (present in ≥ 5% but < 10% of strains) (Table S2; Figure 2a).

To systematically investigate gene family expansions in P. parvum , we examined copy number variations via duplication events inferred by OrthoFinder. A total of 221 putative duplication events (Support > 0.5) were retained, distributed across 126 OGs (Table S2). These events were classified into two categories: 169 (76%) were strain‐specific terminal events, whereas 52 (24%) were shared across multiple strains. Among the shared events, 14 (distributed across 8 OGs) were present in all P. parvum strains (Support = 1.0), representing core species‐level duplications (Table S2). These duplications were detected in transporter genes (OG0000000, OG0000016, and OG0000019), pectate lyase genes (OG0000006), β‐glucosidase genes (OG0000017), and aspartate ammonia‐lyase genes (OG0000019). The remaining core duplications occurred in genes encoding hypothetical proteins (OG0000002 and OG0000003), suggesting the presence of previously uncharacterized genes that may contribute to species adaptation.

Gene Expression During in Planta Infection

RNA‐Seq identified 578 genes with expression exceeding that of the reference gene recA (logTPM = 8.26) (Table S3). Enriched KEGG pathways were associated with protein synthesis, energy metabolism, and bacterial motility (Figure 2c), indicating active bacterial proliferation and motility during infection.

To prioritize novel virulence factors, we first focused on genes encoding secreted proteins, as these are most likely to interact with host plants. A total of 14 classical secreted proteins (CSPs) and 50 non‐classical secreted proteins (NCSPs) were predicted. Among CSPs, six PCWDEs, one gene encoding an NPP1 family protein (Necrosis‐inducing Phytophthora Protein 1), and two genes encoding spore coat protein U (SCPU) domain‐containing proteins exhibited high expression levels (Figure 2d). Among NCSPs, 17 genes were highly expressed, including three encoding flagellar components (FliC, FlgL, and FlgD).

We then examined secreted proteins belonging to expanded OGs, as species‐level duplication often indicates adaptive significance. Notably, four paralogous genes (LOZ86_RS03665‐85) belonging to the same OG (OG0000002), which we identified as an expanded OG in the pan‐genome analysis (Table S2), exhibited consistently high expression (Figure 2d). All P. parvum strains harboured four paralogs of this OG, which originated from three duplication events (Table S2). The other expanded OG with high expression was OG0000006 (LOZ86_RS03025‐35), which encodes pectate lyases (PelA, PelB, and PelC) (Table S2; Figure 2d).

In contrast, the SPI‐1‐like T3SS gene cluster, although present in the core‐genome, exhibited low expression, suggesting a limited role in planta (Table S2; Figure 2d). Reverse transcription‐quantitative real‐time PCR (RT‐qPCR) validated the RNA‐Seq results (Figure 2e). Expression of fliC and LOZ86_RS03680 (one paralog of OG0000002) was significantly elevated relative to recA (9.69‐ and 6.92‐fold, respectively), whereas the SPI‐1‐like T3SS marker gene sicA, which encodes a chaperone protein required for effector secretion, showed minimal expression (C t > 34).

Structural and Evolutionary Analysis of PSMB Family

Gene LOZ86_RS03680 encodes a protein lacking known domains and exhibits no homology to characterized enzymes. BLASTp against UniProtKB revealed a distant homology to RhiA from Rhizobium leguminosarum (Economou et al. 1989). Despite only 23% identity, AlphaFold‐predicted structures of the two proteins are highly similar, differing mainly by an N‐terminal extension of 20 amino acids in RhiA (Figure 3a). This gene family is widely distributed across Pectobacterium and related genera. Phylogenetic analysis classified family members into two subfamilies comprising 10 clades (Figure 3b). Subfamily 1 (clades 1a–1 h) consists of 209 amino acids and features a symmetrical ‘butterfly‐wing’ β‐sandwich core, in which antiparallel β‐strands form a rigid binding groove (Figure S2a). Subfamily 2 (clades 2a and 2b) comprises longer sequences (220–230 residues) with a distinctive protrusion on the right‐wing shoulder. Fine structure alignment using T‐Coffee revealed that conserved residues are predominantly located in surface β‐sheets (Figure S2b).

Gene copy number analysis revealed species‐ and strain‐specific distribution patterns (Figure 3c). At the species level, Pantoea cypripedii retains only clade 1g, whereas Yersinia ruckeri and Dickeya solani each possess two paralogs (clades 1h+ 2a and 1g+ 2b, respectively). Several Pectobacterium species and strains have undergone lineage‐specific expansions. Specifically, P. parvum carries clades 1a, 1b, 1d, and 1e, whereas P. peruviense exhibits a 1f + 2a combination. Strain‐specific patterns were also observed. For example, certain strains of D. oryzae and D. zeae harbour combination 1g+ 2b (Figure 3c); and combinations such as 1a + 1b + 1d + 1e and 1f + 2a are also present in several strains of P. brasiliense , P. carotovorum , and P. versatile. Additionally, clade 1c appears to have evolved specifically in P. carotovorum 2A via tandem duplication (Figure 3c).

PSMB1a Binds Defensive PSMs

Molecular docking against the NCBI PubChem identified 151 potential high‐affinity ligands, predominantly alkaloids (39.73%). Screening of the L6000 Plant Secondary Metabolites (PSMs) Library yielded 1274 candidate ligands enriched in terpenoids (31.5%) and flavonoids (19.3%) (Figure S3). We designated this gene family as PSM‐Binding (PSMB) family, and assigned LOZ86_RS03680 as PSMB1a based on phylogeny (Figure 3b). For experimental validation, we selected two representative compounds: solanidine, a steroidal alkaloid characteristic of solanaceous plants, and salicylic acid (SA), a ubiquitous plant defence signal. His‐tagged PSMB1a was expressed in Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) and purified, with SDS‐PAGE confirming a single band (Figure 3d). Microscale thermophoresis (MST) demonstrated concentration‐dependent binding to solanidine (Kd = 0.103 ± 0.02 μM) and SA (Kd = 9.23 ± 2.35 μM, Figure 3e).

PSMB1a Attenuates Proliferation in P. polare

To assess whether PSMB family contributes to virulence, we adopted a heterologous expression strategy by introducing the representative member PSMB1a into P. polare , a sister species that naturally lacks PSMB homologues. This approach enables functional evaluation in a clean genetic background. Western blot confirmed the expression of PSMB1a:GFP and GFP in transformants (Figure 4a). Growth curve analysis revealed that CM22112PSMB1a:GFP consistently exhibited lower OD600 than CM22112GFP during the 12 h incubation period, with a reduction of 0.03 per hour (p < 0.001) (Figure 4b). These results indicate that PSMB1a partially suppresses proliferation in P. polare .

PSMB1a Enhances P. polare Virulence on Chinese Cabbage

The transformant CM22112PSMB1a:GFP caused larger lesions (106.58 ± 25.74 mm2) compared to CM22112GFP (30.82 ± 15.15 mm2, p < 0.001) (Figure 4c), and yielded higher bacterial recovery from infected tissues (4.73 × 107 CFU vs. 1.33 × 107 CFU, p < 0.001) (Figure 4d). Confocal laser microscopy revealed extensive cellular necrosis and maceration in samples infected with CM22112PSMB1a:GFP, whereas the GFP control exhibited limited intercellular colonization with relatively intact structure (Figure 4e).

PSMB1a Enhances PSM Tolerance in P. polare

To assess whether PSMB1a enhances tolerance to defensive PSMs, we measured the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC), defined as the lowest concentration at which bacterial growth is inhibited, as a key indicator of tolerance. Expression of PSMB1a increased MICs of solanidine and SA from 0.1 mg/mL to 0.2 mg/mL, and coumalic acid from 0.05 mg/mL to 0.2 mg/mL (Figure 4f). Under solanidine stress, CM22112PSMB1a:GFP exhibited enhanced proliferation, exceeding CM22112GFP by 0.02 (OD600) per hour (Figure 4g), demonstrating that PSMB1a confers increased tolerance to defensive PSMs.

Deletion of the PSMB Pathogenicity Island Enhances Proliferation in P. parvum

To further evaluate the HGT origin of PSMB family, we performed codon usage bias analysis and genomic island prediction. In P. parvum , the four PSMB paralogs are organized as a 5.9 kb pathogenicity island (PI) designated PI‐PSMB (Table S2; Figure 5a). The PSMB family exhibited a significantly lower Codon Adaptation Index (CAI) compared to the genomic background (0.62 ± 0.01 vs. 0.71 ± 0.06, p < 0.001), indicating atypical codon usage consistent with horizontally acquired genes.

For mutagenesis, P. parvum YT22221 was selected, as FN20211 failed to transform with exogenous plasmids. To reveal the virulence function of this gene family and circumvent functional redundancy among the multiple copies, the entire PI‐PSMB was deleted. Deletion of PI‐PSMB, YT22221ΔPI‐PSMB, exhibited enhanced proliferation during the exponential growth (4 to 14 h), with an increase of 0.40 in OD₆₀₀ compared to the wild type (WT, Figure 5b). This result aligns with the growth suppression observed in P. polare (section 2.7).

Deletion of PI‐PSMB Attenuates P. parvum Virulence

To assess whether the virulence defect of the ΔPI‐PSMB mutant is consistent across different host plants, we tested its pathogenicity on three plant species. On Chinese cabbage, the YT22221ΔPI‐PSMB mutant caused smaller lesions (2.18 ± 0.80 cm2) compared to YT22221WT (3.33 ± 0.55 cm2, p < 0.01) (Figure 5c). Similarly, on potato stems, lesion lengths were reduced from 43.33 ± 3.51 mm (YT22221WT) to 26.67 ± 5.03 mm (YT22221ΔPI‐PSMB, p < 0.01) (Figure 5d). On radish tuber, the YT22221ΔPI‐PSMB mutant also caused significantly smaller maceration weight (4.98 ± 0.74 g) compared to YT22221WT (6.72 ± 0.77 g, p < 0.001) (Figure 5e). These results demonstrate that the PSMB gene family contributes to full virulence across multiple host species.

Discussion

Although soft rot caused by P. parvum has long been present in agricultural production, unresolved taxonomy has hindered in‐depth study of its pathogenic mechanisms. Through phylogenetic analysis, we reclassified five strains from NCBI GenBank, including four strains isolated from Chinese cabbage in China (Chen et al. 2021) and one strain from oilseed rape ( B. rapa var. oleifera) in Japan (Morohoshi et al. 2019), identifying Chinese cabbage as an important host, consistent with its high virulence observed on this host plant (Figure 1b). It appears that P. parvum has a broader host range in brassica plants when compared to P. polare . On potato, P. parvum displays tissue specificity, causing stem infections comparable in severity to related species (Figure 1c) while showing weak virulence on tubers (Pasanen et al. 2020; Han et al. 2023).

Pectobacterium parvum exhibits considerable genomic plasticity. Despite genome reduction resulting in a smaller core‐genome than in related species (Zoledowska et al. 2018; Mallick et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2022), P. parvum maintains an open pan‐genome enriched with genes likely acquired through HGT and duplications. Such plasticity, also reported in P. brasiliense , may compensate for gene losses and facilitate host adaptation (Jonkheer et al. 2021; Elhalag et al. 2025). Notably, P. parvum uniquely possesses a SPI‐1‐like T3SS, a system rarely present in other related species. However, expression profiling suggests that this T3SS is not functionally associated with plant infection (Figure 2d,e).

Multi‐omic analysis revealed an expansion of the PSMB family (RhiA homologues) in P. parvum , suggesting an important role in pathogenicity. Although RhiA has been hypothesized to participate in plant–rhizobia interactions by binding specific PSMs, direct experimental evidence was limited (Economou et al. 1989; Cubo et al. 1992; Rodelas et al. 1999). In this study, we confirmed that PSMB1a binds solanidine and SA with micromolar affinity. Notably, the binding affinity for solanidine (Kd = 0.103 μM) was approximately two orders of magnitude higher than that for SA (Kd = 9.23 μM), indicating selectivity for specific PSMs. The PSMB family in P. parvum likely originated via HGT. The genomic region forms an AT‐rich PI containing a transposase pseudogene, suggesting transposon‐ or phage‐mediated insertion (Figure 5a). This hypothesis is supported by the significantly lower CAI of PSMB family and the identification of PI‐PSMB as a genomic island. Following its acquisition, gene duplications drove a marked expansion of this family in P. parvum , with three sequential duplication events generating four paralogs, a level of amplification not observed in related genera (Figure 3c).

Although deletion of PI‐PSMB in P. parvum did not completely abolish pathogenicity, it significantly attenuated virulence on potato stems, Chinese cabbage, and radish, indicating that the PSMB family contributes to full virulence rather than serving as essential determinants (Figure 5c–e). The representative member PSMB1a exhibits a dual role depending on the presence of PSM stress. Under PSM stress, PSMB1a enhances PSM tolerance and promotes virulence in P. polare on Chinese cabbage. Under PSM‐free conditions, its expression reduces the basal growth rate of P. polare , consistent with the enhanced growth rate upon PI‐PSMB deletion in P. parvum . These findings suggest a trade‐off between virulence and basal fitness, similar to the SPI‐1 T3SS in S. enterica serovar Typhimurium, which imposes growth retardation in vitro but is indispensable for host infection (Sturm et al. 2011). This trade‐off supports our hypothesis that the growth suppression observed with the PSMB family reflects an adaptive strategy involving investment in virulence functions that are costly outside the host yet critical for successful infection. However, whether the four PSMB paralogs are functionally redundant or ligand‐divergent remains to be determined. Previous studies have characterized an isothiocyanate hydrolase (SaxA) in P. versatile and P. odoriferum that effectively degrades isothiocyanates, the primary defensive PSMs produced by brassicas (van den Bosch et al. 2020). Given the role of PSMB1a in virulence on Chinese cabbage, it is possible that PSMB1a may similarly interact with defensive PSMs in this host, a hypothesis that requires further biochemical validation.

Defensive PSMs can directly affect soft‐rot bacterial growth and viability, as well as indirectly modulate virulence traits (Grupa‐Urbańska et al. 2025). PSMs enriched in wild potato stems and tubers, such as alkaloids, phenolics, amines, and peptides, suppress N‐acyl homoserine (AHL) production in P. brasiliense , leading to reduced expression of PCWDEs and attenuated extracellular enzyme activity (Joshi et al. 2021). Similarly, carvacrol, an antibacterial essential oil, reduces swimming motility, biofilm formation, and extracellular polymeric substance synthesis in P. carotovorum (Gutierrez‐Pacheco et al. 2018). Beyond its role as a central hormone orchestrating plant immune responses, SA can directly bind ExpI and interfere with QS signalling, ultimately reducing virulence (Joshi et al. 2016, 2020). Considering the high affinity of PSMB1a for PSMs, we hypothesize that PSMBs function by sequestering defensive PSMs away from intracellular targets under stress conditions. The expansion of the PSMB family in P. parvum may therefore represent a specialized adaptive strategy to counteract defensive PSMs in a pathogen.

Experimental Procedures

Genomic Characterization and Phylogenetic Analysis

Species identity of strain YT22221 was confirmed by MLSA and citrate utilization assay. Whole‐genome sequencing and genome assembly were performed as previously described (Wang et al. 2022). The final assembly was annotated with the NCBI Prokaryotic Genome Annotation Pipeline. All available Pectobacterium genomes (excluding P. parvum ) were retrieved from GenBank (as of June 2025) and screened by BLASTn of four housekeeping genes (gapA, dnaX, rpoS, and recA). Strains sharing more than 97% nucleotide identity with P. parvum s0421T across all loci were retained. cgMLSA was conducted using PhyloPhlAn v. 3.0.2 to identify phylogenetic markers, and a maximum‐likelihood tree was constructed using RAxML v. 8.2.12, with P. aquaticum and P. carotovorum as outgroups. Taxonomic assignments were validated through ANI analysis in pyani v. 0.2.11 and isDDH using GGDC v. 3.0.

Pathogenicity Assays

To evaluate the virulence of P. parvum in its host plants, strains FN20211 and YT22221 were compared with P. brasiliense , P. versatile, and P. polonicum strains previously characterized for their virulence on tubers (Portier et al. 2019; Waleron et al. 2019; Han et al. 2023). Virulence on Chinese cabbage was assessed using surface‐sterilized petioles inoculated with 5 μL of bacterial suspensions (OD600 = 0.7; 107 CFU/mL), while the control group received saline. Macerated areas were measured at 40 h post‐inoculation (hpi) using ImageJ. Data were analysed with the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by pairwise Wilcoxon tests in R v. 4.5.0. Virulence on potato stems was evaluated using stems of cultivar Xisen 6. Seedlings were inoculated with 10 μL of bacterial suspensions (OD600 = 0.65), with saline as control. Lesion lengths were measured at 48 hpi and analysed by one‐way ANOVA with post hoc tests.

For virulence comparisons between the P. parvum mutant YT22221ΔPI‐PSMB and the wild type YT22221WT, the same procedures were followed except that bacterial inocula were adjusted to an OD600 of 0.1. Macerated areas on Chinese cabbage and lesion lengths on potato stems were measured at 24 hpi, and data were analysed using Student's t‐test. Virulence on radish was assessed using tubers of cultivar Shawo. Healthy radish tubers were surface sterilized, and conical wounds were made using a sterile pipette tip. Each wound was inoculated with 5 μL of bacterial suspension (OD600 = 0.1), with saline as a negative control. The wounds were then sealed with Vaseline. Macerated tissue was weighed at 48 hpi. Data were analysed using Student's t‐test. For comparisons between P. polare transformants CM22112PSMB1a:GFP and CM22112GFP, macerated areas were measured at 48 hpi.

Pan‐Genome Analysis

Pan‐genome analysis was conducted using high‐quality genome assemblies with BUSCO score exceeding 95% (Table S1). OGs and gene duplications were identified using OrthoFinder v. 2.5.5. CSPs were predicted by detecting Sec/Tat signal peptides with SignalP v. 6.0, followed by extracellular localization prediction using DeepLocPro v. 1.0, and exclusion of membrane proteins via DeepTMHMM v. 1.0. For NCSPs, the subset of the proteome lacking Sec, Tat and Lipo signal peptides was analysed using SecretomeP v. 2.0.

RNA Sequencing and Data Analysis

Potato stems were inoculated with P. parvum FN20211 (OD600 = 0.27). Total RNA was extracted from inoculated potato stem tissues collected at 48 hpi using TRIzol, followed by rRNA depletion with Epicentre Ribo‐Zero rRNA Removal Kit and the Zymo‐Seq RiboFree Total RNA Library Kit. Sequencing libraries were prepared using NEBNext Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit, purified with AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter), and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw reads were quality‐trimmed using fastp v. 0.20.1 and aligned to the reference genome (GCF_020971565.1) with HISAT2 v. 2.1.0 in non‐spliced mode. Gene expression was quantified using HTSeq v. 0.12.4, and counts were normalized to transcripts per million (TPM) with recA as internal reference. Genes with TPM higher than recA were defined as highly expressed. KEGG enrichment analysis was conducted using Fisher's exact test (adjusted p < 0.05) in clusterProfiler v. 4.4.4.

RT‐qPCR

For RT‐qPCR validation, an independent set of inoculated stem samples was prepared under the same conditions and collected at 48 hpi. Total RNA was extracted from inoculated stem tissues with the TransZol Up Plus RNA Kit and reverse‐transcribed using the TransScript One‐Step gDNA Removal and cDNA Synthesis SuperMix Kit (TransGen Biotech). Quantitative PCR was carried out on a CFX96 Touch Real‐Time PCR Detection System (Bio‐Rad) using PerfectStart Green qPCR Supermix (TransGen Biotech). Primer specificity was verified by conventional PCR and melting curve analysis. Relative expression levels were normalized to recA.

Sequence Analysis of a Multicopy Gene Family

Protein domains were predicted using SMART, and putative homologues were searched via BLASTp against UniProtKB, NCBI nr, and the EBI Enzyme Portal. To elucidate evolutionary divergence within the family, multiple sequence alignment was performed using MAFFT. The protein alignments were converted to codon‐based nucleotide alignments with PAL2NAL, trimmed using trimAl prior to phylogenetic reconstruction with IQ‐TREE. Protein structures for representative clades and RhiA were predicted using AlphaFold3.

Codon Usage Analysis and Genomic Island Prediction

To evaluate the HGT origin of the PSMB family, codon usage bias was assessed by comparing the CAI of the four PSMB paralogs to that of the genomic background. All protein‐coding sequences were extracted from the genome of P. parvum FN20211. CAI values were calculated using the R package seqinr with the standard bacterial genetic code. Statistical significance was determined using Welch's two‐sample t‐test. Genomic island prediction was performed using IslandViewer v. 4.

In Silico Docking of PSMB1a With PSMs

Molecular docking was performed to screen for potential PSM ligands of PSMB1a. A chemical library was constructed by combining 2847 plant‐derived molecules from NCBI PubChem, 40,789 natural products from the TargetMol L6000 PSMs Library, yielding a total of 43,636 molecules. All chemical structures in SDF format were converted to PDBQT format using Open Babel. The PSMB1a structure predicted by AlphaFold3 was prepared with PyMOL. Docking simulations were conducted with AutoDock Vina with an affinity threshold of −9 kcal/mol to identify high‐affinity ligands.

Prokaryotic Expression of PSMB1a

The PSMB1a coding sequence was amplified from gDNA using primers PSMB1a‐F/R (Table S4). The PSMB1a amplicon was ligated into the linearized pET28a using T4 DNA ligase, generating an N‐terminal 6 × His tag fusion. The recombinant plasmid (pET28a‐His‐PSMB1a) was transformed into Escherichia coli DH5α via heat‐shock, then into E. coli BL21(DE3) for protein expression. Cultures (OD600 = 0.6) were induced with 0.2 mM IPTG and incubated at 25°C with shaking at 180 rpm for 16 h. Bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation, lysed by ultrasonication on ice, and PSMB1a was purified using His‐Tagged Protein Purification Kit (CWBIO), followed by dialysis. Protein concentration was determined by BCA assay, and purity was assessed by SDS‐PAGE.

MST Binding Assays

Purified PSMB1a was labelled with RED‐NHS dye and incubated for 30 min. Labelled protein was purified using a desalting column, diluted 3‐fold with phosphate‐buffered saline with Tween 20 (PBST), and loaded into Mo‐K022 Monolith capillaries. Solanidine and SA were prepared as 2 mM stock solutions in PBST. A 1:1 serial dilution of each ligand was prepared. Subsequently, 10 μL of labelled PSMB1a was added to each tube and incubated at room temperature for 15 min. Samples were loaded into capillaries, and MST measurements were performed on a Monolith NT.115 system with automatic excitation power and medium MST power. Dissociation constant (Kd) was calculated by the system software.

Heterologous Expression of PSMB1a in P. polare

The coding sequence of PSMB1a was amplified from P. parvum gDNA using primers PSMB1a‐GFP‐F/R (Table S4) and fused with GFP at the C‐terminus. Fragments were assembled into linearized pBBR1MCS2 (digested with SmaI) using the Gibson Assembly Cloning Kit (TransGen Biotech). The constructed plasmids, pBBR1‐PSMB1a:GFP and pBBR1‐GFP, were transformed into E. coli S17‐1λ(pir) and introduced into P. polare CM22112 by conjugation. Positive transformants were verified by PCR and sequencing (Table S4) and designated CM22112PSMB1a:GFP and CM22112GFP. For western blot analysis, transformants were grown to an OD600 of 0.8, harvested, and lysed by ultrasonication. Proteins were separated by SDS‐PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, blocked with 5% skim milk, and incubated with primary antibody followed by secondary antibody. Chemiluminescent signals were detected using ECL reagents. Membranes were treated with alkaline stripping solution and reprobed with anti‐GAPDH antibody as an internal control.

Growth Curve Analysis

The effect of PSMB family on bacterial growth was evaluated using a Scientz MGC‐200 Microbial Growth Curve Analyser. Overnight Luria Bertani (LB) broth cultures of P. polare transformants or P. parvum mutants were diluted into an initial OD600 of 0.01. Aliquots (200 μL) of each bacterial suspension were transferred to 96‐well plates, with 16 replicates per strain, and LB served as blank control. To assess PSMB1a‐mediated tolerance to solanidine, growth assays were conducted in LB supplemented with 2 mM solanidine, with 12 replicates per treatment. PSM‐supplemented medium without bacteria was used as a negative control. Growth data were analysed using a linear mixed‐effects model in R.

Effect of PSMB1a on Virulence of P. polare

The effect of PSMB1a on virulence was evaluated by comparing the maceration of CM22112PSMB1a:GFP and CM22112GFP on Chinese cabbage, following the procedures described in section 4.2. Tissue samples were excised from the junction between macerated and healthy regions, homogenized in LB broth, incubated for 20 min, and serially diluted to 10−3, 10−4 and 10−5. A 50 μL aliquot from each dilution was plated on LB agar, and colonies were counted at 48 hpi. Petiole samples were examined by confocal laser microscopy. GFP‐tagged bacteria were visualized under fluorescence with excitation and emission at 488 nm and 510 nm, while bright‐field imaging simultaneously captured host cellular integrity.

MIC Assays

MICs of solanidine, SA, and coumalic acid against transformants were determined to evaluate PSMB1a‐mediated tolerance to PSMs. Single colonies were suspended in sterile water, streaked on Mueller–Hinton agar. After 48 h of incubation, colonies were resuspended in saline and adjusted to 0.5 McFarland standard and diluted 1:100 v/v. Broth microdilution assays were performed in 96‐well plates using twofold serial dilutions from 0.8 to 0.025 mg/mL. Column 8 contained positive control with bacterial suspension only and negative control with buffer only. Each well received 100 μL of bacterial suspension and 10 μL resazurin, followed by incubation at 28°C for 48 h. The MIC was defined as the lowest concentration that prevented a colour change to red.

Targeted Mutagenesis

The PI‐PSMB in P. parvum YT22221, containing four PSMB paralogs (NV347_RS03420, NV347_RS03430, NV347_RS03435 and NV347_RS03440) and an IS256 family transposase pseudogene (NV347_RS03425), was deleted using λRed recombination as described (van den Bosch et al. 2020). Due to the natural ampicillin resistance of YT22221, the tetracycline‐resistant helper plasmid pKD46tet was introduced into electrocompetent cells. To delete PI‐PSMB, homology arms of 50 bp flanking the target region were fused to primers amplifying a chloramphenicol resistance cassette from pKD3 (Table S4). The resulting fragment was electroporated into YT22221 cells carrying pKD46tet, induced with 0.1% L‐arabinose. Recombinants were selected on LB agar plates supplemented with chloramphenicol. The pKD46tet was eliminated by culturing positive transformants at 37°C with shaking for 24 h. Deletion of PI‐PSMB and loss of pKD46tet were confirmed by PCR and sequencing, yielding mutant YT22221ΔPI‐PSMB.
