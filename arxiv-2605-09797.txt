A Classical Two-Part First-Threshold Proof of Global Smoothness for Navier–Stokes: Axisymmetric Swirl Closure and Full-System Reduction
Rishad Shahmurov

Abstract
. We prove global smooth continuation for smooth finite-energy solutions of the three-dimensional incompressible Navier–Stokes equations by a two-part first-threshold argument. Part I proves the axisymmetric-with-swirl theorem in the exact five-dimensional lifted formulation. The central variables are the lifted vorticity ratio G = ω θ / r G=\omega_{\theta}/r , the regularized swirl derivative F = u θ / r F=u^{\theta}/r , and the squared source density H = F 2 H=F^{2} . In these variables the derivative source in the G G -equation and the compressive feedback generated by the recovered strain U = u r / r U=u^{r}/r form a single pair-transfer mechanism. The proof combines localized energy identities, Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev and Sobolev interpolation estimates, pair-threshold absorption, finite-overlap descendant exclusion, localized temporal source-to-score estimates, compactness of endpoint profiles, projected Pohozaev–Morawetz strictness, and an auxiliary recovery estimate for F F . Part II gives a full three-dimensional finite-threshold front-end. Starting from a hypothetical singular terminal packet, it removes leakage, shell, pressure, tail, fragmentation, passive-strain, angular phase-lock, and transfer-active temporal channels by finite-overlap descendants or strict terminal loss. A zero final defect forces the active frame measure into either a constant-frame locally two-dimensional class or a physical azimuthal orbit around one fixed axis. The first alternative is excluded by the classical two-dimensional Navier–Stokes theory, and the second is precisely the axisymmetric-with-swirl class proved in Part I. The analytic ingredients are localized energy inequalities, Caccioppoli estimates, Calderón–Zygmund recovery, Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev and Sobolev interpolation, parabolic compactness, finite-dimensional compactness, finite-overlap covering, and projected Pohozaev identities. All full-system terms used in Part II are defined before the estimates in which they appear.

1.
Introduction

We consider the three-dimensional incompressible Navier–Stokes equations

(1)

\partial_{t}u+u\cdot\nabla u+\nabla p=\Delta u,\qquad\nabla\cdot u=0,

for smooth finite-energy divergence-free initial data on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The proof is divided into two parts. Part I treats the axisymmetric-with-swirl class in full detail. Part II treats the full three-dimensional front-end and reduces every terminal class to either the axisymmetric theorem of Part I or the classical two-dimensional theory.

For the axisymmetric class we write, in cylindrical variables,

u=u^{r}(r,z,t)e_{r}+u^{\theta}(r,z,t)e_{\theta}+u^{z}(r,z,t)e_{z}.

The central quantities are

\Gamma=ru^{\theta},\qquad F=\Gamma_{\!e}=\frac{\Gamma}{r^{2}}=\frac{u^{\theta}}{r},\qquad H=F^{2},\qquad G=\frac{\omega_{\theta}}{r}.

The five-dimensional lift is not an additional physical model. It is the exact representation of an axisymmetric scalar, written as

\widetilde{f}(y,z)=f(|y|,z),\qquad y\in\mathbb{R}^{4}.

In this representation the radial measure is
d\mu_{5}=r^{3}drdz
, and the scalar radial Laplacian is

\Delta_{5}=\partial_{r}^{2}+\frac{3}{r}\partial_{r}+\partial_{z}^{2}.

The formal construction, the measure identity, the lifted balls
B_{R}^{\rm axis}
, and the terminal parabolic cylinders are specified in
Section

2
; the displayed variables are the basic quantities used throughout Part I.

The difficulty is the derivative source in the lifted vorticity equation,

(2)

D_{t}G=\Delta_{5}G+\partial_{z}H,\qquad D_{t}=\partial_{t}+u^{r}\partial_{r}+u^{z}\partial_{z}.

A direct energy estimate for
G
produces the recovered-strain transfer
\int U[G]G^{2}
and the oriented source work
-\int H\partial_{z}G
, while the entropy identity for
H
produces the signed nonlocal compressive term
-\int U[G]H^{2}
. The main point of Part I is that these terms should not be separated into “principal” and “error” pieces. They form a single pair-transfer mechanism for
(G,H)
. The source closure is therefore built on
(G,H)
, while the physical swirl derivative needed for continuation is recovered later from the lower-order equation for
F
. The principal pair visibility
\mathbb{A}_{\chi}[G,H]
, the pair transfer
\mathbb{J}_{\chi}[G,H]
, the corrected score
\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}
, and the lower-order budget
\mathbb{E}_{\rm dir}
are defined in
Section

2
. Informally,
\mathbb{A}_{\chi}
is the localized dissipation/entropy visibility,
\mathbb{J}_{\chi}
is the nonlinear work done by the recovered strain, and
\mathbb{E}_{\rm dir}
is the sum of leakage, tail, pressure, edge, frequency, and descendant terms which are not principal.

Main Theorem 1.1

(Global axisymmetric continuation)
.

Let
u_{0}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
be divergence-free, axisymmetric, and of finite energy. Let
u
be the corresponding smooth axisymmetric Navier–Stokes solution on its maximal smooth interval
[0,T_{*})
. Then
T_{*}=\infty
.

Main Theorem 1.2

(Full three-dimensional continuation)
.

Let
u_{0}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
be divergence-free and of finite energy. Let
u
be the corresponding smooth Navier–Stokes solution on its maximal smooth interval
[0,T_{*})
. Then
T_{*}=\infty
.

1.3.
The terminal contradiction mechanism in Part I

The axisymmetric proof is organized around the following terminal contradiction mechanism. A hypothetical loss of smooth continuation produces a canonical first-threshold terminal packet. On that packet the localized energy and entropy identities give the coefficient-one terminal balance

\mathbb{A}_{\chi}[G,H]\leq\mathbb{J}_{\chi}[G,H]+\mathbb{E}_{\rm dir}.

Thus the nonlinear transfer
\mathbb{J}_{\chi}
is the only possible mechanism that can keep the terminal visibility
\mathbb{A}_{\chi}
alive. To contradict this balance one needs a strict reverse estimate

|\mathbb{J}_{\chi}[G,H]|\leq\theta\,\mathbb{A}_{\chi}[G,H]+C\mathbb{E}_{\rm dir},\qquad 0<\theta<1.

The strict number
\theta<1
is the decisive gain. After terminal errors are shown to be a small fraction of
\mathbb{A}_{\chi}
, the two displayed inequalities force

\mathbb{A}_{\chi}\leq\frac{1+\theta}{2}\mathbb{A}_{\chi},

which is impossible for a nonzero terminal packet. If the best bound had
\theta=1
, the same argument would only read
\mathbb{A}_{\chi}\leq\mathbb{A}_{\chi}+\text{errors}
, which contains no contradiction. This is why the strict bridge, not merely a boundedness estimate, is the core of Part I.

1.4.
What the classical estimates prove, and where they stop

The classical part of Part I gives the soft bridge

|\mathbb{J}_{\chi}[G,H]|\leq\varepsilon\mathbb{A}_{\chi}[G,H]+C_{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{M}_{G}^{3}+\mathbb{M}_{H}^{3})+C\mathbb{E}_{\rm dir},

where the local masses
\mathbb{M}_{G},\mathbb{M}_{H}
are defined in
Section

2
. This estimate is the analogue of a localized energy or entropy inequality with a nonlinear work term: HLS controls the recovered strain, Sobolev controls the active density, and Young’s inequality absorbs the subcritical part into the left-hand dissipation. It removes every packet whose local mass remainder is small relative to its visibility; this is the subcritical regime formalized in
Section

2.4
.

The only case not removed by this classical estimate is the scale-normalized critical packet, where the mass term has exactly the same scaling as the visibility. In that case ordinary harmonic analysis cannot see enough of the shape of the packet to force a coefficient below one. Sign cancellation is unavailable because
U[G]
changes sign and can align with either
G^{2}
or
H^{2}
. A direct global
L^{4}
-estimate for the swirl source is also insufficient because the entropy identity for
H
contains the same compressive term
\int U[G]H^{2}
. The proof therefore treats the critical case by combining finite-stage compactness with endpoint homogeneity. The temporal descendant and localized inverse-potential estimates rule out transfer-active spikes and give a strong endpoint profile. The amplitude balance is then a quotient homogeneity identity for that profile, while the spatial-dilation balance is the zero-error Pohozaev–Morawetz identity. Their incompatible homogeneities force a strict terminal bridge.

1.5.
Literature, standard tools, and relation with the first-threshold approach

The starting point is Leray’s construction of finite-energy weak solutions and the classical continuation philosophy behind the Beale–Kato–Majda criterion

We do not use a Caffarelli–Kohn–Nirenberg epsilon-regularity theorem as a black-box estimate in the proof below; rather, the argument uses localized energy identities and Caccioppoli-type bookkeeping in the same local spirit. Thus no step of the proof rests on an unquoted CKN regularity criterion.

The axisymmetric problem has a special history. In the no-swirl case, global regularity was proved by Ladyzhenskaya and independently by Ukhovskii–Yudovich
[

This is why the axisymmetric swirl problem is often treated as a central testing ground for three-dimensional regularity methods: it preserves a genuine stretching mechanism and a singular cylindrical geometry, but it still permits the exact scalar variables
\Gamma,G,F,H
and the five-dimensional recovery formula used here.

1.6.
Why endpoint strictness is needed

The same obstruction can be stated in the notation used throughout the proof. The classical estimates are intentionally pushed first, and they give the sharp soft bound

|\mathbb{J}_{\chi}[G,H]|\leq\varepsilon\mathbb{A}_{\chi}[G,H]+C_{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{M}_{G}^{3}+\mathbb{M}_{H}^{3})+C\mathbb{E}_{\rm dir}.

This estimate is indispensable because it removes all subcritical configurations by ordinary HLS, Sobolev, interpolation, and Young inequalities. However, it cannot by itself give a coefficient strictly smaller than one in the remaining normalized critical case. The strict inequality is necessary because the terminal balance has the form

\mathbb{A}_{\chi}\leq\mathbb{J}_{\chi}+\text{lower-order errors}.

To obtain a contradiction one must insert an estimate

|\mathbb{J}_{\chi}|\leq\theta\mathbb{A}_{\chi}+\text{lower-order errors},\qquad\theta<1,

and then absorb the right-hand principal term back into the left. If the best coefficient were merely
\theta=1
, the inequality would reduce to
\mathbb{A}_{\chi}\leq\mathbb{A}_{\chi}+\text{errors}
, which gives no contradiction and no gain. The reason classical estimates stop at this point is scaling: the pair transfer is cubic in the active amplitude, while the pair visibility is quadratic. Any global HLS or Gagliardo–Nirenberg estimate must be valid for the entire admissible function class; it therefore produces a universal constant but cannot distinguish the precise terminal coherent profile from all other shapes strongly enough to force a strict coefficient below one. This is a familiar limitation of norm inequalities: they measure size, integrability, and differentiability, but not the full geometric arrangement of a possible singular packet. In the present problem the decisive information includes the relative sign of the recovered strain, the parity of the source density, the alignment of the active core with the axis, and the way the two cubic transfers interact under dilation. These features are invisible to a purely scalar norm bound, just as an
L^{p}
norm in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
cannot by itself distinguish two vector fields with the same amplitude distribution but different vorticity alignment or parity.

This is the reason a terminal strictness step is introduced. The endpoint part of the proof does not replace the classical estimates; it begins only after those estimates have reached their scale-neutral endpoint. In the normalized terminal regime, ordinary inequalities leave open the possibility of a coefficient-one saturator. The finite-stage Pohozaev–Morawetz argument tests that potential saturator on the original smooth terminal packets: the amplitude homogeneity of
\int G^{2}U[G]
, the amplitude homogeneity of
\int U[G]H^{2}
, and the spatial dilation homogeneity of the Dirichlet visibilities give two incompatible identities up to the terminal error budget. Thus the localized Pohozaev–Morawetz strictness theorem is the mechanism that breaks the scaling tie left by classical harmonic analysis.

A detailed obstruction to replacing this step by a purely quadratic source-potential energy is given in
Theorem

B.2
and
Appendix

B
. That appendix shows, at the level of the exact lifted model, that the natural bilinear cross energy improves the linear derivative source but necessarily creates a trilinear term of the wrong amplitude homogeneity. This explains why the proof uses terminal Pohozaev–Morawetz strictness at the final endpoint rather than treating the issue as a matter of presentation.

The detailed dictionary and control mechanism for each term are given in Section
7.1
, immediately before the terminal contradiction. This convention fixes the notation:

denotes cutoff/collar leakage,
\mathcal{P}_{\rm tail}

\mathcal{R}_{\rm low}

2.3.
Common analytic estimates

The proof uses three standard analytic estimates repeatedly. We state here exactly what is meant in order to avoid later shorthand.

(1)

Order
-1
recovery.
The operator
U[G]=-\partial_{z}(-\Delta_{5})^{-1}G
is a Riesz potential of order one followed by a harmless Riesz transform in the lifted five-dimensional variables. Thus, whenever
1<p<5
and
1/q=1/p-1/5
,

\|U[G]\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{5})}\leq C_{p}\|G\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{5})}.

In particular,
p=2
gives
q=10/3
, which is the exponent used in the soft bridge.

(2)

Five-dimensional Sobolev.
For compactly supported lifted functions,

\|f\|_{L^{10/3}(\mathbb{R}^{5})}\leq C\|\nabla_{5}f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{5})}.

This is the endpoint Sobolev embedding
\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{5})\hookrightarrow L^{10/3}(\mathbb{R}^{5})
.

(3)

Soft absorption by Young.
Whenever an estimate has the scale-critical form

|\mathcal{T}|\leq CM^{3/4}V^{3/4},

we use Young’s inequality to write

|\mathcal{T}|\leq\varepsilon V+C_{\varepsilon}M^{3}.

The first term is absorbed into visibility; the second term is exactly the mass remainder that forces the later compact-critical reduction.

(Pair visibility, pair transfer, and local mass remainders)
.

Let

\chi

\mathbb{A}_{\chi}[G,H;I]=\mathbb{V}_{\chi}[G;I]+\beta\mathbb{W}_{\chi}[H;I],

\mathbb{V}_{\chi}

\mathbb{W}_{\chi}

\mathbb{J}_{\chi}[G,H;I]=\mathbb{T}_{G,\chi}[G;I]-\alpha\mathbb{T}_{H,\chi}[G,H;I],

\mathbb{T}_{G,\chi}[G;I]=\iint_{I}\chi^{2}G^{2}U[G]\,d\mu_{5}dt,\qquad\mathbb{T}_{H,\chi}[G,H;I]=\iint_{I}\chi^{2}U[G]H^{2}\,d\mu_{5}dt.

Definition 2.2

.

Fix a large number

\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}(z_{0},t)\geq Q_{*},\qquad\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}(z_{0},t)=\lambda\int_{B^{\rm axis}_{\lambda}(z_{0})}G(t)^{2}\,d\mu_{5}.

2.5.
Analytic conventions: CZ, Riesz potentials, and HLS

The operator
(-\Delta_{5})^{-1}
is the Newtonian potential in the lifted five-dimensional variables. Its first derivatives are Riesz potentials of order one. We use the abbreviation CZ for Calderón–Zygmund singular integral estimates, although the most important estimate here is the order
-1
Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev estimate. In dimension five, the Riesz potential
I_{1}=(-\Delta_{5})^{-1/2}
satisfies

\|I_{1}f\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{5})}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{5})},\qquad\frac{1}{q}=\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{5},\qquad 1<p<5.

Taking
p=2
gives
q=10/3
. Since
U[f]=-\partial_{z}(-\Delta_{5})^{-1}f
has the same order as
I_{1}f
, we use

\|U[f]\|_{L^{10/3}(d\mu_{5})}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{2}(d\mu_{5})}.

All such estimates are applied to lifted
SO(4)
-radial functions; the constants are the standard five-dimensional constants, up to the harmless factor
|\mathbb{S}^{3}|
coming from radial integration.

2.6.
Why the five-dimensional corridor is used

The lift is not a change of the physical problem. It is an exact way to write the singular cylindrical weights as ordinary radial weights. In three-dimensional cylindrical variables, the axisymmetric equations contain terms such as
r^{-1}\partial_{r}
,
u^{r}/r
, and
\omega_{\theta}/r

-1
recovered strain, and a scalar parabolic source system.

For any scalar

\widetilde{f}(y,z)=f(|y|,z),\qquad y\in\mathbb{R}^{4}.

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}\times\mathbb{R}}\widetilde{f}(y,z)\,dy\,dz=|\mathbb{S}^{3}|\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(r,z)r^{3}\,dr\,dz.

The standard axisymmetric identities give

Also

The recovered strain is

This is a five-dimensional order

\|U[f]\|_{L^{10/3}(d\mu_{5})}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{2}(d\mu_{5})}.

3.
Localized pair identities

Let
I
be a time interval and let
\chi
be a smooth cutoff supported in a lifted axis ball. Define the pair visibilities

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{\chi}[G;I]

\displaystyle=\int_{I}\int\chi^{2}|\nabla_{5}G|^{2}\,d\mu_{5}dt+\int_{I}\int|\nabla_{5}\chi|^{2}G^{2}\,d\mu_{5}dt,

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}_{\chi}[H;I]

\displaystyle=\int_{I}\int\chi^{2}|\nabla_{5}H|^{2}\,d\mu_{5}dt+\int_{I}\int|\nabla_{5}\chi|^{2}H^{2}\,d\mu_{5}dt,

where
H=F^{2}
. The pair visibility is

(8)

\mathbb{A}_{\chi}[G,H;I]=\mathbb{V}_{\chi}[G;I]+\beta\mathbb{W}_{\chi}[H;I]

with
\beta>0
fixed later. Define the principal pair transfers

(9)

\mathbb{T}_{G,\chi}[G;I]=\int_{I}\int\chi^{2}G^{2}U[G],\qquad\mathbb{T}_{H,\chi}[G,H;I]=\int_{I}\int\chi^{2}U[G]H^{2},

where all integrals are with respect to
d\mu_{5}dt
. Set

(10)

\mathbb{J}_{\chi}[G,H;I]=\mathbb{T}_{G,\chi}[G;I]-\alpha\mathbb{T}_{H,\chi}[G,H;I].

Lemma 3.1

(Localized
G
-identity)
.

For every cutoff
\chi
,

(11)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int\chi^{2}G^{2}(t_{2})-\frac{1}{2}\int\chi^{2}G^{2}(t_{1})+\int_{I}\int\chi^{2}|\nabla_{5}G|^{2}

(12)

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{I}\int\chi^{2}U[G]G^{2}-\int_{I}\int\chi^{2}H\partial_{z}G+\mathcal{R}_{G}[\chi;I],

where

|\mathcal{R}_{G}[\chi;I]|\leq C\int_{I}\int|\nabla_{5}\chi|^{2}G^{2}+C\int_{I}\int(|\partial_{t}\chi|+|u\cdot\nabla\chi|)\chi G^{2}+C\int_{I}\int\chi^{2}H^{2}.

Proof.

We give the computation because the lifted divergence is one of the main bookkeeping points. Multiply (
5
) by
\chi^{2}G
and integrate with respect to
d\mu_{5}
. The time derivative gives

\int\chi^{2}G\partial_{t}G\,d\mu_{5}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int\chi^{2}G^{2}\,d\mu_{5}-\int\chi\partial_{t}\chi\,G^{2}\,d\mu_{5}.

For the diffusion term,

\int\chi^{2}G\Delta_{5}G\,d\mu_{5}=-\int\chi^{2}|\nabla_{5}G|^{2}\,d\mu_{5}-2\int\chi G\nabla_{5}\chi\cdot\nabla_{5}G\,d\mu_{5},

and the last term is bounded by

\frac{1}{4}\int\chi^{2}|\nabla_{5}G|^{2}\,d\mu_{5}+C\int|\nabla_{5}\chi|^{2}G^{2}\,d\mu_{5}.

The meridional transport is divergence-free in the physical measure
r\,drdz
, but in the lifted measure it satisfies

\operatorname{div}_{5}u=\partial_{r}u^{r}+\frac{3}{r}u^{r}+\partial_{z}u^{z}=2\frac{u^{r}}{r}=2U.

Therefore

\int\chi^{2}G\,u\cdot\nabla_{5}G\,d\mu_{5}=\frac{1}{2}\int\chi^{2}u\cdot\nabla_{5}(G^{2})\,d\mu_{5}=-\int\chi G^{2}u\cdot\nabla_{5}\chi\,d\mu_{5}-\int\chi^{2}UG^{2}\,d\mu_{5}.

After moving the transport contribution to the right-hand side, the final term contributes

\int\chi^{2}U[G]G^{2}
, which is precisely
\mathbb{T}_{G,\chi}
. The source term gives

\int\chi^{2}G\partial_{z}H\,d\mu_{5}=-\int\chi^{2}H\partial_{z}G\,d\mu_{5}-2\int\chi HG\partial_{z}\chi\,d\mu_{5}.

The second term is a cutoff-source contribution and is placed in
\mathcal{R}_{G}
, where it is estimated by Cauchy–Schwarz and Young against
\int|\nabla_{5}\chi|^{2}G^{2}
and the local
H^{2}
-mass channel. Integrating in time over
I=[t_{1},t_{2}]
gives (
12
) with the displayed remainder bound.
∎

Lemma 3.2

(Localized
H=F^{2}
-entropy)
.

Let
H=F^{2}
. Then

(13)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int\chi^{2}H^{2}(t_{2})-\frac{1}{2}\int\chi^{2}H^{2}(t_{1})+c_{H}\int_{I}\int\chi^{2}|\nabla_{5}H|^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad=-c_{U}\int_{I}\int\chi^{2}U[G]H^{2}+\mathcal{R}_{H}[\chi;I],

where

are universal constants and

|\mathcal{R}_{H}[\chi;I]|\leq C\int_{I}\int|\nabla_{5}\chi|^{2}H^{2}+C\int_{I}\int(|\partial_{t}\chi|+|u\cdot\nabla\chi|)\chi H^{2}.

Proof.

Since
H=F^{2}
, equation (
4

D_{t}H=2FD_{t}F=\Delta_{5}H-2|\nabla_{5}F|^{2}-4UH.

Multiplying by

\int\chi^{2}H\Delta_{5}H\,d\mu_{5}=-\int\chi^{2}|\nabla_{5}H|^{2}\,d\mu_{5}-2\int\chi H\nabla_{5}\chi\cdot\nabla_{5}H\,d\mu_{5},

where the second term is bounded by

\frac{1}{4}\int\chi^{2}|\nabla_{5}H|^{2}\,d\mu_{5}+C\int|\nabla_{5}\chi|^{2}H^{2}\,d\mu_{5}.

The term
-2\chi^{2}H|\nabla_{5}F|^{2}
is nonpositive and is discarded. The compressive part gives the principal source-density transfer

The lifted transport term is handled using

\int\chi^{2}H\,u\cdot\nabla_{5}H\,d\mu_{5}=-\int\chi H^{2}u\cdot\nabla_{5}\chi\,d\mu_{5}-\int\chi^{2}UH^{2}\,d\mu_{5}.

Combining this contribution with the explicit

∎

Proposition 3.3

(Coefficient-calibrated pair balance)
.

There exist fixed positive constants
\alpha,\beta
such that every terminal cylinder satisfies

(15)

\mathbb{A}_{\chi}[G,H;I]\leq\mathbb{J}_{\chi}[G,H;I]+C\mathbb{E}_{\rm dir}(I)+C\mathbb{B}_{\rm end}(I).

Here
\mathbb{E}_{\rm dir}
contains cutoff, collar, edge, pressure, and exterior-tail terms, and
\mathbb{B}_{\rm end}
contains the two endpoint masses of
\chi G
and
\chi H
.

Proof.

Add (
12
) to a positive multiple of (
14
). Choose the multiplier so the coefficient of
\int\chi^{2}|\nabla H|^{2}
on the left is
\beta
, and define
\alpha>0
as the corresponding coefficient of the signed principal transfer
-\int\chi^{2}UH^{2}
. Together with the
G
-transport contribution
\int\chi^{2}U[G]G^{2}
, this gives
\mathbb{J}_{\chi}=\mathbb{T}_{G,\chi}-\alpha\mathbb{T}_{H,\chi}
. The oriented derivative source work

\left|\int\chi^{2}H\partial_{z}G\right|\leq\delta\int\chi^{2}|\nabla G|^{2}+C_{\delta}\int\chi^{2}H^{2},

∎

4.

Lemma 4.1

(Soft bridge for the pair transfer)
.

Let
\chi_{1}\equiv 1
on
\operatorname{supp}\chi
. For every
\varepsilon>0
,

(16)

\left\lvert\mathbb{J}_{\chi}[G,H;I]\right\rvert\leq\varepsilon\mathbb{A}_{\chi}[G,H;I]+C_{\varepsilon}\int_{I}\Big(\mathcal{M}_{G,\chi_{1}}(t)^{3}+\mathcal{M}_{H,\chi_{1}}(t)^{3}\Big)dt+C\mathbb{E}_{\rm dir}(I),

where

\mathcal{M}_{G,\chi_{1}}(t)=\int\chi_{1}^{2}G^{2}(t)\,d\mu_{5},\qquad\mathcal{M}_{H,\chi_{1}}(t)=\int\chi_{1}^{2}H^{2}(t)\,d\mu_{5}.

Proof.

The estimate for
\mathbb{T}_{G}
follows from HLS, Hölder, interpolation, Sobolev, and Young:

\int\chi^{2}G^{2}U[\chi_{1}G]\leq\|U[\chi_{1}G]\|_{10/3}\|\chi G\|_{20/7}^{2}\leq C\|\chi_{1}G\|_{2}^{3/2}\|\nabla(\chi G)\|_{2}^{3/2}

\leq\varepsilon\|\nabla(\chi G)\|_{2}^{2}+C_{\varepsilon}\|\chi_{1}G\|_{2}^{6}.

The exterior part
U[(1-\chi_{1})G]
is estimated by dyadic shell summation and is part of
\mathbb{E}_{\rm dir}
. The
H
-transfer is identical, with
H
replacing
G
:

\int\chi^{2}H^{2}U[\chi_{1}G]\leq\|U[\chi_{1}G]\|_{10/3}\|\chi H\|_{20/7}^{2}.

This is bounded by
\varepsilon\mathbb{W}_{\chi}[H]+C_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{M}_{H,\chi_{1}}^{3}+C\mathbb{E}_{\rm dir}
after the same interpolation and the terminal mixed-mass inequality; the mixed factor involving
G
is absorbed by Young into the two displayed mass terms. Summing the two estimates proves (
16
).
∎

5.
Endpoint compactness and Pohozaev–Morawetz strictness

The subcritical alternatives were removed in
Section

4
. The remaining normalized terminal case is not handled by an ordinary finite-stage energy identity: testing the Navier–Stokes equations against
G
and
H
gives the coefficient-one terminal balance, not the homogeneity identity
3\mathbb{J}=2\mathbb{A}
. The coefficients
3
and
2
arise only after passing to an endpoint profile which is stationary for the scale-invariant pair-transfer quotient. The role of the finite-stage PDE estimates is therefore different and more classical: they give compactness and rule out temporal or frequency defects. Once this is done, the final strictness calculation is an elementary amplitude/dilation homogeneity argument on an actual strong limit
(g,h)
, not on a measure-valued relaxed profile.

Let

\mathbb{A}[g,h]=V[g]+\beta W[h],\qquad\mathbb{J}[g,h]=J_{1}[g]-\alpha J_{2}[g,h],

where, on the unit terminal core,

V[g]=\int\chi^{2}|\nabla_{5}g|^{2}+\int|\nabla_{5}\chi|^{2}g^{2},\qquad W[h]=\int\chi^{2}|\nabla_{5}h|^{2}+\int|\nabla_{5}\chi|^{2}h^{2},

J_{1}[g]=\int\chi^{2}g^{2}U[g],\qquad J_{2}[g,h]=\int\chi^{2}U[g]h^{2}.

Here
h
denotes the source density
H=F^{2}
. The coefficient
\alpha
is the fixed coefficient chosen in the pair terminal balance, and
\beta
is the corresponding source-entropy weight. The precise constants are immaterial for the algebra below, provided the same constants are used in the terminal balance and in the quotient.

We use the abbreviation
\mathrm{TSD}
for the Temporal Source-to-Score Descendant mechanism defined in the dictionary of
Section

2
. In the next proposition, TSD compactness means compactness after excluding every transfer-active temporal source burst by that descendant mechanism.

Proposition 5.1

(Temporal Source-to-Score Descendant (TSD) compactness for endpoint-saturating packets)
.

Let
(G_{n},H_{n})
be a normalized compact-critical first-threshold terminal sequence satisfying

\mathbb{A}_{\chi}[G_{n},H_{n};I_{n}]=1,\qquad\mathbb{E}_{\rm dir}[G_{n},H_{n};I_{n}]+\mathbb{B}_{\rm end}[G_{n},H_{n};I_{n}]\to 0,

and suppose that the soft bridge is saturated in the sense that

\mathbb{J}_{\chi}[G_{n},H_{n};I_{n}]\to\Lambda_{*}>0.

After parabolic normalization to the unit terminal cylinder, there is a subsequence and functions
(g,h)
such that

G_{n}\to g,\qquad H_{n}\to h

strongly in the local spaces needed to pass to the pair transfer and visibility. In particular,

\mathbb{A}[g,h]=1,\qquad\mathbb{J}[g,h]=\Lambda_{*}.

Moreover no square-density or temporal transfer defect remains in the limit.

Proof.

The uniform visibility bound gives weak compactness of
G_{n}
and
H_{n}
in the localized energy spaces, and the negative-Sobolev time-compactness estimate in
Appendix

A
gives strong local compactness away from named error channels. It remains only to exclude concentration of the transfer density
H_{n}^{2}U[G_{n}]
on small time intervals or in high-frequency square-density packets. The temporal detector is applied to the actual derivative source
\partial_{z}H_{n}
; square-density concentration is first reduced either to a linear
U[G_{n}]H_{n}
response or to a named source-amplitude descendant.

Assume that such a concentration persists. Then there are subintervals
I^{\prime}_{n}
, axis balls
B_{\rho_{n}}^{\rm axis}(z^{\prime}_{n})
, and cutoffs
\eta_{n}
with
|I^{\prime}_{n}|\simeq\rho_{n}^{2}
such that, after choosing an orientation sign,

\left|\iint_{I^{\prime}_{n}\times B_{\rho_{n}}^{\rm axis}(z^{\prime}_{n})}\eta_{n}^{2}H_{n}^{2}U[G_{n}]\,d\mu_{5}dt\right|\geq c_{0}>0.

The localized inverse-potential identity of
Appendix

A
then implies that either the localized negative energy

\mathcal{N}_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int\eta_{n}^{2}G_{n}(-\Delta_{5})^{-1}(\eta_{n}^{2}G_{n})\,d\mu_{5}

has a positive endpoint response, or the localized
L^{2}
-mass of
G_{n}
is positive on a smaller parabolic cylinder. By the inverse inequality for the Dirichlet inverse and the time-averaging argument in
Appendix

A
, this gives a descendant packet with positive corrected score. The descendant is strictly smaller in the canonical first-threshold order, contradicting minimality. Hence transfer-active temporal and frequency concentration cannot occur.

Consequently the transfer measures are uniformly absolutely continuous in time and space relative to the finite-stage packet scale. Vitali convergence, combined with the strong local convergence of
U[G_{n}]
supplied by the order
-1
recovery estimate, gives

\mathbb{J}_{\chi}[G_{n},H_{n};I_{n}]\to\mathbb{J}[g,h].

Lower semicontinuity gives
\mathbb{A}[g,h]\leq 1
, while saturation and the normalization force equality; otherwise the normalized quotient would improve after rescaling the limit, contradicting the definition of
\Lambda_{*}
. Thus
\mathbb{A}[g,h]=1
and the endpoint profile is an actual function pair, with no relaxed defect measure.
∎

(Endpoint quotient Euler identities)
.

Let

be the strong endpoint profile from

Then the gauge-free amplitude identities hold:

The quotient is gauge-free; no separate

Proposition 5.3

(Zero-error localized Pohozaev–Morawetz identity)
.

The endpoint profile
(g,h)
satisfies

(20)

\frac{3}{2}\mathbb{J}[g,h]=2\Lambda_{*}\mathbb{A}[g,h].

Apply the mass-preserving spatial dilation in the lifted five-dimensional variables,

Consequently both transfer terms have degree

The finite-stage localized Pohozaev–Morawetz identity is the integration-by-parts version of this dilation computation. All cutoff, transport, tail, endpoint, and square-density concentration commutators belong to

by

; these quantities vanish along the zero-error sequence in

The symbol

D_{\rm leak}^{\rm tot}

8.

The terminal hierarchy separates compact critical profiles from noncompact error channels. The finite-stage Pohozaev–Morawetz identities handle the terminal packet directly, while the present section proves that all noncompact collar, tail, source, low-frequency, frequency, and fragmentation channels are perturbative at a canonically minimal first-threshold packet.

Let

\mathbb{E}_{\rm dir}=D_{\rm leak}^{\rm tot}+\mathcal{P}_{\rm tail}^{1/2}+\mathcal{P}_{\rm tail}+\mathcal{R}_{\rm low}+S_{\Gamma}+D_{\rm frag}+D_{\rm freq}

Definition 8.1

(Canonical first-threshold order and descendants)

A first-threshold packet is canonical if it is minimal among admissible packets with positive corrected score under the order

\displaystyle\text{earlier endpoint time}\prec\text{smaller active scale}\prec\text{fewer separated active components}

\displaystyle\prec\text{more localized residual channel}.

A descendant is an admissible packet obtained from endpoint reselection, collar recentering, dyadic-shell recentering, source-to-score reselection, low-frequency finite-shell selection, or separated-component selection, inheriting the local envelopes on a fixed enlargement and satisfying a lower score bound

Every comparable-scale family of lifted axis balls admits a Vitali subfamily whose fixed enlargements cover the same active set with overlap bounded by a dimensional constant

In the lifted coordinates

The low residue is split into affine moment, finite-shell, and very-low pieces. The affine moment is removed by the packet normalization. The active capacity is therefore additive up to perturbative error. One component carries a fixed fraction of the active capacity; selecting it gives a packet with fewer active components and hence lower selection order.

Proposition 8.4

(Endpoint descendant and finite-overlap exclusion)

Full-system reduction in Part II

Part II proves the full three-dimensional front-end reduction. It starts from an arbitrary hypothetical singular smooth solution of (

The proof is written as a finite-threshold argument. It uses no unintroduced terminology. Every object used below is defined in

Step 1.:

Step 2.:

Main Theorem 11.1

(Two-part global regularity theorem)

Definition 12.4

(iii)

(iv)

Lemma 13.1

(Local regularity contrapositive)
.

If
T_{*}<\infty
is the first singular time of a smooth finite-energy solution, then there are
x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
t_{n}\uparrow T_{*}
, and
r_{n}\downarrow 0
such that

C(u,p;Q_{r_{n}}(x_{n},t_{n}))\geq\varepsilon_{\rm CKN}>0.

Proof.

This is the contrapositive of the Caffarelli–Kohn–Nirenberg local regularity criterion. If every sufficiently small cylinder ending before
T_{*}
had critical size below the universal CKN threshold, the velocity would be locally bounded near every candidate singular point and the standard parabolic bootstrap would continue the solution past
T_{*}
. Therefore some sequence of cylinders must carry a fixed critical size.
∎

Lemma 13.2

(Local pressure splitting)
.

On a normalized packet
Q_{2}
, the pressure decomposes on
B_{3/2}
as

p=p_{\rm loc}+p_{\rm harm},\qquad-\Delta p_{\rm loc}=\partial_{i}\partial_{j}(\chi_{2}u_{i}u_{j}),

where

\|p_{\rm loc}\|_{L^{3/2}(B_{3/2})}\leq C\|u\|_{L^{3}(B_{2})}^{2},

and
p_{\rm harm}
is harmonic on
B_{3/2}
and obeys the standard interior estimates. Harmonic boundary contributions are assigned to the shell or tail channel in
Section

15
.

Proof.

Let
N
be the Newtonian potential in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and set

p_{\rm loc}=\partial_{i}\partial_{j}N*(\chi_{2}u_{i}u_{j}).

Calderón–Zygmund boundedness on
L^{3/2}
gives the displayed bound. The difference
p-p_{\rm loc}
is harmonic in
B_{3/2}
because the localized source agrees with
u_{i}u_{j}
on that ball. Interior estimates for harmonic functions bound gradients and oscillations of
p_{\rm harm}
in
B_{1}
by its values on the surrounding shell. Those shell values are included in the shell and tail budgets.
∎

Lemma 13.3

(Budget inheritance)
.

Let
Q^{\prime}
be an admissible descendant of a normalized parent packet
Q
. After the standard parabolic normalization of
Q^{\prime}
, the local energy, pressure, CKN size, vorticity Dirichlet budget, and tail budgets on fixed compact subcylinders are bounded by constants depending only on the parent bounds and the universal covering constants. If
Q^{\prime}
has positive corrected score before normalization, it has positive corrected score after normalization.

Proof.

Translations and parabolic Navier–Stokes scalings preserve the form of the equations and the scale-invariant CKN size. Restriction to a subcylinder cannot increase the local energy outside a fixed multiplicative cutoff constant. Pressure is handled by
Section

13
; the local part is Calderón–Zygmund stable and the harmonic part becomes a shell or tail budget. A finite-overlap recentering changes constants only by the overlap number. Selecting one component from separated supports reduces the total energy and pressure budgets by subadditivity. The endpoint term in
\mathfrak{Q}
scales exactly as
r\int_{B_{r}}|\omega|^{2}
, so positivity of the corrected score is invariant under normalization up to the fixed constants used in the descendant threshold.
∎

Proposition 14.1

(Canonical minimal packet)
.

If

, then there exists a sequence of normalized packets

approaching

is canonically minimal among all admissible packets with comparable positive corrected score.

Proof.

By

Among all cylinders with corrected score at least a fixed small threshold, first choose the earliest endpoint time up to a vanishing tolerance, then the smallest parabolic scale among those, then the smallest number of separated active components, and finally the most localized residual channel. A standard diagonal selection gives a sequence satisfying this lexicographic minimality. The local energy inequality, pressure splitting, and

give the uniform budgets. If a smaller descendant with comparable positive score existed, it would be earlier or smaller in this order, contradicting the selection.
∎

Lemma 14.2

(Full-system terminal balance and strict-loss convention)
.

Let
Q_{n}
be a canonically minimal terminal packet normalized so that its terminal vorticity budget satisfies
D_{Q_{n}}=1+o(1)
. Then, after passing to a subsequence, the localized vorticity identity on the normalized core has the coefficient-one form

(25)

D_{Q_{n}}\leq T_{Q_{n}}+R_{Q_{n}}+o(1),

where
T_{Q_{n}}
is the principal active stretching contribution and

is the sum of lower-order channels listed in (

\kappa>0
,

(26)

T_{Q_{n}}\leq(1-\kappa)D_{Q_{n}}+o(1),

while the remaining terms satisfy

or produce an admissible descendant. Under these two conditions (

Proof.

Multiply the vorticity equation by the localized active vorticity, integrate over the normalized packet, and use the local energy inequality together with the pressure splitting of

. The only term with coefficient one in the terminal balance is the active stretching term

Lemma 15.3

(Comparable-scale finite-overlap covering)

Let

and let

Lemma 15.4

(Finite-overlap defect-to-descendant principle)
.

Let
Q_{*}
be canonically minimal. If one of the physical-space channels
\mathfrak{D}_{\rm leak}
,
\mathfrak{D}_{\rm shell}
, or
\mathfrak{D}_{\rm frag}
is larger than a fixed fraction of the active terminal budget, then either that channel is perturbatively absorbed by the local energy inequality, or there is an admissible descendant
Q^{\prime}\prec Q_{*}
with positive comparable corrected score.

Proof.

We treat the channels separately.

Leakage.
If leakage is large in the collar, time averaging gives a time slice or subinterval on which the collar carries a fixed fraction of the endpoint score or Dirichlet budget. Cover the collar by comparable balls and apply
Section

15
. One ball carries positive corrected score or positive localized vorticity energy. If the latter is not already an endpoint score, the endpoint energy-to-score selection uses the local vorticity equation and the same averaging argument as
Section

16
to produce an earlier endpoint. The selected ball is smaller or more localized, hence a descendant.

Shell and tail.
Decompose the exterior into dyadic shells. The Biot–Savart and pressure kernels have summable off-core decay after subtracting constant and affine parts on the core. If the sum is large, one shell is large after applying the dyadic weights. Cover that shell by comparable balls. The selected ball either has positive score or gives a pressure/tail budget whose local pressure splitting from
Section

13
creates a score descendant. Otherwise the shell term is perturbative.

Fragmentation.
If the active core splits into separated positive-capacity pieces, the terminal budget and corrected score decompose up to exponentially small interaction errors. Select the component with maximal score-to-budget ratio. It has fewer separated components and comparable positive score. This is a descendant in the canonical order. If no component has comparable score, then the interaction is subcritical and the fragmentation contribution is perturbative.
∎

Definition 16.1

(Transfer-active temporal burst)
.

Let
Q=(-1,0]\times B_{2}
be a normalized packet and let
S_{\rm act}=\nabla U_{\rm act}+\nabla U_{\rm act}^{T}
be the strain generated by the active vorticity component. A subinterval
I^{\prime}\subset(-1,0]
is a transfer-active temporal burst if

\left|\iint_{I^{\prime}\times B_{1}}S_{\rm act}:\omega\otimes\omega\,dxdt\right|\geq\varepsilon_{0}

for a fixed positive threshold while
|I^{\prime}|
is small compared with the parent time length.

Proposition 16.2

(Temporal transfer burst gives a descendant)
.

Let
Q_{*}
be canonically minimal. If
Q_{*}
contains a transfer-active temporal burst on intervals
I^{\prime}_{n}
with
|I^{\prime}_{n}|\to 0
, then an admissible descendant
Q^{\prime}_{n}\prec Q_{*}
with positive corrected score exists. Therefore a canonically minimal packet has no transfer-active temporal concentration.

Proof.

Set
\tau_{n}=|I^{\prime}_{n}|
and
\rho_{n}=\tau_{n}^{1/2}
. By the finite-overlap covering
Section

15
, after passing to a subinterval of fixed sign and to one spatial ball
B_{\rho_{n}}(x_{n}^{\prime})
, there is a cutoff
\eta_{n}
supported in
B_{2\rho_{n}}(x_{n}^{\prime})
, equal to one on
B_{\rho_{n}}(x_{n}^{\prime})
, such that the localized transfer remains bounded from below:

(28)

\left|\iint_{I^{\prime}_{n}\times B_{2\rho_{n}}(x_{n}^{\prime})}\eta_{n}^{2}S_{\rm act}:\omega\otimes\omega\,dxdt\right|\geq c\varepsilon_{0}.

Let
L=-\Delta
on
B_{4\rho_{n}}(x_{n}^{\prime})
with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition and set

\psi_{n}=L^{-1}(\eta_{n}^{2}\omega_{\rm act}).

The full vorticity equation is

\partial_{t}\omega+u\cdot\nabla\omega-\Delta\omega=\omega\cdot\nabla u.

Testing the active component against
\psi_{n}
gives the localized negative-Sobolev identity

(29)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{n}(t_{+})-\mathcal{N}_{n}(t_{-})+\int_{t_{-}}^{t_{+}}\|\eta_{n}\omega_{\rm act}\|_{2}^{2}\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{-}}^{t_{+}}\int\eta_{n}^{2}S_{\rm act}:\omega\otimes\omega\,dxdt+\operatorname{Err}_{n},

where

\mathcal{N}_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int\eta_{n}^{2}\omega_{\rm act}\,L^{-1}(\eta_{n}^{2}\omega_{\rm act})\,dx.

The error
\operatorname{Err}_{n}
consists of transport, cutoff, pressure-free commutators, shell terms, passive-strain terms, and nonlocal commutators between the local inverse and the global Biot–Savart law. Each error is either bounded by a small multiple of the left side, or belongs to one of the final defect channels in (
27
). If an error channel dominates the lower bound in (
28
),
Section

15
or
Section

18
already produces a descendant. Otherwise

|\operatorname{Err}_{n}|\leq\frac{1}{2}c\varepsilon_{0}.

Thus the left side of (
29
) is bounded below by
c\varepsilon_{0}
.

Consequently either

\sup_{t\in I^{\prime}_{n}}\mathcal{N}_{n}(t)\geq c\varepsilon_{0}\quad\text{or}\quad\iint_{I^{\prime}_{n}}\|\eta_{n}\omega_{\rm act}\|_{2}^{2}\,dt\geq c\varepsilon_{0}.

In the second case, averaging over
|I^{\prime}_{n}|=\rho_{n}^{2}
gives a time
t^{\prime}_{n}\in I^{\prime}_{n}
with

\int_{B_{2\rho_{n}}(x_{n}^{\prime})}|\omega(t^{\prime}_{n})|^{2}\,dx\geq c\varepsilon_{0}\rho_{n}^{-2},

hence

\rho_{n}\int_{B_{2\rho_{n}}(x_{n}^{\prime})}|\omega(t^{\prime}_{n})|^{2}\,dx\geq c\varepsilon_{0}\rho_{n}^{-1}>0.

In the first case, the inverse estimate on a ball of radius
\rho_{n}
gives

\mathcal{N}_{n}(t)\leq C\rho_{n}^{2}\int_{B_{2\rho_{n}}(x_{n}^{\prime})}|\omega(t)|^{2}\,dx,

which yields the same positive corrected score. Thus a packet of radius comparable to
\rho_{n}
has positive corrected score. Its time scale is
\rho_{n}^{2}=|I^{\prime}_{n}|
, strictly smaller than the parent scale. If
I^{\prime}_{n}
is interior, choose the earlier endpoint of the subinterval; if it touches the terminal endpoint, choose the smaller scale. In both cases the resulting packet is an admissible descendant, contradicting canonical minimality.
∎

17.
Active frames and finite-dimensional phase-lock

Definition 17.1

(Frame space)
.

The frame space
\mathcal{F}
is the compact space whose points are triples
(P,e,\sigma)
, where
P
is an oriented two-plane,
e
is a unit direction orthogonal or tangent to
P
according to the active component, and
\sigma\in\{-1,1\}
records the sign of the active transfer orientation. Two frames differing by the same rigid rotation of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
are considered equivalent when only relative phase is measured. The compactness is the compactness of the Grassmannian and the unit sphere.

Definition 17.2

(Active frame measure)
.

On a normalized packet, decompose the active vorticity into finitely many Littlewood–Paley and angular pieces after discarding subcritical tails. Each piece with nonzero transfer is assigned its dominant frame in
\mathcal{F}
and is weighted by its absolute transfer contribution. The normalized weighted counting measure is the active frame measure
\mu_{\rm fr}
. Passing to a subsequence gives weak convergence of
\mu_{\rm fr}
because
\mathcal{F}
is compact.

Definition 17.3

(Rigid frame families)
.

There are two rigid zero-defect families:

(i)

the constant-frame family
\mathcal{R}_{2D}
, where all active vorticity frames share one fixed direction after rotation;

(ii)

the azimuthal orbit family
\mathcal{R}_{axi}
, where the frames are exactly the cylindrical angular orbit around one fixed axis.

Set
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}_{2D}\cup\mathcal{R}_{axi}
.

Lemma 17.4

(Finite-dimensional phase-lock separation)
.

There is
\kappa_{\rm ph}>0
with the following property. If an active frame measure
\mu_{\rm fr}
gives mass at least
m_{0}>0
to the complement of a sufficiently small neighborhood of
\mathcal{R}
, then the normalized terminal transfer coefficient is at most
1-\kappa_{\rm ph}m_{0}
. Hence zero phase defect forces
\operatorname{dist}(\operatorname{supp}\mu_{\rm fr},\mathcal{R})\to 0
.

Proof.

The transfer coefficient of a finite family of active frames is a continuous function on the compact product of frame spaces. The coefficient-one equality cases are precisely the two rigid families in
Section

17
. We justify this algebraically. Equality in the local stretching bound requires simultaneous alignment of the strain eigenframe with every active vorticity direction and equality in the divergence-free compatibility relations. If all active directions share a fixed frame, the resulting terminal equations are locally two-dimensional. If the frame rotates, compatibility with incompressibility and Biot–Savart recovery forces the rotation to be the physical cylindrical
m=1
angular orbit around a single axis. No third finite-dimensional equality case exists because any mismatch of two active frames creates a positive angle between their maximizing eigendirections, and the continuous transfer coefficient drops by a definite amount on compact subsets away from the equality set.

Let
U_{\delta}(\mathcal{R})
be a
\delta
-neighborhood of
\mathcal{R}
. The compact set
\mathcal{F}\setminus U_{\delta}(\mathcal{R})
contains no maximizer. Therefore the coefficient is at most
1-\kappa_{\delta}
there. Integrating the pointwise finite-dimensional loss against
\mu_{\rm fr}
gives the stated loss with
\kappa_{\rm ph}=\kappa_{\delta}
.
∎

18.
Passive strain and zero-defect rigidity

Lemma 18.1

(Passive strain visibility)
.

If passive strain contributes a fixed fraction of the terminal stretching on the active core, then at least one of the channels in (
27
) is positive.

Proof.

Let
\omega^{\rm act}
denote the active vorticity component selected in the core and let
S^{\rm pass}
be the strain generated by the remaining vorticity and pressure field. The passive contribution to stretching is

\mathcal{P}_{\rm pass}=\iint_{Q_{2}}\chi_{1}^{2}\,\omega^{\rm act}\cdot S^{\rm pass}\omega^{\rm act}\,dxdt.

Assume that
|\mathcal{P}_{\rm pass}|\geq c_{0}D_{Q}
along a terminal subsequence. We split the passive source with the cutoffs of
Section

12
:

1=\chi_{4/3}+(\chi_{3/2}-\chi_{4/3})+(1-\chi_{3/2})=\chi_{\rm near}+\chi_{\rm col}+\chi_{\rm far}.

The strain generated by
\chi_{\rm col}\omega
is estimated directly by the collar term. Indeed the Calderón–Zygmund kernel is locally bounded from the collar to the core after the finite buffer is inserted, and therefore

|\mathcal{P}_{\rm col}|\leq C\,\mathfrak{D}_{\rm leak}^{1/2}D_{Q}^{1/2}+o(D_{Q}).

Thus a nonperturbative collar contribution implies
\mathfrak{D}_{\rm leak}\geq cD_{Q}
.

For the far field, decompose the Biot–Savart kernel into dyadic shells. On the core the shell contribution has a constant part, an affine part, and a summable remainder. The summable remainder is bounded by
\mathfrak{D}_{\rm shell}D_{Q}+o(D_{Q})
. The constant part does not contribute to trace-free stretching after pressure normalization. If the affine part contributes a fixed fraction, it determines a preferred eigendirection on the active core. If that direction is not already represented by the active frame measure, the angular spread defect
\mathfrak{D}_{\rm ang}
is positive; if it is represented but carries the wrong phase or sign in the stretching form, the phase-lock defect
\mathfrak{D}_{\rm phase}
is positive.

It remains to consider the near passive piece. Apply the finite-overlap active-core decomposition from
Section

14
. A near passive packet that is spatially separated from the selected active packet gives a separated positive-capacity component, hence
\mathfrak{D}_{\rm frag}>0
. A near passive packet that is not separated but contributes with a different frame either enlarges the active frame measure or gives positive angular spread. If it is absorbed into the active frame measure, its contribution is no longer passive. If it is not absorbed, it is recorded by
\mathfrak{D}_{\rm ang}
or
\mathfrak{D}_{\rm phase}
.

Every alternative contradicts the assumption that all channels in (
27
) vanish. Hence a nonperturbative passive strain contribution forces a positive final defect channel.
∎

Lemma 18.2

(Constant-frame limit is locally two-dimensional)
.

Let a zero-defect terminal branch have active frame measure supported in
\mathcal{R}_{2D}
. Then, after a rigid rotation, every terminal limit is locally two-dimensional.

Proof.

After rotation, the active vorticity direction is fixed, say parallel to
e_{3}
, and zero angular spread implies that all active vorticity components have no component outside this direction. Zero passive visibility upgrades active convergence to full vorticity convergence on the core. Thus the terminal vorticity has the form
\omega=\omega^{3}(x_{1},x_{2},t)e_{3}
in the limit. Since
\nabla\cdot\omega=0
, this implies
\partial_{3}\omega^{3}=0
. Biot–Savart recovery then gives a velocity independent of
x_{3}
up to a harmonic gradient; the harmonic part is removed by the pressure normalization and shell-zero condition. Hence the limiting velocity is a two-dimensional Navier–Stokes flow in the variables
(x_{1},x_{2})
. Classical two-dimensional regularity rules out a singular terminal branch.
∎

Lemma 18.3

(Azimuthal orbit limit is axisymmetric with swirl)
.

Let a zero-defect terminal branch have active frame measure supported in
\mathcal{R}_{axi}
. Then, after a rigid rotation, every terminal limit is axisymmetric with swirl around one fixed axis.

Proof.

The support condition in
\mathcal{R}_{axi}
gives one axis
\ell
and cylindrical coordinates
(r,\theta,z)
around
\ell
such that the active vorticity frame rotates as the physical angular frame. Zero phase defect excludes angular drift relative to this orbit. Zero passive visibility implies that all strain relevant on the core is generated by the same orbit. Zero shell and leakage defects exclude exterior forcing and collar escape. Therefore the whole terminal velocity is invariant under simultaneous rotation of space and frame around
\ell
. In cylindrical coordinates it has the form

u=u^{r}(r,z,t)e_{r}+u^{\theta}(r,z,t)e_{\theta}+u^{z}(r,z,t)e_{z}.

The swirl is allowed because
u^{\theta}
may be nonzero; the regular variables are exactly those of Part I,

\Gamma=ru^{\theta},\qquad G=\omega_{\theta}/r,\qquad F=u^{\theta}/r.

Thus the terminal limit belongs to the axisymmetric-with-swirl class closed in
Section

1
.
∎

Theorem 18.4

(Zero-defect rigidity)
.

Let
Q_{n}
be a canonically minimal terminal branch with

\mathfrak{D}_{\rm final}^{3D}(Q_{n})\to 0.

Then, after passing to a subsequence and applying a fixed rigid rotation, every terminal limit is either locally two-dimensional or axisymmetric with swirl around one fixed axis.

Proof.

We prove the assertion by following the vanishing of each term in (
27
).

Step 1: compact core.

The conditions
\mathfrak{D}_{\rm leak}\to 0
and
\mathfrak{D}_{\rm shell}\to 0
imply that the core neither loses vorticity through the cutoff collar nor receives leading-order strain from exterior shells. The pressure splitting of
Section

13
then gives a pressure limit on the core whose harmonic part is affine and whose affine contribution has already been subtracted in the definition of the shell defect. Consequently the terminal stretching form is determined by the active core up to an
o(1)
error.

Step 2: one active component.

The vanishing of
\mathfrak{D}_{\rm frag}
rules out two separated positive-capacity active components. Indeed, if two components retained fixed fractions of the corrected score,
Section

12
would allow selection of one of them as a descendant with smaller component complexity, contradicting canonical minimality. Hence the active core is coherent and admits a single active frame measure after passing to a subsequence.

Step 3: no passive leading strain.

By
Section

18
, any passive strain visible at leading order would force a positive leakage, shell, fragmentation, angular, or phase defect. Since all these terms vanish, passive strain is perturbative. Therefore the leading terminal stretching coefficient is represented by the active frame measure alone.

Step 4: finite-dimensional equality set.

The active frame measures form a compact family of probability measures on the frame space of
Section

17
. The phase-lock defect
\mathfrak{D}_{\rm phase}
measures distance from the equality set of the finite-dimensional stretching coefficient. Since
\mathfrak{D}_{\rm phase}\to 0
,
Section

17
implies that every subsequential limit of the active frame measures is supported in

\mathcal{R}_{2D}\cup\mathcal{R}_{axi}.

The two sets are compact and disjoint after the axis in
\mathcal{R}_{axi}
is fixed. Passing to a further subsequence, the limiting measure is supported in one of the two alternatives.

Step 5: recovery of the physical branch.

If the limiting frame measure is supported in
\mathcal{R}_{2D}
,
Section

18
gives, after one fixed rotation, a terminal velocity independent of one coordinate and tangent to the remaining two variables. This is the locally two-dimensional alternative. If the limiting frame measure is supported in
\mathcal{R}_{axi}
,
Section

18
gives one fixed axis and a cylindrical representation

u=u^{r}(r,z,t)e_{r}+u^{\theta}(r,z,t)e_{\theta}+u^{z}(r,z,t)e_{z},

which is the axisymmetric-with-swirl class of Part I.

The two alternatives exhaust the zero-defect equality set by
Section

17
. This proves the theorem.
∎

19.
Positive final defect and full-system closure

Theorem 19.1

(Positive final defect gives loss or descendant)
.

Let
Q_{n}
be a canonically minimal terminal branch. If

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathfrak{D}_{\rm final}^{3D}(Q_{n})>0,

then the coefficient-one terminal balance has a strict loss or an admissible descendant exists. Hence a minimal nonregular terminal survivor must satisfy
\mathfrak{D}_{\rm final}^{3D}(Q_{n})\to 0
.

Proof.

After passing to a subsequence, at least one nonnegative summand in (
27
) has a positive lower bound. The constants are chosen in the following order: first the finite-overlap constant and the descendant score threshold, then the absorption fraction in
Section

14
, then the collar thickness, shell cutoff, temporal subwindow tolerance, and angular separation parameter. With this order fixed, a nonperturbative channel either produces a descendant before the terminal contradiction is invoked, or it enters the coefficient of the principal transfer and gives the strict loss (
26
). We consider the cases separately.

Leakage.

If
\mathfrak{D}_{\rm leak}\geq c_{0}>0
, the cutoff collar contains a fixed fraction of the active budget. The collar is covered by a bounded number of comparable balls. Finite overlap gives one ball with comparable corrected score, and recentering there gives an admissible descendant. If the leakage contribution is below the descendant threshold, it is absorbed into the local energy inequality. This is exactly the leakage case of
Section

15
.

Shell and tail.

If
\mathfrak{D}_{\rm shell}\geq c_{0}>0
, decompose the exterior into dyadic shells. The Calderón–Zygmund kernel has summable off-core decay. Hence either the dyadic sum is perturbative or one shell carries a definite weighted contribution. Covering that shell by comparable balls gives a shell descendant. Thus a nonperturbative shell defect is incompatible with canonical minimality.

Fragmentation.

If
\mathfrak{D}_{\rm frag}\geq c_{0}>0
, the active core splits into at least two separated positive-capacity components. The component with maximal local efficiency has comparable corrected score and strictly fewer components than the parent. Selecting it gives a descendant in the canonical order.

Temporal transfer.

If
\mathfrak{D}_{\rm temp}\geq c_{0}>0
, there is a transfer-active source burst on a time interval whose length is smaller than the parent parabolic scale.
Section

16
converts the localized negative-Sobolev response into a positive corrected-score packet at the smaller scale. This is an admissible temporal descendant.

Passive strain.

If
\mathfrak{D}_{\rm pass}\geq c_{0}>0
,
Section

18
shows that the passive strain is either collar, shell, fragmented, angularly misaligned, or phase-incompatible. Hence one of the preceding cases occurs, or an angular/phase defect is positive.

Angular and phase defects.

If
\mathfrak{D}_{\rm ang}
or
\mathfrak{D}_{\rm phase}
has a positive lower bound, the active frame measure puts a fixed amount of mass away from the equality set
\mathcal{R}_{2D}\cup\mathcal{R}_{axi}
. By the finite-dimensional compactness and separation theorem
Section

17
, the terminal stretching coefficient drops by a fixed amount:

\text{terminal transfer coefficient}\leq 1-\kappa,\qquad\kappa>0.

This is strict terminal loss.

In every case positive final defect gives either an admissible descendant or strict loss in the coefficient-one terminal balance. A descendant contradicts canonical minimality. Strict loss contradicts
Section

14
. Hence no minimal nonregular terminal branch can have positive final defect.
∎

Appendix A
Detailed temporal descendant module

This appendix gives the full local proof of the Temporal Source-to-Score Descendant (TSD) lemma used in

and
5.1
. The argument is classical: parabolic localization, a localized inverse-potential test, collar commutator estimates, an endpoint negative-Sobolev inverse inequality, and the canonical first-threshold descendant order. Background for the local energy bookkeeping and finite-overlap selections may be found in

,
21
,

,
26
]
; the only point specific to the present problem is that the transfer density is detected by the exact recovery identity

U[g]=-\partial_{z}(-\Delta_{5})^{-1}g.

Throughout this appendix, on a small axis ball we write

L=-\Delta_{5},\qquad\psi=L^{-1}(\eta^{2}g),

where

denotes the Dirichlet inverse on a fixed enlargement of the selected ball. The source density is denoted by

. Thus the actual derivative source in the

Lemma A.1

(Parabolic localization of a transfer burst)
.

Let
I^{\prime}\subset I
have length
\tau
, set
\rho=\tau^{1/2}
, and suppose that on the normalized terminal core

\left|\iint_{I^{\prime}\times B_{2}^{\rm axis}}\chi^{2}U[g]h^{2}\,d\mu_{5}dt\right|\geq\varepsilon_{0}.

Then there is an axis ball
B_{\rho}^{\rm axis}(z^{\prime})
, a cutoff
\eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(B_{2\rho}^{\rm axis}(z^{\prime}))
with
\eta\equiv 1
on
B_{\rho}^{\rm axis}(z^{\prime})
, and a sign
s\in\{-1,1\}
such that

(30)

s\iint_{I^{\prime}\times B_{2\rho}^{\rm axis}(z^{\prime})}\eta^{2}U[g]h^{2}\,d\mu_{5}dt\geq c\varepsilon_{0}.

The overlap constant
c>0
depends only on the fixed dimension and the cutoff family.

Proof.

Cover the core
B_{2}^{\rm axis}
by a Vitali family of axis balls
B_{\rho}^{\rm axis}(z_{j})
and choose a subordinate partition
\{\eta_{j}^{2}\}
with uniformly bounded overlap. If every localized integral were smaller than the right side of (

∎

Lemma A.2

(Localized square-density recovery commutator)
.

Let
\eta
be supported in
B_{2\rho}^{\rm axis}(z^{\prime})
, equal to one on
B_{\rho}^{\rm axis}(z^{\prime})

\psi=L^{-1}(\eta^{2}g)

with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary data on a fixed enlargement, say
B_{4\rho}^{\rm axis}(z^{\prime})

(31)

\int\eta^{2}U[g]h^{2}\,d\mu_{5}=-\int h^{2}\partial_{z}\psi\,d\mu_{5}+\operatorname{Comm}_{\eta},

where

(32)

\operatorname{Comm}_{\eta}=\int h^{2}\{\eta^{2}U[g]-U[\eta^{2}g]\}\,d\mu_{5}.

Moreover, for every
\delta>0
,

(33)

|\operatorname{Comm}_{\eta}|\leq\delta\,\mathbb{A}_{\eta}[g,h]+C_{\delta}\bigl(D_{\rm collar}+\mathcal{P}_{\rm tail}+\mathcal{R}_{\rm low}\bigr),

where the three terms on the right are the collar, exterior-tail, and low-frequency components of the direct error budget on the enlarged ball. Consequently, if
|\operatorname{Comm}_{\eta}|
is not perturbative compared with a localized transfer burst, then one of the named collar, tail, or low-frequency descendant channels is active.

Proof.

Since
-\Delta_{5}\psi=\eta^{2}g
,

\partial_{z}\psi=\partial_{z}(-\Delta_{5})^{-1}(\eta^{2}g)=-U[\eta^{2}g].

This gives (
31
) after adding and subtracting
\eta^{2}U[g]
. This identity is only a recovery identity for the square-density entropy transfer; it is not the derivative source identity for the
g
-equation.

It remains to estimate (

\eta\prec\eta_{1}\prec\eta_{2},

where
\eta_{1}\equiv 1
on
\operatorname{supp}\eta
and
\eta_{2}\equiv 1
on
\operatorname{supp}\eta_{1}
. Decompose

The inner piece is controlled by the standard Coifman–Meyer/Calderón–Zygmund commutator estimate for the order
-1

\|\eta^{2}U[\eta_{1}g]-U[\eta^{2}g]\|_{L^{10/3}}\leq C\rho\|\nabla\eta\|_{\infty}\|g\|_{L^{2}(B_{2\rho})}+C\rho\|\nabla_{5}g\|_{L^{2}(C_{\eta})},

is the collar. Pairing with

\|h\|_{L^{20/7}}^{2}\lesssim\|h\|_{L^{2}}^{1/2}\|\nabla_{5}h\|_{L^{2}}^{3/2},

and Young’s inequality gives the first term

plus collar leakage. The transition piece

-1

∎

Lemma A.3

(Square-density burst reduction)

∎

Lemma A.4

(Localized linear source-recovery commutator)

Lemma A.5

(Localized negative-Sobolev identity)
.

Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\eta}(t_{+})-\mathcal{N}_{\eta}(t_{-})+\int_{t_{-}}^{t_{+}}\|\eta^{2}g(t)\|_{2}^{2}\,dt

\displaystyle=-\int_{t_{-}}^{t_{+}}\int\eta^{2}h\partial_{z}\psi\,d\mu_{5}dt

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{t_{-}}^{t_{+}}\bigl(\mathcal{C}_{\Delta,\eta}+\mathcal{C}_{{\rm tr},\eta}+\mathcal{C}_{{\rm src},\eta}+\mathcal{R}_{\eta}\bigr)(t)\,dt.

\int_{t_{-}}^{t_{+}}\left|\mathcal{C}_{\Delta,\eta}+\mathcal{C}_{{\rm tr},\eta}+\mathcal{C}_{{\rm src},\eta}+\mathcal{R}_{\eta}\right|dt\leq\delta\int_{t_{-}}^{t_{+}}\|\eta^{2}g\|_{2}^{2}dt+C_{\delta}\mathbb{E}_{\rm loc}(I^{\prime},B_{4\rho}),

Proof.

Because

Substitute

For the diffusion term, write

Since

\int\Delta_{5}(\eta^{2}g)\psi\,d\mu_{5}=-\int(\eta^{2}g)^{2}\,d\mu_{5}.

The commutator

Poincaré on the collar, the local inverse estimate

For the transport contribution,

Write

Appendix B
Why a purely quadratic source-potential energy does not close

This appendix records the direct-energy obstruction that motivates the finite-stage Pohozaev–Morawetz endpoint argument used in the main proof. The discussion is included to separate two issues. First, the derivative source in the lifted vorticity equation is harmless at the level of classical energy estimates when the source density is transported and diffused independently. Second, in the Navier–Stokes swirl system the same source density is compressed by the recovered strain. That compression has the same critical scaling as the pair visibility and cannot be removed by a quadratic Lyapunov correction.

The obstruction should also be understood geometrically. Standard energy, Sobolev, HLS, and Calderón–Zygmund estimates are scalar norm estimates. They control how large a field is, how much integrability it has, and how much derivative energy it carries. They do not record whether a normalized packet is arranged with the parity, orientation, and strain-source alignment needed for a coefficient-one transfer saturation. Two lifted profiles can have the same
L^{2}
,
H^{1}
, and critical interpolation norms while generating different signs or alignments in
U[G]H^{2}
. The endpoint strictness argument tests this lost geometric information through amplitude homogeneity of the strong endpoint profile and the localized Pohozaev–Morawetz dilation homogeneity.

Throughout this appendix we work on the lifted five-dimensional model without cutoffs. The localized version contains only commutators supported in the transition collar, and those commutators are exactly the terms recorded in the terminal error budget. Let

L=-\Delta_{5},\qquad P=\partial_{z}L^{-1},\qquad U[G]=-PG.

The relevant model system is

(42)

G_{t}+LG=\partial_{z}H,\qquad H_{t}+LH=-4U[G]H.

The term
-4U[G]H
is the model form of the compressive part of the exact equation for

H=F^{2}
. The dissipative term
-2|\nabla_{5}F|^{2}
in the exact equation has a favorable sign and is therefore omitted here; omitting it only makes the obstruction harder to avoid.

Lemma B.1

(The linear derivative source is classically harmless)
.

If the compressive term in (
42
) is removed, namely

G_{t}+LG=\partial_{z}H,\qquad H_{t}+LH=0,

then the usual energy method gives

\sup_{0<t<T}\bigl(\|G(t)\|_{2}^{2}+\|H(t)\|_{2}^{2}\bigr)+\int_{0}^{T}\bigl(\|\nabla_{5}G\|_{2}^{2}+\|\nabla_{5}H\|_{2}^{2}\bigr)\,dt\leq C_{T}\bigl(\|G(0)\|_{2}^{2}+\|H(0)\|_{2}^{2}\bigr).

Proof.

Multiplying the first equation by
G
gives

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|G\|_{2}^{2}+\|\nabla_{5}G\|_{2}^{2}=-\int H\,\partial_{z}G.

For every
\varepsilon>0
,

\left|\int H\,\partial_{z}G\right|\leq\varepsilon\|\nabla_{5}G\|_{2}^{2}+C_{\varepsilon}\|H\|_{2}^{2}.

The heat equation for
H
gives

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|H\|_{2}^{2}+\|\nabla_{5}H\|_{2}^{2}=0.

Adding the two inequalities and applying Gronwall proves the claim. Thus the derivative source
\partial_{z}H
is not, by itself, the obstruction.
∎

The obstruction enters through the compressive feedback. Multiplying the second equation in (
42
) by
H
gives

(43)

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|H\|_{2}^{2}+\|\nabla_{5}H\|_{2}^{2}=-4\int U[G]H^{2}.

The right hand side is sign-indefinite because
U[G]
is sign-indefinite. Classical harmonic analysis gives only a soft estimate. Indeed, by the order
-1
recovery estimate and the five-dimensional Sobolev interpolation used in
Appendix

C
,

(44)

\left|\int U[G]H^{2}\right|\leq\varepsilon\|\nabla_{5}H\|_{2}^{2}+C_{\varepsilon}\|G\|_{2}^{4}\|H\|_{2}^{2}.

Consequently the direct pair energy yields, at best, an ODE model of the form

E_{G}^{\prime}\lesssim M_{H},\qquad M_{H}^{\prime}\lesssim E_{G}^{2}M_{H},

which is not a global a priori bound for arbitrary large data. A direct proof must therefore find a cancellation, not merely an estimate.

The natural cancellation candidate is the sign-corrected bilinear source-potential energy

\mathcal{C}[G,H]=-\int GPH.

The next proposition shows that this candidate cannot remove the compressive transfer for arbitrary amplitudes.

Theorem B.2

(Obstruction to bilinear source–potential closure)
.

Consider energies of the form

\mathcal{E}_{a,b,c}[G,H]=a\|G\|_{2}^{2}+b\|H\|_{2}^{2}-c\int GPH,\qquad a,b>0,

for the model system (
42
). No choice of constants
a,b,c
eliminates the critical compressive transfer for all amplitudes of
(G,H)
. More precisely, differentiating
\mathcal{E}_{a,b,c}
necessarily leaves either the cubic term

\int U[G]H^{2},

or the distinct trilinear term

\int G\,P(U[G]H),

and these two terms have different amplitude homogeneities:

\int U[G]H^{2}\sim GH^{2},\qquad\int G\,P(U[G]H)\sim G^{2}H.

Thus a quadratic source-potential correction cannot cancel the nonlinear compression uniformly in the relative sizes of
G
and
H
.

Proof.

The derivative of the sign-corrected cross term is

\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(-\int GPH\bigr)=-\int G_{t}PH-\int GPH_{t}.

Substituting (
42
) gives

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(-\int GPH\bigr)

\displaystyle=\int(LG)PH-\int(\partial_{z}H)PH+\int GP(LH)+4\int GP(UH).

The source contribution has a favorable sign:

-\int(\partial_{z}H)PH=-\int\partial_{z}H\,\partial_{z}L^{-1}H=-\|\partial_{z}L^{-1/2}H\|_{2}^{2}.

This shows why
\mathcal{C}
is the only plausible quadratic cross term: it detects the linear derivative source. The heat contributions are lower-order cross dissipation terms which may be estimated by
\|\nabla G\|_{2}^{2}+\|\nabla H\|_{2}^{2}
. The nonlinear contribution, however, is

4\int GP(UH).

Since
U=-PG
, this is of amplitude type
G^{2}H
. The compressive energy term in (
43
) is of amplitude type
GH^{2}
. Under independent amplitude scaling

G\mapsto\lambda G,\qquad H\mapsto\mu H,

these two terms scale respectively as

\lambda^{2}\mu,\qquad\lambda\mu^{2}.

A single constant coefficient
c
cannot cancel both amplitude laws for all
\lambda,\mu>0
. Therefore any quadratic energy
\mathcal{E}_{a,b,c}
leaves a critical cubic transfer term. This proves the proposition.
∎

Corollary B.3

(Necessity of the compact-critical strictness step)
.

The classical quadratic energy method, even after adding the natural bilinear source-potential cross term, cannot produce a closed large-data a priori estimate for the pair
(G,H)
. The direct HLS–Sobolev bridge removes all subcritical configurations, but in the scale-normalized critical regime it gives only boundedness of the transfer relative to the pair visibility. A further shape-sensitive strictness mechanism is therefore necessary to exclude coefficient-one saturation.

Proof.

By
Appendix

B
, the derivative source is harmless without compression. By (
44
), ordinary harmonic analysis controls the compression only by a soft estimate with a critical mass remainder. By
Theorem

B.2
, the only natural quadratic cross energy detects the linear source but cannot cancel the nonlinear compression because the resulting trilinear has the wrong amplitude homogeneity. Hence the endpoint critical regime cannot be closed by a coercive quadratic energy. The main proof resolves exactly this endpoint by first using finite-stage descendant estimates to obtain a strong endpoint profile, and then applying amplitude and localized Pohozaev–Morawetz dilation balances to that profile. These balances use the geometric shape of the normalized terminal packet rather than only its size.
∎

Remark B.4

(Shape blindness of norm-only estimates)
.

The preceding obstruction is the analytic form of a geometric limitation. Norm estimates such as HLS, Sobolev interpolation, Calderón–Zygmund estimates, and Young’s inequality are insensitive to the arrangement of the active packet beyond size and derivative scale. They cannot distinguish, for example, whether the recovered strain is aligned with the positive source density, whether the active profile has the parity needed to maximize the transfer, or whether the mass lies in a collar/tail arrangement that should instead be reselected as a descendant. This is why the proof first uses classical estimates to eliminate all subcritical configurations and then passes, only at the scale-neutral endpoint, to a compact-critical profile where the actual geometry of the packet can be tested by amplitude and dilation identities.
