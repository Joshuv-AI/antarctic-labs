 Sean K. Lehman
                                    March 26, 2026

 Abstract
 During my education in mathematics, engineering and physics, I learned transform pairs
and their usage mechanics but I never remember seeing the derivations of the solutions to
second order ordinary differential equations (ODE) and difference equations. A solution to a
question posed in a potential funder meeting put me on a path to solving second order systems
using the five principal Fourier based methods: Fourier transform (FT), Z-transform (ZT), dis-
crete time Fourier transform (DTFT), discrete Fourier transform (DFT) and Laplace transform
(LT).

0 Introduction
Three Ph.D.s were sitting in a room with a sponsor interested in resonant ultrasound spectroscopy [1]
(RUS) who asked why is there a 180◦ phase shift around the resonance of the second order ordinary
differential equation (ODE),
                                ẍ(t) + 2ζω0 ẋ(t) + ω02 x(t) = y(t).                          (0.1)
Memory’s haze informs me this is taught and hopefully learned in a sophomore level circuits II
course where Laplace transforms are introduced. Two of the Ph.D.s taught the course decades prior
yet none of the three could offer a cogent reply other than the arctangent undergoes a 180◦ when its
argument passes infinity. The equation under scrutiny was the Fourier transform (up to a gain) of
Eqn. (0.1). This is the second order transfer function (TF) or frequency response function (FRF):
 1 ω02
                                F (ω) =                          ,                             (0.2)
                                            g −ω + 2iζω0 ω + ω02
 2

which will be exhaustively studied in Section 1. Note:
 Parameter Quantity
 g gain
 ω radial frequency
 ω0 natural or resonant frequency
 ζ damping
 Bothered by our shortcoming, I wrote a monograph which appears as Section 1 and a MATLAB [2]
script, listed in Appendix K, which demonstrates graphically everything there is to know about
Eqn. (0.2) using three different methods:
 1. The full transfer function as modeled by Eqn. (0.2),
 2. The partial fraction expansion of Eqn. (0.2) as shown in Eqn. (1.19), and
 3. The transfer function as a MATLAB System Object constructed from the zpk() function.
 I emailed the monograph and MATLAB script to the Ph.D.s and all was well for just over a
year until a young student approached one of the Ph.D.s wanting to learn about signal processing
and transfer functions. The young one identified an error in one of my derivations for which I
was grateful and impressed. But then he asked for a proof of the 180◦ phase reversal as shown in
Eqns 1.12 and 1.13 of Section 1.
 Section 1.1 provides a mathematical proof of the 180◦ phase reversal. But this proof requires
proof of the integral form of the arctangent followed by a proof the arctangent is an odd function.
These are shown in Sections 1.2 and 1.3, respectively.
 Eqn. (0.2) is derived from Eqn. (0.1) (up to the gain g) via a (continuous time) Fourier trans-
form. But there are other Fourier-based techniques for analyzing Eqn. (0.2) and its discrete time
equivalent. So, following the thorough answering of the original question, Section 2 presents the
statement of the second order ordinary differential equation (ODE) and the corresponding second
order difference equation to be studied using five Fourier-based transformations:

through the development of the solution in a manner I never learned as a student because these
transforms were never taught in a single course and text.
 I recall learning all the skills as an undergraduate using Oppenheim and Schafer [4] for signal

Anton [7] for linear algebra, and Boyce and DiPrima [8] for differential equations but a Ph.D.
colleague suggested some of this material was learned at the graduate level, in my case from
Arfkin [9].

scaling. For example, as an undergraduate in engineering, the sign of the forward transform was

G.2 Stability and Aliasing
The stability criterion changes form between continuous and discrete time:
 • Continuous-time (Laplace): Poles must be in the left half-plane for stability,
 • Discrete-time (Z-transform): Poles must be inside the unit circle for stability.
When discretizing a continuous-time system for digital implementation, pole locations transform
according to z = es∆t .
 The aliasing phenomenon also emerges from this relationship: Frequencies ω and ω + 2π/∆t
both map to the same point on the unit circle.

G.3 Summary
The Laplace transform generalizes the Fourier transform by expanding to a complex frequency.
The result is an exponential growth/decay kernel factor through the σ term. This allows analysis
of unstable systems or signals that do not have a Fourier transform in the classical sense.
 The Fourier transform provides the frequency response (magnitude and phase), while the
Laplace transform reveals pole-zero locations in the complex plane which determine stability and
transient behavior. The Z-transform extends these concepts to discrete-time systems and digital
signal processing.
 For practical engineering problems,

 • The Fourier transform is used for frequency-domain analysis of stable signals and systems,

 • The Laplace transform is necessary when analyzing continuous-time system stability or
 designing analog controllers,

 • The Z-transform is essential for digital filter design, sampled-data systems, and implement-
 ing DSP algorithms.

When designing digital filters from analog prototypes, the mapping z = es∆t provides the bridge
between continuous and discrete domains.

J.2 What if the Function is Non-Causal?
How does the Kramers-Kronig derivation fail when g(t) is non-causal?
  The Laplace transform must include negative time:
 Z ∞
                 G(s) =          dt g(t) e−st ,                                             (J.18)
 −∞
 Z 0 Z ∞
                        =        dt g(t) e−σt e−iωt +     dt g(t) e−σt e−iωt .              (J.19)
 −∞ 0

The e−σt kernel in the first integral grows without bound as t → −∞. Thus, unless g(t) decays
faster than e−σt , the integral diverges in the upper half-plane. This implies the G(s) has upper
half-plane pole(s) and the function is no longer analytic. The integral of Eqn. (J.9) must include
the residue(s) of these pole(s) and the Kramers-Kronig relations break down.
