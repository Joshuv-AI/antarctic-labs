Foundational Research for Automated Mobility District
Implementation
 The transportation sector is experiencing significant transformation driven by the simultaneous
advancement of electrification, automation, and on-demand services. These developments are
actively influencing vehicle technology integration, infrastructure planning, and system
management to optimize operational performance.

Furthermore, the interplay among these transformations introduces additional complexity to the
design and implementation of each. The National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) conducts
research on these trends and their broader implications, evaluating both current and projected
effects on public mobility operating concepts and strategies. This includes analysis of factors that
will determine the long-term efficacy and resiliency of multimodal transportation systems.

 NREL’s original concept of AMDs depicted many types of major activity centers within an urban
 area, all within proximity in the metropolitan core.
An automated mobility district (AMD) envisions
a system of integrated mobility options that serves
major activity centers such as campuses, central
business districts, and large medical facilities.
This mixture of automated transport services
would be designed to ensure a high-quality
mobility option providing both internal circulation

with other modes providing access to the district,
thereby reducing the necessity for personal motor
vehicles.

AMD Refinement of Concept – Phase III
Findings included in a series of technical papers and presentations by NREL researchers at various
industry conferences between 2022 and 2024 underpin Phase III AMD research. Building on
earlier phases, these research papers refined the concept of an AMD as providing convenient and
efficient mobility within a specific district through multiple automated fleets of varying vehicle
sizes and types. These unique AV “systems” would comprise fleets managed under both public
and private operations – a “system of systems” designed for dense urban areas.
 • Conference Paper: Stanley E. Young and J. Sam Lott. 2024. “Automated Mobility
 Districts – A Conceptual Definition.” Presented at the Infrastructure Intelligence
 Conference, Austin, TX, 6–7 June 2023. www.nrel.gov/docs/fy24osti/86453.pdf.

AMD Phase I, II, and III Top-Level Research Findings
By studying early pilot deployments of AV technology for public mobility as well as existing
advanced transit systems that are fully automated in their operation, NREL researchers have
gained valuable insight into the challenges of automated transport systems within AMDs. In
Phase III, research has expanded to include the integration of vehicle fleet electrification and the
importance of efficient, resilient station (curbfront) operations. Their work has also drawn from
historical assessments of automated transit network (ATN) systems, which used small,
automated vehicles on dedicated pathways for fully automated, on-demand service.
A 2009 study used detailed simulations to analyze an ATN system for various vehicle and
operating modes, based on a specific flight schedule and passenger activity at Newark airport.

vehicles at the regional rail station to prevent shortages and delays during periods of peak

The following key findings from AMD Phase I–III research are presented with respect to
operational complexity, fleet size considerations, and required infrastructure investments.
Additionally, these complexities introduce significant challenges in fleet management for large-
scale AMD deployments. The research emphasizes that comprehensive AMD implementation
necessitates the application of a systems engineering approach, starting with early concept
planning and continuing through preliminary engineering and design stages.

Intelligent Infrastructure: Intelligent roadway infrastructure is highly beneficial for enabling
cooperative automation among multiple vehicle fleets and conventional traffic management
systems. A core purpose is to enhance operational safety, efficiency, and resiliency in AMDs. For
example, early AV deployments often faced safety risks due to slow AV speeds at busy
intersections and near pedestrians. Intelligent infrastructure can be applied to mitigate this safety
hazard resulting from speed differentials within normal traffic flows at busy roadway junctions,
especially where pedestrians may also be present.

As AV deployments grow to hundreds of vehicles operating in multiple fleets within an AMD,
intelligent infrastructure complements and enhances AV operations. SAE International notes that
autonomous vehicles and intelligent roadway infrastructure can collaborate through "cooperative
driving automation." 1 Intelligent infrastructure that supports cooperative driving automation
enables vehicles to “see around corners” in crowded cities, thereby allowing the AV fleets to more
consistently maintain their operating speed and performance within the surrounding traffic stream.
If the infrastructure fails (such as during power outages or communications loss), AV fleets can
still operate safely, but at lower speeds and with some disruption to normal traffic flow. Intelligent
infrastructure provides jurisdictions an independent, verified source for traffic incidents, as well
as a wealth of high-definition vehicle behavior to enable proactive safety and efficiency, such as
‘near-misses’ and performance metrics for signal timing.

Off-Line Stations with Parallel Berth Configuration: Stations in high-demand areas strongly
affect a transport system’s operational efficiency and throughput capacity. Locating stations off
main travel lanes, especially with parallel berths, requires significant design consideration.
Allowing passengers to board and exit independent of other vehicles dwelling activity through
provision of parallel berths improves the station operational flexibility and minimizes disruptions,
even when some vehicle delays occur. This configuration boosts system resilience, since a single
vehicle dwell time delay has limited impact on the system as a whole, which in turn ultimately
benefits energy use, fleet management, and total system cost.
 • Conference Paper: J. Sam Lott, Stanley E. Young, Andrew Duvall, and Alejandro
 Henao. 2022. “Passenger Boarding Station and Curbfront Configuration Concepts for
 On-Demand Services with Small Automated Vehicles.” Presented at the International
 Conference on Transportation and Development, Seattle, WA, 31 May–3 June 2022.
 www.nrel.gov/docs/fy22osti/81976.pdf.

Vehicle Fleet Electrification: Recently announced collaboration to bring new combinations of
vehicle, automated driving system and fleet management companies to the transportation
marketplace clearly indicate that AV technology is a natural fit with electric-propulsion vehicle
fleets. However, the deployment of battery-electric vehicles has major implications as it converges
with AMD operations.
 • Conference Paper: J. Sam Lott and Stanley E. Young. 2023. “The Convergence of
 Vehicle Electrification and Automation—Enabling Sustainable Automated Mobility
 Districts.” Presented at ASCE Inspire 2023, Arlington, VA, 16–18 Nov. 2023.
       doi.org/10.1061/9780784485163.025.
Key aspects of the beneficial combination of electrification with automation which have been
identified in the Phase III research are:
 • Off-line stations with a parallel berth configuration allow vehicles to dwell for an extended
 time until they are dispatched into service—presenting an ideal location for automated
 battery charging.
 • Battery charging in station berths allows for “opportunity charging” during off-peak
 periods without disrupting station capacity and performance levels.
 • “Deep charging” of vehicle batteries at high-power, DC fast charge locations with
 moderate charging times can also be accomplished in fully automated mode in the depot
 or in storage areas when these facilities are specifically designed for this purpose.
 • Fully automated fleets will be best served by fully automated battery charging capability
 for optimal energy and service management.

Analysis of AMD Operational Complexity: Phase III research highlights the challenges of
managing AMD operations, especially when integrating multiple AV fleets with battery-electric
vehicles for on-demand service. The study recommends using a systems engineering methodology
(SEM) to comprehensively analyze key variables such as vehicle and fleet size, storage and
charging locations, battery capacity, and operational policies.
SEM is well suited to study the impacts of passenger trip patterns and connections within the
multimodal AMD network transport system. It is especially effective in analyzing time-of-day
ridership fluctuations, such as at regional transit intermodal stations with ridership demand surge
flows when passengers connect with AMD network small-vehicle circulator systems. It can also
serve as a tool for resiliency testing and failure analysis within multimodal AMD networks.
 • Conference Paper: J. Sam Lott and Stanley E. Young. 2024. “Analysis of Automated
 Transit Network Systems with Battery-Electric Vehicles in Automated Mobility Districts.”
 Presented at the International Conference on Transportation and Development, Atlanta,
       GA, 15–18 June 2024. www.nrel.gov/docs/fy24osti/88848.pdf.

 Systems engineering methodology (SEM) provides an analytical approach with key performance
 indicator derivations suitable for AMD network planning and design using a digital twin.

Conclusions on Resilient AMD Implementation
The following points summarize selected, high-level conclusions drawn from the composite of the
Phase I, II and III research:

 • A system-of-systems view and approach is required to achieve the full vision of effective,
 safe, and efficient public mobility using AV fleets.
 • An appropriate jurisdictional authority for management and monitoring of the AMD
 network transport system is anticipated to be necessary for large-scale deployments.
 • Maximizing safety and throughput capacity of AV systems may be significantly enhanced
 with “intelligent infrastructure” at roadway intersections, and potentially at AV

 8

 stations/curbfronts which are specifically identified as passenger pickup and drop-off
 zones.
 • Electrifying AV fleets will require trade-off analyses of vehicle type, fleet configurations,
 and service mode operation, combined with battery-electric vehicle charging strategies and
 vehicle characteristics of operating range, charging time, charging facility size, and
 infrastructure power supply requirements.
 • Demand-responsive operations using small- to medium-sized vehicles can align energy
 usage and fleet management with passenger demand by adjusting vehicle size and
 movement according to ridership patterns.
 • A systems engineering design and analysis methodology creating a “digital twin” that
 simulates all modes, vehicles, and travelers is needed to analyze the critical parameters that
 determine overall performance of the AMD network mobility system.

Continuing Phase IV Research Priorities
The next phase of AMD research is underway, beginning with a comprehensive reassessment of
technological advancements and the evolving landscape of companies and AV applications. As
part of this updated view of the components comprising an AMD, the application of SEM will be
undertaken to verify and refine the usefulness of the analysis process for planning and conceptual
design of the systems.
Observation and analyses of human behavioral responses to fully automated transport systems are
also being addressed in Phase IV studies, both on the part of the individual rider and the general
public as a whole. Understanding these human behaviors is crucial for managing systems utilizing
AV technology and for ensuring effective oversight from operations control centers by fleet
management personnel.
