Bounds on the F F -Pure Threshold of Isolated Hypersurface Singularities
Yotam Svoray

Abstract
In this note, we obtain bounds for the F F -pure threshold of isolated hypersurface singularities over an algebraically closed field of positive characteristic in terms of classical singularity invariants, notably the Milnor and Tjurina numbers. For curve singularities, we show that the F F -pure threshold admits bounds, and often explicit computations, in terms of the generators of the associated value semigroup, yielding a positive-characteristic analogue of Igusa’s formula for the log canonical threshold. As applications, we derive bounds on the log canonical threshold and the Briançon–Skoda exponent of complex isolated hypersurface singularities.



1
Introduction

The goal of this note is to show that we can use techniques and tools from the study of isolated hypersurface singularities in order to bound and study the
F-
pure threshold (see Definition
2.5
) of such singularities. Specifically, this note has three main results: First, we show how we can use the Milnor and Tjurina numbers (see Definition
2.1
) of an isolated hypersurface singularity in order to bound its
F-
pure threshold:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
f\in k[[\underline{x}]]
such that
\tau(f)<\infty
.

1.

If
\mu(f)<\infty
then
\fpt(f)\geq\frac{1}{\tau(f)}-\frac{1}{\mu(f)}
.

2.

If
\mu(f)=\infty
then
\fpt(f)\geq\frac{1}{\tau(f)}

Second, we show how we can use the semigroup of values of a curve singularity (see Definition
3.1
), specifically, the first two elements in its minimal generating sequence (see Lemma
3.2
), in order to compute and bound the
F-
pure threshold of such singularities. This result is inspired by a similar result for the log canonical threshold in the complex case that was proven by Igusa in
[
38
]
(for more details see Remark
3.4
). In addition, it allows us to reduce the computation of the
F-
pure threshold of a curve singularity whose semigroup of values is generated by to elements to the computation of the
F-
pure threshold of a diagonal hypersurfaces, as given in Theorem 3.1. of
[
36
]
(and to its generalization for Thom-Sebastiani type polynomials, as given in Theorem A of
[
25
]
):

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
f\in\mathfrak{m}^{2}\subset k[[x,y]]
be irreducible Then

1.

\fpt(f)\leq\frac{1}{\beta_{0}}+\frac{1}{\beta_{1}}
.

2.

If
\Gamma(f)
is generated by two elements then
\fpt(f)=\fpt(x^{\beta_{0}}+y^{\beta_{1}})
.

Third, we use the results above to prove analogous and related results regarding the log canonical threshold (see Definition
4.1
) of a power series with integer coefficients. In addition, we also get a bound on the Briançon-Skoda exponent (see Definition
4.5
) of such power series:

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{Z}[[\underline{x}]]
such that
\mu(f)<\infty
. Then:

1.

\lct(f)\geq\frac{1}{\tau(f)}-\frac{1}{\mu(f)}
.

2.

\lct(f)\cdot\mu(f)+1\geq e^{\textup{BS}}(f)
.

F
-Pure Threshold of Isolated Singularities

In this chapter we show how we can relate between the F-pure threshold of an isolated singularity together with its Milnor and Tjurina number. There has been some past result that compute and understand the F-pure threshold of isolated singularities, especially for homogeneus and quasi-homogeneuis polynomials with isolated singularities, as in
[
33
,
46
,
17
,
40
]
. Fore more information of
F-
pure threshold in general, we refer to
[
54
]
.

We set
k
to be an algebraically closed field of characteristic
p>0
, and set
k[[\underline{x}]]=k[[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]]
to be the ring of power series over
k
in variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
, which is a local ring with maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}=\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\rangle
. We use multi-index notation when discussing power series.

We first recall some definitions and properties of isolated singularities that we use throughout this chapter:

Definition 2.1
.

Let
f,g\in k[[\underline{x}]]
.

1.

The Jacobian ideal
of
f
is defined to be
\Jac(f)=\langle\partial_{1}(f),\dots,\partial_{n}(f)\rangle
, the ideal generated by the (formal) partial derivatives of
f
.

2.

The Milnor number
of
f
is defined to
\mu(f)=\dim_{k}\left(\frac{k[[\underline{x}]]}{\Jac(f)}\right)
.

3.

The Tjurina number
of
f
is defined to
\tau(f)=\dim_{k}\left(\frac{k[[\underline{x}]]}{\Jac(f)+\langle f\rangle}\right)
.

4.

We say that
f
and
g
are
contact equivalent
and denote
f\overset{\mathcal{K}}{\sim}g
if we have a
k-
isomorphism
\frac{k[[\underline{x}]]}{\langle f\rangle}\cong\frac{k[[\underline{x}]]}{\langle g\rangle}
.

5.

We say that
f
and
g
are
right equivalent
and denote
f\overset{\mathcal{R}}{\sim}g
if there exist some
k-
automorphism
\varphi
of
k[[\underline{x}]]
such that
\varphi(f)=g
.

6.

Given an ideal
I\subset k[[\underline{x}]]
we denote by
\ord_{I}(f)
the largest
N
such that
f\in I^{N}
, and we denote
\ord_{\mathfrak{m}}(f)=\ord(f)
.

In the following proposition we summarize some known results about isolated singularities that we use throughout this text:

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
f\in k[[\underline{x}]]
. Then:

1.

If
\tau(f)<\infty
then
\mu(f)<\infty
if and only if
f\in\sqrt{\Jac(f)}
. In addition, if
f^{N}\in\Jac(f)
then
\tau(f)\leq\mu(f)\leq N\cdot\tau(f)
.

2.

(Detecting Smoothness):
\mu(f)=0
if and only if
\tau(f)=0
if and only if
f\overset{\mathcal{R}}{\sim}x_{1}
.

3.

(Stability under
\overset{\mathcal{K}}{\sim}
): Given some
g\in k[[\underline{x}]]
, if
f\overset{\mathcal{K}}{\sim}g
then
\tau(f)=\tau(g)

4.

(Stability under
\overset{\mathcal{R}}{\sim}
): Given some
g\in k[[\underline{x}]]
, if
f\overset{\mathcal{R}}{\sim}g
then
\mu(f)=\mu(g)
.

5.

(Contact Determinacy):
\tau(f)<\infty
if and only if there exists some
N>0
such that for every
g\in k[[\underline{x}]]
with
g-f\in\mathfrak{m}^{N+1}
we have that
f\overset{\mathcal{K}}{\sim}g
. In this case we can choose
N=2\tau(f)-\ord(f)+2
.

6.

(Right determinacy):
\mu(f)<\infty
if and only if there exists some
N>0
such that for every
g\in k[[\underline{x}]]
with
g-f\in\mathfrak{m}^{N}
we have that
f\overset{\mathcal{R}}{\sim}g
. In this case we can choose
N=2\mu(f)-\ord(f)+2
.

7.

If
\mu(f)<\infty
(resp.
\tau(f)<\infty
) then
\mathfrak{m}^{\mu(f)}\subset\Jac(f)
(resp.
\mathfrak{m}^{\tau(f)}\subset\Jac(f)+\langle f\rangle
).

Proof.

For the first item, see Lemma 4.21 in
[
58
]
(which in turn is based upon
[
45
]
) and Proposition 2.1 in
[
31
]
. For the second item, see Lemma 2.44 in Chapter I of
[
27
]
. For third and fourth items, see Lemma 1.2.7. in
[
14
]
. For items fifth and sixth items, see Corollary 2.4 and Theorem 2.8 in
[
13
]
. For the seventh item, see Proposition 3.1.17 in
[
14
]
.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

With respect to the first item of Proposition
2.2
, the quotient
\frac{\mu(f)}{\tau(f)}
has been studied and bounded when
f
is a power series with complex coefficient. For example, Liu in
[
45
]
showed that this quotient is bounded by
n
(the number of variables), in the case where

it was shown in
[
2
]
than this quotient is bounded above by
\frac{4}{3}
, and for a few families of surfaces with isolated singularities it was shown that this quotient is smaller than
\frac{3}{2}
. For more information, see
[
5
,
4
,
1
]
. In addition, Saito in
[

]
showed that for complex power series one has that

Lemma 2.4
.

If
f\in\mathfrak{m}^{[p^{e}]}
then
\Jac(f)\subset\mathfrak{m}^{[p^{e}]}
.

Proof.

If
f\in\mathfrak{m}^{[p^{e}]}
then there exists some
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in k[[\underline{x}]]
such that
f=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}x_{j}^{p^{e}}
. Therefore, for every
i
we have that
\partial_{i}(f)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\partial_{i}(a_{j})x_{j}^{p^{e}}+a_{j}\partial_{i}\left(x_{j}^{p^{e}}\right)
. Yet, since
k
is of characteristic
p
, we must have that
\partial_{i}\left(x_{j}^{p^{e}}\right)=0
for every
i,j
. Thus
\partial_{i}(f)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\partial_{i}(a_{j})x_{j}^{p^{e}}\in\mathfrak{m}^{[p^{e}]}
and the result follows.
∎

We now define the
F-
pure threshold of a power series:

Definition 2.5
.

Given an ideal
J
and some
f\in\sqrt{J}
, we denote by
\nu_{e}(f,J)
the largest
N
such that
f^{N}\notin J^{[p^{e}]}
, and we denote
c(f,J)=\lim_{e\to\infty}\frac{\nu_{e}(f,J)}{p^{e}}
. If
J=\mathfrak{m}
We denote
\nu_{e}(f)=\nu_{e}(f,\mathfrak{m})
and
\fpt(f)=c(f,\mathfrak{m})
, which we call the
F-pure threshold
of
f
.

Remark 2.6
.

Observe that
\fpt(f)
is stable under contact (and therefore also under right) equivalence, since if
\varphi
is a
k-
automorphism of
k[[\underline{x}]]
, then by the Jacobian criterion
\varphi
must be local and therefore
\varphi(\mathfrak{m}^{[p^{e}]})=\mathfrak{m}^{[p^{e}]}
.

Example 2.7
.

Assume that
p>2
and let
f\in k[[\underline{x}]]
be of order
2
. Then by the splitting lemma (see, for example, Theorem 2.1 in
[
29
]
) we have that
f\overset{\mathcal{R}}{\sim}x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{r}^{2}+g(x_{r+1},\dots,x_{n})
, and so if
r\geq 2
then by Fedder’s criterion (see
[
23
]
or Chapter 4.1 in
[
54
]
) we have that
\nu_{e}(f)=p^{e}
and so
\fpt(f)=1
.

The following Lemma is an based upon the proof of Lemma 3.3. in
[
54
]
and upon the proof of Proposition 1.9. in
[
47
]
:

Lemma 2.8
.

Given an ideal
I\subset k[[\underline{x}]]
and
f\in\sqrt{I}
, for every
e
we have that

p\nu_{e}(f,I)\leq\nu_{e+1}(f,I)\leq p(\nu_{e}(f,I)+1)-1.

In particular we have that
\nu_{0}(f,I)+1\geq c(f,I)\geq\nu_{0}(f,I)
.

Proof.

Note that since
f^{\nu_{e}(f,I)}\notin I^{[p^{e}]}
then we have that
f^{p\nu_{e}(f,I)}\notin I^{[p^{e+1}]}
. Therefore, we can conclude that
p\nu_{e}(f,I)<\nu_{e+1}(f,I)
, and so by induction we have that
p^{e}\nu_{0}(f,I)\leq\nu_{e}(f,I)
. Similarly, since
f^{\nu_{e}(f,I)+1}\in I^{[p^{e}]}
we have that
f^{p(\nu_{e}(f,I)+1)}\in I^{[p^{e+1}]}
, and so
p(\nu_{e}(f,I)+1)\geq\nu_{e+1}(f,I)+1
. Thus, we can conclude that
\nu_{0}(f,I)\leq\frac{\nu_{e}(f,I)}{p^{e}}\leq\nu_{0}(f,I)+1
, and the result follows by taking
e\to\infty
.
∎

Example 2.9
.

Assuming
p>5
, given some
f\in k[[x,y]]
, then by

Proposition 2.10
.

Let
f\in k[[\underline{x}]]
. Then there exists some
N\gg 0
such that for every

\fpt(f)=\fpt(f+g)

Proof.

From Theorem 2.4 and Corollary 2.22 in

Lemma 2.12
.

If
\mu(f)<\infty
then:

1.

\fpt(f)\mu(f)\geq c(f,\Jac(f))
.

2.

\fpt(f)\tau(f)\geq c(f,\Jac(f)+\langle f\rangle)
.

Proof.

Since
\mu(f)<\infty
then
\tau(f)<\infty
as well, and so by Proposition
2.2
we have that
\mathfrak{m}^{\mu(f)}\subset\Jac(f)
and
\mathfrak{m}^{\mu(f)}\subset\Jac(f)+\langle f\rangle
. Therefore
\left(\mathfrak{m}^{[p^{e}]}\right)^{\mu(f)}\subset(\Jac(f))^{[p^{e}]}
and
\left(\mathfrak{m}^{[p^{e}]}\right)^{\tau(f)}\subset(\Jac(f)+\langle f\rangle)^{[p^{e}]}
. By the definition of
\nu_{e}(f)
we have that
f^{\nu_{e}(f)+1}\in\mathfrak{m}^{[p^{e}]}
. Therefore
f^{\mu(f)\cdot(\nu_{e}(f)+1)}\in(\Jac(f))^{[p^{e}]}
and
f^{\tau(f)\cdot(\nu_{e}(f)+1)}\in(\Jac(f)+\langle f\rangle)^{[p^{e}]}
. Thus, we have that
\mu(f)(\nu_{f}(e)+1)\geq\nu_{e}(f,\Jac(f))
and
\tau(f)(\nu_{f}(e)+1)\geq\nu_{e}(f,\Jac(f)+\langle f\rangle)
, and the result follows.
∎

Corollary 2.13
.

If
\mu(f)<\infty
then
\fpt(f)\geq\frac{\nu_{0}(f,\Jac(f))}{\mu(f)}
.

Proof.

Follows from Lemma
2.8
and Lemma
2.12
.

Corollary 2.14
.

If
\tau(f)<\infty
then
\fpt(f)\geq\frac{1}{\tau(f)}\left(1-\frac{1}{\ord(f)}\right)

Proof.

Note that
f\notin\mathfrak{m}^{\ord(f)+1}
and that
\Jac(f)\subset\mathfrak{m}^{\ord(f)-1}
. Therefore, from item 3 of Proposition 1.7 in
[

]
we have that
c(f,\Jac(f)+\langle f\rangle)
is bounded below by
1-\frac{1}{\ord(f)}
, and the result follows from Lemma
2.12
.

Proof of Part
1
of Theorem

.

Follows from Lemma
2.12

which tells us that
\frac{\mu(f)}{\tau(f)}\leq\nu_{0}(f,\Jac(f))+1

Part
1
of Theorem
1
leads us directly to the question of what happens if
\tau(f)
if finite but
\mu(f)
is not. In this case (as "
\frac{1}{\mu(f)}=0
") one would expect that
\fpt(f)\geq\frac{1}{\tau(f)}

Lemma 2.15
.

Let
f\in k[[\underline{x}]]
such that
\mu(f)=\infty
and
\tau(f)<\infty
. Then there exists some
a\in\mathfrak{m}\setminus\mathfrak{m}^{2}
and some
l_{0}\gg 0
such that for every
l>l_{0}
that is not divisible by
p
we have that
\mu(f+a^{l})<\infty
.

Proof.

Note that since
\tau(f)<\infty
then we can conclude that
\langle f\rangle+\Jac(f)
is
\mathfrak{m}-
primary, and since
\mu(f)=\infty
, then
\frac{k[[\underline{x}]]}{\Jac(f)}
is one-dimensional. Since the sequence of elements
\partial_{1}(f),\dots,\partial_{n}(f)
cannot be a regular sequence, then by Theorem 1.7 in
[
61
]
(alternatively, by
[
37
]
) we have that for (Zariski) almost every sequence
\{a_{i,j}\}_{i,j}\in k
the ideal defined by
\langle\sum_{i}a_{i,1}\partial_{i}(f),\dots,\sum_{i}a_{i,n-1}\partial_{i}(f)\rangle
has co-dimension one. Yet, if the
k-
homomorphism
\varphi
by sending
x_{j}\mapsto\sum_{i}a_{i,j}x_{i}
is an isomorphism then by Lemma 1.2.7. in
[
14
]
we get that that
J(\varphi(f))=\langle\sum_{i}a_{i,1}\partial_{i}(f),\dots,\sum_{i}a_{i,n}\partial_{i}(f)\rangle
. Therefore, we can choose some
\{a_{i,j}\}_{i,j}
for which
\varphi
, and so up to right equivalence we have that
\mathfrak{a}=\langle\partial_{2}(f),\dots,\partial_{n}(f)\rangle
satisfies that
\frac{k[[\underline{x}]]}{\mathfrak{a}}
is a one-dimensional ring. Therefore, there exists some
a\in\mathfrak{m}\setminus\mathfrak{m}^{2}
such that
\mathfrak{a}+\langle a\rangle
is
\mathfrak{m}-
primary, and up to a
k-
automorphism of
k[[\underline{x}]]
, we can assume that
a=x_{1}
. Denote by
\mathfrak{p}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{p}_{r}
the non-maximal prime ideals in the primary decomposition of
\mathfrak{a}
. Note that for every
i
there exists at most one
l_{1}
such that
f+x_{1}^{l_{1}}\in\mathfrak{p}_{i}
. This is true since if
\partial_{1}(f)+x_{1}^{l_{1}},\partial_{1}(f)+x_{1}^{l_{2}}\in\mathfrak{p}
with

then we would get that

, but this is impossible as then

and
\Jac(f)+\langle x_{1}\rangle
is
\mathfrak{m}-
primary. Therefore, there exists some
l_{0}
such that for every
l>l_{0}
that is not divisible by

we have that
\partial_{1}(f)+lx_{1}^{l-1}\notin\bigcup_{i}\mathfrak{p}_{i}

Remark 2.16
.

Note that in Lemma
2.15
, we must have that
\mu(f+a^{l})\to\infty
as
l\to\infty
(assuming
l
is not divisible by
p
). This is true since otherwise the set
\{2\mu(f+a^{l})-\ord(f)+2\}_{l>l_{0}}
is finite and so has a maximum

(noting that
\ord(f)=\ord(f+a^{l})
for

). Therefore, by right determinacy (Proposition

), for every
l
and for every
g\in k[[\underline{x}]]
such that

we have that

. Since

, for every
l>N
we have that

and therefore
\mu(f)=\mu(f+a^{l})<\infty
, which is a contradiction.

Proof of Part
2
of Theorem
1.1
.

By Lemma
2.15
, there exists some
a
such that the sequence
f_{l}=f+a^{l}
satisfies
\mu(f_{l})<\infty
and
f_{l}\to f
as
l\to\infty
. Yet, since
\mu(f_{l})<\infty
for every
l
, then by Part
1
of Theorem
1
we have that
\fpt(f_{l})\geq\frac{1}{\tau(f_{l})}-\frac{1}{\mu(f_{l})}
. Since
\mu(f_{l})<\infty
for every
l
we have that
\tau(f_{l})<\infty
, and so by contact determinacy (Proposition
2.2
) for every

we have that
\tau(f_{l})=\tau(f)
. In addition, by Remark

we have that
\mu(f_{l})\to\infty
. Therefore, from Corollary 3.4 in
[

]
, by taking

we get that
\fpt(f_{l})\to\fpt(f)
, and we can conclude that
\fpt(f)\geq\frac{1}{\tau(f)}
, as desired.
∎

3
F-
Pure Threshold and Semigroup of Values

In this section we show how we can use the generators of the semigroup of values of an irreducible power series in two variables over
k
in order to bound and compute its
F-
pure threshold. Throughout this section, we assume that
n=2
, and we denote the ring of power series
k[[x_{1},x_{2}]]
as
k[[x,y]]
.

Definition 3.1
.

Given some irreducible
f\in k[[x,y]]
, the semigroup of values of
f
, denoted
\Gamma(f)
, is defined to be the set of all values
i(f,g)=\dim_{k}\left(\frac{k[[x,y]]}{\langle f,g\rangle}\right)
where
g\in k[[x,y]]\setminus\langle f\rangle
.

Proof of Part

Proof of Part

Since

Log Canonical Threshold and Reduction mod

In this section we show how we can apply the previous results to the log canonical threshold of an integer polynomial (and conclude Corollary
1.3
). For more information on log canonical threshold, see Chapter 9.3B in
[
44
]
or
[

,

]
. Note that the connection between contact equivalence, the Milnor number of a complex power series, and the log canonical threshold has been studied in the past, for example, in

.

Definition 4.1
.

Given some power series
f\in\mathbb{C}[[\underline{x}]]
, the
log canonical threshold
of
f
, denoted
\lct(f)
, is the infimum over all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
for which the function
\frac{1}{|f|^{\lambda}}
is
L^{2}
(i.e. there exists a neighborhood
0\in U\subset\mathbb{R}
such that
\int_{U}\frac{d\mu}{|f|^{2\lambda}}<\infty
where
\mu
is the standard Lebesgue measure).

From now until the end of this section, let
f\in\mathbb{Z}[\underline{x}]
be a polynomial with integer coefficients, and for every prime
p
, let
f_{p}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[[\underline{x}]]
be the reduction of
f
mod
p
. The following proposition, presented in
[
30
]
, shows the relation between
\lct(f)
and
\fpt(f_{p})
. For more information on this relation, see Chapter 6.4 in
[
54
]
or
[
48
]
.

Proposition 4.2
.

For every prime
p
we have that
\fpt(f_{p})\leq\lct(f)
. In addition,
\lim_{p\to\infty}\fpt(f_{p})=\lct(f)
.

An analogous result for the Tjurina and Milnor number exists, presented as Corollary 55 in
[
28
]
. Given some
f\in\mathbb{Z}[[\underline{x}]]
, we define
\mu_{p}(f)
and
\tau_{p}(f)
to be the Milnor and Tjurina numbers of
f_{p}
, and by
\mu(f)
and
\tau(f)
the Milnor and Tjurina numbers of
f\in\mathbb{C}[[\underline{x}]]
, respectively.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{C}[[\underline{x}]]
. Then

1.

If
\mu(f)<\infty
then
\mu(f)=\mu_{p}(f)
for every
p\gg 0
.

2.

If
\mu_{p}(f)<\infty
for some
p
then
\mu(f)\leq\mu_{p}(f)
and
\mu_{q}(f)\leq\mu_{p}(f)
for every
q\gg 0
.

Remark 4.4
.

An analogous result to that of Proposition
4.3
is true for
\tau(f)
and
\tau_{p}(f)
, respectively, with the proof appearing in the same reference.

Proof of Part
1
of Corollary
1.3
.

Since
\mu(f)
is finite then
\tau(f)
is finite as well, and so from Proposition
4.3
and Remark
4.4
we can conclude that for every
p\gg 0
we have
\mu_{p}(f)=\mu(f)
and
\tau_{p}(f)=\tau(f)
. Therefore, from Theorem
1
we have that

\lct(f)\geq\fpt(f_{p})\geq\frac{1}{\tau_{p}(f)}-\frac{1}{\mu_{p}(f)}=\frac{1}{\tau(f)}-\frac{1}{\mu(f)},

and the result follows.
∎

Similarly, we present an analogue result for Corollary
2.13
.

Definition 4.5
.

Given some
f\in\mathbb{C}[[\underline{x}]]
, the
Briançon-Skoda exponent
of

, denoted

, is the smallest

such that

.

Remark 4.6
.

Then name Briançon-Skoda comes from the Briançon-Skoda theorem (see
[
55
]
), since it tells us that
e^{\textup{BS}}(f)\leq n
for every
f\in\mathbb{C}[[\underline{x}]]
(where
n
is the number of variables). Similarly, by the Briançon-Skoda theorem for power series in positive characteristic, as explained in Remark 4.5 of
[
31
]
, we have that if
f\in\sqrt{\Jac(f)}
then
\nu_{0}(f,\Jac(f))+1\leq n
. The Briançon-Skoda exponent has been studied in the context of topology and complex isolated singularities, for example, in
[
39
]
and in Section 3 of
[
6
]
(which in turn is based upon
[
62
]
).

Lemma 4.7
.

For every
p\gg 0
we have that
\nu_{0}(f_{p},\Jac(f))+1=e^{\textup{BS}}(f)
.

Proof.

Note that for every
p
we have that
N_{p}=\nu_{0}(f_{p},\Jac(f))+1
is the smallest integer such that
f_{p}^{N_{p}}\in\Jac(f)
. In addition,
N_{p}<\infty
for every
p\gg 0
since
\mu(f)<\infty
and from Proposition
4.3
we have that
\mu(f_{p})<\infty
for every
p\gg 0
and therefore
f_{p}\in\sqrt{\Jac(f_{p})}
from Proposition
2.2
. Denote
N=e^{\textup{BS}}(f)
. Since
\mathbb{Q}\to\mathbb{C}
is a faithfully flat map then we can assume that
f^{N}\in\Jac(f)\subset\mathbb{Q}[[\underline{x}]]
, and therefore we can find some
c,c_{1},\dots,c_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
cf^{N}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\partial_{i}(f)
. Thus, for every prime
p>\max\{c,c_{1},\dots,c_{n}\}
we have that
\overline{c}=c\,\operatorname{mod}\,p
is not zero and so
f_{p}^{N}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\overline{c}^{-1}\overline{c}_{i}\partial_{i}(f_{p})\in\Jac(f_{p})
and therefore

. On the other hand,

and so we have that
\frac{\Jac(f)+\langle f^{N-1}\rangle}{\Jac(f)}\neq 0
. Therefore, for every

we have that
\frac{\Jac(f_{p})+\langle f_{p}^{N-1}\rangle}{\Jac(f_{p})}=\frac{\Jac(f)+\langle f^{N-1}\rangle}{\Jac(f)+p}\neq 0
, i.e.

, which gives us that

, and the result follows.
∎

Proof of Part
2
of Corollary
1.3
.

As in Corollary
1
we have that

for every
