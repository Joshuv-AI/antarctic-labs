The complete defensive strategy: Defining complementary pitching and fielder positioning strategies in baseball

William Melville, Christopher Archibald, David Grimsman

ABSTRACT
In baseball, the characteristics of a pitch have an effect on a batted ball’s trajectory. For example, batters are more likely to hit a fastball to the opposite side of the field and to pull a changeup. In this paper, we present novel matchup and pitch-specific approaches to positioning fielders to maximize outs and minimize runs allowed against opposing hitters. Unfortunately, the pitch-specific approach may reveal information about the upcoming pitch to the opposing batter. Thus, we combine our pitch-specific positioning strategy with an existing pitch selection game theory model that assumes the batter can observe the pitch up to a decision point, at which point they must decide whether to swing at or take the pitch. The combined pitching and positioning strategies, which we call the complete defensive strategy, complement each other by mixing in an occasional bluff where the pitcher throws the wrong pitch for the positioning strategy. Thus, the batter cannot use the positioning strategy to perfectly predict the upcoming pitch, and the defense is able to utilize the pitch-specific positioning strategy. We show that the matchup and pitch-specific approaches to positioning are not significantly more effective at preventing hits or runs than a previously defined batter-specific approach. Thus, although a baseball team could use our methodology to define pitch-specific positioning strategies that do not tip the pitch to the batter, they could define similarly effective positioning strategies using much simpler models that only consider the batter’s tendencies.

1. Introduction In baseball, it is the responsibility of a team’s defense to prevent runs by getting opposing hitters out. One prominent strategic decision a defense must make is how to position their seven fielders behind the pitcher and catcher. Fielder positioning strategy has been thoroughly researched [ 1 – 7 ]. One common criticism is that the positioning strategy and pitching strategy are defined separately when really they should complement each other. Fig 1 shows the distribution of Major League Baseball (MLB) batted ball spray angles and launch angles from 2019 through 2023 [ 8 – 10 ]. Typically a spray angle of −45 corresponds to the third baseline and 45 corresponds to the first baseline, but we flipped the sign of the spray angles for left-handed batters so we could compare them directly with right-handed batters. Thus, a pulled batted ball has a negative spray angle and an opposite field batted ball (oppo) has a positive spray angle. It is apparent that hitters generally pull non-fastballs more than fastballs, and they hit low pitches with lower launch angles than high pitches. It follows that if a pitcher plans to attack a hitter with breaking balls below the strike zone, a complementary positioning strategy may set infielders on the pull side of the infield to field pulled ground balls. Similarly, if a pitcher plans to attack a hitter with elevated fastballs, a complementary positioning strategy may shade outfielders to the opposite side to field oppo fly balls. Even when the positioning strategy is defined separately from the pitching plan, it may still benefit from considering the pitcher’s repertoire. For example, it may be optimal to shade fielders more oppo for a pitcher who throws 98 mph fastballs than for a pitcher who throws 88 mph fastballs whether they plan to attack the opposing hitter with fastballs or not. 10.1371/journal.pone.0341045.g001 Fig 1 MLB spray and launch angles. Distribution of MLB spray angles partitioned by pitch type and launch angles partitioned by pitch height. It is evident that batters tend to pull non-fastballs more than fastballs and tend to hit low pitches lower than high pitches. In this paper, we address the concern that positioning strategies do not appropriately complement pitchers and their pitching strategies by adapting the batter-specific positioning model of [ 7 ] to define pitcher and pitch-specific positioning strategies. In particular, we make four key contributions: 1 A novel model of batted ball trajectory probability distributions that is made up of three density neural networks [ 11 ]. These neural networks consider both batter tendencies and the effect that pitches have on batted balls, and they are more accurate than the batter-only models of [ 7 ]. 2 A matchup (pitcher and batter) specific positioning strategy that prevents more runs and hits than the batter-specific positioning strategy of [ 7 ]. 3 A pitch-specific positioning strategy that prevents more runs and hits than the matchup-specific positioning strategy as well as the batter-specific strategy of [ 7 ]. Of course, a team may hesitate to adopt the pitch-specific positioning strategy since using a different positioning strategy for every pitch would tip the pitch to the batter. Rather than choosing a pitch and then the corresponding pitch-specific positioning strategy, the pitching strategy and positioning strategy should be defined so as to ensure that the positioning strategy does not reveal too much information about the upcoming pitch and to ensure that the pitcher throws pitches that if hit in play will result in batted balls that the fielders are in a good position to field. Thus, in Section 2.3 we present the fourth and final contribution of this paper: 4 A complete defensive strategy defined by complementary positioning and pitching strategies. In [ 7 ], they define an optimal positioning strategy as one that minimizes the expected runs allowed over a simulation of batted balls, i.e., a ψ * satisfying ψ * ∈ arg min ψ ∈ Ψ 𝔼 𝐛 ~ d [ f ( 𝐛 , ψ ) ] (1) where Ψ is the set of admissable positioning strategies, 𝐛 is a random variable of batted ball trajectories drawn from a batter-specific batted ball trajectory probability distribution d , and f is the objective function representing expected runs allowed. We adapt this model to define pitcher and pitch-specific positioning strategies by replacing d with our pitch-specific batted ball trajectory density neural networks (Section 2.1 ). The complementary pitching and positioning strategies that make up the complete defensive strategy are given by an equilibrium in a zero-sum game where the defense chooses the positioning strategy and pitching strategy to minimize expected runs, and the batter chooses their swing-decision strategy to maximize expected runs. An equilibrium is a positioning strategy ψ * , pitching strategy p * , and batter swing decision strategy s * satisfying ψ * , p * ∈ arg min p ∈ Δ P ψ ∈ Ψ max s ∈ [ Δ S ] U ( p , s , c , ψ ) (2a) s * ∈ arg max s ∈ [ Δ S ] min p ∈ Δ P ψ ∈ Ψ U ( p , s , c , ψ ) , (2b) where Ψ is again the set of admissable positioning strategies, Δ P is the set of pitching strategies, [ Δ S ] is the set of batter swing-decision strategies, c is the game context which is defined by the batter, pitcher, and ball and strike count, and U is the utility function that outputs expected runs given a defensive strategy, batter strategy, and game context. We describe Δ P , [ Δ S ] , and U in more detail in Section 2.3 . A key difference between (1) and (2) is in (1) the batter does not choose a counterstrategy to the defense’s positioning strategy ψ , but in (2) the batter chooses their counterstrategy s to maximize utility against the defense’s positioning and pitching strategies. This difference requires different objective functions, hence why in (1) the objective function f is a function of just the batted ball and the positioning strategy, but in (2) the objective function U is a function of the pitching strategy, positioning strategy, and batter strategy. The game model in (2) allows us to utilize the pitch-specific positioning strategies without completely revealing the upcoming pitch to the batter. The matchup-specific positioning strategy and the complete defensive strategy effectively address the criticism that positioning strategies should complement the pitcher or the pitching strategy. However, we find that these approaches are much more complex than a batter-specific approach, and they do not result in significantly fewer hits or runs allowed ( Table 1 ). We therefore conclude that baseball teams that wish to prioritize simplicity with a positioning strategy that only considers the batters’ tendencies are justified in doing so, and they will not necessarily have a significantly worse defense than teams with more complex, pitcher or pitch-specific positioning strategies. 10.1371/journal.pone.0341045.t001 Table 1 Comparison of the positioning strategies. Methods and results 2.1. The batted ball trajectory neural networks Denote h , v , and ϵ as the horizontal spray angle, vertical launch angle, and exit speed of a batted ball. Our goal is to model the joint distribution 𝒫 ( h , v , ϵ ) . We learn this model using MLB ball tracking data, which provides the exit speed, launch angle, and hit coordinates of batted balls [ 8 – 10 ]. We define the spray angles using the hit coordinates with the formula defined by [ 12 ], which gives balls hit down the third baseline an angle of − 45 ∘ and balls hit down the first baseline an angle of 45 ∘ . By the chain rule of probability, we can write the joint distribution 𝒫 ( h , v , ϵ ) as 𝒫 ( h , v , ϵ ) = 𝒫 ( h ) 𝒫 ( v | h ) 𝒫 ( ϵ | v , h ) . (3) Thus, we model the joint distribution using three density neural networks. First, we train a neural network to model the spray angle distribution 𝒫 ( h ) , then we train a neural network to model the launch angle distribution conditioned on spray angle 𝒫 ( v | h ) , and finally we train a neural network to model the exit speed distribution conditioned on both launch and spray angle 𝒫 ( ϵ | v , h ) . 2.1.1. The spray angle distribution 𝒫 ( h ) . We prepare the data by first flipping the sign of spray angles for left-handed batters, as was done in Fig 1 , which transforms them into right-handed batters. This gives all pulled balls negative spray angles and all oppo balls positive spray angles. Information can now be shared among all hitters without effects from handedness. We then remove spray angles outside of the interval [ − 55 , 55 ] . Finally, we shift and scale the spray angles so they are between 0 and 1, which allows us to learn Beta distributions which have support [0,1]. The spray angle density neural network models the shifted and scaled spray angle distribution using a mixture of Betas. Each batter is represented by an index, which is first passed through an embedding layer that outputs a 15-dimensional batter embedding vector. This embedding vector is concatenated with the other inputs, which are the batter and pitcher handedness and the z-scores of a pitch’s release speed, horizontal and vertical movement, and horizontal and vertical plate location. The batter embedding ensures that the resulting spray angle distribution is batter specific, and the pitch characteristics ensure that it is pitch specific. The concatenated vector is passed through a feed forward network made up of four linear layers with ReLU activation functions between them. The output of the feed forward network is passed through three separate components: the weights component, the α component, and the β component. The weights component is a linear layer with a softmax activation to ensure the weights sum to one. The α component is a linear layer whose output is passed through a ReLU activation function and finally added to [1,1]. The β component is a linear layer whose output is passed through a ReLU activation function and finally added to [1,1]. The purpose of adding the ReLU outputs to [1,1] is to ensure that the α and β parameters in the Beta distributions are positive. Fig 2 depicts the spray angle neural network architecture. The network was trained to minimize the average negative log likelihood of the spray angle data in the output distribution. 10.1371/journal.pone.0341045.g002 Fig 2 𝒫 ( h ) neural network. This takes as input a pitch and a batter and outputs a spray angle distribution that is batter and pitch-specific. We trained the neural network on all MLB batters’ spray angle data from 2020-2022 and validated on data from 2023. For each batted ball in the validation set, we sampled a spray angle from the corresponding neural network spray angle distribution. The results are shown in Fig 3 . Defining and evaluating optimal positioning strategies Recall, we define an optimal positioning strategy as a ψ * satisfying Eq (1) , ψ * ∈ arg min ψ ∈ Ψ 𝔼 𝐛 ~ d [ f ( 𝐛 , ψ ) ] . [7] defined two different objective functions: f outs and f woba . f outs represents the negative expected outs, so minimizing it is equivalent to maximizing expected outs. f woba represents the expected weighted on base average (wOBA). wOBA is related to runs by an affine transformation [ 13 ], so minimizing f woba is equivalent to minimizing runs. f outs and f woba are defined in more detail in Appendix A. We defined our positioning strategies by minimizing f woba . Like [ 7 ], we find an approximately optimal solution to (1) using stochastic gradient descent with momentum. Stochastic gradient descent is an unconstrained optimization method, however, the positioning strategies ψ are constrained to be in the set of admissable positioning strategies Ψ . To meet the constraints created by the 2023 shift limits [ 14 ], we use projected stochastic gradient descent [ 15 ]. After each step of gradient descent we check that three conditions are met: The first baseman must be within 40 feet of first base. If not, his position is projected back into Ψ by maintaining the relative angle with first base while decreasing the distance back down to 40 feet. Every infielder must be in the infield dirt, i.e., they must be within 95 feet of the pitching rubber [ 16 ]. If not, their position is projected back into Ψ by maintaining the relative angle with the rubber while decreasing the distance back down to 95 feet. There must be two infielders on either side of second base. If the third baseman or shortstop have spray angles greater than 0 ∘ , they are projected back into Ψ by maintaining the depth from home plate while decreasing the spray angle back down to 0 ∘ . Likewise, if the second baseman has a spray angle less than 0 ∘ , his position is projected back into Ψ by maintaining the depth from home plate while increasing the spray angle back up to 0 ∘ . The difference between the batter-specific positioning strategies of [ 7 ] and our novel matchup and pitch-specific positioning strategies is the choice of batted ball trajectory distribution d used to simulate the batted balls 𝐛 . For the matchup-specific positioning strategies, we start by taking all available pitch tracking data where the pitcher in the matchup faced a batter of the same handedness as the batter in the matchup. For example, to find a positioning strategy when right-handed pitcher Nick Anderson faces right-handed batter Mookie Betts, we start with all the pitches that Anderson threw to any right-handed batter. The resulting set of pitches are run through a machine learning model that predicts the probability of a ball in play given the pitch characteristics and the handedness of the batter and pitcher. If p i is the predicted probability of a ball in play on the i th pitch in the data, then we choose to keep pitch i in our dataset with probability p i and remove it with probability 1 − p i . Finally, we sample some number of pitches with replacement from the remaining dataset. The resulting sample represents a set of pitches that the pitcher in the matchup might realistically throw to the batter in the matchup and that the batter in the matchup could realistically hit into play. We finally run each pitch as well as the batter’s index through our density neural networks from Section 2.1 , and we sample one batted ball from each of the resulting joint probability distributions over spray angle, launch angle, and exit speed. We use the exact same approach for our pitch-specific positioning strategies only we filter the initial pitch dataset down to only include the pitch type of interest. For example, to find a positioning strategy for Anderson’s curveball against Betts, the initial pitch dataset would be all the curveballs that Anderson threw to a right-handed batter. When drawing batted ball samples, we use Tropicana Field’s outfield dimensions, acquired from [ 17 ], to identify batted balls that would land foul or over the Trop’s outfield fence. Tropicana Field was chosen because it is a dome; its atmospheric conditions are consistent. Thus, we can more consistently estimate the landing spot and hang time of a batted ball given its spray angle, launch angle, and exit speed assuming it was hit at the Trop rather than an outdoor stadium with inconsistent weather conditions. Nevertheless, it is straightforward to estimate the landing spot and hang time models in more extreme, outdoor environments for baseball teams who are not playing indoors [ 7 ]. To test the effectiveness of our matchup and pitch-specific positioning strategies compared to [ 7 ]’s batter-specific positioning strategy, we used f outs and f woba to calculate the expected outs and wOBA of each positioning strategy on 61,200 batted balls in 2023 that were not home runs and that would have stayed in Tropicana Field. To give an idea of the effect that the pitch has on positioning, Fig 11 gives a histogram of the distance between the batter-specific positioning and the pitch-specific positioning on each of the 61,200 batted balls broken down by the outfield, middle infield (shortstop and second base), and corner infield (first and third base). To get from the batter-specific positioning to the pitch-specific positioning, a corner infielder has to move an average of 5 feet, a middle infielder has to move an average of 9.5 feet, and an outfielder has to move an average of 11 feet, so the pitch has a noticeable effect on the recommended positioning. 10.1371/journal.pone.0341045.g011 Fig 11 Histograms giving the distance that a corner infielder, middle infielder, or outfielder has to travel to get from the batter-specific positioning to the pitch-specific positioning. All fielders will change their positioning by a noticeable amount when the pitch is incorporated into the positioning strategy, with outfielders moving the furthest, followed by middle infielders, and then corner infielders moving the least. Table 1 shows the expected batting average, the expected hits allowed per team, the expected wOBA, and the expected weighted runs above average (wRAA) per team of each positioning strategy on the 61,200 batted balls. Note that wRAA was estimated by applying the affine transformation to wOBA to get runs [ 13 ]. Table 1 also reports p values in two-sample t-tests comparing the mean expected batting averages and expected wOBAs of the matchup and pitch-specific positioning strategies with the means of the batter-specific strategies. The alternative hypotheses in those t-tests are that the matchup and pitch-specific means are less than the batter-specific means. The results show that all three positioning strategies effectively prevent runs compared to MLB average, as measured by wRAA. The pitch-specific positioning strategy is the best at preventing runs and hits, followed by the matchup-specific positioning strategy. However, none of the improvements over the batter-specific positioning strategy are statistically significant at a significance threshold of α = 0.05 except for the pitch-specific strategy’s improvement to expected batting average. Even that is only worth two fewer hits per one thousand batted balls. This result partially contradicts the common criticism that effective positioning strategies must complement the pitching strategy or the pitcher’s repertoire. It seems that defenses can create effective positioning strategies that only consider the batter’s tendencies. Nevertheless, some teams who are desperate enough for a small competitive advantage may be willing to take on the added complexity of the pitch-specific positioning approach. Thus, in the next section we present our complete defensive strategy, which allows defenses to use the pitch-specific strategies without tipping their pitch to the batter. 2.3. The complete defensive strategy A complete defensive strategy must consider the interplay between the positioning, pitching, and batter swing-decision strategies and their effect on the expected run value of a matchup between batter and defense. That is why we propose the zero-sum batter/defense game model with equilibrium (2) as an appropriate solution to identifying a complete defensive strategy. This game is largely based on the pitching game from [ 18 ], which we review in the following subsection. We then show how to incorporate the positioning strategy into this pitching game to get our complete defensive strategy. We conclude with an example implementation of our complete defensive strategy. 2.3.1. The pitching game. This subsection serves as a review of the pitching game of [ 18 ]. The pitching game is a zero-sum game between pitcher and batter where the batter has to choose whether or not to swing at a pitch based on the information they can observe about the pitch. Therefore, each pitch can be partitioned into some properties that are observable and some that are unobservable to the batter. Denote the set of possible observable properties as P o and the set of possible unobservable properties as P u . The pitcher takes action by choosing an intended pitch from the action set P = P o × P u . It is important to note that the pitcher’s action is an intended pitch not an actual pitch. Pitchers do not always hit their intended target, so [ 18 ] developed a model of pitcher command skill which they used to estimate the probability distribution over actual pitches in P given a choice of intended pitch in P . The pitcher’s pitching strategy is the decision rule that they use to choose an action in P . The pitcher is allowed to use a behavioral strategy , which is a probability distribution over all of the possible actions in P . The pitcher takes action under a behavioral strategy by randomly sampling an intended pitch from the behavioral strategy distribution. It is important to allow the pitcher to use a behavioral strategy because it allows them to take advantage of the uncertainty the batter has about the incoming pitch due to the unobservable properties. If the pitcher chose their pitch deterministically instead of randomly, the batter would have no uncertainty about the incoming pitch, e.g., if a pitcher only throws their fastball and never their curveball, batters know to expect a fastball. Denote the pitcher’s strategy set as Δ P , which is the set of all probability distributions over P . Thus, for p ∈ Δ P and π = ( π o , π u ) ∈ P , p ( π ) is the probability that the pitcher intends to throw pitch π with observable properties π o and unobservable properties π u under strategy p . The batter’s action set is S = {swing, take}. The batter’s behavioral strategy is a probability distribution over S for each information set . In this context, an information set is a set of pitches that are indistinguishable to the batter given what they can observe about the pitch. For example, if we assume the batter can observe everything about the pitch before deciding to swing, then the batter has | P | information sets, one for each pitch in P . If we assume the batter can observe nothing about the pitch before deciding to swing, then the batter has just one information set corresponding to all of the pitches in P which are indistinguishable to the batter. The set of observable properties of the pitch P o define the information sets; the batter has one information set for every element of P o unless P o = ∅ , in which case the batter has only one information set. Denote Δ S as the set of probability distributions over S and [ Δ S ] as the set of possible behavioral strategies of the batter. For s ∈ [ Δ S ] , denote s π o ∈ Δ S as the probability distribution the batter uses to sample an action under strategy s when they observe a pitch with observable properties π o ∈ P o . Thus for τ ∈ S , s π o ( τ ) is the probability that the batter takes action τ under strategy s after observing π o ∈ P o . This notation illustrates that the batter strategizes and takes action based on what they observe about the pitch ( Fig 13 ). Note that a behavioral strategy by definition is a probability distribution over actions in each of an agent’s information sets. We can define the pitcher’s behavioral strategy as a probability distribution over their action set P because the pitcher only has one information set since they make the first move in the game. Therefore, defining their strategy as a distribution over P is equivalent to defining their strategy as a distribution over P for each of their information sets. Denote C as the set of all game contexts, where a game context is characterized by a batter, pitcher, and ball and strike count. Both the pitcher and batter have access to a utility function U : Δ P × [ Δ S ] × C → ℝ which outputs the expected run value given the pitcher strategy, batter strategy, and game context. Naturally, negative run values favor the pitcher, whereas positive run values favor the batter. Therefore, the pitcher’s goal is to use the pitching strategy p ∈ Δ P that minimizes U , and the batter’s goal is to use the strategy s ∈ [ Δ S ] that maximizes U . It takes some small amount of time for a batter to complete their swing. Naturally, there is a decision point along a pitch’s trajectory where if the batter has not decided to swing by that point, they no longer have enough time to complete their swing before the ball reaches the catcher’s glove. Therefore, a realistic assumption is that the batter can only observe the full pitch up to this decision point. A pitcher can throw multiple pitch types through the same decision point, so the batter does not know exactly where the pitch will end up after the decision point because they are not sure which pitch type the pitcher threw. For example, a fastball that ends up at the top of the strike zone may share a decision point with a curveball that ends up at the bottom of the strike zone ( Fig 12 ). Pitchers often use a pitching strategy known as tunneling where they try to throw consecutive pitches of different types along a similar trajectory up to the decision point [ 20 ]. Comparing the complete defensive strategy and the pitch-specific positioning strategy. By including the matchup-specific positioning strategy as one of the candidate positioning strategies for the complete defensive strategy, we guarantee that the complete defensive strategy will always be at least as effective as the matchup-specific positioning strategy. It seems obvious that the complete defensive strategy will also be better than the pitch-specific positioning strategy because it does not tip the pitch to the batter. However, it is possible that the value of the pitch-specific positioning outweighs the cost of tipping the pitch. In this section, we investigate that question. Recall, in the pitching game we partition the pitch into the set of observable and unobservable properties P o and P u . In the complete defensive strategy, we assume that P o = D and P u = T . If the defense chose to use the pitch-specific positioning strategy for every pitch, then we would have P o = D × T and P u = ∅ . Equivalently, we would have P o = L × T and P u = ∅ because there is a known mapping from D × T to L . In other words, if the defense uses the pitch-specific positioning strategy corresponding to the pitcher’s pitch, we assume the batter can observe the full trajectory of the pitch when making their swing decision. In [ 18 ], they also define the optimal pitching strategy when P o = L × T . In a perfect information game, a pure strategy Nash equilibrium exists, which is an equilibrium that does not require randomizing. Thus, the optimal pitching strategy can be defined as the single intended pitch that minimizes expected utility under the assumption that the batter will observe the full pitch trajectory and make the maximizing swing decision for the pitch they observe. Conclusion In this paper, we addressed the common criticism that positioning strategies do not complement the pitching strategy. We learned novel pitch-specific batted ball trajectory distributions and showed how they can be used to define matchup and pitch-specific positioning strategies. To implement the pitch-specific positioning strategy without tipping the pitch to the batter, we combined our pitch-specific positioning strategies with an existing pitching model to determine how frequently a pitcher needs to bluff in order for the value of the pitch-specific positioning to outweigh the cost of revealing more information about the upcoming pitch. We call the complementary positioning and pitching strategies the complete defensive strategy. The complete defensive strategy allows baseball defenses to utilize the most effective pitch-specific positioning strategies without revealing too much information about the upcoming pitch to the batter. In an illustrative example where pitcher Nick Anderson faced off against batter Mookie Betts, we found that the complete defensive strategy was more effective than both the matchup-specific positioning strategy that does not risk tipping the pitch to the batter and the pitch-specific positioning strategy that completely reveals the pitch to the opposing batter. Ultimately, we found that positioning strategies that incorporate information about the pitch/pitcher are not significantly more effective than strategies that only consider the batters’ tendencies, but they are significantly more difficult to implement. We therefore conclude that the criticism that positioning strategies do not complement the pitching strategy is unwarranted, and most baseball teams would likely be better off using a simple batter-specific positioning strategy rather than the complete defensive strategy. B. OptimusPitch OptimusPitch (OP) is a neural network developed by [ 18 ] for the pitching game. It is used to estimate 𝒫 ( x | π o , π u , τ , c ) for all pitch outcomes x ∈ 𝒳 in the expected run value functions (5) and (10). The original version of OP was a recurrent neural network that considered the full prior sequence of pitches and their outcomes when predicting the outcome of the next pitch. That version would have made calculating the complete defensive strategy impractical because the strategy for the next pitch cannot be calculated until the outcome of the current pitch is known. Thus, we created a simpler version of OP that just takes as input the release speed, movement, spin rate, and location of the current pitch along with the batter and pitcher handedness, the balls and strikes, and a batter-specific vector embedding. The batter embedding is similar to the embeddings used in the density neural nets and ensures that the pitching strategies are batter-specific. C. Pitcher execution error distributions To estimate the execution error distributions for individual pitchers, we utilize the Joint Estimation of Execution and Decision-making Skill (JEEDS) method [ 27 ]. The JEEDS algorithm takes as input the observed actions of an agent acting in a domain, and produces as output an estimate of the execution error distribution (execution skill) and a parameter representing the agent’s decision-making skill. Crucially, JEEDS does not require knowledge of the action the agent intended, only the one that actually was executed. JEEDS also needs to know the reward that the agent would get for any action that could be executed. In our case each observed action is a pitch from a pitcher. To estimate pitcher accuracy we specifically focus on the location where the pitch crosses the plate. Using OptimusPitch (OP) (see Appendix B) combined with an expected run value function, the reward for any possible location the pitcher could have located the observed pitch is computed. This is done by modifying the pitch location while keeping the other characteristics of the pitch (velocity, movement, spin, ...) fixed, and then passing the modified pitch data into OP and the expected run function, resulting in a single value estimated for that location. This constitutes the reward information required by JEEDS. In our work JEEDS produced an execution error estimate for each pitcher and pitch type using up to 1000 most recent pitches of that type available for each pitcher. The estimate produced by JEEDS is the standard deviation of a symmetric two-dimensional multivariate Gaussian distribution. This distribution, centered on an intended target location, is used as the distribution over actual pitch locations required elsewhere in this work. The other pitch characteristics are assumed to be the pitcher’s average for each pitch type.
