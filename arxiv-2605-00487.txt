Abstract
. We introduce a technology to formally verify that a software system satisfies a temporal specification of functional correctness, without revealing the system itself. Our method combines a deductive approach to model checking to obtain a formal certificate of correctness for the system, with zero-knowledge proofs to convince an external verifier that the system—kept secret—complies with its specification of correctness—made public. We consider proof certificates represented as ranking functions, and introduce both an explicit-state and a symbolic scheme for model checking in zero knowledge. Our explicit-state scheme assumes systems represented as transition graphs. We use polynomial commitments to convince the verifier that the public proof certificates correspond to the secret transition relation. Our symbolic scheme assumes systems specified as linear guarded commands and uses piecewise-linear ranking functions. We apply Farkas’ lemma to obtain a witness for the validity of the ranking function with public and secret components, and employ sigma protocols for matrix multiplication and range proofs to convince the verifier of the witness’s existence. We built a prototype to demonstrate the practical efficacy of our two schemes on linear temporal logic verification examples. Our technology enables formal verification in domains where both the safety and the confidentiality of the system under analysis are critical.



1.
Introduction

Model checking is the problem of determining whether a software, hardware, or cyber-physical system satisfies a formal specification of correctness. Unlike traditional testing, which is inherently non-exhaustive, model checking provides formal proof that the system behaves as intended
(
7
;
10
;
18
)
. It is an established standard in electronic design automation, protocol verification, and control software development in domains where safety and security are highly critical. Moreover, it is increasingly viewed as a promising avenue for the safety assurance of AI systems. Formal proofs of correctness guarantee the absolute (or probabilistic) absence of violations with respect to a given specification, thereby enabling verifiable compliance with safety standards and fostering trust between technology providers, regulators, and users of such systems.

Scalability has been a central focus of the formal methods community for decades, yet the industrial adoption of model checking faces another pressing challenge: confidentiality.
The traditional formal verification algorithms require disclosure of the system description in order to convince an external verifier of the system’s compliance with its specification.
In a setting where formal verification is a requirement potentially involving multiple jurisdictions, standardisation bodies, or collaborating companies, the obligation to disclose a system description is likely to create tension between regulators’ or customers’ need to ensure compliance with safety requirements and providers’ desire to protect their intellectual property.
To date, no technology exists that can provide an external verifier with a formal proof of safety and correctness in zero knowledge, without disclosing the system itself.

Zero-knowledge proofs provide a cryptographic mechanism by which a prover can convince a verifier that a given statement is true without revealing any information beyond the validity of the statement itself. They are widely used in areas such as cryptographic protocol design, blockchain and cryptocurrency systems, privacy-preserving authentication and identity management, secure electronic voting, and confidential data access control. In the context of system verification and certification, zero-knowledge proofs offer a the opportunity to demonstrate compliance with formal specifications while preserving confidentiality, as correctness claims can be validated without disclosing system internals, proprietary designs, or sensitive customer data,
eliminating reliance on trust between parties.
Despite their maturity and broad adoption in multiple domains, the application of zero-knowledge proofs to formal verification remains rare
(
56
;
55
;
53
)
.

We introduce the first approach to model checking in zero knowledge:
Zero-Knowledge Model Checking
(ZKMC). Our method combines proof-theoretic model checking with zero-knowledge proofs to construct a proof certificate that the system satisfies the specification and then convince an external verifier that our
public
artefacts—the specification and the proof certificate—are valid with respect to our
secret
artefact—the system under analysis—without revealing any information about the system itself.

Our setting considers the model checking of reactive systems with discrete state spaces against

\omega
-regular specifications, subsuming linear temporal logic (LTL)
(
61
)
.
We formulate the model checking problem as establishing that the Büchi automaton for the complementary specification never accepts an execution of the system, that is, that the language of the corresponding synchronous composition is empty. To prove this, we require a ranking function showing that the automaton cannot accept any execution
(
73
;
72
)
. Accordingly, our proof certificate consists precisely of this ranking function. Building on this premise, we realise ZKMC through two complementary algorithms: an explicit-state and a symbolic algorithm.

Our explicit-state algorithm assumes systems represented as finite-state transition graphs whose states are labelled with atomic propositions, i.e., as Kripke structures (
Section

4
). We associate the system with a ranking function over the state space of the system alongside an appropriate automaton. The task is to convince an external verifier of the validity of the proof certificate without revealing the system’s initial states or its transition relation. Our scheme employs
KZG polynomial commitments with batch evaluation
to ensure that any hypothetical state or transition that would violate the publicly disclosed proof certificate cannot correspond to an actual initial state or transition of the secret system. Upon successful verification, which is guaranteed by the standard cryptographic properties of polynomial commitments
(
47
)
, the proof of correctness follows by contraposition.
Our proof is two group elements regardless of state-space size; prover work is quadratic in the number of state of the system.

Our symbolic algorithm assumes reactive systems represented by integer variables and guarded commands defined through linear constraints with integer coefficients, potentially yielding very large or infinite state spaces.
We require state propositions to be represented as Boolean combinations of linear inequalities, and ranking functions to be expressed as piecewise-defined affine functions; we derive equivalent proof obligations in a uniform form, each having a secret component corresponding to the coefficients of the original reactive system.
The task is to convince an external verifier that each obligation holds without revealing the secret component. Our scheme computes a Farkas witness for each obligation and then, to establish the validity, employs specialised sigma protocols for zero-knowledge matrix multiplication and equality sub-protocols over two-tier Pedersen commitments
(
21
)
, and we give a new zero-knowledge range proof construction (
\mathsf{zkrp}
). Upon successful verification, we obtain a zero-knowledge proof of correctness for the reactive system under analysis.
Prover and verifier cost are independent of the state space and polynomial in the number of guarded commands and automaton transitions.

We developed a prototype implementation and evaluated our two algorithms on a range of liveness verification tasks. Our benchmarks include a 3-way handshake protocol with exponential backoff, a model of DHCP, and a round-robin scheduling algorithm (
Section

6
).
Our explicit-state algorithm can verify systems with up to approximately
2^{9}
states within a
2\text{\,}\mathrm{h}
time limit. Under the same time constraint, our symbolic algorithm handles significantly larger tasks, scaling to approximately
2^{21}
states. We emphasise that the explicit-state algorithm makes no assumptions about the structure of the system, whereas the symbolic algorithm requires the system to be expressed as linear guarded commands. These results therefore highlight a trade-off between expressivity and performance, with the explicit-state and symbolic approaches addressing different use cases.

In summary, our contribution is threefold. First, we introduce the first framework for model checking in zero knowledge, combining cryptographic principles with proof techniques for formal verification. Second, we present two schemes that realise this framework: an explicit-state algorithm for finite-state transition graphs and a symbolic algorithm for guarded command languages. Third, we implement both schemes in a prototype and demonstrate the practical efficacy of our framework on LTL model checking tasks applied to standard communication and coordination protocols.

Zero-Knowledge Model Checking

We address the problem of convincing an external verifier that a state transition system

Our framework combines three complementary technologies, corresponding to the three phases of our approach:

First, we require a proof certificate witnessing

Remark 2.1 (Soundness and Completeness).

We stress that soundness and completeness for the certifier with respect to the model checking problem are distinct from those of the zero-knowledge proof system with respect to the commitment and certification itself, and must not be conflated. Under our definitions, a malicious prover
\mathcal{P}
cannot, except with negligible probability
\epsilon

\mathcal{V}
that

(3)

\implies\mathbb{P}\left[\mathsf{out}_{\cal V}\langle\mathcal{P}(w,u),\mathcal{V}(u)\rangle=1\right]\leq\epsilon

Moreover, an honest prover can always convince the verifier when it possesses a valid certificate; a negative outcome, however, does not imply that
\mathcal{M}\not\models\phi
.

We assume a probabilistic polynomial-time adversary that may corrupt either party. The cryptographic guarantees of our framework follow the standard formulations introduced above: soundness against a malicious prover (
Remark

2.1
), binding of
c_{\mathcal{M}}
to a unique system across sessions, and zero knowledge against an honest-but-curious verifier with respect to the protocol transcript. We instantiate these guarantees, and the hardness assumptions on which they rest, in

and
5
.

Remark 2.2 (Specification- and Certification-Induced Leakage).

Our framework ensures that neither the commitment
c_{\cal M}
nor the proof
\pi
reveals any information about
\cal M
. However, selecting a secure specification
\phi
and certificate
v_{\phi}
remains the user’s responsibility. In the extreme case where the public specification

or the certificate

uniquely determine the secret system

, any party capable of verifying the proof could, in principle, reconstruct the system regardless of the protocol’s cryptographic guarantees. Developing systematic specification and certification schemes that mitigate this risk is beyond the scope of this work and constitutes an open problem.

We further emphasise that side channels arising from the prover’s computation, denial-of-service attacks, or attacks that combine our protocol with auxiliary channels (such as the timing of certificate generation, which may depend on
\mathcal{M}
) are addressed by standard countermeasures in the literature.

Summarising, we require the design of three components: a certification scheme for model checking, a commitment scheme for the system, and a zero-knowledge proof scheme that jointly captures certification and commitment. We instantiate our framework for LTL model checking in
Section

, and present two alternative commitment and zero-knowledge schemes in

Suppose there exists a function
V\colon S\times Q\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{+\infty\}
such that, for every

and

, the following three conditions hold:

\displaystyle s\in S_{0}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow q\in Q_{0}\Rightarrow V(s,q)\neq\infty

\displaystyle(s,s^{\prime})\in T

\displaystyle\Rightarrow V(s,q)\neq\infty\land(q,\langle\!\langle s\rangle\!\rangle,q^{\prime})\in\delta\Rightarrow V(s,q)\geq V(s^{\prime},q^{\prime})

\displaystyle(s,s^{\prime})\in T

\displaystyle\Rightarrow V(s,q)\neq\infty\land(q,\langle\!\langle s\rangle\!\rangle,q^{\prime})\in F\Rightarrow V(s,q)>V(s^{\prime},q^{\prime})

\displaystyle\underbrace{\phantom{\smash{(s,s^{\prime})\in T}}}_{\text{secret}}

\displaystyle\phantom{\smash{\Rightarrow}}\underbrace{\phantom{\smash{V(s,q)\neq\infty\land(q,\langle\!\langle s\rangle\!\rangle,q^{\prime})\in F\Rightarrow V(s,q)>V(s^{\prime},q^{\prime})}}}_{\text{public}}

Intuitively, the first condition ensures that all initial composed states are assigned a finite bound (base case),
the second condition enforces monotonicity of the ranking function along all transitions (inductive step), and
the third condition guarantees strict progress whenever a fair transition is taken, thereby ruling out infinite fair executions (fair step).

Remark 3.5 (Soundness and Completeness of Ranking Functions).

We emphasise that the existence of a ranking function satisfying the conditions of

Under this setting, our framework requires the implementation of a certifier that is decidable in polynomial time and admits a commitment and a zero-knowledge proof scheme. We present two such strategies: an explicit-state approach that enumerates the state space, described in

Cryptographic Properties

The KZG scheme satisfies the standard properties of completeness, polynomial binding, evaluation binding, and polynomial hiding.
Informally, completeness ensures that the verifier accepts honestly generated proofs; polynomial binding makes it computationally infeasible to open a commitment

to two distinct polynomials; evaluation binding makes it computationally infeasible to produce a valid proof of a false evaluation; and polynomial hiding ensures that

reveals no information about

Used as a zero-knowledge proof, the scheme additionally provides knowledge soundness and zero knowledge in the sense of

Remark 5.1 (Soundness and Completeness of Farkas Witnesses).

Farkas’ lemma characterises the unsatisfiability of systems of linear inequalities over the rationals, whereas our witnesses
(\lambda_{\sf s},\mu_{\sf s})
are integer and restricted to the range
[-M,M]
. This approach is sound for the obligations in
Eqs.

22
,
23
and
24
, since every integer solution is also a rational one. However, the converse fails on two accounts. First, the integer restriction of the dual system may be unsatisfiable even when its rational counterpart is satisfiable, in which case no witness can be found. Second, a rational witness may exist but require coefficients outside
[-M,M]
, making it unrepresentable in our encoding. Thus, our witnesses are sufficient but not necessary, and a prover may fail to produce one even when the obligation holds.

Each obligation of the form of
Eq.

28
comprises three classes of operations: (i) matrix–vector multiplications and additions, (ii) range membership proofs, and (iii) equality proofs. This decomposition suggests a modular approach: we design a specialized zero-knowledge sub-protocol for each class, prove each independently and then compose them into a single zero-knowledge proof.

Cryptographic Requirements

Our encoding imposes two requirements on the cryptographic setting.
First, since we operate over matrices and vectors, we need a commitment scheme that exposes their row and column structure to the sub-protocols.
Second, since we operate over integers while many cryptographic protocols operate over a prime-order field

Two-Tier Pedersen Commitments

The matrix extension works over a bilinear group
(\mathbb{G}_{1},\mathbb{G}_{2},\mathbb{G}_{T})
of prime order
p
with pairing
e\colon\mathbb{G}_{1}\times\mathbb{G}_{2}\to\mathbb{G}_{T}

(
13
;
35
)
.
Bilinearity,
e(g^{a},g^{\prime b})=e(g,g^{\prime})^{ab}
, lets us aggregate column-level commitments in
\mathbb{G}_{1}
into a single matrix-level commitment in
\mathbb{G}_{T}
that still binds to each column.
Concretely, each column
\mathbf{a}_{j}
of
A\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{m\times n}
is committed as a vector commitment
c_{j}\in\mathbb{G}_{1}
, and the columns are then aggregated into a matrix commitment
\hat{c}_{A}=\prod_{j=1}^{n}e(c_{j},g^{\prime}_{j})\in\mathbb{G}_{T}
for public generators
g^{\prime}_{j}\in\mathbb{G}_{2}
.
A sub-protocol can thus open at the column level (via
c_{j}
) or at the matrix level (via
\hat{c}_{A}
) without re-committing.

Integer Encoding

We address the second requirement with the standard bounded encoding of signed integers
(
24
;
41
)
: for a bound
M
with
M^{2}\ll p
, the range
[0,M]
represents non-negative integers and
[p-M,p-1]
represents the negatives
[-M,-1]
, so that field arithmetic preserves integer semantics on values whose magnitudes stay below
\sqrt{p}
.
For our benchmarks, a 256-bit prime with
M=2^{32}
keeps the relevant products
A_{\sf s}^{\sf T}\lambda_{\sf s}
and
b_{\sf s}^{\sf T}\lambda_{\sf s}
below
n\cdot M^{2}\approx 2^{74}\ll p
.

Non-Interactive Protocols

To avoid the round-trip cost of interaction, we apply the Fiat–Shamir transform
(
32
)
in the random oracle model: each sub-protocol derives its challenges by hashing the prover’s first messages with a hash function
H
, which the verifier can replay locally.

Matrix Multiplication and Addition

We first observe that the commitment scheme enjoys an additively homomorphic property.
As a consequence, proving and verifying a commitment to an addition can be carried out solely using the commitment algorithms.
To commit to a matrix product
AB
given commitments to its operands
c_{A}
and
c_{B}
, we build a scheme based on zero-knowledge matrix multiplication
(
21
)
. Our construction provides the routine
\mathsf{zkmm.Prove}(\mathsf{sp},A,B,c_{A},c_{B},r_{A},r_{B};r_{AB})\to(\pi_{AB},c_{AB})
, which, for matrices
A
and
B
with corresponding commitments
c_{A},c_{B}
and randomnesses
r_{A},r_{B}
, outputs a proof
\pi_{AB}
and a commitment
c_{AB}
to the product
AB
, using randomness
r_{AB}
. The corresponding verification algorithm has the interface
\mathsf{zkmm.Verify}(\mathsf{sp},c_{A},c_{B},c_{AB},\pi_{AB})
, and outputs
1
if and only if the proof is valid, 0 otherwise. The scheme implements the following relation:

\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{zkmm}}=\mathsf{PoK}\begin{Bmatrix}(A\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{m\times l},B\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{l\times n},r_{A},r_{B},r_{AB});c_{A},c_{B},c_{AB}:\\
c_{A}=\mathsf{Com}(\mathsf{sp},A;r_{A})\land c_{B}=\mathsf{Com}(\mathsf{sp},B;r_{B})\\
\land\ c_{AB}=\mathsf{Com}(\mathsf{sp},AB;r_{AB})\end{Bmatrix}

Range Proof

Our obligation requires positivity checks over the range
[0,M]
, as illustrated in
Eq.

28
, and bound checks
A_{\mathsf{s}},b_{\mathsf{s}}\in[-M,M]
on signed coefficients. We reduce the signed checks to non-negative ones via the offset
A_{\mathsf{s}}+M\in[0,2M]
and
b_{\mathsf{s}}+M\in[0,2M]
, so it suffices to prove that every entry of a committed matrix lies in
[0,M]
. We package this guarantee as a routine
zkrp
(
Figure

4
), with prover
\mathsf{zkrp.Prove}(\mathsf{sp},\allowbreak A,\allowbreak c_{A},\allowbreak r_{A},\allowbreak M)\to\pi
that generates a proof
\pi
for a matrix
A
committed in
c_{A}
with randomness
r_{A}
, and verifier
\mathsf{zkrp.Verify}(\mathsf{sp},c_{A},M,\pi)\to\{0,1\}
that accepts iff the proof is valid. The relation realised by
zkrp
is

\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{zkrp}}=\mathsf{PoK}\begin{Bmatrix}(A\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{m\times n},r_{A});c_{A},M:\\
c_{A}=\mathsf{Com}(\mathsf{sp},A;r_{A})\land\forall i,j\colon a_{ij}\in[0,M]\end{Bmatrix}

We construct

by flattening
A
and committing to each entry under an individual Pedersen commitment

, then discharging two sub-tasks: per-element range checks on the
c_{i}
, and a consistency check that ties the
\{c_{i}\}
back to the matrix commitment
\hat{c}_{A}
. The resulting protocol is complete, knowledge-sound in the algebraic group model with a random oracle, and zero-knowledge in the random oracle model; we sketch the argument in Appendix
Appendix

B
.

Per-element Range Proofs

The per-element non-negative checks are discharged by Bulletproofs
(
14
)
, a logarithmic-size
\sigma
-protocol for proving range membership of values committed under Pedersen commitments. We capture its interface through the relation

\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{Bullet}}=\mathsf{PoK}\big\{(\{v_{i},r_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k});(\{c_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k},M):\forall i,\;c_{i}=g^{v_{i}}h^{r_{i}}\land v_{i}\in[0,M]\big\},

together with a prover
\mathsf{Bullet.Prove}(\mathsf{sp},\{v_{i},r_{i},c_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k},M)\to\pi
and a verifier
\mathsf{Bullet.Verify}(\mathsf{sp},\{c_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k},M,\pi)\to\{0,1\}
that accepts iff
\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{Bullet}}
holds.

Equality Proof

Our protocol requires two equality proof operations of the form
y_{\mathsf{s}}=A_{\mathsf{p}}x_{\mathsf{s}}
, where
A_{\mathsf{p}}
is public and
x_{\mathsf{s}},y_{\mathsf{s}}
are secret. The first corresponds to the proof obligation
A_{\mathsf{s}}^{\top}\lambda_{\mathsf{s}}=-G_{\mathsf{p}}^{\top}\mu_{\mathsf{s}}^{\top}
appearing in
Eq.

28
. The second arises from the commitment
c_{\gamma}
to
-h_{\mathsf{p}}^{\top}\mu_{\mathsf{s}}^{\top}
required by our protocol construction (see
Fig.

5
). We batch these two operations using the routine

\mathsf{zkmmeq.Prove}\bigl(\mathsf{sp},\allowbreak\{(c^{(j)},r^{(j)},A^{(j)})\}_{j=1}^{k},\allowbreak(x,c_{x},r_{x})\bigr)\to\pi,

which outputs a proof that, for each
j
, the commitment
c^{(j)}
with randomness
r^{(j)}
opens to
A^{(j)}x
, where
x
itself is committed as
c_{x}
using randomness
r_{x}
. The verification algorithm

\mathsf{zkmmeq.Verify}\bigl(\mathsf{sp},\allowbreak\{(c^{(j)},A^{(j)})\}_{j=1}^{k},\allowbreak c_{x},\allowbreak\pi\bigr)

accepts if and only if all commitments are consistent. Conceptually,
\mathsf{zkmmeq}
encodes the public matrices
A^{(j)}
into the commitment bases and reduces the statement to a proof of discrete-logarithm equality
(
17
)
, a standard protocol to show that two group elements share the same exponent under different bases.
This realises the following relation:

\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{zkmmeq}}=\mathsf{PoK}\begin{Bmatrix}(x,r_{x},\{r^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{k});\{A^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{k},c_{x},\{c^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{k}:\\

We note that setting
A^{(j)}=I
yields a proof that multiple commitments (possibly under different bases) open to the same value. This property is used in the range-proof component to link element-wise and matrix commitments.

Complexity

In contrast to the explicit-state scheme, the cost of our symbolic
scheme is independent of the size of the state space. The number of
obligations of the form of

Eqs.

22
,
23
and
24

is polynomial in the number of guarded commands

, the automaton
transitions
|\delta|
, and the ranking function parameters

and

,
while the matrix dimensions of each obligation scale with the number of
state variables. Computing each Farkas witness reduces to a linear
program and is therefore polynomial-time, and generating the

. Writing

and
\hat{n}^{\prime}
for the row and column
dimensions of

.

Experimental Evaluation

We implemented an end-to-end prototype
2
2

2

https://github.com/aiverification/zkmc
of both the explicit-state and symbolic algorithms and use it to investigate three research questions.
(RQ1)
Can either scheme verify realistic protocols within practical time and memory budgets?
(RQ2)
How do the two schemes compare in scalability on these protocols (explicit-state in terms of state-space size, symbolic in terms of the number of proof obligations)?
(RQ3)
In which settings is each scheme preferable?

Our main finding is that the two approaches are complementary. The explicit-state scheme performs well on small systems with negligible setup cost but degrades quadratically in
|S|
. By contrast, the symbolic scheme scales to systems with up to
2^{20.9}
states and
2^{12.2}
proof obligations within a two-hour budget, albeit with a higher constant overhead.

Setup

Our front-end includes a toy certifier input language for guarded-command programs, Büchi automata, and piecewise-linear ranking functions, and computes batches and obligations, using Z3
(
25
)
to derive the Farkas witnesses. Our front-end functionalities are written in Python and our zero-knowledge protocols are written in Rust and receive their input in JSON format.
We ran our benchmarks on AWS EC2

The explicit-state back-end builds on the Arkworks

On top of this, we trade memory for time to exploit the 32 cores of our test

For our largest benchmark (

Related Work

Our work lies at the intersection of zero-knowledge proofs and automated reasoning, a new research direction for the cybersecurity and formal methods communities. Prior work has proposed zero-knowledge protocols for theorem proving with different logical backends, including zkUNSAT for propositional unsatisfiability
(
56
)
, zkSMT for first-order logic unsatisfiability in decidable theories
(
55
)
, and zkPi for interactive theorem proving
(
53
;
27
)
.
Although model checking can be broadly viewed as a special case of theorem proving, our setting differs fundamentally from prior work from both formal methods and security and privacy perspectives. We target the automated verification of software systems and operate at a different level of abstraction than the existing approaches, which focus on pure validity questions over logical statements. Crucially, prior work assumes a public theorem and a secret proof witness; this assumption does not directly carry over to our setting, where the theorems—in this paper, the obligations of
Theorem

3.2
—contain both public and secret components.
Even more importantly, our approach mandates commitments to the system as a core component, a requirement considered tangentially in prior work. Commitments are essential to prevent attacks in which a malicious prover replaces the system under analysis with a different one, whether during, before, or after the proof.

Privacy-preserving verification is a broader, emerging area at the intersection of computer-aided verification and applied cryptography. Prior work has explored this space through the lens of multi-party computation (MPC), with solutions for CTL model checking and runtime verification
(
46
;
42
)
. To the best of our knowledge, our work is the first to implement a privacy-preserving formal verification algorithm using zero-knowledge proofs.

Proof certificates are rooted in program logics and the axiomatic semantics of software systems
(
33
)
. The proof rule of
Theorem

3.2
builds on results on fair termination in nondeterministic Büchi automata
(
73
;
40
)
. Our scheme extend to alternative proof rules for generalised Büchi automata
(
50
)
and to other temporal specifications
(
22
;
23
)
, assuming systems and proof obligations are represented either explicitly or as linear guarded commands, as in
Sections

4
and
5
.
In principle, our approach is also amenable to proof rules for probabilistic model checking
(
28
;
5
;
4
;
43
;
51
;
3
)

and to the verification of cyber-physical systems
(
65
;
64
)
. However, extending ZKMC to reasoning over continuous domains, which is needed for probabilistic reasoning and for systems with continuous state spaces, requires a generalisation of our cryptographic routines and remains future work.

An important application domain for ZKMC is AI systems, where we identify two main directions: verification of robustness properties of neural networks, both in isolation and under feedback control
(
68
;
66
;
45
;
69
;
9
;
70
;
74
)
, and neural certificates for software
(
36
;
38
;
37
)
and for cyber-physical systems
(
16
;
1
;
2
;
58
;
54
;
75
;
76
)
. Although this setting is, in principle, a special case of our approach, in practice it is likely to yield an intractable number of proof obligations and will therefore require the development of specialised symbolic techniques for neural networks, which we leave to future work.

Our symbolic algorithm combines Farkas witnesses with zero-knowledge proofs for linear algebra in a novel way. The duality of linear programming, of which Farkas’ lemma is a special case, is a standard technique in the synthesis and verification of ranking functions
(
19
;
62
)
, while zero-knowledge proofs for linear-algebraic relations are now well studied
(
14
;
21
)
. To the best of our knowledge, our approach is the first to use linear programming duality as the structural backbone of a zero-knowledge proof.

A natural alternative is to encode a model-checking proof as an arithmetic circuit and verify its execution using a general-purpose zkSNARK or zkVM
(
39
;
34
;
67
;
53
)
. We deliberately avoid this approach for two reasons. First, the arithmetisation cost of explicit-state search scales with the size of the state space and quickly becomes prohibitive. Even for our medium-sized DHCP benchmarks (approximately 10
5 states), the resulting circuit would contain hundreds of millions of constraints. Second, and more fundamentally, a proof-theoretic perspective decouples the
certificate
of correctness from the
procedure
that produced it. The prover may use any model-checking algorithm, whether explicit-state, symbolic, abstraction-based, or even manual, provided that the resulting ranking function satisfies the public obligations of
Theorem

3.2
. Our schemes verify these obligations directly, with costs determined by the size of the certificate rather than by the effort required to construct it.

8.
Conclusion

We have presented the first approach to model checking in zero knowledge, enabling formal verification in scenarios where the confidentiality of the system is crucial.
Our implementation has demonstrated the practical viability of our new technology. Our work raises many scientific questions, from the security implications related to commitment before and after a model checking proof, as well as the information a public specification may reveal about a secret system.

Appendix B
Proof Sketch of
\mathsf{zkrp}

Completeness holds by construction.
We argue knowledge soundness in the algebraic group model (AGM) augmented with a random oracle (ROM), and zero knowledge in the random oracle model.

Soundness.

Given an accepting proof
\pi
, we construct an extractor
\mathcal{E}
that recovers a witness for the relation
\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{zkrp}}
.
The extractor first invokes the Bulletproofs extractor on
(\{c_{i}\},\pi_{\mathsf{Bullet}})
to obtain per-entry openings
(v_{i},r_{i})
satisfying
c_{i}=g^{v_{i}}h^{r_{i}}
and
0\leq v_{i}\leq M
.
The extractor then recovers the matrix-level blinding factor
r_{A}
.
Under the AGM, the prover outputs
\hat{c}_{A}
together with its algebraic representation over the public pairing bases
\{e(g,g^{\prime})^{\alpha^{k}}\}
and
e(g,h^{\prime})
, and the extractor reads
r_{A}
directly as the coefficient of
e(g,h^{\prime})
in this representation.
What remains is to show that
(\{v_{i}\},\{r_{i}\},r_{A})
is consistent with
\hat{c}_{A}
.
Substituting the algebraic representation of
\hat{c}_{A}
into the verification equation reduces it to the polynomial identity
f(\alpha)-f(z)=(\alpha-z)\cdot q(\alpha)
at the challenge
z=H(\hat{c}_{A}\|c_{1}\|\cdots\|c_{l})
, where
f
is the matrix polynomial defined by
\{v_{i}\}
.
Since the random oracle distributes
z
uniformly, the Schwartz–Zippel lemma implies coefficient-wise equality of the two polynomials with overwhelming probability.
Therefore
\hat{c}_{A}
commits exactly to the matrix defined by
\{v_{i}\}
with blinding
r_{A}
, and the extractor outputs the witness
(\{v_{i}\},\{r_{i}\},r_{A})
.

Zero Knowledge.

We construct a simulator
\mathcal{S}
that, given only
\hat{c}_{A}
, produces transcripts indistinguishable from those of an honest prover.
We grant
\mathcal{S}
the setup trapdoors
\alpha
and
\beta
, which is a standard simulation assumption.
On input
\hat{c}_{A}
, the simulator proceeds as follows:

•

Sample
c_{i}\in\mathbb{G}_{1}
uniformly for
i=1,\dots,l
, and invoke the Bulletproofs simulator to obtain
\pi_{\mathsf{Bullet}}
.

•

Compute the challenge
z=H(\hat{c}_{A}\|c_{1}\|\cdots\|c_{l})
and the aggregate
c=\prod_{i=1}^{l}c_{i}^{z^{n^{\prime}+qm^{\prime}}}
.

•

Sample
\pi_{\mathsf{eq}}\in\mathbb{G}_{1}
uniformly, and define
\theta=\bigl(\hat{c}_{A}\cdot e(c,g^{\prime})^{-1}\bigm/e(\pi_{\mathsf{eq}},(g^{\prime})^{\alpha-z})\bigr)^{1/\beta}
, which makes the verification equation hold by construction.

•

Output
\pi=(\{c_{i}\},\pi_{\mathsf{eq}},\theta,\pi_{\mathsf{Bullet}})
.

Each component of the simulated transcript is indistinguishable from its real counterpart.
The Pedersen commitments
c_{i}
are perfectly hiding, so the simulated
c_{i}
have the same distribution as the honest ones.
The Bulletproofs simulator produces range proofs computationally indistinguishable from real ones.
Finally,
(\pi_{\mathsf{eq}},\theta)
is uniform among the pairs that satisfy the verification equation, matching the honest prover, who randomizes
\pi_{\mathsf{eq}}
via
\mu
and absorbs the resulting offset into
\theta
.
Hence no probabilistic polynomial-time distinguisher separates the simulated transcript from a real one, and
\mathsf{zkrp}
is zero knowledge in the random oracle model.
