Variance-Tilted Diffusion Models for Diverse Sampling
Iskander Azangulov, Leo Zhang, Kianoosh Ashouritaklimi

Abstract
Diffusion models are typically sampled independently, even when the downstream objective is to obtain a diverse set of candidates. We introduce a variance-weighted batch distribution that favors collections of samples with large empirical spread after a prescribed linear feature map. The target is specified explicitly, and the sampler is derived as the corresponding Doob h h -transform of independent diffusion dynamics. The resulting correction has a compact form: an interaction term that repels posterior denoised means, together with a curvature term that moves particles to the region of higher feature variance. This yields an interacting-particle sampler with a transparent probabilistic target rather than a heuristic repulsive drift.



1
Introduction

Diffusion models
(
Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015
;
Ho et al., 2020
;
Song et al., 2020
)
have established themselves as the gold standard for generative modeling, achieving unprecedented fidelity in domains ranging from high-resolution image synthesis
(
Dhariwal and Nichol, 2021
;
Rombach et al., 2022a
)
to scientific discovery, such as protein folding
(
Watson et al., 2023
;
Abramson et al., 2024
)
and molecular docking
(
Prat et al., 2025
)
. However, the standard sampling paradigm—drawing independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) samples—is often suboptimal for downstream tasks. In many settings, the goal is not merely to generate high-probability modes, but to uncover a set of candidates that covers distinct regions of the data manifold. For instance, in de novo drug design, generating structurally diverse ligands is crucial to mitigate the risk of late-stage failure due to shared toxicity profiles. Similarly, in robotic motion planning, a diverse set of trajectory proposals provides robustness against unforeseen environmental obstacles.

To enforce such diversity, approaches such as particle guidance
(
Corso et al., 2023
)
introduce repulsive potentials into the sampling dynamics. While effective at increasing diversity, these methods modify the drift heuristically, resulting in an implicit target distribution that is computationally intractable to evaluate or control precisely.

Contributions.

In contrast to prior work, we define the batch target distribution first and subsequently derive the exact sampler that realizes it. For a batch of
n
samples
\bm{x}=(x^{(1)},\ldots,x^{(n)})
, we study the variance-weighted distribution

\pi_{0}^{A}(\bm{x})\propto\Var_{n}^{A}(\bm{x})\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{0}(x^{(i)}),

(1)

where
A:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
is a linear operator that maps samples to a task-specific feature space, and the empirical variance in this space is defined as

\Var_{n}^{A}(\bm{x}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lVert A(x^{(i)}-\bar{x})\right\rVert^{2},\text{ with }\bar{x}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x^{(i)}.

(2)

The linear operator
A
dictates the representation used to encourage diversity; for example, it can select specific coordinates, isolate low-frequency Fourier coefficients, mask spatial regions, or extract other relevant linear features.

Our primary contributions are as follows:

•

Target-first formulation.
We frame diverse generation as an exact sampling problem from the explicit batch target (
1
), rather than relying on an uncontrolled repulsive force.

•

Closed-form Doob correction.
We compute the Doob
h
-transform induced by the variance tilt and provide the corresponding score correction for a general linear operator
A
.

•

Tractable sampling algorithm.
We introduce a discretized interacting-particle sampler. The leading interaction term is computed efficiently using vector-Jacobian products, while the curvature term can be estimated via finite differences or alternative trace/Laplacian estimators.

Remark 1.1
.

We present our theoretical results under the Variance Exploding (VE) SDE parameterization, as it yields considerably simpler formulas.
However, the obtained results readily generalize to other parametrizations (i.e. general linear diffusion models) such as VP-SDEs.

Notation.

We use boldface symbols to denote batches of particles. For instance,
\bm{x}=(x^{(1)},\ldots,x^{(n)})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}
represents a batch of states, and
\bm{X}_{t}=(X_{t}^{(1)},\ldots,X_{t}^{(n)})
denotes the batch trajectory at time
t
. For any function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
, applying it to a batch implies element-wise application:
f(\bm{x}):=\big(f(x^{(1)}),\ldots,f(x^{(n)})\big)
. This convention naturally extends to linear operators, where
A\bm{x}:=(Ax^{(1)},\ldots,Ax^{(n)})
, and to gradients, where
\nabla_{\bm{x}}F(\bm{x}):=\big(\nabla_{x^{(1)}}F(\bm{x}),\ldots,\nabla_{x^{(n)}}F(\bm{x})\big)
.

Background

2.1
Diffusion Models

Let
p_{0}
be the target data distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We consider a forward process over the interval
[0,T]
starting at
p_{0}
which gradually noises samples towards pure noise:

dX_{t}=\sqrt{2}\,dB_{t},\quad X_{0}\sim p_{0},

(3)

where
B_{t}
is standard Brownian motion.
The marginal distribution at time
t
can be represented as
X_{t}=X_{0}+\sqrt{2t}\varepsilon
, where
\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Id_{d})
. We denote the density of
X_{t}
as
p_{t}
.

Under mild conditions
(
Anderson, 1982
)
, the reverse process
Y_{t}:=X_{T-t}
satisfies the following SDE:

dY_{t}=2s_{T-t}(Y_{t})\,dt+\sqrt{2}\,d\bar{B}_{t},\qquad Y_{0}\sim p_{T},

(4)

where
s_{t}=\nabla\log p_{t}
is the score function and
\bar{B}_{t}
is another standard Brownian motion.

By simulating the backward process (
4
), one can draw a sample
Y_{T}\sim p_{0}
from the target distribution. In practice, the score
s_{t}
is replaced by a neural network approximation, the initial distribution
p_{T}
is approximated by
\mathcal{N}(0,2T\cdot\Id_{d})
, and the backward dynamics (
4
) are discretized. We refer readers to
Karras et al., 2022
for further details.

2.2
Terminal Reweighting and Doob Transforms

Suppose that a target distribution
q_{0}
is obtained by tilting
p_{0}
by the function
f
:

q_{0}(x)\propto f(x)p_{0}(x),\qquad f(x)\geq 0.

(5)

Let
q_{t}
denote the marginal distribution at time
t
obtained by running the same forward process (
3
) starting from
q_{0}
. Then,

\!\!\!\!q_{t}(x)\!\propto\!h_{t}(x)p_{t}(x)\text{ with }h_{t}(x)\!:=\!\mathbb{E}[f(X_{0})\!\mid\!X_{t}=x].

(6)

Consequently, we can sample from
q_{0}
by simulating the reverse process (
4
) under the modified score
\nabla\log q_{t}
where

\nabla\log q_{t}(x)=s_{t}(x)+\nabla\log h_{t}(x).

(7)

We refer to
h_{t}
as the Doob
h
-transform for the tilt
f
.
We see that this provides the exact score correction to
s_{t}
induced by the terminal weight
f
. We refer to
Denker et al., 2025
for further details.

Crucially for our method, this formulation naturally extends to joint distributions over batches. Starting from the independent batch distribution
p_{0}^{\otimes n}
and tilting by a non-negative function
f(\bm{x})
yields the modified batch score:

\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log q_{t}(\bm{x})=s_{t}(\bm{x})+\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log h_{t}(\bm{x}),

(8)

where the batch Doob
h
-transform is defined as:

h_{t}(\bm{x}):=\mathbb{E}[f(\bm{X}_{0})\mid\bm{X}_{t}=\bm{x}].

(9)

The remainder of this paper is dedicated to identifying this exact quantity for the setting of the variance-weighted distribution (
1
).

3
Main Result

In this paper, we propose sampling from the following reweighting of the target distribution to encourage batch diversity:

\pi_{0}^{A}(\bm{x})\propto\Var_{n}^{A}(\bm{x})\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{0}(x^{(i)}),

(10)

where we assume access to an approximation of the score
s_{t}
for
p_{0}
,
A:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
is a linear operator projecting the images to the feature space in which we want to encourage diversity and
\Var_{n}^{A}(\bm{x})
is the empirical variance of the features, namely

\Var_{n}^{A}(\bm{x}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lVert A(x^{(i)}-\bar{x})\right\rVert^{2},\text{ where }\bar{x}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x^{(i)}.

(11)

This is not a product distribution. It favors batches whose elements remain likely under the original model while being collectively spread out after applying
A
. Note that we enforce diversity directly at the data space
x_{0}
, compared to
Corso et al., 2023
which forces diversity on the noised latents
x_{t}
.

As discussed in Section
2.2
, in order to sample from this distribution, we need to estimate the Doob
h
-transform associated with (
10
)

h_{t}^{A}(\bm{x}_{t}):=\mathbb{E}\left[\Var_{n}^{A}(\bm{X}_{0})\mid\bm{X}_{t}=\bm{x}_{t}\right].

(12)

Once
h_{t}^{A}
is available, the exact target score is

s_{t}^{\pi^{A}}(\bm{x}_{t})=s_{t}(\bm{x}_{t})+\nabla_{\bm{x}_{t}}\log h_{t}^{A}(\bm{x}_{t}).

(13)

The target sampler is obtained from the usual reverse diffusion by replacing the original score with (
13
).

In the next section, we show how to compute the correction analytically.

3.1
Closed-Form Doob Correction

We start by recalling Tweedie’s identities, connecting the score and its derivatives with the moments of the posterior distribution:

\mu_{t}(x):=\mathbb{E}[X_{0}\mid X_{t}=x]=x+2t\,s_{t}(x),

(14)

and

\Sigma_{t}(x):=\Cov(X_{0}\mid X_{t}=x)=2t\Id_{d}+4t^{2}\nabla s_{t}(x),

(15)

where
\nabla s_{t}
denotes the Jacobian of the score function.
We provide their derivations in Appendix
B
.

Since
h_{t}^{A}
is the expectation of the batch variance, by applying a bias-variance decomposition (see Appendix
C
for details), we can express
h_{t}^{A}
in terms of the posterior mean
\mu_{t}
and the posterior covariance
\Sigma_{t}
in the following way

\displaystyle h_{t}^{A}(\bm{x}_{t})=\@Enonstarred\left[\Var_{n}^{A}(\bm{X}_{0})\mid\bm{X}_{t}=\bm{x}_{t}\right]

(16)

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!=\!\Var_{n}\left(\@Enonstarred\left[A\bm{X}_{0}\!\mid\!\bm{X}_{t}=\bm{x}_{t}\right]\right)\!+\!\frac{n-1}{n^{2}}\!\sum_{i=1}^{n}\!\Var[AX_{0}|X_{t}\!=\!x^{(i)}_{t}]

This decomposition represents
h_{t}^{A}
as a sum of two tractable terms.
The former rewards the repelling of the posterior means of the particles, while the latter rewards the particles for being in the region where the posterior distribution of features is diverse.

Substituting
{\Var[AX_{0}|X_{t}\!=\!x^{(i)}_{t}]=\Tr\left(\!A\Sigma_{t}(x_{t}^{(i)})A^{\top}\!\right)}
and Tweedie’s identities (
14
) and (
15
) into (
16
) we rewrite
h_{t}^{A}
in terms of the score function and its derivatives

\displaystyle h_{t}^{A}(\bm{x}_{t})=\Var_{n}^{A}(\bm{x}_{t}+2t\,s_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}))

(17)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{n-1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[2t\Tr(A^{\top}A)+4t^{2}\Tr\left(A\nabla s_{t}(x_{t}^{(i)})A^{\top}\right)\right].

Finally, the guidance term of interest in (
13
) has the form

\nabla_{\bm{x}_{t}}\log h_{t}^{A}(\bm{x}_{t})=\frac{\nabla h_{t}^{A}(\bm{x}_{t})}{h_{t}^{A}(\bm{x}_{t})}=\frac{\big(g_{t}^{A,1}(\bm{x}_{t}),\ldots,g_{t}^{A,n}(\bm{x}_{t})\big)}{h_{t}^{A}(\bm{x}_{t})},

(18)

where

\displaystyle g_{t}^{A,i}(\bm{x}_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n}J_{\mu,t}(x_{t}^{(i)})^{\top}A^{\top}A\left(\mu_{t}(x_{t}^{(i)})-\bar{\mu}_{t}(\bm{x}_{t})\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{n-1}{n^{2}}4t^{2}\Div\left(A^{\top}A\nabla s_{t}(x_{t}^{(i)})\right)

(19)

and

J_{\mu,t}(x):=I_{d}+2t\nabla s_{t}(x),\quad\bar{\mu}_{t}(\bm{x}_{t})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu(x^{(i)}_{t}).

We note that the divergence in (
18
) is interpreted column-wise.

Remark 3.1

(Projection specialization.)
.

When
P=A^{\top}A
is the orthogonal projection onto a subspace

U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}
denote the orthonormal
basis of
U
from the rows of
A
. Then the divergence term in (
19
) is the Laplacian of
s_{t}
projected onto
U
, i.e.

\Div\left(A^{T}A\nabla s_{t}(x)\right)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\partial_{a_{\ell}}^{2}s_{t}(x)=:\Delta_{U}s_{t}(x),

where
\partial_{a_{\ell}}
denotes the derivative in the
direction
a_{\ell}
.
For further details, see Appendix
E
.

3.2
Practical Implementation

We sample from (
10
) by discretizing the reverse-time SDE and using the target score (
13
). At a reverse time corresponding to noise level
t
, each particle receives the usual score
s_{t}(x_{t}^{(i)})
plus the Doob correction (
18
). The leading term in
g_{t}^{A,i}
can be computed efficiently with a vector-Jacobian product through the denoiser
\mu_{t}
. The divergence term requires computation of the second-order derivatives of
s_{t}
; in our implementation, we estimate it by finite differences. Other estimators, including Hutchinson-type stochastic trace methods or automatic-differentiation-based higher-order derivatives, can be substituted.

5
Discussion and Conclusion

We proposed a target-first approach to diverse diffusion sampling. By reweighting independent samples according to empirical variance after a linear feature map, we obtain an explicit batch distribution and derive its exact Doob correction under Gaussian noising. The resulting sampler has a clear decomposition into denoised-mean repulsion and a curvature correction. This provides a probabilistic alternative to heuristic particle repulsion, while retaining practical implementation paths based on vector-Jacobian products and numerical estimators for the curvature term.

Limitations.

While our method qualitatively produces more diverse outputs, it can negatively affect image quality somewhat. Future work could explore how to anneal the Doob correction term to mitigate these effects, as well as quantitatively measuring the performance of our method. Additionally, when including the full divergence term, our method is more expensive than standard sampling from diffusion models due to the cost of estimating the divergence.
Future work could explore approaches for reducing this cost while retaining diverse sampling.

Appendix A
Doob
h
-Transform for Terminal Reweighting

We first record the identity underlying the construction. Let
P
denote the law of the forward process initialized from
p_{0}
, and let
Q
be the path measure obtained by reweighting the initial point by a nonnegative function
f
:

\frac{dQ}{dP}(X_{0:T})=\frac{f(X_{0})}{\mathbb{E}_{P}[f(X_{0})]}.

(20)

Let
p_{t}
and
q_{t}
denote the corresponding time-
t
marginals. For any test function
\varphi
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\varphi(X_{t})]

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{P}[\varphi(X_{t})f(X_{0})]}{\mathbb{E}_{P}[f(X_{0})]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{P}[\varphi(X_{t})\mathbb{E}_{P}[f(X_{0})\mid X_{t}]]}{\mathbb{E}_{P}[f(X_{0})]}.

(21)

Hence

q_{t}(x)=\frac{h_{t}(x)p_{t}(x)}{\mathbb{E}_{P}[f(X_{0})]},\qquad h_{t}(x):=\mathbb{E}_{P}[f(X_{0})\mid X_{t}=x].

(22)

Assuming differentiability and positivity of
p_{t}
and
h_{t}
, this gives

\nabla\log q_{t}(x)=\nabla\log p_{t}(x)+\nabla\log h_{t}(x).

(23)

The same argument applies to the batch process
\bm{X}_{t}=(X_{t}^{(1)},\ldots,X_{t}^{(n)})
initialized from
p_{0}^{\otimes n}
with
f
replaced by any nonnegative batch functional
f(\bm{x})
.

Appendix B
Conditional Moments for Linear-Gaussian Corruption

Consider a linear-Gaussian noising model

X_{t}=\alpha_{t}X_{0}+\sigma_{t}\varepsilon,\qquad\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}),\qquad\alpha_{t}>0,\quad\sigma_{t}>0.

(24)

Let
p_{t}
be the density of
X_{t}
and
s_{t}(x)=\nabla_{x}\log p_{t}(x)
. The conditional density of
X_{t}
given
X_{0}=x_{0}
is Gaussian with covariance
\sigma_{t}^{2}I_{d}
. Differentiating the marginal density gives

\nabla_{x}p_{t}(x)=\int-\frac{x-\alpha_{t}x_{0}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}p_{t|0}(x\mid x_{0})p_{0}(x_{0})\,dx_{0}.

(25)

Dividing by
p_{t}(x)
yields

s_{t}(x)=-\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\left(x-\alpha_{t}\mathbb{E}[X_{0}\mid X_{t}=x]\right).

(26)

Therefore the posterior mean is

\mu_{t}(x):=\mathbb{E}[X_{0}\mid X_{t}=x]=\frac{1}{\alpha_{t}}\left(x+\sigma_{t}^{2}s_{t}(x)\right).

(27)

Differentiating (
27
) gives

J_{\mu,t}(x):=\nabla_{x}\mu_{t}(x)=\frac{1}{\alpha_{t}}\left(\Id_{d}+\sigma_{t}^{2}\nabla s_{t}(x)\right).

(28)

The posterior covariance follows from the second-derivative identity

\nabla_{x}\mu_{t}(x)=\frac{\alpha_{t}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\Cov(X_{0}\mid X_{t}=x),

(29)

which is obtained by differentiating the posterior expectation under the Gaussian likelihood. Combining this identity with (
28
) gives

\Sigma_{t}(x):=\Cov(X_{0}\mid X_{t}=x)=\frac{\sigma_{t}^{2}}{\alpha_{t}^{2}}I_{d}+\frac{\sigma_{t}^{4}}{\alpha_{t}^{2}}\nabla s_{t}(x).

(30)

Appendix C
Bias-Variance Decomposition for the Batch Variance

We now justify the decomposition used to compute the Doob term. Condition on
\bm{X}_{t}=\bm{x}_{t}
. Define

Z_{i}:=AX_{0}^{(i)},\qquad\bar{Z}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}Z_{j},

(31)

and

\mu_{i}^{A}:=\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}\mid\bm{X}_{t}=\bm{x}_{t}]=A\mu_{t}(x_{t}^{(i)}),\qquad\Sigma_{i}^{A}:=\Cov(Z_{i}\mid\bm{X}_{t}=\bm{x}_{t})=A\Sigma_{t}(x_{t}^{(i)})A^{\top}.

(32)

Since the forward noising is independent across particles,
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}
are conditionally independent given
\bm{X}_{t}=\bm{x}_{t}
. The empirical variance can be written as

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lVert Z_{i}-\bar{Z}\right\rVert^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lVert Z_{i}\right\rVert^{2}-\left\lVert\bar{Z}\right\rVert^{2}.

(33)

For the first term,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert Z_{i}\right\rVert^{2}\mid\bm{X}_{t}=\bm{x}_{t}\right]=\left\lVert\mu_{i}^{A}\right\rVert^{2}+\Tr(\Sigma_{i}^{A}).

(34)

For the second term, conditional independence gives

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\bar{Z}\right\rVert^{2}\mid\bm{X}_{t}=\bm{x}_{t}\right]=\left\lVert\bar{\mu}^{A}\right\rVert^{2}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Tr(\Sigma_{i}^{A}),

(35)

where

\bar{\mu}^{A}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}^{A}.

(36)

Substituting (
34
) and (
35
) into (
33
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lVert Z_{i}-\bar{Z}\right\rVert^{2}\middle|\bm{X}_{t}=\bm{x}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lVert\mu_{i}^{A}\right\rVert^{2}-\left\lVert\bar{\mu}^{A}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\frac{1}{n}-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Tr(\Sigma_{i}^{A}).

(37)

The first line is the empirical variance of the conditional means,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lVert\mu_{i}^{A}\right\rVert^{2}-\left\lVert\bar{\mu}^{A}\right\rVert^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lVert\mu_{i}^{A}-\bar{\mu}^{A}\right\rVert^{2}.

(38)

Moreover,

\frac{1}{n}-\frac{1}{n^{2}}=\frac{n-1}{n^{2}}.

(39)

Therefore

\mathbb{E}\left[\Var_{n}^{A}(\bm{X}_{0})\mid\bm{X}_{t}=\bm{x}_{t}\right]=\Var_{n}^{A}(\mu_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}))+\frac{n-1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Tr\left(A\Sigma_{t}(x_{t}^{(i)})A^{\top}\right).

(40)

Using cyclicity of the trace and
B:=A^{\top}A
, this is equivalently

\mathbb{E}\left[\Var_{n}^{A}(\bm{X}_{0})\mid\bm{X}_{t}=\bm{x}_{t}\right]=\Var_{n}^{A}(\mu_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}))+\frac{n-1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Tr\left(B\Sigma_{t}(x_{t}^{(i)})\right).

(41)

Appendix D
Gradient of the Doob
h
-Transform

We compute the gradient of
h_{t}^{A}
for a generic linear diffusion
X_{t}=\alpha_{t}X_{0}+\sigma_{t}\epsilon
.

Let
B=A^{\top}A
, and write the Doob
h
-transform as

h_{t}^{A}(\bm{x}_{t})=\Var_{n}^{A}(\mu_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}))+\frac{n-1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Tr\left(B\Sigma_{t}(x_{t}^{(i)})\right).

(42)

To compute the gradient of the first term, we first define

\bar{\mu}_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{t}(x_{t}^{(j)}).

(43)

We now compute the gradient by

\displaystyle\nabla_{x_{t}^{(i)}}\Var_{n}^{A}(\mu_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}))

\displaystyle=\nabla_{x_{t}^{(i)}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left\lVert A\mu_{t}(x_{t}^{(j)})-A\bar{\mu}_{t}(\bm{x}_{t})\right\rVert^{2}

(44)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{x_{t}^{(i)}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left\lVert A\mu_{t}(x_{t}^{(j)})\right\rVert^{2}-\nabla_{x_{t}^{(i)}}\left\lVert A\bar{\mu}_{t}(\bm{x}_{t})\right\rVert^{2}

(45)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n}\left(AJ_{\mu,t}(x_{t}^{(i)})\right)^{\top}A\mu_{t}(x_{t}^{(i)})-2\left(AJ_{\bar{\mu},t}(x_{t}^{(i)})\right)^{\top}A\bar{\mu}_{t}(x_{t}).

(46)

where
J_{\mu,t},J_{\bar{\mu},t}
denote the Jacobian of
\mu_{t}
and
\bar{\mu}_{t}
with respect to
x_{t}^{(i)}
respectively.
From (
29
), we have

\displaystyle J_{\mu,t}(x_{t}^{(i)})=\frac{1}{\alpha_{t}}\left(\Id_{d}+\sigma_{t}^{2}\nabla s_{t}(x_{t}^{(i)})\right),

(47)

and by the definition of
\bar{\mu}_{t}
, we have

\displaystyle J_{\bar{\mu},t}(x_{t}^{(i)})=\frac{1}{n}J_{\mu,t}(x_{t}^{(i)}).

(48)

Putting this together, we obtain for the gradient of the first term:

\displaystyle\nabla_{x_{t}^{(i)}}\Var_{n}^{A}(\mu_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}))

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n}J_{\mu,t}(x_{t}^{(i)})^{\top}B\left(\mu_{t}(x_{t}^{(i)})-\bar{\mu}_{t}(x_{t}^{(i)})\right).

(49)

Regarding the second term, we have

\nabla_{x_{t}^{(i)}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Tr\left(B\Sigma_{t}(x_{t}^{(j)})\right)=\nabla_{x_{t}^{(i)}}\Tr\left(B\Sigma_{t}(x_{t}^{(i)})\right).

(50)

Using (
30
), we have

\Tr(B\Sigma_{t}(x_{t}^{(i)}))=\frac{\sigma_{t}^{2}}{\alpha_{t}^{2}}\Tr(B)+\frac{\sigma_{t}^{4}}{\alpha_{t}^{2}}\Tr(B\nabla s_{t}(x_{t}^{(i)})).

(51)

The first term is independent of
x_{t}^{(i)}
, so

\nabla_{x_{t}^{(i)}}\Tr(B\Sigma_{t}(x_{t}^{(i)}))=\frac{\sigma_{t}^{4}}{\alpha_{t}^{2}}\nabla_{x_{t}^{(i)}}\Tr(B\nabla s_{t}(x_{t}^{(i)})).

(52)

Moreover, as the above expression is a linear combination of the third derivatives of
\log p_{t}
and they commute, we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{x_{t}^{(i)}}\Tr(B\Sigma_{t}(x_{t}^{(i)}))=\frac{\sigma_{t}^{4}}{\alpha_{t}^{2}}\Div(B\nabla s_{t}(x_{t}^{(i)})),

(53)

where the divergence is applied column-wise.
To see this, we look at the
k
-th component of
\nabla_{x}\Tr(B\nabla s_{t}(x))
:

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{k}}\Tr(B\nabla s_{t}(x))

\displaystyle=\partial_{x_{k}}\sum_{j}(B\nabla s_{t}(x))_{jj}

(54)

\displaystyle=\partial_{x_{k}}\sum_{j,l}B_{jl}[\nabla s_{t}(x)]_{lj}

(55)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,l}B_{jl}\partial_{x_{k}}\partial_{x_{l}}\partial_{x_{j}}\log p_{t}(x),

(56)

and the
k
-th component of
\Div(B\nabla s_{t}(x))
:

\displaystyle\Div(B\nabla s_{t}(x))_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l}\partial_{x_{l}}(B\nabla s_{t}(x))_{lk}

(57)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l}\partial_{x_{l}}\sum_{j}B_{lj}[\nabla s_{t}(x)]_{jk}

(58)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l,j}B_{lj}\partial_{x_{l}}\partial_{x_{j}}\partial_{x_{k}}\log p_{t}(x).

(59)

As the derivatives of
\log p_{t}
commute, we can conclude.

Appendix E
Projection Specialization

We consider the
i
-th component of the LHS of the identity:

\displaystyle\Div(A^{\top}A\nabla s_{t}(x))_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell}\partial_{x_{\ell}}(A^{\top}A\nabla s_{t}(x))_{\ell i}

(60)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell}\partial_{x_{\ell}}\sum_{j}(A^{\top}A)_{\ell j}[\nabla s_{t}(x)]_{ji}

(61)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell,j}(A^{\top}A)_{\ell j}\partial_{x_{\ell}}\partial_{x_{i}}(s_{t})_{j}(x)

(62)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell,j}(A^{\top}A)_{\ell j}\partial_{x_{\ell}}\partial_{x_{j}}(s_{t})_{i}(x)

(63)

where we recall the divergence is considered column-wise and the final equality holds as the derivatives of
\log p_{t}
commute.
Furthermore, the
i
-th component of the RHS of the identity satifies:

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell}\partial^{2}_{a_{\ell}}(s_{t})_{i}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell}\partial_{a_{\ell}}\sum_{j}(a_{\ell})_{j}\partial_{x_{j}}(s_{t})_{i}(x)

(65)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell,j}(a_{\ell})_{j}\partial_{a_{\ell}}[\partial_{x_{j}}(s_{t})_{i}(x)]

(66)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell,j}(a_{\ell})_{j}\sum_{k}(a_{\ell})_{k}\partial_{x_{k}}\partial_{x_{j}}(s_{t})_{i}(x)

(67)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k}\left[\sum_{\ell}(a_{\ell})_{j}(a_{\ell})_{k}\right]\partial_{x_{k}}\partial_{x_{j}}(s_{t})_{i}(x)

(68)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k}(A^{\top}A)_{jk}\partial_{x_{k}}\partial_{x_{j}}(s_{t})_{i}(x),

(69)

where the last equality holds as
a_{\ell}
is the
\ell
-th row of
A
.
Finally, we see that the two sides are equal from the fact
A^{\top}A
is symmetric and the derivatives of
\log p_{t}
commute.

Appendix F
Coordinate Masks

When
A
is a coordinate mask,
B=A^{\top}A
is diagonal. Writing
B=\diag(b)
,

\Tr(B\nabla s_{t}(x))=\sum_{a=1}^{d}b_{a}\partial_{x_{a}}s_{t,a}(x)=\sum_{a=1}^{d}b_{a}\partial_{x_{a}}^{2}\log p_{t}(x).

(70)

Thus the trace term is the masked scalar Laplacian of
\log p_{t}
. Its gradient is the corresponding coordinatewise vector Laplacian of the score,

\nabla_{x}\Tr(B\nabla s_{t}(x))=\sum_{a=1}^{d}b_{a}\partial_{x_{a}}^{2}s_{t}(x).

(71)

For binary masks, masking by
A
and masking by
B=A^{\top}A
coincide. For soft masks, one should specify whether the intended weights are those in
A
or those in
A^{\top}A
.

Appendix G
Further Details on Experiments

This section describes our empirical implementation.

Base CFG score.

We use the classifier-free guidance score term
s_{\mathrm{cfg}}
defined by

s_{\mathrm{cfg}}(x_{t},t)=s_{\mathrm{cond}}(x_{t},t)+w\big(s_{\mathrm{cond}}(x_{t},t)-s_{\mathrm{uncond}}(x_{t},t)\big),

where
w
is some scaling factor and
s_{\mathrm{cond}}
denotes the score conditioned on some prompt and
s_{\mathrm{uncond}}
denotes the unconditional score.
For all of our experiments, we set this to 1.

Doob
h
-transform.

We compute the posterior mean considered under the CFG score
s_{\mathrm{cfg}}
where we use Tweedie’s formula.
Similar to CFG, we also scale the Doob correction
\nabla\log h_{t}^{A}
with a controllable parameter
\lambda
; for all of our experiments we set this to 0.5.
For the first term in
g_{t}^{A,i}
involving
J_{\mu,t}^{\top}
, we compute this with a vector-Jacobian product.
We include the second term
\frac{\sigma_{t}^{4}}{\alpha_{t}^{2}}\Div(B\nabla s_{t}(x_{t}^{(i)}))
if we are including the divergence term in the correction.
Additionally, even if the divergence term is included, we only include it if
\sigma_{t}^{4}/\alpha_{t}^{2}\leq c_{\text{cutoff}}
where we set
c_{\text{cutoff}}
to be 16 in all of our experiments to prevent instabilities.
Furthermore, we evaluate
\frac{\sigma_{t}^{4}}{\alpha_{t}^{2}}\Div(B\nabla s_{t}(x_{t}^{(i)}))
with a Hutchinson estimator with
L=8
orthogonalized Gaussian
probes. Concretely, the implementation draws a Gaussian matrix in the
flattened batch-feature space, applies a QR factorization, rescales the
orthonormal columns by the square root of the feature dimension, reshapes each
probe to the batch feature shape, and lifts it back to latent space through

A^{\top}
. Thus each latent probe
u_{\ell}
satisfies

\mathbb{E}[u_{\ell}u_{\ell}^{\top}]=B
, while different probes are orthogonalized
within the same estimator call.
Additionally, we estimate the divergence term in
g_{t}^{A,i}
with finite differences.
