Nature of Work
Describe the research (summary of Scope of Work and principal objectives of the CRADA):

The challenges posed by high building energy bill necessitate proactive implementation of
energy-efficient strategies to minimize the uses of both electricity and heating gas and promote
the use of free solar energy sources. "Smart" window films/glass, leveraging thermochromic
vanadium dioxide (VO2), offer an adaptive approach to harness solar energy, significantly
reducing the thermal load of buildings. This is achieved by reflecting the infrared portion of the
solar spectrum through a phase transition in the monoclinic M-phase (M) of VO2 induced by
heating. In this study, a scalable continuous flow synthesis process invented by Argonne
was employed to explore a wide parameter space, targeting the controlled synthesis of
monoclinic VO2 (M) nanoparticles with well-controlled sizes and morphologies.
Additionally, a doping strategy and surface modifications were utilized for high-throughput tuning
of the metal-to-insulator transition temperature. Strategies to enhance the solar modulation
properties of VO2 nanoparticles in polymer films were investigated through (i) morphological
transformation from spherical to nanorod structures, (ii) surface modification with low refractive
index ligands, and (iii) incorporation of additional thermochromic materials for improved solar
energy modulation and aesthetically appealing colors in smart films. Furthermore, the study
outlines scaling-up synthesis methods for VO2 nanoparticles in a continuous flow reactor using
hydrazine monohydrate. This comprehensive investigation provides valuable insights into the
upscaling synthesis and design of advanced VO2/polymer composite smart window films with
enhanced functionality, solar energy modulation and visible light transmittance, driving the
technology a step close for industrial application.

Conclusions drawn from this CRADA; include any major accomplishments:

Strategic adjustments to the thermochromic and optical properties of VO2 nanoparticles resulted
in significant achievements. The as-synthesized VO2/polymer composite film exhibited over 10%
solar energy modulation, while PFDA- VO2/NLET hybrid smart films achieved a promising up to
19.9% solar energy modulation. Adopting VO2 nanoparticles with a predominant nanorod
morphology, as opposed to nanospheres, led to more than a twofold increase in thermochromic
performance (ΔT at 2000 nm) and a 68% enhancement in solar energy modulation when
combined with ligands possessing low refractive index. The key ultrasmall M-phase VO2
nanoparticles can be continuously synthesized by using our patented hydrothermal continuous
flow reactor which is scalable and promising for manufacturing high-quality of VO2 material for
large area of smart film or glass for energy-efficiency applications.

Summary of technology transfer benefits to industry and, if applicable, path forward/anticipated next
steps towards commercialization:

Technology-transfer value to industry: The patented continuous-flow reactor platform for synthesizing
ultrasmall, M-phase VO₂ nanoparticles directly addresses the primary barriers that have prevented
thermochromic VO₂ from industrial adoption: scalability, cost, and phase consistency. Unlike batch
hydrothermal or vapor-phase routes, the continuous process enables tightly controlled nucleation and
growth at high throughput, yielding uniform sub-50 nm M-phase VO₂ with reproducible thermochromic
performance and minimal post-processing. This manufacturing-ready architecture is inherently
compatible with industrial roll-to-roll polymer film production and glass-integrating and/or coating supply
chains, reducing capital and operating costs while improving yield and quality control.
For industry partners in smart glazing, automotive glass, and energy-efficient building materials, the
technology offers a clear path to lower cost per square meter, stable automatic optical switching, and
scalable integration, thereby de-risking commercialization of passive, solar-responsive thermal
management products.

Path forward and anticipated commercialization steps: The next phase focuses on scale-out and product
integration rather than fundamental materials discovery. Near-term activities include (i) kilogram
continuous nanoparticle production campaigns to establish cost curves and supply reliability; (ii)
formulation of VO₂ nanoparticle inks and dispersions optimized for lamination, large-scale overcoating,
or interlayer incorporation in laminated safety glass, as well as VO2 embedded glass; and (iii) durability
and cycling tests aligned with building and automotive standards by closely collaborating with interested
industrial partners. These steps naturally support licensing or cooperative development agreements with
glass manufacturers and film producers, enabling pilot-scale demonstration windows or roof panels (car).

Longer-term commercialization is expected to proceed through non-exclusive or field-limited licensing
of the reactor technology and materials know-how, followed by partner-led scale-up to commercial

production lines, positioning the technology for rapid market entry in energy-efficient glazing without
reliance on external power or control systems.

Urgency is reflected by rapid tightening of building-energy codes targeting envelope solar heat gain,
combined with rising thermal-management penalties in electric vehicles—where passive load reduction
directly translates into extended driving range. These situations create immediate commercial demand
for scalable thermochromic glazing.

Funding & industrial partners are being sought for active engagement to move the technology to the
next level by evaluating this VO₂ technology and materials for advanced real-world applications.

 1. INTRODUCTION

The concept of “zero-energy” buildings has been proposed to reduce our reliance on energy based

on fossil fuel energy.[1] It is estimated that energy-efficient window technology can potentially

save up to 15~18% of building heating/cooling energy, as 30% of a home's heating energy is lost

through windows.[2-4] Thus energy loss through windows is a major factor in building related

carbon dioxide (CO2) emission, which causes threats to ecosystems and human society. Therefore,

there is an urgent need to improve the efficiency of the window in order to reduce current energy

consumption. Windows and other similar installations enable the usage of sunlight to minimize

artificial lighting and improve indoor aesthetics. Yet, solar radiation through windows is

responsible for 40-70% of the cooling load in buildings.[5]

Thermochromic materials enable limiting energy consumption through windows, and the

challenge in their application is to find a simple scalable process to produce the most efficient

materials. Thermochromic materials, specifically monoclinic M-phase vanadium dioxide (VO2

(M)), have the potential to be used for energy-efficient windows in buildings owing to simple

structure, automatic thermochromic operation, low-cost, and zero-energy input characteristics.

VO2 films have attracted much attention because they can modulate near-infrared (NIR)

transmittance via their reversible and ultra-fast transition (in seconds) between the semiconducting

monoclinic phase (M) and the metallic rutile phase (R) at ~68°C.[6,7] The crystal structures are

shown in Figure 1-1. The semiconductor-to-metal transition (SMT) adjusts the axial lengths, axial

angles, symmetry, and unit cell shape. The unit cell shape for VO2 (R) is a perfect cube, as VO2

(M) is not a perfect cube with skewed axial lengths.[8] So-called “smart” windows incorporating

VO2 (R) can efficiently block NIR light from entering the indoors when the temperature is above

the transition point, thus reducing energy consumption caused by air conditioning in summer. Also,

 2

VO2 (M) can allow NIR light to enter the indoors when the temperature is low, raising the indoor

temperature (Figure 1-2).

The temperature triggered semiconductor-to-metal transition (SMT) of VO2, associated with up to

2~4 orders of magnitudes of reversible change in electrical conductivity, has been first observed

by Morin in 1959.[9] Since then, both theoretical and experimental studies of this solid-state phase-

change material and a variety of its activating methods (light, electrical, chemical, mechanical, and

magnetic) have been intensively conducted to elucidate the transition mechanism and develop

various applied VO2 technologies, e.g., smart windows, optical switches, switchable mirrors,

modulators, terahertz devices, nanoactuators, sensors, and smart radiators, as summarized in the

following review papers.[10-13] For smart window applications, two parameters are important to

quantify the effectiveness of such thermochromic materials: 1) solar energy modulation (SM) and

2) luminous transmittance (𝑇𝑇𝑙𝑙𝑙𝑙𝑙𝑙 ). SM characterizes the change in radiation transmittance between

hot and cold phases: higher SM results in the better modulation/usage of solar energy while 𝑇𝑇𝑙𝑙𝑙𝑙𝑙𝑙

 4

measures the amount of light passing through the film.[14] Three outstanding technical barriers

must be overcome in order to enable wide film adoption in the market, including 1) relatively low

visible light transmittance (VLT, <40%), 2) limited solar energy modulation (SM, <10%) between

its two operating phases, as well as 3) unpleasant brownish-yellow color of the VO2 film. In

addition, the SM and VLT are especially difficult to improve simultaneously for bulk films. The

fabrication of thin films made using a gas-phase process in a vacuum environment enabled the

improved modulation performance (~10~15% SM); yet the high cost of this approach has limited

their implementation in commercial products.[15] However, in 2010, the concept of “nanoparticle

based thermochromism” has been introduced by Li-Niklasson-Granqvist. They theoretically

predicted that nanoparticle VO2/polymer(glass) composite film could potentially achieve >20%

SM at >60% VLT.[16] This prediction was confirmed experimentally by Luo’s group in 2014,

demonstrating a VO2/polyurethane smart film having 22.3% SM at 45% VLT.[17] This

encouraging SM improvement triggered a new round of research interest worldwide since it sheds

light on the possibility of putting VO2-based smart film technology into practice for real-world

application in the feasible future.[18-21] The achievements made thus far largely result from an

in-depth understanding of thermochromic phenomena in VO2 and film development using

hybrid/composite materials at the nanometer scale.[22-24]

Given the dual functions of metal/semiconductor for the nano-VO2, various strategies that were

originally developed for tuning the properties of semiconductor quantum dots and metallic

nanoparticles can be used to improve the thermochromic performance of VO2 nanoparticles,

including manipulating the particle size and shape. To improve the thermochromic film properties

for smart window application, the size of VO2 nanoparticles synthesized is a critical

parameter.[25,26] By reducing the nanoparticle size to well below 40 nm in diameter, the solar

 5

energy modulation has been seen to improve with limited dampening of the luminous

transmittance.[27] Surface modification through either a core-shell structure[28-31] or surfactant

coating[32] has also been shown to result in high-performing films due to limiting aggregation of

the nanoparticles. In addition, the focus of the nanoparticle's aspect ratio towards changing the

morphology allows for a more significant thermochromic effect.[33] Moreover, adjusting the VO2

optical and electrical properties by doping elements with tungsten,[34,35] tin,[36,37]

fluoride,[38,39] zirconium,[40-42] and germanium[43] has been used to tune transition

temperature and vary its electrical properties. According to D. Liu et al., the tungsten dopant has

shown the best results in essentially reversing the transition temperature for the film

produced.[44,45] It is predicted by Prof. Arigong’s team[46] that the desired ideal switching profile

of a composite film can reach 37% solar modulation and 90% luminous transmission (Note: The

ideal switching profile indicates that (1) in the metallic state, in summer, 90% of visible light and

10% infrared are transmitted through the window, and (2) in the semiconducting status, in winter,

90% of all solar spectra including visible and infrared light are transmitted). By driving the

thermochromic performance close to the target through synthesizing and optimizing VO2

nanoparticles and their composite, smart window film applications begin to enter the market,

impacting building CO2 emissions.

Despite impressive progress in raising solar energy modulation and adding more functions to the

VO2 smart film, the scalability of making nano-VO2 (M) nanoparticles remains a technical

challenge. The majority of reported high-performing VO2 nanoparticles based thermochromic

films utilized VO2 nanoparticles synthesized by time consuming batch/autoclave reactors.[47]

These approaches often require the annealing of as-synthetized nanoparticles to achieve

monoclinic VO2 (M) nanoparticles, which can cause the sintering of particles at elevated annealing

 6

temperatures.[48,49] In addition, the annealed nanoparticles require surface modification to

prevent their agglomeration and to achieve their compatibility with the polymer.

Continuous flow hydrothermal (CFHT0 synthesis promises massive manufacturing of high-quality

nano-scale materials with good scalability,[50-52] but it has yet to be used to synthesize

nanoparticles for adequate high-quality thermochromic films. In 2015, Powell, M. J. et al. reported

the synthesis of nano-VO2 using a CFHT approach, obtaining relatively large and mixed-phase

VO2 nanoparticles (50~200 nm) that cannot provide high solar energy modulation.[53,54] Later,

Malarde reported the CFHT synthesis of the target VO2 (M) nanoparticles in a single step, however,

the thermochromic performance of such nanoparticles was very limited, with only 3.8% of Δ𝑇𝑇𝑠𝑠𝑠𝑠𝑠𝑠

at 15% of VLT.

Recently, we have shown that the CFHT synthesis method can enable the synthesis of monoclinic

VO2 (M) nanoparticles in a one-step process.54 The smart thermochromic film technology can be

advanced by elucidating the relationship between SM and the composite material structure at the

nanoparticle scale (i.e., particle size, morphology, loading, dispersity, doping, and surface

modification, as well as film thickness and porosity) and thereby develop strategies to optimize

high-performance smart VO2/polymer composite film with increased SM without diminishing the

VLT. In this work, we have conducted systematic parametric studies to understand the influence

of the above-mentioned particle/film structure/properties on the thermochromic performance of

VO2/PVB smart films by using VO2 nanoparticles (nanospheres and nanorods) synthesized via a

one-step CFHT synthesis reactor. We discuss in detail the effect of VO2 nanoparticle morphology,

surface modification, and doping on the properties of hybrid VO2/polymer composite smart films.

These parameters encompass variations in nanoparticle size, structure, and surface modifications.

This systematic approach enabled us to finely tune the SMT temperature using dopants in an

 7

efficient, high-throughput manner. Additionally, we investigated strategies to enhance the solar

modulation properties of VO2 nanoparticles incorporated into hybrid polymer films. These

strategies involved (i) transforming the nanoparticle morphology from spherical to nanorod

structures, (ii) modifying the surface of VO2 nanoparticles using low refractive index ligands, and

(iii) introducing supplementary thermochromic materials to improve solar energy modulation and

achieve a more visually appealing green color in the smart film design. Through strategic

adjustments to the thermochromic and optical properties of the VO2 nanoparticles, we successfully

achieved a solar energy modulation of 10.0% for the as-synthesized VO2/polymer composite film

and 19.9% solar energy modulation for PFDA-VO2/NLET hybrid smart films.

The particle size, morphology, and dispersion of VO2 nanoparticles can significantly influence

luminance transmittance and solar energy modulation in the composite film. As illustrated in

begins to intersect when the VO2 nanoparticle size exceeds ~90 nm. The calculated

semiconducting room temperature and metallic high temperature (90℃) UV-Vis-NIR spectra are

The simplest calculation of the transmittance through a VO2-polymer composite film would use

the Maxwell-Garnett theory for calculating the index of refraction and dielectric constant, which

would then be used by the effective medium theory to determine the transmittance and reflectance

without scattering. The effective medium theory assumes that the particle diameter is small enough

not to cause scattering, so the composite film can be approximated as a homogenous material with

an effective index of refraction that is a mix of the index of refraction of the polymer and that of

the particle. However, as the VO2 particle size increases, scattering becomes increasingly

important, and alternative calculations must be used to take this into consideration. Therefore, in

our calculations, Mie scattering theory was used to determine the scattering coefficients, which the

four-flux method uses to account for light scattering effects when calculating the transmission at

 9

normal incidence.[55-57] These calculations were used to analyze the effect of particle size on the

optical properties of a 10 μm thick film of spherical VO2 nanoparticles embedded in a polyvinyl

butyral matrix with a filling factor of 0.01.

It is also reported that the nanoparticles synthesized should be maintained at less than 40 nm to

achieve high transmittance due to more prominent light scattering.[58] The concept has been

demonstrated that VO2 nanoparticles reaching this low diameter can contribute to high luminance

In other words, by synthesizing ultra-small nanoparticles, the increased SM can be achieved

without the expense of a reduced VLT. The physics behind this lies in the fact that the intensity of

producing a higher VLT. Meanwhile, reduced size of VO2 can increase surface area and enhance

localized surface plasma resonance, increasing the SM.

In the CFHT process to synthesize the VO2 nanoparticles, the supercritical water (500°C) mixes

with the room temperature precursor (25°C) to rapidly heat the precursor to ~400°C which is

 11

required to synthesize ultrasmall VO2 nanoparticles by following the so-called “heating-up”

methodology. Such a process can also drastically lower the residence time of the nanoparticles

synthesized in the reactor in a matter of seconds, which is much shorter than that of days in a

conventional autoclave reactor. The higher flow rates from either the precursor pump or the

supercritical water pump can increase Reynolds number (turbulence), leading to enhanced fluid

mixing, which drives the formation of supersaturated solution and stimulates the rate of nucleation

and homogeneous nucleation, resulting in smaller crystals.[60,61]

It should be pointed out that there is a limitation for a higher flow rate since it results in the reduced

residence time within the reactor before leading toward the heat exchanger to cool down the

aqueous colloidal solution. This can cause us to obtain non-desired VO2 (B) or VO2 (A). The

desired phase, VO2 (M), is typically obtained for a long time at a higher temperature.[62] Through

parameter experiments, we have identified the optimal flow rates and temperature settings to

repeatably achieve 50 nm pure monoclinic VO2 (M) nanoparticles with the reactor outlet

maintaining above 395 °C and the precursor flow rates holding about 25% of the supercritical

water flow rate. When the precursor flow rate exceeds ~25% of the supercritical water flow rate,

the resulting nanoparticles are a mixture of VO2 (M) and VO2 (B) (Figure 2-3a). Monoclinic VO2

(B) is characterized by a very high irreversible transition temperature (450°C),[63] and therefore,

VO2 (B) cannot be utilized in smart window film applications.

In-situ coating experiments can be implemented in the existing continuous flow setup as the

coating solution can be introduced to the precursor before or after the VO2 nanoparticle nucleation

point within the reactor. This dynamic versatility of the hydrothermal reactor allows the attachment

of ligands to the nanoparticle surface to decrease aggregation in the separation process and allow

 12

a one-step process. XRD characterization of the specific phase structure of pure monoclinic VO2

(M) nanoparticles (Figure 2-3b) is crucial to producing quality thermochromic film.

 2.2.Morphology Control for As-Synthesized VO2 Nanoparticles

Besides size, the nanoparticle morphology could also have a large influence on thermochromic

performance since it has been demonstrated that the aspect ratio of metallic nanorods (NR) can

determine the localized surface plasma resonance (LSPR).[64-66] Synthesizing ultrasmall VO2

rods with optimized morphology is promising and experimentally investigated. We used sulfuric

acid (H2SO4) as the directing agent to grow the VO2 nanorods. With the addition of H2SO4 to our

precursor before the synthesis of nanoparticles, morphology can consist dominantly of NR instead

of entirely nanospheres (NS). The formation mechanism could be attributed to the zero potential

and dissolution crystallization since adding H2SO4 to the precursor before the synthesis can affect

the balance of crystallization and dissolution. [67] The VO2 nanoparticles are nonstable in a highly

acidic environment as they are soluble in strong acids and alkalis, returning to tetravalent vanadium

acyl ions. In our experiment, we observed that aggregation and dissolution of the nanoparticles

were more prevalent when the pH value for the precursor synthesized was below 0.60 (Figures 2-

4c and 2-4f). NS formation is prominent for the original precursor (no H2SO4 added) pH of 1.35

(Figures 2-4a and 2-4d). The precursor pH volume of 0.72 (Figures 2-4b and 2-4e) allows for the

 14

NR formation and the stable environment of the nanoparticles. The optimization of the precursor

preparation allows for the rate of crystal growth to decrease for NR and NS to be collected.

 2.3.Effect of Surface Modification

The surfaces of VO2 nanoparticles should also be carefully designed to prevent any nanoparticle

aggregation, which can be prominent for the bare nanoparticles when preparing the VO2 ink. The

existence of any large agglomerate for dispersed VO2 nanoparticles within the polymer, as

typically reflected as an overlap (or hump) between the two spectrum curves for the low/high

temperature in the visible light region, will significantly reduce the SM of the film. As an example,

the two UV-VIS-IR curves, notably crossing at low/high temperature for 139 nm VO2

nanoparticles in 186 nm thickness film shows a much lower SM of 4.8% as compared to 11.3%

SM for the 205 nm thickness film with 39 nm VO2 nanoparticles, as demonstrated by K. Li et

al.[69] This suggests nanoparticles aggregation must be avoided, to the maximum extent, when

being dispersed in the process of preparing ink and making the composite film. Using the uncoated,

bare VO2 nanoparticles (Figure 2-8a) can make improving the solar energy modulation difficult

 18

while maintaining a suitable VLT. The coating or surface modification of the VO2 nanoparticles is

critical to reduce the surface free energy, thus increasing dispersity. The stabilized VO2

nanoparticles can freely disperse without aggregation.

 2.4.Tuning of the Transition Temperature with Tungsten Dopant

Further progress can be made for the smart window film to lower the SMT temperature of 68°C

by adding a tungsten dopant. The V-V pair interaction strength can be reduced by tungsten added

into the VO2 crystal lattice.[73] Various amounts of tungsten dopant, ranging from 0.5 at% to 2.0

at%, were added to the precursor before the synthesis. The synthesis had the addition of H2SO4 to

the precursor for NR formation and in-situ DA coating. As shown in Figure 2-10, the addition of

the tungsten dopant can thoroughly reduce the transition temperature from 68.5°C to 38.5°C at 2

at% W.

 3. CURRENT AND FUTURE ASPECT OF RESEARCH

 3.1.Introduction to Heating-Up Method for Nanoparticle Synthesis

The heating-up method is adapted from the "hot-injection" method used to synthesize nanoscale

materials. In the hot-injection method, a high-temperature solvent is employed to introduce the

precursor in a batch reactor, leading to the nucleation and growth of particles. However, scaling

up this process presents significant challenges as it involves handling large volumes of high-

temperature solvent, which can be hazardous. Additionally, the heating-up method is superior due

to its more efficient mixing time and better heat management. Thus, using the heating-up method

is a scalable approach to obtaining a high yield of nanoparticles.

The four stages of the heating-up method are monomer formation, nucleation, growth, and

equilibrium. To effectively minimize the diameter, three key factors must be addressed when using

the heating-up method: i) nucleation burst, ii) size-focusing growth, and iii) delayed Ostwald

ripening.[78,79]

The nucleation burst, responsible for forming nuclei from precursors and monomers, is influenced

by various factors in the system, including temperature, concentration, residence time, pressure,

pH, stirring/mixing, and solvent selection. By carefully controlling these parameters, it's possible

to achieve a rapid nucleation rate. As shown in Figure 3-1, the increased nucleation (dN/dt) can be

achieved with a supersaturated precursor, minimal surface energy, and increased temperature. The

key idea is that the heating-up synthesis induces burst-nucleation by precursor decomposition at a

critical temperature.[80]

 34

Figure 3-1. The nucleation rate in relation to the change in supersaturation and B) surface energy.

Various C) supersaturations and surface energies for the nucleation rate as a function of

temperature. The nucleation rates were calculated using D = 10–11 m2 s–1, rm = 2 × 10–10 m, [M∞]

= 0.001 mol m–3, and u = 0.45. Image obtained from J. Van Embden et al.[79]

When heated to a critical temperature, the precursor undergoes immediate decomposition,

resulting in the generation of an excess of monomers. This excess forms a highly supersaturated

reaction environment, initiating a burst-nucleation process. Within this process, numerous nuclei

grow in unison and swiftly consume the available monomers, thereby impeding further particle

growth. This approach is commonly shown as size focusing for the synthesis of nanoparticles.

As shown in Figure 3-2, the final quality of the nanoparticles is primarily determined by two

factors: the decomposition rate of the precursor (rd) and the growth rate of the grains (rg). When

rd ≪ rg, the quantity of monomers is insufficient, restraining the growth of smaller grains and

 35

broad particle size distribution (PSD). Conversely, when rd ≫ rg, the supersaturated monomer

solution generates an excess of nuclei. With increasing concentration, these nuclei tend to

aggregate. Therefore, in a hydrothermal reaction, prior to nucleation, monomers accumulate at

very high concentrations, leading to elevated levels of nuclei, and these nuclei aggregate before

growth commences. To present a narrow PSD of nanoparticles synthesized, the decomposition

and growth rate need to be equivalent.

Figure 3-2. The mechanism for size focusing based upon the decomposition rate (rd) and the

growth rate (rg) for the synthesis of nanoparticles. The ideal process has equal rd and rg to yield

unform particles. The rd<<rg yields a broad PSD due to the monomers being insufficient. The

rd>>rg yields aggregated particles due to the supersaturated monomer solution generating an excess

of nuclei.

In our specific process, using a CFHT reactor, the precursor solution is introduced upstream and

mixed with high temperature supercritical water. The residence time, from initial mixing to

cooling, is just a few seconds. A rapid change in temperature from room temperature to the

supercritical regime provides the necessary energy for nucleation and nanocrystal growth. The

 36

fast-heating rate also provides smaller particles as slow heating rates tend to aggregate

particles.[82] By analyzing the activation energy for the precursor, we can determine the PSD.

The activation energy for the reaction has great importance towards the nanoparticles PSD. As

shown by Embden et al. in Figure 3-3, precursor X has a low activation energy, leading to

simultaneous rapid nucleation and growth, resulting in a broad PSD. The rapid broadening of the

PSD is caused by the difference in size between growing nuclei and the formation of new,

smaller nuclei. Precursor Y, with moderate reactivity, produces a balanced nucleation and

inhibited growth, resulting in a narrow PSD. Precursor Z, with high activation energy, leads to

cluster nucleation and inhibited growth. Furthermore, slow nucleation enhances Ostwald

ripening, yielding larger diameter sizes and a broader PSD. By the careful consideration of the

activation energy of the reaction, the PSD can be tuned to be narrow.

 37

Figure 3-3. The particle size distribution for nanoparticle synthesized depending on the activation

energy of the precursor. Precursor X has a low activation energy, precursor Y has a moderate

reactivity, and precursor Z has a high activation energy. Image obtained from J. Van Embden et al.

[79]

During the growth and equilibrium stages of nanocrystal formation, it is essential to carefully

examine Ostwald ripening to prevent the formation of large particles.[83] The stability of the

nanoparticles immediately after the growth stage is crucial. Excessive temperatures can render the

 38

nanoparticles unstable, leading to the dissolution of smaller particles and their growth onto larger

ones. Additionally, the solvent can influence the stability of the nanoparticles after synthesis. In

our experiment results using H2SO4, the nanoparticles begin to dissociate into the solution as well

as aggregate. Without the addition of H2SO4, the colloidal VO2 solution remained dark. To limit

the extent of Ostwald ripening, it is important to maintain a narrow PSD.

 3.2.VO2 Nanoparticle Synthesis Using Hydrazine Monohydrate

The understanding of nanoparticle synthesis in the heating-up method which rapidly heats the

precursor solution allows for achieving a narrow PSD and reducing the diameter size. Fine-tuning

parameters, such as the temperature inside the heating zone and at the reactor outlet, as well as

adjusting flow rates and the length of supercritical water insertion, allows control over the

residence time. The precise control of these parameters allows for the prevention of large particles

and broad PSD.

The initial step involves preparing the V4+ precursor. When preparing the V4+ precursor using

hydrazine monohydrate (NH2NH2·H2O) as a reducing agent, the pH is adjusted with H2SO4 before

the addition of the hydrazine monohydrate. As H2SO4 mixes with V2O5, the hydrazine

monohydrate is added drop wise to reduce V5+ to V4+. Since hydrazine monohydrate is a strong

reducing agent, it is critical to slowly add the reducing agent to not over reduce. We have found

the ideal ratio between V2O5 and hydrazine monohydrate to be 1:0.5. When exceeding the ration

to 1:0.75 or higher, V2O3 (V3+) nanoparticles will be formed. Once the proper amount of hydrazine

monohydrate is added to the precursor under vigorous mixing, a clear blue color will be obtained

containing VO2+ cations. The key to switching the reducing agents from oxalic acid dihydrate to

hydrazine monohydrate is many research groups have shown promising VO2 nanoparticle sizes of

 39

less than 20 nm. These synthesis methods have included a hydrothermal autoclave reactor and

microwave assisted hydrothermal method.[59]

Since hydrazine monohydrate is used, faster reaction rates enable a decrease in residence time,

reducing the potential for particle aggregation. This, in turn, allows for higher precursor flow rates

and concentrations. The precursor flow rate has been increased to 50 mL/min to obtain pure VO2

(M) particles, whereas the precursor flow rate using oxalic acid dihydrate was maximized at 30

mL/min. However, challenges have been encountered with the formation of large particles, ranging

from 100 nm to over 500 nm, leading to filter blockages. As shown in Figure 3-4, when adjusting

the concentration of the precursor solution, the average nanoparticle size was smallest at 4x diluted.

The PSD remained broad due to the change in the reducing agent. Although the specific VO2

crystal structure is obtained, the thin film produced using these large particles is not valuable.

To address the challenge of synthesizing large particles, the pH of the precursor solution can be

adjusted using weak bases such as urea (H2NCONH2) and ammonia (NH3), or stronger bases like

sodium hydroxide (NaOH). This pH adjustment causes the VO2+ cations to precipitate, forming an

amorphous, black, cloudy solution. Precise pH control of the precursor is critical for producing

ultra-small particles.[35,59,84] As explained by J. Son et al, when the pH of the precursor exceeds

10, the nanoparticle size ranged from 20 to 50 nm. Additionally, nanocrystals were able to be

obtained once the pH exceeded 5.5 rather than microcrystals.[85] When adjusting the pH to

high levels, we have noticed the particle size of the precipitate decrease. (Figure 3-5)

The hydrothermal autoclave reactor is the preferred method for precipitating amorphous particles

in the V4+ precursor, enabling the synthesis of the basic precursor solution and the production of

VO2 (M) nanoparticles in a one-step process.[35,59,84] Compared to a CFHT, the autoclave

 42

reactor offers the advantage of avoiding blockages. Both systems use the same heat-up synthesis

method for nanoparticles as the precursor is rapidly heated. In the CFHT process, a slurry pump is

required to introduce the precursor into the supercritical water, mimicking the autoclave's concept.

However, using our previous high-pressure precursor pump, filters can obstruct the system and

lead to unsafe over-pressurization. Additionally, the basic solution can be fed through the system

to mix with the precursor prior to the heating zone. This will successfully allow the precursor to

precipitate without the trouble of changing the precursor pump. The high-pH precipitated precursor

will rapidly form the VO2 nanocrystals in the heat zone.[86] As the precursor becomes prepared

properly, the temperatures and flow rates of the synthesis will need to be readily tuned.

Since the other methods using an autoclave reactor vary from the CFHT system, it is difficult to

determine the proper temperature of the heating zone. Additionally, the residence times are much

shorter in the continuous flow system in comparison to the hours and days of the autoclave reactor.

The decomposition/formation temperature can be used to estimate the reaction temperature needed

to fully convert the VO2 nanoparticles to VO2 (M). VO2 (M) nanoparticles have been shown to be

~240°C. [87]

 3.3.Film Optimization Techniques

The thermochromic thin film produced with VO2 (M) nanoparticles can be optimized for high-

performance SM. By using the as-synthesized VO2 (M) nanoparticles, the surface attraction

between particles can lead to agglomeration. The instability is due to Van der Waals forces.[88] A

way to counter this issue is to introduce a surface modified nanoparticle. Surface modification with

effective coatings such as small ligands or surfactants increases stability by minimizing these

forces.[89] Previous successful coatings include DA, PFDA, and SDS, which improved the

 43

nanoparticle stability and dispersion. Achieving high-quality thermochromic thin films

necessitates nanoparticle surface modification.

In preparing the ink, adjusting the particle loading percentage is vital to prevent sedimentation and

aggregation. Our experiments indicate that when particle loading exceeds 7.5 wt%, solar energy

modulation decreases due to particle aggregation and overlap in the visible light region. The high

particle loading will cause the nanoparticles to come together once dried in the thin film.

Film thickness is another crucial parameter that can be tuned. The wet thickness can be varied in

relation to the dry thickness, with ethanol content kept low to increase polymer concentration and

prevent nanoparticle aggregation during drying. Solar energy modulation and film thickness are

directly related, but excessive thickness causes decreased transmittance due to scattering.

Changing the polymer can be varied as ethyl cellulose, polyurethane, polyvinyl alcohol [90], and

PVB have been used in literature. The RI is not heavily influenced as PVA, PVB, and EC are ~1.47

with PU at 1.67. Although the film thickness and particle loading can be attributed to vital

parameters in the thermochromic thin film preparation, the drying process is critical to have the

VO2 nanoparticles stay dispersed.

The viscosity can be tuned to decrease the aggregation of the VO2 nanoparticles with a highly

viscous solvent. From X. Yang et al, the increased amount of thickener in the ink can increase the

SM from 6.6% to 14.5%. [91] By increasing the viscosity of the solvent, the nanoparticles will be

prevented from aggregation as the fluidity would be decreased. If using a low concentration of

PVB or no viscous solvent, the nanoparticles would begin to aggregate because of the good fluidity

of ink. The aggregated particles in the drying process can be shown in Figure 3-6.

 44

Figure 3-6. The aggregation of nanoparticles when preparing in the thermochromic thin film from

Additionally, the method of dispersion for the nanoparticles within the ink is crucial to achieve a

solar energy modulation thin film. Typically, an ultrasonication bath is used to properly

disperse the nanoparticles an ethanol solution for ~2 hours. The time given is from previous

experiments, where VO2 powder has been dispersed in a water solution. The VO2 nanoparticle

size is measured with the DLS over time increments. After 1 ½ hours of ultrasonication in a

batch, the original as-synthesized particle size was achieved. Similar dispersion techniques have

been shown in literature but uses a secondary stage of centrifuge. After a certain amount of time

in the ultrasonication bath, the solution is centrifuged for a small amount of time at a lower

revolution per minute to precipitate the larger particles within the solution.[92] This will allow

the larger nanoparticles to precipitate

 45

with the smaller nanoparticle’s dispersion in the solvent. The reduction of larger particles will

increase the solar energy modulation when preparing the thin films.

The preparation process is crucial to the optical and thermochromic performance of the thin films.

By utilizing a highly viscous solvent, the as-synthesized VO2 nanoparticles can remain well

dispersed once transferred from the ink to the dried film. The attraction of nanoparticles within the

film during the drying process will begin to cause aggregation to further decrease the film

performance. The use of a high concentration of polymer and a viscous solvent will allow the

particles to disperse evenly. The particle loading can be increased by the more stable particles to

occupy the appropriate space with the thin film fabricated.

 4. MATERIALS AND METHODS

 4.1.Preparation of VO2 nanoparticles Using Oxalic Acid Dihydrate

As described in our previous work, a continuous-flow hydrothermal reactor is used to prepare the

VO2 nanoparticles.[93] In a typical synthesis, the V4+ precursor (0.0089 M) is prepared by the

addition of 1.619 g of vanadium (V) oxide, V2O5 (Sigma-Aldrich), and 4.488 g of oxalic acid

dihydrate, C2H2O4·2H2O (Sigma-Aldrich) to one liter of deionized water. The solution is shaken

and ultrasonicated (VWR Ultrasonic Cleaner, Model 97043-968, Operating Frequency 35 kHz)

for ~6 hours until the solution becomes clear blue. The precursor pH value is ~1.35. The

experimental setup is schematically shown in Figure 4-1, which typically operates under

supercritical water. The DI water is heated up to make supercritical water which flows down and

mixes with an up-flowing precursor at room temperature in a ½” tubing reactor to trigger

nanoparticle nucleation and growth. The system pressure is controlled by using a backpressure

regulator.

 4.2. Preparation of VO2 nanoparticles Using Hydrazine Monohydrate

In a typical synthesis, 400 mL of water is heated in a bath on a hot plate to ~60-70°C. A stir bar is

added to the water solution and undergoes vigorous stirring. 0.647 g of vanadium (V) oxide is

added to the water. After, a certain amount of H2SO4 is added to the solution. Next, 86 µL of

vial. The diluted hydrazine-water solution to added dropwise to the precursor. After the hydrazine-

water solution is fully added, the precursor solution will turn from cloudy orange to transparent

blue. The nanoparticle synthesis undergoes the same protocols as 4.1. to obtain the VO2 (M)

 48

particles. The precursor flow rate is adjusted to 50 mL/min to avoid any blockages in the

continuous flow hydrothermal system.

 4.3.Preparation of decanoic acid coated VO2 nanoparticles

The decanoic acid (DA) coating of VO2 nanoparticles is applied through an in-situ method for a

one-step synthesis and coating. Depending on the molar ratios between the V4+ precursor and the

DA coating solution, DA was added to a 50% ethanol @50% deionized water solution. Also, a

certain amount of H2SO4 is added to the VO2 precursor prior to the synthesis. The V4+ precursor

was fed separately from the DA solution to mix before being introduced to the reactor in the

continuous flow hydrothermal reactor. The DA-coated VO2 nanoparticle colloidal solution was

collected, washed with ethanol and water, and dried at room temperature.

 4.4.Synthesis of W-doped VO2 nanoparticles

The W-doped V4+ precursor (0.0089 M) is prepared by the addition of 1.619 g of vanadium (V)

oxide, V2O5 (Sigma-Aldrich) and 4.488 g of oxalic acid dihydrate, C2H2O4·2H2O (Sigma-Aldrich)

to one liter of deionized water. The solution is shaken and ultrasonicated for ~6 hours until the

solution becomes clear blue. A certain amount of sodium tungstate dihydrate, Na2WO4·2H2O

(Sigma-Aldrich) is added to the V4+ blue precursor depending on the dopant percentage. The

decanoic acid coating was performed, so a certain amount of H2SO4 was added to the V4+

precursor. The decanoic acid solution was prepared as stated above. The V4+ precursor was fed

separately from the decanoic acid solution to mix before being introduced to the reactor. The DA-

coated VO2 nanoparticle colloidal solution was collected, washed with ethanol and water, and dried

at room temperature.

 49

 4.5.Preparation of perfluorodecanoic acid coated VO2 nanoparticles

The perfluorodecanoic acid (PFDA, Sigma-Aldrich) VO2 coating is performed in an ex-situ

reaction at room temperature.[94] First, 89 mg of VO2 nanoparticles are added to 36 mL of

deionized water and ultrasonicated for one hour to disperse particles evenly within the solution.

Second, 1.580 g of tetrabutylammonium bromide (TBAB, Sigma-Aldrich) is added to 25 mL of a

50% acetonic solution and mixed till transparent. The solutions are combined and stirred

vigorously for one hour at room temperature in ambient conditions. After one hour, 428 mg of

PFDA is added to the solution under stirring for 10 minutes. The solution is centrifuged, washed

with acetone and water, and dried at room temperature.

 4.6.Preparation of sodium dodecyl sulfate coated VO2 nanoparticle ink

The sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) coating is done through an ex-situ method once the VO2

nanoparticles have been separated and dried. 40 mg of the VO2 nanoparticles are dispersed in 5

mL of ethanol for 1 hour to prevent any aggregation of the particles and to have them uniformly

suspended in the solution. Next, sonicate a certain amount of SDS in 5 mL of ethanol for 1 hour

to completely dissolve the SDS. Finally, the SDS solution is mixed with the VO2 ethanol dispersion

and then sonicated for another 1 hour. A certain amount of polyvinyl butyral (PVB, Sigma-Aldrich)

is added to the VO2/SDS ethanol dispersion and goes under ultrasonication for ~6 hours.

 4.7.Preparation of VO2 composite films

The as-prepared bare and coated VO2 nanoparticles are added to 5 mL of ethyl alcohol and

ultrasonicated for two hours. Then, 0.5 g of PVB is added to 5 mL of ethyl alcohol in a secondary

container under ultrasonication until fully dissolved. The two solutions are mixed with one another

to undergo ultrasonication for five hours to ensure the dispersion of the VO2 nanoparticles. The

 50

VO2 ink is uniformly cast onto polycarbonate (PC) film using a doctor blade and dried at 80°C for

one hour.

 4.8.Preparation of NLETS/VO2 composite film

The as-prepared PFDA-coated or uncoated VO2 nanoparticles are added to 10 mL of ethyl alcohol

and ultrasonicated for two hours. Then, 914 mg of nickel (II) perchlorate (Sigma-Aldrich), 781 mg

of neopentyl glycol (Sigma-Aldrich), and 3.224 g of TBAB are added to the solution and stirred

for one hour. 0.5 g of PVB is added to the solution under ultrasonication and shaking for 30 minutes

to stabilize the suspension. The NLETS/ VO2 ink is uniformly cast onto PC film using a doctor

blade and dried at 80°C for one hour.
