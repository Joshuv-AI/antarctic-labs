The Therapeutic Potential of Stem Cell Therapy for Doxorubicin‐Induced Cardiotoxicity: A Narrative Review
Authors: Ali Mirzaei, Pouria Rezaei, Reza Golchin Vafa, Saleheh Jahani, Reza Arefnezhad, Hossein Fatemian, Mohammad Hossein Nikoo, Amirhossein Sahebkar, Fatemeh Rezaei‐Tazangi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsDoxorubicin (DOX) is a potent anthracycline chemotherapeutic agent whose clinical utility is significantly constrained by severe side effects, most notably DOX‐induced cardiotoxicity (DIC). Pathophysiological mechanisms of DIC include oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, programmed cell death, and inflammation. Given the therapeutic potential of stem cell technology, this narrative review aims to summarize current evidence regarding the use of various stem cell types, particularly mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), as a potential treatment for DIC.MethodsThis study conducted a comprehensive review of the literature concerning stem cell‐based therapies in the context of DIC. We evaluated evidence regarding both pluripotent stem cells (including embryonic and induced pluripotent stem cells) and multipotent adult stem cells (such as bone marrow, adipose, and umbilical cord‐derived MSCs) to assess their role in mitigating DOX‐induced cardiac damage.ResultsCurrent research indicates that stem cells, particularly MSCs, possess significant regenerative capabilities that may counteract the multifaceted pathophysiology of DIC. Evidence suggests that these cells can modulate inflammatory responses, alleviate oxidative stress, and support mitochondrial repair in damaged cardiac tissue, thereby offering a promising strategy to protect against or reverse chemotherapy‐induced heart injury.ConclusionStem cell‐based therapy represents a promising and innovative frontier for the management of DOX‐induced cardiotoxicity. While preclinical and emerging data highlight the therapeutic potential of MSCs in cardiac protection, further standardized research is required to fully elucidate their mechanisms and ensure clinical safety and efficacy for patients undergoing chemotherapy.Keywords: cardiotoxicity, doxorubicin, mesenchymal stem cells, stem cell therapy

Introduction

Among the anthracycline family of chemotherapeutic agents, doxorubicin (DOX) is the most frequently used medication in the treatment of numerous malignancies, including lymphomas, leukemias, sarcomas, breast cancer, gastric cancer, and solid tumors [1, 2, 3, 4]. By intercalating into the DNA double helix of cancer cells, DOX can effectively disrupt transcriptional activity [5]. Despite its tremendous therapeutic effects, some serious side effects hinder its clinical use [6, 7, 8]. Based on previous studies, hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, fat metabolism dysfunction, fertility concerns, and neurotoxicity limit the maximum dose of DOX administration [9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. It is documented that the long‐term use of DOX is associated with a considerable risk of heart failure, reversible and irreversible cardiomyopathy, and cardiac arrhythmia [16, 17, 18, 19]. Reversible cardiotoxic effects of DOX consist of pericarditis, left ventricular dysfunction, and arrhythmia [20]. It is worth mentioning that DOX‐induced cardiotoxicity (DIC) can occur even years after stopping its administration [21]. Numerous molecular mechanisms have been proposed for DIC, such as the formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), disruption of mitochondrial function, topoisomerase II inhibition, Ca2+ homeostasis dysregulation, intracellular iron aggregation, induction of autophagy and apoptosis, as well as myofibrillar irregularity and loss (Figure 1) [22, 23, 24].

Doxorubicin can exert cardiotoxicity through various occurrences.

It has been addressed some cellular and molecular mechanisms against DIC. For instance, the transcription factor Nrf2 serves a functional role in this context by promoting antioxidant enzymes such as HO‑1 and NQO1. It is well‐demonstrated that the RNA methyltransferase NSUN2 protects against DOX‑caused myocardial injury by enhancing m5C methylation of Nrf2 mRNA, thus potentiating Nrf2 stability and function [25].

Several approaches have been recommended for preventing DIC, including co‐administration of dexrazoxane, utilization of β‐blockers, phytochemicals, and angiotensin inhibitors [26]. However, these methods have mainly been evaluated in vitro, and no effective preventive tool against this serious side effect has been suggested yet [27]. Stem cell therapy is considered an appealing therapeutic strategy in cardiovascular diseases [28], neurological disorders [29, 30], diabetes mellitus [31], pulmonary diseases [32], infertility [33], and even dermatologic diseases [34]. Stem cells are described as a class of immature cells possessing the capacity to regenerate and recover tissues in light of their specific capabilities, such as differentiation, proliferation, and self‐renewal potentials [35]. They also have several biological effects, such as suppression of apoptosis and inflammation, as well as angiogenesis stimulation, and cell recruitment [36, 37, 38, 39, 40].

It is important to distinguish between distinct stem cell subsets when assessing therapeutic capacity.

Multipotent stem cells, especially mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), have more limited differentiation capacity but offer benefits, such as ease of isolation, scalability, and low immunogenicity. These mesenchymal sources can be obtained from different tissues, for instance, bone marrow, adipose tissue, umbilical cord, and dental pulp [41, 42]. The potential therapeutic value of multipotent stem cells, including MSCs, has been shown in various studies against a variety of disorders [43, 44, 45].

Pluripotent stem cells (ESCs and iPSCs) possess unrestricted self‐renewal potential and can differentiate into all three germ layers, suggesting benefits for cardiac regeneration purposes theoretically [46]. However, ESCs raise ethical concerns and immunogenicity issues, while iPSCs, although patient‐specific and ethically acceptable, carry risks of genetic instability and residual reprogramming factor activity [47, 48].

It is worthwhile to mention that human pluripotent stem cell‐derived cardiomyocytes (hiPSC‑CMs) have become a useful approach for modeling DIC and discovering new cardioprotective targets [49, 50, 51]. As an example, human hiPSC‑CMs originated from pediatric oncology cases who experienced acute anthracycline‑induced cardiotoxicity displayed elevated DOX sensitivity, sarcomeric disarray, and oxidative stress in comparison with the control groups [52].

Interestingly, some clinical trials reflected hopeful findings of stem cell therapy for cardiovascular disorders, namely ischemic cardiomyopathy, myocardial infarction, heart failure, and DIC [53, 54, 55, 56, 57]. Although most evidence for stem cell therapy in DIC is obtained from experimental data, clinical translation has been initiated, mainly by concentrating on the safety and feasibility of allogeneic MSCs.

The most well‐known of these is related to the NIH‐sponsored SENECA (Stem Cell Injection in Cancer Survivors) trial, which evaluated 37 cancer survivors with chronic anthracycline‐induced cardiomyopathy (AIC), receiving allogeneic bone marrow‐derived MSCs or a placebo. The attained data of this research showed that this strategy was well‐tolerated without any new tumor formation [57, 58].

Besides SENECA, other early‐phase trials are appraising various delivery‐based methods and cell sources. In line with this notion, a randomized pilot Phase I trial (NCT02962661) is exploring the safety of transendocardial and intravenous delivery of donor MSCs in subjects with anthracycline‐caused heart failure [59]. An earlier finished Phase I trial (NCT02408432) also evaluated the safety and delivery ways for bone marrow‐derived MSCs to ameliorate heart function in similar patient populations (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT02408432).

Therefore, the present review study has been conducted to summarize evidence concerning the impacts of stem cell therapy in DIC.

Stem Cells and Chemotherapy‐Induced Organ Toxicity

MSC‐based therapies, as well as pluripotent stem cell approaches, have provided a new chance for counteracting organ toxicities induced by chemotherapeutic drugs. For instance, stem cell therapy showed a promising role against chemotherapy‐associated neurotoxicity [60]. This type of toxicity exhibits a broad range of pathological conditions such as chronic and subacute encephalopathies, acute cerebellar dysfunction, posterior reversible encephalopathy, chronic cognitive defects, meningitis, and myelopathy [61]. To prevent or treat these complications, some authors demonstrated that neurons originating from human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) showed resistance to peripheral neuropathy caused by chemotherapy [62]. The therapeutic effectiveness of iPSCs and ESCs is pertinent to their pluripotency characteristics, which are regulated by key transcription factors, such as Nanog, Oct4, and Sox2. These molecular agents repress differentiation processes while triggering genes sustaining a proliferative, undifferentiated status [63]. Beyond pluripotent cells, MSCs have demonstrated efficacy in chemotherapy‐induced nephrotoxicity, which results from a failure in detoxification, normal filtration, and excretive activities of the kidneys [64, 65]. In this direction, Markovic and colleagues assessed the impacts of animal bone marrow‐derived MSCs (BM‐MSCs) in a murine model of cisplatin‐induced nephrotoxicity [64]. Finally, they showed that intraperitoetneal (ip) administration of BM‐MSCs considerably diminished nephrotoxicity, interleukin‐17 (IL‐17), and tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α) while enhancing the serum levels of anti‐inflammatory factors such as IL‐6, IL‐10, kynurenine, and nitric oxide (NO) [64]. In addition, BM‐MSCs have shown beneficial effects on paclitaxel‐induced peripheral neuropathy in rat models by inhibiting inflammation, apoptosis, and oxidative stress [66]. The favorable effects of BM‐MSCs on cisplatin‐induced hepatotoxicity, which includes damage to bile ducts, liver sinusoids, and hepatocytes, have also been accentuated in a recently published paper [67, 68]. Azzam et al. declared that BM‐MSCs combined with retinoic acid, a differentiating agent expressed in hepatocytes, have a mitigating role in cisplatin‐induced hepatotoxicity in vivo by attenuating hepatic damage indices, such as aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), TNF‐α, nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidase, oxidative stress markers, MDA (malondialdehyde), catalase, and GSH (glutathione) [67]. Pulmonary complications are one of the detrimental effects of chemotherapy, which can stem from the direct effects of chemotherapy agents, secondary immunodeficiency, or the body's immune response to changes in the chemotherapy regimen or doses [69, 70]. Several studies have shown that MSCs possess high therapeutic potential for acute pneumonia, pulmonary fibrosis, and acute lung injuries [71, 72]. Premature ovarian insufficiency (POI) and infertility are other complications of chemotherapy that adversely affect the quality of life [73, 74, 75, 76]. In this context, Shin et al. showed that stem cell therapy by administering embryonic stem cell‐derived mesenchymal progenitor cells (ES‐MP) led to improved ovarian function by restoring ovarian follicles, enhancing anti‐müllerian hormone (AMH), estrogen levels, and oocyte quality, as well as attenuating apoptosis in female mice receiving cisplatin [77]. Taken together, these studies highlighted the beneficial therapeutic effects of stem cells, especially MSCs, on the prevention or regeneration of chemotherapy‐induced injury.

Stem Cells and Doxorubicin‐Induced Cardiotoxicity: Therapeutic Applications

In Vitro Investigations

The evaluations on in vitro models of DIC have also revealed that stem cells derived from bone marrow and amniotic fluid rescue H9c2 cells from apoptosis and senescence [78, 79]. Regarding this matter, Chen et al. declared that co‑culture with BM‐MSCs avoided DOX‑caused senescence of H9c2 cells by mediation of the VEGF/Notch/TGF‑β signaling axis. Using a Transwell system, they revealed that MSCs elevated VEGF release, which triggered Jagged‑1/Notch‑1, subsequently repressing TGF‑β1 secretion, decreasing p53/p16 expression, and modifying telomerase function [79]. Likewise, Lazzarini et al. stated that the secretome (conditioned medium) of human amniotic fluid stem cells (hAFS)—especially under hypoxic preconditioning—has a protective role for H9c2 cells and for primary neonatal cardiomyocytes from DOX‑induced senescence and apoptosis. Mechanistically, hAFS‑CM triggered the PI3K/Akt pathway, giving rise to NF‑κB nuclear translocation, promotion of gene expression of anti‑apoptotic agents (Il6, Cxcl1), and stimulation of the efflux transporter Abcb1b, which in turn promotes DOX extrusion [78]. These two aforementioned studies are solely in vitro studies using H9c2 cells or neonatal mouse cardiomyocytes, which do not completely address the complex pathophysiology of adult human hearts or the progressive, chronic nature of DIC. Chen et al. utilized a relatively low concentration (0.5 µM) of DOX and a co‑culture system that may have overrated paracrine influences in light of high local factor concentrations. Lazzarini et al. relied on obtained hAFS samples from donors of different ages (25–46 years). Moreover, neither study evaluated prolonged impacts beyond 48 h, nor did they validate their outcomes in in vivo models of induced DIC. Finally, the H9c2 cell line is of embryonic origin and may not precisely reflect the senescence response of terminally differentiated adult cardiomyocytes [78, 79].

In another scientific study, Dargani et al. studied the cardioprotective role of embryonic stem cell‐derived exosomes (ESCs‐EXOs) on DOX‐induced pyroptosis [80]. Pyroptosis is a cell death pathway facilitated by an infection and plays an important role in DIC [81]. The aforementioned research team found that treatment with exosomes derived from embryonic stem cells decreased the biomarkers of pyroptosis including toll‐like receptor 4 (TLR4)‐NOD‐, LRR‐ and pyrin domain‐containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, as well as pro‐inflammatory factors such as TNF‐α, IL‐12, and Fas ligand; these exosomes promoted anti‐inflammatory mediators such as IL‐4, IL‐9, and IL‐13 (Figure 2). This outcome is mechanistically critical because it identifies pyroptosis, rather than apoptosis, as an important therapeutic target in DIC. However, the exclusive usage of embryonic stem cells in this research can raise translational concerns regarding immunogenicity and ethical issues, which could be circumvented by using clinically more accessible adult stem cell sources [80].

Stem cell therapy, especially mesenchymal sources, can have ameliorating effects on DOX‐induced cardiotoxicity via diverse mechanisms such as suppressing inflammation, pyroptosis, ferroptosis, oxidative stress, immune cell infiltration, collagen fiber accumulation, iron levels, and promoting angiogenesis.

In Vivo Investigations

The potential of stem cells for alleviating DIC has been emphasized in several studies. In this regard, Pinarli et al. explored the cardioprotective effects of adipose‐derived mesenchymal stem cells (AD‐MSCs) (2 × 106 cells/kg/dose) alone and in combination with resveratrol (a polyphenolic and antioxidant agent) on Wistar–Albino rats treated with DOX. The results revealed that AD‐MSCs alone raised left ventricle developed pressure (LVDP) and the ratio of pressure change in the ventricular cavity during the isovolumic period (+dp/dt) and lowered necrosis compared with the DOX‐only group, demonstrating a functional benefit [82]. The possible mechanisms of AD‐MSCs in this study may be attributed to the differentiation of AD‐MSCs into cardiomyocytes, cytokine‐related paracrine effects, which in turn lead to cardiac regeneration and reduced toxicity of DOX [82]. However, AD‐MSCs combined with resveratrol showed the best numerical hemodynamic values and largely normalized myocardial histology, while resveratrol alone displayed only moderate protection. Importantly, the difference between AD‐MSCs alone and the combination did not reach statistical significance for hemodynamic parameters, revealing that the added value of resveratrol may need larger sample sizes. Notwithstanding that, the histopathological superiority of the combination approach can raise an important translational question: could adjunctive therapy by resveratrol promote stem cell engraftment or paracrine activity? This project also accentuates that resveratrol alone is not sufficiently efficient to fully reverse DIC [82].

One of the pathogenic events in DIC is ferroptosis, which is a non‐apoptotic cell death along with iron‐dependent accumulations of lethal lipid reactive oxygen species (ROS) [83]. In a recent investigation, Yu and colleagues found that hypoxic human umbilical cord MSCs‐derived exosomes (Hypo HucMSCs‐Exo) suppress ferroptosis via activating mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) signaling, which in turn decreases MDA and iron levels [84]. This research team also compared normoxic versus hypoxic human umbilical cord MSC‑derived exosomes (HucMSCs‑Exo) in a mouse model of acute DIC and indicated that normoxic exosomes had a trend toward promoting cardiac function; however, only hypoxic HucMSCs‐Exo considerably decreased mortality rate, enhanced LVEF/LVFS, and inhibited classical ferroptosis landmarks (i.e., MDA, iron, Ptgs2 mRNA) while modifying the protein levels of GSH and GPX4 [84]. Exosomes from other sources, like trophoblast and cardiac stem cells, have also revealed their cardioprotective role in DIC through downregulation of YAP signaling and cardiac fibrosis and induction of apoptosis [80, 85]. An interesting result obtained from Yu et al. is the engagement of the mTORC1‑SLC7A11‑GPX4 axis. DOX repressed mTORC1 function in cardiomyocytes, and Trx1 recovered mTORC1‑related phosphorylation of S6K and 4EBP1. The authors further uncovered that SLC7A11, a cystine transporter, is required for GPX4 protein synthesis downstream of mTORC1 [1]. the acute DOX model (4 days) does not reflect the chronic cardiotoxicity observed in cancer survivors and its long‐term efficacy is unknown [2]; only Trx1 was inspected, while hypoxic exosomes comprise multiple bioactive agents that may participate [3]; the mechanism by which mTORC1 activation diminishes iron overload (independent of GPX4) remains elusive; and [4] the lack of anti‑apoptotic influences in this model may not be usable in other DIC settings. Hence, the next research should compare ferroptosis compared with apoptosis suppression among diverse models and MSC sources [84].

Santos et al. harnessed fully differentiated mouse embryonic stem cell‑derived cardiomyocytes (CM‑mESCs)—a novel strategy compared to the more common utilization of undifferentiated stem cells or MSCs in DIC research. In mice with induced DIC, a single injection of CM‑mESCs intramyocardially significantly promoted cardiac function (i.e., ejection fraction, stroke volume, end‑systolic volume) and normalized QT/QTc intervals for at least 30 days. Although transplanted cells were detectable for only 11 days, the sustained advantages offered a paracrine mechanism. Proteomic assay of CM‑mESC‑conditioned medium also pointed out 92 released proteins with angiogenic, anti‑apoptotic, and antioxidant features, pertaining to considerable diminution of apoptotic cardiomyocytes [86]. Despite favorable outcomes, the mentioned study has several limitations. For example, the administration of mouse embryonic stem cells can raise ethical problems and carries a risk of teratoma formation, especially since about 20% of the transplanted cell population contained non‑cardiomyocytes. Also, while cardiac parameters were enhanced, the study did not observe a decrease in myocardial fibrosis—a result that contrasts with many MSC‑based DIC studies—recommending that CM‑mESCs may serve mainly through anti‑apoptotic characteristics without addressing established collagen content.

Other researchers have expressed the beneficial role of BM‐MSCs and AD‐MSCs in the treatment of cardiotoxicity caused by chemotherapeutic agents such as DOX and Daunorubicin. These agents act via inhibiting inflammation‐associated cell death, immune cell infiltration, collagen deposition, and T lymphocytes [87, 88, 89]. However, there are remarkable differences in the experimental design of these studies that hamper direct comparison and limit generalization. Chen and co‐workers utilized daunorubicin (a less common anthracycline) in a prophylactic regimen in which MSCs were administered prior to each drug dose, whereas Ammar et al. and Tian et al. used a therapeutic or co‑treatment protocol. Also, Ammar et al. worked with a diabetic rat model—a clinically pertinent comorbidity—and discovered that both BM‐MSCs and AD‐MSCs were equally effective; however, their study used a relatively low cumulative DOX dose (2.5 mg/kg × 6) and a single MSC injection, which may have underrated the required cell dose. On the contrary, Tian and colleagues particularly concentrated on exosomal lncRNAs, providing mechanistic insight but lacking a comparison with whole MSCs. A prevalent limitation among all three studies is the short observation period (≤ 4 weeks) and the use of acute or subacute DIC models, which do not recapitulate the late‑onset cardiomyopathy observed in cancer survivors. Moreover, none of these studies assessed whether the advantages are maintained following MSC clearance or whether repeated MSC administrations are needed [87, 88, 89].

Recently, hopeful news has been reported regarding 3D bioengineering‐related methods, for example, cardiac organoids and heart‑on‑a‑chip technologies, which can promise to modify both disease modeling and stem cell‑based therapies for DIC [106]. However, more experimental research is still required to warrant such findings.

Challenges and Future Perspectives

Although stem cell therapy has been introduced as a pioneering therapeutic approach for diverse pathological and non‐pathological conditions, like DIC, it faces some challenges and difficulties in reality.

In the current medical era, stem cells can be regarded as a double‐edged sword against diverse abnormal conditions, particularly malignancies. In light of their potential for proliferation and self‐renewal, they can elevate the risk of tumor formations, especially for pluripotent stem cells (i.e., iPSCs and ESCs). They can undergo epigenetic and genetic abnormalities during culture that are linked with cancer. Although MSCs are generally considered to have a lower risk of malignant transformation, some studies have reported that they can enhance tumor growth or resistance under specific conditions [107, 108]. As an example, it has been addressed that MSCs may promote tumor cell resistance to DOX [109]. Another investigation on human and animals with metastasis has pointed out that some populations of MSCs may potentiate tumor growth, indicating a tumorigenic capacity for these sources of stem cells [110].

Another point is that after stem cell transplantation into the injured tissue, transplanted cells face a harsh milieu with stimuli related to cell death, like ROS and hypoxia, leading to poor long‐term engraftment and survival of transplanted cells [111]. These transplanted cells can themself cause a life‐threatening arrhythmia, which is a main safety worry for them. The mechanisms explaining this occurrence include the immature electrical features of transplanted cardiomyocytes, their insufficient electrical coupling with the host myocardium, and the induction of cardiac nerve sprouting [112]. Allogeneic MSCs can stimulate an immune reaction that gives rise to their rejection and diminishes their therapeutic effectiveness. The immune microenvironment in the recipient of such cells may polarize MSCs into an inflammatory phenotype [113]. In the end, the heterogeneity and lack of a standardized method for preparation of these cells make it difficult to compare outcomes from different studies and clinical translation. There are differences in selected cell sources, donor health status, isolation techniques, and culture situations. This heterogeneity contributes to inconsistency and mainly non‐reproducible findings [114]. These challenges have drawn researchers to find alternative options with higher safety and lower risk of immune rejection and complications.

A growing body of evidence recommends that the therapeutic effects of stem cells are largely mediated by their secretome (conditioned medium), a compilation of soluble growth factors, extracellular vesicles (EVs), and cytokines [115, 116]. Several studies have assessed the curative potential of CM derived from diverse sources of stem cells against cardiac disorders and heralded hopeful results [116, 117]. Also, EVs, particularly exosomes, are assumed to be the key actors of the paracrine impacts [118]. EVs can freely cross physiological obstacles and are loaded with different cargos, for instance, nucleic acids, proteins, and soluble factors, through a highly managed sorting process [119]. MSC‐originated exosomes suggest a modified “cell‐free” therapy, and their robust cardioprotective role has been demonstrated in vivo [120].

In addition to cell‐free products, there are other pioneering technologies that may reshape the outlook of DIC treatment, for example, 3D Bioprinting and cardiac organoids are known techniques revolutionizing drug testing with promise for personalized regenerative therapy [121]. Moreover, advanced gene‐editing platforms (e.g., CRISPR‐Cas9) and miRNA‐engineered therapies have recently attracted the attention of scientists to improve the efficacy of drugs like stem cells [122, 123].

The Translational Gap: From Animal Models to Human Patients

One pressing problem with the results of the current research is that they were almost conducted on small‑animal experiments—mostly young, healthy rodents and rabbits that are genetically very similar. Although these models have provided valuable mechanistic insights, they do not completely address the clinical profile of cancer cases who are candidates for DOX therapy. Patients with cancer are typically older, often present with comorbidities (e.g., diabetes, prior cardiac disease, and hypertension), and may be immunocompromised due to their malignancy or prior therapies [124, 125]. These factors remarkably affect the pharmacokinetics of DOX, the baseline cardiac reserve, and the response to stem cell therapy–factors that are rarely incorporated into animal studies. Ergo, while the preclinical evidence for stem cell therapy in DIC is hopeful, it must be interpreted with caution. Future translational research should have more attention to large‑animal models with pertinent comorbidities, patient‑derived xenograft or iPSC platforms, and, finally, well‑designed clinical investigations that consider the heterogeneity of the cancer population.

Conclusions

Although DOX possesses a massive therapeutic potential as a chemotherapeutic agent, serious organ toxicities such as cardiotoxicity, neurotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, and hepatotoxicity have limited its clinical administration. Cardiotoxicity is considered one of the most common life‐threatening adverse effects of this medication. The underlying mechanisms that have been introduced are the formation of ROS, mitochondrial impairment, Ca2+ homeostasis dysregulation, intracellular iron accumulation, and cell death‐related processes. Recently, the stem cells derived from different sources such as bone marrow, adipose tissue, umbilical cord, embryo, and myocytes have been demonstrated to counteract the chemotherapy‐induced complications.

The current preclinical evidence has revealed that among stem cell types, MSCs (particularly BM‐MSCs, AD‐MSCs, and UC‐MSCs) can ameliorate DIC through different mechanisms such as attenuating pyroptosis, ferroptosis, and oxidative stress, minimizing immune cell infiltration, T lymphocytes, iron levels, and pro‐inflammatory mediators (e.g., TNF‐α, IL‐12, and Fas ligand), and suppressing collagen fiber deposition. They also enhance angiogenesis and anti‐inflammatory mediators (i.e., IL‐4, IL‐9, and IL‐13). However, these results are at the level of experimental data, and clinical trials are warranted to validate these findings.
