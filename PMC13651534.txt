Enhancing the Stability
of Pt/Carbon with Ti3C2 MXene-Derived TiO2 for the Oxygen Reduction
Reaction in Acidic Media

Mehmet Turan Görüryilmaz, Bastian Schmiedecke, Aline Alencar Emerenciano, Iris Dorbandt, Karuppasamy Dharmaraj, Thorsten Schultz, Xuyun Guo, Bing Wu, Jiri Sturala, Namrata Sharma, Michael Haumann, Christian Göllner, Karl J. J. Mayrhofer, Zdenek Sofer, Norbert Koch, Valeria Nicolosi, Tristan Petit, Michelle P. Browne

Abstract

Platinum on carbon remains the proton-exchange membrane
fuel cell
cathode state-of-the-art catalyst; however, durability losses tied
to support corrosion and Pt coarsening hinder this catalyst. This
study investigates utilizing different variations of Vulcan XC-72
and Ti3C2 MXene to improve the oxygen-reduction
ORR activity and stability. Material characterization results provide
in-depth knowledge of the Pt, carbon, and MXene composites. X-ray
photoelectron spectroscopy of the synthesized samples shows that the
Ti3C2 has been oxidized, and high-resolution
transmission electron microscopy further reveals that the Pt(111)
fringes are present with local anatase TiO2 derived from
Ti3C2 MXene, which is also confirmed by X-ray
microscopy, indicating that the active support is a Ti3C2-derived TiO2/carbon support. After a U.S.
Department of Energy-derived accelerated stress test (AST), the Pt/75%C+25%Ti3C2 catalyst outperforms an in-house-made Pt/C prepared
by the same method. This improvement is accompanied by reduced Pt
particle growth after the AST for the Pt/75%C+25%Ti3C2 when compared to the Pt/C, which is attributed to stabilization
of Pt at the MXene-derived anatase surface through Pt–O–Ti
bonds, consistent with interfacial anchoring, together with the greater
corrosion resistance of the oxide, in a composition that retains sufficient
carbon for electronic percolation and gas transport. This study is
the first coupled cathode half-cell performance and potential-cycling
durability testing for MXene-derived composites benchmarked against
Pt/C and provides vital information on material discovery utilizing
MXenes for the ORR.

Introduction

Proton-exchange membrane fuel cells (PEMFCs)
rely on platinum (Pt)
as an electrocatalyst at the cathode due to the high activity toward
the slow oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) kinetics, which can operate
with stability under harsh acidic conditions.
,
 ORR
overpotential on Pt comes from the binding energy compromise across
intermediates, which means that tuning the electronic structure of
the Pt is more important than changing the mechanism.

Commercially, Pt/C is the industry standard as it
is the best-performing
ORR material and has many advantages such as carbon’s conductivity
and low cost, as well as its ability to disperse Pt well.
,
 However, Pt/C loses surface area and activity over time due to carbon
corrosion at high cathode potentials or local fuel starvation.
 Pt also dissolves
and ripens under cycling, which further lowers the electrochemically
active surface area (ECSA) and shifts size distributions. These issues make the support the weak link for
long-term durability, which means that a better support type is crucial
for a longer lifetime. The support type can influence Pt’s
specific activity even without changing its particle size or shape
by modifying the Pt–support interactions, through the alteration
of Pt’s d-band center and oxophilicity. Because of this, the support chemistry and surface states matter
as much as the Pt compositions and morphology.

MXenes are atomically
thin layers of transition metal carbides,
nitrides, or carbonitrides that are synthesized from their MAX-phase
precursor. They are represented by the general formula M
n+1AX
n
, where M is an
earlier transition metal, A is usually a group 13 or 14 element, and
X represents carbon or nitrogen. MXenes combine an attractive surface
and compositional area ideal to boost synergetic effects with other
elements. In particular, Ti3C2TX is
an attractive support due to its conductivity, hydrophilicity, and
well-tunable surface terminations represented by TX in
its chemical formula M
n+1X
n
TX (−O, −OH, −F,
etc.), which can be tailored for optimized metal–support interactions
and interfacial wetting.

 Ti3C2TX can be produced with controlled etching, delamination,
and surface chemistry, which allows balancing conductivity, surface
oxygen content, the number of layers, and flake size for catalyst
inks for energy conversion applications. However, bare Ti3C2TX can oxidize
under anodic polarization in acid. It has been shown that partial
substitution of Ti with tantalum (Ta) in the transition metal (M)
layer of the MXene shifts the onset of oxidation to more anodic potentials,
extending the potential window over which Ti3C2TX remains stable above 0 V vs the reversible hydrogen
electrode.

Beyond electrochemical
conditions, Ti3C2TX is also chemically
and thermally sensitive. In the presence
of an oxidant, it converts to TiO2 and carbon, and this
oxidation is strongly accelerated at elevated temperatures.
,
 Hydrothermal treatment of Ti3C2 in water even
at moderate temperatures yields anatase TiO2 together with
disordered carbon. The resulting Ti3C2-derived TiOX/carbon material is closely
related to the TiO2-carbon supports widely studied for
the ORR, where oxide domains improve cathode durability.
,
 A further oxidation route arises from the reducing character of
Ti3C2 itself, which can donate electrons to
reducible metal ion species and become oxidized in the process.

Another important consideration when evaluating
any new ORR catalyst
is benchmarking the catalyst under protocols comparable to those of
the industry. The rotating disk electrode (RDE) method is sensitive
to ink formulation, film thickness, Pt loading on the glassy carbon
(GC) disk, potential scales, and mass transport corrections.
,
 Inconsistent protocols can give multifold differences for the same
Pt/C-coated electrode, so reporting and benchmarking must follow strict
procedures,
 However, even good and consistent RDE metrics do not translate into
membrane-electrode assemblies (MEAs). Therefore, half-cell methods
that operate at higher current density and realistic gas transport,
including gas diffusion electrode (GDE) testing, help bridge the gap
to device conditions.

Furthermore,
accelerated stress tests (ASTs) are essential because
they compress the thousands of operating hours required to evaluate
proton-exchange membrane fuel cell (PEMFC) durability into practical
laboratory protocols, enabling rapid, mechanism-specific screening
of materials and MEAs and standardized benchmarking across programs.
Performance decline is quantified by metrics such as mass activity
loss and support stability, providing go/no-go criteria and comparability
across laboratories. According to the
DoE, it is important to reach the goal of less than 10% performance
loss at the end of 5000 h by 2030, ultimately 30,000 h by 2050.

Zhang et al. dispersed Pt on accordion-like
Ti3C2 (20 wt % Pt) and, in 0.5 M H2SO4, reported an improved half-wave potential compared
to Pt/C in an RDE cell, with an acidic current retention of 89.9–93.6%
after 1500 cycles against 70.6–85.6% for Pt/C and notably smaller
Pt coarsening (up to 5.49 nm vs 8.86 nm for Pt/C). Xu et al. self-assembled a CNT-Ti3C2 hybrid support for Pt and achieved roughly 3.4 times higher mass
activity than that of Pt/C with much lower ECSA loss (6%) after 2000
cycles in the RDE compared to Pt/C (27%). Zhang et al. designed a sandwich-structured Ti3C2/carbon support for Pt that limited MXene restacking
and delivered higher ORR activity, only 1% loss with the composite
in half-wave potential compared to 16 mV for Pt/C, and stability tests
showed 3.05% loss in mass activity for the composite compared with
36.8% for Pt/C in the RDE. These results suggest that mixing carbon
and Ti3C2 in one catalyst layer can couple the
strengths of each material: carbon’s percolation and low density
and MXene’s strong metal–support interaction (SMSI)
and surface functionality. Notably, all prior Pt/MXene- and Pt/TiO2-based durability data were obtained in RDE or MEA configurations;
coupled half-cell (GDE) activity and potential-cycling durability
for a Ti3C2-derived composite have not been
reported, which allows for more realistic results closer to a fuel
cell device.

Parallel improvements have been reported for Pt
supported on TiO2- and TiOX-carbon composites,
where the benefit
is consistently traced to strong metal–support interaction
(SMSI). Liu et al. showed that adding
20 mass % TiO2 to Pt/C raised the ORR kinetic current density
by 20.5% and halved the degradation rate after SO2 poisoning
relative to Pt/C, attributing the gain to the Pt–TiO2 interaction and the abundant hydroxyl groups on the hydrophilic,
electrochemically stable oxide. Chen et al. engineered a TiOX/C support with abundant oxygen vacancies;
the vacancies were found to anchor highly dispersed Pt nanoparticles
and to strengthen the SMSI through electron transfer between Pt and
TiOX, yielding electron-rich Pt, enhanced ORR activity,
and markedly higher activity retention after accelerated durability
testing, whereas Pt/C decayed sharply. Pham et al. conducted operando near-ambient pressure X-ray photoelectron
spectroscopy (XPS) on Pt/TiOX/C and provided direct evidence
of this effect, showing that the partially reduced TiOX layer in the SMSI contact with Pt strongly suppresses Pt oxidation
at high potentials, with the residual oxidation originating only from
Pt deposited on carbon rather than on TiOX. At the support
level, Ayyubov et al. demonstrated that
mixed Ti(1–X)MoXO2–carbon
composites deliver higher Pt stability than that of plain carbon,
since the electrocorrosion-resistant TiO2 skeleton suppresses
support and dopant dissolution. Across these studies, the recurring
theme is that a TiO2/TiOX phase in intimate
contact with Pt anchors the particles, modulates their electronic
structure through SMSI, and resists carbon-type corrosion, thereby
improving durability.

Beyond charge transfer, TiO2 can stabilize supported
Pt by binding it more strongly at under-coordinated surface sites:
density functional calculations give per-atom Pt binding energies
on anatase that rise from 2.87 eV on the stoichiometric (101) surface
to 5.05 eV at reduced, under-coordinated surface sites, so that Pt
nucleates preferentially at these sites and resists migration and
coalescence. Scanning-tunneling and first-principles
studies of anatase (101) confirm that Pt monomers bind at terrace,
step, and defect sites strongly enough to suppress sintering. The oxide additionally protects the carbon support:
at the 1.0–1.5 V transient potentials generated by fuel starvation
and start–stop events, carbon corrodes rapidly, whereas a TiO2 interlayer roughly
halves the electrochemical surface area loss of Pt/C under high-potential
cycling by covering reactive carbon sites and limiting their contact
with water. This protection is possible
because TiO2 is thermodynamically passive across a wide
potential window in acid and far more corrosion-resistant than carbon
at these potentials. Finally, the polymorph
that forms matters: anatase has a markedly lower surface energy than
that of rutile (0.4 versus 2.2 J m–2) and is the
thermodynamically stable phase for TiO2 crystallites below
∼14 nm, which is why moderate-temperature oxidation of Ti3C2 yields predominantly nanoscale anatase. It should be noted, however, that such geometric
and electronic contributions are not cleanly separable. They are generally
understood to act together, as different manifestations of the same
underlying metal–support interaction, and a recent critical
review cautions that the various MSI subclassifications are difficult
to assign and can be misleading in practice.

Despite these promising results, three gaps remain in MXene-based
composites for fuel cells. First, the carbon-to-Ti3C2 ratio has not been varied systematically to map the trade-off
between carbon’s conductivity, percolation, and oxidation-prone
but Pt-anchoring chemistry of Ti3C2 in realistic
conditions. Second, the consequences of Ti3C2 oxidation for the ORR activity and durability are rarely quantified.
Third, durability for these supports has been assessed almost exclusively
by the RDE rather than under the higher current, realistic mass transport
conditions of a half-cell. In this study, Pt/C, Pt/Ti3C2, and three Pt/C+Ti3C2 compositions
are synthesized and benchmarked under a DoE AST-based protocol in
GDL flow setups to address both support effects relevant for the industry
and allow for protocol-robust benchmarking of the MXene-based composites
compared to the in-house-made Pt/C.

Results and Discussion

PEMFCs suffer from slow ORR
kinetics and the instability of the
Pt/C electrocatalyst. Improving performance and, more importantly,
the long-term stability of the catalysts can facilitate the progress
toward widespread industrial applications. We present our material
characterization and electrochemical evaluation results on the synthesized
Pt electrocatalyst with carbon, Ti3C2, or C/Ti3C2 at different ratios as support structures.

The synthesis of Pt on different support types was achieved via
facile solvothermal synthesis, as given in a. Before the Pt electrocatalyst synthesis,
Ti3C2 MXene was prepared by etching of the MAX
phase (Ti3AlC2) to remove the Al layer and obtain
multilayered Ti3C2. After that, multilayered
Ti3C2 was sonicated in a bath, leading to delaminated
Ti3C2 flakes. Pt/C+Ti3C2 composites were synthesized using different ratios of delaminated
Ti3C2 and carbon at 180 °C for 8 h. Pt/C
and Pt/Ti3C2 were also synthesized for comparison.
The details of the synthesis route are given in the experimental section.

clusters increases with the increasing amount of Ti3C2 in the sample. The smaller, individual Pt nanoparticles can
be seen as blurry white dots in the images due to their small size,

Bright-field transmission electron microscopy (TEM) shows Pt nanoparticles
uniformly distributed on the support, with no large agglomerates (). High-resolution
TEM (HRTEM) images resolve lattice fringes that were indexed to fcc
Pt (111); this assignment to fcc Pt (111) matches the Pt (111) XRD
reflection at ∼39.8°. At selected areas of the support,
localized fringes consistent with anatase TiO2 (101) appear
at the surface; Ti 2p XPS also indicates oxidation to anatase TiO2.

Bright-field TEM confirms that Pt nanoparticles
are deposited on
the supports in all compositions and are predominantly present as
discrete particles across the imaged regions. In Ti3C2-containing catalysts, the support retains a flake-like, sheet
morphology characteristic of MXene, and STEM elemental maps show Pt
colocated with the support framework: Pt overlaps with carbon-rich
domains in Pt/C and with Ti-containing regions in the composites,
supporting successful Pt deposition throughout the mixed C-Ti3C2 architecture. Pt nanoparticles are uniformly
distributed on the Pt/C sample, with a size distribution centered
in the few-nanometer range (mean: 5.3 nm), with most particles lying
between 2 and 6 nm (∼68%). SAED patterns exhibit ring reflections
that can be indexed to fcc Pt (111), (200), (220), and (311), confirming
crystalline metallic Pt.

Pt remains finely dispersed on the
mixed support for Pt/75%C+25%Ti3C2 (mean: 5.2
nm), with the majority of particles
in the 2–6 nm window (∼75%). SAED shows fcc Pt rings
at (111), (200), (220), and (311). In addition, SAED reveals rings
attributable to anatase TiO2 at (101), (004), (105), and
(204), indicating that Ti3C2 layers turned into
an anatase-like oxide component. Pt particle size distribution remains
largely within the few-nanometer regime for Pt/50%C+50%Ti3C2 (mean: 5.2 nm), with ∼72% of particles between
2 and 6 nm. SAED again confirms fcc Pt via (111), (200), (220), and
(311) rings and shows the anatase TiO2 ring set ((101),
(004), (105), (204)) in the Ti3C2-containing
support, consistent with oxidation of the MXene.

Increasing
the Ti3C2 fraction broadens the
Pt size distribution and shifts the mean to a slightly larger value
for Pt/25%C+75%Ti3C2 (mean: 5.7 nm), with ∼66%
of particles between 2 and 6 nm and a more visible large-size tail
(∼6% above 10 nm). SAED patterns display the same fcc Pt ring
sequence ((111), (200), (220), (311)) and, for the Ti3C2-containing support, anatase TiO2 rings ((101),
(004), (105), (204)), indicating that Pt retains the same crystalline
phase, while the support includes an anatase-like oxide signature.

Pt/Ti3C2 shows the smallest mean size among
the series (mean: 4.8 nm), with most particles between 2 and 6 nm
(∼83%) and only a small fraction above 10 nm (∼3%).
SAED confirms fcc Pt via (111), (200), (220), and (311) rings, while
anatase TiO2 rings at (101), (004), (105), and (204) are
also observed, demonstrating that Ti3C2-containing
samples consistently exhibit an anatase-like oxide contribution. Together
with HRTEM observations of Pt (111) lattice fringes and localized
anatase TiO2 (101) fringes at the support, these diffraction
results indicate that Pt crystallizes as fcc nanoparticles in all
samples, whereas Ti3C2-based supports exhibit
oxidation to anatase TiO2 without evidence for a new Pt
phase introduced by MXene addition.

Overall, the microscopy
and diffraction data show that changing
the carbon-to-Ti3C2 ratio primarily influences
the Pt particle size distribution and dispersion uniformity, while
the Pt crystalline structure remains fcc across all catalysts. The
consistent appearance of anatase TiO2 rings in all Ti3C2-containing samples supports a common structural
feature of these composites: Ti3C2-based flakes
persist in the catalyst layer but with an anatase-like oxide component
detectable by electron diffraction and local HRTEM indexing.

ADF-STEM and the corresponding elemental maps () were used to evaluate nanoscale
element distribution in the catalyst particles and to verify how the
support composition evolves across the C+Ti3C2 series. In contrast to SEM-EDX, ADF-STEM EDX and EELS maps indicate
a homogeneous Pt nanoparticle distribution in all imaged regions for
every sample, including Pt/Ti3C2, with no evidence
of Pt-rich “islands” at the mapping length scale. Across
the composite series, the relative carbon signal decreases as Ti3C2 content increases, while the Ti signal increases
accordingly. Oxygen is consistently colocated with Ti and strengthens
with increasing Ti3C2 fraction, consistent with
progressive oxidation associated with the Ti-containing support domains.

Pt and C maps overlap broadly across the carbon
support for Pt/C,
indicating uniform Pt dispersion on carbon within the analyzed areas;
no Ti or O signal is expected or observed. Pt is uniformly distributed
across the mixed support for Pt/75% C+25% Ti3C2. The Ti map confirms the presence of Ti3C2 domains, while the oxygen signal remains comparatively faint on
Ti, relative to other samples. This suppressed O intensity suggests
that carbon-rich coverage in this composition partially masks or limits
the detectable oxidation of Ti-containing regions, consistent with
carbon providing a shielding effect.

Pt remains homogeneously
dispersed across the support for Pt/50%C+50%Ti3C2. Ti and O signals are stronger than those in
Pt/75%C+25%Ti3C2, and oxygen closely follows
the Ti distribution, indicating more prevalent oxidized Ti-containing
regions as the Ti3C2 fraction increases. Pt
mapping still shows uniform dispersion, while the C signal becomes
noticeably weaker compared to the carbon-richer composites for Pt/25%C+75%Ti3C2. Ti and colocated O signals are prominent, indicating
that Ti-containing (and partially oxidized) support regions dominate
the mapped areas at this composition. Pt nanoparticles are also uniformly
distributed on the Ti-based support at the nanoscale for Pt/Ti3C2. The carbon signal is minimal as expected. Ti
is strongest in this sample, and oxygen matches Ti spatially, indicating
an extensive Ti-associated oxygen signal consistent with an oxidized
component of the Ti3C2-derived support.

Overall, ADF-STEM and elemental mapping results support that Pt
dispersion is homogeneous at the nanoscale for all catalysts (including
Pt/Ti3C2), the C and Ti contrast tracks the
designed support ratios, and oxygen preferentially follows Ti-rich
regions, with the comparatively weaker O signal in Pt/75%C+25%Ti3C2 suggesting partial carbon coverage that mitigates
detectable Ti oxidation in that composition.

XPS was used to
determine the atomic concentration and the oxidation
states of the samples. The Ti 2p, C 1s, and Pt 4f core levels are
shown in a.
Platinum is present purely as metal for all samples, as indicated
by two sharp, asymmetric peaks located at 71.2 and 74.5 eV, without
any significant changes in the spectra for different amounts of Ti3C2 added. While the pristine Ti3C2 is of excellent quality (no signs of oxidation in the Ti
2p spectrum, a strong Ti–C peak at ∼282 eV in the C
1s spectrum), it is completely oxidized for all mixed samples. The
oxidation of the MXene to TiO2 could result from two scenarios
during the synthesis. The first scenario is due to the reaction conditions
during the solvothermal process. The second is that the MXene itself
acts as a reducing agent for the reduction of the Pt precursor to
the Pt metal. The reduction of Pt4+ to metallic Pt is a stepwise redox process (Pt­(IV) →
Pt­(II) → Pt(0)) that necessarily oxidizes an electron donor;
in conventional colloidal syntheses, this role is filled by added
reductants or by electron-rich solids and polymers such as cellulose,
which reduce the metal ions while being oxidized themselves.

 In the absence of a dedicated reductant, the electron-rich
Ti3C2 surface can serve this function, donating
electrons to the precursor and being oxidized to TiOX in
the process, making an oxygen-rich external atmosphere not required.
Although the synthesis is carried out under in an N2-saturated
DMF/ethylene glycol medium, this precursor-driven redox proceeds even
in an inert environment (although the vessel was sealed in air), while
residual water in the hygroscopic solvents can additionally oxidize
the carbide as a reagent. The 180 °C
solvothermal conditions accelerate both pathways.

The Ti/Pt ratio, shown as the inset in a (iii), increases
linearly with the added
amount of Ti3C2. We further noticed an increase
in intensity in the C 1s spectrum around 292 eV with increasing Ti3C2 content, which cannot be attributed to oxidation
alone (typically in the range of 286–290 eV). C–FX bonds, typically observed at this energy, can be ruled out
too, as no fluorine was observed for these samples. Peaks at this
energy are often assigned to surface plasmon losses.
,
 The interaction of platinum with the Ti3C2 seems to introduce some sort of energy loss that increases linearly
with the Ti/Pt ratio until it saturates at large Ti3C2 content, as shown in the inset in a (ii). The origin of this loss is not clear
at the moment, but it correlates with the decrease in performance
for larger amounts of Ti3C2. Inset plots are
also given in Figure SI4 on a larger scale.

Across the composition series, the Pt L3-edge XANES
(b,c) shows
only small variations in the edge position and white line intensity
that do not trend with Ti3C2-derived TiO2 content, and Pt supported on pure Ti3C2-derived TiO2 is spectroscopically indistinguishable from
Pt on carbon; the EXAFS (d) is dominated by metallic Pt–Pt scattering,
within which any minor Pt–Ti contribution is not separately
resolvable, and the Pt 4f binding energy is almost invariant in XPS
(−0.08 eV shift for only C+MXene mixed samples). These small
spectral differences reflect Pt nanoparticle size and a slight degree
of surface oxidation common to all supports, rather than metal-to-support
charge transfer, so an electronic metal–support interaction
between the Pt and Ti can be excluded. This does not exclude an interfacial
Pt–support bond. Metal–support interactions encompass
structural and enthalpic effects as well as electronic ones, and the data are consistent with an interfacial
anchoring interaction in which Pt is bound at the anatase surface
through Pt–O–Ti bonds without charge transfer to or
from the metal. Consistent with this, high-resolution MAADF-STEM shows
Pt NPs anchored on TiO2 nanoflakes (Figure SI5). From the literature, this has been shown by that
the DFT-calculated Pt adhesion energy is nearly doubled relative to
the ideal surface, pinning the particles against migration and Ostwald
ripening without altering their oxidation state.
,
 Also, bare TiO2 conducts electrons poorly, so too much
TiO2 (or a thick oxide shell) can limit charge transfer
and lower accessible Pt area unless carbon pathways are present. Previously reported Pt/TiO2 cathodes
show the advantage of TiO2 for the ORR. Liu et al. showed
that Pt/TiO2/C keeps ∼76% of its initial ECSA after
an accelerated aging test, while Pt/C falls to ∼43%, and Pt
growth is far less severe on TiO2 than on carbon. Pt/TiO2 activity and stability also
depend on oxide particle size and Pt loading; for example, 12 wt %
Pt on 30 nm TiO2 showed the highest ORR activity and only
∼5.9% ECSA loss after accelerated stress test. Composites that add conductive carbon around TiO2 recover percolation and boost performance while keeping the stabilization
benefit of the oxide.

These trends match our observations. HRTEM shows
localized fringes
consistent with anatase TiO2(101) on the Ti3C2-derived support, and SAED, Ti L-edge XANES, and Ti
2p XPS together confirm a fully oxidized Ti­(IV) anatase phase. Because anatase is a corrosion-resistant, nanoscale-stable
polymorph, it can survive the potentials that can degrade the support
material, and by covering reactive carbon domains, it can slow their
oxidation.
,
 Overall, a thin, discontinuous
anatase layer on Ti3C2 acts as an anchoring
and stabilization motif for Pt, where an overly continuous oxide skin
would penalize conductivity; carbon + Ti3C2 composites
appear to offer the best balance for the PEM ORR.

The Ti L3 and L2 peaks correspond to the transition
from Ti 2p3/2 and 2p1/2 orbitals to the Ti 3d
orbital, respectively. In an octahedral ligand field, the Ti 3d orbitals
split into a lower energy 3-fold-degenerate t2g set (d

, d

) and a higher energy 2-fold-degenerate
eg set (d

2), with the energy separation between them defining 10Dq. Peak positions were determined by locating the
local intensity maxima in the first derivative, and 10Dq was calculated
as the simple energy difference: 10Dq = E­(L3 eg) – E­(L3 t2g). This approach is well-established
for Ti L-edge spectroscopy. The two prominent
peaks in the L3 manifold correspond directly to transitions
from the Ti 2p3/2 core level to the unoccupied 3d t2g and eg states. The magnitude of their separation
reflects the strength of the local crystal field imposed by the surrounding
ligands. The L3 t2g (457.50 eV) is shifted to
lower energy relative to Pt/75%C+25%Ti3C2 (457.75
eV), and the L3 eg at 459.25 eV shows no splitting,
yielding a reduced 10Dq of 1.75 eV consistent with the weaker ligand
field of the mixed carbide/surface-termination environment and lower
oxidation state of titanium. In the L2 region, the t2g at 463.0 eV is well-resolved, but the eg manifests
only as a weak shoulder around 464.5 eV, indicating significant broadening
and suppression of the octahedral orbital character.

The Pt/75%C+25%Ti3C2 hybrid exhibits much
more prominent splitting for both L2 and L3 edges
with a shoulder to L3 eg peak showing spectral
features close to the TiO2 anatase phase. The oxidation state of Ti for pristine Ti3C2 and Pt/75%C+25%Ti3C2 could also be
directly compared by estimating their L2eg/t2g peak intensity ratio. Ti has
an estimated oxidation of 3.78 for Pt/75%C+25%Ti3C2 compared to 2.59 for pristine Ti3C2, corresponding to their L2eg/t2g peak intensity ratio of 3.78 and 0.79, respectively (Figure SI6c). This suggests that Ti in Pt/75%C+25%Ti3C2 is significantly more oxidized, approaching
+4, than the pristine one.

The O K-edge corroborates this picture:
the main absorption peak,
reflecting O 2p hybridization with Ti 3d states, shifts systematically
from 530.42 eV in Pt/75%C+25%Ti3C2 to 530.75
eV in Ti3C2, tracking the change in the Ti–O
bonding character from surface termination groups to the rutile phase. The intermediate position of Pt/75%C+25%Ti3C2 across all spectral markers (10Dq, eg splitting, L2eg resolution, and O K-edge)
collectively demonstrates that Pt decoration introduces localized
Ti oxidation having an oxidation state close to +4. The intermediate
oxidation state (+3.8 rather than +4) and the persistence of flake
morphology indicate that the Ti3C2-derived domains
are not fully converted to bulk anatase but remain as an almost completely
oxidized flake-bound TiO2 phase in electronic contact with
the carbon network.

The oxidation state and local coordination
of the Ti-containing

Freeze-dried Ti3C2 and a crystalline anatase
TiO2 standard were measured under identical conditions
as reduced- and oxidized-Ti references. The Ti3C2 reference shows the broad, poorly resolved L3 envelope
characteristic of carbidic Ti, in which the t2g and eg features are barely separated. In contrast, all Pt-containing
composites display the sharp four-feature multiplet of Ti4+ in an oxide environment, with the L3 t2g at
458.2 eV, the L3 eg centroid at 460.5 eV, and
the corresponding L2 features at 463.7 and 465.8 eV. These
energies coincide with those of the anatase reference (t2g 458.3 eV, eg 460.5 eV), and no carbidic contribution
is detectable in any composite, including the carbon-free Pt/Ti3C2 sample. Oxidation of the Ti3C2 precursor to anatase TiO2 is therefore complete
throughout the probed surface region, independent of support composition,
in agreement with the anatase phase identified by SAED, HRTEM, and
XPS. The L3 t2g position and eg centroid
are furthermore constant to within 0.08 and 0.05 eV across the full
composition series, showing that the Ti oxidation state and local
coordination do not change with Ti3C2 loading.
The composite spectra nevertheless differ from crystalline anatase
in the degree of multiplet definition. All samples show a higher eg/t2g intensity ratio (1.02–1.24) than that
of the anatase reference (0.89) together with a shallower valley between
the t2g and eg features, while the peak energies
remain unchanged. Because the eg/t2g ratio is
an intensity ratio, it is independent of energy calibration, and the
combination of matched peak positions with reduced multiplet resolution
is the signature of increased local structural disorder rather than
a different Ti phase or oxidation state. Taken with the crystalline
anatase observed by SAED, XAS, XPS, and XRD, these data indicate a
crystalline anatase core terminated by a structurally disordered surface
layer, a morphology that provides both a corrosion-resistant oxide
scaffold and the under-coordinated surface sites at which Pt is anchored
through Pt–O–Ti bonds. The reason for observing a completely
oxidized Ti (+4) in XPS and XAS compared to partially oxidized Ti
(+3.8) in STXM is the probing depth difference between the methods.
XAS and XPS are surface-sensitive measurements that probe about 10
nm depth, while STXM is a bulk measurement method that can reveal
information about the whole flake. STXM averages the fully oxidized
surface together with a less-oxidized, carbide-retaining core, yielding
a flake-averaged value (+3.8) below the +4 seen by the surface-sensitive
probes. The possible presence of carbon-bound Ti in the core of the
flake, however, can be neglected in terms of performance as the almost
completely oxidized flake (where completely oxidized Ti in the form
of TiO2 occurs at the surface) will behave like TiO2.

these results indicate that the Ti support remains largely intact

dominated by Pt particle growth rather than support loss. The total
Ti released during the accelerated stress test is small (1.6–2.0
μg per electrode), and multivalent-cation poisoning of perfluorosulfonic
acid ionomers scales with the concentration of mobile, exchangeable
cations, which for such sub-microgram quantities remains far below
the level associated with measurable proton conductivity loss. Unlike
strongly Fenton-active contaminants such as Fe3+, dissolved
Ti4+ hydrolyzes readily in acidic media and tends to reprecipitate
as insoluble hydrous TiO2 rather than persisting as a mobile
cation that can ion-exchange into the ionomer, which limits its capacity
to poison the membrane. This is consistent with our postmortem Ti
L-edge XANES and Ti 2p XPS, which show the Ti4+ oxide phase
to be invariant after cycling, indicating that any restructuring of
the titania occurs in place and retains Ti on the electrode rather
than releasing it. The contribution of dissolved Ti or TiO2 restructuring to ionomer or electrolyte contamination is expected
to be minor under the tested conditions, although direct confirmation
over extended operation would require long-term measurements in an
MEA configuration. Residual fluoride and etchant species from MXene
synthesis are expected to be minimal, as the Ti3C2 was washed to neutral pH prior to use and then oxidized into TiO2 during the catalyst synthesis; combined with the low absolute
Ti release measured here, any dissolved Ti or fluoride interaction
with the ionomer or electrolyte is expected to be negligible over
the tested protocol. Taken together, the suppressed Pt dissolution
and coarsening, the preserved carbon framework, and the invariant
Ti4+ oxide identify the durability gain of the MXene-containing
supports as a combination of Pt anchoring at the anatase surface and
the superior corrosion resistance of that oxide, rather than an electronic
metal–support interaction.

rank the extent of carbon structural change under the support AST
in the order of Pt/50%C+50%Ti3C2, followed by
Pt/C, and finally Pt/75%C+25%Ti3C2, in agreement
with the electrochemical durability results, where the Pt/50%C+50%Ti3C2 composition showed the largest high-current
performance loss and the Pt/75%C+25%Ti3C2 composition
showed the best retention.

Under the more aggressive support-corrosion AST
(1.0–1.5
V), dissolution of both Pt and Ti increases substantially compared
to the Pt AST, as expected for the higher potential protocol. Pt dissolution
reaches 17.4% of the initial Pt loading for the in-house Pt/C, the
highest of the series, whereas both composites lose considerably less,
10.3% for Pt/75%C+25%Ti3C2 and 13.2% for Pt/50%C+50%Ti3C2, and the commercial Pt/C loses 8.2%. The lower
Pt loss of the MXene-containing catalysts relative to the in-house
Pt/C, under precisely the conditions that drive support degradation,
indicates that the Ti3C2-derived oxide helps
retain Pt against dissolution. Ti release under this protocol is also
higher11.28 and 15.20 μg of Ti per mg of Ti in the support
(1.128% and 1.520% of the initial Ti) for Pt/75%C+25%Ti3C2 and Pt/50%C+50%Ti3C2, respectively;
the smaller fractional Ti loss of the 25% Ti3C2 composite indicates that the lower MXene support is the more chemically
robust of the two. In both tests, the fraction of Ti lost remains
below ∼1.5%, confirming that the oxide support is largely retained
even under high-potential cycling, consistent with the invariant postmortem
Ti 2p XPS and Ti L-edge spectra. The surface chemistry of the electrodes
after the support-degradation AST was examined by XPS (e). Despite the more oxidative
1.0–1.5 V window, the Ti 2p and Pt 4f core levels are essentially
unchanged between the pre- and postmortem states for all catalysts.
The Ti 2p region is described throughout by a single Ti4+ anatase doublet at 458.7–458.9 eV, with no Ti3+ or reduced/suboxide Ti component emerging after 30,000 cycles, indicating
that the MXene-derived anatase support is neither further oxidized
nor reduced under the support AST. Platinum remains fully metallic,
with the Pt 4f7/2 and 4f5/2 peaks retained at
71.2 and 74.5 eV, respectively, and no growth of an oxidized Pt species.
The atomic concentrations and Ti4+/Pt0 component
ratios likewise remain constant within error, showing no measurable
change in surface composition. Taken together with the invariant Ti
L-edge multiplet, these results establish that the support and the
Pt surface undergo no oxidation-state change even under the corrosive
support-degradation protocol; the durability differences between compositions
therefore arise from physical degradation, namely Pt crystallite growth
and carbon-framework restructuring evidenced by postmortem XRD, SEM,
and Raman, rather than from a chemical transformation of the support.
The chemical inertness of the anatase component under these conditions
is consistent with its role as a corrosion-resistant, stabilizing
support rather than an electronically active participant in the degradation.

XRD data were collected on carbon cloth electrodes before and after
30,000 AST cycles, and the sharp 18° peak was chosen for normalization
(a for the
Pt AST and b for the support AST). The patterns are dominated by the carbon
cloth and the sample holder below 30°; the catalyst-derived features
are the face-centered cubic Pt reflections at 39.8° (111), 46.3°
(200), 67.7° (220), and 81.4° (311), together with the carbon
(002) band at ∼25.8°. All electrodes retained the fcc
Pt structure after cycling. The principal postmortem change is a systematic
narrowing and intensification of the Pt reflections in every electrode,
indicating growth of the Pt crystallite size during potential cycling,
and this narrowing is visibly more pronounced after the support AST
(1.0–1.5 V) than after the Pt AST (0.6–0.95 V). The
comparison between the Pt (111) peaks of Pt for pre- and postmortem
electrodes reveals the size differences of the Pt NPs due to modest
sharpening and narrowing of the peaks. Taller and narrower peaks for
postmortem electrodes indicate Pt coarsening, even more so after the
support AST than the Pt AST. In-house-made Pt/C develops the tallest
and the narrowest Pt (111), while Pt/75%C+25%Ti3C2 and Pt/50%C+50%Ti3C2 show similar reflections,
retaining markedly broader profiles. The MXene-containing supports
therefore suppress Pt particle growth relative to carbon under both
protocols, with the largest effect under the more corrosive support
degradation cycles, where loss of the carbon support and the associated
detachment and coalescence of Pt particles dominate the degradation
of the in-house-made Pt/C benchmark.

Ti L2,3-edge spectra were also recorded
on the Pt/75%C+25%Ti3C2 and Pt/50%C+50%Ti3C2 electrodes
before and after both accelerated stress tests, using the acquisition
and normalization procedure applied to the powders (d). In every case, the spectra
retain the sharp Ti4+ anatase multiplet, with no emergence
of reduced-Ti features and no loss of multiplet definition. Across
all six electrode spectra, the L3 t2g position
lies within 458.20–458.28 eV and the L3 eg centroid lies within 460.50–460.55 eV, total spreads of 0.08
and 0.05 eV, respectively, comparable to the reproducibility of repeat
scans of the same specimen. The eg/t2g ratio
likewise shows no systematic change between the pre- and postmortem
states of either composition. These observations are corroborated
by Ti 2p XPS, which is described by a single Ti4+ doublet
at 458.7–458.9 eV in every electrode, with no statistically
justified Ti3+ component. The Ti speciation and local coordination
of the support are therefore unchanged by 30,000 potential cycles
under either protocol, including the 1.0–1.5 V support-degradation
test. This is a direct measure of support-phase stability under the
conditions that drive carbon corrosion, and it contrasts with the
changes undergone by the other components of the same electrodes;
postmortem XRD shows pronounced growth of the Pt crystallites, and
Raman spectroscopy shows measurable modification of the carbon framework
over the same tests. The MXene-derived anatase is thus not a participant
in the degradation but a stable, corrosion-resistant component of
the support, consistent with the reduced Pt coarsening and lower Pt
dissolution measured for the MXene-containing catalysts. Table SI3 shows a literature comparison of this
work and studies on other Ti3C2- and TiO2-based materials for the ORR in acidic media.

Conclusions

This study provides the first AST protocol
in a GDL flow cell with
potential-cycling durability for Pt/C+Ti3C2 catalysts,
enabling a direct benchmark against Pt/C in acidic media for the ORR.
Incorporating both carbon and Ti3C2 MXene as
the support for the active Pt allows simultaneous tuning of activity
and durability; across all measurements, the carbon-rich composite
(Pt/75%C+25%Ti3C2) shows the best overall balance.
Structurally, XRD confirms fcc Pt on all samples, and SEM and TEM
show thin Ti3C2 MXene flakes and uniformly distributed
Pt nanoparticles, with ∼100 nm Pt clusters appearing more frequently
in MXene-rich samples. HRTEM, STXM, XAS, and XPS reveal local anatase
TiO2 on the Ti3C2 surfaces, and pre/postmortem
XPS shows unchanged Pt 4f and Ti 2p states, indicating that degradation
is dominated by particle growth rather than a change in the chemical
state. The BET surface area decreases as the Ti3C2 amount increases due to restacking, except for the Pt/75%C+25%Ti3C2, which retains a larger surface area than that
of Pt/C. RDE measurements show the highest ORR performance for Pt/75%C+25%Ti3C2 and Pt/C, with similar onset potentials and
the strongest limiting currents. This is followed by Pt/50%C+50%Ti3C2 and then the Ti3C2-rich
samples. The ECSA derived from RDE tests follows this trend. All Tafel
slopes cluster near 60 mV/dec regardless of the support type, consistent
with Pt kinetics, which shows that the RDE captures intrinsic kinetics
but misses mass transport issues. GDL flow cell results confirm the
hierarchy at the high-current regime: at 1 A/cm2, Pt/75%C+25%Ti3C2 delivers the highest voltage, followed by Pt/C
and Pt/50%C+50%Ti3C2, while the Pt/25%C+75%Ti3C2 and Pt/Ti3C2 are well
below the ORR range, showing the dominance of transport and kinetic
limits. GEIS confirms that the lowest charge transfer/transport losses
belong to Pt/75%C+25%Ti3C2, followed by Pt/C
and then Pt/50%C+50%Ti3C2. The stability of
the catalysts under the AST reveals complementary results. There is
no voltage loss at 1 A/cm2 for the Pt/50%C+50%Ti3C2 sample, followed by only 4% loss for Pt/75%C+25%Ti3C2, which concludes that Ti3C2 addition increases the stability under this protocol, while Pt/C
shows 6% loss after 30,000 cycles. However, the Pt/75%C+25%Ti3C2 sample still yields the best overall ORR performance
after the AST and outperforms in-house-made Pt/C. TEM results show
Pt coarsening for all samples, with the smallest growth observed for
Pt/75%C+25%Ti3C2, followed by Pt/50%C+50%Ti3C2, and the largest growth for Pt/C. Raman D/G
changes follow the same trend, which shows less carbon damage when
MXene is present, but not dominant under this protocol. Under the
more aggressive support-degradation AST (1.0–1.5 V), the compositional
trade-off inverts. Pt/50%C+50%Ti3C2 suffers
the largest high-current loss (599 mV at 1000 mA/cm2),
whereas Pt/75%C+25%Ti3C2 retains the best performance,
confirming that a carbon-rich composite is required to withstand the
corrosive potential window and that 25% Ti3C2 is the practical optimum across both protocols. Postmortem XRD shows
that the MXene-derived TiO2-containing supports suppress
Pt crystallite growth under both tests, most strongly under the support
AST, where Pt/C coarsens to 9.5 nm against 6.9 and 7.6 nm for the
25% and 50% Ti-containing composites, respectively. ICP-OES confirms
lower Pt dissolution for the composites under the same protocol (10.3%
for Pt/75%C+25%Ti3C2 versus 17.4% for the in-house
Pt/C), while less than 1.5% of the support Ti is released, establishing
that the anatase is largely retained and that degradation is dominated
by Pt coarsening and carbon restructuring/loss. Pre- and postmortem
Ti L2,3-edge XAS and Ti 2p XPS show the Ti4+ anatase phase to be invariant after 30,000 cycles under either protocol,
with no emergence of reduced Ti, identifying the MXene-derived anatase
as a stable, corrosion-resistant component rather than a participant
in degradation. Consequently, a small amount of Ti3C2 improves the durability of the catalyst layer under the applied
protocol, accompanied by suppressed Pt coarsening and, as indicated
by postmortem Raman, less carbon structural change, while maintaining
the electronic percolation and gas-transport pathways. This durability
arises from a predominantly metal–support interaction, consistent
with interfacial anchoring of Pt at the MXene-derived anatase surface
through Pt–O–Ti bonds, together with the corrosion resistance
of that oxide, and not from an electronic perturbation of Pt, which
our Pt L3-edge XAS and Pt 4f XPS exclude even for the carbon-free
composition. The excessive MXene amount lowers the ECSA, impairs mass
transport, and diminishes activity. The Pt/75%C+25%Ti3C2 composition defines a practical window that maintains superior
high-current performance after stress while avoiding the penalties
of MXene-rich layers. By delivering the first coupled GDL flow cell
performance-stability benchmark for Pt/C+Ti3C2, this study provides both an optimal composition target and a reproducible
half-cell protocol for future Pt/MXene screening.
