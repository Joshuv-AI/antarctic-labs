ADAR1 regulates radiosensitivity in ESCC through cIAP2-NF-κB signaling and C16orf46 editing
Authors: Liangchao Sun, Ahmed Ghanim Glayl, Ming Zhang, Chenglong Zhao, Jialin Sun, Kaikai Zhao, Jiaxin Sun, Xue Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryEsophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) frequently exhibits resistance to radiotherapy, limiting treatment efficacy. We investigated the role of an RNA-editing enzyme, adenosine deaminase acting on RNA (ADAR1), in regulating radiosensitivity, using patient samples, ESCC cell lines, and xenograft models. ADAR1 showed high expression in ESCC and was associated with a poor response to neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy. ADAR1 loss suppressed tumor growth and enhanced radiosensitivity in vitro and in vivo. ADAR1 depletion promoted gasdermin E (GSDME)-mediated pyroptosis, thereby sensitizing tumor cells to irradiation. Transcriptome-wide RNA-editing analysis identified C16orf46 (chromosome 16 open reading frame 46) as a functionally relevant ADAR1-dependent editing target, and reduced editing of C16orf46 contributed to radiosensitization. ADAR1 depletion also altered cIAP2 (cellular inhibitor of apoptosis protein 2) and NF-κB signaling, suggesting an additional RNA editing-independent mechanism. These findings identify ADAR1-dependent RNA editing as a regulator of ESCC radiotherapy response and support ADAR1 as a potential target for radiosensitization.

Introduction

Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), the predominant subtype of esophageal carcinoma (ESCA) in Asia, accounts for over 90% of cases in China and remains a major contributor to cancer-related mortality worldwide.1,2 Owing to the lack of early symptoms, most patients are diagnosed at locally advanced stages, with a poor 5-year survival rate of 15%–35%.3 Radiotherapy (RT), alone or combined with chemotherapy (CRT), is a standard treatment for unresectable or locally advanced ESCC.4,5 However, its clinical efficacy is often limited by intrinsic or acquired radioresistance, leading to treatment failure and recurrence.6,7 A better understanding of the molecular mechanisms of radioresistance could provide more effective strategies and therapeutics for treatment.

Most studies have emphasized transcriptional regulation, overlooking the contribution of post-transcriptional modification to cancer initiation and progression. Adenosine-to-inosine (A-to-I) RNA editing, catalyzed by the adenosine deaminase acting on RNA (ADAR) protein family, represents a widespread post-transcriptional modification that alters RNA processing, ultimately altering protein function.8,9 Recently, ADAR1 has emerged as a key oncogenic regulator involved in tumor progression, immune evasion, and resistance in multiple malignancies.10,11,12,13,14 In ESCC, ADAR1 facilitates tumor survival under SOX2-driven oncogenic stress by suppressing immunogenic double-stranded RNAs (dsRNAs)15 and promotes malignancy by editing transcripts such as AZIN1, leading to pro-tumorigenic gain-of-function variants.16 These findings suggest that ADAR1 is functionally important in ESCC biology. Nevertheless, whether ADAR1 influences the response of ESCC to RT has not been established.

Mechanistically, ADAR1 regulates cancer cell fate through both RNA editing-dependent and -independent pathways.17 In its canonical role, ADAR1 edits endogenous dsRNAs to prevent inappropriate activation of immune sensors such as MDA5 and downstream type I interferon (IFN-I) responses. Loss of ADAR1 has been shown to enhance IFN-mediated inflammation, sensitizing tumors to RT and immunotherapy in melanoma, highlighting an RNA editing-dependent mechanism.18 Moreover, in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), ADAR1 promotes radioresistance through an RNA editing-independent mechanism, upregulating the DNA repair factor Rad18 through E2F3-mediated transcription and direct interaction.11 How ADAR1 contributes to radioresistance in ESCC through RNA editing-dependent and -independent mechanisms remains largely unclear. Elucidating the dual function of ADAR1 in modulating radiosensitivity in ESCC could provide new strategies for overcoming radioresistance.

In the present study, ADAR1 levels were markedly elevated in ESCC, and this elevation was associated with poor response in patients receiving neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (nCRT). We further showed that ADAR1 depletion enhanced radiosensitivity in ESCC by triggering pyroptosis via both an RNA editing-independent cIAP2-NF-κB (cellular inhibitor of apoptosis protein 2-nuclear factor κB) pathway and an RNA editing-dependent reduction in C16orf46 (chromosome 16 open reading frame 46) editing. Together, our findings introduced RNA editing as a novel mechanism underlying ESCC radioresistance and highlight ADAR1 as a promising therapeutic target to increase the RT efficacy.

Results

ADAR1 was upregulated in ESCC and correlated with poorer response to nCRT

The expression of A-to-I RNA editing enzymes (ADAR1, ADAR2, and ADAR3) was first analyzed via data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), which included both ESCC and esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC) cases. Among them, only ADAR1 was significantly upregulated in tumor tissue (n = 182) compared with adjacent tissues (n = 286) (Figures S1A–S1C), suggesting its potential involvement in esophageal tumorigenesis. We then focused specifically on ADAR1 expression in ESCC. Mammalian cells express two ADAR1 isoforms, p110 and p150, which are located in the nucleus and cytoplasm.19 We measured the mRNA levels of both p110 and p150 isoforms in six pairs of ESCC tumor and adjacent non-tumor tissues. Next, we evaluated the clinical relevance of ADAR1 expression in ESCC patients receiving nCRT (Figure 1B). Immunohistochemical analysis showed that ADAR1 expressed in both the nucleus and cytoplasm of tumor cells, with relatively more prominent nuclear staining and weaker cytoplasmic signals. The nuclear and cytoplasmic staining intensities were tightly coordinated across samples; cases with strong nuclear staining consistently exhibited strong cytoplasmic staining, and cases with weak nuclear staining showed correspondingly weak cytoplasmic staining. Notably, a high ADAR1 expression correlated with higher post-treatment N stage and poorer tumor regression grade (TRG), whereas no significant associations were observed with sex, age, alcohol, smoking, pre-treatment T and N stages, or post-treatment T stage (Figure 1C and Table S1). These results indicated that ADAR1 expression was higher in ESCC and associated with poor treatment response, highlighting its potential as a predictive biomarker for therapeutic response.

ADAR1 promoted radioresistance in ESCC

To further validate the involvement of ADAR1 in ESCC radiosensitivity, we utilized ADAR1-knockdown models to assess cell viability following irradiation. MTT assays revealed that siADAR1-mediated knockdown of ADAR1 significantly reduced cell viability post-irradiation (Figure 2C). siADAR1#1 was selected for subsequent experiments. Colony formation assays demonstrated a notable decrease in the surviving fraction of irradiated cells upon ADAR1 depletion (Figures 2D and 2E). Although ADAR1 knockdown suppressed proliferation, even in the absence of irradiation (Figure S2), a two-way ANOVA confirmed a significant interaction between ADAR1 knockdown and irradiation (p < 0.05), supporting a radiosensitizing effect. This suggests that ADAR1 loss confers radiosensitization rather than a purely additive antiproliferative effect. In a radioresistant ESCC cell model (KYSE410-RT, Figure S3A) ADAR1 levels were markedly elevated in KYSE410-RT cells compared with those in their parental counterparts (Figure 2F), and silencing ADAR1 restored radiosensitivity, reinforcing its role in mediating radioresistance (Figures 2G and S3B). To dissect the isoform-specific contributions of ADAR1, either ADAR1 p110 or ADAR1 p150 was ectopically expressed in ADAR1-silenced ESCC cells (Figure S3C). Both isoforms partially restored the viability of siADAR1-transfected cells after irradiation, indicating that both the ADAR1 p110 and p150 isoforms contributed to enhancing post-irradiation survival (Figure 2H).

Encouraged by these results, we further assessed ADAR1 depletion on radiosensitivity in vivo via a subcutaneous xenograft model with KYSE410-shADAR1 and KYSE410-shControl cells (Figure 3A). After tumor establishment, the mice were treated with local RT (8 Gy × 3 fractions) (Figure 3B). ADAR1 knockdown significantly suppressed tumor growth and enhanced the RT efficacy, with the shADAR1 + RT group showing 55% tumor growth inhibition at day 15 (Figures 3C and 3D) and lower tumor weight than the group treated with RT alone (p = 0.0128) (Figure 3E). H&E staining revealed characteristic irradiation-induced histological damage in the RT-treated tumors, while the most extensive necrosis and reduction in viable tumor cells were observed in the shADAR1 + RT group (Figure 3F). Furthermore, in RT-treated tumors, Ki-67 expression was reduced, with the greatest decrease in the shADAR1+ RT group (Figures 3G and 3H). Consistently, morphological changes were observed in irradiated tumors, including enlarged cell size and cytoplasmic expansion, which are indicative of radiation-induced cellular alterations. ADAR1 downregulation enhanced radiosensitivity via pyroptosis in ESCC cell lines

Having confirmed the radiosensitizing effect of ADAR1 depletion in ESCC, we next explored the underlying mechanism. Annexin V/PI analysis showed that ADAR1 knockdown combined with irradiation markedly increased the proportion of annexin V+/PI+ cells in KYSE410 cells (15.97% ± 2.46% vs. 3.03% ± 0.97%, p = 0.0327) (Figures 4A and 4B), with similar results in the KYSE150 and TE-1. The increase in Annexin V+/PI+ cells, characteristic of late-stage membrane-compromised death, rather than early apoptotic Annexin V+/PI− cells, suggests that ADAR1 knockdown enhances cell death through a non-classical pathway. Consistent with pyroptotic features, ADAR1 knockdown plus irradiation markedly increased lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels (Figure 4C), accompanied by elevated levels of IL-1β and IL-18 in the culture supernatant (Figures 4D and 4E). Western blotting analysis further demonstrated robust cleavage of gasdermin E (GSDME), a key effector of pyroptosis, in siADAR1 + RT cells (Figure 4F). ADAR1 suppression activated the cIAP2-NF-κB-GSDME pathway to promote pyroptosis in response to RT

To elucidate the mechanisms by which ADAR1 regulated pyroptosis, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed in ADAR1-deficient and control KYSE410 cells. A total of 2,255 differentially expressed genes were identified upon ADAR1 knockdown, including 1,524 upregulated ones (Figure 5A). KEGG analysis revealed the enrichment of NF-κB signaling (Figures 5B, S4A, and S4B), which was validated by RT-qPCR (Figure S4C). Consistently, western blotting analysis confirmed increased expression of the phosphorylated NF-κB p65 (p-p65, Ser536) following ADAR1 depletion (Figure 5C). Given that NF-κB activation is known to promote IL-1β production, which is a critical event in pyroptosis,21 we hypothesized that ADAR1 knockdown enhanced pyroptosis via the activation of NF-κB. To test this hypothesis, BAY 11–7082 was used to inhibit NF-κB activity in ADAR1-deficient KYSE410 cells. Previous studies have shown that cIAP2 could positively regulate NF-κB via IκB degradation. Our RNA-seq data revealed that cIAP2 was markedly upregulated in ADAR1-depleted cells (Figure 5E), a finding further corroborated at the protein level by western blotting (Figure 5F). These observations led to the hypothesis that ADAR1 loss promotes pyroptosis, at least in part, via cIAP2-mediated activation of NF-κB signaling. Consistently, knockdown of cIAP2 reduced NF-κB activation (Figure 5G) and partially reversed the radiosensitivity induced by ADAR1 suppression (Figure 5H). Importantly, no editing sites were detected in cIAP2 transcripts, indicating an RNA editing-independent mechanism. Collectively, these results indicated that ADAR1 promoted radioresistance in ESCC, at least in part, by activating the cIAP2-NF-κB-GSDME pathway through an RNA editing-independent mechanism.

ADAR1-mediated RNA editing of C16orf46 enhanced radioresistance in ESCC

ADAR1 regulates cancer progression and therapeutic resistance through both RNA editing-independent and -dependent mechanisms. Having demonstrated that ADAR1 enhanced radioresistance through RNA editing-independent activation of the cIAP2-NF-κB-caspase-3-GSDME pathway, we next investigated the contribution of its RNA editing activity. Global analysis of A-to-I RNA editing events following ADAR1 knockdown revealed a marked reduction in A>G conversions following ADAR1 depletion, confirming its essential role in A-to-I editing (Figure 6A). After excluding germline SNPs, we identified 70 ADAR1-dependent A-to-I editing events in siControl cells that were diminished upon ADAR1 depletion. These events were classified on the basis of their genomic location (e.g., 3′ untranslated region [UTR], 5′ UTR, intronic, and intergenic) and their impact on protein-coding sequences (missense, nonsense, and synonymous mutations). Focusing on editing events predicted to induce missense mutations, we shortlisted several candidate targets, including NBPF12, C16orf46, GPR37, and AKR1B10. To evaluate their functional relevance, we first transfected plasmids encoding the wild-type and edited forms of these candidates (without epitope tags), confirmed their expression by qRT-PCR, and assessed their effects on radiosensitivity (Figure S5). For preliminary screening, a fixed radiation dose was used to allow comparison of radiosensitivity among candidate targets. Among the candidates tested, C16orf46 exhibited the most consistent and pronounced functional effects. C16orf46 is a protein-coding gene located at 16q23.2. Its encoded protein remains largely uncharacterized and localizes primarily to the cytosol and nucleoplasm. To date, only one cancer-related report has identified C16orf46 as part of a six-gene prognostic signature for hepatocellular carcinoma,22 with no functional characterization. To further confirm that the A>G RNA editing event at the C16orf46 site is dependent on ADAR1, we performed Sanger sequencing of the C16orf46 editing site in both wild-type (siControl) and ADAR1-knockdown (siADAR1) cells. Representative chromatograms showed a clear reduction of the edited G peak and restoration of the unedited A peak in the siADAR1 group, confirming that this editing event is ADAR1 dependent (Figure S6). We then constructed Flag-tagged plasmids expressing the wild-type (WT-C16orf46) and edited (edit-C16orf46, p.Tyr335His) forms of the protein and transduced KYSE410 cells to establish stable cell lines overexpressing each form (Figures 6B and 6C). Function assays revealed that the unedited form (WT-C16orf46) expression inhibited the proliferation of KYSE410 cells, as shown by the results of the CCK-8, colony formation, and EdU assays (Figures 6D–6F). Importantly, WT-C16orf46 expression significantly sensitized cells to irradiation (Figures 6G and 6H). Moreover, WT-C16orf46 overexpression was associated with increased LDH release and cleavage of GSDME following irradiation, suggesting that C16orf46 editing contributes to resistance by modulating pyroptotic cell death pathways (Figures 6I and 6J). To further explore whether C16orf46 editing events are functionally relevant to the oncogenic role of ADAR1, we reintroduced edit-C16orf46 into ADAR1-knockdown KYSE410 cells. Notably, the expression of edit-C16orf46 partially reversed the increased radiosensitivity induced by ADAR1 knockdown, restoring radioresistance in these cells (Figure 6K). These findings suggested that ADAR1 enhanced radioresistance in ESCC, at least in part, through an RNA editing-dependent mechanism involving the modification of C16orf46.

Overall, our work illuminated the mechanism underlying radioresistance in ESCC. These data demonstrated that ADAR1 knockdown promoted GSDME cleavage and enhanced pyroptosis to confer radiosensitivity through both an RNA editing-independent mechanism (via cIAP2-NF-κB signaling) and an RNA editing-dependent mechanism (through C16orf46 modification). These findings highlight ADAR1 not only as a potential prognostic biomarker for predicting treatment response in ESCC patients but also as a promising therapeutic target to enhance the efficacy of RT.

Discussion

Radioresistance remains a major obstacle to achieving durable clinical benefits in ESCC.23 Despite considerable efforts to uncover the mechanisms underlying radioresistance, few have translated into clinically actionable targets. Here, we demonstrated that ADAR1 was significantly upregulated in ESCC and that its high expression corresponded to a markedly worse prognosis in patients receiving nCRT. Both in vitro and in vivo, ADAR1 knockdown suppressed tumor growth and enhanced the therapeutic efficacy of RT, establishing a direct role for ADAR1 in mediating radioresistance in ESCC. This is the first study to systematically define both RNA editing-dependent and -independent functions of ADAR1 in ESCC radioresistance.

RNA editing, particularly A-to-I conversion catalyzed by ADAR1, is increasingly being recognized as a driver of cancer progression and therapy resistance.24,25,26,27 Using an ADAR1-knockdown ESCC model, RNA-seq revealed a global reduction in A-to-I editing, highlighting its role in shaping the tumor transcriptome in ESCC (Figure 6A). Among the identified targets, we discovered a novel A-to-I RNA editing event in C16orf46, a protein-coding gene located on human chromosome 16, resulting in a missense mutation (Y335H). Although the function of C16orf46 is poorly understood, prior studies have linked it to prognostic gene signatures in hepatocellular carcinoma.22 Given that A-to-I RNA editing at coding regions can lead to amino acid substitutions that alter protein structure, stability, or activity, such editing events may critically influence cellular processes such as protein-protein interactions, signal transduction, immune modulation, and cell survival mechanisms. For example, AZIN1 is a well-known ADAR1 editing target whose edited form (S367G) shows increased affinity for antizyme, thereby stabilizing oncogenic proteins such as ODC and CCND1 and enhancing tumor cell proliferation.28 Similarly, ADAR1-mediated editing of the tumor suppressor BLCAP disrupts its interaction with STAT3, leading to unchecked STAT3 activation and contributing to tumor growth in cancers such as hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), colorectal, and cervical cancers.29 Thus, the Y335H substitution in C16orf46 may alter its functional role in intracellular signaling or tumor regulatory networks, which merits further mechanistic investigation.

This study is the first to analyze the functional importance of A-to-I RNA editing in the regulation of radiosensitivity in ESCC. Although direct evidence linking individual editing sites to RT sensitivity remains limited, growing data suggest a close association between RNA editing and RT. For instance, CRT has been shown to induce ADAR1-mediated RNA editing in colorectal cancer, leading to the generation of neoantigens, such as edited cyclin I and enhanced immune recognition.30 These findings, along with our data in ESCC, suggest that RNA editing may serve as an adaptive mechanism to cope with radiation-induced cellular stress and modulate therapeutic outcomes. Our study adds to the growing body of evidence that implicates RNA editing in the RT response. Given that ADAR1 exists in two major isoforms with distinct subcellular localizations and functions, it is also highly probable that both p110 and p150 participate in this editing process31; however, our current experimental design did not distinguish between isoform-specific contributions, which should be clarified in future studies using isoform-selective constructs or rescue assays.

In addition to its canonical role in A-to-I RNA editing, ADAR1 also performs editing-independent functions by modulating cellular signaling pathways. A well-characterized editing-independent function involves its Zα domain, which binds to and sequesters Z-form RNAs (Z-RNAs), thereby preventing their recognition by the Zα domain of ZBP1.32 In our study, we found that ADAR1 suppresses the cIAP2-NF-κB axis in ESCC. Knockdown of ADAR1 led to increased cIAP2 expression and NF-κB activation, which, in turn, triggered GSDME-dependent pyroptosis. No RNA editing events were detected in the cIAP2 transcript, suggesting that this regulatory effect does not rely on ADAR1 deaminase activity. Prior studies have shown that ADAR1 can regulate transcripts by binding to dsRNAs and competing with RNA-binding proteins in an editing-independent manner.33 For example, cytoplasmic ADAR1p110 competes with RNA-binding proteins such as STAUFEN1 for access to structured 3′ UTRs, thereby stabilizing transcripts such as RAD51 or ATM, which are involved in DNA damage repair and cellular stress responses.34 In addition, large-scale proteomic screens have revealed ADAR1’s associations with transcription factors, ribosomal components, and endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-associated proteins,35,36 suggesting a broader roles for ADAR1 in co-transcriptional regulation and translational control beyond its RNA-editing function. Whether ADAR1 regulates the cIAP2-NF-κB pathway through direct protein-protein interactions, RNA scaffolding, or indirect modulation of the transcriptional machinery remains to be elucidated and represents an important direction for future research.

Previous studies have shown that ADAR1 knockdown promotes cell death across various cancer types.37,38 However, this effect is not mediated by canonical intrinsic apoptosis, but rather involves alternative forms of cell death.39 For instance, ADAR1 Zα domain can bind to Z-RNAs and block ZBP1-RIPK3 signaling, thereby inhibiting necroptosis or PANoptosis.32 Consistent with this notion, we found that ADAR1 knockdown sensitizes ESCC cells to irradiation-induced pyroptosis. Pyroptosis, a highly inflammatory form of programmed cell death driven by GSDM-mediated pore formation in membrane and the release of cytokines, has increasingly been implicated in cancer therapy responses.40,41,42 In our model, ADAR1 depletion enhanced LDH release, GSDME cleavage, and cytokine secretion following radiation, which are hallmarks of pyroptotic death and support a mechanistic role for ADAR1 in suppressing this pathway. Beyond direct tumor killing, pyroptosis can promote antitumor immunity by releasing damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and inflammatory mediators, which enhance dendritic cell maturation and promote antigen presentation to T cells.43 This process may convert poorly immunogenic tumors into “hot” tumors with improved immune cell infiltration. Given the immunogenic role of RT,44 combining ADAR1 inhibition with RT may synergize with immune checkpoint blockade to overcome resistance in immunologically “cold” tumors. However, further studies are needed to evaluate the clinical relevance and feasibility of this strategy.

Although our study focused on ESCC, we also explored whether the ADAR1-C16orf46 axis might have broader relevance in EAC. Analysis of the TCGA dataset, which includes both ESCC and EAC cases, revealed that ADAR1 is broadly upregulated across both histological subtypes, suggesting that its dysregulation is not restricted to squamous cell carcinoma. In addition, preliminary experiments in the EAC cell line SK-GT-4 showed that ADAR1 knockdown enhanced radiosensitivity, consistent with our findings in ESCC models and that the expression of edited C16orf46 partially reversed this effect (Figure S7). While these observations raise the possibility that ADAR1-mediated RNA editing of C16orf46 may play a subtype-independent role in esophageal cancers, we acknowledge that the EAC data remain exploratory at this stage. Systematic validation in larger EAC cohorts and additional cell lines is required to extend our conclusions to adenocarcinoma.

In summary, our study highlights the dual functional role of ADAR1 in regulating radiosensitivity in ESCC through both RNA editing-dependent regulation of oncogenic transcripts such as C16orf46 and editing-independent signaling via the cIAP2-NF-κB-GSDME axis. The evidence supports ADAR1 as a promising biomarker and therapeutic target for radiosensitization and provides a mechanistic foundation for the development of ADAR1-based combination strategies to improve treatment outcomes in ESCC.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, although our data demonstrate that ADAR1 depletion alters cIAP2 and NF-κB signaling and promotes GSDME-mediated pyroptosis, the precise molecular mechanisms linking ADAR1 to cIAP2 regulation remain incompletely understood and warrant further investigation. Second, we identified C16orf46 as a functionally relevant ADAR1-dependent RNA editing target and demonstrated its contribution to radiosensitivity; however, the biological functions and downstream molecular mechanisms of C16orf46 remain largely unexplored. Third, the clinical cohort included a relatively limited number of ESCC patients, and larger independent cohorts are required to further validate the predictive value of ADAR1 as a biomarker of RT response. Fourth, although both ADAR1 p110 and p150 isoforms were implicated in regulating radiosensitivity, their individual contributions and isoform-specific mechanisms were not comprehensively dissected in the present study. Finally, preliminary experiments performed in the EAC cell line SK-GT-4 suggest that some findings may extend beyond ESCC; however, these observations remain exploratory and require validation in additional EAC models and clinical samples. Despite these limitations, our findings provide evidence that ADAR1 regulates ESCC radiosensitivity through both RNA editing-dependent and -independent mechanisms.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human subjects

A total of 42 patients with esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) who received neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (nCRT) were included in this study. Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor specimens from these 42 patients were used for immunohistochemical analyses. All patients were recruited from a Chinese population. The cohort included both male and female patients, and patient demographic and clinicopathological characteristics are summarized in Table S1. No significant association was observed between ADAR1 expression and either sex or age in this cohort. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment and sample collection. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shandong Cancer Hospital and Institute (Approval No. SDTHEC202505031). In addition, six paired fresh ESCC tumors and matched adjacent normal tissues were collected for molecular analyses.

Cell lines

The human ESCC cell lines KYSE410, KYSE150, and TE-1, as well as the human esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC) cell line SK-GT-4, were used in this study. Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. The SK-GT-4 cell line was obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. and authenticated by the supplier. The KYSE410, KYSE150, and TE-1 cell lines were maintained in our laboratory. No additional cell line authentication or mycoplasma testing was performed in this study.

Animals

Five-week-old female BALB/c-nu/nu mice were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) and maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. All animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Shandong Cancer Hospital and Institute (Approval No. SDTHEC202505031). All animal procedures were performed in accordance with the institutional guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals.

Method details

Patient samples

Six paired ESCC tumors and contralateral normal tissue were collected for ADAR1 expression analysis. Additionally, 42 ESCC patients who received nCRT between 2021 and 2023 were included to evaluate ADAR1 expression and its correlation with treatment response. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shandong Cancer Hospital and Institute (Approval No. SDTHEC202505031). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to sample collection. Clinical and pathological data, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) score, smoking and alcohol history, pretreatment and posttreatment of tumor (T) and nodal (N) stages and tumor regression grade (TRG), were collected for all patients. Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor samples were obtained from the Pathology Department. Patients with TRG = 0 were defined as having high nCRT efficacy, whereas those with TRG = 1-3 were classified as having low efficacy.

Immunohistochemistry

Tissue sections (3-4 μm) were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and processed for antigen retrieval in EDTA (pH 8.0). After blocking endogenous peroxidase with 3% hydrogen peroxide, the sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, DAB staining and hematoxylin counterstaining. The slides were scanned via a Nanozoomer scanner.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) and transcriptome analysis

RNA was isolated using Trizol reagent (Accurate Biotechnology (Hunan) Co., Ltd., Hunan, China). cDNA was synthesized from 500 ng of RNA with the Evo M-MLV RT Mix Kit (Accurate Biotechnology (Hunan) Co., Ltd.). qRT-PCR was performed with SYBR Green Master Mix (Accurate Biotechnology (Hunan) Co., Ltd.). The primers were designed to specifically amplify the unique exon regions of ADAR1 p110 and ADAR1 p150, respectively. Primers used in this study are listed in Table S2.

For transcriptome analysis, total RNA was extracted from samples, and RNA quality and integrity were assessed using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. RNA libraries were constructed using poly(A) mRNA enrichment and reverse transcription, followed by fragmentation, cDNA synthesis, adaptor ligation, and PCR amplification. Library quality was evaluated using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer and qRT-PCR. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate 150 bp paired-end reads. Raw sequencing reads were processed using fastp to remove adaptor sequences, low-quality reads, and reads containing ambiguous bases. Quality metrics including Q20, Q30, and GC content were calculated, and only high-quality clean reads were used for downstream analysis. Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2 (v2.0.5). Gene expression levels were quantified using featureCounts (v1.5.0-p3) and normalized as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM).

Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 R package, and p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Genes with adjusted p value ≤ 0.05 and |log2 fold change| ≥ 1 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Functional enrichment analysis, including Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), Reactome, Disease Ontology (DO), and DisGeNET pathway analyses, was performed using the clusterProfiler R package. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted using the GSEA software to identify significantly enriched pathways.

Cell culture

The ESCC cell lines KYSE410, KYSE150, and TE-1, as well as the EAC cell line SK-GT-4, were used in this study. Cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. All cell lines were routinely tested and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination prior to use.

Plasmid construction

All constructs were generated using the pcDNA3.1(+) vector. The full-length coding sequences of human ADAR1 p110 and p150 were amplified from cDNA and cloned into pcDNA3.1(+). Similarly, the full-length coding sequences of human C16orf46, NBPF12, GPR37, and AKR1B10 were amplified and cloned into the same vector. Editing-deficient mutants were generated by site-directed mutagenesis to abolish protein function while preserving expression. The specific mutations introduced were as follows: GPR37 (chr7:124747320 A>G), AKR1B10 (chr7:134537074 A>G), C16orf46 (chr16:81061346 A>G), and NBPF12 (chr1:146993652 A>G). All constructs were verified by Sanger sequencing.

For subsequent functional and mechanistic studies of C16orf46, additional constructs were generated in which the wild-type and edited coding sequences of C16orf46 were fused with a C-terminal 3×FLAG tag and cloned into pcDNA3.1(+). These FLAG-tagged constructs were synthesized and sequence-verified by Boshang Biotech Jinan, China.

Transfection

ADAR1 siRNA was obtained from APPLICATION AREA (Anhui, China) (Table S3). siRNA was transfected using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Plasmid transfection was performed by jetPRIME®Transfection reagent (Polyplus).

Protein extraction and western blot analysis

Total proteins from ESCC cells were extracted via RIPA buffer with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein samples on PVDF membranes were blocked and incubated with primary antibodies against ADAR1 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, MA, USA), gasdermin E (GSDME) (1:1 000, Cell Signaling Technology), NF-κB p65 (1: 1000, Cell Signaling Technology), phospho-NF-κB p65 (Ser536, 1: 1000, Cell Signaling Technology), cIAP2 (1:1 000, Proteintech Group), FLAG (DYKDDDDK tag Monoclonal antibody (Binds to FLAG® tag epitope), 1:1000, Proteintech), and GAPDH (1:50 000, ABclonal, Wuhan, China). The membranes were exposed to HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:1 000, ABclonal) for 1 h, and visualized using the enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection method.

Cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was detected with CCK-8, colony formation, and EdU assays. For the CCK-8 assay, cells transfected with siADAR1 or control siRNA for 24 h were seeded in 96-well plates (4×103 cells/well). After incubation, the cells were treated with 10% CCK-8 solution, and the optical density was measured at 450 nm. To assess clonogenic capacity, the cells were inoculated into 6-well plates and cultured for 2 weeks. Colonies were fixed and stained with crystal violet. Following thorough washing and drying, colonies were counted. The EdU assay was performed using a commercial kit (CellorLab).

Radiotherapy sensitivity assay

Radiosensitivity of ESCC cells were evaluated by MTT and colony formation assays. For MTT, the cells were seeded into 96-well plates, irradiated with 0-8 Gy (Xstrahl CIX2). After irradiation, the cells were incubated for 72 h and exposed to MTT reagent (5 mg/mL). The cell viability was evaluated as a percentage of the absorbance in the irradiated wells relative to the that in non-irradiated wells. For colony formation, 2000 cells/well were seeded in 6-well plates and irradiated with 2 Gy the following day. After a 2-week culture, colonies were fixed, stained and counted. The surviving fraction was calculated as the number of colonies in irradiated wells relative to that in the untreated control wells.

Generation of irradiation-resistant esophageal cancer cells

KYSE410 cells were subjected to fractionated irradiation using a linear accelerator (Xstrahl CIX2) at 2 Gy per fraction. The total dose administered was 40 Gy. The control cells were sham-irradiated with no irradiation applied.

In vivo animal studies

Five-week-old female BALB/c-nu/nu mice were randomized into four groups: (1) siControl; (2) siADAR1; (3) siControl+RT; and (4) siADAR1+RT. KYSE410-shControl or KYSE410-shADAR1 cells (5 × 106 in 0.1 mL of PBS) were subcutaneously injected into the right hind limb. When the tumors reached ∼100 mm3, the mice were locally exposed to 8 Gy irradiation daily for a total of 3 treatments (Xstrahl CIX2). The tumor volume was measured every 2 days using the following formula: (length × width2)/2. Tumor specimens were processed for histological (H&E) and immunohistochemical (Ki-67) evaluations.

Flow cytometry for cell death analysis

An annexin V-FITC apoptosis detection kit (Solarbio, China) was applied to evaluate the death of ESCC cells after RT. The targeted cells were suspended in Annexin V binding solution and stained with FITC-conjugated annexin V and propidium iodide (PI) for 20 min at 2-8°C. The samples were analyzed on a BD AccuriC6 Plus instrument and the data were analyzed with FlowJo.

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assay

LDH release, an indicator of cell membrane integrity, was quantified with an LDH Cytotoxicity Assay Kit. The supernatants from the control and treated cells were collected and analyzed according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

ELISA for IL-1β and IL-18 detection

The concentrations of interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and interleukin-18 (IL-18) in the cell culture supernatants were measured using ELISA kits (Elabscience, Wuhan, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v9.0 software. Comparisons between different groups were conducted using a t-test, or ANOVA, as appropriate. The data are presented as the mean ± standard deviations (SDs) and statistical significance was accepted at p < 0.05. ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001. All statistical details, including the exact test used, the value of n, and the computed p-values, can be found in the figure legends.
