Capacity-Constrained Online Convex Optimization with Delayed Feedback
Alexander Ryabchenko, Idan Attias, Daniel M. Roy

Abstract
Online learning with delayed feedback typically assumes that the learner can track all pending rounds until their feedback arrives. In practice, tracking resources are finite, and feedback from untracked rounds is permanently lost. In this paper, we study delayed online convex optimization (OCO) under a hard capacity constraint, where at most C C pending rounds can be tracked at any time. To model delay information, we introduce a semi-clairvoyant model that refines the clairvoyant assumption from prior work: rather than requiring delays to be known at prediction time, the learner observes delay expirations online, consistent with the classical unconstrained delayed setting. Our approach proceeds via a reduction to a novel “delayed and weighted” OCO problem, using a scheduler that randomizes tracking decisions and importance-weights the resulting observations. For this base problem, we propose and analyze Delayed-Weighted FTRL and its bandit analogue, establishing regret bounds that explicitly characterize the interaction between time-varying weights and delayed feedback. Combining these base learners with our schedulers yields the first regret guarantees for capacity-constrained OCO under convex and strongly convex losses, for both first-order and bandit feedback. For first-order feedback, capacity C = Ω ⁡ ( log ⁡ T ) C=\Omega(\log T) suffices to recover standard delayed OCO rates up to logarithmic factors. For bandit feedback, the regret rates are modulated by powers of ( 1 + σ max / C ) (1+\sigma_{\text{max}}/C) , where σ max \sigma_{\text{max}} is the maximum number of pending observations at any time. This allows the regret bound to degrade gracefully when C < σ max C<\sigma_{\text{max}} , while remaining sublinear.

Introduction

Online convex optimization is a canonical framework for sequential decision-making under uncertainty
[
18
,
34
]
: a player repeatedly selects an action from a convex domain, incurs a convex loss, and receives either full gradient information (
first-order feedback
) or only the incurred scalar loss (
bandit feedback
). We refer to these two settings as Online Convex Optimization (OCO) and Bandit Convex Optimization (BCO), respectively.
A long line of work studies delayed feedback in OCO and BCO, as well as in sequential decision-making more broadly, where the observation from round
t
arrives only at round
t+d_{t}

[
21
,
38
,
9
]
. The vast majority of this literature, however, takes for granted that the player has unlimited capacity to track pending rounds, that is, every outstanding feedback will eventually be observed, regardless of how many are pending simultaneously.

In many practical settings, only a limited number of rounds can be tracked concurrently. A natural example arises in clinical trials: a study can actively monitor only a bounded number of patients at any given time, and outcomes are observed only for the patients selected for monitoring — side effects may still manifest in unmonitored patients, but the trial never observes them.

[
39
]
formalized this limitation as a hard
capacity constraint
, capping the number of tracked rounds by a
capacity
parameter
C
, and studied it in the multi-armed bandit setting.

This paper extends the capacity-constrained framework from finite-action settings to convex domains, deriving regret bounds for both OCO and BCO with explicit dependence on the capacity
C
.
The bandit setting is substantially more challenging even without delays or capacity constraints, as the player must optimize over a continuous domain from scalar loss observations alone. Moreover, we relax the clairvoyant delay model of prior work, which assumes delays are known at prediction time; our framework instead reveals delays only upon their expiration, matching the information available in standard delayed online learning. This information structure is also closer to the standard clairvoyant model in online job scheduling (e.g.,
[
6
]
): a job’s processing time is revealed upon arrival, but the number of future jobs arriving during its processing interval is unknown in advance.

Contributions.

Following the reduction approach of
[
39
]
, we decompose capacity-constrained delayed OCO into two components: a
scheduler
, which randomly selects the rounds to track subject to the capacity constraint, and a
base learner
, which receives the resulting feedback stream with importance weights correcting for untracked rounds. Extending this reduction to convex domains introduces two new challenges: first, designing OCO and BCO algorithms that can accommodate feedback that is simultaneously delayed and non-uniformly weighted; and second, controlling the stochastic weight process induced by the scheduler in the presence of convex losses and bandit gradient estimation. Our contributions are as follows:

•

Delayed-weighted OCO and BCO
(
Section

3
).
We introduce OCO and BCO with feedback that is simultaneously delayed and weighted, extending the scale-free online learning framework of
[
33
]
to delayed feedback. We analyze a delayed-weighted variant of Follow-the-Regularized-Leader and its bandit analogue, obtaining regret bounds that identify pairwise interactions between weights of rounds with overlapping delays as the structural cost of combining weighting with delays. These results are new for convex domains and may be of independent interest.
This framework forms the base problem to which we reduce the capacity-constrained setting.

•

Unified proxy-delay scheduling
(
Section

4
). To handle delays that are unknown at prediction time,
[
39
]
introduced the idea of sampling
proxy delays
as surrogates for the true delays. We unify such constructions into a general framework parameterized by proxy-delay distributions, and introduce a new scheduler calibrated to the maximum number of pending observations,
\sigma_{\textnormal{max}}
, that yields uniform importance weights, a property crucial for our strongly convex and bandit guarantees. Importantly, the algorithm does not require prior knowledge of
\sigma_{\textnormal{max}}
, as it can be removed via the doubling trick (Appendix
F
).

•

Capacity-dependent regret bounds
(

). Combining the delayed-weighted regret bounds with the proxy-delay schedulers yields regret guarantees for capacity-constrained OCO and BCO; see Table

Remark 1.1
(Extensions)

Our framework allows the player to
preempt
tracked rounds—that is, to stop tracking them before their feedback arrives. There is also a non-preemptive variant of the problem, in which once a round is tracked, it cannot be dropped until its feedback is delivered. Several of our schedulers are non-preemptive by construction and achieve analogous guarantees in this setting; see Appendix
G
.
Additionally, the same guarantees extend to the fully non-clairvoyant setting, where delays
d_{t}
are revealed only at times
t+d_{t}
for rounds whose feedback is observed, provided that sufficient information about delay-related quantities such as
d_{\textnormal{tot}}
or
\sigma_{\textnormal{max}}
is available for tuning the algorithm parameters.

We study a

2.1
Notation for Delays and the Tracking Set

The interplay between delays and the capacity constraint requires keeping careful track of which past rounds have produced feedback, which are still pending, and which the player is currently tracking. We introduce this notation and present a few standard identities in delayed online learning.

Observed and backlog sets.

At the start of each round
t\in[T]
, the past rounds split into those whose feedback has already arrived and those whose feedback is still pending. We denote these by

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{t}\;=\;\{s\in[t-1]:s+d_{s}<t\},\qquad\mathcal{B}_{t}\;=\;\{s\in[t-1]:t\leq s+d_{s}\},

respectively; together they partition the past,
\mathcal{O}_{t}\cup\mathcal{B}_{t}=[t-1]
and
\mathcal{O}_{t}\cap\mathcal{B}_{t}=\emptyset
.
We write
\sigma_{t}=|\mathcal{B}_{t}|
for the backlog set size (or just
backlog
) at round
t
, and define

\textstyle\sigma_{\textnormal{max}}\;=\;\max_{t\in[T]}\sigma_{t},\qquad d_{\textnormal{max}}\;=\;\max_{t\in[T]}d_{t},\qquad d_{\textnormal{tot}}\;=\;\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}d_{t},

the maximum backlog, maximum delay, and total delay over the game. The next lemma collects two identities relating these quantities, which we will use repeatedly to bound the cost of the backlog.

Lemma 2.6
(Backlog properties)

For any delays
(d_{t})_{t=1}^{T}
with
d_{t}\in\{0,\ldots,T-t\}
, the following hold:

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sigma_{t}=d_{\textnormal{tot}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma_{\textnormal{max}}\leq\min\{\sqrt{2d_{\textnormal{tot}}},\,d_{\textnormal{max}}\}.

The first identity is the standard average-case quantity governing regret in delayed online learning
[
9
]
; the second controls the worst-case backlog.

Tracking set evolution.

Among the pending rounds, the player tracks only a subset. To reason about which rounds are tracked at each point in time, we consider the post-preemption snapshot

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{t}\;=\;\left\{s\in[T]:\text{$s$ lies in $\mathcal{S}$ immediately after preemption in round $t$}\right\}.

It satisfies
\mathcal{S}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{t}
,
|\mathcal{S}_{t}|\leq C
, and
\mathcal{S}_{t+1}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{t}\cup\{t\}
.
The first inclusion holds because every round in
\mathcal{S}
has been played but has not yet delivered feedback and round
t
itself has not yet been added at this point. The second is the capacity constraint. The third reflects that between round
t
and round
t+1
, the set may only gain index
t
(if added) and may only lose indices (through feedback or preemption).

2.2
Notation for Bandit Feedback and Smoothing

The bandit setting requires constructing gradient estimates from function values alone. We follow the standard single-point approach of
[
15
]
, which replaces each loss function with a smoothed surrogate whose gradient admits an unbiased estimator from a single bandit query.

Smoothing.

Under Assumption
2.5
, the inclusion
r\mathbb{B}^{k}\subseteq\mathcal{K}
implies
(1-\delta/r)\,\mathcal{K}+\delta\,\mathbb{B}^{k}\subseteq\mathcal{K}
for all
\delta\in(0,r]
, and so any point of the shrunken domain
(1-\delta/r)\mathcal{K}
admits a
\delta
-ball of perturbations that stays in
\mathcal{K}
. For such
\delta
and any integrable
f:\mathcal{K}\to\mathbb{R}
, we define the
\delta
-smoothing
of
f
as

f^{\delta}:(1-\delta/r)\mathcal{K}\to\mathbb{R},\qquad f^{\delta}(x)\;=\;\E_{v\sim\textnormal{Unif}(\mathbb{B}^{k})}\bigl[f(x+\delta v)\bigr].

The key properties of
f^{\delta}
are summarized in the following theorem.

2.7
[
15
]

For any
\delta\in(0,r]
and integrable
f:\mathcal{K}\to\mathbb{R}
:

1.

The
\delta
-smoothing
f^{\delta}
is differentiable with gradients
\nabla f^{\delta}(x)=\tfrac{k}{\delta}\E_{u\sim\textnormal{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{k-1})}[f(x+\delta u)\,u]
.

2.

If
f
is convex (
\lambda
-strongly convex), so is
f^{\delta}
.

3.

If
f
is
G
-Lipschitz, then
|f^{\delta}(x)-f(x)|\leq G\delta
and
\|\nabla f^{\delta}(x)\|\leq G
for
x\in(1-\delta/r)\mathcal{K}
.

The first item underpins single-point gradient estimation; the remaining two ensure that
f^{\delta}
preserves the regularity of

and stays uniformly close to it.

Online Convex Optimization with Delayed and Weighted Feedback

This section introduces a weighted variant of OCO with delayed feedback (Figure
2

The game itself involves no tracking set management; the connection to capacity-constrained OCO emerges only through the reductions. Beyond its role in our reductions, this game gives, to our knowledge, the first treatment of

online convex optimization
[
33
]
under delayed feedback.

In this game, the adversary fixes losses
f_{t}:\mathcal{K}\to\mathbb{R}
, delays
d_{t}\in\{0,\ldots,T-t\}

in advance. At each round
t
, the player predicts
x_{t}\in\mathcal{K}
and incurs the weighted loss
w_{t}f_{t}(x_{t})
. The feedback for round
t
is revealed at the end of round
t+d_{t}
, together with the weight
w_{t}
.

3.1
First-Order Feedback: DW-FTRL

Given a non-increasing learning-rate sequence
(\eta_{t})_{t=1}^{T}\subset(0,\infty)
, define the
regularization increments

\alpha_{1}=1/\eta_{1}
and
\alpha_{t}=1/\eta_{t}-1/\eta_{t-1}
. Starting from
x_{1}\in\mathcal{K}
,
delayed-weighted FTRL
(DW-FTRL) updates are given by

\displaystyle\makebox[50.00008pt][c]{\text{DW-FTRL:}}\quad x_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\operatornamewithlimits{arg\,min}_{x\in\mathcal{K}}\,\langle{x,\textstyle\sum_{s\in\mathcal{O}_{t+1}}w_{s}\nabla f_{s}(x_{s})}\rangle+\textstyle\sum_{s=1}^{t}\frac{\alpha_{s}}{2}\,\|x_{s}\!-\!x\|^{2}.

(1)

Each update aggregates the weighted gradients revealed so far and regularizes against the running sequence of iterates. The following theorem bounds the weighted regret of DW-FTRL.

3.1

For all
u\in\mathcal{K}
, predictions
(x_{t})_{t=1}^{T}
generated by DW-FTRL (
1

\displaystyle R^{w}_{T}(u)\leq\tfrac{D^{2}}{2\eta_{T}}+G^{2}\,\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t-1}(w_{t}^{2}+w_{t}\textstyle\sum_{s\in\mathcal{B}_{t}}w_{s}),

where

6
Discussion and Future Work

This work gives the first regret bounds for capacity-constrained OCO, in both first-order and bandit settings under convex and strongly convex losses. For first-order OCO, logarithmic capacity suffices to match unconstrained delayed rates; for BCO, capacity enters the dimension-dependent terms through powers of
(1+\sigma_{\textnormal{max}}/C)
.

Several directions remain open. First, our BCO bounds for convex losses scale as
\sqrt{T\sigma_{\textnormal{max}}}
in the delay-dependent term rather than the optimal
\sqrt{d_{\textnormal{tot}}}
of the unconstrained setting; closing this gap or establishing a separation is an open problem. Second, the capacity-constrained framework bears a structural resemblance to finite-buffer queueing systems, where jobs that find the buffer full are dropped. Our schedulers exploit this connection, but the precise relationship between queueing theory and online learning under capacity constraints warrants deeper exploration. Third, extending from oblivious to
adaptive
adversaries, who select losses and delays based on the player’s history, appears to require fundamentally new tools and is a compelling direction for future work.

Appendix A
Additional Related Work

Comparison to
[
39
]
.

The capacity-constrained framework was recently introduced by
[
39
]
for finite-action settings, specifically prediction with expert advice and multi-armed bandits. To manage the tracking set under the capacity limit, they developed a modular approach that decouples the observation scheduling policy from the base learning algorithm, proposing techniques such as batch partitioning and Pareto-distributed proxy delays. While we adopt their high-level separation of schedulers and base learners, extending this framework to continuous convex domains (OCO and BCO) introduces fundamentally new theoretical challenges. In particular, the proxy-delay scheduler of
[
39
]
inherently induces non-uniform observation probabilities; while discrete-action algorithms can absorb these via time-varying loss scales, such variable importance weights are highly detrimental to continuous bandit gradient estimation and strongly convex objectives. To address this, we introduce the delayed-weighted OCO and BCO framework, and design a novel unified proxy-delay scheduler calibrated to the maximum number of pending observations
\sigma_{\textnormal{max}}
that yields the uniform importance weights necessary for our continuous-domain bounds. Furthermore, their preemptive scheduler requires prior knowledge of the maximum delay
d_{\textnormal{max}}
in the non-clairvoyant setting. In contrast, our approach accommodates a semi-clairvoyant model and removes the need for prior knowledge of delay bounds via the doubling trick.

Connections to online scheduling.

Our model is closely related to online job and interval scheduling

[
27
,
51
,
6
,
35
]
.
In online interval scheduling, jobs arrive sequentially with release times and processing intervals, and the algorithm must assign jobs to a bounded number of machines while respecting overlap constraints.
Under the correspondence with our setting,
rounds become jobs,
tracking capacity corresponds to the number of available machines,
and delays determine the durations for which machines remain occupied.
Tracking a round occupies one unit of capacity until feedback arrives or the round is preempted.
The quantity
\sigma_{\textnormal{max}}
corresponds to the peak concurrency level of overlapping jobs, whereas
d_{\textnormal{max}}
reflects the longest processing duration and may therefore be much larger.
This perspective helps explain regimes in which

\sigma_{\textnormal{max}}\ll d_{\textnormal{max}}
,
which naturally arise in queueing systems with moderate average load but heavy-tailed service times.
The distinction between preemptive and non-preemptive schedulers in our framework also parallels classical scheduling models.

Connections to streaming algorithms.

Our framework also bears conceptual similarities to streaming algorithms

[
4
,
32
]
.
Streaming algorithms process massive data streams under strict memory constraints, typically maintaining compact randomized summaries or sketches rather than storing the entire stream.
Analogously, in our setting the learner cannot retain all pending observations and must selectively track only a bounded subset of rounds.
Both settings therefore rely on randomized sampling and compressed representations to preserve global performance guarantees despite limited resources.
Classic examples include the AMS sketch

[
4
]
,
Count-Min Sketch

[
10
]
,
and HyperLogLog

[
13
]
.

Online convex optimization.

Online convex optimization (OCO) and its feedback models have been studied extensively. Early work by
[
55
]
introduced online convex programming and established
O(\sqrt{T})
regret bounds for online gradient methods. For first-order feedback, subsequent research established the standard
\Theta(\sqrt{T})
minimax scaling for general convex losses (see, e.g.,
[
1
]
). For
\lambda
-strongly convex losses, logarithmic regret is achievable; in particular,
[
16
]
provided
O(\ln T)

\Omega(\ln T)

[

]
. Comprehensive background and further developments can be found in standard texts
[

,
34
]
.

Bandit convex optimization.

Bandit convex optimization (BCO) was initiated by
[
15
]
, who introduced the classical single-point gradient estimator and obtained a regret of order
O(T^{3/4}k^{1/2})
. Subsequently,
[
2

developed optimal algorithms for multi-point bandit feedback and sharpened the theoretical guarantees. For two-point feedback, they achieved
\widetilde{O}(T^{1/2}k^{2})
regret for convex losses and
O(k^{2}\ln T)

T^{2/3}

[

[

\Omega(k\sqrt{T})

[

(see, e.g.,
[

17

Fact B.1

Fact B.2

Lemma B.4
(
[

Let
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
be a convex compact set. For

, let function

be differentiable and
\lambda_{i}
-strongly convex with respect to

on
\mathcal{K}

\displaystyle\tfrac{\lambda_{0}+\lambda_{1}}{2}\|z_{0}-z_{1}\|^{2}\leq\langle{w_{0}-w_{1},\,z_{1}-z_{0}}\rangle+(\phi_{1}(z_{0})-\phi_{0}(z_{0}))-(\phi_{1}(z_{1})-\phi_{0}(z_{1})).

.

w_{t}=\tfrac{\mathbb{I}(|\mathcal{S}_{t}|<C)\,\mathbb{I}(d^{\prime}_{t}\geq d_{t})}{\mathbb{P}(d^{\prime}_{t}\geq d_{t})},\quad\E[w_{t}]\leq 1,\quad\E[w_{t}^{2}]\leq\tfrac{1}{\mathbb{P}(d^{\prime}_{t}\geq d_{t})},\quad\E[w_{t}w_{s}]\leq 1,

t,s\in[T]
. Moreover, the expected regret satisfies

\widebar{R}_{T}(u)\;\leq\;\E\!\left[\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}w_{t}\bigl(f_{t}(x_{t})-f_{t}(u)\bigr)\right]+GD\,\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}\bigl(|\mathcal{S}_{t}|=C\bigr).

Proof

From the structure of Algorithm
1
, feedback from round
t
is observed in round
t+d_{t}
if and only if
|\mathcal{S}_{t}|<C
and
d^{\prime}_{t}\geq d_{t}
. Consequently, we have the identity
w_{t}=\tfrac{\mathbb{I}(|\mathcal{S}_{t}|<C)\,\mathbb{I}(d^{\prime}_{t}\geq d_{t})}{\mathbb{P}(d^{\prime}_{t}\geq d_{t})}
.

Therefore, for each
t\in[T]
and
s\in[T]\setminus\{t\}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\E[w_{t}]\leq\E\left[\tfrac{\mathbb{I}(d^{\prime}_{t}\geq d_{t})}{\mathbb{P}(d^{\prime}_{t}\geq d_{t})}\right]=1,\quad\E[w_{t}^{2}]\leq\E\left[\tfrac{\mathbb{I}(d^{\prime}_{t}\geq d_{t})}{(\mathbb{P}(d^{\prime}_{t}\geq d_{t}))^{2}}\right]=\tfrac{1}{\mathbb{P}(d^{\prime}_{t}\geq d_{t})},

\displaystyle\E[w_{t}w_{s}]\leq\E\left[\tfrac{\mathbb{I}(d^{\prime}_{t}\geq d_{t})\,\mathbb{I}(d^{\prime}_{s}\geq d_{s})}{\mathbb{P}(d^{\prime}_{t}\geq d_{t})\,\mathbb{P}(d^{\prime}_{s}\geq d_{s})}\right]=\E\left[\tfrac{\mathbb{I}(d^{\prime}_{t}\geq d_{t})}{\mathbb{P}(d^{\prime}_{t}\geq d_{t})}\right]\,\E\left[\tfrac{\mathbb{I}(d^{\prime}_{s}\geq d_{s})}{\mathbb{P}(d^{\prime}_{s}\geq d_{s})}\right]=1,

where the last inequality applies the fact that proxy delays
d^{\prime}_{t}
and
d^{\prime}_{s}
are sampled independently.

For the regret bound, we write

\displaystyle\widebar{R}_{T}(u)

\displaystyle=\E\!\left[\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}(f_{t}(x_{t})-f_{t}(u))\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(a)}}}{{\leq}}\E\!\left[\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}(|\mathcal{S}_{t}|<C)\,(f_{t}(x_{t})-f_{t}(u))\right]+GD\,\E\!\left[\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}(|\mathcal{S}_{t}|=C)\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(b)}}}{{=}}\E\!\left[\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\tfrac{\mathbb{I}(|\mathcal{S}_{t}|<C)\,\mathbb{I}(d^{\prime}_{t}\geq d_{t})}{\mathbb{P}(d^{\prime}_{t}\geq d_{t})}\,(f_{t}(x_{t})-f_{t}(u))\right]+GD\,\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{S}_{t}|=C)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(c)}}}{{=}}\E\!\left[\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}w_{t}\,(f_{t}(x_{t})-f_{t}(u))\right]+GD\,\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{S}_{t}|=C),

where (a) combines Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
, (b) uses the fact that
d^{\prime}_{t}
is sampled from
\mathfrak{D}_{t}
at the start of round
t
independently of
S_{t}
and
x_{t}
, and (c) applies the identity
w_{t}=\tfrac{\mathbb{I}(|\mathcal{S}_{t}|<C)\,\mathbb{I}(d^{\prime}_{t}\geq d_{t})}{\mathbb{P}(d^{\prime}_{t}\geq d_{t})}
.

Lemma
4.3
(Restated)

Consider proxy delay distributions
\mathfrak{D}=(\mathfrak{D}_{t})_{t=1}^{T}
such that for every
t\in[T]

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{s\in\mathcal{B}_{t}\cup\{t\}}\mathbb{P}(d^{\prime}_{s}\geq t-s)\leq\frac{C}{8}.

Then,
\mathbb{P}\bigl(|\mathcal{S}_{t}|=C\bigr)\leq\exp(-C)
for every
t\in[T]
.

Proof

Fix
t\in[T]
. For each
s\in\mathcal{B}_{t}\cup\{t\}
, define
X_{s}=\mathbb{I}(d^{\prime}_{s}\geq t-s)
.

By independence of the proxy-delay draws across rounds, the random variables
(X_{s})_{s\in\mathcal{B}_{t}\cup\{t\}}
are independent Bernoulli random variables. Moreover, by construction of the proxy scheduler,
|\mathcal{S}_{t}|\leq\sum_{s\in\mathcal{B}_{t}\cup\{t\}}X_{s}
. Therefore, writing
X=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{B}_{t}\cup\{t\}}X_{s}
,
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{S}_{t}|=C)\leq\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{S}_{t}|\geq C)\leq\mathbb{P}(X\geq C).

Let
\mu=\E[X]=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{B}_{t}\cup\{t\}}\mathbb{P}(d^{\prime}_{s}\geq t-s)
. By assumption,
\mu\leq C/8
.

If
\mu=0
, then
\mathbb{P}(X\geq C)=0
. Assume henceforth that
\mu>0
, and define

\displaystyle\alpha=C/\mu-1\in[7,\infty).

By the multiplicative Chernoff bound (e.g., see Theorem 2.3.1 from
[
46
]
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X\geq C)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\bigl(X\geq(1+\alpha)\mu\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{e^{\alpha}}{(1+\alpha)^{1+\alpha}}\right)^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(C\left(\tfrac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}-\ln(1+\alpha)\right)\right).

As the function
f(x)=\ln(1+x)-\frac{x}{1+x}
is increasing on
[0,\infty)
,
\alpha
satisfies

\displaystyle\tfrac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}-\ln(1+\alpha)\leq 7/8-\ln 8<-1.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigl(|\mathcal{S}_{t}|=C\bigr)\leq\mathbb{P}(X\geq C)\leq e^{-C}.

Corollary
4.4
(Restated)

For each
t\in[T]
, let
H_{t}=\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{t}\tfrac{1}{j}
. Take
\mathfrak{D}_{t}
to be the shifted floored Pareto distribution

\mathfrak{D}_{t}\;=\;\lfloor\textnormal{Pareto}\bigl(\tfrac{C}{16H_{t}},\,1\bigr)\rfloor-1.

Then
\mathbb{P}(d^{\prime}_{t}\geq d_{t})=\min\bigl\{1,\,\tfrac{C}{16H_{t}(d_{t}+1)}\bigr\}
and
\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{S}_{t}|=C)\leq\exp(-C)
for every
t\in[T]
.

Proof

Fix
t\in[T]
. By the Pareto tail formula,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(d^{\prime}_{t}\geq t-s)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\!\left(\textnormal{Pareto}\!\left(\tfrac{C}{16H_{t}},1\right)\geq t-s+1\right)=\min\left\{1,\tfrac{C}{16H_{t}(t-s+1)}\right\}.

It remains to verify the condition of Lemma
4.3
. It follows from Fact
B.2
for harmonic numbers.

Thus, Lemma
4.3
now yields
\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{S}_{t}|=C)\leq e^{-C}
.

Corollary
4.5
(Restated)

Let
p=\min\{1,\tfrac{C}{8(\sigma_{\textnormal{max}}+1)}\}
. For every
t\in[T]
, let the proxy distribution
\mathfrak{D}_{t}\in\Delta(\{-1,\infty\})
be given by

\displaystyle\textstyle\mathbb{P}_{d^{\prime}\sim\mathfrak{D}_{t}}(d^{\prime}=\infty)=p,\qquad\mathbb{P}_{d^{\prime}\sim\mathfrak{D}_{t}}(d^{\prime}=-1)=1-p.

Then,
\mathbb{P}(d^{\prime}_{t}\geq d_{t})=p
and
\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{S}_{t}|=C)\leq\exp(-C)
for every
t\in[T]
.

Proof

Fix
t\in[T]
. Since
d_{t}\geq 0
and
d^{\prime}_{t}\in\{-1,\infty\}
,
\mathbb{P}(d^{\prime}_{t}\geq d_{t})=\mathbb{P}(d^{\prime}_{t}=\infty)=p
.

Thus, the condition of Lemma
4.3
is satisfied

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in\mathcal{B}_{t}\cup\{t\}}\mathbb{P}(d^{\prime}_{s}\geq t-s)=p\,|\mathcal{B}_{t}\cup\{t\}|=p(\sigma_{t}+1)\leq\tfrac{C(\sigma_{t}+1)}{8(\sigma_{\textnormal{max}}+1)}\leq C/8.

Applying Lemma
4.3
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{S}_{t}|=C)\leq\exp(-C).

This completes the proof.

Appendix E
Guarantees for Capacity-Constrained OCO and BCO: Proofs

In this section, we prove the main regret bounds for capacity-constrained OCO and BCO stated in

, covering both convex and strongly convex losses.

Theorem
5.1
(Restated)

Suppose

, and consider
\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{A},\mathfrak{D})
, where
\mathcal{A}
is DW-FTRL (
1
). With learning rates
\eta_{t}=\tfrac{D/G}{\sqrt{d_{\textnormal{tot}}+T}}

\mathfrak{D}
from Corollary
4.4

\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{A},\mathfrak{D})

\displaystyle\widebar{R}_{T}(x^{*})=O\left(GD\,{\sqrt{d_{\textnormal{tot}}+T}}\right).

Under
\lambda
-strong convexity (
2.3
), for rates

from Corollary
4.5

\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{A},\mathfrak{D})

Note that

General convex losses:

4.1
,
3.1
and Corollary
4.4

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{D^{2}}{\eta}+G^{2}\eta\,\E\!\left[\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}(w_{t}^{2}+w_{t}\textstyle\sum_{s\in\mathcal{B}_{t}}w_{s})\right]+GD\,T\exp(-C)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(b)}}}{{\leq}}\tfrac{D^{2}}{\eta}+17G^{2}\eta\,\left(d_{\textnormal{tot}}+T\right)+GD

\displaystyle\leq\E\!\left[\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}w_{t}(f_{t}(x_{t})-f_{t}(u))\right]+GD\,\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{S}_{t}|=C)

\displaystyle\leq\E\!\left[\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\tfrac{\alpha_{t}-w_{t}\lambda}{2}\,\|x_{t}\!-\!u\|^{2}\right]+G^{2}\,\E\!\left[\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t-1}(w_{t}^{2}\!+\!w_{t}\textstyle\sum_{s\in\mathcal{B}_{t}}\!w_{s})\right]+\tfrac{2G^{2}}{\lambda}\,Te^{-C}

To control the first term in (

where (a) uses the fact that

is sampled independently of

, and (b) lets

For the second term in (

\displaystyle G^{2}\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}

\displaystyle\E\left[\tfrac{k^{2}M^{2}w_{t}^{2}}{G^{2}\delta_{t}^{2}}+\tfrac{kMw_{t}}{G\delta_{t}}\sqrt{\textstyle\sum_{s\in\mathcal{B}_{t}}w_{s}^{2}}+w_{t}\textstyle\sum_{s\in\mathcal{B}_{t}}w_{s}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{18G^{2}}{\lambda}\,\left(\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\tfrac{1}{t}\right)\Big(\sigma_{\textnormal{max}}+(\tfrac{k\nu}{\delta_{T}/r})^{2}(1+\tfrac{\sigma_{\textnormal{max}}}{C})\Big)

\displaystyle=O\left(\tfrac{G^{2}}{\lambda}\left(\sigma_{\textnormal{max}}\ln T+T^{2/3}(\ln T)^{1/3}({1+\tfrac{\sigma_{\textnormal{max}}}{C}})^{1/3}(\nu k)^{2/3}\right)\right).

Combining the bounds above for
u=x^{*}
yields

\displaystyle\widebar{R}_{T}(x^{*})=O\left(\tfrac{G^{2}}{\lambda}\left(\sigma_{\textnormal{max}}\ln T+T^{2/3}(\ln T)^{1/3}({1+\tfrac{\sigma_{\textnormal{max}}}{C}})^{1/3}(\nu k)^{2/3}\right)\right).

This completes the proof.

Appendix F
Doubling Trick over Maximum Backlog

The strongly convex and bandit results of
Theorems

5.1
and
5.2
require tuning with knowledge of
\sigma_{\textnormal{max}}
, which may not be available in advance. Since the learning rates and smoothing parameters in all our results depend on the round index
t
but not on the horizon
T
, our base algorithms are anytime in
T
, and we need only double over
\sigma_{\textnormal{max}}
. This avoids the two-dimensional doubling over
(T,d_{\textnormal{tot}})
used by
[
5
]
.

Epoch structure.

Define backlog guesses
S_{j}=2^{j}-1
for
j\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. The wrapper initializes at epoch
j=0
and restarts the base algorithm with parameters tuned to maximum backlog
S_{j}
. Within each epoch, the algorithm runs until the observed backlog exceeds
S_{j}
, at which point the epoch ends,
j
is incremented, and the algorithm restarts with the updated guess. Upon starting a new epoch, the wrapper clears the tracking set, discarding any pending observations from the previous epoch. The final epoch index is
J=\lceil\,{\log_{2}(\sigma_{\textnormal{max}}+1)}\,\rceil
.

F.1
Doubling over
\sigma_{\textnormal{max}}

Suppose the base algorithm is anytime in
T
: for any backlog guess
\widetilde{S}
, on any interval of length
T^{\prime}
with maximum backlog at most
\widetilde{S}
, it guarantees regret at most

\displaystyle R(T^{\prime},\widetilde{S})\;\leq\;\varphi_{0}(T^{\prime})+\textstyle\sum_{m=1}^{M}\varphi_{m}(T^{\prime})\,(\widetilde{S}+1)^{b_{m}},

where
b_{m}>0
and each
\varphi_{m}:\mathbb{N}\to[0,\infty)
for
m\in\{0\}\cup[M]
is non-decreasing. Then the doubling wrapper guarantees

\displaystyle R(T,\sigma_{\textnormal{max}})\;=\;O\!\left(\varphi_{0}(T)\log(\sigma_{\textnormal{max}}\!+\!1)+\textstyle\sum_{m=1}^{M}\varphi_{m}(T)\,(\sigma_{\textnormal{max}}+1)^{b_{m}}\right).

Proof

The wrapper produces at most
J+1
epochs, indexed
j=0,1,\ldots,J
with
J=\lceil\,{\log_{2}(\sigma_{\textnormal{max}}+1)}\,\rceil
. Let
L_{j}
denote the number of rounds in epoch
j
. Since
L_{j}\leq T
and each
\varphi_{m}
is non-decreasing, the regret decomposes as

\displaystyle R(T,\sigma_{\textnormal{max}})\;\leq\;\textstyle\sum_{j=0}^{J}R(L_{j},S_{j})\;\leq\;(J\!+\!1)\,\varphi_{0}(T)\;+\;\textstyle\sum_{m=1}^{M}\varphi_{m}(T)\textstyle\sum_{j=0}^{J}2^{b_{m}j}.

The first term equals
O(\varphi_{0}(T)\log(\sigma_{\textnormal{max}}+1))
. For the second, since
b_{m}>0
, the geometric series satisfies
\textstyle\sum_{j=0}^{J}2^{b_{m}j}=O\big((\sigma_{\textnormal{max}}+1)^{b_{m}}\big)
. Summing over
m\in[M]
completes the proof.

Remark F.2

Every term in the regret bounds of
Theorems

5.1
and
5.2
that depends on
\sigma_{\textnormal{max}}
has
b_{m}>0
, so the geometric series absorbs the epoch count for these terms. The
\sigma_{\textnormal{max}}
-independent terms (captured by
\varphi_{0}
) incur a
\log(\sigma_{\textnormal{max}}+1)
overhead because, unlike
d_{\textnormal{tot}}
or
T
, the maximum backlog does not accumulate across epochs: each restart effectively begins a new game, and
\varphi_{0}
is charged in full for every epoch. The general convex OCO bound of
Theorem

5.1
does not depend on
\sigma_{\textnormal{max}}
; for it, we use the doubling trick of
[
5
]
over
T
and
d_{\textnormal{tot}}
instead.

Appendix G
Non-Preemptive Framework

[
39
]
also studied a more restrictive variant of capacity-constrained online learning in which preemption is disallowed: once a round enters the tracking set
\mathcal{S}
, it must remain until its feedback arrives. Our framework recovers this variant by restricting the support of proxy delay distributions
\mathfrak{D}_{t}
to
\{-1,\infty\}
(Section
4
), so that each round is either rejected outright (
d^{\prime}_{t}=-1
) or committed to until feedback delivery (
d^{\prime}_{t}=\infty
). The two-point schedulers in Corollaries
4.5
and
G.1
are non-preemptive in this sense.

Corollary G.1

Assume the framework is clairvoyant. For each
t\in[T]
, define
H_{t}=\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{t}\frac{1}{j}
and
p_{t}=\min\left\{1,\tfrac{C}{16H_{t}(d_{t}+1)}\right\}
. Let the proxy distribution
\mathfrak{D}_{t}\in\Delta(\{-1,\infty\})
be given by

\displaystyle\textstyle\mathbb{P}_{d^{\prime}\sim\mathfrak{D}_{t}}(d^{\prime}=\infty)=p_{t},\qquad\mathbb{P}_{d^{\prime}\sim\mathfrak{D}_{t}}(d^{\prime}=-1)=1-p_{t}.

Then,
\mathbb{P}(d^{\prime}_{t}\geq d_{t})=p_{t}
and
\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{S}_{t}|=C)\leq\exp(-C)
for every
t\in[T]
.

Proof

Fix
t\in[T]
. Since
\mathfrak{D}_{t}\in\Delta(\{-1,\infty\})
and
d_{t}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, we immediately have
\mathbb{P}(d^{\prime}_{t}\geq d_{t})=\mathbb{P}(d^{\prime}_{t}=\infty)=p_{t}
. The rest follows analogously to the proof of Corollary
4.4
.

Since both schedulers satisfy the same saturation and weight moment bounds as their preemptive counterparts, combining them with the analysis of Theorems
5.1
and
5.2
yields identical regret guarantees in the non-preemptive setting.
