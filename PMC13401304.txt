Known to crews, missing from the literature - electrostatic discharge during helicopter hoist operations
Authors: Jostein Rødseth Brede
Published: 2026

Background

Rescue hoist or underslung techniques are often used to access patients in helicopter search and rescue (SAR) and helicopter emergency medical services (HEMS). Episodes of electrostatic discharge (ESD) during these rescue operations are well-known for the personnel, commonly referred to as ‘static’. Helicopters generate considerable electrostatic charge during flight and aviation regulations recognize ESD as a risk [1–3]. Despite this, ESD frequency, causes, operational consequences or clinically meaningful outcomes for rescue personnel or patients are remarkably understudied.

Two recent nationwide Norwegian studies address this discrepancy and suggest that ESD should not merely be regarded as an occupational inconvenience, but as a clinical and operational safety risk [4, 5].

First, a cross-sectional survey among rescue paramedics and physicians demonstrated widespread exposure: all rescue paramedics and 73% of physicians reported previous ESD, often repeatedly, and 23% had experienced events classified as severe [4]. The findings were complemented by a nationwide prospective study in which 28 ESD events were registered during 839 hoist operations [5]. The consequences of ESD included severe pain, involuntary muscular or neurological symptoms, falls, impaired communication, medical assessment, and termination of hoist operations.

Consequences

The consequences of ESD may be more than just the electrical injury. The risk is determined not only by the energy transferred, but by the task being performed at the instant of discharge. During a hoist or underslung operation, communication failure or an involuntary movement caused by pain or shock may be potentially dangerous. Thus, a clinically minor event in another setting may substantially reduce the operational safety margin during a demanding rescue. ESD should therefore be seen as a systems-level risk that involves both aircraft, equipment, environment, and human performance.

The data does not establish chronic health effects. It does however establish ESD as a repeated occupational exposure and a mental strain – 24% of the personnel actively worry about the long-term health effects. This represents a previously unaddressed problem, sufficient to justify longitudinal studies on the health of rescue paramedics and physicians.

“Known” does not mean “understood”

The studies identify important uncertainties. The true incidence of ESD remains difficult to establish due to sampling bias, with incomplete reporting of both ESD events and uneventful operations. Environmental variables were not systematically recorded for operations without ESD; hence causality assessment of risk factors is limited. Importantly, these findings originate from the Norwegian SAR service and incidence from other HEMS or SAR services remain unreported. Although the physical mechanisms that create electrostatic charging are universal, exposure frequency and consequences may vary across the globe. Comparable data from other SAR and HEMS systems are required to establish an international scope of the problem.

Further, there is a lack of standardized definition or validated severity classification to distinguish clinical and mission consequences. The studies suggest a conflict between familiarity and concern; only 30% considered ESD as a problem for the service, however 69% were concerned that it could influence hoist operations. This may reflect a normalization of exposure, but underlying reasons cannot be established. Hence, a standardized, non-punitive way to report this is warranted.

Finally, in most reported incidents a discharge occurred despite reported prior contact between the discharge line and the surface. This does not demonstrate failure of antistatic equipment, but it does however justify a systematic evaluation of real-world performance of current mitigation devices. It should also encourage collaboration between rescue personnel, aerospace engineers and equipment manufacturers to develop and evaluate improved mitigation strategies.

Flight safety

These studies provide a reason to move ESD from an operational inevitability into formal safety science. Crew safety in HEMS and SAR is pivotal, however the studies demonstrate that ESD is a clinical risk, addressing a blind spot in both emergency medicine literature and safety protocols.

Rescue personnel should not need to experience ESD on a regular basis, as a “cost of doing business”. It is time for prehospital medicine to protect their own.
