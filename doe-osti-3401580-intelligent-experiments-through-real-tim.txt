Abstract

The overall vision of this project was to integrate real-time artificial intelligence (AI) directly into
the data acquisition and detector-control systems of nuclear physics experiments, including both
fast online event selection and an autonomous detector-control feedback loop. The work carried out
under the award focused on the fast online event-selection half of that vision: the efficient recording
of low-momentum heavy-flavor (HF) hadron decays in proton-proton collisions at the sPHENIX
experiment at the Relativistic Heavy Ion Collider (RHIC)—an observable that requires fast tracking
and topological trigger selection not previously demonstrated at RHIC, and that is essential for QCD
studies at future facilities such as the Electron-Ion Collider (EIC). The autonomous detector-control
(GPU-based feedback) component named in the project title remained a design concept and was
not implemented under this award. The Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) group led
the offline simulation and data processing needed to train the machine-learning (ML) models,
the translation of trained models to Field-Programmable Gate Array (FPGA) firmware using the
hls4ml framework, and the physics validation of heavy-flavor reconstruction. Over the award period,
the team developed and hardware-tested the principal components of an AI-based heavy-flavor
trigger on simulated and recorded sPHENIX tracker data: a software Bipartite Graph Attention
Network (BiGAT) trigger model reaching > 95% signal efficiency at 99% background rejection; an
FPGA-native hit clusterizer matching the offline clustering; smaller networks synthesized to FPGA
within the required sub-10 µs latency; and an assembled decoder–clusterizer–inference firmware
chain exercised on the FELIX readout board. A complete, fully integrated hardware demonstrator
was not finished within the award period. This report documents the project goals, the MIT group’s
contributions, the technical accomplishments, and the outlook toward applications at the future
EIC ePIC detector.

Executive Summary

All major high-energy nuclear and particle physics experiments face the challenge of processing the
very large data volumes generated by high-resolution, high-rate detectors, subject to hard limits on
data acquisition (DAQ) bandwidth and offline computing. This project addressed that challenge for
the sPHENIX experiment at RHIC by developing the components of a selective streaming-readout
system built around real-time, AI-based fast data processing, aimed at identifying rare physics
signals in real time while keeping the recorded data volume within the available DAQ and storage
budget. The project’s broader vision also included an autonomous detector-control feedback loop;
that component was not implemented under this award (see Section 1.1), and the work reported
here concerns the online event-selection system.
The scientific target was heavy-flavor (charm and beauty) hadron production in p+p collisions.
Heavy-flavor observables provide direct access to the strong interaction, and p+p measurements are
an essential reference for understanding the strongly coupled quark-gluon plasma (QGP) produced
in heavy-ion collisions. Selecting these low-momentum decays requires fast tracking and topological
triggering, which cannot be achieved with the traditional high-momentum trigger approach.
The MIT group, led by co-PI and sPHENIX co-spokesperson Gunther Roland, held primary
responsibility for three pillars of the work: (i) offline PYTHIA+GEANT simulation and offline data
processing used to train the ML models; (ii) the translation of trained neural networks to FPGA
firmware using the hls4ml framework co-developed by MIT team member Philip Harris; and (iii)
coordination and physics validation of heavy-flavor reconstruction. Within this scope, MIT team
members Cameron Dean and Hannah Bossi carried out central technical work: Dr. Dean developed
the heavy-flavor simulations, the initial software multilayer-perceptron (MLP) tagger used as the
performance baseline, and the sPHENIX-data validation using reconstructed KS0 decays; Drs. Dean
and Bossi jointly developed the FPGA-native hit clusterizer; and Dr. Bossi led the hls4ml firmware
translation and the FPGA implementation and optimization that brought the trigger latency below
the 10 µs requirement.
By the end of the award the team had: developed and hardware-tested the principal components
of an AI-based heavy-flavor trigger on simulated and recorded sPHENIX tracker data; designed
a software Bipartite Graph Attention Network (BiGAT) trigger model that reaches > 95% signal
efficiency at 99% background rejection and whose structure is amenable to FPGA implementation;
built the first FPGA-based decoder and clusterizer for the MVTX ALPIDE sensor data with 13.5 µm
spatial precision; synthesized smaller networks (a layer-wise MLP tagger and TrackGNN/FlowGNN
variants) to the FPGA within the required sub-10 µs latency; and exercised an assembled decoder–
clusterizer–inference firmware chain on the FELIX board. A complete, fully integrated demonstrator,
whose integration was primarily a national-laboratory responsibility, was not finished within the
award period. Although the unexpected early end of RHIC Run 23 (a RHIC magnet failure)
precluded a real-time in-beam demonstration, the work established the technical foundation for a
data-based demonstration in the final RHIC runs and for AI-based streaming readout at the future
EIC ePIC detector.

1 Background and Introduction

With an ever-increasing demand for high-precision data for discovery science and precision mea-
surements, all major high-energy nuclear and particle experiments confront the problem of how to

 1

handle the very large volume of raw data produced by sophisticated, high-rate detectors. These data
must be reconciled with the hardware and cost limits of the DAQ bandwidth and offline computing
resources available to capture, store, and process the events of interest. Two prototypical cases
motivate this work: the sPHENIX experiment—the DOE next-generation heavy-ion experiment at
RHIC—and the future EIC experiments planned to come online circa 2030 at BNL.
At sPHENIX, the RHIC interaction rate in p+p collisions reaches roughly 3 MHz, while the DAQ
bandwidth corresponds to about 15 kHz, requiring a data-rate reduction of more than a factor of
200. Some of the most important measurements—in particular heavy-flavor production in p+p
collisions—involve particles produced at low momentum. This precludes the traditional trigger
strategy of selecting rare, high-momentum probes: the heavy-flavor decay products of interest are
soft, and must instead be identified by fast tracking and by the displaced-vertex topology of the
decay. Recording these events therefore requires real-time reconstruction and topological trigger
selection inside the readout loop—a capability not previously demonstrated at RHIC.
To meet this challenge, the project developed a selective streaming-readout and control system
comprising AI-based fast data processing and autonomous detector control. The system is designed
to sample the full collision rate delivered by the accelerator while keeping the final data throughput
for offline storage within the available DAQ, storage, and computing budget. Two complementary
co-processors are integrated: NVIDIA GPUs for training and Xilinx FPGAs for inference. Trained
models are translated to FPGA firmware using industry-standard tools and the community-developed
hls4ml package, minimizing hardware complexity while maximizing the sampled luminosity for
discovery science. The system concept also foresees an anomaly-detection layer that would recognize
when detector or beam conditions have changed and require re-optimization of the online model,
closing an autonomous control loop around the online AI algorithms; this feedback layer was part of
the original design but was not implemented within the scope of this award.

1.1 Overall Project Goals and Objectives

The project aimed to transform data acquisition and detector control for nuclear physics experiments
by integrating advanced real-time AI directly into detector readout systems, specifically to enable
efficient recording of low-momentum heavy-flavor hadron decays in p+p collisions. Four major
objectives were defined:
1. Integration of FPGA-based online processing into the sPHENIX Streaming Readout
 (SRO) system. Working with the experts responsible for the readout of the MVTX and INTT
 silicon detectors of the sPHENIX inner tracking system, establish SRO for these detectors and
 enable data transfer to the FELIX FPGA used for real-time execution of ML-based tracking
 algorithms.
2. Design of advanced deep neural networks. Design, implement, and verify physics-aware
 neural-network models optimized for sparse, high-resolution detector data, subject to the memory
 and latency constraints of fast, low-power hardware such as the Xilinx FPGA on the FELIX
 processor board.
3. Deployment of real-time AI on FPGA hardware. Using hls4ml, transfer the deep neural
 networks to the FELIX-based DAQ system to achieve real-time reconstruction and identification
 of rare heavy-flavor events.
4. Implementation of autonomous feedback loops. As originally envisioned, a GPU-based
 monitoring system would continuously track beam and detector conditions to ensure robust,
 high-fidelity data collection under evolving experimental conditions. In practice, effort was

 concentrated on Objectives 1–3; a GPU-based monitoring/feedback system was not studied or
 implemented during the award, and this objective remains a direction for future work.
Together, the developments under Objectives 1–3 provide a first demonstration of AI-based heavy-
flavor event selection for sPHENIX and lay a technical foundation for future applications at the
EIC. The project was organized around four interconnected key tasks, shown in Figure 1.

3.1 Task 1: Physics Simulation and Offline Data Processing

Simulation data were produced with the sPHENIX Fun4All framework, an object-oriented framework
based on ROOT [1] that provides event generation, detector simulation, event reconstruction, and
analysis. The p+p events were generated with PYTHIA [2], and the generated particles were
propagated through the detectors with the GEANT4 [3] transport package, producing the position
and energy deposit (hits) of each particle in the sensitive detector regions. Hits are converted to
digits through a simulation of the detector response and stored in the raw-data format used by the
acquisition system, providing a common starting point for the reconstruction of both Monte Carlo
and real data. The MIT group led this effort, which supplied the labeled training samples for all
downstream ML development.

Simulation realism from 2024 p+p operations. In 2024 the sPHENIX collaboration recorded
a large sample of p+p collisions with both the MVTX and INTT in full streaming readout. This
sample and the operational experience it provided allowed the simulations to be brought into much
closer correspondence with reality. During the final year of the project the noise and dead-channel
simulations were improved by incorporating the true maps of detector regions that cannot be read
out (either excluded from the readout or with inoperable channels). Mechanical and optical survey
information for both detectors was used to place the sensors and material where they physically
reside, including an opening in the INTT vertical plane and a 5 mm westward displacement of the
MVTX relative to the collision point in the sPHENIX coordinate system. Because the ML models
are trained on these simulations, these refinements directly improve model accuracy on real data:
every local hit position now maps to the correct global position, and non-instrumented regions are
correctly represented in training.

From charm to beauty decays. In generating simulated heavy-flavor decays, the focus shifted
from open-charm to open-beauty decays. With the decrease in RHIC luminosity and collision rate,
together with the increase in the sPHENIX streaming rate from a planned 10% to a realized 30%,
sPHENIX is able to record the vast majority of the open-charm sample, which occurs in roughly one
of every 60 collisions. Beauty production is significantly rarer—roughly one in every 1000 collisions.
For the trigger models, the principal difference in the decay topology is the larger displacement of
the beauty-hadron decay vertex from the collision vertex, typically about three times larger than

 5

for charm. Concentrating the simulations on beauty decays therefore tunes the algorithms to tag
the rarer decays that the baseline sPHENIX data-acquisition model may not capture. This work
was carried out by the MIT group (Cameron Dean, with support from graduate student Hao-Ren
Jheng).

3.2 Task 2: Heavy-Flavor Tagging with Machine Learning

The heavy-flavor tagging effort produced an end-to-end pipeline that balances FPGA resource usage
against ML accuracy across a range of operating conditions, from idealized inputs (ground-truth
tracks, no pileup) to realistic experimental inputs (detected hits with pileup). The pipeline comprises
edge/tracklet candidate creation, tracklet classification with a graph neural network (GNN), track
prediction, an optional pileup-division stage, track construction, and a final trigger-prediction stage.

Bipartite Graph Attention Network and pileup division. A central algorithmic result
is the Bipartite Graph Attention Network (BiGAT), which handles the large MVTX integration
window—in which several overlapping events are read out together—by exploiting the high-resolution
timing of the INTT to separate the pileup into distinct events. This reduces the track reconstruction
complexity from quadratic to linear in the number of MVTX hits. The integrated tracking and
pileup-division algorithm retains a median of ∼ 70% of non-pileup (signal) hits per event, rejects
∼ 99% of out-of-time pileup hits, and retains ∼ 90% of the trigger hits for the majority of trigger
events, yielding an ∼ 20-fold improvement in signal-to-noise and an ∼ 20-fold reduction in data
volume in a twenty-event pileup scenario. As a software (offline) trigger model, the BiGAT identifies
events containing D0 and beauty-hadron decays with > 95% signal efficiency at 99% background
rejection, and its bipartite structure was designed to be amenable to FPGA implementation. The
BiGAT is the physics-performance model; it is distinct from the smaller networks actually synthesized
and executed on the FPGA (the layer-wise MLP tagger and the TrackGNN/FlowGNN variants of
Section 3.3).

Physics validation on real sPHENIX data (MIT). The MIT group led the physics validation
of the pipeline on recorded data. Using the 2024 p+p data, KS0 meson decays were reconstructed
with the full sPHENIX tracking and reconstruction software at very high purity. These decays are
topologically similar to the heavy-flavor decays the final system will select, but are more plentiful
and have a larger decay length (centimeters rather than millimeters), which makes them a practical
benchmark. Several such decays were extracted and translated into a form readable by the tracking
and triggering algorithms, allowing a direct comparison of the online methods to the current offline
methods used at sPHENIX. The BiGAT’s tracking stage was also shown to be effective at rejecting
the high beam-halo background present in the 2024 p+p run: because the simulation does not
model beam halo, the model’s selectivity was verified on data by comparing the ∆Z distributions of
accepted and rejected hits, where the additional rejection of high-∆Z candidates in real data—absent
in simulation—is consistent with halo removal.

Hardware-friendly taggers (MIT). In parallel with the full GNN pipeline, the MIT group
developed hardware-friendly models aligned with the available FPGA resources for the initial
firmware implementations, as the synthesis tooling for GNNs matured. These models operate
directly on detector clusters and do not require explicit track reconstruction. Two were defined: a

3.3 Task 3: hls4ml Firmware Translation and FPGA Implementation

The goal of Task 3 was to translate the trained neural networks into FPGA firmware that
meets the sPHENIX real-time constraints. The test system uses the same Front-End Link eX-
change (FELIX) PCIe boards used to read out the sPHENIX tracking detectors—specifically the
FELIX-712 board (the BNL-designed FLX-712) carrying an AMD/Xilinx Kintex UltraScale FPGA
(xcku115-flvf1924-2-e). This board is referred to as the FELIX-712 throughout this report. Two
FELIX boards are used: one interfaces the DAQ and the detector front-end electronics, and the
second (the “AI engine”) performs the real-time selection of the desired heavy-flavor decay topologies.
The central challenge is that the AI engine must reach a trigger decision in ∼ 10 µs while fitting
within the FPGA resource budget of the FELIX board. As experts on the hls4ml framework, the
MIT group led the development and testing of the FPGA implementation of the AI models.

From offline model to firmware. The MIT-led translation follows the hls4ml workflow shown
in Figure 2: a model trained in PyTorch Geometric is serialized, converted to an HLS project, and
synthesized through the Vivado backend to C synthesis, logic synthesis, and IP export. hls4ml [5] is
a compiler that takes a model expressed in Keras, PyTorch, or ONNX and automatically produces
HLS code implementing the network as spatial dataflow. The MVTX-only MLP layer-wise tagger
was successfully synthesized, with the aggregation and prediction networks synthesized separately
using Vitis HLS and Vivado 2024.1; the resulting resource utilization on the FELIX-712 board is
given in Table 3.

Two synthesis paths and latency. Two complementary approaches to deploying the ML models
on FPGA were pursued. The first uses FlowGNN [6], a flexible dataflow architecture for GNN
acceleration in which a PyTorch Geometric GNN is manually translated into C++ HLS using
FlowGNN building blocks and then synthesized with Vitis HLS. An early track-construction GNN
with 92 nodes and 142 edges achieved a measured latency of 8.82 µs per graph at a 285 MHz clock;
a later version performing the triggering directly from clustered hits achieved 9.2 µs per graph at
180 MHz. Recent developments extended FlowGNN to additional GNN types (e.g., EdgeConv)
and improved the PyTorch-to-Verilog conversion using GNNBuilder [7]. The second approach uses
hls4ml directly for automatic translation. The MIT team led the optimization that brought the
online model within the timing budget: initial testing showed excessive resource utilization and
timing, and further work, including simplification of the TrackGNN implementation, achieved the
required latency of less than 10 µs using HLS techniques and the hls4ml conversion of models such
as the hit-based MLP taggers and the graph-level aggregation trigger networks. Figure 3 shows the
measured per-graph latency of the improved model as a function of graph size. This optimization
was led by Hannah Bossi with Philip Harris.

3.4 Task 4: Demonstrator Implementation

The demonstrator integration was primarily the responsibility of the collaborating national-laboratory
groups (LANL and ORNL) rather than the MIT team, and a complete, fully integrated demonstrator
was not finished within the award period; the summary below reports the collaboration-wide status,
with the MIT-specific contributions (the FPGA-native clusterizer and the standalone FELIX testbed)
noted explicitly. The largest engineering challenge of the project was to fit the AI logic onto the
target FELIX-712 FPGA (Kintex UltraScale) while satisfying the timing requirements for data
processing and AI inference. The demonstrator comprises PCIe communication and optical data
links, data decoding, data clustering, event building, coordinate transformation, and the AI inference
module. Individual modules were validated in simulation, on the FPGA using data provided through
the FELIX PCIe interface, with MVTX telescope data, and with the full MVTX detector. A
conversion macro turns real fully streamed MVTX p+p data collected by sPHENIX in 2024 into a
raw data stream that can be sent to the board at up to 65% of the real detector rate. The MIT
group operated a standalone FELIX testbed to support this development and led the clusterizer
effort described below.

FPGA-native hit clusterizer (MIT). A key MIT contribution, developed jointly by Cameron
Dean and Hannah Bossi, is a new hit-clustering method that reconstructs clusters directly on the
FPGA and is compatible with the data-delivery scheme, minimizing both resource usage and latency.
The clusterizer is implemented in the HLS workflow and provides 13.5 µm spatial precision. It
was originally anticipated from simulation that the mean cluster size would be four pixels, so the
maximum cluster size on the FPGA was set to six; with the 2024 data the mean cluster size was
found to be 7.3 pixels, so the maximum was increased to 15, which doubled the required FPGA
resources. A single clusterizer unit uses 3802 LUT and 2961 FF. Figure 4 compares the FPGA

Decoder, event building, and coordinate transformation. The demonstrator includes the
first FPGA-based decoder for the MVTX ALPIDE sensor data, consisting of a data-frame decoder,
an ALPIDE sensor decoder, and memories to collect decoded frames and pixels; a single FeeID
decoder (3 ALPIDE chips) uses 1375 LUT, 1346 FF, and 6 BRAM, and takes on average ∼ 2.5
RHIC clock cycles (∼ 250 ns) to decode a single hit. With the MVTX-only setup the event building
is straightforward; adding the INTT stream introduces a frame-matching challenge because the
MVTX (5 µs) and INTT (12.7 µs) readout frames have no small least common multiple. The PCIe
interface, originally used to deliver optical-link data to the DAQ computer, was reconfigured to send
real detector data from the computer to the FPGA for controlled validation.

Firmware-chain test. A firmware chain assembling the VHDL-based MVTX decoders, the
HLS-based clusterizer, and an hls4ml neural network (the MVTX-only layer-wise MLP tagger, run
untrained as a data-flow test rather than for physics performance) was assembled on a single FELIX
board and exercised both in the Vivado simulator and in hardware via the PCIe uplink. In one
test the input of 9 events containing 675 pixel hits produced 150 clusters and 9 neural-network
outputs (one per event), matching the expected counts and confirming correct data flow through
the assembled stages. This test verified the decoder–clusterizer–inference chain at the level of
data integrity and counts; it was not a physics benchmark, and did not constitute a complete,
production-ready integrated demonstrator, which remained unfinished at the end of the award.

4 Status at End of Grant and Outlook

Overall, the project team developed and hardware-tested the principal components of AI-based
heavy-flavor triggering based on simulated and recorded tracker data of the sPHENIX experiment.

As a software trigger model, the Bipartite Graph Attention Network (BiGAT) reaches > 95% signal
efficiency at 99% background rejection, and its structure is amenable to FPGA implementation. The
team assembled a firmware chain—VHDL-based decoders, an HLS-based clusterizer, and a smaller
hls4ml network—on the FELIX board and exercised it in simulation and hardware with test event
loads, though a complete integrated demonstrator was not finished within the award period.
While the unexpected end of RHIC Run 23, caused by a RHIC magnet failure, did not allow a
real-time in-beam demonstration of AI-based heavy-flavor event selection, the work described here
laid the groundwork for a data-based demonstration in the final RHIC runs and for applications in
the future EIC ePIC experiment. Toward the EIC, the team has already studied porting the lpGBT
firmware and its integration with the front-end electronics: because current ePIC-family FELIX
prototypes are in short supply, a commercial FPGA development board based on the same FPGA
family was used to port and verify the lpGBT firmware against an lpGBT development board
carrying the lpGBT ASIC and the VTRx+, with data multiplexing toward the AI FPGA under
development. The technology developed here also enables work toward a DIS-electron tagger using
EIC simulation data and hardware-accelerated AI pipelines, reusing the sPHENIX heavy-flavor
GNN framework with silicon-tracker and electromagnetic-calorimeter inputs.

6 Conclusions

This project developed and hardware-tested the principal components of a real-time, AI-based
heavy-flavor triggering system for the sPHENIX experiment and established the technical foundation
for its extension to the future EIC ePIC detector; a complete, integrated in-beam demonstration was
not carried out within the award. The MIT group led the offline simulation and data processing used
to train the models, the hls4ml translation of trained networks to FPGA firmware, and the physics
validation of heavy-flavor reconstruction, and it operated a standalone FELIX testbed in support
of the development. MIT team members Cameron Dean and Hannah Bossi carried out central
technical work: Dr. Dean developed the heavy-flavor simulations, the initial software MLP tagger
used as the performance baseline, and the KS0 -based validation on 2024 sPHENIX data; Drs. Dean
and Bossi jointly developed the FPGA-native hit clusterizer; and Dr. Bossi led the hls4ml firmware
translation and the FPGA optimization that met the sub-10 µs latency requirement.
The team designed AI-based, physics-aware deep neural networks for real-time event reconstruction
and heavy-flavor tagging, integrating NVIDIA GPUs for training and Xilinx FPGAs for inference
through the community-developed hls4ml framework and standard HLS tools. As a software trigger
model the BiGAT reaches > 95% signal efficiency at 99% background rejection; the smaller networks
synthesized to the FPGA (the layer-wise MLP tagger and TrackGNN/FlowGNN variants) meet
the sub-10 µs latency requirement within the FPGA resource budget; the FPGA-native decoder
and clusterizer reproduce the sPHENIX offline clustering at 13.5 µm precision; and an assembled
decoder–clusterizer–inference firmware chain was exercised on the FELIX board. Although the early
end of RHIC Run 23 precluded an in-beam real-time demonstration, the components developed
here position the system for a data-based demonstration in the final RHIC runs, and the methods
and firmware developed here are applicable to AI-based streaming readout for the EIC.
