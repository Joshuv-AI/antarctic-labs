Outcomes of 3D-printed titanium cages for surgical reconstruction in infectious spondylodiscitis: a systematic review
Authors: Wentian Li, Akina Li, Gayani Petersingham, Prashanth J Rao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInfectious spondylodiscitis remains a complex spinal condition that may require surgical debridement and interbody reconstruction in cases of instability, deformity, or failure of medical management. Conventional reconstructive options include titanium mesh cages (TMC) and polyetheretherketone (PEEK) cages; however, three-dimensional printed titanium (3DPT) cages have been increasingly adopted due to their porous architecture and potential to enhance osseointegration. The clinical performance of 3DPT cages in the setting of spinal infection has not been well defined. This study aimed to qualitatively summarise the currently available direct and contextual evidence regarding the use of 3DPT cages for spinal reconstruction in infectious spondylodiscitis.MethodsA systematic literature search of EMBASE, MEDLINE, and Cochrane Library was conducted up to December 2025. Studies reporting outcomes following spinal reconstruction using 3DPT cages in adult patients with infectious spondylodiscitis were included. Surgical parameters, radiological outcomes (including fusion and subsidence), and clinical outcomes were extracted and synthesised qualitatively due to heterogeneity in study design and outcome reporting.ResultsA total of seven studies were included in this review. Among these, only one study directly evaluated 3DPT cages specifically in infectious spondylodiscitis, while the remaining studies provided indirect or contextual evidence from mixed-pathology or degenerative spinal cohorts. Overall, the available evidence remains highly limited and heterogeneous, with substantial variability in study design, outcome reporting, and follow-up duration.ConclusionsOnly limited direct evidence is currently available regarding the use of 3DPT cages in infectious spondylodiscitis. While early reports suggest that these implants may be feasible for spinal reconstruction, the current evidence base is highly limited, indirect, and heterogeneous, preventing definitive conclusions regarding clinical effectiveness or superiority over conventional interbody devices. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than practice-changing. Future well-designed prospective and comparative studies with standardised outcome measures are required to better define their role and enable meaningful meta-analysis.Keywords: Infectious spondylodiscitis, spinal infection, interbody reconstruction, three-dimensional printed titanium cage (3DPT cage), spinal fusion

Introduction

Infectious spondylodiscitis is a complex spinal infection involving the intervertebral disc and adjacent vertebral endplates. Initial management typically involves targeted antimicrobial therapy, pain control, spinal immobilisation or bracing where appropriate, and close clinical and radiological monitoring. Surgical intervention may be required in cases of spinal instability, deformity, neurological compromise, abscess formation, or failure of medical management (1). Common procedures include anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) and lumbar interbody fusion techniques such as anterior lumbar interbody fusion (ALIF), and transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF). These procedures utilise interbody cages to restore disc height, provide anterior column support, and facilitate bony fusion (2).

Interbody reconstruction plays a critical role following debridement. Titanium mesh cages (TMC) and polyetheretherketone (PEEK) cages have traditionally been used for this purpose and have demonstrated acceptable fusion rates and clinical outcomes in both degenerative and infective spinal pathology (3). However, concerns remain regarding subsidence, stress shielding, and suboptimal osseointegration, particularly in the setting of compromised bone quality and infection-related endplate destruction (4).

Recent advances in three-dimensional (3D) printing technology have enabled the development of porous titanium cages with tailored architecture, elastic modulus closer to cancellous bone, and enhanced surface characteristics that promote bony ingrowth (5,6). These biomechanical and biological properties suggest potential advantages in spinal reconstruction, particularly in infected environments where stable fixation and rapid osseointegration are critical (7).

While 3D printed titanium (3DPT) cages have been increasingly adopted in degenerative spinal surgery, their role in infectious spondylodiscitis remains less clearly defined (8,9). Existing studies are limited by small sample sizes, heterogeneous methodologies, and variable reporting of radiological and clinical outcomes. A focused synthesis of the available evidence is therefore warranted. However, the currently available literature remains extremely limited, with very few studies directly evaluating 3DPT cages specifically in infectious spondylodiscitis.

This review summarises the current studies examining the surgical, radiological, and clinical outcomes of 3DPT cages in the treatment of infectious spondylodiscitis across cervical, thoracic, and lumbar levels. Timely and effective management of spondylodiscitis remains challenging, particularly in patients with structural instability, making the choice of interbody device critical for both infection control and long-term spinal stability. We present this article in accordance with the PRISMA reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-2026-1-0017/rc).

Methods

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across EMBASE, MEDLINE, and Cochrane Library through December 2025. The literature search incorporated combinations of terms related to infectious spondylodiscitis, spinal infection, 3DPT cages, porous titanium cages, interbody fusion, and spinal reconstruction using Boolean operators (AND/OR). Detailed electronic search strategies for each database are provided in Appendix 1.

Included adult patients (≥18 years) undergoing surgical treatment for infectious spondylodiscitis.

Additional studies involving other cage types or mixed cohorts were included only for contextual interpretation and were not considered primary evidence for the review objective. Studies not directly evaluating 3DPT cages in infectious spondylodiscitis were included solely to provide background regarding reconstructive strategies and radiographic outcomes in related spinal settings and were not interpreted as direct evidence supporting clinical effectiveness in infection.

The following were excluded: case reports, conference abstracts, editorials, and narrative reviews; animal or cadaveric studies; studies not reporting outcomes specific to interbody cage use; studies focusing exclusively on degenerative pathology without an infectious cohort.

The initial search yielded 287 records, of which 208 remained after duplicate removal. After title and abstract screening, 133 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 126 were excluded for reasons including absence of a 3DPT cage arm, non-infectious or degenerative populations, conference abstracts, editorials or letters, or lack of alignment with the review objective. A total of seven studies were included in this review, with variations in cage materials, spinal levels treated, and follow-up durations. Data extraction focused on surgical, radiological, and clinical outcomes reported for patients undergoing interbody reconstruction. Risk of bias assessment

Methodological quality of the included observational studies was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS). Studies were evaluated across three domains: selection, comparability, and outcome assessment. Given the heterogeneity in study design and outcome reporting, the assessment was used to provide an overall appraisal of study quality rather than for quantitative weighting. Detailed NOS assessment results are provided in Table S1.

The review protocol was not prospectively registered in PROSPERO, which represents a methodological limitation of the present review.

Data synthesis

Owing to anticipated heterogeneity in spinal region, surgical approach, comparator type, follow-up duration, and outcome definitions, a meta-analysis was not planned. Findings were therefore synthesised narratively and organised by outcome domain (surgical, radiological, and clinical outcomes). Where relevant, differences in cervical vs. thoracolumbar/lumbar cohorts and comparative vs. non-comparative study designs were highlighted to avoid inappropriate direct comparisons.

Overall, the methodological quality of included studies was moderate. Most studies were retrospective in design and limited by small sample sizes, heterogeneous outcome reporting, and relatively short follow-up durations.

To improve interpretability, terminology and reporting formats were standardised across the outcome tables where possible.

Results

In this literature review, we measured the operative time, intraoperative blood loss, and length of hospital stay, which were variably reported across studies (Table 1). Deng et al. conducted a prospective trial comparing 3DPT and PEEK cages in 76 patients (36 ACDF, 40 TLIF). No statistically significant differences were observed in operative time, blood loss, or length of hospital stay between groups, although 3DPT cages were associated with slightly longer procedures, reduced blood loss, and shorter hospital stays in both ACDF and TLIF cohorts. No complications were reported in either group at 3- and 6-month follow-up (10). In a comparative study of 30 patients undergoing ALIF, Selim et al. found no significant differences in operative duration, hospital stay, or complication rates between the TMC group (n=15) and the autogenous iliac bone graft group (n=15) (11). Similarly, Schomacher et al. reported no perioperative complications or revisions in their series, which included patients treated with either TMC (n=21) or PEEK cages (n=16) across cervical, thoracic, and lumbar levels (12).

Of the studies on titanium cages alone, Heo et al. retrospectively reviewed 19 patients and reported one postoperative death due to acute respiratory distress syndrome. No other complications occurred within the 6-month follow-up period (13). Yen et al. reported a mean hospital stay of 5.8 days (range, 3–8 days) and no complications in 4 lumbar patients (14).

Kim et al. conducted a retrospective study involving 83 patients who underwent single-level minimally invasive TLIF (MIS-TLIF), with 40 patients receiving 3DPT and 43 patients receiving PEEK cages. The study found no significant differences between the two groups in terms of operative time, intraoperative blood loss, or complication rates. Notably, there were no reported cases of intraoperative dural tears, infections, or cage migrations in either group (9) (Table 2).

The highest reported incidence of complications was in the study by Cucchi et al., with four intra-operative complications: three injuries to the dural sac and one injury to the presacral venous plexus, five wound healing problems requiring revision, one relapse requiring revision, and two cases of adjacent segment degeneration, one requiring revision (15). However, this variation in complication incidence is likely due to inconsistency in what was considered a complication—for example, Cucchi et al. included intraoperative events that were successfully resolved during the surgery, and Selim et al., who also had a higher incidence, included kyphosis (11,15).

Overall, reported surgical complication and revision rates were low across included studies; however, interpretation is limited by heterogeneous study designs and small sample sizes.

Radiological outcomes

Radiographic parameters such as segmental angle correction, IH, subsidence, and fusion status were inconsistently reported across studies (Table 3). Four studies reported radiological outcome metrics. Again, there was heterogeneity in the exact variables reported. Schomacher et al. reported average relative segmental correction as a postoperative/preoperative ratio to account for differences in spinal locations. The ratio was 1.87±1.78 in the PEEK group and 1.574±0.94 in the TMC group, which was not a statistically significant difference (P=0.746) (12). Selim et al. reported to have assessed kyphotic and Cobb angles, but the exact pre- and post-operative figures were not reported, and the difference between the TMC group and autogenous iliac bone graft group was not statistically significant (11). Heo et al. reported an average 6.96° improvement in segmental angle from 20.07 degrees (range 4.5° to 35.7°); postoperative figures were not provided. However, at follow-up (average 11.16 months, range between 4 and 64 months), there was a mean loss of correction of 4.86° (P=0.031) compared to immediately after the operation (13). Yen et al. found statistically significant improvements in the mean lumbar lordosis from 18° to 51° (P=0.006) and segmental angle from 6.5° to 18° (P=0.01). Other metrics also showed improvement but not to a statistically significant degree, including reduction of sagittal vertical axis from 11.3 to 8.4 cm (P=0.126), coronal shift from 2.8 to 0.9 cm (P=0.195), and Cobb angle from 8.8° to 2.8° (P=0.262).(14) Wang et al. evaluated cervical radiological parameters in patients undergoing three-level ACDF with 3DPT cages. The segmental angle improved from 3.91°±2.17° preoperatively to 8.76°±1.87° postoperatively (P<0.001), while disc height increased from 5.10±0.93 to 6.17±0.83 mm (P<0.001). All patients achieved bony fusion by the final follow-up (mean, 25.6 months), with no reported cage subsidence or migration (16). Kim et al. compared 3DPT and PEEK cages in MIS-TLIF. Both groups showed similar postoperative maintenance of disc and foraminal height. At 12-month follow-up, radiographic fusion was achieved in 92.5% of the 3DPT group and 90.7% of the PEEK group. Subsidence occurred in both groups at a comparable rate, with no statistically significant difference and no cases of cage migration (9).

In the comparative studies, Deng et al. assessed fusion using the Brantigan, Stefee’s grade and union area ratio (UAR). Fusion was defined as Grade IV or higher. In ACDF patients, fusion rates were comparable between 3DPT and PEEK cages at both 3 months (96.2% vs. 83.3%, P=0.182) and 6 months (100% vs. 91.7%, P=0.225). UAR was consistently higher in the 3DPT group than in the PEEK group, but the difference was only statistically significant in the coronal lower endplate at 3 months after surgery (83.8±11.0 vs. 73.6±13.1) (P=0.005). In the TLIF patients, there was also no statistically significant difference in fusion rate at 3 months (84.6% vs. 58.3%) (P=0.059) or 6 months (92.3% vs. 75%) (P=0.132). UAR was significantly higher in the 3DPT group than the PEEK group in all areas at 6 months. More patients in the 3DPT group achieved grade V fusion, but the difference was not statistically significant (10). Schomacher et al. reported 90.5% fusion rate in the PEEK group and 100% of the titanium group (P=0.204) at minimum 6-month follow-up (12). Selim et al. recorded fusion according to the Bridwell scale and also found no significant difference between the TMC group and the autologous iliac bone graft group (fusion scores: 9.3±3.1 vs. 8.7±2.7 cm, P=0.576), although the meaning of these numerical values was not clarified (11). In the studies on TMC alone, Cucchi et al. reported 87% of patients achieved good fusion; however, it was not clear if this number was in the infective group, degenerative group, or across both (15). Yen et al. reported complete fusion in all four patients treated with 3DPT cages (14). Wang et al. evaluated fusion in 25 patients undergoing three-level ACDF with 3DPT cages. All patients achieved bony fusion by a mean follow-up of 25.6 months, with no reports of cage migration, implant failure, or pseudoarthrosis. Radiographs demonstrated continuous bridging bone across all levels (16). Kim et al. assessed fusion in patients undergoing MIS-TLIF and reported a fusion rate of 92.5% in the 3DPT group and 90.7% in the PEEK group at 12 months, with no statistically significant difference. No cases of cage migration or implant failure were observed (9).

Subsidence

Subsidence was reported in six studies, typically defined as a postoperative loss of IH of ≥2 mm. However, threshold definitions and measurement protocols varied considerably, limiting comparability (Table 5). In the study by Deng et al., IH was measured 1 week, 3 months, and 6 months postoperatively. In the ACDF group, subsidence was significantly lower in the 3DPT group compared to the PEEK group at both 3 months (0.4±0.2 vs. 0.9±0.7 mm) (P=0.004) and 6 months (0.7±0.3 vs. 1.5±0.8 mm) (P<0.001). The 3DPT group had no incidence of subsidence, whereas the PEEK group had 2 cases at 3-month follow-up (8.33%) and 3 cases at 6 months (12.5%). In the TLIF group, subsidence was lower in the 3DPT group compared to the PEEK group both at 3 months (0.9±0.7 vs. 1.2±0.9 mm) (P=0.136) and 6 months (1.6±1.0 vs. 2.0±1.0 mm) (P=0.200) but not to a statistically significant degree. However, IH was significantly higher in the 3DPT group than in the PEEK group at both 3 months (12.8±2.0 vs. 11.5±2.0 mm) (P=0.020) and 6 months (12.1±2.2 vs. 10.7±1.9 mm) (P = 0.017). Subsidence rates were not significantly different at 3 months (15.4% vs. 16.7%) (P=1) or 6 months (34.6% vs. 37.5%) (P=1) (10). Schomacher et al. reported subsidence incidence of 66.67% in the PEEK group and 75% in the TMC group, but this did not reach statistical significance (P=0.583) (12). Selim et al. found no statistically significant difference in decrease in IH between the TMC group (1.9±0.6 cm) and autogenous iliac bone graft group (2.1±0.7 cm) (P=0.563). Notably, however, these values were approximately an order of magnitude higher than reported in other studies and across literature. This was not discussed by the authors, and it is unclear if the numbers are accurate or if it was a reporting error in units of measurement (mm instead of m, for example, which would be more in line with findings of other studies) (11). Yen et al. reported no incidence of subsidence (14). Wang et al. documented an average subsidence of 0.9±0.6 mm in patients undergoing three-level ACDF with 3DPT cages, with no cases of cage migration or radiographic failure (16). Kim et al. reported no statistically significant difference in subsidence between 3DPT and PEEK cages following MIS-TLIF at 12 months. No clinically significant migration or implant failure was observed (9).

Clinical outcomes were variably reported across studies, with outcome measures including pain (VAS or PainDETECT), neurological function (JOA or Frankel grade), disability indices (ODI, NDI, or RMDQ), general physical function (SF-12), and patient satisfaction (Odom’s criteria) (Table 6).

Cucchi et al. observed statistically significant improvements across all reported domains. VAS was reported in mm rather than score, with a reduction of from a median of 90 mm preoperatively to 20 mm (P<0.0001) postoperatively, translating to a score of 9 and 2, respectively. Median PainDETECT score decreased from 10 to 4 (P=0.0039). Intense lower back pain also decreased in radiation from a median of 90 to 22.5 mm (P<0.0001). Level of disability was measured with ODI, decreased significantly from median 61 to 15 (P<0.0001), and RMDQ, which decreased significantly from median 15 to 5 (P<0.0001). Patient function as reported on the SF-12 improved significantly from a median of 34.5 to 39 (P<0.0001) (15). Deng et al. found no statistically significant difference in VAS, JOA, NDI/ODI, SF-12, or Odom’s criteria across all groups. However, in the ACDF 3DPT group, mean VAS decreased from 4.3±2.0 to 1.0±1.2 at 6 months, JOA improved from 14.9±2.0 to 16.5±1.1, NDI decreased from 22.4±13.3 to 10.8±10.3, and SF-12 improved from 61.4±20.4 to 78.6±14.9. In the TLIF patients, mean VAS decreased from 5.3±1.9 to 1.9±1.7, JOA improved from 15.8±6.2 to 25.0±5.6, ODI decreased from 39.5±17.6 to 14.1±14.7, and SF-12 increased from 51.6±16.3 to 74.6±18.1 (10). Heo et al. reported that mean VAS score improved from 7.8 to 2.4. Neurological function was assessed via the Frankel grade. Preoperatively, there were 2 patients classified as type A, 2 as type C, and 15 as type D. Postoperatively, the neurological status improved by 1 grade in 7 patients, but the exact numbers were not reported (i.e., how many from each grade improved) (13). Yen et al. reported mean back VAS improved from 9.5 to 1.5 (P=0.006), and leg pain VAS score improved from 8.3 to 1 (P=0.007). Mean ODI improved significantly from 68.5 to 23 (P=0.0003) (14). Schomacher et al. did not report clinical outcomes (12). Selim et al. reported no statistically significant difference between TMC and autogenous iliac bone graft groups in VAS, Frankel’s grade, or ODI, but did not provide the pre- and post-operative scores, or how many points they improved by (11).

Wang et al. assessed clinical outcomes using JOA, NDI, and SF-12. Mean JOA scores improved significantly from 13.1±2.5 to 15.5±2.3 (P<0.05), and NDI scores decreased from 34.7±7.6 to 13.8±6.3 (P<0.001). SF-12 physical component scores increased from 31.5±5.9 to 41.1±4.8 (P<0.001), indicating marked improvement in cervical function and quality of life at final follow-up (16).

Kim et al. reported that both groups experienced improvement in VAS and ODI at 12 months post-MIS-TLIF, with no statistically significant differences between the 3DPT and PEEK groups. In the 3DPT group, VAS decreased from 6.3±1.9 to 2.2±1.4 (P<0.001), and ODI improved from 54.6±15.7 to 22.6±10.2 (P<0.001). The PEEK group showed similar reductions: VAS from 6.5±2.1 to 2.4±1.3, and ODI from 56.2±17.4 to 24.8±9.5 (9).

Discussion

As 3DPT is a novel and developing technology, this systematic review summarises the currently available evidence evaluating the use of 3DPT cages for interbody reconstruction in infectious spondylodiscitis. There are increasing studies suggesting its potential advantages in various spinal procedures, but few specifying what condition was being treated, and papers specifically evaluating its outcomes in infectious spondylodiscitis. The potential advantages of 3DPT cages may be related to their porous architecture, which facilitates osseointegration and promotes bone in-growth. This may be particularly relevant in the setting of infection, where achieving stable fusion and biological incorporation is critical. In addition, their structural properties may allow improved load distribution and reduce the risk of subsidence. Recent studies have further supported the role of 3DPT cages in enhancing osseointegration and mechanical stability in spinal reconstruction, particularly in complex or infection-related settings (18-21).

Several material properties of 3DPT cages may contribute to their potential advantages in the setting of spinal infection. The elastic modulus of porous titanium structures can be tailored to more closely approximate that of cancellous bone, potentially reducing stress shielding and improving load sharing. Furthermore, the ability to engineer controlled micro- and submicron-scale structures enhances biological fixation and supports bony ingrowth.

Importantly, these characteristics may be particularly relevant in infectious environments, where stable fusion plays a critical role in eradicating infection. Improved osseointegration and fusion may help restore spinal stability and reduce the persistence of infection. While titanium itself is not inherently antimicrobial, its surface properties and ability to support rapid bone incorporation may indirectly contribute to improved infection control. However, these proposed advantages remain largely theoretical and require further validation in clinical studies.

A key limitation of the current literature is the substantial heterogeneity across included studies. The reviewed studies encompassed different surgical approaches, including ACDF, anterior corpectomy and fusion, and MIS-TLIF across cervical, thoracic, and lumbar regions. Study designs were predominantly retrospective and frequently non-comparative, and outcome reporting varied considerably between studies. In particular, different patient-reported outcome measures were used across studies, including VAS, ODI, NDI, SF-12, and PainDETECT, limiting the ability to directly compare clinical outcomes. This methodological heterogeneity precluded quantitative pooling of results and therefore a qualitative synthesis of findings was performed. Some of the studies included were less reliable than ideal, and so the findings should be interpreted with caution. For example, the study by Yen et al. had a very small sample of only four patients (14). Moreover, reporting of the method and variables lacked detail and rigour in the paper by Selim et al. (11).

As 3DPT technology continues to evolve, an increasing number of studies have demonstrated its overall effectiveness and favorable mechanical and biological properties in various spinal procedures. However, few have specifically addressed its role in the treatment of infectious spondylodiscitis, limiting the clinical generalizability of these findings.

A major limitation of the current evidence base is that only one included study directly evaluated 3DPT cages specifically in infectious spondylodiscitis. Most of the available literature consists of indirect or contextual evidence derived from degenerative cohorts, mixed pathology populations, or studies involving conventional interbody devices such as TMC or PEEK cages. As a result, the present review should not be interpreted as evidence of definitive clinical effectiveness or superiority of 3DPT cages in the setting of spinal infection. Rather, the available findings primarily highlight the early and exploratory nature of current evidence and support the need for further dedicated investigation. Future studies should also incorporate more standardised functional recovery and rehabilitation-related outcome measures to better evaluate long-term patient recovery following spinal reconstruction. Persistent spinal pain and functional impairment remain important contributors to long-term disability and reduced quality of life across a wide range of spinal disorders, further highlighting the importance of comprehensive postoperative recovery assessment beyond radiographic outcomes alone (22).

Compared with conventional interbody devices such as PEEK cages or TMC, 3DPT implants may offer theoretical advantages in biological integration and structural stability. However, given the lack of high-quality comparative data, these potential benefits should be interpreted with caution. Inclusion of indirect and contextual studies may also introduce interpretative bias, particularly when extrapolating findings from degenerative or mixed-pathology cohorts to the setting of spinal infection. A key challenge in synthesizing available evidence lies in the heterogeneity of reported outcome measures and methodological rigor. Variability in how fusion was assessed, definitions of subsidence, and follow-up duration complicate cross-study comparisons. Additionally, the quality of included studies was uneven. For example, Yen et al. reported promising results but included only four patients, raising concerns about statistical reliability (14). Similarly, Selim et al. lacked methodological transparency, omitting detailed data on fusion grading and clinical outcomes (11).

Overall, the current evidence remains insufficient to support definitive conclusions regarding the clinical role of 3DPT cages in infectious spondylodiscitis. Future prospective and comparative studies with standardized outcome reporting are required to better define their safety, radiographic performance, and long-term clinical effectiveness.

Limitations of the evidence

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the number of studies specifically addressing 3DPT cage use in infectious spondylodiscitis remains small, and most are retrospective in design. Sample sizes were limited, and patient populations were heterogeneous with respect to infection severity, spinal level, surgical approach, and adjunctive fixation strategies. Additionally, heterogeneity in outcome measures and study design limited direct comparison between studies and prevented quantitative meta-analysis. Second, outcome reporting was inconsistent, particularly for radiological parameters such as subsidence and fusion grading. Third, follow-up durations were often short, limiting assessment of long-term implant performance and recurrence risk. Finally, publication bias cannot be excluded, as studies reporting unfavourable outcomes may be underrepresented.

Future directions

Future research should focus on prospective, multicentre studies comparing 3DPT cages with established interbody devices using standardised surgical protocols and outcome measures. Uniform definitions of fusion and subsidence, longer follow-up periods, and inclusion of infection-specific outcomes such as recurrence and implant-related complications will be essential. Such data are required to determine whether the theoretical advantages of 3DPT cages translate into clinically meaningful benefits in the management of infectious spondylodiscitis.

Conclusions

The current literature provides only extremely limited direct evidence regarding the use of 3DPT cages in infectious spondylodiscitis. Although early reports suggest potential feasibility in spinal reconstruction, the available evidence remains insufficient to draw definitive conclusions regarding clinical effectiveness, fusion performance, or superiority over conventional interbody devices. Future prospective and comparative studies specifically focused on infectious spondylodiscitis are required before meaningful clinical recommendations can be made.
