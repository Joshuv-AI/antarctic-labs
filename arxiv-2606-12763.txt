Adaptive Weighted Averaging
Aditya Bhaskara, Ashok Cutkosky, Ravi Kumar, Manish Purohit

Abstract
We study the problem of selecting the largest among n n unknown values x 1 , … , x n x_{1},\dots,x_{n} given only a single unbiased estimate y i y_{i} for each x i x_{i} . We design strategies that are simultaneously admissible (not uniformly dominated by any other strategy) and also never worse than a given baseline such as uniform random selection. We provide an application to stochastic optimization, where we obtain online-to-batch conversion bounds with a desirable “no-compromise” guarantee: they are never worse than standard random iterate selection, and yet can be significantly better in benign settings.



1
Introduction

We consider a fundamental aggregation problem:
given
n
unknown numbers
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
and a single unbiased estimate
y_{i}
for each, how well can one identify the index of the maximum
x_{i}
?
This problem and its solutions arise throughout learning theory, with links to analysis of empirical risk minimization
[
23
,
28
]
, bandits
[
3
,
1
]
, experts, and stochastic optimization
[
5
,
6
]
. We work with a standard generalization of this problem: rather than picking a specific index, we choose a categorical distribution over the indices
w\in\Delta_{n}
. Then, an intuitive measure of the quality of such a
w
is the inner product
\langle w,x\rangle
.

Although this problem is simple to state, there are surprising nuances to consider when designing a strategy.
One intuitive approach is the simple
Empirical Risk Minimization
(ERM) rule that chooses the index
i
with the highest value of
y_{i}
. This is optimal in the sense of being “admissible” (which we will define presently), but it is widely recognized that one usually obtains better results by employing some form of regularization to move away from ERM.
Alternatively, consider the naive approach of simply choosing an index uniformly at random. On the surface, this method might seem absurd: it completely ignores the estimates! Nevertheless, it is surprisingly effective in some applications. For example, this is the method employed by the so-called “online-to-batch conversion” that yields minimax optimal algorithms in many stochastic optimization problems.

1.1
No-compromise adaptivity

Given these two extremes, our focus is twofold: we attempt to build a method for choosing
w
that is (i)
never
worse than any arbitrary baseline “prior” over the choice of index (such as uniform sampling), and is (ii) also optimal in the sense that no other method can uniformly improve upon it.

By doing so, we are able to provide techniques that go beyond minimax optimality in certain stochastic optimization problems. Specifically, we obtain estimates that are never worse than the standard minimax optimal estimate, but provably obtain better loss in favorable settings. This goes beyond the standard “adaptivity” story in stochastic optimization. Typical adaptive methods may do better in a benign setting, but sacrifice some performance in the worst-case setting. For example, adaptive gradient descent methods like AdaGrad
[
11
]
perform better when the gradients are small, but their worst case performance is worse than the minimax rate. In contrast, we do better in benign settings without sacrificing anything in the hard settings.

1.2
Problem setup

Let
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in[0,1]^{n}
be an unknown arbitrary vector and let
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})\in[0,1]^{n}
be an unbiased estimate of
x
; we assume that the
y_{i}
’s are independent. We define a strategy
S
as an algorithm that uses the estimate
y
to output a distribution
S(y)
over the indices
\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

The
value
of a strategy
S
is
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim D}[\langle S(y),x\rangle]
. A strategy
S

dominates

S^{\prime}
if its value is always at least that of
S^{\prime}
no matter the value of
x
or the distribution of
y
.
S

strictly dominates

S^{\prime}
if, in addition, there is some setting of
x
and distribution
y
for which the value of
S
is strictly larger than the value of
S^{\prime}
.
S
is said to be
admissible
if it is not strictly dominated by any other strategy. We note that admissibility is a classic notion from Bayesian decision theory (e.g., see,
[
25
]
).

We also assume that we are given some
benchmark
strategy
S_{\mathrm{det}}
, where
S_{\mathrm{det}}
is a “constant” strategy that does not make use of the observation
y
at all. For example, one might consider the uniform benchmark
S_{\mathrm{unif}}(y)=(1/n,\dots,1/n)
. Our goal is to design a strategy that is simultaneously admissible and also dominates any given
S_{\mathrm{det}}
.

1.3
Contributions

The
S_{\mathrm{Bern}}
strategy.

We first introduce a strategy
S_{\mathrm{Bern}}
, derived via the multilinear extension of a base strategy optimized for Bernoulli observations (
Section

5
). (In that simpler case, the base strategy would select uniformly at random from among indices where
y_{i}=1
.) We prove that
S_{\mathrm{Bern}}
is admissible and strictly dominates the uniform strategy. Specifically, we show that the value of
S_{\mathrm{Bern}}
improves over the mean
\mu(x)
by a term related to the variance of the means
\sigma^{2}(x)
:

\text{value}(S_{\mathrm{Bern}};D)\geq\mu(x)+\left(1-\prod_{k=1}^{n}(1-x_{k})\right)\cdot\frac{\sigma^{2}(x)}{\mu(x)}.

Arbitrary benchmarks.
Moving beyond uniform strategies, we next consider arbitrary fixed deterministic strategies. For any such strategy
S_{\mathrm{det}}
, we construct a new strategy (
S_{\mathrm{peel}}
) that is both admissible and dominates
S_{\mathrm{det}}
(
Section

6
).

Impossibility Results.

We consider the harder setting where the observations
y_{i}
are not independent and in particular study the sequential setting where
y_{j}
(and the mean
x_{j}
) can depend on previous observations
y_{i}
for
i<j
. In this case, we construct a simple counterexample (
Section

8
) that shows uniform sampling is in fact admissible and cannot be dominated by any strategy. Even when the observations are independent, we show that for any two fixed benchmark strategies, there does not exist a strategy that simultaneously dominates both of them (
Section

9
).

Applications.
We apply these results to stochastic optimization and establish a new online-to-batch conversion bound that uniformly improves upon the standard techniques by a term depending on amount of variation observed in the losses during the iteration of the online algorithm (
Section

7
). This provides a rigorous way to take advantage of the intuition that one should not put much weight on the early iterations of an optimization algorithm without sacrificing the worst-case optimality of uniform averaging.
We also discuss other applications to ensemble methods and federated learning.

2
Related work

Optimal decision theory.

Our setting can be precisely phrased in the language of Bayesian decision theory (see
[
25
]
for an excellent survey). In this language, our decision space is the
n
-dimensional simplex, and we seek to maximize the utility
\langle w,x\rangle
(equivalently, minimize the loss
-\langle w,x\rangle
). Our goal is to design an estimator that is not only admissible, but also uniformly dominates any given benchmark strategy. This classical literature already suggests a natural approach for finding admissible strategies: start with any prior over decisions and choose a maximum a-posteriori strategy. This approach makes it fairly easy to see that the ERM rule is admissible. However, it is unclear how to design priors for which this approach actually dominates a given benchmark. In fact, it is surprisingly difficult to design a prior that does not simply yield the ERM strategy.

Prophet inequalities.

Our setting has some conceptual similarity to the prophet-inequality literature, though the objectives differ. Prophet inequalities
[
18
,
13
]
study online selection from a
sequence of independent random variables with the goal of approximating the expected maximum. Tight constant-factor bounds are known for
i.i.d. and non-i.i.d. settings
[
7
,
19
]
and
some recent works combine prior samples with online decisions
[
26
,
14
]
.
Our problem, on the other hand, does not involve online stopping or maximizing selection reward.

Statistical estimation.

Our setting also has a loose relationship to standard statistical
estimation problems, e.g., empirical Bayes and shrinkage
methods, which use information sharing across coordinates to improve
mean estimation or attempt to estimate rankings in high probability
[
24
,
12
,
2
]
.
Classical results such as Stein’s phenomenon

[
27
,
15
]
typically assume
Gaussian noise and seek to estimate the
entire
vector of means
under squared-error loss, whereas in our setting the distributions are arbitrary, only a single sample is
observed from each, and the target is a scalar that depends on the mean
vector.

Iterate averaging and online-to-batch conversions.

Our setting is also closely connected to the concept of iterate averaging in stochastic optimization.
Formally, if
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is some objective function, one might run some algorithm like stochastic gradient descent for
N
iterations to obtain iterates
z_{1},\dots,z_{N}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and then set
\overline{z}
to be a uniformly selected
z_{i}
. This immediately implies:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[F(\overline{z})-F(z^{\star})]\underbrace{=}_{\text{definition of $\overline{z}$}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[F(z_{i})-F(z^{\star})]=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\text{regret}]}{N}.

This is usually justified by observing that it yields minimax optimal algorithms in certain cases. However, the worst case examples for minimax optimality often somewhat degenerate. For example, the standard minimax adversary for non-smooth convex optimization forces
F(z_{i})=F(z_{j})
for all
i,j

[
4
]
, so that there is no “information” available in the actual observed values of the
F(z_{i})
. In practice, however, one expects some variation in the values. We would like an alternative weighted iterate averaging approach that can take advantage of such variation.

Prior literature frequently employs some limited forms of non-uniform averaging: non-adaptive polynomial weights are often used when the losses are known to have some additional smooth and convex structure
[
16
,
17
,
20
,
8
]
, while more advanced “adaptive” weighting schemes attempt to improve on uniform averaging more generally
[
10
,
21
,
9
]
. However, these approaches are usually restricted to the causal regime, where the weight assigned to
y_{t}
is a function solely of the historical sequence
\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{t-1}
and they all under-perform uniform averaging in difficult settings in expectation. In contrast, our framework relaxes the causality constraint, allowing weights for
y_{t}
to incorporate information from future iterates. Crucially, we also provide guarantees that our method is never outperformed by uniform averaging.

3
Preliminaries

Let
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Let
D_{1},\ldots,D_{n}
be distributions supported on
[0,1]
. We observe a single sample
y_{i}\sim D_{i}
, independently drawn, for each
i\in[n]
. Let
x_{i}=\mathbb{E}[y_{i}]
; we assume that
x_{i}
is not known to the algorithm. Let
y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
,
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=(\mathbb{E}[y_{1}],\ldots,\mathbb{E}[y_{n}])=:\mathbb{E}[y]
, and
D=D_{1}\times\cdots\times D_{n}
. Let
\mathrm{Bern}(x)
denote the Bernoulli distribution with mean
x
.

Let
\Delta_{n}
denote the probability simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and for a vector, its
i
th coordinate is accessed using the subscript
i
. Let
\mu(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}
and let
\sigma^{2}(x)=\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}\right)-\mu^{2}(x)
.

Definition 1

(Strategy and value)
.

A
strategy
is a function
S:[0,1]^{n}\to\Delta_{n}
. The
value
of a strategy
S
for a product distribution
D
is the expected value of the mean outcome, i.e.,
\mathrm{value}(S;D)=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim D}[\langle S(y),\mathbb{E}[y]\rangle]=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim D}[\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\cdot S(y)_{i}]
.

Definition 2

(Benchmark strategy)
.

A
strategy

S:[0,1]^{n}\to\Delta_{n}
is called a
benchmark
if it maps every outcome to a fixed probability distribution, i.e.,
S(y)=p,\forall y\in[0,1]^{n}
for some fixed
p\in\Delta_{n}
. For ease, we abuse notation and use
S=p
to denote a benchmark strategy.

We define
S_{\mathrm{unif}}
to be the benchmark strategy that places uniform mass on
[n]
regardless of its input, i.e.,
S_{\mathrm{unif}}(y)=(1/n,\ldots,1/n)
. Clearly,
\mathrm{value}(S_{\mathrm{unif}};D)=\mu(x)
.

Definition 3

(Dominance and admissibility)
.

A strategy
S

dominates
strategy
S^{\prime}
, denoted
S\succeq S^{\prime}
, iff
\mathrm{value}(S;D)\geq\mathrm{value}(S^{\prime};D)
, for all product distributions
D
.
S

strictly dominates

S^{\prime}
, denoted
S\succ S^{\prime}
, iff both
S\succeq S^{\prime}
and there is a product distribution
D
such that
\mathrm{value}(S;D)>\mathrm{value}(S^{\prime};D)
.
A strategy
S
is
admissible
iff it is not strictly dominated by any other strategy
S^{\prime}
, i.e., there is no
S^{\prime}
such that
S^{\prime}\succ S
.

3.1
Failure of simple approaches

Figure 1:
Naive single-step exponential weights based strategies fail to dominate the benchmark.

Before describing our algorithms, we take a moment to appreciate how one intuitively reasonable approach might fall short—even if we are satisfied with simply dominating a benchmark without considering admissibility. The reader familiar with standard optimization and learning theory might imagine that taking a single step of exponential weights with an appropriately small learning rate
\eta
, initialized at a benchmark, would suffice. This strategy is essentially taking one step of SGD on the value function. However, this strategy fails to dominate the benchmark, even for very small learning rates
\eta
. Indeed, we verify this numerically in Figure
1
, where we consider the case
D=(\mathrm{Bern}(x_{1}),\mathrm{Bern}(x_{2}))
. For any learning rate
\eta
, we perform a grid search over all tuples
(x_{1},x_{2},p)
where
p\in[0,1]
specifies a benchmark strategy
S_{\text{det}}=(p,1-p)
. We plot the maximum over all such tuples of the benchmark value
x_{1}p+(1-x_{2})\cdot(1-p)
minus the expected value obtained by taking one step of exponential weights with learning rate
\eta
initialized at
(p,1-p)
, i.e.,
S_{\text{exp}}=\left(\frac{p\exp(\eta y_{1})}{p\exp(\eta y_{1})+(1-p)\exp(\eta y_{2})},\frac{(1-p)\exp(\eta y_{2})}{p\exp(\eta y_{1})+(1-p)\exp(\eta y_{2})},\right)
. Figure
1
shows that, no matter the
\eta
value, there is always an
(x_{1},x_{2},p)
tuple for which
S_{\text{exp}}
has smaller value than
S_{\text{det}}
.

4
Warm up: Two items

We first consider a simple setup with just two items (i.e.,
n=2
) and further let
D=D_{1}\times D_{2}
where
D_{1}=\mathrm{Bern}(x_{1})
and
D_{2}=\mathrm{Bern}(x_{2})
. Since
D
is completely characterized by
x=(x_{1},x_{2})
, we define
\mathrm{value}(S;x):=\mathrm{value}(S;D)
for convenience. In this setting,
\mathrm{value}(S_{\mathrm{unif}};x)=\frac{x_{1}+x_{2}}{2}
. Our goal is to design a strategy that is admissible and strictly dominates
S_{\mathrm{unif}}
.

Since each
y_{i}\in\{0,1\}
, we explicitly construct such a strategy
S_{\mathrm{n=2}}
as follows:

S_{\mathrm{n=2}}((0,0))=S_{\mathrm{n=2}}((1,1))=(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
,
S_{\mathrm{n=2}}((0,1))=(0,1)
and
S_{\mathrm{n=2}}((1,0))=(1,0)
. In other words, the strategy deterministically chooses the item with the higher outcome, but reverts to the uniform distribution when the outcomes are equal. We omit the proof that
S_{\mathrm{n=2}}
is admissible here since we prove a more general result in Section
5.2
but we observe that
S_{\mathrm{n=2}}\succeq S_{\mathrm{unif}}
as follows.

\displaystyle\mathrm{value}(S_{\mathrm{n=2}};x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}[y_{1}=y_{2}]\cdot\left(\frac{x_{1}+x_{2}}{2}\right)+\mathbb{P}[y=(0,1)]\cdot\left(x_{2}\right)+\mathbb{P}[y=(1,0)]\cdot\left(x_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(x_{1}x_{2}+(1-x_{1})(1-x_{2})\right)\left(\frac{x_{1}+x_{2}}{2}\right)+(1-x_{1})x_{2}(x_{2})+x_{1}(1-x_{2})(x_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{1}+x_{2}}{2}+\frac{(x_{1}-x_{2})^{2}}{2}=\mathrm{value}(S_{\mathrm{unif}};x)+\frac{(x_{1}-x_{2})^{2}}{2}.

4.1
Extension to
n>2

When
D
is a product of
n
Bernoulli distributions, where
n>2
, one can extend the
S_{\mathrm{n=2}}
strategy to dominate
S_{\mathrm{unif}}
. Let
S_{\mathrm{pair}}
be a strategy that first chooses two indices
i<j
uniformly at random and then uses the
S_{\mathrm{n=2}}
strategy to pick among items
i
and
j
. Formally, for any
y
, we have
S_{\mathrm{pair}}(y)=\dfrac{1}{\binom{n}{2}}\cdot\sum_{i<j}S_{\mathrm{n=2}}((y_{i},y_{j}))
. Once again, we observe that
S_{\mathrm{pair}}\succeq S_{\mathrm{unif}}
as follows.

\displaystyle\mathrm{value}(S_{\mathrm{pair}};x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\binom{n}{2}}\cdot\sum_{i<j}\left(\frac{x_{i}+x_{j}}{2}+\frac{(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}}{n}+\frac{1}{\binom{n}{2}}\cdot\sum_{i<j}\frac{(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}}{2}=\mathrm{value}(S_{\mathrm{unif}};x)+\frac{n}{n-1}\cdot\sigma^{2}(x).

Thus, while
S_{\mathrm{pair}}
shows that improvement over
S_{\mathrm{unif}}
is possible for product of Bernoulli distributions, it is unclear if something better exists. We therefore turn our attention to designing a strategy that is never worse than
S_{\mathrm{unif}}
, is admissible, and works for all product distributions. This leads us to a new general strategy, described in Section
5
.

5
An admissible strategy:
S_{\mathrm{Bern}}

We now consider the general setting when the distributions
D_{i}
are arbitrary and hence we have
y_{i}\in[0,1]
. In this section, we design a new strategy
S_{\mathrm{Bern}}
that dominates
S_{\mathrm{unif}}
and is also admissible.

It is easiest to describe the strategy
S_{\mathrm{Bern}}
as a randomized process (although all strategies are deterministic by nature). Let
y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
be a fixed outcome. First, for each item
i
, we sample a new independent Bernoulli random variable
z_{i}
with mean
y_{i}
. Next, we choose an item uniformly at random from the items that have
z_{i}=1
; if all
z_{i}=0
, then we choose a uniformly random item. The strategy
S_{\mathrm{Bern}}
is defined as the expectation of this process.

Let
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\Delta_{n}
be a function defined over vertices of the Boolean hypercube as
f(z)=(\frac{z_{1}}{\sum_{j}z_{j}},\ldots,\frac{z_{n}}{\sum_{j}z_{j}})
for
z\neq 0
and
f(0,\ldots,0)=(\frac{1}{n},\ldots,\frac{1}{n})
. Then formally, the strategy
S_{\mathrm{Bern}}
is defined as the unique multilinear extension of
f
over the domain
[0,1]^{n}
. Mathematically, we can express
S_{\mathrm{Bern}}(y)=(q_{1}(y),\ldots,q_{n}(y))
where we have:

\displaystyle q_{i}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\prod_{j=1}^{n}(1-y_{j})+\sum_{S:i\in S}\frac{1}{|S|}\cdot\prod_{j\in S}y_{j}\cdot\prod_{k\notin S}(1-y_{k}),

(1)

and, equivalently, using inclusion-exclusion
1
1

1

Lemma
13
in the Appendix contains a full proof of the equivalence.
, this can be expressed as:

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\prod_{j=1}^{n}(1-y_{j})+\sum_{S:i\in S}\frac{(-1)^{|S|-1}}{|S|}\prod_{j\in S}y_{j}.

(2)

It is easy to see that the strategy is valid, i.e.,
S_{\mathrm{Bern}}(y)\in\Delta_{n}
for all
y\in[0,1]^{n}
.

5.1
Value

First, notice that since
q_{i}(y)
is linear in each
y_{j}
and all the
y
’s are independent, we have
\mathbb{E}[q_{i}(y)]=q_{i}(x)
. Hence, we have
\mathrm{value}(S_{\mathrm{Bern}};D)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\cdot\mathbb{E}[q_{i}(y)]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\cdot q_{i}(x)
. Next, we focus on the properties of
q_{i}(x)
.

Lemma 1
.

For any two indices
i
and
j
,
q_{i}(x)-q_{j}(x)=(x_{i}-x_{j})\cdot K_{i,j}(x)
where

K_{i,j}(x)=\int_{t=0}^{1}\left(\prod_{k\notin\{i,j\}}(1-tx_{k})\right)dt.

Further,
K_{i,j}(x)\geq 0
, so
x_{i}>x_{j}\iff q_{i}(x)>q_{j}(x)
.

This immediately leads to a simple but loose lower bound on
K_{i,j}(\cdot)
.

Corollary 2
.

For any pair
i,j\neq i
of distinct indices and any vector
x
, we have
K_{i,j}(x)\geq\frac{1}{n-1}
.

Proof.

For any
x_{k}
, we have
1-tx_{k}\geq 1-t
. So we have
K_{i,j}(x)\geq\int_{t=0}^{1}(1-t)^{n-2}dt=\frac{1}{n-1}
.
∎

We now obtain a stronger lower bound. Towards this, we define the multivariate auxiliary function:

F(x):=\frac{1-\prod_{k=1}^{n}(1-x_{k})}{\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}}.

We first argue that
F(x)
in non-increasing in each of its coordinates.

Lemma 3

(Monotonicity)
.

F(x)=F(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
is a monotonically decreasing function of each of its variables
x_{k}\in[0,1]
.

We use this to show an improved lower bound on
K_{i,j}(\cdot)
.

Lemma 4
.

For any pair
i,j\neq i
of distinct indices and any vector
x
, we have
K_{i,j}(x)\geq F(x).

Proof.

To simplify notation, let
\bar{N}_{i,j}=[n]\setminus\{i,j\}
and let
\bar{P}_{i,j}=\prod_{k\in\bar{N}_{i,j}}(1-x_{k})
. We will first show that

K_{i,j}(x)\geq\frac{1-\prod_{k\in\bar{N}_{i,j}}(1-x_{k})}{\sum_{k\in\bar{N}_{i,j}}x_{k}}=\frac{1-\bar{P}_{i,j}}{\sum_{k\in\bar{N}_{i,j}}x_{k}}.

Observe that for
t\in[0,1]
, the function
(1-y)^{t}
is concave
and hence it lies below its tangent, i.e.,

(1-y)^{t}\leq 1-ty
. Applying this, we obtain

K_{i,j}(x)=\int_{t=0}^{1}\left(\prod_{k\in\bar{N}_{i,j}}(1-tx_{k})\right)dt\geq\int_{t=0}^{1}\left(\prod_{k\in\bar{N}_{i,j}}(1-x_{k})\right)^{t}dt=\int_{t=0}^{1}\bar{P}_{i,j}^{t}dt=\frac{\bar{P}_{i,j}-1}{\log\bar{P}_{i,j}}.

(3)

Next we claim
\log\bar{P}_{i,j}\leq-\sum_{k\in\bar{N}_{i,j}}x_{k}
. Indeed, by concavity as before, we have for all
t\in[0,1]
,
\log(1-y)\leq\frac{\log(1-ty)}{t}
and hence
\log(1-y)\leq\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\frac{\log(1-ty)}{t}=-y
. The claim follows by applying this inequality pointwise to each term in
\log\bar{P}_{i,j}
.

Using this claim in (
3
), we obtain

\displaystyle K_{i,j}(x)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\bar{P}_{i,j}-1}{-\sum_{k\in\bar{N}_{i,j}}x_{k}}=\frac{1-\prod_{k\in\bar{N}_{i,j}}(1-x_{k})}{\sum_{k\in\bar{N}_{i,j}}x_{k}}=F(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i}=0,\ldots,x_{j}=0,\ldots,x_{n})

\displaystyle\geq F(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}),\mbox{
using the monotonicity of $F$ (Lemma~\ref{lemma:mono}).}\qed

Finally, we apply the lower bounds on
K_{i,j}(x)
to bound the value of
S_{\mathrm{Bern}}
.

Theorem 5
.

We have the following two lower bounds:

•

\mathrm{value}(S_{\mathrm{Bern}};D)\geq\mu(x)+\frac{n}{n-1}\cdot\sigma^{2}(x),

•

\mathrm{value}(S_{\mathrm{Bern}};D)\geq\mu(x)+\left(1-\prod_{k=1}^{n}(1-x_{k})\right)\cdot\frac{\sigma^{2}(x)}{\mu(x)}.

Proof.

As before, let
S_{\mathrm{Bern}}(y)=(q_{1}(y),\ldots,q_{n}(y))
. Recall that

\mathrm{value}(S_{\mathrm{Bern}};D)=\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[\sum_{i}x_{i}\cdot q_{i}(y)\right]=\sum_{i}x_{i}\cdot\mathbb{E}[q_{i}(y)]=\sum_{i}x_{i}\cdot q_{i}(x).

We begin with the following identity

\displaystyle\sum_{i}a_{i}b_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}a_{i}\sum_{i}b_{i}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i<j}(a_{i}-a_{j})(b_{i}-b_{j}).

Substituting
a_{i}=q_{i}(x)
and
b_{i}=x_{i}
, using
\sum_{i}q_{i}(x)=1
, and using Lemma
1
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i}x_{i}\cdot q_{i}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}x_{i}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i<j}(x_{i}-x_{j})(q_{i}(x)-q_{j}(x))=\mu(x)+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i<j}(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}K_{i,j}(x).

Now, lower bounding

using Corollary

\sum_{i}x_{i}\cdot q_{i}(x)\geq\mu(x)+\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i<j}(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}=\mu(x)+\frac{n}{n-1}\cdot\sigma^{2}(x).

Instead, lower bounding

using Lemma

, we obtain:

\sum_{i}x_{i}\cdot q_{i}(x)\geq\mu(x)+\frac{1-\prod_{k=1}^{n}(1-x_{k})}{n\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}}\sum_{i<j}(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}\\
=\mu(x)+\left(1-\prod_{k=1}^{n}(1-x_{k})\right)\cdot\frac{\sigma^{2}(x)}{\mu(x)}.\qed

Theorem

5
shows that

dominates

strategy we discussed in Section

Note that the two bounds in

are incomparable as they depend on the sparsity of

. This trade-off can be seen from
the coefficient multiplying

in each bound. The first

while the second bound has a coefficient
of
(1-\prod_{k=1}^{n}(1-x_{k}))/\mu(x)\geq 1
(Lemma

).

Indeed, consider the setting when
\ell
of the

\approx 1
and the others are
\approx 0

and

Admissibility

In this section, we show that

Lemma 6
.

If

.

Proof.

Let

For any product distribution

\displaystyle\mathrm{value}(S^{\prime};D)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\pi\in\mathbb{S}_{n}}\mathrm{value}(S;D\circ\pi)\geq\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\pi\in\mathbb{S}_{n}}\mathrm{value}(S_{\mathrm{Bern}};D\circ\pi)=\mathrm{value}(S_{\mathrm{Bern}};D),

where the inequality follows since

∎

Lemma 7

If

Proof.

The proof proceeds by contradiction, assuming there is a strategy

Arbitrary benchmarks

We now consider the general problem of designing strategies that dominate an arbitrary benchmark strategy rather than just the uniform strategy

. Let

be an arbitrary benchmark strategy. We design a new strategy
S_{\mathrm{peel}}
that dominates
S_{\mathrm{det}}
and is also admissible.

Analog of the uniform-on-ones strategy.

Given a benchmark

, a natural way to extend the uniform-on-ones strategy is the following: observe the values
y
, and let
J
be set of indices with
y_{j}=1
. Then we output the vector
p_{J}/\|p_{J}\|_{1}
, where

is the vector
(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})
restricted to the indices
J
(by setting the entries in

to

). As before, if

, we output
(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})
.

Interestingly, this approach fails for non-uniform choices of

. Consider the following example with
n=2
:
S_{\mathrm{det}}=(\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3})
. Suppose
(x_{1},x_{2})=(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}+\delta)
, for some
\delta>0
. In this case, the strategy above outputs:

\begin{cases}\left(\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3}\right)\qquad\text{ with prob. }\frac{1}{2}\\

For a small value of

(e.g., in

), we can observe that in expectation, this strategy does not dominate

. Essentially, the last two cases create a move towards

than the baseline of

.

6.1
Peeling decomposition

Without loss of generality, let
p_{1}\leq\dots\leq p_{n}
. Let
w_{k}=(n-k+1)(p_{k}-p_{k-1}),\forall 1\leq k\leq n
(where
p_{0}
is defined to be zero). We note that
w\in\Delta_{n}
and observe that the benchmark strategy
S_{\mathrm{det}}
can be decomposed into a weighted combination of uniform strategies on nested subsets of items. Formally, let
S_{\mathrm{unif}}^{k}
denote the strategy that places equal probability mass on items
k
or higher, i.e., for any outcome
y
we have,
S_{\mathrm{unif}}^{k}(y)_{j}=\frac{1}{n-k+1}
if
j\in\{k,\ldots,n\}
and zero otherwise. Then,

S_{\mathrm{det}}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}w_{k}\cdot S_{\mathrm{unif}}^{k}.

This decomposition suggests a natural way to construct a dominating strategy since each of the uniform strategies in the decomposition can be replaced with a strategy that dominates it. Let
S_{\mathrm{Bern}}^{k}
denote the strategy defined in Section
5
that is restricted to items
k
or higher. Formally we have

S_{\mathrm{Bern}}^{k}(y)=(q_{1}(y),\ldots,q_{n}(y))
where:

\displaystyle q_{i}(y)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }i<k\\
\frac{1}{n-k+1}\prod_{j=k}^{n}(1-y_{j})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset\{k,\ldots,n\}\\
i\in S\end{subarray}}\frac{(-1)^{|S|-1}}{|S|}\prod_{j\in S}y_{j},&\text{ if }i\geq k.\end{cases}

(4)

Then, we define the strategy

as:

S_{\mathrm{peel}}(y)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}w_{k}\cdot S_{\mathrm{Bern}}^{k}(y).

Value and admissibility

The following theorem specifies the gain of

over the benchmark strategy

.

Theorem 9
.

We have the following two lower bounds:

•

\mathrm{value}(S_{\mathrm{peel}};D)\geq\mathrm{value}(S_{\mathrm{det}};D)+\sum_{k=1}^{n}w_{k}\cdot\left(1-\prod_{j=k}^{n}(1-x_{j})\right)\cdot\frac{\sigma^{2}(x;k)}{\mu(x;k)},

If

7
Application: Improved online-to-batch conversions

The classical online-to-batch conversion provides a simple technique for converting regret bounds in online optimization into stochastic optimization guarantees
[
5
]
. We briefly recapitulate the classical argument here: suppose that
f(x,z)\in\mathbb{R}
is some loss function taking as input a parameter
x
to be optimized and a data point
z
. Our goal is to minimize the function
F(x)=\mathbb{E}_{Z}[f(x,Z)]
where
Z
follows some unknown distribution. Let
z_{1},\dots,z_{N}
be an i.i.d. data set. Then we run an online learning algorithm to obtain iterates
x_{1},\dots,x_{N}
such that
x_{i}
is independent of
z_{j}
for
j\geq i
. Then if
\bar{x}
is chosen uniformly at random from
x_{1},\dots,x_{N}
, we have for any
x_{\star}
(usually
x_{\star}\in\mathop{\text{argmin}}F)
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[F(\bar{x})-F(x_{\star})]=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[f(x_{i},z_{i})-f(x_{\star},z_{i})]}{N}.

The numerator of this quantity is exactly the
regret
that an online learning algorithm keeps under control (typically growing as
\sqrt{N}
). Thus,
\mathbb{E}[F(\bar{x})]
approaches the optimal value.

However, with our techniques we can do better: suppose that
f(x,z)\in[0,1]
. Now, for
i\leq N/2
, Define
X_{i}=1-F(x_{i})
and
Y_{i}=1-f(x_{i},z_{i+N/2})
. For all
i\leq N/2
, we have
x_{i}
and
z_{j+N/2}
are independent. Therefore,
Y_{i}
and
Y_{j}
are independent given
(X_{1},\dots,X_{N/2})
for all
i,j\leq N/2
. Now, let
w_{1},\dots,w_{N/2}
be the weighting obtained by our algorithm applied to
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{N/2}
Clearly,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N/2}w_{i}X_{i}\right]\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{2}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N/2}X_{i}\right].

Set
\hat{x}=x_{i}
with probability
w_{i}/2
if
i\leq N/2
and
2/N
if
i>N/2
. Then, we will have a value that is never worse than the standard “random iterate selection”, but may be better in the reasonable case that the
X_{i}
values are not all equal to each other. Thus, by Lemma
5
, we have (with formal statement in Theorem
16
):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[F(\hat{x})-F(x_{\star})]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[F(\bar{x})-F(x_{\star})]-\mathbb{E}\left[O\left(\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N/2}X_{i}^{2}-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N/2}X_{i}\right)^{2}}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N/2}X_{i}\right)}\right)\right].

Essentially, we obtain a significant gain if there is a large variance in the population loss values in the first half of the online learning procedure. Intuitively, this is exactly where we should expect the largest change in population loss. However, one seemingly lossy step in the above is the partitioning of data and the use of some points twice, once to generate iterates and once to generate our adaptive weighting. In Section
8
we show that in general, a natural single-pass alternative is impossible.

8
Sequentially dependent
x_{i}^{\prime}s

As discussed, in Section
7
, we use many of the data points in our training sample twice: once to generate iterates for the online algorithm, and once to generate our adaptive weighting. One might wonder if this is necessary: can we simply use the single-pass of observations generated during the online optimization procedure to also set the averaging weights? In this section we see that without some further assumptions or relaxation, the deterministic uniform random strategy is already admissible.

To formalize the situation, we consider the case that the
x_{i}
are not fixed up-front, but are in fact dependent on the
y_{i}
in a sequential manner. That is, the
x_{i}
are random variables satisfying
x_{i}=F_{i}(y_{1},\dots,y_{i-1})
. This models certain sequential problems in which the
x_{i}
represent decisions that are themselves generated via some adaptive process. This might be the case if the
x_{i}
are iterates of some online optimization procedure, for which
x_{t+1}
depends on the previously observed loss value
y_{t}
. In this case, we show that it is impossible to dominate the uniform strategy.

.

Let
N=2
and let
S:[0,1]^{N}\to\Delta_{2}
, and suppose
S
is not equal to
(1/2,1/2)
for all inputs. Then there is a
x_{1}\in[0,1]
and a function
x_{2}:\{0,1\}\to[0,1]
such that with random variables

, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\langle S(y_{1},y_{2}),x\rangle]<\mathbb{E}[\langle(1/2,1/2),x\rangle].

At first glance, this suggests that our adaptive weighting schemes cannot be extended to improve the classical online-to-batch conversion. However, in Section
7
we already showed that in fact the stochastic optimization setup has slightly more structure, which does enable us to improve.

In this section, we show that it is not possible to design a strategy that simultaneously dominates more than one benchmark strategy.

Theorem 12
.

Let

and let
S_{1}=(p,1-p)
and
S_{2}=(p^{\prime},1-p^{\prime})
be two distinct benchmark strategies. There exists no strategy
S:[0,1]^{2}\rightarrow\Delta_{2}
that simultaneously dominates
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
.

Proof.

Suppose for contradiction that there exists
S:[0,1]^{2}\rightarrow\Delta_{2}
that dominates
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
. By definition, we must have
\mathrm{value}(S;D)\geq\mathrm{value}(S_{1};D)
, for all product distributions
D=D_{1}\times D_{2}
. We restrict our attention to Bernoulli distributions,
D_{i}=\mathrm{Bern}(x_{i})
for arbitrary
x_{1},x_{2}\in[0,1]
. Under this restriction, the samples
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
only take values in
\{0,1\}
. So the strategy
S
is only evaluated at four points. For

be the four constants that fully determine strategy
S
.

Let

be the expected probability that
S
chooses item 1. Because the distributions are Bernoulli, this is exactly:

q(x_{1},x_{2})=a_{0,0}(1-x_{1})(1-x_{2})+a_{0,1}(1-x_{1})(x_{2})+a_{1,0}(x_{1})(1-x_{2})+a_{1,1}(x_{1})(x_{2}).

Crucially, note that
q(x_{1},x_{2})
is a polynomial in
(x_{1},x_{2})
and is thus continuous with respect to
(x_{1},x_{2})
. We now compute the value of strategy
S
and the two benchmark strategies
S_{1}
and
S_{2}

\displaystyle\mathrm{value}(S;D)

\displaystyle=x_{2}+(x_{1}-x_{2})q(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle\mathrm{value}(S_{1};D)

\displaystyle=x_{2}+(x_{1}-x_{2})p

\displaystyle\mathrm{value}(S_{2};D)

\displaystyle=x_{2}+(x_{1}-x_{2})p^{\prime}.

Since
\mathrm{value}(S;D)\geq\mathrm{value}(S_{i};D)
for all product distributions, we have for all
(x_{1},x_{2})\in[0,1]^{2}
:

\displaystyle(x_{1}-x_{2})(q(x_{1},x_{2})-p)\geq 0,\quad\text{ and }\quad(x_{1}-x_{2})(q(x_{1},x_{2})-p^{\prime})\geq 0.

Since

is continuous, taking the limit

requires that

to satisfy the first inequality, and similarly requires that
q_{1}(x,x)=p^{\prime}
to satisfy the second inequality. But since

, this is a contradiction.

10
Conclusion

The problem we study in this paper is both fundamental and pleasingly simple: given a single observation for each of
n
values, how well can you pick the largest value? Simply selecting a value at random is minimax optimal, but is intuitively unsatisfying because it does not depend on the data. On the other hand, the ERM rule, which is admissible, may be too brittle. We take a principled approach to resolve this issue: we ask for admissibility subject to the constraint that our strategy must dominate a benchmark.

While we study perhaps the simplest and most basic setting, our work raises natural follow-up questions. For example, what should be done if more than one sample is available? What if the observations are not bounded? What is an admissible strategy for stochastic convex optimization that dominates SGD with iterate averaging? In general, we hope our dual focus on admissibility and dominating a given benchmark may inspire further work in the machine learning theory community.

\displaystyle\quad-\mathbb{E}\left[\left(1-\prod_{i=1}^{N/2}F(x_{i})\right)\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N/2}(1-F(x_{i}))^{2}-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N/2}1-F(x_{i})\right)^{2}}{2\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N/2}1-F(x_{i})\right)}\right].\qed

Appendix C
Applications

We now describe a few ML applications where adaptive weighted averaging arises naturally. Recall that our results show that our adaptive algorithms provably outperform any (non-adaptive) baseline, in expectation. The bounds show that our method is especially effective in the setting where the underlying means are sufficiently different from one another.

Online-to-batch conversion (Iterate averaging).

Our original motivation for studying the problem is iterate averaging. In stochastic convex optimization, an online learning algorithm generates a sequence
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{T}
of iterates. The standard approach to convert these online iterates into a final solution is to output the uniform average
\bar{\theta}
. (E.g., see,
[
4
]
). However, later iterates are often superior to earlier ones, suggesting that uniform averaging is suboptimal (in practice; in the worst case, it is well known that uniform averaging obtains the min-max optimal results).

This setting fits into our framework with
n=T
: the
x_{i}
values correspond to the
utility
(negative loss) of the iterate
\theta_{t}
. To obtain an estimate of
x_{t}
, we can use a small set/mini-batch of examples for each
t
, to obtain
y_{t}
. Using these values, we can use our results to obtain a new weighted average.

Re-weighting an ensemble of models under distribution shifts.

Model ensembles are ubiquitous in modern ML applications. To solve a task, we train a collection
M_{1},\dots,M_{n}
of models, with the intuition that (i) different
M_{i}
may perform better on different “regions” of the input space, and (ii) for uncertain inputs, a weighted average prediction can provide a more reliable output. For the training distribution
\mathcal{D}
, the training process ensures that the average of the model outputs yields the best prediction for an input
x\sim\mathcal{D}
.

Now suppose we have a shift in the distribution, obtaining
\mathcal{D^{\prime}}
(some regions of the space are now more/less important than before). It is natural to ask if there is a better weighting of the models. Once again, this falls into our framework, where the
x_{i}
values correspond to the negated loss of
M_{i}
on
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
. We can estimate
x_{i}
as before, using a small number of samples from
\mathcal{D^{\prime}}
to obtain a better method for combining the models.

Aggregation in federated learning.

Our framework can also be applied when aggregating updates that have varying impacts on a model. For example, in the federated learning setting
[
22
]
, we have
n
clients each providing a candidate update to a model. The loss reduction (to a global objective of interest) provided by each update is now the
x_{i}
. The server can obtain an estimate
y_{i}
of this quantity using a minibatch as before; our framework then gives new weights for aggregation that can improve upon the simple average.
