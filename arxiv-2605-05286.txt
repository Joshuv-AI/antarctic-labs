Paraconsistent Semantics for Extended Fuzzy Logic Programs via Approximation Fixpoint Theory [Extended Version]
Pascal Kettmann, Hannes Strass, Jesse Heyninck, Jeroen Spaans

Abstract
In logic programming, negation can be interpreted in various ways. Probably best known is the concept of “negation as failure”, where “ 𝑛𝑜𝑡 ​ p \mathit{not}\,p ” is true if we have no evidence for p p . On the other hand, strong negation requires that we have evidence for p p being false. Defining semantics for logic programs containing both kinds of negation is a challenging task, and this becomes even more challenging when combining this with other extensions of logic programming, e.g. fuzziness. In this work, we use the framework of approximating fixpoint theory to formulate well-behaved semantics for fuzzy logic programs containing both “by-failure” and strong negation. We show that this framework generalizes several existing semantics as well as giving rise to a host of new semantics.

Introduction

Logic programs (LPs) are among the oldest rule-based formalisms within the field of artificial intelligence
[
25
]
.
While originally used for declarative programming, their modern incarnation in answer set programming
[
7
,
15
]
enjoys widespread practical success for declarative problem-solving, while using the same basic constructs at the core of the language.
Researchers noted early on that the inherent restriction to two truth values limits applicability, and proposed generalizations from
crisp
(two-valued) to
fuzzy
(many-valued) logics – for resolution in general
[
26
]
, and for (definite) logic programs in particular
[
36
,
39
,
20
]
.

Naturally, “weak” negation (indicating absence of provability and thus also known as negation by failure
[
8
]
) has been combined with fuzziness, leading to
normal
fuzzy logic programs offering various semantics, including stable models
[
42
,
9
]
and well-founded semantics
[
27
]
.
On the other hand, crisp normal LPs have also been extended with “strong” negation (indicating established falsity
[
16
]
), leading to
extended
logic programs with both kinds of negation.
While the original proposal of semantics for extended LPs required answer sets (stable models) of programs to be consistent, that is, disallowed the presence of an atom and its classical negation in the model, it did not take long until
paraconsistent
semantics were proposed for extended LPs – again both well-founded semantics
[
34
]
and stable model semantics
[
33
]
.
The idea of paraconsistent reasoning is that inconsistency is “allowed” to occur in a model, but is “locally contained” and thus does not lead to the usual
ex falsum quodlibet
issues of classical logic
[
5
]
, i.e. it avoids trivializing logical reasoning.
And while
extended fuzzy
LPs with stable model semantics do exist, they either do not offer paraconsistent reasoning at all
[
32
,
21
]
, or do not offer it in full generality: some works
[
2
,
12
,
1
]
base their semantics on the
coherence principle
, which requires them to make restricting assumptions on the negation functions that can possibly be employed.

The common theme in all of these works on various extensions of normal LPs is their piecemeal nature, the high amount of manual labour involved, and the sheer repetitiveness with which authors took the same ideas, such as the monotone one-step consequence operator of van Emden and Kowalski
[
38
]
and the reduct of Gelfond and Lifschitz
[
16
]
, and manually adapted them to cover the ever next extension.
The result is a scattered landscape with no uniform syntax, semantics with the same name but formally unclear relationships, and varying assumptions on possible sets of truth values and logical connectives.

In this paper, we unify a considerable portion of the existing work in this area.
First, we employ a syntax that covers major recurring features of the literature on extended fuzzy logic programming.
Second, and more importantly, we define an operator that captures a considerable number of existing semantics.
This is possible because we employ the powerful framework of
approximation fixpoint theory
(AFT)
[
13
]
.
AFT reduces the amount of manual labour required to a minimum, as it already incorporates – within its standard definitions – ideas that previous authors had to rework explicitly in their respective settings, such as the program reduct of Gelfond and Lifschitz
[
16
]
.
More concretely, we show how our
single
operator reconstructs the semantics of Sakama
[
34
]
, Sakama and Inoue
[
33
]
, Saad
[
32
]
, and Cornejo et al.
[
10
]
, while also giving rise to new semantics, in particular the first stable model and well-founded semantics offering paraconsistent reasoning for extended fuzzy LPs that allows for non-involutive negations (where double negation is not always identity, e.g. Gödel negation).

The rest of the paper proceeds as follows.
In the next section, we give the necessary background.

Section

3
introduces the syntax of the language we use, while
Section

4
defines our main operator.
The following two sections reconstruct existing semantics of extended (fuzzy) logic programs, with
Section

7
providing additional results beyond mere reconstruction.
We finally discuss avenues for future work.

Background

2.1
Approximation Fixpoint Theory (AFT)

A particular way of defining semantics for programming languages is to associate a program
P
with a
transformation
operator
T_{P}
that transforms a given input-output relation
R
into an updated relation
T_{P}(R)
.
In this view, the semantics of program
P
is given by the least fixpoint of operator
T_{P}
.
Van Emden and Kowalski
[
38
]
applied this way of defining semantics to logic programming, developing the first one-step logical consequence operator and employing its monotonicity in conjunction with Tarski’s fixpoint theorem
[
37
]
to define the semantics of definite logic programs with potentially recursive predicate definitions.

More formally, an
operator
is a (total) function
O\colon V\to V
on a partially ordered set
(V,\leqslant)
.
Here, we even require
(V,\leqslant)
to be a
complete lattice
, that is, we require that every subset
S\subseteq V
has a

least upper bound (lub)

\bigvee\!S\in V
and

greatest lower bound (glb)

\bigwedge\!S\in V
;
this entails that
(V,\leqslant)
has both a least element
0=\bigwedge\!V=\bigvee\!\emptyset
and a greatest element
1=\bigvee\!V=\bigwedge\!\emptyset
.
An operator
O\colon V\to V
is

monotone
iff
x\leqslant y
implies
O(x)\leqslant O(y)
, and

antimonotone
iff
x\leqslant y
implies
O(y)\leqslant O(x)
.
An element
x\in V
with
O(x)=x
is a
fixpoint
of
O
;
an
x\in V
with
O(x)\leqslant x
is a
pre-fixpoint
of
O
;
an
x\in V
with
x\leqslant O(x)
is a
post-fixpoint
of
O
.

A fundamental result in lattice theory, Tarski’s fixpoint theorem
[
37
]
, states that whenever
O
is a monotone operator on a complete lattice
(V,\leqslant)
, the set
\left\{x\in V\ \middle|\ O(x)=x\right\}
of its fixpoints forms a complete lattice itself, and so has a least element
\mathit{lfp}(O)
and a greatest element
\mathit{gfp}(O)
.
While this result is immensely useful, it is not constructive;
however, constructive versions exist and tell us how to actually construct least fixpoints
[
29
,
11
]
:
To this end, one defines, for any
x\in V
, the iterated (transfinite) application of
O
to
x
via

O^{0}(x)\mathrel{:=}x
,

O^{\mathord{\alpha+1}}(x)\mathrel{:=}O(O^{\alpha}(x))
for successor ordinals, and

O^{\gamma}(x)\mathrel{:=}\bigvee\!\left\{O^{\alpha}(x)\ \middle|\ \alpha<\gamma\right\}
for limit ordinals.
It is then known that (for monotone
O
) there exists an ordinal
\alpha
such that
O^{\alpha}(0)=\mathit{lfp}(O)

[
6
]
;
by duality, we have
\mathit{gfp}(O)=O^{\kappa}(1)
for some
\kappa
.

In order to apply Tarski’s fixpoint theorem
[
37
]
(or its constructive versions), it is thus necessary to have an operator that is monotone, a condition that is no longer fulfilled when moving from
definite
to
normal
logic programs, i.e., when allowing weak negation in rule bodies.
To still have a useful fixpoint theory in the case of non-monotone operators, Denecker, Marek, and Truszczyński (DMT)
[
13
]
fundamentally generalized the theory underlying the fixpoint-based approach to semantics, thereby founding what is now known as
approximation fixpoint theory
.
Its underlying idea is that when an operator of interest does not have properties that guarantee the existence of fixpoints, one can
approximate
this operator in a more fine-grained algebraic structure where fixpoint existence can be guaranteed.

Formally – following ideas of Belnap
[
5
]
, Ginsberg
[
17
]
, and Fitting
[
14
]
– DMT
[
13
]
moved from a complete lattice
(V,\leqslant)
to its associated
bilattice
on the set
V^{2}\mathrel{:=}V\times V
;
where elements of
V
correspond to interpretations, the pairs contained in
V^{2}
correspond to
approximations
of such interpretations.
More technically, a pair
(x,y)\in V^{2}
approximates all
z\in V
with
x\leqslant z\leqslant y
.
A pair
(x,y)\in V^{2}
is called
ordered
iff
x\leqslant y
, in other words, iff the interval
[x,y]\mathrel{:=}\left\{z\in V\ \middle|\ x\leqslant z\leqslant y\right\}
is non-empty;
1
1

1

Ordered pairs are called “consistent” in the AFT literature
[
13
]
. In this paper, we will use “consistent” in a logical sense and want to avoid confusion.

a pair
(x,x)
is called
exact
.

There are two natural orderings on
V^{2}
:
(1) the
truth ordering
extending the (truth) lattice ordering
\leqslant
,
(x_{1},x_{2})\leq_{t}(y_{1},y_{2})\mathrel{:\mkern-11.0mu\mathord{\iff}}x_{1}\leqslant y_{1}\;\text{and}\;x_{2}\leqslant y_{2}
,
and
(2)
the
precision ordering

(x_{1},x_{2})\leq_{p}(y_{1},y_{2})\mathrel{:\mkern-11.0mu\mathord{\iff}}x_{1}\leqslant y_{1}\;\text{and}\;y_{2}\leqslant x_{2}
,
comparing intervals by precision of approximation.
The lattice operations induced by
\leq_{t}
are
glb
\wedge_{t}
with
(x_{1},x_{2})\wedge_{t}(y_{1},y_{2})\mathrel{:=}(x_{1}\wedge y_{1},x_{2}\wedge y_{2})
and
lub
\vee_{t}
with
(x_{1},x_{2})\vee_{t}(y_{1},y_{2})\mathrel{:=}(x_{1}\vee y_{1},x_{2}\vee y_{2})
.
The lattice operations induced by
\leq_{p}
are
glb
\otimes
with

(x_{1},y_{1})\otimes(x_{2},y_{2})\mathrel{:=}(x_{1}\wedge x_{2},y_{1}\vee y_{2})
and
lub
\oplus
with

(x_{1},y_{1})\oplus(x_{2},y_{2})\mathrel{:=}(x_{1}\vee x_{2},y_{1}\wedge y_{2})
.

Just as pairs of
V^{2}
approximate elements of
V
, a foundational idea of DMT
[
13
]
was that operators
O\colon V\to V
can be approximated by operators on
V^{2}
:
An
approximator
is an operator
\mathcal{A}\colon V^{2}\to V^{2}
that is (a)
\leq_{p}
-monotone and (b) maps exact pairs to exact pairs.
By condition (b), we say that
\mathcal{A}

approximates
the operator
O\colon V\to V
with
O(x)=\mathcal{A}(x,x)_{1}
(where
(x,y)_{1}=x
and
(x,y)_{2}=y
);
by condition (a),
\mathcal{A}
is guaranteed to possess (
\leq_{p}
-least) fixpoints.
An approximator
\mathcal{A}
is
symmetric
iff for all
(x,y)\in V^{2}
, we have
\mathcal{A}(x,y)_{2}=\mathcal{A}(y,x)_{1}
;
thus symmetry allows to specify
\mathcal{A}
by giving only
\mathcal{A}(\cdot,\cdot)_{1}
.

The main contribution of DMT
[
13
]
was the association of the
stable approximator
\mathcal{A}^{\mathit{st}}
to an approximator
\mathcal{A}
:
for a complete lattice
(V,\leqslant)
and an approximator
\mathcal{A}\colon V^{2}\to V^{2}
, define the

stable approximator for
\mathcal{A}
as
\mathcal{A}^{\mathit{st}}\colon V^{2}\to V^{2}
by

\mathcal{A}^{\mathit{st}}(x,y)\mathrel{:=}(\mathit{lfp}(\mathcal{A}(\cdot,y)_{1}),\mathit{lfp}(\mathcal{A}(x,\cdot)_{2}))
, where
\mathcal{A}(\cdot,y)_{1}
(resp.
\mathcal{A}(x,\cdot)_{2}
) denotes the operator that maps any
z\in V
to
\mathcal{A}(z,y)_{1}
(resp. to
\mathcal{A}(x,z)_{2}
).
Among other results, DMT
[
13
]
also showed that this construction is well-defined because
both relevant operators

\mathcal{A}(\cdot,y)_{1}\colon V\to V
and

\mathcal{A}(x,\cdot)_{2}\colon V\to V

are
\leqslant
-monotone (as
\mathcal{A}
is an approximator) and thus possess least fixpoints.
Furthermore, any approximator maps ordered pairs to ordered pairs, and the stable approximator
\mathcal{A}^{\mathit{st}}
maps ordered post-fixpoints of
\mathcal{A}
to ordered pairs.
Consequently, not only is
\mathit{lfp}(\mathcal{A})
ordered (and called the
Kripke-Kleene fixpoint
of
\mathcal{A}
), but so is
\mathit{lfp}(\mathcal{A}^{\mathit{st}})
, the
well-founded fixpoint
of
\mathcal{A}
.
Moreover, a fixpoint
(x,y)
of
\mathcal{A}^{\mathit{st}}
, called a
stable fixpoint
of
\mathcal{A}
, is always a
\leqslant
-minimal fixpoint of
O
(when
\mathcal{A}
approximates
O
).
The term “stable” is not coincidental: When using (the normal logic program version of) the transformation operator
T_{P}

[
38
]
as
O
and defining a suitable approximator
\mathcal{A}
(see
Section

4
for a generalization of such an approximator), then the exact fixpoints of
\mathcal{A}^{\mathit{st}}
correspond one-to-one to the stable models of
P

[
13
]
.

2.2
Fuzzy Logic Programming

Generalizing
crisp
logic programming over
V=\left\{0,1\right\}
,
fuzzy
logic programming allows more general sets
V
of truth values, with a prominent example being
V=[0,1]
, the real unit interval
[
26
]
.
This necessitates also generalizing the syntax of rule bodies from mere conjunctions of atoms as in the crisp case to fuzzy logics’ richer offering of connectives
[
18
]
.
Formally, given a complete lattice
(V,\leqslant)
of truth values,
a set
\Pi
of propositional atoms, and
a family
\mathcal{F}
of fuzzy connectives (where each
f\in\mathcal{F}
has an associated
arity

n\in\mathbb{N}
denoted by
f^{(n)}
when important),
designating a finitely representable subset
V^{\prime}\subseteq V
of truth values that can be explicitly mentioned in programs,
2
2

2

Allowing truth values to occur explicitly in formulas is with the intended usage of being able to represent
some
truth values in rule bodies.
This does not necessarily work for arbitrary sets of truth values (e.g. it does not work for
V=[0,1]\subseteq\mathbb{R}
by sheer cardinality) – in those cases we want to allow to restrict the syntax to a meaningful subset of truth values that can be finitely represented, e.g. the rational unit interval
V^{\prime}=[0,1]\cap\mathbb{Q}
.
the syntax of a
positive fuzzy formula
(
1
) and a
positive fuzzy logic program rule
(
2
) is defined via

\varphi\mathrel{::=}c\mathbin{\mid}p\mathbin{\mid}f(\varphi,\dots,\varphi)

(1)

p\overset{\vartheta}{\leftarrow}\mathcal{B}

(2)

where
c\in V^{\prime}
,
p\in\Pi
, and
f\in\mathcal{F}
, and where
p\in\Pi
,
\vartheta\in V^{\prime}
,
\overset{}{\leftarrow}
is an implicator, and
\mathcal{B}
is a positive fuzzy formula. The atom
p
in (
2
) is called the
head
of the rule,
\mathcal{B}
is called its
body
, and
\vartheta
its
weight
(intuitively indicating the degree of truth assigned to the rule).
A
positive fuzzy logic program
(pFLP)
P
is then a finite set of rules (
2
).

Every connective
f^{(n)}\in\mathcal{F}
has an associated truth function
f^{\text{\raisebox{0.90417pt}{\large$\mkern-1.0mu\mathord{\cdot}$}}}\colon V^{n}\to V
on the lattice
(V,\leqslant)
of truth values;
we require that each
f\in\mathcal{F}
that occurs in a rule body is monotone in each argument,
and each implicator
\overset{}{\leftarrow}^{\text{\raisebox{0.90417pt}{\large$\mkern-1.0mu\mathord{\cdot}$}}}
is monotone in the first and antimonotone in the second argument.

For a complete lattice
(V,\leqslant)
of truth values and
\Pi
a set of atoms,
a
non-paraconsistent (fuzzy) interpretation
is a total function
K\colon\Pi\to V
.
The set of all non-paraconsistent interpretations is denoted by
\mathcal{K}
and again forms a complete lattice with the pointwise lifting of
\leqslant
.
The evaluation of atoms by an interpretation
K
extends to arbitrary formulas
\varphi
via structural induction, where we denote the truth value of
\varphi
under
K
by
\left[K\right]\!\left(\varphi\right)
:
for connective
f^{(n)}\in\mathcal{F}
, we define
\left[K\right]\!\left(f(\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n})\right)\mathrel{:=}f^{\text{\raisebox{0.90417pt}{\large$\mkern-1.0mu\mathord{\cdot}$}}}(\left[K\right]\!\left(\varphi_{1}\right),\ldots,\left[K\right]\!\left(\varphi_{n}\right))
(and in the base case clearly
\left[K\right]\!\left(p\right)\mathrel{:=}K(p)
).
Given an interpretation
K\in\mathcal{K}
, a weighted rule
p\overset{\vartheta}{\leftarrow}\mathcal{B}
is
satisfied
by
K
iff

\vartheta\leqslant K(p)\overset{}{\leftarrow}^{\text{\raisebox{0.90417pt}{\large$\mkern-1.0mu\mathord{\cdot}$}}}\left[K\right]\!\left(\mathcal{B}\right)
.
An interpretation
K
is a
model
of the program
P
iff it satisfies all rules in
P
.
Intuitively,
K
satisfies a rule if it makes the rule “at least as true” as its weight
\vartheta
– the typical notion of fuzzy modelhood.

To guarantee soundness of inference, fuzzy logic programming usually employs pairs of implicators
\overset{}{\leftarrow}
and conjunctors
\land
that “belong together”:
formally, a pair
(\overset{}{\leftarrow}^{\text{\raisebox{0.90417pt}{\large$\mkern-1.0mu\mathord{\cdot}$}}},\land^{\text{\raisebox{0.90417pt}{\large$\mkern-1.0mu\mathord{\cdot}$}}})
is called an
adjoint pair

[
30
]
iff
for all
x,y,z\in V
of a partially ordered set
(V,\leqslant)
,
x\land^{\text{\raisebox{0.90417pt}{\large$\mkern-1.0mu\mathord{\cdot}$}}}y\leqslant z\mathrel{\text{ iff }}y\leqslant z\overset{}{\leftarrow}^{\text{\raisebox{0.90417pt}{\large$\mkern-1.0mu\mathord{\cdot}$}}}x
.
In view of adjoint pairs, satisfaction of a rule
p\overset{\vartheta}{\leftarrow}\mathcal{B}
by an interpretation

can equivalently be stated as
\vartheta\land^{\text{\raisebox{0.90417pt}{\large$\mkern-1.0mu\mathord{\cdot}$}}}\left[K\right]\!\left(\mathcal{B}\right)\leqslant K(p)
.
For one, this allows to define a fuzzy generalization of the one-step consequence operator given by van Emden and Kowalski
[
38
]
:
For pFLP
P
, operator
T_{P}\colon\mathcal{K}\to\mathcal{K}

[
43
,
30
]

is defined by setting, for each
p\in\Pi
,

T_{P}(K)(p)\mathrel{:=}\bigvee\!\left\{\vartheta\land^{\text{\raisebox{0.90417pt}{\large$\mkern-1.0mu\mathord{\cdot}$}}}\left[K\right]\!\left(\mathcal{B}\right)\ \middle|\ \mathord{p\overset{\vartheta}{\leftarrow}\mathcal{B}}\in P\right\}

(3)

The semantics of a pFLP
P
can then be characterized by the pre-fixpoints of
T_{P}

[
43
, Theorem 2.2]
:
interpretation
K\in\mathcal{K}
is a model of

iff
T_{P}(K)\leqslant K
, generalizing a classical result by van Emden and Kowalski
[
38
]
.
An equally useful consequence of adjoint pairs is that they allow us to “move” weights of rules into the body, i.e. writing (
2
) equivalently as its
weight-free
version
p\overset{}{\leftarrow}\vartheta\land\mathcal{B}
(using the
\land

(\overset{}{\leftarrow}^{\text{\raisebox{0.90417pt}{\large$\mkern-1.0mu\mathord{\cdot}$}}},\land^{\text{\raisebox{0.90417pt}{\large$\mkern-1.0mu\mathord{\cdot}$}}})
is an adjoint pair).
As this allows for a simpler notation without sacrificing expressiveness (e.g. the RHS of (

\bigvee\!\left\{\left[K\right]\!\left(\mathcal{B}\right)\ \middle|\ p\overset{}{\leftarrow}\mathcal{B}\in P\right\}
), in what follows we consider only weight-free rules.

2.3
Normal Fuzzy Logic Programs

Syntactically, normal FLPs (nFLPs) extend (
1
) with the case
\mathord{\sim}p
where
\mathord{\sim}
is “negation as failure”.
The remaining notions for positive formulas, rules, and programs carry over to the normal case as expected; in particular, the evaluation of a normal fuzzy formula
\varphi
under
K\in\mathcal{K}
has the additional inductive case
\left[K\right]\!\left(\mathord{\sim}\varphi\right)\mathrel{:=}\mathord{\mathord{\sim}^{\text{\raisebox{0.90417pt}{\large$\mkern-1.0mu\mathord{\cdot}$}}}\mkern-2.0mu}\left[K\right]\!\left(\varphi\right)

This directly extends the one-step consequence operator (
3
) to the case of

FLPs, with models of
P
and pre-fixpoints of

still being in one-to-one correspondence.
Alas, as in the crisp case, the occurrence of negation
\mathord{\sim}
leads to

no longer being necessarily monotone, whence several authors generalized (crisp) stable-model and well-founded semantics to the fuzzy case

28

21
,
9
]
, by hand-crafting the definitions.

Recently, Kettmann et al.
[
23
]
have presented an approximator

of

for the case of nFLPs, and showed that this one approximator (via the standard definitions of approximation fixpoint theory) uniformly reconstructs several hand-crafted semantics:
the well-founded fixpoint of
\mathcal{A}_{P}
coincides with the fuzzy well-founded semantics defined by Loyer and Straccia
[
28
]
;
the exact stable fixpoints of
\mathcal{A}_{P}
coincide with the fuzzy stable models defined by Cornejo et al.
[
9
]
. The operator we will present in Section

is a generalization of
\mathcal{A}_{P}
.

Kettmann et al.
[
23
]
have not explicitly discussed the work of Janssen et al.
[
21
]
;
however, as the latter base their semantics on the definitions of Medina et al
[
30
]
, it follows that the semantics used in
[
21
]
is also covered by
\mathcal{A}_{P}
.

3
Extended Fuzzy Logic Programs

In this paper, we are interested in
extended
fuzzy logic programs.
Firstly, “extended” means that we allow two kinds of negation –

weak (default) negation

\mathord{\sim}
, which captures the absence of a proof, and
strong (classical) negation

\neg
, which expresses explicit falsity.
Secondly, we abstract away from concrete sets
V
of truth values, e.g.
V=\left\{0,1\right\}
in the Boolean case, or
V=[0,1]
in the case of fuzzy LPs
[
43
]
, but merely require the set
V
of truth values to be equipped with a (possibly partial) truth ordering
\leqslant
such that
(V,\leqslant)
forms a complete lattice.

Syntactically, the move from normal to
extended
FLPs sees us replacing atoms with literals across definitions. Based on a set
\Pi
of propositional atoms, we define the set of
literals
as
\Pi^{\pm}\mathrel{:=}\Pi\cup\Pi^{-}
, where
\Pi
is the set of
positive
literals and
\Pi^{-}\mathrel{:=}\left\{\neg p\mid p\in\Pi\right\}
the set of
negative
literals.

Definition 1

Let
(V,\leqslant)
be a complete lattice of truth values with

V^{\prime}\subseteq V
computable and

\Pi
be a set of atoms.
With
c\in V^{\prime}
,
\ell\in\Pi^{\pm}
, and
f\in\mathcal{F}
, an
extended fuzzy formula
\varphi
is of the form

\varphi\mathrel{::=}c\mathbin{\mid}\ell\mathbin{\mid}\mathord{\sim}\ell\mathbin{\mid}f(\varphi,\dots,\varphi)

(4)

and an
extended fuzzy logic program rule
is of the form
\ell\overset{}{\leftarrow}\mathcal{B}
,
where, differently from (
1
) and (
2
),
\ell\in\Pi^{\pm}
, and
\mathcal{B}
is an extended fuzzy formula.
An
extended fuzzy logic program
(
eFLP
)

Definition 2

A

In the special case of fuzzy logic with

The first step in our reconstruction is defining a consequence operator that characterizes this model semantics.

Definition 3

Let
P
be an extended fuzzy logic program.
Its associated

is given by

\displaystyle T_{P}(I)(p)

\displaystyle\mathrel{:=}\left(T_{P}^{+}(I)(p),T_{P}^{-}(I)(p)\right)\text{ with}

\displaystyle T_{P}^{+}(I)(p)

\displaystyle\mathrel{:=}\bigvee\!\left\{\widehat{I}\!\left(\mathcal{B}\right)\ \middle|\ p\leftarrow{}\mathcal{B}\in P\right\}\text{ and }T_{P}^{-}(I)(p)\mathrel{:=}\bigvee\!\left\{\widehat{I}\!\left(\mathcal{B}\right)\ \middle|\ \neg p\leftarrow{}\mathcal{B}\in P\right\}

T_{P}
is still a van-Emden-Kowalski

Proposition 1

For any eFLP
P
,
I\in\mathcal{I}
is a model of
P
iff

T_{P}(I)\leq_{t}I
.

Proof

We first note that for
any two paraconsistent fuzzy interpretations

, we have

\displaystyle I\leq_{t}J

\displaystyle\iff\forall p\in\Pi:I(p)_{1}\leqslant J(p)_{1}\;\text{and}\;I(p)_{2}\leqslant J(p)_{2}

\displaystyle\iff\forall p\in\Pi:\widehat{I}\!\left(p\right)\leqslant\widehat{J}\!\left(p\right)\;\text{and}\;\widehat{I}\!\left(\neg p\right)\leqslant\widehat{J}\!\left(\neg p\right)

\displaystyle\iff\forall\ell\in\Pi^{\pm}:\widehat{I}\!\left(\ell\right)\leqslant\widehat{J}\!\left(\ell\right)

Furthermore, for any rule
p\leftarrow{}\mathcal{B}\in P
, we have

\widehat{I}\!\left(\mathcal{B}\right)\leqslant T_{P}^{+}(I)(p)=T_{P}(I)(p)_{1}=\widehat{T_{P}(I)}\!\left(p\right)

and similarly for any rule
\neg p\leftarrow{}\mathcal{B}\in P
, we get

\widehat{I}\!\left(\mathcal{B}\right)\leqslant T_{P}^{-}(I)(p)=T_{P}(I)(p)_{2}=\widehat{T_{P}(I)}\!\left(\neg p\right)

whence for any rule
\ell\leftarrow{}\mathcal{B}\in P
, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{I}\!\left(\mathcal{B}\right)\leqslant\widehat{T_{P}(I)}\!\left(\ell\right)

(
\dagger
)

“if”:

Let
T_{P}(I)\leq_{t}I
.
We directly get

\forall\ell\in\Pi^{\pm}:\widehat{T_{P}(I)}\!\left(\ell\right)\leqslant\widehat{I}\!\left(\ell\right)

and with (
\dagger
) obtain that for any
\ell\leftarrow{}\mathcal{B}\in P

\widehat{I}\!\left(\mathcal{B}\right)\leqslant\widehat{T_{P}(I)}\!\left(\ell\right)\leqslant\widehat{I}\!\left(\ell\right)

whence

is a model of
P
.

“only if”:

Assume
\widehat{I}\!\left(\mathcal{B}\right)\leqslant\widehat{I}\!\left(\ell\right)
for all

Let

be arbitrary and define

By assumption,

We can also show that for formulas not containing default negation
\mathord{\sim}
, evaluation of formulas behaves monotonically with respect to the relevant truth orderings.

Lemma A.1

For all
I,J\in\mathcal{I}
and
\mathord{\sim}
-free formula
\varphi
, we have

I\leq_{t}J\implies\widehat{I}\!\left(\varphi\right)\leqslant\widehat{J}\!\left(\varphi\right)

Proof

Let
I\leq_{t}J
.
We use induction on the structure of
\varphi
to show the claim.

\varphi=p
:

We have
\widehat{I}\!\left(p\right)=I_{1}(p)\leqslant J_{1}(p)=\widehat{J}\!\left(p\right)
.

\varphi=\neg p
:

We have
\widehat{I}\!\left(\neg p\right)=I_{2}(p)\leqslant J_{2}(p)=\widehat{J}\!\left(\neg p\right)
.

\varphi=f(\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n})
:

By induction hypothesis, for any
1\leq i\leq n
, we have
\widehat{I}\!\left(\varphi_{i}\right)\leqslant\widehat{I}\!\left(\varphi_{i}\right)
.
The claim now follows from pointwise

.∎

Thus for

-free extended logic programs, the consequence operator is

-monotone.

Proposition 2

For a
\mathord{\sim}
-free eFLP

Assume

.
Let

be arbitrary and denote

\mathbf{B}_{\ell}\mathrel{:=}\left\{\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\ \middle|\ \ell\leftarrow{}\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\in P\right\}

Now for any

4
An Approximator for Extended Fuzzy LPs

We now define an approximator of the immediate consequence operator for the full language of eFLPs with weak negation.
The relevant lattice-theoretic structure for the immediate consequence operator, i.e. the operator we approximate, is
(\mathcal{I},\leq_{t})
.
As is customary for AFT, this means that our approximator will operate on the bilattice based on
(\mathcal{I},\leq_{t})
, which we denote by
(\mathcal{I}^{2},\leqq_{p})
and refer to as the (precision)
tetralattice
.
3
3

3

The notion of tetralattice is applicable since
(\mathcal{I},\leq_{t})
is technically isomorphic to the (truth) bilattice based on
(\mathcal{K},\leqslant)
.

More formally, for two pairs
(L,U),(M,T)\in\mathcal{I}^{2}
we have

(L,U)\leqq_{p}(M,T)\mathrel{:\mkern-11.0mu\mathord{\iff}}L\leq_{t}M\;\text{and}\;T\leq_{t}U
.
The least element of
(\mathcal{I}^{2},\leqq_{p})
is the pair
(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{1})
, where
\mathbf{0}\mathrel{:=}\left\{p\mapsto(0,0)\ \middle|\ p\in\Pi\right\}
and
\mathbf{1}\mathrel{:=}\left\{p\mapsto(1,1)\ \middle|\ p\in\Pi\right\}
are the least and greatest element of
(\mathcal{I},\leq_{t})
, respectively.

Definition 4

For eFLP
P
, define symmetric approximator
\mathcal{T}_{P}\colon\mathcal{I}^{2}\to\mathcal{I}^{2}
with

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{P}(L,U)

\displaystyle\mathrel{:=}\left(\mathcal{T}_{P}(L,U)_{1},\mathcal{T}_{P}(U,L)_{1}\right)\hskip-3.58795pt

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{P}(L,U)_{1}(p)

\displaystyle\mathrel{:=}\left(\mathcal{T}_{P}^{+}(L,U)_{1}(p),\mathcal{T}_{P}^{-}(L,U)_{1}(p)\right)

\displaystyle\hskip-8.8543pt\mathcal{T}_{P}^{+}(L,U)_{1}(p)

\displaystyle\mathrel{:=}\bigvee\!\left\{\widehat{\widehat{(L,U)}}\!\left(\mathcal{B}\right)\ \middle|\ p\leftarrow{}\mathcal{B}\in P\right\}\hskip-3.58795pt

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{P}^{-}(L,U)_{1}(p)

\displaystyle\mathrel{:=}\bigvee\!\left\{\widehat{\widehat{(L,U)}}\!\left(\mathcal{B}\right)\ \middle|\ \neg p\leftarrow{}\mathcal{B}\in P\right\}

There,
\widehat{\widehat{(L,U)}}\!\left(\varphi\right)
is defined via induction with base cases

\widehat{\widehat{(L,U)}}\!\left(\ell\right)\mathrel{:=}\widehat{L}\!\left(\ell\right)

\widehat{\widehat{(L,U)}}\!\left(\mathord{\sim}\ell\right)\mathrel{:=}\mathord{\mathord{\sim}^{\text{\raisebox{0.90417pt}{\large$\mkern-1.0mu\mathord{\cdot}$}}}\mkern-2.0mu}\widehat{U}\!\left(\ell\right)

\widehat{\widehat{(L,U)}}\!\left(f(\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n})\right)\mathrel{:=}f^{\text{\raisebox{0.90417pt}{\large$\mkern-1.0mu\mathord{\cdot}$}}}\!\!\left(\widehat{\widehat{(L,U)}}\!\left(\varphi_{1}\right),\ldots,\widehat{\widehat{(L,U)}}\!\left(\varphi_{n}\right)\right)
for any
f^{(n)}\in\mathcal{F}
.

Note that again,
\widehat{\widehat{(L,U)}}\!\left(\varphi\right)\in V

We look at an example to illustrate the operator, with special attention to the treatment of weak and strong negation.

Example 2

Recall eFLP

(over

Applying the approximator to the pair

Lemma A.2

For any eFLP

In this section, we restrict our attention to the classical two truth values

and

Existing (paraconsistent) semantics for extended logic programs

We start off with showing that our notion of interpretation is actually order-isomorphic to such sets of literals.

Proposition 4

For

\zeta\colon\mathcal{I}\to 2^{\Pi^{\pm}}

\zeta(I)\mathrel{:=}\left\{p\in\Pi\ \middle|\ I(p)_{1}=1\right\}\cup\left\{\neg p\ \middle|\ I(p)_{2}=1\right\}

Then
\zeta
is an order-isomorphism from

to

(1)

(2) for all

I\leq_{t}J\iff\zeta(I)\subseteq\zeta(J)

Proof

(1)

\zeta
is injective:

Assume w.l.o.g. that
\ell\in\zeta(I)\setminus\zeta(J)
.
Then
\widehat{I}\!\left(\ell\right)\neq\widehat{J}\!\left(\ell\right)
whence
I\neq J
.

\zeta
is surjective:

We give the inverse of
\zeta
.
For any
S\subseteq\Pi^{\pm}
, define interpretation
I_{S}
such that

I_{S}(p)\mathrel{:=}(\left|\left\{p\right\}\cap S\right|,\left|\left\{\neg p\right\}\cap S\right|)
for any
p\in\Pi
.
It is then easy to see that
\zeta(I_{S})=S
.

(2)

“
\implies
”:

Let
I\leq_{t}J
and consider
p\in\zeta(I)
.
Then
I(p)_{1}=1
and by
I\leq_{t}J
we get
J(p)_{1}=1
.
Thus,
p\in\zeta(J)
.
Likewise, if
\neg p\in\zeta(I)
, then
I(p)_{2}=1
.
By
I\leq_{t}J
, we get
J(p)_{2}=1
, whence
\neg p\in\zeta(J)
.

“
\impliedby
”:

Let
p\in\Pi
be arbitrary.
If
I(p)_{1}=0
or
I(p)_{2}=0
there is nothing to show.
If
I(p)_{1}=1
then
p\in\zeta(I)\subseteq\zeta(J)
and thus
J(p)_{1}=1
.
Likewise, if
I(p)_{2}=1
then
\neg p\in\zeta(I)\subseteq\zeta(J)
whence
J(p)_{2}=1
.

This order-isomorphism thus preserves all lubs and glbs: for example, we have
\zeta(\bigsqcup\mathcal{J})=\bigcup\zeta(\mathcal{J})
for any
\mathcal{J}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
.
We will employ this later on.

5.1
Paraconsistent Well-Founded Semantics
[
34
]

Sakama
[
34
]
defines a well-founded semantics for extended (crisp) logic programs.
His definition uses operators and is based on Przymusinski’s definition
[
31
]
of the well-founded semantics for normal logic programs
[
40
]
.
Again, Sakama’s model theory is seemingly different:
an interpretation
\langle\sigma,\delta\rangle\in 2^{\Pi^{\pm}}\times 2^{\Pi^{\pm}}
is a pair of sets of literals where
\sigma
represents
proven facts
while
\delta
represents
default facts
.
Similarly to (simple) paraconsistent interpretations, for
V=\left\{0,1\right\}
these interpretations are isomorphic to pairs
(L,U)\in\mathcal{I}^{2}
of paraconsistent interpretations via

\zeta_{2}\colon\mathcal{I}^{2}\to 2^{\Pi^{\pm}}\times 2^{\Pi^{\pm}}
with

\zeta_{2}(L,U)\mathrel{:=}\left(\zeta(L),\overline{\zeta(U)}\right)
, where

\overline{S}\mathrel{:=}\Pi^{\pm}\setminus S
.

For an interpretation
\langle\sigma,\delta\rangle
, the
satisfaction
of a formula
F
, denoted
\langle\sigma,\delta\rangle\Vdash F
, is defined inductively as follows:
for any literal
\ell\in\Pi^{\pm}
,

\langle\sigma,\delta\rangle\Vdash\ell
iff
\ell\in\sigma
, and

\langle\sigma,\delta\rangle\Vdash\mathord{\sim}\ell
iff
\ell\in\delta
; then,

\langle\sigma,\delta\rangle\Vdash F\land G
iff
\langle\sigma,\delta\rangle\Vdash F
and
\langle\sigma,\delta\rangle\Vdash G
.

Sakama’s paraconsistent well-founded semantics
[
34
]
for extended LPs is then defined using the following operators:

\displaystyle\Phi_{P,\langle\sigma,\delta\rangle}(\alpha)

\displaystyle\mathrel{:=}\left\{\ell\in\Pi^{\pm}\ \middle|\ \exists\ell\leftarrow\mathcal{B}\in P:\mathcal{B}^{\not\mathord{\sim}}\subseteq\sigma\cup\alpha\text{ and }\mathcal{B}^{\mathord{\sim}}\subseteq\delta\right\}

\displaystyle\Psi_{P,\langle\sigma,\delta\rangle}(\alpha)

\displaystyle\mathrel{:=}\left\{\ell\in\Pi^{\pm}\ \middle|\ \forall\ell\leftarrow\mathcal{B}\in P:\mathcal{B}^{\not\mathord{\sim}}\cap(\alpha\cup\delta)\neq\emptyset\text{ or }\mathcal{B}^{\mathord{\sim}}\cap\sigma\neq\emptyset\right\}

\displaystyle\Theta_{P}(\sigma,\delta)

\displaystyle\mathrel{:=}(\sigma\cup\mathit{lfp}(\Phi_{P,\langle\sigma,\delta\rangle}),\delta\cup\mathit{gfp}(\Psi_{P,\langle\sigma,\delta\rangle}))

where the
Sakama well-founded model
of
P
is given by
\mathit{lfp}(\Theta_{P})
in the complete lattice
(2^{\Pi^{\pm}}\times 2^{\Pi^{\pm}},\leq_{S})
with
(A,B)\leq_{S}(C,D)
iff
A\subseteq C
and
B\subseteq D
.

In what follows, we will formally establish that the Sakama well-founded model of an extended FLP
P
coincides (modulo
\zeta_{2}
) with the standard AFT definition of the well-founded fixpoint of our approximator
\mathcal{T}_{P}
from
Definition

4
.
To this end, we first introduce some more notation.
To indicate the interpretations during the construction of the Sakama well-founded model, we use superscripts, that is,

(\sigma^{(0)},\delta^{(0)})\mathrel{:=}(\emptyset,\emptyset)
and subsequently
(\sigma^{(i+1)},\delta^{(i+1)})\mathrel{:=}\Theta_{P}(\sigma^{(i)},\delta^{(i)})
for
i\geq 0
.
Analogously, we denote the steps of the corresponding nested fixpoint constructions of newly proven facts at step
i
by

\alpha^{(i,0)}\mathrel{:=}\emptyset
and
\alpha^{(i,j+1)}\mathrel{:=}\Phi_{P,\langle\sigma^{(i)},\delta^{(i)}\rangle}(\alpha^{(i,j)})
, and
for the default facts use
\beta^{(i,0)}\mathrel{:=}\Pi^{\pm}
and
\beta^{(i,j+1)}\mathrel{:=}\Psi_{P,\langle\sigma^{(i)},\delta^{(i)}\rangle}(\beta^{(i,j)})
.
Similarly, for the construction of the well-founded fixpoint of
\mathcal{T}_{P}
, we denote the sequence of constructed interpretation pairs via

(L^{(0)},U^{(0)})\mathrel{:=}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{1})
and

(L^{(i+1)},U^{(i+1)})\mathrel{:=}(\mathit{lfp}(\mathcal{T}_{P}(\cdot,U^{(i)})_{1}),\mathit{lfp}(\mathcal{T}_{P}(\cdot,L^{(i)})_{1}))
for
i\geq 0
.
For the nested least fixpoint constructions, we denote

I^{(i,0)}\mathrel{:=}\mathbf{0}
and
I^{(i,j+1)}\mathrel{:=}\mathcal{T}_{P}(I^{(i,j)},U^{(i)})_{1}
when constructing new lower bound
L^{(i+1)}
, and

J^{(i,0)}\mathrel{:=}\mathbf{0}
and
J^{(i,j+1)}\mathrel{:=}\mathcal{T}_{P}(J^{(i,j)},L^{(i)})_{1}
for new upper bound
U^{(i+1)}
.

We first observe that any rule body
\mathcal{B}
evaluates to
1
under a pair of paraconsistent interpretations
(I,J)
iff

\mathcal{B}
is satisfied by the corresponding Sakama interpretation
\zeta_{2}(I,J)
.

Lemma A.3

For any
I,J\in\mathcal{I}
and rule body
\mathcal{B}
, we have

\widehat{\widehat{(I,J)}}\!\left(\mathcal{B}\right)=1\iff\left(\zeta(I),\overline{\zeta(J)}\right)\Vdash\mathcal{B}.

Proof

For all interpretations
I,J\in\mathcal{I}
and body formula
\mathcal{B}=\ell_{1}\land\ldots\land\ell_{m}\land\mathord{\sim}\ell_{m+1}\land\ldots\land\mathord{\sim}\ell_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbin{\phantom{\iff}\,}\langle\zeta(I),\overline{\zeta(J)}\rangle\Vdash\mathcal{B}

\displaystyle\iff\mathcal{B}^{\not\mathord{\sim}}\subseteq\zeta(I)\text{ and }\mathcal{B}^{\mathord{\sim}}\subseteq\overline{\zeta(J)}

\displaystyle\iff\text{ for all }\ell\in\mathcal{B}^{\not\mathord{\sim}}:\widehat{I}\!\left(\ell\right)=1\text{ and }\text{for all }\ell\in\mathcal{B}^{\mathord{\sim}}:\widehat{J}\!\left(\ell\right)=0

\displaystyle\iff\widehat{I}\!\left(\ell_{1}\right)\land^{\text{\raisebox{0.90417pt}{\large$\mkern-1.0mu\mathord{\cdot}$}}}\ldots\land^{\text{\raisebox{0.90417pt}{\large$\mkern-1.0mu\mathord{\cdot}$}}}\widehat{I}\!\left(\ell_{m}\right)=1\text{ and }\mathord{\sim}^{\text{\raisebox{0.90417pt}{\large$\mkern-1.0mu\mathord{\cdot}$}}}\widehat{J}\!\left(\ell_{m+1}\right)\land^{\text{\raisebox{0.90417pt}{\large$\mkern-1.0mu\mathord{\cdot}$}}}\ldots\land^{\text{\raisebox{0.90417pt}{\large$\mkern-1.0mu\mathord{\cdot}$}}}\mathord{\sim}^{\text{\raisebox{0.90417pt}{\large$\mkern-1.0mu\mathord{\cdot}$}}}\widehat{J}\!\left(\ell_{n}\right)=1

\displaystyle\iff\widehat{\widehat{(I,J)}}\!\left(\mathcal{B}\right)=1

∎

In turn, satisfaction of a rule body coincides with the truth of the rule head in the approximator’s return value.

Lemma A.4

For all
I,J\in\mathcal{I}
and literals
\ell\in\Pi^{\pm}

\widehat{\mathcal{T}_{P}(I,J)_{1}}\!\left(\ell\right)=1\iff\exists\ell\overset{}{\leftarrow}\mathcal{B}\in P\text{ with }\widehat{\widehat{(I,J)}}\!\left(\mathcal{B}\right)=1

Proof

We do a case distinction on the syntactic form of
\ell

\ell=p\in\Pi
:

From
Definition

4
, it follows directly that

\displaystyle\mathbin{\phantom{\iff}\,}\widehat{\mathcal{T}_{P}(I,J)_{1}}\!\left(p\right)=1

\displaystyle\iff\mathcal{T}_{P}(I,J)_{1}(p)_{1}=1

\displaystyle\iff\mathcal{T}_{P}^{+}(I,J)_{1}(p)=1

\displaystyle\iff\bigvee\!\left\{\widehat{\widehat{(I,J)}}\!\left(\mathcal{B}\right)\ \middle|\ p\leftarrow{}\mathcal{B}\in P\right\}=1

\displaystyle\iff\exists\text{ rule }p\overset{}{\leftarrow}\mathcal{B}\in P\text{ with }\widehat{\widehat{(I,J)}}\!\left(\mathcal{B}\right)=1

\ell=\neg p
for some
p\in\Pi
:

Likewise,
Definition

4

\displaystyle\mathbin{\phantom{\iff}\,}\widehat{\mathcal{T}_{P}(I,J)_{1}}\!\left(\neg p\right)=1

\displaystyle\iff\mathcal{T}_{P}(I,J)_{1}(p)_{2}=1

\displaystyle\iff\mathcal{T}_{P}^{-}(I,J)_{1}(p)=1

\displaystyle\iff\bigvee\!\left\{\widehat{\widehat{(I,J)}}\!\left(\mathcal{B}\right)\ \middle|\ \neg p\leftarrow{}\mathcal{B}\in P\right\}=1

\displaystyle\iff\exists\text{ rule }\neg p\overset{}{\leftarrow}\mathcal{B}\in P\text{ with }\widehat{\widehat{(I,J)}}\!\left(\mathcal{B}\right)=1

This leads to the first correspondence result on the involved operators:

and

Proposition 7

Theorem 5.1

For any extended LP

\zeta_{2}(\mathit{lfp}(\mathcal{T}_{P}^{\mathit{st}}))=\mathit{lfp}(\Theta_{P})

Proof

6
Reconstructions: Stable-Model Semantics for Extended Fuzzy Logic Programs

We now again consider the general case of truth values

This in particular subsumes the case of multi-adjoint fuzzy logic programming, where we reconstruct two stable model semantics for extended
fuzzy
logic programs.
It should be noted that neither of the two existing approaches we subsequently reconstruct allow for
paraconsistent

6.1
Saad’s Stable Models for Extended Fuzzy Logic Programs
[
32
]

Saad
[
32
]
uses a slightly different syntax: instead of the rules themselves, all
literals
occurring in rules are annotated.
Concretely, a Saad rule
[
32
]
has the form

\ell_{0}\mathord{:}c_{0}\leftarrow{}\ell_{1}\mathord{:}c_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m}\mathord{:}c_{m},\mathord{\sim}\ell_{m+1}\mathord{:}c_{m+1},\ldots,\mathord{\sim}\ell_{n}\mathord{:}c_{n}

(5)

with
\ell_{i}\in\Pi^{\pm}
and
c_{i}\in V^{\prime}
for
0\leq i\leq n
, where
intuitively
\ell\mathord{:}c
expresses a lower truth value bound
c
for literal
\ell
.
The relevant notion of interpretation for the work of Saad
[
32
]
is the set
\mathcal{G}
of
extended
fuzzy interpretations, where
G\in\mathcal{G}
is a
partial
function
G\colon\Pi^{\pm}\to[0,1]
.
By permitting literals to remain
undefined
, the interpretation can distinguish between explicit falsity and the absence of information.
Then,
G

satisfies

\ell\mathord{:}c
iff
G(\ell)\geq c
and
\ell
is in the domain of
G
(denoted
\ell\in\mathrm{dom}(G)
),

G

satisfies

\mathord{\sim}\ell\mathord{:}c
iff
\ell\notin\mathrm{dom}(G)
or [
\ell\in\mathrm{dom}(G)
and
G(\ell)<c
],
and
G

satisfies
a rule (
5
) iff the head is satisfied or some body literal is not satisfied.
An interpretation
G
is considered
inconsistent
if there exist complementary literals
\ell,\neg\ell\in\mathrm{dom}(G)
such that
G(\neg\ell)\neq 1-G(\ell)
.

As in crisp logic programs,
fuzzy stable models
are defined in terms of a reduct.
Given a Saad program
Q
and a (guessed) interpretation
G
, the
reduct
w.r.t.
G
is defined as
Q_{G}\mathrel{:=}\left\{r_{G}\mid r\in Q\text{ and }G\text{ satisfies all }\mathord{\sim}\ell_{j}\mathord{:}c_{j}\text{ in }r\right\}
, where
r_{G}
is obtained from
r
by removing all negative body literals. Then, Saad
[
32
]
shows that
G\in\mathcal{G}
is a
fuzzy stable model
of
Q
iff it is the least fixpoint of the immediate consequence-operator
O_{Q_{G}}
on the complete lattice
(\mathcal{G},\leq_{A})
with
H_{1}\leq_{A}H_{2}
iff
\mathrm{dom}(H_{1})\subseteq\mathrm{dom}(H_{2})
and
H_{1}(\ell)\leq H_{2}(\ell)
for all
\ell\in\Pi^{\pm}
.
For a Saad program
Q
, the operator
O_{Q}\colon\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{G}
is defined by:

O_{Q}(G)(\ell)\mathrel{:=}\max\left\{c\ \middle|\ \begin{array}[]{l}\ell\mathord{:}c\leftarrow{}\ell_{1}\mathord{:}c_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m}\mathord{:}c_{m},\mathord{\sim}\ell_{m+1}\mathord{:}c_{m+1},\ldots,\mathord{\sim}\ell_{n}\mathord{:}c_{n}\in Q\\
\text{and }G\text{ satisfies all body literals of the rule}\end{array}\right\}

and is undefined otherwise (i.e., whenever
\ell
has no satisfied rules in
Q
).
However, the least fixpoint of
O_{Q}
may be inconsistent.
Following the approach of Gelfond and Lifschitz
[
16
]
, Saad
[
32
]
stipulates that any inconsistent fuzzy stable model is replaced by the mapping
\Pi^{\pm}\to\left\{1\right\}
.
In what follows, we reconstruct this least-fixpoint characterization within our framework (by means of a translation).

Firstly, to an extended interpretation
G\in\mathcal{G}
, we associate a corresponding paraconsistent interpretation
I_{G}\colon\Pi\cup\left\{d_{\ell}\ \middle|\ \ell\in\Pi^{\pm}\right\}\to V^{2}
, where the fresh atoms

serve to record whether a literal
\ell
belongs to the domain of
G
.
We set

I_{G}(p)_{1}\mathrel{:=}G(p)
if

and
0
otherwise;

I_{G}(p)_{2}\mathrel{:=}G(\neg p)
if
\neg p\in\mathrm{dom}(G)
and
0
otherwise, for

;

I_{G}(d_{\ell})\mathrel{:=}(1,0)
if
\ell\in\mathrm{dom}(G)
and
(0,0)
otherwise.

The idea for our translation is to introduce, for each

occurring in a Saad program
[
32
]
, a new unary connective
\mathord{\trianglerighteq_{c}}
with associated truth function

\mathord{\trianglerighteq_{c}}^{\text{\raisebox{0.90417pt}{\large$\mkern-1.0mu\mathord{\cdot}$}}}(x)\mathrel{:=}1\text{ if }x\geq c\text{ and }0\text{ otherwise}
, and to use it to check lower bounds in rule bodies.
For any literal
\ell
occurring as
\ell\mathord{:}0

\mathord{\trianglerighteq_{0}}(\ell)
simply becomes the atom

used to check whether “
\ell\in\mathrm{dom}(G)

Discussion

We already discussed relations with a host of related work in the reconstructive sections (Section
5
and
6
).
Here, we survey other related work.
One line of work on extended logic programs that we did not reconstruct is that done by Alferes, Damásio, Pereira, and Alcântara
[
2
,
12
,
1
]
.
They introduce the
well-founded semantics for extended logic programs
.
Their semantics is a straightforward adaption of the well-founded semantics for normal logic programming
[
41
]
, but is based on what they call the
principle of coherence
:
“believing
\neg p
entails believing
\mathord{\sim}p
”.
By double negation, this also means that believing
p
entails believing
\mathord{\sim}\neg p
.
In the presence of contradictions, the AFT-perspective we take (and, as shown by the reconstructions, also underlies other approaches) is incompatible with the principle of coherence.
Indeed, as we only consider pairs
(L,U)
s.t.
L\leq_{p}U
, any time we believe
p
and
\neg p
(i.e.
p,\neg p\in L
), we also believe in the non-falsity of
p
and
\neg p
(i.e.
p,\neg p\in U
), which precludes us from believing
\mathord{\sim}\neg p
respectively
\mathord{\sim}p
.
On the other hand, Alcântara et al.
[
1
]
need some additional, restricting assumptions on their negation functions to make full use of the coherence principle:
both classical and default negations must be involutions (self-inverse bijections), and furthermore they must commute.
In our framework, we need no such assumptions and can thus also accommodate Gödel negation (which is not involutive).

Another related work is that of Killen and You
[
24
]
, who use tetralattices to propose an alternative definition of stable revision.
Essentially, they use tetralattices to obtain pairs
(T,F,U,P)
, where
T
gives necessary truth,
F
well-established falsity,
U
possible falsity and
P
possible truth.
On the other hand, we consider pairs of pairs
((L_{1},L_{2}),(U_{1},U_{2}))
where
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
[
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
] represent the possible [necessary] truth of
p
respectively
\neg p
.

We also provide a proof-of-concept implementation in ASP, using the
meta-programming
-capabilities of clingo
[
22
]
. We restricted our implementation to fixpoint and Kripke-Kleene semantics for crisp eFLPs.
In meta-programming we encode a grounded logic program as a set of facts called a
reification
.
The semantics is encoded in a logic program called a
meta-program
, and applied by feeding both the reification and meta-program into clingo for grounding and solving.
Since clingo’s grounder gringo performs some simplifications and defines away strong negation to a constraint, we had to introduce a predicate
snot
which encodes strong negation (instead of using clingo’s strong negation
-
).
More details can be found in the online repository at
https://github.com/JeroenSpaans/sningo
.

Janssen et al.
[
21
]
showed that several common extensions of normal fuzzy logic programs, such as constraints and extended aggregators, can be simulated by normal rules while preserving the fuzzy answer set semantics.
For the extension allowing strong negation, however, the semantics is based on a slightly different notion of modelhood, using the approach of Van Nieuwenborgh et al.
[
42
]
.
In this setting, two special aggregators are required to determine the extent to which a fuzzy interpretation
I\colon\Pi^{\pm}\to V
is
(1) consistent with respect to the consistency values induced by an associated consistency function, and
(2) accumulates the truth values of all rules of a program
P
to obtain the degree to which
I
is a model of
P
.
We aim to generalize this approach in future work.
Other future work foreseen includes the generalization of this work to extended or fuzzy logic programs with disjunction, using non-deterministic AFT
[
19
]
.
Furthermore, we see several potential applications of extended fuzzy logic programs, e.g. in law, where conflicting obligations have to be combined with fuzzy concepts
[
3
]
, or in neuro-symbolic AI, where fuzzy logic is often used to integrate logical rules in a loss function
[
35
]
, and where hallucinations might cause inconsistencies.
