 Preface
 This report presents the results of an in-depth partnership between the community of Molokai,
 Hawaii, and the National Laboratory of the Rockies through the U.S. Department of Energy’s
 Energy to Communities program. Although technical in nature, it reflects the strength of a
 community-led vision—one centered on resilience, access, and locally guided decision-making
 on energy infrastructure. This project supports that vision by translating community inputs and
 needs into actionable infrastructure scenarios and validating those scenarios with trusted analysis
 and tools.

 The National Laboratory of the Rockies’ role was not to prescribe a solution but to provide
 information, data, and modeling insights that help the community weigh options for decisions
 ranging from identifying sites with lower grid upgrade costs to evaluating inverter-based
 resource strategies and understanding what makes systems viable in various regions of the island.

 This report provides both technical guidance and a foundation for the next phase of action. It is
 intended not only to serve Molokai but also to offer a model of how trusted collaboration
 between communities and technical experts can deliver strong, place-based outcomes.

 Executive Summary
 The Molokai, Hawaii, Energy to Communities Energyshed project is a partnership between the
 National Laboratory of the Rockies and a community coalition comprising Shake Energy
 Collaborative, the Molokai Clean Energy Hui, Sustāinable Molokai, and Hoʻāhu Energy
 Cooperative Molokai to advance the Molokai Community Energy Resilience Action Plan
 (CERAP). The project was supported by resource advisors Hawaiian Electric Company and the
 Hawaii State Energy Office. This project supports a community-defined portfolio of energy
 solutions that aims to enhance energy resilience and build local capacity for informed decision-
 making on Molokai, Hawaii.

 This project builds on the direction already set in Hawaiian Electric’s Integrated Grid Plan (IGP)
 and Molokai’s community-led energy initiatives. Energy-to-Communities’ work provides
 additional technical and community-informed detail to help implement the priorities identified in
 the IGP including solar, battery storage, and distributed energy resources.

 The primary goal of this project was to present information that can help determine viable
 scenarios for Molokai, prioritizing the community’s values-based energy system requirements
 while capturing key project infrastructure specifications such as physical location, size/capacity,
 and technology/equipment type.

 The secondary goals are as follows:

 • To perform a community co-design process that satisfies the community’s need to make
 informed, confident decisions on preferred grid configuration scenarios and realistic
 trade-offs associated with each option
 • To produce a community co-design product that satisfies the needs of the community,
 local electric utility, and government agencies to readily implement specific renewable
 energy projects that have been deemed technologically, economically, and socially valid.
 The following key findings emerged from the technical and community-informed analyses.
 These insights can guide the community, utility, and future consultants as the island advances
 select energy projects toward implementation.

 Energy Generation and Storage Needs
 The project team conducted an initial analysis to determine the range of sizing for projects across
 the island. The base forecast from the IGP 2023 study included an annual load of ~28 gigawatt-
 hours compared with the largest load observed in the previous 5 years―approximately 32
 gigawatt-hours, and slightly above the IGP forecast for the next decade (Hawaiian Electric
 2023). We considered a higher load to better understand the potential impacts to grid upgrades
 discussed in the next subsection. Only on-island generation was considered. The following are
 the key findings of our analysis of the island’s potential energy generation and storage needs:

 • To achieve approximately 90% locally generated energy, i.e. on-island generation,
 Molokai would require approximately 15–21 megawatts of solar and 55–90 megawatt-
 hours of battery storage, depending on siting and configuration. The range of outputs

 Grid Upgrade Considerations
 Findings related to grid upgrades include the following:

 • New generation in all areas will trigger grid upgrades.
 • Limiting distributed energy resource size, adjusting location, or sequencing projects can
 avoid or minimize costly upgrades in some cases. Sequencing projects allows for
 incremental adjustments to utility operations and equipment settings, which may be low-
 or no-cost responses to marginal increases in variable resources. Naturally, large projects
 will require more significant changes. Ultimately, incremental projects may reach a point
 in which costly upgrades might be unavoidable as shown in the loads analysis in the
 previous section. Considerable collaborative discussion on the role of location and sizing
 were held during the first two rounds of this analysis.

 Inverter Ride-Through and Reliability
 Analysis results showed that many commercial inverters currently do not meet Molokai’s strict
 grid voltage ride-through standards (0.80 volts per unit). Solutions such as static synchronous
 compensators or static VAR (Volt-Ampere Reactive) compensators can help fill this gap to
 maintain grid stability. 1
 Siting and sizing outcomes depend on ongoing input from Hawaiian Electric Company and final
 system design. In addition, the load considered throughout this report focuses on a high-load
 scenario to account for grid upgrades. In addition, cost and feasibility estimates assume current
 technology prices and do not account for incentives, tariffs, or permitting timelines.

 This work equips the Molokai community with data to inform and ensure effective

 1 Introduction
 The Molokai Energy to Communities (E2C) Energyshed In-Depth Partnership is an element of
 the Molokai Community Energy Resilience Action Plan (CERAP) 2.0, a community-led
 initiative to develop energy generation and grid upgrade solutions for Molokai. The activities
 undertaken during this project and for CERAP 2.0 were directed by the results of the community
 planning and analysis conducted by the Molokai community during the initial CERAP process in
 2021–2023 2. CERAP 2.0 is supported by the community through an islandwide, co-design
 process and continues to build community capacity and consensus around Molokai’s priority
 project portfolio co-designed in CERAP 1.0. This partnership is supported by a community
 coalition comprising the CERAP leadership team, including Shake Energy Collaborative
 (Shake), the Molokai Clean Energy Hui, Sustʻāinable Molokai, and Hoʻāhu Energy Cooperative
 Molokai. The community coalition collaborated with the National Laboratory of the Rockies
 (NLR) technical team by leading community engagement efforts to provide critical input to the
 technical analysis and ensure the analysis properly reflects the community’s needs. Additional
 technical collaboration was provided by the Hawaiian Electric Company (HECO) and by the
 Hawaii State Energy Office (HSEO), a resource advisor tasked with conducting economic
 analysis.

 This E2C Energyshed In-depth Partnership includes two phases:

 • Phase 1 – Technical Assistance. Technical analysis led by NLR provided to the island
 of Molokai in collaboration with the community coalition. This phase culminates in the
 submission of an implementation plan (see Table 1) by the community coalition that
 outlines how the community will implement findings from the technical analysis.
 • Phase 2 – Implementation Phase. Execution of the implementation plan by the
 community coalition.

 This project provided technical validation and economic evaluation of various energy scenarios
 proposed by the community. The project modeled the distribution system in NLR’s Advanced

 2 Community Objectives
 The CERAP 2.0 vision aims to develop a portfolio of energy projects that are “ready for
 approval and/or procurement” to achieve 100% renewable energy for Molokai island that is
 economically and technically feasible, respectful of Molokai’s culture and environment, and
 strongly supported through an islandwide planning process that is community initiated, driven,
 and led.

 The main goal of this project is to present information that can help determine a culturally,
 economically, and technically viable scenario for Molokai, prioritizing the community’s values-
 based energy system requirements while capturing key project infrastructure specifications such
 as physical location, size/capacity, and technology/equipment type. Secondary goals include the
 following:

 • To perform a co-design process that satisfies the community’s need to make informed,
 confident decisions on preferred grid configuration scenarios and realistic trade-offs
 associated with each option
 • To produce a co-design product that satisfies the needs of the community, local electric
 utility, and government agencies to readily implement specific renewable energy projects
 that have been deemed technologically, economically, and socially valid.
 In addition to the broader project goals outlined earlier, the technical assistance was intended to
 equip the community coalition and Molokai community with actionable information to validate
 their proposed project concepts. Specifically, the analysis evaluated project siting and sizing as
 well as the grid upgrades required for technical and economic viability. By exploring the range
 of siting and sizing variables, the community was provided the parameters they needed to refine
 project specifications and advance toward implementation.

 The results from Phase 1 informed the community of viable energy solutions that could be
 implemented in Phase 2. When conducting Phase 2, the solutions presented in this report could
 be used to engage the local utility and a professional engineering firm to draft and execute the
 development plan―including finalized designs, draft electrical drawings, and a quote on the
 work―and to execute the engineering effort to install the new energy assets and any grid
 upgrades that may be required. Because Molokai has been designated into three zones—west,
 central, and east—the community will need to determine whether a staged implementation is
 preferable and better reflects budgetary and local concerns or whether all three zone plans should
 be implemented simultaneously, which will incur higher immediate costs but allow for a faster,
 islandwide change. To determine the positioning of new assets and upgrades, a certified
 engineering firm and the local utility will need to be engaged. Although the results described in
 this report can provide guidance on estimated costs and complexity, ultimately the community,
 engineering firm, and local utility will determine the implementation process.

 3 Study Approach
 In this project, the study of energy systems for Molokai involved a suite of modeling platforms—
 ARIES (ETAP and RSCAD) and REopt—each serving distinct but complementary purposes.
 The study team used the base forecast in the IGP 2023 to provide a range of technology sizes to
 pursue for each project. The base forecast has an annual load of 28 gigawatt-hours (Figure 2).
 Note, the late 1990s had a significantly higher load due the presence of the Molokai Ranch,
 which was a significant energy user, but ceased operations in 2008 (McAvoy 2008).

 Figure 2. The IGP load forecast based on historic consumption
 (Hawaiian Electric 2023)

 REopt 3, a decision support and optimization tool for distributed energy resource planning, was
 used in the energy planning phase to scale scenarios to meet energy targets, such as the 90%
 renewable energy requirement. The tool helped optimize technology mixes (e.g., solar, storage,
 and diesel backup) and system sizing to align with regional demand estimates. REopt is intended
 for use in prefeasibility planning, initial estimates of technology sizing and economics,
 evaluating multiple energy scenarios, and performing sensitivity analyses. Engineering analysis,
 final system design, assessing grid impacts, additional equipment or upgrade costs, and more
 detailed financial assessments can be determined using the results from a REopt analysis.

 This economic and technical prescreening using REopt ensured the selected scenarios were both
 technically viable and appropriately sized before more extensive power system validation was
 performed in ETAP and ARIES. ARIES provides the backbone for integrating software and
 hardware simulations. It was used in this project to ensure simulation-based scenarios for load
 flows, system resilience, and control schemes could ultimately be tested or emulated in physical
 experiments. The energy needs analysis reduces risk in field deployment by anticipating system-
 level issues before implementation. ETAP and RSCAD were used for load flow and real-time
 power flow analyses. These simulations focused on detecting voltage and thermal violations that
 could damage equipment or degrade system performance. These utility-grade modeling tools

 4 Energy Needs
 4.1 Energy Needs Approach
 The purpose of this study was to assess how much energy generation and storage capacity is
 required to support Molokai’s power grid, as well as inform both community and utility
 decision-making to ensure alignment with Hawaiian Electric’s IGP for the island. In addition to
 specifying the total resources needed to transition away from diesel, the study provided the
 community coalition with guidance on project sizing.

 The team performed techno-economic analysis using REopt (see Section 3 for more details on
 the tool). This analysis examined a range of energy scenarios, including various technology
 combinations of solar photovoltaics (PV); wind; diesel and biodiesel generation; battery energy
 storage systems (BESS); and pumped storage hydropower, as well as hydrogen energy storage
 systems (HESS). 4 The study team performed additional scenarios to determine how the system
 economics and sizing were affected based on percent renewable energy targets. 5

 4.1.1 Pumped Storage Hydropower
 Pumped storage hydropower (PSH) was considered as a storage opportunity for Molokai. We
 obtained the cost and economics of PSH from the in-depth analysis conducted and presented as
 part of ETIPP’s Assessment of Energy Technology Options for the Island of Molokai, Hawaii
 report (Rosenlieb et al. 2026). That assessment aimed to examine and identify potential options
 for integrating PSH into the Molokai power grid. The concepts presented represent a range of
 potential alternatives, paired with various locations. The average nominal capital cost determined
 based on nine locations was $20,700/kW, which was the installed cost used in this analysis. A
 10-hour storage capacity was adopted based on the ETIPP report. Additional cost assumptions
 were obtained from NLR’s Annual Technology Baseline. Based on the ETIPP analysis, PSH was
 considered as an alternative to BESS in this analysis.

 4.1.2 Biodiesel Fuel Analysis
 The primary objective of the Molokai community was to evaluate the renewable, affordable, and
 community acceptable energy solutions across a range of stakeholders using proven energy
 technologies such as solar, BESS, and wind. However, we explored biodiesel as an alternate fuel
 for the generators at the request of HSEO. The community shared concerns that biodiesel might
 be expensive, have lower commercial readiness, and increase negative impacts. Insights from the
 following analysis could therefore help decide whether biodiesel is a suitable solution for
 Molokai. Substituting biodiesel for diesel in scenarios with generators, we performed the
 analysis again to determine the optimal size of the generator selected when the fuel is less cost-
 effective than diesel.

 To study the possibility of using biodiesel to support the grid, HSEO supplied NLR with a
 biodiesel price forecast for 2021 to 2050 for Maui (Table 4). The cost of biodiesel started from
 $33.6/million British thermal units (or $4.67/gallon [gal]) in 2026 with a 25-year annual
 compounded growth rate of 2.8% per year. Note that the cost of biodiesel is approximately two
 times that of diesel ($2.37/gal) in the same dataset. However, the current cost of diesel in Hawaii
 is $5.4/gal, which was the input used in the analysis in the previous sections. Therefore, to keep
 the analysis inputs consistent, the cost of biodiesel used in the analysis was $10.8/gal, which
 maintains the 1:2 ratio provided in the HSEO dataset as well as the current diesel pricing.
 Furthermore, we assumed a 2.8% nominal cost escalation for fuel cost for biodiesel. The capital
 and operations and maintenance costs of the generator were the same as those included in Table

 4.2 Analysis Results for Integrated Grid Plan Load Forecast
 The results presented in this section reflect a system-level evaluation of multiple technology
 pathways to determine feasible generation and storage sizes for Molokai. Using REopt and

 15

 related modeling tools, the study assessed a range of configurations, including varying levels of
 solar PV, battery storage, diesel backup, long duration storage systems, and other options, to
 identify technology combinations capable of meeting defined renewable penetration targets
 while maintaining reliability. From this broad evaluation, a set of representative technology sizes
 and configurations was selected during this phase of work. These selected sizes were then carried
 forward as inputs into the detailed load flow analysis conducted using ETAP and ARIES
 platform (Section 5). By leveraging both conventional power system modeling and real-time
 simulation within ARIES, the project team was able to evaluate voltage performance, thermal
 loading, and dynamic behavior, providing a higher level of confidence in how these systems are
 expected to perform once implemented in the field.

 The scenario that performed the worst economically was the “Wind + BESS” scenario. This is

 5.1 Community Definitions and Community Cases
 To evaluate the range of possible outcomes, three scenario classifications were developed in
 collaboration with the community team: maximum (max), minimum (min), and medium (med).
 These classifications reflect different assumptions about siting priorities and grid impacts.

 The maximum (max) cases represent community-selected locations and sizes that represent the
 most challenging configurations from a grid integration standpoint and therefore the most likely
 to require significant infrastructure upgrades and associated costs.

 The minimum (min) cases were intentionally defined to avoid grid upgrades wherever possible.
 These configurations prioritize electrical locations that can accommodate new generation with
 little to no system modification. The primary motivation for these designs was to understand the
 lower bound of interconnection cost and infrastructure impact.

 The medium (med) cases maintain the same generation size as the maximum scenarios but vary
 the interconnection location to reduce grid impacts. For these cases, generation was tested across
 all feasible electrical connection points on the distribution system. This approach constituted an
 exhaustive analysis to identify both best-case and worst-case interconnection outcomes, allowing
 for a clearer understanding of how siting alone influences upgrade requirements and cost.

 From the full set of defined cases, the community team identified three starting scenarios for
 focused evaluation: two community-preferred configurations and one benchmark scenario based
 on Hawaiian Electric’s Integrated Grid Planning (IGP) Preferred Plan. These starting cases

 36

 provide a structured basis for comparison and further refinement as the project advances into
 implementation planning.

 5.1.1 West End Case 1: WE “Max” Case
 The West End Case 1, referred to as the WE “Max” Case, represents a community-scale solar PV
 and battery energy storage project intended to serve the majority of the West End’s electricity
 demand. This scenario reflects a locally identified site and sizing approach developed through
 the community planning process and will likely require grid upgrades as shown in the load flow
 analysis.

 The project concept consists of a ground-mounted solar PV array paired with a battery energy
 storage system. The system is proportionally sized to meet approximately 90% of the West End
 circuit’s annual energy needs. This sizing framework aligns with the broader project objective of
 evaluating high-penetration PV scenarios while maintaining system reliability.

 The proposed site is located on a Maui County parcel at the intersection of Maunaloa Highway
 and Kaluakoi Road (Tax Map Keys 510040350000 and 510040340000). This location was
 identified by the community as a viable site for development based on land availability and
 proximity to existing distribution infrastructure.

 This use case serves as an important test case within the broader analysis to determine the level
 of grid upgrades, voltage regulation measures, and equipment reinforcements that may be
 required to support a large-scale installation in this region. The results from this case help define
 the upper range of infrastructure impacts and associated costs for West End development.

 5.1.2 Central Molokai Case 1: CM “Max” Case
 The Central Molokai Case 1, referred to as the CM “Max” Case, represents a community-scale
 floating solar installation paired with battery energy storage. This scenario was developed to
 evaluate a high-penetration renewable configuration intended to serve the majority of the Central
 Molokai circuit’s electricity demand.

 The project concept consists of a floating PV array installed on the Kualapuu Reservoir, coupled
 with a battery energy storage system. The floating solar configuration was identified as a
 potential approach to use existing water surface area while minimizing land use impacts. The
 system is community sized or proportionally sized to meet approximately 90% of the Central
 Molokai circuit’s annual energy needs, consistent with the broader 90% island-wide renewable
 penetration objective evaluated in this study (see Table 16).

 The proposed location is the Kualapuu Reservoir (Tax Map Keys 520120270000 and
 520120040000). This site was identified through the community planning process as a feasible
 area for renewable energy development, subject to engineering, environmental, and permitting
 considerations.

 As a “Max” case, this scenario reflects a community-preferred location and scale that was not
 pre-screened to minimize grid impacts. Therefore, it is treated in the analysis as a potentially
 higher-impact interconnection case. Modeling of this configuration allows the project team to
 assess the magnitude of distribution system upgrades, voltage regulation requirements, and

 37

 protection coordination adjustments that may be necessary to integrate a large, circuit-scale
 floating PV and storage facility in Central Molokai. The results help define the upper bound of
 infrastructure complexity and cost associated with development in this region.

 5.1.3 A Note on the East End Case
 The East End case reflects the East End community’s preference to prioritize distributed rooftop
 solar installations rather than a centralized, utility-scale facility. This approach emphasizes local
 participation and distributed ownership while recognizing the physical and operational
 constraints of the existing distribution infrastructure on the East End. Community feedback and
 recent coastal flood modeling indicate vulnerability along sections of the existing transmission
 and distribution lines. As a result, the community has expressed a preference to avoid major grid
 upgrades in this region at this time.

 For modeling purposes in the loads analysis, the East End was represented as a single electrical
 node located at the boundary of the circuit’s three-phase reach near Ualapue. Beyond this point,
 the East End circuit transitions to double-phase and single-phase infrastructure. In this analysis,
 the precise locations of individual distributed energy resources (DERs) along the feeder were not
 modeled individually. Instead, aggregate generation was assumed at the defined node. This
 simplification was necessary because the available model did not provide sufficient granularity

 38

 to simulate distributed placements along the full 20+ mile feeder with varying phase
 configurations.

 The project size for this case was set at approximately 1 MW, representing the estimated line-
 limited hosting capacity of the East End circuit under current infrastructure conditions. This
 value reflects the maximum generation that can be accommodated without triggering significant
 system upgrades, based on a sensitivity study conducted at the beginning of this analysis.

 Modeling results indicate that integrating more than approximately 1 MW of additional DER
 capacity would require substantial infrastructure upgrades, depending on export levels and
 system configuration. While pairing rooftop PV with battery storage can help mitigate some
 voltage and backfeed concerns, larger-scale deployment would still necessitate line
 reconductoring, voltage control equipment, or other reinforcements. Given the feeder length,
 upgrade costs could exceed approximately $1.25 million per mile in affected segments.

 This case therefore represents both a community-driven preference for distributed energy
 development and a technically constrained portion of the island’s grid. The findings highlight the
 importance of carefully sequencing and sizing DER deployment in the East End to align with
 existing infrastructure limitations and community priorities.

 6 Inverter-Based Resources Grid Integration and
 Ride-Through Evaluation
 To maintain system reliability and meet utility operational standards, this section explores a suite
 of third-party mitigation strategies that can enhance the low-voltage ride-through capabilities of
 inverters that are noncompliant with the local utility technical requirements. These strategies
 include static synchronous compensators (STATCOMs), static VAR compensators (SVCs), and
 capacitor banks—each offering different technical capabilities and cost considerations.

 6.1 Challenges
 Grid-connected inverters must remain online during voltage disturbances to avoid cascading
 failures and islandwide outages. In the Molokai system, loss of inverter support during
 disturbances could result in voltage collapse, unintentional islanding, or delayed recovery.
 HECO’s interconnection requirements require inverter-based resources to operate continuously
 under low-voltage conditions, as shown in Figure 16, which are beyond the Institute of Electrical
 and Electronics Engineers Std (Hawaiian Electric 2021[a]; Hawaiian Electric 2021[b]). 1547-
 2018 performance standard. Generally, inverter manufacturers might not meet these ride-through
 requirements (see Figure 17). Because inverter firmware or control logic may not be easily
 altered, external grid support mechanisms may be necessary to ensure compliance. Grid forming
 inverters were not addressed in this study and are subject to future work.

 6.2 Technical Overview of Mitigation Options
 Following are several ways to operate the inverters outside of their standard operational ranges.

 6.2.1 Static Synchronous Compensators
 A STATCOM is a fast-acting, power-electronics-based device that can inject or absorb reactive
 power dynamically to stabilize grid voltage and indirectly support frequency by improving the
 voltage environment during disturbances. Specific details on STATCOMS include the following:

 • Performance. High-speed response (less than 20 milliseconds), continuous voltage
 regulation, and ride-through enhancement can maintain voltage stability during low-
 voltage events by controlling reactive power injection at a specific point of
 interconnection.
 • Cost. Costs of commercial-scale STATCOM systems are from $200,000–$500,000 per
 MW, depending on ratings and site integration needs (Midcontinent Independent System
           Operator 2018; 2024).
 • Integration complexity. Implementation requires a detailed engineering study, and is
 best suited for centralized deployment at substations or large inverter clusters.

 6.2.2 Static VAR Compensators
 SVCs use thyristor-controlled reactors and capacitors to provide stepwise reactive power
 support. Although slower than STATCOMs, SVCs offer robust voltage regulation in medium-
 speed applications. Specific details on SVCS include the following:

 45

 • Performance. Moderate-speed control (~100-millisecond response), suitable for
 addressing sustained voltage dips and limited voltage-related instability.
 • Cost. Typical SVC installations range from $100,000 to $300,000 per megawatt
 (Midcontinent Independent System Operator 2018; 2024).
 • Integration complexity. Simpler control logic than STATCOMs; more mature
 technology with established deployment practices.

 6.2.3 Mechanically Switched Shunt Capacitor Banks
 Capacitor banks provide static reactive power support and are the most basic grid stabilization
 tool available. They do not respond to frequency disturbances but can help maintain voltage
 levels during transients, supporting inverter ride-through.

 • Performance. No real-time control. Limited usefulness for fast-changing disturbances or
 severe grid events.
 • Cost. Costs can range from $20,000 to $50,000 per megawatt (Midcontinent Independent
           System Operator 2018; 2024).
 • Integration complexity. Easy to deploy. Suitable for distribution circuits with light
 dynamic support needs.

 6.4 Implementation Considerations
 The effectiveness of these devices depends on their proximity to the inverter. Centralizing
 STATCOMs at substations may benefit multiple projects simultaneously, whereas SVCs and
 capacitors may be best suited for site-specific enhancements.

 Any external support device must coordinate with inverter controls, especially when multiple
 DERs are present on a feeder. Poor coordination could result in instability or unintentional
 tripping. Specific considerations include the following:

 • Prioritize STATCOMs for high-priority sites where fast fault recovery and high
 renewable penetration intersect. These devices offer the best performance for addressing
 both voltage and frequency excursions, even if indirectly.

 47

 • Use SVCs in moderate-cost deployments or as interim solutions where ride-through gaps
 are less severe or where real-time dynamic performance is less critical.
 • Deploy capacitor banks where low cost and ease of installation are paramount and grid
 conditions are relatively stable.

 7 Conclusion
 The Molokai E2C Energyshed Project has established a technically rigorous and community-
 centered foundation for long-term system planning on the island. Through Phase 1, the project
 team applied Hybrid Optimisation of Multiple Energy Resources and ARIES (specifically ETAP
 and RSCAD) modeling platforms to evaluate multiple system configurations across Molokai’s
 feeders, integrating local values with engineering and cost parameters. This analysis was not
 conducted in isolation; it directly complements Hawaiian Electric’s IGP, which identifies a
 portfolio of solar, storage, energy efficiency, and distributed resources for Molokai.

 The results demonstrate that Molokai could achieve high levels of local energy genertion. The
 cost, reliability, and upgrades to equipment depend heavily on the scale, siting, and sequencing
 of new projects. A portfolio approaching 90% local generation and storage emerged as a
 practical balance, whereas 100% scenarios―while technically possible―would require
 significantly larger storage capacity, more complex grid upgrades, and higher overall system
 costs. These findings are consistent with IGP resource planning forecasts, which project on the
 order of ~20 MW of solar capacity and ~100 MWh of storage for Molokai. Regional grid
 constraints illustrated that no single islandwide solution is sufficient without considering local
 grid conditions.

 The project also examined reliability of inverter-based resources under Molokai’s unique
 interconnection standards, which require strict low-voltage ride-through performance.
 Commercial inverters do not consistently meet these requirements, creating a potential barrier to
 project deployment. To address this, the analysis evaluated external support devices, including
 STATCOMs and SVCs, as pathways to bridge gaps between equipment capabilities and utility
 standards. These solutions would allow Molokai to expand its technical options while
 maintaining system stability.

 Equally important, this effort built a collaborative and transparent planning framework that
 strengthens community capacity to engage with utilities, regulators, and developers. The maps,
 scenarios, and siting recommendations generated here provide actionable tools for decision-
 making, sequencing investments, and refining Molokai’s project portfolio. By linking
 community-defined priorities with Hawaiian Electric’s IGP resource pathways, the E2C
 Energyshed Project demonstrates how bottom-up planning can align with system-level
 requirements. This approach ensures that future phases of work are not only technically sound
 but also positioned for implementation within the state’s broader grid planning process.

 Appendix A. Advanced Research for Integrated Energy
 Systems
 Energy Design and Economic Optimization
 Transforming an island power system typically begins with techno-economic modeling using
 tools like Hybrid Optimisation of Multiple Energy Resources (HOMER). It allows engineers to
 simulate different combinations of photovoltaic (PV) generation, battery energy storage, and
 backup generators to meet the community’s electricity demand at the lowest cost. This process
 identifies an optimal mix of solar PV capacity and battery storage needed to reliably supply
 critical loads while minimizing fuel use. The HOMER modeling step produces an economically
 optimized design (e.g. specifying PV array size [to harness abundant sun], battery size [to buffer
 solar variability and provide nighttime power], and any supplemental generation that can meet
 the island’s load profile requirements.

 However, HOMER’s optimization focuses on long-term performance (hourly energy balance and
 cost) and assumes ideal operation. It does not simulate fast transients, control dynamics, or
 instability scenarios. Therefore, after finalizing a HOMER-optimized design, the project must
 validate that the system will operate stably under real-world conditions. This is where the
 National Laboratory of the Rockies’ (NLR) Advanced Research on Integrated Energy Systems
 (ARIES) platform comes in. ARIES provides a high-fidelity “virtual grid” environment to test
 the HOMER design in real time, and under a range of operating scenarios that go beyond its
 scope.

 Platform Overview and Real-Time Simulation Capabilities
 ARIES is a cutting-edge research platform at NLR that enables integration and validation of
 complex energy systems at scale. It bridges the gap between simulation and real-world
 deployment by combining advanced real-time computing, digital simulation, and hardware
 experimentation. ARIES can emulate power systems from the scale of a small microgrid up to
 regional grids in real time. In fact, NLR now operates 24 real-time simulation clusters capable of
 emulating scenarios across this spectrum―from microgrid “building blocks” to large
 transmission networks. These real-time digital simulators (using platforms like OPAL-RT and
 RTDS) run detailed models of power electronics, generation, load, and network components with
 submillisecond time steps, so that dynamic behavior (e.g., voltage/frequency transients, control
 responses, protection events) is accurately reproduced.

 A key feature of ARIES is its ability to perform hardware-in-the-loop experiments. This includes
 power-hardware-in-the-loop (PHIL) testing, in which actual physical equipment, such as a PV
 inverter or battery controller, is connected to the simulated grid environment. The ARIES
 platform at NLR’s Energy Systems Integration Facility has megawatt-scale PHIL capability that
 links real hardware (e.g., PV and battery inverters, grid-edge devices, and even utility control
 equipment) to a lab-simulated grid environment. In practice, this is achieved using controllable
 alternating current (AC) grid emulators (bidirectional power amplifiers) that interface between
 the equipment and the real-time digital simulator. The hardware “sees” a realistic grid voltage at
 its terminals, generated by the simulator, and can exchange actual power with the simulated
 network. Through PHIL, ARIES can evaluate how the real inverter and battery would behave in

 51

 the designed microgrid – testing its control algorithms and performance under a myriad of
 conditions, and without risking an actual outage on Molokai. Researchers can impose
 disturbances (e.g., voltage sags, frequency excursions, loss of phases) via the simulated grid to
 see if the hardware and controls respond appropriately. This level of integration provides
 confidence that the HOMER-optimized design will be operationally robust.

 Grid-Forming Inverters: Key to Islanded Microgrid Stability
 A critical aspect in the design of a PV-battery based islanded microgrid is the role of grid-
 forming inverters. In an islanded system (completely disconnected from a larger utility grid), at
 least one device must establish the grid’s voltage and frequency reference. Traditionally, diesel
 generators (with spinning mass) fulfilled this role by imposing grid frequency via their rotational
 speed. In a renewable-based microgrid, advanced inverters with grid-forming control are tasked
 with this job. A grid-forming inverter can regulate voltage and frequency on its output,
 effectively “forming” the grid as a stable AC source that other inverters and loads can follow.
 Battery inverters (and potentially other power sources like fuel cells or even wind turbines with
 appropriate controls) are typically configured in grid-forming mode to maintain stability in 100%
 renewable microgrids.

 The ARIES platform has been used extensively to research and validate the performance of grid-
 forming inverters in microgrid applications. For instance, NLR demonstrated a grid-forming fuel
 cell inverter in ARIES that could support microgrid resiliency, validating that such inverters are
 essential for stability when a high share of resources are inverter-based. In general, ARIES
 findings confirm that grid-forming inverter technology is needed for grid stability as systems
 approach 100% power-electronics-based generation (e.g., solar, wind, batteries). Unlike
 conventional grid-following PV inverters that require an external grid to sync to, grid-forming
 units provide the reference and can operate autonomously. This capability is also tied to new
 black-start paradigms in microgrids: a grid-forming inverter (often a battery system) can start up
 without an existing grid, establishing voltage/frequency so that other generation assets (e.g., PV,
 wind) can be brought online in a controlled manner. ARIES researchers have been addressing
 challenges like how to coordinate multiple grid-forming inverters, how to seamlessly transition
 between grid-connected and islanded modes, and how to update interconnection standards for
 these inverters. By leveraging ARIES’s real-time simulations and PHIL, they can experiment
 with different control settings and inverter interoperability in a risk-free environment, ensuring
 that the chosen PV battery inverter for Molokai will maintain stable voltage and frequency under
 varying conditions.

 ARIES Validation Under Realistic Scenarios vs. HOMER Simulation
 One of the major advantages of ARIES is its ability to perform reliability and resilience analysis
 under dynamic conditions that tools like HOMER cannot capture. HOMER’s optimization might
 tell us that (hypothetically) a 5-megawatt PV array with 20 megawatt-hours of battery and a
 small diesel backup yields the lowest cost for Molokai’s needs. HOMER’s hourly simulation
 granularity and simplified dispatch logic cannot answer these questions. ARIES addresses this
 gap by subjecting the microgrid model to a gamut of real-world disturbances and verifying its
 performance. For example, ARIES can inject high-frequency details like subsecond solar
 irradiance fluctuations or voltage flicker and study the inverter and battery response at the
 microsecond timescale. In one utility partnership, researchers used ARIES to validate a solar

 52

 inverter’s model against the real device’s electrical response at the microsecond level, ensuring
 the inverter model was accurate and that no unexpected instability would occur when thousands
 of these inverters are deployed. This kind of high-fidelity model validation and stability testing is
 something HOMER does not do―it requires a platform like ARIES.

 Appendix B. Molokai Long-Duration Storage Q&A
 Overview
 High penetration of renewables and nuclear resources, especially for islanded and microgrid
 systems, require energy storage technology or a combination of technologies. The choice of
 storage technology depends on the scale, duration, and load type. Figure A-1 shows various
 solutions at their optimum power and duration discharge. Hydrogen can offer the benefits of
 being stored in large quantities for long periods of time with minimum loss; hence, it is suitable
 for long and seasonal duration storage. On the other hand, batteries can store low to medium
 power quantities, typically within hours but could extend to days, depending on the battery type.
 Thus, batteries are more suitable for short-to-medium-duration applications. The two
 technologies can often be combined as they are complementary in their power and duration and
 consequently, result in cost savings while improving energy resilience (Figure A-2).

 Hydrogen Storage Technologies
 Hydrogen can be stored physically, as a gas, compressed or cold/cryo compressed, or a liquid.
 Storing hydrogen as a gas typically requires high-pressure tanks. Storing it as a liquid requires
 extremely low temperatures. Cold/cryo-compressed hydrogen combines low temperatures with
 high pressures to further optimize physically based storage.

 Gaseous hydrogen storage in tanks makes up the largest portion of hydrogen storage worldwide.
 Gaseous storage is commonly categorized into stationary, vehicular, and bulk transportation
 applications. For stationary and vehicular applications, typical operating pressures range from 20
 megapascals (MPa) to 100 MPa. In bulk transportation applications, hydrogen is transported in
 tube trailers comprising steel or composite pressure vessels. Current tube trailer pressures range
 from 25 to ~50 MPa and can reach up to 1,100 kilograms (kg) of hydrogen.

 Compressed hydrogen storage tanks are commonly classified into four types depending on the
 amount of overwrap they comprise. Types I–IV come in a cylindrical shape, but Types II–IV
 commonly take a polymorph or toroid form. The major differences, such as technological and
 economic performance, are listed in Table A-1. Type I tanks can store 1 weight percent (wt%) of
 hydrogen while Type III and IV are able to reach 4.8 wt% of hydrogen. Type I vessels are made
 of metal— typically steel—and do not have any wrapping. The low cost of manufacturing them,
 along with economies of scale, contribute to their low cost. Type I tank weight, however, is the
 heaviest of all hydrogen storage tank types at approximately 1.4 kg/liter (L). Type II vessels are
 made of steel or aluminum and are hoop-wrapped in fiber-resin composite. Normally they cost
 twice as much to produce as Type I vessels do, but their weight is about 30%–40% less. Type III
 vessels feature a metal liner, typically aluminum, and are fully wrapped with a fiber-resin
 composite. Type IV vessels have a polymer liner, such as high-strength-to-density-ratio
 polyethylene. The structural loads of Type III and IV tanks are carried by the composite
 materials they comprise. Both tanks are lighter than Type I and II vessels are, and the density is
 in the range of 0.3–0.45 kg/L, but they cost 3.5–4 times more than Type I vessels do.

 Metal Hydrides
 Metal/alloy reacts with hydrogen in a reversible reaction forming metal hydrides. When
 hydrogen is absorbed into the metal structure, the reaction is exothermic. The reaction is at
 equilibrium at 0.1–1 MPa hydrogen pressure and room temperatures; hence it is inherently safe.
 During dehydrogenation, temperatures decrease and, as a result, hydrogen desorption rates slow
 down. This feature acts as an additional safety protection preventing equipment failures. The

 55

 features of the reaction allow high hydrogen volumetric density of about 100 g/L in the metal
 hydride matrix, making storage compact and efficient.

 Metal hydrides can be divided into two groups:

 • Light metal hydrides refer to the group of lightweight metals such as lithium, magnesium,
 boron, and aluminum. Among metallic hydrides, magnesium dihydrides are preferred
 because of their heat resistance, reversibility, and recyclability. Additionally, they are
 inexpensive and have a high gravimetric hydrogen storage capacity (i.e., approximately
 7.6 wt%).
 • Complex metal hydrides, such as Mg2FeH6 and Al(BH4)3, differ from metallic hydrides
 in the transition to covalent compounds during hydrogen absorption. The previously
 mentioned complex hydrides have demonstrated the volumetric hydrogen density as
 being three times higher than the ultimate U.S. Department of Energy volumetric
 capacity goals.

 Question

 Please describe other potential risks, threats, and impacts of developing this technology on
 Molokai. For example, is there an increased risk of fire (considering hydrogen is highly
 combustible)?

 Answer

 Given that Molokai is experiencing mostly sunny conditions with a breezy east wind, with highs
 around 80°F and wind speeds ranging from 15 to 25 miles per hour, hydrogen and battery
 storage should be ideally Independent-System-Operator-containerized with adequate thermal and
 ventilation systems inside to ensure the temperature remains within desired range. Molokai does
 not have a high risk of earthquakes but does have a high risk of wildfires. Regardless of those
 risks, the safety protocols would require alarms and automatic shut down in case of fire or
 hydrogen leak detection.

 If you would like to not repurpose your current equipment to store hydrogen, and hence, build a
 system from scratch, you would typically consider the following:

 • Infrastructure
 o For compressed hydrogen storage overground, a vessel type I would be most
 commonly deployed and will require a compressor for compressing hydrogen, an
 electrolyzer for producing the hydrogen, and a fuel cell or reciprocating engine
 for converting hydrogen to electricity. Additionally, piping, valves, enclosures,
 power electronics, a monitoring system, and safety equipment, such as pressure
 relief valves and an emergency shut down system, would be needed.
 • Personnel
 o For operation and maintenance of the equipment, approximately 2–3 people are
 required. Construction would require roughly triple the estimated personnel for
 operating the system.
