Asymptotic boundary structure of Lagrangian gauge theories
Ivan Dneprov, Maxim Grigoriev †, Mikhail Markov

Abstract
Given a local gauge theory on spacetime with boundary, it naturally defines another gauge theory which can be regarded as a theory of the boundary values. For Lagrangian theories, it comes equipped with the presymplectic structure which can be used to define one or another version of Hamiltonian-like formulation of the initial model. This relation is especially manifest for AKSZ sigma models and more-generally gauge PDEs with compatible presymplectic structure in which case the boundary system is again a gauge PDE with presymplectic structure. In the context of (flat space) holography one is interested in boundaries at infinity, also known as asymptotic boundaries. The gauge PDE framework naturally extends to this setup, resulting in the notion of gauge PDE with asymptotic boundaries. Although this works perfectly well at the level of equations of motion, the extension to Lagrangian systems appears quite subtle because the presymplectic structure capturing the Lagrangian is divergent at the boundary. We show that any Q Q -cocycle in the bulk (and presymplectic structure in particular) determines a pair of compatible Q Q -cocycles of the boundary gauge PDE: the renormalized one of the same ghost-degree, and the anomaly cocycle of degree one lower. For the latter, the construction is somewhat analogous to the residue map known in the context of b-geometry. The general formalism is exemplified by scalar and Maxwell fields on AdS and Minkowski spaces. It turns out that in the AdS case the natural action determined by the anomaly presymplectic structure is precisely the one known as the holographic Weyl anomaly in the AdS/CFT context while its null-infinity counterpart was known in a few very particular cases only.

Introduction

The concepts of boundary at infinity and conformal compactification of spacetime play a prominent role
in the physical interpretation of gravity and gauge theories of direct physical interest as they allow one to define isolated systems and conserved charges for gravity
[

If a (gauge) theory is defined on a manifold with a boundary or a codimension 1 submanifold, the natural question is what structure is naturally induced therein? There are various approaches to this issue among which the apparently simplest one is to regard the boundary as an analog of a Cauchy surface so that the boundary structure can be described as a (version of) the Hamiltonian formulation of the initial theory. In the case of gauge theories this is generally a constrained Hamiltonian system so that the boundary configurations describing the corresponding phase space are subject to differential (algebraic in the case of mechanics) equations known as constraints. For instance, considering the Maxwell field on
\mathbb{R}^{4}
with coordinates
t\equiv x^{0},x^{i}
and focusing on the submanifold
t=const
one finds that the induced phase space is parametrized by configurations of
A_{i}(x)
and its conjugate momenta
\pi^{i}(x)\equiv F^{0i}(x)
subject to the constraint equation
\partial_{i}\pi^{i}=0
. The message is that the induced structure on the boundary can be naturally seen as a theory defined on the boundary at the level of equations of motion. Moreover, if the starting point system is Lagrangian then the phase-space comes equipped with the (pre)symplectic structure
[
7
]
, e.g. equivalent to
\int\delta\pi^{i}\wedge\delta A_{i}
in the above example of Maxwell. In the case of general partial differential equations (PDEs) this relation can be made very geometrical if the PDE in question is defined in intrinsic terms as a fibre bundle over the spacetime, equipped with Cartan distribution
[
8

[

10

In physics we are mostly interested in gauge theories, in which case the extra structures are present. These structures are best described using one or another version of Batalin-Vilkovisky (BV) formalism. It has been known for quite some time that the constrained Hamiltonian system induced on the boundary can be directly found in terms of the BV formulation in the bulk if the initial theory is formulated as the AKSZ sigma-model. To illustrate this point consider an AKSZ model

The gauge PDE approach to boundary structure of gauge fields has proved fruitful in the study of general AdS space gauge fields. In this context the conformal boundary of spacetime can be turned into a submanifold
of the ambient space and the boundary structure is obtained by a pullback of the corresponding gPDE
[

The Lagrangian field theories are described in the gPDE formalism by gPDEs equipped with compatible presymplectic structures. Under some regularity conditions, these data define not only a natural action functional on the space of sections but also the full-scale local BV formulation on the symplectic quotient of the corresponding space of supersections. The resulting BV system is generally weaker (has more gauge-inequivalent solutions) than the starting point gPDE so that the additional condition ensuring the equivalence is to be imposed. Such a gPDE with compatible presymplectic structure can be pulled back to the de Rham complex of the boundary (or more general submanifold) giving a new gPDE with the compatible presymplectic structure. However the degree of this presymplectic structure equals the spacetime dimension so that the resulting BV-like formulation induced on the quotient of the space of supersections is naturally interpreted as the BFV one
[
22
]
, see also
[
29
]
. This extends the AKSZ approach to boundary structure to the case of general gauge theories. It is important to stress, however, that in contrast to AKSZ setup it is not a priori guaranteed that the boundary BFV system obtained this way is a correct BFV formulation of the system in question and some extra regularity conditions are to be imposed.

In this work we are concerned with gauge theories on spacetimes with asymptotic boundaries. The asymptotic boundary, also known as boundary at infinity, is not a submanifold of the initial spacetime but is a boundary of its compactification i.e. a spacetime with boundary such that the initial spacetime is identified with the interior of the compactified spacetime. Given a compactified spacetime, the asymptotic boundary can be treated as a usual one. The important subtlety, however, is that if we are interested in a gauge theory its extension to the compactified space-time is not given by default and has to be decided and constructed. Moreover, the choice of space-time compactification typically involves space-time metric which itself can be a dynamical field of the theory under consideration. All in all this leads to the notion of compactification of a gauge theory.

It turns out that the language of gauge PDEs is well-suited not only to study boundary structures of gauge theories but also to study their compactification. More precisely, we introduce a notion of a gauge PDE with asymptotic boundary. This is roughly speaking a
Q
-bundle where both the spacetime and the fiber are manifolds with boundaries and solutions are by definition sections whose restrictions to the boundary of spacetime belong to the boundary of the field space. In plain terms, this means that among the fields there is one describing a boundary defining function while gauge transformations describe its ambiguity.

Such a system naturally defines at least two gauge PDEs. The first one, the interior, is obtained by restricting to the interior of the base followed by the restriction to the interior of the resulting total space. The second one is the boundary of the restriction of the initial bundle to the boundary of the base. The first one is an equivalent of the initial system to be compactified while the second one is regarded as the gPDE describing the corresponding boundary structure. In slightly different terms the notion of gPDE with asymptotic boundaries was already in
[
23
,
33
]
, see also
[
21

where the related considerations were put forward using the ambient space formalism.

Although gPDEs with asymptotic boundary give a natural setup to study compactifications and boundary structures associated to asymptotic boundaries, the extension to Lagrangian gauge theories appears to be subtle and at the same time interesting. The point is that while the compactification at the level of equations of motion can be almost systematically performed for usual models of gravity and gauge fields, the presymplectic structure encoding the Lagrangian is usually polynomially divergent at the boundary and does not determine a presymplectic structure for the boundary theory in a natural way. Nevertheless, as we show in this work this difficulty can be overcome systematically using solely the invariant geometrical tools. More precisely, we demonstrate that any
Q
-cocycle that diverges polynomially at the boundary generally gives rise to a pair of
Q
-cocycles
of the boundary gPDE. One of the same ghost degree (called renormalised) and that of degree 1 lower (called anomaly); both having the same form-degree. While the appearance of the renormalised cocycle is expected, the anomaly cocycle is less obvious and the mechanism it arises is somewhat similar to the residue map in the context of b-geometry
[
34
]
, where the de Rham cocycle in the bulk defines a de Rham cocycle on the singular boundary, which is 1 form-degree lower than the initial cocycle.

We demonstrate that when applied to the presymplectic structure on gPDE with asymptotic boundary the corresponding renormalised presymplectic structure on the boundary gPDE defines a BFV system, which can be considered as a counterpart of the boundary BFV system in the non-asymptotic case. At the same time the anomaly, if nontrivial, defines a new BV system on the boundary. In the case of asymptotically-AdS space the corresponding action functional coincides with the well-known holographic Weyl anomaly which in the usual AdS/CFT considerations emerges as a log-divergent piece of the effective action. For instance, for the critical scalar of Weyl weight
w=\ell-d/2
,
\ell=1,2,\ldots
on
d+1
-dimensional AdS space, the action determined by the anomaly presymplectic structure is precisely the 1st order action of the higher-order singleton field on the boundary, whose equations of motion in Cartesian coordinates read as
\Box^{\ell}\varphi=0
.

As applications of the general framework proposed in the paper we consider scalar field and Maxwell field on AdS and Minkowski space and explicitly derive their boundary structure, including the renormalised and anomaly presymplectic structures and their associated BFV and BV systems. While in the AdS case the resulting objects are known in one or another approach, in the case of Minkowski space and its asymptotic boundary at null-infinity the full-scale gauge PDE description of the boundary structure was not known, see however
[
23
]
, and the analogs of holographic Weyl anomalies were not studied systematically. In this work we fill this gap by explicitly constructing a gPDE with asymptotic boundaries describing the compactification of flat Minkowski space together with the scalar and the Maxwell field defined on its background. Moreover, we compute the renormalised and the anomaly presymplectic structures on the boundary and explicitly derive the action functional determined by the anomaly. It turns out that the latter gives an infinite tower of manifestly conformally-Carroll invariant theories which generalize the magnetic limits of the scalar and Maxwell fields
[
35
]
.

Although the examples explicitly discussed in this work are limited to constant curvature backgrounds (or, better, Cartan-flat ones) the general approach perfectly applies to full-fledged gravity and gauge fields defined on its background. More precisely, in the present terms the construction of
[
33
]
is the gauge PDE with boundaries for asympotically-AdS gravity and gauge fields on its background considered at the level of equations of motion while its analog from
[
23
]
can be readily completed to the gauge PDE with asymptotic boundaries for asymptotically flat gravity. As for the presymplectic structures for these theories and their associated structures on the boundary, we leave their analysis for a future work.

2.3
Background fields

. Such description is very economical, more invariant, and captures the information of how fields transform under the underlying spacetime symmetry in a manifest way. This is precisely what we employ in our study of gauge fields on asymptotic boundaries of homogeneous spaces. Note that gauge PDEs describing gauge fields coupled to the gravity background also have the factorized structure (
2.10
).

The extension of gPDEs over background to
Lagrangian systems is a straightforward generalisation of gPDEs with presymplectic structures. One asks that

be equipped with extra data

obeying the following compatibility conditions:

\begin{gathered}\omega=d\chi\,,\qquad gh(\omega)=dimX-1\,,\qquad i_{Q}\omega+dH\in\mathcal{I}_{B}\end{gathered}

(2.11)

analogous to (
2.4
) with the only modification being that

is replaced with
\mathcal{I}_{B}
, i.e. the ideal generated by forms
{\pi_{B}}^{*}\alpha
,
\alpha\in\bigwedge^{1}(B)
.
Restricting to a solution
\sigma_{0}
of the background gPDE, one obtains a gPDE with presymplectic structure. Moreover, the resulting intrinsic action is invariant under the background gauge transformations which affect both the background fields and the dynamical ones. If a particular background solution is fixed, the residual symmetries are precisely the space-time symmetries of the system in this background.

In the case of fields on homogeneous spaces or gravitational backgrounds the presymplectic structure as well as
\chi
and
H
can be defined on
(E_{0},Q_{0})
so that one can disregard the spacetime and work solely with the bundle
(E_{0},Q_{0})

2.4

For further use, let us illustrate the approach recalled above in the case of Maxwell theory coupled to a flat AdS background.

3.1

-boundaries

We first need to recall the notion of a graded manifold with boundary. A graded manifold

In applications to gauge theories and, first of all, theories on manifolds with boundaries we are interested in generalised boundaries. Namely, let

of the body. In particular,

3.2
Gauge PDEs with asymptotic boundaries

Let us recall Penrose’s definition of an asymptotically simple space

The idea is to think of metric

and try to reformulate the notion in terms of the theory itself rather than its particular solution. In particular, this implies that there is a gauge theory defined on
X

is equivalent to the starting point theory. This vague idea can be formalised using the language of gauge PDEs as follows:

•

. We also emphasize that for the given gPDE
(\widetilde{E},Q,T[1]\widetilde{X})
there may exist different compactifications. In the examples presented later in this text, the choice of compactification reflects the choice of asymptotic behavior of the fields.

For our purposes it is often enough to restrict to gPDEs which are globally-trivial as
Q
-bundles. In this case the notion of a gPDE with asymptotic boundary simplifies as follows. Namely,
(E,Q,T[1]X)
is globally trivial, i.e.

\begin{gathered}(E,Q,T[1]X)=(T[1]X,\mathrm{d}_{X})\times(F,q)\end{gathered}

(3.7)

and
(F,q)
is a
Q
-manifold with
Q
-boundary. In this case it is also natural to restrict ourselves to the situation where the initial gauge PDE is also globally-trivial, i.e. is given by
(T[1]\widetilde{X},\mathrm{d}_{\widetilde{X}})\times(\widetilde{F},q)

and
(\widetilde{F},q)
is equivalent to
(F,q)
. Of course, it goes without saying that
\widetilde{X}=Int(X)
.

Remark 3.5
.

Suppose that
F
is parameterized by coordinates
\{{z}^{A},\Omega,\xi\}

and
\partial^{Q}F
is singled out by
\Omega=0,\xi=0
. Restricting these coordinates to
Int(F)
gives the coordinate system therein. Let
\sigma:T[1]X\to F
be a solution, meaning that
\mathrm{d}_{X}\circ\sigma^{*}=\sigma^{*}\circ q
and
\sigma^{*}(\Omega)
vanishes on
\partial X
and
\sigma^{*}(\Omega)>0
on
Int(X)
. Note that
\sigma^{*}(\xi)|_{\partial^{Q}(T[1]X)}=0
thanks to the equations of motion.

Now, let
\widetilde{z}^{A}
be a coordinate system on
Int(F)
and we do not assume that
\widetilde{z}^{A}
arise as restrictions of some coordinates on
F
. The restriction of
\sigma
to
Int(X)

can be seen as a map
T[1]\widetilde{X}\to Int(F)
.
Note that
\sigma^{*}(\widetilde{z}^{A})
do not generally extend to
T[1](\partial X)
and one might need to rescale
\widetilde{z}^{A}
by a power of
\Omega
in order for
\sigma^{*}(\widetilde{z}^{A})
to be well-defined on the boundary. This is obvious in the example where on
Int(F)
the coordinates are related by

z^{A}=\Omega^{k}\widetilde{z}^{A}
. Because
F
is a compactification of
Int(F)
, it is clear that the choice of compactification determines the behavior of fields of
Int(F)
as
\Omega\to 0
.

Remark 3.6
.

In applications it is often convenient to employ gPDEs defined implicitly. In the globally-trivial setup which we restrict ourselves to, this means that the gPDE is defined as a submanifold
Z_{\mathcal{E}}
in
(F,q)
, which is the zero locus of a
q
-invariant ideal
\mathcal{E}\subset\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(F)
. In what follows, we denote such an implicit realisation of
(Z_{\mathcal{E}},q)
by
(F,q,\mathcal{E})
.

The respective modification of Definition
3.2
is that both
\widetilde{E}
and
E
are equipped with
Q
-invariant ideals denoted, respectively, as
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}
and
\mathcal{E}
such that their zero loci are
Q
-subbundles and moreover

(\widetilde{E},Q,\widetilde{\mathcal{E}},T[1]X)
is equivalent to
(Int(F),Q,\mathcal{E}^{\prime})
where
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
is the ideal generated by
\mathcal{E}
restricted to
Int(F)
.

3.3
Induced
Q
-cocycles on the boundary

Assuming that we deal with a globally-trivial system the analysis reduces to that of the fiber
(F,Q)
which is a
Q
-manifold with
Q
-boundary. What we are interested in now is the near-boundary analysis so that without loss of generality we can assume
F
to be a sufficiently small neighborhood of its
Q
-boundary. In this case one can take
\Omega
and
\xi\equiv Q\Omega
to be
globally-defined so that the
Q
-boundary is a submanifold singled out by
\Omega=0
and
\xi=0
.

In applications we often encounter objects which are well-defined on
\widetilde{F}\equiv Int(F)
only. Suppose that we are given a
Q
-cocycle
\widetilde{v}\in\bigwedge^{\bullet}(\widetilde{F})
, i.e.
L_{Q}\widetilde{v}=0
, such that
v=\Omega^{p}\widetilde{v}
extends to the boundary and hence can be seen as a form on
F
. It turns out that under some mild technical assumptions such a cocycle determines a pair of cocycles of the boundary system:

\begin{gathered}v^{\mathcal{R}}\in\bigwedge^{\bullet}(\partial^{Q}F)\,,\qquad gh(v^{\mathcal{R}})=gh(\tilde{v})\,,\\
v^{\mathcal{A}}\in\bigwedge^{\bullet}(\partial^{Q}F)\,,\qquad gh(v^{\mathcal{A}})=gh(\tilde{v})-1\end{gathered}

(3.8)

whose cohomology classes in
(\bigwedge^{\bullet}(\partial^{Q}F),L_{Q})
are determined by the cohomology class of
\widetilde{v}
in
(\bigwedge^{\bullet}(\widetilde{F}),L_{Q})
.

To construct these cocycles we assume that
F
admits a vector field
\mathfrak{d}
,
\mathrm{gh}(\mathfrak{d})=-1
such that

\mathfrak{d}\Omega=0\,,\hskip 20.00003pt\mathfrak{d}\xi=1\,,\hskip 20.00003pt\mathfrak{d}^{2}=0\,.

(3.9)

Recall, that we assumed that the boundary defining coordinate
\Omega
is a fixed globally-defined function and
\xi\equiv Q\Omega
. That such
\mathfrak{d}
exists locally is obvious by taking
\mathfrak{d}\equiv\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.5pt}{\footnotesize$\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}$}\kern 1.0pt}{\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}}{\raisebox{0.5pt}{\footnotesize$\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}$}\kern 1.0pt}{\raisebox{0.5pt}{\footnotesize$\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}$}\kern 1.0pt}
in a local coordinate system
\{\Omega,\xi,z^{A}\}
. Its global (meaning in a neighborhood of the boundary) existence is a simplifying assumption which is anyway fulfilled in all our examples.
Having chosen
\mathfrak{d}
, one defines another vector field

\nabla=[Q,{\mathfrak{d}}]

(3.10)

obeying
[Q,\nabla]=0
. It follows that

\displaystyle\nabla\Omega=1,\hskip 10.00002pt\nabla\xi=0.

(3.11)

For any
v\in\bigwedge^{\bullet}(F)
we define

\begin{gathered}v^{(N)}\equiv(L_{\nabla})^{N}v\,,\qquad\bar{v}^{(N)}\equiv(L_{\nabla})^{N}L_{\mathfrak{d}}v\,.\end{gathered}

(3.12)

We have the following:

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
(F,Q)
be a
Q
-manifold with
Q
-boundary,
b:\partial^{Q}F\hookrightarrow F
and
\tilde{v}\in\bigwedge^{\bullet}(\widetilde{F})
be a
Q
-cocycle such that
v=\Omega^{p}\tilde{v}
smoothly extends to
\partial^{Q}F
(i.e.
v
is a restriction to
Int(F)
of an element from
\bigwedge^{\bullet}(F)
). Then

\begin{gathered}v^{\mathcal{R}}=b^{*}\frac{1}{p!}v^{(p)}\,,\\
v^{\mathcal{A}}=b^{*}(-1)^{tot(v)}\frac{1}{(p-1)!}\bar{v}^{(p-1)}\,.\end{gathered}

(3.13)

are
Q
-cocycles in
\bigwedge^{\bullet}(\partial^{Q}F)
and their cohomology classes do not depend on the choice of
\mathfrak{d}
and
\Omega
. Here
tot(v)=\mathrm{fdeg}(v)+\mathrm{gh}(v)
is the total degree of
v
.

Proof.

On
\widetilde{F}
one has

\Omega L_{Q}v=\Omega L_{Q}(\Omega^{p}\tilde{v})=p\xi v+\Omega^{p+1}L_{Q}\tilde{v}=p\xi v

(3.14)

Applying
L_{\nabla}^{N+1}
to this equation one gets

(N+1)L_{Q}v^{(N)}+\Omega L_{Q}v^{(N+1)}=p\xi v^{(N+1)}

(3.15)

Because
v\equiv\Omega^{p}\widetilde{v}
can be seen as a form on
F
and
\nabla
is well-defined on
F
both sides of the above equations are forms on
F
. Therefore, applying
b^{*}
and
b^{*}\circ Q=Q\circ b^{*}
one gets

L_{Q}b^{*}v^{(N)}=0\,,\hskip 20.00003pt\forall N\,{\geqslant}\,0\,.

(3.16)

Although
b^{*}v^{(N)}
is a
Q
cocycle for any
N
, we are going to see later that it is trivial unless
N=p
.

Applying
L_{\mathfrak{d}}
to (
3.14
) one gets

\displaystyle\Omega L_{Q}\bar{v}=\Omega v^{(1)}-pv+p\xi\bar{v}\,.

(3.17)

Then, acting by
b^{*}(L_{\nabla})^{N}
one gets

\displaystyle NL_{Q}b^{*}\bar{v}^{(N-1)}=(N-p)b^{*}v^{(N)}\,.

(3.18)

This equation can be read in two ways: on the one hand, it states that
b^{*}v^{(N)}
is
Q
-exact
\forall N\neq p
, on the other hand it verifies

L_{Q}b^{*}\bar{v}^{(p-1)}=0\,,

(3.19)

showing the first part of the statement.

The independence of the cohomology class of
v^{\mathcal{R}},v^{\mathcal{A}}
on the choice of
\mathfrak{d}
is shown in Appendix
A
.
∎

Remark 3.8
.

The number
p
featured in the above theorem in
v=\Omega^{p}\widetilde{v}
is not required to be minimal, the resulting expressions for
v^{\mathcal{R}}
and
v^{\mathcal{A}}
coincide for any such
p
satisfying the conditions of the above Theorem.

Indeed, if
\Omega^{p}\widetilde{v}
is smoothly prolonged to the boundary, then so is
\Omega^{p+1}\widetilde{v}
. The expression for
v^{\mathcal{R}}
calculated using the
p
expression is

v^{\mathcal{R}}(p)=b^{*}\frac{1}{p!}L_{\nabla}^{p}\Omega^{p}\widetilde{v}

(3.20)

while

v^{\mathcal{R}}(p+1)=b^{*}\frac{1}{(p+1)!}L_{\nabla}^{p+1}\Omega^{p+1}\widetilde{v}=b^{*}(\frac{1}{p!}L_{\nabla}^{p}\Omega^{p}\widetilde{v}+\Omega\frac{1}{(p+1)!}L_{\nabla}^{p+1}\Omega^{p}\widetilde{v})\,,

(3.21)

where we used
L_{\nabla}^{2}\Omega=0
. The second term is automatically zero giving

v^{\mathcal{R}}(p+1)=v^{\mathcal{R}}(p)
. A similar calculation proves the claim for
v^{\mathcal{A}}
.

Let us explore the general properties of the map determined by (
3.13
). It is convenient to define the algebra
\bigwedge^{\bullet}_{\mathrm{fop}}(Int(F))\subset\bigwedge^{\bullet}(Int(F))
of forms on
Int(F)
that are allowed to have poles of finite order as
\Omega\to 0
. More precisely,

\bigwedge^{\bullet}{}_{\mathrm{fop}}(Int(F))=\{\widetilde{\alpha}\in\bigwedge^{\bullet}(Int(F))|\exists p\in\mathbb{N},\alpha\in\bigwedge^{\bullet}(F)\,:\,\Omega^{p}\widetilde{\alpha}=\alpha|_{Int(F)}\}\,,

(3.22)

where “fop” refers to “finite-order pole”. We note that this definition, as well as the minimal possible value of
p
, does not depend on the choice of the boundary-defining function
\Omega
. Any smooth vector field on
F
restricts to a vector field on
Int(F)
, which in turn acts by Lie derivative on
\bigwedge{}^{\bullet}_{\mathrm{fop}}(Int(F))
. Then we define the following maps:

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{R}:\bigwedge^{\bullet}{}_{\mathrm{fop}}(Int(F))\xrightarrow{}\bigwedge^{\bullet}(\partial^{Q}F)\,,\qquad\mathcal{R}(\widetilde{\alpha})=b^{*}(\frac{1}{p!}\alpha^{(p)})\,,\end{gathered}

(3.23)

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{A}:\bigwedge^{\bullet}{}_{\mathrm{fop}}(Int(F))\xrightarrow{}\bigwedge^{\bullet}(\partial^{Q}F)\,,\qquad\mathcal{A}(\widetilde{\alpha})=b^{*}(\frac{(-1)^{tot(\widetilde{\alpha})}}{(p-1)!}\bar{\alpha}^{(p-1)})\end{gathered}

(3.24)

where
p
is any number featured in (
3.22
) and
tot(\widetilde{\alpha})=form(\widetilde{\alpha})+ghost(\widetilde{\alpha})
is the total degree of
\widetilde{\alpha}
.
Recall that we assumed that
\Omega
and
\mathfrak{d}
are fixed and globally defined. Remark
3.8
implies that the above maps do not depend on the choice of
p
. In the setting of Theorem
3.7
one has:
\alpha^{\mathcal{R}}=\mathcal{R}(\widetilde{\alpha})
and
\alpha^{\mathcal{A}}=\mathcal{A}(\widetilde{\alpha})
. Note that maps (
3.23
) and (
3.24
) are generally defined for all forms from
\bigwedge^{\bullet}{}_{\mathrm{fop}}(Int(F))
, not only
Q
-cocycles. Moreover, the following lemma is a straightforward generalization of Theorem
3.7
.

Lemma 3.9
.

Maps
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{A}
both commute with
L_{Q}
and
d
.

The proof is given in Appendix
B
.

In applications we often encounter equivalence classes of forms modulo certain ideals. Let
\mathcal{I}\subset\bigwedge^{\bullet}(Int(F))
be a
Q
-invariant differential ideal, i.e.
\mathcal{I}
is closed under both
d
and
L_{Q}
. We say that
\mathcal{I}
is extendable if it can be generated by a set of forms
\widetilde{f}_{i}
that extend to
\partial^{Q}F
, i.e.
\widetilde{f}_{i}=f_{i}|_{Int(F)}

for some
f_{i}\in\bigwedge^{\bullet}(F)
and moreover
L_{\mathfrak{d}}\mathcal{I}\subset\mathcal{I}
.
Any extendable
\mathcal{I}
defines a differential ideal
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{fop}}\subset\bigwedge^{\bullet}_{\mathrm{fop}}(Int(F))
generated by
\widetilde{f}_{i}
. At the same time,
\mathcal{I}
also defines a differential ideal
\mathcal{I}_{\partial}\subset\bigwedge(\partial^{Q}F)
generated by
b^{*}f_{i}
, where
\widetilde{f}_{i}=f_{i}|_{Int(F)}
.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be extendable. Then maps
\mathcal{R},\mathcal{A}
are well-defined on the corresponding quotient algebras:

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{R}:\bigwedge^{\bullet}{}_{\mathrm{fop}}/\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{fop}}\xrightarrow{}\bigwedge^{\bullet}(\partial^{Q}F)/\mathcal{I}_{\partial}\\
\mathcal{A}:\bigwedge^{\bullet}{}_{\mathrm{fop}}/\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{fop}}\xrightarrow{}\bigwedge^{\bullet}(\partial^{Q}F)/\mathcal{I}_{\partial}\end{gathered}

(3.25)

Proof.

The proof is relegated to Appendix
B
.
∎

3.4
Interpretation of the induced cocycles

We now provide an interpretation of the cocycle
v^{\mathcal{R}}=\frac{1}{p!}b^{*}v^{(p)}
. First, note that on
Int(F)
the form
v^{(p)}
admits the following equivalent representation:

Proposition 3.11
.

In the setting of Theorem
3.7
the following relation holds on
Int(F)
:

\displaystyle v^{(p)}=p!\widetilde{v}+L_{Q}\left(\sum_{s=1}^{p-1}p(s-1)!\Omega^{-s}\bar{v}^{(p-s-1)}+\bar{v}^{(p-1)}\right)-p\frac{\xi}{\Omega}\bar{v}^{(p-1)}\,,

(3.26)

where, by convention,
\sum_{s=1}^{0}(\dots)=0
for
p=1
.

The proof of this Proposition is given in Appendix
C
.
The proposition
3.11
can be interpreted by saying that, up to
Q
-exact terms, the polynomially divergent form
\widetilde{v}
can be represented as the sum of
v^{(p)}
and a term proportional to
\frac{\xi}{\Omega}
. Moreover, the coefficient of the latter term is directly related to the anomalous boundary cocycle; see (
3.13
).

Remark 3.12
.

To make contact with the b-calculus of Melrose (see e.g.
[
34
]
) let us consider a simple example of a
Q
-manifold with
Q
-boundary given by
(M,Q)=(T[1]X,\mathrm{d}_{X})
, where
X
is a smooth manifold with boundary
\partial X
. In this case
Q
-cocycles (in functions) of
T[1]X
are just de Rham cocycles of
X
. Let
\Omega
be a boundary defining coordinate with
\mathrm{d}_{X}\Omega=\xi
and consider a cocycle
\widetilde{v}
on the interior with
p=1
. Using (
3.26
) we obtain:

\displaystyle\widetilde{v}=\frac{\mathrm{\mathrm{d}_{X}}\Omega}{\Omega}\bar{v}^{(0)}+(v^{(1)}-L_{d_{X}}\bar{v}^{(0)})\,.

(3.27)

This is the standard decomposition of a
b
-form near the boundary and the mapping of the cohomology is the content of the Mazzeo-Melrose theorem which states that

{}^{b}H^{k}(X)=H^{k}(X)\oplus H^{k-1}(\partial X)\,.

(3.28)

Observe that the last term in (
3.26
) can be rewritten as follows:

\displaystyle-p\frac{\xi}{\Omega}\bar{v}^{(p-1)}=-pL_{Q}(\ln\Omega\bar{v}^{(p-1)})+p\ln\Omega L_{Q}\bar{v}^{(p-1)}.

(3.29)

In Appendix
C
, we show that, since
\bar{v}^{(p-1)}
is a
Q
-cocycle modulo terms vanishing at the boundary, the last term in (
3.29
) can be rewritten iteratively as the sum of a
L_{Q}
-exact term and a remainder. This leads to the following result:

Corollary 3.13
.

In the setting of Theorem
3.7
the following relation holds on
Int(F)
for every integer
N\,{\geqslant}\,1
:

v^{(p)}=p!\widetilde{v}\,+\\
L_{Q}\left(\sum_{s=1}^{p-1}p(s-1)!\Omega^{-s}\bar{v}^{(p-s-1)}+\bar{v}^{(p-1)}-p\sum_{j=0}^{N}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{j!}\Omega^{j}\ln\Omega\bar{v}^{(p-1+j)}\right)+r_{N}[v]\,,

(3.30)

where

r_{N}[v]=p\xi\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{(-1)^{i}}{i!}\Omega^{i-1}\bar{v}^{(p+i-1)}\,+\\
\frac{(-1)^{N}}{N!}\Omega^{N}\ln\Omega((p+N)L_{Q}\bar{v}^{(p+N-1)}-Nv^{(p+N)})\,.

(3.31)

We note two properties of the remainders
r_{N}[v]
that are important for us. First, for any
N
, they have a well-defined limit at
\partial^{Q}F
-boundary and vanish there. Although by assumption
v\equiv\Omega^{p}\widetilde{v}
is smooth, the corresponding
r_{N}[v]
is only
(N-1)
times differentiable because of the term
\Omega^{N}\ln\Omega
.

Corollary
3.13
shows that, for a given
Q
-cocycle
\widetilde{v}\in\bigwedge^{\bullet}_{\mathrm{fop}}(Int(F))
, the induced cocycle
v^{\mathcal{R}}\equiv\mathcal{R}(v)
can be regarded as the renormalized value of the singular form
\widetilde{v}
at the boundary, where the renormalization amounts to the addition of
L_{Q}
-exact terms.

Remark 3.14
.

Corollary
3.13
can be interpreted in the spirit of the holographic renormalization procedure known in the AdS/CFT context
[
53
,
54
]
. To illustrate this, we consider a globally trivial gPDE with asymptotic boundary
(T[1]X,\mathrm{d}_{X})\times(F,q)
, where

\displaystyle X=\mathbb{R}_{\,{\geqslant}\,0}\times\Sigma,\hskip 20.00003pt\partial X\cong\Sigma.

(3.32)

Let
v
be a function of degree
\dim(X)
on
F
, and consider a solution
\sigma
such that
\rho=\sigma^{*}\Omega
, where
\rho
is a boundary defining function on
\mathbb{R}_{\,{\geqslant}\,0}
. Defining
X_{\varepsilon}\equiv\{p\in X|\rho(p)\,{\geqslant}\,\varepsilon\}\subset X
we obtain from
(
3.30
)

\frac{1}{p!}\int_{T[1]X_{\epsilon}}\sigma^{*}(v^{(p)}-r_{N}[v])=\int_{T[1]X_{\varepsilon}}\sigma^{*}\widetilde{v}+\sum_{s=1}^{p-1}\frac{(s-1)!}{(p-1)!}\varepsilon^{-s}\int_{\rho=\varepsilon}\sigma^{*}\bar{v}^{(p-s-1)}-\\
\frac{1}{(p-1)!}\ln\varepsilon\int_{\rho=\varepsilon}\sigma^{*}\bar{v}^{(p-1)}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{0})\,,

(3.33)

where we have used that
\sigma
is a solution, i.e. that
\mathrm{d}_{X}\circ\sigma^{*}=\sigma^{*}\circ Q
, and hence
\int\sigma^{*}(L_{Q}\alpha)=\int_{T[1]X_{\varepsilon}}\partial_{X}\sigma^{*}(\alpha)=\int_{\rho=\varepsilon}\sigma^{*}(\alpha)
.
The left-hand side of this formula can be interpreted as the renormalization of
\int_{T[1]X_{\varepsilon}}\sigma^{*}\widetilde{v}
as it has a well-defined limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
and differs from

\int_{T[1]X_{\varepsilon}}\sigma^{*}\widetilde{v}
by the
\ln\varepsilon
term and terms with poles of positive order in
\varepsilon
. The former is interpreted as the anomaly while the latter are the boundary counterterms. In the context of holographic renormalization, one typically renormalizes the on-shell action, in which case the anomaly is known as the holographic Weyl anomaly. This explains our notation for the
\mathcal{R}
(renormalization) and
\mathcal{A}
(anomaly) maps.

Finally, we note that in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
the expression (
3.30
) does not depend on the integer
N
since, as shown in Appendix
C
, we have

\displaystyle r_{N}[v]=pL_{Q}(\frac{(-1)^{N}}{(N+1)!}\Omega^{N+1}\ln\Omega\bar{v}^{(p+N)})+r_{N+1}[v]

(3.34)

and hence the difference gives the boundary term that vanishes in the
\varepsilon\to 0
limit.

3.5
Renormalization of presymplectic structures

Our focus is Lagrangian gauge theories. In the gPDE approach Lagrangians are encoded in compatible presymplectic structures defined on the total space of a gPDE. Let us assume that the system in question is Lagrangian and let
(E,Q,T[1]X)
be a compactification of its gPDE. Identifying
(Int(E|_{T[1]Int(X)}),Q,T[1](Int(X)))
with the initial gPDE we assume that it is equipped with a compatible presymplectic structure, i.e. a 2-form

\widetilde{\omega}
satisfying

\begin{gathered}i_{Q}\widetilde{\omega}+d\widetilde{H}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}\,,\qquad d\widetilde{\omega}=0\,,\qquad gh(\widetilde{\omega})=dimX-1\,,\end{gathered}

(3.35)

where
\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}
is the ideal generated by the pullback of forms of positive degree from the base
T[1]Int(X)
or, more generally, the corresponding background gPDE. In the case of implicit gPDEs it is convenient to assume that
\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}
also involves the ideal of the equations. Note that (
3.35
) implies

L_{Q}\widetilde{\omega}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}\,.

(3.36)

To proceed further we also need to assume that
\widetilde{\omega}
and
\widetilde{H}
belong to
\bigwedge^{\bullet}_{\mathrm{fop}}(Int(E))
. It follows that one can safely replace
\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}
with
\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathrm{fop}}\equiv\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}\cap\bigwedge^{\bullet}_{\mathrm{fop}}(Int(F))
.
As we are going to see, this assumption is fulfilled in applications.

Now we are in the setting of Lemmas
3.9
and
3.10
which imply that
\widetilde{\omega}
induces two presymplectic structures on the boundary:
\omega^{\mathcal{R}}=\mathcal{R}(\widetilde{\omega})
and
\omega^{\mathcal{A}}=\mathcal{A}(\widetilde{\omega})
. By construction one has
gh(\omega^{\mathcal{R}})=dimX-1=dim(\partial X)
and
gh(\omega^{\mathcal{A}})=dim(\partial X)-1
. Moreover, picking any presymplectic potential
\widetilde{\chi}
such that
\widetilde{\omega}=d\widetilde{\chi}
one gets

\begin{gathered}\omega^{\mathcal{R}}=d\mathcal{R}(\widetilde{\chi})\,,\qquad L_{Q}\omega^{\mathcal{R}}\in\mathcal{I}_{\partial}\\
\omega^{\mathcal{A}}=d\mathcal{A}(\widetilde{\chi})\,,\qquad L_{Q}\omega^{\mathcal{A}}\in\mathcal{I}_{\partial}\,,\end{gathered}

(3.37)

where
\mathcal{I}_{\partial}
is the ideal generated by the pullback of the generators of
\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathrm{fop}}
to the boundary.

Because both
\omega^{\mathcal{R}}
and
\omega^{\mathcal{A}}
are
Q
-invariant modulo the ideal there exist Hamiltonians
H_{l}^{\mathcal{R}}
and
H^{\mathcal{A}}_{l}
such that
i_{Q}\omega^{\mathcal{R}}+dH_{l}^{\mathcal{R}}\in\mathcal{I}_{\partial}
and

i_{Q}\omega^{\mathcal{A}}+dH_{l}^{\mathcal{A}}\in\mathcal{I}_{\partial}
. Let us stress that
H^{\mathcal{R},\mathcal{A}}_{l}
are apparently different from
H^{\mathcal{A}}\equiv\mathcal{A}(\widetilde{H})
and
H^{\mathcal{R}}\equiv\mathcal{R}(\widetilde{H})
, respectively.
Given the presymplectic potential and the Hamiltonian, these data determine BV-AKSZ action functional on the space of supersections of the induced boundary gPDE. Assuming that the presymplectic structures induced on the space of supersections by
\omega^{\mathcal{R}}
and
\omega^{\mathcal{A}}
are regular one can pass to the respective symplectic quotients. In the case of
\omega^{\mathcal{R}},H_{l}^{\mathcal{R}}
the respective BV-AKSZ action has degree
1
and is to be interpreted as the BFV-BRST charge of the BFV system induced on the quotient. It is natural to interpret this system as the renormalised BFV system induced on the asymptotic boundary.
At the same time
\omega^{\mathcal{A}}
has the standard ghost degree and the associated BV-AKSZ action can be identified with the BV extension of the holographic Weyl anomaly, see e.g.
[
54
]
, if one passes to the corresponding symplectic quotient. Recall that this anomaly can be also seen as an action functional
whose equations of motion is the
Fefferman-Graham obstruction.

Although
H_{l}^{\mathcal{R}}
and
H^{\mathcal{A}}_{l}

4
Applications and examples: fields on (A)dS space

In this section we study the boundary structure of gauge fields on (A)dS space. This is done by treating the (A)dS structure as a solution of “flat AdS gravity”, i.e. the truncation of the full gravity where the Weyl tensor is set to zero. In the next step the (gauge) fields on (A)dS space are reformulated as fields defined on the background of flat (A)dS gravity or, more precisely, its conformal compactification. In the gPDE language this corresponds to
Q
-bundles over the
Q

4.1.1
Field-theoretical interpretation

Just like the system
(\mathfrak{g}_{\Lambda}[1],\mathrm{d}_{\mathfrak{g}})\times(T[1]X,\mathrm{d}_{X})
, the boundary gPDE
(\partial^{Q}F_{\mathrm{red}},Q)\times(T[1]\partial X,d_{\partial X})
describes flat connections valued in the orthogonal algebra. However, their field-theoretic interpretation differs. The most evident difference is that the dimension of the base differs by one. Moreover, from the viewpoint of Klein geometry, understood as flat Cartan geometry (see, for example,
[
57
]
), an important additional piece of data is which part of the connection is interpreted as the frame (soldering form) and, accordingly, required to be nondegenerate. In the first case, the natural candidate is
\sigma^{*}\widetilde{\xi}^{a}
, see (
4.8
), which corresponded to the realization of (A)dS space as the coset
SO(D-1,2)/SO(D-1,1)
. In the boundary system, interpreting
\sigma^{*}\widehat{\xi}^{A}\equiv e^{A}{}_{\mu}\theta^{\mu}
as the soldering form, we arrive at the realization of conformal space as the quotient of the orthogonal group by its parabolic subgroup.

Using the natural gauge transformations encoded in
Q
and the zero-curvature equations satisfied by
\sigma
one can set to zero all the components of the connection save for the frame
e^{A}\equiv\sigma^{*}\widehat{\xi}^{A}
which can be set to

\displaystyle\sigma^{*}\widehat{\xi}^{A}=\delta^{A}_{\mu}\theta^{\mu}\,.

(4.20)

We refer to this gauge as the coordinate-like one. Note that if
\sigma
is interpreted as a Cartan connection its natural gauge transformations are not sufficient to achieve (
4.20
). More precisely, to bring the local frame to the above form one also needs to employ the freedom in choosing local coordinates on
\partial X
.

Although the coordinate-like gauge simplifies the expressions, it is at the same time very restrictive and is not useful for keeping track of the background conformal transformations. It is often more convenient to work with a less restrictive gauge (strictly speaking, partial gauge), namely
\sigma^{*}\widehat{\lambda}=0
, in which all connection components except
e^{A}{}_{\mu}
can be expressed in terms of

\gamma_{\mu\nu}\equiv e^{A}{}_{\mu}g_{AB}e^{B}{}_{\nu}
using the zero-curvature condition. In particular, in this gauge one finds

\displaystyle e^{A}{}_{\mu}\sigma^{*}\lambda_{A}=-\frac{1}{d-2}(R_{\mu\nu}[\gamma]-\gamma_{\mu\nu}\frac{R[\gamma]}{2(d-1)})\theta^{\nu}\equiv-P_{\mu\nu}[\gamma]\theta^{\nu}\,,

(4.21)

where
P_{\mu\nu}[\gamma]
is the Schouten tensor constructed from the metric
\gamma_{\mu\nu}
. We refer to this gauge as the metric-like gauge, see, for example,
[
33
]
for details, including the curved case.

Remark 4.6
.

Let
\sigma:T[1]\partial X\to\partial^{Q}F_{\mathrm{red}}
be a solution in the metric-like gauge, i.e.
\sigma^{*}(\widehat{\lambda})=0
. The conserved charge determined by
f^{\mathcal{A}}
from Example
4.5
,can be expressed in terms of the metric
\gamma_{\mu\nu}
and takes the following form:

\displaystyle\int_{T[1]\partial X}\sigma^{*}(f^{\mathcal{A}})=\int_{{\partial X}}d^{4}x\sqrt{\gamma}(P_{\mu\nu}P^{\mu\nu}-P^{2})\,.

(4.22)

This expression formally coincides with the action of conformal gravity up to a topological term. Of course, this should not be interpreted literally as a holographic construction of the conformal gravity action,
since the
Q
-map condition on
\sigma
implies that the metric
\gamma
is conformally flat. In particular, the above integrand can be set to zero, at least locally, by a gauge transformation.

4.2
Fields on compactified (A)dS background

For the remainder of this section we are interested in Lagrangian theories defined on the background of compactified flat (A)dS space.

Our starting point is the gPDE describing flat AdS gravity:
(\mathfrak{g}_{\Lambda}\times T[1]X,\mathrm{d}_{\mathfrak{g}}+\mathrm{d}_{X})
, where
\mathfrak{g}_{\Lambda}
is the AdS algebra.
This system is diffeomorphism-invariant by construction. In the gPDE formulation such systems correspond to gPDEs that are locally trivial as
Q

8
8

8

If gPDE in question is a nontrivial bundle, the additional piece of data on top of the typical fiber and the spacetime manifold is the global structure of the bundle described by e.g. its gluing cocycles. To simplify the exposition, in this work we restrict ourselves to gPDEs which are globally trivial.

What we are really interested in, is not the flat AdS gravity itself but rather a gauge theory defined on its background. Such a system is naturally described as a gPDE over background, where the base gPDE is precisely the flat AdS gravity:

(\mathfrak{g}_{\Lambda},\mathrm{d}_{\mathfrak{g}}

(\widetilde{F}^{\phi},\widetilde{Q})\to(\mathfrak{g}_{\Lambda},\mathrm{d}_{\mathfrak{g}})

Recall that
(\mathfrak{g}_{\Lambda},\mathrm{d}_{\mathfrak{g}})

introduced in Section

In this work our focus is on the extra structure defined on a gPDE, which encodes its (partially) Lagrangian formulation. More specifically, we are interested in compatible presymplectic structures.
Suppose
(\widetilde{F}^{\phi},\widetilde{Q})\xrightarrow{}(\mathfrak{g}_{\Lambda},\mathrm{d}_{\mathfrak{g}})
be equipped with a compatible presymplectic structure:

L_{\widetilde{Q}}\widetilde{\omega}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathfrak{g}_{\Lambda}[1]}\,.

(4.23)

Then it defines a structure on
Int(F^{\phi})
obeying

L_{Q}\widetilde{\omega}\in\mathcal{I}_{Int(F)}\,,

(4.24)

where by some abuse of notations we use the same symbol to denote
\pi_{\mathfrak{g}_{\Lambda}}^{*}\widetilde{\omega}
. By construction the ideal
\mathcal{I}_{Int(F)}
is extendable, therefore, if
\widetilde{\omega}\in\bigwedge_{\mathrm{fop}}^{2}(Int(F^{\phi}))
one can apply
\mathcal{R},\mathcal{A}
maps to obtain cocycles on
\partial^{Q}F^{\phi}
.

In general the starting point gPDE
(\widetilde{F}^{\phi},\widetilde{Q})\xrightarrow{}(\mathfrak{g}_{\Lambda},\mathrm{d}_{\mathfrak{g}})
might be implicit, with some ideal
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\phi}
and equation (
4.23
) might only be satisfied up to
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\phi}
. The whole construction goes through if the corresponding ideal on
Int(F^{\phi})
is extendable which will be the case in our examples.

Let us collect all the bundles in question into one diagram, each arrow is a
Q
-map and arrows without labels are canonical:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{F}^{\phi}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\pi_{\mathfrak{g}_{\Lambda}})^{*}\widetilde{F}^{\phi}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F^{\phi}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{\mathrm{red}}^{\phi}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\partial^{Q}F_{\mathrm{red}}^{\phi}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathfrak{g}_{\Lambda}[1]}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Int(F)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{\mathrm{red}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\partial^{Q}(F_{\mathrm{red}})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{\pi}^{\phi}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces compactification}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi^{\phi}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{\mathfrak{g}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces compactification}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces r}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces b}

Here
F^{\phi}_{\mathrm{red}}\equiv r^{*}F^{\phi}
and
\partial^{Q}F^{\phi}_{\mathrm{red}}=b^{*}F^{\phi}_{\mathrm{red}}
.

To conclude, under the assumption that the ideals are extendable and the compactification of
\widetilde{F}^{\phi}
is fixed, the initial gPDE
(\widetilde{F}^{\phi},\widetilde{Q})
equipped with the compatible presymplectic structure induces a boundary gPDE equipped with a pair of compatible presymplectic structures
\omega^{\mathcal{A}}
and
\omega^{\mathcal{R}}
. The structure
\omega^{\mathcal{R}}
has degree equal to the boundary dimension. Hence, the associated presymplectic BV-AKSZ action has degree one and is to be interpreted as a BFV-BRST-like charge. The structure
\omega^{\mathcal{A}}
, on the other hand, carries precisely the right degree to define a compatible presymplectic structure on the induced boundary gPDE. The associated intrinsic action (
2.5
) is therefore of degree zero and is interpreted as an action functional on the space of asymptotic boundary data.

4.3
Example: Scalar

In this section, we illustrate the general construction with the example of Klein-Gordon (KG) field on the background of (compactified) flat AdS gravity. Instead of constructing the compactification of this system step by step, we present the result and then comment on possible ways to derive it. Namely, consider an implicit gPDE

\displaystyle(F^{\varphi},Q,\mathcal{E}^{\varphi})\to(F,Q)

(4.25)

with fiber coordinates
\nabla_{(a)}\varphi\equiv\{\nabla_{(a_{1}}\dots\nabla_{a_{n})}\varphi,n\,{\geqslant}\,0\}
.
Here, following
[
33
]
and by analogy with the example of Section
2.4
, we use convenient coordinates generated by the vector fields
\nabla_{a}=\left[Q,\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi^{a}}\right]
.
Let us note that, for the chosen background
F
, the nilpotency of
Q
implies, in particular,
[\nabla_{a},\nabla_{b}]=0
.
The action of
Q
on the fiber coordinates is given by:

\displaystyle Q\varphi=\xi^{a}\nabla_{a}\varphi+w\lambda\varphi

(4.26)

and prolonged to other coordinates using
[\nabla_{a},Q]=0
. Note that we are actually dealing
with the family of systems parameterized by
w\in\mathbb{R}
. The ideal
\mathcal{E}^{\varphi}
is generated by
\nabla_{b}
-prolongations of

K~~=~~\Omega^{2}\nabla_{a}\nabla^{a}\varphi~+~\Omega((1-2w-d)n^{a}\nabla_{a}\varphi~+~(d+1)w\tau\varphi)~+\\
(w(d+w)n_{a}n^{a}+m^{2})\varphi\,.

(4.27)

Recall that by
\nabla_{a}
prolongations of
K
we mean
\nabla_{(b_{1}}\ldots\ldots\nabla_{b_{k})}K,\,k>0
. It is easy to check that on
Int(F)
, i.e. where
\Omega>0
, this system is just a reformulation of the usual gPDE description of KG field. Indeed, the equivalent reduction to
\Omega=1,Q\Omega=0,n^{a}=n_{0}^{a},Qn^{a}=0
brings it back.

This system can be obtained in two ways. The first is to view it as the zero-curvature limit of the Klein-Gordon equation on a general asymptotically AdS background, as described in
[
33
]
. The second approach is to consider the standard implicit gPDE for the Klein–Gordon equation on the background
\mathfrak{g}_{\Lambda}[1]
, with fiber coordinates
\widetilde{\nabla}_{(a)}\widetilde{\varphi}
, and then lift it to
Int(F)
using the projection
\pi_{\mathfrak{g}_{\Lambda}}
introduced in Proposition
4.1
, thereby introducing new near-boundary coordinates

\displaystyle\nabla_{(a)}\varphi\equiv\nabla_{(a)}(\Omega^{w}\pi_{\mathfrak{g}_{\Lambda}}^{*}\widetilde{\varphi})

(4.28)

and rewriting accordingly the ideal generated by
\widetilde{K}\equiv\widetilde{\nabla}_{a}\widetilde{\nabla}^{a}\widetilde{\varphi}+m^{2}\widetilde{\varphi}
so that
\mathcal{E}^{\varphi}
is generated by the
\nabla_{a}
-prolongations of
\Omega^{w}\pi_{\mathfrak{g}_{\Lambda}}^{*}\widetilde{K}
.

Let us constrain the parameters
m,w
via

\displaystyle w=\ell-\frac{d}{2},\hskip 10.00002ptm^{2}=-\epsilon w(d+w),\hskip 10.00002pt\ell\in\mathbb{N}/2,

(4.29)

where the choice of mass is standard in the AdS/CFT context; the restriction of
\ell
to half-integer numbers, as we anticipate, will be related to the divergence behaviour of the presymplectic potential. Note that there can be nontrivial boundary systems even if the above relations do not hold but they are not of direct interest in the present context. See however, the discussion in
[
33
]
.

We now apply the reduction described in Proposition
4.3
. We denote by
F^{\varphi}_{\mathrm{red}}\equiv r^{*}F^{\varphi}
, where
r
denotes the embedding of
F_{\mathrm{red}}
into
F
, the corresponding pullback bundle. Splitting the index
\{a\}=\{\Omega,A\}
and introducing notation
\varphi^{(N)}\equiv(\nabla)^{N}\varphi
coordinates of the fiber can be chosen as follows:

\displaystyle\nabla_{(A)}\varphi^{(N)},\hskip 10.00002pt\varphi^{(N)}\equiv\nabla^{N}\varphi,\hskip 10.00002ptN\,{\geqslant}\,0\,.

(4.30)

Pulling back to the
Q
-boundary of the base, we obtain

\displaystyle(\partial^{Q}F_{\mathrm{red}}^{\varphi},Q,\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{\varphi})\to(\partial^{Q}F_{\mathrm{red}},Q)

(4.31)

with fiber coordinates
\nabla_{(A)}\widehat{\varphi}
. Here we use the notation
b^{*}f\equiv\widehat{f}
for all
f\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(F^{\varphi}_{\mathrm{red}})
and also
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{\varphi}\equiv b^{*}\mathcal{E}^{\varphi}.
Let us note that the vector fields
\nabla_{A}
on
F^{\varphi}
are tangent to both
F^{\varphi}_{\mathrm{red}}\subset F^{\varphi}
and
\partial^{Q}F^{\varphi}_{\mathrm{red}}\subset\partial^{Q}F^{\varphi}
. Therefore, we denote their restrictions by the same symbol
\nabla_{A}
.

The ideal
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{\varphi}
is generated by
\nabla_{A}
-prolongations of

\displaystyle(N-2\ell)\widehat{\varphi}^{(N)}+(N-1)\varepsilon\nabla_{A}\nabla^{A}\widehat{\varphi}^{(N-2)}\,,\hskip 10.00002ptN\,{\geqslant}\,1\,.

(4.32)

It follows that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widehat{\varphi}^{(i)}\propto\Box\widehat{\varphi}^{(i-2)}+\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{\varphi}\,,\quad i\neq 0,1,2\ell\,,\end{split}

(4.33)

where
\Box\equiv\nabla_{A}\nabla^{A}.
By iterating this procedure one concludes that all
\widehat{\varphi}^{(i)}
can be expressed in terms of the remaining ones modulo
\mathcal{E}^{\varphi}
, except for
\widehat{\varphi}^{(0)}
and
\widehat{\varphi}^{(2\ell)}
, which are interpreted as the leading and subleading values of the scalar field, respectively. Moreover, when
\ell
is an integer, we have

\displaystyle\Box^{\ell}\widehat{\varphi}\in\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{\varphi}

(4.34)

which can be interpreted as the flat limit of the GJMS equation; see
[
33
]
for an analogous analysis in the curved case.

The action of
Q
on
\partial^{Q}F_{\mathrm{red}}^{\varphi}
can be easily obtained using Proposition
4.4
and the action of
Q
in the bulk (
4.26
):

\displaystyle\begin{split}&Q\widehat{\varphi}^{(i)}=\widehat{\xi}^{A}\nabla_{A}\widehat{\varphi}^{(i)}+\frac{i(i-1)}{2}\epsilon\widehat{\lambda}^{A}\nabla_{A}\widehat{\varphi}^{(i-2)}+(\ell-i-d/2)\widehat{\lambda}\widehat{\varphi}^{(i)}\,.\\
\end{split}

(4.35)

Now let us turn our attention to presymplectic structures. The presymplectic potential for KG on the background of flat AdS gravity is given by, see e.g.
[
42
]

\widetilde{\chi}^{\prime}_{\varphi}=\widetilde{\xi}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d]$}}_{a}\widetilde{\nabla}^{a}\widetilde{\varphi}d\widetilde{\varphi}\,,

(4.36)

where we assumed that the background flat AdS gravity is described by a minimal gPDE
(\mathfrak{g}_{\Lambda}[1],\mathrm{d}_{\mathfrak{g}})\times(T[1]X,\mathrm{d}_{X})
with fiber coordinates
\widetilde{\xi}^{a},\widetilde{\rho}^{ab}
. Note that the same expression holds true for generic gravitational background
[
47
]
. This presymplectic potential can be pulled back to
(Int(F^{\varphi}_{\mathrm{red}}),Q)
by the canonical projection
\pi^{\mathrm{red}}_{\mathfrak{g}_{\Lambda}}:Int(F_{\mathrm{red}})\to\mathfrak{g}_{\Lambda}[1]
given by (
4.13
), making

(Int(F^{\varphi}_{\mathrm{red}}),Q)
into an equivalent gPDE with compatible presymplectic structure. Indeed,
representing
(Int(F_{\mathrm{red}}),Q)
as a product
Q
-manifold
(\mathfrak{g}_{\Lambda}[1],\mathrm{d}_{\mathfrak{g}})
and a contractible piece one finds that
(Int(F^{\varphi}_{\mathrm{red}}),Q)
factorizes into the starting point gPDE with presymplectic structure over
(\mathfrak{g}[1],\mathrm{d}_{\mathfrak{g}})
and the contractible piece. The latter is clearly in the kernel of the presymplectic structure and hence does not contribute to the corresponding BV formulation and the intrinsic action in particular.

Details of the derivation of the explicit form of
\chi^{\prime}_{\varphi}
pulled back to
Int(F^{\varphi}_{\mathrm{red}})

are relegated to Appendix
E
. Here we immediately present the equivalent (differing by the exact 1-form and terms from the ideal) presymplectic potential and state its properties:

Proposition 4.7
.

1-form

\displaystyle\widetilde{\chi}_{\varphi}=\Omega^{-d/2-\ell+1}(\xi^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d]$}}_{\Omega}\epsilon\varphi^{(1)}+\xi^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d]$}}_{A}\nabla^{A}\varphi)d(\Omega^{d/2-\ell}\varphi)

(4.37)

on
(Int(F_{\mathrm{red}}^{\varphi}),Q)
is a compatible presymplectic potential., i.e. it satisfies

\displaystyle L_{Q}\widetilde{\chi}_{\varphi}=d(-\widetilde{H}_{\varphi}-i_{Q}\widetilde{\chi}_{\varphi})+\mathcal{I}_{Int(F)}+\mathcal{E}^{\varphi}\,.

(4.38)

Covariant Hamiltonian
H_{\varphi}
is given explicitly by:

\widetilde{H}_{\varphi}=\frac{(-1)^{d+1}}{2}\Omega^{-2\ell}[\xi^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d+1]$}}(\Omega(\epsilon(\varphi^{(1)})^{2}+\nabla_{A}\varphi\nabla^{A}\varphi)\,\,+\\
(d-2\ell)\epsilon\varphi\varphi^{(1)})+(d/2-\ell)\Omega\varphi^{2}\lambda^{A}\xi_{A}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d]$}}]\,.

(4.39)

Moreover, this system is equivalent to the starting point minimal gPDE over
\mathfrak{g}_{\Lambda}[1]
with presymplectic structure (
4.36
)
in the sense that it can be represented as the product of this gPDE and the contractible gPDE with trivial presymplectic structure. Strictly speaking this applies to
\widetilde{\chi}_{\varphi}
modified by the
d
-exact term and the term from the ideal
\mathcal{E}^{\varphi}
.

It is clear that
\chi_{\varphi}\equiv\Omega^{2\ell}\widetilde{\chi}_{\varphi}
,
H_{\varphi}\equiv\Omega^{2\ell}\widetilde{H}_{\varphi}
extend to the boundary and hence
\chi^{\mathcal{R}}
and
\chi^{\mathcal{A}}
are well-defined and can be explicitly obtained by the procedure of Section
3.5
with
p=2\ell
. This explains why we assumed
\ell\in\mathbb{N}/2
.

Now we calculate the induced structures on the boundary.

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
p=2\ell
be odd. Then

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\chi^{\mathcal{R}}_{\varphi}=\frac{\epsilon}{(2\ell-1)!}\widehat{\xi}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d]$}}\widehat{\varphi}^{(2\ell)}d\widehat{\varphi}+\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{\varphi}\,,\qquad\chi^{\mathcal{A}}_{\varphi}\in\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{\varphi}\,,\end{split}

(4.40)

where
\widehat{\xi}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[N]$}}_{A_{1}\dots A_{d-N}}\equiv b^{*}\xi^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[N]$}}_{\Omega A_{1}\dots A_{d-N}}
and
d=D-1
.

Proof.

On
F^{\varphi}_{\mathrm{red}}
we calculate:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\chi_{\varphi}=\Omega(\xi_{\Omega}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d]$}}\epsilon\varphi^{(1)}+\xi_{A}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d]$}}\nabla^{A}\varphi)d\varphi+(d/2-\ell)\varphi(\xi_{\Omega}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d]$}}\epsilon\varphi^{(1)}+\xi_{A}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d]$}}\nabla^{A}\varphi)d\Omega\,,\\
&\bar{\chi}_{\varphi}=-\Omega\xi^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d-1]$}}_{\Omega A}\nabla^{A}\varphi d\varphi+d\Omega(reg)\,,\\
\end{split}

(4.41)

To prove the result, note that (
4.32
) implies that for odd
i<2\ell
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\varphi}^{(i)}\in\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{\varphi}\,.

(4.42)

We also note that Proposition
4.4
implies that, for odd
i
,

\displaystyle b^{*}(\nabla)^{i}\xi_{\Omega}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d]$}}=b^{*}(\nabla)^{i}\xi^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d-1]$}}_{\Omega A}=b^{*}(\nabla)^{i-1}\xi^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d]$}}_{A}=0\,.

(4.43)

∎

Using the coordinate-like gauge on the boundary it is easy to see that the presymplectic structure induced by
d\chi_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{R}}
on the space of supersections is proportional to

\displaystyle\int d^{d}x\delta\varphi^{(0)}(x)\wedge\delta\varphi^{(2l)}(x)\,,

(4.44)

where by some abuse of conventions
\varphi(x)\equiv\mathrm{ev}^{*}(\widehat{\varphi})
,
\varphi^{(r)}(x)\equiv\mathrm{ev}^{*}(\widehat{\varphi}^{(r)})
. The respective symplectic quotient does not have coordinates of nonvanishing degree and can be identified with the phase-space of the scalar field in
D=d+1
dimensions. Note that the respective BV-AKSZ action (which is to be identified with the BFV-BRST charge) clearly vanishes by the degree reasoning.

Further examples follow from direct calculations, by applying
L_{\nabla}
to (
4.41
).

Example 4.9
.

Let
\ell=1
. Then, up to
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{\varphi}
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\chi_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{R}}=(\widehat{\xi}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d]$}}\epsilon\widehat{\varphi}^{(2)}+\widehat{\lambda}\widehat{\xi}_{A}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d-1]$}}\nabla^{A}\widehat{\varphi})d\widehat{\varphi}\,,\qquad\chi_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{A}}=(-1)^{d}\widehat{\xi}_{A}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d-1]$}}\nabla^{A}\widehat{\varphi}d\widehat{\varphi}\,,\\
&H_{l}^{\mathcal{A}}=\widehat{\xi}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d]$}}\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{A}\widehat{\varphi}\nabla_{A}\widehat{\varphi}+\frac{d-2}{4}\widehat{\varphi}^{2}\widehat{\lambda}^{A}\widehat{\xi}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d-1]$}}_{A}-\frac{d-2}{2}\widehat{\varphi}\widehat{\lambda}\widehat{\xi}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d-1]$}}_{A}\nabla^{A}\widehat{\varphi}\,,\end{split}

(4.45)

where
H^{\mathcal{A}}_{l}
is the covariant Hamiltonian associated with the presymplectic structure
\omega^{\mathcal{A}}=d\chi^{\mathcal{A}}
, obtained via (
3.44
).

By some abuse of notation we define coordinates on the space of sections
\sigma:T[1]\partial X\xrightarrow{}\partial^{Q}F^{\varphi}_{\mathrm{red}}
by

\begin{gathered}\sigma^{*}\widehat{\varphi}=\varphi\,,\quad\sigma^{*}\nabla_{A}\widehat{\varphi}=\varphi_{A}\,,\quad\sigma^{*}\widehat{\xi}^{A}\equiv e^{A}{}_{\mu}\theta^{\mu}\,,\quad\sigma^{*}\widehat{\lambda}_{A}=\lambda_{A|\mu}\theta^{\mu}\,,\qquad\sigma^{*}\widehat{\lambda}=\lambda_{|\mu}\theta^{\mu}\end{gathered}

(4.46)

and denoting
\partial_{A}\equiv e^{\mu}{}_{A}\partial_{\mu}
,
\lambda_{A|B}=\lambda_{A|\mu}e_{B}{}^{\mu}
,
\lambda_{|B}=\lambda_{|\mu}e_{B}{}^{\mu}
, the intrinsic action associated with
\chi_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{A}}
takes the form:

\displaystyle S[\varphi,\varphi^{A}]=-\int e^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d]$}}(\varphi^{A}\partial_{A}\varphi-\frac{1}{2}\varphi^{A}\varphi_{A}+\frac{2-d}{4}\varphi^{2}\lambda^{A}{}_{|A}+\frac{d-2}{2}\varphi\lambda_{|A}\varphi^{A})\,.

(4.47)

Picking a background configuration subject to the metric-like gauge
introduced in Section
4.1.1
,
\gamma_{\mu\nu}\equiv e^{A}{}_{\mu}g_{AB}e^{B}{}_{\nu},
and eliminating the auxiliary field
\varphi^{A}
, the action can be rewritten as

\displaystyle S[\varphi]=-\frac{1}{2}\int d^{d}x\sqrt{\gamma}(\partial_{\mu}\varphi\partial^{\mu}\varphi+\frac{d-2}{4(d-1)}\varphi^{2}R[\gamma])\,,

(4.48)

which is the standard form of the conformally coupled scalar action. By construction this action is invariant under the background gauge transformations preserving the metric-like gauge. Note that we restricted ourselves to flat-backgrounds and hence the invariance is guaranteed only for them. However, in this particular case the above action is conformally invariant even for curved
\gamma
but this is not by construction. In fact, in this case it is straightforward to replace flat AdS gravity background with the full gravity and obtain the boundary system as in
[
33
]
, giving a conformally-coupled scalar on general background.

Example 4.10
.

Let
\ell=2
. Then, up to
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{\varphi},

\displaystyle\chi_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{A}}=\epsilon\frac{(-1)^{d}}{4}(\widehat{\xi}_{A}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d-1]$}}\nabla^{A}\widehat{\varphi}d\Box\widehat{\varphi}+\widehat{\xi}_{A}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d-1]$}}\nabla^{A}\Box\widehat{\varphi}d\widehat{\varphi}+2\widehat{\lambda}^{B}\widehat{\xi}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d-2]$}}_{AB}\nabla^{A}\widehat{\varphi}d\widehat{\varphi})\,,

(4.49)

and

H^{\mathcal{A}}_{l}=-\frac{\epsilon}{4}(\widehat{\xi}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d]$}}(\frac{1}{2}(\Box\widehat{\varphi})^{2}-\nabla_{A}\Box\widehat{\varphi}\nabla^{A}\widehat{\varphi})-\widehat{\lambda}^{B}\widehat{\xi}_{B}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d-1]$}}\nabla_{A}\varphi\nabla^{A}\varphi+\\
\frac{4-d}{2}\widehat{\lambda}^{A}\widehat{\xi}_{A}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d-1]$}}\widehat{\varphi}\Box\widehat{\varphi}+\frac{4-d}{2}\widehat{\lambda}^{A}\widehat{\lambda}^{B}\widehat{\xi}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d-2]$}}_{AB}\varphi^{2}+\frac{d}{2}\widehat{\lambda}\widehat{\xi}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d-1]$}}_{A}\nabla^{A}\varphi\Box\varphi+\\
(4-d)\widehat{\lambda}^{B}\widehat{\lambda}\widehat{\xi}_{AB}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d-2]$}}\nabla^{A}\widehat{\varphi}\widehat{\varphi}+\frac{d-4}{2}\widehat{\lambda}\widehat{\xi}_{A}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d-1]$}}\varphi\nabla^{A}\Box\varphi)\,.

(4.50)

The corresponding intrinsic action can be immediately read-off from the above data in terms of the conformal Cartan geometry of the background. For brevity, we illustrate its form in the metric-like gauge of the background:

S[\varphi,\varphi_{\alpha},\Box\varphi,\Box_{\alpha}\varphi]=-\frac{\epsilon}{4}\int d^{d}x\sqrt{\gamma}((\Box^{\mu}\varphi+2P^{\sigma}{}_{\nu}\delta^{\nu\mu}_{\rho\sigma}\varphi^{\rho})\partial_{\mu}\varphi+\varphi^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}\Box\varphi+\\
\frac{1}{2}(\Box\varphi)^{2}-\Box_{\mu}\varphi\varphi^{\mu}+P\varphi_{\mu}\varphi^{\mu}+\frac{d-4}{2}P\varphi\Box\varphi-\frac{d-4}{2}P^{\mu}{}_{\sigma}P^{\nu}{}_{\rho}\delta^{\sigma\rho}_{\mu\nu}\varphi^{2})\,,

(4.51)

where
P^{\sigma}{}_{\nu}
is the Schouten tensor of
\gamma
. All fields except for
\varphi
are auxiliary, and eliminating them yields, up to a boundary term:

S[\varphi]\propto\int d^{d}x\sqrt{\gamma}((\nabla_{\alpha}\nabla^{\alpha}\varphi)^{2}-(4P^{\mu\nu}-(d-2)Pg^{\mu\nu})\partial_{\mu}\varphi\partial_{\nu}\varphi+\\
\frac{d-4}{2}(-\nabla_{\mu}\nabla^{\mu}P+\frac{d}{2}P^{2}-2P^{\mu\nu}P_{\mu\nu})\varphi^{2})\,.

(4.52)

Just like in the previous example this action happens to be conformally invariant on generic background and its equation of motion is the Paneitz equation
[
58
]
. For
d=4
this action was proposed even earlier in
[
59
]
.

Example 4.11
.

Finally, let us consider the general case with
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and to simplify the considerations restrict ourselves to the coordinate-like gauge where
\sigma^{*}(\xi^{A})=\theta^{A}
and all other background fields are set to zero. On this background

\displaystyle\begin{split}\chi_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{R}}&=\frac{\epsilon}{(2\ell-1)!}\theta^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d]$}}\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}C^{2i}_{2\ell-1}\widehat{\varphi}^{(2\ell-2i)}d\widehat{\varphi}^{(2i)}\,,\\
\chi_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{A}}&=\frac{(-1)^{d}}{(2\ell-2)!}\theta^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d-1]$}}_{A}\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}C^{2i}_{2\ell-2}\nabla^{A}\widehat{\varphi}^{(2\ell-2-2i)}d\widehat{\varphi}^{(2i)}\,,\\
H^{\mathcal{A}}_{l}&=-\frac{1}{2(2\ell-2)!}\theta^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d]$}}\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}(\epsilon C^{2i-1}_{2\ell-2}\widehat{\varphi}^{(2\ell-2i)}\widehat{\varphi}^{(2i)}-C^{2i}_{2\ell-2}\nabla_{A}\widehat{\varphi}^{(2\ell-2-2i)}\nabla^{A}\widehat{\varphi}^{(2i)})\,,\end{split}

(4.53)

up to terms from
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{\varphi}
. The intrinsic action associated with

takes the form

S[\varphi^{(2i)},\varphi^{(2i)|A}]\propto\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\int d^{d}x[C^{2i}_{2\ell-2}\varphi^{(2\ell-2-2i)|A}\partial_{A}\varphi^{(2i)}+\\
\frac{1}{2}(\epsilon C^{2i-1}_{2\ell-2}\varphi^{(2\ell-2i)}\varphi^{(2i)}-C^{2i}_{2\ell-2}\varphi_{A}^{(2\ell-2-2i)}\varphi^{(2i)|A})]\,.

(4.54)

One can easily see that all the fields save for

are auxiliary and their elimination gives

\displaystyle S[\varphi^{(0)}]\propto\int d^{d}x\varphi^{(0)}(\partial_{A}\partial^{A})^{\ell}\varphi^{(0)}\,.

(4.55)

In other words, (

) is the first-order action of the higher-order singleton field. Although this action can easily be obtained by e.g. applying the intrinsic action construction of
[
37
]
,
the present derivation is based on holographic considerations and guarantees the conformal invariance.
It can also be generalised to a non-flat case in a systematic way.

4.4
Example: Maxwell

Consider an implicit gPDE over background

\displaystyle(F^{\mathcal{M}},Q,\mathcal{E}^{\mathcal{M}})\to(F,Q)

(4.56)

whose fiber is coordinatized by the following overcomplete coordinates

\displaystyle C,\hskip 10.00002pt\nabla_{(a)}F_{bc},\hskip 20.00003ptgh(F_{bc})=0\,,\hskip 10.00002pt\,gh(C)=1\,,

(4.57)

subject to the
\nabla_{a}

\displaystyle\nabla_{{[a}}F_{bc]}=0,\hskip 20.00003ptF_{ab}=-F_{ba}\,.

(4.58)

The action of

\displaystyle QC=\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.5pt}{\footnotesize$\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}$}\kern 1.0pt}{\frac{1}{2}}{\frac{1}{2}}{\frac{1}{2}}\xi^{a}\xi^{b}F_{ab}\,,\hskip 20.00003ptQF_{ab}=\xi^{c}\nabla_{c}F_{ab}+\rho_{a}{}^{c}F_{cb}+\rho_{b}{}^{c}F_{ac}-2\lambda F_{ab}

(4.59)

and the ideal

is generated by the

Field-theoretical interpretation

Just like in the (A)dS case, the boundary gPDE
\partial^{Q}E\equiv(\partial^{Q}F_{\mathrm{red}},Q)\times(T[1]\partial X,d_{\partial X})
describes flat connections, except that they now take values in the Poincaré algebra. Indeed, the
Q
-map condition on sections
\sigma:T[1]\partial X\to\partial^{Q}E
is the flatness condition, as can be seen from (
5.11
).
However, the field-theoretic interpretation of this connection is not entirely standard, since the base is
d\equiv(D-1)
-dimensional. Introducing
\sigma^{*}\widehat{\xi}^{\alpha}\equiv e^{\alpha}(x,\theta)\equiv e^{\alpha}{}_{\mu}(x)\theta^{\mu}
we require
e^{\alpha}{}_{\mu}
to be nondegenerate, which is again not entirely standard, since from the viewpoint of the Poincaré algebra they do not correspond to the translational part. In other words, from the viewpoint of Klein geometry, we consider a different coset, see
[
64
]
for a related discussion.

It is easy to see that

\sigma^{*}(\xi^{\alpha})=\theta^{\alpha}\,,\hskip 20.00003pt\sigma^{*}(\text{the rest})=0

(5.16)

is clearly a solution satisfying the above nondegeneracy condition. Moreover, as for any flat connection, any other solution is locally related to the above one by a gauge transformation. We will refer to the gauge (
5.16
) as the coordinate-like gauge.

As in the (A)dS case, we can introduce a more general gauge. To this end, we assume that
\partial X
has the topology
\mathbb{R}\times\Sigma
for some
\Sigma
, and we introduce adapted coordinates
\{x^{\mu}\}\equiv\{u,y^{i}\}
, where
u
is a coordinate on
\mathbb{R}
. We then fix the notation
\sigma^{*}\widehat{C}^{I}\equiv C^{I}(x,\theta)\equiv C^{I}{}_{|\mu}(x)\theta^{\mu}
for all coordinates except
\widehat{\xi}^{\alpha}
, for which we keep the convention
\sigma^{*}\widehat{\xi}^{\alpha}=e^{\alpha}{}_{\mu}(x)\theta^{\mu}
, and require

\displaystyle e^{A}{}_{u}=0\,,\hskip 10.00002pte^{u}{}_{\mu}=\partial_{\mu}u\,,\hskip 10.00002pte^{i}{}_{A}C^{A}{}_{|i}=0\,,\hskip 10.00002pt\lambda_{|\mu}=0\,,

(5.17)

where
e^{i}{}_{A}
is the inverse of
e^{A}{}_{i}
. Note that the nondegeneracy of
e^{\alpha}{}_{\mu}
together with
e^{u}{}_{\mu}=\partial_{\mu}u
implies the nondegeneracy of
e^{A}{}_{i}
, so the inverse is well defined. Following
[
64
]
, we will refer to this gauge as the Bondi gauge, although in this gauge we do not require the scalar curvature of
\gamma_{ij}\equiv e^{A}{}_{i}g_{AB}e^{B}{}_{j}
to be constant. In this gauge, the flatness condition implies in particular

\begin{gathered}\partial_{u}\gamma_{ij}=\lambda_{A|u}=\lambda_{[i|j]}=C_{B|u}=C_{[i|j]}=0\,,\qquad\lambda_{i|j}\big|_{tf}=\partial_{u}C_{i|j}\,,\\
2(d-2)\lambda^{i}{}_{|i}=-R[\gamma]\,,\qquad 2(d-2)\lambda^{u}{}_{|u}=\frac{R[\gamma]}{d-1}\,,\qquad(d-2)\lambda^{u}{}_{|i}=-D^{j}C_{j|i}\,,\end{gathered}

(5.18)

where we used
e^{A}{}_{i}
to convert indices, e.g.
\lambda_{i|j}\equiv e^{A}{}_{i}\lambda_{A|j}
. Here
R[\gamma]
is the Ricci scalar of
\gamma_{ij}
,
|_{tf}
denotes the trace-free part, and
D_{i}
is the Levi–Civita connection, both defined with respect to
\gamma_{ij}
. In the context of gravity at null infinity,
C_{i|j}
is usually referred to as the asymptotic shift.

5.2
Example: Scalar

The examples of fields on a background with vanishing cosmological constant closely follow those on the
\Lambda\neq 0
background discussed in Sections
4.3
and
4.4
. For this reason, we will be rather brief in what follows, focusing primarily on the key formulas and the final results.

We start directly with the reduced model
(F^{\varphi}_{\mathrm{red}},Q,\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{red}}^{\varphi})\xrightarrow{}(F_{\mathrm{red}},Q)
. As coordinates on the fiber of this bundle, we choose

\displaystyle\nabla_{(\alpha)}\varphi^{(N)}\equiv\nabla_{(\alpha)}\nabla^{N}\varphi\,,\hskip 20.00003ptN\,{\geqslant}\,0\,,

(5.19)

with the action of
Q
given by

\displaystyle Q\varphi=\xi\varphi^{(1)}+\xi^{\alpha}\nabla_{\alpha}\varphi+(\ell-d/2)\lambda\varphi-\lambda\Omega\varphi^{(1)}\,,

(5.20)

where
\ell\in\mathbb{N}/2
. The ideal
\mathcal{E}^{\varphi}_{\mathrm{red}}
is generated by
\nabla_{a}
prolongations of

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Omega(2\nabla_{u}\varphi^{(1)}+\nabla_{A}\nabla^{A}\varphi)+(1-2\ell)\nabla_{u}\varphi\end{split}

(5.21)

which can be obtained as the pullback of (
4.27
) to
F_{\mathrm{red}}
upon setting
m=0
,
w=\ell-\frac{d}{2}
.

The induced boundary gPDE for
(F^{\varphi}_{\mathrm{red}},Q,\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{red}}^{\varphi})\xrightarrow{}(F_{\mathrm{red}},Q)

is given by
(\partial^{Q}F^{\varphi}_{\mathrm{red}},Q,\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{\varphi})\to(\partial^{Q}F_{\mathrm{red}},Q)
. Using (
5.19
) pulled back to
\partial^{Q}F_{\mathrm{red}}
as the fiber coordinates and employing (
5.20
) and Proposition
5.5
one finds:

Q\widehat{\varphi}^{(N)}=\widehat{\xi}^{\alpha}\nabla_{\alpha}\widehat{\varphi}^{(N)}+N\widehat{C}^{A}\nabla_{A}\widehat{\varphi}^{(N-1)}+\\
N\widehat{\lambda}^{u}(\ell-\frac{d}{2}-N+1)\widehat{\varphi}^{(N-1)}+\widehat{\lambda}(\ell-\frac{d}{2}-N)\widehat{\varphi}^{(N)}\,.

(5.22)

The ideal
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{\varphi}=b^{*}\mathcal{E}^{\varphi}
determined by (
5.21
), is generated by
\nabla_{\alpha}
prolongations of:

\displaystyle(2N-2\ell+1)\nabla_{u}\widehat{\varphi}^{(N)}+N\nabla^{A}\nabla_{A}\widehat{\varphi}^{(N-1)}\,,\hskip 10.00002ptN\,{\geqslant}\,0\,.

(5.23)

Now we are ready to turn our attention to the presymplectic potentials. In complete analogy with Proposition
4.7
, we have

Proposition 5.7
.

1
-form

\displaystyle\widetilde{\chi}_{\varphi}=\Omega^{-d/2-\ell+1}(\xi_{\Omega}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d]$}}\nabla_{u}\varphi+\xi^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d]$}}_{u}\varphi^{(1)}+\xi^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d]$}}_{A}\nabla^{A}\varphi)d(\Omega^{d/2-\ell}\varphi)\,,

(5.24)

on
Int(F^{\varphi}_{\mathrm{red}})
is a compatible presymplectic potential, i.e. it satisfies

L_{Q}\widetilde{\chi}_{\varphi}=d(-\widetilde{H}_{\varphi}-i_{Q}\widetilde{\chi}_{\varphi})+\mathcal{I}_{Int(M)}+\mathcal{E}^{\varphi}\,,

(5.25)

where

\widetilde{H}_{\varphi}=\frac{(-1)^{d+1}}{2}\Omega^{-2\ell}[\xi^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d+1]$}}(\Omega(2\varphi^{(1)}\nabla_{u}\varphi+\nabla_{A}\varphi\nabla^{A}\varphi)+\\
(d-2\ell)\varphi\nabla_{u}\varphi)+(\frac{d}{2}-\ell)\Omega\varphi^{2}\lambda^{\alpha}\xi_{\alpha}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d]$}}]\,.

(5.26)

Moreover, this system is equivalent to the scalar field minimal gPDE over
\mathfrak{iso}[1]
with presymplectic structure (
4.36
), provided
\widetilde{\chi}_{\varphi}

is modified by a
d
-exact term and terms belonging to
\mathcal{E}^{\varphi}
.

It is clear that
\chi_{\varphi}=\Omega^{2\ell}\widetilde{\chi}_{\varphi}
and
H_{\varphi}\equiv\Omega^{2\ell}\widetilde{H}_{\varphi}
admit smooth extensions to the
Q
-boundary, and therefore we choose
p=2\ell
.
Unlike in the (A)dS case, the distinction between integer and half-integer
\ell
is less substantial. We therefore proceed directly to the examples.

Example 5.8
.

Let
\ell=1/2.
Then

\displaystyle\chi^{\mathcal{R}}_{\varphi}=\widehat{\xi}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d]$}}\nabla_{u}\widehat{\varphi}d\widehat{\varphi},\hskip 20.00003pt\chi_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{A}}=0\,.

(5.27)

In general, the presymplectic structure
\omega^{\mathcal{R}}\equiv d\chi^{\mathcal{R}}
induces a presymplectic structure on the space of configurations of the boundary values. In this particular case, this can be related to the Hermitian product introduced by R. Sachs in
[
65
]
.

Example 5.9
.

Let
\ell=1
. Then

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\chi^{\mathcal{A}}_{\varphi}=(-1)^{d}(\widehat{\xi}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d-1]$}}_{u}\widehat{\varphi}^{(1)}+\widehat{\xi}_{A}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d-1]$}}\nabla^{A}\widehat{\varphi})d\widehat{\varphi}\,,\\
&H^{\mathcal{A}}_{l}=\frac{1}{2}\widehat{\xi}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d]$}}\nabla_{A}\widehat{\varphi}\nabla^{A}\widehat{\varphi}+\frac{d-2}{2}\widehat{\varphi}(\frac{\widehat{\varphi}}{2}\widehat{\lambda}^{\alpha}\widehat{\xi}_{\alpha}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d-1]$}}-\widehat{\lambda}\widehat{\xi}_{u}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d-1]$}}\widehat{\varphi}^{(1)}-\widehat{\lambda}\widehat{\xi}_{A}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d-1]$}}\nabla^{A}\widehat{\varphi})\,.\end{split}

(5.28)

Introducing coordinates on the space of sections of
\sigma:T[1]\partial X\to\partial^{Q}F^{\varphi}_{\mathrm{red}}
according to

\sigma^{*}\nabla_{\alpha_{1}}\dots\nabla_{\alpha_{n}}\widehat{\varphi}^{(N)}\equiv\varphi^{(N)}_{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}}(x)
,
the intrinsic action takes the following form:

S[\varphi^{(1)},\varphi,\varphi^{A}]=-\int e^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d]$}}[\varphi^{(1)}\partial_{u}\varphi+\varphi^{A}\partial_{A}\varphi-\frac{1}{2}\varphi_{A}\varphi^{A}+\frac{2-d}{4}\varphi^{2}\lambda^{\alpha}{}_{|\alpha}+\\
\frac{d-2}{2}\varphi(\lambda_{|u}\varphi^{(1)}+\lambda_{|A}\varphi^{A})]\,,

(5.29)

where
\partial_{\alpha}\equiv e^{\mu}{}_{\alpha}\partial_{\mu}
, see Section
5.1.1
for the notation in the background sector. For
d\,{\geqslant}\,3
, working in the Bondi gauge for the background and eliminating
\varphi^{A}
, the action can be rewritten using (
5.18
) as

\displaystyle S[\varphi,\varphi^{(1)}]=-\int dud^{d-1}y\sqrt{\gamma}(\varphi^{(1)}\partial_{u}\varphi+\frac{1}{2}\partial^{i}\varphi\partial_{i}\varphi+\frac{d-2}{8(d-1)}\varphi^{2}R[\gamma])\,,

(5.30)

where
\{x^{\mu}\}=\{u,y^{i}\}
and
\gamma_{ij}\equiv e^{A}{}_{i}g_{AB}e^{B}{}_{j}.

The corresponding equations of motion are:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\partial_{u}\varphi=0\,,\qquad\partial_{u}\varphi^{(1)}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\partial_{i}(\sqrt{\gamma}\partial^{i}\varphi)-\frac{d-2}{4(d-1)}\varphi R=0\,.\end{split}

(5.31)

The theory defined by these equations is known in the literature as the magnetic Carrollian scalar. For a constant metric
\gamma
, the action (
5.30
) was obtained in
[
35
]
as the ultra-relativistic limit of the Klein–Gordon action. See also
[
66
]
, where the magnetic scalar was studied at the level of equations of motion from the holographic perspective.

Example 5.10
.

Let
\ell=3/2.
Then

\chi^{\mathcal{A}}_{\varphi}=(-1)^{d}[(\widehat{\xi}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d-1]$}}_{u}\widehat{\varphi}^{(2)}+\widehat{C}^{A}\widehat{\xi}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d-2]$}}_{uA}\widehat{\varphi}^{(1)}+\widehat{\xi}_{A}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d-1]$}}\nabla^{A}\widehat{\varphi}^{(1)}+\widehat{C}^{B}\widehat{\xi}_{AB}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d-2]$}}\nabla^{A}\widehat{\varphi})d\widehat{\varphi}+\\

(5.32)

H^{\mathcal{A}}_{l}=\widehat{\xi}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d]$}}\nabla_{A}\widehat{\varphi}^{(1)}\nabla^{A}\widehat{\varphi}+\frac{1}{2}\widehat{C}^{B}\widehat{\xi}_{B}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d-1]$}}\nabla_{A}\widehat{\varphi}\nabla^{A}\widehat{\varphi}+\frac{d-3}{4}\widehat{\lambda}^{\alpha}\widehat{\varphi}(2\widehat{\xi}_{\alpha}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d-1]$}}\widehat{\varphi}^{(1)}+\widehat{C}^{B}\widehat{\xi}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d-2]$}}_{\alpha B}\widehat{\varphi})-\\
\frac{d-1}{2}\widehat{\varphi}^{(1)}\widehat{\lambda}(\widehat{\xi}_{u}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d-1]$}}\widehat{\varphi}^{(1)}+\widehat{\xi}_{A}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d-1]$}}\nabla^{A}\widehat{\varphi})-\frac{d-3}{2}\widehat{\varphi}[(\widehat{\lambda}^{u}\widehat{\xi}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d-1]$}}_{u}+2\widehat{\lambda}\widehat{C}^{C}\widehat{\xi}_{uC}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d-2]$}})\widehat{\varphi}^{(1)}+\\
\widehat{\lambda}\widehat{\xi}_{u}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d-1]$}}\widehat{\varphi}^{(2)}+(\widehat{\lambda}^{u}\widehat{\xi}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d-1]$}}_{A}+2\widehat{\lambda}\widehat{C}^{C}\widehat{\xi}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d-2]$}}_{AC})\nabla^{A}\widehat{\varphi}+\widehat{\lambda}\widehat{\xi}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d-1]$}}_{A}\nabla^{A}\widehat{\varphi}^{(1)}]\,.

(5.33)

From these data, one can directly construct the intrinsic action with the background written in terms of a Poincaré-valued connection. For simplicity, we present it in the Bondi gauge introduced in Section
5.1.1
. Introducing coordinates on the space of sections analogously to the previous example and using (
5.18
), we have for
d\,{\geqslant}\,3
,

S=-\int dud^{d-1}y\sqrt{\gamma}[\varphi^{(2)}\partial_{u}\varphi+(\varphi^{(1)|i}-C^{i}{}_{|j}\varphi^{j})\partial_{i}\varphi+\varphi^{i}\partial_{i}\varphi^{(1)}+\varphi^{(1)}\partial_{u}\varphi^{(1)}-\varphi_{i}^{(1)}\varphi^{i}-\\
\frac{d-3}{2(d-2)}\varphi\varphi^{i}D^{j}C_{j|i}+\frac{d-3}{8}\varphi^{2}\partial_{u}(C^{i|j}C_{i|j})+\frac{d-3}{4(d-2)}R\varphi\varphi^{(1)}]\,,

(5.34)

where
D_{i}
is the Levi-Civita covariant derivative for the
d-1
dimensional metric
\gamma
. The Euler-Lagrange equations can be split into the algebraic

\displaystyle\varphi_{i}=\partial_{i}\varphi,\hskip 10.00002pt\varphi^{(1)}_{i}=\partial_{i}\varphi^{(1)}-C_{i|j}\partial^{j}\varphi-\varphi\frac{d-3}{2(d-2)}D^{j}C_{j|i}\,,

(5.35)

and the remaining ones:

\displaystyle\partial_{u}\varphi=0\,,\hskip 20.00003ptD_{i}D^{i}\varphi-\frac{d-3}{4(d-2)}R\varphi=0\,,

(5.36)

\partial_{u}\varphi^{(2)}+D_{i}\varphi^{(1)|i}-C^{i|j}D_{i}D_{j}\varphi-\frac{d-1}{2(d-2)}D^{i}\varphi\,D^{j}C_{j|i}-\\
\frac{d-3}{4}\varphi\,\partial_{u}\!\left(C^{i|j}C_{i|j}\right)-\frac{d-3}{4(d-2)}R\varphi^{(1)}=0.

(5.37)

Note that (
5.36
) simply states that
\varphi
describes a solution of the conformal Laplace equation on a
(d-1)
-dimensional space. We also note that in the case

the formulas simplify considerably.

Similarly to Example
4.11
, by putting the background in the coordinate-like gauge, we can obtain
\chi^{\mathcal{R}}
and
\chi^{\mathcal{A}}
in the general case:

Proposition 5.11
.

Restricting to the solution of the background given by
\widehat{\xi}^{\alpha}=\theta^{\alpha}
and all other background fields equal to zero, for
\ell\,{\geqslant}\,1
we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\chi^{\mathcal{R}}_{\varphi}=\frac{1}{(2\ell-1)!}\theta^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d]$}}\sum_{i=0}^{2\ell-1}C^{i}_{2\ell-1}\nabla_{u}\widehat{\varphi}^{(2\ell-1-i)}d\widehat{\varphi}^{(i)}\,,\\

Eliminating the auxiliary fields

, the solutions of this system are described by a collection of scalar fields

,

. The equations (

]

Remark 5.12
.

Note that, as follows from (

),

carries the structure of an infinite

\displaystyle\dots\to(\partial^{Q}F^{\varphi}_{\mathrm{red}})^{(i)}\to(\partial^{Q}F^{\varphi}_{\mathrm{red}})^{(i-1)}\to\dots\to(\partial^{Q}F^{\varphi}_{\mathrm{red}})^{(0)}\to\partial^{Q}F_{\mathrm{red}}\,,

5.3
Example: Maxwell

The exposition here parallels that of Section
4.4
until the introduction of the reduced model
(F^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{red}},Q,\mathcal{E}^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{red}})\to(F_{\mathrm{red}},Q)
with an overcomplete set of fibre coordinates
C
,
\nabla_{a_{1}}\dots\nabla_{a_{n}}F_{bc}
. Using the splitting of the index
\{a\}=\{\Omega,\alpha\}=\{\Omega,u,A\}
we introduce the following notation

\displaystyle J_{A}\equiv F_{\Omega A}\,,\hskip 10.00002pt\nu\equiv F_{\Omega u}\,.

(5.42)

Then we can use this decomposition of the index to explicitly solve part of the Bianchi identities (
4.58
):

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\nabla F_{uA}=\nabla_{u}J_{A}-\nabla_{A}\nu\,,\\
&\nabla F_{AB}=\nabla_{A}J_{B}-\nabla_{B}J_{A}\,,\\
\end{split}

(5.43)

passing to overcomplete coordinates

\displaystyle C\,,\hskip 10.00002pt\nabla_{(\alpha)}F_{\beta\gamma}\,,\nabla_{(\alpha)}\nu^{(N)}\,,\nabla_{(\alpha)}J_{B}^{(N)}\,,\hskip 20.00003ptN\,{\geqslant}\,0\,.

(5.44)

The ideal
\mathcal{E}^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{red}}
is generated by the
\nabla_{a}
-prolongations of

\displaystyle\begin{split}&Y_{\Omega}=(D-4)\nu-\Omega\nu^{(1)}-\Omega\nabla^{A}J_{A}\,,\quad Y_{u}=\Omega(\nabla_{u}\nu-\nabla^{A}F_{uA})\,,\\
&Y_{A}=(4-D)F_{uA}+\Omega(2\nabla_{u}J_{A}-\nabla_{A}\nu+\nabla^{B}F_{BA})\,,\end{split}

(5.45)

which can be obtained by pulling back (
4.60
) to
\mathcal{E}^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{red}}
and using the above decomposition.

The action of
Q
can be deduced by substituting the corresponding indices into

\displaystyle\begin{split}QF_{ab}&=\xi\nabla F_{ab}+\xi^{\alpha}\nabla_{\alpha}F_{ab}+\rho_{a}{}^{c}F_{cb}+\rho_{b}{}^{c}F_{ac}-2\lambda F_{ab}-\lambda\Omega\nabla F_{ab}\,,\\
QC&=(\xi-\lambda\Omega)\xi^{\alpha}F_{\Omega\alpha}+\frac{1}{2}\xi^{\alpha}\xi^{\beta}F_{\alpha\beta}\end{split}

(5.46)

and using
[Q,\nabla]=0=[Q,\nabla_{\alpha}]
.

The asymptotic boundary gPDE for
(F^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{red}},Q,\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{M}})\to(F_{\mathrm{red}},Q)
is given by

\displaystyle(\partial^{Q}F^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{red}},Q,\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{M}})\to(\partial^{Q}F_{\mathrm{red}},Q)\,.

(5.47)

Using as fiber coordinates on
\partial^{Q}F^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{red}}
functions (
5.44
), the action of
Q
on them is obtained from (
5.46
) using Proposition
5.5
.

Using (
5.43
) together with (
5.45
), one can check that the ideal
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{{\mathcal{M}}}\equiv b^{*}\mathcal{E}^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{red}}
is generated by the
\nabla_{\alpha}
-prolongations of

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\widehat{Y}_{\Omega}^{(N)}=(d-3-N)\widehat{\nu}^{(N)}-N\nabla^{A}\widehat{J}_{A}^{(N-1)}\,,\\
&\widehat{Y}_{u}^{(d-2)}=(d-2)(\nabla_{u}\widehat{\nu}^{(d-3)}-\nabla^{A}\widehat{F}^{(d-3)}_{uA})\,,\\
&\widehat{Y}_{A}^{(0)}=(3-d)\widehat{F}_{uA}\,,\\
&\widehat{Y}_{A}^{(N+1)}=(5-d+2N)\nabla_{u}\widehat{J}_{A}^{(N)}+(d-4-N)\nabla_{A}\widehat{\nu}^{(N)}+(N+1)\nabla^{B}\widehat{F}_{BA}^{(N)}\,,\end{split}

(5.48)

where

. The expressions for

and
\widehat{F}_{uA}^{(N)}
in terms of the boundary coordinates can be obtained simply by applying

are not independent and can be removed from the generating set of the ideal. An analogous feature already appeared in the AdS case, see (

In complete analogy with Proposition

Proposition 5.13

\displaystyle\widetilde{\chi}_{\mathcal{M}}=\Omega^{4-D}(-2\xi_{\Omega u}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[D-2]$}}\nu+2\xi_{uB}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[D-2]$}}J^{B}+2\xi_{\Omega A}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[D-2]$}}F_{u}{}^{A}+\xi_{AB}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[D-2]$}}F^{AB})dC

Moreover, the present system is equivalent to the gPDE with presymplectic structure described in Section

Example 5.14

Let

.

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathcal{R}}=-2(\widehat{\xi}_{u}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[2]$}}\widehat{\nu}-\widehat{\xi}_{A}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[2]$}}\widehat{F}_{u}{}^{A})d\widehat{C},\hskip 20.00003ptH^{\mathcal{R}}=0\,,\hskip 10.00002pt\chi^{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{M}}=0\,.

Appendix A
Independence of the Boundary Cocycles

Suppose that
(M,Q)
is a
Q
-manifold with a
Q
-boundary and
\Omega
is a defining function for the boundary,
\xi\equiv Q\Omega
. Let us choose a vector field
\mathfrak{d}
such that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{d}:\hskip 10.00002pt\mathrm{gh}(\mathfrak{d})=-1\,,\hskip 10.00002pt\mathfrak{d}\xi=1\,,\hskip 10.00002pt\mathfrak{d}^{2}=0\,.

(A.1)

Let
v
be a form on
M
satisfying

\displaystyle\Omega L_{Q}v=p\xi v\,,

(A.2)

then on the boundary we obtain cocycles
v^{\mathcal{R}}(\mathfrak{d})
and
v^{\mathcal{A}}(\mathfrak{d})
, whose definition (
3.13
) a priori depends on the choice of
\mathfrak{d}
, as indicated here by the notation. We now show that their cohomology classes are independent of the choice of admissible
\mathfrak{d}
.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{d}^{\prime}
be another vector field satisfying the properties (
A.1
). Then the corresponding boundary cocycles satisfy

\displaystyle v^{\mathcal{R}}(\mathfrak{d}^{\prime})=v^{\mathcal{R}}(\mathfrak{d})+L_{Q}(\dots)\,,\hskip 10.00002ptv^{\mathcal{A}}(\mathfrak{d}^{\prime})=v^{\mathcal{A}}(\mathfrak{d})+L_{Q}(\dots)\,.

(A.3)

In other words, their cohomology classes with respect to
L_{Q}
are independent of the choice of
\mathfrak{d}
.

Proof.

For a given
\mathfrak{d}
, any admissible
\mathfrak{d}^{\prime}
can be written as
\mathfrak{d}^{\prime}=\mathfrak{d}+X
, where the vector field
X
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathrm{gh}(X)=-1\,,\hskip 10.00002ptX\xi=0\,,\hskip 10.00002pt[\mathfrak{d},X]+\frac{1}{2}[X,X]=0\,.

(A.4)

Introducing an auxiliary parameter
t
, we define

\displaystyle\mathfrak{d}_{t}\equiv\mathfrak{d}+tX\,,\hskip 10.00002pt\nabla_{t}\equiv[Q,\mathfrak{d}_{t}]\,.

(A.5)

Clearly, one has

\displaystyle X=\frac{d}{dt}\mathfrak{d}_{t}\,,\hskip 10.00002pt\mathfrak{d}_{t}\xi=1,\hskip 10.00002pt\dot{\nabla}_{t}=[X,Q]\,.

(A.6)

Now consider
p!v^{\mathcal{R}}(\mathfrak{d}_{t})\equiv b^{*}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p}v
and differentiate it with respect to
t
:

p!\frac{d}{dt}v^{\mathcal{R}}(\mathfrak{d}_{t})=b^{*}\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{i}L_{[Q,X]}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p-1-i}v=L_{Q}b^{*}\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{i}L_{X}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p-1-i}v+b^{*}\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{i}L_{X}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p-1-i}L_{Q}v\,.

(A.7)

We show that the last sum vanishes. Acting on (
A.2
) with
b^{*}\sum_{i=0}^{p}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{i}L_{X}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p-i}
, we see that the right-hand side vanishes, while the left-hand side becomes

b^{*}\sum_{i=0}^{p}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{i}L_{X}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p-i}(\Omega L_{Q}v)=b^{*}\sum_{i=0}^{p}L^{i}_{\nabla_{t}}(\Omega L_{X}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p-i}L_{Q}v+(p-i)L_{X}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p-i-1}L_{Q}v)=\\
=(p+1)b^{*}\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}L^{i}_{\nabla_{t}}L_{X}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p-i-1}L_{Q}v\,.

(A.8)

Integrating (
A.7
) with respect to
t
, we obtain

\displaystyle v^{\mathcal{R}}(\mathfrak{d}^{\prime})-v^{\mathcal{R}}(\mathfrak{d})=\frac{1}{p!}L_{Q}\int_{0}^{1}dt(b^{*}\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{i}L_{X}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p-1-i}v)\,.

(A.9)

Similarly,

(-1)^{tot(v)}(p-1)!\frac{d}{dt}v^{\mathcal{A}}(\mathfrak{d}_{t})=\frac{d}{dt}b^{*}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p-1}L_{\mathfrak{d}_{t}}v=b^{*}\sum_{i=0}^{p-2}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{i}L_{[Q,X]}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p-2-i}L_{\mathfrak{d}_{t}}v+b^{*}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p-1}L_{X}v=\\
=L_{Q}(b^{*}\sum_{i=0}^{p-2}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{i}L_{X}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p-2-i}L_{\mathfrak{d}_{t}}v)+b^{*}\sum_{i=0}^{p-2}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{i}L_{X}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p-2-i}L_{Q}L_{\mathfrak{d}_{t}}v+b^{*}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p-1}L_{X}v=\\
=L_{Q}(b^{*}\sum_{i=0}^{p-2}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{i}L_{X}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p-2-i}L_{\mathfrak{d}_{t}}v)+b^{*}\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{i}L_{X}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p-1-i}v-b^{*}\sum_{i=0}^{p-2}L^{i}_{\nabla_{t}}L_{X}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p-2-i}L_{\mathfrak{d}_{t}}L_{Q}v.

(A.10)

Acting on (
A.2
) with
\frac{1}{p}b^{*}\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}L^{i}_{\nabla_{t}}L_{X}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p-1-i}L_{\mathfrak{d}_{t}}
a straightforward computation gives

\displaystyle b^{*}\sum_{i=0}^{p-2}L^{i}_{\nabla_{t}}L_{X}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p-2-i}L_{\mathfrak{d}_{t}}L_{Q}v=b^{*}\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}L^{i}_{\nabla_{t}}L_{X}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p-1-i}v\,.

(A.11)

Substituting this identity into (
A.10
) and integrating with respect to
t
we obtain

\displaystyle v^{\mathcal{A}}(\mathfrak{d}^{\prime})-v^{\mathcal{A}}(\mathfrak{d})=\frac{(-1)^{tot(v)}}{(p-1)!}L_{Q}\int_{0}^{1}dt(b^{*}\sum_{i=0}^{p-2}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{i}L_{X}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p-2-i}L_{\mathfrak{d}_{t}}v)\,.

(A.12)

∎

We then turn to the independence of the map the choice of boundary defining function
\Omega
. The corresponding change in
v^{\mathcal{R}}
and
v^{\mathcal{A}}
arises from the change in
\Omega
and
v\equiv\Omega^{p}\widetilde{v}
.

Proposition A.2
.

Let
\widetilde{v}
be a
Q
-cocycle, and
\alpha\in C^{\infty}(M)
. For

\displaystyle\Omega^{\prime}=\exp(\alpha)\Omega,\hskip 10.00002ptv^{\prime}=\exp(p\alpha)v\,,

(A.13)

we have

\displaystyle v^{\prime\mathcal{R}}-v^{\mathcal{R}}=L_{Q}(\dots),\hskip 20.00003ptv^{\prime\mathcal{A}}-v^{\mathcal{A}}=L_{Q}(\dots)\,,

(A.14)

so that the cohomology classes of
v^{\mathcal{R}}
and
v^{\mathcal{A}}
remain unchanged.

Proof.

We interpolate between the initial and the rescaled defining functions by introducing the one-parameter family

\displaystyle\Omega_{t}=\exp(\alpha t)\Omega,\hskip 10.00002ptv_{t}=\exp(p\alpha t)v\,,\hskip 10.00002pt\xi_{t}\equiv Q\Omega_{t}\,.

(A.15)

The corresponding vector fields
\mathfrak{d}_{t}
are chosen so that
\mathfrak{d}_{t}\xi_{t}=1
. It follows that

\displaystyle\dot{\mathfrak{d}}_{t}\xi_{t}=-\mathfrak{d}_{t}\dot{\xi}_{t}=-\mathfrak{d}_{t}Q(\alpha\Omega_{t})=-\nabla_{t}(\alpha\Omega_{t})\,,

(A.16)

where
\nabla_{t}=[Q,\mathfrak{d}_{t}]
. We also have

\displaystyle\Omega_{t}L_{Q}v_{t}=p\xi_{t}v_{t}\,.

(A.17)

Differentiating the definition of
v_{t}^{\mathcal{R}}
with respect to
t
, we obtain

p!\frac{d}{dt}v^{\mathcal{R}}_{t}=b^{*}\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}L^{i}_{\nabla_{t}}L_{[Q,\dot{\mathfrak{d}}_{t}]}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p-i-1}v_{t}+pb^{*}L^{p}_{\nabla_{t}}(\alpha v_{t})=\\
=L_{Q}b^{*}\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}L^{i}_{\nabla_{t}}L_{\dot{\mathfrak{d}}_{t}}L^{p-i-1}_{\nabla_{t}}v_{t}+b^{*}\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}L^{i}_{\nabla_{t}}L_{\dot{\mathfrak{d}}_{t}}L^{p-i-1}_{\nabla_{t}}L_{Q}v_{t}+pL^{p}_{\nabla_{t}}(\alpha v_{t}).

(A.18)

Now we act with
b^{*}\sum_{i=0}^{p}L^{i}_{\nabla_{t}}L_{\dot{\mathfrak{d}}_{t}}L^{p-i}_{\nabla_{t}}
on both sides of (
A.17
). The left-hand side gives

\displaystyle b^{*}\sum_{i=0}^{p}L^{i}_{\nabla_{t}}(\Omega_{t}L_{\dot{\mathfrak{d}}_{t}}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p-i}+(p-i)L_{\dot{\mathfrak{d}}_{t}}L^{p-i-1}_{\nabla_{t}})L_{Q}v_{t}=(p+1)b^{*}\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}L^{i}_{\nabla_{t}}L_{\dot{\mathfrak{d}}_{t}}L^{p-i-1}_{\nabla_{t}}L_{Q}v_{t}

(A.19)

while the right-hand side becomes

pb^{*}\sum_{i=0}^{p}L^{i}_{\nabla_{t}}L_{\dot{\mathfrak{d}}_{t}}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p-i}(\xi_{t}v_{t})=pb^{*}\sum_{i=0}^{p}L^{i}_{\nabla_{t}}(-L_{\nabla_{t}}(\alpha\Omega_{t}))L^{p-i}_{\nabla_{t}}v_{t})=\\
=-pb^{*}\sum_{i=0}^{p}\sum_{j=0}^{i}(j+1)C^{j}_{i}L^{j}_{\nabla_{t}}\alpha L^{p-j}_{\nabla_{t}}v_{t}=-pb^{*}\sum_{j=0}^{p}(\sum_{i=j}^{p}C^{j}_{i})(j+1)L^{j}_{\nabla_{t}}\alpha L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p-j}v_{t}=\\
=-pb^{*}\sum_{j=0}^{p}(j+1)C^{j+1}_{p+1}L^{j}_{\nabla_{t}}\alpha L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p-j}v_{t}=-p(p+1)b^{*}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p}(\alpha v_{t})\,.

(A.20)

Substituting the result into the expression for
\frac{d}{dt}v^{\mathcal{R}}_{t}
and integrating over
t
gives

\displaystyle v^{\prime\mathcal{R}}-v^{\mathcal{R}}=\frac{1}{p!}L_{Q}\int_{0}^{1}dt(b^{*}\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}L^{i}_{\nabla_{t}}L_{\dot{\mathfrak{d}}_{t}}L^{p-i-1}_{\nabla_{t}}v_{t})\,.

(A.21)

The proof for
v^{\mathcal{A}}
proceeds analogously. Differentiating the definition of
v_{t}^{\mathcal{A}}
with respect to
t
, we obtain

(-1)^{tot(v)}(p-1)!\frac{d}{dt}v^{\mathcal{A}}_{t}=b^{*}\frac{d}{dt}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p-1}L_{\mathfrak{d}_{t}}v_{t}=\\
=b^{*}\sum_{i=0}^{p-2}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{i}L_{[Q,\dot{\mathfrak{d}}_{t}]}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p-2-i}L_{\mathfrak{d}_{t}}v_{t}+b^{*}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p-1}(L_{\dot{\mathfrak{d}}_{t}}v_{t}+p\alpha L_{\mathfrak{d}_{t}}v_{t})=\\
=L_{Q}b^{*}\sum_{i=0}^{p-2}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{i}L_{\dot{\mathfrak{d}}_{t}}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p-2-i}L_{\mathfrak{d}_{t}}v_{t}+b^{*}\sum_{i=0}^{p-2}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{i}L_{\dot{\mathfrak{d}}_{t}}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p-2-i}L_{Q}L_{\mathfrak{d}_{t}}v_{t}+b^{*}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p-1}(L_{\dot{\mathfrak{d}}_{t}}v_{t}+p\alpha L_{\mathfrak{d}_{t}}v_{t})

(A.22)

Applying
\frac{1}{p}b^{*}\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}L^{i}_{\nabla_{t}}L_{\dot{\mathfrak{d}}_{t}}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p-1-i}L_{\mathfrak{d}_{t}}
to the relation (
A.17
), a straightforward computation gives

\displaystyle b^{*}\sum_{i=0}^{p-2}L^{i}_{\nabla_{t}}L_{\dot{\mathfrak{d}}_{t}}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p-2-i}L_{\mathfrak{d}_{t}}L_{Q}v_{t}=b^{*}\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}L^{i}_{\nabla_{t}}L_{\dot{\mathfrak{d}}_{t}}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p-1-i}v_{t}+pb^{*}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p-1}(\alpha L_{\mathfrak{d}_{t}}v_{t})\,.

(A.23)

Substituting this identity into the previous expression, using

, and integrating with respect to

\displaystyle v^{\prime\mathcal{A}}-v^{\mathcal{A}}=\frac{(-1)^{tot(v)}}{(p-1)!}L_{Q}\int_{0}^{1}dt(b^{*}\sum_{i=0}^{p-2}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{i}L_{\dot{\mathfrak{d}}_{t}}L_{\nabla_{t}}^{p-2-i}L_{\mathfrak{d}_{t}}v_{t})\,.

(A.24)

Appendix B
Properties of
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{A}
maps

Lemma B.1
.

The maps
\mathcal{R},\mathcal{A}
both commute with
L_{Q}
and
d
.

Proof.

Let
\widetilde{\beta}\in\bigwedge^{\bullet}{}_{\mathrm{fop}}(Int(F))
and
\widetilde{\alpha}=L_{Q}\widetilde{\beta}
. Choose
p
such that
\beta=\Omega^{p-1}\widetilde{\beta}
is smooth on the boundary. Let us calculate
\mathcal{R}(L_{Q}\widetilde{\beta})=\mathcal{R}(\widetilde{\alpha})
:

\alpha^{\mathcal{R}}=\frac{1}{p!}b^{*}L^{p}_{\nabla}\Omega^{p}L_{Q}\widetilde{\beta}=\frac{1}{p!}b^{*}(L_{Q}(L^{p}_{\nabla}\Omega^{p}\widetilde{\beta})-p\xi L^{p}_{\nabla}\Omega^{p-1}\widetilde{\beta})=\\
=L_{Q}b^{*}\frac{1}{p!}((L^{p}_{\nabla}\Omega^{p}\widetilde{\beta}))=L_{Q}\beta^{\mathcal{R}}

(B.1)

and for
\mathcal{A}(\widetilde{\alpha})
:

\alpha^{\mathcal{A}}=\frac{(-1)^{tot(\widetilde{\beta})+1}}{(p-1)!}b^{*}L^{p-1}_{\nabla}\Omega^{p}L_{\mathfrak{d}}L_{Q}\widetilde{\beta}=\frac{(-1)^{tot(\widetilde{\beta})+1}}{(p-1)!}b^{*}(L^{p-1}_{\nabla}\Omega^{p}L_{\nabla}\widetilde{\beta}-L^{p-1}_{\nabla}\Omega^{p}L_{Q}L_{\mathfrak{d}}\widetilde{\beta})=\\
=\frac{(-1)^{tot(\widetilde{\beta})+1}}{(p-1)!}b^{*}\Big[L^{p}_{\nabla}\Omega^{p}\widetilde{\beta}-pL^{p-1}_{\nabla}\Omega^{p-1}\widetilde{\beta}-L_{Q}L^{p-1}_{\nabla}\Omega^{p}L_{\mathfrak{d}}\widetilde{\beta}+pL^{p-1}_{\nabla}\xi\Omega^{p-1}L_{\mathfrak{d}}\widetilde{\beta}\Big]=\\
=\frac{(-1)^{tot(\widetilde{\beta})+1}}{(p-1)!}b^{*}\Big[\Omega L_{\nabla}^{p}\beta-L_{Q}L^{p-1}_{\nabla}\Omega^{p}L_{\mathfrak{d}}\widetilde{\beta}+p\xi L^{p-1}_{\nabla}L_{\mathfrak{d}}\beta\Big]=\\
=L_{Q}\frac{(-1)^{tot(\widetilde{\beta})+1}}{(p-1)!}b^{*}(-L^{p-1}_{\nabla}\Omega^{p}L_{\mathfrak{d}}\widetilde{\beta})=L_{Q}\frac{(-1)^{tot(\widetilde{\beta})}}{(p-1)!}b^{*}(L^{p-1}_{\nabla}\Omega^{p}L_{\mathfrak{d}}\widetilde{\beta})=L_{Q}\beta^{\mathcal{A}}

(B.2)

Therefore, we have shown that
\mathcal{R}(L_{Q}\widetilde{\beta})=L_{Q}\mathcal{R}(\widetilde{\beta})
and
\mathcal{A}(L_{Q}\widetilde{\beta})=L_{Q}\mathcal{A}(\widetilde{\beta})

To show that
\mathcal{R},\mathcal{A}
intertwine the de Rham differential we consider
\widetilde{\alpha}=d\widetilde{\beta}
and choosing
p
such that
\beta=\Omega^{p-1}\widetilde{\beta}
is smooth on the boundary, we compute:

\begin{gathered}\alpha^{\mathcal{R}}=b^{*}\frac{1}{p!}L^{p}_{\nabla}\Omega^{p}d\widetilde{\beta}=\frac{1}{p!}b^{*}(dL^{p}_{\nabla}\Omega^{p}\widetilde{\beta}-pL^{p}_{\nabla}d(\Omega)\beta)=d\beta^{\mathcal{R}}\end{gathered}

(B.3)

and

\alpha^{\mathcal{A}}=\frac{(-1)^{tot(\widetilde{\beta})+1}}{(p-1)!}b^{*}L^{p-1}_{\nabla}\Omega^{p}L_{\mathfrak{d}}d\widetilde{\beta}=\\
=\frac{(-1)^{tot(\widetilde{\beta})+1}}{(p-1)!}b^{*}(-dL^{p-1}_{\nabla}\Omega^{p}L_{\mathfrak{d}}d\widetilde{\beta}+p(d\Omega)L^{p-1}_{\nabla}L_{\mathfrak{d}}\beta)=d\beta^{\mathcal{A}}

(B.4)

proving that
\mathcal{R}(d\widetilde{\beta})=d\mathcal{R}(\widetilde{\beta})
and
\mathcal{A}(d\widetilde{\beta})=d\mathcal{A}(\widetilde{\beta})
.
∎

Lemma B.2
.

If
\mathcal{I}
is extendable the maps
\mathcal{R},\mathcal{A}
can be restricted to

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{R}:\bigwedge{}^{\bullet}_{\mathrm{fop}}/\mathcal{I}|_{\mathrm{fop}}\xrightarrow{}\bigwedge^{\bullet}(\partial^{Q}F)/\mathcal{I}_{\partial}\\
\mathcal{A}:\bigwedge{}^{\bullet}_{\mathrm{fop}}/\mathcal{I}|_{\mathrm{fop}}\xrightarrow{}\bigwedge^{\bullet}(\partial^{Q}F)/\mathcal{I}_{\partial}\end{gathered}

(B.5)

Proof.

One has to show that
\mathcal{R},\mathcal{A}
are well defined on equivalence classes. Suppose we pick an equivalence class
[\widetilde{\alpha}]
and two representatives
\widetilde{\alpha},\widetilde{\alpha}^{\prime}\in[\widetilde{\alpha}]
, i.e.
\widetilde{\alpha}-\widetilde{\alpha}^{\prime}=\widetilde{\beta}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{fop}}
. Because the maps are linear over
\widetilde{\alpha}
we only have to show that they map elements of
\mathcal{I}|_{\mathrm{fop}}
to
\mathcal{I}_{\partial}
. We have

\beta^{\mathcal{R}}=b^{*}(\frac{1}{(p+1)!}L^{p+1}_{\nabla}\Omega^{p+1}\widetilde{\beta})

(B.6)

\beta^{\mathcal{A}}=(-1)^{tot(\widetilde{\beta})}b^{*}(\frac{1}{p!}L^{p}_{\nabla}\Omega^{p+1}L_{\mathfrak{d}}\widetilde{\beta}))

(B.7)

Because
\Omega^{p}\widetilde{\beta}
is smooth on the boundary and using that
L_{Q},L_{\mathfrak{d}}
and consequently
L_{\nabla}
preserve
\mathcal{I}
we conclude that the claim is true.
∎

Proof of Proposition

Proof.

By definition (
3.12
),

v^{(p)}=L_{\nabla}^{p}v=L_{\nabla}^{p}(\Omega^{p}\widetilde{v})=\sum_{i=0}^{p}C^{i}_{p}\nabla^{i}(\Omega^{p})L_{\nabla}^{p-i}\widetilde{v}=p!\widetilde{v}+\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}C^{i}_{p}\frac{p!}{(p-i)!}\Omega^{p-i}L_{\nabla}^{p-i-1}L_{Q}L_{\mathfrak{d}}\widetilde{v}\,,

(C.1)

where in the last equality we used
\nabla=[Q,\mathfrak{d}]
and
L_{Q}\widetilde{v}=0
. Next, we move
L_{Q}
to the left and rewrite
\widetilde{v}=\Omega^{-p}v
,
\bar{v}\equiv L_{\mathfrak{d}}v
:

v^{(p)}=p!\widetilde{v}+L_{Q}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}C^{i}_{p}\frac{p!}{(p-i)!}\Omega^{p-i}L_{\nabla}^{p-i-1}(\Omega^{-p}\bar{v})\right)-\\
\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}C^{i}_{p}\frac{p!}{(p-i)!}(p-i)\Omega^{p-i-1}(Q\Omega)L_{\nabla}^{p-i-1}(\Omega^{-p}\bar{v})\equiv p!\widetilde{v}+L_{Q}A+B\,,

(C.2)

where we have introduced the notation
A
and
B
for the corresponding sums.

Let us compute
A
, again using the Leibniz rule and
\nabla^{j}\Omega^{-p}=(-1)^{j}\frac{(p+j-1)!}{(p-1)!}\Omega^{-p-j}
:

\displaystyle A=\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}\sum_{j=0}^{p-i-1}C^{i}_{p}C^{j}_{p-i-1}\frac{p!}{(p-i)!}(-1)^{j}\frac{(p+j-1)!}{(p-1)!}\Omega^{-i-j}\bar{v}^{(p-i-j-1)}\,.

(C.3)

Making the change of summation variable
s=i+j
and interchanging the order of summation, we arrive at

\displaystyle A=\sum_{s=0}^{p-1}(\sum_{i=0}^{s}C^{i}_{p}C^{s-i}_{p-i-1}\frac{p}{(p-i)!}(-1)^{s-i}(p+s-i-1)!)\Omega^{-s}\bar{v}^{(p-s-1)}\equiv\sum_{s=0}^{p-1}a_{p,s}\Omega^{-s}\bar{v}^{(p-s-1)}\,.

(C.4)

Let us compute
a_{p,s}.
Clearly,
a_{p,0}=1
. For
s>0,
factoring out the terms independent of the summation index and rewriting the factorials in terms of binomial coefficients, we get

\displaystyle a_{p,s}=p(-1)^{s}(s-1)!\sum_{i=0}^{s}(-1)^{i}C^{i}_{p}C^{s-i}_{p-i-1}C^{p-i}_{p+s-i-1},.

(C.5)

We now use the identities
C^{i}_{p}=C^{p-i}_{p}
,
C_{p-1-i}^{s-i}=C^{p-1-s}_{p-1-i}
together with
C^{p-i}_{p}=\frac{p}{p-i}C^{p-i-1}_{p-1}
:

\displaystyle a_{p,s}=p(-1)^{s}(s-1)!\sum_{i=0}^{s}(-1)^{i}\frac{p}{p-i}C^{p-i-1}_{p-1}C^{p-1-s}_{p-1-i}C^{p-i}_{p+s-i-1}\,.

(C.6)

Using the standard identity for binomial coefficients,

C^{p-i-1}_{p-1}C^{p-1-s}_{p-1-i}=C^{s}_{p-1}C^{i}_{s}
,
and factoring out the terms independent of
i
, we obtain

\displaystyle a_{p,s}=p^{2}(-1)^{s}(s-1)!C^{s}_{p-1}\sum_{i=0}^{s}(-1)^{i}\frac{1}{p-i}C^{i}_{s}C^{p-i}_{p+s-i-1}\,.

(C.7)

To compute this sum, note that
C^{p-i}_{p+s-i-1}
is a polynomial in
i
of degree
s-1\,{\geqslant}\,0

s=1
it is equal to
1
, while for
s>1
we expand it around the point
p-i

C^{p-i}_{p+s-i-1}=1+(p-i)P(i)
,
where
P(i)

0\,{\leqslant}\,r\,{\leqslant}\,s-1

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{s}(-1)^{i}C^{i}_{s}i^{r}=0\,.

(C.8)

Therefore, the term involving
P(i)
does not contribute to the sum, and we may rewrite (

) as

\displaystyle a_{p,s}=p^{2}(-1)^{s}(s-1)!C^{s}_{p-1}\sum_{i=0}^{s}(-1)^{i}C^{i}_{s}\frac{1}{p-i}\,.

(C.9)

Let us compute the sum:

\sum_{i=0}^{s}(-1)^{i}C^{i}_{s}\frac{1}{p-i}=\sum_{i=0}^{s}(-1)^{i}C^{i}_{s}\int_{0}^{1}t^{p-i-1}dt=\int_{0}^{1}t^{p-1}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{s}(-1)^{i}C^{i}_{s}t^{-i}\right)dt=\\
=\int_{0}^{1}t^{p-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{t}\right)^{s}dt=(-1)^{s}\int_{0}^{1}t^{p-1-s}(t-1)^{s}dt=(-1)^{s}\frac{(p-s-1)!s!}{p!}\,,

(C.10)

where in the last equality we used the standard expression for the Euler beta function. Substituting this into (
C.9
), we obtain

\displaystyle a_{p,s}=p(s-1)!\,,\hskip 20.00003pts\,{\geqslant}\,1\,.

(C.11)

Using this in (
C.4
), we obtain the desired expression for
A
.
We now compute

\displaystyle B\equiv-\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}C^{i}_{p}\frac{p!}{(p-i)!}(p-i)\Omega^{p-i-1}(Q\Omega)L_{\nabla}^{p-i-1}(\Omega^{-p}\bar{v})\,.

(C.12)

We see that compared to the previous case, the only difference is the factor
-(p-i)\Omega^{-1}Q\Omega
. Therefore,

B=-\sum_{s=0}^{p-1}(\sum_{i=0}^{s}C^{i}_{p}C^{s-i}_{p-i-1}\frac{p}{(p-i)!}(-1)^{s-i}(p+s-i-1)!(p-i))\Omega^{-s-1}Q\Omega\bar{v}^{(p-s-1)}\equiv\\
\equiv\sum_{s=0}^{p-1}b_{p,s}\Omega^{-s-1}Q\Omega\bar{v}^{(p-s-1)}\,.

(C.13)

Repeating the argument used for
a_{p,s}
, we see that only the term with
s=0
contributes, and hence
b_{p,s}=-p\delta_{s,0}
. Thus,

\displaystyle B=-p\Omega^{-1}Q\Omega\bar{v}^{(p-1)}\,.

(C.14)

The lemma follows by substituting
A
and
B
into (
C.2
).

Appendix E
Derivation of the presymplectic potentials

Although in the main text we simply present the symplectic potential on
F^{\varphi}_{\mathrm{red}}
and check that it is equivalent to the standard one, it is useful to show how it arises from the standard presymplectic potential on
(\widetilde{F}^{\varphi},Q)\to(\mathfrak{g}_{\Lambda}[1],\mathrm{d}_{\mathfrak{g}})
:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\chi}^{\prime}_{\varphi}=\widetilde{\xi}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d]$}}_{a}\widetilde{\nabla}^{a}\widetilde{\varphi}d\widetilde{\varphi}\,,\hskip 10.00002pt\widetilde{H}^{\prime}_{\varphi}=\frac{(-1)^{d+1}}{2}\widetilde{\xi}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d+1]$}}(m^{2}\widetilde{\varphi}^{2}+\widetilde{\nabla}_{a}\widetilde{\varphi}\widetilde{\nabla}^{a}\widetilde{\varphi})\,.

(E.1)

Using
\xi^{a}=\Omega\widetilde{\xi}^{a}
and (
4.28
) we can uplift
\widetilde{\chi}^{\prime}_{\varphi}
and
\widetilde{H}^{\prime}_{\varphi}
to
Int(F^{\varphi})
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\widetilde{\chi}_{\varphi}^{\prime}=\Omega^{-d-w}\xi^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d]$}}_{a}(\Omega\nabla^{a}\varphi-w(n^{a}\varphi))d(\Omega^{-w}\varphi)\,,\\
&\widetilde{H}_{\varphi}^{\prime}=\frac{(-1)^{d+1}}{2}\Omega^{-2w-d-1}\xi^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d+1]$}}(\Omega^{2}\nabla_{a}\varphi\nabla^{a}\varphi-2w\Omega\varphi n^{a}\nabla_{a}\varphi+(m^{2}+n_{a}n^{a}w^{2})\varphi^{2})\,.\end{split}

(E.2)

It turns out to be convenient to choose a different representative within the equivalence class of the presymplectic potential. To see this, note that the term

\displaystyle\begin{split}&-w(\Omega^{-d-w}\xi^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d]$}}_{a}(n^{a}\varphi)d(\Omega^{-w}\varphi)=-\frac{w}{2}\Omega^{-d}\xi_{a}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d]$}}n^{a}d(\Omega^{-2w}\varphi^{2})=d\alpha+\beta\,,\quad\text{where
}\\
&\alpha=(-1)^{d+1}\frac{w}{2}\Omega^{-d-2w}\xi_{a}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d]$}}n^{a}\varphi^{2}\,,\qquad\beta=(-1)^{d}\frac{w}{2}\varphi^{2}\Omega^{-2w}d(\Omega^{-d}\xi_{a}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d]$}}n^{a})\in\mathcal{I}_{Int(F)}\,.\end{split}

(E.3)

We exploit the freedom in the definition of the presymplectic potential and define

\displaystyle\widetilde{\chi}_{\varphi}\equiv\widetilde{\chi}_{\varphi}^{\prime}-d\alpha-\beta=\Omega^{-d-w+1}\xi^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d]$}}_{a}\nabla^{a}\varphi d(\Omega^{-w}\varphi)\,.

(E.4)

Under this shift, the covariant Hamiltonian also changes:

\widetilde{H}_{\varphi}=\widetilde{H}_{\varphi}^{\prime}+i_{Q}\beta=\frac{(-1)^{d+1}}{2}\Omega^{-2w-d-1}[\xi^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d+1]$}}(\Omega^{2}\nabla_{a}\varphi\nabla^{a}\varphi-2w\Omega\varphi n^{a}\nabla_{a}\varphi+\\
+(m^{2}+n_{a}n^{a}w(d+w))\varphi^{2})+w\Omega\varphi^{2}(\xi^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d+1]$}}\tau-\Omega\lambda^{a}\xi_{a}^{\scalebox{0.78}{$\scriptstyle[d]$}})]\,.

(E.5)

The pullback of
\widetilde{\chi}_{\varphi}
and
\widetilde{H}_{\varphi}
to
Int(F^{\varphi}_{\mathrm{red}})
completes the proof of the statement.
