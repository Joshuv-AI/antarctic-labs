Dysplasia epiphysealis hemimelic in the knee: a case report
Authors: Mengyao Wang, Jin Cao, Huanye Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDysplasia epiphysealis hemimelica (DEH) is a rare congenital bone dysplasia characterized by overgrowth of the epiphyseal cartilage on one side, with its etiology remaining unclear. The condition predominantly affects weight-bearing joints of the lower limbs, typically presenting with localized pain due to a rapidly growing mass around the joint. In severe cases, symptoms such as limb length discrepancy and limb deformity may occur. Early diagnosis is challenging, and delayed diagnosis and treatment can easily lead to limb deformity and functional impairment.Case presentationThis paper reports the case of a 9-year-old male patient who was initially misdiagnosed with osteochondritis dissecans of the medial femoral condyle at a community hospital. After two months of immobilization and rest without improvement in pain, he was referred to our hospital, where the diagnosis was revised to DEH. We performed an open surgical excision of the lesion, and postoperative histopathological examination confirmed DEH. At 3-month follow-up, the patient had resumed normal walking. At 6-month follow-up showed complete resolution of pain and restoration of normal knee joint range of motion. At 2-year follow-up, there were no signs of recurrence, and knee function had recovered well.ConclusionDEH is a rare condition that is prone to misdiagnosis. Establishing a correct diagnosis early requires a combination of clinical presentation, imaging studies and histopathological findings. This case suggests that DEH should be considered in pediatric knee lesions mimicking osteochondritis dissecans, and that complete excision can achieve good outcomes in selected patients.

Introduction

Dysplasia Epiphysealis Hemimelica (DEH), also known as Trevor's disease, is a rare benign skeletal disorder primarily affecting the epiphyses of long bones, leading to asymmetric overgrowth (1). It is characterized by abnormal growth of one or multiple epiphyses, predominantly on one side and often involving the medial aspect (2). The condition typically manifests during childhood, with an estimated incidence of 1 in a million, and the prevalence is three times more frequently in males than in females (3). The foot and ankle are the most commonly affected sites, although the knee, hip, spine, and upper limbs may also be involved (4). Rare presentations in the upper extremity, including the shoulder, have been documented even in adult patients, further underscoring the wide anatomical spectrum of the disease (5). Common clinical symptoms include a progressively enlarging mass on the affected limb, leading to pain, deformity, and functional impairment. Owing to its rarity, DEH is frequently misdiagnosed, which may delay appropriate treatment. Reports of DEH involving the knee in children are scarce, and most cases are identified only after substantial deformity has developed. The novelty of this case details the early diagnosis and management of a pediatric patient with DEH that initially mimicked osteochondritis dissecans, highlighting the diagnostic challenge and the favorable outcome achieved with early complete excision without cartilage restoration for a small, contained defect. Informed consent was obtained from the patient's guardian for this case report.

Case presentation

A 9-year-old male patient was admitted due to right knee pain and limited mobility persisting for over two months. The symptoms began with mild right knee pain following a fall on level ground, he could walk independently and did not seek medical attention. Over the ensuing two months, the pain persisted during walking. The patient visited a community hospital where an x-ray suggested a diagnosis of “osteochondritis dissecans of the right medial femoral condyle”. He was advised to rest and avoid strenuous activities but was not referred for further investigation or treatment. Subsequently, the right knee pain worsened, and he gradually developed significant limitation in squatting. The patient requested a referral to our hospital for further evaluation and management.

Physical examination revealed no obvious limb deformity, significant swelling, or skin lesions. There was marked tenderness over the medial aspect of the right knee. The patient was unable to squat because of pain (Figure 1). Active knee range of motion was restricted to flexion was 50° with a 0° extension, limited by pain on squatting. x-rays indicated abnormal calcification with local epiphyseal bone destruction in the right medial femoral condyle (Figure 2), a finding more clearly delineated on CT (Figure 3). MRI demonstrated a mass with low-to-intermediate heterogeneous signal intensity on T1-weighted images and intermediate-to-high signal intensity on T2-weighted images, consistent with cartilaginous components, interspersed with low-signal areas suggesting ossification nuclei (Figure 4). The patient had no family history of genetic disorders and denied any history of fever. Blood test results were within normal limits.

Following preoperative evaluation and informed discussion with the parents, an excisional biopsy was performed. A longitudinal incision approximately 10 cm in length was made over the medial aspect of the right knee. After layer-by-layer dissection and anterior retraction of the vastus medialis muscle, the medial femoral condyle was exposed. An abnormal bony proliferation of the medial femoral condyle presented as an irregular, cauliflower-like protrusion of the articular surface with well-defined margins from the surrounding tissue (Figure 5). The lesion was detached along its margins using a periosteal elevator, and the resulting defect provided direct access to the underlying cancellous bone. Under C-arm fluoroscopic guidance, multiple hardened nuclei within the cancellous bone were palpated and meticulously curetted. Specifically, after detachment of the main lesion, a high-speed round burr was used under continuous irrigation to remove the sclerotic nuclei, followed by copious pulsed lavage to minimize potential residual chondrocyte contamination. Care was taken to avoid injury to the distal femoral physis and to preserve as much of the healthy articular cartilage surface as possible. The resultant full-thickness cartilage defect measured 8 mm × 12 mm, with stable surrounding cartilage margins and an intact subchondral bone plate. Given that the defect was located in a non-weight-bearing area and was of small size, no cartilage reconstruction or microfracture repair was performed. Postoperative histopathological examination revealed hyaline cartilage with evidence of endochondral ossification forming trabecular bone (Figure 6).

Postoperatively, the patient was kept non-weight-bearing for 4 weeks with a hinged knee brace locked in extension, followed by a gradual progression to full weight-bearing and range-of-motion exercises. At 3 months, independent ambulation without aids was achieved, and knee range of motion reached 0°–130° (contralateral side, 0°–135°). At the two-year follow-up, there was no clinical or imaging evidence of recurrence. Physical examination demonstrated a pain-free knee (visual analog scale score, 0/10), full and symmetrical squatting ability, a normal gait pattern, and preserved mechanical axis alignment without angular deformity. No significant limb length discrepancy (Figure 7).

At the two-year follow-up, there were no significant signs of recurrence, both knees exhibit normal function and appearance.

Discussion

The pathogenesis of dysplasia epiphysealis hemimelica (DEH) is not fully understood (6, 7). Trevor proposed that it results from a congenital defect in the epiphyseal germinal cells, leading to persistent hyperplasia of the articular cartilage (8). To date, no associated genetic or environmental predisposing factors have been identified (3). The molecular mechanisms driving asymmetric epiphyseal overgrowth in DEH remain elusive, dysregulation of cartilage development pathways has been hypothesized but no causative somatic or germline mutations have been identified in isolated cases. Future studies employing next-generation sequencing of excised tissue and multicenter long-term outcome registries are warranted to clarify these pathogenic mechanisms.

The primary clinical manifestation of DEH is a gradually enlarging localized mass; the condition may even be completely asymptomatic, depending on the lesion's location and its impact on surrounding tissues. Although typically slow-growing and progressive, patients often present due to secondary joint deformity. Reports indicate that severe cases can lead to significant limb deformity and osteoarthritis due to continuous mass enlargement (9). Early radiographic imaging is characteristic (3, 7, 10), showing scattered stippled or irregular calcifications that may gradually coalesce with the native epiphysis, forming a homogeneous mass and causing asymmetric epiphyseal enlargement. Preoperative x-ray, CT, and MRI findings in our case confirmed this. Intraoperatively, the lesion contained multiple relatively independent ossification centers, consistent with the imaging studies.

The patient was initially misdiagnosed with osteochondritis dissecans at a community hospital. This underscores that DEH is a rare condition often overlooked in its early stages. For differential diagnosis, DEH must be distinguished from osteochondritis dissecans, osteochondroma, and synovial chondromatosis (2, 8, 10). Unlike osteochondritis dissecans, our patient had only a minor trauma history, and MRI showed the lesion originated from the epiphysis rather than the cartilage. Notably, CT in this case demonstrated corticomedullary continuity between the lesion and the parent bone, a hallmark that distinguishes DEH from synovial-origin lesions and from osteochondritis dissecans. Both DEH and osteochondroma maintain structural continuity with the underlying bone. However, the epiphyseal location and irregular, cauliflower-like contour seen here are characteristic of DEH rather than osteochondroma. Ultimately, histopathological examination provides the most reliable diagnostic confirmation.

According to Azouz et al. (11), DEH can be classified into three types. Localized form, involving a single lower limb joint. Classical form, involving characteristic hemimelic distribution of the disease is seen, inmore than one area, in a single lower extremity particularly the talus, the distal femoral and the distal tibial epiphyses. Generalized or severe form, The lower extremity is involved from the pelvis to the foot or ankle. Within the pelvis, the trigeminal cartilage may be involved, potentially affecting the femoral head, pubic symphysis, or acetabulum. Due to the rarity of DEH, literature primarily consists of case reports, and clinical utility of the classification requires further validation.

There is no established standard treatment protocol for DEH. Management is individualized, with appropriate strategies tailored to each case (6–8). The importance of early diagnosis and timely intervention is consistently emphasized. Surgical intervention before the development of secondary limb deformity can restore normal skeletal growth and reduce the complexity of later reconstructive procedures. Current surgical strategies for DEH remain controversial. While recent literature has reported arthroscopic resection with advantages such as minimal invasiveness and faster recovery (12, 13). We were concerned that achieving a thorough excision might be technically challenging arthroscopically, and that any residual lesion could contribute to recurrence. Given that this experience is drawn from a single case, the choice between open and arthroscopic techniques should be individualized to the lesion's characteristics and the surgeon's preference, rather than implying superiority of one approach over the other. Regardless of the surgical approach, complete excision of the lesion is recommended as it can effectively alleviate mechanical symptoms, improve joint function, and reduce the risk of local recurrence.

In the present case, the resultant full-thickness cartilage defect measured 8 mm × 12 mm, given that the defect was located in a non-weight-bearing area and was of small size, no cartilage reconstruction or microfracture repair was performed. This decision was based on several considerations. First, the defect was small and fully contained by healthy cartilage borders, a configuration known to favor spontaneous repair through fibrocartilaginous coverage (14). Second, the patient was a young child with robust intrinsic healing capacity, yield satisfactory functional outcomes without formal resurfacing (14). Nevertheless, alternative cartilage restoration techniques merit consideration in other settings. Microfracture is widely employed as a first-line procedure for small, isolated full-thickness defects (15). Osteochondral autograft transplantation may be indicated for larger or more weight-bearing lesions requiring structural restoration (16).

In our case, early diagnosis and timely surgical treatment were followed by a satisfactory recovery, with no evidence of recurrence or significant secondary deformity at the 2-year follow-up (Figure 7). While the result is encouraging, continued long-term surveillance remains necessary.

Conclusion

This case highlights DEH can closely mimic osteochondritis dissecans in the pediatric knee, warranting a high index of suspicion. DEH is a rare and frequently misdiagnosed condition, and early recognition with accurate diagnosis remains essential to avoid treatment delay (17). In carefully selected symptomatic patients, early complete surgical excision may help prevent progressive joint deformity and secondary osteoarthritis. However, management should be individualized according to lesion location, symptom severity, degree of joint involvement, and the risk of deformity. Future studies, including molecular analyses of excised tissue and multicenter long-term outcome registries, are warranted to elucidate the mechanisms driving epiphyseal overgrowth and to establish evidence-based, individualized treatment algorithms.
