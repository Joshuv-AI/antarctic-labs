Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

This project aims at developing and demonstrating successful implementation of breakthrough
approaches in real-time data visualization as well as real-time distributed DER control and
optimization to provide ample benefits to both utilities and end users. The National Renewable
Energy Laboratory (NREL), Holy Cross Energy (HCE), National Rural Electric Cooperative
Association (NRECA) and Survalent are collaborating to enable Cooperative and Municipal
utilities to fully leverage DERs as part of their strategies for providing safe, reliable, and
affordable electric services to their customers and help meet DOE Grid modernization goal of
achieving at least 10% active devices to provide grid flexibility by 2035. This project will use
novel real-time control algorithms and approaches for distributed control recently developed
under DOE-funded projects, using the date from the Survalent’s basic SCADA engine, GIS and
AMI engines deployed at HCE combined with NRECA’s globally-used MultiSpeak® software
interoperability specification for seamless and real-time communications between electric utility
enterprise software to embrace DER as part of their strategies for providing safe, reliable and
affordable electric service to their customers.

Summary of Research Results:

Task 1. Identify Candidate Feeders

Pursuant to a non-disclosure agreement, HCE will provide NREL with controlled access to HCE
system data and field measurements, including GIS, SCADA, AMI, engineering models
(Milsoft) and field control device settings as necessary. Based on this data, NREL and HCE will
identify 2-3 candidate HCE distribution feeders for further analysis. To the extent possible, these
feeders will correspond to the candidate field deployment (Task 9) sites and will be used for
modeling the benefits (Task 3- Task 5) and HIL evaluation (Task 6 to Task 8).

Distribution Grid Model

NREL worked with HCE and selected the Basalt substation as the test system in this project.
HCE shared the Milsoft model of the entire substation, and the NREL team converted the model
into OpenDSS format. Historical AMI data was also provided and used to model time-series load
profiles. The NREL team modeled a high penetration of PV scenario and added the models into
the distribution grid model. Figure 1 shows the topology of the distribution feeder selected for
study. This feeder has 4039 buses and 1137 loads. Among the 1137 loads, we identified there
were 163 rural all electric loads. For each of these 163 all electric loads, we modeled it to be
composed of a base load that is not controllable and multiple distributed energy resources
(DERs) that are fully controllable. The DERs include HVAC load, electric water heater load,
rooftop PV, battery, and electric vehicle. Figure 2 (a)-(e) show the examples of the behaviors of
these DERs.

Task 2. Develop Data Interoperability Plan

NREL, HCE, Survalent and NRECA will jointly develop a plan for utilizing the MultiSpeak
Specification to enable real-time integrated data exchange across multiple tools and platforms to
be used in this project. In particular, NRECA will work with NREL and HCE to identify
candidate DERs to be used in this project and ensure they are compatible with MultiSpeak.
NRECA will conduct the development of the MultiSpeak Specification and connectivity as
needed to support the types DERs that

NREL and/or HCE would like to be used in this project. Survalent will identify the SCADA and
AMI points necessary to perform the control and will stream the data through the DMS.

Overall Control Architecture

A hierarchical control architecture is developed to use grid-edge DERs to provide grid services.
As shown in Figure 3, the control architecture includes two layers: the top layer is a coordinator
that coordinates local DERs to achieve system-level benefits based on real-time measurements
received from the advanced distribution management system (ADMS); the bottom layer includes
multiple local DER controllers that solve the optimal power setpoints for controlled DERs. The
coordinator solves a system-level optimization problem, and broadcasts reference signals to
individual local DER controllers. Based on the reference signal, each local controller will
determine the actual operation status for individual DERs. Such two-layer control can enable
voltage regulation, peak load management, and more services.

Task 3. Develop and Validate Distribution Feeder Model

Using a software simulation tool (like OpenDSS or GridLab-D), NREL will model the candidate
HCE distribution feeders identified in Task 1 along with any existing DERs on those feeders
(e.g. solar PV, energy storage, electric vehicles and flexible loads such as HV AC, lighting,
water heaters). NREL will perform multiple time series simulation to obtain and visualize the
baseline performance of HCE feeders in terms of voltage; power quality and other measures as
may be determined by NREL and HCE. The simulation results will be compared with HCE's
Survalent SCADA measurements and other field AMI measurements to validate model accuracy.

Software Model in OpenDSS

Figure 1 and Figure 2 have shown the simulation results of the OpenDSS model, which is used to
validate the control performance at first.

Task 4. Identify Potential Use Cases

Based on the time-series simulation results obtained from Task 3, and a high DER penetration
forecast for the next 5-10 years, NREL will perform hypothetical simulation to identify the
potential control needs and issues. NREL and HCE will identify potential locations on the HCE
system where advanced distributed control applications of DERs, including but not limited to
conservation voltage reduction (CVR), volt-var optimization (VVO), and real-time demand
response (DR), could provide benefits to HCE.

NREL will repeat the modeling conducted in Task 3, this time with multiple scenarios
representing higher penetration levels of DER and incorporating the distributed control
algorithms that NREL has developed under its ARPA-E NODES project to control DERs.
Results from this simulation will be compared by NREL and HCE to those from Task 3 to
identify and analyze the potential impact of these advanced controls on improving HCE grid
performance. NREL and HCE will identify a most feasible use case among the candidate use
cases for HIL and field deployments.

Task 5. Conduct Cost-Benefit Analysis

Based on the simulation results obtained from Task 4, NREL will work with HCE to perform
cost benefit analysis and identify the economic value of using advanced distributed control to
manage DERs on the HCE system. NREL and HCE will jointly develop a business case for one
specific functionality (CVR, VVO, or DR, etc.) that has the highest potential value, using HCE
rate and tariff data, and available equipment cost data (such as capital cost, O&M cost) to help
NREL calculate the economic value.

Cost-Benefit Analysis

With autonomous and distributed control over all DER types, we can achieve the following
benefits for distribution utilities:

 • Defer transmission and distribution system upgrades by providing new control and
 operation solutions
 • Provide non-wire solutions to increase penetrations of solar, wind, EVs, and storage and
 realize decarbonization and emission goals
 • Provide advanced control solutions for DERs to become valuable assets to both
 distribution and transmission grids by delivering diverse grid services
 • Improve grid reliability and resilience by controlling DERs
 • Apply industry standards related to DERs and communication protocols to address
 interoperability requirements
 • Provide and demonstrate cutting-edge control algorithms and adaptable operation
 schemes that are compatible with existing utility setups.

 7

A simulation study is conducted to compare the costs for utilities to upgrade their system using
voltage regulators and capacitors versus adopting the proposed distributed controls to manage
voltages. The unit cost data of regulators and capacitors were obtained from NREL’s distribution
grid integration unit cost database, and the cost of devices to implement the AES algorithm were
obtained from a market survey conducted in 2018. In this study, the implementation of AES
control over existing DERs yields comparable voltage regulation performance to adding three
voltage regulators and three capacitor banks in the distribution system. The former is estimated
to cost $588,500 with each unit costing $500 and total 1177 controllers needed. Whereas the
latter option is estimated to cost $710,700 in total. In addition to reducing the costs of operation
compared to using traditional voltage regulation devices, the same AES implementation can
bring economic benefits, such as reducing peak demand and bringing additional revenue for
utilities as well as deferring system upgrades or installing utility-scale batteries for peak shaving.

Task 6. Initialize ADMS Testbed with HCE System

NREL will initialize its ADMS testbed located at the NREL with the feeder model developed in
Task 3 (including the advanced controls incorporated in Task 4) and the Survalent ADMS as
desired by HCE. NREL will work with Survalent to incorporate the SCADA and AMI
measurement data needed for control and NRECA to incorporate MultiSpeak into the ADMS
testbed. NREL will evaluate complete end-to-end use cases in this environment to demonstrate
the technical and economic benefits of the proposed mentods. Once completed, NREL will host
HCE and NRECA for an ESIF site visit to demonstrate the use case on the ADMS testbed, the
results obtained from the project to date, and to show a "proof of concept" visualization of
advanced distributed control of DERs to HCE operators.

ADMS Testbed for Laboratory Hardware-in-the-loop

In this project, we also conducted hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) experiment using NREL’s ADMS
test bed to validate the developed controllers. As shown in Figure 7, the distribution grid is
modeled using OpenDSS, with a small segment is modeled using Opal-RT. Most of DERs are
simulated and modeled using OpenDSS and Python, but we included six hardware devices and
integrated them into the test bed. These hardware devices include four PV inverters, one battery
system, and one EV charger with a Nissan Leaf plugged in. The top left block “RTOPF
DERMS” represents the hierarchical control described in Task 2, and it interfaces with Survalent
ADMS using Multispeak. Survalent ADMS can provide different types of distribution grid
management functions. However, since HCE only uses Survalent SCADA, we follow this and
only uses it to provide system measurements and interface with the RTOPF DERMS. The core
part of the test bed is “Testbed Coordinator”, which uses HELICS co-simulation engine to link
different pieces together, including the OpenDSS model, the Opal-RT model with hardware
devices integrated, and the controllers.

 Figure 7. HIL experiment test bed.

Task 7. Evaluate Cybersecurity for Data Transfer and Controller Communication

NREL will work with NRECA to validate the cybersecurity of the MultiSpeak Specification
during the hardware-in-the-loop evaluation conducted in Task 8. NRECA will perform
MultiSpeak Interoperability and compliance testing between the different software applications
by utilizing its new onine testing harness to ensure compatibility, functionality and cyber
security. This evaluation will be performed at the HIL stage.

Two Layers of Communication

As shown in Figure 7, we developed two layers of communication for the HIL experiment,

Task 8. Conduct Hardware-in-the-Loop Testing at ESIF

NREL will use the ADMS testbed initialized with the HCE system in Task 6 to drive HIL

SolarEdge battery inverters, residential-level thermostats, and electric vehicle supply equipment.
If desired, HCE will provide additional DERs of interest for HIL evaluation and testing. NREL
will compare the HIL simulation results against the modeling results from Task 4 and validate
the effectiveness of the advanced distributed control algorithms.

Task 9. Field Deployment at HCE Sites

Once the simulation results are available from Task 3, HCE will identify one or more candidate
field deployment sites and engage selected HCE member-customers to gain their participation in
a field deployment involving advanced distributed control by HCE of DERs located at their
premises. HCE will work with its selected member-customers and with NREL to design a field
deployment plan, including the specification and location of DERs (total number of DERs in the
field to control will be determined by both HCE and NREL in advance) to be controlled from
HCE's dispatch center using one or more use cases defined in Task 4.

Once the HIL testing in Task 8 is complete, HCE will procure, take delivery, and install any new
DERs required for the field deployment in coordination with its selected member-customer in
accordance with the field deployment plan. NREL will provide to HCE the physical controllers
used in Task 8, and HCE will install them on the HCE system in accordance with the field
deployment plan.

HCE in coordination with NREL, NRECA and Survalent will establish the right SCAD A and
AMI links, communication to the selected DER sites through Multispeak communications and to
the control algorithms as validated in the HIL evaluation for real-time monitoring and control of
the DER.

Once installation is complete, NREL and HCE will conduct field tests as described in the field
deployment plan and analyze the performance of the physical controllers, DERs, and the
advanced control algorithms in providing the grid services desired in each of the use cases
defined in Task 4. NREL will also validate the economic cost-benefit analysis from Task 5 using
actual data from the field deployment on the HCE system.

Field demonstration was conducted at Basalt Vista, specifically in a newly constructed net-zero
energy community near the Basalt High School. HCE led the deployment of the controllers,
already validated in NREL’s lab, into four homes. The controllers were used to manage the four
homes as virtual power plants and improve the energy efficiency and resilience for the homes.

Task 10: Project Management and Delivery

NREL will serve as project manager, with HCE, Survalent and NRECA as part of the project
team. HCE, Survalent and NRECA will designate a project lead to manage their respective
contributions, and to coordinate with the NREL project team. NREL will conduct weekly project
team meetings with HCE, Survalent and NRECA to review progress, track status of milestones,
and address any deficiencies in project budget, schedule, or performance.

NREL will work with HCE, Survalent and NRECA to publish one or more technical reports
during the period of project performance, documenting the activities and findings of the project.
A final report and one to two publications for public access will be published at the conclusion of
the project to disseminate the learnings from the project to broader audiance.

NREL will also work with HCE, Survalent and NRECA to conduct a final industry workshop
with broader co-operative and municipal utilities during the period of project performance,
highlighting project results, software, devices, and best practices that can be easily used by other
rural cooperatives and small/mid-size utilities to ease the adoption of DER on their systems.
