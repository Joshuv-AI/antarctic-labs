Methodological considerations on a meta-analysis of TEAS for post-anesthetic shivering
Authors: Mengjin Yu, Xiaoyan Liu, Lei Li, Yang Yang
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

In clinical practice, evidence-based interventions are crucial for improving patient outcomes and ensuring the effectiveness of treatments. As the field of perioperative research continues to evolve, it becomes increasingly important to assess the reliability of the research methods and conclusions that support clinical guidelines. We read with interest the meta-analysis by Miao et al, entitled “Effect of transcutaneous electrical acupoint stimulation on post-anesthetic shivering: a meta-analysis and trial sequential analysis”[1]. The topic is clinically relevant, and the authors report a marked reduction in post-anesthetic shivering (RR = 0.41) with low statistical heterogeneity. However, several methodological and interpretive concerns merit further discussion to improve the robustness and clinical applicability of the findings. This article is consistent with the TITAN 2025 guidelines for the use of AI[2].

First, the search strategy may be insufficient to support the secondary conclusions regarding PONV. The search strategy was primarily constructed around transcutaneous electrical acupoint stimulation (TEAS)-related terms combined with shivering-related terms, and the authors acknowledge in the limitations that PONV was not specifically searched. From the standpoint of systematic review methodology, this approach may lead to incomplete retrieval of evidence for PONV outcomes. For example, some randomized controlled trials may have evaluated TEAS effects on PONV/PON/POV but did not emphasize “shivering” in the title or abstract and thus could have been systematically missed under a “TEAS + shivering” strategy. Consequently, the pooled estimates for PONV may be at risk of incomplete retrieval bias. Accordingly, conclusions concerning PONV should be stated more cautiously and framed as findings derived from trials identified through a shivering-focused search, rather than as definitive evidence. In addition, the absence of a standalone “post-anesthetic” term is not necessarily a limitation because the shivering search terms already encompass peri-anesthetic contexts. However, to improve recall and reproducibility, the strategy could be further strengthened by adding broader context terms.

Second, the overall risk of bias across the included trials appears substantial, whereas the conclusions are expressed with relatively high certainty. Most studies reported limited information on allocation concealment and blinding, and only one trial explicitly described the blinding of both participants and outcome assessors. Under these conditions, effect estimates may be inflated by selection bias and by performance or detection bias. Inadequate allocation concealment can introduce predictability during enrollment and assignment, compromising baseline comparability despite randomization. In addition, although the Bedside Shivering Assessment Score is structured, it still relies on observer judgment; without assessor blinding, outcome classification may shift systematically. Even if the pooled results suggest benefit, the strength of inference should therefore be tempered. We suggest presenting GRADE assessments for key outcomes and conducting risk-of-bias–informed sensitivity analyses to align the certainty of conclusions with the quality of the underlying evidence.

Third, the interpretation of the trial sequential analysis (TSA) requires greater restraint. The authors describe the evidence as “sufficient” based on TSA. However, TSA is designed primarily to address random error in cumulative meta-analysis and is highly dependent on prespecified parameters (e.g., α = 5%, power = 80%, and an assumed relative risk reduction of 20% for the required information size). In the setting of an elevated risk of bias, a geographically concentrated evidence base, and variation in anesthesia type, patient populations, and stimulation protocols, TSA alone does not justify the assertion that further research is unnecessary. A more defensible interpretation is that, under the authors’ assumptions, additional trials with designs similar to those already included may be less likely to materially change the pooled estimate; nonetheless, well-designed, multicenter, cross-regional studies with standardized outcome definitions remain needed to reduce systematic bias and confirm generalizability.

Finally, we noted an apparent discrepancy in the publication year of the Xia et al[3] study between Table 1 and the reference list, which may affect the stated inclusion time window. Overall, the authors address an important perioperative question and provide encouraging pooled estimates. Greater alignment between secondary outcomes and the search strategy, a more conservative interpretation of TSA, and correction of minor inconsistencies would strengthen the rigor and clinical relevance of the conclusions. The synthesis of evidence for interventions such as TEAS is not only an academic exercise but an important step for the future integration of artificial intelligence in medicine. As mentioned by a recent global-scale bibliometric analysis of large language models in medicine, it reveals significant progress in understanding the potential and challenges of AI in healthcare, particularly focusing on applications like clinical decision support and patient education[4].
