Specific whole‐chromosome identification of the A01/C1 homoeologs through oligonucleotide‐based chromosome painting in Brassica species
Authors: Mariana Baez, Ludwig Mann, Daniela Quezada‐Martinez, Vinita Ramtekey, Tony Heitkam, Annaliese S Mason
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYDespite the high importance of Brassica species as crops, their complex genomic relationships and their small, poorly differentiated chromosomes are challenging for analysis. Brassica diploids have AA, BB, and CC genomes, whereas allopolyploids can occur in different subgenome combinations. During interspecific hybridization, frequently used for crop improvement, chromosome fragments are often exchanged, potentially impacting crop traits. Hence, the ability to produce chromosome karyotypes for individual plants is highly desirable. Here, we developed molecular cytogenetics probes designed from unique regions identified from genomic sequence data to differentiate each chromosome pair from A, B, and C subgenomes, generating two complementary oligo libraries, a density‐ and a synthesis‐optimized library. Specifically, we tested the highly similar (homoeologous) A01 and C1 chromosomes, which undergo frequent exchanges and translocation events in Brassica hybrids. Unique signals for one single chromosome pair were observed for the A01 painting probe in B. rapa and B. napus species and for the C1 painting probe in B. oleracea and B. napus. In addition, one A01/C1 translocation in B. napus Surpass400_024DH and a translocation of an A01 chromosome fragment into a C1 chromosome in an allohexaploid Brassica (B. carinata × B. rapa) were identified. Our subgenome chromosome‐specific probes may help to uncover rearranged fragments and nonhomologous chromosome pairing involving the Brassica A, B, and C genomes. In summary, we present a subgenome‐specific and chromosome‐specific cytogenetic tool to unravel the complex dynamics between the highly agronomically relevant Brassica crop genomes.Keywords: oligo‐painting, chromosome specific painting, Brassica, chromosome translocation, nonhomologous pairing, chromosomic specific probe

INTRODUCTION

The genus Brassica, in the Brassicaceae, includes six major vegetable or oil crops: the three diploid species, B. rapa (AA genome composition, 2n = 20), B. nigra (BB, 2n = 16), and B. oleracea (CC, 2n = 18), and the three allotetraploid species, B. carinata (BBCC, 2n = 34), B. juncea (AABB, 2n = 36), and B. napus (AACC, 2n = 38). Interspecific hybridization between these species can generate useful new variation for breeding improvement through introgression of desirable traits from one species to another (Mason & Chèvre, 2016; Prakash et al., 2009). A high level of synteny and DNA sequence homology continues to exist between Brassica genomes, with known regions of conserved gene order or synteny (Cheng et al., 2012; Parkin et al., 2014), which potentially could enhance hybridization and allow introgression breeding to transfer useful traits between genomes (Mason & Snowdon, 2016).

With increasing improvements in sequencing methods, structural variants such as translocations, inversions, and presence–absence variants have been found to be very common in the Brassica species (Chalhoub et al., 2014; Golicz et al., 2016; He et al., 2021). These structural variants are not just of academic interest, but are increasingly known to be associated with specific, crop‐relevant phenotypes (reviewed by Schiessl et al., 2019, Fu et al., 2016). However, even with advances in sequencing technologies, identification and characterization of these variants can be challenging (Edwards et al., 2013; Liu et al., 2012). Comprehensive identification of all structural variants, such as translocations and inversions, within an individual often requires de novo genome assembly or specific isolation of hybrid long reads, with both approaches remaining relatively expensive and computationally complex (Edwards et al., 2013).

As chromosome sizes and morphologies are very similar within the Brassica genus, Brassica karyotype characterization has been challenging. This is further complicated by the small chromosome size and the absence of cytological landmarks that allow easy and specific identification of each chromosome pair (Agrawal et al., 2020; Mason & Snowdon, 2016). Fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) is an important molecular cytogenetic method to characterize karyotype variation at chromosome level by direct localization of DNA sequences onto chromosomes (Jiang, 2019), enabling identification of chromosomes even in species with small and morphologically indistinguishable chromosomes such as Brassica. In situ hybridization using genomic DNA (GISH) can distinguish between different Brassica genomes from related species and interspecific hybrids (Snowdon et al., 1997). Furthermore, integration of probes from specific DNA sequences, such as ribosomal RNA genes, centromeric sequences (centBr1 and centBr2; Lim et al., 2007), and BACs (bacterial artificial chromosomes), has been used to identify each individual chromosome pair and to corroborate a high degree of meiotic instability and genome reshuffling in synthetic B. napus (Xiong et al., 2011). Moreover, comparative chromosome painting using BAC contigs of Arabidopsis thaliana has been useful to investigate multiple chromosome variations resulting from polyploidization events in different Brassicaceae tribes (Mandakova et al., 2017). However, BAC hybridization approaches are still technically challenging, as many specific BACs for each chromosome pair are needed and as BAC storage and maintenance is laborious and time‐consuming.

More recently, the availability of whole reference genome assemblies gave rise to a new type of cytogenetic probes that consist of massive pools of synthetic oligonucleotides associated with a specific chromosome location (Jiang, 2019). Use of massive pools of short oligonucleotides as probes for chromosome in situ hybridization allows, in principle, labelling of any part of a chromosome as a band or as chromosome paint (Braz et al., 2018; Šimoníková et al., 2019). The oligonucleotide libraries are a set of unique sequences, selected in silico from an assembled genome sequence with chromosomal region specificity. These are highly sensitive and provide consistent chromosome labeling and signal intensity. These oligonucleotide libraries have been used as robust FISH probes in many plant species, for example, as a barcode to construct molecular cytogenetic karyotypes in potato and licorice (Braz et al., 2018; Meng et al., 2024), as chromosome paint to characterize chromosomal rearrangements and visualize homoeologous pairing between chromosomes belonging to related species (Han et al., 2015; Qu et al., 2017; Xin et al., 2018), to integrate pseudomolecules of reference genome sequence to individual chromosomes in situ in Musa acuminata spp. malaccensis “DH Pahang” (Šimoníková et al., 2019) and to directly visualize meiotic crossovers on metaphase chromosomes in F1 and F2 progenies of maize B73 x Mo17 hybrids (do Vale Martins et al., 2019).

Oligonucleotide‐based chromosome painting has become a powerful tool for cytogenetic studies, especially for plant species with extremely similar chromosome morphology. The probes may be useful for determining chromosome evolution and homology in Brassica, and are applicable in genomic studies and in plant breeding aiming to exploit the wider germplasm pool.

A genome sequence‐based cytogenetic barcode from a whole genome assembly of B. rapa has been successfully implemented (Agrawal et al., 2020). These oligonucleotide probes produced specific in situ hybridization patterns that identified 18 specific chromosome regions across chromosomes from the A, B, and C genomes of Brassica, and the R genome of Raphanus sativus, which enabled the detection of structural variations within Brassica species (Agrawal et al., 2020). However, all these oligonucleotide libraries are based on the 10 pseudochromosomes of the A subgenome and not only recognize its chromosome pair, but also the corresponding homoeologous chromosome pairs and regions across the other three subgenomes B, C, and R. In consequence, these libraries are not specific for a unique chromosome pair within hybrids and allopolyploids. On the other hand, these libraries are restricted to specific locations in the chromosomes, making them unsuitable to identify and trace other chromosome regions within a specific chromosome pair.

To demonstrate the applicability of oligonucleotide‐based painting probes for precisely identifying subgenome‐specific chromosome pairs in Brassica diploids and allopolyploids, we designed probes for specifically painting chromosomes A01 and C1 from the A and C Brassica subgenomes, respectively. This method allows high‐precision analysis of chromosome rearrangements and introgression events between the Brassica subgenomes in established and synthetic allopolyploids and other interspecific hybrids (Figure 1).

Experimental workflow for the development of the chromosome‐specific paintings and chromosomal contribution evaluation on different Brassica species and allopolyploids.

RESULTS

Identification of unique oligonucleotide sequences along Brassica chromosomes

In order to validate these oligo painting probes, we focused on two chromosome‐specific oligonucleotide paintings for the homoeologous chromosome pairs 1 in the A and C subgenomes, respectively. For this, we analyzed in silico the A01 chromosome of B. rapa (39.2 Mbp) and C1 chromosome of B. oleracea (52.0 Mbp). In total, we identified 1 475 667 unique 45 nt oligonucleotides (oligos) with high in silico specificity to these chromosomes within diploids and allopolyploids containing the A, B, and C genomes (Figure 2, all identified oligos in Figure S1, https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17544190). The number of unique 45‐mers oligos selected was 20 000 and 27 000, with an oligo density of 0.51 and 0.52 oligos per kb in the A01 and C1 probes, respectively. Some minor exceptions were observed, where small regions showed in silico cross‐detection among these two sets of oligos (for A01 and C1) but with very low oligo‐sequence density (<25 oligos, for details see Figure S2, https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17544190). A lack of unique oligos was observed in the putative centromeric region of the A01 chromosome (Figure 2a). As the centromeric regions are usually composed of repetitive DNAs, these gaps in oligonucleotide coverage cannot be avoided. Oligo‐based sequences specific to the entire A01 and C1 chromosome pairs were identified, and were able to discriminate in silico between these chromosome pairs in both B. rapa (AA) and B. oleracea (CC) genetic backgrounds and within the three different allotetraploid backgrounds of B. juncea (AABB), B. napus (AACC), and B. carinata (BBCC) (Figure S3, https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17544190). In addition, candidate oligo painting probes for the subgenome‐specific identification of all the Brassica subgenome A, B, and C chromosome pairs were developed and selected. There are two complementary libraries available: the density‐optimized library (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17544309) and the synthesis‐optimized library (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17544327). The density‐optimized library had been optimized for a consistent and even distribution oligo density of around 0.5 probes per 1 kb and in the synthesis‐optimized library we limited the amount of oligos per chromosome to the commercially available synthesis pool sizes (max 27 k probes) with densities between 0.37 and 0.54 probes per kb. The density‐optimized library ranges from 11 k to 37 k painting probes per chromosome with 715 k painting probes in total, while for the synthesis‐optimized library, the maximum probe count per chromosome is capped at 27 k resulting in a total amount of 642 k painting probes (Table S1). In silico evaluation of these probe sets confirmed uniform genomic coverage and minimal cross‐hybridization potential, supporting their suitability for downstream experimental validation and chromosome painting applications (Figure S4, https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17544309; Figure S5, https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17544327).

Chromosome‐specific identification in mitotic metaphase in homozygous backgrounds of different Brassica species

To confirm the specificity of the chromosome painting probes, fluorescence in situ hybridization analysis using the A01 and C1 oligo‐painting probes on different homozygous Brassica species was carried out. In mitotic chromosomes of a diploid B. rapa (AA) and B. napus (AACC), it was possible to observe specific signals for a single chromosome pair with the A01 probe (Figure 3b–c, h–i). This suggests that targeting of chromosome 1 of the A subgenome has been successful in high specificity, both in an A‐only background (Figure 3b,c) and in an allopolyploid background (Figure 3h–i). Unspecific hybridization of any homoeologous region of the C‐specific chromosome 1 was successfully avoided.

For both targeted chromosomes 1 from the A and C subgenomes, both chromosome arms were completely covered. This shows that the oligo painting probes are indeed chromosome‐specific for a single chromosome. In addition, in an allopolyploid background, subgenome‐specificity was retained. This implies that the synteny between these chromosomes in different species containing the same subgenomes is maintained. The centromeric region of the A01 chromosome in B. rapa and B. napus lacked a strong oligo signal, which correlates with the in silico selection pattern of this chromosome (Figure 3a–c, h–i).

Identification of chromosome rearrangements in Brassica allotetraploid and allohexaploid

To evaluate the specificity of the oligo painting probes as a more informative marker to characterize chromosome structure across Brassica species and to confirm potential chromosome rearrangements within Brassica allopolyploids, A01 and C1 oligo paints were hybridized to two exemplary hybrid genotypes with putative translocation events comprising chromosomes A01/C1: B. napus cv. Surpass400_024DH (2n = 4x = AACC) and a Brassica allohexaploid carirapa (2n = 6x = B. carinata × B. rapa, AABBCC). To have sequence‐based confirmation for these two allopolyploids, chromosome copy numbers were estimated based on the Log2R ratio values obtained from the Illumina Infinium Brassica 90 K SNP array (Illumina, USA). Based on Log2R ratio, we identify duplicated (blue segments in Figure 5a,b,e,f) and deleted regions (red segments in Figure 5a,b,e,f) in A01 and C1 chromosomes in both allopolyploids analyzed. Based on homoeology, these events are putative translocations between these chromosome pairs (Figure 5c,g).

To assess the presence/absence of the putative translocations identified through the array, we hybridized somatic mitotic and meiotic cells with our A01/C1 oligo paintings. The meiotic cells were collected one generation before the samples analyzed in the SNP chip. After hybridizing the mitotic chromosomes of both allopolyploids with the oligo painting probes, it was possible to identify specific signals in one single chromosome pair for A01 oligos and one single chromosome pair for C1 oligos in both genotypes (Figure 4). These oligo probes facilitate the identification of one translocation between the A01 and the C1 chromosome pairs in B. napus cv. Surpass400_024DH, where it was possible to observe a cross‐hybridization signal of the oligo probes in the distal region of one of the arms in two chromosome pairs. Two chromosome pairs labeled with the A01 oligos carry a distal/terminal signal for C1 oligos, and two chromosome pairs labeled with C1 oligos carry a distal/terminal signal for A01 oligos (Figures 4a–d, 5d), indicating translocations between these chromosome pairs. These observations differed from the expected rearrangements suggested by the SNP analysis, where one subterminal fragment of A01 chromosome appeared in the C1 chromosome pair and two subterminal fragments of C1 chromosome in an A01 chromosome pair (Figure 5c). The two separated translocated fragments from C1 could be observed as a single fragment, likely due to close proximity and condensation of the chromosomes. In the allohexaploid, it is also possible to observe one chromosome pair labeled with C1 oligos carrying an A01 signal in the distal/terminal region of one of the arms (Figures 4e–h, 5h), indicating a translocation of a fragment of the A01 chromosome pair into a C1 chromosome pair. A similar translocation was observed in 3/5 plants analyzed by SNP chip, although they also had more deletion/duplication events not observed with the oligo painting probes. In this case, due to the unstable genome of the allohexaploid, the events we observed in the progeny may not have been present in the sample analyzed with the oligo probes; hence, the discrepancies in the events observed versus expected. These oligo paints showed even further specificity in this allohexaploid than observed in the allotetraploid B. napus background due to the presence of the additional B subgenome and no cross‐hybridization signals for the specific oligo paints in any other chromosome pair.

In meiotic cells from B. napus Surpass400_024DH and Brassica carirapa allohexaploid C05 (B. carinata × B. rapa), bivalents containing either A01‐ or C1‐specific oligos with visible, separate signals were observed; however, it was not possible to observe the chromosome translocations (images not shown). This could be due to the lower condensation of the chromosomes in these stages of cell division and the insufficient amount of oligos in these particular regions.

Meiotic chromosome pairing of A01 and C1 and nonhomologous pairing formation involving chromosome pair A01 in Brassica napus cv Drakkar

To further analyze the potential of the oligo probes in determining chromosome pairing behavior during meiotic stages, particularly in prophase I during diakinesis and anaphase I, we used the A01‐ and C1‐specific chromosome paints for the B. napus cv. “Drakkar” line. Bivalents containing either A01‐ or C1‐specific oligos provided visible, separate signals, showing the correct homologous pairing for each pair (Figure 6a–e). In addition, nonhomologous chromosome associations involving the A01 and C1 chromosome pairs were observed during diakinesis (Figure 6f–j). The average frequency of A01‐C1 pairing was ~13%. In a few cells, it was possible to observe an A01 bivalent and a C1 bivalent carrying an A01 fragment (Figure 6k–n). These results indicate chromosome pairing and translocations during meiosis can still occur between these chromosome pairs in this established Brassica line. During anaphase I, we observed the correct chromosome segregation for A01 and C1 (Figure 6o–s).

DISCUSSION

Identification of individual chromosome pairs and homoeologous chromosomes from different subgenomes within Brassica is challenging. Here, we were able to exploit reference genome sequences to develop oligonucleotide‐based chromosome painting probes for all chromosome pairs of Brassica subgenomes A, B, and C and to individually identify subgenome‐specific chromosome pairs A01 and C1 from the Brassica A and C subgenomes. These specific painting probes recognize the length of the targeted chromosome pairs in diploid species as well as in allotetraploids and in synthetic hybrids carrying A and C subgenomes. Hence, it is clearly and unequivocally possible to differentiate homoeologous chromosomes between two different subgenomes (here A and C). Here, these subgenome‐ and chromosome‐specific paints are designed for a specific complete chromosome pair. They are robust and identify mitotic as well as meiotic chromosomes in different Brassica karyotype configurations. In addition, the paints identify chromosome rearrangements and give insight into homologous and nonhomologous chromosome pairing involving these chromosome pairs. Therefore, they offer a significant improvement on previous chromosomal marker technologies such as the hybridizations of tandem repeats (Lim et al., 2005, 2007), BACs (Xiong et al., 2011; Xiong & Pires, 2011), and oligo‐based barcode probes (Agrawal et al., 2020).

The probe density is an important factor to compare the adequate resolution of these probes to specifically identified chromosome pairs and to recognize chromosome rearrangements. Here, we have designed the oligo painting probes with a density of 0.51 and 0.52 oligos per kb in the A01 and C1 probes, respectively. These oligo densities are similar, if a bit lower, compared to the probes developed for banana, M. acuminata “DH Pahang” (0.9–2.1 oligomers per kb) with a similar genome size (Šimoníková et al., 2019), but higher than in other crop species, like the 0.052 or 0.014 oligos per kb in wheat (Li et al., 2021; Song et al., 2020), or 0.012 oligos per kb in Thinopyrum elongatum, both with bigger genome sizes (Chen et al., 2023). A density of 0.012 oligos per kb was determined as sufficient to achieve a balance between cost‐effectiveness and a reasonable resolution level in species with bigger genomes like Thinopyrum and wheat, with good signals along the length of the chromosomes pairs, considering the presence of dispersed repetitive sequences (Chen et al., 2023; Li et al., 2021), indicating that a lower oligo density for the chromosome pairs of the different subgenomes within Brassica species may be a suitable and advantageous approach. Nevertheless, a higher oligo density would still be possible for these and other chromosome pairs from the Brassica subgenomes A, B, and C, and would be even recommended for the identification of small chromosome rearrangements in meiotic chromosomes. The synthesized library (Immortal Library) and PCR products can also be used as templates for secondary amplification (Bi et al., 2020). Thus, the bulked A01 and C1 oligo painting probes can be permanently used as a resource to identify the A and C chromosome pairs 1 in Brassica species and hybrid species derivatives with high efficiency.

Hybridization between species, even if they are related, can be challenging. Identifying specific chromosomes of interest and determining their chromosomal behavior in hybrids, regarding chromosomic translocation, chromosomic elimination, the presence of unreduced gametes or meiotic behavior, is fundamentally important for the successful implementation of introgression programs. To identify specific chromosomes and analyze chromosome structure in hybrids with putatively identified rearrangements using a SNP array, we applied the A01‐ and C1‐chromosome‐specific oligo painting probes in two allopolyploid species with different subgenome composition. In B. napus, Surpass400_024DH, “S4” (2n = 4x = 38, AACC), besides successfully identifying both A01 and C1 chromosome pairs, the oligo painting probes were able to identify a fixed translocation between these chromosome pairs in the distal chromosomic region in one of the chromosome arms, with an A01 pair carrying a chromosome fragment coming from the C1 chromosome pair and a C1 pair carrying a fragment from the A01 pair. This observation corroborates at least two of the three fixed translocations identified in silico in this line using high‐resolution molecular karyotyping (Higgins et al., 2018; Quezada‐Martinez et al., 2023). However, both C1 translocated fragments in the A01 chromosome pair could being observed as a single one due to close proximity and condensation of the chromosomes. SNP genotyping analysis of Brassica synthetics has shown that extra copies of the C genome often replace a region of the A genome. Less often, extra copies of the A genome replace a region of the C genome. Nevertheless, translocations that involve reciprocal exchanges cannot be detected via SNP chip analysis (Quezada‐Martinez et al., 2023). We cannot exclude the possibility of the observation of two nonreciprocal translocations of different chromosomic fragments in very close chromosomic proximity. In the Brassica allohexaploid “C05” (AABBCC = 2n = 6x = 54), it was possible to specifically identify A01 and C1 chromosome pairs, as well as a chromosomic translocation involving these chromosome pairs. The C1 pair carries a chromosome fragment from the A01 chromosome pair. The translocation was characterized in several allohexaploid lines using high‐resolution molecular karyotyping in one generation after the generation of the sample analyzed here cytogenetically (Quezada‐Martinez et al., 2023). Similar to the allotetraploid B. napus, both A01 expected fragments translocated into the C1 chromosome pair could be being observed as a single one due to close proximity and condensation of the chromosomes. These results show the importance of physical chromosomic confirmation by hybridization with specific probes for some of the chromosomal rearrangements in Brassica.

Synthetic allopolyploids within Brassica are commonly meiotically unstable. Different events can occur as a consequence of this leading to chromosomal rearrangements due to nonhomologous recombination between homoeologous regions of the different subgenomes, most commonly between A and C subgenomes (Gaebelein et al., 2019). In resynthesized Brassica allopolyploids, chromosome exchanges between A and C subgenomes lead to extensive chromosomal rearrangements and structural variants, and the increase of these changes are usually related to the decrease in fertility (Gaebelein et al., 2019; Katche et al., 2023; Quezada‐Martinez et al., 2022). Comparisons between A and C genomes in B. napus genetic map has shown a high level of synteny, with most genomic regions sharing primary homoeology, suggesting A/C genomes with equivalent genomic content and only a few major chromosome rearrangements responsible for the chromosome number difference between subgenomes (Parkin et al., 1995, 2003). Chromosome pairs A01 and C1 are one of the most rearranged chromosomes pairs in many different types of synthetic allopolyploids. Different types and degrees of deletions, insertions, and reciprocal and nonreciprocal translocations were observed, caused by their high homoeology (Gaebelein et al., 2019; Quezada‐Martinez et al., 2023). Nonhomologous recombination in an allohexaploid (B. napus × carinata) × B. juncea was detected in a high frequency (8%–14%) between A01‐C1, A02‐C2, and A03‐C3 chromosome pairs, which share the highest sequence homoeology (Mason et al., 2015). In early segregating populations derived from this cross, novel unbalanced homoeologous exchanges were more frequent between chromosome pairs with larger homoeology blocks, with A01‐C1 being one of them. In addition, a higher frequency of novel duplications and deletions per chromosome pair was observed (Gaebelein et al., 2019). In later generations, many of these deletions and duplications became fixed, especially along the A01 and C1 chromosomes (Quezada‐Martinez et al., 2023). Chromosome‐specific probes to identify the pairs involved in chromosome rearrangements will allow the detailed analysis of their meiotic behavior. Here, we showed this in the established B. napus “Drakkar” line, in which A01 and C1 pairs still go through nonhomologous pairing during a stable meiosis, potentially leading to future nonhomologous chromosome rearrangements, as observed in some cell were one of these pair carry a fragment of the other chromosome pair. These detailed analyses will allow us to better understand the processes that lead to these genomic rearrangements in specific lines or specific chromosome pairs of interest and to apply this knowledge in future breeding programs.

Regarding the resolution, in mitotic chromosomes we were able to identify structural changes that occupy a few Mb. As the chromosomes are less condensed in the early stages of meiosis, the resolution is higher and potentially allows the identification of rearranged fragments of a shorter size. Considering the advantage of higher spatial resolution during meiotic division, these oligo painting probes will be suitable to visualize meiotic processes such as chromosome pairing, crossing over, and synapsis of specific chromosome pairs. Nevertheless, a higher oligo density in the chromosome of interest is suggested to recognize small‐sized rearrangements, as the rearrangements analyzed here were not consistently recognized in meiotic diakinesis stages.

The oligo painting probes developed and validated in this study for the subgenome‐specific identification of chromosome pairs A01 and C1 from the Brassica A and C subgenomes provide a proof of concept for extending this approach to other Brassica chromosome pairs. Two libraries are available: the density optimized library, with consistent oligo density, and synthesis optimized library, with the amount of oligos per chromosome suitable to the commercially available synthesis pool sizes (max 27 k probes). Both oligo painting probe libraries represent a robust foundation for future cytogenetic and comparative genomic applications.

Besides the implications for the analysis of Brassica hybrids, these subgenome‐ and simultaneously chromosome‐specific probes in the model genus Brassica (Mabry et al., 2024) provide a test case for the design of similar probes in other allopolyploids, in which the chromosomal contribution needs to be evaluated. On one hand, this is relevant on the level of fundamental science, like in the Tragopogon genus, in which a similar genomic constellation (three diploids that can hybridize to form three tetraploids) is actively researched (Lim et al., 2008). On the other hand, it is relevant to inform breeding and can be applied to crops such as triploid banana (Šimoníková et al., 2019), triploid saffron crocus (Schmidt et al., 2019), and breeding of further allopolyploid crops like quinoa (Heitkam et al., 2020), peanut (Du et al., 2018), potato (He et al., 2018), and even crop‐related species with reduced availability of sequence data, for example, in the Triticeae (Li et al., 2021).

CONCLUSIONS

Bulked oligonucleotide‐based probes can offer potential advantages for future research and breeding approaches in Brassica. Here, we present the instruction for oligo paints for 27 Brassica chromosomes, including chromosomes A01‐A10, B1‐B8, and C1‐C9. They can potentially be used to detect chromosome rearrangements that occur during the hybridization of Brassica species. We test their functionality and specificity for A01 and C1 in different species and hybrid contexts, including mitotic and meiotic chromosomes. Use of synthetically labeled oligonucleotides is convenient and efficient in ensuring that chromosome pairs are similarly labeled. We are able to specifically identify and map translocations, and show how the cytogenetics can extend the information obtained from SNP array data. Oligonucleotide pools will be of significant value toward identifying and tracing alien introgressions in Brassica crops and hybrids, allowing far greater resolution and accuracy than genomic in situ hybridization approaches. Finally, we conclude that our bulked oligos for Brassica work very well to differentiate subgenomes and chromosomes alike, hence proving their high applicability for research and breeding.

Oligo‐based probe design for Brassica chromosomes A01 and C1

For probe design and visualization, we followed the protocol of Mann and Maiwald (2023). In short, diploid A, B, and C genome assemblies from B. rapa (Istace et al. 2021), B. nigra (Perumal et al., 2020), B. oleracea (Belser et al. 2018), and allotetrapolyploid subgenome assemblies AB, AC, and BC from B. juncea (Kang et al., 2021), B. napus (Rousseau‐Gueutin et al., 2020), and B. carinata (Song et al., 2020) served as input to guide the oligonucleotide design (for details, see Table 1). Chorus2 software v2.0.1 (Liu & Zhang, 2023: https://github.com/zhangtaolab/Chorus2) was used for the design of the oligonucleotide‐probes (Zhang et al., 2021). Short‐reads from genome assemblies of B. rapa, B. nigra, B. oleracea, and B. napus (ID: ERR2564158, SRR11906214, ERR2564372, ERR4344198) were used for repeat filtering. The design strategy used the presence of unique sequences identified in a unique genomic region: oligonucleotide‐based FISH probes present only in one subgenome (A, B, or C). Chorus2 was run with all diploid genomes in one input file (A, B, C genomes), followed by filtering for repetitive sequences using short read data (k‐mer‐based using Chorus filter and select functions). All identified oligos are provided in supplement files (Data S1 Brassica_ABC_genomes.fasta_all_oligos_selected, https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17544190). Designed oligos were aligned against all diploid A, B, and C genomes individually to check for haplotype‐specificity and cross‐hybridization to other genomes/species (AB, AC, BC genomes) (minimum 60% identity over 60% length). Oligos specific to chromosomes B. napus A01 (An01) and B. rapa A01 (Ar01) and to B. napus C1 (BnC1) and B. oleracea C1 (Bo01) were selected for experimental analysis. Sets of 20 000 oligos for A01 (B. rapa, subgenome A, 39.2 Mb chromosome length) and 27 000 oligos for C1 (B. oleracea, C subgenome, 52.0 Mb chromosome length) were selected for each chromosome‐specific probe (Data S2 A01_0_39217_45nt‐oligos_20k.(fasta/bed), https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17544030; Data S3 C1_0_52030_45nt‐oligos_27k.(fasta/bed), https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17544130). Oligo libraries were designed to achieve a density of 0.51 and 0.52 oligos per 1‐kb chromosome sequence for A01 and C1, respectively. Each 45 nt oligo was predicted to be present only in one subgenome (A or C), within the single subgenomes of B. rapa, B. nigra, or B. oleracea. Each set of chromosome‐specific A01 and C1 probes per library was synthesized and directly labeled with ATTO550 and ATTO488, respectively, by Arbor Biosciences (via BioCat GmbH, Germany). Sets of oligos targeting all other chromosomes (“ready‐to‐synthesize” oligo sets) are available in the supplement files density_optimized_library.zip (Data S4 https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17544309 and Data S5 synthesis_optimized_library.zip, https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17544327). Two separate libraries are provided, with one optimized for a unified density of around 0.5 oligos per 1‐kb chromosome sequence and one optimized for commercially available pool sizes (max. 27 k probes per pool) with a density of 0.3–0.5 oligos per 1‐kb chromosome sequence.

Mitotic and meiotic slide preparation and oligo‐based fluorescent in situ hybridization (Oligo‐FISH)

For mitotic preparations, actively growing root tips were collected and treated with 8‐hydroxyquinoline 2 mM at 6°C for 24 h, fixed in ethanol: acetic acid (3:1, v/v), and stored in −20°C until use. Fixed root tips were used to prepare mitotic chromosome slides following the dropping technique with modifications (Aliyeva‐Schnorr et al., 2015). Three to five root tips were washed in distilled water and digested in 20 μL of an enzymatic mixture (1% pectyolase Y‐23 and 2% cellulase “Onozuka” R‐10, in citric buffer) for 1.5–2 h at 37°C, washed in 70% ethanol, and the root section was carefully broken with a needle. Slides with a high density of mitotic metaphases were selected.

For meiosis preparations, immature unopened flower buds were collected and fixed as described above (ethanol: acetic acid 3:1 v/v, RT). Out of six anthers, one was squashed and stained with 1% acetocarmine to identify meiotic stages, while the remaining five anthers were digested in the same enzymatic mixture described above, incubated for 1–1.5 h at 37°C, washed, and then macerated in 70% ethanol. A pellet obtained after a quick centrifugation containing meiocytes was dried and resuspended in 100% acetic acid. The meiocyte solution was dropped onto slides and incubated for 5 min in a humid chamber at room temperature. Thirty cells were analyzed for a robust interpretation of meiotic chromosome behavior at diakinesis. Chromosome configurations were scored as bivalent, univalent, or multivalent at diakinesis. Other chromosomal aberrations such as anaphase bridges, laggards, or chromosome fragments at anaphase/telophase stages of meiosis I and meiosis II were also observed.

Selected slides were mounted in 8 μL of DAPI Vectashield® antifade mounting medium (H‐1200‐10, Vector Laboratories) to visualize chromosomes. Oligo‐FISH was carried out following Montenegro et al. (2022) with small modifications. Briefly, a hybridization mixture consisting of 50% formamide, 10% dextrane sulphate, 2 × saline sodium citrate (SSC), 200 ng of the A01 probe (red, ATTO550), and 400 ng of the C1 probe (green, ATTO488), in a total volume of 10 μL was applied per slide. In order to clearly identify univalent, bivalent, and multivalent chromosomes, DNA from the centromere‐specific satellite repeat sequences CentBrI and CentBrII (Xiong & Pires, 2011) was labeled with Cy5‐dUTP, using a nick translation kit (11 745 808 910, Roche) following the manufacturer's protocol. The hybridization mixture and the chromosomes on the slides were denatured together for 5 min at 75°C and hybridized for 2–3 days at 37°C. Chromosomes were then counterstained with 1 μg/mL DAPI‐Vectashield ready‐to‐use antifade solution (Vector Laboratories). Oligo‐FISH images were captured using an AxioCam 305 color camera coupled to a Zeiss Axio Observer 7 fluorescence microscope and an ApoTome.2, with ZEN blue imaging software. The images were adjusted and optimized for brightness and contrast using Adobe Photoshop CC (2019).

DNA extraction and SNP genotyping data analysis

Five progeny seeds from B. napus Surpass400_024DH and Brassica allohexaploid carirapa C05 (B. carinata × B. rapa) were grown under greenhouse conditions at the Justus‐Liebig University (Giessen, Germany). A leaf sample was collected on ice for each genotype and used for DNA extraction using the Doyle and Doyle method (Doyle & Doyle, 1990). DNA samples were treated with RNase following the manufacturer's instructions (Carl Roth, Germany). DNA quantity and quality were estimated using a Qubit fluorometer with a dsDNA BR assay kit (Thermofisher Scientific, Germany) and agarose gel electrophoresis, respectively.

Lyophilized DNA was sent for genotyping using the Illumina Infinium Brassica 90 K SNP array (Illumina, USA). Once the data were received, the SNP markers were filtered for specificity to each subgenome, removing all markers binding to more than one subgenome. For chromosomes A01 and C1, 708 and 1578 markers were kept for further analysis, respectively.

Copy number estimations and plotting during the SNP analysis

Log2R ratios were used to estimate the presence of duplication or deletion events. To reduce noise in this data, a further two‐step normalization was applied. Initially, Log2R ratios were normalized by calculating the sample‐wise median and subtracting it from each SNP value. Afterward, for each sample, a reference chromosome was selected and used as a baseline, based on the absence of obvious CNV events and the expectation that it is present in two copies. For the Surpass400_024DH sample, chromosome A05 was used, and for the C05 allohexaploid sample, chromosome A06 was used as a baseline for normalization. After this, we subset the normalized Log2R ratios for chromosomes A01 and C1 for each sample for further analysis.

To estimate duplication and deletion events in each sample, we used the DNAcopy package v. 1.76.0 (Seshan & Olshen, 2023) in R v. 4.3. First, each sample was smoothed using the function smooth with the default parameters. Next, segmentation was performed using the segment function with default parameters for Surpass400_024DH and an alpha = 0.05 for the C05 carirapa sample. The segment output was then examined, and based on the data, a threshold of 0.15 was set to color the segment events as duplications (blue, >0.15) or deletions (red, <0.15). The plots were done using the plot function in R.

Based on the obtained segments and the primary homoeology between chromosomes A01 and C1 (Chalhoub et al., 2014), a molecular karyotype was drawn depicting the putative translocations between chromosomes. A potential translocation event was classified as a translocation if, for example, a duplication event in A01 was observed and a deletion or missing copy was present in the corresponding primary homoeologous region in C01. Chromosomes were drawn in R v. 4.3 using the R package chromDraw v. 2.38.0 (Janečka & Lysak, 2016) with further editing in Gimp v. 2.10.20 (The GIMP Development Team, 2020). Only events of at least 0.5 MB were considered. Events separated by less than 0.5 MB were combined into one.
