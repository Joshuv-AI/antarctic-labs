Clinical course and surgical outcomes of cerebral cavernous malformations: Insights from a large single-center experience

Pavlina Lenga, Janis T. Linke, Edgar Tessmann, Bogdana Suchorska, Johannes Walter, Sandro M. Krieg, Moritz Scherer

Abstract

IntroductionClinical decision-making for cerebral cavernous malformations (CCMs) requires balancing hemorrhage risk against surgery-related morbidity, yet clinically applicable risk stratification remains limited.Research questionTo examine hemorrhage during recorded follow-up, postoperative complications, and functional outcomes after conservative and surgical management of CCMs.Material and methodsWe retrospectively analyzed 268 patients treated between 2014 and 2021. Clinical, radiological, and treatment variables were collected. Functional outcome was assessed using the modified Rankin Scale (mRS), analyzed as an ordinal outcome using paired nonparametric tests. Recorded untreated observation was censored at surgery; hemorrhage rates were reported with exact 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsAt baseline, most patients presented with hemorrhage and 25.4% of lesions were infratentorial. Most were managed conservatively (72.8%), whereas 27.2% underwent surgery. Median time from diagnosis to surgery was 48 days. Postoperative neurological complications occurred in 20/73 patients (27.4%), including seizure-related events (11.0%), diplopia in (4.1%), and hemiparesis in (4.1%). Among 68 surgical patients with paired assessments, median mRS was 1 [IQR 1–1] at baseline and 0 [0–1] at last follow-up; residual disability was greater in brainstem lesions. Two new radiographic hemorrhages were documented during 250.3 person-years in 129 patients with untreated observation (0.80/100 person-years; 95% CI 0.10–2.89). Age was not associated with postoperative complications (OR 1.01/year, 95% CI 0.97–1.04).Discussion and conclusionIn this tertiary-center cohort, favorable functional recovery was observed in selected surgical patients, but brainstem CCMs remained associated with residual morbidity. Sparse hemorrhage events and confounding by indication precluded treatment comparisons or a validated risk-stratification framework.

Introduction

Cerebrovascular malformations represent an important group of intracranial pathologies and carry a significant clinical and socioeconomic burden due to their variable natural and clinical course (; ). Cavernous malformations (CCM) account for 10-25% of intracranial vascular malformations, with an overall incidence of 0.1 - 0.8% in the general population (; ; ; ). A recent study reported an age-standardized incidence of 2.01/100.000 patients (). Presentation in the general population is mostly sporadic; however, familial incidence is up to 20% (; ).

The natural course of CCMs is heterogeneous, ranging from asymptomatic in most cases to presentation with neurologic deficits with recurrent symptomatic hemorrhages (; ; ). Localization is of particular clinical importance as it dictates symptoms, and lesions situated in the basal ganglia or the brainstem are associated with higher morbidity (; ). The reported annual hemorrhage risk per lesion ranges from 1.7% to 2.5%, but in previously hemorrhaged CCMs it is up to 30% at 5 years (; ; ; ). Hemorrhage rates depend on differences between patient cohorts, with prior hemorrhage and brainstem location accounting as the strongest predictors of future bleeding (; ).

Management of CCMs remains a subject of ongoing debate, with conservative or surgical therapy as the main strategies. At the same time, radiotherapy or medication is not a standard therapy and is only used subordinately (; ; ). Surgical resection provides a potential treatment, particularly in cases of recurrent hemorrhage (; ; ; ). Furthermore, surgical resection of CCMs has been shown to reduce seizure recurrence in patients with CCM-associated epilepsy (; ; ). However, surgery itself carries significant perioperative risks of neurological deficits, especially when CCMs are located in the brainstem (; ). Conservative management avoids surgical morbidity but leaves patients at risk for future hemorrhage and progressive neurological deficits (). Moreover, conservative management of previously hemorrhaged CCM can significantly burden patients by revoking driving or machinery operation license for up to two years (; ). Although recent studies emphasize the importance of individualized, risk-adapted decision-making, there is still no universally accepted model for categorizing patients into high- or low-risk groups (; ).

For brainstem CCMs, proposed grading systems include the Lawton–Garcia and Essen/Dammann–Sure systems for estimating surgical outcome and the BSCM score developed by Yang, Bozinov and colleagues to support treatment selection (; ; ). The international Delphi consensus led by Dammann and the updated Alliance guideline emphasize clinical history, anatomical accessibility, surgical timing, and individualized decisions (; ).

Accordingly, we sought to examine hemorrhage during recorded follow-up and postoperative complications in a large single-center cohort of patients with CCMs and to describe functional trajectories after conservative and surgical management. Associations were considered exploratory, not evidence of comparative treatment effectiveness or a validated prediction model.

Methods

Study design

We designed a retrospective study of patients who presented to the Department of Neurosurgery at Heidelberg University Hospital between 2014 and 2021 with a diagnosis of cerebral cavernous malformation (CCM). The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was approved by the local ethics committee under the number S-667/2021. Written informed consent was waived due to the study's retrospective nature. All patients underwent either conservative or surgical treatment after discussion of each case and consensus among the department's senior neurosurgeons, following guidelines and standardized operating procedures. Surgical resections were performed by senior neurosurgeons with experience in CCM surgery. The conservative management consisted of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and clinical follow-up at our outpatient clinic.

Selection considered the clinical presentation, hemorrhage, neurological deficits, lesion location and perceived accessibility, and senior neurosurgical judgment. The exact interval from the latest hemorrhage, brainstem midline crossing, lesion dimensions, and detailed operative anatomy were not systematically encoded. Therefore, their independent influence on selection could not be quantified, and no brainstem grading score was retrospectively reconstructed.

Data collection

Clinical data were retrieved from the institution's electronic medical records. The following data were collected: (1) age at index diagnosis/presentation, (2) sex, (3) symptoms at presentation as well as at the 1st, 2nd and 3rd recorded follow-up, (4) comorbidities, (5) modified Rankin Scale (mRS), (6) localization, (7) hemorrhagic presentation at index diagnosis, (8) multiplicity, (9) presence of developmental venous anomaly (DVA), (10) anticoagulant use at presentation, (11) treatment form, (12) occurrence of postoperative complications, (13) postoperative deficits, and (14) radiographic status at follow-up. The outcome assessment was performed using the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) at baseline, discharge after surgery, and follow-up.

The patient was the unit of analysis. In patients with multiple CCMs, multiplicity was recorded as a patient-level characteristic and localization referred to the index lesion recorded in the dataset; each patient contributed once. Anticoagulant exposure referred to use at index presentation, not a prescription initiated because of the CCM diagnosis. It did not include antiplatelet therapy. Specific agents, continuation, interruption, resumption, and new prescriptions during follow-up were not systematically available.

The mRS was retrospectively reconstructed from contemporaneous neurological examinations, functional descriptions, discharge summaries, and follow-up documentation. The last available valid recorded follow-up score, rather than the lowest score across follow-up, was selected. Follow-up visits were not fixed study time points; the number of available assessments is reported separately for each management group. A valid score could contribute to functional analysis even if its date could not support an interval calculation.

The variable previously labeled “previous bleeding” represented recorded hemorrhagic presentation at index diagnosis, not the number of earlier hemorrhages. Baseline hemorrhagic status was not uniformly adjudicated as symptomatic hemorrhage. A new radiographic hemorrhage during follow-up required an explicit report of new CCM bleeding, rather than stable blood products. Rebleeding denoted a subsequent hemorrhage in a patient with hemorrhagic presentation; a new hemorrhage without baseline hemorrhage was classified separately as a first recorded event. Symptomatic hemorrhage required new or worsened symptoms temporally attributable to the radiographic event. Event timing was based on the dated follow-up record documenting detection; exact onset was not systematically recorded.

Recorded untreated observation began at the recorded diagnosis date and ended at the first new radiographic hemorrhage, surgery, or the last documented clinical/radiological follow-up, whichever occurred first. Non-event observations were censored at surgery or last follow-up. Death was not a systematically captured endpoint. Patients without positive, chronologically useable intervals did not contribute person-time. Surgical dates inconsistent with admission, discharge, or postoperative follow-up chronology were excluded from surgery-dependent interval analyses; no dates were guessed. Postoperative functional follow-up was measured from surgery and conservative follow-up from diagnosis. reports diagnosis-to-follow-up intervals in both groups to identify a common time origin for its mixed-management presentation.

Postoperative neurological complications comprised focal neurological deficits and seizure-related events recorded during the index surgical admission through discharge. They were analyzed separately from perioperative hemorrhagic/CSF-related events, residual-lesion progression, and revision surgery. The recorded principal category was used for the mutually exclusive summary; a combined seizure/aphasia entry was assigned to the seizure-related group. Persistence was reported only when the same deficit remained documented at last follow-up; non-documentation did not establish resolution or permanence. Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with follow-up intervals and ordinal mRS scores reported as median [interquartile range (IQR)]. Baseline group comparisons used Welch's t-test for age, the Mann–Whitney U test for mRS, and Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. These comparisons describe clinical selection rather than treatment effects. Postoperative neurological complications were analyzed only among the 73 surgical patients (20 events). Age was assessed by univariable logistic regression, with an unadjusted odds ratio (OR) per year and Wald 95% CI; categorical associations used Fisher's exact tests and exact conditional ORs with 95% CIs. No finite OR was reported for zero-cell or invariant exposures. No multivariable complication model or ROC-AUC was retained because the small number of events and sparse covariate strata did not support reliable adjusted risk prediction. Hemorrhage incidence was calculated per 100 person-years with exact Poisson 95% CIs. Only two subsequent hemorrhages were identified, precluding multivariable Cox regression. The mRS was treated as ordinal: repeated assessments in complete surgical pairs were evaluated using the Friedman test and pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, with Bonferroni correction for the three comparisons. Conservative baseline-to-follow-up change used a paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Wilcoxon tests used a two-sided normal approximation with tie correction, without continuity correction; zero differences were omitted. These methods do not assume normally distributed mRS values; they do not establish causality or equal clinical spacing between score categories. Localization-specific results were descriptive; McNemar's test and longitudinal ordinal regression were not retained. Missing outcomes were not imputed. Except for the three surgical paired comparisons, analyses were exploratory without multiplicity correction. Statistics for the revision were calculated using SciPy and statsmodels in Python, with matplotlib for figures. Two-sided p-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient data

Our cohort comprised 268 adult patients with cerebral cavernous malformations (CCMs) with a mean age of 44.4 ± 15.2 years, of whom 64.9% were women. The majority of the patients had hemorrhagic presentation (179/268, 66.8%). Motor and cranial nerve deficits at admission were uncommon (16/268, 6.0%, and 17/268, 6.3%, respectively) and were not mutually exclusive with hemorrhage. Most lesions were supratentorial (74.6%), 8.6% of patients had multiple CCMs, and 6.3% had an associated DVA. Anticoagulant use was documented at presentation in 7/268 patients (2.6%); all seven were managed conservatively. Most patients were managed conservatively (195/268, 72.8%). Surgical patients were younger (39.1 ± 14.8 versus 46.4 ± 14.9 years; p < 0.001) and more frequently had hemorrhagic presentation (68/73, 93.2%, versus 111/195, 56.9%; p < 0.001). Postoperative complications

Among surgically treated patients, 53/73 (72.6%) had no recorded postoperative neurological complication. The 20/73 patients (27.4%) with such complications included seizure-related events in 8/73 (11.0%), diplopia in 3/73 (4.1%), and hemiparesis in 3/73 (4.1%) (). One seizure-related event also included aphasia. Other recorded focal deficits occurred in 6/73 patients (8.2%): one each of hemianopsia, hand paresis, dysphagia, hemihypaesthesia, isolated aphasia, and gait disturbance. Seizure-related events do not imply new-onset epilepsy. The same focal deficit remained explicitly documented at last follow-up in 5/73 patients (6.8%); lack of a subsequent symptom entry was not interpreted as definitive resolution. Two perioperative hemorrhagic/CSF-related events were recorded separately: hemorrhage with CSF fistula (n = 1) and epidural hemorrhage with hydrocephalus (n = 1). These categories may overlap with neurological complications and must not be summed as independent patient counts.

Among surgically treated patients, revision surgery was required in three cases (3/73, 4.1%): one each for wound infection, cerebrospinal fluid leak, and resection of a residual lesion following progression. Discussion

Principal findings

This study describes the clinical course and surgical outcomes of CCMs within a tertiary referral cohort. First, patients selected for surgery differed substantially from those managed conservatively, particularly in age and hemorrhagic presentation. Second, postoperative neurological complications were documented in 20/73 surgical patients, but unadjusted associations were imprecise and did not establish independent predictors. Third, functional recovery after surgery was generally favorable in the available paired observations, with greater residual morbidity in brainstem CCMs. Only two new radiographic hemorrhages were documented during recorded untreated observation, of which one was a symptomatic rebleed. These findings do not establish a practical risk-stratification model.

Natural history and rebleeding risk

Our cohort of 268 patients has demographic and clinical features comparable to previously described CCM populations, although referral selection and incomplete follow-up limit representativeness. The mean age of 44 years is consistent with prior reports of symptomatic presentation in middle adulthood (; ; ). Although 64.9% of our cohort were female, larger studies suggest equal sex distribution but report similar patterns (; ; ). Lesion distribution was comparable to that reported in published series, with 75% supratentorial localization (; ). Established CCM natural-history studies identify hemorrhagic presentation and brainstem or deep location as important risk factors (; ; ; ; ), and temporal clustering can influence observed rates (). However, our two subsequent hemorrhages—only one of which was a rebleed—cannot independently confirm these associations or support multivariable modeling. Sex has not consistently been a predictor in previous cohorts (; , ); no sex-specific conclusion can be drawn here. The low recorded rate should not be interpreted as a definitive natural-history estimate because observation was retrospective, some diagnoses preceded referral, and follow-up was incomplete.

Surgical outcomes and interpretation

Surgical treatment was associated with lower recorded mRS scores at follow-up, although this observation does not establish causality. The fact that improvement was not always immediate but continued until follow-up is clinically relevant. Early postoperative deficits, especially following deep or brainstem surgery, should not automatically be interpreted as indicative of poor long-term outcomes (; ; ; ; ). Recovery was more favorable for lobar lesions and less favorable for brainstem CCMs, consistent with previous reports (). Nevertheless, the composite complication rate includes seizure-related events as well as focal deficits, and it is not a rate of permanent surgical disability. The revised univariable age estimate was close to unity, so this cohort does not establish age-related operative risk. Infratentorial complications were numerically more frequent, but the exact CI was wide and included unity. No inference of protection from baseline hemorrhage is justified: only five surgical patients lacked hemorrhagic presentation, and none had a recorded complication. Timing and operative anatomy are recognized considerations (; ), but lesion-specific surgical corridors, cleavage planes, and hemorrhage-to-surgery timing were not systematically captured and cannot explain an association in this dataset.

The clinical question remains the balance between natural history and operative risk. Surgical intervention may be considered when the lesion is accessible and the untreated risk is elevated, such as after symptomatic hemorrhage or in cases of CCM-related epilepsy (; ; ). Conversely, the threshold for intervention should be higher for deep-brainstem lesions, where both the natural history and the surgical procedure are associated with substantial morbidity (; ; ). Our records did not contain a postoperative CCM rebleed, but complete resection was not systematically verified; therefore, the cohort cannot establish durable protection specifically attributable to complete resection. One revision was performed for residual-lesion progression, which is distinct from hemorrhage.

Clinical implications

Overall, our data illustrate the need to consider both untreated disease burden and operative morbidity rather than a binary surgery-versus-observation approach. Anatomical location remains an important consideration in management strategy (; ). For accessible lesions, particularly following symptomatic hemorrhage or in cases of epilepsy, resection may be considered in the context of guideline recommendations and individual circumstances (; ; ). In contrast, brainstem CCMs represent a challenging subgroup because of neurological disease burden and operative risk. These cases require highly selective indications, careful timing, and management in experienced centers (; ; ). The primary value of this study is the description of clinical selection, postoperative events, and functional trajectories in routine care. It does not demonstrate that a new algorithm improves patient selection or validate a bedside risk calculator.

The Lawton–Garcia system incorporates lesion size, axial-midpoint crossing, DVA, age, and hemorrhage-to-surgery interval; the Essen/Dammann–Sure classification is another outcome-oriented tool (; ). The BSCM score instead models treatment-selection considerations (). These instruments and the Dammann Delphi consensus address different aspects of decision-making and do not substitute for detailed case assessment (). With only 10 surgical brainstem cases and incomplete score variables, our cohort neither validates these systems nor supplies an alternative. The revised interpretation is therefore consistent with the updated Alliance guideline (), without proposing a new decision algorithm.

Limitations

We acknowledge several limitations of our study. First, case inclusion was retrospective, based on referral to a tertiary academic center, which may have introduced bias typically associated with similar study protocols. Secondly, treatment allocation was nonrandomized, introducing confounding by indication. While treatment decisions were made by senior consultants of vascular neurosurgery or a multidisciplinary board, surgeons may have selected healthier patients with more accessible lesions, while higher-risk cases were managed conservatively (). Baseline imbalances and natural recovery after a presenting hemorrhage prevent attribution of within-group mRS change to surgery alone. Third, follow-up duration was heterogeneous and ascertainment differed substantially: last-follow-up mRS was available for 68/73 surgical patients but only 63/195 conservative patients. Events treated elsewhere could be missed unless external reports reached our institution. Recorded diagnosis dates sometimes preceded referral; the resulting diagnosis-based intervals are not equivalent to prospectively monitored natural history. Chronologically inconsistent dates were excluded from affected interval calculations without assigning replacement dates. Fourth, only two subsequent radiographic hemorrhages and 20 postoperative neurological complications were identified. The sparse event counts, five nonhemorrhagic surgical cases, small DVA and multiplicity strata, and lack of validation prevent robust multivariable prediction. The small number of patients with baseline anticoagulant exposure also limits conclusions; antiplatelet exposure and longitudinal medication changes were not captured, although previous reports address antithrombotic safety (; ). Fifth, mRS was reconstructed from routine records and follow-up symptom documentation was not standardized; missing values were not imputed and symptom absence was not equated with resolution. Lesion dimensions, eloquence/depth, brainstem sublocation, midline crossing, detailed prior hemorrhage history, operative approach, extent of resection, and standardized postoperative imaging were not consistently available. As a tertiary referral cohort, this series is likely enriched for symptomatic and surgically relevant lesions, which may account for the high rate of hemorrhagic presentation and may limit generalizability to incidental CCMs.

Future directions

This study's limitations identify priorities for future risk-stratification and management research. Multicenter prospective studies with standardized imaging, outcome definitions, and follow-up are essential for developing and validating risk models. Collaborative studies will enable larger, more heterogeneous patient samples and yield more robust, generalizable risk estimates. Incorporating detailed imaging features may further enhance the prediction of future hemorrhage. High-resolution MRI sequences can more accurately quantify lesional permeability, assess surrounding hemosiderin, and detect associated DVAs (; ). Genetic information, such as CCM gene mutations in familial cases, and circulating biomarkers could also further refine individualized risk estimates (; ). After appropriate validation, including these variables in digital risk calculators could enable clinicians to estimate hemorrhage and operative risks in real time, thereby supporting patient-specific decision-making. The present study does not determine the optimal timing of intervention. Cohort studies or decision-analysis models could evaluate whether risk-guided strategies improve outcomes, including patient-reported outcomes and quality-of-life measures (). The effectiveness of emerging therapies, such as stereotactic radiotherapy or medical treatments, should also be explored in defined risk scenarios (; ; ). In summary, progress in CCM management will require collaborative, data-driven approaches, personalized risk assessment tools, and prospectively validated predictive models that are practical and applicable in clinical settings.

Conclusion

In this tertiary-center cohort, patients selected for surgery frequently had hemorrhagic presentation, and favorable functional outcomes were observed in patients with available follow-up, particularly in lobar lesions. Brainstem CCMs retained greater residual functional burden. However, sparse hemorrhage events, incomplete and differential follow-up, and confounding by indication preclude independent risk prediction or causal conclusions about management strategies. The study provides exploratory observations for counseling and prospective investigation, not a validated patient-selection framework.
