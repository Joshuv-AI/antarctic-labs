 ABSTRACT

Permanently installed holdup monitoring would provide a myriad of benefits to nuclear facilities, ranging
among decreased facility burden, increased safety bases, and increased accuracy of relevant material
controls and accountancy metrics. However, the cost per detection system must be affordable to provide
reliable coverage. For this project, the target unit price per system has been $1,000. Recent work has
focused on pushing costs lower through developing a plastic scintillator and silicon photomultiplier
(SiPM)–based front end, which are components that are not commercially available in conjunction with
each other. Other work has included further system development and use of a low-cost SiPM. Finally, a
limited deployment at a processing facility was achieved. Results from this deployment and the
development process are discussed in detail.

 1. INTRODUCTION

The Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) is developing holdup monitors suitable for deployment at
nuclear facilities. During FY 2025, the project has seen two major thrusts: further system development to
bring unit price below $1000/unit and a deployment at Nuclear Fuel Services (NFS) to demonstrate
holdup monitoring within its application space.

Nuclear facilities that process fuel on both the front and back end of the nuclear fuel cycle employ High
Efficiency Particulate Air (HEPA) filtering to prevent the escape of special nuclear material (SNM) into
the surrounding environment. Some escape of material into the ductwork beyond the filters is inevitable.
Current remediation methods involve periodic manual spot checks. Manual checks, however, are often
onerous as there can be hundreds of necessary monitoring locations, and those locations may be difficult
to reach, presenting a safety hazard to facility personnel. Furthermore, mobile manual measurements are
intrinsically prone to systematic errors due to changing detector placements and measurement durations.

Permanently installed holdup monitors, the development of which is described herein, will address these
concerns. Permanently installed detectors would reduce facility burden through reduced staff hours, as
well as provide material reductions in systematic error by measuring directly at the desired location
without the positional deviation inherent to a portable detector. The detectors may also be collimated
reducing contributions from neighboring sources. Furthermore, a permanent, constantly counting system
will provide immediate notification of atypical facility conditions, such as broken filters, that could go
unrecognized for months with periodic measurements.

 2. THE HOLDUP SYSTEM

2.1 SYSTEM DESCRIPTION

2.1.1 General System Description

The broad goal of the holdup monitor effort is to provide inexpensive, user-friendly, modular, quality-
assured detectors that can be permanently installed via a hub system or temporarily placed with long-lived
battery-based units. Considerable focus has been devoted to the ease of use of the ORNL detectors. The
system has also been specifically designed so that it can be modified to a particular facility’s needs, even
if the specifications vary by measurement location, without any change to the back-end hardware.

The hardware and software are integrated in such a way that they start up automatically with appropriate
settings when powered. No proprietary commercial hardware or software is required, and each detector
does not require its own dedicated general-purpose computer, as many commercial devices do. Data are
stored locally with enough storage for months; and, in the case of the hub model, are automatically
exported to an external computer where they are stored in a database for backup and automated analysis
purposes. The design leverages several technologies from the industrial internet of things (IoT) and edge
computing fields which are most suitable for autonomous systems and remote monitoring.

An additional benefit is the unit price point. For locations where it may benefit a facility to have more
detectors with less individual detection efficiency, ORNL is aiming for a unit price of <$1,000 per
detector module by using plastic scintillators. The goal with this low price is to bring the system cost
below the expected employee-hour cost for manual surveys. The detailed cost analysis is presented later
in this report comes in well below the $1,000 goal. For facilities, or measurement locations, that require
higher efficiency, or eventually spectroscopic measurements, the scintillator may be switched to an
inorganic scintillator such as lanthanum bromide (LaBr) or sodium iodide (NaI) with no other changes to
hardware.

Modularity is a focus of this effort with the detectors capable of operating in two modes—namely a hub
mode and stand-alone mode. With further development these individual detectors could also be a basis for
a potentially low-cost gamma-ray imager.

2.1.1.1   Hub Mode

When operating in hub mode, the detector units are designed to be constantly measuring with power and
communications over ethernet (a recent addition in FY25). Data are both saved locally in non-removeable
storage and transmitted back to the central hub node where the status and history of all detectors in the
hub network are displayed. Significant effort has been invested in making the detectors “plug and play”
so that the central hub automatically registers how many and which detectors are connected. There is a
rechargeable battery back-up, also used in the standalone mode, so that the units will continue to operate
even in the absence of facility power. Detector settings are saved in non-volatile memory so that they will
restart immediately on power up should battery power also be lost. The units are self-contained and
collimated on three sides to minimize contributions from sources other than the intended measurement
locations.

Algorithmic development is still underway and scheduled for future fiscal years. The aim of this effort
will be to have a cohesive network of detectors providing up the minute facility status reports. False
positive and negative alarms will be reduced as the detectors will work together to identify and adjust to
changes in background and facility status. Notification of off-normal events both in the facility and in the
detector network will be immediate.

2.1.1.2 Standalone Mode

The holdup monitors have a second mode of operation, standalone mode, that is intended to provide
facilities flexibility for longer term spot measurements or to place detectors in locations where running
cables may not be feasible. The hardware is the same as described above. When in standalone mode the
peripherals (screen/light-up button) may be put to sleep to save power. The unit itself also enters a low-
power mode, going to sleep and only waking to perform periodic measurements, the timing of which is
set by the user. Data are saved entirely locally in non-volatile memory. Currently, the units may run for in
excess of 1 month on a single battery charge when operating in low-power mode.

2.1.2 Previous FYs Developments

Previous fiscal years focused on the back end of the data acquisition electronics and initial field tests of
the system. Commercial detectors, NaI scintillators from Luxium with SiPM readout, were used. This
allowed focus to be placed on the low-power micro-controller-based acquisition. This effort proved
highly successful as units, even with unoptimized commercial detectors, could run for a month or more at
a time on battery. Furthermore, power and communications over universal serial bus (USB) were used as
the USB protocol was easier to implement than the currently used power and communications over
ethernet.

2.2 CURRENT SYSTEM DEVELOPMENTS

After a successful demonstration of the acquisition system, this FY has seen development focus shift to
the front-end portion of the detector. The holdup monitors, shown in Figure 1, now do not require
commercially packaged scintillators with integrated SiPMs, making them lower cost, lower power, and
scintillator agnostic. The scintillator is by far the most expensive portion of the monitors, organic
scintillators in particular range from $1,000-$15,000 each for a 2” NaI or LaBr respectively. Plastics are
significantly cheaper, costing $50/unit for a 2” detector at bulk pricing. Being scintillator agnostic allows
for application and cost flexibility, which allows our holdup system to meet the needs of a broader range
of potential end users.

 Figure 1. FY25 detectors packed for their deployment at NFS. Pipe mounts are separate.

2.2.1 Scintillators

The scintillators for FY25 are 2” EJ200-NF organic plastic scintillators from Eljen Technologies. EJ200-
NF was chosen both for its price point, a 2” diameter by 2” length crystal is only $50 when bought in
bulk, and for its toughness. These scintillators, unlike other organic scintillators, are shock and
temperature resistant. The NF designation at the end of the model number indicates that they are also

resistant to fogging, a known issue in plastic scintillators where humidity in the air seeps into the crystal
over time resulting in an apparent haze that interferes with scintillator performance [1]. EJ200-NF is,
however, both non-spectroscopic and approximately 1/3 as efficient as an equivalent volume of NaI. To
recover some efficiency a range of scintillator lengths were procured to allow adjustments to the detection
volume, a sampling of which is shown in Figure 2. Because smaller SiPMs are required to meet the
$1000/unit price point goal, some scintillators are also tapered to match the SiPM size. A full comparison
with the standard flat-faced scintillator will occur in future years, but the aim of the taper was to improve
the light collection and further recover some efficiency in the detector.

 Figure 2. Two-inch EJ200-NF scintillators in range of lengths and with and without tapers (integrated light guides).

Because the scintillators were purchased bare, the SiPMs had to be mounted onto the crystals and the
crystals had to be wrapped. A photo of the scintillators pre and post wrapping is shown in Figure 3. The
wrapping included 4 layers of matte white polytetraflouroethylene (teflon) tape to provide a non-spectral
reflection surface, a step necessary to eliminate intense reflections within the scintillator. The crystals
were then wrapped with an outside layer of black electrical tape to prevent the entrance of outside light
into the detector. While this is standard practice for lab-prepared scintillators, it is quite onerous, delicate,
and time consuming. In future FYs we will explore alternate methods for scintillator preparation
including dipping the scintillators in flat matte white paint with an outer vinyl wrap to ease the process.

2.2.2 SiPM Array and Readout

The initial effort for FY25 was to develop custom front-end electronics to reduce cost and power while
supporting different scintillator types for both gross counting and spectroscopic applications. During the
first half of the year, we prototyped the initial design of the readout electronics and detector power
supply. The initial prototype leveraged a connectorized commercial-off-the-shelf (COTS) high fill-factor
SiPM 4x4 array (ARRAYJ-30035-16P-PCB) based on the Onsemi J-Series SiPM sensors (MICROFJ-
30035-TSC). A printed circuit board (PCB) for the initial design of the preamplifier and pulse shaping
electronics was designed, manufactured, and tested with the COTS SiPM array.

The readout electronics incorporates comparators with adjustable hysteresis on both the preamplifier and
pulse shaped output signals. Threshold voltages on both can be set via digital-to-analog converters
(DACs). The comparator with suitable threshold on the preamplifier output can be used to detect high
count rate overload due to pile-up while the comparator on the pulse shaped signal is used for gross
counting. In addition, a 16-bit analog-to-digital converter (ADC) was included to sample the pulse shaped
signal to develop future spectroscopic capabilities.

In the second half of the year, we designed a custom SiPM array with roughly the same active sensing
area, but increased gain and fill-factor leveraging the larger MICROFJ-60035-TSV SiPM in a 2x2 array
configuration. In addition to better performance, the custom SiPM array reduced cost by an order of
magnitude and resolved supply chain issues compared to the COTS array which is primarily for low
volume prototyping. In addition, the analog readout electronics were further refined and integrated with
the digital system and detector power supply, discussed in future sections on a single PCB. The SiPM
array and connector is shown in Figure 3.

 Figure 4 A custom 2x2 SiPM array and connector.

2.2.3 Cockroft Walton SiPM Power Supply

To achieve both low cost and low power, a custom SiPM power supply was developed. Additional
requirements included high voltage output stability at microamp output currents, very low output voltage
ripple, and minimal switching-noise radiation. The required output voltage for the OnSemi SiPMs was
determined to be between 30-35 V, and the current draw for low rates was less than 50 uA. The chosen
design was an 8-stage Cockroft-Walton (CW) voltage multiplier, because of the simple AC drive
requirements (25 kHz square wave, above human hearing) and the extremely low current requirements for
the SiPM while allowing a voltage boost from 4-5 V to over 40 V using a switched-mode power supply.
The output voltage set point was implemented with a digital control loop which senses the existing SiPM
supply voltage with an ADC. The desired voltage is then output to the loop from a DAC based on linear
calibration curves which eventually will also correct for temperature sensitivity of the SiPM. A prototype
system tested in the first half of the year using a microcontroller development board (STM32H745I-
DISCO) for control of the analog front end and power supply is shown in Figure 4. The prototype design

presently requires 1.4 mA supply current and delivers only 870 uV rms ripple to the detector. An
invention disclosure has been submitted on this circuitry.

 Figure 5 Initial system prototype of readout electronics and detector power supply

2.2.4 Digital Processing and Control

A significant portion of the effort in the second half of FY25 was developing the hardware and firmware
for the digital processing and control of the new detector design. In addition to control of the detector
electronics, this also includes system level support for communications, data storage, power management,
and the human-machine interface (HMI). The design uses the NXP MCXN947 series microcontroller
based on dual high-performance Arm® Cortex®-M33 cores running up to 150 MHz, with 2 MB of flash
with optional full ECC RAM, a DSP co-processor and an integrated NPU. The processor is integrated
with the readout electronics, power supplies, mixed-signal electronics (e.g. ADCs, DACs, comparators)
and other peripherals on an 85 mm by 65 mm PCB shown in Figure 6.

 Figure 6 First revision of fully integrated holdup monitor design.

The design initially focused on leveraging power-over-ethernet (PoE) and IP networking for data
collection and monitoring. However, as we became aware of facility requirements in the second half of
the year, we began to adapt the design to focus more on battery power and local storage. The design
includes 10/100 Ethernet with PoE for communications and power, but the physical layer is powered
down by default to reduce power consumption. A battery charger is also incorporated which can charge
and power the system from either PoE, USB, or an external power supply or suitable solar panel. The
charger regulates the system input power from these sources and/or the removable 10000 mAh lithium-
ion battery when available. Several more switching and linear regulators generate the various voltages
required by the analog and digital subsystems and many can be disabled by software to further reduce
power consumption when not needed. A small rechargeable coin cell battery allows the system to retain
the system time and a limited set of registers when the primary power source is disconnected. The HMI
includes an RGB LED push button and OLED display. An accelerometer is also available to detect
movement of the detector.

The firmware for the digital subsystem is based on the open-source Zephyr real-time operating system
(RTOS) which provides a kernel with real-time capable scheduling policies and a platform with vendor
agnostic device driver models and an ecosystem of libraries for developing complex and modular
applications for embedded systems. A digital control loop for the SiPM power supply using the integrated
ADC and DAC peripherals of the microcontroller. The control loop is implemented as a high-priority
thread on the primary processor for less computationally demanding gross counting applications or
optionally on the secondary co-processor for the spectroscopic application requires more demanding
signal processing on the main processor and DSP accelerator. The gross counting function requires almost
no intervention by the processor because it is implemented using the advanced integrated timer
peripherals. We have implemented only basic power management which allows the device to enter a low-
power sleep state and wakeup via a low-power timer or external pushbutton. The time duration the device
sleeps and counts are configurable at runtime and, by default, is configured to count for 5 minutes out of
every hour. An external 16 MB NOR FLASH and 128 MB NAND FLASH is available for data storage.
The front and back of the first revision of PCB is shown in Figure 6.

2.2.7 Collimation

 Figure 11. 3D printed collimator molds for the holdup monitors.

It is expected that any given measurement point within a facility will have significant background
contributions from nearby sources. To ensure that each holdup monitor is only measuring its correct
location, the detectors are heavily collimated. The cables between electronics and the SiPM are routed
circuitously through the collimator to eliminate any direct line of sight to the detector from any face but
the forward one, as shown in Figure 10. It should be noted that traditional machined collimators are
expensive. To solve this issue, the collimators in the holdup monitors are 3D printed molds, shown in
Figure 11, that are then packed with tungsten grit. The tungsten grit is filled with epoxy, Figure 12, to
generate a solid collimating volume.

 Figure 12. One of the collimator molds filled with tungsten grit and epoxy.

2.2.8 Spectroscopic Detector Development

Initial testing of the front-end electronics with a commercial multichannel analyzer and a NaI(Tl)
scintillator showed it could also be used for a spectroscopic system. The microprocessor used in the latest
design was chosen to support the digital signal processing (DSP) needed for the MCA while still being
capable of low-power operation needed for the standalone gross counter. A 2 MSPS 16-bit ADC on the
buffered output of the pulse shaped signal can optionally be enabled for spectroscopic capability.
Significant device driver and signal processing software support needs to be developed for this capability,
and we are attempting to continue developing this in the remainder of the year under projects with similar
scope. Future funding will be needed for full functionality. The current prototype spectroscopic system
coupled to a NaI(Tl) crystal is shown in Figure 13.

2.3 COST BREAKDOWN

During year 1 of this work package, extensive discussions were held with potential industry partners,
namely BWX Technologies and its subsidiary, Nuclear Fuel Services (NFS). In these discussions,
industry partners indicated that any holdup monitoring system they deployed would need to be as or more
cost effective than periodic spot holdup measurements. They specified that a per unit price of $1,000
would be considered ideal. As mentioned in Section 2.1, a major focus of work this FY has been on
developing the front-end electronics to enable just such a low-cost system. The two biggest factors in unit
price are the scintillator and the SiPM. Assuming a 2” plastic scintillator ($50/unit at 1,000 units) and a
17 mm SiPM ($114/unit at 1,000 units) the total unit price is $491, less than half of the ideal number
quoted by industry partners. The full breakdown of cost by component is shown in Table 1. Here

detector. The case is 3D printed with the shielding including the cost of the tungsten grit and epoxy used
to make the collimator. Costs could be further reduced by using less collimation, as the tungsten grit is
expensive.

2.4 MONTE CARLO SIMULATION TOOL

A monte carlo simulation tool has been developed as an aid for facilities to better understand optimal
placement and choice of detector around their monitoring locations. The simulation has been developed
with the GEANT4 toolkit. The simulation tool produces four executables, three different simulation
geometries and one to allow for proper energy smearing of the raw output. The simulations utilize typical
macro files from standard GEANT4 applications, and the primary particle generator has been modified
from the built-in general particle source to allow for inputting of ENSDF json files or general text files
containing binned spectra. The simulations output ROOT files containing a single tree for each of the
detector's cumulative energy deposit for each event.

The modeled geometry of the detectors has been developed using the available drawings from the
manufacturer in combination with x-ray images, as seen in Figure 14, to provide further information
where the drawings were lacking information.

 Figure 14. X-ray image of 1 cc. CZT detector from Mirion (left) and 2” x 2” NaI(Tl) detector (right).

The combined information from the drawings and x-ray image were used to develop the initial
implementation of the material composition and dimensions for the detectors. These models were verified
against measurements of radioactive sources including 133Ba, 60Co, 137Cs, and 241Am The measurement

setup is shown in Figure 15 and Figure 16, detectors were mounted to an 80/20 frame to minimize the
error associated with their placement in the simulation space as well as providing a consistent reference
point for measuring the distance between the source and detectors. The source is held upright with foam
in a groove cut in a PMMA sheet. The groove provides a consistent reference surface that minimizes
placement error within the simulation space. The foam and PMMA were chosen to minimize gamma ray
scattering as well as to hold the source centered within the pipe. The pipe is representative of those within
facilities as to provide insight into the attenuation of gamma rays passing through.

Figure 15. Top view of the verification setup. From top to bottom the detectors are 1.5” x 1.5” LaBr(Ce) from Luxium, 1 cc. CZT
 from Mirion, and 2” x 2” NaI(Tl) from Luxium.

 Figure 16. Side view of the verification setup. Source being measured is placed inside of representative pipe.

A render of the final geometry implemented for the verification measurements is shown in Figure 17.
The simulated response to Co-60 and measured data for the LaBr(Ce) detector are shown in Figure 18.
The measured data has been background subtracted which also removes the inherent internal activity. The
oscillations in Figure 18 around 50 keV and above 1400 keV are caused by slight gain shifts between the
background measurement and measured Co-60 spectrum, aside from these features which are expected
when performing background subtraction there is excellent agreement between the simulated response
and measured spectrum. Similar agreement can be seen for the other two detectors as well.

2.5 FUTURE SYSTEM DEVELOPMENTS

2.5.1 Further Development for Spectroscopic Detectors

An initial spectroscopic-capable acquisition system is designed. FY26 plans include non-trivial firmware
developments including additional device drivers and DSP implementation. A spectroscopic-capable
detector will also require pole-zero adjustment circuitry and a bias correction circuitry to stabilize the
SiPM in the presence of changes to ambient temperature.

2.5.2 Environmental Testing

One of the goals of the holdup monitor project is to ruggedize the detectors, enabling their placement both
indoors and outdoors (currently the detectors may only be used indoors). SiPMs are notoriously
temperature sensitive, demonstrating large gain drifts in response to temperature changes. This effect can
be remedied with the inclusion of a temperature sensor coupled to gain adjustment circuitry. Many
commercial SiPM-based radiation detectors already contain just such temperature compensation circuitry.

The first step in accounting for these drifts is to place the entire detector inside a temperature chamber and
record how gain shifts versus temperature. The whole detector is used to catch and account for smaller
drifts from components other than the SiPM, such as the scintillator which also have some temperature-
dependence in their response. This work will be performed in FY26, with temperature chamber
measurements performed early in the FY to enable design and testing of temperature compensation
circuitry later in the FY.

2.5.3 Large-Scale Inexpensive Plastic-based Gamma-Ray Imager

Extensive effort has been expended thus far on this project to make detectors that can work together as a
system. With future work, the individual detectors could be leveraged to serve as single pixels of a large-
format imaging array. Generally, imaging arrays are small, making the field of view and resolution of the
detector highly limited. Making an array out of plastic scintillators could make a larger array financially
feasible. Such an array could be highly useful in conditions where measurement locations may not be
reachable, space it too tight to place a monitor, or there are too many monitoring locations that are too
close together for the collimation on the monitors to allow for discrimination between them. It could also
be useful in other applications that require or can benefit from for mapping of facility radiation fields
beyond the standardly expected holdup points. Furthermore, ORNL has extensive expertise in imaging
arrays and their necessary data analysis algorithms.

 3. NFS DEPLOYMENT

3.1 DEPLOYMENT DESCRIPTION

Nine detectors were deployed to Nuclear Fuel Services (NFS) on August 7th, 2025 and installed by the
facility on August 13th, 2025. NFS installed the detectors on HEPA filters that are known to accumulate
holdup, inlets to those filters, a damper, and other duct locations around the facility where holdup is
known to occur. A preliminary data analysis is included below.

3.2 DEPLOYED DETECTOR DESCRIPTION

The detectors deployed to NFS, numbered 01 to 09, were battery-powered standalone models with the
ethernet ports disabled per the facility’s request. The internals of this year’s unit are shown in Figure 20.

 Figure 20. Single deployed unit with electronics removed for display.

3.3 PRE-INSTALLATION TESTING

All detector measurement capabilities and accuracy were thoroughly tested prior to deployment. A suite
of Python and C++ (firmware) software and firmware were developed to automate this process. The
PCBs were individually evaluated. SiPM preamplifier gains were set to detect gamma rays at a minimum
energy of ~50 keV. Pole-zero values were also adjusted to minimize signal ringing, which could
artificially increase count rates. Following these adjustments, the detectors could be assembled into their
housings.

3.3.1 Threshold Setting

The next step in detector preparation involved setting the trigger threshold, a setting that governs the
minimum gamma ray energy detected. Thresholds values were found empirically for each detector, as
small variations in detector-to-detector gains are to be expected, through an automated sweep algorithm
that is a hybrid combination of C-based internal firmware on the device and a Python wrapper program.
The Python program handled communication with the device and plotting threshold data. Leaving
threshold as an adjustable value would allow a given facility to exclude undesired contributions to
detector counts, an example would be setting the threshold above 100 keV to exclude 241Am
contributions.

For this deployment, thresholds were found with a 235U source 2” away from detector face, as shown in
Figure 21.

 Figure 21. Picture of source and detector setup for finding threshold values.

The sweep counted for 60 seconds at each threshold interval, recording once a second. The first and last
measurement were removed in case the threshold was still in flux during that count providing 58
datapoints per threshold setting. Following the sweep, two metrics were used to find the appropriate
threshold value: average counts versus threshold for each 60 second counting period and variance (chi-
squared) of those counts versus threshold. The measured counts per second versus threshold for detector
02 is shown in Figure 22.

 Figure 22. Average counts (per 60 second counting period) versus threshold for detector 02.

This metric shows the average counts rising as the trigger value gets closer to this detector’s baseline
value, indicating that more counts from the 235U source are being measured, before dropping precipitously
to zero. This drop to zero indicates that the threshold is at or above baseline and the trigger is always
positive. A threshold value of 4570 mV was chosen based on this graph to provide a slight buffer against
temperature-driven gain shifts in the detector.

The second metric, variance versus threshold, was used as a final check of the selected threshold value. It
is useful because as the detector enters the noisy region surrounding the baseline, the distribution of the
count rates from the detector will no longer be Poisson in nature and the variance will diverge from one.
An example of the results from this test with detector 02 is shown in Figure 23. Here a threshold value of
4,570 mV is confirmed as a good threshold choice as the chi-squared value was ~0.9 with variance then
dropping as the threshold approached baseline.

 Figure 23. Variance in counts per second over each 60 second threshold counting period versus threshold.

3.3.2 Source Testing

After setting the threshold each unit collected 1 minute of data on a variety of sources placed at the face
of the detector: 241Am, 137Cs, 60Co, 235U. These sources represent a broad array of gamma ray energies,
from ~50 keV for 241Am to 1.3 MeV for 60Co. This step was performed to ensure that each detector was

counting roughly the same number of counts for each source, and that threshold values were correctly set.
Both were observed to be true for each detector.

3.3.3 Collimation

Finally, collimation on the detector units were tested with a 235U source, chosen as that is the material the
detectors are expected to encounter at NFS. It was observed that the source at the uncollimated face of the
detector produced ~3,000 cps in each detector. When that same source was moved to the collimated sides
and rear face only ~20 cps was observed. This is 5-10 cps above room background and represents a two
order of magnitude decrease in signal from the sides and back of the detector, indicating that the detectors
are well collimated for their anticipated source.

3.4 PRELIMINARY RESULTS

The batteries on the detectors died before expected, it was discovered upon recovering them that most of
the detectors had been in a standby mode. As the standby mode could be entered into with an extra press
of the button on the front the devices, this error can be addressed with a small firmware change. This
setting was only intended for use during testing and can be disabled by default and a small change will
render the mode unable to be entered via the push button on the detector. ORNL is already talking to NFS
about re-deploying with updated firmware at the beginning of FY26. This also represents a major lesson
learned and demonstrated that the worst-case power estimation was accurate. This suggests our runtime
with power management enabled, estimated to be ~1 month, will also be accurate. Most detectors only
captured a short amount of data once the batteries started running out of charge. It is believed that the
power brownouts from low battery reset the units which by design would then enter the default
deployment mode and left them to start recording counts. However, one detector, detector 06 did manage
to capture six days of data which is enough for an initial analysis.

 4. CONCLUSIONS

4.1 SUMMARY

The primary goal of FY25 was to meet the $1,000/unit price goal set by discussions with industry
partners in the first year of the holdup monitor effort. This was met with resounding success as the current
unit cost is $491.87 assuming bulk component pricing. This was achieved through developing a custom
front-end acquisition eliminating the need to employ costly pre-packaged scintillators. This step provided
the additional benefit of making the system scintillator agnostic, allowing any potential end users the
flexibility to tune the system to their particular facility’s needs. A Monte-Carlo-based tool has been
developed to aid facilities in making this decision.

Spectroscopic-capable detector development is underway. A prototype PCB has been designed in house,
with further development planned for next FY.

Finally, a limited deployment of nine standalone detector units at NFS was achieved during this fiscal
year. Despite an issue with the firmware allowing the detectors to be disabled, one detector, detector 06,
collected six days worth of data. This option will be disabled in the firmware in the future and represents
a major lesson learned. ORNL has reached out to NFS about redeploying the units for longer data
collects.

4.2 OUTLOOK

Planned FY26 activities include further development of the spectroscopic-capable detector acquisition. A
first prototype has been built but will require features such as pole-zero correction and DAQ firmware.

A large push is planned to make the detector units capable of being deployed outdoors. As SiPMs are
known to drift with temperature, the front-end acquisition will require temperature-compensation
circuitry, a common practice already employed in commercially available detectors from Luxium. The
temperature response will be mapped through placing the detectors in temperature chambers at the start of
FY26 with the compensation circuitry to follow.

Further facility deployments are planned with the specific locations yet to be determined. These
deployments will aid in planned algorithm development to calculate holdup live while simultaneously
adjusting for fluctuating background contributions and detecting malfunctioning units.

Finally, the team believes it could be highly beneficial to leverage the ethernet-based detector system into
a large-format plastic-scintillator-based gamma-ray imager. Such a unit could fill a significant hole in
current capabilities as imaging units are generally small and expensive (>$100k). An imager could be
mounted permanently to a wall to map facility radiation fields and detect holdup in locations where it is
not feasible to mount individual detectors.
