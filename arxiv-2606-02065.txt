Inverting Poisson-Laguerre tessellations
Thomas van der Jagt, Geurt Jongbloed, Martina Vittorietti

Abstract
While it is well-known how to compute the cells of a Laguerre tessellation for a given set of weighted generator points, it is not obvious how to invert a Laguerre tessellation. That is, given that one observes a Laguerre tessellation, how can one retrieve the weighted generators corresponding to the observed cells. In this paper, we consider inversion of a class of random Laguerre tessellations known as Poisson-Laguerre tessellations. The weighted generators of observed cells of a Poisson-Laguerre tessellation are of interest because knowledge of these weighted generators is useful for statistical inference of Poisson-Laguerre tessellations. For general Laguerre tessellations we provide a characterization of all configurations of weighted generator points which yield the same Laguerre tessellation. For Poisson-Laguerre tessellations we propose a method for consistent inversion, meaning that as one observes the tessellation through increasing observation windows, a closer approximation of the original weighted generators can be obtained. In a simulation study we examine both performance of the inversion procedure, as well as the use of the obtained approximated weighted generators for nonparametrically estimating the weight distribution function corresponding to a Poisson-Laguerre tessellation.

Introduction

Voronoi tessellations and their weighted generalizations have been successfully applied in a wide range of applications and fields. We refer to
[
19
,
23
]
for an overview of these tessellations and examples of their applications. In this paper we focus on a weighted generalization of the Voronoi tessellation known as the Laguerre tessellation. From an application point of view, Laguerre tessellations have shown to be accurate models of materials microstructures for a range of different materials. See for instance
[
9
]
,
[
15
]
,
[
16
]
, and
[
29
]
for examples.

The Voronoi tessellation is defined via a set of points, referred to as the generators, and each generator corresponds to a cell in the tessellation. For Laguerre tessellations, the generators are weighted points. As opposed to generators in the Voronoi model, not all generators will necessarily generate a cell in the Laguerre setting. That is, the cell corresponding to some generator may be the empty set. For Voronoi- and Laguerre tessellations algorithms have been developed for computing the cells in the tessellation for a given set of generator points, see also
[
19
]
and
[
2
]
. Additionally, in the case of the Voronoi tessellation it is known how to perform the inverse process. That is, given some description of the cells of a Voronoi tessellation it is possible to retrieve the generator points used to obtain this Voronoi tessellation. See for instance
[
24
]
for an algorithm to perform this procedure. In the case of Laguerre tessellations it is not possible to uniquely determine the set of weighted generator points that was used to obtain the Laguerre tessellation. More specifically, in
[
8
]
it was shown via simulations that multiple configurations of weighted points may result in the same Laguerre tessellation, meaning that Laguerre tessellations are overparameterized. Questions related to the reconstruction and inversion of Laguerre tessellations have also been considered from several different perspectives in the literature. In
[
4
,
5
]
, the authors study a related problem through semi-discrete convex order and establish reconstruction results for Laguerre tessellations based on cell volumes and centers of mass, revealing a close connection between reconstruction and inversion. In a more applied setting,
[
21
]
proposes a reconstruction procedure for polycrystalline materials from incomplete data, where weighted generator points are estimated using the Cross-Entropy Method. Furthermore, practical optimization-based approaches for fitting Laguerre-type polyhedral structures to experimental data have also been developed, see for instance
[
22
]
.

In Figure
1
two configurations of generators are shown which yield the same Laguerre tessellation. The circle around each generator point has radius equal to the weight of this generator. To the best of our knowledge there is no known result which presents a characterization of all configurations of weighted points which yield the same Laguerre tessellation. The process of retrieving generators from a given Voronoi- or Laguerre tessellation is referred to as inverting the Voronoi- or Laguerre tessellation, which explains the title of this paper.

:
Example highlighting the overparameterization of a Laguerre tessellation.

In the literature, various instances of random Laguerre tessellations have been studied, see
[
14
]
,
[
11
]
and
[
26
]
. These random Laguerre tessellations are obtained by taking the weighted generator points to be a realization of a (marked) point process. The fact that it is not possible to uniquely recover the weighted generator points of a given Laguerre tessellation is somewhat disappointing in the context of statistical inference for random Laguerre tessellations. This being the case because the usual approach to performing statistical inference for random Laguerre tessellations is to consider the point process of weighted generators corresponding to observed cells to be known, and to then use these points to estimate the parameter(s) of the underlying point process model. This approach is for instance taken in
[
26
]
and
[
27
]
.

In this paper we first study general Laguerre tessellations, and then we shift our focus towards Poisson-Laguerre tessellations. For general Laguerre tessellations, we show that under a set of commonly satisfied regularity conditions it is possible to fully characterize all configurations of weighted generator points which yield the same Laguerre tessellation. Poisson-Laguerre tessellations were first studied in
[
14
]
and
[
13
]
. A Poisson-Laguerre tessellation in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is obtained by taking the weighted generators to be a realization of a Poisson (point) process on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times E
for some set
E\subset\mathbb{R}
. Throughout, we consider the choice
E=(0,\infty)
, and we assume that the underlying Poisson process
\eta
has intensity measure
\nu_{d}\times\mathbb{F}
. Here,
\nu_{d}
is Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathbb{F}
is a locally finite measure on
(0,\infty)
. While a Poisson-Laguerre tessellation is a tessellation of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, in many practical settings it is common to take a so-called observation window
W\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and to only observe a part of the tessellation through this window. We provide sufficient conditions for being able to consistently invert the observed Poisson-Laguerre tessellation, as the observation window expands unboundedly to the whole space. This essentially means that while it is in general not possible to uniquely determine the weighted generator points of a Laguerre tessellation, if one observes a Poisson-Laguerre tessellation one can get a closer and closer approximation of the original weighted generator points corresponding to observed cells as one observes the Poisson-Laguerre tessellation through observation windows of increasing sizes.

Taking these results into account, we are interested in whether the estimators for the distribution function
F(z)=\mathbb{F}((0,z])
,
z\geq 0
, as proposed in
[
27
]
still perform well if these estimators are computed based on our proposed approximation of the generator points instead of the true generator points. That is, we want to know if it is possible to estimate
F
, when we only rely on the cells of the tessellation which are (partially) observed through the observation window, without prior knowledge of the generators. We address this question via a simulation study.

In a practical context, it is often the case that one does not actually have Laguerre tessellation data. Instead, one may have some image data, for example a microscopic image of a materials microstructure. Then, one may fit a Laguerre tessellation to this image data. We refer to
[
1
]
and references therein for an overview of methods which can fit a unique Laguerre tessellation to image data. Typically, these procedures also provide a configuration of weighted generators which generate the fitted Laguerre tessellation. If one has a Laguerre tessellation without a corresponding configuration of weighted generators, then algorithm 1 in
[
8
]
may be used to obtain such a configuration. Once a fitting procedure is performed one may apply the methodology proposed in this paper, to obtain a specific configuration of the weighted generators which is suitable for statistical analysis. This approach will especially be sensible if the image data at hand may approximately be considered a realization of a Poisson-Laguerre tessellation.

This paper is organized as follows. In section
2
we introduce necessary notation and definitions. We present a characterization of the overparameterization of Laguerre tessellations in section
3
. Inspired by this result we propose a method for inverting a Poisson-Laguerre tessellation in section
4
. Sufficient conditions for consistent inversion of Poisson-Laguerre tessellations are given in section
5
. Then, we perform some simulations in section
6
. Here, we apply the proposed inversion procedure and use the resulting approximation of the weighted generators to compute estimates of
F
. Finally, we provide some conclusions in section
7
.

Preliminaries

In this section we introduce necessary notation and definitions. Let

denote Lebesgue measure on

\sigma_{d-1}
Lebesgue measure on the sphere

B(x,r)=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|x-y\|<r\}

. Let

denote the volume of the

-dimensional unit ball:

\kappa_{d}:=\nu_{d}\left(\bar{B}(0,1)\right)=\frac{2\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}}{\Gamma\left(1+\frac{d}{2}\right)}.

Let
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then the sum of sets is defined as:
A+B=\{a+b:a\in A,b\in B\}
. If
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we also write:

. For

.

2.1
Point processes

We now introduce several definitions related to point processes. For more background on the theory of point processes we refer to
[
6
]
,
[
7
]
and
[
12
]
. Suppose
\mathbb{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty)
, and let
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space. A measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{X}
is locally finite if
\mu(B)<\infty
for all bounded
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{X})
. Here,
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{X})
denotes the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of
\mathbb{X}
. Let
\mathbf{N}(\mathbb{X})
denote the space of locally finite counting measures (integer-valued measures) on
\mathbb{X}
. We equip
\mathbf{N}(\mathbb{X})
with the usual
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{X})
, which is the smallest
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathbf{N}(\mathbb{X})
such that the mappings
\mu\mapsto\mu(B)
are measurable for all
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{X})
. A point process on
\mathbb{X}
is a random element
\eta
of
(\mathbf{N}(\mathbb{X}),\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{X}))
, that is a measurable mapping
\eta:\Omega\to\mathbf{N}(\mathbb{X})
. The intensity measure of a point process
\eta
on
\mathbb{X}
is the measure
\Lambda
defined by
\Lambda(B):=\mathbb{E}(\eta(B))
,
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{X})
.

Definition 1
.

Suppose
\Lambda
is a
\sigma
-finite measure on
\mathbb{X}
. A Poisson process with intensity measure
\Lambda
is a point process
\eta
on
\mathbb{X}
with the following two properties:

1.

For every
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{X})
, the random variable
\eta(B)
is Poisson distributed with mean
\Lambda(B)
.

2.

For every
m\in\mathbb{N}
and pairwise disjoint sets
B_{1},\dots,B_{m}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{X})
, the random variables
\eta(B_{1}),\dots,\eta(B_{m})
are independent.

Let
\delta
denote the Dirac measure, hence for
x\in\mathbb{X}
and
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{X})
:
\delta_{x}(B)=\mathds{1}\{x\in B\}
. A counting measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{X}
is called simple if
\mu(\{x\})\leq 1
for all
x\in\mathbb{X}
. Similarly, a point process
\eta
on
\mathbb{X}
is called simple if
\mathbb{P}\left(\eta(\{x\})\leq 1,\text{ }\forall x\in\mathbb{X}\right)=1
. Let
\mathbf{N}_{s}(\mathbb{X})
be the subset of
\mathbf{N}(\mathbb{X})
containing all simple measures. Define:
\mathcal{N}_{s}(\mathbb{X}):=\{A\cap\mathbf{N}_{s}(\mathbb{X}):A\in\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{X})\}
. Then, a simple point process on
\mathbb{X}
is a random element
\eta
of
(\mathbf{N}_{s}(\mathbb{X}),\mathcal{N}_{s}(\mathbb{X}))
. Simple point processes can be identified with their support, meaning that one may view the point process as a random set of discrete points in
\mathbb{X}
. We may, for example, write
x\in\eta
instead of
x\in\mathrm{supp}(\eta)
. It is common practice to switch between the interpretations of a simple point process as a random counting measure or as a random set of points, depending on whichever interpretation is more convenient. We will also do this throughout this paper. Enumerating the points of a simple point process in a measurable way we may write:

\eta=\{x_{1},x_{2},\dots\},\text{ \quad and \quad}\eta=\sum_{i=1}^{\eta(\mathbb{X})}\delta_{x_{i}}.

For
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
let
S_{v}
denote the shift operator. Suppose
\eta=\{(x_{1},h_{1}),(x_{2},h_{2}),\dots\}
is a point process with
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
h_{i}>0
. Then, we define
S_{v}\eta:=\{(x_{1}-v,h_{1}),(x_{2}-v,h_{2}),\dots\}
. We call a point process
\eta
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
stationary if
S_{v}\eta
and
\eta
are equal in distribution for all
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Throughout,
(W_{n})_{n\geq 1}
denotes a so-called convex averaging sequence. That is, each
W_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is convex and compact, and the sequence is increasing:
W_{n}\subset W_{n+1}
. Finally, the sequence
(W_{n})_{n\geq 1}
expands unboundedly in all directions:
\sup\{r\geq 0:B(x,r)\subset W_{n}\text{ for some }x\in W_{n}\}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
.

2.2
Laguerre tessellations

In this section we describe general Laguerre tessellations as well as Poisson-Laguerre tessellations. By a tessellation of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
we mean a countable collection
T=\{C_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}\}
, of sets
C_{i}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
(the cells of the tessellation) satisfying some conditions. Specifically, the
C_{i}
’s have pairwise disjoint interiors, the union of all
C_{i}
’s equals
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the collection
T
is locally finite and each
C_{i}
is a compact and convex set with interior points. Local finiteness of
T
means that only finitely many
C_{i}
’s have a non-empty intersection with any bounded
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We should note that (Laguerre) tessellations are often defined on some bounded domain
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, instead of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then,
T
should be a finite collection instead and the union of the
C_{i}
’s equals
A
.

Now, we introduce the Laguerre diagram. Let
\varphi=\{(x_{i},h_{i})\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, with
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
h_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
. Assume moreover that
x_{i}\neq x_{j}
for
i\neq j
. The Laguerre cell associated with
(x,h)\in\varphi
is defined as:

C((x,h),\varphi)=\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|y-x\|^{2}+h\leq\|y-x^{\prime}\|^{2}+h^{\prime}\text{ for all }(x^{\prime},h^{\prime})\in\varphi\right\}.

(1)

For
i\in\mathbb{N}
, the cell
C((x_{i},h_{i}),\varphi)
may be written as the intersection of half spaces:

C((x_{i},h_{i}),\varphi)=\bigcap_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:2\langle y,x_{j}-x_{i}\rangle\leq\|x_{i}\|^{2}-\|x_{j}\|^{2}+h_{j}-h_{i}\right\}.

(2)

Here,
\langle{\mspace{2mu}\cdot\mspace{2mu}},{\mspace{2mu}\cdot\mspace{2mu}}\rangle
is the usual inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The Laguerre diagram generated by
\varphi
is the set of non-empty Laguerre cells, and is denoted by
L(\varphi)
:

L(\varphi):=\left\{C((x,h),\varphi):(x,h)\in\varphi\text{ and }C((x,h),\varphi)\neq\emptyset\right\}.

A Laguerre diagram is not necessarily a tessellation, conditions on
\varphi
are needed to ensure that
L(\varphi)
is locally finite and that all cells are bounded. The random Laguerre diagrams we consider in this paper are in fact tessellations, as we will discuss in a moment. A Laguerre diagram has an interesting interpretation as a crystallization process. From the definition of a Laguerre cell it follows that:

x\in C((x_{i},h_{i}),\varphi)\iff\exists t\geq h_{i}:x\in\bar{B}\left(x_{i},\sqrt{t-h_{i}}\right)\text{ and }x\notin\bigcup_{j\neq i}B\left(x_{j},\sqrt{(t-h_{j})_{+}}\right),

with
(x)_{+}=\max\{x,0\}
. Hence, we may associate the ball
B_{i}(t):=\bar{B}(x_{i},\sqrt{(t-h_{i})_{+}})
with the pair
(x_{i},h_{i})
. This ball starts growing at time
t=h_{i}
. The ball initially grows fast, and then its growth slows down due to the square root. If
B_{i}
is the first ball to hit a given point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then
x\in C((x_{i},h_{i}),\varphi)
. It is possible that
x_{i}
lies in another cell
C((x_{j},h_{j}),\varphi)
,
i\neq j
and yet
C((x_{i},h_{i}),\varphi)
may be non-empty. It can also be that
(x_{i},h_{i})
does not generate a cell, essentially because its ball starts growing too late.

Let
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a full-dimensional polytope. A subset
F
of
P
is called a face of
P
if either
F=\emptyset
,
F=P
or if there exists a supporting hyperplane
H
of
P
such that
F=P\cap H
.
H
is a supporting hyperplane of
P
if
H\cap P\neq\emptyset
and if
P
is contained in only one of the closed half spaces bounded by
H
. Note that each face of a convex polytope is again a convex polytope. A
k
-dimensional face of
P
is called a
k
-face. Usually,
0
-faces are called vertices,
1
-faces edges and
(d-1)
-faces facets. A tessellation
T=\{C_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}\}
is called face-to-face if the intersection of any two cells
C_{i}
and
C_{j}
is either empty or a
k
-face (
k\leq d-1
) of both cells. If moreover every
k
-face is contained in the boundary of exactly
d-k+1
cells (
k=0,\dots,d-1
), then the tessellation is called normal. Let
\varphi=\{(x_{i},h_{i})\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
. We say that
\varphi
satisfies the regularity conditions if:

1.

\conv\{x_{i}:(x_{i},h_{i})\in\varphi\}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

2.

Only finitely many
(x_{i},h_{i})\in\varphi
satisfy
\|x_{i}-y\|^{2}+h_{i}\leq t
for any
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and any
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

We say that the points of
\varphi
are in general position if:

1.

A
(k-1)
-dimensional affine subspace contains at most
k

x_{i}
’s
(k=2,\dots,d)
.

2.

At most
d+1
points
(x,h)\in\varphi
satisfy
\|x-y\|^{2}+h=t
for any
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

If
\varphi
satisfies the regularity conditions, then the Laguerre diagram generated by
\varphi
is a face-to-face tessellation. If the points of
\varphi
are also in general position, then all cells of this Laguerre tessellation have dimension
d
and this tessellation is normal. The aforementioned results can be found in
[
14
]
.

We are now ready to introduce Poisson-Laguerre tessellations. The Poisson-Laguerre tessellation is a generalization of the well-known Poisson-Voronoi tessellation, and was first studied in
[
14
]
and
[
13
]
. We will mostly follow the description of the Poisson-Laguerre tessellation as given in
[
11
]
. Throughout,
\eta
is a Poisson process on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty)
with intensity measure
\nu_{d}\times\mathbb{F}
. Here,
\mathbb{F}
is a locally finite measure concentrated on
(0,\infty)
. In
[
11
]
it was shown that with probability one
\eta
satisfies the regularity conditions and its points are in general position. As a consequence,
L(\eta)
is with probability one a normal tessellation, which is also face-to-face. The random tessellation
L(\eta)
is known as the Poisson-Laguerre tessellation. One may define the distribution function
F(z)=\mathbb{F}((0,z])
for
z\geq 0
. Consistent estimators for
F
based on weighted generators corresponding to observed cells were introduced in
[
27
]
.

In this paper we will also use the following notation. As shown in Lemma 1 in
[
27
]
, for
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
h>0
we have
y\in C((x,h),\eta)-x\iff\eta(A_{x,h,y})=0
, with:

A_{x,h,y}=\left\{(x^{\prime},h^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty):\|y\|^{2}+h-h^{\prime}>\|x+y-x^{\prime}\|^{2}\right\}.

(3)

Moreover,

\mathbb{P}\left(y\in C((x,h),\eta)-x\right)=\exp\left(-\kappa_{d}\int_{0}^{\|y\|^{2}+h}\left(\|y\|^{2}+h-t\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}\mathrm{d}F(t)\right).

(4)

For deterministic
(x,h)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
, the set
C((x,h),\eta)
is a so-called random closed set. Let
\mathcal{F}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denote the system of closed subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
\mathfrak{F}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denotes the
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathcal{F}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
which is generated by all families
\mathcal{F}^{K}=\{F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):F\cap K=\emptyset\}
,
K\in\mathcal{K}^{d}
. Here,
\mathcal{K}^{d}
denotes the space of convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and a convex body is a convex and compact set with non-empty interior. Then, a random closed set is a random element of
(\mathcal{F}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\allowbreak\mathfrak{F}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. Robbins’ theorem states that for any random closed set
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
p\in\mathbb{N}
we have:

\mathbb{E}\left(\nu_{d}(X)^{p}\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\dots\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{P}(y_{1},\dots,y_{p}\in X)\mathrm{d}y_{1}\dots\mathrm{d}y_{p}.

(5)

Robbin’s theorem is essentially Fubini’s theorem in the context of random closed sets. We refer to
[
18
]
for more details on random closed sets and Robbins’ theorem. From the proof of Theorem 6 in
[
27
]
we recall that for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left(\nu_{d}(C((x,h),\eta))\right)\mathrm{d}F(h)=

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{P}\left(y\in C((x,h),\eta)-x\right)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}F(h)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp\left(-\kappa_{d}\int_{0}^{\|y\|^{2}+h}\left(\|y\|^{2}+h-t\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}\mathrm{d}F(t)\right)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}F(h)=1.

(6)

3
The overparameterization of Laguerre tessellations

For a Laguerre tessellation it can be seen from the definition of a Laguerre cell that transforming all weights
h_{i}
via
h_{i}\mapsto h_{i}+z
for a fixed
z\in\mathbb{R}
does not change the tessellation. In
[
8
]
it was shown via simulations that there also exist various other choices of generator points and weights which generate the same Laguerre tessellation. However, to the best of our knowledge there is no explicit characterization of the class of all configurations of points and weights which yield the same Laguerre tessellation. In this section, we obtain such a characterization for a class of Laguerre tessellations generated by weighted points satisfying commonly used regularity conditions. The following lemma from
[
17
]
highlights a large class of generator points and weights which result in the same Laguerre tessellation.

Lemma 1

(Proposition 6 in
[
17
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{I}\subset\mathbb{N}
and
\varphi=\{(x_{i},h_{i})\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
. Let
\lambda>0,c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
z\in\mathbb{R}
. For
i\in\mathcal{I}
define:

\displaystyle x_{i}^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=\lambda x_{i}+c

\displaystyle h_{i}^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=\lambda h_{i}-\lambda(\lambda-1)\|x_{i}\|^{2}-2\lambda\langle x_{i},c\rangle+z.

Set
\psi=\{(x_{i}^{\prime},h_{i}^{\prime})\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
. Then,
L(\varphi)=L(\psi)
. In fact,
C((x_{i},h_{i}),\varphi)=C((x_{i}^{\prime},h^{\prime}_{i}),\psi)
for all
i\in\mathcal{I}
.

Remark 1
.

If we exclude the case
\lambda=1
in Lemma
1
, then
x_{i}^{\prime}
and
h^{\prime}_{i}
may be written in the following form:

\displaystyle x_{i}^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=\lambda(x_{i}-c^{\prime})+c^{\prime}

\displaystyle h^{\prime}_{i}

\displaystyle:=\lambda h_{i}-\lambda(\lambda-1)\|x_{i}-c^{\prime}\|^{2}+z^{\prime},

for some
c^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
. This form highlights in particular how each weight
h^{\prime}_{i}
depends on the distance of
x_{i}
to
c^{\prime}
.

Remark 2
.

Lemma
1
was used to construct the example in Figure
1
.

In order to prove a converse of Lemma
1
we need some additional assumptions. For instance, from the crystallic growth interpretation of a Laguerre tessellation as described in section
2.2
, we see that one can always add a weighted point to a configuration of weighted points with a sufficiently large weight such that this weighted point will generate an empty cell. After all, the cell corresponding to this weighted point will essentially ”start growing too late”. As such, we can at best characterize the class of weighted points which generate the same Laguerre tessellation, when restricting ourselves to the weighted points corresponding to the non-empty cells. In the theorem below we present such a characterization.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\varphi=\{(x_{i},h_{i})\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
,
d\geq 2
. Assume that
\varphi
satisfies the regularity conditions and the points of
\varphi
are in general position. Let
\psi\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
be a countable set of distinct points. Assume that
C((x,h),\varphi)\neq\emptyset
for each
(x,h)\in\varphi
and
C((x,h),\psi)\neq\emptyset
for each
(x,h)\in\psi
. Then,
L(\varphi)=L(\psi)
if and only if
\psi=\{(x_{i}^{\prime},h_{i}^{\prime})\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
with:

\displaystyle x_{i}^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=\lambda x_{i}+c

\displaystyle h^{\prime}_{i}

\displaystyle:=\lambda h_{i}+\lambda(\lambda-1)\|x_{i}\|^{2}+2\lambda\langle x_{i},c\rangle+z,

for some
\lambda>0
,
c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
z\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Let
i\in\mathbb{N}
. If
x_{i}^{\prime}
and
h^{\prime}_{i}
are as in the statement of the theorem then
L(\varphi)=L(\psi)
by Lemma
1
. Now it remains to show the converse. Hence, we assume
L(\varphi)=L(\psi)
. Note that the points of
\psi
can always be relabeled in such a way that we obtain
C((x_{i},h_{i}),\varphi)=C((x_{i}^{\prime},h^{\prime}_{i}),\psi)
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Throughout this proof we write
C_{i}=C((x_{i},h_{i}),\varphi)
and
C_{i}^{\prime}=C((x_{i}^{\prime},h^{\prime}_{i}),\psi)
for
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence it remains to show that if
C_{i}=C_{i}^{\prime}
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
, then
x_{i}^{\prime}
and
h^{\prime}_{i}
are as in the statement of the theorem. Choose
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
with
i\neq j
such that
C_{i}\cap C_{j}\neq\emptyset
. Since the Laguerre tessellation
L(\varphi)
is a normal face-to-face tessellation,
C_{i}
and
C_{j}
share a facet. By (
2
) this facet is contained within the supporting hyperplane:

H_{ij}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:2\langle x,x_{i}-x_{j}\rangle=\|x_{i}\|^{2}-\|x_{j}\|^{2}-h_{j}+h_{i}\}.

We now seek conditions such that
\psi
generates the same Laguerre cells. If so, we necessarily have
H_{ij}=H_{ij}^{\prime}
with:

H_{ij}^{\prime}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:2\langle x,x_{i}^{\prime}-x_{j}^{\prime}\rangle=\|x_{i}^{\prime}\|^{2}-\|x_{j}^{\prime}\|^{2}-h^{\prime}_{j}+h^{\prime}_{i}\},

this being the case because
H_{ij}^{\prime}
still needs to be a supporting hyperplane of
C_{i}^{\prime}
and
C_{j}^{\prime}
. Note that the normal vectors of
H_{ij}
and
H_{ij}^{\prime}
are given by
x_{i}-x_{j}
and
x_{i}^{\prime}-x_{j}^{\prime}
respectively. As a result there exists a
\lambda_{ij}\neq 0
such that:
x_{i}^{\prime}-x_{j}^{\prime}=\lambda_{ij}(x_{i}-x_{j})
. Now take another
k\in\mathbb{N}
, with
k\neq i
and
k\neq j
such that
C_{i}
,
C_{j}
and
C_{k}
share a vertex. Due to normality of the Laguerre tessellation such a
k
exists. As a result,
C_{j}
and
C_{k}
share a facet and
C_{i}
and
C_{k}
share a facet. Arguing as before, there exists
\lambda_{ik}\neq 0
and
\lambda_{jk}\neq 0
such that:

\displaystyle x_{i}^{\prime}-x_{j}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{ij}(x_{i}-x_{j})

(7)

\displaystyle x_{i}^{\prime}-x_{k}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{ik}(x_{i}-x_{k})

(8)

\displaystyle x_{j}^{\prime}-x_{k}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{jk}(x_{j}-x_{k}).

(9)

Suppose that
\lambda_{ik}\neq\lambda_{ij}
, we show that this leads to a contradiction. Equation (
7
) may be written as:
\lambda_{ij}x_{i}=\lambda_{ij}x_{j}+x_{i}^{\prime}-x_{j}^{\prime}
. Subtracting (
9
) from (
8
) yields:
x_{i}^{\prime}-x_{j}^{\prime}=\lambda_{ik}(x_{i}-x_{k})-\lambda_{jk}(x_{j}-x_{k})
. Combining these two expressions yields:

\lambda_{ij}x_{i}=\lambda_{ij}x_{j}+\lambda_{ik}(x_{i}-x_{k})-\lambda_{jk}(x_{j}-x_{k}).

By solving for
x_{i}
we obtain:

x_{i}=\left(1-\frac{\lambda_{jk}-\lambda_{ik}}{\lambda_{ij}-\lambda_{ik}}\right)x_{j}+\left(\frac{\lambda_{jk}-\lambda_{ik}}{\lambda_{ij}-\lambda_{ik}}\right)x_{k}.

This means that
x_{i}
is a linear combination of
x_{j}
and
x_{k}
, and therefore these three points are collinear. This is in contradiction with the assumption that the points of
\varphi
are in general position. Hence,
\lambda_{ik}=\lambda_{ij}
. By symmetry:
\lambda\equiv\lambda_{ij}=\lambda_{ik}=\lambda_{jk}
. Because the Laguerre tessellation is a face-to-face tessellation we can iteratively consider neighboring cells such that we eventually find:

x_{i}^{\prime}-x_{j}^{\prime}=\lambda(x_{i}-x_{j})\text{ for all }i,j\in\mathbb{N}.

(10)

Fix
i\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
j\in\mathbb{N}
. Choose
c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
x_{i}^{\prime}=\lambda x_{i}+c
. From (
10
) we now obtain that also
x_{j}^{\prime}=\lambda x_{j}+c
. Hence,
x_{i}^{\prime}=\lambda x_{i}+c
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
. We will argue that
\lambda>0
at the end of this proof. Now, we need to determine weights
h^{\prime}_{1},h^{\prime}_{2},\dots\in\mathbb{R}
such that the weighted points
\psi:=\{(x_{i}^{\prime},h^{\prime}_{i})\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
. generate the same Laguerre cells. Consider once again
j\neq i
such that
C_{i}\cap C_{j}\neq\emptyset
. Following the notation for
H_{ij}
and
H_{ij}^{\prime}
as before, plugging in
x_{i}^{\prime}=\lambda x_{i}+c
we obtain:

H_{ij}^{\prime}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:2\langle x,\lambda(x_{i}-x_{j})\rangle=\lambda^{2}\|x_{i}\|^{2}+2\lambda\langle x_{i},c\rangle-\lambda^{2}\|x_{j}\|^{2}-2\lambda\langle x_{j},c\rangle-h^{\prime}_{j}+h^{\prime}_{i}\}.

It is now evident that
H_{ij}=H_{ij}^{\prime}
if and only if:

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\|x_{i}\|^{2}+2\lambda\langle x_{i},c\rangle-\lambda^{2}\|x_{j}\|^{2}-2\lambda\langle x_{j},c\rangle-h^{\prime}_{j}+h^{\prime}_{i}=\lambda\left(\|x_{i}\|^{2}-\|x_{j}\|^{2}-h_{j}+h_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle h^{\prime}_{i}-h^{\prime}_{j}=\lambda\left(h_{i}-(\lambda-1)\|x_{i}\|^{2}-2\langle x_{i},c\rangle\right)-\lambda\left(h_{j}-(\lambda-1)\|x_{j}\|^{2}-2\langle x_{j},c\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle h^{\prime}_{i}-h^{\prime}_{j}=\lambda(f_{i}-f_{j}),

(11)

with
f_{i}:=h_{i}-(\lambda-1)\|x_{i}\|^{2}-2\langle x_{i},c\rangle
. Analogous to (
10
) we may argue that (
11
) holds for all
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
. Fix
i\in\mathbb{N}
and let
j\in\mathbb{N}
, then choose
z\in\mathbb{R}
such that
h^{\prime}_{i}=\lambda f_{i}+z
. From (
11
) we now obtain that also
h^{\prime}_{j}=\lambda f_{j}+z
. Hence, for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
we have:

h^{\prime}_{i}=\lambda f_{i}+z=\lambda h_{i}-\lambda(\lambda-1)\|x_{i}\|^{2}-2\lambda\langle x_{i},c\rangle+z.

This is precisely the form of
h_{i}^{\prime}
as in the statement of the theorem. Finally, we conclude that we must have
\lambda>0
. This is the case because for
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
with
C_{i}\cap C_{j}\neq\emptyset
the supporting hyperplane
H_{ij}
also defines a half space containing
C_{i}
, recall (
2
). Similarly, the half space induced by the supporting hyperplane
H_{ji}
contains
C_{j}
. In order to have
C_{i}=C_{i}^{\prime}
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
we need not only to preserve these supporting hyperplanes but also the orientation of the corresponding half spaces, which requires
\lambda>0
. After all, choosing
\lambda<0
will flip the orientation of each half space.
∎

Remark 3
.

The arguments in the proof of Theorem
2
can also be used to show an analogous result for Laguerre tessellations in bounded domains. Then,
\varphi
and
\psi
should be finite sets of the same size. In the bounded case the points of
\varphi
should also be in general position but
\varphi
does not need to satisfy the regularity conditions.

4
Inverting Poisson-Laguerre tessellations via weighted least-squares

In view of Theorem
2
it is evident that whenever one observes a (random) Laguerre tessellation through a bounded window it is not possible to exactly reconstruct the original set of weighted generators corresponding to the observed cells. However, we can already intuitively understand that in many cases some configurations of the weighted points are more likely than others. Consider for example Figure
1
. If one is given the information that the original generator points are uniformly distributed within the observation window, then the configuration in the left panel seems far more likely than the configuration in the right panel. This suggests that it may still be possible to get very close to the original configuration, even though exact reconstruction is not possible.

We now turn our attention towards Poisson-Laguerre tessellations. Recall that
\eta
is a Poisson process on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty)
with intensity measure
\nu_{d}\times\mathbb{F}
. Suppose we observe the Poisson-Laguerre tessellation
L(\eta)
through a bounded observation window
W_{n}
, where
(W_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is a convex averaging sequence. In view of algorithm 1 in
[
8
]
we can construct a configuration of weighted generators corresponding to the (partially) observed cells of the tessellation. Because we are in some sense interested in the ”best” configuration of the weighted generators, and because all configurations are related to the original configuration via the form presented in Theorem
2
, it does not matter which configuration we start with. As such we consider it to be of no loss of generality that we observe weighted generator points of
\eta^{*}
up to a deterministic transformation of the form presented in Theorem
2
. Here,
\eta^{*}
denotes the set of the so-called extreme points of
\eta
, and is given by:

\eta^{*}=\{(x,h)\in\eta:C((x,h),\eta)\neq\emptyset\}.

Indeed, we need to stress that we can only hope to approximate the points of
\eta^{*}
in an observation window, instead of the points of
\eta
. This being the case because there is no way to obtain any information on points of
\eta
which do not generate a cell. Only points of
\eta^{*}
can be determined up to a transformation of the form presented in Theorem
2
.

Hence, we consider the following. For all points
(x,h)\in\eta^{*}
with
x\in W_{n}
we assume that the Laguerre cells corresponding to these points are observed. Additionally, the original points (elements of
\eta^{*}
) themselves are considered to be known up to a transformation of the form in Theorem
2
. Specifically, suppose that
\lambda_{0}>0
,
c_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and define the function
f_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
via:

f_{0}(x,h)=\left(\frac{x}{\lambda_{0}}-\frac{c_{0}}{\lambda_{0}},\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}}h+\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}}-1\right)\|x\|^{2}-\frac{2}{\lambda_{0}}\left\langle x,\frac{c_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}\right\rangle\right).

We do not consider the additive constant
z
in Theorem
2
. We do this because the distribution of a Poisson-Laguerre tessellation is invariant under shifts of the distribution function
F(z)=\mathbb{F}((0,z])
. As such, we cannot do better than estimating
F
up to a shift.

For any point
(x,h)\in\eta^{*}
with
x\in W_{n}
we observe
f_{0}(x,h)
instead of
(x,h)
where
\lambda_{0}
and
c_{0}
are unknown. Define:
W_{n}^{0}=\smash{\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}}}W_{n}-\smash{\frac{c_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}}
. We wish to estimate
\lambda_{0}
and
c_{0}
. For this purpose we define the following criterion function
T_{n}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
:

\displaystyle T_{n}(\lambda,c):=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in f_{0}(\eta)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}^{0}}(x)\int_{C((x,h),f_{0}(\eta))}\|\lambda x+c-y\|^{2}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)\int_{C((x,h),\eta)}\left\|\lambda\left(\frac{x}{\lambda_{0}}-\frac{c_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}\right)+c-y\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}y

(12)

Note that while
T_{n}
contains a sum over elements of
\eta
(or
f_{0}(\eta)
), only elements from
\eta^{*}
(or
f_{0}(\eta^{*})
) contribute to this sum. Intuitively, we believe that this criterion function is asymptotically minimized when taking
\lambda
and
c
such that
\lambda(x/\lambda_{0}-c_{0}/\lambda_{0})+c=x
which corresponds to
\lambda=\lambda_{0}
and
c=c_{0}
. That is, given the transformation
f_{0}
we are interested in finding the inverse transformation. First, we compute the expected value of
T_{n}(\lambda,c)
. To this end, we need some additional notation. Let
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and define the probability density function
p_{F}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty]
via:

p_{F}(y)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\kappa_{d}\int_{0}^{\|y\|^{2}+h}\left(\|y\|^{2}+h-t\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}\mathrm{d}F(t)\right)\mathrm{d}F(h).

The fact that
p_{F}
is a probability density function follows from (
6
).

Lemma 2
.

For all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
:
p_{F}(y)\leq 1/(\kappa_{d}\|y\|^{d})
. Additionally, for all
q\in\mathbb{N}
:
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|y\|^{q}p_{F}(y)\mathrm{d}y<\infty
.

The proof of Lemma
2
is given in section
9
. Lemma
2
ensures that the expected value of
T_{n}(\lambda,c)
, as given in the next lemma, is finite.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\lambda>0
and
c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then:

\mathbb{E}\left(T_{n}(\lambda,c)\right)=\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\int_{W_{n}}\left\|\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{0}}-1\right)x+c-\frac{\lambda c_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|y\|^{2}p_{F}(y)\mathrm{d}y.

And in particular:

\mathbb{E}\left(T_{n}(\lambda_{0},c)\right)=\|c-c_{0}\|^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|y\|^{2}p_{F}(y)\mathrm{d}y.

Proof.

Substituting
\tilde{y}=y-x
, and then writing
y
instead of
\tilde{y}
in (
12
) we obtain:

\displaystyle T_{n}(\lambda,c)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)\int_{C((x,h),\eta)-x}\left\|\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{0}}-1\right)x-\frac{\lambda c_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}+c-y\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\|\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{0}}-1\right)x-\frac{\lambda c_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}+c-y\right\|^{2}\mathds{1}\{y\in C((x,h),\eta)-x\}\mathrm{d}y

Note that
C((x,h),\eta)=C((x,h),\eta+\delta_{x,h})
. Hence, via the Mecke equation (Theorem 4.1. in
[
12
]
) and Fubini we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(T_{n}(\lambda,c)\right)=

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\int_{W_{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\|\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{0}}-1\right)x-\frac{\lambda c_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}+c-y\right\|^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(y\in C((x,h),\eta)-x\right)\mathrm{d}F(h)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\int_{W_{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\|\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{0}}-1\right)x-\frac{\lambda c_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}+c-y\right\|^{2}p_{F}(y)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\int_{W_{n}}\left\|\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{0}}-1\right)x-\frac{\lambda c_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}+c\right\|^{2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p_{F}(y)\mathrm{d}y\right)\mathrm{d}x+

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\ -\frac{2}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\left\langle\int_{W_{n}}\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{0}}-1\right)x-\frac{\lambda c_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}+c\mathrm{d}x,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}yp_{F}(y)\mathrm{d}y\right\rangle+

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\ +\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\int_{W_{n}}\mathrm{d}x\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|y\|^{2}p_{F}(y)\mathrm{d}y.

Because the probability density function
p_{F}
is symmetric around
0
and because

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|y\|p_{F}(y)\mathrm{d}y<\infty
by Lemma
2
, it follows that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}yp_{F}(y)\mathrm{d}y=0
. As a consequence:

\mathbb{E}\left(T_{n}(\lambda,c)\right)=\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\int_{W_{n}}\left\|\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{0}}-1\right)x+c-\frac{\lambda c_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|y\|^{2}p_{F}(y)\mathrm{d}y.

∎

Remark 4
.

By considering any non-empty cell
C((x,h),\eta)
with
(x,h)\in\eta
and
x\in W_{n}
to be fully observed we do not incorporate edge effects. We discuss this issue in section
6
.

4.1
Definition and computation of an estimator

From its expression, it is evident that the function
(\lambda,c)\mapsto\mathbb{E}\left(T_{n}(\lambda,c)\right)
attains its global minimum in
(\lambda_{0},c_{0})
. This inspires the definition of the following estimators for
\lambda_{0}
and
c_{0}
:

(\hat{\lambda}_{n},\hat{c}_{n})=\argmin_{(\lambda,c)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}T_{n}(\lambda,c).

Applying this inversion procedure in practice means the following. For
\lambda\in\mathbb{R},c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
define
f({\mspace{2mu}\cdot\mspace{2mu}};\lambda,c):\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
via:

f((x,h);\lambda,c)=(\lambda x+c,\lambda h-\lambda(\lambda-1)\|x\|^{2}-2\lambda\langle x,c\rangle).

Then, the inversion procedure boils down to computing the following point process:

\hat{\eta}_{n}^{*}:=f(f_{0}(\eta);\hat{\lambda}_{n},\hat{c}_{n})\cap(W_{n}\times(0,\infty)),

(13)

which may be considered an approximation of
\eta^{*}\cap(W_{n}\times(0,\infty))
. Let us provide some further motivation for estimating
(\lambda_{0},c_{0})
by minimizing
T_{n}
. We intuitively expect that for a point
(x,h)\in\eta^{*}
,
x
should be quite close to the center of its cell
C((x,h),\eta)
. Suppose that we consider for the center of a cell its centroid, also known as center of mass. We will now show that minimizing
T_{n}
corresponds to minimizing the sum of the volume weighted squared distances of each generator to the center of mass of its cell. For a Borel set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
of positive volume its centroid
c(K)
is defined as:

c(K)=\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(K)}\int_{K}x\mathrm{d}x.

The following equation highlights a convenient property of
c(K)
, for
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have:

\int_{K}\|x-a\|^{2}\mathrm{d}x=\int_{K}\|x-c(K)\|^{2}\mathrm{d}x+\nu_{d}(K)\|a-c(K)\|^{2}.

(14)

Note that the LHS of (
14
) is minimized by
a=c(K)
. In words, the centroid minimizes the integrated squared distance to a given set. Let us introduce the following notation, for the volume and the centroid corresponding to the Laguerre cell associated with
(x,h)
:

v_{x,h}:=\nu_{d}(C((x,h),\eta)),\text{\quad}c_{x,h}:=c(C((x,h),\eta))

Suppose that
(x^{\prime},h^{\prime})=f_{0}(x,h)
for
(x,h)\in\eta^{*}
, because the function
f_{0}
does not affect the resulting Laguerre tessellation we have:

v_{x^{\prime},h^{\prime}}:=\nu_{d}(C((x^{\prime},h^{\prime}),f_{0}(\eta)))=\nu_{d}(C((x,h),\eta))=:v_{x,h}.

Similarly we also have
\smash{c_{x^{\prime},h^{\prime}}}=\smash{c_{x,h}}
. We will frequently use this when we switch from summing over elements from
\eta
to summing over elements from
\smash{f_{0}(\eta)}
, or vice-versa. Throughout, we will set
\smash{c_{x,h}}=0
whenever
\smash{v_{x,h}}=0
. Applying (
14
) to the definition of
T_{n}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle T_{n}(\lambda,c)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in f_{0}(\eta)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}^{0}}(x)\left(\int_{C((x,h),f_{0}(\eta))}\|y-c_{x,h}\|^{2}\mathrm{d}y+v_{x,h}\left\|\lambda x+c-c_{x,h}\right\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)\left(\int_{C((x,h),\eta)}\|y-c_{x,h}\|^{2}\mathrm{d}y+v_{x,h}\left\|\lambda\left(\frac{x}{\lambda_{0}}-\frac{c_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}\right)+c-c_{x,h}\right\|^{2}\right).

As such, we have written
T_{n}
as the sum of two terms, and only the second term depends on
\lambda
and
c
. Therefore, we also have:

(\hat{\lambda}_{n},\hat{c}_{n})=\argmin_{(\lambda,c)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in f_{0}(\eta)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}^{0}}(x)v_{x,h}\left\|\lambda x+c-c_{x,h}\right\|^{2}.

(15)

So indeed,
\smash{(\hat{\lambda}_{n},\hat{c}_{n})}
minimizes the sum of volume weighted squared distances of the generators to the centers of mass of their cells. From (
15
), we can see that
T_{n}
is a strictly convex function. As a consequence, we can obtain
\smash{(\hat{\lambda}_{n},\hat{c}_{n})}
by computing the critical point of
T_{n}
(indeed, there is only one critical point). Taking the partial derivatives of
T_{n}
w.r.t.
\lambda
and
c
yields:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial T_{n}(\lambda,c)}{\partial\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in f_{0}(\eta)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}^{0}}(x)v_{x,h}\left(\lambda\|x\|^{2}+\left\langle c-c_{x,h},x\right\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial T_{n}(\lambda,c)}{\partial c}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in f_{0}(\eta)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}^{0}}(x)v_{x,h}\left(\lambda x+c-c_{x,h}\right).

Setting these partial derivatives equal to zero and solving for
\lambda
and
c
yields a unique solution, which is given by:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{\lambda}_{n}&=\frac{\frac{\sum_{(x,h)\in f_{0}(\eta)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}^{0}}(x)v_{x,h}\langle c_{x,h},x\rangle}{\sum_{(x,h)\in f_{0}(\eta)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}^{0}}(x)v_{x,h}}-\left\langle\frac{\sum_{(x,h)\in f_{0}(\eta)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}^{0}}(x)v_{x,h}c_{x,h}}{\sum_{(x,h)\in f_{0}(\eta)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}^{0}}(x)v_{x,h}},\frac{\sum_{(x,h)\in f_{0}(\eta)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}^{0}}(x)v_{x,h}x}{\sum_{(x,h)\in f_{0}(\eta)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}^{0}}(x)v_{x,h}}\right\rangle}{\frac{\sum_{(x,h)\in f_{0}(\eta)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}^{0}}(x)v_{x,h}\|x\|^{2}}{\sum_{(x,h)\in f_{0}(\eta)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}^{0}}(x)v_{x,h}}-\left\|\frac{\sum_{(x,h)\in f_{0}(\eta)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}^{0}}(x)v_{x,h}x}{\sum_{(x,h)\in f_{0}(\eta)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}^{0}}(x)v_{x,h}}\right\|^{2}}\\
\hat{c}_{n}&=\frac{\sum_{(x,h)\in f_{0}(\eta)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}^{0}}(x)v_{x,h}c_{x,h}}{\sum_{(x,h)\in f_{0}(\eta)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}^{0}}(x)v_{x,h}}-\hat{\lambda}_{n}\frac{\sum_{(x,h)\in f_{0}(\eta)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}^{0}}(x)v_{x,h}x}{\sum_{(x,h)\in f_{0}(\eta)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}^{0}}(x)v_{x,h}}.\end{split}

(16)

Or, if we write
f_{0}(\eta)\cap(W_{n}^{0}\times\mathbb{R})=\{(x_{1},h_{1}),\dots,(x_{m},h_{m})\}
and
c_{i}=c_{x_{i},h_{i}}
,
v_{i}=v_{x_{i},h_{i}}
, then
\hat{\lambda}_{n}
may be written more compactly as:

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}\langle c_{i},x_{i}\rangle}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}}\right)-\left\langle\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}c_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}},\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}x_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}}\right\rangle}{\left(\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}\|x_{i}\|^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}}\right)-\left\|\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}x_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}}\right\|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}\langle c_{i},x_{i}\rangle\right)-\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}c_{i},\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}x_{i}\right\rangle}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}\|x_{i}\|^{2}\right)-\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}x_{i}\right\|^{2}}.

Similarly,
\hat{c}_{n}
may be written as:

\hat{c}_{n}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}c_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}}-\hat{\lambda}_{n}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}x_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}}.

In order for
\hat{\lambda}_{n}
to be well-defined we need to verify that the denominator in its definition is not equal to zero. Whenever
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}
are distinct points, we have by Jensen’s inequality:

\left\|\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}x_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}}\right\|^{2}<\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}\|x_{i}\|^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}}.

Because
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}
are distinct points with probability one,
\hat{\lambda}_{n}
is almost surely well-defined. As is frequently the case with least-squares estimators, these can often be computed via an explicit formula such as (
16
) or by solving a system of linear equations. In this case, note that we may write:

\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}\left\|\lambda x_{i}+c-c_{i}\right\|^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}}=\beta^{T}X\beta-2u^{T}\beta+K.

Here,
X
is a
(d+1)\times(d+1)
matrix which is given by the following block matrix:

X=\begin{pmatrix}I_{d}&\left(\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}x_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}}\right)\\
\left(\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}x_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}}\right)^{T}&\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}\|x_{i}\|^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}}\end{pmatrix}.

Each vector is to be interpreted as a column-vector and
\beta^{T}
denotes the transpose of
\beta
. The matrix
I_{d}
is the unit matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Finally,
u
and
\beta
are vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
and
K
is a constant, these are given by:

u=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}c_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}}\\
\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}\langle c_{i},x_{i}\rangle}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}}\end{pmatrix},\ \beta=\begin{pmatrix}c\\
\lambda\end{pmatrix},\ K=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}\|c_{i}\|^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}}.

Then,
(\hat{\lambda}_{n},\hat{c}_{n})
may be computed by solving the linear system
X\beta=u
for
\beta
. In simulations this was observed to be more numerically stable compared to using (
16
).

4.2
Limiting behavior of the criterion function

As a first important step towards understanding how
(\hat{\lambda}_{n},\hat{c}_{n})
may behave as
n\to\infty
, we study how the criterion function
T_{n}
behaves as
n\to\infty
. Before we can determine the limiting behavior of
T_{n}
, we need two lemmas which allow us to control the sizes of the Laguerre cells that are summed over in the definition of
T_{n}
.

Lemma 4
.

Let
r>0
and
\varphi=\{(x_{i},h_{i})\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
. Let
(x,h)\in\varphi
. Define the following set:

\displaystyle D_{x,h}

\displaystyle=\left\{(x^{\prime},h^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}:h^{\prime}\leq r^{2}+h,\ \|x-x^{\prime}\|<r+\sqrt{r^{2}+h-h^{\prime}}\right\}.

Then, if
(\varphi\setminus\{(x,h)\})\cap D_{x,h}=\emptyset
we have
\bar{B}(x,r)\subset C((x,h),\varphi)
.

Proof.

Suppose
(\varphi\setminus\{(x,h)\})\cap D_{x,h}=\emptyset
. Let
y\in\bar{B}(x,r)
, it remains to show that
\|x-y\|^{2}+h\leq\|x^{\prime}-y\|^{2}+h^{\prime}
for all
(x^{\prime},h^{\prime})\in\varphi\setminus\{(x,h)\}
. Let
(x^{\prime},h^{\prime})\in\varphi\setminus\{(x,h)\}
. If
h^{\prime}\leq r^{2}+h
then by assumption
\|x-x^{\prime}\|\geq r+\sqrt{r^{2}+h-h^{\prime}}
. As a consequence of this fact and
\|x-y\|\leq r
we obtain:

\|x-y\|^{2}+h-h^{\prime}\leq r^{2}+h-h^{\prime}\leq\left(\|x-x^{\prime}\|-r\right)^{2}\leq\left(\|y-x^{\prime}\|+\|x-y\|-r\right)^{2}\leq\|x^{\prime}-y\|^{2}.

So indeed,
\|x-y\|^{2}+h\leq\|x^{\prime}-y\|^{2}+h^{\prime}
. It remains to consider the case
h^{\prime}>r^{2}+h
. In that case we also obtain the desired result:

\|x-y\|^{2}+h\leq r^{2}+h<h^{\prime}\leq\|x^{\prime}-y\|^{2}+h^{\prime}.

∎

The lemma below is essentially an intermediate result obtained in the proof of Proposition 3.1 in
[
10
]
. For the sake of completeness, the proof is presented in section
9
.

Lemma 5
.

Let
z>0
,
R>2\sqrt{z}
and
\varphi=\{(x_{i},h_{i})\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,z]
. Let
(x,h)\in\varphi
. Suppose that
J\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\mathcal{C}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{C}_{J}
are convex cones with non-empty interiors with
\smash{\cup_{j=1}^{J}}\mathcal{C}_{j}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Additionally, assume these convex cones have disjoint interiors and satisfy
\langle u,v\rangle\geq\frac{3}{4}\|u\|\cdot\|v\|
whenever
u,v\in\mathcal{C}_{j}
. Define the following sets for
j\in\{1,\dots,J\}
:

B_{x,j}=\left(\left(B(x,R)\setminus\bar{B}(x,2\sqrt{z})\right)\cap(\mathcal{C}_{j}+x)\right)\times(0,z].

If
\varphi\cap B_{x,j}\neq\emptyset
for all
j\in\{1,\dots,J\}
then
C((x,h),\varphi)\subset B(x,R)
.

In the theorem below we show that
T_{n}
almost surely converges pointwise to a function
T
. Note that the integral in the definition of
T
is finite by Lemma
2
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\lambda>0
and
c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then with probability one we have :

\lim_{n\to\infty}T_{n}(\lambda,c)=T(\lambda,c):=\begin{cases}\|c-c_{0}\|^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|y\|^{2}p_{F}(y)\mathrm{d}y&\text{if }\lambda=\lambda_{0}\\
\infty&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The proof of Theorem
3
is given in section
9
. In the remainder of this section we derive a few additional limit results, which we need later.

Lemma 6
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
define:

\displaystyle D_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)v_{x,h}

(17)

\displaystyle B_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)v_{x,h}\left(c_{x,h}-x\right).

(18)

Then, with probability one:
\lim_{n\to\infty}D_{n}=1
, and
\lim_{n\to\infty}B_{n}=0

Proof.

The fact that
\lim_{n\to\infty}D_{n}=1
almost surely, was shown in the proof of Lemma 5 in
[
27
]
, where the authors denoted
D_{n}
by
\tilde{F}_{n}^{V}(\infty)
. Let
c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we may write:

\displaystyle T_{n}(\lambda_{0},c)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)\int_{C((x,h),\eta)}\|x-c_{0}+c-y\|^{2}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)\bigg(v_{x,h}\|c-c_{0}\|^{2}+2\left\langle c-c_{0},\int_{C((x,h),\eta)}x-y\mathrm{d}y\right\rangle+\\
&\phantom{=}\ +\int_{C((x,h),\eta)}\|x-y\|^{2}\mathrm{d}y\bigg)\end{split}

\displaystyle=D_{n}\|c-c_{0}\|^{2}-2\langle c-c_{0},B_{n}\rangle+T_{n}(\lambda_{0},c_{0}).

Indeed, note that for any
(x,h)\in\eta^{*}
:

\int_{C((x,h),\eta)}y-x\mathrm{d}y=\int_{C((x,h),\eta)-x}y\mathrm{d}y=v_{x,h}c(C((x,h),\eta)-x)=v_{x,h}(c_{x,h}-x).

As a consequence:

\langle c-c_{0},B_{n}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\left(D_{n}\|c-c_{0}\|^{2}+T_{n}(\lambda_{0},c_{0})-T_{n}(\lambda_{0},c)\right).

(19)

By Theorem
3
, and since
\lim_{n\to\infty}D_{n}=1
almost surely, we obtain via the continuous mapping theorem the following almost sure limit:

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle c-c_{0},B_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\|c-c_{0}\|^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|y\|^{2}p_{F}(y)\mathrm{d}y-\|c-c_{0}\|^{2}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|y\|^{2}p_{F}(y)\mathrm{d}y\right)

\displaystyle=0.

Keeping in mind that this holds for any
c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we may for instance take
c=c_{0}+e_{j}
where
e_{j}
with
j\in\{1,\dots,d\}
is the
j
-th standard unit basis vector of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Taking this
c
we observe that the
j
-th component of
B_{n}
converges to 0 almost surely. Because this holds for any
j\in\{1,\dots,d\}
,
\lim_{n\to\infty}B_{n}=0
almost surely.
∎

For any
c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
\bar{\lambda}_{n}(c)=\argmin_{\lambda}T_{n}(\lambda,c)
. Note that
\lambda\mapsto T_{n}(\lambda,c)
is convex. Recalling that the partial derivatives of
T_{n}
were derived in section
4.1
, a straightforward computation yields:

\bar{\lambda}_{n}(c)=\frac{\sum_{(x,h)\in f_{0}(\eta)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}^{0}}(x)v_{x,h}\left\langle c_{x,h}-c,x\right\rangle}{\sum_{(x,h)\in f_{0}(\eta)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}^{0}}(x)v_{x,h}\left\|x\right\|^{2}}.

(20)

Computing the partial second derivative of
T_{n}
w.r.t.
\lambda
yields:

\frac{\partial^{2}T_{n}(\lambda,c)}{\partial\lambda^{2}}=\frac{2}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in f_{0}(\eta)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}^{0}}(x)v_{x,h}\|x\|^{2}=:2M_{n}.

(21)

From this, it immediately follows that for any
c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
T_{n}({\mspace{2mu}\cdot\mspace{2mu}},c)
is strongly convex with parameter
2M_{n}
. Recall that a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is strongly convex with parameter
m>0
if for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

f(y)\geq f(x)+\nabla\langle f(x),y-x\rangle+\frac{m}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}.

Observe that if
x^{*}
is the global minimizer of
f
, then:

\|y-x^{*}\|^{2}\leq\frac{2}{m}\left(f(y)-f(x^{*})\right).

(22)

A sufficient condition for
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
to be strongly convex with parameter
m>0
is that
f
is twice differentiable and
f^{\prime\prime}(x)\geq m
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. We now show that the strong convexity parameter of
T_{n}({\mspace{2mu}\cdot\mspace{2mu}},c)
diverges to infinity as
n\to\infty
:

Lemma 7
.

With probability one,
\lim_{n\to\infty}M_{n}=\infty
. Here,
M_{n}
is as in (
21
).

Proof.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space supporting the Poisson process
\eta
. As in the proof of Theorem
3
it is sufficient to show that for all
M>0
there exists a
\Omega_{M}\in\mathcal{A}
with
\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{M})=1
such that for all
\omega\in\Omega_{M}
:
\liminf_{n\to\infty}M_{n}(\omega)>M
. Let
M>0
. Choose
\Omega_{M}\in\mathcal{A}
with
\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{M})=1
such that for all
\omega\in\Omega_{M}
:
L(\eta(\omega))
is a tessellation and
\lim_{n\to\infty}D_{n}(\omega)=1
. Such a
\Omega_{M}
exists by Lemma
6
and the fact that Poisson-Laguerre tessellations are well-defined. Let
\omega\in\Omega_{M}
, then we may write
M_{n}(\omega)
as:

\displaystyle M_{n}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta({\mspace{2mu}\cdot\mspace{2mu}};\omega)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)v_{x,h}(\omega)\left\|\frac{x}{\lambda_{0}}-\frac{c_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta({\mspace{2mu}\cdot\mspace{2mu}};\omega)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)v_{x,h}(\omega)\left\|\frac{x}{\lambda_{0}}-\frac{c_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}\right\|^{2}\mathds{1}\left\{\left\|\frac{x}{\lambda_{0}}-\frac{c_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}\right\|\leq\sqrt{M}\right\}+

(23)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\ +\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta({\mspace{2mu}\cdot\mspace{2mu}};\omega)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)v_{x,h}(\omega)\left\|\frac{x}{\lambda_{0}}-\frac{c_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}\right\|^{2}\mathds{1}\left\{\left\|\frac{x}{\lambda_{0}}-\frac{c_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}\right\|>\sqrt{M}\right\}

(24)

We consider (
23
) and (
24
) separately. Note that the following expression is an upper bound for (
23
):

\frac{M}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\left(\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta({\mspace{2mu}\cdot\mspace{2mu}};\omega)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}\cap\bar{B}(c_{0},\lambda_{0}M)}(x)v_{x,h}(\omega)\right).

(25)

For sufficiently large
n
,
W_{n}\cap\bar{B}(c_{0},\lambda_{0}M)=\bar{B}(c_{0},\lambda_{0}M)
. Hence, for such
n
, the term in brackets in (
25
) does not depend on
n
. Because the tessellation
L(\eta(\omega))
is locally finite and has cells of finite volume, this term is finite. Because
\nu_{d}(W_{n})\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
we see that (
23
) vanishes. For (
24
) we obtain the following lower bound:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta({\mspace{2mu}\cdot\mspace{2mu}};\omega)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)v_{x,h}(\omega)\left\|\frac{x}{\lambda_{0}}-\frac{c_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}\right\|^{2}\mathds{1}\left\{\left\|\frac{x}{\lambda_{0}}-\frac{c_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}\right\|>\sqrt{M}\right\}

\displaystyle\quad>D_{n}(\omega)M-\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta({\mspace{2mu}\cdot\mspace{2mu}};\omega)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)v_{x,h}(\omega)M\mathds{1}\left\{\left\|\frac{x}{\lambda_{0}}-\frac{c_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}\right\|\leq\sqrt{M}\right\}.

(26)

By the choice of
\Omega_{M}
, the first term of (
26
) converges to
M
as
n\to\infty
, while the second term of (
26
) vanishes, as
n\to\infty
. The fact that the second term of (
26
) vanishes can be shown via the same argument as used for (
23
). Hence:
\liminf_{n\to\infty}M_{n}(\omega)>M
.
∎

As a consequence we find that a strongly consistent estimator for
\lambda_{0}
can be obtained even if we do not optimize for
c
.

Corollary 1
.

Let
c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then with probability one:
\lim_{n\to\infty}\bar{\lambda}_{n}(c)=\lambda_{0}
.

Proof.

Applying (
22
) to the function
T_{n}({\mspace{2mu}\cdot\mspace{2mu}},c)
, and taking into account that
T_{n}({\mspace{2mu}\cdot\mspace{2mu}},c)
is non-negative, we obtain:

\left\|\lambda_{0}-\bar{\lambda}_{n}(c)\right\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{M_{n}}\left(T_{n}(\lambda_{0},c)-T_{n}(\bar{\lambda}_{n}(c),c)\right)\leq\frac{T_{n}(\lambda_{0},c)}{M_{n}}.

(27)

By Lemma (
7
), Theorem
3
and the continuous mapping theorem we obtain that the RHS of (
27
) vanishes almost surely, which yields the result.
∎

5
Consistency of the inversion procedure

In the previous section we obtained various limit results, which we will need in this section for proving consistency of the inversion procedure. That is, we will show that under reasonable conditions the proposed estimator
(\hat{\lambda}_{n},\hat{c}_{n})
for
(\lambda_{0},c_{0})
is consistent. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
define the following random vector:

A_{n}=\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)\nu_{d}(C(x,h),\eta)x.

(28)

That is,
A_{n}
may be seen as a weighted average of the generators corresponding to non-empty cells, the weights being the volumes of the cells. One might argue that for a proper weighted average we should divide
A_{n}
by
D_{n}
, with
D_{n}
as in (
17
). However, by Lemma
6
we know that
D_{n}
converges to
1
almost surely as
n\to\infty
. Hence, when studying the behavior of
A_{n}
as
n\to\infty
, it is not needed to divide
A_{n}
by
D_{n}
because this normalization will not lead to a different limit of
A_{n}
, if it exists in some sense of stochastic convergence. As a first observation, the expected value of
A_{n}
is the centroid of
W_{n}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(A_{n}\right)=\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\int_{W_{n}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}(\nu_{d}(C(x,h),\eta))\mathrm{d}F(h)x\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\int_{W_{n}}x\mathrm{d}x=c(W_{n}).

This follows from the Mecke equation (Theorem 4.1. in
[
12
]
) and (
6
). As it turns out, the behavior of
A_{n}
is essential for obtaining a consistency result for
(\hat{\lambda}_{n},\hat{c}_{n})
, as is highlighted by the theorem below.

Theorem 4

(Consistent inversion)
.

text

1.

If the sequence
(A_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is uniformly tight, then:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\hat{\lambda}_{n},\hat{c}_{n}\right)\overset{\mathbb{P}}{=}(\lambda_{0},c_{0}).

2.

If the random variable
\sup_{n\geq 1}\|A_{n}\|
is almost surely finite, then:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\hat{\lambda}_{n},\hat{c}_{n}\right)\overset{\text{a.s.}}{=}(\lambda_{0},c_{0}).

Proof.

Let
A_{n}
,
B_{n}
and
D_{n}
be as in (
28
), (
18
) and (
17
) respectively. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
observe that:

\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)v_{x,h}c_{x,h}=B_{n}+A_{n}.

Let
\bar{\lambda}_{n}(0)
and
M_{n}
be as in (
20
) and (
21
) respectively. We may write
\hat{\lambda}_{n}
(recall (
16
)) as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{D_{n}\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in f_{0}(\eta)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}^{0}}(x)v_{x,h}\langle c_{x,h},x\rangle-\left\langle B_{n}+A_{n},A_{n}\right\rangle}{D_{n}\left(\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in f_{0}(\eta)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}^{0}}(x)v_{x,h}\|x\|^{2}\right)-\left\|A_{n}\right\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=\frac{D_{n}\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in f_{0}(\eta)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}^{0}}(x)v_{x,h}\langle c_{x,h},x\rangle-\left\|A_{n}\right\|^{2}-\left\langle B_{n},A_{n}\right\rangle}{D_{n}\left(\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in f_{0}(\eta)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}^{0}}(x)v_{x,h}\|x\|^{2}\right)}\cdot\\
&\phantom{=}\ \cdot\frac{D_{n}\left(\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in f_{0}(\eta)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}^{0}}(x)v_{x,h}\|x\|^{2}\right)}{D_{n}\left(\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in f_{0}(\eta)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}^{0}}(x)v_{x,h}\|x\|^{2}\right)-\left\|A_{n}\right\|^{2}}\end{split}

\displaystyle=\left(\bar{\lambda}_{n}(0)-\frac{\left\|A_{n}\right\|^{2}+\left\langle B_{n},A_{n}\right\rangle}{D_{n}M_{n}}\right)\frac{1}{1-\frac{\|A_{n}\|^{2}}{D_{n}M_{n}}}

By Corollary
1
we have
\lim_{n\to\infty}\bar{\lambda}_{n}(0)=\lambda_{0}
almost surely. Suppose condition 1 of the theorem is satisfied. By the uniform tightness of
A_{n}
, and since
\lim_{n\to\infty}B_{n}=0
almost surely by Lemma
6
, we obtain that
\|A_{n}\|^{2}+\langle B_{n},A_{n}\rangle
is uniformly tight. Because
\lim_{n\to\infty}M_{n}=\infty
almost surely this yields:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\left\|A_{n}\right\|^{2}+\left\langle B_{n},A_{n}\right\rangle}{D_{n}M_{n}}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{=}0\text{ \quad and \quad}\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{1-\frac{\|A_{n}\|^{2}}{D_{n}M_{n}}}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{=}1.

(29)

Here we also used the rule which is often written as
o_{P}(1)O_{P}(1)=o_{P}(1)
in stochastic o notation. Hence, via the continuous mapping theorem we obtain
\lim_{n\to\infty}\hat{\lambda}_{n}=\lambda_{0}
in probability. If condition 2 of the theorem is satisfied then the limits in (
29
) become almost sure limits, yielding
\lim_{n\to\infty}\hat{\lambda}_{n}=\lambda_{0}
almost surely. We now consider
\hat{c}_{n}
(recall (
16
)), which may be written as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{(x,h)\in f_{0}(\eta)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}^{0}}(x)v_{x,h}c_{x,h}}{\sum_{(x,h)\in f_{0}(\eta)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}^{0}}(x)v_{x,h}}-\hat{\lambda}_{n}\frac{\sum_{(x,h)\in f_{0}(\eta)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}^{0}}(x)v_{x,h}x}{\sum_{(x,h)\in f_{0}(\eta)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}^{0}}(x)v_{x,h}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)v_{x,h}c_{x,h}}{\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)v_{x,h}}-\hat{\lambda}_{n}\frac{\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)v_{x,h}\left(\frac{x}{\lambda_{0}}-\frac{c_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}\right)}{\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)v_{x,h}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)v_{x,h}\left(c_{x,h}-x\right)}{\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)v_{x,h}}+\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}}(\lambda_{0}-\hat{\lambda}_{n})\frac{\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)v_{x,h}x}{\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)v_{x,h}}+\frac{\hat{\lambda}_{n}}{\lambda_{0}}c_{0}.

\displaystyle=\frac{B_{n}}{D_{n}}+\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}}(\lambda_{0}-\hat{\lambda}_{n})\frac{A_{n}}{D_{n}}+\frac{\hat{\lambda}_{n}}{\lambda_{0}}c_{0}.

(30)

By Lemma
6
,
\lim_{n\to\infty}B_{n}/D_{n}=0
almost surely. If condition 1 of the theorem is satisfied, then
\lim_{n\to\infty}\hat{\lambda}_{n}=\lambda_{0}
in probability. Hence,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{(\lambda_{0}-\hat{\lambda}_{n})}{\lambda_{0}D_{n}}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{=}0,

here was also used that
\lim_{n\to\infty}D_{n}=1
almost surely by Lemma
6
. As a consequence, the second term of (
30
) vanishes when taking the limit in probability (
o_{P}(1)O_{P}(1)=o_{P}(1)
), since
A_{n}
is uniformly tight. Via the continuous mapping theorem we obtain
\lim_{n\to\infty}\hat{c}_{n}=c_{0}
in probability. If condition 2 of the theorem is satisfied, then
\lim_{n\to\infty}\hat{\lambda}_{n}=\lambda_{0}
almost surely. Via similar arguments we obtain
\lim_{n\to\infty}\hat{c}_{n}=c_{0}
almost surely.
∎

It seems necessary that some conditions on
(c(W_{n}))_{n\geq 1}
need to be imposed for any of the two conditions in Theorem
4
to hold. As is commonly done we may for instance choose a convex averaging sequence
(W_{n})_{n\geq 1}
which is centered at the origin. That is, we may take
W_{n}
such that
c(W_{n})=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Obviously, if
A_{n}
then converges almost surely to 0, this would imply condition 2 in Theorem
4
. While we do not know if
A_{n}
converges in general, we will now highlight via an example why the almost sure convergence of
A_{n}
to 0 should not necessarily be expected, especially if
d=2
.

Example 1
.

The vector
A_{n}
may essentially be seen as a weighted average of Poisson points, the weights being the volumes of the Laguerre cells. Let us now consider what happens if we consider the arithmetic mean of Poisson points instead. The results in this example are given without proof, as they can be obtained via relatively straightforward computations. Suppose
\psi
is a homogeneous Poisson process on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with intensity 1. For convenience, we take
W_{n}=[-\frac{1}{2}n^{1/d},\frac{1}{2}n^{1/d}]^{d}
. That is,
W_{n}
is the centered cube of volume
\nu_{d}(W_{n})=n
. Then, we define:

\bar{A}_{n}=\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{x\in\psi}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)x.

Of course, for an actual arithmetic mean we should normalize with
\psi(W_{n})
instead of
\nu_{d}(W_{n})
. However, by the spatial ergodic theorem
\lim_{n\to\infty}\psi(W_{n})/\nu_{d}(W_{n})=1
with probability one. So this normalization will not lead to a different limit of
\bar{A}_{n}
, if it exists in some sense of stochastic convergence. It can be shown that
\bar{A}_{n}
has the following limit in distribution:

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\bar{A}_{n}=\begin{cases}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{1}{12}I_{2}\right)&\text{ if }d=2\\
0&\text{ if }d\geq 3.\end{cases}

Here,
\mathcal{N}(0,\smash{\frac{1}{12}}I_{2})
denotes the multivariate normal distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with mean
0
and covariance matrix
\smash{\frac{1}{12}}I_{2}
,
I_{2}
being the
2\times 2
identity matrix. Recall that convergence in distribution to a constant implies convergence in probability to the same constant. So if
d\geq 3
the convergence also holds in probability. It can be shown that the
L^{2}
-norm of
\bar{A}_{n}
is given by:

\mathbb{E}\left(\|\bar{A}_{n}\|^{2}\right)=\frac{d}{12n^{1-\frac{2}{d}}}.

The
L^{2}
-norm highlights that the rate of convergence of
\bar{A}_{n}
to
0
in
L^{2}
for
d\geq 3
is faster in higher dimensions.

In the remainder of this section we will show that under reasonable conditions the
L^{2}
-norm of
A_{n}
is also of order
O(n^{-1+2/d})
. As a consequence, under those conditions,
(A_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is uniformly tight by Markov’s inequality. In order to compute a bound for this
L^{2}
-norm we need several results which are stated below. The proofs of these statements are given in section
9
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
h\geq 0
and
p\in\mathbb{N}
. We have the following inequalities:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\nu_{d}(C((x,h),\eta))^{p}\right)\leq pd\kappa_{d}^{p}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\kappa_{d}\int_{0}^{r^{2}+h}\left(r^{2}+h-t\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}\mathrm{d}F(t)\right)r^{pd-1}\mathrm{d}r\leq\frac{p!}{F(h)^{p}}.

Here, we set
p!/F(h)^{p}=\infty
if
F(h)=0
.

For proving Proposition
1
we use a similar technique as was used in Proposition 2.3 in
[
20
]
to derive a bound on the second moment of the volume of a typical Poisson-Voronoi cell. While the second inequality in Proposition
1
provides a finite bound for large values of
h
it is not yet clear whether the
p
-th moment is finite for all
h\geq 0
. We show this in the lemma below.

Lemma 8
.

Let
p\in\mathbb{N}
. There exists a constant
0<\alpha_{p}<\infty
such that for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
h\geq 0
, we have
\mathbb{E}\left(\nu_{d}(C((x,h),\eta))^{p}\right)<\alpha_{p}
.

Theorem 5
.

Let
p\in\mathbb{N}
. There exists a constant
0<\beta_{p}<\infty
such that for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left(\nu_{d}(C((x,h),\eta))^{p}\right)\mathrm{d}F(h)\leq\beta_{p}.

Lemma 9
.

Let
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then:

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left(\nu_{d}(C((x_{1},h_{1}),\eta+\delta_{(x_{2},h_{2})}))\nu_{d}(C((x_{2},h_{2}),\eta+\delta_{(x_{1},h_{1})}))\right)\mathrm{d}F(h_{1})\mathrm{d}F(h_{2})\leq 4.

We set
W_{n}=n^{\frac{1}{d}}W
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, where
W\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a convex body satisfying
\nu_{d}(W)=1
and
c(W)=0
. Via a direct computation we may write:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left(\left\|A_{n}\right\|^{2}\right)=\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)\nu_{d}(C(x,h),\eta)^{2}\|x\|^{2}\right)+\\
&\ +\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{(x_{1},h_{1}),(x_{2},h_{2})\in(\eta)_{\neq}^{2}}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x_{1},x_{2})\nu_{d}(C(x_{1},h_{1}),\eta)\nu_{d}(C(x_{2},h_{2}),\eta)\langle x_{1},x_{2}\rangle\right).\end{split}

(31)

Here,
(\eta)_{\neq}^{2}
denotes the set of all distinct pairs of points of
\eta
. That is, if
(x_{1},h_{1}),(x_{2},h_{2})\in(\eta)_{\neq}^{2}
, then
(x_{1},h_{1})\neq(x_{2},h_{2})
. Via the Mecke equation and Theorem
5
the first term of (
31
) is bounded from above by:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)\nu_{d}(C(x,h),\eta)^{2}\|x\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\int_{W_{n}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left(\nu_{d}(C(x,h),\eta)^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}F(h)\|x\|^{2}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\beta_{2}}{n^{2}}\int_{W_{n}}\|x\|^{2}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\beta_{2}}{n^{2}}\int_{W_{n}}\mathrm{diam}(W_{n})^{2}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta_{2}\mathrm{diam}(W)^{2}}{n^{1-\frac{2}{d}}}.

(32)

Here we also used:
\mathrm{diam}(W_{n})=n^{1/d}\mathrm{diam}(W)
. Notice that this upper bound is similar to the
L^{2}
-norm of
\bar{A}_{n}
as in example
1
. In the case of
\bar{A}_{n}
, the cross-terms vanish, due to independence. If this is also the case for
A_{n}
, this would mean the second expectation in (
31
) is equal to zero. Define the function
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
via:

\displaystyle\phi(x_{1},x_{2})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left(\nu_{d}(C((x_{1},h_{1}),\eta+\delta_{(x_{2},h_{2})}))\nu_{d}(C((x_{2},h_{2}),\eta+\delta_{(x_{1},h_{1})})\right)\mathrm{d}F(h_{1})\mathrm{d}F(h_{2}).

Then, via the multivariate Mecke equation (Theorem 4.4 in
[
12
]
) the second term in (
31
) is given by:

\frac{1}{n^{2}}\int_{W_{n}}\int_{W_{n}}\phi(x_{1},x_{2})\langle x_{1},x_{2}\rangle\mathrm{d}x_{1}\mathrm{d}x_{2}.

(33)

By Lemma
9
we know that
|\phi(x_{1},x_{2})|\leq 4
. Taking this into account and by applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we obtain the following upper bound for (
33
):

\left|\frac{1}{n^{2}}\int_{W_{n}}\int_{W_{n}}\phi(x_{1},x_{2})\langle x_{1},x_{2}\rangle\mathrm{d}x_{1}\mathrm{d}x_{2}\right|\leq\frac{4}{n^{2}}\int_{W_{n}}\int_{W_{n}}\left|\langle x_{1},x_{2}\rangle\right|\mathrm{d}x_{1}\mathrm{d}x_{2}\leq\mathrm{diam(W)^{2}}4n^{\frac{2}{d}}.

Combining this expression with (
32
) yields:

\mathbb{E}\left(\|A_{n}\|^{2}\right)\leq\frac{\beta_{2}\mathrm{diam}(W)^{2}}{n^{1-\frac{2}{d}}}+\mathrm{diam(W)^{2}}4n^{\frac{2}{d}}.

(34)

Unfortunately, the RHS of (
34
) is not uniformly bounded in
n
. It does yield
\mathbb{E}(\|A_{n}\|^{2})<\infty
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
which we will need later. Currently, it seems that deriving a tight upper bound for
\mathbb{E}\left(\|A_{n}\|^{2}\right)
is hard in general. However, under certain assumptions on
\mathbb{F}
it becomes possible to obtain a bound of order
O(n^{-1+2/d})
. To prove this, we require the notion of stabilization, which is often used in stochastic geometry for deriving laws of large numbers and central limit theorems, we refer to
[
25
]
for an overview. In the context of Poisson-Laguerre tessellations stabilization techniques were used in
[
10
]
for proving asymptotic normality of estimators of geometric characteristics of cells.

Theorem 6
.

[Propositions 3.1 and 3.2 in
[
10
]
]
Suppose
\mathbb{F}
is concentrated on
(0,M)
for some
M>0
, and let
(x,h)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,M)
. There exists a random variable
R_{x}(\eta)>0
(the radius of stabilization) such that:

1.

With probability one, for all
(x^{\prime},h^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,M)
, with
\|x-x^{\prime}\|>R_{x}(\eta)
:

C((x,h),\eta)=C\left((x,h),\eta+\delta_{(x^{\prime},h^{\prime})}\right).

2.

With probability one:

C((x,h),\eta)\subset\bar{B}(x,R_{x}(\eta)).

3.

The random variable
R_{x}(\eta)
has exponentially decaying tails. That is, there exists constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that for all
r\geq 0
:

\mathbb{P}(R_{x}(\eta)>r)<c_{1}e^{-c_{2}r}.

4.

The distribution of
R_{x}(\eta)
does not depend on
x
. Additionally,
R_{x}(\eta+\delta_{x})=R_{x}(\eta)
and
R_{x}(\eta+\delta_{x^{\prime}})\leq R_{x}(\eta)
for all
x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Additionally, we recall the Poincaré inequality, which is useful for deriving bounds of the variance of so-called Poisson functionals, if one can control the influence of inserting an additional point into the point process.

Lemma 10

(Poincaré inequality, Theorem 18.7 in
[
12
]
)
.

Suppose
\eta
is a Poisson process on the measurable space
(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{X})
with
\sigma
-finite intensity measure
\Lambda
. Suppose
f:\mathbf{N}(\mathbb{X})\to\mathbb{R}
is such that
\mathbb{E}(f(\eta)^{2})<\infty
, then:

\mathrm{Var}(f(\eta))\leq\int_{\mathbb{X}}\mathbb{E}\left((D_{x}f(\eta))^{2}\right)\Lambda(\mathrm{d}x),

with
D_{x}f(\eta)=f(\eta+\delta_{x})-f(\eta)
, for
x\in\mathbb{X}
.

Proposition
10
and Theorem
6
may be used to prove the theorem below. The proof of Theorem
7
is given in section
9
.

Theorem 7
.

Let
A_{n}
be as in (
28
) with
W_{n}=n^{\frac{1}{d}}W
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, where
W\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a convex body satisfying
\nu_{d}(W)=1
and
c(W)=0
. If
\mathbb{F}
is concentrated on
(0,M)
for some
M>0
, then there exists a constant
0<\gamma<\infty
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
:

\mathbb{E}\left(\left\|A_{n}\right\|^{2}\right)\leq\frac{\gamma}{n^{1-\frac{2}{d}}}.

As a consequence,
\lim_{n\to\infty}A_{n}=0
in
L^{2}
for
d\geq 3
. If
d\geq 2
, the sequence
(A_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is uniformly tight.

Theorem
7
guarantees consistency of the inversion procedure for a large class of choices for
\mathbb{F}
. Whether the inversion procedure is consistent for all locally finite measures
\mathbb{F}
on
(0,\infty)
is an open problem.

Remark 5
.

In general, if one does not wish to assume a uniform upper bound for the weights, it is not clear whether there is still a way to apply stabilization techniques. In the recent paper
[
3
]
a different technique, called region-stabilization, is used in the context of Poisson-Laguerre tessellations for deriving central limit theorems. Here, various parametric models are considered with unbounded weights. Perhaps similar techniques can be used to derive a bound for
\mathbb{E}(\|A_{n}\|^{2})
without assuming an upper bound for the weights.

6
Simulations

In this section we perform various simulations to empirically study the behavior of the estimator
(\hat{\lambda}_{n},\hat{c}_{n})
. We also consider a variant of
(\hat{\lambda}_{n},\hat{c}_{n})
, which takes into account edge effects. By computing these estimators, we effectively invert the observed Poisson-Laguerre tessellations. Keeping in mind the main motivation for inverting Poisson-Laguerre tessellations, we then study how well the function
F
can be estimated if the original weighted generators are unknown. That is, first we observe a Poisson-Laguerre tessellation through a bounded window and apply the inversion procedure. Then, we compute a variant of the estimator
\hat{F}_{n}^{0}
for
F
, as defined in Definition 3 in
[
27
]
, which is based on the weighted generator point obtained via the inversion procedure (
\hat{\eta}_{n}^{*}
) instead of the actual weighted generators (
\eta^{*}
). For all simulations we simulate planar (
d=2
) Poisson-Laguerre tessellations.

and

6.2
Estimation of
F

In this section we essentially investigate whether it is possible to estimate
F
, if the only available information is a region of a Poisson-Laguerre tessellation observed through a bounded observation window
W_{n}
. That is, the underlying weighted generators are considered to be unknown. This also means that cells at the boundary of the window are only partially observed. The simulations in this section may be seen as a continuation of the previous section, as the same choices for
F
are considered, and the estimates of
F
are computed based on weighted generators obtained via the inversion procedure defined via
\smash{(\bar{\lambda}_{n},\bar{c}_{n})}
.

Let
\hat{\eta}_{n}^{*}
denote the configuration of weighted generators obtained via the inversion procedure defined via
(\bar{\lambda}_{n},\bar{c}_{n})
, recall (
13
). Then,
\hat{\eta}_{n}^{*}
may be seen as an approximation of
\eta^{*}\cap(W_{n}\times\mathbb{R})
. Write
\hat{\eta}_{n}^{*}=\{(\hat{x}_{1},\hat{h}_{1}),\dots,(\hat{x}_{m},\hat{h}_{m})\}
, with
\hat{h}_{1}\leq\hat{h}_{2}\leq\dots\leq\hat{h}_{m}
. Then, analogously to the estimator
\hat{F}_{n}^{0}
for
F
as defined in
[
27
]
, we now define the estimator
\bar{F}_{n}^{0}
for
F
as follows. For
z\geq 0
define:

\bar{G}_{n}(z):=\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(\hat{x},\hat{h})\in\hat{\eta}_{n}^{*}}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(\hat{x})\mathds{1}_{(0,z]}(\hat{h})\mathds{1}\{\hat{x}\in C((\hat{x},\hat{h}),\hat{\eta}_{n}^{*})\}.

(37)

This is essentially an adaptation of the estimator
\hat{G}_{n}
for
G_{F}
as defined in
[
27
]
. We define the estimator
\bar{F}_{n}^{0}
as a piece-wise constant distribution function with jump locations at
\hat{h}_{1},\dots,\hat{h}_{m}
. Hence, we only need to specify the values
\bar{F}_{n}^{0}(\hat{h}_{i})
for
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
. Let
\hat{h}_{0}<\hat{h}_{1}
and set
\bar{F}_{n}^{0}(\hat{h}_{0})=0
. For
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
, the function
\bar{F}_{n}^{0}
is recursively defined via:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bar{F}_{n}^{0}(\hat{h}_{i})&=\bar{F}_{n}^{0}(\hat{h}_{i-1})+\left(\bar{G}_{n}(\hat{h}_{i})-\bar{G}_{n}(\hat{h}_{i-1})\right)\exp\left(\kappa_{d}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\left(\hat{h}_{i}-\hat{h}_{j}\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}\left(\bar{F}_{n}^{0}(\hat{h}_{j})-\bar{F}_{n}^{0}(\hat{h}_{j-1})\right)\right).\end{split}

(38)

is given by (
35
), in the lower panel
F

). In these simulations,
P_{n}=1000
.

As such, we now have an estimator
\bar{F}_{n}^{0}
for
F
which can be computed if the weighted generators of the (partially) observed cells are a priori unknown. We compare the obtained estimates to realizations of
\hat{F}_{n}^{0}
, which can be computed if the weighted generators of the (partially) observed cells are considered known. Then, we can see whether not knowing the generators has a noticeable effect on the resulting estimates. We should mention that for the obtained realizations of
\bar{F}_{n}^{0}
it was needed to apply a shift. Recall that the distribution of a Poisson-Laguerre tessellation is not affected by shifts of the underlying distribution function. As such, if two estimates of
F
are equal op to a shift, these estimates are considered equally accurate. Hence, to each realization of
\bar{F}_{n}^{0}
a shift was applied which essentially minimizes the average distance to the true function
F
. The results of these simulations are shown in Figure
3
.

First, consider the estimates corresponding to
F_{1}
. Visually, it appears as if
\bar{F}_{n}^{0}
yields estimates which are smoothed versions of
\hat{F}_{n}^{0}
. Overall, both estimators appear to yield accurate estimates. The estimator
\bar{F}_{n}^{0}
appears to be less accurate in the left and right tail of
F
, and it is very accurate in the middle of its support (
z
close to
0.5
). Meanwhile, realizations of
\hat{F}_{n}^{0}
are very accurate for
z
close to 0 and slowly become more inaccurate as
z
becomes larger. The estimates corresponding to
F_{2}
paint a rather different picture. While both estimators appear to provide accurate estimates of
F
for say
z<9.5
, the estimates obtained via
\bar{F}_{n}^{0}
become very inaccurate for larger values of
z
. Some of these realizations of
\bar{F}_{n}^{0}
have an upper bound which is much too large to fit in the frame of the plot. We should mention here that it is not unexpected that estimation of
F(z)
is more difficult for large values of
z
, as also discussed in
[
27
]
. Essentially, points with large weights are sampled much more rarely, since points with large weights often do not generate a cell. We also expect that for large values of
z
changes in
F(z)
only have a small effect on the distribution of the corresponding Poisson-Laguerre tessellation. However, at present we do not have a formal result to quantify this statement, it is not yet clear when an estimate of
F
may be considered accurate.

7
Concluding remarks

In this paper we derived an inversion procedure for Poisson-Laguerre tessellations. The main motivation for wanting to retrieve the weighted generators of the observed cells is for their use in statistical inference, as in
[
27
]
. We obtained various theoretical results and provided conditions for the inversion procedure to be consistent. In simulations we observed that the inversion procedure yields a very close approximation of the original weighted generators. Additionally, we have shown how these weighted generators may be used to estimate the distribution function
F
. It is however evident that if there is no prior knowledge of the original weighted generators, the resulting estimates of
F
are less accurate compared to when estimates of
F
can be computed based on the original weighted generators. Whether the estimators for
F
based on weighted generators obtained via the inversion procedure are in general consistent is an important open problem. Finally, we should mention that our inversion procedure does not take into account edge effects. A simple way to deal with edge effects in practice is described in section
6
, but perhaps more sophisticated approaches for dealing with edge effects can be incorporated into the inversion procedure in future research.

Proof of Lemma

.

Let
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
, by (
50
) we have:

\int_{0}^{\|y\|^{2}+h}\left(\|y\|^{2}+h-t\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}\mathrm{d}F(t)\geq F(h)\|y\|^{d}.

Via this inequality we obtain:

\displaystyle p_{F}(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\kappa_{d}\int_{0}^{\|y\|^{2}+h}\left(\|y\|^{2}+h-t\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}\mathrm{d}F(t)\right)\mathrm{d}F(h)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\kappa_{d}F(h)\|y\|^{d}\right)\mathrm{d}F(h)

\displaystyle=\int_{F(0)}^{F(\infty)}\exp\left(-\kappa_{d}u\|y\|^{d}\right)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\kappa_{d}\|y\|^{d}}\left(\exp\left(-\kappa_{d}\cdot 0\cdot\|y\|^{d}\right)-\exp\left(-\kappa_{d}\cdot F(\infty)\cdot\|y\|^{d}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\kappa_{d}\|y\|^{d}}.

Here, we substituted
u=F(h)
. Let
q\in\mathbb{N}
, then substituting
y=r\theta
, with
r\geq 0
and
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
we obtain:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|y\|^{q}p_{F}(y)\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|y\|^{q}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\kappa_{d}\int_{0}^{\|y\|^{2}+h}\left(\|y\|^{2}+h-t\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}\mathrm{d}F(t)\right)\mathrm{d}F(h)\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=d\kappa_{d}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\kappa_{d}\int_{0}^{r^{2}+h}\left(r^{2}+h-t\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}\mathrm{d}F(t)\right)r^{q+d-1}\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}F(h).

This expression is very similar to an expression which appears in the proof of Theorem
5
. Indeed, if we were to set
q=(p-1)d
,
p\in\mathbb{N}
, then the proof of Theorem
5
yields
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|y\|^{q}p_{F}(y)\mathrm{d}y<\infty
. Because Theorem
5
holds for any
d,p\in\mathbb{N}
we may simply choose
p
sufficiently large such that
q\leq(p-1)d
and then we obtain the result via Hölder’s inequality:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|y\|^{q}p_{F}(y)\mathrm{d}y\leq\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|y\|^{(p-1)d}p_{F}(y)\mathrm{d}y\right)^{\frac{(p-1)d}{q}}<\infty.

∎

Proof of Lemma

Assume
\varphi\cap B_{x,k}\neq\emptyset

k\in\{1,\dots,J\}

y\in C((x,h),\varphi)-x

y\in\mathcal{C}_{j}
for some
j\in\{1,\dots,J\}
. Choose
x_{j}\in\varphi\cap B_{x,j}
. Then,
2\sqrt{z}<\|x_{j}-x\|<R
and
x_{j}-x\in\mathcal{C}_{j}
. Because
y\in C((x,h),\varphi)-x
and
x_{j}\in\varphi
we have:

\displaystyle\|y\|^{2}+h\leq\|x_{j}-x-y\|^{2}+h_{j}=\|x_{j}-x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2}-2\langle x_{j}-x,y\rangle+h_{j}

Using
2\sqrt{z}<\|x_{j}-x\|

2\langle x_{j}-x,y\rangle\leq\|x_{j}-x\|^{2}+h_{j}-h\leq\|x_{j}-x\|^{2}+z<\frac{5}{4}\|x_{j}-x\|^{2}.

(39)

Because
y\in\mathcal{C}_{j}
,
x_{j}-x\in\mathcal{C}_{j}
we have by the definition of
\mathcal{C}_{j}
:

\langle x_{j}-x,y\rangle\leq\frac{3}{4}\|x_{j}-x\|\cdot\|y\|\iff\|y\|\leq\frac{4}{3}\frac{\langle x_{j}-x,y\rangle}{\|x_{j}-x\|}

Combining this with (
39

\|y\|\leq\frac{4}{3}\frac{\langle x_{j}-x,y\rangle}{\|x_{j}-x\|}<\frac{5}{6}\frac{\|x_{j}-x\|^{2}}{\|x_{j}-x\|}<\|x_{j}-x\|<R.

Indeed,
C((x,h),\varphi)-x\subset B(0,R)\iff C((x,h),\varphi)\subset B(x,R)
.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

First, consider the case
\lambda=\lambda_{0}
. Equation (
12
) may be written as:

\displaystyle T_{n}(\lambda_{0},c)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\int_{C((x,h),\eta)}\|x-c_{0}+c-y\|^{2}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\int_{C((x,h),\eta)-x}\|c-c_{0}-y\|^{2}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\int_{C((0,h),S_{x}\eta)}\|c-c_{0}-y\|^{2}\mathrm{d}y

Applying the spatial ergodic theorem (see Proposition 13.4.I. in
[
7
]
),
T_{n}(\lambda_{0},c)
converges almost surely to its expected value (see Lemma
3
). Now, consider the case
\lambda\neq\lambda_{0}
. Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space supporting the Poisson process
\eta
. In order to show that
\lim_{n\to\infty}T_{n}(\lambda,c)=\infty
almost surely, it is sufficient to show that for all
M>0
there exists a
\Omega_{M}\in\mathcal{A}
with
\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{M})=1
such that for all
\omega\in\Omega_{M}
:
\liminf_{n\to\infty}T_{n}(\lambda,c;\omega)>M
. Indeed, setting
\Omega_{0}=\cap_{M\in\mathbb{N}}\Omega_{M}
, we have
\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{0})=1
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}T_{n}(\lambda,c;\omega)=\infty
for all
\omega\in\Omega_{0}
. Let
M>0
. We need some additional notation before we choose
\Omega_{M}
. Pick
z>0
such that
F(z)>0
. Take
R>0
large enough such that
R>2\sqrt{z}
. Take
r>0
small enough such that
r+\sqrt{r^{2}+z}<2\sqrt{z}
. One may for instance take
r=\smash{\sqrt{z/3}}
. For any
(x,h)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty)
let the set
D_{x,h}
be as in Lemma
4
and the sets
B_{x,j}
,
j\in\{1,\dots,J\}
as in Lemma
5
. For
(x,h)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty)
define the following event:

O_{x,h}=\{\eta(D_{x,h}\setminus\{(x,h)\})=0,\eta(B_{x,j})>0\ \ \forall j\in\{1,\dots,J\}\}.

Note that
D_{x,h}\cap B_{x,j}=\emptyset
for all
j\in\{1,\dots,J\}
. Additionally, the
B_{x,j}
’s have disjoint interiors and therefore we have:

\mathbb{P}(O_{x,h})=\mathbb{P}(\eta(D_{x,h}\setminus\{(x,h)\})=0)\prod_{j=1}^{J}\mathbb{P}\left(\eta(B_{x,j})>0\right).

We will now argue that
\mathbb{P}(O_{x,h})=\mathbb{P}(O_{0,h})\geq\alpha>0
, where
\alpha
is a constant which does not depend on
h
. Note that proving this implies the following:

\int_{0}^{z}\mathbb{P}(O_{0,h})\mathrm{d}F(h)\geq\alpha F(z)>0.

(40)

Since
(\nu_{d}\times\mathbb{F})(\{(x,h)\})=0
we have
\mathbb{P}(\eta(D_{x,h}\setminus\{(x,h)\})=0)=\mathbb{P}(\eta(D_{x,h})=0)
, which may be computed as:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\eta(D_{x,h})=0)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{0}^{r^{2}+h}\mathds{1}\{\|x-x^{\prime}\|<r+\sqrt{r^{2}+h-h^{\prime}}\}\mathrm{d}F(h^{\prime})\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\kappa_{d}\int_{0}^{r^{2}+h}\left(r+\sqrt{r^{2}+h-h^{\prime}}\right)^{d}\mathrm{d}F(h^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle\geq\exp\left(-\kappa_{d}\int_{0}^{r^{2}+z}\left(z+\sqrt{r^{2}+z}\right)^{d}\mathrm{d}F(h^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\kappa_{d}F(r^{2}+z)\left(z+\sqrt{r^{2}+z}\right)^{d}\right)>0.

Let
j\in\{1,\dots,J\}
, then:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\eta(B_{x,j})>0\right)

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}\left(\eta(B_{x,j})=0\right)

\displaystyle=1-\exp\left(-F(z)\nu_{d}\left(\left(B(x,R)\setminus\bar{B}(x,2\sqrt{z})\right)\cap(\mathcal{C}_{j}+x)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=1-\exp\left(-F(z)\nu_{d}\left(\left(B(0,R)\setminus\bar{B}(0,2\sqrt{z})\right)\cap\mathcal{C}_{j}\right)\right)>0.

So indeed, there exists some
\alpha>0
such that for all
h\in[0,z]
we have
\mathbb{P}(O_{x,h})=\mathbb{P}(O_{0,h})\geq\alpha>0
. We now use the event
O_{x,h}
to obtain a lower bound for
T_{n}(\lambda,c)
:

\displaystyle T_{n}(\lambda,c)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)\mathds{1}_{(0,z]}(h)v_{x,h}\left\|\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{0}}-1\right)x-\frac{\lambda c_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}+c-(c_{x,h}-x)\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)\mathds{1}_{(0,z]}(h)v_{x,h}\left\|\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{0}}-1\right)x-\frac{\lambda c_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}+c-(c_{x,h}-x)\right\|^{2}\mathds{1}\{O_{x,h}\}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\kappa_{d}r^{d}}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)\mathds{1}_{(0,z]}(h)\left\|\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{0}}-1\right)x-\frac{\lambda c_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}+c-(c_{x,h}-x)\right\|^{2}\mathds{1}\{O_{x,h}\}.

The final inequality follows from the fact that if the event
O_{x,h}
occurs then
B(x,r)\subset C((x,h),\eta)
, hence
v_{x,h}\geq\kappa_{d}r^{d}
. Let
N>0
be large enough such that:

\kappa_{d}r^{d}\left(\frac{N-\|c_{0}\|}{\lambda_{0}}\left|\lambda-\lambda_{0}\right|-\|c-c_{0}\|-R\right)^{2}\int_{0}^{z}\mathbb{P}(O_{0,h})\mathrm{d}F(h)>M.

Choose
\Omega_{M}\in\mathcal{A}
with
\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{M})=1
such that for all
\omega\in\Omega_{M}
the following two properties hold:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta({\mspace{2mu}\cdot\mspace{2mu}};\omega)}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)\mathds{1}_{(0,z]}(h)\mathds{1}\{O_{x,h}(\omega)\}=\int_{0}^{z}\mathbb{P}(O_{0,h})\mathrm{d}F(h)

\eta(\bar{B}(0,N)\times(0,z];\omega)<\infty

Such a set exists by the spatial ergodic theorem (Proposition 13.4.I. in
[
7
]
) and by the fact that a Poisson random variable with a finite rate parameter is almost surely finite. Suppose
(x,h)\in\eta
,
\|x\|>N
and the event
O_{x,h}
occurs such that
C((x,h),\eta)\subset B(x,R)
and therefore
\|c_{x,h}-x\|<R
. Then, we claim that the following holds:

\left\|\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{0}}-1\right)x-\frac{\lambda c_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}+c-(c_{x,h}-x)\right\|\geq\frac{N-\|c_{0}\|}{\lambda_{0}}|\lambda-\lambda_{0}|-\|c-c_{0}\|-R.

(41)

We will now verify this claim.

\displaystyle\left\|\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{0}}-1\right)x-\frac{\lambda c_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}+c-(c_{x,h}-x)\right\|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}}\left|\lambda-\lambda_{0}\right|\cdot\left\|x-\left(\frac{\lambda c_{0}-\lambda_{0}c+\lambda_{0}(c_{x,h}-x)}{\lambda-\lambda_{0}}\right)\right\|.

(42)

Consider the term in brackets in the RHS of (
42
), we obtain the following upper bound on its norm:

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\lambda c_{0}-\lambda_{0}c+\lambda_{0}(c_{x,h}-x)}{\lambda-\lambda_{0}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{|\lambda-\lambda_{0}|}\left(\left\|\lambda c_{0}-\lambda_{0}c\right\|+\lambda_{0}\left\|c_{x,h}-x\right\|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{|\lambda-\lambda_{0}|}\left(\left\|\lambda c_{0}-\lambda_{0}c_{0}\right\|+\left\|\lambda_{0}c_{0}-\lambda_{0}c\right\|+\lambda_{0}R\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{|\lambda-\lambda_{0}|}\left(|\lambda-\lambda_{0}|\|c_{0}\|+\lambda_{0}\|c-c_{0}\|+\lambda_{0}R\right)

\displaystyle\leq\|c_{0}\|+\frac{\lambda_{0}}{|\lambda-\lambda_{0}|}\left(\|c-c_{0}\|+R\right).

Proof of Proposition

.

We start with proving the first inequality in the proposition for

. Note that:
\nu_{d}(C((x,h),\eta))=\nu_{d}(C((x,h),\eta)-x)
by the translation invariance of Lebesgue measure. For
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
recall that
y\in C((x,h),\eta)-x\iff\eta(A_{x,h,y})=0
with
A_{x,h,y}
as in (
3

5
) we now obtain:

\mathbb{E}\left(\nu_{d}(C((x,h),\eta))^{p}\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\dots\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{P}\left(\eta(A_{x,h,y_{1}})=0,\dots,\eta(A_{x,h,y_{p}})=0\right)\mathrm{d}y_{1}\dots\mathrm{d}y_{p}.

(46)

Instead of integrating over
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{p}
we may also integrate over the union of all sets of the form:

\mathcal{C}_{I}=\left\{(y_{1},\dots,y_{p})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{p}:\|y_{i_{1}}\|\leq\|y_{i_{2}}\|\leq\dots\leq\|y_{i_{p}}\|\right\},

where
I=(i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{p})
is a permutation of
(1,2,\dots,p)
. Note that the integrand in (
46
) is symmetric in
y_{1},\dots,y_{p}
. Hence, when integrating over any set
\mathcal{C}_{I}
the result is the same for every choice of
I
. Because there are
p!
permutations of
(1,2,\dots,p)
, we may write:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\nu_{d}(C((x,h),\eta))^{p}\right)

\displaystyle=p!\idotsint_{\mathcal{C}}\mathbb{P}\left(\eta(A_{x,h,y_{1}})=0,\dots,\eta(A_{x,h,y_{p}})=0\right)\mathrm{d}y_{1}\dots\mathrm{d}y_{p}

\displaystyle\leq p!\idotsint_{\mathcal{C}}\mathbb{P}\left(\eta(A_{x,h,y_{p}})=0\right)\mathrm{d}y_{1}\dots\mathrm{d}y_{p}

\displaystyle=p!\idotsint_{\mathcal{C}}\exp\left(-\kappa_{d}\int_{0}^{\|y_{p}\|^{2}+h}\left(\|y_{p}\|^{2}+h-t\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}\mathrm{d}F(t)\right)\mathrm{d}y_{1}\dots\mathrm{d}y_{p}.

(47)

The final equality follows from (
4
) and
\mathcal{C}
is given by:

\mathcal{C}=\left\{(y_{1},\dots,y_{p})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{p}:\|y_{1}\|\leq\|y_{2}\|\leq\dots\leq\|y_{p}\|\right\}.

Next, we substitute
y_{i}=r_{i}\theta_{i}
with
r_{i}\geq 0
and
\theta_{i}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
for
i\in\{1,\dots,p\}
. Then (
47
) is given by:

\displaystyle p!(d\kappa_{d})^{p}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{r_{p}}\dots\int_{0}^{r_{3}}\int_{0}^{r_{2}}\varphi(r_{p})r_{1}^{d-1}\cdots r_{p}^{d-1}\mathrm{d}r_{1}\dots\mathrm{d}r_{p}

\displaystyle=p!(d\kappa_{d})^{p}\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi(r_{p})\left(\int_{0}^{r_{p}}\dots\int_{0}^{r_{3}}\int_{0}^{r_{2}}r_{1}^{d-1}\cdots r_{p}^{d-1}\mathrm{d}r_{1}\dots\mathrm{d}r_{p-1}\right)\mathrm{d}r_{p},

(48)

with:

\varphi(r_{p})=\exp\left(-\kappa_{d}\int_{0}^{r_{p}^{2}+h}\left(r_{p}^{2}+h-t\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}\mathrm{d}F(t)\right).

The integral in brackets in (
48

\int_{0}^{r_{p}}\dots\int_{0}^{r_{3}}\int_{0}^{r_{2}}r_{1}^{d-1}\cdots r_{p}^{d-1}\mathrm{d}r_{1}\dots\mathrm{d}r_{p-1}=\frac{1}{(p-1)!d^{p-1}}r_{p}^{dp-1}.

Plugging this back into (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\nu_{d}(C((x,h),\eta))^{p}\right)

\displaystyle\leq pd\kappa_{d}^{p}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\kappa_{d}\int_{0}^{r_{p}^{2}+h}\left(r_{p}^{2}+h-t\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}\mathrm{d}F(t)\right)r_{p}^{pd-1}\mathrm{d}r_{p}.

(49)

In the case
p=1
(
5
) may be used to show that (
49
) becomes an equality as none of the techniques used to obtain an upper bound for the case
p\geq 2
need to be used. Consider the second inequality for general

. Via integration by parts, we obtain:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{r_{p}^{2}+h}\left(r_{p}^{2}+h-t\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}\mathrm{d}F(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{2}\int_{0}^{r_{p}^{2}+h}\left(r_{p}^{2}+h-t\right)^{\frac{d}{2}-1}F(t)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\geq\frac{d}{2}\int_{h}^{r_{p}^{2}+h}\left(r_{p}^{2}+h-t\right)^{\frac{d}{2}-1}F(t)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\geq F(h)\frac{d}{2}\int_{h}^{r_{p}^{2}+h}\left(r_{p}^{2}+h-t\right)^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=F(h)r_{p}^{d}.

We now plug this lower bound into (

Here, we substituted

\mathbb{E}(A_{n})=0
we may write:

\mathbb{E}\left(\left\|A_{n}\right\|^{2}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(A_{n,1}^{2}+A_{n,2}^{2}+\dots+A_{n,d}^{2}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathrm{Var}(A_{n,j}).

(58)

We will apply Lemma
10
to
\mathrm{Var}(A_{n,j})
for each
j
. The fact that
\mathbb{E}(A_{n,j}^{2})<\infty
follows from (
34
). Fix
j\in\{1,\dots,d\}
and set
f(\eta)=A_{n,j}
. Let
(x^{\prime},h^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,M)
. For
(x,h)\in\eta
let
R_{x}(\eta)
be as in Theorem
6
, via property 1 in Theorem
6
we may write:

\displaystyle f(\eta+\delta_{(x^{\prime},h^{\prime})})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta+\delta_{(x^{\prime},h^{\prime})}}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)\nu_{d}(C((x,h),\eta+\delta_{(x^{\prime},h^{\prime})}))x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x^{\prime})\nu_{d}(C((x^{\prime},h^{\prime}),\eta))x^{\prime}+

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)\nu_{d}(C((x,h),\eta+\delta_{(x^{\prime},h^{\prime})}))x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x^{\prime})\nu_{d}(C((x^{\prime},h^{\prime}),\eta))x^{\prime}+

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)\mathds{1}\{\|x-x^{\prime}\|\leq R_{x}(\eta)\}\nu_{d}((C(x,h),\eta+\delta_{(x^{\prime},h^{\prime})}))x

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)\mathds{1}\{\|x-x^{\prime}\|>R_{x}(\eta)\}\nu_{d}(C((x,h),\eta))x.

Similarly, we may write:

\displaystyle f(\eta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)\mathds{1}\{\|x-x^{\prime}\|>R_{x}(\eta)\}\nu_{d}(C((x,h),\eta))x+

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\frac{1}{\nu_{d}(W_{n})}\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)\mathds{1}\{\|x-x^{\prime}\|\leq R_{x}(\eta)\}\nu_{d}(C((x,h),\eta))x.

Using these expressions for
f(\eta+\delta_{(x^{\prime},h^{\prime})})
and
f(\eta)
we derive an upper bound for

|D_{(x^{\prime},h^{\prime})}f(\eta)|
. Taking into account that
\|x\|\leq\mathrm{diam}(W)n^{1/d}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle|D_{(x^{\prime},h^{\prime})}f(\eta)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x^{\prime})\nu_{d}(C((x^{\prime},h^{\prime}),\eta))\|x^{\prime}\|+

\displaystyle\phantom{\leq}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}\cap\bar{B}(x^{\prime},R_{x}(\eta))}(x)\left|\nu_{d}(C((x,h),\eta))-\nu_{d}(C((x,h),\eta+\delta_{(x^{\prime},h^{\prime})}))\right|\|x\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathrm{diam}(W)}{n^{1-\frac{1}{d}}}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x^{\prime})\nu_{d}(C(x^{\prime},h^{\prime}),\eta)+

\displaystyle\phantom{\leq}+\frac{\mathrm{diam}(W)}{n^{1-\frac{1}{d}}}\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)\mathds{1}\{\|x-x^{\prime}\|\leq R_{x}(\eta)\}\nu_{d}(C((x,h),\eta)).

Here, we also used the fact:
C((x,h),\eta+\delta_{(x^{\prime},h^{\prime})})\subset C((x,h),\eta)
. Note that this upper bound does not depend on
j
. Via Lemma
10
and (
58
) we obtain:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left(\left\|A_{n}\right\|^{2}\right)&\leq\frac{\mathrm{diam}(W)^{2}d}{n^{2-\frac{2}{d}}}\Bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{0}^{M}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x^{\prime})\mathbb{E}\left(\nu_{d}(C((x^{\prime},h^{\prime}),\eta))^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}F(h^{\prime})\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\\
&\phantom{\leq}\ +\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{0}^{M}\mathbb{E}\Bigg(\bigg(\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)\mathds{1}\{\|x-x^{\prime}\|\leq R_{x}(\eta)\}\nu_{d}(C((x,h),\eta))\bigg)^{2}\Bigg)\mathrm{d}F(h^{\prime})\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\Bigg).\end{split}

(59)

We now separately determine upper bounds for both of the integrals appearing in (
59
). For the first integral we obtain the following:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{0}^{M}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x^{\prime})\mathbb{E}\left(\nu_{d}(C((x^{\prime},h^{\prime}),\eta))^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}F(h^{\prime})\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x^{\prime})\beta_{2}\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}=\beta_{2}n.

(60)

Here,
\beta_{2}
is as in Theorem
5
. Let
(\eta)_{\neq}^{2}
denotes the set of all distinct pairs of points of
\eta
. That is, if
(x_{1},h_{1}),(x_{2},h_{2})\in(\eta)_{\neq}^{2}
, then
(x_{1},h_{1})\neq(x_{2},h_{2})
. Now, consider the second integral in (
59
). Expanding the square and applying Fubini, we obtain:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{0}^{M}\mathbb{E}\Bigg(\bigg(\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)\mathds{1}\{\|x-x^{\prime}\|\leq R_{x}(\eta)\}\nu_{d}(C((x,h),\eta))\bigg)^{2}\Bigg)\mathrm{d}F(h^{\prime})\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{0}^{M}\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)\mathds{1}\{\|x-x^{\prime}\|\leq R_{x}(\eta)\}\nu_{d}(C((x,h),\eta))^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}F(h^{\prime})\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}+

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{0}^{M}\mathbb{E}\Bigg(\sum_{(x_{1},h_{1}),(x_{2},h_{2})\in(\eta)_{\neq}^{2}}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x_{1})\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x_{2})\mathds{1}\{\|x_{1}-x^{\prime}\|\leq R_{x_{1}}(\eta)\}\cdot

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\cdot\mathds{1}\{\|x_{2}-x^{\prime}\|\leq R_{x_{2}}(\eta)\}\nu_{d}(C((x_{1},h_{1}),\eta))\nu_{d}(C((x_{2},h_{2}),\eta))\Bigg)\mathrm{d}F(h^{\prime})\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}

\displaystyle=F(M)\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathds{1}\{\|x-x^{\prime}\|\leq R_{x}(\eta)\}\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\nu_{d}(C((x,h),\eta))^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+F(M)\mathbb{E}\Bigg(\sum_{(x_{1},h_{1}),(x_{2},h_{2})\in(\eta)_{\neq}^{2}}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x_{1})\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x_{2})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathds{1}\{\|x_{1}-x^{\prime}\|\leq R_{x_{1}}(\eta)\}\cdot

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\cdot\mathds{1}\{\|x_{2}-x^{\prime}\|\leq R_{x_{2}}(\eta)\}\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\nu_{d}(C((x_{1},h_{1}),\eta))\nu_{d}(C((x_{2},h_{2}),\eta))\Bigg)

\displaystyle=F(M)\kappa_{d}\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)R_{x}(\eta)^{d}\nu_{d}(C((x,h),\eta))^{2}\right)

(61)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\phantom{=}+F(M)\mathbb{E}\Bigg(\sum_{(x_{1},h_{1}),(x_{2},h_{2})\in(\eta)_{\neq}^{2}}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x_{1})\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x_{2})\nu_{d}(\bar{B}(x_{1},R_{x_{1}}(\eta))\cap\bar{B}(x_{2},R_{x_{2}}(\eta)))\cdot\\
&\phantom{=}\cdot\nu_{d}(C((x_{1},h_{1}),\eta))\nu_{d}(C((x_{2},h_{2}),\eta))\Bigg).\end{split}

(62)

An upper bound for the term in (
61
) may be computed via the Mecke equation as follows:

\displaystyle F(M)\kappa_{d}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)R_{x}(\eta)^{d}\nu_{d}(\bar{B}(x,R_{x}(\eta)))^{2}\right)\leq

\displaystyle\leq F(M)\kappa_{d}^{3}\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{(x,h)\in\eta}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)R_{x}(\eta)^{3d}\right)

\displaystyle=F(M)\kappa_{d}^{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{0}^{M}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x)\mathbb{E}(R_{x}(\eta+\delta_{x})^{3d})\mathrm{d}F(h)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq F(M)^{2}\kappa_{d}^{3}\mathbb{E}\left(R_{0}(\eta)^{3d}\right)n.

(63)

Here, we also used point 4 of Theorem
6
. Because
R_{0}(\eta)
has exponentially decaying tails by point 3 of Theorem
6
, all of its moments are finite, hence
\mathbb{E}(R_{0}(\eta)^{3d})<\infty
. Next, we consider the term in (
62
). This term may be computed via the multivariate Mecke equation (Theorem 4.4 in
[
12
]
). By also using points 2 and 4 of Theorem
6
we obtain:

\displaystyle\phantom{=}F(M)\mathbb{E}\Bigg(\sum_{(x_{1},h_{1}),(x_{2},h_{2})\in(\eta)_{\neq}^{2}}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x_{1})\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x_{2})\nu_{d}(\bar{B}(x_{1},R_{x_{1}}(\eta))\cap\bar{B}(x_{2},R_{x_{2}}(\eta)))\cdot

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\cdot\nu_{d}(C((x_{1},h_{1}),\eta))\nu_{d}(C((x_{2},h_{2}),\eta))\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq F(M)\kappa_{d}^{2}\mathbb{E}\Bigg(\sum_{(x_{1},h_{1}),(x_{2},h_{2})\in(\eta)_{\neq}^{2}}\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x_{1})\mathds{1}_{W_{n}}(x_{2})\nu_{d}(\bar{B}(x_{1},R_{x_{1}}(\eta))\cap\bar{B}(x_{2},R_{x_{2}}(\eta)))\cdot

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\cdot R_{x_{1}}(\eta)^{d}R_{x_{2}}(\eta)^{d}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=F(M)\kappa_{d}^{2}\int_{W_{n}}\int_{0}^{M}\int_{W_{n}}\int_{0}^{M}\mathbb{E}\Bigg(\nu_{d}(\bar{B}(x_{1},R_{x_{1}}(\eta+\delta_{x_{2}}))\cap\bar{B}(x_{2},R_{x_{2}}(\eta+\delta_{x_{1}})))\cdot

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\cdot R_{x_{1}}(\eta+\delta_{x_{2}})^{d}R_{x_{2}}(\eta+\delta_{x_{1}})^{d}\Bigg)\mathrm{d}F(h_{1})\mathrm{d}x_{1}\mathrm{d}F(h_{2})\mathrm{d}x_{2}

\displaystyle\leq F(M)^{3}\kappa_{d}^{2}\int_{W_{n}}\int_{W_{n}}\mathbb{E}\Bigg(\nu_{d}(\bar{B}(x_{1},R_{x_{1}}(\eta))\cap\bar{B}(x_{2},R_{x_{2}}(\eta)))R_{x_{1}}(\eta)^{d}R_{x_{2}}(\eta)^{d}\Bigg)\mathrm{d}x_{1}\mathrm{d}x_{2}

(64)

Note that for any
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\displaystyle\nu_{d}(\bar{B}(x_{1},R_{x_{1}}(\eta))\cap\bar{B}(x_{2},R_{x_{2}}(\eta)))

\displaystyle=\nu_{d}(\bar{B}(x_{1},R_{x_{1}}(\eta))\cap\bar{B}(x_{2},R_{x_{2}}(\eta)))\cdot

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\ \cdot\mathds{1}\{\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|\leq R_{x_{1}}(\eta)+R_{x_{2}}(\eta)\}

As a consequence, (
64
) may be written as:

\displaystyle F(M)^{3}\kappa_{d}^{2}\int_{W_{n}}\int_{W_{n}}\mathbb{E}\Bigg(\nu_{d}(\bar{B}(x_{1},R_{x_{1}}(\eta))\cap\bar{B}(x_{2},R_{x_{2}}(\eta)))R_{x_{1}}(\eta)^{d}R_{x_{2}}(\eta)^{d}\cdot

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\cdot\mathds{1}\{\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|\leq R_{x_{1}}(\eta)+R_{x_{2}}(\eta)\}\Bigg)\mathrm{d}x_{1}\mathrm{d}x_{2}

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=F(M)^{3}\kappa_{d}^{2}\int_{W_{n}}\int_{W_{n}}\mathbb{E}\Bigg(\nu_{d}(\bar{B}(x_{1},R_{x_{1}}(\eta))\cap\bar{B}(x_{2},R_{x_{2}}(\eta)))R_{x_{1}}(\eta)^{d}R_{x_{2}}(\eta)^{d}\cdot\\
&\phantom{=}\cdot\mathds{1}\{\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|\leq R_{x_{1}}(\eta)+R_{x_{2}}(\eta)\}\mathds{1}\{R_{x_{1}}(\eta)\leq R_{x_{2}}(\eta)\}\Bigg)\mathrm{d}x_{1}\mathrm{d}x_{2}+\end{split}

(65)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\phantom{=}+F(M)^{3}\kappa_{d}^{2}\int_{W_{n}}\int_{W_{n}}\mathbb{E}\Bigg(\nu_{d}(\bar{B}(x_{1},R_{x_{1}}(\eta))\cap\bar{B}(x_{2},R_{x_{2}}(\eta)))R_{x_{1}}(\eta)^{d}R_{x_{2}}(\eta)^{d}\cdot\\
&\phantom{=}\cdot\mathds{1}\{\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|\leq R_{x_{1}}(\eta)+R_{x_{2}}(\eta)\}\mathds{1}\{R_{x_{1}}(\eta)>R_{x_{2}}(\eta)\}\Bigg)\mathrm{d}x_{1}\mathrm{d}x_{2}\end{split}

(66)

We now derive an upper bound for the expression in (
65
), the obtained upper bound will also hold for (
66
) by symmetry in
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
. The following is an upper bound for (
65
):

\displaystyle\phantom{\leq}F(M)^{3}\kappa_{d}^{2}\int_{W_{n}}\int_{W_{n}}\mathbb{E}\Bigg(\nu_{d}(\bar{B}(x_{2},R_{x_{2}}(\eta)))R_{x_{2}}(\eta)^{2d}\mathds{1}\{\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|\leq 2R_{x_{2}}(\eta)\}\cdot

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\cdot\mathds{1}\{R_{x_{1}}(\eta)\leq R_{x_{2}}(\eta)\}\Bigg)\mathrm{d}x_{1}\mathrm{d}x_{2}

\displaystyle\leq F(M)^{3}\kappa_{d}^{3}\int_{W_{n}}\mathbb{E}\Bigg(R_{x_{2}}(\eta)^{3d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathds{1}\{\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|\leq 2R_{x_{2}}(\eta)\}\mathrm{d}x_{1}\Bigg)\mathrm{d}x_{2}

\displaystyle=F(M)^{3}\kappa_{d}^{4}2^{d}\mathbb{E}(R_{0}(\eta)^{4d})n.

(67)

Plugging (
60
), (
63
) and (
67
) back into (
59
) yields the desired result.

\mathbb{E}\left(\left\|A_{n}\right\|^{2}\right)\leq\frac{\mathrm{diam}(W)^{2}d}{n^{1-\frac{2}{d}}}\left(\beta_{2}+F(M)^{2}\kappa_{d}^{3}\mathbb{E}\left(R_{0}(\eta)^{3d}\right)+F(M)^{3}\kappa_{d}^{4}2^{d+1}\mathbb{E}\left(R_{0}(\eta)^{4d}\right)\right).

∎
