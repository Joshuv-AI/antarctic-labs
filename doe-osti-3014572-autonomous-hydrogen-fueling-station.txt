 3. Executive Summary

This project “Autonomous Hydrogen Fueling Station” covered the autonomous refueling
with both gaseous hydrogen and liquid hydrogen. The part on gaseous hydrogen focused
on the development of an autonomous robotic fueling arm that would couple to a fuel cell
engine for hydrogen refueling without guidance from the forklift operator and budget
period. Research was also covered for the robotic fueling with a commercial vehicle.
The second phase of the project created the baseline for an autonomous liquid hydrogen
transfer system that would minimize boil off losses by operating at thermodynamically
efficient state points.
For the development of the robotic fueling arm, testing was conducted to establish a
baseline measurement of the accuracy and repeatability of a human operator positioning
a lift truck in front of a dispenser. The goal was to establish the range of motion required
for an autonomous fueling mechanism to mate a hydrogen nozzle with a receptacle on a
fuel cell system installed in a forklift. The final design comprised a selective compliance
articulated robot arm (SCARA)-type mechanism with two arms for horizontal motion and
a ball screw for vertical movement and color and LIDAR cameras were used for marker
identification and proximity awareness to guide the robotic arm to its target receptacle.
Initial tests resulted in 199 out of 200 successful attempts at autonomous coupling of the
dispensing coupler and a fuel cell engine, without hydrogen. The dispenser prototype was
modified to include tubing for both hydrogen fuel and air purge lines, but subsequent tests
were confounded by the shoulder motor over current errors which limited the robot from
getting to the fully inserted position to achieve a positive latch. Robotic hydrogen refueling
was successfully demonstrated over 1.5 hours of testing, Plug completed 29 successful
latches with an average number of 4 sequential latches before failure. However, a robot
capable of placing the nozzle with more force is required for higher reliability.
For budget period two, a small scale (10 kg / transfer) automated control system was
designed that would operate valves to control pressure and flow of liquid nitrogen
between a source and receiving tank with an aim to minimize boil off losses by operating
at the most thermodynamically efficient state points. Control system logic flow and a P&ID
were developed prior to system safety characterization via HAZOP. A control narrative
and system state points were defined. Delays in approval for a change of project objective
and procurement issues precluded the construction and test of the final prototype system.

 5. Background

As one of the leading suppliers of fuel cell systems and hydrogen infrastructure in the
world, Plug Power (Plug) is familiar with the challenges faced by customers in siting and
training for hydrogen (H2) fueling stations. These challenges include safety concerns for
personnel handling high pressure components, difficulty training operators in high
turnover industries like material handling and maintenance costs associated with
mishandling of equipment, and also minimizing the loss of hydrogen during transfer. Fuel
cell infrastructure must exceed the standards and expectations set by gasoline fueling to
facilitate more widespread adoption of the technology. Automating the hydrogen fueling
process so that fuel dispensing equipment can autonomously mate and efficiently
interlock with vehicles in need of refueling offers maximum safety as well as a stronger
value proposition for fueling station operators and end users and provides a differentiator
versus gasoline fueling or battery charging. Fueling of on-road hydrogen vehicles is also
a growing need, however the requirements for such applications are distinct from those
of materials handling.
For liquid hydrogen solutions, the system should be able to transfer the hydrogen safely
and autonomously with minimal evaporation losses. The convenience of automated
fueling and automated fueling algorithms for liquid hydrogen would allow drivers and
operators to spend their time more effectively on other tasks. Automated fueling also
enables the adoption of autonomous vehicles, both in the material handling and
passenger vehicle industries, by eliminating the need for human interaction to provide
round-the-clock service of the vehicle.
 6. Objectives

The first program objective is to develop an autonomous H2 fueling dispenser with a H2
nozzle controlled by a robotic arm for a field-deployable automated Autonomous Guided
Vehicle (AGV). The dispenser shall achieve a reduction in connection time of 20% versus
a human operator as well as a connection success rate of 99% in a controlled
environment and 95% or better in a real-world trial.
Concepts and requirements were also developed for the automated fueling of commercial
on-road vehicles with a target connection success rate of 95% using a modified off-the-
shelf robot.
Another objective was to improve the efficiency of automated fueling of heavy-duty
vehicles with liquid hydrogen, specifically to increase the transfer efficiency of liquid
hydrogen from tanker. While some loss of hydrogen is inevitable during these pressure
transfer events, the aim of this research is to identify the optimum pressure and transfer
rates depending on the fill level of supply and receiving tanks to minimize vented mass.
To reliably and consistently meet these pressure and flow rate targets, we will determine
which aspects of the transfer process can be automated using a control system to
regulate valve open and close events. This work will leverage prior liquid hydrogen
transfer modeling research funded internally at Plug to direct the design and execution of
small- and full-scale experiments that will test our theoretical hypotheses. Process
dynamics observed in the lower cost, small-scale system, are used to inform hardware
requirements and process control points for the larger full-scale system.

 7. Project Results and Discussion:

7.1 Demonstration of autonomous fueling of gaseous H2 for materials handling
Development of positioning accuracy of robotic fueling arm
Testing was conducted to establish a baseline measurement of the accuracy and
repeatability of a human operator positioning a lift truck in front of a dispenser. The goal
was to establish the range of motion required for an autonomous fueling mechanism to
mate a hydrogen nozzle with a receptacle on a fuel cell system installed in a forklift. The
assumption is that the human operator is a worse case (less accurate, repeatable) than
an AGV. This assumption will be tested in the next quarter by performing similar testing
using an AGV.
The testing was performed with several operators using all three classes of lift truck
commonly used in distribution centers (Class 1 sit-down, Class 2 reach and Class 3 end-
riders). For each type of truck, the operator was instructed to attempt to achieve an ideal
position for fueling with the truck parallel to the dispenser at a fixed distance and lined up
with the nozzle. A vision system consisting of a webcam and associated software reading
AR (Augmented Reality) tags was used to measure this “home” position relative to the
fixed guardrail surrounding the dispenser. Figure 1 shows the home position of Plug’s
Class 3 end-rider truck relative to the yellow guardrail. The AR tags on the guardrail and
Plug’s GenDrive fuel cell unit were used to measure the truck position relative to the
guardrail.
The operator then approached the dispenser from three different directions, ten times
each, for a total of 30 trials. The operator attempted to drive as quickly as possible to the
“home” position without reversing direction or taking great pains to achieve alignment
(Figure 2). The error in truck position from the home position was then calculated by the
vision system.

Servo motors at the shoulder and elbow joints provide a wide range of motion in the
horizontal plane while a small servo motor at the wrist adjusts rotation of the nozzle about
the vertical axis. A fourth servo motor actuates the cam to lock the nozzle to the
receptacle. The nozzle is not required to angle out of the horizontal plane in the AGV
application as it can be assumed that the truck is on a flat, level surface and the receptacle
is parallel to the ground. In this application, we have the luxury of controlling the design
of the fuel cell system; the automotive application will require more degrees of freedom.
The robotic mechanism will be sited next to a manual hydrogen dispenser (See Figure 5)
so that they can share a common set of plumbing and the manual dispenser can be used
as a backup during the early phases of reliability testing.

This device includes two cameras, a 1920 x 1080 @ 30Hz color video camera and a 1024
x 768 @ 30Hz lidar. The lidar uses a single near-infrared (NIR) laser beam, swept over
the field of view (FOV) using a micro-electromechanical system (MEMS). The lidar’s
output consists of a depth map and a NIR image. Ambient light affects the two cameras
in opposite ways. The color camera requires at least some light to operate, but it can
handle full sunlight. The lidar is its own light source and can therefore operate in complete
darkness, but it gets overpowered by sunlight. Marker detection can be done using either
the color or NIR image, but accurate 3D pose and yaw estimation requires the depth map.
A stereo camera could also be used, since it produces both an image (color or
monochrome) and a depth map. In our experimentation so far, depth accuracy was
somewhat better using lidar than a stereo camera.
Size and placement of the ArUco markers was optimized to improve performance. The
design for this project included a pair of 4.2 cm markers (on a 5.6 cm sticker, since the
marker’s black edge needs a surrounding white border). As shown in Figure 15, the
markers are placed near the left and right edges of the fuel cell, just below the receptacle.
The left marker provides both 3D pose and yaw, while the right marker provides additional
accuracy in yaw. Pose estimation require a precise calibration of the distance from the
left marker’s center to the receptacle’s center (whereas the right marker just needs to be
roughly aligned with the left one). Having two markers also enables a much larger number
of uniquely identifiable fuel cells. The largest ArUco dictionary has 1,000 unique IDs,
which is potentially not enough for one site. Using a pair of markers provides 1,000 x
1,000 = 1 million unique permutations.

 Figure 15: ArUco marker placement

The OpenCV marker detection code provides the image coordinate of the detected
marker’s center. Since we also know the depth value at that image coordinate (because
the depth map and the image are aligned) we can compute the marker center’s 3D

                                        Page 16 of 31

spherical coordinates (rho, theta, phi), which can then be converted to 3D Cartesian
coordinates (X, Y, Z) in the arm’s frame of reference.
Determining the rho component of the spherical coordinates is the most complex part,
requiring de-projecting the depth measurement at the marker center’s pixel coordinate.
This is needed because the lidar’s depth measurement represents distance along a
normal from the image plane to the scene location, whereas rho is the Euclidian distance
from the camera center to the scene location. To account for the camera lens distortion,
the de-projection needs to take the camera’s intrinsic calibration into account. The
conversion from spherical to Cartesian is straightforward, but the resulting X,Y,Z is in the
camera’s reference frame. Transforming to the ARM base reference frame requires
careful calibration of the camera’s extrinsics with respect to the ARM base.
The principle behind determining the yaw of a surface with respect to the camera plane
is based on trigonometry. Knowing two points on the same horizontal line on the fuel cell’s
front surface, the angle between the vector connecting those two points and the camera
plane can be derived directly from the trigonometric definition of the cosine. One point is
the center of the left marker, which was used for estimating the 3D pose. Using the exact
same technique for the right marker provides the second point.
Demonstration (without Hydrogen)
The autonomous dispenser prototype accomplished a huge technical milestone by
successfully completing 199 out of 200 connection attempts, although these connections
were done without hydrogen. For this test, the fuel cell was placed on a pallet moved by
an AGV. The AGV was programmed to move near the robot and stop. The robot then
aligned the nozzle with the receptacle, mated the two and lock the nozzle to the
receptacle. Once completed, the robot would then disconnect the nozzle and return to
its home position. The AGV would then move the fuel cell away from the dispenser and
then return for the next attempt.

Demonstration with hydrogen
Plug continued to debug the robot on a real fuel cell. The first completed items were fine
tuning the robot to target positioning to account for the new target positions on a real fuel
cell (Figure 19). Shoulder motor over current errors limited the robot from getting to the
fully inserted position to achieve a latch on a real system.
Plug optimized the robot, locator tag (bar codes), and nozzle location to get the best
results for the robot. The challenge for this robot is the location of the locator tag to the
nozzle location needs to be very precise because this camera does not “see” the nozzle,
only the locator tag. This approach is a limitation for production systems. With the location
correct within 0.25mm the robot could approach the receptacle reliably.
Even with a precise location, the robot did not have enough force to fully engage the
nozzle onto the receptacle and latch. Now the robot is in a real-world environment with
the H2 hose supported by the robot chassis, air hosing to allow H2 purging of the motors,
and connecting to a real system. The robot approaches the nozzle and starts the insertion,
but robot cannot push the last 3-6 mm to get to the latch position. Many work arounds
were tried including re-mounting the H2 hoses and air hoses, trying to drive the robot
deeper (with the locator to nozzle position deeper in the robot software), and by “helping”
the robot the final distance. Ultimately only helping the robot drive the final distance
worked to allow the robot to latch onto the H2 receptacle.
Plug proceeded to attempt the 200 connection cycles by “helping” the robot.
Unfortunately, the pulling of the robot during insertion lead to a gradual increase in robot
positional errors. The best outcome was 10 successful insertions before the robot could
no longer approach the receptacle properly. The worst was 2 successful insertions. Over
1.5 hours of testing Plug completed 29 successful latches with an average number of
successful latches of 4 before failure. Data from these latches was captured and video
taken as well.

7.2 Liquid Hydrogen Transfer Efficiency
For this part of the project, the following activities were envisioned:

 • Review existing LH2 transfer data to evaluate the range of hydrogen venting
 associated with sending and receiving tank fill levels and pressures. A previously
 developed thermodynamic model will be used to simulate these scenarios for
 model validation before using the model to identify optimum transfer pressures,
 flow rates, and duration that minimize cool down and pressure blow down losses.
 • Use results from objective 1 to size control valves and process logic of the control
 system for a small scale ~10 kg liquid transfer system.
 • Process dynamics observed in the small-scale system during budget period 2 will
 be used to inform hardware requirements and process control points for the larger
 full-scale system with a goal of achieving a minimum 20% reduction in transfer
 losses compared to a transfer using the incumbent transfer protocol.
Initial work focused on finalizing system definition and pivoting from a liquid hydrogen to
liquid nitrogen-based test platform to reduce cost of system components and lead time of
critical components. A preliminary logic flow for the control system was constructed. A
system PID was created to establish a relationship between process gas components,
sensors, and system ID tags, along with a controls and reference schematic (Figure 33).

 8. Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions:

This project successfully showed multiple paths for autonomous fueling of vehicles
including:

 • Designed and demonstrated autonomous fueling of a materials handling truck
 with gaseous hydrogen using a low-cost robotic arm.
 • Designed and carried out preliminary demonstrations of autonomous fueling of a
 commercial fuel cell vehicle through the building of a robotic fixture with real
 hydrogen components including nozzle and hose.
 • Developed the baseline technology for autonomous fueling with liquid hydrogen.

 9. Path Forward:

A baseline understanding of the key elements of robotic refueling have been
demonstrated. For the materials handling application, robotic refueling may become
feasible in autonomous warehouses. Similarly, the robotic fueling of commercial

                                                                                                                                   Page 30 of 31

vehicles may help accelerate the commercial adoption of hydrogen. Lastly, the
demonstration of autonomous liquid hydrogen refueling is still in early phases, however
baseline designs and safety requirements have been established.

Looking at the conceptual operations for autonomous warehouses, a co-bot, or a
semiautonomous arm cooperating with a human, is likely the best path forward for this
application. The autonomous warehouses will need human supervision, and
autonomous refueling with minimal oversight or assistance is likely a practical solution.
The study of autonomous refueling of passenger vehicles in Figure 32 shows a
decrease in fueling time from 77 to 9 seconds through use of a robotic arm, showing a
path to assisted fueling with partial autonomy for increased time savings. Further,
modern robotics is rapidly advancing and future efforts that can leverage advanced
robotics will likely play a role in hydrogen refueling.
