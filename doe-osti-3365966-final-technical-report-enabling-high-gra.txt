1 Major Goals of the Project

The overarching goal of this research program is to demonstrate simultaneously high-gradient ac-
celeration with enhanced efficiency using the Structure Wakefield Accelerator (SWFA) technology.
Beam-driven SWFA accelerators rely on high-charge ”drive” bunches passing through slow-wave
structures (SWSs) to excite electromagnetic wakefields. The produced wakefields can be directly
used to accelerate a trailing ”main” bunch in the same beamline (collinear-wakefield acceleration, or
CWA) or be extracted through a coupler into an optimized accelerating structure that accelerates
the main bunch in a parallel beamline (two-beam acceleration, or TBA).
SWFA utilizing terahertz (THz) and sub-THz structures is highly advantageous compared to con-
ventional RF architectures because a higher frequency leads to a more intense beam-wave interac-
tion, which can dramatically increase the structure gradient. Achieving high-efficiency in SWFA
requires a large transformer ratio (R = max |E+ |/ max |E− |) so that the energy gain experienced
by the witness bunch is much higher than the initial energy of the drive bunch. However, conven-
tional time-symmetric beam distributions are bound by a fundamental limit where the transformer
ratio cannot exceed 2. To bypass this limit, precise high-precision phase-space control is required.
Using a precisely-shaped, time-asymmetric current distribution—such as a doorstep or ramped
profile—can significantly enhance the transformer ratio, allowing maximum energy extraction from
the drive bunch.
To systematically address these physics and engineering challenges, the specific goals of the program
are organized around four main objectives:

 • Objective I: Bright and high-charge beams generation and transport.

 • Objective II: High-precision phase-space control.

 • Objective III: Designing efficient high-gradient slow-wave structures.

 • Objective IV: Maximizing efficiency and conserving brightness during acceleration.

2 Accomplishments Under These Goals

2.1 Objective I: Bright and High-Charge Beams Generation and Transport

The realization of extreme accelerating fields in SWFA requires the meticulous optimization of
the drive beam-structure interaction. Analytical wakefield scaling laws dictate that the amplitude
of the fundamental accelerating field scales favorably with reduced structure apertures (Ez ∝
Q/a2 , where Q is the bunch charge and a is the characteristic aperture radius). However, as
the aperture decreases into the sub-millimeter regime appropriate for sub-THz frequencies, the
beam’s transverse emittance must be strictly minimized to prevent physical interception with the
structure walls and to mitigate the excitation of destructive transverse wakefields (dipole modes)
that induce single-bunch beam breakup instabilities. Consequently, the investigation initiated
an exhaustive computational modeling effort to maximize beam brightness (B⊥ ≃ Q/(2⊥ σz )) at
the AWA photoinjector, balancing the inherent trade-offs between transverse emittance (⊥ ) and
longitudinal bunch length (σz ).

2.2 Objective II: High-Precision Phase-Space Control

Symmetric drive bunches are strictly limited by the fundamental theorem of beam loading, which
caps the transformer ratio at R ≤ 2. To surpass this limit and maximize energy extraction, shaping
the longitudinal current profile into asymmetric distributions is essential, as this allows for a more
uniform decelerating field across the drive bunch. We pursued various approaches to achieve this
high-precision control: a novel laser-based shaping method, an established mask-based emittance
exchange (EEX) technique for slow-wave structure testing at AWA, and a new design for an EEX-
integrated bunch compressor based on the current AWA beamline.

ble of producing electron bunches with linearly-ramped current profiles for application to collinear
wakefield accelerators. The method implements a photocathode-laser pulse shaping technique de-

2.3 Objective III: Design and Demonstration of Efficient High-Field Slow Wave
 Structures

To capitalize on the inherent advantages of the sub-THz regime—namely, the highly favorable
scaling of the shunt impedance (r/L ∝ f 1/2 ) and the corresponding exponential decrease in RF
breakdown probability linked to shorter RF pulse durations—the project undertook the electro-
magnetic design and physical fabrication of multiple high-frequency slow-wave structures. These
structures were subsequently tested at AWA, integrating the beam generation, transport, and shap-
ing techniques developed in Objs. I and II.

2.3.2 Dielectric-Lined Waveguides and Transverse Topology Control

Our research investigated dielectric-lined waveguide (DWFA) structures with various transverse
cross-sections to understand and control wakefield topologies. While cylindrical structures are
common, exploring non-cylindrical geometries, such as square and rectangular cross-sections, pro-
vides a strategy to suppress transverse instabilities by damping dipole modes. Transitioning from
cylindrical symmetry to two-fold symmetry structures allows us to systematically study the evolu-
tion of the transverse field topology.
The beam-wave interaction within these DWFAs was modeled using warp-X, a finite-difference
time-domain particle-in-cell (PIC) program. The simulations evaluated the transverse wakefield
forces behind the drive bunch and revealed that the null points of the transverse force undergo
symmetry-breaking pitchfork bifurcations (see Fig. 8). Specifically, the square-section structure
exhibits a single zero-force null point. In contrast, the rectangular-section structure features three
distinct null points aligned along the horizontal x-direction. Systematically exploring these geome-
tries provides a pathway for tailoring wakefields for specific applications in advanced accelerator
concepts, beam manipulation, and control of radiated fields.

Beyond transverse field topologies, we also investigated the generation and extraction of THz
radiation for potential energy recovery schemes. Nominally, the THz pulse propagates in the same
direction as the electron bunch. A simple oblique cut allows the radiation to diffract at an angle
relative to the bunch direction. Figure 9 visually demonstrates this extraction process, showing
the longitudinal accelerating wakefield (Ez ) decoupling from the structure geometry and radiating
outward due to the angled cut.
The physical DWFA structures are fabricated using bare borosilicate glass tubes, and the outer
surface of the structures is coated with a metallic copper layer. This deposition process utilizes an
RF magnetron sputtering technique at Euclid Techlabs LLC. Following fabrication and cleaning,
the structures were prepared for experimental studies at AWA. They were mounted on a custom in-
sertion assembly—a movable platform designed to hold up to six different structures—which allows
them to be remotely inserted into the electron beam path. To experimentally evaluate the beam-
wave interaction, the electron bunches are transported to a diagnostic station located approximately
10 m downstream of the device under test. This station utilizes a single-shot longitudinal phase
space (LPS) diagnostic system, comprising a 1.3-GHz vertically deflecting cavity and a horizontally
bending dipole spectrometer, to map the precise energy and temporal modifications induced by the
wakefields. The AWA beamline (see Fig. 10) was optimized for various cases.
While the initial experimental run provided valuable practical experience, the measured data
was inconclusive due to operational constraints. Specifically, limited beam time and non-optimal
quadrupole settings prevented the formation of a precise beam waist at the LPS screens, degrading

2.4 Objective IV: Maximizing Efficiency and Conserving Brightness During
 Acceleration

The research program investigated the beam dynamics required to maximize energy extraction and
maintain beam quality in structure wakefield accelerators (SWFAs). Specifically, we explored the
computational onset of beam-break-up (BBU) instabilities during long-distance propagation and
developed the conceptual and mechanical framework for implementing wakefield energy recovery
using dielectric-lined waveguides.
BBU instability study: As part of a collaboration with Dr. Zholents we have attempted to use
warp-X to propagate beams over long distances and investigate the onset of beam-break-up insta-

structures considered in the present research (f ≃ 180 GHz) and uses the macroscopic electromag-
netic solver. In order to increase precision and reduce running time, a moving-window approach is
implemented where the fields are only computed over a limited computational domain surrounding
and moving with the drive bunch. Despite the cylindrical symmetry of the problem, the model
was implemented in a Cartesian domain to properly account for 3D beam-dynamics effect arising
from the interaction with high-order multipole components of the wakefields. Figure 11(lower plot)
shows the evolution of slices’ centroids along the accelerator beamline and confirms that the cen-
troid of slice #5 oscillates with an exponentially-growing amplitude. Similar features are observed
in the evolution of the centroid associated with slice #0 albeit at the sub-micron level. These
results indicate that the BBU instability due to misalignment occurred in all slices, but differed
in growing amplitude. It confirms that the instability mainly affects the tail of the bunch. In the
present case, no initial misalignment were introduced and the observation of the onset of the BBU
instability is the result of small misalignments (coming from numerical noise) at the sub-micron
level. Such imperfections will most likely be present in the drive-beam produced from in realistic
accelerator.

Energy Recovery: While SWFAs can achieve energy gradients well above conventional RF limits,
innovative energy recovery schemes are required to enhance overall efficiency and make compact
SWFAs practical. The project explored the concept of energy recovery, which involves recycling the
Cherenkov radiation emitted by the drive bunch after it exits the accelerating structure. Instead
of allowing this radiation to dissipate, it can be captured and redirected back into the structure
entrance to constructively interfere with the wakefields generated by a subsequent drive bunch. Over
multiple successive bunches, this coherent addition substantially amplifies the wake amplitude,
maximizing the accelerating field available to a final witness bunch (see Fig. 12). To manage
residual wakefields, a loading bunch injected at ∆zL = (n + 0.5)λ (where integer n ≥ 1 and λ is
the fundamental wavelength) destructively interferes with and suppresses the wake.

Figure 12: Illustration of an energy recovery scheme using a structure wakefield accelerator. The
electron drive bunch travels from left to right, and the exiting radiation (gray) is decoupled at an
angle. The radiation is captured by an off-axis parabolic (OAP) mirror and redirected toward the
structure entrance to constructively interfere with the wakefields of subsequent bunches.

The experiments under Obj. III.2 serve as foundational R&D for this energy recovery concept.
To extract the radiation, the DWFA exit face was machined at a 45° angle to form a Vlasov
antenna. WARP-X simulations confirm this asymmetric boundary diffracts the THz pulse outward
at approximately 60°. Capturing this extracted radiation with off-axis parabolic mirrors into a
Michelson interferometer will provide time-domain autocorrelation measurements of wake onset
and suppression. This ultimately validates the core mechanics essential for building future resonant
energy-recovery architectures.
