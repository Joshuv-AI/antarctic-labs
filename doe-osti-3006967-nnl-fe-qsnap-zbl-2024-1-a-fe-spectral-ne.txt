Overview
The NNL.Fe.qSNAP-ZBL.2024.1 machine-learned potential (MLP) has been generated to support the
development of an elemental body-centered cubic (BCC) Fe athermal recombination corrected neutron damage
model (Reference (a)) and simulations of primary recoil atom (PRA) cascades in BCC Fe. This MLP is a quadratic
spectral neighbor analysis potential (qSNAP; Reference (b)) hybridized with the universal Ziegler-Beirsack-
Littmark (ZBL) potential (Reference (c)) at short-range and is named according to Naval Nuclear Laboratory MLP
naming conventions (NNL.material-system.MLP-type.year.version). Training set calculations for Fe are
presented along with the subsequent MLP fitting procedure. A key criterion of the fitting procedure is that ZBL
describes the short-range interaction with minimal impact on the MLP. The MLP is compared to density functional
theory (DFT) predicted properties relevant to radiation damage simulation, including threshold displacement
energies, for validation. The NNL.Fe.qSNAP-ZBL.2024.1 potential is considered suitable for molecular dynamics
(MD) simulations of radiation defects up to 800 K and PRA cascades in BCC Fe up to around 10 keV. The
potential can additionally be used on a limited basis for recoils of 10–20 keV, within which range the emergence
of structures outside the training set in cascade simulations may cause system instabilities.

Training and Fitting
Training and Test Set Calculations

Training and Test sets for NNL.Fe.qSNAP-ZBL.2024.1 were prepared from ab initio MD (AIMD) simulations
using the MedeA atomistic simulation environment (References (b) – (e)) for a variety of structures relevant to
radiation damage and metallurgy in Fe, listed in Table 1. The structures can be grouped into pristine, stacking
faults, disordered, defects, midpoint states, and strained. Pristine BCC and face-centered tetragonal (FCT) lattice
were ferro and one-dimensional anti-ferromagnetic, respectively. The hexagonal close-packed (HCP) structure is
non-magnetic. Pristine structures were initially subject to relaxation based on magnetic symmetry of the system,
whereby FCT is anisotropic with c/a near 1.07. No other structures were permitted to relax prior to initiation of
AIMD. Strained structures are unit cells of BCC and were not subject to AIMD. Stacking faults, defects, midpoint
states and strained structures were only computed for the BCC phase. BCC stacking faults are intended to support
dislocation modeling with the MLP and were constructed according to References (f) and (g). BCC defect
structures were prepared by simple atom removal for vacancies or by atom insertion with atom position adjustment
for equal atomic spacing about the defect center for interstitials. Midpoint states were added to mitigate
extrapolation in diffusion processes during MD simulations using the MLP. These midpoint states were
constructed such that a single atom was placed at the mid-point of a hop between defect lattice sites. The
disordered state is intended to capture the thermal spike of a collision cascade within the training set and was
constructed by quasi-randomly distributing the atoms within the simulation box while ensuring that atom spacing
generally are greater than 2 Å. Representative initial structures from each group are provided in the Appendix.

Training set calculations involved a two-stage process. First, low-accuracy AIMD simulations were performed
with Vienna Ab Initio Simulation Package (VASP) v6 (Reference (h) and (i)) for each structure. Subsequently,
high-accuracy single-point VASP calculations were run on a subset of AIMD frames for each structure. Low-
accuracy calculations used a 350 eV planewave cutoff, a maximum k-spacing of 0.5 Å-1, and a self-consistent
field convergence of 10-5 eV. High-accuracy calculations used a 450 eV planewave cutoff, a maximum k-spacing
of 0.2 Å-1, and a self-consistent field convergence of 10-6 eV; these parameters were found to be sufficient to
converge energy to within approximately 0.001 eV/atom. Both high and low-accuracy calculations used the
standard Fe plane-augmented wave (PAW) potential ([Ar]4s13d7) from the VASP PAW dataset v.54 (Reference
(j)), GGA-PBE (Reference (k)) treatment of the exchange correlation functional, Gaussian smearing about the
Fermi energy with a smearing width of 0.05 eV, an accurate grid for fast-Fourier transforms, and real space
integration. AIMD simulation parameters and the frequency with which frames were selected for high-accuracy
training set calculations are listed Table 2. The number of high-accuracy training structures evaluated for each
initial structure is listed in Table 1. Infrequently, a high-accuracy calculation failed, and the corresponding frame
was omitted from the training set. High-accuracy calculations of disordered structures did not always converge
within 10-6 eV in 200 self-consistent steps; however, each supercell calculation did generally converge to much
less than 10-3 eV, which is considered sufficient for MLP training.

For each structure group, 10% of the structures were withheld for testing. To mitigate interference with ZBL
fitting, all structures where the closest interatomic spacing was less than approximately 1.5 Å were pruned, which
in this case was a single test structure from the <111> self-interstitial atom (SIA) artificial diffusion midpoint
position. The NNL.Fe.qSNAP-ZBL.2024.1 training and test sets consist of 1461 and 162 structures, respectively.

Fe qSNAP were generated for a matrix of ZBL parameters: inner radii of 0.5 Å and 1 Å, and outer radii of 1.3 Å
and 2.0 Å. The default ZBL parameters in the MedeA MLPG are 0.5 Å and 2 Å. Prior to fitting the qSNAP, the

MLPG subtracts the analytic ZBL contribution from the total energy of each training structure using the provided
parameters. Additionally, the MLPG shifts the total energy of each training and test structure by the DFT energy
of an isolated Fe atom (3.352475 eV) for each Fe atom in the supercell. In the MedeA 3.7 MLPG, the test set must
be appended to the end of the training set. The fraction of test set structures is approximately 0.1. For each ZBL
parameter set the qSNAP radial cutoff was optimized to minimize the error between DFT and qSNAP to within 2
decimal places, using an initial guess of 4.8 Å. The maximum integer angular eigenvalue of the qSNAP
hyperspherical harmonic descriptor was set to 2𝐽𝑚𝑎𝑥 = 8.

Verification and Validation
Verification of the statistical and functional performance of the Fe qSNAP for each ZBL parameter set is presented
in subsections Statistics and ZBL parameters, respectively. From these ZBL parameter sets, 𝑟1 = 1 Å and 𝑟𝑐 =
1.3 Å were selected for NNL.Fe.qSNAP-ZBL.2024.1 because the inner cutoff is consistent with convention and
the outer cutoff ensures that ZBL is independent of training set structures. Properties for validation of
NNL.Fe.qSNAP-ZBL.2024.1 were selected to support application of the potential to radiation damage and include
the following categories: Stability, Lattice Parameter, and Thermal Expansion; Mechanical Properties; Phonons;
Defect Formation Energies; Stacking Fault Energies; Diffusion Energetics; and Radiation Damage.
NNL.Fe.qSNAP-ZBL.2024.1 is validated with DFT computed properties in these categories, and in select cases
validated for physical accuracy with measured values. Throughout the verification and validation subsections
NNL.Fe.qSNAP-ZBL.2024.1 is given the short-hand notation Fe.2024.1. Unless otherwise stated, DFT reference
calculations use the same parameters as the training set calculations. All calculations with NNL.Fe.qSNAP-
ZBL.2024.1 use LAMMPS as the engine whereas all DFT calculations use VASP6 as the engine and are
completed within the MedeA v3.7 environment.

Statistics

Metrics for statistical performance of MLPs are default outputs of the MLPG. The root mean-square error (RSME)
between DFT and qSNAP energies and forces is tabulated in Table 3 for each ZBL parameter set. The energy
error was computed as the difference in the qSNAP and DFT supercell energy, whereas the force error was
computed as the difference between qSNAP and DFT for each force component of each atom in the training or
test set supercells. The energy and force RSME are small for both training and test sets, regardless of ZBL
parameters. For all ZBL parameter sets the ratio of energy and force RSME is reasonably close to 1:1, indicating
that the qSNAP are not overfit. No training or test set structures contain interatomic distances less than around 1.5
Å; therefore, the slight decrease in RSME with decreasing cutoff outer radius occurs because the ZBL alters
training set data prior to fitting for 𝑟𝑐 = 2.0 Å but not for 𝑟𝑐 = 1.3 Å. Accordingly, when 𝑟𝑐 = 1.3 Å the qSNAP
part of the potential does not vary with 𝑟1 , only the ZBL varies.

NNL.Fe.qSNAP-ZBL.2024.1 (𝑟1 = 1.0 Å and 𝑟𝑐 = 1.3 Å) distributions of energy error and force errors are
illustrated in Figure 1. Distributions of both test and training set data are well distributed about zero error with
few outliers. The greater degree of statistical fluctuations in the test set energy error distribution is attributed to
the small sample size (162) compared to the larger training set (1461).

ZBL parameters

In addition to statistical metrics, the performance of Fe qSNAP with varying ZBL parameters was assessed via a
quasistatic drag method presented in Reference (q). The energy profile of an atom dragged toward its nearest
neighbor is illustrated in Figure 2 for each ZBL parameter set. Energy profiles computed for qSNAP with ZBL
(qSNAP-ZBL) were decomposed into different contributions to the overall potential.

At short interatomic distances, the LAMMPS ZBL function (Eq. (2)) is harder than DFT. Consequently, qSNAP-
ZBL tends to be harder than DFT, except for unphysical attractive behaviors at short distances found in ZBL
parameter sets with 𝑟𝑐 = 2.0 Å.

 9

The qSNAP part at interatomic distances greater than 𝑟𝑐 is verified to be consistent with DFT. At interatomic
distances less than 𝑟𝑐 , the qSNAP part for 𝑟𝑐 = 1.3 Å extrapolates well to DFT whereas qSNAP for 𝑟𝑐 = 2.0 Å
becomes highly attractive. Because of this unphysical behavior, qSNAP-ZBL with 𝑟𝑐 = 2.0 Å are considered
unsuitable for PRA simulations. In contrast both qSNAP-ZBL with 𝑟𝑐 = 1.3 Å are considered suitable for cascade
simulations. Although 𝑟1 = 1.0 Å, 𝑟𝑐 = 1.3 Å was selected for NNL.Fe.qSNAP-ZBL.2024.1, 𝑟1 = 0.5 Å, 𝑟𝑐 =
1.3 Å can also be considered for cascade simulations with no impact to the validation results below, except for
possible minor changes to the direction dependent threshold displacement energy.

Stability, Lattice Parameter, and Thermal Expansion

The NNL.Fe.qSNAP-ZBL.2024.1 potential was trained on three crystalline phases, ferromagnetic BCC,
antiferromagnetic FCT, and non-magnetic HCP. BCC is the only phase of interest and the other two are included
as reference structures in the MLP to mitigate undesired phase transitions or extrapolation. Table 4 compares the
Fe.2024.1 predicted ground-state lattice parameters to DFT computations using the same VASP parameters as for
training; additionally, the difference in ground-state energy between phases is compared. Fe.2024.1 and DFT
lattice parameters generally differ by less the 0.3% for all phases. The Fe.2024.1 and DFT predicted BCC ground
state lattice parameters are in reasonable agreement with the experimental value at 293 K. The difference in ground
state energy between phases differs by less than 0.08% between Fe.2024.1 and DFT. The most to least stable
phase is BCC, HCP, FCT.

Thermal expansion of BCC iron predicted with Fe.2024.1 is validated to be in excellent agreement with
experiment in Figure 3. The illustrated lattice expansion is the relative change in lattice parameter from 293 K.
The deviation between Fe.2024.1 and experiment below 200 K occurs because classical MD neglects zero-point
atomic motion (Reference (t)). Fe.2024.1 lattice parameters were extracted from LAMMPS MD computation of
a 20x20x20 BCC Fe supercell (16,000 atom) at temperatures of 10–800 K. Initially the system was equilibrated
under an nVT thermostat for 40 ps with 0.001 ps then equilibrated for a further 40 ps under an anisotropic nPT
thermostat. Subsequently, the lattice parameter was averaged over 40 ps under nPT. The anisotropic nPT
thermostat permits a possible phase change. No phase change was observed for the studied temperatures, thereby
verifying that BCC Fe is stable in this range.

Phonons

Phonons of BCC Fe were computed within MedeA using the PHONON lattice dynamics code (Reference (x)),
which uses a direct displacement method to obtain input forces. Fe.2024.1 and DFT forces were generated for a
3x3x3 BCC Fe supercell; the corresponding lattice parameters from Table 4 are used for the calculations. Phonon
densities of states were generated with 106 Monte Carlo wavevector samples in the Brillouin zone, tallied in 0.2418
THz (0.001 eV) bins.

Fe.2024.1 and DFT dispersion relations and phonon densities of states, shown in Figure 4 and Figure 5, are in
good agreement. Computed dispersion curves are in overall good agreement with experiment at room temperature;
deviations between computation and experiment are due to the use of harmonic approximation in computation,
which omits anharmonic effects that soften phonon modes at room temperature. Computed phonon densities of
states are in good agreement with measurement at 30 K, and peaks in the densities of states have similar
frequencies (energies).

Diffusion Energetics

Diffusion has been found to play an important role in defect recombination during quenching of the collision
cascade thermal spike (Reference (bb)). Transition state energy profiles were evaluated for a variety of migration
pathways. Vacancy transition state energy profiles are illustrated in Figure 15 and Figure 16. SIA transition state
energy profiles are illustrated in Figure 17–Figure 22 and are primarily related to the stable <110> SIA dumbbell.
The <110> dumbbell diffusion pathways were selected from Reference (cc). DFT results were computed with the
Transition State Search module in MedeA whereas Fe.2024.1 results were computed with standalone LAMMPS
nudged elastic band (NEB) computations. Both Fe.2024.1 and DFT calculations used 5–7 images and a spring
constant of 5 eV/Å2 between reaction coordinates; forces were converged to within 0.1 eV/Å with a maximum of
50 steps. Each pathway consists of seven or more reaction coordinates that were initially linearly interpolated
between the initial and final state. The initial and final defect structures use the lattice parameters in Table 4 but
atom positions were relaxed to a force tolerance of 0.001 eV/Å and supercell energy tolerance of 1e-6 eV.

Overall transition state energy profiles computed with Fe.2024.1 are in overall good agreement with DFT.
Fe.2024.1 activation energies of vacancy migrations pathways are within 0.1 eV of DFT. A larger relative error
occurs for lower energy <111> nearest neighbor transition (13%) when compared to the <100> transition (4%).
The effect of this error in the minimum energy <111> vacancy migration on diffusion kinetics is likely less than
the observed 13% because Fe.2024.1 and DFT agree within 0.01 eV for structures associated with the <111>
vacancy midpoint included in the training set (see Appendix). Fe.2024.1 activation energies of <110> dumbbells
are within 0.025 eV of DFT. The largest relative error, 5%, occurs for the lowest energy <110> dumbbell migration
pathway, whereby the dumbbell translates in the <111> direction with a 𝜋/3 rotation. The transition state profile
of the <110> dumbbell rotation to a <111> crowdion illustrates the instability of the <111> crowdion state to
perturbation for both Fe.2024.1 and DFT; though in the latter case, the state is weakly metastable with an
activation barrier of less than 0.0005 eV. The bias in activation energy between the <110> dumbbell and <111>
crowdion is equal to the biases in Table 7. The largest overall relative error in transition state is observed for the
<111> crowdion translation along the <111> direction; however, this barrier is expected to be unimportant given
the instability of the <111> crowdion. Increasing the number of NEB images revealed that the <100> SIA
dumbbell translation and onsite rotation do not follow a saddle-point but may be unstable to the <111> translation.

LAMMPS MD simulations with Fe.2024.1 were conducted for 400–900 K to examine migration mechanisms and

supercell sized containing a single defect with the equilibrium lattice constant for the respective temperature.
Initially the supercell was equilibrated for 20 ps under an nVT ensemble. Defect trajectories were then tracked
under an nVE ensemble for ≥ 5 ns. Defective atom positions were identified using a Wigner-Seitz analysis
technique.

In the Wigner-Seitz technique, the defect position was mapped to its referential lattice site using Voronoi
tessellations of the pristine lattice; the SIA orientation was further mapped to the nearest <110> direction using a
minimum angle criterion. The time series data was then filtered to the states where a defect state change occurred

<111> translation for temperatures of 400–900 K.

Radiation Damage

The principal use of NNL.Fe.qSNAP-ZBL.2024.1 is radiation damage simulations. The damage from a PRA
cascade is dependent on the force-field at short interatomic distances that are less than those of equilibrium
properties. Quasistatic drag energy profiles to short interatomic distances and directionally dependent threshold
displacement energies were selected as simple properties to qualify the use of Fe.2024.1 for PRA cascade
simulations.

Quasistatic drag energy profiles were computed using the method described in Reference (q). Profiles for high
symmetry drag directions for Fe.2024.1 are shown in Figure 24. For interatomic separation distances greater than
𝑟𝑐 (1.3 Å) Fe.2024.1 is generally within 3% of DFT – the maximum error is typically at 𝑟𝑐 . Below 𝑟𝑐 Fe.2024.1
is significantly harder than DFT and the deviation between these two is primary (but not entirely) attributable to
the ZBL contribution.

Threshold displacement energies were computed along high symmetry directions from MD simulations of PRAs
using standalone LAMMPS compiled with the KOKKOS package option and accelerated with GPUs. Initially a
20x20x20 Fe supercell (16000 atoms) was equilibrated under an nVT thermostat at 30 K for 20 ps; the box size
was set with the lattice Fe.2024.1 parameter from Table 4 and thermal expansion from Figure 3. Subsequently,
the periodic simulation box was partitioned into a bath region 2 unit cells thick along the outer shell and an inner
region to host the PRA cascade. The bath used a Berendsen rescaling thermostat with a period of 0.07 ps while a
nVE thermostat was used in the inner region. Prior to initializing a cascade these regions were equilibrated for an
additional 10 ps and the system state was saved to a restart file; cascade simulations were initialized from this
restart file. Simulations of PRA were performed for recoil energies from 10 eV increasing recursively at 1 eV
intervals until a stable Frenkel point defect pair (i.e., vacancy and <110> dumbbell) remained after approximately
3 ps. Electronic stopping was applied to atoms above 10 eV (as recommend in Reference (gg)) using the Stopping
Rang in Matter (SRIM) calculations (Reference (c)) of Reference (hh). A Wigner Seitz analysis technique based
on Voronoi tessellations, described in Reference (ii), was used to determine the presence of point defects. The
recoil atom was randomly sampled from a small sphere at the center of the simulation box containing around 100
atoms. 10 samples were used for each examined recoil energy and the direction was randomly selected from the
symmetry equivalent family of directions (i.e., <hlk>). The mean threshold displacement energy for a given
direction and standard deviation was computed from these 10 samples.

The threshold displacement energy, 𝐸𝑑 , predicted with Fe.2024.1 at 30 K for the <100>, <110>, and <111>
directions is tabulated in Table 10. Threshold displacement energies for directions outlining the symmetry unique
directions in BCC Fe are illustrated in Figure 25. These reported values are actual atom recoil energy and are not
adjusted for electronic stopping losses as done for use in damage models such as NRT (Reference (jj)) and
athermal recombination corrected (Reference (a)). 𝐸𝑑 are compared to DFT ab initio molecular dynamics
simulations of PRAs at 30 K from Reference (q), which use the same Fe PAW but a reduced planewave cutoff of
 24

250 eV. Fe.2024.1 𝐸𝑑 in the <100>, <110>, and <111> directions are within 2σ of these DFT results. Fe.2024.1
results for <100> and <110> are within 2σ of electron irradiation experiments at 30 K (Reference (kk)) but differ
for the <111>. Along the high symmetry trace in Figure 25, Fe.2024.1 𝐸𝑑 trend well with the DFT from Reference
(q). Quantitative agreement is generally strong near the minimum 𝐸𝑑 ; however, Fe.2024.1 underpredicts DFT for
𝐸𝑑 > 25 eV. Several factors may suppress defect generation in the DFT calculations causing a greater 𝐸𝑑 . One
factor suspected to be important is that the 504 atom supercells used in DFT are too small to prevent self-
interaction from the cascade pressure wave.

For each examined direction in Figure 25, interatomic distances were verified to remain greater than 𝑟𝑐 . As such,
𝐸𝑑 from Fe.2024.1 are in principle determined only from the qSNAP component of the potential and are not
influenced by ZBL; though, ZBL will influence cascade evolution from recoil energies above 100 eV. PRA
simulations up to 60 keV (not reported here) have been performed. Recoil energies up to nearly 10 keV have been
generally verified to complete successfully. Between 10 keV and 20 keV, structures outside the training set can
emerge during cascade quenching causing simulation failure through lost atoms. The failure rate at 10 keV and
20 keV is around 4% and 20%, respectively. Simulations of recoil atoms above 20 keV fail at higher rates (100%
for 60 keV recoils). Preliminary simulation of PRAs with energy greater than 1 keV suggest changing 𝑟1 = 0.5 Å
in the ZBL component of the qSNAP will affect the initial evolution of the collision cascade but that the number
of defects created through quenching of the thermal spike will be similar, consistent with the finding that diffusion
strongly influences athermal recombination (Reference (bb)).
