Building bridges to care: A co-created community palliative care package for resource-limited settings
Authors: Ngozi Idemili-Aronu, Ikechukwu Alex Okoli, John Oluwaseyi Jemisenia, John Olajide Olawepo, Tonia Chinyelu Onyeka, Jean Hunleth, Karla T Washington, Gregory A Aarons, Echezona Edozie Ezeanolue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPalliative care (PC) needs are substantial in Nigeria, yet access to specialist PC services remains limited, particularly in rural and peri-urban communities.ObjectivesTo co-create an implementation-ready, community-delivered PC package for rural Nigerian settings without PC specialists and specify its components, delivery roles, and referral pathways.DesignSequential explanatory mixed-methods, participatory co-creation design in which quantitative prioritization informed qualitative refinement.MethodsParticipants (health professionals, traditional and religious leaders, patients, caregivers, advocates, and policymakers) used Mentimeter® to generate and rank feasible non-pharmacologic components, followed by an adapted Group Model Building (GMB) process to identify key health-system and sociocultural determinants, specify the most appropriate delivery settings and cadres, and define delivery processes and referral pathways to basic PC providers at facilities. The process included three pre-workshop virtual sessions (n=51) and a 5-day in-person GMB workshop in Enugu, Nigeria (n=45), with diverse participants from all six geopolitical regions recruited via the ICON-3 Practice-Based Research Network. Outputs were refined through participant validation and technical expert review to enhance feasibility and implementation readiness.ResultsParticipants prioritized effective pain and symptom relief, strengthened communication and shared decision-making, intentional engagement of religious leaders, and improved community–primary care collaboration. The resulting package comprises three components (pain and symptom management, psychosocial support, spiritual care) delivered via a hub-and-spoke model in which CHEWs/CHAs serve as community “Anchors,” while facility-based basic PC providers and tertiary specialists provide oversight and manage complex cases. Key barriers included limited training and stigma, medication access constraints, and weak referral systems; proposed solutions emphasized training with supportive supervision, improved medicines access/financing and strengthened referral pathways and community support structures.ConclusionA participant-designed, task-shared, hub-linked community PC package is feasible in principle for Nigerian settings without PC specialists and provides an implementation-ready blueprint for feasibility testing, adaptation, and scale-up planning.Keywords: palliative care, community health, task sharing, group model building, implementation, Nigeria, serious health-related suffering

Introduction

Palliative care (PC) improves quality of life for patients and families facing life-threatening illness by preventing and relieving suffering through early identification, assessment, and treatment of pain and other physical, psychosocial, and spiritual problems. 1 PC is a global ethical responsibility and a key component of integrated, people-centered health services. 2

Globally, 56.8 million people require PC, including about 25.7 million individuals nearing the end of life but only 14% of these people obtain these services and the majority are from high-income countries. 1 The heaviest burden of serious health-related suffering is concentrated in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where access to PC and pain relief remains constrained by workforce shortages, limited medicines availability (especially opioids), weak service integration, and financing barriers. 3 Notably, over 80% of those requiring PC reside in LMICs.3,4 An estimated 9.67 million people need PC services in Africa. 5

In Nigeria, the exact number of people in need of PC services is unknown, but likely substantial. About 25 million people live with chronic diseases, including HIV and other non-communicable diseases (NCDs) like cancers, cardiovascular and chronic kidney diseases. 6

Nigeria faces a dual burden of communicable diseases and NCDs, with many patients experiencing prolonged illness trajectories accompanied by pain, distress, caregiver strain, and spiritual/existential suffering.1,7,8 In many communities, particularly rural and peri-urban settings, access to PC specialists and structured PC services is limited,9,10 making community-anchored, non-specialist PC models a practical pathway to expand coverage. While the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) Harmonized Guidelines for Sub-Saharan Africa provide resource-adapted recommendations across the cancer care continuum (including PC), they remain largely oriented to facility-based service delivery and multidisciplinary oncology pathways, and they do not provide a sufficiently practical implementation blueprint for community-delivered PC in hard-to-reach settings.11,12

Community-based PC models can reduce avoidable suffering by: (i) bringing basic symptom relief closer to households, (ii) strengthening communication and shared decision-making, (iii) organizing psychosocial and caregiver support, and (iv) integrating culturally congruent spiritual care. 13 Such models often rely on task sharing, delegating defined PC activities to trained non-specialist providers with supervision, and referral linkages to higher-level clinicians.

Our study was designed to address a pragmatic gap: what are the essential, context-appropriate components of a PC service package that can be delivered in Nigerian communities without PC experts, and how should it be organized for feasible implementation? In a previous publication, we presented key barriers and implementation strategies identified by participants. In this manuscript, we describe the co-created package components and present an implementation blueprint suitable for feasibility testing and adaptation.

Methods

Study design

Following literature review of the broad compartments of PC services,8,14–17 this study used a sequential explanatory mixed-methods design 18 guided by an adaptive generative design19,20 to generate and categorize services, in line with existing literature, into three broad compartments (i) pain and symptom management, (ii) psychosocial support, (iii) spiritual support.

A mixed-methods approach was appropriate because the study required both quantitative prioritization of feasible package components and qualitative participatory refinement to specify how the selected components could be delivered, by whom, where, and under what referral conditions in resource-limited community settings.

We used Mentimeter®, an anonymous interactive polling software, to rank candidate package components generated by the participants, an approach commonly used in consensus and priority-setting workshops to enable anonymous voting and reduce dominance effects.21–26 We then conducted qualitative co-creation using Group Model Building (GMB), a participatory approach broadly applied to stimulate practitioners to think systematically.27,28 Events of the virtual and in-person sessions are detailed in a previous publication. 29

Following component generation and prioritization, we used a structured GMB process to translate participant inputs into an implementation-ready, multicomponent PC package. For each PC domain; pain and symptom management, psychosocial support, and spiritual support, participants worked collaboratively to specify: (1) the care activities that could be delivered safely in rural community settings (what); (2) culturally appropriate delivery strategies (how); (3) the cadre responsible for delivery (who); (4) the most appropriate delivery setting (where); and (5) explicit clinical and contextual thresholds for referral to facility-based basic PC services (threshold). In parallel, participants identified systemic and sociocultural determinants likely to influence delivery, uptake, and sustainability of each component. We iteratively refined outputs through plenary discussions, participant validation, and technical expert review to produce a coherent, contextually grounded, and operationally feasible care package.

Setting and sample

We conducted three pre-workshop meetings via Zoom followed by a 5-day in-person GMB workshop in Enugu, Nigeria. Participants included healthcare workers and community actors involved in different levels and aspects of care, including clinicians, nurses, allied professionals, policymakers, clergy, community health extension workers (CHEWs), patient advocates, patients, and caregivers. In Nigeria, Community Health Extension Workers (CHEWs) and Community Health Assistants (CHAs) are frontline primary health care cadres trained to provide basic preventive, promotive, and selected curative services within primary health care facilities and surrounding communities. CHEWs generally receive more extensive formal training than CHAs and often undertake broader Primary Health Centre (PHC) responsibilities, including community mobilization, health education, basic assessment, follow-up, referral, and support for maternal, child health, communicable disease, and non-communicable disease services, according to national task-sharing arrangements 30 and facility-level supervision.

We recruited participants through the ICON-3 Practice-Based Research Network which has 12 sites spanning Nigeria’s six geopolitical regions. 31 Participants were required to have at least a secondary level of education, an email address, an Android device, a WhatsApp account, and access to the Zoom web-conferencing application. Participants were excluded if they did not speak English. Eligible participants were approached by research team members, oral consent was obtained via Zoom for the virtual pre-workshop sessions and in written form for the in-person GMB workshop.

Eligibility criteria

Participants were eligible if they were stakeholders involved in, affected by, or positioned to influence palliative care delivery in Nigeria, including healthcare workers, community health workers, policymakers, clergy, traditional leaders, patient advocates, patients, and caregivers. Eligible participants were required to have at least secondary-level education, an email address, an Android device, a WhatsApp account, access to Zoom, and the ability to communicate in English. Participants were excluded if they were unable to communicate in English, lacked access to the required communication platforms for the virtual sessions, declined consent, or were unavailable to participate in the scheduled workshop activities.

Recruitment

Eligible participants were contacted by phone to confirm their interest and availability for the workshop. Interested participants were sent invitation letters containing information about three (3) virtual meetings and the in-person workshop by email or WhatsApp and were required to accept or reject the invitation within seven days. Participants who accepted the invitation were added to a WhatsApp group and those who did not respond within the seven-day response period were called or sent WhatsApp messages. Those who declined during the second contact were removed from the recruitment list, and other eligible participants were contacted. Participants were provided stipends at five thousand Naira each to procure phone credits and internet data, and those who resided outside Enugu State were provided funds for accommodation and transportation. A total of 71 eligible participants were invited to the pre-workshop virtual meetings, of whom 51 (72%) attended. All 51 attendees were subsequently invited to the 5-day in-person GMB workshop, and 45 participated. In anticipation of attrition, we invited multiple participants within the same categories to preserve representation across groups if some invitees were unable to attend. Attrition between invitation, virtual meetings, and in-person participation was primarily due to practical, non-systematic constraints, including competing clinical or administrative duties, scheduling conflicts, travel and logistical limitations, and intermittent connectivity challenges affecting planning and confirmation.

To assess whether attrition altered participant representation, we compared characteristics of those invited to the in-person workshop and those who ultimately participated (participant category, sex, and geopolitical zone). There were no statistically significant differences between invited and attending participants across these characteristics (sex p = 0.874; geopolitical zone p = 0.982; participant category p = 1.000), suggesting that attrition was unlikely to have systematically excluded specific participant voices (Table 1).

Refinement process

Following the workshop, a structured refinement process was undertaken to ensure the accuracy, feasibility, and contextual relevance of the outputs. Preliminary findings, including the causal loop diagrams, prioritized barriers, and proposed strategies were synthesized into a summary report and circulated among all workshop participants for validation. Participants were invited to review the materials and provide written or verbal feedback during follow-up virtual sessions; 11 participants provided feedback (verbal and/or written). In parallel, the research team conducted technical review of the system maps and solution strategies to assess coherence, evidence alignment, and implementation potential. Revisions were incorporated based on both participant and expert input, resulting in a set of co-developed, participant-endorsed, and technically robust recommendations for improving PC delivery in Nigeria.

Results

The co-created community PC package (titled as “Building Bridges to Care”) was informed by priorities that participants repeatedly emphasized as essential for acceptability and feasibility in Nigerian communities without PC experts. Participants underscored that a responsive package should (i) foreground effective pain treatment and symptom relief, (ii) strengthen communication facilitation and shared decision-making among patients, families, and providers, (iii) intentionally motivate and engage religious leaders, and (iv) reinforce collaboration between communities and primary care providers.

Based on these priorities, we structured the package as two interlinked layers. The first layer defines the core service components, “what is delivered?” comprising: pain and symptom management, psychosocial support, and spiritual care. The second layer specifies the delivery platform and enabling systems: “how care is delivered?” safely and consistently. This includes a task-sharing workforce model anchored by Community Health Extension Workers (CHEWs) and other community-level providers, structured training with supportive supervision, reliable medicines access and clear referral pathways, pragmatic documentation with confidentiality safeguards and simple quality monitoring, and community engagement and governance mechanisms.

To operationalize delivery, participants proposed a “hub-and-spoke” model. At the spoke level, trained community providers (CHEWs/CHAs and, where appropriate, selected community support persons) deliver basic PC functions near households, including identifying patients in need, conducting basic assessments, providing non-pharmacologic support, coaching caregivers, supporting medication adherence, and facilitating navigation. At the hub level, a designated primary care clinician or focal person provides clinical oversight, reviews complex cases, supports access to medicines within scope, and coordinates referrals. The model incorporates explicit linkages to secondary and tertiary services, with clear escalation pathways for uncontrolled symptoms, suspected complications, or psychosocial/spiritual crises requiring specialized input. Collectively, this structure reflects participant emphasis on strengthening community–primary care collaboration and reducing delays to follow-up through clearer referral processes.

Below, we describe each core service component of the package and then summarize the CHEW’s role, participant-identified barriers, and participant-proposed implementation strategies.

Participants co-created a multicomponent, community-delivered PC package organized across three interrelated domains: pain and symptom management, psychosocial support, and spiritual support (Figure 1). For each domain, participants identified non-pharmacologic PC activities that were feasible in rural, resource-limited settings and suitable for delivery by trained community lay workers. These included symptom relief strategies, emotional and social support practices, and culturally grounded spiritual care activities that aligned with community norms and patient preferences.

Co-created community-delivered palliative care package domains and delivery logic (what, how, who, where, and referral thresholds).

Across domains, participants clearly delineated roles and delivery pathways by specifying who should deliver each component, where care should be provided (home, community, or outpatient settings), and how care should be delivered with appropriate supervision. Importantly, participants defined explicit thresholds for referral, outlining clinical, psychosocial, and contextual indicators that necessitate escalation from community-based care to basic PC services at secondary-level facilities. This helped establish clear boundaries between community-delivered support and facility-based care, reducing ambiguity in care responsibilities.

In addition, participants identified key systemic and sociocultural determinants that could influence implementation, including stigma related to HIV and cancer, caregiver burden, limited health system capacity, cultural expectations around illness and spiritual care, and trust in lay providers. These determinants informed refinements to the package design and delivery strategies. Together, this co-creation process yielded an integrated, culturally grounded, and implementation-ready PC package with clearly defined components, delivery roles, and referral mechanisms tailored to the realities of underserved communities.

Pain and symptom management strategies

Participants prioritized rapid, reliable relief of pain and other distressing symptoms as the minimum viable function of community PC, with clear escalation pathways when symptoms are severe or refractory. The package delivers pain and symptom management through a task-shared model in which community-based providers (primarily Community Health Extension Workers [CHEWs] and Community Health Assistants [CHAs] serving as community “Anchors”) provide frontline support, while basic palliative care providers at secondary-level facilities and specialist teams at tertiary facilities provide clinical oversight and manage complex cases. At first contact, CHEWs/CHAs conduct a preliminary pain assessment to clarify the pain experience, suspected etiology or prior diagnoses, and patient goals for comfort and function, and they reassess at each encounter while activating referral triggers when symptoms are uncontrolled or complicated. Basic PC providers complete a comprehensive assessment, titrate therapy to achieve patient-specific goals, and coordinate referral for complex pain or complications.

For continuous pain, the package uses standardized opioid principles for facility-linked PC providers: regularly scheduled dosing with additional breakthrough doses, addition of long-acting formulations after stabilization on short-acting opioids, and dose escalation when breakthrough dosing is consistently required. Breakthrough dosing is specified as 10–20% of the total daily long-acting opioid dose, and opioid rotation is considered when adequate control cannot be achieved because adverse effects limit escalation. Safe delivery is reinforced through routine monitoring for adherence and higher-risk aberrant use behaviors, proactive management of opioid adverse effects (particularly constipation), and structured patient/caregiver education on safe use, storage, and disposal of opioids. Follow-up is embedded as a core workflow: teams establish a scheduled review plan, support continuity during transitions of care, and provide a written follow-up pain plan with clinician contact details.

For pain crises, basic PC providers rapidly titrate and reassess (e.g., reassessing every 60 minutes for oral opioids and every 15 minutes for IV/subcutaneous opioids), increasing doses when pain remains inadequately controlled and referring to tertiary facilities when comfort goals cannot be achieved safely. Beyond pain, the package includes practical symptom pathways (e.g., constipation prevention and treatment) that can be initiated and monitored in the community with clear escalation triggers for suspected impaction, obstruction, or refractory symptoms. Across all symptom-control encounters, teams are instructed to address psychosocial and spiritual concerns that amplify symptom burden to maintain whole-person care.

Psychosocial and emotional support framework

The psychosocial component was designed as a layered support system that strengthens coping, reduces isolation, and improves continuity for both patients and caregivers. At community level, CHEWs/CHAs provide consistent emotional support through regular visits, companionship, and reassurance; organize and facilitate peer support groups; and deliver family education on basic caregiving, including recognizing when to seek medical help. They also provide caregiver respite support, assist with day-to-day logistics such as transportation for appointments and medication refills, and help families access tele-health platforms when travel is difficult. Bereavement support is integrated into routine continuity of care through follow-up with families after a death and linkage to grief counseling where available.

Where feasible, basic psychosocial support staff extend this foundation with more structured interventions, including coping skills training (stress management and relaxation), facilitation of communication and shared decision-making as intermediaries between patients, families, and providers, and more in-depth caregiver training under clinician direction for complex home needs. They may also coordinate high-need care plans (including after-hours support arrangements), provide tele-counseling, liaise with pharmacies to support medication access (including home delivery coordination where feasible), and offer advanced financial counseling and support with complex assistance applications.

Spiritual care model

Spiritual care was formalized as a core package component, delivered through structured collaboration between faith leaders, communities, and primary care providers, and designed to remain patient-centered and coordinated with clinical care. The model includes spiritual self-care supports such as guidance on safe fasting practices in collaboration with healthcare providers, and personalized or group prayer delivered in person or remotely, alongside religious counsel that supports meaning-making during illness. Religious leaders provide regular encouragement through counseling, teachings, blessings, and participation in structured support groups (scriptural insights, group prayer, and individualized guidance). With patient and family permission, faith leaders may serve as liaisons between patients, healthcare providers, and the faith community to ensure spiritual beliefs are respected and reflected in care planning, and faith communities may mobilize practical support (e.g., volunteer services or fundraising) to reduce isolation and financial stress. Delivery options include home-based spiritual care (home visits and rites where applicable) and tele-spiritual care to maintain continuity when in-person visits are not possible. For crises and end-of-life periods, the package specifies round-the-clock spiritual availability coordinated alongside medical care, and it outlines transparent approaches to financial support for spiritual care needs.

Participant-proposed implementation strategies

Participants proposed actionable strategies aligned with implementing and sustaining the package. To address workforce gaps, training and retraining of personnel, particularly CHEWs and other frontline cadres was the dominant recommendation. To address medication access and affordability, participants emphasized funding for procurement, advocacy to government for medication provision, policies supporting local production, and financing mechanisms (including subsidized drugs and broader insurance coverage) to reduce out-of-pocket costs. To reduce delays and referral breakdown, participants emphasized establishing more health centers closer to communities, strengthening referral services (including after-hours referral where feasible), and sensitizing caregivers on when and where to refer. For psychosocial support, proposed actions included partnerships with NGOs and funding mechanisms, communication training for providers and patients, ethics education to protect confidentiality, and strengthening patient and family support groups to counter stigma. For spiritual care, proposed actions centered on community advocacy and sensitization to address cultural misconceptions, governance and policy measures to deter exploitation by dubious leaders, and ethics education for spiritual leaders to protect confidentiality and trust.

Discussion

The objective of this study was to co-create an implementation-ready, community-delivered PC package for rural Nigerian settings without PC specialists, and to specify its core components, delivery roles, and referral pathways. Our findings suggest that “Building Bridges to Care” is feasible in communities without PC specialists because it deliberately aligns service delivery with existing primary health care structures and roles.2,32 The package’s hub-and-spoke design, anchoring routine, low-intensity PC functions in the community while linking complex clinical decisions and escalation to a primary care “hub” fits established recommendations to integrate PC into primary care through task sharing, structured supervision, and referral linkages.33–36 The explicit delineation of responsibilities for community providers (e.g., CHEWs/CHAs as Anchors), who are an established cadre embedded within Nigeria’s primary health care system and routinely deliver community-facing preventive and basic clinical services along with facility-linked clinicians mitigate the common feasibility constraint in low-resource settings: a limited specialist workforce. This is consistent with evidence that community health workers (CHWs) can contribute meaningfully to PC delivery when systems support such as training, supervision, and integration with clinical services are in place.37,38

The co-design and GMB process was central to the development of this package because it enabled diverse stakeholders to move beyond identifying broad palliative care needs toward specifying practical delivery roles, referral thresholds, and implementation supports.19,20,27,28 Similar to participatory co-design approaches used in other low-resource settings, the process provided a structured platform for integrating experiential knowledge from patients, caregivers, community actors, religious/traditional leaders, and frontline providers with technical input from clinicians and implementation scientists.27,39,40 Anonymous Mentimeter ranking helped reduce dominance effects and supported transparent prioritization,21–23 while facilitated GMB activities helped participants examine how health-system constraints, sociocultural beliefs, stigma, medication access, workforce capacity, and referral systems interact to shape palliative care delivery.27,28 As this manuscript does not report pilot implementation or training delivery, its key contribution is the demonstration of a participatory process for translating stakeholder priorities into a contextually grounded, implementation-ready community palliative care package.

Feasibility, however, is conditional in solving several implementation bottlenecks that participants identified, especially training gaps, medication access, and functional referral pathways. The package’s enabling systems (supportive supervision, simple monitoring, confidentiality safeguards, and community governance) are therefore not “optional add-ons,” but the mechanisms that make task sharing safe and acceptable. This emphasis mirrors WHO guidance that PC integration into primary care requires competency-building, supportive systems, and reliable access to essential medicines, rather than relying solely on clinical protocols.41,42

If implemented as designed, the package has clear pathways to improve both access and quality of PC. Access is likely to improve through (i) decentralized entry points (community identification and follow-up); (ii) reduced travel and opportunity costs via household-level support; and (iii) clearer escalation routes that reduce delays in follow-up and specialty input. These mechanisms reflect core features of community-based PC approaches in other settings, where community networks and primary care linkages expand reach to populations otherwise excluded from facility-based PC.37,42

The package aligns with community-based PC models that mobilize community participation while strengthening health-system linkages. Kerala’s widely cited community PC approach highlights the value of community participation and sustainability; our model similarly emphasizes community engagement but places stronger emphasis on routine clinical oversight through a defined primary care “hub”.43,44 In addition, the package’s hub-and-spoke logic converges with primary PC capacity-building models (e.g., Project ECHO-style hub-supported learning and case-based support), while its formalized collaboration with faith leaders represents a context-specific adaptation likely to improve acceptability in Nigeria. 34

Sustained implementation depends on the enabling conditions participants highlighted, especially workforce preparation, reliable access to essential medicines, and functional referral networks each emphasized as critical in WHO guidance for integrating PC and symptom relief into PHC. 33 Governance mechanisms are also essential to maintain confidentiality and trust, particularly where stigma and concerns about exploitation may limit uptake, a recurrent theme in community-based PC literature. 44

Scalability is supported by the package’s modular structure (three service components) and platform orientation (hub-and-spoke plus enabling systems), allowing phased rollout as local capacity grows, an approach consistent with WHO’s PHC integration framework. 41 For adaptation across settings, the “fixed” elements should include role clarity, escalation/referral pathways, minimum training and supervision standards, and confidentiality safeguards; adaptable elements include which community actors provide support and how faith leader engagement is structured. Evidence on CHW integration into PC underscores that successful spread requires clear role definition, training, and integration with clinical teams rather than standalone community activity. 45

Limitations

This study should be interpreted in light of several limitations. Because some co-creation activities were conducted virtually and required at least secondary education, English proficiency, and access to email, an Android device, WhatsApp, and Zoom, participants with lower literacy or limited digital connectivity, particularly in hard-to-reach rural communities, may have been under-represented, which could affect transferability. In addition, as with most participatory workshops, group dynamics and social desirability may have influenced discussions and the final package design, although anonymous component ranking and structured facilitation were used to reduce dominance effects. Also, because this was a participatory co-creation study rather than a hypothesis-testing study, no formal power analysis was conducted. Although the sample was selected to maximize stakeholder and geographic diversity, the findings should be interpreted as the output of a structured participatory development process rather than as statistically representative of all palliative care stakeholders in Nigeria. Finally, our findings represent an implementation-ready blueprint rather than an evaluated intervention; feasibility, fidelity, acceptability, and early outcomes will need to be tested through real-world pilots before broader scale-up.

Future work should test the package in real-world pilots and quantify implementation and patient or caregiver outcomes across diverse Nigerian contexts, including hard-to-reach rural communities. These pilots should intentionally recruit participants with lower literacy and limited digital connectivity using community-based, low-tech engagement approaches (e.g., in-person PHC or community sessions, paper-based ranking, and phone-based participation), while documenting adaptations required for settings with lower connectivity and varying service capacity.

Conclusion

We co-created a community PC package (“Building Bridges to Care”) for Nigerian communities without PC specialists. Participants prioritized pain and symptom relief, improved communication and shared decision-making, intentional engagement of religious leaders, and stronger community–primary care collaboration. The resulting package integrates three core components; pain/symptom management, psychosocial support, and spiritual care delivered through a hub-and-spoke model with CHEWs/CHAs providing community-level identification, basic support, and continuity, and a primary care hub providing oversight and referrals. Key barriers included limited training and stigma, poor medication access, and weak referral systems; participants proposed training, policy/financing actions to improve medicines, and strengthened community support structures.

This task-shared, hub-linked model offers a practical pathway to expand whole-person PC within existing primary health care and community structures, while improving reach and continuity for underserved populations.

Next steps include piloting the package across diverse settings with structured CHEW training and supportive supervision, strengthened medicine access and referral agreements, and implementation evaluation of feasibility, acceptability, fidelity, reach, and early patient/caregiver outcomes.
