 SUMMARY

 InnoSepra’s project, “Transformational Sorbent-Based Process for a Substantial Reduction in
the Cost of CO2 Capture,” utilized computational tools, materials characterization, and lab and
pilot scale testing to optimize previously identified materials to determine their performance under
post-combustion capture conditions. InnoSepra utilized the test results to determine the energy
required for regeneration and to develop a process design/analysis to demonstrate the application
of developed materials for post-combustion capture. A techno-economic analysis was performed
to fully assess the potential of the materials for post-combustion capture. InnoSepra also updated
the State Point Data Table and completed the environmental, health, and safety (EH&S) Risk
Assessment.

  The InnoSepra CO2 capture technology has the potential for a significant reduction in the CO2
capture cost for the power plant and industrial flue gases.

• It is possible to obtain very high recovery (90-95%) and high purity (>98%) CO2 with physical
  sorbents while meeting the EOR/sequestration product specifications.
• The technology has the potential to reduce the thermal parasitic power by more than 65%, and
  the capital required by about 45% leading to about 45% reduction in the CO2 capture cost for
  the coal-based power plant flue gas.
• After demonstration at the pilot scale and further process optimization, the process has the
  potential for a capture cost of around $40/MT with no increase in Levelized Cost of Electricity
  (LCOE) and with very little loss in power output.

1.1 Major Project Goals
 The project goals for InnoSepra’s project, “Transformational Sorbent-Based Process for a
Substantial Reduction in the Cost of CO2 Capture,” were to demonstrate, through materials
selection and lab-scale testing, that a transformational CO2 capture technology based on physical
sorbents can help achieve DOE’s CO2 capture program goals in the short term. The project was
to utilize computational tools, bench-scale testing, and detailed engineering design to estimate
equipment sizes and parasitic power needs, and a techno-economic analysis to fully assess the
potential of the process. Because the InnoSepra process uses materials with low heats of sorption,
and heat extraction occurs at a much lower temperature than in solvent-based processes, the impact

 8

on power loss due to steam extraction is significantly lower. Based on a preliminary techno-
economic analysis, the InnoSepra team was to confirm projections that the process can lead to
more than 50% reduction in capital cost (excluding CO2 compressor), and more than 70%
reduction in power loss due to steam extraction relative to the leading solvent-based technologies
such as Shell’s Cansolv solvent technology (Case B12B in DOE’s 2019 Baseline Report).
 The objectives of this project were to demonstrate that the InnoSepra process can achieve a
projected CO2 capture cost below $40/tonne. A pathway to achieve a capture cost below $30/tonne
was to be provided as well. After lab-scale testing of the process, another objective of this project
was to integrate all the process components and test with an actual flue gas source in support of
the engineering study and the techno-economic analysis. Yet another objective of this project was
to provide a Technology Maturation Plan to show a pathway toward achieving these objectives.
 The ultimate goals of the Project were to confirm the expected performance at the lab and
bench-scale and to provide sufficient data to enable a detailed techno-economic analysis for a
commercial-scale plant, confirming the projected economics. This was to provide a high degree
of confidence in the applicability, cost-effectiveness, and practical feasibility of this process and
demonstrate that this technology can meet the overall performance goal for the development of
advanced CO2 capture and separation technologies (90% CO2 removal at a capture cost of less
than $40/tonne and preferably below $30/tonne) and limit the increase in LCOE by more than 30%
compared to the current approaches.

the InnoSepra Process can be as low as 2.5% if an NGCC plant is used to provide the electrical
power for capture. These results are in line with the project goals in the previous section.

Task 3 – Identification of Suitable Materials based on Literature Review, Computational
Tools, and Lab Scale Measurements

 The objectives of this task were to identify suitable sorbent materials (4-5) for lab-scale testing
based on literature review, predictions based on computational tools, and measurement of
identified material properties using the adsorption microbalance.

 A detailed literature search was carried out to identify sorbent materials and regenerants
suitable for the proposed process. The materials identified through the literature search were
further narrowed based on Monte Carlo simulations and measurements of sorption isotherms in
the adsorption microbalance. The identified regenerants needed to have low CO2 solubility at both
the regeneration temperature as well as the adsorption temperature. Finally, based on
environmental considerations, the chosen regenerants needed to have a low global warming
potential and low ozone depletion potential.

Results and Discussion:

 The results of the literature search indicated that commercially available sorbents, as well as
their modified forms, will be suitable for the process. This will provide materials with different
pore sizes. The absolute CO2 capacities for the identified materials range between 14 and 17-wt%
(at 25oC and a CO2 partial pressure of 0.15 atma), and the CO2/N2 selectivities range between 15-
20 for the commercial materials and over 100 for the modified materials. The two most likely
regenerants included liquids with boiling points between about 95 °C and 120 °C.

 Monte Carlo simulations and sorption isotherm measurements confirmed the literature search
results. The effect of modifications on CO2 and N2 capacities for one commercial material, as
predicted by Monte Carlo simulation, is shown in Figure 3.1. As the modification level approaches
about 20% the material has essentially no N2 capacity, and the loss in CO2 capacity is about 25%.
At higher modification levels, the material has a very high CO2/N2 selectivity of 660 and a
moderately lower CO2 capacity compared to commercially available material.

 13

  Figure 3.1: Effect of modification levels on CO2 and N2 capacities for a commercial material

 A procedure for modifying commercial materials was developed. The CO2 & N2 isotherms as
a function of temperature (0, 20, 40, 60, 80, 100oC) were measured on these materials using
Micromeritics ASAP-2020. Sorption isotherms for CO2 and N2 on commercial materials and
modified materials are in line with literature values as well as Monte Carlo simulations. One of
these isotherms is shown in Figure 3.2. For a commercial material with 50%-modification, a CO2
capacity of about 17-wt% for a CO2 partial pressure of about 150 mm Hg (15% CO2 in flue gas at
1-atm) can be obtained.

Task 5: Laboratory-Scale Breakthrough and Cyclic Testing of Selected Materials to
Determine CO2 Capture Performance

 The objectives of this task were to conduct lab-scale testing with up to 4 selected materials
under a variety of conditions in breakthrough as well as cyclic modes to determine CO2 capacities,
CO2 purities, and recoveries, and the energy needed for regeneration.

 Breakthrough tests with two materials (ZA1 and ZA2) at 25oC using a feed containing 15%
CO2 in N2 were carried out to enable sorbent downselection.

 19

Results and Discussion:

 The results for the ZA2 sorbent are shown in Figure 5.1 and indicate that the breakthrough
times and the CO2 capacities at the expected adsorption temperature (~25oC) are significantly
higher than the breakthrough times and the CO2 capacities at the expected regeneration
temperature (~100 oC). The results for the ZA1 material were similar to those for the ZA2 material,
with much higher capacities at the expected adsorption temperature (~25oC) and lower capacities
at the expected regeneration temperature (~100 oC). In general, CO2 capacities for the ZA1
material are lower than those for the ZA2 material, and the breakthrough curve is more spread out
compared to the breakthrough curve for the ZA2 material.

 All the sorbents tested during Budget Period 1 have very good CO2-N2 separation factors, 26-
654, as indicated in Table 3.1. These separation factors are more than adequate to produce high-
purity CO2 (>95%) in the InnoSepra Process. Based on breakthrough testing and isotherm
measurements carried out during this budget period, the ZA2 material has the highest net CO2
capacity (difference in the capacity between adsorption and regeneration) and a very good CO2-
N2 selectivity. The regeneration energy consists of the following:

 Regeneration Energy = Heat of Adsorption + Energy Needed to Heat the Sorbent + Energy
 Needed to Heat the Vessel + Sensible heat of CO2

 20

 Figure 5.1: CO2 breakthrough curves for the ZA2 material

 The heat of adsorption for all the sorbents is nearly the same, about 38 kJ/mol of CO2. Because
of the highest net CO2 capacity, the ZA2 material will have the lowest amount of total energy
needed, as it needs the lowest amount of energy to heat the sorbent and the vessel. For this reason,
the ZA2 material was chosen for further testing. The regenerants with the lowest ozone depletion
potential and global warming potential, and low CO2 solubility, EM3 and EM4, are suitable for
use with the ZA2 material.

 The regeneration tests were carried out with the regenerant in both liquid and vapor form. Later
tests were carried out with regenerant vapor, as this approach can reduce the regenerant flow by a
factor of more than 20 compared to liquid regenerant. This is due to the fact that the latent heat of
evaporation is significantly higher than the liquid sensible heat. EM4 was used for these tests at a
temperature of 110 oC. We were able to reduce the liquid flow by a factor of over 5, and the vapor
phase regeneration gave a higher CO2 capacity compared to regeneration with liquid alone.

 For regeneration tests with EM4 vapor, a synthetic mixture of CO2 and N2 was prepared by

 21

mixing pure streams of the individual gases, and the concentration of feed CO2 was varied between
6 and 12%. The mixture was fed to the adsorbent bed at a flow rate between 7.5 and 10 slpm until
the CO2 concentration at the bed exit was 1-2 % At that point, the regenerant was fed counter-
currently to the feed at a flow rate of 0.5 to 1 slpm for about 7 min. This was followed by another
7-minute step in which the regenerant flow was stopped, and a vacuum pump was turned on to
complete the removal of the adsorbed carbon dioxide and any regenerant remaining in the bed.
The bed was then cooled down with a nitrogen stream to be ready to start the cycle again and
receive the dry flue gas stream. Several experiments were carried out to ensure that results were
reproducible and that the system reached a steady state, namely, the amounts adsorbed and
desorbed converged, and there was no CO2 accumulation in the beds.

Task 6: Process Simulation

 The objective of this task was to update the process model based on the results of lab-scale
testing. The deliverable from this task was a process model that combined that could be used to
predict the performance of full-size CO2 capture plants, for process optimization, and for sizing

 25

various process equipment.

  A process simulation model integrating the CO2 capture process and the coal-based power plant
was developed to take advantage of potential synergies. Aveva’s ProII process simulation software
was licensed for this work.

Results and Discussion:

 The initial work was focused on optimizing the CO2 compression and liquefaction parts of the
capture. The starting point is compressed CO2 after the 4th compression stage. The CO2 stream at
45 bara is obtained after a 4-stage compression of CO2 from the CO2 capture process. This gaseous
CO2 stream is cooled in the compressed CO2 cooler before being sent to the CO2 condenser. The
liquid CO2 is pumped to a pressure of 150 bara in the process. The ProII model for the process,
including four compression stages and the stream table for the process, was provided in the
quarterly reports. A techno-economic analysis is provided under Task 7.

 A complete process simulation was set up in Aveva Pro/II to evaluate the performance of the
CO2 recovery and compression process for a 550 MW power plant. The feed (74,092 kmol/hr)
enters the process at 57 oC and 100 kPa, and its composition is 68.1% N2, 13.5% CO2, 15.17%
H2O, 8.2% Ar, and 0.02% O2. This flow rate is very close to the flow rate in Case B12A of the
2015 DOE Baseline Report (Cost and Performance Baseline for Fossil Energy Plants, Vol 1a:
Bituminous Coal (PC) and Natural Gas to Electricity, Rev 3, DOE/NETL-2015/1723, July 6,
2015).
 The feed in Figure 6.1 first goes to a SO2 removal unit (EX-SO2_REM), its pressure is raised
to 114.1 kPa, and it is then cooled down to 27.8 oC in a direct contact cooler unit (T1). The resulting
stream enters a PSA dryer (EX_DRYER) in which water vapor is adsorbed, and the dry stream
enters the CO2 adsorption beds (EX_CO2_BEDS) containing the CO2 sorbent. The adsorbed CO2
is desorbed in two stages; in the first stage, a hot regenerant flows down the bed to heat it and
facilitate the CO2 desorption, and, in the second stage, a vacuum is applied to remove an additional
amount of CO2 as well as any regenerant remaining in the bed. The CO2/regenerant stream is sent
to a tank (T-102), where gas/liquid separation takes place, and the resulting stream is reheated to
100oC to be used during the regeneration step for the CO2 beds. The rich CO2 stream is cooled

 26

down to 10 oC to condense vapor from the regenerant and then enters an adsorbent bed where any
remaining regenerant vapor is adsorbed. The product stream now contains >99.0% CO2 and less
than 0.02% O2. This stream is compressed to 5,580 kPa through a four-stage compression (C1 to
C4). The compressed gas goes through two heat exchangers, E1 and E2, to condense it and is
pumped to 15,270 kPa for pipeline transport. The product flow rate is 9,516.5 kmol/hr, which
represents a 95% recovery of CO2. A summary of the utilities required for the process flowsheet
in Figure 6.1 is provided in Table 6.1.

Regenerant Heater / Vaporizer
 One of the concerns that arose during the detailed design in Q1-2022 was the sizing of the
regenerant heater/vaporizer HE-304. We rigorously modeled this heat exchanger using the data
provided by one of the vendors (CEXI). The calculation indicated that the heat exchanger did not
have enough surface area to vaporize the required quantity of EM4. After we shared our results
with the vendor, the vendor withdrew the bid.

 A quote was also received from Graham Manufacturing (Helicoil heat exchanger). The vendor
uses a proprietary design, and there was no way to verify their design using the available tools. It
was decided to develop our own design tools for this vaporizer using rigorous methods (Chen,
Correlation for Boiling Heat Transfer to Saturated Fluids in Convective Flow, I&EC Pro Des Dev,
v5, n3, p322, July 1966) as well as the Aveva ProII simulator. The results from both methods were
within 25% of each other. Using these tools, we were able to model a commercially available heat
exchanger from McMaster-Carr and an existing InnoSepra heat exchanger (Figure 10.3).

 The simulation results indicate that using two McMaster heat exchangers in parallel or using
one McMaster heat exchanger in series with the existing InnoSepra heat exchanger (Figure 10.3)
will provide enough heat transfer area to vaporize 100% of EM4. Further lab testing coupled with
simulation indicated that the existing InnoSepra heat exchanger is sufficient to vaporize 100% of
EM4, provided EM4 flows on the tube side.. Only about 60% of the tube area is needed for EM4
vaporization. The remaining area is used for superheating.

 As the list of trips and alarms was finalized, procedures for different types of shutdowns were
developed. In a Normal Shutdown, CO2 beds are shut down, and Bed A is manually pressurized
with Instrument Air (IA). Also, Dryer beds, Hot Glycol/Regenerant/Low Temp Demineralized
Water (DMW), Feed, and Utilities (Process water, IA, Chiller) are shut down. In a Warm
Shutdown, only the Dryer beds and CO2 beds are shut down.

 In a Soft Shutdown, CO2 beds continue cycling to complete the step they are in. Then, CO2
beds, Dryer beds, Hot Glycol/Regenerant/Low Temp DMW, and Feed are shut down. The Utilities

Flue Gas Purification

 A collection of the NO2 removal performance data from the Mongstad Heat Plant (MHP) and
RFCC flue gases utilizing InnoSepra’s flue gas purification technology was carried out. The MHP
flue gas has 8-9% CO2. NO2 is an impurity in the flue gas and would end up in the CO2 product
for the InnoSepra Process. NO2 is also a catalyst for solvent degradation in solvent-based capture
processes. Successful performance would be very beneficial since it can lead to a significant
reduction in solvent degradation and loss.

 For most tests, the flue gas flow rate was set to 300 nm3/hr (FT103), while a few additional
tests were conducted at 400 nm3/hr and 500 nm3/hr. Bed temperature and pressure were the same
as the flue gas supply temperature and pressure. The NO2 analyzer was calibrated and validated
against TCM’s online NO2 measurement.

 NO2 concentration in the feed varied between 12 and 20+ ppm, and NO2 in the flue gas exiting
the purification bed was continuously measured. The results confirm that the purification bed was
performing satisfactorily, and NO2 was removed to below the analyzer detection limit, <0.03 ppm,
for the entire test period. Figure 11.1 shows the performance for the period spanning from October
2023 to January 2024 for MHP flue gas. All of the feed NO2 was removed during the entire test
 65

period, while little or no NO (Nitric Oxide) was removed. Only NO2 is known to cause amine
degradation, and NO removal is not required to prevent this. Not having to remove NO provides
a significantly lower NOX removal cost for the InnoSepra Process compared to other technologies,
such as SCR (selective catalytic reduction), that remove both NO and NO2.

CO2 Breakthrough Curves

 The original test plan was to run the 4 adsorber beds on a cycle, but due to the issues associated
with the regenerant heating and a leak in one of the heat exchangers, the plan was modified to run
single-bed adsorber tests to collect relevant CO2 capture data. As part of the effort, the primary
focus was to identify any issues and improve the heating performance of the adsorber bed with the
regenerant.
 Tests were performed to get the CO2 breakthrough curves, which provide essential information
for the operation of the test unit. Tests were done at three different flue gas flow rates: 300, 400,
and 500 nm3/hr. All tests utilized MHP flue gas. The 300 nm3/hr test was performed with 9.5%
CO2 in the feed. The 400 nm3/hr and 500 nm3/hr tests were performed with 8.5% CO2 in the feed.
The CO2 breakthrough curves are shown in Figures 11.5 to 11.7. For the 300 nm3/hr test, there
was no cooling water flowing through the heat exchanger. No heat was removed during adsorption,
and there was no fluctuation in the outlet CO2 concentration. On the other hand, cooling water did
flow through the heat exchanger for the 400 nm3/hr and 500 nm3/hr tests. The removal of some of
the heat of adsorption leads to fluctuation in the bed outlet CO2 concentration.
 68

 For the 300 nm3/hr test, the concentration and temperature fronts closely follow each other
(Figure 11.5). If the adsorption stopped at about 30-32 minutes, virtually all the heat of adsorption
would stay in the bed, and the heat of adsorption would not be needed during regeneration.
Retaining the heat of adsorption does increase the average adsorption temperature and reduces the
CO2 capacity. However, the savings from retaining the heat of adsorption, 0.6-0.8 GJ/MT,
significantly outweigh the additional heat needed to heat the additional sieve and the vessel, <0.1
GJ/MT. For low-feed CO2 concentrations (<5% CO2), more than 75% of the heat of adsorption
can be retained without a significant impact on capacity. For higher feed CO2 concentrations
(>10% CO2), at least some heat of adsorption, 25-50%, would need to be removed to get acceptable
CO2 capacities.

Operational Issues

 Regenerant analyzer calibration: Not finalized until December 2023; only skids 1 and 3 could
be tested until mid-October, and only closed-loop heating for skids 2 and 3 until mid-December.
 Chiller Operation: A low-temperature chiller (HE301B) was installed to condense most of the
regenerant vapor at -30 oC before it goes to the guard bed. Despite many attempts to fix the chiller,
we were not able to operate the chiller below -5 oC.
 Regenerant Heating: Regenerant heating issues in HE-304 made it very difficult to transfer
enough heat to the CO2 beds for regeneration. We were able to get temperatures over 100 oC in
both Skid 2 (CO2 beds) and Skid 3 (regenerant heating) in manual operation mode. However, this
could not be repeated. An alternative regenerant was identified and tested after the HS&E
evaluation. It was loaded in the Hot Liquid Tank, but only limited tests were carried out.
 Hot air regeneration after the adsorption step was used to determine the energy needed for
regeneration. In a single-bed cycle, the adsorption data (capacities, recoveries) can be obtained
independently of the regeneration cycle.
 Freezing issues: Due to the unusual and continuous low ambient temperatures sustained during
Winter at Mongstad, several freezing episodes were observed that affected the operation of the

 73

plant. The plant was not designed to operate in the freezing weather, and several sections of the
plant had to be thawed, and the purification bed had to be emptied to prevent further issues.
 Emissions & Environment: There were no adverse emissions incidents during the testing

was, however, within the permissible limits.

 The InnoSepra Process that forms the basis of this project and its integration with a pulverized
coal-fired power plant are shown in Figure 12.2. In Figure 12.2, the additional unit operations
needed for CO2 capture are shown in blue, new cooling water lines are shown in green, and new

fan/blower by about 2-2.5 psig. The flue gas exiting the FGD, typically containing 50-100 ppm

SOX in the flue gas is removed in the direct contact cooler through the use of sodium hydroxide.
The flue gas stream exiting the DCC is sent to a flue gas purification stage wherein nearly 100%
of the remaining SOX and NO2 are removed. Not all the unit operations in this flowsheet, including
SOX removal, were tested in this project. The purified flue gas is sent to a two-stage adsorption
system. Particulate adsorbents are used in both stages. In the moisture removal stage, moisture is
removed to below 1 ppm. Both the flue gas purification part of the process (removal of SOX and
NO2 to below 0.1 ppm levels) and moisture removal to below 1 ppm level were demonstrated at
Technology Centre Mongstad (TCM).

 The CO2 from dry flue gas is adsorbed in the second adsorption stage. The CO2 adsorption
beds contain a sorbent for CO2 adsorption. The process configuration in Figure 12.2 protects the
CO2 adsorbent from the flue gas contaminants as well as moisture. The adsorbents used in the
InnoSepra process have a significantly higher affinity for CO2 compared to N2, O2, or NO. Nitric
oxide (NO) is not removed in the moisture beds or the CO2 adsorption beds, and the NO impurities
remain in the dry CO2-depleted stream 10 leaving this section.

 The CO2 adsorption section consists of multiple beds to enable a continuous process with each
bed undergoing the steps of adsorption, rinse with carbon dioxide, heating with evacuation,
purging with CO2-depleted product, and cooling in sequence. The adsorbed CO2 is desorbed using
a combination of heat and vacuum to produce high-purity CO2 and is sent to the compressor train.

 87

 The sorbents in the CO2 beds are heated by the regenerant, which in turn is heated by steam
from the power plant in a separate heat exchanger (not shown). Regeneration is carried out at
temperatures of 100-110 0C. During the heating part of the cycle, CO2 is removed using a vacuum
blower and, after cooling to separate the regenerant, is sent to the first stage of the CO2 compressor.
The CO2 compressor has four or more stages and compresses the CO2 to about 2,200 psig for
pipeline transport. The CO2 product after the 4th compression stage can optionally be condensed
at a temperature of about 7oC (45 bara) using cooling water at about 5oC. Liquid CO2 at 7 oC is
pumped to a pressure of 2,200 psig. Compared to a standard 7-stage compression process, this
configuration saves more than 15% of the energy needed for CO2 compression. The CO2 purity
from the InnoSepra process (<10 ppm O2, <5 ppm each of SO2 and H2O) is adequate for direct use
in EOR or sequestration applications. Part of the LP steam from the LP/IP crossover, stream 17, is
used for heating the regenerant, and condensate, stream 18, is sent to the boiler feed water (BFW)
preheater to heat the condensate from the LP turbine. Since the LP stream is at a pressure higher
than what is needed for the regeneration of the CO2 beds, it is expanded in a backpressure turbine
to produce additional power. Streams 20 and 21 from the BFW preheater are further heated and
sent to the PC boiler for steam generation. A small amount of the cooling water stream leaving
DCC is purged to prevent salt build-up.

 The InnoSepra Process, in its commercial embodiment, will use several fixed sorbent beds in
parallel to preferentially adsorb CO2 from the flue gas stream, and subsequently liberate the CO2
at high purity via heating the material and subsequently pulling a vacuum to further regenerate the
material and produce additional CO2 (Figure 12.3). A dehydration unit is used to remove moisture
from the CO2-containing flue gas stream before being introduced to the CO2 adsorption unit. This
dehydration unit consists of a direct contact cooler used to reduce the temperature in the gas stream
and saturation concentration of water, followed by several parallel fixed beds of desiccant that are
used to dehydrate and polish the moisture from the flue gas stream. These beds are regenerated
through purging with dehydrated flue gas from which the CO2 has been removed.

 The feed pressurization is accomplished with a forced draft (FD) fan that takes flue gas from
the FGD outlet stream before the stack in a traditional coal-fired power plant. The outlet from this
FD fan is to the direct contact cooling (DCC) tower, which circulates water with a small amount
of sodium hydroxide present in order to cool the incoming flue gas, lower the moisture content of
 88

the incoming gas, and also reduce the acid gas content (SOX and NOX) of the gas below the existing
FGD removal levels. After the DCC and flue gas purification, the flue gas is split into several trains
of adsorption beds for further moisture removal and CO2 separation.

 Figure 12.3. The InnoSepra Process integrated with the power plant

 The InnoSepra Process integrated with the power plant for the techno-economic analysis is
shown in Figure 12.3.

 Because of the scale of the separation required, the adsorption component of the process is
designed to run in several independent parallel trains, each of which consists of two drying beds
and three CO2 adsorption beds. Due to the size constraints of the largest vessels and the vacuum
pumps, the minimum number of trains that need to be employed to capture the CO2 from a 650
MW coal-fired plant is five parallel trains. These five trains produce CO2 at atmospheric pressure
and then feed into one compression train that compresses the CO2 product to a pipeline pressure
of 2,215 psia (152.7 bar).

 Heating for the process is provided by two different mechanisms. In the CO2 adsorption beds,

 89

the heat transfer medium is the regenerant heated in a heat exchanger by condensing steam
extracted at the IP/LP turbine crossover. Because there is significant recoverable energy in the
steam between the extraction temperature and pressure and the ultimate temperature and pressure
of the steam used to heat the regenerant, a letdown or backpressure turbine is used to recover what
would otherwise be wasted recoverable energy. The regenerant from this heating cycle process
flows through sorbent beds in the CO2 adsorption system to heat the sorbent and release the CO2.

 The other heat requirement is for regenerating the moisture removal beds. These moisture
removal beds are based on the heatless dryer concept, where humid gas is contacted with a sorbent
during the drying step and the bed is subsequently regenerated through purging with a dry gas
stream. A small amount of heat may be required to regenerate the dryer beds. The heat required
for this step can be fairly low quality. In the case of the InnoSepra process, the heating for the
dehydration step can be achieved through heat recovery from the last three stages of the CO2
compression system.

 CO2 compression is achieved first within each adsorption train via a vacuum pump operating
at pressures from 1 bar down to 0.15 bar (14.7-2.2 psia) with an outlet of 1 bar pressure. The
atmospheric CO2 stream is then cooled and compressed in a single train of 4 centrifugal
compressors with intercoolers and heat integration, and a liquid pump to the required pipeline
pressure of 152.7 bar (2,215 psia).

 An ASPEN Plus simulation for a 650 MWnet SCPC plant with CO2 capture using the InnoSepra
process was carried out (Case B12C), and the flowsheet is shown in Figure 12.4. Flue gas
(1FLUEGAS) enters the system at 57 oC and 1 bar pressure. Any liquid carryover is removed in a
separator (CONDSEP). It flows then (2FDPUR) through a purification bed (PURBED) where NO2
and SO2 are removed. The resulting stream (3FDBLWR) enters the blower (C-101), where the
pressure is raised to 1.141 bar (4FDDCC) and enters a Direct Contact Cooler (DCC) to cool down
the gas. This saturated stream (5FDDRYR) enters a dryer to generate a dried stream (6FDCO2BD)
that goes to the CO2 adsorber beds (ADSCO2BD). The dryer is regenerated with a CO2-depleted
stream (7CO2DEPD). The stream coming out of the dryers (8CO2DEPM) is released into the
atmosphere.

 The CO2-rich stream that comes out of the CO2 adsorber beds during the liquid regeneration
 90

and vacuum steps (33RGNXAD) goes through a splitter (VPSPLIT) that separates the liquid and
vapor phases. The liquid stream (34RGNGRV) flows into a hot liquid tank (RGNTNK). The
vacuum pump (P-103) is a two-stage unit that sends the vapor stream to the same hot liquid tank.
The liquid regenerant stream (30RGNFD) is fed to a liquid pump P-102 that in turn feeds
(31RGNHTR) the vaporizer HX-101 and leaves the unit as a vapor (32RGNADS) that is used to
regenerate the CO2 adsorption beds.

 The CO2-rich stream out of the hot liquid tank (37RGNHEX) is cooled down in a heat
exchanger (HX-103) using water at 25oC. The resulting stream (9FDCMP) enters a 4-stage
compressor in which the pressure is raised to 55 bar. The compressed stream (10CO2XCM) passes
to a guard bed (RGNGB) to remove any traces of the regenerant. The purified stream (11CO2GTL)
goes to a heat exchanger (HX-104) cooled by water and, as the temperature drops to 10 oC, the
compressed CO2 condenses (11CO2LTP). A liquid pump P-102 raises the liquid CO2 pressure to
155 bar (12CO2LPD).

Lessons Learned from TCM Testing

• Need to finalize any regulatory issues, such as approval for chemicals used in the process,
 before testing.
• Project delays pushed the testing to Q1-24, leading to significant freezing issues that were not
 accounted for in the equipment design. In hindsight, the equipment should have been
 winterized.
• A prior relationship with the project team is very helpful in executing various project tasks.
• Lower TRL unit operations are much more challenging to commission and test at TCM. This
 is due to field updates and modifications needed to the equipment, and delays in getting
 replacement parts and supplies. Additional spares could have been ordered to mitigate some of
 the delays.
• Despite all the issues, the test results are very valuable for not only adsorption-based capture
 technologies but also for solvent and membrane-based capture technologies. The flue gas
 purification part of the process is very valuable for the solvent-based capture processes as it
 removes nearly 100% of SOX and NO2, and significant amounts of aerosols. Removal of these
 impurities prior to solvent-based capture would significantly reduce solvent degradation and
 environmental emissions. The flue gas drying part of the process can benefit both the
 membrane-based capture processes and the capture processes based on freezing. The CO2
 compression cycle developed during this project can benefit all capture technologies.

Summary

 The InnoSepra CO2 capture technology has the potential for a significant reduction in the CO2
capture cost for the power plant and industrial flue gases. The process can provide both very high
recovery (90-95%) and high purity (>98%) CO2 with physical sorbents while meeting the
EOR/sequestration product specifications. The process has the potential to reduce the thermal
parasitic power by more than 65%, and the capital required by about 45% leading to about 45%
reduction in the CO2 capture cost for the coal-based power plant flue gas compared to amine-based
capture processes. After demonstration at a bigger scale and further process optimization, the
process has the potential for a capture cost of around $40/MT with no increase in LCOE and with
very little loss in power output.
 100

Summary of Capture Process Co-benefits

 While InnoSepra’s CO2 capture technology can provide a significant reduction in the CO2
capture cost, it also provides significant co-benefits for all the CO2 capture technologies.
 InnoSepra’s flue gas purification technology provides a low-cost option for the removal of
NO2 and SOX to sub-ppm levels and a significant reduction in aerosol emissions, and is applicable
to all solvent-based capture technologies. It can significantly reduce emissions from solvent-based
capture processes.
 InnoSepra’s CO2 compression technology can reduce the cost of CO2 compression to pipeline
pressures by about 20%. It is applicable to both solvent-based capture (3.4 bara CO2 pressure) as
well as membrane and sorbent-based capture (~1 bara CO2 pressure).
 InnoSepra’s flue gas drying technology can be used with other capture technologies, such as
membranes and capture by freezing, to reduce capture costs. It can also be upgraded for
regenerative removal of NO2, SO2, and Hg, potentially eliminating the need for new FGDs and
SCRs for industrial capture.
