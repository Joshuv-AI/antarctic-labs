High efficiency gold extraction by 2D metallic 1T/1T′ phase transition metal dichalcogenides
Authors: Liang Mei, Mingzi Sun, Ruijie Yang, Ting Ying, Ruixin Yan, Weikang Zheng, Yue Zhang, Honglu Hu, Damien Voiry, Kenneth M. Y. Leung, Chuyang Y. Tang, Bolong Huang, Shihong Lin, Zhiyuan Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recovery of precious metals from waste has high social and economic value, but in practice, recycling is often inefficient due to limits in technologies and materials. Here we show a group of materials—two-dimensional (2D) metallic 1 T/1 T′ phase transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs, MoS2, WS2, TaS2, and TiS2)—that allows for efficient, selective, and scalable extraction of valuable gold (Au) from leaching solutions of end-of-life electronics. The materials exhibit rapid kinetics, delivering an impressive 99.99% extraction efficiency of Au3+ in merely 0.25 min. Furthermore, they showcase strong selectivity for Au3+ adsorption, featuring a distribution coefficient (Kd) 5-6 orders of magnitude higher than that of other competitive cations (Cu2+, K+, Ni2+, Na+, Zn2+, Ca2+, and Mg2+). Amongst, TiS2 exhibits an unprecedented extraction capacity of 9093 mg⋅g−1 toward Au3+, which is approximately 1 order of magnitude higher than most other benchmark adsorbents (silver-organic frameworks). Importantly, through batch production of TiS2 nanosheets (5 g) and subsequent membranes fabrication for gold extraction, we demonstrated 0.15 g gold (23.8 Karat) recovery by treating 30 discarded central processing units (CPUs). Our work provides a foundation for the efficient and selective recovery of high-value metals from waste, contributing to a sustainable and circular economy.

Introduction

Global E-waste Monitor 2020 expects a substantial surge in global electronic waste (e-waste) from 9 million tons in 2020 to 75 million tons by 20301. E-waste still contains metals (gold, for example)2,3 that are often critical elements with high economic value. Their recycling is thus critical to a circular economy4–10. Typically, the high-value metals can be precipitated from the leaching solutions of e-waste by adding chemicals or selectively extracted using a more soluble solvent11–13. The target metals can also be selectively adsorbed by an adsorbent or ion exchange resin14. Selective associations with a carrier molecule in liquid membranes or electrochemical reduction and deposition onto cathodes are also feasible technologies for metal recovery15,16. Among the various available technologies, adsorption holds significant promise due to its simplicity and room temperature operation17–19.

Although many adsorbents have been applied for the recovery of metals, they each face challenges of having insufficient efficiency or inadequate selectivity that make them less likely to be sustainable materials for metal recovery at industrial scale17. For instance, metal-organic frameworks (MOFs)20,21 and covalent organic frameworks (COFs)22,23—typical nanoporous materials—exhibit good adsorption kinetics and selectivity for gold ion reduction, due to their intrinsic porosity and purpose-designed functional groups. However, their low extraction capacity (<2100 mg g−1) significantly limits their practical application. Reduced graphene oxide (rGO), a well-known two-dimensional (2D) material, exhibits an extraordinary extraction capacity of 9059 mg g−1 toward gold from a dilute gold solution (10 ppm) at 60 °C24. However, adsorption using rGO requires a relatively long retention time of 30 min. Other adsorbents based on 2D materials, such as semiconducting (2H-phase) molybdenum disulfide (MoS2) and its composites typically exhibit limited gold extraction performance (adsorption capacities of ~1100 mg g−1), and relatively slow kinetics (40–100 min)25,26.

In this study, we report a group of materials—electrochemically exfoliated 2D metallic 1T/1T′ phase transition metal dichalcogenide (TMD) nanosheets (NSs) (Fig. 1a), including MoS2, WS2, TaS2, TiS2—that can achieve highly efficient extraction of gold with superior selectivity and kinetics (see Fig. 1b). In particular, TiS2 exhibits an unprecedented adsorption capacity of 9093 mg g−1 at room temperature (25 °C) in just 0.25 min, surpassing all the other reported adsorbents to date. Results and discussion

Highly efficient gold extraction

The 2D metallic 1T/1T′ phase TMD NSs, including MoS2, WS2, TaS2, and TiS2, were prepared from their bulk counterparts (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2) by using an electrochemical lithium-ion (Li+) intercalation and exfoliation method (see Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. 3, Methods for details)27–30. After exfoliation, the 2D TMD NSs disperse well in deionized water, forming an opaque suspension (Supplementary Fig. 4) due to their negative surface charge (from −63.9 mV to −50.8 mV, Supplementary Fig. 5). Atomic force microscopy (AFM) images reveal that MoS2 and TaS2 are monolayers less than 1 nm thick, while WS2 and TiS2 are bilayers <2 nm thick (Supplementary Fig. 6). Aberration-corrected high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (ADF-STEM) images show the 1T′ structure in MoS2 and WS2 (Supplementary Fig. 7a, b) and the 1 T structure in TaS2 and TiS2 (Supplementary Fig. 7c, d). 1T′-MoS2 and 1T′-WS2 can be derived from their bulk 2H counterparts. This phase transition occurs during Li+ intercalation, where electrons are transferred from the s orbitals of the guest lithium to the d orbitals of the host transition metal atoms (Mo or W) to maintain charge neutrality. When the number of transferred electrons exceeds a critical threshold (0.29 and 0.30 electrons per formula unit for MoS2 and WS2, respectively)31,32, the 2H phase becomes less stable than the 1T or 1T′ phases, thereby inducing a transition from 2H to 1T or 1T′. This method enables the high-yield (≥92%) production of mono- or bilayer TMD NSs that are predominantly in the metallic 1T/1T′ phase and carry much negative surface charge. These features are particularly beneficial for enhancing both the adsorption capacity and kinetics in gold extraction. Therefore, we selected this method to prepare 2D 1T/1T′ TMD NSs.

Ideal gold adsorbents should be highly selective. To assess their selectivity, the TMD (MoS2, WS2, TaS2, TiS2) NSs were tested in mixed solutions containing Au3+ and seven competing metal cations (Cu2+, K+, Ni2+, Na+, Zn2+, Ca2+, and Mg2+), each at a concentration of 30 ppm (see Fig. 1b and Methods for details). Compared with the 1–9% removal rate of competitive metal cations, 99.99% of Au3+ can be removed by MoS2 (Fig. 2a) and TiS2 (Fig. 2c), demonstrating superior selectivity of TMD NSs for Au3+ extraction. Moreover, the distribution coefficients (Kd, mL g−1) were calculated to quantify the affinity between various cations and TMD NSs (Methods for details)33. The Kd values of Au3+ for MoS2, and TiS2 are 6.0 × 108 mL g−1 (Fig. 2b) and 1.1 × 109 mL g−1 (Fig. 2d), respectively. These values are 5–6 orders of magnitude higher than those of other competitive metal cations (Cu2+, K+, Ni2+, Na+, Zn2+, Ca2+, and Mg2+), further suggesting the excellent selectivity of the TMD NSs toward dynamic uptake of Au3+ in water. The adsorption capacities of MoS2 and TiS2 for Au3+ under multi-ion conditions are 1428 and 2400 mg g−1 (Fig. 2a, c), respectively—closely reaching their single-ion adsorption capacities (Supplementary Fig. 8)—thereby confirming their strong selectivity toward Au3+. This high selectivity remains unaffected by the presence of different types of anions in the solution. In different Au3+ solutions containing common anions (SO42−, NO3−, and Cl−) at an initial concentration of 1.0 mol L−1, the Au3+ extraction rate of the TMD NSs consistently reached 99.99% (Supplementary Fig. 9). Beyond high selectivity, the TMD NSs also exhibited impressive extraction kinetics, taking only 0.5 min for MoS2, and 0.25 min for TiS2 to attain 99.99% Au3+ extraction with an initial concentration of 30 ppm (Fig. 2e, Supplementary Fig. 10), which is 1–2 orders of magnitude faster than state-of-the-art adsorbents (Supplementary Table 1). Figure 2f shows the Au3+ extraction capacity of MoS2 and TiS2 NSs at room temperature as a function of equilibrium concentration. The extraction capacity increases sharply with rising Au3+ equilibrium concentration, reaching maximum capacity at very low equilibrium concentration (~0.5 ppm). The maximum extraction capacity is 9093 mg g−1 for TiS2, and 7100 mg g−1 for MoS2 at room temperature (pH 6), and increases further at elevated temperatures (Supplementary Fig. 11), indicating temperature-enhanced chemisorption of Au3+. Compared with state‑of‑the‑art materials, TiS2 NSs exhibit a remarkable capacity at low concentration of Au3+ (30 ppm) (Fig. 2g) and achieve 99.99% extraction within 0.25 min (Fig. 2h, Supplementary Table 1), surpassing reported adsorbents in extraction kinetics. WS2 and TaS2 also demonstrate outstanding selectivity (Kd > 108 mL g−1, Supplementary Fig. 12a–d), fast kinetics (1 min to achieve 99.99% extraction, Supplementary Fig. 12e), and high capacity (3662 mg g−1, 4320 mg g−1, Supplementary Fig. 12f) for gold extraction. To investigate the phase effect on gold extraction, we prepared 2H-MoS2 (Methods). Compared with 1T′-MoS2, 2H-MoS2 exhibits a slightly lower capacity (6207 mg g−1, Supplementary Fig. 13a) and slower kinetics (1 min to attain 99.60% extraction, Supplementary Fig. 13b). This difference is attributed to the lower charge density and weaker affinity of the 2H phase for Au3+ adsorption and reduction (Supplementary Fig. 14)25. Nevertheless, 2H-MoS2 can still reduce Au3+ because its Fermi level remains higher than the reduction potential of the [AuCl4]−/Au redox couple34, consistent with previous reports26,35. Additionally, these TMD NSs also exhibit high extraction capacity for other precious metal (like silver ion-Ag+), with a capacity of 6639 mg g−1 for TiS2, 4385 mg g−1 for MoS2, 3513 mg g−1 for TaS2, and 2757 mg g−1 for WS2 (Supplementary Fig. 15). They also achieve extraction efficiencies over 99.9% for Ag+ in just 1 min and demonstrate high selectivity against other cations (Supplementary Figs. 16–24). The high extraction performance of TMDs for Ag+—in terms of capacity, kinetics, and selectivity—is attributed to their strong affinity for Ag+ and the fact that its reduction potential lies below the Fermi level of TMDs, as confirmed by the theoretical calculation section.

Gold extraction mechanism

To elucidate the extraction (adsorption) mechanism, transmission electron microscope (TEM), X-ray Diffraction (XRD), and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) were conducted. TEM images (Fig. 3a, d) show abundant Au nanoparticles (NPs) densely loaded on the surfaces of MoS2 and TiS2 NSs after adsorption in a solution with 30 ppm Au3+. Notably, the loading density of Au NPs on TiS2 is higher (Fig. 3d) than that on MoS2 (Fig. 3a), consistent with the higher extraction capacity of TiS2 than MoS2 (Fig. 2g). The high-resolution TEM images reveal Au (111) lattice fringes with a spacing of 0.23 nm on MoS2 and TiS2 NSs (Fig. 3b, e)36. The loaded Au NPs exhibit sizes of ~3, 11, 5, and 8 nm on MoS2, WS2, TaS2, and TiS2 NSs, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 25). Besides, the lattice spacing of 0.27 nm corresponding to (100) planes of MoS2 are also identified in the MoS2-Au sample (Fig. 3b). No obvious lattice fringes of TiS2 (Fig. 3e) are detected, suggesting that TiS2 becomes amorphous after Au3+ adsorption. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping were performed on MoS2-Au and TiS2-Au (Fig. 3c, f), where high-density brighter nanoparticles are identified as Au NPs. Moreover, the XRD patterns display distinct peaks corresponding to Au (111), Au (200), Au (220), as well as TiS2 (001) and MoS2 (002) in the TiS2–Au and MoS2–Au samples (Fig. 3g, Supplementary Fig. 26), confirming the high loading amount of Au NPs on both MoS2 and TiS2 NSs after Au3+ adsorption. These results directly demonstrate that MoS2 and TiS2 NSs can adsorb the Au3+ and in situ reduce them to the Au NPs on their surface. To further elucidate the interaction between Au3+ and TMDs, XPS were conducted both on pristine KAuCl4 and Au NPs on TMDs. The emergence of a strong peak in the binding energy range of 84-88 eV is attributed to Au 4f (Supplementary Fig. 27), confirming the successful adsorption of Au3+ by MoS2 and TiS2 NSs. In the high-resolution Au 4f spectra (Fig. 3h), the Au 4f7/2 and 4f5/2 peaks in MoS2-Au and TiS2-Au shift to lower binding energies (~84.3 and 88.0 eV, respectively, corresponding to Au0)24 compared to pristine KAuCl4 (~87.1 and 90.8 eV, respectively). These results reveal that the adsorbed Au3+ on TMD NSs are reduced to metallic Au, consistent with the TEM observations (Fig. 3b, e) and XRD results (Fig. 3g). Concurrently, the Mo 3d5/2–3/2 (Fig. 3i) and Ti 2p3/2–1/2 (Fig. 3j) peaks shift to higher binding energies, accompanied by the emergence of strong MoO3 and TiO2 features and the near disappearance of MoS2 and TiS2 signals, indicating near-complete oxidation of the TMD NSs after saturated Au3+ adsorption35. These observations collectively indicate a redox process between the TMDs (MoS2, TiS2) and Au3+, in which the TMDs reduce Au3+ to Au0 while being oxidized to transition metal oxides (Eqs. 1–2). This mechanism is further supported by XRD: the characteristic (002) and (001) reflections of MoS2 and TiS2 (Supplementary Fig. 28a) disappear after adsorption, leaving only diffraction peaks of metallic Au (Supplementary Fig. 28b). The absence of MoO3 peaks is attributed to its amorphous nature37. Based on the redox stoichiometry shown in Eqs. 1–2, the theoretical adsorption capacities of MoS2 and TiS2 are calculated to be 7384 mg g−1 and 9381 mg g−1, respectively. However, the experimentally measured capacities are 7100 mg g−1 for MoS2 and 9093 mg g−1 for TiS2, which are lower than the theoretical values. This discrepancy may be attributed to the presence of vacancies (transition metal or sulfur) or partial oxidation of the exfoliated TMD NSs29. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations

To further elucidate the adsorption mechanism, DFT calculations were performed. The electronic structures of MoS2 and TiS2 NSs were first compared using projected density of states (PDOS) analysis. The d orbitals of Mo and Ti exhibit good overlap with the S 3p orbitals, indicating the structural stability of MoS2 and TiS2 NSs during Au3+ adsorption (Fig. 4a, b). Notably, 1T′-MoS2 displays nearly metallic properties with high conductivity, accelerating the electron transfer towards the Au3+ (Fig. 4a). Meanwhile, the 1T-TiS2 shows higher electron density near the Fermi level (EF) (Fig. 4b), indicating the more effective electron transfer to adsorbed Au3+ and thus accelerating the reduction processes38. Compare the PDOSs of Mo-4d orbitals in MoS2 and Ti-3d orbitals in TiS2 before and after Au3+ adsorption (Supplementary Fig. 32a, b), where a distinct upshift of the d orbitals is observed. This upshift indicates electron transfer to Au3+ and corresponds to changes in the transition metal valence states as characterized by XPS results (Fig. 3h–j). In addition, we analyzed the Au-5d orbitals in AuCl4−, where the main peaks are located near EV of −3.0 eV, displaying a good overlap with S-3p orbitals (Supplementary Fig. 32c, d). Then, we propose the reduction mechanism for Au3+. The surface S sites play a critical role in stabilizing the Au3+ through the p-d orbital coupling. Then, the transition metals of TMDs effectively transfer the electrons to the surface S sites and then to the adsorbed Au3+ ions, leading to the fast reduction of Au3+. Therefore, the reduction process of the Au3+ is accomplished through the d-p-d orbital coupling in TMDs based on the surface S-bridge to effectively transfer electrons. Our electrochemically exfoliated TMD NSs exhibit a high yield of mono- and bilayers, along with excellent dispersibility in deionized water. These attributes allow full exposure of S-rich surface sites for Au3+ binding, thereby enabling excitingly rapid adsorption kinetics. Besides, we compared the adsorption energies of different metal ions on the MoS2 and TiS2 surfaces (Fig. 4c, d). Both the MoS2 and TiS2 NSs show the lowest adsorption energies toward Au3+ compared to the other metal cations, implying that Au3+ adsorption on TMDs is more favorable than competitive cations. Amongst, 1T-TiS2 exhibits the lower Au3+ adsorption energies, suggesting its stronger adsorption and higher extraction capability. Additionally, the corresponding adsorption of Ag+ is also very strong, which is only slightly weaker than that of Au3+ on TMDs. To further evaluate the reduction capacity of different TMDs for metal ions, we demonstrated the band alignment between the Fermi level of TMDs and the standard reduction potential of metal ions (Fig. 4e)39. For 1T′-MoS2 and 1T-TiS2, the Fermi level based on the absolute energy-level alignment is higher than the reduction potential of [AuCl4]−/Au and Ag+/Ag. This leads to the spontaneous electron transfer to extract Au and Ag ions with high selectivity, supporting the experimental results. The larger energy difference between the Fermi level of TMDs and the reduction potential of [AuCl4]-/Au guarantees the stronger extraction of gold, supplying the highest gold extraction capability of 1T-TiS2. Meanwhile, some other factors may also contribute to the gold extraction capability. For example, the high gold extraction of 1T-TiS2 is potentially benefited from the metallic-like nature and the highest d-band center, which supplies the electron conductivity to reduce Au ions (Supplementary Fig. 33). For 1T′-MoS2, the S-3p orbitals display the highest p-band center to the Fermi level, which provides more active sites for adsorbing Au ions. Accordingly, for the adsorption of Au3+ on the TMDs surface, we reveal that the stronger adsorption results in a shorter bond length and lower charge of Au (Fig. 4f). Owing to the dominant energy level differences, along with other contributing electronic structure factors, the gold extraction capability shows an order of 1T-TiS2 > 1T′-MoS2 > 1T-TaS2 > 1T′-WS2, which is consistent with the experimental results. Furthermore, we investigated the energy changes of Au3+ during its reduction process (from AuCl4− to Au0, Fig. 4g). For 1T-TiS2, and 1T′-MoS2, the maximum uphill energy barriers they encounter are 0.26, and 0.42 eV, respectively, implying that their Au3+ extraction capability follows the order of 1T-TiS2 > 1T′-MoS2, which is consistent with the calculated Fermi level (Fig. 4e). We also compared the energy that was consumed for increasing Au NP formation on TMD surfaces (Fig. 4h). With the increasing Au NP sizes, the TiS2 NSs show a smaller barrier than the MoS2 (0.51 eV <0.74 eV), and the formation energy of MoS2 becomes energetically unfavored at the size of 3 nm, which is consistent with the experimental characterizations of the loaded Au NPs (Supplementary Fig. 25). We further find that the calculated energy barriers correlate well with the experimentally observed Au NP sizes, with a higher barrier leading to smaller particle sizes (Supplementary Fig. 34). Specifically, the energy barriers follow the order MoS2 (0.74 eV) > TaS2 (0.55 eV) > TiS2 (0.51 eV) > WS2 (0.36 eV), corresponding to loaded Au NP sizes of ~3, 5, 8, and 11 nm, respectively. Note that, the calculated results for WS2 and TaS2 also support their high selectivity toward Au3+/Ag+ adsorption and reduction than other ions (Supplementary Figs. 35 and 36). Scalable preparation of TiS2/MoS2 and gold extraction from e-waste

To demonstrate scalable production, TiS2 and MoS2 NSs were selected due to their superior performance among the investigated TMDs. Taking TiS2 as an example, which was prepared in a large quantity (5 g in 30 L DI water) via soft pack battery fabricated using a roll-to-roll process (TiS2 as anode, LiFePO4 as cathode) (see Methods, Fig. 5a, b, and Supplementary Figs. 37–40 for details). Similarly, MoS2 NSs were produced on a large scale using the same method (see Methods and Supplementary Fig. 41). A 20 mL suspension of TiS2 NSs (3.2 mg) was then used to extract Au from 20 mL of diluted leachate obtained from discarded central processing units (CPUs) (Fig. 5c, see Methods and Supplementary Figs. 42–44 for leaching details). The leachate contains 0.072 ppm Au3+, 22.83 ppm Cu2+, 1.09 ppm Ni2+, 0.28 ppm Fe2+, 4.56 ppm Sn4+, and 0.092 ppm Al3+ (Supplementary Fig. 42, Supplementary Table 2). Remarkably, ~99.90% of Au3+ was extracted by TiS2 NSs within 1 min (Supplementary Fig. 45), demonstrating the ultrafast and complete adsorption of Au3+ from the e-waste leaching solution. This performance was further validated in undiluted leachate, where TiS2 achieved a 99.67% gold extraction efficiency even in the presence of high concentrations (hundreds of ppm) of coexisting cations (Supplementary Table 3). Notably, the high concentration of Cu2+ has a negligible impact on the gold extraction performance of TiS2. This is because the adsorbed copper predominantly forms Cu–S species (Supplementary Fig. 46), which can be readily removed by calcination in air followed by acid washing, as discussed in the next section. For practical application, we fabricated 10 TiS2-based adsorptive membranes (5 cm in diameter, Supplementary Figs. 47 and 48) via a vacuum filtration method and used them for continuous gold extraction from e-waste (Fig. 5d) (see Methods for details). The membrane exhibits an extraction efficiency of 99.9% for Au3+ from e-waste with a high permeance of 75–150 L m−2 h−1 bar−1 (Fig. 5e). After adsorptive filtration, the surface of the TiS2 membrane became densely covered by Au NPs (Fig. 5f), which was confirmed by SEM cross-section image, the corresponding EDS mapping (Supplementary Figs. 49 and 50), and results from XRD (Supplementary Fig. 51). Similar Au extraction and deposition phenomena were also observed for other TMD (MoS2, WS2 and TaS2) membranes (Supplementary Figs. 52 and 53). The TMD membranes can be regenerated after extraction using thiourea and HCl, where thiourea acts as both a reducing agent and a sulfur source to restore oxidized and sulfur-deficient TMDs, while hydrochloric acid provides an acidic environment that promotes thiourea decomposition and the repair process26. Notably, the MoS2 membrane shows better regeneration performance than TiS2, retaining ~90% extraction efficiency after five adsorption–desorption cycles (Supplementary Figs. 54–60). After Au extraction by the TiS2 membrane, the material was calcined in air (800 °C, 2 h; Fig. 5g) to convert co-adsorbed ions into metal oxides, followed by hydrochloric acid dissolution and water washing to remove them. Owing to its high chemical stability, Au remains unchanged throughout the process. Finally, high-purity gold (23.8 Karat) was collected, as confirmed by ICP (Supplementary Table 4) and XPS analysis (Supplementary Fig. 61), XRD (Fig. 5h), and SEM results (Supplementary Figs. 62 and 63). In total, 0.15 g of gold was recycled from 30 CPUs. Similarly, silver extraction with efficiency up to 99.9% by filtration of the Ag+ solution (2 ppm) through the TiS2 membrane was also achieved (Supplementary Figs. 64–66). Note that the TiS2 membrane with a diameter up to 10 cm can be easily fabricated via scaling up the mold (Supplementary Fig. 67), which hold great promise for practical e-waste extraction.

Techno-economic analysis

To evaluate the economic feasibility of the TMDs-based adsorption method for recovering gold from e-waste leachate, a comprehensive technoeconomic analysis (TEA) was conducted, focusing on the specific material flow involved in gold recovery using MoS2. The determination of technological parameters is thoroughly discussed in Supplementary Method, Supplementary Fig. 68, and Supplementary Tables 5–14. A detailed cost breakdown is shown in Fig. 6a, which includes the processes of CPU waste leaching, sorbent production, gold recovery, and purification. The Sankey diagram (Fig. 6a) reveals that the largest portion of material costs stems from CPU acquisition, accounting for 77.35%, followed by labor costs at 8.33%. Owing to the simple and cost-effective synthesis route for MoS2, the total capital and material costs for adsorbent production represent only 6.98% of the overall expenses. According to the TEA results, the projected annual income is estimated at US$86,600 kg−1 Au (Fig. 6b), substantially exceeding the total annual input cost of US$7,090 kg−1 Au, yielding a remarkable input-output ratio of 1221%. Notably, our cost is significantly lower than other reported studies (US$87,000 kg−1 Au)14. Together, these findings highlight the strong economic potential of MoS2-based adsorption for gold recovery from e-waste. As shown in the sensitivity analysis (Fig. 6c), the economic efficiency of this method could be further improved by reducing labor costs and scaling up production. Considering that TaS2 is the most expensive among the four TMDs (TaS2: 27,510 $/kg, MoS2: 138 $/kg), we further conducted a TEA based on TaS2. Despite its high material cost, TaS2 still demonstrates strong economic feasibility. In summary, we developed a family of 2D TMD materials (MoS2, WS2, TaS2, and TiS2) for gold recovery from mixed ionic solutions. Among them, TiS2 exhibits an adsorption capacity of 9093 mg g−1 towards Au3+ and achieves 99.99% extraction within 0.25 min. These 2D TMDs show a strong preference for Au3+ over competing cations, with Kd values that are 5–6 orders of magnitude higher than those of competing ions. The practical applicability of this approach was demonstrated by recovering 0.15 g of gold (23.8 Karat) from 30 discarded CPUs, highlighting the potential of 2D TMDs for precious-metal recovery from electronic waste. Beyond its practical relevance, this work provides insights into the special interactions between noble-metal ions and 2D TMDs, offering guidance for the rational design of next-generation materials for selective metal recovery and resource recycling.

Methods

Materials

All chemicals were used without further purification unless otherwise specified. Molybdenum disulfide (MoS2, 99.5%), Tungsten disulfide (WS2, 99.9%), Tantalum disulfide (TaS2, 99.9%), Titanium disulfide (TiS2, 99.9%), and LiFePO4 (99.8%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Lithium foils and the electrolyte, consisting of 1 M LiPF6 dissolved in ethyl carbonate (EC), dimethyl carbonate (DMC), and ethyl methyl carbonate (EMC) in a 1:1:1 volume ratio, were obtained from DodoChem. Super-P was acquired from Bay Carbon Inc., and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) was sourced from Sigma-Aldrich. Polypropylene (PP) film (Celgard 2300), N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP, 99%), sodium chloride (NaCl, 99.5%), potassium chloride (KCl, 99.5%), magnesium chloride hexahydrate (MgCl2·6H2O, 99.8%), calcium chloride (CaCl2, 96%), nickel chloride hexahydrate (NiCl2·6H2O, 99.9%), zinc chloride (ZnCl2, 98%), copper chloride dihydrate (CuCl2·2H2O, 99.7%), potassium tetrachloroaurate (KAuCl4, 98%), silver nitrate (AgNO3, 99%), hydrochloric acid (HCl, 37%), and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, 30%) were used in the experiments. Deionized water was produced using a Milli-Q CLX 7040 Water Purification System.

Preparation of 2D TMD NSs (MoS2, WS2, TaS2, and TiS2)

1T′-MoS2, 1T′-WS2, 1T-TaS2, and 1T-TiS2 NSs were synthesized using a battery intercalation-based exfoliation method (Supplementary Fig. 3)40–42. Electrochemical lithium intercalation was achieved in a coin cell via applying a bulk TMDs coated collector as the cathode, lithium foil as the anode, and 1 M LiPF6 dissolved in a solution of EC, DMC, and EMC in a 1:1:1 volume ratio as the electrolyte. Galvanostatic discharge was employed to facilitate the lithium intercalation into the interlayer of bulk TMDs (4 mg). The current density and cutoff voltage were set as follows: 0.0125 A g−1 and 0.9 V for MoS2; 0.00625 A g−1 and 0.9 V for TaS2 and TiS2; 0.02 A g−1 and 0.7 V for WS2. This intercalation process was followed by sonication of the lithiated TMDs sample in deionized (DI) water to complete the exfoliation process (5–10 min). The obtained TMD NSs were purified through two rounds of centrifugation: first at 208 × g for 15 min to collect the upper supernatant, followed by centrifugation at 762 × g for 20 min to obtain the sediment at the bottom. The sediment was then redispersed in DI water, and the centrifugation process was repeated once more. 2H-MoS2 NSs were prepared by heating a solution of 1T′-MoS2 NSs at 100 °C for 10 h.

Scalable preparation of TiS2 and MoS2 NSs via TiS2 (MoS2)/LiFePO4 soft pack batteries

TiS2//LiFePO4 soft pack batteries were fabricated using a commercial processing system. Initially, electrode slurries were prepared by mixing and stirring LiFePO4 or TiS2 powders, super-P, and polyvinylidene fluoride dissolved in N-methyl pyrrolidone (0.062 g g−1) for 12 h, with a mass ratio of 95 % for LiFePO4 and 80 % for TiS2. The cathode (LiFePO4) and anode (TiS2) slurries were then coated onto one side of aluminum foil via a roll-to-roll process. The mass loadings of active materials (LiFePO4 or TiS2) were controlled by the gap distance between the blade and foils during this process. The resulting electrodes were dried in a vacuum at 100 °C for 12 h, achieving mass loadings of 12.5 mg cm−2 for LiFePO4 and 8.4 mg cm-2 for TiS2. Subsequently, the dried electrode films were punched into sheets with a size of 5 cm × 11 cm. These LiFePO4 cathode and TiS2 anode sheets were alternately stacked with Celgard membranes as separators to assemble the soft pack batteries. 10 cathode and 10 anode sheets were used in one soft pack battery. The tabs of the overlapped electrodes were welded to external metal tabs (aluminum for cathodes and nickel for anodes). After injecting the electrolyte (1 M LiPF6 in a mixture of ethyl carbonate, dimethyl carbonate, and ethyl methyl carbonate in a 1:1:1 volume ratio) and heat-sealing the aluminum covers, the TiS2//LiFePO4 soft pack batteries were completed. One soft pack battery contained ~5 g TiS2. The soft pack battery was then slowly charged at 0.003 A g−1 to 1.9 V (Supplementary Fig. 39) to facilitate lithium intercalation of TiS2. After that, the lithiated anodes were placed in DI water and sonicated to fully exfoliate the bulk TiS2 into 2D NSs. Similarly, the scalable preparation of MoS2 NSs followed the same procedure, replacing bulk TiS2 with bulk MoS2 powder. The MoS2//LiFePO4 soft-pack battery was charged at 0.0125 A g−1 to 2.8 V (Supplementary Fig. 41), and the resulting lithiated MoS2 was exfoliated in DI water to yield the final MoS2 NSs.

Preparation of the TMD membranes

The MoS2, WS2, TaS2, and TiS2 membranes were prepared by vacuum filtration of their respective NSs solution onto a porous polymer substrate (PVDF, 220 nm pore size, 5 cm or 10 cm in diameter). The thickness of the resulting membrane can be controlled by adjusting the quantity of filtered TMD NSs43–46. A larger-sized membrane (10 cm in diameter) can be easily fabricated through scaling up the vacuum filtration equipment (Supplementary Fig. 67).

Selective adsorption of Au3+

Batch experiments were conducted to assess the selective adsorption of Au3+ by MoS2, WS2, TaS2, and TiS2 NSs. A mixed solution containing Au3+ and seven competitive metal cations (Cu2+, Ni2+, Zn2+, K+, Na+, Ca2+, and Mg2+) was prepared. All the competitive cations were in the form of chloride salts to prevent potential interference from anions. The exfoliated TMD NSs (MoS2, WS2, TaS2, and TiS2) were mixed with the prepared solution at a concentration of 30 ppm for each metal cation, which were subsequently stirred for 12 h. The resulting suspension was filtered through a 220 nm PVDF syringe filter, and the filtrate was subsequently analyzed by ICP–OES or ICP–MS to quantify the residual metal ion concentration. Au3+ adsorption kinetics

The procedure involved mixing equal volumes of a TMD NSs suspension and an Au3+ solution (60 ppm) for varying durations (0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1, 2, 4, and 8 min). Following each duration, the mixture underwent filtration, and the resulting filtrate was collected for subsequent analysis using ICP-MS to ascertain the remaining concentration of Au3+. The stability of the TMDs during prolonged adsorption (12 h) is confirmed, as the Au3+ extraction efficiency remains nearly unchanged after 12 h of adsorption (Supplementary Fig. 71).

Gold extraction from e-waste

A discarded CPU, obtained from computer waste, underwent a gold leaching process. Initially, it was immersed in an 8 M NaOH solution for 96 h to remove the protective coating from the electronic surfaces. Subsequently, the CPU was thoroughly rinsed and soaked in aqua regia for 24 h. The leaching solution underwent a rapid color change from clear to a greenish hue within a few hours. After that, the leaching solution underwent filtration to remove any undissolved material and was then diluted for further elemental analysis and extraction experiments (see Supplementary Fig. 43). After determining the concentrations of competitive metal ions and Au3+ using ICP-OES, a solution containing TMD NSs was introduced into the leaching solution. The mixture was stirred for 1 min. Subsequently, the concentration of remaining cations was determined using ICP-MS. All experimental procedures were conducted at room temperature.

Gold extraction via filtration-through the membrane

The experiment was conducted using pressure-assisted filtration. For the scale-up recycling process, 30 CPUs were soaked in 200 mL of aqua regia for 24 h. The resulting leachate was collected by filtration and then diluted with DI water to a total volume of 5.5 L. Subsequently, the solution pH was adjusted to 6 using NaOH. The leachate was diluted and its pH controlled to minimize acid-induced corrosion of the inner wall of the stainless steel pressure cell (HP4750) during filtration, which could otherwise damage the cell and release metal ions that may interfere with adsorption performance. To verify that no corrosion occurs under these conditions, the leachate was soaked in the filtration cell without a TMDs membrane for the same duration. The elemental concentrations remained unchanged (Supplementary Table 15), confirming negligible metal leaching (e.g., Fe) from the cell. The recycling procedure for a single CPU followed the same protocol as the scaled-up process. A TiS2 membrane was placed into a dead-end pressure cell (Fig. 5d). The cell chamber was filled with 550 mL of diluted leaching solution, and 10 TiS2 membranes consumed 5.5 L of leachate. Applying 1 bar pressure using N2 gas from the top side facilitated the gradual penetration of the leaching solution through the membrane, completing the filtration in approximately 3 h. Periodically, the filtrate was collected and subjected to ICP-MS analysis to determine the extraction efficiency. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 72a, c, after 6 h of immersion in undiluted acid leachate, the MoS2 membrane exhibits minimal surface oxidation, while the TiS2 membrane shows more surface oxidation. Calculation setup

To investigate the gold extraction capability of different TMD materials, the DFT calculations were performed using the CASTEP package in Materials Studio 847. The generalized gradient approximation (GGA) and Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functionals were chosen in this work to supply accurate descriptions of the exchange-correlation interactions48–50. The cutoff energy was default set to 440 eV based on the ultrafine quality with the selection of the ultrasoft pseudopotentials. For all the energy minimization processes, the k-point setting has been set to coarse quality with the selection of the Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shannon (BFGS) algorithm51. In this work, the four types of TMD 1T′-MoS2, 1T′-WS2, 1T-TaS2, and 1T-TiS2 surfaces are constructed based on (001) surfaces with a single layer in 6 × 6 × 1 supercells. For all the geometry optimizations, we have applied the following convergence criteria for geometry optimizations, including: 1) Hellmann-Feynman forces should not exceed 0.001 eV/Å, and 2) the total energy difference should not be over 5 × 10−5 eV/atom.
