Executive Summary
The concept of fuel debundling proposes that all non-fuel components of a fuel assembly be removed
prior to beginning the recycling process. It was introduced to reduce the quantity of and cost of disposing
high level radioactive waste during recycling in the United States. However, nuclear material accountancy
and control programs could also benefit from fuel debundling due to a reduction in self-shielding material
for individual fuel rods compared to a fuel assembly. Current NRC regulations and guidance point to an
independent fuel debundling facility with a material-possession-only license as the best option for
implementing this concept into a used fuel recycling scheme in the United States. This allows the facility
to comply only with simple NMAC requirements under Subpart B of 10 CFR Part 74 without the need to
submit an NMAC plan to the NRC, exempting it from more tedious and complex NMAC requirements.
However, a recycling facility may desire a Pu mass estimate from measurements of each individual fuel
rod to improve accountancy into the dissolver. Many technologies and methodologies were considered for
performing NMAC in an independent fuel debundling facility in the United States. Few were able to meet
anticipated throughput demands of up to 3,300 rods/day and achieve measurement uncertainties suitable
for input accountancy for a recycling facility. The best option was a combination of passive gamma-ray
spectroscopy and neutron coincidence counting to meet all requirements for the proposed Fuel
Debundling Detector (FUDD). Follow-on work will include a full uncertainty analysis to inform what
assumptions can be made to meet input accountancy objectives before beginning experimental activities.

 Introduction
Evaluation of the spent nuclear fuel recycling process by the United States Department of Energy (US
DOE), Office of Nuclear Energy NE-43 Office of Materials and Chemical Technologies has led to a
recommendation to remove all non-fueled, stainless-steel components from the waste stream to reduce the
cost of disposal. In the chop-and-leach process, fuel assemblies are chopped up and subjected to the
leaching process, resulting in all parts being disposed of as high-level radioactive waste (HLW).
Separating the stainless-steel structural components from a fuel assembly prior to chopping allows these
parts to be disposed of as low-level radioactive waste (LLW) instead of HLW. Reducing the volume of
HLW generated from the recycling process has been calculated to save millions of dollars on repository
costs.

The fuel rods can be separated from the non-fuel components of the fuel assembly using technology that
was developed for consolidation to minimize storage requirements for spent fuel. That process was done
at a demonstration level at Idaho National Laboratory (INL) pulling single rods in a dry hot cell and a
small-scale multi-rod underwater operational test at Millstone Nuclear Power Plant in the mid to late
1980’s. In those projects, the fuel rods removed from two assemblies were consolidated into one canister
with the same dimensions as one original assembly, resulting in a 2:1 volume reduction 6-34[1].

The Materials Protection Accounting and Control Technologies (MPACT) organization is engaged in
evaluating measurement of separated rods compared to bulk assemblies for nuclear material accountancy
and control (NMAC) purposes. A facility’s NMAC program ensures the safe and secure use of nuclear
materials and that the facility has implemented and maintained a system of material control and
accounting measures that can prevent, deter, or detect loss, theft, diversion, or misuse of nuclear
materials. Fuel debundling offers several advantages for an NMAC program in addition to cost-saving
measures for waste disposal. One is that a full fuel assembly has significant self-shielding, which makes
neutron and gamma-ray techniques less sensitive to removal of inner fuel pins when measuring from the
outside of the bundle. A second effect is the helpful reduction in gamma dose rates and heat by up to 200
times, which reduces detector exposure. Lastly, the reduced mass of a fuel rod allows for more accurate
values of heavy metal input relative to the complete assembly.

This report documents the initial effort to develop an NMAC framework for fuel debundling in the United
States. Applicable regulations established by the Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) were first
explored to determine a viable path forward. Throughput parameters were also defined prior to examining
all possible options for performing NMAC on a single fuel rod. Ultimately, a couple of technologies were
put forth as practical options for meeting NMAC needs. Each of these steps is detailed in the following
sections.

 Nuclear Regulatory Commission Regulations
The NRC is the governing body in the United States designated by Congress to regulate commercial
nuclear power plants and other uses of nuclear material through licensing, inspection, and enforcement.
Title 10, “Energy”, of the Code of Federal Regulations (10 CFR) contains all rules established by the
NRC.

 10 CFR 74—Material Control and Account of Special Nuclear Material

To possess and utilize special nuclear material (SNM), an applicant must develop and demonstrate
programs and processes for handling and accounting for SNM. The primary focus of this was 10 CFR
Part 74, “Material Control and Accounting of Special Nuclear Material”, which establishes regulatory
requirements for NMAC programs in facilities with SNM [2]. References are also made to 10 CFR Part
70, “Domestic Licensing of Special Nuclear Material”, and 10 CFR Part 50, “Domestic Licensing of
Production and Utilization Facilities”, for determining the best regulatory path for a fuel debundling
facility [3][4]. A facility for fuel debundling would need to be licensed and operated according to the
applicable parts of these regulations for possession of SNM. Therefore, the type and quantity of SNM
(e.g., fresh fuel or irradiated fuel), the chemical form of SNM, and the onsite processing of SNM should
be considered when developing the NMAC program for the debundling facility.

10 CFR Part 74 contains requirements to ensure the safe and secure handling of SNM at fixed sites and
during transfers. It is subdivided into six parts labeled A through F, as shown in Figure 1. Subparts A, B,
and F are applicable to all facilities containing SNM, but subparts C, D, and E are applicable only to
facilities that fall within certain requirements for SNM.

Irradiated fuel, which, by its original fissile material content, is included as Category I or II before

limits for Category II materials, a reprocessing or recycling facility may be categorized as a Category I
facility in the future. The NRC neither pursues any current rulemaking nor provides additional
regulations and guidance to address the specific NMAC aspects for recycling facilities under the Category
I criteria. The regulations exclude reprocessing from Category I requirements in 10 CFR 74.51(a) when
Subpart E of Part 74 was issued in March 1987. An NMAC rulemaking, initiated in 2008, proposed
removing that exclusion from Category I regulations to properly recategorize reprocessing as Category I.
Nevertheless, that NMAC rulemaking was terminated via SRM-SECY-18-0104 in 2018 [5]. In addition,
the NRC discontinued the spent fuel recycling rulemaking via SECY-21-0026 in March 2021 [6]. These
regulatory impacts will place uncertainties on any recycling applicant implementing adequate regulatory

 Reporting Requirements

The regulations in 10 CFR 70.22(b) require in part that each application for a license to possess and use
SNM in a quantity exceeding one effective kilogram to contain a full description of the program for
control and accounting of such SNM. However, this part of the regulation also excludes licensees
governed by 10 CFR Part 50, which includes nuclear power reactors and nonpower reactors, for uses of
SNM as sealed sources, and operations involved in waste disposal. The same exclusions are contained in
the NMAC requirements under Subparts C, D, and E of 10 CFR Part 74. By default, a debundling facility
could fall into this exclusion with respect to the licensing process.

In addition, the regulations in 10 CFR 74.2, in part, require all licensees that possess, receive, or transfer
SNM in any form in a quantity of one gram or more containing 235U, 233U, or Pu to be subject to the
general reporting and recordkeeping of 10 CFR Part 74, Subpart B. The requirements of Subpart B are
summarized as follows:
 • 10 CFR Part 74.11, “Reports of loss or theft or attempted theft or unauthorized production of
 special nuclear material,” requires, in part, that licensees notify the NRC of any such events
 within one hour of discovery.
 • 10 CFR Part 74.13, “Material status reports,” requires licensees to submit material status reports
 for certain quantities of SNM.
 • 10 CFR Part 74.15, “Nuclear material transaction reports,” requires licensees to complete
 transaction reports when transferring, receiving, or adjusting specified quantities of SNM.
 • 10 CFR Part 74.19, “Recordkeeping,” requires that licensees maintain and retain records of the
 receipt, inventory, acquisition, transfer, and disposal of all SNM in their possession. This part
 also requires that licensees establish, maintain, and follow written NMAC procedures and
 conduct physical inventories of all SNM at intervals not to exceed once every twelve months.

The NMAC regulatory differences from Category II to Category I fundamentally affect various aspects of
the facility’s overall NMAC program. The gaps between Category I and Category II reprocessing
facilities in regulation and guidance consequently pose additional regulatory challenges for any license
application following anticipated reprocessing operations to comply with the NRC requirements. A
modification to the facility’s license application may be needed, addressing technical challenges and
modifying the facility’s NMAC program to demonstrate compliance with existing NMAC criteria to
implement reprocessing operations associated with both Category I and II materials.

Existing predefined regulatory limits in Subparts D and E of 10 CFR Part 74 may pose challenges for a
material accounting management program applied to reprocessing facilities due to commercial-scale large
material throughputs, measurement uncertainty thresholds, inventory frequencies, inventory difference
limits on the permissible significant uranium and plutonium inventory goal quantities, material holdup in
process equipment, waste incidental for in situ disposal, and accounting for the many recycle streams in a
highly integrated reprocessing facility. Another consideration discussed in this report remains the
availability, applicability, and limitations of different measurement methods and techniques within the
debundling operation and expanding to the entire reprocessing processes to obtain the desired precision
and accuracy of measured values associated with measurement uncertainties and bias corrections in
meeting the required measurement control program.

 Regulatory Recommendations for a Fuel Debundling Facility

Given the limitations of NMAC regulatory requirements, the licensing process presents a challenge
because the NRC’s NMAC regulations have not been updated to accommodate reprocessing and
recycling operations. Since there is no reprocessing facility or debundling facility that has been
previously licensed, no guidance or precedence exists for such a facility’s NMAC program. Due to the
uncertainty on whether a fuel debundling facility would fall under Category I or II facility, the necessary
approach is first to present the general reporting and recordkeeping requirements of Subpart B in 10 CFR
Part 74, then highlight the similarities and differences between NMAC requirements for Category I and
Category II facilities under Subparts D and E, and finally put forward approaches suitable for a fuel
debundling facility.

The debundling operation is assumed to remove the spent fuel rods from the fuel assemblies at the first
operation of a reprocessing facility before processing by the subsequent cladding dissolution and
recycling operations. Consequently, performance-based NMAC requirements over the existing
requirements in Category II fuel cycle facilities should be considered to ensure that Category II
reprocessing facilities produce only enriched uranium of moderate strategic significance or Category II
material. In addition, a befitting NMAC approach should be considered, aiming to establish suitability
and compatibility for meeting the performance-based NMAC requirements for a Category II reprocessing
facility in the context of adapting the current NRC regulations and guidance for Category II fuel cycle and
then modifying and expanding them to suit the needs for a Category I facility. This exigency should be
considered when the recycling operations exceed Category II quantity limits. NMAC requirements for
the Category I fuel cycle should then be considered and applied if the total quantities of plutonium and
uranium exceed the prescribed limits for Category II. This places more stringent requirements on the
facility’s overall NMAC program, which is evaluated for material production in the appropriate Category
II level range.

However, except for damaged fuel rods during the debundling operation, individual fuel rods would be
considered sealed sources when separating them from the fuel assemblies. Under 10 CFR 74.4, sealed
source means any special nuclear material that is physically encased in a capsule, rod, element, etc., that
prevents the leakage or escape of the special nuclear material and that prevents removal of the special
nuclear material without penetration of the casing. Thus, this debundling operation can be alleviated as an
item-only facility, complying only with simple NMAC requirements under Subpart B of 10 CFR Part 74
without the need to submit a facility’s NMAC plan to the NRC and being exempted from more tedious
and complex NMAC requirements under 10 CFR 74.41(a) of Subparts D and 10 CFR 74.51(a) of Subpart
E for Category II and I facilities, which in turn require an NRC-approved FNMC Plan or facility’s
NMAC Plan.

Considering the applicable regulations for a general reprocessing operation facility and a simplified fuel
debundling operation facility, two facility concepts deserve possible consideration during the design of
recycling or reprocessing activities:

 Option 1 - A reference single facility where the separated fuel rods are directly introduced into the
 conventional cladding dissolution and recycling processes. The type of facility will be adopted based
 on the suitability and compatibility of Category I and Category II NMAC requirements regarding
 potential regulatory impacts, as discussed above.

Los Alamos National Laboratory Page 2-11

 Option 2 - Two facilities for a stand-alone fuel debundling facility separate from the reprocessing
 facility, taking advantage of the sealed source exemption status in applying NMAC requirements.
 The stand-alone fuel debundling facility will need to only comply with the basic NMAC requirements
 under Subpart B of 10 CFR Part 74 due to the exemption status from Subparts C, D, and E of 10 CFR
 Part 74. The type of facility can be licensed as a material-possession-only facility, permitting the
 storage of unbundled fuel rods before transferring to the next facility for starting the reprocessing
 processes.

The NMAC program for debundling is relatively straightforward given that the spent fuel assemblies are
intact when receiving, the fuel rods are still sealed during the debundling activities, and the spent fuel is
still contained within discrete fuel rods. Consequently, the most logical choice for a facility’s NMAC
program for a debundling facility would be Option 2 associated with a stand-alone facility and a material-
possession-only license.

 Debundling Facility Throughput
An independent fuel debundling facility will need to meet throughput needs of a recycling facility so that
it does not become a bottleneck in the process. Bounding the throughput needs for this facility in the
United States is challenging because such a facility does not exist nor is it under development. However,
a recycling facility in the United States would likely have throughput volumes on par with international
recycling facilities.

Existing recycling facilities—Rokkasho Reprocessing Plant (RRP) in Japan and Orano La Hague in
France—have maximum throughputs of 800 ton-U/y and 1,600 ton-U/y, respectively, which were used as
bounds for throughput in the United States [7][8]. The used fuel is expected to come from light water
reactors (LWR), namely pressurized water reactors (PWR) and boiling water reactors (BWR). The fuel
assemblies in each of these reactor types contain different numbers of fuel rods, resulting in an upper and
lower bound for the required number of fuel rods to meet throughput demands. The most common PWR
fuel is a 17x17 lattice of fuel rods, which contains about 450 kg-U per fuel assembly and 264 rods per
fuel assembly [9]. BWR fuel is typically arranged in a 10x10 lattice containing about 109 kg-U per fuel
assembly and 91 rods per fuel assembly [9]. Combining the annual throughput with the typical parameters
for PWR and BWR fuel assemblies resulted in a calculated fuel rod throughput of 1,200-3,300 rods per
day in an independent debundling facility.

Measurement times for an NMAC program in the fuel debundling facility must also be short enough to
meet the daily fuel rod throughput. Assuming 24/7 operations at the facility, this translates to
measurement times of about 30-60 s/rod, accounting for about 10 s/rod to enter and exit the detection
system. However, this assumes that the facility would only have one measurement system available,
whereas it is more typical at a full-scale facility to have multiple stations available. Not only does this
prevent a single point of failure in the system, but it also increases the time for measuring each rod by as
many factors as systems are available. For example, if a facility has three systems, then the measurement
time can be increased by a factor of three to 90-180 s/rod. Additionally, the operation of the debundling
effort as an independent facility allows for loose rod inventory to accumulate in a campaign strategy,
reducing or eliminating the time sensitivity of the debundling rate to recycling facility demand. Adopting
this type of strategy could also result in longer measurements per rod.

The minimum time of 30-s for each fuel rod is long enough to acquire gross defect measurements to
verify the presence of used fuel in each rod. Gross defect measurements are satisfactory to meet NRC
requirements for a material-possession-only license. However, the operator of the recycling facility may
desire a quantitative measurement with low uncertainty to understand how much material is going into the
input accountancy tank. Suitable measurement options were explored to meet both criteria.

 Measurement Options for Characterizing Used Fuel Rods
A multitude of options are available in the modern day to assess used fuel characteristics for gross defect
and quantitative measurements. The primary options for non-destructive assay (NDA) of used fuel are
gamma-ray and neutron detectors, but a range of other technologies and methodologies were explored to
determine if they are more suitable for characterizing individual fuel rods than traditional NDA
techniques.

 Gamma-Ray Detectors

Active and passive gamma-ray spectroscopy has long been used as a technique to assay both fresh and
spent nuclear fuel. Active gamma-ray spectroscopy methods employ an external neutron source to induce
fission in the fuel so that the resulting gamma-ray signature can be used to determine enrichment. In
contrast, passive gamma-ray spectroscopy methods use the inherent gamma radiation emitted from the
fuel assembly to estimate spent fuel characteristics, such as burn-up and cooling time. Application of
these methods to individual fuel rods are explored in this section. Although some of the techniques
described below were initially studied and developed to assay full fuel assemblies, the signatures in the
gamma-ray spectrum are not expected to change from a full fuel assembly to individual fuel rods. This
allows direct translation of the methods from one application to another.

 Active Gamma-Ray Spectroscopy

The active gamma spectroscopy methods examined consisted of neutron activation methods where the
fuel assembly or rod was irradiated with an external neutron source and the resulting fission product
gamma rays were used to determine the attributes of the fuel. An example of this is the High-Energy
Delayed Gamma Spectroscopy (HEDGS) system shown in Figure 2. Illustration of the High-energy
Delayed Gamma SpectroscopyFigure 2 6-34[10]. In this system, the fuel rod is irradiated with a
deuterium-tritium (DT) neutron generator, inducing fission in the remaining fissionable isotopes in the
fuel rod. Short-lived fission products decay by beta emission with accompanying gamma emission.
These gamma-rays are of different energies than those from the longer-lived fission products dominating
the spectra from spent fuel and tend to be of higher energies than those from longer-lived fission products.
These higher-energy gamma rays are very well-discriminated from the lower-energy gamma rays and in a
portion of the spectrum with a lower overall continuum count rate, providing lower uncertainties in the
peak areas and corresponding peak area ratios. Sample simulated spectra from such an assay are shown
in the Figure 3.

 Neutron Detectors

Neutron detectors have been commonly used to quantify fissile uranium and total plutonium in
unirradiated material forms like oxides, metals, etc., for decades [15]. For used fuel, many neutron
techniques have been applied to determine burnup or multiplication of assemblies [16]. Both quantities
(burnup and multiplication) are related to the fissile mass in a spent fuel assembly. However, current
international nuclear safeguards approaches do not require the determination of 235U or total plutonium in
spent fuel. To satisfy NMAC requirements for a facility that will recycle irradiated fuel, a different
approach must be explored to quantify important material streams.

This section explores the application of coincidence counting—traditionally used to measure the mass of
Pu in items—to single used fuel rods using both thermal neutron detectors and fast neutron detectors.

 Coincidence Counting for Used Fuel

Isotopes that undergo fission release multiple free neutrons on average. If two or more of these correlated
fission neutrons from a given fission event are detected, this is an indication of the presence of fissioning
isotopes. Furthermore, the rate of detection of the correlated neutrons is proportional to the quantity of
the fissioning material. This concept is the foundation of neutron coincidence counting.

The coincidence counting technique utilizes a time sequence analysis to quantify pairs of neutrons
detected within a set time window. Correlated neutron pairs, or Reals (R), can be distinguished from
uncorrelated neutron pairs, or Accidentals (A), by keeping track of the time between detected neutrons.
Visually, this concept is observed on a Rossi-alpha distribution, shown in Figure 9. The Rossi-alpha
distribution is a histogram that sorts detected events into time bins based on the time between a pair of
detected neutrons. The distribution peaks near the time a neutron is detected (t=0) and then decays
exponentially when correlated fission neutrons are present. If no correlated neutrons are present, the
Rossi-alpha distribution becomes a flat distribution, where all time bins are equally probable, because the
neutron pairs are uncorrelated. For fissioning sources of neutrons, correlated and uncorrelated neutrons
always occur together, therefore, a time-correlation analysis method is needed to quantify the correlated
neutron pairs separately from the uncorrelated pairs. Effectively, the coincidence counting technique
integrates the Rossi-alpha distribution in two separate time domains and for a set amount of time called
the Gate Width (G). The early time after a neutron is detected is the Reals and Accidentals (R+A) count,
and the late or delayed time is the Accidentals (A) count. The Real coincidence rate is the difference
(R+A) – (A) divided by the count time. The Reals rate, also called the Doubles rate, is directly
proportional to the mass of fissioning isotopes in the sample. In the Rossi-alpha plot there is a period after
a neutron is detected where the detector system has a lower effective efficiency because a preamplifier is
busy processing a charge pulse. This is seen by the slight downturn in the counts near the axis at t=0. The
Pre-delay (P) is used to delay the onset of integration to avoid biasing the (R+A) tally due to the reduced
efficiency. The length of the long delay is set to ensure that neutrons in the (A) tally cannot be truly
coincident with the neutron that was detected at time t=0. The Gate Width is generally related to the
lifetime of neutrons in the detector system. For thermal systems, the moderation process takes on the

Los Alamos National Laboratory Page 4-19

order of tens of microseconds, therefore, any coincident gate that is used to determine the Doubles count
rate from a given fission event will also capture neutrons from other processes like (α,n) neutrons or
neutrons from other fission events. These Accidental coincidences only contribute to the uncertainty in
the Doubles rate. The higher the Accidental coincidence count rate, the higher the uncertainty in the
Doubles rate. Therefore, it is advisable to keep the Gate width as short as possible to avoid unnecessarily
increasing the Doubles rate uncertainty. For fast neutron detection systems there is no moderation needed
and the detection time after neutron “birth” is relatively quick, on the nanosecond time scale.

 Figure 9. Illustration of a Rossi-alpha distribution for coincidence counting

The main sources of neutrons from used fuel are (α,n) reactions and spontaneous fission (SF) of higher
actinides like curium and, to a lesser extent, plutonium. Neutrons from (α,n) reactions are emitted when
an α particle from the radioactive decay of transuranic isotopes in the fuel are absorbed by light elements
in the fuel, particularly 17O and 18O in the ceramic UO2 matrix. These neutrons are generated singly at
random and uniformly in time and only contribute to the total detected neutron rate, also called the
Singles.

Isotopes that undergo SF typically emit multiple neutrons per decay simultaneously. As described above,
neutrons detected close together in time contribute to Doubles, and all detected neutrons from fission also
contribute to Singles. Non-zero Doubles indicates the presence of actinides that undergo spontaneous
fission, namely plutonium and curium in used fuel from LWRs. The measured Doubles rate can then be
correlated to Pu mass in the used fuel via calibration curve, even though the dominant source of correlated
neutrons are from curium. This is possible because the buildup of curium tracks with the buildup of
plutonium and, for an intact fuel pin, there has been no elemental separation.

 Preliminary Neutron Counters

Two neutron detection modalities were considered for this problem: thermal neutron detection via capture
and fast neutron detection via proton recoil.

Thermal Neutron Counter

The energy of fast neutrons emitted from spontaneous fission are typically 1-2 MeV and must first be
thermalized to maximize the number of neutrons detected in a thermal neutron detector. A low-Z
moderator, such as high-density polyethylene (HDPE), is placed around the detectors to slow the fast
neutrons down to thermal energies of less than 1 eV for efficient capture. The detectors typically used for
this are gas proportional counters using 3He gas as the target isotope or boron-lined tubes enriched in 10B.

In this work, the simulated thermal neutron counter was a ring of eight 3He detectors embedded in HDPE
with the spent fuel rod in the center. Cross-sectional views are shown in Figure 10. Note that the
illustration in the Y-Z plane does not show the entire length of the fuel rod. The ring of tubes was set at a
radius of 10 cm from the centerline of the spent fuel pin. The HDPE was an annulus with an inner radius
of 1 cm and an outer radius of 20 cm. The 3He detectors were 2.54 cm in diameter, had an active length of
80 cm and a partial pressure of 4 atm of 3He. A pre-delay of 4 µs, and a gate width of 70 µs were used for
detecting coincidences. The simulated Singles and Doubles count rates were determined for the (α,n) and

Fast Neutron Counter

For fast neutrons detectors, the mechanism for detection is proton recoil in a solid organic scintillator. The
fast neutrons from fission displace hydrogen nuclei from the lattice as an energetic proton. The energetic
proton loses energy in the scintillator and produces photons that can be detected by a photomultiplier tube
or other light sensitive device. Because the neutrons are effectively detected at their “birth” energy, they
do not need to be moderated and are thus detected as correlated events in a significantly shorter
timeframe. This allows the coincidence gate to be set to approximately 5 – 20 ns, which is three orders of
magnitude shorter than the coincidence gate for thermal neutron detectors. The short coincidence gate

Los Alamos National Laboratory Page 4-21

means that Accidentals are rare and do not inflate the Doubles uncertainty as often observed in thermal
neutron counting, leading to shorter count times. The downside to using a bulk scintillator is that they are
sensitive to gamma rays, so pulse-shape discrimination (PSD) is needed to separate pulses from neutrons
and gamma rays. Unfortunately, PSD can result in some neutron pulses not being counted, which
typically leads to an increase in count time.

The fast neutron detection system can be much more compact relative to the thermal neutron system since
moderation of the neutrons is not a requirement for efficient detection. For this study, the organic
scintillator, Stilbene, was chosen for its good pulse shape discrimination attributes. However, other
organic scintillators should be explored if this is determined to be the best option. Being bulk materials,
organic scintillators are generally much more sensitive to gamma radiation relative to gas proportional
counters like 3He tubes or boron straws, but some have properties that allow for the discrimination of
gamma ray interactions from neutron interactions.

A major complicating factor in trying to determine plutonium mass in a full spent fuel assembly is the
high gamma-ray and neutron emission rates. With a high enough gamma ray dose rate in a gas
proportional tube like 3He, gamma rays can “pile up” and produce enough charge that they are mistaken
for neutron events, thus, causing a bias in the measured neutron rates leading to a bias in the assayed Pu
mass. These so-called gamma ray pile-up events are well known [19] and require consideration, like
adding tungsten gamma ray shielding, when measuring spent fuel. However, a single fuel pin has roughly
200x lower gamma-ray and neutron emission rates, because the pin has been separated from the
assembly. Thus, the potential for gamma ray pile-up is greatly reduced, but must still be monitored.

Effects of Burnup and Cooling Time on Pu Mass Estimates

More sophisticated analyses can provide a determination of plutonium mass with higher confidence.
Gamma-ray spectroscopy allows for an independent confirmation of burnup (BU) and/or cooling time
(CT). BU is the fraction of 235U that has undergone fission in a nuclear reactor to produce energy. It is
used to determine the amount of plutonium produced in the fuel rod. CT is the length of time since the
used fuel has been in an operating reactor core. Radioactive decay of isotopes within the fuel rod needs to
be accounted for during this timeframe to accurately estimate plutonium mass in the fuel rod. The use of
BU or CT as a calibration parameter yields a more robust determination of plutonium mass.

The reader is reminded of the assumption that fuel pin initial enrichment can be taken as a known
quantity. This assumption is presumed legitimate in the United States due to the NRC guidance and
practice for accepting the shipper’s declared values as a receiving facility [21]. This is important because
previous work has illustrated the dependency of Pu mass on initial enrichment [16]. In addition, most
neutrons from the simulated fuel in this work come from curium isotopes that grow in as a function of
burnup, but since the fuel pins in this postulated de-bundling facility remain sealed, the correlation of
plutonium to curium is a valid assumption and can be utilized directly. In downstream processes of a fuel
recycling facility, when fuel is dissolved, this correlation no longer holds.

Finally, the thermal detector system shows similar behavior in terms of data dependency and fitting and
so should not be ruled out as a potential measurement solution.

Conclusion

Neutron systems with high enough efficiency to measure coincidence neutrons in spent fuel pins should
be investigated further to understand issues that cannot be adequately understood in simulation space.
These include the effect of gamma rays on the ability of either a fast or thermal neutron counter to
provide accurate count rates. The inclusion of gamma ray shielding could be considered if it is
determined that gamma ray interactions with the neutron detectors negatively impact accuracy.

For fast neutron systems, there is a nonzero chance that a neutron that scattered in one detector element
could have a second scattering event in a neighboring detector element and be counted twice. This would
cause a bias in the detected neutron Doubles rate that must be accounted for or be prevented from
happening. Exploration of ways to prevent this double counting need to be investigated. Options include
raising the event threshold for proton recoil energy such that scattered lower energy neutrons are not
counted and/or placing a material like borated polyethylene between fast neutron detectors that absorb
lower energy neutrons. This double counting effect does not happen in thermal neutron systems as the
neutron must be absorbed to be detected.

Investigation into other neutron sensors that could be used in both the thermal and fast systems is needed
to ensure adequate efficiency, value for cost, and measurement time that meet the requirements of any
future de-bundling facility.

 Other Technologies and Methodologies

Although gamma-ray spectroscopy and neutron detection are cornerstones for NDA in NMAC
applications, many other technologies and methodologies have been developed that could meet the
NMAC goals for an independent fuel debundling facility. Included in this category are destructive assay
(DA) techniques, alternative NDA technologies that use alternative signatures from spent fuel, and
containment and surveillance methods. A complete list of technologies and methodologies considered is
presented in Table 3.

DA techniques require the item, or a small portion of it, to undergo processes that result in the assayed
material being unusable after analysis. Acquiring the sample to be analyzed can also cause damage to the
container holding the material to be assayed. A criterion was established for the fuel debundling facility
that individual fuel rods must remain intact so that requirements can be met for an item-only facility. This
disqualified all destructive analysis techniques for supporting NMAC in a fuel debundling facility.
Specific methodologies considered were headspace gas analysis, alpha spectrometry, and mass

Los Alamos National Laboratory Page 4-30

spectrometry. Although alpha spectrometry is not inherently a DA technique, the sample preparation
needed for a good measurement requires DA techniques.

Alternative NDA techniques considered were Cherenkov detection, acoustics, densitometry, optical
pyrometry, and calorimetry. Cherenkov detection is typically used to verify completeness of a spent fuel
assembly [22]. It requires the fuel assembly to be in water, because the Cherenkov radiation is emitted
only in water. However, the fuel debundling facility is expected to operate in a gas-filled hot cell, thus
rendering this technique unfeasible for this application. Neither acoustic methods nor densitometry
provided information needed for NMAC in a fuel debundling facility and were thus eliminated from
consideration.

Acoustics, densitometry, and optical pyrometry provide information on different physical characteristics
of used fuel. Acoustics sends sound waves through spent fuel to determine the composition of fission gas
[23]. However, it cannot provide isotopic information about the fuel meat, which is the quantity of
interest for NMAC. Densitometry uses x rays or gamma rays to determine the density of the material in
an item, but this quantity is not required to determine fissile mass in used fuel [15]. Optical pyrometry
uses infrared energy emitted by the item to relate it to a temperature. Although useful in other
applications, such as measuring the temperature of the reactor core after a loss of coolant accident [24], it
is not a useful quantity to relate to fissile mass remaining in a used fuel assembly. Ultimately, all three of
these methods were ruled out for not providing information relevant to performing NMAC in a fuel
debundling facility.

Calorimetry measures the heat released from an item during radioactive decay and relates it to mass [15].
This technique can produce results with low uncertainty but does not produce results rapidly. Measuring
the heat requires the system to reach equilibrium, which can take upwards of 24 hours per item. Because
the allotted count time for each fuel rod is only a couple of minutes, the measurement time for calorimetry
has disqualified it from this application.

 Fuel Debundling Detector

The proposed fuel debundling detector (FUDD) will consider combining both gamma spectroscopy and
neutron detection techniques to determine plutonium mass in a fuel rod for NMAC in an independent fuel
debundling facility in the United States. The gamma ray system can be used to determine the pin burnup
using isotopic ratios, and the neutron coincidence Doubles rate can be used with the burnup to determine
the plutonium mass. At this time, it is believed that either a thermal or fast neutron counting system
would provide adequate count rates, but there are likely limitations of each that may direct this research to
one technology over the other.

A full uncertainty analysis will need to be conducted to ensure the combined gamma ray and neutron
assay system uncertainties are adequately propagated into the measured plutonium mass. For the gamma
ray determination of burnup, the uncertainties in net peak areas and calibration parameters need to be
propagated into the calculated burnup. For the neutron system, the Doubles rate uncertainty and the
burnup uncertainty will impact the overall uncertainty in the plutonium mass.

 Conclusions
Fuel debundling could be coupled with recycling used nuclear fuel to both reduce the cost of HLW
disposal and improve measurements for NMAC. The recommendation, based on current NRC regulations
and guidance, is to build an independent fuel debundling facility. This allows fuel debundling to be in a
facility that requires a material-possession-only license, thus negating the need to meet NMAC
requirements for a Category I or II facility. However, it might be desired to acquire Pu mass
measurements for better input accountancy into the dissolver.

Many technologies and methodologies were considered for performing NMAC in an independent fuel
debundling facility in the United States. However, most would not be able to meet throughput demands
and achieve measurement uncertainties suitable for input accountancy in a recycling facility. The
exceptions were passive gamma-ray spectroscopy and neutron coincidence counting, which were shown
to meet throughput demands and potentially provide mass values for input accountancy for a recycling
facility. The proposed FUDD design will incorporate both methodologies to provide a Pu mass estimate
for both NMAC and input accountancy. A full uncertainty analysis will inform what assumptions can be
made to meet input accountancy objectives before finalizing a FUDD design and beginning experimental
activities.
