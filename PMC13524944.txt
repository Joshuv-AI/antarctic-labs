Sequential game dynamics produce subgame perfect equilibria in sports networks through bounded rationality
Authors: Chulwook Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStrategic decision-making in networked systems unfolds sequentially, with each agent’s choice shaped by the observable actions of those who decide earlier. We extend bounded rationality from simultaneous to sequential decision structures on scale-free networks, showing how modified backward induction yields approximate subgame perfect equilibria that converge to their exact counterparts as the rationality parameter increases, forming bounded-rationality refinements of Nash equilibria. Agents ordered by network centrality condition on their predecessors’ actions through a centrality-weighted observation term, with social learning and strategic exploration jointly defining a behavioral regime that ranges from winner-takes-all configurations concentrated among hub agents to broader participation with flattened centrality gradients. In the four equilibrium scenarios examined, the sequential protection distribution stochastically dominates the simultaneous baseline, and centrality-based ordering outperforms random and inverse alternatives. Introduction

Complex sports systems present a distinctive class of strategic interactions. Agents, whether individual athletes, functional units, or teams, decide sequentially, observing and responding to predecessors’ actions within interconnected network structures1–4. Both simultaneous formulations and asynchronous updating have informed network-game studies, including our own demonstration that Nash equilibria emerge from bounded rationality in simultaneous sports networks5. Centrality-ordered sequential play with explicit observation of predecessors has received less systematic attention as a route to equilibrium selection on scale-free networks.

Sequential strategic interactions in sports networks

In reality, strategic adaptation in sports follows sequential patterns, where teams observe rivals’ tactical innovations before responding, athletes adjust techniques after witnessing competitors’ performances, and coaching decisions cascade through organizational hierarchies6,7. The sequential nature of sports competition is not merely incidental but structurally fundamental to strategic outcomes. Championship series in professional baseball, basketball, and hockey are paradigmatic sequential games. Two teams play a sequence of contests, and each game’s outcome alters the strategic landscape for subsequent games, creating state-dependent incentive structures that simultaneous models cannot capture8. In these settings, game trees map the evolving state space and backward induction identifies the subgame perfect equilibrium (SPE), the solution concept that requires optimal play in every possible continuation of the game.

Ferrall and Smith8 formalized this structure for best-of- n championship series, solving the SPE and estimating its parameters across three professional sports using data from World Series (1922–1993), NBA Finals (1955–1994), and Stanley Cup Finals (1939–1994). Beyond multi-game series, sequential structure pervades sports at finer scales. In penalty shootouts, kickers and goalkeepers adjust strategies based on earlier outcomes. In football, offensive play selection, run versus pass and formation choice, triggers defensive counter-adjustments in real time. In baseball, batting order construction reflects sequential considerations, where each lineup position faces a different game state. These examples share three defining characteristics of sequential games. First, decisions occur in a specific temporal order. Second, agents possess information about predecessors’ actions. Third, optimal strategies are determined by thinking ahead through backward induction rather than through simultaneous best-response calculation.

These sequential models in sports, however, have remained dyadic. Rosen9 analyzed how elimination tournament structures require escalating prizes to sustain effort incentives across sequential rounds, while Lazear and Rosen10 established that rank-order compensation schemes can replicate the efficiency of piece-rate contracts when monitoring is costly. Ferrall and Smith’s8 subgame perfect equilibrium analysis is confined to two contestants or a single hierarchical ladder. Our contribution extends sequential game logic from these dyadic contests to multi-agent networked systems, where the decision ordering is determined endogenously by network centrality rather than by an external schedule, and where bounded rationality replaces the assumption of perfect strategic calculation.

However, when strategic interactions unfold sequentially, the appropriate solution concept shifts from NE to SPE. The extensive form representation of sequential games was formalized by Kuhn15, who established the equivalence of mixed and behavioral strategies under perfect recall, providing the structural foundation on which Selten16 introduced the SPE refinement. The relationship between rationality assumptions and equilibrium outcomes is central to this transition. Classical decision theory characterizes risk attitudes through the concavity of the Bernoulli utility function , where concave u corresponds to risk aversion, linear u to risk neutrality, and convex u to risk loving17,18. The formal treatment, including Jensen’s inequality and the Arrow-Pratt risk aversion measures that connect these utility properties to the softmax parameter in our model, is developed in SI Text S3.

Bounded rationality and the present study

Under bounded rationality, agents do not perform exhaustive utility calculations but instead rely on heuristic processes including social learning and strategic exploration5,19,20. Three established approaches modify classical backward induction to accommodate imperfect strategic reasoning. Quantal response equilibrium (QRE) replaces deterministic best responses with probabilistic choice proportional to expected payoffs, using a logit specification whose precision parameter controls the degree of rationality21. McKelvey and Palfrey22 extended QRE to extensive form games through the agent quantal response equilibrium, which imposes a statistical version of sequential rationality and yields a unique selection from the set of subgame perfect equilibria in generic games. Cognitive hierarchy theory23 posits that players reason with heterogeneous depths of strategic thinking, where step- k thinkers best-respond to a believed distribution of lower-step opponents. Our softmax-modified backward induction (Eq. 12 below) draws on the QRE tradition, in which the rationality parameter beta plays the same role as the QRE precision parameter, while the social learning and exploration mechanisms (, ) from our published model5 provide the network-specific adaptation process absent in standard QRE and cognitive hierarchy treatments. When these processes operate within sequential structures, agents use imperfect backward reasoning combined with observational learning, producing a modified form of backward induction that converges to approximate SPE. The inclusion of subgame perfect equilibria within the Nash set is a standard relationship from extensive-form refinement theory15,16, whereas the existence and convergence of -subgame perfect equilibria are proven in this work (SI Text S2), and the first-order stochastic dominance of the sequential protection distribution over the simultaneous baseline across the four scenarios is reported as a model-based numerical finding (Fig. 4). The novelty of our approach lies in embedding QRE-type probabilistic choice within a network setting where social learning and strategic exploration drive equilibrium emergence, a combination absent from the existing sequential game literature on sports.

Our previous work demonstrated that Nash equilibria emerge from bounded rationality in simultaneous games on scale-free networks, identifying four distinct equilibrium scenarios determined by the interplay between maximum protection capacity () and combined failure-propagation risk5. A complementary analysis has further demonstrated that network centrality drives optimal protection investment in scale-free networks, establishing that nodes with higher eigenvector centrality invest proportionally more in protection through social learning and centrality-based heuristics24. The present study extends this approach to sequential game structures, addressing three fundamental questions, namely (i) Do subgame perfect equilibria emerge from bounded backward induction in networked agents? (ii) How does the temporal ordering of decisions, determined by network centrality, affect equilibrium selection compared to simultaneous play? (iii) Under what conditions do sequential and simultaneous game structures produce equivalent versus distinct equilibrium outcomes?

Results

Sequential game structure on scale-free networks

The extensive form game is constructed on Barabási–Albert networks with N=100 nodes and connectivity m=5. Agents are ordered by eigenvector centrality (Eq. 8), with the most central agents moving first according to the decision order (Eq. 9). Each agent observes the protection decisions of all predecessors before choosing their own strategy, creating a perfect information sequential game where later movers incorporate predecessors’ actions through the centrality-weighted observation term (Eq. 15b). The sequential protection function augments the simultaneous model with γ = 0.3, producing a modified backward induction process in which social learning () and strategic exploration () operate within the sequential ordering rather than in parallel (Table 1). Four parameter regimes, defined by the balance between maximum protection capacity () and the protection scaling constant are examined under this sequential structure to characterize how centrality-based decision ordering shapes equilibrium outcomes (Fig. 2). The four scenarios under sequential play preserve the qualitative equilibrium classification established under simultaneous play5 while exposing structural features unique to the sequential setting.

Analogous centrality–investment patterns across simulated and empirical networks

The centrality-dependent fate selection observed in the simulated network (Fig. 4E) raises the question of whether scale-free systems with centrality-based decision ordering exhibit comparable investment stratification across domains. Discussion

This study extends bounded rationality game theory from simultaneous to sequential structures on scale-free networks, producing three principal findings. First, modified backward induction under bounded rationality yields -subgame perfect equilibria that converge to exact SPE as the rationality parameter increases (SI Text S2), and these equilibria form refined subsets of Nash equilibria (SPE() NE()), with the refinement strength controlled by the observation weight . Second, in all four equilibrium scenarios examined, the sequential protection distribution under centrality-based decision ordering first-order stochastically dominates the simultaneous baseline (Fig. 4A–D). Third, the centrality–investment relationship seen in the simulated network (Fig. 4E) also appears across the empirical domains examined, where sequential observation improves the correlation fits by 13–26% relative to the simultaneous baseline (Fig. 5A).

SPE emergence through bounded-rationality backward induction

The SPE NE inclusion, established within the extensive form structure15,16 and resting on the Nash existence proved through Kakutani’s fixed point theorem13, suggests theoretical continuity with the simultaneous NE existence established in our earlier work5. The refinement operates through a mechanism distinct from classical backward induction. Softmax action selection21,22 combined with social learning and exploration19,20 produces -SPE that converge to exact SPE as the rationality parameter increases (SI Text S2). The convergence rate scales as , with social learning () accelerating and exploration () bounding the steady-state neighborhood (SI Text S2, Eqs. S26–S30). This convergence property distinguishes our approach from cognitive hierarchy models23, which require prior specification of reasoning depths, and from strategic stability refinements25 that impose axiomatic restrictions on equilibrium selection rather than deriving refinement endogenously from sequential observation. Standard evolutionary dynamics on networks26 and iterated multiplayer games under asynchronous best-response dynamics27 lack the sequential observation channel that drives refinement in our model. The finding that sequential observation cannot rescue structurally constrained systems (scenario , Fig. 4D) is consistent with results on strategic investment in networked systems28, where protection capacity bounds constrain equilibrium outcomes regardless of information availability, and with broader game-theoretic principles of sequential rationality29.

Centrality-based ordering and equilibrium selection

The centrality-based ordering creates a temporal structure with functional parallels to spatial organization in cyclic dominance games30. In rock-paper-scissors dynamics on complex networks, spatial heterogeneity sustains biodiversity through spiral waves and defensive alliances. Our ordering sustains equilibrium differentiation through an analogous mechanism, giving hub nodes a first-mover commitment advantage while peripheral nodes exploit observational information (Fig. 4A; SI Fig. S1). This specialization resonates with evidence that evolutionary dynamics produce role differentiation in strategic communities31 and that coevolutionary feedback between strategies and structure shapes equilibrium selection26,32. The sports parallel is concrete. Playmakers occupy high-centrality positions and constrain teammates’ subsequent choices14, athletes in multi-agent motor interactions converge to Nash equilibria through adaptive control33, cycling leaders set pacing that followers accommodate34, and first-mover tactical innovations cascade through team hierarchies7.

The survival threshold in Fig. 4E gives individual-level confirmation of the ordering advantage. Nodes above ≈ 0.39 invest more through the sequential observation mechanism and accumulate capacity sufficient to resist failure cascades, while nodes below it lack the sustained investment to remain viable. This parallels the “too central to fail” logic in financial networks, where systemically important institutions hold higher capital buffers because their structural prominence demands greater protection24,35. In our model the same mechanism operates endogenously, where high-centrality nodes, acting as early movers, commit to higher protection that later movers observe and partially emulate, producing the dominance in Fig. 4B. Such centrality-dependent protection intensifies under multilayer coupling36, consistent with the investment gradient steepening with connectivity. The protection function (Fig. 5C) follows a hyperbolic form across domains, outperforming alternatives for sports ( = 0.926) and banking ( = 0.783), while the sequential model shows a marginally better logistic fit ( = 0.852 vs. hyperbolic 0.803), consistent with the sharper threshold in Fig. 4E, and epidemiology is competitive across all forms.

The model also connects abstract risk attitudes to operational bounded rationality. The softmax parameter controls the effective curvature of the utility response, with high approximating risk-averse behavior that favors known high-payoff actions (concave u, < 0) and low producing risk-seeking exploration (convex u, > 0)17,18. This connection between classical risk theory and operational rationality (SI Text S3) supplies a bridge that standard behavioral game-theoretic treatments37 identified as essential but did not formalize for network settings. Penalty-shootout goalkeepers show systematic action bias rather than optimal mixed play38, and field studies of soccer players confirm departures from minimax predictions39, consistent with the bounded rationality our parameter captures. The resource-rational perspective40 supports this reading, framing bounded rationality as an adaptive response to computational limits rather than a deficiency.

Behavioral regimes and sports-oriented dynamics

Beyond their role as mechanism parameters, the social learning rate and the exploration rate define a behavioral regime that governs how protection investment distributes across the network5,19,20. This pairing of imitation and exploration acts as an adaptive strategy update rule, and recent analysis of such rules in social dilemmas finds that the revision rule shapes the population outcome as strongly as the payoffs do41. Related work on networked evolutionary dynamics shows that self-interaction learning, where an agent weighs its own prior strategy, acts as a selection amplifier biasing which equilibrium the population reaches42.

When both parameters are low, peripheral agents update and explore rarely, so investment concentrates among the early-moving hubs and the system settles into a monopolistic configuration where a few high-centrality nodes dominate the protection landscape. When both are high, peripheral agents update and explore actively, and investment spreads across a broader segment of the population, producing a distributed configuration in which the centrality gradient is preserved but flattened. This positions and as regime indices rather than auxiliary settings, since within the same topology and scenario class their choice determines whether the equilibrium is winner-takes-all or more egalitarian, even with the centrality ordering and protection capacity held constant. The regime view also explains why the variance reduction in Fig. 3C strengthens with higher (, ), since the same parameters that flatten the investment distribution also tighten the strategy distribution around the SPE.

This regime view connects to a recurrent feature of professional sports markets, where rewards and visibility are heavy-tailed and a few star players or teams capture a disproportionate share of attention and resources9,10. Read through the bounded-rationality model, this winner-takes-all pattern corresponds to the low-(, ) regime, where limited learning and exploration leave hubs structurally favored and the path from peripheral to central position narrows. Increasing (, ) widens that path, as peripheral agents revise and explore more and accumulate the conditions under which their investment can approach that of the early movers. The model thus accounts for how a sports market migrates between monopolistic and distributed configurations as the institutional setting changes the rates at which workers learn from one another and explore alternative team affiliations. Worker mobility across teams, modeled as repeated participation in a sequence of stage games rather than a one-shot decision8, is the empirical channel through which (, ) act, since a player who switches teams samples additional decision contexts and operates closer to the high-(, ) end of the regime axis. The boundary between the two configurations corresponds to where the early-mover and late-mover best-response curves intersect at the SPE, and its position shifts with (, ) and the observation weight , as the Fig. 6 schematic makes explicit.

Cross-domain applications

The system-of-systems perspective advanced by Hochrainer-Stigler et al.43 provides a natural interpretive context for our cross-domain findings. The first resilience dividend, avoided losses through protection investment, maps onto within-scenario equilibrium dynamics (Fig. 2), while the observation weight in Eq. 15a captures how sequential information flow generates second-order dividends through cross-agent coordination. Although the present model holds the network fixed, its protection dynamics relate to evolutionary dynamics on time-varying networks, where power-law activation patterns shape which strategies fixate44 and bursty switching of activity can drive the collapse of network topologies45. Knowledge transfer across organizational boundaries46 operates through a mechanism analogous to the centrality-weighted observation term . Agents with bridging positions in the network facilitate information flow that accelerates convergence to equilibrium. For financial stability, centrality-based ordering (Eq. 9) offers a principled mechanism for sequencing regulatory intervention, where globally systemically important banks act as early movers whose capital adequacy decisions constrain subsequent responses by smaller institutions24. Read as a multi-agent system, sequenced capital requirements operate as a dynamic sanctioning mechanism that holds cooperative behavior in place across agents of differing size47. For public health crisis response, the conditional probability structure (Eq. 6) governs how later-responding units update vaccination or containment strategies based on predecessors’ observed outcomes, consistent with the evolutionary dynamics governing biological cooperation under selection pressure48. Because containment is a public good, this setting also connects to the coevolution of population strategies and institutional rewards, where incentive structures and individual choices adjust to each other over the course of the response49.

The present empirical validation, drawing on cross-domain calibration data analysis8,24, confirms that network topology generates strategic differentiation invisible to two-team models. This finding motivates a structured empirical program targeting three sports contexts. Penalty shootout sequences, where kickers and goalkeepers adjust strategies based on observed earlier outcomes38,39, provide a natural test of the observation mechanism. NFL draft order and batting lineup construction present sequential optimization contexts where evidence of departures from optimal play50 maps directly onto the ordering. Each setting features a determinate decision order, observable predecessor actions, and centrality-differentiated strategic roles.

Limitations and future directions

Several boundaries of the present analysis should be stated clearly. The decision ordering is fixed and based on static eigenvector centrality, whereas in real systems the network structure and the centrality ranking co-evolve with the game dynamics, so the ordering used here is a snapshot rather than a fully dynamic schedule. All agents share a common rationality parameter , whereas real populations exhibit heterogeneous strategic sophistication23,37, so the present results describe a homogeneous-rationality regime. The observation term assumes accurate perception of predecessors’ actions and omits the noise and partial observability that characterize many real strategic settings15. A sensitivity analysis that adds noise to the observed predecessor protection shows that the stochastic dominance and the survival threshold persist across the noise range, while the graded centrality-investment correlation weakens once the noise reaches a moderate level (SI Text S7.5, SI Fig. S2).

A second set of boundaries concerns empirical scope. Although the main analyses use a fixed network size, the four-scenario ordering and the relative position of the centrality survival threshold hold across networks from N = 100 to N = 500. The linear coefficient between node centrality and protection investment declines as the network grows, because max-normalized eigenvector centrality concentrates toward zero at larger N and compresses the range of the predictor, so the linear value reported here applies to N = 100 while the rank ordering it summarizes persists across sizes (Section S7.6). The empirical calibration treats the model as a stylized representation of each domain rather than a fitted predictor, so the cross-domain correspondence in Fig. 5 and SI Table S2 is consistency evidence for a shared mechanism and not a direct empirical estimate.

Future extensions include dynamic ordering that co-evolves with the game state, agent-specific distributions calibrated to empirical behavioral data22, evolutionary games on higher-order structures such as simplicial51, and expanded multilayer network representations that explicitly couple tactical, physiological, and social interaction layers. The zero-sum assumption could be relaxed to incorporate cooperative elements observed in team sports, where partial cooperation coexists with inter-team competition5,48.

Network construction and agent representation

The network topology follows the Barabási–Albert preferential attachment model52, generating scale-free networks with N nodes and average connectivity m, where new nodes attach with probability proportional to degree to produce the power-law degree distribution characteristic of sports interaction networks1,4,5. This topology is appropriate for two structural reasons. First, sports networks form multilayer coupled systems, where a tactical decision layer, a physiological performance layer, and a social coordination layer share the same nodes but follow layer-specific dynamics, so that localized disruptions propagate more aggressively than in single-layer systems, following power-law scaling that accelerates failure transmission across layers (Fig. 6A). Second, empirical team passing networks, player interaction patterns, and coaching hierarchies show heavy-tailed degree distributions1,4,53, in which a few highly connected hub nodes disproportionately influence system-wide outcomes.
