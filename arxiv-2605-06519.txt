Efficient Techniques for Data Reconstruction, with Finite-Width Recovery Guarantees
Edward Tansley, Roy Makhlouf, Estelle Massart, and, Coralia Cartis

Abstract
Data reconstruction attacks on trained neural networks aim to recover the data on which the network has been trained and pose a significant threat to privacy, especially if the training dataset contains sensitive information. Here, we propose a unified optimization formulation of the data reconstruction problem based on initial and trained parameter values, incorporating state-of-the-art proposals. We show that in the random feature model, this formulation provably leads to training data reconstruction with high probability, provided the network width is sufficiently large; this unprecedented finite-width result uses PAC-style bounds. Furthermore, when the data lies in a low-dimensional subspace, we show that the network width requirement for successful reconstruction can be relaxed, with bounds depending on the subspace dimension rather than the ambient dimension. For general neural network models and unknown data orientations, we propose an efficient reconstruction algorithm that approximates the low-dimensional data subspace through the change in the first-layer weights during training and uses only the last-layer weights for reconstruction, thus reducing the search space dimension and the required network width for high-quality reconstructions. Our numerical experiments on synthetic datasets and CIFAR-10 confirm that our subspace-aware reconstruction approach outperforms standard full-space techniques.

Introduction

The rapid deployment of deep neural networks in privacy-sensitive domains, such as healthcare or identity verification, has raised critical concerns regarding the confidentiality of training data. Data reconstruction attacks seek to reconstruct training samples from a trained model using only publicly available information, such as the model’s weights or architecture. These attacks can pose a threat to privacy, particularly when the model is trained on personal data. For example, in facial recognition systems, an attacker may be able to reconstruct facial images from the original training set, potentially exposing individuals without their consent. Understanding when such reconstruction is theoretically possible remains an open question.

Various types of data reconstruction methods have been proposed, including gradient-based
[
24
]
and neural network attacks, that train a surrogate neural network for the reconstruction of a portion of the dataset
[
1
]
. By contrast, our work here addresses strategies that exploit implicit biases of model training to reconstruct the data solely from the trained parameters. A first step in this direction is
[
7
]
, which used the fact that, in the case of overparameterized homogeneous ReLU feed-forward neural networks for binary classification with the logistic loss, gradient flow converges to the solution of a set of KKT equations of an associated maximum-margin problem, see
[
15
]
and
[
11
]
. KKT equations imply that the trained parameters are a linear combination of the gradients of the model with respect to the parameters at the training data that are “on the margin”; inverting these equations then allows for reconstructing these data points. The theoretical foundation for these attacks relies on overparameterization: KKT equations provide a set of
p
equations (the number of the model parameters) in
nd
unknowns (the number of data points to recover multiplied by their dimension), which makes data reconstruction theoretically tractable. The authors of
[
7
]
formulate data reconstruction as an optimization problem minimizing the violation of the KKT conditions, and validate it numerically. Their approach was subsequently extended to general losses and the multi-class setting
[
3
]
.

Building on these findings, the works
[
14
]
and
[
9
]
aim to provably reconstruct the entire dataset for the neural tangent kernel (NTK) regime
[
10
]
and the random feature (RF) model
[
17
,
18
]
, respectively. In
[
14
]
, the authors show that KKT conditions derived in the NTK regime for classification with mean squared error (MSE) loss ensure that the model parameters are a linear combination of the gradients of the neural network with respect to the model parameters at the data points. The authors of
[
14
]
exploit this finding in the design of a novel data reconstruction algorithm that penalizes the misalignment between the trained parameters and the subspace spanned by these gradients and provide convergence guarantees establishing recovery of the entire dataset in the infinite-width limit. For RFs, it was shown in
[
9
]
that approximate reconstruction of the training samples becomes theoretically possible when the number of model parameters
p
scales with
nd
. Iurada et al.
[
9
]
developed a reconstruction algorithm that minimizes the projection of the trained final-layer parameters onto the subspace spanned by the random features corresponding to the data, but do not provide recovery guarantees for this reconstruction algorithm, either in the infinite-width limit or in the finite case. The first goal of this paper is to provide a unified framework for data reconstruction with finite-width recovery guarantees that bridges the gap between the RF model analysis in
[
9
]
and the NTK approach in
[
14
]
.

As a second goal, we explore the potential benefits of structured data for the success of data reconstruction algorithms.
This is motivated by the common assumption that many datasets lie in a neighborhood of (possibly a union of) a low-dimensional manifold(s)
[
19
,
13
]
. This common belief is at the core of generative modeling strategies
[
5
]
and was recently leveraged to understand the vulnerability of deep neural networks to adversarial attacks
[
16
,
4
]
. We assume here the simplest form of such a structure, namely, the existence of a low-dimensional subspace in which the data lies, and explore the impact of this structure on the performance of our data reconstruction algorithm. We then propose a novel data reconstruction algorithm that exploits such low-dimensional structures.

Our contributions are as follows:

•

We establish PAC-style guarantees for data reconstruction in RF models, providing recovery guarantees for the algorithm proposed in
[
9
]
. Specifically, inspired by the methodology in
[
14
]
, we show that, for RF models with sufficiently large width, the training data can be approximately recovered with high probability.

•

We prove that when the training data lies in a low-dimensional subspace of dimension
r
, the theoretical width requirement depends on
r
rather than the ambient dimension
d
.

•

Exploiting such structure in the reconstruction process requires knowledge of the underlying subspace. In the case of 2-layer neural networks, we show numerically that this subspace is implicitly captured by the change in the first-layer weights during training. This leads to an efficient variant of the reconstruction algorithm in
[
9
]
that leverages this information to project the original search space onto a lower-dimensional one.

•

We investigate the impact of network depth on the success of reconstruction. We show that reconstruction using only the last layer parameters still leads to strong results in deeper networks, while providing substantial computational benefits. In addition, when low-dimensional structure is present in the data, our subspace reconstruction methods outperform full space methods.

The paper is structured as follows.
Section 2

Section 3

Section 4

refines these guarantees when the data lies in a low-dimensional subspace, while
subsection 4.2

Section 5
presents numerical results.

2
Data reconstruction without data structure assumptions

Let
({\bm{X}},{\bm{y}})
be a dataset, where
{\bm{X}}=[{\bm{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{x}}_{n}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
contains
n
training samples
{\bm{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{x}}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
contains the corresponding labels. Throughout this work, we assume that a parameterized model
f(\cdot,{\bm{\theta}})
, with parameters
{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, is trained to minimize the MSE loss:

\min_{{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{train}}({\bm{\theta}};{\bm{X}},{\bm{y}}),

(P-train)

where

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{train}}({\bm{\theta}};{\bm{X}},{\bm{y}}):=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(f({\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{\theta}})-y_{i})^{2}.

We assume that (
P-train
) is solved using gradient descent, starting from initialization
{\bm{\theta}}_{0}
, converging to a point
{\bm{\theta}}_{f}
, and we write
\Delta{\bm{\theta}}:={\bm{\theta}}_{f}-{\bm{\theta}}_{0}
. In this paper, we consider the task of reconstructing the data matrix
{\bm{X}}
solely from the knowledge of
\Delta{\bm{\theta}}
and the trained model gradients
\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}f(\cdot,{\bm{\theta}}_{f})
. We let
\hat{\bm{X}}=[\hat{\bm{x}}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\bm{x}}_{n}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
contain the
n
reconstructed data
\hat{\bm{x}}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\bm{x}}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

To ensure tractability of the reconstruction for homogeneous neural networks, we assume that the data is normalized as in
[
14
,
9
]
.

Assumption 1
.

The training data
{\bm{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{x}}_{n}
lie on the sphere
\sqrt{d}\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
.

For data reconstruction, we consider a loss that penalizes the misalignment between
\Delta{\bm{\theta}}
and the subspace spanned by the gradients of the model at the data points, i.e., we solve for
\hat{\bm{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
and coefficients
\hat{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\hat{\bm{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d},\hat{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\\
\|\hat{\bm{x}}_{i}\|_{2}=\sqrt{d}\ \forall i\in[n]\end{subarray}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{recon}}(\hat{\bm{X}},\hat{\bm{\alpha}}),

(P-recon)

where

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{recon}}(\hat{\bm{X}},\hat{\bm{\alpha}}):=\left\|\Delta{\bm{\theta}}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\alpha}_{i}\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}f(\hat{\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{\theta}}_{f})\right\|_{2}^{2}.

This reconstruction loss was initially proposed in
[
7
]
for homogeneous ReLU neural networks trained with the logistic loss. It also matches the one used in
[
14
]
(with
\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}f(\hat{\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{\theta}}_{f})
replaced by
\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}f(\hat{\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{\theta}}_{0})
due to the NTK assumption). We explore its use here for more general architectures.

Note that (
P-recon
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\hat{\bm{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}\\
\|\hat{\bm{x}}_{i}\|_{2}=\sqrt{d}\ \forall i\in[n]\end{subarray}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{recon}}(\hat{\bm{X}},\hat{\bm{\alpha}}^{*}(\hat{\bm{X}})),

where
\hat{\bm{\alpha}}^{*}(\hat{\bm{X}}):=\argmin_{\hat{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{recon}}(\hat{\bm{X}},\hat{\bm{\alpha}})
are the optimal coefficients given
\hat{\bm{X}}
. Thus (
P-recon
) can be formulated in terms of the projection of
\Delta{\bm{\theta}}
onto the orthogonal complement to the subspace spanned by the model gradients at the data points, as presented in
Algorithm 1
.

Algorithm 1

Data reconstruction technique for general data structures

1:

Let
{\bm{\theta}}_{f}
be obtained by applying gradient descent to problem (
P-train
), starting from initialization
{\bm{\theta}}_{0}
, and let
\Delta{\bm{\theta}}:={\bm{\theta}}_{f}-{\bm{\theta}}_{0}
.

2:

Solve

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\hat{\bm{X}}=[\hat{\bm{x}}_{1},\dots,\hat{\bm{x}}_{n}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}\\
\|\hat{\bm{x}}_{i}\|_{2}=\sqrt{d}\ \forall i\in[n]\end{subarray}}\|{\bm{P}}_{{\bm{G}}}^{\perp}(\Delta{\bm{\theta}})\|_{2}^{2},

(2.1)

where
{\bm{P}}_{{\bm{G}}}^{\perp}
is the orthogonal projection onto the orthogonal complement of the subspace spanned by the rows of
{\bm{G}}=[\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}f(\hat{\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{\theta}}_{f}),\dots,\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}f(\hat{\bm{x}}_{n},{\bm{\theta}}_{f})]^{\top}
.

3:

Return the reconstructed data matrix
\hat{\bm{X}}
.

While (
P-recon
) and (
2.1
) are equivalent, we rely on (
P-recon
) in our theoretical results, which explicitly involves the coefficients
\hat{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and use the more computationally friendly expression (

) in our numerical experiments, the computation of which is detailed in
Appendix B
. Note that formulation (

) also coincides with the reconstruction proposed in

]
for RF models. Then,

3
Non-asymptotic guarantees for recovery in random features models

In this section, assuming that gradient descent applied to (
P-train
) has converged exactly to a minimizer
{\bm{\theta}}^{*}
, we derive theoretical recovery guarantees for
Algorithm 1
applied to RF models in the case where (
2.1
) is solved exactly (equivalently, for reconstructed data matrix
\hat{\bm{X}}^{*}
and coefficients
\hat{\bm{\alpha}}^{*}
solving exactly (
P-recon
)). Specifically, we consider a two-layer neural network in which only the second layer is trained, while the first layer is fixed and randomly initialized. In contrast with existing works, our results are non-asymptotic, i.e., do not assume the network width to tend to infinity. Note that recovery guarantees were already obtained in the NTK regime, in the infinite-width limit
[
14
]
.

We consider the random feature (RF) model

f_{\mathrm{RF}}({\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\phi({\bm{V}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{b}})^{\top}{\bm{\theta}}=\varphi({\bm{x}})^{\top}{\bm{\theta}},

(3.1)

where
{\bm{V}}_{i,j}\sim_{\text{i.i.d}}\mathcal{N}(0,1/d)
,
{\bm{b}}_{i}\sim_{\text{i.i.d}}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and
\varphi({\bm{x}}):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\phi({\bm{V}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{b}})
, see
[
17
,
18
]
.
The corresponding kernel is given by
k_{p}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{p}\phi({\bm{V}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{b}})^{\top}\phi({\bm{V}}{\bm{x}}^{\prime}+{\bm{b}})=\varphi({\bm{x}})^{\top}\varphi({\bm{x}}^{\prime})
, with infinite-width limit
k_{\infty}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})=\mathbb{E}[\phi({\bm{v}}^{\top}{\bm{x}}+b)\phi({\bm{v}}^{\top}{\bm{x}}^{\prime}+b)]
, where
{\bm{v}}_{i}\sim_{\text{i.i.d}}\mathcal{N}(0,1/d)
and
b\sim_{\text{i.i.d}}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. We denote the related reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHSs) by
\mathcal{H}_{p}
and
\mathcal{H_{\infty}}
, respectively. We let
{\bm{\Phi}}:=[\varphi({\bm{x}}_{1}),\ldots,\varphi({\bm{x}}_{n})]^{\top}
be the feature map corresponding to the training data. We further make the following assumption.

Assumption 2
.

The activation function
\phi
is continuous, nonlinear, not a polynomial, bounded and has a bounded derivative, i.e.,
|\phi(x)|\leq M
and
|\phi^{\prime}(x)|\leq L
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Assumption 2
covers a wide family of activations, such as the hyperbolic tangent and the sigmoid. A nonlinear and nonpolynomial activation is required to ensure universality of the kernel
k_{\infty}

[
21
]
, a property that will be used to derive recovery guarantees for
Algorithm 1
.

We let
{\bm{\theta}}_{0}=0
for simplicity. With any choice of step size that ensures convergence, gradient descent applied to problem (
P-train
) in the overparameterized regime (
p>n
) converges to the minimum-norm interpolator, i.e.,
{\bm{\theta}}_{f}={\bm{\theta}}^{*}
with

{\bm{\theta}}^{*}={\bm{\Phi}}^{+}{\bm{y}}={\bm{\Phi}}^{\top}({\bm{\Phi}}{\bm{\Phi}}^{\top})^{-1}{\bm{y}}={\bm{\Phi}}^{\top}{\bm{\alpha}},

(3.2)

where we assumed that
{\bm{\Phi}}{\bm{\Phi}}^{\top}
is invertible, and with
{\bm{\alpha}}:=({\bm{\Phi}}{\bm{\Phi}}^{\top})^{-1}{\bm{y}}
, see
[
2
, eq. 3.3]
.

As mentioned in
Section 2
, since
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f_{\mathrm{RF}}(\cdot,{\bm{\theta}})=\varphi(\cdot)
, setting the parameterized model in (
P-recon
) to be
f\equiv f_{\mathrm{RF}}
reduces
Algorithm 1
to the one proposed in
[
9
]
, for which no recovery guarantees have been established. In particular, note that
{\bm{G}}={\bm{\Phi}}
in the RF setting.

3.1
Data reconstruction guarantees without data structure assumptions

Inspired by the work
[
14
]
, we provide recovery guarantees for
Algorithm 1
–equivalently, for
({\bm{X}}^{*},{\bm{\alpha}}^{*})
solving (
P-recon
) exactly– in the case of RF models with finite-width

, showing that the training data can be approximately reconstructed when

is sufficiently large. In order to achieve this, we first need to quantify the deviation of the finite-width kernel from the infinite-width one, which is the focus of the next lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
Assumption 1
and
Assumption 2
hold. Let
\varepsilon>0
and
\delta>0
be such that

\delta\leq\frac{4(1+2d)LM}{\sqrt{d}\varepsilon}.

(3.3)

If

p\geq\frac{8M^{4}(2d+1)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\ln\left(\frac{2+4d}{\delta}\right)+\frac{2d}{2d+1}\ln\left(\frac{6LM}{\sqrt{d}\varepsilon}\right)\right),

(3.4)

then

\mathbb{P}\left[\sup_{{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\in\sqrt{d}\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}|k_{p}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})-k_{\infty}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})|\leq\varepsilon\right]\geq 1-\delta.

(3.5)

Proof sketch.

We follow the same methodology as in the proof of Claim 1 in
[
17
]
, which addresses translation-invariant kernels. Computations are here adapted to account for the fact that the RF kernel is in general not translation-invariant. The complete proof is given in
Appendix A
.
∎

From
Lemma 3.1
, we now derive the following theorem, whose proof is given in
Appendix A
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
Assumption 1
and
Assumption 2
hold, and assume that the training samples satisfy
\|{\bm{x}}_{i}-{\bm{x}}_{j}\|_{2}\geq\Delta
for all
i\neq j
and for some
\Delta>0
. Additionally, assume that the coefficients
\alpha_{i}
in (
3.2
) are bounded away from zero, i.e.,
|\alpha_{i}|\geq c>0
for all
i\in[n]
. Let
\hat{\bm{X}}^{*}
and
\hat{\bm{\alpha}}^{*}
be optimal solutions to (
P-recon
), for
{\bm{\theta}}_{f}={\bm{\theta}}^{*}
given in (
3.2
). If
\delta>0
satisfies

\delta\leq 16C(1+2d)LM\frac{(\|{\bm{\alpha}}\|_{1}+\|\hat{\bm{\alpha}}\|_{1})^{2}}{\sqrt{d}c^{2}}

for some positive constant
C
that depends on
\Delta
, and if
p
satisfies

p\geq\frac{128C^{2}M^{4}(2d+1)(\|{\bm{\alpha}}\|_{1}+\|\hat{\bm{\alpha}}\|_{1})^{4}}{c^{4}}\left(\ln\left(\frac{2+4d}{\delta}\right)+\frac{2d}{2d+1}\ln\left(\frac{24CLM(\|{\bm{\alpha}}\|_{1}+\|\hat{\bm{\alpha}}\|_{1})^{2}}{\sqrt{d}c^{2}}\right)\right),

then, with probability
1-\delta
, for all
j\in[n]
, there exists a reconstructed sample
\hat{\bm{x}}_{i}
such that
\|{\bm{x}}_{j}-\hat{\bm{x}}_{i}\|_{2}<\Delta
.

Proof sketch.

This result follows from standard connections between RKHS norms and the maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) between measures
[
6
]
. For universal kernels defined on compact sets, the MMD is injective
[
20
]
. Based on this property, the work
[
14
]
showed that, in the infinite-width limit of the NTK regime, a vanishing MMD implies exact recovery of the training data. In our setting, the kernel
k_{\infty}
remains universal
[
21
]
, so injectivity of the MMD still holds. Although the MMD is no longer zero for a finite-width

[
22
]
and
Lemma 3.1
, it can be made arbitrarily small as the width gets larger, which in turn yields approximate recovery of the training data.
∎

Theorem 3.1
proves that, for finite-width RF models, the training data can be approximately reconstructed from the trained weights and model architecture alone, using

4
Data reconstruction for data with special structure

If we assume that the data has some additional structure, one can hope to reduce the network width requirement for data reconstruction to be possible. In this regard, we make the following assumption, as an alternative to
Assumption 1
.

Assumption 3
.

The rows of
{\bm{X}}
lie on the intersection of the sphere
\sqrt{d}\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
and some
r
-dimensional subspace
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
spanned by the columns of an orthogonal matrix
{\bm{U}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
, for some
r<d
.

Let
{\bm{U}}
be defined in
Assumption 3
. Then, note that (
P-recon
) is equivalent to the lower-dimensional problem

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\hat{\bm{Z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r},\hat{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\\
\|\hat{\bm{z}}_{i}\|_{2}=\sqrt{d}\ \forall i\in[n]\end{subarray}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}(\hat{\bm{Z}}{\bm{U}}^{\top},\hat{\bm{\alpha}}).

(P-recon-subspace)

In particular, if
(\hat{\bm{Z}}^{*},\hat{\bm{\alpha}}^{*})
is an optimal solution of (
P-recon-subspace
), then
(\hat{\bm{Z}}^{*}{\bm{U}}^{\top},\hat{\bm{\alpha}}^{*})
is an optimal solution of (
P-recon
).

4.1
Recovery guarantees for RF models

Under this data assumption, we may use the rotational invariance of the Gaussian distribution to derive the following corollary, for RF models described in last section.

Corollary 4.1
.

Let
Assumption 3
hold. Let
(\hat{\bm{Z}}^{*},\hat{\bm{\alpha}}^{*})
be any optimal solution of (
P-recon-subspace
) for
{\bm{\theta}}_{f}={\bm{\theta}}^{*}
given in (
3.2
), and let
\hat{\bm{X}}^{*}:=\hat{\bm{Z}}^{*}{\bm{U}}^{\top}
. Then,
(\hat{\bm{X}}^{*},\hat{\bm{\alpha}}^{*})
benefits from the recovery guarantees of
Theorem 3.1
(under the same assumptions), replacing

by

in the bounds on

and

.

Proof.

For any
{\bm{x}}\in\sqrt{d}\mathcal{S}^{d-1}\cap\mathcal{X}
, let
{\bm{z}}\in\sqrt{d}\mathcal{S}^{r-1}
be such that
{\bm{x}}={\bm{U}}{\bm{z}}
, where
{\bm{U}}
is defined in

. By the rotational invariance of the Gaussian distribution, there holds

\varphi({\bm{x}})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\phi\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\sqrt{r}}{\bm{V}}{\bm{U}}\frac{\sqrt{r}}{\sqrt{d}}{\bm{z}}+{\bm{b}}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\phi({\bm{V}}^{\prime}{\bm{z}}^{\prime}+{\bm{b}}),

4.2
A practical reconstruction algorithm for data with special structure

While the previous section shows that the low-dimensional data reconstruction problem (
P-recon-subspace
) allows data recovery under the assumption that the data subspace is known, this assumption is often too strong in practice. We propose here a strategy to learn the data subspace based on the first-layer parameters. As this section addresses algorithmic considerations, we move away from RF models and revert back to the general setting described in
Section 2
.

We estimate the subspace to which the data belongs as the span of the leading right singular vectors of
\Delta{\bm{W}}_{1}:={\bm{W}}_{1,f}-{\bm{W}}_{1,0}
, where
{\bm{W}}_{1}
are the first-layer weights of the neural network
f
(we assume here the dimension of the subspace to be known
1
1

1

). Indeed, by the chain rule,

.

3:

Solve

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\hat{\bm{Z}}=[\hat{\bm{z}}_{1},\dots,\hat{\bm{z}}_{n}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}\\
\|{\bm{z}}_{i}\|_{2}=\sqrt{d}\ \forall i\in[n]\end{subarray}}\|{\bm{P}}_{{\bm{G}}}^{\perp}(\Delta{\bm{\theta}})\|_{2}^{2},

(4.2)

where
{\bm{P}}_{{\bm{G}}}^{\perp}
is the orthogonal projection onto the orthogonal complement of the subspace spanned by the rows of
{\bm{G}}=[\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}f(\hat{\bm{U}}^{\top}\hat{\bm{z}}_{1},{\bm{\theta}}_{f}),\dots,\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}f(\hat{\bm{U}}^{\top}\hat{\bm{z}}_{n},{\bm{\theta}}_{f})]^{\top}
.

4:

Return the reconstructed data matrix
\hat{\bm{X}}:=\hat{\bm{Z}}\hat{\bm{U}}^{\top}
.

Using the first-layer parameters to learn the data subspace questions the need to then use all parameters in the reconstruction algorithm.
Indeed, the reconstruction method proposed in
[
9
]
was already only relying on the last-layer parameters (but without learning the data subspace). We will show numerically in next section that using the first-layer parameters for subspace learning and the last-layer for reconstruction are sufficient in practice to recover the dataset, leading to strong computational benefits.

Numerical results

In this section, we compare the different reconstruction algorithms discussed in the paper, for real and synthetic datasets and various architectures.

Data generation.

Our experiments involve two types of data: CIFAR-10 images

For the CIFAR-10 data, we use

Conclusion.

We introduced a unified formulation of the data reconstruction problem based on initial and trained parameter values, and derived finite-width recovery guarantees for data reconstruction using
Algorithm 1
for RF models.
We further demonstrated that the network width requirement for successful reconstruction is related to the intrinsic dimensionality of the data. Specifically, when the training data lies in a low-dimensional subspace, this requirement can be relaxed.
To account for situations where the data subspace is unknown, we showed that, for feedforward neural networks, the change in the first-layer weights during training can be used to approximate this subspace, leading to
Algorithm 2
.
Our numerical experiments on synthetic datasets and CIFAR-10 further confirm that this subspace-aware reconstruction approach outperforms standard full-space techniques that do not exploit data structure.

The key motivation for this research was to provide conditions under which data reconstruction may be possible in general and when certain data structures are present. We acknowledge that our reconstruction techniques may raise concerns regarding privacy,
which we hope will lead to further research in developing
mechanisms that protect from such neural network vulnerabilities. In this sense, a take-away message of our work is that we may want to ensure that publicly available models are not operating in the wide network regime where training accuracy is attained through memorization of data points rather than learning generalizing functions.

Appendix A
Proof of theoretical results

We recall Hoeffding’s inequality, which will be useful to bound, with high probability, the deviation of the finite-width kernel from the infinite-width one.

Theorem A.1

(Hoeffding’s inequality
[
8
]
)
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{p}
be independent random variables such that
a_{i}\leq X_{i}\leq b_{i}
almost surely. Consider the sum of these random variables,

S_{p}=X_{1}+\ldots+X_{p}.

Then, for all
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}[|S_{p}-\mathbb{E}[S_{p}]|\geq t]\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{2t^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{p}(b_{i}-a_{i})^{2}}\right).

(Proof of
Lemma 3.1
).

The general idea of the proof comes from
[
17
]
, which considers the case of translation-invariant kernels.

For a realization of the random variables
{\bm{V}}
and
{\bm{b}}
, define
f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}):=k_{p}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})-k_{\infty}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
for all
{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in\sqrt{d}\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
. Consider an
r
-net cover of this sphere, which by
[
23
]
consists of at most
T:=\lfloor(1+2\sqrt{d}/r)^{d}\rfloor\leq(3\sqrt{d}/r)^{d}
balls of radius
r
(
r\leq\sqrt{d}
) with centers in
\sqrt{d}\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
, and denote these centers by
\{{\bm{c}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{T}=:\mathcal{C}
.

Fix
{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in\sqrt{d}\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
. We apply here Hoeffding’s inequality (see
Theorem A.1
) with

X_{i}=\frac{1}{p}\phi({\bm{V}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{x}}+b_{i})\phi({\bm{V}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{y}}+b_{i}),

where
{\bm{V}}_{i}
denotes the
i
-th row of
{\bm{V}}
. Clearly, these random variables are independent and satisfy
X_{i}\in[-\frac{M^{2}}{p},\frac{M^{2}}{p}]
. Moreover, notice that
S_{p}=k_{p}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
and
\mathbb{E}[S_{p}]=\mathbb{E}[k_{p}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})]=k_{\infty}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
. Hence, choosing
t=\varepsilon/2
in
Theorem A.1
, there holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[|f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})|\geq\varepsilon/2]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}[|k_{p}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})-k_{\infty}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})|\geq\varepsilon/2]

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{2(\varepsilon/2)^{2}}{p(2M^{2}/p)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=2\exp\left(-\frac{p\varepsilon^{2}}{8M^{4}}\right).

Applying this inequality to each
{\bm{x}}_{c},{\bm{y}}_{c}\in\mathcal{C}
gives, by the union bound,

\mathbb{P}\left[\sup_{{\bm{x}}_{c},{\bm{y}}_{c}\in\mathcal{C}}|f({\bm{x}}_{c},{\bm{y}}_{c})|\geq\varepsilon/2\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[\bigcup_{{\bm{x}}_{c},{\bm{y}}_{c}\in\mathcal{C}}\left\{|f({\bm{x}}_{c},{\bm{y}}_{c})|\geq\varepsilon/2\right\}\right]\leq 2T^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{p\varepsilon^{2}}{8M^{4}}\right).

Moreover, note that

\nabla_{\bm{x}}k_{p}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\phi^{\prime}({\bm{V}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{x}}+b_{i})\phi({\bm{V}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{y}}+b_{i}){\bm{V}}_{i}

and

\nabla_{\bm{x}}k_{\infty}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})=\mathbb{E}[\phi^{\prime}({\bm{v}}^{\top}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{b}})\phi({\bm{v}}^{\top}{\bm{y}}+{\bm{b}}){\bm{v}}].

Hence,

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\bm{x}}f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla_{\bm{x}}k_{p}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\|+\|\nabla_{\bm{x}}k_{\infty}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}|\phi^{\prime}({\bm{V}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{b}}_{i})\phi({\bm{V}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{y}}+{\bm{b}}_{i})|\|{\bm{V}}_{i}\|+\mathbb{E}[|\phi^{\prime}({\bm{v}}^{\top}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{b}})\phi({\bm{v}}^{\top}{\bm{y}}+{\bm{b}})|\|{\bm{v}}\|]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{LM}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\|{\bm{V}}_{i}\|+LM\mathbb{E}[\|{\bm{v}}\|].

Let
L_{\bm{x}}:=\sup_{{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in\sqrt{d}\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}\|\nabla_{\bm{x}}f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\|
. It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[L_{\bm{x}}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{LM}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\mathbb{E}[\|{\bm{V}}_{i}\|]+LM\mathbb{E}[\|{\bm{v}}\|]

\displaystyle=2LM\mathbb{E}[\|{\bm{v}}\|]

\displaystyle\leq 2LM\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\|{\bm{v}}\|^{2}]}

\displaystyle=2LM.

Therefore, by Markov’s inequality,

\mathbb{P}[L_{\bm{x}}\geq\varepsilon/(4r)]\leq 4r\mathbb{E}[L_{\bm{x}}]/\varepsilon\leq(8rLM)/\varepsilon.

Additionally, notice that
L_{\bm{y}}:=\sup_{{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in\sqrt{d}\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}\|\nabla_{\bm{y}}f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\|=L_{\bm{x}}
by symmetry of the kernels. Finally, if

\sup_{{\bm{x}}_{c},{\bm{y}}_{c}\in\mathcal{C}}|f({\bm{x}}_{c},{\bm{y}}_{c})|<\varepsilon/2\quad\text{and}\quad L_{\bm{x}}<\varepsilon/(4r)

(A.1)

then, for all
{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in\sqrt{d}\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
and
{\bm{x}}^{\prime},{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
such that
\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\|\leq r
and
\|{\bm{y}}-{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\|\leq r
(which are possible to find since
\mathcal{C}
is an
r
-net cover of
\sqrt{d}\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
),

\displaystyle|f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})|

\displaystyle\leq|f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})-f({\bm{x}}^{\prime},{\bm{y}}^{\prime})|+|f({\bm{x}}^{\prime},{\bm{y}}^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq|f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})-f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime})|+|f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime})-f({\bm{x}}^{\prime},{\bm{y}}^{\prime})|+|f({\bm{x}}^{\prime},{\bm{y}}^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq L_{\bm{y}}\|{\bm{y}}-{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\|+L_{\bm{x}}\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\|+\sup_{{\bm{x}}_{c},{\bm{y}}_{c}\in\mathcal{C}}|f({\bm{x}}_{c},{\bm{y}}_{c})|

\displaystyle<2L_{\bm{x}}r+\varepsilon/2

\displaystyle<\varepsilon

and hence
\sup_{{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in\sqrt{d}\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}|f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})|\leq\varepsilon
. Condition (
A.1
) holds with probability

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\sup_{{\bm{x}}_{c},{\bm{y}}_{c}\in\mathcal{C}}|f({\bm{x}}_{c},{\bm{y}}_{c})|<\varepsilon/2\quad\text{and}\quad L_{\bm{x}}<\varepsilon/(4r)\right]

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}\left[\left\{\sup_{{\bm{x}}_{c},{\bm{y}}_{c}\in\mathcal{C}}|f({\bm{x}}_{c},{\bm{y}}_{c})|\geq\varepsilon/2\right\}\cup\left\{L_{\bm{x}}\geq\varepsilon/(4r)\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\geq 1-\mathbb{P}\left[\sup_{{\bm{x}}_{c},{\bm{y}}_{c}\in\mathcal{C}}|f({\bm{x}}_{c},{\bm{y}}_{c})|\geq\varepsilon/2\right]-\mathbb{P}[L_{\bm{x}}\geq\varepsilon/(4r)]

\displaystyle\geq 1-2T^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{p\varepsilon^{2}}{8M^{4}}\right)-(8rLM)/\varepsilon,

where we have used the union bound in the penultimate inequality. Therefore,

\mathbb{P}\left[\sup_{{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\in\sqrt{d}\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}|k_{p}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})-k_{\infty}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})|\leq\varepsilon\right]\geq 1-2\left(\frac{3\sqrt{d}}{r}\right)^{2d}\exp\left(-\frac{p\varepsilon^{2}}{8M^{4}}\right)-\frac{8rLM}{\varepsilon}.

As
0<r\leq\sqrt{d}
can vary freely, we maximize the right-hand side of the above expression with respect to
r
, by first ignoring the upper bound on
r
. In this regard, denote the right-hand sight of the above inequality by
g(r)
. We have
g(r)=1-Ar^{-2d}-Br
, with

A:=2\cdot(3\sqrt{d})^{2d}\exp\left(-\frac{p\varepsilon^{2}}{8M^{4}}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad B:=\frac{8LM}{\varepsilon},

which are both positive. Moreover,

g^{\prime}(r)=2d\frac{A}{r^{2d+1}}-B\quad\text{and}\quad g^{\prime\prime}(r)=-2d(2d+1)\frac{A}{r^{2d+2}}.

Clearly,
g^{\prime\prime}(r)<0
for all
r>0
,
\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}g(r)=\lim_{r\to+\infty}g(r)=-\infty
and
g
admits a unique critical point
r^{*}
. Hence,
r^{*}
is a global maximizer of
g
on
(0,+\infty)
and is given by

r^{*}=\left(2d\frac{A}{B}\right)^{\frac{1}{2d+1}}=\left(\frac{d(3\sqrt{d})^{2d}\varepsilon}{2LM}\right)^{\frac{1}{2d+1}}\exp\left(-\frac{p\varepsilon^{2}}{8M^{4}(2d+1)}\right).

To ensure
r^{*}\leq\sqrt{d}
,
p
should then satisfy

\left(\frac{d(3\sqrt{d})^{2d}\varepsilon}{2LM}\right)^{\frac{1}{2d+1}}\exp\left(-\frac{p\varepsilon^{2}}{8M^{4}(2d+1)}\right)\leq\sqrt{d}

\Leftrightarrow\exp\left(-\frac{p\varepsilon^{2}}{8M^{4}}\right)\leq\frac{2LM\sqrt{d}}{d3^{2d}\varepsilon}

\Leftrightarrow p\geq\frac{8M^{4}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\ln\left(\frac{d3^{2d}\varepsilon}{2LM\sqrt{d}}\right).

(A.2)

Furthermore,

\displaystyle g(r^{*})

\displaystyle=1-\left(\frac{A}{{r^{*}}^{2d+1}}+B\right)r^{*}

\displaystyle=1-\left(\frac{B}{2d}+B\right)\left(2d\frac{A}{B}\right)^{\frac{1}{2d+1}}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1+2d}{2d}B(2d)^{\frac{1}{2d+1}}\left(\frac{1}{B}\right)^{\frac{1}{2d+1}}A^{\frac{1}{2d+1}}

\displaystyle=1-(1+2d)\left(\frac{B}{2d}\right)^{\frac{2d}{2d+1}}A^{\frac{1}{2d+1}}

\displaystyle=1-(1+2d)\left(\frac{4LM}{d\varepsilon}\right)^{\frac{2d}{2d+1}}(2\cdot(3\sqrt{d})^{2d})^{\frac{1}{2d+1}}\exp\left(-\frac{p\varepsilon^{2}}{8M^{4}(2d+1)}\right)

\displaystyle=1-(1+2d)\left(\frac{2LM}{d\varepsilon}\right)^{\frac{2d}{2d+1}}2^{\frac{2d}{2d+1}}2^{\frac{1}{2d+1}}(3\sqrt{d})^{\frac{2d}{2d+1}}\exp\left(-\frac{p\varepsilon^{2}}{8M^{4}(2d+1)}\right)

\displaystyle=1-(2+4d)\left(\frac{6LM\sqrt{d}}{d\varepsilon}\right)^{\frac{2d}{2d+1}}\exp\left(-\frac{p\varepsilon^{2}}{8M^{4}(2d+1)}\right).

For
g(r^{*})
to be larger than
1-\delta
, where
\delta>0
,
p
should then satisfy

1-(2+4d)\left(\frac{6LM\sqrt{d}}{d\varepsilon}\right)^{\frac{2d}{2d+1}}\exp\left(-\frac{p\varepsilon^{2}}{8M^{4}(2d+1)}\right)\geq 1-\delta

\Leftrightarrow\frac{\delta}{2+4d}\left(\frac{d\varepsilon}{6LM\sqrt{d}}\right)^{\frac{2d}{2d+1}}\geq\exp\left(-\frac{p\varepsilon^{2}}{8M^{4}(2d+1)}\right)

\Leftrightarrow\ln\left(\frac{\delta}{2+4d}\right)+\frac{2d}{2d+1}\ln\left(\frac{d\varepsilon}{6LM\sqrt{d}}\right)\geq-\frac{p\varepsilon^{2}}{8M^{4}(2d+1)}

\Leftrightarrow p\geq\frac{8M^{4}(2d+1)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\ln\left(\frac{2+4d}{\delta}\right)+\frac{2d}{2d+1}\ln\left(\frac{6LM\sqrt{d}}{d\varepsilon}\right)\right).

For
r^{*}
to be less than
\sqrt{d}
, it is therefore enough to choose
p
as above and a
\delta
such that the right-hand side of the above inequality is larger than the one of (
A.2
), i.e.,

\frac{8M^{4}(2d+1)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\ln\left(\frac{2+4d}{\delta}\right)+\frac{2d}{2d+1}\ln\left(\frac{6LM\sqrt{d}}{d\varepsilon}\right)\right)\geq\frac{8M^{4}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\ln\left(\frac{d3^{2d}\varepsilon}{2LM\sqrt{d}}\right)

\Leftrightarrow(2d+1)\ln\left(\frac{2+4d}{\delta}\right)\geq\ln\left(\frac{d3^{2d}\varepsilon}{2LM\sqrt{d}}\right)-2d\ln\left(\frac{6LM\sqrt{d}}{d\varepsilon}\right)

\Leftrightarrow(2d+1)\ln\left(\frac{2+4d}{\delta}\right)\geq\ln\left(\frac{d3^{2d}\varepsilon}{2LM\sqrt{d}}\left(\frac{d\varepsilon}{6LM\sqrt{d}}\right)^{2d}\right)

\Leftrightarrow(2d+1)\ln\left(\frac{2+4d}{\delta}\right)\geq\ln\left(\left(\frac{d\varepsilon}{2LM\sqrt{d}}\right)^{2d+1}\right)

\Leftrightarrow\ln\left(\frac{2+4d}{\delta}\right)\geq\ln\left(\frac{d\varepsilon}{2LM\sqrt{d}}\right)

\Leftrightarrow\delta\leq\frac{4(1+2d)LM}{\sqrt{d}\varepsilon},

which concludes the proof.
∎

(Proof of
Theorem 3.1
).

If
p
satisfies condition (
3.4
) in
Lemma 3.1
, then, with probability
1-\delta
, using (
3.2
), we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}(\hat{\bm{X}},\hat{\bm{\alpha}})

\displaystyle=\|{\bm{\theta}}^{*}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\alpha}_{i}\varphi(\hat{\bm{x}}_{i})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\varphi({\bm{x}}_{i})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\alpha}_{i}\varphi(\hat{\bm{x}}_{i})\right\|_{2}^{2}

Appendix B
Additional information on numerical experiments

Here we provide further details of the numerical experiments. We will release the code used to conduct the experiments upon publication.

Our experiments were conducted on a range of servers. Smaller training and reconstruction tasks (typically
p\leq 1000
) were conducted on a 64 core CPU system. Larger experiments were conducted on systems with either 4x RTX 3080ti GPUs or 4x Nvidia H100 GPUs. The latter being required due to the memory requirements of the all-layer reconstruction method for the widest networks.

Computing the loss function

We use the same observation regarding the symmetry and idempotence of
{\bm{P}}_{{\bm{G}}}^{\perp}
from
[
9
]
, that

\displaystyle\|{\bm{P}}_{{\bm{G}}}^{\perp}(\Delta{\bm{\theta}})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=(\Delta{\bm{\theta}})^{\top}(\Delta{\bm{\theta}})-(\Delta{\bm{\theta}})^{\top}{\bm{G}}^{\top}({\bm{G}}{\bm{G}}^{\top})^{-1}{\bm{G}}(\Delta{\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle=(\Delta{\bm{\theta}})^{\top}(\Delta{\bm{\theta}})-(\Delta{\bm{\theta}})^{\top}{\bm{G}}^{\top}{\bm{\alpha}},

where
{\bm{\alpha}}
solves

({\bm{G}}{\bm{G}}^{\top}){\bm{\alpha}}={\bm{G}}(\Delta{\bm{\theta}}).

(B.1)

For deep networks, including those with multiclass outputs, computing the matrix
{\bm{G}}
is likely to be infeasible.
In these cases, we may make use of Jacobian-vector and vector-Jacobian products, both of which are implemented in PyTorch. Specifically, we can compute
{\bm{G}}(\Delta{\bm{\theta}})
through a Jacobian-vector product. To solve (
B.1
), we can make use of the conjugate gradient (CG) method. The CG method requires the ability to compute matrix-vector products with
{\bm{G}}{\bm{G}}^{\top}
. For a given vector
{\bm{v}}
, we may compute
{\bm{G}}{\bm{G}}^{\top}{\bm{v}}
through two Jacobian-vector products as

({\bm{G}}{\bm{G}}^{\top}){\bm{v}}={\bm{G}}{\bm{u}}
for
{\bm{u}}=({\bm{v}}^{\top}{\bm{G}})^{\top}
.

For reconstruction methods using the final layer only, it is typically feasible to explicitly form the matrix
{\bm{G}}{\bm{G}}^{\top}
and avoid using the CG method, which leads to cost savings and greater scalability of the method.
The gradient of the loss with respect to the reconstruction data
\hat{{\bm{X}}}
can be computed via automatic differentiation.

There is a clear trade-off between the memory requirements. Methods that explicitly compute
{\bm{G}}
have a greater memory requirement than methods that use CG. However, these methods run significantly faster.
