 Executive Summary
 Manufactured homes represent one of the most affordable paths to home ownership for
 American households, but minimum-efficiency equipment and poor-quality home
 installation can lead to excess energy use and high operating costs. Heating,
 ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC) systems included in industry-standard
 manufactured homes can be characterized as low-cost systems with high operating
 costs that deliver conditioned air through ductwork prone to leakage. Until the
 introduction of Clayton’s Zero Energy Ready Home (ZERH) line 1 in 2023, the vast
 majority of the roughly 100,000 new manufactured homes shipped annually were
 equipped with minimum-efficiency gas or electric furnaces, and many remain this way
 today. Errors in the home-siting process—along with occasional damage during
 transport—can lead to envelope and duct leakage issues that go undetected unless
 they are severe enough to trigger a customer complaint due to moisture issues, poor
 comfort, or high energy bills.

 The objective and challenge of this project, which is funded by the U.S. Department of
 Energy’s Building Technologies Office, is to find solutions that increase the HVAC
 performance of manufactured homes and that are attractive to manufacturers, dealers,
 and buyers without significantly slowing down home production or substantially raising
 the purchase price.

 Slipstream, with partners from the Florida Solar Energy Center (FSEC), Northwest
 Energy Works, and Washington State University, identified and tested manufactured
 home HVAC innovations targeted for new construction offering the most promising mix
 of improved energy performance and likelihood of industry uptake. The project team
 focused on answering two research questions:

 1. What manufactured home HVAC innovations targeted for new construction offer
 the most promising mix of improved energy performance and likelihood of
 industry uptake?
 2. Among the most promising innovations, how much energy performance can be
 achieved and what are the pathways for industry uptake?

 To answer these research questions, the project team: (1) implemented a feasibility
 assessment that identified the most promising innovations based on market drivers and
 barriers, energy impacts, and cost-effectiveness; (2) implemented proof-of-concept
 testing and field trials with industry partners on the most promising innovations to further
 demonstrate feasibility; and (3) provided detailed guidance to the manufactured home
 industry for widespread adoption of HVAC-related innovation.

 Methodology
 The feasibility assessment focused on identifying, assessing, and recommending HVAC
 innovations for new manufactured homes for field testing (Stendel et al. 2021). The
 team performed a literature review to understand the major influences in the
 manufactured home industry, interviewed 41 industry market actors, and analyzed cost-
 effectiveness using EnergyPlus® 9.5 with IDF input files generated by BEopt 2.8.

 We developed two prototypes to model and analyze cost-effectiveness: a single section
 (aka, “single-wide”) and a two section (aka, “double-wide”) with improvement analysis
 compared against a baseline of typical current construction by a majority of
 manufacturers (Gu et al. 2024). We chose weather characteristics from eight cities to
 represent a combination of U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD)
 thermal zones and Building America climate zones.

 We assessed 13 potential innovations and selected four for field testing: (1) partial
 factory install of ducted heat pumps; (2) improved HVAC quality assurance protocols;
 (3) improved crossover duct designs; and (4) comparative testing of different crossover
 approaches. We further analyzed these four innovations in EnergyPlus using the same
 prototypes and cities but comparing a HUD home manufacturer current practice
 baseline model to the U.S. Department of Energy’s (DOE’s) ENH Manufactured Homes
 Version 1 requirements that included the creation of a “belly” zone, peak hour energy
 use, and estimated annual carbon dioxide equivalent associated with higher-efficiency
 HVAC systems and improved building envelopes.

 Of the four innovations, ducted heat pump options showed the highest impact, with
 potential HVAC energy cost savings in the range of 10% to almost 60% compared to
 manufactured homes with electric-resistance heating. Ducted heat pump innovations
 are likely to be cost-effective, though this varies on a regional basis. In many parts of
 the country, natural gas prices are lower than electricity prices, and thus our analysis
 finds that the operating cost for even an advanced heat pump (high-efficiency and
 variable-speed) is higher than that of an 80% efficient natural gas furnace.

 Duct-sealing-related innovations have estimated energy savings that are generally less
 than 10% of space-conditioning costs. However, the duct-related innovations are likely
 to be less expensive than, say, upgrading to a heat pump, so they can still be cost-
 effective. Crossovers used to connect duct systems for multi-section homes are a
 particular point of vulnerability, though the potential market for crossover innovation is
 limited to the 43% of the market that is multi-section homes.

 Field Test Results
 Each of the four innovations underwent some form of field testing. Labor and material
 supply chain issues from the COVID-19 pandemic and the phaseout of
 hydrofluorocarbon refrigerants affected the project team’s work with industry partners on
 some of the innovations.

 Partial Factory Install of Ducted Heat Pumps
 This innovation involves installing an air handler matched for use with a high-efficiency
 variable-capacity heat pump compressor, a chaseway for refrigerant and condensate
 lines, and indoor heat pump coil at the factory. The matching outdoor compressor model
 information is shipped with the home for local HVAC contractors to reference. It is a
 potential near-term solution to the technical and market barriers to full factory
 installation of high-efficiency heat pumps. This approach helps ensure that the local on-
 site HVAC contractors will install the equipment intended by the home manufacturer,
 avoiding situations in which a heat pump may not be installed at all, or may be
 oversized by local installers due to lack of confidence in the home’s thermal
 performance.

 We were unable to fully field test this innovation due to HVAC supply chain issues and
 limited factory availability. However, we believe it is promising based on results from
 monitoring a ducted heat pump in an occupied home in Oregon (sited and built with a
 refrigerant line chaseway suitable for a heat pump installation) and one at the FSEC
 Manufactured Housing Laboratory. We monitored electricity consumption, supply-and-
 return air temperature and humidity, and temperature and humidity in various rooms for
 about 6 months with the same model of heat pump in both locations. For the Oregon
 home, we also conducted an analysis of only auxiliary electric-resistance heat system
 operation for a few cold weeks to compare heat pump performance with a conventional
 electric-resistance-based heating system. The technical heat pump analysis on the field
 monitoring data showed:

 • The Carrier heat pump 40MBAA installed in a new Clayton manufactured home
 in Oregon (cold climate) effectively managed the entire heating load throughout
 the winter, with minimal reliance on backup electric-resistance heat. The heat
 pump showed over 60% energy savings in heating (kilowatt-hours) compared to
 electric-resistance heat at this site.
 • The heat pumps at both sites were generally able to maintain comfortable supply
 air conditions.
 • This heat pump model showed significant power modulation in heating mode as
 expected from variable-capacity systems. There was very limited power
 modulation in cooling mode. The air handler model tested in this study was
 discontinued by the manufacturer in June 2022 and replaced with a second-
 generation model of the unit, 40MBAB, which remains in production.

 We also explored a “plug-and-play” refrigeration connection system for this innovation
 that would eliminate the need for an HVAC technician to be on-site during home setup.
 Results are promising based on a proof-of-concept test for a “plug-and-play” precharged
 line set with reusable, quick-connect couplings by Excel Air, that is heavy duty and
 designed to connect and disconnect without losing refrigerant. However, the line set
 coupling manufacturer is currently navigating the approval process for using it with the
 new refrigerants replacing hydrofluorocarbon refrigerants and needs more time to work

 Improved HVAC Quality Assurance Protocols
 Because duct leakage testing could help reveal envelope and duct leakage issues that
 would lead to HVAC performance issues, we evaluated a simplified protocol for
 assessing envelope and duct leakage intended for home installers and inspectors. The
 protocol is designed to be quick and easy to implement using low-cost, easy-to-carry
 equipment, and would require minimal training—especially if implemented as a mobile
 device app in the future. The protocol uses existing home exhaust fans (e.g., bath,
 kitchen range hood, whole-house exhaust) to depressurize the home and assess
 envelope leakage based on the level of depressurization. Duct leakage is assessed
 similarly by measuring the level of home depressurization during air handler operation
 when no return ducts are present (most manufactured homes). If exhaust-fan flow rates
 associated with the assessment are also measured, the protocol can produce
 quantitative estimates of building envelope and duct leakage and measurements of
 ventilation fan air flow rates. The protocol is meant to help home installers and
 inspectors easily flag serious envelope and duct leakage issues in the field that could
 otherwise lead to discomfort and performance issues that result in callbacks or even
 health hazards.

 We conducted proof-of-concept testing of the protocol and evaluated it against standard
 blower-door and duct-pressurization testing with an automated setup at the unoccupied
 FSEC lab home that allowed for repeated measurement under a variety of exhaust-flow,
 duct leakage, and wind conditions. It was also field evaluated in 35 homes in the
 Midwest, Southeast, and Northwest, selected based on project partner locations (Pigg,
 Stendel, and Cautley 2023). The results from this testing showed that with sufficient
 exhaust flow and reasonably calm conditions, the protocol did a good job of estimating
 envelope and duct leakage on average; however, low-exhaust fan flows combined with
 windy conditions resulted in estimates with more uncertainty. The protocol appears to
 be most suitable for efficient new homes that are expected to have airtight envelopes
 (<4 air changes per hour at 50 pascals [ACH50]) and that have 100 to 200 cubic feet
 per minute of total existing exhaust-fan capacity. To be useful in conventionally
 constructed manufactured homes with leakier envelopes and lower existing exhaust-fan
 capacity, supplementary exhaust flow during evaluation would be needed in most
 cases.

 We also put together a manufactured home factory duct leakage testing guide as a
 resource to support the manufactured home industry (Slipstream 2023b). Factory-floor
 testing using a duct leakage tester (e.g., Duct Blaster) can be used on the production
 line to test the ducts in every home section as it gets built, making it a basic early
 function check of the duct system’s airtightness. The test can be performed in under 3
 minutes when the home is at the bare floor stage, and before the walls are installed.

 The minimal costs of factory-floor duct leakage testing can be offset to some extent by
 reducing callbacks.

 Improved Crossover Duct Designs
 Crossover duct failures are a significant source of comfort complaints and energy waste
 in multi-section homes. This innovation strove to improve the design of the crossover
 duct connection required for multi-section homes by employing metal elbow
 connectors and a superinsulated flex duct-in-duct approach. Our project team proved it
 was able to create a superinsulated flex duct by putting a 12-inch-diameter, nominal R-8
 duct inside a 16-inch-diameter, nominal R-8 duct for a total R-16 flex duct assembly.

 There is a potential technical installation issue regarding the size of the superinsulated
 flex duct and the height clearance in the crawl space. When homes are set with
 minimum allowed clearance underneath, the crossover duct can become a tight
 squeeze between the chassis and the ground. To address this issue, it could be
 possible to have ductwork lying on a plastic crawl space cover without needing to strap
 the duct every 4 feet, which could improve airflow. The project team initiated a
 conversation with a flex duct manufacturer serving the manufactured home industry, to
 explore this innovation, but they were not interested in pursuing it further. More research
 is needed to explore new ways to improve crossover duct designs in manufactured
 homes.

 Comparative Testing of Different Crossover Approaches
 Manufacturers, installers, and dealers have many opinions, but limited data are
 available on the strengths and weaknesses of through-the-rim versus traditional flex-
 duct crossovers for multi-section homes. We hoped to identify and test homes with both
 types of duct systems for leakage in various regions in the United States to
 compare field air delivery with factory duct design calculations. However, we were not
 able to identify enough multi-section homes in the simple protocol testing process to
 have valuable data to support this analysis. A future study focused on measuring the
 real-world performance for duct leakage and durability on through-the-rim vs. traditional
 flex-duct crossover ductwork for multi-section homes over time would be valuable to the
 manufactured home industry. If the through-the-rim approach can be shown to be less
 leak-prone over time, more thermally efficient, and provide good air distribution
 throughout the home, it could spur wider adoption in the industry.

 Conclusions
 Manufactured homes are an important source of affordable housing in the United
 States. Below are the major findings from this work:

 • Quality assurance protocols used in home setup processes for tighter homes
 with high levels of exhaust-fan flow that can identify envelope and/or duct

 leakage issues before the home is occupied, can result in reduced expenses
 from customer callbacks. 2
 • Heat pump preparation packages that include installing a chaseway for
 refrigerant and condensate lines that is designed to reduce damage to the
 home’s belly (the enclosed area beneath the subfloor containing the duct system,
 plumbing, insulation, and road barrier) when the heat pump is installed
 (Slipstream 2023a) can ease installation challenges.
 • Partial installation of heat pumps in the factory would enable more heat pump
 adoption (Slipstream 2023a).
 • Building homes with ducts in the attic in thermal zones 1 and 2 often leads to
 reduced energy performance.
 • There are research gaps surrounding crossover duct designs in manufactured
 homes.
 • There are research gaps surrounding the real-world performance and durability

 multi-section homes.

 1 Introduction
 Manufactured homes represent one of the most affordable paths to home ownership for
 American households. Ten million such homes have been built since 1976, when
 federal regulation of the industry began under the U.S. Department of Housing and
 Urban Development (HUD; 1994). HUD-code manufactured homes, which may be
 single or multi-section, are built on a permanent metal chassis in one of 145 dedicated
 facilities around the country, sold through a network of dealers, then transported and set
 up at their final location (Revere 2023). According to the U.S. Census manufactured
 housing survey, home shipments in recent years have averaged around 100,000 per
 year.

 The manufactured home industry operates in a market wherein buyers tend to be highly
 price sensitive, and one of the key attractions of a manufactured home is that a person
 can own one for less than half the cost per square foot of a site-built home (Revere
 2023). However, residents of manufactured homes spend more on energy per square
 foot to heat and cool their homes than site-built homes (Talbot 2012).

 The heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC) systems currently included in
 industry-standard manufactured homes can be characterized as low-cost systems with
 high operating costs that deliver conditioned air through ductwork prone to leakage.
 Manufactured home ductwork is typically located in the floor assembly—above the floor
 insulation and road barrier, in a “belly” zone—making it difficult to access for future
 repairs.

 Most new manufactured homes without above-code certifications are shipped with
 minimum-efficiency gas or electric furnaces. Errors in the home-siting process—along
 with occasional damage during transport—can lead to envelope and duct leakage
 issues that go undetected unless they are severe enough to trigger a customer
 complaint due to moisture issues, poor comfort, or high energy bills. Local HVAC
 contractors are sometimes involved with the home setup process, such as when a
 home receives a central air conditioner or heat pump that is installed on-site. The
 challenge is finding pathways to improved manufactured home HVAC performance that
 are attractive to manufacturers, dealers, and buyers without significantly slowing down
 home production or substantially raising the purchase price.

 Slipstream, with partners from the University of Central Florida—Florida Solar Energy
 Center (FSEC), Northwest Energy Works, and Washington State University, explored
 and tested manufactured home HVAC innovations offering the most promising mix of
 improved energy performance and likelihood of industry uptake. Funded under DOE’s
 Building Technology Office’s Advanced Building Construction initiative, the project team
 focused on answering the following two research questions:

 1. What manufactured home HVAC innovations targeted for new construction offer
 the most promising mix of improved energy performance and likelihood of
 industry uptake?

 2. Among the most promising innovations, how much energy performance can be
 achieved and what are the pathways for industry uptake?

 The project objectives to support answering the research questions were to:

 • Implement a feasibility assessment that identifies the most promising innovations
 based on market drivers and barriers, energy impacts, and cost-effectiveness.
 • Implement proof-of-concept testing and field trials on the most promising
 innovations to further demonstrate feasibility.
 • Provide detailed guidance to the manufactured home industry for widespread
 adoption of effective HVAC-related innovations.

 Innovations in HVAC systems could improve energy efficiency, durability, and indoor air
 quality to benefit manufactured home residents and industry. The team initially
 considered HVAC-related innovations for new manufactured homes in two broad
 categories: duct-system improvements and increased adoption of properly installed heat
 pumps for space conditioning. We hypothesized that advances in heat pump technology
 and duct design and testing techniques could save 30% in manufactured home annual
 energy costs even when compared to an above-code manufactured home baseline and
 could reduce duct leakage by 75% compared to homes built to typical industry practice
 for HUD code, with as little as a 5% increase in the purchase cost of a new
 manufactured home.

 2 Methodology
 The project’s research had three main components: (1) an up-front feasibility
 assessment to identify HVAC innovations with the most promise for energy efficiency
 improvement and industry uptake; (2) modeling and cost-effectiveness analysis to
 inform HVAC innovation selection and impact; and (3) innovation proof of concept and
 field testing. The following sections summarize our research methodology, data
 collection approach, and analysis methods for each of these components.

 2.1 Feasibility Assessment
 Research partners conducted an initial project feasibility assessment focused on
 identifying, assessing, and recommending HVAC innovations for new manufactured
 homes for field testing (Stendel et al. 2021). We started by performing a literature
 review to understand the major influences in this industry, then interviewed various
 industry market actors, and assessed the feasibility of the innovations based on that
 information combined with the cost-effectiveness modeling analysis.

 Great Recession. In recent years, sales have slowly risen to about 100,000 homes per
 year, while ENERGY STAR rated manufactured home production has increased its
 market share (Figure 2).

 Ductwork
 Ductwork in manufactured homes is typically located in the home’s floor assembly
 (Figure 6 and Figure 12). In-floor ductwork is located above the floor insulation and road
 barrier, in a “belly” zone that has high thermal regain into the home.

U.S. Department of Energy | Office of Energy Efficiency & Renewable Energy                               6

 Figure 6. Manufactured home ductwork in a floor assembly on a factory floor.
 All report photos by Brady Peeks, Northwest Energy Works, unless otherwise noted

 This is very different than under-floor duct systems in site-built construction, in which the
 ducts are typically located in unconditioned space below the floor insulation.
 Manufactured homes are typically built with a trunk duct in each floor, made of either
 continuously formed sheet metal or fiberglass duct board. The ductwork in each floor of
 a multi-section home is connected by what is called a crossover duct, which is either a
 flexible duct run below the home to connect each home section’s trunk duct together or
 ductwork that runs from the trunk duct and extends through the marriage line rim joist.
 Flexible crossover ducts under the home can be vulnerable to damage by animals,
 service technicians, flooding, and general deterioration.

 However, in some cooling-dominated areas, the duct system is built into the attic of
 each home section. Ducts in the attic are likely to have a lower HVAC distribution
 efficiency because—unlike floor ducts—they run outside the thermal envelope of the
 home. In manufactured homes with “overhead ducts,” the crossover connection
 between supply plenums in adjacent sections is made “through the ridge.” In this
 context, “ridge” refers to the attic framing at the marriage line that is akin to a ridge
 beam in its location under the peak of the roof. This approach is something of a hybrid
 between a traditional flex-duct crossover and the through-the-rim approach used for
 under-floor duct systems. Connections to flexible ductwork in the attic are not readily
 accessible for repair or inspection.

U.S. Department of Energy | Office of Energy Efficiency & Renewable Energy                               7

 Ducts located in the attic are the norm in Florida, and in parts of Texas and Arizona.
 They are offered as a standard or optional practice by some manufacturers in South
 Carolina, Alabama, Kentucky, and Louisiana. In the Southeast, there is a degree of
 flexibility at the point of sale, depending on the model, that allows buyers to choose floor
 or overhead ducts. Buyer selection of overhead ducts may be driven by a desire to
 avoid blocking supply registers and customer perceptions that having ducts in the attic
 make the homes appear more like site-built homes in cooling-dominated climates where
 ceiling ducts are the norm. This practice echoes duct placement in the general housing
 stock wherein slab on grade foundations is the norm. Housing developers strive to build
 homes that look as site-built as possible.

 In rare cases, homes ship from the factory with an external package unit, often a heat
 pump. These units have flexible ducts running through the crawl space, similar to
 crossover ducts, from the package unit to each home section’s ductwork. In addition,
 the home will have a return grill in the floor that is connected by flexible ductwork to the
 package unit.

 Industry Stakeholder Engagement
 The research team focused its interviews and stakeholder engagement on industry
 stakeholders who are directly involved in building, selling, and setting up the HVAC
 system or new home. The manufactured home and general HVAC industries were
 experiencing supply chain disruptions at the time of engagement in 2021 that impacted
 the ability to get the materials needed to build homes and provide HVAC equipment. For
 example, several manufactured home plants were having ongoing difficulty getting
 flexible ducting and insulation products from their regular distributors. Manufactured
 home plants engaged for this research report having purchased insulation from retail
 home improvement box stores and online resellers to prevent home production
 slowdowns. These supply chain issues and higher material costs increased the price of
 new manufactured homes at the time and continue to do so, though to a lesser extent at
 the time of report publication.

 At the same time, the manufactured home industry was seeing a high volume of home
 orders causing long customer wait times for home deliveries. Plants that previously
 fulfilled orders for new HUD-code homes in weeks had wait times of more than 12

 challenges to their daily operations and had limited time to focus on research,

 Innovation Assessment
 Our assessment process of potential innovations followed a structured approach that
 considered the following four key attributes:

 1. Energy savings potential. Innovations that offer significant national or regional
 energy savings potential are a higher priority than those with less technical
 potential for savings.
 2. Energy cost-effectiveness. Innovations that are cost-effective in energy terms
 are more likely to be adopted than those in which incremental costs exceed
 expected lifetime energy cost savings, and innovations with shorter payback
 periods are more likely to be adopted than those with longer paybacks—though
 we also recognized that other motivators, such as improved comfort or durability,
 are often very important factors as well.
 3. Market motivators and barriers. Innovations with notable non-energy market
 drivers and few market barriers are more feasible than those that lack additional
 drivers or face other challenges to bring to market.
 4. Market readiness. Innovations that can be readily deployed at scale are more
 immediately feasible than those that require additional development. We judged
 the market readiness of each innovation according to the degree to which it
 involves existing products, services, and market infrastructure versus needing
 additional development in any of these areas.

 Industry stakeholders provided input on processes, costs, HVAC considerations, and
 feedback on the proposed HVAC innovations. We developed a detailed characterization
 of—and weighting factors for—the new manufactured homes market (e.g., regional
 sales, home type, HVAC system type and fuel, and duct system characteristics). We ran
 energy models (see Section 2.3) to represent the variety of new manufactured homes
 across the country and estimated the energy savings for various innovations relative to
 current industry practice.

 We initially considered HVAC-related innovations for new manufactured homes in two
 broad categories: duct-system improvements and increased adoption of heat pumps for
 space conditioning. Table 2 shows the full list of 13 potential innovations that we
 ultimately vetted. Items in bold were part of our starting list of potential innovations; the
 others came up in discussions with industry stakeholders. In two cases (improved
 crossover duct designs and factory-enabled heat pumps), we expanded an original

 2.2 Innovation Testing and Industry Engagement
 The following describes our approach to testing, analyzing data, and engaging with the
 industry for each of the four innovations selected for proof of concept or field testing

 Partial Factory Install of Ducted Heat Pumps and Heat-Pump-Ready Furnaces
 We explored a potential near-term solution to technical and market barriers to full
 factory installation of high-efficiency heat pumps in the form of partial factory installation
 of a heat pump—in which an air handler matched for use with a high-efficiency variable-
 capacity heat pump, a chaseway for refrigerant and condensate lines, and indoor heat
 pump coil are installed at the factory and the matching outdoor compressor model
 information is shipped with the home for local HVAC contractors to reference. With this
 approach, the factory retains nearly the same HVAC performance risks as with the
 furnace alone and increases their potential profit because they are installing part of the
 heat pump in the factory. The HVAC performance risk may be reduced because
 properly sized equipment is installed on-site and a local contractor is involved with the
 heat pump system installation and servicing. By having the local HVAC contractor
 engaged in equipment setup, we expect them to be more likely to want to service the
 equipment, which is an important part of the business model of most HVAC contractors.
 Homeowners would benefit from good HVAC performance because the heat pump is
 properly sized and is a high-efficiency, variable-capacity system. They would also
 benefit from a cost standpoint because the contractor can access the original equipment
 manufacturer compressor match for the furnace and indoor coil already in the home
 without first needing to remove a base-efficiency, factory-supplied air handler.

 We were unable to fully field test the partial install of a heat pump in the factory
 approach, due to HVAC supply chain issues and limited factory availability. However,
 we believe it is promising based on the ducted heat pump model that we monitored in
 an occupied home in Oregon and at the FSEC Manufactured Housing Laboratory. The
 home in Oregon was already built prepared for a heat pump installation with an
 electrical circuit for the outdoor compressor, a refrigerant line set, and condensate
 chaseway that prevented damage to the home’s belly when the heat pump was installed
 (Slipstream 2023a). The home was already sited, and we worked with a local HVAC
 contractor to first setup the heat pump’s air handler as a stand-alone electric furnace
 and substitute it for the original furnace, making it as similar as possible to how it might
 have come from the factory if we had been able to get the equipment in time for it to be
 installed when the home was built in the factory. The technicians then reconfigured the
 electric furnace to operate as a heat pump air handler, installed the heat pump
 compressor, commissioned the system, and set the aux lockout temperature to 35°F.
 We consulted with the installation technicians to get their impressions of the equipment
 and how factory partial installation might affect future installations.
U.S. Department of Energy | Office of Energy Efficiency & Renewable Energy                                11

 To demonstrate heat pump performance in manufactured homes in cold and hot-humid
 climates, we tracked electricity consumption, supply-and-return air temperature and
 humidity, and temperature and humidity in various rooms for about 6 months of field
 monitoring of the same heat pump model in both locations. For the Oregon home, we
 also had a few weeks of only auxiliary electric-resistance heat system operation to
 compare heat pump performance with a conventional electric-resistance-based heating
 system.

 For this innovation we also explored a “plug-and-play” refrigeration connection system.
 If we could eliminate the need for HVAC technicians on-site during home setup by
 improving refrigerant connections, home manufacturers could streamline heat pump
 installation, control equipment selection and capture value from the sale of heat pump
 equipment. Equipment servicing could be simplified if manufactured home service
 technicians could “unplug” faulty components in the heat pump system and “plug in”
 new or refurbished equipment, without needing to handle refrigerant, and then take the
 faulty components back to a service facility for reconditioning.

 We performed a proof-of-concept test for a “plug-and-play” precharged line set by Excel
 Air (Figure 6). The line set is similar to a heavy-duty hydraulic hose, in that it is resistant
 to kinking or other physical damage and its couplers are designed to connect and
 disconnect without losing refrigerant (Figure 7 and Figure 8). The line set can be longer
 than needed with any excess line set coiled together, and it does not need to match the
 exact length for how it will be used. We used a refrigerant leakage tester with a rated
 sensitivity better than 0.03 ounces per year and detected no refrigerant leakage
 throughout the system, except a small amount briefly detectable at the coupling while
 the connection was being made or broken (Figure 9). The coupler design relies on hand
 threading the two halves of the coupling body until a lock ring springs into position,
 giving positive feedback to the user that a proper connection has been achieved.
 Disconnection involves pulling down on the lock ring and unthreading the coupling.

 This approach builds on heat pump preparation work done by the home manufacturer
 during production, which includes installing a chaseway (Figure 10 and Figure 11)
 through the furnace closet floor and extending beyond the bottom of the floor assembly.
 The purpose of this chaseway is to provide a pathway for refrigerant piping and control
 wiring to the outdoor unit and possibly the condensate line as well, thereby preventing
 damage to the home’s floor system during heat pump installation.

 Improved HVAC Quality Assurance Protocols
 We evaluated a simplified protocol for assessing envelope and duct leakage intended
 for home installers and inspectors. The protocol is based on a technique used by Brady
 Peeks (Northwest Energy Works) and Bobby Parks (Healthy Homes of Louisiana) when
 evaluating homes for quality control or complaint-resolution purposes. The protocol is a
 quick way to assess whether it is worth the time and effort to conduct standard blower-
 door and duct-pressurization testing on homes. They found that if a home with all doors
 and windows closed became noticeably depressurized relative to the outdoors when all
 exhaust fans were turned on, it was unlikely that blower-door testing would reveal major
 envelope leakage. In effect, the exhaust fans in the home act like a miniature built-in
 blower door.

 The protocol is designed to be quick and easy to implement using low-cost, easy-to-
 carry equipment, and would require minimal training—especially if implemented as a
 mobile device app in the future. The protocol uses existing home exhaust fans (e.g.,
 bath, kitchen range hood, whole-house exhaust) to depressurize the home and assess
 envelope leakage based on the level of depressurization. If exhaust-fan flow rates
 associated with the assessment are also measured, the protocol can produce
 quantitative estimates of building envelope and duct leakage and measurements of
 ventilation fan air flow rates. The protocol is meant to help home installers and
 inspectors easily flag serious envelope and duct leakage issues in the field that could
 otherwise lead to discomfort and performance issues that result in callbacks or even
 health hazards.

 Duct leakage is assessed similarly by measuring the level of home depressurization
 during air handler operation when no return ducts are present (most manufactured
 homes). A unique aspect of manufactured home duct systems is they typically only
 have ducts for delivering supply air to rooms and no return ductwork in unconditioned
 space. Any duct-system leaks will thus be on the supply side of the system and will tend
 to depressurize the home. This contrasts with the much trickier situation found with site-
 built homes where both supply and return ducts are present, and where leaks on one
 side of the system may be offset by leaks on the other side, resulting in a home with a
 leaky duct system that nonetheless shows little pressure response when the air handler
 is operated.

 We conducted proof-of-concept testing of the protocol in October 2021 in an occupied
 home in the Pacific Northwest and in the FSEC Manufactured Housing Laboratory. The
 protocol was evaluated against standard blower-door and duct-pressurization testing
 with an automated setup at the unoccupied FSEC lab home that allowed for repeated
 measurement under a variety of exhaust-flow, duct leakage, and wind conditions. It was
 also evaluated in the field in 35 additional homes in the Midwest, Southeast, and
 Northwest that were selected based on project partner locations (Pigg, Stendel, and
 Cautley 2023).

 Improved Crossover Duct Designs
 Our project team proved it was able to create a super insulated flex duct by putting a
 12-inch-diameter, nominal R-8 duct inside a 16-inch-diameter nominal, R-8 duct for a
 total nominal R16 flex duct assembly. We talked with industry stakeholders about a
 technical installation issue regarding height clearance in the crawl space when homes
 are set with a minimum allowed clearance underneath, the crossover duct can become
 a tight squeeze between the chassis and the ground. Initial conversations with industry
 stakeholders suggest that due to the increased diameter of the super insulated flex duct
 compared to traditional products, it could be possible to have ductwork lying on a plastic
 crawl space cover without needing to strap the duct every 4 feet, which could improve
 airflow. Eliminating the strapping could reduce installation labor and material costs and
 improve airflow because strapping the ductwork, if not done properly, can cause kinks in
 the ductwork, thereby restricting airflow.

 2.3 Modeling and Cost-Effectiveness
 We conducted an extensive modeling and cost-effectiveness analysis using EnergyPlus
 9.5 with IDF input files generated by BEopt 2.8. We conducted the analysis in two
 phases with two separate technical reports summarizing the efforts and results (Pigg et
 al. 2021 and Gu et al. 2024). Phase one modeling included the development of single-
 wide and double-wide prototypes with improvement analysis based against the 1994
 HUD-code envelope that was used in the feasibility assessment. We chose weather
 characteristics from eight cities during phase one to represent a combination of HUD
 thermal zones and Building America climate zones (Figure 16). Phase two used the
 same prototypes and cities from the first phase with a HUD-code manufacturer current
 practice baseline model compared to the latest ENH requirements.

 Several innovations focused on ducts and duct leakage, which we modeled by altering
 the quantity of duct leakage, the insulation value of the ducts, and the location of the
 ductwork. Heat pump modeling used an existing EnergyPlus model after entering key
 performance parameters.

 Phase two modeling included creating a zone representing the “belly,” an improved
 envelope based on the ENH program, and peak hour energy use and estimated annual
 carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2e). The belly is the space below the home’s floor deck
 and above the crawl space that typically contains the ductwork and an insulated blanket
 beneath. The airtightness of this space is not well characterized in the field and was
 modeled at various levels of tightness. Because there is no thermal barrier between the
 belly ducts and the house, duct heat gain or loss did not impact the home’s energy use
 as much as crawl space or attic-mounted ducts that are located outside the thermal
 envelope. We modeled an improved envelope based on the ENH program compared to
 a baseline envelope representing current typical practice because the ENH program
 was not available during the first modeling phase. We captured the peak hour energy
 use and estimated annual CO2e using the RESNET method based on expected grid
 emissions by location for the next 25 years (Lixing et al. 2024)

 We calculated the cost-effectiveness of various improvements from the simulations. It
 was evaluated by determining the first cost that could be added to a baseline home to
 achieve the modeled savings using prevalent purchase financing rates and shipment-
 weighted regional average utility costs. The method takes current energy costs of
 regions of the country where manufacturers ship housing and the associated savings
 projected by the simulation. Figure 16 shows the study regions and cities chosen for
 modeling. We employed the same methodology in both phases and included additional
 discussion in phase two of the economic value of peak electrical savings and CO2e
 emission savings. Highlights of the analysis are presented in Section 3 of this report.

 3 Results
 3.1 Feasibility Assessment
 We assessed innovations for market applicability, potential energy savings, cost-
 effectiveness, and likelihood of adoption by the industry based on information collected
 from industry stakeholders. Table 3 summarizes the assessment results for each
 innovation, including cost-effective likeliness and key market drivers and barriers for
 adoption. See the full feasibility assessment report for more details (Stendel et al.
 2021).

 With potential energy cost savings in the range of 10% to almost 60% compared to
 manufactured homes with electric-resistance heating, ducted heat pumps (e.g., H1,
 H1a, H1b, H1c, and H4, as shown in Table 3) are the highest-impact innovations. We
 determine ducted heat pump innovations as likely to be cost-effective, though cost-
 effectiveness varies on a regional basis, and our analysis finds that the operating cost
 for even an advanced (high-efficiency and variable-speed) heat pump is higher than that
 of an 80% efficient natural gas furnace in many parts of the country at current fuel
 prices. Although homes with natural gas heat comprise only about 10% of the national
 market for manufactured homes, they make up more than half of new home sales in the
 Midwest. Energy savings, comfort—particularly humidity control in hot-humid climates—
 and controlling HVAC sizing are industry drivers for these innovations, whereas up-front
 and operating cost is a barrier.

 For ductless heat pumps, we consider the most likely path to market acceptance to be
 partially offsetting the space-conditioning load of a central, ducted system with a single-
 head ductless system in a central portion of the home. This offset reduces the energy
 savings potential, but if properly integrated with controls to maximize the benefit of the
 heat pump (H2), it could still be a cost-effective upgrade for new manufactured homes.
 Advanced, quick-connect refrigerant fittings for these units (H3) could further reduce
 field installation costs.

 Duct-sealing innovations (e.g., D1, D2, D2a, D3, and D4) have estimated energy
 savings that are generally less than 10% of space-conditioning costs. However, the
 innovations themselves are likely to be less expensive than, say, upgrading to a heat
 pump, so they can still be cost-effective. Crossovers used to connect duct systems for
 multi-section homes are a particular point of vulnerability, though the potential market
 for crossover innovation (D2 and D2a) is limited to the 43% of the market that is multi-
 section homes. In general, there is more uncertainty in savings potential and cost-
 effectiveness associated with duct-related innovations due to a lack of data from
 modern manufactured homes about typical existing leakage levels, the incidence and
 magnitude of leakage issues, and in some cases the efficacy of particular innovations in
 mitigating those issues. Reducing energy waste callbacks is a driver for these
 innovations, whereas home manufacturer motivation to address duct leakage and costs
 are barriers.

 3.2 Innovation Testing
 The project team selected four innovations to move into the testing phase based on
 their feasibility for industry adoption and potential energy savings. Ducted heat pumps
 show the highest impact with potential energy cost savings and are likely cost-effective
 except when competing against natural gas heating in colder climates. Ductwork is a
 known area of concern impacting resident comfort and energy usage. The
 manufactured home industry is motivated to look at duct-related innovations that could
 solve resident comfort and energy issues. Three ductwork-focused innovations (e.g.,
 D1, D2, and D2a) are immediately feasible, address a well-known issue in the
 manufactured home industry, offer medium savings potential, are likely or possibly cost-
 effective, and support both duct leakage reduction and HVAC-energy-reduction project
 goals.

 H1a: Partial Factory Install of Ducted Heat Pumps
 This innovation showed big savings potential, seemed feasible by working with heat
 pump equipment currently available, is likely cost-effective, and is compatible with all-
 electric installations.

 We put together a guide to support the manufactured home industry in adopting
 construction practices to enable future heat pump installations (Slipstream 2023a). A
 summary of the results from the field monitoring technical heat pump analysis and
 exploration of the “plug-and-play” line set are described in this section. Additional
 detailed information on the heat pump technical analysis can be found in
 (Panchabikesan et al. 2024).

 The technical heat pump analysis of the field monitoring data showed the 2-ton Carrier
 heat pump installed in a new Clayton manufactured home in Oregon (heating load of 22
 kBtuh at a 26°F heating design temp) effectively managed the entire heating load
 throughout the winter, with minimal reliance on backup electric-resistance heat. We
 used the backup heat for a brief period of 8 days, each time for less than 10 minutes
 and likely in response to thermostat setpoint changes. The heat pump showed over
 60% heating energy savings (kilowatt-hour) compared to electric-resistance heat at this
 site.

 The heat pumps at both sites were generally able to maintain comfortable supply air
 conditions. At the Oregon site, the residents reported their home to be comfortable in
 the heating season. Summertime supply air temperature and relative humidity delivered
 by the Florida system were in the expected range for central cooling systems.

 This heat pump model showed significant power modulation in heating mode as
 expected from variable-capacity systems. There was very limited power modulation in
 cooling mode. It was unexpected to observe that the cooling system operated more like
 a single-stage system demonstrating short cycles during low cooling load periods. We
 confirmed proper installation, and the manufacturer could not determine a cause for the
 cooling performance observed in both the FSEC lab home and occupied Oregon home.
 After the study began, the 40MBAA air handler model tested in this study was

U.S. Department of Energy | Office of Energy Efficiency & Renewable Energy                   25

 discontinued by the manufacturer in June 2022 and replaced with a second-generation
 model of the unit, 40MBAB, which remains in production. The manufacturer was
 unaware of this study at the time of product changes.

 Running the heat pump cooling connected to attic ducts used 11.5% more daily cooling
 energy during a typical Florida summer day compared to when connected to the floor
 duct system. Both duct systems were reasonably airtight, but the floor ducts are within
 the primary thermal barrier of the house and attic ducts outside the thermal barrier in a
 very hot location.

 The Excel Air “plug-and-play” precharged line set showed promise during proof-of-
 concept testing to be a heavy-duty line set for this application. We connected the Excel
 Air line set manufacturer with Nortek, who provides HVAC equipment for many
 manufactured homes. However, the line set manufacturer is currently navigating the
 process for their line set to be approved for use with the new refrigerants—a federal
 change from the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency under the American Innovation
 and Manufacturing Act. The line set manufacturer needs more time to work through the
 approval process before continuing to engage with the HVAC manufacturers serving
 manufactured homes.

 We also considered exploring a heat pump that uses a precharged copper line set.
 However, copper line sets are less flexible and prone to kinking, making them more
 difficult to install and potentially more prone to failure. A factory that has used this
 system reported having had problems with refrigerant leakage at the couplings and
 difficulty obtaining service for the heat pump equipment from local HVAC contractors, so
 we did not pursue this further.

 D1. Improved HVAC Quality Assurance Protocols
 This innovation supported the project’s duct leakage reduction goal and can reduce
 both air and duct leakage in homes and help solve resident comfort and energy issues.

 The automated measurements in Florida showed that with sufficient exhaust flow and
 reasonably calm conditions, the protocol did a good job of estimating envelope and duct
 leakage on average; however, low-exhaust fan flows combined with windy conditions
 resulted in estimates with more uncertainty and downward bias in the estimated leakage
 rates.

 The field evaluation showed a strong difference between Northwest homes—where
 there is a well-established program, Northwest Energy Efficient Manufactured Housing
 Program to provide technical assistance on building practices and incentivize energy-
 efficient new manufactured housing—and those in the other two regions. Homes built in
 the Northwest region generally had tighter envelopes and duct systems. They also had
 higher available exhaust flow for the protocol due to widespread use of whole-house
 exhaust fans to meet HUD ventilation requirements, which is different from typical
 industry practice in other regions of the country that use an undampered passive duct to
 the furnace return. These two factors combined to produce protocol estimates of
 envelope leakage in the Northwest that aligned with blower-door testing.

U.S. Department of Energy | Office of Energy Efficiency & Renewable Energy              26

 In contrast, the homes in the other two regions (Midwest and Southeast) had leakier
 envelopes and low exhaust fan flow rates. The protocol consequently produced
 envelope leakage estimates for these homes with less reliable results when compared
 to blower-door values.

 Duct leakage estimates are inherently more variable than envelope leakage estimates
 using the protocol, because assessing duct leakage requires an additional house-to-
 outside pressure measurement. This additional measurement affords more opportunity
 for wind variation to affect the calculated result. The ability to assess duct leakage is
 also contingent on having a reasonably tight envelope so that depressurization effects
 from duct leakage can be measured.

 We used the data from the study to develop a stochastic model of variability in results
 under different wind and exhaust-flow conditions. The results suggest that the protocol
 appears to be the most suitable for efficient new homes that are expected to have tight
 envelopes (<4 ACH50) and that have 100 to 200 cubic feet per minute (cfm) of total
 existing exhaust-fan capacity. To be useful in conventionally constructed manufactured
 homes with leakier envelopes and lower existing exhaust-fan capacity, supplementary
 exhaust flow during evaluation would be needed in most cases.

 The protocol is highly amenable to being coded into an easy-to-use smartphone app,
 preferably connected to a digital manometer to automate the measurement and
 calculation process. A gauge-connected app could also dynamically assess wind effects
 on pressure measurements and adjust the measurement period as needed to reduce
 assessment uncertainty.

 Further development of the field protocol should include better characterization of how
 wind affects assessment variability in a wider variety of efficient new homes, and
 development and evaluation of a field app for implementing the protocol. Conducting the
 protocol in the field provides a commissioning test when homes are complete to identify
 air leakage and fan performance issues before customers identify them as issues. The
 field protocol cannot be used in the factory because multi-section homes are not
 connected in the factory and factories often do not have enough power on the
 production line to power electric furnaces. For implementing the protocol in typical
 existing manufactured housing, a means of providing supplementary exhaust during
 assessment would need to be developed.

 More detailed results can be found in Pigg, Stendel, and Cautley (2023).

 Factory Duct Leakage Testing Guide
 Our research team also put together a manufactured home factory duct leakage testing
 guide as a resource to support the manufactured home industry (Slipstream 2023b).
 Factory-floor testing using a duct leakage tester (e.g., Duct Blaster) can be used on the
 production line to test every home section as it gets built. For homes that are built with
 ducts in the floor, it is often possible to test the duct system for leakage while ducts are
 still easily accessible and before any walls are installed. Register openings and
 crossover takeoffs can be blocked with foam or taped over and the duct testing fan set

U.S. Department of Energy | Office of Energy Efficiency & Renewable Energy                 27

 over a furnace or register opening. The test can be performed in under 3 minutes when
 the home is at the bare floor stage, before walls are installed. This test would provide a
 clear indication of duct leakage, and it can be quick and easy enough to perform so that
 plants could adopt it for every home section, making it a basic early function check of
 the duct system’s airtightness.

 Factory-floor duct leakage testing has been shown to reduce callbacks and help
 factories meet Northwest Energy Efficient Manufactured Housing Program duct
 tightness requirements. Northwest home manufacturers have found factory testing
 reduces the occurrence of vinyl sheet flooring lifting off the subfloor due to duct leakage
 and eliminates failing to connect registers to flex branch ducts through the subfloor,
 which can be causes for callbacks. Factory-floor duct leakage testing can reduce these
 costs for factories.

 D2. Improved Crossover Duct Designs
 This innovation sought to improve the design of the crossover duct connection required
 for multi-section homes by employing metal elbow connectors and a super insulated flex
 duct-in-duct approach. Our project team proved it was possible to create a
 superinsulated flex duct by putting a duct inside a duct, thereby increasing the total R
 value.

 We also initiated a conversation with J.P. Lamborn Co., a flex duct manufacturer
 serving the manufactured home industry, to explore this innovation, but they were not
 interested in pursuing it further. More research is needed to explore innovative ways to
 improve crossover duct designs in manufactured homes.

 D2a. Comparative Testing of Different Crossover Approaches
 This innovation compared energy performance and durability of existing crossover
 ductwork designs with a goal of reducing duct leakage in multi-section homes.

 We were not able to collect enough data to complete this analysis. However, we think a
 future study focused on measuring the real-world performance on through-the-rim vs.
 traditional flex-duct crossover ductwork for multi-section homes over time would be
 valuable to the manufactured home industry. The industry would benefit from
 understanding how the performance from each duct crossover design performs over
 time for both duct leakage and durability.

 3.3 Modeling and Cost-Effectiveness
 The findings from the economic evaluations and modeling simulation results in eight
 cities shed light on the advantages of heat pumps over electric-resistance furnaces, the
 potential benefits of variable-speed heat pumps, the impact of belly and duct leakage on
 energy savings, and the efficacy of a ENH manufactured home in reducing energy
 consumption. Additionally, the assessment extends to the consequential CO2e emission
 reductions associated with higher-efficiency HVAC systems and improved building
 envelopes. Following is a summary of the results.

U.S. Department of Energy | Office of Energy Efficiency & Renewable Energy                28

 Heat pumps deliver significant electrical energy savings compared to electric-resistance
 heat. The energy savings achieved using heat pumps, compared to electric furnaces,
 for double-wide homes vary from 11% in Phoenix to 59% in Seattle, with the most
 substantial absolute savings in Chicago, exceeding 24,000 kBtu. In the case of single-
 wide homes, the savings are 10% and 57% in Phoenix and Seattle, respectively, with
 Chicago experiencing absolute savings just over 15,000 kBtu. The adoption of heat
 pumps results in a reduction in energy consumption, leading to annual cost savings
 ranging from approximately $70 to $900 relative to electric resistance. These savings
 are contingent on the study region and home type, with the greatest cost benefits
 observed in colder climates and for double-wide homes.

 In general, variable-speed heat pumps have higher efficiencies than single-speed heat
 pumps for both cooling and heating. In theory, variable-speed heat pumps offer an
 advantage over single-speed heat pumps in cold climates because they can be sized
 for the heating load without compromising cooling dehumidification performance. The
 range of modeled electrical savings across eight locations from this study is 5% and
 around 500 kBtu in Phoenix to 20% and almost 3,000 kBtu in Raleigh relative to the
 single-speed heat pump modeled. Higher-efficiency, variable-speed heat pumps have a
 higher initial cost than single-speed heat pumps, and the return on investment for this
 more efficient option varies depending on climate and baseline HVAC system. For
 variable-speed heat pumps, the cost-effectiveness evaluation suggests annual cost
 savings of $17 to $117 compared to a single-speed heat pump, depending on study
 region and home type. Variable-speed heat pumps have the additional advantages of
 running at lower speeds for smaller loads, thereby preventing short cycling, which can
 lead to comfort problems from elevated relative humidity.

 Belly leakage impacts energy savings. An important distinction between this modeling
 and prior envelope modeling research is the simulation of the belly area as a thermal
 zone. The impact of annual HVAC system energy consumption using a baseline belly
 zone airtightness of 15 ACH50 and tightening it to 3 ACH50 was rather minor across all
 eight cities—this tightening represents reducing the effective leakage area from 12.5 to
 2.5 square inches in a double-wide belly, which is much tighter than what is typically
 observed in practice. Subsequently, new simulations were conducted for Baltimore
 baseline belly zone airtightness of 30 and 60 ACH50—representing an effective leakage
 area of 25 and 50 square inches, respectively, in a double-wide belly (aligning with what
 is observed for well-installed homes). This greater range of simulated tightening
 resulted in about 6% predicted site energy savings in double-wide homes and 4% in
 single-wide savings. More research on the hole sizes and airtightness of the belly zones
 in actual sited manufactured homes is needed for more precise predictions of energy
 use differences.

 Duct leakage impacts energy savings. Based on the limited field data available, savings
 of 2%‒3% of HVAC energy use may be achieved by tightening the ducts in single-wide
 and some double-wide units. This savings represents the difference when going from a
 Qn (normalized duct leakage or cfm leakage per square foot of floor area) of 0.08
 cfm/ft2 to 0.04 cfm/ft2, about a 50% reduction. Homes with a baseline of leaky crossover
 connections may expect savings of 6% when tightened. When a ENH envelope with a

U.S. Department of Energy | Office of Energy Efficiency & Renewable Energy             29

 single-speed heat pump is upgraded to tighter ducts, the annual cost savings could be
 as high as $46, depending on study region and home type. The economic viability of
 energy savings only becomes apparent when tighter ducts are adopted as a standard
 practice.

 Manufacturers of homes in Florida and some other Southern locations have placed
 ductwork in the attic. This more closely matches the typical practice of site-built housing.
 However, the increased heat gain of duct runs in attics in locations dominated by
 cooling is thermally detrimental, increasing energy use. This simulation study estimated
 ducts located in the belly saved about 5,000 kBtu or 20% of total heating and cooling
 energy use relative to attic ducts for a baseline house in the Southeastern United
 States.

 ENH manufactured home envelope changes reduce energy use with meaningful cost
 savings. The estimated cost savings for ENH manufactured home envelope measures
 ranged from 9% to 16%, depending on building type and study region. If the envelope
 measures were applied to a single-wide home in Seattle with a heat pump, savings
 could be as low as under 1,000 kBtu, due to the milder climate compared to other
 regions. On the other hand, savings of over 6,000 kBtu are possible when ENH
 manufactured home envelope measures are applied to a gas furnace home in Chicago.
 In terms of economics, the modeling suggests annual energy cost savings ranging from
 $25 to $166, depending on baseline assumptions, study region, and home size. The
 improved envelope can lead to additional cost savings when the ENH manufactured
 home improvement leads to HVAC downsizing. Regional percentage cost savings

 Conclusion
 Manufactured homes are an important source of affordable housing in the United
 States, but minimum-efficiency equipment and poor-quality home installation can lead to
 excess energy use and high operating costs. Below are the major findings from this
 work:
 • Quality assurance protocols used in home setup processes for tighter homes
 with high levels of exhaust-fan flow that can identify envelope and/or duct
 leakage issues before the home is occupied, can result in reduced expenses
 from customer callbacks. 4
 • Heat pump preparation packages that include installing a chaseway for
 refrigerant and condensate lines that is designed to reduce damage to the
 home’s belly (the enclosed area beneath the subfloor containing the duct system,
 plumbing, insulation, and road barrier) when the heat pump is installed
 (Slipstream 2023a) can ease installation challenges.
 • Partial installation of heat pumps in the factory would enable more heat pump
 adoption (Slipstream 2023a).
 • Building homes with ducts in the attic in thermal zones 1 and 2 often leads to
 reduced energy performance.
 • There are research gaps surrounding crossover duct designs in manufactured
 homes.
 • There are research gaps surrounding the real-world performance and durability
 over time of through-the-rim versus traditional flex-duct crossover ductwork in
 multi-section homes.
