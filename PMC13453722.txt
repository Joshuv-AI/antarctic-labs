Upper common bile duct neuroendocrine tumor presenting with obstructive jaundice: a case report and literature review
Authors: Tian Gui Zhang, Tauqeer Muhammad, Zhu Ying Lin, Xiao Lei Yang, Jia Lin Shi, Ting Ting He, Yao Yang, Zhong Fu Zhao, Shuai Yu Zhang, Jiang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe common bile duct (CBD) neuroendocrine tumor (NET) is a rare biliary tumor accounting for less than 2% of gastrointestinal neuroendocrine neoplasms (NEN). It is usually non-functional and grows submucosally, making the diagnosis of the condition during surgery difficult, and may be confused with cholangiocarcinoma.Case presentationWe report a case of a 61-year-old woman who presented with 1-month obstructive jaundice and 6-kg weight loss. Laboratory test results showed increased levels of bilirubin whereas levels of AFP, CEA, CA19-9, and CA125 showed normal results. The CT and MRI findings showed upstream dilation of the bile ducts with a hypervascular mass in the superior CBD measuring 1.7–1.8 cm. Hyperenhancement in the arterial phase and normal serum CA19–9 levels were suggestive of NET as one of the possible differentials. The patient underwent resection of the CBD, cholecystectomy, regional lymph node dissection, and Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy with R0 margins confirmed via frozen section. The patient did not get any disease for the 24-month follow-up.Literature reviewA systematic PubMed search using strict anatomical and pathological criteria identified 25 previously published primary CBD NET cases since 2010; combined with the index case, 26 cases were analyzed. The mean age was 47.3 ± 22.9 years, and the mean tumor diameter was 2.19 ± 0.87 cm, with a near-even male-to-female ratio (54%:46%). Jaundice (65%), abdominal pain (46%), and pruritus (35%) were the most common symptoms. Well-differentiated NET was the predominant subtype (62%), most often G2 (56% of NET cases), followed by neuroendocrine carcinoma (27%) and mixed neuroendocrine–non-neuroendocrine neoplasm (11%). CBD resection with lymphadenectomy and Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy was the most common procedure (46%), followed by pancreaticoduodenectomy (31%), mainly for lower-segment lesions. No recurrence was reported in 69% of cases at last follow-up.ConclusionsCBD NET should be considered in those with obstructive jaundice, with hypervascular biliary lesions, and without raised tumor markers. Surgical treatment should include R0 resection and regional lymphadenectomy. Pathological grade, Ki-67 index, lymph node status, and resection margins are the most important factors in prognosis. An important principle is to keep watching for long periods after complete resection of low and intermediate grade tumors.

Introduction

A heterogeneous state of epithelial tumors that are based on the cells of neuroendocrine differentiation is the neuroendocrine neoplasm (NEN), which is located in the body in almost every part and frequently in the gastrointestinal tract, pancreas, and lungs (1). The 2019 World Health Organization (WHO) classification further subclassifies the NENs into well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) (graded as G1 Ki-67 <3%), G2 (Ki-67 3%-20%), or G3 (Ki-67 >20%)) and poorly differentiated neuroendocrine carcinomas (NECs) (2). This grading system has standardized prognostic stratification and treatment planning for all institutions.

Primary biliary NENs are rare (less than 2% of all gastrointestinal neuroendocrine neoplasm), and NETs developing in the common bile duct (CBD) are the rarest of the family (3, 4). Neuroendocrine cells in the biliary tract are sparsely distributed, and the mechanisms of malignant transformation are yet to be completely understood; possible pathways involve the neoplastic transformation of biliary epithelial native neuroendocrine cells, intestinal metaplasia as a result of chronic inflammation, and the aberrant differentiation of the pluripotent progenitor cell (5). CBD NETs are virtually non-functional, unlike gastro-entero-pancreatic NETs which often develop hormonal syndromes; they are characterized by obstructive jaundice, pruritus, and abdominal pains as the predominant symptoms of the disease, and carcinoid syndrome is extremely uncommon (6). CBD NET is hard to diagnose preoperatively. The hypervascular mass appears on cross-sectional imaging, such as computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), resulting in biliary dilation, which also presents significantly with cholangiocarcinoma and, more rarely, with metastatic disease or hepatocellular carcinoma invading the bile duct (7). Traditionally associated serum tumor markers like carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) and carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA19-9) are often normal, which can be confusing with biliary obstruction whereby CA19–9 raises should be witnessed in case of cholangiocarcinoma (8). ERCP with brush cytology has poor sensitivity of submucosal lesions, and even ERCP on endoscopic ultrasound-guided biopsy may give inadequate tissue in the CBD (9). Consequently, its ultimate diagnosis relies virtually on postoperative histopathology and an array of neuroendocrine immunohistochemical activators such as Synaptophysin (Syn), Chromogranin A (CgA), CD56, and INSM1 (10).

Given the rarity of this entity, evidence on optimal surgical management, pathological determinants of outcome, and long-term prognosis comes almost entirely from isolated case reports and small series. Surgical resection remains the only potentially curative treatment; the extent of resection varies by tumor location, ranging from limited bile duct excision with hepaticojejunostomy to pancreaticoduodenectomy for lower-segment lesions (11). Here, we report a case of a G2 NET arising from the upper CBD in a 61-year-old woman who underwent R0 resection and remained disease-free at 24 months and present a systematic review of all primary CBD NET cases published in PubMed from 2010 onward to characterize the clinical, pathological, and surgical features of this rare tumor in the modern WHO classification era.

Case presentation

A 61-year-old woman presented with a 1-month history of decreased appetite and jaundice of the skin and sclerae. Abdominal ultrasound found dilation of the left and right hepatic ducts and the upper common bile duct, with a solid occupying lesion in the upper CBD. She had lost 6 kg in the 3 months before admission. There was no significant internal medical diseases history or surgical history, malignant tumor history of the liver or gallbladder system, neuroendocrine tumor history, or family history of known hereditary tumor syndrome. No psychological and social stress factors for this case were found. The patient had not developed any clinical manifestations and no significant family history of a hereditary tumor syndrome; thus, genetic testing was not performed. The patient had not received bile duct drainage, surgery, anticancer therapy, or any other procedures prior to admission which involved treating this condition.

Physical examination showed mild icterus of the skin and sclerae. The abdomen was flat and soft, with no palpable mass, no tenderness, rebound tenderness, or muscle guarding. Murphy’s sign showed a negative result. The costovertebral angle is for kidneys I suppose.

There was no tenderness over the liver, spleen, or kidneys. No costovertebral angle tenderness was noted. Shifting dullness showed a negative finding. Results for bowel sounds were normal.

Laboratory findings

Liver function tests showed a total bilirubin level of 107.8 μmol/L, a direct bilirubin level of 79.6 μmol/L, an ALT level of 104.1 IU/L, and an AST level of 80.1 IU/L; all other routine parameters showed normal results. Tumor markers AFP, CEA, CA19-9, and CA125 all showed normal findings.

Imaging findings

Abdominal ultrasound results showed intrahepatic biliary dilation (Figure 1A). Abdominal CT results showed a nodular soft-tissue density lesion measuring approximately 1.7 cm × 1.8 cm × 1.6 cm in the upper CBD, with marked enhancement on contrast-enhanced imaging and causing upstream intra- and extra-hepatic biliary dilation; no definite lymph node enlargement or distant metastasis was seen (Figures 1B–D). Abdominal MRI/MRCP findings showed the lesion in the upper CBD, appearing isointense to slightly hypointense on T1WI (Figure 2A), isointense to slightly hyperintense on T2WI (Figure 2B), with marked enhancement on dynamic contrast-enhanced sequences (Figures 2C, D), and high signal on DWI and low signal on ADC (Figures 2E, F). MRCP showed a focal filling defect at the level of the lesion with marked dilation of the upstream biliary system, the widest diameter approximately 1.5 cm (Figures 2G, H). Overall, imaging indicated a hypervascular occupying lesion in the upper CBD causing obstructive biliary dilation.

Based on clinical history, laboratory findings, and imaging, a preoperative diagnosis of common bile duct tumor with obstructive jaundice was established. Laparoscopic exploration was planned, followed by local excision or pancreaticoduodenectomy depending on intraoperative findings. Intraoperatively, the tumor was found in the upper CBD (Figure 3A). The superior margin was below the confluence of the left and right hepatic ducts, and the inferior margin was above the pancreas; the tumor had clear borders. No obvious hilar lymph node enlargement was seen. An aberrant right hepatic artery (arising from the superior mesenteric artery) had a clear plane from the tumor (Figure 3B). Intraoperative frozen sections of the distal and proximal bile duct margins were both negative (−). The patient ultimately underwent common bile duct resection, cholecystectomy, end-to-side Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy, and regional lymph node dissection (Figures 3C, D). The operative and subsequent plan opted to proceed with a resection of the CBD with Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy instead of pancreaticoduodenectomy, which was based on the identification during the operation that the inferior edge of the tumor was well above the pancreatic parenchyma and there was no involvement of the head of the pancreas. This procedure eliminated the morbidity associated with pancreaticoduodenectomy yet maintained oncologically appropriate margins similar to those of surgery in association with lesions found in the upper and middle portions of the CBD today. The Roux-en-Y limb was created at 60 cm, and the hepaticojejunostomy was created with interrupted absorbable sutures. A closed-suction drain was inserted in the vicinity of the anastomosis. Estimated blood loss was 100 mL, and the mean operative time was 300 min. The patient was treated postoperatively with the same enhanced recovery methods, and clear liquid was started on the 2nd postoperative day whereas liquid diet was given on day 3. The patient recovered and was discharged 12 days postoperatively with no complications.

Postoperative histopathology showed a tumor measuring 2.0 cm × 2.0 cm × 1.5 cm in the wall of the upper CBD, with infiltrative growth interwoven with the surrounding stroma and marked fibrous stromal reaction. Tumor cells were arranged mainly in trabecular cords and irregular nests. Cell borders were indistinct, with scant to moderate pale or lightly eosinophilic cytoplasm; nuclei were round to oval with fine, evenly distributed chromatin, and moderate nuclear atypia was seen focally (Figure 4A). Immunohistochemistry showed positive results on the tumor cells for Syn, CgA, CD56, and INSM1, supporting neuroendocrine differentiation; the Ki-67 proliferation index was approximately 10%-20%, consistent with moderate proliferative activity and NET G2 classification; strong SSTR2 positivity indicated preserved somatostatin receptor expression, supporting a well-differentiated NET phenotype; CK7 negativity argued against a typical cholangiocarcinoma immune profile; retained RB1 expression further argued against poorly differentiated NEC (Figures 4B–I). Combining the morphological and immunohistochemical findings, the final diagnosis was Grade 2 (G2) neuroendocrine tumor.

Clinical assessment

Each follow-up visit includes a detailed medical history and physical examination, with close attention to symptoms suggesting recurrence or metastasis such as jaundice, abdominal pain, and weight loss. Laboratory monitoring focuses on liver function. It should be noted that there are currently no validated tumor markers specifically for NET recurrence surveillance; therefore, imaging remains the cornerstone of follow-up, with contrast-enhanced abdominal CT or MRI as the primary modality.

Patient perspective

The patient was interviewed during the postoperative follow-up period and agreed to share her medical experience. She recalled feeling extremely uneasy when she first noticed progressive jaundice of the skin and sclera, significant fatigue, and unexplained weight loss. Prior to her diagnosis, the most anxiety-provoking period was the time leading up to surgery while waiting for a definitive diagnosis at that point; it was unclear what the nature of the bile duct obstruction was. When the surgical team informed her that the tumor had been completely removed and that she did not need chemotherapy after surgery, she felt a tremendous sense of relief. Two years after the surgery, she had returned to normal life, with no restrictions on her activities, and her appetite and physical strength had fully recovered. She is willing to share her experience to help other patients with similar rare bile duct tumors.

Discussion

Common bile duct NETs are among the rarest biliary malignancies. Population-based data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database estimate an annual incidence of gastrointestinal NETs at approximately 3.56 per 100,000 persons, but biliary NETs represent fewer than 1% of this figure, and primary CBD NETs are even less frequent (1). Because neuroendocrine cells in the bile duct mucosa are sparse and their neoplastic transformation is poorly understood, hypotheses for pathogenesis include malignant change within resident neuroendocrine cells, intestinal metaplasia driven by chronic biliary inflammation, and aberrant differentiation of multipotent progenitor cells (5, 12). All these mechanisms have not been clearly determined, and no genetic or molecular risk profile has been determined, which can form the basis of future surveillance. The CBD NETs, in contrast to functional gastro-entero-pancreatic ones, are nearly universally non-functional; hormonal syndromes are absent, and the pathotype is determined by biliary obstruction. This is consistent with the symptom pattern reported in the literature, in which jaundice, abdominal pain, and pruritus are the most common presenting manifestations (Table 3). CBD NETs therefore act as an obstructive, not hormone-secreting disease, hence obscuring clinical suspicion and contributes to the high incidence of preoperative misdiagnosis as cholangiocarcinoma (6, 13). Compounding this, the tumor characteristically grows submucosally, limiting the sensitivity of ERCP brush cytology and superficial endo-biliary biopsy; definitive diagnosis therefore remains dependent on postoperative immunohistochemistry in most of the cases (9, 14).

The present patient was a 61-year-old woman with progressive obstructive jaundice and weight loss. CT and MRI both showed a hypervascular lesion of approximately 2 cm in the upper CBD, and all conventional biliary tumor markers (AFP, CEA, CA19-9, and CA125) were within normal limits. The combination of arterial-phase hyperenhancement and normal CA19–9 is a key differentiating feature: cholangiocarcinoma is typically hypovascular on contrast imaging and is associated with CA19–9 elevation in most patients with biliary obstruction, whereas NETs retain the rich vascular network characteristic of neuroendocrine tumors at other sites (8, 15). Diffusion restriction on MRI (high DWI signal, low ADC) in the present case is consistent with the high cellularity reported in well-differentiated NETs and has been described in biliary NETs in isolated case series (16). Somatostatin receptor scintigraphy or 68Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT, which exploits the high somatostatin receptor expression typical of well-differentiated NETs, could potentially improve preoperative characterization; however, experience with these modalities in primary CBD NETs remains very limited, and the literature does not yet support their routine use as a diagnostic standard (17, 18). Somatostatin receptor (SSTR)-targeted functional imaging, particularly 68Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT, may complement conventional CT/MRI in both the diagnostic work-up and postoperative assessment of well-differentiated biliary NETs. In a patient with a hypervascular bile-duct lesion and normal CA19-9, avid SSTR uptake may support the suspicion of a well-differentiated NET and improve whole-body staging by detecting otherwise occult nodal, hepatic, osseous, or other distant disease. This potential value is especially relevant in the present case because the tumor showed strong SSTR2 expression. During follow-up, 68Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT may serve as a problem-solving or restaging examination when CT/MRI findings are equivocal or when recurrence is clinically suspected (19, 20); if recurrent or metastatic disease is demonstrated, receptor positivity may also help identify candidates for somatostatin-analog therapy or peptide receptor radionuclide therapy with 177Lu-DOTATATE (17, 21). However, evidence specific to primary CBD NETs remains limited and is largely derived from isolated case reports (22). Therefore, SSTR-PET/CT should complement rather than replace contrast-enhanced CT/MRI, and a negative examination should not be used to exclude microscopic disease or to reduce the extent of oncologic resection. For resectable lesions, the clinical and imaging findings described above should be sufficient to prompt NET as a differential diagnosis and to inform surgical planning, even without preoperative histological confirmation.

To ensure grading comparability, this review included only cases published from 2010 onward, a period in which the WHO mitotic count and Ki-67 framework had been widely adopted for gastrointestinal and biliary NENs (2, 23). A literature search was performed in the PubMed database with the search string: (common bile duct) AND (neuroendocrine tumor) OR (neuroendocrine carcinoma)) NOT gallbladder NOT ampulla NOT (common hepatic duct) for the period 2010-2026. To make grading comparable, the lower age limit of 2010 was chosen, because following this year most gastrointestinal and biliary NETs were classified via the mitotic Ki67 framework, developed by the WHO. From the initial search, 115 papers were identified including case reports, small case series, narrative reviews, pooled analyses, etc. Of the 97 records that were screened for title and abstract, after discarding 18 duplicates, 41 records were excluded because they contained tumor origin tumors clearly far removed from the true common bile duct (ampullary, hepatic duct confluence, or cystic duct primaries), were publication predating 2010, had a title or abstract language not interpreted as English, and/or were identified as review articles that did not contain individual case-level extractable data. Of these, the remaining 56 articles were retrieved in their entirety and subjected to a four-stage pre-specified eligibility process based on (1) confirmed primary typology of the tumor was bile duct; (2) standard neuroendocrine immunohistochemistry (Synaptophysin, Chromogranin A, CD56, and/or Ki-67) was used for pathological diagnosis; (3) was sufficiently detailed to enable the assignment of a WHO 2010/2019 pathological grade (G1, G2, G3, NEC, or MiNEN); and (4) were published from 2010 onward. A second 31 articles were excluded at full-text review, 8 because they had a primary tumor arising from the ampulla or hepatic duct, 5 because the primary tumor was from the gallbladder or cystic duct, 7 were published prior to the WHO grading era or contained no Ki-67 index data, 6 for duplicate reporting of the report of an article already included, and 6 for cases with insufficient extractable data. All 25 of these previously published cases were found to be eligible for the pooled analysis, and the authors’ own case was included in the current index case; a total of 26 cases was thus combined to provide the descriptive analysis presented in Tables 3, 4.

Collectively, men slightly outnumbered women (54% vs. 46%), the mean age was 47.3 ± 22.92 years (range 10-84), and the mean tumor diameter was 2.19 ± 0.87 cm. The wide age range confirms that CBD NET can occur across the full spectrum of age, including in pediatric patients, a pattern also noted in larger biliary NEN series (47). From a pathological standpoint, the immunohistochemical profile in this case, including positivity for Syn, CgA, CD56, and INSM1, a Ki-67 index of 10%-20%, and strong SSTR2 expression, confirmed the diagnosis of a G2 well-differentiated NET. INSM1, a nuclear transcription factor, has emerged as a sensitive and specific marker of neuroendocrine differentiation that retains its utility in poorly fixed or limited specimens where CgA and Syn staining may be equivocal (10, 48). Strong SSTR2 expression is characteristic of well-differentiated NETs and is relevant beyond diagnosis: In patients who develop recurrence or metastatic disease, high SSTR2 expression identifies tumors likely to respond to somatostatin analog therapy or peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT) with 177Lu-DOTATATE (17, 21). Retained RB1 expression in this case argued against poorly differentiated NEC, a distinction with direct prognostic and therapeutic implications, since NEC is managed with platinum-based chemotherapy rather than surgery alone (23, 49). The subtypes should not be grouped with G1/G2 NETs for management purposes, and multidisciplinary review is warranted before and after surgery in all NEC and MiNEN cases to determine whether adjuvant systemic therapy is appropriate.

Surgical strategy for CBD NET should be guided by precise tumor location, longitudinal extent of ductal involvement, margin accessibility, and regional lymph node status rather than by a fixed operative template. For upper- and middle-segment lesions without pancreatic involvement, CBD resection combined with regional lymph node dissection and Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy is the most used approach and was employed in 46% of reviewed cases; pancreaticoduodenectomy was required in 30%, predominantly for lower-segment or head-of-pancreas-adjacent tumors (11). In the case under consideration, the tumor was of superior margin of less than the confluence of hepatic ducts, and inferior margin of above the pancreas, which result in frozen-section confirmation of clear margins at both the levels and hence spared pancreaticoduodenectomy. The main determinant of the long-term outcome in localized disease is R0 resection, which has been associated with better prognosis in biliary NEN series (50, 51). Resection should be accompanied by regional lymph node dissection irrespective of grade as lymph node metastasis was also observed with G1 and small tumors (52). In this case, the significance of preoperative vascular mapping and quality dissection was emphasized by the fact that damage to the aberrant right hepatic artery, which originated at the superior mesenteric artery, induced a high probability of hepatic ischemia and anastomotic failure when damaged during hilar dissection (52). It is advisable to carry out postoperative surveillance by cross-sectional imaging on a regular time basis in all resected CBD NETs because recurrence has still been observed to occur late even after seemingly successful resection of well-differentiated tumors (50).

Several practical points emerge from this case and the pooled review. First, for any biliary lesion presenting with obstructive jaundice, a hypervascular enhancement pattern on CT or MRI combined with normal serum CA19–9 suggest NET as a differential diagnosis alongside cholangiocarcinoma, treating all such lesions as cholangiocarcinoma by default delays appropriate management (8). However, neither finding is fully specific, since hypervascular and non-hypervascular enhancement patterns are seen across a range of pancreaticobiliary lesions and vascularity alone has not reliably discriminated tumor entity even within other hypervascular pathology (53). Second, the limitations of preoperative tissue diagnosis are well established for submucosal biliary tumors; ERCP brush cytology and superficial biopsy have low diagnostic yield, and for clearly resectable lesions, extended attempts at preoperative histological confirmation should not delay surgery (9, 14). Intraoperative frozen-section evaluation of bile duct margins is the standard approach to ensuring R0 resection. Third, lymph node dissection should be included in all resections regardless of apparent tumor size or grade, as nodal metastasis from small and low-grade CBD NETs has been documented (52). Fourth, for NEC and MiNEN subtypes, the role of adjuvant chemotherapy typically a platinum-etoposide regimen for NEC should be evaluated in a multidisciplinary setting, although the evidence base from CBD-specific series is absent and guidance is extrapolated from gastro-entero-pancreatic NEC data (23, 54). Finally, because the natural history of CBD NET is not fully characterized and late recurrence has been reported, structured long-term imaging follow-up is necessary even after R0 resection of G1/G2 tumors; no consensus guideline currently defines the optimal interval or duration for surveillance in this specific entity, and practice is largely guided by extrapolation from gastrointestinal NET follow-up frameworks (55).

Strengths and limitations

This report has several strengths which support validity. A detailed immunohistochemical panel comprising Syn, CgA, CD56, INSM1, SSTR2, CK7, and RB1 allowed for definitive WHO 2019 grade assignment and ruling out poorly differentiated NEC. There was an intraoperative freeze section that confirmed the R0 resection. Prespecified selection criteria were used to minimize selection bias, and the systematic literature review criteria applied restricted potential data to only those from 2010 or later, which allowed for grading in a similar way as in the modern WHO mitotic/Ki-67 framework.

There are, however, several aspects to acknowledge as well as weaknesses in the design. First is that this is a single-institution case report and conclusions on surgical intervention and prognosis should not be generalized. Secondly, the pooled analysis is based on 25 previously published cases, many of which are again single case reports described with varying degrees of reporting quality and follow-up (limited number of months) and different pathological documentation (inconsistent). Thirdly, the absence of histological diagnosis before the operation, which does not depart from the normal procedure for resections of submucosal biliary lesions, led to the assumption of an operation plan based solely on imaging type. Fourth, there was no functional imaging with (68)Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT performed which could have offered an extra etiological finding. Follow-up information was reassuring for 24 months, although this length of time could prove to be too short to detect late recurrence, which has been described after 5 years in well-differentiated NETs at other locations.

Conclusion

In summary, NET of the upper common bile duct is an extremely rare biliary tumor that is highly susceptible to preoperative misdiagnosis. For patients presenting with obstructive jaundice, a focal hypervascular biliary lesion on imaging, and normal routine tumor markers, awareness of this diagnosis should be heightened. This case shows that, based on accurate assessment of tumor location and extent of invasion, CBD resection combined with regional lymph node dissection and Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy can serve as an effective radical procedure for localized upper-segment lesions. Pathological grades, Ki-67 index, lymph node status, and resection margin status remain the core determinants of prognostic assessment and subsequent management. This study, using post-2010 WHO cases as the basis for comparison, highlights the clinical value of reexamining CBD NET characteristics and treatment outcomes within a modern pathological framework. This case not only adds to the limited evidence base for G2 NET of the upper CBD but also reinforces the importance of maintaining differential diagnosis, individualized surgery, standardized pathological grading, and long-term follow-up in clinical practice, rather than defaulting to the diagnostic and treatment pathways for cholangiocarcinoma.
