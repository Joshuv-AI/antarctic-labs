Quantitative rapid stabilization for parabolic equations via the linear quadratic theory
Shengquan Xiang, Yu Xiao, Can Zhang

Abstract
This paper addresses the problem of quantitative rapid stabilization via the linear quadratic (LQ) theory. Specifically, for non-self-adjoint parabolic equations, by virtue of the LQ theory, we derive the quantitative rapid stabilization by selecting a special cost functional and combining it with a quantitative observability inequality. Furthermore, this approach reveals the equivalence between the quantitative rapid stabilization and the quantitative observability inequality for linear systems. In addition, we apply this framework to the Navier–Stokes equations and establish the quantitative rapid stabilization around nontrivial steady states. Finally, we extend this methodology to finite-dimensional feedback laws in self-adjoint cases.



Keywords.
LQ theory, quantitative stabilization, observability inequality, non-self-adjoint

1.1
Quantitative rapid stabilization problem

While the classical rapid stabilization is qualitative in nature, a more refined question concerns the dependence of the stabilization cost on the prescribed decay rate. This leads to the notion of
quantitative rapid stabilization
, which consists in constructing rapidly stabilizing feedback laws together with explicit estimates on the constant
M_{\lambda}
in terms of
\lambda
. Such a quantitative description is of independent interest and has proved to be of considerable significance.

From a practical viewpoint, the quantitative rapid stabilization provides explicit information on the behavior of the closed-loop system. In particular, it makes it possible to estimate the time required for the energy to fall below a prescribed threshold, to compute half-life-type quantities, and to assess robustness with respect to perturbations. For example, under an additional bounded perturbation of size
\varepsilon
, standard perturbation arguments (cf.
[
39
]
) typically yield an estimate of the form

\|y(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq M_{\lambda}e^{-(\lambda-\varepsilon M_{\lambda})t}\|y_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\quad\forall t\geq 0.

Hence, in order for the perturbed closed-loop system to remain exponentially stable, one needs at least
\varepsilon M_{\lambda}<\lambda
. This shows that the growth of
M_{\lambda}
directly affects the admissible size of perturbations. Furthermore, if
M_{\lambda}=e^{C\lambda^{\alpha}}
for
\lambda>1
, then, for
\alpha>1
, the decay term
e^{-\lambda t}
cannot effectively compensate for the growth of
M_{\lambda}
over short and intermediate times, which may result in a very large stabilization time. Consequently, it is particularly important to ensure that the stabilization cost grows at most like
e^{C\lambda}
.

From the mathematical viewpoint, the quantitative rapid stabilization is closely connected with several other issues in control theory. On one hand, quantitative estimates for rapidly stabilizing feedback laws can be exploited to derive the small-time feedback null controllability with explicit control costs, as well as the finite-time stabilization through iterative or time-varying constructions. Relevant ideas were introduced in
[
9
]
, and further investigated in
[
42
,
44
]
. On the other hand, this quantitative viewpoint can be combined with other problems; in particular, the frequency-Lyapunov method
[
44
]
has further inspired observer-based control, disturbance problems
[
18
]
, and controllability for stochastic heat equations
[
19
]
. In this sense, the quantitative rapid stabilization is not merely a strengthened form of the rapid stabilization, but rather a useful framework that bridges feedback stabilization, control cost estimates, and constructive controllability properties.

In recent years, several of the above methods used for rapid stabilization have been refined to yield quantitative results. For one-dimensional equations, substantial progress has been made. More precisely, backstepping techniques provide quantitative estimates by exploiting sharp bounds on the kernels of the associated Volterra transformations; see
[
9
]
. Moreover, Volterra backstepping has also been applied to the KdV equation
[
42
]
, while in
[
33
]
the Gramian operator method was used to study the finite-time stabilization for the KdV equation as well. We also refer to the recent work
[
16
]
, where a quantitative version of the Fredholm backstepping method was finally successfully established in the self-adjoint and skew-adjoint settings, and to the ongoing work
[
41
]
, where the modal decomposition method combined with the frequency-Lyapunov method yields quantitative results.

By contrast, for multidimensional parabolic equations, the quantitative rapid stabilization problem remains largely open. In the self-adjoint case, such as the heat equation, the frequency-Lyapunov method integrates a low-frequency feedback design with Lebeau–Robbiano–type spectral inequalities to derive explicit bounds for stabilization costs; see
[
43
,
44
]
. However, the
non-self-adjoint case
has not yet been addressed. The main difficulty is that the spectral and orthogonality properties available in the self-adjoint setting are no longer present, which makes it much harder to derive quantitative estimates for the low-frequency dynamics. There is currently no quantitative rapid stabilization result for non-self-adjoint parabolic equations.

1.2
Quantitative rapid stabilization for multidimensional parabolic equations

The first main result can be stated as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

There exists a constant
\tilde{C}=\tilde{C}\left(\Omega,d,\omega,a,b\right)>0
such that for any
\lambda>1
, there is a stationary feedback law
\mathcal{K}_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{L}(L^{2}(\Omega))
so that the Cauchy problem (
1.1
) with
u=\mathcal{K}_{\lambda}y
admits a unique solution
y\in C([0,+\infty);L^{2}(\Omega))
verifying

\|u(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|y(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq e^{\tilde{C}\sqrt{\lambda}}e^{-{\lambda}t}\|y_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\;\;\forall t>0.

(1.5)

Moreover, the stabilizing feedback can be represented in the form
u=-\chi_{\omega}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}y
, where
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{L}(L^{2}(\Omega))
is the solution of the operator Riccati equation

\left\langle(\Delta-a-b\cdot\nabla+\lambda I){z},\,\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}{z}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\frac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}{z}\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla{z}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=0,\quad\forall{z}\in H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H_{0}^{1}(\Omega).

(1.6)

Remark 1.2
.

Several comments are given in order.

1.

This is the first result on the quantitative rapid stabilization for multidimensional non-self-adjoint parabolic equations. Note that the non-quantitative rapid stabilization of this model has been achieved in earlier works

2.

Our method combines LQ theory directly with quantitative observability inequalities, and the analysis relies on two key ingredients. On one hand, we adopt a well-chosen cost functional

\mathcal{J}(y,u;y_{0})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{2\lambda t}\left(\|(-\Delta)^{\alpha}y(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|u(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)dt,\quad\alpha\in[0,1],

(1.7)

In this sense, the present approach is of a different nature from the more explicit constructions arising, for instance, in backstepping or frequency-Lyapunov methods. A related use of the Gramian method and control costs also appears in

3.

In fact, when

1.3
An abstract equivalence between quantitative rapid stabilization and quantitative observability inequality

Our second main result is an abstract formulation of the above method. Let
X
and
U
be separable Hilbert spaces. Let
A:D(A)\subset X\to X
be a strictly positive self-adjoint operator with a dense domain and compact resolvent. The perturbation operator
A_{0}
belongs to
\mathcal{L}\big(D(A^{1/2}),X\big)
and admits a continuous extension to
\mathcal{L}\big(X,D(A^{1/2})^{\prime}\big)
. Let the control operator
B\in\mathcal{L}(U,X)
. We consider the linear control system

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}y+Ay+A_{0}y=Bu\quad\text{for }t>0,\\
y(0)=y_{0}\in X,\end{cases}

(1.8)

where the control
u
belongs to
L^{2}(0,+\infty;U)
. The equation (
1.8
) admits a unique solution
y\in C([0,+\infty);X)\cap L^{2}_{\text{loc}}(0,+\infty;D(A^{1/2}))
; see
[
27
]
.

Definition 1.3
.

We say that the system (
1.8
) is
e^{\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{\frac{p}{1+p}})}
-rapidly stabilizable if there exists a constant
C>0
such that, for every
\lambda>1
, one can find a feedback operator
K_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{L}(X,U)
, for which the solution of the closed-loop system with
u=K_{\lambda}y
satisfies

\|u(t)\|_{U}+\|y(t)\|_{X}\leq e^{C\lambda^{\frac{p}{1+p}}}e^{-\lambda t}\|y_{0}\|_{X},\quad\forall t>0.

(1.9)

Definition 1.4
.

We say that the system (
1.8
) is
e^{\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{T^{p}})}
-observable if there exists a constant
C>0
such that, for every

, the following observability estimate holds:

\|\phi(0)\|_{X}^{2}\leq e^{\frac{C}{T^{p}}}\int_{0}^{T}\|B^{*}\phi(t)\|_{U}^{2}\,dt,

(1.10)

for every solution
\phi
to the adjoint system

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\phi-(A+A_{0})^{*}\phi=0\quad\text{for }t\in(0,T),\\
\phi(T)\in X.\end{cases}

Definition

introduces

has been extensively studied, starting with Seidman’s work
[
35
]
on the one-dimensional heat equation, which relies on solving a “window problem” for series of complex exponentials. Subsequent developments include the moment method
[
28
,
37
]
, global Carleman estimates
[
14
,
15
]
, the spectral inequality method
[
1
,
32
]
, the frequency-Lyapunov method
[
43
,
44
]
, the frequency function method
[
34
]
, and propagation of smallness
[

]
.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
p>0
. The control system (
1.8
) is
e^{\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{\frac{p}{1+p}})}\text{-rapidly stabilizable}
if and only if it is
e^{\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{T^{p}})}
-observable.

Remark 1.6
.

Based on the equivalence between weak observability inequalities and exponential stabilization introduced by Trélat-Wang-Xu
[
38
]
, the authors in
[
29
]
further established a family of weak observability inequalities to characterize the rapid stabilization. Notably, the dependence among the parameters involved in these inequalities has not yet been clarified. In this work, we propose quantitative observability inequalities as a strengthened version of those in
[
29
]
. Using the explicit form of the observation cost, we obtain a sharper result on the quantitative rapid stabilization. A possible further direction is to obtain quantitative rapid stabilization from a sequence of weak observability inequalities.

1.4
Further application to Navier–Stokes equations around non-trivial states

As an application to nonlinear equations, we establish the quantitative rapid stabilization for the two-dimensional Navier–Stokes equations around steady states. Define

\mathcal{H}:=\{y\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{2}:\ \operatorname{div}y=0\text{ in }\Omega,\ y\cdot n=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega\}\quad\text{with }\|y\|_{\mathcal{H}}:=\|y\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{2}}\text{ for }y\in\mathcal{H},

\mathcal{H}_{1}:=\{y\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)^{2}:\ \operatorname{div}y=0\text{ in }\Omega\}\quad\text{with }\|y\|_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}:=\|\nabla y\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{2}}\text{ for }y\in\mathcal{H}_{1}.

Let
(y_{e},p_{e})\in H^{2}(\Omega)^{2}\times H^{1}(\Omega)
be a solution of the stationary system

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\Delta y_{e}+(y_{e}\cdot\nabla)y_{e}+\nabla p_{e}=f&&\text{in }\Omega,\\
&\nabla\cdot y_{e}=0&&\text{in }\Omega,\\
&y_{e}=0&&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

We consider the following controlled Navier–Stokes equation

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}y-\Delta y+(y\cdot\nabla)y+\nabla p=f+\chi_{\omega}u&&\text{in }(0,+\infty)\times\Omega,\\
&\nabla\cdot y=0&&\text{in }(0,+\infty)\times\Omega,\\
&y=0&&\text{on }(0,+\infty)\times\partial\Omega,\\
&y(0,\cdot)=y_{0}&&\text{in }\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

(1.11)

The theorem below reveals the quantitative rapid stabilization of (
1.11
) around the steady state
y_{e}
.

Theorem 1.7
.

There exists a constant
\overline{C}=\overline{C}\left(\Omega,\omega,y_{e}\right)>0
with the following property: for any
\lambda>1
, there exists a stationary feedback law
\mathcal{K}_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H},L^{2}(\Omega)^{2})
such that, for any
y_{0}-y_{e}\in\mathcal{H}_{1}
satisfying
\|y_{0}-y_{e}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq e^{-\overline{C}\sqrt{\lambda}}
, the equation (
1.11
) with
u=\mathcal{K}_{\lambda}(y-y_{e})
admits a unique strong solution
(y,p)
verifying

y-y_{e}\in C([0,+\infty);\mathcal{H}_{1})
and

\|u(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{2}}+\|y(t)-y_{e}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq e^{\overline{C}\sqrt{\lambda}}e^{-\lambda t}\|y_{0}-y_{e}\|_{\mathcal{H}},\;\;\forall t>0.

(1.12)

Remark 1.8
.

The proof of Theorem
1.7
relies on the following quantitative observability inequality for the adjoint Oseen system associated with the linearized Navier–Stokes equations:

\|\varphi(0,\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq e^{C(\Omega,\omega,y_{e})/T}\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}((0,T)\times\omega)^{2}}.

The quantitative observability inequality for Oseen systems is implied in
[

]
by means of global Carleman estimates with cost

Hydrodynamic stability is an important topic, concerned with the stability of flows near a steady state or a Couette flow, and is of practical relevance. In particular, stabilization by means of finite-dimensional feedback laws is especially meaningful, both from the theoretical and practical perspectives; relevant results on finite-dimensional rapidly stabilizing feedback laws for the Navier–Stokes equations can be found in

1.5
A finite-dimensional feedback extension for the heat equation and an open problem

As demonstrated in Theorem
1.1
, the feedback control law is constructed via an infinite-dimensional operator Riccati equation. In fact, for the heat equation, our quantitative LQ framework is also applicable to finite-dimensional controls expressed as follows, and it recovers the quantitative result by Xiang
[
44
]
,

u(t,x)=\sum_{1\leq j\leq N}u_{j}(t)\varphi_{j}(x).

The precise statement is given in Section
5
. Finite-dimensional feedback controls are particularly relevant in practice, as they involve only finitely many control channels and are thus easier to implement.

Nevertheless, this finite-dimensional extension relies heavily on the self-adjoint structure of the Laplacian. This method can also be applied to the finite-dimensional quantitative rapid stabilization for the Navier–Stokes equations around the zero steady state. Whether a finite-dimensional feedback law can be established for the non-self-adjoint parabolic equation (
1.1
) still remains unclear. In particular, whether the frequency-Lyapunov approach
[
44
]
that relies on spectral estimates can be used for non-self-adjoint cases is still largely open.

Explicit derivation of the lower bound relies on energy estimates, whereas the upper bound is deduced from quantitative observability inequalities.

Let

z(t)=e^{\lambda t}y(t),\qquad v(t)=e^{\lambda t}u(t).

This reformulates (

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}z-\Delta z+az+b\cdot\nabla z-\lambda z=\chi_{\omega}v&\text{in }(0,+\infty)\times\Omega,\\[5.69054pt]

).

We now formulate the optimal control problem

\Phi_{2}(y_{0}):={\inf}\left\{\mathcal{J}_{2}(z,v;y_{0})\;\middle|\;\begin{array}[]{l}(z,v)\in W(0,+\infty;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega),H^{-1}(\Omega))\times L^{2}(0,+\infty;L^{2}(\Omega)),\\[2.84526pt]
(z,v)\ \text{satisfies \eqref{TRANS-EQ}}\end{array}\right\},

(2.4)

where

\mathcal{J}_{2}(z,v;y_{0})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\|\nabla z(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|v(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)\,dt.

(2.5)

It is straightforward to verify that

\Phi_{2}(y_{0})=\Phi_{1}(y_{0}),\;\;\forall y_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega).

We first investigate the upper bound for (
2.1
) (equivalently, (
2.4
)). To this end, we construct a feasible control starting from (
1.2
). For the parabolic equation (
1.1
), this inequality has been proved through a variety of techniques, notably Carleman estimates
[
14
,
15
]
, spectral inequalities
[
23
,
24
]
, the frequency function method
[
34
]
, and the propagation of smallness for analytic functions
[
1
,
12
]
. By the classical duality principle between observability and null controllability, this estimate immediately yields a null control with an explicit cost.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
T=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}
. There exists a control
u\in L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega)
such that the corresponding solution to (
1.1
) satisfies
y(T,\cdot)=0
. Moreover, the associated control cost satisfies

\|u\|_{L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega)}\leq e^{C(\Omega,d,\omega,a,b)\sqrt{\lambda}}\|y_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

(2.6)

The control constructed in Lemma
2.1
serves as a feasible control for (
2.1
), and the control cost is crucial for establishing the quantitative upper bound below.

Lemma 2.2
.

There exists a constant
\tilde{C}_{1}=\tilde{C}_{1}(\Omega,\omega,d,a,b)>0
such that

{\Phi_{1}}(y_{0})\leq e^{\tilde{C}_{1}\sqrt{\lambda}}\|y_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},\;\;\forall y_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega).

(2.7)

Proof.

We denote by
u
and
y
, respectively, the control and the corresponding solution to the equation (
1.1
) obtained in Lemma
2.1
. Multiplying (
1.1
) by
2y
, we have

\frac{d}{dt}\left(\|y(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)=-2\|\nabla y(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}-2\langle ay(t),y(t)\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-2\langle b\cdot\nabla y(t),y(t)\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+2\langle\chi_{\omega}u(t),y(t)\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

By Young’s inequality, there exists a constant
C_{0}=C_{0}(a,b)>0
such that

\frac{d}{dt}\|y(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq-\|\nabla y(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+C_{0}\|y(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|u(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Integrating over
t\in(0,T)
and using the control cost estimate (
2.6
), we get

\|y(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla y(s)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}ds\leq\|y_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+C_{0}\int_{0}^{t}\|y(s)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}ds+e^{2C(\Omega,d,\omega,a,b)\sqrt{\lambda}}\|y_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

By Grönwall’s inequality,

\|y(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq 2e^{2C(\Omega,d,\omega,a,b)\sqrt{\lambda}}\|y_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}e^{\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}}C_{0}ds}\leq e^{C_{1}(\Omega,d,\omega,a,b)\sqrt{\lambda}}\|y_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Substituting back into the previous inequality, we immediately obtain that

\int_{0}^{T}\|\nabla y(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}dt\leq e^{C_{2}(\Omega,d,\omega,a,b)\sqrt{\lambda}}\|y_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Finally, using the control cost estimate again, we easily deduce

\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}}e^{2\lambda t}\left(\|\nabla y(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|u(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)dt\leq e^{C_{3}(\Omega,d,\omega,a,b)\sqrt{\lambda}}\|y_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Define
\widetilde{u}=\begin{cases}u&\text{on }(0,T],\\
0&\text{on }(T,+\infty).\end{cases}
Then the corresponding solution is
\widetilde{y}=\begin{cases}y&\text{on }(0,T],\\
0&\text{on }(T,+\infty).\end{cases}

Hence,

\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{2\lambda t}\left(\|\nabla\widetilde{y}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\widetilde{u}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)dt\leq e^{C_{3}(\Omega,d,\omega,a,b)\sqrt{\lambda}}\|y_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Since
y(T)=0
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, the zero extension
\widetilde{y}
belongs to

W(0,+\infty;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega),H^{-1}(\Omega))
. It readily follows that
(\widetilde{y},\widetilde{u})
is a feasible pair for
\Phi_{1}(y_{0})
. By the definition of
\Phi_{1}(y_{0})
, we complete the proof.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

Instead of directly using the observability inequality, we transform it into a null controllability result with an explicit control cost. By constructing a feasible control, an upper bound for the optimal control problem is derived, and the quantitative form of this upper bound follows from the quantitative control cost. The quantitative observability inequality has been extensively studied in the literature, including classical parabolic equations (
1.1
), higher-order parabolic equations
[
12
]
, degenerate parabolic equations
[
31
]
, Oseen systems
[
7
,
13
]
, and other related models.

The lower bound can be obtained via a straightforward energy estimate.

Lemma 2.4
.

There exists a constant
\tilde{C}_{2}=\tilde{C}_{2}(\Omega,d,a,b)>0
such that

{\Phi_{2}}(y_{0})\geq\tilde{C}_{2}\|y_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},\;\;\forall y_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega).

(2.8)

Proof.

Since
\Phi_{2}(y_{0})=\Phi_{1}(y_{0})<\infty
, the standard minimizing sequence argument combined with the convexity of the cost functional (
2.5
) implies that
\Phi_{2}(y_{0})
is attained by a unique optimal pair
(z^{*},v^{*})
, so that the infimum is actually a minimum.

Multiplying the equation (
2.3
) by
2z^{*}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\|z^{*}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=-2\|\nabla z^{*}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}-2\langle az^{*}(t),z^{*}(t)\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-2\langle b\cdot\nabla z^{*}(t),z^{*}(t)\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

(2.9)

\displaystyle+2\lambda\|z^{*}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+2\langle\chi_{\omega}v^{*}(t),z^{*}(t)\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

From

\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\|\nabla z^{*}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|v^{*}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)dt<\infty,

together with Poincaré’s inequality, we conclude that

\liminf\limits_{t\to\infty}\|z^{*}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=0
.
Hence, there exists an increasing sequence
\{T_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}}
satisfying
T_{n}>T_{n-1}
and
\|z^{*}(T_{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{1}{n},\forall\,n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
.

Integrating (
2.9
) over
(0,T_{n})
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|y_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}-\|z^{*}(T_{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{T_{n}}\bigg(\|\nabla z^{*}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\langle az^{*}(t),z^{*}(t)\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\langle b\cdot\nabla z^{*}(t),z^{*}(t)\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

(2.10)

\displaystyle-\lambda\|z^{*}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}-\langle\chi_{\omega}v^{*}(t),z^{*}(t)\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\bigg)dt.

Combined with
\|z^{*}(T_{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{1}{n}
, this yields

\|y_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}}+2\int_{0}^{T_{n}}\bigg(\|\nabla z^{*}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\langle az^{*}(t),z^{*}(t)\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\langle b\cdot\nabla z^{*}(t),z^{*}(t)\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\langle\chi_{\omega}v^{*}(t),z^{*}(t)\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\bigg)dt.

Applying Young’s inequality and taking
n\to\infty
, we deduce the lower bound

\tilde{C}_{2}(\Omega,d,a,b)\|y_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\big(\|\nabla z^{*}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|v^{*}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\big)dt=\Phi_{2}(y_{0}).

(2.11)

This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 2.5
.

We further compare our cost functional with the classical low-gain functional in the LQ theory, which corresponds to
\alpha=0
in (
1.7
). In the low-gain case, explicit lower bounds for the associated optimal control problem cannot be established. In contrast, we adopt a high-gain functional with
\alpha=1/2
in this paper, and explicit lower bounds can be directly deduced by means of energy estimates, as presented in (
2.11
).

The following lemma is a standard consequence of the LQ theory for parabolic equations. We omit its proof and refer the reader to
[
5
]
for a detailed argument.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\lambda>1
. For every
y_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, let
(z^{*},v^{*})
be the optimal pair of the problem (
2.4
). Then there exists a self-adjoint, positive operator
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{L}(L^{2}(\Omega))
such that

\Phi_{2}(y_{0})=\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}y_{0},y_{0}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\quad\forall y_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega),

(2.12)

and the optimal pair satisfies

v^{*}(t)=-\chi_{\omega}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}z^{*}(t),\;\;t>0.

Moreover,
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{L}(L^{2}(\Omega))
is the solution of the operator Riccati equation (
1.6
).

From (
2.7
), the self-adjoint positive operator
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}
satisfies

\|\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}\|_{\mathcal{L}(L^{2}(\Omega))}=\sup_{\|y\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq 1}|\langle\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}y,y\rangle|\leq 2e^{\tilde{C}_{1}\sqrt{\lambda}}.

Now, we have all the ingredients to prove Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.
It can be readily shown that for all
t\geq 0
, the time-shifted pair
(z^{*}(t+\cdot),v^{*}(t+\cdot))
constitutes an optimal pair corresponding to the initial state
z^{*}(t)
. Accordingly,

\Phi_{2}(z^{*}(t))=\frac{1}{2}\int_{t}^{\infty}\left(\|\nabla z^{*}(s)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|v^{*}(s)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)\,ds,\quad\forall t\geq 0.

By Lemmas
2.2
and
2.4
, it holds that

\tilde{C}_{2}\|z^{*}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\Phi_{2}(z^{*}(t))\leq\Phi_{2}(y_{0}){=\Phi_{1}(y_{0})}\leq e^{\tilde{C}_{1}\sqrt{\lambda}}\|y_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},\quad\forall t\geq 0,

which implies that

\|z^{*}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\tilde{C}_{2}^{-1}e^{\tilde{C}_{1}\sqrt{\lambda}}\|y_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},\quad\forall t\geq 0.

Considering
y(t)=e^{-\lambda t}z^{*}(t)
, the previous estimate yields

\|y(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=e^{-2\lambda t}\|z^{*}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\tilde{C}_{2}^{-1}e^{\tilde{C}_{1}\sqrt{\lambda}}e^{-2\lambda t}\|y_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},\quad\forall t\geq 0.

(2.13)

Recalling that
v^{*}=-\chi_{\omega}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}z^{*}
, we infer that

u(t)=e^{-\lambda t}v^{*}(t)=-e^{-\lambda t}\chi_{\omega}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}z^{*}(t)=-\chi_{\omega}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}y(t),\quad\forall t>0.

In particular,

\|u(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\|\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}\|_{\mathcal{L}(L^{2}(\Omega))}\|y(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq 2\tilde{C}_{2}^{-\frac{1}{2}}e^{\frac{3}{2}\tilde{C}_{1}\sqrt{\lambda}}e^{-\lambda t}\|y_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\quad\forall t>0.

(2.14)

Hence, (
2.13
) and (
2.14
) yield the desired quantitative rapid stabilization estimate.
\square

3
Equivalence between quantitative rapid stabilization and quantitative observability

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.5
. The proof is divided into two parts. First, for systems admitting the quantitative rapid stabilization, we adopt the time iteration strategy proposed in
[
9
]
and follow the parameter selection scheme in
[
44
]
. We construct piecewise feedback laws with increasing decay rates to achieve the null controllability with explicit control costs. The quantitative observability inequality is then established via the duality principle. Conversely, the quantitative LQ framework developed in Section
2
gives the quantitative rapid stabilization.

Step 1. We first prove that
e^{\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{\frac{p}{p+1}})}
-rapidly stabilizable implies
e^{\mathcal{O}({\frac{1}{T^{p}}})}
-observable.

To this end, we introduce the sequence

T_{0}=0,\qquad T_{n}=T_{n-1}+\frac{T}{2^{n}},\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}^{+},

so that
\lim\limits_{n\to+\infty}T_{n}=T
. We also fix constants

Q=\max\{2^{p+2}C,2\}\quad\text{and}\quad M=\frac{Q^{p+1}(2^{p+1}-1)}{2^{p+2}(2^{p}-1)},

and define

\lambda_{n}=\frac{Q^{p+1}2^{(p+1)n}}{T^{p+1}},\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}.

For each interval
(T_{n},T_{n+1}]
, we apply the feedback law associated with the parameter
\lambda_{n}
, namely the following time-varying feedback control

u(t)=\mathcal{U}(t,y(t)):=K_{\lambda_{n}}y(t),\quad\forall t\in(T_{n},T_{n+1}].

(3.1)

Therefore, since the control is given by a linear bounded feedback law on each interval, the closed-loop system admits a unique solution
y\in C([0,T);X)
.

By the quantitative stabilization estimate corresponding to
\lambda_{0}
,
we obtain on the first interval
(T_{0},T_{1}]
that

\|y(T_{1})\|_{X}\leq e^{C\lambda_{0}^{\frac{p}{p+1}}-\lambda_{0}\frac{T}{2}}\|y_{0}\|_{X}\leq e^{\frac{Q^{p+1}}{T^{p}}\frac{1-2^{p+1}}{2^{p+2}}}\|y_{0}\|_{X}=e^{-\frac{M(2^{p}-1)}{T^{p}}}\|y_{0}\|_{X}.

Iterating the same argument on each time interval yields

\|y(T_{n})\|_{X}\leq\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}e^{-2^{kp}\frac{M(2^{p}-1)}{T^{p}}}\|y_{0}\|_{X}=e^{-\frac{M(2^{np}-1)}{T^{p}}}\|y_{0}\|_{X},\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}.

Moreover, for
t\in(T_{n},T_{n+1}]
with
n\geq 1
, we have

\|y(t)\|_{X}\leq e^{C\lambda_{n}^{\frac{p}{p+1}}}e^{-\frac{M(2^{np}-1)}{T^{p}}}\|y_{0}\|_{X}\leq e^{-\frac{M(2^{(n-1)p}-1)}{T^{p}}}\|y_{0}\|_{X}.

Note that
p>0
. Letting
n\to\infty
, and using
\lim\limits_{n\to+\infty}T_{n}=T
, we conclude that
\lim\limits_{t\to T^{-}}\|y(t)\|_{X}=0
.

We now estimate the corresponding control cost. For every
n\geq 1
and
t\in(T_{n},T_{n+1}]
, the quantitative bound on the feedback law gives

\|u(t)\|_{U}\leq e^{C\lambda_{n}^{\frac{p}{p+1}}}\|y(T_{n})\|_{X}\leq e^{-\frac{M(2^{(n-1)p}-1)}{T^{p}}}\|y_{0}\|_{X}\leq e^{\frac{M}{T^{p}}}\|y_{0}\|_{X}.

Similarly, for
t\in(T_{0},T_{1}]
,

\|u(t)\|_{U}\leq e^{C\lambda_{0}^{\frac{p}{p+1}}}\|y_{0}\|_{X}\leq e^{\frac{Q^{p+1}}{2^{p+2}T^{p}}}\|y_{0}\|_{X}\leq e^{\frac{M}{T^{p}}}\|y_{0}\|_{X}.

Therefore, we obtain that the equation (
1.8
) is null controllable with the following control cost,

\|u\|_{L^{2}(0,+\infty;U)}=\|u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;U)}\leq T^{1/2}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(0,+\infty;U)}\leq e^{\frac{M}{T^{p}}}\|y_{0}\|_{X}.

Since
u\in L^{2}(0,+\infty;U)
, it follows that
y\in C([0,T];X)
. From
\lim\limits_{t\to T^{-}}\|y(t)\|_{X}=0
, we derive
y(T)=0
in
X
. By the classical duality principle, this completes the proof that the original system is
e^{\mathcal{O}({\frac{1}{T^{p}}})}
-observable.

Step 2. We prove the converse implication.

In what follows,
C_{j}
denote generic positive constants independent of
y_{0}
and
\lambda
, unless otherwise specified. Arguing as in the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we introduce the weighted variables

z(t)=e^{\lambda t}y(t),\quad v(t)=e^{\lambda t}u(t).

Then the system (
1.8
) is transformed into

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}z+Az+A_{0}z-\lambda z=Bv\quad\text{for }t>0,\\[2.84526pt]
z(0)=y_{0}\in X.\end{cases}

(3.2)

We consider the following infinite-horizon LQ optimal control problem

\Phi_{3}(y_{0}):={\inf}\left\{\mathcal{J}_{3}(y,u;y_{0})\;\middle|\;\begin{array}[]{l}(y,u)\in W_{\lambda}(0,+\infty;D(A^{\frac{1}{2}}),D(A^{\frac{1}{2}})^{\prime})\times L^{2}_{\lambda}(0,+\infty;U),\\[2.84526pt]
(y,u)\ \text{satisfies \eqref{ABSTR-EQ-INTR}}\end{array}\right\},

(3.3)

where

\mathcal{J}_{3}(y,u;y_{0})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{2\lambda t}\left(\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}y(t)\|_{X}^{2}+\|u(t)\|_{U}^{2}\right)\,dt,

(3.4)

and its equivalent problem

\Phi_{4}(y_{0}):={\inf}\left\{\mathcal{J}_{4}(z,v;y_{0})\;\middle|\;\begin{array}[]{l}(z,v)\in W(0,+\infty;D(A^{\frac{1}{2}}),D(A^{\frac{1}{2}})^{\prime})\times L^{2}(0,+\infty;U),\\[2.84526pt]
(z,v)\ \text{satisfies \eqref{ABSTR-EQ-2}}\end{array}\right\},

(3.5)

where

\mathcal{J}_{4}(z,v;y_{0})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}z(t)\|_{X}^{2}+\|v(t)\|_{U}^{2}\right)\,dt.

(3.6)

It holds that

\Phi_{4}(y_{0})=\Phi_{3}(y_{0}),\qquad\forall y_{0}\in X.

By the quantitative observability inequality (
1.10
) and the standard duality argument, taking
T=\lambda^{-\frac{1}{p+1}}
, we obtain a control
u\in L^{2}(0,T;U)
such that the solution
y\in W\big(0,T;D(A^{1/2}),D(A^{1/2})^{\prime}\big)
to the equation (
1.8
) satisfies
y(T)=0
. Moreover,

\|u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;U)}\leq e^{C\lambda^{\frac{p}{p+1}}}\|y_{0}\|_{X}.

(3.7)

Next, applying the energy estimate to (
1.8
), we obtain

\frac{d}{dt}\|y(t)\|_{X}^{2}=-2\langle Ay(t),y(t)\rangle_{X}-2\langle A_{0}y(t),y(t)\rangle_{X}+2\langle Bu(t),y(t)\rangle_{X},\;\;\forall t>0.

Since
A_{0}\in\mathcal{L}(D(A^{\frac{1}{2}}),X)
, by Young’s inequality, there exists a constant
C_{0}=C_{0}(A_{0},B)>0
such that

\frac{d}{dt}\|y(t)\|_{X}^{2}\leq-\|A^{1/2}y(t)\|_{X}^{2}+C_{0}\|y(t)\|_{X}^{2}+\|u(t)\|_{U}^{2},\;\;\forall t\geq 0.

(3.8)

Using (
3.7
) and Grönwall’s inequality, we see that

\|y(t)\|_{X}^{2}\leq e^{C_{1}\lambda^{\frac{p}{p+1}}}\|y_{0}\|_{X}^{2},\quad\forall t\in[0,T].

Substituting this estimate back into the previous inequality (
3.8
), we also obtain

\int_{0}^{T}\|A^{1/2}y(t)\|_{X}^{2}\,dt\leq e^{C_{2}\lambda^{\frac{p}{p+1}}}\|y_{0}\|_{X}^{2}.

(3.9)

Define
\widetilde{u}=\begin{cases}u&\text{on }(0,T],\\
0&\text{on }(T,+\infty).\end{cases}
Then the corresponding solution is
\widetilde{y}=\begin{cases}y&\text{on }(0,T],\\
0&\text{on }(T,+\infty).\end{cases}
Since
y(T)=0
in
X
, the zero extension
\widetilde{y}
belongs to
W(0,+\infty;D(A^{1/2}),D(A^{1/2})^{\prime})
. Hence,
(\widetilde{y},\widetilde{u})
is a feasible pair for
\Phi_{3}(y_{0})
. From (
3.7
) and (
3.9
), we have

\Phi_{3}(y_{0})\leq e^{C_{3}\lambda^{\frac{p}{p+1}}}\|y_{0}\|_{X}^{2},\quad\forall y_{0}\in X.

(3.10)

Since
\Phi_{4}(y_{0})=\Phi_{3}(y_{0})<\infty
, the continuous embedding of
W\big(0,T;D(A^{1/2}),D(A^{1/2})^{\prime}\big)
into
C([0,T];X)
together with the minimizing sequence analysis ensures that the infimum defining
\Phi_{4}(y_{0})
is attained by an optimal pair
(z^{*},v^{*})
, so the infimum is actually a minimum. Uniqueness follows from the convexity of the cost functional (
3.6
). Furthermore, by analogous arguments to those for the parabolic case, we obtain the coercive lower bound

C_{4}\|y_{0}\|_{X}^{2}\leq\Phi_{4}(y_{0}),\quad\forall y_{0}\in X.

(3.11)

Combining (
3.10
) and (
3.11
), we infer that
\Phi_{4}
is a continuous, positive quadratic form on
X
, induced by the symmetric bilinear form
\Upsilon\colon X\times X\to\mathbb{R}
, where

\Upsilon(y,w)=\frac{1}{4}\big(\Phi_{4}(y+w)-\Phi_{4}(y-w)\big),\;\;\forall y,w\in X.

Hence, by the Riesz representation theorem for continuous quadratic forms on Hilbert spaces, there exists a bounded self-adjoint positive operator
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{L}(X)
such that

\Phi_{4}(y_{0})=\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}y_{0},y_{0}\rangle_{X},\quad\forall y_{0}\in X,

(3.12)

and
\|\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}\|_{\mathcal{L}(X)}=\sup\limits_{\|y\|_{X}\leq 1}|\langle\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}y,y\rangle_{X}|\leq 2e^{C_{3}\lambda^{\frac{p}{p+1}}}.

Given any
T\in(0,+\infty)
, consider the following Bolza problem

\Phi_{T}(y_{0}):={\inf}\left\{\mathcal{J}_{T}(z,v;y_{0})\;\middle|\;\begin{array}[]{l}(z,v)\in W\big(0,T;D(A^{1/2}),D(A^{1/2})^{\prime}\big)\times L^{2}(0,T;U),\\[2.84526pt]
(z,v)\text{ satisfies \eqref{ABSTR-EQ-2}},\end{array}\right\},

(3.13)

where

\mathcal{J}_{T}(z,v;y_{0})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\left(\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}z(t)\|_{X}^{2}+\|v(t)\|_{U}^{2}\right)dt+\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}z(T),z(T)\rangle_{X}.

(3.14)

According to the dynamic programming principle (cf.
[
6
, Lemma 3.10]
), the optimal pair
(z^{*},v^{*})
of
\Phi_{4}(y_{0})
, when restricted to
(0,T)
, is exactly the optimal pair for
\Phi_{T}(y_{0})
.

By the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) theorem for (
3.13
) (see
[
6
, Theorem A.1]
), there exists a Lagrange multiplier
(\zeta,q)\in X\times L^{2}(0,T;D(A^{1/2}))
such that for any
(\xi,\eta)\in W\big(0,T;D(A^{1/2}),D(A^{1/2})^{\prime}\big)\times L^{2}(0,T;U)
, it holds that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\big(\langle A^{1/2}z^{*}(t),A^{1/2}\xi(t)\rangle_{X}+\langle v^{*}(t),\eta(t)\rangle_{U}\big)dt+\langle\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}z^{*}(T),\xi(T)\rangle_{X}

(4.4)

Lemma 4.1

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.

We apply the linear bounded feedback law
u=-B^{*}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}w
. Since

, for any
T\in(0,+\infty)
, the closed-loop system (

) admits a unique strong solution

(cf.
[
5
, Theorem 1.7]
and
[
36

). It follows that

.

Consider the Lyapunov function

\mathcal{V}(t):=\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}w(t),\,w(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}},\;\;t\geq 0.

Differentiating with respect to
t
yields
\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{V}(t)=\langle w_{t}(t),\,\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}w(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
. It readily follows that

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{V}=-\langle Aw,\,\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}w\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}-\langle A_{0}w,\,\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}w\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}-\|B^{*}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}w\|_{U}^{2}-\langle a(w,w),\,\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}w\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{H}_{1}}.

Together with the Riccati equation (
4.8
), we further deduce

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{V}(t)+2\lambda\mathcal{V}(t)\leq-\frac{1}{2}\|w(t)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}^{2}+|\langle a(w(t),w(t)),\,\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}w(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{H}_{1}}|.

Using the estimate (
4.2
) for the Navier–Stokes bilinear term, together with (
4.10
), we infer that

\bigl|\langle a(w(t),w(t)),\,\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}w(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{H}_{1}}\bigr|\leq e^{\widetilde{C}_{4}\sqrt{\lambda}}\|w(t)\|_{\mathcal{H}}\|w(t)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}^{2},

for some

. Hence, we arrive at

(4.15)

we have

Finite-dimensional quantitative rapidly stabilizing laws

This section presents the extension of our quantitative LQ framework to finite-dimensional feedback laws. We first observe that the control in Theorem
1.1
is in general infinite-dimensional. For self-adjoint systems such as the heat equation, we derive low-frequency quantitative estimates via spectral inequalities and employ the frequency-Lyapunov method for the finite-dimensional quantitative rapid stabilization.

Lemma 5.1
.

The range of
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}
given by Theorem
1.1
is not finite-dimensional.

Proof.

We argue by contradiction. Assume that the range of
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}
is finite-dimensional. Then
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}
is a finite-rank operator. Hence, there exists
y\in L^{2}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}
such that
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}y=0
. It readily follows from (
2.8
) and (
2.12
) that
y=0
. This contradiction shows that the range of
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}
cannot be finite-dimensional.
∎

Let
\{(\lambda_{n},\varphi_{n})\}_{n\geq 1}
be the eigenvalue-eigenfunction pairs of
-\Delta
in
H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, with
0<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots
and
\{\varphi_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
forming an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\Omega)
. Let
N(\lambda)
be the number of eigenvalues
\lambda_{n}\leq\lambda
, counted with multiplicity; equivalently,
\lambda_{N(\lambda)}\leq\lambda<\lambda_{N(\lambda)+1}
. We then define the low-frequency projection
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{L}(L^{2}(\Omega))
by

\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}f=\sum_{j=1}^{N(\lambda)}\langle f,\varphi_{j}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,\varphi_{j},\quad\forall f\in L^{2}(\Omega).

(5.1)

Using the frequency-Lyapunov method
[
44
,
45
]
, we establish the finite-dimensional quantitative rapid stabilization for the heat equation. This is accomplished by incorporating refined low- and high-frequency separation into the Lyapunov functional design and balancing their contributions through suitable low-frequency estimates.

Theorem 5.2
.

There exists a constant
\widehat{C}=\widehat{C}(\Omega,d,\omega)>0
such that, for any
\lambda>1
, one can find
N=N(\lambda)
and a positive definite matrix
P_{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
such that for every
y_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, the equation

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}y-\Delta y=\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq j\leq N}u_{j}\,\chi_{\omega}\varphi_{j}&\text{in }(0,+\infty)\times\Omega,\\[2.84526pt]
y=0&\text{on }(0,+\infty)\times\partial\Omega,\\[2.84526pt]
y(0,\cdot)=y_{0},\end{cases}

(5.2)

with the following feedback law

U(t):=\begin{pmatrix}u_{1}(t)\\
\vdots\\
u_{N}(t)\end{pmatrix}=-\,B_{1}^{\top}P_{\lambda}\begin{pmatrix}\langle y(t),\varphi_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
\vdots\\
\langle y(t),\varphi_{N}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\end{pmatrix},\quad B_{1}=\bigl(\langle\varphi_{i},\varphi_{j}\rangle_{L^{2}(\omega)}\bigr)_{1\leq i,j\leq N},

(5.3)

admits a unique solution
y\in C\bigl([0,+\infty);L^{2}(\Omega)\bigr)
satisfying

\|U(t)\|_{2}+\|y(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq e^{\widehat{C}\sqrt{\lambda}}e^{-\frac{\lambda}{2}t}\|y_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\quad\forall t>0.

(5.4)

Moreover,
P_{\lambda}
is the unique positive definite solution of the algebraic Riccati equation

A_{1}^{\top}P_{\lambda}+P_{\lambda}A_{1}-P_{\lambda}B_{1}B_{1}^{\top}P_{\lambda}+I=0,

(5.5)

where
A_{1}=\text{diag }(\frac{\lambda}{2}-\lambda_{1},\dots,\frac{\lambda}{2}-\lambda_{N})
.

Set
z(t)=e^{\frac{\lambda}{2}t}y(t)
, let
v_{j}(t)=e^{\frac{\lambda}{2}t}u_{j}(t)
for
j=1,\dots,N
, and denote the control vector by
V(t)=(v_{1}(t),\dots,v_{N}(t))^{\top}
. Then
(z,V)
satisfies

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}z-\Delta z-\frac{\lambda}{2}z=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\chi_{\omega}v_{j}\varphi_{j}&\text{in }(0,+\infty)\times\Omega,\\
z=0&\text{on }(0,+\infty)\times\partial\Omega,\\
z(0,\cdot)=y_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega).\end{cases}

(5.6)

Define the low-frequency mode vector of
z(t)
as
Z(t)=\left(\langle z(t),\varphi_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\dots,\langle z(t),\varphi_{N}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)^{\top}
.
We then perform a spectral decomposition on (
5.6
) to obtain the low-frequency system

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}Z(t)

\displaystyle=A_{1}Z(t)+B_{1}V(t).

(5.7)

Let
Y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. We proceed with the stabilization of this low-frequency system first. A classical approach is to formulate the finite-dimensional optimal control problem

\Phi_{5}(Y_{0}):={\inf}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\|Z(t)\|_{2}^{2}+\|V(t)\|_{2}^{2}\right)dt\;\middle|\;\begin{array}[]{l}(Z,V)\in L^{2}(0,+\infty;\mathbb{R}^{N})\times L^{2}(0,+\infty;\mathbb{R}^{N}),\\[2.84526pt]
(Z,V)\ \text{satisfies \eqref{ODE-EQ} and $Z(0)=Y_{0}$}\end{array}\right\}.

(5.8)

The lemma below is used to derive the quantitative upper bound of (
5.8
) and follows directly from spectral inequalities of the Laplace operator; see
[
44
, Lemma 2.6]
and the relevant spectral inequalities in
[
23
,
24
,
25
]
.

Lemma 5.3
.

There exists a constant
C=C(\Omega,d,\omega)\geq 1
such that for any
Y_{N(\lambda)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N(\lambda)}
, it holds that

Y_{N(\lambda)}^{\top}B_{1}Y_{N(\lambda)}\geq e^{-C(\Omega,d,\omega)\sqrt{\lambda}}\|Y_{N(\lambda)}\|^{2}_{2}.

(5.9)

For the problem
\Phi_{5}(Y_{0})
, there exists
C_{1}=C_{1}(\Omega,d,\omega)>0
such that

\Phi_{5}(Y_{0})\leq e^{C_{1}\sqrt{\lambda}}\|Y_{0}\|_{2}^{2},\quad\forall Y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

(5.10)

Indeed, we set the control in (
5.7
) as the feedback law
V(t)=-\lambda e^{C\sqrt{\lambda}}Z(t)
. It readily follows that

\frac{d}{dt}\|Z(t)\|_{2}^{2}\leq Z(t)^{\top}\Bigl({\lambda}I-2\lambda e^{C\sqrt{\lambda}}B_{1}\Bigr)Z(t).

Applying (
5.9
), we obtain
\sigma_{\max}(\lambda I-2\lambda e^{C\sqrt{\lambda}}B_{1})\leq-{\lambda}
. Hence,
\frac{d}{dt}\|Z(t)\|_{2}^{2}\leq-{\lambda}\|Z(t)\|_{2}^{2}
, which implies that

\|Z(t)\|_{2}^{2}+\|V(t)\|_{2}^{2}\leq(1+\lambda^{2}e^{2C\sqrt{\lambda}})e^{-{\lambda t}}\|Y_{0}\|_{2}^{2},\quad\forall t\geq 0.

Thus (
5.10
) can be derived directly from the definition of
\Phi_{5}(Y_{0})
.

Thus, as seen in
[
30
]
, there exists a unique positive definite matrix
P_{\lambda}
solving (
5.5
), such that

\Phi_{5}(Y_{0})=\frac{1}{2}Y_{0}^{\top}P_{\lambda}Y_{0},\quad\forall Y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

(5.11)

For a symmetric matrix
F
, let
\sigma_{\max}(F)
and
\sigma_{\min}(F)
denote its largest and smallest eigenvalues, respectively. The following lemma gives the low-frequency estimates, which are crucial to derive the quantitative result of the original system (
5.2
) within the framework of the frequency-Lyapunov method.

Lemma 5.4
.

There exists
\tilde{C}=\tilde{C}(\Omega,d,\omega)>0
such that

\sigma_{\max}(P_{\lambda})\leq e^{\tilde{C}\sqrt{\lambda}},\quad\sigma_{\min}(P_{\lambda})\geq e^{-\tilde{C}\sqrt{\lambda}}.

(5.12)

Proof.

On one hand, from (
5.10
) and (
5.11
), we readily
deduce that
\sigma_{\max}(P_{\lambda})\leq 2e^{C_{1}\sqrt{\lambda}}
. On the other hand, since
P_{\lambda}
is the solution of the Riccati equation (
5.5
), we have (see
[
30
]
)

\sigma_{\min}(P_{\lambda})\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma_{\min}^{2}(A_{1})+\sigma_{\max}(B_{1}B_{1}^{\top})}-\sigma_{\min}(A_{1})}\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{9\lambda^{2}/4+N(\lambda)^{2}}+3\lambda/2}.

This completes the proof by Weyl’s law (cf.
[
39
, Section 3.6]
), which implies that
N(\lambda)\leq C(\Omega,d)\lambda^{\frac{d}{2}}
.
∎

We proceed to prove Theorem
5.2
.

Proof of Theorem
5.2
.
Let
Y(t)=\left(\langle y(t),\varphi_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\dots,\langle y(t),\varphi_{N}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)^{\top}
stand for the low-frequency mode vector of
y(t)
. We adopt the feedback law
U=-B_{1}^{\top}P_{\lambda}Y
. For any
T\in(0,+\infty)
, the closed-loop system admits a unique solution
y\in W(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),H^{-1}(\Omega))
; we refer to
[
44
, Theorem 2.3]
.

Define
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}^{\perp}\in\mathcal{L}(L^{2}(\Omega))
by
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}^{\perp}=I-\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}
. We introduce the Lyapunov function

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}(t)=\gamma_{\lambda}Y(t)^{\top}P_{\lambda}Y(t)+\|\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}^{\perp}y(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},\;t\geq 0,

(5.13)

where the constant
\gamma_{\lambda}\geq 1
is to be determined. Clearly,
\mathcal{V}(t)
is well-defined. A simple calculation gives

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{V}(t)+\lambda\mathcal{V}(t)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{\lambda}Y(t)^{\top}(A_{1}P_{\lambda}+P_{\lambda}A_{1})Y(t)-2\gamma_{\lambda}Y(t)^{\top}(P_{\lambda}B_{1}B_{1}^{\top}P_{\lambda})Y(t)

\displaystyle+\left\langle 2\Delta y(t)+\lambda y(t)+2\sum_{j=1}^{N}u_{j}(t)\chi_{\omega}\varphi_{j},\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}^{\perp}y(t)\right\rangle_{H^{-1}(\Omega),H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}.

Using the Riccati equation (
5.5
) satisfied by
P_{\lambda}
,
we derive

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{V}(t)+\lambda\mathcal{V}(t)

\displaystyle\leq-\gamma_{\lambda}\|Y(t)\|_{2}^{2}-2\|\nabla\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}^{\perp}y(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\lambda\|\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}^{\perp}y(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+2\sum_{j=1}^{N}|u_{j}(t)|\|\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}^{\perp}y(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

From the spectral property
-\|\nabla\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}^{\perp}y(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq-\lambda\|\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}^{\perp}y(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}
and Young’s inequality, we further derive

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{V}(t)+\lambda\mathcal{V}(t)\leq\bigl(N\sigma_{\max}^{2}(B_{1})\sigma_{\max}^{2}(P_{\lambda})-\gamma_{\lambda}\bigr)\|Y(t)\|_{2}^{2}.

In view of Lemma
5.4
, we take
\gamma_{\lambda}=e^{C_{3}\sqrt{\lambda}}
with a sufficiently large constant
C_{3}=C_{3}(\Omega,d,\omega)>0
such that

N\sigma_{\max}^{2}(B_{1})\sigma_{\max}^{2}(P_{\lambda})-\gamma_{\lambda}\leq 0,

which yields
\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{V}(t)\leq-\lambda\mathcal{V}(t)
. Using the eigenvalue bounds of
P_{\lambda}
, the choice of
\gamma_{\lambda}
, and
\|U(t)\|_{2}\leq\sigma_{\max}(B_{1})\sigma_{\max}(P_{\lambda})\|Y(t)\|_{2}
, we obtain (
5.4
).
\square

Remark 5.5
.

We recover the finite-dimensional quantitative result by Xiang
[
44
]
. Compared with
[
44
]
, our feedback minimizes the low-frequency energy in (
5.8
) for the transformed equation (
5.6
).
