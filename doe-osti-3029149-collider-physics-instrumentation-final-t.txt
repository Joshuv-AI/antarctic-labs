 Collider Physics Instrumentation

Introduction
This project met the goals that were proposed. In the course of the work, related instrumentation
questions arose that spawned new research projects, in some cases leading to new collaborations
and excellent opportunities for students. This research was conducted by PI Sally Seidel,
Research Electrical Engineers Martin Hoeferkamp and Jiahe Si, graduate students Joey
Sorenson, Andrew Gentry, Hijas Farook, Easwar Narayanan, and Saheed Oyeniran, and
undergraduate Honors Physics students Jordan Hernandez, Luis Flores-Sanchez, and Seth
Medina.

Goals
(1) development of thin 3D silicon sensors that multiply charge before and after irradiation, as a
strategy for overcoming charge loss due to radiation damage;
(2) characterization of DC-LGADs to assess the effect of design features such as carbon
implantation and densification of inter‐electrode spacing, to improve their charge collection
ability;
(3) development of AC-LGADs as an evolution of this timing technology aimed at 100% fill
factor.
(4) analysis of the effects of radiation damage upon the ATLAS silicon pixel detectors;
(5) application of ATLAS Run 1 and Run 2 pixel leakage current data to calibrate simulations of
future conditions in the silicon and predict the lifetime of the detector;
(6) leadership of irradiations of new technologies on behalf of HEP experimenters;
(7) development of silicon module quality assurance procedures, and participation in testbeams
for new detector technologies;
(8) projects directed to advancement of research in the HEP community as a whole, and projects
directed to particle physics instrumentation education;
(9) exploratory studies aimed at the FCC.

Accomplishments and Dissemination of Results
(1) We observed controlled charge multiplication in a 3D sensor of pitch 25 microns x 25
microns. We reported this result at several conferences (2nd DRD3 Workshop, CPAD2022,
CPAD2024), and published it in 2024 JINST 19 P12024 (A. Gentry et al.). It is a centerpiece in
the dissertation of UNM doctoral student Andrew Gentry (advised by Seidel). To handle the
small and fast signal in the irradiated sensor, Jiahe Si designed a custom readout system with
large bandwidth and low noise amplification. The sensor was operated in a reduced temperature
environment. Controlled charge multiplication onset at 90 V, consistent with simulation, and by
105 V (still below breakdown) the amplification factor was 1.25. This study was also the basis
for the Honors thesis of Jordan Hernandez.

We initiated a related program of studies of trench-isolated 3D sensors, in collaboration with
G.F. Dalla Betta of Trento University. This program will use the Transient Current Technology
(TCT) facility acquired with DOE support and commissioned by Jiahe Si.

(2) We characterized the operation of DC-LGAD detectors, including measurements of surface
resistivity and acceptor removal constant, as a function of proton exposure and gamma exposure.
This work required development of a custom tracking telescope by Martin Hoeferkamp. Gamma
irradiation reduced the surface resistance by 4 orders of magnitude after application of 2.2 MGy.
Studies following the proton exposure indicated timing resolution on the order of 35 picoseconds
and operability maintained up to about 7 x 1014 neq/cm2. Devices whose material was processed
with carbon co-implantation showed superior resistance to acceptor removal under proton
irradiation. The Honors thesis of Seth Medina is based on his studies of DC-LGADs.

We reported these results (ATLAS HGTD Workshop and PIXEL2022 for gamma results;
VERTEX2023 and CPAD2023 for proton results) and published them in Frontiers in Physics as
www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/ fphy.2022.838463/ (by M. Hoeferkamp et al. for the
gamma studies) and 2024 JINST 19 P05012 (by J. Sorenson et al., for the proton studies). These
studies were a centerpiece of the dissertation of Joey Sorenson, who worked closely on them
with Martin Hoeferkamp and defended his thesis in March 2026. (Joey then accepted his first-
choice postdoctoral position, at Los Alamos.)

(3) Graduate student Hijas Farook collaborated with Si and Seidel as well as BNL scientists
Alessandro Tricoli and Gabriele Giacomini to explore multiple aspects of AC-LGAD behavior, a
central element of his dissertation. These led to publications on "Gain characterization of LGAD
sensors with beta particles and 28-MeV protons," M. Hijas Mohamed Farook et al., 2025 JINST
20 P10016 and "Response of AC-coupled low gain avalanche detectors to ionizing and non-
ionizing radiation damage," J. Si et al., 2026 JINST 21 P02042. In the first of these papers, data
supported by simulation showed that pulse amplitudes and areas are significantly larger for
signals produced by 28-MeV protons (representative of non-MIPs) than for Sr-90 beta particles
(MIPs), and that gain suppression is larger in the non-MIP case. This has implications for use of
AC-LGADs in studies of low-energy rare processes and in medical, biological, and chemical
applications. In the second paper, the characteristics of AC- and DC-LGADs were compared for
doses from zero to 10 MGy and proton fluences from zero to a few times 1015 neq/cm2,
permitting predictions for their operation at future experiments and guiding design choices to
further improve radiation tolerance. Jiahe Si designed and built a custom test stand that was
critical for these studies. Hijas presented his work in an invited plenary talk at CPAD2025. We
also presented it at the 3rd and 4th DRD3 Workshops, the IEEE NSS/MIC, and at the US-Japan
Workshop.

(4) Analysis of the radiation damage effects in the ATLAS silicon pixel detector has been a
central element of the dissertations of Hijas Farook and Saheed Oyeniran, both guided by Seidel
and members of the ATLAS Radiation Damage Working Group. We compared ATLAS leakage
current measurements to predictions from the Hamburg Model, validating the model for planar
sensors at fluences that were not available at the time it was developed. We also made these
data-to-model comparisons for the first time for 3D sensors, a technology that did not exist at the
time that the model was developed; this required careful study of depletion volume and sensor
temperature. Farook also developed a new method for determining the effective band gap of
highly irradiated silicon. We reported results at several meetings (APS-4CS2025, TIPP2023);

(5) Projection of silicon detector characteristics in future operating scenarios was a central
element of the dissertation of Easwar Narayanan (UNM Ph.D. 2024, now an ATLAS postdoc at
Southern Methodist University). Easwar, advised by Seidel, used dedicated ATLAS runs
involving Pixel high voltage scans to explore alternative Hamburg Model parameterizations as
well as to predict operational conditions through the end of LHC Run 3.

(6) In support of Seidel's program, UNM renewed its 2019 CRADA with Sandia National
Laboratories in 2024, providing a further 60 months of irradiation time at the Gamma Irradiation
Facility, Ion Beam Laboratory, and Annular Core Research Reactor. Over the duration of this
grant, Seidel utilized these facilities to provide irradiations of hundreds of samples of new
technologies for particle detection, on behalf of the UNM group as well as colleagues across the
US and worldwide. We also traveled to the Fermilab Irradiation Test Area (ITA) every 2 years
to lead proton irradiations of hundreds of devices similarly on behalf of our own research and
that of the HEP community; the first of these irradiation campaigns was led by Martin
Hoeferkamp, and the most recent by Jiahe Si. In all these cases we handled planning, custom
fixturing, oversight during the run itself, dosimetry, and transport. For many of the devices we
used our UNM measurement laboratory to provide before-and-after operational
characterizations.

(7) In 2023 Easwar Narayanan relocated temporarily to IJC-Lab (Paris) to develop and apply
silicon module quality assurance procedures. This was a collaboration between UNM and
Dimitris Varouchas who was leading ITk pixel module assembly. Easwar's accomplishments
included development of procedures for gluing, coordinate measurement, and records
maintenance. His gift for teaching led him to be quickly promoted to training the local student
and engineer teams at that lab. Also in 2024 Joey Sorenson relocated temporarily to Genova
where he developed software for real time operation of ITk modules in a CERN testbeam; this
was a collaboration between UNM and Claudia Gemme of the INFN.

Student Outcomes and Honors
Following graduation in 2025, Honors student Luis Flores-Sanchez was accepted into the
Physics graduate program at the University of Colorado where he is now a research assistant on
ALICE. Honors student Seth Medina has been accepted into the graduate program at Kansas
where he anticipates joining the LHC group. Honors student Jordan Hernandez was
commissioned as an officer in the US Army in 2024 and is now applying to Physics graduate
programs. Seth Medina was a recipient of the New Mexico Space Grant Undergraduate
Research Scholarship.

Graduate student Joey Sorenson was a recipient of a DOE-INFN Travel Grant to Genova for the
test beam preparations, and a DOE-SCGSR Award that permitted one year's relocation to LBNL.
At LBNL he collaborated with Maurice Garcia-Sciveres and Timon Heim on development of
front end electronics for 4D tracking. He also received the 2023 GIRA Fellowship Honorable
Mention.

Graduate student Andrew Gentry was a recipient of a DOE-SCGSR Award that supported one
year's relocation to SLAC where he collaborated with Chris Kenney and Julie Segal on
development of a new process for fabricating active edge detectors and a process for reducing
defects in MOS devices by using microwave annealing. Two publications on this work are in
preparation now. Andrew also received two New Mexico Space Grant Graduate Research
Fellowships.

Graduate student Hijas Farook received two ATLAS-ATC awards that allowed him to relocate
twice to BNL, where he collaborated with Tricoli and Giacomini on AC-LGAD studies including
operations at the BNL testbeam.

Graduate student Easwar Narayanan received a Chateaubriand Fellowship that supported his
travel expenses to relocate to IJC-Lab for silicon module development studies. He also received
the Sigma Xi Outstanding Graduate Researcher Award, the William G. Larson Memorial Award,
and the Alexander Graham Bell Scholarship.
