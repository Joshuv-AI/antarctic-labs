Luminescence properties of europium doped
and sodium co-doped LiCaAlF 6
TAIT KUCHENBROD
Abstract: This paper serves as a literature review of scintillating thermal neutron detectors,
along with a study of sodium and europium doped LiCaAlF6 (LiCAF) polycrystalline material.
A set of polycrystalline LiCAF material was synthesized with 2 molar percent of europium
(Eu2+) and co-doped with 0, 1, and 2 molar percent of sodium (Na+), with a stoichiometric
synthesis approach in a glassy-carbon crucible. The quantum yield and photoluminescence
spectrum at room temperature was analyzed. For the first time, we have demonstrated that Na-
co-doping directly alters the quantum yield. The quantum yield for LiCAF:Eu2+, with 0, 1, and
2 molar percent of sodium (Na+) was 0.578, 0.635, and 0.580, respectively. The
photoluminescence (PL) spectrum was also analyzed for all samples. The photo-luminescence
excitation (PLE) spectrum showed a maximum at 300 nm, while the emission spectrum showed
a maximum at 370 nm, corresponding to the Eu2+ 5d-4f transitions.
1. Introduction
Thermal neutron detectors are used across a wide range of applications, such as in the medical
field for cancer treatment, homeland security, astrophysics and nuclear reactor monitoring1.
Neutrons are difficult to detect due to their neutral charge; however, indirect detection becomes
possible using special isotopes. Thermal neutrons, or free neutrons that have slowed to thermal
equilibrium with their environment (≈25 meV), have high capture cross sections with several
isotopes (e.g. 3He, 6Li, and 10B)2. For decades, the gold standard for thermal neutron detection
has been 3He-based detectors, due to its high probability of neutron capture and insensitivity to
gamma radiation2. However, due to a global shortage of 3He, new neutron detection materials
need to be developed to reduce supply chain disruptions. When neutrons are present, there is
always a gamma-ray radiation background present. One of the most important parts of neutron
detection is the ability to distinguish true neutron detection events from a gamma-ray
interaction.
 Lithium-containing, scintillating particle composite materials can serve as an attractive,
cost-effective solution to this problem, offering fast decay times, high dynamic range and a
solid-state form for various field applications. Due to the fact the scintillating particle size can
be controlled, we are able to distinguish neutron and gamma events by differences in energy
deposition. Lithium-containing, scintillating particle composites take advantage of the
moderation process, that reduces the kinetic energy of energetic neutrons to thermal energy
ranges. Due to conservation of momentum, the best way to slow down a neutron, which
possesses a mass of 1 au, is through collision events with particles of the same mass, the most
common being the hydrogen atom. For this reason, the most common solid-based moderators
for neutrons are organic materials (e.g. such as polyethylene3 and acrylic). The neutron
detection process for lithium-containing, scintillating particle composites works as follows: A
neutron capture event, involving 6Li nuclei, induces the 6Li(n,α)3H reaction4, according to the
following equation:

Because current lithium-containing, scintillating particle manufacturing approaches are not
streamlined for new scintillating material evaluation, this paper will explore LiCAF doped with
divalent europium, and monovalent sodium, as a new candidate material for scintillating
particle composite manufacturing efforts.

2. LiCAF:Eu,Na Overview
Vital performance metrics for lithium-containing, scintillating particles are luminescence
properties. LiCAF does not produce scintillation light efficiently on its own. This is where our
dopants come in. Europium (Eu2+) takes the place of calcium (Ca2+) in the LiCAF crystal
structure, and sodium (Na+) takes the place of lithium (Li+) or can occupy an interstitial site.
While Eu and Na ions will luminesce with sharp emission lines, when doped into the LiCAF
structure, they are modified by perturbations from the crystal’s electric field7. Previous
literature5,6 has shown that europium is the fluorescing element inside of the crystal structure.
As a rare earth ion, this means that perturbations on europium from LiCAF’s crystal field are
small, creating a characteristically narrow emission spectrum7.
 By absorbing energy from the ionizing alpha and tritium particles, created by thermal
neutrons, europium and sodium are excited to higher level state, altering energy transfer
processes, before transiting back down to the ground state again via an optical transition,
releasing a photon. The resulting photons can travel out of the crystal and be detected by a
photo-sensitive device. (e.g. Photomultiplier Tube (PMT)) which can measure our scintillation
light output.
Previous studies on the effect of sodium co-doping into LiCAF:Eu2+ have produced conflicting
results. In Tanaka et al. (2011), measured light yield values for the LiCAF:Eu 2+ with 0, 0.01,
0.05 and 0.1 molar percent sodium were 21,800, 24,500, 21,000 and 19,800 photons/neutron,
respectively, with the 0.05 molar percent sample outputting the best light yield 5. In contrast to
Tanaka et al. (2011), Yanagida et al. (2010) explored LiCAF: Eu2+, with 0, 0.5, 1 and 2 molar
percent of sodium, which produced light yield values of 29,000, 32,000, 32,500 and 40,000
photons/neutron6, respectively. Based on the existing literature, the results suggest that co-
doping sodium into the LiCAF structure creates energy levels that can make europium

transitions more probable, as light output increased with sodium co-doping, but no shift in the
spectrum compared to LiCAF:Eu was observed. Notably, the europium doped samples, with
no co-dopant, from these studies varied by about 30%, making it necessary to complete our
own luminescence properties study on europium and sodium doped LiCAF. Ultimately, the
purpose of this study is to investigate the effect of sodium co-doping, through
photoluminescence (PL/PLE) techniques and quantum yield measurements, as a surrogate
indicator of luminescence performance.

3. Experimental
3.1 LiCAF Synthesis
LiCaAlF6 is prepared through a stoichiometric mix of LiF, CaF2, and AlF3 as seen in following
equation.
 𝐿𝑖𝐹 + 𝐶𝑎𝐹2 + 𝐴𝑙𝐹3 → 𝐿𝑖𝐶𝑎𝐴𝑙𝐹6

 The precursor material is added to a carbon crucible and heats at a rate of 5°C/min to a
temperature of 900°C for 12 hours, then cools at a rate of 0.5°C/min to room temperature. This
heating process creates unrefined LiCAF polycrystalline material. Now, using the unrefined
LiCAF material, europium is doped into the structure at 2 mol percent, replacing calcium. This
is done by adding EuF2 and LiCAF powder to a carbon crucible and heating at 5°C/min to
850°C for 2 hours before cooling at 5°C/min to room temperature. Next, sodium is doped into
the material, using NaF, in two separate synthesis steps at 1 and 2 molar percent. The sodium
doping follows the same procedure, but the mix stays at 850°C for 6 hours.
3.2 Photoluminescence
To understand the effect, if any, of co-doping sodium into the LiCAF: Eu2+ on its fluorescence
characteristics, PL and PLE measurements of the synthesized samples were taken. The
excitation spectrum tells us which wavelengths are the strongest drivers of optical transitions.
The emission spectrum tells us specifically about what optical transitions are occurring as
electrons fall from excited levels to the ground state. Previous literature has determined the
excitation and emission spectrum of europium-doped LiCAF. Aghalte, Omanwar and Moharil
reported an emission spectrum at room temperature with a single peak at ≈370 nm and a
broader excitation spectrum with peaks at ≈300, 310 and 340 nm8. Kirm et al measured both
the emission and excitation spectrums at 10 K. A sharp emission peak was seen at 370 nm
caused by the Eu 5d-4f transitions. The excitation spectrum varied between emission
wavelengths9. Excitation and emission spectra have been measured for small concentrations of
Na co-doping in Tanaka et al. (2011) Emission was measured with a peak at 370 nm with no
shift in peak position between different concentrations of Na-doping. Excitation exhibited a
peak at 340 nm, with spectral shifts between Na concentrations around 350 nm.
 In our photoluminescence measurements, we used a Hamamatsu Quantaurus-QY
spectrometer system. Notably, our samples possessed a higher sodium co-dopant amount,
compared to Tanaka et al. (2011). Powdered LiCAF samples with sodium molar percentages
of 0, 1 and 2 were placed in a high-purity quartz sample holder and inserted into the Quantaurus
integrating sphere for measurements. For excitation, the Quantaurus system excited the powder
samples at 250-410 nm with a 1 nm step size. An integrating sphere, equipped on the unit,
ensured that the excitation and emission photons were efficiently captured. Using an integrating
sphere, emission light can combine and subsequently be measured with a CCD array.
3.3 Quantum Yield Measurement
Quantum yield (QY) is governed by the probability that a photon absorbed by the sample
creates an excitation that releases another photon. 100% quantum efficiency means the same

amount of light is emitted from a sample as was absorbed. QY is a good surrogate indicator of
scintillation light output and is therefore a good first step in determining the scintillation light
output of our samples. All samples contained 2 mol% doped Eu, while Na co-doping was
varied at 0, 1 and 2 mol%. Measurements were performed using a Hamamatsu Quantaurus-QY
spectrometer over a spectrum of 250-350nm. QY is measured by illuminating the sample with
monochromatic light at a wavelength within its excitation range and detecting the resulting light
intensity inside an integrating sphere. Due to energy conservation, photoluminescence occurs
at a lower energy (longer wavelength) than the absorbed excitation light. The Quantaurus
system uses a CCD array detector to spectrally separate and measure both the excitation light
and photoluminescence.
 To determine the number of photons absorbed by the sample, the difference in excitation
intensity is taken between the sample and a reference. This decrease in excitation intensity
corresponds to the absorbed photons, while the emitted photoluminescence provides the

4. Results and Discussion
4.1 Photoluminescence
Figure 1 displays the photoluminescence spectra, together with the photo-excitation spectra of
all the LiCAF:Eu,Na samples explored in this study. LiCAF is excited into optical transitions
by a range of wavelengths from 270 to 370 nm, with maximum excitation at 300nm, and a
secondary peak at 330 nm for all the samples. Emission occurs at 370 nm, corresponding to the
Eu2+ 5d-4f transitions. This excitation and emission spectrum agrees with previous work from
Aghalte et al. (2007) for LiCAF:Eu, however, it contradicts another PL study on LiCAF:Eu,Na
by Tanaka et al. where excitation peaks occur at 340 and 350nm. Emission results are the same
as LiCAF:Eu, showing that Europium is the only fluorescing dopant.

 Figure 1. Plot of excitation and emission for all samples of LiCAF from 250 to 410 nm. Excitation peak occurs at
 300nm, emission peak occurs at 370nm.

4.2 Quantum Yield
The same samples used in the PL/PLE measurements were used for quantum yield
measurements. Figure 2 summarizes the results of the quantum yield measurements.

 Here, we can see that co-doping LiCAF:Eu2+ with sodium leads to an increase in QY
of the samples. At 300 nm, Na 1% and Na 2% concentrations have peak quantum yields of
0.635, and 0.580 compared to 0.578, in for the non-co-doped sample. This tells us that co-
doping LiCAF:Eu with sodium has the effect of increasing the QY of the scintillating material
and therefore supports the idea that co-doping sodium would also increase the light yield of a
scintillation event. The improvement of Na 1% doping is especially significant, performing 9%
better than the non-co-doped sample, suggesting that co-doping LiCAF:Eu2+ with sodium at 1
molar percent will provide the best light yield for a scintillation event.

5 Conclusion
In conclusion, we have shown that LiCAF:Eu,Na supports fluorescence, are strongly excited in
a range of 270 to 370nm, and emit light centered around 370 nm. We have experimentally
demonstrated that sodium co-doping at 1 percent can directly affect the quantum yield.
Additionally, with sodium co-doping at 1 molar percent, quantum yield as high as 63.5 percent
is achieved, performing 9% better than LiCAF:Eu. This is a positive sign that the light output
from a scintillating event will be high. This also supports the hypothesis from previous work
that sodium does not fluoresce in the LiCAF crystal but rather creates energy levels that make
the 5d-4f Eu2+ transition more probable. Future work will focus on proving that scintillation
events in LiCAF:Eu,Na create a high light yield through radioluminescence experiments.
