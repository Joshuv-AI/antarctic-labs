Clinicians’ and patients’ views and experiences on virtual hospital care: a systematic review of qualitative evidence
Authors: Riyaas A Mohamed, Márcia Rodrigues Franco, Stephanie Mathieson, Marie March, Alessandra Marcelo, Gustavo Machado, Manuela Ferreira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo appraise and synthesise the literature on patient and clinician experiences with hospital-based virtual care services.MethodsWe searched Medline, Embase, CINAHL and PsycINFO (via Ovid) in October 2023. An updated search was performed in January 2025, with alerts maintained until May 2025. We included qualitative or mixed-methods studies evaluating patient or clinician experiences of hospital-based virtual care.ResultsOut of 15 434 identified articles, 19 studies involving 514 clinicians and 215 patients were included. The majority of the studies were from high-income countries. Our analysis identified five themes influencing virtual hospital care experiences: perceived value, confidence and readiness, digital and health literacy, therapeutic relationships, and infrastructure and system support.DiscussionThe potential of virtual care services is mediated by a complex relationship between personal, interpersonal and systemic factors. The acceptability of virtual hospital care is highly dependent on tailoring services to meet the individual needs of both patients and clinicians. The findings of this review demonstrate the need to improve clinician capacity and confidence through validated virtual tools and ongoing training.ConclusionTo ensure virtual hospital care is successful and equitable, the virtual health model must be co-designed with its end-users. In addition to addressing systemic barriers, emphasis must be placed on refining care delivery to support the needs of diverse patients and professions. Further research, particularly in low- and middle-income countries and through longitudinal studies, is critical for informing future health policy.

Introduction

Healthcare systems are constantly strained with the growing prevalence of non-communicable chronic diseases accounting for at least 80% of the utilisation of healthcare resources worldwide.1 As the gap between the increasing demand for more equitable healthcare and the decline in the global healthcare workforce widens,2 virtual hospital services provide a potential solution to address some of the challenges. While virtual care is not a new concept, its integration into hospital systems had been limited until the COVID-19 pandemic. In the context of this review, virtual hospital services refer specifically to care provided remotely by hospital-based practitioners to patients who would otherwise require an in-person outpatient visit if virtual options were unavailable. Such services are often delivered through various technologies, including calls, video conferencing, text messaging and data monitoring devices.3 4

Early studies evaluating user experiences of virtual care revealed fragmented perceptions, often focused on: a single stakeholder (patients or clinicians), a specific context (eg, oncology or palliative care) or a single modality (video or telephone consultations), which limits the generalisability of the findings.5–8 As the WHO’s strategy on digital health emphasises, tailoring services to the specific needs of its users is critical for equitable implementation.9 However, there is a lack of comprehensive understanding of the shared and unique experiences of both patients and clinicians across diverse hospital services.

Therefore, this systematic review aims to synthesise qualitative evidence to understand the facilitators and barriers that patients and clinicians experience with virtual hospital services. This will highlight key implications for clinical practice and policy to guide the development of more effective and equitable virtual care.

Methods

This review was prospectively registered on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (CRD42023469643). Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) 2020 statement guidelines were used to guide the reporting of this systematic review10 (online supplemental appendix 1).

Search strategy

The search strategy was developed in consultation with the Faculty of Medicine and Health librarian (The University of Sydney). It consisted of three concepts: virtual health services, hospital settings and study designs. An initial search was conducted on 4 October 2023 in Medline, Embase, CINAHL and PsycINFO (via Ovid). An update was conducted on 22 January 2025 with alerts maintained until May 2025. There were no restrictions on language or search dates (ie, from the inception of the respective databases). Full search strategies are listed in online supplemental appendix 2.

Inclusion criteria

Studies evaluating clinicians’ and patients’ perspectives were included in this review. This included qualitative and mixed-methods studies that reported qualitative data separately. For this review, virtual services that involved less than 50% in-person visits, two-way communication between patients and clinicians, and delivered through personal devices (eg, computer, mobile phone) were included. Studies including patients less than 18 years of age, caregivers or family members, non-patient-facing staff, acute care settings (eg, primary care, urgent care programmes or emergency departments), Artificial intelligence-based conversational agents and one-way communication platforms were excluded.

Screening and data extraction

Two reviewers independently screened the titles, abstracts and full-text articles. To ensure consistency, 50 articles were randomly screened before screening to formulate a screening guide. Disagreements were resolved through discussions and when needed, arbitrated by a third reviewer. Data were extracted independently by two reviewers, using a predefined template that captured author(s), year of publication, country, study design, health condition(s), participants and key findings.

Assessing the quality and confidence of included studies

Methodological quality was assessed by two independent reviewers using the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) checklist11 to evaluate rigour and contribution to the analysis. Reviewers resolved any discrepancies through discussion. The level of confidence in the main findings was assessed using the Confidence in the Evidence from Reviews of Qualitative Research (GRADE-CERQual) approach.12

Data synthesis

A thematic synthesis approach was adopted to analyse the data inductively in three phases as outlined by Thomas and Harden.13 The results, discussion and supplemental material (if applicable) were coded line-by-line to generate initial codes. Clinicians’ and patients’ views were coded separately to preserve the nuances in their respective views. In the second phase, codes were grouped to create descriptive themes. In the final phase, the descriptive themes were compared with generate higher-order analytic themes. This process was iterative, involving multiple reviewers to refine the analysis and reach consensus. During this synthesis, the implementation timeframe (pre-pandemic vs pandemic-driven) was considered. However, as the generated themes were found to be consistent across all levels of the socioecological framework, the results are presented as a unified synthesis. The socioecological model was used as a guide to inform but did not dictate the analysis. This framework is useful for understanding how individual, interpersonal, organisational and societal factors interact to influence health experiences and behaviour.14

Results

Characteristics of included articles

This review included 18 qualitative studies and 1 mixed-method study (table 1 and figure 1), including a total of 514 clinicians and 215 patients. Studies were mainly excluded due to interventions, populations or study designs outside the scope of this review. Seven studies evaluated patients’ views, ten studies examined clinicians’ views and two studies assessed both. Included studies were published between 2017 and 2024 and conducted across eight countries: Canada (n=5), Australia (n=5), the UK (n=2), the USA (n=3), Pakistan (n=2), China (n=1) and Norway (n=1). Five studies evaluated virtual care in rural or remote areas in Pakistan (n=2), Australia (n=1) and the USA (n=2). Eleven articles focused on care delivery during COVID-19, while eight studies examined ongoing implementation of virtual hospital care models. Multiple clinical areas were represented such as surgery, oncology, mental health, physiotherapy and other rehabilitation services (table 1).

Quality and confidence

The overall methodological quality of the included studies was rated as good quality using the CASP checklist (online supplemental appendix 3). While most studies demonstrated clear aims and rigorous data analysis. A consistent limitation was the lack of documentation on the researcher-participant relationship. Other methodological issues included the use of convenience sampling and small sample sizes.15 16 No studies were excluded based on the results. Using the GRADE-CERQual approach, the overall confidence in all five themes was determined to be low (online supplemental appendix 4 and table 2). This was primarily driven by moderate concerns about methodological limitations and minor concerns relating to the generalisability to low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).

Themes

Five overarching themes were identified in patients’ and clinicians’ experiences with virtual hospital services including perceived value, confidence and readiness, health and digital literacy, therapeutic relationships, as well as infrastructure and system support (figure 2). The findings with representative quotes can be found in online supplemental appendix 5.

Perceived value

For patients, the value of virtual care was strongly correlated with convenience, including reduced travel time and costs16–25 and lower levels of anxiety associated with hospital visits.20 22 23 26 Services meeting specific personal needs, such as enabling women to seek same-sex clinicians23 or providing a discreet avenue to seek mental care services without social stigmatisation,27 were perceived as high value. However, the value proposition was lower for those who perceived virtual examinations to be inferior to in-person examinations, and patients living in rural or remote areas with complex care who perceived virtual care as less convenient compared with a single day, multi-specialist in-person visit.15 25

For clinicians, the value of care was associated with clinical appropriateness and efficiency. Virtual care was considered to be useful for gathering information about the patients and their living conditions,17 routine monitoring, and for managing single chronic conditions.21 23 24 28 29 Furthermore, clinicians appreciated the gains in work efficiency due to reduced wait times and better time management.15 17 20 30 However, acceptability was discipline specific. Professions such as speech therapy perceived virtual care to be useful as they were able to adapt to the virtual modality better.26 Professions needing hands-on assessment (eg, physiotherapists) highlighted difficulties in conducting virtual examinations, affecting their clinical judgements and management plans,17 26 28 29 31 particularly when managing patients with deteriorating conditions or those who require closer monitoring.24 31

Confidence and readiness

Experiences with virtual hospital services were shaped by users’ confidence and readiness. Prior experience using virtual platforms was associated with increased readiness to engage such services.16 23 Higher levels of readiness were observed among patients with family members providing technological assistance,20 31 and with clinicians who received formal training, mentorship and collaborative learning opportunities in providing virtual care.17 23 26 28 32 However, clinicians with a long-standing history of delivering traditional care stated they were less ready to change their established practice.31

Clinicians’ confidence was also dependent on context, lowering when managing patients with acute or complex conditions virtually due to concerns around unclear patient-reported symptoms with few clinician-led assessments.20 24 28 29 31 Clinicians also described challenges of interpreting subtle clinical signs remotely, creating uncertainties in their clinical judgements.31 This diagnostic concern led to some fearing potential medico-legal repercussions, lower patient satisfaction and reduced professional reputation.29 Similar apprehension towards diagnostic inaccuracies was echoed by patients who stated that the lack of in-person physical assessments reduced their assurance in the virtual diagnoses, thus lowering their acceptance of virtual services.19 21 24 To overcome diagnostic uncertainties, a frequently proposed strategy was a hybrid care model, where a comprehensive physical assessment can be carried out during an initial in-person appointment before transitioning to virtual follow-up.21 24 26

Health and digital literacy

Patients’ and clinicians’ experiences with virtual care were linked with users’ health and digital literacy levels. Reduced patient health literacy emerged as a barrier to communication, as highlighted in 14 studies. For patients, this meant not reporting symptoms adequately, leading to frustration and feelings of neglect.15 19 Clinicians shared this concern, stating that unclear patient-reported information reduces diagnostic accuracy and increases diagnostic concerns.16 23 24 28 29 While higher health literacy was linked to greater patient involvement in their care,20 24 28 it also led some patients to deliberately under-report their symptoms to avoid hospitalisation.25

In parallel, patients’ acceptance of virtual care was highly dependent on their digital literacy and support systems. Prior technology experience31 and strong family support20 31 acted as facilitators to virtual care use. In contrast, a lack of these enablers created significant access barriers especially for older,18 29 32 inexperienced,17 24 socioeconomically disadvantaged16 individuals and those living alone.20 These challenges resulted in increased clinician workload for patients who required increased technical support.18 32 Furthermore, when clinicians themselves lacked proficiency in virtual care, it resulted in patient frustration and dissatisfaction with the virtual services.27

Therapeutic relationships

The impact of virtual care on therapeutic relationships was reported in six studies. Better therapeutic alliance was stated by clinicians who delivered virtual healthcare as part of a hybrid health model.21 24 26 Many acknowledged its perceived ability to strengthen a wider network of relationships such as improving engagement with family support systems.19 21 It also fostered better interprofessional collaboration between hospital staff and local primary care providers, contributing to the continuity of care during the COVID-19 pandemic.15 17 20 23 33

Despite these benefits, patients consistently viewed face-to-face interactions as irreplaceable for deeper personal connections and receiving emotional support,15 16 19–21 especially when receiving distressing information (eg, cancer diagnosis).24 Privacy concerns were expressed by both groups, affecting the development of therapeutic alliance and trust.18 22 26 27 For patients, the lack of a secure physical environment led to fears of being overheard by family or clinicians viewing their home space.19 20 22 24 27 Data security concerns were also raised for sharing confidential and sensitive information over the internet.18 For clinicians, some were concerned about patient privacy when providing care in an open-plan hospital setting.26 28 31

The specific technology used was also a critical factor. Both groups acknowledged that video-based virtual care was superior for facilitating engagement.15 16 24 Telephone consultations were widely regarded as a barrier to building rapport as non-verbal communication was eliminated.19 21 Within group video calls, while some patients appreciated its ability to connect with others through shared experiences, some found the communication lacked natural flow and spontaneity.22

Infrastructure and system support

Organisational factors were critical to the success of virtual hospital care. Positive experiences were facilitated by timely technology16 and administrative support.28 Strong interprofessional collaboration and peer mentorship acted as enablers.28 However, these enablers were often undermined by deficits in clinical governance. Clinicians cited barriers such as a lack of formal training,23 28 30–32 unclear referral pathways27 31 and poor communication between virtual and in-person teams.30 31

Widespread infrastructure and equity barriers also posed challenges. Inconsistent internet connectivity16–18 20 24 26 28 and platform-related issues16 28 29 affected communication and care delivery, particularly in rural settings.27 This contributed to digital divide among patients with limited access to suitable or affordable devices.16 18 For clinicians, these issues were compounded by a lack of dedicated space and equipment,16 17 23 28 31 insufficient financial incentives17 29 or lack of governmental support.28

Discussion

This qualitative systematic review appraised and synthesised the literature on patient and clinician experiences with virtual care in hospital-based settings. Our findings demonstrate that virtual care is perceived as being highly efficient for specific cohorts. For many patients, the efficiency of virtual care was a key driver of acceptance, specifically through reduced travel time, lower associated costs and decreased anxiety associated with physical hospital environments, especially during the pandemic. Furthermore, virtual care models were effective when they met specific individual needs. This review also presents with some challenges related to satisfaction with care, therapeutic relationships and equitable access, logistical constraints and lower health and digital literacy.

While virtual care is aimed to improve access, some groups face challenges due to inequity in healthcare access due to the social determinants of health. For older adults, this often takes the form of social isolation, or a lack of resources or expertise to use digital tools. For others, this inequity was related to income, costs or low literacy. This finding is supported by the broader literature on digital divide and its impact on certain populations.34–36 While the potential for successful implementation is higher among younger, digitally literate patients, it remains significantly lower for older individuals living alone, a demographic where high rates of digital exclusion occur.34 Especially in underserved areas, more is needed to address the underlying social determinants of health to promote the use of more efficient modalities. For instance, despite the highlighted challenges associated with telephone consultations in this review, higher use of this modality was found to be associated with race, lower education and income, even in well-established health systems, such as the USA and Australia.35 36 To mitigate the risk of inadvertently marginalising vulnerable populations, targeted digital inclusion strategies and careful considerations for those at the highest risk of exclusion are required when designing and delivering virtual hospital services.

Clinician-related and organisational factors were also critical. The suitability and perceived value of virtual care varied across professions. The potential for successful implementation is higher for ‘verbally driven’ specialities, such as speech therapy or psychiatry. In contrast, the lower confidence and value proposition of virtual care were reported by other disciplines, such as physiotherapy, due to increased diagnostic concerns and difficulties adapting practice to virtual modality.17 26 28 These barriers may result from a scarcity of validated virtual assessment tools and lack of targeted training or mentorship to build confidence in new workflows. In a large Australian study, involving more than 800 allied health practitioners, more than half the respondents reported telehealth as ineffective due to the lack of physical assessments and formal training.37 By supporting clinicians with more evidence-based virtual tools and formalised training, this could reduce diagnostic concerns, improve acceptance of virtual care and facilitate skills adaptation.

Privacy was a multifaceted concern for both groups who struggled to secure a private space for virtual consultations. This review identified a tension between the clinical utility of observing patients’ home environments and patients’ concerns about privacy invasions (particularly with unfamiliar clinicians), data security breaches and confidentiality issues.18 20 22 26 27 29 These concerns may stem from underlying fears around emerging digital risks, such as scams and data misuse,38 which particularly increased during the pandemic.39 Given that virtual health models were rapidly implemented within hospitals worldwide during the pandemic, most institutions adopted commercial communication platforms. Our review suggests that to improve trust and address privacy concerns associated with the use of common commercial platforms, platforms designed specifically for healthcare use can improve acceptance by patients and clinicians.

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive review to synthesise the dual perspectives of patients and clinicians across various hospital specialties. However, several limitations should be considered. The evidence is drawn primarily from high-income countries, which limits the generalisability of findings to LMICs. Additionally, as most of the studies were conducted during or immediately after the COVID-19 pandemic, some findings might reflect the challenges of rapid, reactive adoption of virtual care rather than planned and well-supported implementation. Nonetheless, our synthesis highlights that several facilitators (eg, structural support and peer mentorship) and challenges (eg, diagnostic uncertainty and the digital divide) appeared universal across both pandemic and pre-pandemic contexts.

Implications and future research

The findings of this review emphasise the need for a shared decision-making approach, where mode of care delivery is tailored to both clinician and patient needs, considering individual and service factors. For health services, there is an urgent need for investment in validated virtual care-appropriate tools and ongoing, specialised training to support clinicians. For policymakers, the priority must address systemic barriers, including infrastructure gaps while developing clear clinical governance to guide clinicians to ensure privacy, safety and quality of care.

To move beyond a binary understanding of barriers and facilitators, future research should adopt a realist evaluation framework40 to understand which specific virtual care models work, for which patient and clinician groups, in what context and why. Understanding these underlying mechanisms is essential to support the development of sustainable, evidence-based health policies. Further research is needed in diverse settings, including LMICs, to understand context-specific factors for co-designing culturally appropriate virtual care models.

Conclusion

The findings of this review highlight several positive experiences with virtual hospital services supporting its continued use in delivering quality care to patients. However, to ensure the care is more equitable, further strategies are needed to support both patients and clinicians including improving health and digital literacy. It is also essential to improve clinicians’ competencies in using virtual care models while improving technological infrastructures. Finally, by involving clinicians and patients in the co-development and refinement of frameworks, virtual care services would be able to provide high-quality and more context-specific care.
