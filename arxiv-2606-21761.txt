Abstract
. We develop a general framework for understanding and computing both the groupoid homology of an ample groupoid and the topological K-theory of its reduced C ∗ \mathrm{C}^{\ast} -algebra, based on two main ideas: discretisation and independent resolutions. Discretisation shows that a special class of ample groupoids we term independent groupoids are homologically and K-theoretically equivalent to discrete groupoids. We introduce the notion of a resolution by independent groupoids and provide a recipe for building a controlled independent resolution of a given ample groupoid of interest, leading to a systematic way of studying its homology and K-theory. In order to illustrate our general ideas and methods, we work out several concrete examples and applications. Garside categories provide a wide range of examples, including higher rank graphs, self-similar groups and spherical Artin–Tits groups. We also present an application to the homology of Stein’s groups.

Theorem A

(Discretisation in K-theory, see Theorem
4.12
)
.

Let
G
be the universal groupoid of a countable inverse semigroup
S
with idempotent semilattice
E
such that
G
is Hausdorff and satisfies the Baum–Connes conjecture. Consider the action of
S
on

satisfies the Baum–Connes conjecture. Then

\K_{*}(\csr(G))\cong\K_{*}(\csr(S\ltimes E^{\times})).

This reduces the K-theory computation for

to that of the discrete groupoid

. The computation breaks down further by Morita equivalence invariance to a K-theory computation for the isotropy groups of the discrete groupoid. Explicitly, in this case we obtain

\K_{*}(\csr(G))\cong\bigoplus_{[e]\in S\backslash E^{\times}}\K_{*}(\csr(S_{e})),

where
S_{e}=\{s\in S\mid s^{*}s=ss^{*}=e\}
is the maximal subgroup at a non-zero idempotent

. Note that

satisfies the Baum–Connes conjecture if and only if

satisfies the Baum–Connes conjecture for each

Theorem A
′

(Discretisation in K-theory, a dynamical perspective)

Suppose

\cdots\to G_{k}\to\cdots\to G_{0}\to G\to\emptyset,

i.e. we have closed invariant subsets

together with isomorphisms

\cs

Dynamical systems

Let us turn to discretisation in groupoid homology. In this setting, unlike for K-theory, no extra hypotheses (such as satisfaction of the Baum–Connes conjecture, Hausdorffness, or finiteness requirements) are needed, and no exactness is required in applications of independent resolutions. Let us now present the main ideas behind the proofs of discretisation and independent resolutions.

The proof strategy for Theorem
A
is similar to the one developed in
[
50
]
, which is based on the ABC spectral sequence and its functoriality with respect to groupoid correspondences. In
[
50
]
, a spectral sequence argument then reduces Theorem
A
to discretisation in groupoid homology (this reduction uses the torsion-free isotropy assumption), which is then established by analysing chain complexes. In general, i.e., without the torsion-free isotropy assumption, we have to work with spectral sequences involving more complicated invariants, and the challenge we overcome in this paper is to establish the desired isomorphisms at the level of these more sophisticated analogues of groupoid homology.

In the context of Theorem
B
, the main novelty of our paper is the development of a systematic way of producing systems of finite covers which lead to independent resolutions recursively following a concrete algorithm. At the technical level, the core exactness result is Theorem
3.13
, which makes sure that our algorithm does indeed produce an independent resolution, and which is the key improvement compared to previous results in
[
38
,
39
]
, where such an exactness result was only established under strong assumptions. We have also developed a systematic approach to finding the data needed to construct an independent resolution, in the language of equivariant representations of semilattices and more generally locally finite weak semilattices. In particular, the notion of a thorough system of finite covers (Definition
3.7
) provides a practical way to check that a system of finite covers produces a resolution of the desired groupoid (see Lemma
3.9
).

The present paper is structured as follows: In Section
2
, we recall the notion of locally finite weak semilattices from
[
47
, § 8]
, study an equivariant version and develop a new, alternative perspective in terms of topological dynamical systems. Independent resolutions are discussed in Section
3
, where we also develop our general construction based on systems of finite covers. Again, we work out an interpretation from the point of view of topological dynamics. Throughout Sections
2
and
3
, we use the concrete example of higher rank graphs and their groupoids to illustrate our ideas. Section
4
treats the crucial idea of discretisation. First, we establish discretisation in groupoid homology in Section
4.1
. Secondly, we formulate discretisation in K-theory in Section
4.2
. Since the proof of K-theoretic discretisation is long and technical, it occupies a separate section (Section
5
). Finally, applications and examples are presented in Section
6
. In Appendix

, we explain how groupoid correspondences lead to
\cs
-algebraic correspondences in an equivariant setting. This is needed in Section

.

2.
Inverse semigroup actions on locally finite weak semilattices

In this section, we recall the notion of locally finite weak semilattices (following
[
47
, § 8]
) and explain how to construct
\cs
-algebras from these structures. We then develop an alternative, dual picture, which emphasizes the topological perspective. Moreover, we consider the equivariant setting by adding an inverse semigroup action.

First we fix notation and conventions for inverse semigroups and étale groupoids. An étale groupoid
G
is a topological groupoid whose range and source maps are local homeomorphisms; in this article the unit space
G^{0}
is assumed to be locally compact and Hausdorff, but
G
may be non-Hausdorff. Inverse semigroups and semilattices are assumed to have a
0
element, and we denote the semilattice of idempotents in an inverse semigroup
S
by
E(S)
. There is a natural partial order
\leq
on
S
given by
s\leq t
if
s\in tE(S)
, or equivalently
s\in E(S)t
. Inverse semigroups
S
act on sets
X
by partial bijections; each
s\in S
acts as a bijection written
x\mapsto s.x
from its domain
\dom_{X}(s)\subseteq X
to its range
\ran_{x}(s)\subseteq X
, and
\dom_{X}(0)=\ran_{X}(0)=\emptyset
. An action of
S
on a locally compact Hausdorff space
X
is by partial homeomorphisms; in this case
\dom_{X}(s)
and
\ran_{X}(s)
are assumed open and
s\colon\dom_{X}(s)\to\ran_{X}(s)
a homeomorphism. The transformation groupoid
S\ltimes X
is an étale groupoid with unit space
X
given by

S\ltimes X\coloneq\left\{(s,x)\in S\times X\;|\;x\in\dom_{X}(s)\right\}/{\sim},

where
(s_{1},x_{1})\sim(s_{2},x_{2})
if and only if
x_{1}=x_{2}
and there exists
s\leq s_{1},s_{2}
with
x_{1}=x_{2}\in\dom_{X}(s)
. We denote the class of
(s,x)
by
[s,x]
, which has range
s.x
and source
x
. Multiplication in
S\ltimes X
, if defined, is given by
[t,s.x][s,x]=[ts,x]
.

2.1.
Locally finite weak semilattices

Definition 2.1
.

A
weak semilattice

L
is a poset with a zero element
0
(a necessarily unique element with the property that
0\leq l
for all
l\in L
) such that for all
l_{1},l_{2}\in L
there exists a finite subset
F\subseteq\left\{l\in L\;|\;l\leq l_{1},\,l\leq l_{2}\right\}
such that for all
l\in L
with
l\leq l_{1}
,
l\leq l_{2}
, there exists
f\in F
with
l\leq f
. Define
l_{1}\mathop{\downarrow}l_{2}
as the set of maximal elements in
\left\{l\in L\;|\;l\leq l_{1},\,l\leq l_{2}\right\}
.

A subset
\bar{F}
of a weak semilattice is called
\mathop{\downarrow}
-closed if for all
x,y\in\bar{F}
,
x\mathop{\downarrow}y\subseteq\bar{F}
. We call a weak semilattice
L

locally finite
if for any finite subset
F\subseteq L
there exists a finite subset
\bar{F}\subseteq L
which is
\mathop{\downarrow}
-closed such that

Remark 2.18

The most canonical action of an inverse semigroup
S
on a locally finite weak semilattice is the action
S\mathbin{\curvearrowright}E
on the semilattice
E
of idempotents in
S
. The action is given at
s\in S
by
\dom_{E}(s)=\left\{e\in E\;|\;e\leq s^{-1}s\right\}
and
s.e\coloneq ses^{-1}
. In this case,
S\ltimes\widehat{E}
is Paterson’s universal groupoid of
S
(see
[
56
, § 4.3]
, but with a small difference arising from the treatment of
0\in S
as pointed out in
[
16
, § 5.5.4]
).

Now let us develop a topological perspective in the equivariant setting, following
[
34
, Definition 2.12]
.

Definition 2.19
.

Let
S
be an inverse semigroup, and let
S
act on a totally disconnected, locally compact Hausdorff space
X
and a locally finite weak semilattice
L
. A representation
U\colon L\to\mathcal{O}_{c}(X)
is
S
-equivariant
if

•

for each
s\in S
we have
\dom_{X}(s)=\bigcup_{l\in\dom_{L}(s)}U(l)
, and

•

for each
s\in S
and
l\in\dom_{L}(s)
, we have
s.(U(l))=U(s.l)
.

Note that, if
U
is
S
-equivariant, then the following are automatically satisfied:

•

\ran_{X}(s)=\bigcup_{l\in\ran_{L}(s)}U(l)
for every
s\in S
,

•

U|_{\dom_{L}(s)}\colon\dom_{L}(s)\to\mathcal{O}_{c}(\dom_{X}(s))
is a representation of the locally finite weak semilattice
\dom_{L}(s)
on
\dom_{X}(s)
, and similarly for
U|_{\ran_{L}(s)}
.

The following is now a straightforward consequence.

Lemma 2.20
.

Let
S
be an inverse semigroup, let
S
act on a totally disconnected, locally compact Hausdorff space
X
and a locally finite weak semilattice
L
, and suppose that
U
is an
S
-equivariant generating representation of
L
on
X
. Then we have an isomorphism of
S
-dynamical systems
(S\mathbin{\curvearrowright}X)\cong(S\mathbin{\curvearrowright}\widehat{L}_{U})
. More precisely, the homeomorphism

from Lemma
2.11
is
S
-equivariant. If

is moreover independent, this yields an isomorphism
(S\mathbin{\curvearrowright}X)\cong(S\mathbin{\curvearrowright}\widehat{L})
.

Definition 2.21
.

Let
S\mathbin{\curvearrowright}X
be an action of an inverse semigroup
S
on a totally disconnected locally compact Hausdorff space
X
. We say
S\mathbin{\curvearrowright}X
is
independent
if there is an
S
-equivariant independent generating representation of a locally finite weak semilattice
L
on
X
. An ample groupoid

is called

if there is an independent action

with

.

3.
Independent resolutions

3.1.
Definitions and general observations

The idea of independent resolutions is to find “resolutions” of ample groupoids by independent groupoids, i.e. those of the form
S\ltimes\widehat{L}
as in §
2.2
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
G
be an ample groupoid. An
independent resolution
of
G
is given by a sequence of ample groupoids
G_{i}
,
i=-1,0,1,2,\dotsc
, with the following extra data and subject to the following conditions:

•

G_{-1}=G
,

•

For every
i
, a decomposition
G_{i}^{0}=C_{i}\amalg O_{i}
, where
C_{i}
is a closed
G_{i}
-invariant subspace (and hence
O_{i}
is open and invariant), and
C_{-1}=G^{0}
,

•

For every
i\geq-1
, a groupoid isomorphism
\psi_{i}\colon G_{i}|_{O_{i}}\xrightarrow{\raisebox{-3.01389pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\sim$}}G_{i+1}|_{C_{i+1}}
, where
G_{i}|_{O_{i}}\coloneq\left\{g\in G_{i}\;|\;r(g),s(g)\in O_{i}\right\}
is the restriction of
G_{i}
to
O_{i}
, and similarly for
G_{i+1}|_{C_{i+1}}
,

•

For each
i\geq 0
, an action
S_{i}\mathbin{\curvearrowright}L_{i}
of an inverse semigroup
S_{i}
on a locally finite weak semilattice
L_{i}
with an isomorphism
G_{i}\cong S_{i}\ltimes\widehat{L_{i}}
.

Note that
\psi_{i}
restricts to a homeomorphism
O_{i}\cong C_{i+1}
.

We call
(G_{i})_{i}
an independent resolution in the strong sense if for each
i
we have
L_{i}=E(S_{i})
(the semilattice of idempotents in
S_{i}
) with the canonical action
S_{i}\mathbin{\curvearrowright}E(S_{i})
.

Let us now explain why independent resolutions always exist (even in the strong sense). First of all, given an ample groupoid
G
, we can always find an inverse semigroup
S
of compact open bisections in
G
such that
G\cong S\ltimes X
(see
[
32
, Proposition 2.2]
). Furthermore, we can choose a family
\mathcal{U}
of compact open subsets of
X
such that
\mathcal{U}
generates the topology of
X
in the sense of
[
15
, § 2]
(see also
[
14
]
), i.e.
\mathcal{U}
generates the entire family of compact open subsets through finite unions, finite intersections and relative complements. Let
E
be the smallest semilattice of compact open subsets of
X
containing
\mathcal{U}
which is
S
-invariant. By construction,
E
is an
S
-invariant generating semilattice in
X
. Thus by Lemma
2.20
we have the following homeomorphism, identifying
X
with a closed
S
-invariant subspace of
\widehat{E}
:

\displaystyle X\to

\displaystyle\left\{\chi\in\widehat{E}\;|\;\begin{subarray}{c}\text{for all }f,e_{1},\dotsc,e_{n}\in E\text{ with }f=\bigcup_{i}e_{i}\text{ in }X,\\
\chi(f)=1\text{ implies }\chi(e_{i})=1\text{ for some }i\end{subarray}\right\},

\displaystyle x\mapsto

\displaystyle\chi_{x},

where
\chi_{x}(e)=1
if and only if
x\in e
. Hence, we can set
G_{0}\coloneq S\ltimes\widehat{E}
, define
C_{0}
as the subspace of
\widehat{E}
homeomorphic to
X
under the map above, and
O_{0}\coloneq\widehat{E}\setminus C_{0}
. Now proceed inductively, with
G_{0}|_{O_{0}}
in place of
G
. This produces an independent resolution (even in the strong sense).

3.2.
Systems of finite covers

The above-mentioned existence statement is quite abstract. Let us now present a more systematic way of producing independent resolutions. Suppose
G\cong S\ltimes X
as before and suppose
U
is an
S
-equivariant, generating representation of a locally finite weak semilattice
L
in
X
. As before, we may by Lemma
2.20
identify
X
with the closed
S
-invariant subspace

C_{-1}=\left\{\chi\in\widehat{L}\;|\;\begin{subarray}{c}\text{for all }l,f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\in L\text{ with }U(l)=\bigcup_{i}U(f_{i}),\\
\chi(l)=1\text{ implies }\exists i\text{ with }\chi(f_{i})=1\end{subarray}\right\}

of
\widehat{L}
. We could as before have arranged
L
to be a semilattice, but the extra flexibility allows us to more easily handle situations naturally endowed with a locally finite weak semilattice.

The inclusion
X\subseteq\widehat{L}
induces a map
C_{c}(\widehat{L},\mathbb{Z})\twoheadrightarrow C_{c}(X,\mathbb{Z})
. The kernel
I
of this map is generated by the indicator functions on relative complements of the form

(3.1)

\widehat{L}(l)\setminus\bigcup_{f\in F}\widehat{L}(f),

where
l\in L
and
F\subseteq L
is a finite set with
l=\bigcup_{f\in F}f
in
X
. This satisfies the property that if
k\in L
is orthogonal to each
f\in F
, it is also orthogonal to
l
. We say that
F
is a
finite cover
of
l
. This motivates the following general definition.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
L
be a locally finite weak semilattice and let
l\in L
. A finite set
F\subseteq L
is a
finite cover
of
l
if

•

f\leq l
for each
f\in F
, and

•

if
k\in L
with
k\perp f
for each
f\in F
, then
k\perp l
.

A finite cover
F
of
l
is
trivial
if
l\in F
. The set of maximal elements in a finite cover is again a finite cover; it is natural to only consider finite covers whose elements are all maximal.

Given a representation
U\colon L\to\mathcal{O}_{c}(X)
of
L
on a totally disconnected locally compact Hausdorff space
X
, we say a finite cover
F
of
l\in L
is
concrete
if
U(l)=\bigcup_{f\in F}U(f)
.

Example 3.3
.

Let
\Lambda
be a finitely aligned higher rank graph and let
L_{\Lambda}=\Lambda\cup\{0\}
be its locally finite weak semilattice (see Examples
2.3
and
2.22
). A finite cover
E
of a vertex

is precisely a finite exhaustive subset in the sense of
[
21
, Definition 3.6]
. Suppose
v\in\Lambda^{0}
is a vertex and
1\leq i\leq k
is an index such that
0<\#\,v\Lambda^{(i)}<\infty
. Then for each
\lambda\in v\Lambda
an explicit finite cover of

is given by

\lambda\Lambda^{(i)}.

This is concrete with respect to the representation

given by

because any infinite path
z\in\partial\Lambda
starting with
\lambda
must start with
\lambda\epsilon
for some
\epsilon\in\Lambda^{(i)}
.

We introduce the following conventions.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
L
be a locally finite weak semilattice and let
F
be a finite cover of an element
l\in L
. We write

(l\mid F)\coloneq l-\bigvee_{f\in F}f\in\mathbb{Z}L

for the relative complement of
F
in
l
(the join
\bigvee
was introduced in Definition
2.15
). Given further an element
k\leq l
, the finite cover

(3.2)

k\mathop{\downarrow}F:={\max}\big(\bigcup_{f\in F}k\mathop{\downarrow}f\big)

is given by the set of maximal elements in
\bigcup_{f\in F}k\mathop{\downarrow}f
, which is itself a finite cover of
k
. For a finite set
\mathcal{F}
of finite covers of
l
, we set

(3.3)

(l\mid\mathcal{F}):=l-\bigvee_{f\in F\in\mathcal{F}}f=\prod_{F\in\mathcal{F}}(l\mid F)

We write
k\mathop{\downarrow}\mathcal{F}
for the finite set
\{k\mathop{\downarrow}F\mid F\in\mathcal{F}\}
of finite covers of
k
.

The following computation is straightforward.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
L
be a locally finite weak semilattice, and let
\mathcal{F}
be a finite set of finite covers of
l\in L
. Then for each
h\in L
we have
h(l\mid\mathcal{F})=\bigvee_{k\in h\mathop{\downarrow}l}(k\mid k\mathop{\downarrow}\mathcal{F})\in\mathbb{Z}L
.

Let us come back to the motivating case where
G\cong S\ltimes X
and
U\colon L\to\mathcal{O}_{c}(X)
is an
S
-equivariant generating representation of a locally finite weak semilattice
L
on
X
. Identifying
C_{c}(\widehat{L},\mathbb{Z})
with
\mathbb{Z}L
, our description of the kernel
I
of the surjection
C_{c}(\widehat{L},\mathbb{Z})\twoheadrightarrow C_{c}(X,\mathbb{Z})
using (
3.1
) is now equivalent to the statement that
I
is generated by the relative complements
(l\mid F)
of certain finite covers in
L
. Consider, for each
l\in L
,

\mathscr{R}(l)=\left\{F\subseteq L\;|\;F\text{ is a finite cover of }l,\,(l\mid F)\in I\right\}.

Because
I
is an ideal in
\mathbb{Z}L
, the system
\mathscr{R}=(\mathscr{R}(l))_{l\in L}
of finite covers is closed under
\mathop{\downarrow}
in the following sense:

(M)

For all
l\in L
,
F\in\mathscr{R}(l)
and
k\in L
with
k\leq l
,
k\mathop{\downarrow}F\in\mathscr{R}(k)
.

Moreover,
\mathscr{R}
is
S
-invariant in the sense that for all
l\in L
,
s\in S
with
l\in\dom_{L}(s)
, and for all
F\in\mathscr{R}(l)
, the finite cover

s.F\coloneq\left\{s.f\;|\;f\in F\right\}

is an element of
\mathscr{R}(s.l)
. This
S
-invariance holds because
I
is
S
-invariant. Let us now turn this into an abstract definition.

Definition 3.6
.

Let
L
be a locally finite weak semilattice. A
system of finite covers in
L
is a tuple

\mathscr{R}=(\mathscr{R}(l))_{l\in L}

where each
\mathscr{R}(l)
is a set of finite covers of
l\in L
, such that

•

for each
l\in L
, the trivial cover
\{l\}
is an element of
\mathscr{R}(l)
, and

•

\mathscr{R}
satisfies condition (M): if for all
l\in L
,
F\in\mathscr{R}(l)
and
k\in L
with
k\leq l
, we have that
k\mathop{\downarrow}F\in\mathscr{R}(k)
.

Given an action
S\mathbin{\curvearrowright}L
of an inverse semigroup
S
, we say that
\mathscr{R}
is
S
-invariant if for all
l\in L
,
s\in S
with
l\in\dom(s)
, and for all

, we have that

\mathscr{R}(s.l)
.

The set
\mathscr{R}(l)
of all finite covers

of

for which

, i.e.

in

, may be impractically large. In concrete applications there is often a natural system of finite covers which is large enough to generate

but small enough to be manageable.

Definition 3.7
.

Let
U\colon L\to\mathcal{O}_{c}(X)
be a generating representation of a locally finite weak semilattice

on a totally disconnected locally compact Hausdorff space

. Let
\mathscr{R}
be a system of concrete finite covers in

. We say that
\mathscr{R}
is

if whenever

, with

and covers

•

F(l)\in\mathscr{R}(l)
,

•

F(l,f_{1})\in\mathscr{R}(f_{1})

,

•

for all

and
f_{2}\in F(l,f_{1})
,

•

\dotso
,

•

F(l,f_{1},f_{2},\dotsc,f_{N-1})\in\mathscr{R}(f_{N-1})

for all
f_{1}\in F(l),f_{2}\in F(l,f_{1}),\dots,
and
f_{N-1}\in F(l,f_{1},f_{2},\dotsc,f_{N-2})
,

f_{1}\in F(l)
,
f_{2}\in F(l,f_{1}),\dots,f_{N-1}\in F(l,f_{1},f_{2},\dotsc,f_{N-1})

Example 3.8
.

Let
\Lambda
be a higher rank graph of rank
k
as in Examples
2.3
,
2.22
and
3.3
, and let us use the same notation as before. As in Example
3.3
, we assume that for every vertex
v
in
\Lambda
and
1\leq i\leq k
, we have
0<\#v\Lambda^{(i)}<\infty
. For
\lambda\in\Lambda={L_{\Lambda}}^{\times}
set

\mathscr{R}(\lambda)=\{\{\lambda\}\}\cup\{\lambda\Lambda^{(i)}\mid 1\leq i\leq k\}

and set
\mathscr{R}(0)=\{\{0\}\}
.

Let us check the family
\mathscr{R}
of finite covers satisfies condition (M). Let
\mu,\lambda\in\Lambda
with
\mu\leq\lambda
(i.e.
\mu\in\lambda\Lambda
) and let

. If in the

, unique factorisation forces
\mu\mathop{\downarrow}\lambda\Lambda^{(i)}=\mu\Lambda^{(i)}\in\mathscr{R}(\mu)

Moreover, the system
\mathscr{R}
is thorough with respect to the generating representation

(see Examples
2.22
and

\lambda,\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}\in\Lambda
satisfy

f_{N}\leq\lambda_{i}
for some

d(f_{N})\geq d(\lambda_{i})
for each

and apply the unique factorisation property. Pick

with
d(\lambda)+d\geq d(\lambda_{i})
for each

, and set

. Pick
i_{0},\dots,i_{N-1}\in\{1,\dots,k\}
such that exactly

are equal to
i
for each
1\leq i\leq k
. Finally, set
F(\lambda)=\lambda\Lambda^{(i_{0})}
and, by recursion on

, given
f_{1}\in F(\lambda),f_{2}\in F(\lambda,f_{1}),\dots,f_{m}\in F(\lambda,f_{1},\dots,f_{m-1})

. By construction, at

, and

, which lies in

for some

it follows by the unique factorisation property that

, i.e.

.

Note that
\bigvee_{\varepsilon\in\lambda\Lambda^{(i)}}\lambda\varepsilon=\sum_{\varepsilon\in\lambda\Lambda^{(i)}}\lambda\varepsilon
because elements in
\lambda\Lambda^{(i)}
are pairwise orthogonal.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
U\colon L\to\mathcal{O}_{c}(X)
be a generating representation of a locally finite weak semilattice

.
Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

\mathscr{R}

4.
Discretisation

Now we come to the crucial idea of discretisation. Let
L
be a locally finite weak semilattice equipped with an action of an inverse semigroup
S
. Write
L^{\times}\coloneq L\setminus\left\{0\right\}
, and consider the restriction
S\mathbin{\curvearrowright}L^{\times}
of our action to
L^{\times}
. Viewing
L^{\times}
as a discrete topological space, we view
S\mathbin{\curvearrowright}L^{\times}
as an action of
S
by partial homeomorphisms on
L^{\times}
and form the partial transformation groupoid
S\ltimes L^{\times}
.

The idea of discretisation is to find a suitable weak equivalence between
S\ltimes L^{\times}
and
S\ltimes\widehat{L}
.

Note that we always have an embedding
L^{\times}\hookrightarrow\widehat{L}
sending
k\in L^{\times}
to the character
\chi_{k}
given by
\chi_{k}(l)=1
if and only if
k\leq l
. Hence we obtain an induced embedding
S\ltimes L^{\times}\hookrightarrow S\ltimes\widehat{L}
. But this is not the desired weak equivalence. Instead, we construct a proper étale correspondence
\Omega\colon S\ltimes L^{\times}\to S\ltimes\widehat{L}
.

To define étale correspondences, we need the notion of an action of an étale groupoid
G
on a topological space
X
. Such an action consists of an
anchor map

\tau\colon X\to G^{0}
assumed continuous, and an
action map

(g,x)\mapsto g.x\colon G\times_{G^{0}}X=\{(g,x)\in G\times X\mid s(g)=\tau(x)\}\to X
satisfying
\tau(g.x)=r(g)
,
\tau(x).x=x
and
(gh).x=g.(h.x)
whenever the expressions make sense. The action is said to be
free
if
g.x=x
implies
g\in G^{0}
,
proper
if
G\times_{G^{0}}X\to X\times X
,
(g,x)\mapsto(g.x,x)
is proper, and
étale
if the anchor map is a local homeomorphism.
The action groupoid
G\ltimes X
is an étale groupoid with arrow space
G\times_{G^{0}}X
and unit space
X
with multiplication given by
(g,h.x)(h,x)=(gh,x)
.

Definition 4.1

(Étale correspondence, see
[
1
]
)
.

Let
G
and
H
be étale groupoids. A
G
-
H

bispace
is a topological space
\Omega
equipped with a left action of
G
and a right action of
H
that commute; that is, the left anchor
\rho\colon\Omega\to G^{0}
is
H
-invariant, the right anchor
\sigma\colon\Omega\to H^{0}
is
G
-invariant and
g.(\omega.h)=(g.\omega).h
for compatible
g\in G
,
\omega\in\Omega
and
h\in H
. An
étale correspondence

\Omega\colon G\to H
is a
G
-
H
bispace
\Omega
such that the right action
\Omega\mathbin{\curvearrowleft}H
is free, proper and étale.
The correspondence
\Omega
is
proper
if the map
\Omega/H\to G^{0}
induced by the left anchor is proper. If
G=G^{0}
and
H=H^{0}
are locally compact Hausdorff spaces, the data of an étale correspondence reduces to a locally compact Hausdorff space
Z
equipped with a continuous map
\rho\colon Z\to X
and a local homeomorphism
\sigma\colon Z\to Y
; we refer to
Z\colon X\to Y
as a
topological correspondence
. Note that
Z\colon X\to Y
is proper if and only if
\rho\colon Z\to X
is proper.

Let us introduce the following notation.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
G
be an étale groupoid, let
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
be right and left
G

\sigma\colon X_{1}\to G^{0}
and
\rho\colon X_{2}\to G^{0}

of

and
X_{2}
over

X_{1}\times_{K}X_{2}\coloneq\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in X_{1}\times X_{2}\mid\sigma(x_{1})=\rho(x_{2})\in K^{0}\}/K

is the quotient defined by
(x_{1}.g,x_{2})\sim(x_{1},g.x_{2})
for

,
x_{2}\in X_{2}
and

\sigma(x_{1})=r(g)
and
\rho(x_{2})=s(g)

for the class of a pair

are open subgroupoids with actions

such that the left and right actions on

commute, we identify
(X_{1}\times_{K_{1}}X_{2})\times_{K_{2}}X_{3}

Remark 4.3
.

In the above,

and

may be viewed as partial

are only defined on some open subset.

The composition
\Lambda\circ\Omega\colon G\to K
of étale correspondences

and

is given by the

Definition 4.4
.

Let
L
be a locally finite weak semilattice. The (topological)
discretisation correspondence

\Omega^{0}\colon L^{\times}\to\widehat{L}
is the topological correspondence

\Omega^{0}=\coprod_{l\in L^{\times}}\widehat{L}(l)=\left\{(l,\chi)\in L^{\times}\times\widehat{L}\;|\;\chi(l)=1\right\},

with left anchor map
\rho(l,\chi)=l
and right anchor map

. The discretisation correspondence is proper because

is compact for each

.

When

for each

and

. The discretisation correspondence
\Omega^{0}\colon L^{\times}\to\widehat{L}
becomes an
S
-equivariant topological correspondence in the following sense.

Definition 4.5
.

Let
S
be an inverse semigroup. An
S
-equivariant topological correspondence
is a topological correspondence

equipped with actions of
S
on
X
,

and

and

for each

The composition of two

-equivariant topological correspondence.
The condition that

for each

ensures well-definition of the naïve action of

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
S
be an inverse semigroup and let

be an
S
-equivariant topological correspondence. Then
S\ltimes X
acts on

with anchor

via

so that

.

This means that

given by
[s,x].[t,z]=[st,z]
defines an action correspondence

. Composing this with the étale correspondence associated to the étale homomorphism
[s,z]\mapsto[s,\sigma(z)]\colon S\ltimes Z\to S\ltimes Y
, we obtain the following étale correspondence.

Definition 4.7
.

Let
S
be an inverse semigroup and let

be an
S
-equivariant topological correspondence. The
associated étale correspondence

has underlying space

, with left action given by

and right action by
(z,[s,y]).[t,y^{\prime}]=(z,[st,y^{\prime}])

and

, we write

, which is the unique element of

.

Note that

is proper if and only if

is proper. The construction of the étale correspondence associated to an

-equivariant topological correspondence is functorial with respect to composition of correspondences.

Definition 4.8
.

Let
L
be a locally finite weak semilattice equipped with the action of an inverse semigroup

. The

is the étale correspondence

associated to the

is proper.

Remark 4.9
.

We have
\Omega\cong\Omega^{0}\times_{\widehat{L}}(S\ltimes\widehat{L})
. However,

cannot be described as
(S\ltimes L^{\times})\times_{L^{\times}}\Omega^{0}
. The reason is that for
[l,s,\chi]\in\Omega
,

might not lie in

. For the same reason, the canonical action

does not describe

as
S\ltimes\Omega^{0}
.

4.1.
Discretisation in groupoid homology

Let
L
be a locally finite weak semilattice equipped with an action of an inverse semigroup
S
. As explained in
[
49
, Corollary 3.6]
,
\Omega
induces the map
\Hlgy_{*}(\Omega)\colon\Hlgy_{*}(S\ltimes L^{\times})\to\Hlgy_{*}(S\ltimes\widehat{L})
in groupoid homology. The argument for the following result is identical to that in
[
49
, Example 3.10]
. We reproduce it here for convenience.

Theorem 4.10
.

For every inverse semigroup action
S\mathbin{\curvearrowright}L
on a locally finite weak semilattice
L
, the discretisation correspondence
\Omega
from Definition
4.8
induces an isomorphism in groupoid homology

\Hlgy_{*}(\Omega)\colon\Hlgy_{*}(S\ltimes L^{\times})\xrightarrow{\raisebox{-3.01389pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\sim$}}\Hlgy_{*}(S\ltimes\widehat{L}).

Proof.

The étale correspondence
\Omega\colon S\ltimes L^{\times}\to S\ltimes\widehat{L}
decomposes into the correspondences associated to the action
S\ltimes L^{\times}\mathbin{\curvearrowright}\Omega^{0}
and the étale homomorphism
S\ltimes\Omega^{0}\to S\ltimes\widehat{L}
. Set
G=S\ltimes L^{\times}
and
H=S\ltimes\widehat{L}
and
K=S\ltimes\Omega^{0}
. Following
[
49
, Examples 3.8 and 3.9]
, the induced map
\Hlgy_{*}(\Omega)
is induced by a chain map
\Phi\colon\mathbb{Z}[G^{\bullet}]\to\mathbb{Z}[H^{\bullet}]
of Matui’s chain complexes. The chain map is induced at
n\geq 0
(contravariantly) by the map
K^{n}\to G^{n},[s_{n-1},\dots,s_{0},l,\chi]\mapsto[s_{n-1},\dots,s_{0},l]
and (covariantly) by the local homeomorphism
K^{n}\to H^{n},[s_{n-1},\dots,s_{0},l,\chi]\mapsto[s_{n-1},\dots,s_{0},\chi]
. This boils down to the map

\displaystyle\Phi_{n}\colon\mathbb{Z}[G^{n}]

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{Z}[H^{n}]

\displaystyle\delta_{x}

\displaystyle\mapsto 1_{V_{x}},

where for each
x=[s_{n-1},\dots,s_{0},l]\in G^{n}
, the compact open set
V_{x}\subseteq H^{n}
is given by

V_{x}=\{[s_{n-1},\dots,s_{0},\chi]\mid\chi\in\widehat{L}(l)\}.

We will show that
\Phi_{n}
is in fact an isomorphism for each
n\geq 0
.

For
x,y\in G^{n}
we set
x\leq y
if
V_{x}\subseteq V_{y}
, this happens exactly if
x=[s_{n-1},\dots,s_{0},k]
and
y=[s_{n-1},\dots,s_{0},l]
with
k\leq l
. It follows that
\{V_{x}\mid x\leq y\}
is a generating independent concrete locally finite weak semilattice in
V_{y}
. By Lemma
2.16
, the restriction
\mathbb{Z}[\{x\mid x\leq y\}]\to\mathbb{Z}[V_{y}]
of
\Phi_{n}
is an isomorphism. Surjectivity of
\Phi_{n}
follows because
\{V_{y}\mid y\in G^{n}\}
covers
H^{n}
and hence
\{\mathbb{Z}[V_{y}]\mid y\in G^{n}\}
spans
\mathbb{Z}[H^{n}]
.

For injectivity of
\Phi_{n}
, let
F\subseteq G^{n}
be finite and let
(a_{x})_{x\in F}
be integers with
\sum_{x\in F}a_{x}1_{V_{x}}=0
. Pick a maximal element
y=[s_{n-1},\dots,s_{0},l]\in F
. Then
0=\sum_{x\in F}a_{x}1_{V_{x}}
evaluated at the element
[s_{n-1},\dots,s_{0},\chi_{l}]\in H^{n}
recovers
a_{y}
because
[s_{n-1},\dots,s_{0},\chi_{l}]\in V_{x}
if and only if
y\leq x
. Thus
a_{y}=0
and by induction we conclude
\sum_{x\in F}a_{x}\delta_{x}=0
.
∎

Since
S\ltimes L^{\times}
is a discrete groupoid, it is Morita equivalent to a disjoint union of isotropy groups. Therefore, we arrive at the following consequence.

Corollary 4.11
.

For every inverse semigroup action
S\mathbin{\curvearrowright}L
on a locally finite weak semilattice
L
, we have the following isomorphism in groupoid homology:

\bigoplus_{[l]\in S\backslash L^{\times}}\Hlgy_{*}((S\ltimes L^{\times})_{l}^{l})\cong\Hlgy_{*}(S\ltimes\widehat{L}).

Here
S\backslash L^{\times}
is the set of orbits for the action
S\mathbin{\curvearrowright}L^{\times}
, and
(S\ltimes L^{\times})_{l}^{l}
denotes the isotropy at the unit
l\in L^{\times}=(S\ltimes L^{\times})^{0}
.

\Omega\colon S\ltimes L^{\times}\to S\ltimes\widehat{L}

5.3.
Isomorphism at the level of ABC spectral sequences

Let us for the rest of this subsection fix a countable locally finite weak semilattice

with an action of a countable inverse semigroup
S
with idempotent semilattice
E
such that
S\ltimes\widehat{L}
is Hausdorff. Let
G=S\ltimes L^{\times}
and

with
X=L^{\times}
and

, and let
\Omega=\Omega^{0}(S)\colon G\to H
be the discretisation correspondence. Let
e_{0},\dots,e_{n}\in E
be idempotents and let
F_{i}\subseteq S_{e_{i}}
be finite subgroups. In this setting, we constructed an
F_{n,\mathop{\downarrow}}
-equivariant topological correspondence (see (
5.2
) and (
5.16
))

Our goal now is to prove the following.

Theorem 5.9
.

The proper étale correspondence
Z_{n}(F_{n,\mathop{\downarrow}})\colon F_{n,\mathop{\downarrow}}\ltimes X_{n}\to F_{n,\mathop{\downarrow}}\ltimes Y_{n}
induces a
\K_{*}
-isomorphism.

For the proof, let us first analyse the space

\mathscr{Y}_{n}\coloneq{}_{H_{n}^{0}}H\times_{H_{n-1}^{0}}\dotso\times_{H_{1}^{0}}H\times_{H_{0}^{0}}H^{0}.

Consider

\mathscr{L}_{n}\coloneq\left\{(s_{n-1},\dotsc,s_{0},\widehat{L}(k))\subseteq\mathscr{Y}_{n}\;|\;s_{m}\in e_{m+1}Se_{m}\text{ for $0\leq m\leq n-1$ },k\in\dom_{L}(s_{n-1}\cdots s_{0})\right\},

where

(s_{n-1},\dotsc,s_{0},\widehat{L}(k))\coloneq\left\{[s_{n-1},\dotsc,s_{0},\chi]\in\mathscr{Y}_{n}\;|\;\chi\in\widehat{L}(k)\right\}.

We note that by construction each member
(s_{n-1},\dotsc,s_{0},\widehat{L}(k))\in\mathscr{L}_{n}
is compact and open, and homeomorphic to its projection
\widehat{L}(k)
onto
H^{0}
.

Lemma 5.10
.

The family
\mathscr{L}_{n}
is a generating independent locally finite weak semilattice in
\mathscr{Y}_{n}
in the sense of Definition
2.10
.

In particular, we have an isomorphism
\cs(\mathscr{L}_{n})\cong C_{0}(\mathscr{Y}_{n}),\,e\mapsto 1_{e}
, which induces the identification
\mathscr{Y}_{n}\cong\widehat{\mathscr{L}_{n}}
.

Proof.

To see that
\mathscr{L}_{n}
is a weak semilattice, take
e_{1},e_{2}\in\mathscr{L}_{n}
. Then
e_{1}\cap e_{2}
is a compact subset of
\mathscr{Y}_{n}
(this uses Hausdorffness). Moreover, it is easy to see that every point of
\mathscr{Y}_{n}
has a neighbourhood given by an element of
\mathscr{L}_{n}
which is completely contained in
e_{1}\cap e_{2}
. It follows that
e_{1}\cap e_{2}
can be written as a finite union
e_{1}\cap e_{2}=\bigcup_{i}f_{i}
for some
f_{i}\in\mathscr{L}_{n}
. Now take
d\in\mathscr{L}_{n}
with
d=(s_{n-1},\dotsc,s_{0},\widehat{L}(k))
such that
d\subseteq e_{1}\cap e_{2}=\bigcup_{i}f_{i}
. As
[s_{n-1},\dotsc,s_{0},\chi_{k}]
lies in
d
, this element has to lie in some
f_{i}
. Suppose that
f_{i}=(t_{n-1},\dotsc,t_{0},\widehat{L}(l))
. Then
\chi_{k}(l)=1
, so that
k\leq l
and
\widehat{L}(k)\subseteq\widehat{L}(l)
. Moreover,
[s_{n-1},\dotsc,s_{0},\chi_{k}]=[t_{n-1},\dotsc,t_{0},\chi_{k}]
implies that there are idempotents
e_{m}\in E^{\times}
with
s_{m}e_{m}=t_{m}e_{m}
and
s_{m-1}\dotsm s_{0}.k=t_{m-1}\dotsm t_{0}.k\in\dom_{L^{\times}}(e_{m})
for all
0\leq m\leq n-1
. It follows that
[s_{n-1},\dotsc,s_{0},\chi]=[t_{n-1},\dotsc,t_{0},\chi]
for all
\chi\in\widehat{L}(k)
, i.e.,
d\subseteq f_{i}
.

To see that
\mathscr{L}_{n}
is locally finite, take a finite subset
F\subseteq\mathscr{L}_{n}
. Proceeding inductively on
\#F
, we want to show that there exists a
\mathop{\downarrow}
-closed finite subset
\widebar{F}
of
\mathscr{L}_{n}
containing
F
. So suppose we have found such a
\widebar{F}
for
F
. Now take an additional element
f\in\mathscr{L}_{n}
, say
f=(s_{n-1},\dotsc,s_{0},\widehat{L}(k))
. The down-set
\{g\in\mathscr{L}_{n}\mid g\leq f\}
carries the weak semilattice structure of
\{l\in L\mid l\leq k\}
. In particular, since
L
is locally finite, we may a finite subset
A\subseteq\{g\in\mathscr{L}_{n}\mid g\leq f\}
which is
\mathop{\downarrow}
-closed and contains the finite set
\{f\}\cup(\widebar{F}\mathop{\downarrow}f)
. Now we claim that
\widebar{F}\cup A
is
\mathop{\downarrow}
-closed. Indeed, given
e\in\widebar{F}
and
g\in A
, we have
e\mathop{\downarrow}g=e\mathop{\downarrow}(f\mathop{\downarrow}g)=(e\mathop{\downarrow}f)\mathop{\downarrow}g
, which lies in
A
because
e\mathop{\downarrow}f\subseteq A
and
A
is
\mathop{\downarrow}
-closed.

The family
\mathscr{L}_{n}
satisfies (GEN) because for each fixed
(s_{n-1},\dots,s_{0},\widehat{L}(k))
, the family

\left\{(s_{n-1},\dots,s_{0},\widehat{L}(l))\;|\;l\leq k\right\}

satisfies (GEN) for
(s_{n-1},\dots,s_{0},\widehat{L}(k))
. Moreover,
\mathscr{L}_{n}
satisfies (IND). Indeed, given elements
e,e_{1},\dots,e_{m}\in\mathscr{L}_{n}
with
e=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}e_{i}
where
e=(s_{n-1},\dots,s_{0},\widehat{L}(k))
, then there is some
1\leq i\leq m
with
[s_{n-1},\dots,s_{0},\chi_{k}]\in e_{i}\subseteq e
, from which it follows that
e_{i}=e
.
∎

Given
(s_{n-1},\dotsc,s_{0},\widehat{L}(k))\in\mathscr{L}_{n}
, we let
[s_{n-1},\dotsc,s_{0},\widehat{L}(k)]
denote its image in
Y_{n}
with respect to the canonical projection
\mathscr{Y}_{n}\twoheadrightarrow Y_{n}
. We set

L_{n}\coloneq\left\{[s_{n-1},\dotsc,s_{0},\widehat{L}(k)]\subseteq Y_{n}\;|\;(s_{n-1},\dotsc,s_{0},\widehat{L}(k))\in\mathscr{L}_{n}\right\}.

We identify
X_{n}
with
L_{n}^{\times}
via
[s_{n-1},\dots,s_{0},k]\mapsto[s_{n-1},\dotsc,s_{0},\widehat{L}(k)]\colon X_{n}\to L_{n}^{\times}
for elements
s_{0},\dots,s_{n-1}\in S
with
s_{m}\in e_{m+1}Se_{m}
for
0\leq m\leq n-1
, and

.
The following is a consequence of Lemma
5.10
.

Lemma 5.11
.

The family
L_{n}
is an
F_{n,\mathop{\downarrow}}
-invariant generating independent locally finite weak semilattice in
Y_{n}
in the sense of Definition

. In particular, we have an isomorphism
\cs(L_{n})\cong C_{0}(Y_{n}),\,e\mapsto 1_{e}
, which induces the identification

Proof.

We view
Y_{n}
as the orbit space of
\mathscr{Y}_{n}
under the action of the finite group
F=F_{n-1}\times\cdots\times F_{0}
. Explicitly, for

and

for the (right) action of

for

Suppose that

for some

taken with the inclusion order is a weak semilattice. It is moreover locally finite, because if

\widebar B

and is closed under
\mathop{\downarrow}
.

To see that

satisfies (IND), suppose

with

. Then the principal filter in
\widehat{\mathscr{L}}_{n}\cong\mathscr{Y}_{n}
associated to

lies in

for some
i
and some

. It follows that

and hence
[d]=[d_{i}]
.

The compact open subsets of

correspond directly to

-invariant compact open subsets of
\mathscr{Y}_{n}
. Finite unions and relative complements of such compact open subsets in

become finite unions and relative complements in
Y_{n}
. It follows that
L_{n}
inherits (GEN) from
\mathscr{L}_{n}
.
∎

Let us now address the question how independent resolutions help to compute or understand groupoid homology and K-theory for reduced groupoid
\cs
-algebras. We then apply our results to concrete examples.

6.1.
Computing groupoid homology using independent resolutions

Suppose that
G
is an ample groupoid, and
G_{i}
(
i=-1,0,1,\dotsc
) is an independent resolution of
G
in the sense of §
3.1
. In the following, we use the same notation as in §
3.1
. The decompositions
G_{i}^{(0)}=C_{i}\amalg O_{i}
, together with the isomorphisms
G_{i}|_{C_{i}}\cong G_{i-1}|_{O_{i-1}}
, induce long exact sequences in groupoid homology:

(6.1)

\dotso\to\Hlgy_{*+1}(G_{i-1}|_{O_{i-1}})\to\Hlgy_{*}(G_{i}|_{O_{i}})\to\Hlgy_{*}(G_{i})\to\Hlgy_{*}(G_{i-1}|_{O_{i-1}})\to\Hlgy_{*-1}(G_{i}|_{O_{i}})\to\dotso

For non-negative integers
p,q
, set
D_{pq}\coloneq\Hlgy_{q}(G_{p}|_{O_{p}})
and
E_{pq}\coloneq\Hlgy_{q}(G_{p})
. Then we obtain an exact couple

\textstyle{D\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{i}
\textstyle{D\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{j}
\textstyle{E\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{k}

Here
i\colon D_{pq}=\Hlgy_{q}(G_{p}|_{O_{p}})\to\Hlgy_{q-1}(G_{p+1}|_{O_{p+1}})=D_{p+1,q-1}
is the boundary map from the long exact sequence (
6.1
),
j\colon D_{pq}=\Hlgy_{q}(G_{p}|_{O_{p}})\to\Hlgy_{q}(G_{p})=E_{pq}
is induced by the canonical inclusion
G_{p}|_{O_{p}}\hookrightarrow G_{p}
, and
k\colon E_{pq}=\Hlgy_{q}(G_{p})\to\Hlgy_{q}(G_{p-1}|_{O_{p-1}})=D_{p-1,q}
is given by the composition

\Hlgy_{q}(G_{p})\to\Hlgy_{q}(G_{p}|_{C_{p}})\to\Hlgy_{q}(G_{p-1}|_{O_{p-1}}),

where the first map is induced by restriction, and the second map is induced by the isomorphism
G_{p}|_{C_{p}}\cong G_{p-1}|_{O_{p-1}}
which is part of the data of an independent resolution (see §
3.1
).

This exact couple gives rise to the spectral sequence
E^{r}_{pq}
, where
E^{0}_{pq}=E_{pq}=\Hlgy_{q}(G_{p})
and
E_{pq}^{1}
is given by the
p
-th homology of the chain complex

\dotso\to\Hlgy_{q}(G_{2})\to\Hlgy_{q}(G_{1})\to\Hlgy_{q}(G_{0})\to\Hlgy_{q}(G)\to 0,

where the chain maps are given by
j\circ k
. The construction of the spectral sequence can be found for instance in
[
74
, § 5.9]
(see in particular
[
74
, Application 5.9.8]
).

Since groupoid homology vanishes in negative degrees, this spectral sequence converges to
0
, i.e., for all
p,q
there exists
r_{pq}
such that
E_{pq}^{r}=0
for all
r\geq r_{pq}
. Then the spectral sequence determines
\Hlgy_{*}(G)=\Hlgy_{*}(G_{-1})
up to extension problems, assuming that we understand
\Hlgy_{*}(G_{p})
and the maps
\Hlgy_{*}(G_{p+1})\to\Hlgy_{*}(G_{p})
for all
p\geq 0
.

Here is an example application.

Corollary 6.1
.

Suppose that we are given an independent resolution for
G=G_{-1}
, consisting of groupoid
G_{i}
which satisfy
\Hlgy_{*}(G_{i})=0
for all
*>0
and
i\geq 0
. Then
\Hlgy_{q}(G)
is given by the
q
-th homology of the chain complex

\dotso\to\Hlgy_{0}(G_{p+1})\to\Hlgy_{0}(G_{p})\to\Hlgy_{0}(G_{p-1})\to\dotso\to\Hlgy_{0}(G_{1})\to\Hlgy_{0}(G_{0})\to 0.

Proof.

This follows immediately from an analysis of the spectral sequence
E_{pq}^{r}
, using that many terms vanish and that it converges to
0
.
∎

Assume now that we are given an independent resolution

Corollary 6.2
.

Assume that we are given a finite length independent resolution
G_{i}=S_{i}\ltimes\widehat{L_{i}}
of an ample groupoid
G
, and suppose that for every
i
, the groupoids
G_{i}
are Hausdorff and exact, and
S_{i}
as well as
L_{i}
are countable. Further assume that for every
i
and every unit

, the isotropy group
(S_{i}\ltimes L_{i}^{\times})_{l}^{l}
satisfies the Baum–Connes conjecture, and that for every
i
, the discretisation correspondence
\Omega_{i}\colon S_{i}\ltimes L_{i}^{\times}\to S_{i}\ltimes\widehat{L_{i}}
from §
4
induces an isomorphism

\K_{*}(\csr(\Omega_{i}))\colon\K_{*}(\csr(S_{i}\ltimes L_{i}^{\times}))\xrightarrow{\raisebox{-3.01389pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\sim$}}\K_{*}(\csr(S_{i}\ltimes\widehat{L}_{i}))
. Then
G
satisfies the Baum–Connes conjecture.

Proof.

Since our groupoids
G_{i}
are exact, our finite length independent resolution yields a long exact sequence of reduced groupoid
\cs
-algebras, which splits into finitely many short exact sequences, which in turn induce finitely many long exact sequences in K-theory. We obtain analogous long exact sequences for the left-hand side of the Baum–Connes conjecture, i.e., in
\mathrm{K}^{\mathrm{top}}_{*}
(see
[
70
, § 7.1]
and
[
71
, § 5]

G_{i}
satisfy the Baum–Connes conjecture for all

, then so does
G
. By Remark
5.14
, our assumptions imply that
G_{i}
satisfy the Baum–Connes conjecture for all

, which completes the proof.
∎

In the following, let us develop a concrete application. We need the following terminology: Given a countable discrete group
\Gamma
, we say that the discretisation formula with arbitrary coefficients is valid for
\Gamma
if for every countable semilattice
E
, every
\Gamma
-action

by order automorphisms and every separable
\Gamma

\cs

Here
\Gamma

Corollary 6.3
.

Let
\Gamma
be a countable discrete group. Assume that
\Gamma
is exact. Suppose that there exists a finite length independent resolution for
G_{-1}=\Gamma
consisting of groupoids

of the form

for countable semilattices

with the property that, for all

, the stabilizer group

is finite. Then
\Gamma
satisfies the Baum–Connes conjecture with coefficients if and only if the discretisation formula with arbitrary coefficients is valid for
\Gamma
.

Proof.

The implication “
\Rightarrow
” follows from
[
15
, Theorem 3.12]
. For the converse, we use the following equivalent formulation of the Baum–Connes conjecture with coefficients. As pointed out in
[
17
, § 5.3]
, a countable discrete group
\Gamma
satisfies the Baum–Connes conjecture with coefficients if and only if the following statement is true: For every separable
\Gamma

\cs
-algebra

\K_{*}(F\ltimes_{r}A)=0
for every finite subgroup
F\subseteq\Gamma
(here the
F

is given by restricting the
\Gamma
-action on

), then
\K_{*}(\Gamma\ltimes_{r}A)=0
. Now suppose that
A
is a separable
G
-
\cs
-algebra such that
\K_{*}(F\ltimes_{r}A)=0
for every finite subgroup
F\subseteq\Gamma
. Since
\Gamma
is exact, our finite length independent resolution for
\Gamma

6.3.
Examples

Garside categories provide a source for examples of groupoids which admit finite length independent resolutions. Let us first give a brief introduction to Garside categories, which in this article will mean small categories with Garside families with particular properties as specified below.
4
4

4

The notion of a Garside category is in general somewhat flexible; the emphasis is on studying Garside families with various desirable properties, but different situations merit different requirements on the Garside family.
We will follow the exposition in
[
36
]
and refer the reader to
[
18
,
35
,
36
]
and the references therein for more details.

Let
\mathfrak{C}
be a small category. The set of objects
\mathfrak{C}^{0}
in
\mathfrak{C}
will be viewed as a subset of the set of morphisms by identifying an object with the corresponding identity morphism. Moreover, we will identify the category with its set of morphisms, again denoted by
\mathfrak{C}
. Let
\mathfrak{d}:\>\mathfrak{C}\to\mathfrak{C}^{0}
and
\mathfrak{t}:\>\mathfrak{C}\to\mathfrak{C}^{0}
be the domain and target maps.
\mathfrak{C}^{*}
denotes the set of invertible elements of
\mathfrak{C}
. We need to introduce some terminology.

Definition 6.7
.

•

\mathfrak{C}
is called left cancellative, if, for all
c,x,y\in\mathfrak{C}
with
\mathfrak{d}(c)=\mathfrak{t}(x)=\mathfrak{t}(y)
,
cx=cy
implies
x=y
.

•

\mathfrak{C}
is called finitely aligned if for all
a,b\in\mathfrak{C}
, there exists a finite subset
\left\{c_{i}\right\}\subseteq\mathfrak{C}
such that
a\mathfrak{C}\cap b\mathfrak{C}=\bigcup_{i}c_{i}\mathfrak{C}
.

•

Given
a,b\in\mathfrak{C}
, we write
a\preceq b
if
a
is a left divisor of
b
, i.e.,
b\in a\mathfrak{C}
. We write
a\prec b
if
b\mathfrak{C}\subsetneq a\mathfrak{C}
. We write
a\mathop{\preceq}\limits^{\vbox to-5.00305pt{\kern-4.2194pt\hbox{$\sim$}\vss}}b
if
a
is a right divisor of
b
, i.e.,
b\in\mathfrak{C}a
. We write
a\mathop{\prec}\limits^{\vbox to-5.00305pt{\kern-4.2194pt\hbox{$\sim$}\vss}}b
if
\mathfrak{C}b\subsetneq\mathfrak{C}a
. We write
a=^{*}b
if
a\in b\mathfrak{C}^{*}
(which is equivalent to
a\mathfrak{C}=b\mathfrak{C}
).

•

A subset
\mathfrak{S}\subseteq\mathfrak{S}
is closed under right comultiples if for all
r,s\in\mathfrak{S}
and
a\in\mathfrak{C}
with
r\preceq a
,
s\preceq a
, there exists
t\in\mathfrak{S}
with
r\preceq t
,
s\preceq t
and
t\preceq a
.

Now we can introduce the concept of a Garside family.

Definition 6.8
.

Suppose
\mathfrak{S}\subseteq\mathfrak{C}
is closed under right comultiples such that
\mathfrak{S}\cup\mathfrak{C}^{*}
generates
\mathfrak{C}
and
\mathfrak{S}^{\sharp}\coloneq\mathfrak{S}\mathfrak{C}^{*}\cup\mathfrak{C}^{*}
is closed under right divisors.

•

A finite sequence

in
\mathfrak{C}
is called a

\mathfrak{d}(s_{k})=\mathfrak{t}(s_{k+1})

.

•

For

, a normal decomposition or normal form of

in
\mathfrak{S}^{\sharp}
with

.

•

\mathfrak{S}
is called a
Garside family
if every element in
\mathfrak{C}
admits a normal decomposition.

In the following, assume that
\mathfrak{C}
is a finitely aligned left cancellative small category, and that
\mathfrak{S}\subseteq\mathfrak{C}
is a Garside family. Further suppose that the following hold:

•

\mathfrak{S}
is
=^{*}
-transverse, i.e., for all
s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathfrak{S}
,
s_{1}=^{*}s_{2}
implies
s_{1}=s_{2}
.

•

\mathfrak{S}\cap\mathfrak{C}^{*}=\emptyset
.

•

\mathfrak{S}
is locally finite, i.e.,

for all
\mathfrak{v}\in\mathfrak{C}^{0}
.

•

For all
\ell\geq 1
,
(\mathfrak{S}^{\leq\ell})^{\sharp}
is closed under left divisors. Here
\mathfrak{S}^{\leq\ell}\coloneq\mathfrak{S}\cup\mathfrak{S}^{2}\cup\dotso\cup\mathfrak{S}^{\ell}
.

Note that
L\coloneq\{a\mathfrak{C}\mid a\in\mathfrak{C}\}\cup\left\{0\right\}
