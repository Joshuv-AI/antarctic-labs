Genome-wide CRISPR screen identifies AMBRA1 as a potential biomarker of response to platinum-based therapies in oral squamous cell carcinomas
Authors: Lucía Acero-Riaguas, Iván López-García, Irene Posse-Alonso, Mariana Yáñez-Bartolomé, Alejandro San Lorenzo-Vaquero, Catalina Berca, César Pérez-Brotóns, Beatriz Castelo, Ana Sastre-Perona
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryOral squamous cell carcinomas (OSCCs) are among the most frequent and lethal cancers worldwide. Advanced stage tumors are still treated with standard chemotherapy; however, most patients develop chemotherapy resistance. To uncover new functional biomarkers of cisplatin response, we performed a synthetic lethality genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 screen in an OSCC cell line. This led to the discovery of AMBRA1 as a regulator of cisplatin sensitivity. In all tested OSCC cell lines and oral primary culture, AMBRA1 knockout cells showed increased sensitivity to cisplatin. Mechanistically, we demonstrated an unknown function of AMBRA1 in OSCCs, where loss of AMBRA1 led to an increase in S phase population, expression of genome instability markers, and DNA damage in basal conditions, predisposing cancer cells to an increased sensitivity to cisplatin and other platinum drugs. In summary, we uncovered AMBRA1 as a potential biomarker of response to platinum-based therapies in OSCCs.

Introduction

Head and neck cancer (HNC) is the sixth most common and lethal cancer worldwide.1 It comprises a diverse group of tumors that arise from oral or nasal cavities, larynx, pharynx, and salivary glands.1 Over 90% of HNC are Head and Neck squamous cell carcinomas (HNSCC) that emerge from mucosal epithelium, and within these, oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is among the most common types of HNSCCs with 40% of the cases. HNSCC are generally classified into two subgroups, according to their etiology: those associated with high-risk subtypes of human papilloma virus (HPV) or associated with tobacco and alcohol consumption. Oropharynx tumors are frequently related to HPV infection while oral cavity and larynx malignancies are mostly HPV-negative. The molecular characteristics of both types of tumors differ, with HPV+ having better prognosis than HPV- due to the high mutational burden of the latter.2,3,4,5

Overall survival of early stages in HNSCC patients has improved as a result of refinement in surgical techniques and prevention campaigns. On the contrary, advanced stages prognosis and survival have not changed over the last decades.6 Specifically, OSCCs are characterized by being very aggressive tumors as they are usually diagnosed at advanced stages, with a 5-year survival of 83% at early stages and 38% at advanced stages.7 First line treatment is surgery, combined with radiotherapy alone or with adjuvant chemotherapy. However, late-stage tumors, which are unresectable or unsuitable for radiotherapy or immunotherapy,6,8 are treated with standard chemotherapy, being cisplatin the gold standard. Still, 60% of these patients develop therapy resistance, so they suffer relapses, which is associated with a very poor outcome.3,5,6

In the clinical practice, chemotherapy treatment is still chosen based on histological factors and the ECOG patient status (Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group), and there are no established or reliable biomarkers that can predict an effective response to the treatment.3,5,6 This becomes very relevant in the case of cisplatin, since it can have a very negative impact on the quality of life of the patients, due to cytotoxic effects that, added to the side effects derived from surgery, can lead to treatment interruption or failure.6,9 Hence, identifying genes that could act either as predictive biomarkers of therapy response or as targets for complementary therapy alongside standard treatment procedures, has a major role in the improvement of prognosis, survival, and quality of life of the patients.

A tool that has been proved very useful for identifying treatment-response biomarkers are the whole-genome CRISPR-Cas9 knockout (KO) screens. These screens are based on the genome editing capacity of the bacterial CRISPR-Cas9 system which, combined with a lentiviral library of single guide RNAs (sgRNAs), allows the generation of individual KOs of all-coding regions of the genome and thus studying the effect of these genes under a selective pressure. Since the establishment of these genome-scale CRISPR-Cas9 KO screens, multiple CRISPR screens have been conducted to identify promising biomarkers of tumor progression, survival, or therapy resistance.10,11,12

In this study, we performed a genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 KO screen on an OSCC cell line to identify genes that confer cisplatin resistance, as cisplatin is the gold standard administered chemotherapy in HNSCC. Of all the candidate genes studied in the validation phase of the screen, AMBRA1-KO showed the highest sensitivity to cisplatin treatment. Further studies showed that AMBRA1 depletion sensitized the cells due to an increase in genome instability. Thus, this work reinforces the use of CRISPR-Cas9 KO screens as a useful tool for identifying predictive biomarkers of response and uncovers AMBRA1 as a potential biomarker to predict cisplatin resistance in OSCC patients.

Results

Whole-genome CRISPR-Cas9 KO screen identifies AMBRA1 as a cisplatin-resistance gene

Since cisplatin is the gold standard chemotherapy in patients with advanced OSCC, we evaluated cisplatin sensitivity in seven OSCC cell lines. We choose the OSC20 cell line for the whole-genome CRISPR-Cas9 KO synthetic lethality screen with cisplatin (Figure 1A), as this cell line derives from a lymph node metastasis of an advanced OSCC and demonstrated the highest resistance to cisplatin, with a ∼35% of live cells at the highest concentration tested (Figure S1A). To avoid intracellular differences in Cas9 expression, we generated clones of the OSC20 cell line expressing Cas9 protein and choose the one that expressed the highest levels of Cas9 protein (OSC20 clone 3, CL3, Figure S1B). Prior to the screening, we verified that clone 3 had a functional Cas9 (Figure S1C) and did not present major differences from its parental line, both in terms of cell growth and resistance to cisplatin (Figures S1D and S1E). We used a whole-genome library that targeted 19,050 genes (3 sgRNAs/gene). OSC20 cells were infected at a <0.3 multiplicity of infection (MOI) to ensure a low MOI, and after puromycin selection, cells were seeded and treated using an IC80 cisplatin concentration (50 μM) (Figure S1F) as a selective pressure for 7 days. IC80 was calculated based on the concentration of cisplatin required to kill 80% of the cells using the conditions from the screen −100 mm dishes at 80% confluency. T0 and untreated cells were collected as controls (Figure 1A). Quality control analyses showed an acceptable Gini index (Figure 1B) and a high sgRNA coverage (>80%, Figure 1C). Model-based Analysis of Genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 Knockout Robust Rank Aggregation (MAGeCK-RRA) analysis was used to uncover increase/decrease of sgRNAs, identifying 903 cisplatin-resistance and 909 cisplatin-sensitivity candidates (p value <0.05, Figure 1D). Considering the distribution of the sgRNAs, the value of log2 fold change (L2FC >±3), rank position and their function, we selected 5 resistance genes (AMBRA1, KEAP1, MRFAP1, TGFBR2, and PDGFD) and 5 sensitivity genes (GSK3B, MORF4L1, FAT2, MED17, and CDK8) for the validation phase of the screen (Figures 1D and 1E). As AMBRA1-KO (AMBRA1-KO) was the most promising candidate, further validation experiments were conducted in these cells. After verifying the loss of AMBRA1 protein expression in OSC20 CL3 KO cells (Figure 2C), we carried out concentration-response curves and measured the percentage of metabolically active cells, confirming that the depletion of AMBRA1 significantly sensitized cells to cisplatin treatment (IC50 sgTomato 4.07, sgAMBRA1-1 3.1, and sgAMBRA1-2 2.22 μM, Figures 2D and 2E). However, when we evaluated autophagy activation through LC3B and p62 expression, no differences were observed between AMBRA1-depleted and control cells, either at basal or cisplatin-treated conditions, or after blocking autophagy with the inhibitor Bafilomycin A1 (Figures 4C–4E). Furthermore, treatment with Bafilomycin A1 did not recapitulate AMBRA1-KO-induced sensibilization to cisplatin in control cells; instead, it appeared to have a protective effect at high concentrations of cisplatin (Figure 4F). These data suggests that the observed effects of AMBRA1 depletion decreasing cisplatin resistance may not be mediated by a defect in autophagy as was previously shown in oropharyngeal HNSCCs.13

AMBRA1 depletion decreases cell growth despite causing cyclin D1 accumulation and increased S-phase cell accumulation

Another recently described function of AMBRA1 is cell cycle regulation through the ubiquitination and degradation of D-type cyclins.14,15,16 Based on this role, we hypothesized that AMBRA1 deficiency could lead to the accumulation of cyclin D, increasing cell proliferation. Since it is known that high proliferation is associated with higher sensitivity to chemotherapy, this could explain the increased susceptibility to cisplatin of cells that lack AMBRA1. However, while we confirmed the accumulation of cyclin D1 protein (Figures 5A and S2D), AMBRA1-depleted cells displayed an overall tendency of decreased cell growth capacity in all cell lines analyzed (OSC20 CL3, OSC20-P, Cal27, and HN5), and even in the primary cell line OSCC43. The delay in cell growth was also sustained under low serum conditions in which we remove the external proliferation stimuli (Figures 5B, S3A, and S3B). To better understand the consequences of cyclin D1 upregulation upon AMBRA1 deletion, we performed EdU/Hoechst assays to measure DNA synthesis on OSC20 CL3 cells. Intriguingly, AMBRA1-deficient cells had an increased cell number in S phase, indicating a higher transition to S phase, although we could not detect a global increase in cell growth (Figure 5C). Additionally, the increase in proliferation was confirmed by measuring an increase in E2F2 mRNA levels on OSC20 CL3 and OSCC43 cells (Figures 5D and S3D). After 24 h of cisplatin treatment, both control and AMBRA1-deficient cells accumulated massively into S phase, reflecting probably S phase DNA damage checkpoint. But while control cells were able to transit to the G2/M phase, so that they can repair the DNA damage induced by cisplatin and overcome treatment, AMBRA1-deficient cells fail to reach G2/M phase and remained accumulated on S phase. Taken together, these data suggest that although AMBRA1-deficient cells accumulate cyclin D1 protein and accumulate in S phase, this is not sufficient to increase their cell number. Furthermore, it suggests that upon damage, AMBRA1-KO cells remain accumulated at the S phase DNA damage checkpoint.

AMBRA1 depletion confers sensitivity to cisplatin-induced apoptosis

To understand the consequence of the accumulation of AMBRA1-KO cells on S phase upon cisplatin treatment, we performed apoptosis assays by flow cytometry in cells cultured under serum starvation conditions, with or without cisplatin treatment. In Cal27, HN5 and OSCC43 AMBRA1-KO cells, cisplatin treatment produced a significant increase in early and/or late apoptotic cells (Figures 5E and S4A). The same 48-h treatment on OSC20 CL3 cells induced only a slight increase in the percentage of apoptotic cells after treatment (10% of apoptotic cells, Figure S4A), showing a tendency toward higher apoptosis on AMBRA1-KO cells. Nevertheless, extending the duration of the treatment to 5 days, we could detect late apoptosis that was increased on AMBRA1-KO cells even at lower concentrations of cisplatin (Figure 5E). Regarding the expression of apoptosis-related genes, we detected an upregulation of the pro-apoptotic gene BCL2L11(Figure S4B). Taken together, these data support that AMBRA1 deficiency increases cisplatin-induced apoptosis.

Depletion of AMBRA1 promotes DNA damage, thereby increasing the toxicity of cisplatin treatment

To better understand the molecular mechanisms involved in cisplatin sensitivity induced by AMBRA1 loss, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) in OSC20 CL3 control and AMBRA1-KO cells with or without cisplatin treatment. The global analysis of down- or up-regulated genes (adjusted p value <0.05 and L2FC > ± 0.5) showed differences in gene expression profiles between AMBRA1-KO and control cells (Figures S5A and S5B; Table S2). While sgAMBRA1 untreated cells modified the expression of a small number of genes (Figure S5A, clusters 4 and 5), we observed that depletion of AMBRA1 regulated a greater number of genes after cisplatin treatment in comparison to control cells (757 upregulated genes) (Figure 6A, blue circle). The same was observed in downregulated genes in AMBRA1-KO cells (Figure S5D). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis of the genes commonly upregulated in both control and AMBRA1-KO cells after cisplatin treatment (235 genes) showed an overall alteration in metabolic pathways, highlighting the increase in inflammation and DNA-binding related pathways (Figure S5C). Interestingly, KEGG analysis of the genes that were regulated exclusively or differentially expressed in AMBRA1-KO-treated condition (797 upregulated and 464 downregulated) revealed that AMBRA1 depletion induced the expression of DNA damage/repair and cell cycle-related genes and a downregulation in genes involved cell-matrix interaction, in comparison with sgTomato cells (Figures 6A and S5D). We validated the RNA-seq results by evaluating the gene expression of DNA repair, chromosome instability and cell cycle checkpoint regulator genes, both in OSC20 CL3 and OSCC43 cells. We could confirm a general upward trend in the expression levels of the evaluated genes. Notably, we observed a significantly upregulated expression of XRCC1, XRCC3, RECQL4, RAD54L, LIG1, MAD2L1, AURKB, and CDC25A; with these differences being more apparent after cisplatin treatment in the case of OSC20 CL3 or under basal conditions in OSCC43 cells (Figure 6B). To confirm this phenotype, we also assessed the protein expression of phospho-p53 (p-p53) and γH2AX, two well-known markers of DNA damage (Figure 6C). In line with the gene expression results, the depletion of AMBRA1 in OSC20 CL3 and OSCC43 caused an increase in both phosphorylated and total p53 and γH2AX after cisplatin treatment. Interestingly, OSC20 AMBRA1-KO cells presented higher levels of p-p53 even in basal conditions. Furthermore, we measure the presence of micronuclei, which is a direct measurement of genome instability. AMBRA1-KO cells presented higher number of micronuclei already in basal conditions, and this increase remained constant upon cisplatin treatment (Figure 6D). Interestingly, AMBRA1-KO cells had the same levels of micronuclei as the tomato control cells treated with cisplatin. To extend our analysis to a larger and more clinically relevant cohort, we analyzed the cBioPortal online database, specifically the Head and Neck Squamous Cell Carcinoma database (The Cancer Genome Atlas, TCGA, PanCancer Atlas).17,18,19 In agreement with our in vitro data, this analysis showed that AMBRA1 low samples exhibited a higher proportion of “fraction of genome altered,” “tumor break load,” and “microsatellite instability (MSI) MANTIS score,” three clinical attributes studied as indicators of genome instability (Figures 6E and S5E). The comparison of upregulated genes in AMBRA1 low samples in cBioportal with those highly expressed in the AMBRA1-KO treated condition from our RNA-seq (clusters 1 and 2; Figure S5A), displayed a coincidence of 157 common genes. In accordance with the previous observations, Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of biological processes of these shared genes showed an enrichment of DNA repair and cell cycle-related genes (Figure 6F). Aligned with these findings, cBioportal gene expression analysis revealed a negative correlation between the gene expression of AMBRA1 and the DNA damage genes UBE2T, MAD2L1BP, XRCC3, and RAD54L (Figures 6G and S5F). Depletion of AMBRA1 confers sensitivity to platinum-based therapies, but not to other chemotherapies

Since the loss of AMBRA1 caused cells to be more responsive to cisplatin, we proceeded to evaluate whether it also enhanced the sensitivity to other chemotherapies frequently administered to patients with HNSCC, regardless of whether they had the same mechanism of action. We evaluated the sensitivity of AMBRA1-depleted cells to carboplatin treatment, which, like cisplatin, is a platinum-based chemotherapy that acts as an alkylating agent that binds directly to DNA and forms DNA adducts.20 We also tested 5-fluorouracil, an analog of uracil which inhibits thymidylate synthase activity,20 and two different taxanes, paclitaxel and docetaxel, which are inhibitors of microtubule polymerization.20 Our data indicated that only carboplatin treatment reproduced the sensitivity profile in all cell lines (OSC20 CL3, OSC20-P, Cal27, and HN5) and the primary cell culture OSCC43, especially under serum starvation conditions (Figures 7A and S5G). Either 5-fluorouracil or the taxanes demonstrated major differences between AMBRA1-KO compared to control cells (Figures 7B and S5G). But docetaxel and paclitaxel-treated AMBRA1-KO cells showed a tendency toward sensitivity to both taxanes especially under serum deprivation (Figures 7C, 7D, and S5G). These results suggest that the absence of AMBRA1 may have a minor but not determinant role in chemotherapy sensitivity on non-platinum-based therapies. Discussion

Overcoming therapy resistance is still one of the major obstacles in the treatment of HNSCC. Treatments are becoming increasingly sophisticated, but the lack of consistent predictive biomarkers prevents proper personalization of therapy. In this work, our main objective was the identification of potential functional biomarkers of cisplatin response, as it is broadly used in HNSCC patients, but also has negative effects on the quality of life of the patients. Considering this, the application of a whole-genome CRISPR-Cas9 KO screen offered a good approach to achieving that objective. We transduced an OSCC cell line with a lentiviral library consisting of 58,028 sgRNAs that interfered 19,050 genes and then we evaluated the enrichment or loss of the sgRNAs after cisplatin treatment. Validation experiments of the selected sgRNAs allowed us to identify one gene, AMBRA1, as a possible biomarker, as AMBRA1-depleted cells became more sensitive to cisplatin treatment. Regarding the failure to identify more response genes, we hypothesize that it could be due to a high rate of false positive sgRNAs in the screen analysis—a common challenge in CRISPR screens21—or a deficient KO efficiency of the sgRNA library, as cells are theoretically only transduced with one lentiviral particle by employing a very low MOI. It could also be attributable to the fact that there was no verification that the sgRNAs were working correctly in the validation phase, so we cannot rule out completely that those genes do not actually confer such sensitivity to cisplatin.

There is an evidence of the dual role of AMBRA1 in therapy resistance and tumor progression, as it seems to be context-dependent.22,23 For example, in melanoma, low levels of AMBRA1 promote tumor aggressiveness24 and resistance to mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) inhibitors,25 while in breast and ovarian cancer, the silencing of AMBRA1 sensitizes the cells to the studied treatment (paclitaxel26 and epirubicin27 in breast cancer and cisplatin in ovarian cancer,25 respectively). In the context of HNSCC, Antonioli et al.13 demonstrated that AMBRA1 expression is decreased in HPV-associated tumors compared to HPV-negative tumors and that low AMBRA1 confers cisplatin sensitivity through the impairment of autophagy in oropharyngeal tumors, which is known to be a resistance mechanism to therapy. In our study, we assessed the role of AMBRA1 in autophagy in our models. While we confirmed that AMBRA1-depleted cells were more sensitive to cisplatin under autophagy induction conditions (serum starvation) in three cell lines (OSC20, Cal27, and HN5) and a primary cell culture (OSCC43), it did not seem to be due to autophagy. Our data showed that Bafilomycin A1 treatment did not induce higher levels of the autophagy markers LC3B-II or p62 on AMBRA1-deficient cells compared to control cells, and importantly it did not recapitulate AMBRA1-KO sensitivity profile in control cells. Overall, this suggests that AMBRA1 deficiency can cause sensibilization to cisplatin treatment through an autophagy-independent mechanism. Additionally, it suggests that HNSCC from different locations (oropharyngeal vs. oral SCC), may have different consequences upon AMBRA1 loss.

Since serum starvation conditions affect cell proliferation and AMBRA1 is involved in cell cycle regulation by ubiquitination of D-type cyclins,14,15 we hypothesized that AMBRA1-depleted cells could respond better to cisplatin due to an increased cell proliferation, a well-known factor causing therapy sensitivity. Surprisingly, overall cell growth was not increased or showed inconsistent results between our models, despite a cyclin D1 protein accumulation. More detailed cell cycle analysis indicated that AMBRA1-KO cells accumulate into S phase in basal conditions, and do not overpass the S phase DNA damage checkpoint in response to cisplatin and cannot reach the G2/M phase. This may indicate that the loss of AMBRA1 is increasing S phase, causing a replicative stress, an accumulation of DNA damage and the S phase checkpoint both in basal conditions, which prevents their faster growth, but also upon cisplatin treatment. The S phase checkpoint monitors DNA integrity, and it is triggered when the machinery detects accumulation of damaged DNA, pausing DNA replication. If the damage is severe, it causes prolonged S arrest and predisposes the cells to enter cell death. Indeed, we demonstrated in all four cell lines that the treatment with cisplatin in AMBRA1-KO cells, elicited a higher percentage of apoptotic cells. The fact that OSC20 cell line required a longer treatment to detect apoptosis can be explained by its longer doubling time, although this remains to be explored in detail.

A recently published study concluded that cisplatin resistance is linked predominantly to DNA damage induction more than to alteration on drug uptake-related pathways in HNSCC. In their transcriptomic analysis, there was an increase in DNA damage and cell cycle genes such as H2AFX, TP53, BRCA1, XRCC2, FANCD2, CDKN1A, and RAD18.28 In addition, another study analyzed cisplatin resistance genes in two tongue squamous cell carcinoma cell lines by performing a CRISPR-Cas9 KO screen, using a lentiviral library designed specifically against genes involved in the control of kinases. They identified STK19 as a potential therapeutic marker in HNSCC and observed that STK19 depleted cells promoted DNA damage through O-6-methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase (MGMT) regulation, dysregulating the levels of different DNA repair genes, including XRCC4, RAD51B, and FANCC, and inducing protein expression of γH2AX.29 These results are in line with our RNA-seq analysis, in which we observed an upregulation of genes involved in DNA repair, cell cycle, and chromosome stability in AMBRA1-depleted cells. The validation of the RNA-seq was demonstrated through gene expression analysis of DNA damage-related genes and protein expression of two canonical DNA damage markers, γH2AX and p-p53, and the measurement of micronuclei, a genome instability well-known marker. This result would explain why AMBRA1-KO cells cannot overcome cell-cycle arrest since they accumulate such DNA damage upon cisplatin treatment that cannot be repaired, leading to cell death.

When extrapolating the results using the TCGA database, survival curves showed no differences based on AMBRA1 expression levels (data not shown). This could be due to the pleiotropic and ubiquitous expression of AMBRA1 in normal tissue and tumor microenvironment. However, this analysis corroborated that patient samples with low AMBRA1 expression have higher fraction of genome altered, tumor break load, and MSI. In addition, we observed a negative correlation between DNA damage genes and AMBRA1 expression. Moreover, part of the upregulated genes in AMBRA1-KO-treated cells overlapped with those upregulated in AMBRA1 low samples from TCGA, reinforcing the findings of our study.

Considering all of the above, the results indicate that AMBRA1 depletion, through cyclin D1 upregulation, causes a replicative stress that increases the DNA damage in basal conditions, thus promoting genome instability and compromising the cells’ ability to respond to the genotoxic stress induced by cisplatin in OSCCs.

AMBRA1 has a wide range of molecular functions and there is no specific AMBRA1 inhibitor available, so pharmacological inhibition does not seem feasible, given the actual knowledge. Instead, this study shows that it could be tested as a promising predictive biomarker to platinum-based eligible patients, depending on its expression levels in biopsies analyses, and enabling a certain degree of therapy personalization.

Limitations of the study

Although we clearly demonstrated that AMBRA1 depletion can sensitize OSCC cells to cisplatin, some limitations to our study should be recognized. Firstly, while we could show how AMBRA1 deletion increased cyclin D1 protein, and that caused an increase in S phase, and a replicative stress-associated DNA damage than sensitized cells to cisplatin, we lack the final evidence that cyclin D1 overexpression is sufficient to reproduce the phenotype, although this has been demonstrated in immortalized human fibroblast.16 Secondly, despite we demonstrated the reproducibility of AMBRA1-loss phenotype on multiple cell lines, using 2D and 3D models, our assays were limited to in vitro models, which lack modification of cisplatin biodistribution, and availability and the effects of the tumor microenvironment provided by in vivo models. Lastly, while we demonstrated significant correlations of AMBRA1 expression with DNA-damage markers using the TCGA retrospective cohort, a validation cohort in a retrospective Formalin-Fixed Paraffin-Embedded (FFPE) patient samples collection to strengthen the clinical value of these correlations.

Experimental model and study participant details

Clinical specimen

One fresh human OSCC surgical discard was obtained from a patient at the Hospital Universitario La Paz (Madrid, Spain) under informed consent and approval of the Biobank and Ethical Committees of the hospital according to Spanish ethical regulations (number PI-3999). The study followed the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki, and patient identity and pathological specimens remained anonymous in the context of the study.

Cell lines

Human OSC19 (from male), OSC20 (from female), HSC4 (from male) (JCRB), Cal27 (from male), SCC9 (from male), 293T (ATCC), Cal33 (from male) (DSMZ) and HN5 (from male) (ICR) cell lines were grown in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS Biowest, #S181A), 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco #25030081), 10 mM HEPES (Gibco #15630080) and 1x Penicillin/Streptomycin solution (Gibco #15140122). OSCC43 primary cells were obtained from a male with stage III invasive tongue squamous cell carcinoma HPV negative, using the protocol described in Schober et al.30 with some modifications.31 Briefly, surgical discard was cleaned from healthy tissue, muscle or adipose tissue, before it was minced on Advanced+++ medium (adDMEM/F12 medium (Gibco #12634010) with 1X GlutaMAXTM (Gibco, #35050061), 1X Penicillin/Streptomycin and 10 mM HEPES) with 10 μM or Rock-inhibitor, Y-27632 (Bio-techne, #1254). Tumor fragments were digested with collagenase I (Sigma-Aldrich #C2674), DNAseI (Roche #04536282001) and trypsin (Gibco #25200056) on Advanced+++ medium for an hour at 37°C on an orbital shaker. Digested tissue was filtered through 40 and 70μM cell strainers (Corning #CLS431750 and #CLS431751), and cells were centrifuged at 450 g for 10 min at 4°C. Single cell suspensions were seeded on 3T3-J2 (Kerafast) mitomycin-C (Santa Cruz #sc-3514) treated feeders and grown in E medium (3:1 DMEM:Ham’s F12 (Biowest #L0135), 10% FBS, 1x Penicillin/Streptomycin, 2 mM GlutaMAX, 10 mM HEPES, Apotransferrin 3.75 μg/mL (Sigma-Aldrich #T2252), Cholera toxin 7.5 pM (Sigma-Aldrich #C8052), Insulin-5 3.75 μg/mL (Sigma-Aldrich #I-1882), Hydrocortisone 30 ng/mL (Sigma-Aldrich #H4001) and T3 15 pM (Sigma-Aldrich #709719)), supplemented with fresh Y-27632 and Epidermal Growth Factor (Sigma-Merck #E9644) at a final concentration of 10 μM and 40 ng/mL, respectively. Primary cells were kept with feeders that were replaced every week for fresh 3T3-J2 MitC treated feeders. For organoid experiments, feeders were removed using washes with PBS1X before OSCC43 cells were detached with trypsin 0.25%. All cell lines were tested regularly for mycoplasma and authenticated by STR profiling.

Method details

Lentiviral production for Cas9 stable cell line and CRISPR/Cas9 knockouts generation

293T cells (ATCC) were seeded in 6-well plates at a 70-80% confluency and VSV-G pseudotyped lentivirus were produced by PEI transfection (Polysciences #24765) with helper plasmids pMD2-VSVg and pPAX2 (0.5 and 1 μg, Addgene plasmids #12259 and #12260, respectively) and the plasmid of interest (2μg), all previously amplified in NEB® Stable Competent E. coli (High Efficiency) cells (New England Biolabs #C3040). The latter consisted of lentiCas9-Blast (Addgene #52962), LV-H2B-RFP (Addgene #26001) or pLKO sgRNA-carrying vectors (cloned in lentiGuide-Puro (Addgene #52963)). 293T cells were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 2 mM L-glutamine, 10 mM HEPES and 1x Penicillin/Streptomycin solution. Viral supernatant was collected 48 and 72 h after transfection and filtered through 0.45-μm polyvinylidene difluoride filters. For the lentiviral infections, 1.5-2.5 × 105 cells/well were plated in 6-well plates and incubated with a 1:2 dilution of viral supernatant containing 8 μg/mL polybrene (Sigma-Aldrich #H9268). Forty-eight hours after infection, cells were selected with blasticidin (5 μg/ml, Sigma-Aldrich #15205, lentiCas9-Blast) or puromycin (1-1.5 μg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich #540411, pLKO sgRNA-carrying) for 72 h. To generate Cas9 expressing stable HNSCC cell lines, single-cell clones were cultured by limited dilution, and positive clones were validated by Western blot analysis with anti-FLAG-Cas9 antibody. The clone with the highest Cas9 expression was selected for further experiments. The effectiveness of Cas9 editing was tested by comparing the RFP expression of H2B-RFP+ parental OSC20 cell line (OSC20-P) and OSC20 clone 3 (OSC20 CL3) before and after sgTomato transduction. Briefly, cells were consecutively transduced with H2B-RFP and sgTomato-containing lentivirus, and RFP expression was quantified using fluorescent imaging (Leica CTR 6000 microscope) or flow cytometry (BD FACS Celesta cytometer).

Lentiviral library production and titer

Amplification of Human GeCKOv2 CRISPR knockout pooled library B (Addgene #1000000049), as well as the following steps, were performed as previously described.10 After the amplification of the library in XL10-Gold Ultracompetent Cells (Agilent #200315), 293T cells were seeded at 70-80% confluency in 100 mm dishes and VSV-G pseudotyped lentivirus were produced by PEI transfection with Library B of Human GeCKO v2 Library (6.18 μg, Addgene #1000000049) and helper plasmids pMD2-VSVg and pPAX2 (1.54 and 3.09 μg, Addgene plasmids #12259 and #12260, respectively). 293T cells were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 2 mM L-glutamine, 10 mM HEPES and 1x Penicillin/Streptomycin solution. Viral supernatant was collected 48 hours after transfection and filtered through 0.45-μm polyvinylidene difluoride filters (Pall #4614). Multiplicity of infection (MOI) was calculated by transducing OSC20 CL3 with serial dilutions of the lentiviral library in 6-well plates and then calculating the percentage of surviving cells after puromycin treatment (1 μg/mL) in 96-well plates.

Genome-scale CRISPR-Cas9 knockout screen in OSC20 clone 3

The pooled genome-scale CRISPR-Cas9 knockout screening was carried out according to the guidelines of Joung et al.32 In summary, Cas9-expressing OSC20 CL3 cells were seeded to obtain a 500x coverage of the library and transduced with the lentiviral library at a MOI <0.3. Forty-eight hours after infection, puromycin-resistant cells were selected with 1 μg/mL puromycin for five days. Then, cells were split into 3 conditions at 165x coverage each: group 1 that was harvested at day 0; group 2 was treated with cisplatin for 7 days at a 90% lethal concentration (IC80, 50 μM), and group 3, control group, was kept untreated and collected at day 7. The calculation of the IC80 was performed by counting the viable cells in 100 mm dishes after 7 days of cisplatin exposure at different concentration (Figure S1F). Subsequently, genomic DNA was extracted using the Quick-DNA Midiprep Plus Kit (Zymo #D4075) and the DNA containing the sgRNAs was amplified by PCRs of all genomic DNA using the KAPA HiFi Hotstart PCR Kit (Roche #07958889001). After PCR cleanup and DNA size selection with AMPure beads (Beckman Coulter #A63882), libraries were sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq 6000. Sequencing results were analyzed using MAGeCKFlute following the recommendations and applying RRA analysis.33 The experiment was performed in technical triplicates.

Design of CRISPR/Cas9 knockouts in OSCC

Resistance/Sensitivity genes candidates were targeted with two independent sgRNAs guided preferably to their first exon. The most efficient guide RNAs were predicted using the CRISPR design tool from Benchling. sgRNA against dTomato was used as a control. sgRNA sequences (pLKO sgRNA-carrying vectors) were cloned into lentiGuide-Puro vector (Addgene #52963). sgRNA sequences are listed in Table S1.

Growth curve

For growth curves cells were seeded in 24-well plate (5000 cells per well), fixed with 1% glutaraldehyde (Merck #1.12179.0025) every other day for a total of 8-10 days and measured by crystal violet (Sigma-Aldrich #C6158) staining and reported as p < 0.05 calculated by Student’s t test. The media of the non-fixed cells was refreshed regularly.

Organoid/tumoroid 3D culture

50.000 cells of control or CRISPR engineered cells were resuspended in 70% BME (Bio-techne #3432) and 30% Advanced Organoid Medium (97% adDMEM/F12 medium, 1x GlutaMAX, 1x Penicillin/Streptomycin and 10 mM HEPES), plated in ultralow adherence plates (Greiner Bio-One #662970) in 50 μl drops,31 and dried upside down until the BME had solidified. Organoids were covered and grown in OncoProTM Medium (Gibco #A5701201), supplemented with FGF10 (Gibco #PHG0372). Media was replaced every other day; organoids were grown for up to 8 days. Bright field 4x images were taken every day (Leica CTR 6000) to quantify the number and size of organoids using a size pixel-based classification algorithm within Ilastik v.1.4.0 software34 and further processed for size measurement using FIJI ImageJ software.

3D cisplatin sensitivity assay

10,000 control or CRISPR engineered cells were seeded in triplicate as 10 μl drops under the 3D culture conditions described above in ultralow-attachment 96-well plates (Greiner Bio-One #662970) and cultured in OncoProTM Medium for 3 days prior to treatment with cisplatin at two concentrations (5 and 10 μM or 1 and 5 μM, depending on the organoid line). After 5 days of treatment, bright-field 10x images were acquired using a Leica CTR 6000 microscope, and viability was assessed using the CellTiter-Glo® assay (Promega #G9682) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Viability of engineered cells at each cisplatin dose was normalized to control cells.

Concentration-response curves and IC50 treatment

5.000 or 10.000 cells/well were seeded in 96-well plates and treated with serial dilutions of cisplatin (0-25, 0-200 or 0-800 μM, Accord 100 mg), carboplatin (0-600 μM, Accord 450 mg), 5-fluorouracil (0-400 μM, Accord 5 g), docetaxel (0-1.5 μM, Accord 160 mg) or paclitaxel (0-3 μM, Fresenius Kabi 300 mg), in standard cell culture conditions (10% FBS) or serum starvation conditions (DMEM with 0.5% FBS, 2 mM L-glutamine, 10 mM HEPES and 1x Penicillin/Streptomycin solution). When indicated, the cells were additionally treated with a constant concentration of bafilomycin A1 (10 nM, MedChemExpress #HY-100558). After 2 or 7 days of treatment, viable and metabolically active cells were assessed by MTS assay (Promega #G5430) following manufacturer's instructions, or adhered cells were measured by fixation with 1% glutaraldehyde and subsequent crystal violet staining, as indicated. Absorbance was measured in Agilent BioTek Epoch Microplate Spectrophotometer in the case of the MTS assay or in TECAN Nanoquant Infinite 200 Pro microplate reader for crystal violet staining.

The 50% lethal concentration (IC50) of cisplatin, carboplatin, 5-fluorouracil, docetaxel and paclitaxel for each cell culture was estimated based on previous results from concentration-response curves. 5.000 cells per well were seeded in 96-well plates and left untreated or treated at the IC50 of each chemotherapy in standard cell culture conditions (10% FBS) or serum starvation conditions (0.5% FBS). These experiments were quantified by MTS assay in the same way as dose-response curves.

Apoptosis assay

3 × 105 cells/well were plated on 6-well plates and left untreated or treated with 10, 20 or 50 μM of cisplatin, as indicated, under serum starvation conditions. After 48h of treatment, supernatant containing apoptotic cells was collected, and combined with the adherent cells detached using trypsin, cells were pelleted and washed. Cells were then incubated with Annexin V-FITC and 7-AAD (Miltenyi #130-092-052 and #3130-111-568) for 15 min at RT protected from light. Cells were analyzed in a BD FACS Celesta and data was analyzed in FlowJo software.

Cell cycle assay

3 × 105 cells/well were plated on 6-well plates and left untreated or treated with 10 μM cisplatin for 24h. After this period, the cell cycle was assessed using the Click-iT™ Plus EdU Flow Cytometry Assay Kit (Invitrogen #C10632) following the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, cells were incubated with 10 μM EdU Alexa Fluor™ 488 for 1h at 37°C. Then, cells were trypsinized, fixed, and permeabilized in accordance with the manufacturer's guidelines. DNA content was assessed by staining fixed cells with 2 μg/mL Hoechst 33342 (Invitrogen #H3570). Cells were analyzed in a BD FACS Celesta and data was analyzed in FlowJo software.

Immuno-fluorescence and imaging

1.5 × 105 cells/well were plated on round coverslips in 6-well plates and left untreated under serum starvation conditions for 6 hours, treated with cisplatin (50 μM) under serum starvation conditions for 24 hours or treated with bafilomycin A1 (100 nM) for 2 hours at a previous exposure to serum deprivation conditions of 4 hours. Then cells were fixed for 10 min with 4% formaldehyde (Thermo Scientific #28908), rinsed with PBS, and blocked for 1 hour (5% normal donkey serum (Jackson Immunoresearch # 017-000-121), 1% BSA (NZYTech #MB04602), 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS (PanReac Applichem #A1388)) and incubated with the primary antibody p62 (1:250; Epitomics #3340) diluted in blocking buffer at 4 °C overnight. After washing with PBS, secondary antibodies, conjugated to RRX (Jackson Immunoresearch #711-295-152) and DAPI (Invitrogen #D1306) were diluted in blocking buffer and incubated with the slides for 1 h at room temperature. After washing, coverslips were mounted in ProLong Diamond (Invitrogen #P36970). Imaging was performed using a Leica Stellaris 8 Spectral Confocal Microscope. Images have been analyzed in FIJI ImageJ software using a macro developed by Zou et al.,35 which allowed the measure of the number of p62 puncta per cell. To assess the presence of micronucleated cells, only DAPI images were analyzed, and the percentage of micronucleated cells per field was calculated.

Western blotting

Protein lysates were prepared using RIPA buffer (150 mM sodium chloride (Supelco #1.06404.1000), 0.1% Triton-X100, 0.5% SDS (Sigma-Merck #8.17.034) and 50 mM Tris-HCl (Millipore #1.08382) pH 8 in ddH2O (Invitrogen #10977035)). To analyze LC3B expression, cells were lysed in a lysis buffer composed of 50 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 0.1% Triton X-100, 1 mM EDTA (Sigma-Aldrich #0000431591), 1 mM EGTA and 0.1% (v/v) fresh β-mercaptoethanol (Sigma-Merck #M3148). Both buffers were supplemented with complete Mini EDTA-free protease and phosphatase inhibitors tablets (Roche #04693159001 and #04906837001). Protein concentration was determined using the Bradford protein Kit (Bio-Rad #5000001) following the manufacturer’s instructions. 20-30 μg of protein were prepared in Laemmli buffer (5x: 6% SDS, 15% β-mercaptoethanol, 30% glycerol (PanReac Applichem #141339), 0.006% bromophenol blue (Merck #108122), 0.188 M Tris–HCl and boiled for 10 min at 95 °C (or 5 min at 90 °C when analyzing LC3B) and loaded with a protein ladder (EZ-Run, Fisher Scientific #10638393; or Precision Plus Protein™ WesternC™, Bio-Rad #1610376). Gel electrophoresis was performed using a 8, 10 or 12% Bis–Tris-gel run for 150-180 min at 110 V, gel was transferred for 60-90 min at 4 °C at 100 V to a 0.45 μm nitrocellulose (Cytiva #15239794) or polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF Millipore #IPVH00010) membrane and transfer was assessed by Ponceau S staining (0.1%(w/v) Ponceau S (Sigma #P6170) in 5% (v/v) acetic acid). Membranes were sectioned in pieces to incubate them simultaneously with different antibodies, then were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk (Thermo Scientific #16694685) in Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween® 20 (Sigma-Aldrich #P1379) detergent (TBST), then incubated with primary antibodies diluted in blocking overnight at 4 °C with gentle agitation. Membranes were rinsed with TBST before incubating with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer for 1 hour at RT. Membranes were washed with TBST before incubating them with SuperSignal™ West Pico (Life Technologies #34080) and exposed in a UVITEC Chemidoc. Antibodies used for western blotting were AMBRA1 (1:1000; EMD Millipore #ABC131), Vinculin (1:10000; Santa Cruz #sc-73614), Cyclin D1 (1:2000; Invitrogen #MA5-14512), LC3B (1:1000; Cell Signaling #3868), FLAG (1:5000; Sigma #F3165), phospho-p53 (1:1000; Cell Signaling #9284), p53 (1:1000; Cell Signaling #9282), yH2AX (1:1000, Cell Signaling #2577), Tubulin (1:5000; Santa Cruz #sc-5274), HRP goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:10000; Invitrogen #A27036), HRP anti-mouse IgGκ (1:2000; Santa Cruz #sc-516102). Intensity was quantified using FIJI in at least 3 independent experiments.

Quantitative reverse-transcription PCR

mRNA was isolated from 3-5 × 105 cells using Direct-zol RNA Mini Prep Kits (Zymo Research #R2052). Samples were quantified using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). Complementary DNA was synthesized from 1-1.5 μg of total RNA using NZY First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit with random primers (NZYTech #MB12502). qRT–PCR was performed with KAPA SYBR® FAST (Kapa #KK4602) on a QuantStudio3 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Measurements were recorded in duplicate. Differences between samples and controls were calculated based on the 2−ΔΔCT method and normalized to RPLP0 or GAPDH. Primer sequences are listed on Table S1.

RNA-seq library preparation

Total RNA was extracted from 500.000 control or CRISPR engineered cells using the Direct-zol RNA Mini Prep Kits (Zymo Research #R2052) according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA quality was defined using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer before we prepared Poly(A) + selected, multiplexed, paired end libraries with the Illumina TruSeq RNA preparation kit. Multiplexed libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSep6000 Genome Analyzer using the 150-base pair paired end read method.

RNA-seq analysis

Human hg38 assembly version was used for the RNA-sequencing alignment, transcriptome quantification and differential expression analysis. More specifically, HISAT2 was used for aligning sequenced reads. Transcriptome quantification and differential expression analysis was performed using the HTseq protocol36 and DESeq2.37 Differential gene expression analyses were performed between sgAMBRA1-2 and sgTomato cisplatin-treated or untreated cells in two independent KO experiments, and are listed in Table S2. Venn diagrams were generated with Molbiotools Multiple List Comparator (https://molbiotools.com/listcompare.php). Gene ontology analyses were performed with ShinyGO v0.85. Heatmaps were done using Morpheus (https://software.broadinstitute.org/morpheus).

TCGA data

TCGA data from the Head and Neck Squamous Cell Carcinoma database (TCGA, PanCancer Atlas17,18,19) were analyzed using the cBioPortal website. mRNA levels and co-expression were analyzed considering the mRNA expression z-scores relative to all samples (log RNA Seq V2 RSEM) and mRNA expression of RSEM (Batch normalized from Illumina HiSeq_RNASeqV2). AMBRA1 high and low expressing samples were selected setting the value of mRNA AMBRA1 expression levels to >0.2 for AMBRA1 high and <-0.2 for AMBRA1 low samples.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis

All experiments were carried out single blinded. All sgRNA-mediated knockdown experiments in vitro were repeated at least three independent times with biological replicates. All quantitative data were collected from experiments performed at least in duplicate, and expressed as mean± SD or SEM, as indicated. Differences between groups were assayed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test, or two-way ANOVA test using Prism 10 (GraphPad Software). Box-and-whisker and violin plots are used to describe the entire population without assumptions about the statistical distribution. Significant differences were considered when p<0.05. Western blots and brightfield or immunofluorescence images were quantified using FIJI. All other graphs were prepared in Prism 10. Figures were assembled using Adobe Photoshop and Illustrator 2024. Additionally, two illustrations from NIAID NIH BioArt Source (bioart.niaid.nih.gov/bioart/386 and bioart.niaid.nih.gov/bioart/129; Next Gen Sequencer and DNA Brush, respectively) were used for the schematic representation of the whole-genome CRISPR/Cas9 knockout screen (Figure 1A).
