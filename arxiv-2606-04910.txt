OLLIVIER–RICCI CURVATURE FOR CAUSAL SETS
Joe Barton, Samuël Borza, Jona Röhrig

Abstract
We introduce a novel notion of Ollivier–Ricci curvature for causal sets using Lorentzian optimal transport. The construction is motivated by a new Lorentzian asymptotic formula of independent interest, which recovers timelike Ricci curvature, up to higher-order terms, from the transport distance between probability measures on nearby causal diamonds. Passing to the discrete setting, this leads to a mesoscopic notion of Ricci curvature defined along maximal chains and built from probability measures on causal diamonds. We study several variants, including idle and Lin–Lu–Yau type curvatures, prove local-to-global propagation results and timelike Bonnet–Myers theorems, and compute the curvature for a range of explicit causal sets. We design high-density Poisson sprinkling numerical experiments recovering the expected constant-curvature signatures of Minkowski, de Sitter, and anti-de Sitter space. These results provide evidence that the construction captures timelike Ricci curvature from order-theoretic data.

Introduction

Causal set theory is an approach to quantum gravity in which spacetime is assumed to be discrete. The universe is modelled as a set of spacetime events, equipped with a partial order that represents the causal relation between them, such that the number of events in any causal diamond is finite. The idea that causality is central to spacetime geometry has a long history and arguably appears already in the work of Weyl and Lorentz. While the first axiomatic framework for causal spaces was given by Kronheimer and Penrose
[
16
]
, causal set theory, as understood nowadays in the literature and as followed in this work, was introduced by Bombelli, Lee, Meyer and Sorkin in their foundational paper
[
6
]
.

The programme of causal sets is often motivated by the Hawking–King–McCarthy–Malament theorem
[
22
,
35
,
14
]
, which states that if there is a bijective map between two past- and future-distinguishing spacetimes that preserves their causal structure, then the map is a conformal isomorphism. The essence of Lorentzian geometry can therefore be viewed as the conjunction of its causal structure and its volume element. Adapted to the discrete setting, this philosophy leads to the now famous “slogan of causal set theory”

Order + Number
\cong
Spacetime Geometry.

Here, “order” represents the partial order relation of a causal set, while “number” indicates that the volume of a region of a discrete spacetime is obtained simply by counting the number of elements it contains.

Over the past decades, there have been numerous results in causal set theory, and it is difficult to do justice to it in just a few lines. One striking achievement is Sorkin’s order-of-magnitude prediction of the cosmological constant
[
29
]
, in agreement with current experimental data. Another important theoretical development is the construction in
[
21
]
of a topology on causal sets using thickened antichains, together with the result that one can recover the homology of a globally hyperbolic spacetime from a faithfully embedded causal set at sufficiently high sprinkling density. For a recent overview of causal set theory, including an extensive bibliography and historical notes, we refer the interested reader to
[
33
]
.

Causal set theory is not without open problems. The
Hauptvermutung
conjectures that one causal set cannot be a Poisson point process approximation of two macroscopically different spacetimes with high probability.
Furthermore, quantum mechanics will have to be reformulated
[
30
]
, and gravity adapted in terms of a discrete structure.

It is this last point that highlights the need for a discrete notion of curvature in order to formulate the causal set analogue of Einstein’s equation. In
[
3
,
13
]
, the authors introduce a family of retarded, Lorentz-invariant, nonlocal d’Alembertian operators on scalar fields over causal sets. For causal sets approximated by curved spacetime, these operators approximate
\square-\tfrac{1}{2}R
, where
R
is the Ricci scalar. This provides a way to define a causal set analogue of scalar curvature.

The present work develops a new Ricci theory for causal sets, motivated by the development of curvature via optimal transport in both metric measure spaces and Lorentzian non-smooth structure, and by the incredible success that Ollivier’s notion of Ricci curvature has had on combinatorial graphs.

Since the seminal works
[
31
,
32
,
20
]
, optimal transport has become a powerful tool for studying curvature on non-smooth spaces of “positive signature”. More recently, a growing body of work has sought to adapt these ideas to non-smooth Lorentzian geometry
[
17
,
24
,
23
,
25
,
10
,
4
]
. In this context, it is natural to attempt to define a new notion of curvature for causal sets using methods from optimal transport.

This approach is also supported by nearly two decades of active research on optimal-transport-based curvature on combinatorial graphs. In the foundational work
[
27
]
, Ollivier introduced the notion of curvature that provides the inspiration for this work. Given two points on a graph, Ollivier curvature compares the original distance between the two points with the optimal transport distance between the uniform measures on small balls around them. Positive curvature means that the optimal transport distance between the balls is smaller than the distance between their centres; negative curvature means that it is larger. Ollivier–Ricci curvature has been successful in turning the classical idea of Ricci curvature into a flexible, transport-based notion that works naturally on discrete metric spaces
[
18
,
15
,
26
,
7
,
11
]
. The original motivation behind Ollivier’s definition of discrete curvature comes from a result in Riemannian geometry
[
27
, Example 7, Section 8]
, which we reinterpret in the Lorentzian setting by replacing metric balls with causal diamonds, see

and

for variants involving non-adjacent vertices. This is again unsuitable in the causal set setting, where the geodesic

\gamma
is too short, the transport distance is dominated by discreteness effects rather than by the large-scale geometry one aims to approximate. In some sense, the causal set curvature defined here is inherently

2.1
Lorentzian geometry

Here is a review of some basic facts about Lorentzian geometry, and we refer the reader to the classic text
[
28
]
for further details.

We denote by
(M,g)
a Lorentzian manifold of dimension
n
, with signature convention
(-+\cdots+)
, admitting a global time-orientation
T
. For any
x\in M
, we say that a tangent vector
v\in\T_{x}(M)
is

\begin{cases}&\text{causal}\\
&\text{timelike}\\
&\text{null}\\
&\text{spacelike}\end{cases}\quad\text{ if }\quad g_{x}(v,v)\quad\begin{cases}&\leq 0\text{ and }v\neq 0\\
&<0\\
&=0\text{ and }v\neq 0\\
&>0\text{ or }v=0\end{cases}

and is
future-directed
if
g_{x}(v,T(x))<0
. A unit future-directed timelike vector satisfies
g_{x}(v,v)=-1
. We shall also use the notation
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{x}\coloneqq g_{x}(\cdot,\cdot)
, and may omit the subscript
x
when it is clear from the context.

A smooth curve
\gamma
is said to be
causal
(resp.
timelike
,
null
,
future-directed
) if its tangent vector
\dot{\gamma}(t)
is
causal
(resp.
timelike
,
null
,
future-directed
) for all
t
. A point
x\in M

causally precedes

y\in M
, a relation that we denote by
x\leq y
, if
x=y
or if there exists a future-directed causal curve
\gamma
joining
x
to
y
. Analogously, we say that
x

chronologically precedes

y
, denoted by
x\ll y
, if there exists a future-directed timelike curve joining
x
to
y
. The
causal
and the
chronological future
of
A\subseteq M
are defined, respectively, as

J^{+}(A)\coloneqq\{y\in M\mid\exists x\in A,\ x\leq y\},\ \text{ and }\ I^{+}(A)\coloneqq\{y\in M\mid\exists x\in A,\ x\ll y\}.

Similarly, one can define the causal and chronological past
J^{-}(A)
and
I^{-}(A)
. The
causal
and
chronological diamonds
of two sets
A,B\subseteq M
are given by

J(A,B)\coloneqq J^{+}(A)\cap J^{-}(B),\ \text{ and }\ I(A,B)\coloneqq I^{+}(A)\cap I^{-}(B).

The
Lorentzian length
of a future-directed causal curve
\gamma:I\to M
is given by

L(\gamma)\coloneqq\int_{I}\sqrt{-g(\dot{\gamma}(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

The
time-separation function

\uptau
between two points
x,y\in M
is defined as

\uptau(x,y)\coloneqq\sup\{L(\gamma)\ |\ \gamma\ \text{is future-oriented causal, joining $x$ to $y$}\}\quad\text{ if }x\leq y,

and we set
\uptau(x,y)\coloneqq 0
if
x
does not causally precede
y
. The time-separation
\uptau
satisfies the
reverse triangular inequality
, i.e.

\uptau(x,z)\geq\uptau(x,y)+\uptau(y,z)\qquad\text{ whenever }x\leq y\leq z.

(3)

At times, we will also equip
\T_{x}(M)
with the Minkowski metric
g_{x}
and denote the corresponding mathematical objects in this causal structure with a tilde, for instance
\tilde{J}^{+}
,
\tilde{J}^{-}
, etc.

The
exponential map
at
x\in M
is defined by
\mathrm{exp}_{x}(v)\coloneqq\gamma_{v}(1)
where
\gamma_{v}
is the unique geodesic with initial conditions
\gamma_{v}(0)=x
and
\dot{\gamma}_{v}(0)=v\in\T_{x}(M)
. A geodesic
\gamma
is a curve such that
\nabla_{\dot{\gamma}}\dot{\gamma}=0
, where
\nabla
denotes the Levi-Civita connection of the Lorentzian metric
g
.

A
convex normal neighbourhood
of a point
x
is an open neighbourhood
U
of
x
such that there exists a unique geodesic segment contained entirely in
U
joining any two points in
U
, and the exponential map is a diffeomorphism from a star-shaped neighbourhood of
0\in\T_{x}(M)
onto
U
. Every point
x\in M
has a convex normal neighbourhood. The energy between
x
and
y
in a convex normal neighbourhood is defined as

\mathrm{E}(x,y)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\langle\dot{\gamma}(t),\dot{\gamma}(t)\rangle\mathrm{d}t,

where
\gamma
the unique geodesic from
x
to
y
. Thus
\mathrm{E}(x,y)
is negative, zero, or positive according as this geodesic is timelike, null, or spacelike. If
x\leq y
and

, then

\uptau(x,y)^{2}=-2\mathrm{E}(x,y).

The
Riemann curvature tensor
is defined by

R(X,Y)Z\coloneqq\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}Z-\nabla_{Y}\nabla_{X}Z-\nabla_{[X,Y]}Z,

and satisfies

R(X,Y)=-R(Y,X),\qquad\langle R(X,Y)Z,W\rangle=-\langle R(X,Y)W,Z\rangle,

\langle R(X,Y)Z,W\rangle=\langle R(Z,W)X,Y\rangle.

The
Ricci tensor
is the contraction

\mathrm{Ric}(X,Y)\coloneqq\mathrm{tr}\bigl(Z\mapsto R(Z,X)Y\bigr)=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\langle R(v,e_{i})e_{i},v\rangle

if
e_{0}=v,e_{1},\dots,e_{n-1}
is a Lorentz-orthonormal frame with
g(v,v)=-1
.

Normal coordinates
centred at
x
are obtained by writing each

uniquely as

y=\mathrm{exp}_{x}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u^{0}v+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}u^{i}e_{i}}}\right).

The tuple
(u^{0},u^{1},\dots,u^{n-1})
gives the normal coordinates of

centred at
x
. In these coordinates, one has that

g_{\alpha\beta}(x)=\eta_{\alpha\beta},\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\sqrt{|\det g_{ij}(w)|}=1-\frac{1}{6}\sum_{i,j=0}^{n-1}\operatorname{Ric}_{ij}(x)w^{i}w^{j}+O(|w|^{3}),

where
\eta=\operatorname{diag}(-1,1,\dots,1)
, and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\cdot}}\right\rvert
is any norm on
\T_{x}(M)
.

The
parallel transport
along a curve
c
will be denoted by
\mathord{\parallel_{s}^{t}}(c):\T_{c(s)}(M)\to\T_{c(t)}(M)
. We recall that it is an isometry and that it sends
v\in\T_{c(s)}(M)
to
V(t)
, where
V
is the unique vector field along
c
satisfying

\nabla_{\dot{c}}V=0,\qquad V(s)=v.

If
\gamma
is a geodesic with initial velocity
v\in\T_{x}(M)
, then
\dot{\gamma}(t)=\mathord{\parallel_{0}^{t}}(\gamma)[v]
.
We also denote by
D_{t}V\coloneqq(c^{*}\nabla)_{\partial_{t}}V
the covariant derivative of
V
along
c
, with
c^{*}\nabla
being the pullback connection on
c^{*}\T(M)
.
If
c
is a smooth curve with
c(0)=x
and
u,v,w,z\in\T_{c(s)}(M)
, then, as
t\to s
, we have

\bigl\langle R_{c(s)}(u,v)w,z\bigr\rangle_{c(s)}=\bigl\langle R_{c(t)}\bigl(\mathord{\parallel_{s}^{t}}(c)u,\mathord{\parallel_{s}^{t}}(c)v\bigr)\mathord{\parallel_{s}^{t}}(c)w,\mathord{\parallel_{s}^{t}}(c)z\bigr\rangle_{c(t)}+O(|t-s|).

(4)

Let
\Gamma:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\times[0,1]\to M
be a smooth two-parameter family of curves, and let
V
be a smooth vector field along
\Gamma
. Set
S\coloneqq\partial\Gamma/\partial s
and
T\coloneqq\partial\Gamma/\partial t
, then the covariant derivatives satisfy the commutator identity

D_{s}D_{t}V-D_{t}D_{s}V=R(S,T)V.

(5)

Suppose now that
\Gamma
is a geodesic variation of
\gamma
, meaning that
\Gamma(0,t)=\gamma(t)
and, for each fixed
s
, the curve
t\mapsto\Gamma(s,t)
is a geodesic. If

J(t)\coloneqq S(0,t)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\frac{\partial\Gamma}{\partial s}}}\right|_{s=0}(t)

is the associated variational field, then

satisfies the Jacobi equation

D_{t}^{2}J+R(J,\dot{\gamma})\dot{\gamma}=0.

(6)

2.2
Causal set theory

We give a brief account of causal set theory, focusing on some mathematical features of the theory rather than its connection to physics. The textbook
[
33
]
provides a good introduction to causal set theory. We start with a definition of causal set, which is basically a locally finite partially ordered set.

Definition 2.1
.

A
causal set
is a pair
(C,\leq)
, where
C
is a set and
\leq
is a relation on
C
satisfying:

(i)

Reflexivity
: for all
x\in C
,
x\leq x
;

(ii)

Antisymmetry
: for all
x,y\in C
, if
x\leq y
and
y\leq x
, then
x=y
;

(iii)

Transitivity
: for all
x,y,z\in C
, if
x\leq y
and
y\leq z
, then
x\leq z
;

(iv)

Local finiteness
: for all
x,z\in C
, the causal diamond

J(x,y)\coloneqq\{y\in C:x\leq y\leq z\}

is finite.

We call the relation
\leq
the causal relation, while the chronological relation
\ll
is defined by
x\ll y
if
x\leq y
and
x\neq y
. One could equivalently define a causal set as a pair
(C,\ll)
, with
\ll
irreflexive and transitive, rather than as a pair
(C,\leq)
, with
\leq
reflexive and antisymmetric. This convention is used in many publications on causal set theory. The causal relation of a globally hyperbolic Lorentzian manifold, as introduced in the previous section, satisfies the first three conditions. It is the local finiteness condition that introduces spacetime discreteness.

The (counting) time-separation distance is defined as

\uptau(x,y)\coloneqq\max\{m\mid x=x_{0}\ll x_{1}\ll\cdots\ll x_{m}=y\},

whenever
x\leq y
, and
\uptau(x,y)=0
otherwise. The function
\uptau
behaves much like the time-separation of a Lorentzian manifold, and in particular the reverse triangle inequality
Equation

3
holds. A curve
\gamma=(x_{0},\ldots,x_{m})
in
C
with
x=x_{0}\ll x_{1}\ll\cdots\ll x_{m}=y
, commonly referred to as a
chain
, is a
geodesic
from
x
to
y
if it realises the time distance between
x
and
y
, that is, if
m=\uptau(x,y)
. The timelike diameter of the causal set is then

\mathrm{diam}_{\uptau}(\mathcal{C})\coloneqq\sup\{\uptau(x,y)\mid x,y\in\mathcal{C},\ x\leq y\}.

We note the following simple but useful fact: if
r
is fixed and
\mathrm{diam}_{\uptau}(\mathcal{C})\geq 2r
, then

\mathrm{diam}_{\uptau}(\mathcal{C})=\sup\{\uptau(x_{0},x_{n+r})\mid\gamma=(x_{0},\dots,x_{n+r})\text{ is a geodesic, }n\geq r\}.

(7)

Remark 2.2
.

Section

2.2
could be generalised to a
weighted causal set
, i.e.
(C,\leq,w)
, where
(C,\leq)
is a causal set and

w\colon\mathcal{L}(C)\to(0,\infty)

is a weight function defined on the set
\mathcal{L}(C)
of pairs
(x,y)\in C\times C
such that
x\ll y
and there is no
z\in C
with
x\ll z\ll y
. The definition of the time separation would have to be adapted accordingly. For the sake of simplicity, we restrict the discussion in this paper to unweighted causal sets, except for a few brief remarks.

One of the most natural ways to produce a causal set is by performing a Poisson sprinkling into a Lorentzian manifold
(M,g)
. We denote by
(M,\mathcal{B}(M))
the Borel measurable space underlying the spacetime and we consider
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
a probability space. We equip
\mathcal{N}(M)
, the space of counting measures on
M
, with the smallest
\sigma
-algebra making evaluation maps

\mathcal{N}(M)\to\mathopen{[}0,+\infty\mathclose{]}:\mu\mapsto\mu(A)

measurable for all
A\in\mathcal{B}(M)
. A
point process
on
M
is a measurable map

N:(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})\longrightarrow\mathcal{N}(M).

Equivalently, for every measurable set
A\in\mathcal{B}(M)
, the map

N(A):\Omega\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}:\omega\mapsto N(\omega)[A]

is a random variable. A point process
N
is called
simple
if

\mathbb{P}\Big(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\omega\mid\forall x\in M,N(\omega)(\{x\})\leq 1}}\right\}\Big)=1,

which means that, with probability one, no point of
M
occurs with multiplicity. In this case,
N
can be identified with the random locally finite subset of
M

C:\Omega\to\mathcal{P}(M)^{<\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}:\omega\mapsto C(\omega)\coloneqq\{x\in M:N(\omega)(\{x\})=1\},

where we have denoted
\mathcal{P}(M)^{<\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}
for the set of locally finite subsets of
M
. The space
\mathcal{P}(M)^{<\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}
is equipped with the counting
\sigma
-algebra, i.e. the smallest
\sigma
-algebra such that for all
A\in\mathcal{B}(M)
, the map
\mathcal{P}(M)^{<\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}:C\mapsto\#(C\cap A)
is measurable. Thus, for every

A\in\mathcal{B}(M)
,

N(\omega)(A)=\#(A\cap C(\omega)).

(8)

Conversely, any random locally finite subset
C
defines a simple locally finite point process
N
by taking
Equation

8
as the definition of its counting measure. Thus simple locally finite point processes and random locally finite subsets are identified via
Equation

8
, up to modification
on a
\mathbb{P}
-null set.

An
sprinkling density
is a Borel-measurable function

\rho:M\to[0,\infty)
such that
\rho\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(M,\mathrm{vol}_{g})
. It defines the
density measure

\Lambda:\mathcal{B}(M)\to[0,\infty],\qquad\Lambda(A)\coloneqq\int_{A}\rho\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{g}.

If
\rho
is constant, then
\Lambda(A)=\rho\,\mathrm{vol}_{g}(A)
, and for pairwise disjoint measurable sets
A_{1},\dots,A_{k}
, it holds that

\Lambda(\cup_{i=1}^{k}A_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Lambda(A_{i}).

In causal set theory,
\rho
is often called the sprinkling density (when constant),
\rho^{-1}
the discreteness volume scale, and
\rho^{-1/\mathrm{dim}(M)}
discreteness length scale

We say that the simple point process
N
, or equivalently the random subset
C
, is a
Poisson sprinkling
with sprinkling density
\rho\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(M,\mathrm{vol}_{g})
if its counting process satisfies:

(i)

Complete independence:
whenever

A_{1},\dots,A_{k}\in\mathcal{B}(M)
are pairwise disjoint sets with

\Lambda(A_{i})<\infty
, the random variables

N(A_{1}),\dots,N(A_{k})

are independent.

(ii)

Mean measure:
for every
A\in\mathcal{B}(M)
with

\Lambda(A)<\infty
,

\mathbb{E}[N(A)]=\Lambda(A)=\int_{A}\rho\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{g}.

Since
\rho
is locally integrable,
\Lambda
is locally finite and thus the sprinkling is locally
finite almost surely:

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\bigl\{\omega\in\Omega:\#\bigl(\mathcal{C}(\omega)\cap K\bigr)<\infty\text{ for every compact }K\subset M\bigr\}\Bigr)=1.

Because
\Lambda
is
\sigma
-finite and non-atomic and
N
is simple, the “mean measure” condition can equivalently be replaced by

N(A)\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\Lambda(A)),\qquad A\in\mathcal{B}(M),\ \Lambda(A)<\infty,

where
\mathrm{Poisson}(\Lambda(A))
denotes the Poisson distribution with parameter
\Lambda(A)
.

Finally, assume that a Lorentzian manifold
(M,g)
is globally hyperbolic, and let
C\subseteq M
be a Poisson sprinkling. The causal relation on
M
is a partial order, and since global hyperbolicity implies causality it induces a partial order on
C(\omega)
by restriction. Causal diamonds in a globally hyperbolic spacetime are compact, and
C
is locally finite almost surely, so the interval
J_{C(\omega)}(x,y)=C(\omega)\cap J_{M}(x,y)
is almost surely finite for all
x,y\in C(\omega)
. Therefore,
(C(\omega),\leq_{C(\omega)})
is a causal set for almost every
\omega\in\Omega
.

2.3
Lorentzian optimal transport

In this section, we survey several results in Lorentzian optimal transport. The general framework is that of
Lorentzian (pre-)length spaces
, also called
metric spacetimes
; see
[
17
,
24
]
, while the general theory of Lorentzian optimal transport can be found in
[
23
,
25
,
9
,
10
,
8
]
. To keep the presentation simple and as self-contained as possible, we work with triples
(X,\leq,\uptau)
, where
X
is either a Lorentzian manifold
M
or a causal set
C
, equipped respectively with its causal structure as described in
Sections

2.1
and
2.2
.

The
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}(X)
is the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of the manifold topology when
X=M
, and the discrete
\sigma
-algebra when
X=C
. We write
\mathcal{P}(X)
for the set of probability measures on
(X,\mathcal{B}(X))
, and denote the support of
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
by
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)
. For a causal set,
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)=\{x\in C:\mu(x)>0\}
and integrals are finite sums,

\int_{C}f\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu=\sum_{x\in C}f(x)\mu(x).

In the smooth case,
\mathcal{P}_{c}(M)\subseteq\mathcal{P}(M)
denotes the compactly supported Borel probability measures; in the causal set case,
\mathcal{P}_{c}(C)
denotes the finitely supported probability measures. The pushforward of
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
by a measurable map
T:X\to Y
is denoted as usual by
T_{\sharp}\mu\in\mathcal{P}(Y)
. We denote the coordinate projections by
\mathrm{pr}_{1},\mathrm{pr}_{2}:X\times X\to X
. If
T:X\to X
is measurable, then
(\mathrm{Id},T)_{\sharp}\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X\times X)
is the graph measure of
T
, given by

(\mathrm{Id},T)_{\sharp}\mu(A\times B)=\mu\bigl(A\cap T^{-1}(B)\bigr),\qquad A,B\in\mathcal{B}(X).

Its first and second marginals are
\mu
and
T_{\sharp}\mu
, respectively. A function
u:E\to\mathbb{R}
, with
E\subseteq X
, is called
1
-steep
if
u(q)-u(p)\geq\uptau(p,q)
for all
p,q\in E
with
p\leq q
.

A
coupling
, or
transport plan
, between
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
is a probability measure
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(X\times X)
with marginals
(\mathrm{pr}_{1})_{\sharp}\pi=\mu
and
(\mathrm{pr}_{2})_{\sharp}\pi=\nu
. Let

X^{2}_{\leq}\coloneqq\{(p,q)\in X\times X:p\leq q\},\qquad X^{2}_{\ll}\coloneqq\{(p,q)\in X\times X:p\ll q\}

denote the graph of the causal (resp. chronological) order. A coupling
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is called
causal
(resp.
chronological
), or
admissible
, if it is concentrated on
X^{2}_{\leq}
(resp.
X^{2}_{\ll}
)
In other words, a causal coupling transports mass only causally. The set of all causal couplings is denoted by
\Pi_{\leq}(\mu,\nu)
. Note that if
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)\times\mathrm{supp}(\nu)\subseteq X^{2}_{\leq}
, then every coupling between
\mu
and
\nu
is causal. A
transport map
is a measurable map
T:X\to X
such that
T_{\sharp}\mu=\nu
. The graph measure
(\mathrm{Id},T)_{\sharp}\mu
is then a coupling, and is causal if
x\leq T(x)
for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in X
.

For
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
, the
1
-Lorentz-Wasserstein distance is defined by

\ell_{1}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\leq}(\mu,\nu)}\int_{X\times X}\uptau(p,q)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi(p,q),

(9)

if
\Pi_{\leq}(\mu,\nu)\neq\varnothing
, and by
\ell_{1}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq-\infty
otherwise. This is the
p=1
case of the
p
-Lorentz-Wasserstein distance of
[
10
]
, and is the only case used in this work. By
[
10
, Proposition 2.5]
,
\ell_{1}
satisfies the reverse
triangle inequality:

\ell_{1}(\mu_{0},\mu_{2})\geq\ell_{1}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})+\ell_{1}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}),\qquad\text{ for any }\mu_{0},\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathcal{P}(X).

Remark 2.3

(Dirac masses)
.

If
p\leq q
, the only coupling between
\delta_{p}
and
\delta_{q}
is
\delta_{(p,q)}
, which is causal; hence
\ell_{1}(\delta_{p},\delta_{q})=\uptau(p,q)
. More generally, if
p\leq z
for every
z\in\mathrm{supp}(\nu)
, then every coupling between
\delta_{p}
and
\nu
is causal, and
\ell_{1}(\delta_{p},\nu)=\int_{X}\uptau(p,z)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(z)
. Similarly, if
z\leq q
for every
z\in\mathrm{supp}(\mu)
, then
\ell_{1}(\mu,\delta_{q})=\int_{X}\uptau(z,q)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(z)
.

A causal coupling
\pi\in\Pi_{\leq}(\mu,\nu)
attaining the supremum in
Equation

9
is called
optimal
. If
X=M
is a globally hyperbolic spacetime and
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(M)
satisfy
\Pi_{\leq}(\mu,\nu)\neq\varnothing
, then that supremum is finite and attained; see
[
10
, Proposition 2.3]
. When
X=C
is a causal set, the maximisation problem in
Equation

9
becomes

\ell_{1}(\mu,\nu)=\max_{\pi}\sum_{a,b\in C}\pi(a,b)\,\uptau(a,b),

(10)

where the maximum is taken over all non-negative matrices
\pi=(\pi(a,b))_{a,b\in C}
satisfying, for all
a,b\in C
,

\pi(a,b)\geq 0,\qquad\sum_{b\in C}\pi(a,b)=\mu(a),\qquad\sum_{a\in C}\pi(a,b)=\nu(b),\qquad\pi(a,b)=0\ \text{if }a\nleq b.

If
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(C)
satisfy

\Pi_{\leq}(\mu,\nu)\neq\varnothing
, then this is a
finite-dimensional linear program, and its
maximum is attained.

We now turn to Kantorovich duality, see
[
10
, Section 2.4]
and
[
8
, Appendix C]
. Let
X=M
be a globally hyperbolic spacetime, and let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(M)
satisfy
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)\times\mathrm{supp}(\nu)\subseteq X^{2}_{\ll}
. Then, it holds that

\ell_{1}(\mu,\nu)=\inf\Big(\int_{M}u\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu-\int_{M}u\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\Big),

(11)

where the infimum is taken over all Borel
1
-steep functions
u:E\to\mathbb{R}
, defined on a Borel set
E\subseteq M
containing
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)\cup\mathrm{supp}(\nu)
, with
u\in L^{1}(\mu)\cap L^{1}(\nu)
. Moreover, the infimum is attained by a function
u:E\to\mathbb{R}
defined on a compact, causally convex set
E\subseteq M
containing
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)\cup\mathrm{supp}(\nu)
. Finally, if
u
is a minimizer of
Equation

11
and
\pi
is optimal, then

u\circ\mathrm{pr}_{2}-u\circ\mathrm{pr}_{1}=\uptau\quad\pi\textnormal{-a.e.}

(12)

Conversely, if
\pi\in\Pi_{\leq}(\mu,\nu)
and
u
is an admissible
1
-steep function satisfying
Equation

12
, then
\pi
is optimal and
u
is a dual minimizer.

In the causal set setting
X=C
, let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(C)
satisfy
\Pi_{\leq}(\mu,\nu)\neq\varnothing
. Then the primal problem defining
\ell_{1}(\mu,\nu)
is a finite-dimensional linear program on
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)\times\mathrm{supp}(\nu)
, and finite-dimensional linear-programming duality gives

\ell_{1}(\mu,\nu)=\inf\Big(\sum_{b\in\mathrm{supp}(\nu)}u(b)\nu(b)-\sum_{a\in\mathrm{supp}(\mu)}u(a)\mu(a)\Big),

(13)

where the infimum is taken over all
1
-steep functions
u:\mathrm{supp}(\mu)\cup\mathrm{supp}(\nu)\to\mathbb{R}
. The infimum is attained, and a feasible pair
(\pi,u)
is primal-dual optimal if and only if
Equation

12
holds.

Remark 2.4

(Representation of dual minimizers)
.

If
X=M
is a globally hyperbolic spacetime,
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(M)
satisfy
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)\times\mathrm{supp}(\nu)\subseteq X^{2}_{\ll}
, and
u:E\to\mathbb{R}
is a minimizer in
Equation

11
, then
u
admits the following representation (see
[
10
, Definition 2.23,Corollary 2.29]
). There exist
A\subseteq\mathrm{supp}(\mu)\subseteq E
and
B\subseteq\mathrm{supp}(\nu)\subseteq E
, with
\mu(A)=1
and
\nu(B)=1
, and a
\mu
-integrable upper semicontinuous function
\phi:A\to\mathbb{R}
such that, for every
z\in B
,

u(z)=\sup_{x\in A}\bigl(\phi(x)+\uptau(x,z)\bigr).

3
Ollivier-Ricci curvature for causal sets

3.1
Definitions and basic properties

We postpone the proof of the smooth Lorentzian expansion
Equation

1
to the following section, and use it here as motivation for a notion of curvature on causal sets. In analogy with Ollivier’s definition for combinatorial graphs equipped with the graph distance, and motivated by the same observation in Riemannian geometry
[
27
]
, the idea is to neglect the higher-order terms in
Equation

1
and isolate the quantity

\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\,\frac{n}{(n+1)(n+2)}\mathrm{Ric}(v,v),

which is then taken as the definition of a directional curvature along the geodesic joining
x
and
y
.

Motivated by the corresponding notions for graphs, we introduce here several versions of Ollivier–Ricci curvature for causal sets: the analogue of Ollivier’s original definition from
[
27
]
, together with the
\alpha
-idleness and Lin–Lu–Yau variants introduced in
[
18
]
.

Definition 3.1

(Ollivier-Ricci curvature for causal sets)
.

Let
(C,\leq)
be a causal set,
\alpha\in[0,1]
, and let
n,r\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
with
n\geq r
. Let
\gamma=(x_{0},\dots,x_{n+r})
be a maximising geodesic, and set
x\coloneqq x_{0}
and
y\coloneqq x_{n}
. Define the measures

\mu_{x}\coloneqq\frac{1}{|J(x,x_{r})|}\sum_{z\in J(x,x_{r})}\delta_{z},\qquad\mu_{x}^{\alpha}\coloneqq\alpha\delta_{x}+\frac{1-\alpha}{|J(x,x_{r})|-1}\sum_{z\in J(x,x_{r})\setminus\{x\}}\delta_{z},

and

\mu_{y}\coloneqq\frac{1}{|J(y,x_{n+r})|}\sum_{z\in J(y,x_{n+r})}\delta_{z},\qquad\mu_{y}^{\alpha}\coloneqq\alpha\delta_{y}+\frac{1-\alpha}{|J(y,x_{n+r})|-1}\sum_{z\in J(y,x_{n+r})\setminus\{y\}}\delta_{z}.

The Ollivier–Ricci curvature along
\gamma
at scale
r
, its
\alpha
-idleness version, and the Lin–Lu–Yau curvature are given by

\kappa_{r}(\gamma)\coloneqq\frac{\ell_{1}(\mu_{x},\mu_{y})}{\uptau(x,y)}-1,\qquad\kappa_{r}^{\alpha}(\gamma)\coloneqq\frac{\ell_{1}(\mu_{x}^{\alpha},\mu_{y}^{\alpha})}{\uptau(x,y)}-1,\qquad\kappa_{r}^{\mathrm{LLY}}(\gamma)\coloneqq\lim_{\alpha\to 1}\frac{\kappa_{r}^{\alpha}(\gamma)}{1-\alpha}.

For notational convenience, we will often write

N_{x}=N_{x}^{(r)}\coloneqq|J(x,x_{r})|,\qquad N_{y}=N_{y}^{(r)}\coloneqq|J(y,x_{n+r})|,

where
\gamma=(x_{0},\dots,x_{n+r})
is the maximising geodesic, which will be clear from the context.

Remark 3.2
.

The assumption
n\geq r
guarantees that every coupling between
\mu_{x}
and
\mu_{y}
, and likewise between
\mu_{x}^{\alpha}
and
\mu_{y}^{\alpha}
, is causal. Indeed, since
\gamma=(x_{0},\dots,x_{n+r})
is a maximising geodesic and
y=x_{n}
, the inequality
n\geq r
implies
x_{r}\leq y
. Hence for every
a\in J(x,x_{r})
and
b\in J(y,x_{n+r})
one has

x\leq a\leq x_{r}\leq y\leq b\leq x_{n+r},

so
a\leq b
. In particular, the sets of causal couplings between
\mu_{x}
and
\mu_{y}
, and between
\mu_{x}^{\alpha}
and
\mu_{y}^{\alpha}
, are non-empty, since they contain
\mu_{x}\otimes\mu_{y}
and
\mu_{x}^{\alpha}\otimes\mu_{y}^{\alpha}
, respectively. Hence
\ell_{1}(\mu_{x},\mu_{y})\in\mathopen{(}0,+\infty\mathclose{)}
and
\ell_{1}(\mu_{x}^{\alpha},\mu_{y}^{\alpha})\in\mathopen{(}0,+\infty\mathclose{)}
.

Remark 3.3
.

Ollivier’s original definition in
[
27
]
is not intrinsically tied
to uniform measures on graph balls. Its basic input is a metric space together
with a Markov kernel
x\mapsto\mu_{x}
, that is, a probability measure
\mu_{x}

assigned to each point. Similarly, one could consider a generalisation of

Section

3.1
in which one is given a
“diamond-indexed probability kernel”: a collection
\mathcal{D}
of causal
diamonds together with a probability measure
\mu_{J}
supported on
J
for each

J\in\mathcal{D}
.

We next study the dependence of
\kappa_{r}^{\alpha}(\gamma)
on the idleness parameter
\alpha
. The main point is that, as in the graph setting considered by Lin, Lu, and Yau
[
18
, Lemma 2.1]
, this dependence is concave. This, together with the fact that
\kappa^{1}_{r}(0)
(see
Section

3.1
below), also justifies taking the limit in the definition of
\kappa_{r}^{\mathrm{LLY}}(\gamma)
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
n,r\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
with
n\geq r
, and let

\gamma=(x_{0},\dots,x_{n+r})
be a maximising geodesic. Then the map

[0,1]\ni\alpha\mapsto\kappa_{r}^{\alpha}(\gamma)

is concave.

Proof.

Write
x\coloneqq x_{0}
and
y\coloneqq x_{n}
. Let
0\leq\alpha<\beta<\gamma\leq 1
, and set

\lambda\coloneqq\frac{\gamma-\beta}{\gamma-\alpha}\in(0,1),\quad\text{ so that }\quad\beta=\lambda\alpha+(1-\lambda)\gamma.

(14)

Let
\pi^{\alpha}
be an optimal causal coupling between
\mu_{x}^{\alpha}
and
\mu_{y}^{\alpha}
, and let
\pi^{\gamma}
be an optimal causal coupling between
\mu_{x}^{\gamma}
and
\mu_{y}^{\gamma}
. Thus

\ell_{1}(\mu_{x}^{\alpha},\mu_{y}^{\alpha})=\sum_{u,v\in C}\pi^{\alpha}(u,v)\,\uptau(u,v),\qquad\ell_{1}(\mu_{x}^{\gamma},\mu_{y}^{\gamma})=\sum_{u,v\in C}\pi^{\gamma}(u,v)\,\uptau(u,v).

We claim that

\pi^{\beta}(u,v)\coloneqq\lambda\pi^{\alpha}(u,v)+(1-\lambda)\pi^{\gamma}(u,v),

(15)

is a causal coupling between
\mu_{x}^{\beta}
and
\mu_{y}^{\beta}
. For all
u,v\in C
, we immediately have
\pi^{\beta}(u,v)\geq 0
and

\sum_{u\in C}\pi^{\beta}(u,v)=\lambda\sum_{u\in C}\pi^{\alpha}(u,v)+(1-\lambda)\sum_{u\in C}\pi^{\gamma}(u,v)=\lambda\,\mu_{y}^{\alpha}(v)+(1-\lambda)\,\mu_{y}^{\gamma}(v).

Since
\mu_{x}^{\alpha}
and
\mu_{x}^{\gamma}
are supported on the same finite set

J(x,x_{r})
, one checks directly that

\mu_{y}^{\beta}=\lambda\,\mu_{y}^{\alpha}+(1-\lambda)\,\mu_{y}^{\gamma}.

Indeed, at the point
y
this is exactly the identity
Equation

14
and, at every point of
J(y,x_{n+r})\setminus\{y\}
, it reduces to

\frac{1-\beta}{N_{y}-1}=\lambda\frac{1-\alpha}{N_{y}-1}+(1-\lambda)\frac{1-\gamma}{N_{y}-1}.

Hence, we have found that, for every
u,v\in C
,

\sum_{v\in C}\pi^{\beta}(u,v)=\mu_{x}^{\beta}(u),\quad\text{ and, with a similar argument,}\quad\sum_{u\in C}\pi^{\beta}(u,v)=\mu_{y}^{\beta}(v).

To prove that
\pi^{\beta}
is causal, let
u,v\in C
be such that
\pi^{\beta}(u,v)>0
. From
Equation

15
and the fact that
\pi^{\alpha}
and
\pi^{\gamma}
are causal couplings, we deduce that
\pi^{\alpha}(u,v)>0
or
\pi^{\gamma}(u,v)>0
, and thus
u\leq v
.

Therefore,
\pi^{\beta}
is indeed a causal coupling between
\mu_{x}^{\beta}
and
\mu_{y}^{\beta}
. By the definition of
\ell_{1}
as a supremum over causal couplings, we obtain

\displaystyle\ell_{1}(\mu_{x}^{\beta},\mu_{y}^{\beta})

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{u,v\in C}\pi^{\beta}(u,v)\,\uptau(u,v)

\displaystyle=\lambda\sum_{u,v\in C}\pi^{\alpha}(u,v)\,\uptau(u,v)+(1-\lambda)\sum_{u,v\in C}\pi^{\gamma}(u,v)\,\uptau(u,v)

\displaystyle=\lambda\,\ell_{1}(\mu_{x}^{\alpha},\mu_{y}^{\alpha})+(1-\lambda)\,\ell_{1}(\mu_{x}^{\gamma},\mu_{y}^{\gamma}).

Dividing by the positive quantity
\uptau(x,y)
and subtracting
1
, we get

\displaystyle\kappa_{r}^{\beta}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell_{1}(\mu_{x}^{\beta},\mu_{y}^{\beta})}{\uptau(x,y)}-1\geq\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\ell_{1}(\mu_{x}^{\alpha},\mu_{y}^{\alpha})}{\uptau(x,y)}-1}}\right)+(1-\lambda)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\ell_{1}(\mu_{x}^{\gamma},\mu_{y}^{\gamma})}{\uptau(x,y)}-1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda\,\kappa_{r}^{\alpha}(\gamma)+(1-\lambda)\,\kappa_{r}^{\gamma}(\gamma).

This proves that
\alpha\mapsto\kappa_{r}^{\alpha}(\gamma)
is concave.
∎

In the context of combinatorial graphs, Ollivier–Ricci curvature is typically evaluated at scale
r=1
, that is, between adjacent vertices. This is because unit-radius neighbourhoods already encode non-trivial combinatorial information, such as branching, degree variation, and overlap between neighbours. As the next result shows, in our causal-set definition, the measures become degenerate when
r=1
, being supported on only two points which renders the transport cost completely rigid.

Proposition 3.5
.

For every
\alpha\in[0,1]
and all
r\geq 1
, we have

\kappa_{1}(\gamma)=\kappa_{1}^{\alpha}(\gamma)=\kappa_{1}^{\mathrm{LLY}}(\gamma)=\kappa_{r}^{1}(\gamma)=0.

Proof.

Write
\gamma=(x_{0},\dots,x_{n+1})
and set
x\coloneqq x_{0}
,
y\coloneqq x_{n}
. If
r=1
, then

J(x,x_{1})=\{x,x_{1}\}
and
J(y,x_{n+1})=\{y,x_{n+1}\}
, so

\mu_{x}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\delta_{x}+\delta_{x_{1}}),\qquad\mu_{y}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\delta_{y}+\delta_{x_{n+1}}),

and

\mu_{x}^{\alpha}=\alpha\delta_{x}+(1-\alpha)\delta_{x_{1}},\qquad\mu_{y}^{\alpha}=\alpha\delta_{y}+(1-\alpha)\delta_{x_{n+1}}.

For
a\in J(x,x_{1})
and
b\in J(y,x_{n+1})
, one has

\uptau(a,b)=n-\uptau(x,a)+\uptau(y,b)
, since the only possibilities are

\uptau(x,y)=n
,
\uptau(x,x_{n+1})=n+1
,
\uptau(x_{1},y)=n-1
, and

\uptau(x_{1},x_{n+1})=n
.

Hence for any causal coupling
\pi
between
\mu_{x}^{\alpha}
and
\mu_{y}^{\alpha}
,

\int\uptau(a,b)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi(a,b)=n-\int\uptau(x,a)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu_{x}^{\alpha}(a)+\int\uptau(y,b)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu_{y}^{\alpha}(b).

Since both integrals on the right hand side are equal to
1-\alpha
, it follows that

\smash{\ell_{1}(\mu_{x}^{\alpha},\mu_{y}^{\alpha})}=n=\uptau(x,y)
. Therefore

\smash{\kappa_{1}^{\alpha}(\gamma)}=0
for all
\alpha\in[0,1]
. In particular,
taking
\alpha=1/2
gives
\kappa_{1}(\gamma)=0
, and then also
\kappa_{1}^{\mathrm{LLY}}(\gamma)=0
.

Finally, for arbitrary
r\geq 1
, if
\alpha=1
, then

\mu_{x}^{1}=\delta_{x}
and
\mu_{y}^{1}=\delta_{y}
, so

\ell_{1}(\mu_{x}^{1},\mu_{y}^{1})=\uptau(x,y)
and thus
\kappa_{r}^{1}(\gamma)=0
.
∎

This suggests that the notion of Ollivier–Ricci curvature introduced in the present work is inherently mesoscopic rather than strictly local. Curvature becomes visible only when the observation window is large enough for the diamonds
J(x,x_{r})
and
J(y,x_{n+r})
to develop genuine internal structure. Having noted that the choice
r=1
does not yield a meaningful notion of curvature, the previous proposition shows that
r=2
is still insufficient. Indeed, we still have
\kappa_{2}(\gamma)=0
, while the
\alpha
-idleness and Lin–Lu–Yau versions are determined entirely by the numbers of points in the diamonds
J(x,x_{r})
and
J(y,x_{n+r})
. To detect genuine curvature effects, it is therefore necessary to consider at least the case
r\geq 3
.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
n\geq 2
, let
\gamma=(x_{0},\dots,x_{n+2})
be a maximising geodesic,
and set
x\coloneqq x_{0}
,
y\coloneqq x_{n}
.
Then, for every
\alpha\in[0,1]
, one has

\kappa_{2}(\gamma)=0,\quad\text{ and }\quad\kappa_{2}^{\alpha}(\gamma)=(1-\alpha)\kappa_{2}^{\mathrm{LLY}}(\gamma)=\frac{1-\alpha}{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{N_{y}^{(2)}-1}-\frac{1}{N_{x}^{(2)}-1}}}\right).

Proof.

As in the case
r=1
, one checks that for all such
a\in J(x,x_{2})
and
b\in J(y,x_{n+2})
,

\uptau(a,b)=n-\uptau(x,a)+\uptau(y,b).

Hence for any causal coupling
\pi
between measures
\mu
and
\nu
supported
on
J(x,x_{2})
and
J(y,x_{n+2})
,

\int\uptau(a,b)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi(a,b)=n-\int\uptau(x,a)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(a)+\int\uptau(y,b)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(b).

For the non-idle measures, exactly one point in
J(x,x_{2})
has

\uptau(x,\cdot)=0
, exactly one has value
2
, and all others have value
1
.
Therefore

\sum_{a\in J(x,x_{2})}\uptau(x,a)=N_{x}
, so

\int\uptau(x,a)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu_{x}(a)=1
. Similarly,

\int\uptau(y,b)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu_{y}(b)=1
. It follows that

\ell_{1}(\mu_{x},\mu_{y})=n
, hence
\kappa_{2}(\gamma)=0
.

For the
\alpha
-idle measures,

\int\uptau(x,a)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu_{x}^{\alpha}(a)=(1-\alpha)\frac{N_{x}}{N_{x}-1},\qquad\int\uptau(y,b)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu_{y}^{\alpha}(b)=(1-\alpha)\frac{N_{y}}{N_{y}-1}.

Therefore

\ell_{1}(\mu_{x}^{\alpha},\mu_{y}^{\alpha})=n+(1-\alpha)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{N_{y}}{N_{y}-1}-\frac{N_{x}}{N_{x}-1}}}\right),

and dividing by
\uptau(x,y)=n
gives the claimed formula for

\kappa_{2}^{\alpha}(\gamma)
. The formula for
\kappa_{2}^{\mathrm{LLY}}(\gamma)

follows by dividing by
1-\alpha
and taking the limit
\alpha\to 1
.
∎

We now show that curvature along maximising geodesics satisfies a local-to-global propagation property, similar to
[
27
, Proposition 19]
and
[
18
, Lemma 2.3]
.

Theorem 3.7

(Local-to-global principle)
.

Let
\gamma
be a maximising geodesic of a causal set
(C,\leq)
, and suppose that
\gamma=\gamma_{1}*\cdots*\gamma_{k}
is a concatenation of subgeodesics with lengths
\geq r
. If
\kappa_{r}(\gamma_{i})\geq K
(resp.
\kappa^{\alpha}_{r}(\gamma_{i})\geq K
,
\kappa^{\mathrm{LLY}}_{r}(\gamma_{i})\geq K
) for all
1\leq i\leq k
, then
\kappa_{r}(\gamma)\geq K
(resp.
\kappa^{\alpha}_{r}(\gamma)\geq K
,
\kappa^{\mathrm{LLY}}_{r}(\gamma)\geq K
).

Proof.

Write
\gamma=(x_{0},\dots,x_{n+r})
and let
0=m_{0}<m_{1}<\cdots<m_{k}=n

be such that

\gamma_{i}=(x_{m_{i-1}},x_{m_{i-1}+1},\dots,x_{m_{i}+r}).

Since the measures
\mu_{x_{m_{0}}},\dots,\mu_{x_{m_{k}}}
lie successively along the same maximising geodesic, the reverse triangle inequality for
\ell_{1}
gives

\displaystyle\kappa_{r}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell_{1}(\mu_{x_{0}},\mu_{x_{n}})}{\uptau(x_{0},x_{n})}-1\geq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\ell_{1}(\mu_{x_{m_{i-1}}},\mu_{x_{m_{i}}})-1

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{k}(m_{i}-m_{i-1})\bigl(\kappa_{r}(\gamma_{i})+1\bigr)-1=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{m_{i}-m_{i-1}}{n}\,\kappa_{r}(\gamma_{i}).

The same inequality can be derived for
\kappa_{r}^{\alpha}(\gamma)
. The claim then follows from the assumptions
\kappa_{r}(\gamma_{i})\geq K
(resp.
\kappa^{\alpha}_{r}(\gamma_{i})\geq K
,
\kappa^{\mathrm{LLY}}_{r}(\gamma_{i})\geq K
) for all
1\leq i\leq k
.
∎

This following results shows that the curvature along a maximising geodesic is universally bounded by the ratio between the observation scale and the separation of the two points being compared. In particular, when the observation scale is small compared with the time-separation of the two base points, all three curvature notions are automatically small.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
\gamma=(x_{0},\dots,x_{n+r})
be a maximising geodesic. Then, it holds that

|\kappa_{r}(\gamma)|\leq\frac{r}{n},\quad\text{ as well as }\quad|\kappa_{r}^{\alpha}(\gamma)|\leq\frac{r}{n}\quad\text{ and }\quad|\kappa_{r}^{\mathrm{LLY}}(\gamma)|\leq\frac{r}{n}.

Proof.

Since the arguments for
\kappa_{r}(\gamma)
,
\kappa_{r}^{\alpha}(\gamma)
, and
\kappa_{r}^{\mathrm{LLY}}(\gamma)
are the same, we only prove the estimate for
\kappa_{r}(\gamma)
. As usual, set
x\coloneqq x_{0}
and
y\coloneqq x_{n}
. For
a\in\mathrm{supp}(\mu_{x})\subseteq J(x,x_{r})
and
b\in\mathrm{supp}(\mu_{y})\subseteq J(y,x_{n+r})
, we have

x\leq a\leq x_{r}\leq y\leq b\leq x_{n+r}.

Since
\gamma
is maximising,
\uptau(x_{r},y)=n-r
and
\uptau(x,x_{n+r})=n+r
. Therefore,

n-r\leq\uptau(a,b)\leq n+r,\quad\text{ and thus }\quad n-r\leq\int\uptau(a,b)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi(a,b)\leq n+r,

for every causal coupling
\pi
of
\mu_{x},\mu_{y}
. Hence
n-r\leq\ell_{1}(\mu_{x},\mu_{y})\leq n+r
, and dividing by
n
and subtracting
1
yields

-\frac{r}{n}\leq\kappa_{r}(\gamma)\leq\frac{r}{n},

as claimed.
∎

This suggests that, if one assumes a uniform positive lower bound on curvature, arbitrarily long maximising geodesics cannot exist. In turn, this forces the causal set to have bounded timelike diameter. This is precisely the content of the timelike Bonnet–Myers theorems that we now prove. The next three results rely on the same mechanism, in analogy with
[
27
]
. We start with the curvature notion

Theorem 3.9

(Bonnet–Myers for non-idle Ollivier–Ricci curvature)
.

Fix

\kappa_{r}(\gamma)\geq K>0
for all maximising geodesics

\uptau(x_{0},x_{n+r})\leq r+\frac{J_{r}(\gamma)}{K},

\mathrm{diam}_{\uptau}(\mathcal{C})\geq 2r

\uptau

\gamma=(x_{0},\dots,x_{n+r})

\displaystyle\leq r-2-\frac{(r-2)(r+1)}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{N_{x}}+\frac{1}{N_{y}}}}\right)<r-2.

Let
\gamma=(x_{0},\dots,x_{n+r})

and
\delta_{x_{n+r}}
be the Dirac masses at
x_{0}
and
x_{n+r}

\displaystyle\uptau(x_{0},x_{n+r})=n+r=\ell_{1}(\delta_{x_{0}},\delta_{x_{n+r}})

\displaystyle\geq\ell_{1}(\delta_{x_{0}},\mu_{x_{0}})+\ell_{1}(\mu_{x_{0}},\mu_{x_{n}})+\ell_{1}(\mu_{x_{n}},\delta_{x_{n+r}})

\displaystyle\geq\ell_{1}(\delta_{x_{0}},\mu_{x_{0}})+(K+1)n+\ell_{1}(\mu_{x_{n}},\delta_{x_{n+r}}).

Rearranging yields

\uptau(x_{0},x_{n+r})\leq r+\frac{J_{r}(\gamma)}{K}.

Taking the supremum over all maximising geodesics
\gamma=(x_{0},\dots,x_{n+r})
gives

\sup\uptau(x_{0},x_{n+r})\leq r+\frac{\sup J_{r}(\gamma)}{K}.

If
\mathrm{diam}_{\uptau}(\mathcal{C})\geq 2r
,
Equation

7
yields the diameter bound.
Since
\mu_{x_{0}}
is uniform on

and each additional point in

∎

Theorem 3.10
.

3.2

We begin by recording a simple but useful formula for the Ollivier–Ricci curvature of a causal set. Assume that there exist functions

such that, for every

Then, if

and

\displaystyle\ell_{1}(\mu_{x},\mu_{y})

\displaystyle=\uptau(x,y)+\sum_{q\in J(y,x_{n+r})}g(q)\mu_{y}(q)-\sum_{p\in J(x,x_{r})}f(p)\mu_{x}(p)

\displaystyle=\uptau(x,y)+\frac{1}{N_{y}}\sum_{q\in J(y,x_{n+r})}g(q)-\frac{1}{N_{x}}\sum_{p\in J(x,x_{r})}f(p),

and therefore

\kappa_{r}(\gamma)=\frac{1}{\uptau(x,y)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{N_{y}}\sum_{q\in J(y,x_{n+r})}g(q)-\frac{1}{N_{x}}\sum_{p\in J(x,x_{r})}f(p)}}\right).

\kappa_{r}^{\alpha}(\gamma)=\frac{1-\alpha}{\uptau(x,y)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{N_{y}-1}\sum_{q\in J(y,x_{n+r})\setminus\{y\}}g(q)-\frac{1}{N_{x}-1}\sum_{p\in J(x,x_{r})\setminus\{x\}}f(p)}}\right),

and thus

\kappa_{r}^{\mathrm{LLY}}(\gamma)=\frac{1}{\uptau(x,y)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{N_{y}-1}\sum_{q\in J(y,x_{n+r})\setminus\{y\}}g(q)-\frac{1}{N_{x}-1}\sum_{p\in J(x,x_{r})\setminus\{x\}}f(p)}}\right).

(19)

, and whenever
y
is an immediate successor of
x
, one has
\rho(y)=\rho(x)+1
. Under that definition, for comparable
p\leq q
one gets

\uptau(p,q)=\rho(q)-\rho(p)=\uptau(x,y)+\uptau(y,q)-\uptau(x,p)

for all
x,y\in C
such that
x\leq p\leq q
and
x\leq y\leq q
, so that
Equation

16
holds.

Let
\gamma=(x_{0},\dots,x_{n+r})
be a maximising geodesic, and set
x\coloneqq x_{0}
and
y\coloneqq x_{n}
as usual. The affine formulas
Equations

17
,
18
and
19
imply that

\kappa_{r}(\gamma)=\frac{1}{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{N_{y}}\sum_{i=1}^{r}i\,N_{y}(i)-\frac{1}{N_{x}}\sum_{i=1}^{r}i\,N_{x}(i)}}\right).

(20)

and

\kappa_{r}^{\alpha}(\gamma)=(1-\alpha)\kappa_{r}^{\mathrm{LLY}}(\gamma)=\frac{1-\alpha}{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{N_{y}-1}\sum_{i=1}^{r}i\,N_{y}(i)-\frac{1}{N_{x}-1}\sum_{i=1}^{r}i\,N_{x}(i)}}\right),

(21)

where we have set, for
0\leq i\leq r
,
N_{x}(i)\coloneqq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\{p\in J(x,x_{r}):\rho(p)-\rho(x)=i\}}}\right|
and
N_{y}(i)\coloneqq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\{q\in J(y,x_{n+r}):\rho(q)-\rho(y)=i\}}}\right|
. All the remaining examples of this section are graded causal sets.

Example 3.15

(Complete layered causal set)
.

Let
C
be a finite causal set of the form

C=L_{1}\sqcup L_{2}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup L_{K},

where the chronological relation is defined by

x\ll y\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x\in L_{i},\ y\in L_{j},\ \text{and }i<j.

This is the causal-set analogue of a complete multipartite poset: points in the same layer are incomparable, while every point in a lower layer is causally below every point in a higher layer.

This causal set is graded by the rank function
\rho(z)=i
for
z\in L_{i}
. Note that if
u\in L_{s}
and
v\in L_{t}
with
s<t
, then a causal curve from
u
to
v
is maximising if and only if it contains exactly one point in each layer
L_{s},\ldots,L_{t}
. Let
\gamma=(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n+r})
be such a maximising geodesic, with

x_{k}\in L_{s+k}
for all
0\leq k\leq n+r
. Set
x\coloneqq x_{0}
and
y\coloneqq x_{n}
.
Since
J(x,x_{r})=\{x,x_{r}\}\cup L_{s+1}\cup\cdots\cup L_{s+r-1}
and
J(y,x_{n+r})=\{y,x_{n+r}\}\cup L_{s+n+1}\cup\cdots\cup L_{s+n+r-1}
, the graded formulas
Equations

20
and
21
give

\kappa_{r}(\gamma)=\frac{1}{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{r+\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}i\,|L_{s+n+i}|}{2+\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}|L_{s+n+i}|}-\frac{r+\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}i\,|L_{s+i}|}{2+\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}|L_{s+i}|}}}\right),

while

\kappa_{r}^{\alpha}(\gamma)=(1-\alpha)\kappa_{r}^{\mathrm{LLY}}(\gamma)=\frac{1-\alpha}{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{r+\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}i\,|L_{s+n+i}|}{1+\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}|L_{s+n+i}|}-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}i\,|L_{s+i}|}{1+\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}|L_{s+i}|}}}\right).

Example 3.16

(Positively-curved complete layered causal set)
.

The complete-layered formula can be used recursively to search for examples of prescribed non-idle curvature. Writing

n_{i}\coloneqq|L_{i}|,\qquad F_{s}\coloneqq\frac{r+\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}i\,n_{s+i}}{2+\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}n_{s+i}},

the previous example gives

\kappa_{r}(\gamma)=\frac{F_{s+n}-F_{s}}{n}.

Thus, for fixed

and

, prescribing
\kappa_{r}(\gamma)=K
for all admissible geodesics
\gamma=(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n+r})
with

is equivalent to imposing
F_{s+n}=F_{s}+nK
for all admissible values of

. Starting at

from suitable initial layer sizes

, this relation can be used recursively to determine successive layer sizes, as long as no denominator vanishes and the resulting numbers remain positive integers.

For instance, take
n=r=3
and
K=\frac{1}{45}
. Then

F_{s}=\frac{3+n_{s+1}+2n_{s+2}}{2+n_{s+1}+n_{s+2}},\qquad F_{s+3}=F_{s}+\frac{1}{15}.

The following layer sizes give a positive-curvature complete layered causal set with
\kappa_{3}(\gamma)=\frac{1}{45}
for each admissible geodesic in this range:

\begin{multlined}(n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{23})=(1,20,23,20,13,20,23,20,41,62,71,197,395,593,2375,\\
6533,13067,84941,339767,934361,13081067,85026941,340107767).\end{multlined}

Attempting to continue this particular recursion by solving for

Example 3.17

(Product causal set)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a locally finite geodesic metric space, and let
\lambda=(\lambda_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a sequence of positive real numbers. Consider the causal set

C_{\lambda}(X)\coloneqq(\mathbb{Z}\times X,\leq).

The strict relation
\ll
is generated by the one-step relations defined by the following property:
(t,u)\ll(t+1,v)
if and only if
d(u,v)\leq\lambda_{t}
. Equivalently, for
t<s
,

(t,u)\ll(s,v)\quad\text{ if and only if}\quad d(u,v)\leq\sum_{m=t}^{s-1}\lambda_{m}.

Since
X
is locally finite, all metric balls of finite radius are finite, and hence the causal intervals are finite. It is a graded causal set with rank function
\rho(t,u)=t
, since each one-step relation increases the time coordinate by one.

The formulas
Equations

20
and
21
apply, where we can write
N_{x}(i)
and
N_{y}(i)
more explicitly as intersections of metric balls in
X
:
for
0\leq i\leq r
,

N_{x}(i)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|B_{X}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z_{0},\sum_{m=t_{0}}^{t_{0}+i-1}\lambda_{m}}}\right)\cap B_{X}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z_{r},\sum_{m=t_{0}+i}^{t_{0}+r-1}\lambda_{m}}}\right)}}\right|,

and

N_{y}(i)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|B_{X}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z_{n},\sum_{m=t_{0}+n}^{t_{0}+n+i-1}\lambda_{m}}}\right)\cap B_{X}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z_{n+r},\sum_{m=t_{0}+n+i}^{t_{0}+n+r-1}\lambda_{m}}}\right)}}\right|.

Example 3.18

(Locally finite Boolean algebra)
.

Every locally finite Boolean algebra is finite, and hence isomorphic to a finite Boolean lattice
B_{m}
. Here
B_{m}
is the set of all subsets of
\{1,\dots,m\}
, ordered by inclusion:
B_{m}\coloneqq\{A\mid A\subseteq\{1,\dots,m\}\}
, with
A\leq B
if and only if
A\subseteq B
. This is a graded causal set with rank function
\rho(A)=|A|
. Therefore, using
Equations

20
and
21
, one obtains
\kappa_{r}(\gamma)=\kappa_{r}^{\alpha}(\gamma)=\kappa_{r}^{\mathrm{LLY}}(\gamma)=0
.

Example 3.19

(Coxeter groups)
.

A
Coxeter system

(W,S)
consists of a group
W
and a generating set

S
such that
W=\langle S\mid(st)^{m_{st}}=1\rangle
, where
m_{ss}=1
and

m_{st}=m_{ts}\geq 2
is either an integer or
\infty
for
t\neq s
, see
[
5
]
. The Coxeter length of an element
w\in W
is

\ell(w)\coloneqq\min\{k\geq 0:w=s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{k}\text{ for some }s_{i}\in S\}.

The
(right) weak order
on
(W,S)
is defined by

u\leq_{\mathrm{weak}}v\quad\text{ if and only if}\quad v=uw\text{ and }\ell(v)=\ell(u)+\ell(w)\text{ for some }w\in W.

The
strong order
, usually called the
Bruhat order
, is defined by
u\leq_{\mathrm{strong}}v
if a reduced expression for
v
contains, after deleting some letters, a reduced expression for
u
; that is to say, if there are generators
s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}\in S
and indices
1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{\ell}\leq k
such that
v=s_{1}\cdots s_{k}
,
k=\ell(v)
,
u=s_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{\ell}}
, and
\ell=\ell(u)
.

For any Coxeter system
(W,S)
, both the weak and the strong order are locally finite posets. We write
(C,\leq)
for the corresponding causal set, where
\leq
denotes either one of the weak orders or the Bruhat order. Both orders are graded by the Coxeter length
\ell
and therefore induce the same formula for the time-separation: for comparable elements
u\leq v
in both ordering systems, one has
\uptau(u,v)=\ell(v)-\ell(u)
. Note that the weak and Bruhat orders give the same formula for the
time-separation on comparable pairs. However, Bruhat order has more comparable
pairs, so the resulting time-separation functions differ precisely on pairs
that are Bruhat-comparable but weak-incomparable: for such pairs,

\uptau_{\mathrm{Bruhat}}(u,v)>0
, whereas

\uptau_{\mathrm{weak}}(u,v)=0
.
The formula for the Ollivier-Ricci curvature of graded causal sets
Equations

20
and
21
again apply here, with

N_{x}(i)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\{z\in J(x_{0},x_{r}):\ell(z)=\ell(x_{0})+i\}}}\right|,

and

N_{y}(i)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\{z\in J(x_{n},x_{n+r}):\ell(z)=\ell(x_{n})+i\}}}\right|.

Example 3.20

(Young’s lattice)
.

Young’s lattice

\mathbb{Y}
is the poset whose elements are integer partitions, that is, finite non-increasing sequences of positive integers,

\mathbb{Y}\coloneqq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})\;\middle|\;k\in\mathbb{N},\ \lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{N},\ \lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{k}>0}}\right\}.

Given two partitions
\lambda
and
\mu
in
\mathbb{Y}
, we extend the shorter one by zeros and define
\lambda\leq\mu
if and only if
\lambda_{i}\leq\mu_{i}
for every
i
. With this order,
(\mathbb{Y},\leq)
is a graded causal set with rank
\rho(\lambda)=|\lambda|\coloneqq\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}
, so its Ollivier–Ricci curvatures are given by
Equations

20
and
21
.

Example 3.21

(Rooted-tree posets)
.

A
rooted tree
T
with root
o
is a connected acyclic graph together with a distinguished vertex
o\in V(T)
. We define a partial order
\leq
on
T
by declaring
u\leq v
if and only if
u
lies on the unique simple path from the root
o
to
v
. If every root-to-vertex path is finite, this gives a causal set. It is graded by the depth function, which counts the number of edges on the unique simple path from
o
to
v
. However, every interval
J(u,v)
is totally ordered, so for any maximising geodesic
\gamma
we have
N_{x}(i)=N_{y}(i)=1
for all
0\leq i\leq r
, and consequently
\kappa_{r}(\gamma)=\kappa_{r}^{\alpha}(\gamma)=\kappa_{r}^{\mathrm{LLY}}(\gamma)=0
.

3.3
Numerical examples

We now describe a numerical procedure for approximating the quantity

\frac{\ell_{1}(\mu_{x},\mu_{y})}{\delta}-1

from a Poisson sprinkling of a Lorentzian manifold; see

Section

2.2
. After rescaling by
\varepsilon^{-2}
and by the
dimensional constant appearing in
Equation

1
, this quantity is intended to approximate
\mathrm{Ric}(v,v)
, where
v
is the tangent vector to the geodesic joining
x
to
y
. The algorithm goes as follows.

(i)

Choose a coordinate chart.

The spacetime region is described in a coordinate chart

\phi:B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}\to M
, where

B=\mathopen{[}a_{1},b_{1}\mathclose{]}\times\cdots\times\mathopen{[}a_{d},b_{d}\mathclose{]}
and
\Omega\subseteq\phi(B)
denotes the spacetime region to be sprinkled.

(ii)

Sprinkle the spacetime region.

We want points distributed in
\widetilde{\Omega}=\phi^{-1}(\Omega)
with density
proportional to
w(u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{1}\dots\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{d}=\phi^{*}\mathrm{vol}_{g}
. Thus we
oversample uniformly in
B
by choosing an upper bound
M\geq\sup_{B}w(u)
.
Computationally, we generate a random integer

N_{\mathrm{cand}}\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\rho M|B|)
and then generate

N_{\mathrm{cand}}
independent uniformly distributed candidate points in
B
.
We then accept a candidate point
u
with probability
w(u)/M
, rejecting it
automatically if
u\notin\widetilde{\Omega}
. In particular, the number
N
of
accepted points is Poisson distributed with mean

\rho\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}w(u)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{1}\dots\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{d}=\rho\,\mathrm{vol}_{g}(\Omega)
. The partial order is inherited from the spacetime causal order.

(iii)

Choice of the time-separation function.

Depending on the numerical experiment, there are several possible choices.
The most intrinsic one is the counting time-separation, defined as the
length of a longest chain from
p
to
q
, as in
Equation

7
.
Computationally, the counting time-separation is obtained by finding longest
paths in the directed acyclic graph. In the
two-dimensional product-order case, where the chart is such that

\phi^{-1}(p)=(u(p),v(p))
and
p\leq_{C}q
if and only if

u(p)\leq u(q)
and
v(p)\leq v(q)
, we use the much
faster longest-increasing-subsequence-type algorithm. When we want to use more of the background spacetime geometry, we instead
take the restricted manifold time-separation between causally related
sprinkled points.

(iv)

Select the endpoints.

One option is to fix
x\in\Omega
and a future-directed unit timelike
vector
v\in\T_{x}(M)
, and then add the two points

x
and
x^{\prime}=\mathrm{exp}_{x}((\delta+2\varepsilon)v)
to the sprinkled causal set.
Alternatively, the endpoints may instead be selected
among the sprinkled points either by prescribing their counting
time-separation
\uptau_{C}(x,x^{\prime})=n+r
, or by first finding a long chain
and taking
x
and
x^{\prime}
its extremal elements. When working with the manifold time-separation, one
may choose a pair for which

|\uptau_{M}(x,x^{\prime})-(\delta+2\varepsilon)|
is minimal.

(v)

Find a maximising chain.

After the endpoints
x,x^{\prime}
have been chosen, we select a maximising chain
\gamma

between them. This step is omitted when
the transport supports are prescribed directly at the continuum level,
rather than obtained from a discrete maximising chain.

(vi)

Split the maximising chain.

We choose the indices
n,r
so that
\gamma=(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n+r})
and

n/(n+r)
approximates
\delta/(\delta+2\varepsilon)
.
Thus
n
represents the separation between the lower tips of the two
diamonds, while
r
represents their time diameter
2\varepsilon
.
In purely counting-time experiments one may instead prescribe the
integers
n,r
directly.

(vii)

Build the transport supports and cost matrix.

The split chain determines the two
causal diamonds
A\coloneqq J_{C}(x_{0},x_{r}),B\coloneqq J_{C}(x_{n},x_{n+r})

which support the two probability measures in

Section

3.1
. The transport cost matrix is

C_{ab}=\uptau_{\mathrm{cost}}(a,b),\qquad a\in A,\ b\in B,

where
\uptau_{\mathrm{cost}}
is either the counting time-separation

\uptau_{C}
or the
restricted manifold time-separation
\uptau_{M}
, depending on the numerical experiment. Alternatively, one may prescribe the two continuum diamonds first and then
set
A
and
B
to be their intersections with the sprinkled causal
set
C
.

(viii)

Solve the Lorentzian transport problem.

The optimal transport problem is solved with the Python Optimal Transport
library. Since the library is written for minimization whereas

\ell_{1}
is a supremum, see
Equation

10
, the implementation
minimizes the shifted or negated cost matrix and then converts back to the
corresponding maximal transport value. Depending on the experiment, we use
either the exact linear-programming solver or a Sinkhorn-regularized
approximation.

(ix)

Form the curvature estimator.
Finally, we compute the renormalised quantity

\widehat{\mathrm{Ric}}_{\rho,\varepsilon}(v,v)\coloneqq\frac{2(d+1)(d+2)}{d\varepsilon^{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\ell_{1}(\mu_{x},\mu_{y})}{\uptau(x_{0},x_{n})}-1}}\right).

Here
\uptau
denotes the time-separation chosen for the transport
cost, and the normalising factor is interpreted according to the chosen
scale convention. If
\varepsilon
is a continuum proper-time scale, the
prefactor is used as written. If the computation is carried out in
counting-chain units, then
\varepsilon
is replaced by the continuum scale
corresponding to the chain height
r
; in dimension two this means

\varepsilon^{2}\simeq r^{2}/(8\rho)
. We then study the behaviour of this
estimator as the sprinkling density
\rho
, the scale
\varepsilon
,
or both are varied.

Curvature estimator on individual sprinkled causal sets.
We ran numerical experiments for the renormalised Ricci curvature of causal sets generated by sprinklings into two-dimensional Minkowski, de Sitter, and anti-de Sitter space, using the counting time-separation. In

How quickly must the sprinkling density grow in the smooth limit?
To recover the smooth Ricci curvature from the Ollivier–Ricci curvature of a Poisson sprinkling, one must send the mesoscopic scales

4
Ollivier-Ricci curvature in smooth Lorentzian geometry

In this section, we prove
Equation

1

[

]
; see also

4.1
Diffeomorphism between tangent and manifold diamonds

On a sufficiently small neighbourhood of a point in a Riemannian manifold, the exponential map is a diffeomorphism from a ball in the tangent space onto a geodesic ball in the manifold.

This picture breaks down for causal diamonds in the Lorentzian setting. Even when the exponential map is a local diffeomorphism, it need not send the tangent-space (Minkowski) diamond onto a manifold diamond. Accordingly, rather than using the exponential map directly, we precompose it with a suitable radial rescaling so that the resulting map is indeed a diffeomorphism (up to a null set) from
\tilde{D}_{\varepsilon}(x,v)
onto
D_{\varepsilon}(x,v)
, where we continue to adopt the notation of
Section

1
.

Let

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
x\in M
and let
u,v\in\T_{x}(M)
be non-collinear future-directed timelike vectors. For
\varepsilon>0

sufficiently small, we have

\tilde{t}^{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{exit}}(x,v,u)=2\varepsilon\frac{\langle u,v\rangle-\sqrt{\langle u,v\rangle^{2}-\langle u,u\rangle\langle v,v\rangle}}{\langle u,u\rangle}.

Proof.

Since
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small,
\mathrm{exp}_{x}
is a diffeomorphism on a neighbourhood containing
\tilde{D}_{\varepsilon}(x,v)
, and thus
\mathrm{exp}_{x}(su)\in\mathrm{exp}_{x}(\tilde{D}_{\varepsilon}(x,v))
if and only if
su\in\tilde{D}_{\varepsilon}(x,v)
. By definition of
\tilde{D}_{\varepsilon}(x,v)
, the exit occurs when the ray
s\mapsto su
meets the null boundary of

J^{-}(2\varepsilon v)
, i.e. at the smallest positive
s
such that

\langle su-2\varepsilon v,su-2\varepsilon v\rangle=0.

Expanding gives the quadratic equation

\langle u,u\rangle s^{2}-4\varepsilon\langle u,v\rangle s+4\varepsilon^{2}\langle v,v\rangle=0,

whose solutions are

s=2\varepsilon\frac{\langle u,v\rangle\pm\sqrt{\langle u,v\rangle^{2}-\langle u,u\rangle\langle v,v\rangle}}{\langle u,u\rangle}.

Taking the smallest positive root (for future-directed timelike
u,v
this corresponds to the minus sign) yields the
claimed formula for
\tilde{t}^{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{exit}}(x,v,u)
.
∎

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
x\in M
and let
u,v\in\T_{x}(M

\varepsilon>0

sufficiently small, we have

(0,x,u,v)

s_{0}\coloneqq s(0,x,u,v)

\langle su-2v,su-2v\rangle=0,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad s_{0}=2\frac{\langle u,v\rangle-\sqrt{\langle u,v\rangle^{2}-\langle u,u\rangle\langle v,v\rangle}}{\langle u,u\rangle},

(24)

with an

(x,u,v)

Proof.

The exit point
\mathrm{exp}_{x}(t^{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{exit}}(u)u)
lies on the boundary of
D_{\varepsilon}(x,v)
; equivalently, it is null-related to the top vertex
\gamma_{v}(2\varepsilon)
. Being on
\partial J^{-}(\gamma_{v}(2\varepsilon))
means that the unique geodesic from
\mathrm{exp}_{x}(tu)
to
\gamma_{v}(2\varepsilon)
is null, and hence its energy vanishes. In other words, the exit time
t^{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{exit}}(u)
is characterised as the smallest positive solution
t
of

\Phi(t,\varepsilon,x,v,u)\coloneqq\mathrm{E}(\mathrm{exp}_{x}(tu),\gamma_{v}(2\varepsilon))=0.

The map
\Phi
is defined for all
(t,\varepsilon,x,v,u)
such that
\mathrm{exp}_{x}(tu)
and
\gamma_{v}(2\varepsilon)=\mathrm{exp}_{x}(2\varepsilon v)
lie in a common convex normal neighbourhood of

(so that the connecting geodesic is unique and the energy
\mathrm{E}
is well defined), which in particular holds when
|t|
and
|\varepsilon|
are sufficiently small and
u
and
v
range over a bounded set.

Applying

A.1
with
\gamma_{u}(t)=\mathrm{exp}_{x}(tu)
and

yields that the function defined by

H(t,\varepsilon,u)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Phi(\varepsilon t,\varepsilon,u)

satisfies, as

and locally uniformly,

H(t,\varepsilon,u)=\frac{1}{2}\langle tu-2v,tu-2v\rangle-\frac{2}{3}\langle R(u,v)u,v\rangle t^{2}\varepsilon^{2}+t^{2}\varepsilon^{2}O((|t\varepsilon|+|\varepsilon|)^{2}).

(26)

Strictly speaking,

is defined only for
\varepsilon\neq 0

by setting

By construction, for each
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small the exit time satisfies

t^{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{exit}}(u)=\varepsilon s(\varepsilon,u),

where

denotes the smallest positive root of the equation

(27)

Note that
s_{0}\coloneqq s(0,x,u,v)
is the smallest positive root of

\langle su-2v,su-2v\rangle=0
, and we recover
Equation

24
.
Using
Equation

26
, we find that

\partial_{s}H(s_{0},0,x,u,v)=\langle s_{0}u-2v,u\rangle=-2\sqrt{\langle u,v\rangle^{2}-\langle u,u\rangle\langle v,v\rangle}\neq 0,

where the final inequality holds since

and

are not collinear. The implicit function theorem implies that

s(\varepsilon)=s_{0}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}(0)}}\right)\varepsilon+\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{2}s}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\varepsilon^{2}}(0)}}\right)\varepsilon^{2}+\frac{1}{6}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{3}s}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\varepsilon^{3}}(0)}}\right)\varepsilon^{3}+O(\varepsilon^{4}),\qquad\text{ as }\varepsilon\to 0.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
x\in M
and let

Then, as

\mathrm{vol}_{g}(D_{\varepsilon}(x,v))={L}(\tilde{D}_{\varepsilon}(x,v))\bigl(1+O(\varepsilon^{2})\bigr).

Identifying

\mathrm{vol}_{g}(D_{\varepsilon}(x,v))=\int_{\tilde{D}_{\varepsilon}(x,v)}\sqrt{|\mathrm{det}\,g^{x}_{ij}(h^{\varepsilon}_{x}(w))|}\,|\mathrm{det}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}_{w}h^{\varepsilon}_{x}|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{L}(w),

where

h^{\varepsilon}_{x}\coloneqq\mathrm{exp}_{x}^{-1}\circ f^{\varepsilon}_{x}\circ\mathrm{exp}_{x}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\cong\T_{x}(M)\to\T_{x}(M)\cong\mathbb{R}^{n}

If

|\mathrm{det}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}_{w}h^{\varepsilon}_{x}|=1+O(\varepsilon^{2}).

The standard expansion of the metric density in normal coordinates yields

\displaystyle\sqrt{|\det g^{x}_{ij}(h_{x}^{\varepsilon}(w))|}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{6}\sum_{i,j=0}^{n-1}R_{ij}(x)\bigl(h_{x}^{\varepsilon}(w)\bigr)^{i}\bigl(h_{x}^{\varepsilon}(w)\bigr)^{j}+O\bigl(|h_{x}^{\varepsilon}(w)|^{3}\bigr)

4.2
Asymptotics on the time-separation function

Using the notation of
Section

1
, we consider the orthogonal complement

v^{\perp}=\{w\in\T_{x}(M)\mid\langle w,v\rangle=0\},

which is an
(n-1)
-dimensional spacelike subspace of
\T_{x}(M)
. We also choose a sufficiently small convex neighbourhood
\mathcal{U}
of

in
\T_{x}(M)
on which
\mathrm{exp}_{x}
is a diffeomorphism. Then, the region

\mathcal{V}\coloneqq\mathrm{exp}_{x}(\mathcal{U}\cap v^{\perp})

is an embedded
(n-1)

, and we let
\mathcal{N}
denote a sufficiently small timelike tubular neighbourhood of

. From now on we take
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{N}
as small as needed, shrinking them further whenever necessary, without changing notation.
Recall that, after fixing a unit normal field
\nu
along

\mathrm{I\!I}_{x}(U,V)\coloneqq\langle\nabla_{U}V,\nu(x)\rangle.

A standard computation in normal (exponential) coordinates centred at

of
\mathcal{V}
at

vanishes identically (see for example
[

).

The
(future-directed) time-separation to
V
is the map given by

u(x^{\prime})\coloneqq\max_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}}\uptau(y^{\prime},x^{\prime}),\qquad x^{\prime}\in J^{+}(\mathcal{V})\cap\mathcal{N}.

For each

, the maximiser in the definition of

Proposition 4.6
.

As
(\varepsilon,\delta)\to(0,0)
, we have the expansion

u(f^{\varepsilon}_{(y,v_{\delta})}\circ\mathrm{exp}_{y}(\varepsilon w_{\delta}))=\delta-\varepsilon\langle v,w\rangle+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\delta}{2}\langle R(w,v)v,w\rangle+O(\varepsilon^{2}\delta^{2})+O(\varepsilon^{3}),

where the remainder term is locally uniform in

and

Proof.

The strategy is to rewrite the square of the time-separation as the negative of the Lorentzian energy of the unique maximizing timelike geodesic segment realising the projection map
\pi
onto
\mathcal{V}
, and then to obtain a second-order expansion by analysing an associated geodesic variation and its Jacobi field.

Let
\gamma:\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}\to M:t\mapsto\mathrm{exp}_{x}(t\delta v)
, and note that
w_{\delta}=\mathord{\parallel_{0}^{1}}(\gamma)[w]
. Then

\theta:\mathopen{[}0,\varepsilon\mathclose{]}\to M:s\mapsto f_{(y,v_{\delta})}^{\varepsilon}\circ\mathrm{exp}_{y}(sw_{\delta})=\mathrm{exp}_{y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s\frac{t^{\varepsilon}_{\rm exit}(y,v_{\delta},w_{\delta})}{\tilde{t}^{\varepsilon}_{\rm exit}(y,v_{\delta},w_{\delta})}w_{\delta}}}\right).

(35)

This is the constant-speed geodesic from
y
in the direction
w^{\prime}
, adjusted so as to correct the mismatch between the tangent-space diamond image
\mathrm{exp}_{y}(\tilde{D}_{\varepsilon}(y,v_{\delta}))
and the manifold diamond
D_{\varepsilon}(y,v_{\delta})
. For
s\in\mathopen{[}0,\varepsilon\mathclose{]}
, by construction of
u_{+}
and
\pi
, the unique maximizing timelike geodesic from
\pi(\theta(s))
to
\theta(s)
is precisely the curve
c_{s}:\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}\to M
given by

c_{s}(t)=\mathrm{exp}_{\pi(\theta(s))}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\,\mathrm{exp}_{\pi(\theta(s))}^{-1}(\theta(s))}}\right).

Thus

is a geodesic variation: varying
s
moves both the endpoint
\theta(s)
and the footpoint
\pi(\theta(s))
. For each fixed

is an affinely parametrised geodesic, and hence
\langle\dot{c}_{s}(t),\dot{c}_{s}(t)\rangle

u_{+}^{2}(f^{\varepsilon}_{(y,v_{\delta})}\circ\mathrm{exp}_{y}(sw_{\delta}))=u_{+}(\theta(s))^{2}=-\int_{0}^{1}\langle\dot{c}_{s}(t),\dot{c}_{s}(t)\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\eqqcolon\mathrm{E}(c_{s}).

We denote by

J_{s}(t)\coloneqq\frac{\partial}{\partial s}c_{s}(t)

the Jacobi field along the curve

J_{s}(1)=\partial_{s}c_{s}(1)=\partial_{s}\theta(s)=\dot{\theta}(s),\qquad J_{s}(0)=\partial_{s}c_{s}(0)=\partial_{s}\pi(\theta(s))\in\T_{\pi(\theta(s))}(\mathcal{V}).

The geodesics

meet
\mathcal{V}
orthogonally at

for every

\dot{c}_{s}(0)\perp T_{c_{s}(0)}\mathcal{V}\qquad\text{for all }s.

In other words, if
\nu
is a smooth unit normal field along

, then

\dot{c}_{s}(0)=-\langle\dot{c}_{s}(0),\nu\rangle\nu\eqqcolon\alpha(s)\nu.

In particular, since
\pi(\theta(0))=x
and
\T_{x}(\mathcal{V})=v^{\perp}
, we have
J_{0}(0)\in v^{\perp}
, and thus

\langle J_{0}(0),\dot{c}_{0}(0)\rangle=\delta\langle J_{0}(0),v\rangle=0.

(36)

Differentiating the identity
\langle J_{s}(0),\dot{c}_{s}(0)\rangle\equiv 0

0=\langle\mathrm{D}_{s}J_{s}(0),\dot{c}_{s}(0)\rangle+\langle J_{s}(0),\mathrm{D}_{s}\dot{c}_{s}(0)\rangle=\langle\mathrm{D}_{s}J_{s}(0),\dot{c}_{s}(0)\rangle+\langle J_{s}(0),\mathrm{D}_{t}J_{s}(0)\rangle.

More explicitly, we have

\mathrm{D}_{t}J_{s}(0)=\mathrm{D}_{s}\dot{c}_{s}(0)=\alpha^{\prime}(s)\nu(c_{s}(0))+\alpha(s)\mathrm{D}_{s}(\nu(c_{s}(0)))=\alpha^{\prime}(s)\nu+\alpha(s)\nabla_{J_{s}(0)}\nu.

Now
\nabla_{J_{0}(0)}\nu
is tangent to
\mathcal{V}
, since
\langle\nu,\nu\rangle
is constant and hence

0=J_{0}(0)\langle\nu,\nu\rangle=2\langle\nabla_{J_{0}(0)}\nu,\nu\rangle
. Since the second fundamental form of
\mathcal{V}
vanishes at
x
, we also have, for all

,
\langle\nabla_{J_{0}(0)}\nu,W\rangle=-\mathrm{I\!I}_{x}(J_{0}(0),W)=0
. Thus we obtain

\langle\mathrm{D}_{s}J_{0}(0),v\rangle=\langle\mathrm{D}_{t}J_{0}(0),J_{0}(0)\rangle=0.

(37)

Moreover, since
J_{s}(1)=\dot{\theta}(s)
,
Equation

35
gives

J_{0}(1)=\dot{\theta}(0)=\frac{t^{\varepsilon}_{\rm exit}(y,v_{\delta},w_{\delta})}{\tilde{t}^{\varepsilon}_{\rm exit}(y,v_{\delta},w_{\delta})}w_{\delta},

(38)

so that, by
Equation

30

\displaystyle\langle J_{0}(1),\dot{c}_{0}(1)\rangle

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
x\in M
and let
v\in\T_{x}(M)
be a unit future-directed timelike vector. The average of
w\mapsto\langle R(v,w)w,v\rangle
over

\fint_{\tilde{D}_{\varepsilon}(x,v)}\langle R(v,w)w,v\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w=\varepsilon^{2}\frac{n}{(n+1)(n+2)}\mathrm{Ric}(v,v).

Proof.

For
w\in\T_{x}(M)
, we define the bilinear form
B(w)\coloneqq\langle R(v,w)w,v\rangle
. We choose an orthonormal basis
e_{0},\dots,e_{n-1}
of
\T_{x}(M)
such that, for

e_{0}=v,\qquad\langle e_{0},e_{0}\rangle=-1,\qquad\langle e_{i},e_{j}\rangle=\delta_{ij}.

Every vector

can be written uniquely as
w=tv+z
, for unique
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
z\in v^{\perp}

|z|
for the Euclidean norm induced on
v^{\perp}

\langle w,w\rangle=-t^{2}+|z|^{2}

\tilde{J}^{+}(0)=\{(t,z):t\geq|z|\},\quad\text{ and }\quad\tilde{J}^{-}(2\varepsilon v)=\{(t,z):2\varepsilon-t\geq|z|\},

and therefore

\tilde{D}_{\varepsilon}(x,v)=\{(t,z):0\leq t\leq 2\varepsilon,\ |z|\leq\rho(t)\},\qquad\text{ where }\rho(t)\coloneqq\min\{t,2\varepsilon-t\}.

By bilinearity and the symmetry properties of the curvature tensor, we have

B(tv+z)=\langle R(v,z)z,v\rangle.

The bilinear form
B^{\perp}
on
v^{\perp}
defined by
B^{\perp}(z_{1},z_{2})\coloneqq\langle R(v,z_{1})z_{2},v\rangle
is symmetric by the pair symmetry of the curvature tensor, and

We now use the following standard Euclidean averaging formula: if
A

\mathbb{R}^{m}

\int_{B_{r}^{m}}A(z,z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z=\frac{\omega_{m}r^{m+2}}{m+2}\mathrm{tr}(A),

\mathbb{R}^{m}

(n-1)

B^{\perp}

\int_{|z|\leq r}B^{\perp}(z,z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z=\frac{\omega_{n-1}r^{n+1}}{n+1}\mathrm{Ric}_{x}(v,v).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\int_{\tilde{D}_{\varepsilon}(x,v)}B(w)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\varepsilon}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{|z|\leq\rho(t)}B^{\perp}(z,z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z}}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=\frac{\omega_{n-1}\mathrm{Ric}_{x}(v,v)}{n+1}\int_{0}^{2\varepsilon}\rho(t)^{n+1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{2\omega_{n-1}}{(n+1)(n+2)}\varepsilon^{n+2}\mathrm{Ric}_{x}(v,v).

Next, the volume of the diamond is

\displaystyle{L}(\tilde{D}_{\varepsilon}(x,v))

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\varepsilon}{L}(B_{\rho(t)}^{n-1})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=\omega_{n-1}\int_{0}^{2\varepsilon}\rho(t)^{n-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

Appendix A
Geodesic deviation in Lorentzian geometry

The energy between two points
x,y
in a convex neighbourhood
\mathcal{U}
is defined as

\mathrm{E}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\langle\dot{\gamma}(t),\dot{\gamma}(t)\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,

where
\gamma:\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}\to M
is the unique geodesic contained in
U
joining
x
to
y
. A sketch of the proof of the following result is given in
[
1
, Lemma 4.12]
in the Riemannian setting, together with references indicating the remaining arguments. For the reader’s convenience, we present here a full proof.

Theorem A.1
.

Let
x\in M
,
v,w\in\T_{x}(M)
, and consider the geodesics
\gamma_{v}(t)\coloneqq\mathrm{exp}_{x}(tv)
and
\gamma_{w}(s)\coloneqq\mathrm{exp}_{x}(sw)
. Then, it holds, as
(t,s)\to(0,0)
, that

\mathrm{E}(\gamma_{v}(t),\gamma_{w}(s))=\frac{1}{2}\langle tv-sw,tv-sw\rangle-\frac{1}{6}\langle R(v,w)v,w\rangle\,t^{2}s^{2}+t^{2}s^{2}O((|t|+|s|)^{2}).

(48)

where the
O
-term is uniform for
v,w
ranging in compact subsets of
\T_{x}(M)
.

Proof.

Let
t,s>0
be small enough, and fix a convex normal neighbourhood of
x
such that the unique geodesic joining
\gamma_{v}(t)
to
\gamma_{w}(s)
is given by

c_{s,t}(u)\coloneqq\mathrm{exp}_{\gamma_{w}(s)}[u\ \mathrm{exp}_{\gamma_{w}(s)}^{-1}(\gamma_{v}(t))]=\mathrm{exp}_{\gamma_{v}(t)}[(1-u)\ \mathrm{exp}_{\gamma_{v}(t)}^{-1}(\gamma_{w}(s))],\quad u\in\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]},

which means that
\mathrm{E}(\gamma_{v}(t),\gamma_{w}(s))=\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\langle\dot{c}_{s,t}(u),\dot{c}_{s,t}(u)\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u
.
Note that
\langle\dot{c}_{s,t}(u),\dot{c}_{s,t}(u)\rangle
is constant in
u
, since

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}\langle\dot{c}_{s,t}(u),\dot{c}_{s,t}(u)\rangle=2\Big\langle\frac{\mathrm{D}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}\dot{c}_{s,t}(u),\dot{c}_{s,t}(u)\Big\rangle=0

and
\frac{\mathrm{D}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}\dot{c}_{s,t}(u)=0
, as
u\mapsto c_{s,t}(u)
is a geodesic. Moreover, since
\mathrm{exp}_{\gamma_{w}(s)}^{-1}(x)=-s\Gamma(\gamma_{w})_{0}^{s}[w]
, we obtain

\langle\dot{c}_{s,0}(u),\dot{c}_{s,0}(u)\rangle=\langle\dot{c}_{s,0}(0),\dot{c}_{s,0}(0)\rangle=s^{2}\langle\Gamma(\gamma_{w})_{0}^{s}[w],\Gamma(\gamma_{w})_{0}^{s}[w]\rangle=s^{2}\langle w,w\rangle.

(49)

Similarly, we have

\langle\dot{c}_{0,t}(u),\dot{c}_{0,t}(u)\rangle=t^{2}\langle v,v\rangle

(50)

Next, we compute

\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\mathrm{E}(\gamma_{v}(t),\gamma_{w}(s))=\int_{0}^{1}\Big\langle\frac{\mathrm{D}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}\dot{c}_{s,t}(u),\dot{c}_{s,t}(u)\Big\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u,

evaluated at
(s,t)=(0,t)
. We have

\Big\langle\frac{\mathrm{D}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}\dot{c}_{s,t}(u),\dot{c}_{s,t}(u)\Big\rangle=\Big\langle\frac{\mathrm{D}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}c_{s,t}(u),\dot{c}_{s,t}(u)\Big\rangle=\frac{\partial}{\partial u}\Big\langle\frac{\partial}{\partial s}c_{s,t}(u),\dot{c}_{s,t}(u)\Big\rangle,

where in the last equality we have used again that
u\mapsto c_{s,t}(u)
is a geodesic. In particular, we can write that

\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\mathrm{E}(\gamma_{v}(t),\gamma_{w}(s))=\Big\langle\frac{\partial}{\partial s}c_{s,t}(1),\dot{c}_{s,t}(1)\Big\rangle-\Big\langle\frac{\partial}{\partial s}c_{s,t}(0),\dot{c}_{s,t}(0)\Big\rangle.

Clearly,
c_{s,t}(1)=\gamma_{v}(t)
does not depend on
s
, while
\dot{c}_{0,t}(0)=tv
and
\tfrac{\partial}{\partial s}c_{s,t}(0)|_{s=0}=\dot{\gamma}_{w}(0)=w
. Thus, we obtain

\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\mathrm{E}(\gamma_{v}(t),\gamma_{w}(s))\big|_{(s,t)=(0,t)}=-t\langle v,w\rangle.

(51)

Similarly, we also have

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathrm{E}(\gamma_{v}(t),\gamma_{w}(s))\big|_{(s,t)=(0,t)}=-s\langle v,w\rangle.

(52)

Let

F(t,s)\coloneqq\mathrm{E}(\gamma_{v}(t),\gamma_{w}(s))-\frac{1}{2}\langle tv-sw,tv-sw\rangle.

With
Equations

49
,
50
,
51
and
52
, we have

F(t,0)=F(0,s)=\frac{\partial F}{\partial s}(t,0)=\frac{\partial F}{\partial t}(0,s)=0.

Since
F
is smooth, Hadamard’s lemma applied twice yields a smooth function
G
such that

F(t,s)=t^{2}s^{2}G(t,s).

We finally consider

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial s^{2}}\mathrm{E}(\gamma_{v}(t),\gamma_{w}(s))={}

\displaystyle\Big\langle\frac{\mathrm{D}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}c_{s,t}(1),\dot{c}_{s,t}(1)\Big\rangle+\Big\langle\frac{\partial}{\partial s}c_{s,t}(1),\frac{\mathrm{D}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}\dot{c}_{s,t}(1)\Big\rangle

\displaystyle-\Big\langle\frac{\mathrm{D}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}c_{s,t}(0),\dot{c}_{s,t}(0)\Big\rangle-\Big\langle\frac{\partial}{\partial s}c_{s,t}(0),\frac{\mathrm{D}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}\dot{c}_{s,t}(0)\Big\rangle

evaluated at
(s,t)=(0,t)
.
We know that
\dot{c}_{0,t}(0)=tv
,
c_{s,t}(1)=\gamma_{v}(t)
, and
c_{s,t}(0)=\gamma_{w}(s)
, so that we have

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial s^{2}}\mathrm{E}(\gamma_{v}(t),\gamma_{w}(s))\big|_{(s,t)=(0,t)}=-\Big\langle w,\frac{\mathrm{D}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}\dot{c}_{s,t}(0)\big|_{s=0}\Big\rangle=-\Big\langle w,\frac{\mathrm{D}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}V_{t}(0)\Big\rangle,

(53)

where
V_{t}(u)\coloneqq(\partial/\partial s)c_{s,t}(u)|_{s=0}
is a Jacobi field satisfying

\frac{\mathrm{D}^{2}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u^{2}}V_{t}(u)+R(V_{t}(u),\dot{c}_{0,t}(u))\dot{c}_{0,t}(u)=0

(54)

with
V_{t}(0)=w
and
V_{t}(1)=0
. Denote by
\mathord{\parallel_{0}^{t}}(u)
the parallel transport along
t\mapsto c_{0,t}(u)
, and consider
\tilde{V}_{t}(u)\coloneqq\mathord{\parallel_{t}^{0}}(u)V_{t}(u)\in\T_{x}(M)
. We can write, as
t\to 0
,

\tilde{V}_{t}(u)=\tilde{V}_{0}(u)+t\tilde{V}^{\prime}_{0}(u)+\frac{t^{2}}{2}\tilde{V}^{\prime\prime}_{0}(u)+\frac{t^{3}}{6}\tilde{V}^{\prime\prime\prime}_{0}(u)+O(t^{4}).

We denote by
(\dot{\ })
the derivative with respect to
u
and by
()^{\prime}
the derivative with respect to
t
. Applying the same parallel transport to the Jacobi equation
Equation

54
yields

\ddot{\tilde{V}}_{t}(u)+\tilde{R}_{t}(u)\tilde{V}_{t}(u)=0

(55)

where, for
Z\in\T_{x}(M)
, we denote

\tilde{R}_{t}(u)Z\coloneqq\mathord{\parallel_{t}^{0}}(u)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[R(\mathord{\parallel_{0}^{t}}(u)Z,\dot{c}_{0,t}(u))\dot{c}_{0,t}(u)}}\right].

Translating the initial conditions gives

\tilde{V}_{t}(0)=\mathord{\parallel_{t}^{0}}(0)w=\mathrm{Id}_{\T_{x}(M)}(w)=w,\quad\tilde{V}_{t}(1)=\mathord{\parallel_{t}^{0}}(1)0=0.

In particular, we have

\tilde{V}_{t}^{\prime}(0)=\tilde{V}_{t}^{\prime\prime}(0)=\tilde{V}_{t}^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)=\tilde{V}_{t}^{\prime}(1)=\tilde{V}_{t}^{\prime\prime}(1)=\tilde{V}_{t}^{\prime\prime\prime}(1)=0,\quad\text{ for all }t\in\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}.

(56)

Since it acts on a fixed vector space, we can also expand
\tilde{R}_{t}(u)
in
t
:

\tilde{R}_{t}(u)=\tilde{R}_{0}(u)+t\tilde{R}^{\prime}_{0}(u)+\frac{t^{2}}{2}\tilde{R}^{\prime\prime}_{0}(u)+\frac{t^{3}}{6}\tilde{R}^{\prime\prime\prime}_{0}(u)+O(t^{4}).

We also have
c_{0,t}(u)=\gamma_{v}(ut)
, and thus we can write, for
Z\in\T_{x}(M)
,

\displaystyle\tilde{R}_{t}(u)Z={}

\displaystyle t^{2}\mathord{\parallel_{t}^{0}}(u)\big[R(\mathord{\parallel_{0}^{t}}(u)Z,\dot{\gamma}_{v}(tu))\dot{\gamma}_{v}(tu)\big].

In particular, we have
\tilde{R}_{0}(u)=\tilde{R}^{\prime}_{0}(u)=0
, and
\tilde{R}^{\prime\prime}_{0}(u)Z=2R_{x}(Z,v)v
. Along the curve
t\mapsto c_{0,t}(u)=\gamma_{v}(tu)
, the vector fields
\mathord{\parallel_{0}^{t}}(u)Z
and
\dot{\gamma}_{v}(tu)
are both parallel. Using the product rule for the Riemann curvature tensor, we obtain

\displaystyle\tilde{R}^{\prime\prime\prime}_{0}(u)Z={}

\displaystyle 6\frac{\mathrm{D}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\Big[R(\mathord{\parallel_{0}^{t}}(u)Z,\dot{\gamma}_{v}(tu))\dot{\gamma}_{v}(tu)\Big]_{t=0}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle 6\Big[(\nabla_{v}R)_{x}(Z,v)v+R\Big(\frac{\mathrm{D}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\mathord{\parallel_{0}^{t}}(u)Z\big|_{t=0},v\Big)v

\displaystyle+R\Big(Z,\frac{\mathrm{D}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\dot{\gamma}_{v}(tu)\big|_{t=0}\Big)v+R\Big(\mathord{\parallel_{0}^{t}}(u)Z,v\Big)\frac{\mathrm{D}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\dot{\gamma}_{v}(tu)\big|_{t=0}\Big]

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle 6(\nabla_{v}R)_{x}(Z,v)v.

Substituting this into the Jacobi equation
Equation

55
gives

\ddot{\tilde{V}}_{0}(u)=0,\quad\ddot{\tilde{V}}^{\prime}_{0}(u)=0,\qquad\ddot{\tilde{V}}^{\prime\prime}_{0}(u)=-2R(\tilde{V}_{0}(u),v)v,

and

\ddot{\tilde{V}}^{\prime\prime\prime}_{0}(u)=-6\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[R(\tilde{V}^{\prime}_{0}(u),v)v+(\nabla_{v}R)_{x}(\tilde{V}_{0}(u),v)v}}\right].

These differential equations can be solved explicitly using
Equation

56
, and we obtain

\tilde{V}_{0}(u)=w(1-u),\quad\tilde{V}^{\prime}_{0}(u)=0,\quad\tilde{V}^{\prime\prime}_{0}(u)=\frac{1}{3}u(u-1)(u-2)R_{x}(w,v)v,

and

\tilde{V}^{\prime\prime\prime}_{0}(u)=u(u-1)(u-2)(\nabla_{v}R)_{x}(w,v)v.

In particular, this shows that

\dot{\tilde{V}}_{t}(0)=-w+\frac{t^{2}}{3}R_{x}(w,v)v-\frac{t^{3}}{6}(\nabla_{v}R)_{x}(w,v)v+O(t^{4}).

We deduce from
Equation

53
that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial s^{2}}\mathrm{E}(\gamma_{v}(t),\gamma_{w}(s))

\displaystyle\big|_{(s,t)=(0,t)}=-\langle\mathord{\parallel_{0}^{t}}(0)w,\dot{\tilde{V}}_{t}(0)\rangle=-\langle w,\dot{\tilde{V}}_{t}(0)\rangle

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\langle w,w\rangle-\frac{t^{2}}{3}\langle R_{x}(w,v)v,w\rangle+\frac{t^{3}}{6}\langle(\nabla_{v}R)_{x}(w,v)v,w\rangle+O(t^{4}).

Since
\partial_{s}^{2}F(t,0)=2t^{2}G(t,0)
, we deduce that

G(t,0)=-\frac{1}{6}\langle R_{x}(w,v)v,w\rangle+\frac{t}{12}\langle(\nabla_{v}R)_{x}(w,v)v,w\rangle+O(t^{2}).

It remains to show that the linear term vanishes. By Bianchi’s (second) identity and the symmetries of
\nabla R
, we have

\displaystyle\langle(\nabla_{v}R)_{x}(w,v)v,w\rangle={}

\displaystyle-\langle(\nabla_{v}R)_{x}(v,w)v,w\rangle

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\langle(\nabla_{v}R)_{x}(w,v)w,v\rangle+\langle(\nabla_{w}R)_{x}(v,v)w,v\rangle

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\langle(\nabla_{v}R)_{x}(w,v)w,v\rangle=-\langle(\nabla_{v}R)_{x}(w,v)v,w\rangle,

which implies that
\langle(\nabla_{v}R)_{x}(w,v)v,w\rangle=0
. Thus

G(t,0)=-\frac{1}{6}\langle R_{x}(w,v)v,w\rangle+O(t^{2}).

By symmetry, we also have

G(0,s)=-\frac{1}{6}\langle R_{x}(w,v)v,w\rangle+O(s^{2}).

These two expansions show that
\partial_{t}G(0,0)=\partial_{s}G(0,0)=0
. Since
G
is smooth, it follows that

G(t,s)=-\frac{1}{6}\langle R_{x}(w,v)v,w\rangle+O((|t|+|s|)^{2}).

Recalling that
F(t,s)=t^{2}s^{2}G(t,s)
, we obtain
Equation

48
.
∎
