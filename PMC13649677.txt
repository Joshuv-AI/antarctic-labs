Natural product-derived interventions for reversing multidrug resistance in resistant K562 leukemia cells: a systematic review and meta-analysis

Jingyi Sun, Changning Song, Yining Hua, Jiayin Pang, Shuaiting Sun

Abstract

BackgroundMultidrug resistance remains a major barrier to effective chemotherapy for leukemia. Natural product-derived interventions have been widely investigated as modulators of drug resistance in adriamycin- or doxorubicin-resistant K562 cell models, but their quantitative reversal effects remain heterogeneous.Methods
In vitro studies evaluating natural product-derived interventions in resistant K562 leukemia sublines were synthesized. The primary meta-analytic endpoint was IC50-based reversal fold (RF), analyzed as log (RF) using an inverse-variance random-effects model. Subgroup, sensitivity, leave-one-out, and small-study-effect analyses were performed. Mechanistic outcomes related to transporter modulation, drug accumulation, apoptosis, and signaling pathways were summarized descriptively.ResultsTwenty-eight in vitro studies were included, and 19 IC50-based study-level effects were entered into the primary meta-analysis. The pooled RF was 6.31 (95% CI: 4.77–8.36), representing an overall chemosensitization signal across the restricted quantitative dataset. Subgroup analysis showed pooled RF values of 5.71 for K562/A02, 4.90 for K562/ADM, and 8.91 for K562/ADR. Heterogeneity was high (I2 = 97.7%), indicating substantial variability across resistant-cell sublines, intervention types, substrate drugs, and experimental conditions. Sensitivity analyses confirmed that the pooled effect remained positive, although exclusion of high-effect studies reduced the pooled RF to 4.51. Trim-and-fill analysis produced an adjusted pooled RF of 5.48 (95% CI: 3.87–7.76). Across individual studies, reported mechanisms included ABC transporter modulation, increased intracellular anthracycline accumulation, reduced efflux, restoration of apoptosis, and modulation of PI3K/Akt, NF-κB, and Nrf2/ARE signaling.ConclusionAcross the heterogeneous studies, the common IC50-based endpoint showed an overall chemosensitization signal; however, this pooled summary does not imply a shared pharmacological action or potency across chemically distinct metabolites. Effect magnitude and mechanisms remained specific to individual metabolites, resistant-cell sublines, substrate drugs, doses, and molecular targets.

Introduction

Multidrug resistance (MDR) remains a major experimental and therapeutic barrier in leukemia chemotherapy, particularly in resistant K562-derived models used to study acquired resistance to adriamycin and doxorubicin. In these models, resistant sublines such as K562/A02, K562/ADM, and K562/ADR are widely used to evaluate whether candidate agents can restore intracellular drug retention, reduce IC50 values, and resensitize cells to anthracycline-based cytotoxicity (; ; ). Because these sublines reproduce key features of drug-resistant leukemia cells, they provide a useful in vitro platform for investigating both transporter-mediated efflux and broader resistance-associated signaling mechanisms. The continuous search for novel chemotherapeutic agents has led to systematic reviews of various chemical derivatives, such as s-triazine derivatives, for their antitumor activities ().

K562 cells are derived from chronic myeloid leukemia and therefore do not fully recapitulate current clinical management of CML, which is dominated by BCR-ABL1 tyrosine kinase inhibitors such as imatinib. Acquired resistance to targeted therapies is also a major challenge in other malignancies, such as non-small cell lung cancer resistance to EGFR-TKIs mediated by non-coding RNAs (; ). However, the rationale for using resistant K562 sublines in this review is specifically experimental rather than therapeutic-first-line modeling. K562/ADM, K562/ADR, and K562/A02 cells are established in vitro systems for studying acquired multidrug resistance, particularly ABC transporter activity, anthracycline efflux, intracellular drug retention, apoptosis escape, and resistance-associated survival signaling. Therefore, these models are appropriate for evaluating whether natural product-derived interventions can modulate MDR mechanisms. Still, the findings should not be interpreted as direct evidence to replace or compete with TKI-based CML therapy.

The most frequently investigated mechanism in adriamycin- or doxorubicin-resistant K562 cells is ATP-binding cassette transporter-mediated drug efflux, especially P-gp/ABCB1 (MDR1). Earlier K562/A02 and K562/ADR studies consistently linked resistance reversal to reduced P-gp activity, increased ADR/DOX or Rh123 accumulation, or suppression of MDR1/P-gp expression (; ; ; ; ; ). However, MDR in these models is not exclusively P-gp-dependent. MRP1/ABCC1 has been implicated in wogonin- and timosaponin A-III-mediated reversal, whereas BCRP/ABCG2 and additional resistance-associated transporters have been reported in later studies involving more complex resistant phenotypes (; ; ; ; ; ). Therefore, MDR reversal in K562 cells should be understood as a transporter-centered, yet mechanistically heterogeneous, phenomenon.

Natural product-derived interventions have attracted sustained interest as MDR modulators because of their chemical diversity and capacity to act through multiple biological mechanisms. The structural diversity of natural products requires advanced analytical approaches for dereplication and identification, such as integrated molecular networking workflows (). The eligible studies in this review investigated structurally diverse agents, including marine or shellfish-derived protein extract, diterpenoids, bisbibenzyl phenolics, coumarins, flavonoids, steroidal saponins, anthraquinones, glycoalkaloids, quassinoids, lignans, sesquiterpene lactones, triterpenoids, diarylheptanoids, and fungal metabolites. Several of these metabolites were evaluated as alternatives or comparators to classical MDR-modulating agents such as verapamil, which have been limited by toxicity, pharmacokinetic constraints, or poor translational suitability in prior MDR-reversal contexts (; ; ; ; ). This pharmacological diversity supports summarizing a common assay-level chemosensitization endpoint while retaining metabolite-specific pharmacological interpretation rather than treating MDR reversal as a uniform class effect.

Mechanistically, natural product-mediated MDR reversal in resistant K562 models extends beyond direct efflux inhibition. Some agents primarily inhibit transporter function, as shown by increased intracellular ADR/DOX or Rh123 accumulation after treatment with riccardin F, pakyonol, and Euphorbia factor L1 (; ; ). Other interventions reduce transporter expression or transcriptional activity, including archangelicin-mediated MDR1/P-gp downregulation, wogonin-mediated MRP1 suppression through Nrf2/ARE inhibition, and timosaponin A-III-mediated reduction of MDR1 and MRP1 through PI3K/Akt pathway inhibition (; ; ). Additional studies link MDR reversal to apoptosis restoration, autophagy-associated mechanisms, NF-κB/IκB-α signaling, PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling, JNK-associated regulation, CXCL12/CXCR4 survival signaling, and Nrf2-centered resistance control (; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ). These overlapping mechanisms necessitate a structured synthesis.

An ethnopharmacological rationale also underpins the present synthesis, as many of the reviewed MDR-modulating metabolites are derived from medicinal plants, fungi, or other natural sources with documented traditional use. Representative validated sources include Ailanthus altissima (Mill.) Swingle [Simaroubaceae], Scutellaria baicalensis Georgi [Lamiaceae], Pueraria montana var. lobata (Willd.) Maesen and S.M.Almeida ex Sanjappa & Predeep [Fabaceae], Cnidium monnieri (L.) Cusson [Apiaceae], Rheum palmatum L. [Polygonaceae], Anemarrhena asphodeloides Bunge [Asparagaceae], Schisandra chinensis (Turcz.) Baill. [Schisandraceae], Ganoderma lucidum (Curtis) P.Karst. [Ganodermataceae], and Tripterygium wilfordii Hook.f. [Celastraceae]; for Dioscorea, the original evidence did not consistently permit species-level assignment, so the source is retained as Dioscorea spp. [Dioscoreaceae] without inference. These sources have been described in traditional or ethnomedicinal systems for inflammatory, chronic, metabolic, febrile, tonic, or disease-resistance-related indications (; ; ; ). Although these traditional uses do not directly correspond to multidrug resistance or K562 leukemia models, they provide a source-based pharmacological rationale for examining whether their bioactive metabolites can modulate experimentally defined resistance mechanisms, including ABC-transporter activity, intracellular anthracycline retention, apoptosis restoration, autophagy regulation, and NF-κB, PI3K/Akt, and Nrf2/ARE signaling pathways.

Despite the number of individual in vitro studies, the evidence base remains fragmented across different resistant K562 sublines, natural product-derived metabolite classes, dose ranges, exposure durations, assay platforms, and endpoint definitions. Some studies report directly extractable ADM/DOX IC50 or reversal-fold outcomes. In contrast, others provide graph-based sensitization data, non-anthracycline substrate assays, transporter-function outcomes, apoptosis endpoints, or pathway-level evidence. This heterogeneity has limited direct comparisons across studies and left uncertainty about the overall magnitude of natural product-mediated MDR reversal in adriamycin-resistant K562 models.

Therefore, this meta-analysis aimed to synthesize original full-text in vitro studies evaluating natural product-derived interventions for reversing MDR in adriamycin- or doxorubicin-resistant K562 cells. The primary objective was to summarize the direction and magnitude of the common ADM/DOX IC50-based reversal endpoint among studies meeting strict comparability criteria; the pooled estimate was not intended to represent a shared mechanism, potency, or class-wide pharmacological effect across chemically distinct metabolites. Secondary objectives were to characterize resistant K562 models, classify natural product-derived interventions, summarize modulation of transporters and resistance markers, evaluate cell death and signaling pathways associated with MDR reversal, and assess methodological concerns influencing quantitative synthesis.

Methods

Study design and reporting framework

This study was designed as a systematic review and meta-analysis of in vitro experimental studies evaluating natural product–derived interventions for reversing multidrug resistance in adriamycin- or doxorubicin-resistant K562 leukemia cells. The methodology followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 framework, with adaptations appropriate for preclinical in vitro studies. The review question was structured around four core elements: a resistant K562 cell model, a natural product–derived intervention, an appropriate resistant-cell comparator, and quantitative multidrug resistance-reversal outcomes. The review framework was aligned with the Frontiers Four Pillars of Best Practice for Ethnopharmacology by extracting traditional/source context, pharmacological model, dose and control information, preparation/source information when reported, taxonomic validation, evidence hierarchy, and quality-assessment variables, while restricting interpretation to the eligible in vitro K562 MDR evidence.

For the quantitative synthesis, the primary meta-analytic effect size was defined as the log-transformed reversal fold [log (RF)] derived from IC50-based adriamycin/doxorubicin resistance-reversal outcomes, allowing pooled estimation of MDR-reversal effects across eligible studies.

The meta-analysis was considered appropriate only for the restricted subset of studies that reported comparable IC50-based ADM/ADR/DOX reversal-fold outcomes with estimable variance. Because the included natural product-derived interventions differed in chemical class, source material, resistant K562 subline, substrate drug, dose, exposure duration, and dominant mechanism, the pooled estimate was interpreted only as an exploratory summary of a common assay-level MDR-sensitization endpoint, not as evidence for a shared mechanism, potency ranking, or uniform pharmacological effect across metabolites. Studies that were mechanistically informative but insufficiently comparable for RF-based pooling were therefore retained only for structured qualitative synthesis.

Literature search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted to identify eligible full-text original studies published between 1 January 2010 and 31 December 2025. The search covered PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science Core Collection, Embase, Cochrane Library, ScienceDirect, SpringerLink, Wiley Online Library, Taylor & Francis Online, SAGE Journals, Google Scholar, Dimensions, OpenAlex, CNKI, Wanfang Data, and VIP Chinese Science and Technology Journal Database. Reference lists of eligible full-text articles were also manually screened to identify additional relevant studies.

The search strategy combined terms related to K562 adriamycin/doxorubicin-resistant leukemia cells, multidrug resistance, natural product–derived interventions, and quantitative MDR-reversal outcomes suitable for meta-analysis. The main search terms included: “K562,” “K-562,” “K562/ADM,” “K562/ADR,” “K562/A02,” “adriamycin-resistant K562,” “doxorubicin-resistant K562,” “adriamycin,” “doxorubicin,” “multidrug resistance,” “MDR,” “drug resistance,” “chemoresistance,” “natural product,” “natural compound,” “phytochemical,” “plant extract,” “botanical extract,” “traditional medicine,” “traditional Chinese medicine,” “flavonoid,” “alkaloid,” “polyphenol,” “saponin,” “terpenoid,” “P-glycoprotein,” “P-gp,” “MDR1,” “ABCB1,” “MRP1,” “ABCC1,” “BCRP,” “ABCG2,” “drug efflux,” “intracellular accumulation,” “IC50,” “reversal,” “reversal fold,” “reversal index,” “sensitization,” “chemosensitization,” “standard deviation,” “standard error,” and “confidence interval.” Boolean operators were adapted to each database’s syntax. Review articles, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, editorials, letters, commentaries, conference abstracts, book chapters, theses, dissertations, patents, protocols, and other non-original publications were excluded at both database filtering and manual screening stages.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they met all predefined criteria: original full-text peer-reviewed in vitro experimental article; publication between 1 January 2010 and 31 December 2025; use of adriamycin- or doxorubicin-resistant K562 leukemia cells, including K562/ADM, K562/ADR, K562/A02, or clearly defined equivalent resistant sublines; evaluation of a clearly natural product–derived intervention; assessment of multidrug resistance reversal, resistance attenuation, or chemosensitization; inclusion of an appropriate resistant-cell comparator; and reporting of quantitative data relevant to MDR reversal. Studies were eligible for the quantitative meta-analysis when they reported directly extractable or derivable IC50-based reversal-fold data for ADM, ADR, or DOX, together with sufficient information to estimate the log-transformed reversal fold [log (RF)] and its sampling variance, including standard deviation, standard error, confidence interval, number of independent experiments, or graphically extractable error estimates. Studies that met the broader eligibility criteria but lacked sufficient variance information for meta-analysis were retained only for qualitative and mechanistic synthesis. The complete eligibility framework is summarized in .

Studies were excluded if they were published outside the predefined period; were reviews, meta-analyses, editorials, letters, conference abstracts, theses, patents, protocols, or other non-original publications; were not full-text articles; did not use adriamycin- or doxorubicin-resistant K562 cells; used parental K562 cells only; used other resistant cancer cell lines without separately extractable K562-resistant data; did not evaluate MDR reversal; used synthetic, gene-based, antibody-based, or pharmaceutical interventions as the primary MDR-reversing agent; lacked an appropriate comparator; provided insufficient quantitative data for either qualitative synthesis or meta-analysis; reported only molecular docking, network pharmacology, or bioinformatics prediction without in vitro validation; contained duplicate or overlapping datasets; or had been retracted. Natural product–nanocarrier formulations were not included in the strict main analysis unless the independent effect of the natural product could be clearly isolated.

Study selection

All retrieved records were imported into a reference management file, and duplicate records were removed prior to screening. Titles and abstracts were first screened against the eligibility criteria. Potentially relevant records were then assessed by full-text review. Studies were selected only when the resistant K562 cell model, natural product–derived intervention, comparator, and extractable MDR-reversal outcome were clearly identifiable. Disagreements during screening were resolved by rechecking the full text against the predefined criteria. The PRISMA-based study-selection flow diagram is shown in . In the flow diagram, quantitative synthesis refers to all included studies contributing quantitative experimental or mechanistic MDR-reversal data. In contrast, the primary meta-analysis was restricted to studies with directly extractable or derivable IC50-based reversal-fold data suitable for log (RF)-based pooling.

The PRISMA selection process identified 863 records, including 826 from database searching and 37 from citation tracking and manual reference screening. After removing 246 duplicates and excluding 81 records based on publication-type filters, 536 records underwent title and abstract screening. Of these, 398 were excluded as irrelevant to the review question. Full texts were sought for 138 reports, of which 12 could not be retrieved. A total of 126 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, and 98 were excluded for predefined reasons. Finally, 28 original full-text in vitro studies were included in the qualitative and quantitative synthesis. Among these, 19 studies reported directly extractable or derivable ADM/ADR/DOX IC50-based reversal-fold outcomes with sufficient information to estimate log (RF) and its variance, and were therefore included in the primary random-effects meta-analysis. The remaining studies contributed to the structured quantitative or mechanistic synthesis but were not pooled in the primary RF-based meta-analysis because their outcomes were not directly comparable to the primary endpoint or lacked sufficient information on variance.

Data extraction

Data were extracted using a standardized extraction form. Extracted information included first author, publication year, country, journal, resistant K562 subline, resistance phenotype, natural product or extract name, source of the intervention, intervention dose or concentration, exposure duration, comparator group, co-administered chemotherapeutic agent, assay type, primary MDR-reversal outcome, secondary mechanistic outcomes, and quantitative values required for meta-analysis. For studies eligible for the primary quantitative meta-analysis, extracted data included the IC50 value in resistant-cell control or ADM/ADR/DOX-only conditions, the IC50 value after co-treatment with the natural product–derived intervention, the reported or calculated reversal fold, log-transformed reversal fold [log (RF)], standard deviation, standard error, confidence interval, number of independent experiments, and any information required to estimate the sampling variance of log (RF). To address pharmacological comparability, we additionally extracted the model type, material type, tested dose range, selected MDR-reversal concentration, minimal active or subtoxic concentration when reported, positive and negative controls, exposure duration, and the main pharmacological endpoint for each included study. When study-level variance was not directly reported, it was derived from available standard deviations, standard errors, confidence intervals, p-values, replicate numbers, or graphically extractable error estimates where methodologically appropriate.

To further strengthen reproducibility, additional methodological parameters were extracted when available, including assay platform, substrate drug identity, concentration range, selected non-cytotoxic reversal concentration, exposure time, positive and negative controls, replicate number, variance source, IC50-estimation basis, and the specific endpoint used for RF calculation. For statistical synthesis, the extracted parameters also included the effect-size scale, standard-error source, heterogeneity statistics, subgroup variable, and sensitivity-analysis condition.

When studies reported multiple concentrations or time points, data were preferentially extracted from the concentration and exposure condition identified by the authors as effective and non-lethally cytotoxic, or from the condition most directly linked to MDR reversal. For this review, an effective and non-lethally cytotoxic concentration was operationally defined as a concentration that produced measurable MDR reversal while preserving ≥80% cell viability when the intervention was used alone, corresponding approximately to an IC10–IC20 or subtoxic range. When a study did not report a numerical viability threshold, the concentration explicitly defined by the original authors as non-toxic, subtoxic, or non-lethally cytotoxic was selected; otherwise, the evidence was retained for descriptive synthesis only.

When multiple eligible intervention arms were available within a single study, one primary arm was selected for the main meta-analysis to avoid unit-of-analysis error, while alternative arms were retained for sensitivity or descriptive analyses. When numerical values were presented only graphically, values were extracted from figures using calibrated digital extraction, provided that axes and error bars were sufficiently clear. Studies with extractable quantitative MDR-reversal data but insufficient information to estimate variance for log (RF) were retained for qualitative and mechanistic synthesis but were not included in the primary pooled meta-analysis.

In addition to experimental MDR-reversal variables, ethnopharmacological information was extracted when available, including the traditional medicinal source, documented traditional use, plant/fungal/animal origin, major metabolite evaluated, and the relationship between the traditional source and the tested MDR-reversal mechanism.

Taxonomic information for biological source materials was checked against recognized taxonomic databases, including Plants of the World Online, World Flora Online, IPNI, Species Fungorum/MycoBank, and MolluscaBase/WoRMS, and the accepted or reported scientific name, authority, family, and validation status were extracted when available. Because this was a secondary review, no unreported species identity, botanical drug identity, preparation detail, or taxonomic authority was inferred; missing source information was recorded transparently as not reported or as the highest taxonomic level supported by the original article.

Outcomes

The primary meta-analytic outcome was reversal of multidrug resistance in adriamycin- or doxorubicin-resistant K562 cells, expressed as the IC50-based reversal fold (RF). RF was defined as the ratio of the IC50 value in resistant-cell control or ADM/ADR/DOX-only conditions to the IC50 value after co-treatment with the natural product–derived intervention. The primary effect size for meta-analysis was the log-transformed reversal fold [log (RF)]. Eligible primary endpoints included directly reported or derivable ADM, ADR, or DOX IC50 reduction, reversal fold, or reversal index outcomes in resistant K562 sublines.

Secondary outcomes included changes in cell viability after ADM/DOX exposure, enhancement of apoptosis, increase in intracellular adriamycin/doxorubicin accumulation, inhibition of drug efflux, and downregulation of MDR-associated markers such as P-glycoprotein, MDR1/ABCB1, MRP1/ABCC1, and BCRP/ABCG2. Additional mechanistic outcomes included changes in signaling pathways related to MDR modulation, including PI3K/Akt, NF-κB, Nrf2/ARE, apoptosis-related proteins, autophagy-related markers, and ferroptosis-related indicators, when reported with quantitative data. These secondary outcomes were synthesized narratively or descriptively when measurement scales were not sufficiently comparable for formal pooling.

Quality assessment and risk of bias

Evidence was hierarchically classified before synthesis to avoid combining fundamentally different levels of pharmacological evidence. Because the review question focused on experimentally defined MDR reversal in resistant K562 sublines, only in vitro K562-based evidence was eligible for inclusion. Animal-only, human clinical, ex vivo-only, and computational-only studies were excluded and were not combined with cell-based evidence. Within the included in vitro evidence, IC50-based ADM/ADR/DOX reversal-fold outcomes with estimable variance were assigned the highest quantitative level and entered into the primary meta-analysis, whereas screening-only, mechanistic, alternative-substrate, or variance-incomplete studies were retained for descriptive or mechanistic synthesis only. The evidence hierarchy and quality-assessment framework are summarized in . For Level 1 evidence, dose justification included whether the selected MDR-reversal concentration was reported as non-toxic, subtoxic, IC10–IC20, or compatible with preserved cell viability.

Methodological quality and risk of bias were assessed using a preclinical in vitro assessment framework adapted for cell-based experimental studies. The assessment considered clarity of cell-line identity, confirmation of resistant phenotype, description of intervention source and preparation, appropriateness of comparator group, reporting of dose and exposure duration, replication of experiments, transparency of assay methods, completeness of quantitative outcome reporting, consistency between methods and results, and disclosure of funding or conflicts of interest. Particular attention was given to whether IC50-based outcomes, replicate numbers, standard deviations, standard errors, confidence intervals, or graphically extractable error estimates were sufficiently reported to be included in the quantitative meta-analysis. Studies with unclear resistant-cell identity, no valid comparator, insufficient quantitative reporting for the relevant synthesis, or retraction were excluded before synthesis.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

Eligible studies were summarized qualitatively and quantitatively according to outcome comparability and data completeness. The primary quantitative meta-analysis focused on studies reporting directly extractable or derivable ADM, ADR, or DOX IC50-based reversal-fold or reversal-index outcomes in resistant K562 sublines. For this endpoint, reversal effects were analyzed on a logarithmic scale using log (RF), where RF was defined as the ratio of the resistant-cell IC50 under control or ADM/ADR/DOX-only conditions to the IC50 after co-treatment with the natural product–derived intervention.

The main statistical parameters predefined for the quantitative synthesis were log (RF) as the effect-size metric, inverse-variance weighting, random-effects pooling, 95% confidence intervals, Cochran’s Q, I2, τ2, prediction interval, subgroup analysis by resistant K562 subline, leave-one-out influence analysis, Egger’s regression test, and trim-and-fill adjustment when the number of available studies was sufficient.

An inverse-variance random-effects meta-analysis was performed using log (RF) as the primary effect size. Study-level standard errors and variances were extracted directly when reported or derived from available standard deviations, standard errors, confidence intervals, p-values, replicate numbers, or graphically extractable error estimates where appropriate. Between-study variance was estimated using a random-effects model, and pooled effects were back-transformed from log (RF) to RF and reported as pooled reversal-fold estimates with 95% confidence intervals.

When multiple concentrations or intervention arms were reported, the primary extraction used the highest concentration meeting the predefined non-lethally cytotoxic criterion, preferably ≥80% viability or an IC10–IC20/subtoxic range, and showing a measurable ADM/ADR/DOX IC50 reduction. To avoid a unit-of-analysis error, only one primary intervention arm per study was included in the main meta-analysis. Alternative eligible arms were retained for sensitivity or descriptive analyses.

Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test, I2 statistic, and between-study variance (τ2). A 95% prediction interval was calculated to estimate the expected range of effects in comparable future in vitro studies. Subgroup meta-analyses were performed according to resistant K562 subline, including K562/A02, K562/ADM, and K562/ADR or equivalent adriamycin-resistant variants, when sufficient studies were available. Additional subgroup analyses by intervention class and dominant MDR mechanism were considered when data were adequate. Differences between subgroups were assessed using a test for subgroup differences.

Sensitivity analyses examined the robustness of the primary pooled RF estimate by excluding very large RF or reversal-index estimates, potential overlapping metabolite evidence, approximated or graph-extracted estimates, minimal-effect estimates, and alternative dose-selection assumptions. Leave-one-out influence analysis was performed by sequentially removing each study and recalculating the random-effects pooled RF to evaluate whether any single study drove the overall estimate.

Small-study effects and potential publication bias were assessed using funnel-plot inspection and Egger’s regression test when at least 10 studies were available for the primary meta-analysis. Trim-and-fill analysis was conducted as an exploratory assessment of the possible influence of funnel-plot asymmetry on the pooled estimate. Outcomes that were graph-based without extractable variance, non-ADM/ADR/DOX-based, or not directly comparable with the primary IC50/RF endpoint were synthesized narratively or used as secondary mechanistic evidence. Mechanistic outcomes, including transporter expression, intracellular drug accumulation, efflux inhibition, apoptosis, autophagy, and signaling pathway modulation, were summarized using a structured qualitative synthesis because the assay platforms and measurement scales were heterogeneous.

Results

Characteristics of included studies

A total of 28 original full-text in vitro studies were included in the review, all of which evaluated natural product-derived interventions in adriamycin- or doxorubicin-resistant K562 leukemia cell models. As summarized in , the included studies covered three main resistant K562 subline categories: K562/A02, K562/ADM, and K562/ADR or equivalent adriamycin-resistant K562 variants. The studies were published between 2010 and 2025. They collectively represented a broad experimental literature on multidrug resistance reversal, including direct IC50-based chemosensitization assays, intracellular drug-accumulation assays, transporter expression analyses, apoptosis measurements, and signaling pathway investigations.

According to the predefined evidence hierarchy, all included studies were in vitro cell-based investigations. Nineteen studies provided Level 1 IC50-based RF evidence suitable for primary meta-analysis. In contrast, the remaining studies contributed Level 2–4 descriptive, mechanistic, alternative-substrate, or contextual evidence. No animal or human clinical evidence was included; therefore, all conclusions are restricted to preclinical in vitro evidence of MDR reversal.

provides the study-level experimental context for each included article, including the resistant K562 model, the intervention identity, the natural-product class or source, the major assay platforms, the comparator conditions, and the principal mechanistic contribution. Most studies used MTT or CCK-8 viability assays to quantify changes in drug sensitivity, commonly supported by flow cytometry, Rh123 or anthracycline-accumulation assays, RT-PCR/qPCR, Western blotting, apoptosis assays, luciferase reporter assays, molecular docking, or pathway-specific analyses.

The distribution of resistant-cell models showed that K562/A02 was the most frequently used experimental system, followed by K562/ADR and K562/ADM. This pattern is visually summarized in , where K562/A02 accounted for the largest number of included studies. The predominance of K562/A02 studies indicates that much of the available in vitro evidence was generated in a P-gp/MDR1-associated adriamycin-resistant background, particularly in studies focused on drug efflux, Rh123 retention, and reversal-fold estimation. K562/ADM studies were fewer but mechanistically diverse, often incorporating analyses of apoptosis, PI3K/Akt, NF-κB, JNK, or the CXCL12/CXCR4 pathway. K562/ADR studies were also well represented and included several more recent studies evaluating transporter function, autophagy, Nrf2 regulation, and leukemia stem-like resistant subpopulations.

The interventions were chemically diverse and included protein extracts, diterpenoids, bisbibenzyl phenolics, coumarin-related metabolites, flavonoids and isoflavones, steroidal saponins, polyphenols, anthraquinones, glycoalkaloids, quassinoids, lignans, sesquiterpene lactones, fungal metabolites, triterpenoids, and diarylheptanoids. As shown in , flavonoid/isoflavone metabolites formed the largest intervention category, followed by coumarin-related plant-derived metabolites, steroidal saponins, and polyphenol/stilbene/gallotannin metabolites. This distribution indicates that a single natural-product scaffold does not dominate the MDR-reversal literature; rather, it spans multiple chemical families with potentially distinct transporter-binding, transcriptional, apoptotic, and signaling effects.

Natural product–derived interventions and resistant K562 models

The included studies evaluated a broad spectrum of natural product-derived interventions across resistant K562 sublines, with substantial variation in metabolite class, exposure concentration, treatment duration, and chemotherapeutic substrate. As summarized in , the resistant models included K562/A02, K562/ADM, K562/ADR, and equivalent adriamycin- or doxorubicin-resistant K562 variants. Most interventions were tested in combination with ADM, ADR, or DOX. In contrast, a smaller subset used alternative resistance contexts, such as VCR or bestatin, or assessed direct resistance-overcoming activity rather than conventional anthracycline co-treatment.

K562/A02 was frequently used for transporter-focused MDR-reversal assays, particularly those involving P-gp/MDR1-mediated resistance, Rh123 retention, and anthracycline accumulation. Studies using this model evaluated structurally diverse interventions, including triptolide, riccardin F, pakyonol, archangelicin, coumarin-related metabolites, wogonin, resveratrol, ailanthone, schizandrol A, Woodwardia unigemmata (Makino) Nakai [Aspleniaceae] flavonoids, Ustilago maydis metabolites, and ethyl lucidenates A. As shown in , these studies commonly used MTT or CCK-8 viability assays in combination with drug-accumulation or efflux assays, supporting the suitability of K562/A02 for evaluating efflux-associated chemosensitization.

K562/ADM studies were fewer but mechanistically broader. This subgroup included interventions such as Haishengsu, oroxylin A, timosaponin A-III, osthole, emodin, solanine, and parthenolide. The K562/ADM studies often incorporated transporter expression analysis alongside apoptosis or signaling endpoints, including PI3K/Akt, NF-κB, JNK, and CXCL12/CXCR4-related mechanisms. shows that this model was particularly useful for examining not only drug-efflux reversal but also survival-pathway suppression and restoration of apoptosis in ADM-resistant cells.

K562/ADR or equivalent adriamycin-resistant K562 models were also well represented, including several more recent studies. Interventions in this subgroup included Euphorbia factor L1, dioscin, costunolide, puerarin, resveratrol, pentagalloyl glucose, S-20, DHH, and TMI. These studies frequently emphasized modulation of functional transporters, high-resistance phenotypes, autophagy-related mechanisms, Nrf2-mediated regulation, or drug-resistant leukemia stem-like cell contexts. As indicated in , the K562/ADR evidence base, therefore, contributed substantially to both quantitative RF-based synthesis and mechanistic interpretation of advanced MDR phenotypes.

The concentration ranges used for MDR assessment varied markedly across studies, from nanomolar exposure for triptolide to micromolar or microgram-per-milliliter concentrations for most other natural products. Exposure durations also differed, ranging from short accumulation assays of approximately 1–4 h to viability, apoptosis, or signaling assays lasting 24–72 h. This variability reflects the diversity of assay objectives and pharmacological properties across intervention classes. It also supports structured synthesis rather than the direct pooling of all experimental conditions without endpoint-specific extraction rules.

As shown in , the included studies were uniformly conducted in vitro but varied substantially in material type, dose selection, exposure duration, comparator design, and endpoint structure; therefore, only studies with comparable IC50-based ADM/ADR/DOX reversal data and estimable variance were included in the primary meta-analysis. In contrast, studies with incomplete reporting of pharmacology or variance were retained for descriptive and mechanistic synthesis.

As shown in , each biological source was checked taxonomically and is reported with the accepted or source-supported scientific name, authority, and family whenever species-level identification was available. When the original article reported only a broad source category, a genus-level source, or a purified metabolite not linked to one species, no species was inferred and the limitation is stated explicitly.

Primary MDR-Reversal outcomes

Before the primary variance-weighted meta-analysis, all extractable RF or reversal-index estimates were descriptively examined to show the distribution of reported MDR-reversal effects across resistant K562 sublines. The primary MDR-reversal outcome was first descriptively examined using study-level ADM/DOX or ADR reversal-fold estimates extracted from studies with directly usable IC50-based outcomes. Modulation of MDR transporters and resistance-related markers

The modulation of MDR transporters and resistance-associated markers represented a central mechanistic theme across the included studies. As summarized in , most interventions affected at least one resistance-related transporter, efflux phenotype, or upstream signaling axis involved in drug resistance. The most frequently investigated target was P-gp/MDR1, although several studies also assessed MRP1, BCRP, Nrf2/ARE signaling, NF-κB, PI3K/Akt, JNK, ERK/autophagy, and CXCL12/CXCR4-related mechanisms. This pattern indicates that natural product-derived interventions did not operate through a single uniform mechanism, but instead modulated MDR through overlapping transporter-level, transcriptional, functional-efflux, and cell-survival pathways.

P-gp/MDR1 regulation was the dominant transporter-related mechanism. shows that several interventions, including triptolide, archangelicin, timosaponin A-III, dioscin, osthole, resveratrol, emodin, ailanthone, schizandrol A, costunolide, puerarin, pentagalloyl glucose, parthenolide, and TMI, were associated with reduced P-gp or MDR1 expression, transcriptional activity, or transporter-linked resistance. This finding is consistent with the central role of ABCB1/P-gp in anthracycline efflux in resistant K562 models. Importantly, the evidence was not restricted to expression changes; several studies also demonstrated increased intracellular drug retention or reduced efflux, supporting a functional reversal of the MDR phenotype.

A distinction emerged between interventions that reduced transporter expression and those that mainly inhibited transporter function. As shown in , P-gp/MDR1 expression was downregulated across a large subset of studies. In contrast, functional P-gp inhibition without clear protein downregulation was observed with riccardin F, pakyonol, Euphorbia factor L1, ethyl lucidenates A, and DHH. This distinction is mechanistically important because it separates transcriptional or translational suppression of efflux proteins from direct functional interference with transporter activity. Agents in the latter group increased intracellular substrate accumulation or decreased efflux while leaving transporter abundance largely unchanged, suggesting transporter blockade or altered substrate handling rather than reduced protein expression.

MRP1 and BCRP also contributed to the MDR-reversal landscape. indicates that MRP1/ABCC1 downregulation or functional modulation was reported in studies involving wogonin, timosaponin A-III, solanine, and S-20, while BCRP/ABCG2 modulation was implicated in studies involving S-20, parthenolide, and TMI. These findings broaden the mechanistic scope beyond P-gp-centered reversal and suggest that selected natural products may target multidrug resistance through multiple ABC transporters. This is particularly relevant for resistant phenotypes in which P-gp alone may not fully explain drug resistance, or where antioxidant defense pathways and stem-like resistant subpopulations contribute to reduced chemosensitivity.

Functional substrate-accumulation and efflux assays provided important confirmatory evidence for transporter modulation. shows that many studies measured intracellular accumulation of ADM, ADR, DOX, Rh123, CFDA, THP, VCR, or bestatin. Increased anthracycline accumulation was reported across several K562/A02, K562/ADM, and K562/ADR studies, indicating restoration of intracellular drug exposure after treatment with natural product-derived interventions. In addition, Rh123, CFDA, THP, VCR, and bestatin transport assays supported functional modulation of P-gp- or MRP-related efflux. These assays strengthen the interpretation that the observed IC50 reductions were not merely nonspecific cytotoxic effects, but were often linked to restoration of intracellular drug retention.

Pathway-level regulation was also prominent. shows that NF-κB/IκB-α signaling was linked to MDR1/P-gp transcriptional suppression and apoptosis sensitization, particularly in studies involving oroxylin A, dioscin, puerarin, and parthenolide. PI3K/Akt signaling was another recurrent pathway, implicated in transporter regulation, survival signaling, and apoptosis resistance in studies involving oroxylin A, wogonin, timosaponin A-III, osthole, ailanthone, schizandrol A, costunolide, and resveratrol. Nrf2/ARE inhibition was associated with reduced MRP1, P-gp, BCRP, and antioxidant-defense signaling, while JNK, ERK/autophagy, and CXCL12/CXCR4 mechanisms were more specific to intervention contexts.

Cell-death and signaling pathways associated with MDR reversal

Cell death restoration and modulation of signaling pathways were recurrent mechanistic features of MDR reversal across the included resistant K562 models. As summarized in , the reviewed studies linked natural product-mediated chemosensitization to apoptosis induction, transporter-associated survival signaling, autophagy-related mechanisms, cell-cycle perturbation, and regulation of upstream pathways such as NF-κB/IκB-α, PI3K/Akt, PI3K/Akt/mTOR, Nrf2/ARE, JNK, ERK/autophagy, and CXCL12/CXCR4. These findings indicate that MDR reversal was not limited to direct inhibition of drug-efflux transporters, but often involved broader reprogramming of pro-survival and cell-death regulatory networks.

Triptolide enhanced ADM-induced apoptosis in K562/A02 cells while suppressing P-gp and MDR1 promoter activity. Similarly, emodin, costunolide, parthenolide, DHH, and TMI were associated with increased apoptosis or apoptosis-related signaling in resistant cells. These findings support a mechanistic interpretation in which MDR reversal is coupled to restoration of drug-induced cell death rather than being explained solely by changes in IC50 values.

Haishengsu induced a dose-dependent increase in apoptosis at 36 h, rising from a low baseline in untreated K562/ADM cells to markedly higher levels at the highest tested concentration. Although the apoptosis endpoints were heterogeneous and not uniformly suitable for pooled quantitative synthesis, they provide consistent biological support for cell-death restoration as a secondary mechanism of MDR reversal.

Quality assessment and risk of bias

The methodological quality assessment indicated that the included evidence base was generally suitable for structured qualitative synthesis and RF-based quantitative analysis, although several recurring concerns required careful analytical handling. As summarized in , the most frequent issue was the presence of multiple-arm or shared-comparator designs, which affected 11 studies and required either preferred-arm selection or conservative analytical treatment to avoid unit-of-analysis distortion. Additional important concerns included dose-selection uncertainty, the presence of studies without a strict ADM/DOX RF endpoint, graph-based or non-tabulated quantitative outcomes, and a subset of high-effect estimates that could exert disproportionate influence on the quantitative synthesis.

The study-level concern profile is visualized in , which demonstrates that the methodological limitations were not uniformly distributed across the evidence base. Instead, specific studies tended to cluster around particular concern domains. For example, non-strict primary endpoints were concentrated in a small subset of studies, which were retained primarily for mechanistic interpretation rather than for strict RF pooling. Likewise, graph-based or non-tabulated outcomes were limited to a defined group of studies, supporting the decision to treat these data cautiously and, where necessary, reserve them for secondary or narrative synthesis. The figure also shows that high-effect influence was restricted to a relatively small number of studies, whereas multiple-arm or dose-related concerns were more widely distributed. This pattern indicates that the principal analytical challenge in the review was not widespread poor study quality, but rather heterogeneity in outcome structure, reporting style, and experimental design.

The frequency summary in further clarifies the overall quality profile of the included studies. Most studies fell into the moderate concern category, whereas a smaller proportion were judged as low-to-moderate concern for quantitative synthesis. This distribution suggests that the evidence base was methodologically usable, but not free of complexity. Importantly, the predominance of moderate concern should not be interpreted as indicating invalid or unreliable findings; rather, it reflects the inherent challenges of synthesizing in vitro MDR-reversal studies that differ in resistant subline, metabolite class, concentration range, endpoint definition, and mechanistic depth.

provides a more detailed explanation of the major methodological concerns affecting synthesis. Five studies lacked a directly comparable ADM/DOX IC50- or RF-based primary endpoint and were therefore excluded from the strict quantitative pool, although they remained informative for mechanistic interpretation. Four studies required graph-based extraction or did not present reversal outcomes in a directly tabulated format, which limited their suitability for primary pooling and justified a more cautious approach to sensitivity assessment. High-effect estimates, defined here as preferred RF or reversal-index values of at least 10, were identified in five studies and were considered particularly important for influence analysis because of their potential to inflate the overall RF-based estimate. Other issues, such as potential metabolite or dataset overlap, non-anthracycline primary substrates, limited mechanistic validation, and subline/model complexity, were less frequent but still analytically relevant.

From an interpretive perspective, the quality assessment supports two main conclusions. First, the major limitations of the included studies were primarily related to comparability and feasibility of synthesis, rather than to a complete absence of relevant biological evidence. Second, the identified concerns were sufficiently transparent and structurally manageable to allow a valid RF-based quantitative synthesis, provided that sensitivity analyses and careful study-selection rules were applied. In this regard, the findings in and are consistent with the analytical strategy adopted in this review, namely the use of a strict primary RF dataset combined with mechanistic narrative synthesis for less directly comparable studies.

Discussion

This systematic review and inverse-variance random-effects meta-analysis identified an overall IC50-based chemosensitization signal across a restricted set of adriamycin- or doxorubicin-resistant K562 experiments, while pharmacological interpretation remains specific to each metabolite, dose, resistant subline, substrate drug, and molecular target. In the primary meta-analysis of 19 study-level IC50-based effects, the pooled RF was 6.31 (95% CI, 4.77–8.36), summarizing the common IC50-based chemosensitization endpoint across the eligible comparisons. However, between-study heterogeneity was high, indicating that the magnitude of reversal varied substantially across resistant-cell sublines, metabolite classes, substrate drugs, and experimental conditions. The highest study-level estimates were observed for ailanthone, pentagalloyl glucose, oroxylin A, dioscin, and puerarin, whereas several other interventions produced moderate but still biologically relevant reversal effects (; ; ; ; ). These findings support further evaluation of the individual metabolites as MDR-modifying candidates, but they do not establish a uniform pharmacological action for natural products as a class.

Compared with earlier K562 MDR studies, the present synthesis extends the literature by placing metabolite-specific observations within a common IC50-based quantitative framework without treating chemically distinct metabolites as a single pharmacological class. Earlier studies in K562/A02 and related resistant sublines mainly showed that individual metabolites could reduce P-gp/MDR1 activity, increase intracellular ADR/DOX or Rh123 accumulation, or suppress MDR1/P-gp expression (; ; ). Similar mechanistic patterns were later reported in K562/ADM and K562/ADR models, where MDR reversal was associated not only with transporter inhibition but also with PI3K/Akt, NF-κB, JNK, Nrf2/ARE, apoptosis-related, and autophagy-related regulation (; ; ). The present meta-analysis is consistent with those earlier findings, but it adds a pooled IC50-based RF estimate for the common chemosensitization endpoint across eligible resistant K562 experiments; this pooled endpoint does not imply that the underlying mechanisms are shared across metabolites. This interpretation is also aligned with recent broader MDR literature showing that natural products can modulate ABC transporters by reducing protein expression, downregulating mRNA expression, inhibiting drug efflux, and affecting indirect regulatory pathways such as NF-κB, PI3K/Akt, ATPase-related activity, Nrf2, and apoptosis-related signaling (; ). However, unlike broader reviews that include multiple cancer types and mixed experimental models, the present analysis remains restricted to resistant K562 leukemia models and directly comparable ADM/ADR/DOX IC50-based reversal outcomes. Therefore, the current findings support the earlier mechanistic literature while providing a more focused quantitative estimate for K562-based MDR reversal.

Quantitatively, the pooled reversal fold of 6.31 lies within the moderate-to-high range of the earlier metabolite-specific reports, whereas the 95% prediction interval of 1.66–23.96 captures their marked dispersion. Thus, the present synthesis confirms the direction of the earlier mechanistic findings but tempers interpretation of the largest single-metabolite effects by showing that those estimates are not representative of the full K562 evidence base.

Accordingly, the pooled RF should not be interpreted as a metabolite-specific potency estimate for any single metabolite or botanical source. Rather, it provides a quantitative summary of the direction and approximate magnitude of IC50-based chemosensitization across a predefined, comparable subset of in vitro K562 MDR studies, while mechanistic and pharmacological differences are addressed through subgroup, sensitivity, and narrative synthesis.

The ethnopharmacological mapping presented in strengthens the interpretation of the present findings by linking the experimental MDR-reversal evidence to traditionally used medicinal sources rather than treating the included interventions as isolated natural product-derived metabolites. Several high-effect metabolites, including ailanthone, pentagalloyl glucose, oroxylin A, dioscin, and puerarin, were derived from sources with documented traditional or ethnomedicinal relevance, such as Ailanthus altissima, Bouea macrophylla, Scutellaria baicalensis, Dioscorea spp., and Pueraria lobata (; ; ; ; ). Similarly, moderate-effect or mechanistically important agents from Euphorbia lathyris, Cnidium monnieri, Solanum nigrum, Curcuma comosa, Schisandra chinensis, Ganoderma lucidum, Tripterygium wilfordii, and Tanacetum parthenium further support a source-based pharmacological rationale for MDR modulation (; ; ). This linkage does not suggest that these traditional medicines were historically used to treat multidrug resistance or K562 leukemia specifically; rather, it indicates that traditionally relevant natural sources may contain bioactive metabolites capable of modulating modern resistance phenotypes through ABC-transporter regulation, improved intracellular anthracycline retention, apoptosis restoration, autophagy regulation, and suppression of NF-κB, PI3K/Akt, and Nrf2/ARE signaling pathways.

The quantitative findings should be interpreted in light of the model-specific distribution of effects. In the descriptive RF distribution, K562/A02 contributed the most study-level effects and showed the broadest response range, extending from modest reversal estimates to the high-effect estimate observed for ailanthone (; ). In the random-effects subgroup analysis, K562/A02 showed a pooled RF of 5.71, whereas K562/ADM showed 4.90. Within K562/ADM studies, oroxylin A represented the strongest study-level estimate in this subgroup (; ; ; ; ). K562/ADR showed the highest subgroup-level pooled RF of 8.91, largely because several studies in this model reported high-effect interventions, including dioscin, puerarin, and pentagalloyl glucose (; ; ). This pattern suggests that the apparent magnitude of MDR reversal is influenced not only by metabolite identity but also by resistant-cell phenotype, transporter dependence, substrate drug, and dose-selection strategy.

Mechanistically, transporter modulation was the dominant explanatory theme. P-gp/MDR1 downregulation or functional inhibition was reported across a large subset of studies, including triptolide, archangelicin, timosaponin A-III, dioscin, osthole, resveratrol, emodin, ailanthone, schizandrol A, costunolide, puerarin, pentagalloyl glucose, parthenolide, and TMI (; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ). Importantly, the evidence suggests at least two transporter-centered phenotypes. Some agents reduced MDR1/P-gp expression or transcriptional activity, whereas others primarily inhibited transporter function without reducing P-gp abundance. Riccardin F, pakyonol, Euphorbia factor L1, ethyl lucidenates A, and DHH support the latter pattern, showing increased substrate accumulation or reduced efflux with limited evidence of P-gp expression suppression (; ; ; ). This distinction is important because direct transporter blockade and transcriptional transporter suppression may have different pharmacological implications.

The review also indicates that MDR reversal extends beyond P-gp. MRP1/ABCC1 modulation was implicated in wogonin, timosaponin A-III, solanine, and S-20 studies, while BCRP/ABCG2-related resistance was relevant in S-20, parthenolide, and TMI studies (; ; ; ; ; ). These findings are consistent with a multidimensional resistance phenotype in which resistant K562 cells may rely on several ABC transporters and stress-response pathways rather than a single efflux mechanism. The Nrf2/ARE axis was particularly relevant for wogonin and TMI, linking antioxidant defense signaling with transporter regulation and resistance reversal (; ). Similarly, solanine suggested an MRP1-centered mechanism associated with JNK signaling ().

Cell death restoration and survival pathway inhibition represented another major interpretive theme. Several interventions increased apoptosis or restored drug-induced apoptotic responsiveness, including Haishengsu, triptolide, Euphorbia factor L1, oroxylin A, wogonin, timosaponin A-III, emodin, costunolide, parthenolide, DHH, and TMI (; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ). PI3K/Akt-related signaling was the most recurrent pathway category, appearing in studies of oroxylin A, wogonin, timosaponin A-III, osthole, ailanthone, schizandrol A, costunolide, and resveratrol-bestatin (; ; ; ; ; ). NF-κB/IκB-α signaling was also important, particularly where MDR1/P-gp transcriptional suppression coincided with enhanced chemosensitivity or apoptosis, as shown for oroxylin A, dioscin, puerarin, and parthenolide (; ; ; ). These findings support a model in which natural products reverse MDR through coordinated effects on efflux transporters, intracellular drug retention, survival signaling, and apoptotic execution.

Several studies broadened the mechanistic landscape beyond classical transporter reversal. Puerarin and S-20 linked MDR reversal to autophagy-associated processes, with S-20 providing evidence for cytotoxic autophagy and an ERK-related reduction in resistance protein levels (; ). Parthenolide extended the discussion to drug-resistant leukemia stem-like populations by suppressing NF-κB/P-gp and enhancing DOX-induced apoptosis in the K562/ADM-derived resistant context (). TMI provided a newer Nrf2-centered mechanism involving downregulation of P-gp and BCRP together with G2/M arrest and caspase-dependent apoptosis (). These studies suggest that future MDR-reversal strategies may need to integrate transporter inhibition with targeting adaptive stress signaling, stem-like resistance phenotypes, and programmed cell death pathways.

Recent evidence further strengthens the contextual relevance of the present K562-focused synthesis. A recent review of ABC transporter-mediated resistance showed that natural products can modulate P-gp, BCRP, and MRP1 through suppression of protein expression, downregulation of mRNA expression, inhibition of drug efflux, and indirect regulation of NF-κB, PI3K/Akt, ATPase-related activity, and other resistance-associated pathways (). A complementary 2026 review focusing on terpenoids emphasized that monoterpenes, sesquiterpenes, diterpenes, and triterpenes may reverse ABC transporter-mediated MDR by blocking efflux pumps and regulating PI3K-AKT, Nrf2, and NF-κB signaling (). Importantly, a recent leukemia-based case example using doxorubicin-resistant HL-60/DOXO cells showed that selected natural flavonoids, particularly apigenin, chrysin, and luteolin, could overcome doxorubicin resistance in association with Nrf2-related regulation, apoptosis, DNA damage, and ROS responses (). Although this leukemia model is not K562 and was therefore not eligible for the primary quantitative pool, it supports the broader mechanistic relevance of Nrf2-linked chemosensitization and apoptosis restoration observed in the present synthesis. Recent broader reviews also support the role of natural products as multi-target MDR modulators, including metabolites such as curcumin, resveratrol, baicalein, chrysin, and plant-derived P-gp modulators from sesquiterpenoid, diterpenoid, chalcone, riccardin, and diarylheptanoid classes (; ). These recent case examples and reviews strengthen the translational rationale of natural product-derived MDR reversal while confirming that the pooled RF estimate should remain restricted to directly comparable IC50-based ADM/ADR/DOX reversal outcomes in resistant K562 sublines.

Four representative case studies illustrate the biological meaning of the pooled signal. Costunolide combined with doxorubicin increased apoptosis to 37.56% compared with 9.09% for doxorubicin alone while suppressing PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling (). Dioscin produced a 15.14-fold reversal in K562/ADR cells through NF-κB-dependent MDR1 suppression and recovery of intracellular adriamycin (). In K562/A02 cells, ailanthone produced a 21.14-fold reversal together with P-gp efflux inhibition and PI3K/Akt-linked MDR1 downregulation (). Pentagalloyl glucose produced a 20.84-fold reversal in K562/ADR cells by jointly reducing P-gp expression and transport activity (). Taken together, these cases show three convergent strategies—functional efflux blockade, transcriptional transporter suppression, and restoration of drug-induced apoptosis—and identify testable candidates for validation in primary or patient-derived leukemia models.

The sensitivity analyses clarify the robustness and limitations of the pooled quantitative signal. Excluding high-effect studies from (; ; ; ) and () reduced the pooled RF from 6.31 to 4.51, but the reversal effect remained positive. Other sensitivity scenarios produced pooled RF values ranging from 6.09 to 6.97, indicating that the direction of the effect was stable across alternative assumptions. Leave-one-out analysis showed that omitting individual studies yielded pooled RF estimates ranging from 5.90 to 6.62. Omitting ailanthone () and pentagalloyl glucose () produced the largest downward shifts, whereas omission of lower-effect studies slightly increased the pooled estimate. These results indicate that no single study determined the overall conclusion, although high-effect studies meaningfully shaped the magnitude of the pooled estimate.

Small-study effect assessment also supported a cautious interpretation of the pooled estimate. The funnel plot showed mild right-sided asymmetry, but Egger’s test did not reach statistical significance, with p = 0.080. Trim-and-fill analysis imputed three studies and reduced the adjusted pooled RF to 5.48, with a 95% CI of 3.87–7.76. Therefore, the pooled chemosensitization signal remained positive after adjustment, although the possibility of small-study effects or selective reporting cannot be excluded in this preclinical literature.

The RF-based effect size also has important statistical limitations. RF is a ratio-based endpoint derived from IC50 values. It is therefore sensitive to differences in IC50 estimation methods, dose–response curve fitting, exposure duration, substrate drug, baseline resistance level, and the selected non-cytotoxic co-treatment concentration. Although log-transformation improves symmetry and allows interpretation of the pooled estimate as a geometric mean RF, it does not remove heterogeneity caused by different assay platforms or pharmacological designs. In addition, incomplete reporting of standard deviations, standard errors, replicate numbers, and confidence intervals limited the number of studies that could contribute to inverse-variance weighting and required exclusion or approximation of some otherwise relevant studies. RF also does not distinguish whether an apparent sensitization effect is driven by true MDR modulation, direct cytotoxicity of the tested intervention, reduced baseline IC50, or interaction with a specific substrate drug. Therefore, the pooled RF should be interpreted as an exploratory summary of relative chemosensitization under heterogeneous in vitro conditions, not as a standardized potency ranking or a directly translatable pharmacological effect size.

The main limitations arise from methodological heterogeneity, small experimental sample sizes, and incomplete standardization of outcome reporting across the included in vitro studies. First, although the primary IC50-based endpoint was analyzed using an inverse-variance random-effects meta-analysis, not all eligible studies reported sufficient variance data for inclusion in the pooled analysis; therefore, studies without directly usable ADM/ADR/DOX IC50-based effect-size and variance information were retained only for structured descriptive or mechanistic synthesis (; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ). Second, several studies used multiple metabolites, multiple dose arms, or shared comparators, requiring predefined primary-arm extraction or sensitivity handling (; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ). Third, not all studies used ADM, ADR, or DOX as the primary substrate; VCR- and bestatin-based studies were mechanistically informative but were not appropriate for the primary anthracycline-based RF meta-analysis (; ). Fourth, some studies reported graph-based, screening-only, non-tabulated, or mechanistic outcomes rather than conventional IC50-based reversal, limiting direct comparability with the primary RF endpoint (; ; ; ; ; ; ). These factors justify the combined use of a primary random-effects meta-analysis and structured mechanistic interpretation. Future research should prioritize complete IC50 data with standard deviations or standard errors, standardized resistant-cell validation, explicit definition of non-cytotoxic reversal concentrations, validated taxonomic and material-source reporting, consistent positive and negative controls, and confirmation of high-effect metabolites such as oroxylin A, puerarin, dioscin, ailanthone, and pentagalloyl glucose in additional leukemia models, primary resistant leukemia cells, and animal studies before clinical translation is considered (; ; ; ; ).

Accordingly, future MDR-reversal studies should report complete IC50 curve parameters, variance estimates, replicate numbers, viability thresholds for non-cytotoxic concentrations, and both absolute and ratio-based sensitization outcomes to improve the statistical robustness of future meta-analyses. Because CML treatment is clinically centered on BCR-ABL1 tyrosine kinase inhibition, the present findings should be interpreted as preclinical evidence of MDR modulation in resistant K562-derived models rather than as direct clinical evidence for CML treatment; future studies should therefore evaluate promising MDR-reversing metabolites in TKI-resistant, primary, and patient-derived leukemia models. Moreover, the clinical landscape of cancer treatment continues to evolve with immunotherapies, which may present their own challenges, such as pembrolizumab-induced autoimmune hemolytic anemia (), underscoring the need for safe and effective therapeutic strategies ().

In relation to Frontiers’ four-pillar expectations, the main reporting limitations of the original evidence base involved incomplete pharmacological standardization, variable reporting of dose selection and non-cytotoxic concentrations, incomplete taxonomic or source-material detail in some studies, inconsistent variance reporting for RF estimation, and the exclusive reliance on in vitro K562-derived models. These limitations were addressed in the present review by adding pharmacological evidence profiling, taxonomic/source validation, evidence hierarchy, quality-assessment criteria, and cautious interpretation restricted to preclinical in vitro MDR-reversal evidence.

This synthesis identifies a common IC50-based chemosensitization signal across the restricted quantitative dataset, while the pharmacological effects and mechanisms remain metabolite-specific. The strongest support comes from studies that combine IC50-based sensitization with functional evidence of increased intracellular drug accumulation, reduced efflux, transporter downregulation, apoptosis restoration, or pathway suppression. The pooled random-effects estimate summarized the common chemosensitization endpoint, but the high heterogeneity indicates that effect magnitude should be interpreted cautiously and in relation to resistant-cell subline, substrate drug, metabolite identity, and dose-selection strategy. Collectively, the findings identify individual metabolites with stronger study-level signals that warrant priority validation, particularly where sensitization was accompanied by experimentally demonstrated transporter or signaling effects. Future studies should report complete IC50 data with standard deviations or standard errors, use standardized resistant-cell validation, define non-cytotoxic reversal concentrations, distinguish transporter function from transporter expression effects, and confirm whether high-effect metabolites retain activity in more clinically relevant leukemia models. Future research could also explore their integration with advanced targeted delivery systems, such as carrier-free nanoassemblies for acute myeloid leukemia ().

Conclusion

In the 19 eligible IC50-based comparisons, the common chemosensitization endpoint yielded a pooled RF of 6.31. This statistical summary does not represent a shared pharmacological action or potency across chemically distinct metabolites. The high heterogeneity indicates that effect magnitude varies substantially with metabolite identity, resistant-cell subline, substrate drug, dose selection, and experimental context.

Across individual studies, reported mechanisms included ABC transporter modulation, increased intracellular drug accumulation, reduced efflux, restoration of apoptosis, and changes in survival or stress-response pathways, including PI3K/Akt, NF-κB, and Nrf2/ARE signaling. Individual metabolites showing both chemosensitization and experimentally supported transporter or pathway effects warrant priority validation; no shared mechanism is inferred for the intervention class. Future studies should report complete IC50 statistics with variance estimates, use standardized resistant-cell validation, clearly define non-cytotoxic reversal concentrations, and confirm whether high-effect natural product-derived metabolites retain activity in more clinically relevant leukemia models.
