Large Language Models and the Future of Peer-Reviewed Medical Publishing: Challenges, Editorial Responses, and a Framework for Responsible Integration
Authors: Hyejin Joo, Munkhzul Radnaabaatar, Jaehun Jung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe public release of large language models (LLMs) in late 2022 has fundamentally altered the landscape of scholarly medical publishing. LLMs now permeate every stage of the academic publishing pipeline, from manuscript drafting and peer review to editorial decision-making, with evidence suggesting that at least 13.5% of biomedical abstracts published in 2024 showed detectable LLM involvement. This rapid adoption has intersected with pre-existing structural vulnerabilities, including escalating article processing charges, publish-or-perish incentives, a chronic peer reviewer shortage, and inadequate editorial resources, creating interconnected challenges that affect all stakeholders. The response from the scholarly publishing community has been substantial but fragmented. International standards bodies, such as the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE), Committee on Publication Ethics (COPE), World Association of Medical Editors (WAME), have established consensus principles prohibiting AI authorship and requiring disclosure, yet individual journals range from restrictive to actively encouraging in their AI policies, and detection-based enforcement approaches have proven fundamentally unreliable, with documented biases against non-native English speakers. This narrative review characterizes the structural challenges of LLM adoption in medical publishing, provides a systematic comparison of editorial policies across major medical journals and publishers, and examines the limitations of current detection and enforcement mechanisms. Rather than focusing on prohibition and policing, the review proposes a forward-looking framework centered on three concrete proposals: 1) a three-tiered disclosure system (assistive, augmentative, substantive) integrated with the CRediT taxonomy; 2) a four-stage artificial intelligence co-editor model for deploying LLM-based tools within editorial workflows under transparent governance principles; and 3) equity-conscious policy design with international coordination through a proposed global editorial summit. The framework aims to shift the editorial paradigm from reactive enforcement to proactive governance, addressing the operational reality that editors are overburdened and underresourced for the expanding expectations placed upon them.Keywords: Large Language Models, Artificial Intelligence, Medical Publishing, Peer Review, Editorial Policy, Research Integrity

INTRODUCTION

The public release of ChatGPT by OpenAI in November 2022 marked a watershed moment for scholarly communication. Within months, large language models (LLMs) including GPT-4, Claude, Gemini, and their successors had permeated virtually every stage of the academic publishing pipeline, from manuscript drafting and literature synthesis to statistical coding, peer review, and editorial decision-making.1,2 The speed and scale of this have been unprecedented in the history of scientific publishing. An analysis of more than 15 million biomedical abstracts indexed by PubMed (2010–2024) revealed that at least 13.5% of abstracts published in 2024 showed evidence of LLM-assisted writing, with the proportion reaching 40% in certain disciplinary, geographic, and journal subcorpora.3 This rate of penetration surpassed the linguistic footprint of the COVID-19 pandemic on biomedical literature, underscoring the magnitude of the shift.3

The rapid integration of LLMs into scholarly workflows has intersected with pre-existing structural challenges in the academic publishing ecosystem, including increasing publication pressures, escalating article processing charges (APCs), reviewer shortages, and limited editorial resources.4,5,6,7,8,9 These conditions amplify both the opportunities and risks associated with LLM adoption and have intensified ongoing debates regarding research integrity, transparency, accountability, and equity in scholarly publishing.

LLMs have introduced both opportunities and risks for scholarly publishing. They may help reduce linguistic barriers for non-native English-speaking researchers, but they also facilitate the rapid production of superficial or fraudulent manuscripts, raising concerns about research integrity and the quality of the scientific literature.10,11 Evidence of widespread LLM-assisted writing has already been observed across the biomedical literature.3

The scholarly publishing community has responded rapidly to the emergence of LLMs. International organizations, journals, and publishers have issued policies emphasizing transparency, disclosure, and human accountability for artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted content.12,13,14,15,16,17,18,19 However, important challenges remain regarding policy implementation, AI detection, editorial oversight, and equity, particularly for non-native English-speaking researchers.20,21

In this context, the role of the medical journal editor is undergoing a fundamental transformation. Editors are no longer solely gatekeepers of scientific quality; they must also navigate emerging technological, ethical, and operational challenges for which historical precedent offers limited guidance. The stakes are particularly high in medical publishing, where literature pollution may contribute to patient harm, distorted clinical guidelines, and erosion of public trust in medical evidence.22,23

This narrative review has three objectives. First, it characterizes the structural challenges that widespread LLM adoption poses to the integrity and sustainability of peer-reviewed medical publishing within the broader scholarly publishing ecosystem. Second, it compares how major medical journals, academic publishers, and international standards bodies have responded through editorial policies, highlighting areas of consensus and persistent gaps. Third, it examines the limitations of existing detection and enforcement mechanisms and proposes a framework for responsible LLM integration based on AI-assisted editorial support. The review concludes by outlining future directions, including the need for greater international consensus on editorial standards for the LLM era.

THE STRUCTURAL CHALLENGES OF LLM ADOPTION IN SCHOLARLY PUBLISHING

The escalating volume of manuscript submissions and the erosion of quality

The availability of LLMs has fundamentally altered the economics of manuscript production. Prior to November 2022, generating a plausible academic manuscript required substantial investment of time, disciplinary expertise, and writing skill. LLMs have dramatically compressed this process, enabling the production of linguistically coherent text at speeds and scales that were previously unattainable.1,2 While this capability can serve legitimate purposes, such as helping researchers articulate complex ideas and overcome language barriers, it has also lowered the barrier to producing superficial, derivative, and in some cases fraudulent manuscripts.

Large-scale analyses have documented a growing linguistic footprint of LLM-assisted writing in the biomedical literature. One study estimated that at least 13.5% of PubMed-indexed abstracts published in 2024 showed evidence of LLM processing, with proportions exceeding 40% in some disciplinary and geographic subcorpora.3

Beyond individual manuscripts, LLMs may further amplify the activities of paper mills, organizations that produce fraudulent manuscripts for profit. More than 32,000 suspected paper mill articles have been identified,24 and large-scale retractions—more than 8,000 articles from Hindawi journals in 2023 alone, an open-access publisher acquired by Wiley in 2021—show the scale of this challenge.25 The combination of AI-generated text with fabricated data, manipulated images, and compromised peer review processes raises serious concerns for research integrity.

LLM-assisted writing may also contribute to the homogenization of scientific communication. AI-generated text often converges toward formulaic structures, standardized phrasing, and generalized language that can obscure the specificity and nuance of genuine scholarly contributions.26 As a result, manuscripts may appear more polished while becoming less informative, making it increasingly difficult for editors and reviewers to distinguish substantive work from superficial content based on writing quality alone.

The economic and structural context: APCs, publication incentives, and the predatory ecosystem

The challenges posed by LLMs do not exist in isolation; they interact with and amplify a set of pre-existing economic and structural pressures that have shaped the scholarly publishing landscape over the past two decades.

APCs have become the dominant revenue model for open access publishing, with fees at some journals exceeding $12,000 per article.4,27 While APCs support editorial operations, they also create a structural incentive to increase publication volume. LLM-enabled manuscript production may further intensify these pressures by increasing submission volume at low cost.

These pressures intersect with the publish-or-perish culture that continues to shape academic careers. Researchers are often evaluated using quantitative metrics, creating incentives to maximize publication output. LLMs may further reinforce this tendency by reducing the effort required to produce manuscripts.6

Predatory journals occupy a particularly problematic position at the intersection of these forces. Predatory publishers, which charge APCs without providing meaningful peer review, editorial oversight, or quality control, have proliferated alongside the growth of open access publishing.28 The combination of LLM-generated submissions and predatory publishers creates a closed loop in which low-quality manuscripts are produced at minimal cost and published with minimal scrutiny, contaminating the scientific literature while generating revenue for both the paper mill operators and the predatory publishers.

Researchers from low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) face both the financial burden of APCs and the risk of publishing in predatory venues when legitimate journals are financially inaccessible.5 LLMs add further complexity by helping overcome language barriers while potentially increasing exposure to AI-detection tools that disproportionately flag non-native English writing.21

The gap between actual LLM usage and existing regulatory frameworks

Perhaps the most fundamental structural challenge facing the scholarly publishing community is the substantial and widening gap between the actual prevalence of LLM use and the capacity of existing frameworks to regulate it.

A 2025 publisher survey reported 84% overall AI-tool use (62% for research or publication tasks), and German survey evidence indicated that roughly three-quarters of researchers had used such tools at least occasionally.29,30 Given the limitations of vocabulary-based detection methods, the true prevalence of LLM use is likely higher than currently measurable.3

This widespread adoption extends to all stakeholders in the publishing process, not only authors.

Evidence of LLM-assisted peer review has emerged through multiple channels. An analysis of peer review reports from major computer science conferences suggested that between 6.5% and 16.9% of reviews since the release of ChatGPT may have been substantially modified by LLMs.31 Although the precise extent remains difficult to quantify, these findings indicate that LLM use among peer reviewers is already a practical reality rather than a hypothetical concern. These developments have prompted proposals for AI-assisted editorial workflows using LLMs to support manuscript triage, language assessment, and administrative tasks within editorial offices.32

International standards bodies, including the ICMJE, COPE, and WAME, have established important principles regarding AI use in scholarly publishing: AI tools cannot be credited as authors, human authors retain full accountability, and AI use must be disclosed.12,13,14 However, these guidelines leave critical questions unresolved. The boundary between “acceptable” AI-assisted language editing and “unacceptable” AI-generated content remains undefined, creating an ethical gray zone that authors, reviewers, and editors must navigate without clear guidance. The practical mechanisms for enforcing disclosure requirements are underdeveloped, and the consequences of non-compliance are inconsistently specified.23

The consequences of LLM limitations for the medical literature

While the challenges described above apply broadly to scholarly publishing, they carry distinctive and heightened consequences in the medical and biomedical context, where the downstream effects of literature pollution directly affect patient care, clinical decision-making, and public health policy.

LLMs are well documented to generate plausible but fabricated content, a phenomenon widely referred to as “hallucination.” In the context of medical publishing, hallucinated content can take particularly consequential forms: fabricated clinical trial results, fictitious drug interactions, incorrect dosage recommendations, and non-existent references cited with convincing bibliographic formatting.22,33 Analogous concerns about AI-assisted, low-quality papers flooding the biomedical cancer literature have been reported.34 An analysis of accepted papers at NeurIPS 2025 identified at least 53 publications containing hallucinated references.35

The implications extend beyond individual manuscripts. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses, which form the foundation of evidence-based medicine, depend on the integrity of the primary studies they synthesize. If a meaningful proportion of primary studies contain LLM-generated or LLM-contaminated data, the reliability of the evidence synthesis chain may be compromised, with downstream effects on clinical practice guidelines, regulatory decisions, and patient care.36

The regulatory dimension adds further urgency. Manuscripts and study reports submitted to agencies such as the U.S. Food and Drug Administration, the European Medicines Agency, and the Korean Ministry of Food and Drug Safety serve as the evidentiary foundation for drug approvals, device clearances, and public health interventions. Undisclosed LLM assistance in the preparation of pivotal regulatory submissions raises medicolegal questions that existing frameworks are not equipped to address.37

HOW MAJOR MEDICAL JOURNALS AND PUBLISHERS ARE RESPONDING: A COMPARATIVE ANALYSIS OF LLM POLICIES

International guidelines and standards bodies

The foundational regulatory framework for LLM use in scholarly medical publishing has been established by three principal international organizations, each of which has issued guidance reflecting a consensus on core principles while diverging in specificity and scope.

The ICMJE has progressively expanded its guidance on AI use in scholarly publishing. Recent revisions require disclosure of AI-assisted activities, emphasize human verification of AI-generated content, and reinforce that AI tools cannot satisfy authorship criteria because they cannot assume responsibility or accountability for published work.12

The COPE similarly maintains that AI tools cannot meet authorship criteria because they cannot assume responsibility for submitted work. While COPE does not prohibit AI use, it requires transparent disclosure and highlights risks to research integrity, including fabrication, copyright concerns, and ethical violations.13

The WAME has adopted a similar position, emphasizing editor responsibility for establishing journal-specific AI policies and prohibiting AI authorship. WAME further recommends detailed disclosure of AI use in manuscript preparation, data analysis, and image generation.14

Major medical journals

The policies adopted by individual medical journals reveal substantial variation in both the scope of permissible AI use and the specificity of disclosure requirements, despite shared adherence to the ICMJE’s prohibition on AI authorship.

The New England Journal of Medicine (NEJM) and NEJM AI. The NEJM requires disclosure of AI use in both the cover letter and the manuscript body.38 In contrast, NEJM AI, its dedicated AI journal, has adopted one of the most permissive positions among major medical journals, explicitly encouraging transparent LLM use while maintaining the prohibition on AI authorship.16

JAMA and JAMA Network Journals. The JAMA Network has developed one of the most comprehensive AI disclosure frameworks among major medical journals. Authors are required to report the AI tools used, their purposes, and the content generated.39,40 JAMA also explicitly acknowledges limited AI use within editorial workflows while maintaining that substantive editorial decisions remain the responsibility of human editors.41

The Lancet. The Lancet occupies one of the most restrictive positions among leading medical journals. Its policy limits AI use to improving “readability and language,” effectively prohibiting AI-assisted content generation, data analysis, or interpretation.42

The BMJ. The BMJ permits AI tool use in submitted manuscripts but requires disclosure of the tool used, its purpose, and the content generated. Distinctively, the BMJ also requires authors to consider and address potential bias introduced by AI tools.43

Nature and the Nature Portfolio. Nature requires disclosure of AI use in the Methods section, with sufficient detail to allow readers to understand the scope of AI involvement. Across all Nature Portfolio journals, AI-generated images are prohibited.44

Journal of Korean Medical Science (JKMS). The JKMS was the first Korean medical journal to establish comprehensive AI usage guidelines in June 2023.23 The JKMS policy requires disclosure of the AI tool used, the prompt, the purpose of use, and the scope of AI involvement, reflecting one of the most detailed disclosure frameworks among medical journals.23

Synthesis: common ground and critical divergences

The comparative analysis reveals a landscape characterized by broad agreement on foundational principles but significant variation in implementation details. Three areas of consensus are clearly established across all surveyed organizations, journals, and publishers: 1) AI tools cannot be credited as authors; 2) human authors bear full accountability for all content, including AI-generated material; and 3) AI use must be disclosed transparently.

However, critical divergences emerge in at least four dimensions. First, the scope of permissible AI use ranges from narrow (“readability and language” only, as with The Lancet) to broad and actively encouraged (NEJM AI), with most journals occupying an intermediate position that permits AI use with disclosure but does not specify clear boundaries between acceptable and unacceptable applications. Second, the specificity of disclosure requirements varies substantially: WAME’s recommendation to include prompts, timestamps, and tool versions contrasts with the more general disclosure expectations of many journals. Third, policies governing reviewer and editor use of AI remain conspicuously underdeveloped. While JAMA has acknowledged limited AI use in its editorial processes, most journals have not issued explicit guidance on whether or how reviewers may use AI tools, despite evidence that reviewer AI use is already widespread.35,45 Fourth, enforcement mechanisms are largely absent across all surveyed entities; policies establish expectations for behavior but provide minimal guidance on detection, verification, or consequences for non-compliance.

Among these divergences, the enforcement gap may represent the most consequential limitation of the current policy landscape. As discussed in the following section, the technical tools available for detecting AI use are fundamentally unreliable, creating a widening disconnect between the policies that journals articulate and their capacity to implement them.

THE LIMITATIONS OF CURRENT DETECTION AND ENFORCEMENT APPROACHES

AI detection tools and their fundamental unreliability

The gap between policy aspiration and practical enforcement identified in the previous section is, in large part, a consequence of the fundamental limitations of AI detection tools. Several commercial and academic tools, including Turnitin’s AI Detection module, GPTZero, Copyleaks, and Originality.AI, have been deployed by journals and institutions to screen manuscripts for LLM-generated content. However, the evidence base regarding their reliability reveals systematic deficiencies that undermine their utility as editorial decision-support tools.

Published evaluations of AI-generated text detectors report substantial variability across tools and contexts. One systematic evaluation found that no detector exceeded 80% accuracy and that AI-generated text was detected far less reliably than human-written text, leading the authors to conclude that current detectors are neither accurate nor reliable.46 Other empirical evaluations similarly suggest that detector outputs are not uniformly reliable across tools and use cases.20 Importantly, detector performance appears substantially poorer for non-native English writing. In one study, human-written TOEFL essays were incorrectly classified as “AI-generated” at an average false-positive rate of 61.3%, raising concerns about potential discrimination against researchers who write in English as a second or third language.21

The accuracy of detection tools degrades further when authors edit or paraphrase AI-generated content. Even when detectors perform well on unedited outputs, paraphrasing and other transformations can reduce detectability, limiting the reliability of detection-based enforcement.47 This finding is particularly significant because it describes the most common use case: researchers rarely submit raw LLM output, instead using LLMs to generate initial drafts that are subsequently revised, restructured, and augmented with original content. Detection tools are thus least reliable in precisely the scenario that is most prevalent.

An additional dimension of obsolescence compounds these limitations. The use of fixed AI-detection thresholds by some journals illustrates the practical challenges of enforcement, as the underlying measurements remain too imprecise to support confident editorial decisions.48

The ethical dimensions of detection-based approaches

Beyond technical limitations, detection-based enforcement strategies raise substantive ethical concerns that the editorial community has been slow to address.

The inequity problem is the most pressing. Detection tools exhibit documented bias against non-native English speakers, who are disproportionately flagged as having used AI assistance.21 This bias intersects with a fundamental irony: non-native English speakers are arguably the population that derives the greatest legitimate benefit from LLM-assisted language editing, yet they are also the population most likely to be penalized by detection-based policies. For international medical journals, which depend on global submissions for their relevance and impact, this bias creates a systemic disadvantage for a substantial proportion of their author base.

The chilling effect extends beyond individual false positives. The perception that detection tools may be deployed punitively can discourage researchers from submitting to journals with aggressive AI screening policies, particularly researchers from LMICs who already face structural barriers to publication. This dynamic risks reinforcing existing inequities in the global distribution of scholarly output rather than addressing them.5

The arms race dynamic further diminishes the long-term viability of detection-based approaches. As detection algorithms improve, AI-generated text becomes more sophisticated and harder to identify, and commercial tools that specifically evade detection are already available. This creates an unsustainable technological cycle that consumes editorial resources without offering a durable solution.37

The authorship and accountability gap

The limitations of detection tools interact with a more fundamental conceptual challenge: the current authorship and accountability framework is not designed to accommodate the role that LLMs now play in the research and publishing process.

The universal prohibition on AI authorship, endorsed by ICMJE, COPE, WAME, and all major publishers, rests on the sound principle that authorship entails accountability and that AI tools cannot assume legal, ethical, or intellectual responsibility for published work.12,13 However, this binary framework (human author versus AI tool) may be insufficient to capture the diverse ways in which LLMs now contribute to scholarly work. When an LLM substantially shapes the conceptual framework of a manuscript, generates the initial literature synthesis, suggests analytical approaches, or produces text that survives largely intact through the revision process, categorizing its role simply as a tool analogous to a spell-checker or reference manager strains the credibility of the disclosure framework.

The Contributor Roles Taxonomy (CRediT), the standard framework for describing author contributions, does not currently include categories for AI involvement.49 Proposals to expand CRediT or develop complementary frameworks have emerged in response to the growing need for more granular attribution of AI contributions.50,51

The accountability vacuum becomes most consequential when LLM-generated content causes harm. When a manuscript contains hallucinated references, fabricated data, or clinically dangerous recommendations that originated from an LLM, the question of responsibility is currently resolved by holding all human authors individually accountable. While this principle is appropriate in establishing that responsibility cannot be abdicated to a machine, it does not address the systemic dimension: the proliferation of LLM-generated errors across the literature reflects structural failures in the publishing ecosystem that cannot be remedied solely through individual author accountability.23

THE REALITY OF MEDICAL JOURNAL EDITING: STRUCTURAL CONSTRAINTS AND THE CASE FOR AI-ASSISTED EDITORIAL SUPPORT

The uncompensated and overburdened editor

Discussions of AI policy in scholarly publishing frequently proceed as though journal editors operate with unlimited time, technical resources, and institutional support. The reality is markedly different, and any framework for responsible LLM integration must be grounded in an honest assessment of the conditions under which editorial work is actually performed.

The majority of medical journal editors, particularly those at society journals, specialty journals, and journals based in LMICs, serve in unpaid or minimally compensated roles.9 Editorial responsibilities are undertaken in addition to, rather than in place of, primary professional obligations as clinicians, researchers, and educators. Unlike editors at large commercial publishers, who may have access to dedicated editorial staff and technical infrastructure, editors at smaller journals often handle tasks ranging from manuscript triage and reviewer selection to copyediting, production oversight, and author correspondence.

The volume and complexity of these responsibilities have expanded substantially in recent years. Beyond the traditional editorial functions of evaluating scientific merit and managing peer review, editors are now expected to develop and enforce AI disclosure policies, assess data sharing compliance, verify ORCID identifiers, monitor for image manipulation, screen for paper mill submissions, and navigate an increasingly complex landscape of publication ethics.52 Each of these tasks requires specialized knowledge that most editors have not been trained to apply, and the accumulation of these demands has created what amounts to a structural crisis of editorial sustainability.

The implications for LLM policy enforcement are direct. Editors who are already working at or beyond capacity cannot be expected to serve as the primary detection mechanism for undisclosed AI use, particularly when the detection tools available to them are unreliable. An enforcement-centered approach to AI governance effectively shifts the burden of a systemic challenge onto individuals who are among the least resourced stakeholders in the publishing ecosystem.

The peer reviewer shortage and its consequences

The editorial workload crisis is compounded by a well-documented and worsening shortage of peer reviewers, which predates the LLM era but has been exacerbated by it.

The scale of the peer review enterprise is substantial. Global estimates indicate that peer reviewers contributed over 130 million hours in 2020, with the value of U.S.-based reviewers’ time alone estimated at roughly US$1.5 billion.7 This labor is heavily concentrated: a modeling study of biomedical peer-review supply and demand estimated that, as of 2015, approximately 20% of researchers performed 69–94% of all reviews, creating a system that depends disproportionately on a small cohort of overburdened individuals.8

The willingness of researchers to participate in peer review has declined steadily. Publons projected that the average number of invitations needed to secure a single reviewer’s agreement would rise from 2.7 in 2017 to approximately 3.6 by 2025.53 This projected increase reflects a growing reluctance among researchers to undertake peer review work that is unpaid, poorly recognized by institutions, and in direct competition with the publication activities on which their career advancement depends.54

The accelerating volume of submissions, further fueled by LLM-enabled manuscript production, has outpaced the available reviewer pool, resulting in longer review times, increased reliance on less-experienced reviewers, and evaluations that may lack the depth necessary to identify LLM-generated content, hallucinated references, or methodological deficiencies.9

The consequences for editorial quality are tangible. When reviewers are scarce, editors face pressure to accept reviews of lower quality, to rely on fewer reviewers per manuscript, or to make editorial decisions with incomplete evaluative input. In this context, the additional expectation that reviewers should also serve as a front line of defense against undisclosed AI use is neither realistic nor sustainable.

The emerging concept of AI as an editorial co-pilot

The structural constraints described above establish a clear imperative: rather than positioning editors and reviewers as enforcers of AI prohibition, the editorial community should explore how AI tools can be deployed to support the editorial process itself. The question is not whether editors will use AI assistance. Evidence suggests that many already do, informally and without systematic governance. The more important question is how to formalize, optimize, and govern that assistance responsibly.

The concept of an “AI co-editor,” a structured framework for integrating LLM-based tools into the editorial workflow as a transparent complement to human judgment, offers a pragmatic response to the operational realities described above. This concept is not without precedent: Springer Nature announced the deployment of an in-house AI tool for automated editorial quality checks in 2025, and multiple publishers have reported that AI-supported workflows have achieved substantial acceleration in manuscript triage times.55,56,57 The framework proposed here extends these initial efforts into a comprehensive, stage-based model.

Stage 1: Pre-review screening (automated triage)

The initial manuscript screening phase presents the most immediate opportunity for AI-assisted editorial support. AI tools could assist with manuscript completeness checks, reference verification, statistical consistency assessments, and language quality evaluation, helping reduce administrative burdens before peer review.35,58

Stage 2: Reviewer support

The second stage aims to reduce the time and cognitive burden associated with manuscript evaluation while preserving reviewer judgment. AI-assisted support may include structured manuscript summaries, reporting guideline assessments, and provision of relevant literature context, enabling reviewers to focus on substantive scientific evaluation.

Stage 3: Editorial decision support

The third stage supports editorial decision-making after peer review. AI tools may assist editors by synthesizing reviewer comments, identifying areas of agreement and disagreement, and tracking revisions across manuscript versions.

Stage 4: Post-publication monitoring

The final stage extends AI assistance beyond editorial decisions to post-publication monitoring. Potential applications include the detection of citation anomalies and the identification of emerging integrity concerns that may warrant further investigation.

Governance principles for AI co-editor implementation

The deployment of AI tools within the editorial process itself raises governance questions that mirror those faced on the author side, and that demand equally transparent answers.

Transparency. Journals that use AI-assisted editorial tools should disclose this practice in their editorial policies, specifying which functions are AI-assisted and how AI outputs inform editorial decisions. The current asymmetry, in which journals require authors and reviewers to disclose AI use while not disclosing their own, is unsustainable.

Human authority. All substantive editorial decisions, including acceptance, rejection, revision requests, must remain the sole responsibility of human editors. The AI co-editor generates recommendations, flags issues, and provides structured information; it does not decide.

Bias auditing. AI-assisted editorial tools must be regularly audited for systematic biases, including language bias (disadvantaging non-native English speakers), institutional bias (favoring submissions from prestigious affiliations), and geographic bias (differential treatment of submissions from different regions).

Data security and confidentiality. Manuscript content processed by AI tools must be handled under strict confidentiality protocols. Journals must ensure that submitted manuscripts are not used to train commercial AI models, and data processing agreements must be transparent and compliant with applicable regulations.

Incremental adoption. Implementation should follow a phased approach, beginning with low-risk, high-value functions (structural checks, reference verification) and expanding to more complex functions (review synthesis, decision support) only after validation and stakeholder acceptance. TOWARD A COMPREHENSIVE EDITORIAL FRAMEWORK: PRINCIPLES FOR RESPONSIBLE LLM INTEGRATION

The preceding sections have established that the challenges posed by LLMs to scholarly medical publishing are structural, multifaceted, and inadequately addressed by current policies. Earlier sections characterized the interacting pressures of manuscript volume, economic incentives, and disclosure gaps; documented the fragmentation of journal and publisher responses; demonstrated the unreliability of detection-based enforcement; and proposed an AI co-editor model to address the operational constraints under which editors work. This section synthesizes these analyses into a set of integrative principles that can guide editorial policy development across the LLM era.

Tiered disclosure standards

The current approach to AI disclosure, which generally requires a binary statement of whether AI was used, is insufficient to capture the diversity of ways in which LLMs contribute to scholarly work. A more informative framework would adopt a tiered disclosure system, standardized across journals through coordination among ICMJE, COPE, WAME, and regional editorial associations.

Tier 1: Assistive use. Language editing, grammar correction, translation assistance, and basic text formatting. Disclosure requirement: brief acknowledgment in the Acknowledgments section.

Tier 2: Augmentative use. Literature search assistance, code generation, data visualization, statistical analysis support, and structured writing assistance with human verification of all outputs. Disclosure requirement: detailed description of the AI tool, version, and functions used in the Methods section.

Tier 3: Substantive use. Significant AI contribution to study design, conceptual framing, analytical strategy, data interpretation, or manuscript drafting where AI-generated content constitutes a substantial portion of the final text. Disclosure requirement: comprehensive Methods section disclosure including representative prompts, the nature and extent of AI-generated outputs, and the process by which human authors verified, revised, and validated the content. Where appropriate, Tier 3 disclosures may be aligned with emerging frameworks such as the Generative AI Delegation Taxonomy (GAIDeT).50

A limitation of any disclosure-based system is its vulnerability to reporting bias, including the underreporting of AI use. Authors may have incentives to classify substantive AI involvement as lower-level use, particularly when norms regarding acceptable AI use remain uncertain across journals.

Therefore, disclosure requirements should be complemented by proportionate verification mechanisms that promote accurate reporting without imposing excessive administrative burdens. Selective audits and consistency checks may provide practical approaches for supporting disclosure compliance while avoiding reliance on punitive detection strategies.

This tiered system should be integrated with the CRediT taxonomy framework to provide a standardized vocabulary for reporting AI contributions that is both machine-readable and human-interpretable.49,50,51

Restructuring peer review for the LLM era

The peer review process must be adapted to reflect the new realities of the LLM era, both in terms of what reviewers evaluate and how they are supported in doing so.

Redefining evaluation criteria. The traditional emphasis on writing quality as a proxy for scholarly rigor loses much of its value when LLMs can generate polished text regardless of underlying scientific merit. Review criteria should increasingly prioritize dimensions such as clinical relevance, methodological appropriateness, causal reasoning, ethical rigor, and genuine novelty.

Structured review templates. Journals should develop review templates that operationalize these LLM-resistant evaluation criteria. Rather than relying heavily on general assessments of writing quality, review templates should encourage reviewers to focus on whether the research question is clinically meaningful, whether the methods are appropriate for the specific context, and whether the conclusions are adequately supported by the data. Such templates may also help reviewers assess the plausibility and specificity of reported findings, areas in which superficially convincing AI-generated manuscripts may be particularly vulnerable.

Explicit reviewer AI policies. Journals should establish clear policies regarding reviewer use of AI tools, including disclosure requirements, permissible uses, and safeguards for maintaining manuscript confidentiality.

Recognition and incentivization. The peer reviewer shortage cannot be resolved through AI assistance alone. Structural reforms, including formal recognition of peer review in academic evaluation, reviewer compensation, and integration of review activities into career advancement metrics, are necessary to sustain the reviewer workforce.53,59

Equity-conscious policy design

The evidence reviewed in this article consistently demonstrates that the impacts of LLM-related policies are distributed unequally, with non-native English speakers and researchers from LMICs bearing disproportionate costs.

Equity-conscious policy design requires several concrete commitments. First, policies must explicitly distinguish between assistive AI use (language support, translation) and generative AI use (content creation, data analysis), ensuring that language assistance, which serves an equity function, is not conflated with intellectual dishonesty. Second, detection tools, if used, must be validated for fairness across linguistic and geographic populations, with their known biases openly acknowledged in editorial policies. Third, journals should consider the economic implications of their AI policies: if LLMs reduce the cost of manuscript preparation for LMIC researchers, this benefit should not be negated by punitive enforcement regimes that disproportionately target the same population.

International coordination is essential to achieving equitable standards. Regional editorial associations, including the Korean Association of Medical Journal Editors (KAMJE), the Asia Pacific Association of Medical Journal Editors (APAME), and the African Journals Partnership Program, should participate actively in the development of international AI guidelines to ensure that the perspectives and constraints of non-Western editorial communities are represented.23,60

Institutional and infrastructure requirements

The framework proposed in this review cannot be implemented by individual editors acting alone. Institutional investment and collaborative infrastructure are necessary preconditions.

AI literacy for editorial boards. Editorial board members need structured training on LLM capabilities, limitations, and the practical implications for editorial decision-making. This training should be incorporated into the existing continuing education frameworks provided by organizations such as COPE, WAME, and KAMJE.

Shared technical infrastructure and the risk of a new editorial digital divide. The development and maintenance of AI co-editor tools require resources that exceed the capacity of most individual journals, particularly society journals and journals from LMICs. This resource asymmetry introduces a significant equity concern that must be confronted directly. If AI-assisted editorial systems become concentrated among well-resourced commercial publishers while smaller journals lack the capacity to adopt similar tools, a new form of editorial digital divide may emerge, widening existing disparities in editorial capacity, efficiency, and quality.

To mitigate this risk, collaborative approaches, including shared and open-source editorial tools developed by editorial associations, library consortia, and scholarly communication organizations, should be actively supported. Such models offer a more equitable and sustainable approach than requiring individual journals to develop proprietary solutions independently.

Funding and sustainability. The costs of AI infrastructure, bias auditing, and editorial training must be addressed explicitly. Options for sustainable funding may include support from publishers, editorial associations, research funding agencies, and collaborative cost-sharing arrangements. Importantly, funding mechanisms should prioritize journals and editorial communities with limited existing capacity, ensuring that AI-assisted editorial systems do not reinforce existing inequities in scholarly publishing.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The framework proposed in this review is conceptual and has not yet been empirically validated. Furthermore, the available evidence is drawn largely from high-income, English-speaking settings, which may limit the generalizability of these recommendations.

FUTURE DIRECTIONS

The trajectory of LLM technology and its implications for publishing

The challenges discussed in this review reflect the current state of LLM technology, but the trajectory of development suggests that the landscape will continue to evolve in ways that demand anticipatory, rather than reactive, editorial responses.

The emergence of multimodal LLMs, capable of generating not only text but also images, figures, statistical analyses, and even experimental designs, extends the challenge of AI-generated content beyond the written word.61 As these systems become increasingly sophisticated, existing editorial policies, including current restrictions on AI-generated images, may face growing implementation challenges. The detection of AI-generated content beyond text remains less developed than text detection and will require further methodological attention.34

Autonomous research agents represent a further frontier. Recent developments in AI systems capable of conducting literature reviews, formulating hypotheses, designing experiments, and generating manuscripts raise important questions regarding authorship, accountability, and the role of human oversight in research.62 Although fully autonomous AI-conducted research remains limited in biomedicine, editorial frameworks should be developed proactively as these capabilities continue to expand.

The convergence of LLMs with clinical data systems adds a medical-specific dimension. The integration of LLMs with electronic health records, clinical trial registries, and real-world evidence platforms is creating new forms of AI-assisted research that existing editorial frameworks were not designed to accommodate.63 Editors will need to evaluate not only whether AI was used in manuscript preparation but also whether AI contributed to the underlying research process, requiring a broader conception of disclosure across the research lifecycle.

Toward provenance and verification infrastructure

The long-term sustainability of scholarly communication in the LLM era may depend on the development of technical infrastructure for establishing the intellectual provenance of manuscripts.

Version control–based provenance tracking has been proposed as one approach for creating an auditable record of how human and AI contributions evolve throughout manuscript development, and has already been implemented in real authoring workflows.64 While technical and implementation challenges remain, the broader principle of verifiable provenance addresses limitations that disclosure statements alone may not fully resolve.

LLM watermarking represents a complementary approach. Techniques for embedding identifiable markers in AI-generated text are currently under development and may provide a more reliable means of identifying AI-generated content than post hoc detection tools.65 However, the effectiveness of watermarking depends on cooperation from AI developers, and its vulnerability to text modification and paraphrasing remains an open technical question.

Open-source verification tools are particularly important for journals with limited resources. Publicly available tools for reference verification, statistical consistency checking, and provenance analysis could help democratize access to editorial quality assurance capabilities that are currently concentrated among well-resourced publishers.

The need for an international consensus

The fragmentation of editorial policies reflects the absence of a coordinated international response to a challenge that is inherently global. Individual journals and publishers have developed policies in relative isolation, resulting in inconsistencies that create confusion for authors, reviewers, and editors operating across multiple venues.

A concerted effort toward international consensus is warranted. One concrete proposal is the convening of a global editorial summit on AI in scholarly publishing, jointly organized by ICMJE, COPE, WAME, and regional editorial associations, with the objective of establishing harmonized minimum standards for AI disclosure, governance, and enforcement.60 Such standards should be designed as a floor rather than a ceiling, establishing baseline requirements that all journals are expected to meet while allowing individual journals to adopt more specific or stringent policies as appropriate to their scope and readership.

Funding agencies and academic institutions have a critical role to play in this process. Research funders can promote responsible AI use by incorporating AI disclosure and governance requirements into grant conditions and institutional compliance frameworks.59 Similarly, universities and research institutions can reinforce responsible AI use by including AI-related publication practices in research integrity training and by recognizing editorial and peer review contributions in promotion and tenure criteria.

CONCLUSION

The integration of LLMs into the scholarly medical publishing ecosystem is neither reversible nor containable within the boundaries of existing editorial frameworks. As this review has documented, LLMs have intersected with pre-existing structural vulnerabilities, including escalating APCs, the publish-or-perish incentive structure, a chronic peer reviewer shortage, and inadequate editorial resources, creating a set of interconnected challenges that affect every stakeholder in the publishing pipeline.

The response from the scholarly publishing community, while substantial in volume, has been fragmented in execution. International standards bodies have established important consensus principles, including the prohibition of AI authorship, the requirement for human accountability, and the necessity of transparent disclosure. However, the translation of these principles into enforceable practice remains incomplete. Major medical journals and publishers occupy a wide spectrum of policy positions, with most journals adopting intermediate stances that leave critical definitional and enforcement questions unresolved. Detection-based approaches have proven fundamentally unreliable, with documented biases against non-native English speakers that raise serious equity concerns for international medical journals.

This review has argued that the path forward requires a shift in orientation: from policing AI use to governing its integration. Three proposals have been advanced. First, a three-tiered disclosure framework provides a more informative alternative to the binary disclosure model that currently predominates. Second, the AI co-editor model offers a practical approach to supporting editors operating under increasing workload and resource constraints. Third, equity-conscious policy design, structured peer review reform, and international coordination provide the institutional foundations necessary to ensure that AI-integrated publishing remains both effective and fair.

The stakes of this transition are particularly consequential in medical publishing. The literature that editors curate and publish directly informs clinical guidelines, treatment protocols, regulatory decisions, and public health policy. Contamination of this evidence base, whether through hallucinated references, fabricated data, or the dilution of genuine scholarship by formulaic AI-generated content, has the potential to cause tangible harm to patients and populations. Editors of medical journals are not merely administrators of a publication process; they are stewards of the evidence base on which clinical medicine depends.

The LLM era does not diminish the importance of this stewardship. It transforms it, demanding greater technological literacy, more nuanced ethical judgment, more systematic governance, and a more collaborative approach to the development of editorial standards. The medical journal editor of the coming decade will need to be not only a clinical and scientific expert but also a technologically informed leader capable of navigating the complex intersection of AI, research integrity, and the public interest. The framework proposed in this review is offered as a contribution to that navigation.
