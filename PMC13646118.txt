Subjective experience of listening to favorite songs and Murottal Al-Qur’an in young adult women: a quantitative EEG case series

Kana Khairunnisa, Yusuf Alam Romadhon

Abstract

IntroductionListening to music and Qur’anic murottal is a conscious auditory experience associated with beneficial health outcomes. Both activities share the same sensory modality called audition; however, murottal listening is additionally linked to spiritual belief, whereas music listening is not necessarily so, although both are reported to have favorable effects on health. The subjective, phenomenological aspect of conscious listening has rarely been examined alongside objective neurophysiological measurements.MethodsIn this descriptive case series, four healthy 19-year-old female participants underwent a fixed-order recording session that compared mean alpha-band (8–12 Hz) power during listening (their favorite song and murottal, 3 min each), with mean alpha power during an eyes-closed, resting baseline, expressed as a relative percentage change: (E − B)/B × 100%. This index reflects relative alpha event-related desynchronization/synchronization (ERD/ERS) rather than the classical event-related potential. Heart rate and unstructured subjective reports were collected concurrently.Results and conclusionIndividual alpha ERD/ERS topography broadly aligned with participants’ self-reported mental states—relaxation, visual imagery, and concentration—during both music and murottal listening, with notable between-subject variability in hemispheric lateralization. Given the small, non-randomized sample and the absence of inferential statistics, these observations are considered as hypothesis-generating rather than generalizable and are intended to inform the design of a larger, counterbalanced study.

Conscious experience of listening to music and Murottal Al-Qur’an

The conscious experience of listening to music arises from the engagement of distributed neural networks following the perception of auditory and rhythmic features produced by the music (). This series of music-related conscious events further evokes neural processing of meaning creation and aesthetic awareness during listening (). Music can also change a person’s state of consciousness, such as into a shamanic state, through auditory rhythm tuning and certain neurofeedback exercises (). In addition, music strongly influences an individual’s emotions, thoughts, actions, and cognitive functions. These changes in behavior, for example, affect shopping behavior and strengthen or weaken motivation, so they are used as a part of psychotherapy (). The sound of music is unique and can become a cultural identity, and individual responses have different uniqueness (; ). Listening to music often generates a sense of pleasure, as a reflection of the performance of the reward circuit in the brain (). Music, with its character of rhythms, tones, and pauses of silence when perceived auditorily, triggers various evocations of neural activity in the brain. The resulting evocations include a sense of deep emotion, foreshadowing of horizons, and triggering of relevant memories related to various life events (; ).

The verses of the Qur’an recited during murottal murottal are, in principle, auditory experience that engages senses and evokes various neural processing, fostering psychospiritual consciousness of the Qur’anic verses heard (). This psychospiritual consciousness is created because of previous beliefs, so that this activity of listening to Qur’anic murottal has a transformative power to evoke a sense of catharsis, relaxation, and relief from psychoemotional tension (). The experience of listening to Qur’anic murottal, phenomenologically, is unique, and according to a study, this experience is influenced by gender differences, education levels, and the intensity of a person’s psychospiritual interaction with the Qur’an (). Exploration related to the experience of psychospiritual consciousness when listening to Qur’anic murottal shows that the activity of listening to Qur’anic murottal triggers the neural networks involved in empathy, “mentalizing,” and episodic memory. As a result, individuals can perceive visual images in the space of consciousness according to the content of the Qur’anic verses they hear (). Another impact of listening to Qur’anic murottal on coma patients in the intensive care unit can increase their level of consciousness (). A 2026 study further corroborates the neurobiological basis of this effect, reporting that structured Qur’an recitation is associated with increased alpha-band activity alongside favorable modulations of neurotrophic and stress-related biomarkers ().

Quantitative EEG

Brain neuron cells produce action potentials that summate into rhythmic electrical activity, which can be recorded non-invasively using electroencephalography (). These rhythms are categorized into δ – delta (< 4 Hz), θ – theta (4–7 Hz), α – alpha (8–15 Hz), β – beta (16–31 Hz), and γ – gamma (> 31 Hz) bands (). Quantitative electroencephalography (qEEG) is a non-invasive method of recording brain electrical activity, representing the summation of postsynaptic potentials aligned across activated pyramidal neurons (). Specifically, qEEG applies spectral power analysis across frequency bands as an indicator of regional cortical engagement during specific cognitive tasks (), with temporal resolution on a millisecond scale (). Distinct mental states are associated with characteristic qEEG signatures; in states of stress and anxiety, for instance, elevated beta-band activity is frequently observed (13–30 Hz), with a corresponding dominance of high beta waves (> 20 Hz) (). Accordingly, qEEG has previously been used to index brain activity during attention- and error-monitoring tasks () in both healthy and clinical populations ().

A distributed set of brain regions is preferentially active when the brain is not engaged in an externally focused, goal-directed task, referred to as the default mode network (DMN) (). Comparing spectral band power (e.g., alpha oscillations) between a resting baseline and a task condition quantifies the relative change in oscillatory power. This change is termed event-related desynchronization (ERD) when power decreases, or event-related synchronization (ERS) when power increases, relative to the baseline (). This block-averaged relative power change is conceptually distinct from the classical event-related potential (ERP), which denotes time-locked, phase-aligned voltage deflections (e.g., P300, N400) occurring within milliseconds of a discrete, repeated stimulus. Because the present experimental design compares alpha power averaged over a 3-min listening block with a 2-min eyes-closed baseline, the appropriate operational term for our outcome measure is relative alpha ERD/ERS, which is used throughout this manuscript. EEG recordings also show a well-documented right–left asymmetry, more pronounced for verbal than visual processing in the temporoparietal region (), commonly attributed to hemispheric/hand dominance ().

Although explicitly stating gender differences, data presented in a volumetric study of the hippocampus showed that there are differences in the mean volume of both the hippocampus as a whole and its constituent components ().

Quantitative EEG and listening to music and Murottals in the female gender

Listening to the auditory recitation of the Qur’an from others, commonly called Al-Qur’an murottal, has a long-standing tradition as a therapeutic modality for promoting psychological well-being among Muslim populations. The acoustic structure of the Qur’ān exhibits inherent rhythmic and aesthetic qualities that evoke psychospiritual tranquility, with favorable effects on mental health (; ). Active engagement with the Qur’ān through memorization, periodic recitation, and listening has been linked to improvements in sleep quality, blood pressure regulation, and other physical and mental health biomarkers (), including reduced anxiety during labor () and significant reductions in heart rate and blood pressure following surgery under general anesthesia, as reported in a case study from Universitas Muhammadiyah Surakarta (). Listening to Qur’anic recitation has been associated with a global decrease in EEG band power across frontal and temporal regions, which are interpreted as neurofunctional correlates of enhanced emotion regulation and relaxation (; ). A 2025 systematic review of 22 EEG studies corroborates this pattern, reporting a consistent association between Qur’an listening and increased alpha- and theta-band power linked to meditative and relaxed states, albeit with substantial heterogeneity across methodological designs ().

Listening to music carries an inherent rhythmicity tailored to its intended use (), and accumulating EEG evidence continues to associate music listening reliably induces relaxed, alpha-dominant oscillatory states, with genre-dependent regional topographical differences (). Consistent with this, an investigation at Universitas Muhammadiyah Surakarta demonstrated that a 15 min session of culturally familiar relaxing music (traditional Tingkilan Kutai music) produced a significant reduction in perceived stress among students prior to a high-stakes academic examination (). Nonetheless, neuroimaging and electrophysiological findings regarding genre-specific neural substrates of remain partially inconclusive (). Music listening also produces emotional–cognitive responses that may differ by sex; one study reported higher average EEG frequency-band responses in women than men ().

Some differences in the characteristics of the female brain compared to men include a thicker cortex in the majority of areas of the brain. Women tend to have a smaller brain volume and lower motor performance than men (). A systematic review found that, in general, men and women share more similarities. However, women consistently excel in auditory processing of phonetic cues during speech perception and production (). In terms of auditory processing, women are stronger than men when in a passive listener position, as found in a study examining event-related potentials (ERP) (). Another difference found in qEEG studies is in terms of working memory. Brain regions involved in working memory among men were more prominent in the posterior dipole (occipital, parietal, and temporal), while in women, they were in the frontal cortex structures (). Women’s voices, as sound stimuli that evoke responses in related brain areas, are stronger than men (). In terms of cerebral blood flow in certain regions, there are some differences. Blood flow in the right precuneus, left superior temporal gyrus, left inferior frontal gyrus, left cerebellar tonsil, and right medial temporal gyrus is higher in women than men. Differences in glucose metabolism speed between certain regions of men and women were also found (). The thickness of the left Heschl’s gyrus was greater in women compared to men. This finding implies that biological factors of sex are involved in the neural network of speech production (). Listening to music and murottal, to some extent, both evoke calmness (). Therefore, the discussion of differences in the prefrontal cortex and amygdala () serves as a reference point for discussing differences in male and female responses to Qur’anic music or murottal stimuli in this study. Neuroimaging studies show that men are more efficient at regulating emotions than women. This reflects lower prefrontal cortex activity levels and the same amygdala activity in men. However, this sex difference depends on the relevant emotion content between the two, as well as emotion generation. Furthermore, this difference is more associated with the rating of the emotional charge involved, where women are more likely to give higher ratings to negatively emotionally charged events ().

Observation method

Recordings were conducted in a quiet room under natural morning daylight without artificial lighting; participants were seated and asked to open and close their eyes for 2 min each. The initial eyes-closed epoch served as the pre-stimulus baseline, followed by 3 min of listening to a favorite song. A 5 min silent washout period followed, after which a second 2-min eyes-closed baseline was recorded, followed by 3 min of listening to familiar music or murottal Al-Qur’an. This order—favorite song always preceding the familiar music/murottal condition—was identical and fixed for all four participants; it was not randomized or counterbalanced, a methodological limitation addressed in the Discussion. Because a fresh baseline was recorded immediately before each listening condition, the baseline heart rate and alpha power reported for the song condition and for the murottal/familiar-music condition of the same participant are independent measurements taken at different points in the session, which accounts for any difference between the two baseline values reported for a given participant. The electroencephalogram was recorded continuously with a Neurology EEG deviceTM (Canada), 20 Ag/AgCl electrodes (international 10–20 system), ipsilateral-ear reference, impedance < 5 kΩ, filter settings of 0.53–35 Hz (low cut–high cut) with notch filter on, sensitivity 7 μV/mm, and paper/recording speed 30 mm/s, as documented in the original instrument printouts retained for each participant. Quantitative spectral power analysis (FFT relative power) and the generation of topographic maps were performed using qEEG analysis software from Neurology EEG device (TM), Applied NeuroScience, Inc., Canada. This analysis was applied to the raw recordings exported from the acquisition system. Artifact-free epochs from the baseline and listening conditions were selected for analysis by visual inspection, with alpha activity defined as 8–12 Hz and averaged per electrode. The percentage change in mean alpha power during listening (E) relative to the mean alpha power during the immediately preceding baseline (B) was calculated as:
(E−B)/B×100%

Yielding relative alpha event-related desynchronization/synchronization (ERD/ERS): a positive value indicates relative synchronization (ERS), and a negative value indicates relative desynchronization (ERD) (). This block-averaged measure is distinct from the classical event-related potential and should not be conflated with it. The analysis was performed at each electrode and presented in tabulated and topographic form. The left electrodes were Fp1, F7, F3, A1, T3, C3, T5, P3, O1; the right electrodes were Fp2, F4, F8, A2, T4, C4, T6, P4, O2; the midline electrodes were Fz, Cz, and Pz. Artifacts considered during recording included eyelid and facial muscle contraction and cardiac electrical activity, identified by visual inspection of the concurrent ECG channel and excluded from the analyzed epochs; a formal automated artifact-rejection pipeline (e.g., independent component analysis) was not applied, which is noted as a limitation below.

Operational definition

Alpha ERD/ERS: the percentage change in mean alpha (8–12 Hz) power during listening compared with the immediately preceding 2-min eyes-closed baseline. Formula: ERD/ERS (%) = (E − B)/B × 100%, where E is the mean alpha power during listening (music or murottal), and B is the mean alpha power at baseline. Instrument: EEG device. Unit: percent. Scale: continuous.Heart rate: the number of heartbeats in a 10-s sampling window, multiplied by six to obtain beats per minute. Unit: beats per minute. Scale: continuous.

Serial report

Four female participants took part in this case series. For each participant, we report the subjective, first-person account of the listening experience; the heart rate at baseline versus during listening; and the corresponding alpha ERD/ERS topography, presented as a standardized red–blue heatmap (red = ERS/relative increase, blue = ERD/relative decrease). All participants listened to the same murottal recitation, Surat Al-Alaq, recited by Muzammil Hasbullah (95–100 beats per minute); favorite songs were individually selected by each participant.

Discussion

Four descriptive patterns recurred across the four cases: (1) increases in middle temporal/auricular alpha ERD/ERS plausibly related to task and setting adaptation (a “laboratory effect”), most pronounced in Participant 4; (2) increases in prefrontal alpha ERD/ERS plausibly related to effortful attention toward stimulus content; (3) increases in occipital alpha ERD/ERS plausibly related to visual imagery, present in Participants 1, 3, and 4; and (4) increases in middle-temporal-to-central alpha ERD/ERS co-occurring with subjective reports of calmness and relaxation, most clearly in Participants 1 and 2. These are within-subject, pattern-level co-occurrences rather than statistically validated associations, given the sample size (N = 4) and the absence of a counterbalanced control condition or inferential statistical testing.

Listening to music is a highly individual experience, with response differences attributable to both sex and musical expertise (). Music listening can influence stimulus–response timing in women, although it may also impair certain cognitive performance measures in both sexes (). Traditional Indonesian karawitan music, like Mozart’s piano sonatas, has been reported to increase frontal lobe activity (). A 2025 study that explicitly used alpha-ERD as an outcome measure found lower alpha-ERD following music listening than after passive rest, interpreted as a marker of restored attentional capacity (), a finding broadly compatible with the relaxation-associated ERD/ERS patterns observed here in Participants 1–3 in the present study.

Binaural beat therapy, using differing sound frequencies between the right and left ears, has similarly been associated with alpha-wave induction in specific brain regions in several studies (; ). The calming, parasympathetically mediated mechanism proposed here is broadly consistent with clinical findings at Universitas Muhammadiyah Surakarta, where a literature review of music therapy in intensive care unit patients similarly reported favorable calming effects on physiological and psychological stress indices (), while pre-treatment music listening measurably reduced dental procedure anxiety alongside a reduction in systolic blood pressure (). Taken together with the qEEG-Quran literature discussed above () these findings support alpha ERD/ERS as a plausible, if still preliminary, neurophysiological correlate of the relaxation and internally generated mental imagery reported by our participants, while underscoring the need for adequately powered, randomized, and counterbalanced designs before causal or generalizable claims can be established.

This case series has both strengths and limitations that directly inform the interpretation of our findings. Its principal strength lies in the integration of simultaneous subjective (first-person phenomenological reports), physiological (heart rate), and quantitative EEG measurements within the same experimental session, using a Qur’anic murottal stimulus standardized across participants and a personally meaningful comparison stimulus (favorite song), which together allow rich within-subject description. Its principal limitations are, first, the small sample (N = 4), restricted to healthy 19-year-old female university students, which inherently limits the generalizability of these observations across broader age cohorts, sex, and clinical status; second, the fixed, non-counterbalanced stimulus order, such that order, fatigue, and habituation effects cannot be fully separated from stimulus-specific effects—most apparent in Participant 4, whose murottal recording shows an extreme, diffuse ERS pattern alongside explicitly reported situational tension; third, the absence of inferential statistics, such that the co-occurrences described above remain observational; and fourth, the fact that alpha ERD/ERS is a block-averaged relative power change that must not be conflated with the classical event-related potential. As this is a single-session experimental design without a clinical intervention, no follow-up or outcomes trajectory in the clinical sense applies, consistent with the descriptive, non-clinical nature of the study. All four participants’ first-person accounts (Serial Report, above) are treated as primary phenomenological data alongside the qEEG measures, in keeping with the study’s phenomenological framing. Fifth, heart rate was originally derived from a 10-s sampling window read directly off the clinical strip, a method susceptible to transient measurement and transcription error; the baseline and listening heart-rate values for Participant 1 originally reported (162 and 144 bpm) were physiologically implausible for a relaxed resting state and have been corrected following calibrated visual counting of QRS complexes on the original raw ECG trace (using the instrument’s own elapsed-time stamps to confirm a 6-s duration per printed page at 30 mm/s paper speed; Supporting File 2). The corrected values (approximately 95–120 bpm across conditions) remain modestly elevated relative to typical resting heart rate in healthy young adults, which may reflect genuine situational anxiety related to the unfamiliar recording setting, consistent with the tension explicitly reported by Participant 4 elsewhere in this case series, rather than a measurement artifact alone. Because this corrected estimate was obtained by manual counting from a scanned printout rather than a software-computed value, it should be treated as an improved approximation rather than an exact instrument readout. Sixth, artifact handling relied on visual inspection of the concurrent EEG and ECG channels to exclude contaminated epochs, rather than a formal automated pipeline such as Independent Component Analysis; the extreme, diffuse alpha ERS values observed in Participant 4’s murottal recording (Section 3.4) are considered most consistent with residual muscle tension or movement artifact under this visual inspection approach, and we flag this as a specific direction for methodological improvement in future work.

Conclusion

The conscious experience of listening to self-selected favorite music and Qur’anic murottal appears, in this exploratory case series, to be accompanied by highly individualized alpha ERD/ERS topographies that broadly co-occur with participants’ self-reported phenomenological and emotional states. Furthermore, Participant 4’s murottal recording illustrates how situational anxiety and fixed stimulus presentation order can produce disproportionately large spectral power shifts in ERD/ERS values, underscoring the importance of counterbalancing and automated artifact decomposition in future work. Given the descriptive, non-randomized, small-sample design, these findings should be regarded as preliminary and hypothesis-generating. Larger, statistically powered, counterbalanced studies incorporating inferential modeling are needed to determine whether the topographic patterns observed here reflect reliable, generalizable neural correlates of auditory and spiritual neuromodulation.
