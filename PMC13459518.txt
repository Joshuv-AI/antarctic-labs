Safety, Tolerability, and Pharmacokinetics of Oral Camlipixant: A Randomized First‐In‐Human Study Using Single and Multiple Ascending Doses
Authors: Nathalie Chauret, Denis Garceau, Laurent Harvey, Zelie Bailes, Elizabeth A Duncan, Rhian McNaughton, Miren Zamacona
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCamlipixant is a highly selective P2X3 receptor antagonist with demonstrated efficacy in pre‐clinical models of evoked cough. We report the safety, tolerability, and pharmacokinetics of camlipixant in healthy participants. This Phase 1, double‐blind, randomized study evaluated single ascending doses (SAD; 50/100/200/400/800/1200 mg) and multiple ascending doses (MAD; 100/200/400 mg, twice daily [BID]) of oral camlipixant versus placebo. Ninety participants were included (SAD n = 60; MAD n = 30). Treatment‐emergent adverse event (TEAE) incidence was similar between camlipixant (32/72) and placebo (9/18). TEAEs were mostly mild (83%), and the most common with camlipixant was dysgeusia, primarily at doses ≥400 mg (n = 13), versus 50–200 mg (n = 1). Camlipixant was rapidly absorbed (time to maximum plasma concentration SAD: 0.77–2.29 h; MAD: 0.50–2.00 h), with a short half‐life (SAD 4.28–6.71 h; MAD 7.60–8.39 h). In both cohorts, maximum plasma concentration (Cmax) increased proportionally with dose. The area under the curve extrapolated to infinity (AUC0–∞) increased proportionally with dose in the SAD cohort; the increase was slightly supra‐proportional in the MAD cohort (steady state reached on Day 2). The geometric mean apparent volume of distribution ranged from 44.9 to 70.2 L and clearance from 5.7 to 10.4 L/h in the SAD cohort. Pharmacokinetics were unaffected by food (geometric mean fed/fasted ratio Cmax 91.4% [90% confidence interval, CI, 69.8–119.6]; AUC0–∞ 105.5% [90% CI, 89.8–124.0]). In summary, camlipixant was well tolerated, with pharmacokinetics supporting BID dosing and further clinical development for refractory chronic cough.Keywords: camlipixant, chronic cough, P2X3 antagonist, pharmacokinetics, refractory chronic cough, safety

Introduction

Refractory chronic cough (RCC) is a disease identified in a subset of patients who experience chronic cough (persistent cough for >8 weeks) despite adequate treatment for underlying cough‐related etiologies. 1 , 2 , 3 Unexplained chronic cough (UCC) describes a chronic cough for which an underlying condition cannot be identified after adequate investigations with diagnostic tests. 4 , 5 In this paper, the term RCC will be used to refer to both RCC and UCC.

Globally, RCC affects 0.79%−3.3% of adults 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 and the daily, frequent coughing experienced by patients with RCC represents a high burden 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 resulting in a reduction in quality of life, with notable psychological and physical impacts. 14 , 16 , 17 Due to a scarcity of approved treatments and limited access to efficacious and well‐tolerated therapies for RCC, clinicians may prescribe off‐label therapies, including neuromodulators such as gabapentin or pregabalin, or low doses of opioids such as morphine. 4 , 18 , 19 , 20 The use of these agents is often limited by poor tolerability due to adverse events (AEs) like fatigue, dizziness, somnolence, misuse, and dependency, coupled with limited efficacy. 18 , 19 Thus, there is an imminent need for new and effective, well‐tolerated treatments. 20 , 21

Patients with RCC present with symptoms and clinical features of cough hypersensitivity syndrome, characterized by an exaggerated cough response to innocuous stimuli. 18 , 22 This cough reflex involves signaling via P2X3 receptors, which are homotrimeric adenosine triphosphate (ATP)‐activated cation channels made up of three P2X3 subunits and are expressed on airway vagal afferent nerves. 20 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 P2X2/3 is the heteromeric form of the receptor comprising two P2X3 subunits and one P2X2 subunit. 23 Antagonism of P2X3 receptors in patients with RCC reduces cough frequency and severity and improves patient‐reported outcomes, 19 , 27 signifying that P2X3 receptors play a role in the neuronal hypersensitivity associated with RCC. However, taste disturbances are an unintended consequence of P2X2/3 receptor blockade due to low or modest selectivity for P2X3. 27 , 28 A need exists for P2X3 antagonists that minimize this effect while retaining efficacy.

Camlipixant (BLU‐5937) is an oral, peripherally acting, highly selective P2X3 receptor antagonist with demonstrated in vitro activity and anti‐tussive effects in cell and animal models. 24 , 29 At concentrations selective for P2X3 antagonism, camlipixant has been shown to block in vitro ATP‐mediated neuron sensitization and reduce chemically stimulated cough frequency. 24 The pre‐clinical pharmacology of camlipixant has been characterized in animal and cell models, exhibiting dose‐dependent reduction in cough frequency and no effect on taste at concentrations known to inhibit P2X3 receptors and lower than that needed to block P2X2/3 receptors. 24

The Phase 2a RELIEF study evaluated the effect of camlipixant on change in awake cough frequency compared with placebo. 30 Although the primary endpoint was not met, clinically meaningful and nominally significant reductions in awake cough frequency were observed with camlipixant compared with placebo in pre‐specified subgroups with a baseline awake cough frequency of >20 and ≥32.4 coughs/h. 30 In the Phase 2b SOOTHE study, treatment with camlipixant led to statistically significant reductions in placebo‐adjusted 24‐h cough frequency compared with placebo. 31 Improvements in patient‐reported outcomes for cough severity and quality of life were also observed. 31 Camlipixant treatment had a favorable safety and tolerability profile in both RELIEF and SOOTHE studies. 30 , 31 CALM‐1 and CALM‐2 are ongoing Phase 3, randomized, placebo‐controlled studies assessing the efficacy, safety, and tolerability of camlipixant in patients with RCC. 32

This first‐in‐human study was conducted to assess the safety, tolerability, and pharmacokinetics (PK) of camlipixant following single and multiple ascending oral doses in healthy participants to support clinical development of the drug for the treatment of patients with RCC.

Methods

Study Participants, Design, and Procedures

This single‐center, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled, randomized, adaptive study assessed the safety, tolerability, and PK of single ascending doses (SAD) and multiple ascending doses (MAD) of oral camlipixant in healthy adult male and female participants (Figure 1). The primary aim was to assess the safety and tolerability of SAD and MAD of camlipixant with secondary objectives to establish its PK profile and the effect of food (single oral dose).

Study design. aactive drug:placebo ratio. bDoses for the multiple ascending dose cohort were determined and adapted as needed based on safety, tolerability, and PK data collected in the single ascending dose cohort.

Healthy adults (defined by medical history and a physical examination) aged ≥18 to ≤55 years, who were non‐ or ex‐smokers (individuals who completely stopped using nicotine products for ≥180 days prior to first study drug administration), and had a body mass index of ≥18.5 to ≤30 kg/m2 were included. Individuals were excluded if their evaluations demonstrated any clinically significant abnormality in their medical history or physical examination or a positive result for human immunodeficiency virus or hepatitis B or C during medical screening.

Planned enrolment was for 90 participants across two cohorts; a SAD cohort with an integrated food‐effect cohort (n = 60), and an MAD cohort (n = 30). The sample size was considered sufficient for the characterization of safety and tolerability of camlipixant. After a 28‐day screening period, enrolled participants were randomized per the randomization scheme established by Algorithme Pharma, using a computer software program, according to the study design and number of participants.

Participants in the SAD cohort were randomized to receive a single dose of either camlipixant or matching placebo (8:2 ratio) within one of the six ascending dose cohorts (50, 100, 200, 400, 800, or 1200 mg camlipixant; n = 10 per cohort) or until a maximum tolerable dose was identified. The maximum per‐protocol upper dose of 1600 mg was deemed unnecessary following review of the safety, tolerability, and PK data (not shown). It was determined that dose escalation for the last SAD cohort could proceed with a single oral dose of 1200 mg as this was determined to provide a sufficient safety margin relative to the dose range planned to be evaluated in the RELIEF Phase 2a study of up to 200 mg twice daily (BID). The MAD dose levels were to be selected based on results from the SAD. Each participant received a single dose of camlipixant or placebo on Day 1, with approximately 240 mL of water, after an 8‐h overnight fast. Water was allowed ad libitum until 1 h pre dose and 1 h after the dose was administered. Participants in the sentinel group received a dose ≥24 h ahead of the remaining cohort and were monitored for safety before the remainder of the cohort was initiated, with the last and first dose of cohorts separated by ≥7 days.

To assess a potential food effect, participants in the SAD cohort receiving camlipixant 200 mg participated in two treatment periods separated by a washout period of 10 days. Participants were given the same dose in each period under either fasted or fed conditions (standard Food and Drug Administration high‐fat, high‐calorie breakfast 30 min prior to drug administration, following an overnight fast of ≥8 h). 33 Progression to the next dose was based on the assessment of the safety and tolerability data and PK of the previous cohort by the Safety Review Committee (SRC), which comprised the principal investigator at the Clinical Research Unit, an independent physician, and the Sponsor's medical personnel.

Participants in the MAD cohort were randomized to receive either camlipixant or matching placebo (8:2 ratio) within one of the three ascending dose cohorts (100, 200, or 400 mg BID camlipixant; n = 10 per cohort) for 7 consecutive days with ≥7 days between cohorts. The MAD cohort was commenced after the favorable review of the safety, tolerability, and PK data of the SAD cohort by the SRC. Participants in the sentinel group received a dose ≥7 days before the remaining cohort and were monitored for safety over 6 days. Participants were required to fast overnight for ≥8 h before the morning dose on Days 1 and 7 and for ≥2 h before the evening dose, while on Days 2 to 6 participants fasted ≥1 h before dosing where possible. The dose was administered with approximately 240 mL of water. On Days 1 and 7, water was allowed ad libitum until 1 h pre dose and 1 h after the dose was administered and on Days 2 to 6, water was allowed ad libitum.

The study doses were selected based on the predicted therapeutic doses for the management of RCC, with additional supratherapeutic doses included to explore the safety profile of camlipixant.

Ethical Approval

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board, Advarra, and all participants provided written consent prior to the start of study procedures. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki 34 and the International Council on Harmonisation Guideline E6 for Good Clinical Practice. 35

Study Assessments

Safety Assessments

Safety assessments, including the evaluation of reported treatment‐emergent adverse events (TEAEs), were conducted by the SRC. Safety was assessed continuously until participants were discharged from the study site. The monitoring period extended from the pre‐trial evaluation until collection of the last blood sample. A taste questionnaire was provided only to participants who self‐reported taste‐disturbance adverse events (TDAEs) following dosing, defined as dysgeusia or hypogeusia.

Pharmacokinetic Assessment

For the SAD cohort, blood samples for PK evaluation were drawn pre dose and then at 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 15, 18, 24, and 48 h post dose.

For the MAD cohort, blood samples were drawn prior to drug administration, then on Day 1 (0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 24 h post dose), Days 3 to 6 (within 5 min prior to morning drug administration) and Day 7 (within 5 min prior to the last drug administration then at 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 15, 18, 24, and 48 h post dose). The PK samples were assayed for camlipixant at the designated bioanalytical facility by validated high‐performance liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry detection. The lower and upper limits of quantitation for camlipixant of the plasma assay were 5.0 and 12,500.0 ng/mL, respectively.

The PK parameters were calculated by non‐compartmental analysis. The PK analysis was done using Phoenix, WinNolin version 8.0, Phoenix Connect version 1.3.1. PK parameters assessed for the SAD cohort were maximum plasma concentration (Cmax), half‐life (T1/2), time to Cmax (Tmax), area under the concentration–time curve extrapolated to infinity (AUC0–∞), apparent total clearance (CL/F), and apparent volume of distribution (VD/F). PK parameters assessed for the MAD cohort included (Day 1): Cmax, observed concentrations 12 h post dose, plasma concentration–time curve over the dosing interval (AUC0–τ), and Tmax; (Days 3–6): pre‐dose concentration observed immediately prior to the next successive dose (Ctrough); and (Day 7): Ctrough, Cmax, AUC0–τ, T1/2, Tmax, CL/F, VD/F, and accumulation ratio (Cmax and AUC). PK parameters (SAD: Cmax and AUC0–∞; MAD: Cmax, and AUC0–τ) were assessed for dose proportionality.

Statistical Analyses

Safety Analysis

Safety data were summarized using descriptive statistics and frequency tables. All participants who were randomized and received ≥1 dose of either camlipixant or placebo were included in the safety analysis. The incidence of AEs was calculated as the proportion of participants who experienced ≥1 AE. Demographic and baseline data were collected for the safety population and summarized using descriptive statistics.

Pharmacokinetic Analysis

The PK population comprised all participants who received study medication and for whom plasma samples were collected. Descriptive statistics were calculated for all PK parameters.

Food‐Effect Analysis

Log‐transformed PK parameters were analyzed using an analysis of variance (ANOVA) model. The ratio of least squares means between camlipixant given under fasted or fed conditions and 90% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for the log natural (ln)‐transformed Cmax and AUC0–∞.

Dose‐Proportionality Analysis

Dose‐proportionality analysis was performed for Cmax and AUC0–∞ using a power model. A linear ln‐transformed model as a continuous effect was fitted and a point estimate and 90% CI were derived for the slope. If the 90% CI of the slope (β) was within 0.80 to 1.25, the parameter could be considered dose proportional.

Statistical analyses were generated using SAS version 9.4 (mixed procedure).

Results

In the SAD cohort, 109 failed screening, due to out‐of‐range laboratory values (n = 24), consent withdrawn (n = 15), out‐of‐range vital signs (n = 13), exclusion due to medical history (n = 8), screened as back‐up participants who were not randomized (n = 12), or other reasons (n = 37) (Figure 2). In the MAD cohort, there were 52 screening failures due to out‐of‐range laboratory values (n = 6) and vital signs (n = 6), five withdrew consent, refused the study (n = 14), or failed screening due to other reasons (n = 21) (Figure 2).

Safety Results

The safety population comprised all 90 participants who received a dose of study medication. The proportion of participants who reported ≥1 TEAE was similar in the camlipixant (32/72) and placebo (9/18) groups (Table 2). In the SAD cohorts, 51 TEAEs were reported among 48 participants receiving camlipixant and 12 TEAEs among participants receiving placebo. Of these, 41/51 were deemed by the investigator to be related to camlipixant and 8/12 were deemed to be related to placebo. TEAEs only occurred at camlipixant doses of 400 mg (n = 8), 800 mg (n = 22), and 1200 mg (n = 11). In the MAD cohorts, 66 TEAEs were reported among 24 participants receiving camlipixant and four TEAEs among six participants receiving placebo. Of these, 47/66 were deemed by the investigator to be related to camlipixant and 4/4 were deemed to be related to placebo. The most common TEAEs amongst participants receiving camlipixant were dysgeusia (14/72 vs 0 for placebo), headache (8/72 vs 1/18), hypoesthesia (7/72 vs 0), dizziness (4/72 vs 0), nausea (6/72 vs 1/18), and dyspepsia (4/72 vs 0) (Table 2).

TEAEs across the camlipixant group were mostly mild (83%; 97/117); 16% (19/117) were moderate and one was severe (1%; 1/117) (hypoesthesia, camlipixant 400 mg BID). No serious AEs or deaths were reported, and there were no withdrawals due to safety reasons.

All TDAEs (predominantly dysgeusia [n = 18], with three events of hypogeusia) were mild (17/21) or moderate (4/21). Participants experienced TDAEs primarily in camlipixant dosing groups 400 to 1200 mg (n = 13) compared with camlipixant 50 to 200 mg (n = 1) (Table 2). In the SAD cohorts, 11 TDAEs were reported (dysgeusia = 10; hypogeusia = 1), showing a dose‐related trend with most occurring in the camlipixant 800 mg group (dysgeusia = 7; hypogeusia = 1). Events were generally mild, with onset typically occurring within approximately 40 min post dose, and resolved without intervention within approximately 1 h to 2 days. In the MAD cohorts, 10 TDAEs were reported (dysgeusia = 8; hypogeusia = 2), also showing a dose‐related trend with most occurring in the camlipixant 400 mg group (dysgeusia = 6; hypogeusia = 2). Events were generally mild to moderate, with a rapid onset (typically within ∼1 h post dose), and resolved without intervention within a few hours (range: 1–10 h).

For the SAD cohort, after a single dose of camlipixant, absorption occurred rapidly with median Tmax ranging from 0.77 to 2.29 h post dose across the dose range of 50 to 1200 mg with the longest Tmax observed at higher dose levels. Plasma concentrations decreased thereafter, with geometric mean T1/2 ranging from 4.28 to 6.71 h over the dose range of 50 to 1200 mg. The geometric mean range for VD/F was 44.9 to 70.2 L and range of the geometric mean CL/F was 5.7 to 10.4 L/h.

The effect of food on the PK of camlipixant was evaluated in the 200 mg single dose group. When comparing primary PK parameters (Cmax, Tmax, and AUC0–∞) under fasted and fed conditions, the peak absorption and extent of exposure were not notably affected by food. The geometric mean fed/fasted ratio for Cmax was 91.4% (90% CI, 69.8–119.6), while the median Tmax was slightly delayed from 1.00 h under fasted conditions to 1.53 h under fed conditions and AUC0–∞ was 105.5% (90% CI, 89.8–124.0).

For the MAD cohort, over the complete dose range, on Days 1 and 7, median Tmax was approximately 0.75 h (range: 0.50–2.00 h). Following the last dose on Day 7, the geometric mean T1/2 was between 7.60 to 8.39 h over the 100 to 400 mg BID dose range. The resulting accumulation ratios (Day 7/Day 1) for Cmax and AUC0–τ ranged from 1.07 to 1.32 and 1.30 to 1.51, respectively. Based on a visual assessment of Ctrough data, steady‐state plasma concentrations of camlipixant were reached on Day 2, consistent with the relatively short T1/2 (Figure 5).

Dose Proportionality

For the SAD cohort, the systemic exposure (Cmax and AUC0–∞) of a single dose of camlipixant was dose proportional over the 50 to 1200 mg dose range. The dose‐proportionality statistical analysis showed that the point estimate and related 90% CI of the regression slopes based on a power model were approximately 0.96 (90% CI, 0.89–1.03) and 1.14 (90% CI, 1.06–1.21), across the 50 to 1200 mg dose range for Cmax, and AUC0–∞, respectively. As the 90% CI for β was within 0.80 to 1.25, both parameters were considered dose proportional.

For the MAD cohort, dose proportionality was confirmed for Cmax (1.03 [90% CI, 0.87–1.20]), while a slightly more than dose‐proportional increase in AUC0–τ was observed (1.25 [90% CI, 1.07–1.44]).

Discussion

This Phase 1 study investigated the safety, tolerability, and PK of single and multiple ascending doses of camlipixant in healthy participants. Camlipixant was generally well tolerated at single doses up to 1200 mg and multiple doses up to 400 mg BID, with almost all TEAEs being mild or moderate in severity. No participants withdrew from the study prematurely due to AEs.

There was a similar overall incidence of TEAEs in the camlipixant and placebo groups; however, AEs considered treatment‐related by the investigator were predominantly mild or moderate in severity and consistent with results from a subsequent Phase 2b trial of camlipixant in adults with RCC. 31 No serious AEs occurred and none of the participants discontinued treatment due to an AE. The most notable AE considered treatment related by the investigator was dysgeusia, which occurred in a dose‐dependent manner and was predominantly mild, with a small number of moderate events; these were transient and resolved without intervention. Other treatment‐related AEs were infrequent and aligned with the expected profile for P2X3 antagonists. No unexpected safety signals were detected. These results were consistent with other P2X3 antagonists gefapixant, sivopixant, and eliapixant. 36 , 37 , 38

Camlipixant demonstrated a PK profile with rapid absorption and a generally dose‐proportional increase in systemic exposure. The T1/2 was relatively short (approximately 4–8 h) and required a BID regimen to sustain the plasma concentrations. Plasma concentrations were sustained throughout the repeat dosing period. The accumulation ratio was consistent with the T1/2 indicating absence of time‐dependent changes in exposure across the evaluated dose range. The PK of camlipixant was not affected by food intake meaning that it may be taken with or without food.

A strength of this study is that it evaluated a wide dose range of camlipixant with no safety concerns arising; notably, while most taste disturbances occurred at doses ≥400 mg, these were rare at the lower dose levels investigated in this trial. As the study was conducted among a small group of healthy participants, further studies need to be conducted in larger numbers of participants and among those living with RCC to understand the camlipixant safety profile and PK activity in the target population. Camlipixant elimination is expected to be primarily by hepatic metabolism with urinary excretion being a minor pathway, however, this will be further evaluated in ongoing clinical studies. Further research is ongoing to establish the safety and efficacy of camlipixant for patients with RCC as part of the camlipixant clinical development program.

Conclusions

Camlipixant was generally well tolerated, demonstrating an acceptable safety profile with a low incidence of dysgeusia at the lower dose levels investigated in healthy participants. The PK profile shows rapid absorption with dose‐proportional increases in systemic exposure, unaffected by food intake, and supports a BID dosing regimen. These findings support the ongoing clinical development program investigating camlipixant as a potential treatment for patients with RCC. In the Phase 3 CALM‐1 and CALM‐2 studies, patients are randomized to 25 mg camlipixant BID, 50 mg camlipixant BID, or placebo BID to evaluate the efficacy, safety, and tolerability of camlipixant as a targeted treatment for RCC. 32 These ongoing studies will confirm whether the positive Phase 2 study results translate into a clinical benefit in a larger patient population.
