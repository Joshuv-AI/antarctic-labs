 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
This report presents a workflow for advancing fuel performance modeling of SiC
composite cladding for light-water reactors by linking microscale, experimental
data-informed finite element analysis with rod-scale fuel performance codes such
as BISON. The workflow uses X-ray computed tomography (XCT) to capture the
actual geometry and processing-induced defects of as-fabricated SiC composite
tube specimens, particularly porosity and wall-thickness variations, and converts
the segmented XCT volumes into image-based finite element meshes for high-
fidelity structural analysis. The modeling outputs, including failure limits and
effective mechanical properties, are intended to support rod-scale fuel performance
predictions and experimental validation, with particular emphasis on pellet–
cladding mechanical interaction as a critical knowledge gap for SiC-based
cladding systems. Preliminary simulations using an XCT-derived Abaqus model
of a SiC composite tube section under internal pressurization show nonuniform
displacement and substantial stress scatter caused by manufacturing-induced
heterogeneities. Localized stress concentrations near the outer surface reach
approximately twice the average stress at comparable wall-depth locations, in
contrast to homogeneous analytical solutions that predict the maximum hoop stress
at the inner surface. These results demonstrate that realistic microstructural
representation can provide a more mechanistic basis for identifying potential
defect-driven damage-initiation mechanisms and for informing both deterministic
failure criteria, such as those based on proportional-limit stress, and probabilistic
failure assessments based on stress distribution and stressed material volume.
Although XCT-based models require greater data-processing and computational
resources than idealized models, they offer improved fidelity for assessing
localized stress concentrations and interpreting experimental results. Future work
will focus on validating and refining the XCT-informed pellet–cladding interaction
models through mechanical testing, including expansion-due-to-compression and
modified burst tests; once validated, the modeling framework can be extended to
assess post-irradiation mechanical behavior and support interpretation of
experiments performed under more prototypic cladding conditions, including
testing at the Transient Reactor Test Facility and the Severe Accident Test Station.

 1. INTRODUCTION
 Silicon carbide fiber–reinforced silicon carbide matrix (SiC/SiC) composites are strong candidates for
improving the accident tolerance of light-water reactor (LWR) cladding, largely due to their high-
temperature strength and resistance to oxidation at elevated temperatures [1]. These composites also meet
key requirements for LWR operation, including low neutron absorption [1, 2] and the retention of
mechanical performance after neutron irradiation at LWR-relevant temperatures [3].
 The U.S. Department of Energy’s Advanced Fuels Campaign drives the evaluation and maturation of
SiC/SiC composite technologies to raise the technology readiness level of SiC-based cladding for LWR
deployment. Related laboratory efforts are organized in accordance with the R&D execution document
[4]. Across the priority R&D areas, the most urgent needs before lead test rod insertion are to demonstrate
hermetic cladding and fuel integrity through integral testing and to develop a fuel performance prediction
tool tailored to SiC cladding systems.
 Fuel performance model development focuses on extending established licensing tools originally
developed for zirconium-based cladding to accommodate SiC-based cladding. Within a comprehensive
fuel performance framework, BISON is central for executing large-scale, integrated simulations that
evaluate SiC/SiC composite materials as an LWR cladding option and support deployment pathways.
Finite element analysis (FEA) studies represent the SiC/SiC composite tube as a homogeneous material,
without explicitly capturing cladding nonuniformities such as pores or fiber bundles [5, 6]. Even with this
simplification, such models have been effective for highlighting key feasibility concerns, including
irradiation-driven loss of hermeticity [7] and cladding bowing [8]. Further development of FEA
approaches is expected to improve the accuracy of performance assessments and enable optimization of
SiC/SiC composite architectures. In addition, fuel performance models that are anchored in experimental
validation are widely viewed as essential for accelerating qualification and licensing [9].
 High-fidelity modeling of SiC systems requires representation of the heterogeneous microstructure of
SiC/SiC composites and a consistent method for linking microscale behavior to macroscale response.
Current state-of-the-art efforts include simulations under test conditions using FEA models derived from
measured specimen microstructures, commonly referred to as image-based modeling [10]. This image-
based framework supports construction of microscale models applicable to coupon-scale test specimens.
This report describes research directed at developing a workflow that connects lower-length-scale FEA to
fuel performance modeling for full-length fuel rods. It also presents preliminary results on the hoop
mechanical properties of SiC tubes to support implementation in a fuel performance code and to improve
modeling and prediction of pellet–clad interaction behavior for SiC-clad rods in LWRs.

 2. WORKFLOW FOR SUPPORTING FUEL PERFORMANCE
 PREDICTIONS

 Figure 1 summarizes a workflow that links lower-length-scale models to fuel performance predictions
for SiC tubes. The workflow is presented as a sequence of labeled elements describing the progression
from measured data to outputs that are ready for full-length rod fuel performance modeling.

 1

 The “Input” consists of SiC tube inspection results obtained via X-ray computed tomography (XCT),
which provides volumetric information on internal features and potential defects relevant to structural
integrity; XCT-based nondestructive inspection of SiC tubes has been routinely applied in SiC research
and development [10, 11]. These inspection data are provided to the “Simulation,” which performs high-
fidelity, lower-length-scale structural analyses (e.g., image-based modeling) of SiC tubes under
mechanical and thermal loading. The “Output” of the lower-length-scale modeling is a set of failure limits
and mechanical properties intended for incorporation into a fuel performance code. Collectively, the
workflow illustrates how XCT inspection data can be translated through structural simulation into
engineering parameters suitable for fuel performance modeling. Benefits include applicability to a range
of SiC cladding system designs and use of actual product microstructures, which can reduce uncertainty
relative to models based only on nominal properties of similar materials.
 The following sections present preliminary results from applying the workflow to hoop stress in SiC
tubes. The overarching goal is to provide failure limits and mechanical properties of SiC tubes under
pellet–cladding mechanical interaction during normal operating conditions and during a reactivity-
initiated accident. The mechanical response of SiC tubes during hard contact with fissile fuel is a critical
knowledge gap that must be addressed to develop fuel performance codes for SiC cladding systems [12].

 3. METHODS: EXPERIMENTS AND MODELING
 3.1 X-ray Computed Tomography Analysis
 XCT scans and reconstructed volumes were used to observe and quantify tube geometry, closed
porosity, and the spatial distribution of fiber bundles in SiC/SiC composite tubes. The composite
consisted of a chemical vapor-infiltrated SiC matrix reinforced with Hi-Nicalon Type S SiC fibers and a
carbon interphase. Details of the material are reported elsewhere [11]. SiC tube specimens were
characterized using a Zeiss Xradia 520 Versa X-ray microscope. Scan energy settings were selected based
on specimen absorption; for SiC/SiC composite samples, tube voltage and power ranged from 60 kV at 5

 2

W to 120 kV at 10 W, depending on the size of the targeted field-of-view (FOV). This system, or an XCT
instrument of comparable capability, is widely used for inspection of SiC-based materials; therefore, the
framework developed using these data is expected to be broadly applicable to related works.
 In XCT data acquisition, spatial resolution and FOV are inversely related. To characterize volumes
comparable to tube specimens used in mechanical testing (e.g., lengths >25 mm), larger-FOV scans were
performed at reduced resolution. For these large-FOV datasets, acquisition parameters were evaluated to
balance sensitivity to microscale features—particularly process-induced porosity—against the need for a
sufficiently large region to support representative-volume analysis. The selected scan configurations were
implemented through the use of scintillator objectives and geometric magnification. A 0.4× scintillator
objective was employed. Each dataset was reconstructed from approximately 1600 radiographs acquired
over 360° of rotation. Detector binning was set to 1×1 for all scans to maximize spatial resolution. A
typical voxel size used in this work was 5 µm.
 Thermo Scientific Avizo software was used to process the XCT datasets. The reconstructed volumes
were filtered and segmented to distinguish constituent phases (i.e., label creation) and quantify porosity.
These steps are required for subsequent generation of finite element meshes for structural analysis.
Details of the segmentation methodology are reported elsewhere [11].

 3.2 Image-Based Finite Element Analysis
 The Avizo software package was also employed to generate finite element meshes for subsequent
structural analysis. As an initial step, a label (mask) was created to isolate regions of interest within the
SiC/SiC composite tube (Figure 2a). The mask was defined to include locations exhibiting sufficient
internal texture (e.g., porosity or variations in X-ray attenuation) to enable tracking of microstructural
changes. To prevent inclusion of non-specimen voids, the mask was constrained to lie entirely within the
reconstructed sample volume, with a safety margin excluding both external and internal air.

 The resulting finite element meshes (Figure 2b) were exported to the commercial FEA package
Abaqus. These image-derived geometries form the basis of image-based models that capture key features
of the SiC/SiC composite microstructure. For comparison purposes, an idealized computer-aided design
(CAD) model was also generated using dimensions comparable to those measured from the XCT
reconstruction. This CAD geometry provided a reference case for evaluating the influence of
microstructural heterogeneity and manufacturing-induced defects on the predicted mechanical response.

 3

Figure 2 (a) Avizo-generated label (mask) defining the region of interest within the XCT-reconstructed
SiC/SiC composite tube; (b) image-derived finite element mesh generated in Avizo and exported to
Abaqus for subsequent structural analysis.

 After import, boundary conditions were applied to the tube meshes, typically by constraining the base
and prescribing displacements on the internal surface to represent mechanical loading. Abaqus enables
boundary conditions to be assigned either to individual nodes or to predefined node sets, providing
precise control for simulating specific loading scenarios and localized responses within the SiC/SiC
composite tube. In general, the XCT-informed meshes were highly compatible with the Abaqus
framework, including assignment of phase-specific properties to the pore and solid domains.

 Image-based finite element analysis enables rapid assessment of the influence of microstructural
defects and geometric features on the mechanical performance of SiC/SiC cladding. By directly
incorporating XCT-derived geometries into the finite element model, it becomes possible to identify
regions of stress concentration associated with pores, low-density regions, irregular wall thickness, or
other manufacturing-induced defects. These analyses can provide valuable insight into potential crack
initiation locations, likely fracture paths, and the overall structural integrity of the cladding under
representative operating conditions.

 Once the mesh is exported to Abaqus, the geometry can be used to define boundary conditions that
replicate reactor environments or mechanical testing conditions. The segmented tube section had
approximate dimensions of 12,097.6 µm in length, an inner diameter of 7,134.2 µm, and a wall thickness
of 1,378.5 µm. Figure 3a shows the FE mesh and boundary conditions applied to the ends of the tube
(orange triangles). The geometry was discretized using 1,142,062 quadratic tetrahedral elements
(C3D10). The nodes at both ends of the tube were constrained in all translational degrees of freedom to
prevent rigid body motion (Figure 3a). A uniform internal pressure of 0.1 MPa (Figure 3b) was applied to
the inner surface of the tube to simulate the pressure generated by fission product release within the
plenum, consistent with the conditions reported in [13]. The SiC/SiC cladding was modeled as a
homogeneous, isotropic, linear-elastic material with representative elastic properties reported in the SiC
cladding handbook [14]. Pores and the solid material (SiC/SiC) are the two material phases modeled in
the finite element analysis, although additional phases can be included through the XCT data. The void
content within the composite is modeled explicitly as a material with a stiffness one hundred times lower
than that of the SiC/SiC material. Research on random-field models for porous materials has
demonstrated the viability and stability of this approach for finite element simulations [15, 16].

 4. RESULTS
 Figure 4 shows the maximum principal displacement magnitudes in a modeled SiC composite tube
under a uniform internal pressurization of 0.1 MPa. The tube microstructure includes both the solid SiC
composite and macroscopic pores, as shown in Figure 2. The displacement magnitudes are nonuniform on
both the inner and outer surfaces in Figure 4; this trend also holds throughout the internal volume (not
shown). Both ends of the tube section exhibit no displacement due to the applied boundary conditions
(see Figure 3a).

Figure 4 Distribution of principal displacement magnitudes in SiC composite tubes, modeled using
image-based FEA under uniform internal pressurization. Inter-bundle pores are incorporated into the
mesh structure (not visible in the figure).

 The FEA model revealed that the stress distribution was fairly non-uniform due to the presence of
relatively large pores (typically inter-bundle pores). Figure 5 presents an example of this non-uniform
distribution. The relative maximum principal stress values were plotted against the distance from the
inner to the outer tube surfaces. The scatter in the data at the same thickness indicates stress variation in
the model due to local microstructural features. Notably, stress concentrations associated with processing
defects were observed at approximately 0.87 and 0.92 mm from the inner surface. At these positions, the
peak stress was about twice the average stress of regions at the same distance.

 5

Figure 5 Deviations in maximum principal stress at different positions from the inner to the outer tube
surfaces in the FEA analysis. Data were taken from a mid-plane along the length of the modeled tube
volume.

 To evaluate the influence of geometric fidelity on the predicted mechanical response, the XCT-
derived model was compared with an idealized CAD representation possessing similar dimensions,
material properties, and boundary conditions. The results shown in Figure 6 demonstrate that the CAD
model produces a comparatively uniform displacement field, reflecting its simplified geometry and
absence of microstructural features. In contrast, the XCT-derived model captures local variations in
displacement arising from pores, wall-thickness variations, and other manufacturing-induced
heterogeneities. These differences highlight the importance of incorporating realistic geometries when
assessing localized stress concentrations and potential failure mechanisms in SiC/SiC composite cladding.

 5. DISCUSSIONS

 Finite element modeling of the SiC/SiC composite tube response to internal pressurization was
performed on a short tube segment using a composite microstructure informed by XCT data. When
compared with the FEA performed on uniform tube geometry, the impact of internal features on the
mechanical behavior was captured for the XCT-based model. Importantly, nonuniformities caused a
higher displacement value on the outer surface of the tube, which results in larger strains and stresses.

 Here, 𝑟𝑟𝑖𝑖 and 𝑟𝑟𝑜𝑜 denote the inner and outer radii of the tubular specimen, respectively. The fracture
stress—taken as the maximum stress at failure—is therefore the hoop stress evaluated at the inner surface,
𝑟𝑟 = 𝑟𝑟𝑖𝑖 . The details of the theoretical analysis and relevant experimental results from miniature monolithic
SiC specimens are available elsewhere [17]. Thus, the analytical solution predicts that the inner surface is
under the highest stress and strain values. On the other hand, XCT-based FEA predicts larger principal
displacements, which are proportional to the principal strains, as well as larger principal stresses near the
outer surfaces. This difference is caused by the nonuniform internal features of the SiC/SiC composite.
 A comparison of the model predictions with the stress state expected from the analytical solution for a
uniform material indicates a fundamentally different failure location. The finite-element model predicts
failure driven by a stress concentration near the outer surface region (Figure 5), whereas the analytical
approach, under idealized homogeneous conditions, predicts failure initiating at the inner surface where
the maximum hoop stress occurs. This discrepancy highlights that the assumed uniform-material stress
distribution is not sufficient to describe the observed behavior, and it underscores the need to explicitly
incorporate composite microstructural heterogeneity to advance the current fuel performance code.

 7

 The experimental data obtained from the SiC/SiC composite tube support the modeling results, which
indicate highly localized stresses near the outer surface. Figure 7 shows an XCT slice of a SiC/SiC
composite tube with a microstructure representative of the modeled composite during the in-situ
expansion-due-to-compression hoop test [12]. The XCT data were acquired under applied hoop stress.
Microcracking was observed at both the inner and outer surfaces, with outer-surface cracking highlighted
in Figure 7. These observations support the localized damage initiation inferred from the stress
concentrations shown in Figure 5. This analysis provides a modeling-based explanation for localized
cracking in SiC/SiC composite tubes that is not readily captured by analytical or fracture-mechanics
models that assume a uniform, homogenized SiC/SiC material, such as the formulation in Eq. [1]. Thus,
explicitly modeling critical microstructural features is essential for developing fuel performance models
for SiC/SiC composite cladding.
 The preliminary results from the combined XCT–FEA workflow provide a basis for direct
comparison between simulation predictions and experimental measurements from expansion-due-to-
compression testing. After validation, this methodology can be applied to reproduce and assess the post-
irradiation mechanical behavior of SiC tubular specimens under relevant loading conditions. The FEA
results can inform deterministic fuel performance models by providing a material-failure criterion for
internal-pressurization limits that account for fission-gas pressure, pellet–cladding mechanical contact,
and irradiation-induced stresses. Such a criterion is commonly based on the proportional-limit stress,
typically about 100 MPa for nuclear-grade SiC/SiC composite tubes fabricated by chemical vapor
infiltration [14]. The FEA output can also support stochastic fuel performance models [18] by enabling
prediction and assessment of failure probability based on the stress distribution and the stressed material
volume.

 Table 1 summarizes the key differences between CAD-based and XCT image-based finite element
modeling approaches. CAD-based models provide a computationally efficient approach for rapid design
evaluations and multiphysics simulations. However, CAD-based models are limited in their ability to
represent manufacturing-induced defects and microstructural heterogeneity commonly found in SiC/SiC
composites. In contrast, XCT image-based models directly incorporate the real geometry of the
component, including pores, density variations, and geometric irregularities, enabling a more realistic
assessment of localized stress concentrations and potential failure mechanisms. However, the complex
geometries derived from XCT data generally require unstructured tetrahedral meshes, often resulting in
substantially larger element counts to accurately represent the microstructure. Although image-based
models require additional processing and computational resources, this approach offers improved fidelity
for evaluating the structural performance of SiC/SiC cladding under representative service conditions or
mechanical testing.

 Another advantage of image-based modeling approaches is their ability to estimate effective material
properties directly from the reconstructed microstructure. These simulations can be used to determine the
representative volume element (RVE) of SiC/SiC composites and to quantify the influence of individual
constituents, such as fibers, matrix, and porosity, on the overall material response. Furthermore, once the
RVE has been identified, defined as the smallest volume over which measurements yield properties
representative of the bulk material, the resulting effective properties can be homogenized and used as
input parameters for larger-scale CAD-based models. This multiscale approach enables the transfer of
microstructure-informed material behavior into component-level simulations while maintaining
computational efficiency.
 Image-based modeling can also contribute to quality control and process improvement efforts. FEA-
based mechanical performance predictions informed by real XCT data can be used to identify which
microstructural features contribute most significantly to stress and strain concentrations. Accumulating
such information helps define the “critical” processing defects that should be avoided during
manufacturing and detected and eliminated during quality control.

 6. SUMMARY AND OUTLOOK
 This report describes a workflow for advancing fuel performance modeling of SiC/SiC composite
cladding for light-water reactors by linking microscale, XCT-informed structural simulations with rod-
scale fuel performance codes such as BISON. The approach uses XCT inspection of as-fabricated
SiC/SiC tube specimens to quantify geometry and processing-induced defects, particularly porosity, and
then converts segmented XCT volumes into image-based finite element meshes for high-fidelity structural
analyses. The resulting failure limits and effective mechanical properties are intended for incorporation
into rod-scale fuel performance predictions and to support experimental validation, with pellet–cladding
mechanical interaction identified as a key knowledge gap.

 The preliminary results compare an XCT-derived Abaqus model of a SiC/SiC tube section, consisting
of approximately 1.14 million tetrahedral elements, with an idealized CAD-based tube model under 0.1
MPa internal pressurization. The XCT-based model predicts nonuniform displacement and substantial
stress scatter caused by manufacturing-induced heterogeneities. Stress concentrations near the outer
surface reach roughly twice the average stress at comparable wall-depth locations, contrasting with
homogeneous analytical solutions that predict the maximum hoop stress at the inner surface. These results
indicate that realistic microstructural representation is important for identifying potential defect-driven
damage-initiation mechanisms and for informing both deterministic failure criteria, such as those based
on proportional-limit stress, and probabilistic failure assessments. The results also highlight the trade-off
between the computational efficiency of CAD-based models and the higher fidelity of XCT-informed
models, as well as the need for direct experimental validation and future multiscale property
homogenization.

 Follow-up efforts will focus on refining the XCT-informed models using results from mechanical
tests relevant to pellet–cladding interaction, including expansion-due-to-compression tests and modified
burst tests [19, 20]. In addition, the modeling framework is expected to support interpretation of
experiments performed under more prototypic cladding conditions, including tests conducted at the
Transient Reactor Test Facility and the Severe Accident Test Station.
