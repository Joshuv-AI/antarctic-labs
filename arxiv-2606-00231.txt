On Asymptotic Outlier Rejection in Bayesian Mixed Poisson Regression Models Under Extreme Target and Covariate Values
Ilaria Pia, Jarno Vanhatalo

Abstract
Bayesian models are claimed to be fully robust against outliers if, asymptotically, observations infinitely far from the other data do not influence the posterior. Early works in robust Bayesian inference concentrated on continuous distributions and i.i.d. observations. Robustness results were then extended to linear regression in the presence of infinite residuals – either through an outlying outcome or an outlying covariate. Recently, Hamura:2025 presented a count regression model, with Poisson-Rescaled Beta (-RSB) target distribution and Gaussian latent variables (GLVs), which is robust against infinitely large counts and able to handle zero-inflation. We continue from the work of Hamura et al. and study the robustness properties of mixed Poisson regression models with GLVs in the presence of outlying data points arising from either corrupted covariates or corrupted target values. While in linear regression the two cases are interchangeable, as both infinite target or covariates lead to infinite residuals, we show that in count regression infinite covariates is not a symmetric case to infinite target. Specifically, we show that mixed Poisson models are not asymptotically robust to outliers resulting from infinite covariates. We then consider three alternative mixed Poissons (Poisson-Gamma, Poisson-log-t, and Poisson-RSB) as target distribution and examine, both theoretically and via simulations as well as real-world case studies, their behavior in the presence of outliers of three alternative types: large target value as well as large and small covariate values. Our results show that models robust to data points with an anomalous target are not robust to data points with anomalous covariates, calling for methodological development for models that are robust for covariate outliers. While we do not provide solution to the challenge of outlying covariates, our work builds theoretically novel and practically valuable understanding on the properties of these models.

Introduction

Observations that strongly deviate from the bulk of the other data points, referred to as outliers, can have a large impact on inference and prediction under standard statistical models, leading to biases in the data analysis results.
For this reason, the development of models that are robust to outliers, capable of automatically detecting and diminishing, or even rejecting, the effect of outliers on inference, has been a long lasting topic in Bayesian research
(
DeFinetti:1961
;
Neyman:1971
;
OHagan+Pericchi:2012
;
Desgagne:2015
)
.
The need for robust methods has grown further in recent years due to the increase in the size of datasets and heterogeneity of data sources, which makes the appearance of outliers increasingly more likely.
In this paper, we present a cohesive classification of different types of outliers in count regression and examine how mixed Poisson regression models, analyzed under the Bayesian framework, behave both in the presence of infinitely large outliers and in the presence of finite but anomalous observations arising from either corrupted target values or corrupted covariate values.

In Bayesian analysis, the presence of outliers generates a conflict between the outlier likelihood component counterpoised to the likelihood of the rest of the data together with the priors
(
OHagan+Pericchi:2012
)
.
The resolution of this conflict is typically expressed in terms of the posterior asymptotic behavior: a Bayesian model is generally claimed to be
fully robust
when the posterior given all the data converges in distribution to the posterior given all but the outlier observations, as the outliers move infinitely far from the bulk of the rest of the data.
Such posterior convergence is generally referred to as
asymptotic outlier rejection
, as well as
outlier-proneness

(
OHagan:1979
)
.
It should be noted that the concept of outlier-proneness was first introduced by
Neyman:1971
to refer to the ability of the data generating process to generate extremely large data. As such, in a regression modeling context it only corresponds to robustness against outlying targets, but it cannot be used to refer to robustness against covariates, as these are not considered a product of a data generating process. We hence define robustness in terms of asymptotic outlier rejection.
Recent studies have provided sufficient conditions for the robustness of linear regression models to outlying target and covariate values
(
Gagnon:2020
;
Desgagne:2021
;
Hamura:2023
)
as well as for the robustness of generalized linear Gamma
(
Gagnon:2023
)
and count regression models
(
Hamura:2025
)
to outlying target values.

Specifically, in regression models, an outlying data point arises as an anomalous combination of covariates and target values with respect to the rest of the data.
Traditional examples are anomalous target values resulting from measurement or reporting errors in the data transcription. However, corrupted covariate observations, also known as high leverage points
(
Plummer:2025
)
, are also possible.
For example, in environmental sciences and spatial data analyses, satellite images and raster maps are routinely used to create covariate data.
These products often contain large errors and biases arising from the inaccuracies in the satellite measurements and map production, which may then lead to a mismatch between a spatially referenced target values and covariates associated with them.
In linear regression, both infinite target and infinite covariate lead symmetrically to infinite residual. We can hence define outliers in terms of their errors, without distinguishing between the source in which the data point presents an anomalous value
(
Desgagne:2021
)
.
However, in count regression, the behavior of likelihood is not symmetric with respect to an infinite covariate and an infinite target.
This non-symmetry has, however, largely been ignored in the literature and we will show that it has important implications to the posterior inference.
Moreover, in count regression, target values are limited to non-negative values.
As suggested by
Hamura:2025
, outliers formed by a zero count associated with large intensity can be framed as a zero-inflation problem. While zero-inflated count models have been extensively studied
(
Cragg:1971
;
Lambert:1992
)
and the recent works by
Hamura:2020
and
Hamura:2025
have significantly advanced our understanding of the treatment of large outlying counts, we still lack a comprehensive understanding of how these models behave with different types of outliers originating from anomalous covariate values.

In this work, we classify outliers in count regression based on the corrupted source: the target (type-
y
) or the covariate (type-
x
).
We further classify outliers as large (L) if the corrupted value is very large, in extreme case
+\infty
, and small (S) if the corrupted value is very small, in the extreme case
-\infty
for outliers of type-
x
and 0 for outliers of type-
y
.
Since the behavior of count models under type-
x
outliers differs depending on whether the target value is 0 or positive, we further separate outliers of type-
x
in two subgroups, based on this division.
We summarize the five alternative types of outliers considered in this work in Table

.
We then study asymptotic robustness to different types of outliers for a selection of mixed Poisson regression models with Gaussian-latent variables (GLVs), which are among the most common models used in the study of count data
(
Wang+Blei:2018
)
.
Together with Poisson and Negative Binomial (or more generally Poisson-Gamma mixtures), which are standard choices when modeling count data, we consider the Poisson-Rescaled Beta
(
Hamura:2025
, -RSB,)
and the Poisson-log Student-
t

(
Gaver87
, first introduced by)
mixtures. The latter distribution can be seen as a more flexible form of Poisson GLMs with GLVs. Indeed, assuming that the logarithm of the varying component of the Poisson rate parameter follows a Student-

distribution is equivalent to adding heteroschedastic Gaussian noise in the GLM for the Poisson rate parameter.
We define robustness to extreme large outliers of type-
y
in count regression in asymptotic terms and refer to it as asymptotic outlier rejection, and rely on
Hamura:2025
’s
(
Hamura:2025
)
results to study these models’ ability to handle such extreme data points.
Specifically, we test whether the Poisson-mixing distribution is right log regularly varying
(
Desgagne:2015
, LRV, )
and whether it has a divergent polynomial behavior at the origin, to handle outliers of type-
y
.
We further extend the definition of asymptotic outlier rejection to outliers of type-
x
, originated by infinite covariates, and provide a proof that mixed Poisson models are not robust to them.

We then show that, in the presence of infinite covariates, the regression coefficients posterior distribution collapses to a point mass distribution at zero, also known as Dirac delta.
This phenomenon is similar to Lindley’s paradox
(
Lindley:1957
)
: in the presence of an infinite covariate, the likelihood becomes zero in all the regression parameters’ space, except for the case with zero valued corresponding coefficient, indicating the model is unable to properly represent the data. However, the regression coefficients posterior results in an extremely certain estimate, putting all the posterior mass at zero.
We further study mixed Poisson models’ behavior in the presence of moderate outliers, generated by finite but anomalous target or covariates via simulations and a real-world case study. We measure robustness to moderate outliers in terms of KL divergence of the posterior given the full dataset with respect to the posterior excluding outliers.
Despite mixed Poisson models not being asymptotically robust against extreme corrupted covariates, in practice, they do perform much better than the baseline Poisson model for data which include moderate outliers. Generally, a Poisson-Gamma (Negative Binomial) model was equally well able to capture outlying data in the
x
direction as the mixed Poisson models that are robust to extreme large outliers of type-
y
.

2
Mixed Poisson count regression

2.1
Mixed Poisson regression models with GLV

In count regression, the standard Poisson model is known to be sensitive to outliers and zero-inflation since the variance and expectation of a Poisson distribution are both equal to the rate parameter.
From a data generating process point of view, we can obtain a more robust distribution, by adding heteroschedasticity to the rate parameter of the Poisson distribution.
This is similar in nature to writing the Student-
t
as a scale mixture of Gaussian distributions in linear regression.
Adding a multiplicative varying component to the Poisson rate parameter following a positive-valued mixing distribution leads to a mixed Poisson distribution which is more flexible than a standard Poisson distribution. However, whether a mixed Poisson distribution is robust to outliers or not depends on the mixing distribution.

We focus our analysis on mixed Poisson regression models, with Gaussian latent variables.
Hereafter we denote by
\mathcal{D}_{n+1}=\{\boldsymbol{d}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{d}_{n+1}\}
a set of regression data such that
\boldsymbol{d}_{i}=(y_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
, with
y_{i}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
being the target and
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in\mathrm{R}^{p}
the vector of covariates for observation
i=1,\dots,n+1
.
We assume observations are conditionally independent.
We consider the target expectation model
\E(y_{i}|\mathcal{D}_{\setminus i},\boldsymbol{x}_{i})=\lambda_{i}\eta_{i}
with
\lambda_{i}=\exp(\textbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\zeta_{i})
, generalized regression model with logarithmic link function, where
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is the
p
-dimensional vector of regression coefficients, with Gaussian prior, and
\zeta_{i}
is a
q
-dimensional Gaussian random effect capturing ’standard’ observations, while
\eta_{i}
is a multiplicative random effect capturing outliers.
Specifically, we consider mixed Poisson regression models, such that

\displaystyle y_{i}|\lambda_{i}\eta_{i}

\displaystyle\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda_{i}\eta_{i})

\displaystyle\eta_{i}

\displaystyle\sim\pi(\cdot)

(1)

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}

\displaystyle=\exp(x_{i}^{\top}\beta+\zeta_{i}):\beta,\zeta_{i}\sim N(\cdot).

Here, the mixed Poisson rate parameter is attained as the product of a noise coefficient
\eta_{i}\sim\pi
and a rate parameter
\lambda_{i}>0
that follows a log-Gaussian distribution. Assuming log Gaussian distribution for
\lambda
is a common choice in count regression models.
Note though, that all following results can be extended to mixed Poisson regression whose regression components follows any bounded continuous prior.

2.2
Alternative mixed Poisson models

Many mixed Poisson distributions have been proposed in the literature, and their properties have been studied, for example, by
Karlis:2005
.
In this work, we compare four of them: Poisson, Negative Binomial (Poisson-Gamma), Poisson-log Student-
t

(
Gaver87
)
, and Poisson-Rescaled Beta (Poisson-RSB)
(
Hamura:2025
)
.
The first two are standard choices in the analysis of count data, the latter two arise as mixtures of Poisson with proper and easy to implement mixing distributions.

For each of the mixed model considered, we study the behavior of the left and right tails of the multiplicative noise
\eta_{i}
. We check whether the right tail of the mixture distribution
\pi
is LRV (
Desgagne:2015
, see Equation (
2
)) and whether the mixing distribution diverges with a polynomial divergence at the origin (Equation (
3
)):

\displaystyle\text{LRV right tail}:\hskip 14.22636pt

\displaystyle\pi(\eta)\approx\eta^{-1}(\log\eta)^{-(1+b)},\ b>0

\displaystyle\text{as }\ \eta\to\infty,

(2)

\displaystyle\text{Polynomial divergence at origin}:\hskip 14.22636pt

\displaystyle\pi(\eta)\approx\eta^{a-1},\ 0<a<1

\displaystyle\text{as }\ \eta\to 0.

(3)

The symbol
\approx
here denotes the asymptotic proportionality between functions;
f(x)\approx g(x)
meaning
f(x)/g(x)=C<\infty
for
x\to 0\text{ or }\infty
.
The left and right tails behavior of the different Poisson-mixing distributions is shown in Figure
1
.

Poisson model

As a baseline model, we will consider the Poisson process:
y_{i}|\lambda_{i}\sim\text{Pois}(\lambda_{i})
.
When assuming that the data are generated from a Poisson process, we implicitly assume that the variability in the data equals their average, as the Poisson rate parameter
\lambda
serves both as scale and as location parameter:
\text{Var}[Y]=\E[Y]=\lambda
. Often, such assumption does not reflect the complexity of the data, which present much higher variability. This leads to overdispersion problems. In particular, it makes the Poisson distribution not robust against outliers: the presence of observations which differ significantly from the others will increase the variance and hence the expectation of the likelihood component in the Bayes Theorem, affecting the posterior of
\lambda
.

The Poisson model can be seen as a mixed Poisson where the multiplicative noise follows
\pi=\delta_{1}
, the one point mass distribution at 1.

Negative Binomial (Poisson-Gamma) model

A common approach to better model overdispersed data is to introduce heteroschedasticity into the data generating process by assigning a Gamma prior for the mixing distribution, so that

\displaystyle y_{i}|\lambda_{i},\eta_{i}

\displaystyle\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda_{i}\eta_{i})

\displaystyle\eta_{i}|r

\displaystyle\sim\text{Gamma}(r,r).

Here, each observation has its own rate parameter
\lambda_{i}\eta_{i}
following a Gamma prior distribution with shape
r
and rate
\frac{r}{\lambda_{i}}
. Marginalizing over
\eta_{i}
we obtain that
y_{i}
follows a Negative Binomial distribution centered in
\lambda_{i}
with overdispersion parameter
r>0

(
Greenwood+Yule:1920
)
:

y_{i}|\lambda_{i},r\sim\text{NegBin}(\lambda_{i},r).

The model converges to the Poisson distribution when
r\to\infty
.

In the Negative-Binomial model, the multiplicative noise
\eta_{i}
follows a Gamma distribution with the same shape and scale parameter (
r
) and density

f_{\text{Gamma}}(x;r,r)\propto x^{r-1}e^{-xr}.

(4)

As
x\to\infty
the density converges to zero at a rate of
e^{-xr}
, hence it is not right LRV. However, as
x\to 0
,
f_{\text{Gamma}}(x)\sim x^{r-1}
which diverges polynomially for
r<1
.
In our experimental studies we set
r=0.5
.

Poisson-Rescaled Beta (Poisson-RSB) model

Following
Hamura:2025
, we derive the Poisson-Rescaled Beta (Poisson-RSB) model as

\displaystyle y_{i}|\lambda_{i},\eta_{i}

\displaystyle\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda_{i}\eta_{i})

\displaystyle\eta_{i}|a,b

\displaystyle\sim\text{RSB}(a,b)

where the parameter
\eta_{i}>0
follows a rescaled beta distribution, with density:

f_{RSB}(x;a,b)=\frac{1}{B(a,b)}\frac{\log(1+x)^{a-1}}{1+x}\frac{1}{[1+\log(1+x)]^{a+b}}.

(5)

A RSB distributed random variable arises as a scale transformation of a beta-distributed random variable: if
U\sim\text{Beta}(a,b)
then
X=\log(1+\frac{U}{1-U})\sim\text{RSB}(a,b)
.
The Poisson-RSB mixture can also be seen as an extension of the Poisson-Gamma mixture. Indeed, the RSB distribution can be obtained as the marginal distribution of the following hierarchical model:
\eta|r\sim\text{Gamma}(1,r),\ r|u,v\sim\text{Gamma}(u+v,1),\ (u,v)\sim g(u,v)
, bivariate joint density such that
g(v,w)\propto\frac{v^{-a}w^{b+a-1}e^{-w}}{v+w}

(
Hamura:2025
, Theorem 1)
.

The rescaled beta has a right LRV density, such that its density
f_{\text{RSB}}(x)\approx x^{-1}(\log x)^{-(1+b)}
as
x\to\infty
. With
0<a<1
, its density diverges at the origin at the polynomial rate of
x^{a-1}

(
Hamura:2025
)
.
Following Hamura’s suggestions, we set the RSB parameters
a
=1/2 and
b
=1/2 in this work.

Poisson-log Student-
t
model

The Poisson-log Student-
t
(or shortly log-t) mixture model for count data, first proposed by
Gaver87
, can be derived by assigning a log Student-
t
prior for the mixing component

\displaystyle y_{i}|\lambda_{i},\eta_{i}

\displaystyle\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda_{i}\eta_{i})

\displaystyle\log\eta_{i}|k

\displaystyle\sim\text{Student-}t_{k}(\cdot).

where each observation has its own rate parameter
\lambda_{i}\eta_{i}
, and
\log\eta_{i}
follows a Student-
t
prior distribution with
k
degrees of freedom. As underlined by
Gaver87
this mixture can be seen as a generalization of the logNormal-mixed Poisson
(
Bulmer:1974
)
which allows for a better fit of extreme outliers, since the Student-
t
has systematically heavier tails than Gaussian.
Further, the log-t mixed Poisson can be seen as a more flexible form of Poisson GLMs with GLVs, and hence represents a natural choice for a more robust count model.
This model is also equivalent to considering a GLM for the rate parameters
\theta_{i}=\lambda_{i}\eta_{i}
, with additional heteroschedastic Gaussian noise.
Given
y_{i}\sim\text{Pois}(\theta_{i}=\lambda_{i}\eta_{i})
, then:

\log\lambda_{i}=x_{i}^{\prime}\beta+\zeta_{i}

\iff

\log\theta_{i}=x_{i}^{\prime}\beta+\zeta_{i}+\log\eta_{i}

\ \ \zeta_{i}\sim N(0,\sigma^{2}),\ \eta_{i}\sim\log-t(k,0,s^{2})

\ \ \zeta_{i}\sim N(0,\sigma^{2}),\ \log\eta_{i}\sim N(0,s_{i}^{2})

\ \ s_{i}^{2}\sim\text{Inv}-\chi_{k}(\cdot)
.

Integrating the Poisson distribution with respect to
\log\eta
, we obtain the Poisson-log-t scale-mixture

f(y|\lambda,k)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\text{Poisson}(y|\lambda\eta)\cdot\text{Student-}t(\log(\eta)|k)\,\ d\{\log(\eta)\}.

The distributional function is analytically intractable though.

The density of a log Student-
t
distribution with
k
degrees of freedom is

f_{\text{log-t}}(x;k)\propto\frac{1}{x}\Big(1+\frac{1}{k}\left(\frac{\log x}{s}\right)^{2}\Big)^{-\frac{k+1}{2}}

(6)

As
x\to\infty
the density
f_{\text{log-t}}(x)\sim x^{-1}\log x^{-(k+1)}
which is LRV with index
k+1
. The lower the LRV index, the fatter the tail; that means setting the log-t degrees of freedom
k=1
gives the fattest tailed distribution, also known as log-Cauchy distribution. As
x\to 0
,
f_{\text{log-t}}(x)
diverges at the polynomial rate of
x^{-1}
.

Mixed Poisson count regression under extreme outliers

3.1
Continuous models robustness to extreme outliers

Robustness against outliers in Bayesian statistics has traditionally been studied in terms of asymptotic outlier rejection.
Based on
Neyman:1971
’s
(
Neyman:1971
)
and
Dawid:1973
’s
(
Dawid:1973
)
work,
OHagan:1979
considered real valued observations
y_{1},\dots,y_{n+1}
and distributions of the form
g(y-\theta)
, with
\theta
location parameter taking values on the full real line, such as Gaussians, and framed outlier robustness in asymptotic terms, focusing on the posterior distribution of the location parameter when outlying observations approach infinite.

Definition 3.1
.

(Asymptotic outlier rejection / Outlier proneness
OHagan:1979
)
Let the observations
y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{n+1}
be i.i.d. given
\theta
with densities
p(y_{i}|\theta)=g(y_{i}-\theta)
.
Then such model is
right (left) outlier prone
of order
n
if

p(\theta|y_{1},\dots,y_{n+1})\to p(\theta|y_{1},\dots,y_{n})\text{ as }y_{n+1}\to+\infty(-\infty).

That is, the effect of a single conflicting observation to the posterior becomes asymptotically negligible as the observation approaches infinity. Right and left outlier-proneness are defined symmetrically, as
y\to+\infty
and
y\to-\infty
respectively.
As shown by O’Hagan, while Gaussian distributions are not outlier prone, Student-
t
distributions, obtained by adding heteroschedasticity to the Gaussian observation model, are outlier prone for the location parameter.
However, later studies have shown how, in linear regression when both regression coefficients and scale parameter are unknown, Student-
t
models are not fully robust, as the convergence defining outlier-proneness is only satisfied for location parameters
(
Gagnon:2023_st
)
.
O’Hagan definition considers i.i.d. observations, but can easily be extended to linear regression models with densities
p(y_{i}|\theta)=g(y_{i}-x_{i}\beta)
. Note that in linear regression, outliers of type-
y
(infinite target) and outliers of type-
x
(infinite covariate) lead to symmetric cases. Indeed, we can identify extreme outliers in terms of infinite error
|y-x\beta|

(
Desgagne:2021
)
.

3.2
Count model robustness to extreme outliers

Count data models, such as the mixed Poisson models considered here, do not fit into the location-scale family summarized above. Count processes have non-negative support, so we cannot define left outlier proneness similarly to models with support in real line.
Moreover, count models depend on a positive rate parameter introducing asymmetry between outliers of type-
y
and type-
x
as shown below.

Hereafter we consider the framework introduced in section
2
, and formally define mixed Poisson models robustness to the large outlier of type-
y
as well as small and large outliers of type-
x
(see Table
1
) in terms of outlier-proneness.
We can extend
OHagan:1979
’s
(
OHagan:1979
)
definition of right outlier prone to mixed Poisson regression models as follows.

Definition 3.2
.

(Asymptotic rejection of large outliers of type-
y
)
A mixed Poisson model performs
asymptotic rejection of large outliers of type-
y
and order
n
if

p(\beta,\zeta|y_{1},\dots,y_{n+1})\to p(\beta,\zeta|y_{1},\dots,y_{n})\text{ as }y_{n+1}\to+\infty.

The above is equivalent to stating that a mixed Poisson model is
right outlier prone
of order
n
.

That is, a count regression model performs asymptotic large outlier rejection of order
n
, if the posterior distribution of the regression model parameters is not affected by one extremely large count.

Since counts are bounded to non-negative values, we do not attempt to define outlier rejection to small counts in asymptotic terms.
Instead, we study asymptotic rejection of outliers presenting a small or large covariate.
It should be noted that the concept of outlier-proneness was first introduced by
Neyman:1971
to refer to the ability of the data generating process to produce extreme observations, it is hence appropriate to define asymptotic rejection of outlying targets, but cannot be used to define robustness against covariates, as these are not considered a product of a data generating process.
As done for outliers of type-
y
, we can define robustness to outliers with infinite covariates in terms of asymptotic outliers rejection, where an observation
d_{n+1}
is an extreme outlier of type-
x
if its covariate vector
x_{n+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is such that for one
j\in\{1,\dots,p\}

x_{j,n+1}\to\pm\infty
.

Definition 3.3
.

(Asymptotic rejection of large (small) outliers of type-
x
)
A mixed Poisson model performs
asymptotic rejection of large outliers of type-
x
and order
n
if

p(\beta,\zeta|y_{1},\dots,y_{n+1})\to p(\beta,\zeta|y_{1},\dots,y_{n})\text{ as }x_{j,n+1}\to+\infty.

Similarly, a mixed Poisson model performs
asymptotic rejection of small outliers of type-
x
and order
n
if

p(\beta,\zeta|y_{1},\dots,y_{n+1})\to p(\beta,\zeta|y_{1},\dots,y_{n})\text{ as }x_{j,n+1}\to-\infty.

We can extend the above definitions to multiple outliers, by substituting the
n+1
outlying observation with a set of observations with either infinite or zero target.

3.3
Robustness of mixed Poisson regression models to extreme outliers of type-
y

Early studies of extreme value theory have shown how the right tails of certain classes of mixed Poisson distributions are asymptotically equal to the right tail of their mixing distribution
(
Willmot:1990
;
Perline:1998
)
.
To study robustness of different mixed Poisson models with GLVs we hence focus on the tail behavior of the mixing component
\eta
. As shown by
Hamura:2025
, having a very heavy LRV right tail, and a diverging behavior at the origin are sufficient conditions for such models’ robustness to large outliers of type-
y
.

Let us consider the mixed Poisson regression model from Equation
2.1
, with
y_{i}
count response variables for
i=1,\dots,n
such that
y_{i}=y_{i}(\omega)\to\infty
as
\omega\to\infty
for
i\in L
and
y_{i}
fixed for
i\in K
, with
K,\ L
non empty index sets such that
|K|+|L|=n
.
We further assume that
|K^{+}|+1\geq|L|+p
, where
K^{+}=\{i\in K|y_{i}\geq 1\}
and
p
is the dimension of
\beta
.
As proved by
Hamura:2025
, under the assumption that
k+1\geq l+p
, a mixed Poisson is asymptotically rejecting large type-
y
outliers, if the mixing distribution
\pi
follows the RSB distribution.

Theorem 3.4
.

(
Hamura:2025
, Theorem 3)

Suppose that
\pi(\eta_{i})
is a RSB distribution with
k
degrees of freedom,
\pi(\zeta,\beta)=\pi(\zeta)\pi(\beta)
,
\pi(\zeta)
is normal, and
\pi(\beta)
is a proper probability density. Then, we have that
p(\beta,\zeta|y_{K},y_{L})\to p(\beta,\zeta|y_{K})
as
\omega\to\infty
.

The key property making the Poisson-RSB model robust against extremely large counts is the very heavy, LRV, right tail of the RSB mixing distribution.
Note that the Poisson log-t mixing distribution also has a LRV right tail (
2
), and is hence robust to extremely large outliers of type-
y
.
We formally present Poisson-log-t right-OP property in the following corollary, and provide a formal proof in the appendix, section
A.1
.

Corollary 3.5
.

Suppose that
\pi(\eta_{i})
is a log-t distribution with
k
degrees of freedom,
\pi(\zeta,\beta)=\pi(\zeta)\pi(\beta)
,
\pi(\zeta)
is normal, and
\pi(\beta)
is a proper probability density. Then, we have that
p(\beta,\zeta|y_{K},y_{L})\to p(\beta,\zeta|y_{K})
as
\omega\to\infty
.

Rejection of small outliers of type-
y
cannot be defined in asymptotic terms, as done for large counts, since
y_{i}
is bounded by zero from the left. That is, the most extreme small outlier of type-
y
is
d_{\text{out}}=(0,x_{\text{out}})
, finite, given
\lambda_{\text{out}}
large.

Hamura:2025
treat these as a zero-inflated problem by focusing on the posterior behavior of the Poisson-RSB mixing component at the origin.

Theorem 3.6
.

(
Hamura:2025
, Theorem 2)

For any
\epsilon>0
, the posterior probability
Pr(\eta_{i}<\epsilon|y_{i}=0;a,b)
is a nondecreasing function of
\lambda_{i}
. Moreover, if
0<a<1<a^{\prime}<\infty
and
s\in(0,1]
, then
Pr(\eta_{i}<\epsilon|y_{i}=0;a,b)/Pr(\eta_{i}<\epsilon|y_{i}=0;a^{\prime},b)\to\infty\text{ as }\epsilon\to 0
.

In the above Theorem, Hamura introduced the following property to characterize Poisson-RSB robustness to zeros:
Pr
({\eta}_{i}<\epsilon|\textbf{d}_{1},\dots,\textbf{d}_{n},\textbf{x}_{i},y_{i}=0)
is a non-decreasing function of
\lambda_{i}
, which indicates that the multiplicative error effect captures the zero-valued outlier while the regression component moves away from zero.
It should be noted that this property does not depend on the mixing distribution and is satisfied by any mixed Poisson, as we show in the Appendix
A.1
.
Moreover, it should be noted that this definition is different from the definitions provided in Section
3.2
and, as will be shown in the next section, does not imply them.

3.4
Non robustness against extreme outliers of type-
x

We now study the asymptotic behavior of mixed Poisson regression models under the framework presented in Section
2
, in the presence of outliers of type-
x
, with a corrupted covariate.
For simplicity, hereafter we assume
x\in\mathbb{R}
, however results can be extended to
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, where outliers are such that one component
x_{j}\to\pm\infty
.
We also assume there is no additional Gaussian random effect
\zeta
and consider a dataset with
n
unbiased observation and one outlier of type-
x
. The result can then be extended to multiple outliers.

When the covariate becomes infinitely large, independently of its direction, mixed Poisson models do not perform asymptotic outlier rejection, as stated in the following Theorem.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
y_{1},\dots,y_{n+1}\in\mathbb{N}
. Under a Bayesian mixed Poisson count regression model framework where the mixing distribution has LRV, or lighter, right tails and polynomial, or lighter, divergence at zero we have that

\frac{p_{x}(\beta|y_{1},\dots,y_{n},y_{n+1})}{p_{x}(\beta|y_{1},\dots,y_{n})}\nrightarrow 1\text{ as }x_{n+1}\to\pm\infty.

Note that the regression coefficients posterior asymptotic behavior changes depending on the target value associated with the outlying covariate.
When the type-
x
outlier target value is positive, the corresponding mixed Poisson likelihood becomes zero everywhere except at zero, indicating a very poor performance of the model.
We state this formally with the following Lemma:

Lemma 3.8
.

Under a mixed Poisson count regression model framework, given
y\in\mathbb{N}
positive, and
\beta\neq 0
, we have that

p_{x}(y|\beta)\to 0\text{ as }x\to\pm\infty.

Hence, with extreme outlier of type-
x
, and an associated
y>0
the origin (in the regression parameter space) becomes the best value of
\beta
under the mixed Poisson likelihood.
While Lemma
3.8
holds for any mixed Poisson model with mixing distribution with at most polynomial divergence at zero and at most LRV right tail, for clarity, we show it here for the simple Poisson model:
p_{x}(y|\beta)=\text{Pois}(y|\exp(x\beta))
.
By applying a change of variable,
\lambda=\exp(x\beta)
, it is easy to see that the likelihood,
\text{Pois}(y|\lambda)=\lambda^{y}e^{-\lambda}/y!
goes to zero as
\lambda\to 0
and as
\lambda\to\infty
, given
\beta\neq 0
. The proof for the mixed Poisson models is given in Appendix
A.2
.

As a result of the asymptotic property of the likelihood, the regression coefficient posterior converges in distribution to a point mass at zero:

Theorem 3.9
.

Under a mixed Poisson count regression model framework, where the mixing distribution has LRV, or lighter, right tails and polynomial, or lighter, divergence at zero, we have that, for
y\in\mathbb{N}
positive:

p_{x}(\beta|y)\to\delta_{0}\text{ as }x\to\pm\infty.

Thus, in contrast to extreme large outliers of type-
y
, in which case the Poisson-RSB and Poisson-log-t provide outlier rejection, the models are not only non-robust to type-
x
outliers but express very radical ill-behavior through posterior of
\beta
collapsing to a point mass at zero.

On the contrary, when the target value of the type-
x
outlier is zero, the likelihood is non zero only for values of
\beta
with opposite sign w.r.t.
x
, such that the regression model
\lambda=\exp(x\beta)\to 0
. If, for instance, we consider the limit
x\to+\infty
and denote by
\Omega_{\beta}=\{\beta<0\}
, subset of the regression parameter space, then the regression coefficient posterior tends to the prior evaluated in (and normalized over)
\Omega_{\beta}
.

Theorem 3.10
.

Under a mixed Poisson count regression model framework, we have that, for
y=0
:

\displaystyle p_{x}(\beta|y=0)

\displaystyle\to\frac{\pi_{\beta}(\beta)\mathbb{I}(\beta<0)}{\int_{-\infty}^{0}\pi_{\beta}(\beta)d\beta}\text{ as }x\to\infty,

\displaystyle p_{x}(\beta|y=0)

\displaystyle\to\frac{\pi_{\beta}(\beta)\mathbb{I}(\beta>0)}{\int_{0}^{\infty}\pi_{\beta}(\beta)d\beta}\text{ as }x\to-\infty.

The full proofs of the above theorems are available in the appendix
A.2
, however, to provide intuition on why the posterior of
\beta
collapses to a point mass given
y>0
, we provide here a sketch of the proof.
In addition to Lemma
3.8
, the key fact in the proof of Theorem
3.9
is to observe that the marginal likelihood
m(y)
goes to 0 as well, but slower than the likelihood
p_{x}(y|\beta)
(with
\beta\neq 0
).
By applying the change of variable
z=x\beta
to the mixed Poisson models we have that

\displaystyle m(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_{x}(y|\beta)\pi_{\beta}(\beta)d\beta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p(y|z)\pi_{\beta}\Big(\frac{z}{x}\Big)dz

\displaystyle\approx\big(\pi_{\beta}(0)/x\big)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p(y|z)dz\text{ as }x\to\pm\infty

where, in the last step,
p(y|z)=\text{Pois}(y|e^{z})
is independent of
x
and
\pi_{\beta}
is bounded by assumption.
This indicates that the marginal decays to 0 at a rate
\frac{1}{x}
, slower than the likelihood.
If we then consider the posterior, by replacing
m(y)
with its asymptotic equivalent we can show that, when
|\beta|>0
, (see Appendix
A.2
)

\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}p_{x}(\beta|y)=\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{p_{x}(y|\beta)\pi_{\beta}(\beta)}{\pi_{\beta}(0)/x\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p(y|z)dz}=0.

Then, given
a>0
, applying DCT and the property above, we get that:

\displaystyle\text{Pr}(|\beta|\geq a|y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\{|\beta|\geq a\}}p_{x}(\beta|y)d\beta

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{\{|\beta|\geq a\}}p_{x}(y|\beta)\pi_{\beta}(\beta)d\beta}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{x}(y|\beta)\pi_{\beta}(\beta)d\beta}

\displaystyle\overset{z=x\beta}{=}\frac{\frac{1}{x}\int_{\{|z|\geq ax\}}p(y|z)\pi_{\beta}(z/x)dz}{\frac{1}{x}\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(y|z)\pi_{\beta}(z/x)dz}

\displaystyle\overset{DCT}{\to}\frac{\pi_{\beta}(0)}{\pi_{\beta}(0)}\frac{\int_{|z|>\infty}p(y|z)dz}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(y|z)dz}=0\text{ as }x\to\pm\infty

that is, the posterior density collapses to the Dirac delta function.

4.2
Behavior of mixed Poisson models under moderate outliers

While the above theoretical results provide insight on the behavior of the models under extreme outliers, their properties under moderate outliers can differ.
With moderate (or non-extreme) outliers we mean data points whose component (the target and covariate) values are within the range of the other data, but under some anomalous combinations with respect to the trends in the remaining observations that cannot be captured by standard Poisson regression.

To empirically study how the posterior distribution for the regression coefficients changes in the presence of moderate outlier observations, we set up two simulation experiments, where we tested the alternative models’ robustness to different types of outliers in a controlled setting and compared their performance. Independently of the source of outlying information, outliers can be further classified based on the target value and its expectation conditional on the rest of the data: a data point is said to be a left hand-side (LHS) outlier if its target value is much smaller than expected, and it is a right hand-side (RHS) outlier if the target value is much higher than expected
(
OHagan:1979
, following )
.
For both experiments, we considered four different designs for outliers, we distinguish between LHS and RHS outliers and between outliers originated from corrupted target (type-
y
) or covariate (type-
x
). We first generated a non-outlier dataset
\D=\{y_{i},x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
for two alternative data sizes
n=\{10,100\}
, by generating the covariates
x_{i}
from a Gaussian distribution with mean 0 and variance 1 and the counts from a Poisson distribution with log-rate
\log\lambda=a+bx
.
The parameters of the log rate were sampled from two uniform distributions
b\sim\text{U}(0.001,2.433),\ a\sim\text{U}(4.868-2b,5.136-2b)
resulting in data with an expected count ranging from 130 to 170 when
x=2
, and from 0.005 to 150 when
x=-2
.
Given the non-outlying data, we then generated series of outliers,
\mathcal{D}^{{}_{\text{out}}}=\{y^{\text{out}},x^{\text{out}}\}
, with the observed outcome increasingly different from the true Poisson rate, so that the discrepancy
\Delta\log y^{{}_{\text{out}}}=\log(y^{{}_{\text{out}}})-\log(\lambda(x^{{}_{\text{out}}}))
increases linearly. We generated 10 outliers of each type as follows:

•

LHS-
y
:
x^{{}_{\text{out}}}=\max(x)
and
y^{\text{out}}\in\{\lambda(\max(x)),\dots,0\}
decreases to 0

•

LHS-
x
:
y^{\text{out}}=\lfloor\lambda(\bar{x})\rfloor
and
x^{{}_{\text{out}}}\in\{\bar{x},\dots,3\max(x)\}
increases

•

RHS-
y
:
x^{\text{out}}=\bar{x}
and
y^{{}_{\text{out}}}\in\{\lfloor\lambda(\bar{x})\rfloor,\dots,3\max(y)\}
increases

•

RHS-
x
:
y^{\text{out}}=\lfloor\lambda(\max{x})\rfloor
and
x^{{}_{\text{out}}}\in\{\bar{x},\dots,\min(x^{{}_{\text{out}}})\}
decreases so that
\lambda(\min(x^{{}_{\text{out}}}))=\min(y)+10^{-3}
.

In the first experiment setting, we added to the unbiased dataset one progressively more extreme outlier of the same type, leading to 11 different simulated data for each outlier type and each data size.
An example of simulated datasets, with 100 unbiased observations and one increasingly larger or smaller outlier of four alternative types is shown in Figure

In the second experiment setting, to test the impact of the number of outliers, we constructed outlier data sets

with

We then fitted the four different mixed Poisson models presented in Section

to each simulated data (with and without outlier(s)), so that

with

Measuring robustness to moderate outliers

To study the properties of the four count regression models defined above under such moderate outliers, we measured the deviation between the posterior distribution with and without moderate outliers using the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence.

Assuming the presence of one outlier,
\textbf{d}_{n+1}
, we measured and compared alternative models’ robustness to it via the KL divergence of the model’s regression parameters’ posterior distribution attained when the outlier is included in the model, with respect to the one obtained after excluding the outlier from the model:

\KL(p(\beta,\zeta|\boldsymbol{d}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{d}_{n})||p(\beta,\zeta|\boldsymbol{d}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{d}_{n},\boldsymbol{d}_{n+1})).

(7)

The lower the divergence, the more robust the model. This divergence is a good robustness measure for RHS and LHS outliers of different type, and can be computed similarly in the presence of multiple outliers.

For all count models considered, we generated Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMMC) samples from the posterior distribution using Stan
(
Stan
)
, in both simulation and real-world case studies.
Since our focus was not in the computational efficiency, we used the same Hamiltion Monte Carlo samplers in Stan for all models despite there exist an efficient Gibbs-sampling method for e.g. the Poisson-RSB mixture.

We estimated the KL divergence between regression parameters MCMC samples, obtained with all data and excluding the outliers, via bias-reduced generalized-NN Divergence Estimator as proposed by
Wang:2009
and available in R in the
kldest
package
(
kldest24
)
.
Given
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n}\in\mathrm{R}^{p}
i.i.d. samples from
p(\beta|y)
and
\beta^{*}_{1},\dots,\beta^{*}_{m}\in\mathrm{R}^{p}
i.i.d. samples from
p(\beta|y,y_{\text{out}})
, the
k
-NN divergence estimator is:

\hat{\KL}_{n,m}(p(\beta|y)||p(\beta|y,y_{\text{out}}))=\frac{p}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\frac{\nu_{k}(i)}{\rho_{k}(i)}+\log\frac{m}{n-1}

where
\rho_{k}(i)
is the Euclidean distance of the
k
-th closest sample
\beta_{j\neq i}
to
\beta_{i}
, and
\nu_{k}(i)
is the Euclidean distance of the
k
-th closest sample
\beta^{*}_{j}
to
\beta_{i}
.
The bias-reduced generalized
k
-NN based KL divergence estimator is such that
k
is no longer fixed, but the numbers of neighbors
k_{i}^{\prime}
and
k_{i}
are chosen differently for
\rho
and
\nu
, and for each
i=1,\dots,n
MC sample. Given the
i
-th sample,
k_{i}^{\prime}
and
k_{i}
are chosen by taking the largest possible numbers of neighbors
k_{i}^{\prime},k_{i}
smaller than
\delta(i):=\max(\rho_{1}(i),\nu_{1}(i))
, so that
\rho_{k^{\prime}{{}_{i}}}(i)
and
\nu_{k{{}_{i}}}(i)
are comparable. Further, a correction term is added to guarantee consistency of the estimator, which becomes:

\hat{\KL}_{n,m}(p(\beta|y)||p(\beta|y,y_{\text{out}}))=\frac{p}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\frac{\nu_{k{{}_{i}}}(i)}{\rho_{k^{\prime}{{}_{i}}}(i)}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}[\psi(k^{\prime}_{i})-\psi(k_{i})]+\log\frac{m}{n-1}

where
\psi(k)=\Gamma^{\prime}(k)/\Gamma(k)
is the Digamma function.

It should be noted that, when using k-NN density estimates, it is possible to get some negative
\hat{\KL}
values, indicating that the two posterior distributions are very similar, basically exchangeable. Indeed, generalized-NN KL estimates become negative when the average log distance of
\beta
samples from the
k^{\prime}
closest
\beta^{*}
s is smaller than their average log distance from the
k
closest
\beta
s.
Thus, we interpreted negative k-NN estimates as zeros.

The Poisson model was strongly affected by all types of moderate outlier.

Kara Sea polar bears case study

As a motivating real world case study we tested the performance of alternative count regression models in species distribution modeling of polar bears (
Ursus maritimus

The data were previously analyzed by
Makinen2018

Our target data consist of 9009 polar bear count observations, including one moderate outlier (see below), collected in three different monitoring campaigns conducted in years 1996-2005, 2005–2013, and 2013, respectively.
The second study campaign consists of presence-only data.
Following the earlier study, we modeled the observed polar bear counts with respect to distance to the coast, ice concentration, and sea surface salinity, as these covariates were demonstrated to be relevant in explaining polar bears’ distribution in the Kara Sea.
The data were available at a 5 km spatial resolution and at a temporal resolution of one month
(
Makinen2018

The dataset contains one clear outlier observation: at one sampling location with zero ice three polar bears were counted (the maximum number of observed polar bears in the whole data is four), while no polar bears were detected in any other location with no ice (see Figure

Ecologically, the ice concentration is atypical for such an high number of polar bears.

6
Discussion and conclusion

In this work, we extended the definition of robustness of Bayesian models against outliers in terms of asymptotic outlier rejection (following
OHagan:1979
’s
(
OHagan:1979
)
outlier-proneness definition for i.i.d. continuous random variables) to count regression models, and defined asymptotic robustness against outliers originating from both infinite target (type-
y
) and infinite covariates (type-
x
).
We showed how, in count regression, corrupted covariates affect inferential results differently from outliers presenting anomalously large counts.
Indeed, this contrasts the symmetry between these two types of outliers in linear regression
(
Gagnon:2020
)
.
For mixed Poisson models, the mixing component is only able to handle large outlying counts when its tails are sufficiently heavy: a LRV right tail makes the model robust to infinitely large target, and a left tail with polynomial diverging behavior allows the model to capture zero inflated data
(
Hamura:2025
)
.
However, as shown here (Theorem
3.7
), independently of the mixing component tails, mixed Poisson models are affected by outliers originating from corrupted covariates.
Asymptotically, as the outlying covariate becomes infinitely large (or small), the likelihood shrinks to zero for any value of
\beta
in the regression parameter space, with the exception of the case
\beta=0
. As a result, the posterior collapses to a point mass distribution, concentrating at zero.

This phenomenon is similar to Lindley’s paradox
(
Lindley:1957
)
in a sense that a very poor model fit indicated by a zero likelihood (almost everywhere) leads to an extremely precise regression coefficient posterior.
At a close look, this degenerate behavior makes perfect sense, as it indicates that, in the presence of an infinite covariate, the best posterior estimate concentrates around zero, as this is the only way to cancel the effect of such extreme outlier.
However, as demonstrated by our simulation studies and real world case study, this lack of asymptotic robustness against outliers of type-
x
, did not always compromise mixed Poisson models performance in the presence of moderate, finite, outliers. Often, when the outlier was still within the range of the rest of the data, already a Negative Binomial (Poisson-Gamma) model was sufficient to properly capture the structure of the studied data.
Interestingly, the Negative Binomial model also had a better fit to the data in many of our moderate outlier of type-
x
experiments than the Poisson-RSB model.
Specifically, the extreme tail of the RSB distribution let the variance in the posterior estimates explode if applied to all data points, making this model not appropriate to fit data that include only moderate outliers.
This behavior was fixed when defining the mixing prior as a mixture of RSB and point mass at one (the Poisson-RSB s-mix model), as shown in the Kara Sea case study.

Our case study on polar bear distribution in the Kara Sea demonstrates that already a very small percentage of moderate outliers (here
1/9009<0.01\%
) can have a significant effect on results.
While the changes in the inferred coefficient values for ice coverage with and without the outlier seem rather marginal (Figure
8
.B), they are in fact rather large from a predictive point of view.
Without the outlier, polar bear density was predicted to increase approximately six fold from lowest ice concentration to highest ice concentration, whereas when the outlier was included into the analysis, the increase was only four fold (Figure
8
.A).
This one third decrease in the predicted relative change can have a large effect, for example, in ecological risk assessment
(
Helle+etal:2020
)
.
Since this change in prediction was caused by a single moderate outlier out of 9009, the result highlights the importance for future work towards count models that are robust for outliers in covariates – and towards methods to detect them
(
Plummer:2025
)
.

While our work is centered more on caveats than solutions, we believe it provides important insight on Bayesian count regression models.
First, our formal proof that mixed Poisson models do not behave symmetrically with respect to outliers of type-
y
and type-
x
has important practical implication.
While practitioners can use LRV heavy tailed models (e.g., Poisson log-t or Poisson-RSB s-mix) to secure against corrupted target values, they should still check their model results for high leverage and influence points to identify potential covariate outliers
(
Plummer:2025
)
.
Second, the results presented here also provide insight into future developments by indicating that mixing component might not be enough to make count regression models robust against outliers of type-
x
.
However, our Theorem
3.7
considers only mixing distributions with at most polynomial divergence at zero, so one future research direction should be whether fast divergence could change the behavior of the model – or should we follow completely different path.
After all, the mixing component
\eta
is by construction part of the data generating process while in generalized linear models covariates are considered fixed by design. Hence, robustness to type-
x
outliers could require outlier generation description for corrected covariates. Alternatively, a spike-and-slab type of mixture formulation for data point-wise regression coefficients could be a solution.
Third, our Corollary
3.5
shows that the log Student-
t
mixed Poisson model is robust to large outliers of type-
y
.
While this result was a straightforward application of the theoretical work of
Hamura:2025
, together with our empirical results provides useful insight to practitioners. Our simulation and case study results indicate that log Student-
t
mixing distribution might behave more nicely in the presence of moderate outliers than the heavier tail rescaled Beta distribution.
Fourth, our results also motivate further studies on how different types of regression models, such as the logistic, behave in the presence of corrupted covariates.
Additionally, it could be worth studying what happens in the presence of outliers presenting corrupted values in multiple directions.

We want to also note that a rigorous and theoretical definition of moderate outliers and of the corresponding model robustness against them is still lacking in Bayesian statistics.
Developing a cohesive, non-asymptotic theory of robustness would be a valuable contribution to the field, especially useful to applied statisticians.
These questions were out of the scope of this work though.

(0,1)

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{u^{y}\pi(u)}{\beta y!}w^{y}\exp(-wu)\log(w)du

where we denote by
w=e^{x\beta}
.

For the limit
w\to 0
(
x\beta\to-\infty
), we use that
e^{-wu}<1
and get that

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{u^{y}\pi(u)}{\beta y!}w^{y}\exp(-wu)\log wdu\leq w^{y}\log(w)\int_{0}^{1}\frac{u^{y}\pi(u)}{\beta y!}du\to 0\text{ as }w\to 0

since, the integral is finite as when
\pi
left tail diverges at zero with rate
a-1
(Equation
A.2
),
u^{y}\pi(u)\approx u^{y+a-1}\to 0
as
u\to 0
, for any
y>0
and
a\in(0,1)
.

Let us now consider the limit
w\to\infty
(
x\beta\to\infty
).
By applying the change of variable
v=uw
, we have that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{u^{y}\pi(u)}{\beta y!}w^{y}\exp(-wu)\log(w)du

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{w}\frac{v^{y}e^{-v}}{\beta y!}\log(w)\frac{\pi(v/w)}{w}dv

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\log(w)}{w}\int_{0}^{w}\sup_{v\in(0,w)}\{\pi(v/w)\}\frac{e^{-v}v^{y}}{\beta y!}dv

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\log(w)}{w}\int_{0}^{w}\frac{v^{a-1}}{w^{a-1}}\frac{e^{-v}v^{y}}{\beta y!}dv

\displaystyle=\frac{\log(w)}{w^{a}}\int_{0}^{w}\frac{e^{-v}v^{y+a-1}}{\beta y!}dv

\displaystyle\to 0\text{ as }w\to\infty,y>0.

where we used that the supremum of
\pi
is reached around 0, so that
\pi(w/v)\approx(w/v)^{a-1}
. Further, the integral is finite as, it is proportional to the integral of a Gamma random variable over
(0,w)
:
\int_{0}^{w}\frac{v^{y+a-1}e^{-v}}{y!}dv=\frac{(y+a)!}{y!}\mathbb{Pr}(V\leq w)\to\frac{(y+a)!}{y!}
as
w\to\infty
, with
V\sim\text{Gamma}(y+a,1)
.

Thus, we have just shown that both integrals in
(0,1)
and
(1,\infty)
go to 0, meaning
xp_{x}(y|\beta)\to 0
as
x\to\pm\infty
.

\square

Lemma A.4
.

Under a mixed Poisson count regression model framework, where the mixing distribution has LRV, or lighter, right tails and polynomial, or lighter, given
y\in\mathbb{N}
positive, we have that

p_{x}(\beta|y)
satisfies the three following properties:

1.

Non negativity:
p_{x}(\beta|y)\geq 0\ \forall\beta\in\mathbb{R},x\in\mathbb{R}
.

2.

Unit mass:
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_{x}(\beta|y)d\beta=1
.

3.

Concentration at zero:
\forall a>0\ \lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\sup_{|\beta|\geq a}|p_{x}(\beta|y)|=0
and

\forall a>0\ \lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\int_{\{|\beta|\geq a\}}p_{x}(\beta|y)d\beta=0
.

proof:

Let us fix
y\in\mathbb{N}
positive.
Properties 1 and 2 are true since
p_{x}(\beta|y)
is a proper distribution, by definition.
Let us focus on condition 3.

The first requirement is uniform convergence of the posterior
p_{x}(\beta|y)=p_{x}(y|\beta)\pi_{\beta}(\beta)/m(y)
.
Let us check the behavior of each component in the limit of
x\to\pm\infty
.

Let us fix
a>0
, we are interested in studying the limit for
x\to\pm\infty
, given
|\beta|\geq a
.
From Lemma
A.2
, we have that for any
\beta\neq 0
,

\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}p_{x}(y|\beta)=0.

Let us now consider the marginal
m(y)
and study its asymptotic behavior. By applying the change of variable
z=x\beta
we have that

m(y)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_{x}(y|\beta)\pi_{\beta}(\beta)d\beta=\frac{1}{x}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p(y|z)\pi_{\beta}\Big(\frac{z}{x}\Big)dz

where
p(y|z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{Pois}(y|e^{z}u)\pi(u)du
.Then we can observe that
m(y)
is asymptotically equivalent to
\big(\pi_{\beta}(0)/x\big)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p(y|z)dz
:

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{m(y)}{\big(\pi_{\beta}(0)/x\big)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p(y|z)dz}

\displaystyle=\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p(y|z)\pi_{\beta}(z/x)dz}{\pi_{\beta}(0)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p(y|z)dz}

\displaystyle\overset{DCT}{=}\frac{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}p(y|z)\pi_{\beta}(z/x)dz}{\pi_{\beta}(0)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p(y|z)dz}=1,

indicating that the marginal decays to 0 at a rate
\frac{1}{x}
slower than the likelihood.
Note that here we can apply DCT since, by hypothesis the prior
\pi_{\beta}(z/x)
is bounded, meaning that there exists
M_{1}>0:\pi_{\beta}(z/x)<M_{1}
. This implies that
f(z)=M_{1}p(y|z)\geq p(y|z)\pi_{\beta}(z/x)\forall z\in\mathbb{R}
.
Further, from the existence of joint moments, the mixed Poisson likelihood is integrable:
\int_{0}^{\infty}p(y|z)dz<\infty
, that is
f(z)
is integrable.

If we then consider the posterior, by replacing
m(y)
with its asymptotic equivalent we have that, when
|\beta|>0
,

\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}p_{x}(\beta|y)=\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{p_{x}(y|\beta)\pi_{\beta}(\beta)}{\pi_{\beta}(0)/x\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p(y|z)dz}=0

since
\frac{\pi_{\beta}(\beta)}{\pi_{\beta}(0)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p(y|z)dz}
is a constant w.r.t.
x
and

xp_{x}(y|\beta)=\int_{0}^{\infty}x(e^{x\beta}u)^{y}\exp(-e^{x\beta}u)/y!\pi(u)du\to 0\text{ as }x\to\pm\infty.

Where the latter limit holds for any mixing distribution
\pi
with tails behavior as in equations
A.2
,
A.1
, as demonstrated in
A.3
.

We now need to verify the second requirement. Given
a>0
, applying DCT and the property above:

\displaystyle\int_{\{|\beta|\geq a\}}p_{x}(\beta|y)d\beta

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{\{|\beta|\geq a\}}p_{x}(y|\beta)\pi_{\beta}(\beta)d\beta}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{x}(y|\beta)\pi_{\beta}(\beta)d\beta}

\displaystyle\overset{z=x\beta}{=}\frac{\frac{1}{x}\int_{\{|z|\geq ax\}}p(y|z)\pi_{\beta}(z/x)dz}{\frac{1}{x}\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(y|z)\pi_{\beta}(z/x)dz}

\displaystyle\overset{DCT}{\to}\frac{\pi_{\beta}(0)}{\pi_{\beta}(0)}\frac{\int_{|z|>\infty}p(y|z)dz}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(y|z)dz}=0\text{ as }x\to\pm\infty

since
\{z:|z|>a|x|\to\infty,\text{ as }x\to\pm\infty\}=\o
and in the last step we can again apply DCT as the integrand is bounded by a function of
z
, since the prior
\pi_{\beta}
is bounded.

\square

Note that the fact that the posterior satisfies property 3 means that
\forall a>0\ Pr(|\beta|>a|y)\to 0\text{ as }x\to\pm\infty
, which is generally sufficient to prove posterior convergence to
\delta_{0}
. However, we will still provide below a formal proof for such convergence, applying the definition of Dirac delta.

Theorem A.5
.

Under a mixed Poisson count regression model framework, where the mixing distribution has LRV, or lighter, right tails and polynomial, or lighter, we have that, for
y\in\mathbb{N}
positive:

p_{x}(\beta|y)\to\delta_{0}\text{ as }x\to\pm\infty.

proof:

By definition,
p_{x}(\beta|y)
converges in the sense of distribution to Dirac delta, iff

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(\beta)p_{x}(\beta|y)d\beta\to\phi(0)\text{ as }|x|\to\infty

for any continuous and bounded function
\phi\in C
.
So, fixed
\epsilon>0
, we want to show that
\exists N>0:|x|>N\implies|\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(\beta)p_{x}(\beta|y)d\beta-\phi(0)|<\epsilon
.

From Lemma
A.4
, property 2 (unit mass) we have that
\phi(0)\overset{p2}{=}\phi(0)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_{x}(\beta|y)d\beta
, then

\Big|\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(\beta)p_{x}(\beta|y)d\beta-\phi(0)\Big|\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_{x}(\beta|y)|\phi(\beta)-\phi(0)|d\beta.

We now use the fact that
\phi
is continuous, hence
\exists\delta>0:|\beta|<\delta\implies|\phi(\beta)-\phi(0)|<\epsilon/2
.
Then we can split the integral so that:

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_{x}(\beta|y)|\phi(\beta)-\phi(0)|d\beta

\displaystyle=\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}p_{x}(\beta|y)|\phi(\beta)-\phi(0)|d\beta

\displaystyle+\int_{\{|\beta|>\delta\}}p_{x}(\beta|y)|\phi(\beta)-\phi(0)|d\beta.

For the integral in the
\delta
surrounding of 0 we have that, by continuity and property 2:

\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}p_{x}(\beta|y)|\phi(\beta)-\phi(0)|d\beta<\epsilon/2\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}p_{x}(\beta|y)d\beta\leq\epsilon/2.

Let us now consider the integral in the tails.
Given that
\phi
is bounded, we have that
|\phi(\beta)|<K\implies|\phi(\beta)-\phi(0)|\leq|\phi(\beta)|+|\phi(0)|<2K
.
Then
\int_{\{|\beta|>\delta\}}p_{x}(\beta|y)|\phi(\beta)-\phi(0)|d\beta\leq 2K\int_{\{|\beta|>\delta\}}p_{x}(\beta|y)d\beta
.
We now use property 3 from Lemma
A.4
:
\lim_{|x|\to\infty}\int_{\{|\beta|>\delta\}}p_{x}(\beta|y)d\beta=0
. Then given
|\beta|>\delta
, we can choose
N
such that:

|x|>N\implies\int_{\{|\beta|>\delta\}}p_{x}(\beta|y)d\beta<\epsilon/(4K)

which results in

\int_{\{|\beta|>\delta\}}p_{x}(\beta|y)|\phi(\beta)-\phi(0)|d\beta<\epsilon/2.

To conclude, we have just shown that

|x|>N\implies\Big|\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(\beta)p_{x}(\beta|y)d\beta-\phi(0)\Big|<\epsilon,

that means
p_{x}(\beta|y)\to\delta_{0}
as
x\to\pm\infty
.

\square

Now, to show non-robustness to infinite covariates in the presence of multiple observations, of which only a subset is outliers, we consider two data points
d,d_{\text{out}}
modeled with a mixed Poisson regression, and let
x_{\text{out}}\to\pm\infty
. The posterior of the regression coefficient given the two observations does not converge to the posterior given only the non-outlying observation, which is the condition required to achieve asymptotic robustness.
The result can be extended to any higher number of observations, including any proportion of outliers.

Theorem A.6
.

Let
y,y_{\text{out}}\in\mathbb{N}
, with
y_{\text{out}}
positive. Under a Bayesian mixed Poisson count regression model framework, where the mixing distribution has LRV, or lighter, right tails and polynomial, or lighter, we have that

\frac{p_{x}(\beta|y,y_{\text{out}})}{p_{x}(\beta|y)}\nrightarrow 1\text{ as }x_{\text{out}}\to\pm\infty.

proof
:
Let us consider
\beta\neq 0
.
By applying the Bayes Theorem to the joint marginal probability we have that

m(y,y_{\text{out}})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{x}(y_{\text{out}}|\beta)p_{x}(y|\beta)\pi_{\beta}(\beta)d\beta=\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{x}(\beta|y_{\text{out}})p_{x}(y|\beta)m(y_{\text{out}})d\beta.

Given
\beta\neq 0
, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{p_{x}(\beta|y,y_{\text{out}})}{p_{x}(\beta|y)}

\displaystyle=p_{x}(y_{\text{out}}|\beta)\frac{m(y)}{m(y,y_{\text{out}})}

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{x}(y_{\text{out}}|\beta)m(y)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{x}(\beta|y_{\text{out}})p_{x}(y|\beta)m(y_{\text{out}})d\beta}

\displaystyle\to\frac{m(y)\pi_{\beta}(\beta)}{p_{x}(y|0)}0=0\text{ as }x_{\text{out}}\to\pm\infty.

In the last step, we use the fact that the posterior
p_{x}(\beta|y_{\text{out}})\to\delta_{0}
and
p_{x}(y|\beta)
is continuous, and apply the definition of Dirac delta to get the limit of the integral at the denominator, and the fact that the likelihood for an infinite covariate outlier decays to 0 faster than its marginal, as discussed in the proof of property 3 from Lemma
A.4
.

\square

We have just shown that, in the presence of infinitely large covariates associated with positive counts, Poisson mixed models do not perform asymptotic outliers rejection. This is sufficient to prove that these models are not theoretically robust to outlying covariates. However, for completeness, let us now study how these models handle infinite covariates associated to 0 counts.

Theorem A.7
.

Under a mixed Poisson count regression model framework, we have that, for
y=0
:

\displaystyle p_{x}(\beta|y=0)

\displaystyle\to\frac{\pi_{\beta}(\beta)\mathbb{I}(\beta<0)}{\int_{-\infty}^{0}\pi_{\beta}(\beta)d\beta}\text{ as }x\to\infty,

\displaystyle p_{x}(\beta|y=0)

\displaystyle\to\frac{\pi_{\beta}(\beta)\mathbb{I}(\beta>0)}{\int_{0}^{\infty}\pi_{\beta}(\beta)d\beta}\text{ as }x\to-\infty.

proof:

As
x
always appears as
x\beta
in the model, we are interested in studying the posterior limit behavior as
x\beta\to\pm\infty
.
We want to show that
p_{x}(\beta|y=0)\to 0\text{ as }x\beta\to\infty,
and
p_{x}(\beta|y=0)\to K\propto\pi_{\beta}(\beta)\text{ as }x\beta\to-\infty.

Let us first consider the likelihood:

\displaystyle p_{x}(y=0|\beta)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{Pois}(0|e^{x\beta}u)\pi(u)du

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-e^{x\beta}u)\pi(u)du

\displaystyle\overset{DCT}{\to}\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }x\beta\to+\infty\\
1&\text{ if }x\beta\to-\infty\\
\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-u)\pi(u)du&\text{ if }\beta=0.\end{cases}

Then, the posterior give
y=0
is:

\displaystyle p_{x}(\beta|y=0)

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{x}(y=0|\beta)\pi_{\beta}(\beta)}{\int_{-\infty}^{0}p_{x}(y=0|\beta)\pi_{\beta}(\beta)d\beta+\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{x}(y=0|\beta)\pi_{\beta}(\beta)d\beta}

and its limiting behavior depends on the sign of
x\beta
.
Let us now study the alternative limit cases separately.

When
x\beta\to\infty
, the likelihood decays to 0, while the marginal
m(y)
does not.
Then, if
\beta>0
, as
x\to\infty
we have that
p_{x}(\beta|y=0)\to 0
.
Similarly, if
\beta<0
, as
x\to-\infty
we have
p_{x}(\beta|y=0)\to 0
.

When
x\beta\to-\infty
, the likelihood goes to 1.
Then, if
\beta<0
, as
x\to\infty
we have
p_{x}(\beta|y=0)\to\pi_{\beta}(\beta)/\int_{-\infty}^{0}\pi_{\beta}(\beta)d\beta
.
If
\beta>0
, as
x\to-\infty
we have
p_{x}(\beta|y=0)\to\pi_{\beta}(\beta)/\int_{0}^{\infty}\pi_{\beta}(\beta)d\beta
.

Note that if the prior for
\beta
is symmetric w.r.t. 0, such as for a Gaussian prior, we have that

p_{x}(\beta|y)\to 2\pi_{\beta}(\beta)\text{ as }x\beta\to-\infty.

\square

A.3
Computation details for Poisson-RSB s-mix model in the Kara Sea case study

Give
i=1,\dots,n
observations and
j(i)\in\{1,2,3\}
study campaign for each observation, the hierarchical framework of the model used in Section
5
, for the Poisson-RSB s-mix model is:

\begin{split}y_{i}|\lambda_{i},\eta_{i}&\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda_{i}\eta_{i})\\
\eta_{i}|s&\sim(1-s)\delta_{1}+s\text{RSB}(0.5,0.5)\\
\log(\lambda_{i})&=x_{i}^{\prime}\beta+\alpha_{j(i)}\\
\beta&\sim N(0,10);\alpha\sim N(0,2);s\sim\text{Beta}(1,1)\end{split}

(A.4)

where
s
represents the proportion of outliers, while
\pi
denotes the different Poisson-mixing distributions.
Marginalizing the observational layer over
s
we can recover an equivalent formulation of the model:

\begin{split}y_{i}|\lambda_{i},\eta_{i},s&\sim s\text{Poisson}(\lambda_{i}\eta_{i})+(1-s)\text{Poisson}(\lambda_{i})\\
\eta_{i}|Z_{i}=1&\sim\text{RSB}(0.5,0.5)\\
\log(\lambda_{i})&=x_{i}^{\prime}\beta+\alpha_{j(i)}\\
\beta&\sim N(0,10);\alpha\sim N(0,2);s\sim\text{Beta}(1,1).\end{split}

(A.5)

In this model, given a r.v.
Z_{i}\sim\text{Bernoulli}(s)
,
\eta_{i}
is only activated when
Z_{i}=1
, while when
Z_{i}=0
the observation
y_{i}
follows a standard Poisson distribution.

When implementing the model in Stan, we follow the latter formulation, but we allow sampling from the posterior of
\eta_{i}
independently of
Z_{i}
. This may result in extremely high values for posterior samples of
\eta_{i}|Z_{i}=0
. To ensure that we only account for the samples
\eta_{i}|Z_{i}=1
we recover the posterior samples of
Z_{i}|y_{i},\eta_{i},s
as follows.
From the Bayes Theorem

p(Z_{i}|y_{i},\eta_{i},\lambda_{i},s)\propto p(y_{i}|\eta_{i},\lambda_{i},s,Z_{i})p(Z_{i}|s)

that results in a Bernoulli r.v. with posterior probabilities:

\displaystyle\text{Pr}(Z_{i}=1|y_{i},s,\lambda_{i},\eta_{i})

\displaystyle\propto s\text{Pois}(y_{i}|\eta_{i}\lambda_{i})

\displaystyle\text{Pr}(Z_{i}=0|y_{i},s,\lambda_{i},\eta_{i})

\displaystyle\propto(1-s)\text{Pois}(y_{i}|\lambda_{i}).

We therefore sample
Z_{i}^{j}
from its posterior, a Bernoulli with rate
\frac{s^{j}\text{Pois}(y_{i}|\eta_{i}^{j}\lambda_{i}^{j})}{(1-s^{j})\text{Pois}(y_{i}|\lambda_{i}^{j})+s^{j}\text{Pois}(y_{i}|\eta_{i}^{j}\lambda_{i}^{j})}
, for each MC sample
j
and set
\eta_{i}^{j}=1
when
Z_{i}^{j}=0
.
