Introduction
This document presents two case studies on nuclear waste disposal to explore the safeguards challenges
and potential solutions present under each scenario. Both involve the disposal of nuclear materials,
either for waste containing safeguarded nuclear materials or waste where safeguards on the nuclear
materials have been previously terminated. The ﬁrst case study is that of a low-level waste (LLW)
repository that is situated near the surface. This facility will accept safeguarded nuclear materials for
disposal. Examples of this type of activity are rare, but the potential for more facilities of this type is very
possible as States become more comfortable with considering safeguards measures in perpetuity on
deep geological repositories. The second study extends this to the case of terminated wastes at an
intermediate depth disposal site. In this case, the reasons why terminated nuclear materials could be
found in these disposal facilities and how obligations under the Comprehensive Safeguards Agreement
(CSA) and Additional Protocol (AP) are met when the disposed waste items are intermediate level waste
(ILW) or high-level waste (HLW).

Intended audience
The goal of this document is to provide a pair of model cases that can be used as a notional starting
point for the design of safeguards measures for similar facilities. This document, or portions thereof,
could be shared via the Oﬃce of International Nuclear Safeguards (NA-241) with the International
Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) and partner States. These scenarios should be viewed as an example that
can be used to guide and shape discussions and not indicative of any speciﬁc facility or State.

General Safeguards Issues to be Examined
States have a right to dispose of nuclear material under safeguards. There is no prohibition against the
practice in the model CSA, which only speciﬁes that the State and Agency shall agree on safeguards
measures to be implemented such that material “is not diverted to nuclear weapons or other nuclear
explosive devices” [1]. The CSA also mentions that safeguards can be terminated on nuclear materials if
they are made “practicably irrecoverable” by being dispersed to the environment or conditioned in a
manner that makes them “unusable for any nuclear activity relevant from the point of view of
safeguards.” However, not all materials fall under this termination clause; indeed, many nuclear
materials will always be subject to safeguards measures such as non-reprocessed spent nuclear fuel.
Therefore, States can dispose of nuclear material under safeguards as long as the State and the Agency
agree on measures to continue to verify that none of the nuclear material is diverted.

Generally, safeguards approaches for the disposal of nuclear material are underdeveloped across the
world. Only one State (Finland) is close to the disposal of spent nuclear fuel and therefore most of the
time and eﬀort invested so far in developing safeguards for waste disposal has been for this facility. To
date, a very comprehensive plan has been developed to safeguard thousands of signiﬁcant quantities
(SQ) of nuclear material that will be disposed of at Onkalo [2]. Fortunately, as of the time of writing,
there are several States that can learn from the Finnish example [3]. However, for non-spent fuel wastes,
including LLW and ILW that contain nuclear materials, there are far fewer examples for States to follow.

There is interest in being able to dispose of safeguarded materials in a low-level waste disposal facility.
There are several circumstances where low-level wastes, from a regulatory and safety perspective, would

 1

still require the application of safeguards. Fuel fabrication and conversion plants can easily generate
wastes exceeding the termination limits of current IAEA policy, which sets requirements on the lower
end of the conditioning scale (perhaps only overpacked or only supercompacted). As such, these wastes
typically contain too much nuclear material to be eligible for termination and the economics of creating
a new treatment facility may not be feasible. In addition, depleted uranium (DU) shielding is a major
problem worldwide as such monolithic, metallic wastes are not eligible for termination [4]. States are
not typically eager to process pieces of DU metal as they would incur signiﬁcant health and exposure
risks to convert them to a form and at a concentration that would be eligible for termination under
current safeguards policies. As such, this source material simply sits in storage and accumulates because
States cannot economically dispose of it. This same is true for bulk thorium wastes and natural uranium
(NU) wastes as well.

In addition, even after safeguards have been terminated for ILW and HLW items containing direct-use
materials, i.e., high enriched uranium (HEU) and plutonium, reporting requirements under the AP
continue. Such materials are typically highly conditioned and stored as glass or ceramic waste forms.
Since there isn’t an operational ILW/HLW disposal facility yet, these materials are currently stored. This
facilitates reporting and complementary access (CA) under the AP, but the process for access after
emplacement and, eventually, closure of a disposal facility has not been exercised in practice. As such,
the roles and responsibilities of States with respect to the AP in this circumstance should be clariﬁed.

Overview: Model Safeguards Approach for a notional
Near Surface Disposal Facility
This section will lay out a notional approach required to adequately safeguard a near surface disposal
facility in a non-nuclear weapons state with a CSA in place. This approach will attempt to identify the
required methods that will need to be applied, document any assumptions about the waste streams, call
out any deﬁciencies noted and suggest potential solutions for those deﬁciencies. As with most waste
facilities, this facility will have three distinct phases: design and construction, operation, closure 1. The
closure phase will require the project team to predict possible containment and surveillance techniques
or other measures, such as satellite surveillance, that will be available within 10-20 years after
commissioning, which is the nominal closure time frame for this type of facility. In addition, the
safeguards approaches applied at the upstream waste treatment facility will have to be discussed due to
its impact on the approach for the near surface disposal facility. Therefore, we will discuss each
component in turn, the treatment facility followed by the disposal facility. For these facilities, only
wastes containing indirect use materials, namely, depleted, natural, and low enriched uranium (DNLEU)
and thorium will be considered. Small amounts of direct use materials (i.e., plutonium) could be
disposed of in such a near surface facility but will not be discussed in detail here.

It should be noted that this approach is tailored to the facilities described below. However, there are
many possible conﬁgurations that a country could pursue. The concepts described below are general in
nature and could be easily adapted to many of these scenarios.

General description of the model facilities
The model near-surface disposal facility is planned to be located near one of the State’s treatment
installations. The disposal facility itself is a vault-type 2 entombment where wastes will be packaged into
primary containers, overpacked in secondaries, and emplaced in one of several vaults (see Figure 1 for
an example of this type of disposal facility for radioactive wastes in Spain). In this facility a temporary
building is moved over the vault being ﬁlled to utilize a heavy crane within and reduce exposure to the
elements prior to sealing 3. The vaults will eventually be grouted shut and backﬁlled with soil. The entire
vault structure will be capped with multi-layered, engineered barriers to prevent water incursions into
the vault area. The entire vault area will have a weeping system for any moisture that does accumulate
that will allow the State to monitor for the presence of radioactive materials post closure. Examples of
comparable facilities are shown in Figure 1.

a mass fraction too concentrated to terminate safeguards on the items. In addition, the State has larger
volumes of monolithic DU and thorium metals and alloys that were collected from industrial centers in
the State.

facility for the foreseeable future than to stand up and commission a large-scale operation to treat the
wastes into forms that would meet the current IAEA criteria for terminating safeguards. Ultimately, it is
recognized that it is the State’s right to dispose of the materials, but if the waste does not meet the

Notional Design
Two scenarios can be envisioned for the start of the application of safeguards measures to the facility: 1)
the facility is being newly constructed and Safeguards by Design (SBD) principles can be incorporated
early; 2) the facility is being converted to handle safeguarded nuclear materials and the measures will
need to be retroﬁtted into the facility. In both cases, the same general steps will be taken but there may
be complications in the second instance. For the purposes of this case study, we will assume that this is a
new build.

Most States have adopted Modiﬁed Code 3.1 in the Subsidiary Arrangements to their CSA, which
requires the early provision of design information as soon as an oﬃcial decision to acquire a facility has
been made. In addition, for States with an AP in force, Article 2.a.(x) requires the State to annually
declare general plans for the subsequent ten-year period. In either case, it would be beneﬁcial for the
State and the IAEA if conversations were started as early as possible in the design process.

This analysis will treat the safeguards at the treatment facility and the disposal facility separately. While
this analysis was originally only focused on the disposal facility, the safeguards at the disposal facility are
intimately tied to the safeguards at the treatment facility.

Treatment Facility
Description
The initial conversations regarding the facilities would need to set the framework for developing the
safeguards approach. The ﬁrst place that must be considered is the treatment facility. This facility will
treat both safeguarded wastes that will be placed in the safeguarded disposal facility as well as
radioactive waste packages that contain no nuclear material or nuclear material at concentrations
suitable for termination. The overview of the facility is shown in Figure 2.

The waste facility will house two diﬀerent waste lines: a thermal treatment facility to incinerate wastes
and concentrate the materials into the ash residue and a cementation line that will be able to immobilize
the waste materials directly (as macroencapsulated waste forms) and to immobilize the incinerator ash
residues (as microencapsulated waste forms) if needed. The incinerator will have potential holdup but
can be washed and cleaned as necessary with the washing liquids collected as a secondary waste. Not all
drums will be immobilized with grout; lower risk/activity materials may be compacted in drums from the
incinerator and packed as is. Finally, drums might be delivered to the facility that are pretreated and
conditioned. The facility has an on-site grout production capability for ﬁlling drums and a buﬀer store of
empty drums and empty overpack containers. The ﬁlling of primary and overpack containers takes place
in the treatment section of the facility.

Disposal Facility
Description
The disposal facility has two separate disposal vaults (see Figure 4). A larger vault was constructed for
non-safeguarded, low-level wastes. The vault consists of 21 concrete disposal vaults, each of which can
hold 108 overpack containers, two cubic meter containers (each holding six 400L drums or eighteen 200L
drums). The total volume of low-level waste that can be disposed of is ~8150 m3. 5 A smaller facility that
will remain under safeguards was constructed alongside the existing facility based on an analysis of the
State’s waste streams. The facility has nine disposal vaults and a total waste disposal volume of ~2300
m3. The safeguarded vaults will be packed more slowly but buﬀer capacity is built in to ensure that
adequate space is available. Based on an analysis of the waste streams, a total of 600-800 kg of 235U will
be disposed of over the facility’s 15-year design lifetime; however, based on loading rates, the facility
might be open for 20 or more years prior to closure. This results in an annual throughput (on a 15-year
basis) of less than 1 SQ per year (~0.6-0.7 SQ).

Nuclear Material Accounting and Control (NMAC)
The treatment and disposal facilities will each consist of a single Material Balance Area (MBA). The
treatment facility will have inventory key measurement points (KMP) at A) the receipt area, B) the drum
buﬀer store, and C) the overpack container store. The disposal facility will have inventory KMPs at A) the
receipt area, B) active emplacement vault(s) that are still accessible, and C) inaccessible closed vaults.

The treatment facilities could choose to 1) accept waste on shipper values and not remeasure or report
shipper/receiver diﬀerences (SRD), or 2) remeasure received waste and report SRD. Either way, the
facilities would generally not report material unaccounted for (MUF). They would simply re-batch
material into overpack containers using shipper values or the values from their measurements. Material
recovered from the process (e.g., washing out the incinerator) could be reported as an accidental gain if
packaged and disposed or used to oﬀset SRD. So long as these net gains were small, there would be no
need to further consider MUF.

General Safeguards Considerations - Construction Phase
The State will ideally declare their intent to construct the disposal facility and any associated treatment
facility early in their respective developmental phases. This is important as any safeguards measures that
will be required can be incorporated at the design phase and reﬂected in the Design Information
Questionnaire (DIQ) when it is submitted. Consultations with the State authority responsible for
safeguards (SRA) and the IAEA should be carried out to ensure there is agreement on the tentative
approaches to be implemented at the facility. DIQs will be required for both the treatment and disposal
facilities.

These initial conversations should identify the following key pieces of information:

 • The location of the treatment facility and any related buildings and structures.
 • The general ﬂow of nuclear material into and out of the facility and a general MBA structure, if
 known.
 • How the facility will segregate safeguarded vs. non-safeguarded wastes.
 • The material types and expected concentration ranges in various wastes streams.
 • Plans for material accountancy and control of nuclear materials.
 • The chemical, physical, and radiological characteristics of the wastes in the following states
 (waste management terms) that will inﬂuence what information is available at each stage of
 waste treatment:
 o Raw Wastes – wastes that have had no conditioning or packaging processes applied to it.
 o Pretreated – wastes that have had their chemical or radiological hazards mitigated or
 otherwise segregated or sorted.
 o Treated – wastes that have been volume reduced, decontaminated or changed in
 composition.
 o Immobilized – creation of a waste form by solidiﬁcation or encapsulation.
 o Overpacked – the placement of a waste form in an overpack container for transport,
 storage or disposal.
 • The last point of veriﬁcation of the nuclear material in the waste items past where current
 technology can no longer reliably verify the presence or absence of nuclear materials.
 • The ability to treat a waste such that it could meet the Agency’s criteria for terminating
 safeguards before shipment to a non-safeguarded disposal facility (or the non-safeguarded
 section of a safeguarded disposal facility).
 • The ability to incorporate SBD concepts into the design of waste containers and waste overpacks
 (e.g., providing sealable containers and overpacks) so that they can be appropriately sealed.
 • Containment and surveillance measures or satellite monitoring techniques that could provide
 assurance that containers, overpacks, and vaults remain intact prior to closure.

A similar, but smaller, set of questions must be asked for the disposal facility:

 • The location of the disposal facility.
 • The ﬂow of nuclear material into the facility.
 • Containment and surveillance measures or satellite monitoring techniques that could provide
 assurance that containers, overpacks, and vaults remain intact prior to closure.
 • General discussions on post closure safeguards measures to be employed.

Construction Challenges and Potential Solutions
The regulator and facility would need to submit a DIQ to the IAEA. The disposal facility would be able to
use the existing DIQ template for a storage facility (which is the closest to the operational reality of the
disposal facility). The treatment facility would likely use the template for an “Other” type facility.

The regulator will need to work closely with the operator and the IAEA to ﬁll out the DIQ for the waste
treatment and agree on a safeguards approach for both facilities. The disposal facility and the treatment
facility should identify as soon as possible if there is a need for surveillance cameras or other
instruments to monitor receipts in the treatment facility and emplacement in the disposal facility. If so,
provisions for their installation should be included in the facility design. Similarly, the design of
containment structures and sealing points is best decided early and included in the facility design.

IAEA inspectors would typically perform a Design Information Veriﬁcation (DIV) after completion of
construction but prior to the receipt of material at the treatment or disposal facilities. This would be to
conﬁrm the design and validity of the planned safeguards approach, especially those facets of the design
that might be relevant to containment measures. Given the lower proliferation value of DNLEU or Th at
waste concentrations, there would not normally be a need to conﬁrm design features via DIV during
construction to look for undeclared access points; intermittent satellite imagery analysis during
construction would likely be suﬃcient.

General Safeguards Considerations – Operational Phase
The IAEA safeguards system categorizes nuclear material into direct-use and indirect-use based on its
attractiveness for proliferation. Direct use materials, which consist of HEU and plutonium, can be directly
used to fabricate nuclear weapons and are therefore subject to more frequent and intense veriﬁcation
requirements than indirect-use materials (DNLEU) and thorium. This results in signiﬁcant diﬀerences
between the safeguards approaches for waste repositories handling primarily DNLEU and thorium verses
those for spent reactor fuel or separated plutonium and HEU.

In general, DNLEU and thorium require only annual veriﬁcation, and apart from large bulk processing
facilities 6 (conversion, enrichment, and fuel fabrication plants) this is typically accomplished using only
non-destructive analysis (NDA) techniques to look for gross and (sometimes) partial defects. Containers
of pure DNLEU or thorium are occasionally sealed, though this is usually an optional measure to reduce
the number of veriﬁcation measurements needed rather than a fundamental aspect of the safeguards
approach. Surveillance is almost never applied to DNLEU or thorium due to the diﬃculty of interpreting
what might constitute diversion. 7

As a result, most DNLEU shipped to the treatment facility would not generally arrive under any kind of
containment or surveillance. Therefore, it would be necessary to verify the material before it becomes

Treatment Facility - Potential Safeguards Approach
As alluded to previously, the safeguards approach for the treatment and disposal facilities would have to
balance eﬀort and cost against the low quantities and attractiveness of the DNLEU and thorium being
disposed.

For the treatment facility, an eﬃcient approach might be an electronic “mailbox” declaration coupled
with short notice access for veriﬁcation; the project recognizes that there are other approaches that
might be equally as valid and that this approach was selected for this study. This approach would work as
follows for the conditioning phase:

 • Waste arrives at the treatment facility unveriﬁed and is accepted on shipper values.
 • The operator conditions the waste items as needed to allow for storage in a buﬀer store.
 • The operator declares relevant data on each prepared drum to an IAEA electronic mailbox
 (e.g., the nuclear material content and other data like masses of safeguarded materials,
 immobilization matrix, drum tare weights, etc. and matrix chemistry). Once this data is sent to
 the IAEA mailbox electronically it cannot be altered. (The operator might make his or her own
 measurement of the drum content at this point to conﬁrm shipper numbers, or that
 determination might have been made at a previous step in the process.) Forwarding this
 declaration information would ideally be automated into the operator’s process control
 software.
 • The operator holds the prepared drums for a minimum period within a buﬀer store (e.g., > 5
 days) to give IAEA the opportunity to implement a short notice inspection.
 • IAEA implements a small number of short (e.g., 1 to 2 hour) notice inspections at which the set
 of prepared drums “in residence” in the buﬀer store are veriﬁed to ensure they match the
 operator’s mailbox declaration. In this way all drums are subject to potential veriﬁcation (even
 though few are actually veriﬁed).
 • The number of required short notice inspections would depend on the desired detection
 probability, the agreed residence time, and the accuracy of the NDA technique. For instance, if
 residence time is 5 days and the facility only works on weekdays, then each inspection would
 capture about 1/50th of annual throughput. Five short notice inspections per year would thus
 yield a 10% selection probability. If the measurement were designed to achieve a 50%

Treatment Facility - Operational Challenges and Potential Solutions
Several challenges are obvious when looking at the list of information above. The most pressing is that
the facility will handle both safeguarded and non-safeguarded wastes. The easiest solution for all parties
would be to place a physical separation between known safeguarded materials that are shipped to the
facility as well as for any accidental gain materials that are found in “non-safeguarded wastes” that
require the application of safeguards and non-safeguarded wastes. This would require input at the
design stage and may involve the installation of a surveillance system (though this is very expensive). An
alternative solution would be to process safeguarded wastes and non-safeguarded wastes in separate
campaigns. It is possible that non-safeguarded wastes will need to be commingled with safeguarded
wastes in order to balance not only the safeguarded material concentration in the waste but also a
multitude of chemical and physical parameters necessary to meet the disposal facility’s waste
acceptance criteria.

As stated above, for the case considered here the wastes become very diﬃcult to measure once they are
incorporated into an overpack container. In these cases, seals would be needed to maintain CoK on the
overpack containers. Therefore, some work must be done to ensure that the overpack containers have a
sealable conﬁguration. This could include sinking a cable run into the grout so that a continuous seal can
be applied or engineering eyebolts into the overpack container itself. This seal will maintain CoK on the
waste materials as there will be little to no radiological signature once the wastes are in their fully
grouted overpack container. The overpack containers would use electronic seals with RMSA so their
position could be tracked, and they could be attached and read without inspector presence. The facility
would need to adequately incorporate the RMSA infrastructure to accommodate data transmission. The
WAC for these wastes require the use of an overpack container due to the nature of the wastes in this
case (alpha activity, chemical form, etc.).

Disposal Facility - Potential Safeguards Approach
The overpack containers would be transferred to the disposal facility for emplacement. This would be
tracked using the RMSA seal (and potentially a second method if required to maintain dual CoK during
the transfer). The overpack waste containers would be transferred by crane into the vault and emplaced.
The operator would declare the ﬁnal location of the waste within the vault and send the declaration via
the mailbox. These operations could be monitored with some form of surveillance, such as a camera, if

 12

an active seal is not used on the overpack. Once the vault is ﬁlled, and with prior agreement between
the State and the IAEA, the waste could be administratively transferred to the category of retained
waste, which allows for customized and, generally, less intensive safeguards to be applied once the vault
is backﬁlled with grout. This would also remove the nuclear material from the active book inventory.

Disposal Facility - Operational Challenges and Potential Solutions
With respect to materials emplaced in the disposal vaults, the inspectorate would need to visually
conﬁrm that closed vaults remain closed and show no signs of deterioration. Open vaults being ﬁlled
would still be able to be interrogated via the RMSA systems.

When an individual vault is ﬁlled, under some sort of CoK, the entire vault is backﬁlled with grout making
a large monolithic form and removing the ability for the RMSA seals to communicate. The IAEA would
need to use intermittent satellite imagery from this point forward to ensure the site remains
undisturbed. The inspectorate could also verify the integrity of the backﬁlled and sealed vaults visually
during periodic DIV of the facility.

The use of retained waste in the manner described above would represent an underutilized, and
potentially useful, interpretation of the mechanism. Retained waste has been used as a way to store
materials temporarily until there was a decision to recover the materials or the materials were to be sent
to a disposal facility. The designation was determined based on economic factors and retained waste has
not been used in a disposal context where there is no intent for retrieval, eﬀectively making the
designation permanent. In practice it would allow both the State and the IAEA to negotiate mutually
agreeable safeguards measures to be applied to materials in the disposal facility.

General Safeguards Considerations - Closure Phase
The safeguards to be applied to a closed near-surface disposal facility have not been applied as of the
writing of this document. In this particular case, the attractiveness of the materials in the safeguarded
disposal facility would remain low. As such, less intense measures would seem appropriate to ensure
that the site remains intact and undisturbed. This section is a notional approach that could be applied
based on the best knowledge and judgment of the authors.

Opportunities may present themselves during the closure to incorporate additional measures into the
disposal facility or the engineered layers that are placed on top of the facility. Also, please note that a
closed disposal facility is still an operational facility and is operating as intended. Therefore, it should still
be included in the State’s set of active facilities under the CSA.

The treatment facility may or may not be closed at the same time as the disposal facility. The treatment
facility may continue to operate well beyond the lifetime of the disposal facility if another disposal
facility is created within the State. For completeness, the closure of both facilities is included here.

Closed Treatment Facility - Potential Safeguards Approach
Like any bulk handling facility, the treatment facility will undergo post operational cleanout and
decommissioning according to the general guidelines and principles set forth in “International
Safeguards Guidelines for the Post-Operational Phases of Nuclear Facilities and Locations Outside
Facilities” (STR-396). In summary, once the decision to cease operations and apply for a
decommissioning license has been made, provisions should be made to prepare for the removal of

 13

nuclear material and processes equipment from the treatment facility. Speciﬁcally, removal and
characterization of any bulk inventory or waste would need to be performed, decontamination and
dismantling of equipment that came into contact with nuclear materials and site wide surveys to
determine the radiological characterization of the site. Once all declared safeguarded materials and any
residual bulk materials have been removed the facility it can be recategorized by IAEA as “closed down
(in a state of decommissioning)”, with respect to safeguards and reporting on nuclear material can cease
and it is no longer subject to inspection. Once the facility can no longer process nuclear material in any
way, the facility can be declared as “Decommissioned for Safeguards Purposes” after which all remaining
safeguards measures cease, and it is no longer subject to DIV. In an AP State, however, CA rights persist
for the purpose of conﬁrming the facility remains decommissioned from a safeguards point of view.

Closed Treatment Facility – Decommissioning Challenges
The biggest uncertainty in this process is the determination that all the nuclear material has been
removed. This is similar to the challenges found at other bulk handling facilities, but the amount of
nuclear material that could be present would be drastically lower. Therefore, discussions between the
State and the IAEA should focus on how to treat and characterize these materials and when they can be
considered to be eﬀectively dispersed into the environment and can be treated as a measured discard
(an approach that is appropriate for very low-level contamination). In certain circumstances, the
materials may be eligible for termination as a measured discard when they are below the thresholds set
for overpacked (containerized) wastes and can be terminated. This process should ideally be started at
the point where the State makes the decision to decommission the treatment facility to allow for the
State and the IAEA to come to an agreement on process.

It should be noted that the facility cannot move to the “closed down (in a state of decommissioning)”
stage (where reporting nuclear material inventories and transactions cease and inspection rights end)
until all nuclear material has been recovered and dispositioned, since it is impossible to report further
nuclear material transactions (like termination or shipment) after a facility enters this lifecycle stage. As a
result, bulk handling facilities often continue in the “shutdown” lifecycle stage (still subject to reporting
and inspection) until almost all nuclear material handling equipment has been cleaned out and
dismantled. This is examined in detail in STR-396, “International Safeguards Guidelines for the Post-
Operational Phases of Nuclear Facilities and Locations Outside Facilities” [6].

Post-closure Disposal Facility - Potential Safeguards Approach
Unlike the treatment facility, safeguards on the disposal facility would continue in perpetuity and the
status of the facility would remain “Operational”. The most important veriﬁcation objective would be to
conﬁrm, via some combination of periodic inspection, DIV, and satellite imagery analysis, that there has
not been an obvious intrusion into the disposal facility. This could include a visual survey of mining or
mining like activities in the surrounding area. An eﬀective radius around the facility would have to be
established for satellite review. Operationally, most States would eﬀectively prohibit all mining near a
disposal facility due to the inherent safety risks of breaching the facility. Therefore, any signs of mining or
drilling would require additional veriﬁcation.

It would seem unlikely that the IAEA would deploy any advanced sensor technology around a DNLEU
repository to monitor for vibrations or drilling signatures unless the incremental cost of the system was
very low. The low strategic value of the indirect use materials in a closed DNLEU repository and the large

 14

amount of eﬀort and time needed for their recovery would argue for a low cost and less resource
intensive safeguards approach.

Post-closure Disposal facility - Operational challenges
Many challenges will remain with respect to breaching technologies that could potentially enter the
vault system and provide a recovery pathway. However, there will likely be one or more signatures
evolved from any such attempt. The challenge in the post-closure years will be to monitor these
technologies and evaluate their threat to the closed facility’s safeguards approach.

Intermediate Depth Disposal Facility Containing
Previously Terminated Wastes
The disposal of ILW/HLW typically requires isolation of the waste from the biosphere at depths
signiﬁcantly deeper than a surface disposal facility. The following deﬁnitions are excerpted from General
Safety Guide 1 (GSG-1) “Classiﬁcation of Radioactive Waste” [7]:

 Intermediate level waste (ILW): Waste that, because of its content, particularly of long lived
 radionuclides, requires a greater degree of containment and isolation than that provided by near
 surface disposal. However, ILW needs no provision, or only limited provision, for heat dissipation
 during its storage and disposal. ILW may contain long lived radionuclides, in particular, alpha
 emitting radionuclides that will not decay to a level of activity concentration acceptable for near
 surface disposal during the time for which institutional controls can be relied upon. Therefore,
 waste in this class requires disposal at greater depths, of the order of tens of metres to a few
 hundred metres.

 High level waste (HLW): Waste with levels of activity concentration high enough to generate
 signiﬁcant quantities of heat by the radioactive decay process or waste with large amounts of
 long lived radionuclides that need to be considered in the design of a disposal facility for such
 waste. Disposal in deep, stable geological formations usually several hundred metres or more
 below the surface is the generally recognized option for disposal of HLW.

In both situations, wastes may be required to be stored in these facilities due to their radiological
content. Waste forms that fall into these categories include highly irradiated reactor internals but may
also include waste forms that are created by the treatment of highly radioactive solutions, such as
reprocessing liquors or concentrated residues from ion exchange resin beds.

In the case of reprocessing liquors and other highly radioactive materials, residues of nuclear material
subject to safeguards will often be present. However, due to their high level of conditioning, these
nuclear materials will often meet IAEA requirements for termination of safeguards 10. [8] This allows such
wastes to be disposed of in a non-safeguarded facility.

States with an AP agree to provide enhanced information and access so that IAEA can provide assurance

update on an annual basis) a declaration containing:

This provision applies speciﬁcally to direct-use materials (HEU, 233U, and Pu) contained in wastes and
speciﬁcally ILW/HLW. As such, the applicability of this provision is limited to a handful of States with
more advanced fuel cycles that will generate ILW & HLW containing those nuclear materials or import
such waste as the byproduct of reprocessing their spent nuclear fuel in another State. Many States will

One complication is that, based on the guidance in GSG-1, it is possible to create waste forms that would
have the physical characteristics of low-level waste but be classiﬁed as intermediate-level waste due to
alpha contamination; for plutonium and 233U, macroencapsulated wastes at the termination level are,
respectively, roughly 100x and 10x the recommended low-level waste limit for long lived alpha emitters
in waste. Therefore, these wastes would be eligible for termination but would be considered ILW and
require the additional isolation from the biosphere that ILW necessitates.

This analysis only considers cases where the nuclear materials in ILW/HLW have been terminated and
where the State has determined it is more economical to dispose of the diluted nuclear material bearing
waste forms in a non-safeguarded site. Were the materials going to be disposed of in a safeguarded site,
there would be no beneﬁt to restricting the concentration of nuclear material beyond what is required to
make a stable waste form that would meet the disposal facility’s WAC. In those cases, the nuclear
materials would simply remain under safeguards.

Safeguards Considerations
States that create ILW or HLW with direct use materials upon which safeguards have been terminated
need to declare where the wastes are (location) and if the nuclear materials in them are processed for
recovery (further processing). It should be noted that simply repackaging the waste materials does not
qualify as further processing. Indeed, the wastes must be processed in a way that elements could be
separated to require reporting under the AP. For example, dissolution of the wastes would qualify as
further processing and need to be reported. In many situations, that would also require that the
materials be brought back under safeguards as they would become usable for a “nuclear activity relevant
from the point of view of safeguards” again.

The State would need to maintain a declaration regarding the location of the terminated ILW/HLW
containing direct use materials throughout the lifetime of the waste 11. Termination of safeguards on the
nuclear material would have been reported (following IAEA approval) in the facility that created the
waste. The wastes would likely be moved to an interim storage location (see Figure 6) for years or
decades while a repository is constructed. The State would report the initial move to the interim storage
location in its annual AP updates and then report “no changes” for the remainder of the period during
which the wastes remained static. Eventually, the wastes would be moved to a repository and emplaced
in a waste silo. A declaration would need to be made regarding the new location of the wastes (by May
15th each year, summarizing changes during the previous calendar year). After closure of the repository,
the State would only need to report “no changes” in its annual AP update each year.

At any point, the IAEA might ask to access the location where these declared wastes are stored, either
the interim store or ﬁnal repository, via CA with 24 hours of notice. During CA, the Agency could ask to
perform visual observation; item counting of nuclear material; non-destructive measurements and
sampling; utilization of radiation detection and measurement devices; examination of records relevant to
the quantities, origin and disposition of the material; or the collection of environmental samples.
However, the State would not be required to provide detailed nuclear material accountancy for nuclear
material in the waste, nor would the material be subject to what the CSA deﬁnes as inspection. The
explicit goal of such a CA would be “to assure the absence of undeclared nuclear material and activities,”
and speciﬁcally not in any way to “verify” nuclear material contained in the waste. Obviously, removal of
individual waste packages from a silo would be impractical, so several activities permissible during CA (in
particular, item counting, NDA, and sampling) might not be possible at the repository location. In
practice, these CAs would likely involve examination of records, visual observation of the site, the
general measurement of radiation levels and signatures, and perhaps environmental sampling with the
goals of detecting signatures inconsistent with the declared materials in the waste or indications of
removal of waste from the location. While the repository was being ﬁlled, individual waste packages not
yet emplaced could also be subject to item counting and/or NDA to spot check operational records
and/or conﬁrm expected radiation signatures.

Repositories holding only HLW and/or ILW containing direct-use materials on which safeguards have
been terminated are not expected to require any specially designed measures to meet AP requirements.
They would be expected only to maintain records relevant to the quantities, origin and disposition of
such materials (which would likely be an operational and regulatory requirement anyway) and to provide
CA on request.

Conclusions
This document presented two case studies on disposal of wastes containing nuclear material—one for
waste containing safeguarded nuclear material and one for waste on which safeguards had been
previously terminated—and then explored the safeguards challenges and potential solutions for each.
Signiﬁcant ﬁndings are highlighted below:

• While States have a right to dispose of waste containing safeguarded nuclear material, if the waste
 does not meet the IAEA’s criteria for terminating safeguards, then safeguards on the materials and
 facility (as agreed upon between the State and the Agency) must continue to be applied in
 perpetuity.

• In cases where waste must be conditioned prior to emplacement in a disposal facility, it is likely that
 the safeguards approach will need to be coordinated across both treatment and disposal facilities,
 even if geographically separated, to maintain CoK on material between conditioning and
 emplacement.

• For obvious reasons, early consultations between States, operators, and IAEA would allow for the
 greatest beneﬁts to be derived from Safeguards by Design. In particular, containment and
 surveillance measures should be integrated into the design as early as possible.

• DNLEU or thorium shipped to a treatment facility may not arrive under any kind of containment or
 surveillance. In these cases, it would be necessary to verify the material before it becomes
 inaccessible or is conditioned into an unmeasurable conﬁguration.

• For treatment and disposal facilities handling only indirect use materials, it is expected that the IAEA
 would adopt a safeguards approach where the level of eﬀort and cost was commensurate with the
 low strategic value of DNLEU or thorium in waste forms. For the treatment facility, an eﬃcient
 approach might consist of electronic “mailbox” declarations coupled with random, short notice
 access for veriﬁcation.

• Veriﬁcation of nuclear material will be impossible once drums are packed and grouted in overpack
 containers, meaning CoK on overpack containers must be maintained between production and
 emplacement. Ideally, overpack containers should be designed with a sealable conﬁguration. RMSA
 seals could be used to avoid the need for frequent inspector visits.

• It is possible that non-safeguarded and safeguarded wastes will need to be commingled in order to
 balance both safeguarded material concentration as well as a multitude of chemical and physical
 parameters necessary to meet the disposal facility’s waste acceptance criteria.

• Once a vault was sealed into a monolithic block and perhaps buried, the IAEA would likely use
 satellite imagery and periodic DIV to monitor its integrity over the lifetime of the facility.

• Safeguards on the disposal facility would continue in perpetuity and the status of the facility would
 remain “Operational”. The most important veriﬁcation objective post-closure would be to conﬁrm,
 via some combination of periodic DIV/inspection, CA, and satellite imagery analysis, that there has
 not been an obvious intrusion into the disposal facility.

 19

• Administratively transferring veriﬁed nuclear material in emplaced waste into the category of
 retained waste would increase ﬂexibility for the State and IAEA to negotiate a bespoke safeguards
 approach for the post-closure phase.

• AP States will need to maintain a declaration regarding the location of terminated ILW/HLW
 containing direct-use materials throughout the lifetime of the waste, as well as records relevant to
 the quantities, origin and disposition of such materials.

• Repositories holding only HLW and/or ILW containing direct-use materials on which safeguards have
 been terminated are not expected to require any specially designed measures to meet AP
 requirements.

 inventory” [153, para. 111]. This is calculated for a material balance area (MBA) over a material
 balance period (MBP) using the material balance equation, commonly written as:

            MUF = (PB + X – Y) - PE

 where the four material balance components are as follows: PB is the beginning physical
 inventory; X is the sum of increases to inventory; Y is the sum of decreases from inventory; and
 PE is the ending physical inventory [12].

• Nuclear Material - Any source material or special ﬁssionable material as deﬁned in Article XX of
 the Statute of the IAEA; see also para. 112 of the CSA [1] and Article 18.h of the AP [14].
• Overpack – A secondary (or additional) outer container for one or more waste packages, used
 for handling, transport, storage or disposal. Secondary Container – a container that is used to
 package treated radioactive wastes to meet disposal requirements [13].
• Pretreatment – Any or all of the operations prior to waste treatment, such as collection,
 segregation, chemical adjustment and decontamination [13].
• Raw Wastes – Waste unchanged from the form and quantity in which it was generated — waste
 that has not been processed [13].

 23

 • Shipper/receiver diﬀerence (SRD) - “[T]he diﬀerence between the quantity of nuclear material
 in a batch as stated by the shipping material balance area and as measured at the receiving
 material balance area” (para. 114 of the CSA [1]). The SRD can be positive or negative (a positive
 SRD is a ‘loss’ of material). For example, when the receiver declares the shipper value to be 100
 kg in a UO2 drum and the receiver declares a measurement of 95 kg, the SRD is 100 – 95 = 5 kg.
 [12]
 • Site – An area delimited by the State in the relevant design information for a facility, including a
 closed-down facility, and in the relevant information on a location outside facilities (LOF) where
 nuclear material is customarily used, including a closed-down LOF where nuclear material was
 customarily used (this is limited to locations with hot cells or where activities related to
 conversion, enrichment, fuel fabrication or reprocessing were carried out). It also includes all
 installations collocated with the facility or location for the provision or use of essential services,
 including hot cells for processing irradiated material not containing nuclear material;
 installations for the treatment, storage and disposal of waste; and buildings associated with
 speciﬁed activities identiﬁed by the State under Article 2.a(iv) of its additional protocol (AP) [11].
 • Treatment – Operations intended to beneﬁt safety and/or economy by changing the
 characteristics of the waste. Three basic treatment objectives are: volume reduction, removal of
 radionuclides from the waste and change of composition. Treatment may result in an
 appropriate waste form.
 • Vault - An above ground or below ground reinforced concrete structure containing an array of
 storage cavities, each of which could hold one or more spent fuel packages or waste packages.
 [13]
