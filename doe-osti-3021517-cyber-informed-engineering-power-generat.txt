Executive Summary and Recommendations
The electric power grid is often referred to as the “world’s largest machine”1 because it operates synchronously
across a vast geographical area. Power generation is critical to this overall infrastructure, and failures in the
generators can result in cascading outages or blackouts. With the anticipated and forecasted increase in load
(such as large data centers as well as general load growth), U.S. generation capacity is expected to increase
significantly to ensure energy abundance.2

Cyber-informed engineering (CIE) offers an opportunity to address cyber-induced impacts for power generation
across the system’s life cycle. This guide explores several generation technologies, including those that
contribute a large percentage to the overall energy mix—such as natural gas, nuclear, wind, and hydropower. It
also examines several other generation technologies that currently have a smaller contribution but whose role
is expected to grow—such as solar, hydrogen, bioenergy, and geothermal.

A common theme that resonates across all these diverse generation technologies is the fact that cyber-induced
impacts must be considered as part of the safety and hazard analysis of system design. This is the core principle
of cyber-informed engineering, which is consequence-focused design. The system design can enumerate the
consequences of a cyber-enabled sabotage or disturbance and create mitigations for those that are unacceptable.
In addition, the enumeration of cyber-induced impacts must be a process that involves both the domain engineers
and cybersecurity practitioners. Cyber-informed engineering aims to bring together operational technology (OT) and
information technology (IT) professionals, engineers, and operations personnel to improve the overall security of
power generation infrastructure.

The CIE Implementation Guide offers a comprehensive series of questions for the 12 CIE principles, which prompt
the system designers to consider the consequences of failures as well as mitigations across the entire project
life cycle. Although the Implementation Guide is applicable for any engineering system, this guide aims to provide
additional context and specificity to the CIE philosophy by responding to the questions in the Implementation
Guide. These responses are provided by subject matter experts in both engineering technology and cybersecurity
and are intended to illustrate the considerations of actual projects. The authors recommend engineers and
security professionals consider the potential impacts of digital components as early as possible in the design
process, regardless of whether the project involves upgrading existing infrastructure or developing a new system.
In addition, this document provides the user with concrete examples of several CIE-based mitigations intended to
help the user define their own CIE-based mitigations for their systems.

 INTRODUCTION TO CYBER-INFORMED ENGINEERING
 Cyber-informed engineering (CIE) is a strategic initiative championed by the U.S. Department of Energy’s Office for
 Cybersecurity, Energy Security, and Emergency Response (CESER) to integrate cybersecurity considerations into
 engineering practices for critical infrastructure. The adoption of CIE principles combines traditional cybersecurity
 mitigations with engineering protections focused on safety, reliability, and performance appropriate for critical
 infrastructure. Traditional cybersecurity mechanisms often depend on post-design measures such as firewalls,
 intrusion detection systems, and authentication mechanisms—all of which are designed for data protection.3
 Cyber-informed engineering, by contrast, integrates cybersecurity directly into the engineering design process,

Introduction removing or mitigating cyber-induced consequences and resulting in a more effective and efficient application
 of cybersecurity controls. This method enhances confidence and trust in security and reliability. The 12 CIE
 principles, detailed in the National Cyber-Informed Engineering Strategy Cyber-Informed Engineering Implementation
 Guide, are applicable throughout the life cycle of critical infrastructure.

KEY POINTS TO CONSIDER WHEN USING THE GUIDE
The following points should be considered when using this guide:

Focus on Grid-Scale Deployments
This guide primarily addresses cybersecurity challenges for utility-scale
deployments, such as large-scale solar and wind installations, and does not
cover distributed energy resources (DERs) such as behind-the-meter solar or
small-scale wind systems.

Applying CIE Principles
The guide emphasizes the application of CIE principles from the earliest
stages of technology development. Principle 1 (Consequence-Focused Design)
questions are included for all eight generation technologies to provide a
common foundation. Each technology category then addresses selected
questions from additional CIE principles. All CIE questions are referenced
using the alphanumeric marker within the CIE Implementation Guide; for
instance, Bullet 3 of Phase A of Principle 2 is referenced as 2.A.3.

Modular Design
The document divides discussion into sections that each act as their own
stand-alone orientation to CIE for a particular generation technology. The
reader is free to engage with as much or as little as suits their needs; this
structure avoids belaboring points not within a reader’s scope of interest.

Complementary Resources
This guide complements the broader set of CIE resources available in the
CIE Resource Library.6 For detailed guidance on specialized topics, such as
microgrid or substation security, users are encouraged to refer to additional
documents and tools.

 Natural gas is the single largest source of energy used to generate electricity in the United States, making
 up 43% of electricity generation in 2023. Natural-gas-fired power plants accounted for the second-most U.S.
 generating capacity additions in 2023. The versatility of natural-gas-fired power plants in meeting a wide
 range of requirements—combined with increasing domestic supply and relatively low natural gas prices—is
 one of the main reasons for their growth. These facts increase the importance of the interrelationship of the
 electric and gas systems in the United States.7

 TECHNOLOGY OVERVIEW
Natural Gas Gas-fired generation, like other generation technologies, has many dependencies; however, a unique characteristic is the essen-
 tial component or element—the gas-fired generation set (genset)—resides at the physical intersection of two fully mature energy
 sectors: natural gas and electricity. Considering that natural gas fuel is typically provided without any/significant dedicated on-site
 reserve gas volume storage, electricity generation inherits a direct dependence on operation (with physical limits and business driv-
 ers) of natural gas delivery infrastructure. Gas-fired generation capabilities depend on gas supply capacity and capability (pipeline
 and/or liquified natural gas [LNG] constraints), gas delivery competitive markets/economics (e.g., firm, interruptible supply/transport
 contracts), and/or load distribution company (LDC) demand (which can influence “generation gas” availability). Determining a gas
 delivery threshold with local, regional, and/or greater security consequences requires an approach that also considers the combined
 effects of the pipeline constraints, LDC demand, market competition/contracting, and weather conditions throughout the year.
 The flexibility of natural-gas-fired generation is supported by the four technology types used in these plants: combined cycle gas
 turbine (CCGT), simple-cycle gas turbine (SCGT), steam turbine (ST), and internal combustion engine (ICE).8 A CCGT uses both
 gas and steam to generate electricity. The gas turbine compresses air and mixes it with natural gas that is heated to a very high
 temperature. The hot air-fuel mixture moves through the gas turbine blades, making them spin, which drives a generator to generate
 electricity. A heat recovery steam generator (HRSG) captures the exhaust heat from the gas turbine and uses it to generate steam
 for the steam turbine. This steam drives another electric generator, generating more electricity and improving the overall plant
 efficiency.9 Electricity from a combined cycle can be on the order of one-third the cost of electricity from a simple cycle. Simple
 cycle efficiencies are in the range of 35% to 40%, whereas combined cycle efficiencies are in the range of 55% to 61%. For every
 megawatt generated, a combined cycle burns about 35% less fuel than a simple cycle.10

 Natural gas generation is a mature technology, with many engineering controls commonly built into plants. These controls include
 pressure relief valves, fail close/fail open valves, visual and thermal fire detection equipment, gas detectors, and vibration
 monitoring. Although these engineering controls were developed as part of rigorous safety engineering to compensate for the
 inherent combustibility of natural gas and, in many cases, predate digital control systems, they are nonetheless consistent with CIE
 principles and do much to address issues of safety and equipment damage that might impact systems under cyberattack. In other
 words, CIE would recommend them, were they not already common practice for natural gas generation plant design. For this reason,
 the natural gas sections of this report focus primarily on consequences arising from loss of generation.

 The nuclear industry has experienced several cyber incidents in recent years (mostly outside the United
 States), highlighting the growing threat of cyberattacks on critical infrastructure. Although the industry has
 made significant strides in improving cybersecurity, the increasing sophistication of cyber threats poses
 ongoing challenges. These incidents have ranged from unauthorized access to plant systems to attempts to
 disrupt plant operations. Although most of these incidents have not resulted in significant physical harm, they
 have underscored the need for continued vigilance and investment in cybersecurity measures to protect the
 nation’s nuclear infrastructure.

Nuclear TECHNOLOGY OVERVIEW
 Cybersecurity is a critical component of nuclear power plant operations, which are already characterized by
 stringent safety cultures and security controls. The U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) plays a pivotal role
 in overseeing nuclear cybersecurity, establishing regulations and guidelines to protect facilities from cyber threats.
 These regulations cover risk assessment, incident response, and information sharing—NRC works closely with
 other federal agencies to coordinate efforts to safeguard the nation’s nuclear infrastructure.

 The robust safety culture within the nuclear industry is a key factor in mitigating cyber risks. This culture
 emphasizes a proactive approach to safety, continuous improvement, and a commitment to learning from past
 incidents. Furthermore, the nuclear industry has a long history of engineering out risks; this approach involves
 designing and implementing systems and processes that are inherently safe and resilient.

The NRC has identified high-consequence events detailed in 10 Code of Federal Regulations (CFR) 70.61,11 with which all licensed nuclear
power plants must comply to safeguard plant and public health and safety. In general, high-consequence events are covered by design-
basis accidents built into nuclear design and regulation for both traditional and SMR designs.

 Additional question: How are identified high-consequence events documented, monitored for change,
 and reassessed?

High-consequence events are required to be documented within the final safety analysis report and probabilistic risk assessment (PRA)
models in accordance with 10 CFR 52.47.12 Changes to the facility or procedures must be documented according to 10 CFR 50.59,13
including a brief report of any changes, tests, and experiments.

 Additional question: What are the critical processes, operations, and/or administrative actions
 required to protect against unacceptable impacts and ensure the execution of critical facility functions?

Various critical processes, operations, and administrative controls are required to protect against unacceptable impacts with unique critical
processes inherent to SMRs. The following list, although not extensive, describes a few measures used to protect critical facility functions:
 • Passive safety design features: Almost all new SMR designs include passive safety systems, the main decay heat removal mechanism
 through some combination of natural convection, radiation, thermal expansion, or capillary effects.14
 • Redundancy and diversity: Critical systems in SMRs are designed with multiple copies of the same system and different methods to
 achieve the same safety function to ensure if one system fails, others can perform the same function.
 • Environmental monitoring: Monitoring the environment around the SMR site ensures any release of radioactive materials is quickly
 detected.
 • Maintenance and testing: Preventive maintenance and regular testing of equipment ensure all safety systems are operational. Some
 SMR designs incorporate predictive maintenance to raise alerts to operators before line blockages or failure events to allow for proactive
 maintenance to reduce downtime.15
 • Cybersecurity procedures and equipment: These procedures protect plant safety, security, and emergency preparedness functions from
 cyberattacks. With the introduction of digital instrumentation and controls into SMR design, data diodes and firewalls are introduced to prevent
 connections to non-plant or external networks.
 • Training and qualification programs: Training ensures operators and plant personnel have adequate knowledge and skills to maintain plant
 safety and the ability to respond to emergencies.
 • Incident reporting and analysis: Systems for reporting, investigating, and analyzing safety and security incidents identify potential
 vulnerabilities and improve safety or security measures.

 1.C.4: What are the critical components and subcomponents in the system design? What are the
 consequences of failure or misuse of each critical component?

Given the variety of SMR designs and subsystems, each reactor type is different from other designs. The nuclear industry has been
identifying critical functions and assets for generations, with similar systems being considered critical between SMRs and older
generation reactor types. In general, the main systems which support critical facility functions can be categorized into include the
following:
 • Reactor core: The central portion of a reactor where fission takes place, containing fuel assemblies, a moderator, neutron poisons,
 control rods, and support structures.16 SMR cores can use advanced fuels and materials.
 • Coolant system: The system used to remove energy from the reactor core and transfer energy directly or indirectly to the steam
 turbine.17 In many SMRs, the coolant system is designed to operate under natural circulation.
 • Steam generator (optional): Heat exchanger used in some reactor designs to transfer heat from the primary (reactor coolant) system
 to the secondary (steam) system.18 Some SMR designs, which require little to no contamination of the secondary system, use steam
 generators for heat transfer.
 • Turbine and generator: Rotary engines with a series of curved vanes on a rotating shaft converting mechanical energy from water or
 steam to electrical energy.19
 • Condenser: Large heat exchanger designed to cool exhaust steam from the turbine below boiling point to return to the heat source
 such as steam generator or reactor core. The heat removed is transferred to a circulating water system and exhausted to the
 environment through a heat sink.20
 • Emergency power system (optional): The system that supplies the plant with the necessary power in an accident the facility is designed
 and built to withstand without the loss of systems, structures, and components (SSCs) necessary to ensure public health and safety,
 known as design-basis accidents, so the plant can be maintained in a safe state—particularly after a loss of off-site power.21 Some
 SMR types do not require emergency power systems to mitigate design-basis events. Many SMR safety-related SSCs do not require
 power to perform safety-related functions following a design-basis event.
 • Emergency core cooling system: The system is designed to remove residual heat from the reactor fuel rods in the event of a failure of
 the reactor coolant system.22 Some SMRs conduct heat decay removal for a limited time without the use of active components in the
 emergency core cooling system.
 • Instrumentation and controls: The system provides operators with critical safety information on plant operation and allows operators
 to control plant safety systems during normal operations. During potential accident events, instrumentation and control systems
 automatically protect the reactor core.23 Many SMR designs use digital instrumentation and controls, allowing for combined safety
 functions and improved control and efficiency.
 • Radiation monitoring systems: Systems that detect and measure radiation levels within the plant and surrounding environment to
 ensure levels stay within safe limits.
 • Security systems: Systems that protect against unauthorized access and other security threats, including physical and cybersecurity
 threats.

PRINCIPLE 4: DESIGN SIMPLIFICATION

 4.C.2: What simplification techniques and procedures can the owner/operator adopt to simplify design?
To achieve simplification, the owner/operator can reduce system functionality and structural complexity. A major push is to prioritize
passive safety systems over complex active safety systems within SMRs. Passive systems rely on natural processes (gravity, convection)
and require less maintenance and fewer safety-related instrumentation and controls, removing potential attack pathways. Examples
include the following:
 • Natural circulation for core cooling instead of pumps (inherently simpler and more reliable).
 • Gravity-driven safety systems for reactor shutdown (avoids complex control systems and reduces reliance on power).

PRINCIPLE 5: LAYERED DEFENSES
 5.C.1: What are the key system components where layered defenses can be implemented?

Current Generation III facilities already apply layered defense in the form of defense-in-depth operation. SMRs should continue to
implement layered defenses for safety-related, important-to-safety, and security functions and to support equipment that facilitates these
functions. A major component of these systems is to use redundant and diverse systems to safeguard the barriers that prevent the
release of radioactivity. These systems are identified as systems that perform critical functions in critical systems, known as critical digital
assets within SMR designs. Regulation 10 CFR 73.5426 mandates the identification and adequate protection of critical digital assets.
Although this regulation focuses on critical digital assets, a layered defense approach—or a graded approach—should be applied to all
digital devices based on their relative importance to safety and security.

Important-to-safety systems include non-safety-related systems that can impact safety if they fail. For example, the component cooling
system provides cooling water to safety systems. Layered defenses might involve redundancy in pumps, heat exchangers, and alarms to
alert operators of potential issues.

Supporting equipment is equipment that directly supports safety-related or important-to-safety systems, for example, power supplies for
safety systems. Layered defenses could involve redundant and diverse power sources (e.g., emergency diesel generators), uninterruptible
power supplies, and automatic load shedding to manage power demands.

Critical digital assets, as defined by 10 CFR 73.54 and proposed 10 CFR 73.110 (updated version)27 require specific protection measures.
Layered defenses could involve secure coding practices, network segmentation (including boundary device requirements and one-way
deterministic device implementation), intrusion detection/prevention systems, diversity and redundancy of critical digital assets, and
backups for critical data.

 5.C.3: Are layered defenses correlated to the reliability of the system and/or consequence magnitude?
NRC RG 5.7128 dictates defensive levels correspond to more controls required in a layer, where the higher the level, the higher the
consequence and required defensive elements, as shown in Figure 3. In SMRs, with the potential for remote operations and modularity,
defensive levels might not correspond to physical security areas because the cables/cloud service running between facilities might not
require immense physical security. However, the service will require proactive cybersecurity to maintain operability for a remotely operated
or autonomous site, such as firewalls between boundary points with “deny-all, permit-by-exception” rules and intrusion detection systems.

 1.A.4: What are the consequences that could result from a failure of unexpected operation of the
 system’s critical functions? What impacts could there be to mission delivery, safety, security, the
 environment, equipment and property, financials, or corporate reputation?

In terms of mission delivery, sudden equipment failures or cybersecurity breaches can lead to significant disruptions in power generation,
potentially causing grid instability and compromising the solar or wind farm’s ability to meet energy production commitments. These
consequences may be further exacerbated by reduced operational awareness because of fragmented ownership and operational
responsibilities, leading to longer restoration times. In addition, cyber vectors can also result in some of these consequences—such as
spoofed metrological data that result in plant shutdown when the weather is not adverse. Compromised monitoring systems could also
result in physical damage to the assets, such as a tower strike on a wind turbine. Corporate reputation can suffer because of repeated
operational issues, environmental concerns, or failure to address community grievances such as noise complaints or visual impacts,
potentially straining relationships with local residents and stakeholders.

 1.A.5: What business areas may be uniquely impacted by a system failure or unexpected operation?
 What consequences (physical or otherwise) are “unacceptable” and must be mitigated?

Energy production—the core business function—faces significant risks from equipment malfunctions or extreme weather events,
potentially leading to substantial revenue losses and grid instability.32 The operations and maintenance segment is particularly vulnerable,
because unexpected failures can necessitate emergency repairs, disrupt maintenance schedules, and pose safety hazards to personnel.
Financial performance is directly tied to consistent energy output, making unexpected downtime or reduced efficiency a major concern.
Any unexpected operation or failure that leads to a compromise in energy production is unacceptable and must be mitigated.

Digital components underpin critical operations such as pitch and yaw controls (wind plants), maximum power point tracking (solar plants),
power conversion from DC to AC, interconnection to grid, and other safety/protection systems. Although these digital technologies help
ensure reliable operation and safety, they also present avenues for malicious actors to cause unacceptable consequences, such as

 1.C.4: What are the critical components and subcomponents in the system design? What are the
 consequences of failure or misuse of each critical component?

In wind and solar farm installations, several digital features pose significant risks if manipulated by adversaries, particularly control
systems such as supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA) systems and inverters. SCADA systems oversee the overall operation
of these farms, managing turbine speed, panel orientation, and power output. If these systems are compromised, a cyber attacker could
potentially shut down entire farms, cause equipment damage, or manipulate power output—leading to grid instability. Similarly, inverters
convert DC power from solar panels or wind turbines to AC power for the grid, and malicious control of these devices could result in power
quality issues or even widespread blackouts if coordinated across multiple installations.34 35

Remote access systems used for monitoring and maintenance also present vulnerabilities; a breach could allow unauthorized control over
farm operations, resulting in equipment damage or safety hazards. In addition, communication networks—both internal and external—
are critical. Compromising these networks could enable attackers to inject false data, disrupting operations or causing inappropriate
responses to grid conditions. Weather monitoring and forecasting systems, although not directly controlling operations, could lead to high-
consequence events if manipulated, because false weather data could prompt incorrect operational decisions, risking equipment damage
or unnecessary shutdowns that incur financial losses.

These consequences can also be exacerbated because of a lack of awareness of the reliability of the digital components and the full
scope of their capabilities. For example, the inverters connecting the wind/solar assets to the grid might offer capabilities such as
voltage/volt ampere reactive (Volt/VAR) support, but if the operator is unaware of these functionalities, or if these functions are not part
of the standard operation procedure, a malicious actor might use this function to impact grid operation.

 2.B.3: Are analog or physical protections engineered into the system (where possible) for each high-
 consequence event identified?

Various engineering mitigations are engineered into the system to protect wind and solar plants and ensure graceful shutdown in case
of high-consequence events. For example, individual wind turbines have their independent safety chains to avoid catastrophic damage to
the physical equipment. Similarly, individual strings of PV arrays are protected against digital manipulation and have local controllers for
functions such as maximum power point tracking. The individual solar and wind units are also capable of graceful (or safe) shutdown to
ensure the individual units do not go into catastrophic failure conditions if the central, plantwide controller were to fail. Wind/solar plants
usually have on-site dispatchable backup generation (such as a diesel generator) for emergencies. It is important to note these systems
are effective only with regular testing and measures against digital compromise.

At the plant level, there are electrical protection requirements to guard against conditions such as overvoltage, under- and overfrequency,
and overcurrent. These protections are enforced by protection relays, which are not connected to any networks and operate based solely
on local measurements. These relays offer a method of protection against the reduced operational awareness highlighted in the digital
challenge, because they operate automatically without operator support and mitigate against worst-case consequences. For example, the
wind and solar plants disconnect from the main electric grid if it senses a failure condition such as over- or underfrequency, protecting
a grid cascading failure. There are also measures such as special protection schemes or remedial action schemes that enable the grid
operators to disconnect these resources when a failure is detected. This helps avoid physical damage to these assets—a more damaging
condition than an outage that might last a few hours. Engineered controls can also be deployed in a more modular fashion: If only a single
turbine is compromised in a wind farm, it might be feasible to disconnect only one turbine and still ensure operations in a degraded state.
Wind and solar farms also incorporate restoration processes in their standard operation procedure, enabling asset owner/operators to
dispatch restoration crews to minimize impact on operational reliability. Additional examples of specific engineered controls are discussed
in the CIE for Power Generation Use Cases section.

Because wind and solar assets are often located in remote geographical areas, support from other regional systems and infrastructure
often plays a critical role in their operations. For example, the deployment of these assets is often managed through a variety of

Key wind/solar farm dependencies include the following:
 • Electrical grid infrastructure: Wind and solar farms depend on the regional power transmission and distribution network to deliver
 generated electricity to consumers.
 • Transportation infrastructure: Roads and transportation systems are important for construction, maintenance, and equipment delivery
 to these remote locations.
 • Telecommunications networks: Reliable communication systems are essential for remote monitoring, control, and data transmission.
 Depending on the location of the wind and solar plants, the choice of telecommunication network depends on specific operations and

 • Weather monitoring systems: Accurate weather forecasting is critical to predict wind patterns/solar irradiance and optimize energy

unaware of the credentials for engineering access to this equipment during emergencies. These considerations should be accounted for
during the design process and suitable mitigation measures be incorporated into the design.

 Hydropower systems come in several forms. Reservoir hydropower is the most common and involves water
 stored behind a dam and then released through turbines to produce consistent electricity. Run-of-river systems
 do not require large dams and divert river flow to generate power with minimal environmental impact. Pumped

 TECHNOLOGY OVERVIEW
Hydropower Hydropower has been a significant contributor to electricity generation in the United States since the invention
 of the Francis turbine and the operation of the first hydropower plant in 1882.37 Hydropower’s share of total
 generation has decreased over time as its capacity has remained relatively stagnant and other technologies have
 deployed. However, it remains crucial as a dispatchable energy resource: Its massive generation units provide
 system stability, absorbing disturbances and fluctuations in load.

 The regulatory landscape for hydropower cybersecurity is complex, spanning multiple sectors such as agriculture,
 fisheries, and energy. Although agencies such as the North American Electric Reliability Corporation (NERC)
 oversee aspects of hydropower cybersecurity, no single entity is responsible for managing the risks associated
 with the sector. Given the unique challenges hydropower presents, this guide focuses on the energy-water nexus—
 particularly the design and cybersecurity mitigations for hydropower dams connected to the electric grid, where
 failures could have far-reaching consequences for energy generation and public safety.

 The increasing number of cyberattacks on critical infrastructure has drawn attention to the cybersecurity risks
 facing hydropower dams.38 Many dams, especially those built before 1980, still rely on legacy control and
 monitoring devices that were not designed with modern cybersecurity threats in mind. These older systems lack
 robust security features and are often located in remote areas, making them attractive targets for malicious
 actors. In addition, the potential use of Internet of Things (IoT) devices in hydro plants for monitoring introduces
 new vulnerabilities, because these devices—while enhancing operational efficiency—create numerous potential
 entry points for cyberattacks.

Hydroelectric power generation failure can lead to power outages and catastrophic events such as blackouts if the generator is particularly

integrity of the dam or spillway is compromised, potentially causing catastrophic flooding or overtopping. Equipment failures, such as the
malfunction of wicket gates, could trigger further breakdowns, whereas generator failures resulting from electrical faults, overspeed, or
other conditions could destroy the generators. Furthermore, electrical hazards pose risks of fires or electrocution.

Security risks include cyberattacks on control systems that could compromise operational integrity. Sabotage by insiders or external
actors could lead to intentional infrastructure damage. Unauthorized physical access to the facilities could result in tampering or theft,
further threatening operational security.

1.C.4: What are the critical components and subcomponents in the system design? What are the
consequences of failure or misuse of each critical component?

In a hydroelectric power plant, several systems perform and support critical facility functions. A reference architecture for a hydroelectric
power plant is shown in Figure 6, and the reader is directed to the complete report for further details. Key systems include 1) power
generation system (turbine, generator, exciter), 2) control systems (supervisory control and data acquisition [SCADA], plant and unit
control systems), 3) water management systems (water intake, penstock, spillway, valves, gates, and reservoirs), 4) electrical systems
(transformers, switchgear, circuit breakers), and 5) security systems (such as electrical protection, hydraulic protection, cybersecurity,
environmental safety, and physical security systems). In all these systems, the critical components usually rely on sensing and actuation
devices to avoid catastrophic failures. In a hydropower installation, the system design also includes fail-safes to ensure graceful failure in
the event of an actuator compromise. The following describes the consequences of sensor tampering and the consequences of misuse:
 • Water flow sensor: Flow meters measure the flow rate of water through the penstock or intake channels, and turbine inlet flow sensors
 monitor the water flow entering the turbine to ensure optimal performance. Misuse of the water flow sensors could result in damage to
 the turbine and associated components such as the generator.
 • Water level sensors: Reservoir level sensors measure the water level to monitor storage capacity, and tailrace level sensors monitor
 the water level in the tailrace (the area where water exits the turbine) to ensure proper downstream flow. Compromise of the water
 level sensor could increase the possibility of a flooding event or compromise the dam’s role in water management during droughts.
 • Pressure sensor: Pressure transducers monitor the pressure in the penstock, turbine casing, and other hydraulics components to
 ensure safe operating conditions. Compromised pressure sensors could result in damage to the water management mechanisms.

 • Temperature sensors: Bearing and generator temperature sensors monitor the temperature to prevent overheating. Compromised
 temperature sensors could cause the generator to become inefficient and, if the situation persists, result in higher-consequence

 • Vibration sensors: Accelerometers measure the vibration levels of turbines, generators, and other rotating equipment to detect

 • Voltage, Current, and Power Meters: These meters measure the voltage output of the generator and current flowing through the
 generator and transmission lines. Power meters measure the total output of the generator. Compromise of these meters could
 result in inefficiencies in power generation and destabilize generation control.

2.B.4: Are analog or physical protections engineered into the system (where possible) for each high-
consequence event identified? Are there opportunities to leverage non-digital controls to reduce risk/
dependencies for key functions of the system and subsystem?
There are several analog controls for high-consequence events. For example, for turbine overspeed protection, the digital control is in the
form of digital governors and sensors that regulate turbine speed. They are backed by analog controls such as mechanical overspeed trip
devices that can be installed to physically shut down the turbine if it exceeds safe speeds, independent of digital controls. For another
example, in water level management, the digital control is in the form of automated gates and valves controlled by SCADA systems to
manage water levels. These controls are backed up by analog controls in the form of float-operated gates, and manual valve controls allow
operators to manage water flow and reservoir levels manually.

Fail-safe mechanisms are also essential for ensuring plant safety. Mechanical interlocks are one such measure, designed to physically
prevent unsafe conditions—such as starting a turbine or opening a valve without meeting the required conditions. These interlocks do not
rely on digital signals, ensuring key safety conditions are always met. Hydraulic and pneumatic actuators offer another layer of protection
because they can be manually operated or controlled through simple analog circuits.

Additional question: What consequences of failure or misoperation are the engineered controls designed to prevent?

Engineered controls are used extensively in hydropower installations to avoid several high-consequence events. The use of engineered
controls is also driven by mechanical and pneumatic safety systems that have historically been used in these plants. An example of an
engineered control to an unacceptable event of turbine operation in a “rough zone” is discussed next.

 Consequence: When a turbine operates in the “rough zone,” it is functioning within a range of conditions that can lead to
 mechanical instability and increased vibrations. This rough zone typically occurs at partial load or when the turbine is not
 operating at its optimal efficiency. It happens when the flow of water through the turbine becomes uneven, creating turbulence,
 cavitation, and pressure fluctuations—which can cause the turbine to vibrate excessively.
 These vibrations, if sustained, can lead to mechanical wear and tear, damage to components such as bearings and seals,
 or even fatigue in the turbine blades. Operating in the rough zone is generally undesirable because it reduces the turbine’s
 lifespan, increases maintenance costs, and can lower the overall efficiency and reliability of the power plant.

 Engineered Control: Turbine governors and speed protection devices automatically adjust water flow and shut down the turbine
 if necessary. In addition, operators have manual controls through which they try to avoid this zone by adjusting the turbine’s
 load, flow, or operational settings to bring it back into a more stable and efficient operating range. For plants with multiple
 units, operators generally leverage the presence of multiple units to have maximum flexibility of net plant power output while
 avoiding rough zones for any individual unit.

4.C.1: Is each of the digital design elements traceable to a specific project requirement or critical
operation/process?

Consider a hydroelectric system that must generate 10 megawatts (MW) of electricity reliably at any time of the year. For the design
element (the turbine), the traceability steps are as follows:
 • Requirement Documentation: Document the requirement for generating 10 MW in the project requirements specification.
 • Design Documentation: Document the specifications of the turbine control and other digital components, such as its communication
 protocols, data processing logic, and control algorithms, in the system’s design documentation.
 • Requirement Trace Management (RTM) Creation: Add an entry in the RTM linking the requirement to the specifications enumerated in
 the design documentation.
 • Verification and Validation: Develop test cases to verify the turbine can generate 10 MW under specified conditions.
 • Configuration Management: Use version control to manage changes to the requirement or turbine specification, ensuring traceability is
 maintained. This includes tracking changes related to security patches and updates.

Additional question: Are there alternative, nondigital means to a digital feature to satisfy a requirement?
In some manual control and operational setups, reliance on analog systems offers a nondigital alternative for monitoring and control:
 • Analog gauges and indicators monitor critical system status parameters such as pressure, temperature, and flow rates. Rather than
 relying on digital displays, traditional analog devices—such as float-based gauges—can provide a direct reading of water levels in a
 dam.
 • Hydromechanical relays provide a nondigital method for protecting electrical circuits. These relays can physically disconnect circuits
 when overcurrent conditions occur, much like electromechanical relays that trip to prevent overloads.
 • Emergency signaling and communication, such as radios, allow operators to convey important operational statuses or alerts.
 • Redundant systems and fail-safes: Nondigital alternatives provide safety and reliability without reliance on electrical power.
 • Gravity-driven mechanisms are used in critical safety operations, such as emergency spillway gates in dams that automatically open
 when the water level rises to a certain point.
 • Spring-loaded devices offer another layer of mechanical fail-safes, such as pressure relief valves that open automatically when
 pressure exceeds a safe threshold, ensuring system protection under dangerous conditions.

3.A.1: What network connectivity links each element of the high-level design? How do the various

• Hydrogen: Programmable logic controllers onboard the field hardware communicate directly with sensors and have minimum

  to affect the sensors/computation requires a malicious actor to be local. Because each component has its own suite of sensors and

  Watchdog functionality exists between sensors and PLCs, which ensures controllers communicate with one another and the rest of the
  plant shuts down if the functionality fails. Usually, cell network connectivity is preferred over Wi-Fi, and this connection can be disabled
  by the operator per request, but depending on the vendor, the connection could be persistent.

3.A.2: How would the loss or manipulation of an external communications channel, or the data within
it, affect the system?
• Hydrogen: The worst-case scenario of manipulation occurs if a malicious actor can manipulate the settings on a PLC. This could
  disable the safety chain, causing hydrogen to vent through a venting system—resulting in an uncontrolled release.
  In addition, pressure manipulation would be a critical consequence of the loss or manipulation of data, which is dictated by American
  Society of Mechanical Engineers (ASME) Standard B31, which aims to prevent overpressure and failure. The next safety chain step
  is to vent the hazardous gases to avoid explosion or fire hazards. If a safety critical sensor is lost (loss of data), the plant goes to a
  shutdown scenario, also called a process shutdown. This process places everything into standby mode, which is different from an
  emergency shutdown. If something impedes the process shutdown, the safety chain is triggered, which shuts things down electrically
  or pneumatically (without relying on digital controls). However, these analog controls must also be periodically validated for a CIE
  implementation.

7.A.3: Are there subsystems within the conceptual system that are specially or uniquely required for
the overall conceptual system to operate, and how might those subsystems be exposed to cyber risk?

• Bioenergy: For bioenergy plants, the system’s engineered controls often rely on digital technologies, such as relays and disconnect
  switches. Although these systems are digital, they are isolated and operate independent of network connectivity. Periodic checks are
  performed to ensure these fail-safes operate as required by safety codes.
• Geothermal: In addition to venting controls (similar to hydrogen systems) and digital controls for ensuring safe shutdown (similar to
  bioenergy plants), geothermal systems also contain H2S (hydrogen sulfide) detection systems. These systems are independent of
  network connectivity, because that is a frequent risk for geothermal plants. In addition, safety procedures mandate the use of self-
  contained breathing apparatus for emergency response.
• Hydrogen: Within a hydrogen-based fuel cell power plant, digital technologies have redundant backup systems. Software-controlled,
  electrically controlled, and mechanically controlled fail-safes are stacked to build a redundant system with overlapping controls to
  mitigate failure modes.

 RETROFITTING NON-POWERED DAMS TO GENERATE HYDROELECTRIC POWER
 PROBLEM SCENARIO
 Retrofitting existing dams to include hydroelectric power generation involves adding turbines
 and other electromechanical equipment to dams originally built for purposes such as irrigation,
 flood control, or water supply. Connecting the generated power to the electrical grid requires
 careful planning to ensure seamless integration. The process involved in the retrofitting of NPDs
 are feasibility study, site assessment, permitting and regulatory approval, engineering design,
 infrastructure upgrades, turbine installation, connection to the grid, testing and commissioning,
 operation and maintenance, monitoring and compliance, community engagement, and continuous
 improvement.

 Hydrogeneration power plants have several controls to maintain and operate the plant as well as
 several controls to integrate the power generated to the grid. All these involve cyber components.
 Although the process of retrofitting NPD provides new power generation capacity, the process does
 not consider security from the initial stages.54

 Æ What are the systems that perform and support critical facility functions?
 • Turbine control system (TCS): The central system that controls the turbine’s speed, output,

 • Governor system: A subsystem of the turbine control that manages the speed of the turbine by
 controlling the water flow through the wicket gates, ensuring stable power generation. Figure 12: Major components of hydro-electric unit.

 Æ Is the storage, movement, and use of hazardous quantities of mass or energy
 (potential and kinetic) controlled by digital technologies?
 • Water flow control:
 • Digital control systems: The turbine control system regulates the movement of
 large volumes of water, which stores significant potential energy. Digital control
 systems manage the opening and closing of wicket gates, which control the
 flow of water from the reservoir or penstock to the turbine. Precise control
 is essential to ensure the kinetic energy of the flowing water is converted
 efficiently into mechanical energy by the turbine.
 • Automated gate control: Digital technologies control the gates that release
 water into the turbine. The timing and degree of gate opening are critical to
 maintaining the desired turbine speed and preventing excessive water flow,
 which could lead to dangerous overspeed conditions or structural damage.
 • Kinetic energy of the turbine:
 • Governor systems: The digital governor system controls the speed of the
 turbine by adjusting the water flow based on real-time feedback. This system is
 important for maintaining stable rotational speed, which is directly tied to the
 kinetic energy of the turbine. The governor ensures the turbine does not exceed
 safe operating speeds, preventing mechanical failures.
 • Speed and load management: The turbine’s kinetic energy, generated by the
 movement of water, is managed through digital control systems that adjust the
 load on the turbine. These systems ensure the turbine operates within safe
 parameters, balancing the conversion of potential energy into kinetic energy
 and then into electrical energy.
 • Energy storage and release:
 • Pumped storage operations: In some hydroelectric plants with pumped storage
 capabilities, digital technologies control the storage of potential energy by
 pumping water to a higher elevation during periods of low electricity demand.
 During peak demand, the water is released to generate power, converting
 stored potential energy back into kinetic and then electrical energy. This
 process is entirely managed by digital control systems to optimize energy
 use and efficiency.

 Network or Remote Access Resilience
 • Layer 7 “Next Generation” Firewall:
 • Use the deep packet inspection capabilities of the firewall to ensure the
 connections from the vendor are within the expected range of commands.
 This will require working with the vendor to obtain the specific protocols
 and the types of traffic they expect to be using. For example, if the vendor
 intends only to monitor the turbine for efficiency, all “write” commands can
 be blocked by the firewall.
 Operational Resilience
 • Conservative operation mode:
 • Upon notification from an appropriate resource (i.e., national/state/local law
 enforcement, balancing authority, vendor, others), remote connectivity to
 the plant can be blocked, and the plant can be operated in a block-loading
 capability. For larger plants that are typically staffed, this mode will likely
 impact the unit’s ability to respond only to shorter-duration ancillary service
 markets (SPIN, NSPN, AGC) but would allow the unit to be voice-dispatched
 to appropriate levels to balance load.
 • Alternatively, connectivity to the balancing authority could be maintained
 while blocking all other remote connections. This would limit the ability
 of an adversary to remotely access the unit while allowing maximum
 grid support.
