Intimacy and Relationships Among Partnered Danes With and Without a History of Cancer: Findings From the Project SEXUS Cohort Study
Authors: Cecilie H. Madsen, Christian Graugaard, Susanne O. Dalton, Mikael Andersson, Pernille E. Bidstrup, Morten Frisch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundIntimacy and relationship dynamics are central to health and well‐being and may buffer sexual difficulties in cancer survivors. Still, population‐based evidence comparing intimacy, reasons for having sex, and relational aspects between cancer survivors and individuals without a history of cancer remains limited and inconsistent.AimTo assess the perceived importance of various types of intimacy, frequent reasons for having sex, and relationship characteristics among partnered individuals with and without a history of cancer.MethodsWe analyzed data from 47,718 partnered individuals from the nationally representative Project SEXUS cohort study, including 3177 cancer survivors and 44,541 individuals without a cancer history. Prevalence estimates were calculated for all measures, and logistic regression models were used to obtain demographically weighted, confounder‐adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals for associations of cancer status with intimacy, sexuality, and relationship indicators, stratified by sex and time since diagnosis.ResultsCancer survivors reported importance of intimacy, frequent reasons for having sex, and relationship characteristics largely comparable to those reported by individuals without a history of cancer. Similar patterns were seen across sexes, both overall and within five years of diagnosis.ConclusionOur findings indicate that, despite the well‐established impact of cancer on sexual activity and functioning, not all aspects of sexual health are equally affected. In particular, the perceived importance of intimacy, reasons for engaging in sex, and relational characteristics appear largely unaffected by cancer. Together, these results underscore the importance of addressing sexual health holistically in survivorship care, encompassing resources embedded in intimacy and relationship dynamics.

Background

Advances in cancer detection and treatment have substantially improved survival rates [1], accompanied by growing scientific attention to survivors' health‐related quality of life [2], in which sexual health is recognized as an important component [3]. In line with the WHO definition, sexual health is understood as a multidimensional aspect of well‐being in relation to sexuality, whereas sexuality more broadly encompasses sexual thoughts, desires, experiences, meanings, and behaviors [4]. An excess burden of sexual health problems – such as reduced sexual desire, coital pain, erectile dysfunction, and orgasmic difficulties – is well documented among cancer survivors across various subpopulations and treatment modalities [5, 6, 7]. Such challenges may lead to reduced sexual activity and satisfaction [8, 9], poorer relationship quality [10], and diminished overall well‐being among cancer survivors [3]. Conversely, evidence indicates that relational resources may attenuate the adverse effects of sexual dysfunction [11, 12]. Partner intimacy, defined as physical and/or emotional closeness between spouses [13], is one such resource. Accordingly, relational dimensions of sexual health warrant consideration alongside sexual activity and function.

Physical and emotional closeness, as well as non‐penetrative sexual activity, have been associated with higher overall sexual satisfaction among cancer survivors, including those experiencing sexual dysfunction or hypoactivity [14, 15]. Likewise, high relationship quality and strong partner support are associated with greater sexual satisfaction among cancer survivors [16, 17]. As relational communication, emotional closeness, and physical intimacy are potentially modifiable factors, they hold considerable promise for improving sexual and relationship satisfaction – and, in turn, overall quality of life – among cancer survivors and their partners [18, 19]. More broadly, relationship characteristics, including sexual desire balance and experiences of partner unfaithfulness, may shape the relational context in which intimacy and partnered sexuality occur. Generally, relationship quality and intimacy are considered important in the general population: around nine‐in‐10 partnered men and women in Denmark rate their current relationship quality as good or very good, while the importance of intimacy is rated equally highly [20]. However, like sexual activity and functioning [21], intimacy and relationship characteristics may vary according to sociodemographic, health‐related, and lifestyle factors. When examining these domains as part of sexual health after cancer, comparisons with individuals without a history of cancer are therefore crucial.

Nonetheless, population‐based research comparing relationship characteristics and the perceived importance of intimacy between cancer survivors and individuals without a history of cancer remains scarce and inconsistent. For example, one study has suggested that cancer may increase relationship agreement, satisfaction, and cohesion [21], while others have found no differences in various expressions of intimacy such as kissing and cuddling [18, 22, 23] or in general relationship satisfaction [20, 24] between cancer survivors and individuals with no cancer history. In addition, some studies have reported reduced expressions of intimacy or affection [8, 19] and relationship satisfaction [8] among cancer survivors compared to cancer‐free individuals. Importantly, previous research has primarily examined intimacy and relationship characteristics using items or subscales embedded in broader measures of sexuality, sexual function, or relationship adjustment, rather than treating them as detailed outcomes in their own right [9, 22, 23, 25, 26]. In addition, many studies are limited by convenience sampling, small sample sizes, and/or selected populations with particular focus on gynecological cancers [9, 24, 25, 27, 28, 29]. Moreover, no previous study with a cancer‐free comparison group has, to our knowledge, examined frequent reasons for having partnered sex among sexually active cancer survivors.

To address these evidence gaps and broaden the perspective on sexual health in cancer survivorship beyond sexual activity and functioning, we conducted the first‐ever large‐scale, population‐based assessment to examine whether key psychosocial and relational aspects of sexual health were impaired among partnered individuals with versus without a history of cancer. Specifically, we examined the perceived importance of different expressions of intimacy (e.g., hugging, holding hands, kissing, and sitting or lying closely together) as indicators of emotional and physical closeness between spouses; frequent reasons for having had partnered sex (e.g., to strengthen the relationship, to achieve emotional closeness, and to please one's partner) as indicators of sexual motivation and the relational aspects of sex); and relationship quality as an indicator of relational health.

This study extends our prior population‐based research comparing sexual activity, function, and satisfaction between cancer survivors and individuals without a history of cancer [5].

Methods

Participants

This cross‐sectional study draws on baseline data from Project SEXUS, a nationally representative cohort study in Denmark whose first data collection took place in 2017–2018 [21, 30]. A stratified, probability‐based sampling strategy was used to identify 215,000 individuals from the Danish Civil Registration System [31] where all Danish residents are registered with a unique 10‐digit personal identification number allowing linkage across Danish health and administrative registers. Exclusion criteria included individuals below 15 or above 89 years of age, those who died prior to the invitation date, those with address protection, and those without an officially designated digital mailbox. After applying these criteria, 187,084 individuals were deemed eligible and invited to participate through their digital mailbox.

A total of 64,706 individuals returned complete or almost‐complete responses. The overall response rate was calculated in accordance with guidelines from the American Association for Public Opinion Research (AAPOR) [32]. After excluding responses that were almost‐complete or logically inconsistent, 62,675 individuals remained in the baseline sample. For the present study, we restricted the cohort sample to 47,718 individuals who reported being married or in a steady relationship on the date of questionnaire completion. Information on the registered sex of participants was obtained from the Danish Civil Registration System [31]. Consent was given by completing and submitting the questionnaire.

Questionnaire

Developed iteratively by the Project SEXUS group, the questionnaire combined validated measurement scales and previously used Danish public health survey items with newly developed items. It was refined through cognitive interviews, expert review, and pilot testing. The final online questionnaire comprised more than 600 items covering a wide range of topics, including socioeconomic conditions, lifestyle factors, and partnership status, as well as mental, physical, and sexual health. Logical branching tailored the questionnaire, resulting in a median of 180 questions and a median response time of approximately 30 minutes.

Cancer Diagnoses

We obtained information from the Danish Cancer Register [33] on all recorded cancer diagnoses (except non‐melanoma skin cancers) according to the International Classification of Diseases, versions 7 (ICD‐7) and 10 (ICD‐10) (Table S1), as well as information about date of diagnosis. We defined nine cancer groups and individual sites, subsequently referred to as cancer sites.

Important Types of Intimacy in the Past Year

The perceived importance of various types of intimacy was assessed with the question “Thinking about the last year, how important did you consider the following types of intimacy between you and your spouse/partner to be?”. The types of intimacy queried included “To give or receive a hug”, “To hold hands”, “To kiss”, “To sit closely together”, “To lie closely together”, “To caress or be caressed with clothes on”, and “To caress or be caressed without clothes on”. The items concerning sitting and lying closely together were combined into one measure, as were the items on caressing or being caressed with and without clothes on. Each type of intimacy was categorized as important if it, or one of its components in the case of combined measures, was rated as “Important”, “Very important” or “Extremely important” (vs. “Not very important” or “Not at all important”).

Frequent Reasons for Partnered Sex in the Past Year

Sexually active participants were asked “In the last year, how often have you had the following reasons to have sex?”, specifying that sex included vaginal intercourse, oral sex, anal sex or hand sex. The question was followed by a list of potential reasons for having had sex, including “Because I feel desire and am in the mood for it”, “To please my partner without being in the mood myself”, “To achieve an orgasm”, “To achieve relaxation and a sense of well‐being”, “To achieve emotional closeness with my partner”, “To strengthen, develop or improve the relationship to my partner”, “To give and receive love and caring”, “To feel confirmed”, “To feel desired”, and “Because my partner pressures me into it”. A particular reason for having had sex was categorized as frequent, if it was reported as occurring “Often” or “Every time” (vs. “Sometimes”, “Rarely” or “Not at all”).

Relationship Characteristics

Relationship characteristics were assessed using items covering relationship quality, masturbation compared to partnered sex, sexual desire balance, and unfaithfulness. Relationship quality was measured by the question “How do you consider the quality of your current marriage/partnership/relationship?” (“Very good” or “Good” vs. “Neither good nor bad”, “Bad”, or “Very bad”). Participants who had masturbated and/or watched pornography in the past year were asked to state whether they felt that these activities gave them “something that a sexual relationship cannot give me” (“Agree” vs. “Disagree”). Perceived balance in sexual desire between partners during the past year was assessed by the following question: “Thinking about the last year, how would you characterize your own and your spouse's/partner's desire to have sex?” with the following three response options: “We feel a desire to have sex about equally often/rarely”, “I feel a desire to have sex more often than my partner”, and “My partner feels a desire to have sex more often than I do”. Respondents' own unfaithfulness was assessed by the question “Have you ever been unfaithful to your current spouse/partner?” (“No, never”, “Yes, once”, “Yes, 2‐4 times”, and “Yes, five or more times”), and perceived partner unfaithfulness was assessed by the question “Has your current spouse/partner ever been unfaithful to you?” (“Yes, certainly”, “Yes, probably”, “No, probably not”, and “No, certainly not”). Respondents' own unfaithfulness and spouse's/partner's probable or certain unfaithfulness were dichotomized as ever versus never.

Sociodemographic and Lifestyle‐Related Covariates

Potential confounders of the association between cancer status and sexual health outcomes were selected a priori based on the existing research literature. Covariates included age at study participation (15–34; 35–44; 45–54; 55–64; 65–74; ≥ 75 years) and self‐reported sociodemographic and lifestyle‐related variables, including duration of relationship (< 5; 5–9; 10–19; 20–29; ≥ 30 years), education (≤ 10 years; Secondary education; Short‐cycle higher education; Medium‐cycle higher education; Long‐cycle higher education), difficulties paying bills in the past year (Not at all; Sometimes; Often), body mass index (< 18.5; 18.5– < 25; 25– < 30; ≥ 30 kg/m2), smoker status (Never; Former; Current), average weekly alcohol consumption in the past year (0; 1–2; 3–7; 8–14; 15–21; ≥ 22 alcoholic beverages), and average weekly moderate to hard physical activity (< ½; ½–1½; 1½–2½; 2½–5; > 5 hours). An English translation of all questionnaire items and response categories is available on the Project SEXUS homepage (https://www.projektsexus.dk/Questionnaire_2017‐2018).

Statistical Analyses

We characterized the study population using frequencies and percentages for sociodemographic, lifestyle, and health‐related factors stratified by cancer status. Differences between groups were analyzed with Pearson's chi‐squared test, and Mood's test was used to assess differences in median age.

For each sex, prevalence estimates for important types of intimacy and frequent reasons for having partnered sex were demographically weighted to achieve national representativeness (accounting for birth year, region of residence, marital status, cultural background, and twin status [34]). In all analyses, respondents answering “I do not know” to a particular question were excluded.

Logistic regression analyses stratified by sex were performed to calculate confounder‐adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for associations between these measures and cancer status overall (ever vs. never diagnosed with cancer) or recent cancer status (diagnosed with cancer within the last five years vs. never diagnosed with cancer), adjusting for age (in 10‐year intervals), education level, difficulties paying bills in the past year, body mass index, smoker status, weekly alcohol consumption over the past year, and average weekly physical activity. As analyses were exploratory and aimed at describing patterns across sexual health and relationship outcomes, we did not adjust for multiple comparisons. Results were interpreted based on overall patterns, effect sizes, and confidence intervals rather than isolated p‐values. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.0, using stats, survey and svyVGAM packages.

Results

The overall AAPOR response rate 1 for the baseline data collection in Project SEXUS was 34.6%. Among respondents providing complete and consistent data, 47,718 individuals were partnered (married or in a steady relationship). Of these, 3177 (1368 women and 1809 men) had been diagnosed with cancer other than non‐melanoma skin cancer prior to participating in the Project SEXUS study, while the remaining 44,541 (23,247 women and 21,294 men) had no recorded history of cancer (Table 1). More than half of the cancer survivors (62.2% of women and 52.3% of men) had received their diagnosis five or more years prior to study participation. Among women, the most common cancer sites were breast cancer, genital cancers, malignant melanoma of the skin, and alimentary tract cancers. Among men, the most common cancer sites were genital cancers, alimentary tract cancers, urinary tract cancers, malignant melanoma of the skin, and lymphatic and hematopoietic cancers.

Compared with respondents without a history of cancer, cancer survivors were significantly older (median age among women: 67 vs. 48 years; among men: 70 vs. 50 years) and had been in their current relationships for longer durations (median duration among women: 36 vs. 15 years; among men: 37 vs. 16 years). Cancer survivors also more frequently reported having had no difficulties paying bills during the past year (women: 93.6% vs. 85.5%; men: 93.5% vs. 88.3%) and were more likely to be former smokers (women: 39.5% vs. 29.1%; men: 53.7% vs. 33.8%). Additionally, cancer survivors of both sexes more often reported an average weekly alcohol consumption of 3‐7 or more alcoholic beverages (women: 54.5% vs. 40.0%; men: 71.4% vs. 60.6%) (Table 1).

Important Types of Intimacy in the Past Year

Cancer survivors and individuals without a history of cancer had comparable odds of rating most of the examined intimacy measures as important (Table 2). Overall, however, female cancer survivors were slightly more likely to rate kissing as important (aOR: 1.38; CI: 1.01–1.88), and among women diagnosed with cancer within the past five years, the same applied to holding hands (aOR: 2.18; CI: 1.28–3.71) and sitting or lying closely together (aOR: 1.73; CI: 1.04–2.88). No statistically significant differences in the ratings of different types of intimacy were observed between male cancer survivors and men without a history of cancer.

Frequent Reasons for Partnered Sex in the Past Year

Sexually active cancer survivors and individuals without a history of cancer had largely comparable odds of indicating various reasons for having had partnered sex during the past year as frequent (Table 3). However, sexually active women diagnosed within the past five years were significantly less likely than individuals without cancer to report “To achieve relaxation and a sense of well‐being” as a frequent reason for partnered sexual activity (aOR: 0.65; CI: 0.47–0.90). Male survivors diagnosed within the past five years were significantly more likely to identify “To please my partner without being in the mood myself” as a frequent reason (aOR: 1.39; CI: 1.03–1.87). Men also had higher odds of reporting “To strengthen, develop or improve the relationship to my partner”, both overall (aOR: 1.24; CI: 1.01–1.52) and when restricting analyses to cancer survivors diagnosed within the past five years (aOR: 1.42, CI: 1.06–1.89).

Relationship Characteristics

Only few differences in relationship characteristics emerged between cancer survivors and individuals with no history of cancer (Table 4). However, female survivors diagnosed within the past five years were significantly less likely to report that they experienced sexual desire more often than their partner (aOR: 0.59; CI: 0.41–0.85). Likewise, male cancer survivors more often reported that they had less sexual desire than their partner, both overall (aOR: 1.52; CI: 1.26–1.83) and when restricting analyses to men diagnosed within the past five years (aOR: 1.38; CI: 1.06–1.81). Correspondingly, male cancer survivors had significantly lower odds of reporting that they experienced sexual desire more often than their partner, both overall (aOR: 0.79; CI: 0.70–0.88) and among those diagnosed within the past five years (aOR: 0.82; CI: 0.70–0.95).

Discussion

This study offers a comprehensive overview of types of intimacy considered important, frequent reasons for having sex, and relationship characteristics among partnered cancer survivors compared with partnered individuals without a history of cancer. With few exceptions, findings among cancer survivors equaled those of individuals without a cancer history, and no consistent patterns emerged across sex or time since diagnosis.

Evidence regarding the perceived importance of different intimacy types among cancer survivors and their partners remains limited. Some studies have reported reduced levels of intimate behaviors in the aftermath of cancer, such as lower engagement in kissing and caressing [24] and reduced expressions of affection [9]. Nonetheless, consistent with our findings, several studies including cancer‐free comparison groups have reported no significant decrease in the frequency of kissing, petting, or fondling, expressions of emotional and physical intimacy [27] or interest in kissing and cuddling [28, 29] following cancer. One larger study from Great Britain compared a mixed population of 561 cancer survivors aged over 50 years with 6129 individuals with no history of cancer using a single intimacy measure (frequency of engagement in activities such as kissing, petting, or fondling). Similar to the present study, no differences between the two groups were found [22].

Drawing on the same cohort study, we have previously documented substantial impairments in sexual health following cancer, including greater odds of sexual inactivity, poorer rating of sex life, and unmet sexual needs among both female and male cancer survivors, across a range of cancer subtypes, and irrespective of time since the most recent diagnosis [5]. Moreover, cancer survivors were significantly more likely than their cancer‐free peers to consider a good sex life unimportant and to report having had no sexual needs in the past year [5]. Against this backdrop of sexual adversity and diminished interest in sexual activity reported by cancer survivors, it is noteworthy that partnered intimacy was not perceived as less important compared with individuals without a cancer history. Indeed, female cancer survivors more often than women without a history of cancer rated selected aspects such as holding hands, kissing, and sitting or lying closely together as important, suggesting that non‐sexual forms of physical closeness may even gain importance in the context of cancer survivorship.

Our results suggest that sexually active cancer survivors report largely the same reasons for having sex as sexually active individuals without cancer. However, significantly more male survivors than cancer‐free peers rated “To please my partner without being in the mood myself” and “To strengthen, develop or improve the relationship to my partner” as frequent reasons for having sex, which aligns well with previously documented reductions in sexual interest and needs among individuals diagnosed with cancer [5]. These findings among men are consistent with prior qualitative and quantitative research showing that cancer survivors frequently engage in sexual activity for relational and emotional reasons (i.e., maintaining intimacy, preserving a sense of normality, expressing love and commitment, or supporting their partner). In concordance with our study, however, explicitly duty‐ or pressure‐based motives are rarely reported [10, 35, 36]. Together, these results may indicate a shift toward more partner‐ and relationship‐oriented motives for sexual behaviors among male cancer survivors. This extends previous research, which has mainly examined reasons for the widespread sexual inactivity among cancer survivors, particularly finding physical problems [37], and cancer‐related body concerns [38] to be operational.

We observed only few differences in relationship characteristics between cancer survivors and individuals without a history of cancer. Relationship quality or satisfaction are by far the most extensively examined relational domains, and previous studies with cancer‐free comparison groups have presented mixed findings, with some showing no differences [23, 25], while others have reported higher [27] or lower [9] quality or satisfaction among cancer survivors. These inconsistencies likely reflect methodological heterogeneity across studies, as well as the frequent reliance on samples predominantly composed of women with gynecological cancer, which limits the generalizability of findings to broader cancer survivor populations.

In addition to overall relationship quality, cancer may also affect the balance between interpersonal and solo sexual activity within relationships. One U.S. study, lacking a cancer‐free comparison group, found that some women with breast cancer reoriented sexually and used solo masturbation and pornography to maintain desire and improve sexual function during and after treatment [39]. Our findings, however, indicate no differences between cancer survivors and cancer‐free individuals in whether these practices were perceived as providing benefits not obtained through partnered sex.

Sexual desire discrepancies have previously been identified as a relational challenge in couples affected by cancer [36, 40]. This aligns with our findings showing that both women and men diagnosed with cancer within the past five years were significantly less likely than cancer‐free peers of the same sex to report having had higher sexual desire than their partner. Furthermore, male cancer survivors were more likely to report that their partner experienced sexual desire more often than they did, irrespective of time since cancer diagnosis.

To our knowledge, unfaithfulness has not previously been examined among cancer survivors. Notably, neither unfaithfulness nor certain or probable partner unfaithfulness differed significantly between individuals with and without a history of cancer.

Clinical Implications

Our findings indicate that although sexual problems and sexual inactivity are known to be more prevalent among individuals with a history of cancer, partnered cancer survivors continue to view most types of intimacy as equally, if not more, important than individuals without a cancer history. Furthermore, we found that sexually active cancer survivors reported largely the same frequent reasons for engaging in sexual activity as their cancer‐free peers, although male survivors more often emphasized pleasing their partner and strengthening the relationship as motivations for having sex.

These findings, which were interpreted in light of a Danish healthcare and sociocultural context, may help clinicians offer a more balanced perspective on sexual health after cancer by acknowledging sexual challenges and dysfunctions while at the same time recognizing relational resources and the continued relevance of non‐coital forms of intimacy [41]. Such a perspective may help legitimize relational intimacy and better align clinical care with cancer survivors' unmet needs for information and counseling regarding sexual and relationship‐based concerns [42]. This, in turn, underscores the need for clinical training that equips health care professionals to address sexual health as a multidimensional construct, encompassing sexual function and activity, intimacy, and relationship quality, as a routine component of survivorship care [43].

Study Limitations

The present study is based on data from a large and nationally representative sample, allowing for robust assessments among both women and men of the impact of both short‐ and long‐term cancer survivorship status on intimacy, reasons for having sex and relationship characteristics.

Nevertheless, some limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. The cross‐sectional nature of the baseline questionnaire data provides no information about how intimacy and relationship dynamics evolve over time and precludes firm causal inferences. The baseline data were collected in 2017–2018, and the findings should therefore be interpreted in light of possible sociocultural and technological changes since data collection. However, as the study focuses on partner intimacy and relationship characteristics rather than more fluctuating patterns of sexual behavior, the findings are unlikely to be substantially affected by shifts in sexual culture over the past decade.

Although the study included survivors of all types of cancer, the limited number of participants in certain diagnostic groups precluded detailed site‐specific analyses. In addition, because most examined intimacy measures were rated as important across the entire sample, our ability to detect small group differences may have been limited, and given the number of outcomes examined, some statistically significant findings may be due to chance. Also, while comparing favorably with participation rates in other recent sexual health surveys, the overall response rate was still modest (34.6%), so potential bias arising from self‐selection into the study should be considered. As information about cancer diagnoses was not collected among non‐responders, it remains unclear whether invited cancer survivors with the most burdensome disease trajectories were underrepresented.

Conclusion

In conclusion, partnered cancer survivors reported perceived importance of different types of intimacy, frequent reasons for having sex, and relationship characteristics that were largely comparable to those of partnered individuals without a history of cancer. These results should encourage care providers and researchers to address sexual health in a holistic manner, incorporating not only sexual activity and functioning but also – equally importantly – potential resources embedded in intimacy and relational dimensions.
