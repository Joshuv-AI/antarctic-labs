A national analysis of outcomes associated with laryngeal complications among older adults undergoing cardiac surgery☆
Authors: Syed Shaheer Ali, Konmal Ali, Melissa Justo, Robert Kropp, Giselle Porter, Deep Mehta, Sona Mahrokhy, Sidney Hinks, Peyman Benharash
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionLaryngeal complications (LC) or dysphagia are often underdiagnosed among those undergoing cardiac surgery. With conflicting reports on the relevance of LC, we characterized the prevalence, risk factors, and outcomes of this complication in a national cohort of older adults receiving cardiac surgery across the U.S.MethodsAdults (≥65 years) receiving coronary, valvular and proximal aortic operations were tabulated from the 2016–2022 Nationwide Readmissions Database. Patients were stratified by the concomitant diagnosis of LC. Mixed regression models were developed to identify the association of LC with outcomes of interest.ResultsAmong 1,092,639 patients, 1.6% comprised LC. Compared to others, LC patients were older (74 Interquartile Range [IQR]: [70–79] vs 72 years [68–77], P < 0.001), more frequently female (31.9 vs 30.1%, P < 0.001) and insured by Medicare (88.6 vs 86.8%, P < 0.001).Following risk adjustment, LC was associated with greater odds of mortality (Adjusted Odds Ratio [AOR] 1.23, 95% Confidence Interval [CI] 1.06–1.55), respiratory (AOR 1.72, 95% CI 1.61–1.83), and infectious complications (AOR 1.51, 95% CI 1.41–1.62). LC was also associated with greater length of stay (β +5.43 days, 95% CI 5.09–5.78), hospitalization costs (β + $17,600, 95% CI 16,100-19,100), and 30-day non-elective readmission (AOR 1.30, 95% CI 1.22–1.39).ConclusionsLC appears to be associated with increased mortality, postoperative complications, and 30-day readmissions. Furthermore, LC is linked with an incremental increase in length of stay and hospitalization expenditures. While causality could not be established, our findings suggest the relevance of early detection and potential interventions to mitigate sequelae of LC in older patients.Keywords: Laryngeal complications, Cardiac surgery, Postoperative complications, Readmissions, Healthcare resource utilization

Introduction

Postoperative laryngeal complications (LC), including dysphagia, vocal cord paresis, and impaired phonation, are increasingly recognized sequelae of cardiothoracic surgery [1], [2], [3]. Dysphagia alone has been reported in up to 16% of patients following cardiac operations, although true incidence may likely be higher due to the absence of routine postoperative screening [4], [5]. These complications are typically attributable to direct laryngeal injury or neuropraxia involving the recurrent laryngeal nerves, arising from prolonged endotracheal intubation, transesophageal echocardiography, or mediastinal dissection [2], [6]. Older adults may be particularly vulnerable, as age-related frailty, sarcopenia, and diminished swallow reflexes increase susceptibility to laryngeal dysfunction after surgical stress [3], [7]. Importantly, LC extends beyond impairments in voice and swallowing, with established associations to aspiration, respiratory failure, and prolonged mechanical ventilation following cardiac surgery [1], [4].

It is thought that LC frequently remains underrecognized in the postoperative setting because vocal fold dysfunction is not reliably identified through auditory assessment alone, and aspiration may occur silently without cough or overt distress [5], [6]. Silent aspiration has been linked to significantly higher rates of pneumonia, as well as tracheostomy and feeding tube placement among cardiac surgery patients [4], [8]. Prior institutional studies have demonstrated LC are associated with 3–5 additional hospital days and 10,000-$20,000 in excess hospitalization costs [5], [7].

Despite increasing recognition of postoperative LC, its incidence, risk factors, and associated outcomes following cardiac surgery has not been well characterized at the national level. Using a nationally representative cohort, the present study characterized the prevalence, and impact of LC among older adults undergoing cardiac operations. We hypothesized LC would be independently associated with increased mortality, complications, resource utilization, and non-elective readmissions.

Methods

Data source and cohort definitions

This retrospective cohort study utilized the 2016–2022 Nationwide Readmissions Database (NRD), maintained by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality as part of the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project [9]. The NRD is the largest publicly available all-payer readmissions database in the United States and samples approximately 17 million inpatient hospitalizations annually, yielding nationally representative estimates for nearly 60% of all U.S. hospitalizations through validated discharge-level survey weights. The NRD contains unique, deidentified patient linkage identifiers permitting tracking of in-state readmissions within a calendar year while excluding rehabilitation and long-term acute care hospitals.

Older adults (≥65 years) undergoing major cardiac operations, including coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), combined as well as isolated valvular operations, and proximal aortic procedures, were identified using relevant International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) procedure codes [10]. Patients undergoing transcatheter interventions, heart transplantation, or durable ventricular assist device implantation were excluded (n = 1846). Records missing key variables as well as discharges in December of each year were excluded to ensure complete 30-day follow-up (n = 3943, Fig. 1).

The LC cohort was identified using ICD-10 diagnosis codes corresponding to dysphagia, vocal cord paralysis/paresis, dysphonia, aphagia, and other laryngeal disorders documented during the index hospitalization (others: No-LC) [11].

Variable definitions and outcomes

Patient-level characteristics included age, sex, race, primary insurance status, admission type (elective vs nonelective), and comorbidities. Comorbidities were quantified using the van Walraven modification of the Elixhauser Comorbidity Index, a validated composite measure encompassing 30 chronic conditions [12]. Individual comorbidities of interest included chronic pulmonary disease, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, congestive heart failure, and obesity. Operative characteristics were categorized by procedure type (coronary, valvular, or proximal aortic surgery). Hospital characteristics were defined in accordance with the NRD data dictionary [13].

The primary outcome of the present study was in-hospital mortality. Secondary outcomes included postoperative complications (categorized as respiratory, infectious, renal, cardiovascular, and neurologic events), and resource use (length of stay [LOS], hospitalization costs, and 30-day nonelective readmission). Hospitalization costs were calculated by applying HCUP-provided hospital-specific cost-to-charge ratios and adjusted to 2022 U.S. dollars using the Personal Health Care Price Index [14].

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are reported as proportions (%) and continuous variables as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). Given the large sample size, the effect size was further examined using standardized mean differences (SMD), with significance defined as >0.1. P-values were additionally reported alongside SMDs in Table 1, Table 2 to provide transparency, though SMD was used as the primary measure of between-group balance given its reduced sensitivity to the large sample size. Baseline characteristics were compared using Pearson's χ2 test for categorical and the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables [30]. Model covariates were selected using elastic net regularization [15]. Entropy balancing was first employed to generate covariate balanced groups [16]. Entropy balancing was selected over propensity score matching given the substantial disparity in cohort size between LC and No-LC patients (1.6% prevalence), as it reweights the entire comparison group to achieve exact covariate balance without discarding unmatched patients, thereby preserving statistical power and the national representativeness of the cohort. Multivariable logistic regression models were then developed to evaluate patient and operative factors independently associated with LC, as well as to assess the association of LC with binary clinical outcomes, including mortality, postoperative complications, non-home discharge, and 30-day nonelective readmission. Linear regression models were used to estimate incremental differences in LOS and hospitalization costs. All multivariable models adjusted for clinically relevant covariates, including patient demographics, comorbidity burden, admission type, operative category, insurance status, and year of admission. Regression results are reported as adjusted odds ratios (AOR) for dichotomous outcomes and beta coefficients (β) for continuous outcomes, each with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). The Nelson-Aalen methodology was used to tabulate the cumulative hazards of readmission within 30 days of index discharge [17]. All analyses incorporated NRD survey weights to generate national estimates. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided α < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata 18.1 (StataCorp, College Station, TX). Given the deidentified nature of the NRD, this study was deemed exempt from full review by the Institutional Review Board.

Results

Patient characteristics

Of an estimated 1,092,639 older adults undergoing major cardiac surgery during the study period, 17,214 (1.6%) were diagnosed with laryngeal complications (LC), with baseline characteristics summarized in Table 1. Compared to No-LC, LC patients were older (74 [IQR 70–79] vs 72 [68–77] years, SMD = 0.29). LC patients demonstrated a lower overall comorbidity burden as measured by the Elixhauser Comorbidity Index (5 [4–7] vs 6 [4–7], SMD = 0.25) yet were more frequently admitted nonelectively (47.6 vs 42.4%, SMD = 0.10). Additionally, LC patients more commonly underwent multivalvular operations (10.1 vs 6.3%, SMD = 0.13).

Unadjusted outcomes

Unadjusted clinical outcomes are detailed in Table 2. Patients with LC experienced higher rates of in-hospital mortality (3.5 vs 2.1%, SMD = 0.11). Furthermore, LC patients experienced increased respiratory (21.2 vs 13.0%, SMD = 0.20), infectious (15.5 vs 9.9%, SMD = 0.17), and renal complications (28.9 vs 22.8%, SMD = 0.13). LC patients also demonstrated longer LOS (13 [8–21] vs 8 [6–13] days, SMD = 0.51), higher hospitalization costs ($68.9 K [IQR 49.0–100.9] vs $52.5 K [38.7–74.6], SMD = 0.35), and increased 30-day non-elective readmission rates (14.0 vs 10.5%, SMD = 0.10).

Risk-adjusted outcomes

Following multivariable risk adjustment, factors independently associated with increased odds of LC included advancing age (AOR 1.04 per year; 95% CI 1.03–1.06), greater comorbidity burden (AOR 1.14 per point; 95% CI 1.13–1.16), higher 51st-75th percentile income (AOR 1.14; 95% CI 1.07–1.22), and Medicare insurance relative to private coverage (AOR 1.18; 95% CI 1.08–1.29). Conversely, female sex (AOR 0.94; 95% CI 0.92–0.96) and elective admission status (AOR 0.76; 95% CI 0.71–0.82) were associated with reduced odds of LC (Fig. 2).

Discussion

In this large-scale and contemporary study, we found LC to remain a relevant and serious postoperative complication following major cardiac operations in older adults. By utilizing the NRD, we were able to assess LC-associated outcomes during the index hospitalization and early post discharge period at the national level (Table 3). Among hospitalizations for coronary, valvular, and proximal aortic surgery, we observed an overall LC incidence of 1.6%. The likelihood of LC varied by patient and operative characteristics, occurring commonly among older, nonelectively admitted patients, and those undergoing complex aortic procedures. We found LC to be independently associated with increased in-hospital mortality and postoperative complications. Importantly, development of LC was also linked with substantial markers of increased resource utilization, including prolonged LOS, higher hospitalization costs, and increased 30-day non-elective readmissions. Given the significance of LC in cardiac surgery, several of our findings warrant further discussion.

Consistent with prior work examining postoperative swallowing and laryngeal dysfunction in cardiac surgery, we found LC to be independently associated with substantial postoperative morbidity, mortality, and resource utilization among older adults. Prior institutional analyses have demonstrated patients developing dysphagia or related laryngeal injury after cardiac operations experience markedly higher rates of respiratory failure, aspiration pneumonia, and need for prolonged ventilatory support, translating into delayed recovery [4], [5]. In contemporary cohorts, LC have been linked with significant increases in hospital LOS and excess costs, reflecting both downstream complications and delays in safe discharge planning [7], [8]. Our findings extend this literature by demonstrating, even after multivariable adjustment, LC remained associated with increased odds of in-hospital mortality and multisystem complications, including respiratory, infectious, and renal events. These associations align with Huston et al. suggesting impaired airway protection and silent aspiration serve as key drivers of postoperative deterioration in this population [6]. Moreover, recent studies have emphasized LC may represent a marker of diminished physiologic reserve rather than an isolated local injury, particularly in older adults undergoing complex cardiac operations [18]. Although prior studies have suggested adverse outcomes attributed to LC may reflect operative complexity or prolonged intubation alone, our national-level findings indicate LC confers independent prognostic significance beyond these factors [19]. The substantial excess LOS, hospitalization costs, and readmission risk observed in the present study underscore the economic burden of LC, highlighting their relevance in contemporary value-based cardiac surgical care.

Determinants of LC are numerous and multifactorial, reflecting patient vulnerability, operative complexity, and social context rather than comorbidity burden alone. Among older cardiac surgery patients in our cohort, LC occurred more frequently in those of advanced age, nonelective admission status, and following complex proximal aortic procedures, findings consistent with prior reports linking physiologic frailty and surgical stress to impaired airway protection [3], [7]. Although LC patients demonstrated a slightly lower measured Elixhauser comorbidity burden, this may reflect limitations of administrative indices in capturing functional reserve and neuromuscular vulnerability, which have been shown to better predict postoperative swallowing dysfunction [20]. Female sex, elective admission, and lower income were each independently protective against LC in our cohort, findings that may reflect differences in perioperative risk exposure and diagnostic ascertainment rather than a true biological effect. Elective admission likely reflects a more controlled perioperative course with lower risk of prolonged, traumatic intubation relative to emergency operations [28], while the protective associations observed with female sex and lower income may instead reflect known sociodemographic disparities in access to postoperative swallow screening and otolaryngology evaluation that limit diagnostic ascertainment of LC in these populations [ 29]. Furthermore, operative factors appear particularly salient, as recurrent laryngeal nerve injury has been closely associated with thoracic aortic pathology and arch reconstruction, with Ishii et al. reporting postoperative nerve paralysis in approximately 9% of patients undergoing thoracic aortic repair [21]. Prolonged intubation further compounds this risk, Skoretz et al. demonstrated dysphagia frequency increased more than tenfold when intubation exceeded 48 h, with a near twofold rise in odds for every additional 12 h of mechanical ventilation [22]. Social and systems-level factors also appear contributory, as disparities in postoperative monitoring, discharge planning, and access to early rehabilitation have been associated with delayed recognition of LC, worsening downstream outcomes [23], [24]. Although prior work has emphasized comorbidity burden as a primary driver of postoperative complications, our findings suggest LC more accurately reflects a convergence of frailty, procedural complexity, and perioperative exposures [7]. Taken together, these results underscore LC as a marker of heightened vulnerability not fully captured by traditional risk stratification, emphasizing the need for targeted perioperative assessment in older cardiac surgery patients.

Under-recognition of LC and the absence of standardized postoperative screening likely contribute to delayed diagnosis and subsequent progression of morbidity in older cardiac surgery patients. Prior studies have demonstrated dysphagia and related laryngeal dysfunction frequently go undetected because many patients aspirate silently, without overt cough or distress, obscuring clinical recognition in the immediate postoperative period [3], [5]. In a prospective evaluation of postoperative cardiac surgery patients, York et al. identified aspiration in 28% of patients, nearly half of whom were silent aspirators, underscoring the limited sensitivity of symptom-based screening approaches [25]. Furthermore, commonly employed bedside screening tools, such as the 3-oz water swallow test, have demonstrated only modest discriminant ability in this population, missing a substantial proportion of clinically significant aspiration events [25], [26]. Variability in screening practices further compounds this challenge, as York et al. reported fewer than 20% of cardiac intensive care unit (ICU) nurses received formal training in dysphagia assessment, with wide heterogeneity in screening timing and methodology [27]. In the present study, factors associated with LC such as advanced age, nonelective admission, and complex operative exposure, mirrored populations most vulnerable to delayed recognition, as illustrated in Figure 2 [7]. Delayed diagnosis may allow progression to aspiration pneumonia, respiratory failure, and prolonged hospitalization, contributing to the substantial excess resource utilization observed [18], [19]. Although targeted swallow screening protocols and early speech-language pathology consultation have demonstrated feasibility, benefitting in select institutions, no universally adopted guideline exists for routine LC detection following cardiac surgery [7]. Taken together, these findings demonstrate a critical systems-level gap, emphasizing the need for standardized, risk-informed screening strategies to facilitate early identification and mitigation of LC-related complications in vulnerable cardiac surgery populations.

Although a causal relationship cannot be established given the observational design, our findings suggest several practical strategies for perioperative teams. Given the strong association of LC with advanced age, nonelective admission, and complex proximal aortic procedures, these factors may serve as a pragmatic, low-cost trigger for targeted postoperative swallow screening in lieu of universal screening, which may not be feasible in all centers [26]. For patients flagged as high-risk, early bedside swallow evaluation prior to resumption of oral intake, with a low threshold for speech-language pathology consultation, may facilitate earlier identification of occult, silent aspiration [7], [25]. Given the substantial excess readmission risk associated with LC in our cohort, confirmation of safe swallowing function and structured discharge planning, rather than resolution of acute symptoms alone, may represent a meaningful checkpoint prior to discharge in this population. While prospective validation of such a targeted screening approach is needed, these steps represent feasible, low-resource interventions that could be integrated into existing perioperative pathways.

The present study has several important limitations inherent to the nature of the NRD as an administrative database. Owing to its retrospective design, causal relationships between LC and postoperative outcomes cannot be established. Additionally, LC may be underreported due to the absence of standardized postoperative screening and the occult presentation of dysphagia, potentially resulting in misclassification of affected patients. The NRD lacks granular clinical detail, precluding assessment of operative duration, intraoperative events, use of transesophageal echocardiography, timing of LC diagnosis, and diagnostic modalities. Furthermore, the database captures only inpatient encounters and cannot identify post-discharge mortality occurring outside the hospital. Despite these limitations, this study utilized validated HCUP methodologies and robust statistical adjustment within a large, nationally representative cohort to characterize the clinical and economic impact of LC following cardiac surgery.

In conclusion, we found LC to be associated with significantly increased odds of postoperative mortality, complications, resource utilization, and 30-day readmissions among older adults undergoing cardiac surgery. These findings underscore LC as a clinically meaningful marker of postoperative vulnerability rather than an isolated local injury. However, LC remains underrecognized in routine care. Future studies to elucidate optimal screening strategies, timing of diagnosis, and targeted perioperative interventions may improve early detection and mitigate downstream morbidity in this high-risk population.
