Abstract

  The streaming operator of the transport equation is derived for spherical coordi-

nates by starting from Newton’s second law for a free particle expressed in spherical

coordinates. We shall show that the partial derivatives with respect to the velocity

variables of the particle, which are absent in the Cartesian coordinate formulation

of the transport equation, arise in the spherical coordinate formulation of the trans-

port equation in response to the centrifugal force which prevents a free particle

from ‘falling into the origin’ of the coordinate system. These partial derivatives also

prevent ray-effects, which are present in the solution to the transport equation in

Cartesian coordinates obtained by the discrete ordinate method, a collocation tech-

nique for discretizing the velocity variables, from appearing in a discrete ordinate

solution to the transport equation in spherical coordinates by coupling the discrete

ordinates, the collocation points of the velocity variables in the discrete ordinate

method. Numerical examples, which are free of ray effects, are presented. In ad-

dition to the derivation of the streaming operator, this paper presents an analytic

Preprint submitted to J. Math. Phys. 11 February 2008

solution to the corresponding characteristic equations of the streaming operator as

well as an algorithm to solve the system of finite difference equations obtained by

discretizing the streaming operator.

Key words: transport equation, spherical coordinates, multi-dimensions,

ray-effects, differential geometry, classical mechanics, characteristics

Our blueprint for the construction of a path through a neighborhood of this

type is the spherical coordinate representation of a straight line which is de-

termined by a system of ordinary differential equations (ode). The right hand

side (rhs) of the system consists of the coefficients which multiply the partial

derivatives in (1). This system is refered to in the literature as the character-

istic equations of the classical solution to the hyperbolic partial differential

equation. The characteristic equations of the transport equation in spherical

coordinates in three dimensions appear daunting at first glance for two rea-

sons. The first is because the equations are nonlinear. The second, which we

believe is the primary reason for its formidable appearance, is because its con-

nection to the physical properties of a straight line is poorly established in the

nuclear engineering literature. However, if a connection between the physics

of a straight line and the characteristic equations can be established, then the

connection would open the characteristic equations to the analysis by a variety

of classical mechanical solution methods.

The transport operator in spherical coordinates is usually derived geometri-

 4

cally. Although the geometrical arguments are convincing, they are, however,

inadequate for the derivation of an analytical solution to the characteristic

equations. The first goal of this paper is to derive the transport operator in

spherical coordinates by the algebraic methods of differential geometry. The

second goal is to solve the characteristic equations by exploiting the differential

geometrical properties of a straight line. We shall validate our analytical solu-

tion by comparing it to the numerical solution obtained by the Runge-Kutta

method for solving ordinary differential equations. The third goal consists of

two parts: the first is to formulate a finite difference stencil, which is compati-

ble with the paths prescribed by the analytic solution, to represent the partial

derivatives on the lhs of (1), and the second is to develop a procedure to solve

the system of finite difference equations. We shall also compare the numerical

solution to the transport equation in spherical coordinates to the numerical

solution of the transport equation in Cartesian coordinates in order to show

that the solution to the transport equation in spherical coordinates is without

ray-effects.

The unifying principle of this paper is that the second derivative of the position

vector of a straight line with respect to (wrt) s, the path parameter (which

can be thought of as the product of the speed of a neutron and time; s will

be described further in §2), is zero. When this principle is applied to u(s),

a function of a single variable, the condition d2 u/ ds2 = 0 implies that the

graph of u(s) wrt s is a straight line since the second derivative of a function

is the curvature of the function. Furthermore, the second order differential

equations which represent a straight line in Cartesian coordinates, d2 x/ ds2 =

0, d2 y/ ds2 = 0, and d2 z/ ds2 = 0, are as simple as d2 u/ ds2 = 0, because the

unit vectors of the Cartesian coordinate system are constant vectors. On the

 5

other hand, the second order differential equations which represents a straight

line spherical coordinates are, as we shall see in (17 -19), complicated, because

the unit vectors of the spherical coordinate system are location dependent.

Since a unit vector of the spherical coordinate system changes as the position

changes, then the derivatives of a unit vector are non-vanishing. Therefore the

second order differential equations which represent a straight line in spherical

coordinates must involve the derivatives of the unit vectors which are the

complicating terms of the differential equations.

When these second order differential equations are transformed into the sys-

tem of first order differential equations (27-31), the characteristic equations,

which underlie (1), velocity variables will appear as the dependent variables

of the first order system as a result of the transformation. Consequently, the

transport equation which overlies the first order system will have a partial

derivative for each velocity variable (in addition to the partial derivatives

which are wrt the spatial variables), and the coefficient which multiplies the

partial derivative will be the rhs of the corresponding equation in the ode

system. We shall find that one of the coefficients which multiplies a partial

derivative wrt to a velocity variable can be associated with the centrifugal

force that appears in a spherical coordinate formulation of Newton’s second

law.

In §2, we shall derive (1) by the methods of differential geometry. We shall solve

the characteristic equations of (1) by the ‘change of variable’ method which

transforms the characteristic equations into a system of differential equations

which is easy to solve because the right hand sides of the transformed equa-

tions are simple functions of the independent variable. In §3, we shall derive

the dependent variables of the transformed system from the mathematical

 6

properties of a straight line. In §4, we shall present the solution to the charac-

teristic equations of (1), and shall compare it to the solution obtained by the

Runge-Kutta method. In §5, we shall present a conservative finite difference

approximation of (1), and an algorithm to solve the system of finite difference

equations. In §6, we shall compare the numerical solution to (1) to the solution

to the cylindrical coordinate formulation of the transport equation in order to

illustrate the absence of ray-effects in the solution of the transport equation

formulated in spherical coordinates. We shall summarize the main points of

this paper in §7.

3 The properties of a straight line which motivate the solution to

 the characteristic equations.

characteristic equations. The properties, which are functions of coordinate

and velocity variables, can be divided into two groups. The first derivative

in the second group is a constant. The functions in the first group are: the

magnitude of the angular momentum, and the z-component of the angular

momentum. On the other hand, the functions in the second group are: r · Ω,

the z-component of the position vector, and the z-component of r × (r × Ω).

7 Discussion

In the following paper in this volume of this journal [4], we present a method,

which systematizes and extends the method developed in this paper, to ob-

 50

tain the transport equation for two types of confocal ellipsoidal coordinate

systems. The straightforward but tedious application of differential calculus

used in this paper to derive Newton’s second law in spherical coordinates is

unwieldy in confocal ellipsoidal coordinates, because the equations which re-

late the Cartesian coordinates to the ellipsoidal coordinates are more difficult

than the equations that relate the Cartesian coordinates to the spherical co-

ordinates. Newton’s second law in confocal ellipsoidal coordinates is obtained

with far less algebra in [4] by starting from Lagrange’s formulation of Newton’s

second law which is especially appropriate for handling generalized coordinate

systems. The parametrization of r′ in (22) by the unit sphere is formalized in

[4] as an imposition of a constraint on the magnitude of r′ . When this con-

straint is built into Lagrange’s equations for a free particle, the recasting of

Lagrange’s equations as a first order system developed in [4] yields a system

of five first order equations. The recasting of Lagrange’s equations by [4] is

different from the recasting of Lagrange’s equations by Hamilton’s method,

because Hamilton’s method, which is an unconstrained method, results in a

system of six first order equations. Furthermore (1) can be derived with far

less reliance on intuition by the method of [4] than by the method of this

paper.

We should also mention that the relationships among the unit vectors of the

spherical coordinate system (er , eθ , eϕ ) and the unit vectors of the Cartesian

coordinate system (ex , ey , ez ), which appear ‘to be pulled out of thin air’ in

(6-8), can be derived by the method used by [4] to obtain the relationships

among the unit vectors of a confocal ellipsoidal system and the unit vectors

of the Cartesian coordinate system.
