ABSTRACT
The segregation of alloying elements in rapidly solidified 316L stainless steel produced using additive
manufacturing technologies is widely reported throughout literature. The microstructure of thin
walls manufactured from 316L stainless steel using laser powder bed fusion are examined for the
presence of alloy segregation, i.e. the rejection of solutes to grain boundaries. Metallographic
methods including mechanical polishing and chemical etching are used to prepare samples for
microanalysis measurements of the sectioned component. Results reveal the material has high
microstructural fidelity, uniform composition and phase content across grains as well as powder
layer boundaries within the thin wall. The results are attributed to a uniform thermal gradient at the
part scale during rapid solidification.

1. INTRODUCTION
Rapid solidification of metal alloys is again of interest as attributable to the prevalence of additively
manufactured (AM) structures in engineered applications. The use of AM has become attractive
where highly complex geometries are highly desirable, and otherwise unachievable using other
manufacturing technologies. Laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) is a particularly popular AM
technology to achieve these objectives, providing a method for composite material blends [1] as well
as prescribed [2] microstructures and properties. Thin wall structures that are just a few-tenths of a
millimeter thick are possible to produce using LPBF. However, significant variation of mechanical
properties including strength and elongation-to-failure are observed and intrinsically related to
variations in both section thickness and the build direction with respect to the component geometry
[3]. Significant mechanical property variation within a single LPBF component poses distinct
challenges for engineering applications that must be further investigated.
It’s well understood from manufacturing technologies as casting and welding that the solidification
rate of the metal governs the resulting microstructure, which governs its mechanical behavior, i.e.
the process-structure-property relationships. To elucidate the intrinsic links in these relationships for
rapidly solidified 316L stainless steel, metallographic characterization techniques are typically applied
to assess the role of microstructural features. This investigation includes microstructural feature
analysis to reveal grain morphology, subgrain refinements, crystalline phase, and composition
throughout the microstructure. In particular, attention is paid to transitions that may occur across
grain boundaries and sub grains to determine whether compositionally homogenous, 316L stainless
steel can be produced in thin wall sections – a key feature to building complex engineered structures.
In the present study, we utilize scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and laser interferometry along
with metallographic optical imaging to reveal surface finish, grain morphology, and microstructure
of thin-walled sections of 316L austenitic stainless steel produced using LPBF. SEM electron back
scattering diffraction (EBSD) is used to assess crystalline phase formation. Electron dispersive
spectroscopy (EDS) is used in trace analysis, to determine elemental composition and to assess
variation within the thin wall section. Particular care is required during metallographic preparation to
ensure the sample is not damaged, leading to the use of electropolishing to prepare pristine surfaces
for observation. It is found that microstructural and compositional homogenous 316L stainless steel
can be produced using LPBF, which contrasts to observations of segregation. The result of this
desirable homogeneity is that an additively manufactured 316L stainless steel has a comparable
hardness to wrought 316L stainless steel. Furthermore, a hypothesis is provided for the origin of this
homogeneity considering the relationship to its thin wall geometry.

2. EXPERIMENTAL METHODS
2.1. Sample Synthesis
The preparation of thin-wall, right-circular cylinders is accomplished using LPBF on a 3D Systems,
Inc. PROX300 Additive Manufacturing Metal 3D-printer as equipped with a 500 W laser and a
powder layering roller that promotes powder particle compaction to facilitate laser energy transfer
between particles at a faster rate. The nominal composition (wt.%) of the 316L alloying elements in
the source powder stock are 16-18 Cr, 10-14 Ni, 2-3 Mo, < 2 Mn, < 0.75 Si, and balance Fe. The
presence of Mo distinguishes 316L from the 304L alloy. The cylinder wall thickness is only 400 μm,
whereas the height and radius of the cylinder are 5.5 mm. LPBF synthesis occurs under a controlled
inert atmosphere of argon with <250 ppm of oxygen to minimize any oxidation during
solidification. The contours/outline feature of the PROX300 is used to build the thin walls instead
of utilizing a hatch strategy. Build parameters of a lower beam energy and scan speed enhance laser
stability and accuracy. To establish boundary overlap (also known as skywriting or compensation)
before setting the laser parameters, it’s verified that the vectors aren’t passing through the outlines.
If the vectors did pass the outlines, then the surface finish can be affected adversely. If the outlines
aren’t contacted, then a result of internal porosity may occur. Scan adjustments are made since a 45°
rotation is used after each layer. The laser parameters used during thin wall synthesis include a 200
W power, 1000 mms-1 scan speed, 50 μm spot size, and 40 μm powder layer thickness. The thin-
wall cylinders are grown orthogonally to the build plate with an 11x11 layout array of samples. Only
three passes of the incident laser are needed to fuse the powders across the 400 μm wide thin wall.
Microscopy of witness coupons using these LPBF build parameters show evidence of only 0.35%
and 0.28% porosity in the longitudinal (build direction) and transverse (build plane) section views,
respectively.

2.2. Metallography
The 316L LPBF AM samples are vacuum potted in an epoxy resin and metallographically sectioned
along the build axis to reveal a radial view of the thin walls. The cross-sectioned surface is examined
after vibratory polishing and as electrochemical etching progresses with time. The mechanical
polishing process progresses using: 400, 600, 1200, and 4000 grit silicon carbide (SiC) for intervals of
30-120 s at each step; followed by 6 and 1 μmdiamond for intervals of 300 s each; colloidal silica for
300 s; and then a final vibratory polish for 1 h with a colloidal-silica suspension (at a pH ~9) that can
induce some topological relief as dependent upon grain orientation. The electrochemical etch is
performed using a solution composition by volume of 55 ml H20, 60 ml 15.8M (70%) HNO3, and
15 ml 0.1M HCl. The 316L AM sample is the anode in the electrochemical cell where the etch is
performed under a driving potential of 1.15V for time periods of 7, 90, and 120 s at a current density
of ~50 mAmm-2.
The surface topology is measured at each step of the mechanical polishing and chemical etching
processes to map the changes in surface roughness. The laser scanning confocal microscopy (LSCM)
method combines white light with a laser light source. A Keyence VK-250 LSCM is used to scan the
surface to acquire optical images and high-resolution topology data. The heights z(x,y) of surface
features over the x-y image plane are measured by analyzing the reflected laser intensity relative to
the z-position of the laser source. The surface is scanned with C-Laser digital interference contrast
(DIC) to provide both gray-scale and color-scale-limited surface images. The surface area A of the
polished material scanned is 1.5 mm2. The values computed for the Keyence LSCM area scans are
quantified using standard formulations [4] for the maximum height Sz, the arithmetic mean height Sa

and the root-mean-square (rms) height Sq. A laser interferometer is used for smaller-area higher-
resolution line scans of corresponding roughness (Rz, Ra, and Rq) over a length L as taken with a 0.2
𝜇𝑚-step increment. A Veeco WYKO NT3300 profilometer is used to measure the surface
roughness computed [4] as the arithmetic average of absolute values of the profile height deviation
from the maximum Rz as well as to compute mean Ra and root-mean-square (rms) Rq values.
Vickers microhardness measurements Hv are taken using a 10g load at six positions along the
centerline of the thin-wall at 125-175 μm separations from the metallographic sample cross-section.
The indenter tip is a four-sided diamond pyramid with an internal angle of 136°. For an average
indent diameter d, the microhardness Hv equals 1.8544∙P/d2.

2.3. Scanning Electron Microscopy
The elemental composition is measured in a scanning electron microscope (SEM) using electron
dispersive spectrometry (EDS). A JEOL 7600 (equipped with an Oxford Silicon Drift Detector) is
operated at 20 keV and used for conducting a trace analysis across the full section of the thin wall
section. An FEI Apreo loVac SEM (equipped with a Bruker XFlash 6160 detector) is operated at 15
keV and used for acquiring finer spatial resolution in area maps. The intensity of characteristic x-rays
generated off the sample surface from the exposure to the incident electron beam is used to
determine the elemental composition of the material. The analysis accounts for the atomic number
(Z), absorption (A), and fluorescence (F) excitation effects. One characteristic peak designated by
the Siegbahn notation is selected for each element to determine the semi-quantitative composition
measurement. The Kα x-ray peaks of Fe at 6.40 keV, Cr at 5.41 keV, Ni at 7.47 keV, Mn at 5.90
keV, and Si at 1.74 keV are first used for each element except for Mo that uses the 2.29 keV Lα
peak. Carbon and oxygen are considered contaminants and excluded from the semiquantitative
surface analysis. For example, if the oxygen Kα x-ray peak at 0.525 keV is included, then computed
values are <0.3 wt.%. Electron back-scatter diffraction (EBSD) is used to determine phase content
using scattered electrons in the reflected mode. The EBSD maps are acquired using a 70° specimen
tilt. In principle, the spacing and geometric pattern for an array of ‘parallel’ bands are used to assess
the crystalline symmetry and lattice spacing of the diffracting volume to determine the crystalline
phase and orientation. From the collected diffraction data, inverse-pole figures (IPF) are generated
as a color-coded map of crystalline orientation within the axial section view.

3.2. Vickers Microhardness
Vickers microhardness measurements were obtained from the LPBF 316L stainless steel samples to
further probe the microstructural homogeneity. For sample no. 39, Vicker microhardness indents
obtained [4] with a 10gf load had an average diagonal is 9.620.22 𝜇𝑚 and a corresponding Vickers
microhardness Hv value of 200.89.3 kgf/mm2 (1.970.09 GPa). For comparison, 33 microhardness
measurements along all three principal directions of bulk 316L AM samples using a 25 g indenter
load had an average value of 208.822.9 kgf/mm2 (2.050.10 GPa). These bulk measurement values
compare well with the average value of 201.29.5 kgf/mm2 (1.970.09 GPa) for the thin wall
section. Differences in the Vickers microhardness are consistent with the effects of applying 1000
C anneal treatments [14] where values for LPBF 316L AM material decrease from 26013 to 2057
kgf/mm2 (2.550.13 to 2.010.07 GPa).

4. DISCUSSION
The columnar growth morphology seen in Figs. 1-2 is common [8-9] for 316L AM material along
the build direction, and the formation of Figs. 5-6 subcellular grain structure as reported for rapidly
solidified 316L AM [10-12]. The composition values for the 316L thin-wall AM structures listed in
Table 1 are consistent with comparable source 316L powder stock wherein inductively coupled
plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) measurements [13] reveal (wt.%) values of 67.80.3 Fe,
16.840.34 Cr, 10.810.22 Ni, 2.050.20 Mo, 1.200.12 Mn, 0.650.10 Si, 0.210.03 Cu, and
0.100.02 Co. This present result is unique as compared to findings often reported in the literature.
A subcellular structure within 316L AM is reported [15] from high-resolution transmission electron
microscopy (HRTEM) images with <10 nm Mn-Si-O particles and 4 wt.% enrichments of Cr within
20-40 nm wide regions of grain boundaries. STEM EDS maps and atom probe tomography show
[12] small amounts (~0.5-1 at.%) of Cr and Mo enrichment at boundaries as attributed to dislocation
cell wall structure in as-deposited LPBF 316L AM. In another 316L study, only 20nm thick bands of
Cr, Mo, Ni and Mn enrichment were imaged by TEM EDS [16] at the boundaries of 0.2 μm sub-
cells. Bands of Cr and Mo enrichment 30-50 nm wide were reported as well in LBPF 316L [17]
using scanning TEM analysis at boundaries of 0.2-0.4 μm sub-cells. The formation of Mn-Si-oxides
between laser melt regions can be resolved [18] in 316L LPBF material at the 0.1-0.5 μm scale, along
with elemental variations in composition across finer-scale discrete boundaries in material subjected
to pitting corrosion. The effects of composition segregation within selected laser melted (SLM) 316L
can be significant as Cr variation is reported [19] to change yield strength through crystal plasticity
modeling.
At present, there is no statistically significant evidence for composition segregation as revealed by
the EDS line trace of Fig. 3a across the full cross-section to the thin walls of the 316L LPBF AM
material. In addition, there is a lack of sub-micron composition segregation either within or across
the grain boundaries as seen at the finer scale 60-80 nm resolution attained using characteristic La x-
ray peaks as seen in Fig. 7.
The presence or lack of composition segregation is dependent upon the processing conditions of the
source material. The effects of macro segregation by solute rejection at subcellular and grain
boundaries is reported [20] in the directional solidification of Pb-Sn alloys under a thermal gradient.
This result supports an assertion that a thermal gradient during rapid solidification of AM materials
is needed for solute segregation to boundaries in ultra-fine scale microstructures. Microscopic
modeling efforts [21] to demonstrate regrowth from laser induced melting show solute uptake at the
liquid-solid interface during directional solidification. The solute concentration at the solid-liquid
interface is higher than its equilibrium composition. A transition to solute trapping from equilibrium
segregation occurs as the velocity of the solidification front exceeds the diffusive speed of the solute
in the liquid phase. The localization of solute enrichment is reported [22] as well for an Al-20 wt.%
Cu alloy during its directional solidification. Fragmentation of the dendritic microstructure occurs at
the solidification front from arrival of the solute-rich liquid phase. Loss of solute, in turn, causes a
remelting of the dendritic root that furthers a detachment of dendritic branches.
A postulate for the segregation of high melt temperature (Tm) elements to grain and cell boundaries
should consider the melt process itself during the 316L AM synthesis. If the centers of the powders
are at higher temperature during the melt process than the exterior surface, then the higher melting
point Tm elements would migrate under the thermal gradient to solidify, i.e. move towards the
cooler surface of the boundary exterior. Temperature gradients that form at the solid-liquid interface
would further drive cellular structure formation and solute rejection from the tips of the

 15

solidification front. For 316L stainless steel, segregation around the cellular structures would create
an enrichment of Cr and Mo at the boundaries, wherein the extent of the temperature gradient
enhances solute segregation within the melt layer.
The uniform composition in the thin wall section at present indicates uniform heating in a
controlled melt-to-rapid solidification of the powder layer has occurred without discrete temperature
gradients within the melt layer. Rapid solidification of the thin-walled 316L does produce the
subcellar dendritic AM microstructure independent of micro-segregation. The lack of detectable
segregation is indicative of the fast solidification rate associated with the AM process without
thermal gradients. This uniform cooling during rapid solidification may be a result of the thin wall
structure itself functioning to radiate heat. The uniformity within the microstructure of the thin wall
sections of 316L as achieved using the LPBF AM process is optimized to ensure a high-density solid
(<0.2% porosity) with minimal surface roughness. Attention was given to use of the
contours/outline build feature to solidify 0.4mm thin walls using a lower beam energy and scan
speed that enhance laser stability and positional accuracy. With a 45° rotation after each layer,
attention is paid to control beam path for ensuring boundary overlap and avoid straying past
boundary outlines. The lack of latent segregation as function of build height demonstrates that a
high degree of temperature management was achieved during the synthesis process without thermal
gradients at the part scale. The wrought-like mechanical behavior evidenced through the lower
Vickers microhardness values of ~2 GPa is again representative of the homogeneous composition
measured throughout the microstructure.

5. SUMMARY
The additive manufacturing of thin wall structures with high structural fidelity is accomplished using
a laser powder bed fusion process. The microstructure of a 316L AM thin wall section, only 0.4 mm
thick, is investigated as sampled from a cylindrical shell. The metallographic preparation procedure
previously developed [4] produces smooth surfaces with only a 0.3 μm rms roughness across the
entire section for conducting microscopy and microanalysis. Scanning electron microscopy reveals a
columnar growth from the rapid solidification of powders in the build direction with a constituent
submicron-cellular structure. Electron back scattering diffraction reveals an austenitic phase without
evidence of secondary crystalline phases. Microanalysis measurements of the component using
characteristic x-rays of energy dispersive spectroscopy reveals the microstructure has a
homogeneous composition and phase content across grains as well as powder layer boundaries
within the thin wall. This is accomplished without evidence of solute rejection, even to the finer
spatial resolution on the order of 50 nm. These present results confirm the prospective use of
additive manufacturing to produce structural components with fine scale features in a homogeneous
microstructure and with established mechanical property behavior.
