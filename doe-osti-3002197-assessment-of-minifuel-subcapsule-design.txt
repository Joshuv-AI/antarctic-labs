 ABSTRACT

MiniFuel describes the class of separate effects nuclear fuels irradiation experiments that have been
conducted in the High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR) since 2018. These experiments comprise a stack of
six fuel-bearing subcapsules contained in a stainless steel target housing that is in contact with HFIR
coolant on its exterior. All MiniFuel targets have a near-standardized architecture, and the primary design
variables that change between experiments are the radial gap size between the subcapsule and housing
and the target fill gas composition. Finite element heat transfer models are used to determine the optimum
gas composition and gap sizes, and recent studies were performed to identify model parameters that
contribute the most uncertainty to fuel specimen temperature predictions. That work, which is referenced
herein, also recommended a set of design modifications to the subcapsule internal architecture and
assembly process. These modifications are intended to reduce fuel temperature uncertainty in future
experiments. In this report, the subcapsule design modifications were retroactively applied to a previously
conducted MiniFuel experiment to determine how these changes affect the established safety and
performance envelope of the experimental capability. These effects were determined in two steps. First,
the modifications were applied to the subcapsule design without any other changes to determine their
isolated effect on the predicted fuel specimen temperatures. This portion of the analysis showed that fuel
temperatures were modestly reduced because the implemented changes improved heat transfer efficacy.
Next, traditional MiniFuel design activities (i.e., sizing the gas gaps and determining the fill gas
composition) were reperformed, and they confirmed that the original desired fuel temperatures could be
achieved while remaining within established safety limits. Therefore, this report demonstrates improved
performance resulting from the subcapsule modifications, which mitigate uncertainty while meeting the
objectives of past experiments. An additional benefit of the design changes is reduced sensitivity of the
fuel temperature to the evolving flux spectrum in HFIR, leading to more stable temperatures and
enhanced utility of MiniFuel as a separate effects irradiation platform.

 1. INTRODUCTION

MiniFuel is a separate effects nuclear fuels irradiation capability developed at Oak Ridge National
Laboratory (ORNL) for deployment in the High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR) to enable accelerated
irradiation testing. Each MiniFuel target comprises a stack of six individually sealed fuel-bearing
subcapsules held within a stainless steel target housing that is in contact with HFIR’s coolant. The initial
capability [1] was deployed in the inner small vertical experiment facilities (VXFs) in HFIR’s permanent
reflector. This capability has more recently been extended for deployment in the removable beryllium
(RB) reflector, nearer to HFIR’s driver fuel [2]. Dozens of MiniFuel targets containing a variety of fuel
forms have been irradiated since the initial test in 2018. MiniFuel targets are designed using predictive
models, and pertinent irradiation parameters (i.e., temperature and discharge burnup) are verified post
irradiation. The agreement between the design temperatures and results from the SiC dilatometry method
[3] used for post irradiation temperature verification varies between experiments, potentially highlighting
uncertainty in the numerical methods. As the capability continues to be increasingly leveraged, a need for
understanding the uncertainty in the predicted temperatures has become increasingly important.

To address this need, Meehan et al. [4, 5] used robust sensitivity and uncertainty analysis techniques to
identify the modeling input parameters that contribute the most variance to the end temperature
predictions and then explored design changes internal to the subcapsule to reduce uncertainty. The first of
these studies [4] indicates that fuel specimen temperature uncertainties are ±40°C for targets irradiated in
the VXF positions and ±80°C for those in RB. The recommended design changes, described in detail in
the second paper by Meehan et al. [5] and reiterated in Section 2 of this report, reduce uncertainty in the
thermal contact conductance (TCC) between the fuel and surrounding components and, by effect, reduce
the uncertainty in fuel temperature predictions to ±32°C and ±53°C for the VXF and RB capabilities,

 1

respectively. Reducing TCC uncertainty required enhancing the absolute value of this parameter, thereby
improving heat transfer efficacy from the fuel to the heat sink. A potential byproduct of these changes is
that the maximum achievable fuel temperatures may be reduced. The RB MiniFuel capability in particular
lends itself to conducting high-temperature irradiations, and the objective of this report is to quantify the
effects that these design changes may have on TCC and the absolute fuel temperature in an RB MiniFuel
experiment.

To achieve this objective, a previous RB MiniFuel experiment was reassessed in two steps. First, the
design recommendations by Meehan et al. [5] were implemented with no other changes to determine how
the fuel temperatures would be affected. Detailed analysis on how these changes affect TCC at relevant
material interfaces is also included in this report. Second, the traditional MiniFuel design workflow was
reconducted with the new design features implemented to determine whether the original goals of the
experiment could be achieved within the established safety bounds for RB MiniFuel. Of particular
concern is the size of the gas gap between each subcapsule and the stainless steel housing. There are
limits on the size of this gap, and it is possible that if TCC is enhanced, the original design temperatures
may not be achievable using allowable gap sizes. There may also be a safety concern regarding increased
gas pressures, so this report discusses the potential effects of the design modifications on the design and
safety of future MiniFuel experiments.

 2. BACKGROUND

2.1 BRIEF OVERVIEW OF MINIFUEL

The details of MiniFuel have been published elsewhere (e.g., [1, 2]); therefore, a brief description of
details relevant to this study is provided here. In summary, MiniFuel targets contain a set of six
subcapsules that each contain miniature fuel specimen(s) sitting on a dish made of molybdenum, and a
molybdenum heater tube is placed above the dish. Most of the subcapsule heat load is generated within
the fuel dish, the tube, and the subcapsule holder (molybdenum or titanium) via absorption of gamma rays
emitted from HFIR’s fuel. Silicon carbide thermometry (TM) rods are placed in the heater tube center
hole for passive temperature measurements conducted post irradiation. Grafoil insulator disks are fitted
between the heater tube and endcap to secure the heater tube, accommodate thermal expansion, and
mitigate heat transfer to the endcap. The molybdenum or titanium endcap is welded onto the holder and
then backfilled with helium gas at atmospheric pressure through a small hole in the center of the cap,
which is then seal-welded to create a hermetic seal. The subcapsules are aligned within the stainless steel
target housing using centering thimbles and end springs. Lastly, the target housing is backfilled with a
mixture of helium and argon and seal-welded. Figure 1 shows a rendering of a MiniFuel target and
subcapsule.

Variable gas gaps between the subcapsules and housing in combination with the fill gas composition
create a thermal resistance that can be tailored to achieve the desired fuel temperature. Large gas gaps
could cause excessive temperatures and melting within the subcapsules, so internal safety calculations
have established bounds on the allowable gas gap size and minimum helium content in the fill gas
mixture. The current bounds are summarized in Table 1. Because the VXF positions are located farther
from HFIR’s driver fuel, the peak temperature is lower in VXF MiniFuel than for the RB capability and
less helium is required to meet safety criteria. A broader range of gas gap sizes is allowed for RB
MiniFuel because this capability was introduced later and lessons learned from earlier experiments were
considered in the safety analysis. Future safety analysis could potentially broaden the bounds for either
capability.

The traditional workflow for designing MiniFuel targets is to calculate the internal heat generation rate
(HGR) within every experimental component using a radiation transport model and then import these
HGRs into a finite element target model that predicts temperature distributions. Aside from the target
position within the reactor, which influences the HGRs, the primary design variables that affect fuel
temperatures are the size of subcapsule–target gas gaps and the target fill gas composition. These
parameters are iteratively adjusted within the safety bounds until the desired time-average volume-
average (TAVA) fuel temperatures are achieved. This optimization accounts for the temporal variation in

 3

temperatures resulting from evolving fuel isotopic composition and HFIR control element movements.
Relative to the VXF positions, the closer proximity of the RB positions to HFIR’s driver fuel leads to
greater magnitude and time-dependent variation of neutron and gamma fluxes—and, in turn, HGRs. This
increased sensitivity to evolving flux conditions causes greater uncertainty in model-predicted fuel
temperatures in RB positions, as shown in previous work [4].

2.2 TECHNICAL BASIS FOR PROPOSED DEISGN MODIFICATIONS

The sensitivity and uncertainty analysis conducted by Meehan et al. [4] used Morris screening and Sobol
methods to identify and rank the model input parameters that contribute the most to fuel temperature
uncertainty. Two pairs of Sobol analyses were conducted in that work, where each pair focused on each
irradiation facility (RB or VXF). Within each pair, one analysis included the primary design features (gas
gap thickness and fill gas composition), and the other omitted any design features that would in practice
be inspected during the experiment assembly. The analyses that considered the design features showed
that these parameters are in fact the most dominant inputs that dictate the output fuel temperature. This is
an important takeaway because it verifies that the engineered features of MiniFuel greatly outweigh
uncertainty in other inputs. The portion of the analysis that omitted design features showed that
uncertainty in molybdenum HGR accounts for the most variance in the fuel temperature and that TCC
between the fuel and dish is the second greatest contributor. Although the molybdenum HGR was found
to be the dominant parameter contributing to uncertainty, the follow-up paper by Meehan et al. [5]
focused on mitigating uncertainty contributed by the TCC.

There were several reasons for not targeting uncertainty reductions in the molybdenum HGR. First, a
9% uncertainty was assigned to the molybdenum HGR. The uncertainty is based on the maximum
neutron flux tilt allowed in HFIR before the reactor scrams [6]. This is a conservative number that bounds
the actual uncertainty assuming that there is not a HFIR scram caused by excessive flux tilt during a
MiniFuel irradiation. The Monte-Carlo-based tools used for reactor simulation typically output HGRs on
the order of < 2% statistical uncertainty (actual value dependent upon the Monte Carlo inputs used by the
analyst), so the true uncertainty on the molybdenum HGR is between the statistical uncertainty and 9%.
Second, variations in the HFIR flux distribution, and therefore the HGRs in experiments, are caused by
cycle-to-cycle variation in the HFIR fuel elements and the presence of neighboring experiments. There is
no tractable approach to robustly determine the true uncertainty of HGRs because the details of each
HFIR fuel element are not public, and it is not possible to forecast the experimental loading during the
experiment design phase that occurs months or more prior to the irradiation. Chandler et al. [6] developed
a model of HFIR that contains a representative experiment loading consisting of routine experiments and
isotope production targets, but this model cannot possibly account for all unique experiment designs that
could perturb the neutron flux distribution. Lastly, the experimenter has little control over the HFIR
experimental loading. At best, any neighboring experiments that may be suspected of significantly
perturbing the neutron flux could be accounted for in simulations conducted post hoc.

Uncertainty mitigation efforts therefore focused on the TCC between the fuel and surrounding
components because these factors can be controlled via targeted design. In the original MiniFuel design,
subcapsules are backfilled with helium at atmospheric pressure and seal-welded. The fuel specimen(s)
rest on the dish, and a small gas plenum exists above the fuel, meaning that heat generated in the fuel
predominantly transfers downward through the dish before flowing radially outward to the heat sink.

These equations are included here to illustrate the effect that several controllable parameters, namely gas
pressure, contact pressure, and surface roughness, have on TCC. As further discussed in Section 4.1, these
conductance values are extremely sensitive in configurations with low gas pressure, low contact pressure,
and rough surfaces. Therefore, two key design changes were recommended to shift the resulting total
TCC into less sensitive regimes:

 1. Increase the subcapsule backfill pressure at room temperature from atmospheric (~0.1 MPa) to
 0.5 MPa.
 2. Increase the solid spot conductance to > 1 × 104 W/m2-K through a combination of applied contact
 pressure and reduced effective surface roughness. Contact pressure on the order of a few
 megapascals is needed to achieve the minimum value for solid spot conductance assuming realistic
 values for effective surface roughness.

If the first recommendation alone were implemented, heat transfer from the fuel to the heater tube above
it (through the true gas gap) and to the dish below (through the effective gap created by surface defects)
would both increase. This change requires no modifications to the subcapsule geometry; it requires only a
change to the assembly procedure. Implementing the second recommendation would require a change to
the mechanical design because there are no forces aside from gravity acting on the fuel specimen in the
current configuration. The simplest approach to implement this recommendation would be to alter the
dish geometry to raise the upper surface of the fuel above the dish, putting it in direct contact with the
heater tube. Then, additional grafoil spacers could be added so that a force must be applied to fully seat
the endcap, and the capsule could be welded in this preloaded condition. An example of this modified
geometry is given in Figure 2. Additionally, the fuel specimen and the contacting molybdenum parts
could be polished to reduce their respective surface roughness.

 3. METHODOLOGY

To understand the effect of the design changes on predicted fuel temperatures, the modifications were
implemented into the 2D axisymmetric (2DA) subcapsule model developed by Meehan et al. [4]. This
model was used to simulate the full irradiation history of the first MiniFuel experiment deployed in the
RB reflector. The goals and design specifications of that experiment are summarized by Gorton et al. [2].
That experiment comprised two targets, designated as RB01 and RB02, with all subcapsules containing a
disc specimen of UO2 doped with chromia (Cr2O3). Although that experiment was designed using a 3D
quarter-symmetric model of the full target, the 2DA model was shown to predict fuel temperatures that
agreed with the reference values within 28°C [4]. The 2DA model with modified geometry is shown in
Figure 3.

 7

 Figure 3. 2DA ANSYS subcapsule model used to evaluate the recommended design changes.

Per the design recommendations, the initial gas pressure assumed in the model was increased from
0.1 MPa to 0.5 MPa. The ideal gas law was used to estimate the gas pressure at the expected irradiation
temperature. A contact pressure of 6 MPa was applied at the fuel-to-dish and fuel-to-heater interfaces, and
a surface roughness of 1.125 m was applied to the contacting surfaces of the fuel, dish, and heater,
equating to an effective surface roughness of 1.6 m at each interface. In practice, a force of 42.4 N
(~9.5 lbf) would need to be applied to a 3 mm diameter fuel specimen to establish this contact pressure.
This combination of contact pressure and surface characteristics provides the recommended minimum
solid spot conductance value of 1 × 104 W/m2-K; additional contact pressure and finer surface finishes
could be applied to further enhance this value.

The effect of the design recommendations was assessed in several steps. First, the irradiation histories of
all subcapsules were simulated with the applied recommendations while keeping the same target fill gas
mixtures and gap distances between the subcapsules and the target inner wall as the original design. This
portion of the evaluation was intended to illustrate the reduction in fuel temperatures caused by the
modifications to the gas pressure, contact pressure, and surface characteristics. Then, the outer diameter
(OD) of each subcapsule was iteratively adjusted to achieve the desired TAVA fuel specimen
temperatures for the experiment (1,075°C for all specimens). This analysis was conducted to ensure that
the resulting combination of fill gas composition and subcapsule–target gas gap thickness remained
within the established safety bounds given in Table 1.

The effects of the design modifications on TCC are accounted for in the ANSYS models via the macro
libraries developed by McDuffee [7]. To further illustrate how the design changes mitigate uncertainty,
Python models were developed using the same formulations to rapidly predict TCC and its constituent
parts for a variety of pressure and surface characteristics. These models were used to compare the original

 8

and modified design conditions and demonstrate how the changes simultaneously enhance the TCC and
reduce temperature uncertainty.

 4. RESULTS & DISCUSSION

4.1 DESIGN IMPLICATIONS ON TCC

Regarding TCC between the fuel and dish, the original MiniFuel design has the following characteristics:

  Gas pressure of 1 atm at room temperature that is increased based on the expected irradiation
 temperature and the ideal gas law
  Unknown fuel and dish surface roughness values
  Contact pressure applied via the weight of the fuel sitting on the dish (effectively nil).

These conditions create an uncertain state for several reasons. First, the operational gas pressure is based
on the expected irradiation temperature, but fairly large temperature gradients (~100°C–200°C) exist
within the subcapsule, making it difficult to precisely determine the average gas temperature. As shown in
Figure 4, the thermal jump distance of helium, 𝑔, is quite sensitive to both the gas pressure and
temperature, spanning several orders of magnitude with an inflection point in the vicinity of 0.1–0.2 MPa.
Above 0.5 MPa, 𝑔 begins to converge on a single value for all gas temperatures, meaning that error in the
predicted subcapsule gas pressure and temperature will contribute less uncertainty to the gas gap
conductance.

The third reason that the original MiniFuel design exists within an uncertain state is that with the solid
spot conductance effectively being near zero, the total TCC is dominated by the gas gap conductance,
which is small in comparison with magnitude of the heat flux. This leads to a large Δ𝑇 across the
interface, and—for reasons already discussed—this value is highly uncertain, leading to a scenario where
the TCC is both a high-impact and highly uncertain parameter. The recommended design changes greatly
enhance the TCC, thereby reducing the Δ𝑇. Furthermore, the geometric design changes needed to apply
pressure onto the fuel specimen create an additional pathway for heat removal from the fuel. Nearly all
the heat generated in the fuel flows through the fuel–dish interface in the original design, whereas roughly
half of the heat load is expected to flow through the fuel–tube interface in the modified design. This
effectively cuts the peak heat flux in half, in turn reducing the Δ𝑇 at each interface by a factor of 2.

Although the operational gas temperature/pressure and the acting contact pressure (materials thermally
expand) will remain difficult to precisely quantify, errors in these parameters will have a diminished
effect on the resulting temperature predictions. These combined effects are shown in Figure 6, which
plots the predicted Δ𝑇 as a function of 𝜎𝑒𝑓𝑓 for a variety of gas and contact pressure inputs representing
the original and modified MiniFuel designs. For the original design, the contact pressure was set to 0, and

 10

the gas pressure was varied within ±20% of the expected operational pressure of a subcapsule filled to 1
atm at room temperature and then elevated to 900°C (about 4 atm). For the modified design, the contact
pressure was varied between 4.8 and 7.2 MPa (the recommended 6 MPa ±20%), and the gas pressure was
varied between 1.8 and 2.2 MPa (2 MPa ±20%), since 2MPa represents the expected operating pressure
of a subcapsule filled to 0.5 MPa at room temperature and then elevated to 900°C. A heat flux of 1.2
MW/m2 was assumed for the original design, and half that value was used for the modified design to
reflect that approximately half the heat generated in the fuel would transfer downward to the dish and the
other half would transfer upward to the tube. The nominal heat flux was based on a UO2 fuel disc 3 mm in
diameter by 0.3 mm thick generating 375 W/g, which is representative of a typical fuel HGR in RB
MiniFuel [2]. The figure shows that the effect of a 20% error on the assumed operating gas pressure at a
fixed 𝜎𝑒𝑓𝑓 leads to an error in the Δ𝑇 prediction of up to 25°C. In the modified design, the combined
effect of ±20% error on both the gas pressure and the contact pressure leads to an error in Δ𝑇 of
approximately 4°C. The key implication is that the design modifications create a state where large
uncertainties in the inputs lead to very small uncertainty in the output and provide more confidence in the
predicted result.

4.2 EFFECTS OF DESIGN CHANGES ON RB01/RB02

In this section, three iterations of the RB01 and RB02 target designs are presented and referred to as the
original, modified, and recalibrated designs. Original refers to the unaltered design of the experiment
described by Gorton et al. [2], modified refers to the design after the modifications recommended by
Meehan et al. [5] were implemented with no other changes, and recalibrated refers to the final iteration,
in which the design modifications were applied and the subcapsule ODs were adjusted to achieve the
desired TAVA fuel temperature of 1,075°C. The range of average fuel specimen temperatures across all
six subcapsules is plotted for each design iteration of RB01 and RB02 in Figure 7 and Figure 8,
respectively. Fewer time steps were evaluated in the original model, so the modified/recalibrated model
curves have slightly different shapes. The original design was developed assuming nominal fuel

dimensions (3 mm diameter × 0.3 mm thick), but the presented results reflect updated predictions that
were made using the as-fabricated fuel dimensions. This leads to the larger temperature variation across
subcapsules in the original and modified designs. The recalibrated design was developed with the as-
fabricated dimensions from the start, leading to less temperature variation across the subcapsules.

The recalibrated design was developed using the as-fabricated fuel dimensions, but the calibration was

shown in Table 2 and Table 3, which summarize the TAVA fuel and TM temperatures across all three

When the subcapsule geometry is adjusted to accommodate contact between the top surface of the fuel
and the bottom surface of the heater, more heat flows in the direction of the TM rod. With this increase in
heat flow, the TAVA TM temperatures increase closer to the TAVA fuel temperatures in comparison with
the original design. Based on the temperatures in Table 2 and Table 3, the average difference between the
TAVA fuel and TM temperature is 75.5°C in the original design and 24°C in the recalibrated design. This
may be beneficial for PIE temperature measurements because less dependence on a model is needed to
map the measured TM temperatures to the predicted fuel temperatures. An additional pathway for heat
flow will also lower the temperature gradient across the fuel, decreasing thermal stress and enhancing the
separate effects utility of the MiniFuel platform. Lower thermal stress can mitigate potential cracking and
fragmentation of the fuel [10]. Alternatively, thicker fuel specimens could be used while still obtaining
temperature gradients that are similar to those expected in the original MiniFuel design.

 5. SUMMARY & CONCLUSIONS

Modifications to the MiniFuel subcapsule design that are expected to reduce simulated predictions of the
fuel specimen temperature were retroactively assessed in a previous experiment design. The design
changes inherently enhance heat transfer out of the fuel; they were therefore expected to reduce the
average fuel temperature. Because safety limits exist on the MiniFuel target fill gas composition and the
thickness of the subcapsule–housing gas gap, the aim of this work was to ensure that the objectives of the
previous experiment could still be achieved within the established safety bounds. This work showed that
the design modifications mitigate thermal contact resistances between the fuel and surrounding
components, and reduced uncertainty is achieved by shifting the TCC to a less sensitive regime.

The assessment was based on the first MiniFuel experiment conducted in HFIR’s RB reflector. This
experiment comprised two targets, RB01 and RB02, containing chromia-doped UO2 specimens targeting
a TAVA fuel temperature of 1,075°C. Increasing the subcapsule internal gas pressure and applying a
contact load via an additional heat transfer pathway from the fuel to the subcapsule heater tube were
predicted to decrease the TAVA fuel temperatures by roughly 40°C–70°C. The subcapsule ODs were
recalibrated to achieve the original design temperature of 1,075°C, and the recalibration exercise showed
that the required design changes were still within the established safety bounds for RB MiniFuel. This
work therefore demonstrated an improved design that can achieve high fuel temperatures with reduced
uncertainty of ±53°C. Although some MiniFuel experiments have targeted higher temperatures that may
be challenging to reach with recommended design changes, this could potentially be remedied via
additional safety analysis. An additional benefit resulting from the design modifications is reduced
temperature gradients in the fuel due to a reduced sensitivity to the evolving irradiation conditions in
HFIR. Because the fuel could be irradiated at more isothermal conditions with reduced uncertainty, these
design changes enhance the separate effects utility of MiniFuel.
