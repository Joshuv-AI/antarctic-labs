Unveiling the ternary synergistic mechanism of B–N–Al coordinated graphene for enhanced quantum capacitance via first-principles calculations
Authors: Shasha Jiao, Hui Shen, Chuanyin Xiong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Precise tuning of quantum capacitance remains critical for advancing graphene-based materials in modern energy storage and chemical sensing applications. Employing sophisticated first-principles calculations, we systematically investigated mono-doped, binary-doped, and ternary-doped graphene frameworks to elucidate exactly how compositional motifs govern structural evolution, electronic states, charge redistribution, and capacitive behavior. Structural analysis demonstrates that while mono-doping induces minimal lattice distortion, B–N co-doping generates robust local polarization, and Al incorporation drives substantial out-of-plane geometric deformation. Furthermore, electronic structure evaluations conclusively reveal that mono-doping preserves the intrinsic graphene framework while introducing classical p-type or n-type modulation. Conversely, B–N co-doping yields a more balanced frontier structure featuring a distinct gap-like character near the Fermi level. Crucially, integrating Al comprehensively reconstructs these frontier states, introducing prominent near-Fermi-level states alongside reduced band dispersion, strongly indicating enhanced electronic localization and reactivity. In addition, quantum capacitance evaluations confirm that the B–N–Al framework effectively suppresses parasitic capacitance at negative potentials and aggressively concentrates its electronic activity, producing a sharp, dominant capacitance surge of nearly +0.0125 µF cm−2 at a positive bias of approximately +1.3 V. Charge density difference and Bader charge analyses demonstrate that the ternary system establishes a robust cooperative charge regulation network, seamlessly synergizing B-induced electron deficiency, N-induced electron enrichment, and Al-mediated charge donation. These findings conclusively establish that the superior capacitance of B–N–Al coordinated graphene originates from a genuine ternary synergistic mechanism, directly offering robust theoretical guidelines for rationally designing advanced multicomponent graphene electrodes with highly optimized electrochemical charge responses.

Introduction

Graphene is a two dimensional honeycomb lattice in which the carbon atoms are sp2 hybridized. Owing to its exceptional electronic, mechanical, and thermal properties, graphene has attracted extensive research interest. In particular, the high theoretical specific surface area (∼2630 m2 g−1) and excellent electrical conductivity make graphene an ideal candidate for energy storage systems, especially for electrode applications in supercapacitors.1–3 However, pristine graphene suffers from a relatively low quantum capacitance (CQ) due to its linear density of states (DOS) near the Dirac point. This intrinsic constraint is particularly pronounced in the negative potential window, where the DOS increases more slowly with energy than in the positive potential region because of electron–hole asymmetry.4–6 Therefore, the charge storage capacity of pristine graphene is restricted, most notably in the negative potential window. Beyond conventional carbon-based frameworks, the rational design of energy storage devices, especially advanced supercapacitors, heavily relies on a diverse array of typical electrode materials. Recent advancements have highlighted the exceptional properties of MXenes, transition metal oxides (e.g., CoO2), transition metal dichalcogenides (e.g., TiS2), and polyanion compounds like FePO4. As comprehensively analyzed in recent critical evaluations, understanding the electronic structure-based intrinsic transport kinetics of these diverse materials is paramount.7 Importantly, differentiating the functional roles of faradaic pseudocapacitance from those of intrinsic CQ is crucial for designing high-performance electrodes. While pseudocapacitive materials offer high energy density via redox mechanisms, the precise tuning of CQ in carbonaceous networks, such as graphene, remains a highly competitive and complementary strategy to fundamentally overcome intrinsic charge storage constraints. To address this issue, this intrinsic drawback has spurred extensive efforts to modulate the electronic structure of graphene. Common strategies in materials science, including heteroatom doping, defect engineering, and surface functionalization, can also be critically applied to optimize the electronic properties of graphene.8–11 For instance, beyond carbonaceous systems, heteroatom doping has been widely used to enhance overall catalytical activity,12,13 while defect engineering such as oxygen vacancies can notably improve ORR/OER activity in two-dimensional Nb2O5.14 Furthermore, surface functionalization has proven highly effective in boosting the electrochemical performance and quantum capacitance of MXene-based electrode materials.15,16 Besides, substitutional doping with boron (B) or nitrogen (N) introduce electron deficiency or excess, respectively.17–20 Specifically, B-doping generates holes near the Fermi level (EF), whereas N-doping contributes additional electrons. Such modifications shift the EF and alter the local DOS. Consequently, both types of doping can enhance the CQ by increasing the DOS at the EF. Moreover, co-doping with B and N has been demonstrated to produce synergistic effects, including band gap opening and charge polarization, which in turn boost the electrochemical performances. Paramasivam et al. investigated pristine graphene, hexagonal boron nitride (h-BN), and graphene/h-BN hybrid nanosheets as electrode materials for supercapacitors using density functional theory (DFT).21 They found that all nanosheets are structurally stable, the B–N incorporation tunes electronic properties, and the C4B14N14 hybrid nanosheet exhibits a quantum capacitance of 22.518 µF cm−2, indicating a synergistic effect between graphene and h-BN. Building on this binary system, the field has progressively embraced more complex doping strategies. Recent advances, in particular, have shifted attention toward ternary and hybrid doping.22–24 Wu et al. employed DFT to elucidate the synergetic coupling effect among P, S and N dopants in ternary-doped graphene during the oxygen reduction reaction.25 The calculations revealed that the co-existence of these heteroatoms optimizes the electronic structure of graphene, thereby promoting a favorable four-electron ORR pathway and accounting for the superior catalytic durability observed experimentally. These approaches serve as versatile platforms for rationally tailoring the electronic properties of graphene, thereby opening new avenues beyond conventional B and N modification.

Recently, DFT calculations have identified metal-functionalized graphene as a promising platform for advanced energy storage.26,27 In particular, this metal-decorated system outperforms conventional carbon-based counterparts by exceeding their theoretical limits. Specifically, such systems deliver remarkably high CQ values, which effectively overcome the persistent capacitance limitation and can make practical use of the intrinsically high surface area of the graphene lattice metal-functionalized graphene.28 Among various candidates, aluminum (Al) stands out as a highly effective p-type dopant, capable of modulating the DOS and shifting EF deep into the valence band. Unlike conventional transition metal dopants, which often suffer from clustering or incur high weight penalties, Al doping induces pronounced structural buckling.29–31 As a result, this buckling enhances local chemical reactivity and facilitates ion adsorption. More precisely, the C–Al bond lengths typically range from 1.71 to 1.74 Å. As revealed by Lin et al., such a buckling configuration arises from distinct Al–C orbital hybridizations and alters the local electronic structure, thus enhancing the local chemical reactivity and facilitating ion adsorption Al doping.32 In parallel, the simultaneous incorporation of three distinct heteroatoms into the graphene lattice, known as ternary co-doping, is gaining prominence and constitutes a paradigm shift toward multi-dimensional materials engineering. Compared with single or binary doping, ternary systems offer a more intricate chemical landscape. This complexity enables synergistic effects, in turn allowing precise modulation of charge distribution, local chemical reactivity, and electronic band structure. Although a growing number of DFT studies have explored various ternary configurations, several critical gaps remain, particularly the scarcity of systematic comparative analyses on B–N-metal archetypes. Moreover, the mechanistic role of metal dopants within a B–N co-doping graphene matrix remains to be fully elucidated. To fill this critical deficiency, our research reveals the specific functions of Al integration within metal–nonmetal ternary networks. Notably, we systematically examined physical phenomena governing Al-modified B–N graphene lattices, encompassing orbital hybridization, multidirectional charge redistribution among constituent dopants, and their subsequent influences on quantum capacitance alongside bandgap manipulation. However, to concretely investigate these effects, four representative carbonaceous architectures were systematically analyzed, including mono-doped configurations of B-doped (B–G) and N-doped (N–G) networks, a binary B–N co-doped structure featuring adjacent heteroatoms (B–N–G) and a structurally complex B–N–Al ternary matrix, in which Al occupies a central hollow site (B–N–Al–G). Firstly, comprehensive geometric optimizations were performed for all candidates to identify their absolute structural minima. Subsequently, we quantified key electronic parameters, including DOS, band dispersion profiles, intrinsic CQ, and spatial electron localization maps. Moreover, Bader charge population analysis was employed to precisely track interfacial electron flow between the pristine carbon scaffold and the embedded metallic or nonmetallic impurities.

In this work, we explored the electronic and capacitive behavior of single-, co-, and ternary-doped graphene systems, aiming to clarify the origins of their enhanced energy-storage performances. Our computational analyses demonstrate that while solitary B or N doping hainduce asymmetric EF shifts, dual B–N incorporation successfully yields narrow semiconductor behavior and amplifies near-Fermi electronic populations. More critically, it is revealed that anchoring electropositive Al atoms within the B–N carbon lattice fundamentally reconstructs the local electronic environment. Acting as a dominant electron donor, Al drives wide-ranging electrostatic rearrangements, creating a highly synergistic cooperative dynamic among the three distinct heteroatoms. Consequently, this unique B–N–Al ternary configuration exhibits unprecedented charge storage capabilities, characterized by exceptionally prominent capacitance peaks predominantly across cathodic voltage regimes. Detailed spatial electron mapping confirms that this performance stems directly from the seamless integration of these multinary dopants. Moreover, by uncovering these nanoscale electronic reconfigurations, our research provides a predictive framework, indicating that multinary architectures outperform conventional systems. These findings offer a theoretical basis for guiding future experimental efforts toward advanced energy-storage materials.

Computational methods

The Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) was employed, utilizing the projector augmented wave (PAW) method.33–35 The exchange–correlation functional was described by the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) in the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) formulation.36,37 A plane-wave cutoff energy of 520 eV was adopted. This value represents a stringent 30% increase over the maximum recommended PAW potential cutoff (ENMAX), effectively eliminating Pulay stress and theoretically guaranteeing high-precision energy convergence. A vacuum layer of at least 15 Å was introduced perpendicular to the graphene plane to eliminate spurious interlayer interactions. Structural optimizations were carried out by fully relaxing both lattice vectors and atomic positions. The convergence criteria were set to 10−5 eV for electronic self-consistency and 0.01 eV Å−1 for ionic forces. This combination of parameters was specifically selected to provide an optimal and robust balance between computational efficiency and rigorous numerical accuracy, which is highly suitable for deriving the band structure, density of states, and charge transfer properties of these large multinary supercell frameworks. van der Waals interactions were corrected using Grimme's DFT-D3 scheme. The Brillouin zone was sampled with Γ-centered k-point meshes: a 3 × 3 × 1 grid for geometry optimization and a denser 7 × 7 × 1 grid for electronic property calculations. The projected DOS (PDOS) were calculated using the tetrahedron method with Bloechl corrections. Spin-up and spin-down channels were treated separately to examine possible magnetic ground states. Band structures were computed along the high-symmetry path Γ → K → M → Γ, with the EF set as the energy reference. An isosurface value of 0.003 e Å−3 was used to visualize electron accumulation and depletion regions using VESTA.40 Bader charge analysis, following the algorithm of Henkelman and co-workers, was performed to quantify net charge transfer between the dopants (B, N, Al) and the graphene lattice.41 All post-processing and data visualization were conducted using VASPKIT and custom Python scripts. All DFT calculations were performed on graphene supercell models. A series of modified graphene models are constructed, including B–G, N–G, B–N–G, and B–N–Al–G configurations, to systematically investigate the effect of heteroatom doping on the electronic properties. The doping concentrations in our models, determined by the ratio of substituted heteroatoms (1 to 3 atoms) within the designated supercell, correspond to an atomic doping level of approximately 3–8 at%. This specific concentration range was deliberately selected to closely mirror the optimal doping levels reported in leading experimental syntheses of high-performance carbon electrodes. Experimental studies consistently indicate that heteroatom concentrations between 2% and 10% maximize electrochemical reactivity while preserving the structural integrity of the π-conjugated network. Concentrations exceeding this threshold frequently lead to severe lattice degradation and metal agglomeration, whereas lower concentrations fail to induce sufficient electronic synergistic effects. Furthermore, the selection of B, N, and lightweight Al aligns with practical experimental strategies aiming to maximize the charge-to-mass ratio without introducing the prohibitive weight penalties associated with heavy transition metals. Thus, the modeled supercells effectively represent the optimized local structural motifs present in highly efficient, experimentally realizable multi-doped graphene matrices.

These atomic structures were initially built using Materials Studio (MS), where the geometrical configurations and doping sites had been defined. Subsequently, the generated structural files (e.g., *.xsd or *.cif) are transferred to VASP for first-principles calculations based on DFT. In this step, full structural optimization and electronic structure calculations were performed, including total energy minimization and convergence of atomic forces. The optimized structures and corresponding output files (e.g., OUTCAR, CHGCAR, and WAVECAR) are then exported for further analysis. Finally, post-processing and property evaluation were carried out using Python scripts, which was used to extract and analyze key physical quantities. The calculated properties include DOS, band structure, CQ, and charge distribution characteristics to assess charge stability.

Results and discussions

Geometric structure and stability

Fig. 1 compares the optimized structures of B–G (a), N–G (b), B–N–G (c), and B–N–Al–G (d) to reveal how different doping patterns influence the local geometry of the graphene framework. In all cases, the fundamental carbon network is maintained after structural optimization, confirming that the doped systems preserve the basic graphene skeleton. Nevertheless, the degree of local deformation differs markedly with the doping pattern. As evident from Fig. 1(a) and (b), the B–G and N–G models stay still essentially planar, implying that single heteroatom substitution only slightly distorts the surrounding local lattice. In contrast, co-doping leads to a more pronounced reconstruction of the local coordination environment, especially when Al is introduced together with B and N, as shown in Fig. 1(c) and (d). Specifically, the B–N–Al–G model in Fig. 1(d) exhibits the strongest deviation from the ideal planar structure. Its side view in the lower part of Fig. 1 displays a noticeable out-of-plane protrusion around the doped region, whereas such a feature is much weaker in the other three systems. The geometric rationale for the severe out-of-plane protrusion observed in Fig. 1(d) lies in the markedly larger atomic radius of Al relative to C, B, and N.

Top and side views of the optimized geometric structures for the (a) B–G, (b) N–G, (c) B–N–G, and (d) B–N–Al–G models.

As illustrated in Fig. 2, the optimized geometric structures with key bond parameters for the single-, dual-, and ternary-doped graphene models were systematically evaluated to understand the structural evolution induced by the dopants. In the single-doped configurations, the substitutional B–C bond in the B–G model of Fig. 2(a) elongates to 1.48 Å, whereas the N–C bond in the N–G model of Fig. 2(b) contracts slightly to 1.41 Å, directly reflecting the differing atomic radii of B and N relative to carbon. For the co-doped B–N–G system in Fig. 2(c), adjacent B and N atoms form a stable B–N bond of 1.44 Å, while the surrounding B–C bonds remain steady at 1.48 Å. Notably, the introduction of Al to form the ternary B–N–Al–G structure of Fig. 2(d) induces a significant localized geometric distortion. The basal B–N (1.44 Å) and B–C (1.48–1.49 Å) bond lengths remain highly stable, but the Al atom anchors securely at the defect site with an Al–C interatomic distance of 2.27 Å. Furthermore, the side-view projection in Fig. 2(d) reveals that the Al atom is not entirely in-plane, however, it protrudes from the two-dimensional carbon matrix with a vertical Al–C distance of 2.17 Å. This distinct out-of-plane anchoring confirms a strong structural interaction between the Al species and the functionalized graphene lattice, which is essential for modulating the local electronic environment and exposing active sites. These observations suggest that Al incorporation introduces a severe geometric mismatch within the graphene lattice, generating intense local strain that compromises structural uniformity near the dopant site.42,43 Thus, this nonplanar distortion modifies the local electronic environment and amplifies site asymmetry, establishing a crucial geometric basis for rationalizing the distinct stability and reactivity profiles of the four modeled systems.

Key bond parameters of the (a) single-doped B–G, (b) single-doped N–G, (c) dual-doped B–N–G, and (d) ternary-doped B–N–Al–G models. The inset in (d) displays the side-view projection, illustrating the vertical out-of-plane distance of the anchored Al atom.

Projected density of states

Fig. 3 present the spin-polarized total and partial DOS of the B–G, N–G, B–N–G, and B–N–Al–G models, offering detailed insight into how heteroatom doping and Al coordination modulate the electronic structure of graphene-based systems. Notably, across all four configurations, the spin-up and spin-down DOS profiles exhibit high symmetry throughout the entire energy range. This observation suggests negligible spin polarization, implying that these systems are essentially non-magnetic in their ground-state electronic structures. In all cases, the electronic features remain dominated by C-derived p states, confirming that the graphene backbone constitutes the principal electronic framework even after doping and coordination.44,45 As illustrated in Fig. 3(a) and (b), the DOS profiles of the mono-doped systems align precisely with the characteristic p-type and n-type electronic modulations induced by B and N substitution, respectively. Specifically, B incorporation triggers a discernible redistribution of electronic states toward the valence band, whereas N doping promotes a corresponding shift toward the conduction band. Notably, since the spectra are referenced to the EF, these features signify a doping-mediated reconfiguration of states relative to EF rather than a direct observation of a Fermi level shift. The near-Fermi states are predominantly governed by the hybridization between C p orbitals and the p states of the dopants, indicating that substitutional atoms perturb the π-conjugated network without long-range structural reconstruction.46 Ultimately, the maintained spin degeneracy and the persistent dominance of C p characteristics underscore that such localized perturbations largely preserve the intrinsic electronic fingerprint of graphene.

Projected density of states (PDOS) distributions for (a) B–G, (b) N–G, (c) B–N–G, and (d) B–N–Al–G, illustrating the orbital contributions and electronic structure evolution near the Fermi level under different doping strategies.

A more pronounced redistribution of frontier states is evident for the co-doped B–N–G model in Fig. 3(c). Owing to the charge compensation effect between electron-deficient B and electron-rich N, a minimum in the DOS appears near EF, and the extreme tendencies associated with the mono-doped systems become considerably mitigated. This behavior suggests that B–N co-doping does not simply superimpose independent p-type and n-type effects, but rather establishes a more balanced local electronic environment through mutual interaction and charge compensation.47 As a result, the Fermi level becomes positioned within a low region of DOS, giving the system an electronic character analogous to that of an intrinsic semiconductor. More rigorously, this feature may be described as a gap-like or pseudogap-like behavior around EF, rather than as definitive proof of a fully developed semiconductor band gap based on DOS alone. Furthermore, the projected DOS reveals appreciable overlap among C p, B p, and N p states across a wide energy window, evidencing robust orbital hybridization within the co-doped region. This strong electronic coupling demonstrates that the B and N heteroatoms are covalently integrated into the graphene lattice. Crucially, this integration preserves the intrinsic continuity of the carbon framework while inducing a locally polarized chemical environment.

Among the investigated configurations, the B–N–Al–G model manifests the most conspicuous alteration of the frontier electronic landscape, as clearly depicted in Fig. 3(d). The incorporation of Al introduces distinct resonance peaks in the subgap region near the EF, indicating that Al coordination substantially modulates the band-edge electronic states. Projected DOS analysis reveals that these emerging states mainly originate from Al-derived s and p orbitals, which exhibit strong spatial and energetic overlap with the p orbitals of neighboring C, B, and N atoms. This evidence indicates the Al dopant is not an electronically isolated site but rather strongly coupled to the B–N–G lattice via robust orbital hybridization. Accordingly, the density of states at EF becomes substantially enhanced relative to the B–G, N–G, and B–N–G systems, thus creating a more electrochemically active localized center. These electronic features are intrinsically linked to the geometric characteristics of the system shown in Fig. 1 and 2. Notably, B–N–Al–G exhibits the most pronounced out-of-plane distortion among the four models, which induces a substantial redistribution of frontier electronic states. This correlation is physically reasonable, as the loss of planarity alters local orbital overlap and coordination environments, thereby promoting the formation of localized states near EF. Consequently, the distinctive electronic signature of B–N–Al–G originates from the synergistic effects of heteroatom co-doping, Al orbital participation, and distortion-induced symmetry breaking.

Broadly, the evolution of DOS underscores a systematic trend in electronic modulation. While mono-doping with B or N induces characteristic p-type or n-type perturbations within the graphene lattice, B–N co-doping facilitates charge compensation, yielding a stabilized quasi-semiconducting state. Conversely, the incorporation of Al fundamentally reshapes the electronic landscape near the Fermi level via Al s/p-orbital hybridization, significantly enhancing the DOS. These features position B–N–Al–G as an electronically activated scaffold with the potential for superior charge transfer and catalytic performance. However, these electronic descriptors necessitate rigorous validation through adsorption energy evaluations and reaction pathway simulations.

Electronic band structure

The band structures of B–G, N–G, B–N–G, and B–N–Al–G presented in Fig. 4 highlight the impact of specific doping strategies on the frontier electronic properties along the high-symmetry Γ-M-K-Γ path. Importantly, the characteristic band-dispersion framework of graphene is preserved across all models, signifying that the π-conjugated carbon network remains electronically recognizable notwithstanding the perturbations from doping and coordination. Nevertheless, pronounced modifications emerge near EF, manifesting the unique influence of mono-doping, B–N co-doping, and Al-coordination on carrier distribution, band-edge configurations, and localization of electronic states. In the mono-doped B–G of Fig. 4(a) and N–G of Fig. 4(b) systems, the band structures largely preserve the characteristic cone-like dispersion near the k point, indicating that isolated B or N substitution exerts a primarily perturbative rather than reconstructive effect on the electronic framework.48,49 Consequently, while the host lattice continues to support delocalized π-type states, the presence of dopants primarily modulates the occupation levels and relative alignment of the frontier bands. In the B–G model, the Dirac-point-related feature is shifted above EF, placing the Fermi level closer to the valence band, which is consistent with the expected p-type behavior resulting from electron-deficient boron substitution. In contrast, for the N–G model, the corresponding feature moves below EF, indicating that the Fermi level is closer to the conduction band, in agreement with the electron-donating nature of nitrogen and the resulting n-type doping character. Although the graphene-like dispersion is largely retained in both B–G and N–G, the ideal gapless crossing at the k point is no longer preserved. Instead, a small but discernible separation emerges between the valence and conduction branches near the Dirac-point region. This observation suggests that substitutional doping disrupts the perfect equivalence of the graphene sublattices and perturbs the local crystal potential, thereby lifting the degeneracy characteristic of pristine graphene. As a result, the electronic structure shifts from ideal semimetallic behavior toward narrow-gap state. Despite this gap opening, its magnitude remains small, indicating that mono-doping primarily affects carrier polarity and local symmetry, while largely maintaining the original graphene-like band topology. From this perspective, B–G and N–G are better described as electronically perturbed graphene systems rather than fully reconstructed semiconductors. However, the B–N–G model shown in Fig. 4(c) displays a more pronounced alteration of the band structure compared to either of the mono-doped counterparts. Most remarkably, the separation between the upper valence band and the lower conduction band near the k point becomes more distinct, reflecting a gap-like opening in the frontier electronic structure. This trend is consistent with DOS behavior shown in Fig. 3(c), where DOS near the Fermi level is more strongly suppressed in the co-doped configuration. The enlarged gap-like feature in B–N–G indicates that the synergistic incorporation of B and N induces a more substantial perturbation of the electronic symmetry of graphene than does single-element substitution alone. Consequently, EF shifts to a region of low DOS near the center of the frontier gap, imparting the system with a more intrinsic-semiconductor-like character.

Calculated electronic band structures along the high-symmetry Γ-M-K-Γ path for (a) B–G, (b) N–G, (c) B–N–G, and (d) B–N–Al–G. The horizontal dashed line represents the Fermi level (EF).

Among all models studied, B–N–Al–G of Fig. 4(d) undergoes the most substantial qualitative electronic reconstruction of its near-edge electronic structure. The incorporation of Al induces a notable flattening of the bands closest to the Fermi level, distinguishing B–N–Al–G from the more dispersive B–G, N–G, and B–N–G models. Such reduced dispersion is indicative of enhanced electronic localization in the vicinity of the frontier states. In terms of band structure, this flattening generally corresponds to a reduced energy dispersion of the participating orbitals over reciprocal space, which is often indicative of localized or weakly delocalized electronic states. Thus, the B–N–Al–G model is distinguished not merely by a shifted or enlarged frontier gap, but by a qualitative reconfiguration of the electronic landscape near the Fermi level. This behavior is fully consistent with DOS displayed in Fig. 3(d), wherein the introduction of Al gives rise to supplementary electronic states around the Fermi level.

In addition to identify the orbital contributions, our analysis of the frontier band curvature offers a deeper physical interpretation of the ternary synergistic effect. As shown in Fig. 4(d), the introduction of Al induces a pronounced flattening of the bands nearest to the Fermi level, in stark contrast to the linear Dirac dispersion of pristine graphene. A flattened energy–momentum relation implies a reduced second derivative, ∂2E/∂k2, which, according to the effective mass m* = ℏ2/(∂2E/∂k2), directly translates into a substantially heavier m* for the charge carriers in the B–N–Al–G system. This heavier m*, however, gives rise to a twofold consequence. On the one hand, it reduces carrier drift velocity and intrinsic mobility, leading to enhanced electronic localization and slightly lower classical conductivity; on the other hand, because DOS is proportional to the m* within the parabolic-band approximation, the accumulation of heavy, low-mobility carriers at relevant energies produces a sharp increase in the available electronic states, which is precisely the origin of the dominant quantum-capacitance enhancement predicted at positive bias. Thus, the observed band flattening not only rationalizes the reduced carrier transport but also directly links the electronic structure to the macroscopic capacitive performance. Together with the orbital-hybridization picture discussed above, this analysis establishes a coherent physical framework where the Al-induced structural buckling breaks local symmetry, promotes sp3-like rehybridisation and strong Al-pn orbital mixing, and ultimately transforms the ternary system into an electronically active configuration that increases the quantum capacitance while lowering classical mobility. This trade-off is characteristic of systems with enhanced electronic localization and can be exploited for energy-storage applications.

Taken together, Fig. 3(d) and 4(d) indicate that the role of Al is not simply to induce minor electronic perturbations in the existing B–N co-doped graphene. Rather, Al coordination drives a more substantial reconstruction of the frontier states. Analysis of the projected DOS reveals that this effect originates primarily from the involvement of Al-derived s and p orbitals, which hybridize with the p states of neighboring C, B, and N atoms. Crucially, the B–N–Al–G model is not merely a wider bandgap analogue of B–N–G. Instead, its band-edge structure displays increased complexity and departs from the idealized picture, reflecting the emergence of Al-related localized electronic contributions superimposed on the co-doped graphene framework. Owing to the reduced dispersion of the band extrema near EF, the resulting frontier-state behavior points to a substantially altered energy cost for local electronic excitations or interfacial charge redistribution, relative to the other three systems. Accordingly, Al incorporation is more accurately characterized by band-edge reconstruction and frontier-state localization, rather than by a simple monotonic widening of the band gap. This distinction is significant, as it avoids overinterpretation of the band structure while still reflecting the essential physical effects of Al coordination.

It is worth noting that the electronic band structures and related pseudogap behaviors in this work are evaluated using the standard PBE-D3 functional framework. While the PBE functional is well known to systematically underestimate the absolute magnitude of electronic band gaps due to self-interaction errors, it remains a highly reliable and widely adopted computational standard for elucidating qualitative electronic structure evolutions and relative shifts in large 2D carbonaceous supercells. Therefore, while the absolute transport gaps might be numerically compressed, the key physical features predicted herein, such as the band-edge flattening, frontier-state localization, and orbital hybridization mechanisms induced by the ternary B–N–Al coordination, remain qualitatively robust and independent of the functional choice.

The unique electronic structure of the B–N–Al–G system, characterized by a high effective mass, low carrier mobility, and an exceptionally high density of states near the Fermi level, opens up distinct prospects for specific device applications. While these features render the material unsuitable for conventional high-speed logic switches (e.g., field-effect transistors), which demand ultra-high mobility for rapid operation, they may be of interest for thermoelectric energy conversion. According to the Mahan–Sofo theory, a sharp distortion in the DOS near the Fermi level can enhance the Seebeck coefficient, and the flat-band-induced localization together with the high DOS positions B–N–Al–G as a candidate for low-dimensional thermoelectric devices, although the reduced electrical conductivity must also be considered. Moreover, the out-of-plane geometric deformation and strongly localized states arising from the Al doping center can act as natural charge-trapping sites, which may also be relevant to low-power single-electron transistors or neuromorphic memristors that mimic synaptic behavior.

The potential dependence of CQ for B–G, N–G, B–N–G, and B–N–Al–G at 300 K is displayed in Fig. 5. These profiles clearly illustrate how specific doping configurations modulate the charge-storage capacity of graphene-based frameworks. Given that quantum capacitance is highly sensitive to both the density and accessibility of electronic states near EF, the CQ curves serve as reliable descriptors for evaluating the electrochemical behavior of these modified graphene systems.50,51 Notably, a systematic evolution in capacitance is observed from mono-doping to binary co-doping and further to ternary synergistic regulation, indicating that the quantum capacitance performance is governed not merely by the presence of dopants, but by the cooperative modulation of frontier electronic states through multiple active elements. Mono-doping with boron induces volatile capacitance fluctuations across the potential window, and nitrogen incorporation substantially reduces the overall magnitude. However, the binary B–N doping system establishes a balanced, neutralized state characterized by a distinct gap-like depression near the potential of zero charge (PZC). Crucially, the transition to the ternary B–N–Al configuration fundamentally reconstructs the electrochemical response from symmetrical, low-magnitude oscillations into a highly asymmetric profile.

Quantum capacitance as a function of applied potential for the (a) B–G, (b) N–G, (c) B–N–G, and (d) B–N–Al–G systems at T = 300 K.

Fig. 5(a) and (b) contrast the CQ behaviors of the mono-doped systems, emphasizing how dopant identity modulates the electronic response of the graphene lattice during charge storage. The B–G system clearly exhibits the most pronounced oscillatory behavior over a broad potential range, with particularly prominent features in the negative-potential region and in the vicinity of the PZC. This behavior suggests that B doping induces a marked, bias-dependent redistribution of accessible electronic states, thereby enabling a significant quantum capacitance response under specific polarization conditions.52 In contrast, the N–G model displays a much weaker overall CQ amplitude, although finite oscillatory features are still present across the studied voltage window. Unlike B–G, the capacitance response of N–G is attenuated and less sharply amplified, reflecting that N doping exerts a comparatively mild influence on the electronic states near EF. Accordingly, mono-doping demonstrates that single-atom substitution can adjust graphene's quantum-capacitance characteristics, yet the effect is strongly dopant-dependent and falls short of delivering a broadly optimized response.

In the binary co-doped B–N–G system, a different capacitance-regulation mechanism is observed, as shown in Fig. 5(c). Relative to the mono-doped models, B–N–G displays a markedly smoothed CQ profile near the PZC, effectively mitigating the pronounced oscillations observed in B–G. Such behavior reflects the charge compensation between electron-deficient B and electron-rich N, resulting in a diminished density of accessible electronic states around the equilibrium potential. Thus, the B–N–G system yields a lower and smoother capacitance response in the vicinity of 0 V, reflecting a frontier-state distribution that is more balanced albeit less strongly amplified. Binary co-doping therefore promotes electronic uniformity and attenuates extreme asymmetry in the potential-dependent behavior, but partially diminishes the quantum-capacitance amplitude in the low-bias regime. Accordingly, the B–N doping mode serves not simply to enhance CQ, but to regulate and stabilize the capacitance behavior through charge compensation effects.

A qualitatively distinct behavior is observed in the ternary B–N–Al–G system shown in Fig. 5(d). Among the four configurations, B–N–Al–G displays the most pronounced positive-potential response, featuring a sharp and intense capacitance peak centered around 1.3–1.4 V, accompanied by several additional peak-like features in the high positive-potential region. The B–N–Al–G framework effectively suppresses parasitic capacitance at negative potentials while exhibiting a sharp and pronounced capacitance peak in capacitance (approaching +0.0125 µF cm−2) under a positive bias of approximately +1.3 V. This pronounced, localized capacitance maximum is consistent with the proposed ternary synergistic mechanism, demonstrating that Al-mediated charge donation shifts the capacitance response from a broadly fluctuating pattern to an asymmetric profile with a pronounced enhancement at positive bias in positive potential regimes. These findings indicate indicate that incorporating Al into the B–N–G framework goes beyond slight modifications of the electronic structure, instead giving rise to a synergistic electronic-activation mechanism. Put differently, the ternary system combines the polarization effects from heteroatom co-doping with the frontier-state modifications driven by the coordinated Al center, resulting in a significantly amplified and highly bias-dependent quantum capacitance response at positive potentials. Moreover, quantum capacitance is underscored as a sensitive and robust descriptor for assessing synergistic electronic optimization within multicomponent carbon materials.

Integrating DOS results from Fig. 3(d) with the band structures in Fig. 4(d) elucidates the electronic origin of the enhanced CQ in the ternary B–N–Al–G system. While the incorporation of Al populates the vicinity of the Fermi level with auxiliary electronic states, the frontier bands simultaneously undergo significant reconstruction, becoming markedly less dispersive. These electronic modifications suggest that, under positive bias, the B–N–Al–G framework can access a considerably denser and more responsive manifold of states compared to its mono-doped or binary co-doped counterparts. Consequently, the sharp capacitance surge observed in Fig. 5(d) represents the macroscopic electrostatic manifestation of this ternary synergistic electronic structure. In this regard, CQ provides an especially valuable metric, because it translates subtle differences in local DOS and band-edge character into a directly comparable electrochemical-response profile.

For practical applications in aqueous electrolytes, the thermodynamic water stability voltage operates strictly up to +0.83 V. Therefore, the theoretical integrated charge density was evaluated up to the positive electrode limit of +0.83 V. The calculations displayed in Fig. 6 yield an integrated charge density of ∼0.00021 µC cm−2 for B–N–Al–G at this boundary, which is significantly higher than those of the single-doped N–G (∼0.00002 µC cm−2) and dual-doped B–N–G(∼0.00015 µC cm−2) configurations. Furthermore, the B–N–Al–G system exhibits a prominent peak of ∼0.00034 µC cm−2 at approximately 0.75 V. This performance remains highly competitive compared to other typical electrode materials, such as transition metal oxides and MXenes, confirming that the ternary synergistic mechanism effectively activates charge storage even within restricted aqueous operational windows.

Interfacial charge redistribution

Fig. 7 shows the charge density difference (CDD) isosurfaces and Bader charge analyses for B–G, N–G, B–N–G, and B–N–Al–G, providing direct evidence of how distinct doping strategies orchestrate local charge redistribution. The CDD maps indicate that electronic perturbations are strongly localized around dopant sites, underscoring the site-specific character of heteroatom incorporation. Notably, the system evolves from localized mono-doping polarization to binary charge-compensation redistribution, culminating in a ternary synergistic rearrangement around the Al center. This sequence delineates the real-space charge-transfer mechanism that drives electronic-structure modulation and the enhanced quantum capacitance discussed earlier.

Charge density differences and calculated Bader charges of the (a) B–G, (b) N–G, (c) B–N–G, and (d) B–N–Al–G systems.

As illustrated in Fig. 7(a) and (b), B–G and N–G systems induce discrete local charge redistributions, breaking the intrinsic electronic uniformity of graphene. In B–G model, the B atom exhibits a Bader charge of −1.91 |e|, reflecting substantial electron depletion and the formation of a polarized p-type center.53 In contrast, N–G demonstrates net electron accumulation at the dopant site with a Bader charge of +1.16 |e|, generating an electron-enriched n-type center.54 Although both systems induce local polarity, the resulting effect remains single-centered and spatially confined to the immediate vicinity of the dopant. A more sophisticated cooperative behavior emerges in the binary B–N–G system in Fig. 7(c), with the Bader charges of B at −1.95 |e| and N at +1.46 |e|, both enhanced relative to the mono-doped systems. This observation indicates that B–N doping does not simply average the effects of individual dopants but reorganizes the electronic landscape into a strongly coupled, spatially extended electrostatic network. An interconnected redistribution domain is observed in the CDD isosurface, pointing to electronic coupling between B and N through the carbon π-network. The pre-configured asymmetry regulates orbital overlap and governs frontier-state accessibility, consistent with the balanced but gap-like electronic features revealed in the DOS.

The most significant transformation occurs in the ternary B–N–Al–G system of Fig. 7(d), where the Al atom, carrying a Bader charge of −0.78 |e|, acts as a secondary activator that donates charge into the pre-polarized B–N framework, where B and N exhibit Bader charges of −1.94 |e| and +1.54 |e|, respectively. Accordingly, Al proves to be an active participant within a complex charge-transfer network, as opposed to a passive dopant. The resulting CDD isosurface reveals a substantially expanded redistribution pattern, driven by a threefold mechanism consisting of (i) heteroatom polarization, (ii) Al-mediated donation, and (iii) structural distortion. Heteroatom polarization originates from the intrinsic B–N local electrostatic field. Al-mediated donation enhances local electron-density redistribution. Structural distortion arises from out-of-plane geometric deformation as shown in Fig. 1(d), which breaks local symmetry and perturbs orbital hybridization. The evolution from mono-doping to dual-center coupling and ultimately to a ternary charge-transfer complex signifies a fundamental restructuring of graphene's electronic behavior. In addition, compared with the other systems, B–N–Al–G features a more pronounced electronic differentiation and a remarkably agile interfacial charge-transfer behavior under bias conditions. Collectively, the CDD and Bader charge analyses establish a direct link between structural modification and electrochemical performance. In particular, the enhanced quantum capacitance of the ternary system does not simply stem from greater compositional complexity. Instead, it arises from an intentionally engineered ternary synergy. This synergy maximizes local polarization and frontier-state reconstruction. Consequently, a clear mechanistic rationale emerges for the superior capacitive behavior observed.

To bridge the gap between theoretical prediction and experimental realization, two complementary synthetic routes are proposed for the B–N–Al ternary-doped graphene frameworks. The first approach employs a scalable hydrothermal-pyrolysis method. Graphene oxide is mixed with N-, B-, and Al-containing precursors and hydrothermally self-assembled into a hydrogel. Following freeze-drying, the intermediate is pyrolyzed at 800–1000 °C under an inert atmosphere. This thermal treatment successfully yields substitutional B–N doping alongside Al anchoring at defect sites. The second approach utilizes a chemical vapor deposition (CVD) process, which is highly suitable for growing high-crystallinity films with precise doping control. This method employs methane, ammonia, diborane, and a volatile aluminum precursor on metal substrates. By carefully tuning the precursor gas flow rates, B and N are effectively co-doped while Al species are subsequently trapped at polar sites. Ultimately, both of these proposed strategies provide viable, practical pathways to translate our computational designs into experimental realization.

Conclusions

In summary, this study systematically investigated the structural transformation, electronic properties, charge rearrangement, and capacitive performance of B–G, N–G, B–N–G, and B–N–Al–G to reveal the precise mechanisms by which different doping strategies modulate graphene's quantum-capacitance behavior. The results demonstrate a clear progression from localized mono-doping perturbations to cooperative charge compensation in B–N–G, and finally to ternary synergistic activation in B–N–Al–G. Structurally, mono-doping preserves the graphene lattice with minimal distortion, B–N dual-doping enhances local polarization, and Al incorporation induces pronounced out-of-plane deformation, generating an asymmetric, strained coordination environment that drives electronic reorganization. The analysis of electronic structures show that mono-doping retains the graphene-like band framework while introducing hole- and electron-type behavior. B–N co-doping suppresses the density of states near the Fermi level, reflecting cooperative charge compensation and enhanced orbital hybridization. The ternary B–N–Al–G system exhibits the most substantial frontier-state reconstruction with additional near-Fermi-level states and localized electronic activation. Quantum capacitance evaluation identifies B–N–Al–G as the most effective configuration, particularly under positive bias, surpassing the performance of mono- or binary-doped systems by efficiently activating electronically accessible states. CDD and calculated Bader charges analyses provide the mechanistic origin that mono-doping generates single-center polarization, while B–N co-doping establishes coupled charge redistribution, and Al incorporation introduces direct charge donation to the pre-polarized framework, forming a multi-center charge-transfer network. These findings demonstrate that the enhanced quantum capacitance of B–N–Al–G arises from a deliberately engineered ternary synergy that combines heteroatom polarization, metal-mediated electronic participation, and local structural distortion. This work provides a mechanistic framework for the rational design of graphene-based electrodes and highlights the power of multicomponent cooperation in optimizing the electrochemical performance of low-dimensional carbon materials.
