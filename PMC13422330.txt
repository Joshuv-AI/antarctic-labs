Examining the intersection of biologic sex and predicted body weight in patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome
Authors: Amira Mohamed, Nikita Reddy, Theresa Paul, Michelle Ng Gong, Elias Baedorf Kassis, Ari Moskowitz
Published: 2026

Lung-protective ventilation using a low tidal volume strategy is a central pillar of evidence-based care for patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). Current guidelines recommend tidal volumes of approximately 6 mL/kg PBW, calculated using the Devine formula, which incorporates patient sex and height as surrogate for lung size [1]. Observational studies have identified sex-based differences in ventilator management and consequent delivery of higher tidal volumes relative to lung capacity in female patients [2]. However, little is known about whether the PBW formula itself sufficiently captures sex-based differences in lung volume, respiratory mechanics, and ventilatory efficiency once guideline-concordant ventilation is achieved. Driving pressure is a physiologic measure that provides insight into lung mechanics including lung compliance and functional lung capacity [3]. Ventilation ratio and arterial pH reflect the relationship between carbon dioxide elimination and minute ventilation, with ventilation ratio serving as a surrogate for physiologic dead space [4]. Accordingly, we conducted a secondary analysis of individual-level data from multiple randomized controlled trials including ARDS patients to examine whether sex is associated with differences in driving pressure, arterial pH, and ventilation ratio.

This study used individual-level, de-identified data from seven landmark randomized clinical trials conducted by the Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS) Network and the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) from the BioLINCC repository: ARMA, ALVEOLI, FACTT, ALTA, EDEN-OMEGA, SAILS, and ROSET (Table S1). Patients who were mechanically ventilated and had available data on sex, height, and weight on day 1 of clinical trial enrollment were included. The primary exposure variable was patient sex (female or male) as recorded at trial enrollment. Driving pressure was defined as plateau pressure minus PEEP pressure. Ventilation ratio was calculated as: ventilation ratio = (minute ventilation × PaCO2) / (predicted minute ventilation × 40), where predicted minute ventilation was defined as 100 mL/kg of PBW per minute [3]. The association between sex and day 1 driving pressure was assessed using mixed-effects multivariable linear regression. Covariates included age, race, ethnicity, the Pao2/FiO2 (P/F) ratio at the time of trial screening, and the set tidal volume indexed to PBW. To account for differences across study protocols and patient populations, the model included trial as a fixed-effect covariate.

After excluding patients with missing data on key variables (Figure S1, Table S2), 5,367 patients were included in the analytic cohort. Of these, 2,468 (46.0%) were female and 2,899 (54.0%) were male (Table S3). At day 1, set tidal volume was lower in females than males (384.1 mL [SD 114.5] vs. 487.2 mL [SD 142.6]). When indexed to PBW, mean tidal volume was similar between groups (7.1 mL/kg PBW [SD 2.9] in females vs. 6.8 mL/kg PBW [SD 2.0] in males). Mean driving pressure was 15.4 cm H2O (SD 6.6) in females and 14.9 cm H2O (SD 6.3) in males. In adjusted analyses, sex was not significantly associated with driving pressure after accounting for age, race, APACHE score, P/F ratio, and tidal volume indexed to predicted body weight (β = − 0.32, 95% CI − 0.72 to 0.08, p = 0.115). In adjusted analyses, female sex was associated with a small but statistically significant difference in pH (β = 0.010, 95% CI 0.001–0.01, p = 0.003). In adjusted analyses, female sex was associated with a significantly lower ventilation ratio compared with male sex after adjustment for age, race, APACHE score, and P/F ratio, and tidal volume indexed to PBW (β = − 0.17, 95% CI − 0.24 to − 0.10, p < 0.001) (Table 1). These findings were consistent across multiple sensitivity analyses, including restriction to shorter patients and exclusion of those receiving higher tidal volumes (Tables S4-S6).

In this pooled analysis of patients with ARDS enrolled in multiple randomized clinical trials, we found no clinically meaningful difference in driving pressure between females and males after multivariable adjustment. Although small statistical differences were observed in arterial pH and ventilation ratio on day 1 of enrollment, the magnitude of these differences was small and unlikely to be clinically significant. Taken together, these results suggest that when tidal volumes are set according to PBW within protocolized trial settings, sex-based differences in driving pressure are negligible. Emerging literature has questioned whether the Devine ideal body weight formula, originally developed in the 1970s for drug dosing rather than mechanical ventilation, adequately captures variability in lung size between sexes [5]. However, in our cohort, differences in ventilation ratio and pH were small despite similar indexed tidal volumes and comparable driving pressures, suggesting that any residual physiologic variation not captured by current formulas is unlikely to translate into meaningful differences in mechanical stress under guideline-concordant ventilation. This was a secondary, observational analysis, and residual confounding cannot be excluded. These findings suggest that current PBW-based approaches to lung-protective ventilation provide comparable mechanical stress exposure across sexes within protocolized trial settings, while acknowledging that minor physiologic variation may persist.
