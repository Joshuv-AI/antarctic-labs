Forecasting and Capacity Planning in Prehospital Emergency Medical Services: The Case of Ankara
Authors: Elif Erbay, Çağdaş Erkan Akyürek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTime savings in prehospital emergency medical services are vital for human life. Ensuring that these services can meet potential demands is essential for successful delivery. This study aimed to forecast the demand for ambulance services within prehospital emergency medical services and to plan the capacity of ambulance stations.MethodsIn this descriptive, cross‐sectional, and analytical study, the population consists of 943,412 cases directed by 139 ambulance stations in the capital of Türkiye. The forecasting was carried out at the district level with a particular focus on seven districts. Various forecasting methods such as Holt‐Winters, ARIMA, MLP, NNAR, and LSTM were employed. The optimal number of ambulances required at various stations to meet response time targets was identified using Monte Carlo simulations to account for uncertainties in call volumes and response times.ResultsA total of 943,412 calls were directed to ambulances. The annual distributions showed slight increases each year. Forecasting performance of various methods was evaluated based on RMSE, MAE, and MAPE metrics, with the ARIMA and NNAR models generally showing the best performance. The study also identified the optimal number of ambulances required at various stations to meet response time targets.ConclusionThe study underscores the importance of accurate demand forecasting and capacity planning for ambulance services in ensuring timely and effective prehospital emergency medical services. By addressing critical response time targets and optimising ambulance capacity, the findings contribute to improving the quality and accessibility of emergency health services, ultimately enhancing human life and health outcomes.Keywords: ambulances, emergency medical services, forecasting, machine learning, prehospital emergency care

Introduction

Demand is defined as consumers' willingness to purchase goods and services at a certain price within a certain period of time, and it indicates the level of interest in products or services. Health service demand, on the other hand, is the amount of health services that will be requested directly by patients themselves, and indirectly by relevant health service providers [1]. Understanding healthcare demand is essential for the effective management of resources and the fulfilment of patients' needs. Healthcare demand forecasting enables healthcare organisations to predict and plan for future demand, allowing them to allocate resources effectively and efficiently [2]. Service providers need to analyse past data trends to better predict fluctuating consumer needs and preferences and allocate resources accordingly. Planning for patient volume and healthcare service needs in different geographical regions in the healthcare sector depends on accurate demand forecasting [3].

The characteristics of healthcare services make forecasting more complex; since human and public health are directly affected, the tolerance for errors is reduced. For this reason, demand forecasting in healthcare services has become not only a management tool but also a fundamental requirement for the quality and sustainability of service delivery. With accurate forecasting models, the timing and magnitude of healthcare demand can be determined in advance, enabling the planning of personnel, equipment, and infrastructure resources [4]. One area where such planning is particularly important in healthcare is prehospital Emergency Medical Services (EMS).

EMS involve providing medical intervention at the scene of an emergency, such as a sudden illness, injury, accident, or disaster, and safely transporting patients to appropriate healthcare facilities. These services include life‐saving interventions such as defibrillation, airway management, and cardiopulmonary resuscitation, and are typically provided by emergency medical technicians and healthcare professionals [5]. In this context, EMS constitute a critical area that can lead to irreversible consequences in terms of human life if not managed effectively.

The management and planning of ambulance services, one of the most important components of EMS, has a direct and decisive impact on the overall efficiency and effectiveness of the healthcare system. In particular, keeping response times short can significantly affect patient outcomes. However, factors such as the irregularity of patient demand and the spatial and temporal variability of case numbers bring uncertainty to service delivery [6]. Effective capacity planning in emergency healthcare requires the allocation of resources in an appropriate manner, taking into account call volume, case types, population density, seasonal variability, and geographical characteristics [7, 8]. Proper planning of the number and location of ambulances ensures uninterrupted service delivery, reducing response times and increasing patient satisfaction [9, 10]. Therefore, the use of management tools such as demand forecasting and capacity planning in these services is important for enhancing sustainability of the system.

Studies show that different societies face similar problems. In Japan, the increase in the elderly population has led to a rise in ambulance requests, while in South Korea, despite the decline in population, demand pressure has continued due to the ageing population [11, 12]. Another study emphasised that the number of calls is significantly higher in societies with an older population structure [13]. In countries such as the United Kingdom, Sweden, and Australia, it has been determined that ambulance calls have increased in areas with a high concentration of elderly people [14, 15, 16]. It has been found that ambulance calls are more frequent in low‐income areas and that some of these calls are medically unnecessary [17, 18, 19]. These studies show that the management and planning of EMS involve common challenges not only in Türkiye but also in other countries, and that these challenges can be solved using scientific methods.

The methods used in demand and capacity planning for ambulance services vary from country to country. For example, in the UK, call numbers, case types, and time intervals are analysed in detail within the scope of EMS, and both response time targets are set, and capacity planning is carried out based on these data [15]. While spatial analysis techniques are prominent in Sweden in order to take geographical inequalities into account, demographic projections based on capacity planning are at the forefront in Japan due to its ageing population [11, 14]. However, in Türkiye, despite the increasing number of cases, especially in large cities, there are very few examples of systematic integration of decision support systems. Current practices often rely on manual planning based on case numbers from previous years for station locations and capacity allocation. Therefore, Türkiye has a significant gap utilising data‐driven modelling approaches, as seen in examples from other countries.

Ambulance demand forecasting studies using basic demographic data such as population density and call volume provide important contributions to the efficient planning of EMS. The vast majority of studies examining how ambulance demand forecasting models are applied in different contexts, use only a single method and do not provide sufficient comparative analysis of these methods with other models [20]. Some studies on demand forecasting in healthcare focus solely on traditional time series methods, while others deal exclusively with artificial neural network models. This makes it difficult to understand which model is more appropriate in which context. Furthermore, there is no consensus in the literature on how the performance of models varies according to different data structures and forecasting objectives [21, 22]. It is stated that there is no universally best model among forecasting methods, and that the method should be selected according to the context and data structure [20]. For instance, multiple linear regression models may produce high error rates in some cases, while time series models can yield successful results in the short term [21]. There are few studies in the literature that apply multiple methods to the same data set and compare their performance using metrics such as MAE, MAPE, and RMSE. Therefore, it is believed that multi‐method comparative analyses in this field will contribute to the literature.

Background in Türkiye

EMS in Türkiye are provided under the 112 Emergency Medical Services system, which is coordinated at the national level by the General Directorate of Emergency Medical Services and at the provincial level by the Emergency Medical Services Directorates affiliated with the Provincial Health Directorates. Ambulance stations are one of the most critical components of EMS. These stations, known as ‘112 Emergency Assistance Stations’ in Türkiye, are established upon the proposal of the directorate and approval of the governorate, and are structured according to response time, staff numbers, and case load. The stations are not limited to patient transport; they also coordinate with healthcare institutions, law enforcement agencies, and other emergency response units to work together in crisis situations. Communication and case management processes are carried out simultaneously with the 112 Command and Control Centre, and all ambulances are equipped with location‐reporting systems to enable dynamic resource allocation decisions.

The structure of emergency health services in Türkiye has undergone a significant transformation over the past 20 years. While there were only 481 stations nationwide in 2002, this number has risen to 3101 as of 2022. During the same period, the total number of ambulances increased from 617 to 6,658, with 5735 of these being emergency response ambulances. With these developments, the population per ambulance decreased from approximately 120,000 in 2002 to 14,870 as of 2022 [23]. While this improvement is a positive development in terms of access to services, there are still significant differences between provinces. These differences between provinces directly affect the quality and accessibility of services beyond mere numbers. In particular, in provinces with high population density and extensive urban sprawl, an increase in the number of calls per ambulance can lead to longer response times, increased workload per station, and a decline in service quality. In this context, resource planning and station location should be based not only on population size but also on case density.

The province of Ankara, with its districts of varying socioeconomic characteristics, high case volume, and station diversity, provides a suitable field for evaluating EMS. The aim of this study, which is structured within the theoretical framework of demand forecasting and capacity planning in healthcare services, is to forecast 112 ambulance calls in Ankara Province, compare the performance of different methods, and conduct station capacity planning based on these forecasts. Thus, the aim is not only to contribute to the gaps in the literature but also to propose a decision support model aimed at improving the effectiveness of EMS in Türkiye. The method presented is adaptable not only to applications in Türkiye but also to healthcare systems in other countries with similar demographic and geographical characteristics.

Methods

There are two stages in this descriptive, cross‐sectional, and analytical research: forecasting of EMS calls and capacity planning. In the forecasting phase, a 1‐year forecast was made from the 3‐year time series using different forecasting methods. In the second phase, the capacities of ambulances at each station were planned. The study population comprised 943,412 cases in which emergency ambulances operating in Ankara were dispatched, involving 139 stations that managed these ambulance referrals. Ankara, the capital city of Türkiye, is a major metropolitan area with a diverse population and complex urban layout, making it a representative sample for studying EMS. The capital city was selected for this study due to its extensive ambulance network, high volume of EMS calls, and varied geographical and demographic characteristics. Data on EMS calls were obtained through official correspondence from the Ministry of Health.

The forecasting phase was conducted at the district level, and the districts were selected based on the Pareto Analysis. As a result of the Pareto Analysis, all cases (n = 798,672) in seven districts were selected for sampling. There was no sampling for the capacity planning process, as the analyses were planned to include all stations. Out of the 139 ambulance stations operating in Ankara, 18 were excluded due to significant data gaps, resulting in capacity planning for 121 stations.

For the analysis, Python and R programming languages were used. In the forecasting phase, Holt‐Winters model, ARIMA models, Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) model, Neural NETwork AutoRegression (NNAR), and Long Short‐Term Memory (LSTM) were used. Three criteria were used in selecting the methods in the forecasting process: the nature of the data set (quantitative and time series structure), the forecasting objective of predicting short to medium‐term call volume, and the preference for methods with high accuracy levels obtained from previous literature. Traditional time series methods such as ARIMA and Holt‐Winters and artificial intelligence‐based methods such as MLP, NNAR, and LSTM were preferred. Time series analyses were included because they provide reliable results in data containing trend and seasonal components. Artificial neural network‐based models such as MLP and NNAR were preferred due to their ability to capture non‐linear and complex patterns. LSTM stands out from other methods due to its long‐term memory structure that takes time dependency into account; it was included in this study to capture more complex patterns. Classification‐focused algorithms like SVM and random forest, as well as models like SARIMA and VAR that require longer‐term and multi‐variable structures, were deemed unsuitable for the study's objectives and data structure and were therefore excluded. To make the final decision, each model's performance was evaluated using RMSE, MAE, and MAPE metrics in selecting methods.

To mitigate the risk of overfitting, several precautions were taken depending on the characteristics of the forecasting models. In the LSTM model, which consists of three layers, a dropout layer is added after each layer to prevent overfitting during training. The model consists of a total of 152,555 parameters, 101,704 of which are optimised. Due to the large number of parameters in the LSTM structure, the model was carefully configured during the learning process to prevent it from overfitting. The NNAR model was run by establishing 20 different neural networks and taking the average of their outputs. This approach reduced model variance, producing more balanced forecasts and lowering the risk of overfitting. In the MLP model, first‐order differences were taken to stabilise the data removing trends and seasonality. This reduced the model's complexity and prevented it from overfitting the overall structure. In traditional time series models such as ARIMA and Holt‐Winters, overfitting is less common due to the limited number of parameters. However, care was taken to determine the most appropriate structure based on AIC, BIC, and residual analyses, when selecting these models.

The goal of the Capacity Planning phase is to maximise the dispatch of ambulances to all cases assigned to a station (100% call response capacity) and to minimise the response time to each case. A Monte Carlo simulation was used to determine the ambulance capacities of the stations during the Capacity Planning phase. This method is preferred because it allows the creation of a large number of stochastic ambulance call scenarios. It also enables the determination of optimum capacity levels that minimise the response time by simulating these scenarios.

Within the scope of the analysis, the aim was to determine the number of ambulances that would provide 5‐, 10‐, and 30‐min response times. These periods were determined according to the standards of the Ministry of Health of the Republic of Türkiye and the World Health Organisation's time to reach life‐threatening and non‐life‐threatening cases. The Monte Carlo Simulation was run 10,000 times to ensure the reliability of the results. The Poisson distribution was used to simulate the number of calls in each simulation.

Forecasting EMS Calls

Forecasts using various methods were systematically reported, and the models were used to forecast the number of calls for each district within 80% and 95% forecast ranges over a 12‐month period. Firstly, the Holt‐Winters Method was used to forecast the number of calls in Çankaya district, since the data had a consistent seasonal model regardless of the overall level of the time series. The ARIMA model was used to forecast the EMS calls. Different parameter adjustments were made to select the best model that minimised error without overfitting. Criteria such as AIC, BIC, ACF, and the Theil index were examined. The ARIMA model parameters were determined iteratively, and the ARIMA (2,1,0) model with the best result was used (Figure 2).

Forecasts of EMS calls using ARIMA.

An MLP model was used to forecast EMS calls. The MLP model was structured with a neuron located in a hidden layer and trained using 20 repetitions. First‐order differences were taken to eliminate trends and seasonality and increase the stability of the dataset (Figure 3).

Forecasts of EMS calls using MLP.

The NNAR Method was used to forecast EMS calls. The NNAR (1,1,2) [12] structure, which was found to provide the best results among the NNAR models, was used. This structure refers to a neural network with delay input, a hidden layer with two neurons, and an output layer. To increase forecast robustness, a group approach was adopted by averaging forecasts from 20 different neural networks. Each network in the groups was structured as 2‐2‐1 (two input neurons, two neurons in the hidden layer, and one output neuron; the structure had a total of nine weights). The networks were configured with linear output units, and the error variance (σ2) of the model was predicted to be 30.237 (Figure 4).

Forecasts of EMS calls using NNAR.

LSTM method was used to forecast EMS calls. The model comprises three layers, each followed by a dropout layer to prevent overfitting. The first LSTM layer comprises 50 units and 10,400 parameters, and it captures the temporal dependencies in the input array and sends the entire data array to the next LSTM layer. The second LSTM layer contains 50 units and 20,200 parameters to capture more complex patterns and dependencies. The third LSTM layer also comprises 50 units, which reduces the output to a single vector. This sums the time series data into a fixed‐size vector characterised by 20,200 parameters. Finally, forecasts are created in the last layer with one unit and 51 parameters. In total, the model comprises 152,555 parameters, and the number of optimising parameters is 101,704 (Figure 5).

Forecasts of EMS calls using LSTM.

The numbers of EMS calls forecasted using several models were compared. The Holt‐Winters model produced higher forecasts in the early months, whereas the ARIMA (2,1,0) model displayed more consistent predictions, with identical forecasts each month. The MLP model generated forecasts that fluctuated moderately between months. The NNAR (1,1,2)[12] and LSTM models tend to produce predictions that either remain consistent or slightly decline over time. The LSTM model forecasted slightly lower values towards the end of the year than the other models. These variations highlight how different forecasting methods produce varying forecasts, with some models showing more fluctuation than others across months (Table 1).

Discussion

This study aims to forecast the demand for ambulance services provided as part of EMS and to plan the capacity of ambulance stations based on these forecasts. Past call data for Ankara province was analysed using forecasting models, revealing the distribution and patterns of call intensity over time. The forecasts obtained were used to determine the number of ambulances required by ambulance stations based on different case response times; capacity planning was conducted accordingly. These findings serve the primary objective of the study by both enhancing the efficiency of service delivery and supporting timely intervention.

The performance of the forecasting models used in the study varied across districts. Overall, the best results were obtained with the NNAR model. The ARIMA model provided consistent and reliable results, especially for short‐term forecasts. Although the MLP model achieved high accuracy in some districts, it did not perform consistently across all districts. Although the LSTM model theoretically has the advantage to model long‐term dependencies, it did not perform as expected in this study due to the limited sample size used. The large number of parameters in an LSTM optimised for large datasets and long time series. Working with short and irregular series prevented the model from fully leveraging its strengths. This demonstrates that models that do not fully align with the data structure may fall short in practice despite their theoretical advantages. Time series‐based methods were successful in short‐term forecasts with stable and low error rates, while artificial neural network‐based models demonstrated superiority in capturing complex patterns within the data. Models such as MLP and NNAR provided better adaptation to the context with their ability to recognise non‐linear patterns, but their performance was sensitive to the structure and size of the training data. Since technical settings such as model parameter determination, learning rate, and the number of hidden layers directly impact success, these models do not automatically produce successful results in every context. The notable differences in models across districts clearly highlight the necessity of context‐specific model selection in forecasting processes.

A systematic review examining 24 studies on ambulance demand forecast models found that ANNs were the most effective approach to capture complex patterns in EMS call data [20]. A study showed that MLP models performed better than time‐series and industry‐specific forecasting methods in forecasting the number of EMS calls, and they can successfully distinguish between spatial and temporal differences [24]. The prominence of the NNAR and MLP models in our study supports these results Time‐series models such as ARIMA and Holt‐Winters also performed well for short‐term forecasts but were less effective for long‐term forecasts [21]. Traditional time series models such as ARIMA and Holt‐Winters have been observed to work consistently and with low error rates in short‐term forecasts. This is consistent with findings in the literature that these models perform well on data where structures such as trends and seasonality are dominant. Similar results were obtained in the forecasting study conducted by [25] using the ARIMA model. In a systematic review, it was suggested that a single method is not universally superior; the choice of method should be compatible with the data and should depend on the context, emphasising the diversity of effective forecasting approaches [20]. For instance, autoregressive models and double‐seasonal ARIMA models with special day effects can forecast the number of EMS calls per day [26]. A combined Poisson neural network model, multiple linear regression, ARIMA, and multivariate grey models can improve forecasting performance [25]. This consistency, which highlighted the performance of forecasting methods among different studies, strengthened the reliability of the forecasting models used in this study and emphasised the importance of considering temporal patterns in EMS planning.

According to the capacity planning findings, it was determined that no station could meet the 5–10 minute transportation targets with its current capacity. In the case of Çankaya district, it was found, in particular, that the number of ambulances needed to be increased at the majority of stations to meet the target times. While three ambulances are required to meet the 5‐min target at some stations, only one or two ambulances are currently available. This situation indicates an inadequacy that negatively affects the effectiveness of service delivery. Additionally, in the planning process conducted using Monte Carlo simulations, it was determined that capacity distributions vary according to the case density and current response times of the districts.

The findings on capacity planning are supported by research that emphasised the positive impact of adding ambulances to areas where services were previously found to be insufficient, enhancing access [27]. Another study considered demographic factors such as age distribution and chronic disease prevalence in addition to the variables included in the model and conducted capacity planning within the scope of these variables [28]. The need to consider traffic scenarios in ambulance capacity planning has been emphasised [29]. Additionally, ambulance capacity was optimised using Monte Carlo simulations to minimise daily operational costs [30]. Furthermore, the use of simulation‐based optimization techniques, integrated with spatiotemporal stochastic demand models, has significantly reduced delays and costs in ambulance services [31]. Another study also designed a model to calculate the number of ambulances required for different response times, maximising the response rate of incoming calls [32]. The Monte Carlo simulation provides a robust framework for optimising ambulance capacity planning considering several factors that affect demand and resource allocation. This approach is compatible with the existing literature and provides practical information to increase the effectiveness of EMS.

Limitations

Within the scope of this discussion, it should be noted that this study has some limitations. In this research, the activities of transport ambulances were excluded from the scope and it was limited to emergency aid ambulances. The forecasting phase of the study was limited to the time from the receipt of the call to the arrival of the patient, and the processes of treating the patient at the scene or transferring the patient to the nearest health institution were excluded.

The forecasting process was conducted for seven districts with high call volumes, while capacity planning covered 121 stations across Ankara. However, this article only presents detailed results for the Çankaya district. Systematic comparisons between districts with different socio‐economic structures could provide more in‐depth analyses in future studies. Grouping districts according to their demographic characteristics or urbanisation levels could increase the generalisability of the findings and facilitate the development of more diverse contextual recommendations for policymakers.

Conclusion

Accurate forecasting of ambulance demand is vital for optimising resource allocation in EMS. This study examined different models to forecast EMS call volumes and improve response times. The findings highlight the importance of strategic capacity planning to enhance the efficiency and effectiveness of EMS. The average response time of 22 minutes is significantly insufficient compared to the response time standards in the EMS. It is recommended that the response time be between 5 and 10 minutes; however, none of the 139 stations met this target. This indicates a serious deficiency in system efficiency and effectiveness. Priority may be given to allocating resources for EMS, including ambulance and medical staff, to districts and stations in high demand to minimise response times. It is recommended to establish more stations in areas with high demand or to adjust the number of ambulances and personnel at the stations accordingly.

The use of different forecasting methods provided sound forecasts and revealed the strengths and weaknesses of each model. It was determined that the ARIMA model demonstrated robust performance, indicating higher accuracy and reliability than the other methods. The NNAR model also produced good forecasts and obtained satisfactory results, especially in terms of MAE and MAPE. Although the Holt‐Winters method occasionally produced good forecasts, it tended to perform less well than the ARIMA and NNAR models. The LSTM model outperformed the traditional methods with lower RMSE and MAE values due to the improved neural network structure. The MLP model did not show a stable performance, while it was successful in some districts, it lagged in others. The importance of selecting appropriate forecasting models that are adapted to the unique characteristics of each district was revealed.

The Monte Carlo simulation revealed the need for strategic ambulance allocation to meet the time required to reach the target case. It was revealed that certain capacity arrangements are necessary to optimise response time and to improve EMS in Ankara.

In this study, the number of calls for the next 12 months was forecasted based on 36 months of data. To better capture the trends and changes in EMS calls, it is recommended to use a larger dataset spread over a longer period for forecasts. In EMS, resource allocation can be optimised, and response times and efficiency can be improved by using advanced machine learning techniques and continuously evaluating model performance. Further research and development into advanced forecasting methods, including deep learning techniques, is recommended to improve forecasting accuracy and flexibility. To ensure the accuracy and continuity of the models, it is recommended to regularly evaluate the performance of the models and to make the necessary adjustments. It is recommended to include traffic accidents, traffic density, population structure, weather conditions, and some epidemiological indicators in forecasting models, as these factors are thought to better explain the number of EMS calls.
