Perturbative Approach to Nonlinear Capacitance Matrix Formulations
Habib Ammari, Clemens Thalhammer

Abstract
. We study a nonlinear Helmholtz system with cubic nonlinearity on high-contrast inclusions in three dimensions, and the solitons that emerge as the contrast δ \delta tends to zero. Using the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator and a capacitance formalism, we develop a perturbative cascade that expands the resonant frequency and field in powers of δ \sqrt{\delta} . Our main result is a rigorous two-way correspondence with a finite discrete nonlinear capacitance system: every discrete solution lifts to a continuous soliton (a convergent expansion, analytic in δ \sqrt{\delta} ), and every continuous family with the natural subwavelength scaling reduces to a discrete one. The construction is algorithmic, giving higher-order corrections in both the subwavelength and non-subwavelength regimes, the latter via a frequency-dependent capacitance matrix. We illustrate the theory numerically and characterise a symmetry-breaking bifurcation in a symmetric dimer.



Keywords.
Nonlinear subwavelength resonance, nonlinear discrete approximation, Helmholtz equation, perturbation theory.

AMS Subject classifications.
35P25, 35B25, 35B34 35C20.

1.
Introduction

High-contrast inclusions embedded in a homogeneous medium support subwavelength
resonances: localized resonant modes whose wavelength greatly exceeds the size
of the inclusions. The canonical example is the Minnaert resonance of an air
bubble in water
[
27
,
5
]
, and the phenomenon underlies much of
the recent activity in metamaterials
[
13
]
and subwavelength
physics
[
4
]
. A central insight of this theory is
that, in the high-contrast limit
\delta\to 0
, the resonant behaviour of the
full continuous scattering problem is governed at leading order by a
finite-dimensional object, the capacitance matrix, whose eigenvalues determine
the resonant frequencies
[
3
]
.

Much less is understood once nonlinearity enters. A well-studied example
[
11
,
17
,
23
]
is the class of Kerr nonlinearities, where the refractive index depends on the local field
intensity
[
22
]
, and the time-harmonic problem becomes a
nonlinear Helmholtz system whose resonant modes are continuous solitons.
A nonlinear analogue of the capacitance reduction was proposed
in
[
10
]
, where existence was established only at first order
in
\delta
. This leaves two natural questions open: does the discrete model
determine the continuous soliton to all orders, with a quantitative range of
validity; and is the reduction faithful, in the sense that every continuous
soliton with the natural subwavelength scaling actually arises from the discrete
system?

In this paper, we answer both questions affirmatively. Recasting the transmission
problem as a weak equation on the interior domain via the exterior
Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator, following the variational approach
of
[
20
,
18
,
11
]
, we expand the frequency and
field as fractional power series in
\sqrt{\delta}
and solve the resulting
cascade recursively. Our first main result,Theorem
3.1
, shows
that any solution of the discrete nonlinear capacitance system lifts, under a
non-degeneracy assumption, to a continuous soliton given by a convergent
expansion with explicit recursive coefficients; in particular, it is analytic in
\sqrt{\delta}
. The principal
difficulty is controlling the convolution sums produced by the coupling of
frequency to field and by the cubic term, which we handle through a
combinatorial concentration estimate. Our second main result
Theorem
3.2
is the converse: every continuous family obeying
the natural bounds reduces, after passing to a subsequence, to a solution of the
discrete system. Together, these establish a two-way correspondence, showing that
the discrete model captures the nonlinear resonances faithfully.

We then extend the perturbative method beyond the subwavelength regime, where
the frequency is
\mathcal{O}(1)
at leading order, the nonlinearity no longer acts as a
perturbation, and the relevant object is a frequency-dependent capacitance
matrix
[
8
]
. For isolated eigenvalues, we obtain asymptotic
expansions to arbitrary order, together with an existence result for
small-amplitude solitons.
Finally, the reduction to a finite discrete system makes techniques from the
discrete self-trapping and discrete nonlinear Schrödinger
literature
[
16
,
29
,
30
]
applicable to subwavelength
problems: as an illustration, we exhibit a symmetry-breaking bifurcation in a
symmetric dimer, with a criterion expressed through the eigenvalues of the
generalised capacitance matrix.

This paper is structured as follows. In Section 2, we introduce the problem formulation and some basic results about the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator and capacitance matrices in the subwavelength regime. In Section 3, we then state our main results. Sections 4 and 5 are then devoted to the proofs of these. In Section 6, we extend the approach to the non-subwavelength regime. Section 7 serves to numerically illustrate these findings. Additionally, we show that the emergence of symmetry breaking bifurcations as an application. In Section 8, we conclude the paper with a brief summary of the findings and a further outlook.

2.
Problem Setting and Preliminaries

2.1.
Problem Formulation

Let
\mathcal{I}
be a finite or infinite index set and let
D=\{D_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
be a collection of pairwise disjoint bounded inclusions in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, each with
\mathcal{C}^{1}
boundary. When
\mathcal{I}
is infinite, we additionally require the arrangement to be periodic, that is, there exists a lattice
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that the collection
D
is invariant under translations by elements of
\Lambda
. To each inclusion
D_{i}
, we assign a wave speed
v_{i}>0
and a contrast parameter
\delta_{i}>0
, and denote by
v
the wave speed in the exterior domain
\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{D}
. We set

(2.1)

v_{\min}:=\min_{i\in\mathcal{I}}v_{i},\qquad v_{\max}:=\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}}v_{i},\qquad\delta:=\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\lvert\delta_{i}\rvert,

and assume throughout that
0<v_{\min}\leq v_{\max}<\infty
and
\delta<\infty
.

We seek nontrivial pairs
(\omega,u)=(\omega(\delta),u(\delta))\in\mathbb{C}\times H^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
that satisfy the nonlinear Helmholtz system

(2.2)

\begin{cases}\Delta u+\dfrac{\omega^{2}}{v^{2}}\,u=0&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{D},\\[8.00003pt]
\Delta u+\dfrac{\omega^{2}}{v_{i}^{2}}\,u+\sigma\dfrac{\omega^{2}}{v_{i}^{2}}\,|u|^{2}u=0&\text{in }D_{i},\quad i\in\mathcal{I},\\[8.00003pt]
u\big|_{+}=u\big|_{-}&\text{on }\partial D_{i},\quad i\in\mathcal{I},\\[8.00003pt]
\delta_{i}\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}\bigg|_{+}=\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}\bigg|_{-}&\text{on }\partial D_{i},\quad i\in\mathcal{I},\\[8.00003pt]
u\text{ is outgoing,}\end{cases}

where
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
is the nonlinearity parameter, with
\sigma<0
corresponding to the focusing and
\sigma>0
to the defocusing case, and the subscripts
\pm
denote the limits taken from outside and inside
D_{i}
, respectively. We refer to such solutions as
continuous solitons
with frequency
\omega^{2}
. The system (
2.2
) arises as the time-harmonic reduction of the nonlinear wave equation
[
10
]
, with cubic nonlinearity modelling a Kerr-type effect that depends on the local field intensity
[
22
]
. The high-contrast limit
\delta\to 0
describes inclusions whose material parameters differ strongly from the surrounding medium, a regime in which subwavelength resonances are known to emerge
[
10
]
. Particular attention will be paid to the
subwavelength regime
, in which
\delta\to 0
and
\omega=\mathcal{O}(\delta^{1/2})
.

2.2.
Dirichlet-to-Neumann Operator and Capacitance Formalism

We begin by introducing the Dirichlet-to-Neumann (DtN) operator, which reduces the exterior problem to a boundary operator. Using it, we reformulate (
2.2
) as a weak equation on the interior domain and introduce the capacitance matrix that governs the leading-order subwavelength behaviour.

Definition 2.1

(Exterior Dirichlet-to-Neumann Operator)
.

Suppose
\lvert\mathcal{I}\rvert<\infty
and
\omega^{2}
is not a Dirichlet scattering resonance of
D
. The exterior Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator
\mathcal{T}^{\omega}:H^{1/2}(\partial D)\to H^{-1/2}(\partial D)
is defined by

(2.3)

\mathcal{T}^{\omega}f=\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}\bigg|_{\partial D},

where
u
is the unique solution to

(2.4)

\begin{cases}\Delta u+\omega^{2}u=0&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{D},\\
u\big|_{+}=f&\text{on }\partial D,\\
u\text{ is outgoing.}\end{cases}

The following are well known properties of the DtN map
[
18
,
2
,
14
]
in the finite case.

Proposition 2.2
.

Assume that
\lvert\mathcal{I}\rvert<\infty
. Then, the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator
\mathcal{T}^{\omega}:H^{1/2}(\partial D)\to H^{-1/2}(\partial D)
is a meromorphic operator valued function in
\omega
with poles at
the scattering resonances of
-\Delta
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{D}
with Dirichlet boundary conditions on
\partial D
.

In the setting of a crystal of resonators in two dimensions, the following weaker result is known
[
10
, Proposition 2.2]
(in this reference only the two-dimensional setting was considered, however using Friedrichs’ inequality, see, e.g.,
[
28
]
, the result can easily be generalised).

Proposition 2.3
.

Assume that
\lvert\mathcal{I}\rvert=\infty
, the resonators form a crystal and that
\omega^{2}_{0}
is not in the spectrum of the exterior Dirichlet Laplacian. Then, the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator
\mathcal{T}^{\omega}:H^{1/2}(\partial D)\to H^{-1/2}(\partial D)
is analytic in a small neighbourhood of
\omega_{0}^{2}
.

In particular, the above results imply that the DtN operator admits a convergent power series expansion at points
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
, away from the exterior Dirichlet spectrum. It is known that for certain arrangements, the exterior Dirichlet Laplacian does exhibit spectral gaps
[
15
,
21
]
.

To obtain a formulation on the interior domain alone, we multiply each equation in (
2.2
) by a test function
w\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, integrate by parts over each
D_{i}
, and use the transmission conditions to replace the exterior normal derivative by
\delta\,\mathcal{T}^{\omega/v}u
on
\partial D
. This yields the weak formulation: Find
u\in H^{1}(D)
such that

(2.5)

\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\int_{D_{i}}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\overline{w}-\frac{\omega^{2}}{v_{i}^{2}}\left(u\overline{w}+\sigma\lvert u\rvert^{2}u\overline{w}\right)\,dx-\delta\langle\mathcal{T}^{\omega/v}u,w\rangle_{\partial D}=0

for all
w\in H^{1}(D)
. This formulation was first used in the one-dimensional
[
20
]
and then extended to three dimensions in
[
18
]
. In these works, the key observation is that (
2.5
) can be treated as a perturbation of the coercive sesquilinear form

(2.6)

a(u,w):=\int_{D}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\overline{w}\,dx+\int_{D}u\,dx\int_{D}\overline{w}\,dx,

whose coercivity follows from the Poincaré–Wirtinger inequality. In particular, the linear theory of the capacitance matrix formalism (see, e.g.,
[
3
,
4
]
) was successfully recovered in the limit
\delta\rightarrow 0
using this approach in
[
18
,
6
]
.

We now introduce the capacitance matrix, which captures the leading-order coupling between the inclusions.

Definition 2.4

(Capacitance Matrix)
.

For each
i\in\mathcal{I}
, let
\mathbf{1}_{D_{i}}
denote the characteristic function of
\partial D_{i}
:

(2.7)

\mathbf{1}_{D_{i}}(x)=\begin{cases}1,&x\in\partial D_{i},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The capacitance matrix
\mathcal{C}=(\mathcal{C}_{ij})
is defined by

(2.8)

\mathcal{C}_{ij}=-\langle\mathcal{T}_{0}\mathbf{1}_{D_{i}},\mathbf{1}_{D_{j}}\rangle,

where
\mathcal{T}_{0}=\mathcal{T^{0}}
is the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator for
\omega=0
.

There are several equivalent definitions of the capacitance matrix; it was originally derived via Gohberg–Sigal theory
[
5
,
3
]
. The formulation above, in terms of the static DtN operator
\mathcal{T}_{0}
, is the most natural for our purposes.
When
\lvert\mathcal{I}\rvert<\infty
, the capacitance matrix has the following properties
[
3
]
in three dimensions.

Lemma 2.5
.

The capacitance matrix
\mathcal{C}
is symmetric and positive definite.

To state the central result of the linear theory, we introduce the mass matrix
\mathbf{M}=\mathrm{diag}\!\left(\lvert D_{i}\rvert/v_{i}^{2}\right)
and the generalised capacitance matrix

(2.9)

\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{gen}}:=\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathcal{C},

whose eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots
govern the leading-order subwavelength resonances.

Theorem 2.6

(Fundamental Theorem of Subwavelength Physics)
.

The linear subwavelength resonance problem (
\sigma=0
) admits
\lvert\mathcal{I}\rvert
resonant frequencies that satisfy

(2.10)

\omega_{n}=\sqrt{\delta\lambda_{n}}+\mathcal{O}(\delta),

where
\lambda_{n}
is the
n
-th eigenvalue of
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{gen}}
.

If instead
\lvert\mathcal{I}\rvert=\infty
and the arrangement is periodic, it is easy to see that the capacitance matrix takes the form of a Laurent operator. Classically, this regime has been studied by first applying the Floquet-Bloch transform to the following linear Helmholtz problem:

(2.11)

\begin{cases}\Delta u+\dfrac{\omega^{2}}{v^{2}}\,u=0&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{D},\\[8.00003pt]
\Delta u+\dfrac{\omega^{2}}{v_{i}^{2}}\,u=0&\text{in }D_{i},\quad i\in\mathcal{I},\\[8.00003pt]
u\big|_{+}=u\big|_{-}&\text{on }\partial D_{i},\quad i\in\mathcal{I},\\[8.00003pt]
\delta_{i}\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}\bigg|_{+}=\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}\bigg|_{-}&\text{on }\partial D_{i},\quad i\in\mathcal{I},\\[8.00003pt]
u\text{ satisfies an outgoing radiation condition.}\end{cases}

Then, layer potential techniques have been used to derive a quasiperiodic capacitance matrix. Concretely, by taking the Floquet-Bloch transform of the linear problem (see
2.11
), one obtains

(2.12)

\begin{cases}\Delta u+\dfrac{\omega^{2}}{v^{2}}\,u=0&\text{in }Y\setminus\overline{D},\\[8.00003pt]
\Delta u+\dfrac{\omega^{2}}{v_{i}^{2}}\,u=0&\text{in }D_{i},\quad i\in\mathcal{I},\\[8.00003pt]
u\big|_{+}=u\big|_{-}&\text{on }\partial D_{i},\quad i\in\mathcal{I},\\[8.00003pt]
\delta_{i}\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}\bigg|_{+}=\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}\bigg|_{-}&\text{on }\partial D_{i},\quad i\in\mathcal{I},\\[8.00003pt]
u\text{ is $\alpha$-quasiperiodic.}\end{cases}

For
\alpha>0
, one can define the
\alpha
-quasiperiodic single layer potential using the Floquet-Bloch Transform of the Green function. The remaining analysis leads to the following formulation of the quasiperiodic capacitance matrix:

(2.13)

\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}_{ij}=-\int_{\partial D_{j}}(\mathcal{S}_{D}^{\alpha})^{-1}[\mathbf{1}_{D_{i}}]dx.

The following result holds

.

Theorem 2.7
.

Suppose that the fundamental cell
Y
contains
N
resonators, and let
\alpha>c>0
. The linear

N

(2.14)

\omega_{i}^{\alpha}=\sqrt{\delta\lambda_{i}^{\alpha}}+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{3/2}),

\lambda_{i}^{\alpha}

2.3.
A Cascade of Equations

We seek a formal solution of (
2.2
) in terms of fractional power series in
\delta
:

(2.15)

u=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\delta^{n/2}u_{n}\quad\text{and}\quad\omega=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\delta^{n/2}\omega_{n}.

The frequency-dependent DtN operator may be expanded as

(2.16)

\mathcal{T}^{\omega/v}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\delta^{n/2}\mathcal{A}_{n},\quad\text{where}\quad\mathcal{A}_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{v^{k}}\mathcal{T}_{k}\sum_{i_{1}+\dots+i_{k}=n}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\omega_{i_{j}},

and
\mathcal{A}_{0}=\mathcal{T}_{0}
. Substituting the ansatz (
2.15
) into the weak formulation (
2.5
) and collecting terms of order
\delta^{n/2}
, we obtain the following cascade of equations:

(2.17)

\displaystyle\int_{D}\nabla u_{n}\cdot\nabla\overline{w}\,dx

\displaystyle-\sum_{k+l+m=n}\int_{D}\dfrac{\omega_{k}\omega_{l}}{v_{i}^{2}}u_{m}\overline{w}\,dx

\displaystyle-\sigma\sum_{k+l+m+r+s=n}\int_{D}\dfrac{\omega_{k}\omega_{l}}{v_{i}^{2}}u_{m}\overline{u}_{r}u_{s}\overline{w}\,dx

\displaystyle-\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}\langle\mathcal{A}_{k}u_{n-k-2},w\rangle_{\partial D}=0.

To solve (
2.17
) iteratively, we decompose
H^{1}(D)=\mathcal{V}_{d}\oplus\mathcal{V}_{0}
, where
\mathcal{V}_{d}
is the space of functions that are constant on each
D_{i}
, and
\mathcal{V}_{0}
is the space of mean-zero fluctuations.

For
n=0
and
n=1
, the sums involving
\omega
and the DtN map in (
2.17
) are empty. We simply obtain:

(2.18)

\int_{D}\nabla u_{n}\cdot\nabla\overline{w}\,dx=0\quad\text{for all }w\in H^{1}(D),

which implies that
u_{0},u_{1}\in\mathcal{V}_{d}
. Let
u_{0}|_{D_{i}}=a_{i}
.

Crucially, evaluating the cascade at
n=2
and restricting the test function
w
to the subspace
\mathcal{V}_{d}
annihilates the gradient term (
\nabla\overline{w}=0
). The equation evaluates precisely to

(2.19)

-\int_{D}\dfrac{\omega_{1}^{2}}{v_{i}^{2}}\left(1+\sigma\lvert u_{0}\rvert^{2}\right)u_{0}\overline{w}\,dx-\langle\mathcal{T}_{0}u_{0},w\rangle_{\partial D}=0.

Recognizing that
\langle\mathcal{T}_{0}u_{0},w\rangle_{\partial D}=-w^{*}\mathcal{C}\mathbf{a}
and integrating the volume terms exactly recovers the discrete nonlinear capacitance system:

(2.20)

(\mathcal{C}-\omega_{1}^{2}\mathbf{M})\mathbf{a}-\sigma\omega_{1}^{2}\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{a})=0,

where
\mathbf{M}=\text{diag}(|D_{i}|/v_{i}^{2})
and
\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{a})
contains the localised cubic interactions.

For
n\geq 2
, the hierarchy is solved recursively. Assume that
u_{0},\dots,u_{n-1}
and
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n}
are known. To compute
u_{n}
and
\omega_{n+1}
, we proceed in three steps. Before undertaking this endeavour, we first require some more notation. We define
F_{n}\in(H^{1}(D))^{*}
by

(2.21)

\displaystyle\langle F_{n},w\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k+l+m=n\\
1\leq m\leq n-3\\
1\leq k,l\leq n-2\end{subarray}}\int_{D}\dfrac{\omega_{k}\omega_{l}}{v_{i}^{2}}u_{m}\overline{w}\,dx+\sigma\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k+l+m+r+s=n\\
\\
1\leq m,r,s\leq n-3\\
1\leq k,l\leq n-2\end{subarray}}\int_{D}\dfrac{\omega_{k}\omega_{l}}{v_{i}^{2}}u_{m}\overline{u}_{r}u_{s}\overline{w}\,dx

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{n-2}\langle\mathcal{A}_{k}u_{n-k-2},w\rangle_{\partial D}.

Note that
F_{n}
depends only on
u_{0},\dots,u_{n-3}
and
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n-2}
. Next, we define the
\mathbb{R}
-linear operator
\mathcal{L}_{u_{0}}:H^{1}(D)\rightarrow(H^{1}(D))^{*}
by

(2.22)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{L}_{u_{0}}(\phi),w\rangle:=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\int_{D_{i}}

\displaystyle\dfrac{\omega_{1}^{2}}{v_{i}^{2}}\phi\overline{w}+2\sigma\dfrac{\omega_{1}^{2}}{v_{i}^{2}}|u_{0,d}|^{2}\phi\overline{w}+\sigma\dfrac{\omega_{1}^{2}}{v_{i}^{2}}u_{0,d}^{2}\overline{\phi}\overline{w}\,dx+\langle\mathcal{T}_{0}\phi,w\rangle_{\partial D}.

For
u\in H^{1}(D)
, we denote by
u_{d}
the projection of
u
into
\mathcal{V}_{d}
and by
u_{0}=u-u_{d}\in\mathcal{V}_{0}
. Finally, let
B:\mathbb{C}\rightarrow(L^{2}(D))^{*}
be defined by

(2.23)

\langle B(\alpha),w\rangle=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\int_{D_{i}}\frac{2\alpha\omega_{1}}{v_{i}^{2}}(1+\sigma\lvert u_{0}\rvert^{2})u_{0}\overline{w}\,dx.

We thus rewrite (
2.17
) in the following much more succinct form:

(2.24)

\langle\nabla u_{n},\nabla w\rangle-\langle\mathcal{L}_{u_{0}}u_{n-2},w\rangle-\langle B(\alpha),w\rangle=\langle F_{n},w\rangle.

Step 1:
u_{n,0}
:
In a first step, we determine
u_{n,0}\in\mathcal{V}_{0}
by testing (
2.24
) at level
n
against
w\in\mathcal{V}_{0}
. The strict coercivity of the Laplacian on
\mathcal{V}_{0}
ensures unique invertibility.

Step 2:
\omega_{n+1}
To compute
\omega_{n+1}
, we test equation (
2.24
) at level
n+2
with
iu_{0}
. Because
iu_{0}
lies in the kernel of
\mathcal{L}_{u_{0}}
, (
2.24
) reduces to

(2.25)

\langle B(\alpha),u_{0}\rangle=\langle F_{n+2},u_{0}\rangle,

for which a unique solution exists, provided that
\lVert u_{0}\rVert^{2}+\sigma\lVert u_{0}\rVert_{4}^{4}\neq 0
.

Step 3:
u_{n,d}
In a final step, we compute the resonator-wise constant contribution
u_{n,d}
. Testing at level
n+2
with a test function in
\mathcal{V}_{d}
, we are tasked with solving

(2.26)

\langle\mathcal{L}_{u_{0}}u_{n,d},w\rangle=\langle F_{n+2}-B(\omega_{n+1}),w\rangle.

Due to our choice of
\omega_{n+1}
, we may require that
u_{n,d}\perp u_{0}
. Thus, we need to invert the operator
\mathcal{L}_{u_{0}}
on the orthogonal complement of
u_{0}
in
\mathcal{V}_{d}
, which we denote by
\mathcal{W}_{u_{0}}
. Herein lies our key assumption:

Assumption 1
.

The operator
\mathcal{L}_{u_{0}}:\mathcal{W}_{u_{0}}\rightarrow\mathcal{W}_{u_{0}}
is invertible.

Remark 2.8
.

It is a classical result of the Lyapunov-Schmidt theory
[
24
,
1
]
that this assumption is satisfied for
\omega_{1}
in a small neighbourhood of an isolated eigenvalue of the generalised capacitance matrix. It is known that the lowest eigenvalue of the generalised capacitance matrix is always simple
[
19
]
, so the results of Section
3
are not vacuous.

3.
Main Results

In this section, we state our main results and contrast them with existing
work. Our central contribution is a rigorous two-way correspondence between
the discrete nonlinear capacitance system (
2.20
) and
the continuous nonlinear Helmholtz problem (
2.2
):
every discrete solution lifts to a continuous one
(assuming a non-degeneracy condition; see Theorem
3.1
), and every continuous
solution with the natural scaling reduces to a discrete one
(Theorem
3.2
). Together, these establish that the discrete
model is faithful in the sense that no solution branch is added or missed by the
capacitance formulation reduction.

The lifting direction extends
[
10
]
, where the existence of
continuous solutions associated with the discrete system was established
only at first order in
\delta
. We construct the continuous solution as a
convergent fractional power series in
\sqrt{\delta}
to arbitrary order, with
explicit recursive coefficients and a quantitative threshold
\delta_{0}
;
analyticity in
\sqrt{\delta}
follows immediately. The reduction direction
has no counterpart in existing work: previous results assume
a
priori
that solutions admit a convergent expansion, whereas we prove
that every continuous family with the bounds of (
3.2
)
is asymptotically discrete.

A complementary approach in the related high-refractive-index regime is
developed in
[
9
]
, where existence is obtained via
Lyapunov-Schmidt reduction. While this approach can also be shown to work in the present setting, the benefit of the more involved series approach is that it also yields an algorithm to compute higher order corrections.

Theorem 3.1

(Discrete-to-continuous lifting)
.

Let
(\mathbf{a},\omega_{1})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}
,
with
\omega_{1}\neq 0
, be a solution of the discrete nonlinear
capacitance system (
2.20
), and suppose that
Assumption
1
holds.
Then, there exists
\delta_{0}>0
such that, for every

\delta<\delta_{0}
, problem (
2.2
) admits
a nontrivial solution
(u,\omega)\in H^{1}(D)\times\mathbb{C}

given by the convergent expansions

(3.1)

u=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\delta^{n/2}u_{n},\qquad\omega=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\delta^{n/2}\omega_{n},

where

4.
Proof of Theorem
3.1

In this section, we show that the iteratively defined sequence of Subsection
2.3
converges for
\delta
sufficiently small, which finalises the proof of Theorem
3.1
. The main obstacle is controlling the convolution sums that arise at each step of the iteration due to the coupling of frequency to the solutions and the nonlinearity.

Our proof strategy is inspired by
[
31
]
; however, a key difference lies in the treatment of certain combinatorial sums. As the decay exponent

p
in the bound of Theorem
4.1
increases, the weight of such sums concentrates at the corners of the simplex
\{(i_{1},\dots,i_{l}):i_{j}\geq 1,\,\sum i_{j}=k\}
, where a single index carries most of the total. Exploiting this concentration yields constants that are bounded independently of

k
, allowing us to close the induction with an explicit threshold on
\delta
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Assume that there exist
\mu,\nu,\alpha>0
such that for some positive integer
p

the following estimates hold for
k=0,\dots,n
:

(4.1)

\lvert\omega_{k}\rvert\leq\frac{\mu\exp(\alpha(k-1))}{k^{p}},\qquad\lVert u_{k}\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{\nu\exp(\alpha k)}{(k+1)^{p}},\qquad\lVert\nabla u_{k}\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{\nu\exp(\alpha k)}{(k+1)^{p}}.

Then, there exists
C_{p}>0
, independent of
n
, such that

(4.2)

\Bigl\lVert\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k+l+m=n+2,\\
m\neq n,\\
k,l\neq n+1\end{subarray}}\frac{\omega_{k}\omega_{l}}{v_{i}^{2}}u_{m}\Bigr\rVert_{H^{1}}\leq\frac{C_{p}\,v_{\min}^{-2}\mu^{2}\nu\exp(\alpha n)}{(n+1)^{p}}.

Moreover,
C_{p}\to 0
as
p\to\infty
.

Proof.

Applying our assumptions, we find that

(4.3)

\displaystyle\Bigl\lVert\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k+l+m=n+2,\\
m\neq n,\\
k,l\neq n+1\end{subarray}}\frac{\omega_{k}\omega_{l}}{v_{i}^{2}}u_{m}\Bigr\rVert_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq v_{\min}^{-2}\mu^{2}\nu\exp(\alpha n)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k+l+m=n+2,\\
0\leq m\leq n-1,\\
1\leq k,l\leq n\end{subarray}}k^{-p}l^{-p}(m+1)^{-p}

\displaystyle=v_{\min}^{-2}\mu^{2}\nu\exp(\alpha n)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k+l+m=n+3,\\
1\leq m\leq n,\\
1\leq k,l\leq n\end{subarray}}k^{-p}l^{-p}m^{-p}.

We are therefore interested in bounding

(4.4)

\displaystyle S_{n}^{(1)}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k+l+m=n+3,\\
1\leq m\leq n,\\
1\leq k,l\leq n\end{subarray}}k^{-p}l^{-p}m^{-p}.

Due to symmetry of the indices, we may assume that
k\leq l\leq m
and multiply the sum by three to obtain

(4.5)

\displaystyle S_{n}^{(1)}

\displaystyle\leq 3\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k+l+m=n+3,\\
1\leq m\leq l\leq k\leq n\end{subarray}}k^{-p}l^{-p}m^{-p}

\displaystyle\leq 3\sum_{m=3}^{\frac{2n}{3}+2}(n+3-m)^{-p}\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}k^{-p}(m-k)^{-p}.

We begin by bounding the inner sum. To this end, we note that
k^{-p}(m-k)^{-p}
is maximised at
(m-1)^{-p}
. This yields

(4.6)

\displaystyle S_{n}^{(1)}

\displaystyle\leq 3\sum_{m=3}^{\frac{2n}{3}+2}\,(m-1)^{-p+1}(n+3-m)^{-p}

\displaystyle\leq 3(n+1)^{-p}\sum_{m=3}^{\frac{2n}{3}+2}(m-1)\left(\frac{n+1}{(m-1)(n+3-m)}\right)^{p}.

The fraction admits two different upper bounds. On the one hand, similar to the previous argument,

(4.7)

\frac{n+1}{(m-1)(n+3-m)}\leq\frac{n+1}{2n},

and on the other hand, using that
m\leq\frac{2n}{3}+2
,

(4.8)

\frac{n+1}{(m-1)(n+3-m)}\leq\frac{3}{(m-1)}.

Hence, for any
1<q<p
,

(4.9)

\displaystyle S_{n}^{(1)}

\displaystyle\leq 3(n+1)^{-p}\sum_{m=3}^{\frac{2n}{3}+1}(m-1)\left(\frac{n+1}{2n}\right)^{p-q}\left(\frac{3}{m-1}\right)^{q}

\displaystyle\leq 3(n+1)^{-p}\left(\frac{n+1}{2n}\right)^{p-q}\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}m\left(\frac{3}{m}\right)^{q}.

For
q>2
, the sum converges and hence

(4.10)

S_{n}^{(1)}\leq\left(\frac{n+1}{2n}\right)^{p-q}\frac{9\cdot 3^{q}(\zeta(q-1)-1)}{\,(n+1)^{p}},

where
\zeta(\cdot)
denotes the Riemann zeta function. The claim follows.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Under the same assumptions as in Lemma
4.1

,
again independent of

(4.11)

\Bigl\lVert\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k+l+m+r+s=n+2,\\

Thus,

\displaystyle S_{n}^{(2)}

\displaystyle\leq 5(n+1)^{-p}\sum_{k=5}^{\frac{4}{5}n+4}\binom{k-1}{3}\left(\frac{n+1}{2n}\right)^{p-q}\left(\frac{5}{k-3}\right)^{q}

\delta\bigl\langle-\mathcal{C}\mathbf{a}^{\delta}+(\omega_{1}^{\delta})^{2}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{a}^{\delta}+\sigma(\omega_{1}^{\delta})^{2}\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{a}^{\delta}),\mathbf{w}\bigr\rangle=O\bigl((\lVert u^{\delta}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\lVert u^{\delta}\rVert_{L^{4}}^{4})^{1/2}\delta^{3/2}\bigr).

Dividing by

∎

The final step is to pass to a nontrivial limit

(\omega_{1},\mathbf{a})
. The argument is inspired

Suppose

Since

If

6.
Beyond the Subwavelength Regime

In this section, we study the applicability of the perturbative approach beyond the subwavelength regime. As we shall soon see, the nonlinearity in the non-subwavelength regime is qualitatively different from the subwavelength regime, which is why in this case we will have to contend ourselves with a weaker result. An upshot, however, of the analysis in this section is that the perturbative approach remains applicable for isolated eigenvalues of the capacitance matrix in the subwavelength and non-subwavelength regimes, hence giving asymptotic expansions to arbitrary order.

6.1.
The Frequency Dependent Capacitance Matrix

This section serves to briefly summarise the result of
[
7
]
. In what follows, we will restrict ourselves to the setting where
\lvert\mathcal{I}\rvert<\infty
.

We now denote by
\sigma(-\Delta_{N},D_{i})
the spectrum of the Neumann Laplacian on
D_{i}
and further define

(6.1)

\sigma(-\Delta_{N},D):=\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{I}}v_{i}^{2}\sigma(-\Delta_{N},D_{i}).

Since each
D_{i}
is compact, we find that
\sigma_{N}(-\Delta_{N},D)
is a countable sequence tending to infinity. Suppose now that
\omega_{0}^{2}\in\sigma(-\Delta_{N},D)
. Define

(6.2)

\mathcal{J}(\omega_{0})=\{i\in\mathcal{I}:w_{0}^{2}\in v_{i}^{2}\sigma(-\Delta_{N},D_{i})\}.

Let
m_{j}(\omega_{0})
denote the multiplicity of
\dfrac{\omega_{0}^{2}}{v_{j}^{2}}
in
\sigma(-\Delta_{N},D_{j})
. We now choose an orthonormal basis
\{u_{j,l}\}_{l=1}^{m_{j}}\in H^{1}(D_{i})
. Furthermore, note that these eigenfunctions embed naturally in
H^{1}(D)
by extension by zero on the other inclusions. We are now able to define the frequency dependent capacitance matrix.

Definition 6.1

(Frequency Dependent Capacitance Matrix)
.

Let
m(\omega_{0})=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}m_{i}(\omega_{0})
. Then, the capacitance matrix
\mathcal{C}(\omega_{0})
is the
m\times m
matrix defined by

(6.3)

\mathcal{C}(\omega_{0})_{(i,k)(j,l)}=-\int_{D_{i}}\mathcal{T}^{\omega_{0}/v}[u_{j,l}]\overline{u_{i,k}}dx.

The generalised frequency dependent capacitance matrix is further defined by

(6.4)

\mathcal{C}^{gen}(\omega_{0})=\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathcal{C}(\omega_{0}),

where
\mathbf{M}
is the diagonal matrix with
\mathbf{M}_{(i,k)(i,k)}=\dfrac{2\omega_{0}}{v_{i}^{2}}
.

To give just one example that the frequency dependent capacitance matrix behaves very differently from the standard capacitance matrix, compare the following Lemma
[
7
, Proposition 3.16]
with Lemma
2.5
.

Lemma 6.2
.

\mathcal{C}(\omega_{0})
is complex symmetric.

Theorem 6.3
.

The resonant frequencies of
2.11
satisfy

(6.5)

\omega(\delta)=\omega_{0}+\delta\omega_{1}+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2}),

where
\omega_{0}^{2}\in\sigma(-\Delta_{N},D)
and
\omega_{1}
is an eigenvalue of the generalised frequency dependent capacitance matrix.

6.2.
Asymptotics for the Linear Problem

We now use the perturbative approach to compute the asymptotic expansion of a resonant pair
(\omega(\delta),u(\omega))
related to a simple eigenvalue of the generalised frequency dependent capacitance matrix. Note that the analysis in this subsection applies mutatis mutandis to the subwavelength regime, for which such an analysis has already been carried out in
[
19
]
. While in the subwavelength regime, both approaches yield essentially the same results, the perturbative one carries the advantage that it gives a systematic way of computing higher order corrections. The benefit of this becomes apparent in Section

We begin by recalling the linear Helmholtz problem (
2.11
).
As in Subsection
2.3
, (

\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\int_{D_{i}}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\overline{w}-\frac{\omega^{2}}{v_{i}^{2}}u\overline{w}\,dx-\delta\langle\mathcal{T}^{\omega/v}u,w\rangle_{\partial D}=0.

Theorem

Remark 6.4
.

The key difference from the subwavelength regime is that the expansion is now in
\delta
as opposed to

. Moreover,
\omega_{0}
is no longer assumed to be zero.

Plugging this ansatz into equation (

\displaystyle\int_{D}\nabla u_{n}\cdot\nabla\overline{w}\,dx

\displaystyle-\sum_{k+l+m=n}\int_{D}\dfrac{\omega_{k}\omega_{l}}{v_{i}^{2}}u_{m}\overline{w}\,dx-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\langle\mathcal{A}_{k}^{\omega_{0}}u_{n-k-1},w\rangle_{\partial D}=0,

The equations at the first two orders are

\displaystyle\int_{D}\nabla u_{0}\cdot\nabla\overline{w}-\dfrac{\omega_{0}^{2}}{v_{i}^{2}}u_{0}\overline{w}\,dx=0,

Next, we proceed as in Subsection

We then proceed as follows:

Step 1:

6.3.
Existence of Small Amplitude Solutions for the Nonlinear Problem

In this subsection we again study (

that is perpendicular to
u_{1}
.

Next, we have to consider the growth of the coefficients. To this end, one chooses the ansatz

(6.28)

\lvert\omega_{k}\rvert\leq\frac{\mu\exp(\alpha(k-1))}{k^{p}},\qquad\lVert u_{k}\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{\nu\exp(\alpha(k-1))}{k^{p}},\qquad\lVert\nabla u_{k}\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{\nu\exp(\alpha(k-1))}{k^{p}}.

The strategy of the preceding sections revolving around avoiding the corner of the simplexes in the sums no longer works. However, the index shift in the above ansatz grants a factor of
\exp(-\alpha)
compared to Lemmas
4.1
and
4.2
, which can be used to control the error terms; for reference, see the technique employed in
[
31
]
. Thus, we obtain our final theorem.

Theorem 6.6
.

Let
\omega_{0}\in\sigma(-\Delta_{N},D)
and
(\mathbf{a},\omega_{1})\in\mathbb{C}^{N}\times\mathbb{C}
be an eigenvector-eigenvalue pair for the generalised frequency dependent capacitance matrix at
\omega_{0}
and suppose that Assumption
2
holds.
Then, there exists
\delta_{0}>0
such that, for every

\delta<\delta_{0}
, problem (
2.2
) admits
a nontrivial solution
(u,\omega)\in H^{1}(D)\times\mathbb{C}

given by the convergent expansions

(6.29)

u=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\delta^{n}u_{n},\qquad\omega=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\delta^{n}\omega_{n},

where
\mathbf{a}
determines the projection of
u
into the space of Neumann eigenfunctions. In particular, the pair
(u,\omega)
is analytic in
\delta
.

7.
Numerical Illustrations

This section presents some numerical illustrations, underlining the theoretical findings. We begin with a description of the numerical implementation of Section
2.3
.

Each cascade order requires a Neumann–Poisson solver on every resonator
together with the exterior coupling. The Poisson problems are discretised
spectrally: spherical harmonics
Y_{\ell}^{m}
up to degree
\ell_{\max}
for the
angular variables, and Chebyshev–Gauss–Lobatto collocation with
k_{\max}

nodes in the radius. As the angular Laplacian is diagonal in
Y_{\ell}^{m}
, each
mode reduces to a one-dimensional radial operator that is LU-factorised once
(with the Neumann condition and the regularity/zero-mean constraint imposed on
the boundary rows) and reused across orders; the Chebyshev basis converges
exponentially in
k_{\max}
, so the spatial error lies below the
cascade-truncation error even for modest
k_{\max}
.

The exterior is eliminated through the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator

T(\omega)
, built from the outgoing multipole expansion and the Helmholtz
addition theorem; its static
limit gives the capacitance matrix, and
T(\omega)
is Taylor-expanded in

\omega
so each order applies precomputed coefficients. The leading-order
nonlinear capacitance eigenproblem is solved by Newton’s method, after which
each higher order reduces to one back-substitution per resonator and a small
dense solve. All results use
\ell_{\max}=8
and
k_{\max}=16
, for which both
truncations converge below the reported errors; the accuracy is verified
against exact references when available
and otherwise through the
L^{2}
residuals of the interior equation and the
transmission conditions.

7.1.
Symmetry Breaking Bifurcations in Symmetric Dimers

In this subsection, we study the nonlinear problem (
2.2
) for a symmetric dimer setup of two identical spherical resonators of radius
r=0.1
, separated by a distance of
d=0.2
, with interior wave speed
v_{1}=v_{2}=1
. By the reflection symmetry of the dimer,
the capacitance matrix has the form

(7.1)

\mathcal{C}=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
b&a\end{pmatrix},

with eigenvalues
\lambda_{\pm}=v_{0}^{2}(a\pm b)/\lvert D_{0}\rvert
corresponding to the symmetric and antisymmetric
eigenmodes. Moreover, the entries
a
and

b
admit closed-form series expressions in bispherical
coordinates
[
26
]
, valid for all separations
including nearly touching configurations. This explicit form enables an
analytical study of the nonlinear branches.
Substituting
\mathbf{a}=(x,y)
into the discrete nonlinear
capacitance system (
2.20
) and writing
\lambda=\omega^{2}
for brevity, we obtain

(7.2)

ax+by=\lambda(x+\sigma x^{3}),\qquad bx+ay=\lambda(y+\sigma y^{3}),

where, by
S^{1}
invariance, we may take
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
. Adding
and subtracting (
7.2
) yields the factored system

(7.3)

\displaystyle(x+y)\bigl[(a+b)-\lambda-\lambda\sigma(x^{2}-xy+y^{2})\bigr]

\displaystyle=0,

(7.4)

\displaystyle(x-y)\bigl[(a-b)-\lambda-\lambda\sigma(x^{2}+xy+y^{2})\bigr]

\displaystyle=0,

from which three nontrivial branches emerge:

Symmetric branch
(
x=y
). The first factor vanishes, yielding

\sigma x^{2}=\frac{a+b-\lambda}{\lambda}.

This branch bifurcates from the trivial solution at
\lambda=a+b

along the symmetric eigendirection.

Antisymmetric branch
(
x=-y
). The second factor vanishes,
yielding

\sigma x^{2}=\frac{a-b-\lambda}{\lambda}.

This branch bifurcates from the trivial solution at
\lambda=a-b

along the antisymmetric eigendirection.

Asymmetric branch
(
x\neq\pm y
). Both factors vanish
simultaneously, yielding

xy=-\frac{b}{\sigma\lambda},\qquad x^{2}+y^{2}=\frac{a-\lambda}{\sigma\lambda}.

This branch is real and distinct from the previous two if and only if

(a-\lambda)^{2}>4b^{2}
and the resulting quadratic for
x^{2},y^{2}

admits two positive roots. It corresponds to states localised
predominantly on one resonator and emerges as a secondary bifurcation
from one of the primary branches at a critical amplitude.

The emergence of symmetry breaking bifurcations is well studied
[
16
,
25
,
12
]
. Indeed, the above characterisation has been done in
[
16
]
for a similar setup, moreover, therein the authors also explicitly treat trimer and quadrimer like systems. Symmetry breaking bifurcations were also proven to emerge in a high-refractive framework
[
9
]
.

8.
Conclusion

In this paper, we have studied the nonlinear Helmholtz problem with
outgoing radiation condition and cubic nonlinearity in the high-contrast
regime. Using perturbative techniques, we have established a two-way
correspondence between the emerging solitons and a discrete model: under
a non-degeneracy assumption on the discrete data, every solution of the
nonlinear capacitance system lifts to a continuous soliton, and every
continuous soliton with the natural subwavelength scaling reduces to a
discrete one. The capacitance matrix therefore faithfully captures the
nonlinear resonant behaviour of the full system in this regime.

The result is constructive: the cascade equations yield an iterative
algorithm for computing correction terms to arbitrary order, in both
the linear and nonlinear, subwavelength and non-subwavelength regimes. Moreover, analyticity is shown in
\sqrt{\delta}
, respectively in
\delta
. To
our knowledge, this is the first existence result for both subwavelength
and non-subwavelength solitons in the high-contrast regime. Beyond
establishing the correspondence itself, the reduction to a finite
discrete system makes techniques from the discrete self-trapping and
discrete nonlinear Schrödinger literature applicable to subwavelength
resonance problems. As a first illustration, we have shown that the
symmetric dimer admits a symmetry-breaking bifurcation with a criterion
expressed cleanly in terms of the eigenvalues of the generalised
capacitance matrix.
