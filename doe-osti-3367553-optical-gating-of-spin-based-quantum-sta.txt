Abstract:

This project developed a generalizable strategy for optical control of spin-based quantum states through
the Photoisomerization-Induced Spin–Charge Excited State (PISCES) process, in which a photochromic
spirooxazine ligand bound to an electronically bistable metal complex enables reversible, room-
temperature optical switching of spin states in both dilute matrices and the solid state. A multidisciplinary
team integrated synthesis, spectroscopy, theory, and surface science to establish design principles for
opto-spintronic quantum materials. Systematic structure–property studies across four spirooxazine series
revealed that donor–π–acceptor interactions govern thermal isomerization, which proceeds via a
rotational pathway. Pulsed EPR measurements demonstrated millisecond-scale coherence times in
cobalt-dioxolene-spirooxazine complexes, establishing their promise as optically addressable molecular
qubits; the approach was extended to Cu(II), Ni(II), and lanthanide complexes, including optical
modulation of zero-field splittings and clock transitions. Two-dimensional IR spectroscopy resolved
picosecond ground-state population exchange dynamics, while ab initio calculations elucidated
photoisomerization mechanisms and parametrized Hamiltonians for spin relaxation and decoherence.
Toward device integration, the team fabricated 2D polymer scaffolds for covalent qubit organization,
developed tip-enhanced Raman imaging of molecular monolayers, and demonstrated optical gating of
gate voltage in spirooxazine-graphene field-effect transistors. The project produced numerous
publications, dissertations, and presentations, and trained over 30 researchers.
Keywords: Opto-spintronics, Molecular qubits, Photochromes, Spin–charge excited states (PISCES),
Quantum coherence, Valence tautomerism, Cobalt-dioxolene complexes, Pulsed EPR spectroscopy, 2D
polymers

1.a Goals
The optical manipulation of spin quantum states (opto-spintronics) provides an important strategy for
quantum control with both temporal and spatial resolution. We have recently reported the generation of
photomagnetic states with unusually long lifetimes (seconds) in the thin film state at T > 300 K.
Incorporation of an optically bistable photochromic ligand (spirooxazine) into an electronically bistable
metal complex (cobalt-dioxolene) leads to a Photoisomerization-Induced Spin–Charge Excited State
(PISCES) process. The photoinduced structural change initiates an intramolecular charge transfer from a
low spin doublet state to a high spin state that is reversible. As the driving force (ΔGº) for metal-to-ligand
charge transfer processes is dependent on redox potential, a change in ligand field modulates the driving
force for the charge-transfer process in the metal complex. As the charge-transfer process is coupled to a
spin transition, the result is optical modulation of the spin state without direct excitation to a metal-
centered excited state.
The advantages of the PISCES strategy are: (i) optical modulation of spin state originates at the single
molecule level, giving rise to an externally addressable spin-based qubit; (ii) the optical gating occurs in
the solid state, allowing incorporation into device architectures; and (iii) a ligand-controlled photomagnetic
state that can be modified through synthesis. The overarching goal of this project was to correlate the
structural and electronic parameters of the ligand set and metal complex that govern the PISCES
process, with the long-term aim of developing a generalized strategy based on PISCES for quantum-
based switching, memory, and sensor applications.
The project aims were to:
 • Aim 1: Determine the fundamental electronic and structural factors of the photochromic ligand
 that govern the PISCES process, including the direction and magnitude of ligand field change
 with photoisomerization.
 • Aim 2: Evaluate the spin dynamics and decoherence times (T₁ and T₂) of the two resting
 magnetic states in the PISCES process, to determine design principles for spin-based qubits.
 • Aim 3: Determine the effect of intermolecular interactions on PISCES processes, spin lifetimes,
 and decoherence in nanostructured thin films and surfaces.

1.b Major Activities
[PI: Frank Group]
Major activities undertaken during the full funding period include three inter-related objectives:
Objective 1 — Structure-Property Relationships in Photochromic Spirooxazines (Aim 1). The factors
governing thermal relaxation rates in spirooxazines are not well understood, as the mechanism of ring
closure/opening along the ground state potential energy surface (PES) is unknown. Four series of
spirooxazines were prepared in which the donor-acceptor (D-A) character was varied: (i)
azahomoadamantyl derivatives (Adecer); (ii) azahomobicyclic derivatives (Alanthadka, Adecer); (iii)
heterocyclic indoline derivatives (Wimalasekera); and (iv) benzimidazole derivatives (Adecer).
Photochromism studies, variable-temperature kinetic measurements, and computational analysis
provided fundamental insight into thermal isomerization pathways and structural principles for controlling
thermal isomerization rates.
Objective 2 — Determination of Spin Dynamics in PISCES Complexes (Aim 2). The spin-lattice relaxation
and coherence times of the Co(III) state of spirooxazine-cobalt dioxolenes were determined by pulsed
EPR at W-band (National High Magnetic Field Laboratory) and X-band (R. David Britt, UC-Davis).
Decoherence times were found to be very long (ms timescale), establishing Co-dioxolene-spirooxazines
as promising for quantum applications. Studies were extended to Cu(II), Ni(II), and lanthanide
photochrome complexes. A new series of benzotriazinyl radical-based materials was initiated as spin
qubits with potential for charge storage. The effect of structure on spin relaxation dynamics, on-site
coulomb energy, and magnetic exchange interactions (entanglement) was investigated.
Objective 3 — Organization of Molecular Qubits in Arrays (Aim 3). Experiments on CoAPSO bulk thin
films revealed complex behavior and structural reorganization. Simpler thin film devices were investigated

[Co-PI: King Group]
Instrumentation and synthetic methodology for 2D polymer fabrication was the central activity. Major
activities included: (i) optimization of the synthesis of the monomeric precursor for the 2D polymer

[Co-PI: Lee Group]
Instrumentation for tip-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (TERS) was the primary focus. The homemade
STM became fully operational with integration of a pyrometer, 533 and 633 nm lasers, and a lock-in
amplifier. Activities centered on: (i) development and validation of in situ plasmonic tip preparation
techniques for silver STM tips; (ii) growth and initial imaging of FANTRIP layers using atomic force
microscopy (Bilkhu); (iii) growth and imaging of 2-naphthalenethiol self-assembled monolayers and
FANTRIP on Au(111) by STM (Thom); (iv) growth and TERS imaging of FANTRIP on Au(111) (Neill); and
(v) density functional theory calculations of 2D layers (Clapp). TERS imaging of the FANTRIP layer on a
single crystal surface is ongoing.
[Co-PI: Tucker Group]
The Tucker group utilized 2D infrared (2D IR) spectroscopy to investigate population exchange dynamics
in the photochromic compound APSO (Azahomoadamantylphenanthroline). Exchange dynamics were
measured via 2D IR to determine relaxation rates between different population states, with temperature-
dependent monitoring. An additional laser source was installed for irradiation-dependent experiments.
The cis/trans exchange rate of APSO was found to be 1.39 ± 0.09 ps, and the trans-PMC to SO open
form exchange rate was measured as 1.1 ± 0.1 ps.
[Co-PI: Varganov Group]
The Varganov group performed electronic structure calculations on PISCES metal complexes using
density functional theory (DFT) and ab initio wavefunction-based methods (CCSD(T) and CASSCF).
Activities included: (i) photoisomerization studies of free photochromic ligands (APSO, APESO, AQSO,
AIQSO) using TD-DFT; (ii) calculations on Cu(II)-N₄-X (X = IPSO, APSO) complexes to determine
photoisomerization mechanisms and EPR parameters; and (iii) ab initio parametrized phenomenological
Hamiltonians to model spin relaxation and decoherence.

2.a Opportunities for Training and Professional Development
[PI: Frank Group]
The research project requires skillsets in organic and inorganic synthesis, spectroscopic investigation
(electronic absorption spectroscopy, EPR, electrochemistry), computational modeling of electronic
structure, magnetic measurements, resistivity measurements, and device fabrication. The project
supported the training of 6+ undergraduate students, 8 graduate students, and 3 postdoctoral fellows in
spin-based functional organic materials for renewable energy and quantum information science
applications.
Dr. Anitha Alanthadka (Postdoctoral Fellow) received hands-on training at the National High Magnetic
Field Laboratory (Tallahassee, FL) and in the EPR laboratory of R. David Britt (UC-Davis). She
successfully completed her fellowship in May 2023 and is now a postdoctoral fellow at Worcester
Polytechnic Institute, MA.
Dr. Subrata Ghosh (Postdoctoral Fellow) received hands-on training in pulsed EPR at the National High
Magnetic Field Laboratory (Tallahassee, FL) and in the EPR laboratory at Caltech (Paul Oyala).
Devon Adecer transitioned from undergraduate researcher (2022) to graduate student (2025), reflecting
the depth of training provided by the program.

information science through attending a Special Topics Course (Spring 2025) delivered by Dr. Frank on
Magnetism and Quantum Information Science.( 3.d Impact on Teaching and Educational Experiences)

Quantum Information Science organized by Dr. Frank at Granlibakken Lake Tahoe., Dec 2024 (100
participants from North and South America).

Section 3: Impact
3.a Impact on the Development of the Principal Discipline(s)
[PI: Frank Group]
This project's primary focus is quantum information science and engineering (QISE). We demonstrated a
proof of concept for leveraging the sensitivity of photochromic ligands to external stimuli to detect
changes in the environment via transduction to spin states, spin decoherence, Rabi frequency, and other
readout parameters for spin-based qubits. This provides a fundamental strategy akin to biological
systems in which quantum phenomena are amplified through conformational changes and signal
transduction pathways for sensing protocols.
[Co-PI: King Group]
The optimization of the synthesis of carboxy-FANTRIP has enabled research in various quantum
computing and sensing applications. The post-polymerization methodology developed provides a route to
organizing molecular qubits into a lattice via covalent attachment on a two-dimensional surface.
[Co-PI: Lee Group]
TERS capability is enabled through instruments supported by DOE. The atomically resolved TERS
technique provides a fundamental analytical capability for characterizing 2D quantum materials, directly
relevant to the development of surface-based quantum architectures.

3.b Impact on Other Disciplines
[PI: Frank Group]
Contribution 1 (Quantum Information Science): By designing systems that transduce external stimuli into
spin quantum states, this project establishes fundamental design criteria for quantum platforms based on
chemical systems.
Contribution 2 (Molecular Switches): Our structural insights into ground state and transition state energies
in photochromic systems significantly enhance understanding of how structural factors affect molecular
switching, broadly applicable to artificial motors, biosensors, and polymer science.
Contribution 3 (Charge Storage): Our studies contribute to fundamental understanding of charge and
mass diffusion kinetics in organic radical materials for energy storage.
Contribution 4 (Organic Radical Optics): Benzotriazinyl radicals were found to be emissive, opening
possibilities for optical initialization/readout of organic spin qubits with low spin-orbit coupling and long
coherence times.

3.d Impact on Teaching and Educational Experiences
[PI: Frank Group]

Teaching. This project enabled the integration of organic, inorganic, physical, quantum, and
computational chemistry into teaching at both the second-year and advanced levels. Research from the
project was incorporated directly into a new advanced course developed by the PI, CHEM 492/692
"Molecular Magnetism" (Spring 2025, 12 students), a combined undergraduate/graduate course providing
a comprehensive treatment of the theoretical and experimental aspects of molecular magnetism,
including magnetization and magnetic susceptibility, the fundamental equations of molecular magnetism,
magnetic exchange, contributions to magnetic anisotropy, spin dynamics, and applications of molecular
spin centers to quantum information science. Exposure to research-derived course content led to
recruitment of undergraduates into the laboratory.

Outreach. The project generated invitations for guest lectures on quantum science in physics and
chemistry courses across campus and at other institutions, as well as tutorials on chemistry in quantum
information science at international conferences.
