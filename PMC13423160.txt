How do ethnic minority women navigate family planning amid symbolic violence? A qualitative study among Kinh and Mong communities in the highlands of Vietnam
Authors: Huyen Thi Hoa Nguyen, Anh Chau Nguyen, My Huyen Hac, Thanh Hai Tran, Quyen Ngoc Bao Nguyen, Giang Huong Nguyen, Tran Ngoc Tran, Ngan Thi Thuy Nguyen, Quyen Thu Do, Huong Thi Xuan Hoang, Duc Quang Tran, Ngan Thi Duc Hoang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn ethnic minority communities like in Vietnam, reproductive decision-making is shaped not only by access to services but by deeply rooted cultural and gender norms. A stark ‘reproductive paradox’ emerges, where high fertility is idealised yet unsafe abortion remains prevalent.ObjectivesThis study explores how women, men and healthcare workers in Son La province perceive family planning and gender-based violence (GBV), with attention to the structural and symbolic forces that shape the reproductive autonomy of the women in the region.MethodsA qualitative study was conducted in March 2022 in Son La province, using semistructured interviews and three group discussions with 18 participants, including mothers, fathers, pregnant women and healthcare workers from both Kinh and Mong ethnic groups. Data were analysed using Krippendorff’s content analysis, framed by Social Constructionism and Symbolic Interactionism, to understand how cultural narratives shape Kinh and Mong women’s reproductive autonomy.ResultsParticipants described how women’s reproductive autonomy is constrained by structural and symbolic violence. Contraceptive choices were often controlled by husbands and mothers-in-law. Some women rejected intrauterine devices to avoid affecting male sexual comfort. ‘Wife snatching’ and early marriage were normalised as tradition. While both ethnic groups preferred large families, Kinh participants cited lineage expectations, whereas Mong participants emphasised ancestral blessings and land. Unsafe abortion, through herbal or informal methods, was common when women’s choices conflicted with stigma or family control.ConclusionsFindings underscore how gendered and ethnic hierarchies restrict reproductive autonomy of Kinh and Mong women in the region. Interventions should integrate family planning and GBV services, promote cultural humility and engage local actors in codesigning solutions that respect both biomedical and indigenous reproductive logics.Keywords: Violence, Sexual Health, Public Health

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

It is well-established that ethnic minority women in Vietnam face significant reproductive health inequities, where deeply rooted cultural norms and gender-based violence (GBV) limit their autonomy and access to care. However, few qualitative studies have directly compared the perspectives of different ethnic groups or used theoretical frameworks to explore how symbolic power shapes reproductive decision-making.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study reveals a ‘reproductive paradox’ where the cultural idealisation of high fertility coexists with unsafe abortion, stemming from a conflict between community values and women’s limited agency. It finds that while both Kinh and Mong groups prefer large families, their justifications differ (lineage vs ancestral blessing) and women’s contraceptive choices are constrained by husbands, mothers-in-law and an internalised responsibility for male sexual comfort. Together, these dynamics create a reproductive paradox in which women are socially expected to maximise fertility while lacking sufficient autonomy to control the timing and number of pregnancies, increasing the likelihood that unsafe abortion becomes a strategy for managing unintended reproduction.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

These findings call for systemic change through community engagement, including the integration of family planning and GBV services, the development of policies that promote cultural humility in healthcare and the codesign of interventions with local stakeholders (eg, spiritual leaders, mothers-in-law and male elders) to ensure solutions are both effective and culturally respectful.

Introduction

Family planning represents far more than individual reproductive choices, for it constitutes a fundamental component of public health that encompasses sexual reproductive health and rights while promoting family well-being and women’s empowerment.1,2 Yet across patriarchal societies globally, women’s ability to exercise meaningful reproductive autonomy remains profoundly constrained by entrenched gender hierarchies, cultural norms and structural power imbalances.3,4 In sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia and Southeast Asia alike, research has consistently documented how patriarchal household structures limit women’s independent decision-making in contraceptive use, birth spacing and desired family size—with husbands, in-laws and community elders frequently exercising decisive authority over reproductive outcomes.5,6 Gender-based coercion in reproductive contexts is not incidental to patriarchal culture but structurally embedded within it, rendering women’s reproductive subordination socially normalised and largely invisible as a form of harm.7,8

These dynamics are particularly pronounced among ethnic minority communities, where intersecting axes of ethnicity, poverty and geographic isolation compound women’s vulnerability and further restrict their access to reproductive healthcare and autonomous decision-making.9,10 Ethnic minorities in Vietnam are the 53 non-Kinh groups recognised by the government, comprising approximately 14% of the population, with the Mong among the largest highland groups.11 Among the Mong people, for instance, women are taught from birth to occupy an ‘inferior position’ and must obey husbands and elders.12,13 These cultural norms create a complex landscape where individual agency intersects with collective social expectations.14 The interconnection between family planning and gender-based violence (GBV) becomes particularly pronounced in these settings. GBV represents a pervasive phenomenon that undermines women’s ability to make autonomous decisions about their bodies and futures.12,15 In patriarchal societies, deeply rooted gender norms legitimise violence, shift accountability onto victims and diminish perpetrators’ responsibility, contributing to the social acceptance of GBV and discouraging women from challenging abusive behaviours.16,17 As violence becomes normalised within families and communities, women’s ability to negotiate contraceptive use, pregnancy timing and other reproductive decisions may be significantly constrained. Therefore, family planning becomes not a matter of individual health decision-making but a reflection of broader power inequalities, especially when women lack control over their reproductive choices due to violence or the threat of violence.

Vietnam’s reproductive health achievements present a paradox of national success alongside persistent gaps in reproductive health outcomes among ethnic minority populations. The country achieved the 2015 maternal mortality reduction target of the United Nations Millennium Development Goals, with maternal mortality ratios declining from 233 per 100 000 live births in the 1990s to 46 per 100 000 in 2019.18,19 However, this national progress masks significant health inequities between the Kinh majority and ethnic minority populations. Such inequities arise from unequal social, economic and geographic conditions rather than biological differences.20 Maternal mortality remains two to three times higher among ethnic minorities, and unmet family planning needs affect 17.7% of ethnic minority women compared with 10.1% nationally.21 These inequities extend beyond reproductive health indicators and are reflected in the broader patterns of violence and constraints on reproductive autonomy. The same year—2019, 63% of ever-married Vietnamese women reported experiencing physical, sexual and/or emotional violence by their husbands, with young women particularly vulnerable, 27.8% of ever-partnered women and girls aged 15 and older experienced violence in the previous year.22

Son La province, located in Vietnam’s northwest highlands, provides an ideal ethnographic setting for examining these intersecting issues. Home to 1.3 million people representing 12 ethnic groups, the province embodies the complexity of Vietnam’s ethnic diversity.23 The Mong constitute one of the largest ethnic minority groups in Son La region and are distributed across several subgroups that share broadly similar cultural traditions.24 Despite being such a large ethnic group, reproductive health outcomes among Mong communities remain disproportionately poor compared with national trends, highlighting ongoing health inequities. Fertility rates remain elevated at 2.2 children per woman compared with the national average of 1.95, with slower declines observed over recent decades.25,26 Adolescent birth rates present even starker contrasts, while national rates stand at 4.2%, northern mountainous regions like Son La experience rates of 15.7%.27,28 These statistics reflect underlying structural barriers including economic difficulties, geographic limitations, limited educational awareness and persistent harmful practices such as ‘wife snatching’ and child marriage.29 Together, these socioeconomic and cultural factors shape a context in which women face substantial constraints on reproductive decision-making and access to essential health services.

While previous research has identified barriers to maternal care and family planning among ethnic minority women, including remote geography, language differences and conflicts between traditional beliefs and biomedical advice, significant gaps remain in our understanding.14 Few studies have employed qualitative methodologies to explicitly compare the Kinh’s and the Mong’s perspectives or connected these perspectives to broader interpretive theoretical frameworks. Additionally, limited research has adequately explored how women’s subordinate social roles specifically affect sexual and reproductive health (SRH) outcomes. Given these gendered power dynamics, this study intentionally centres women’s lived experiences and positions their accounts as primary sources of knowledge. Perspectives from men and healthcare workers are included to provide contextual understanding and to examine how broader social and institutional structures shape women’s experiences, rather than to privilege these more culturally dominant viewpoints. To further guard against bias, particularly given that all members of the research team are Kinh, the study was approached through two complementary theoretical lenses. Social Constructionism directed the team to treat fertility ideals, son preference and reproductive practices as outcomes of social processes specific to each community’s own history and context, rather than as inherent traits of a particular ethnic group to be judged against a biomedical or majority-culture standard.30,31 Symbolic Interactionism, in turn, kept analytic attention on the meanings participants themselves attached to their practices and language, rather than on meanings the research team might otherwise impose from an outsider’s perspective.32,33 Together, these frameworks functioned as an explicit check against the risk of interpreting Mong practices through an external, deficit-based lens.

Methods

Study design

This study used the qualitative approach to collect data regarding the perception of men, women, and healthcare providers about sexual and reproductive health and rights (SRHR). This study was informed by social constructionism and symbolic interactionism to analyse the sociocultural influences on family planning and GBV perceptions in Son La province. Social Constructionism emphasises how individuals and groups create shared meanings and realities through social processes, interactions and shared meanings.30 This perspective allowed us to explore how cultural norms, traditions and gender roles shape perceptions of family planning and GBV. Symbolic Interactionism focuses on the micro-level interactions between individuals, emphasising the role of symbols and language in shaping meaning and behaviour.34,35 This lens helped us understand how individuals interpret and respond to social norms related to family planning and GBV, and how these interpretations influence their decision-making and actions.

Settings and participants

Data were collected in March 2022 in two communes of Thuan Chau district and four wards of Son La City, Vietnam. Participants were recruited via a purposive sampling technique, aiming for variation in gender, ethnicity (Kinh and Mong) and professional background. This sampling technique allows us to select participants with specific characteristics to better explore the research topic. Participants were selected following a set criteria.

The target population consists of women and men from Kinh and Mong ethnic groups who had a child under 1 year old or were expecting parents. Healthcare workers were also included to provide professional perspectives on the reproductive autonomy of women. By analysing the similarities and differences of their opinions, we can form objective and holistic conclusions.

Commune authorities assisted in identifying and recruiting eligible participants. The sample includes 18 participants: six mothers with children under 1 year old, five fathers (or expectant fathers), four pregnant women and three healthcare workers. The number of participants in each group is determined by the availability of individuals identified as suitable by commune authorities. This sample size aligns with the qualitative research tradition, enabling an in-depth exploration of the experiences and perspectives of Vietnamese individuals regarding family planning and GBV.36

Despite all health workers being Kinh, the sampling was deemed sufficient due to data saturation: after these interviews we observed repeating themes and no new major information emerged.

Household commodities incentive was provided for the participants as an appreciation for their time. Ethical approval and consent forms were provided to participants before the team started the data collection process; refusal would not affect access to healthcare or community services, and they could withdraw at any time without consequences.

Data collection

Data were collected through semistructured in-depth interviews. The interview guide was developed by the research team with reference to our theoretical framework (social constructionism and symbolic interactionism) and existing literature on family planning and gender norms37 (the full guide is provided as online supplemental file 1). We piloted the guide with three community members and refined questions accordingly. We worked with healthcare workers and community leaders to gain their insights in order to better culturally adapt our interview questions. This ensured the comprehension of the interviewees and the representation of the actual community. Topics included family size preferences, decision-making about contraception, perceptions of sons versus daughters and experiences or views of domestic violence. Given the gendered hierarchy in topics of sexual health and violence, male and female interviewers facilitated gender-matched interviews when possible. While language support was offered via bilingual healthcare workers, all discussions were conducted in Vietnamese, with real-time translation assistance provided when necessary for Mong participants.

Three focus group discussions (FGDs) and six individual interviews were conducted. Each FGD included both men and women from the same ethnic group (either Kinh or Mong) to encourage cultural affinity during open dialogue. Mixed gender based interviews allow the research team to observe the power dynamic, social norms and how the negotiation about reproduction can be made in a shared setting. Additionally, due to logistical constraints and local availability, some FGDs included both genders within the same session. It is acknowledged that this methodological decision may have affected participants’ willingness to express sensitive perspectives—especially on SRH and GBV, given prevailing gender and social hierarchies.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using Krippendorff’s qualitative content analysis. Meaning units were coded inductively, then reduced and grouped thematically.38 Additional comparative coding was conducted across ethnic groups (Kinh vs Mong) to identify both shared themes and culturally specific patterns. Gender and generational power hierarchies were also examined as analytical dimensions to surface how intersectional inequalities shape SRH perceptions. Quotes were selected to illustrate not just thematic categories, but also the embedded cultural narratives and relational dynamics within each group.

Throughout, the research team discussed how our own backgrounds (all Kinh researchers) might influence interpretation, and we took care to acknowledge any potential biases. For example, we noted possible bias in assuming biomedical views were ‘correct’ and reflected on how Mong traditional health beliefs shape participants’ responses.

In order to improve the validity of the data, four reliable approaches were applied according to the guidelines suggested39. First, to increase credibility, prolonged engagement with the data and investigator triangulation were used: two investigators coded the transcripts separately before discussion and reaching a consensus. The member checking strategy was also used in the form of discussing preliminary findings with some participants to see whether the analysis corresponds to their experience. Second, the transferability of the results is enhanced through providing thick descriptions of the studied community, characteristics of its participants and the cultural environment in which they live. Third, to ensure dependability, an audit trail was kept during the study, documenting the process of coding and analysing the data. Fourth, confirmability can be achieved through reflexive journaling that helps to reveal and bracket the assumptions based on our Kinh background at each step of the analysis.

Patient and public involvement

Neither patients nor members of the general public were engaged in any stage of the study process. The study objectives and methodology were defined by the researchers who belong to the university research community and are based on prior literature and theoretical knowledge (Social Constructionism and Symbolic Interactionism). Patients acted as subjects during the interview and discussion sessions but had no direct role in designing and implementing the methodology and the study results measures. In the course of data analysis and its presentation, the obtained results were communicated to relevant healthcare institutions and the community stakeholders residing in Son La province via structured feedback sessions and written reports. This was done to fulfil our obligations within the parent project entitled ‘Improving Reproductive Health & Preventing Child Marriage in Son La Province, 2021–2022’.

Result

Based on interview analyses, two main themes of perception about family planning and GBV among 18 women and men of both Kinh and Mong ethnic groups, and Kinh healthcare providers were identified. Six sub-themes were also identified. The comments of healthcare workers were based on their own observation of local Kinh and Mong people; whereas, the men and women participants were speaking from their own experience in their respective ethnic groups.

Perceptions of family planning

High fertility norm

Despite challenging economic conditions and limited access to healthcare, the persistence of traditional views on large families remains prevalent. A village health worker shared her perception regarding the Mong people: “Some Mong families live in very small houses but have large families, typically with 5 to 7 children. They believe there is ample land available and no penalties for having many children, so they continue to have children without planning.” This perspective was echoed by other Mong participants, who described large families as a source of wealth and spiritual blessing. A health worker observed that some families pursue additional children in hopes of balancing gender: “The local economy has improved, which has led some families to desire more children. For example, some families with two sons wish to have a daughter, so they decide to have another child.”

While both Kinh and Mong participants expressed a preference for larger families, the justifications varied. Among Mong participants, high fertility was frequently linked to cultural beliefs around ancestral blessings and the perception of land abundance. In contrast, Kinh participants tended to frame high fertility in terms of lineage continuation and social expectations, though slightly more likely to acknowledge state policies and economic considerations as limiting factors.

Entrenched decision-making power in contraceptive use

While there has been progress in increasing women’s participation in SRHR-related activities, decision-making power remains constrained by deep-rooted gender norms. In many cases, women’s autonomy is still subordinated to their husbands’ authority. Although women in these communities were reported to be able to join in discussions regarding family planning and reproductive decisions by both men and women, the final verdict ultimately falls on the husbands. As one health worker explained, ‘Traditionally, the woman’s decision to place an intrauterine device (IUD) or do family planning depends on the husband. It is not just intellectual but also cultural’. Another resident woman shared her discomfort with the idea of using an IUD, particularly due to how it might affect her partner: ‘Placing an IUD can make it uncomfortable for my husband so I refuse to use it’. This suggests that women not only accept male authority in reproductive planning, but also internalise responsibility for maintaining sexual comfort and satisfaction in the marital relationship. The expectation to prioritise a husband’s sexual preference over one’s own reproductive needs represents an important symbolic dimension of subordination.

Among both Kinh and Mong participants, men and the elders were consistently described as the final decision-makers in contraceptive use. However, the mechanisms varied: Kinh women more often cited negotiation or influence via the Women’s Union or maternal health sessions, while Mong women described hierarchical family structures in which elders, especially mothers-in-law, exerted significant influence, sometimes overriding both husband and wife preferences, “Although women can attend meetings [in Women’s Union] and express their opinions, the mother-in-laws ultimately makes the final decisions. For instance, no woman willingly wants to have a third child.”

Men’s own accounts of their role in family planning decisions did not always align with health workers’ observations of the same dynamic. When asked directly, several male participants described decision-making as a discussed or joint process. A young male community member explained, “When there is a view to share within the family, the woman can express her opinion and discuss it with her husband.” A man in a mixed-gender focus group similarly framed reproductive decisions as mutual: “If my wife does not agree to have a child, who else would? So we just have to discuss it together.” A male health volunteer, however, offered a more critical reading of this same pattern: “Women do participate in meetings and have their say, but husbands remain patriarchal and still make the decision on the wife’s behalf. Take a third child, for example—no wife actually wants that.” This divergence between men’s self-reported attitudes and health workers’ direct observations suggests that male authority over reproductive decisions is frequently exercised through processes that appear consultative on the surface while the substantive decision remains with the husband.

Unsafe abortion

Unsafe abortion methods—such as herbal self-abortion and informal provider interventions, were reported particularly in the area. Many of these abortion methods were sought out by underage girls, ranging from secondary to high schoolers, with unplanned pregnancies. A village health worker provided an account of young women who, despite being advised to seek professional medical care, opted for self-abortion or assistance from unqualified individuals. She explained, “There are cases of girls that were advised to go to hospitals and professional health centers, however, they chose to do it at home by themselves or with retired people’s help. In the end, the incomplete abortion leads to severe infection, which requires urgent help.” Another obstetrician-gynaecologist health worker shared, “There are many cases of performing abortion by using herbs. The woman then has prolonged periods or blood clots that she must go to the health center. When we checked her uterus, the embryo like a fist was still inside.” This reveals a complex contradiction: while children are valued as wealth and blessing, women—particularly young or unmarried—may still seek abortion due to social stigma, lack of readiness or coercion. These abortions occur in conditions shaped by both structural access and family power dynamics.

Mong women more often reported use of herbal or elder-assisted methods, reflecting a reliance on traditional knowledge in contexts of limited formal access. Kinh participants mentioned pharmacy pills or private clinics. Despite cultural ideals around large families, abortion emerged when reproductive control was out of sync with relational power, stigma or immediate survival needs.

GBV and reproductive coercion

Culture-rooted relationship inequality

In examining the impact of cultural norms on gender dynamics, it becomes evident that traditional practices such as ‘wife snatching’ persist in shaping women’s status and autonomy—“The girl is captured and forced to live together against their will. If the boy likes her, he can ask for her hand in marriage; if not, he will kick her out of the house”.

Another account elaborates on this norm: “If a boy likes a girl, he takes her home and they live together. The girl has no say in the matter. If the boy decides he doesn’t like her, she is sent away. If he does like her, he will bring cows, chickens, and dowry to formally ask for her hand in marriage.”

These practices were primarily reported by Mong participants, where girls are subjected to coercive and non-consensual conditions. Among Kinh, such forms of marital coercion were not mentioned. The Mong custom of forced marriage shows how cultural traditions intersect with gender hierarchy to normalise coercion.

Domestic violence

Domestic violence was described as both common and under-acknowledged, “In some families, husbands beat their wives, and no one intervenes. Sometimes even the mother-in-law joins in, and despite the wife’s attempts to resist, she is unsuccessful.” A man remarked, “If husband and wife fight, they will soon reconcile; it’s not seen as a big deal. These conflicts are considered inevitable, and they are not reported because they are usually not perceived as serious.” There is insufficient attention to the management of GBV—a health worker noted, “These incidents happen quietly. We may hear arguments among neighbors, but we rarely see any cases at the clinic. I think there was only one reported case last year.” emphasises the invisibility of domestic violence within the healthcare system. Similarly, another woman shared the same thought, “The women in the association have never reported cases of domestic violence. If they are upset, they might share a little during regular association activities, but overall, the focus is on stabilizing the family”.

Both groups acknowledged domestic violence, but Mong participants often described multigenerational complicity in violence. Among Kinh, women referenced informal disclosures through community meetings. Across both, GBV was internalised as a private issue—normalising control over women’s bodies and limiting their capacity to seek care.

Gender prejudices

Gender prejudices, particularly surrounding the preference for sons over daughters, remain deeply embedded in the social fabric of many communities. One account from a health worker illustrates the persistence of gender bias: “There is still a prejudice against giving birth to girls as usually the husbands make the decisions. The wives are mostly farmers with low intellectual levels. I know this woman giving birth to three girls that receive discrimination; relatives even advise the husband to marry another woman.” Even among families with higher levels of education and employment, the preference for sons continues to drive reproductive choices—a woman working in People’s Council shared a story: “Like my friend who is a government employee, she tried to give birth to a third child in the hope of a boy. Or another cousin of mine who has health issues but still wants a boy so the couple still goes to Hanoi for health checkups.” The preference for sons is also linked to risky reproductive decisions, such as resorting to unsafe abortions—a health worker commented, “If the third or fourth child is a girl, some couples resort to private clinics for abortions, often without full awareness of the procedure.”

Both Kinh and Mong participants described son preference as a key driver of continued childbearing. However, while Kinh participants related this to social image and inheritance, Mong women described it as tied to lineage rituals and spiritual responsibility. This difference shaped decisions around abortion, contraception and intergenerational expectations.

Discussion

This study reveals how reproductive autonomy in Son La is constrained by overlapping systems of gender, ethnicity and family structure. A central contradiction, what we term a ‘reproductive paradox’ emerges: communities that valorise fertility also practice unsafe abortion, particularly in settings where structural and relational power limits women’s agency.

Among Mong participants, childbearing is seen as a spiritual obligation and symbol of prosperity aligning with anthropological literature on fertility as cultural capital.40,41 Children were perceived as ancestral blessings tied to land abundance beliefs, with high fertility norms reinforced by perceptions of resource availability and absence of penalties—consistent with previous research showing 22.3% of Son La mothers having third children, with 81% citing desire for more children.42 This was also supported by Abdi et al, who highlight that men express a preference for larger families based on the belief that more children represent greater wealth and prestige. From a religious perspective, every child is seen as a gift from God, with their own provisions and blessings. These results are consistent with what has been documented previously.43,44 These pronatalist beliefs constitute the first dimension of the reproductive paradox by establishing strong social expectations that women should bear multiple children.

Yet healthcare workers frequently encounter abortion complications from herbal remedies or private pills, revealing a disjuncture between cultural ideals and lived experiences. The contradiction reflects generational tensions, stigma around premarital pregnancy and limited contraceptive negotiation power—especially within coercive relationships.45,46 Thus, fertility is publicly praised while control over it remains institutionally and interpersonally constrained.

Women’s reproductive decisions are shaped not only by male authority but also by internalised expectations about their role in sexual relationships. Avoidance of IUDs due to husbands reported ‘discomfort’ reflects more than concern for physical effects—it suggests women assume responsibility for male sexual satisfaction. This form of embodied patriarchy, where women self-regulate their contraceptive choices to protect male comfort, reveals deep-rooted symbolic and relational subordination.47,48

While decision-making dynamics varied between groups, gender hierarchies remained dominant. Kinh women described limited negotiation through formal channels like Women’s Unions. Mong women described strict family hierarchies, where mothers-in-law often exercised reproductive authority. In both cases, women’s autonomy was shaped by multiple actors and cultural scripts that idealised fertility while restricting its management.49 The coexistence of pronatalist expectations and restricted reproductive agency explains why unsafe abortion emerges not as a rejection of fertility ideals, but as a coping strategy for navigating unintended pregnancies within unequal power relations. This dynamic was echoed in men’s own accounts of decision-making. While several male participants described family planning as a discussed or jointly agreed matter, healthcare workers observed a more patriarchal reality in practice, in which women’s participation in discussion did not translate into a substantive say in the outcome. This gap between consultative appearance and unilateral decision is itself a further expression of symbolic violence, in which male authority is reproduced without needing to be asserted overtly or contested.8

GBV reinforced these dynamics. Practices like ‘wife snatching’ institutionalised coercion under the guise of tradition, while domestic violence was normalised and underreported. Healthcare workers noted a gap between community acknowledgement of GBV and formal system response—often reporting ‘only one case’ despite visible signs. This invisibility stems from both cultural silence and professional hesitancy, reflecting an institutional failure to address harm.43,44,50 These forms of coercion further reinforce the reproductive paradox by reducing women’s capacity to exercise reproductive choice while maintaining social expectations surrounding fertility and family continuity.

Comparative analysis highlights distinct ethnic vulnerabilities alongside shared structural inequalities, which is maintained and fostered by patrilineal or patrilocal kinship systems.51–53 Mong participants faced compounded barriers: marginalisation, language difference and reliance on traditional systems. Their use of herbal abortions reflected both cultural continuity and healthcare access gaps. In contrast, Kinh participants engaged more with formal systems, though son preference and male control persisted. Across groups, patriarchy, reproductive coercion and son preference were consistent themes, which can lead to sex-selective abortions and higher fertility rates among families without sons.54–56 Although the pathways differed between Kinh and Mong communities, both groups ultimately reproduced the same reproductive paradox through distinct cultural and structural mechanisms.

Effective programming requires integrated approaches that address family planning and GBV together, specifically weaving GBV screening, contraceptive counselling and culturally sensitive education into the same encounters.57–60 Cultural sensitivity must go beyond language translation to include epistemological respect and cocreation of interventions.61–63 This means engaging local spiritual leaders, mothers-in-law and male elders in the design and delivery of messaging, not merely consulting them post hoc.64 It also involves training health providers in cultural humility and narrative-based communication skills that honour diverse reproductive logics.62,65

Legal reforms targeting harmful practices like wife snatching should be framed through community engagement rather than cultural condemnation.66–68 Policies should support local advocates—such as young couples or female elders, while improving rural service logistics (eg, mobile outreach, confidential transport for abortion care) and fostering gender equity through culturally grounded storytelling and peer influence.69,70 Ultimately, reproductive justice in Son La depends on recognising how structural, cultural and symbolic forces constrain choice.

Limitations

The use of purposive sampling may have introduced selection bias and limited generalisability. Although both Kinh and Mong participants were included, the study did not capture perspectives from other ethnic groups in Son La. The interview guide was approved by the Mong community leaders; however, healthcare workers of Mong ethnicity were absent during the research. Most participants were aged 20–30, and no unmarried individuals or older adults were included. Discussions were conducted in Vietnamese, with translation provided for Mong participants when needed, which may have limited expression of cultural nuances or resulted in partial loss of meaning. The interview guide was piloted with three healthcare workers and community leaders rather than with individuals matched to the actual participant demographic (mothers, fathers or pregnant women); as such, some nuances of question wording may not have been verified with the population ultimately interviewed.

Additionally, some FGDs included both men and women within the same session. Given prevailing gender-based power imbalances, especially around SRH and GBV, this may have constrained open dialogue, particularly among women. Future studies should consider conducting same-gender, culturally homogenous focus groups to better capture intragroup hierarchies and cultural specificity. Moreover, all three healthcare workers interviewed were Kinh, which may have biased interpretations of Mong practices through the lens of external professionals. While Kinh health workers sometimes framed high fertility among Mong families as irrational or harmful, Mong participants themselves described these practices as embedded in spiritual and ecological logics. We note that future studies should include Mong health volunteers to capture minority insider views. This insider–outsider dissonance highlights the importance of local epistemologies.

Conclusions

Misconceptions about family planning, GBV and societal structures in Son La province, Vietnam continue to shape SRHR. Future efforts to advance reproductive justice in Vietnam’s ethnically diverse regions must prioritise integrated, culturally grounded strategies that address family planning and GBV as interconnected issues. Programmes should move beyond information dissemination to actively engage community actors in reshaping social norms around fertility, gender roles and violence. Health systems must invest in provider training that centres cultural humility, narrative-based counselling and gender-sensitive care, particularly for ethnic minority populations. Legal reforms targeting harmful practices like wife snatching should be community-led, pairing rights-based frameworks with local moral reasoning to foster internal critique and sustainable change. Future research should deepen comparative analysis across ethnic subgroups and generational lines, incorporating participatory methodologies to surface underrepresented voices and lived strategies of resistance. Ultimately, transforming reproductive health outcomes will require shifting from top-down biomedical interventions towards long-term partnerships that centre equity, respect and shared ownership of solutions.
