Enumerating Inclusion-Maximal Arithmetic Progressions
Brian Bemman, Maximilien Gadouleau, Oliver W. Gnilke, George B. Mertzios

Abstract
We present a simple 𝒪 ⁡ ( n 2 ​ log ⁡ N log ⁡ log ⁡ N + N ) \mathcal{O}\left(n^{2}\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}+N\right) enumeration algorithm for solving a problem from mathematical and computational music analysis where, given a strictly increasing integer sequence, S S , with n n entries and maximum value N N , the task is to enumerate all m m inclusion-maximal arithmetic progressions (IMAPs) in this sequence. An IMAP is a subsequence, S ′ ⊆ S S^{\prime}\subseteq S with k > 2 k>2 integers, in which (i) the difference between any two consecutive integers is the same number, d d (i.e., S ′ S^{\prime} is an arithmetic progression ), (ii) S ′ S^{\prime} cannot be further extended to the left or to the right with any additional integers from S S while still remaining an arithmetic progression (i.e., S ′ S^{\prime} is a maximal arithmetic progression), and (iii) there is no other maximal arithmetic progression, S ′′ ⊆ S S^{\prime\prime}\subseteq S , which properly contains S ′ S^{\prime} (i.e., S ′ S^{\prime} is an inclusion-maximal arithmetic progression). We further provide proofs for the expected number of IMAPs in random integer sequences, S S , and a bound on their order of growth. Finally, we provide empirical experiments comparing both (a) the practical running time performance of the proposed algorithm against that of a previously known algorithm which has higher time complexity 𝒪 ⁡ ( N 2 + o ⁡ ( 1 ) ​ n ) \mathcal{O}(N^{2+o(1)}n) , and (b) the actual enumerated number of IMAPs to that of their mathematically expected number. Notably, the proposed algorithm demonstrates a significant improvement in running time over the previously known algorithm, and in immediate practical applications, will allow for more efficient analysis of large and rhythmically complex musical pieces.

Introduction

Let
S=\langle s_{0},s_{1}\ldots,s_{n-1}\rangle
be a strictly increasing sequence of positive integers. An
inclusion-maximal arithmetic progression (IMAP)

[
19
]
is a subsequence,
t_{0},\dots,t_{k-1}
, of non-trivial length,
k>2
, such that

1.

t_{j+1}=t_{j}+d
for
0\leq j<k-1
where
d>0
is the constant
common difference
between any two consecutive integers (
arithmetic progression
);

2.

neither
t_{0}-d
nor
t_{k-1}+d
are elements of
S
(
maximal
arithmetic progression); and

3.

it is not
properly
contained within any other subsequence of
S
that satisfies (1) and (2) (
inclusion-maximal
arithmetic progression).

By way of an example, consider the following sequence,
S=\langle 1,2,3,4,5,6,8\rangle
, which has exactly three IMAP subsequences, namely,
S^{\prime}_{1}=\langle 1,2,3,4,5,6\rangle,\,S^{\prime}_{2}=\langle 2,4,6,8\rangle
, and
S^{\prime}_{3}=\langle 2,5,8\rangle
. Note that while the subsequence,
\langle 1,3,5\rangle
, of
S
is a maximal arithmetic progression (MAP) as it cannot be further extended, it is not
inclusion-maximal
because it is contained within another IMAP subsequence, namely,
S^{\prime}_{1}=\langle\underline{\textbf{1}},2,\underline{\textbf{3}},4,\underline{\textbf{5}},6\rangle
. While (1) and (3) above are sufficient to define an IMAP, (2) is necessary to distinguish between two closely related problems in computing: enumerating all MAPs (i.e., (1) and (2) above), which forms the basis of our proposed algorithm, and enumerating all IMAPs, which is a special case of the former. It is important to also note that in enumerating IMAPs, we are concerned here with
all
IMAPs rather than the single longest arithmetic progression.

Computational and mathematical methods have long been applied to music (e.g., use of the AllDifferent global constraint from constraint programming to solve the all-interval series problem in music
[
1
]
), while equally, problems originating from music have proved novel within computational and mathematical domains (e.g., special cases of set-covering from music analysis and composition
[
24
,
23
,
3
]
). IMAPs are a clear example of both that were introduced in
[
19
]
through simplicial complexes and for computing a so-called
Inner Metric Analysis
. IMAPs are an important feature of Inner Metric Analysis used to carry out various computationally- and mathematically-based music analysis tasks largely pertaining to metrical structure, such as meter induction
[
8
]
, detecting and predicting rhythmic syncopation
[
25
,
17
,
2
]
, and predicting musical genre
[
7
]
. The relevance of IMAPs to music is relatively straightforward insofar as being able to investigate all equally-spaced points in time within a sequence of
n
discrete musical events can provide insight into the metrical regularity and structure directly from the music itself absent of any additional information that may otherwise be provided by the printed score (e.g., time signature). While a larger
N
relative to
n
generally indicates a more rhythmically-complex piece of music (i.e., one containing both small and more diverse rhythmic durations requiring a larger range of
N
integers by which to properly encode them), the regularity and non-random nature of music presents an interesting case since the density of sequences remains high yet even when it decreases, most events are near-evenly spaced such that relatively high numbers of IMAPs still emerge.

In more directly related work, algorithms exist for finding the longest arithmetic progression and all MAPs
[
9
]
as well as proofs concerning various bounds on, and the expected number of, similar mathematical and combinatorial objects, such as
k
-term arithmetic progressions and MAPs
[
10
,
16
,
5
,
4
,
9
]
. In the case of IMAPs, however, no such proofs exist. Moreover, only a single procedural algorithm exists in open-sourced form capable of enumerating IMAPs
[
18
]
but is relatively inefficient, which has limited its use in large-scale music analytical tasks, and details concerning its design have so far remained unpublished outside of music.

1.1
Contribution

In this paper, we provide the first-known efficient algorithm for enumerating IMAPs as well as prove for the first time the expected number of such progressions in random integer sequences and a bound on their order of growth. We show that our proposed algorithm is near-optimal when
N=\Theta(n)
. Additionally, we offer empirical results in the form of comparisons between the practical running time performances of the proposed algorithm and the aforementioned existing algorithm as well as between the actual enumerated number of IMAPs and their expected number as provided in our proof and exact construction. In applied terms, our contribution provides mathematical and computational music analysts with the ability to more efficiently analyze large and rhythmically complex musical pieces.

Enumerates inclusion-maximal arithmetic progressions (IMAPs) based in part on the algorithm offered in

As an introduction to our algorithm shown in Alg.

, the general procedure amounts to calling Alg.

, which is a modified version of the bottom-up dynamic programming algorithm for finding the single longest arithmetic progression
[

]
shown in section

Lemma 4

(IMAP characterization via primes)
.

A MAP with common difference,

, is inclusion-maximal if and only if it is not properly contained in any other MAP with common difference,
c=\frac{d}{p}
, for any prime,

Proof.

Let

be a MAP that is not an IMAP. Therefore, there is a MAP

with common difference,
d^{\prime}<d
, that properly contains

. Noting that
d^{\prime}\mid d
, let

be any prime factor of
\frac{d}{d^{\prime}}
. Then, the arithmetic progression,
B=\langle b_{0}=a_{0},b_{1}=a_{0}+\frac{d}{p},\dots,b_{p(k-1)}=a_{0}+(k-1)d=a_{k-1}\rangle
, has common difference,
\frac{d}{p}
, and properly contains

As such, for any integer,

, and any prime factor,

as the

By the proof of Theorem

On the other hand, consider the function,

, and recall from the proof of Lemma

\displaystyle=\sum_{p\leq N}\psi(p)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{p\leq N}\frac{nN}{3p}

\displaystyle=\frac{nN}{3}\left(\log\log N+A+o\!\left(\frac{1}{\log N}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{nN}{3}\log\log N+\mathcal{O}(nN)

If

Proposition 7.1

(Lower bound)
.

Any algorithm that enumerates all IMAPs contained in a strictly increasing sequence,
S
, of
n
positive integers must perform at least
\Omega(n^{2})
queries in the 3-linear decision tree model. Therefore, Alg.
2
is time-optimal up to a factor of

relative to this lower bound whenever

Proof.

We reduce the problem of finding the single longest arithmetic progression, which is shown in
[

]
to require
\Omega(n^{2})
queries in the 3-linear decision tree model, to the problem of enumerating IMAPs. By definition, the longest arithmetic progression of length,
k>2
, in
S
is an IMAP, so it appears in the output of any correct algorithm for enumerating IMAPs, where its length equals the maximum length over the collection of enumerated IMAPs (and equals 2 if no IMAPs exist). If an algorithm,
\mathcal{A}
, correctly enumerates all IMAPs, one may append a post-processing procedure that scans the output of
\mathcal{A}
and returns the IMAP of maximum length. Since scanning requires reading only the already-produced output with no further queries to the input,
\mathcal{A}
solves the longest arithmetic progression problem using exactly the same number of 3-linear queries. Combined then with Theorem

, when

, the running time of
\mathcal{O}(n^{2+o(1)})
exceeds the

decision-tree lower bound by only the factor,

, so Alg.

is time-optimal up to
\log\log n
.∎

5
Expected Number of Inclusion-Maximal Arithmetic Progressions

In this section, we are concerned with determining the expected number of IMAPs in a random sequence of a given length and sampled from a specified range. Towards this end, we begin by providing definitions needed for first formalizing and then counting the number of so-called
centered
,
low/high
, and
wide progressions
, which we will use to compute the expected number of IMAPs and conclude with bounds on their order of growth. Recall that we will assume here that
S=\langle s_{0},s_{1},\dots,s_{n-1}\rangle
with
1\leq s_{0}<s_{1}<\dots<s_{n-1}\leq N
and drawn uniformly at random from
\binom{N}{n}
such sequences. While this differed from our earlier assumptions where
s_{n-1}=N
, this change will affect the number of IMAPs by at most only a linear term, which becomes asymptotically negligible.

5.1
Maximal Arithmetic Progressions

We will use the notation,
[t_{0},d,k]
, for an arithmetic progression containing the elements,
\{t_{0}+di\;|\;0\leq i<k\}
. In order to calculate the expected number of IMAPs, we will first determine, for each arithmetic progression, the number of sequences in which they are maximal. There are three different cases below that we need to consider.

5.1.1
Centered Arithmetic Progressions

We will refer to any arithmetic progression that can be extended left and right as a
centered arithmetic progression (c-ap)
. For such an arithmetic progression,
t=\langle t_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{k-1}\rangle
, with
t_{0}>d
and
N-t_{k-1}\geq d
, we see that it is maximal in a sequence
(s_{i})
if and only if both
t_{0}-d
and
t_{k-1}+d
are not contained in
(s_{i})
. Therefore, such an arithmetic progression is maximal in exactly
\binom{N-k-2}{n-k}
different sequences
(s_{i})
. Let us further fix
0<d<N
and
2<k\leq n
. We denote by
T_{c}(d,k)
the number of c-ap’s of common difference,
d
, and length,
k
.
If
[t_{0},d,k]
is a c-ap then the following expressions hold:

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle<t_{0}

and

\displaystyle t_{k-1}=t_{0}+(k-1)d

\displaystyle\leq N-d

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow t_{0}

\displaystyle\leq N-kd.

By combining these expressions, we see that
d<t_{0}\leq N-kd
. Therefore,
T_{c}(d,k)=\max\{0,N-(k+1)d\}
.

5.1.2
Low and High Arithmetic Progressions

We will refer to any arithmetic progression that can only be extended on its right or left side as a
low or high arithmetic progression (l-ap or h-ap)
, respectively. Such an arithmetic progression is maximal in exactly
\binom{N-k-1}{n-k}
sequences. We denote by
T_{\ell}(d,k)
the number of l-aps with common difference,
d
, and length,
k
. We see that an arithmetic progression
[t_{0},d,k]
can be only extended to the right if

\displaystyle 0<t_{0}

\displaystyle\leq d

and

\displaystyle t_{k-1}=t_{0}+(k-1)d

\displaystyle\leq N-d

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow t_{0}

\displaystyle\leq N-kd.

Combining these expressions, we see that
T_{\ell}(d,k)=\max\left\{0,\min\{d,N-kd\}\right\}
. By symmetry, the number of h-aps is also given by
T_{h}(d,k)=\max\left\{0,\min\{d,N-kd\}\right\}
.

5.1.3
Wide Arithmetic Progressions

We will refer to any arithmetic progression that cannot be extended a
wide arithmetic progression (w-ap)
. Such an arithmetic progression is maximal in exactly
\binom{N-k}{n-k}
sequences. We denote by
T_{w}(d,k)
the number of w-aps with common difference,
d
, and length,
k
. We see that
[t_{0},d,k]
cannot be extended if

\displaystyle 0<t_{0}

\displaystyle\leq d

and

\displaystyle N-d<t_{k-1}=t_{0}+(k-1)d

\displaystyle\leq N

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow N-kd<t_{0}

\displaystyle\leq N-(k-1)d.

By combining these expressions, we see that
T_{w}(d,k)=\max\{0,d-|N-kd|\}
.

5.2
Inclusion-Maximal Arithmetic Progressions

For calculating the expected number of IMAPs in a random sequence, we will count the number of sequences,
(s_{i})
, in which a given MAP,
[t_{0},d,k]
, is inclusion-maximal. In what follows, we let

N^{\prime}=\begin{cases}N_{c}=N-2&\text{ if $[t_{0},d,k]$\ is centered}\\
N_{\ell}=N_{h}=N-1&\text{ if $[t_{0},d,k]$\ is low or high}\\
N_{w}=N&\text{ if $[t_{0},d,k]$\ is wide.}\end{cases}

For a fixed arithmetic progression,
[t_{0},d,k]
, the number of sequences containing it is given by
|S_{\text{ap}}|=\binom{N-k}{n-k}
, while the number of sequences in which
[t_{0},d,k]
is maximal is given by

|S_{\text{ap}}^{\text{max}}|=\binom{N^{\prime}-k}{n-k}.

We will now work exclusively within the set,
S_{\text{ap}}^{\text{max}}
, and determine when the MAP,
[t_{0},d,k]
, is inclusion-maximal.

Lemma 12

(Disjointness of gap-filling sets)
.

Let
[t_{0},d,k]
be an arithmetic progression and
p
and
q
be two different primes dividing
d
. Then, the intersection of the two refinements,
[t_{0},\frac{d}{p},p(k-1)+1]
and
[t_{0},\frac{d}{q},q(k-1)+1]
, is just the elements of
[t_{0},d,k]
.

Proof.

Assume we have an element of the intersection,
x\in[t_{0},\frac{d}{p},p(k-1)+1]\cap[t_{0},\frac{d}{q},q(k-1)+1]
. Then,
x=t_{0}+\frac{d}{p}i=t_{0}+\frac{d}{q}j\Leftrightarrow qi=pj
. It follows that
p\mid i=pm
and therefore,
x=t_{0}+dm\in[t_{0},d,k]
.∎

Remark 4

(Prime refinement of inclusion-maximality)
.

Given a sequence,
(s_{i})\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}
, of length
n
and a MAP,
[t_{0},d,k]\subseteq(s_{i})
, we see that
[t_{0},d,k]
is inclusion-maximal if, for all primes,
p\mid d
, we have the following:
T_{p}([t_{0},d,k]):=\{t_{j}+\frac{d}{p}\ell\;:\;0\leq j<k-1,1\leq\ell<p\}\nsubseteq(s_{i})
. Lemma
12
shows that
T_{p}
and
T_{q}
are disjoint for primes
p\neq q
.

Given an arithmetic progression,
[t_{0},d,k]
, we will count the number of sequences in which it is contained but not inclusion-maximal. We define sets of sequences in which
[t_{0},d,k]
is not inclusion-maximal for different reasons and calculate the size of their union using the inclusion-exclusion principle
[
22
]
. From section
5.1
, we know the expected number of sequences in which a given arithmetic progression is maximal, and it is from this that we will now deduct the number of sequences in which it is not inclusion-maximal. The arithmetic progression,
[t_{0},d,k]
, is not inclusion-maximal in a sequence,
(s_{i})
, if and only if
T_{p}\subset(s_{i})
for at least one prime,
p\mid d
. The set,
T_{p}
, contains
(k-1)(p-1)
elements. Therefore, there are
|S_{p}|=s([t_{0},d,k],\{p\}):=\binom{N^{\prime}-k-(k-1)(p-1)}{n-k-(k-1)(p-1)}
sequences in which
[t_{0},d,k]
is maximal but not inclusion-maximal because the arithmetic progression,
[t_{0},\frac{d}{p},p(k-1)+1]
, is also contained in
(s_{i})
. Next, we define the sets,
\bigcap_{i}S_{p_{i}}
, which contain sequences in which
[t_{0},d,k]
is maximal but not inclusion-maximal because all the arithmetic progressions,
[t_{0},\frac{d}{p_{i}},p_{i}(k-1)+1]
, are also contained in
(s_{i})
. Since the sets,
T_{p_{i}}
, are pairwise disjoint, we see that

\left|\bigcap_{i}S_{p_{i}}\right|=s([t_{0},d,k],\{p_{i}\})=\binom{N^{\prime}-k-\sum_{i}|T_{p_{i}}|}{n-k-\sum_{i}|T_{p_{i}}|}.

By inclusion-exclusion, the number of sequences in which
[t_{0},d,k]
is maximal but not inclusion-maximal is

\left|\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{i}\\
p_{i}|d\end{subarray}}S_{p_{i}}\right|=\sum_{J\subseteq\{p_{i}\}}(-1)^{|J|+1}s([t_{0},d,k],J).

For a given arithmetic progression,
[t_{0},d,k]
, that is centered (
c
), low (
\ell
), high (
h
), or wide (
w
), we define the functions,
M_{c}(d,k)
,
M_{\ell}(d,k)
,
M_{h}(d,k)
, and
M_{w}(d,k)
, respectively, using each of their corresponding
N^{\prime}
, that count how many sequences contain
[t_{0},d,k]
as an IMAP as

M_{*}(d,k)=\binom{N^{\prime}-k}{n-k}-\left|\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{i}\\
p_{i}\mid d\end{subarray}}S_{p_{i}}\right|.

5.2.1
Expected Number and Bounds on Order of Growth

We can now collect the results from the subsections above and fix the values,
N
and
m
, for the possible range and size of a sequence,
(s_{i})
, respectively. We define arithmetic progressions,
[t_{0},d,k]
, for any
0<d<\frac{N}{2}
and
2<k\leq n
and have defined the number of possible arithmetic progressions for the following parameters:

1.

T_{c}(d,k)=\max\{0,N-(k+1)d\}
(centered);

2.

T_{\ell}(d,k)=T_{h}(d,k)=\max\{0,\min\{d,N-kd\}\}
(low and high); and

3.

T_{w}(d,k)=\max\{0,d-|N-kd|\}
(wide).

For all sequences with parameters,
N
and
n
, we calculate the total number of IMAPs that we will encounter when checking all possible sequences as

S^{*}=\sum_{d=1}^{\lfloor\frac{N-1}{2}\rfloor}\sum_{k=3}^{n}\big[T_{c}(d,k)M_{c}(d,k)+T_{\ell}(d,k)M_{\ell}(d,k)+T_{h}(d,k)M_{h}(d,k)+T_{w}(d,k)M_{w}(d,k)\big].

From this, the expected number of IMAPs in random sequences of values from
1
to
N
and length
n
is

\mathbb{E}[\#\mathrm{IMAP}]=\frac{S^{*}}{\binom{N}{n}}.

Alternatively, the probability that a randomly chosen ordered pair of distinct elements of
S
begins an IMAP is

P(\mathrm{IMAP})=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\#\mathrm{IMAP}]}{\binom{n}{2}}=\frac{S^{*}}{\binom{N}{n}\binom{n}{2}}.

Theorem 13

(Order of growth)
.

The expected number of IMAPs in a sequence,
S
, of
n
elements in
\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
, where
N=\Theta(n)
, grows as
\Theta(n^{2})
and the same holds for MAPs.

Lemma 14

(Bounds)
.

The total number of MAPs in a sequence,
S
, of
n
elements is at most
\frac{1}{2}\binom{n}{2}
. Consequently, the number of IMAPs is also at most
\frac{1}{2}\tbinom{n}{2}
.

Proof of Lemma
14
.

Consider the set of ordered pairs,
A=\{(a,b):a,b\in S,a<b\}
. Clearly, for any
(a,b)\in A
, there can only be one MAP in
S
that begins with
(a,b)
. Therefore, consider the set,
A_{0}
, of pairs,
(a,b)\in A
, that begin a MAP. Let
A_{1}=\{(a,b)\in A:c=b+(b-a)\in S\}
and
A_{2}=\{(b,c)\in A:a=b-(c-b)\in S\}
while
A_{0}\subseteq A_{1}\setminus A_{2}
. If
(a,b)\in A_{0}
, then
(b,c)\in A_{2}
, where
c
is the next term of the IMAP, and the mapping,
(a,b)\mapsto(b,c)
, is an injection from
A_{0}
to
A_{2}
. Since
A_{0}
and
A_{2}
are disjoint, we obtain

2|A_{0}|\leq|A_{0}|+|A_{2}|\leq|A|=\binom{n}{2}.

Therefore, the number of MAPs is
|A_{0}|\leq\frac{1}{2}\binom{n}{2}
, and since every IMAP is a MAP, the same bound holds for IMAPs.∎

Proof of Theorem
13
.

The upper bound of
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
is immediate from Lemma
14
, so we now establish the matching lower bound. Let
N=\lambda n
and say, a pair
(a,b=a+d)\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}^{2}
begins a
3
-IMAP if
(a,a+d,a+2d)
is an IMAP in
S
. If
a+2d>N
, or equivalently
d>\frac{a+N}{2}
, then
(a,b)
does not begin a
3
-IMAP. This event occurs with a probability of
\frac{1}{2}+o(1)
, where
o(1)
is a term that tends to
0
as
n
tends to infinity (and is not necessarily positive). The exact probability is given by
P=\lfloor\frac{(N-a)}{2}\rfloor\frac{1}{N-a}
, and with high probability, we can choose
a
such that
P\geq\frac{1}{2}+o(1)
. Assuming now that
d\leq\frac{a+N}{2}
, then
(a,b)
begins a
3
-MAP if and only if
a+2d\in S
and
a-d,a+3d\notin S
. This event occurs with a probability of
\frac{1}{\lambda^{3}}+o(1)
. If
(a,b)
begins a
3
-MAP but not a
3
-IMAP, then there exists a prime number,
p
, such that
(a,a+\frac{d}{p},\dots,a+(2p-1)\frac{d}{p},a+2d)\in S
. This event occurs with a probability of
\frac{1}{\lambda^{2(p-1)}}+o(1)
. Therefore, the probability that
(a,b)
does not begin a
3
-IMAP, ignoring the
o(1)
term, is at most

\sum_{p=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(\lambda^{2})^{p-1}}=\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}-1}.

Overall, the probability is at least

c=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{\lambda^{3}}\cdot\frac{\lambda^{2}-2}{\lambda^{2}-1}+o(1).

The expected number of IMAPs of length
3
is then at least

c\binom{N}{2}+o(1)=\frac{\lambda^{2}c}{2}n^{2}+o(1).\hfill\qed

As a corollary, we obtain that the expected number of IMAPs that intersect in a given number could be linear in
n
.

Corollary 14.1

(Point of maximum density)
.

Under Theorem
13
, there exists an integer,
a\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
, with
N=\Theta(n)
, such that the expected number of IMAPs that intersect in
a
is
\Theta(n)
.

Remark 5
.

By Lemma
14
, the number of IMAPs in any sequence of length,
n
, is at most
\frac{1}{2}\binom{n}{2}
, and by Theorem
13
, this
\Theta(n^{2})
bound is met in expectation whenever
N=\Theta(n)
. Therefore, the worst-case output size is
\Theta(n^{2})
, and any algorithm that explicitly enumerates all IMAPs must spend at least
\Omega(n^{2})
time merely to write its output. Combined with Proposition
7.1
, Alg.
2
is thus both time-optimal and output-size optimal up to a factor of
\log\log n
when
N=\Theta(n)
.
