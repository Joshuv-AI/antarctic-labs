Total-port vs. utility-incision robotic pulmonary segmentectomy: a propensity score–based analysis of 418 patients
Authors: Yulong Chen, Hui Chen, Yongkuan Guo, Ran Zhang, Haidi Xu, Jian You
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The optimal robotic approach for pulmonary segmentectomy remains debated, and objective standards to verify anatomical precision are lacking. We compared the effectiveness of total-port (TP) versus utility-incision (UI) robotic segmentectomy and validated a 3D-CT metric for assessing surgical completeness. A retrospective propensity score-matched and inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) analysis was performed on 418 patients undergoing robotic segmentectomy (January 2020–December 2024). Surgical completeness was objectively assessed using postoperative 3D-CT airway reconstruction, blindly reviewed by independent radiologists, to confirm exact transection of the target bronchus. Secondary outcomes included perioperative complications and blood loss. Of 418 patients, 203 underwent TP and 215 underwent UI. The TP approach was more frequently utilized for complex segmentectomies (59.6% vs. 38.6%; p < 0.001) and required less preoperative localization (1.0% vs. 17.7%; p < 0.001). Interobserver agreement for the 3D-CT completeness metric was substantial (κ = 0.65). In the IPTW-adjusted analysis, the TP group demonstrated significantly higher verified surgical completeness (95.8% vs. 80.0%; adjusted OR, 5.72; p < 0.001). Additionally, the TP group had lower intraoperative blood loss (45.0 vs. 58.3 mL; adjusted mean difference, -13.28 mL; p < 0.001) and fewer 30-day early complications (3.6% vs. 10.3%; adjusted OR, 0.33; p = 0.01). The TP robotic approach offers superior surgical precision and improved perioperative outcomes compared with the UI approach, particularly for complex segments. Introduction

Robotic-assisted thoracic surgery (RATS) has become a standard of care for early-stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), offering precise dexterity and high-definition 3D visualization [1, 2]. Within this domain, anatomical pulmonary segmentectomy has gained prominence following the JCOG0802 trial, which established the oncological noninferiority of segmentectomy compared with lobectomy for small peripheral NSCLC [3, 4].

Currently, two distinct robotic approaches are widely utilized: the utility-incision (UI) approach (often involving 3 to 4 arms with an auxiliary utility incision or mini-thoracotomy) and the total-port (TP) approach (closed thoracic cavity technique using continuous CO₂ insufflation). While the UI approach allows for tactile feedback and rapid emergency conversion via the utility incision, the TP approach theoretically maximizes the benefits of the robotic platform—such as stable pneumothorax and improved exposure of deep hilar structures—potentially facilitating complex segmentectomies [3–6]. An operative video (Video S1) of total-port robotic segmentectomy is provided in the Supplementary Materials, illustrating port placement, the four-arm technique, application of the Maryland bipolar forceps, and delineation of the intersegmental plane using the robotic fluorescence mode.

However, existing literature lacks large-scale, real-world comparative evidence between these two approaches. Furthermore, a critical gap remains in the quality assurance of segmentectomy: determining whether the target segment has been completely and accurately resected. Traditional assessment relies on subjective intraoperative judgment, which is prone to bias.

This study aims to (1) compare the perioperative outcomes and safety of TP versus UI approaches in a large, real-world cohort; and (2) validate a novel, objective quality control metric—postoperative thin-slice CT 3D airway reconstruction—to assess surgical completeness. We hypothesize that the TP approach offers superior technical advantages for complex segmentectomies and that CT-based verification provides a reproducible standard for surgical quality.

Methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study included 418 consecutive patients who underwent robotic anatomical pulmonary segmentectomy at Tianjin Medical University Cancer Hospital between January 2020 and December 2024. This retrospective study, which analyzed clinical data from January 2020 to December 2024, was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Tianjin Medical University Cancer Institute & Hospital on March 10, 2026 (IRB Approval No. bc20261090). All data extraction and analysis for research purposes were conducted only after this approval was obtained. The requirement for individual patient consent was waived by the board [Figure S1].

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients were included if they: (1) had a solitary pulmonary nodule suspected or confirmed as cT1N0M0 NSCLC; (2) underwent RATS anatomical segmentectomy; and (3) had complete perioperative and follow-up data.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) conversion to thoracotomy or lobectomy; (2) wedge resection only; (3) salvage surgery; or (4) lack of postoperative thin-slice CT data.

Surgical techniques

All procedures in both the utility-incision (UI) and total-port (TP) groups were performed using the da Vinci Xi Surgical System (Intuitive Surgical, Sunnyvale, CA, USA). Therefore, the only difference between the two groups was the surgical approach and port configuration, while the robotic platform remained identical throughout the study.

Utility-incision (UI) Group: A 3 to 4 cm utility incision (mini-thoracotomy) was made in the 4th or 5th intercostal space without continuous CO₂ insufflation (or with low pressure). This approach relies on the utility incision for specimen retrieval and assistance during the procedure.

Total-port (TP) Group: A closed thoracic cavity approach using four robotic arms was employed. Continuous CO₂ insufflation was maintained to expand the operative space. No utility incision was made until specimen extraction, which was performed through a widened port site at the end of the procedure (Figure S6).

Approach Selection and Surgeons: The choice of total-port (TP) versus utility-incision (UI) robotic segmentectomy was not randomized. During the study period, all TP procedures were performed by a single surgeon (corresponding author), whereas UI procedures were performed by three surgeons from different clinical teams; these UI surgeons did not perform TP procedures. Therefore, the approach was primarily determined by surgeon preference and operative workflow, with additional consideration of anticipated segment complexity and exposure requirements.

Endpoints and definitions

Primary Endpoint (Surgical Completeness): Assessed objectively using postoperative thin-slice CT (1 mm). Three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction of the bronchial tree was performed to visualize the bronchial stump. “Complete Resection” was defined as the exact anatomical transection of the target segmental bronchus as planned, verified by 3D imaging. Images were independently assessed by two blinded thoracic radiologists. Discrepant cases were adjudicated by a third senior reviewer to reach a consensus (Fig. 1, Figure S2, Figure S7).

Complex Segmentectomy: Defined as procedures involving two or more intersegmental planes, combined subsegmentectomy, or anatomically complex resections requiring intricate intersegmental dissection. Specific complex variants included LS1 + 2+3, LS3, LS8, LS9, LS10, RS3, RS8, RS9, RS10, combined subsegments (e.g., LS3b + c+LS1 + 2c, RS2b+3a, RS6b+S9a).

Adequate nodal assessment was defined according to the NCCN Guidelines as evaluation of at least one N1 station and three mediastinal (N2) stations.

Statistical analysis

To minimize selection bias inherent in the retrospective study design and to balance baseline characteristics between the total-port (TP) and utility-incision (UI) groups, both inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) and propensity score matching (PSM) were utilized. Propensity scores were estimated using a multivariable logistic regression model incorporating the following covariates: age, sex, tumor size, and segment complexity. For the PSM sensitivity analysis, a 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching algorithm without replacement was applied with a caliper width of 0.02. Covariate balance before and after adjustment was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with an SMD of less than 0.10 indicating adequate balance.

Continuous variables are presented as means (SDs) or medians (IQRs) and were compared using the unpaired t test or Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages and were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher exact test. Treatment effects on outcomes were estimated using appropriate regression models incorporated with IPTW weights to report odds ratios (ORs), risk differences (RDs), or mean differences (MDs) alongside 95% CIs. All hypothesis tests were 2-sided, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using R software, version 4.1.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Baseline characteristics

Between January 2020 and December 2024, 418 patients were included in the final analysis (215 in the UI group and 203 in the TP group). Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The mean (SD) age was 57.09 (10.36) years in the UI group and 57.28 (10.22) years in the TP group (P = 0.85). Sex distribution, BMI, smoking history, and tumor characteristics were well balanced between the groups. Conversion to thoracotomy was rare, occurring in two patients in the UI group and one patient in the TP group (P = 0.98).

Distribution of target segments and complexity

The distribution of target pulmonary segments differed between the approaches (Table S1). The UI group predominantly underwent standardized single-segment procedures. In contrast, the TP group demonstrated a higher proportion of anatomically complex procedures, including combined subsegments (n = 35) and single subsegmentectomy (n = 17), compared with only 2 and 3 cases in the UI group, respectively.

Overall, the TP group performed complex segmentectomies in 59.6% of cases (121 of 203; 95% CI, 52.9%-66.4%), compared with 38.6% in the UI group (83 of 215; 95% CI, 32.1%-45.1%; P < 0.001). Preoperative or intraoperative localization was required in 17.7% of UI patients compared with only 1.0% of TP patients (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2).

Validation of surgical completeness metric

Interobserver agreement between the two blinded thoracic radiologists for 3D-CT surgical completeness was substantial (Cohen’s κ = 0.65; 93.1% agreement). Among 418 cases, 389 were concordantly classified. Overall unadjusted surgical completeness was significantly higher in the TP group (95.6%; 95% CI, 91.7%-97.7%) than in the UI group (82.8%; 95% CI, 77.1%-87.2%; P < 0.001).

Perioperative outcomes

Perioperative outcomes are summarized in Table 2. The TP approach was associated with lower intraoperative blood loss than the UI approach (mean [SD], 45.02 [25.82] mL vs. 57.40 [35.33] mL; P < 0.001). Operative time was longer in the TP group (mean [SD], 118.29 [31.29] minutes) than in the UI group (99.74 [29.69] minutes; P < 0.001). Postoperative recovery parameters, including length of hospital stay and chest tube duration, were comparable between groups. However, postoperative drainage volume on day 1 (mean [SD], 149.43 [81.75] mL vs. 262.41 [91.85] mL; P = 0.02) and total drainage (mean [SD], 455.84 [430.90] mL vs. 812.81 [461.82] mL; P < 0.001) were significantly lower in the TP group.

With respect to lymph node assessment, the UI group had a higher total lymph node count (mean [SD], 13.10 [9.31] vs. 6.87 [4.74]; P < 0.001) and more nodal stations sampled (mean [SD], 5.79 [2.39] vs. 4.34 [1.90]; P < 0.001).The proportion of patients meeting the NCCN-recommended nodal assessment criteria was 68.5% in the TP group and 83.7% in the UI group (P < 0.001).

Early 30-day postoperative complications occurred in 21 patients (9.8%) in the UI group and 7 patients (3.5%) in the TP group (P = 0.02), all classified as Clavien-Dindo grade I.

Propensity score–based adjusted analyses

After IPTW and PSM adjustment, all baseline covariates achieved satisfactory balance (standardized mean differences < 0.10). In the IPTW-adjusted analysis, the TP approach remained associated with a significantly higher surgical completeness rate (95.8% vs. 80.0%), yielding an adjusted odds ratio (OR) of 5.72 (95% CI, 2.66–12.29; P < 0.001). For secondary perioperative outcomes after IPTW adjustment, the TP group maintained significantly lower intraoperative blood loss (adjusted mean difference [MD], -13.28 mL; P < 0.001) and a lower rate of early complications (adjusted OR, 0.33; 95% CI, 0.14–0.78; P = 0.01). PSM sensitivity analyses yielded highly consistent results (Table 3).

Discussion

With the increasing implementation of lung cancer screening and the widespread detection of small peripheral pulmonary nodules, anatomical segmentectomy has become an important surgical option for early-stage non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Several studies have demonstrated that, for tumors ≤2 cm, segmentectomy can achieve oncologic outcomes comparable to lobectomy, particularly in carefully selected patients with peripheral lesions or ground-glass opacity–dominant tumors [7, 8]. As a result, the demand for precise anatomical segmental resection continues to increase.

The robotic-assisted thoracic surgery (RATS) platform offers advantages for such procedures because of its high-definition three-dimensional visualization and articulated instruments, which facilitate precise bronchovascular dissection. Previous reports have suggested that robotic technology can improve the safety and accuracy of segmentectomy and subsegmentectomy compared with conventional thoracoscopy [9–12]. However, within robotic surgery, different technical approaches exist, most notably utility-incision approach (UI), which includes a utility incision, and the total-port approach (TP), which is performed entirely through ports within a closed thoracic cavity. Direct comparisons between these approaches remain limited.

In this propensity score-based analysis of 418 patients, we evaluated the comparative effectiveness of the total-port (TP) approach versus the utility-incision (UI) approach for robotic anatomical pulmonary segmentectomy. We found that the TP approach, despite being applied more frequently for complex segmentectomies, was associated with a significantly higher rate of surgical completeness as objectively verified by postoperative 3D-CT airway reconstruction. Furthermore, the TP technique was associated with reduced intraoperative blood loss, less postoperative drainage, and fewer 30-day early complications, alongside a marked reduction in the need for preoperative localization.

Several technical factors may explain these findings. The TP technique is performed in a closed thoracic cavity with low-pressure CO₂ insufflation, which expands the operative space and improves visualization of hilar structures. This environment may facilitate precise bronchovascular dissection and reduce the need for lung traction [12, 13], thereby minimizing tissue injury. In contrast, the UI approach relies on a utility incision, which may limit the ability to maintain a stable, insufflated operative field.

Regarding lymph node assessment, the higher yield observed in the UI group may reflect surgeon-dependent decision-making rather than a technical limitation of the TP approach. We acknowledge that current guidelines continue to recommend adequate nodal evaluation for early-stage NSCLC. In our cohort, selective nodal sampling was more frequently adopted in carefully selected patients (e.g., GGO-predominant or Pure GGO lesions) based on intraoperative judgment [14–17]. NCCN-suggested nodal assessment criteria (≥ 1 N1 and ≥ 3 N2 stations) were met in 139/203 (68.5%) of TP cases and 180/215 (83.7%) of UI cases, respectively. However, this should not be interpreted as a recommendation to de-escalate nodal assessment in general.

A major methodological strength of this study is the introduction of postoperative 3D-CT bronchial reconstruction as an objective, auditable metric for evaluating surgical quality. Traditionally, surgical completeness has relied on subjective intraoperative visual assessment by the operating surgeon. Our blinded, dual-radiologist evaluation demonstrated substantial interobserver agreement (κ = 0.65), establishing this metric as a reproducible standard for clinical practice and future trials. The significantly higher completeness rate observed in the TP group suggests that improved operative visualization may reduce anatomical misidentification and enhance surgical precision.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective, single-center study, the findings may be subject to selection bias. Although we utilized robust propensity score methods to balance covariates, residual confounding—such as surgeon experience, learning curve effects, or nuanced tumor anatomy—cannot be entirely excluded. Second, the longer operative time observed in the TP group likely reflects the greater complexity of targeted segments and the time required for complete port placement. Third, it is crucial to clarify that our proposed 3D-CT completeness metric assesses only the technical precision of the parenchymal resection. It does not evaluate the extent or adequacy of lymph node dissection, which is another critical, independent component of oncologic quality. We did not evaluate long-term oncologic outcomes. Thus, whether improved technical completeness translates into superior oncologic outcomes requires verification in prospective, multicenter cohorts with standardized margin reporting and extended follow-up. Finally although all cases achieved negative margins (R0), detailed pathological margin metrics (e.g., margin distance or margin/tumor ratio) were not consistently available and could not be analyzed.

Conclusions

In this retrospective cohort, the total-port robotic approach was associated with improved perioperative outcomes and higher anatomical precision of the parenchymal resection, as verified by postoperative 3D-CT. The TP approach appeared particularly useful for complex segmentectomies and reduced the need for preoperative localization. While postoperative 3D-CT offers an objective tool to evaluate resection quality, the overall oncologic equivalence of the TP approach requires validation in future prospective studies that also scrutinize nodal staging and long-term outcomes.
