Sequential Measurements as a Resource for Quantum Metrology
Koray Mentesoglu, Rahul Trivedi, Sara Mouradian

Abstract
We present a protocol in which sequential weak measurements of a quantum harmonic oscillator enable simultaneous estimation of both quadratures of a displacement channel. Calculations of the quantum Fisher information show that the measurement backaction can increase the information gained for a range of measurement strengths. The protocol distributes information over a N N -bit string after N N weak measurements. Thus we find that post-processing can be used to avoid information loss due to phase wrapping, increasing the effective dynamic range. Finally, the periodic information extraction makes the protocol robust to decoherence. Our results establish mid-sensing measurement as a resource for single- and multi-parameter quantum metrology.



I
Introduction

Quantum metrology aims to estimate a physical parameter of a signal via the response of a controllable quantum system coupled to the signal of interest.
Generally, quantum sensing protocols follow a simple pattern
Degen et al. 2017
; first, the system is prepared in an engineered initial state, then the system interacts with the signal of interest, and finally the system’s state is measured. Non-classical resources such as entanglement
Conlon et al. 2023
;
Marciniak et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2015
;
Hempel et al. 2013
or squeezing
Eckner et al. 2023
;
Burd et al. 2019
;
Tse et al. 2019
;
Bond et al. 2026
and optimal measurements can provide quantum-enhanced precision
Grochowski and Filip 2025
;
Dey et al. 2025
;
Valahu et al. 2025
;
Labarca et al. 2026
, though these must be optimized with respect to prior information about the signal
Kaubruegger et al. 2021
. Quantum control throughout the integration time can also be used to improve sensitivity
Kotler et al. 2011
;
Mouradian et al. 2021
;
Allen et al. 2025
, though this also relies on learned information about the signal.

While most quantum metrology protocols use measurements solely to extract information, work in quantum error correction and non-classical state preparation has shown that the backaction of the measurement can be embraced as an operational principle
Flühmann et al. 2019
;
Grochowski and Filip 2025
;
Ofek et al. 2016
. Indeed, recent works have explored using measurements as a resource in metrology
Montenegro et al. 2022
;
Ritboon et al. 2022
;
Ilias et al. 2022
;
O’Connor et al. 2024
. However, these protocols have been restricted to single-parameter estimation or to particular hardware platforms.

In this work, we present a platform-agnostic metrology protocol that uses measurements as a resource for quantum-enhanced single- and multi-parameter displacement sensing in a quantum harmonic oscillator (QHO), a natural setting for a wide array of applications such as force sensing
Wang et al. 2019
, gravitational wave detection
Aasi et al. 2013
, magnetometry
Eisenach et al. 2021
, electric-field sensing
Gilmore et al. 2021
, and exotic particle detection
Braggio et al. 2025
. We borrow the ancilla-assisted structure of Kitaev’s phase estimation algorithm and its descendants
Kitaev et al. 2002
;
Kimmel et al. 2015
;
Higgins et al. 2007
;
Terhal and Weigand 2016
, but critically do not require the ancilla to be prepared in an eigenstate of the channel of interest. The measured sequence of outcomes from ancilla measurements through the signal integration forms a correlated stochastic process
O’Connor et al. 2024
;
De Pasquale et al. 2017
that distributes signal information across time, enabling more efficient estimation. This approach joins a growing interest in analog quantum processing, where complex dynamics inherent to readily implementable Hamiltonians are leveraged for in-situ computation
Shaw et al. 2025
;
Senanian et al. 2024
;
Geim et al. 2026
;
Dong et al. 2025a
. The proposed protocol is compatible with any system supporting oscillator-ancilla coupling such as trapped ions, superconducting qubits, and neutral atoms
Leibfried et al. 2003
;
Aspelmeyer et al. 2014
;
Liu et al. 2026
;
Li et al. 2026
.

Previous works have demonstrated that a QHO can be used for simultaneous parameter estimation of non-commuting variables. These works relied on the faithful preparation of complex, non-classical states
Bond et al. 2026
;
Valahu et al. 2025
, often requiring adaptive experimental control and optimization. Here we show that complex state preparation and adaptive operations are not necessary for multi-parameter estimation within a single interrogation time. Our proposed protocol leverages the backaction of repeated measurements to independently map information about both quadratures to the ancilla qubit. We find this technique also increases the sensor’s dynamic range and reduces its susceptibility to noise.

This work is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
, we outline the building blocks for the ancilla-assisted measurement of a QHO and introduce a simple protocol with a single measurement as a baseline. In Sec.
III
, we present the sequential-measurement protocol central to this work. In Sec.
IV
, we compare the quantum Fisher information (QFI) of both protocols for single-parameter estimation, showing that sequential measurements extract more information, and are more robust to changes in measurement strength. In Sec.
V
, we extend the protocol to simultaneous estimation of two non-commuting displacement parameters, e.g. the real and imaginary components, within a single interrogation time. Our key finding is that this improves the information gain over the single-parameter protocol, even at matched signal interactions. In Sec.
VI
, we pick an ancilla measurement basis and evaluate the classical Fisher information (CFI), showing it saturates the QFI bound. We also show that post processing can eliminate the effect of phase wrapping, increasing the dynamic range. Finally, in Sec.
VII
, we demonstrate that sequential measurements provide resilience to noise on the QHO.

II
Ancilla-Assisted Weak Measurements of a Quantum Harmonic Oscillator State

We consider the joint estimation of the quadratures
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
of a displacement operator
\hat{D}(\beta)=\exp(\beta\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\beta^{*}\hat{a})
where
\beta=\beta_{1}+i\beta_{2}
(Fig.
1
(a)). We treat
\hat{D}(\beta)
as an unknown unitary channel applied
N
times to a known probe state. Concretely, this could arise from a resonant force with a constant but unknown amplitude and phase, e.g. an electric field on a trapped ion, for a time
T=N\tau
where
\tau
is the duration of a single application.

Weak measurements of the QHO allow for estimates of quadrature observables without fully projecting the state of the QHO
Aharonov et al. 1988
. Here, we consider an ancilla qubit coupled to the QHO via a controlled state-dependent displacement of the form
\hat{D}(\hat{\sigma}_{\bar{\phi}}\alpha)=e^{(\alpha\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\alpha^{*}\hat{a})\hat{\sigma}_{\bar{\phi}}}
with
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
. Such controlled displacement operations are available across superconducting, trapped-ion, neutral-atom, and photonic platforms
Liu et al. 2026
.

The simplest scheme for single-quadrature ancilla-assisted displacement sensing is shown in Fig.
1
(b). First, the QHO and ancilla qubit are prepared in their respective ground states. Then the signal displacement is applied creating a displaced coherent state
\hat{D}(\beta)\ket{\downarrow}\ket{0}=\ket{\downarrow}\ket{\beta}
. The controlled displacement entangles the QHO and ancilla states:

\hat{D}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{x}\alpha\right)\ket{\downarrow}\ket{\beta}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{+}\ket{\beta+\alpha}+\ket{-}\ket{\beta-\alpha}\right),

(1)

where
\ket{+}=(\ket{\downarrow}+\ket{\uparrow})/\sqrt{2}
,
\ket{-}=(\ket{\downarrow}-\ket{\uparrow})/\sqrt{2}
, and we have chosen
\hat{\sigma}_{\bar{\phi}}\equiv\hat{\sigma}_{x}
. Finally, we measure the qubit in the computational basis.

The complex parameter
\alpha
controls two aspects of the ancilla-assisted measurement. First, the magnitude
|\alpha|
determines the spacing of the two coherent wavepackets in phase space, where larger separation reduces correlations between them
Flühmann et al. 2018
. Second,
\text{arg}(\alpha)
determines which quadrature of
\beta
is measured
Sidhu and Kok 2020
. Choosing
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
makes the interaction sensitive to the
\hat{p}
quadrature of the QHO state, while
\alpha\in i\mathbb{R}
gives sensitivity to
\hat{x}
. Therefore,
\alpha
is a tunable parameter that controls both the effective strength and direction of the QHO measurement. Joint estimation of
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})
thus requires measurements with at least two distinct probe directions in phase space.

Repeating this protocol
M
times provides an estimate of one quadrature of
\beta
with a variance that scales as
1/M
, the standard quantum limit (Eq.
1
). If the signal is allowed to accumulate through
N
repeated displacements before a single weak ancilla-assisted measurement (Fig.
1
(b)) estimation variance scales as
1/(MN^{2})
in the absence of decoherence. This method effectively matches the Heisenberg limit scaling achieved with multi-probe entanglement by trading spatial resources for time
Luis 2002
. In the following, we refer to this protocol as the single-measurement protocol.

III
Sequential Measurements

By leveraging the tunable QHO measurement presented in Sec.
II
, we present a sequential-measurement protocol (Fig.
1
(c)) that interrupts the coherent accumulation of signal on the QHO with periodic ancilla-assisted weak measurements. This enables extraction of information at every round while allowing the effect of the signal to integrate on the QHO and exploiting measurement backaction as a resource. The protocol starts with initialization into the system’s ground state,
\ket{\downarrow}\ket{0}
and the first round consists of a signal displacement followed by an ancilla-assisted measurement. Depending on the outcome, the QHO is projected into one of two symmetric superpositions of coherent states, with a phase set by the measurement outcome. After measurement, the qubit is reset while the QHO state is carried forward into the next round. The full protocol operates for
N
rounds, each consisting of a signal interaction, and an ancilla-assisted weak measurement of the QHO (Fig.
1
c). After
N
rounds, the joint-state
\ket{\psi}
of the sequential protocol is

\ket{\psi}=\frac{1}{2^{N/2}}\sum_{a\in\{+,-\}^{N}}\ket{a}\otimes\ket{N\beta+\textstyle\sum_{n}a_{n}\alpha_{n}},

(2)

where we have mapped
N
measurements performed on a single ancilla to one measurement performed on each of
N
ancillae,
\ket{a}
. Like the single-measurement protocol, Eq.
2
includes the coherent accumulation of the signal,
N\beta
. In contrast to the single-measurement protocol, the final QHO state is dependent upon previous rounds through
\sum_{n}a_{n}\alpha_{n}
. For the remainder of this section we focus on single-parameter estimation,
\alpha_{n}=\alpha
for all
n
. In Sec.
V
, we will relax this to enable multi-parameter estimation.

In this
sequential-measurement
protocol, information about
\beta
is encoded in an
N
-bit string. This is in contrast to the
single-measurement protocol
that returns a single bit of information. The number of possible bitstrings,
a\in\{+,-\}^{N}
, increases exponentially: there are
2^{N}
possible measurement outcomes after
N
rounds. This generally makes asymptotic study of sequential measurements difficult
Yang et al. 2023
;
O’Connor et al. 2024
. However, in this setting, we note that the final QHO state only depends on the number of
\ket{+}
or
\ket{-}
outcomes, not the order in which they are observed, i.e. the protocol is insensitive to permutations of the qubit labels. Thus, we adopt a basis that groups states by their total excitation number, collecting all permutationally equivalent states into a single basis element, e.g. the Dicke basis:

\ket{\phi_{k}}=\binom{N}{k}^{-1/2}\sum_{a:w(a)=k}\ket{a},

(3)

where
w(a)
is the number of
\ket{+}
outcomes in the measurement record
a
, or Hamming weight. This reduces the
2^{N}
-dimensional computational basis into an
(N+1)
-dimensional symmetric subspace without loss of information. We can then rewrite Eq.
2
in this permutationally-invariant basis

\ket{\psi}=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\sqrt{\gamma_{k}}\ket{\phi_{k}}\otimes\ket{N\beta+(2k-N)\alpha},

(4)

where
\gamma_{k}=

N\choose k

/2^{N}
, and we have continued to assume
\alpha
is the same for each round.

After
N
rounds of measurement, the
N
ancillas hold information about both the signal and QHO state evolution due to the coupling displacement. Thus, we can perform displacement estimation even without access to the full state of the QHO:

\rho^{\text{seq}}=\text{Tr}_{\text{osc}}\left(\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{N}\tilde{\rho}_{k,k^{\prime}}\ket{\phi_{k}}\bra{\phi_{k^{\prime}}},

(5)

where
\tilde{\rho}_{k,k^{\prime}}=\sqrt{\gamma_{k}\gamma_{k^{\prime}}}e^{-2(k-k^{\prime})^{2}|\alpha|^{2}}e^{-2iN(k-k^{\prime})\text{Im}(\alpha\beta^{*})}
.

As shown by the phase term,
2N(k-k^{\prime})\text{Im}(\alpha\beta^{*})
, aligning
\alpha
on a single quadrature gives

\Phi^{\text{seq}}=\begin{cases}\phantom{-}2N(k-k^{\prime})|\alpha|\beta_{2},&\alpha=|\alpha|\\
-2N(k-k^{\prime})|\alpha|\beta_{1},&\alpha=i|\alpha|.\end{cases}

(6)

The phase of
\alpha
selects which component of
\beta
the protocol estimates. In Sec.
IV
, we quantify the metrological performance of this single-parameter protocol and later extend it to simultaneous estimation of both quadratures in Sec.
V
.

To understand the power of sequential measurements, we begin with single-parameter estimation. We compare the quantum Fisher information (QFI) of the two sensing protocols introduced above (Fig.
2
). As we do not have access to the full state of the QHO, the QFI of the post-protocol qubit state quantifies the maximum achievable information. The derivation of the QFI for the single- and sequential-measurement protocols are found in App.
A
and App.
C
, respectively. The QFI of the full system including the QHO is derived in App.
D
for both protocols for completeness.

Both protocols are compared at matched signal interactions
N
for a range of controlled displacement amplitudes
|\alpha|
. The sequential protocol offers more information for all values of
|\alpha|
(Fig.
2
(a)). While the single-measurement protocol has a single optimal amplitude,
|\alpha|=1/2
, the sequential protocol exhibits a broad range of amplitudes
|\alpha|\in[0.14,0.46]
where
\mathcal{F}^{Q}
remains within
2\%
of its maximum.

We attribute these advantages to the repeated probing of the QHO for information throughout the interrogation time. In our construction, the qubit subsystem of the single-measurement protocol has a fixed dimension of
2\times 2
, so all information extracted about the signal must be contained within those four matrix elements. Meanwhile, the sequential protocol spreads signal information across a linearly increasing matrix dimension. In particular, each new measurement adds a new permutationally invariant state with a unique, conditional signal encoding held within
\rho^{\text{seq}}
(Eq.
5
).

The sequential protocol also offers an increased rate of information gain. In the single-measurement protocol, coherent accumulation of a signal displacement optimally achieves
1/N^{2}
scaling (App.
A
). If direct measurements of the QHO were possible, we show in App.
F
that
\alpha_{n}
can be chosen to tailor the scaling with
N
. If
|\alpha|
is kept constant as in this manuscript, the qubit-QHO system optimally achieves a
N^{3}
scaling (App.
D
). This enhancement can be attributed to two channels of influence: the repeated signal interaction and the repeated measurement. The former enables the
N^{2}
scaling while the latter enables an enhancement by a factor of
N
.

When restricted to ancilla-assisted measurements, the QFI scales as
N^{p}
where the power
p
depends on both
N
and
\alpha
as seen in Fig.
2
(b). As
N
grows, the off-diagonal coherence terms that enable the accumulation of phase (
\propto e^{-2(k-k^{\prime})^{2}|\alpha|^{2}}
) are dampened. To further characterize this suppression, we find
|k-k^{\prime}|\sim\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{N})
, which leads to the effective suppression rate
e^{-N|\alpha|^{2}}
, defining the scaling regimes implemented by the chosen
(N,|\alpha|)
parameters (App.
E
). This effect is made clearer in Fig.
2
(c) where we see a clear transition in the scaling power
p
from
N^{3}
for
N|\alpha|^{2}\ll 1
to
N^{2}
for
N|\alpha|^{2}\gg 1
for all
N
.

V
Extension to two parameters

The single-measurement protocol is confined to single-parameter estimation within a given round. To estimate both quadratures of a displacement channel, this sensing protocol would need two copies of the QHO-ancilla sensor system. However, the sequential protocol can be modified to glean information about both quadratures of the displacement channel with only one sensor system.

To do this, we vary the complex parameter
\alpha
which controls the state-dependent displacement,
\hat{D}(\hat{\sigma}_{x}\alpha/2)
. In contrast to the single-parameter case (Sec.
IV
), where
\alpha
is the same for all
N
, two-parameter estimation interleaves another set of
N
projections on the orthogonal quadrature (
2N
measurements total), while still maintaining the same coupling strength
|\alpha|
throughout (Eq.
6
).

By separately probing each quadrature of the QHO, the two-parameter sequential protocol includes two sets of outcomes, dependent on the probed quadrature which take the form
s_{\text{Re}}(a)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{2j-1}
and
s_{\text{Im}}(a)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{2j}
. The two sets of outcomes can be used to define permutationally invariant basis states as in the single-parameter case (Sec.
II
). Now accounting for the new alternation of quadratures, the density matrix of the qubit subsystem becomes

\rho^{\text{seq(2)}}=\sum_{k_{r},k_{i}}\sum_{k^{\prime}_{r},k^{\prime}_{i}}\tilde{\rho}_{k_{r},k_{i},k_{r}^{\prime},k_{i}^{\prime}}^{(2)}\ket{\phi_{k_{r},k_{i}}}\bra{\phi_{k_{r}^{\prime},k_{i}^{\prime}}},

(7)

where there are now two Hamming weight indices
k_{r},k_{i}
corresponding to probing the real and imaginary components of
\beta
(App.
G
).

Importantly, the QFI of the two-parameter protocol is now a matrix where the diagonal terms represent the QFI bounds for each of the parameters of interest

\textbf{F}^{Q}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{F}_{\beta_{1}\beta_{1}}^{Q}&\mathcal{F}_{\beta_{1}\beta_{2}}^{Q}\\
\mathcal{F}_{\beta_{2}\beta_{1}}^{Q}&\mathcal{F}_{\beta_{2}\beta_{2}}^{Q}\end{bmatrix},

(8)

with matrix elements

\mathcal{F}_{ab}^{Q}=\sum_{jk}\frac{2}{p_{j}+p_{k}}\bra{j}\partial_{a}\rho^{\text{seq}(2)}\ket{k}\bra{j}\partial_{b}\rho^{\text{seq}(2)}\ket{k}^{*},

(9)

which are constructed using
\partial_{a}\rho^{\text{seq(2)}}=\frac{1}{2}(\rho^{\text{seq(2)}}L_{a}+L_{a}\rho^{\text{seq(2)}})
where
L_{a}
is the symmetric logarithmic derivative (SLD) associated with parameter
a
, and
a,b\in\{\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\}
. In general, the SLDs may not commute, and therefore the QFI would not be physically saturable and is thus an insufficient metric for evaluating information gain
Górecki et al. 2020
;
Liu et al. 2020
. In our analysis we find the SLDs do commute, and therefore, like in the single-parameter case, the QFI provides the maximum achievable information gain of the two-parameter protocol. Furthermore, we find Eq.
8
to be diagonal (App.
H
).

In Fig.
3
, we compare
\mathcal{F}^{Q}_{11}
of the two-parameter protocol to the QFI of the single-parameter protocol. Though both protocols perform
N
measurements along the quadrature of interest, the two-parameter protocol yields a higher QFI per parameter. For reference, we also plot the single-parameter protocol after
2N
measurements to match the total measurement budget of the two-parameter protocol, which yields comparable performance to the two-parameter protocol. Crucially, however, the single-parameter protocol only extracts information about one quadrature, as illustrated in the inset of Fig.
3

\mathcal{F}^{Q}_{11}=\mathcal{F}^{Q}_{22}
by symmetry of the protocol.

We attribute this enhancement to the higher-dimensional Hamming weight space that the two-parameter protocol naturally implements. At matched measurement budgets, the single-parameter protocol’s evolution can be described by a
(2N+1)
-dimensional space indexed by Hamming weight
k
, whereas the two-parameter protocol spans an
(N+1)^{2}
-dimensional space indexed by
(k_{r},k_{i})
. As the strength of measurement,
|\alpha|
, increases, off-diagonal coherences of
\rho^{\text{seq}(2)}
are modulated by a Gaussian suppression factor
\exp\left[-2|\alpha|^{2}\left((k_{r}-k_{r}^{\prime})^{2}+(k_{i}-k_{i}^{\prime})^{2}\right)\right]
, which separates across two indices, each resembling the single-parameter case:
\exp\left[-2|\alpha|^{2}(k-k^{\prime})^{2}\right]
(App.
G
). However, the single-parameter protocol concentrates
2N
measurements within a single index, where the maximum difference is
k-k^{\prime}=2N
. In the two-parameter protocol, the suppression is distributed across two indices, each with maximum difference
N
, which reduces the maximum suppression by a factor of four. This reduced suppression contributes to the wider range of optimal
|\alpha|
observed for the two-parameter protocol (Fig.
3
).

With this two-parameter extension, the observer infers the full signal displacement exclusively through ancilla measurements. For direct measurements on a QHO, the conjugate relationship between position and momentum,
[\hat{x},\hat{p}]=i
, limits the optimal measurement strategy to heterodyne detection
Demkowicz-Dobrzański et al. 2020
. However, indirect measurement through an ancillary system avoids this limitation by preserving information about both quadratures between measurements, creating signaling across outcomes, a uniquely quantum resource, which allows for improved estimation
Flühmann et al. 2018
. We note that the two-parameter extension resembles the creation of certain bosonic codes that themselves enable multi-parameter estimation
Bond et al. 2026
;
Valahu et al. 2025
(App.
I
).

VI
Estimation Performance and Measurement Basis

While the QFI provides the upper bound for the amount of information that can be extracted from a post-signal sensor state, it does not tell us the optimal protocol to saturate such a bound. In practice, the optimal measurement may be unknown or experimentally inaccessible. Instead, the classical Fisher information (CFI) gives the information gain from a particular measurement protocol given a post-signal sensor state.

Here, we measure each qubit in the computational basis
\{\ket{\downarrow},\ket{\uparrow}\}
. The probability of observing a particular bitstring
a
in the chosen basis is

\displaystyle p_{\beta_{1}}(a)=

\displaystyle\bra{a}\rho_{Q}^{\text{seq}}\ket{a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{N\choose w(a)}^{-1}\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}V_{k,w(a)}\tilde{\rho}_{k,k^{\prime}}V_{k^{\prime},w(a)},

(10)

where
V_{k,k^{\prime}}=\bra{\tilde{\phi}_{k^{\prime}}}\phi_{k}\rangle
maps between permutationally invariant bases and
w(a)\in[0,1,...,N]
are all possible Hamming weights (App.
B
). During operation of the protocol, the number of possible bitstrings scales as
2^{N}
, however, due to the permutational invariance of the final QHO state, we reduce analysis to
N+1
unique outcomes, where the output bitstrings are grouped by their excitation number.

\sum_{k}(\partial_{\beta_{1}}P(k))^{2}/P(k)\approx 0

It is important to emphasize here that in the presented protocol, the information about the signal is encoded in a
N
-bit string which in practice would be acquired sequentially. Thus, partial estimates of
\beta_{1}
are available at each round. The sequential-measurement protocol accumulates information in time and so post-processing measurement data after an interrogation time can intentionally omit measurements that lose information, even without prior knowledge about
\beta
. This effectively removes dips in the CFI, as indicated by the dashed line in Fig.
5
(a). In contrast, the single-measurement protocol only performs a single measurement after the full interrogation time and thus the information dip cannot be mitigated in post-processing (orange line). Moreover, the availability of partial estimates at each round of the sequential protocol could enable adaptive strategies to improve information gain in real time, a possibility unavailable to the single-measurement protocol.

In Fig.
5
(b,c), we compare the two protocols’ outcome distributions for
N=40
. For the selected coupling strength
|\alpha|=0.2
, the phase
2N\beta_{1}|\alpha|
accumulates with each interaction. At
\beta_{1}=0.05
, the accumulated phase for both protocols is
\sim 0.8\text{rad}
, and both protocols produce distributions skewed toward the ground state:
\ket{\downarrow}
for the single-measurement protocol and
0^{\otimes N}
for the sequential-measurement protocol. At
\beta_{1}=0.1
, the accumulated phase reaches
\sim 1.6\text{rad}
, and the outcome distributions for both protocols become nearly symmetric.

While both protocols exhibit qualitatively similar behavior based on the two operating points in Fig.
5
(b,c), they differ in how signal information is encoded. As discussed above, the probability curve in Fig.
4
(b) for the sequential protocol is the average over many conditional curves, each offset by a phase determined by the prior measurement record. The signal information is therefore not concentrated in a single curve but distributed across the
N+1
possible Hamming weight outcomes

(\partial_{\beta_{1}}P(k))^{2}/P(k)
, and as
N
increases, the sequential protocol accesses an increasingly high-dimensional outcome space. The single measurement protocol instead compresses all signal information into a binary outcome whose dimensionality remains fixed as
N
increases.

Having established how the computational basis extracts signal information in the single-parameter case, we now evaluate its efficiency for joint estimation when the protocol alternates between quadratures. To do this, we move from the scalar CFI to the CFI matrix
\textbf{F}^{C}
and invoke the Cramér-Rao Bound (CRB)

\text{Var}(\beta_{1})+\text{Var}(\beta_{2})\geq\text{Tr}\left[\left(\textbf{F}^{C}\right)^{-1}\right],

(11)

which bounds the total variance of an unbiased estimator for both parameters. In general, a
suboptimal measurement basis introduces off-diagonal elements in
\textbf{F}^{C}
that couple the
estimation of both parameters, and
\text{Tr}[(\textbf{F}^{C})^{-1}]
accounts for this cross-coupling.

In accordance with the destructive interference noted for the single-parameter CFI, the single-measurement protocol exhibits a characteristic checker-board interference pattern stemming from the phase wrapping condition

VII
Decoherence

Finally, we evaluate the performance of the sequential measurement protocol in the face of environmental noise. Our analysis in Secs.
IV
-
VI
has characterized the sensing interrogation time by
N\beta
displacements. Inherently, then, we have assumed initialization, control, and measurement operations are fast relative to the signal interaction time. Now, we maintain this assumption but introduce a time variable,
t
, to include the impact of noise on the QHO evolution.

Each round of the protocol has duration
t=\sqrt{2}|\beta|/g
, set by the interaction time needed for a signal with coupling rate
g
to produce a displacement with amplitude
|\beta|
. Then, a constant signal that produces such a displacement grows linearly in
t
. When impacted by decoherence, the displacement after a single round becomes

|\beta_{d}(t)|=\frac{\sqrt{2}g}{\Gamma}\left(1-e^{-\Gamma t/2}\right),

(12)

where
\Gamma\equiv\gamma+\gamma_{d}
is the effective decay rate of the coherent state amplitude, with the QHO loss and dephasing rate represented by
\gamma
and
\gamma_{d}
, respectively. We parameterize the decoherence strength by
\tau_{eff}=\Gamma t/2
, which quantifies the linear decay of the coherent state amplitude accumulated per round. For the sequential protocol,
N
rounds correspond to a total interrogation time
Nt
with measurements at each interval, while the single-measurement protocol accumulates signal over the same total time but measures only at the end.

We plot the impact of noise on both protocols in Fig.
7
(a). The CRB is computed from the state
\rho^{\text{seq}(2)}
evolved under the Lindblad master equation and plotted as a function of interrogation time (App.
L
). The single-measurement protocol exhibits an optimal measurement time, beyond which decoherence degrades the single-shot measurement faster than the signal accumulates. The sequential protocol, by contrast, extracts information at each round, causing its CRB to decrease monotonically, even in the face of decoherence. As the per-round information gain becomes negligible, the CRB of the sequential protocol will asymptotically saturate.

. This feature relaxes the constraint on measurement timing. The single-measurement protocol must time the final measurement to simultaneously extract substantial signal information and avoid decoherence effects, whereas the sequential protocol is more robust since each round of signal interaction extracts and stores information in a classical measurement record.

VIII
Conclusion

In this work, we have presented a protocol for ancilla-assisted displacement sensing of a QHO that uses sequential measurements as a metrological resource. By borrowing the ancilla-assisted structure of quantum phase estimation algorithms, the protocol benefits from accumulating signal phase. Instead of a single terminal measurement, the protocol uses interleaved measurements to accumulate information about the signal and perturb system dynamics. This approach yields
N^{3}
scaling of the quantum Fisher information in the pre-asymptotic regime, and exceeds the information gain from performing a single measurement. Moreover, since information is extracted throughout the interrogation time, the protocol increases the sensor’s effective dynamic range, where subsets of measurement data can be selected to avoid destructive interference. Furthermore, later measurement rounds benefit from information gained in earlier rounds which, as we demonstrate, provides resilience to decoherence. Interestingly, we find that the presented two-parameter protocol outperforms its single-parameter counterpart when estimating a single quadrature. The proposed protocol also enables simultaneous estimation of conjugate observables within a single interrogation time, without relying on complex state preparation or specialized measurements.

In the presented protocol, the qubit measurements are an irreversible action that, over time, allow the QHO to traverse a unique, signal-dependent trajectory. These ancilla measurements effectively serve as a non-linear resource for sensing
Senanian et al. 2024
, allowing the sensor to sample a wider range of operating points during its interrogation time. Future work could leverage such a non-linearity to extend this protocol to time-dependent signals which may be error prone or follow complex probability distributions. Such extensions could include variational optimization of control parameters
Marciniak et al. 2022
and incorporating repeated intermediate measurements, recently shown to independently increase the rate of information gain
Valahu et al. 2025
. Furthermore, adaptive classical and quantum control can be applied, as is conventionally done in single-measurement metrology, to analyze the advantage of sequential measurements for different sensing problems.

This work joins a growing interest in leveraging analog quantum system evolution, where a quantum system’s inherent dynamics are used to perform processing, sensing, or simulation tasks
Dong et al. 2025a
;
Shaw et al. 2025
;
Kashyap et al. 2025
. Besides displacement sensing, such signal-dependent QHO trajectories could be adapted for non-classical state generation
Simoni et al. 2025
;
Matsos et al. 2024
. Though the presented protocol relies on QHO-qubit coupling, the principle of monitoring a quantum system through repeated ancilla-assisted measurements can be generalized to other architectures e.g., qubit-qubit
Montenegro et al. 2022
and QHO-QHO. This motivates further study of sequential measurement protocols across diverse hardware platforms and applications. Combined with non-classical probe states
Labarca et al. 2026
;
Bond et al. 2026
and adaptive control
Allen et al. 2025
;
de Neeve et al. 2025
, mid-sensing measurement-based protocols could offer new opportunities for quantum-enhanced parameter estimation.

Appendix B
PERMUTATIONAL INVARIANCE

Since the sequential protocol operates forwards in time, each measurement presents an exponentially scaling (
2^{N}
) number of trajectories. However, by construction of the protocol, the order of measurement outcomes is not important for the final QHO state. Rather, the number of excited,
\ket{+}
, versus non-excited,
\ket{-}
, outcomes distinguishes the final QHO probe state. This allows us to define a set of permutationally invariant basis states

\begin{Bmatrix}\ket{\phi_{0}}=\ket{---\cdots-}\\
\ket{\phi_{1}}=N^{-1/2}(\ket{+--\cdots-}+\ket{-+-\cdots-}+\cdots)\\
\ket{\phi_{2}}={N\choose 2}^{-1/2}(\ket{++-\cdots-}+\ket{+-+\cdots-}+\cdots)\\
\vdots\\
\ket{\phi_{N}}=\ket{+++\cdots+}\end{Bmatrix}

(18)

which are defined in Eq.
3
and describe all possible trajectories for
N
sequential measurements. In the sequential protocol, we measure in the
\{\ket{\downarrow},\ket{\uparrow}\}
basis which motivates the usage of another set of basis states,
\{\ket{\tilde{\phi}_{i}}\}
, defined similarly to Eq.
18
.

We can show that both these bases,
\{\ket{\phi_{i}}\}
and
\{\ket{\tilde{\phi}_{i}}\}
, are valid permutationally invariant states. First, we consider a general
N
qubit state

\ket{\psi}=\sum_{i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{N}}\psi_{i,i_{2},...,i_{N}}\ket{i_{1},...,i_{N}}.

(19)

The definition of a permutationally invariant state is that a rearrangement of any elements in the state will not change the final state outcome. This characteristic is a principal component of the sequential-measurement protocol, and without it simplification would not be possible (i.e. the QHO state is equal for all
N!
permutations of
N\choose k
terms). We can write this for the invariant states of interest as
U_{\pi}\ket{\phi_{i}}=\ket{\phi_{i}}
and
U_{\pi}\ket{\tilde{\phi}_{i}}=\ket{\tilde{\phi}_{i}}
, where
U_{\pi}
is a permuting unitary. Applying this to Eq.
19
gives

U_{\pi}\ket{\psi}=\sum_{i_{1},...,i_{N}}\psi_{i_{1},...,i_{N}}\ket{i_{\pi(1),...,\pi(N)}}=\ket{\psi},

(20)

where the order of qubits has been permuted as a function of
\pi(\cdot)
. As a result of performing the sequential-measurement protocol, there are
N!
permutations on the subsystem of qubits and their application can be summarized as

\displaystyle\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{\pi}U_{\pi}\ket{\psi}

(21)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1},...,i_{n}}\psi_{i_{1},...,i_{N}}\left[\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{\pi}\ket{i_{\pi(1)},...,i_{\pi(N)}}\right].

(22)

This means the below interpretations are identical

\begin{gathered}\text{X basis:}\begin{Bmatrix}\ket{i_{1},...,i_{N}}=\ket{---\cdots-}\\
\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{\pi}\ket{i_{\pi(1)},...,i_{\pi(N)}}=\ket{---\cdots-}\end{Bmatrix},\\[4.30554pt]
\text{Z basis:}\begin{Bmatrix}\ket{i_{1},...,i_{N}}=\ket{\downarrow\downarrow\downarrow\cdots\downarrow}\\
\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{\pi}\ket{i_{\pi(1)},...,i_{\pi(N)}}=\ket{\downarrow\downarrow\downarrow\cdots\downarrow}\end{Bmatrix}.\end{gathered}

(23)

Since coupling is performed in one basis, we must map from one permutationally invariant basis to another (App.
J
).

\displaystyle 4N|\alpha|\left[\beta_{1}(k_{i}-k_{i}^{\prime})+\beta_{2}(k_{r}^{\prime}-k_{r})\right]+

\displaystyle|\alpha|^{2}\left[4(k_{i}k_{r}^{\prime}-k_{i}^{\prime}k_{r})+2N(k_{i}^{\prime}-k_{i}+k_{r}-k_{r}^{\prime})\right],

(39)

which was derived using
\braket{\gamma^{\prime}|\gamma}=e^{-(|\gamma|^{2}+|\gamma^{\prime}|^{2})/2}e^{(\gamma^{\prime})^{*}\gamma}
.

Appendix H
SLD COMMUTATION

In multi-parameter quantum metrology, the nature of conjugate observables poses a constraint on estimation capability. In particular, two parameters,
\beta=\beta_{1}+i\beta_{2}
, encoded in the state,
\rho^{\text{seq}(2)}
, can be related by

\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}(\rho^{\text{seq}(2)}\{L_{a},L_{b}\})=\sum_{j,k}\frac{2}{p_{j}+p_{k}}\bra{j}\partial_{a}\rho|k\rangle\bra{k}\partial_{b}\rho|j\rangle,

(40)

where
a,b\in\{\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\}
and the SLDs are defined as
\partial_{a}\rho^{\text{seq}(2)}=\frac{1}{2}(L_{a}\rho^{\text{seq}(2)}+\rho^{\text{seq}(2)}L_{a})
with
L_{a}
being the SLD for parameter
\beta_{a}

Górecki et al. 2020
. The real component of Eq.
40
gives the QFI (App.
C
). The imaginary component of Eq.
40
gives insight into the capability of the metrological protocol. In particular, the QFI offers a saturable bound for both parameters when

\text{Im}\left[\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}(\rho^{\text{seq}(2)}\{L_{a},L_{b}\})\right]=0.

(41)

Otherwise, other more general bounds can be invoked
Liu et al. 2020
or optimization to find the best realizable measurement can be performed.

We show the two-parameter protocol satisfies Eq.
41
, therefore allowing the QFI to be used as a saturable bound for this multi-parameter displacement sensing protocol. The two-parameter protocol (App.
G
) can be written in the form of Eq.
40
giving

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}\!\left(\rho^{\text{seq}(2)}\{L_{a},L_{b}\}\right)

\displaystyle=-(4N|\alpha|)^{2}\times

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{j,k}\frac{2(k_{i}-k_{i}^{\prime})(k_{r}^{\prime}-k_{r})}{p_{j}+p_{k}}|\tilde{\rho}^{(2)}_{j,k}|^{2},

(42)

where the indices of
\tilde{\rho}
have been omitted (Eq.
G
). Every factor in this summation is real-valued, meaning Eq.
41
is satisfied.

Furthermore, Eq.
H
shows that the two-parameter QFI is diagonal. The alternating orthogonal projection of
\alpha
imprints
\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}
on independent Hamming weight indices
k_{i}
and
k_{r}
. This can be seen from the prefactor
(k_{i}-k_{i}^{\prime})(k_{r}^{\prime}-k_{r})
being antisymmetric, causing all off-diagonal elements to be zero.

Appendix I
FROM GRID STATES TO THE SEQUENTIAL PROTOCOL

Grid states encode a qubit’s logical basis states in an infinite dimensional QHO. The benefit to this construction is the ability manipulate the otherwise conjugate observables
[\hat{x},\hat{p}]=i
into a commuting form. In particular, carefully chosen unitaries, referred to as stabilizers, can detect small displacements up to a modulo value. To achieve this, stabilizers are defined as displacements along each quadrature in the phase space of a QHO
Gottesman et al. 2001

\hat{S}_{x}=\hat{D}(\gamma_{x}),\ \hat{S}_{p}=\hat{D}(\gamma_{p}).

(43)

These displacements follow the commutation relation

\displaystyle[\hat{D}(\gamma_{x}),\hat{D}(\gamma_{p})]

\displaystyle=2ie^{i\Phi}\sin(\Phi)\hat{D}(\gamma_{x}+\gamma_{p}),

(44)

where
\Phi=\text{Im}(\gamma_{x}\gamma_{p}^{*})
. Then logical basis states can be defined in terms of the displacement stabilizers

\ket{l}\propto\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{D}(k\lambda_{p})\ket{x_{l}},\quad l\in\{\downarrow,\uparrow\},

(45)

which represents two offset superpositions formed in position space and
\{\lambda_{p},\lambda_{x}\}\in\mathbb{C}
are chosen to satisfy
\sin(\Phi)=0
, ensuring the stabilizers commute. Due to their conjugate property, position and momentum space can be related through the Fourier transform

\ket{x}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dpe^{ipx}\ket{p},

(46)

and therefore the comb structure where peaks separated by
\lambda_{p}
in position space correspond to peaks separated by
\lambda_{x}
in momentum space. The periodicity in dual quadratures makes changes up to
x\ \text{mod}\ \lambda_{p}
and
p\ \text{mod}\ \lambda_{x}
detectable in both quadratures simultaneously. This is the promise of grid states for fault-tolerant computation and sensing applications. Namely, the ability to detect signals up to the modulo displacement value without disturbing the conjugate observable.

To reliably create grid states, one must not only satisfy the condition in Eq.
45
, but one must also ensure the encoded logical state
\ket{l}
is an eigenstate of both stabilizers
\{\hat{S}_{x},\hat{S}_{p}\}
. In practice, one way of creating this is through repeated application of both of the aforementioned stabilizers, with each application pushing the QHO closer to the +1 eigenspace of the corresponding operator, e.g.
\hat{S}_{x}\ket{\psi}\approx\ket{\psi}
and
\hat{S}_{p}\ket{\psi}\approx\ket{\psi}
. Another interpretation would be that the creation of grid states searches for the joint eigenspace shared between stabilizers which can be stated as a multi-parameter phase estimation problem
Terhal and Weigand 2016
.

The sequential protocol is inspired by the creation of grid states. As stated above, once a QHO has been nudged into a known state, it is easier to characterize deviations of said state. To see this, consider
N=1
for the two-parameter protocol, but
\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}=0
which gives the relevant matrix elements

\displaystyle\rho^{\text{seq}(2)}=\frac{1}{4}\begin{bmatrix}1&e^{-2|\alpha|^{2}+i|\alpha|^{2}}&e^{-2|\alpha|^{2}-i|\alpha|^{2}}&e^{-4|\alpha|^{2}}\\
e^{-2|\alpha|^{2}-i|\alpha|^{2}}&1&e^{-4|\alpha|^{2}+i2|\alpha|^{2}}&e^{-2|\alpha|^{2}+i3|\alpha|^{2}}\\
e^{-2|\alpha|^{2}+i|\alpha|^{2}}&e^{-4|\alpha|^{2}-i2|\alpha|^{2}}&1&e^{-2|\alpha|^{2}-i3|\alpha|^{2}}\\
e^{-4|\alpha|^{2}}&e^{-2|\alpha|^{2}-i3|\alpha|^{2}}&e^{-2|\alpha|^{2}+i3|\alpha|^{2}}&1\end{bmatrix},

(47)

obtained from Eq.
G
. The commutator for the applied displacement operators is

[\hat{D}(|\alpha|),\hat{D}(i|\alpha|)]=2i\sin(|\alpha|^{2})\hat{D}(|\alpha|+i|\alpha|).

(48)

If we strategically pick
|\alpha|=\sqrt{n\pi}
with
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, the displacements will commute and Eq.
47
becomes

\rho^{\text{seq}(2)}=\frac{1}{4}\begin{bmatrix}1&\pm x&\pm x&x^{2}\\
\pm x&1&x^{2}&\pm x\\
\pm x&x^{2}&1&\pm x\\
x^{2}&\pm x&\pm x&1\end{bmatrix},

(49)

where
x=e^{-2n\pi}
and
\pm
corresponds to even and odd
n
respectively. Then, in general, each matrix element will be

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{k_{r},k_{i},k_{r}^{\prime},k_{i}^{\prime}}=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\gamma_{k_{r}}\gamma_{k_{i}}\gamma_{k_{r}^{\prime}}\gamma_{k_{i}^{\prime}}}\times

(50)

\displaystyle\qquad\exp\left[-2n\pi\big((k_{r}-k_{r}^{\prime})^{2}+(k_{i}-k_{i}^{\prime})^{2}\big)\right],

which can be recognized as the overlap of coherent states. As we scale
N
, we will see the qubit subsystem reflecting the QHO state: a superposition of coherent states with periodic spacing.

An interesting point here is to observe the two commutation conditions: Eq.
48
and Eq.
41
. The former is more restrictive on displacement operators and the latter guarantees saturability of the QFI. By construction of the two-parameter protocol, the former is generally not satisfied while the latter is (App.
H
).

Appendix J
MAPPING FROM ONE PERMUTATIONALLY INVARIANT SPACE TO ANOTHER

In App.
B
, we have shown that two bases are valid permutationally invariant states. To map one basis to another we consider

\ket{\phi_{k}}=\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{N}V_{k,k^{\prime}}\ket{\tilde{\phi}_{k^{\prime}}}.

(51)

So the two permutationally invariant bases are connected through application of a unitary where each element is
V_{k,k^{\prime}}=\bra{\tilde{\phi}_{k^{\prime}}}\phi_{k}\rangle
. Both bases take the form of Eq.
3
so the overlap becomes

V_{k,k^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{{N\choose k}{N\choose k^{\prime}}}}\sum_{w(a)=k,w(a^{\prime})=k^{\prime}}\bra{a^{\prime}}U^{\otimes N}\ket{a},

(52)

where
a^{\prime}
denotes the computational basis. Here
U
is a general unitary transformation of the form

U^{\otimes N}=e^{i\theta_{x}S_{x}+\theta_{y}S_{y}+\theta_{z}S_{z}},

(53)

with
S_{x}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{x}^{(i)}
. Applying this to Eq.
52
gives

\displaystyle V_{k,k^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\binom{N}{k}}{2^{N/2}\sqrt{\binom{N}{k}\binom{N}{k^{\prime}}}}\times

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{s=\max(0,\,k+k^{\prime}-N)}^{\min(k,\,k^{\prime})}\binom{k}{s}\binom{N-k}{k^{\prime}-s}\times

\displaystyle\qquad u_{00}^{s}\,u_{10}^{k-s}\,u_{01}^{k^{\prime}-s}\,u_{11}^{N-k-k^{\prime}+s},

(54)

where
s=a\cdot a^{\prime}
is the shared excitation count between the bit-string outcomes and
u_{ii}
denotes the matrix elements of the unitary
U
.

Appendix K
CLASSICAL FISHER INFORMATION AND MEASUREMENT BASIS

The CFI measures the attainable information within the state
\rho^{\text{seq}}
after it is measured in a particular basis. This means we must map from one permutationally invariant basis to another

\rho^{\text{seq}}=\sum_{i,j}\left(\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}V_{k,i}\tilde{\rho}_{k,k^{\prime}}V_{k^{\prime},j}\right)\ket{\tilde{\phi}_{i}}\bra{\tilde{\phi}_{j}}.

(55)

Using this, we can compute the CFI

\mathcal{F}^{C}=\sum_{a\in\{0,1\}^{N}}\frac{1}{p_{\beta}(a)}\left(\frac{d}{d\beta}p_{\beta}(a)\right)^{2},

(56)

where
p_{\beta}(a)
(Eq.
VI
) is the probability of observing a bit string of outcomes in the new basis. In this case the CFI becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{C}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{1}{\sum_{m,m^{\prime}}V_{m,k}\tilde{\rho}_{m,m^{\prime}}V_{m^{\prime},k}}\times

\displaystyle\qquad\left(\frac{d}{d\beta}\left[\sum_{m,m^{\prime}}V_{m,k}\tilde{\rho}_{m,m^{\prime}}V_{m^{\prime},k}\right]\right)^{2},

(57)

and since we have chosen the computational basis
\{\ket{\downarrow},\ket{\uparrow}\}
for measurement, the transfer matrix is

\displaystyle V_{k,k^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\binom{N}{k}}{2^{N/2}\sqrt{\binom{N}{k}\binom{N}{k^{\prime}}}}\times

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{s=\max(0,\,k+k^{\prime}-N)}^{\min(k,\,k^{\prime})}(-1)^{s}\binom{k}{s}\binom{N-k}{k^{\prime}-s}.

(58)

which corresponds to the application of
U^{\otimes N}=e^{i\pi/4S_{y}}
(App.
J
). The application of
N
identical measurements is not necessarily the optimal case, hence the interference dips observed (Sec.
VI

Appendix L
DECOHERENCE MODEL

In practical scenarios, the sensor will act as an open quantum system. As the oscillator interacts with signals, it will be continuously disturbed by the environment. We model the system-environment interaction through the master equation

\dot{\rho}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{H}_{int},\rho]+\sum_{j}\mathcal{L}_{j}(\rho),

(59)

where
\rho
is the joint-state density matrix. Here, the interaction picture
\hat{H}_{int}
will pass through Markovian noise channels of the form
\mathcal{L}_{j}(\rho)=L_{j}\rho L_{j}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{L_{j}^{\dagger}L_{j},\rho\}
. In this case, the oscillator is modeled as being subject to loss, heating, and dephasing with
L_{1}=\sqrt{\gamma(1+n_{th})}\hat{a}
and
L_{2}=\sqrt{\gamma n_{th}}\hat{a}^{\dagger}
and
L_{3}=\sqrt{\gamma_{d}}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}
, respectively
Grochowski and Filip 2025
. In this picture, a coherent state will experience a decay in its amplitude
\langle\hat{a}\rangle
proportional to the rate it couples to the external noise bath following
\langle\hat{a}(t)\rangle=\langle\hat{a}(0)\rangle e^{-\Gamma t/2}
where
\Gamma\equiv\gamma+\gamma_{d}
is the effective decay rate and
t
is a timestep during system evolution. Here we take
n_{th}\approx 0
.
