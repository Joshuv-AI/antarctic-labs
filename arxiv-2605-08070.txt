VecCISC: Improving Confidence-Informed Self-Consistency with Reasoning Trace Clustering and Candidate Answer Selection
James PetulloSonny GeorgeDylan CashmanNianwen XueComputer Science Department, Brandeis University jamespetullo@brandeis.edu

Abstract
A standard technique for scaling inference-time reasoning is Self-Consistency, whereby multiple candidate answers are sampled from an LLM and the most common answer is selected. More recently, it has been shown that weighted majority voting (e.g. Confidence-Informed Self Consistency (CISC)), which assigns a confidence value to each candidate answer and chooses the answer with the largest accumulated score, tends to be more accurate on a wide range of popular benchmarks. In practice, weighted majority voting necessitates calling a critic LLM on each candidate’s reasoning trace to produce the answer’s confidence score. This secondary series of LLM calls greatly increases the overhead and cost of weighted majority voting, despite its potential performance benefits. To reduce this expense, we propose VecCISC, a lightweight, adaptive framework that uses a measure of semantic similarity to filter reasoning traces that are semantically equivalent to others, degenerate, or hallucinated, thus decreasing the number of candidate answers that must be evaluated by the critic. To ensure adequate experimental thoroughness, we evaluate VecCISC on five challenging, widely-adopted datasets spanning the domains of mathematics, chemistry, biology, commonsense reasoning, and the humanities. Our results demonstrate that VecCISC reduces the total token usage by 47%, while maintaining or exceeding the accuracy of CISC.



1
Introduction

Scaling inference-time reasoning has become an effective and popular approach to improving the performance of LLMs on reasoning tasks
30
;
19
;
25
;
37
. At its core, scaling inference-time reasoning involves sampling multiple outputs from an LLM and, from them, producing a final answer. The underlying assumption is that a larger set of diverse reasoning traces will lead to a greater number of potential answers, thus improving coverage of the solution search space. The foundational technique for inference scaling is Self-Consistency
33
, whereby an LLM is called multiple times for a given prompt, and the most common answer is returned. Since these methods can be costly, efforts have been made to decrease sampling costs while maintaining accuracy
6
. These approaches include early stopping
10
;
17
;
2
, mixture of models
5
;
38
, and query routing
22
. Recently, it has been shown that confidence-informed Self-Consistency (CISC)
32
can be utilized in conjunction with weighted majority voting to improve the accuracy of Self-Consistency. CISC, and the broader concept of LLM confidence calibration, belongs to the
“think twice” paradigm

15
, whereby an LLM first generates an answer and an associated reasoning trace, and then a critic LLM is prompted to assess the answer-trace pair. The critic produces a score that reflects its confidence that the answer is correct. These scores are then used as part of a weighted majority vote to select the final answer. Throughout this paper, we use the term “think twice” to refer to any method that necessitates calling a critic for confidence estimation.

However, a significant drawback to “think twice” approaches is that the inference overhead increases significantly, as each sample requires at least one additional LLM call, often with a token-heavy prompt. Furthermore, naive CISC makes no distinction between hallucinated, degenerate samples and higher quality outputs, running the critic LLM on all regardless.

As such, we introduce VecCISC, a flexible, conceptually straightforward candidate answer aggregation framework that reduces the number of samples needed to be evaluated by the critic LLM through clustering candidate answers via embeddings of their corresponding reasoning traces. By adding only minimal overhead through the application of an embedding model, our clustering approach filters redundant traces that possess the same semantic meaning (see Appendix
A.3
) and can aid in isolating answers that contain flawed, hallucinated, and degenerate reasoning (see Appendix
A.4
). After clustering, a representative answer from each cluster is selected to be passed to the critic LLM, thus significantly reducing the number of calls that have to be made to the latter. In sum, our contributions are as follows:

•

We introduce VecCISC, a novel method to reduce the cost of the “think twice” paradigm by utilizing reasoning trace clustering to eliminate redundant and low quality samples from the pool to be analyzed;

•

We empirically demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach by performing extensive experiments on a range of models and datasets, with VecCISC achieving an average total token budget reduction of 47% across the entire inference pipeline, all while maintaining and even exceeding the performance of CISC;

•

We compare the performance of VecCISC with two different clustering approaches against random candidate selection of
K
samples, demonstrating the efficacy of our proposed clustering approach;

•

We will release our code publicly to support further research.

Methodology

This section details each step in the VecCSIC pipeline, including embedding generation from answer reasoning traces, clustering, and candidate answer selection. The overall procedure is summarized in Figure
1
, and a comparison of VecCISC’s methodology to that of Self-Consistency and CISC can be found in Figure
2
.

Figure 1:
Overview of the VecCISC pipeline. Embeddings of the sampled reasoning traces are clustered within each unique answer returned from the LLM. A representative trace is chosen from each cluster, and these representatives are then used to generate the confidence scores for the answers. Lastly, weighted majority vote is performed to choose the final answer.

2.1
Sampling

Given a sampling budget
n
, a large language model
LLM_{gen}
, and a question
q
, we first sample
n
reasoning trace-answer pairs from
LLM_{gen}
:

{(r_{1},a_{1}),…(r_{n},a_{n})}\sim LLM_{gen}(q)

(1)

The samples are then added to the answer candidate pool
P
:

P=\{(r_{1},a_{1}),…(r_{n},a_{n})\}

(2)

2.2
Embedding Generation

For each reasoning trace in the pool, a text embedding is produced via an embedding model
Emb
:

\displaystyle P=\{(r_{i},\mathrm{Emb}(r_{i}),a_{i})\mid(r_{i},a_{i})\in P\},

(3)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Emb}(r_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

Where
d
is the embedding dimension.

These embeddings enable the pipeline to capture semantic details in each reasoning trace that allow for clustering and outlier detection in subsequent steps.

2.3
Grouping By Answer

To preserve the set of distinct candidate answers, we group embeddings by answer before clustering. That is, for each unique answer
a
present in the sampled outputs:

\displaystyle G_{a}=\{(r_{i},\mathrm{Emb}(r_{i}))\mid

\displaystyle(r_{i},\mathrm{Emb}(r_{i}),a_{i})\in P,

(4)

\displaystyle a_{i}=a\}

Where
G_{a}
is the group of embedded reasoning traces that lead to answer
a
.

2.4
Clustering

Next, we apply a clustering algorithm
Cluster
to each reasoning trace group
G_{a}
:

\displaystyle\text{CL}_{a}=\text{Cluster}\Big(

\displaystyle\{\mathrm{Emb}(r_{i})\mid(r_{i},\mathrm{Emb}(r_{i}))\in G_{a}\},

(5)

\displaystyle\min(K,|G_{a}|)\Big)

Where
K
is a hyperparameter controlling the number of clusters to be formed. If
K
exceeds the number of samples in
G_{a}
, then
|G_{a}|
clusters are created instead. In our experiments, we analyzed KMeans and Hierarchical Agglomerative (HAC) clustering, as both methods allow for a distinct number of clusters to be specified. For both clustering algorithms, we performed a grid search on a holdout set from each
(dataset,model)
combination to choose
K
. The hyperparameters selected and further technical details are listed in Appendix A.

We choose KMeans and HAC over Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise (DBSCAN)
8
, another popular choice for clustering embeddings, as DBSCAN requires a distance threshold
D
to define clusters.
D
specifies the maximum distance between two vectors
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
for them to be considered part of the same neighborhood. Thus,
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
are clustered together if
dist(V_{1},V_{2})\leq D
. In high-dimensional language model embedding spaces, small changes to
D
can drastically alter cluster assignments, making threshold selection both difficult and prone to producing suboptimal clusters.

2.5
Cluster Representative Selection

Upon creation of the clusters, we then pick a representative from each cluster
C_{i}\in CL_{a}
. To do so, we first compute the centroid
u_{i}
of
C_{i}
:

U_{i}=\frac{1}{|C_{i}|}\sum_{e\in C_{i}}e

(6)

Finally, we choose the reasoning trace
r_{rep,i}\in C_{i}
that is closest to the centroid:

r_{\text{rep},i}=\arg\min_{r\in C_{i}}\,\text{cos\_sim}(\mathrm{Emb}(r),u_{i})

(7)

Here, we use the cosine similarity function as our definition of distance, as it emphasizes the angle
\theta
between vectors instead of the magnitude, thus making it more suitable for high-dimensional spaces requiring the preservation of semantic overlap
17
. Cosine similarity is defined in Equation
8
:

\text{cos\_sim}(\mathrm{emb_{1}},\mathrm{emb_{2}})=\frac{\mathrm{emb_{1}}\cdot\mathrm{emb_{2}}}{\|\mathrm{emb_{1}}\|\cdot\|\mathrm{emb_{2}}\|}

(8)

Selecting the reasoning trace closest to the centroid follows from the observation that the least semantically deviant trace would be the least likely to contain anomalous reasoning errors. At the end of the selection process, each answer now possesses a set of representative traces:

\displaystyle\text{Rep}_{a}

\displaystyle=\{r_{\text{rep},1},\dots,r_{\text{rep},k}\},

(9)

\displaystyle\text{where }k

\displaystyle=|\text{Clusters}_{a}|,\quad k\leq|G_{a}|

2.6
Confidence Scoring

For each unique answer
a
and its associated representative traces
r_{i}\in Rep_{a}
, we derive a new prompt query
q\textquoteright_{i}=[instruction]\circ q\circ r_{i}\circ a
, where
\circ
is the concatenation operator and
[instruction]
prompts the critic LLM to reflect on the original question, reasoning trace, and answer, and produce a confidence score in the range of 0 to 1:

c_{a,i}=LLM_{critic}(q\textquoteright_{i})

(10)

Following
32
, we normalize each confidence score using softmax:

\hat{c}_{a,i}=\frac{\exp\left(\frac{\text{c}_{a,i}}{T}\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\exp\left(\frac{\text{c}_{a,j}}{T}\right)}

(11)

Where
T
is a tunable hyperparameter of softmax that controls the smoothness of the normalization distribution. As with the cluster number
K
, we perform a grid search to find
T
. Please see Appendix A for more details and
T
values from our experiments.

We select the final answer by using a weighted majority vote:

A_{\text{final}}=\arg\max_{a}\sum_{(a_{j},\hat{c}_{a,j})\in P}1[a_{j}=a]\cdot\hat{c}_{a,j}

(12)

Figure 2:
Comparison of VecCISC to Self-Consistency (SC) and CISC. While CISC represents an improvement upon SC via incorporation of confidence scores and weighted majority voting, the inference overhead costs of CISC increase twofold over SC. By incorporating language embeddings, clustering, and representative trace selection, the confidence scorer
LLM_{critic}
can be run on a smaller set of higher quality reasoning traces while still preserving relative performance.

3
Experiments

3.1
Datasets

We evaluated the performance of VecCISC on five popular QA datasets:

•

AQuA_Rat

18
: A multiple choice QA dataset consisting of algebraic word problems. We sampled 1000 questions from the training set.

•

CommonsenseQA

31
: A dataset that requires commonsense reasoning capabilities to solve problems across a spectrum of different domains. We sampled 1000 problems from the validation set.

•

ARC-Challenging

7
: A subset of the most challenging questions in the original ARC dataset, including grade-school level multiple choice science questions. We sampled 1000 problems from the test set.

•

MMLU-Pro

34
: A more challenging set of questions from the original MMLU dataset, designed to assess reasoning faculty across disciplines including math, science, economics, and history. We sampled 1000 problems from the test set.

•

GPQA

28
: A multiple-choice dataset of very challenging questions crafted by experts in biology, physics, and chemistry. We used all 546 questions in the dataset.

3.2
Models

For our experiments, we selected five widely used open and closed source LLMs: GPT-4o mini
23
, Llama 3.1 8B
3
, Llama 3.3 70B Versatile
20
, Qwen 2.5 7B
27
, and Mistral 7B v0.1
4
. Model hyperparameters can be found in Appendix A.

3.3
Embeddings

For all experiments, we used OpenAI’s text-embedding-3-small model
24
for the embedding model
Emb
. This model was chosen to balance cost and general purpose applicability across a plethora of different reasoning tasks. As our goal is to design a lightweight, adaptive framework that will be performant regardless of reasoning trace composition, text-embedding-3-small was most suitable for both our task’s scope and budget limitations.

3.4
Baselines

We compare VecCISC to both standard Self-Consistency and CISC. In particular, we apply three distinct flavors of methodology:
VecCISC (random)
,
VecCISC + KMeans
and
VecCISC + HAC
. Instead of clustering,
VecCISC (random)
randomly samples
K
reasoning traces from each answer-grouped set,
G_{a}
, thus acting as an ablation for our method. All prompts were zero-shot. For the sake of experimental validity and to ensure a proper apples-to-apples comparison, we reimplemented both Self-Consistency and CISC, executing them on the same set of sampled questions used to evaluate the various VecCISC configurations. Following
32
, we selected verbal confidence as the confidence extraction method, whereby the critic LLM is asked to rate its confidence in the range of 0 to 1.

3.5
Metrics

3.5.1
Budget Call Reduction

We report the reduction in the number of calls made to
LLM_{critic}
with VecCISC, as opposed to CISC:

\text{Reduction}=100\times\frac{\#\text{ of critic calls in VecCISC}}{\#\text{ of calls in CISC}}

(13)

We computed the reduction for each
(dataset,model)
combination by running our pipeline on each question in the dataset with its corresponding model and recorded the average across all questions and experiments.

3.5.2
Token Usage Reduction

In addition, we report the reduction in token usage exhibited by VecCISC as opposed to CISC. Within VecCISC, we compare two cluster representative selection strategies: our proposed minimum centroid (min-centroid) approach and random cluster value selection (rand-trace), the latter acting as an ablation of the former.

3.5.3
Accuracy

Lastly, we report the accuracy attained by VecCISC and its variants, CISC, and Self-Consistency (SC) on each dataset and model. KMeans clustering and random trace sampling are non-deterministic, thus, we ran each method 10 times for every
(dataset,model)
combination and reported both the best and average.

4.1
Call Budget Reduction

VecCISC + KMeans
and
VecCISC + HAC
significantly reduces the number of calls to
LLM_{critic}
, while achieving comparable or improved performance over CISC. Across all models and datasets,
VecCISC + KMeans
averages a 34.68% reduction in the number of critic calls (Table
1
), while
VecCISC + HAC
averages a 30.2% reduction in the number of critic calls (Table

VecCISC + KMeans
averages a 17.34% total LLM call reduction and
VecCISC + HAC
averages a 15.1% total call reduction.

4.2
Token Usage Reduction

By reducing the number of critic calls made, VecCISC variants reduce the number of tokens utilized by
LLM_{critic}
. Our proposed cluster representative selection (min-centroid) records the lowest token usage rates as opposed to random representative selection (rand-trace) on 15 of the 25 model-dataset combinations (60%) on
VecCISC + KMeans
, as shown in Table
3
. On
VecCISC + HAC
, min-centroid records the lowest token usage rates on 17 of the 25 model-dataset combinations (68%), as shown in Table
4
. These results demonstrate that our min-centroid approach leads to the selection of higher quality, lower-token reasoning traces as opposed to purely random reasoning trace selection from each cluster. On average,
VecCISC + KMeans
reduces critic token usage by 36.2% ( Table
3
), while
VecCISC + HAC
records a 31.69% token usage reduction (Table
4
).
LLM_{critic}
calls are the most token-heavy of the entire pipeline, accounting for 77% of all token usage, thus, at scale, these reductions represent significant cost savings. Averaging token consumption across the entire pipeline, both variants of our proposed methodology reduce token usage by 47%

Related Work

5.1
Embeddings and Clustering for Scaling Inference Time Reasoning

Recent work has studied the application of LLM embeddings and clustering to improve model accuracy on reasoning and calibration benchmarks.
12
uses embeddings of reasoning traces to directly compute a sample score for use in weighted majority voting. Although conceptually straightforward, this approach is unrelated to the “think twice” paradigm, relies upon a fleet of domain-specific, fine-tuned embedding models for different benchmarks, and exhibits inconsistent performance improvements, often dropping pipeline accuracy below baseline Self-Consistency.
13
utilizes an LLM’s hidden state as context embeddings to cluster reasoning traces in tree-of-thought (ToT) pipelines and improve the accuracy of LLM uncertainty estimation. Although lightweight in practice, their proposed method is only applicable to white-box LLMs, limiting its reach and accessibility, while not addressing the need for candidate trace selection for weighted majority voting. Lastly,
1
clusters embeddings of LLM responses to detect instances of hallucinations.

5.2
Think-twice Approaches

Utilizing an LLM as a critic of its own outputs (or the outputs of other LLMs) has been the topic of much investigation. In particular, efforts have been made to use LLM-as-a-judge approaches to improve the accuracy of standard self-consistency.
16
samples a set of unique answers from an LLM and then prompts a second LLM to generate a justification for each candidate answer, before using a third LLM call to produce a final confidence score. Unlike CISC, this methodology does not make use of the sample reasoning traces, choosing instead to analyze only the final answers themselves, overlooking key contextual clues in the provided answers’ reasoning traces. Lastly,
35
applies Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) with an LLM-as-a-judge reward function to improve the accuracy of code-writing pipelines.

6
Conclusion

While the “think twice” paradigm can increase model performance on a variety of reasoning tasks, the additional inference overhead required can render it prohibitively expensive. In this work, we propose a novel framework for reducing the cost of model confidence estimation at inference time by clustering embeddings of candidate answer reasoning traces. Our experimental results demonstrate that choosing answer representatives from embedding clusters can significantly decrease inference costs during the critic component of the “think twice” approach without a commensurate decline in performance. Indeed, our results indicate that passing a smaller number of higher-quality reasoning traces to the critic can lead to small gains in accuracy. In this way, our methodology emerges as an effective, lightweight augmentation of pre-existing think-twice pipelines.

7
Limitations

Our study emphasizes the adaptability of our framework. To this end, we adopt a general text embedding model for our experiments. Pipelines for more specialized problems might benefit from applying custom embedding models, fine-tuned to capture key semantic representations. As our work demonstrates performance gains and budget improvements across a wide range of different tasks, we defer the application of fine-tuned embedding models for domain-specific problems to future research. In addition, the choice of hyperparameters
K
and
T
are important in ensuring that the pipeline’s accuracy on the datasets does not fall below that of standard Self-Consistency. While our hyperparameter search process is fundamentally data-driven, future research should investigate adaptive approaches for choosing
K
and
T
in the absence of analogous data
a priori
.
