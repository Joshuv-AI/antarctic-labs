Executive Summary
The primary objective of this work is to develop practical and robust technologies for detecting
anomalies associated with solar inverters and other inverter-based resources (IBRs). As IBRs
become more prevalent in modern power systems, their interactions with the grid pose critical
challenges, particularly regarding grid stability during the transition from traditional radial-fed
power systems to distributed generation.
While phasor measurement units (PMUs) are used in some distribution systems, they lack the
granularity to capture transients and phasor metrics of individual IBRs. To address this
limitation, the work focused on bridging this visibility gap by designing tools capable of precise
monitoring and management of IBR-induced grid dynamics. The solution aimed to strike a
balance between technical capability and cost-effectiveness, aligning with the needs of the power
community.
The ideal approach involves continuous analysis of voltage waveforms from each IBR by a
central server to detect anomalies in magnitude, phase, frequency, and harmonics. However,
bandwidth and hardware cost constraints necessitated the development of a more practical
solution. This included event-detection methods to identify anomalies, compression and data-
transmission techniques to deliver high-resolution event data, and continuous basic waveform
data outside event windows. Prototype hardware was designed using modern, cost-efficient
components, and system costs, including data transmission and storage, were carefully estimated
to ensure feasibility.
Collaboration with industrial partners Convault Energy, Inc., and DigiCollect played a crucial
role in refining the system’s design and identifying commercialization pathways. Input from
potential customers shaped the system's features, ensuring alignment with industry needs.
Rigorous field tests were conducted with grid-edge devices deployed at multiple distributed
energy resource (DER) sites. These tests validated the technology's performance, successfully
detecting and capturing transient events. The results demonstrate the system's reliability,
highlighting its potential to significantly enhance grid monitoring and stability in the evolving
energy landscape.

Event Detection
Implementing this system requires the transmission of detailed voltage waveforms to a central
aggregation server, where advanced analyses classify and interpret IBR behavior and their
interactions with other grid components. However, due to finite communication bandwidth and
storage constraints, a practical approach is necessary. In such systems, anomaly detection tasks
are delegated to grid-edge sensors. These sensors identify transient anomalies locally and
transmit fully detailed waveforms of these events to the server. During uneventful periods, which
dominate the system's operational time, the sensors transmit lower-resolution or compressed data
to conserve resources.
The following sections outline the theoretical basis and testing of transient detection methods,
followed by the development and evaluation of data compression techniques. This two-pronged
approach ensures that the system remains efficient while providing high-resolution data for
critical events and sufficient contextual data for continuous monitoring.

Data compression
The operational stability of future smart grids will likely depend on monitoring different
switching actions behavior with high time resolution to correctly assess the power grid status.
While some power grid measurement applications rely on data sampled at frequencies in the
kilohertz range, the level of aggregation and the reporting or frame rate differ. Phasor
Measurement Units (PMUs) usually report 30 to 60 frames per second (phasor, frequency, etc.).
However, short transients are not well reported by PMUs. To develop the event location and
event recurrence analyses, raw measurements or so-called Point-on-Wave (PoW) data are critical
for the reconstruction of the event 0.
During an event, high raw-data sampling rates from 2 kHz to 100 kHz are preferred for detailed
analysis. Transmission of the raw data at these rates presents a real challenge to the data transfer
efficiency and the required storage space. Importantly, the data loss ratio would be more likely to
increase with the data rate, causing the data quality to be reduced. To remedy these challenges,
data compression may be required for the high-density sampling data in the wide-area
monitoring system.
In recent decades, many different compression methods have been designed, such as the Lempel
Ziv-Welch, Gzip, Bzip2, XZ, and LZ4 0. Some of them are suitable for images and large
volumes of data. Unfortunately, with limited length event data, these methods might even
increase the length of the data.
In this report, real-time lossless compression methods, as well as the experiments, are explored
using the Periodic Recursive Compression (MPRC) algorithm and Lempel-Ziv-Markov chain-
Algorithm (LZMA).
Non-event data compression
Methodology
The motivation of MPRC is to compress the PoW data by reducing the magnitude sampled from
the waveform measurements. that the data resolution depends on the resolution of the analog to
digital converter (ADC) where 16-bit analog data is utilized as an example here since our test
unit is with 16-bit ADC. To save space, the first step is to convert the data into the point with a
low magnitude. For example, if 16-bit data is compressed into 8-bit data, then a compression
ratio of 2 can be achieved.

Practical development of the new monitor
Cost model
This cost model analyzes the development of a point-on-wave (PoW) sensor for monitoring
photovoltaic solar inverters. Its purpose is to determine if the technology being developed will be
cost competitive and to understand how specific system design choices impact system cost. The
model considers five cost driver categories: hardware, communication, data storage, operations,
and maintenance. It also compares the cost and functionality of established technologies in the
same market. While cost forecasting at the pre-prototype stage is imprecise, the model provides a
holistic analysis of the relative importance of different cost factors, which has significant
implications for product development. The report highlights that while hardware is important,
storage scheme details are equally influential cost factors. Furthermore, the model suggests that
while data compression ratios affect costs, their impact is minimal compared to overall system
costs. The key evaluation criteria for the sensor's development are achieving a per-unit cost of
$1000 or less for 10,000 to 20,000 units and ensuring its price is no more than 60% of competing
technologies' costs. Despite the uncertainties of cost forecasting in the pre-prototype phase, the
model indicates that both criteria are met, encompassing the total costs of production,
installation, maintenance, communication and storage over a three-year period, assuming that an
Internet connection is already available at the installation sites and is not additionally billed.
Overall, this cost model provides valuable insights into the cost considerations for the PoW
sensor's development, guiding decision-making and highlighting the key factors that should be
addressed throughout the product development process.
System Description and Cost Model Outline
The point-on-wave grid analyzer (PGA) is a new low-cost and hardware-friendly instantaneous
waveform measurement technology that will enable widespread monitoring of electric
distributed energy resources (DERs), including solar photovoltaic (PV) inverters. In brief, the
PGA detects, compresses, and transfers point-on-wave (PoW) measurements of power grid
waveforms during power system transients. The system hardware is derived from universal grid
analyzer (UGA) technology previously developed by the CURENT group at the University of
Tennessee [1], addressing the unmet need for cost-effective PoW measurement data
corresponding to grid asset waveform anomalies.
This report describes the expected costs of the PGA product for the purpose of forming a
competitive business model. Established competing technologies are primarily Digital Fault
Recorders (DFRs) and Phasor Measurement Units (PMUs). The North American Electric
Reliability Corporation (NERC) has identified limited capability of capturing grid waveforms
near solar PV inverters during power grid events, like faults [2-5]. This shortcoming is attributed
to the cost-limited deployment of DFRs, usually only to the Point of Connection (PoC) of a DER
site, which is ineffective for sensing waveform events at resources such as PV inverters [6].
Regardless of cost, PMU devices are unable to provide the PoW measurements required to
reliably monitor and record certain fast transients due to their methods of signal filtering and
processing [6]. The PGA fills the gap between the need for measuring waveforms of PV inverters
and the lack of suitable low-cost waveform-recorder instruments. While established technologies
are expected to remain far more costly than the PGA under development, the cost-effective PoW-
monitoring technology gap targeted by the PGA has been clearly identified by industry and

 20

government organizations [6], and it is foreseeable that additional low-cost technologies more
comparable to the PGA will be emerging on the market in coming years. The probable
emergence of comparable technologies underscores the need to maximize the cost-effectiveness
and performance of the PGA, even if direct competition with established technologies seems
unlikely.
The PGA monitors a grid asset’s voltage waveform continuously and detects the occurrence of
anomalous events (e.g., magnitude change, phase angle change, spike, high-frequency
oscillations) using a proprietary real-time transient-detection trigger. When an event is detected,
the event PoW waveform along with a period of PoW immediately preceding and succeeding the
event is recorded in the memory of the monitor. Then, data compression is used to reduce the
size of the PoW data. Lastly, the compressed data is transferred to a remote data server via an
internet connection, where the data may be analyzed by a customer. The waveform data is
timestamped using GPS timing.
In addition to transferring PoW event data to the remote server, the system may optionally
provide continuous streaming of non-event PoW data to a server customer, compressed using a
higher compression ratio (CR) algorithm than that used for the event data compression. This
non-event data may then be used for frequency tracking, harmonic analysis, or other functions
more commonly provided by PMUs.
The targeted initial application for the monitor is single-phase, nominally 60 Hz voltage at a PV
inverter at 110 V to 240 V rms. Costs are estimated herein assuming a low-volume
manufacturing scale in the range of 10 to 20 thousand units.
Hardware Costs
The costs associated with producing the PGA encompass various aspects of hardware
production. This includes expenses related to electronic components, manufacturing and
assembly of printed circuit boards (PCBs), enclosure assembly (including cable harnesses and
connectors), system testing, packaging, and shipping. Each of these hardware cost components is
influenced by specific details of the PGA, such as component selection, tradeoffs between PCB
complexity and performance specifications, and the capabilities of the manufacturer involved.
For instance, integrating PGA components at a lower density would necessitate a larger circuit
board area, which raises the cost of both the PCB and the enclosure. On the other hand,
minimizing the PCB's footprint by increasing the number of layers and using smaller
components may result in higher manufacturing costs due to more complex PCB and assembly
processes. Moreover, the assembly and testing of the PGA system are sensitive to particular
manufacturing processes. Design choices, such as the placement positions of connections, can
impact the manufacturability and cost of the system, including non-recurring engineering (NRE)
costs. These design-manufacturing relationships are typically specific to a given manufacturer's
capabilities.
Considering these factors, optimizing manufacturing costs requires comprehensive knowledge of
detailed design, manufacturing processes, and testing procedures. Design-for-manufacturing
(DFM) and design-for-test (DFT) principles play a vital role in tailoring the design to achieve
lower per unit costs at scale. However, DFM and DFT analysis are generally performed after the
prototype design phase and the selection of a manufacturer.

Communication
The monthly cost of connecting PGA data to the Internet is expected to cost $20-$60 per month,
though it is foreseeable that most deployment scenarios would be at locations (e.g., businesses or
residences) already paying for broadband Internet, so adding PGA communication to this
network would actually incur no added cost. The communication and storage requirements
depend strongly on whether the PGA is implemented (1) as a continuous streaming monitor or
(2) as an event-based streaming monitor. In addition to these two options, the level of

of additional loss of some power spectral density in the frequency domain, as well as some
additional higher harmonic distortion. Certain customers wishing to perform more detailed,
higher-fidelity analysis may desire to minimize compression-related distortion at the cost of

higher communication and storage requirements. Here, the cost for non-event CR of 8 and 15 are

Deployment, Operations, and Maintenance
Aside from the hardware costs already discussed, deployment costs are anticipated to be
dominated by labor for a qualified technician to install the unit on an inverter. While installation
on a single unit is anticipated to be quick (well under 1 hour), the cost will vary widely
depending on the travel time of the technician, and the number of units installed at a location.
Assuming total technician labor costs of $100/hr. Including compensation and insurance and an
average labor time of 2 hr./unit including travel time, deployment costs are estimated at $200
/unit on average. The PGA is anticipated to require minimal operations and maintenance costs.
Aside from the incidental involvement of technicians to service units which may experience
premature failure, operational costs are expected to be dominated by recurring data
communication and storage costs discussed previously. Maintenance costs are anticipated to
entail the eventual replacement of the unit, at approximately the same initial $180 unit cost, after
several years of deployment, plus the labor of a qualified technician to perform the PGA
replacement. Here, a 3-4 year unit lifespan is assumed as a conservative number based on
previously developed similar hardware [1], and the cost of replacing the unit is not included in
the 3-year lifecycle cost estimate. Alternatively, new inverter installations could include the PGA
equipment, either separate or incorporated within the system controller. In that case, separate
installation costs would be avoided.
Alternative Technology Comparison (Competitors)
The closest competing established technologies for inverter monitoring include phasor
measurement units (PMUs) and digital fault recorders (DFRs). While PMUs are in general
unable to capture many types of transient events [6], they are widely established for disturbance
monitoring and should be considered. DFR technologies on the other hand are capable of
measuring PoW event data at similar fidelity as the PGA. Based on industry survey responses to
a survey conducted by the NERC [10], the cost of DFR devices averages $50k to $60k, with a
low near $36k. The same report notes the total cost is much higher, given the cost of installation
is nearly the same as device cost, and maintenance costs average $500 to $2000 each
maintenance cycle, bringing the total cost of ownership per device to $86k at the low end, to
over $100k for average deployed device costs. The cost of PMU devices is reported to be lower
than DFR devices, with overall costs on average in the $50k-$60k range, with the lowest costs
reported near $40k [10, 11]. While PMU devices are unable to perform the PoW event tracking
function, they do provide similar use data as the PGA’s compressed non-event data could
provide. However, established commercial applications for DFR-based disturbance recording
hardware typically monitor far more nodes than a single voltage or current channel with each
DFR unit, which significantly increases the pricing compared to what is as applicable to PV
inverter monitoring (one to two analog input channels).
To better compare the cost of the PGA under development to commercial DFR solutions, quotes
for DFR units were obtained from AMTEK Power Instruments and from ELSPEC North
America, Inc. A DR300 DFR unit manufactured by AMTEK was quoted at $14,951, and an
ELSPEC G5 DFR was quoted at $11,469. The AMTEK unit is capable of recording transients at
up to a 15 kHz sampling rate while the ELSPEC unit is capable of sampling at up to 61 kHz.
Both systems were quoted with the most economical GPS timing option available, and with the
least costly system configurations. While both systems provide the hardware required for
connecting the devices to the Internet, and the ELSPEC tool offers sophisticated interfaces for
data inspection, neither product includes communication or long-term (e.g. 90-day) capable

 26

storage options in their cost estimates. These features would add some cost on top of the quoted
hardware pricing. Additionally, a key feature of the PGA under development is the robust
transient detection algorithm which is not an option for either of the quoted systems. While it
may be feasible to implement such an algorithm in the hardware for these commercial devices,
doing so would require significant engineering time, and would likely only be viable option for
customers if these manufacturers integrated these algorithms in their products.
In summary, while established DFR products are available with significantly higher sampling
rate than the PGA system, these tools are not configured for continuous PoW monitoring or with
transient triggers comparable to the proprietary event detector incorporated in the PGA and the
minimum price for these commercial devices is 5-30 times the forecast cost of the PGA.

Two important implications of the cost model analysis performed here must be kept in mind.
Firstly, when considering a new internet connection solely for monitoring a single device, the
projected unit cost exceeds the $1,000 threshold. This implies that the cost of establishing an

is important to note that situations requiring a new internet connection to support a single PGA

connection for a single device is not a typical scenario and is likely to be the rare exception
rather than the norm.
Secondly, for cloud-based data storage, the frequency of updates for non-event data has a
substantial impact on the overall cost. Higher frequency updates of non-event data significantly
contribute to the expenses. Therefore, when evaluating the cost-effectiveness of higher frequency
data updates, the value and necessity of such frequent updates should be carefully assessed for

The cost comparison between the PGA and the established DFR technology, which is the closest

forecast to be well below 60% of the cost of competing established technologies, regardless of
requirements for Internet connectivity.

Based on prior, comparable circuits, the team can estimate required area for a four-layer board at
approximately 5”x 7”. Based solely on component area, a comparable single layer board would
need to be at least twice this surface area. However, because of the need for routing power and
signal lines throughout the surface of a single layer board, the surface area requires would be
more than twice as much. For an order of 1000 boards fabricated from PCBCart.com, a 4-layer
design with a 5” x 7” area would cost approximately $6.40 per board, while single later design
would cost approximately $8 per board assuming twice the area is needed, which are typical
estimates for PCB production. Based solely on board-fabrication costs, considering costs such as
module assembly and enclosure cost are anticipated to be higher per unit for single layer designs,
a multiple layer board is more desirable from a cost perspective. From a performance perspective
it is also more desirable to provide internal power and ground layers to aid with EMI protection.
Similarly, the multiple layer design allows or reduced parasitic capacitance, resistance, and
inductance be reducing the required lengths of interconnect on the circuit board. Therefore, the
team has decided to pursue a multiple layer prototype design with four layers.

also with GPS timing accuracy, was deployed to provide a reference for comparison. This setup
enabled a detailed assessment of the monitors' transient detection, offering insights into their
performance and alignment with the high-resolution reference device.

Conclusion
This work has demonstrated a practical approach to providing continuous visibility into DER
systems. Methods were developed to reliably detect transient events under varying noise levels
and frequency fluctuations. Compression techniques were also designed and tested, effectively
reducing the communication burden of high-rate PoW data while preserving the integrity of
transient event recordings.
Legacy devices were fully redesigned with modern, cost-conscious components, addressing
aspects such as component selection, board design, installation, data transmission, storage,
analysis, and maintenance. Although the first prototype failed to operate properly as a cohesive
system, its individual components were successfully validated. Subsequent field tests with
reprogrammed legacy devices achieved success, aligning with the objectives described in this
report. Data streams from three remote sites to a central server captured events that were
independently verified by external monitors.
The technology has attracted interest from several potential customers, including utility-scale PV
owners, residential solar providers, and existing distribution and transmission utilities. As DER
penetration in the power grid continues to grow, the demand for distributed sensors to maintain
grid stability will increase. The advancements made during this work establish a solid foundation
for deploying such sensors effectively in the future.
