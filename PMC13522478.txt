Control of associative learning by the mediodorsal thalamus-orbitofrontal cortex circuit
Authors: Ziwei Le, Can Huang, Wen Zhang, Dechen Liu, Jingyu Ren, Haishan Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMediodorsal thalamus (MD) projections to the prefrontal cortex are crucial for adaptive cognitive functions. Using a Go/No-Go olfactory discrimination task in male mice, we investigate how MD input to the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) regulates associative learning and modulates cue-value and outcome representations in OFC neurons. We show that inactivating MD projections to the OFC impairs learning but does not affect performance in the well-trained phase. During learning, cue value selectivity increased and outcome selectivity was dynamically reshaped in both MD and OFC, and inactivating MD projections disrupted these learning-associated changes in OFC neurons. Furthermore, MD projections targeted OFC parvalbumin (PV) interneurons, whose inactivation attenuated the learning-related enhancement of cue value selectivity and altered outcome selectivity dynamics in OFC broad-spiking cells. Thus, MD inputs engage PV interneuron-mediated feedforward inhibition to dynamically shape cue-value and outcome selectivity in the OFC, supporting associative learning.Subject terms: Cognitive neuroscience, Neural circuits

Introduction

The mediodorsal thalamus (MD), a higher-order thalamic nucleus, plays a critical role in cognitive functions, including learning and decision-making1–6. The MD forms reciprocal connections with the prefrontal cortex (PFC)1, a key brain region essential for cognition and adaptive behaviors7. In addition to its connections with the PFC, the MD receives inputs from the piriform cortex, reticular thalamic nucleus, amygdala, basal ganglia, brain stem, and deep cerebellar nuclei8,9. Damage to the MD leads to deficits in various cognitive and executive functions, including behavioral flexibility, reward evaluation, attention, learning, and memory1,5. For example, MD lesions in monkeys impaired postoperative learning of scene memory, object-reward association memory, and object-in-place scene discrimination tasks10–13. Similarly, in rodents, MD damage affected olfactory discrimination learning14,15 and two-choice sensory discrimination16, while MD inhibition impaired the acquisition of a delayed non-matched-to-sample task17. Additionally, MD lesions or inactivation disrupted working memory18 and impaired performance in tasks requiring adaptive decisions, such as reinforcer devaluation, reversal learning, contingency degradation, and attentional set-shifting16,17,19–23. The functional deficits resulting from MD damage resemble those observed with PFC dysfunction, highlighting the close interaction between these two regions in supporting cognitive function1–3,5.

A growing body of evidence demonstrates the importance of MD-PFC interactions in higher-order cognitive functions. For instance, MD inhibition disrupted the working memory-induced modulation of beta-range synchrony between the MD and medial PFC (mPFC)17. MD inputs stabilized task-related delay activity in the mPFC, which was required for spatial working memory24. In an attentional control task, the MD enhanced functional connectivity between dorsomedial PFC neurons to maintain rule representations crucial for task performance25. Moreover, in tree shrews performing a hierarchical decision task with rule reversals, the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC)-MD-mPFC pathway mediated task switching and rule reconfiguration in the mPFC26. In rats performing a goal-directed instrumental task, inhibition of MD neurons projecting to dorsomedial PFC impaired performance when the current knowledge of causal relationships between actions and outcomes must be considered27. These studies indicate that MD-PFC interactions play a significant role in shaping mental representations by maintaining or updating task-relevant information4,28.

The MD is composed of medial, central, and lateral subdivisions5,9, each of which projects to distinct regions of the PFC29–31. In rodents, MD neurons projecting to the mPFC and OFC are largely segregated31. The OFC plays a central role in reward processing, stimulus-reward association, and decision-making32–34. Previous studies have shown that OFC lesions or inactivation impair stimulus-reward association learning and adaptive decision-making, particularly in tasks that require establishing or updating model-based representations or cognitive maps35–45. Furthermore, neural encoding in the OFC undergoes dynamic changes during associative learning39,40,46–48. Although the MD-OFC circuit has been implicated in such learning processes3,4,49,50, how MD projections shape OFC neural representations to support learning remains poorly understood.

MD projections to the PFC not only provide feedforward excitation but also recruit local inhibitory interneurons26,51–53. In the MD-mPFC circuit, such feedforward inhibition plays an important role in regulating social interaction18 and decision-making under uncertainty52. In the OFC, inhibitory neurons have been shown to be essential for learning54–56, behavioral switching57, and cognitive control of instrumental choice58. Notably, slice recordings have demonstrated that MD projections strongly activate PV interneurons in the OFC53,58, suggesting that this feedforward inhibition may regulate cue-reward associative signaling, a hypothesis that remains to be tested.

In this study, we investigated the functional role of the MD-OFC circuit in learning using a Go/No-Go olfactory discrimination task in mice. We found that inactivating MD-to-OFC projections impaired olfactory discrimination learning. During learning, neuronal activity in both the MD and OFC showed increased cue value selectivity, along with dynamic changes in outcome selectivity. Inactivation of the MD projections disrupted these learning-dependent adaptations in the OFC. Furthermore, selective inhibition of MD-input-defined PV interneurons also impaired these neural adaptations. These findings demonstrate that MD input contributes to learning-dependent modulation of cue-value and outcome encoding in the OFC.

Results

The MD projections to OFC contribute to associative learning

Head-fixed mice first underwent a conditioning phase to associate an odor cue with a water reward (Supplementary Fig. 1). Each trial consisted of a 1-s odor cue, followed by a 0.5-s delay period and a 1.5-s response window, during which licking was rewarded with water. Trials were separated by an inter-trial interval of 3–8 s. This conditioning phase allowed mice to form an odor-reward association and learn the temporal contingency between the odor cue and reward delivery. After at least 3 conditioning sessions, mice progressed to Go/No-Go olfactory discrimination training using a novel pair of odor cues, which was completed within a single session (10–12 blocks, 20 trials per block; Fig. 1a−c). In Go trials, licking during the response window was rewarded with water (Hit). In No-Go trials, licking and no lick during the response window were considered as false alarm (FA) and correct rejection (CR), respectively.

To investigate the role of MD projections to the OFC, we bilaterally injected AAV-CaMKIIα-eOPN3.0 (or AAV-CaMKIIα-eGFP for control mice) into the MD and implanted optic fibers in the OFC (Fig. 1d). In vivo recordings in awake mice confirmed that optogenetic activation of eOPN suppressed the spontaneous firing of MD neurons (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b), which recovered quickly after laser offset. Optogenetic inhibition of MD axons in the OFC also produced significant firing rate modulation in a large fraction of OFC neurons (Supplementary Fig. 2c−e), confirming effective projection-specific manipulation.

In both eGFP and eOPN mice, we applied laser stimulation (532 nm, 20 mW) from 1 s before to 5 s after odor onset in every trial throughout discrimination training. Compared to control mice, eOPN mice exhibited significantly slower increases in d′ (F(1, 19) = 16.10, p = 7.45 × 10−4) and a slower decrease in the FA rate (F(1,19) = 14.77, p = 0.0011, two-way ANOVA with mixed design, eGFP mice: n = 11, eOPN mice: n = 10, Fig. 1e−g) during training. Analysis of lick latency revealed no significant difference between eOPN and eGFP mice in Go or No-Go trials (Supplementary Fig. 3a), indicating that the slower learning in eOPN mice was not attributable to altered impulsivity. We next tested the effect of laser stimulation restricted to the cue-delay period in a separate session using a new odor pair. The eOPN mice again learned significantly slower than control mice (for d′: F(1, 19) = 16.78, p = 6.14 × 10−4; for FA rate: F(1, 19) = 10.48, p = 0.004; two-way ANOVA with mixed design, eGFP mice: n = 11, eOPN mice: n = 10, Supplementary Fig. 4a, b, d), while lick latency remained similar to controls (Supplementary Fig. 4e, f). Similarly, laser stimulation during the response window was tested in a separate session with another odor pair, where eOPN mice continued to show slower learning (for d′: F(1, 18) = 18.23, p = 4.61 × 10−4; for FA rate: F(1, 18) = 15.03, p = 0.001; two-way ANOVA with mixed design, eGFP mice: n = 10, eOPN mice: n = 10, Supplementary Fig. 4g, h, j), with lick latency unaffected (Supplementary Fig. 4k, l). To exclude the possibility that the observed effects are due to the influence of prior training, we trained a new group of naive mice (eGFP vs eOPN). Optogenetic inhibition of MD axons in the OFC during the cue-delay period again slowed learning (for d′: F(1, 20) = 5.37, p = 0.031; for FA rate: Finteraction (11, 220) = 2.67, pinteraction = 0.003; two-way ANOVA with mixed design, eGFP mice: n = 11, eOPN mice: n = 11, with laser power reduced to 10 mW to minimize potential off-target effects, Supplementary Fig. 4m, n, p) without altering lick latency (Supplementary Fig. 4q, r). When this new group of mice was subsequently trained with a new odor pair without light stimulation, learning did not differ between eGFP and eOPN mice (F(1, 20) = 0.77, p = 0.39, two-way ANOVA with mixed design, eGFP mice: n = 11, eOPN mice: n = 11, Supplementary Fig. 4s), indicating that the prior effect of inactivating MD projections does not carry over to affect learning of a new odor pair. These results suggest that the activity of MD projections to the OFC during both the cue-delay period and response window is crucial for associative learning.

To test whether inhibition of MD projections to the OFC during early learning exerts persistent effects on subsequent behavioral performance in the absence of continued manipulation, a laser-on/laser-off paradigm59 was used in an independent group of mice. Laser stimulation (532 nm, 10 mW; from 1 s before to 5 s after odor onset) was applied throughout the first 5 blocks of discrimination training (20 trials/block), and performance was assessed in subsequent blocks without laser stimulation. We found that eOPN mice exhibited significantly lower d′ and higher FA rate than eGFP mice by the end of the laser-on phase (5th block: p = 0.019 for d′, p = 0.037 for FA rate, Wilcoxon rank-sum test; eGFP mice: n = 10, eOPN mice: n = 10, Fig. 1h), indicating impaired acquisition during early learning. This impairment persisted after laser offset, with eOPN mice showing reduced performance across the subsequent 5 blocks (d′: F(1, 18) = 12.54, p = 0.002; FA rate: F(1, 18) = 7.2, p = 0.015; eGFP mice: n = 10, eOPN mice: n = 10, two-way ANOVA with mixed design, Fig. 1h). These results indicate that the activity of MD-to-OFC projections during early learning is not only necessary for immediate task performance but also exerts a persistent influence on subsequent learning, providing further causal evidence for its role in associative learning.

To test the causal role of the OFC in this task, we bilaterally injected AAV2/5-CaMKIIα-eOPN3.0-mScarlet-WPRE (or AAV2/5-CaMKIIα-eGFP-WPRE-pA as control) into the OFC and applied laser stimulation (6 mW) from 1 s before to 5 s after odor onset during each trial of discrimination training. OFC inactivation significantly impaired learning (for d′: F(1, 14) = 5.83, p = 0.03; for FA rate: Finteraction (11, 154) = 6.48, pinteraction = 8.32 × 10−9; two-way ANOVA with mixed design, eGFP mice: n = 8, eOPN mice: n = 8, Supplementary Fig. 5a), without affecting lick latency in Go or No-Go trials (Supplementary Fig. 5b). OFC inhibition also reduced Hit rate in early blocks of Go trial (Supplementary Fig. 5a), an effect that was occasionally observed following inactivation of MD-to-OFC projections (Supplementary Fig. 4c, i, o). Both OFC inhibition (Supplementary Fig. 5a) and inactivation of MD projections to OFC (Fig. 1g, and Supplementary Fig. 4d, j, p, including two independent cohorts) increased FA rate during later blocks of No-Go trials. Together, these results indicate that while OFC inactivation may disrupt both local processing and downstream outputs, the convergent effect of both manipulations suggests that the MD-to-OFC pathway contributes, at least in part, to No-Go trial learning via its influence on OFC neuronal activity.

We also examined the role of MD activity by bilaterally injecting AAV-CaMKIIα-eNpHR into the MD and implanting optic fibers above the injection sites (Fig. 1i). Laser stimulation (20 mW) was applied from 1 s before to 5 s after odor onset during each trial. Inactivation of the entire MD impaired both the Hit rate and FA rate (Supplementary Fig. 3e), leading to markedly slower improvement of d′ compared to control mice (F(1,16) = 9.81, p = 0.0064, two-way ANOVA with mixed design, n = 8 and 10 for eGFP and eNpHR mice, Fig. 1i). MD inactivation did not affect lick latency (Supplementary Fig. 3f) but reduced the lick rate within 0–3 s relative to odor onset during Go trials (Supplementary Fig. 3g, h).

We further targeted the MD neurons projecting to the OFC (MDOFC neurons) by bilaterally injecting AAV-Retro-Cre into the OFC, AAV-DIO-eNpHR (or AAV-DIO-eGFP for control mice) into the MD, and implanting optic fibers above the injection sites in the MD (Fig. 1j). In pilot experiments, we observed that 20 mW laser stimulation substantially reduced the lick rate in both control and eNpHR mice. As a result, we used a lower laser power (3 mW) for this experiment, with stimulation applied from −1 to 5 s relative to odor onset. Inactivating MDOFC neurons significantly decreased the Hit rate (F(1, 26) = 8.12, p = 0.0085, Supplementary Fig. 3i) and impaired the improvement of d′ (F(11, 286) = 3.40, pinteraction = 1.87 × 10−4, two-way ANOVA with mixed design, n = 14 and 14 for eGFP and eNpHR mice, Fig. 1j) compared to the control mice, without affecting the lick latency (Supplementary Fig. 3j). Lick rate analysis revealed a significant reduction during Go trials throughout learning (Supplementary Fig. 3k, l), suggesting that inactivating either the whole MD or MDOFC neurons diminished licking motivation60. The results suggest that MD neurons influence behavior through projections to multiple target regions beyond the OFC.

Is the activity of the MD-OFC circuit necessary for performance in well-trained mice? To address this, we tested the role of MD-to-OFC projections using the subset of mice from Fig. 1g. Both eGFP and eOPN mice were trained to discriminate the odor pair until their performance reached d′ ≥3, indicating that they had become well-trained at the task. When laser stimulation (20 mW) was applied during the window of −1 to 5 s relative to odor onset, no significant differences were observed in the change of Hit rate, FA rate, or d′ between eOPN and eGFP mice (p > 0.05, Wilcoxon rank-sum test, Fig. 2a−c). Lick latency and lick rate were also unaffected in both groups (Supplementary Fig. 3c, d). Similarly, in well-trained mice, inactivation of either MDOFC neurons (Fig. 2d−f, Supplementary Fig. 3m, n) or OFC neurons (Supplementary Fig. 5c−g) had no significant impact on behavioral performance, lick latency, or lick rate (p > 0.05, Wilcoxon rank-sum test). These findings suggest that while the MD-OFC circuit is critical during the learning phase, it does not play a significant role in performance once the mice are well-trained.

Experience-dependent changes in MD and OFC neuronal responses

To investigate the dynamics of neural coding in MD and OFC neurons, we used neuropixels probes to simultaneously record activity from both regions while the mice performed the learning task within a single session (Fig. 3a). As shown by the example neurons in Supplementary Fig. 6, the peri-stimulus time histograms (PSTHs) averaged across all trials showed distinct response patterns in Go and No-Go trials. To analyze activity related to odor cues, licking, and outcomes, we applied a general linear model (GLM) to fit the response of each neuron (Fig. 3b). To track learning-related changes in cue and outcome selectivity, we adopted the approach described in a previous study38: for each task variable, the regressor spanned multiple time bins relative to its corresponding event, with a single coefficient shared across bins, rather than a time-varying set of coefficients. All regressors were binary except for the lick rate, which was continuous. Our GLM model included eight task variables (Fig. 3b): the two odor cues, the response window onset cue, lick rate, and four outcomes (Hit, CR, FA, and Miss). For each neuron, the PSTH from each session was divided into multiple blocks, and the GLM was fitted to each block of PSTH data. To quantify the contribution of cue, outcome, and lick-related variables to the variance of neuronal activity, we computed the coefficient of partial determination (CPD)61. In both MD and OFC neurons, the CPD for odor cues was significantly higher than that for outcomes, which was, in turn, higher than that for lick rate (for MD neurons: cue versus outcome, p = 1.67 × 10−7; cue versus lick, p = 3.64 × 10−33; outcome versus lick, p = 1.52 × 10−26. For OFC neurons: cue versus outcome, p = 3.11 × 10−23, cue versus lick, p = 1.96 × 10−48, outcome versus lick, p = 2.4 × 10−26. Two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test; MD: n = 471 neurons; OFC: n = 415 neurons; from 5 mice; Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 7a, e). Control analyses using circularly shuffled GLM regressors confirmed that the CPDs for odor cues and outcomes were significantly higher than those obtained from shuffled data in both MD and OFC neurons (p < 0.001, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, Supplementary Fig. 7b, f). These results indicate that in both MD and OFC neurons, encoding of odor cues was the strongest, whereas encoding of lick rate was the weakest.

For each neuron, we examined how the GLM coefficients for odor cues, outcomes, and lick rate changed with learning across trial blocks (Fig. 3d−f, h−j, 50 trials/block). The preferred odor cue was defined as the one with the larger GLM coefficient averaged across blocks where d′ >1.5. To quantify cue value selectivity in each block, we computed a cue selectivity index (SI) as the difference between βpref and βnonpref, normalized by the average of the absolute values of these coefficients across all blocks, where βpref and βnonpref represent the GLM coefficients for the preferred and nonpreferred odors, respectively. We then performed a linear regression of cue SI on d′ across blocks. As shown by the example OFC and MD neurons in Fig. 3d, h, both exhibited a positive slope of cue SI, which aligns with the observation that PSTHs for the two odor cues within the cue-delay period became more distinct as behavioral performance improved (Fig. 3g, k). Similarly, to quantify outcome selectivity in each block, we computed an outcome SI as the difference between βFA and βHit, normalized by the average of the absolute values of these coefficients across all blocks, with βFA and βHit representing the GLM coefficients for FA and Hit, respectively. A positive outcome SI indicates a stronger outcome response in FA trials compared to Hit trials, whereas a negative outcome SI indicates a stronger response in Hit trials compared to FA trials. The example OFC and MD neurons in Fig. 3e, i both showed changes in outcome SI over blocks.

At the population level, the regression slope of cue SI on d′ was significantly greater than zero for both MD and OFC neurons (MD: p = 2.38 × 10−35, n = 471; OFC: p = 1.11 × 10−31, n = 415; from n = 5 mice, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, Fig. 3l). When the GLM regressor labels were circularly shuffled, the cue SI slope obtained from the shuffled data was significantly smaller than that from the real data (Supplementary Fig. 7c, g), indicating that the observed increase in cue selectivity with behavioral performance could not be explained by chance correlations. To further investigate the source of this enhanced selectivity, we performed linear regressions of βpref and βnonpref against d′. The regression slope of βpref on d′ was significantly positive in MD neurons (p = 1.72 × 10−5), while the slope of βnonpref was significantly negative in both MD and OFC neurons (p < 0.001, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, Supplementary Fig. 8a). These findings suggest that the increase in cue value selectivity was mediated by enhanced responses to the preferred odor in MD and suppressed responses to the nonpreferred odor in both regions. Additionally, a higher percentage of MD neurons than OFC neurons showed a preference for the odor cue in the Go trial (Supplementary Fig. 8b, c).

To assess outcome-related activity, we divided each session into early, middle, and late phases (Supplementary Fig. 8d) to ensure a sufficient number of FA trials in each phase, enabling more robust estimation of βFA and computation of outcome SI (Supplementary Fig. 8e, f). In both MD and OFC neurons, we found that the slope of outcome SI was negatively correlated with its intercept (MD: r = −0.56, p = 8.71 × 10−40, n = 471 neurons; OFC: r = −0.56, p = 6.69 × 10−36, n = 415 neurons; from 5 mice, Fig. 3m, n). Applying circularly shuffled GLM regressors to outcome SI showed that the magnitudes of both slope and intercept of the shuffled data were significantly smaller than those in the real data (Supplementary Fig. 7d, h), indicating that the observed outcome selectivity dynamics were not due to chance. Notably, neurons with negative outcome SI slopes had positive intercepts (Fig. 3m, n), indicating stronger FA than Hit responses in the early phase, with this preference declining as learning progressed. Neurons with positive outcome SI slopes had negative intercepts (Fig. 3m, n), reflecting an early bias toward Hit responses that also weakened with learning. This pattern suggests that different subsets of neurons dynamically adjusted their sensitivity to outcome across learning.

In contrast to the changes in cue and outcome coefficients, the regression slope of βlick rate on d′ was near zero for both MD and OFC neurons (Supplementary Fig. 8g, h), indicating that lick-related activity remained stable throughout learning.

To investigate learning-related changes in MDOFC neurons, we used calcium imaging to record their activity throughout the learning process within a single session (Fig. 4a−c). Each session was divided into early, middle, and late phases (Supplementary Fig. 9a). We applied a GLM to fit the PSTH in each phase and performed linear regressions of cue SI and its associated coefficients (βpref, βnonpref) (Fig. 4d), as well as outcome SI and coefficients (βFA, βHit) across learning phases (Fig. 4g). As shown in Fig. 4e, the cue SI slope of MDOFC neurons was significantly positive (p = 1.04 × 10−25, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, n = 146, from 9 mice), indicating increased cue value selectivity with learning. To enable statistical inference with sufficient data points, we further divided trials into five learning phases (Supplementary Fig. 9f−h) and applied linear mixed-effects models, treating phase as a continuous fixed effect and neuron and mouse as random intercepts. This analysis revealed a significant positive relationship between cue SI and learning phase (slope = 0.32, SE = 0.015, t(728) = 21.27, p < 0.0001), confirming robust enhancement of cue value selectivity during learning. The enhancement in cue SI was accompanied by a significantly positive slope of βpref (three learning phases: p = 4.46 × 10−21 in Fig. 4f; five learning phases: p = 2.43 × 10−22 in Supplementary Fig. 9i) and a significantly negative slope of βnonpref (three learning phases: p = 1.57 × 10−4 in Fig. 4f; five learning phases: p = 0.039 in Supplementary Fig. 9i) across learning phases. By the end of learning, all MDOFC neurons preferred the odor cue in Go trials (Supplementary Fig. 9b). For outcome-related activity, 75% and 25% of MDOFC neurons (Fig. 4h) exhibited negative and positive outcome SI slopes, respectively. Across the population, the average outcome SI slope was significantly negative (p < 0.001, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, n = 146, from 9 mice; three learning phases: Fig. 4h; five learning phases: Supplementary Fig. 9k). This pattern likely reflects predominantly increasing βHit responses and more variable βFA changes with learning (Supplementary Fig. 9c, d), along with significantly positive intercepts for both outcome components (Supplementary Fig. 9e). Together, these findings demonstrate that MDOFC neurons gradually enhanced their cue value selectivity while dynamically adjusting their outcome selectivity over the course of learning.

MD projections to OFC support cue value selectivity and learning-related adaptations in the OFC

To investigate how MD projections influence neural coding in the OFC, we expressed inhibitory opsins (eNpHR or eOPN) in MD neurons and recorded from lateral OFC using an optotrode, with or without laser stimulation of MD axons (Fig. 5a). We first assessed the effect of laser stimulation in well-trained mice, in which one laser-on trial followed every two laser-off trials. Laser stimulation began 1 s before odor cue onset and lasted for 4 s. Since the laser stimulation was applied unilaterally, neither behavioral performance nor lick rate was affected (Supplementary Fig. 10a−c). Inactivating MD-to-OFC projections significantly decreased firing rates in 29.9% (59/197) and increased firing rates in 44.2% (87/197) of OFC neurons (Fig. 5b, and Supplementary Fig. 12a, n = 197 neurons, from 6 mice). Firing rate changes were weakly correlated with recording depth for neurons with increased firing (r = −0.24, p = 0.027, n = 87) and not correlated for neurons with decreased firing (r = 0.21, p = 0.11, n = 59, Supplementary Fig. 12b). Because well-trained mice had few FA trials (Supplementary Fig. 10c), precluding reliable analysis of outcome selectivity, we focused on neural discriminability between the two odors. Given that lick behavior did not differ between laser-off and laser-on conditions (Supplementary Fig. 10a), we assessed odor-related activity using spikes within 0.5‒1.5 s after odor cue onset (Fig. 5c). For both laser-off and laser-on trials, we applied receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis62 to compare spike count distributions in Go versus No-Go trials. We then computed a discrimination index (DI) as 2×(ROCarea−0.5), where ROCarea is the area under the ROC curve63; negative and positive DI values indicate neural preference for odor cues in No-Go and Go trials, respectively (Fig. 5d). Among neurons with a significant positive DI (Go stimulus preference) under the laser-off condition, DI magnitude was significantly reduced following inhibition of MD axons (p = 2.15 × 10−6, n = 111, from 6 mice, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, Fig. 5e right), and the magnitude of DI changes was weakly correlated with recording depth (r = 0.22, p = 0.018, Supplementary Fig. 12c). A similar reduction in DI magnitude was observed in neurons with a significant negative DI (No-Go stimulus preference) under the laser-off condition (p = 0.035, n = 21, from 6 mice, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, Fig. 5e left), and the magnitude of DI changes in these neurons was not significantly correlated with recording depth (r = 0.36, p = 0.11, Supplementary Fig. 12c). We further classified OFC neurons into broad-spiking and narrow-spiking cells based on their spike waveforms (Supplementary Fig. 11), corresponding to putative excitatory and inhibitory neurons, respectively. In broad-spiking cells, inactivating MD projections reduced firing in 20.9% (33/158) of neurons and increased firing in 54.4% (86/158) (Fig. 5f). In neurons that showed a significant DI under the laser-off condition, laser stimulation reduced the DI magnitude (neurons with positive DI under laser-off: p = 7.17 × 10⁻⁴, n = 82; neurons with negative DI under laser-off: p = 0.06, n = 20; from 6 mice, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, Fig. 5g). In contrast, 66.7% (26/39) of narrow-spiking cells showed a significant reduction in firing rate, whereas only 2.6% (1/39) showed an increase (Fig. 5h). For those narrow-spiking cells with a significant positive DI under the laser-off condition, DI magnitude was also significantly reduced upon inactivation of MD projections (p = 0.001, n = 29, from 6 mice, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, Fig. 5i). These findings support a critical role of MD input in maintaining cue value selectivity in the OFC. Notably, even among broad-spiking cells that showed significantly increased firing during MD input inactivation, the DI magnitude was significantly reduced in those exhibiting a significant positive DI under the laser-off condition (p = 0.02, n = 41, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, Supplementary Fig. 13), suggesting that this modulation may involve local inhibitory circuits.

Optogenetic inhibition of MD terminals in the OFC might influence the activity of MD cell bodies, which could in turn affect MD outputs to other targets. To test this, we recorded neuronal activity in MD while unilaterally inhibiting MD terminals in the OFC in well-trained mice (Supplementary Fig. 14a). We found that while a small fraction of MD neurons (~10%) exhibited a significant increase in firing rate during light delivery, the vast majority (90%) showed no significant change (Supplementary Fig. 14b), indicating that the impact of OFC terminal inhibition on MD activity is limited. To examine population-level changes in cue value selectivity, we classified all recorded MD neurons into four groups based on whether their DI was significant or non-significant under laser-off and laser-on conditions (Supplementary Fig. 14c). Compared with OFC neurons recorded during inhibition of MD-to-OFC projections (Supplementary Fig. 14f), the proportion of MD neurons that gained selectivity (i.e., changed their DI from non-significant to significant) was significantly higher (24% in MD vs 12% in OFC, p = 0.02, Fisher’s exact test, Supplementary Fig. 14c, f), whereas the proportion of MD neurons that lost selectivity (i.e., changed their DI from significant to non-significant) was lower (9% in MD vs 18% in OFC, p = 0.09, Fisher’s exact test, Supplementary Fig. 14c, f). In contrast to the pronounced reduction in Go stimulus preference observed in OFC neurons (Fig. 5d, e right), MD neurons did not show significant decrease in Go stimulus preference during terminal inhibition (Supplementary Fig. 14d, e). On the other hand, MD terminal inhibition reduced the No-Go stimulus preference in both OFC (Fig. 5e left) and MD (Supplementary Fig. 14d, e) neurons. This raises the possibility of a feedback influence on MD activity. However, given the limited proportion of MD neurons with significantly affected firing rate (Supplementary Fig. 14b) and the absence of reduction in Go stimulus preference in MD (Supplementary Fig. 14d, e), such an effect is insufficient to account for the primary changes observed in OFC neurons (Fig. 5d, e).

Next, we examined the effect of laser stimulation during odor discrimination learning, applying the laser once every three trials. Laser stimulation was applied from −1 to 3 s relative to odor cue onset. Each session was divided into early, middle, and late phases based on behavioral performance. Since MD projections were inhibited unilaterally, d′ across learning phases did not differ significantly between laser-off and laser-on conditions (Supplementary Fig. 15a). For each neuron, we performed separate GLM analyses on the PSTHs from laser-off and laser-on conditions within each learning phase, obtaining cue- and outcome-related coefficients (Fig. 5j, m).

To quantify learning-related changes in cue value selectivity, we regressed cue SI across the three learning phases (Fig. 5j). Inactivating MD axons significantly reduced the slope of cue SI in OFC neurons (p = 0.044) without affecting the intercept (p = 0.25, n = 79, from 7 mice, Wilcoxon signed rank test, Fig. 5k); a similar effect was observed in broad-spiking cells (cue SI slope: p = 0.045, cue SI intercept: p = 0.26, n = 60, from 7 mice, Wilcoxon signed rank test, Fig. 5l). We further divided trials into five learning phases (Supplementary Fig. 16) and applied a linear mixed-effects model to more rigorously assess learning-related changes in cue value selectivity. This analysis revealed that cue SI increased significantly across learning phases during laser-off trials (main effect of phase: coefficient = 0.12, SE = 0.02, t(786) = 4.87, p = 6.79 × 10−7), and inactivation of MD axons significantly attenuated this learning-induced increase (phase × laser interaction: coefficient = −0.09, SE = 0.03, t(786) = −2.66, p = 0.004). These results confirm that MD input facilitates the learning-dependent enhancement of odor discrimination in OFC neurons.

We next analyzed outcome-related selectivity by regressing outcome SI across the three learning phases (Fig. 5m, and Supplementary Fig. 17a). Regardless of whether neurons showed negative or positive outcome SI slopes under the laser-off condition, their slopes approached zero following inactivation of MD projections (cells with positive outcome SI slopes under laser-off: p = 9.17 × 10−3, n = 29; cells with negative outcome SI slopes under laser-off: p = 3.2 × 10−6, n = 50, from 7 mice, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, Fig. 5n). Broad-spiking cells showed a comparable reduction in the magnitude of outcome SI slopes (cells with positive outcome SI slopes under laser-off: p = 0.012, n = 26; cells with negative outcome SI slopes under laser-off: p = 4.55×10−5, n = 34, from 7 mice, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, Fig. 5o). Consistent with these observations, when we divided trials into five learning phases (Supplementary Fig. 18) and analyzed with a linear mixed-effects model, we also found that inactivation of MD axons significantly reduced the magnitude of outcome SI slope (phase × laser interaction for neurons with negative slopes under laser-off condition: coefficient = 0.24, SE = 0.049, t(486) = 4.88, p = 7.35 × 10−7; phase × laser interaction for neurons with positive slopes under laser-off condition: coefficient = −0.23, SE = 0.079, t(296) = −2.91, p = 0.002). Moreover, neurons with negative outcome SI slopes under the laser-off condition showed significantly positive intercepts, which shifted toward zero upon MD axon inactivation (three learning phases: p = 0.004, n = 50, Supplementary Fig. 17a; five learning phases: p = 0.003, Supplementary Fig. 18c; Wilcoxon signed-rank test, from 7 mice). Likewise, neurons with positive outcome SI slopes had a mean negative intercept, which shifted toward positive values under laser-on conditions (Supplementary Fig. 17a: p = 0.018, n = 29, from 7 mice, Wilcoxon signed-rank test; and Supplementary Fig. 18c), suggesting that MD inputs contribute to establishing initial outcome selectivity in OFC neurons during early learning. Changes in cue SI slopes or outcome SI slopes between laser-on and laser-off conditions were not correlated with recording depth (Supplementary Fig. 12d−f). Together, these results indicate that MD input is essential for the learning-dependent modulation of both cue and outcome coding in OFC.

MD projections to OFC recruit PV-mediated feedforward inhibition

Inactivation of MD axons significantly suppressed firing in 66.7% of narrow-spiking cells while enhancing firing in 54.4% of broad-spiking cells (Fig. 5f, h), consistent with previous reports that MD axons target inhibitory interneurons in the OFC26,53. We next performed slice recordings from OFC pyramidal neurons in mice injected with AAV-CaMKIIα-ChR2-mCherry into the MD (Fig. 6a). Optogenetic stimulation of MD axons evoked excitatory postsynaptic currents (EPSCs) in 65% (15/23) and inhibitory postsynaptic currents (IPSCs) in 30% (7/23) of pyramidal neurons (from 7 mice, Fig. 6b, c). The EPSCs were blocked by tetrodotoxin (TTX) and restored by 4-aminopyridine (4-AP) (Fig. 6d), confirming a monosynaptic excitatory connection from MD to OFC pyramidal neurons64. In contrast, IPSCs were eliminated by TTX and not restored by 4-AP (Fig. 6e), and exhibited longer latencies than EPSCs (Fig. 6f), indicating that MD-driven IPSCs in pyramidal neurons are mediated by local inhibitory neurons in the OFC.

To examine which subtype of inhibitory neuron mediates the IPSCs, we injected AAV2/8-pAAV-hSyn-hChR2(H134R)-mCherry into the MD and AAV2/5-CAG-FLEX-EGFP-WPRE into the OFC of PV-Cre, SST-Cre, or VIP-Cre mice, and performed slice recordings from OFC inhibitory neurons (Fig. 6g, h). We found that activating MD neurons evoked EPSCs in 85% (22/26) of PV neurons (from 7 mice, Fig. 6h, i) compared to 14% (1/7) of SST neurons (from 2 mice) and 22% (2/9) of VIP neurons (from 3 mice, Fig. 6h), indicating that MD axons preferentially target PV interneurons in the OFC.

As in pyramidal neurons, EPSCs in PV neurons were abolished by TTX and recovered by 4-AP (Fig. 6j). In PV neurons in which both EPSCs and IPSCs were recorded, 19% showed laser-evoked IPSCs (Fig. 6k), with latencies shorter than EPSCs (Fig. 6l). These findings indicate that MD axons form direct excitatory connections onto PV neurons in the OFC, while also recruiting local inhibitory circuits to drive feedforward inhibition onto PV neurons.

MD-input-defined PV neurons regulate cue value selectivity and learning-related adaptations in OFC broad-spiking cells

How do PV neurons innervated by MD projections influence task-related activity in the OFC? To address this question, we injected the anterograde transsynaptic virus AAV1-DIO-FlpO65 into the MD and AAV-fDIO-eNpHR into the lateral OFC of PV-Cre mice, enabling eNpHR expression in OFC PV neurons receiving MD input (MD-input-defined OFCPV neurons). Immunohistochemistry showed that the labeling was largely restricted to PV neurons, with 87% ±1% of neurons co-expressing PV (Supplementary Fig. 19). MD-input-defined PV neurons accounted for 49%±3% of PV neurons in the lateral OFC (Supplementary Fig. 19). We recorded neuronal activity in this OFC region using an optotrode, with or without laser stimulation (Fig. 7a−c).

Similar to the MD axon inactivation experiments, we first examined the effects of laser stimulation on OFC responses in well-trained mice, in which neither lick rate nor behavioral performance was affected (Supplementary Fig. 10d−f). Inactivating MD-input-defined OFCPV neurons significantly increased firing rates in 61.7% (71/115) and decreased firing rates in 13.0% (15/115) of broad-spiking cells, while significantly decreasing firing rates in 47.4% (9/19) and increasing firing rates in 21.0% (4/19) of narrow-spiking cells (from 5 mice, Fig. 7b). This manipulation also significantly reduced the DI magnitude in broad-spiking cells exhibiting a significant positive DI (Go stimulus preference) under the laser-off condition (p = 1.73 × 10−6, n = 42) and significantly reduced DI magnitude in those with a significant negative DI (No-Go stimulus preference) under the laser-off condition (p = 6.1 × 10−4, n = 15, from 5 mice, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, Fig. 7d), indicating that MD-driven PV neurons regulate cue value selectivity in OFC excitatory neurons.

Next, we examined the effect of laser stimulation during odor discrimination learning by dividing each session into early, middle, and late phases (Supplementary Fig. 15b). We performed GLM analysis on the PSTHs from laser-off and laser-on conditions within each phase and then regressed cue SI (or outcome SI) against the three learning phases (Fig. 7e, f, h, i, and Supplementary Fig. 17c). For cue-related activity, inactivation of MD-input-defined OFCPV neurons significantly reduced the slope of cue SI in broad-spiking cells (p = 6.8 × 10−5), without affecting the intercept (p = 0.39, n = 61, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, Fig. 7g). For outcome-related activity, regardless of outcome SI slope polarity under the laser-off condition, laser stimulation significantly reduced the magnitude of outcome SI slope (cells with positive outcome SI slopes under laser-off: p = 2.06 × 10−5, n = 35; cells with negative outcome SI slopes under laser-off: p = 2.96 × 10−4, n = 26, from 6 mice, Fig. 7j) and rendered the intercept not significantly different from zero (Supplementary Fig. 17c). To further validate these findings, we divided trials into five learning phases (Supplementary Fig. 20) and analyzed with a linear mixed-effects model, which yielded results consistent with three-phase analyses. Together, these results highlight the essential role of MD-input-defined OFCPV neurons in mediating the learning-dependent enhancement of cue selectivity and modulation of outcome selectivity in OFC excitatory neurons (Fig. 7k).

Discussion

Using a Go/No-Go olfactory discrimination task, we demonstrated that MD-to-OFC projections played a crucial role during learning. Both MD and OFC neurons encoded information about odor cues and behavioral outcomes, with learning accompanied by enhanced cue value selectivity and dynamic adjustment of outcome selectivity. Disrupting MD input to the OFC impaired the learning-related enhancement of cue value selectivity and attenuated the modulation of outcome-related signals. Moreover, inactivating MD-input-defined OFCPV neurons similarly disrupted these learning-related changes. These findings provide insight into how the MD-OFC circuit regulates OFC activity to facilitate associative learning.

Previous studies in both primates and rodents have demonstrated the critical role of the MD in learning. In monkeys, bilateral MD lesions impaired stimulus-reward associative learning10 and disrupted postoperative acquisition of new associations in tasks of scene memory and object-reward association memory11–13. However, these lesions did not affect the performance of stimulus-reward associations learned preoperatively11,13. Similarly, in rats engaged in an attentional set-shifting task, MD lesions impaired the acquisition of sensory discrimination but had no effect once the response strategy was established16. Our study builds on these findings by providing direct evidence for a specific thalamo-frontal circuit through which the MD supports associative learning. Specifically, we found that inactivating MDOFC neurons or MD-to-OFC projections impaired learning, but once the task was well-learned, it had no effect on performance, mirroring the phase-specific role previously observed with MD lesions. Thalamic circuits are increasingly recognized as active contributors to cortical plasticity by modulating or updating cortical representations3,4,28. Previous work has shown that medial thalamic inputs modulate OFC neuronal activity66, but whether this modulation causally influences associative learning remained unclear. Our study fills this gap by showing that MD input plays a causal role in associative learning through its regulation of cue and outcome encoding dynamics in the OFC. Notably, although inactivation of MDOFC neurons and MD-to-OFC projections both impaired learning, they had differential effects on licking motivation, potentially due to MD neuron collaterals projecting to other prefrontal regions, such as the insular cortex67 and mPFC60. These results highlight the broader influence of the MD on the prefrontal cortex and emphasize the distinct functional role played by its projections to the OFC.

The role of the OFC in learning and behavioral flexibility has been extensively studied using paradigms such as reversal learning, outcome devaluation, and over-expectation paradigms34,68,69. Early lesion studies reported that the OFC was not required for the initial acquisition of discrimination35,37. However, other work found that OFC damage impaired both discrimination and reversal learning in monkeys70 and disrupted olfactory discrimination learning in rodents71. In mice, OFC inhibition has been shown to impair the learning of discriminative Pavlovian trace conditioning, without affecting performance once learning is established38. Consistent with these findings, we also observed that OFC inhibition impaired associative learning in our paradigm. Moreover, chemogenetic inhibition of the OFC during cue-outcome association impaired subsequent performance in a probe test following devaluation of one cue, indicating that the OFC is critical for updating associative models or cognitive maps41. Notably, the same manipulation also impaired asymptotic performance during initial acquisition41. In our study, the olfactory discrimination task was preceded by an initial conditioning phase during which mice learned that an odor cue predicted reward. We found that inactivating MD-to-OFC projections impaired olfactory discrimination learning that followed this conditioning phase, reinforcing the view that OFC activity—shaped by MD input—is essential for updating associative models during learning41–45,72.

Many studies have demonstrated the importance of thalamocortical interaction, including the MD-PFC connection, in higher-order cognitive functions28,73. For instance, in mice performing an attentional control task based on different rules, MD activity sustained rule representations in mPFC by amplifying the functional connectivity of local mPFC neurons25. During a complex decision-making task involving hierarchical reasoning, MD neurons represent task-relevant cue and rule uncertainty, and MD-frontal interactions enable error monitoring in the ACC to trigger mPFC rule reconfiguration during behavioral switching26. Additionally, the MD-mPFC circuit plays a role in maintaining working memory24 and regulating social dominance status74. In rats performing an odor-guided decision task, MD neurons encoded information related to odor, movement, and choice, while their phase locking to OFC oscillations varied dynamically across task periods75. Moreover, MD neuronal responses to sensory stimuli were modulated by reward contingencies and behavioral responses during learning76–78. Beyond sensory and movement encoding, MD neurons also represented outcome information79,80. Building upon previous findings, we demonstrated that both MD and OFC neurons encoded stimulus and outcome information. Inactivation of MD axons in the OFC during either the cue-delay period or the response window impaired learning, suggesting that MD input supports the encoding of stimulus and outcome signals in the OFC, which is critical for learning. As learning progressed, MDOFC neurons and OFC neurons exhibited increasingly selective responses to sensory cues. In parallel, outcome-related signals were dynamically modulated during learning, potentially serving as a complementary mechanism for updating value representations based on behavioral feedback69. These learning-dependent changes in OFC activity were disrupted when MD input was inhibited, indicating that the MD-OFC circuit is essential for shaping associative representations in the OFC to guide behavior.

In sensory cortices, thalamocortical feedforward inhibition plays a crucial role in modulating sensory processing by cortical neurons81. Inhibitory neurons in these regions exhibit distinct response properties from excitatory neurons82,83, and their manipulation can alter the stimulus selectivity of excitatory neurons84. Similarly, in the PFC, feedforward inhibition driven by thalamic inputs is critical for cognitive processes such as working memory, social preference, and attentional switching28,85. In our study, we found that MD projections targeted PV interneurons in the OFC and also recruited feedforward inhibition onto these interneurons. Inhibiting MD-input-defined OFCPV neurons significantly disrupted task-related activity in OFC broad-spiking cells, including impaired cue value selectivity and its learning-dependent enhancement, as well as altered modulation of outcome selectivity during learning. These results suggest that MD input regulates stimulus-reward association signals in OFC neurons through feedforward inhibition, providing evidence for how MD-prefrontal interactions contribute to adaptive learning.

In conclusion, MD-to-OFC projections are critical for discrimination learning by enhancing cue selectivity and dynamically regulating outcome-related representations in the OFC. This process relies on feedforward inhibition mediated by OFC PV interneurons.

Methods

Animals

All animal procedures (IACUC No. NA-013-2025) were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee at the Institute of Neuroscience, Center for Excellence in Brain Science and Intelligence Technology, Chinese Academy of Sciences. The experiments were conducted in accordance with the Center’s guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. The following mice were used in the experiments: C57BL/6 (SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Shanghai, China), PV-Cre (The Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME, USA, stock No. 008069), SST-Cre (The Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME, USA, stock No. 013044), and VIP-Cre (the Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME, USA, stock No. 010908). Adult (>7 weeks old) male mice were used for the experiments. Mice were housed under standardized conditions (temperature: 22–23 °C, humidity: 40–70%, 12-h light/12-h dark cycle) in the Institute’s animal facility.

Surgery and virus injection

Prior to surgery, mice received an intraperitoneal injection of a mixture of xylazine (40 mg/kg) and Zoletil (30 mg/kg). The mice were then placed in a stereotaxic apparatus, the scalp was shaved, and lidocaine jelly was applied to the incision site for local anesthesia. A craniotomy (~0.5 mm diameter) was performed above the virus injection site. The virus (0.5−15 × 1012 viral particles/ml) was delivered using a glass pipette (10–20 μm tip diameter) connected to a syringe pump (Harvard Apparatus, Holliston, MA, USA) at a controlled rate of 100 nL/min.

To inactivate MD axon terminals in the OFC, 200 nL of AAV2/5-CaMKIIα-eOPN3.0-mScarlet-WPRE (or AAV2/5-CaMKIIα-eGFP-WPRE-pA for randomly assigned control mice) was bilaterally injected at a depth of 3200 μm into the MD (AP −1.4 mm, ML ± 0.5 mm), and optic fibers (400 μm diameter, NA 0.5) were inserted at a depth of 1600 μm into the lateral OFC (AP + 2.5 mm, ML ± 1.5 mm). To inactivate OFC neurons, 400 nL of AAV2/5-CaMKIIα-eOPN3.0-mScarlet-WPRE (or 400 nL of AAV2/5-CaMKIIα-eGFP-WPRE-pA as control) was bilaterally injected into the lateral OFC at a depth of 1800 μm, and optic fibers (400 μm diameter, NA 0.5) were implanted 300 μm above the virus injection sites. To inactivate MD neurons, 200 nL of AAV2/5-CaMKIIα-eNpHR3.0-mCherry-WPRE-pA (or 150 nL of AAV2/5-CaMKIIα-eGFP-WPRE-pA as a control) was bilaterally injected into the MD at a depth of 3250 μm, and optic fibers (300 μm diameter, NA 0.5) were implanted 350 μm above the virus injection sites. To inactivate MDOFC neurons, 450 nL of AAV-Retro-hSyn-Cre-P2A-dTomato was bilaterally injected into the OFC at a depth of 1800 μm, 400 nL of AAV5-EF1α-DIO-eNpHR3.0-EYFP (or AAV2/5-CAG-DIO-EGFP-WPRE-pA as control) was bilaterally injected into the MD at a depth of 3300 μm, and optic fibers (400 μm diameter, NA 0.5) were implanted 300 μm above the virus injection sites in MD. To inactivate MD-input-defined OFCPV neurons, 200 nL of AAV1-EFIα-DIO-FlpO-WPRE-hGHpA was injected into the MD and 400 nL of AAV2/9-hEF1α-fDIO-eNpHR3.0-EYFP-WPRE-pA into the ipsilateral lateral OFC of PV-Cre mice. These PV neurons were optogenetically silenced via the optotrode during OFC recordings.

To image MDOFC neurons, 400−600 nL of AAV-Retro-hSyn-Cre-P2A-dTomato was injected into the OFC at a depth of 1600−1900 μm, and 300−500 nL of AAV2/5-CAG-DIO-GCaMP6s-WPRE-hGHpA was injected into the ipsilateral MD at a depth of 3100−3400 μm.

For slice recordings from OFC pyramidal neurons while activating MD axons, 200 nL of AAV2/8-pAAV-hSyn-hChR2(H134R)-mCherry was injected into the MD at a depth of 3300 μm, and 400−500 nL of AAV2/9-CaMKIIα-EGFP-WPRE-hGHpA was injected into the lateral OFC at a depth of 1800 μm in C57BL/6 mice. For slice recordings from OFC PV (or SST, VIP) neurons, 200 nL of AAV2/8-pAAV-hSyn-hChR2(H134R)-mCherry was injected into the MD at a depth of 3300 μm, and 400−500 nL of AAV2/5-CAG-FLEX-EGFP-WPRE was injected into the lateral OFC at a depth of 1800 μm in PV-Cre (or SST-Cre, VIP-Cre) mice.

For behavioral experiments, a stainless-steel headplate was fixed to the mice’s skull using dental cement. For extracellular recordings, the skull region above the MD (AP −1.4 mm, ML 0.5 mm) or lateral OFC (AP + 2.5 mm, ML 1.5 mm) was marked with permanent ink. After surgery, mice received Rimadyl in their drinking water as an analgesic for 3 days and were given at least 10 days to recover before starting behavioral training.

Behavioral task

Prior to training, mice underwent water deprivation for 2 days and were head-fixed to the apparatus for 30 min per day to adapt to the environment. During training, each mouse was head-fixed to an apparatus and placed inside an acrylic tube within the behavioral chamber. A lick spout was positioned approximately 2 mm in front of the mouse’s nose and 2 mm below its lower lip. Licks were detected either by a custom-made electrical lick sensor or by interruption of an infrared beam. Water delivery was controlled by a peristaltic valve (Kamoer, Shanghai, China). An Arduino microcontroller was used to control odor cue presentation, lick detection, water delivery, laser stimulation, and data acquisition. Task-related events and lick signals were sampled at 1000 Hz. For calcium imaging and electrophysiology experiments, task-related signals were transmitted from the Arduino to a computer and recorded using custom Processing scripts. In addition, a trigger marking trial onset was simultaneously recorded by the imaging or electrophysiology acquisition system to enable precise alignment of neural activity with behavioral events. For optogenetic manipulation experiments, signals related to trial onset, trial type, laser stimulation, and licks were transmitted to a computer via a multifunction I/O device (USB-6001, National Instruments, Texas, USA) and recorded using custom MATLAB scripts.

Behavioral training consisted of three phases: habituation, conditioning, and Go/No-Go olfactory discrimination. During habituation (2–4 days), the mouse learned to lick from a lickspout to receive water rewards. In the conditioning phase (at least 3 days), each trial included a 1-s odor cue, a 0.5-s delay, a 1.5-s response window, and a 3–8 s inter-trial interval. Licks during the response window triggered the delivery of 3–5 μL of water. This conditioning phase aimed to establish an expectation of reward following the cue and to ensure consistent engagement during the response window. In the subsequent Go/No-Go discrimination phase, two distinct odors (odor 1 and odor 2) were introduced as the Go and No-Go cues. The duration of the odor cue, delay period, response window, and inter-trial interval was similar to those in the conditioning phase. Using this protocol, mice could acquire the discrimination task within a single session (usually 200–300 trials), enabling examination of neural activity dynamics during learning with acute electrophysiological recordings. In mice used for electrophysiological recordings and calcium imaging, the onset of the response window was signaled by a 0.5-s presentation of isopentyl acetate. In a Go trial, licking within the response window resulted in a reward of 3–5 μL of water (Hit), while no lick was counted as a Miss. In a No-Go trial, licking during the response window was considered a FA, and no lick was a CR. Each mouse performed the task for at least 200 trials per session. In the conditioning phase, either citral or propyl acetate was used as the odor stimulus. In the discrimination phase, odor 1 and odor 2 were selected from the following set: propyl formate, butyl formate, ethyl acetate, propyl acetate, butyl acetate, ethyl propionate, methyl butyrate, ethyl butyrate, butyl butyrate, methyl tiglate, salicylaldehyde, and octane.

Optogenetic stimulation

Optical activation of eNpHR (eOPN) was induced by green light, and activation of ChR2 was induced by blue light. A green (532 nm) or a blue (473 nm) laser (Shanghai Laser & Optics Century Co., Shanghai, China) was connected to an output optical fiber. Laser stimulation was controlled by an Arduino microcontroller. For inactivation of MD-to-OFC projections, laser power was at 20 mW or 10 mW. For inactivation of MD neurons, MDOFC neurons, and OFC neurons, laser power was at 20 mW, 3 mW, and 6 mW, respectively.

To examine the effect of optogenetic inhibition on learning behavior, we employed multiple stimulation paradigms. In one set of experiments, laser stimulation was applied during each trial throughout discrimination training, each targeting a different time window relative to odor cue onset: (1) −1 – 5 s, including the periods of cue, delay, and response window, (2) −1 – 1.5 s, covering the cue and delay periods, (3) 1.5–3 s, covering the response window only. In a separate set of experiments, laser stimulation was restricted to the first 5 blocks of discrimination training (20 trials per block), with no stimulation in subsequent blocks. In this paradigm, laser stimulation was delivered from −1 to 5 s relative to odor cue onset. To assess the effect of inhibition on task performance in well-trained mice, laser-off and laser-on trials were interleaved, with laser stimulation applied from −1 to 5 s relative to odor cue onset.

To examine the effect of optogenetic inhibition on neural coding of OFC (or MD) neurons in well-trained or learning mice, each laser-on trial was preceded by two laser-off trials. Laser stimulation was applied from −1 to 3 s relative to odor cue onset, covering the cue, delay, and response window periods.

Extracellular recording

Mice that had completed at least 3 days of conditioning training were used for extracellular recordings during the olfactory discrimination learning task. Prior to recording, mice were head-fixed to a holder attached to a stereotaxic apparatus under isoflurane (1–2%) anesthesia. A craniotomy (~1 mm diameter) was made above the MD (AP −1.4 mm, ML 0.5 mm) or lateral OFC (AP + 2.5 mm, ML 1.5 mm), and the dura was removed. The craniotomy was then covered with silicone elastomer (Kwik-Cast, WPI, Sarasota, FL, USA). The mice were returned to their home cage and allowed to recover from anesthesia for at least 1 h before recording began.

For recordings from the MD with optogenetic inactivation of MD axons in OFC and for simultaneous recordings from the MD and OFC, Neuropixels probes (Neuropixels 1.0 probe, Imec, Leuven, Belgium) were used. For OFC recordings combined with optogenetic inactivation of MD axons in OFC or with optogenetic inactivation of MD-input-defined OFCPV neurons, we used optotrodes (ASSY-37-32-1-9mm, Diagnostic Biochips, Maryland, USA) equipped with a 100-µm core optic fiber. After each recording session, the electrode was retracted, the craniotomy was cleaned with sterile saline, and protected with silicone elastomer. To visualize the electrode track post hoc, probes were coated with fluorescent dye (DiI or DiO, Invitrogen, Eugene, OR, USA) before insertion. Electrophysiological signals were acquired using either the Neuropixels system (Imec, Leuven, Belgium) or the Cerebus 64-channel system (Blackrock Microsystems, Salt Lake City, UT, USA).

For the Neuropixels system, data from the Neuropixels probes was recorded using the SpikeGLX software (https://github.com/billkarsh/SpikeGLX). Offline analysis of the data was performed using the MATLAB-based Kilosort2.5 software (https://github.com/MouseLand/Kilosort/releases/tag/v2.5) to obtain the spiking data of individual neurons. The resulting units were then manually curated with Phy (https://github.com/cortex-lab/phy) in Python to ensure accurate unit isolation throughout the recording session86. Because recordings were performed within a single session during which mice acquired the task, the same units could be reliably tracked across trials.

Neuropixels-recorded neurons were assigned to brain regions by aligning electrode tracks in histological brain slices to the Allen CCF using SHARP-Track (https://github.com/cortex-lab/allenCCF)86,87. Only neurons localized to the MD and OFC were included in subsequent analyses.

For recordings with the Cerebus 64-channel system, signals were band-pass filtered between 250 and 7500 Hz, and spike waveforms were detected using a threshold at ≥3.5 standard deviations of the background noise. Spiking signals were sampled at 30 kHz. Offline spike sorting was performed using Offline Sorter V3 (Plexon Inc., Dallas, TX, USA) based on principal component cluster analysis. Spike clusters were classified as single units if the interspike interval exceeded 1 ms and they passed a multivariate analysis of variance test (p < 0.05).

Slice preparation and recording

We used C57BL/6 (n = 7), PV-Cre (n = 7), SST-Cre (n = 2) and VIP-Cre (n = 3) mice for slice recordings. In C57BL/6 mice, we injected AAV2/8-pAAV-hSyn-hChR2(H134R)-mCherry into the MD and AAV2/9-CaMKIIα-EGFP-WPRE-hGHpA into the lateral OFC. In PV-Cre (or SST-Cre, VIP-Cre) mice, we injected AAV2/8-pAAV-hSyn-hChR2(H134R)-mCherry into the MD and AAV2/5-CAG-FLEX-EGFP-WPRE into the OFC. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and subsequently perfused transcardially with ice-cold, oxygenated (95% O2/5% CO2) high-sucrose solution containing (in mM): 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 2 Na2HPO4, 2 MgSO4, 213 sucrose, and 26 NaHCO3. Coronal slices (250 μm thick) were prepared using a vibratome (VT1200S, Leica, Deer Park, IL, USA) in the ice-cold, oxygenated high-sucrose solution. Slices containing the OFC were incubated in artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF) at 34 °C for 1 h. The ACSF contained (in mM): 126 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 1.25 Na2HPO4, 2 MgSO4, 10 Glucose, 26 NaHCO3, and 2 CaCl2. The intracellular solution for recordings contains (in mM): 135 K-gluconate, 4 KCl, 10 HEPES, 10 sodium phosphocreatine, 4 Mg-ATP, 0.3 Na3-GTP, and 0.5 biocytin (pH: 7.2, 276 mOsm). Recording electrodes (3–5 MΩ) were pulled from borosilicate glass (Sutter Instrument, Novato, CA, USA) using a micropipette puller (model P97, Sutter Instrument). Whole-cell recordings were performed using a MultiClamp700B amplifier and a Digi-data 1440A interface (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA). Data were recorded with Clampex 10.2 (Molecular Devices), low-pass filtered at 2 kHz, and sampled at 20 kHz. All recordings were conducted at 33 °C using a temperature controller (TC324B, Warner, Holliston, MA, USA).

For synaptic transmission recordings, repetitive single blue light pulses (10 ms duration, 12 mW/mm2) were delivered to the brain slice through a 40× objective using an X-Cite LED light source (Lumen Dynamics, Mississauga, Canada). Neurons were recorded in voltage-clamp mode at a holding potential of 0 mV to isolate inhibitory currents, and at −70 mV to isolate excitatory currents. To assess monosynaptic connectivity, tetrodotoxin (TTX, 1 μM; Absin, Cat# abs44200985a, Shanghai, China) and 4-aminopyridine (4-AP, 1 mM; Alomone Labs, Cat# A-115, Jerusalem, Israel) were sequentially added to the bath solution.

GRIN lens implantation and in vivo calcium imaging

For mice used in calcium imaging experiments, the calcium indicator GCaMP6s was expressed in MDOFC neurons. The surgery of GRIN lens implantation was performed at least 2 weeks after the virus injection. The overlying brain tissue was aspirated using a 27 G needle connected to a vacuum. A 25 G needle was then used to puncture 300–400 µm above the virus injection site. To avoid blood clotting, the tissue in the cranial window was irrigated with sterile saline solution. When there was no bleeding, a GRIN lens (500 μm in diameter and 7.48 mm in length, InFocus Grin Corp., Nantong, China) was implanted 0‒200 μm above the GCaMP6s expression site. The lens was secured to the skull with dental cement. Once the lens was in place, a head plate was mounted. The lens was wiped with anhydrous ethanol, and a 0.2 mL EP tube (with the bottom removed) was placed over the lens and secured with dental cement.

The custom-built wide-field single-photon fluorescence microscopy system included: an objective lens (×10, NA 0.3, RMS10X-PF, Olympus), an LED excitation light source (470 nm), an LED driver, and a GFP filter set. Imaging signals collected by a CCD camera (Qimaging, Retiga R1) were recorded using Micro-manager software at a sampling rate of 11–16 Hz. Motion correction was performed using ANTsPy88 (https://github.com/ANTsX/ANTsPy). Cell detection and extraction of single-neuron fluorescence signals were performed using suite2p software (https://suite2p.readthedocs.io/en/latest). Because mice acquired the discrimination task within a single session, the same neurons could be reliably tracked across trials.

Histology

The mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane and transcardially perfused with 30 mL of saline followed by 30 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). The brains were extracted and postfixed in 4% PFA overnight at 4 °C. Coronal sections (60 μm thick) were prepared using a vibratory slicer (Leica VT1200S, Vizsla, Germany). For PV labeling, sections were rinsed in PBS, blocked in 20% ABC with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 2 h at room temperature, and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibody against parvalbumin (guinea pig anti-PV, Catalog No. 195308, 1:750, Synaptic Systems). After washing in PBS, sections were incubated with donkey anti-guinea pig-Cy3 (Catalog No. 706-166-148, 1:1000, Jackson Immuno Research) for 2 h at room temperature. Sections were stained with DAPI (Solarbio, C0065, Beijing, China) and mounted using antifading mounting medium (Solarbio, S2100, Beijing, China). The fluorescence images were acquired using a VS120 microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and analyzed with ImageJ (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA). Schematic illustrations of mouse brain anatomy were redrawn by the authors based on the mouse brain atlas by Franklin and Paxinos89.

Data analysis

Analyses were performed using MATLAB (MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA, USA) or GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA).

Behavioral performance was quantified as follows:

Hit rate = number of Hit trials/(number of Hit trials + number of miss trials),

FA rate = number of FA trials/(number of FA trials + number of CR trials),

Behavioral discriminability (d′) = norminv(Hit rate) – norminv(FA rate), where norminv is the inverse of the cumulative normal function. If Hit rate or FA rate was equal to 1, it was set to [1-1/(2n)], in which n is the number of all possible Hit or FA trials. If Hit rate or FA rate was equal to 0, it was set to 1/(2n)90. Lick latency was defined as the time of the first lick occurring within the 0−3 s window following stimulus onset. Only trials in which a lick occurred within this window were included in the calculation of lick latency; trials without a lick in this window (e.g., correct rejection trials) were not assigned a lick latency. In experiments without electrophysiological recordings, behavioral performance was computed in blocks of 20 trials. For each block, lick latency was calculated separately for Go and No-Go trials as the mean across trials that contained a lick within 0–3 s. Lick rate was calculated separately for Go and No-Go trials as the mean number of licks per trial within the 0–3 s window, averaged across all trials of the same type within a block. For experiments involving electrophysiological recordings, performance was computed in larger blocks as specified in the GLM analysis.

For optogenetic experiments in well-trained mice, changes in behavioral performance (ΔHit rate, ΔFR rate, Δd′, Δlick rate or Δfirst lick latency) were calculated as the difference between laser-on and laser-off trials for each measure (laser-on minus laser-off).

We performed one-way ANOVA to compare neuronal activity across three task epochs—baseline (1 s before odor onset), stimulus-delay (0.5 s to 1.5 s after odor onset), and outcome (1.5 s following outcome delivery)—in Hit or CR trials under the laser-off condition. Neurons with a p-value < 0.01 in either trial type were classified as task-modulated. For electrophysiology recordings, only task-modulated neurons with sufficient firing rates were included. Specifically, for well-trained mice, we included neurons whose mean firing rate across the entire session exceeded 1 spike/s. For learning mice, we included neurons whose mean firing rate during the 0.5–1.5 s window after odor cue onset exceeded 1 spike/s.

We used a general linear model (GLM) to quantify the task-related activity of MD or OFC neurons. The spike counts from each session were binned into PSTH using a 250-ms bin size, and the PSTH was divided into multiple blocks (50 trials/block, with a 25-trial moving window). For each session, we included blocks up to the one with the highest d′ that had a nonzero FA rate. For analyses dividing each session into early, middle, and late phases, trials were first organized into blocks of 20 consecutive trials. Blocks with d′ ≤ 0.5 were classified as the early phase, whereas blocks with d′ > 2.5 were assigned to the late phase (d′ was computed from laser-off trials when laser stimulation was present). If the late-phase blocks contained fewer than three FA trials, preceding blocks were added until at least three FA trials were obtained. All remaining blocks were designated as the middle phase. Accordingly, the PSTH for each session was grouped into three phase-specific PSTHs corresponding to the early, middle, and late learning phases. In addition to the three-phase analysis (early, middle, late), we also divided trials into five learning phases. Because each session contained a limited number of trials, phase blocks were created using session-specific block size and step size, with consecutive blocks partially overlapping to ensure sufficient events for reliable GLM estimation. Phase 1 included trials in which d′ was less than 1, and phase 5 was defined such that at least one FA trial occurred. All phases within a session contained the same number of trials.

For each neuron, the GLM was fitted to each block of the PSTH. For OFC recordings with optogenetic inactivation of MD axons or MD-input-defined OFCPV neurons, only spikes within −1 ‒ 3 s relative to odor cue onset were used to construct the PSTH. The explanatory variables in the GLM included two odor cues, response window onset cue, lick rate, and four outcomes (Hit, CR, FA, and Miss). Except for lick rate, each variable spanned multiple bins relative to the event38, coded as 1 when the event was present in a bin and 0 otherwise. This coding allows the corresponding coefficient to quantify the average change in neuronal activity associated with that variable, while controlling for others. Since our goal was to fit each PSTH block to estimate changes in cue and outcome selectivity during learning, we did not use time-varying kernels for the model coefficients. For each neuron, the GLM was fit separately to each PSTH block; the CPD values were averaged across blocks. To assess the significance of cue- or outcome-related CPDs, we performed a circular shuffle control in which each trial label of the corresponding regressor was circularly shifted relative to the neuronal data. The GLM was then refitted for each circular shift, and CPDs were recomputed. This procedure was repeated across all possible circular shifts for each neuron. For each neuron, the mean CPD across shuffled iterations was calculated. The population of real CPDs was compared to the population of mean shuffled CPDs.

To examine how cue SI, outcome SI, or GLM coefficients (βpref, βnonpref, βFA, βHit) changed with learning, we performed a linear regression of each variable, either as a function of d′ or across learning phases. For regressions of cue SI or GLM coefficients on d′, the intercept represents the fitted value at d′ = 0. For regressions on learning phase (coded as an ordinal variable), the intercept corresponds to the fitted value at phase 1.

We also performed a shuffle control analysis for cue and outcome SI. For each neuron, the trial labels of the corresponding regressors were circularly shifted relative to the neuronal data, and GLM coefficients were recomputed. Cue and outcome SI were then recalculated from the shuffled coefficients. Linear regression of cue SI, outcome SI, or GLM coefficients against d′ or learning phase was performed on the shuffled data to obtain slope and intercept values. This procedure was repeated across all possible circular shifts for each neuron. For each neuron, the mean slope and intercept across shuffled iterations were calculated. At the population level, the real slopes and intercepts were compared to the mean shuffled values.

To assess the effect of inactivating MD axons (or OFCPV neurons) on OFC activity or the effect of inactivating MD axons in OFC on MD activity, we used a Wilcoxon signed-rank test to compare firing rates between laser-off and laser-on conditions within a 0.5–1.5 s window after odor cue onset.

For neurons recorded from well-trained mice, we conducted ROC62 separately for laser-off and laser-on conditions, using spike counts from Go versus No-Go trials. For each condition, the area under the ROC curve (ROCarea) was computed and converted into a discrimination index (DI): DI = 2 × (ROCarea−0.5)63, where negative values indicate a preference for the odor cue in No-Go trials and positive values indicate a preference for the odor cue in Go trials, ranging from −1 to 1. Statistical significance of the DI was assessed using a permutation test. We randomly shuffled the trial labels (Go vs. No-Go), recalculated the ROCarea, and repeated this procedure 1000 times to generate a null distribution. The p value was defined as the fraction of shuffled values that exceeded the actual DI. A significance threshold of α = 0.05 was used. Neurons with significant DI in the laser-off condition were used to compare DI across laser conditions.

Based on the spike waveforms, we classified OFC neurons as broad-spiking and narrow-spiking cells91, which correspond to putative excitatory and putative inhibitory neurons, respectively. For each neuron, spike waveforms were aligned to the trough and averaged. Peak width was defined as the width of the peak at half its maximum amplitude. Neurons with peak widths <0.33 ms were classified as narrow-spiking, and those with peak widths ≥0.33 ms as broad-spiking.

To statistically infer learning-related changes, we applied linear mixed-effects models across the five learning phases92. This approach provided sufficient data for reliable estimation while accounting for the hierarchical structure of the data, with repeated measures (phases) on neurons and neurons nested within mice. Learning phase was treated as a continuous fixed effect, and neuron and mouse were included as random intercepts, avoiding unstable estimation of random slopes when each neuron contributed only five observations. Learning-related changes in cue SI were assessed by testing whether the slope of cue SI across phases was significantly greater than zero. To evaluate the effect of laser stimulation on learning-related changes in cue SI or outcome SI, the model was extended to include a phase × laser interaction. The linear mixed-effects models were fitted using restricted maximum likelihood in MATLAB (fitlme).

Statistical analysis and reproducibility

Sample sizes were comparable to those used in the field. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample sizes. The statistical analyses were performed using MATLAB or GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software). Wilcoxon signed-rank test (two-sided), Wilcoxon rank-sum test (two-sided), one-way ANOVA, two-way ANOVA with mixed design, Fisher’s exact test, or mixed-effects model was used to determine the significance of the effect. Statistical significance was denoted as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01 and ***p < 0.001. Correlation values were computed using Pearson’s correlation. Data were not collected in a blinded fashion. Unless otherwise stated, data were reported as mean ± SEM.

All histological images, viral expression patterns and electrode tracks shown in the figures are representative examples. The corresponding experiments were independently replicated in multiple mice with similar results. The numbers of biological replicates for each experiment are provided in the relevant figure legends.
