Experimental dataset for system identification of a dual-phase gas–liquid transmembrane pressure system
Authors: Trung Dat Phan, Cong Toai Truong, Huu Nhan Nguyen, Hai Dang Ho, Huy Hung Nguyen, Jinpil Ha, Thandapani Marimuthu, Tan Tien Nguyen, Hee-Tae Jung, Van Tu Duong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe data presented in this article describe the dynamic input–output responses of a dual-phase gas–liquid transmembrane pressure system operating under different water flow rates and actuator excitations. The dataset is acquired from a laboratory-scale experimental platform consisting of interconnected CO₂ gas and water channels separated by a porous polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) membrane module. The gas and water control valves are used to regulate the gas and liquid flow paths, while the liquid channel is supplied by a controlled water pump. Gas inlet pressure and water outlet pressure are measured using industrial pressure transducers and synchronously acquired by a Siemens S7–1200 programmable logic controller at a sampling frequency of 1 Hz.Data are collected under three water flow-rate conditions. For each condition, three open-loop excitation profiles—sine-wave, sawtooth, and multi-step commands—are applied to gas and water control valves. Each experiment lasts 20 min and generates 1200 samples. The complete dataset consists of nine XLSX files containing 10,800 synchronized samples. Each file contains five channels: time, commanded gas-valve position, commanded water-valve position, gas inlet pressure, and water outlet pressure, with clearly labeled variables and physical units.This dataset can be reused to analyze the system responses under different excitation profiles and water flow-rate conditions, characterize transient and coupled input–output behavior, as well as develop and validate linear and nonlinear multiple-input multiple-output system identification methods. It can also support data-driven dynamic modelling, as well as the subsequent design and evaluation of model-based control strategies for gas–liquid transmembrane pressure systems.Keywords: Gas–liquid transmembrane pressure system, Parameter estimation, System identification, Multi-input multi-output nonlinear system control

Background

Gas–liquid membrane contactors provide a non-dispersive interface between gas and liquid phases, with their dynamic behavior influenced by membrane characteristics, fluid flow conditions, and transmembrane pressure [1]. Fougerit et al. investigated CO₂ absorption in water under different transmembrane pressures and membrane-wetting conditions [2]. Li et al. examined CO₂ removal using PTFE hollow-fiber contactors with variations in membrane geometry and flow conditions [3]. Tran et al. evaluated the influence of gas–liquid pressure difference on CO₂ absorption and developed a mass-balance-based membrane model [4]. Rosli et al. reported transient CO₂ absorption measurements for validating a dynamic transport model involving gas, membrane, and liquid phases [5]. These experimental studies primarily provide absorption, mass-transfer, or model-validation data. Their experimental variables were selected to characterize membrane transport performance rather than excitation-response relationships required for identification. They do not offer synchronized actuator–pressure datasets specifically designed for control-oriented MIMO system identification. In contrast, the present dataset records gas- and water-valve commands together with the corresponding gas inlet and water outlet pressures. Measurements were obtained under sine-wave, sawtooth, and multi-step excitations at three liquid-flow conditions. This structure captures transient responses and cross-coupling across operating conditions, enabling reusable data for nonlinear MIMO identification, dynamic-model validation, and subsequent controller development.

The experiments ran for 20 min each with the sampling rate of 1 Hz, resulting in the generation of 1200 data points per XLSX file. Therefore, the complete dataset has a total of 10,800 data points collected across nine independent experimental trials. The three operating conditions corresponded to water-pump flow rates of 0.45 L/h, 0.9 L/h, and 1.35 L/h.

In each XLSX file, there is time series data on 5 channels, while the first line is a header describing the signal identification and physical units. Time data in the first column is taken at intervals of 1.0 s. The commanded stepper-motor positions for the gas and water control valves are given in columns 2 and 3, respectively. A percentage of 100% means one full revolution of the motor shaft. Columns 4 and 5 represent the system responses: gas inlet pressure and water outlet pressure.

Experimental Design, Materials, and Methods

Experimental setup overview

Fig. 2 presents a simplified representation of the experimental DGLTPS, retaining only the pressure-related components relevant to the system responses investigated in this study. The DGLTPS consists of a water channel and a CO₂ channel coupled through a transmembrane module. In the CO₂ channel, CO₂ from the CO₂ container passes through a gas control valve (BPR1) before entering the membrane module. The gas-side pressure upstream of the membrane is indicated by pressure gauge PG1 and continuously measured by pressure transducer PT1. After passing through the membrane module, the gas flows through the mass flow controller (MFC1) before being discharged to the gas collector.

Schematic diagram of the experimental DGLTPS.

In the water channel, water from the tank is pressurized by a pump and delivered to the membrane module. After passing through the membrane module, the water-side pressure is monitored by pressure gauge PG2 and pressure transducer PT2. A water control valve (BPR2) regulates the downstream water pressure before the water is returned to the tank, forming a recirculating loop. The pressure signals from PT1 and PT2 are acquired by an industrial control system based on a Siemens S7–1200 programmable logic controller (PLC). The PLC performs real-time control and transmits the recorded data to a host computer via Ethernet (PROFINET) at a sampling rate of 1 Hz.

Instrumentation and sensors

To capture the system’s dynamic responses, the industrial-grade pressure transducers (M5256-C3079E-3.5BG, Sensys, Gyeonggi-do, Korea) are installed to measure the gas inlet and water outlet pressures. These sensors feature a 0 to 3.5 bar gauge measurement range, a standard 4–20 mA two-wire current loop output, and an accuracy rating of ±0.5% full scale (FS). Manufactured with wet parts made of STS630 stainless steel without organic o-rings or internal welds, the transducers offer high resistance to fluid corrosion and mechanical vibration. A multi-point static verification procedure is conducted prior to data collection to guarantee measurement reliability and eliminate zero-point drift. Each transducer is cross-verified against a high-accuracy digital pressure gauge (Fluke 700G06, Fluke Corp., USA, accuracy ±0.05% FS) across 0.5 bar incremental steps over its entire 0 to 3.5 bar operational span. Linear regression applied to the calibration readings confirms that the overall measurement error remained strictly within ≤ 0.5% FS across all channels. The transmembrane test section employs a tubular porous PTFE membrane module with an effective length of 318 mm, an inner diameter of 1.5 mm, and an outer diameter of 2.5 mm. The downstream gas mass flow controller (MC Series, Alicat Scientific, USA) regulated the baseline exhaust gas flow at a steady rate of 10 cm³/min. The controller employs laminar differential-pressure measurement technology and provides simultaneous measurements of mass flow, volumetric flow, temperature, and pressure. The MC Series supports a measurement and control range of 0.01–100% with a turndown ratio of up to 10,000:1 and a control response time as low as 30 ms.

System actuation is performed by driving the liquid flow with a positive-displacement solenoid-driven dosing pump (BL1.5–2, Hanna Instruments, Rhode Island, USA), providing a maximum flow rate of 1.5 L/h at a rated pressure of 13 bar. The pump output is continuously adjustable from 0 to 100% of its maximum capacity. The pump operation is controlled through a Toshiba variable frequency drive (VF-nC3M, Toshiba Schneider Inverter Corp., Japan).

Meanwhile, two NEMA 17 stepper motors (42HS series, Leadshine, China) together with accurate motorized gas and water control valves controlled the gas and liquid orifice openings. All sensor signals are recorded with an industrial control panel based on a Siemens S7–1200 PLC (CPU 1215C DC/DC/DC, Siemens AG, Germany). The 4–20 mA current signals from the two Sensys pressure transducers are digitized by a Siemens SM 1231 AI analog input expansion module (6ES7231–4HF30–0XB0, Siemens AG, Germany) with 13-bit resolution, which converted the analog signals into raw integer values (0 to 27,648) in TIA Portal. The PLC sent the PWM control signals to the stepper drivers and streamed multi-channel sensor data to a host computer via an Ethernet interface at a deterministic sampling rate of 1 Hz.

Experimental conditions and method

All experiments are carried out in a laboratory under strict control where the temperature is maintained at 25 °C, and the pressure at 101.3 kPa, so that any variation in the environment would not affect fluid properties like density and viscosity. In order to avoid any transient effects of the previous trial and maintain identical initial conditions for each experiment, pre-conditioning steps are carried out. The liquid pathway of the transmembrane module is first filled with water until it is fully saturated, and the gauge pressure baseline reading is shown as 0 bar. The gas flow path is subsequently purged using compressed air for 1 s, after which the water pump is run at the predetermined flow rate (0.45, 0.9, or 1.35 L/h). All pressure signals are acquired synchronously by the PLC, which is synchronized to start recording at t = 0 s, corresponding to the instant when the gas and water control valves receive the control commands.

To satisfy the persistency of excitation criterion required for robust non-linear parameter identification, three different command profiles are designed by the Siemens S7–1200 PLC for driving the gas and water control valves. Such trajectories are chosen on the basis of their differing derivatives, allowing sufficient informational content to be provided at low and high frequencies:

•Sine-wave trajectory: The voltage signal applied to the gas and water control valves is sinusoidally amplitude-modulated, affecting an alternate change in the cross-sectionally-averaged flow area of the channel for the fluid between its smallest and largest limits. This trajectory captures fundamental frequency-domain behavior, phase lag, and dynamic coupling between the gas and liquid lines under continuous motion.

•Sawtooth trajectory: The voltage signal applied to the two throttle valves rises linearly from its minimum to maximum limit and then drops sharply back to the baseline, generating a periodic asymmetric ramp profile. The purpose of this trajectory is to use a continuous sweep of the entire operating stroke to detect static nonlinearities, valve dead zones, and flow saturation boundaries.

Limitations

The collected dataset has several limitations. First, all measurements were obtained using a single tubular porous PTFE membrane module with exclusively a CO2 gas and pure water fluid pair under controlled laboratory conditions at 25 ∘C. Consequently, dynamic characteristics such as transmembrane breakthrough pressure, fluid resistance, and cross-coupling gains cannot be directly generalized to contactors employing alternative membrane materials (e.g., PVDF, ceramics), hollow-fiber configurations, or chemical absorbents with differing surface tensions and viscosities. Second, all time-series signals were acquired at a deterministic sampling frequency of 1 Hz for 20 min durations. While this temporal resolution is well-suited for low-frequency macroscopic MIMO system identification, state-space estimation, and process controller design, it is insufficient to capture fast hydraulic transient phenomena, acoustic waves, or microsecond valve flutter dynamics. Finally, the dataset is restricted to three nominal liquid-flow settings of 0.45, 0.90, and 1.35 L/h and a baseline gas flow rate of 10 cm³/min within the investigated pressure range. Instantaneous gas and liquid flow rates and velocities were not recorded as synchronized time-series channels. Consequently, analyses requiring dynamic flow or velocity information would require additional measurements or assumptions beyond the provided dataset.
