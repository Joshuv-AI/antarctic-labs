Complete decongestive therapy for breast cancer-related lymphedema: An evidence summary
Authors: Chenxi Zhu, Mingxuan Cai, Yun Li, Shengqun Hou, Xia Zhang, Jiajia Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo identify, appraise, and synthesize the best available evidence on complete decongestive therapy (CDT) for people with breast cancer-related lymphedema (BCRL).MethodsUsing an evidence-based health care pyramid approach, we searched bibliographic databases, guideline and professional organization websites, and clinical decision support sources from inception to October 1, 2025. Eligibility was defined using the PIPOST framework. Methodological quality was independently appraised by three reviewers for guidelines and by two reviewers for other evidence types using evidence-type-specific appraisal tools. Two reviewers extracted and synthesized the evidence. Evidence levels were assigned using the JBI 2014 system, and recommendation grades were determined through expert-panel assessment of feasibility, appropriateness, meaningfulness, and effectiveness.ResultsThirty-one evidence sources were included, comprising clinical decision support sources, guidelines, evidence summaries, systematic reviews, and expert consensus documents. Thirty-seven evidence statements were synthesized across three domains: implementation principles, the intensive treatment phase, and the maintenance treatment phase. Nineteen statements (51.4%) were supported by Level 1 evidence, and 25 (67.6%) received Grade A recommendations.ConclusionsThis evidence summary provides an evidence-informed framework for CDT across the intensive and maintenance phases, with explicit attention to transition planning and self-management. The evidence is stronger for several core interventions than for implementation strategies and long-term self-management. Further high-quality primary and implementation research is needed.

Introduction

According to the Global Cancer Statistics 2022, breast cancer accounts for a substantial proportion of newly diagnosed cancers worldwide.1 With improving survival rates, breast cancer is increasingly characterized as a chronic condition. Consequently, the prevention and management of treatment-related complications have emerged as critical priorities in oncology rehabilitation and nursing care.

Breast cancer–related lymphedema (BCRL) is a common and serious chronic complication following breast cancer treatment. It primarily results from impaired lymphatic drainage due to axillary procedures (e.g., axillary lymph node dissection and sentinel lymph node biopsy), radiotherapy, and other treatment-related factors. Research indicates that the incidence of upper-limb lymphedema among people who have completed breast cancer treatment exceeds 20% and this risk increases with longer survival.2

Complete decongestive therapy (CDT) is considered the cornerstone of conservative lymphedema management. It integrates four core components—manual lymphatic drainage (MLD), compression therapy, skin care, and therapeutic exercise—within a two-phase strategy comprising an intensive (decongestive) phase and a maintenance phase. CDT has demonstrated significant efficacy in reducing limb volume, alleviating symptoms, and improving quality of life.3 CDT protocols are complex and resource-intensive. Several high-quality clinical practice guidelines, evidence summaries, and systematic reviews have addressed individual components or specific phases of CDT. Despite these advances, several critical gaps remain in the existing evidence base: a lack of guidance on continuity of care from the intensive phase to the maintenance phase, and a lack of actionable recommendations for patient self-management.4,5 As BCRL becomes a chronic condition requiring long-term management, the absence of structured transition and self-management support leads to symptom recurrence, reduced quality of life, and increased health care burden.6,7 An evidence-based implementation framework is urgently needed to guide resource allocation, workforce training, and service delivery, particularly in low-resource and primary care settings where specialized lymphedema services are limited. This study synthesizes high-quality international evidence to define core principles, intensive-phase protocols, maintenance-phase self-management strategies, and implementation approaches for diverse clinical settings. We aim to provide a process-oriented evidence framework for whole-course CDT management in BCRL and to identify key areas for future research.

Methods

Clinical question formulation

The PIPOST framework, developed by the Fudan University JBI Centre for Evidence-Based Nursing,8 was used to structure the evidence-based questions for this study: P (Population): Patients with BCRL; I (Intervention): CDT (including MLD, compression therapy, skin care, and therapeutic exercises); P (Professional): Clinicians administering CDT (including lymphedema therapists and specialized nurses); O (Outcome): Measurable indicators of lymphedema improvement, such as changes in arm circumference, limb volume, and lymphedema-related symptoms; S (Setting): Clinical environments where CDT is applied, specifically lymphedema outpatient clinics, inpatient wards, and patients' homes; T (Type of evidence): High-level evidence sources, including content from clinical decision support systems, clinical practice guidelines, evidence summaries, meta-analyses, systematic reviews, and expert consensus documents.

Sources of evidence and search strategy

We conducted a systematic top-down search following the principles of the evidence-based health care pyramid 5.0. The search resources comprised three main categories: clinical decision support systems and guideline websites, including BMJ Best Practice, UpToDate, the National Cancer Institute (NCI), the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN), the Scottish Intercollegiate Guidelines Network (SIGN), the New Zealand Guidelines Group (NZGG), the Registered Nurses' Association of Ontario (RNAO), the Canadian Medical Association (CMA) Clinical Practice Guidelines website, the China Guideline Clearinghouse (CGC), and the Medlive Clinical Guidelines website; evidence summary databases and professional association websites, such as the JBI Evidence Summary database, ACP Journal Club, the Oncology Nursing Society (ONS), the American Physical Therapy Association (APTA), Cancer Care Ontario (CCO), the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO), the National Lymphedema Network (NLN), and the International Society of Lymphology (ISL); and domestic and international journal databases, including the Cochrane Library, PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, CINAHL, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang Data, VIP Database, and the China Biomedical Literature Database (CBM).

Search strategy

A systematic search strategy was developed using a combination of MeSH terms and free-text keywords, based on the key elements of the PIPOST framework, to identify literature on CDT for BCRL. Studies published from the inception of each database through October 1, 2025, were included. The complete search strategy for PubMed is shown in the Supplementary Files.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria for guidelines: (1) Clinical practice guidelines involving patients with BCRL; (2) Guidelines published from database inception to October 1, 2025; for updated guidelines, the latest version was included; (3) Guidelines published in Chinese or English. Exclusion criteria for guidelines: (1) Chinese versions of guidelines directly translated from foreign guidelines; (2) Guidelines with incomplete content or information; (3) Duplicate guidelines or those with poor quality evaluation.

Inclusion criteria for other types of literature: (1) The target population involves patients with BCRL; (2) The intervention is CDT (including MLD, compression therapy, skin care, and therapeutic exercises); (3) Published from database inception to October 1, 2025; (4) Published in Chinese or English. Exclusion criteria for other types of literature: (1) Literature with incomplete data; (2) Literature for which full text is unavailable.

After the initial search, relevant records were imported into reference management software to remove duplicates. Two reviewers independently screened the titles, abstracts, and full texts based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria to identify eligible articles, followed by cross-checking. Disagreements were resolved by consultation with a third reviewer.

Quality assessment

Methodological quality was independently appraised by three reviewers for guidelines and by two reviewers for other evidence types using evidence-type-specific appraisal tools. Disagreements during the evaluation process were resolved through team discussion. Priority was given to including higher-quality evidence, with additional consideration given to publication recency and the authority of the source journal. Clinical practice guidelines were assessed by the Appraisal of Guidelines for Research and Evaluation II (AGREE II) instrument.9 Systematic reviews and expert consensus statements were assessed using the corresponding critical appraisal tools from the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Evidence-Based Health care Center (2016 version).10 For evidence summaries, the original studies cited within them were retrieved and appraised. The type of source document determined the selection of the appropriate JBI critical appraisal checklist for evaluation.11

Evidence synthesis and grading

Evidence extraction and integration. Consistent with the evidence-based health care pyramid 5.0 (the “5S” model), we prioritized pre-appraised, synthesized evidence sources—including clinical decision support systems (UpToDate, BMJ Best Practice), clinical practice guidelines, evidence summaries, and systematic reviews. Evidence extraction and synthesis were performed collaboratively by two reviewers through discussion. The synthesis process adhered to the following hierarchical principles: 1) when evidence from different sources conflicted, preference was given to evidence from higher-quality sources and more recently published literature; 2) when evidence was complementary and of a similar level, it was logically combined into a coherent body of evidence; 3) when identical evidence was presented across multiple sources, the wording that was both professionally accurate and readily understandable was prioritized.

Evidence level and recommendation grade. Evidence was graded using the JBI Pre-grading System (2014 version), with levels ranging from 1 (highest) to 5 (lowest). For guidelines and evidence summaries, the level was assigned based on the primary studies cited. Recommendation grades (A = strong, B = weak) were determined by an expert panel through integrated assessment of JBI level and FAME (Feasibility, Appropriateness, Meaningfulness, Effectiveness) evaluation. The expert panel included three evaluators, all of whom held a bachelor's degree or above, with a mean of 19.3 years of clinical or research experience, and expertise covering BCRL nursing, evidence-based nursing, and nursing management. In addition, there was one facilitator with a methodological background and one third-party evaluator. Three evaluators independently rated each piece of evidence across the four FAME dimensions using a 3-point scale (1 = low, 2 = moderate, 3 = high). Items with any dimension rated “low” or a total score ≤ 9/12 were discussed in panel meetings; disagreements were resolved through moderated discussion or, if needed, by third-party adjudication. Grade A was assigned if: (a) Level 1–2 evidence with FAME score ≥ 10/12, or (b) Level 3–5 evidence with FAME score ≥ 11/12 when addressing patient safety, contraindications, core CDT principles, or self-management behaviors with consistent support from authoritative sources. Grade B was assigned in all other cases. The JBI evidence level reflects the methodological design of the primary studies, whereas the recommendation grade represents the expert panel's integrated judgment combining the JBI level with FAME-based clinical applicability. Thus, the two are not directly equivalent.

These consisted of 8 clinical decision support tools/clinical practice guidelines, 8 evidence summaries, 10 systematic reviews, and 5 expert consensus documents. Summary and description of evidence

A total of 37 best evidence items were identified and categorized into three domains: implementation principles, intensive treatment phase, and maintenance treatment phase. Implementation principles encompass the fundamental requirements for CDT delivery, the target population, the core components of the therapy, and risk assessment. Intensive treatment phase covers the recommended treatment cycle and the key implementation points of each of the four core CDT components (manual lymphatic drainage, compression therapy, skin care, and therapeutic exercise) during this phase. Maintenance treatment phase addresses the optimal timing for transitioning to this phase, the adapted application of the four core measures, the role of adjunctive therapies, follow-up and monitoring protocols, and strategies for patient education and self-management. The level of evidence for the included recommendations ranges from 1a to 5b. Each evidence item was further evaluated using the FAME framework, which assesses feasibility, appropriateness, meaningfulness, and effectiveness for clinical application. Based on a synthesis of the evidence level and its FAME attributes, a recommendation grade was assigned to each item: Grade A (strong recommendation) or Grade B (weak recommendation). Discussion

Main findings

This evidence summary integrates 37 evidence items with their graded recommendations across three core domains: implementation principles, intensive-phase interventions, and maintenance-phase strategies. Among the 37 evidence items, 19 (51.4%) were classified as Level 1 evidence, and 25 (67.6%) received a Grade A recommendation. Some Grade A recommendations were based on lower-level evidence, including contraindications, core CDT principles, and essential self-management behaviors. This is mainly due to ethical constraints or the complexity of long-term interventions, which make high-quality studies difficult to conduct. However, these recommendations largely reflect their critical clinical importance, as well as consistent endorsement from authoritative sources and expert consensus.

Unlike modality-focused systematic reviews,40 the core contribution of this summary is a whole-course CDT framework that links implementation principles, intensive-phase interventions, maintenance-phase strategies, and self-management support. This framework offers three specific advances beyond existing guidelines and systematic reviews. First, it provides an integrated evidence framework reflecting CDT as a longitudinal care pathway. Second, it operationalizes the intensive-to-maintenance transition through explicit timing criteria, compression adjustments, and adjunctive therapy roles. Third, it integrates patient self-management as a core evidence domain, offering graded recommendations on follow-up frequency, self-monitoring, and personalized education—content that is essential for long-term adherence yet often relegated to general statements in existing guidelines. Collectively, these contributions transform fragmented evidence into an actionable, continuity-oriented clinical roadmap. The following subsections discuss the practical implementation of this framework in multidisciplinary systems, intensive-phase optimization, maintenance-phase patient-centered care, and translation to diverse clinical settings.

Establishment of a standardized, multidisciplinary CDT implementation system

Effective CDT delivery requires a structured implementation system. This system rests on three foundational elements. Evidence items 1–3 emphasize that certified therapists should perform core interventions (MLD, compression) and collaborate with breast surgery, rehabilitation, and oncology specialists to establish a coordinated multidisciplinary care pathway covering prevention, assessment, intervention, and follow-up. This is especially critical when contraindications (active infection, DVT, uncontrolled heart failure, or metastasis) are present, where multidisciplinary evaluation is paramount for risk-stratified treatment plans.41 Many settings lack such integrated mechanisms, highlighting the need for referral networks.42,43

CDT must follow a phased approach (Evidence 5–8), with individualized treatment plans. The intensive phase aims for maximal volume reduction,36 while the maintenance phase focuses on preventing fluid reaccumulation and preserving quality of life.14,27 In terms of risk assessment, Evidence items 9–11 on contraindications are largely derived from expert consensus documents.13,15,39 The potential risk of MLD promoting tumor recurrence in active disease remains debated.3 Patients with metastatic disease require pre-treatment multidisciplinary evaluation to weigh benefits and risks.21

Optimizing the intensive treatment phase: tailoring protocols to disease severity and patient need

The intensive phase aims to achieve maximal volume reduction through sufficient, continuous, and standardized treatment (Evidence 12–27). Given the complexity of intervention and heterogeneity across studies, establishing a single optimal regimen remains challenging. However, the evidence broadly supports a concentrated strategy of “five sessions per week for 2–4 weeks” during the intensive phase, leveraging cumulative lymphodynamic effects and allowing therapists to adjust based on individual patient responses.16,27

Among the core interventions, both MLD and compression therapy are effective in reducing limb volume, although their efficacy as standalone treatments does not differ significantly.16 For mild-to-moderate BCRL, an adequate course of MLD (≥ 20 sessions or > 2 weeks) yields more pronounced edema control; however, it cannot substitute for the essential role of compression therapy in advanced stages.22,30 The effectiveness of compression therapy is stage-dependent. In early-stage disease, sustained compression can facilitate lymphatic return and alleviate edema, whereas moderate-to-severe lymphedema is often characterized by tissue fibrosis and adipose deposition, where the primary goal is to control progression and maintain stability.32 During the intensive phase, multi-layer short-stretch bandaging typically achieves significant decongestive effects but is technically demanding, necessitating enhanced guidance for patients and caregivers.

As foundational components, therapeutic exercise and skin care should be integrated throughout the treatment trajectory. Therapeutic exercise should ideally be undertaken under professional guidance, targeting not only the affected limb but also engaging large muscle groups throughout the body. Multimodal exercise approaches, particularly resistance training, are recommended and should be combined with compression therapy.12 Progressive resistance training is safe and effective, promoting lymphatic flow through skeletal muscle contraction.21,37 Skin care primarily aims to prevent secondary infections,23 and patient education is paramount to enhance self-observation, skin hygiene, and moisturization.3,13,37

Adopting a patient-centered approach in the maintenance phase: emphasizing adherence, continuity, and self-management

The maintenance phase focuses on transitioning from therapist-led to self-managed care supported by clinicians. Evidence 28 underscores that this transition should begin once edema reduction stabilizes. Currently, high-quality evidence on optimal intervention intensity during the maintenance phase remains limited, so treatment protocols require dynamic adjustment based on individual patient response.

Regarding specific interventions, Evidence 29 recommends transitioning from bandaging to daytime compression garments (e.g., sleeves, gauntlets) to reduce treatment burden, with nocturnal bandaging if needed. Systematic follow-up and self-management education are paramount for long-term success (Evidence 32–37). Follow-up frequency should be tailored to edema severity: mild cases every 3–6 months, moderate-to-severe cases more frequently.13 In patient education, clinicians should operationalize “risk avoidance” into specific behaviors: avoiding blood pressure, venipuncture, or injection on the affected arm when possible; limiting heat and immobility; avoiding constrictive items; avoiding heavy lifting without guidance; and maintaining a healthy weight.3,13,37 These precautions minimize stress on the compromised lymphatic system and reduce the risk of exacerbation.

Translating evidence into clinical practice: practical considerations

While the 37 evidence items provide an evidence-informed framework for CDT delivery, their implementation in real-world clinical settings faces substantial practical challenges. Here we discuss four key considerations for translating this evidence into practice, particularly in low-resource or primary care contexts.

Implementing CDT in low-resource or primary care settings. Full CDT is resource-intensive and may not be feasible in primary care or low-resource settings.42 In such contexts, a tiered or stepped-care model is recommended.45 For mild lymphedema (ISL stage I) or people at risk of BCRL, primary care providers could focus on foundational interventions: patient education, simplified self-MLD, and affordable compression options (e.g., adjustable wraps). For moderate-to-severe cases, referral pathways to specialized centers may be considered where feasible, using intermittent intensive blocks combined with home-based maintenance. Task-sharing with trained community health workers or nurses may also expand access, provided that clear protocols are in place.45,46

Practical barriers to long-term adherence and follow-up. Long-term adherence to maintenance-phase CDT is challenging. Common barriers include physical discomfort, financial constraints, low health literacy, forgetfulness, and lack of social support.47 To address these, clinicians could adopt shared decision-making when selecting compression products. A graded introduction of compression may improve tolerance. For follow-up, patients could use standardized self-assessment tools to reduce clinic visits. Structured follow-up protocols could be integrated into nursing workflows.

The role of telehealth and other support tools. Telehealth may represent a promising avenue to support CDT delivery, particularly for rural or underserved patients. Video-based instruction may help guide patients and caregivers in performing self-MLD and applying compression.48 Remote follow-up consultations may reduce travel burden. Emerging evidence supports mobile health (mHealth) tools for self-monitoring and reminders. Social media-based education groups (e.g., via WeChat or WhatsApp) may enhance peer support, though privacy must be managed.49 Telehealth is best used to complement, not replace, in-person assessments when complications are suspected.

The specific role of specialist nurses in coordinating CDT care. Specialist nurses can play an important and often under-recognized role in CDT delivery. Their responsibilities may extend to: risk assessment and early detection; patient education and self-management training; coordination of multidisciplinary team communication; follow-up monitoring and adherence support; and psychosocial support.45,50 Nurse-led lymphedema clinics or advanced practice nurse roles have been described as promising models for enhancing care coordination and patient education. These roles could be integrated into cancer survivorship programs.51

Importantly, the specialist nurse may assume a leadership role in coordinating the multidisciplinary team, driving the development of standardized protocols, and advocating for patient-centered care pathways. This leadership could extend to training other health care professionals, conducting regular team meetings, and serving as the primary point of contact for both patients and referring physicians. Establishing a formal nurse-led lymphedema service with designated clinical nurse specialists may be a valuable model for optimizing CDT implementation and continuity of care, though local feasibility and context should guide adoption.

Limitations

This evidence synthesis has several limitations. First, considerable heterogeneity existed across the included studies in terms of patient staging, specific intervention combinations, treatment frequency, and outcome measures. This variability, inherent to the complexity of CDT research, posed challenges for evidence synthesis and cross-study comparison, and consequently limited the precision with which some recommendations could be formulated. Second, while the synthesized evidence provides a robust framework for clinical practice, the interventions may not be fully adaptable to all health care systems or resource settings. The feasibility and applicability of certain recommendations, particularly in primary care or resource-limited environments, require further investigation and contextual validation. Third, existing research has predominantly focused on the efficacy of individual CDT components (e.g., MLD or compression) rather than on implementation strategies for delivering CDT effectively across different care settings. This gap limits the direct applicability of our recommendations, as we can advise on what to implement but less on how to implement it across diverse settings. Future implementation science research is urgently needed to address this gap. Fourth, our top-down approach prioritizing synthesized evidence over primary RCTs is appropriate for implementation-focused evidence summaries but may have missed emerging findings from recent RCTs not yet incorporated into guidelines or evidence summaries.

Conclusions

This study, grounded in systematic evidence-based methodology, synthesized a substantial body of literature to generate 37 best evidence items for CDT in patients with BCRL. The evidence is structured across three core domains: overarching implementation principles, key interventions during the intensive treatment phase, and essential components of the maintenance phase and long-term management. By focusing on CDT across the full care continuum, this evidence summary delineates the distinct therapeutic goals and implementation priorities for each phase, thereby providing synthesized evidence to support and standardize clinical practice. By explicitly operationalizing the intensive-to-maintenance phase transition and providing phase-specific recommendations, this evidence summary directly addresses the continuity gap identified in the existing literature. However, the review also identified that the evidence underpinning the foundational aspects of CDT delivery and patient self-management strategies remains relatively weak. Furthermore, practical implementation strategies—including tiered care models for low-resource settings, telehealth integration, and the formalization of specialist nurse roles—are essential to bridge the gap between evidence and real-world clinical practice. To address these gaps and improve long-term outcomes for patients with BCRL, future research efforts should prioritize high-quality primary studies and rigorous implementation science investigations to facilitate the translation and sustained application of this evidence into diverse clinical settings. Our synthesis also highlights that the maintenance phase and self-management support remain relatively under-evidenced, representing a priority area for future implementation research.
