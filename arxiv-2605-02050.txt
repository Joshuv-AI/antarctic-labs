Principles and Guidelines for Randomized Controlled Trials in AI Evaluation
Christopher Kelly †‡, Angelica Chowdhury ‡, Alexandra Campili ‡, Bimpe Ayoola §, Devin Barbour §, Thomas Chen Dawson §, Ze Shen Chin ∘ \circ, Rokas Gipiškis ∘ \circ

Abstract
This work establishes a foundational framework for standardizing AI evaluation RCTs (sometimes called human uplift studies). Drawing on established experimental practices from disciplines with established RCT traditions, including software engineering, economics, clinical and health sciences, and psychology, we adopt the ( 29 ) four-validity framework and extend it with a fifth principle on transparency, repeatability, and verification adapted from the Transparency and Openness Promotion (TOP) Guidelines ( 8 ) . We operationalize all five principles into 33 guidelines adapted for AI evaluation RCT contexts, expressed as requirements with rationales, implementation instructions, and evidence bases. We position the principles and guidelines as serving three key roles for AI evaluation RCTs: a design tool for planning studies, an evaluation rubric for assessing existing work, and a blueprint for standard setting as the field converges on norms. Our framework extends prior work by centering evaluation on human performance rather than model output alone, formalizing causal inference through RCT methodology for AI contexts, integrating heterogeneity analysis and practical significance assessment, implementing a graded transparency and repeatability framework, and addressing AI-specific challenges including model versioning, human-AI interaction dynamics, contamination and spillover effects, and equitable impact assessment.

Related Work and Gaps

Several efforts have attempted to establish standards for AI evaluation, though none adequately address the unique challenges of AI evaluation RCTs.

STARE-HI
(
31
)
represents an early effort to structure reporting standards for evaluation studies of complex digital systems in healthcare. It emphasizes that evaluations should capture not just technical performance, but also context, user interaction, workflow integration, and unintended consequences. However, it predates the rise of machine learning and adaptive AI systems and therefore lacks provisions for model evolution, data drift, prompting, and domain-specific constraints within healthcare.

CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials)
(
28
)
is a widely adopted checklist for reporting randomized controlled trials, designed to ensure transparent and complete reporting of trial design, conduct, and outcomes. CONSORT-AI
(
17
)
extends this framework for clinical AI interventions, adding 14 new items to address algorithm versioning, training data provenance, model update policies, and human-AI interaction during clinical trial execution. SPIRIT (Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials)
(
9
)
provides complementary guidance for clinical trial protocol development, specifying what information should be included before a trial begins. SPIRIT-AI
(
27
)
adapts this for AI contexts, requiring pre-specification of the AI system’s intended use, input data, human-AI interface, and plans for handling model updates. Both frameworks assume medical contexts with static interventions, clinical outcomes, and well-established regulatory oversight.

STREAM
(
18
)
introduces a structured framework for reporting evaluations of AI models, initially focusing on chemical and biological threat assessment. It defines criteria across six domains aimed at improving reproducibility, comparability, and accountability. However, STREAM explicitly excludes AI evaluation RCTs, focusing instead on benchmark evaluations.

The RAND Perspective
(
25
)
is a set of preliminary best practice recommendations that identifies challenges in broader AI evaluation efforts: weak construct validity, inconsistent measurement of real-world risk, and insufficient reproducibility. RAND proposes cross-cutting best practices drawn from disciplines such as psychology, economics, and biology, organized around the four life-cycle stages of evaluation: design, implementation, execution, and documentation. Although preliminary, the RAND framework directly calls for clearly set-out best practices guiding evaluation of GPAI systems.

Complementing these normative frameworks,
26
provide empirical grounding through interviews with 16 expert practitioners who have conducted human uplift RCTs across domains including biosecurity, cybersecurity, education, and labor. Their findings document a recurring tension between standard causal inference assumptions and the distinctive properties of frontier AI systems: rapidly evolving models, shifting user baselines, heterogeneous AI literacy, and porous real-world settings that strain internal, external, and construct validity. Where Paskov et al. identify and map practitioner-experienced challenges, our work provides a normative framework of principles and operationalized guidelines designed to address precisely those challenges.

Several gaps remain when adapting these frameworks to AI evaluation RCT contexts. Treatment definition and versioning is inadequately addressed because many existing guidelines assume static interventions, while AI systems evolve rapidly through updates, prompt changes, or newly fine-tuned variants. Spillover and interference are similarly neglected, as existing frameworks do not account for the potential for unsanctioned model use in multi-user AI environments, which can violate classical RCT assumptions
(
16
)
. Human-AI interaction reporting is largely absent from existing frameworks, which do not provide guidance on documenting user instructions, degree of autonomy, or how outputs were modified or accepted. Construct validity is underspecified for AI contexts, where standard performance metrics, such as accuracy or completion time, may poorly reflect intended outcomes like reasoning quality, trust, or creativity
(
14
)
. Finally, reproducibility is under-addressed: many AI models are closed-source, meaning existing transparency provisions designed around model weights and training data do not translate to API-based systems, where reproducibility depends on logs, prompts, and metadata
(
25
)
.

Principles & Guidelines Overview

Our work rests on five principles: four drawn from the Shadish-Cook-Campbell validity framework
(
29
)
and a fifth adapted from the Transparency and Openness Promotion (TOP) Guidelines
(
8
)
. These principles function as interpretive tools for determining what claims a given study can and cannot support.

Each principle also addresses a distinct dimension of what can undermine the credibility and validity of research conclusions.
Appendix C

(
29

and extended with AI evaluation RCT-specific examples. It includes the name of the threat, a description of the threat, and an example of what that could look like in AI evaluation RCTs. This framework thus helps readers calibrate confidence in reported findings and identify the specific inferential boundaries that a study’s methods impose.

3.1
Construct Validity

The construct validity principle asks whether outcome measures in an AI evaluation RCT actually capture the underlying constructs of interest, such as coding competence, diagnostic reasoning, or writing quality, rather than narrow proxies that are easy to score but misaligned with the real capability question. This follows the classic view of construct validity as the degree to which a measure supports appropriate inferences about an underlying attribute
(
10
;
29
)
.

In settings involving AI, construct validity is strained by both the fact that AI tools can change the nature of the task itself and the temptation to focus on metrics like speed or token counts that models can optimize for without genuinely enhancing human skill. We highlight threats such as construct underrepresentation, where outcomes only cover the slice of performance that the model makes easier, and construct-irrelevant variance, where AI boosts scores through superficial fluency or verbosity. When construct validity is weak, a study may show that scores increased with AI, but it cannot credibly claim that human capability in the targeted domain has improved.

3.2
Internal Validity

The internal validity principle concerns whether observed differences between conditions can be credibly attributed to access to a specific AI system rather than to confounders
(
29
)
. In AI evaluation RCTs, internal validity is threatened not only by familiar issues such as selection, attrition, and concurrent external events, but also by AI-specific phenomena: contamination of control groups through unsanctioned model use, spillovers and interference within teams or classes, model updates during the study, and differential compliance with AI usage instructions.

Our treatment of this principle catalogs these threats and emphasizes design choices, such as aligning the unit of randomization with the interference structure, monitoring compliance, and pinning model versions. When a study lacks internal validity, it may still offer useful descriptive information about performance under specific conditions, but it cannot support clear causal conclusions about the effect of providing versus withholding access to AI.

3.3
External Validity

The external validity principle focuses on to whom, for what tasks, and in which settings the findings of an AI evaluation RCT are intended to generalize. External validity is often described as the question of whether a causal relationship holds over variations in persons, settings, treatments, and outcomes
(
29
)
.

In our setting, this includes careful definition of the target population (novice versus expert practitioners, AI-naive versus AI-literate users), the task and domain, and the organizational and technical context in which AI is used. Threats include heavy reliance on convenience or WEIRD (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic) samples, short artificial tasks that do not reflect real work, deployment contexts that differ radically from study conditions, and AI systems that are loosely specified or rapidly changing. For each study, we articulate an explicit inferential scope: results may legitimately inform expectations about one population in one context but not another.

3.4
Statistical Conclusion Validity

The statistical conclusion validity principle addresses whether quantitative evidence is sufficient and appropriately interpreted for any claimed effect. Shadish, Cook, and Campbell treat statistical conclusion validity as distinct from internal validity, focusing on issues such as low power, unreliability, and violations of statistical assumptions
(
29
)
.

In AI evaluation RCTs, this encompasses evidentiary thresholds for novel claims, power and precision (especially for heterogeneity and interaction effects), appropriate treatment of multiple outcomes and analyses, and a shift from dichotomous significance testing toward estimation and graded evidence. We set
\alpha=0.005
for novel causal claims, emphasize effect size estimation with confidence intervals, and require sensitivity analyses. Threats include underpowered designs, noisy or low-reliability measures, extensive researcher degrees of freedom without pre-specification, and over-interpretation of fragile or marginal results.

GPAI uplift studies are particularly vulnerable to opacity. Key details about model family and version, prompts and settings, fine-tuning data, interface design, usage policies, compliance monitoring, and deviations from original research and analysis plans are often under-reported or omitted entirely. Transparency and repeatability are especially critical in this context for four reasons. First, AI evaluation RCT research informs high-stakes decisions about deployment, workforce impacts, and policy, and must establish credibility through verifiable, repeatable findings. Evidence from the social and behavioral sciences suggests these are not minor concerns: only about half of published claims replicate with the same pattern when tested with new data, and only about one-third of reanalyses closely match original results
(
32
;
2
)
. Second, the many hidden degrees of freedom in AI evaluation RCTs—model specifications, prompt engineering, preprocessing decisions, analytical choices—create substantial opacity without active transparency efforts. Third, rapid AI system evolution means that without detailed documentation, studies become impossible to interpret or reproduce as systems change. Fourth, research errors are common: approximately one quarter of articles in fields with data and code sharing requirements contain non-trivial coding errors
(
6
)
, and transparency enables their detection and correction. Critically, journals requiring data sharing empirically produce more reproducible results
(
20
)
, suggesting that the specific practices mandated by this framework are not merely normative recommendations but empirically supported interventions.

We implement a four-level transparency, repeatability, and verification framework in which the strength of causal claims should scale with verifiability. The first three levels form a genuine hierarchy in which each builds on the previous:
Level 1, Disclosure
, requires that researchers report whether each open science practice was conducted and, if so, how—enabling critical readers to assess what was and was not done.
Level 2, Sharing
, requires that the outputs of each practice are deposited in accessible locations and linked in the paper, so that independent reuse and reproduction become practically possible; data sharing requirements empirically improve reproducibility
(
20
)
.
Level 3, Verification
, requires that an independent party—a journal, auditor, or registered report process—confirms that what was claimed at Levels 1 and 2 is genuinely and completely fulfilled, including that code runs and produces the reported outputs and that pre-registrations are complete and timestamped before data collection.
Level 4, Repeatability
, groups three partially independent dimensions that together provide the strongest transparency, repeatability, and verification evidence
(
23
;
2
)
:
Reproducibility
(same data, same analysis) requires that independent parties rerun the analysis on shared data and code and confirm reported results—the minimum form of repeatability, but insufficient alone.
Robustness
(same data, different analysis) requires that independent parties apply alternative justifiable analytical choices and assess whether qualitative conclusions hold; large-scale evidence shows that while 74% of reanalyses reach the same qualitative conclusion as originals, only 34% closely match the original quantitative estimates
(
2
)
.
Replicability
(new data, same question) requires that independent teams test the same research question with new participants and settings; replication effect sizes in the social and behavioral sciences are typically less than half the magnitude of original estimates
(
32
)
, suggesting that single-study AI evaluation RCT findings warrant corresponding caution. These three dimensions are empirically near-zero correlated and should not be assumed to imply one another
(
23
)
.

Studies meeting only Level 1 warrant cautious interpretation; studies achieving Level 3 verification and Level 4 repeatability assessment provide substantially stronger grounds for belief in reported effects. The appropriate strength of causal claims should scale with which dimensions of repeatability have been assessed.

3.7
Extensions Beyond Prior Work

Our framework extends prior work in several ways. On theory of change and failure modes, where RAND briefly mentions mechanisms and other frameworks focus on reporting or evaluation rigor, we explicitly articulate collecting data on mechanisms cause and effect, linking AI outputs to human behavior, and listing plausible failure modes to guide design and interpretation. On heterogeneity, where prior work mentions stratification but lacks rigorous pre-specification and power planning, we pre-specify subgroups including baseline skill, AI literacy, demographics, and task characteristics, and we calculate power for interactions while distinguishing exploratory from confirmatory analyses. On AI-specific contamination and spillover, where prior work offers limited guidance on human-AI interaction contamination, we introduce spillover-aware designs, monitoring of AI usage, contamination prevention, and cluster randomization to handle real-world AI diffusion. On practical significance, where prior work emphasizes statistical significance with limited guidance on interpretation, we integrate practical significance, cost-benefit framing, equity and heterogeneity impact, ceiling and floor effects, sustainability, and domain-contextual interpretation. On evidentiary thresholds, where standard
\alpha=0.05
thresholds are common, we set
\alpha=0.005
for novel causal claims and emphasize effect size estimation, confidence intervals, and sensitivity to small-sample biases. On AI literacy stratification, where prior work defines population by domain or general demographics, we incorporate AI literacy stratification, explicit screening for AI experience, and non-WEIRD population sampling to improve external validity and heterogeneity analysis.

4
Applications

We intend for these principles and guidelines to function as shared infrastructure for the emerging field of AI evaluation RCTs. We see three main use cases.

4.1
Design Aid for Researchers

Researchers planning new AI evaluation RCTs can use the guidelines as a pre-study checklist, prompting explicit decisions about the unit of randomization, contamination controls, heterogeneity analysis, evidentiary thresholds, transparency commitments, and other methodological choices. The guidelines operationalize abstract principles into concrete implementation steps accessible to engineers and applied scientists without extensive RCT training.

4.2
Evaluation Rubric for Reviewers and Institutions

Funders, journals, policymakers, and internal governance bodies can use the framework as a review rubric. Linking guidelines to overarching principles allows readers to better understand the consequences of failing to meet a given standard. The principles sit at a higher level than individual checklist items, articulating which causal, measurement, and generalization claims are warranted. Readers can see why a particular omission matters and how it shapes the strength and scope of a study’s claims.

4.3
Field-Level Standard Setting

The principles and guidelines can act as a roadmap for field-level standard setting. The detailed guideline list can be selectively elevated into formal requirements (such as mandatory pre-registration or minimum reporting items) as the field matures and institutions converge on norms. These three use cases all lead to the kind of credible knowledge production that is necessary for evidence-based policies and better AI safety frameworks.

5
Limitations and Future Work

Our framework focuses exclusively on experimental designs for assessing causal effects of AI systems on human outcomes. This deliberate scope limitation means we do not address observational studies, purely benchmark-based evaluations, or quasi-experimental designs that may be more feasible in certain organizational contexts. The comprehensive nature of our guidelines, particularly requirements for pre-registration, multi-modal data collection, heterogeneity analysis with adequate statistical power, and tiered transparency frameworks, imposes substantial resource demands that smaller research teams may find challenging.

AI systems evolve at a rate that outpace traditional experimental timeframes. Our guidelines address model versioning and drift monitoring, but the fundamental challenge of evaluating rapidly changing systems persists. As AI capabilities advance toward more agentic and autonomous systems, human-AI interaction dynamics may shift in ways our current frameworks cannot fully anticipate.

This framework has not yet been validated through systematic community review, pilot implementation, or empirical assessment of whether adherence actually improves research quality. Future work should develop implementation tools such as pre-registration templates and statistical power calculators tailored to AI studies, conduct empirical research examining how well these guidelines predict research quality, and create domain-specific adaptations of the core framework. Given the nascent state of AI evaluation science, we anticipate iterative refinement through practical application and dialogue among researchers, practitioners, policymakers, and affected communities.

6
Conclusion

The advancement of AI capabilities demands evaluation practices that are both rigorous and responsive to the unique challenges these systems present. This work establishes a cross-disciplinary framework for standardizing AI evaluation research, synthesizing established experimental practices into actionable guidance structured around five core principles: construct validity, internal validity, external validity, statistical conclusion validity, and transparency, repeatability, and verification.

We extend prior reporting standards by formalizing causal reasoning through RCT methodology adapted for AI’s dynamic nature, integrating heterogeneity analysis with adequate statistical power, implementing stringent evidentiary thresholds, establishing a graded transparency framework, and addressing AI-specific challenges including model versioning, contamination effects, and equitable impact assessment. Rigorous, transparent, and repeatable evaluation practices are essential safeguards ensuring that AI development proceeds on credible evidence, that benefits are distributed equitably, that risks are identified proactively, and that claims about AI capabilities align with empirical reality.

Impact Statement

This paper presents work whose goal is to advance the field of AI Safety. By establishing rigorous standards for evaluating how AI systems affect human performance, we aim to improve the credibility of evidence informing deployment decisions, safety assessments, and governance policies. There are many potential societal consequences of our work, none which we feel must be specifically highlighted here.

Define the subset of humans the study represents using relevant dimensions: domain expertise and AI literacy. Justify why this population was chosen for the research question. Report how well the achieved sample compares to the target population, both overall and by treatment arm.

Vague population definitions (e.g., “users,” “workers”) prevent generalizability assessment. AI effects vary substantially by expertise and prior AI exposure; sample composition is critical for interpretation.

Design; Reporting; Discussion

EV; CV

Implement participant quality control

Apply pre-specified inclusion/exclusion criteria: domain knowledge tests, attention checks, language proficiency verification, technical setup verification, performance-based thresholds. Post-hoc exclusions must be pre-registered. Report all exclusions with counts and reasons. Conduct sensitivity analyses with and without exclusions.

Inattentive or unqualified participants introduce noise, reduce power, and can bias effect estimates. AI tools may mask participant quality, making QC especially critical in RCT studies. Design; Analysis; Reporting

IV; SCV; EV

Design; Analysis; Reporting

IV; CV; SCV

Design; Analysis; Reporting

CV; IV; EV

Design; Analysis; Reporting

CV; IV

19

State study design, population, intervention, framework, and phase in title and abstract

Title must specify: study design (e.g., parallel-group RCT), population (e.g., “novice programmers”), AI tool, and comparison condition. Abstract must state conceptual framework (superiority, non-inferiority, equivalence, or exploratory) and trial phase (proof-of-concept, efficacy, effectiveness, or implementation). Avoid vague language (e.g., “we examine AI’s effects”).

Descriptive titles and abstracts enable rapid relevance assessment, evidence synthesis, and detection of outcome switching or protocol deviations. Framework statements prevent misinterpretation of exploratory findings as confirmatory evidence.

Design; Reporting; Pre-Registration

State population definition with relevant dimensions (expertise, AI literacy). List inclusion criteria (qualification thresholds, technical requirements, availability) and exclusion criteria (prior exposure, conflicts of interest, technical limitations). Define “screened,” “enrolled,” “completed,” and “analyzed.” Report AI literacy stratification approach.

Design; Reporting

EV; TRV; IV

Pre-specify harm categories: skill atrophy, automation complacency, over-reliance, de-skilling, workflow disruption, equity effects, psychological impacts. Publication bias already favors positive findings; selective non-reporting of harms compounds this. AI harms may be cognitive/psychological and emerge only after sustained use or tool removal.

Design; Pre-Registration

CV; IV; EV

25

Establish causality through RCT design with carefully controlled conditions

Use RCTs as gold standard. Ensure conditions are identical except for AI access. Specify and justify control condition (no tool, placebo interface, alternative tool, or standard practice). Identify and address AI-specific confounds: Hawthorne effects, novelty effects, differential motivation. Avoid bundling AI with other changes (better UI, additional training). When RCTs are infeasible, use strongest available quasi-experimental design (RD, DiD, IV) and explicitly discuss assumptions required.

Voluntary AI adoption creates severe selection bias in observational studies. Random assignment is the only credible method for estimating the causal effect of making AI available.

Design

IV; CV; SCV

Define the Overall Evaluation Criterion (OEC) before the experiment begins

Pre-specify a single primary outcome (or pre-registered composite with justified weighting) representing what “success” means. Distinguish primary outcomes (confirmatory, adequately powered) from secondary outcomes (potentially exploratory). Align OEC with deployment decision type. Document minimum detectable effect and practical significance threshold tied to OEC. Do not change primary outcome after seeing data; if necessary, report both original and modified analyses.

Without a pre-specified OEC, researchers face large temptation to explore multiple outcomes and report selectively. Pre-committed composites force explicit tradeoff specification before results are known.

Design; Pre-Registration

SCV; CV; IV

Assess inter-rater reliability, test-retest reliability, and internal consistency. Validate rubrics against expert judgment. Check for ceiling/floor effects. Test full technical infrastructure (randomization system, AI integration, timing). Report all pilot findings and resulting protocol modifications.

Standardized instruments for measuring AI’s impact on human performance largely do not exist; bespoke measures require validation. AI tool integration introduces technical failure modes detectable only through piloting.

Design; Pre-Registration

CV; SCV; IV

Satisfy ethical requirements including informed consent appropriate for digital studies

Use electronic consent with affirmative action (no pre-checked boxes). Allow granular consent for different data uses. Include comprehension checks. Online and remote studies face unique consent challenges; AI-specific elements require explicit disclosure. Ethics review and informed consent are foundational to RCT validity and participant protection.

Design; Reporting; Pre-Registration

TRV; IV; EV

29

Conduct and report robustness checks and sensitivity analyses

Check robustness to: alternative model specifications, different covariate adjustments, alternative variable measures, different subsamples (outlier exclusion), missing data handling, and functional form. Examine sensitivity to assumption violations, influential observations, timing of effects, AI usage heterogeneity, compliance variation, learning effects, task order effects, and AI system changes during study. Report all sensitivity analyses; distinguish pre-specified from post-hoc.

Every analysis involves judgment calls. Findings robust across specifications inspire greater confidence. Transparency about sensitivity analyses helps identify boundary conditions and prevents motivated reporting.

Design; Reporting

IV; SCV; TRV

Stipulate procedures for linking baseline, treatment, and outcome data

Real-time monitoring enables early detection and correction of data quality problems. AI tool access may change participant behavior in unexpected ways detectable only through active monitoring. Baseline data collected after randomization introduces bias. AI literacy and domain expertise are the strongest expected moderators of AI effects; failing to measure them means missing the most important heterogeneity in the data.

Construct Validity (CV)

Are we measuring what we claim to measure?

The measures used validly represent the theoretical constructs of interest (e.g., decision quality, coding correctness, clinical reasoning), so estimated effects pertain to those constructs rather than to artifacts, proxies, or construct-irrelevant variance. Observed changes reflect genuine differences in the intended capability or performance construct, not merely in surface metrics such as speed, token count, or verbosity.

Causation (that is the role of internal validity) or generalization to other contexts (external validity). That other operationalizations of the same construct would behave identically without additional validation. That measured gains reflect specific mechanisms (e.g., genuine reasoning improvement vs. template reuse) absent targeted mediator tests. That construct validity in one condition guarantees measurement equivalence across conditions — invariance testing is required.

“Our outcome operationalization is a valid measure of X (evidence: content, criterion, and/or convergent-discriminant validation).”

“We interpret effects as changes in X, not merely in proxy Y (e.g., tokens, lines of code, time-on-task).”

“Results hinge on this operationalization; alternative measures of X may yield different magnitudes or directions.”

Internal Validity (IV)

Did AI access cause the difference?

The observed difference in outcomes was caused by the AI intervention as defined (e.g., assignment to access, or access to a pinned model version) for the study units over the study period. Alternative explanations — including selection, history, maturation, instrumentation, contamination, and interference (if addressed by design) — are unlikely to account for the observed effect. ITT effects reflect the impact of being assigned AI access; TOT effects reflect the impact among those who actually used it; these estimands are distinct and should be reported separately.

Generalization to other populations, tasks, organizations, or AI system versions (that is the role of external validity). The mechanisms through which AI produced the effect unless directly tested with mediator designs or process measures. Effects for participants who never complied with their assigned condition, or under different treatment definitions (e.g., different model versions, temperatures) than those studied. That the effect will persist beyond the study period.

\Delta
(ITT estimate, 95% CI [lower, upper]).”

“For participants who actually received and used AI, the estimated causal effect is [TOT estimate].”

Avoid: “AI causes improvement in X” without specifying the treatment definition, estimand, and study period.

External Validity (EV)

To whom and where do findings generalize?

The estimated effects generalize to explicitly specified target populations, tasks, time frames, settings, and AI implementations for which the study’s sampling and transport assumptions plausibly hold. Findings are robust across the meaningful variations that were prospectively planned or replicated (e.g., across user expertise bands, across related model families, across study sites). The inferential scope is explicit: results apply to [defined target context] under [stated conditions].

Universal applicability across all users, tasks, organizations, or future model generations — especially amid rapid model drift. Causation in the absence of internal validity. Transportability to contexts with substantially different constraints (e.g., different oversight intensity, incentive structures, risk levels) without additional evidence or explicit transport analysis. That findings from convenience or WEIRD samples apply to underrepresented populations without replication.

“Findings are likely to generalize to [defined target context], given our sampling frame, replication across [sites/conditions], and model family checks.”

“Generalization to [new context, model, or version] is uncertain and requires replication or transport analyses.”

“Effects may differ for [subgroups or contexts not studied]; findings should not be interpreted outside their stated scope.”

Is there really an effect, how large is it, and how certain are we?

There is (or is not) an effect, with quantified uncertainty appropriate to the design — accounting for clustering, repeated measures, and interference-aware estimands where applicable. The magnitude and direction of the effect are estimated with acceptable precision; error rates are controlled given multiplicity and researcher degrees of freedom. Statistical model assumptions are adequately met or robustly addressed. Effect sizes and confidence intervals are reported alongside
p

That statistically significant effects are practically important — or that non-significant results imply no effect — without power and precision context. Transparency, Repeatability & Verification (TRV)

Can others see what was done, verify it was done, and check whether findings hold?

The study’s procedures, data transformations, code, and AI system details are sufficiently disclosed (Level 1) and accessible (Level 2) to permit critical appraisal and independent reuse. What was claimed to be shared has been independently verified as genuinely accessible and complete (Level 3). Reported results survive at least one form of independent repeatability assessment — reproducibility, robustness, or replicability — with all three being the strongest form of evidence (Level 4).

Transparency itself does not establish causality (IV), measurement correctness (CV), or generalizability (EV). Independent verification that materials are accessible (Level 3) does not guarantee findings are correct. Reproducibility does not imply robustness or replicability — these three dimensions are empirically partially independent and should not be assumed to predict one another

Level 2 — Sharing:
“Data, code, and materials are deposited at [DOI]; pre-registration is at [URL].”

Level 3 — Verification:
“An independent audit confirmed all materials are accessible and code reproduces reported results.”

Level 4 — Repeatability:

Notes on the interdependence of validity principles.

Construct validity is prerequisite to meaningful internal validity:
a causally identified effect on an invalid measure is not evidence of AI impact.

Internal validity is prerequisite to meaningful external validity:
without a credible causal identification strategy, there is no valid effect to generalize.

Statistical conclusion validity supports but does not substitute for the other principles:
precise estimation of a causally or construct-invalid effect does not improve its evidentiary value.

TRV modulates the strength of claims warranted by the other four principles:
a perfectly transparent study with serious validity problems transparently produces the wrong answer. The three dimensions of repeatability at Level 4 — reproducibility, robustness, and replicability — are empirically partially independent
(
23
;
2
)
and should each be interpreted on their own terms.

The appropriate strength of causal claims should scale with the overall validity profile.

Construct Validity (CV)
—
Whether outcome measures actually capture the theoretical constructs of interest, rather than proxies, artifacts, or constructs transformed by AI assistance itself.

Inadequate construct explication

Constructs are defined vaguely or incompletely, leaving ambiguity about what is being measured.

A study claims to measure “writing quality” but operationalizes it only as word count or grammar score, without defining what quality means in an AI-assisted context.

Construct confounding

The operationalization captures more than one construct, making it impossible to attribute effects to the intended construct alone.

Providing the treatment group with AI plus dedicated training means observed gains could reflect AI access, training, or their interaction — the construct “AI effect” is confounded.

Mono-operation bias (treatment)

Only one version of the treatment is used, so findings may reflect the specific implementation rather than the broader construct.

Testing only one AI model (e.g., GPT-4) and concluding about “AI assistance” in general, without considering that other models or prompting strategies may produce different effects.

Mono-operation bias (outcome)

Only one operationalization of the outcome is used, leaving construct breadth uncovered.

Measuring programming ability with a single coding task; a one-item test cannot represent the full construct of programming competence.

Mono-method bias

All outcome measures rely on the same method (e.g., all self-report, or all automated metrics), conflating method variance with construct variance.

Assessing “code quality” only through automated linting scores, missing dimensions like readability, maintainability, and correctness that require human judgment.

Confounding constructs with levels

Findings from a narrow range of construct levels are generalized to the full construct.

Using simple, routine coding tasks (e.g., LeetCode “easy”) and drawing conclusions about AI’s impact on software engineering broadly.

Reactive self-report changes

Participants alter their responses due to social desirability, evaluation apprehension, or concern about how their answers will be perceived.

Participants overreport satisfaction with AI tools because they want to appear tech-savvy, or underreport AI usage because they fear being seen as dependent on it.

Experimenter expectancy

Researchers’ beliefs about expected outcomes bias how they interact with participants, evaluate outputs, or design study materials.

A researcher enthusiastic about AI provides richer onboarding to the treatment group, or evaluates AI-assisted outputs more favorably when aware of condition assignment.

Demand characteristics / hypothesis guessing

Participants infer the study’s hypothesis and adjust their behavior accordingly.

Participants in the treatment group try harder because they know they have AI access and believe the study expects them to outperform controls.

Treatment-sensitive factorial structure

The factor structure of an outcome measure changes across conditions due to the intervention itself, meaning the construct being measured differs between groups.

“Programming skill” assessed before AI access involves syntax, logic, and debugging as distinct dimensions; with AI assistance, those dimensions may collapse into a single factor (task completion), making pre-post comparisons invalid.

Multiple treatment interference

Participants receive multiple overlapping treatments, making it impossible to isolate the effect of the focal intervention.

Study participants also use Grammarly, web search, and other AI tools alongside the assigned tool, preventing attribution of effects to the specific AI system under study.

Internal Validity (IV)
—
Whether observed differences in outcomes can be attributed to the AI intervention rather than to alternative explanations such as confounding, selection, or concurrent events.

Selection bias

Treatment and control groups differ systematically at baseline on characteristics related to outcomes.

Self-selection into AI use means early adopters tend to be more technically skilled or motivated than non-adopters, inflating apparent AI effects in observational studies.

History / cointerventions

Concurrent events or interventions — other than the focal treatment — affect outcomes during the study period.

A major AI model update is released mid-study, changing control group behavior as participants begin using publicly available tools outside the study protocol.

Maturation

Participants improve over time due to practice, learning, or natural development independent of the AI intervention.

A coding study runs over 8 weeks; participants in both groups improve simply from practice, making it difficult to isolate the AI effect from skill development.

Attrition / differential dropout

Participants drop out of the study, and dropout is related to condition or to outcomes, biasing the remaining sample.

Participants in the control group who find the tasks most difficult are more likely to drop out, leaving a more skilled control group and inflating the apparent AI effect.

Testing effects

Repeated measurement sensitizes participants to the outcome construct or improves performance through practice with the measurement instrument itself.

Participants complete the same coding assessment at baseline and follow-up; familiarity with the task format produces gains that are incorrectly attributed to AI access.

Instrumentation

The measurement instrument or procedure changes across timepoints or conditions, creating artificial differences.

The AI interface is updated mid-study with a new user experience, changing how participants in the treatment group interact with the tool and confounding the treatment effect.

Regression to the mean

Participants selected for extreme baseline scores tend to score closer to the mean on subsequent measurements, regardless of intervention.

A study recruits low-performing employees and finds that AI access “improves” their performance; part of the gain reflects natural regression rather than AI effect.

Contamination

Control group participants gain access to the treatment, reducing or eliminating the contrast between conditions.

Control group participants learn about the AI tool from treated colleagues and begin using it informally, reducing or eliminating the intended treatment contrast.

Interference / spillover

Treatment of one participant affects the outcomes of others, violating the stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA).

A treated employee shares AI-generated templates with untreated colleagues in the same team, improving control group performance and attenuating the apparent treatment effect.

Resentful demoralization

Control group participants, aware they are not receiving a potentially beneficial treatment, reduce their effort or motivation.

Control group participants who know they lack AI access become discouraged and reduce effort, inflating the apparent AI treatment effect beyond its true value.

Compensatory rivalry

Control group participants, aware of the treatment, increase their effort to compensate, reducing the apparent treatment effect.

Control participants told they are in the “no AI” condition work harder to prove they can match AI-assisted performance, compressing the estimated treatment effect.

Model version drift

The AI system changes during the study due to updates, fine-tuning, or parameter changes, introducing heterogeneity in the treatment itself.

An API-accessed model is silently updated mid-study, changing output quality in ways not visible to the research team and creating within-condition heterogeneity.

Differential compliance

Participants in the treatment group do not use the AI as intended, or use it in ways that differ substantially from the protocol.

Treatment group participants use the AI tool for only a fraction of tasks, or use it in ways not anticipated by the study design, weakening the intended treatment contrast.

Non-equivalent control condition

The control condition differs from the treatment condition in ways beyond AI access, confounding attribution.

The control condition provides participants with no additional resources, while the treatment condition provides both AI access and a structured workflow, making it impossible to isolate the AI effect.

Demand-supply mismatch

The AI tool’s capabilities do not match the demands of the assigned tasks, producing null effects that reflect task-tool mismatch rather than absence of AI impact.

An AI coding assistant is tested on tasks requiring creative problem-solving that the model handles poorly; null results reflect tool-task misalignment rather than absence of AI impact.

Novelty / Hawthorne effects

Observed effects reflect participants’ responses to novelty or being observed rather than to the AI intervention itself.

Treatment group performance improves initially because participants are excited about using a new AI tool; effects decay as novelty wears off, but the study is too short to detect this.

Confounded randomization unit

The unit of randomization does not match the unit of analysis or the interference structure, producing biased estimates.

Individuals within the same team are randomized independently, but team-level communication means interference is team-level; individual-level analyses underestimate standard errors and overstate precision.

Ceiling / floor effects

Outcome measures do not have sufficient range to detect effects at the extremes of the performance distribution.

Expert participants are tested on tasks they already perform near-perfectly; AI access cannot improve scores that are already at the ceiling, producing null results that do not reflect absence of AI impact for non-expert populations.

Insufficient follow-up

The study does not measure outcomes at time points sufficient to capture delayed, sustained, or decay effects.

A study measures outcomes immediately after a two-hour AI-assisted session; it cannot detect whether effects persist over weeks or whether skill transfer occurs after AI access ends.

External Validity (EV)
—
Whether findings generalize beyond the specific sample, tasks, settings, and AI system version studied.

WEIRD sampling

Samples drawn predominantly from Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic populations limit generalizability to other demographic and cultural contexts.

A study recruits participants exclusively from US university student pools; effects may not generalize to workers in non-WEIRD countries or to populations with different educational backgrounds and AI exposure.

Convenience sampling

Participants are recruited for accessibility rather than representativeness, limiting generalizability to target populations.

An AI evaluation RCT study recruits participants through online platforms (e.g., Prolific, MTurk) who are atypically tech-savvy and experienced with online experiments compared to the target workforce population.

Artificial task design

Tasks used in the study differ substantially from real-world tasks in complexity, duration, stakes, or context.

A study tests AI’s effect on short, well-defined coding problems that bear little resemblance to the open-ended, collaborative, multi-day tasks that characterize professional software engineering.

Ecological invalidity

The study environment differs from the deployment context in ways that affect how participants interact with the AI.

Participants complete tasks in a controlled experimental interface with no distractions, time pressure, or organizational context; real-world performance may differ substantially.

Treatment specification mismatch

The AI system studied differs from those used in deployment contexts in model family, version, configuration, or access modality.

A study tests a specific, carefully configured AI system; findings may not transfer to off-the-shelf deployments where users interact with default configurations and less controlled prompting.

Model version generalizability

Findings from one model version may not generalize to subsequent versions due to capability changes, alignment updates, or behavioral shifts.

Results from a study using GPT-4 may not apply to GPT-4o or later versions, even for similar tasks, because model behavior changes with updates.

Short study duration

Brief studies cannot capture long-term adaptation, skill transfer, dependency formation, or decay effects.

A one-hour experiment shows AI impact for a coding task, but cannot determine whether workers develop sustainable new skills or become dependent on AI in ways that reduce capability when access is removed.

Interaction of selection and treatment

The effect of the AI intervention differs across subpopulations in ways that limit generalizability to populations with different characteristics.

AI assistance strongly benefits novices but provides no benefit to experts; findings from a novice-heavy sample should not be generalized to expert practitioners without separate evidence.

Statistical Conclusion Validity (SCV)
—
Whether the statistical evidence is sufficient and appropriately interpreted to support claimed effects.

Low statistical power

The study lacks sufficient sample size to reliably detect effects of plausible magnitude, producing high rates of false negatives and inflated effect size estimates when effects are detected.

A study with 30 participants per condition has less than 20% power to detect a moderate effect (
d{=}0.4
), meaning most true effects of that size will not be detected, and those that are detected will be substantially overestimated.

Unreliable outcome measures

Outcome measures have low reliability, attenuating observed effect sizes and reducing statistical power.

A coding quality rubric with low inter-rater reliability (ICC
<0.6
) introduces substantial measurement noise, attenuating effect size estimates and requiring larger samples to achieve adequate power.

Violated statistical assumptions

Analyses assume properties of the data (e.g., independence, normality, homoscedasticity) that do not hold, producing invalid inference.

Participants within the same team share information and strategies, violating the independence assumption of standard regression; standard errors are underestimated and significance tests are anticonservative.

Multiple comparisons without adjustment

Testing many hypotheses without controlling the familywise or false discovery rate inflates the probability of spurious significant findings.

A study tests AI causal effect across 12 outcome variables and 4 subgroups without multiplicity correction; at least one spuriously significant finding is expected by chance even under the null.

Researcher degrees of freedom

Undisclosed analytical flexibility — in outcome selection, covariate inclusion, exclusion criteria, or analysis timing — inflates false positive rates.

A researcher analyzes data multiple ways and reports only the specification that yields a significant result, without disclosing the alternatives explored.

Lack of established benchmarks

The field lacks consensus on what constitutes practically meaningful effect sizes for AI impact, making it difficult to interpret the practical significance of findings.

A study reports
d{=}0.3
without any basis for determining whether this effect is large enough to justify implementation costs, because no domain-specific benchmarks exist.

HARKing

Hypotheses are formulated or modified after examining data and presented as if they were pre-specified, inflating apparent confirmatory evidence.

Exploratory subgroup analyses conducted post-hoc are reported as pre-specified confirmatory analyses, overstating the evidential value of the findings.

Selective outcome reporting

Only statistically significant or favorable outcomes are reported, biasing the published literature toward positive findings.

A study collects 8 outcome measures but reports only the 2 that showed significant treatment effects, without disclosing that others were collected.

Underpowered interaction tests

Subgroup and moderation analyses require substantially larger samples than main effect tests, but this is rarely reflected in sample size planning.

A study powered to detect a main effect of
d{=}0.4
tests whether the effect differs between novices and experts but has only 10% power to detect a plausible interaction, generating uninformative null moderation results.

Transparency, Repeatability & Verification (TRV)
—
Whether the research process and findings can be understood, independently verified, and shown to hold when aspects of the research process are repeated.

Disclosure failures (Level 1)

Selective reporting

Only significant or favorable results are published or disclosed, creating a distorted picture of the evidence base.

A research team runs three AI evaluation RCT studies; only the one showing positive results is published, while null results remain unpublished.

Incomplete methods description

Insufficient detail to allow others to evaluate or reproduce the study’s procedures.

The study does not report the specific model name and version, the exact prompts used, parameter settings, or the human-AI interface design, making replication impossible.

Undisclosed researcher degrees of freedom

Analytical decisions made during data processing and analysis are not reported, preventing assessment of how sensitive findings are to those choices.

The paper does not disclose that several alternative outcome definitions were considered, or that the primary analysis was changed after an interim look at the data.

Conflict of interest non-disclosure

Financial or other interests that could bias design, analysis, or reporting are not disclosed.

Researchers whose employment or funding depends on demonstrating AI effectiveness do not disclose this relationship, preventing readers from assessing potential for motivated reporting.

Undisclosed prompt engineering

The prompts used to elicit AI responses are not reported, even though small variations can substantially affect outputs and outcomes.

A study reports that participants used “an AI writing assistant” without disclosing the specific instructions given to the AI or how the human-AI interaction was structured.

Sharing failures (Level 2)

Inaccessible materials

Data, code, or study materials are nominally reported as available but cannot actually be accessed, used, or verified by others.

Raw participant data and analysis code are not deposited in a public repository, or links are broken, or the repository requires permissions that are not granted, making it impossible for others to verify reported results or conduct re-analyses.

AI system opacity

Key properties of the AI system used are unknown, undisclosed, or inaccessible to other researchers.

A study uses a proprietary model accessed via API without reporting model version, temperature settings, system prompt, or the organization’s internal fine-tuning, preventing meaningful replication.

Missing version control

Analytical code, study materials, and interfaces are not version-controlled, making it impossible to identify what was used at any given point in the study.

Analysis code is modified multiple times after data collection begins, with no record of which version was used for which analysis, creating unreproducible results.

Data privacy and proprietary constraints

Privacy requirements or organizational confidentiality agreements prevent sharing data or materials that would otherwise be necessary for verification.

A study conducted with proprietary organizational data cannot share participant-level data, limiting what the community can verify and narrowing the appropriate strength of claims.

Verification failures (Level 3)

Deviation from pre-registered protocol

The study deviates from the pre-registered design without documentation or justification, undermining the pre-registration’s purpose.

The primary outcome measure is changed after data collection begins due to ceiling effects, but the deviation is not disclosed in the published report.

Undocumented human-AI interaction

Actual usage patterns differ from the described protocol, and deviations are not documented.

Participants develop idiosyncratic interaction strategies not captured in the protocol; these strategies contribute to outcome variation but are invisible in the published report.

System evolution

The AI system changes over time, making previously reported results difficult or impossible to reproduce.

The API endpoint used in the study no longer returns the same outputs because the underlying model has been updated, and the original model version is no longer accessible.

Insufficient transparency to calibrate claims

The overall level of transparency is insufficient for the strength of claims being made; strong claims require more verifiability than weak ones.

A study claiming that AI “definitively improves cognitive performance” provides only a brief methods section with no shared data, code, or pre-registration, making the strong claim unverifiable.

Repeatability failures (Level 4)

Non-reproducibility

Independent parties cannot confirm the reported results by rerunning the analysis on the shared data and code — either because materials are incomplete, code does not run, or results differ from those reported.

A researcher shares data and code but the analysis script contains errors or undocumented dependencies; independent analysts obtain different numerical results from the same inputs.
