Uniformity of Consistency in Arithmetic and Gödel’s Second Incompleteness Theorem: Ein Märchen
Harald Grobner

Abstract
. In much discussed work Artemov has recently shown that, for PA \mathrm{PA} , the consistency schema admits a form of uniform verification via selector proofs, despite the unprovability of the corresponding uniform consistency sentence Con ⁡ ( PA ) \mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{PA}) . In this note, we recast that this phenomenon extends to all sufficiently strong arithmetizable theories: For such theories T T , there exists a primitive recursive selector producing proofs of all instances of the associated consistency schema. This results – a soft version of a classical result of Pudlák – yields a form of computational uniformity, despite the fact that it cannot be internalized as the uniform consistency sentence of Gödel’s Second Incompleteness Theorem. Our main goal is to analyze this gap and to locate selector proofs within the broader framework of provability and reflection.

Introduction

\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{PA})

\mathrm{PA}

Indeed, in that spirit, and taken all together, the above results suggest a refinement of the usual (rather apodictic) interpretation of Gödel’s 2.UVS: The relevant boundary is not simply between provability and unprovability of consistency statements within a given theory, but between
two distinct forms of uniformity
: On the one hand, there is a form of computational uniformity, embodied – implicitly by Pudlák’s results on proof-complexity and explicitly by selector proofs – in which a single effective procedure produces proofs of all instances of a
given property (proofs, which are in this sense uniform). On the other hand, there is the ordinary uniform consistency assertion,
expressed by the single universal arithmetical sentence
\mathrm{Con}(T)
or by a
reflection principle.

Selector proofs show that the former can exist even when the latter
is provably unattainable. In this sense, they isolate a genuine
intermediate level of proof: stronger than mere and trivial instantwise
verification, yet strictly weaker than uniform reflection. In
particular, the limitation identified by Gödel’s 2.UVS is not a
limitation on the existence of uniform computational witnesses, but on
the passage from such witnesses to the ordinary universal consistency
sentence. From this perspective, the significance of schema-based
constructions clearly lies not in challenging Gödel’s 2.UVS, but in
revealing a form of uniform computational content that does not
collapse into uniform reflection.

1.
Standing assumptions and minor recaps

1.1.
First-order theories

Unless otherwise stated, in this note,
T
denotes a recursively axiomatized theory, formulated in first-order predicate logic (out of a system
\mathcal{L}
of axioms, say), extending a weak arithmetic base sufficient for elementary syntactic coding. By the latter we shall (at least) mean elementary arithmetic
\mathrm{EA}
in the sense of

I\Delta_{0}+\mathrm{Exp}
, i.e. Robinson arithmetic together with
induction restricted to
\Delta_{0}
-formulas and the totality of
exponentiation. However, for stronger metamathematical arguments, in particular those involving
derivability conditions and reflection principles, we work over

I\Sigma_{1}
, i.e., arithmetic with induction restricted to

\Sigma_{1}
-formulas and we shall indicate this in the statement of our results.

1.2.
Basics on provability and consistency

Given a system of axioms
\mathcal{L}
with theory
T
as in Sect.
1.1
, we choose and fix a standard Gödel numbering and a standard proof predicate

\mathrm{Proof}_{T}(p,x),

where
\mathrm{Proof}_{T}(p,x)
expresses that
p
codes a
T
-derivation of the formula with Gödel number
x
. The very details in its formalization as an expression of
\mathcal{L}
are assumed to be fixed once and for all throughout the paper.

Definition 1.1
.

The associated
provability predicate
is

\mathrm{Prov}_{T}(x)\equiv\exists p\,\mathrm{Proof}_{T}(p,x).

The standard
consistency sentence
is

\mathrm{Con}(T)\equiv\neg\mathrm{Prov}_{T}(\ulcorner 0=1\urcorner),

i.e.,
\mathrm{Con}(T)\equiv\forall p\,\neg\mathrm{Proof}_{T}(p,\ulcorner 0=1\urcorner).

Lemma 1.2
.

For a standard arithmetization of syntax,
\mathrm{Proof}_{T}(p,x)
is elementary, indeed primitive recursive, and hence representable in
\mathrm{EA}
. In common codings it may be taken to be
\Delta_{0}
after the usual coding of bounded computations.

Proof.

This is the standard representability theorem for primitive recursive relations. (Verification that a finite sequence is a formal derivation is obtained by checking, for each line, whether it is an axiom or follows from earlier lines by one of finitely many rules. Since the axiom set
\mathcal{L}
of
T
is recursive, this verification is primitive recursive relative to the chosen recursive axiom predicate, and in the usual elementary presentations it is representable already in
\mathrm{EA}
. See, for example,
[
Háj-Pud93
, Chp. I]
.)
∎

When
T
extends a sufficiently strong fragment of arithmetic, such as
I\Sigma_{1}
(and, for standard codings, already
\mathrm{EA}
in the usual presentations), the standard Hilbert–Bernays–Löb derivability conditions, underlying the usual proof of Löb’s theorem and Gödel’s 2.UVS, hold for the standard provability predicate
\mathrm{Prov}_{T}
, assuming a standard coding of syntax, see, e.g.,
[
Hil-Ber39
,
Löb55
]
,
[
Háj-Pud93
, Ch. I–II]
, and
[
Smo85
]
. For weaker base theories, such as Robinson arithmetic
Q
, these conditions
may fail, in particular the third condition (D3):

(D1)

If
T\vdash\varphi
, then
T\vdash\mathrm{Prov}_{T}(\ulcorner\varphi\urcorner)
.

(D2)

T\vdash\mathrm{Prov}_{T}(\ulcorner\varphi\to\psi\urcorner)\to(\mathrm{Prov}_{T}(\ulcorner\varphi\urcorner)\to\mathrm{Prov}_{T}(\ulcorner\psi\urcorner))
.

(D3)

T\vdash\mathrm{Prov}_{T}(\ulcorner\varphi\urcorner)\to\mathrm{Prov}_{T}(\ulcorner\mathrm{Prov}_{T}(\ulcorner\varphi\urcorner)\urcorner)
.

Remark 1.3

(Jeroslow’s trick)
.

The third derivability condition (D3) is not strictly necessary for the derivation of Gödel’s 2.UVS. Following Jeroslow, cf.
[
Jer71
]
, one may replace the standard consistency statement by a slightly modified formula for which the second incompleteness theorem can be established using only (D1) and (D2). Thus, the essential content of the theorem does not depend on the full strength of the Hilbert–Bernays–Löb conditions.

Definition 1.4
.

For each standard
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define the
n
-th
consistency instance

\mathrm{Con}_{n}(T)\equiv\neg\mathrm{Proof}_{T}(\overline{n},\ulcorner 0=1\urcorner).

The associated
consistency schema
is the external family

\mathrm{Con}^{S}(T):=\{\mathrm{Con}_{n}(T):n\in\mathbb{N}\}.

It is important that
\mathrm{Con}^{S}(T)
is not a single formula of the object language. It is a meta-level family of formulas indexed by the standard natural numbers.

Proposition 1.5
.

For each standard
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the statement

\mathrm{Proof}_{T}(\overline{n},\ulcorner 0=1\urcorner)
is decidable by a finite computation
formalizable in
\mathrm{EA}
. In particular, if
T
is consistent and
extends
\mathrm{EA}
, then

T\vdash\mathrm{Con}_{n}(T).

Proof.

By Lem.
1.2
, the predicate
\mathrm{Proof}_{T}(p,x)
is primitive recursive and hence decidable on each fixed standard input. Thus, for each standard
n
,
\mathrm{EA}
proves either
\mathrm{Proof}_{T}(\overline{n},\ulcorner 0=1\urcorner)
or its negation. If
T
is consistent, then no standard
n
codes a
T
-proof of
0=1
, so the second case holds. Hence
\mathrm{EA}\vdash\mathrm{Con}_{n}(T)
, and therefore
T\vdash\mathrm{Con}_{n}(T)
since
T
extends
\mathrm{EA}
.
∎

Quite obviously, the assertion “
\forall n\,T\vdash\mathrm{Con}_{n}(T)
” is not by itself a meaningful consistency notion: If
T
is inconsistent, then
T
proves every sentence, including instances
\mathrm{Con}_{n}(T)
which are false, when interpreted in the standard model
\mathbb{N}
. The relevant point is rather that, when
T
is consistent, each
\mathrm{Con}_{n}(T)
is true in
\mathbb{N}
and is moreover finitely verifiable, and hence provable in
T
.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
T
be as in Sect.
1.1
and assume in addition that it is a consistent extension of
I\Sigma_{1}
with the standard provability predicate. Then, the theory

T+\neg\mathrm{Con}(T)+\mathrm{Con}^{S}(T)

is consistent. In other words, there is a model
M\models T
such that
M\models\mathrm{Con}_{n}(T)
, for every standard
n\in\mathbb{N}
, but
M\models\neg\mathrm{Con}(T)
, or, equivalently,
M\models\exists x\,\mathrm{Proof}_{T}(x,\ulcorner 0=1\urcorner).
Any such witness is necessarily non-standard.

Proof.

Since
T\nvdash\mathrm{Con}(T)
by Gödel’s 2.UVS, the theory
T+\neg\mathrm{Con}(T)
is consistent. Moreover, each standard instance
\mathrm{Con}_{n}(T)
is provable in
T
by Prop.
1.5
. Therefore adjoining any finite subset of
\{\mathrm{Con}_{n}(T):n\in\mathbb{N}\}
to
T+\neg\mathrm{Con}(T)
preserves consistency. By compactness, the full theory is consistent and (hence) has a model
M
. In
M
, all standard instances hold by construction, while
\neg\mathrm{Con}(T)
also holds. Therefore, if
a\in M
witnesses
\mathrm{Proof}_{T}(a,\ulcorner 0=1\urcorner)
,
a
cannot be the interpretation of any standard numeral
\overline{n}
.
∎

This theorem gives the semantic reason why the schema does not collapse to the universal sentence: The schema says that each standard numeral fails to be a contradiction proof. The universal sentence says that every element of every model fails to be such a proof code. Non-standard models separate these requirements: Whereas the consistency schema controls standard proof codes only, the universal sentence also controls non-standard objects in non-standard models.

2.
Selector based proofs of consistency

2.1.
Weak Syntactic Bases and Selector Proofs

The previous section treated
\mathrm{Con}^{S}(T)
as an external family.
Artemov’s formulation goes further, cf.
[
Art01
]
: A proof of a serial property is not merely the assertion that each member is separately provable, but a selector together with a verification that the selector returns a proof of the required instance. To this end, we adopt an arithmetized version of Artemov’s notion of selector proof. More precisely, we formalize Artemov’s notion of selector proof in terms of the
arithmetized proof predicate
\mathrm{Proof}_{T}
in the following

Definition 2.1
.

Let
F(x)
be a primitive recursive series of formulas. We say that
T

selector-proves
the series
F
, if there exists a primitive
recursive function
s
such that

T\vdash\forall x\,\mathrm{Proof}_{T}(s(x),\ulcorner F(\dot{x})\urcorner).

Remark 2.2
.

This is a deliberately schematic definition. In a fully intensional treatment, the expression
\ulcorner F(\dot{x})\urcorner
is represented by a primitive recursive function giving the Gödel number of the instance
F(\overline{x})
. Nothing in the following argument depends on the particular coding.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
T
be a recursively axiomatized first-order theory extending
\mathrm{EA}
, cf. Sect.
1.1
, with the standard proof predicate
\mathrm{Proof}_{T}
. Then
T
selector-proves its consistency schema
\mathrm{Con}^{S}(T)
. More precisely, there exists a primitive recursive function
s_{T}
such that

T\vdash\forall x\,\mathrm{Proof}_{T}\bigl(s_{T}(x),\ulcorner\neg\mathrm{Proof}_{T}(\dot{x},\ulcorner 0=1\urcorner)\urcorner\bigr).

Proof.

Since
\mathrm{Proof}_{T}(x,\ulcorner 0=1\urcorner)
is primitive recursive, there exists a primitive recursive procedure deciding, on input
x
, whether
x
codes a
T
-proof of
0=1
. We define a primitive recursive function
s_{T}(x)
by cases.

Case 1:
If
x
does not code a
T
-proof of
0=1
, then the negative computation verifying
\neg\mathrm{Proof}_{T}(x,\ulcorner 0=1\urcorner)
can be converted into a formal
T
-derivation of

\neg\mathrm{Proof}_{T}(\overline{x},\ulcorner 0=1\urcorner).

Let
s_{T}(x)
be the code of this derivation.

Case 2:
If
x
does code a
T
-proof of
0=1
, then from this proof one obtains a
T
-derivation of any formula, in particular of

\neg\mathrm{Proof}_{T}(\overline{x},\ulcorner 0=1\urcorner).

More explicitly, there is a fixed primitive recursive procedure which, given
x
, produces a
T
-derivation of

0=1\to\neg\mathrm{Proof}_{T}(\overline{x},\ulcorner 0=1\urcorner).

Let
s_{T}(x)
be the code of the derivation obtained by appending this derivation to the proof coded by
x
.

The construction of
s_{T}
is primitive recursive, since proof verification, substitution of numerals, and concatenation of proof codes are primitive recursive operations. Moreover, the correctness of this construction is formalizable already in
\mathrm{EA}
, and hence in
T
. Therefore,

T\vdash\forall x\,\mathrm{Proof}_{T}\bigl(s_{T}(x),\ulcorner\neg\mathrm{Proof}_{T}(\dot{x},\ulcorner 0=1\urcorner)\urcorner\bigr),

as claimed.
∎

Remark 2.4

(Pudlák’s finitistic consistency statements)
.

Theorem
2.3
gives a genuine and self-contained selector proof in the sense of Def.
2.1
. Its proof-theoretic core, however, is closely related to, and in the cases of interest translatable from Pudlák’s work on finitistic consistency statements
[
Pud86
]
, Thm. 5.5. Pudlák considers, for an axiomatization
A
, formulas of the form
\mathrm{Con}_{A}(m)
, expressing that there is no
A
-proof of contradiction of length at most
m
. Thus
\mathrm{Con}_{A}(m)
is a bounded-length consistency statement,
whereas our instance

\mathrm{Con}_{n}(T)\equiv\neg\mathrm{Proof}_{T}(\overline{n},\ulcorner 0=1\urcorner)

is a code-wise statement about one particular alleged proof code.

For sequential theories and for suitable presentations of their axiomatizations, Pudlák obtains polynomial upper bounds on the lengths of
proofs of such finitistic consistency statements. In particular, rightly interpreted, his results yield polynomially bounded proofs of the corresponding bounded consistency statements of
\mathrm{PA}
and
\mathrm{ZF}
, see
[
Pud86
]
, p. 192. It is therefore implicit, that for theories covered by Pudlák’s hypotheses, his
bounded-length result implies the existence of primitive recursive
proof-producing functions for the individual code-wise instances: Given a proof code
n
, choose a length bound
m

large enough to cover the proof coded by
n
, use the proof of

\mathrm{Con}_{A}(m)
, and then infer that
n
itself is not a proof of
contradiction. In this sense, Pudlák’s theorem contains the
proof-complexity substance of the selector phenomenon considered by Artemov,
[
Art19
,
Art25a
]
, and indeed in a quantitative form.

The point of Thm.
2.3
is therefore primarily conceptual and
structural, namely to recast the phenomenon in
the arithmetized language of selector proofs: The statement proved
there is the internal selector assertion

(2.5)

T\vdash\forall x\,\mathrm{Proof}_{T}\bigl(s_{T}(x),\ulcorner\neg\mathrm{Proof}_{T}(\dot{x},\ulcorner 0=1\urcorner)\urcorner\bigr),

which says that
T
verifies a single primitive recursive
proof-producing operation for the whole consistency schema.

2.2.
An extending example: The case of
\mathrm{ZF}

As already hinted in Rem.
2.4
, the same distinction as elaborated on in Sect.
2.1
applies – with the usual
ante praeparanda
– to
\mathrm{ZF}
set theory: Fix a standard arithmetization of the syntax of first-order set theory and let
\mathrm{Proof}_{\mathrm{ZF}}(p,x)
express that
p
codes a
\mathrm{ZF}
-proof of the formula with Gödel number
x
. Since
\mathrm{ZF}
interprets weak arithmetic and formalizes primitive recursive syntax, the finite verification argument above applies verbatim.

Definition 2.6
.

For each standard
n
, define

\mathrm{Con}_{n}(\mathrm{ZF})\equiv\neg\mathrm{Proof}_{\mathrm{ZF}}(\overline{n},\ulcorner 0=1\urcorner).

Let

\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZF})\equiv\forall p\,\neg\mathrm{Proof}_{\mathrm{ZF}}(p,\ulcorner 0=1\urcorner).

Proposition 2.7
.

If
\mathrm{ZF}
is consistent, then for every standard
n
,
\mathrm{ZF}\vdash\mathrm{Con}_{n}(\mathrm{ZF}).
Moreover, the proof of each instance may be chosen to be the image of
n
under a primitive recursive selector of the kind described in Thm.
2.3
.

Proof.

The verification that a fixed finite object is or is not a
\mathrm{ZF}
-proof is primitive recursive and hence formalizable in a weak arithmetic interpreted by
\mathrm{ZF}
. If
\mathrm{ZF}
is consistent, no standard
n
codes a proof of contradiction. Therefore each true instance is provable in the weak verifying base and hence in
\mathrm{ZF}
. The selector assertion follows from Thm.
2.3
, taking
\mathcal{L}=\mathrm{ZF}
and using the standard interpretation of elementary arithmetic in
\mathrm{ZF}
.
∎

Theorem 2.8
.

If
\mathrm{ZF}
is consistent, then
\mathrm{ZF}\nvdash\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZF}).

Proof.

This is Gödel’s 2.UVS applied to
\mathrm{ZF}
, which interprets enough arithmetic and whose standard proof predicate satisfies the derivability conditions.
∎

We summarize this in our

Corollary 2.9
.

Assuming
\mathrm{ZF}
is consistent,
\mathrm{ZF}
proves each standard instance of its own consistency schema, and these instance proofs may be selected primitively recursively. Nevertheless,
\mathrm{ZF}
does not prove the corresponding uniform consistency sentence.

3.
Concluding remarks

3.1.
A reflection on Reflection

Let us locate the schema/uniformity distinction in relation to reflection hierarchies. Reflection principles and their iterated forms play a central role in
the proof-theoretic analysis of arithmetic. They provide a systematic
way of measuring the strength of theories in terms of their internal
soundness properties. The resulting hierarchies of theories, obtained
via iterated reflection or consistency extensions, have been studied
extensively, in particular in the work of Beklemishev

[
Bek95
,
Bek05
,
Bek10
]
. A closely
related perspective is given by provability algebras and polymodal
provability logics, where such hierarchies are analysed in a modal
framework, allowing for a fine calibration of reflection principles
and their iterations (see also
[
Vis01
]
). In particular, in
Beklemishev’s
\mathrm{GLP}
-framework, one considers a family of modal
operators, denoted by
[n]
(and their duals
\langle n\rangle
), each intended to
formalize a provability predicate of increasing strength. The
corresponding iterated consistency statements, usually denoted
\langle n\rangle\top
,
serve as canonical measures of proof-theoretic strength. For general
background on arithmetized provability and reflection we refer to

[
Háj-Pud93
]
.

Slightly more precisely, let
T
be as in Sect.
1.1
, and assume in addition that

T\supseteq I\Sigma_{1}
. Iterated reflection progressions are
commonly defined by

T_{0}:=T,\qquad T_{\alpha+1}:=T_{\alpha}+\mathrm{RFN}_{\Gamma}(T_{\alpha}),

where
\mathrm{RFN}_{\Gamma}(T)
denotes the uniform reflection schema over

\Gamma
, with limit stages obtained by unions (or, more generally,
along ordinal notation systems, cf.
[
Bek05
,
Bek10
]
).
In Beklemishev’s framework, such progressions admit a natural
interpretation in terms of the modal operators
[n]
of
\mathrm{GLP}
, where
successive reflection principles correspond to increasing modal
strength, and consistency extensions are represented by formulas of
the form
\langle n\rangle\top
.

The consistency schema
\mathrm{Con}^{S}(T)
, however, does not correspond to a
single reflection principle, but rather to a family of pointwise
assertions. There are three distinct operations one might consider
a priori
:

(i)

adjoining each standard instance
\mathrm{Con}_{n}(T)
as a separate axiom schema,

(ii)

adjoining the universal closure
\forall x\,\neg\mathrm{Proof}_{T}(x,\ulcorner 0=1\urcorner)
, i.e.
\mathrm{Con}(T)
,

(iii)

adding a rule permitting passage from finite verification of arbitrary numerals to the universal sentence.

Operation (ii) is the usual consistency extension. Operation (iii) is a
genuine uniformization principle and is not formalizable in
T
in view
of Gödel’s 2.UVS. Operation (i), by contrast,
does not yield a genuine extension: if
T
is consistent, then each
instance
\mathrm{Con}_{n}(T)
is already provable in
T

(cf. Prop.
1.5
).

3.2.
Postlegomena

Theorem
2.3
gives a genuine selector proof in the sense of Def.
2.1
. Thus, we are led to conclude that the consistency schema
\mathrm{Con}^{S}(T)
admits a form of
uniform provability
at the level of proof construction: There exists a single primitive recursive procedure producing proofs of all its instances, and the correctness of this procedure is
internally
verified as a selector statement. The latter is just the very assertion of (
2.5
).

However, this uniformity clearly does not collapse to the universal consistency sentence
\mathrm{Con}(T)
. In particular, the existence of such a selector
s_{T}
does not yield a proof of
\mathrm{Con}(T)
within
T
itself. The gap between selector-verifiable uniformity and the universal consistency sentence is exactly where Gödel’s 2.UVS applies. Thus, selector proofs exhibit a form of computational uniformity which remains strictly below uniform reflection.

In summary, our results naturally lead to a structural distinction between two notions of
uniformity, to be compared with
[
Art25b
]
:

(Unif1)

Computational uniformity
: the existence of a single
algorithm (selector) producing proofs of all instances,

(Unif2)

Internal uniformity
: the existence of a single
arithmetical sentence expressing the corresponding universal
property.

Gödel’s 2.UVS can then be seen as asserting that, for sufficiently strong theories, selector-verifiable computational uniformity does not entail the ordinary uniform consistency sentence: The selector exists, and its correctness is internally verified as a selector statement, but this does not yield a single reflection-like assertion such as
\mathrm{Con}(T)
.

From this perspective, the consistency schema
\mathrm{Con}^{S}(T)
even serves as a canonical example to witness computational uniformity as a selector-provable property. Otherwise put, the significance of schema-based approaches lies in exhibiting a general proof-theoretic phenomenon, namely the existence of exactly the above mentioned uniform computational witnesses, which do not collapse into uniform reflection principles. Here it is worth noting that selector proofs occupy a strictly intermediate position in a natural hierarchy: They are stronger than mere pointwise, or, instance-wise verification of each sentence
\mathrm{Con}_{n}(T)
, yet
strictly weaker than any form of uniform reflection. This intermediate status is not accidental, but reflects a structural feature of formal theories capable of representing their own syntax.

Thus, we are led to an alternative and more refined reading of Gödel’s 2.UVS, namely the dictum that the true boundary revealed by Gödel’s 2.UVS is not between provability and unprovability, but between
computational uniformity and its uniform internalization.

3.3.
An orthogonal appraoch – different provability predicates

Another way of resisting the standard interpretation of Gödel’s 2.UVS
is to alter the provability predicate itself. This approach is
conceptually distinct from the schema-based strategy discussed above.
Rather than weakening the form of the consistency assertion, one
replaces the standard provability predicate
\mathrm{Prov}_{T}(x)
by a predicate

\mathrm{Prov}_{T}^{*}(x)
which may still be extensionally related to theoremhood,
but which fails to satisfy one or more of the structural conditions
required for the usual derivation of Gödel’s theorem, see, e.g.,

[
Det79
,
Det01
,
Vis01
]
.

Recall that under our standing assumptions, cf. Sect.
1.1
,
the standard provability predicate
\mathrm{Prov}_{T}(x)
satisfies the
Hilbert–Bernays–Löb derivability conditions, and that these are
essential for the classical proof of Gödel’s 2.UVS. A predicate

\mathrm{Prov}_{T}^{*}(x)
may therefore evade the theorem, if it violates a
relevant portion of these conditions. In this sense, one may obtain
“consistency statements” which are provable in
T
, but only at the
cost of departing from the standard structural notion of provability
à la Hilbert–Bernays–Löb.

A humble, non-standard example is given by

\mathrm{Prov}_{T}^{*}(x)\;\equiv\;\mathrm{Prov}_{T}(x)\wedge x\neq\ulcorner 0=1\urcorner.

Then,
\mathrm{Prov}_{T}^{*}(\ulcorner 0=1\urcorner)
is trivially refutable already in very weak arithmetic
and hence in
T
. However, this predicate fails to satisfy the
Hilbert–Bernays–Löb derivability conditions, as announced.

More sophisticated constructions, such as Rosser-style provability
predicates, are considerably subtler, see, for instance,

[
Smo85
,
Vis01
]
. Their philosophical significance has
been extensively discussed in the literature, most notably by
Detlefsen
[
Det79
,
Det86
,
Det01
]
. The central
question is whether the Hilbert–Bernays–Löb conditions should be
regarded as essential to the very notion of formal provability, or
merely as one convenient formalization among others.

From the perspective of the present paper, the key point is that this
approach operates at a different level from the schema-based
distinction analysed above. The latter preserves the standard notion
of provability and weakens the form of the consistency assertion,
whereas the present strategy modifies the notion of provability
itself. The two approaches are therefore orthogonal: One weakens the
form of the consistency assertion, the other the underlying notion of
provability.
