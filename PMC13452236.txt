Exploratory comparison of extracellular proteomic, metabolomic, and lipidomic profiling of inulin-fortified gelatin capsule waste gel fermented by Lactiplantibacillus plantarum
Authors: Passakorn Kingwascharapong, Anurak Uchuwittayakul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This exploratory study evaluated whether partial replacement of sucrose with inulin was associated with differences in the proteomic, metabolomic, and lipidomic profiles of gelatin capsule waste gel fermented by Lactiplantibacillus plantarum. A sucrose-based Control and an INU-50 formulation, in which 50% of sucrose was replaced with inulin, were compared using parallel proteomic, untargeted metabolomic, and untargeted lipidomic profiling. The Control and INU-50 groups separated clearly across all datasets. Proteomic analysis identified four proteins with higher abundance in the INU-50 formulation, including proteins putatively associated with carbohydrate transport, pentose phosphate metabolism, purine biosynthesis, and translation-related functions. Untargeted metabolomics detected 1885 molecular features, among which 426 differential features with putative metabolite annotations were identified in the original exploratory analysis. These features were mainly linked to carbohydrate metabolism, sugar interconversion, amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism, cofactor metabolism, and intermediary carbon pathways. Untargeted lipidomics detected 6005 lipid-related molecular features, including 563 differential features with putative annotations, associated with fatty acid metabolism, glycerophospholipid-related compounds, cardiolipin-related species, ceramide-associated molecules, and secondary metabolite-linked lipid features. Integrated analysis indicated that the observed protein abundance patterns and extracellular molecular features were associated with differences between the two fermented formulations. These findings provide an exploratory molecular dataset describing associations between inulin fortification and complementary omics profiles during gelatin capsule waste fermentation. Because non-fermented controls and targeted validation were not included, mechanistic interpretation should be considered cautiously.

Introduction

Industrial upcycling of food- and pharmaceutical-grade by-products is increasingly recognized as a practical strategy to improve sustainability while generating higher-value biomaterials and functional foods (Caponio et al., 2022). Gelatin capsule waste (GCW) from soft gelatin capsule manufacturing is a protein-rich by-product with strong potential for repurposing into hydrogel-based food matrices (Sanprasert, Kumnerdsiri, et al., 2025). Previous studies showed that GCW can act as a gelling base and that formulation adjustments, such as polysaccharide incorporation, can modify its rheological and textural properties, supporting its use in soft food systems. Its high protein content further highlights its suitability as a substrate for biotransformation and functional ingredient development (Sanprasert, Uchuwittayakul, et al., 2025).

At the same time, gel-based food reformulation increasingly aims to reduce sucrose while incorporating low-digestible carbohydrates or dietary fibers with physiological benefits (Wilson & Whelan, 2017). Inulin is an inulin-type fructan composed mainly of β-(2 → 1)-linked fructosyl units and is widely used as a functional dietary fibre (Roberfroid, 2007). The ISAPP consensus defines a prebiotic as a substrate selectively utilized by host microorganisms that confers a health benefit (Gibson et al., 2017). The present in vitro, single-strain fermentation study did not assess selective utilization within a host microbial community or any health benefit. Accordingly, it does not test prebiotic activity; here, inulin is considered only as a formulation ingredient and alternative carbohydrate source.

Lactic acid bacteria (LAB) play central roles in food fermentation through substrate acidification, inhibition of spoilage organisms, and modification of flavor and bioactive compounds via enzymatic activities (Roberfroid, 2007). Among them, Lactobacillus plantarum is notable for its metabolic versatility, ecological adaptability, and wide use in fermented and functional foods (Anumudu et al., 2024). This versatility reflects its broad enzymatic capacity, including carbohydrate-active enzymes for fibre-derived substrates and proteolytic systems that enable utilization of protein-rich matrices (Yilmaz et al., 2022). In such environments, protein hydrolysis generates peptides and amino acids that support bacterial growth while also producing fragments with potential biological activity, making protein-based gels promising platforms for fermentation-driven functionalization (Kleerebezem et al., 2003).

Some Lactiplantibacillus strains can utilize fructo-oligosaccharides or inulin, although utilization is strain- and condition-dependent (Renye et al., 2021). Evidence from other strains or food matrices does not establish how inulin behaves in the present GCW gel-derived medium. It therefore remained an empirical question whether fermented Control and INU-50 formulations would differ in bacterial-growth estimates or molecular profiles. A previous study using the same GCW-based formulation system reported higher L. plantarum TISTR 1465 growth estimates and differences in extracellular molecular features for INU-50 relative to a sucrose-based Control (Sanprasert, Uchuwittayakul, et al., 2025). Because the formulations differed before inoculation and unfermented controls were not analysed, those extracellular differences could have reflected starting composition, fermentation-associated changes, or both. The present study used this observation to motivate an exploratory comparison across additional analytical platforms, not to assume altered microbial metabolism. We hypothesized that the two fermented formulations would show distinguishable proteomic and extracellular small-molecule profiles. The objective was to compare the proteomic, extracellular metabolomic, and extracellular feature profiles of fermented Control and INU-50 formulations after incubation with Lactiplantibacillus plantarum TISTR 1465 and to identify candidate associations for future validation.

Materials and methods

Materials

κ-Carrageenan was purchased from Thai Food and Chemical Co., Ltd. (Samut Prakan, Thailand). Chicory root-derived inulin, manufactured in Belgium, was supplied by Siam Ultimate Laboratories Co., Ltd. (Bangkok, Thailand). According to the supplier, the inulin contained 88 g/100 g dietary fibre and 0.02 g/100 g sodium. GCW was obtained from Service Pack Manufacturing Co., Ltd. (Pathum Thani, Thailand). The GCW was washed with 95% ethanol, air-dried, repackaged in polyethylene bags, and stored at 4 °C. Its previously reported approximate composition was 74% protein, 6% fat, 18% moisture, and 2% ash (Kumnerdsiri et al., 2024). Lactiplantibacillus plantarum TISTR 1465 was obtained from the Microbiological Resource Center, Thailand Institute of Scientific and Technological Research (Pathum Thani, Thailand).

Preparation of gelatin capsule waste gel formulations

The gel formulations used in this study were adapted from the previously developed jelly-based system, with the present work focusing exclusively on the Control and INU-50 formulations (Sanprasert, Uchuwittayakul, et al., 2025). The base mixture consisted of chamomile tea, gelatin capsule waste, carrageenan, and sweetening agents. Chamomile tea was prepared by infusing 10 g of dried chamomile flowers in 1000 mL of boiling water for 5 min. The flowers were then removed, and the filtrate was used as the aqueous base.

The Control formulation contained approximately 85.7% chamomile tea, 5.2% GCW, 0.6% carrageenan, and 8.6% sucrose. INU-50 contained approximately 85.7% chamomile tea, 5.2% GCW, 0.6% carrageenan, 4.3% sucrose, and 4.3% inulin (Table 1). Experimental design

This exploratory two-group study compared fermented Control and INU-50 formulations selected from the previous screening study (Sanprasert, Uchuwittayakul, et al., 2025). It was not designed to determine dose response, isolate the effect of fermentation from starting formulation, or establish mechanisms. Because only post-fermentation samples were compared, any between-formulation difference may have been present before inoculation, arisen during incubation, or reflected both sources.

Three independently prepared and fermented batches were analysed per formulation (n = 3 biological replicates per group). This small sample size provides limited power and unstable estimates for high-dimensional untargeted analyses. PCA and all feature-level comparisons are therefore descriptive or exploratory and require confirmation in a larger independent experiment.

In vitro growth estimation and sampling

The assay evaluated whether each gel-derived medium supported growth of L. plantarum TISTR 1465 under the stated in vitro conditions. It was not a prebiotic activity assay and did not assess selective utilization by a host microbial community or a health benefit. Before inoculation, each gel batch was heated to 50 °C for 10 min to obtain a uniform gel-derived fermentation medium.

Each independent fermentation replicate was prepared using 90 mL of the Control or INU-50 gel-derived medium in a sterile 200-mL Erlenmeyer flask. The medium was inoculated with 10% (v/v) of the prepared Lactobacillus plantarum TISTR 1465 suspension, resulting in an initial bacterial concentration of approximately 1 × 105 CFU/mL and a final working volume of 100 mL per Erlenmeyer flask. Cultures were incubated at 37 °C for 18 h under aerobic conditions at 120 rpm. MRS broth and phosphate-buffered saline were included only as positive and negative growth controls, respectively, and were not included in the comparative multi-omics analyses.

The growth of L. plantarum TISTR 1465 in the Control and INU-50 gel-derived fermentation media was evaluated using both optical density and viable-cell counts. Optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀) was measured at 0, 6, 12, and 18 h using a spectrophotometer. Before each measurement, the cultures were mixed thoroughly to obtain uniform suspensions, and a 1.0 mL aliquot was transferred to a cuvette. Uninoculated Control and INU-50 media incubated under identical conditions were used as formulation-specific blanks to correct for background turbidity and absorbance associated with the different gel matrices. Blank-corrected OD₆₀₀ was calculated by subtracting the absorbance of the corresponding uninoculated medium from that of the inoculated culture. The change in optical density was calculated as ΔOD₆₀₀ = blank-corrected OD₆₀₀ at each sampling time minus blank-corrected OD₆₀₀ at 0 h.

To confirm bacterial growth using a direct viable-cell measurement, samples collected at 0, 6, 12, and 18 h, were serially diluted in sterile 0.85% NaCl. Appropriate dilutions were spread-plated on MRS agar and incubated at 37 °C for 18 h under aerobic conditions. Colonies were counted. The viable-count results were used as the primary measure of bacterial growth, whereas blank-corrected OD₆₀₀ was retained as a complementary estimate of culture turbidity.

Following incubation, samples from the Control and INU-50 groups were collected for proteomic, metabolomic, and lipidomic analyses. Samples intended for extracellular proteomic, metabolomic, and lipidomic profiling were immediately chilled on ice and centrifuged at 5000 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove bacterial cells and particulate material. The resulting supernatants were clarified by a second centrifugation at 16,000 ×g for 10–15 min at 4 °C and filtered through 0.22-μm syringe filters into pre-chilled microcentrifuge tubes. The clarified filtrates were stored at −80 °C until analysis. Samples for proteomic analysis were processed separately according to the protein extraction procedure described in Section 2.5.1.

Each biological replicate was extracted and prepared independently. For LC–MS/MS analysis, each prepared sample was injected in triplicate. Repeated injections of the same sample were considered technical replicates and were used to evaluate analytical repeatability. When technical replicate injections were performed, their measurements were averaged to obtain one value for each biological replicate before statistical analysis. Accordingly, the statistical sample size was based on the number of independent formulation and fermentation batches, with n = 3 biological replicates per treatment.

Complementary extracellular omics analyses

Extracellular proteomic profiling

Protein extraction and digestion

The extracellular cell-free supernatant samples were mixed with 300 μL of 8 M urea containing protease inhibitor at 10% of the extracellular protein extract volume. The mixture was centrifuged at 14,100 ×g for 20 min, and the supernatant was collected for protein analysis. Protein concentration was determined using the Bradford assay, and the remaining extracellular protein extract was stored at −80 °C until further processing. For enzymatic digestion, 50 μg of extracted protein from each sample was reduced with 200 mM dithiothreitol at 37 °C for 1 h. The reduced samples were subsequently diluted eightfold with 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate buffer and digested overnight at 37 °C with trypsin at an enzyme-to-protein ratio of 1:25.

Reversed-phase peptide fractionation

Protein digestion was terminated by the addition of 50 μL of 0.1% formic acid. The resulting peptide mixtures were desalted and fractionated using C18 reversed-phase columns. Briefly, each column was activated with 100 μL of 100% acetonitrile and equilibrated with 100 μL of 0.1% formic acid. Peptide samples, containing no more than 30 μg per loading, were applied to the column and centrifuged at 1200 rpm for 3 min. The columns were then washed twice with 100 μL of 0.1% formic acid, followed by one wash with 100 μL of pH 10 water. Peptides were sequentially eluted using step gradients of acetonitrile in pH 10 water at 6%, 9%, 12%, 15%, 18%, 21%, 25%, 30%, 35%, and 50%. Eluted fractions were pooled into three combined fractions as follows: 6%, 15%, 25%, and 50%; 9%, 18%, and 30%; and 12%, 21%, and 35%. The pooled fractions were lyophilized and stored at −80 °C until LC–MS/MS analysis.

Peptide identification by LC–MS/MS

Extracellular proteomic analysis was performed by Biomarker Technologies Corporation (Beijing, China). Lyophilized peptide fractions were reconstituted in 20 μL of 2% methanol containing 0.1% formic acid and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatants were transferred to autosampler vials, and 10 μL was injected for analysis. Peptide separation was carried out using nano-flow liquid chromatography at a loading flow rate of 300 nL/min for 15 min and an analytical separation flow rate of 300 nL/min for 120 min. Mobile phase A consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water, whereas mobile phase B consisted of 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile. The gradient program was set as follows: 4% B for 8 min, 4% to 10% B over 3 min, 10% to 25% B over 77 min, 25% to 50% B over 10 min, 50% to 99% B over 4 min, held at 99% B for 6 min, reduced to 4% B over 4 min, and re-equilibrated at 4% B for 8 min.

Mass spectrometric analysis was performed using a Thermo Orbitrap Fusion mass spectrometer operated in label-free acquisition mode. Full MS scans were acquired over an m/z range of 250–1450 at a resolution of 120,000, followed by collision-induced dissociation MS/MS scans of the 20 most intense precursor ions from each survey scan. The normalized collision energy was set at 30%, with an activation time of 50 ms. Fragment ion spectra were acquired in linear ion trap fast mode with an automatic gain control target of 7000, a maximum injection time of 35 ms, and a dynamic exclusion time of 18 s.

Protein identification and differential analysis

Raw data were processed using MaxQuant v1.5.2.8 and searched against the UniProtKB species-level L. plantarum database (Taxon ID 1590; downloaded 10 October 2024), with common contaminants and reversed decoys. A TISTR 1465 genome-derived proteome was unavailable and gelatin-derived sequences were not included in the original search. These choices weaken assignment specificity and create a risk of matrix-peptide misassignment. Proteins supported by a single unique peptide and nominal P ≤ 0.05 were retained in the original exploratory analysis; no multiplicity-adjusted protein remained independently validated. The four reported proteins are therefore described as provisional candidates, not confirmed differential proteins or mechanistic evidence, and require targeted validation using multiple proteotypic peptides.

Untargeted extracellular metabolomic feature profiling

Untargeted extracellular metabolomic analysis was performed by Biomarker Technologies Corporation (Beijing, China). Untargeted metabolomic profiling was conducted using an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system coupled to a high-resolution quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer (Waters Acquity I-Class PLUS UPLC coupled with Xevo G2-XS QTOF, Waters, Milford, MA, USA). Chromatographic separation was achieved on an Acquity UPLC HSS T3 column (1.8 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm, Waters). For both positive and negative electrospray ionization modes, mobile phase A consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water, and mobile phase B consisted of 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile. The injection volume was 2 μL.

A pooled quality-control sample was prepared by combining equal-volume aliquots from all metabolomic samples. The pooled QC sample was used to condition the analytical system through five consecutive injections before analysis of the study samples and was subsequently injected after every five study-sample injections throughout the analytical sequence. Solvent blanks were analysed at the beginning and end of the sequence to assess background contamination and carryover. Study samples were injected in a randomized order. Analytical performance was evaluated using retention-time stability, mass accuracy, total ion intensity, principal component analysis of pooled QC samples, and the relative standard deviation of individual feature intensities across QC injections. Features were retained when they were detected in at least 80% of pooled QC injections and showed a relative standard deviation of no more than 30% across the pooled QC samples. Features failing these criteria were excluded before normalization and statistical analysis. The numbers of detected features before and after QC-based filtering were recorded.

Mass spectrometric data were acquired in MSE mode under MassLynx V4.2 control. During each acquisition cycle, low- and high-collision-energy data were collected simultaneously. The low collision energy was set to off, and the high collision energy ranged from 10 to 40 V, with a scan time of 0.2 s per spectrum. Source parameters were as follows: capillary voltage 2500 V in positive mode and − 2000 V in negative mode, cone voltage 30 V, source temperature 100 °C, desolvation temperature 500 °C, cone gas flow 50 L/h, and desolvation gas flow 800 L/h.

Raw LC–MS data acquired using MassLynx v4.2 were processed in Progenesis QI for peak extraction, retention-time alignment, and molecular-feature detection. Peak areas were normalized to the total peak area of each sample. Subsequent analyses were performed in R v3.6.1. PCA was conducted using prcomp after unit-variance scaling, and Spearman correlation analysis was used to examine sample and quality-control consistency. Given the small sample size (n = 3 biological replicates per formulation), PCA was interpreted descriptively.

The analytical service provider originally screened features using FC > 1, nominal P < 0.05 from Student's t-test, and VIP > 1 from OPLS-DA. OPLS-DA was performed using ropls v1.6.2, with sixfold cross-validation and 200 permutation tests. However, because supervised modelling and VIP-based selection were not considered reliable with only six biological samples, these criteria were not used for inferential feature selection in the revised analysis. Instead, feature-level P values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Features with FDR < 0.05 were classified as statistically differential, whereas features meeting only nominal P < 0.05 were treated as exploratory signals. Fold change and log₂ fold change were reported as effect-size estimates. No retrospective 1.5-fold or 2-fold threshold was applied because neither threshold was prespecified in the original company protocol.

Features were annotated putatively by searching the online METLIN database and Biomark's in-house library through Progenesis QI, with additional classification information obtained from KEGG, HMDB, and LIPID MAPS. Because authentic standards were not analysed and feature-specific spectral evidence was incomplete, no Level 1 identifications were claimed. All compound names are presented as putative annotations, and biologically implausible matches are not interpreted as confirmed compounds. Database pathway mappings are reported only as exploratory annotation context and not as evidence of pathway alteration.

Untargeted extracellular feature profiling using a lipid-oriented LC–MS method

Untargeted extracellular molecular-feature profiling using a lipid-oriented LC–MS method was performed by Biomarker Technologies Corporation (Beijing, China). Untargeted lipidomic profiling was performed using a Waters Acquity I-Class PLUS UPLC system coupled to a Waters Xevo G2-XS QTOF mass spectrometer operating in MSE mode. Lipid species were separated on an Acquity UPLC CSH C18 column (1.7 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm, Waters) maintained at 55 °C. The injection volume was 5 μL. In both positive and negative ion modes, mobile phase A consisted of 60% acetonitrile in water containing 10 mM ammonium acetate and 0.1% formic acid, whereas mobile phase B consisted of 90% isopropanol-acetonitrile containing 10 mM ammonium acetate and 0.1% formic acid.

For lipidomic quality control, equal-volume aliquots from all lipid extracts were combined to produce a pooled QC sample. The pooled QC was injected five times before the analytical sequence to condition the column and mass spectrometer and was then injected after every five study samples to monitor analytical stability. Study samples were analysed in randomized order, and solvent blanks were included to identify background and carryover signals. Lipidomic features were evaluated based on their detection frequency and variability across pooled QC injections. Features detected in fewer than 80% of the QC samples or showing a relative standard deviation greater than 30% in pooled QC samples were removed before downstream analysis. QC performance was additionally assessed through PCA clustering, retention-time reproducibility, mass accuracy, and total ion intensity stability.

The mass spectrometer was operated with low collision energy set at 2 V and elevated collision energy ramped from 10 to 40 V. Spectra were acquired over an m/z range of 50–2000 with a scan frequency of 0.2 s. Source parameters were set as follows: capillary voltage 2500 V in positive mode and − 2000 V in negative mode, cone voltage 30 V, source temperature 120 °C, desolvation temperature 550 °C, cone gas flow 50 L/h, and desolvation gas flow 900 L/h.

Raw data acquired using MassLynx v4.2 were processed in Progenesis QI (Waters) for peak extraction, retention-time alignment, and molecular-feature detection. The original peak areas were normalized to the total peak area of each sample before statistical analysis. The service provider's analytical summary did not report the specific feature-deconvolution parameters, permitted adducts, blank-filtering criteria, missing-value handling, or signal-drift and batch-correction procedures. These unreported procedures were therefore not assumed.

Feature annotations were generated in Progenesis QI by matching the detected signals against the online METLIN database and Biomarker Technologies' in-house library. Theoretical fragment matching was performed using a reported mass-deviation tolerance of 100 ppm. Additional chemical-class and pathway information was obtained from KEGG, HMDB, and LIPID MAPS.

Authentic standards were not analysed under identical chromatographic and mass-spectrometric conditions. Therefore, none of the compound annotations were considered confirmed MSI Level 1 identifications. Because the service provider did not supply feature-specific spectral-matching scores or diagnostic-fragment evidence, compound names are reported only as putative database annotations. Features without sufficient structural evidence are described as unknown molecular features. Biologically implausible database matches were not interpreted as confirmed compounds.

Because the workflow generated numerous putative matches to flavonoids, glycosides, terpenoids, and other non-lipid secondary metabolites, the dataset is described as untargeted extracellular molecular-feature profiling using a lipid-oriented LC–MS method rather than definitive lipidomics. Putative matches to lipid classes are not interpreted as confirmed lipid species or evidence of bacterial membrane remodelling.

Bioinformatic analysis, statistical processing, and database annotation

Statistical analyses were performed in R v3.6.1. PCA was performed using the prcomp function after unit-variance scaling. Heatmaps were generated using pheatmap v1.0.2 after unit-variance scaling, and Spearman correlation analysis was used to examine sample consistency. Given the small sample size of three biological replicates per formulation, PCA, heatmaps, and correlation analyses were interpreted descriptively.

In the original service-provider analysis, between-group differences were assessed using Student's t-test. Fold change was calculated from the normalized mean peak areas of the INU-50 and Control groups. The original feature-screening criteria were FC > 1, nominal P < 0.05, and VIP > 1. OPLS-DA was performed using ropls v1.6.2, with sixfold cross-validation because fewer than seven samples were available, and 200 permutation tests.

However, because only six biological samples were analysed, OPLS-DA, cross-validation, permutation testing, and VIP-based feature selection were not considered sufficiently reliable for inferential analysis. These outputs were therefore not used to define differential features or support biological conclusions in the revised manuscript. Feature-level P values from the two-group comparisons were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Features with FDR < 0.05 were classified as statistically differential. Features meeting nominal P < 0.05 but not FDR < 0.05 were described only as nominal exploratory signals.

Fold change and log₂ fold change were reported as effect-size estimates. No additional 1.5-fold or 2-fold criterion was applied because neither threshold was prespecified in the original analytical protocol. Database classification and pathway mapping were retained only as exploratory annotation summaries. Hypergeometric pathway enrichment was not interpreted as evidence of pathway activation, inhibition, or altered metabolic flux.

Metabolite and lipid annotations were considered putative unless confirmed by authentic standards. For metabolite and lipid annotation, differential features were searched against public databases, including the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG; http://www.genome.jp/kegg/), the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB; https://hmdb.ca/), the LIPID MAPS Structure Database (LMSD; https://lipidmaps.org/), and METLIN (https://metlin.scripps.edu/).

COG functional classification showed that the differentially abundant proteins were mainly assigned to functional classes related to nucleotide transport and metabolism, carbohydrate transport and metabolism, and translation, ribosomal structure, and biogenesis (Fig. 1C). KEGG pathway annotation further indicated that the upregulated proteins were associated with ABC transporters, pentose phosphate pathway, pentose and glucuronate interconversions, carbon metabolism, purine metabolism, biosynthesis of amino acids, biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, microbial metabolism in diverse environments, and general metabolic pathways (Fig. 1D-F).

Database annotations associated these candidates with pentose phosphate metabolism, purine biosynthesis, carbohydrate transport, and translation-related functions. These annotations describe the reported functions of the matched proteins but do not demonstrate increased enzyme activity, substrate transport, carbon flux, nucleotide synthesis, or protein synthesis in the present system. Moreover, the use of a species-level protein database, absence of gelatin-derived sequences from the search database, and lack of targeted validation limit confidence in the assignments.

Discussion

This exploratory study compared the extracellular proteomic and molecular-feature profiles of two fermented gelatin capsule waste (GCW) formulations: a sucrose-based Control and INU-50, in which 50% of the sucrose was replaced with inulin. The study extends the GCW formulation model previously reported by Sanprasert et al. (2025b), but the present findings must be interpreted as associations between two post-incubation formulations. Because corresponding unfermented Control and INU-50 samples were not included, the observed differences cannot be attributed specifically to fermentation. They may have been present before inoculation, arisen during incubation, or reflected a combination of initial composition and fermentation-associated changes.

The extracellular proteomic analysis identified four provisional protein candidates with higher relative abundance in INU-50 in the original nominal comparison (Table 2). These candidates were putatively annotated as a maltose/maltodextrin-binding protein, ribulose-phosphate 3-epimerase, phosphoribosylformylglycinamidine synthase, and a GTP-binding GTPase. Their reported annotations are associated with carbohydrate transport, pentose phosphate metabolism, purine biosynthesis, and translation-related functions, respectively. These functional categories are biologically relevant to the metabolic versatility of Lactiplantibacillus plantarum described in previous studies (Cui et al., 2021). However, none of the four candidates remained significant after multiple-testing correction, some were supported by only one unique peptide, and none was independently validated. Furthermore, the use of a species-level protein database and the exclusion of gelatin-derived sequences reduced assignment specificity. These proteins should therefore be considered hypothesis-generating candidates rather than confirmed differentially abundant proteins or mechanistic evidence.

The putative maltose/maltodextrin-binding protein annotation is compatible with a carbohydrate-transport function, but its relative abundance does not demonstrate increased substrate binding, transporter activity, or carbohydrate uptake. Previous studies have described transcriptional and membrane-associated responses of L. plantarum to fructooligosaccharide utilization and broader carbohydrate-utilization capabilities in this species (Chen et al., 2015; Cui et al., 2021; Zhu et al., 2021). Nevertheless, findings from other strains and experimental systems cannot establish increased transport activity in the present GCW formulation. Direct transport assays, substrate-depletion measurements, or isotope-tracing experiments would be required to test this possibility.

Similarly, the putative ribulose-phosphate 3-epimerase annotation is associated with carbon interconversion within the pentose phosphate pathway. This pathway can generate metabolic intermediates and reducing equivalents that support cellular biosynthesis and redox balance (Stincone et al., 2015). However, the present study did not measure enzyme activity, intracellular ribulose-phosphate, NADPH production, or isotope-resolved carbon flux. The observed protein signal therefore does not demonstrate increased pentose phosphate pathway activity. The tentative annotations of phosphoribosylformylglycinamidine synthase and the GTP-binding GTPase likewise do not establish increased purine biosynthesis, translation, or protein synthesis. Although inulin-associated microbial functions have been reported in other experimental systems (Ferrocino et al., 2023; Hughes et al., 2022), those studies provide biological context rather than direct support for the mechanisms proposed in the present formulation.

Untargeted extracellular metabolomic profiling detected 1885 molecular features, of which 426 met the original service provider's exploratory screening criteria. These comprised 275 signals with higher and 151 signals with lower normalized intensities in INU-50. These signals represent molecular features rather than confirmed metabolites. Studies of fermented foods have demonstrated the value of complementary omics approaches for generating hypotheses concerning microbial and chemical differences (Jiang et al., 2023; Shi et al., 2022). However, the small sample size and absence of authentic-standard confirmation in the present study limit the reliability of compound-level and pathway-level interpretation.

The putative annotations listed in Table 3 should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Several database matches, including Miraxanthin-V, methanopterin derivatives, cobalt-precorrin 7, gabaculine, and nogalamycin-related compounds, are biologically implausible in this experimental system. Although previous studies provide information about raffinose-family oligosaccharides (Elango et al., 2022; Kasprowicz-Potocka et al., 2022), bacterial amino-acid degradation (Knorr et al., 2018), cell-envelope-related enzymes (Kumar Pal & Kumar, 2023), and cobalt-dependent cofactor biosynthesis (Osman et al., 2021), these references cannot confirm the identities or biological origins of the molecular features detected here. Without diagnostic MS/MS evidence and authentic standards, the database matches should not be interpreted as evidence that the corresponding compounds were present, accumulated, depleted, produced, or transformed.

The different starting compositions provide a plausible non-microbial explanation for at least some extracellular feature differences. INU-50 initially contained less sucrose and included inulin, whereas the Control contained a higher sucrose concentration and no inulin. The lower sucrose-associated signal in INU-50 is therefore consistent with its initial formulation and cannot be interpreted as evidence of higher microbial utilization. Inulin and related carbohydrates can influence the persistence or metabolic behaviour of L. plantarum under appropriate experimental conditions (Cui et al., 2021; Fuhren et al., 2021), but the present design cannot separate this possibility from starting-composition effects. Similarly, previous reports concerning microbial transformation of phenolic compounds, sugar derivatives, or related metabolites provide contextual information (Bae et al., 2023; Burrichter et al., 2018; Tonetti et al., 1998; Wei et al., 2022), but they do not validate the tentative feature annotations or demonstrate comparable transformations in the present system.

Exploratory KEGG mapping placed some putatively annotated features into broad categories related to carbohydrate, energy, amino acid, cofactor, and secondary-metabolite metabolism (Fig. 5A–C). These database mappings do not demonstrate that the corresponding pathways were activated, inhibited, or altered. This limitation is particularly important because some pathways were supported by only one or very few putatively annotated features. Accordingly, the pathway plots are retained as descriptive outputs from the original database analysis, but they are not interpreted as evidence of carbon redistribution, pathway enrichment, or altered metabolic flux.

The lipid-oriented LC–MS workflow detected 6005 extracellular molecular features, including 563 features that met the original exploratory screening criteria. Among these, 277 signals showed higher and 286 showed lower normalized intensities in INU-50. However, many database matches corresponded to flavonoids, glycosides, terpenoids, and other secondary metabolites rather than lipids (Table 4). Such features may reflect the broad detection range of the analytical platform, limitations of database matching, or constituents of the chamomile-containing formulation. Although lipidomic approaches can provide information not captured by proteomics or conventional metabolomics (Ji et al., 2025a, Ji et al., 2025b; Shi et al., 2022), the composition of the present feature set does not support a general claim of lipid remodelling.

Some molecular features received tentative matches to phosphatidylglycerol-, cardiolipin-, phosphatidylserine-, or ceramide-related structures. Previous studies have shown that bacterial membrane lipids can change in response to environmental stress and that cardiolipin and phosphatidylglycerol may contribute to membrane organization and adaptation (Guerzoni et al., 2001; Kato et al., 2019). More recent work has also described relationships between environmental conditions and cellular metabolic or lipid profiles in L. plantarum and other probiotic bacteria (Bustos et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026). Nevertheless, the present analysis was conducted on extracellular cell-free supernatants rather than isolated bacterial cells or membrane fractions. Therefore, even if the putative lipid annotations are subsequently confirmed, their extracellular intensities would not directly demonstrate bacterial membrane composition, lipid turnover, membrane integrity, or cellular adaptation.

Several other molecular features were putatively matched to flavonoids, glycosides, terpenoids, and related secondary metabolites. Lactic acid bacteria can transform flavonoid glycosides and phenolic compounds under certain fermentation conditions (König et al., 2023; Park et al., 2021), and fermentation-related changes in flavonoids have been reported in complex plant matrices (Gao et al., 2024). However, these reports cannot confirm that the tentatively annotated features in the present study underwent comparable transformations. The original database mappings to fatty acid biosynthesis, unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis, α-linolenic acid metabolism, flavonoid degradation, carotenoid biosynthesis, and polyketide-related pathways (Fig. 9A–D) were generally supported by only one putatively annotated feature per pathway. Although these categories are relevant to the general microbial and lipid metabolism (Wang et al., 2021), the present mappings do not provide meaningful evidence of pathway alteration and are retained only as exploratory annotation context.

The three analytical datasets showed formulation-associated differences, but the study did not perform a validated mechanistic cross-omics integration. Broad similarity among database categories, such as carbohydrate metabolism, biosynthetic functions, or lipid-related processes, does not establish coordinated biochemical regulation. The proteomic evidence was limited to four unvalidated candidates, whereas the metabolomic and lipid-oriented datasets comprised molecular features with uncertain compound identities. Therefore, the datasets should be regarded as complementary descriptive observations rather than a coherent causal model. Previous multi-omics studies illustrate the potential of integrating validated molecular datasets in fermented-food research (Jiang et al., 2023; Shi et al., 2022), but the present data do not demonstrate that inulin enhanced substrate acquisition, carbon flux, biosynthetic activity, or lipid-associated adaptation.

The findings also do not establish prebiotic activity or improved probiotic-fermentation performance. Inulin can be selectively utilized by particular microorganisms under appropriate conditions and has recognized potential as a functional carbohydrate (Hughes et al., 2022). However, the present experiment evaluated one bacterial strain in two compositionally different in vitro media and did not assess selective utilization within a host microbial community or a resulting health benefit. Thus, the previously reported difference in bacterial growth and the present molecular profiles (Sanprasert et al., 2025b) should be interpreted as formulation-associated observations rather than evidence of a prebiotic effect.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the absence of unfermented Control and INU-50 formulations prevents separation of initial compositional differences from incubation-associated changes. Second, three biological replicates per formulation provide limited statistical power for high-dimensional untargeted analyses and undermine the reliability of supervised multivariate modelling. Third, OD₆₀₀ is sensitive to differences in matrix turbidity and should be interpreted together with direct viable-count data. Fourth, the provisional protein candidates and small-molecule annotations were not independently confirmed. Fifth, the extracellular cell-free supernatants cannot establish intracellular metabolic flux, biosynthetic activity, or bacterial membrane adaptation. Finally, database pathways supported by one or very few unconfirmed features cannot provide reliable evidence of pathway alteration.

Future studies should include a factorial design incorporating unfermented and fermented samples of both formulations, additional independently prepared biological replicates, carbohydrate-matched controls, multiple inulin concentrations, and time-resolved sampling. Viable-cell enumeration, strain-specific proteomic searching that includes gelatin-derived sequences, direct analysis of bacterial cellular fractions, targeted MS/MS with authentic standards, enzyme and transport assays, and stable-isotope tracing would be required to test the biological hypotheses generated by this exploratory analysis.

Conclusion

This exploratory study found that fermented Control and INU-50 GCW formulations showed distinguishable extracellular proteomic and molecular-feature profiles under the tested conditions. Four provisional protein candidates and several putatively annotated extracellular features were identified for possible future investigation. However, the small sample size, absence of unfermented formulation controls, limited proteomic evidence, and lack of authentic-standard confirmation restrict interpretation. The findings do not establish that inulin increased substrate uptake, intracellular carbon flux, nucleotide biosynthesis, protein synthesis, lipid remodelling, membrane adaptation, or fermentation-specific biotransformation. Instead, the results describe formulation-associated differences between two post-incubation samples and provide exploratory candidates for targeted validation using an expanded factorial design.
