Introduction
 One function of Continuous Air Monitor (CAM) samplers at LANL (Los Alamos
National Laboratory) is to alert workers to the presence of airborne alpha-emitting (e.g.)
plutonium particles.
 The typical (alpha-sensing) CAM sampler pulls air through an inlet to collect airborne
particulate matter onto a disk-shaped (paper or membrane) filter. Ideally, the CAM sampler
should have optimal collection of particulates on the filter with minimal deposition of
particulates on its internal surfaces.
 This experimental study measured the aerosol deposition on three distinct (internal
surface) areas for two different CAM samplers from two different manufacturers.
 LANL is assisting Bladewerx LLC (Rio Rancho NM) by measuring internal aerosol
surface deposition in their KatanaGBM™ (Glove Box Monitor) and in the ThermoFisher™
Alpha7 CAM sampler.

Background
Previously, the LANL Aerosol Engineering Facility measured the aerosol particle collection
efficiency and detector face deposition of a prototype KatanaGBM™ (Moore, NeSmith and
Baltz 2025) at three air flow rates of 5, 42, and 70 ALPM (ambient liters per minute).
Measurements were performed using two sizes of oleic acid (liquid droplet) particles (3±1 and
10±1 μm AED (aerodynamic equivalent diameter). Additionally, the KatanaGBM™’s aerosol
collection efficiency and detector face deposition was measured for three orientations of the
wind tunnel air flow (compared to the KatanaGBM™ filter face) at angles of 0°, 45° and 90°
degrees.

Previous results with the KatanaGBM™
 (1) For 4.3 μm AED aerosol.
 For an aerosol diameter of 4.3 μm AED ± 0.8 GSD (geometric standard deviation) at
three flowrates (5, 42 and 70 ALPM) and three angles (0°, 45° and 90°), the average ratio of
aerosol deposition (Figure 3) on the detector face (compared to a reference filter) was:

• Less than 0.03 for all conditions, except,
• About 0.06 and 0.07 for 45° and 90° degrees, respectively at 5 ALPM, although,
• Some individual values were about 0.10 for the detector face deposition.

 7

Figure 3. Detector deposition for 4.3 μm AED particles (Moore, NeSmith and Baltz 2025).

 (2) For 10.8 μm AED aerosol.
For an aerosol diameter of 10.8 μm AED ± 0.7 GSD (geometric standard deviation) at three
flowrates (5, 42 and 70 ALPM) and three angles (0°, 45° and 90°), the average ratio of aerosol
deposition (Figure 4) on the detector surface (compared to a reference filter) was:

• Less than 0.10 for most conditions, except,
• About 0.3 (average of three readings) at 90 (ninety) degrees for 5 ALPM.
• One of these individual values at 90 degrees was about 0.6. At the time of the original
experiments, there was no evidence that this could have been an anomalous result.

Methods and Materials

Aerosol wind tunnel
Tests were performed in an aerosol wind tunnel (Figure 5) at the LANL Aerosol Engineering
Facility. Low velocity air was mixed with generated aerosol particles and transported to the
tunnel’s test section. The uniformity profiles across the wind tunnel test section for aerosol
concentration and air velocities had been previously validated (Moore et al 2010). In these tests,

COV for 10 µm AED glycerol droplet aerosols. Both COV values were located within the inner
2/3rds of the duct cross-sectional area.

Liquid glycerol aerosol particles
Fluorescent-tagged glycerol droplets were generated by a model 1520 TSI Inc FMAG (Flow
Focusing Monodisperse Aerosol Generator, Figure 17). The feedstock solution for the aerosol
generator was an ultrapure (UPW) water solvent with added glycerol and sodium fluorescein (in
a 10% mass ratio to the added glycerol). Although the glycerol-to-water ratio was varied to yield
different aerosol particle diameters (Table 4), the mass ratio of sodium fluorescein to glycerol
was kept at a 10% value for both feedstock solution mixtures (Figure 16).

Aerosol collection efficiency
 To measure the sampler aerosol collection efficiency, a 47 mm diameter filter was placed
(Figure 7) in the reference holder (Model ILPH-47, HI-Q Inc, San Diego CA) and a 37 mm
diameter filter was placed in the tested sampler (KatanaGBM™ or ThermoFisher™ Alpha7™).
Both reference and test filters were pre-cut from filter material sheet stock (IW Tremont™
RB5211) with an arch punch (C.S. Osborne Inc) of the appropriate diameter.

Figure 17. TSI Inc model 1520 Flow Focusing Monodisperse Aerosol Generator

 While the wind tunnel were performed, real-time monitoring of the aerosol particle sizes
were conducted at 15-second intervals. A sample of measured (un-corrected) AED values
(Figure 19) are given, representing a 68 minute length of time, where the indicated AED value
(8.5 µm) is adjusted by a stretch elongation factor (Baron 1986). This correction factor accounts
for the stretching of a liquid droplet aerosol particle in the high-speed sensing volume of the TSI
model 3321 Aerosizer instrument, where
                        D(actual, µm) = 0.0259*(DI)2 + 0.9286 DI + 0.0595
DI = the indicated AED value from the instrument.

Figure 20. Examples of the reference and test filters in isopropyl and water solution.

 After collecting test aerosol over the 3-minute test interval, the “reference” and “test”
filters were placed into a measured solution of water to dissolve the collected (glycerol and 10%
sodium fluorescein) aerosol. The water feedstock for the analytical solution is 2.1x10-6 M NaOH
(at about 8.3 pH) to optimize the response of the fluorometer (Kesavan et al 2001).
 In addition to the “reference” and “test” air filters, clean cotton swabs from the three
different swipes were also placed into solutions in labeled beakers (Figure 20 is a generic
example of the test beakers).

Figure 21. The Turner model 450 fluorometer with example beakers and punchlist.

Fluorescent aerosol concentration on a filter.
 The matched sets of “reference” and “test” filters and cotton swabs were allowed to soak
(at least) overnight in their individual labeled beakers. After dissolving the fluorescent glycerol
droplets from the filters, the fluorescence concentration of the liquid solutions in the beakers
were measured in a (Turner model 450) fluorometer (US EPA 2006).
