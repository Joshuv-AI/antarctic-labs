 P. Titus, S. Gerhardt, A. Brooks, J. Fang, F. Cai, B. Meiner, and J. Wisniewski

 Abstract—The National Spherical Torus Experiment Upgrade (NSTXU) centerstack casing surrounds the central magnet core of
NSTXU. It provides the inner vacuum boundary of the plasma chamber and supports the radially inboard plasma facing components
lining the plasma chamber. The casing is supported like a cantilever, fixed at the lower flange by a connection to the TF Bundle through
a G-10 ring. It is flexibly supported at the top by bellows, and insulated shims intended to react the largest part of the inventory of the
disruption loads at the upper support, thereby protecting the upper and lower connections from excessive loads.
 A large part of the qualification of the casing and tiles requires addressing and quantifying disruption loads. These have been estimated
based on extensions of the behavior of NSTX during its run period roughly 15 years ago. Structural qualification of the Upgrade used
these estimates of the loading as input to calculations to demonstrate adequacy for a “most probable” disruption. The estimates are
included in a requirements document to guide conservative design of all components loaded by disruptions. However, the changes made
for the upgrade made the extrapolation of disruption behavior uncertain. Disruptions include axisymmetric loading from axisymmetric
currents induced in the vessel shells. In addition, more complex currents called Halo currents are a result of plasma poloidal currents
impinging on the casing. The halo currents add another layer of uncertain behavior and net lateral loading to the casing. As a result,
NSTXU is equipped with a number of instruments intended to assess the nature and severity of disruptions.
 The casing shims are required for lateral support of the casing, but these have also been converted to load cells to provide another
measure of the magnitude of symmetric and asymmetric loading. Some of the logic in the development of the requirements document
are presented. Electromagnetic analysis of axisymmetric and halo loading is described. Global simulations of the casing transient
dynamic response, and local analysis and testing of the shim/load cell are included. The shim load cells and other instrumentation will
be an important guide in planning NSTXU experiments beyond its next run period expected in late 2026 or early 2027.

Index Terms— Disruption, NSTXU, Instrumentation, Transient Dynamic Analysis.

 I. INTRODUCTION

T HE NSTXU centerstack casing surrounds the central magnet core of NSTXU. It provides the inner vacuum boundary of
 the plasma chamber, and supports the radially inboard plasma facing components lining the plasma chamber. The casing is
 supported like a cantilever, fixed at the lower flange by a connection to the TF Bundle through a G-10 ring. It is flexibly
supported at the top by bellows, and eight insulated shims (figure 10), intended to react the largest part of the inventory of the
disruption loads at the upper support, thereby protecting the bellows and lower G-10 ring from excessive loads. Figure 1a shows
the general arrangement and location of the shims.

 NSTXU is a low aspect ratio spherical tokamak (ST) with physics behavior similar to other ST devices like MAST and ST-
40. Structurally they share small radial builds of the central column making it more susceptible to lateral loading. The central
column vacuum boundary is a thin Inconel casing, and is thermally insulated from central column coils and not supported by the
coils except at the lower extremity of the casing.
 A large part of the qualification of the casing and tiles requires addressing and quantifying disruption loads. These have been
estimated based on extensions of the behavior of NSTX during its run period roughly 15 years ago. Structural qualification of the
Upgrade used these estimates of the loading as input to calculations to demonstrate adequacy for a “most probable” axisymmetric
and Halo disruption. The estimates are included in a requirements document[1] to guide conservative design of all components
loaded by disruptions. However, the changes made for the upgrade made the extrapolation of disruption behavior uncertain.
Disruptions include axisymmetric loading from axisymmetric currents induced in the vessel shells from translations and quench
of the plasma current. In addition, more complex currents called Halo currents are a result of plasma poloidal currents impinging
on the casing. The halo currents add another layer of uncertain behavior and net lateral loading to the casing. As a result, NSTXU
is equipped with a number of instruments intended to assess the nature and severity of disruptions.
 The casing shims are required for lateral support of the casing, but these have also been converted to load cells to provide
another measure of the magnitude of symmetric and asymmetric loading. Some of the logic in the development of the requirements
document are included in section II. Electromagnetic analysis of axisymmetric and halo loading will be described in sections III
and IV. Global simulations of the casing transient dynamic response, and local analysis and testing of the shim/load cell in section
V.

 III. SIMULATION OF AXISYMMETRIC DISRUPTIONS
 The centerstack casing (CS Casing) is one component of many that requires derivation of disruption loads. A global model of
NSTXU was developed with the casing and most other loaded components included. This model was used for passive plate
loading[3], axisymmetric loading of the casing and thus the G-10 ring [4], and recently for developing response spectra at
diagnostic mounting locations [16]. The passive plates are conducting plates used for stabilization of the plasma and are strongly
affected by a plasma disruption. The model used for these analyses are shown in figures 4 and 5a. In figure 4, “air” or vacuum
space is included, which is needed for the ANSYS vector potential electromagnetic solution. Some results from this model are
shown in Figure 5a, which also shows the model, at left, with “air” removed .

 IV. SIMULATIONS OF NON-AXISYMMETRIC DISRUPTIONS AND HALO LOADING
 Reference [8] included analysis of the non-axisymmetric component of disruption loading from prescribed halo current
specification in [1]. Table I lists the disruption scenarios considered in the halo analysis. In the previous section, axisymmetric
plasma currents were considered. Reference [7] evaluates halo loads, or non-axisymmetric loads as an electromagnetic transient
with interactions with poloidal currents in the plasma. The current distribution in the plasma during a disruption is more

complicated than the axisymmetric case. The current in general follows the helical magnetic field lines. From an engineering
viewpoint it is convenient to decompose the distribution into toroidal and poloidal currents. Most disruption analysis concerns
itself with the rapid movement and decay of the toroidal currents discussed in section III. Here there is only an inductive coupling
between the toroidal currents representing the plasma and the induced eddy currents in the structures. Where there are poloidally
electrical continuous structures surrounding the plasma, the inductive coupling of the poloidal currents (both large scale and small
- i.e. the spiraling of electrons around field lines), characterized by toroidal flux changes in the plasmas, can have a significant
impact as well. And when the plasma contacts the structure, a portion of the current flowing in the plasma is intercepted and
effectively shorted through the structure. It is these currents that are addressed here. While they do not occur alone, it is useful to
separate them to try and understand their impact. This independent derivation of loads necessitates summations of loading in the
analysis described in section V. Further, the halo is assumed to occur due to a plasma instability that distorts the shape and position
of the plasma causing an asymmetry of the intercepted halo currents. An ANSYS 3D Electromagnetic Model was generated of the
CS casing and excited by a set of TF like coils representing the plasma halo region as shown in figure 6a. A half plane of vacuum
elements is removed to expose the interior.

Fig. 6a Halo Current Simulation Model. From [7]

 The model, shown in figure 6a, uses solid97 elements with eddy current capability activated for the CS. The CS is inconel with
a resistivity of 130.e-8 ohm-m. The coils representing the plasma halo are assumed to carry an initial current distribution totaling
400 kA (2 MA plasma current with 20% halo current fraction, HCF) but modulated to provided a 35% toroidal peak factor, TPF
(from Ref 1: TPF=1+.07/HCF) or a j=jave*(1+.35*cos(phi)) distribution. Where j is the halo current density, jave is the
axisymmetric or average halo current density and phi is the toroidal angle. The halo current strike is assumed to occur, as specified
in Ref [1], as a triangular waveform which starts at the beginning of the current quench, peaks in the middle and returns to zero at
the end. This is shown in Figure 3. This analysis focuses on the three halo current scenarios associated with a centered disruption
which load up the most of the center stack. These are excerpted below from Ref 1.

 V. TRANSIENT DYNAMIC STRUCTURAL SIMULATIONS OF THE CENTERSTACK ASSEMBLY
 Early modeling of the casing described in sections III, and IV assumed it was fixed at the bottom, and restrained at the top by
insulated shims bridging the gap between the centerstack casing upper flange and the PF1c case and Bellows assembly. The initial
analyses of the transient dynamic loads on the centerstack casing were performed to quantify the loads at the base of the casing
support where the casing is bolted to the TF coil assembly using the insulating G-10 ring. A dynamic bolting analysis [9] was
conducted to verify the design quality of a G10 ring. Loads on the centerstack(CS) casing were dominated by disruption loads, but
the full inventory of loads from normal operation, and error field loads was included. To accommodate the thermal expansion of
the centerstack resulting from plasma heating through the pulse, there is a .030 inch or .7 mm gap between the shim and casing.
The 8 shim locations in the model are shown in figure 10. They are not preloaded. Disruption loading may occur with the gap or
with it closed near the end of the pulse. The simulation includes modeling of the impact resulting from closing the gap and is
included in the forces used to qualify the shim hardware.

 VI. SHIM DETAILED DESIGN AND ANALYSIS

 Fig. 12 Lateral Shim Solid Model.

  The Recovery Project’s lateral support shim’s current design accounts for the structural, electrical isolation, and instrumentation
needs; of which the prior design (NSTXU) was deemed inadequate. The shim details were qualified by NSTXU calculations, [10]
and [5] The modified shim is a two-part design featuring an Inconel body and a Yttria Partially Stabilized Zirconia (PSZ) Shim.
While technically a “brittle” material, it is considered one of the toughest and least brittle of the ceramics. It was found to be
within allowable limits, for the most extreme load case. Originally, these two parts were mated with an Inconel pin going through
both, but after realizing that the assembly was over-constrained it was substituted for a stainless-steel wire rope to allow for a solid
but slightly compliant assembly.
 The analysis of the shim covers various loading scenarios including loads that are transmitted to the shim, normal to the PSZ
surface, loads that are at an angle toroidally and loads that have a vertical component in the event that the center stack has a vertical
displacement. These load types are shown in Figure 13. Additionally, a location for a pair of strain gauges is identified and an
expected strain measurement is available for each load case. The shim block strain gauges are calibrated with respect to the lateral
load during load testing. In most cases, both stress and fatigue life are found to be within the allowable.

 Fig. 13 Shim Test Load Types.

 VII. DISCUSSION OF VERTICAL LOADING ON THE SHIM

  The calculation of record is J. Fang’s casing/TF G-10 ring Transient structural analysis. This calculation [9] has loads at the
shims/load cells plotted in time and summarized. Thermal growth is not the source of the vertical load. It occurs too slowly. The
source of the vertical load is first, the rotation at the flange due to the bending behavior of the casing under the Halo lateral loads.
There is an upward load on one side and possibly a downward load as well depending on the vibration. The other source of the
vertical load is the VDE disruption in which the plasma moves up or down then quenches. The currents induced in the shell can
be above or below the equatorial plane and cause upward or downward loading when reacted by the poloidal coil fields. These
usually precede the halo load, but the dynamic response of the casing may cause these loads to be concurrent at some point in the
transient. In [9], J. Fang constructed a number of load cases with these loads superimposed - Halo loads from A. Brooks[7] and
VDE loads from P. Titus [8].

A. Results of the Tests

 The first test sample cracked after getting through 90% of the 90% performance level. It was able to withstand at least 10 cycles
of the highest load – 27838 N. After investigation into the failed components, it was observed that a machining error in the parts
resulted in a bump on the Inconel part, so the load was not distributed evenly across the PSZ surface.
 To resolve the issue, the bump/protrusion in the Inconel was machined out. Additionally, copper foil was put in between the
Inconel and PSZ wedge to further smooth out the contact surface and provide a means to adjust the assembly's rigidity.

Fig. 16 Removal of “Bump” or Protrusion.

The test now passed for all 3 samples. However, afterwards it was found that the vertical load case did not evenly distribute the
load across the desired contact area of the PSZ which would invalidate the test. Further analysis refinements which included adding
in a small wedge representing the centerstack hitting the LSS improved the results and resulted in all load cases being under the
allowable stress. It also showed that the vertical load case that was tested, albeit not as intended, does result in similar stress patterns
and magnitudes as the loading case in real life and therefore these parts have been validated.

 IX. CONCLUSION
  Lateral support shims have been qualified for the expected loads. These are instruments to address uncertainties in the halo
loading on the machine. This translates to uncertainties in the qualification of the shims.
  The shims will be installed on NSTXU with the provision that either NSTXU will replace them (either a new design or just new
parts) if we exceed our limit from the tests, OR we realize that the Halo loads impart more or less force to the LSS.
  The shims are being installed to better understand the severity of the structural response of the machine to disruptions, and better
understand plasma disruption behavior of NSTXU operating at full performance parameters.
  The lateral loading analytically was predicted for the original TF/CS bundle that had a Ohmic Heating (OH) coil G-10 support
ring that had gone through the VPI process. This degraded strength and modulus. The new bundle has a much stronger OH support
ring but retains the design and analysis of the old ring.
 Initial testing has been done to calibrate the strain gauges that will be on these shims and provide feedback on the force that
these shims will experience. This data will give much insight into the nature and effect of the Halo loads on the Center stack casing
(CSC).
