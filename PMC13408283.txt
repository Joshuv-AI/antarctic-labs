Impact of hepatic radiotherapy on survival and safety outcomes in small cell lung cancer patients with liver metastases: a retrospective study
Authors: Xiaoli Liu, Xinrui Yu, Zhonghui Wei, Xiaolong Wang, Minghao Yu, Wenxing Cui, Yankang Li, Jinming Yu, Kaikai Zhao, Xiangjiao Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Patients with extensive-stage small cell lung cancer (ES-SCLC) and liver metastases (LM) are known to have particularly poor clinical outcomes, creating a significant therapeutic dilemma regarding optimal management of hepatic metastases beyond conventional systemic therapies.Objectives:This study sought to investigate the impact of adding LM-directed radiotherapy to systemic therapy on survival outcomes and treatment-related adverse events in patients with ES-SCLC and liver metastases.Design:This retrospective study enrolled ES-SCLC patients with LM treated at our institution between January 2019 and March 2025, all of whom received at least one cycle of systemic therapy. Patients were grouped by whether they received LM‑directed radiotherapy after LM diagnosis.Methods:Propensity score matching (PSM) was used to balance baseline characteristics between patients in the two groups. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were plotted to estimate survival endpoints. Treatment-related adverse events were graded per Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0 (CTCAE v5.0).Results:After PSM, a total of 495 patients were analyzed, among whom 138 underwent LM-directed radiotherapy. After a median follow-up of 38.1 months, patients in the radiotherapy group had a median overall survival (OS) of 12.1 months, significantly longer than the 6.7 months observed in the nonradiotherapy group (hazard ratios (HR), 0.51; 95% confidence intervals (CI): 0.41–0.63; p < 0.001). The 1-year OS rates were 50.1% and 21.2%, respectively. Among patients who received LM-directed radiotherapy during first-line systemic treatment, both systemic progression-free survival (PFS) (5.1 vs 3.7 months; HR, 0.71; 95% CI: 0.54–0.93; p = 0.012) and intrahepatic PFS (6.7 vs 3.9 months; HR, 0.59; 95% CI: 0.45–0.79; p < 0.001) were significantly prolonged compared with systemic therapy alone. Treatment-related toxicity was mild, with only one patient experiencing grade ⩾3 transaminase elevation and one patient with grade ⩾3 hyperbilirubinemia. The incidence of grade ⩾3 hematologic toxicity was low, and leukocytopenia was the most common event at 14.50%.Conclusion:In patients with ES-SCLC and LM, the addition of LM-directed radiotherapy to systemic therapy provides a significant survival benefit with acceptable treatment-related toxicity. These findings suggest that LM-directed radiotherapy can serve as a safe and effective treatment option for this patient population with poor prognosis.Keywords: extensive-stage small cell lung cancer, hepatic lesion irradiation, liver metastases, safety, survival benefits

Introduction

Small cell lung cancer (SCLC) is a highly aggressive neuroendocrine malignancy characterized by rapid proliferation and a strong propensity for early metastasis. 1 Accounting for approximately 14% of all lung cancer diagnoses, SCLC poses a significant clinical challenge, with a dismal 5-year relative survival rate of only 8%. 2 This poor prognosis is particularly evident in the extensive-stage (ES-SCLC) disease, which is present in about two-thirds of patients at initial diagnosis. These individuals face a median overall survival (OS) of approximately 10 months and a 2-year survival rate below 7%. 3

The high metastatic potential of SCLC commonly involves distant sites such as the liver, bone, brain, and adrenal glands. 4 The presence of extra-thoracic metastases is a well-established marker of worsened prognosis. 5 Notably, among these lesions, liver metastases (LM) have the highest rate, occurring in over 30% of patients and often emerging early in the disease course. 6 Approximately 17.5% of SCLC patients present with LM at diagnosis—a rate nearly five times higher than that observed in nonsmall cell lung cancer (NSCLC; 3.8%). 7 Moreover, SCLC patients with liver involvement exhibit the worst survival outcomes compared to those with metastases confined to other sites, such as the brain or bone. 8 Multivariate analyses from prospective studies have further confirmed that baseline LM and a metastatic burden of ⩾3 lesions serve as independent negative prognostic factors for OS. 9

In recent years, the addition of antiprogrammed cell death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) immune checkpoint inhibitors to conventional chemotherapy regimens of platinum-based agent and etoposide have further extended the OS by 2–4 months in ES-SCLC patients. 10 Currently, the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) SCLC panel recommends platinum-based chemotherapy plus etoposide plus atezolizumab or durvalumab followed by immunotherapy maintenance as the preferred regimens for patients with ES-SCLC. 11 However, while specific therapeutic strategies exist for bone or brain metastases, evidence-based systemic approaches for patients with LM remain limited. Moreover, patients with ES-SCLC and LM derive limited benefit from immunotherapy, underscoring the urgent need for novel and effective treatment strategies to improve their outcomes.

The rationale for this study is grounded in two key scientific observations. First, hepatic metastases establish profoundly immunosuppressive microenvironments through multiple mechanisms, which may facilitate tumor progression and impair response to systemic therapy. 12 Second, radiotherapy exerts dual therapeutic effects: direct local tumor control and systemic immune modulation through abscopal responses. 13 However, the abscopal effect currently remains largely a theoretical hypothesis and has yet to be established as a definitive clinical therapeutic effect. Therefore, in the absence of prospective randomized controlled evidence, this study rigorously evaluates the clinical efficacy of liver-directed radiotherapy, which holds important practical significance for guiding clinical decision-making and identifying patient subgroups most likely to benefit.

Materials and methods

Study design and patients

The retrospective study was designed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of radiotherapy to LM in patients with ES-SCLC. We analyzed data from patients diagnosed with SCLC and LM at Shandong Cancer Hospital affiliated with Shandong First Medical University from January 2019 to March 2025. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was authorized by the Institutional Review Board of Shandong Cancer Hospital affiliated with Shandong First Medical University.

The inclusion criteria were (1) age of ⩾18 years old, (2) pathologically confirmed SCLC, (3) extensive-stage disease with LM as defined by the Veterans Administration Lung Study Group (VALG) criteria, with hepatic metastases diagnosed via contrast-enhanced abdominal computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and PET-CT or pathological confirmation for equivocal lesions, (4) receipt of at least one line of systemic anticancer therapy. Patients with other malignancies and those who could not tolerate antitumor treatment due to physical condition were excluded.

Treatment

This study retrospectively enrolled patients diagnosed with ES-SCLC and LM who had received at least one line of anticancer therapy. For patients who received liver-directed radiotherapy, prescription dose and fractionation were determined by the radiation oncologist according to tumor burden, lesion location, and hepatic functional reserve. Patients were treated with conventional fractionation or hypofractionated radiotherapy, among which hypofractionation was the predominant regimen. Patients were immobilized in the supine position using customized fixation devices, and simulation was performed using contrast-enhanced CT alone or in combination with MRI. Based on the acquired simulation images, the gross tumor volume (GTV) and other target volumes were delineated in accordance with the recommendations outlined in the International Commission on Radiation Units and Measurements (ICRU) Reports 50, 62, and 83. Organs at risk (OARs) contoured included the normal liver (total liver minus GTV), stomach, duodenum, small bowel, bilateral kidneys, and spinal cord. Intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) was applied in most cases, and stereotactic ablative radiotherapy (SBRT) was adopted for patients with limited hepatic metastatic lesions. As this is a retrospective study, radiotherapy plans, dose-volume histograms, fractionation records, and on-treatment imaging data were retrieved from the institutional treatment planning system and electronic medical records.

Outcomes

Baseline characteristics encompassed age, gender, smoking history, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status (ECOG PS), presence of distant metastases, the timing and number of LM, prior treatment lines, prior thoracic radiotherapy, immunotherapy history, and prior prophylactic cranial irradiation (PCI) history. The primary endpoints of this respective analysis were OS and safety. The secondary endpoints were systemic progression-free survival (PFS) and intrahepatic PFS in patients treated with radiotherapy combined with first-line systemic therapy following the diagnosis of LM. OS was defined as the time from clinical diagnosis of SCLC with LM to the date of death from any cause or the last follow-up time. Systemic PFS was defined as the time from the clinical diagnosis of ES-SCLC with LM to disease progression (according to RECIST v1.1) or death from any cause. Intrahepatic PFS was defined as the time from clinical diagnosis to progression of intrahepatic lesions or death. Safety evaluations primarily focused on hepatic function dynamics during and postradiotherapy, with extended follow-up monitoring, along with hematologic toxicities induced by radiation. All adverse events were rigorously graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE v5.0).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were employed to summarize the baseline and therapeutic characteristics of the study population. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages, while continuous variables were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). To address baseline imbalances in covariates between the two groups, propensity score matching (PSM) was performed. The propensity score for each patient was estimated using a multivariable logistic regression model that included age, gender, ECOG PS, smoking status, presence of other distant metastases, the timing and number of LM, prior treatment lines, prior thoracic radiotherapy, immunotherapy history, and prior PCI history. Patients from the two cohorts were matched in a 1:3 ratio using the nearest neighbor method, with a caliper width of 0.2. Comparisons of baseline characteristics were conducted using chi-square tests for categorical variables and t-tests for continuous variables. OS and PFS were estimated using Kaplan–Meier method, and survival curves were compared with the log-rank test. For exploratory subgroup analyses, unstratified Cox proportional hazards models were utilized to calculate HRs and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), with results visualized in a forest plot. Univariate and multivariable Cox regression analyses were conducted to identify significant prognostic factors, with variables reaching p < 0.05 in univariate analysis being included in the multivariable model. All statistical tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Moreover, all statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS (version 27.0) and R software (version 4.4.3).

This manuscript was prepared in accordance with the STROBE reporting guideline, 14 and the corresponding STROBE checklist is provided as Supplemental Appendix.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 832 patients with ES-SCLC and LM were enrolled in this retrospective study between January 2019 and March 2025, and 141 of them received liver-directed radiotherapy (Figure 1). The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are listed in Table 1. Overall, most patients were male (82.1%), and the majority of patients had had a good performance status. Patients in the LM radiotherapy group were more likely to have developed LM during the treatment after being diagnosed with SCLC compared with those in the nonradiotherapy group (p < 0.001). Besides, 36.2% of patients in the LM radiotherapy cohort were diagnosed with less than three LM, while 22.1% had less than three LM in the nonradiotherapy cohort (p < 0.001).

After 1:3 PSM, a total of 495 patients were included, among whom 138 received radiotherapy for LM. Baseline characteristics were well balanced between the two groups, with no statistically significant intergroup differences observed across all matched variables. Regarding metastatic burden, the presence of extrahepatic metastases was similar. Half of the patients in the LM radiotherapy group had received prior thoracic radiotherapy (50.0%), compared with 44.0% in the nonradiotherapy group. Among the 138 patients receiving liver-directed radiotherapy, hepatic irradiation was delivered in the first-line setting for 73 patients (52.90%). Most patients received radical-intent radiotherapy (85.51%). Non-SBRT regimens dominated the treatment modalities (98.55%), and only two patients were treated with SBRT. Descriptive dosimetric analyses were performed among patients with complete available radiotherapy planning data. The median equivalent dose in 2 Gy fractions (EQD2) of liver irradiation was 48.75 Gy (IQR: 39.81–55.25 Gy, range: 4.58–78.12 Gy), delivered over a median of 15 fractions (IQR: 10–17, range: 3–30), with a median per-fraction dose of 3.00 Gy (IQR: 3.00–3.00 Gy, range: 1.00–8.00 Gy). The median GTV was 49.10 cm 3 (IQR: 12.75–183.60 cm 3 ), and the median mean liver dose (minus GTV) was 10.01 Gy (IQR: 6.44–14.52 Gy) (Table 2).

Survival outcomes

With follow-up data collected up to June 30, 2025, the median follow-up time for OS of the entire study cohort was 38.1 months. Patients who received radiotherapy for LM had a significantly longer median OS of 12.1 months (95% CI: 10.7–13.8) compared to 6.7 months (95% CI: 6.2–7.2) in those who did not (HR, 0.51; 95% CI: 0.41–0.63; p < 0.001, Figure 2). The 1-year OS rates were 50.1% (95% CI: 42.2%–59.6%) and 21.2% (95% CI: 17.2%–26.1%) in the two groups, respectively. In this study, the timing of LM radiotherapy initiation was not uniform, and some patients received hepatic irradiation after disease progression. Consequently, the OS analysis may be subject to immortal time bias. To assess the potential impact of this bias on our conclusions, we performed landmark analyses as sensitivity analyses (Figure S1). Given that the median time from the diagnosis of LM to the initiation of LM radiotherapy in the radiotherapy group was 3.88 months, we selected 4 months as the primary landmark point and additionally performed landmark analyses at 3 and 5 months to verify the robustness of the results. The landmark analysis showed that patients receiving LM radiotherapy consistently exhibited a trend toward better OS at all selected time points. However, owing to the relatively small sample size of irradiated patients, the difference between group failed to reach statistical significance. Using 4 months as the primary landmark point, the analysis yielded a HR of 0.74 (95% CI: 0.54–1.02; p = 0.062). The 3-month and 5-month landmark analyses yielded HRs of 0.80 (95% CI: 0.58–1.10; p = 0.174) and 0.74 (95% CI: 0.54–1.01; p = 0.056), respectively. The HRs from the 4-month and 5-month landmark analyses both suggested a potential survival benefit of LM radiotherapy, with p values at the margin of significance. Taken together, these landmark results consistently showed a survival benefit of LM radiotherapy in the same direction across different time points, suggesting that the primary conclusion is unlikely to be substantially influenced by immortal time bias and that the findings are robust.

To assess the consistency of the survival benefit associated with LM radiotherapy across diverse patient subgroups, exploratory subgroup analyses were further conducted across predefined clinical stratifications for OS (Figure 3). In the vast majority of predefined subgroups, patients receiving liver-directed radiotherapy combined with systemic therapy achieved significantly longer OS than those treated with systemic therapy alone. No statistically significant interaction was identified across any stratified clinical factor. Notably, survival benefits from hepatic radiotherapy were consistently observed irrespective of whether patients received subsequent systemic immunotherapy or antiangiogenic targeted agents after the development of LM. However, the survival advantage associated with liver irradiation was numerically attenuated among patients who had received ⩾2 prior lines of systemic treatment and those with a history of prior immunotherapy.

To identify independent prognostic factors influencing patient outcomes, univariate, and multivariable Cox regression analyses were performed (Table 3). The final multivariate Cox analysis showed that LM radiotherapy was strongly associated with significant improvements in OS, with reducing the risk of death by 44% (HR, 0.56; 95% CI: 0.44–0.70; p < 0.001). Female patients demonstrated a trend toward superior OS compared with male counterparts (HR, 0.64; 95% CI: 0.51–0.82; p < 0.001). Additionally, post-LM administration of immunotherapy and antiangiogenic targeted therapy were both independent protective prognostic factors for OS (p < 0.001). However, the presence of ⩾3 liver metastatic lesions (HR, 2.06; 95% CI: 1.65–2.58; p < 0.001) and prior receipt of two or more lines of systemic therapy (HR, 1.49; 95% CI: 1.07–2.07; p = 0.018) were significantly associated with inferior OS and constituted independent adverse prognostic factors. Moreover, univariate Cox regression revealed that prior immunotherapy and post-LM thoracic radiotherapy were significantly correlated with favorable OS (p < 0.05). Nevertheless, neither factor retained statistical significance after multivariate adjustment for confounding variables.

Since some patients received LM radiotherapy after disease progression, we selected those who received LM radiotherapy during first-line systemic therapy after LM diagnosis from the total of 141 irradiated individuals to evaluate its effect on PFS. After PSM, 72 patients in the LM radiotherapy group and 267 in the nonradiotherapy group were included (Figure S2 and Table S1). Data analysis results indicate that the PFS was significantly longer in patients who received LM radiotherapy than in those who did not (5.1 vs 3.7 months; HR, 0.71; 95% CI: 0.54–0.93; p = 0.012). For local control of intrahepatic lesions, the radiotherapy group demonstrated significantly longer median intrahepatic PFS compared to the nonradiotherapy group (6.7 vs 3.9 months; HR, 0.59, 95% CI: 0.45–0.79; p < 0.001). Figure 4 illustrates the efficacy of LM radiotherapy in achieving tumor reduction in a representative case. Moreover, the median OS was 11.9 months (95% CI: 9.0–15.0 months) in the radiotherapy group versus 6.5 months (95% CI: 6.0–7.3 months) in the nonradiotherapy (HR, 0.54; 95% CI: 0.40–0.74; p < 0.001, Figure 5(a)–(c)). These consistent results across efficacy endpoints indicate that combining LM radiotherapy confers significant improvements in both overall PFS and local tumor control.

Safety

Treatment safety was assessed by monitoring hepatic function within the first month and at three months after LM radiotherapy, as well as hematologic toxicity during the treatment period. A total of 95 patients had liver function test results available within 1 month after LM radiotherapy, and 49 patients had data at the three-month follow-up. Analysis showed that nearly half of the patients maintained normal liver function after radiotherapy. Throughout the 1- and 3-month follow-up periods after radiotherapy, the most common liver function abnormality was Grade 1 hypoalbuminemia, followed by Grade 1 elevation of transaminases. Only one case of Grade 3 transaminase elevation was noted at one month after radiotherapy, and one case of Grade 3 hyperbilirubinemia was recorded at the three-month follow-up. Overall, liver function tolerance following radiotherapy for liver metastases was favorable (Table 4). As for the hematologic toxicity, 131 patients underwent continuous blood cell monitoring during the LM radiotherapy. The most frequently reported adverse event was leukopenia (50.38%), while Grade 3 or higher adverse events were relatively uncommon, with leukopenia remaining the most prevalent (14.50%). The incidence of neutropenia, anemia, and thrombocytopenia of any grade did not exceed 30% (Table S2). Overall, the treatment was associated with a favorable tolerability profile and an acceptable burden of treatment-related adverse events.

Discussion

The results of this study demonstrate that, for patients with ES‑SCLC and LM, the addition of liver‑directed radiotherapy to systemic therapy significantly prolongs OS compared with systemic therapy alone (12.1 months vs 6.7 months; HR, 0.51; 95% CI: 0.41–0.63; p < 0.001), with a manageable toxicity profile. These results challenge the conventional palliative pharmacotherapy paradigm for ES-SCLC with LM, demonstrating that incorporating localized therapeutic interventions during active treatment can yield meaningful survival benefits.

Patients with ES-SCLC generally have a poor prognosis, particularly those with LM. In the immunotherapy era, etoposide-platinum combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has become the standard first-line treatment for ES-SCLC, significantly improving long-term outcomes. Among the pivotal phase III trials investigating this combination, only the ASTRUM-005 study (with serplulimab) and the RATIONALE-312 study (with tislelizumab) have demonstrated a survival benefit in the subgroup of patients with LM.15–21 Yu et al. also reported that melanoma and NSCLC patients presenting with LM, yet without involvement of other organs, showed significantly reduced responsiveness to ICIs relative to patients free from metastases. 22 This may be attributed to the liver’s intrinsically immunosuppressive microenvironment—characterized by Kupffer cells and tolerogenic dendritic cells (DCs) with potent immune-inhibitory functions and concurrent systemic T cell dysfunction evidenced by reduced peripheral T cell numbers as well as diminished tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte diversity and functionality.22–25

Mechanistically, radiotherapy not only induces tumor cell apoptosis but also promotes the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and enhances immune cell infiltration into tumors through multiple pathways, thereby converting immunologically “cold” tumors into “hot” tumors. 26 In a retrospective study of treatment-naïve SCLC patients with LM, 69 patients received localized radiotherapy during systemic treatment. 27 The radiotherapy group showed numerically longer OS compared to systemic therapy alone (13.40 vs 10.33 months), while this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.082). These findings suggest that incorporating liver-directed radiotherapy into treatment paradigms may create new opportunities for survival improvement. Our findings validate and extend prior observations. Compared to nonradiotherapy controls, the addition of radiotherapy not only significantly improved OS and intrahepatic PFS (6.7 vs 3.9 months) but also significantly enhanced systemic PFS (5.1 vs 3.7 months). Moreover, multiple landmark analyses were performed to correct for potential immortal time bias in OS assessment. Despite the limited sample size of the radiotherapy group after adjustment, a survival benefit favoring hepatic irradiation was still observed with borderline statistical significance, which further strengthens the robustness of our findings. This systemic benefit cannot be fully attributed to improved local control alone, suggesting that liver-directed radiotherapy may elicit abscopal antitumor effects, thereby exerting a broader therapeutic impact beyond the irradiated site.

In the multivariable analysis, post-LM immunotherapy and antiangiogenic targeted therapy primarily with anlotinib showed survival benefits of comparable magnitude to radiotherapy. This indicates that, in the immunotherapy era, a combined local and systemic treatment paradigm may confer superior disease control for ES-SCLC patients with LM. This effect was further corroborated by subgroup analyses, which showed that the survival benefit of LM radiotherapy persisted regardless of subsequent receipt of immunotherapy or targeted therapy. Previous studies have focused on the survival impact of thoracic radiotherapy in ES-SCLC. Although thoracic radiotherapy improves local control and outcomes in selected patients, its benefit for patients with LM remains controversial. A recent meta-analysis reported that patients with LM did not derive a benefit from thoracic radiotherapy.28,29 In our univariate analysis, thoracic radiotherapy after LM was associated with improved OS, but it was not an independent prognostic factor after multivariable adjustment, suggesting that the benefit of thoracic local control in patients with LM may be offset by the rapid progression of hepatic lesions and other biological factors. This observation requires further validation in large-scale, stratified prospective studies. Additionally, we found that having three or more LM was an adverse prognostic factor. However, in subgroup analysis, even patients with multiple hepatic metastases benefited from radiotherapy. This finding aligns with the concept in the recent RISE trial protocol that metastasis-directed therapy can be considered for up to 10 metastatic lesions, reflecting a shift in the radiotherapy indication from lesion-number based criteria definition to clinical-feasibility guided selection. 30 Findings from the BRALIBREST trial have demonstrated that clinical subtypes of LM (such as induced and recurrent oligometastatic disease) and overall intrahepatic tumor burden are strong prognostic determinants, implying that classification solely based on metastatic lesion count may obscure underlying biological heterogeneity of tumors. 31 Constrained by the retrospective nature of our cohort, most enrolled patients presented with multiple or diffuse hepatic metastatic lesions, precluding accurate quantitative assessment of total liver tumor burden. Future studies should incorporate quantitative measures of total metastatic burden and clinical classification of LM to further stratify patients, thereby exploring the therapeutic benefits of radiotherapy across different subgroups.

In terms of radiotherapy techniques and dosimetry, the regimens in this study were predominantly non-SBRT (98.55%). Recent technical comparative studies have indicated that, apart from SBRT, interventional techniques such as CT‑guided interstitial high‑dose‑rate brachytherapy (HDR‑IRT) also offer important clinical value.32 –34 Dosimetric analyses suggest that HDR-IRT confers superior advantages over SBRT in sparing normal hepatic parenchyma and adjacent vital organs, with a markedly lower mean whole-liver radiation dose, making it ideal for patients with limited hepatic functional reserve or repeated long-term treatment and for metastatic lesions of oligo-persistence/oligo-progression subtype or those adjacent to radiosensitive critical organs.35–37 However, constrained by real‑world factors such as patient performance status and lesion number (most patients had >3 hepatic lesions), the choice of radiotherapy modality in this study was inherently limited. In the final analytic cohort, only two patients received SBRT, eight patients received low‑dose palliative whole‑liver irradiation, and none were treated with HDR-IRT.

Regarding safety, previous studies demonstrated that repeated irradiation can be well tolerated with favorable safety profiles in carefully selected patients, even those with prior histories of liver radiotherapy or ablation, when performed in strict compliance with dosimetric criteria.38,39 Our study confirms that LM radiotherapy in ES-SCLC patients does not lead to an abnormally high incidence of severe adverse events and is consistent with previous SBRT studies, which show that rigorous adherence to protocol-specified radiotherapy is safe and associated with a low rate of severe radiation-induced liver disease.40,41 However, the safety evaluation in this study has several limitations. The liver function monitoring data were incomplete; only a small subset of patients had available liver function follow‑up data at 3 months after radiotherapy. Additionally, to minimize bias in the retrospective study, we included only objective laboratory abnormalities in the statistical analysis, while subjective gastrointestinal adverse events such as nausea and abdominal distension were not analyzed. This may have led to an underestimation of the impact on patients’ quality of life and symptom burden. Postradiotherapy liver function assessment should include longer‑term follow‑up and a more comprehensive battery of liver function tests. Therefore, future prospective studies should incorporate more robust dynamic liver function assessments and patient‑reported physical symptoms to more comprehensively evaluate treatment safety.

The findings of this study have direct implications for clinical practice. Integrating the survival and safety data, local therapy for hepatic metastases is not simply a matter of choosing a single radiotherapy modality. We recommend individualized treatment decisions for ES-SCLC patients with LM. For patients with multiple or diffuse hepatic metastases, radiotherapy may be selectively applied to dominant lesions (e.g., larger lesions or those adjacent to the liver capsule or bile ducts) to palliate symptoms. In terms of technique selection, the anatomical characteristics of the lesions, the patient’s general condition, prior treatment history, and the anticipated subsequent systemic therapies should be considered when individualizing the radiotherapy modality and dose. A long-term safety and quality-of-life monitoring system should also be established. At the same time, the inherent limitations of this study must be acknowledged. First, owing to its retrospective nature, even with propensity score matching, residual selection bias and unmeasured confounding cannot be entirely eliminated. Second, despite the multitime-point sensitivity analyses, heterogeneity in the timing of liver radiotherapy and in systemic treatment regimens may still confound the efficacy and safety results. Hence, prospective clinical trials are warranted to confirm our findings. Based on these results, we have initiated a single-arm phase II trial investigating the efficacy and safety of LM radiotherapy combined with systemic pharmacotherapy in ES-SCLC, which is currently in the recruitment phase. This trial will help fill the existing gaps in evidence and further define the clinical value of radiotherapy in improving outcomes for ES-SCLC patients with LM.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this retrospective study reveals that in patients with ES-SCLC and LM, the integration of liver-directed radiotherapy with systemic therapy is associated with a significantly prolonged survival compared to conventional approaches, and the treatment-related toxicities are controllable and manageable. Our findings provide a promising therapeutic concept for this patient population with an exceptionally poor prognosis. Future prospective randomized controlled trials are warranted to validate these results and to further define the optimal timing of hepatic irradiation and patient selection criteria in this setting.
