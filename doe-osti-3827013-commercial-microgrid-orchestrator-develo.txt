 ABSTRACT

This report presents the commercialization and validation of the Microgrid Orchestrator, a supervisory
control technology for networked microgrids developed by Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) in
collaboration with New Sun Road (NSR). Networked microgrids, defined as electrically interconnected
and independently operable microgrids, offer significant opportunities to improve both economic
performance and resilience through coordinated control of distributed energy resources. In grid-connected
operation, coordinated dispatch can reduce energy costs by optimizing power sharing, energy storage
usage, and interactions with the utility grid. In islanded operation, particularly during extreme events,
coordinated management of available generation and storage can reduce load shedding and extend the
duration of autonomous service.

To bring this technology toward commercial deployment, NSR integrated the Microgrid Orchestrator into
its Stellar Microgrid Operating System (MOS) platform, a cloud-based microgrid control system with
global field deployment experience. A representative test system consisting of three interconnected
microgrids linked by distribution feeders was developed to evaluate performance. Two use cases were
examined using both numerical simulation and hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) testing in the Typhoon HIL
environment: (1) cost reduction during normal grid-connected operation and (2) resilience enhancement
during islanded operation under grid outage conditions. Results from these studies validate the core
control functions of the commercial software implementation and demonstrate the potential of the
Microgrid Orchestrator to deliver economic and resilience benefits in networked microgrid applications.
The project also provides insight into market readiness and commercial value of coordinated control
technologies for advanced distributed energy systems.

 1. INTRODUCTION

1.1 BACKGROUND

The power grid is under increasing stress to meet electricity demand. Electrification of vehicles and
buildings and the effects of large data centers are necessitating investment in new assets to ensure
resilience during emergency events. Distributed energy resources (DERs) and microgrids will become
invaluable tools for harnessing renewable, local power during grid outages and under normal operating
conditions as local distribution systems become overloaded. By leveraging local renewable generation,
microgrids have the capability to disconnect from the main grid and operate independently in island
mode. This feature not only bolsters resilience but also ensures the continuous powering of critical loads
when the main grid is unavailable. Although microgrids offer great promise for energy resilience, they
also have limitations. As depicted in Figure 1 [1], current microgrid technology creates individual islands
with rigid boundaries. In a highly distributed grid organized across multiple microgrids, this rigid
approach would create fragmented energy silos with controllers that do not collaborate with one another.
The problem with this approach is that renewable generation and demand may be unevenly distributed:
one microgrid may have excess renewable generation that goes to waste, whereas another may have to
shut down facilities because of insufficient energy. Networked microgrids can help tackle this challenge
by allowing power exchange among microgrids and maximizing the use of renewable energy while
increasing the system resilience.

 (b) Networked microgrids collaborate to achieve higher levels of resilience by integrating more renewables.

Networked microgrids are clusters of colocated, electrically connected, independent microgrids that
together can function as a single, aggregate power system. Crucially, a networked microgrid is greater
than the sum of its parts: by connecting systems with heterogeneous loads and generation, resources can
be used more efficiently, providing greater reliability and islanding capacity at lower cost [2]. Networked
microgrids improve community resilience by extending the geographic scope of islandable microgrids to

 2

serve more customers and allowing resilience to be expanded modularly (i.e., networked microgrids can
initially target the most critical emergency services and then add new nodes to extend resilience to
additional critical services, businesses, and homes).

1.2 CHALLENGES

A key challenge in networked microgrids is managing a large and diverse array of assets and integrating
microgrid and equipment controllers from various manufacturers. The proposed technology addresses the
complexity, scalability, and information-sharing issues through decentralized control. New Sun Road
(NSR), a Public Benefit Company and the project's commercialization partner, addresses interoperability
with its vendor-agnostic Stellar platform.

In traditional centralized energy management, all the devices, distribution networks, and control
objectives are modeled in a single, large optimization problem [3]. This approach suffers from three core
issues. First, centralized energy management requires detailed information about the configuration of each
microgrid and its components and loads and may even require direct control over those components. This
can create friction and threaten projects because microgrid customers are usually reluctant to allow
utilities to directly access and control their components for both safety and privacy reasons. Second,
adding new microgrids to the network requires updating the entire centralized problem, requiring new
modeling and development. Third, centralized methods are generally subject to “the curse of
dimensionality”: as the number of microgrids, utility- and customer-owned DERs, and intelligently
controlled grid-edge devices increases, the solution efficiency of centralized energy management
decreases rapidly.

These limitations highlight the need for a scalable, privacy-preserving approach to the control and
management of networked microgrids. A decentralized framework can enable coordination across diverse
assets and stakeholders without requiring full disclosure of sensitive customer information or complete
centralized control. Such an approach is essential for supporting the continued growth of interconnected
microgrids while maintaining operational flexibility, security, and customer trust.

1.3 OBJECTIVES

To enable scalable control of networked microgrids for improved system efficiency, reliability, and
resilience, the US Department of Energy’s (DOE’s) Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) has
developed a distributed, scalable networked microgrid controller, referred to as the Microgrid
Orchestrator. The Microgrid Orchestrator is a control layer developed by ORNL to coordinate the
operation of interconnected microgrids. It uses decentralized optimization to manage power and energy
exchanges among microgrids while incorporating protocols for stability, synchronization, and fault
handling [4].

The Microgrid Orchestrator employs a well-known, scalable distributed optimization approach: the
alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM) [5]. Under this approach, the Microgrid Orchestrator
influences the behavior of independent microgrids without imposing set points on all assets within each
microgrid. This approach is more scalable and maintains microgrid ownership and control at the local
microgrid-controller level, thereby addressing the shortcomings of centralized approaches [6, 7].

The ORNL Microgrid Orchestrator has been developed with support from the Solar Energy Technologies
Office (SETO) under Grant No. 37771. It has been demonstrated in both control-hardware-in-the-loop
(CHIL) environments and laboratory-scale networked microgrids at ORNL’s Grid Research Innovation
and Development Center (Grid-C) facilities, and its underlying methods have been extensively
documented in scientific publications and conference proceedings.

 3

Although the technology has been developed by ORNL, the lack of commercially available products for
coordinating networked microgrids remains a significant barrier to deployment by early adopters. Notable
demonstrations, such as the Citadel project at EPB [8] and the networked microgrids at ORNL’s Grid-C
[9], have highlighted the technology’s potential in real-world settings. However, transitioning this
capability from laboratory environments to commercial products is essential to enable early adopters to
deploy the technology in practice, support the case for regulatory adaptation, and ultimately drive
widespread adoption.

To address the evolving challenges associated with the reliable, resilient, and cyber-secure integration of
energy storage and other inverter-based variable resources, this project proposes to commercialize the
Microgrid Orchestrator, a foundational control technology for enabling networked microgrids. The
technology was developed by ORNL in partnership with NSR, with support from the DOE Office of
Technology Commercialization (OTC) under Contract No. L110-1539.

NSR is a public benefit company with a mission to accelerate communities’ access to renewable energy.
Its commercially available Stellar Microgrid Operating System (MOS) cloud-based microgrid control
platform is used in more than 1,500 microgrids in 22 countries across applications including disaster
relief, commercial and agriculture facilities, telecommunications, and community- and utility-owned
microgrids. Stellar MOS provides cloud-based fleet management, remote control, data ingestion and
normalization, analytics tools, and optimized power and energy management using AI. NSR’s on-site
microgrid controller, the Stellar Edge, performs local control, communicates with microgrid equipment,
and functions as a gateway for remote monitoring and control. Like other commercial microgrid
controllers on the market, the current Stellar MOS controls individual microgrids and does not have the
capability to coordinate multiple electrically connected microgrids.

NSR’s Stellar platform is a key advantage for networked microgrid deployments, supporting dozens of
inverter, battery, smart meter, and controllable-load vendors. In a typical deployment (Figure 2), the
Stellar Edge serves as a gateway, supporting a wide range of communication protocols to interface with
field devices while streaming data to the cloud for visualization and processing within Stellar MOS. In
addition, Stellar MOS can integrate with other microgrid controllers and directly with web-enabled
devices. This flexibility aligns well with the Microgrid Orchestrator’s vendor-agnostic control
architecture.

1.4 SCOPE

This project will commercialize the Microgrid Orchestrator, a control technology for coordinating
networked microgrids, through a partnership between ORNL and NSR. Networked microgrids enable
colocated, electrically connected microgrids to share distributed generation (DG) and energy storage
resources, improving economic performance during grid-connected operation and resilience during
islanded operation. Under this project, ORNL and NSR will integrate the Microgrid Orchestrator into the
commercially available Stellar platform, validate the performance of the developed commercial Microgrid
Orchestrator in controlling networked microgrids through a CHIL demonstration, and assess the market
opportunity for this capability, thereby establishing a pathway to commercial deployment.

 2. METHODOLOGY

2.1 MICROGRID ORCHESTRATOR TECHNOLOGY DEVELOPED BY ORNL

The Microgrid Orchestrator technology developed by ORNL employs ADMM, a well-known and
scalable distributed optimization approach. Under this approach, the Microgrid Orchestrator influences
the behavior of independent microgrids without imposing set points on all assets within each microgrid.
This approach is more scalable and maintains ownership and control of the microgrids at the local
microgrid-controller level, thereby addressing the shortcomings of centralized approaches.

Given the different ownerships, objectives, and requirements for autonomy and data privacy, a multi-
objective optimization model considering various network operational objectives and constraints was
formulated to coordinate the operation of all active participants to improve economic performance and/or
system-operator performance objectives. The resulting optimization problem was iteratively solved in a
distributed manner using the ADMM algorithm. During each iteration, all participants could adjust their
schedules based on their own objectives and the price and nodal imbalance signals they received. The
Microgrid Orchestrator then updates the nodal price signals according to the generation-load imbalance at
the corresponding bus, as calculated using the previously updated schedules of all participants. The
updated price and nodal imbalance signals are distributed to participants at the corresponding buses for
another iteration until the calculated generation-load imbalances at all buses are sufficiently small (i.e.,
power balance is achieved at each bus under the updated price signals). Figure 3 illustrates the
information and data flow required by the proposed ADMM-based distributed energy management.

Under the proposed distributed control structure, microgrid controllers shield information about internal
microgrid components and energy consumers from the high-level controller by communicating only
power measurements at the point of common coupling (PCC) with the high-level controller (i.e., the
Microgrid Orchestrator). In this way, the autonomy and privacy of microgrid components and energy
consumers are preserved. In addition, the proposed distributed structure enables plug-and-play operation
for emerging controllable devices at the grid edge.

The Microgrid Orchestrator technology developed by ORNL uses a scalable, ADMM-based distributed
optimization approach to coordinate networked microgrids without requiring centralized control of
individual assets. By allowing each microgrid to optimize its own operation while exchanging only
limited information at the PCC, the technology preserves local autonomy, ownership, and data privacy.
This architecture also improves scalability, supports plug-and-play integration of new controllable

 5

devices, and enables coordinated operation that can enhance both economic performance and system
resilience.

2.2 COMMERCIAL MICROGRID ORCHESTRATOR DEVELOPMENT

The Microgrid Orchestrator technology was transferred from ORNL to NSR through the preparation of
existing code modules with clear and detailed documentation and instructions for execution and
extension; the documentation of a reference deployment architecture describing networking, processing,
and data storage layers; the identification of device-level control functions that power systems must
support and expose to the Microgrid Orchestrator; and the review of the reference deployment
architecture for deployment in NSR’s Microsoft Azure cloud environment. The team also reviewed the
functions of the Networked Microgrid Orchestrator (NMO) and summarized how the NMO will be
deployed with the Stellar platform to enable a complete power control system and operator interface as
the minimum viable product (MVP) for the commercialization project.

The Microgrid Orchestrator technology, originally developed by ORNL, is built on NSR’s commercially
available Stellar MOS platform. Stellar MOS provides cloud-based fleet management, remote control,
data ingestion and normalization, analytics, and AI-enabled optimization for power and energy
management. NSR’s on-site controller, the Stellar Edge, executes local control, communicates with
microgrid equipment, and serves as the gateway for remote monitoring and control. Figure 4 illustrates
the Stellar platform product with the Microgrid Orchestrator enabled.

The Orchestrator and local optimizer instances initiate and maintain message queuing telemetry transport
(MQTT) connections with NSR’s Azure IoT Hub to facilitate the exchange of price and quantity values
during the distributed optimization process. The local optimizers access telemetry data for their respective

 6

microgrids via the Stellar API server and can update the control states of their corresponding Stellar Edge
devices in the field by sending messages through the IoT Hub.

 Figure 4. Microgrid Orchestrator realized on the commercial Stellar platform.

Like many commercial microgrid controllers, Stellar MOS is designed to manage individual microgrids
and does not natively coordinate multiple electrically connected microgrids. Through a DOE Office of
Technology Commercialization–funded project, the Microgrid Orchestrator was integrated into the Stellar
platform, creating a commercial offering that extends Stellar’s capabilities to networked microgrids.

2.2.1 Controller Requirements to Support the NMO

The NMO interfaces with individual microgrid controllers at the tertiary level. For a microgrid controller
to be compatible with the NMO, it typically must include both cloud-based and on-premises components
and support the following functions:

• Implement the ADMM “local optimization subproblem.”
 o Given a schedule of prices, a reference net power injection, and penalty parameter from the
 NMO, solve for the optimal net power injection of the microgrid by minimizing local costs plus a
 penalty term. The penalty term is computed as the sum, over all time periods, of the penalty
 parameter multiplied by the square of the difference between the chosen power injection and the
 reference. Thus, a cost is imposed for deviating from the reference.
 o Obtain forecasts of load and variable renewable energy availability over the optimization horizon
 and formulate and solve an optimization model of microgrid operations that considers all costs
 and benefits within the microgrid over the horizon, as well as constraints on DER operation.
 o At a minimum, the controller should support a schedule spanning 24 h at hourly intervals. Shorter
 intervals, such as 5, 15, or 30 min, and longer time horizons of up to 72 or 96 h are recommended
 to improve performance. The flexibility to define prices and quantities more granularly, using

 7

 shorter intervals in the near term and coarser intervals toward the end of the horizon, is also
 recommended to limit optimization problem size and computation time while realizing
 performance gains.
 o The optimization must treat load as price-responsive, with a defined, finite private cost of load
 shedding (in dollars per kilowatt hour) for each load, referred to as the load’s “reserve price.”
 This implies that, at a sufficiently high price, consumption within each microgrid can be reduced
 to zero; therefore, a feasible dispatch and a “market-clearing” price always exist.
 o The optimization problem should have a configurable timeout on the order of 15 minutes. An
 NMO implementation will require responses to be returned within a specified time before
 proceeding to the next iteration of the ADMM algorithm. A solver algorithm that can return a
 feasible, “best-seen” solution if it exits early is recommended, as is one that can “warm-start”
 from the previous iteration.
• Execute secondary controls to follow the net dispatch.
 o Control all DERs within a microgrid so that net power at the interconnection points, including
 uncontrollable or unmetered sources and loads, follows the dispatch. This includes executing load
 shedding if prices exceed the reserve price.
 o Some deviation will always be present because of forecast error, model error, and the imperfect
 controllability of DERs. Whether and how participants are penalized or rewarded for high or low
 tracking error can be determined through agreements among participants.
• Communicate with the NMO using an appropriate messaging protocol and transport layer.
 o For the MVP commercial implementation, this project will use the MQTT messaging protocol
 over Transmission Control Protocol/Internet Protocol (TCP/IP), brokered by Microsoft Azure’s
 IoT Hub, with a JavaScript Object Notation (JSON)-based message format.

2.2.2 Operator Interface to Support the NMO

Networked microgrid deployment requires an operator interface to manage and analyze the system.
Although some deployments may require an on-premises control center, the standard interface should
support a remote control center and provide a dashboard that enables operators to perform the following
functions:

• Visualize real-time system states and forecasts, including:
 o Power and energy data
 o Alarms and the connectivity status of communication systems
 o Nodal prices, individual system dispatches, and tracking errors
 o Power exchange between microgrids in the network and with the grid
 o NMO status
• Adjust the configuration and control settings of the networked microgrid, including:
 o Defining the topology of the microgrids in the network and the power flow limits between them
 o Adjusting NMO parameters (e.g., timing and convergence tolerance)
• Access and analyze historical data, including:
 o Energy generation and consumption
 o System performance
 o Incidents and snapshots
• Optionally, simultaneously supervise multiple microgrid networks (i.e., manage a fleet of networked
 microgrids in different locations)

2.2.3 Cybersecurity Requirements to Support the NMO

Networked microgrid deployments should implement the following cybersecurity best practices for power
systems:

 8

• Restricted access to physical equipment, human–machine interfaces, and networking hardware and
 cabling
• Encryption of data communications between sites and the cloud, both in transit and at rest
• Cybersecurity training for all personnel with access to computers and information systems
• Application of the principle of least privilege to system access and user-based access control
• Routine audits and updates of software packages to address security vulnerabilities
• Alternative communication methods and operational protocols for use in the event of a cybersecurity
 breach (incident response plan)
• Disabling unnecessary services, ports, and protocols and enabling firewalls where possible

Because networked microgrids can include assets owned and operated by different entities, additional
care must be taken to ensure that software systems segregate data and permissions and do not expose one
entity’s private information to another. The implementation of networked microgrids using the Microgrid
Orchestrator employs a hierarchical, decentralized approach; no peer-to-peer or direct communication
occurs between microgrid controllers, nor can one microgrid control assets within another. In this
hierarchical approach, the primary risk is that one microgrid controller could be compromised and
become a bad actor. A rogue controller could cause physical disturbances in the network that affect the
primary and secondary control systems or protection systems of other microgrids, potentially leading to
faults or instability. It could also manipulate the NMO’s negotiation algorithm to prevent convergence to
optimal outcomes. These potential risks arising from indirect attacks are open research areas that warrant
further investigation but are beyond the scope of the MVP.

2.2.4 NSR’s Implementation of the NMO

The NMO implementation will consist of two new services integrated with NSR’s existing Stellar MOS
and deployed within the existing Azure Kubernetes Service (AKS) environment. Figure 5 shows the
anticipated deployment. The project and its new development efforts leverage a broad range of
preexisting functionality within the Stellar platform—NSR’s flagship product, which has been used in
more than 1,500 power systems by utilities, energy service companies, battery system manufacturers,
electric vehicle charger providers, and microgrid developers in more than 20 countries since its launch in
2016. The core elements of the platform are (1) Stellar MOS, which provides remote control, AI-enabled
optimization, and fleet management, and (2) the Stellar Edge, which is an on-premises microgrid
controller and IoT gateway.

 9

 Figure 5. Stellar Microgrid Orchestrator architecture.

The initial implementation will feature both “orchestrator” and “local” optimization services running
within the Azure cloud. The architecture will consist of one global orchestrator service and one local
optimizer instance for each participating microgrid. The global orchestrator will initiate an optimization
for each connected group of microgrids on a regular schedule (e.g., every 15 min). The orchestrator will
determine which local optimizers to group together by consulting microgrid metadata stored on the Stellar
API server.

The orchestrator and local optimizer instances will initiate and maintain MQTT connections with NSR’s
Azure IoT Hub to facilitate the exchange of price and quantity values during the distributed optimization
process. The local optimizers will access telemetry data for their respective microgrids via the Stellar API
server and will be able to update the control state of their corresponding Stellar Edge devices in the field
by sending messages through the IoT Hub.

A full optimization round consists of the following steps:

1. Orchestrator broadcast: The Orchestrator sends a provisional energy price and target quantity to the
 local optimizers. Initially, these values are the final price and target quantity from the previous
 optimization round; if no prior round exists, preconfigured initial values are used.
2. Local response: Each local optimizer computes and returns its preferred production quantity based
 on the provided price and target quantity.
3. Balance check and update: After receiving all preferred quantities, the Orchestrator checks the
 overall balance of the interconnected system. If the total quantity produced is within a predefined
 tolerance of the anticipated demand, the process terminates until the next scheduled run. Otherwise,

 10

 the Orchestrator adjusts the price upward (if total production is too low) or downward (if total
 production is too high) by an amount expected to improve system balance, and the process repeats
 from Step 1.
It is anticipated that the local optimizers will be allowed to update target set points on their corresponding
Stellar Edge devices each time they receive a target quantity, based on the assumption that a more recent
target quantity is likely more optimal than an older target quantity, even if the overall optimization has not
yet converged.

The local optimizer implementation is designed with the expectation that in the future, local optimization
may take place directly on the Edge devices, with telemetry processing and control-state updates taking
place on the devices. In this future paradigm, Edge devices will share price and quantity information
directly with the orchestrator service via the IoT Hub, without the need for a “local” optimizer in Azure.

Initially, the cloud-based local optimizers will obtain load and power production forecasts from NSR’s

 3. TESTING MICROGRID ORCHESTRATOR PROTOCOL

Compared with individually controlled microgrids, the Microgrid Orchestrator provides additional
benefits by coordinating power and energy sharing across electrically connected microgrids:

• Grid-connected operation (economics): By sharing generation and leveraging the flexibility of
 distributed energy storage, the Orchestrator can reduce purchases from the utility during peak-price
 periods and/or avoid exporting power during low-price (“valley”) periods. This coordinated operation
 can improve microgrid economics by more than 10%.
• Islanded operation (resilience): During islanding—particularly during extreme events that damage
 some DG—the Orchestrator coordinates surviving generation and available energy storage across the
 network to reduce load shedding and/or extend the duration of islanded operation, thereby improving
 resilience.

In both modes, the Microgrid Orchestrator serves as the supervisory coordination layer for networked
microgrids, enabling economic and resilience gains beyond those that individually controlled microgrids
can achieve on their own.

To validate the performance and benefits of the developed Microgrid Orchestrator, a test networked
microgrids system comprising three microgrids interconnected by distribution feeders was designed. Two
use cases were then developed for both numerical simulation and HIL testing. The first use case focuses
on revealing the benefits of the Microgrid Orchestrator in reducing operating costs of networked
microgrids in the grid-connected normal operation mode. The second use case aims to validate the
advantage of the Microgrid Orchestrator in enhancing networked microgrid resilience in the islanded-
operation mode during grid outages caused by extreme events. The designed test networked microgrid
system was also constructed in a Typhoon HIL testing environment, where the two use cases validated the
commercial software implementation of laboratory-developed technology to achieve its core functions for
coordinating networked microgrids.

The daily operation of the networked microgrid test system is simulated using 1 h time intervals. The
distribution substation is modeled as a slack bus with a fixed voltage magnitude of 1.0 p.u. Bus voltages
are constrained to the range of 0.95–1.05 p.u., with a preferred range of 0.98–1.02 p.u. Voltages outside
the preferred range are permitted but penalized as voltage deviations. The penalty factor r is assumed to
be 0.1. Both the centralized and proposed distributed optimization formulations are mixed-integer
quadratically constrained programming (MIQCP) problems. The branch-and-bound (B&B) algorithm
provides an efficient approach for solving MIQCP problems. The B&B algorithm solves MIQCP
problems by breaking them into smaller subproblems and using a bounding function to eliminate
subproblems that cannot contain the optimal solution. This process continues until all subproblems
generated in the search space have been examined. The B&B algorithm has been implemented in the
CPLEX Mixed-Integer Optimizer. All optimization models in the case studies are formulated in
MATLAB and solved using IBM ILOG CPLEX Optimization Studio 12.6. The solution time for each
iteration of the proposed distributed optimization is typically less than 10 s.

3.2 USE-CASE DEVELOPMENT

To validate the benefits of networked microgrids coordinated by the Microgrid Orchestrator, two use
cases were developed and analyzed through numerical simulation: grid-connected normal operation and
islanded operation. In the grid-connected normal-operation case, the ability of the Microgrid Orchestrator
to reduce operating costs was evaluated by comparison with a scenario using independent microgrid
controllers. In the islanded-operation case, which considers grid outages caused by extreme events such as
hurricanes and wildfires, the ability of the Microgrid Orchestrator to reduce load shedding and improve
power supply resilience was evaluated by comparison with a scenario using independent microgrid

 21

controllers. In addition, the Microgrid Orchestrator could improve system performance (e.g., voltage
profiles, line losses, and power factor) in both cases.

• Grid-connected normal operation: In this case, the main microgrid was directly connected to the grid
 and operated in grid-following mode. Microgrids 1 and 2 were each directly connected to the main
 microgrid and also operated in grid-following mode. Each microgrid directly controlled its PCC
 switch. By sharing resources, allocating lower-cost DER generation, and using batteries for energy
 arbitrage, networked microgrids reduced operating costs. The two scenarios were simulated and their
 results were compared to validate the value proposition of networked microgrids.
 o Scenario 1: The three microgrids were electrically interconnected but independently controlled by
 three microgrid controllers. For microgrids 1 and 2, the PCC power of each microgrid was limited
 to 500 kW. Each microgrid could purchase or sell the power at its PCC according to the utility
 rate. For the main microgrid, the PCC power exchanges of microgrids 1 and 2 were not
 considered when scheduling its battery and load, and its PCC power was limited to 500 kW.
 Thus, the actual power exchanged between the main microgrid and the substation was limited to
 1,500 kW.
 o Scenario 2: The three microgrids were electrically interconnected and were coordinately
 controlled by the Microgrid Orchestrator. For microgrids 1 and 2, the PCC power of each
 microgrid was limited to 1,000 kW. The Microgrid Orchestrator coordinated the schedules of the
 three microgrids by sending and updating nodal prices. Nevertheless, the exchanged power at the
 substation remained limited to 1,500 kW.
• Islanded Operation: In this case, the main microgrid was islanded from the grid and operated in grid-
 forming mode. Initially, microgrids 1 and 2 were also islanded. Both microgrids were then
 resynchronized separately with the main microgrid. Through coordination, load shedding across the
 microgrids and overall system costs were reduced. The two scenarios were simulated and their results
 were compared to validate the value proposition of networked microgrids.
 o Scenario 3: The three microgrids were electrically islanded from the grid and from one another
 and were independently controlled by three microgrid controllers.
 o Scenario 4: The three microgrids were electrically islanded from the grid but remained connected
 with one another. They were coordinately controlled by the Microgrid Orchestrator.

 5. DISCUSSION

The Microgrid Orchestrator represents a significant advancement in the operation and coordination of
DERs across interconnected microgrids. As the first commercially available networked microgrid
controller designed specifically to support adoption of microgrids and the transition from conventional
distribution systems to resilient active distribution networks, the technology addresses a critical need
facing modern electric power systems. Utilities and site operators are increasingly challenged to integrate
high penetrations of renewable generation and energy storage while maintaining reliability, affordability,
and operational flexibility. The Microgrid Orchestrator directly responds to these challenges by enabling
coordinated, intelligent, and scalable management of multiple microgrids.

A key strength of the technology is its combination of technical innovation and practical deployability.
The distributed and scalable optimization algorithm allows multiple microgrids to coordinate operations
without relying on a single centralized architecture, making the system more robust and adaptable as
networks expand. The privacy-preserving import/export management strategy is also important because it
allows individual microgrids to participate in coordinated decision-making without exposing sensitive
internal operational details. This feature is particularly valuable in real-world deployments involving
multiple asset owners, operators, or vendors. In addition, the vendor-agnostic control approach reduces
barriers to adoption by allowing the orchestrator to integrate across diverse hardware and software
environments, increasing the likelihood of commercial uptake.

The HIL demonstration provides particularly compelling evidence of the technology’s value because it
validates performance under realistic operating scenarios rather than solely through simulation.
Demonstrating successful operation through representative test cases strengthens confidence that the
orchestrator can perform reliably under field-relevant conditions. This type of validation is important for
potential customers, utilities, and other stakeholders who must evaluate not only whether a new control

 31

technology is innovative, but also whether it can be trusted to operate effectively in complex, dynamic
grid environments.

The quantified performance improvements further highlight the practical significance of the technology.
During grid-connected operation, the Microgrid Orchestrator reduced total operating costs by 11%,
demonstrating its ability to improve the economic performance of interconnected microgrids. This
reduction reflects more effective coordination of DERs, improved dispatch decisions, and better use of
available generation and storage assets. In a power system environment where cost control and
operational efficiency are major priorities, this level of improvement is meaningful and demonstrates
clear customer value.

The benefits during outage conditions are arguably even more significant. During islanded operation, the
orchestrator improved average uptime by 20% through intelligent load management and prioritization of
critical loads. This result demonstrates that the technology is not only economically beneficial under
normal operating conditions, but it also materially improves resilience when the grid is disrupted. The
ability to preserve service during outages is one of the central promises of microgrids, and the Microgrid
Orchestrator enhances that value by enabling coordinated action across multiple interconnected systems
rather than isolated operation of individual microgrids.

The most important demonstration result is that the orchestrator enabled three microgrids to share
resources during a 24 h outage, maintaining continuity of service across all microgrids for the full event
duration. Without orchestration, one of the microgrids experienced a loss of service. This outcome
illustrates the broader system-level value of networked microgrid coordination: the orchestrator
transforms a collection of individual microgrids into a more resilient and cooperative energy network.
Rather than each microgrid acting independently and risking unequal outcomes during stressed
conditions, the coordinated system can dynamically allocate available resources to sustain overall service
continuity. This capability is particularly relevant for critical infrastructure, campuses, military
installations, remote communities, and utility service territories seeking to enhance resilience against
extreme weather events, cyber-physical disruptions, or other grid disturbances.

More broadly, the technology has implications beyond the demonstrated use cases. As utilities modernize
their distribution networks, the need for solutions that can manage DG, storage, controllable loads, and
multiple autonomous subsystems will continue to grow. The Microgrid Orchestrator provides a
framework for this transition by supporting interoperability, scalability, and resilience in increasingly
decentralized power systems. Its successful demonstration suggests that networked microgrid
coordination can become a practical pathway for enabling higher renewable penetration while
maintaining system reliability and operational security.

In summary, the Microgrid Orchestrator is significant not only because it introduces a novel technical
capability, but because it demonstrates measurable economic and resilience benefits in realistic conditions
and offers a commercially relevant solution to a pressing grid modernization challenge. The combination
of first-of-a-kind commercial availability, validated operational performance, and clear customer value
positions this technology as an important contribution to the evolution of modern electric distribution
systems.

 6. CONCLUSIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS

6.1 CONCLUSIONS

Networked microgrids are the next innovation for power systems, building on foundational microgrid
principles and offering greater scale and resilience through interconnected operations.

 32

ORNL and NSR were selected for a DOE Technology Commercialization Fund (TCF) award to
commercialize the Microgrid Orchestrator, a control technology that optimizes the performance of
networked microgrids.

A pilot demonstration at the GRID-C test bed at ORNL showed the benefits of coordinating connected
microgrids to improve resilience and economics. The pilot was constructed to mimic a behind-the-meter
campus scenario, with a single point of interconnection to the utility grid and three electrically connected
individual microgrids owned by the same customer.

Two use cases were defined to demonstrate 24 h scenarios. The first use case tested grid-connected
operation during normal conditions. The second use case tested an emergency condition where the
microgrids were islanded during a grid outage. ORNL and NSR demonstrated benefits for the customer in
both use-case scenarios. During grid-connected operation, networking the microgrids reduced the
customer’s total operating costs by 11%. More importantly, by allowing the microgrids to share resources
during the grid outage, all three microgrids were able to maintain continuity of service for the entire 24 h
period. Without networking, one of the islanded microgrids had a loss of service for the customer.

The project advanced the Microgrid Orchestrator technology from adoption readiness level (ARL) 1 (low
readiness) to 5 (medium readiness) and from technology readiness level (TRL) 5 to TRL 6. It created the
first commercially available networked microgrid controller, which will facilitate the adoption of
microgrids and accelerate the transition of traditional utility distribution networks to modern active
distribution networks with high penetration of DG and energy storage.

6.2 RECOMMENDATIONS

Several gaps remain that must be addressed to accelerate broader adoption of networked microgrids and
enable key resilience benefits in the market, including standardization, vendor interoperability, and
cybersecurity. In the TCF project, the higher levels of the control system were implemented entirely on
NSR’s products using standardized primary controls onboard commercial inverters, but standardizing
interfaces between the layers of the control system would enable microgrid developers to use multiple
control vendors and enable adjacent microgrids with different controllers to network. Interoperability
requires additional development of registration and authentication systems, clear definitions of roles to
manage access and authorize participation in networked microgrids, and trusted third parties to issue
certificates.

At the product level, NSR will continue to improve its capabilities. This project demonstrated a grid-
forming/grid-following paradigm where, in islanded mode, one microgrid is designated grid-forming, and
the others are following. This functions reliably for networks with few microgrids, but for scalability and
stability in large networks, and for redundancy, networked microgrids will need to use more distributed
grid-forming controls tailored for inverter-based resources. As part of this, expanded testing and
validation with a focus on transient stability and seamless transfer is needed. In addition, the Microgrid
Orchestrator would benefit from being able to accommodate dynamic reconfiguration and topology
management. Network operators might manually and automatically reconfigure the network in response
to events or to improve the security of operations. The key technical challenge is coordinating
reconfiguration and topology management with the primary control and protection systems for smooth
transitions.

The team believes that networked microgrids are the next innovation for power systems and will continue
to develop this technology for broad commercial adoption.
