Abstract

Puerto Rico’s current electricity generation heavily relies on imported fossil fuels. This results in
an average cost of electricity higher than the U.S. mainland average in all sectors (residential,
commercial, and industrial), despite abundant local offshore wind resources, which have the
potential to provide secure, low-cost energy generation and consequent economic prosperity.
However, effects on atmospheric and oceanic circulation resulting from large-scale deployments
of offshore wind farms have not been previously studied at tropical latitude. This project addressed
this knowledge gap through a computational modeling effort designed to capture the coupled
dynamics of the atmosphere and the ocean in presence of offshore wind farms. Results indicate
that wind farm wakes can alter wind stress, generate Ekman-driven vertical transport, and
potentially affect nutrient distribution. While full model coupling remains challenging, progress in
parameterization and large-eddy simulations provides a foundation for future research. The project
contributes to DOE’s Earth System modeling efforts and supports STEM workforce development.

Project motivation and summary

As per the Energy Information Administration latest reports, Puerto Rico’s current electricity
generation heavily relies on fossil fuels, which accounts for almost 93% of the total amount. All
these fossil fuels are imported, which makes electricity prices expensive and subject to market
volatility. As a results, the average cost of electricity in Puerto Rico is consistently higher than in
the U.S. mainland in the residential and commercial sectors, and even more than twice as much in
the industrial sector. Leveraging local natural resources, such as offshore wind, provides a pathway
towards secure, low-cost energy generation and consequent economic prosperity. Additionally,
Puerto Rico Act 17-2019 mandates the island to transition to 100% renewable energy generation
by 2050. Large-scale deployment of offshore wind power plants would contribute to achieve the
envisaged transformation of the electrical system.

However, to generate electricity, wind turbines extract energy from the incoming wind, creating a
wake downstream with low momentum and increased turbulence. Wakes from individual turbines
extend for several diameters downstream the machine, coalesce and can persist over large
distances potentially affecting wind-driven ocean circulation (because of the reduced stress), air-
sea fluxes and temperature and nutrient distributions associated with vertical wind-driven motions
in the ocean. Most previous studies have focused on mid-latitude regions, such as the North Sea
in Europe, leaving a knowledge gap on such effects in tropical latitude.

The aim of this project is to address this gap through a computational modeling effort (see
conceptual framework in Figure 1), tailored to conditions in Puerto Rico. The proposed work
consists in developing a numerical framework based on a coupled ocean-atmosphere regional
circulation model, including wind turbine/wind farm parameterization, thereby allowing to mimic
the actual impact of farm wakes on weather patterns. The research program has three focus area:
(i) atmospheric modeling and wind turbine/farm interactions; (ii) wind/wake/waves interactions;
(iii) ocean response to wind turbine and wind farms. The project aims to develop tools and
knowledge that aligns and support DOE’s efforts in Earth System modeling, such as the Energy
Exascale Earth System (E3SM), that is being developed in the Earth and Environmental System
Sciences Division (EESSD) within the Biological and Environmental Research (BER) program of
DOE.

Summary of progress
Atmospheric modeling and wind turbine/farm interactions

Work in the atmospheric module of the coupled ocean-atmosphere model has focused on obtaining
climatological conditions of the wind circulation around Puerto Rico, and the implications on
offshore wind potential. The Weather Research and Forecast (WRF) model has been employed in
stand-alone mode (i.e., not coupled to the ocean and wave modules) to produce characteristic
regional wind patterns. WRF has been fed with boundary conditions from NAM for a period
spanning one year of data (December 2021 to November 2022). Comparison with climatological
data from climate reanalysis shows that simulated average wind speed and direction fields are
within the normal trend in the area. In addition, comparison against available wind observations
(low-level CariCOOS buoys anemometers and NWS weather globe) also confirms a favorable
agreement for numerically generated wind circulation data.

Wind/wake/waves interactions

Work in this area has focused on addressing turbine-scale interactions between the atmosphere and
the ocean. The main objective of this research task is the development of a parameterization of
wind farm effects that is amenable to implementation in coupled meso-scale models, such as both
WRF (atmosphere) and ROMS (ocean). Progress has been made in such direction, although the
current product falls short of direct implementation in such models.

Large-eddy simulations (LES) with rotating actuator disk and a newly developed method include
one-way coupling with prescribed evolving sea-surface gravity waves. Figure 3 shows example

Ocean response to wind turbine and wind farms

Work for this final task has been focused on the ocean response to deployment of large offshore
wind farms, informed by the worked in the previous task. We have used the Regional Ocean
Modeling System (ROMS) model, an open-source meso-scale ocean circulation simulation model.

Due to computational cost and project time constraints, simulations have been performed assuming
idealized flat bathymetry. A 100-turbine farm (representative of Region I and III wind farms
envisaged for deployment in PR) has been considered. The presence of the wind farm in the ocean
circulation model has been included through a parameterization developed based on our LES
results and previous work in the literature on wind farm wake models.

We have analyzed various scenarios varying the latitude in the ocean circulation model to address
the gap in literature. Varying the latitude in the hydrodynamic model mainly changes rotational
effects due to the Coriolis force, which may be significant over the large scales involved by wind

Summary of challenges and next steps

The development of a fully coupled ocean-atmosphere model within the timeframe of the project
has proved more challenging than originally anticipated. The main difficulty is related to the
development of a parameterization of wind farm effect that is amenable to direct implementation
in meso-scale codes such as WRF or ROMS. The physical problem itself involves several
interplaying factors including background atmospheric turbulence, waves and air-sea interactions,
and evolution of the wake, which makes the parameterization harder. Large-eddy simulations
(LES) have proved a key tool in capturing some of these interactions and thus providing direction
for the parameterization efforts, albeit at quite large computational cost and at smaller
spatiotemporal scales that are typically spanned by meso-scale models. Nevertheless, progress has
been made in this project and ongoing work continues in this area in the research community,
which provide further progress in the future regarding the main scientific questions of this work.
