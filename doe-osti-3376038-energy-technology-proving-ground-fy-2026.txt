 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
 The Energy Technology Proving Ground, or Proving Ground, is the cornerstone of Idaho National
Laboratory’s (INL’s) forward-focused strategy to enable rapid commercialization of advanced energy
technologies through industry partnerships. The Proving Ground exists to accelerate and support research
on integrated energy systems necessary to address the global need for robust (resilient, reliable,
affordable, accessible and secure) energy solutions.
 The Energy Technology Proving Ground Program focuses on developing multimission infrastructure
required to implement the research, development, and demonstration (RD&D) activities of the
Accelerating Integrated Energy Systems (IES) Initiative. This infrastructure enables the development of
advanced energy research capabilities to investigate transformative energy technologies, incorporating
advanced nuclear reactors, distributed energy generation, microgrid networks, energy-transmission
architecture, long-duration energy storage, carbon valorization and utilization, and synthetic fuels and
chemicals production. These advanced capabilities will provide research-ready facilities and
infrastructure to investigate robust energy systems and reduce industry research costs. Combining Proving
Ground capabilities with industrial engagement, the IES Initiative fast-tracks the transition from early-
stage research to market-ready technologies.
 The Proving Ground vision also includes adjacent, industry-focused activities relevant to
reinvigorating the supply of critical materials domestically. The Proving Ground will be composed of
newly constructed multiprogram research facilities and research areas that are connected through
functional adjacencies and common inputs, outputs, and products built on the four primary energy
currencies: heat, electricity, hydrogen, and carbon. Each integrated facility accommodates multiple
building types and functions. Continuing INL’s 75 years of applied research experience, test beds will be
established to demonstrate alignment between industrial supply chains and energy applications.
 By focusing on new facilities and expanding capabilities, INL’s Proving Ground will emerge to serve
the advancement of U.S. industry by:
 • Closing the gap between research and deployment
 • Integrating energy synergies and adjacencies
 • Accelerating the development of energy technologies
 • Advancing intelligent systems integration and scale-up
 • Building integrated research infrastructure
 • Implementing advanced energy technologies at INL
 • Demonstrating INL’s world-class research capabilities.
 Creating the close alignment between energy sources and delivery systems, the Proving Ground will
provide large-scale capabilities for industry partners to narrow the gap between research and
commercialization and to apply advanced digital engineering tools to test and propel operational
paradigms, while creating low- or no-waste ecosystems. Derisking the advancement of domestic
industries and supply chains increases resilience and lowers the cost of commercial advancement while
demonstrating achievement of national energy goals. The Energy Technology Proving Ground will be the
epicenter of INL’s furtherance of the energy renaissance.

 1. OVERVIEW
 Established in 1949, the Idaho National Laboratory (INL) is the lead U.S. nuclear energy laboratory
and has continually adapted its research to meet national energy and security demands. Technological
advancements and industrial growth require action to address changing energy needs. Developing new
energy-production and delivery methods is essential for long-term national health. Therefore, the nation
requires an appropriately scaled capability to test and validate technologies that will inform
energy-modernization strategies, reduce operating costs, and mitigate energy-delivery risk while
enhancing system security, reliability, and resilience. INL’s 890-square-mile Site west of Idaho Falls
provides this unique, safe, and secure location where advanced facilities can be constructed to establish
this capability, known as the Energy Technology Proving Ground or Proving Ground.
 This Energy Technology Proving Ground Program Plan outlines the development of mission-ready
research infrastructure at INL’s 890-square-mile Site, detailing strategies, tactics, and procedures for
creating multiple test beds and facilities necessary to support advanced energy research, development, and
demonstration (RD&D). This program plan will be updated regularly as concepts evolve.

 2. INTRODUCTION
 INL’s Integrated Energy Systems (IES) Initiative enables advanced energy RD&D activities. To date,
IES pilot demonstrations were limited by distributed infrastructure and a lack of large-scale facilities to
accommodate industry-scale research. The IES Initiative plans to eliminate these constraints by
establishing the Energy Technology Proving Ground at INL’s desert location (the Site). The Energy and
Environment Science and Technology (EES&T) Directorate, one of five INL RD&D organizations, leads
the development of the Proving Ground by focusing on resilient-energy technologies that anchor industry-
enabling research to be conducted at its newly constructed facilities. The IES Initiative leads Proving
Ground development and is the owner of this program plan with the EES&T Directorate as its champion.
 The Proving Ground, located near INL’s Central Facilities Area (CFA), will combine innovative
energy systems into a lean, integrated test bed of independent multiscale capabilities available for the
government and commercial industries to perform research. The Proving Ground is envisioned to support
the transition from lower technology readiness levels (TRLs) to precommercial or pilot-scale
demonstrations and to derisk development and deployment activities. Placement of large scale
(>1 MWe/th) systems requiring support for longer-duration research testing will occur at the Site while
established laboratory-scale research will occur at INL’s in-town Research and Education Campus (REC).
This approach follows the path where advanced reactors achieve criticality within the Demonstration of
Microreactor Experiments (DOME) and the Laboratory for Operation and Testing in the United States
(LOTUS) facilities at the Materials and Fuels Complex (MFC) and then transition to integrated systems
testing at the Proving Ground.
 This Program Plan provides guidance for building and integrating research facilities at the Proving
Ground to offer plug-and-play pilot testing of IES technologies and systems, including nuclear fission and
other resilient and reliable-energy sources for industry-driven application platforms. Though the focus of
this plan is on new facilities and infrastructure at the Site, new facilities and infrastructure at the REC can
also be informed by the strategies, tactics, and procedures within this document.

facilities and infrastructure. as described in the Modular Design Concept (Figure 1). It outlines goals 1,

Program Plan will be revised to include ever-increasing scope definition and incorporate new information
required to inform the timely development of Proving Ground assets. The Program Plan priorities are to:
 • Achieve mission-readiness status across the Proving Ground
 • Assure integration and alignment of requirements across research programs and mission areas
 • Describe initial purpose and develop cost estimates to support the development of mission-ready
 infrastructure and research facilities
 • Provide a coordinated approach to develop research infrastructure and facilities that meet mission
 needs and INL operational goals.

 2.3. Alignment with Institutional Strategy
 Institutionally, the IES Initiative, the driving force behind the Modular Design Concept, identifies the
need for new facilities to house research focused on robust energy solutions. This initiative fast-tracks the
development of advanced energy systems, creating a transformative energy ecosystem that incorporates
advanced nuclear reactors, distributed energy systems, microgrid networks, carbon-utilization processes,
and synthetic fuels and chemicals systems. These advanced systems depend on new facilities to secure
research funding and deliver research results.
 The Proving Ground is the cornerstone of this initiative and has already enabled INL to attract over
$50 million of investments that will enable rapid commercialization of such advanced energy
technologies as high-temperature hydrogen production, catalytic creation of synthetic fuels and
chemicals, and long-duration thermal-energy management and distribution. While the demand signal
from industry remains strong for testing locations that derisk technology advancement, the typical lead
time from bare ground to functioning test bed is 3 years, and that underpins the program’s drive forward
on scheduling.

 3. CURRENT-STATE ASSESSMENT
 INL follows a standard process for evaluating and managing current spaces and offering guidance for
the design of new space. Once constructed, new facilities become part of INL’s operation, maintenance,
and management portfolio. However, the Proving Ground reflects a transformative shift for INL, from
laboratory to pilot-scale demonstrations, requiring the development of modular spaces for research. As a
result of this transition to large-scale validation and verification research systems, an analysis of existing
facilities determined that they were unsuitable due to their size limitations and location restrictions.
 A major theme from the discovery process is the need to move research to higher readiness levels,
which necessitates new infrastructure for large-scale validation and verification. Advancing technologies
to higher TRLs, for example, involves additional iterative research to incorporate manufacturing (MRL),
integration (IRL), and system (SRL) readiness levels. This iterative research process helps to mature
technologies and integrated systems efficiently. Proving Ground facilities will support the evolution of
technologies along all readiness levels by combining various energy systems in a lab setting with
multiscale capabilities as requested by government and commercial entities.
 In general, the Proving Ground, with its associated facilities, may add up to 300,000 square feet of
conditioned space and up to 100 acres of outdoor testing space within approximately 400 acres of
disturbed and undisturbed land near CFA. Facilities will be based on the starting points outlined in the
Modular Design Concept along with other INL initiatives, such as Critical and Strategic Materials.
 3.1. Site Facilities
 The Proving Ground will be composed of newly constructed multiprogram research facilities and
research areas that are connected through functional adjacencies and common inputs, outputs, and
products built on the four primary energy currencies of heat, electricity, hydrogen, and carbon. Each
integrated facility accommodates multiple building types and functions. While capability and adjacency
needs may evolve, the basis for each new facility will remain the same: space to conduct larger-scale,
more complex, integrated research activities.
 At the Site, 2,500 acres have been identified for the placement of Proving Ground facilities, with
initial natural and cultural resource studies starting in FY-2025 on the first 383 acres. The proposed 383-
acre area designated for the Proving Ground includes both disturbed and undisturbed lands. Current uses
are limited, although the CF-686 high bay has recently been retrofitted to support the High Temperature
Test Facility and Synthetic Fuels and Chemicals Facility, which are the first two Proving Ground projects
to be placed at CFA. There are several other facilities within the initial 383-acre Proving Ground
boundary that are used for storage, which will be transitioned to appropriate Proving Ground activities.
The retrofitted CFA-686 high bay and adjacent pads will support up to 10 MW of testing capability for
hydrogen solid-oxide electrolyzer cells and provide space and utilities for a biomass to synthetic-fuels
pilot plant. As research facilities and installations are created, usage metrics will be developed.
 During the planning phase for new facilities, the Campus Development Office (CDO) will seek
additional siting information to refine the Proving Ground concept. This will inform the land-use analysis
and Campus Master Plan, providing up to 200,000 NSF 2 of open space to test microreactors, microgrids,
and energy-storage. It will also provide approximately 300,000 NSF of environmentally controlled space,
including hangars, sprung structures, high bays, laboratories, energy storage, and control facilities. The
Modular Design Concept identified five new facilities and two open spaces for the Proving Ground;
however, that vision continues to mature. Figure 4 shows notional future facilities and the flow of four
primary energy currencies between these facilities within the Proving Ground near CFA.

 4. PROGRAM GOALS AND OBJECTIVES
 The National Reactor Innovation Center (NRIC) is commissioning the DOME and the LOTUS test
facilities within MFC at INL to support advanced microreactor demonstrations. As constructed, those two
test beds will support industry efforts to achieve important criticality milestones. Required longer-
duration testing capabilities to verify and validate system dynamics, including steady-state and variable
operations, are needed. In addition, the Critical and Strategic Minerals and Materials (CSMM) Initiative
seeks to add new capabilities at the site to support the emerging needs for operating advanced
mineral-separation pilot plants. These efforts are central to their critical materials supply chain, including
powering remote mining operations with future reactor technologies.
 The Proving Ground program, guided by the strategic priorities outlined in the Accelerating
Integrated Energy Systems Initiative Strategic Plan (the IES Strategic Plan), will facilitate the needs of the
CSMM Initiative for a Critical Materials Facility and NRIC for a multi-bay microreactor test bed. To
meet IES strategic priorities and support the needs of CSMM and NRIC, the Program Plan presents key
goals derived from facility priorities and describes the objectives necessary to achieve these goals.
 Since establishment of the initiative and the publication of the Modular Design Concept, the Proving
Ground has the following achievements.
• Coordinating the construction of the High Temperature Testing Facility (HTTF), including the
 installation of up to 10 MW of commercial-scale solid-oxide electrolysis cells, with storage and
 delivery capability
• Coordinating the construction of test bays with utility support for biomass-preprocessing equipment
 installation (tons/day) to produce sized materials for combustion, generating high-quality carbon for
 synthetic fuels and chemicals production
• Conducting siting studies to support a bounding-level National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA)
 environmental assessment to support the categorical-exclusion determinations for microreactor test
 beds and support facilities necessary to derisk longer duration microreactor research
• Coordinating facility-development activities to establish pilot-scale high-temperature
 chemical-facility designs that consume thermal energy from microreactors while maturing high-
 temperature chemical-reaction processes
• Collaborating with NRIC on the development of a multi-bay testing facility for industry-supplied
 microreactors and providing thermal energy for other beneficial research actions
• Collaborating on the creation of thermal- and electrical-energy storage facilities to support research
 on integrated generation and storage systems
• Collaborating on the integration of a research data center and advanced nuclear reactor to verify and
 validate unit technologies, system performance, and operating parameters across multiple energy
 generation, distribution, and storage systems
 4.1. Key Goals
 Development of the facilities identified in the Modular Design Concept directly aligns with the IES
Initiative priority titled, “Build the Energy Technology Proving Ground,” and is supported by the
initiative’s five research pillars. The Proving Ground research facilities are intended to provide
industrial-scale capabilities that include, but are not strictly limited to:
 • Thermal energy management
 • Electric grid and energy storage resiliency

 9

 • High-temperature hydrogen production
 • Carbon valorization and utilization
 • Synthetic fuels and chemicals production
 • Advanced nuclear reactor testing and integration.
 Though these capabilities are expected to evolve, they provide the basis and purpose for the Program
Plan and are central to establish the following key goals.
 • Thermal-Energy Management. Develop a system at megawatt-thermal scale to capture, store,
 and transport thermal energy from various sources, including advanced reactors and demonstrate
 its use in industrial processes.
 • Microgrid Component Testing and System Control. Create a microgrid test range to validate
 component performance under various environmental conditions using advanced control systems.
 • Hydrogen Production and Storage. Build a high-temperature electrolysis test facility for
 MWe-scale hydrogen production, storage, and use.
 • Biomass and Waste Material Preprocessing. Build a facility for carbon capture, storage, and
 usage to supply purified carbon for consumer and energy products.
 • Fuels and Chemicals Synthesis. Develop a pilot-scale system to test technologies using nearby
 carbon and hydrogen sources for fuel and chemical synthesis.
 • Microreactor Testing. Build a facility for testing and operating advanced microreactor systems
 within the Proving Ground and demonstrate their thermal and electrical integration with other
 energy sources.
 • System Integration and Testing. Integrate the input and output from all test facilities and test
 beds to optimize system performance and demonstrate efficiencies gained by adjacent and
 connected energy systems.
 • Dynamic Operation of Integrated Systems. Develop, test, and incorporate AI tools to
 dynamically adjust systems to emulate changing energy demands and market conditions.
 The following expectations are to be incorporated into facility planning to provide opportunities to
incorporate infrastructure modification quickly as research objectives evolve over the lifespan of a
facility.
 • Streamlined Design Processes. Implement digital-engineering tools and frameworks to
 streamline the design and development of energy technologies, reducing time to market and
 enhancing the accuracy and reliability of prototypes.
 • Cyber-Informed Designs. Incorporate cyber informed engineering (CIE) concepts into all
 facilities to identify key facility-infrastructure requirements and strengthen facilities against
 cyberthreats.
 • Stakeholder Collaboration. Identify and work with technology providers and stakeholders to
 establish the requirements for energy system development and deployment and to establish
 resilient supply chains.

 4.2. Key Objectives
 The addition of Proving Ground facilities, as outlined in the Modular Design Concept, is the key
strategic program goal, with the expectation those facilities support the following RD&D and operations
objectives. Successful development of Proving Ground facilities is dependent on these key objectives.
 • Thermal Management of Heat Sources. Combine heat from nuclear, geothermal, concentrated
 solar, biomass, and other sources to provide thermal energy for offtake.
 • Thermal Storage Systems. Develop thermal technologies to store surplus heat from a broad
 range of heat sources.
 • Thermal Transport Systems. Design efficient, research-oriented heat-transport systems to move
 thermal energy from various sources to integrated test systems and facilities.
 • Flexible Feedstock Preprocessing Systems. Develop and test scalable preprocessing
 technologies, like sorting and fractionation, to convert raw materials, including municipal solid
 waste, into usable feedstocks based on performance parameters.
 • Innovative Chemical-Conversion Processes. Develop and operate innovative chemical-
 conversion processes to convert hydrogen and carbon feedstocks into synthetic fuels and
 industrial chemicals.
 • Advanced Chemical Reactors. Deploy novel chemical reactors to enable precision heating and
 efficient adaptation for variable feedstocks to generate high-value industrial feedstocks.
 • Recovery and Purification of Critical Materials. Improve the recovery and purification
 processes of critical materials through innovative extraction and separation systems to refine
 valuable elements from mined materials and waste streams.
 • Development of Advanced Materials. Deploy advanced-manufacturing processes, methods, and
 feedstocks to ensure efficient production of advanced materials.
 • Accelerated Construction Techniques. Deploy emerging accelerated construction methods to
 facilitate adoption of cost-lowering and schedule-reducing construction methods for energy
 facilities.

 5. PROGRAM DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION
 This Proving Ground Program Plan is focused on developing multimission infrastructure to support
cross-cutting RD&D programs aligned with the priorities outlined in the Analytics for Integrated Energy
Systems (AIES) and CSMM Initiative plans. This plan incorporates input from programs researching the
five energy currencies, as well as contributions from the Materials, Manufacturing, and Bioenergy
Science and Technology division regarding a Critical Materials Facility, and from NRIC on a multi-bay
microreactor test bed.
 To support effective indirect-funds management and address emerging space demands—including
those arising from the AIES and CSMM Initiatives—INL initiated a collaborative 10-year infrastructure
roadmap. This infrastructure roadmap is designed to coordinate and prioritize facility needs identified by
the research community and incorporate stakeholder input while balancing needed research capabilities
with infrastructure-management demands. While some facility needs can be met using existing resources,
many will require the development of new facilities and spaces to safely and securely support research
activities.
 The Proving Ground program applies risk-informed, disciplined program- and project-management
strategies, using structured processes to determine the appropriate level of rigor for each project. These
processes typically begin with defining the scope of work, deliverables, schedules, and cost estimates,
followed by sponsor concurrence and formal authorization to proceed. INL’s capital construction efforts
are guided by established protocols, including GDE-70, “General Project Management Methods,”
management control procedure (MCP)-7001, and other documents that outline a graded approach to
managing capital projects.
 As outlined in the Modular Design Concept, new infrastructure will be composed of knowable and
buildable elements. These modular components are designed to be adaptable, allowing them to be
modified to meet specific research and operational needs.

revise DOE Order 413.3B project thresholds, to expand the use of the “OSHA-Plus” safety framework for
construction project, to assess the benefits and risks of removing construction labor-agreement provisions,
and to revise contract clauses on employee compensation. These actions are anticipated to significantly
moderate conventional capital construction-process costs and, in turn, impact how the local DOE office
approaches capital projects. Development of guidance will be forthcoming. As such, historic cost
estimates and approaches to capital projects are anticipated to change.
 The Secretary articulated four actions to be implemented immediately.
 1. Revise delegated project authority within DOE Order 413.3B from $50 million to $300 million
 specific to the national laboratories managed under management and operation contracts. Tailor

 points on projects between $300 million and $1 billion, subject to sustained successful project
 execution. Capital asset projects with a total project cost of more than $1 billion shall continue to
 follow the full scope of requirements established in DOE Order 413.3B.
 2. Expand the use of the National Nuclear Security Administration’s successful “OSHA-Plus”
 framework for subcontracted construction projects at the national laboratories. The framework
 uses a tailored, graded approach to meet Title 10 Code of Federal Regulations Part 851, “Worker
 Safety and Health Program,” which increases competition and reduces costs while maintaining a
 safe work environment.
 3. Assess the benefits and risks of removing construction labor-agreement provisions from national
 laboratory contracts. Risks to be evaluated include increased potential for labor strikes and
 local-community concerns.
 4. Revise national-laboratory contract clauses on employee compensation: pay and benefits to
 eliminate requirements that are not mandated by statute or regulation or are not necessary to
 monitor DOE’s financial liabilities related to defined-benefit plans. The national laboratories
       must continue to comply with FAR 31.205-6, DEAR 970.5216-7 and DEAR 970.3102-05-6 and
 will be accountable for pay and benefit decisions, subject to annual audits.

 8. EVALUATION AND MONITORING
 8.1. Performance Metrics and Data Collection
 Setting performance metrics and implementing data collection are crucial to evaluate the
effectiveness and efficiency of any process or project. Performance metrics provide clear, quantifiable
targets that align with organizational goals, enabling teams to focus their efforts and resources on what
truly matters. Data collection, on the other hand, offers empirical evidence needed to assess progress
toward these metrics, identify trends, and make informed decisions. Together, they create a feedback loop
that supports continuous improvement, enhances accountability, and drives strategic planning, ultimately
leading to higher performance and better outcomes.
8.1.1. Standard Construction Project Metrics
 Prior to turnover of newly constructed or revised facilities to the various program offices for
installation of program- and project-specific hardware, construction projects will follow the processes and
protocols outlined in the current INL infrastructure management documents. These processes and
procedures are outlined in MCPs, LWPs and in iQ WorkSmart workflows. In the future, most MCPs and
LWPs will be converted to workflows.
 For INL infrastructure construction projects, setting performance metrics and implementing data
collection are essential to ensure successful project completion and stakeholder satisfaction. Key
performance metrics in construction projects typically include schedule adherence, budget compliance,
quality of work, safety incidents, and resource utilization.
 Schedule-adherence metrics track whether the project milestones and deadlines are met, helping to
identify potential delays early. Budget-compliance metrics monitor actual expenditures against the project
budget, ensuring financial resources are managed effectively, and cost overruns are avoided. Quality of
work metrics assess the workmanship and materials used, ensuring that the project meets the required
standards and specifications. Safety-incident metrics measure the frequency and severity of accidents on
site, promoting a safe working environment and compliance with regulatory requirements. Resource
utilization metrics evaluate the efficiency in using labor, equipment, and materials, optimizing resource
allocation and minimizing waste.
 By systematically collecting data on these metrics, project managers can gain valuable insights into
the project’s performance, identify areas needing improvement, and make data-driven decisions to
enhance project outcomes. This approach not only supports continuous improvement and accountability
but also fosters transparency and trust among all stakeholders involved in the construction project.
8.1.2. Key Performance Indicators
 Beyond the standard or typical performance indicators applied to construction projects, each Mission
Organization has established or will establish KPIs. While most are focused on the development and
execution of research projects, those KPIs may be integrated into program- and project-specific plans.
 KPIs for these research-infrastructure projects include defining quantifiable metrics used to gauge the
success and performance of the project. These KPIs help ensure that the project meets its goals in terms of
time, cost, quality, safety, and stakeholder satisfaction. Some essential KPIs for an infrastructure project:
 • Schedule Adherence: Measures the extent to which the project is on track with its planned
 timeline. This includes indicators such as on-time completion of milestones, percentage of tasks
 completed on schedule, and overall project-timeline variance.
 • Budget Compliance: Tracks whether the project is completed within the allocated budget. This
 includes monitoring actual spending against the budget, cost variance, and cost performance
 index (CPI).

 22

 • Quality of Work: Assesses the quality and standards of the work performed. This can include
 metrics such as defect rates, rework levels, and compliance with specifications and standards.
 • Safety Performance: Measures the safety of the construction site, including the number and
 severity of incidents or accidents. Common safety metrics are the total recordable-incident rate,
 lost-time injury frequency rate, and near-miss reporting.
 • Resource Utilization: Evaluates how efficiently resources such as labor, equipment, and
 materials are used. Metrics can include labor-productivity rates, equipment-utilization rates, and
 material-wastage levels.
 • Stakeholder Satisfaction: Gauges the satisfaction levels of all stakeholders involved, including
 clients, contractors and the community. This can involve surveys, feedback forms, and
 stakeholder-engagement levels.
 • Environmental Impact: Monitors the project’s adherence to environmental regulations and its
 impact on the environment. Indicators can include waste-management efficiency, energy
 consumption, and emissions levels.
 • Change-Order Frequency: Tracks the number and impact of change orders issued during the
 project to understand their impact on scope, timeline, and budget.
 Regularly monitoring these KPIs helps project managers assess the project's health, identify issues
early, and take corrective actions to ensure the successful delivery of the infrastructure project.
8.1.3. Evaluation Framework
 INL will apply Earned Value Management System (EVMS) standards to the proposed research and
construction projects to ensure appropriately tailored project management and control. By integrating
EVMS, INL ensures that project performance is measured and monitored accurately against prepared
project baselines and that change control is implemented appropriately. This system enables program and
project managers to identify variances and implement corrective actions promptly, ensuring that projects
are completed on time and within budget. Use of standard EVMS metrics and regular estimation of the
same will provide action triggers when the CPI or schedule performance index (SPI) falls below 0.75 (or
another value dictated by project requirements).
 • CPI is a measure of project cost efficiency. CPI is determined by comparing the anticipated dollar
 value of work scope to the actual cost to complete that work scope. Roughly speaking, a value
 greater than 1 indicates the project is performing efficiently, less than 1 indicates the project is
 underperforming. Hence, use of a 0.75 trigger is mean to provide early warning, while allow
 normal variances to occur.
 • SPI is a measure of project schedule efficiency. SPI is determined by comparing the anticipated
 schedule to the actual schedule incurred to complete defined work scope. Roughly speaking, a
 value greater than 1 indicates the project is performing efficiently, less than 1 indicates the project
 is under performing. As before, use of a 0.75 trigger is mean to provide early warning, while
 allow normal variances to occur.
 The use of EVMS at INL meets federal requirements and industry standards, offering a structured
approach to project management that improves decision-making, accountability, and project outcomes.
8.1.4. Reporting and Feedback Mechanisms
 Individual projects will prepare and participate in weekly and monthly reporting of project
performance in accordance with F&SS standard processes and guidelines following a graded approach to
DOE Order 413.3b. Generally, projects report accomplishments, address issues and concerns, provide
analysis of cost and schedule variance, review milestone progress, and provide updates to the risk register.

 Draft NEPA Scoping Statement
 To meet long-term federal-agency mission objectives to enable discovery and advance science, the
Department of Energy (DOE) must provide laboratory space and associated infrastructure for existing
RD&D capabilities at the Idaho National Laboratory (INL) located west of Idaho Falls, Idaho. In addition,
DOE would require facilities to support new research capabilities. To accomplish its scientific mission,
INL requires a variety of facilities and equipment, including radiological and other specialized
laboratories, advanced computational facilities and office space. New or replacement facilities would be
needed that could be adequately and appropriately secured and that could be efficiently managed for the
potential public risks associated with operations. New or replacement facilities should be constructed for
the efficient conduct of DOE’s RD&D mission, with considerations of minimizing energy use, life-cycle
costs, and project uncertainty. The continuity of federal ownership and control should be considered in
the siting and ownership of new and replacement facilities and infrastructure, along with the varying

 To meet long-term needs, INL anticipates the development of the Energy Technology Proving
Ground’s facilities, along with secure (National and Homeland Security [NHS]) facilities and (Nuclear
Science and Technology [NS&T]) radiological facilities. The Energy Technology Proving Ground (the
Proving Ground) i will provide research facilities, capabilities, and expertise to uncover new methods of
energy production and distribution required to meet energy goals and demands across all U.S. energy
sectors. INL’s approach will be forward-looking, comprehensive, affordable, resilient, reliable, and
secure. There is a critical path to develop infrastructure that INL will take to build globally important
capabilities for a lab- to pioneer-scale demonstration proving ground. The Proving Ground directly
supports the achievement of INL’s mission, demonstrates INL’s research capabilities, and ensures INL
aligns future research infrastructure and development capabilities with mission need and laboratory goals.
Fundamental to the development of Proving Ground infrastructure will be development of this National
Environmental Protection Act (NEPA) impact analysis.
 Proving Ground infrastructure will be composed of research facilities that may include laboratories,
high bays, environmental enclosures, common areas, roads, utility corridors, concrete and gravel pads,
space and access to state-of-the-art research facilities. This combination of facilities will provide safe and
secure access over multiple decades to narrow the gap between research and commercial deployment of
advanced energy technologies that harness the four primary energy currencies: heat, electricity, hydrogen,
and carbon. Proving Ground facilities will be designed to embrace these four primary energy currencies
and will provide the framework to establish the associated supporting integration, utility, and transport
infrastructure.
 This action accommodates the size of prototype and pilot-scale testing sought by industry, frees up
space within the Research and Engineering Campus, and places potentially high-hazard work within the
controlled environment of the INL. It is initially estimated that up to 400,000 square feet of enclosed
space, with approximately 400 acres of internal land, will be provided for research activities.

The primary Proving Ground Programs are:
• Biomass and Waste Preprocessing
 The Biomass and Waste Feedstocks program is focused on capturing, storing, preprocessing and
 using underutilized or waste biogenic and plastic materials for the creation of fungible fuels and
 chemicals. This program is home to the Biomass Feedstock National User Facility (BFNUF),
 which is a multi-scale, modular feedstock preprocessing research system capable of managing
 variability for a wide range of feedstocks. The focus of BFNUF research is innovation to advance
 the economic production of biofuels from nonfood and waste materials.
• High Temperature Hydrogen Production and Electrochemistry
 The High Temperature Hydrogen Production program focuses on ultrahigh-efficiency hydrogen
 production using a variety of power sources, including simulated nuclear reactors (AC current
 and heat) and integrated energy resources (DC current).
 The High Temperature Electrochemistry program develops fundamental processes around the
 structure, composition, morphology, and defects that impact the electrochemical and catalytic
 properties of ionic and electronic conductors. Specifically, the research focuses on developing
 new strategies, novel materials/structures, and new processes for efficient and cost-effective
 chemical and energy transformation. Using this new understanding, advanced techniques will
 demonstrate bench-scale to demonstration-scale processes to produce fuels and chemicals as part
 of Proving Ground research, development, and demonstration (RD&D) actions.
• Thermal Energy Management and Waste Carbon Utilization
 The Thermal Energy Management program focuses on research into efficiently managing thermal
 energy from both traditional and innovative heat sources that can be applied to a range of
 industrial processes. The potential for thermal-energy storage systems to be co-optimized with a
 variety of other energy systems, particularly nuclear, makes INL uniquely positioned to test and
 demonstrate thermal-storage capabilities as a Proving Ground capability.
• Transportation Innovation Center
 The Transportation and Electric Storage Facility and program are focused on enhancing
 understanding of how key interactions across multiple scales and multiple components impact the
 overall life and performance of vehicles and energy storage systems. Included in this work is the
 synergistic testing and evaluation of electric vehicle charging, hydrogen fuel cell systems, and
 synthetic fuel systems within the larger energy production and distribution ecosystem.
• Microgrid and Critical Infrastructure Test Range Complex Expansion
 The Distributed Energy Systems program will provide essential investigation and testing of
 infrastructure and capabilities to advance the integration and demonstration of clean base-load
 resources, such as nuclear, with distributed energy resources. It will do so on a modern microgrid
 framework, as a testbed, that employs an integrated energy system strategy. Successful
 demonstration of these technologies has significant potential to advance reliable and resilient
 carbon- and pollution-free electricity generation as well as industrial and clean-hydrogen
 feedstock production.

 25

• Microreactor Testing and Operations
 A recognized need for low-impact energy base-power generation has renewed the nation’s
 interest in nuclear power, particularly in the development and deployment of small modular and
 microreactors. Within the context of IES, these new advanced reactors can provide more than just
 electrons on the grid but can provide thermal energy for such industrial processes as production
 of hydrogen and synthetic fuels, manufacture of steel and cement, mining, and other thermal-
 energy-intense processes. With a variety of research focus areas that demand both electrical and
 thermal energy, advanced reactors are the heart of this energy-demonstration campus.
 Buildout of the research capabilities will require the development of basic infrastructure to
 include power and water delivery, waste management, roadways, walkways and other
 transportation or movement elements. These elements are show in the INL Campus Development
 Master plan.
 Construction and operation of these research facilities may create impacts on the human
 environment. This environmental assessment will evaluate the potential impacts represented by
 the proposed action to determine whether any would be categorized as significant and require
 further evaluation.
The facilities proposed to be adjacent to the Proving Ground include:
• Critical and Strategic Minerals and Materials
 The CSMM program aims to address the growing demand for essential materials crucial to
 energy technologies and national security. This initiative focuses on developing resilient domestic
 supply chains for critical and strategic minerals which are vital for applications in electric
 vehicles, nuclear energy, and defense systems. INL leverages its expertise in geosciences,
 separations chemistry, and materials engineering to overcome technical challenges and enhance
 the efficiency of extraction and processing methods. Through targeted RD&D, INL is positioned
 to lead the nation in critical mineral advancements, ultimately contributing to a more self-
 sufficient energy landscape.
• Proving Ground Substation and Switchyard
 To support continued development of the Proving Ground, a new substation and switchyard is
 required to provide for increased power demand due to increased load and the addition of new
 research equipment and processes that require more electricity. Substation 7 will also enhance
 reliability and reduce the risk of power outages by providing a more robust and resilient power
 infrastructure that can also support research requirements. Last, this new substation will improve
 voltage regulation and power quality, necessary to support the research environments
 requirements for “clean” power to ensuring that Proving Ground operations run smoothly and
 efficiently. The new substation is also anticipated to allow for the addition of behind-the-meter
 power integration from potential energy sources associated with research on advanced
 technologies.
