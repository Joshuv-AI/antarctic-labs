Rethinking Vacuity for OOD Detection in Evidential Deep Learning Thanks: Accenture Labs Thanks: Trinity College Dublin.
Claire McNamara

Abstract
Vacuity, or Uncertainty Mass (UM), is commonly used as a metric to evaluate Out-of-Distribution (OOD) detection in Evidential Deep Learning (EDL). It generally involves dividing the number of classes ( K K ) by the total strength of belief ( S S ) of the model’s predictions, where S S is derived from summing the Dirichlet parameters. As such, UM is sensitive to the cardinality of K K . In particular, it is unlikely in practice that there is a linear relationship between K K and S S as K K and S S increase due to the nature of EDL (suppressing incorrectly assigned evidence). As a result, when comparing In Distribution (ID) and OOD results, it is important that K ID K_{\mathrm{ID}} and K OOD K_{\mathrm{OOD}} are equal; something that is not always ensured in practice. We provide an empirical demonstration of how results for AUROC and AUPR can substantially differ when class cardinality between ID and OOD differs by 1, with AUROC differing by as much as 0.318 and AUPR by 0.613 for standard EDL, and AUROC by 0.360 and AUPR by 0.683 for IB-EDL. More concretely, our findings isolate an evaluation artefact: when K differs between ID and OOD, AUROC/AUPR can be artificially inflated without any change in model predictions. We further discuss the evaluation of EDL over causal language models using Multiple-Choice Question-Answer (MCQA) datasets and argue for clearer definitions of ID and OOD in this context. Our primary contribution is an empirical and theoretical demonstration that vacuity-based OOD detection in EDL-fine-tuned LLMs is highly sensitive to uncontrolled differences in evaluated class cardinality. 1
Introduction

Evidential Deep Learning (EDL)
[
18
]
has emerged as a promising way to quantify uncertainty in causal large language models (LLMs) by fine-tuning them to operate as next token classifiers over Multiple-Choice Question-Answer (MCQA) datasets
[
13
,
5
]
. The utilisation of datasets like MCQAs rather than free-text generation stems from how EDL operates. More specifically, it requires a set number of classes with which to create Dirichlet parameters over. Summing the Dirichlet parameters gives you the model’s total strength of belief (
S
) and from this, vacuity can then be understood as the uncertainty mass,
\frac{K}{S}
. It is important to note that in EDL, uncertainty mass (UM) is a quantitative measure, vacuity is its interpretation as ignorance-based uncertainty, and epistemic uncertainty is the broader concept that vacuity approximates but they are not strictly identical. For the purpose of this paper however, when we refer to
vacuity
we mean
UM
. While calculating vacuity-based uncertainty in this manner can be useful
[
18
,
1
,
2
]
, as we will present in this paper, i) vacuity-based Out-of-Distribution (OOD) detection can be highly sensitive to class number (
K
) specification in MCQA-based EDL settings, and (ii) as such, due to this sensitivity, when using it to compare results across datasets, great care must be taken to ensure that the value of
K
remains constant. This issue is particularly salient in MCQA-based LLM evaluation because the class set is not a fixed semantic label space in the same way as standard image classification (where EDL originates
[
18
]
). The labels, such as A - D, are placeholders whose semantic content changes with each question, and the number of answer options can differ across datasets. Therefore, class-dependent uncertainty quantities may reflect changes in task format or answer-option structure rather than only epistemic uncertainty about the input. Specifically, the paper highlights an implementation-sensitive failure mode in vacuity-based OOD detection for MCQA fine-tuned LLMs, rather than proposing a new uncertainty method. As such, this paper demonstrates that vacuity-based OOD detection in EDL-fine-tuned LLMs can be highly sensitive to uncontrolled differences in effective class cardinality (
K
), leading to substantially inflated AUROC and AUPR estimates.

This paper makes three contributions:

1.

We empirically demonstrate that mismatched effective class cardinality can substantially inflate AUROC and AUPR for vacuity-based OOD detection in EDL-fine-tuned LLMs.

2.

We show formally that vacuity,
\frac{K}{S}
, is not invariant to class expansion unless the added class receives concentration equal to the mean concentration of the original classes.

3.

We discuss why MCQA-based LLM evaluation differs from fixed-label classification and why clearer definitions of ID, OOD, and task-format shift are needed when applying EDL to LLMs.

The paper first introduces EDL and a variation of it more formally, it then presents how vacuity-based uncertainty is used currently to detect OOD inputs, next it shows the impact of (i) and (ii) above in practice, and finally, the impact of the current utilisation of uncertainty within the context of LLMs and the broader EDL domain is discussed.

2
Background and Related Work

As mentioned in the introduction, in order to work around the fact that causal language models operate by outputting tokens sequentially rather than performing classification tasks, it is common to fine-tune an LLM using Parameter Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) methods like LoRA
[
12
]
on MCQA datasets when examining how they perform with EDL
[
13
,
5
]
. Working this way yields a constrained set of possible next token options - the options (classes) of the MCQA dataset. This section details how EDL operates over MCQA datasets with LLMs and outlines a variation of EDL - Information-Bottleneck EDL (IB-EDL)
[
13
]
.

2.1
Evidential Deep Learning (Standard EDL)

In standard EDL, instead of using an activation function like
Softmax
over all logits at the output head of the model, a non-negative activation function like
Softplus
is used over just the target logits (where the targets correspond to the answer options of the MCQA). The result is what is referred to as the "evidence" or support that the model has assigned each class (
e
). From that the Dirichlet parameters are calculated as
\alpha_{i}=e_{i}+1,\forall_{i}\in\{1,..K\}
. Finally, the prediction is taken as the expected probability of a chosen class,
\hat{y}=\underset{i}{\operatorname*{arg\,max}}\mathbb{E}[p_{i}]=\underset{i}{\operatorname*{arg\,max}}\frac{\alpha_{i}}{S}
, where
S=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\alpha_{i}
corresponds to the strength of belief the model has overall. A more formal representation is as follows:

\mathrm{Dir}(\boldsymbol{p}|\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_{0})}{\prod^{K}_{i=1}\Gamma(\alpha_{i})}\prod^{K}_{i=1}p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}-1}

Where,

\mathbb{E}[p_{k}]=\frac{\alpha_{k}}{\alpha_{0}},\qquad\alpha_{0}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}

From this, as introduced previously, vacuity (Uncertainty Mass, UM) can be expressed as,

\frac{K}{S}
where the
S=\alpha_{0}
(we use
S
,
S_{K}
, and
\alpha_{0}
interchangeably to denote total Dirichlet strength).

When fine-tuning the LLM, following
[
18
]
, the loss function typically operates as follows:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{EDL}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MSE}}+\lambda\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Reg}}(\theta)

where
\lambda>0
,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MSE}}=\sum^{K}_{i=1}(y_{i}-\frac{\alpha_{i}}{\alpha_{0}})^{2}+\frac{\alpha_{i}(\alpha_{0}-\alpha_{i})}{\alpha_{0}^{2}(\alpha_{i}+1)}
, and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Reg}}(\theta)=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathrm{Dir}(\boldsymbol{p}|\tilde{\alpha})||\mathrm{Dir}(\boldsymbol{p}|1))
.

2.2
Information-Bottleneck EDL (IB-EDL)

A commonly raised issue with standard EDL is that, as a result of the fact that the model is only penalised for assigning evidence to the wrong class, the amount of evidence the model assigns to the correct class is unbounded. This can result in an overconfident model which is the issue that the authors of IB-EDL sought to address through the addition of an
information-bottleneck
. In other words, to encourage the model to keep only the most necessary evidence that still results in a correct prediction.

The loss function for IB-EDL is instead:

\underset{\theta}{\mathrm{min}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IB-MSE}}+\beta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IB-Info}}(\theta)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IB-MSE}}
follows the standard EDL MSE objective in form, but is re-derived within the Information Bottleneck framework, and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IB-Info}}=

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IB\text{-}Info}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{p(x)}\!\left[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(p(z\mid x)\,\|\,r(z)\right)\right]\propto\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{p(x)}\!\left[\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}+\|\sigma\|_{2}^{2}-2\sum_{i=1}^{C}\log(\sigma_{i})\right]
.

The bottleneck term regularises the information retained in the latent representation, which in turn constrains the evidence available for prediction. The result is a loss function that penalises too much evidence assigned to a prediction (encouraging the model to only keep the most necessary information needed to make a prediction) along with the standard EDL objective of encouraging evidence assigned to the wrong class to 0.

2.3
Metrics Used in Evaluation

There are a variety of methods used to assess a fine-tuned LLMs performance when fine-tuned using EDL or one of its variations. One of the most commonly used ones is Expected Calibration Error (ECE)
[
17
,
13
,
16
]
. Another method used to assess model performance in general across modalities (Computer Vision, CV, for instance) is an assessment of how well the model performs when conducting Out-of-Distribution (OOD) detection. This is typically carried out by using the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (AUROC)
[
3
]
or Area Under the Precision-Recall curve (AUPR)
[
14
]
using metrics like UM (vacuity) with ID and OOD inputs
[
13
,
2
,
4
]
. We raise concerns over evaluating model performance using OOD with UM in this manner as will be presented below. Broader skepticism over vacuity-based uncertainty measured as
\frac{K}{S}
has been raised describing the formulation as "somewhat arbitrary"
[
9
]
noting that it does not follow from subjective logic and may not achieve practical effectiveness.

3
Impact of Altering K

The problem can be more generally understood by the following:

Proposition 1

(Vacuity Invariance Under Class Expansion)
.

Let

u_{K}=\frac{K}{S_{K}},\qquad S_{K}=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\alpha_{i}

denote the vacuity for a
K
-class EDL classifier. Suppose one additional class is introduced with concentration parameter
\alpha_{K+1}
, giving

u_{K+1}=\frac{K+1}{S_{K}+\alpha_{K+1}}.

Then vacuity remains unchanged, i.e.

u_{K+1}=u_{K},

if and only if

\alpha_{K+1}=\frac{S_{K}}{K}.

Equivalently, since
\alpha_{K+1}=e_{K+1}+1
,

e_{K+1}=\frac{S_{K}}{K}-1.

Thus, vacuity is invariant to an increase in classes only when the newly introduced class receives concentration equal to the pre-existing mean concentration across classes. Proof follows by setting
u_{K+1}
=
u_{K}
and rearranging; full proof is provided in the Appendix
7.1
.

In practice, such conditions are unlikely to hold due to the nature of EDL. Namely, that it suppresses evidence assigned to the wrong class. This non-invariance directly explains the AUROC/AUPR inflation observed under mismatched K in the next section.

4
Impact in Practice

This section details how using different numbers of K can impact results in practice using a published account.

4.1
OOD using UM and MP

A motivating example of the unreliability of using vacuity-based uncertainty as a means of detecting OOD inputs in practice can be seen through a faithful reproduction of the work of the authors of IB-EDL
[
13
]
. As discussed above, IB-EDL aims to reduce spurious evidence by applying a
bottleneck
regularisation over the evidence. The results show that when Llama3-8b
[
8
]
is fine-tuned on variations of EDL and equivalent methods (e.g., R-EDL
[
4
]
and I-EDL
[
7
]
, IB-EDL generally maintains or increases accuracy across the MCQA datasets tests and reports generally lower ECE results. Thus it suggests that the IB-EDL does indeed generally work better in practice when calibrating an LLM.

An important consideration that arose during reproduction however was that OOD detection was achieved using the reciprocal of Uncertainty Mass (UM) and Max Probability (MP) with ID=1 and OOD=0. AUROC results from
[
13
]
report that both using the UM and MP results in strong OOD detection across EDL methods, models, and datasets indicating that both metrics are useful when used in the context of detecting OOD inputs. Specifically, the reported AUROC for reciprocal of UM across all tested MCQA datasets for Llama3-8b with IB-EDL was reported to be between 85.45 and 94.77, and the MP was reported to be between 88.14 and 89.16. During reproduction, while it was possible to achieve close to the reported number using the author’s provided GitHub implementation
1
1

1

https://github.com/sandylaker/ib-edl/
, it was not possible to recreate close to the reported numbers for UM nor MP using an independent implementation. A detailed account of the full reproduction procedure can be found in the Appendix
7.2
. This prompted an inspection of the released dataset helper code that indicated that for all OOD tests in the main paper, while the number of classes (
K
) for the ID dataset was four, the number of classes for the OOD datasets tested was five. The result was a much larger difference between ID and OOD uncertainties and probabilities. This highlighted two issues: 1., vacuity-based uncertainty is sensitive to K (naturally as
\frac{K}{S}
) more specifically, when class cardinality differs, the impact is a much larger separation between ID and OOD in AUROC; from which the second point directly follows, 2., when corrected, UM and MP perform weakly at detecting OOD in this context. It is important to note that we include MP for completeness in line with IB-EDl
[
13
]
but is not governed by the same
\frac{K}{S}
dependence as vacuity and is not a primary object of analysis.

4.3
Results of Implementation A

As previously stated, implementation A refers to the code provided by the authors of
[
13
]
. In reviewing implementation A, we observed that the variable for K (n_labels) had been set to 5 rather than 4 for ARC-C and ARC-E. After a re-run of implementation A changing
K
to equal 4 for ARC-C and ARC-E, the AUROC drops substantially. We also show the AUPR for completeness which similarly displays a significant drop. To examine the effect more thoroughly, we also removed the fifth option in CSQA ("E") to assess the impact of comparing outputs of a four-class MCQA dataset to a five-class one. Namely, examples whose correct answer was option E were excluded, so that the four-option evaluation did not create instances without a valid correct label. Because CSQA is used here as OOD input rather than for accuracy evaluation, this manipulation is intended to isolate the effect of changing the evaluated output space. We further examined the impact on standard EDL, which showed the same overall pattern as IB-EDL. Both sets of results, IB-EDL and standard EDL, can be seen in Tables
3
and
4
respectively. It is important to note that these results are obtained using the author’s implementation, where ARC-C and ARC-E were evaluated with five-dimensional evidence/concentration vectors despite having four answer options. This matters because the expected probability for each class is calculated as
\alpha_{i}/S
, where
S
is the total Dirichlet strength over the evaluated class set. Increasing the effective output dimensionality can therefore lower the maximum expected probability for OOD examples, increasing separation from the four-class ID scores and thereby inflating AUROC and AUPR (as seen in both tables). The same issue stands for CSQA where, while the correct number of classes is five (
K=5
) as was implemented, when compared against outputs for the four-class OBQA dataset, the overall max probability is lower as the probabilities are distributed across a greater number of classes. More specifically, the formal
\frac{K}{S}
dependence applies specifically to UM (vacuity). MP is included because it is also computed over the evaluated class set through the Dirichlet expected probabilities,
\frac{\alpha_{i}}{S}
and therefore may change when the effective output space changes. However, MP should be understood as indirectly affected by output-space construction rather than directly defined by
\frac{K}{S}
.

Results of Implementation B

As mentioned previously, implementation B is our separate implementation of fine-tuning Llama3-8b with IB-EDL and standard EDL on the OBQA MCQA dataset using the same setup (hyperparameters, training steps, etc.) as
[
13
]
and using their code implementation and paper for reference.

A core difference between implementations A and B is the fact that the predictions for implementation B with ARC-C and ARC-E were generated using four classes rather than the five that are present in implementation A. As seen in Table
5

Another important difference between implementation A and B is that we observed that padding strategy can affect logit extraction when using fixed positional indexing (e.g., logits[:, -1]). To account for this, implementation B standardises on left-padding to ensure alignment between sequence end and prediction position.

5
Discussion

We do not argue that EDL or IB-EDL is ineffective as a calibration method and these findings as such should be interpreted as a clarification of evaluation conditions rather than a critique of prior work. When class cardinality is controlled, IB-EDL continues to exhibit strong calibration properties; however, OOD separability measured via class-dependent metrics requires careful interpretation. Indeed, our reproduced fine-tuning results show competitive accuracy, ECE, and NLL. Our claim is narrower: vacuity-based OOD comparisons can be misleading when the effective number of evaluated classes differs between ID and OOD datasets. This phenomenon is not unique to IB-EDL. For example,
[
16
]
evaluate a model trained on a 2-class dataset (Amazon Reviews
[
10
]
) on a 10-class dataset (Yahoo Answers
[
24
]
). While their work does not rely on vacuity, their uncertainty measures (predictive entropy, expected entropy, and mutual information) are defined over the output simplex and therefore scale with the number of classes K. As such, differences in uncertainty between ID and OOD settings may also reflect differences in output dimensionality rather than solely differences in data distribution. More generally, this highlights that evidential uncertainty measures are defined with respect to a particular output space. When the dimensionality or semantics of this space change between ID and OOD settings, the resulting uncertainty values are not strictly comparable, even when computed correctly. In practice, this suggests that controlling for class cardinality, or applying appropriate normalisation, is important when interpreting uncertainty-based OOD comparisons.

The consideration of what metric to use when detecting OOD inputs raises a larger issue relating to the ambiguity of what constitutes OOD input in the context of an EDL evaluation with LLMs. In the context of an LLM fine-tuned on common-sense MCQA data, should we consider a different MCQA common-sense dataset truly OOD? While it may not reflect the precise data the model was trained on, it likely can still be considered within the realm of its ID distribution. In other words, it may be more apt to consider such datasets as
near OOD

[
22
]
datasets. As seen above, when confronted with input data from a different domain to its training data (Math MMLU), MP and UM perform better as OOD detectors. Such datasets perhaps can be considered as
far OOD

[
22
]
ones. Furthermore, due to the nature of LLMs and the method by which they are fine-tuned, there is another distinction we can make -
task OOD
. If considering an LLM that has been fine-tuned to distinguish between four options of an MCQA input, can we consider providing it with five options instead as an alternate source of OOD?

A related, though not identical, consideration arises in CV classification scenarios with EDL, the domain from which it originates. In standard CV OOD evaluation, the classifier output space usually remains fixed by the ID training labels; for example, a CIFAR-10 classifier still produces CIFAR-10 outputs when evaluated on CIFAR-100 images. Therefore, the explicit MCQA class-cardinality mechanism demonstrated here does not directly transfer to CV. However, methods such as R-EDL
[
4
]
, while reducing the explicit dependence of UM on the number of classes through a learnable prior weight, may still raise broader questions about how vacuity-based scores vary across OOD datasets with different semantic granularity. Consequently, comparisons across heterogeneous OOD datasets may still risk conflating epistemic uncertainty with differences in semantic granularity, domain shift, or task mismatch. A related consideration also arises for I-EDL
[
7
]
: although it does not explicitly use UM, it relies on the total evidence strength
S
, which can vary with the model’s evidence allocation over its evaluated output space. Thus, while the explicit class-cardinality mismatch demonstrated in MCQA is less applicable to standard CV classification because the output space remains fixed by the training labels, care is still needed when interpreting vacuity- or evidence-based OOD scores across heterogeneous OOD benchmarks. Shen et al.
[
19
]
suggest that the current definitions of epistemic and aleatoric uncertainty and the way in which they are measured within context of EDL is misaligned due to the absence of model uncertainty and that while methods appear to work downstream, that is in spite of their poor ability to capture uncertainty. In other words, EDL in its current form may reflect properties of the predicted distribution rather than fully capturing model uncertainty in the Bayesian sense.

Reasoning over these problems and considerations surfaces an underlying assumption - that causal language models operating over MCQAs can be considered comparable to CV models operating over image classification. The problem is that MCQA fundamentally differs from CV classification in that the output space is dynamically defined by the input. More specifically, in MCQA, the semantic meaning of classes is instance-dependent, unlike fixed semantic labels in standard image classification. As a result, uncertainty or belief measures that depend on output dimensionality or class semantics are not invariant under changes in the number of answer options, even when the underlying task remains identical. This makes MCQA a less than ideal proxy for evaluating class-based uncertainty or causal belief models without explicit correction. There are further concerns raised about using MCQAs in general to evaluate uncertainty in LLMs
[
23
]
where the output may reflect less about uncertainty and more general decoding variability. Other work has surfaced the fact that LLMs may default to choosing the "least incorrect option"
[
21
]
raising questions about their performance when no correct option is available.

6
Conclusion

Our findings do not argue against evidential uncertainty itself, but against comparing vacuity-based OOD scores across mismatched class cardinalities. We argue that when considering using vacuity as UM for OOD detection, it is imperative that
K_{\mathrm{ID}}
and
K_{\mathrm{OOD}}
are equal and demonstrate how K-variance can impact results in practice. We further raise questions surrounding the direct transferability of using EDL in the context of causal language models over MCQAs and a need for greater clarity in what exactly is being thought of as OOD input. It is hoped that our work will contribute to greater transparency in how ID and OOD datasets are defined within the context of EDL with LLMs and result in greater care being taken when calculating and comparing UM as a metric for OOD detection. Our follow-up work aims to be able to clarify types of OOD input in this context and how they affect EDL in practice. In short, vacuity-based OOD comparisons are only meaningful under controlled output dimensionality; otherwise, they reflect evaluation artefacts rather than model behaviour.

7.1
Vacuity Invariance Under Class Expansion Proof

Proof.

Let

u_{K}=\frac{K}{S_{K}},\qquad S_{K}=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\alpha_{i}

denote the vacuity of a
K
-class EDL classifier. Suppose one additional class is introduced with concentration parameter
\alpha_{K+1}
, so that the updated total strength becomes

S_{K+1}=S_{K}+\alpha_{K+1},

and the new vacuity is

u_{K+1}=\frac{K+1}{S_{K}+\alpha_{K+1}}.

We seek the condition under which vacuity remains unchanged after adding the new class, i.e.

u_{K+1}=u_{K}.

Substituting the definitions of
u_{K}
and
u_{K+1}
, we obtain

\frac{K+1}{S_{K}+\alpha_{K+1}}=\frac{K}{S_{K}}.

Cross-multiplying gives

(K+1)S_{K}=K(S_{K}+\alpha_{K+1}).

Expanding the right-hand side,

(K+1)S_{K}=KS_{K}+K\alpha_{K+1}.

Rearranging,

KS_{K}+S_{K}=KS_{K}+K\alpha_{K+1}.

Subtracting
KS_{K}
from both sides yields

S_{K}=K\alpha_{K+1}.

Hence,

\alpha_{K+1}=\frac{S_{K}}{K}.

This establishes that vacuity is invariant under the addition of one class if and only if the newly introduced class receives concentration equal to the mean concentration of the original
K
classes.

Finally, since in EDL

\alpha_{K+1}=e_{K+1}+1,

it follows that

e_{K+1}=\frac{S_{K}}{K}-1.

Therefore, vacuity remains unchanged if and only if the added class receives evidence satisfying

e_{K+1}=\frac{S_{K}}{K}-1.

∎

As mentioned in the main text, such a condition where
e_{K+1}=\frac{S_{K}}{K}-1
is unlikely to hold in practice as it depends on the evidence scaling linearly with K.
