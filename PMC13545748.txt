Effect of luteal phase support with progesterone on thyroid function: a sub-study of the FET optimizing trial
Authors: Diana Grove-Laugesen, Marte Saupstad, Anette Vestergaard Gabrielsen, Eva Ebbehøj, Kristine Løssl, Ulla Breth Knudsen, Anja Pinborg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveInfertility is a major health issue, and fertility treatments, including frozen embryo transfer (FET), are common. Progesterone, essential for endometrium development and receptivity, is used to improve implantation rates. This study investigates the effect of luteal phase support with progesterone on thyroid function in modified natural FET cycles.MethodsThis is a sub-study of a randomized controlled trial (FET optimizing trial). Data were collected at two fertility centers from January 2019 to October 2022. Euthyroid women receiving single frozen–thawed embryo transfer in a modified natural cycle were included. The study participants were categorized into two groups: the luteal phase support (LPS) and the no LPS groups. The LPS group received vaginal progesterone 100 mg three times daily starting at day 4 after ovulation trigger with human chorionic gonadotropin (day 0). A pregnancy test was performed on day 16. Thyroid function was repeatedly measured. Linear mixed modeling was used to test the effect of progesterone on thyrotropin (TSH, primary outcome) and free thyroxine (fT4). Interaction with thyroid autoimmunity was tested.ResultsIn total, 85 women were included: 45 received progesterone LPS and 40 did not. Participants were 34 ± 4 years of age and had a BMI of 23 (IQR: 21; 26), and 25% were positive for thyroid autoimmunity. Serum progesterone increased with LPS, but TSH and fT4 levels were not affected by LPS. There was no interaction with thyroid autoimmunity. In the progesterone LPS group, 44% conceived compared with 55% in the no LPS group, P = 0.33.ConclusionProgesterone LPS does not affect thyroid function during the luteal phase.Trial registration numberThis trial was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03795220) and EudraCT (2018-002207-34) and was completed.

Introduction

Thyroid hormones (THs) are vital to reproductive health and pregnancy maintenance (1). The level of THs is meticulously controlled by negative feedback from the pituitary, reflected in the thyrotropin (TSH) level. Any inadequacy of THs, either changes in production or changes in the free hormone fraction, will thus read out in the level of TSH.

Another hormone important for pregnancy maintenance is progesterone (pro gestation). In early pregnancy, progesterone is produced by the corpus luteum and supports the endometrium, preparing it for implantation (2). Thus, progesterone is essential for development and receptiveness of the endometrium, and low levels of progesterone are associated with miscarriages (3). The progesterone receptor is a steroid receptor, and it belongs, like the thyroid hormone receptor (THR), to the nuclear receptor superfamily. They exhibit striking structural and functional similarities (4). Progesterone affects the expression of several THR isoforms on the protein and mRNA level in human villous trophoblasts (5). Progesterone supplementation in the luteal phase of the natural frozen embryo transfer cycle has been the standard of care without any real proof of concept (6).

Whether treatment with progesterone affects thyroid function in a fertility setting is not clear. Only a few studies examined the thyroid function in relation to endogenous or exogenous progesterone. A previous study showed that the administration of synthetic progesterone (medroxyprogesterone acetate) does not alter the level of thyroxin-binding protein (TBG) (7). However, in vitro cell studies (8) suggest effects of natural progesterone on TH production, and two small randomized controlled trials (RCTs) found that progesterone seems to increase the free thyroxin (fT4) level and decrease the TSH level (9, 10). Therefore, we aimed to test whether luteal phase support (LPS) with natural progesterone affects the thyroid function as assessed by TSH and fT4.

Methods

This study investigates the effect of LPS with vaginal progesterone supplementation on thyroid function in modified natural cycle (mNC) frozen–thawed embryo transfer (FET). The study is a sub-study of an RCT investigating the effect of progesterone LPS and the timing of blastocyst transfer in mNC FET on the live birth rate (11). The mNC-FET is characterized by the transfer of a frozen–thawed embryo in a natural menstrual cycle where the administration of a single dose of hCG (LH activity) is used to induce endometrial luteinization and endometrial–embryo synchronicity at the time of the embryo transfer. The luteal phase approximately corresponds to the period between the day of hCG trigger and the day of pregnancy testing. The main study was conducted between January 2019 and March 2024 at eight Danish public fertility clinics. The sub-study was conducted between January 2019 and October 2022 at Horsens Regional Hospital and Copenhagen University Hospital Rigshospitalet. The study protocol (12) and the reproductive outcomes of the RCT were previously published (11). Overall, the progesterone LPS and the timing of blastocyst transfer did not affect the live birth rate or other obstetric outcomes.

We adhere to the 2025 CONSORT statement for the reporting of RCT (13).

Participants

Participants were 18–41 years of age, had regular menstrual cycles (23–35 days), and underwent mNC-FET treatment with single blastocyst transfer derived after homologous IVF/ICSI. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria have already been described (12). All participants underwent postponed FET, meaning at least one full menstrual cycle passed between ovarian stimulation and oocyte retrieval and the subsequent FET cycle. In this sub-study, any known thyroid disease was an additional exclusion criterion, e.g. treatment with antithyroid drugs, supplementation with levothyroxine or triiodothyronine, or a baseline TSH level outside the reference range. All women participating in the main study were consecutively invited to participate in the sub-study. Participation required a separate informed consent and storage of blood samples in a biobank. Recruitment to the sub-study ended when at least 80 women had been randomized to LPS or no LPS.

Treatment and randomization

Participants were allocated to one of the four study groups (1:1:1:1): (A) blastocyst transfer day 6 following hCG trigger and progesterone LPS, (B) blastocyst transfer day 7 and progesterone LPS, (C) blastocyst transfer day 6 and no LPS, and (D) blastocyst transfer day 7 and no LPS.

The randomization was performed on the day of hCG trigger or the day before and was conducted by research staff, using a computerized program running a minimization algorithm (Doxos CDS, Sweden). The randomization algorithm has previously been described (12).

Procedure

Women attended a screening visit on cycle days 2–5 where screening blood samples were drawn. The included women were followed with transvaginal ultrasonography from the mid-follicular phase until the dominant follicle reached ≥17 mm (day 0) when the ovulation trigger, 250 μg hCG (Ovitrelle®, Merck), was administered at 10 pm. Women allocated to progesterone LPS received vaginal suppositories with progesterone (100 mg*3/daily, Lutinus®, Ferring Pharmaceuticals, Denmark) starting on day 4 following hCG trigger. A single blastocyst transfer was performed on day 6 or 7 after ovulation trigger (according to randomization group). If the blastocyst did not survive thaw and no other blastocyst was available, treatment was canceled. At day 16, following the hCG trigger, a pregnancy test (s-hCG) was conducted. An s-hCG level ≥5 IU/L was considered a positive test. When s-hCG was between 5 and 49 IU/L, patients were followed as per local instruction with repeated s-hCG measurements until a sufficient increase in s-hCG or a fall in s-hCG level <5 IU/L (pregnancy loss) was confirmed. Patients with an s-hCG level <5 IU/L were considered non-pregnant.

Blood samples were drawn at screening, day of hCG trigger (day 0), day of blastocyst transfer (6- or 7-days following ovulation trigger), and days 11, 14, 16, and 19 after hCG trigger.

Outcomes

The primary outcome is the change in TSH during the luteal phase in women receiving or not receiving progesterone LPS (study group A + B vs study group C + D). Secondary outcomes include the change in fT4 and serum (s-) progesterone between groups.

Biochemistry

At study site 1 (Copenhagen University Hospital Rigshospitalet, Copenhagen, Denmark), TSH, fT4, and s-progesterone were measured by electrochemiluminescence immunoassays (Cobas 8000, e801 Roche Diagnostic, Germany). The detection level for TSH was 0.01 IU/L with a CVmax of 4% at both 2 × 10−3 IU/L and 6 × 10−3 IU/L. For fT4, the detection level was 1.3 pmol/L with a CVmax of 6% at both 15 and 40 pmol/L. For s-progesterone, the detection level was 0.6 nmol/L with a CVmax of 10% at 3 nmol/L. Thyroid peroxidase antibody (TPOab) and thyroglobulin antibody (TGab) were analyzed using an immunofluorometric assay (B.R.A.H.M.S Kryptor Compact PLUS). For TPOab, the detection level was 50 ie/L and CVmax at 140 and 260 ie/L was 13 and 8%, respectively. For TGab, the detection level was 33 ie/L and CVmax at 100 and 340 ie/L was 7 and 5%, respectively. At study site 1, a TPOab value ≥60 ie/L and a TGab value ≥33 ie/L were considered positive. The reference ranges were 0.4 to 4.8 × 10−3 IU/L for TSH and 12–22 pmol/L for fT4.

At study site 2 (Regional Hospital Horsens, Denmark), TPOab, TGab, s-progesterone, and fT4 were measured by electrochemiluminescence immunoassays (Cobas 8000 Roche Diagnostics). For fT4, the detection level was 1.3 pmol/L with CVmax at 14 and 37.2 pmol/L of 6 and 8%, respectively. For s-progesterone, the detection level was 0.7 nmol/L with CVmax at 37 nmol/L of 8%. For TPOab, the detection level was 10 × 103 ie/L with CVmax at 46 ×103 and 109 ×103 ie/L of 16 and 10%, respectively. For TGab, the detection level was 25 ×103 ie/L with CVmax at 95.7 × 103 and 336 ×103 ie/L of 20 and 12%, respectively. TSH was analyzed by chemiluminometric method using Atellica IM analyzer (Siemens Healthcare Diagnostics Inc., USA) with a detection level of 0.01 × 10−3 ie/L, CVmax at 0.688 × 10−3 and 29.61 ×10−3 ie/L of 12 and 10%, respectively. At study site 2, a TPOab value ≥33 ie/L and a TGab level ≥436 ie/L were considered positive. The reference range was 0.3 to 4.5 × 10−3 IU/L for TSH and 12–20 pmol/L for fT4.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean with standard deviation (SD), median with interquartile range (IQR), or number (n) and frequencies (%). The effect of progesterone LPS on thyroid function (TSH and fT4) was analyzed using linear mixed modeling with time and randomization group (±progesterone LPS) and their interaction as fixed factors and participants as random effects. The unequal standard errors and correlations in the two groups were taken into account in the analysis when relevant. Model validation was performed by inspection of QQ-plots of the residuals. We investigated the difference in change from hCG trigger (day 0) to the day of pregnancy testing (day 16). In prespecified analysis, we tested whether the effect of progesterone showed interaction with thyroid autoimmunity (TAI; TPOab and/or TGab positive) and conception by including the relevant interaction term in the model, e.g. TAI status × study group and pregnancy × study group, and in an exploratory analysis, TPOab × study group. We also examined the effect of blastocyst transfer on day 6 versus transfer on day 7 by estimating the interaction of transfer day and development in TSH. Furthermore, we explored whether the effect of progesterone differed according to a high TSH (above the 50th percentile) versus a low TSH level (below the 50th percentile) on trigger day by adding the interaction term of randomization and trigger-day TSH level to the primary linear mixed model described.

The association between s-progesterone and TSH was explored using linear regression adjusted for age, BMI, and TAI.

We applied an intention-to-treat approach and included all randomized participants in the analysis.

The statistical significance level was set at 0.05. The included sample size allowed the detection of a between-group difference in TSH of 0.48, assuming a standard deviation of the TSH difference of 0.83, α 0.05, and β 0.80. Analyses were performed using STATA version 15 (STATA Corp. LP, USA).

Ethical approval

All women enrolled in the trial provided written informed consent for trial participation, including a separate informed consent for participation in the sub-study and storage of blood samples in a biobank.

The study received ethical approval across Denmark by the Capital Region of Denmark Centre for Ethics (H-18025839) and the Danish Medicines Agency (63569). Study medication was provided to the patients free of charge.

Results

Of the 92 eligible women, 85 women fulfilled the inclusion criteria and none of the exclusion criteria and were included in the study (Fig. 1). Forty-five women were randomized to receive progesterone LPS and 40 women were randomized to no LPS. All women received the allocated intervention; one was lost to follow-up. Overall, randomization was successful with comparable groups (Table 1). The participants were 34 ± 4 years of age and had a BMI of 23 (IQR: 21; 26), and 25% were positive for thyroid autoimmunity (TAI). The frequency of TPOab differed between groups, however, with TPOab positivity of 2% vs 23%, P = 0.005, whereas the frequency of TGab was comparable, P = 0.97 (Table 1). Sixty-six percent of women had previously conceived, while 22% had previously given birth. The most common cause of infertility in the study population was male factor infertility. Reproductive outcomes are given in Table 2. In the progesterone LPS group, 44% conceived compared with 55% in the no LPS group, P = 0.33.

Discussion

In this study, randomized allocation to either progesterone LPS or no LPS did not affect the TSH or fT4 level trajectories during the luteal phase. The presence of thyroid autoimmunity did not change these findings.

Only a few studies examined thyroid function in response to treatment with progesterone without estrogen (14). One RCT (n = 69) investigating the effects of progesterone in postmenopausal women found an increase in the fT4 level and a trend toward a decrease in the TSH level after 12 weeks of treatment (10). A small RCT (n = 8) in a similar population of postmenopausal women found a decrease in the nocturnal TSH level in response to three weeks of non-micronized progesterone treatment (9).

A non-randomized comparative study of women treated with 150 mg depot medroxyprogesterone acetate showed slightly increased fT4 levels but similar TSH levels after 12 months compared to women treated with a copper intrauterine device (15). The authors suggested that the mechanism might be a suppressed activity of the deiodinases responsible for conversion of T4 to triiodothyronine (T3), resulting in a compensatory increase in fT4 to maintain T3 levels. In contrast, no increase in fT4 was observed during short-term treatment with vaginally applied natural progesterone in our study.

Progesterone does not affect the TH-binding protein, such as estrogen (7). However, progesterone has been suggested to affect the production of THs and the impact of THs at the transcriptional level or by interacting with the TH receptor. The presence of progesterone receptors in human thyroid follicular cells has been detected in some (16), but not all studies (17). In a study of cultured normal human thyroid cells, treatment with progesterone significantly enhanced the upregulatory effect of high-dose TSH treatment on several genes important for thyroid function and growth, including an upregulation of the enzyme thyroperoxidase involved in TH production (8). The addition of mifepristone, a progesterone antagonist, abolished this effect. These data suggest that progesterone affects the production of THs, but whether the effect is dependent on the TSH level is not clear. Other in vitro cell studies have demonstrated a complex coactivator-mediated interaction between the progesterone and the TH receptors (18). Thus, the effect of THs on target tissue may be modulated by progesterone, as shown in the human placenta (5).

Ovarian stimulation affects the level of TSH, although data on the degree and duration have been diverging. A systematic review found that the evidence was inconclusive, but that TSH concentrations may be increased during or within 1 month after OS (19). In the very recent ATA guideline on thyroid disease in preconception, pregnancy, and postpartum, it was recommended to postpone a TSH measurement until two weeks after ovarian stimulation (20). With the postponed FET in our study, it seems unlikely that thyroid function tests were affected by prior ovarian stimulation.

Strengths and limitations

Treatment duration, treatment dose, and formulation of progesterone supplements vary between the few available studies and, therefore, contribute to differences in findings. Furthermore, a longer observation period might have given different results as we only investigated thyroid function through the luteal phase until day 16 after hCG trigger. However, the neutral effect of progesterone LPS on thyroid function within the short-time window examined in our study is important as it demonstrates that with a normal thyroid function, progesterone does not affect the thyroid function, and thus, thyroid testing is not required in the time period until after a pregnancy is confirmed.

The possible coactivator-mediated interaction between receptors for progesterone and THs is complex (18). It is unknown whether the effect of progesterone varies with the TSH level. In our study, we did not detect deviation in results when comparing women with a low-normal or high-normal TSH level. However, all women had a normal thyroid function, and our finding is not readily translatable to situations with an abnormal TSH level.

The strength of our study is the randomized design and the frequent thyroid testing. Overall, the presence of TAI (TGab and TPOab) was as expected (21); however, the frequency of isolated TPOab was lower and differed among randomization groups. We speculate that this may reflect recommendation of previous guidelines to offer levothyroxine for euthyroid TPOab-positive infertile women who would then be non-eligible for this study (22). We performed interaction analysis and found that the occurrence of TPOab did not change the overall results of a neutral effect of progesterone LPS on TSH and fT4.

This is the largest RCT to examine the impact of natural progesterone LPS on thyroid function during mNC-FET treatment. We demonstrate that progesterone does not affect TH level trajectories in euthyroid women in the earliest period during embryo implantation, indicating no need for additional thyroid function testing until pregnancy has been confirmed. Future studies should examine possible long-term effects of progesterone LPS on thyroid function.
