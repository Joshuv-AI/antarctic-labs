Origin of Dark Current Robustness in Photomultiplication Organic Photodetectors Enabled by Ionic Conjugated Electron‐Blocking Layers
Authors: Yelim Kang, Min Hun Jee, Shuai Zhang, Sang Heon Lee, Jung Min Ha, Xingchao Zhao, Xiaoling Ma, Huiseong Shin, Changhwan Shin, Soonyong Lee, Fujun Zhang, Jae Won Shim, Han Young Woo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPhotomultiplication‐type organic photodetectors (PM OPDs), which rely on signal amplification via electron trapping, suffer from a fundamental trade‐off: high gain is typically accompanied by a steep increase in dark current under high reverse bias. Here, we overcome this challenge by employing ionic conjugated polyelectrolyte (CPE)‐based electron blocking layers (EBLs) with an alternating fluorene‐co‐triphenylamine backbone. CPE‐based EBLs in PM OPDs enable robust dark current stability under reverse bias up to −10 V while maintaining external quantum efficiencies (EQE) exceeding 2000%. Charge‐dynamics analysis using Fowler–Nordheim plots reveals that a nanometer‐thick CPE layer provides electron‐blocking performance comparable to that of a ∼20 nm Al2O3 layer. At the same time, we show that the nature of the ionic side chains (cationic vs anionic) in CPEs governs interfacial energy‐level alignment, thereby modulating hole selectivity and photocarrier dynamics. The design principles establish a general framework for interfacial and field engineering across a broad range of photodetector platforms—from organic to hybrid systems—guiding the development of next‐generation photodetectors.Keywords: charge dynamics, conjugated polyelectrolyte, dark current robustness, electron blocking layer, electron selective insulating, interfacial dipole, photomultiplication organic photodetector

Introduction

Organic photodetectors (OPDs) are widely regarded as promising candidates for next‐generation photodetection devices owing to their spectral tunability and mechanical compatibility with flexible and stretchable substrates [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. Among these devices, photomultiplication‐type OPDs (PM OPDs) are particularly attractive for low‐light applications because they realize internal gain and thus optical signal amplification without requiring external amplification circuitry [6]. This directly supports the deployment of PM OPDs in ultralow‐light imaging, wearable biosensing, and miniaturized spectrometers [7, 8, 9]. By design, PM OPDs can achieve external quantum efficiencies (EQE) exceeding 100%, and a substantial body of prior research has focused on further increasing their EQE values [10, 11].

To achieve a high photocurrent gain, PM OPDs are typically operated under a high reverse bias [12]. In donor‐rich bulk heterojunction systems, the acceptor phase lacks a continuous percolation network, which promotes electron accumulation in trap states and intensifies band bending at the organic photoactive layer/cathode interface. The resulting electrostatic field at the cathode‐side Schottky contact effectively thins the interfacial barrier into a triangular tunneling barrier, enabling efficient hole tunneling as the operative gain pathway [13, 14]. However, in the dark, a strong external reverse bias can drive electron injection from the anode, pre‐establishing similar barrier thinning. As a result, the same tunneling pathway remains accessible even in the absence of light, leading to an increased dark current density (J D). The concomitant increase in the rate of injection events amplifies shot noise and trap‐assisted flicker noise, which in turn increases noise equivalent power (NEP) and degrades specific detectivity (D *) [15, 16]. An elevated dark current also increases the lower bound of linear dynamic range (LDR), narrowing the reliable operating optical power window under practical conditions [17]. Consequently, for practical weak‐light detection in PM OPDs, a central design objective is to preserve photocurrent gain while achieving dark current robustness by suppressing the voltage‐dependent escalation of J D across the reverse‐bias range.

To address the aforementioned challenges in PM OPDs, earlier studies primarily attempted to reduce the dark current by engineering the energy‐level alignment of charge‐transport layers [18, 19, 20]. Nevertheless, these approaches have mainly focused on lowering the absolute dark current level at selected operating biases, whereas suppressing bias‐induced dark‐current escalation throughout the high reverse bias region remains a largely unresolved challenge. Therefore, a design strategy capable of maintaining a low and nearly bias‐invariant dark current while preserving photomultiplication gain is urgently needed for PM OPDs. Our group previously reported a near bias‐invariant dark current in PM OPDs incorporating a conjugated polyelectrolyte (CPE) interfacial layer, but a fundamental understanding of this phenomenon, supported by rigorous mechanistic analysis, is still needed [21].

CPEs consist of a hydrophobic π‐conjugated backbone and hydrophilic ionic side chains, forming semiconducting polymers that exhibit orthogonal solubility with respect to conventional organic semiconductors owing to their amphiphilic architecture. They also possess pH‐neutral characteristics that are fundamentally distinct from PEDOT:PSS (conventional poly(3,4‑ethylenedioxythiophene):polystyrene‑sulfonate, Al4083), positioning CPEs as highly versatile interfacial materials that can be integrated without compromising the integrity or stability of adjacent organic layers. More importantly, the presence of ionic side chains endows CPEs with unique electronic and interfacial functionalities that are inaccessible to conventional conjugated polymer‐based electron‐blocking layers (EBLs). The interfacial energy alignment of CPEs can be systematically tuned through molecular design of the conjugated backbone, the chemical nature of the ionic groups, and the choice of counterions [22, 23, 24, 25]. In addition, the ionic moieties significantly enhance the insulating character of the interlayer and strongly influence charge‐blocking behavior. Through this combined control of energy levels, insulation, and dipolar effects, CPEs can achieve highly selective electron blocking and hole extraction that surpass those of traditional EBLs [26].

In this study, we demonstrate P3HT:PC71BM(100:1 wt/wt)‐based PM OPDs incorporating ionic conjugated CPEs with either cationic or anionic side chains as EBLs. This donor‐rich system was selected as an active layer because the crystalline P3HT phase supports hole transport and provides favorable highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) alignment for efficient tunneling‐assisted hole injection, while the highly diluted PC71BM domains can serve as electron‐trapping sites without forming continuous electron‐percolation pathways [27]. This well‐established PM OPD platform enables the role of newly introduced CPE interlayers to be evaluated more clearly. Two p‐type CPEs based on a fluorene–triphenylamine backbone, TPAFA‐7Br and TPAFS‐7TMA, were compared with a conventional PEDOT:PSS device. The CPE‐incorporated devices exhibit more than three orders of magnitude suppression of J D and maintain nearly voltage‐independent J D up to −10 V, indicating effective suppression of electron injection from the anode. Although the external quantum efficiency at −10 V (615 nm) was moderately reduced compared with the PEDOT:PSS device, the EQE remained well above 100%, confirming preserved PM operation [28]. As a result, the shot noise‐limited specific detectivity (D shot *) reached up to 3.44 × 1014 cm·Hz0.5·W−1 at −10 V under 630 nm illumination with an active area of 0.1 cm2, primarily due to the drastic reduction in shot noise enabled by the suppressed dark current. Combined experimental and electronic analyses reveal how the electronic and chemical properties of CPE‐based EBLs regulate electron injection, hole extraction, and noise behavior. These findings provide design guidelines for achieving high‐gain, low‐noise PM OPDs and offer a general strategy for interfacial engineering in various photodetector architectures.

Results and Discussions

Dark Current Robustness Enabled by the Electron‐Selective Insulating Nature of CPEs

PM OPDs were fabricated with the ITO/EBL/photoactive layer/Al architecture, as schematically illustrated in Figure 1A. The energy‐level band diagram in the device is summarized in Figure 1B [19, 29, 30], and the chemical structures of the active and EBL materials, including the CPEs, are shown in Figure 1C. We synthesized two poly‐(triphenylamine‐co‐fluorene)‐based CPEs, TPAFA‐7Br (cationic, with alkylammonium bromide side chains) and TPAFS‐7TMA (anionic, with alkyl sulfonate side chains with tetramethylammonium (TMA+) counterions) for use as EBLs. The synthesis methods and characterizations of the two CPE materials are detailed in Supplementary Text 1. For the EBL, we deposited either (i) TPAFA‑7Br, (ii) TPAFS‑7TMA, or (iii) the benchmark polymeric hole‑transport/electron‑blocking PEDOT:PSS material by spin coating. Subsequently, a donor‐rich bulk heterojunction photoactive layer comprising poly(3‐hexylthiophene‐2,5‐diyl) (P3HT) and [6, 6]‐phenyl‐C71‐butyric acid methyl ester (PC71BM) was spin‐coated at a donor‐to‐acceptor weight ratio of 100:1. The resulting active layer had a thickness of approximately 200 nm, as verified by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Figure S5). Finally, a 100‑nm‑thick Al electrode was thermally evaporated to establish a Schottky barrier contact. Detailed fabrication parameters are provided in the Experimental Section.

Figure 1D compares the average J D of the three device variants obtained from ten independently measured devices. At −10 V, the PEDOT:PSS control exhibits 1.36 × 10−5 A·cm−2, whereas the TPAFA‐7Br and TPAFS‐7TMA devices show markedly reduced J D values 3.90 × 10−9 A·cm−2 and 3.49 × 10−9 A·cm−2, respectively corresponding to a suppression by over three orders of magnitude. Notably, unlike the PEDOT:PSS device, whose J D increases monotonically with reverse bias, the CPE‐based devices exhibit an essentially bias‐independent J D up to −10 V, indicative of a robust injection‐limited regime. The EQE spectra recorded at −10 V reverse bias are shown in Figure 1E. At the spectral maximum near 615 nm, the PEDOT:PSS control device reaches a peak EQE of 5542%, whereas devices incorporating TPAFA‐7Br and TPAFS‐7TMA achieve peak EQEs of 2328% and 3291%, respectively. Although the absolute gain is reduced upon CPE incorporation, the EQE values remain well above 100%, unequivocally confirming the preservation of the PM operating mode. Together, these results indicate that the CPE interlayers suppress reverse bias dark current escalation by blocking electron injection in the dark, while still enabling extraction of photogenerated holes under illumination to sustain the PM mode.

Additionally, to quantify the tunneling distance under FN operation, the negative‐slope region of each FN plot was fitted using Equation (1). Using a hole effective mass m * = 1.74 m 0 (9.109 × 10−31 kg) and a barrier height Ф B = 0.90 eV (the difference between the Al work function and the P3HT HOMO level), the effective tunneling thicknesses (d eff, tunneling) were extracted as 1.50, 2.28, and 2.40 nm for the PEDOT:PSS, TPAFA‐7Br, and TPAFS‐7TMA devices, respectively [32]. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the incorporation of CPEs increases both the absolute V FN, onset and d eff, tunneling, thereby in turn mitigating electron injection from the anode under large reverse bias [33].

Based on these results, the reverse bias operating mechanism in the dark can be rationalized as follows. In the PEDOT:PSS device, injection‐induced trap filling occurs more readily, leading to enhanced band bending with increasing reverse bias. In contrast, in the CPE‐based devices, suppressed carrier injection limits trap pre‐filling, thereby maintaining an injection‐limited regime and preventing bias‐induced dark current escalation throughout the high reverse bias region (Figure 2C,D).

This phenomenon likely arises from two mutually reinforcing contributions. First, the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) of CPEs is considerably shallower than that of PEDOT:PSS, leading to a much higher electron injection barrier at the ITO interface [34, 35]. As shown in Figure 1B, the LUMO of PEDOT:PSS is located at −3.5 eV, giving an electron barrier of only 1.2 eV relative to the ITO work function (−4.7 eV). By contrast, both TPAFA‐7Br and TPAFS7‐TMA exhibit LUMO energies of −2.39 and −2.43 eV, increasing the barrier to 2.31 and 2.27 eV, respectively, which are more than twice that of PEDOT:PSS. This large offset renders electron injection energetically unfavorable even under a strong reverse bias of −10 V.

Second, although the TPAFA‐7Br and TPAFS‐7TMA interlayers are ultrathin, with atomic force microscopy (AFM)‐measured thicknesses of 2.8 ± 0.3 nm and 3.8 ± 0.3 nm, respectively (Figure S6), they nevertheless function as selectively electron‐insulating layers that efficiently suppress electron injection [36]. This is consistent with prior reports that ionic side chains in CPEs can disrupt film packing and reduce electronic conductivity [37, 38]. In contrast to the ionically doped and highly conductive PEDOT:PSS, the CPEs, therefore, provide a higher effective insulation, which reinforces electron‐injection blocking at the anode interface.

To quantitatively substantiate the electron‐blocking strength of the CPE, we replaced the interlayer with atomic layer deposited Al2O3 and measured the reverse bias dark current and FN characteristics (Figure S7A). The absolute V FN, onset increased with Al2O3 thickness: −8.55 V (1 nm), −9.68 V (20 nm), and −10.22 V (30 nm), while FN tunneling was not observed within the accessible bias window for 40 nm. Notably, these results indicate that an ultrathin CPE (∼3 nm) provides electron‐blocking performance comparable to that of an ∼20‐nm‐thick Al2O3 layer. This unusually high insulating capability originates from the ionic nature of the CPE, which is not observed in conventional conjugated polymer‐based EBLs.

Crucially, however, the CPE is not merely an insulating dielectric. Unlike Al2O3 control devices—where EQE remains > 100% only for 1 nm but rapidly collapses toward zero as thickness increases (Figure S7B)—the CPE maintains a hole‐selective transport pathway while blocking electrons. This combination enables stable photomultiplication with EQE on the order of 1000% under high reverse bias, accompanied by an almost bias‐invariant dark current. However, compared with the highly conductive PEDOT:PSS interlayer, the more insulating nature of the CPEs can partially limit hole extraction under illumination, which likely accounts for the relatively lower EQE of the CPE‐based devices. Importantly, to the best of our knowledge, such a simultaneous realization of reverse bias dark current robustness and super‐unity EQE enabled by CPE‐based EBLs has not been reported for alternative interlayers in PM OPDs, underscoring the substantial performance impact of this interfacial design strategy.

Correlation Between CPE Ionic Side Chain‐Driven Energetics and Photo‐Multiplicative Gain

We showed in the preceding section that CPE EBLs suppress reverse bias dark electron injection, yielding a robust, nearly bias‐invariant dark current up to −10 V. A key remaining question is what governs the magnitude of photomultiplication under this dark current–robust operating regime. Here, we attribute the photo‐response differences to ionic side chain–induced interfacial dipoles in the CPE, which modulate the interfacial energy‐level alignment [39].

To examine the ITO/CPE/P3HT:PC71BM stack, ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) was employed to determine the work function of each relevant interface (Figure S8). Following the actual fabrication sequence, the ITO substrates were treated with O2 plasma and then coated with TPAFA‐7Br or TPAFS‐7TMA, yielding measured work functions of Φ ITO/TPAFA‐7Br = 4.28 eV and Φ ITO/TPAFS‐7TMA = 4.58 eV, respectively. This indicates that both CPEs induce interfacial dipoles at the ITO surface. We next investigated the energetics at the CPE/photoactive‐layer interface, which is more directly relevant to photocarrier extraction. The work function of the P3HT:PC71BM film deposited on ITO was measured to be Φ P3HT:PC71BM = 4.35 eV. Upon coating TPAFA‐7Br, the work function decreased substantially to Φ P3HT:PC71BM/TPAFA‐7Br = 3.80 eV. In contrast, deposition of TPAFS‐7TMA resulted in only a marginal change, yielding Φ P3HT:PC71BM/TPAFS‐7TMA = 4.29 eV.

This stark difference implies that TPAFA‐7Br induces a much stronger interfacial potential rearrangement at the CPE/photoactive‐layer interface, which is expected to result in pronounced band bending (Figure 3A). Such band bending can create an energetic barrier for holes migrating toward the ITO contact, thereby limiting hole extraction [40, 41]. Conversely, the minimal work‐function perturbation induced by TPAFS‐7TMA suggests weak band bending at the interface (Figure 3B), enabling more efficient hole extraction.

Energy level alignment diagram at equilibrium using (A) TPAFA‐7Br and (B) TPAFS‐7TMA EBLs. To probe interfacial effects under conditions representative of device fabrication, the change in Fermi level was measured after coating each CPE onto the P3HT:PC71BM blend. The Fermi level of the P3HT:PC71BM blend is illustrated within the energy levels of P3HT, assuming that band bending occurs predominantly within the P3HT levels due to the P3HT:PC71BM mass ratio (100:1).

In addition, with TPAFA‐7Br, the energy offset between the P3HT HOMO and the CPE‐modified ITO work function is 1.30 eV, whereas it decreases to 0.81 eV for TPAFS‐7TMA. This reduced discontinuity indicates smoother hole‐transport energetics from P3HT through the CPE to ITO for TPAFS‐7TMA, favoring hole transport and extraction relative to TPAFA‐7Br [42, 43].

The origin of this contrast can be attributed to differences in molecular dipole moments and their interfacial orientation. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were therefore performed to quantify the intrinsic dipole moments of the CPEs (Figure S9). TPAFA‐7Br exhibits a larger dipole moment (13.96 D) than TPAFS‐7TMA (10.99 D). This difference arises from the nature of the anionic species: the localized negative charge of Br− in TPAFA‐7Br generates a stronger molecular dipole, whereas resonance‐assisted charge delocalization within the sulfonate anion of TPAFS‐7TMA stabilizes the negative charge and reduces the overall dipole magnitude. Beyond the intrinsic dipole strength, the orientation of these dipoles at the hydrophobic P3HT:PC71BM surface is also critical. For TPAFA‐7Br, hydrophobic interactions favor adsorption of the conjugated backbone onto the photoactive layer, positioning the Br− counterions such that a strong interfacial dipole is established, leading to a pronounced reduction in work function of ITO. In contrast, although TPAFS‐7TMA is also expected to adsorb onto the photoactive layer through backbone‐driven interactions, the delocalized charge distribution of the sulfonate anion, together with the bulky TMA+ counterion, prevents the formation of a well‐defined, uniformly oriented interfacial dipole. As a result, the interfacial dipoles are more randomly oriented, producing a smaller net work‐function change.

To verify that the CPE interlayer alters hole extraction at the device level, we quantified the hole mobility (𝜇h) using a space‐charge‐limited‐current (SCLC) model (see Supplementary Text 2). The 𝜇h of the PEDOT:PSS‐based device was calculated to be 1.49 × 10−4 cm2V−1s−1. In comparison, the TPAFA‐7Br and TPAFS‐7TMA devices exhibited lower mobilities of 3.37 × 10−7 cm2V−1s−1 and 3.78 × 10−5 cm2V−1s−1, respectively. Notably, the effective mobility of TPAFA‐7Br is two orders of magnitude lower than that of TPAFS‐7TMA. This significant reduction is consistent with the unfavorable interfacial energetics discussed earlier. Although this mobility difference is substantial, the corresponding EQE values remain within the same order of magnitude because the PM gain is determined not only by hole transport but also by trap‐assisted interfacial band bending, tunneling‐assisted hole injection, and field‐assisted carrier collection under reverse bias [12, 19, 44, 45]. Therefore, the SCLC‐derived mobility serves as a complementary indicator of interfacial hole‐transport characteristics that supports the observed EQE trend, rather than as a sole determinant of the absolute EQE.

To further investigate the effect of the same interlayers on hole extraction, we fabricated organic photovoltaic (OPV) control devices incorporating PEDOT:PSS, TPAFA‐7Br, and TPAFS‐7TMA (see Supplementary Text 3). The open‐circuit voltages (V OC) of the resulting devices were 421.1, 274.1, and 394.8 mV, respectively, while the built‐in potentials (V bi) extracted from the Mott–Schottky plots were 0.56, 0.48, and 0.55 V, respectively. These trends are fully consistent with the interfacial energetics discussed above, further supporting that TPAFS‐7TMA provides a more favorable energetic landscape for hole extraction than TPAFA‐7Br.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that optimizing the electronic and chemical nature of the ionic side chains in CPEs is critical for suppressing the formation of interfacial extraction barriers. In particular, the use of ionic units to mitigate unfavorable dipolar alignment emerges as a key design strategy for facilitating hole extraction and maximizing external quantum efficiency.

Key Characteristics of PM OPDs

To evaluate the key performance metrics of the PM OPDs, devices utilizing PEDOT:PSS, TPAFA‐7Br, and TPAFS‐7TMA as the EBL were characterized (Figure 4). This discrepancy indicates that the total noise is dominated by low‐frequency fluctuation processes rather than by white noise components such as shot and thermal noise.

To identify the dominant low‐frequency noise component, we further analyzed the frequency‐dependent noise current spectral density (i n), as shown in Figure S14. Flicker noise is generally observed in the low‐frequency regime and is commonly attributed to carrier‐number and/or mobility fluctuations arising from repeated trapping and detrapping of charge carriers at localized trap states, interfacial defects, and leakage‐related transport pathways [49]. The rms flicker noise current (I rms, 1/ f ) values were estimated from the i n spectra over the flicker noise‐dominated low‐frequency regime using the same 1 Hz electrical bandwidth basis used for the I rms analysis. The extracted I rms, 1/ f values are 1.96 × 10−10, 4.71 × 10−11, and 1.16 × 10−11 A for the PEDOT:PSS, TPAFA‐7Br, and TPAFS‐7TMA devices, respectively. The detailed calculation method is provided in Supplementary Text 5. These values are comparable to the experimentally measured total I rms, and their substantial reduction in the CPE‐based devices demonstrates that replacing PEDOT:PSS with CPE EBLs effectively suppresses the dominant low‐frequency noise source.

To probe the underlying mechanism, we calculated the trap density‐of‐states (T DOS) distributions for three devices (see Supplementary Text 6 for details). The demarcation energy E ω in Figure S16 denotes the energy depth of the traps above the HOMO of the photoactive layer. Across the energy range, the CPE‐based devices showed a lower T DOS than PEDOT:PSS‐based device, and their T DOS peak occurred at shallower energy levels in measurement window—0.170 eV for TPAFA‐7Br and 0.169 eV for TPAFS‐7TMA—compared with 0.189 eV for the PEDOT:PSS‐based device. Notably, trap‐assisted injection and leakage at the interface can elevate the reverse bias dark current, and the corresponding shot noise floor increases accordingly [16, 50]. In addition, trap‐assisted parasitic leakage effectively lowers the R P thereby increasing the Johnson–Nyquist noise contribution [51]. Beyond these white noise components, the i n spectra reveal differences in low‐frequency fluctuations. In the flicker noise region, the i n follows power‐law dependence (1/f γ ), with γ values of 1.28, 1.09, and 1.03 for the PEDOT:PSS, TPAFA‐7Br, and TPAFS‐7TMA devices, respectively. The larger γ value of the PEDOT:PSS based device suggests a greater contribution from slow fluctuations associated with deeper traps and longer relaxation times [52, 53]. Consistent with their lower T DOS and shallower trap distributions, the CPE‐based devices exhibit γ values closer to unity and reduced I rms,1/ f . Therefore, the CPE EBLs suppress both white noise and trap‐related flicker noise components, accounting for the superior practical detectivity of the CPE‐based PM OPDs compared with the PEDOT:PSS reference device.

Furthermore, to characterize the dynamic response of PM OPDs, we measured the time dependent response (Figure 4E). The response speed is evaluated based on the rise time (τ r) and fall time (τ f), defined as the time required for the photocurrent to transition from 10% to 90% (rise) and from 90% to 10% (fall) of its maximum value [56, 57]. The measurements revealed that the response speed of CPE‐based devices is notably slower compared to the PM OPDs utilizing PEDOT:PSS as the interfacial layer. Specifically, the PEDOT:PSS‐based device exhibited a fast response with a τ r of 16 ms and a τ f of 155 ms. In contrast, the TPAFS‐7Br device displayed the slowest response with a τ r of 32 ms and τ f of 248 ms, while the TPAFS‐7TMA device showed a τ r of 25 ms and τ f of 220 ms.

The slightly prolonged response times in CPE‐based devices might be attributed to the reduced hole mobility, which increases the carrier transit time. However, this represents a strategic trade‐off for superior noise characteristics. While the high mobility of PEDOT:PSS enables rapid extraction, it simultaneously creates leakage pathways that compromise sensitivity. In contrast, the CPE layers effectively regulate carrier injection, yielding a significantly higher D * that outweighs the marginal decrease in response speed.

Finally, the LDR was characterized, with the detailed methodology provided in Supplementary Text 8. Resulting apparent LDR values of ≥ 15.1 dB (PEDOT:PSS), ≥ 42.0 dB (TPAFA‐7Br), and ≥ 41.3 dB (TPAFS‐7TMA) were obtained (Figure S18), reflecting the extended linear response of the CPE devices at lower optical powers owing to their robust dark current at high reverse bias.

To provide a balanced assessment of the advantages and trade‐offs associated with the different interfacial layers, a radar chart analysis comparing the normalized OPD performance metrics, including J D, EQE, I rms, NEP, D shot *, LDR, and response speed, is presented in Figure 4F. In this radar chart, each metric was normalized such that values located farther from the center indicate superior performance. The detailed normalization procedure used to construct the radar chart is described in Supplementary Text 9. As shown in Figure 4F, the CPE‐based devices exhibit clear advantages in dark current suppression, noise characteristics, detectivity, and LDR, whereas the PEDOT:PSS device retains advantages in EQE and response speed. These results highlight that the CPE‐based devices are not intended to maximize EQE or response speed alone. Rather, they are specifically advantageous under high reverse bias and weak‐light detection conditions, where suppressed dark current, reduced noise, and improved detectivity are more critical than maximizing photomultiplication gain.

Conclusion

This study establishes a mechanistic framework for engineering reverse‐bias dark current robustness in PM OPDs. We validated that CPE‐based EBLs effectively suppress anode‐side electron injection, even under high reverse bias, as evidenced by Fowler–Nordheim tunneling analysis. Furthermore, we elucidated the role of ionic side chains in modulating interfacial dipole strength and energetic barriers for hole extraction, explaining the distinct EQE trends among different CPEs. Complementary noise analysis revealed that EBL selection governs the R P and interfacial T DOS, which dictates noise characteristics.

By integrating injection control, interfacial electrostatics, and trap landscapes, we provide a unified model for dark current robustness and photomultiplication at high reverse bias. Furthermore, we utilized a rigorous measurement framework to extract NEP and D * directly under weak illumination, ensuring quantitatively reliable performance metrics that avoid the inaccuracies of high‐power extrapolations. A comprehensive radar chart analysis of normalized performance metrics further revealed that the performance enhancement provided by the CPE layers should be understood as a balanced optimization rather than the maximization of a single parameter. The concurrent achievement of dark current robustness and photomultiplication further suggests that CPE interlayers may be applicable to other photoactive layer systems, provided that electron injection is effectively suppressed and that the photoactive layer morphology and interfacial energetics are appropriately tailored to support trap‐assisted hole tunneling.

Ultimately, these insights shift the PM OPD development paradigm from a responsivity‐centric focus to a holistic engineering of high‐bias dark current, energetics, and trap states, providing a practical roadmap for next‐generation, high‐sensitivity organic optoelectronics.

Experimental Section/Methods

Materials

PEDOT:PSS(Al4083) was purchased from Ossila. Chloroform, aluminum, and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) were purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich. Poly(3‐hexylthiophene‐2,5‐diyl) (P3HT) and [6,6]‐phenyl C71 butyric acid methyl (PC71BM) were purchased from Luminescence Technology Co.

OPD Device Fabrication

Indium tin oxide (ITO)‐coated glass substrates were sequentially cleaned by ultrasonication in a detergent solution, deionized (DI) water, acetone, and isopropyl alcohol (IPA) for 20 min each. Subsequently, the substrates were dried under nitrogen atmosphere. Prior to the deposition of the subsequent layer, the substrates were treated with O2 plasma at 55 W for 90 s to enhance the surface wettability. The poly(3,4‐ethylenedioxythiophene):polystyrene sulfonate (PEDOT:PSS, Al4083) solution was filtered through a 0.45 µm PVDF syringe filter. The PEDOT:PSS solution was spin‐coated onto the plasma‐treated ITO substrates at 5000 rpm for 30 s, followed by thermal annealing at 150°C for 10 min in air. The CPE solution was prepared by dissolving CPE in methanol at a concentration of 2 mg/mL and stirring the solution for 1 h. Then, the CPE layer was spin‐coated onto the ITO‐coated glass substrate layer at 5000 rpm for 30 s and annealed at 100°C for 10 min. Two different solutions were prepared for each photoactive layer. Solution 1 consisted of poly(3‐hexylthiophene) (P3HT) dissolved in chloroform (40 mg/mL), and Solution 2 consisted of [6, 6]‐phenyl‐C71‐butyric acid methyl ester (PC71BM) dissolved in chloroform (10 mg/mL). Both solutions were stirred individually at 40°C for 6 h. The final blend was prepared by adding Solution 2 (40 µL) to Solution 1 (1 mL) to achieve a P3HT:PC71BM mass ratio of 100:1 and stirring for an additional hour at 40°C. The photoactive layer was then formed by spin‐coating the blended solution onto the EBL layer at 1500 rpm for 30 s, followed by annealing at 100°C for 10 min. Finally, a 100‐nm‐thick Al top electrode was deposited by thermal evaporation in a high‐vacuum environment (at a pressure below 1 × 10−7 Torr).

OPV Device Fabrication

The OPV devices were fabricated following an identical procedure until the formation of the EBL. The photoactive layer was prepared by dissolving P3HT and PC71BM in chlorobenzene at a mass ratio of 1:1 with a total concentration of 40 mg/mL. Prior to deposition, the solution was filtered through a 0.45 µm PTFE syringe filter. The filtered solution was then spin‐coated onto the EBL at 800 rpm for 30 s, followed by thermal annealing at 100°C for 10 min. Finally, an electron transport layer and top cathode were deposited via sequential thermal evaporation under high vacuum (pressure < 1 × 10−7 Torr). A 25 nm‐thick Ca layer was deposited, followed by a 100 nm‐thick Al layer.

Material Characterization

1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were recorded on a Bruker Avance III HD spectrometer operating at 500 MHz. The number‐average molecular weight (M n), weight‐average molecular weight (M w), and polydispersity index (Đ) of the polymers were determined by gel permeation chromatography (GPC, Agilent 1200 series) at 80°C using 1,2‐dichlorobenzene (o‐DCB) as the eluent, calibrated with polystyrene standards. Dipole moments were calculated using density functional theory (DFT) calculations with a 3‐21G basis set implemented in Gaussian 16 W, with molecular structures visualized using GaussView 6.

Thin Film Characterization

UV–vis absorption spectra were measured using a JASCO V‐630 spectrophotometer. Ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) measurements were carried out using a Nexsa system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a He I (21.22 eV) ultraviolet source. Film thicknesses were measured using a Bruker Dektak XT surface profilometer. The SEM image was measured using field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE‐SEM, S‐4800, Hitachi, Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) operated at 10 kV. AFM height profiles were measured using Park Systems XE‐100 with non‐contact mode (NCM).

Device Characterization

The EQE of PM OPDs is calculated according to the equation: EQE (λ) = J pi (λ) ∙h ∙ν/(P in (λ) ∙e), where e is the elementary charge, h is the Planck constant, and ν is the frequency of light [58, 59]. The J pi is photoinduced current density and defined as the difference between photocurrent density (J L) and J D. The J L and J D of PM OPDs were measured using a Keithley‐2400 source meter in dark or under the white light (1 mW∙cm−2). The P in is the power density of incident monochromatic light and measured using a Thorlabs S120VC power meter. The white light (1 mW∙cm−2) is obtained from a 150 W xenon lamp. The monochromatic light is obtained from a 150 W xenon lamp coupled with an Omni‐λ150 monochromator. The time dependent response of PM OPDs was measured using a Keithley‐2400 source meter under 630 nm pulse light with a light intensity of 2 mW∙cm−2. The time‐dependent root‐mean‐square noise current was measured using a Keithley 2636 B instrument. Rectangular step variable metallic neutral‐density (ND) filters (NDL‐10S‐4, Thorlabs) were used to precisely adjust the input light power. The noise current spectral density (i n) was obtained using a measurement setup consisting of a Stanford Research Systems SR570 low‐noise current preamplifier, an Agilent 33250A function generator, and a Stanford Research Systems SR830 lock‐in amplifier. The bias voltage was applied using a Keithley 2601A source meter. The instruments were interfaced through a custom‐built in‐house program, which was used to control the measurement sequence and extract the frequency‐dependent i n spectra. C‐V and C‐F curves were obtained using a Keithley 4200‐SCS instrument. Illumination at 630 nm was generated using a Ø5.6 mm laser diode (Thorlabs). The temperature and power of the laser diodes were precisely controlled to ensure stable and accurate operation within the exclusive mounts (LDM56, Thorlabs). Dedicated temperature controllers (TED200C, Thorlabs) maintained the temperature of each laser diode at a typical operating point of 25°C, while current controllers (LDC205C, Thorlabs) regulated their power. The laser power was measured using a digital optical power and energy meter (PM100D, Thorlabs Inc.) in conjunction with a Slim Photodiode Power Sensor (S130VC, Thorlabs Inc.). The J‐V characteristics of the OPV devices were measured using a Keithley 2401 instrument.
