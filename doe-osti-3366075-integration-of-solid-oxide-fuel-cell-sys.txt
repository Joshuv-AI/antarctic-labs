EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
This report presents the results of a techno-economic analysis (TEA) evaluating the economic
benefits of integrating solid oxide fuel cell (SOFC) systems and artificial intelligence (AI) data
centers. The TEA was carried out in two phases: In the first phase of this study, a scoping-level
analysis was performed to identify impactful forms of integration from the comprehensive list
of proposed options in Exhibit ES-1, which formed the basis for a more detailed TEA in the
second phase.

Amongst these options, heat integration and power electronics integration were found to
improve the data center’s energy efficiency and were included for a more detailed TEA. In
addition, the scoping analysis showed that overbuilding SOFC capacity was required to ensure
the expected 99.999% availability of data centers. Due to the importance of the reliability
requirement, this aspect was more thoroughly examined in the TEA to elucidate economic
implications. The cost of recovering water from the SOFC exhaust for use in the data center was
found to be over an order of magnitude more expensive than typical water costs and so was not
considered further. Air integration between the data hall and SOFC was also excluded from the
final analysis since performance gains were found to be negligible. The land use analysis found
that while SOFC are less power dense than some other on-site generation technologies, in a
worst-case scenario, they should only require twice the land area of a data hall. Land use is not
expected to be a prohibitive constraint for on-site SOFC adoption at data centers.

Key takeaways are as follows:
 • Due to their modularity, SOFC can meet the 99.999% availability requirement of data
 centers with minimal additional installed capacity and costs. For the current SOFC
 technology, availability requires an LCOE increase of 4.5 percent over the standard utility
 installation. In the advanced technology scenario, the LCOE increase falls to 2 percent.
 • Currently, the LCOE of an on-site SOFC installation is estimated to be more than double
 the 2024 average grid electricity price. However, further R&D (advanced technology
 scenario) could decrease it to 30 percent below the grid price. Electricity prices have
 risen since 2024 and are expected to continue rising making current day SOFC prices
 increasingly competitive. Furthermore, AI companies are willing to pay high prices for
 electricity if it means time to power is low. The economics of SOFC are further improved
 when considering that on-site SOFC eliminate the need for grid connection,
 transformers, high-voltage switch gear, and backup generators, effectively reducing the
 LCOE of each case by $14/MWh.
 • Heat integration via absorption chillers decreases data center electricity consumption at
 the tradeoff of increased water consumption. Higher SOFC exhaust temperatures are

1 INTRODUCTION
The growth of artificial intelligence (AI) has increased demand for electricity to power data
centers for the training and running of neural networks on large amounts of data. In 2023, data
center electricity consumption accounted for about 4 percent (176 TWh) of total U.S.
consumption (Shehabi, 2024). By 2030, this demand is projected to reach up to 400 TWh,
resulting in 12 percent of total electricity consumption (OnLocation, 2024).
Finding the power needed to train frontier AI models is becoming increasingly challenging. The
existing electricity grid doesn’t have sufficient transmission and generation capacity to meet the
several hundred TWh increase in demand. Gas turbines are on backorder for multiple years and
have seen costs more than double. Companies are increasingly looking to on-site generation
with either a single large natural gas combined cycle power plant or several simple cycle gas
turbines in the tens of MW range. Additionally, in certain areas, there is community resistance
to on-site generation due to environmental and noise concerns.
This economic environment provides a historic opportunity for solid oxide fuel cell (SOFC)
commercialization. SOFC adoption has been limited due to high costs. The high costs may be
less important to data centers for several reasons including:
 1. The race-like dynamics of the push to develop advanced AI have companies prioritizing
 time to power over electricity costs.
 2. Data centers are very capital intensive and electricity costs represent a relatively small
 portion of the total cost of ownership (15-20 percent).
 3. SOFC technology advances as well as increasing costs of competing technologies such as
 gas turbines have decreased the costs differentials. The SOFC technology costs are
 competitive with small modular nuclear reactors, which are being currently considered,
 while eliminating the time-intensive permitting schedules of the latter.
 4. More importantly, SOFC could potentially be less susceptible to permitting or
 community resistance delays. As seen in Exhibit 1-1, SOFC are significantly quieter
 (Bloom Energy, 2025) and have orders of magnitude fewer air pollutant emissions than
 several other on-site generating technologies (CAT, 2025), (EPA, 2015). SOFC do not
 consume water during steady state operation and are one of the most efficient
 generating technologies.

the economic benefits.

Many of the elements of the scoping-level analysis are further refined or expanded upon in the
TEA. As such, the background, assumptions, and results of those elements will be covered in
detail in later sections of this report. The purpose of this section is to provide a high-level
overview and the key takeaways from the scoping-level analysis.
Data Center Modeling
Power and water consumption for a roughly 50 MW data center were calculated using the
National Energy Technology Laboratory’s (NETL) data center energy model. The studied data
center employed air cooled information technology (IT) equipment with a water-cooled chiller
and cooling tower. The effects of air-side or water-side economizers were not considered.
SOFC Modeling
The SOFC heat and material balances were calculated in Aspen Plus® (Aspen) using an existing
distributed generation natural gas fuel cell model (Buchheit, Noring, & Iyengar, March 17, 2023).
The model was scaled from 5 MW to 50 MW to match the data center power consumption.
Heat Integration

 17

 CENTERS

SOFC contain significant amounts of waste heat in their exhaust. Absorption chillers can be used
to generate chilled water from this waste heat for cooling the data center. Results of the analysis
showed this is a promising integration option that could reduce data center electricity
consumption up to 13 percent depending on the assumptions used. For this reason, it was
included in the TEA.
Power Electronics Integration
Traditional data centers receive power from the electric grid in the form of alternating current,
(AC) but the IT equipment ultimately uses DC electricity. There are several power conversion
steps within a data center, each resulting in inefficiencies. SOFC produce DC electricity directly,
so powering data center with on-site SOFC could be more efficient. Results found electricity
savings of about eight percent.
Water Recovery and Reuse
Data centers consume large amounts of water providing cooling to the IT equipment. This
analysis investigated if the water contained in the SOFC exhaust could be condensed and used
in a data center.
The primary source of data center water consumption is from cooling tower losses. To be useful
for this purpose any recovered water vapor from the SOFC must be cooled to the assumed
cooling tower return temperature of 80°F. Cooling the SOFC exhaust to this temperature can
theoretically yield 16 percent of the data center’s water consumption.
There are three methods generally available for cooling the SOFC exhaust. Using cooling water
(generated in a cooling tower) is not feasible as more water is evaporated from the cooling
tower than can be recovered from the SOFC exhaust. Air-cooled chillers consume large amount
of electricity making them very expensive. The energy use alone, at an electricity price of
$60/MWh, results in a water price of $35/1000 gallons, significantly higher than typical water
prices ($2.30/1000 gallons) (NETL, 2022, October 14). This results in combined heat and power
applications being the only viable option. However, data centers do not have significant hot
water needs, so an additional hot water user would need to be located nearby greatly limiting
applicability.
This option was not considered for the TEA.
Air Integration
An additional opportunity for heat integration between the SOFC and data center is to take the
hot air from the data hall and use it as a source of pre-heated air on the cathode side of the
SOFC system to improve performance. A typical data center air return temperature of 80°F did
not have significant impacts on SOFC performance, increasing the SOFC’s overall efficiency by
under a percentage point. It is unlikely the capital cost could be justified by such a small
increase in performance, so the option was not considered for the TEA.
SOFC Availability
A preliminary mathematical model of SOFC availability was developed to determine how many
redundant SOFC modules are required to provide 99.999% availability to a data center. The

Land Requirements

3.1 REFERENCE AI DATA CENTER
The design basis for the reference AI data center will be discussed here. Changes made to data
center assumptions as part of different cases will be discussed separately. Power and water
consumption for the data center were calculating using NETL’s data center energy model.
The reference data center is representative of a modern data center intended for training large
AI models, sometimes referred to as an “AI factory”. The IT load can be assumed to primarily
consist of NVIDIA H100 graphical processing units (GPU), by far the most common GPU
currently in use (Emberson, 2025). This analysis is primarily concerned with the amount of
power data centers are consuming not how many computations they are producing. Thus, the
exact breakdown of the IT load between GPU, networking, and storage devices, etc. is outside
the scope of this analysis.
The data center receives electricity from the grid and has a fleet of diesel generators providing
100% capacity redundancy. The IT system is fully liquid cooled. About two-thirds of AI data
centers currently use liquid cooling and the industry is rapidly shifting in that direction as the
power densities of AI chips continue to increase (Shehabi, 2024).

3.1.1 Data Center Size and Capacity Factor
The amount of electricity used to train frontier AI models has increased exponentially over the
period from 2020 to 2025. A data center capacity of 100 MW was selected to represent the size
of training runs as of early 2025 (You, 2025).
The capacity factor (CF) is the ratio of the actual amount of electricity consumed in a year to the
total amount that could be consumed if the plant operated at full capacity during the 8760
hours of the year. For data centers, the CF mainly accounts for operation at a reduced capacity
as availability and reliability are very high. All cases are assumed to have a CF of 85 percent
based on typical reported values for AI training (Dylan Patel, 2024).

3.1.2 Power Chain
A primary function of a data center is to provide high quality and continuous power to the IT
equipment. This is achieved through a typical power chain as shown in Exhibit 3-2. A utility
supplies high-voltage AC power (345 kV) from a transmission line to the on-site substation,
which includes a high-voltage grid isolating transformer to step down to 13.8 kV for safe
distribution to the various data halls. Outside each data hall a medium-voltage transformer
steps down to 480 V for distribution within the buildings to the IT and other mechanical loads.
Data centers use diesel generators to remain operational during power outages. These
generators can take a few minutes to start up. To avoid power interruption to critical IT loads
during this period, uninterruptible power supplies (UPSs) are used to near-instantaneously
provide electricity to the IT equipment from a battery bank. UPSs also serve to filter the
incoming power from overvoltage and other distortions that could harm the IT equipment.
The output of the UPS is conditioned 480 V AC electricity that is then supplied to the power
distribution units (PDU). The PDU step down the voltage to 415 V and distribute it to the AI IT

additional conversion steps post-rack by the PSU can be improved through power electronics
integration with SOFC. In this report IT load will be broken into PSU losses and power delivered
to servers to account for this adjustment.

3.1.3 Cooling System
The second main function of a data center is to remove heat from the IT equipment. A
schematic of the heat flows in a data center is shown in Exhibit 3-4. The IT equipment is cooled
with a chilled-water distribution system within the IT racks. Water-cooled rear door heat
exchangers ensure the IT equipment contributes no heat to the data hall. While the IT loads do
not contribute to heating the air in the data hall, PDU and UPS losses and miscellaneous load
still contribute heat. Heat is removed using a computer room air handler (CRAH), which
circulates hot air over chilled water coils. The power used by the CRAH fan is also considered to
heat air within the data hall and require cooling. The heat absorbed by the chilled water is
ultimately rejected to the atmosphere in an evaporative cooling tower. A water-cooled chiller is
used to generate the cooling water. The power used by the chiller’s compressor is assumed to

4.3 CASES 1A & 2A – HEAT INTEGRATION
Cases 1A and 2A represent AI data centers powered with on-site SOFC with heat integration.
The waste heat in the SOFC exhaust is used to power absorption chillers that produce chilled
water for data center cooling.
There are no changes to the power chain between Cases 1 and 2 and Cases 1A and 2A, only the
addition of the absorption chillers’ electricity consumption to the total auxiliary load. The
updated flows of heat for these cases are shown in Exhibit 4-16. The SOFC exhaust introduces a
new heat source that must ultimately be rejected to the atmosphere by the cooling tower. A
portion of the chilled water is now produced by the absorption chiller resulting in less heat
removed by the chiller refrigeration system.

 45

4.3.1 Absorption Chillers
Absorption chillers are a type of refrigeration system that produce chilled water using heat
instead of mechanical compression. A black box approach is used to model the absorption
chillers according to Exhibit 4-17. An energy balance around the absorption chiller requires the
amount of heat lost by the SOFC exhaust and chilled water to be equal to the amount gained by
the cooling water. The ratio of the heat removed from the chilled water to the heat supplied by
the SOFC is determined by the COP. Higher COP indicate more efficient systems.

 Exhibit 4-17. Absorption Chiller

Absorption chiller performance data were obtained from World Energy’s double-effect, exhaust-
gas-driven absorption machine product data sheet (World Energy, 2012). Operating parameters
are summarized in Exhibit 4-18.

 46

 CENTERS

The exhaust of multiple SOFC is manifolded together to take advantage of the economies of
scale of larger absorption chillers. However, the number of SOFC that can be manifolded
together is limited by the travel distance and thermal expansion of the ductwork. Due to the
smaller SOFC module sizes in Case 1A the absorption chillers are smaller than the ones used in
Case 2A by about a factor of three.
The SOFC exhaust temperature is different between the two cases. In Case 2A it is calculated
from the SOFC model whereas in Case 1A it is taken from the Bloom Energy data sheet (Bloom
Energy, 2025).

4.3.3 Cost Estimates

power that must be purchased to supply a fixed IT load. In this way, absorption chillers can be

electricity saved by the absorption chillers is independent of the electricity produced by the
SOFC in Cases 1 and 2 (i.e., if a data center chooses to install on-site SOFC that does not
necessitate the installation of absorption chillers), so it is reasonable to calculate an LCOE for
the absorption chiller cases that doesn’t include any of the existing SOFC costs.

4.4 CASES 1B & 2B – POWER ELECTRONICS INTEGRATION
Cases 1B and 2B represent AI data centers powered by on-site SOFC with power electronics
integration. DC electricity is used to directly power the IT equipment instead of first converting
it to AC as in Cases 1 and 2.
The updated data center power chain is shown in Exhibit 4-24. A DC/DC converter provides
conditioned 380 V DC electricity from the SOFC modules directly to the racks. A portion of the
electricity must still be inverted to 480 V AC to power the mechanical loads. In this
configuration, the UPS serves as an automatic switch between SOFC and battery power. DC
electricity input no longer requires the rectifier and inverter components, eliminating those
power losses. In this configuration energy to the servers only goes through two conversion steps
compared to six for the reference data center.

 51

 CENTERS
 Exhibit 4-24. Data Center Power Chain for Power Electronics Integration Cases

Component efficiencies for the AC and DC configurations are shown in Exhibit 4-25. The
efficiencies for the reference data center with an AC distribution scheme were shown previously
in Exhibit 3-3. The power electronics integration cases no longer have power losses associated
with high and medium-voltage transformers, UPSs and PDU transformers. Conventional PSUs
consist of an AC/DC conversion and a DC/DC voltage step down. In the power electronics
integration cases the AC/DC conversion step is eliminated resulting in a more efficient process.
In NETL’s SOFC studies the SOFC power electronics, split between a DC/DC converter and a
DC/AC inverter, are assumed to be 97 percent efficient (NETL, 2022, April). DC/DC converters
can be up to 98 percent efficient (Nymand, 2010). With these numbers the SOFC inverter can be
calculated to be 99 percent efficient. By multiplying all the component efficiencies together, the
overall efficiency of the DC configuration can be estimated to be 95 percent compared to 89
percent for the reference data center.

4.4.2 Cost Estimates
While the performance results of power electronics integration are very promising, NETL does
not have the required data to perform a detailed cost analysis. Instead, this section will include
a brief discussion on the cost implications of power electronics integration and a sensitivity
analysis on the LCOE for these cases.
DC power electronics and distribution equipment are not currently used for data centers;
present-day capital costs are expected to be larger due to smaller manufacturing volumes. Some
equipment specific considerations are as follows:
 • The power electronics components of the UPS (rectifier and inverter) are no longer
 required as power conditioning can be performed by the SOFC’s DC/DC converter.
 Batteries are still required to smooth out power swings during AI training. Capital costs
 could drop due to the elimination of the power electronics, but this is unlikely by a large

 53

 CENTERS

 percentage as batteries make up the bulk of the costs. DC switchgear will still be
 required to control power flows between the SOFC and the batteries.
 • DC distribution can eliminate the need for 480 V/415 V within the PDU. Switch gear and
 circuit breakers must now be designed for DC.
 • Lower voltage distribution (380 V vs 480 V) could increase copper requirements, but this
 might be offset by a simpler layout (3-phase, 4-wire AC vs. 2-wire DC).
 • The PSU no longer requires an in-rack rectifier potentially reducing size and costs
We estimate the combined cost of UPS, PDU, and PSU in a 100 MW data center with AC
distribution is roughly $100 million. This estimate was made using a combination of figures from
Thunder Said Energy (Thunder Said Energy, 2025), Schneider Electric (Schneider Electric, 2025),
RSMeans (RSMeans, 2025) and Advanced Energy (Advanced Energy, 2025). With that figure in
mind, we perform a sensitivity of the LCOE for the power electronics integration cases to the
incremental cost of the DC power electronics equipment up to twice the ballpark value for the
AC equipment (Exhibit 4-27). The LCOE increases by $15/MWh per every $10 million increase in
incremental capital cost. A capital cost increase of $53 million results in electricity prices
equivalent to the grid. If power electronics integration increased capital costs by $100 million,
the LCOE would be $150/MWh.

4.6.1 Performance Results
Exhibit 4-31 shows the total data center load for each case at a constant amount of power
supplied to the servers. All cases result in reduced electricity consumption by the data center.
The on-site SOFC cases (without any integration) result in the smallest load reductions at 0.8

efficient. Case 2AB results in the largest electricity savings at 14.5 percent. The advanced SOFC
technology scenario results in larger reductions than the current technology scenario. The
higher SOFC exhaust temperature leads to larger volumes of chilled water production in the
absorption chillers.

water usage. All cases involving heat integration increase water usage because SOFC heat must
be rejected in the cooling tower after being used in the absorption chillers. Case 2A consumes
the largest amount of water, 24.7 percent more than the reference data center. The power

4.6.2 Cost Results
The LCOE breakdown for each case is shown in Exhibit 4-33. For the cases with power
electronics integration the incremental cost of DC power electronics equipment is fixed at $50
million. Most cases except for Case 2 and the power electronics integration cases are more

5 CONCLUSIONS
The results show that SOFC, due to their modular installation, can meet the 99.999% availability
requirement of data centers with minimal additional installed capacity and costs. For the
current SOFC technology, 8 percent additional capacity is required, increasing the LCOE by 4.5
percent. In the advanced technology scenario, 4 percent additional capacity is required
increasing the LCOE by 2 percent.
Currently, the LCOE of an on-site SOFC installation is estimated to be more than double the
2024 average grid electricity price. However, further R&D (advanced technology scenario) could
decrease it to 30 percent below the grid price. Electricity prices have risen since 2024 and are
expected to continue rising, making present day SOFC prices increasingly competitive.
Furthermore, AI companies are willing to pay high prices for electricity if it means time to power
is low. The economics of SOFC are further improved when considering on-site SOFC eliminate
the need for grid connection, transformers, high-voltage switch gear, and backup generators,
effectively reducing the LCOE of each case by $14/MWh.
Heat integration via absorption chillers decreases electricity consumption at the tradeoff of
increased water consumption (Case 1A: 12.7 percent, Case 2A: 26 percent). Higher SOFC
exhaust temperatures are important for achieving larger electricity savings; Case 2A reduces
electricity consumption by 6.3 percent compared to only 3.4 percent for Case 1A. More
importantly, larger SOFC modules are required to utilize larger absorption chillers with lower
capital costs; this effect is primarily responsible for the 44 percent reduction in LCOE from Case
1A to Case 2A.
The benefits of power electronics integration are independent of the SOFC technology assumed.
Power electronics integration with SOFC DC electricity can reduce electricity consumption by 9
percent and reduce water consumption by 6.4 percent. Capital costs are uncertain, but LCOE
could be competitive with grid electricity prices, assuming a DC power electronics system costs
under 60 percent more than an AC system.
For Cases 1AB and 2AB the performance benefits of the integration option are additive and do
not compound. In the current SOFC technology scenario all the integration cases have lower
LCOE than the on-site SOFC case, which suggests they should be pursued before building
additional SOFC capacity. However, in the future technology scenario, the integration options
are more expensive than the electricity produced by SOFC. In these scenarios, energy efficiency
or environmental concerns would need to drive the decisions for the integration options.

5.1 FUTURE WORK
To better understand the economics of the power electronics integration cases, a more detailed
data center capital cost model needs to be developed. Equipment costs could be determined by
requesting quotes from vendors, purchasing access to an online database such as RSMeans, or
identifying data center design companies to develop an estimate.
Based on the positive results for this study, the next step might be to consider a real-world
demonstration of the integration options. A small-scale demonstration project can be designed

and budgeted based on a single rack cooled by an absorption chiller and powered by DC-
integrated SOFC.
This study uses assumptions for the financial parameters from the power generation industry.
Estimates for the debt-to-equity ratio, desired return on equity and interest rates representative
of the modern data center industry could be developed for more accurate LCOE calculations.
The effects of dynamic load profiles could be considered. Diurnal and seasonal temperature
fluctuations change the chiller and cooling tower performance. If the data center is used for AI
inference, the IT load profile could change throughout the day. It is important to capture these
fluctuations as the efficiency of the chillers and power electronics change based on the
percentage of full load. The interplay between partial load operation and redundancy
requirements is also important to understand.
