Nurses’ experiences and challenges in postnatal stabilisation of newborns: a phenomenological study
Authors: Ridvan Akdogan, Seda Nur Yürük Yucakuş
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPostnatal stabilisation of newborns is a critical phase that directly influences neonatal survival and long-term outcomes. Neonatal nurses play a central role in this process, yet limited research has explored their lived experiences, particularly regarding clinical responsibility, organisational constraints, and emotional demands. This study aimed to explore nurses’ lived experiences of stabilising newborns during the immediate postnatal period and to identify the clinical, organisational, relational, and emotional dimensions shaping this practice.MethodsA descriptive phenomenological study was conducted using in-depth, semi-structured interviews with 17 neonatal nurses working in delivery room and operating theatre units of a tertiary hospital. Data were collected between August and December 2025. Interviews were analysed using Colaizzi’s phenomenological analysis method to identify themes reflecting nurses’ lived experiences.ResultsFive interrelated themes emerged: clinical competence and standardisation; structural and hierarchical barriers; relational dynamics and safety culture; emotional labour and moral distress; and strategic defence and professional development. Nurses experienced postnatal stabilisation as a high-stakes threshold requiring rapid, coordinated, and protocol-based actions to ensure newborn survival. While clinical protocols enhanced confidence and supported decision-making, limited authority, environmental constraints, and communication gaps generated ethical tension and professional frustration. Emotional involvement, empathic identification with newborns, and fear of irreversible error contributed to psychological burden, whereas documentation practices and continuous education were perceived as protective strategies.ConclusionsPostnatal stabilisation is experienced by nurses as a multidimensional practice rather than a purely technical intervention. Clinical responsibility, organisational context, teamwork dynamics, and emotional labour collectively shape this process. The findings suggest that organisational support, effective communication, and ongoing professional development may help address challenges experienced during postnatal stabilisation and inform improvements in neonatal nursing practice.

Background

Postnatal stabilisation of newborns represents one of the most critical phases of neonatal care, directly influencing neonatal survival and long-term health outcomes [1, 2]. Globally, approximately 15 million infants are born prematurely each year, and nearly one million neonatal deaths are attributed to complications related to prematurity [1]. The immediate period following birth is widely recognised as a critical period for preventing morbidity and mortality, during which timely, coordinated, and effective interventions are essential [2, 3]. Particularly for preterm and high-risk newborns, optimal stabilisation practices are fundamental to maintaining vital functions, preventing neurodevelopmental complications, and improving overall outcomes [4, 5].

Within this critical process, nurses play a central role as frontline providers of postnatal stabilisation. Neonatal nurses are responsible for rapid assessment, thermoregulation, airway management, monitoring vital signs, initiating life-saving interventions, and documenting clinical data, often under intense time pressure. Beyond technical responsibilities, nurses coordinate interdisciplinary care and support families during the earliest moments of neonatal life. However, their experiences during postnatal stabilisation are shaped not only by clinical competence but also by working conditions [6], organisational structures [6], and emotional resilience [7].

Existing literature identifies multiple challenges faced by neonatal nurses, including heavy workload, insufficient staffing, emotional stress, ethical dilemmas, and difficulties in interprofessional and family communication [8–10]. Deficits in knowledge or training may compromise pain management and developmental care, while high-acuity environments and sustained stress negatively affect nurses’ psychological well-being and quality of care [7]. Furthermore, empathy, as a fundamental component of nursing care that facilitates therapeutic connection and patient-centred practice, although enhances caregiving, may also expose nurses to emotional labour and an increased risk of burnout [11]. Ethical dilemmas arising from conflicts between responsibility and authority have been shown to generate moral distress among nurses working in intensive and neonatal care settings [12].

From a systems perspective, postnatal stabilisation is influenced not only by individual competence but also by organisational and environmental factors. Physical space design, availability of equipment, and team communication patterns directly affect workflow efficiency and response time during emergencies [13]. Patient safety literature emphasises that outcomes in high-risk clinical settings are shaped by organisational structures, safety culture, and human factors embedded within care systems as much as by individual clinical expertise [14].

Despite the high clinical stakes of postnatal stabilisation, existing research has largely focused on technical performance, protocol adherence, and clinical outcomes [3]. The organisational, ethical, relational, and emotional dimensions of nurses’ experiences during this process remain insufficiently explored. In particular, the early postnatal period frequently places nurses in situations where they carry substantial clinical responsibility while possessing limited formal decision-making authority. This tension between responsibility and authority, compounded by hierarchical dynamics, communication gaps, and environmental constraints, represents a critical yet underexamined area in neonatal nursing practice.

Emerging evidence suggests that simulation-based and continuous education programmes can enhance nurses’ clinical confidence, decision-making capacity, and patient safety in neonatal care [15]. However, without a nuanced understanding of nurses’ lived experiences during postnatal stabilisation, efforts to improve education, organisational structures, and policy may remain incomplete, as such efforts risk failing to address the practical, emotional, and contextual realities that shape clinical practice and limit the effectiveness and sustainability of proposed improvements.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to explore nurses’ lived experiences and challenges during the postnatal stabilisation of newborns using a phenomenological approach. By examining this process through nurses’ perspectives, the study seeks to illuminate the clinical, organisational, relational, and emotional dimensions of postnatal stabilisation and to contribute evidence that may inform nursing practice, education, and organisational policy in neonatal care settings.

Methods

Study design

The aim of this study was to explore nurses’ lived experiences and challenges during postnatal stabilisation of newborns. This study employed a descriptive phenomenological design grounded in Husserl’s philosophy [16], with data analysed using Colaizzi’s [17] method. Descriptive phenomenology was chosen to elucidate the essence of nurses’ lived experiences of postnatal newborn stabilisation, focusing on describing the phenomenon as it is consciously experienced rather than interpreting it [18]. In line with Husserlian phenomenology, the researchers aimed to approach participants’ experiences as they were lived, while systematically engaging in bracketing to minimise the influence of prior professional knowledge and assumptions. Bracketing (epoché) refers to the process of suspending the researcher’s preconceptions, clinical experiences, and assumptions in order to describe the phenomenon as it is experienced by participants. This approach is fundamental in descriptive phenomenology and allows the essence of the lived experience to emerge more clearly [16, 18].

The study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) guidelines to ensure transparency and methodological rigour.

Participants and setting

The study was conducted between August 2025 and December 2025 at Van Training and Research Hospital, a tertiary-care referral hospital with a neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) providing advanced neonatal care for preterm and high-risk newborns.

The study focused specifically on nurses working in the delivery room and operating theatre who are responsible for the immediate postnatal stabilisation of newborns at birth, prior to transfer to the NICU when required. A total of 28 nurses were employed in these units, all of whom had completed Neonatal Resuscitation Program (NRP) training and were qualified registered nurses.

During neonatal stabilisation, nurses were responsible for the initial assessment and management of newborns, including thermoregulation, airway positioning and suctioning, stimulation, positive pressure ventilation, monitoring vital signs, oxygen support, and preparation for advanced interventions when necessary. Nurses initiated immediate stabilisation procedures at birth and worked collaboratively with the neonatal team. A neonatologist was available on a 24-hour on-call basis and attended high-risk deliveries or situations requiring advanced neonatal intervention. Following initial stabilisation, newborns requiring ongoing intensive care were transferred to the NICU.

Data collection

Sociodemographic data

Data were collected using a Personal Information Form consisting of 8 questions covering participants’ sociodemographic and professional characteristics. These included age, gender, marital status, educational level, years of neonatal nursing experience, weekly working hours, Neonatal Resuscitation Program (NRP) training status, and unit of employment.

Interviews

Data were collected through individual, in-depth phenomenological interviews. In line with descriptive phenomenology, each interview began with a single, open-ended question inviting participants to describe their lived experience of postnatal newborn stabilisation in depth. The primary opening question was:Please describe, in as much detail as possible, your experience of stabilising a newborn during the immediate postnatal period.

Please describe, in as much detail as possible, your experience of stabilising a newborn during the immediate postnatal period.

Following this initial narrative, participants were encouraged to elaborate freely on aspects of their experience that they considered meaningful. Open-ended prompts and clarifying probes (e.g., “Can you tell me more about that?”, “How did that situation affect you?”) were used only when necessary to support narrative depth and clarification, without directing participants’ accounts.

In accordance with Husserlian phenomenology, the researcher consciously bracketed prior clinical knowledge and existing literature throughout the interview process to allow the phenomenon to emerge from participants’ descriptions. The interview approach and prompts were reviewed by three faculty members specialising in paediatric nursing to ensure clarity and appropriateness for phenomenological inquiry (Table 1).

Data collection procedure

Of the total 28 eligible nurses employed in the units, 17 who met the inclusion criteria and initially volunteered were included in the study. Data collection continued until data saturation was achieved. Saturation was considered to have occurred when no new meanings, themes, or experiential insights emerged from successive interviews and the existing thematic structure became sufficiently rich and repetitive. Therefore, the remaining 11 eligible nurses were not approached for participation.

Data were collected through individual in-depth interviews. Prior to the main data collection, two preliminary interviews were conducted with individuals outside the study sample to familiarise the researcher with the interview process and to refine the flow of the opening question.

Participants were contacted in advance to schedule interview dates. One-on-one interviews were conducted in a quiet and private meeting room within the hospital or in locations preferred by participants. An audio-recording device was used with participants’ written and verbal consent. Field notes were taken when deemed necessary. Each interview lasted approximately 30–60 min and was conducted by a single researcher. Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim by the researcher on the same day as each interview to preserve contextual detail and enhance familiarity with the data.

All interviews were conducted by a single researcher with 7 years of clinical experience in neonatal nursing and formal training in qualitative research methods. The interviewer was supervised throughout the research process by an experienced qualitative researcher to ensure methodological rigor and consistency in data collection.

Data analysis and scientific rigour

Data were analysed using Colaizzi’s [17] seven-step analysis method. Interview recordings were transcribed verbatim and read repeatedly to obtain a general understanding. Significant statements related to the phenomenon were identified, and formulated meanings were derived. These meanings were clustered into subthemes and themes, which were synthesised to define the fundamental structure of the phenomenon. These meanings were clustered into subthemes and themes, which were synthesised to describe the fundamental structure of the phenomenon. In phenomenological research, this process refers to the abstraction of formulated meanings into higher-order thematic structures that reflect the essence of lived experience [18]. Member checking was conducted by sharing summaries of the preliminary themes and interpretations with all participants to ensure that the findings accurately reflected their experiences. Member checking refers to the process in which participants review and validate the researchers’ interpretations and findings to enhance the credibility and accuracy of qualitative data [19].

To enhance the rigour of the study, the criteria of trustworthiness proposed by Lincoln and Guba [19] were applied. In this framework, credibility refers to confidence in the truth of the findings and was ensured through data saturation, participant validation (member checking), and expert review. Transferability refers to the extent to which findings can be applied to other contexts and was supported through thick description of the research setting and procedures. Dependability was strengthened by consistent data collection conducted by a single researcher and independent review of the process of identifying significant statements and formulating meanings. Confirmability was achieved through systematic bracketing and maintenance of an audit trail. No qualitative data analysis software was used during the transcription or thematic synthesis phases; all data analysis steps were executed manually to ensure close immersion in the lived experiences of the participants.

Researcher reflexivity

Reflexivity was integral to the phenomenological process. The primary researcher is a neonatal nurse with clinical experience in delivery room and neonatal care settings, however, she was not currently employed in the institution at the time of data collection and did not have any ongoing professional or hierarchical relationship with the participants. The relationship between the researcher and participants was collegial and non-hierarchical; participants were informed that participation or non-participation would not affect their professional roles, and confidentiality was strictly maintained. This clinical background facilitated rapport with participants and a nuanced understanding of clinical workflows during postnatal stabilisation, while also creating a potential risk of preconceptions. To address this, ongoing bracketing was practiced throughout data collection and analysis to minimise the influence of prior assumptions. Reflexive notes were recorded before and after interviews to critically examine the researcher’s thoughts, feelings, and potential biases. In addition, analytic decisions were regularly discussed with an experienced qualitative researcher who was not involved in data collection to enhance reflexive rigor and ensure analytical neutrality.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Van Yüzüncü Yıl University (Decision No: 2025/09–12, Date: 19 September 2025) and from the relevant institutional authority. Participants were informed about the purpose of the study, confidentiality, and voluntary participation, and written informed consent was obtained. To protect anonymity, participants were assigned identification codes (e.g., Participant 1, Participant 2). The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Characteristics of participants

A total of 17 nurses involved in the postnatal stabilisation process of newborns participated in this study. Participants ranged in age from 24 to 42 years, and the majority were female. Most participants were married. The majority also held a bachelor’s degree, while a small number had either Vocational Health High School or postgraduate education. Weekly working hours ranged from 40 to 48 h, although a limited number of participants reported longer working schedules.

All participants had completed Neonatal Resuscitation Program (NRP) training, indicating a homogeneous sample in terms of baseline clinical competence and adherence to standardised care practices. Neonatal intensive care experience ranged from 3 months to 17 years. This wide range enabled the exploration of postnatal stabilisation experiences across different levels of professional seniority and clinical exposure (see Table 2).

Essence of nurses’ lived experiences

The lived experience of neonatal nurses during the postnatal stabilisation process unfolds as a complex, high-stakes practice shaped by urgency, responsibility, and emotional intensity. Nurses described the first minutes after birth as a decisive threshold between life and vulnerability, requiring rapid, standardised, and coordinated action to secure newborn survival. Although clinical competence and protocol-based interventions provided a structured foundation for care, nurses’ experiences were profoundly influenced by organisational constraints, hierarchical decision-making structures, and the physical environment.

Throughout this process, nurses carried substantial clinical responsibility while often operating within limited formal authority, creating persistent ethical tension and moral distress. Team cohesion, shared responsibility, and effective communication emerged as critical protective factors that mitigated stress and enhanced patient safety. Simultaneously, nurses experienced deep emotional labour, driven by empathic identification with newborns and fear of making irreversible errors.

To cope with these pressures, nurses developed strategic defence mechanisms, including self-protective documentation practices and a strong reliance on continuous education to sustain professional confidence. Collectively, these experiences reveal postnatal stabilisation not merely as a technical intervention, but as a multidimensional nursing practice embedded within clinical, organisational, relational, and emotional contexts. This structure of experience is reflected in five interrelated themes: clinical competence and the pursuit of standardisation; structural and hierarchical barriers; relational dynamics and safety culture; emotional labour and moral distress; and strategic defence and professional development (Table 3).

Theme 1: Clinical competence and the pursuit of standardisation

Nurses experienced clinical competence as the capacity to prioritise and coordinate interventions within a structured, protocol-based framework, enabling systematic action under the intense time pressure of the immediate postnatal period.

Management of the golden minutes: initiating and maintaining vital functions

Nurses described the immediate postnatal period as a critical window requiring rapid and prioritised interventions to sustain newborn life. Maintaining warmth, airway patency, stimulation, and early physiological assessment were perceived as central to successful stabilisation.

One nurse emphasised early assessment, noting that,

These accounts illustrate how managing the golden minutes was experienced as a structured and highly prioritised clinical process requiring speed, focus, and systematic clinical judgement.

Professional safety protocols: error prevention and identification

Standardised protocols were described as essential safety mechanisms that guided nurses’ actions under pressure. Adherence to Neonatal Resuscitation Program (NRP) protocols reduced uncertainty and supported systematic care delivery.

Discussion

This phenomenological study reveals that postnatal stabilisation is not a purely technical intervention but a complex, multidimensional nursing practice shaped by clinical judgement, ethical responsibility, team dynamics, and emotional labour. Nurses navigate high-stakes decisions during the earliest and most critical moments of neonatal life while operating within a persistent tension between responsibility and authority — a tension that systemic and hierarchical constraints make difficult to resolve.

Clinical competence and standardisation during the “golden minutes”

Nurses consistently identified the immediate postnatal period as decisive for neonatal survival, consistent with international resuscitation and stabilisation guidelines emphasising rapid assessment, thermoregulation, airway management, and circulatory support [3, 20]. NRP algorithms were described not only as technical tools but as cognitive safety nets that support decision-making under pressure — a function well documented in acute clinical environments [21, 22]. However, standardisation alone proved insufficient when not supported by decision-making authority, effective team coordination, and adequate physical conditions.

Systemic and hierarchical barriers: the tension between responsibility and authority

One important finding of this study was the perceived mismatch between nurses’ clinical responsibilities and their limited formal decision-making authority. Nurses routinely assumed responsibility for stabilisation while remaining dependent on physician availability, placing them in an ethical and professional dilemma that echoes prior research on the gap between “actual responsibility” and “formal authority” in neonatal intensive care [23]. Human factors research consistently shows that unclear authority structures impair team performance and compromise patient safety [14]. Recognising and legitimising nurses’ clinical judgement within defined legal and organisational frameworks could substantially strengthen the safety of the stabilisation process. Our findings suggest that participants perceived greater recognition of nurses’ clinical judgement as potentially beneficial for supporting the stabilisation process. However, the effectiveness of organisational or legal changes was beyond the scope of this study.

Physical environment and team dynamics

The physical separation between delivery rooms and operating theatres complicated equipment access and team coordination, contributing to perceived loss of critical time. This aligns with evidence that spatial design in neonatal units shapes workflow efficiency and response times [13, 24]. Team cohesion emerged as a key protective factor, mitigating both clinical risk and emotional burden, while communication breakdowns — particularly the absence of prenatal and perinatal information prior to newborn arrival — forced nurses to act under uncertainty and rapidly degraded safety culture [25]. These findings suggest that effective interdisciplinary communication plays an important role in supporting safe postnatal stabilisation.

Emotional labour and moral distress

Nurses frequently described empathetic identification with newborns, which simultaneously strengthened motivation and intensified emotional burden. Fear of error, perceived inadequacy, and unresolved ethical dilemmas contributed to moral distress — a recognised precursor to burnout in intensive care nursing [12, 26]. Professional satisfaction following successful stabilisation offered temporary relief, but without systemic support this balance is unlikely to be sustained.

Professional development and defence strategies

Nurses’ reliance on documentation as self-protection reflects individual coping strategies that emerge when organisational structures fail to provide adequate safeguards. Participants strongly emphasised the value of continuous, practice-based education; consistent with existing evidence, scenario-focused and simulation-based training may support clinical confidence and reduce cognitive load in high-risk situations [15, 27], though this study did not examine whether such training directly improves stabilisation outcomes. The findings suggest that reducing the gap between theoretical knowledge and clinical practice—particularly in high-risk neonatal situations [8]—may represent an important area for future educational initiatives.

Potential implications for nursing practice and international relevance

The findings of this study suggest several potential implications at two levels: transferable considerations applicable across diverse healthcare systems and context-specific considerations grounded in the regulatory and organisational characteristics of the study setting.

At a transferable level, the tension between clinical responsibility and limited decision-making authority identified in this study reflects a widely documented challenge in neonatal nursing internationally [23]. Our findings suggest that clearer role definitions may help reduce role ambiguity during time-critical stabilisation; however, the most appropriate mechanisms for achieving this are likely to depend on local regulatory and organisational contexts. Communication challenges identified in this study, including handover gaps and insufficient prenatal information transfer, similarly reflect universal interprofessional difficulties. The use of structured communication tools, such as ISBAR (Identification–Situation–Background–Assessment–Recommendation), may represent one possible approach to improving information exchange during postnatal stabilisation. The emotional burden of neonatal stabilisation—encompassing moral distress, empathic identification, and fear of error—is likewise a cross-cultural concern. Institutional support mechanisms, including peer debriefing and reflective practice, may help nurses manage the emotional demands described by participants.

At a context-specific level, participants described limited decision-making authority and the physical separation of clinical areas as important challenges during postnatal stabilisation. These findings may warrant consideration by healthcare organisations when evaluating local neonatal care processes. However, this study did not evaluate the effectiveness of specific organisational, legal, or structural interventions. Therefore, suggestions regarding nurse-led stabilisation protocols, decision-making authority, or environmental redesign should be interpreted as potential implications derived from participants’ experiences rather than evidence-based recommendations.

Overall, rather than introducing entirely new challenges, the findings deepen understanding of how well-recognised clinical, organisational, and emotional issues intersect within nurses’ lived experiences during postnatal stabilisation. This phenomenological perspective provides a richer understanding of how these interconnected factors shape nurses’ experiences in the immediate postnatal period and may help inform future research and context-sensitive improvements in neonatal care.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several notable strengths. The descriptive phenomenological design enabled an in-depth exploration of nurses’ lived experiences during postnatal stabilisation, offering rich qualitative insights into an underexplored area of neonatal nursing. The use of Colaizzi’s seven-step method provided a rigorous and systematic analytical framework. Scientific rigour was strengthened through data saturation, member checking with all 17 participants, expert review of interview guides, and the application of Lincoln and Guba’s trustworthiness criteria. The homogeneity of the sample in terms of NRP training ensured a comparable baseline of clinical competence, while the wide range of neonatal experience (3 months to 17 years) enabled exploration of perspectives across different levels of professional seniority. In addition, the study was conducted and reported in accordance with the SRQR guidelines, enhancing methodological transparency. However, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings.

First, the study was conducted at a single tertiary-care hospital in Turkey, within a healthcare system characterised by hierarchical decision-making structures and specific organisational norms. These contextual features may limit the transferability of the findings to healthcare systems in other countries where nursing autonomy, staffing models, and neonatal care pathways differ.

Second, although detailed contextual descriptions were provided to support transferability, the experiences reported by participants may reflect country-specific regulatory, cultural, and institutional factors influencing neonatal nursing practice.

Third, data collection relied on individual interviews, which may be influenced by recall bias or social desirability. Additionally, the perspectives of other healthcare professionals involved in postnatal stabilisation were not included, despite the inherently interdisciplinary nature of neonatal care.

Despite these limitations, the findings may be transferable to other healthcare settings characterised by high-acuity neonatal care, hierarchical clinical structures, and constrained nursing autonomy. Future cross-cultural research involving diverse healthcare professionals and international nursing models—particularly systems in which Neonatal Nurse Practitioners have broader clinical autonomy—is needed to further refine and contextualise these findings.

Conclusion

This study provides an in-depth phenomenological understanding of nurses’ experiences of postnatal stabilisation, illustrating how clinical judgement, organisational context, team relationships, and emotional labour interact to shape this multidimensional nursing practice. Nurses navigate complex decision-making processes during a critical window between life and death, guided by standard protocols but constrained by organisational and systemic factors. The findings suggest that organisational support, effective communication, and opportunities for continuing professional development may help address the challenges nurses experience during postnatal stabilisation. Further research across different healthcare settings is warranted to explore how these findings may inform practice and organisational improvement.
