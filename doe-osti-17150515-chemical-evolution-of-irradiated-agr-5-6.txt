 ABSTRACT

In this work, single-particle neutron diffraction was used to evaluate how the uranium carbide–bearing
phases in AGR-5/6/7 UCO tristructural-isotropic fuel evolve during irradiation. The measurements show
that instead of consumption of the UC2−x-type phase and conversion of the kernel to UO2 and free carbon,
irradiation-induced chemical evolution occurs, with UC2−x decreasing and an oxygen-bearing rocksalt
U(C,O) phase becoming increasingly prevalent with burnup. The results distinguish between uranium
retained in crystalline carbide + oxycarbide phases and carbon retained in uranium carbide bonding
environments. This distinction is important for fuel specification optimization because uranium carbide is
included in UCO kernels to buffer oxygen and suppress CO formation. The diffraction results provide an
experimental basis for estimating how much of that uranium carbide reservoir is consumed during
irradiation, which may assist in refining kernel composition specifications to provide sufficient CO
suppression without maintaining unnecessary excess oxygen-gettering capacity in the kernel.

 1. INTRODUCTION

Tristructural isotropic (TRISO) coated particle fuel consists of a spherical fuel kernel surrounded by a
porous carbon buffer, inner pyrolytic carbon (IPyC), silicon carbide (SiC), and outer pyrolytic carbon
(OPyC). The SiC layer is the primary structural layer, pressure vessel, and barrier to fission products
released from the kernel. The PyC layers provide mechanical support, fission-gas accommodation, and
protection of the SiC layer. The kernel chemistry directly influences the oxygen potential within the
particle and, consequently, fission product chemistry, CO generation, and interactions among the kernel,
buffer, and SiC coating. Uranium carbide uranium oxide (UCO) kernels contain a heterogeneous mixture
of uranium oxide and uranium carbide phases. As-fabricated AGR kernels contain a heterogeneous
mixture of UO2, UC, and UC2−x; monocarbide is often present as a significant carbide phase before
coating. During TRISO coating and subsequent heat treatment, much of the UC converts to UC2−x through
reaction with the surrounding carbonaceous layers. The carbide component is designed to provide reduced
uranium that can be oxidized as fissioning UO2 releases oxygen:

                                          UC! + 2O → UO" + C

This reaction suppresses CO generation by consuming oxygen before it reacts with carbon. If the initial
uranium carbide inventory is too low, then excess oxygen may oxidize carbon to CO, increasing internal
pressure and potentially promoting kernel migration or SiC oxidation. If the initial carbide inventory is
too high, then the kernel oxygen potential may be too low to oxidize selected fission products, particularly
rare earth species, increasing the likelihood of fission product mobility and SiC interaction. The UCO
design is therefore an optimization problem. The fuel must contain enough carbide to suppress CO
generation throughout irradiation but not so much carbide that fission product oxidation and retention are
unnecessarily compromised.

Homan et al. (1977) developed one of the earliest practical frameworks for UO2–UC2 fuel chemistry
where the oxygen potential of fuel and fission product oxide/carbide equilibria was considered. This
estimate was based on a thermodynamic interpretation of measured gas release from irradiated oxide fuel
particles. Experimental oxygen-potential values for irradiated high-temperature gas-cooled reactor
(HTGR) oxide fuels had been obtained by breaking individual particles at temperature and measuring the
moles of CO released; equivalent in-particle PCO values were then calculated and converted to oxygen
potentials using the C–CO–O2 equilibrium (Clough et al. 1972; Bildstein and Strigl 1972; Lindemer and
Pearson 1975; Homan et al. 1977). These oxygen-potential data were used together with oxide–carbide
equilibrium calculations for uranium and major fission products to estimate how much UC2 was needed to
buffer oxygen released during fission.

 1

The mass-balance calculation assumed 75% fissions per initial metal atom (FIMA) and thermal fission
product yields for 235U (Homan et al. 1977). Homan et al. calculated that each 235UO2 fission produced
fission product oxides that consumed only about 1.62 oxygen atoms, leaving approximately 0.38 oxygen
atoms per fission available to react with unfissioned UC2 to form UO2. They concluded that UO2–UC2
kernels could provide a stoichiometric range that avoids both high CO pressure and excessive rare earth
carbide mobility and potential release from intact particles. The Homan work reports that weak acid resin
kernels containing less than about 15% UC2 would develop excessive CO overpressure, whereas kernels
containing greater than about 70% UC2 would contain insufficient oxygen to oxidize rare earth fission
products at the maximum burnup considered for that work, 75% FIMA.

The Homan calculation is important for the present work because it was later reflected in the historical
General Atomics design-basis treatment of carbide depletion in UCO kernels (Scheffel and Tang 1989).
Based on the Homan et al. (1977) calculations, the chemical evolution of a UO2–UC2 kernel as burnup
proceeds was predicted under the oxygen potential fixed by coexistence of UO2, UC2, carbon, and fission
products. The Homan-derived GA curve is best treated as a legacy stoichiometric estimate for burnup-
dependent carbide depletion, whereas later calculations by McMurray et al. (2017) and Poschmann et al.
(2025) are lower-bound design estimates for the minimum uranium carbide needed to suppress CO
pressure.

For example, Table I in Homan et al. (1977) gives the cumulative initial UC2 required at 75% FIMA for
sequential chemical outcomes in the UO2–UC2 kernel. The cumulative value through SrC2 formation,
43.6% UC2 at 75% FIMA, represents the stoichiometric carbide demand associated with CO suppression
and the predicted oxide/carbide speciation of barium, zirconium, europium, and strontium under the UO2–
UC2 oxygen-potential constraint. This basis gives an approximate UC2 depletion rate of 0.58 percentage
points per percent 235U burnup. After conversion from the 235U burnup basis used by Homan et al. to a
total-metal FIMA basis, the corresponding historical Homan/GA-style carbide-depletion line is
approximately 0.62 percentage points UC2 per percent FIMA. This result is consistent with the UC2 vs.
burnup design-basis curve reported by Scheffel and Tang (1989).

McMurray et al. revisited the minimum required Uranium carbide concentration using depletion
calculations and an oxygen-balance equation derived from the Ellingham diagram (McMurray et al.
2017). The McMurray calculation accounts for plutonium, neptunium, rare earths, strontium, barium, and
zirconium, and concluded that 5.1–5.5 mol % uranium carbide is required to keep CO pressure below
1 atm for 15.5% enriched UCO fuel irradiated to 16.1% FIMA. This is a lower-bound design calculation,
not a direct prediction of residual crystalline carbide. The calculation gives substantial oxygen-gettering
credit to fission and transmutation products, reducing the amount of oxygen that must be buffered by
uranium carbide.

Poschmann et al. (2025) extended the McMurray-type approach using detailed equilibrium
thermodynamic calculations with Thermo-Calc and TAF-ID. This calculation explicitly included 26
elements and represented 99.9206 mol % of the irradiated fuel inventory at 21.3% FIMA. A lower
uranium carbide bound was defined based on limiting CO pressure, and an upper uranium carbide bound
was defined based on maintaining oxidation of radionuclide fission and transmutation products. The
calculated lower bound increased from 4.16% uranium carbide at 12.4% FIMA to 7.44% uranium carbide
at 21.3% FIMA, and the upper bound decreased from 45.63% to 35.46% over the same burnup range.
Poschmann et al. concluded that AGR-2 and modular HTGR-350 reference fuel compositions are within
the calculated safe range. This work therefore supports the conclusion that the minimum uranium carbide
needed to suppress CO is substantially lower than current AGR carbide specification targets. However,
the calculation remains an equilibrium thermodynamic bounding analysis. It does not directly measure
how much crystalline carbide remains in real irradiated particles.

 2

The US DOE-NE Advanced Gas Reactor Fuel Development and Qualification program has established
the modern standard for TRISO fuel through fabrication campaigns that progressed from laboratory-scale
development to industrial-scale fabrication and integral irradiation testing in the Advanced Test Reactor
at Idaho National Laboratory. Each campaign used a fuel specification informed by prior international
HTGR fuel development and historical UCO design guidance (Demkowicz et al. 2023). AGR-5/6/7 was
the final irradiation experiment in the program and used fuel fabricated entirely at engineering scale
(Demkowicz et al. 2025). The AGR-5/6/7 UCO kernel specification provides a broad allowable carbide
composition range (Marshall 2017). This range provides substantial margin relative to modern lower-
bound calculations for CO suppression, but that margin is based primarily on thermochemical models,
historical guidance, and bulk fuel characterization.

The amount and chemical form of uranium carbide retained during irradiation remain important
uncertainties in UCO fuel performance. Historical and modern thermochemical treatments have been used
to estimate the minimum carbide content needed to suppress CO formation and to define acceptable UCO
kernel compositions. However, these models generally treat carbide consumption from a stoichiometric or
equilibrium perspective and do not directly measure the residual crystalline phases present in irradiated
particles. In addition, prior advanced microscopy of irradiated AGR UCO kernels has shown that the
carbide-bearing phase may not remain as stoichiometric UC2, but can evolve toward an oxygen-bearing
monocarbide or oxycarbide phase, commonly described as UCxOy (van Rooyen et al. 2021).

In the present work, single-particle neutron diffraction was used to evaluate the crystalline phase
composition of individual AGR-5/6/7 UCO TRISO particles before and after irradiation. The objective
was to quantify how the uranium-bearing carbide + oxycarbide reservoir changes with burnup and
irradiation temperature and to determine whether the residual carbide-bearing phase is better described as
retained UC2, oxygen-bearing rocksalt U(C,O), or a mixture of both. The resulting phase fractions were
used to estimate both the fraction of kernel uranium retained in crystalline carbide + oxycarbide phases
and the UC2-equivalent uranium-bound carbon remaining after irradiation. These measurements provide a
direct experimental basis for evaluating carbide retention and oxycarbide formation in irradiated UCO
fuel, helping connect fabrication specifications and thermochemical models to the actual crystalline phase
evolution observed after irradiation.

 2. MATERIALS AND METHODS

2.1 FUEL MATERIALS AND PARTICLE SELECTION

The fuel analyzed in this work consisted of AGR-5/6/7 UCO TRISO particles. The AGR-5/6/7 fuel was
fabricated at engineering scale and used UCO kernels designed to provide sufficient uranium carbide to
suppress CO generation while avoiding unnecessarily reducing kernel conditions that could compromise
fission product retention (Demkowicz et al. 2026). The present measurements were performed on
individual particles rather than bulk particle populations so that residual crystalline carbide could be
evaluated at the particle level. Five unirradiated AGR-5/6/7 particles were analyzed to establish the initial
carbide baseline using the same diffraction and refinement workflow applied to the irradiated particles.
Irradiated particles were selected from three AGR-5/6/7 compacts spanning low, intermediate, and high
compact-average burnup. Parameters for the particles analyzed in this work are listed in Table 1.

2.2 SINGLE-PARTICLE NEUTRON DIFFRACTION APPROACH

Single-particle neutron diffraction was performed on the Spallation Neutrons and Pressure (SNAP)
diffractometer at the ORNL Spallation Neutron Source. SNAP is a high-flux time-of-flight neutron
diffractometer with tunable resolution, large-area detectors, and beam-focusing optics (Calder et al.
2018). Its microfocusing optics and small-sample capability were essential for this work because
conventional neutron powder diffraction measurements would average over multiple particles, mounting
materials, sample holder contributions, and background. By contrast, the SNAP configuration allowed
diffraction to be isolated from individual intact TRISO particles, enabling direct evaluation of particle-to-
particle variability and residual carbide inventory (Ice et al. 2009). The measurement approach is
illustrated in Figure 1. Individual TRISO particles were double encapsulated and positioned in the neutron
beam so that diffraction data were collected from single intact particles. The small illuminated volume
allowed the uranium-bearing kernel phases and coating phases to be evaluated on a particle-specific basis.

  Figure 1. Schematic of the single-particle neutron diffraction approach. Individual TRISO particles were
 double encapsulated and positioned in a focused neutron beam on SNAP.

2.3 PARTICLE HOLDER AND SAMPLE POSITIONING

Particles were mounted in a custom holder insert designed to position individual TRISO particles
reproducibly in the neutron beam. The holder used an aluminum insert and quartz spacers to isolate and
locate particles within the beam path. A CAD rendering of the holder insert and a reconstructed x-ray
computed tomography (XCT) image of the loaded holder are shown in Figure 2. The reconstructed XCT
image was used to verify particle locations within the holder. Streaking or smearing near the high-Z
kernels was attributed to reconstruction artifacts and was not used for quantitative analysis.

2.4 DIFFRACTION REFINEMENTS AND PHASE IDENTIFICATION

Diffraction data were analyzed by Rietveld refinement using the General Structure Analysis System II
software package (Toby and Von Dreele 2013). The refinement models included fluorite UO2 with space
group Fm3)m, tetragonal UC2 with space group I4/mmm, rocksalt U(C,O) with space group Fm3)m, cubic
3C-SiC with space group F4 ) 3m, and graphite-like hexagonal carbon for the PyC contribution with space
group P63/mmc. The graphite-like carbon model was used as an effective representation of crystalline
scattering from PyC and was not intended to imply fully ordered graphite. The uranium carbide phases
were evaluated using UC2 and rocksalt U(C,O) phase models. The U(C,O) phase was included because
prior advanced microscopy of irradiated AGR UCO kernels identified an oxygen-bearing uranium
monocarbide phase, designated UCxOy, in irradiated fuel kernels. In van Rooyen et al. (2021) it was
reported that irradiated kernels consisted primarily of UO2 and UCxOy and that much of the original UC2
was oxidized to UCxOy or UO2 during irradiation. For each particle, the final uranium-bearing kernel
phase model consisted of UO2 plus the carbide + oxycarbide phase or phases supported by the refinement.
Where both UC2 and U(C,O) were retained, both phases were included in the calculation of the crystalline
uranium carbide + oxycarbide reservoir. Where UC2 was not retained, the carbide-bearing contribution
was represented by U(C,O) alone. Refined phase fractions were then used to calculate uranium
partitioning and uranium-bound carbon retention, as described in Section 2.6. Refined unit cell parameters
were retained for the principal crystalline phases to evaluate phase-specific structural changes with
irradiation. For UO2, UC2, U(C,O), SiC, and PyC, unit cell-parameter trends were used as supporting
evidence for changes in phase chemistry, irradiation-induced strain, or structural disorder; however, these
parameters were not interpreted independently as quantitative measurements of composition except for
the constrained U(C,O) oxygen-occupancy estimate described in Section 2.5.

2.5 ESTIMATION OF OXYGEN OCCUPANCY IN THE U(C,O) PHASE

occupancy on the anion site. Oxygen occupancy was estimated from the refined U(C,O) unit cell
parameter using a simple ionic-radius interpolation based on Shannon effective ionic radii (Shannon

 3. RESULTS

3.1 AS-FABRICATED BASELINE SAMPLES

The five unirradiated AGR-5/6/7 particles provided a baseline for evaluating irradiation-induced changes
in the kernel phase inventory. The unirradiated particles were refined using UO2 and UC2-type uranium
carbide as the primary uranium-bearing kernel phases. The resulting uranium fraction in the carbide-
bearing phase is shown in Figure 3. The unirradiated particles showed a narrow range in uranium carbide
fraction, with an average value of f0 = 0.2877 ± 0.0057. On a kernel-normalized weight-fraction basis, this
value corresponds to 28.16 ± 0.56 wt % UC2-type uranium carbide and was used as the initial uranium
carbide–bearing baseline for the irradiated-particle calculations. The diffraction-derived baseline is
slightly higher and less dispersed than the image-analysis-derived AGR-5/6/7 estimate reported by
Helmreich et al. (2023), fC = 0.255 ± 0.28, but agrees closely with the independent bulk-chemistry
estimate, fC = 0.280, derived from LECO carbon and Davies–Gray uranium measurements (Helmreich et
al. 2023). This agreement supports use of the neutron diffraction baseline as the reference condition for
the irradiated-particle analysis.

3.3 EVOLUTION OF THE URANIUM CARBIDE + OXYCARBIDE PHASES

Expanded diffraction regions were examined to evaluate changes in the uranium carbide-bearing
contribution with burnup. These regions are shown in Figure 5. The unirradiated particle exhibits
diffraction features consistent with the as-fabricated uranium carbide phase, represented in the
refinements as UC2-x. With irradiation, the carbide-bearing diffraction contribution changes in
both intensity and position, indicating that the irradiated carbide reservoir is not adequately
described as UC2-x alone. In Fig. 5a, the unirradiated particle exhibits a two-feature UC2-x-
associated structure in the 7400–7600 time-of-flight region. With irradiation, these UC2-x -
associated features become less prominent, while a lower-time-of-flight U(C,O)/UCxOy-type
feature becomes more apparent. Figure 5b shows a feature associated with the unirradiated
uranium carbide contribution is present in the as-fabricated material, whereas the irradiated
particles show a new contribution shifted to lower time of flight. This feature continues to shift
with increasing burnup, consistent with progressive lattice contraction of a rocksalt U(C,O)-type
phase as oxygen occupancy increases. Figure 5c isolates a UC2-x-associated feature that
decreases in relative intensity after irradiation and is no longer resolved in the high-burnup
Compact 2-6-1 particle.

3.6 URANIUM PARTITIONING AND UC2-EQUIVALENT URANIUM-BOUND CARBON

The refined phase fractions were converted to two uranium-normalized metrics, as described in Section
2.6. The first metric, fU,carb+oxy, represents the fraction of kernel uranium contained in crystalline uranium
carbide + oxycarbide phases, including both UC2−x and U(C,O). This metric describes where the uranium
resides among the refined crystalline kernel phases. The second metric, fUC2,eq, represents the uranium-
bound carbon inventory expressed on a UC2-equivalent basis. This second metric accounts for the
reduced carbon occupancy in the oxygen-bearing U(C,O) phase and is more directly related to uranium
carbide consumption. Compact-average values are summarized in Table 6. The difference between
fU,carb+oxy and fUC2,eq shows that uranium partitioning and uranium-bound carbon retention are not

uranium-bound carbon is depleted from that reservoir. In other words, irradiation causes both conversion
of UC2−x-type material to oxygen-bearing U(C,O) and progressive loss of uranium-bound carbon.

 4. DISCUSSION

4.1 AS-FABRICATED CARBIDE BASELINE AND METHOD COMPARISON

The as-fabricated AGR-5/6/7 particles provide the baseline for interpreting carbide consumption after
irradiation. The five unirradiated particles measured by single-particle neutron diffraction gave
fC,O = 0.2877 ± 0.0057, corresponding to 28.16 ± 0.56 wt % UC2−x-like carbide on a kernel-normalized
basis. This value agrees closely with the independent AGR-5/6/7 bulk chemistry value of fC = 0.280
calculated from LECO carbon and Davies–Gray uranium measurements (Helmreich et al. 2023). The
agreement between diffraction and bulk chemistry supports use of the diffraction-derived value as the
initial carbide inventory for calculating carbide consumption.

The diffraction baseline differs slightly from the image-analysis-derived value reported for AGR-5/6/7
particles, fc = 0.255 ± 0.028 (Helmreich et al. 2023). That comparison should be interpreted carefully
because the AGR-5/6/7 image analysis was performed on coated TRISO particles rather than bare
kernels. Although the method can be applied to coated particles, coating-layer attenuation and cross

 15

sectioning path-length corrections introduce additional uncertainty compared with image analysis of
exposed or isolated kernels. These factors can broaden the apparent particle-to-particle distribution. By
contrast, neutron diffraction quantifies resolved crystalline phases from the illuminated volume of intact
particles. Therefore, the lower mean and larger standard deviation from image analysis may reflect a
combination of true particle-to-particle variability and method-specific effects. Additional potential
contributors to the difference in measured averages and standard deviations include preferential loss or
alteration of the carbide-rich kernel surface during sample preparation and sensitivity of image
segmentation to image acquisition settings. These issues are not explored in detail here because the
present work focuses on irradiated-particle neutron diffraction. A more detailed evaluation of image
analysis bias, segmentation sensitivity, and carbide-skin effects will be the subject of separate
investigations. For this work, the close agreement between the neutron diffraction baseline and
independent bulk chemistry supports use of the diffraction-derived baseline for the irradiated-particle
comparison.

Image analysis from optical micrographs should not be treated as an irradiated-fuel composition
comparator in this context. It is useful for as-fabricated UCO kernel characterization, but it is not suitable
for quantifying residual oxide and carbide inventories in irradiated kernels, where fission product
accumulation, irradiation damage, phase redistribution, and changes in contrast complicate segmentation
and phase assignment. This limitation reinforces the importance of the present diffraction measurements:
no corresponding bulk composition measurements directly quantify residual UO2 and UC2 inventories in
irradiated AGR UCO fuel.

4.2 IRRADIATION-INDUCED EVOLUTION FROM UC2−x TO U(C,O)

The irradiated-particle refinements indicate a progressive change in the crystalline uranium carbide +
oxycarbide reservoir from UC2−x-rich material toward an oxygen-bearing rock salt U(C,O) phase. This
change is evident in the expanded diffraction regions shown in Figure 5, where the UC2−x-associated
feature decreases with irradiation and a lower time-of-flight feature consistent with U(C,O)/UCxOy
becomes increasingly prominent. The Compact 5-6-3 particles, irradiated to 7.03% FIMA and a TAVA
irradiation temperature of 643°C, retained both UC2−x and U(C,O) in the refinements. The Compact 5-1-3
particles, irradiated to 9.38% FIMA and a TAVA irradiation temperature of 721°C, also retained both
phases, but the refined uranium carbide + oxycarbide contribution shifted strongly toward U(C,O). For
the Compact 2-6-1 particles, irradiated to 14.89% FIMA and a TAVA irradiation temperature of 838°C,
no UC2−x contribution was retained in the final refinements, and the residual crystalline uranium carbide–
bearing contribution was represented by U(C,O).

This trend is consistent with prior advanced microscopy of irradiated AGR UCO fuel kernels. In van
Rooyen et al. (2021) it was reported that irradiated AGR-1 and AGR-2 kernels consisted primarily of UO2
and an oxygen-bearing uranium monocarbide phase designated UCxOy and that much of the original UC2
was oxidized to UCxOy or UO2 during irradiation. The present neutron diffraction results provide
complementary particle-average evidence from intact AGR-5/6/7 particles that the uranium carbide–
bearing reservoir evolves toward an oxygen-bearing oxycarbide phase during irradiation. The distinction
between UC2−x and U(C,O) is important because a UC2−x-only refinement would treat the irradiated
carbide-bearing signal as residual dicarbide-like material. This interpretation instead indicates that part of
the original uranium carbide inventory is chemically transformed into an oxygen-bearing rock salt phase.
Furthermore, this more physically consistent interpretation of the broad irradiated carbide + oxycarbide
diffraction feature avoids assigning all residual scattering in this region to retained UC2−x.

The burnup range of the irradiated AGR-1 and AGR-2 particles examined by van Rooyen et al. (2021)
also provides context for the present results. Several particles in that study were irradiated to compact-
average burnups of approximately 10.8–12.6% FIMA, with additional AGR-1 particles at higher burnups.

 16

These burnups are higher than those experienced by Compacts 5-6-3 and 5-1-3 examined here and
approach or overlap the burnup for Compact 2-6-1, where no UC2-x contribution was retained in the
neutron diffraction refinements. This comparison suggests that UC2-x becomes increasingly difficult to
resolve at higher burnup, although the transition is approximate rather than as a discrete burnup threshold.

4.3 URANIUM PARTITIONING VERSUS URANIUM-BOUND CARBON

The refinements show that uranium partitioning and uranium-bound carbon retention must be treated as
separate quantities. The fraction of kernel uranium retained in crystalline uranium carbide + oxycarbide
phases, fU,carb+oxy, decreases with burnup, but not as rapidly as the UC2-equivalent uranium-bound carbon
inventory, fUC2,eq. This difference arises because U(C,O) retains uranium in a carbide + oxycarbide crystal
structure while containing less carbon per uranium atom than UC2−x. For the Compact 5-6-3 particles,
fU,carb+oxy decreased modestly relative to the unirradiated baseline, corresponding to a crystalline carbide +
oxycarbide uranium reservoir depletion of 9.3% ± 2.5%. However, the UC2-equivalent uranium-bound
carbon depletion was larger, 22.3% ± 3.0%. This result indicates that measurable uranium-bound carbon
loss or replacement by oxygen occurred even at the lowest burnup condition examined, despite substantial
retention of uranium in crystalline carbide + oxycarbide phases. For the Compact 5-1-3 particles, the
distinction became more pronounced. The crystalline carbide + oxycarbide uranium reservoir depletion
was 16.4% ± 4.6%, whereas the UC2-equivalent uranium-bound carbon depletion was 55.8% ± 5.3%. The
large decrease in fUC2,eq reflects the reduced UC2−x contribution and the increased U(C,O) contribution in
these particles. Thus, although a significant fraction of uranium remained in the crystalline uranium
carbide + oxycarbide reservoir, much of that reservoir was no longer UC2−x-like in carbon content. For the
Compact 2-6-1 particles, the refined uranium carbide–bearing phase was represented by U(C,O) alone.
These particles showed 37.1% ± 7.6% depletion of the crystalline carbide + oxycarbide uranium reservoir
but 75.3% ± 3.9% depletion of the UC2-equivalent uranium-bound carbon inventory. This result indicates
that, by the highest burnup examined, most of the original uranium-bound carbon inventory had been
consumed or replaced by oxygen, while a smaller but still measurable fraction of uranium remained in a
crystalline oxycarbide phase.

These results suggest that describing irradiation evolution only as uranium carbide depletion is
incomplete. Rather, irradiation causes redistribution of uranium from carbide-bearing phases toward UO2
as well as chemical evolution of the remaining carbide-bearing phase toward oxygen-bearing U(C,O).
The latter process causes the uranium-bound carbon inventory to decrease more rapidly than the uranium
fraction in crystalline carbide + oxycarbide phases.

4.4 SIGNIFICANCE OF THE ESTIMATED U(C,O) OXYGEN OCCUPANCY

The estimated oxygen occupancies in the U(C,O) phase provide additional support for the interpretation
that the residual uranium carbide–bearing phase becomes increasingly oxycarbide-like with irradiation.
The average U(C,O) unit cell parameter decreased from 4.9162 ± 0.0080 Å for Compact 5-6-3 particles to
4.8810 ± 0.0152 Å for Compact 5-1-3 particles and 4.8682 ± 0.0148 Å for Compact 2-6-1 particles. All
these values are contracted relative to ideal UC, consistent with partial oxygen occupancy on the anion
sublattice. Using the approximate interpolation described in Section 2.5, the average U(C,O) formulas
were estimated as to UC0.91O0.09 for Compact 5-6-3 particles, to UC0.82O0.18 for Compact 5-1-3 particles,
and to UC0.78O0.22 for Compact 2-6-1 particles. These formulas should be interpreted as approximate
descriptions of the refined rock salt U(C,O) phase, not as bulk kernel compositions. The apparent increase
in oxygen occupancy is consistent with the progressive loss of UC2−x diffraction features and the
increasing importance of the U(C,O)-type contribution. The trend also agrees qualitatively with
microscopy-based observations that irradiated AGR UCO kernels contain UCxOy-type phases rather than
only stoichiometric UC2 (van Rooyen et al. 2021). However, the oxygen occupancies derived here should
be considered semiquantitative because unit cell parameters may also be affected by uranium valence,

 17

vacancies, fission product substitution, irradiation damage, residual stress, finite coherent domain size,
and uncertainty in fitting broad diffraction peaks.

4.5 COMPARISON WITH STOICHIOMETRIC URANIUM CARBIDE CONSUMPTION
 ESTIMATES

The Homan/GA calculation is a stoichiometric evolution estimate for uranium carbide demand during
irradiation. The McMurray and Poschmann calculations are lower-bound design estimates for the
minimum uranium carbide content needed to suppress CO formation in UCO kernels. None of these
approaches explicitly predicts formation of an oxygen-bearing U(C,O) phase or separates uranium
partitioning from uranium-bound carbon retention. When the experimental results are expressed as
fU,carb+oxy, the measured Compact 5-6-3 value is close to the McMurray and Poschmann estimates and
above the Homan/GA line. The Compact 5-1-3 value remains near the Homan/GA estimate but below the
McMurray and Poschmann estimates. The Compact 2-6-1 value falls below all three model estimates.
This comparison indicates that the crystalline uranium carbide + oxycarbide reservoir decreases with
burnup more than would be expected from the modern lower-bound design estimates, particularly for the
highest-burnup particles.

The comparison changes when the experimental results are expressed as fUC2,eq, the UC2-equivalent
uranium-bound carbon inventory. This metric more directly reflects carbon retained in uranium carbide +
oxycarbide bonding environments; therefore, it is more comparable to stoichiometric uranium carbide
consumption estimates. Using this metric, the measured values decrease substantially below the model
curves for Compact 5-1-3 and Compact 2-6-1 particles. The comparison shows that a substantial fraction
of the uranium-bound carbon initially present in UC2−x-type material is no longer present as uranium-
bound carbon after irradiation. The divergence between fU,carb+oxy and fUC2,eq also helps explain why a
UC2−x-only interpretation can be misleading. A UC2−x-only model would tend to interpret loss of the
UC2−x feature as direct loss of the uranium carbide phase. The U(C,O)-based interpretation shows that
some uranium remains in an oxycarbide phase, even as the UC2-equivalent uranium-bound carbon
inventory decreases strongly. Thus, the experimentally observed irradiation response involves both
uranium redistribution and chemical conversion of the residual uranium carbide–bearing phase.

Several factors may contribute to this divergence. The Homan/GA, McMurray, and Poschmann
treatments necessarily simplify the kernel as a chemically communicating system in which oxygen
released during fission can be partitioned among uranium, carbon, and fission product oxygen sinks
according to assumed stoichiometric or equilibrium constraints. However, in an actual UCO kernel,
fission occurs within a spatially heterogeneous mixture of UO2, UC2−x, U(C,O), porosity, and fission
product–bearing phases. Therefore, oxygen release, fission product precipitation, and uranium carbide
oxidation may occur locally rather than according to a fully equilibrated bulk kernel average. If fission
product oxides do not form as completely, rapidly, or uniformly as assumed by the models, then their
effective role as oxygen sinks would be reduced, leaving more oxygen available to consume uranium
carbide or convert UC2−x to U(C,O).

A second possible contributor is the changing fission source with burnup. As 239Pu and 240Pu accumulate,
an increasing fraction of fissions may occur in plutonium rather than 235U. Fission of bred plutonium from
238
 U can alter the oxygen balance because oxygen initially associated with actinide oxide must be
redistributed as actinides are converted to fission products. Calculated actinide inventories provide
qualitative support for this effect: on an atom basis, the ratio of (239Pu + 240Pu) to residual 235U increases
from approximately 0.12 for Compact 5-6-3 particles to 0.25 for Compact 5-1-3 particles and 0.65 for
Compact 2-6-1 particles (Sterbenz 2020). Although this inventory ratio does not directly quantify the
fraction of fissions from plutonium, it supports the expectation that transmutation products become
increasingly important to the actinide and oxygen balance at the highest burnup examined here.

 18

Finally, the diffraction-derived fUC2,eq reflects uranium-bound carbon retained in the refined crystalline
uranium carbide + oxycarbide phases. Carbon associated with highly disordered, nanocrystalline,
amorphous, or fission product–bearing phases would not necessarily be quantified in the same way.
Therefore, the comparison should be interpreted as a difference between model-equivalent uranium
carbide inventory and the crystalline uranium-bound carbon inventory measured by neutron diffraction,
rather than as a direct failure of the thermochemical models. Overall, the model comparison supports the
central interpretation that irradiation does not simply deplete a UC2−x-type phase. Instead, uranium is
redistributed between UO2 and carbide + oxycarbide phases while the remaining uranium carbide–bearing
reservoir evolves chemically toward oxygen-bearing U(C,O). This phase evolution causes uranium-bound
carbon to be depleted more rapidly than uranium is removed from the crystalline carbide + oxycarbide
reservoir.

4.6 UNIT CELL PARAMETER TRENDS AND COATING LAYER RESPONSE

The unit cell parameter trends provide supporting evidence for phase-specific irradiation effects in both
the kernel and coating layers. The UO2 unit cell parameter increased with burnup, which is consistent
with irradiation-induced evolution of the oxide phase and swelling due to radiation damage and
incorporation of fission products. The U(C,O) unit cell parameter decreased with burnup group and
remained contracted relative to ideal UC, supporting the interpretation of increasing oxygen content in the
rocksalt uranium carbide + oxycarbide phase. The PyC unit cell parameter decreased with burnup,
consistent with irradiation-induced structural evolution of the PyC layers such as densification in the
buffer layer or contraction of the PyC layers.

The SiC unit cell parameter changed little compared with the other phases in the particles. The slight
contraction observed in some irradiated SiC measurements could be consistent with modest compressive
strain in the SiC layer, potentially associated with irradiation-induced dimensional change in the
surrounding PyC layers. However, the effect is small, so the SiC unit cell parameter should be treated
only as qualitative evidence of possible strain in the coating layer and should not be interpreted as a
quantitative stress measurement.

Peak broadening in the uranium carbide + oxycarbide diffraction region also supports qualitative
structural evolution of the carbide-bearing reservoir. The broadening may reflect irradiation-induced
disorder, reduced coherent domain size, compositional heterogeneity, or overlapping contributions from
UC2−x and U(C,O). Because the relevant reflections are broad and limited in number, these contributions
were not deconvoluted. Therefore, the peak-broadening behavior is used qualitatively to support the
phase-evolution interpretation rather than as a quantitative measure of microstrain or domain size.

4.7 IMPLICATIONS FOR UCO FUEL SPECIFICATION AND IRRADIATION
 PERFORMANCE INTERPRETATION

The practical value of these measurements is that they provide an experimental basis for estimating how
much uranium carbide is actually consumed during irradiation. UCO kernels are designed with a uranium
carbide–bearing phase to buffer oxygen and suppress CO formation, but the optimum composition is not
necessarily the maximum achievable carbide content. An optimized UCO specification should provide
enough uranium carbide to consume oxygen released during fission and prevent excessive CO generation,
while avoiding unnecessary excess oxygen-gettering capacity in the kernel that could limit formation of
stable oxide-bearing fission-product phases.

Therefore, the UC2-equivalent uranium-bound carbon metric, fUC2,eq, is the most directly useful metric for
specification assessment. The fraction of uranium retained in crystalline carbide + oxycarbide phases,
fU,carb+oxy, describes phase partitioning, but it does not by itself indicate how much oxygen-buffering

 19

capacity remains. By contrast, fUC2,eq estimates the uranium-bound carbon inventory remaining after
accounting for oxygen substitution in U(C,O). This metric more closely tracks the consumption of the
original uranium carbide reservoir. The presence of U(C,O) should not be interpreted as evidence that the
residual oxycarbide phase has the same oxygen-gettering capacity as fresh UC2-x. U(C,O) contains
uranium in a carbide + oxycarbide crystal structure, but part of its anion sublattice is already occupied by
oxygen. Therefore, its remaining capacity to consume additional oxygen is expected to be lower than that
of UC2-x and is not directly measured in this work.

Using the unirradiated baseline of f0 = 0.2877, the Compact 5-6-3 particles retained fUC2,eq = 0.2236 ±
0.0086, corresponding to 22.3 ± 3.0% depletion of the initial UC2-equivalent uranium-bound carbon
inventory. The Compact 5-1-3 particles retained fUC2,eq = 0.1271 ± 0.0152, corresponding to 55.8 ± 5.3%
depletion. The Compact 2-6-1 particles retained only fUC2,eq = 0.0712 ± 0.0114, corresponding to
75.3 ± 3.9% depletion. Thus, even at 14.89% FIMA and a TAVA irradiation temperature of 838°C,
approximately one quarter of the initial UC2-equivalent uranium-bound carbon inventory remained in the
measured particles.

These results suggest that the initial uranium carbide content in AGR-5/6/7 fuel provided substantial
oxygen-gettering margin over the burnup range examined. However, the data also show that the residual
uranium carbide–bearing phase at high burnup is not retained primarily as UC2−x but as oxygen-bearing
U(C,O). Therefore, specification optimization should not be based only on the amount of carbide present
in the as-fabricated kernel. It should also account for how that carbide is consumed, how much
UC2-equivalent uranium-bound carbon remains after irradiation, and whether the residual phase
assemblage provides the desired balance between CO suppression and fission product oxidation
chemistry.

These data do not define a revised lower limit for uranium carbide content because the required margin
depends on burnup, temperature, enrichment, kernel stoichiometry, fission product inventory, and
acceptable CO pressure. However, they demonstrate the type of experimental constraint needed to refine
that limit. By measuring the retained UC2-equivalent uranium-bound carbon inventory after irradiation,
single-particle neutron diffraction can be used to test whether current uranium carbide specifications are
overly conservative, insufficient, or appropriately balanced for a given fuel design and irradiation
envelope. The combination of diffraction-derived uranium carbide consumption, thermochemical
modeling, and postirradiation fission product retention data should provide a path toward optimizing
UCO kernel composition for both internal gas control and fission product stability.

 5. CONCLUSIONS

Single-particle neutron diffraction was used to measure crystalline phase evolution in individual
AGR-5/6/7 UCO TRISO particles before and after irradiation. The study focused on as-fabricated
particles and irradiated particles from Compact 5-6-3, Compact 5-1-3, and Compact 2-6-1, which
represent increasing burnup and slightly increasing irradiation temperature conditions. The five as-
fabricated particles provided a baseline for the initial uranium carbide–bearing inventory. The average
uranium fraction in the refined carbide-bearing phase was f0 = 0.2877 ± 0.0057, corresponding to 28.16 ±
0.56 wt % UC2−x-type uranium carbide on a kernel-normalized uranium-bearing phase basis. This baseline
agrees closely with the independent bulk-chemistry estimate for AGR-5/6/7 kernels and provides the
reference condition for evaluating irradiation-induced uranium carbide consumption. The irradiated-
particle refinements show that the uranium carbide–bearing phase evolves from UC2−x-rich material
toward oxygen-bearing U(C,O). Compact 5-6-3 particles retained both UC2−x and U(C,O), with UC2−x
remaining the larger carbide-bearing contribution. Compact 5-1-3 particles also retained both phases, but
the uranium carbide–bearing contribution shifted strongly toward U(C,O). In Compact 2-6-1 particles, no

 20

UC2−x contribution was retained in the refinements, and the residual uranium carbide–bearing phase was
represented by U(C,O) alone.

Two separate metrics were needed to describe the irradiated kernels. The fraction of kernel uranium
retained in crystalline carbide + oxycarbide phases, fU,carb+oxy, decreased from 0.2610 ± 0.0072 for
Compact 5-6-3 particles to 0.2405 ± 0.0131 for Compact 5-1-3 particles and 0.1811 ± 0.0218 for
Compact 2-6-1 particles. Relative to the as-fabricated baseline, these results correspond depletion of the
crystalline carbide + oxycarbide uranium reservoir of 9.3 ± 2.5%, 16.4 ± 4.6%, and 37.1 ± 7.6%,
respectively. The UC2-equivalent uranium-bound carbon inventory decreased significantly. The fUC2,eq
values were 0.2236 ± 0.0086, 0.1271 ± 0.00152, and 0.0712 ± 0.0114 for particles from Compacts 5-6-3,
5-1-3, and 2-6-1, respectively. These values correspond to uranium-bound carbon depletion of 22.3 ±
3.0%, 55.8 ± 5.3%, and 75.3 ± 3.9%. Thus, by 14.89% FIMA, approximately one quarter of the initial
UC2-equivalent uranium-bound carbon inventory remained in the measured Compact 2-6-1 particles.
These results show that uranium partitioning and uranium-bound carbon retention are not equivalent after
irradiation. A measurable fraction of uranium remains in crystalline carbide + oxycarbide phases at high
burnup, but much of that reservoir has been converted from UC2−x-type material to oxygen-bearing
U(C,O). Therefore, a UC2−x-only interpretation would underrepresent the chemical transformation of the
uranium carbide–bearing reservoir and could misrepresent the amount of uranium-bound carbon
consumed.

The comparison with Homan/GA, McMurray, and Poschmann estimates indicates that diffraction-derived
measurements provide an experimental constraint on uranium carbide consumption. The model
comparisons should be interpreted carefully because those estimates do not explicitly predict U(C,O)
formation or distinguish uranium partitioning from uranium-bound carbon retention. These results
suggest that future specification work should focus on the functional uranium carbide inventory needed to
buffer oxygen and suppress CO, rather than only the as-fabricated uranium carbide phase fraction.

Overall, the data support a UCO TRISO kernel composition specification-optimization framework in
which the uranium carbide content is high enough to suppress CO formation throughout irradiation but
not higher than necessary. Avoiding excess oxygen-gettering capacity may be beneficial if it allows
fission products to form more stable oxide-bearing phases within the kernel. Single-particle neutron
diffraction, combined with thermochemical modeling and fission product retention measurements,
provides a path toward experimentally informed optimization of UCO kernel composition.
