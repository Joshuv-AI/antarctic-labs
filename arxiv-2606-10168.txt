Generalized torsion in triangular matrix groups
Júlia Kato, Douglas V. P. Silva

Abstract
. An element of a group is called generalized torsion if a finite product of its conjugates equals the identity. We characterize the generalized torsion subset of upper triangular matrix groups over commutative rings and determine when these groups satisfy generalized identities in terms of torsion and exponent properties of the unit group of the underlying ring. As applications, we construct new families of infinite finitely generated solvable groups satisfying generalized identities of prescribed lengths and provide a counterexample to a question of Sherman.

20H25, 20F16, 16U60

1.
Introduction

An element
g
of a group
G
is called
generalized torsion
if there exist elements
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\in G
such that

g^{x_{1}}g^{x_{2}}\cdots g^{x_{k}}=1,\qquad g^{x}=x^{-1}gx.

The smallest such integer
k
is called the
generalized order
of
g
.

Generalized torsion has been studied in several contexts, notably as an obstruction to bi-orderability
[
16
,
5
]
, and more recently in several classes of groups from a topological perspective
[
18
,
8
,
12
,
13
]
.

The set of all generalized torsion elements of
G
is denoted by
{\sf T_{\bullet}}(G)
. In general, this set need not be a subgroup and is not necessarily closed under taking powers
[
2
]
. Consequently, determining
{\sf T_{\bullet}}(G)
is often a difficult problem.

Moreover, the size of
{\sf T_{\bullet}}(G)
can vary dramatically. At one extreme,
{\sf T_{\bullet}}(G)=\{1\}
for torsion-free nilpotent groups, free groups, and Thompson’s group
F
(see
[
3
,
6
]
). At the opposite extreme, when
{\sf T_{\bullet}}(G)=G
, we say that
G
is a
generalized torsion group
. Examples of such groups arise in a variety of settings
[
9
,
10
,
15
]
.

Even within linear groups, generalized torsion can exhibit very different behaviors. Indeed, Witte
[
19
]
proved that
SL_{n}(K)
is a generalized torsion group whenever
n>2
or
|K|>3
, whereas
UT_{n}(R)
has trivial generalized torsion whenever
R
has characteristic zero, since it is torsion-free nilpotent. This suggests that linear groups provide a rich setting in which to study generalized torsion.

Closely related to generalized torsion are
generalized identities
. Following
[
1
]
, a polynomial word in one variable with coefficients in
G
is an expression of the form

w(x)=x^{g_{1}}x^{g_{2}}\cdots x^{g_{k}},

where
g_{1},\dots,g_{k}\in G
are fixed. The integer
k
is called the
length
of
w
.
We say that
G
satisfies the generalized identity
w
if

w(g)=1\qquad\text{for all }g\in G.

Generalized identities have been studied in several works
[
7
,
1
,
2
,
4
]
. In the context of solvable groups, their existence often imposes strong finiteness conditions. For instance,
[
1
]
showed that if a solvable group satisfies a generalized identity of prime length
p
, then it must be a finite
p
-group. Thus, the possible identity lengths are often highly rigid.

These contrasting phenomena motivate the study of generalized torsion and generalized identities in solvable linear groups.

In this paper, we study the solvable linear group of upper triangular matrices

T_{n}(R)=UT_{n}(R)\rtimes D_{n}(R^{\times})

over a commutative ring with identity
R
.

Our first main result gives a complete description of the generalized torsion elements of
T_{n}(R)
, showing that they are entirely determined by the torsion of the unit group
R^{\times}
.

Theorem A
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring with identity and
n\geq 1
. Then

{\sf T_{\bullet}}(T_{n}(R))=UT_{n}(R)\rtimes D_{n}({\sf T}(R^{\times})),

where
{\sf T}(R^{\times})
denotes the torsion subgroup of
R^{\times}
.

In particular:

(1)

{\sf T_{\bullet}}(T_{n}(R))
is a characteristic subgroup of
T_{n}(R)
;

(2)

UT_{n}(R)\subseteq{\sf T_{\bullet}}(T_{n}(R))
; and

(3)

{\sf T_{\bullet}}(T_{n}(R))=T_{n}(R)
if and only if
R^{\times}
is a torsion group.

In fact, we prove that
{\sf T_{\bullet}}(T_{n}(R))
is itself a generalized torsion group, providing, for every commutative ring
R
, a linear nilpotent-by-abelian generalized torsion group. Moreover, the same techniques allow us to guarantee generalized identities in
{\sf T_{\bullet}}(T_{n}(R))
and in related intermediate subgroups, leading to the following characterization.

Theorem B
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring with identity and
n\geq 1
. Then
T_{n}(R)
satisfies a generalized identity if and only if
R^{\times}
has finite exponent.

A noteworthy feature of
Theorem B
is that the possible identity lengths are determined by suitable torsion elements of
R^{\times}
.

More generally, the key tool underlying both theorems is that diagonal conjugation acts on
UT_{n}(R)
by rescaling superdiagonal entries. Combined with the nilpotent structure of
UT_{n}(R)
, this reduces questions about generalized torsion and generalized identities in
T_{n}(R)
to the arithmetic properties of the unit group
R^{\times}
.

We illustrate this dependence on the base ring by providing examples of triangular matrix groups with markedly different behavior. For instance, if
R
is the ring of integers of an algebraic number field, then
T_{n}(R)
satisfies a generalized identity if and only if the underlying field is either
\mathbb{Q}
or an imaginary quadratic field. Likewise, if
R
is the integral group ring of a finite abelian group
G
, then
T_{n}(R)
satisfies a generalized identity if and only if
G
has exponent
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
, or
6
.

Moreover, as applications of the developed theory, we construct triangular matrix groups satisfying generalized identities of new lengths, extending the constructions of
[
1
,
2
]
, and provide a counterexample to a question of Sherman
[
17
]
concerning groups generated by elements of infinite order.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
establishes notation and background on triangular matrix groups and generalized identities. Section
3
contains the proof of
Theorem A
. Section
4
treats generalized identities and contains the proof
of
Theorem B
and the results on intermediate subgroups.
Section
5
presents families of rings illustrating Theorems A and B. Section
6
contains the applications to metabelian groups and
the Sherman counterexample.

2.
Preliminaries

Throughout the paper,
R
denotes a commutative ring with identity, and

R^{\times}
denotes its group of units. We consider the triangular matrix group

T_{n}(R)=UT_{n}(R)\rtimes D_{n}(R^{\times}).

For a subgroup
S\leq R^{\times}
, we define

G_{n}(S)=\pi^{-1}(D_{n}(S)),

where
\pi:T_{n}(R)\to D_{n}(R^{\times})
is the canonical projection onto the diagonal subgroup. Explicitly,

G_{n}(S)=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}d_{1}&a_{1,2}&\cdots&a_{1,n}\\
0&d_{2}&\cdots&a_{2,n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&d_{n}\end{pmatrix}\;\middle|\;d_{i}\in S,\;a_{i,j}\in R\right\}.

Thus, the groups
G_{n}(S)
interpolate between the unitriangular subgroup and the full triangular group:

UT_{n}(R)=G_{n}(\{1\}),\qquad T_{n}(R)=G_{n}(R^{\times}).

The group
UT_{n}(R)
is nilpotent of class
n-1
, and its lower central series is given by

\gamma_{k}(UT_{n}(R))=\left\{M\in UT_{n}(R)\mid M_{i,j}=0\text{ whenever }0<j-i<k\right\}.

In other words,
\gamma_{k}(UT_{n}(R))
consists of the matrices whose first
k-1
superdiagonals vanish.

In what follows, we will prove not only that
UT_{n}(R)
is always a generalized torsion subset of
T_{n}(R)
, but also that one can choose a single polynomial word in
T_{n}(R)
that vanishes on all unitriangular matrices.

Following
[
1
]
, this motivates the definition of a generalized identity for a subset
N
of a group
G
. We say that
N
satisfies a generalized identity in
G
if there exists a polynomial word

w(x)=x^{g_{1}}x^{g_{2}}\cdots x^{g_{n}},

with
g_{i}\in G
, such that

w(g)=1\qquad\text{for all }g\in N.

Finally, following the notation introduced in
[
7
]
, we denote by
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{n}
the class of groups satisfying a generalized identity of length
n
.

The following lemma shows that the multiplication of elements in
\gamma_{k}(UT_{n}(R))
behaves additively on a fixed superdiagonal. This observation will be used to construct generalized identities in
UT_{n}(R)
.

These results are well known, but we include proofs for the reader’s convenience.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
M_{1},\dots,M_{r}\in\gamma_{k}(UT_{n}(R))
. Then, for every
j\leq n-k
,

\left(\prod_{i=1}^{r}M_{i}\right)_{j,j+k}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}(M_{i})_{j,j+k}.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the case
r=2
, since the general case follows by induction.

Let
A,B\in\gamma_{k}(UT_{n}(R))
, and fix
j\leq n-k
. We compute

(AB)_{j,j+k}=\sum_{\ell=j}^{j+k}A_{j,\ell}B_{\ell,j+k}.

If
j<\ell<j+k
, then
\ell-j<k
, so
A_{j,\ell}=0
. Hence only the terms
\ell=j
and
\ell=j+k
contribute, and

(AB)_{j,j+k}=A_{j,j}B_{j,j+k}+A_{j,j+k}B_{j+k,j+k}.

Since both matrices are unitriangular,
A_{j,j}=B_{j+k,j+k}=1
, and therefore

(AB)_{j,j+k}=A_{j,j+k}+B_{j,j+k}.

∎

In order to exploit this, we will consider products of conjugates by successive powers of a
single
diagonal matrix. The key point is that conjugation by
such a matrix rescales the entries on a fixed superdiagonal by powers of a single ratio, so that the resulting sum can be arranged to vanish.
This will allow us to eliminate one superdiagonal at a time.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
M\in\gamma_{k}(UT_{n}(R))
and let
D=\operatorname{diag}(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
be a diagonal matrix. Then, for every
j\leq n-k
,

(M\,M^{D}\cdots M^{D^{k}})_{j,j+k}=\left(1+\frac{d_{j+k}}{d_{j}}+\cdots+\left(\frac{d_{j+k}}{d_{j}}\right)^{k}\right)M_{j,j+k}.

Proof.

Since
\gamma_{k}(UT_{n}(R))
is normal, each
M^{D^{r}}
lies in
\gamma_{k}(UT_{n}(R))
.
Applying Lemma
2.1
to the product
M\,M^{D}\cdots M^{D^{k}}
, we obtain

(M\,M^{D}\cdots M^{D^{k}})_{j,j+k}=\sum_{r=0}^{k}(M^{D^{r}})_{j,j+k}.

Using

(M^{D^{r}})_{j,j+k}=\left(\frac{d_{j+k}}{d_{j}}\right)^{r}M_{j,j+k},

the result follows.
∎

3.
Proof of Theorem A

First, we show that the subgroup
UT_{n}(R)
satisfies a generalized identity in
T_{n}(R)
. For rings
R
of positive characteristic, this follows from the fact that
UT_{n}(R)
is a torsion group of finite exponent, as we will see next.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
R
be a ring with
\operatorname{char}R=s>0
, then
UT_{n}(R)
satisfies a generalized identity of length
s^{n-1}
in
T_{n}(R)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.2
, for any

M\in\gamma_{i}(UT_{n}(R))
, we have

(M^{s})_{j,j+i}=(\underbrace{1+\dots+1}_{s})\,M_{j,j+i}=0,

and therefore

M^{s}\in\gamma_{i+1}(UT_{n}(R)).

Since
UT_{n}(R)
is nilpotent of class
n-1
,
M^{s^{n-1}}=\mathrm{Id}_{n}
. Thus
UT_{n}(R)
is a torsion group whose exponent divides
s^{n-1}
. Consequently,
UT_{n}(R)
satisfies a generalized identity of length
s^{n-1}
in
T_{n}(R)
.
∎

In the case of a ring
R
of characteristic zero, we can still show that
UT_{n}(R)
satisfies a generalized identity of length
s^{n-1}
in
T_{n}(R)
, provided that
R
contains a unit
\zeta
such that

1+\zeta+\cdots+\zeta^{s-1}=0
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring, and let
\zeta\in R^{\times}
satisfy

1+\zeta+\cdots+\zeta^{s-1}=0.

Then
UT_{n}(R)
satisfies a generalized identity of length
s^{\,n-1}
inside
G_{n}(\langle\zeta\rangle)\subseteq T_{n}(R)
.

Proof.

For each
1\leq i\leq n-1
, define a diagonal matrix

D_{i}=\operatorname{diag}(d^{(i)}_{1},\dots,d^{(i)}_{n})\in G_{n}(\langle\zeta\rangle)

whose diagonal entries are constant on blocks of length
i
, alternating between
\zeta
and
1
, that is,

D_{i}=\operatorname{diag}(\underbrace{\zeta,\dots,\zeta}_{i},\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{i},\underbrace{\zeta,\dots,\zeta}_{i},\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{i},\dots).

With this choice, for every
j\leq n-i
, the indices
j
and
j+i
lie in consecutive blocks, and therefore

\frac{d^{(i)}_{j+i}}{d^{(i)}_{j}}\in\{\zeta,\zeta^{-1}\}.

For each
i\geq 1
, define recursively a word
w_{i}(x)
by

w_{1}(x)=x,\qquad w_{i+1}(x)=w_{i}(x)\,w_{i}(x)^{D_{i}}\cdots w_{i}(x)^{D_{i}^{\,s-1}}.

We will show that for every
M\in UT_{n}(R)
, one has
w_{n}(M)=1
, which yields a generalized identity of length
s^{\,n-1}
in
G_{n}(\langle\zeta\rangle)
.

We prove by induction that
w_{i}(M)\in\gamma_{i}(UT_{n}(R))
for all
i
.

For
i=1
, this is clear since
\gamma_{1}(UT_{n}(R))=UT_{n}(R)
. Assume that
w_{i}(M)\in\gamma_{i}(UT_{n}(R))
. Applying Lemma
2.2
, for
j\leq n-i
we obtain

(w_{i+1}(M))_{j,j+i}=\left(1+\frac{d^{(i)}_{j+i}}{d^{(i)}_{j}}+\cdots+\left(\frac{d^{(i)}_{j+i}}{d^{(i)}_{j}}\right)^{s-1}\right)(w_{i}(M))_{j,j+i}.

Since
\frac{d^{(i)}_{j+i}}{d^{(i)}_{j}}\in\{\zeta,\zeta^{-1}\}
and both satisfy

1+\zeta^{\pm 1}+\cdots+(\zeta^{\pm 1})^{s-1}=0,

it follows that
(w_{i+1}(M))_{j,j+i}=0
for all
j\leq n-i
. Since
w_{i+1}(M)
is a product of conjugates of
w_{i}(M)
, it lies in
\gamma_{i}(UT_{n}(R))
, and the vanishing of the
i
-th superdiagonal implies that

w_{i+1}(M)\in\gamma_{i+1}(UT_{n}(R)).

Thus
w_{i}(M)\in\gamma_{i}(UT_{n}(R))
for all
i
. Since
UT_{n}(R)
is nilpotent of class
n-1
, we obtain
w_{n}(M)=1
.

By construction,
w_{n}
has length
s^{\,n-1}
, which proves the claim.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

Notice that
-1\in R^{\times}
always satisfies
1+(-1)=0
.
Therefore, taking
\zeta=-1
, Lemma
3.2
shows that
UT_{n}(R)
always satisfies a generalized identity of length
2^{n-1}
inside
T_{n}(R)
.

However, in general,
UT_{n}(R)
may satisfy generalized identities of different lengths in
T_{n}(R)
, as in the case of rings with
\operatorname{char}(R)\neq 2
considered in Proposition
3.1
. We will exploit generalized identities whose lengths are not powers of two in Section
6
. When
\operatorname{char}(R)=2
, the identity obtained above coincides precisely with the one given in Proposition
3.1
.

Now we proceed with the proof of
Theorem A
.

Proof of Theorem A.

Let
M\in{\sf T_{\bullet}}(T_{n}(R))
. Then
\pi(M)
is a generalized torsion element of
D_{n}(R^{\times})
. Since

D_{n}(R^{\times})\simeq(R^{\times})^{n}

is abelian, every generalized torsion element has finite order. Therefore, each entry of
\pi(M)
has finite order, and hence
\pi(M)\in D_{n}({\sf T}(R^{\times}))
.

Consequently,

{\sf T_{\bullet}}(T_{n}(R))\subseteq UT_{n}(R)\rtimes D_{n}({\sf T}(R^{\times})).

Conversely, let

M\in UT_{n}(R)\rtimes D_{n}({\sf T}(R^{\times})).

Then
\pi(M)\in D_{n}({\sf T}(R^{\times}))
, and therefore
\pi(M)
has finite order. Let
k\geq 1
be such that
\pi(M)^{k}=\mathrm{Id}_{n}
. It follows that
M^{k}\in UT_{n}(R)
. Hence, it suffices to show that every element of
UT_{n}(R)
is generalized torsion in
T_{n}(R)
.

By Lemma
3.2
(with
\zeta=-1
and
s=2
), the group
UT_{n}(R)
satisfies a generalized identity of length
2^{n-1}
inside
T_{n}(R)
. Hence,

UT_{n}(R)\subseteq{\sf T_{\bullet}}(T_{n}(R)).

This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

As observed in the proof of
Theorem A
, the subgroup

{\sf T_{\bullet}}(T_{n}(R))=G_{n}({\sf T}(R^{\times}))

is itself a generalized torsion group. Indeed, for every element of this subgroup, the generalized torsion identities constructed in the proof use only conjugation by diagonal matrices in
D_{n}({\sf T}(R^{\times}))
.

4.
Generalized identities in triangular matrix groups

In the previous section, we showed that
UT_{n}(R)
always satisfies a generalized identity inside
T_{n}(R)
, and that every generalized torsion element of
T_{n}(R)
lies in

G_{n}({\sf T}(R^{\times}))=UT_{n}(R)\rtimes D_{n}\big({\sf T}(R^{\times})\big).

We now study when the subgroups
G_{n}(S)
satisfy generalized identities.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring and let
S\leq R^{\times}
be a subgroup containing an element
\zeta\in S
such that

1+\zeta+\zeta^{2}+\cdots+\zeta^{s-1}=0.

Then
G_{n}(S)
satisfies a generalized identity if and only if
\exp(S)<\infty
. In this case,

G_{n}(S)\in\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{s^{\,n-1}\exp(S)}.

Proof.

Since
\zeta\in S
, Lemma
3.2
implies that
UT_{n}(R)
satisfies a generalized identity of length
s^{\,n-1}
inside
G_{n}(S)
.

Assume first that
\exp(S)=k<\infty
. Let
M\in G_{n}(S)
. Since
\pi(M)
is an element of
D_{n}(S)\simeq S^{n}
, we have
M^{k}\in UT_{n}(R)
. Applying the generalized identity for
UT_{n}(R)
to
M^{k}
, we obtain

w_{n}(M^{k})=1,

where
w_{n}
is the word constructed in Lemma
3.2
. Equivalently, defining
w_{0}(x)=x^{k}
, we have

w_{n}(w_{0}(M))=1\qquad\text{for all }M\in G_{n}(S).

Hence
G_{n}(S)
satisfies a generalized identity of length
s^{\,n-1}k
.

Conversely, suppose that
G_{n}(S)
satisfies a generalized identity of length
m
. Since
D_{n}(S)
is a subgroup of
G_{n}(S)
, it also satisfies the same identity. In particular, for every
s\in S
, the matrix
D(s)=\diag(s,\dots,s)
has generalized order at most
m
. But
D(s)
is central, so conjugation is trivial and every generalized identity reduces to an ordinary power identity. Therefore,

D(s)^{m}=\mathrm{Id}_{n}\qquad\text{for all }s\in S.

In particular, every element of
S
has order dividing
m
, and thus
\exp(S)<\infty
.
∎

As a direct consequence, we have the following proof for
Theorem B
.

Proof of Theorem B.

Apply Theorem
4.1
with
S=R^{\times}
,
\zeta=-1
, and
s=2
.
∎

We end this section with an example where the generalized order of elements in
T_{n}(R)
need not be the upper bound obtained in Theorem
4.1
.

Remark 4.2
.

By Theorem
4.1
, the group
T_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
satisfies a generalized identity of length
4
. However, every nontrivial element has generalized order
2
.

Indeed, for

M=\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{1}&x\\
0&\varepsilon_{2}\end{pmatrix},\qquad X=\begin{pmatrix}1&\varepsilon_{1}x\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix},

one has
X^{-1}MX=M^{-1}
, and hence
MM^{X}=1
.

5.
Examples

In this section, we present examples of classes of rings for which
G_{n}({\sf T}(R^{\times}))
and/or
T_{n}(R)
satisfy a generalized identity by applying
Theorem B
and Theorem
4.1
.

We begin with two classes of commutative rings for which
G_{n}({\sf T}(R^{\times}))
always satisfies a generalized identity, whereas
T_{n}(R)
does so only in specific, classifiable cases.

5.1.
Rings of integers of algebraic number fields

Let
K
be an algebraic number field and let
O_{K}
denote its ring of integers. Let
r_{1}
be the number of real embeddings of
K
, and let
r_{2}
be the number of pairs of conjugate complex embeddings.

By Dirichlet’s Unit Theorem (see
[
14
]
), one has

O_{K}^{\times}\cong\mu_{K}\times\mathbb{Z}^{r},

where
\mu_{K}
is the group of roots of unity in
K
and
r=r_{1}+r_{2}-1.

In particular,
{\sf T}(O_{K}^{\times})
is finite. Hence, by Theorem
4.1
, we obtain the following.

Example 5.1
.

The group
G_{n}({\sf T}(O_{K}^{\times}))
satisfies a generalized identity.

Moreover,
O_{K}^{\times}={\sf T}(O_{K}^{\times})
if and only if
r=0
. Therefore, by
Theorem B
, we have the following characterization.

Example 5.2
.

The group
T_{n}(O_{K})
satisfies a generalized identity if and only if
K=\mathbb{Q}
or
K
is an imaginary quadratic field.

Proof.

If
r=0
, then
r_{1}+r_{2}=1
. Hence either
r_{1}=1
and
r_{2}=0
, or
r_{1}=0
and
r_{2}=1
.

In the first case,
K=\mathbb{Q}
. In the second case,
[K:\mathbb{Q}]=2
, and
K
has no real embeddings. Therefore,
K
is an imaginary quadratic field.
∎

5.2.
Integral group rings over finite abelian groups

Let
G
be a finite abelian group. Denote by
n_{2}
the number of elements of order
2
in
G
, and by
c
the number of cyclic subgroups of
G
.

A theorem of Higman (see
[
11
]
) states the following.

Theorem 5.3

(Higman)
.

Let
G
be a finite abelian group. Then

\mathbb{Z}G^{\times}\cong\pm G\times\mathbb{Z}^{r},

where

r=\frac{|G|+1+n_{2}-2c}{2}.

In particular,
{\sf T}(\mathbb{Z}G^{\times})
is finite. Hence, by Theorem
4.1
, we obtain the following.

Example 5.4
.

Let
G
be a finite abelian group. Then the group
G_{n}({\sf T}(\mathbb{Z}G^{\times}))
satisfies a generalized identity.

Moreover,
\mathbb{Z}G^{\times}={\sf T}(\mathbb{Z}G^{\times})
if and only if
r=0
. Therefore, by
Theorem B
, we obtain the following characterization.

Example 5.5
.

Let
G
be a finite abelian group. Then
T_{n}(\mathbb{Z}G)
satisfies a generalized identity if and only if
G
has exponent
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
, or
6
.

Proof.

Let
a_{d}
be the number of cyclic subgroups of order
d
. Since each cyclic subgroup of order
d
has
\varphi(d)
generators,

|G|=1+n_{2}+\sum_{d\geq 3}\varphi(d)a_{d},\quad c=1+n_{2}+\sum_{d\geq 3}a_{d}.

Hence

|G|+n_{2}+1-2c=\sum_{d\geq 3}(\varphi(d)-2)a_{d}.

Now
\varphi(d)-2=0
exactly for
d=3,4,6
, and
\varphi(d)-2>0
for all
d\notin\{3,4,6\}
. Therefore,
r=0
if and only if every cyclic subgroup of
G
has order
1,2,3,4,
or
6
, which is equivalent to the condition of
G
having an exponent equal to
1,2,3,4,
or
6
.
∎

If
R
is a field, then
{\sf T}(R^{\times})
has finite exponent if and only if
{\sf T}(R^{\times})
is finite. We conclude this section by presenting two classes of fields illustrating when this phenomenon does and does not occur.

5.3.
Orderable fields

Let
(K,\leq)
be an orderable field. Then

{\sf T}(K^{\times})=\{-1,1\}.

Indeed, if
x>1
, then clearly
x\notin{\sf T}(K^{\times})
. Likewise, if
0<x<1
, then
x\notin{\sf T}(K^{\times})
. Hence, if
x\in{\sf T}(K^{\times})
, then either
x=1
or
x<0
.

Suppose now that
x<0
. If
n
is odd, then
x^{n}<0
, so
x^{n}\neq 1
. Therefore, if
x
has finite order, this order must be even. Hence, there exists
m\geq 1
such that

x^{2m}=1.

Thus
(x^{2})^{m}=1
. Since
x^{2}>0
, the only positive torsion element is
1
, and therefore
x^{2}=1
. Consequently,
x=\pm 1
.

Therefore,
{\sf T}(K^{\times})
is finite, while
K^{\times}
is not. Hence, we obtain the following.

Example 5.6
.

Let
K
be an orderable field. Then
G_{n}({\sf T}(K^{\times}))
satisfies a generalized identity, while
T_{n}(K)
does not.

5.4.
Algebraically closed fields

Let
K
be an algebraically closed field. Then, for every
n\geq 1
, the equation

x^{n}=1

has a solution in
K
. Consequently,
{\sf T}(K^{\times})
contains elements of arbitrarily large order. Therefore

\exp{\sf T}(K^{\times})=\infty.

Hence, we have the following:

Example 5.7
.

Let
K
be an algebraically closed field. Then neither
G_{n}({\sf T}(K^{\times}))
nor
T_{n}(K)
satisfy a generalized identity.

6.
Applications

In
[
1
]
, the authors study the existence of infinite finitely generated solvable groups
G
in the class
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{n}
. They show that no such groups exist when
n
is prime, while examples are constructed when
n
is a square. In
[
2
]
, a family of examples is obtained for every integer
n
divisible by
p^{3}
, for a given prime
p
. We extend these results while imposing stronger structural conditions on the groups.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
p
be a prime and let
n>0
be such that
p^{2}\mid n
. Then there exists an infinite finitely presented metabelian group in
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{n}
.

Proof.

Write
n=p^{2}m
, and let
R=\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{pm}]
be the ring of
pm
-th cyclotomic integers. Let
\zeta_{p}\in R
be a primitive
p
-th root of unity in
\langle\zeta_{pm}\rangle
, and consider

G_{2}(\langle\zeta_{pm}\rangle)=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}d_{1}&x\\
0&d_{2}\end{pmatrix}\in T_{2}(R)\;\middle|\;d_{i}\in\langle\zeta_{pm}\rangle,\;x\in R\right\}.

Then
G_{2}(\langle\zeta_{pm}\rangle)
is an infinite, finitely presented metabelian group. Moreover, since
\zeta_{p}\in\langle\zeta_{pm}\rangle
and
\exp(\langle\zeta_{pm}\rangle)=pm
, Theorem
4.1
applies, and we obtain

G_{2}(\langle\zeta_{pm}\rangle)\in\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{p^{2}m}=\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{n}.

∎

Conjecture 6.2
.

There are no infinite finitely generated solvable groups
G
satisfying a generalized identity of length
n
, where
n
is a square-free integer.

Throughout this paper, we have constructed generalized identities using conjugating elements of finite order. This fact relates to the work of G. J. Sherman
[
17
]
, who studied groups whose torsion subset forms a subgroup. In particular, he proved the following.

Proposition 6.3

(Sherman)
.

If a group
G
contains an element
g
such that

g^{x_{1}}g^{x_{2}}\cdots g^{x_{k}}\neq 1

for every
x_{i}\in{\sf T}(G)
, then
G
is generated by the set of elements of infinite order.

G. J. Sherman asked whether the converse holds. We provide a counterexample to this question.

Theorem 6.4
.

There exists a group
G
generated by elements of infinite order, which satisfies a generalized identity

g^{x_{1}}g^{x_{2}}\cdots g^{x_{k}}=1\quad\text{for all }g\in G,

where each
x_{i}
is a torsion element of
G
.

Proof.

Let
R=\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
. Then
R^{\times}=\{\pm 1\}\times\{\pm 1\}
, so
{\sf T}(R^{\times})=R^{\times}
. By
Theorem B
, the group
T_{2}(R)
satisfies a generalized identity.

More precisely, taking

D=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},

one obtains a generalized identity of the form

w(x)=x^{2}(x^{2})^{D},

where the conjugating element
D
has finite order. Thus
T_{2}(R)
satisfies a generalized identity in which all conjugating elements are torsion.

Now consider the subset

S=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}u&x\\
0&v\end{pmatrix}\in T_{2}(R)\;\middle|\;ux+vx\neq 0\right\}.

We claim that every element of
S
has infinite order. Since
D_{2}(R^{\times})
has exponent
2
and
UT_{2}(R)
is torsion-free, an element
M\in T_{2}(R)
has finite order if and only if
M^{2}=\mathrm{Id}
. For

M=\begin{pmatrix}u&x\\
0&v\end{pmatrix},

one computes

M^{2}=\begin{pmatrix}u^{2}&ux+vx\\
0&v^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

Thus
M^{2}=I
if and only if
ux+vx=0
. By definition of
S
, this never occurs, and hence every element of
S
has infinite order.

We now show that
S
generates
T_{2}(R)
. Every nontrivial unitriangular matrix lies in
S
, so it remains to generate
D_{2}(R^{\times})
.

Let
u,v\in R^{\times}
and choose
y\in R^{\times}\setminus\{-u,-v\}
. Then

\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\
0&vy\end{pmatrix},\qquad\begin{pmatrix}u&y\\
0&y\end{pmatrix}\in S,

and a direct computation shows that

\begin{pmatrix}u&0\\
0&v\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\
0&vy\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u&y\\
0&y\end{pmatrix}.

Thus
D_{2}(R^{\times})\subseteq\langle S\rangle
, and therefore
S
generates
T_{2}(R)
.

Hence
T_{2}(R)
is generated by elements of infinite order and satisfies a generalized identity with torsion conjugating elements.
∎
