Executive Summary
We report on efforts to mitigate the generation of isotopic anomalies during the ablation of
micrometer-sized uranium oxide particles and analysis by MC-ICP-MS. The results of testing on
particles of U200 indicate that laser fluence and frequency can affect isotopic data produced by
laser ablation, but no settings were tested that could eradicate the signal spiking effect and
generation of anomalous isotopic data for 234U/238U and 236U/238U. These anomalies are more
frequent in samples with higher 235U enrichments, which is expected given their higher
abundances of 234U and 236U. Of the standards tested here, only U005-A was anomaly-free. Efforts
to compare the laser traces for particles with normal and anomalous isotopic compositions
showed subtle differences in the behavior of the 234U/238U and 236U/238U ratios. However, it would
be challenging to identify anomalous data points from a population of unknowns using this
distinction, i.e., this is unlikely to be diagnostic. Thus, using current analytical hardware, we cannot
eradicate the signal spiking phenomenon and cannot unambiguously identify isotopic anomalies
from laser ablation traces. Ultimately, laser ablation ICP-MS would either require dramatic
improvement to the ablation process and generation of more homogenous aerosols and/or
improvements to detector electronics to identify and correct for the spiking phenomenon. Even if
the reliability of the technique could be improved, a broader question to address is whether current
data quality is of a high enough standard for laser ablation MC-ICP-MS to be used as a
complementary technique to LG-SIMS for Safeguards.

Introduction
Work performed at LLNL during previous funding cycles demonstrated that laser ablation MC-
ICP-MS can produce rapid, accurate U isotopic data from micrometer-sized particles, and that
this could be highly relevant to support of the IAEA’s Safeguards operations. However, a major
obstacle to the mission readiness of this technique is the observation that, when ablating particles
of known composition, approximately 10% of those particles produce anomalous isotopic data
that cannot easily be identified or corrected for by instrument software. These anomalies arise
from short spikes of signal on instrument ion counters (used for low abundance isotopes such as
234
 U and 236U) that cause shutdowns in the operation of detectors at the micro-second level,
resulting in undercounting of signal and anomalously low 234U/238U and 236U/238U ratios. Given that
a key capability of safeguards operations is being able to identify signatures of undeclared
activities in samples from environmental sampling, the generation of such isotopic anomalies
cannot be tolerated from a mission perspective. In this end of year report, we document efforts to
better understand the prevalence of signal spiking during particle ablation with a focus on the
effects of laser fluence and frequency on the generation of anomalous data. We also determine
whether the generation of isotopic anomalies are tied to sample composition (i.e., uranium
enrichment factor) and the relative abundance of minor isotopes 234U and 236U. Finally, we
evaluate whether, with current technology, the spiking effect can be eradicated or if more work is
needed and the implications of this for the future use of laser ablation MC-ICP-MS for Safeguards.

Uranium Isotopic Analyses of Particle Reference Materials
Instrument setup
Setup and testing of the laser ablation MC-ICP-MS system at LLNL has been documented at
length elsewhere (Wimpenny et al., 2021; 2023a, b). Testing performed during FY25 involved the
same laser system (Photon Machines 193nm excimer) and MC-ICP-MS (Thermo Scientific
Neptune with ion counting package) as in previous studies.

The Neptune was tested and calibrated in solution mode prior to the start of the laser ablation
sessions to ensure robust performance. During each session, the instrument was tuned to
maximum sensitivity using the NIST 610 glass standard with ~500 ppm U. Using a 50-micrometer
spot size, 5mJ energy and 7Hz frequency intensities of 0.2-0.3V of 238U were obtained in low
resolution mode.

During the laser ablation analyses, the mass bias and ion counter gains was corrected for by
ablation of the in-house uranium glass CAS-53-500, which has sub equal amounts of 235U and
238
 U and well characterized minor isotope abundances (see Wimpenny et al., 2023a). All isotopic
data was reduced using the LARA software package (v 3.0) developed in R.

Test Materials
Previous isotopic analyses had shown clear evidence of signal spiking during the analysis of the
NBL reference material U200, whereas no spiking was observed during analyses of depleted
uranium from Savannah River National Laboratory (SRNL) (Wimpenny et al., 2023a). Based on
these observations, U200 was selected as the ideal test material to assess the importance of
laser parameters on the prevalence of signal spiking. After this initial testing, the goal was to
assess whether the frequency of anomalies is directly proportional to the uranium isotopic
composition of the samples. To this end, we selected a series of reference materials produced by
New Brunswick Laboratory (NBL) with known isotopic compositions of uranium and a range of
234
 U and 236U abundances (Table 1). These were U005-A, U050, U100 and U200 that came in the
form of oxide powders that were dispersed onto silicon in ethanol. In addition, we also analyzed
a sample of U020 that was provided as part of a suite of dynamic range samples supplied by
SRNL in FY23.

Experimental design
Anomalous data caused by signal spiking is a result of two problems that occur during ablation of
particles and collection of data. The first is the handling of signal spikes by the mass spectrometer
electronics and front-end software. The spikes are of sufficient magnitude to saturate the ion
counters, which results in undercounting (or suppression) of the ion beam. In addition to detector
saturation, the spiking also results in shutdowns of the detector at the microsecond scale which
exacerbates the signal suppression. A clear example of this is documented in Wimpenny et al.,
(2023a), where a subset of measured particles of U200 had anomalously low 234U/238U and
236
 U/238U ratios consistent with suppression of >80% of the 234U and 236U signal. One method to
correct spiking and suppression is to modify the instrument electronics and data processing
software to record the magnitude of spikes at much higher resolution, but this type of development
work is well outside of the scope of the current project.

Figure 1. Schematic showing good vs bad ablation. On the left-hand side, the ablation produces a

The second problem relates to the generation of the sample aerosol during the laser ablation
process (Fig. 1). The particle size in the aerosol plume should be as close to homogenous as
possible to ensure signal stability, similar to aerosol generated during solution mode ICP-MS
measurements. However, depending on the sample substrate, it is likely that some degree of
particle size heterogeneity is inherent to aerosol generated during laser ablation. During ablation
of glasses and homogenous solids it is possible to ablate the sample over a period of tens of
seconds or minutes, which allows any initial instability to be ignored and final data to be integrated
over the most stable period of the trace. As a consequence, although signal spiking may still
occur, it has little to no impact on the final data. In contrast, during particle analysis only seconds
of data are generated, meaning the entire laser ablation trace must be integrated to calculate final
isotope ratios. Thus, signal spiking, particularly during the onset of the aerosol hitting the plasma,
will have a much bigger effect on final isotope ratios. It is unclear whether complete homogeneity
of the plume can be achieved from such transient signals using current laser ablation hardware.
However, it will be possible to determine whether changes to laser ablation parameters can result
in an improved sample aerosol and reduction in the proportion of anomalous data points. If so,
these observations may help us to better understand how the ablation process could be improved
in the future.

Micrometer-sized particles are typically consumed within a few seconds, depending on the
fluence used during ablation and frequency of the laser pulses. During the ablation of solid
materials such as glass or silicate minerals, there is an optimum laser fluence at which the signal
is relatively stable and, during depth profiling, the signal decays relatively slowly. There is also a
threshold level, at which the laser will not couple effectively to the sample surface, usually < 1
J/cm2, depending on the substrate. Whether an optimum fluence also exists for micrometer sized
particles, for which the laser spot size is much larger than the sample, is unclear. To address this
uncertainty, we have tested the ablation of U200 under laser fluences of 0.3 to 2.1 J/cm 2 to
constrain whether the fluence affects the frequency and size of the anomalies in 234U/238U and
236
 U/238U. Laser frequency also affects the stability of the signal generated during traditional
ablation, but it’s not clear whether it also affects the homogeneity of the plume or would be an

generation of anomalous data changed over a range of frequencies from 3 to 100 Hz.

Results of Laser Ablation Optimization
Laser fluence
Isotopic analyses were performed with laser fluence between 0.34 and 2 J/cm2 and a frequency
of 3Hz, and between 0.5 and 1.6 J/cm2 with a frequency of 25 Hz (see Table 2, Figure 2). When
plotting 234U/238U vs 236U/238U, we observe a clear difference in the number of anomalous data
points, the extent of these anomalies and the spread of data as a function of fluence. As shown
in Fig. 2a and b, the majority of uranium isotopic data obtained with fluences <1 J/cm2 plot near
to the certified composition of U200, with a subset (~10%) being significantly offset towards the
origin, consistent with spiking of signals and shutdowns of the 234U and 236U ion counters. In
contrast, at higher fluences the data are spread along a line towards the origin (Fig. 2 c, d)
indicating that signal spiking is more prevalent within the particle population, but the magnitude
of signal suppression for 234U and 236U is lower.

 9

Figure 2. 234U/238U vs 236U/238U ratios for particles of the U200 reference material plotted as a function of
laser fluence. All analyses were performed with a frequency of 3Hz. The certified composition of U200 is
provided for comparison (Richter et al., 2011).

The cause of this difference cannot be ascertained from the current study. We hypothesize that it
stems from differences in the coupling of the laser energy with the uranium particle with laser
fluence. At fluences <1 J/cm2, the relatively low energies produce uranium intensities that are
relatively low, ~30mV maximum for 238U. In this case, any small heterogeneities in the plume
produce small spikes of signal for the minor isotopes that are too small to saturate the detectors.
Hence the majority of particles produce 234U/238U and 236U/238U ratios that are consistent with the
certified values. However, in a subset of particles the low energies do not couple well with the
uranium particles, causing fracturing and the generation of large fragments that enter the plasma.
These result in large over saturation ‘events’ that result in prolonged shutdowns of the affected
ICs and significant anomalies in the 234U/238U and 236U/238U ratios reflecting suppression of over

 Document Name 10

80% of the 234U and 236U. At higher fluences, the coupling of the laser with the uranium particles
is more consistent and generates larger 238U signals of >100mV. Due to higher signal intensities,
we hypothesize that saturation of the ion counters occurs with greater frequency, resulting in an
increased prevalence of signal spiking and more particles that are affected by suppression of 234U
and 236U. At higher fluence, the fracturing and generation of large particle fragments does not
occur, so the more extreme suppression of 234U and 236U signal is less likely to be observed.

Laser Frequency
A series of analyses were performed with a constant fluence (0.8 J/cm2) and variations in laser
frequency from 3 to 100 Hz. These analyses yielded similar results (Fig. 3), albeit with a slightly
greater spread of isotopic compositions at 3Hz. The similarity in data from 25Hz to 100Hz
indicates that increasing the frequency >25Hz had minimal effect on the proportion of anomalous
data. However, we note that testing at 100 Hz was performed on a smaller subset of samples (n
= 28), so continued testing at this higher frequency would be required to confirm this hypothesis.
Furthermore, newer commercial laser ablation systems are capable of achieving frequencies up
to 1KHz. More testing would be required to ascertain whether such high frequency could be
beneficial in creating a more homogeneous sample aerosols.

Preferred laser parameters
Based on testing on U200, our preferred laser ablation parameters are relatively low fluence (0.8
J/cm2) and high frequency (25 µm) as these generate a better constrained population of particles
that are more consistent with the published composition of U200. That being said, all laser
parameters tested here generate similar data; the majority of data points being broadly consistent
with reference values for U200, with a subset of anomalous data at the ~10% level. No laser
parameters were tested that prevented the generation of anomalous 234U/238U and 236U/238U ratios
during particle ablation.

Uranium Isotopic Composition of NBL Reference Materials
Isotopic analyses were performed on uranium particles with a range of isotopic compositions
(listed in Table 1 and illustrated in Fig. 4). We aimed to obtain populations of at least 100 particles
per standard to obtain meaningful information about the relationship between composition and
signal spiking. Results show that uranium samples with higher 235U enrichment factors and minor
isotope abundances had a higher frequency of anomalous isotopic data for 234U/238U and
236
 U/238U. The proportion of anomalous data points ranges from 0 to 7%, with the largest fraction
observed for U200 and no anomalous data produced for U005A. This is consistent with previous
published work (Wimpenny et al., 2023a) that compared the prevalence of anomalous data for
U200 and depleted uranium derived from Savannah River National Laboratory (SRNL-DU).
Particles with higher minor isotope abundances tend to generate higher count rates on the ion
counters. In these samples, the fracturing of particles during ablation are more likely to cause
detector saturation and shutdown, resulting in more particles with undercounted 234U and 236U.
Typical uncertainties (1σ) for the 235U/238U ratio in individual particles ranges from 1% for U200 to
40% for U005A. For context, uncertainties in the 235U/238U ratio of individual particles of UPu-100A
(~5% 235U) measured by LG-SIMS were ~0.2% (Groopman et al., 2025; Foley et al., 2025). The
variation in precision of the laser ablation data is a simple reflection of the 235U content of the
sample and measured intensities of 235U on the Faraday detector; for U005A the ablation of each
particle generated 235U intensities up to 2-3 mV, which is only just above the detector background.
In contrast 235U intensities for U100 and U200 could be >100 mV. Typical internal uncertainties

for 234U/238U and 236U/238U ranged from <1% in U200 to 5-10% in U005-A, which scales with minor
isotope intensities on the ion counters. In comparison, precision on the 234U/238U and 236U/238U
ratios of particles of UPu-100A were typically 0.6% and 5% respectively by LG-SIMS (Groopman
et al., 2025; Foley et al., 2025). We note that direct comparison between the two instruments will
depend on the isotopic composition of the samples being analyzed. The reference material UPu-
100A has a 236U/238U ratio of ~2×10-5, which is most comparable to U005-A in the current study.

One complication in linking the isotopic composition of particles to the generation of anomalies is
particle size; ideally all particles tested would all be close to 1 micrometer in diameter, similar to
samples synthesized for laser ablation and SIMS analyses by SRNL (and used here for U020)
that have measured particle size distributions of 1 ± 0.1 µm. However, without such specialized

 13

synthesis procedures this degree of particle size homogeneity cannot be attained. The particle
sizes of the standards used here were not characterized prior to ablation but based on observation
spanned a wide range of sizes up to tens of µm. To ensure that the ablated particles were on the
order of 1 µm the ablation process was performed manually, rather than via rastering. Even so, it
is likely that particle sizes of 1-5 micrometers were ablated for U005A, U050 and U200. For U100,
the particle sizes were typically larger (5-10 micrometers) and generated signal intensities of up
to 3V for 238U. As shown in Fig. 5, the ablation of U100 particles yielded a higher proportion of
anomalous data, potentially due to larger particle sizes and larger mass of material being
aerosolized during ablation. The 236U/238U (or 234U/238U) data population does not correlate with
235
 U/238U, which is strong evidence that the anomalies are analytical in origin rather than
representing compositional heterogeneity within the sample. One unresolved question is why the
234
 U/238U vs 236U/238U data do not trend back to zero if the anomalies were generated by detector
shutdowns, but we are unable to address this in the current study.

Analysis of mixed uranium-plutonium particles
In addition to the uranium reference materials, we also analyzed mixed U-Pu particles (so called
‘dynamic range’ samples) that were originally synthesized by SRNL to measure Pu isotope ratios
by laser ablation MC-ICP-MS (see Wimpenny et al., 2023b). Particle synthesis involved the use
of an aerosol-based process to monodisperse U-Pu particles with a controllable particle size and
chemical composition. Particles have well characterized sizes (~1 µm) and densities (~8.3 g cm-
3
 ) and an estimated 103-105 particles were deposited onto each silicon substrate. The particles
were comprised of U-oxide (U3O8) and the concentration of Pu in each particle population ranged
from 0 to 1000 ppm. Final results of uranium isotopic analyses are presented in Table 3. As shown,
the measured uranium isotopic compositions of the mixed U-Pu particles are consistent with the
certified composition of U020. These compositions are independent of the Pu concentration,
indicating that, up to a concentration of 1000 ppm, the plutonium concentration in a uranium oxide
particle has no effect on the measured uranium composition. Internal uncertainties are ~10% (1σ)
for 235U/238U and 2-3% for 234U/238U and 236U/238U. The slightly larger uncertainty for 235U/238U
reflects the relatively low abundance of 235U in U020 and the fact that 235U intensities ae close to
the detector background.

If the 234U/238U is plotted vs 236U/238U for the dynamic range samples (Figure 6) we also observe
anomalies in the particle population. These mixed U-Pu samples were synthesized from U020,
so the frequency of anomalous data caused by signal spiking was relatively low (<5%). We note
data points with anomalously high minor isotope ratios in 30396 (Figure 6d), the cause of which
is unclear, but is unlikely to be related to the presence of Pu in the sample, given the homogeneity
of these samples.

Comparing ‘normal’ and anomalous particle data
There is no clear difference between normal and anomalous particle traces from simply studying
the measured intensities, either the 238U signal on Faraday detector or the intensity of 234U or 236U
on ion counters. As previously described, the spikes in signal are too short (on the order of
microseconds) to be visible in individual millisecond-level integrations. Instead, we have
investigated whether the signal suppression during spiking events is visible in the 236U/238U (or
234
 U/238U) ratio at the individual integration level. The theory here is relatively straightforward; one
would expect samples suffering from signal spikes to have anomalously low 236U (or 234U)
intensities relative to 238U in integrations that have been affected by signal spiking. If so, there
could be a systematic difference to the laser trace of a particle that generated an anomalous
isotopic composition to one that was not affected by signal spiking. To investigate this, laser traces
were plotted from particles that give 236U/238U ratios that are consistent with reference values (here
termed ‘normal’) and particles that show evidence of 236U suppression (termed ‘anomalous’)
during ablation of U020, U050 and U200 (Fig. 7).

Results show that there is a subtle difference between laser traces that generate normal and
anomalous 236U/238U ratios and that this is visible in each of the three uranium standards. Both
normal and anomalous traces are characterized by an initial spike in the 236U/238U ratio as the first
signal arrives at the detectors. A normal particle trace is characterized by the 236U/238U ratio
decreasing to a semi-stable value, whereas anomalous traces have 236U/238U ratios that drop
significantly after the initial spike and increase again towards the end of the ablation (Fig. 7b, d,
f). This decrease in the measured 236U/238U ratio is consistent with 236U suppression due to signal
spiking. Although the identification of such traces could be used to identify anomalous particle
data, whether this could be diagnostic in unknowns is unclear. Even in ‘normal’ traces, there are
significant fluctuations in the 236U/238U ratio, which could be mistaken for anomalous behavior.
Similarly, signal spiking and detector suppression can take throughout the data collection period,
meaning it doesn’t necessarily affect the same part of the trace every single time. Furthermore,
the dip in 236U/238U in anomalous particles is only apparent relative to the ‘normal’ particles from
the same standard population. In operational samples, the appearance of a ‘normal’ benchmark
trace would not exist, meaning there is no context within which to judge a particle as anomalous.
Thus, without more conclusive evidence it is unlikely that the 236U/238U (or 234U/238U) traces
generated during particle ablation could be used to unambiguously determine whether a single
data point suffers from signal spiking or not. Of course, we cannot rule out that the development
of other proxies might help in identification of signal spiking in the future.

Summary and Outlook for Laser Ablation ICP-MS for
Safeguards
Laser ablation MC-ICP-MS is a rapid technique that can be used to obtain isotopic data for
uranium in micron sized uranium oxide particles. However, given the fact that it consumes
particles at the micrometer scale, it needs to be highly robust, i.e., we need confidence in the

results as it is not possible to confirm anomalous data by this or any other technique. Thus, signal
spiking and the resulting undercounting of minor isotope data measured using ion counters is a
fundamental obstacle to laser ablation MC-ICP-MS being adopted as an operational technique
that complements existing methods (LG-SIMS and ft-TIMS). The testing performed here indicates
that the laser ablation parameters used to analyse micrometer-sized particles do affect the
aerosol generated during ablation but cannot be tuned to eradicate the signal spiking
phenomenon. Sample composition is also important because minor isotope abundances are
typically higher (and so count rates closer to saturation) at higher 235U enrichment levels. As such,
we observed a higher proportion of anomalous data in standards with higher 235U enrichment
factors and no anomalous data in particles of the depleted uranium standard U005-A. However,
given that operational samples may have a wide range of uranium isotopic compositions, this
observation is not helpful from a Safeguards perspective. Ultimately, we show that changes in
ablation characteristics using current technology cannot improve the reliability of laser ablation
MC-ICP-MS data. Development work focused on the detector electronics, where signals are
processed, will be required to improve this technique for Safeguards. Although there has been
some promising early development work to observe and mitigate the signal spiking phenomenon
at the instrument detectors, more effort would be required to develop and test technology to
overcome this problem. Whether or not this should be pursued will depend on whether the laser
ablation data that is not affected by signal spiking (some 90%+ of all particle data) is of a high
enough quality to be useful for Safeguards. It is clear that laser ablation MC-ICP-MS cannot match
LG-SIMS for precision when analyzing individual uranium oxide particles. However, it could play
a role as a screening tool for triage prior to selection of samples for further analysis.
