Executive Summary
The Glen Canyon Dam (GCD) is a Colorado River Storage Project (CRSP) power resource that
is a component of the Salt Lake City Area Integrated Projects (SLCA/IP). The 2016 record
of decision (ROD) for the GCD long-term experimental and management plan (LTEMP) final
Environmental Impact Statement (EIS) specifies criteria for GCD monthly water releases, daily
and hourly operating limits, and experimental releases.
 This report presents a financial analysis of the Smallmouth bass (Micropterus dolomieu)
(SMB) flows implemented at GCD during Calendar Year (CY) 2025. These bypass flows were
introduced by the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation (USBR) as an emergency response to the growing
threat posed by invasive SMB in the Colorado River ecosystem downstream of the dam.
 SMB are a non-native predatory species that pose a significant threat to native fish popula-
tions, including the endangered humpback chub (Gila cypha). The thermal regime below GCD,
typically cold due to hypolimnetic releases from Lake Powell, has historically served as a thermal
barrier limiting SMB establishment. However, persistently low reservoir levels in recent years
have resulted in warmer epilimnetic releases creating conditions below GCD that may allow for
establishment of SMB, prompting urgent ecological concerns.
 To mitigate the risk of SMB establishment, the USBR implemented a series of bypass flows in
2024 and 2025. Drawn from a lower elevation than the penstocks, the bypass structures released
cooler water downstream. These short-duration bypass flows aimed to keep temperatures cool
enough to prevent SMB from spawning, thereby reducing the ecological threat posed by this
invasive species.
 Although motivated by ecological objectives, these bypass flows came with financial trade-
offs. Releasing water through the bypass structures instead of the turbines at GCD reduced
hydropower generation and required Western Area Power Administration (WAPA) to purchase
replacement energy to fulfill customer commitments. The cost of these replacement purchases is
funded by the CRSP Basin Fund and is considered nonreimbursable (i.e., they are not collected
through WAPA’s wholesale rate).
 This report analyzes the financial impact of the SMB flows implemented from August to
October 2025. The experimental releases resulted in an estimated cost of approximately $6.12
million, driven primarily by the replacement-power purchases required to offset the generation
loss due to the substantial volume of water bypassing the generators. This study applies an
integrated set of tools to estimate WAPA financial impacts by simulating GCD under two types
of cases; namely, (1) a “With Experiment” case that mimics the water operations that actually
occurred, including the SMB bypass flows, and (2) a “Without Experiment” case that simulates
operations under the assumption that the SMB flows did not occur. Both cases comply with
LTEMP hourly and daily operating criteria, and the monthly water release volumes are assumed
to be identical under both cases.
 The Colorado River Storage Project Python-based model (CRiSPPy) model [1] was the main
modeling tool used to simulate the dispatch of the GCD hydropower plant and associated water
releases from Lake Powell. In the modeling process, the research team used extensive data sets
and historical information on SLCA/IP power plant characteristics, hydrologic conditions, and
WAPA’s power purchases and sales prices.

 VI

1 Introduction
The GCD Powerplant (referred to as the Powerplant in this report) consists of eight generating
units with a continuous operating capacity of 1,320 megawatts (MW) at unity power factor. It
is one component of a larger system known as the SLCA/IP. Electricity produced by GCD is
marketed by WAPA and helps serve the demand of 5.8 million consumers in 10 states that are
located in the Western Interconnection (WI). Before 1990, GCD had few operating restrictions.
Except for a minimum water release requirement, the daily and hourly operations of the Pow-
erplant were initially constrained only by the physical limitations of the dam structures and
its water storage reservoir, Lake Powell. CRSP Firm Electric Service (FES) customer loads
and market price signals were the principal dispatch drivers. When operations are unrestricted,
these drivers often result in large fluctuations of the plant’s power output and associated water
releases.
 Concerns about the impact of GCD operations on downstream ecosystems and endangered
species, including those in Grand Canyon National Park, prompted the USBR to conduct a
series of research releases from June 1990 to July 1991 as part of an environmental studies
program. Based on an analysis of these releases, USBR imposed operational flow constraints on
August 1, 1991 [2]. These constraints were in effect until February 1997, when new operational
rules and management goals specified in the GCD EIS ROD were adopted [3]. More recently, in
December 2016, a new ROD mandating the preferred alternative prescribed by the LTEMP EIS
has been adopted [4]. The 2016 ROD operating criteria shape monthly water release volumes
from Lake Powell and limit hourly average maximum and minimum water release rates. The
2016 ROD criteria also constrain the change in water release between consecutive hours and
restrict the range of hourly releases on a rolling 24-hour (hr) basis.
 Recent prolonged periods of drought and low-runoff conditions in the Colorado River Basin
have caused the water elevation at Lake Powell to decline. The decrease in water elevation has
made it more likely for nonnative fish in Lake Powell to become entrained, passing through the
dam and downstream into the Colorado River. In addition, as Lake Powell elevations decline,
warmer water is discharged, which may create suitable conditions for warmwater nonnative
fish, including SMB, to reproduce and eventually establish populations. This is a concern
because SMB and other predatory invasive fish pose a threat to federally listed fish species
and other native fish downstream of GCD. As a result, in February 2023, USBR released a
draft Environmental Assessment (EA) entitled GCD/ SMB Flow Options [5]. The draft EA
identifies and analyzes operational alternatives at GCD that may serve to disrupt spawning
of SMB and other warmwater invasive fish. Based on the EA analysis, USBR concluded that
additional analysis was warranted. In October 2023, USBR issued a notice of intent to prepare a
Supplemental EIS (SEIS) for the 2016 ROD [6]. The aim of the SEIS is to analyze flow options
to prevent SMB and other warmwater invasive nonnative fish from establishing below GCD
(by preventing additional spawning) and to analyze new information regarding the sediment
accounting window associated with the LTEMP high flow experiment (HFE) protocol. For
the LTEMP SEIS scoping process, five flow options were being considered by USBR, and the
proposed duration of the flow options would potentially run through 2027. Four out of the five
flow options consider routing a significant portion of the water through GCD bypass tubes in
order to achieve lower water temperature, as the bypass pipes draw colder water from a deeper
elevation in the reservoir relative to the turbine intake. Although beneficial to disrupt the
spawning of warmwater invasive fish, these bypass water releases do not produce electricity and

 1

thus result in a loss of power generation and in a financial cost for WAPA. In addition, these
bypass releases further restrict operators’ ability to respond to market signal and customer loads,
which results in additional financial costs.
 In July 2024, the USBR released the Final GCD LTEMP SEIS and ROD [7]. The SEIS
evaluated six operational alternatives, ultimately selecting the “Cool Mix” flow regime for im-
plementation in 2024. In 2024, Cool Mix flows would be initiated when the average daily water
temperature at River Mile (RM) 61 exceeded 15.5 °C (60 °F) for three consecutive days. In 2025,
USBR implemented a modified Cool Mix flow regime at GCD. Unlike the 2024 implementation,
which targeted water temperatures at RM 61, the 2025 operation used a less aggressive tem-
perature target at RM 30, started later, ended earlier, and was designed to reduce hydropower
impacts while continuing to mitigate SMB establishment downstream of the dam. Cool Mix
flow operations occurred intermittently from August through October 2025, involving the use
of bypass releases at GCD to help reduce downstream water temperatures.
 This report analyzes the financial impact of the loss in power generation at GCD during
the CY 2025 due to the implementation of the adjusted SMB Cool Mix flows. The CRiSPPy
model [1] was used to estimate the financial impacts of the SMB flows by simulating GCD
operations under two cases; namely, (1) a “With Experiment” case that mimics the operations
that actually occurred and (2) a “Without Experiment” case that simulated operations under
the assumption that the SMB flows did not occur. The financial cost of the 2025 SMB flows is
calculated as the difference in WAPA’s financial position between the two cases. GCD monthly
water release volume decisions are assumed to be identical under both cases. As a result,
the analysis focuses on the period from August to October 2025, when bypass flow operations
occurred. In both cases, GCD operations are assumed to comply with LTEMP hourly and daily
operating criteria.
 This report is the second in a series analyzing the financial impacts of SMB flows at GCD,
and follows the 2024 SMB report [8].

2 Glen Canyon Dam operating rules
The 2016 LTEMP ROD describes the operating constraints at GCD. These include (1) the
allocation of monthly Lake Powell water releases, (2) daily and hourly operating criteria, and
(3) experiments that are periodically conducted at GCD.

2.1 Hourly and Daily Operating Criteria and Exceptions
Operating criteria specified in the 2016 ROD are intended to temper the rate of change in
hourly and daily water releases. The criteria selected were based on the LTEMP Alternative as
described in the final ROD [4]. These criteria were put into practice by WAPA from October
2017.
 Flow restrictions under the 2016 ROD are shown in Table 2.1. For comparison purposes,
they are represented along with operational limits in effect between February 1997 and October
2017 that were complying with the 1996 ROD, and operational limits prior to June 1, 1991. The
2016 ROD criteria require water release rates to be 8,000 cubic feet per second (cfs) or greater
between the hours of 7:00 a.m. and 7:00 p.m. and at least 5,000 cfs at night. The criteria also
limit how quickly the release rate can increase and decrease in consecutive hours. The maximum
hourly increase (i.e., the up-ramp rate) is 4,000 cfs/hr, and the maximum hourly decrease (i.e.,
the down-ramp rate) is 2,500 cfs/hr.
 The 2016 ROD operating criteria also restrict the range of release rates during rolling 24-
hr periods. This change constraint depends on the monthly volume of water releases. Daily
fluctuation (in cfs/24-hr) is limited to 10 times the monthly release specified in thousands of
acre-feet (TAF) from June to August. From September to May, it is limited to 9 times the
monthly release volume. The daily range can never exceed 8,000 cfs.
 The maximum flow rate is limited to 25,000 cfs under the 2016 ROD operating criteria. It is,
however, allowed to exceed this limit in order to avoid spills or flood releases during high-runoff
periods. Under very wet hydrological conditions, defined as when the average monthly release
rate is greater than 25,000 cfs, the flow rate may also be exceeded; however, water must be
released at a constant rate. Exceptions to the operating criteria are also made to accommodate
experimental releases.
 In addition to the 2016 ROD operating rules, WAPA follows other scheduling practices.
These practices fall within ROD operational boundaries but are not ROD requirements. One
practice involves releasing the same volume of water during weekdays. It also requires weekend
and holiday release volumes to be no larger than weekday release volumes but not smaller than
85 percent of weekday release volumes.

factors [9]. Release decisions are made based on simulation results from the Colorado River
Simulation System (CRSS) or Colorado River Mid-term Modeling System (CRMMS) models

2.3 Scheduling guidelines
The hourly scheduling of SLCA/IP hydropower plant operations is performed by WAPA’s En-
ergy Management and Marketing Office (EMMO) located in Montrose, Colorado. Schedulers
make decisions based on a set of scheduling priorities and guidelines, including a directive to
comply with several reservoir-specific environmental operating criteria.
 As operational constraints were imposed on SLCA/IP resources, scheduling guidelines and
goals shifted from objectives driven primarily by market prices to objectives driven by customer
loads. Within the boundaries of these operating constraints, SLCA/IP power resources are used
to serve firm load. Because of operational limitations, WAPA staff may need either to purchase
or sell varying amounts of energy on an hourly basis in the day-ahead and/or real-time market.
In the past, the volume of these variable market purchases and sales used to be relatively small.
However, evolving energy market and dry conditions led to more significant transactions. As
a result, in December 2021, WAPA implemented the Deliverable Sales Amount (DSA) [14].
The DSA determines each customer’s quarterly scheduling rights and delivery entitlements for
hydropower capacity and energy, based on the forecasted SLCA/IP generation. Note that,
unlike capacity products in organized wholesale markets (e.g., PJM’s Reliability Pricing Mode),
DSA capacity represents an allocated share of available hydropower generation rather than a
standalone resource adequacy commitment.
 The load-following objective and DSA rules create a strong link between WAPA’s contrac-
tual obligations and SLCA/IP hydropower plant operations, requiring dispatch among the hy-
dropower plants to be closely coordinated. This interdependency exists because loads and
hydropower resources are balanced whenever feasible. WAPA is able to affect the shape of its
FES customer load requests indirectly through specifications in its contract amendments. In
turn, these customer loads affect both SLCA/IP powerplant operations and hourly reservoir
releases. The load-following dispatch directive minimizes scheduling problems and helps WAPA
avoid noncompliant water releases.
 An unwritten scheduling practice followed by WAPA involves maintaining a number of ramp-
ing cycles that is consistent with customer load patterns but not greater than two. This practice
applies to all CRSP plants and is mainly followed to minimize turbine wear and tear.

3 Experimental release description
Between August and October 2025, USBR implemented modified Cool Mix flows at GCD to
mitigate the establishment of invasive SMB in the Colorado River downstream of the dam [15].
These actions were guided by the 2024 ROD and the GCD LTEMP SEIS, which authorized SMB
flows during 2024–2027 if conditions warranted [7]. In 2025, USBR implemented an adjusted
Cool Mix regime that built on the 2024 experiment while reducing hydropower impacts. The
primary goal of the experiment was to reduce SMB reproduction and survival by disrupting
their preferred spawning and rearing conditions through targeted releases of colder water from
the river outlet works, blended with warmer hydropower releases through the penstocks.
 Unlike the 2024 implementation, which applied the temperature target at RM 61, the 2025
experiment applied the same target at RM 30, reducing the volume of bypassed water needed
due to the shorter distance over which downstream warming could occur. The experiment was
triggered when average daily water temperatures at RM 30 exceeded 15.5 ◦ C, or 60 ◦ F, for three
consecutive days. USBR initiated the 2025 flows on August 3, paused them on August 5 due
to changes in hydrology and observed river temperatures, and implemented the flows in earnest
beginning August 12 [15]. The flows concluded on October 20, 2025, and GCD returned to
normal operations on October 21, 2025.
 To reduce experimental costs, USBR coordinated with WAPA to reduce hydropower impacts
while maintaining the ecological objectives of the experiment. Compared with the 2024 Cool
Mix implementation, the 2025 operation targeted a shorter downstream reach, started later,
ended earlier, and was designed to require substantially less bypass volume. Specifically, the
target location was moved from RM 61 to RM 30, and the 2025 operation was expected to bypass
less than one quarter of the water bypassed during the previous year’s experiment [15]. Note
that the total water releases (i.e., combined turbine and bypass releases) adhered to LTEMP
constraints. The hydrograph of the 2025 SMB bypass flows is depicted in Figure 3.1.

4 Methodology
4.1 Modeling tool
The 2025 SMB flow analysis was performed using the CRiSPPy model [1]. The CRiSPPy model
is a Python-based hydropower scheduling model specifically designed and developed for WAPA
CRSP. It uses advanced machine learning (ML) techniques to automatically update the physical
rules of SLCA/IP reservoirs based on historical data. It also uses state-of-the-art optimization
techniques to rapidly identify optimal hydropower operations of the system [1]. The model
computes optimal hourly water release schedules driven by customer loads and energy prices
while taking into account monthly release targets and physical and operational rules. Users can
also manually fix specific hourly power and non-power releases for certain plants. The model
operates at an hourly level, simulating power and non-power release schedules, power generation,
reservoir storage volume and forebay elevation, as well as the system’s day-ahead and real-time
energy transactions.

4.2 Study scope and modeling cases
Monthly release volumes at GCD during CY 2025 are assumed to remain unchanged regardless
of whether the SMB flows took place. Consequently, the financial impact of the 2025 SMB flows
is confined to the months in which the bypass flows occurred (August to October 2025). As
a result, the analysis time scope is limited to those months. Other SLCA/IP resources were
required to compensate for the reduced operational flexibility of the GCD during the SMB flows.
As a result, all SLCA/IP resources (including Flaming Gorge, Blue Mesa, Morrow Point, and
Crystal) were being modeled in the analysis.
 For the 2025 SMB flows analysis, financial impacts were calculated by comparing simulated
results between two cases: (1) a “With Experiment” case that mimics the operations that
actually occurred and (2) a “Without Experiment” case that simulated operations under the
assumption that the SMB bypass flows did not occur.
 In the “Without Experiment” case, it is assumed that the SMB flows did not occur, and
the release schedule of all SLCA/IP resources, including GCD, is optimized for the entire study
period. Releases at GCD are optimized by the CRiSPPy model following the operating rules
outlined in section 2. Releases at the other SLCA/IP resources are optimized according to their
respective RODs; for example, Flaming Gorge adheres to its minimum flow requirements and
downstream flow constraints, both of which are embedded in the CRiSPPy model.
 In contrast, the “With Experiment” case assumes the SMB flows did occur. The hourly
non-power (i.e., bypass) release rates at GCD are fixed during the entire study period and set
equal to their historical values, reflecting the actual SMB flow pattern. Total (i.e., turbine and
bypass) GCD releases are optimized following the operating rules outlined in section 2. To
isolate the effect of the SMB flows on GCD, the release schedules of all other SLCA/IP plants
are held fixed to the values obtained in the “Without Experiment” case.

4.3 Input data
Hydrology data for the reservoirs and power plants of the SLCA/IP system are based on his-
torical monthly data from the USBR hydrologic database [13]. This information is used by the
CRiSPPy model to calculate model parameters such as water release volumes, water-to-power
conversion factor (PCF), power capacities, and maximum daily flow fluctuations. Parameters
such as PCFs are calculated by CRiSPPy using embedded pre-trained ML models. More details
can be found in the CRiSPPy report [1].
 Hourly non-power water release data at GCD are imported from the USBR hydrologic
database [13] and held fixed in the “With Experiment” case.
 Outage data are based on planned outage operations files called “CRSP and LAP Unit
Availability” and provided by EMMO. This information is used by the CRiSPPy model to
calculate adjusted hourly plant capacity.
 Load data are based on pre-scheduled operations files called “Load and Resource” and pro-
vided by EMMO. For the sake of simplicity, customer load is aggregated with other types of
loads to represent the total amount of energy withdrawn from the system (Customer AHP load,
Miscellaneous load, Pump operations at Deer Creek, Transmission losses, and SLCA/IP system
energy exchanges into and out of the system).
 Although the power generation of the main hydropower plants (GCD, Flaming Gorge, As-
pinall, Fontenelle) is optimized by the CRiSPPy model, generation from smaller units within
the CRSP footprint is considered fixed and imported from the pre-scheduled operations files
provided by EMMO. These smaller units include Upper and Lower Molina, Deer Creek, and
Energy interchange into the SLCA/IP system.
 Energy price data are based on historical hourly energy transactions records called “Daily
Deals Reports” and provided by EMMO. For a given hour, the energy price is calculated as the
energy-weighted average of pre-scheduled and real-time energy transaction prices.

4.4 Calculation of financial costs
The financial cost of the SMB flows is calculated as the difference in WAPA’s financial position
between the “With Experiment” and “Without Experiment” cases. For each case, the financial
position in a given hour is computed as the product of the total CRSP generation during that
hour and the corresponding energy price. Note that, based on the modeling assumptions, the
energy price remains the same under both cases. In addition, the generation profile of all power
plants except GCD is also identical under both cases. As a result, the financial cost in a given
hour can be expressed as the product of the difference in generation at GCD between the two
cases and the corresponding energy price. In other words, the financial cost of the experimental
release at GCD can be expressed as:

5.1 Operational results
The optimal water and water operations are computed with the CRiSPPy model under the

with historical water operations, 378 TAF of water was bypassed at GCD due to the SMB flows
in the “With Experiment” scenario. This volume represents 23.8% of the total water released
during the experiment period, from August 3rd through October 20th , and 21.0% of the total
water released from August 1st through October 31st , 2025 (Figure 5.1).
