Influenza vaccination associates with reduced risk of incident ischemic stroke among older adults with diabetes: A population-based retrospective cohort study
Authors: Tianchi Yang, Xingqiu Ying, Hanwen Yang, Xiaoqing Wu, Junzhe Shao, Lixia Ye, Jia Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEvidence suggests that a substantial part of influenza-related morbidity and mortality is due to cardiovascular events. Limited population-level evidence exists regarding whether seasonal influenza vaccination is associated with a lower risk of ischemic stroke (IS) in older adults with diabetes. We conducted a retrospective cohort study using data from the Regional Health Information Platform. Among 143,841 diabetic adults aged ≥ 60 y alive and free of prior IS as of January 1, 2022, 131,045 met inclusion criteria and were followed until IS occurrence, death, or July 31, 2022. The primary outcome was first-ever IS. Vaccination exposure was receipt of the 2021/22 seasonal inactivated influenza vaccine. Subdistribution hazard ratios (sHR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were estimated using Fine-Gray competing risk models, with death as a competing risk. Pneumococcal vaccination served as a negative control. Of 131,045 participants, 54,617 (41.7%) received influenza vaccination. During 75,593 person-years of follow-up over approximately 7 months, 1,427 IS cases occurred (incidence rate: 18.9 per 1,000 person-years). After multivariable adjustment, influenza vaccination was associated with a 15% reduced IS risk within 7 months of follow-up (adjusted sHR = 0.85; 95% CI: 0.76–0.95). This inverse association was consistent across influenza activity phases and was most pronounced during high antigenic similarity epidemic periods (sHR = 0.74; 95% CI: 0.61–0.89) and among patients with coronary heart disease (sHR = 0.46; 95% CI: 0.25–0.86; interaction P = .034). The negative control analysis showed no association between pneumococcal vaccination and IS risk. Seasonal influenza vaccination was associated with a significantly reduced risk of incident IS within 7 months among older adults with diabetes, supporting its potential inclusion as a low-cost stroke prevention strategy in this high-risk population. Randomized controlled trials are warranted to confirm causality.KEYWORDS: Diabetes in elderly, influenza vaccination, ischemic stroke, retrospective study, multivariate survival analysis

Introduction

Ischemic stroke (IS) remains a leading cause of disability and mortality among elderly patients with diabetes mellitus (DM), with its pathophysiological basis rooted in chronic hyperglycemia-induced endothelial dysfunction1 and age-related vascular remodeling.2 Global epidemiological evidence demonstrates a 20% increased risk of first ischemic stroke3 and a 1.3- to 2.0-fold higher poststroke mortality4,5 in individuals with diabetes compared to age-matched non-diabetic counterparts. Amidst the dual challenges of population aging and the diabetes epidemic, there exists a critical unmet need for evidence-based primary prevention strategies targeting this high-risk population.6

Accumulating evidence implicates influenza infection as a transient yet significant risk factor for cerebrovascular events through multiple pathways, including systemic inflammation,7 and atherosclerotic plaque destabilization.8 Temporal associations have been observed between viral infections such as influenza and population-level trends in ischemic stroke incidence.9,10 Laboratory-confirmed influenza infections specifically correlate with elevated thromboembolic risk.11 A population-based self-controlled case series study of 2.23 million Spanish adults revealed a 1 to 4 fold increased risk of atherothrombotic events within 14 d of laboratory-confirmed influenza.12 These findings support a biologically plausible hypothesis that influenza vaccination may mitigate stroke risk through infection prevention.

Recent meta-analyses suggest an overall 19% reduction in stroke incidence/hospitalization (odds ratio [OR]=0.81; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.77–0.86) associated with influenza vaccination, though substantial heterogeneity persists across studies (I2 = 86%).13 While three randomized controlled trials have explored this association, results remain inconclusive.14–16 Critically, no studies have specifically targeted elderly patients with diabetes – a population characterized by suboptimal vaccination coverage (global < 75%;17–20 mainland China < 10%)21 and disproportionately elevated stroke risk.

Using the Ningbo Regional Health Information Platform (RHIP), we conducted a population-based retrospective cohort study employing competing risk models to evaluate the association between influenza vaccination and first-ever IS among elderly patients with diabetes. Confirmatory findings would provide pivotal evidence for integrating influenza control and stroke prevention strategies in this vulnerable population, addressing a major evidence gap in clinical practice and public health policy.

Methods

Study design

This population-based retrospective cohort study employed a real-world data approach to systematically evaluate the epidemiological association between influenza vaccination and the risk of first-ever IS among elderly patients with diabetes. Data were sourced from a multi-institutional, heterogeneous database maintained by a regional health information platform in a developed coastal region of China (28°51‘−30°33‘N, 120°55‘−122°16‘E). This platform serves a registered population of over 9.4 million residents and provides longitudinal health records spanning the entire lifespan through deterministic linkage of electronic medical records (diagnoses coded using the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision [ICD-10]), immunization registries (vaccine batch and administration dates), and vital statistics (cause and date of death) via national identity numbers. The RHIP achieved Level 4A certification in China’s National Medical Health Information Interoperability Maturity Assessment in 2015 and Level 5B certification in 2020, ensuring high-quality and standardized data. Prior studies have validated this database for real-world evidence generation.22–24

The study protocol received ethical approval from the Ningbo Center for Disease Control and Prevention Institutional Review Board (IRB No: 202,511). Given the use of de-identified secondary data without intervention, informed consent was waived.

Study population

The cohort was defined using the following inclusion criteria: (1) alive at study initiation (January 1, 2022); (2) aged ≥ 60 y (born ≤ 1962); (3) preexisting diagnosis of type 2 diabetes mellitus (ICD-10: E11); (4) no prior diagnosis of IS (ICD-10: I63). Participants were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) enrolled in the RHIP after January 1, 2021; (2) classified as transient or nonresident population; (3) received ≥ 2 influenza vaccine doses between August 1 and December 31, 2021; (4) had < 2 healthcare encounters during the observation period (January 1-July 31, 2022); (5) received influenza vaccination during the observation period. The study utilized data from the RHIP to include all eligible individuals without sampling.

Sample size calculation

Published evidence indicates influenza vaccination may reduce stroke risk by 14% to 23%.13 Considering the vaccine’s low cost and high safety profile alongside the substantial morbidity and mortality burden of stroke among diabetic patients, we established a clinically meaningful minimum hazard ratio of 0.86 as our primary effect size. Sample size projections used Cox proportional hazards modeling with these parameters. Baseline incidence of ischemic stroke in unvaccinated patients was set at 1.679% per year with expected vaccination coverage of 40%.25 We specified a two sided alpha level of 0.05 and statistical power of 90%. The calculation determined a minimum total sample of 122,152 participants comprising 48,876 vaccinated and 73,276 unvaccinated individuals.

The RHIP identified 131,045 eligible patients meeting inclusion exclusion criteria including 54,617 vaccinated individuals and 76,428 unvaccinated individuals. The cohort size exceeds the minimum requirement for the sample size, providing sufficient statistical power to detect a risk ratio of 0.86 or lower.

Exposure, outcome, and covariates

The primary exposure was receipt of a single dose of inactivated influenza vaccine (trivalent or quadrivalent) between August 1 and December 31, 2021, with vaccination occurring ≥ 14 d prior to IS onset, death, or study termination (July 31, 2022). The primary outcome was IS (ICD-10 code: I63), including fatal cases confirmed by physician diagnosis. Follow-up commenced on January 1, 2022, and continued until the earliest of: (1) IS occurrence, (2) death, or (3) study termination (July 31, 2022). Covariates included demographic characteristics (age, sex, and residential area type), clinical comorbidities (hypertension, tumor, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [COPD], chronic kidney disease [CKD], coronary heart disease [CHD], arrhythmia, atrial fibrillation, diabetic complications, and debilitated status), medication use (antihypertensive, hypoglycemic, antilipemic, and anticoagulant agents), and pneumococcal vaccination status.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages, with comparisons made using Pearson’s χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests (when expected cell frequencies were < 5). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using Student’s t-tests, while non-normally distributed variables were summarized as median with interquartile range and compared using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests.

Fine-Gray competing risks regression was used to estimate subdistribution hazard ratios (sHR) and 95% confidence intervals for ischemic stroke, treating non-IS mortality as a competing event. Three progressively adjusted models were constructed. Model 1 included demographic characteristics (age, sex, and residential area type). Model 2 added adjustments for clinical comorbidities (hypertension, tumor, COPD, CKD, CHD, arrhythmia, atrial fibrillation, diabetic complications, and debilitated status). Model 3 further incorporated medication use (antihypertensive, hypoglycemic, antilipemic, and anticoagulant agents) and pneumococcal vaccination status. The proportional hazards assumption was verified using the global likelihood ratio test (Supplementary Table 1).

Based on the national influenza surveillance system of the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention, influenza activity was categorized into epidemic periods (defined as any week in which the weekly influenza-like illness (ILI) positivity rates ≥ 10%, a threshold commonly used in other countries or regions to delineate the onset of influenza epidemics)26 and non-epidemic periods (weekly ILI positivity rates < 10%). Stratified analyses were conducted accordingly. Effect modification was assessed through pre-specified subgroup analyses, with interaction terms tested using likelihood ratio tests.

Three sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of our findings. These included propensity score matching using a 1:1 nearest-neighbor algorithm with a caliper width of 0.02 standard deviations based on influenza vaccination status. We also excluded participants with documented influenza or ILI (Supplementary Table 2) diagnoses within six months before baseline. Additionally, we performed a negative control analysis using pneumococcal vaccination status as a comparator intervention to evaluate potential residual confounding.

All analyses were conducted using R version 4.2.2 with two-tailed significance thresholds set at α = 0.05.

Results

Study cohort

As of January 1, 2022, we identified 143,841 diabetic adults aged ≥ 60 y who were alive and had no prior diagnosis of IS. After applying rigorous inclusion/exclusion criteria (Figure 1), the final analytic cohort comprised 131,045 participants.

Flow diagram for study enrollment.

Baseline characteristics

Among the 131,045 participants, 54,617 (41.7%) received the seasonal inactivated influenza vaccine, with 96.2% receiving the trivalent formulation. During 7 months of follow-up, we documented 1,427 IS cases and 252 deaths due to non-IS causes. The total follow-up person-years amounted to 75,593 person-years, with a median follow-up duration of 0.6 y. The incidence rate of IS events was 18.9 cases per 1,000 person-years, and the incidence rate of non-IS-related deaths was 3.3 cases per 1,000 person-years.

Baseline characteristics are presented in (Table 1). Compared with the unvaccinated group, the influenza-vaccinated group had significantly higher proportions of individuals aged ≥ 70 y, females, and those with comorbidities including hypertension, tumor, COPD, CKD, CHD, arrhythmia, atrial fibrillation, and debilitated status. Additionally, the vaccinated group showed higher utilization of antihypertensive, hypoglycemic, antilipemic, and anticoagulant therapies, as well as pneumococcal vaccination.

Univariate analysis revealed that older age, male, and comorbidities including hypertension, COPD, CKD, CHD, arrhythmia, diabetic complications, and debilitated status were associated with higher risk of ischemic stroke. Additionally, individuals receiving antihypertensive, antilipemic, or anticoagulant therapies exhibited increased stroke risk. In contrast, urban residence was associated with lower risk of ischemic stroke (Supplementary Table 3).

Influenza vaccination and IS risk

The crude incidence rates of IS were 19.6 cases per 1,000 person-years in the vaccinated group and 18.3 cases per 1,000 person-years in the unvaccinated group, yielding an unadjusted sHR of 1.07 (95% CI: 0.96–1.19). However, after adjusting for demographic characteristics, vaccination was associated with a lower risk of stroke (adjusted sHR = 0.87; 95% CI: 0.78–0.97). This inverse association persisted with further adjustment for clinical comorbidities (adjusted sHR = 0.86; 95% CI: 0.77–0.96) and remained significant after additional adjustment for medication use and pneumococcal vaccination (adjusted sHR = 0.85; 95% CI: 0.76–0.95) (Table 2). Stratified analysis by influenza activity level and antigenic similarity between circulating and vaccine strains showed that influenza vaccination was associated with a lower risk of IS across all phases: non-epidemic phase, low antigenic similarity epidemic phase, and high antigenic similarity epidemic phase. Notably, a significant lower risk was observed during the high antigenic similarity epidemic phase (adjusted sHR: 0.74, 95% CI: 0.61–0.89).

Sensitivity analyses

In sensitivity analyses using propensity score matching (sHR = 0.84; 95% CI: 0.74–0.94; Supplementary Table 4) and excluding individuals diagnosed with influenza/ILI within 6 months before baseline (adjusted sHR = 0.87; 95% CI: 0.77–0.97; Supplementary Table 5), the results remained robust. Unlike influenza vaccination, pneumococcal vaccination was not significantly associated with a lower risk of IS (adjusted sHR = 0.68; 95% CI: 0.43–1.08; Supplementary Table 6 and Supplementary Table 7).

Discussion

This population-based cohort study provides novel evidence that seasonal inactivated influenza vaccination is associated with a 15% lower risk of first-ever IS among older adults with diabetes. This inverse association exhibited significant effect modification by CHD status, with a 54% lower risk observed in vaccinated individuals with concurrent CHD vs. a 13% lower risk in those without CHD. Notably, the association was most pronounced during periods of antigenic match between circulating strains and vaccine components (26% lower risk). These findings provide robust evidence of an association between vaccination and lower IS risk in this high-risk population and suggest that viral epidemiology may modulate this inverse association through strain-specific mechanisms.

The inverse association of influenza vaccination with first-ever IS observed in our study aligns with the results of prior investigations. For example, a case-control study conducted using the Spanish primary care database (spanning from 2001 to 2015) and involving individuals aged 40 to 99 y reported an adjusted OR of 0.88 (95% CI: 0.84–0.92) for the inverse association of influenza vaccination with first-ever IS.27 Another nationwide cohort study that leveraged the Danish National Registry Database (from 2007 to 2016) and enrolled diabetic patients aged 18 to 100 y revealed an adjusted hazard ratio of 0.85 (95% CI: 0.74–0.98) for the inverse association of vaccination with stroke/acute myocardial infarction mortality.28 These data support the recommendation to prioritize influenza vaccination in high-risk groups, even though the prevention of IS has not yet been recognized as an independent indication for influenza vaccination.29 Notably, subgroup analysis among patients with CHD revealed a 54% lower risk of IS, a notable finding that suggests a potential synergistic inverse association with cardiovascular outcomes, particularly in diabetics with CHD.

The temporal association between vaccination and viral epidemiology provides mechanistic support for the infection-prevention hypothesis.30 A significant inverse association (26% lower risk; adjusted sHR = 0.74, 95% CI: 0.61–0.89) was observed during weeks 22–30 (influenza epidemic period), when the predominant circulating strain, A/Cambodia/e0826360/2020 (H3N2)-like virus, exhibited high antigenic homology with the vaccine strain (>85% similarity, see Supplementary Table 8). In contrast, no significant inverse association was observed during weeks 1–11 of the epidemic period (adjusted sHR = 0.92, 95% CI:0.77–1.10), when the dominant strain was B/Victoria lineage/Washington/02/2019-like virus (subclade 1A.3a.2), which showed lower antigenic reactivity with the vaccine strain (33.8% homology). Additionally, no significant association was detected during the non-epidemic period (weeks 12–21; adjusted sHR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.73–1.09). These findings suggest that a measurable lower risk of IS may require sufficient viral circulation and antigenic matching, thereby supporting the hypothesis that the observed inverse association may be mediated through the prevention of systemic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction induced by influenza.

Our study has several advantages. First, the utilization of real-world healthcare data (e.g., electronic health records) minimized recall bias and misclassification to the greatest extent possible. Second, focusing on the elderly diabetic population to assess first-ever ischemic stroke reduced confounding from recurrent events while efficiently evaluating potential primary prevention benefits. Third, the competing risks model (Fine-Gray model) accounted for non-ischemic stroke deaths, a critical source of bias in elderly populations where mortality often precedes stroke.31 Fourth, the territory-wide sampling framework minimized selection bias. Finally, propensity score matching and negative control analyses using pneumococcal vaccine confirmed that the observed lower risk of ischemic stroke was unlikely attributable to confounding from healthcare utilization.

However, there are certain limitations. Reliance on administrative data precluded adjustment for lifestyle confounders (e.g., smoking, alcohol consumption, diet), though comorbidity analyses and sensitivity analyses mitigated this issue. The restriction to data from the 2021/22 influenza season necessitates validation across multiple years. Lastly, the observational design precludes causal inference, though the dose-response relationship observed with viral epidemiology strengthens biological plausibility.

Conclusions

The findings, based on approximately 7 months of follow-up, support considering the expansion of recommendations for influenza vaccination among older adults with diabetes during antigenically matched epidemic periods, particularly those with concurrent coronary heart disease. Randomized controlled trials with longer follow-up are warranted to confirm these observations and assess the durability of the association.
