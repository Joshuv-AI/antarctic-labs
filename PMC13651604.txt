Where Do Proteomics
People Come from?

Benjamin C. Orsburn

Abstract

The
field of proteomics is one of the fastest-growing
scientific
disciplines in the world today. Beginning in 2020, ambitious market
predictions anticipated proteomics to more than double in market size
in 4–6 years and to even surpass the well-established genomic
sciences industry in 8–12 years. While the market is still
growing, it has not come close to matching the initial projections.
To better understand the root cause of this, the author has collected
field surveys and direct interviews of over 200 scientists actively
involved in proteomics research today. While ultimately incomplete,
it is worth noting that over 68% of respondents received their technical
training in proteomics during either their doctoral or postdoctoral
training. In stark contrast, only 4.3% of students obtained training
during their undergraduate education. Given the 5.7-to-9.4-year average
cycle for traditional doctoral and postdoctoral training in the life
sciences, it appears the limiting factor in growing the proteomics
field may be linked directly to current practices in training a qualified
workforce.

Introduction

The
term “proteomics” was
first coined by Mark Wilkins
in the late 1990s and was based on the recent increase in capabilities
in the genomic sciences. Like “gene-omics”, “protein-omics”
implies a measurement of all the proteins present in an organism,
tissue, or cell. Unlike genomics, which
has had the capacity to analyze nearly all of the approximately 30,000
human genes for decades, comprehensive measurement of the human proteome
is fairly recent. It was not until 2017 when Bekker-Jennsen et al.
first demonstrated a depth of coverage matching that of next-generation
sequencing with 14,200 protein isoforms measured in a cell line in
approximately one calendar day. Today,
similar depth of coverage can be obtained in many sample types in
up to 100 samples per instrument per day. In addition, new “next-generation” proteomics technologies
have demonstrated the ability to selectively target thousands of proteins
in human plasma, perhaps the most challenging of human materials.

In late 2021, an article in Forbes magazine
described the field
of proteomics as “The Next Massive Investment Opportunity”. Optimistic market analyses the following year
expanded on these hopes, with many groups estimating a rapid doubling
of the value of the field. Some public analyses went as far as predicting
proteomics to rapidly overtake the established, and still growing,
genomics market. In 2026, it is obvious that the size of the field
of proteomics has ultimately expanded, primarily in the pharmaceutical
industry, but not to the level of those
early market expectations.

The rise
of genomics in the early 2000s led to the rapid development
of formal curricula in both undergraduate and graduate studies. By
the early 2000s, it was possible to obtain a bachelor of science in
genomics or genomic sciences from at least 40 universities in the
United States (US). If you increase the breadth of this definition
to undergraduate or graduate programs that offer an optional emphasis
or focus on genomics, the number of institutions is easily over 120. Conversely, in four years of searching, the author
has not identified a single undergraduate program focused exclusively
on proteomics anywhere in the world.

This is strange if the
two fields were equivalent in terms of the
complexity of the challenge of analyzing their respective molecules.
However, this is even more strange when the scope of the respective
challenges is considered. Today it is well accepted that there are
less than 30,000 human genes. In the simplest
organisms or introductory textbooks, each of these genes leads to
the production of a single protein. In reality, these numbers may
be very different depending on how the protein is assessed. The HUPO
Human Proteomics Project has been collecting evidence for predicted
protein-coding regions of DNA for decades and currently catalogues
19,435 genes as having any evidence of protein-level expression, which
is a far lower number than the Human Genome Project first predicted. However, many proteins have the function of
modifying a certain group of proteins under specific conditions. These
modifications can include cutting the produced protein to produce
a shortened form, splicing two separate protein forms together, or
adding chemical modifications such as a phosphate group or chain of
sugars to the protein. Furthermore, other proteins may then modify
just some of the proteins modified by the first set of modifying proteins
under other, more specific, conditions. For example, a phosphorylated
protein may be actively moved to another region of the cell where
proteins are waiting to chemically modify those proteins that arrive. The effect of these modifications is typically
not trivial. A phosphorylated protein, for example, may have a completely
different function, activity, or location than the unmodified counterpart.
Many versions of each protein may exist within each cell and may work
in tandem or completely counteract the activity of other versions.
This process of proteomic modification is not at all rare. There are
easily more than 100,000 active protein versions, or proteoforms,
in work in every one of us today, and the number may be far higher. The number of proteins is not the only thing
that is higher. One metric of the relative value of measuring genes
compared to proteins directly may be encapsulated in the respective
number that has been effectively used as drug targets. Currently,
over 90% of all drugs approved for use in the United States target
specific proteins, rather than any intermediate molecule such as mRNA.
More specifically, over 60% of all drugs target and/or inhibit specific
isoforms of membrane-spanning proteins. From a cost-benefit perspective, it is no wonder that many drug
development enterprises are skipping large-scale genomic studies and
moving to proteomics first.

If a
scientific field is to double in total size, then it may not
be necessary for the number of scientists within the field to double.
The number of scientists would, however, absolutely have to increase.
If there is no formalized education in the form of a degree for proteomics
scientists, then the author thought to ask the obvious question. Where
do experts in proteomics receive the training and skills that make
them capable of accurately identifying and quantifying proteins in
complex human samples? Or, more briefly, where do proteomics people
come from?

Results

Survey Results

Of 226 proteomics
researchers surveyed
or researched from background interviews, 212 (93.8%) could be lumped
relatively cleanly into a few categories based on the source of their
training in proteomics. The final categories included undergraduate,
master’s, doctorate, postdoctoral, or on-the-job training.
A final category for “other” was necessary to encapsulate
researchers who stated that they received their training from reading
scientific literature or online content such as blogs or online videos.
These have been summarized in and Supplemental Table 1. Over half (52.2%) of all scientists in this survey who are in the
proteomics field learned proteomics during a Ph.D. or M.D./Ph.D. program.
An additional 16.66% of scientists received their training during
postdoctoral fellowships or research. On-the-job training is the third
most common source of proteomics expertise today with 13.1% of responses.
While some of these scientists likely learned from experts at their
place of work, many also likely received vendor-specific training
that was included or purchased along with analytical hardware. Undergraduate-
or master’s-level training in proteomics, by contrast, is relatively
rare, with 4.3% and 7.5% of responses, respectively. To put this in
perspective, nearly 69% of all proteomics researchers have the most
lengthy and advanced training in the academic sciences with at least
a doctorate and many beyond that, with years of postdoctoral training.
The level of this disparity in training is further amplified when
the specific source is explored. While the results in Supplemental Table 1 have been anonymized for
readers, they have not been for the author. The author feels it does
no harm whatsoever to share that every proteomic scientist who was
trained during their undergraduate degree received this instruction
at Brigham Young University in Provo, Utah. Similarly, no researcher
who received training during their master’s degree received
this training in the US except for two students who worked with this
author.

Training Length

According to the 2023 National Center
for Education Statistics, the average length of a Ph.D. in the life
sciences is 5.7 years from program start. This is generally after
the completion of a bachelor’s degree, which still averages
approximately 4 years. Postdoctoral fellowships in the US in the life
sciences average an additional 3.7 years. This suggests that the minimum
amount of time necessary to create a new proteomics scientist requires
9.7 years of undergraduate and graduate training or 13.4 years of
education if postdoctoral training was also received.

Life Science Workforce Metrics

A
comprehensive and
recent analysis of the US life science workforce is encapsulated in
the 2020 joint publication from the National Academies of Sciences,
Engineering, and Medicine work “Safeguarding the Bioeconomy”. In this work, “Bioeconomy” refers
to all branches of the agricultural, pharmaceutical, and biological
sciences that contribute to the overall US economy.

While the
difficulty in counting the number of workers in the life sciences
is highlighted by these authors, it appears obvious that increasing
the size of the bioeconomy requires increasing the number of available
scientists in that field. The author would argue that the doubling
of the size of the US bioeconomy from 2000 to 2014 was directly linked
to the 160% increase in Ph.D. graduates in the life sciences over
this same approximate period of time.

Discussion

Proteomics, in any form, is still a young
field. Many of the scientists
who invented the core techniques that make it possible are still researching
and leading programs today. For example, Professor John Yates leads
active research and one of the most respected journals in the field
of proteomics. His Ph.D. thesis, “Protein sequencing by tandem
mass spectrometry”, is a seminal work that framed the opportunities
and challenges that we still face today. His groups later went on
to develop critical technologies such as online two-dimensional fractionation and the first search engine for protein mass
spectral data. While few lab leaders
today have played such pivotal roles in the development of this, or
any field, many had such significant parts that it is difficult to
identify where they truly obtained their technical training. The author
feels it is unlikely, however, that Dr. Yates will apply for a job
at your company when you need to expand your proteomics discovery
pipeline. With many experts happily entrenched in their careers, this
leaves the question of how a field that analysts believe could double
in size will obtain the scientific workforce that can perform these
experiments. If trainees require a minimum of 5.7 to 9.4 years of
training after completing their undergraduate degrees to contribute
meaningfully to proteomics experiments, it seems like doubling the
number of workers would require a minimum of that amount of time if
we all started working on it today. In the hypothetical situation
where the author and every other academic proteomic researcher did
recruit twice as many students in the fall of 2026, we would undoubtedly
increase the number of qualified proteomics scientists. But would
that be enough?

For an extreme example, Emeritus Professor Don
Hunt of the University
of Virginia is responsible for training many of the most respected
scientists in the field of proteomics today. When the author introduced
Don at an invited lecture in Washington, DC, in 2017, the author said
that he had mentored 123 Ph.D. students, and he did not correct the
author. If that is the benchmark for success, and it should be, and
there are international awards named for Don Hunt, is training more
Ph.D. students even possible? At this point in the author’s
career, if the author hoped to match Don’s number of Ph.D.
students with a possibly manageable 10 students at a time and they
all required the median 5.7 years to completion, the final batch would
graduate right around the author’s 109th birthday, which would
set back the author’s current retirement plans by more than
40 years. Possible? Maybe, but certainly not practical.

Conclusions

The author started this multiyear off-and-on-again
investigation
to better understand the author’s own field. And the author
thinks to have an answer to where current experts in the field get
their training. Most proteomics people have a very similar story to
the author’s, where they were in the right place at just the
right time. They were lucky enough to study under some rare expert
somewhere for a Ph.D. and eventually specialized in analyzing proteins.
Again, like the author, many went on to do postdoctoral work in proteomics
and found a fantastic job market that was hungry for their skills.
However, if they are to grow this field to become the next genomics, they may need to think about revolutionary new
avenues for training the next generation. The author is encouraged
to see multiple recent reviews that are essentially textbooks on the
field, and not only because it means
the author will not have to go to the trouble of updating some books
the author once wrote. In addition, they
have seen some educational publications that have focused on implementing
and outcomes of proteomic training. The author does not know what the answer is,
but the author has sometimes thought that the author might be able
to measure a protein concentration by fluorescence or clean a metal
lens the author took out of a vacuum chamber without the author’s
12 years of combined undergraduate, graduate, and postdoctoral training.

Study
Limitations

The obvious limitation of this study
is the sample bias. Not every proteomics scientist is on the social
media platforms that the author knows how to use. It is highly possible
that the ones that would see a survey the author posted are within
some branch of the author’s social network that is biased toward
scientists who are in academic-type research and enriched for researchers
with longer educational routes.

Materials
and Methods

Social Media Surveys

Between the summer of 2023 and
the spring of 2025, the author twice posted on social media services,
such as LinkedIn and BlueSky, a request for proteomics experts to
self-report the source of their proteomic training. The text of these
surveys was downloaded, and the results were painfully extracted manually
into an Excel spreadsheet. As the results were fully anonymized, no
IRB was obtained or required for this study.

Expert Interviews

The US Human Proteomics organization
posts interviews of proteomics experts on their website (www.us-hupo.org). The text from
the US HUPO “Popstars”, as well as the handwritten notes
from guests featured on The Proteomics Show podcast, was scraped by
the author with his big heavy bifocals to extract training information
for each interviewed expert. Following redundancy filtering by name
in Excel, approximately 60 additional expert backgrounds were added
to the final spreadsheet.
