A Abstract
The project “Symmetry in Correlated Quantum Matter” aimed to characterize, classify, and eluci-
date the properties of highly entangled forms of quantum matter, with particular attention to the
roles of symmetry and the closely related geometry of crystalline lattices. A primary focus of the
research over the lifetime of the award has been on fracton phases of matter, which represent a
novel and rapidly developing class of quantum phases characterized by excitations whose mobility
is intrinsically restricted to points, lines, or planes. The award investigated fundamental theoretical
questions regarding these phases, and laid the foundation for a comprehensive classification and
characterization of fracton orders that is now being developed in work beyond the scope of the
project. Furthermore, the research significantly advanced our understanding of topological phases
protected by the geometrical symmetries of crystal lattices, as well as the thermodynamic properties
and heat conduction in strongly correlated magnetic insulators.

B Major Achievements
 • Crystalline topological phases: Many important topological phases are protected by the
 spatial symmetries of crystalline solids, and are thus dubbed crystalline symmetry-protected
 topological (cSPT) phases. This project developed a general method to classify cSPT phases,
 which had previously been a significant challenge. The approach developed transformed what
 was thought to be a difficult problem into a one that is simpler than its analog for internal
 (i.e. non-spatial) symmetries. The work successfully demonstrated that cSPT phases are
 adiabatically connected to real-space crystalline patterns of lower-dimensional topological
 states, and applied this to electronic topological crystalline insulators across all 230 space
 groups.

 • Coupled layer constructions of fracton phases: Developed a foundational coupled-
 layer construction for fracton orders in three spatial dimensions. The project demonstrated
 how complex fracton phases can be theoretically constructed from stacked layers of simpler
 two-dimensional topological phases or three-dimensional fracton phases via “p-string” and
 “p-membrane” condensation mechanisms.

 • Non-abelian fracton phases: Introduced some of the first models for fracton phases with
 non-abelian excitations, and showed that it is possible to have non-abelian excitations in
 three dimensional systems that do not simply arise from two-dimensional non-abelian exci-
 tations. This conceptually expanded the landscape of known fracton phases, demonstrating
 that inherently complex, non-abelian braiding processes – which are highly sought after for
 fault-tolerant topological quantum computation – can exist in systems strictly governed by
 restricted mobility.

 • Theory of fracton statistics: Developed the basic aspects of the theory of statistics for
 restricted-mobility excitations in fracton phases. In particular, demonstrated that completely
 immobile fractons can enjoy self-statistics, analogous to the familiar self statistics of bosons,
 fermions and anyons. This addressed a fundamental open question regarding whether self-
 exchange statistics could even exist for particles that cannot move independently, and pro-
 vided a rigorous theoretical framework for defining these exotic statistical properties.

 • Algebraic theories of fracton order: Developed algebraic theories of certain classes of
 fracton order, including “planon-modular” and “planon-only” fracton orders. For planon-

 modular orders – where any non-trivial point-like excitation can be detected by braiding with
 excitations restricted to a plane – the project introduced new computable phase invariants
 and established close connections to entanglement renormalization group flows. For planon-
 only systems, the research upgraded the concept of remote detectability to a generalized
 “excitation-detector principle” as a necessary condition for physical realizability, encoding
 the intricate fusion and statistical data of these phases into a novel mathematical framework.

 • Thermal transport in magnetic insulators: Formulated a minimal model requiring only
 Zeeman splitting and magnetoelastic coupling to accurately describe heat conduction in para-
 magnetic effective spin-1/2 magnetic insulators (like CsYbSe2 ). The work revealed that hy-
 bridized excitations – formed from acoustic phonons and localized spin-flip excitations – are
 responsible for the non-monotonic magnetic field dependence of longitudinal thermal conduc-
 tivity observed in a wide range of materials.
