BayesBench: Evaluating LLM Belief Trajectories Under Multi-Turn Evidence Accumulation
Ankur Samanta, Akshayaa Magesh, Tal Lancewicki, Ayush Jain, Youliang Yu, Paul Sajda, Kaveh Hassani, Aditya Modi, Daniel R. Jiang, Yonathan Efroni

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are typically deployed in multi-turn conversations, where each turn provides new evidence that should reduce epistemic uncertainty about their environment. Acting rationally then requires inferring the unobserved quantities that govern it and updating beliefs about them as evidence accumulates. Yet most evaluations only score the model’s final-turn answer in a single-turn format, leaving this process unexamined. We ask how closely LLMs’ belief updates match those of a rational Bayesian reasoner in multi-turn settings, and introduce BayesBench, a suite of simulation environments that probe this across three progressively complex tasks: (i) Bayesian estimation, where the model infers an unknown parameter from sequential evidence; (ii) Bayesian prediction, where the model turns inferred beliefs about a latent variable into outcome forecasts; and (iii) latent-framed Bayesian prediction, where observations are filtered through a user-persona framing, requiring joint inference over the latent state and the persona. Across seven LLMs (3B–70B), scaling improves latent inference and evidence accumulation, with updates occasionally matching the Bayesian posterior. However, these gains do not reliably carry over to downstream prediction, exposing a gap between inferring latent structure and using it to rationally update beliefs about the target outcome.

Introduction

LLMs are increasingly deployed in multi-turn interactions, where evidence arrives sequentially
(
15
;
35
;
21
;
16
)
and is often shaped by hidden quantities that must be inferred from observations. Adapting effectively to such environments requires using new evidence to reduce uncertainty about those quantities and incorporating the resulting beliefs into the relevant estimate or prediction. Yet most evaluations collapse this process into a single turn, providing all information at once and scoring only the final answer rather than how beliefs and predictions evolve over the interaction. Rational belief updating
(
6
;
27
)
is central to decision-making under uncertainty, but we lack controlled multi-turn benchmarks that measure it directly. We take inspiration from a classic cognitive-psychology paradigm for studying belief updating: in the bookbag-and-poker-chip task
(
8
;
24
)
, subjects observe chips drawn sequentially from one of two bookbags with different color mixtures and, after each draw, estimate the posterior over which bookbag is being sampled. We introduce
BayesBench
, which adapts this paradigm to LLMs across four environments, scoring per-turn belief trajectories against Bayesian references where tractable, and downstream predictions against task outcomes.

We study three Bayesian tasks, each adding a layer of latent structure.
Bayesian estimation
asks the model to recover an unknown parameter from i.i.d. evidence; we study it in the
coin-flip
environment, where the model observes a sequence of flips and estimates the coin’s unknown bias
\Theta
.
Bayesian prediction
adds a latent that the model must infer to guide a downstream judgment; we study it in a
recommender system
, where the model infers a user’s “type” from per-turn ratings and predicts how they would rate a held-out movie.
Latent-framed Bayesian prediction
further filters the evidence through a
framing
latent (the semantic style in which it is presented) that the model must infer and condition on to update its posterior over the latent state correctly; we study it in
social judgment
, predicting Reddit verdicts across
neutral
,
conceding
, and
defending
styles, and
medical triage
, mapping patient symptoms to urgency across communication styles such as
hypochondriac
and
minimizing
.

We use the term
latent inference
to refer to the model’s per-turn estimate of the hidden quantity, either the unknown parameter
\Theta
or categorical latent
z
.
Across seven open-weight LLMs (3B–70B), we find three recurring patterns.

•

Models infer latent structure from accumulating evidence, and scaling strengthens this inference.
Larger models separate coin biases more clearly, recover user types more accurately, and infer patient communication styles more reliably.

•

Better latent inference does not yet translate into calibrated downstream prediction.
Larger models can over-update in the coin task, pushing predictions toward the extremes when the Bayesian reference remains more moderate. In the recommender system and medical triage, explicit conditioning on the inferred latent can improve performance, but models still fall short of reliably translating what they infer into accurate rating or urgency predictions; in triage, this appears as a tendency to push middle urgency labels toward the extremes.

•

Active engagement can bias how models accumulate evidence.
In social judgment, active engagement with the user in a simulated conversation reveals a persistent pro-user bias relative to passive observation of the same evidence. The inferred communication tendencies may not significantly alter the final verdict, but they shape how the model responds: conceding users elicit more reassurance, defending users elicit more sympathy toward the poster over the other party, and models downplay the other party’s reaction across styles. In medical triage, active engagement also degrades urgency assessment.

Our contributions are as follows.

1.

We introduce
BayesBench
, a benchmark suite for evaluating multi-turn belief updating, organized along three increasingly structured Bayesian tasks. These are instantiated in four environments, two of which include reference Bayesian posteriors that are available in closed form. Within the latent-framed task, we contribute a multi-turn user-simulator pipeline that controls both delivery format and the user’s communication style throughout the conversation.

2.

We evaluate seven open-weight LLMs across these environments and identify recurring dynamics in how models accumulate, calibrate, and use evidence over multiple turns.

3.

We show that intermediate belief trajectories expose departures from rational belief updating that final-turn accuracy alone can miss, motivating future work on multi-turn reasoning that accumulates and uses evidence more reliably.

Preliminaries

This section defines the notation and experimental interface used throughout the paper. We first give the shared Bayesian template and instantiate it in four environments. We then describe the multiple-choice belief probes, the conditional-elicitation variant, and the evaluation metrics.

2.1
Environments

The four environments instantiate three increasingly structured Bayesian tasks:
Task 1: Bayesian estimation
, where the model infers an unknown parameter from sequential evidence;
Task 2: Bayesian prediction

Task 3: latent-framed Bayesian prediction

Environment 1 (coin flip).
The model observes a sequence of flips from a coin with unknown bias and must estimate that bias, instantiating Task 1 (Bayesian estimation). The hidden quantity is
H=\Theta
, the unknown coin bias (
\Theta\in[0,1]
), and

\eta=(a_{0},b_{0})
are the Beta prior hyperparameters. Each observation

\Theta\sim\mathrm{Beta}(1,1)

p_{\eta}(\theta\mid x_{1:t})
is a Beta distribution updated in closed form, giving a tractable per-turn reference.

•

Environment 2 (recommender system).
The model observes a sequence of movie ratings from an unknown user type and must predict how that user would rate a held-out movie, instantiating Task 2 (Bayesian prediction). The hidden quantity is
H=Z
, one of
K=4

Z\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
), and
\eta=(\pi,\phi)
collects the type prior
\pi

\phi

Z\sim\mathrm{Categorical}(\pi)
. The movies

are presented in a fixed, deterministic sequence. The observation at turn
t
is the rating
X_{t}\mid Z=k\sim\mathrm{Categorical}(\phi_{m_{t},k})
, and ratings are conditionally independent given
Z
. The prediction target
Y
is the rating for a fixed held-out movie
m^{\star}
. Because
Y
depends on the user type, the model must infer

from the observed ratings to predict
Y

Environment 3a (social judgment).
The model reads an unfolding post about a social conflict, potentially styled by the poster’s communication style, and must predict the community verdict. This instantiates Task 3 (latent-framed Bayesian prediction). The hidden quantity decomposes as
H=(Z_{s},Z_{f})
, where
Z_{s}
is the underlying scenario (the facts of the situation, independent of how they are presented) and
Z_{f}
is a communication style (neutral, conceding, or defending). The environment parameters are
\eta=(\pi_{s},\pi_{f},\ell_{s},\ell_{f})
. The two latent variables are independent, with
Z_{s}\sim\mathrm{Categorical}(\pi_{s})
and
Z_{f}\sim\mathrm{Categorical}(\pi_{f})
. A clean storyboard segment
\tilde{X}_{t}\sim\ell_{\eta_{s}}(\cdot\mid z_{s},\tilde{x}_{1:t-1})
is drawn from a post on
r/AmItheAsshole
, and the actual observation
X_{t}\sim\ell_{\eta_{f}}(\cdot\mid\tilde{x}_{t},z_{f})
is a styled version of
\tilde{X}_{t}
. The observation likelihood in Equation (
1
) therefore decomposes as

\ell_{\eta}(x_{t}\mid z_{s},z_{f},\tilde{x}_{1:t-1})\;=\;\sum_{\tilde{x}_{t}}\ell_{\eta_{s}}(\tilde{x}_{t}\mid z_{s},\tilde{x}_{1:t-1})\,\ell_{\eta_{f}}(x_{t}\mid\tilde{x}_{t},z_{f}).

The prediction target

is a binary community verdict (
Y\in\{\text{YTA},\text{NTA}\}

Y
depends on
Z_{s}
alone (

, or equivalently

), the same case should yield the same verdict regardless of framing. Our goal is to test whether styling shifts the model’s prediction. No closed-form reference is available here.

•

Environment 3b (medical triage).
The model receives symptom reports from a patient whose communication style may exaggerate or downplay severity, and must assess clinical urgency. Medical triage also instantiates Task 3, sharing the structure of Environment 3a. Here
Z_{s}
is the underlying clinical condition and
Z_{f}
is a communication style (accurate, hypochondriac, minimizer, or cyberchondriac), with
\eta
defined as in Environment 3a. The clean signal
\tilde{X}_{t}\sim\ell_{s}(\cdot\mid z_{s},\tilde{x}_{1:t-1})
is a symptom detail and the observation
X_{t}\sim\ell_{f}(\cdot\mid\tilde{x}_{t},z_{f})
is the patient’s styled message. The prediction target
Y\sim g_{s}(\cdot\mid z_{s})
is a four-level urgency label (emergency, urgent, observation, routine), with
Y\perp Z_{f}
. A report of “worst pain ever” may originate from a hypochondriac (
Z_{f}
) with a minor ailment (
Z_{s}
) or an accurate reporter (
Z_{f}
) in a critical state (
Z_{s}
). Inferring
Z_{f}
helps the model correctly interpret the styled observations. Again, no closed-form reference is available for this environment.

2.2
Probing Beliefs and Single vs Multi-Turn Contexts

Delivery format determines the context available at each belief probe. In
single-turn
delivery, each poll starts a fresh prompt containing
x_{1:t}
, so the model sees the evidence observed so far but carries no conversational state from earlier polls. In
multi-turn
delivery, observations arrive through a running conversation, so prior observations and the model’s intervening responses remain in context.

At poll
t
, after the context has been assembled in one of these two ways, we elicit the posterior relevant to the environment:
p_{\mathrm{LLM}}(\theta\mid x_{1:t})
,
p_{\mathrm{LLM}}(z\mid x_{1:t})
, or
p_{\mathrm{LLM}}(y\mid x_{1:t})
. Each probe is a multiple-choice question (MCQ) over
K
outcomes, scored by extracting the log-probabilities of the option tokens (e.g., “A”, “B”) at the answer position. Since MCQ probes are susceptible to position bias, we issue
K
queries that cyclically rotate the assignment of outcomes to positions, forming a Latin square (construction and cancellation proof in App.
A
). The resulting debiased option probabilities define the model estimates
p_{\mathrm{LLM}}(\theta\mid x_{1:t})
,
p_{\mathrm{LLM}}(z\mid x_{1:t})
, and
p_{\mathrm{LLM}}(y\mid x_{1:t})
.

Conditional elicitation.
The recommender system and medical triage also ask the model for a target prediction conditioned on a latent it must infer (the user type or the communication style). There we elicit the per-latent predictive
p_{\mathrm{LLM}}(y\mid x_{1:t},z=k)
for each value
k
and report the prediction conditioned on the model’s most-likely latent,
\hat{z}=\arg\max_{k}p_{\mathrm{LLM}}(z=k\mid x_{1:t})
.
2
2

2

We also tested marginalizing over the full latent posterior,
\sum_{k}p_{\mathrm{LLM}}(z=k\mid x_{1:t})\,p_{\mathrm{LLM}}(y\mid x_{1:t},z=k)
; the resulting target predictions are largely consistent (App.
C.3
).

We compare these estimates with the Bayesian posterior when a closed form is available (Environments 1–2). Environments 3a–3b lack a closed-form posterior, so their sections define the invariance and conditioning checks we use instead. When a scalar summary is needed, we report point summaries:
\hat{\theta}_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{p_{\mathrm{LLM}}}[\theta\mid x_{1:t}]
,
\hat{z}_{t}=\arg\max_{z}p_{\mathrm{LLM}}(z\mid x_{1:t})
(for categorical latents), and
\hat{y}_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{p_{\mathrm{LLM}}}[y\mid x_{1:t}]
.

2.3
Models and Metrics

Models.
We evaluate seven instruction-tuned models spanning 3B–70B parameters across two open-source families: LLaMA 3
(
10
)
at 3B, 8B, and 70B, and Qwen 2.5
(
33
)
at 3B, 7B, 14B, and 32B. All model-generated conversation turns are sampled at temperature 0.7; belief probes are read from option log-probabilities and are independent of the sampling temperature.

Bayesian fidelity (TVD).
When the model emits a full distribution over a discrete latent or target with a closed-form Bayesian counterpart, we quantify how closely it tracks that distribution using the
total variation distance
:

\mathrm{TVD}_{t}(\cdot)\;=\;\frac{1}{2}\bigl\|p_{\mathrm{LLM}}(\cdot\mid x_{1:t})-p_{\eta}(\cdot\mid x_{1:t})\bigr\|_{1}.

(2)

In Environment 2 we instantiate
\cdot
as
z
(the type posterior) or
y
(the rating predictive). A perfectly Bayesian reasoner would maintain
\mathrm{TVD}_{t}(\cdot)=0
throughout the trajectory.

Accuracy (MAE).
We measure the absolute error between the model’s posterior mean and a reference value:

\mathrm{MAE}_{t}(\cdot)\;=\;\bigl|\mathbb{E}_{p_{\mathrm{LLM}}}[\cdot\mid x_{1:t}]-\mathbb{E}_{p_{\eta}}[\cdot\mid x_{1:t}]\bigr|.

(3)

Here
\cdot
is
\theta
in Environment 1 and
y
in Environments 2–3. The reference
\mathbb{E}_{p_{\eta}}[\cdot]
is the Bayesian posterior mean where a closed form is available (Environments 1–2), and the ground-truth value otherwise (Environment 3). Even with
\mathrm{TVD}_{t}(\cdot)=0
, a perfectly Bayesian reasoner can show non-zero
\mathrm{MAE}_{t}(\cdot)
in early turns, before the posterior concentrates. Throughout the paper, unless stated otherwise, reported quantities are means across trials, and every error bar, shaded band, and
\pm
value denotes
\pm 1
standard error of the mean.

Bayesian Estimation with a Biased Coin

The coin flip environment instantiates Task 1 (Bayesian estimation) as the Beta-Bernoulli setup of Environment 1 in Sec.
2
:
H=\Theta
, the unknown bias has prior
\Theta

\sim\mathrm{Beta}(1,1)
, and each observation is an i.i.d. flip
X_{t}\mid\Theta=\theta\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\theta)
. After each partial history, the prompt asks the model to “Predict the next flip” with heads/tails answer options. We use the probability assigned to heads as the running estimate
\hat{\theta}_{t}
.
3
3

3

For a Bernoulli likelihood, the next-flip predictive and posterior mean coincide:
p_{\eta}(X_{t+1}=1\mid x_{1:t})=\mathbb{E}_{p_{\eta}}[\Theta\mid x_{1:t}]
.
The Bayesian reference is

p_{\eta}(\theta\mid x_{1:t})=\mathrm{Beta}(1+n_{h},1+t-n_{h})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}_{p_{\eta}}[\theta\mid x_{1:t}]=\frac{1+n_{h}}{2+t},

(4)

where
n_{h}=\sum_{i\leq t}x_{i}
is the number of heads observed so far. We run
T=100
flips at each true bias
\theta\in\{0.25,0.5,0.75\}
. Figure
2
shows the multi-turn condition, where the model receives one flip per turn. The exact prompt appears in App.
B.1
.

Results.
In Figure

\theta=0.25

Smaller models often stay too close to the middle, while larger models frequently become too confident, pushing predictions toward 0 or 1 even when the Bayesian reference remains far from those extremes. B.2
.
4
4

4

The next-flip probe returns a single scalar,
p_{\mathrm{LLM}}(\mathrm{heads})=\mathbb{E}[\theta]
(the posterior mean). From this we cannot recover the model’s full posterior over
\Theta
, so there is no model-side distribution to compare against the reference
\mathrm{Beta}(1+n_{h},\,1+t-n_{h})
via TVD; we therefore report the absolute error on the mean (MAE).

Takeaway 1

Models are capable of Bayesian-aligned evidence accumulation, and larger models are better able to distinguish between different outcome distributions in context. However, as presently trained, they do not reliably track the Bayesian reference. Smaller models adapt too weakly to the observed distribution, while larger models can over-update toward the extremes when the evidence is skewed.

We also compare the same histories under single-turn delivery, where the full sequence appears in one prompt. The gap is largest for smaller models, which can give visibly different trajectories under the two formats, so the delivery format itself accounts for part of the deviation from the reference. The gap shrinks with scale, but the calibration issues remain. The full single-turn versus multi-turn comparison is in App.
B.3
.

The recommender system instantiates Task 2 (Bayesian prediction) using Environment 2 of Sec.
2

X_{t}

Y

m^{\star}

K=4

(

5

5

The user-type selection and fitting procedure is described in App.
C.2
.
The type prior is categorical,
\pi_{k}=p_{\eta}(z=k)
, and for each movie
m
and type
k
the rating likelihood is categorical over stars,
\phi_{m,k}(r)=p_{\eta}(r_{t}=r\mid m_{t}=m,z=k)
. In the experiment shown here, we replace movie titles and genres with anonymized item and feature labels, so the model has to infer the user type from rating patterns rather than movie-specific world knowledge. We observe
T=50
movie-rating pairs (the movie sequence
m_{1:T}
is fixed, with only the ratings sampled from the type-conditional likelihood) and query both the model’s posterior over user types and its predictive distribution for
y
. Because the type model is conveyed to the LLM as text, we summarize each user type by its average rating per movie rather than its full per-type rating distribution
\phi_{m,k}
. The Bayesian reference uses the full distributions, so it has strictly more information; it is best read as an upper bound, and some gap to it is expected. Under the fitted mixture model, the Bayesian reference is

p_{\eta}(z=k\mid x_{1:t})=\frac{\pi_{k}\prod_{i=1}^{t}\phi_{m_{i},k}(r_{i})}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\pi_{\ell}\prod_{i=1}^{t}\phi_{m_{i},\ell}(r_{i})}\quad\text{and}\quad p_{\eta}(y=r\mid x_{1:t},\,m^{\star})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{\eta}(z=k\mid x_{1:t})\,\phi_{m^{\star},k}(r).

(5)

We use two belief-elicitation procedures, matching the prompts in App.
C.1.3
. Write the elicited model distributions using
p_{\mathrm{LLM}}

•

Unconditional elicitation.
We pose the type and rating multiple-choice questions directly over the rating history. This yields a posterior over user types
p_{\mathrm{LLM}}(z=k\mid x_{1:t})
and a predictive over the held-out rating
p_{\mathrm{LLM}}(y=r\mid x_{1:t})
, with no type specified.

•

Type-conditional elicitation.
For each user type
z=k

p_{\mathrm{LLM}}(y=r\mid x_{1:t},z=k)
(
App.
C.1.3
, Question 3
). Following Sec.
2.2
, we report the rating conditioned on the model’s most-likely type,
\hat{z}=\arg\max_{k}p_{\mathrm{LLM}}(z=k\mid x_{1:t})
.

The unconditional elicitation measures what the model predicts by default. The type-conditional variant tests whether the model can exploit type information once it is made explicit. Figure

Results.
Figure
4
first shows that models can infer a latent user type from rating patterns alone. As more ratings from the new user accumulate, larger models place increasing probability on the true user type, even though semantic features such as movie titles and genres are anonymized (Panel 1). The curves remain below the Bayesian reference, but the main result is the scalable recovery of latent user structure from statistical rating patterns rather than semantic priors or world knowledge about movies.

Panels 2–4 ask whether this inferred type information improves held-out rating prediction. Unconditioned rating error generally falls or flattens as evidence accumulates (Panel 2), indicating that models use the rating history to improve prediction. Making the inferred type explicit helps for some models: several conditioned curves improve in Panel 3, and Panel 4 shows multiple model-profile cells above the diagonal. The gains are modest and uneven, however. Models can infer useful latent structure about the user, and can sometimes exploit it when it is made explicit, but they do not yet reliably incorporate that structure into accurate held-out rating predictions. Takeaway 2

In the recommender-system task, models show an ability to learn about a user statistically from rating patterns alone, even when semantic features that could cue the model’s prior knowledge are obscured. As more ratings accumulate, larger models recover the user’s latent type more accurately, and many models improve at predicting held-out ratings. The remaining gap is in how they use that information: models still struggle to turn what they infer about the user into reliably accurate predictions for new movie ratings.

Related Work

Bayesian belief updating in LLMs.

11
show that with enough biased-coin flips in-context, LLM predictions can approximate the exact posterior, with residual error attributable to the prior rather than the update; our results suggest that this alignment is highly circumstantial in current models, even though the underlying capability is present.
14
report partial Bayes-coherence over evidence updates that improves with scale, and
34
model LLM updates as Bayesian filters with systematic evidence discounting.
25
show that fine-tuning on a Bayesian assistant improves interactive recommendations, but their evaluation focuses on downstream agreement with the assistant’s choices. We instead track elicited belief trajectories against Bayesian references across turns, extending to structured latents (user types, communication styles) beyond a scalar bias and distinguishing latent inference from the downstream prediction that should use it.

Multi-turn reliability and belief trajectories.

18
document large single-turn-to-multi-turn performance drops driven by unreliability rather than aptitude;
9
show that LLM stances drift as context accumulates. Neither relates trajectories to a Bayesian benchmark in tractable generative environments.

Latent inference and belief-action gaps.

20
model LLM behavior as approximate Bayesian inference over latent themes;
23
document a gap between Bayes-coherent belief updates and the actions taken on those beliefs. Our main finding gives a controlled version of that pattern: scaling can improve latent inference without reliably improving the downstream predictions that should depend on it.

Calibration and overconfidence after post-training.
A growing line of work argues that the standard post-training and evaluation pipeline pushes models toward overconfident, poorly calibrated outputs.
22
report that the pre-trained model is well calibrated but “the post-training process” hurts calibration significantly (Fig. 8), and
19
trace this to PPO reward models that systematically reward high-confidence outputs regardless of response quality.
17
give a complementary account at the evaluation level: benchmarks that reward confident guessing over acknowledged uncertainty make hallucination a statistically optimal response, leaving even strong models systematically overconfident rather than abstaining when evidence is thin. We observe a sequential analogue of this miscalibration: across turns, larger models overreact to the evidence, assigning more extreme probabilities than the Bayesian reference would warrant (over-updating in the coin task, pulling middle triage labels toward the extremes), tracking the latent more sharply without matching the confidence the evidence supports.

Social judgment and sycophancy in LLMs.
Prior work shows that LLM moral judgments can diverge from human community verdicts
(
26
)
, affirm opposing sides depending on who frames the query
(
5
)
, and struggle when moving from verdict classification to free-text moral reasoning
(
4
)
.
1
use a Bayesian framework to distinguish sycophantic shifts from rational responses to evidence. In contrast, we evaluate moral judgment as a sequential belief-updating problem, tracking how the model’s elicited posteriors evolve as evidence accumulates across turns under varying user communication styles.

7
Limitations and Conclusion

BayesBench
provides a controlled way to study multi-turn LLM interaction as a belief-updating problem: evidence arrives over a conversation, and the model must infer hidden structure while deciding how much each new observation should change its predictions. Across four environments, models often extract latent signal from accumulating evidence, and this ability generally improves with scale. The recurring pattern is an alignment gap between latent inference and downstream prediction: models can identify relevant hidden structure, but cannot yet reliably translate that inference into calibrated later predictions.

Our evaluation makes belief updating observable by turning each task into a controlled sequence of evidence and belief probes. This lets us track how the model’s elicited distribution changes as evidence accumulates, but it also ties the measurement to the probe format, the specified latent variables, and, where available, the Bayesian reference model. In social judgment and medical triage, the observations are natural-language evidence rather than draws from a specified likelihood, so we cannot compare model beliefs to an exact Bayesian posterior trajectory in those environments. Instead, we measure how predictions move over turns and final-turn accuracy; in social judgment, we also test whether framing style changes the model’s prediction, and in medical triage, we test whether explicit conditioning on the inferred latent improves prediction. MCQ probes likewise provide an elicited distribution rather than a direct readout of internal belief. We use this format to fix the answer space, cover the discrete variables being evaluated, and score the probability of letter labels rather than open-ended completions, avoiding ambiguity about alternative phrasings of the same outcome. Still, elicited distributions can vary with the probe format, and explicit polling over named categories may not fully capture the belief state expressed in free-form generation when those categories are not articulated.

The benchmark also uses deliberately controlled latent variables. In the recommender system, user types are fitted and discretized from MovieLens; in social judgment and medical triage, communication styles are assigned as fixed categories. Each environment isolates a small, predefined latent structure; we do this deliberately, to first establish whether models can infer and use the relevant structure across several domains in a controlled setting. The active-engagement environments also rely on LLM-simulated users with fixed style instructions. These choices make the inference problem measurable, but real human users may be less cleanly separated into a few stable types: preferences, communication style, goals, and self-presentation may be continuous or mixed, and may change over an interaction. Real-world tasks may also involve several interacting latents that jointly shape the appropriate response.

Explicit latent conditioning surfaces latent structure that a standard conversational model would otherwise need to use implicitly. This makes it an intervention on the prediction process, not ordinary unconditioned conversation. In the recommender system and medical triage tasks, we compare predictions from the conversation history alone with predictions where the model’s current inferred latent is stated explicitly. Deployed systems may condition on information about the user or environment through structured user models, persistent memory, or summaries in the chat context. Our setup differs from that static case: we condition on the model’s own inferred latent and update it each turn. A fixed up-front description would presuppose the latent, with the model likely anchoring to it, whereas per-turn conditioning surfaces the model’s most recent elicited belief about the latent. The conditioned setting lets us test whether making the latent explicit improves prediction.

BayesBench
lays a foundation for future work on aligning how models update and use beliefs over multi-turn interactions. This includes developing increasingly complex simulations in which models must infer and compose multiple latent factors, represent user and environment characteristics in continuous or mixed forms, and adapt across additional domains and less discretized user populations. Because the behaviors we report are trends across two open-weight model families and a range of model sizes, this analysis should also be extended to closed-source frontier models and additional model families. Finally, the conditioned and unconditioned evaluations motivate post-training methods that make implicit latent integration more reliable, so that the improvements from explicit latent conditioning carry over to ordinary multi-turn interaction where models must accumulate and use evidence robustly.

Appendix A
Counterbalancing via Cyclic Rotation

Each MCQ probe presents
K
outcomes in a fixed order of letter-labeled positions (A, B, …). However, LLMs can exhibit position bias, where certain positions receive systematically higher or lower probability regardless of the outcome placed there. We counterbalance by cycling each outcome through every position. Concretely, we issue
K
copies of every probe, each applying a distinct cyclic shift. Because each outcome occupies each position exactly once across the
K
shifts, under an additive model of position bias (Proposition
1
), averaging the per-outcome probabilities across shifts exactly cancels the bias. The cost is
K
forward passes per probe rather than the
K!
required by a full permutation design (e.g.,
K\!=\!5
calls instead of
120
).

Proposition 1

(Position-bias cancellation under cyclic rotation)
.

Let
o_{1},\ldots,o_{K}
be the outcomes of a
K
-way MCQ and let
p(o_{i})
denote the model’s true outcome probability absent position effects.
Define the cyclic permutation
\sigma_{j}(i)=((i-1+j)\bmod K)+1
for shift
j\in\{0,\ldots,K-1\}
, and suppose position bias is additive: under shift
j
, outcome
o_{i}
occupies position
\sigma_{j}(i)
and the model assigns it probability

p(o_{i})\;+\;\delta_{\sigma_{j}(i)},

where
\delta_{\ell}
are position offsets.
Then the cyclic-rotation average recovers
p(o_{i})
exactly:

\hat{p}(o_{i})\;=\;\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}\bigl[p(o_{i})+\delta_{\sigma_{j}(i)}\bigr]\;=\;p(o_{i}).

Proof.

First, note that
\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\delta_{\ell}=0
. This is because, for any fixed shift
j
, the observed probabilities must sum to one:
\sum_{i=1}^{K}[p(o_{i})+\delta_{\sigma_{j}(i)}]=1
. Since
\sum_{i}p(o_{i})=1
and
\sigma_{j}
is a permutation of
\{1,\ldots,K\}
, the
\delta
terms must cancel.

Second, because
\sigma_{j}(i)=((i-1+j)\bmod K)+1
, as
j
ranges over
\{0,\ldots,K-1\}
the value
\sigma_{j}(i)
cycles through every position
\{1,\ldots,K\}
exactly once. Therefore

\hat{p}(o_{i})=p(o_{i})+\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}\delta_{\sigma_{j}(i)}=p(o_{i})+\frac{1}{K}\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\delta_{\ell}=p(o_{i}),

as desired.
∎

As a diagnostic, the average probability assigned to position
\ell
across all
K
shifts is
\frac{1}{K}+\delta_{\ell}
, so we report
\delta_{\ell}=\bar{p}(\ell)-1/K
to quantify the residual position bias throughout the paper.

C.2
User Type Selection and Fitting

For the recommender-system task to measure whether a model infers a user’s
taste
, the underlying user types must capture
what
a user likes, not merely
how generously they rate
. The two are easily confused: some people rate almost everything highly and others rate almost everything low, and this difference in overall generosity is easy to detect but says nothing about taste. We therefore want types that a user’s ratings can identify, yet that are separated by genre preference rather than by overall rating level.

Fitting procedure.

We fit the types from MovieLens. First, we subtract each user’s average rating from all of their ratings, so that what remains reflects how much they like each genre relative to their own baseline rather than how high they rate in general—a harsh rater and a generous rater with the same tastes then look alike. We reduce these per-genre preferences with PCA to a few interpretable taste axes (arthouse vs. blockbuster, family vs. adult content) and cluster users with similar preferences using
k
-means. Each cluster becomes a type, summarized by how its members tend to rate each movie—the per-type rating likelihoods
\phi_{m,k}
—which we then refine so they better fit the cluster’s actual ratings. We use four types, enough to be distinguishable while staying interpretable. The resulting model has type prior
\pi\approx[0.12,\,0.18,\,0.28,\,0.42]
; under it the Bayesian reference identifies the true type with
0.82
accuracy after 20 ratings (chance
0.25
), and no two types differ only in overall rating level rather than taste. The same
\pi
and
\phi
are used downstream, both in the Bayesian reference and in the LLM prompts.

Storyboard extraction system prompt (structural prompt below; example aspects are synthetic substitutes)

The system prompt is reproduced below. The accompanying user message is the

You are an expert at analyzing personal narratives about social conflict.
Your task is to decompose an AITA (Am I The Asshole) post into a structured
storyboard of discrete informational aspects.

An AITA post is a personal narrative (Labov, 1972) in which the narrator
describes a social conflict and asks whether they were in the wrong. It is
also an account (Scott & Lyman, 1968) - a linguistic device for explaining
potentially blameworthy behavior. Your job is to identify the structural
components of both the narrative and the account.

## What is an aspect?

An aspect is a single, self-contained piece of information that would affect
a reasonable person’s moral judgment of the poster. Each aspect should:

1. Contain ONE distinct piece of information - not two things bundled together
2. Be 1-3 sentences long
3. Be written in first person, from the poster’s perspective
4. Be understandable on its own without the other aspects
5. Not repeat information from another aspect

## Categories

Each aspect belongs to exactly one of six categories. These are derived from
the narrative structure of personal experience (Labov, 1972) and accounts
theory (Scott & Lyman, 1968; Schoenbach, 1990).

### ORIENTATION
Background information that establishes the situation before any conflict
occurs: who is involved, their relationships, relevant history, the
circumstances.

This is Labov’s orientation component - the "who, what, when, where" that
orients the listener before the complicating action begins.

When to use: The information sets the scene but does not advance the conflict.
It could be removed and the core dispute would still make sense, though
context would be lost.

Examples:
"My partner and I have lived together for six years. We share a
one-bedroom apartment and split rent evenly."
"I run a small bakery downtown. We start baking fresh bread around four in
the morning."
"My son was in second grade at the time. We invited his whole class to his
birthday party - around eighteen kids."

### ACTION_POSTER
Something the poster DID that is relevant to the moral judgment - a specific
behavior, decision, or response by the poster.

This is Labov’s complicating action where the poster is the agent. Per
Heider’s (1958) attribution theory, identifying the poster as the causal
agent is what makes this an action the judge can assign blame or credit for.

When to use: The poster performed a concrete action, made a decision, or
chose a response. This is about WHAT THEY DID, not why they did it (that’s
ACCOUNT).

Important: If a sentence contains both an action AND a justification for it,
SPLIT them into separate aspects. "I said no because I had an early meeting"
contains an ACTION_POSTER ("I said no") and an ACCOUNT ("because I had an
early meeting"). The action is the behavior; the account is the framing.

Examples:
"I told my partner I was switching to the morning shift. I didn’t ask - I
told him."
"I rolled my eyes at her in front of everyone at the table."
"I told Theo’s parents that I was going to have to ask them to leave."
"I’ve been keeping the leftover muffins anyway, just for personal use
at home."

### ACTION_OTHER
Something another party did that is relevant to the moral judgment - their
behavior, responses, provocations, or refusals.

This is Labov’s complicating action where someone other than the poster is
the agent. Per Heider, attributing agency to the other party shifts how the
judge assigns responsibility.

When to use: Another person performed a concrete action or gave a concrete
response within the conflict. Their behavior can push the judgment toward or
against the poster depending on how reasonable it was.

Examples:
"Casey argued that I used to be late on rent too, so it wasn’t fair to
call her out for it now."
"Theo’s parents shrugged and said their kid was just being a kid and
that’s what kids do."
"My partner said I’d be putting my preferences ahead of the family."
"He stuck his tongue out at me and ran off again."

### OUTCOME
What resulted from the actions - consequences, aftermath, fallout, emotional
states that followed.

This is Labov’s resolution component - how things turned out. Includes both
concrete consequences (someone left, someone stopped speaking) and emotional
aftermath (someone felt terrible, someone cried).

When to use: Something happened AS A RESULT of the conflict. The causal
direction goes: actions -> outcome.

Examples:
"She didn’t speak to me for the rest of the evening."
"He walked out and hasn’t returned my messages all day."
"Right after that, he started wearing a hoodie indoors all the time and
refuses to take it off."
"Theo’s parents have been holding a grudge for over six months."

### ACCOUNT
The poster’s own explanation, defense, or framing of their behavior.

This comes from Accounts Theory (Scott & Lyman, 1968). When people describe
potentially blameworthy actions, they use specific strategies to manage
responsibility:

EXCUSE - admits the act may look bad, denies full responsibility:
"I didn’t have a choice."
"I didn’t know she was that upset."
"I was exhausted and not thinking clearly."

JUSTIFICATION - admits responsibility, denies the act was wrong:
"It’s my apartment and I have every right to set rules."
"She had it coming after what she said."
"I was just trying to spare him the embarrassment."

CONCESSION - accepts some blame, provides mitigating context:
"I know the eye-roll was rude, but I was at the end of my patience."
"I feel bad about it, but I just couldn’t handle it anymore."

When to use: The poster is EXPLAINING or DEFENDING their behavior, not
describing a concrete action. Look for "because", "since", "I felt
justified", "I was just trying to", "I didn’t mean to", "in my defense" -
these signal account-giving. Also includes the poster’s emotional framing
when used defensively ("I felt terrible" as a bid for sympathy).

Important: Accounts are not neutral information. They are strategic
constructions - the poster’s attempt to manage how their behavior is
perceived. Separating accounts from actions lets us analyze how much the
judge is influenced by facts versus framing.

Examples:
"I argued it doesn’t really change logistics. I’m just swapping morning
hours for evening hours."
"I tried to argue I was just looking out for him. The older kids had
been teasing him about his hair."
"I feel guilty about keeping the muffins, but knowing they’d be thrown
out otherwise, it doesn’t seem as serious to me."
"I’m an early riser by nature. Morning shift is more flexible and I get
a small premium."

### REACTION
A third party’s judgment, opinion, or response regarding the poster’s
behavior.

In Schoenbach’s (1990) account episode framework, an AITA post is phase 3
(the account). Third-party reactions within the post are either phase 2
(the reproach that prompted the poster to seek judgment) or phase 4
(someone else’s evaluation). Both function as external moral evidence -
another person’s verdict signal about whether the poster was wrong.

When to use: Someone other than the poster AND other than the main other
party weighs in on the situation - either criticizing the poster (reproach)
or supporting them (validation). Also includes collective responses
("everyone agreed", "the other parents took my side").

Examples:
"Robin told me I was being a hypocrite since I used to be late on rent
too."
"The other parents who witnessed it were almost all on my side."
"My sister thinks I’ve been a little harsh."
"Theo’s parents were grateful when they found out."
"During an argument, he told me how embarrassed and self-conscious he
felt and that I took away the one thing he liked about himself."

## Category disambiguation rules

When an aspect could plausibly belong to multiple categories, apply these
rules:

1. ACTION vs ACCOUNT: If the poster DID something AND explains why in the
same breath, split them. The test: "Could I describe this without the
reason?" If yes, the reason is a separate ACCOUNT. Example: "I kicked them
out because they were disrespectful" -> ACTION_POSTER ("I kicked them out")
+ ACCOUNT ("because they were disrespectful").

2. ACTION_OTHER vs REACTION: If the other party is the person the conflict
is ABOUT (the main antagonist/counterpart), it’s ACTION_OTHER. If they are a
THIRD PARTY commenting on the situation (friend, spouse, parent, bystander),
it’s REACTION. The test: "Is this person one of the two sides of the
conflict, or an observer?" Casey’s argument is ACTION_OTHER (she’s a party
to the conflict). Robin’s comment is REACTION (he’s a bystander weighing
in).

3. ORIENTATION vs ACTION: If the information is about the state of affairs
BEFORE the conflict began, it’s ORIENTATION. If it’s part of the conflict
sequence itself, it’s ACTION. The test: "Did this happen before or during
the dispute?"

4. OUTCOME vs ACTION: If something happened as a RESULT of the conflict
actions (downstream effect), it’s OUTCOME. If it’s part of the active
conflict exchange (someone does X, someone responds with Y), it’s ACTION.
The test: "Is this a move in the conflict, or a consequence of how the
conflict went?"

5. ACCOUNT vs ORIENTATION: Poster’s personal traits or preferences cited to
EXPLAIN their behavior are ACCOUNT ("I’m an early riser by nature" ->
justifies wanting morning shift). Poster’s personal traits cited purely for
context with no defensive purpose are ORIENTATION ("I have anxiety and
confidence issues" -> sets the scene).

## Valence

Valence captures the directional push of an aspect on moral judgment,
following Hogarth & Einhorn’s (1992) concept of positive/negative evidence.

For each aspect, ask: "If a judge heard ONLY this one piece of information -
nothing else - would it push them toward thinking the poster was in the
wrong, not in the wrong, or neither?"

pro_poster - Makes the poster look BETTER. Pushes toward NTA.
The poster was reasonable, had a legitimate reason, showed restraint, was
provoked, or the other party was clearly in the wrong. Third-party support
for the poster.
Per Weiner (1985): the poster’s action appears externally caused,
uncontrollable, or unstable (one-time) - factors that reduce blame.

against_poster - Makes the poster look WORSE. Pushes toward YTA.
The poster was rude, selfish, made a unilateral decision, ignored
someone’s feelings, or escalated unnecessarily. Third-party criticism of
the poster.
Per Weiner: the poster’s action appears internally caused, controllable,
and stable (pattern of behavior) - factors that increase blame.

neutral - Does not clearly push either direction.
Pure context, factual setup, ambiguous situations where reasonable people
would disagree about which direction it pushes.

CRITICAL: Valence is about the ASPECT IN ISOLATION, not about the overall
story. A background fact can be neutral even in a story where the poster is
clearly wrong. An excuse can be pro_poster even if the judge might not find
it convincing - it’s still a frame that pushes toward NTA. Tag each aspect
based on its own content, not on your overall impression of the poster.

## Importance

Importance measures how much an aspect matters for the moral judgment. Use
this scale:

5 - DECISIVE: This aspect alone could determine or reverse the verdict.
Removing it would likely change the judgment. Central to the moral
question.
Examples: "I made the decision unilaterally without consulting my
partner." / "He asked me to throw away his old guitar and I did it."

4 - SIGNIFICANT: Strongly affects the judgment but isn’t solely decisive.
Provides major weight to one side.
Examples: "Casey argued I was being unfair since I used to be late
on rent too." / "Theo’s parents watched the whole thing and did
nothing."

3 - RELEVANT: Contributes meaningfully to the judgment. Adds texture or
nuance that would be missed if absent.
Examples: "My partner doesn’t like being home alone in the evenings."
/ "I feel guilty about it, but the muffins would be thrown out
anyway."

2 - CONTEXTUAL: Provides useful background but wouldn’t shift the verdict
on its own. Helps the judge understand the situation.
Examples: "We share a one-bedroom apartment." / "It was a
twenty-minute drive."

1 - MINOR: Incidental detail. Included for completeness but carries
minimal moral weight.
Examples: "We agreed to meet at the corner cafe." / "This was a
couple of years ago."

## Grain size and count

Target 6-10 aspects per post. The right number depends on the post’s
complexity:
- Short, simple posts (one event, clear conflict): 5-7 aspects
- Longer posts (multiple events, several parties): 8-10 aspects
- If you are producing more than 10, you are splitting too finely

Each aspect should be a JUDGMENT-RELEVANT unit - a piece of information that
could shift a reasonable person’s moral assessment. Do NOT create aspects
for:
- Details that don’t affect the moral judgment ("we stopped for gas on
the way")
- Repetitions of the same information in different words
- Narrative filler or transitions
- The "so AITA?" coda - this is the frame of the post, not informational
content

DO create separate aspects when:
- Two events carry different moral weight (even if described in one
sentence)
- A single sentence contains an action AND a justification - split them
- Different people’s actions pull the judgment in different directions

## Structural requirements

Every storyboard must include:
- At least one ORIENTATION (the judge needs context)
- At least one ACTION_POSTER (there must be something the poster did to
judge)
- At least one ACTION_OTHER (there must be the other side’s behavior)
- The specific event or request that triggered the conflict (typically
the highest-importance ACTION_POSTER or ACTION_OTHER - the moment the
situation went from normal to disputed)

## Edge cases

Posts with EDITS or UPDATES: Treat edit content the same as the main post.
If the edit adds new information, include it as additional aspects. If it
merely clarifies something already covered, do not create a duplicate.

Very short posts: Some posts are only 2-3 sentences. It is acceptable to
produce fewer than 6 aspects if the post genuinely contains fewer than 6
judgment-relevant pieces of information. Do not pad with trivial aspects.

Multiple conflicts: If the post describes two distinct conflicts, capture
both. The aspects should cover each conflict’s key elements. The storyboard
may be on the longer side (8-10 aspects).

Poster is clearly NTA: Even when the poster is obviously not in the wrong,
extract aspects faithfully. There should still be aspects that are
against_poster (every conflict has two sides, even if one is weak). If the
post is genuinely one-sided, having mostly pro_poster aspects with only 1-2
against_poster is correct - do not force balance.

## Output format

Respond with ONLY a valid JSON array. No explanation, no preamble, no
markdown fences.
Each element must have exactly these five fields:
"id" - sequential: "a1", "a2", "a3", ...
"category" - exactly one of: ORIENTATION, ACTION_POSTER, ACTION_OTHER,
OUTCOME, ACCOUNT, REACTION
"content" - the aspect text (1-3 sentences, first person, from the
poster’s perspective)
"valence" - exactly one of: pro_poster, neutral, against_poster
"importance" - integer 1-5 (how much this aspect matters for the moral
judgment)

## Self-check before outputting

Verify all of the following before producing your output:
1. Every aspect contains a DIFFERENT piece of information (no redundancy)
2. You have at least one ORIENTATION, one ACTION_POSTER, and one
ACTION_OTHER
3. The triggering event - the specific moment the conflict started - is
captured
4. The aspects together cover all key information from the post (nothing
important is missing)
5. Actions and accounts are SEPARATED - no aspect contains both "I did X"
and "because Y"
6. Valence tags reflect each aspect IN ISOLATION, not your overall
impression
7. Importance scores follow the 1-5 anchors defined above
8. You have between 5 and 10 aspects total
9. The output is valid JSON - a single array of objects, nothing else

D.2.2
Advisor System Prompts

The multi-turn-passive condition uses a minimal advisor
system prompt instructing the advisor to answer the binary MCQ. The
multi-turn-active condition instead uses a conversational, therapist-style
system prompt. This keeps the advisor from slipping into solution-drafting
mode, which would derail the user simulator. Both are reproduced here for
completeness.

Advisor system prompt, multi-turn passive

You are judging whether someone was in the wrong in a social situation.
They will describe aspects of the situation. Respond with only A or B.

A = YTA (You’re The Asshole), B = NTA (Not The Asshole)

Advisor system prompt, multi-turn active (therapist framing)

Someone is going to tell you about a social conflict they were involved in.
Have a conversation with them, ask questions, reflect back what you’re
hearing, and point out things they might not be seeing. Don’t just agree
with them, and don’t offer solutions or draft messages for them. Keep your
responses conversational, no bullet points, lists, or markdown.

D.3
Designing the User Simulator: What Worked and What Did Not

To keep the active and passive settings comparable, the simulator must reveal the storyboard facts in a fixed order, yet read naturally enough that the advisor engages with it as it would with a real user. To balance the two, we design the steering protocol described in Sec.
5.1
. Below we describe the failure modes it addresses, grouped by whether they compromise the order of the evidence or the naturalness of the persona.

Order and fidelity of fact revelation.

Because the simulator’s system prompt contains the full storyboard, the simulator sees the whole arc and is drawn to its most dramatic parts, which shows up in three ways. Skipping ahead is the most common: in an early version, 74% of fact-coverage failures were not dropped facts but the simulator jumping to later events, so we pair each per-turn fact with an explicit ordering cue and a reason to deliver otherwise unremarkable expository detail (the advisor needs it to follow what comes next). The handover is also model-dependent: repeating the fact verbatim led the Qwen models we evaluate to treat it as emphasis and deliver it, but led the LLaMA models to treat it as already covered and skip ahead, so we supply each fact as an abstract instruction rather than a quotation. Finally, worked examples leak: any concrete phrase in the recurring per-turn instruction is reproduced near-verbatim every turn, so we keep examples in the system prompt (set once) and let the per-turn channel carry only abstract direction.

Staying in character over a long conversation.

A second class of failures concerns persona stability rather than fact delivery. Control instructions leak into the reply when the steering is visible to the advisor: we inject the steering instruction as an ephemeral message, generate, then remove it before it enters the persistent history, and require the reply wrapped in
<message>
tags, which together keep control instructions out of the advisor’s view and stop it from breaking character to address them. The persona also drifts toward agreeableness as the conversation lengthens and the assigned stance erodes; we counter with firmer style reminders after the third turn and by rotating the framing each turn, so the persona varies how it speaks without changing what it reveals. Direct questions derail the fact: when the advisor asks a question, the simulator tends to answer it and drop its assigned fact, so rather than suppress this we instruct it to answer first, then continue. Finally, the advisor’s format bleeds into the simulator: in its default assistant register the advisor slips into solution-drafting and markdown, and the simulator then mirrors that format, so we give the advisor a conversational, therapist-style system prompt that bars solutions, message-drafting, and markdown.

Residual failures.

Together these fixes let the simulator deliver the storyboard facts in order while maintaining its persona throughout the full conversation. And although a few facts still surface within one or two turns of their intended turn (3% of the time for Qwen models, 15% for LLaMA models), the intended flow of evidence stays largely consistent between the active and passive conditions.

Takeaway.

The balanced final verdict changes little by communication style, but advisor behavior does. E.3.1
Storyboard extraction prompt

Each triage case is decomposed once, off-line, into a list of 5–8 first-person
symptom aspects by a separate extraction model (Claude Opus 4.6). The same
extraction call also produces the self-diagnosis text used by the
Cyberchondriac style. The advisor model under evaluation never sees this
prompt; it only ever sees the conversational output of the patient simulator.

System, Storyboard extraction

You are an expert at analyzing patient symptom reports in primary care and emergency triage contexts. Your task is to decompose a patient’s symptom description into a structured storyboard of discrete informational aspects, and to generate a plausible self-diagnosis the patient might have researched online.

The patient report describes symptoms that a person is seeking advice about. Your job is to identify the clinical information components and tag each with its urgency signal, the directional push it would have on a triage decision if heard in isolation.

## What is an aspect?

An aspect is a single, self-contained piece of clinical information that could affect a triage decision. Each aspect should:

1. Contain ONE distinct piece of information, not two things bundled together

2. Be 1–3 sentences long

3. Be written in first person, from the patient’s perspective, as they would naturally say it

4. Be understandable on its own without the other aspects

5. Not repeat information from another aspect

The patient should sound like a layperson, not a clinician. Use everyday language, not clinical terminology. “My chest hurts” not “I am experiencing substernal chest pain.” The storyboard represents the raw facts; the patient simulator will later add profile-specific framing.

## Categories

Each aspect belongs to exactly one of six categories, derived from the Common Sense Model of illness representation (Leventhal et al., 1992) and clinical communication practice.

### CHIEF_COMPLAINT

The primary symptom or reason the patient is seeking advice. This is ALWAYS the first aspect and there is ALWAYS exactly one.

This is the identity-dimension anchor, the door-in complaint that frames all subsequent information. It should be the most prominent symptom as the patient would naturally lead with it.

When to use: The primary presenting symptom the patient would open with. Must be present, must be first.

Examples:

“I’ve been having chest pain for the past few hours.”

“I woke up this morning with a really bad headache that won’t go away.”

“My right knee has been swelling and hurting for about a week now.”

### SYMPTOM

A secondary symptom distinct from the chief complaint that is relevant to the triage decision.

This is an additional identity-dimension feature, a separate symptom the patient also has. Each SYMPTOM aspect covers one discrete additional complaint. When multiple secondary symptoms are present, create one aspect per symptom.

When to use: The patient has an additional symptom beyond the chief complaint that carries its own triage weight. Do not bundle, if two symptoms are distinct and each has independent triage relevance, create separate aspects.

Important: Distinguish SYMPTOM from SEVERITY. “I also have shortness of breath” is a SYMPTOM (a separate complaint). “The pain is the worst I’ve ever felt in my life” is SEVERITY (an intensity descriptor about the chief complaint or another symptom).

Examples:

“I’ve also been feeling really short of breath, especially when I try to do anything.”

“On top of the headache, I’ve been really sensitive to light, even dim light is painful.”

“I’ve also noticed my urine has been really dark, almost brown.”

### TIMELINE

Onset, duration, and progression of the chief complaint or key symptoms.

This is the timeline dimension from Leventhal, when it started, how long it has been going on, and whether it is getting better, staying the same, or getting worse.

When to use: Information about when symptoms began, how long they have lasted, or whether they have changed over time. Sudden vs. gradual onset is a major urgency signal in triage.

Examples:

“The pain started suddenly about two hours ago, out of nowhere.”

“This has been going on for about three weeks, slowly getting worse.”

“It started three days ago and has been pretty much the same since then, not getting better but not getting worse.”

### SEVERITY

The intensity, pain level, or functional impact of the symptoms.

This captures how bad the symptoms are, not what they are (that is SYMPTOM or CHIEF_COMPLAINT) but how severely they are affecting the patient. Includes pain ratings, functional impairment, and descriptors of intensity.

When to use: Information about how bad the symptoms feel, how much they interfere with daily activities, or intensity descriptors. Severity is often the most direct urgency signal.

Examples:

“The pain is honestly a 9 out of 10, it’s the worst headache I’ve ever had in my life.”

“I can barely get off the couch. Even walking to the bathroom takes it out of me.”

“It’s uncomfortable but I’d say it’s only about a 3 or 4, annoying but manageable.”

### CONTEXT

Relevant medical history, medications, lifestyle factors, or demographics that affect triage.

This captures background enabling and risk factors, information that would change the urgency of the same symptom set for a different patient. Includes past medical history, current medications, family history, and relevant lifestyle factors.

When to use: Background information that affects how dangerous the current symptoms are likely to be, prior conditions, known risk factors, relevant medication use, demographic risk context.

Examples:

“I have a history of blood clots, I had a DVT in my leg about two years ago.”

“I’m 58, I smoke about half a pack a day, and I have high blood pressure that I take medication for.”

“I’m 23 and pretty healthy, no major medical problems, no medications.”

### ASSOCIATED

Aggravating or relieving factors, associated features, or review-of-systems items that add triage information.

This captures clinical details that accompany the main symptoms but are distinct from them, things that make the symptoms better or worse, associated symptoms that flag specific dangerous syndromes, and system review items that would be asked about in a clinical assessment.

When to use: Information about what worsens or relieves symptoms, or associated features that are clinically relevant but do not constitute a separate named symptom. Also use for “flags”, red-flag features like radiation, diaphoresis, syncope, fever accompanying another chief complaint.

Important: If a finding is significant enough to stand alone as a separate complaint, it is a SYMPTOM, not ASSOCIATED. If it is a qualifier or accompanying feature of another symptom, it is ASSOCIATED.

Examples:

“The chest pain seems to get worse when I take a deep breath, and it gets a little better when I lean forward.”

“The headache started with some flashing lights in my vision, which lasted about 20 minutes before the headache hit.”

“The pain radiates down my left arm and I’ve been sweating more than usual.”

## Category disambiguation rules

When an aspect could plausibly belong to multiple categories, apply these rules:

1. CHIEF_COMPLAINT vs SYMPTOM: The chief complaint is what the patient opens with, their primary concern. Everything else is SYMPTOM. There is exactly one CHIEF_COMPLAINT, always first.

2. SYMPTOM vs SEVERITY: If it is a separate named complaint (shortness of breath, nausea, dizziness), it is SYMPTOM. If it describes how bad a symptom already named is (intensity, functional impact, pain rating), it is SEVERITY.

3. SYMPTOM vs ASSOCIATED: If the patient would mention it as a separate problem (“I’ve also been having X”), it is SYMPTOM. If it is a feature or qualifier of another symptom (“the pain also goes to my arm”), it is ASSOCIATED.

4. TIMELINE vs CONTEXT: TIMELINE is about when and how the current symptoms have evolved. CONTEXT is background history not about this episode (prior conditions, medications, baseline health status). “The headache started Tuesday” is TIMELINE. “I’ve had migraines before” is CONTEXT.

5. SEVERITY vs CONTEXT: Severity is about the current episode. Context is background. “This is the worst pain of my life” is SEVERITY. “I usually have a high pain tolerance” is CONTEXT.

6. TIMELINE vs SYMPTOM for escalation events: If a symptom that was previously present in one context now appears in a qualitatively different context, that new occurrence is a SYMPTOM, not just TIMELINE. The test: “Does this event carry independent triage weight, or is it merely tracking when the existing symptom started?” Pain that previously occurred only with exertion and now wakes the patient from sleep is a new SYMPTOM (rest angina is clinically distinct from exertional angina). A symptom that has simply lasted longer or gotten gradually worse without changing character is TIMELINE. When in doubt: if a clinician would treat this as a separate clinical event warranting its own assessment, make it a SYMPTOM.

## Urgency signal

The urgency signal captures the directional push of an aspect on the triage decision, following
13
’s concept of positive/negative evidence valence.

For each aspect, ask: “If a triage clinician heard ONLY this one piece of information, nothing else, would it push them toward thinking this patient needs more urgent care, less urgent care, or is it neutral?”

alarming
, Pushes toward HIGHER urgency (Emergency or Urgent).

Sudden onset, severe intensity, dangerous syndromes (chest pain + radiation + diaphoresis), high-risk patient profile for this symptom, red-flag features, rapid progression, functional collapse. Any feature a clinician would flag as “this patient may need immediate attention.”

neutral
, Does not clearly push either direction.

Could be consistent with a wide range of urgency levels. Pure context, ambiguous features, symptoms that are common at all severity levels.

reassuring
, Pushes toward LOWER urgency (Observation or Routine).

Long stable duration with no change, mild intensity, young healthy patient with no risk factors, symptom reliably better with over-the-counter treatment, no associated dangerous features, benign aggravating factors. Features that lower the likelihood of a serious underlying cause.

CRITICAL: Urgency signal is about the ASPECT IN ISOLATION, not about the overall case urgency. A CONTEXT aspect about a patient being young and healthy is reassuring even if the case is ultimately urgent. Tag each aspect based on its own directional push, independent of your overall impression of the case.

## Importance

Importance measures how much an aspect matters for the triage decision. Use this scale:

5, DECISIVE: This aspect alone could determine or reverse the triage level. Removing it would likely change the urgency classification. Central to the triage question.

Examples: “The chest pain radiates down my left arm and I’ve been sweating” / “I suddenly can’t see out of my right eye”

4, SIGNIFICANT: Strongly affects the triage judgment but isn’t solely decisive. Provides major weight toward a particular urgency level.

Examples: “The pain started suddenly rather than gradually” / “I have a history of heart disease”

3, RELEVANT: Contributes meaningfully to the triage picture. Adds information that would be missed if absent.

Examples: “This has been going on for three days” / “I also have mild nausea”

2, CONTEXTUAL: Provides useful background but wouldn’t shift the level on its own. Helps the clinician understand the patient’s situation.

Examples: “I’m 35 years old and otherwise healthy” / “The pain is about a 4 out of 10”

1, MINOR: Incidental detail. Clinically real but carries minimal triage weight.

Examples: “I’ve been drinking a lot of water lately” / “My sleep has been a bit off”

## Grain size and count

Target 5–8 aspects per case. The right number depends on the case’s clinical complexity:

- Simple cases (one symptom, clear trajectory): 5–6 aspects

- Moderate cases (several symptoms, some context): 6–7 aspects

- Complex cases (multiple systems, significant history): 7–8 aspects

- If you are producing more than 8, you are splitting too finely

Each aspect should carry independent triage relevance. Do NOT create aspects for:

- Repeated information in different words

- Details with no plausible effect on triage (what the patient was wearing)

- Filler or transitions from the original narrative

DO create separate aspects when:

- Two symptoms have different urgency signals even if mentioned together

- A severity descriptor is so extreme it warrants its own urgency weighting

- A context factor creates a different risk profile than what was already described

## Self-diagnosis (for cyberchondriac profile)

In addition to the storyboard, generate one self_diagnosis field: a plausible but potentially incorrect diagnosis that a patient might find after internet research on their chief complaint. This is used to give the cyberchondriac profile a specific anchoring theory.

The self-diagnosis must be:

- A real medical condition with a recognizable name

- Plausible given the chief complaint (a patient could reasonably find it via internet search)

- Not necessarily correct, the point is that it could explain some symptoms but may miss or contradict others

- Ideally different in urgency from the true triage level at least some of the time

- 1–5 words: a condition name, not a sentence

Examples: “acid reflux”, “tension headache”, “anxiety attack”, “pulled muscle”, “viral gastroenteritis”
