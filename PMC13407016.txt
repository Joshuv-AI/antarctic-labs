Longitudinal Breath Sound Changes During Erythromycin Therapy in Paediatric Diffuse Panbronchiolitis
Authors: Katsuhito Iikura, Hideshi Fujinaga, Haruka Tokoro, Junichi Koinuma, Yuji Takizawa, Shinji Oana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDiffuse panbronchiolitis (DPB) is a chronic inflammatory lung disease of unknown aetiology predominantly affecting middle‐aged adults, characterized by chronic sinusitis, productive cough and exertional dyspnea. DPB is rare in children, and its clinical course remains poorly understood. We report a case of DPB in a 13‐year‐old girl in whom breath sounds were recorded throughout the treatment course using an electronic stethoscope. The patient was treated with low‐dose macrolide therapy, which led to improvement in clinical symptoms and pulmonary function, accompanied by parallel changes in abnormal breath sounds. Breath sound analysis revealed that coarse crackles resolved first, followed by sequential amelioration of adventitious continuous sounds from the peripheral to the central airways. Furthermore, a treatment duration of at least 6 months was suggested to be necessary. Breath sound analysis may provide objective information regarding respiratory function and contribute to a deeper understanding of the pathophysiology of this rare disease.Keywords: diffuse panbronchiolitis, electronic stethoscope, lung sounds, paediatrics, spectrogram

Introduction

Diffuse panbronchiolitis (DPB) is a chronic inflammatory lung disease of unknown aetiology that develops predominantly in middle‐aged adults in East Asia [1]. The primary site of inflammation is the respiratory bronchioles, and it is characterized by chronic sinusitis, productive cough, exertional dyspnea and diffuse centrilobular nodular opacities on chest computed tomography (CT). Diagnosis requires all three of the following major criteria: (1) persistent cough, sputum, and exertional dyspnea, (2) a history of chronic sinusitis and (3) diffuse small nodular shadows on chest x‐ray or centrilobular nodular shadows on chest CT; along with two of the following minor criteria: (1) coarse crackles, wheezes, rhonchi or squawks, (2) FEV1/FVC < 70% and PaO2 < 80 mmHg and (3) cold agglutinin titre ≥ 64 [2].

Although DPB was once associated with high mortality, low‐dose macrolide therapy has dramatically improved prognosis [3]. However, DPB is rare in children [4], and its clinical course remains poorly understood. Here, we report a paediatric case with serial breath sound recordings using an electronic stethoscope to add to the limited clinical evidence in this population.

Case Report

A 13‐year‐old Japanese girl with a 10‐year history of bronchial asthma was referred to our department because of persistent productive cough, wheezing and exertional dyspnea that was unresponsive to inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) therapy.

At presentation, oxygen saturation was 93% on room air. Auscultation revealed stridor and coarse crackles bilaterally. Laboratory evaluation showed a white blood cell count of 7200/μL, C‐reactive protein of 0.28 mg/dL and an elevated cold agglutinin titre (1:128).

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated reduced FVC (75.9% predicted) and FEV1 (55.6% predicted) without bronchodilator reversibility (Table 1). CT revealed centrilobular micronodules, bilateral lower lobe consolidation and findings consistent with sinusitis. Based on the diagnostic criteria, DPB was diagnosed and low‐dose macrolide therapy with erythromycin (200 mg/day) was initiated. ICS therapy was discontinued, and a mucolytic agent was continued concurrently.

Breath sounds were recorded at each outpatient visit using a 3 M Littmann Model 4000 electronic stethoscope in diaphragm mode and analysed with the Littmann Stethoscope Assistant software using spectrogram‐based frequency analysis (0–1000 Hz). Recordings were obtained from the bilateral posterior chest walls for 8 s each. Breath sounds from the right posterior chest are shown in Figure 1. Auscultatory findings were described using terminology documented in the medical charts. The term ‘continuous adventitious sounds’ was used to collectively describe wheezes and rhonchi.

Coarse crackles absent during inspiration; continuous adventitious sounds present during both phases (peak 240‐250 Hz). (d) Six months after treatment. No abnormal sounds during inspiration; low‐frequency continuous adventitious sound (peak ~125 Hz) persisting during expiration only. (e) Seven months after treatment. No abnormal sounds were detected. (f) Twelve months after treatment. No abnormal sounds were detected.

At the first visit, spectrogram analysis revealed coarse crackles throughout inspiration and expiration, along with inspiratory continuous adventitious sounds at peak frequencies of approximately 125 Hz (Figure 1a).

After 1 month, pulmonary function improved (Table 1), though productive cough and exertional dyspnea persisted. Auscultation revealed wheezing and fine crackles in the bilateral lower lung fields. Spectrogram analysis showed reduced coarse crackles, persistent inspiratory continuous adventitious sounds (125–250 Hz), and newly developed expiratory continuous adventitious sounds at 125 Hz (Figure 1b).

At 3 months, exertional dyspnea had improved. Auscultation revealed persistent stridor and wheezing. Spectrogram analysis (Figure 1c) showed intermittent inspiratory continuous adventitious sounds at 250 Hz, while the background coarse crackles had disappeared.

Expiratory coarse crackles and continuous adventitious sounds at 240 Hz persisted.

By 6 months, coarse crackles had resolved, though wheezing and rhonchi persisted. Spectrogram analysis (Figure 1d) showed no inspiratory abnormalities, with a residual low frequency continuous adventitious sound at 125 Hz during expiration.

From 6 months after treatment initiation onward, no abnormal breath sounds were detected by either auscultation or spectrogram analysis (Figure 1e,f). Low‐dose macrolide therapy was continued for 24 months and discontinued without recurrence (Table 1).

Discussion

Breath sound analysis in this case enabled objective tracking of the clinical course of paediatric DPB. Abnormal findings on sound spectrograms resolved in parallel with improvements in clinical symptoms and pulmonary function. Coarse crackles improved in the early stage of treatment. Crackles are thought to originate from airway collapse and reopening associated with airway inflammation and retained secretions [5], and their improvement likely reflected reduced inflammation and decreased secretions with treatment.

Concurrent with crackle improvement, continuous adventitious sounds, which had been present only during inspiration at baseline, became audible during both inspiratory and expiratory phases. These sounds may reflect airflow through narrowed airways [5], and this change may reflect recovery of airway patency and airflow following improvement in retained secretions, making the sounds audible during both respiratory phases. Subsequently, the sounds became confined to expiration before eventually disappearing, with the peak frequency decreasing from approximately 250 to 100 Hz. This frequency shift may suggest that airway narrowing improved first in the peripheral airways and subsequently in the central airways [5]. Breath sound analysis also showed that abnormal breath sounds resolved between 6 and 7 months after treatment initiation, suggesting the potential utility of breath sound analysis as an objective indicator for determining treatment duration. This finding was consistent with the recommended treatment duration of at least 6 months reported in the literature [1]. Furthermore, although the medical records described wheezes, fine crackles and stridor, which are generally regarded as high‐frequency breath sounds, spectral analysis did not identify corresponding high‐frequency components. Instead, the detected sounds consisted entirely of frequencies below 250 Hz. This suggests that breath sound analysis may provide objective findings independent of subjective auscultatory interpretation. Although this single case may not be generalizable to all paediatric cases, the present case suggests that breath sound analysis may provide novel insights into disease pathophysiology and objective acoustic evidence to support treatment decisions.
