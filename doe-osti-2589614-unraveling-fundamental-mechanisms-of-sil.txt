ABSTRACT
This research project investigates the fundamental mechanisms of silicon nitride (SiN) crystal-
lization, aiming to enhance the understanding of this critical material in microelectronics manu-
facturing. Through a collaborative effort between Sandia National Laboratories, the University
of Tennessee, and the University of Florida, we developed a comprehensive framework that in-
tegrates experimental techniques, atomistic modeling, meso-scale simulations, and an integrated
multi-scale model to capture this physical phenomenon on multiple time and length scales . The
project developed a new machine learning based atomistic potential and utilized advanced phase
field modeling to capture the complexities of polycrystalline growth and the influence of mechanical
stresses on crystallization dynamics. By employing a grain tracker algorithm, the meso-scale model
effectively identified and tracked individual crystal grains, enabling the simulation of anisotropic
growth behaviors reflective of SiN’s physical properties.
The integration of atomistic simulations with meso-scale modeling created a powerful multi-scale
framework that validated atomistic inputs and enhanced predictive accuracy for crystallization
dynamics at larger scales, validated experimentally. This adaptable modeling capability not only
accelerates development times by informing manufacturing processes but also serves as a valuable
starting point for understanding crystallization physics in similar materials. The insights gained
from this research unlock new opportunities for the development of advanced materials tailored for
future microelectronics and photonics applications. Overall, this project represents a significant
advancement in understanding of fundamental physics of SiN and establishes a foundation for future
research in material science, bridging the gap between atomic-level phenomena and macroscopic
material behaviors for practical applications.

1. INTRODUCTION

The development of advanced microelectronics is critical to maintaining the United States’ strategic
capabilities, particularly in the realm of nuclear deterrence. At Sandia National Laboratories, the
fabrication of silicon nitride (SiN) is a fundamental capability; this material is used in the fabri-
cation of microelectronics across multiple applications. There is a need to better understand the
underlying physics behind the phenomenon of anomalous crystallization in SiN to further ensure
reliability of fabrication processes especially as complexity of devices and film stacks increases and
development times are expedited. The primary objective of this project is to close the knowledge
gaps in the fundamental understanding of the nucleation and crystallization physics in SiN, which
will improve overall reliability for long lifetime microelectronics applications while speeding up
development times for future projects. This project, titled "Unraveling Fundamental Mechanisms
of Silicon Nitride Crystallization in Microelectronics Manufacturing," aims to address these chal-
lenges by developing multi-scale predictive capabilities that elucidate the underlying physics of SiN
crystallization. A key component of this research involves collaboration between Sandia and the
University of Florida to develop a new machine learning-based atomistic potential. This innovative
approach will enhance the accuracy and efficiency of atomistic simulations, allowing for a more
precise characterization of the crystallization processes. A second collaboration between Sandia
and the University of Tennessee for experimental growth and characterization of crystalline SiN
that feeds into model development and validation across multiple scales. The primary objective of
this research is to decouple the fundamental physical mechanisms governing nucleation and crystal-
lization processes in SiN. By employing a combination of atomistic modeling, including the newly
developed machine learning potentials, and meso/continuum-scale modeling, this study seeks to
refine and validate models through the aforementioned experimental data on samples fabricated at
MESA. The integration of inputs from the atomistic models into the meso/continuum-scale frame-
work will enable a comprehensive understanding of how local phenomena influence larger-scale
behaviors, ultimately leading to predictive capabilities that can inform manufacturing processes.
The successful implementation of these models is expected to enhance Sandia’s microelectronics
manufacturing processes, particularly for nuclear deterrent applications, by drastically reducing de-
velopment times, improving device yields, and increasing confidence in long-term device reliability.
By leveraging unique experimental techniques, computational modeling, and collaboration with
academic partners, this project represents a pivotal step toward predictive process optimizations in
SiN fabrication and ensuring the reliability of critical microelectronic devices.

2. METHODOLOGY

2.1. Overall research approach

This project aims to address the challenges associated with knowledge gaps in the underlying
physics of anomalous crystallization of silicon nitride (SiN) in microelectronics. The primary
objective is to develop multi-scale predictive capabilities that elucidate the underlying physics of SiN
crystallization, thereby enhancing microelectronics manufacturing processes by enabling predictive
failure analysis through modeling and simulation and potentially unlocking new material capabilities
for future microelectronics devices via a controlled crystallization process. The overall research
approach is characterized by a synergistic combination of experimental techniques, computational
modeling, and collaborative efforts with academic partners. This integrated strategy allows for
a comprehensive exploration of the crystallization mechanisms in SiN, leveraging the strengths
of each component to inform and validate the others. By employing advanced experimental
methods such as in situ TEM and laser-induced heating, the project captures real-time crystallization
dynamics, while atomistic simulations provide insights into the fundamental interactions at the
atomic level. Meso-scale modeling complements these efforts by simulating the crystallization
processes at engineering scales, enabling the examination of how local phenomena influence larger-
scale behaviors. This holistic approach not only facilitates the identification of critical parameters
affecting crystallization but also fosters the development of predictive models that can be applied
to optimize manufacturing processes in microelectronics.

2.2. Experimental approach

The research commenced with the establishment of a collaborative laboratory effort involving
Sandia National Laboratories and the University of Tennessee, where a state-of-the-art facility was
set up from scratch in the first FY of this project. This project served as one of the first exemplars for
the new facility, showcasing its capabilities and establishing a foundation for future collaborations.
Low-pressure chemical vapor deposition (LPCVD) was employed to fabricate SiN films on silicon
substrates, with thermal cycling treatments ranging from 1000 °C to 1150 °C applied to observe
crystallization behavior under various processing conditions. The crystallization dynamics were
monitored using in situ transmission electron microscopy (TEM), specifically a JEOL 2100 TEM
operating at 200 keV, combined with laser-induced heating from an IPG Photonics 1064 nm fiber
laser. This setup allowed for real-time observation of crystallization processes with high spatial
and temporal resolution. Image processing techniques were utilized to extract quantitative data on
crystalline size and density, enabling the calculation of nucleation and growth kinetics. Automated
Crystallographic Orientation Mapping (ACOM) and Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS)
were employed to analyze the crystallized regions, providing insights into the structural and chemical

 15

characteristics of the resulting crystals. The characterization of materials was a pivotal aspect of
this research, leveraging the advanced facilities and expertise available at both Sandia National
Laboratories and the University of Tennessee. This collaboration enabled a comprehensive analysis
of the crystallization processes and material properties of silicon nitride. At Sandia, state-of-
the-art characterization techniques, including in situ transmission electron microscopy (TEM)
and advanced imaging methods, provided real-time insights into the crystallization dynamics and
structural evolution of SiN films. Concurrently, the University of Tennessee contributed its expertise
in materials science and access to complementary characterization tools, such as X-ray diffraction
(XRD) and atomic force microscopy (AFM), which facilitated the assessment of crystallographic
orientation, phase purity, and surface morphology. This integrated approach not only enhanced
the understanding of the fundamental mechanisms governing SiN crystallization but also allowed
for the validation of computational models against experimental observations. By combining the
strengths of both institutions, the research team was able to achieve a more holistic characterization
of materials, ultimately leading to improved predictive capabilities and enhanced reliability in
microelectronics manufacturing.

2.3. Atomistic scale modeling approach

In parallel with the experimental work, atomistic scale modeling was conducted to gain a deeper
understanding of the crystallization mechanisms. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were
performed using Sandia’s LAMMPS code, allowing for the study of the impact of stress, temper-
ature, defects, and composition on the crystallization of SiN. The modeling efforts began with the
development of a machine learning-based atomistic potential in collaboration with the University
of Florida, aimed at enhancing the accuracy and efficiency of atomistic simulations. This involved
creating and optimizing an ultra-fast force field (UF3) potential that accurately models the interac-
tions within silicon nitride. The simulations were designed to bridge the gap between fundamental
mechanisms and observable phenomena at the component scale, allowing for the exploration of
nucleation and growth dynamics in a controlled environment. The atomistic models were validated
against experimental data, ensuring that the simulations accurately reflected the physical processes
governing crystallization.

2.4. Meso-scale modeling approach

To complement the atomistic simulations, meso-scale modeling was employed to provide insights
into crystallization phenomena at engineering length and time scales. An advanced phase field
model was developed to simulate the SiN crystallization processes, incorporating a grain tracker
algorithm to identify and track individual crystal grains during nucleation and growth. This model
utilized a single order parameter to describe the phase transition between amorphous and crystalline
states, capturing the evolution of the crystal interface during solidification. The phase field equations
were coupled with tensor mechanics to account for the effects of external mechanical loading, such
as residual stress, which can significantly influence crystallization dynamics. The model also
incorporated the effects of internal stresses caused by volume shrinkage during crystallization and

 16

the compressive stress induced by phosphorus diffusion, which preferentially occurs through grain
boundaries.

2.5. Multi-scale modeling integration

The integration of atomistic and meso-scale modeling was a critical component of the research
methodology, allowing for a comprehensive understanding of how local phenomena influence
larger-scale behaviors. The insights gained from the atomistic simulations informed the param-
eterization of the phase field model, particularly the mobility parameter that governs the rate of
atomic attachment at the crystal interface. By linking the experimental data with the atomistic
and meso-scale models, the research team aimed to develop predictive capabilities that could en-
hance the reliability and performance of microelectronic devices. This multi-scale approach not
only facilitated the validation of models at different scales but also enabled the exploration of the
interplay between crystallization dynamics and material properties, ultimately leading to improved
manufacturing processes. During the initial phases of the project, while the atomistic models were
being developed, placeholder values were established for the meso-scale modeling to facilitate
early simulations and analyses. These initial values were derived from literature and preliminary
experimental data, providing a foundational framework for the phase field model. Key parameters,
such as mobility coefficients, thermal diffusivity, and latent heat contributions, were assigned based
on typical values observed in silicon nitride systems. This allowed for the exploration of crys-
tallization dynamics and the assessment of various growth scenarios in a controlled environment.
As the atomistic models progressed and more accurate data became available, these placeholder
values were systematically refined and replaced with inputs derived from the atomistic simulations.
This parallel development ensured that the meso-scale models could be continuously updated and
improved, ultimately leading to a cohesive multi-scale modeling framework that integrated the
detailed insights from atomistic simulations with the broader behaviors captured at the meso-scale.
The iterative process of updating the meso-scale parameters based on atomistic inputs signifi-
cantly enhanced the predictive accuracy of the multi-scale models, allowing for a comprehensive
understanding of the crystallization mechanisms in silicon nitride.

2.6. Metrics for success and milestones accomplished

Success in this project was measured through several key metrics, including the successful char-
acterization of reaction kinetics associated with SiN crystallization on samples produced and
characterized at MESA, validation of models at atomistic and meso/continuum scales through
experimental data, and the establishment of both a machine learning interatomic potential that
accurately models the crystallization process in SiN and a meso-scale model that captures longer
time and length scale characteristics using quantitative inputs from the atomistic model. Significant
milestones included the completion of experimental setups and initial data collection, the develop-
ment of the machine learning-based atomistic potential, the development of meso-scale phase field
models, and the identification of potential follow-up opportunities for future collaborations with
Sandia. Multiple publications, conference papers/presentations, and a U.S. patent were all realized

3. EXPERIMENTAL DETERMINATION OF REACTION
 KINETICS

The investigation into the crystallization kinetics of silicon nitride (SiN) has yielded significant
insights into the fundamental mechanisms governing nucleation and growth processes in micro-
electronics applications. Through a series of carefully designed experiments, we have characterized
the reaction kinetics associated with SiN crystallization under various processing conditions, ulti-
mately leading to a deeper understanding of how to manipulate these kinetics for improved device
performance. Using low-pressure chemical vapor deposition (LPCVD), SiN films were fabricated
in standard microelectronics film stacks on silicon substrates. The films underwent thermal cycling
treatments ranging from 1000°C to 1150°C, allowing us to observe the crystallization behavior as
a function of temperature, dopant profiles, and local stress perturbations at film interfaces. Image
processing techniques were employed to extract quantitative data on crystalline size and density,
enabling the calculation of nucleation and growth kinetics. The reaction rate kinetics were ana-
lyzed using the Arrhenius equation, which relates the rate of a chemical reaction to temperature.
Our findings revealed activation energies for nucleation in the range of 730-765 kJ/mol, indicating
a strong sensitivity of nucleation time to temperature variations. These results provide critical
parameters for modeling the crystallization phenomena and highlight the potential for tailoring
reaction conditions to achieve desired material properties. Additionally, we explored the effects of
intentional modifications to the SiN films, such as doping profiles and surface imperfections, on the
reaction kinetics. By systematically altering these parameters, we demonstrated the ability to con-
trol the crystallization process, either accelerating or decelerating nucleation and growth rates. This
capability opens new pathways for engineering SiN materials with specific characteristics tailored
for microelectronics applications. The experimental results not only contribute to the understand-
ing of SiN crystallization mechanisms but also provide essential data for future atomistic, meso,
and continuum-scale modeling efforts. By linking the insights gained from these experiments to
advanced modeling techniques, we aim to develop predictive capabilities that will further enhance
the reliability and performance of microelectronic devices. In summary, the experimental deter-
mination of reaction kinetics has established a foundational understanding of SiN crystallization
processes, paving the way for innovative approaches to material design and fabrication in the field
of microelectronics.

4. EXPERIMENTAL GROWTH OF CRYSTALLINE SIN

The experimental section of the study on in situ transmission electron microscopy (TEM) crystal-

5.1. Overview of molecular dynamics goals and work performed

Blooms of crystallization from amorphous silicon nitride in microelectronics has been observed
experimentally in conditions which Sandia uses to process microchips. While the blooms can be
avoided by altering the fabrication process, we would like to understand the drivers for nucleation
and growth of these pest blooms. While crystal growth is observed on time and length scales better
suited for continuum simulation, the physical and chemical mechanisms are not known to allow such
modeling to be parameterized. Quantum calculations, on the other hand resolve scale which are far
to small and short to be applicable to experimetns. Molecular dynamics simulations, especially with
quantum-accurate machine learned interatomic potentials, have the advantage being able to bridge
the gap between fundamental mechanisms and component-scale observables. Using atomistic
simlations in Sandia’s LAMMPS code, we are able to study the impact of stress, temperature,
defcts and composition on the crystallization of Si3 N4 and use the results to inform meso and
continuum scale simulations. An example of Si3 N4 devitrification is shown in Fig. 5-1.

The goal of this study is to build amorphous silicon nitride and study its properties using several
interatomic potentials of increasing complexity. We begin with comparisons of known quantities
and evaluate the accuracy of each interatomic model. Initial studies with empirical potentials from
the literature are summarized in Section 5.2.4. While we show that these models can be extrapolated
effectively to experimental time scales for some properties. We learned that a machine learned
quantum-accurate potential was needed to extend to carbon-rich (non-stoiciometric) silicon nitride,

We found in this work that molecular dynamics can resolve the fundamental mechanism for

Machine-learned interatomic potentials (MLIPs) allow for quantum-accurate interatomic poten-

However, optimizing the training of MLIPs remains a significant challenge. The difficulty of de-

simulation necessitates diverse, high-quality training data. We found in this study that data diver-
sity affects the performance of MLIPs using the Ultra-Fast Force Field (UF3 ) to model amorphous
silicon nitride. Specifically, we found that the UF3 variant trained on a subset of the training data,
in which nitrogen-rich structures were removed, offered vastly better prediction and simulation
accuracy than any other variant.
This work provides important context for future work exploring the influence of deviations in
stoichiometry, addition of impurities, and imposition of external stress/pressure. More advanced
potentials will significantly improve property prediction and extend our work into more complex

5.2. Understanding Si3 N4 crystallization through large-scale molecular dynamics
 and empirical potentials

5.2.1. Introduction

Amorphous silicon nitride (a-SiN) is used extensively in microelectronics devices as an insulating
diffusion barrier against charge and dopants.[118] As illustrated in Fig. 5-2a, a-SiN is typically
incorporated in a layer stack. In this example, a-SiN acts as a barrier for phosphorus between a doped
polysilicon layer and a silicon layer with thermal oxide. Local stresses present at the interfaces
between layers in these structures are imparted by differences in thermal expansion coefficients,
lattice mismatch, and chemical diffusion. The impact of these stresses is prevalent under elevated
temperatures during annealing. Over extended, high-temperature (>1300 K) annealing conditions,
these stresses may contribute to nucleated phase transition of a-SiN into crystalline α − Si3 N4 .

 (a) (b)

Figure 5-2. (a) Sketch of example layer-stack, not to scale (b) Optical micrograph of an α − Si3 N4 region in an
 amorphous silicon nitride matrix.

Crystalline α − Si3 N4 has been observed to form near the polysilicon/a-SiN interface then grow
into the a-SiN layer. This growth proceeds anisotropically, with certain growth vectors propagating
faster than others, as depicted in Fig. 5-2b. Crystallization is a significant nuisance because the
crystalline phase lacks the insulating properties of a-SiN. The crystallization changes the overall
structure and capability of the thin film stack; therefore, it is crucial to understand both the underlying
kinetics and the conditions by which we can control its occurrence.

Molecular dynamics (MD) approaches are uniquely positioned to enhance understanding of early-
stage crystal growth by providing nanoscale resolution to support microscale experimental obser-
vation. For example, experiments typically assume an isotropic growth for >1D growth of crystal
nuclei, where MD can characterize specific crystal facets. Two available potentials (discussed in
greater detail below) were adopted to reproduce dynamic growth processes and quantitatively com-
pare to experimental activation energies.[125, 116, 123, 152] Mechanisms of crystallization were
examined by comparing facet-dependent growth rates as well as structural and diffusion properties.
We will show that diffusion coefficients can be used to predict both temperature-dependence of
crystal growth as well as the relative velocities of unique growth facets.

 25

As summarized by Dasmahapatara and Kroll,[26] several empirical interatomic potentials[36, 132,
93, 38, 141, 78] have been proposed for structural characterization of Si3 N4 systems. Because none
of these models have been applied directly to amorphous-to-crystal phase transitions, we selected
and rejected interatomic potentials based on their ability to reproduce structural and thermodynamic
properties. For example, the Garofalini[36] model predicts excellent agreement in local structure
versus density functional theory (DFT)[26], but we found that dynamics stagnated below very high
temperatures (>3500 K), far above our simulation regime. The Tersoff potential[132, 78] produced
reasonable densities and diffusion and was considered for crystal growth. However, this model was
later rejected due the dominant appearance of a secondary β-Si3 N4 phase, despite seeding for α.
Our search concluded with two viable interatomic potentials for crystallization studies. The 2-body
classical model by Marian, Gastreich and Gale[93] (annotated throughout this text as ‘MG2’) has
been shown to reproduce amorphous structural quantities accurately[26] and was chosen due to
this apparent robustness outside of the crystal properties it was designed to predict. The 3-body
classical model by Vashishta, et al.[142, 141], under elevated temperatures, has shown to be prone
to structural ordering[26] and was chosen for this study with the aim of modeling the dynamics of
crystal growth.

5.2.2.      Methods

All classical molecular dynamics simulations were performed in Large-scale Atomic Molecular
Massively Parallel Simulator (LAMMPS)[134]. PLUgin for MolEcular Dynamics (PLUMED)
v2.8.1[13, 139, 14] was used for additional order parameter capabilities. Two interatomic potentials
were used separately: MG2 was implemented using two-body tables; Vashishta was invoked using
a pair style available in LAMMPS. Full functional forms and parameters for both potentials are
included in the Supplemental Material. A 1.0 fs timestep was used for all simulations. Unless
otherwise noted, Nose-Hoover thermostats and anisotropic barostats were invoked at 1.0 bar with
1.0 ps damping coefficients.

5.2.2.1. Structure generation

Seeded growth simulations require two initial structures: the crystal seed and the amorphous.
These structures are brought into contact with the direction of growth perpendicular to the planar
interface.

 27

Crystal seeds were generated using the same approach for each potential. The a- and c- planes of
the unit cell were cleaved for independent seed contacts to probe anisotropic growth. A crystal unit
cell for α − Si3 N4 (mp-2245)[111] was replicated to ensure at least 2.6 nm thickness in the direction
of growth (a- or c-) and at least 5nm × 5nm in lateral dimensions orthogonal to the direction of
growth. To account for thermal expansion at each of the growth temperatures, the crystal structure
was equilibrated at constant pressure for 1.0 ns.

For simulation simplicity, we generated amorphous Si3 N4 structures using a melt-quench approach.
Whereas typical experimental amorphous films are generated via low pressure chemical vapor
deposition (LPCVD) and/or sputtering techniques[66, 97, 79, 1, 123]. Using the MG2 potential,
a 5×5×5 nm3 box of α − Si3 N4 was melted at 4000 K for 100 ps at constant pressure and was
quenched at a rate of 1.0 K/ps to 2100 K. The lateral dimensions of the box were then adjusted
under constant volume to match the dimensions of the crystal seed. Finally, with a barostat applied
only along the direction of growth, the system was quenched by 1.0 K/ps to the run temperature
and equilibrated for at least 1.0 ns, until system density converged.

Vashishta systems were equilibrated from the post-melt-quench MG2 structure and relaxed to
account for the 10% amorphous density difference between the potentials. The full details of this
relaxation procedure can be found in the Supplemental Material, along with all initial amorphous
and crystal densities for each potential and temperature.

5.2.4. Conclusions

We have modeled the crystal growth of α − Si3 N4 in amorphous silicon nitride using molecular
dynamics and were able to probe the impact of local environment on crystallization. We have
shown the aptitude of two interatomic potentials (MG2 and Vashishta) with distinct structural and
dynamic properties to characterize crystallization of stoichiometric silicon nitride, Si3 N4 . Detailed
structural analysis in radial and angular distributions show that the Vashishta model underestimates
secondary structural features due to a constrained 3-body term, while MG2 tends to slightly
overestimate secondary features. Activation energies for crystallization predicted by both models
were among the lower values measured by experiment. These models capture many features present
in experimental crystallization but underperform in others (e.g. overestimation in 𝑇𝑔 and 𝑇𝑚 ).
The direct application of diffusion coefficients to predict facet-dependent growth rates holds pro-
found consequences for exploring crystallization at near-𝑇𝑔 temperatures where dynamics are greatly
reduced. Lower-cost diffusion simulations enable an efficient alternative for characterizing growth
velocities over multiple facets for predicting crystal shape evolution and morphology. We will
leverage these predictive capabilities in future work, extending our time and length scale of crystal
growth simulations to mesoscale via phase field modeling.
We have provided important context for future work exploring the influence of deviations in
stoichiometry, addition of impurities, and imposition of external stress/pressure. Exploring the
impact of stoichiometry could resolve the extent of its impact on growth mechanisms in experimental
settings, where even nominally stoichiometric films may deviate from the 3:4 ratio. Present
investigation of crystallization using MG2 and Vashishta will provide essential foundations for
comparison. We will consider the development of more advanced potentials to improve upon
experimental property prediction and extend our work into more complex systems.

5.3. Creating and optimizing machine learned ultra-fast force field (UF3) potentials
 for silicon nitride crystallization

5.3.1. Introduction

Since the inception of machine-learned interatomic potentials (MLIPs) [10, 37], their use has
garnered both excitement and skepticism. The excitement is due to their profound ability to
accurately learn and subsequently model a targeted region of the quantum mechanical potential
energy landscape (PEL) with the favorable linear scaling of classical empirical potentials [27]. The
skepticism is due to their lack of an interpretable functional form, which can lead to aberrations
when attempting to model regions of the PEL not sampled in the training set used to parameterize
the MLIP [108].
While various forms of MLIPs have been proposed, most leverage either simple representations
coupled with complex neural networks [67, 129] or complex representations coupled with simple
learning models [135, 148, 30]. The required complexity in either the learning model or representa-
tion has hampered the interpretability of the aforementioned MLIPs, which can lead to unidentified
holes in the learned PEL [83]. The simulated aberrations and lack of interpretable functional forms
of the MLIPs have led to a community focus on developing high-quality training data [31].
Many techniques for a priori data generation based on chemical intuition [160] have been proposed.
However, since these methods rely on expert intuition and it can be extremely difficult to know
which motifs the MLIP will encounter during simulation, it cannot be guaranteed that the dataset
has sufficient coverage to enable the modeling of complex material properties or avoid overfitting
the MLIP [95]. This, coupled with the conventional wisdom that increased data diversity improves
transferability [121, 58], has led to the development of autonomous data generation techniques that
maximize the informational entropy of the training data [59, 98], and active learning techniques that
iteratively refit an MLIP to a growing training set until it can accurately reproduce desired material
properties [96]. A unifying principle of these methods is to maximize the coverage of the dataset
while minimizing the cost of generating the dataset, i.e., the number of ab inito calculations.
While this principle may be advantageous to MLIPs that employ neural networks, which abide by
the universal approximation theorem [48], it does not account for the finite complexity of MLIPs
that employ alternative learning algorithms. This finite complexity can yield an underfit MLIP that
lacks the complexity to adequately learn the hypersurface represented by the training data. It has
been argued that learning the high-energy regions of the PEL is unnecessary [81], while others have
shown that sampling specific regions of phase space offers better accuracy for particular allotropes
at the cost of poor transferability to others [120]. However, the issue of underfitting from a dataset
that covers too broad a region of the PEL has not been explicitly defined or fully explored. We
define this critical issue as “diversity-induced underfitting,” where the inclusion of a wide variety
of training data introduces complexity that exceeds the learning capacity of the MLIP, resulting
in suboptimal simulation performance. By recognizing the limits imposed by diversity-induced
underfitting, researchers will be better equipped to fine-tune data selection and model complexity,
driving improvements in the simulation accuracy of MLIPs across a wide range of applications. In
this manuscript, we aim to elucidate the nuanced requirement of the MLIP training data and identify
diversity-induced underfitting by performing an ablation study, which consists of fitting the highly

 39

interpretable Ultra-Fast Force-Field (UF3 ) [149] to subsets of a database consisting of both expert
generated and autonomously generated data. While past works have eluded to diversity-induced
underfitting [120] or examined limited transferability resulting from overfitting [121], this work
explores both phenomena as part of a broader trade-off.

5.3.2.      Methods

As a test case, we produce an MLIP to model the crystallization of amorphous silicon nitride.
Understanding the underlying physical mechanisms that cause the crystallization of amorphous
silicon nitride and understanding growth dynamics is of paramount interest, as crystallization can
modify the film’s electrical, mechanical, and optical properties, which can impact microelectronics
fabrication and design. Studying the entire growth process would require simulating systems on
the micron scale for hours, which is computationally infeasible. However, studying only the early
stages of growth requires simulating systems on the nanoscale for fractions of microseconds, which
is accessible with empirical potentials. Unfortunately, existing empirical potentials for silicon
nitride [92, 25, 136] do not offer sufficient transferability for nonstoichiometric interactions [52],
and previously developed MLIPs [96, 151] do not offer sufficient speed.
To develop an MLIP of sufficient speed and accuracy, we adopt the UF3 framework, which has
been shown to accurately model silicon carbide [90] and aid in the discovery of hard material [9].

To train UF3 , we generated two distinct datasets: the stoichiometric and non-stoichiometric datasets.
The stoichiometric dataset was generated following a chemically inspired workflow similar to that
of Zou et al. [160]. We conducted ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations at ambient
pressure and various temperatures of 300, 1200, 1800, and 3200 K on the primitive cell of the
𝛼, 𝛽, and 𝛾 phases of Si3 N4 . Each simulation was run for 5 ps with a timestep of 1 fs, ensuring
structurally diverse data specific to the Si3 N4 stochiometry. To sample distorted near equilibrium
structures, relaxation trajectories used to compute the elastic constants were included. Lastly,
to sample the repulsive regions of atomic interactions, structures sampled from high-pressure
relaxations at up to 200 GPa were included. For the non-stoichiometric dataset, we leveraged
a genetic algorithm (GA) structure search using the Genetic Algorithm for Structure and Phase

Prediction python package [114, 137, 115]. Sampling the relaxation trajectories of the GA-produced

while focusing on the low-energy regions of the PEL [90, 9]. It is important to note that while
we refer to this dataset as non-stoichiometric, it still contains stoichiometric phases sampled by the
GA. To sample relevant energies and forces, we excluded structures with forces exceeding 20 eV/Å
and energies exceeding 3 eV/atom above the convex hull.

scheme and smearing of 100 meV for the Brillouin zone integration, and a cutoff energy of 400 eV
for the plane-wave basis set.

5.3.3. Results and discussion

To evaluate the effects of diversity-induced underfitting, we fit four variants of UF3 . First, we
partition the stoichiometric dataset into a test/train split, reserving the 3200 K AIMD trajectory
for testing and the remaining data for training and fit one variant to the stoichiometric training set
(UF3Stoic. ). The 3200 K AIMD was selected as the test dataset to provide a particularly difficult
test case as the high-temperature motifs are unlikely present in the training data. Next we partition
the non-stoichiometric dataset into an 80/20 train/test split and remove from the test set structures
with a nitrogen content greater than the Si3 N5 composition. We fit a second variant to the
non-stoichiometric training set (UF3Nonstoic. ), and a third variant to both the stoichiometric and
non-stoichiometric training sets (UF3Total ). A final variant was fit to the stoichiometric training
set and a subset of the non-stoichiometric training set where structures with a nitrogen content
greater than Si3 N5 are removed (UF3Final ). The UF3Final variant’s hyperparameters were optimized
and subsequently used for all other variants.
A critical advantage of UF3 is the coupling of cubic B-splines to represent atomic interaction
with linear regression, which provides visualization of the functional form that the MLIP learned.
Figure 5-10 shows the learned 2-body interactions for the variants. This visualization is an initial
check to ensure the MLIP has learned the proper representation of the PEL. For example, UF3Stoic. has
incorrectly learned that Si-Si and N-N interactions are always repulsive. The stoichiometric dataset
consists primarily of motifs where silicon is tetrahedrally coordinated with nitrogen, and nitrogen is
three-fold coordinated with silicon. Without data on shorter-range interactions, the stoichiometric
dataset was not sufficiently diverse to enable the MLIP to learn same species attraction.
Because the non-stoichiometric dataset incorporates additional compositions and local geometries,
the UF3Nonstoic. , UF3Total , and UF3Final variants have learned attractive wells for all interactions.
Features of these variants, such as the barrier around 2 Å for the N-N interaction that likely prevents
N2 sublimation in high-temperature simulations, provide confidence that the MLIP will provide
desired physical results. However, further interpretation of these visualizations is rather nuanced
without analysis of the force predictions and simulated results. As such, the variants were further
validated against the DFT computed forces and their ability to reproduce DFT computed elastic
constants and the amorphous structure.

While UF3Final did obtain the best overall force predictions, we cannot conclude the variant’s quality

accurately predict macroscopic observables that describe material properties or reveal detailed
physical mechanisms [35], which requires highly accurate representations of specific regions of the
PEL, not just accurate predictions on forces from the distribution it was trained on. As such, we
further validate our MLIPs against the simulated properties stated previously.

Figure 5-12(c) compares the accuracy of the elastic properties predicted by the variants for the
𝛼 and 𝛽 phases. UF3Stoic. , performs well in computing the elastic constants, obtaining a mean
absolute percent error (MAPE) of 12.0%. The performance on the elastic properties is justified
as the variant was trained on only stoichiometric motifs, which is what would be encountered
in simulating elastic properties. However, as seen in Fig. 5-11, the UF3Stoic. variant is unable to
generalize to nonstoichiometric motifs. This inability to generalize is difficult to identify without

 45

an explicit nonstoichiometric test set, as it does not necessarily hinder the MLIP’s ability to model
stoichiometric interactions. Rather, it underutilizes the flexibility of UF3 and prevents the proper
modeling of non-stoichiometric motifs. This only becomes apparent in Figs. 5-12(a,b), which shows
the amorphous radial and angular distribution function computed by the variant. The UF3Stoic. MLIP
under-predicts the density and predicts an overly broad ADF and unphysical peaks at roughly 1.9 Å
in the RDF and 65 °in the ADF.

Contrarily, although UF3Nonstoic. shows modest agreement with DFT for the amorphous density,
RDF, and ADF, this variant had the highest MAPE for elastic properties, suggesting the model has
not learned proper responses to distortions. While the UF3Total variant had improved performance
in computing the elastic properties compared to UF3Nonstoic. , it overestimated amorphous density.

The improved performance for elastic properties but simultaneous degradation in amorphous prop-
erties of UF3Total compared to UF3Nonstoic. indicates diversity-induced underfitting. If UF3Total had
sufficient complexity to capture the entire training set, it should have improved its predictions of the
elastic properties while maintaining reasonable predictions of the amorphous properties, given that
the training set simply had additional data specific to the Si3 N4 stoichiometry. Nitrogen behavior
differs significantly between nitrogen-rich and silicon-rich environments—forming N2 gas in the
former and three-fold coordinated nitrogen with silicon in the latter. This diverse behavior within
the training set highlights the need for alignment between the training data’s diversity and the
model’s complexity, ensuring balanced and accurate predictions.

The simulated validation results from UF3Final , which is fit to the stoichiometric and filtered non-
stoichiometric data, provides empirical evidence of the diversity-induced underfitting of UF3Total .
Although UF3Final was fit to less data, it obtained an MAPE of 8.5% (Fig. 5-12(c)), which is better
than any other variant. Further, the amorphous density, radial distribution function, and angular
distribution function shown in Fig 5-12(a,b) show close agreement with DFT. Based on the speed
tests, visualizations, force predictions, and simulated validation results, this MLIP is of sufficient
speed and accuracy to warrant a detailed investigation of the crystallization of amorphous silicon
nitride, which we intend to pursue in a subsequent study.

5.3.4. Conclusions

Our major conclusion is that the training data requirements of MLIPs are far more nuanced than
previously thought. Care must be taken when increasing the diversity of the training data as
presenting a region of the potential energy landscape that is too broad for the complexity of an
MLIP can lead to diversity-induced underfitting. On the other extreme, a training set consisting
only of deformations of crystalline phases will be too easy for an MLIP to learn, yielding an overfit
MLIP with erratic simulation behavior when it is forced to extrapolate to unseen domains. While
we recognize it is impossible to determine a priori the motifs an MLIP will encounter during
simulation, our concluding recommendation is that the training data should be sufficiently diverse.
Still, paramount effort must be taken to exclude motifs that are clearly beyond the region of the
potential energy landscape that the MLIP will explore, ensuring the training data is within the
complexity that the MLIP can capture.

 46

One can accomplish this by following the ablation study outlined in this manuscript. If removing
specific subsets of the training yields more accurate simulated results, the original dataset likely
spans too broad a region of the PEL for the MLIP to capture correctly and the MLIP suffers from
diversity-induced underfitting. Such is the case when comparing UF3Total to UF3Final . Alternatively,
if any of the simulated validation results portray aberrations, regardless of whether other validation
results show close agreement with DFT, it is likely the training is not sufficiently diverse to
produce an MLIP that can generalize. Such is the case for UF3Stoic. where the MLIP provided
reasonable predictions of the elastic properties but predicted unphysical peaks in the amorphous
radial distribution function. While we focus our investigation on a single material system using
only the UF3 , we speculate that this manuscript’s findings will generally apply to the many MLIP’s
of finite complexity [30, 135, 148]. However, we do not expect these findings to apply to MLIPs
that leverage neural networks [153, 8, 23, 67]. This is because the ability to scale to arbitrary
complexity and approximate any continuous function is an inherent feature of neural networks [48],
enabling MLIPs’ that leverage neural networks to capture a much broader region of the potential
energy landscape. Additionally, we note that the complexities involved in describing the Si-N
system make the challenges discussed here more pronounced than they would likely be for systems
like simple metals.

5.4.1. Additional guidance for polymorphic phase transitions

Silicon nitride possesses several metastable crystal structures (polymorphs), including the 𝛼 and 𝛽
phases. While crystallization experiments identified only the 𝛼 phase, molecular dynamics studies
yielded some 𝛽 structure. Identifying and mitigating the formation of 𝛽 was therefore a requirement
for characterizing solely 𝛼 growth.

The formation of 𝛽 (P63/m), despite seeding for 𝛼 (P31c), was related to structural similarity of
the two unit cells (in Figure 5-13). Both structures possess atomic coordination of sp3 -Si linked to
sp2 -N; densities and a/b lattice vectors are nearly identical but differ along the c-axis[111, 112, 44].
The 𝛽 unit cell propagates with ABAB-stacking along the c-axis. By contrast, the 𝛼 unit cell is
twice as long as 𝛽 in the c direction with ABCD-stacking, where AB layers are identical to the
𝛽 phase and CD layers are mirror inversions of A and B. The layer stacking similarity enabled 𝛽
formation, as we only observed 𝛽 growth along the c axis.
The 𝛼 and 𝛽 phases could be distinguished in post-analysis using a slightly modified implementation
of the Steinhardt order parameter briefly introduced in Section 5.2.4. To distinguish 𝛼 and 𝛽, 𝑞 6
was applied to the all-atom (Si+N) system with a cutoff of 4.8 Å; a threshold of 𝑞 6 = 0.1 separated
the two phases. Figure 5-14 shows a representative image of a crystal growth cell generated with the

and 𝛼 atoms in gray. Simulations which revealed this 𝛽 formation were excluded from 𝛼 growth

We observed the 𝛽 phase tended to grow at higher temperatures, so we limited our crystallization
study to the temperature range for which we could characterize appreciable 𝛼 growth. We further

verified the potential energies of interface initial configurations. Unfavorable initial conditions
tended to produce 𝛽 structures at earlier stages of crystallization. These methods were successful
in refining our crystallization study to 𝛼 growth for the MG2 and Vashishta potentials addressed in
Section 5.2.4. Mitigating 𝛽 formation led to the rejection of other interatomic potentials, such as
the Tersoff model. For the Tersoff model, the requisite temperature range to avoid 𝛽 formation was
so low that it became computationally prohibitive due to limited thermodynamics.
In brief, crystallization of single-crystal structures for known polymorphic materials can introduce
unexpected challenges. Invocation of precise order parameters to validate structures is advised as
a necessary precaution. Unintended structures may be mitigated by adapting simulation conditions
or revising choice of interatomic potential.

5.4.2. Notes for future work

A key future direction for this work is the extension to Si-rich systems (i.e. Si>3 N4 ), which are
relevant to experimental conditions. Si enrichment is nontrivial for interatomic potentials since
it can lead to structural instability and generally incorrect physical models. Implementing the
UF3 potential is a promising solution since it has already been trained to incorporate some Si-rich
structures.
A present challenge is modeling stable, crystalline Si within Si𝑥 N𝑦 . Experimentally, cubic-diamond
Si nanocrystals are observed to form in response to high silicon concentration. To observe Si crystals
in molecular dynamics, the UF3 model must predict a Si melting point below the temperature range
of the simulation (>1500K). UF3 is under active development to meet this criterion by adding
additional solid Si training data and incorporating additional DFT models[28].
Synergy between refining growth simulation methodologies 5.2.4 and potential development 5.3
provide a core foundation for future work. With these capabilities, future simulation efforts are well-
positioned to characterize the impact of impurities and stress on silicon nitride crystallization.

6. MESO AND CONTINUUM SCALE MODELING OF
 CRYSTALLINE SILICON NITRIDE

6.1. Introduction

While molecular dynamics (MD) simulations provide fundamental understanding of crystallization
and diffusion mechanisms in atomic level, meso-scale models offer insights into these phenomena
in engineering length and time scales. In this section, we will discuss the advanced phase field
model employed to simulate the SiN crystallization processes.
The model used in this work is derived from the original Kobayashi phase field model [68, 156],
which was initially developed to simulate dendritic crystal growth. The Kobayashi model employs
a single order parameter to describe the phase transition between two states, capturing the evolution
of the crystal interface during solidification. While effective for modeling dendritic growth, the
original formulation does not account for the complexities of polycrystalline systems, such as the
presence of multiple grains with distinct crystallographic orientations. To address this limitation,
we have extended the Kobayashi model by incorporating a grain tracker algorithm [107], enabling
the simulation of spherulitic growth in polycrystalline systems.
The grain tracker algorithm is a computational tool designed to identify and track individual
crystal grains during nucleation and growth. Upon nucleation, each grain is assigned a unique
identifier, referred to as a grain ID, which is linked to a predefined crystallographic orientation.
These orientations are specified based on the texture data or random initialization, depending
on the simulation requirements. The grain tracker algorithm ensures that each grain retains its
unique identity throughout the simulation, allowing for the differentiation of grains based on their
orientation. Using this approach, anisotropic (directional) material properties can be assigned to
individual grains by associating each grain ID with its corresponding crystallographic orientation,
which governs overall anisotropic growth behavior of the crystal. For example, the growth rate and
interface mobility can be made orientation-dependent, reflecting the physical properties of different
materials. By leveraging the grain tracker algorithm, the model avoids the need to compute multiple
order parameters for each grain, significantly reducing the computational cost. Instead, a single
order parameter is used to describe the phase transition, while the grain tracker algorithm handles
the identification and orientation assignment of individual grains.
The grain tracker algorithm approach offers two key advantages. First, it enhances computational
efficiency by minimizing the number of variables that need to be updated at each time step, making
the model suitable for large-scale simulations. Second, it provides a robust framework for studying
polycrystalline growth, as the grain tracker algorithm allows for the identification and analysis of
individual grain orientations. This capability is particularly important for investigating the effects
of crystallographic texture on spherulitic growth and material properties.

 51

Another aspect to modeling crystallization of the amorphous SiN of interest is to include internal
stress effects caused by 1. Volume shrinkage of material due to crystallization and 2. Compressive
stress caused by lattice constant difference between amorphous/crystallized SiN and phosphorus
diffused scenarios. The latter also involves modeling of phosphorus diffusion, which will often
preferentially diffuse through grain boundaries and crystalline phases from the phosphorus doped
polysilicon layer to the film as crystallization occurs. It has been shown that crystallization of SiN
under different ineternal stress can lead to different rates of crystallization growth rate, and show
that the under tensile stress, crystal growth rate is often increased [103].

The details of the extended phase field model, diffusion of phosphorus, implementation of phospho-
rus induced stress, and crystallization volume shrinkage, including the mathematical formulation
and implementation of the grain tracker algorithm, are discussed in the following sections.

6.2. Advanced Phase Field Model for Polycrystalline Spherulite Growth

6.2.4. Grain Tracker Algorithm and Feature Flood Count for Crystal Identification

In our phase field model, the identification and tracking of individual nucleated crystals are achieved
using the Grain Tracker algorithm [106] and the Feature Flood Count method [107], both imple-
mented within the MOOSE framework [43]. These tools provide a robust computational approach
for assigning unique grain IDs to each nucleated crystal, enabling the application of orientation-
dependent material properties to simulate anisotropic crystal growth.
The Feature Flood Count method is designed to identify and count connected components within
a solution field, which in this case corresponds to regions of the phase field variable representing
individual grains. The algorithm begins by inspecting the solution field and applying a flood-fill
method to recursively visit neighboring elements on the unstructured mesh. This process identifies
topologically connected regions where the solution value exceeds a specified threshold, effectively
isolating individual grains.
The flood-fill algorithm operates locally on each processor in a parallelized simulation environment,
marking connected regions and extending a "halo" one neighbor beyond the identified region. This
halo is critical for detecting potential collisions between disjoint regions and ensuring accurate
identification of grain boundaries. Once the connected components are identified locally, the data
is serialized and sent to the root processor, where global stitching is performed to assemble a
complete picture of all grains across the simulation domain. This process ensures that each grain
is uniquely identified, even in large-scale simulations with hundreds or thousands of processors.
The Grain Tracker algorithm builds upon the Feature Flood Count method by assigning unique
grain IDs to each identified component and tracking these grains across subsequent time steps.
During the first invocation, the algorithm assigns contiguous and unique IDs to all grains based on
their spatial connectivity and minimum element ID. These IDs serve as stable identifiers for each
grain, ensuring consistent tracking throughout the simulation.
In subsequent time steps, the Grain Tracker compares the current list of grains with the previous
list to maintain continuity. The algorithm minimizes centroid distances between grains to match
features from the current timestep with those from the previous timestep. This ensures that grains
retain their unique IDs even as they grow, interact, or merge. In cases where grains split or new
grains are nucleated, the algorithm assigns new IDs to these features while marking inactive grains
that have disappeared or been absorbed.
The Grain Tracker also includes a grain remapping mechanism to handle situations where grains
come into close proximity or overlap. Using a recursive backtracking algorithm, the Grain Tracker
resolves conflicts by remapping grain IDs to maintain a valid graph of grain relationships. This
ensures that grains are properly identified and tracked, even in complex scenarios involving grain
merging or splitting.
Once individual grains are identified and assigned unique grain IDs, the model applies orientation-
dependent material properties to simulate anisotropic crystal growth. Each grain ID is associated

 56

with a predefined crystallographic orientation, which governs the directional properties of the grain,
such as growth rate, interface mobility, and thermal conductivity. These properties are incorporated
into the phase field equations, ensuring that the evolution of each grain reflects its unique orientation
and anisotropic behavior.

The Grain Tracker algorithm and Feature Flood Count method provide a computationally efficient
and accurate framework for simulating polycrystalline systems. By enabling the identification and
tracking of individual grains, these tools allow for the application of orientation-dependent material
properties, enhancing the realism and predictive capability of the phase field model. This approach
is particularly valuable for studying the effects of crystallographic texture on microstructural evo-
lution and material performance.

6.2.5. Coupling Between Phase Field and Mechanics

In the current phase field model, the effects of external mechanical loading, such as residual stress,
are incorporated by coupling the phase field equations with tensor mechanics. This coupling is
achieved by including the elastic free energy contribution in the total free energy functional, which
is then summed with the chemical and gradient energy contributions. This approach allows the
model to account for the influence of mechanical stresses on phase evolution and crystal growth
dynamics.

The elastic free energy is computed using the ElasticEnergyMaterial class from the
MOOSE framework, which calculates the strain energy density based on the stress-strain rela-
tionship. The elastic free energy density, 𝑓elastic , is expressed as:

where, 𝝈 is the stress tensor and 𝝐 is the strain tensor. This term captures the energy stored in
the material due to elastic deformation, enabling the model to simulate the effects of mechanical
loading on phase transitions. The elastic free energy is added to the chemical and gradient energy
contributions in the total free energy functional, ensuring that mechanical effects are fully integrated
into the phase field model.

The coupling between phase field and tensor mechanics allows the model to account for the influence
of mechanical stresses on phase evolution, such as the impact of residual stress on nucleation and
growth. To simulate the effects of eigenstrain, we employ the ComputeEigenstrain class,
which calculates the strain induced by phase transformations or external loading. Eigenstrain,
𝝐 eigen , is defined as the strain that arises independently of external forces, such as thermal expansion
or phase transformation strain. The total strain tensor, 𝝐, is then expressed as:

The solid mechanics module in the MOOSE framework provides the foundation for solving the
quasi-static equilibrium equations governing mechanical deformation. The governing equation is
expressed as:

where, 𝝈 is the stress tensor and 𝒇 represents body forces. The quasi-static formulation assumes

as those encountered in phase transformations. The solid mechanics module also supports the
inclusion of material properties such as elastic moduli and Poisson’s ratio, enabling the simulation
of anisotropic mechanical behavior.

The elasticity tensor used in this work is calculated using reported elastic properties in literature
review, which for crystalline alpha Si3N4 gives 𝐶11 = 434.4 GPa and 𝐶12 = 144.8 GPa [155].
For amorphous SiN, calculated values are 𝐶11 = 311.1 GPa and 𝐶12 = 116.7 GPa, also calculated
using literature reported values [64]. The elastic properties used here are assumed to be relatively
isotropic for amorphous SiN, and which was shown in literature that while amorphous SiN do tend
to exhibit anisotropic and non-symmetric properties, such effects are only obvious in atomistic
scales, and are negligible in our current length-scale [100].

6.2.6. Mobility parameterization in the Phase Field Model Using Experimental Data

The phase field model for crystal growth requires accurate parameterization of mobility to simulate
the dynamics of crystallization and match experimental observations. In this study, we utilized
experimental data obtained from in-situ transmission electron microscopy (TEM) combined with
laser-induced heating to parameterize the mobility parameter.

The experimental system consisted of ultra-pure amorphous silicon nitride (a-SiN) membranes,
widely used in microelectronics and microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) as insulating films
and structural supports. While theoretical models suggest that a-SiN resists crystallization at
temperatures up to 1300 °C, recent findings revealed that ultra-pure thin layers of a-SiN can
crystallize at much lower temperatures. This premature crystallization poses challenges for device
reliability, as it can degrade insulating properties and introduce crystalline defects.

and 200𝑛𝑚) and sizes (1.0 × 1.0𝑚𝑚 and 0.1 × 0.1𝑚𝑚 windows) were examined, with larger and

6.2.8. Internal stress calculation and strain

For the model in Figure 6-4, plane strain conditions are applied to the left and right boundaries
in the X direction, and fixed displacement to the bottom boundary in the Y direction. Internal
stress in this work comes for two factors, volume shrinkage due to crystallization, and phosphorus
atom lattice strain when substituting nitrogen atoms within the layer. Both aspects are highly
dependent on cases, and can differ due to the stoichiometry of the amorphous SiN as well as the

 62

phosphorus concentration, so we should evaluate the range at which we examine sensitivity of these
parameters.

Calculating ball park range of volume shrinkage will require literature review on this matter under
different conditions. For instance, at different temperature, in Tesia et al. [51], it can be calculated
that at 1500 K, the volume shrinkage approximates to 3.131%, however at 1725 K, the volume
shrinkage can increase up to 3.501%. If we take stoichiometry into account, Watts, Thomas
Frederic [145] have shown lattice constants where, when calculated, gives volume shrinkage of
0.362% for amorphous Si3N, up to volume shrinkage of 1.081% for fully stoichiometric Si3N4.
Rough measurements of our sample density yields an approximate volume shrinkage of 4%, hence
we will be setting parameter range maximum around this value.

For phosphorus atom induced strain, Lee, Chang Seung et al. [82] have shown at phosphorus
concentration of 3 ∗ 1020 , lattice strain equates to approximately −1.6 ∗ 10−3 , whereas if we
directly calculate the phosphorus induced strain using average covalent radii [113, 57], it can be
approximated such that phosphorus induced strain in a single substituted atom is roughly estimated
to be 0.6957. Assuming fully substitutional behaviour for phosphorus atoms, at Si:N stoichiometry
of 1:0.17, phosphorus atoms will have no effect on strain, while above that, phosphorus atoms
contributes to compressive stress, and vice versa. We can further calculate the supposed ball park
value to use in our model by using the approximate values at which phosphorus concentration (≈ 0.5
unit-less at the top layer for most cases), and assuming complete stoichiometry Si:N = 1:1.33, after
normalization with the experiment measurement of phosphorus being 0.025 relative to silicon, the
phosphorus induced strain equates approximately to 0.0016, so pstrain in our sensitivity study is to
be set to range from 0 to 0.002.

6.3.3. Other Factors Influencing the Spherulite Growth

6.3.3.1. Influence of Thermal Fluctuation and Artificial Grating on Spherulite Growth

The growth dynamics of spherulites can be influenced by various factors, including impurity
segregation [62, 63], thermal fluctuation [87], and artificial grating [60], as observed in the literature.
While it is challenging to directly measure thermal fluctuations, these effects are evidently present
in experimental SiN wafer manufacturing processes. Impurity segregation, such as the presence
of phosphorus, also plays a significant role in altering crystallization behavior. In this section,
we focus on implementing and testing two influencing factors—thermal fluctuation and artificial
grating—that can change the dynamics of spherulite growth.

Impurity segregation, such as the presence of phosphorus or other elements, can significantly

model length-scale was scaled up in units and overall length multiplier, the normalized diffusivity
should fall on 2.036 ∗ 10−7 cm2 /sec. This value is relatively high compared to those in literature
review, which often lies between 1.0 ∗ 10−10 to 3.3 ∗ 10−13 [49, 86, 56] depending on several factors
such as temperature and the dopant concentration, but we have to note that there were also factors
not included in our diffusivity model. For instance, the absence of stress dependent modification to
diffusivity is a huge contributor to this discrepancy, where diffusivity of phosphorus will often be
increased under high tensile internal stress[42]. Another factor to consider is that we have scaled
the dopant concentration to a unit-less value of 1 in our model, and since diffusivity increases
drastically with increasing concentration [49, 94], the diffusivity used in our model may be skewed
upwards in magnitude.

7. CONCLUSION

In conclusion, this research project has successfully unraveled fundamental mechanisms of silicon
nitride (SiN) crystallization, significantly enhancing our understanding of the processes that govern
this critical material in microelectronics manufacturing. Through a collaborative effort between
multiple orgs at Sandia National Laboratories (5200/MESA, 1800, 1500), the University of Ten-
nessee, and the University of Florida, we have established a robust framework that integrates exper-
imental techniques, atomistic modeling, and meso-scale simulations. This multi-faceted approach
has provided invaluable insights into the crystallization dynamics of SiN, revealing the intricate
relationships between local phenomena and larger-scale behaviors all validated experimentally.
The successful implementation of machine learning-based atomistic potentials and the development
of this adaptable phase field model have laid the groundwork for a comprehensive modeling
framework that can be applied to SiN and potentially other materials. This framework is designed
to accelerate development times by providing predictive capabilities that inform manufacturing
processes, ultimately enhancing device yields and reliability. Furthermore, the insights gained
from this research serve as a valuable starting point for understanding crystallization physics in
similar materials, paving the way for future investigations into the behavior of other semiconductor
and dielectric materials.
The meso-scale modeling component of this research utilized an advanced phase field model,
which effectively captured the complexities of polycrystalline growth and the influence of mechan-
ical stresses on crystallization dynamics. By incorporating a grain tracker algorithm, the model
was able to identify and track individual crystal grains, allowing for the simulation of anisotropic
growth behaviors that reflect the physical properties of SiN. This capability was crucial for under-
standing how variations in crystallographic orientation and local stress conditions affect the overall
crystallization process. The integration of these meso-scale insights with atomistic simulations
created a powerful multi-scale modeling framework that not only validated the atomistic inputs but
also enhanced the predictive accuracy of the crystallization dynamics at larger scales.
As we move forward, the methodologies and models developed in this project will not only facilitate
ongoing research into SiN but also inspire new studies aimed at exploring the crystallization
mechanisms of various materials. The lessons learned and the collaborative spirit fostered through
this endeavor will undoubtedly contribute to the advancement of microelectronics and photonics
technologies, ensuring that we remain at the forefront of innovation in these critical fields. Overall,
this research represents a significant step in improving our understanding of fundamental physics of
SiN as it pertains to various fabrication methods, extending to a path towards controlled fabrication
of crystalline SiN that unlocks a new suite of material properties not previously accessible in
microelectronics fabrication, and establishing a foundation for future advancements in material
science, particularly through the lens of multi-scale modeling that bridges the gap between atomic-
level phenomena and macroscopic material behaviors.
