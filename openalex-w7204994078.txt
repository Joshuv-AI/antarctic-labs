The impact of intraoperative small intestine arrangement on
postoperative intestinal function recovery in patients with
laparoscopic colectomy: A single-center, double-blinded, randomized
controlled trial
Shengzhe Ma1 · Xiaoming Zhu1 · Zheng Lou1 · Zijian Zhou1 · Haifeng Gong1 · Yonggang Hong1 · Liqiang Hao1 ·
Guanyu Yu1 · Wei Zhang1
Received: 16 February 2026 / Accepted: 27 July 2026

Abstract
Background This study aimed to evaluate the effect of small intestine arrangement on the bowel function after laparoscopic
colectomy.
Methods A total of 160 patients were randomly divided into two groups. The control group received conventional treatment,
while the experimental group underwent small intestine arrangement. The primary outcome was the time to initial postoperative gas discharge.
Results The experimental group showed a significant difference in the time of initial postoperative gas discharge compared
to the control group (45.2 ± 19.7 vs. 54.9 ± 21.8 h, P = 0.004). Additionally, the experimental group had a significantly shorter
time to first tolerability of liquid food (64.4(43.0,68.3) vs. 67.5(60.5,86.8) hours, P = 0.005). One case involved the identification of a Meckel’s diverticulum and a small intestinal stromal tumor through intervention.
Conclusions Arranging the small intestine may promote the postoperative intestinal function and improve the detection of
complications, thereby avoiding the potential risk of reoperation.
Registration NCT05485857.
Keywords Small intestine arrangement · Colon cancer · Laparoscopic surgery · Intestinal function

Introduction
Colorectal cancer is a globally prevalent malignant tumor,
with a noticeable increase in the incidence and mortality
rates in recent years. According to the World Health Organization, approximately 1.8 million new cases were reported
in 2023 [1].
Enhanced Recovery after Surgery (ERAS) is currently
the standard process for patients with colon cancer during
their entire hospital stay. In colon surgery, the purpose of
enhanced recovery after surgery is to recover the bowel
function, including gas and stool elimination, which can be
achieved through various measures [2, 3].
A meta-analysis of seven randomized controlled trials
(RCTs) revealed that coffee can expedite postoperative gastrointestinal recovery in the digestive tract after gynecological surgery [4, 5] but only in patients with prolonged fasting.
Another meta-analysis study, involving 12 RCTs with 1019
patients, demonstrated a significant reduction in postoperative gas discharge time with the use of chewing gum [6, 7].
Multiple studies have indicated that intraoperative electroacupuncture therapy can effectively enhance the intestinal function in patients after colectomy [8–10].
The time of initial postoperative gas discharge in patients
with gastrointestinal tumors is a crucial indicator for surgeons to assess the recovery of the intestinal function. This
helps evaluate the condition of intestinal recovery. It also
provides essential clinical diagnostic and therapeutic information for auxiliary diagnosis, medication, and prognosis
intervention of the disease.
At our center, it is standard procedure to return the small
intestine to its natural position within the abdominal cavity
at the end of laparoscopic colectomy. This approach may
prevent an unordered small intestine position caused by
manipulation during the operation, which often necessitates
repositioning the patient’s peristalsis on the bed post-surgery. This may lead to a delayed recovery of function and
small intestine adhesions.
This study aimed to explore the impact of small intestine
arrangement on postoperative intestinal function recovery
in patients who underwent laparoscopic colectomy. We
hope to provide new treatment methods with a simple procedure for improving intestinal function recovery.

Materials and methods
Study design
A total of 160 patients with colon cancer scheduled for surgery at the Changhai Hospital of Naval Medical University
from November 2022 to April 2023 were selected for this

13

single-center, double-blind, randomized, controlled study.
The patients were stratified and randomly assigned to either
the experimental or control group in a 1:1 ratio based on age
(patients > 60 or ≤ 60 years old) and tumor location (tumor
located in the left-sided colon or right-sided colon). General
information such as sex, age, and tumor location between
the two groups was compared.

Selection criteria
Inclusion criteria required pathological confirmation of
colon adenocarcinoma and planned resection. The exclusion criteria included stoma creation, preoperative bowel
obstruction/chronic constipation, T4b tumors with adjacent
organ invasion, neurological disorders (stroke/spinal cord
injury), chronic opioid use, and prior abdominal surgery.
Withdrawal triggers included disease progression, patient
requests, and mortality. All assessments followed the intention-to-treat principles using the Full Analysis Set.

Enhanced recovery colon surgery intestine
arrangement protocol
The control group received conventional surgical treatment and D3 lymph node dissection (CMV + CVL), which
involved flushing the abdominal cavity and inspecting
potential bleeding points by randomly flipping the small
intestine after anastomosis of the colon in conventional
robot-assisted or laparoscopic colon cancer surgery before
closing the abdomen. After completion of the anastomosis, the mesenteric defects were not closed in all patients.
Anastomosis was performed extracorporeally using a stapling device. Anastomotic reinforcement uses a triple-layer
closure: anterior seromuscular burying, bilateral figure-ofeight sutures at the dog ears to eliminate the corners, and
posterior figure-of-eight or interrupted sutures with mesentery or epiploic appendages to cover the staple line. During
the procedure, patients were typically placed in the headdown tilt position (Trendelenburg position) when arranging
the small intestine to utilize gravity to assist the displacement of the small bowel out of the upper abdomen.
In the experimental group, an additional step of intestinal arrangement was performed using laparoscopic forceps
from the anastomosis site of the colon and small intestine or
ileocecal valve to Treitz’s ligament. This involved restoring
the small intestine in a systematic manner, moving 10 cm at
a time, and the omentum to their natural position after surgery, followed by closure of the abdominal cavity (Fig. 1).
In addition, we performed the same postoperative ERAS
measures for both groups of enrolled patients, postoperative
catheterization, multimodal pain management analgesia,
early ambulation, and nutritional support (Goal-Directed

epidural analgesia (PCEA) postoperatively. Non-steroidal

removed on postoperative day one.

Outcomes
The primary outcome of this study was the time of initial
postoperative gas discharge after surgery. The secondary
outcomes included the time to first tolerability of liquid
food, time to first bowel movement, time to first ambulation after surgery, incidence of postoperative complications,
and identification of potential lesions during small intestine
exploration.
This randomized controlled study was approved by the
Institutional Review Board of the Changhai Hospital of
Naval Medical University (CHEC2022-306). All participants provided their written informed consent. The trial was
registered on ClinicalTrial.com with the registration number
NCT05485857.

Randomization
Randomization was conducted using a stratified block
method. The method involved sealed envelopes with opaque,
hidden, and sequentially numbered labels. The stratification
criteria included the patient’s baseline age (greater than 60
years or not) and the tumor location (right-sided colon, right
transverse colon, left transverse colon, left-sided colon, or
sigmoid colon). A third party, independent of the patients or
surgeons, generated a random sequence based on the criteria,
resulting in a block randomization table. The sequence was
then placed in opaque sealed envelopes, which were opened

by the randomization operator during surgery. This study
employed a modified double-blind design in which the participants and data analysts were blinded to group allocation,
whereas the intervention performers remained unblinded
because of the necessity of procedural accuracy. Blinding of
the surgical team was logistically infeasible because of the
distinct nature of the experimental and control procedures.
To mitigate potential bias, all post-intervention data collection, processing, and statistical analyses were performed
by researchers who were blinded to group allocation. The
intervention protocols were strictly standardized, and the
performers adhered to predefined guidelines to minimize
variability. Randomization was performed shortly before
the completion of surgery. Following surgery, the attending
doctor and nurse regularly checked the patient’s condition
and recorded the primary and secondary outcomes.

Statistical analysis
We conducted a sample size calculation based on an assumed
type I error rate of 0.05 and a power of 80%. Our preliminary experiments indicated that the primary outcome, small
intestine transition time, was expected to be 55 ± 14 h in the
experimental group and 62 ± 16 h in the control group, with
a coefficient of variation of 0.5. Considering a potential
dropout rate of 10% and the possibility of the actual coefficient of variation deviating from our estimated value of
0.5, we determined that the optimal sample size for each
group was 80 patients. The PASS2020 software program
was used for this calculation. A comprehensive analysis was
conducted on the main results of the analysis set, and all statistical tests were two-sided. A data analysis was performed
using the SPSS 22.7 software program. Normally distributed continuous data are presented as the mean ± SD, and
independent sample t-tests were used for between-group
comparisons. Non-normally distributed data are presented
as median (interquartile range), and the Mann-Whitney U

Results
Patients inclusion
From November 29, 2022, to February 1, 2023, 160 patients
who met the inclusion criteria were consecutively enrolled
(Fig. 2). The two groups participating in the study were
comparable at baseline (Table 1). A total of 160 patients
were included in the Full Analysis Set (FAS), consisting of
98 males and 62 females. The mean age of the experimental
group was 61 ± 10 years, while that of the control group was
61 ± 12 years.

Primary outcomes and secondary outcomes
The primary outcome showed that the time of initial postoperative gas discharge in the experimental group was significantly shorter than that in the control group by 9.7 h
(45.2 ± 19.7 vs. 54.9 ± 21.8 h, P = 0.004).
In addition, compared with the control group, the experimental group had a significant difference in the time tolerability of liquid food (64.4(43.0,68.3) vs. 67.5(60.5,86.8)
hours, P = 0.005) and first defecation time (60.0(44.7,75.3)

intestine. There was no difference in the median time of
the first walk after surgery and the time of nasogastric tube
placement (Table 2).
Table 3 summarizes the details of intraoperative and postoperative complications. During surgery, one patient in the
experimental group experienced mesenteric bleeding and
intestinal wall injury. There was no significant difference in
the overall incidence of complications between the groups,
although the time of occurrence of postoperative intestinal
obstruction in the small bowel resection group was the same
as that in the control group. Postoperatively, one patient in
the experimental group required a second surgery owing to
complications, specifically an anastomotic leak.
Additionally, during surgery, a case of small intestinal
stromal tumor and a case of Meckel’s diverticulum were
found in one patient in the experimental group (Table 2).

Adverse effect
In the experimental group, there was one AE (1%) related to
the arrangement of the small intestine, whereas the control
group had three AEs (4%) (Table 3). The treatment-related
adverse events were mild and transient, and no patients
withdrew from the study because of these events. Furthermore, no serious adverse events were reported in either
group (Table 4). In the experimental group, patients with
bowel injury passed flatus at 40.62 h postoperatively.

Subgroup analysis
Based on an exploratory subgroup analysis, we determined
that the composition of the experimental group in individuals aged less than 60 years (P = 0.020) and the subgroup
with tumor location in the left-sided colon (P = 0.05) had a
significant impact on the overall outcomes in the entire analysis population. However, in the subgroup of individuals

aged > 60 years or with a tumor located in the right-sided
colon, there was no significant improvement in the postoperative bowel function. Nonetheless, there was a numerical
tendency towards a shorter time of initial postoperative gas
discharge (Fig. 3).

Discussion
This randomized controlled trial investigated the impact of
small intestine arrangement before closing the abdomen during colon cancer surgery on postoperative intestinal function recovery. The results showed a trend of shorter time
of initial postoperative gas discharge after surgery, with an
average difference of 9.7 h compared to the control group.
The operative time for small bowel arrangement was automatically recorded by the electronic anesthesia record system in the operating room. Importantly, this procedure can
be completed within 5–8 min without adding extra surgical
time or complications, making it applicable to different surgical teams. However, further evidence from larger cohort
studies is needed to determine the patient characteristics
that would benefit the most from this procedure. Younger
patients tend to recover faster and may be less affected by
this intervention [11, 12].
Intestinal peristalsis drives food particles through the
small intestine via coordinated contractions. The concept
of arranging the small intestine before closing the abdomen
was initially inspired by the use of intestinal plication to
prevent the recurrence of obstructive adhesions after small
intestine adhesions, which has been found to decrease the
rate of postoperative recurrence and allow the intestine to
return to its natural position [13–15]. This is evident from
the earlier postoperative intestinal ventilation time, quicker
return to a semi-liquid diet, and shorter duration of intravenous fluid administration [16].
During the arrangement of the small intestine, we serendipitously encountered two instances of small intestinal
lesions. The first case involved the identification of a 2.0 cm
× 4.0 cm lump in the patient’s small intestine during the
procedure, which was directly resected. The subsequent
postoperative pathological report indicated the presence of a
Meckel’s diverticulum. In another case, while arranging the
small intestine, we discovered a tumor measuring 1.0 cm *
1.0 cm * 2.0 cm, which was later confirmed as a small intestinal stromal tumor through the postoperative pathological
diagnosis. The process of arranging the small intestine not
only facilitated exploration of the small intestine during
general surgical procedures but also aided the identification
of potential small intestine lesions.
Additionally, it provides an opportunity to examine
bleeding points and additional intraoperative complications.

a single center. This procedure has been validated by various surgical teams at our center, demonstrating no serious
complications or safety incidents. Meticulous preparation

in surgical time is a worthwhile trade-off for the potential improvements in patient recovery and overall surgical
success rate. In this study, the incidence of postoperative
intestinal obstruction was relatively low compared to that
in previous studies. Previous studies have suggested that
the use of opioid drugs can impact the recovery of postoperative intestinal function [17–19]. To eliminate the
influence of opioid drugs on the recovery of postoperative
intestinal function, we carefully compared the use of opioid drugs between the two patient groups and found that
the doses of opioid analgesics used were similar. The main
focus of ERAS postoperative intestinal function recovery

This study has several limitations. This was a singlecenter study, and further validation is needed from multiple
international centers, including those in which solid food
is provided on the first day post-surgery. Therefore, it is
important to handle the mesentery gently and avoid direct
contact with the intestinal wall during surgery. In our study,
only one patient experienced mesenteric bleeding and
small intestinal wall injury during the process of intestinal
arrangement.
In conclusion, this study highlights the impact of laparoscopic arrangement of the small intestine on postoperative
intestinal function recovery in patients with obesity. This
study scientifically demonstrated that this procedure can
significantly improve intestinal function recovery, such as
the time of initial postoperative gas discharge after surgery.
These findings provide valuable insights for colorectal surgeons, and it is hoped that this study will be verified by multicenter randomized controlled trials in the future.
