 ABSTRACT

Within the context of recent executive orders pertaining to the acceleration of advanced nuclear
deployment, as well as recent initiatives to rapidly accelerate nuclear fuel and cladding innovation within
the Advanced Fuels Campaign, there is a growing need to leverage the capabilities within the light-water
reactor and advanced reactor testbed within the program to (1) validate legacy performance data and
(2) rapidly screen new fuel/cladding candidates. Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) developed a
separate effects test, MiniFuel, to understand fuel forms under irradiation by isolating the thermal effects
brought on by gamma heating in the structural components of the experiment instead of heat brought on
by fission reactions in the fuel. This research used an existing 2D axisymmetric finite element model to
simulate temperatures relevant to fuel–cladding chemical interaction (FCCI) in U-10Zr fuel (uranium fuel
that is 10 wt % zirconium) with coated and bare HT-9 cladding. Incorporating both fuel and cladding
layers into the MiniFuel experiment vehicle is a first-of-a-kind approach at ORNL. The main purpose of
this study was to perform a scoping analysis to determine the feasibility of desired design average
interface temperatures (575 to 625°C) between 1% and 2.5% enriched U-10Zr fuel disks and HT-9
cladding disks in the MiniFuel experiment that reflect FCCI conditions while avoiding large temperature
transients between cycles. These efforts brought forth results of FCCI fuel–cladding interface
temperatures that are feasible in the MiniFuel test vehicle and modifications to the current MiniFuel
geometry. The results can be used to support the design of future experiments that can be inserted in the
High Flux Isotope Reactor.

 1. INTRODUCTION

Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) designed an irradiation experiment test vehicle called MiniFuel
[1, 2] for fuel irradiation experiments that are inserted in the High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR) [3].
MiniFuel was designed to be a separate effects test capability for examining fuel thermal behavior by
uniformly heating the fuel through a molybdenum heater tube encased in a sealed titanium holder tube
held within a stainless steel target housing. The heating from the heater tube is driven by gamma heating,
which largely trumps fission effects from the miniature fuel component that also reduces large axial
temperature gradients. The gamma heating produced from the holder generates the fuel temperature rather
than relying on heat derived from the fission process. In this work, the MiniFuel irradiation experiment
vehicle design was modified to simulate fuel–cladding chemical interaction (FCCI) under prototypical
conditions in U-10Zr metallic fuel (uranium fuel that is 10 wt % zirconium).

FCCI is a nuclear fuel cladding phenomenon in which the outer surface of the fuel and the inner surface
of the cladding interact chemically and form an interaction layer stemming from the diffusion of
lanthanide fission products through volumetric and grain boundary diffusion pathways within the
surrounding cladding material [4]. There are three distinct phases in which the entirety of the
phenomenon occurs: The first is lanthanide transport, the second is fuel cracking, and the third is
fuel/cladding interdiffusion [4]. The lanthanides produced from fission products radially migrate through
preexisting cracks in the rind in the fuel from fabrication and then finally to the inner surface of the
cladding, where the fuel and cladding interdiffuse due to new phases of lanthanide production [4]. This
diffusion layer is also referred to as wastage because it is assumed that this layer cannot accommodate
load and the resulting cladding has lost a fraction of its load-bearing capacity [5]. Due to solid and
gaseous diffusion from irradiation in a reactor, the fuel swells large enough to close the gas gap between
the pellet fuel and annular cladding. This also means that as the burnup of the fuel increases, the chance
of FCCI occurring also increases due the inevitable thermal expansion of the fuel.

In this work, a scoping analysis was conducted to evaluate the feasibility of the existing MiniFuel
irradiation capability in the vertical experiment facility (VXF) at HFIR to investigate FCCI phenomena in

 1

metallic fuels to facilitate rapid screening of advanced fuel forms for fast reactors. A performance
envelope of interface temperatures between the fuel and cladding layers was targeted to reach between
575°C and 625°C (an average of 600°C) to obtain sufficient diffusion coupling but avoid melting that
may develop in these eutectic regions [5].

Whereas previous experiments have mainly looked at fuel disks, kernels, or particle fuel forms [8],
MiniFCCI will investigate FCCI phenomena observed between fuel and cladding. The average interface
temperatures between the fuel and cladding are an important parameter of interest when investigating
FCCI behavior because this is where the chemical interactions between the fuel and cladding layers occur.
The subcapsules are thermally insulated by a helium/argon gas mixture in the stainless steel target. The
MiniFuel design temperatures are controlled by varying the gas gap composition and thickness between
the subcapsule holder outer diameter (OD) and the target housing inner diameter. A total of six
subcapsules located by a subcapsule position (S) that contain inner subcapsule components (including the
fuel) are seal welded, stacked, and loaded into a target or housing capsule located by an axial position
(A). The MiniFuel targets are then stacked and loaded in an aluminum basket that can hold three targets
within three radial (R) positions in the VXF. The MiniFuel subcapsule location is fully denoted as radial-
axial-subcapsule (RAS) positions. The MiniFuel subcapsule inner components are composed of the fuel, a
molybdenum heater tube, a titanium holder that encapsulates the components, and a SiC rod in the center
of the molybdenum tube that is used as passive thermometry to verify experiment temperature in
postirradiation examination (PIE).

Higher fuel enrichment makes temperature control more challenging because higher temperatures
introduce higher levels of uncertainty. Therefore, controlling the fuel temperature fluctuations becomes

 2

difficult to predict. A recent study conducted by Meehan et al. [9] quantified the uncertainty of
temperature predictions in the MiniFuel design. This study performed a sensitivity analysis on a
simplified 2D axisymmetric representative MiniFuel ANSYS finite element [10] model that examined
several design parameters that influence key figures of merit, including the fuel temperature and
thermometry temperature. This study concluded that fuel temperature predictions are most sensitive to the
molybdenum heat generation rate (HGR) and thermal contact conductance between the fuel and
surrounding materials, and the estimated uncertainty in VXF fuel temperature predictions was ±40°C. For
this reason, a conservative boundary condition was applied to the 2D axisymmetric ANSYS model to
assume that contact pressure existing in the subcapsule is only influenced by gravity, which is an accurate
representation of the thermometry contact in the MiniFuel experiment. Notably, the uncertainty analysis
by Meehan et al. [9] assumed UO2 fuel disks as the fuel form, so somewhat different uncertainty may
result for metallic fuel.

One of the main design concerns for metallic fuel is the swelling of the fuel during reactor operation.
Fission gas bubbles within the fuel microstructure cause the fuel to expand and come into contact with the
cladding. The two materials chemically bond by diffusion. This diffusion of lanthanides degrades the
active cladding layer. With 75% smear density, the fuel and cladding interact when the fuel has an
equivalent burnup of approximately 2% FIMA [11]. Understanding fuel swelling phenomena is a topic
extensively studied in legacy Experimental Breeder Reactor-II (EBR-II) experiments [12]. However, as
the interest in metallic fuels has expanded, research has further expanded into U-10Zr fuel swelling
phenomena.

The release of fission gas correlates to the respective %FIMA attained in the fuel and is a measurement
that can also be leveraged during PIE to obtain fission product inventories produced during irradiation.
For this design, targets of 2.6% FIMA and 5.2% FIMA were used to ensure that FCCI occurs in the
experiment. Due to the lower burnups targeted in these prospective experiments, fuel may also be
produced with preexisting lanthanide inventories to accelerate FCCI behavior while additional inventory
may be generated during irradiation. The purpose of this report, therefore, is to demonstrate the feasibility
of obtaining prototypical design temperatures in the MiniFuel experiment vehicle. The scoping analysis
used U-10Zr as an initial relevant fuel system of interest. This design will leverage the MiniFuel
irradiation test vehicle to demonstrate only the effects brought on by the FCCI phenomenon under
isothermal and uniform burnup conditions.

 3. MINIFUEL SCOPING STUDY

The scoping analysis examined key parameters that can be modified or optimized to tailor the existing
MiniFuel subcapsule into targeting the design temperature regime of 575°C–625°C while reaching
2.6% FIMA and 5.2% FIMA either using 1% or 2.5% enriched U-10Zr fuel. Although commercial fuel is
expected to have much higher enrichment levels, the reduction in fuel enrichment will be shown to be
necessary to ensure that the specimen temperature is controlled not by fission heating but instead by
gamma heating, resulting in lower temperature variation as the fuel depletes. The first objective of this
study was to examine the geometry of the existing MiniFuel design, and the second objective was to
examine the variability of the fuel and cladding materials in the model. Both are discussed in the
following sections.

3.1 GEOMETRY

An existing 2D axisymmetric ANSYS model of a MiniFuel single subcapsule developed by Meehan et al.
[9] was used for the MiniFCCI design. Figure 1 shows the 2D axisymmetric MiniFuel subcapsule
ANSYS model and its modifications. These modifications were made to the current MiniFuel design to
incorporate two fuel and cladding diffusion couples. Both thicknesses of the fuel and claddings are

 3

variable dimensions in the ANSYS model. These critical dimensions can be varied per design to achieve
different target temperatures. Another modification made to the MiniFuel 2D axisymmetric model was
adding a molybdenum disk that rests above the cladding of the fuel/clad disk subassembly. This disk
ensures that the thermometry does not directly interact with the cladding during irradiation to preserve as
much of the SiC thermometry as possible for PIE. Previous MiniFuel designs utilized the fuel dish as only
an inner subcapsule component that holds the fuel. As an added capability, the fuel dish material can be
substituted for a cladding material of interest where the thickness of the dish is also variable. This was
incorporated as an additional functionality to essentially perform two experiments in one, where the
performance of two materials can be compared under the same irradiation conditions.

Another consideration for the redesign of the existing MiniFuel subcapsule geometry was to simplify the
manufacturing of subcapsule components. The addition of a molybdenum disk that resides between the
fuel dish and clad disk and heater tube allows for a through hole to be drilled in the heater tube (Figure
1[c]). This eliminates the dimensional uncertainty of the previous blind hole bottom surface (Figure 1[a]).
This supports not only for the bottom surface of the thermometry to make adequate contact during
irradiation but also brings the thermometry closer to the clad/fuel/clad diffusion couple assembly,
supporting better temperature monitoring.

The goal of the proposed MiniFCCI irradiation experiment is to form diffusion coupling via chemical
interaction between the cladding specimens and the fuel specimens. These specimens will fuse by means
of contact with each other through a chemical bond. To circumvent the possibility of samples getting
stuck in the molybdenum heater, the cutoffs are removed, and the added molybdenum disk provides a
barrier between the fused cladding/fuel/cladding specimen. Removing the standoffs from the MiniFuel
holder places the fuel dish in direct contact with the heater tube, allowing for more contact to be
established from the tube and dish. This also allows the fuel dish to hold a larger fuel disk if needed.

 4

Another design consideration for MiniFCCI was disassembly post irradiation in the hot cell facilities at
ORNL. Preventing damage of the diffusion couple specimens during disassembly of the capsules is a
crucial design consideration. Reducing the size of the fuel to a millimeter scale allows the temperatures of
the fuel to be controlled via gamma heating instead of fission heating while reducing large temperature
gradients. Therefore, having the heater tube completely flat against the fuel dish separated by a
molybdenum disk allows for simple removal post irradiation from the subcapsule holder by pouring out
the inner subcapsule components onto the hot cell working surface for ease of retrieval of specimens.

3.2 U-10ZR

U-10Zr is a metallic fuel that has been widely investigated for use in advanced reactors, specifically fast
reactors. Experiments from the EBR-II reactor are frequently referenced in the literature [12]. Though
these experiments provided a foundation for the U-10Zr/HT-9 system, the MiniFuel capability can expand
on the previous work to provide a greater understanding of FCCI via two pathways: (1) refining FCCI
models to account for integrated time-at-temperature diffusion pathways and (2) assessing potential FCCI
barriers using the dual-interface design.

The addition of zirconium raises the melting point of the fuel [12]. This helps alleviate the limitations that
traditional UO and mixed oxide fuels have when considering fast reactor design. However, the onset of
eutectic formation in the U-10Zr/HT-9 system, driven by the formation of liquid phases in the fuel
brought on by iron formations [5, 13], occurs at approximately 725°C. Figure 2 shows one of these
eutectic points denoted as 𝛾1 (bcc U-rich solid mixture of U-Zr) [13].

3.3 METHODOLOGY

The MiniFCCI target temperatures are 575°C to 625°C to examine diffusion coupling between the U-
10Zr fuel and HT-9 cladding. However, the thickness of the cladding must be sufficiently maintained for
the fuel to create a diffusion layer where enough of the cladding can be preserved for PIE. This range of
temperatures should be maintained during the irradiation experiment, all while preserving the cladding
and fuel layers and introducing a diffusion layer. Additionally, the subcapsules are thermally insulated by
a helium/argon gas mixture within the capsule housing, thereby creating a gas gap between the capsule
housing and the subcapsule holder. For these analyses, a 100% helium mixture was used. Helium has a
high thermal conductivity in comparison with other inert gases. Controlling the gas gap thickness allows
the irradiation vehicle to be optimized to a target temperature. Thus, the critical characteristics of the
design are the fuel height, radius, cladding height, cladding radius, fuel dish height, fuel dish inner radius,
and holder OD. Manipulating these dimensions and varying the fuel enrichment allows the desired
average interface temperatures between the fuel and cladding disk as well as the fuel and fuel dish to be
obtained.

The thermal analyses were performed using ANSYS, which utilizes HGR data for the various
components and gas gaps in the MiniFuel subcapsule in the form of design analysis and calculation
(DAC) APDL macros [14]. These HGRs are created from HFIRCON neutronic data, where the heating
rates are positionally dependent. The HGRs and material property macros are called by the solve macros
and applied to the respective components to support the thermal calculation.

A study by Meehan et al. [9] developed a 2D axisymmetric MiniFuel ANSYS model and performed a
sensitivity analysis, demonstrating that the thermal contact conductance between the fuel, dish, and holder
is a parameter that influences the fuel temperature. Therefore, the contact definitions are closely examined
in the next section to capture the fuel behavior during the irradiation.

3.3.1 Boundary Conditions

The contact between the SiC thermometry and the molybdenum heater tube, the molybdenum heater tube
and the cladding, the fuel and the dish, and the dish to the holder are only influenced by gravity.
Additionally, the subcapsules are not internally pressurized. Because of this, a conservative boundary
condition was applied to the contact pressures for the SiC thermometry touching the molybdenum heater
tube, the molybdenum heater tube touching the cladding, the fuel touching the dish, and the dish touching
the holder, all in the axial direction. The contact pressure in these areas are set to zero. When this contact
pressure is set to zero, the solve macros consider no pressure at these points when calculating the resulting
interface temperatures. Thus, a lower contact pressure results in higher temperatures due to the reduction
in heat transfer.

Another boundary condition that was explicitly defined is the contact pressure between the fuel outer face
in contact with the inner wall of the fuel dish in the radial direction. This is an important contact to
examine because not only does fuel generally swell due to irradiation, but metallic fuel swells at a larger
scale in comparison with traditional UO2 fuel at similar temperatures. Special attention is drawn to the
radial direction of the fuel because the fuel tends to swell radially more than it does axially [11]. With this
consideration, the following calculation can be used to define the pressure that the fuel creates due to
thermal expansion over the course of the desired temperature regime in HFIR:

dish in the ANSYS model used for these thermal analyses.

capability at ORNL and to assess whether it can be expanded to include MiniFCCI. The results presented
herein are intended to help determine what the optimal configuration of MiniFuel design is and can be
utilized to support future MiniFCCI experimental designs. Figure 3 shows the thermal spectra of the
MiniFCCI 2D axisymmetric singular subcapsule model in the (324) RAS position. As shown in Figure 3,
the fuel is the component with the highest temperature due to gamma heating brought on by the
subcapsule components.

 5. CONCLUSIONS

This scoping study utilized a 2D axisymmetric single subcapsule model built in ANSYS to simulate 1%
and 2.5% enriched U-10Zr with HT-9 cladding. A key takeaway from this work was determining an
optimal enrichment of 1% U-10Zr fuel to maintain steady average interface temperatures between the
claddings and fuel over multiple cycles. As such, it is desirable to premix lanthanides and other elements
that would form via fission over longer periods of time into the fuel to start with. This work made
additions and modifications to the existing MiniFuel irradiation experiment vehicle design, such as
adding a cladding disk and substituting the fuel dish for a cladding material to expand the MiniFCCI
capability. A highlight from this work is that the MiniFCCI design can be used to design irradiation
experiments that use bare HT-9 cladding (or other cladding of choice) versus an FCCI barrier coated
sample. This is advantageous because two different samples can be irradiated under the same boundary
conditions to test the effects of the FCCI phenomenon. This not only reduces uncertainty in experiment-
to-experiment comparisons but also reduces time of irradiation experiment execution.

With the modifications made to the MiniFuel design, drawings will be updated at a later time to
accommodate changes to the design to suit the needs of the MiniFCCI design. Thermal calculations will
also be performed to bound the heat loads observed in the updated design as needed.
