On centerless unimodular contact Lie algebras. Thanks: This work was partially supported by CONICET and SECyT-UNC (Argentina)
Agustín Garrone

Abstract
. We provide an elementary proof that, in a (transversely) unimodular contact Lie algebra, the adjoint action of the Reeb vector is nilpotent except when the Lie algebra is isomorphic to either 𝔰 ​ 𝔩 ​ ( 2 , ℝ ) \mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R}) or 𝔰 ​ 𝔲 ​ ( 2 ) \mathfrak{su}(2) . We introduce a class of contact Lie algebras, called DS-contact Lie algebras , containing all K-contact Lie algebras, and deduce from the previous result that the only centerless unimodular examples in this class are precisely 𝔰 ​ 𝔩 ​ ( 2 , ℝ ) \mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R}) and 𝔰 ​ 𝔲 ​ ( 2 ) \mathfrak{su}(2) . This gives an alternative proof of the previously known fact that centerless unimodular Sasakian Lie algebras are isomorphic to either 𝔰 ​ 𝔩 ​ ( 2 , ℝ ) \mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R}) or 𝔰 ​ 𝔲 ​ ( 2 ) \mathfrak{su}(2) . Some other results known to hold for Sasakian Lie algebras are generalized as well. We investigate several properties of DS-contact Lie algebras in relation to Frobenius Lie algebras, and also classify them in dimension five. Some implications for the contact Lefschetz condition are explored.



1.
Introduction

A contact form on a
(2n+1)
-dimensional Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is a
1
-form
\eta\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
such that
\eta\wedge(d_{\mathfrak{g}}\eta)^{n}\neq 0
. Such a form determines a unique element
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
, called the
Reeb vector
, characterized by
\eta(\xi)=1
and
\iota_{\xi}d\eta=0
. It is known that the center of a contact Lie algebra is either trivial or generated by the Reeb vector (see Proposition
2.3
). Moreover, contact Lie algebras with nontrivial center are precisely the one-dimensional central extensions of symplectic Lie algebras (see Proposition
2.4
). Since symplectic Lie algebras are somewhat better understood, this correspondence provides useful structural information about contact Lie algebras with nontrivial center, and much of what is known comes from this fact

The situation is different for centerless contact Lie algebras, since no analogous reduction to symplectic Lie algebras is available. Several approaches have been used, including deformations and contractions of Lie algebras
[
24
]
, invariants
[
33
]
, and double extensions
[
3
,
4
,
32
]
. Another common strategy is to focus on special classes of contact Lie algebras, such as those with abelian nilradical
[
2
]
, or with a codimension-one Frobenius ideal
[
18
]
, or the so-called
seaweed
Lie algebras
[
15
,
16
,
29
]
. Considerable attention has also been devoted to compatible geometric structures, especially Sasakian structures and related variants
[
5
,
6
,
14
,
21
,
28
]
. Several of these techniques also work for the nontrivial center case. It should be noted that, by results of Gromov
[
25
]
and Borman-Eliashberg-Murphy
[
11
]
, every odd-dimensional Lie group admits a possibly noninvariant contact structure. Notice that not every odd-dimensional Lie algebra admits a contact form (e.g., the abelian ones).

Despite these efforts, relatively little is known about the general structure of centerless contact Lie algebras. One notable exception is the the fact that the only centerless unimodular Sasakian Lie algebras are
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
and
\mathfrak{su}(2)
. This arises as an immediate consequence of
[
1
, Theorem 2.1]
, where a classification of unimodular Sasakian Lie algebras up to modification is established: besides
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
and
\mathfrak{su}(2)
, they are Heisenberg Lie algebras
\mathfrak{h}_{2n+1}
. To achieve this classification, fundamental results on the structure of unimodular Kähler Lie algebras of Hano
[
26
]
and Nakajima
[
30
]
are used, as well as properties of the aforementioned modification technique.

While not a full-blown classification result, a much more general result can be established by rather elementary means. Its proof is the main content of this article:

Theorem 1.1
.

With the exception of
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
and
\mathfrak{su}(2)
, the map
\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}
in a (transversely) unimodular contact Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},\eta)
is nilpotent, where
\xi
is the Reeb vector.

Recall that a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is said to be unimodular if
\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{ad}_{x})=0
for all
x\in\mathfrak{g}
. There is a well-known equivalence between unimodularity and the validity of Poincaré duality for the Chevalley-Eilenberg cohomology of
\mathfrak{g}
. If
\mathfrak{g}
admits a contact form
\eta
, then a similar equivalence for the
\xi
-basic cohomology can be defined, where
\xi
is the Reeb vector of
(\mathfrak{g},\eta)
: the relevant notion is that of
transverse unimodularity
, meaning that
\operatorname{tr}(M_{x})=0
for all
x\in\ker(\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}|_{\mathfrak{h}})
; here,
M_{x}:=\mathrm{pr}_{\mathfrak{h}}\circ\operatorname{ad}_{x}|_{\mathfrak{h}}
, where
\mathfrak{h}:=\ker\eta
and
\mathrm{pr}_{\mathfrak{h}}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{h}
is the linear projection associated with the vector space splitting
\mathfrak{g}=\mathbb{R}\xi\oplus\mathfrak{h}
. Of course, unimodularity implies transverse unimodularity, but not the other way around. We refer to Subsection
2.3
for details.

Unimodular contact Lie algebras with nilpotent
\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}
do exist, as seen in Example
4.10
. The statement that contact Lie algebras with nilpotent
\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}
are unimodular is unsurprisingly false, as seen in Examples
2.12
and
2.13
. Since K-contact Lie algebras have skew-symmetric
\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}
, we obtain an immediate consequence:

Corollary 1.2
.

Centerless unimodular K-contact Lie algebras are either
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
or
\mathfrak{su}(2)
.

The same is true for centerless contact Lie algebras
(\mathfrak{g},\eta)
admitting a vector space splitting
\mathfrak{g}=\ker\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}\oplus\operatorname{im}\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}
, which we refer to as
DS-contact Lie algebras
. The “DS” part in the name stands for
direct sum
. Notice that not all contact Lie algebras are of this shape, for the splitting is equivalent to the condition
\ker\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}^{2}=\ker\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}
: it is thus a constraint on the Jordan form of
\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}
. Section
5
is devoted to the study of this class of contact Lie algebras.

For a DS-contact Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},\eta)
, set
\mathfrak{t}:=\ker\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}
,
\mathfrak{q}:=\operatorname{im}\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}
, and
\mathfrak{t}_{0}:=\mathfrak{t}\cap\mathfrak{h}
. A straightforward verification shows that the restrictions
\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}:=-(d_{\mathfrak{g}}\eta)|_{\mathfrak{t}}
and
\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}:=-(d_{\mathfrak{g}}\eta)|_{\mathfrak{q}}
are nondegenerate on
\mathfrak{t}_{0}
and
\mathfrak{q}
, respectively (see Lemma
3.2
). Moreover, it can be checked just as easily that
\mathfrak{t}_{0}
has a natural Lie bracket structure for which
\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}
is closed, and that
\mathfrak{t}
is a 1-dimensional central extension of
\mathfrak{t}_{0}
with associated cocycle
\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}
(we refer to Subsection
5.1
for details). As an interesting byproduct of the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we obtain the next result:

Theorem 1.3
.

\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}
is an exact symplectic form on
\mathfrak{t}_{0}
. Thus, neither
\mathfrak{t}_{0}
nor
\mathfrak{t}
is unimodular.

Lie algebras admitting exact symplectic forms are known as
Frobenius Lie algebras
in the literature. An explicit primitive of
\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}
can be given (see Theorem
5.4
). The Frobenius Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{t}_{0},\omega_{\mathfrak{t}})
is somewhat restricted (see Corollary
5.3
). A natural question, which we call the
realization problem
, can be posed: which Frobenius Lie algebras can be realized as the
\mathfrak{t}_{0}
-part of some DS-contact Lie algebra? For a given Frobenius Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{a},\omega_{\mathfrak{a}})
, set

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{a},\omega_{\mathfrak{a}}):=\{d\in 2\mathbb{N}\,|\,(\mathfrak{a},\omega_{\mathfrak{a}})\text{ is realizable with $\dim\mathfrak{q}=d$}\},

\displaystyle\nu(\mathfrak{a}):=\min\{\dim J\,|\,\text{$J$ is a nonzero ideal in $\mathfrak{a}$}\}.

The next result summarizes what has been proven in relation to the realization problem in this article (see Proposition
5.6
, Proposition
5.7
, and Corollary
5.8
):

Proposition 1.4
.

.

(i)

2\in\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{a},\omega_{\mathfrak{a}})
for any Frobenius Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{a},\omega_{\mathfrak{a}})
.

(ii)

\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{a},\omega_{\mathfrak{a}})=2\mathbb{N}
for any Frobenius Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{a},\omega_{\mathfrak{a}})
with a one-dimensional ideal.

(iii)

If
k^{2}<\nu(\mathfrak{a})
for some
k\geq 2
then
2k\notin\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{a},\omega_{\mathfrak{a}})
.

Another interesting byproduct of the proof of Theorem
1.1
is the following result, which also generalizes
[
6
, Proposition 8.1]
. Here,
\mathfrak{g}
is
not
assumed to be DS-contact:

Proposition 1.5
.

Let
(\mathfrak{g},\eta)
be a contact Lie algebra for which
\ker(K_{s})=\mathbb{R}\xi
, where
K_{s}
denotes the semisimple part of
\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}
. Then
\mathfrak{g}
is isomorphic to either
\mathfrak{su}(2)
or
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
. Thus,
\mathfrak{g}
is three-dimensional and simple, and admits compatible Sasakian structures.

An important application of Theorem
1.1
is cohomological in nature, and concerns the study of Lefschetz-type conditions for contact Lie algebras. Recall that the usual Lefschetz condition is defined for symplectic Lie algebras: a
2n
-dimensional symplectic Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{h},\omega)
is said to be
s
-Lefschetz, where
0\leq s\leq n-1
, if the maps

\displaystyle L^{n-k}:H^{k}(\mathfrak{h})\to H^{2n-k}(\mathfrak{h}),\quad L^{n-k}([\alpha]):=[\omega^{n-k}\wedge\alpha]

are bijective for all
k\leq s
. There are two main contact analogues for compact contact manifolds: one using basic cohomology, as put forward by
[
22
]
, and another using de Rham cohomology, as introduced in
[
13
]
. Both can be considered at the Lie algebra level: the former for basic cohomology, the latter for Chevalley-Eilenberg cohomology. Remarkably, in both
[
22
]
and
[
13
]
it is shown that compact Sasakian manifolds are
(n-1)
-Lefschetz, or
hard-Lefschetz
, in each corresponding sense. It is proven in
[
27
, Theorem 6.3]
that, for a compact
(2n+1)
-dimensional K-contact manifold and
0\leq s\leq n-1
, the basic
s
-Lefschetz condition holds if and only if the de Rham
s
-Lefschetz condition holds. A similar equivalence can be formulated at the Lie algebra level.

Corollary
1.2
ensures that, in the centerless K-contact setting, the two versions of the Lefschetz condition fail simultaneously already at the
s=0
level (except for
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
and
\mathfrak{su}(2)
, which are Sasakian and hence hard-Lefschetz). More generally, Theorem
1.1
ensures that the same simultaneous failure holds for any contact Lie algebra for which
\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}
is not nilpotent, even though Lin’s equivalence is not known to hold in such generality. The Lefschetz questions considered here, both in the symplectic and contact settings, are connected with previous work of the author
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
. The status of Lin’s equivalence for general contact Lie algebras remains open; in particular, to the best of the author’s knowledge, there has been no systematic study of any version of the contact Lefschetz condition when
\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}
is nilpotent. We intend to address this in future work.

The article is organized as follows: Section

and

a general vector space decomposition of contact Lie algebras is discussed, as well as many consequences stemming from the interaction between the Lie bracket and said decomposition, all serving to establish Theorem

is given. The full proof of Theorem

is contained in Section

5.1
lists elementary properties of DS-contact Lie algebras, Subsection
5.2
contains the proof of Theorem
1.3
and Proposition
1.4
, Subsection
5.3
contains a classification of 5-dimensional (centerless) DS-contact Lie algebras, and Subsection
5.4
contains a refinement of some ideas presented in Subsection
2.3
.

2.
Preliminaries on contact Lie algebras

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a finite-dimensional real Lie algebra. A
contact form
on
\mathfrak{g}
is a
1
-form
\eta\in\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\bigwedge$}^{1}\mathfrak{g}^{*}
with

\displaystyle\eta\wedge(d\eta)^{n}\neq 0,\quad\text{where $\dim\mathfrak{g}=2n+1$}.

The pair
(\mathfrak{g},\eta)
is called a
contact Lie algebra
. In every contact Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},\eta)
, the conditions

\displaystyle\iota_{\xi}\eta=1\quad\text{ and }\quad\iota_{\xi}(d_{\mathfrak{g}}\eta)=0

uniquely determine a vector
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
, called the
Reeb vector
of
(\mathfrak{g},\eta)
.

Lemma 2.1
.

The following conditions hold.

(i)

\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}(\ker\eta)\subseteq\ker\eta
.

(ii)

\operatorname{im}(\operatorname{ad}_{\xi})\subseteq\ker\eta
.

Proof.

Notice that

\displaystyle 0=\iota_{\xi}(d_{\mathfrak{g}}\eta)=(d_{\mathfrak{g}}\circ\iota_{\xi}+\iota_{\xi}\circ d_{\mathfrak{g}})(\eta)=\mathcal{L}_{\xi}\eta=\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}^{*}\eta.

(1)

(i)

If
x\in\ker\eta
then
\eta([\xi,x])=\eta(\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}x)=\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}^{*}\eta(x)=0
. Thus
\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}(\ker\eta)\subseteq\ker\eta
.

(ii)

If
y\in\operatorname{im}\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}
then
y=\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}x
for some
x\in\mathfrak{g}
, and then
\eta(y)=\eta(\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}x)=\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}^{*}\eta(x)=0
. Therefore,
\mathrm{im}(\operatorname{ad}_{\xi})\subseteq\ker\eta
. ∎

Thus,
\mathfrak{h}:=\ker\eta
is an
\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}
-invariant subspace of
\mathfrak{g}
. Also, the contact condition on
\eta
implies that
\omega:=-(d_{\mathfrak{g}}\eta)
is a nondegenerate
2
-form on
\mathfrak{h}
. Let
p_{\mathfrak{h}}\colon\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{h}
denote the canonical linear projection associated with the vector space splitting
\mathfrak{g}=\mathbb{R}\xi\oplus\mathfrak{h}
, and denote

\displaystyle\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\bigwedge$}_{\xi}^{*}\mathfrak{g}^{*}:=\{\alpha\in\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\bigwedge$}^{*}\mathfrak{g}^{*}\,|\,\iota_{\xi}\alpha=0\}.

The following result follows from direct, unenlightnening computations.

Lemma 2.2
.

The induced map
p_{\mathfrak{h}}^{*}:\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\bigwedge$}^{*}\mathfrak{h}^{*}\to\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\bigwedge$}^{*}\mathfrak{g}^{*}
is a linear isomorphism onto
\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\bigwedge$}_{\xi}^{*}\mathfrak{g}^{*}
. Its inverse is the map
\mathrm{res}:\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\bigwedge$}_{\xi}^{*}\mathfrak{g}^{*}\to\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\bigwedge$}^{*}\mathfrak{h}^{*}
given by restriction
\mathrm{res}(\alpha):=\alpha|_{\mathfrak{h}}
.

We identify
\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\bigwedge$}_{\xi}^{*}\mathfrak{g}^{*}\cong\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\bigwedge$}^{*}\mathfrak{h}^{*}
in what follows. Notice that this is
not
an isomorphism of cochain complexes, since in general there is no natural differential on
\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\bigwedge$}^{*}\mathfrak{h}^{*}
.

2.1.
Contactization

The class of contact Lie algebras with nontrivial center is structurally well understood.

Proposition 2.3
.

[
5
, Proposition 1]

If the center
\mathfrak{z}(\mathfrak{g})
of
(\mathfrak{g},\eta)
is nontrivial then
\mathfrak{z}(\mathfrak{g})=\mathbb{R}\xi
.

Proof.

Let
z\in\mathfrak{z}(\mathfrak{g})
be written as
z=a\xi+x
for some
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
x\in\ker\eta
. Since
\iota_{\xi}(d_{\mathfrak{g}}\eta)=0
(due to the definition of
\xi
) and
\iota_{z}(d_{\mathfrak{g}}\eta)=0
(since
z\in\mathfrak{z}(\mathfrak{g})
), we get

\displaystyle 0=\iota_{z}(d_{\mathfrak{g}}\eta)=a\iota_{\xi}(d_{\mathfrak{g}}\eta)+\iota_{x}(d_{\mathfrak{g}}\eta)=\iota_{x}(d_{\mathfrak{g}}\eta).

The nondegeneracy of
d_{\mathfrak{g}}\eta
on
\ker\eta
implies
x=0
, and thus
z=a\xi
. Therefore, either
\mathfrak{z}(\mathfrak{g})=0
(when
a=0
) or
\mathfrak{z}(\mathfrak{g})=\mathbb{R}\xi
(when
a\neq 0
).
∎

The following result is well-known, and it established by routine computations.

Proposition 2.4
.

[
5
, Proposition 2]

If
\mathfrak{z}(\mathfrak{g})=\mathbb{R}\xi
then
\mathfrak{h}
is a Lie algebra with bracket
[\cdot,\cdot]_{\mathfrak{h}}
uniquely determined by declaring
p_{\mathfrak{h}}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{h}
to be a Lie algebra morphism. In this case,
\mathfrak{g}
is a
1
-dimensional central extension of
\mathfrak{h}
by
\omega
, meaning

\displaystyle[x,y]_{\mathfrak{g}}:=\omega(x,y)\xi+[x,y]_{\mathfrak{h}}\text{ for all $x$, $y\in\mathfrak{h}$},\quad[\xi,\mathfrak{h}]=0.

(2)

A
1
-dimensional central extension by a symplectic cocycle is sometimes called
contactization
. In fact, contact Lie algebras with nontrivial center are in bijective correspondence with symplectic Lie algebras, contactization being the correspondence (see
[
5
, Proposition 2]
): Starting with a symplectic Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{h},\omega)
, equation (
2
) defines a Lie bracket on the vector space
\mathfrak{g}:=\mathbb{R}\xi\oplus\mathfrak{h}
, and the formula
\eta(a\xi+x)=a
for all
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
x\in\mathfrak{h}
defines a contact form on
\mathfrak{g}
with Reeb vector
\xi
; certainly, this construction gives
\mathfrak{z}(\mathfrak{g})=\mathbb{R}\xi
, so contactization occurs if and only if
\mathfrak{g}
has nontrivial center. There is no similar result characterizing all centerless contact Lie algebras. Certainly, two symplectic Lie algebras are isomorphic if and only if their contactizations are isomorphic (see
[
5
, Proposition 4]
).

Remark 2.5
.

If
p_{\mathfrak{h}}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{h}
is a Lie algebra morphism then the transpose map
p_{\mathfrak{h}}^{*}:\mathfrak{h}^{*}\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}
induces a cochain map between
(\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\bigwedge$}^{*}\mathfrak{h}^{*},d_{\mathfrak{h}})
and
(\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\bigwedge$}^{*}\mathfrak{g}^{*},d_{\mathfrak{g}})
, and a cochain isomorphism between
(\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\bigwedge$}^{*}\mathfrak{h}^{*},d_{\mathfrak{h}})
and
(\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\bigwedge$}_{\xi}^{*}\mathfrak{g}^{*},d_{\mathfrak{g}})
. Following the remarks in the last paragraph, the maps
p_{\mathfrak{h}}
and
p_{\mathfrak{h}}^{*}
can be turned into a Lie algebra morphism and a cochain map respectively precisely when
(\mathfrak{g},\omega)
arises as contactization from a symplectic Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{h},\omega)
.

Remark 2.6
.

If
(\mathfrak{g},\eta)
is a contact Lie algebra obtained from a symplectic Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{h},\omega)
via contactization then, with respect to the vector space splitting
\mathfrak{g}=\mathbb{R}\xi\oplus\mathfrak{h}
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{ad}_{x}^{\mathfrak{g}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&*\\
0&\operatorname{ad}_{x}^{\mathfrak{h}}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{for all $x\in\mathfrak{h}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}$}.

In particular,
\mathfrak{g}
is unimodular, nilpotent, (completely) solvable if and only if
\mathfrak{h}
is unimodular, nilpotent, (completely) solvable: notice that Engel’s theorem is used to establish the equivalence for nilpotency, and Cartan’s criterion is used to establish the equivalence for solvability. In the unimodular case, it follows from
[
17
, Theorem 11]
that
\mathfrak{h}
is solvable since it is symplectic.

2.2.
Sasakian and K-contact Lie algebras

On any contact Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},\eta)
, there exist an inner product
g
and a
(1,1)
-tensor field
\Phi
subject to the following identities:

\displaystyle\eta=\iota_{\xi}g,\quad d\eta=2g(\cdot,\Phi\,\cdot),\quad\Phi^{2}=-\operatorname{Id}_{\mathfrak{g}}+\eta\otimes\xi.

All these imply at once that
\Phi\xi=0
and
\eta\circ\Phi=0
, as well as the compatibility condition

\displaystyle g(\Phi x,\Phi y)=g(x,y)-\eta(x)\eta(y)\text{ for all $x$, $y\in\mathfrak{g})$}.

One can adapt the arguments in
[
10
, Section 4]
for the Lie algebra setting to establish these results. A triple
(\eta,g,\Phi)
satisfying all of the above is called a
metric contact structure
. Special metric contact structures include
K-contact
and
Sasakian
structures, which we now define.

Denote by
\mathcal{L}_{\xi}
the Lie derivative operator
\mathcal{L}_{\xi}
on
\mathfrak{g}
with respect to
\xi
, by
\nabla
the Levi-Civita connection associated with the inner product
g
on
\mathfrak{g}
, and by
N_{\Phi}
the Nijenhuis tensor associated to
\Phi
. Recall that it satisfies, in the metric contact setting, the following identities:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\xi}\Phi=[\operatorname{ad}_{\xi},\Phi],\quad(\mathcal{L}_{\xi}g)(x,y)=g(\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}x,y)+g(x,\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}y)\text{ for all $x$, $y\in\mathfrak{g}$},

A K-contact Lie algebra is a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
with a metric contact structure
(\eta,g,\Phi)
in which any, and therefore all, of the following equivalent statements hold:

Proposition 2.7
.

The following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

\mathcal{L}_{\xi}g=0
.

(ii)

\mathcal{L}_{\xi}\Phi=0
.

(iii)

\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}
is skew symmetric with respect to
g
.

(iv)

\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}
and
\Phi
commute.

(v)

\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}\circ\Phi
is symmetric with respect to
g
.

(vi)

\Phi x=-\nabla_{x}\xi
for all
x\in\mathfrak{g}
.

Proof.

It follows by adapting
[
10
, Theorem 6.2]
and
[
10
, Lemma 6.2]
to the Lie algebra setting.
∎

As a direct consequence, in any K-contact Lie algebra there is a
g
-orthogonal direct sum decomposition of
\mathfrak{g}
into
\Phi
-invariant subspaces:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}=\ker\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}\oplus\operatorname{im}\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}.

(3)

A Sasakian Lie algebra is a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
with a metric contact structure
(\eta,g,\Phi)
in which any, and therefore all, of the following equivalent statements hold:

Proposition 2.8
.

The following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

N_{\Phi}(x,y)=-d\eta(x,y)\xi
for
x
,
y\in\mathfrak{g}
.

(ii)

(\nabla_{x}\Phi)y=g(x,y)\xi-\eta(y)x
for
x
,
y\in\mathfrak{g}
.

Proof.

It follows by adapting
[
10
, Theorem 6.3]
to the Lie algebra setting.
∎

Recall that all Sasakian Lie algebras are K-contact (as pointed out in
[
10
, Corollary 6.3]
), but the converse does not hold in general: for instance, it is known that the only Sasakian nilpotent Lie algebras are the Heisenberg Lie algebras (see
[
5
, Theorem 3.9]
), while all nilpotent Lie algebras are K-contact (since they have nontrivial center and thus
\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}=0
is skew-symmetric with respect to any compatible metric).

2.3.
Cohomology remarks

It is well-known that a
m
-dimensional Lie algebra
\mathfrak{k}
is unimodular if and only if
H^{m}_{CE}(\mathfrak{k})\neq 0
, meaning that the differential map
d_{\mathfrak{k}}:\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\bigwedge$}^{m-1}\mathfrak{k}^{*}\to\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\bigwedge$}^{m}\mathfrak{k}^{*}
is zero. Here,
H^{*}_{CE}(\mathfrak{g})
stands for Chevalley-Eilenberg cohomology with trivial coefficients. This follows from the fact that the map
x\mapsto\iota_{x}\mu
is an isomorphism between
\mathfrak{k}
and
\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\bigwedge$}^{m-1}\mathfrak{k}^{*}
, as well as the identity

\displaystyle d_{\mathfrak{k}}(\iota_{x}\mu)=\mathcal{L}_{x}\mu=-\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{ad}_{x})\mu\quad\text{for all $x\in\mathfrak{k}$ and $\mu\in\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\bigwedge$}^{m}\mathfrak{k}^{*}$}.

We refer to
[
9
, Section 3.1]
for the fleshed-out details. The next result appears to be new.

Proposition 2.9
.

If a contact Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},\eta)
is unimodular then
\xi\in[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]
.

Proof.

Assume
\xi\notin[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]
. Then there exists
\alpha\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
such that
\alpha(\xi)=1
and
\alpha([\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}])=0
. In particular,
d_{\mathfrak{g}}\alpha=0
. Notice that
\alpha\wedge\omega^{n}
is exact, for
d_{\mathfrak{g}}\omega=0
and then

\displaystyle d_{\mathfrak{g}}(\alpha\wedge\eta\wedge\omega^{n-1})=-\alpha\wedge d_{\mathfrak{g}}\eta\wedge\omega^{n-1}=\alpha\wedge\omega^{n}.

There is
\alpha_{0}\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
such that
\iota_{\xi}\alpha_{0}=0
and
\alpha=\eta+\alpha_{0}
. Since
\iota_{\xi}\omega^{n}=0
as well, we see that
\alpha_{0}\wedge\omega^{n}
is identified with a form of degree
(2n+1)
in
\mathfrak{h}=\ker\eta
, and thus it is the zero form. This implies
\alpha\wedge\omega^{n}=\eta\wedge\omega^{n}
, whence the volume form
\eta\wedge\omega^{n}
is exact and
H^{2n+1}(\mathfrak{g})=0
.
∎

From Proposition
2.9
we get a particular case of Theorem
1.1
for unimodular solvable Lie algebras: since
\xi\in[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]
holds for this class of Lie algebras, where
[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]
is a nilpotent ideal, Engel’s theorem implies that
\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}
is nilpotent.

The converse of Proposition
2.9
need not hold. The following set of examples exhibit diverse ways in which it fails.

The first two examples arise as contactization of symplectic Lie algebras, and thus have nontrivial center and
\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}=0
. One is solvable while the other is not. According to
[

, Corollary 5]
, there are no 5-dimensional nonsolvable examples of this kind. It is remarked in
[

, Remark 3.11]
that, for this class of contact Lie algebras,
\xi\in[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]
holds precisely when
\omega:=-d_{\mathfrak{g}}\eta
is nonexact on
\mathfrak{h}
.

Example 2.10
.

Let
\mathfrak{h}

\displaystyle[e_{1},e_{4}]=-e_{1},\quad[e_{3},e_{4}]=-e_{2}.

\mathfrak{h}

Example 2.12

\mathfrak{g}

Example 2.13
.

Let

Example 2.15
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be the Lie algebra with basis
\{\xi,e_{1},e_{2},e_{3},e_{4},e_{5},e_{6}\}
and bracket given by

\displaystyle[e_{1},e_{2}]=e_{2}+2\xi,\quad[e_{3},e_{4}]=e_{4}+2\xi,\quad[e_{5},e_{6}]=2\xi,

\displaystyle[\xi,e_{6}]=-e_{5},\quad[e_{2},e_{6}]=-2e_{5},\quad[e_{4},e_{6}]=-2e_{5},

\displaystyle[\xi,e_{5}]=\phantom{+}e_{6},\quad[e_{2},e_{5}]=-2e_{6},\quad[e_{4},e_{5}]=-2e_{6}.

It is not unimodular since

, and it is not solvable. According to

, it admits a Sasakian structure for which

Denote by
H_{B}^{*}(\mathfrak{g})
the basic cohomology of
(\mathfrak{g},\eta)
, consisting of forms that are closed (in the usual Chevalley-Eilenberg sense) and
\xi
-basic (meaning both
\iota_{\xi}\alpha=0
and
\mathcal{L}_{\xi}\alpha=0
) modulo exact
\xi
-basic forms. Notice that
\omega=-d_{\mathfrak{g}}\eta
is
\xi
-basic since
\iota_{\xi}\omega=-\iota_{\xi}d_{\mathfrak{g}}\eta=0
and
\mathcal{L}_{\xi}\omega=d_{\mathfrak{g}}\iota_{\xi}\omega=0
.

Lemma 2.16
.

The following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

\xi\in[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]
.

(ii)

Every class in
H^{1}_{CE}(\mathfrak{g})
has a
\xi
-basic representative.

(iii)

The class
[\omega]_{B}
of
\omega:=-d_{\mathfrak{g}}\eta
in
H^{2}_{B}(\mathfrak{g})
is nonzero.

Proof.

.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii): It follows from the identification
H^{1}_{CE}(\mathfrak{g})=Z^{1}_{CE}(\mathfrak{g})
, since closed forms vanish on
[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]
by definition.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii): If
[\omega]_{B}=0
then there is some basic
1
-form
\theta
such that
\omega=d_{\mathfrak{g}}\theta
; then, the 1-form
\alpha:=\eta+\theta
is closed and non-exact, and satisfies
\alpha(\xi)=1
, contradicting (ii).

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(i): If
\xi\notin[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]
then there is a closed
1
-form
\alpha\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
such that
\alpha(\xi)=1
, and thus
\theta:=\eta-\alpha
is
\xi
-basic and
\omega=-d_{\mathfrak{g}}\theta
.
∎

Let
\mathrm{pr}_{\mathfrak{h}}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{h}
denote the canonical linear projection associated with the vector space splitting
\mathfrak{g}=\mathbb{R}\xi\oplus\mathfrak{h}
. For each
x\in\mathfrak{h}=\ker\eta
, define
M_{x}:=\mathrm{pr}_{\mathfrak{h}}\circ\operatorname{ad}_{x}|_{\mathfrak{h}}
. Setting
\nu:=\omega^{n}
, where
\omega:=-d_{\mathfrak{g}}\eta
and
\dim\mathfrak{g}=2n+1
, a straightforward computation shows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\xi}(\iota_{x}\nu)=\iota_{[\xi,x]},\quad d_{\mathfrak{g}}(\iota_{x}\nu)=\mathcal{L}_{x}\nu=-\operatorname{tr}(M_{x})\nu

for all
x\in\mathfrak{h}
. Thus, a form
\iota_{x}\nu
is basic if and only if
x\in\ker(\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}|_{\mathfrak{h}})
, and
d:\Omega_{B}^{2n-1}(\mathfrak{g})\to\Omega_{B}^{2n}
is zero if and only if
\operatorname{tr}(M_{x})=0
for all
x\in\ker(\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}|_{\mathfrak{h}})
. Contact Lie algebras
(\mathfrak{g},\eta)
are said to be
transversely unimodular
when this last property holds; that is, when each
M_{x}
is traceless for any
x\in\ker(\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}|_{\mathfrak{h}})
. Hence, we might say that the transverse Poincaré duality amounts to the equivalence between
H^{2n}_{B}(\mathfrak{g})\neq 0
and transverse unimodularity.

Corollary 2.17
.

If a contact Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},\eta)
is transversely unimodular then
\xi\in[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]
.

Proof.

While a similar argument as in the proof of Proposition
2.9
works, it is easier to point out that transverse unimodularity implies that
[\omega^{n}]_{B}
is a nonzero class in
H_{B}^{2n}(\mathfrak{g})
, which in turn implies
[\omega]_{B}
is a nonzero class in
H^{2}_{B}(\mathfrak{g})
, from where we get
\xi\in[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]
by Lemma
2.16
.
∎

Since
[\xi,x]=0
for all
x\in\ker(\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}|_{\mathfrak{h}})
, the decomposition of
\operatorname{ad}_{x}
with respect to the vector space splitting
\mathbb{R}\xi\oplus\mathfrak{h}
is just

\displaystyle\operatorname{ad}_{x}=\begin{pmatrix}0&*\\
0&M_{x}\end{pmatrix},\quad M_{x}:=\mathrm{pr}_{\mathfrak{h}}\circ\operatorname{ad}_{x}\in\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{h}),

and thus
\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{ad}_{x})=\operatorname{tr}(M_{x})
for all
x\in\ker\eta
. Since transverse unimodularity concerns only those
x\in\mathfrak{h}
lying in
\ker(\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}|_{\mathfrak{h}})
, we can only conclude that unimodular contact Lie algebras are transversely unimodular, but not the other way around.

The fact that Theorem
1.1
gives the failure of transverse unimodularity on the stated hypothesis is of relevance in connection to the two main versions of the Lefschetz conditions proposed for the contact setting: one for the Chevalley-Eilenberg cohomology, related to the one introduced in
[
13
]
, and another for the basic cohomology, as put forward in
[
22
]
. That is to say, since contact Lie algebras where the adjoint action
\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}
of the Reeb vector is not nilpotent are not transversely unimodular (except for
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
and
\mathfrak{su}(2)
), this means that no such Lie algebra can be
0
-Lefschetz in either of the two versions.

3.
A useful decomposition for contact Lie algebras

A natural
\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}
-invariant vector space splitting for a given contact Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},\eta)
comes from the Jordan-Chevalley decomposition of
K:=\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}
: if
K_{s}
and
K_{n}
denote the semisimple part and the nilpotent part of
K
respectively, then

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}=\ker K_{s}\oplus\operatorname{im}K_{s}

(4)

as vector spaces, since
\ker K_{s}^{2}=\ker K_{s}
precisely because
K_{s}
is semisimple. For convenience, set

\displaystyle\mathfrak{h}:=\ker\eta,\quad\mathfrak{t}:=\ker K_{s},\quad\mathfrak{q}:=\operatorname{im}K_{s},\quad\mathfrak{t}_{0}:=\mathfrak{t}\cap\mathfrak{h}.

Thus, according to our conventions,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{t}\oplus\mathfrak{q}=\mathbb{R}\xi\oplus\mathfrak{t}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{q},\quad\mathfrak{h}=\mathfrak{t}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{q},

again as vector spaces. Clearly,
\mathfrak{q}\subseteq\mathfrak{h}
. As
K_{s}
acts as the zero map in the
0
-eigenspace of
K=\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}
, we also get
\xi\in\mathfrak{t}
. Since
K_{s}
and
K_{n}
are commuting polynomials in
K
, the subspaces
\mathfrak{h}
,
\mathfrak{t}
and
\mathfrak{q}
are invariant under
K
,
K_{s}
and
K_{n}
. In particular, the restrictions of any of these operators to either
\mathfrak{h}
,
\mathfrak{t}
, or
\mathfrak{q}
are all well-defined. Clearly,
K|_{\mathfrak{h}}=K_{s}|_{\mathfrak{h}}+K_{n}|_{\mathfrak{h}}
is the Jordan-Chevalley decomposition of
K|_{\mathfrak{h}}
; the same is true for the restrictions to
\mathfrak{t}
and
\mathfrak{q}
. Notice that
K|_{\mathfrak{q}}=K_{s}|_{\mathfrak{q}}\left(\operatorname{Id}_{\mathfrak{q}}+(K_{s}|_{\mathfrak{q}})^{-1}K_{n}|_{\mathfrak{q}}\right)
, so
K|_{\mathfrak{q}}
is invertible; in contrast,
K|_{\mathfrak{t}}=K_{n}|_{\mathfrak{t}}
. We shall use all these facts without further mention.

The condition
\mathfrak{t}=\mathbb{R}\xi
holds precisely when
\mathfrak{h}=\mathfrak{q}
(that is,
\mathfrak{t}\cap\mathfrak{h}=0
), and has the special significance of rendering most of our treatment vacuous. However, up to isomorphism, only two contact Lie algebras fulfill this property.

Proposition 3.1
.

If
\mathfrak{t}=\mathbb{R}\xi
then
\mathfrak{g}
is isomorphic to either
\mathfrak{su}(2)
or
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

The statement can be established with a minor variation on the argument used in
[
6
, Proposition 8.1]
. We present an alternative, more elementary proof. Notice that the nilradical
\mathfrak{n}
of
\mathfrak{g}
is
K_{s}
-invariant since it is
K
-invariant. Assume there is some
x\in\mathfrak{n}
whose
\xi
-component is nonzero, and apply the spectral projector onto
\ker K_{s}=\mathbb{R}\xi
to show that
\xi\in\mathfrak{n}
. As
\mathfrak{n}
is nilpotent, Engel’s theorem implies that
\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}|_{\mathfrak{n}}
is nilpotent. On the other hand, since
\mathfrak{n}=(\mathfrak{n}\cap\mathbb{R}\xi)\oplus(\mathfrak{n}\cap\mathfrak{h})
and
K=\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}
vanishes on
\mathbb{R}\xi
but is invertible on
\mathfrak{h}=\mathfrak{q}
, the map
\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}|_{\mathfrak{n}}
can be nilpotent only if
\mathfrak{n}\cap\mathfrak{h}=0
, meaning
\mathfrak{n}=\mathbb{R}\xi
. This situation is impossible, because otherwise

\displaystyle[\xi,\mathfrak{h}]=K(\mathfrak{h})=\mathfrak{h}\subseteq\mathfrak{n}\cap\mathfrak{h}=0,

contradicting the invertibility of
K|_{\mathfrak{h}}
. We conclude that every element of
\mathfrak{n}
lies in
\mathfrak{h}
. Now, let
x\in\mathfrak{n}
. Since
\mathfrak{n}
is an ideal in
\mathfrak{g}
and
\mathfrak{n}\subseteq\mathfrak{h}
,
[x,y]\in\mathfrak{n}\subseteq\mathfrak{h}
for all
y\in\mathfrak{h}
, and thus

\displaystyle\iota_{x}(d_{\mathfrak{g}}\eta)(y)=d_{\mathfrak{g}}\eta(x,y)=-\eta([x,y]).=0\text{ for all $y\in\mathfrak{h}$}

The nondegeneracy of
d_{\mathfrak{g}}\eta|_{\mathfrak{h}}
implies that
x=0
, and thus
\mathfrak{n}=0
. Now observe that every nonzero solvable ideal contains a nonzero nilpotent ideal, namely its commutator. Thus, the vanishing of
\mathfrak{n}
implies that the radical of
\mathfrak{g}
is zero. Therefore,
\mathfrak{g}
is semisimple. By
[
12
, Theorem 5]
, the only semisimple contact Lie
algebras are
\mathfrak{su}(2)
and
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
.
∎

For the rest of the discussion, we implicitly assume that
\dim\mathfrak{t}>1
. Denote by
\eta|_{\mathfrak{t}}:=\eta_{\mathfrak{t}}
the restriction of
\eta
to
\mathfrak{t}
, and by
\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}
and
\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}
the restrictions of
\omega=-d_{\mathfrak{g}}\eta
to
\mathfrak{t}_{0}
and
\mathfrak{q}
, respectively.

Lemma 3.2
.

The following identities hold:

(i)

K|_{\mathfrak{h}}
,
K_{s}|_{\mathfrak{h}}\in\mathfrak{sp}(\mathfrak{h},\omega)
.

(ii)

\ker K_{s}|_{\mathfrak{h}}=(\operatorname{im}K_{s}|_{\mathfrak{h}})^{\bot_{\omega}}
.

(iii)

\omega(\mathfrak{t}_{0},\mathfrak{q})=0
.

(iv)

\omega=\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}+\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}
.

(v)

\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}
is symplectic on
\mathfrak{t}_{0}
.

(vi)

\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}
is symplectic on
\mathfrak{q}
.

Proof.

.

(i)

For all
u
,
v\in\mathfrak{h}
, Jacobi on
(\xi,u,v)
gives

\displaystyle\omega(K|_{\mathfrak{h}}u,v)+\omega(u,K|_{\mathfrak{h}}v)

\displaystyle=-d\eta(K|_{\mathfrak{h}}u,v)-d\eta(u,K|_{\mathfrak{h}}v)=\eta([[\xi,u],v]+[u,[\xi,v]])

\displaystyle=\eta([\xi,[u,v]])=d\eta(\xi,[u,v])=(\iota_{\xi}d\eta)([u,v])=0.

This shows
K|_{\mathfrak{h}}\in\mathfrak{sp}(\mathfrak{h},\omega)
. The second claim follows from
[
23
, Theorem 9.20]
applied to the inclusion
\mathfrak{sp}(\mathfrak{h},\omega)\hookrightarrow\mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{h})
, which is a representation of
\mathfrak{sp}(\mathfrak{h},\omega)
.

(ii)

For all
u\in\ker K_{s}|_{\mathfrak{h}}
and
v=K_{s}|_{\mathfrak{h}}w\in\operatorname{im}K_{s}|_{\mathfrak{h}}
, it follows from (i) that

\displaystyle\omega(u,v)=\omega(u,K_{s}|_{\mathfrak{h}}w)=-\omega(K_{s}|_{\mathfrak{h}}u,w)=-\omega(0,w)=0.

This shows
\ker K_{s}|_{\mathfrak{h}}\subseteq(\operatorname{im}K_{s}|_{\mathfrak{h}})^{\bot_{\omega}}
. Equality follows from dimension counting, since

\displaystyle\dim(\operatorname{im}K_{s}|_{\mathfrak{h}})^{\bot_{\omega}}=\dim\mathfrak{h}-\dim\operatorname{im}K_{s}|_{\mathfrak{h}}=\dim\ker K_{s}|_{\mathfrak{h}},

(iii)

It is immediate from (ii), since
\ker K_{s}|_{\mathfrak{h}}=\mathfrak{t}_{0}
and
\operatorname{im}K_{s}|_{\mathfrak{h}}=\mathfrak{q}
.

(iv)

It is immediate from (iii).

(v)

Let
x\in\mathfrak{t}_{0}
such that
\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}(x,\mathfrak{t}_{0})=0
. From (iii), we have
\omega(x,\mathfrak{q})=0
as well. Thus
\omega(x,\mathfrak{h})=0
. Since
\omega
is nondegenerate on
\mathfrak{h}
, it follows that
x=0
. Therefore
\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}
is nondegenerate on
\mathfrak{t}_{0}
.

(vi)

It is similar to (v). ∎

Since
K
is a derivation on
\mathfrak{g}
, both
K_{s}
and
K_{n}
are derivations on
\mathfrak{g}
: this fact follows from a standard argument using the decomposition of
\mathfrak{g}
into generalized eigenspaces of
K
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Both
[\mathfrak{t},\mathfrak{t}]\subseteq\mathfrak{t}
and
[\mathfrak{t},\mathfrak{q}]\subseteq\mathfrak{q}
hold.

Proof.

For the first claim, take
x
,
y\in\mathfrak{t}
, and note that

\displaystyle K_{s}([x,y])=[K_{s}(x),y]+[x,K_{s}(y)]=0.

For the second claim, take
x\in\mathfrak{t}
and
y\in\mathfrak{q}
, where
y=K_{s}(z)
for some
z\in\mathfrak{g}
, and note that

\displaystyle[x,y]

\displaystyle=[x,K_{s}(z)]=K_{s}([x,z])-[K_{s}(x),z]=K_{s}([x,z])\in\operatorname{im}K_{s}=\mathfrak{q}.\qed

Compare Lemma
3.3
with
[

, Proposition 8 (2)]
. Notice that their proof does not carry over to our setting.

Lemma
3.2
(v) and Lemma

imply that
(\mathfrak{t},\eta_{\mathfrak{t}})
is a contact Lie subalgebra of
(\mathfrak{g},\eta)
. Lemma
3.2
(vi) implies that
\mathfrak{q}
is never a Lie subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
, for otherwise
[\mathfrak{q},\mathfrak{q}]\subseteq\mathfrak{q}\subseteq\mathfrak{h}=\ker\eta
and
\eta([\mathfrak{q},\mathfrak{q}])=0
, meaning that
\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}=-(d\eta)|_{\mathfrak{q}}
is identically zero. Moreover, using Lemma

below, it is easy to find conditions ensuring

.

Consider the skew-symmetric maps

\displaystyle\alpha\colon\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\bigwedge$}^{2}\mathfrak{t}^{*}\to\mathfrak{t}_{0},\quad\beta\colon\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\bigwedge$}^{2}\mathfrak{q}^{*}\to\mathfrak{t}_{0},\quad\gamma\colon\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\bigwedge$}^{2}\mathfrak{q}^{*}\to\mathfrak{q},

defined as the
\mathfrak{t}_{0}
-component of
[\mathfrak{t},\mathfrak{t}]
, the
\mathfrak{t}_{0}
-component of
[\mathfrak{q},\mathfrak{q}]
, and the
\mathfrak{q}
-component of
[\mathfrak{q},\mathfrak{q}]
, respectively. A short computation shows that

\displaystyle[x,y]=\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}(x,y)\xi+\alpha(x,y)\text{ for all $x$, $y\in\mathfrak{t}$},

\displaystyle[u,v]=\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(u,v)\xi+\beta(u,v)+\gamma(u,v)\text{ for all $u$, $v\in\mathfrak{q}$}.

Indeed, if
\delta_{1}(\cdot,\cdot)\xi
and
\delta_{2}(\cdot,\cdot)\xi
denote the projection of
[\mathfrak{t},\mathfrak{t}]
and
[\mathfrak{q},\mathfrak{q}]
into
\mathbb{R}\xi
respectively, then

\displaystyle\delta_{1}(x,y)=\eta([x,y])=-(d_{\mathfrak{g}}\eta)(x,y)=\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}(x,y)\text{ for all $x$, $y\in\mathfrak{t}$},

\displaystyle\delta_{2}(u,v)=\eta([u,v])=-(d_{\mathfrak{g}}\eta)(u,v)=\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(u,v)\text{ for all $u$, $v\in\mathfrak{q}$}.

In combination with the fact that
\mathfrak{q}=\operatorname{im}K_{s}
is
K
-invariant, Lemma
3.3
allows for the good definition of the following operators:

\displaystyle C:=\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}|_{\mathfrak{t}}\in\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{t}),\quad A:=\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}|_{\mathfrak{q}}\in\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{q}),\quad\rho(x):=\operatorname{ad}_{x}|_{\mathfrak{q}}\in\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{q})\quad(x\in\mathfrak{t}_{0}).

We now derive from the Jacobi identity a number of relations and constraints for all the maps above, only a handful of which are of direct use for establishing Theorem
1.1
. For its statement, we employ the usual pullback notation on bilinear forms: for example, if
x
,
y\in\mathfrak{t}_{0}
, then
(C^{*}\omega_{\mathfrak{t}})(x,y)
means
\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}(Cx,y)+\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}(x,Cy)
. We also regard
\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{q})
as a Lie algebra with the usual commutator:
[M,N]:=MN-NM
for all
M
,
N\in\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{q})
.

Lemma 3.4
.

The following identities hold for all
x
,
y
,
z\in\mathfrak{t}_{0}
and
u
,
v
,
w\in\mathfrak{q}
:

(i)

C^{*}\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}=0
. That is,
C\in\mathfrak{sp}(\mathfrak{t}_{0},\omega_{\mathfrak{t}})
.

(ii)

A^{*}\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}=0
. That is,
A\in\mathfrak{sp}(\mathfrak{q},\omega_{\mathfrak{q}})
.

(iii)

(C^{*}\alpha)(x,y)=C(\alpha(x,y))
.

(iv)

(A^{*}\beta)(u,v)=C(\beta(u,v))
.

(v)

(A^{*}\gamma)(u,v)=A(\gamma(u,v))
.

(vi)

[A,\rho(x)]=\rho(Cx)
.

(vii)

[\rho(x),\rho(y)]=\rho(\alpha(x,y))+\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}(x,y)A
.

(viii)

(\rho(x)^{*}\omega_{\mathfrak{q}})(u,v)=\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}(x,\beta(u,v))
.

(ix)

(\rho(x)^{*}\beta)(u,v)=\alpha(x,\beta(u,v))-~\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(u,v)Cx
.

(x)

(\rho(x)^{*}\gamma)(u,v)=\rho(x)(\gamma(u,v))
.

(xi)

\mathfrak{S}_{x,y,z}\;\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}(\alpha(x,y),z)=0
.

(xii)

\mathfrak{S}_{u,v,w}\;\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(\gamma(u,v),w)=0
.

(xiii)

\mathfrak{S}_{u,v,w}\;\beta(\gamma(u,v),w)=0
.

(xiv)

\mathfrak{S}_{x,y,z}\;\alpha(\alpha(x,y),z)=\mathfrak{S}_{x,y,z}\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}(x,y)Cz
.

(xv)

\mathfrak{S}_{u,v,w}\gamma(\gamma(u,v),w))=\mathfrak{S}_{u,v,w}T(u,v,w)
.

Here,
\mathfrak{S}
denotes cyclic sum; also,
T(u,v,w):=\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(u,v)Aw+\rho(\beta(u,v))w
.

Proof.

Lemma
3.2
and Lemma
3.3
are being implicitly used throughout all the proof.

For all
x
,
y\in\mathfrak{t}_{0}
, Jacobi on
(\xi,x,y)

\displaystyle C(\alpha(x,y))

\displaystyle=[\xi,\alpha(x,y)]=[\xi,[x,y]]=[[\xi,x],y]+[x,[\xi,y]]

\displaystyle=[Cx,y]+[x,Cy]=C^{*}[x,y]=(C^{*}\omega_{\mathfrak{t}})(x,y)\xi+(C^{*}\alpha)(x,y).

The
\xi
-component gives (i), and the
\mathfrak{t}_{0}
-component gives (iii).

For all
u
,
v\in\mathfrak{q}
, Jacobi on
(\xi,u,v)
gives

\displaystyle C(\beta(u,v))+A(\gamma(u,v))

\displaystyle=[\xi,\beta(u,v)+\gamma(u,v)]=[\xi,[u,v]]=[[\xi,u],v]+[u,[\xi,v]]

\displaystyle=[Au,v]+[u,Av]=A^{*}[u,v]=(A^{*}\omega_{\mathfrak{q}})(u,v)\xi+(A^{*}\beta)(u,v)+(A^{*}\gamma)(u,v).

The
\xi
-component gives (ii), the
\mathfrak{t}_{0}
-component gives (iv), and the
\mathfrak{q}
-component gives (v).

For all

and
u\in\mathfrak{q}
, Jacobi on
(\xi,x,u)
gives

\displaystyle\rho(Cx)u=[[\xi,x],u]

\displaystyle=[\xi,[x,u]]+[x,[u,\xi]]=A\rho(x)u-\rho(x)Au=[A,\rho(x)]u,

whence (vi) follows.

For all
x
,
y\in\mathfrak{t}_{0}
and
u\in\mathfrak{q}
, Jacobi on

\displaystyle\rho(\alpha(x,y))u+\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}(x,y)Au

\displaystyle=\rho(x)(\rho(y)u)-\rho(y)(\rho(x)u)=[\rho(x),\rho(y)]u,

whence (vii) follows.

For all
x\in\mathfrak{t}_{0}
and
u
,
v\in\mathfrak{q}

gives

\displaystyle[x,\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(u,v)\xi+\beta(u,v)+\gamma(u,v)]

\displaystyle=[x,[u,v]]=[[x,u],v]+[u,[x,v]]=[\rho(x)u,v]+[u,\rho(x)v]

\displaystyle=(\rho(x)^{*}\omega_{\mathfrak{q}})(u,v)\xi+(\rho(x)^{*}\beta)(u,v)+(\rho(x)^{*}\gamma)(u,v).

The LHS is certainly equal to
\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}(x,\beta(u,v))\xi+\alpha(x,\beta(u,v))-\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(u,v)Cx+\rho(x)\gamma(u,v)
. Thus, the
\xi
-component gives (viii), the
\mathfrak{t}_{0}
-component gives (ix), and the
\mathfrak{q}
-component gives (x).

For all
x
,
y
,

\displaystyle[x,\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}(y,z)\xi+\alpha(y,z)]

\displaystyle=[x,[y,z]]=[[x,y],z]+[y,[x,z]]

\displaystyle=[\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}(x,y)\xi+\alpha(x,y),z]+[y,\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}(x,z)\xi+\alpha(x,z)]

\displaystyle=(\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}(\alpha(x,y),z)+\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}(y,\alpha(x,z)))\xi

\displaystyle\quad+\alpha(\alpha(x,y),z)+\alpha(y,\alpha(x,z))+\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}(x,y)Cz-\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}(x,z)Cy.

The LHS is certainly equal to
\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}(x,\alpha(y,z))\xi+\alpha(x,\alpha(y,z))-\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}(y,z)Cx
. Thus, the
\xi
-component gives (xi), and the
\mathfrak{t}_{0}
-component gives (xiv).

For all
u
,
v
,

, Jacobi on

gives

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=[u,[v,w]]+[v,[w,u]]+[w,[u,v]]

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{S}_{u,v,w}([u,\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(v,w)\xi+\beta(v,w)+\gamma(v,w)]).

The summand inside
\mathfrak{S}_{u,v,w}
is certainly equal to

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(u,\gamma(v,w))\xi+\beta(u,\gamma(v,w))+\gamma(u,\gamma(v,w))-\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(v,w)Au-\rho(\beta(v,w))u.

The
\xi
-component gives (xii), the
\mathfrak{t}_{0}
-component gives (xiii), and the
\mathfrak{q}
-component gives (xv).
∎

Notice that Lemma

(i) and (ii) follow from

. In fact,

\displaystyle 0=(\mathcal{L}_{\xi}\omega)(x,y)=(\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}^{*}\omega)(x,y)=(C^{*}\omega_{\mathfrak{t}})(x,y)\text{ for all $x$, $y\in\mathfrak{t}_{0}$},

\displaystyle 0=(\mathcal{L}_{\xi}\omega)(u,v)=(\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}^{*}\omega)(u,v)=(A^{*}\omega_{\mathfrak{q}})(u,v)\text{ for all $u$, $v\in\mathfrak{q}$}.

Similarly, Lemma

and

by evaluating in

,

,

,

, and

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=-(d_{\mathfrak{g}}\omega)(x,u,v)=\omega([x,u],v)+\omega([v,x],u)+\omega([u,v],x)

\displaystyle=\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(\rho(x)u,v)+\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(u,\rho(x)v)-\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}(x,\beta(u,v)),

and analogously for the other ones. Interestingly enough, upon evaluating in

.

We collect the following immediate consequences of Lemma

for future reference.

Corollary 3.5
.

Set
\mathfrak{b}:=\operatorname{im}\beta
and
\mathfrak{b}^{\omega}:=\mathfrak{b}^{\bot_{\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}}}
. Let
x\in\mathfrak{t}_{0}
.

(i)

Both
\mathfrak{b}
and
\mathfrak{b}^{\omega}
are
C
-stable.

(ii)

[A,\rho(\mathfrak{b}^{\omega})]\subseteq\rho(\mathfrak{b}^{\omega})
.

(iii)

\rho(x)\in\mathfrak{sp}(\mathfrak{q},\omega_{\mathfrak{q}})
if and only if
x\in\mathfrak{b}^{\omega}
.

(iv)

\operatorname{tr}(\rho(x))=0
for every
x\in\mathfrak{b}^{\omega}
.

Proof.

.

(i)

Since
\operatorname{im}\beta
is contained in
\operatorname{im}(A^{*}\beta)
, Lemma
3.4
(iv) shows that
\mathfrak{b}
is
C
-stable. On the other hand, Lemma
3.4
(i) shows that
\mathfrak{b}^{\omega}
is
C
-stable.

(ii)

It follows from Lemma
3.4
(vi) and (i).

(iii)

It is immediate from Lemma
3.4
(viii).

(iv)

It follows from (iii), since every symplectic endomorphism is traceless. ∎

Lemma
3.4
(ix) can be rephrased as
(\rho(x)^{*}\beta)(u,v)=[x,\beta(u,v)]
. Were
\mathfrak{t}_{0}
a Lie algebra, this would imply that
\mathfrak{b}
is an ideal in
\mathfrak{t}_{0}
. This is usually not the case: as Lemma
3.4
(xiv) and (xv) show, in general neither
\alpha
nor
\gamma
are Lie brackets on
\mathfrak{t}_{0}
or
\mathfrak{q}
. While we cannot give a clean characterization for when
\gamma
is a Lie bracket, the situation for

is different.

Corollary 3.6
.

\alpha
is a Lie bracket on
\mathfrak{t}_{0}
if and only if either
\dim\mathfrak{t}_{0}=2
or
C=0
, the latter of which happens precisely when
\xi
is central in
\mathfrak{t}
.

Proof.

According to Lemma
3.4
(iv),
\alpha
is a Lie bracket on
\mathfrak{t}_{0}
if and only if the alternating trilinear map defined as
S(x,y,z):=\mathfrak{S}_{x,y,z}\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}(x,y)Cz
vanishes identically. If
\dim\mathfrak{t}_{0}=2
then this is automatic for dimensional reasons. If
\dim\mathfrak{t}_{0}\geq 4
then, for a fixed
x\in\mathfrak{t}_{0}
, the dimension of
x^{\bot_{\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}}}
is at least
3
and so there exist
y
,
z\in\mathfrak{t}_{0}
such that
\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}(x,y)=\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}(x,z)=0
and
\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}(y,z)=1
, from where it follows that the RHS is just
Cx
. The rest of the claim readily follows.
∎

The situation when
C=0
, and thus
\alpha
is a Lie bracket, is discussed in detail in Section
5
below. In there, contact Lie algebras for which
C
is the zero map are called
DS-contact Lie algebras
.

If we are careful to define
\rho(\xi):=A
then Lemma
3.4
(vii) becomes simply
[\rho(x),\rho(y)]=\rho([x,y])
for all
x
,
y\in\mathfrak{t}
. Together with Lemma
3.4
(vi), this shows that
\mathfrak{r}:=\rho(\mathfrak{t})\subseteq\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{q})
is a Lie subalgebra. The tautological action of
\mathfrak{r}
on
\mathfrak{q}
induces an action on
\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\bigwedge$}^{2}\mathfrak{q}^{*}
, hence on
\operatorname{Hom}(\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\bigwedge$}^{2}\mathfrak{q}^{*},\mathfrak{q})
. By Lemma
3.4
(v) and (x), the map
\gamma\in\operatorname{Hom}(\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\bigwedge$}^{2}\mathfrak{q}^{*},\mathfrak{q})
is
\mathfrak{r}
-equivariant. This severely constrains
\gamma
: in fact, for every
D\in\mathfrak{r}
, the fact that
D^{*}\gamma=D\gamma
gives

\displaystyle\gamma(E_{\lambda}(D),E_{\mu}(D))\subseteq E_{\lambda+\mu}(D)\text{ for all $\lambda\in\operatorname{spec}(D_{\mathbb{C}})$},

where
E_{\lambda}(D)
denotes the generalized eigenspace of the complexification
D_{\mathbb{C}}
of
D
with eigenvalue
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. In particular,
\gamma
is identically zero as soon as there exists
D\in\mathfrak{r}
such that

\displaystyle\operatorname{spec}(D_{\mathbb{C}})\cap(\operatorname{spec}(D_{\mathbb{C}})+\operatorname{spec}(D_{\mathbb{C}}))=\emptyset.

Since Lemma
3.4
(xii), (xiii), and (xv) constrain
\gamma
even further, one should expect
\gamma
to vanish in most cases. As the next example shows, this is not always true.

Example 3.7
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be the Lie algebra with basis
\{\xi,x,y,e_{1},e_{2},e_{3},e_{4}\}
and bracket given by

\displaystyle[\xi,e_{1}]=e_{1},\quad[\xi,e_{2}]=2e_{2},\quad[\xi,e_{3}]=-e_{3},\quad[\xi,e_{4}]=-2e_{4},

\displaystyle[x,e_{1}]=e_{1},\quad[x,e_{2}]=e_{2},[x,y]=\xi+y,

\displaystyle[y,e_{1}]=-e_{1},\quad[y,e_{2}]=-2e_{2},\quad[y,e_{3}]=e_{3},\quad y,e_{4}]=2e_{4},

\displaystyle[e_{1},e_{3}]=\xi+y,\quad[e_{2},e_{4}]=\xi+y,\quad[e_{3},e_{2}]=e_{1}.

Notice
\eta=\xi^{*}
is a contact form on
\mathfrak{g}
with Reeb vector
\xi
. It is straightforward to check that, for
\mathfrak{t}:=\operatorname{span}\{\xi,x,y\}
and
\mathfrak{q}:=\operatorname{span}\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3},e_{4}\}
, both
\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}|_{\mathfrak{t}}=0
and
A:=\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}|_{\mathfrak{q}}=\operatorname{diag}(1,2,-1,-2)
. Clearly,
\gamma(e_{e},e_{2})=e_{1}
, so
\gamma\neq 0
.

Along the same lines as the last paragraph’s remarks, Lemma
3.4
(iii) shows that
\alpha
is
C
-equivariant, and so it is somewhat restrained. However, we are most interested in the case when
C
is identically zero, not
\alpha
, as this is the main object of interest in Section

.

Apropos of the previous discussion, the next result can be established by elementary means.

Corollary 3.8
.

If
\dim\mathfrak{q}=2
then
\gamma=0
.

Proof.

If
\dim\mathfrak{q}=2
then
\dim\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\bigwedge$}^{2}\mathfrak{q}^{*}=1
, and therefore
\gamma
is just a single vector in
\mathfrak{q}
. From Lemma
3.4
(ii) and (v), we get
A\gamma=A^{*}\gamma=\operatorname{tr}(A)\gamma=0
, and hence
\gamma=0
since

is invertible.
∎

Corollary

generalizes the findings in
[

, where it is shown by direct computation that

when

and

is K-contact. We obtain a “dual”​ result for

.

Corollary 3.9
.

If
\beta=0
then
\dim\mathfrak{q}=2
.

Proof.

Notice that
\mathfrak{k}:=\mathbb{R}\xi\oplus\mathfrak{q}
is a Lie subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
because
\mathfrak{q}
is
\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}
invariant and
[\mathfrak{q},\mathfrak{q}]\subseteq\mathfrak{k}
since

. Moreover,
\lambda:=\eta|_{\mathfrak{k}}
is a contact form on
\mathfrak{k}
with Reeb vector

, since both

and

is invertible by definition of

, it follows that

. Proposition

gives

, and thus

.
∎

There are examples of contact Lie algebras with

and

and

Proof of the main result

Assume throughout this section that
\mathfrak{q}\neq 0
and
\dim\mathfrak{t}\geq 3
(which, following Proposition

, merely excludes the Lie algebras
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
and
\mathfrak{su}(2)
). Let
\ell\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
be the

\mathfrak{g}
defined by

\displaystyle\ell(\xi)=0,\quad\ell|_{\mathfrak{q}}=0,\quad\ell(x):=\frac{1}{\dim\mathfrak{q}}\operatorname{tr}(A^{-1}\rho(x))\quad\text{($x\in\mathfrak{t}_{0}$)},

(5)

and let
\sigma\in\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\bigwedge$}^{2}\mathfrak{t}^{*}
be the

\mathfrak{t}

\displaystyle\iota_{\xi}\sigma=0,\quad\sigma(x,y):=\frac{1}{\dim\mathfrak{q}}\operatorname{tr}(A^{-1}[\rho(x),\rho(y)])\quad\text{($x,y\in\mathfrak{t}_{0}$)}.

Proposition 4.1

The following identities hold.

(i)

\ell\circ C=0

(ii)

.

(i)

\displaystyle(\dim\mathfrak{q})\,\ell(Cx)=\operatorname{tr}(A^{-1}\rho(Cx))=\operatorname{tr}(A^{-1}[A,\rho(x)])=\operatorname{tr}(\rho(x)-A^{-1}\rho(x)A)=0.

x
,

\displaystyle(d_{\mathfrak{t}}\ell)(\xi,x)

\displaystyle=-\ell([\xi,x])=-\ell(Cx)=0\quad\text{and}\quad\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}(\xi,x)=\sigma(\xi,x)=0.\qed

\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}
is nondegenerate on
\mathfrak{t}_{0}

on
\mathfrak{t}_{0}

\displaystyle\operatorname{ad}_{e}=\begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{ad}_{e}|_{\mathfrak{t}}&0\\

\mathfrak{g}

\mathfrak{t}

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{ad}_{e})=\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{ad}_{e}|_{\mathfrak{t}})+\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{ad}_{e}|_{\mathfrak{q}}).

Our goal is to show that the first term is positive, and that the second is nonnegative.

Lemma 4.2
.

If

\iota_{z}\sigma=0

[\xi,e]=0

Proof.

According to Lemma

, and so

\displaystyle A^{-1}[\rho(z),\rho(x)]=[A^{-1}\rho(z),\rho(x)]\text{ for all $x\in\mathfrak{t}_{0}$}.

Since the trace of a commutator vanishes, we obtain

\displaystyle(\iota_{z}\sigma)(x)=\sigma(z,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\dim\mathfrak{q}}\operatorname{tr}([A^{-1}\rho(z),\rho(x)])=0\text{ for all $x\in\mathfrak{t}_{0}$}.

For the second part, notice that Proposition
4.1
(i) implies

\displaystyle 0=\ell(Cx)=-\omega(e,Cx)=\omega(Ce,x)\text{ for all $x\in\mathfrak{t}_{0}$},

thus the nondegeneracy of
\omega
forces
0=Ce=[e,\xi]
, hence
\iota_{e}\sigma=0
from the first part.
∎

Proposition 4.3
.

\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{ad}_{e}|_{\mathfrak{t}})=\frac{\dim\mathfrak{t}_{0}}{2}>0
. In particular,
\mathfrak{t}
is not unimodular.

Proof.

The first part of Lemma
4.2
implies that

\displaystyle\operatorname{ad}_{e}|_{\mathfrak{t}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&*\\
0&M\end{pmatrix},\quad M:=\mathrm{pr}_{\mathfrak{t}_{0}}\circ\operatorname{ad}_{e}|_{\mathfrak{t}_{0}}\in\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{t}_{0}),

where
\mathrm{pr}_{\mathfrak{t}_{0}}:\mathfrak{t}\to\mathfrak{t}_{0}
denotes the canonical linear projection with respect to the vector space splitting
\mathfrak{t}=\mathbb{R}\xi\oplus\mathfrak{t}_{0}
. In particular,
\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{ad}_{e}|_{\mathfrak{t}})=\operatorname{tr}(M)
. Since
\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}
is nondegenerate on
\mathfrak{t}_{0}
, there exists a unique endomorphism
N\in\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{t}_{0})
satisfying

\displaystyle\sigma(x,y)=\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}(Nx,y)\text{ for all $x$, $y\in\mathfrak{t}_{0}$}.

Notice that
N\in\mathfrak{sp}(\mathfrak{t}_{0},\omega_{\mathfrak{t}})
since
\sigma
is skew-symmetric. By Proposition
4.1
(ii), we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{e}\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}=d_{\mathfrak{t}}(\iota_{e}\omega_{\mathfrak{t}})=-d_{\mathfrak{t}}\ell=-\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}+\sigma,

which is to say

\displaystyle-\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}(Mx,y)-\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}(x,My)=-\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}(x,y)+\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}(Nx,y)\text{ for all $x$, $y\in\mathfrak{t}_{0}$}.

Equivalently,
M-\frac{1}{2}(\operatorname{Id}-N)\in\mathfrak{sp}(\mathfrak{t}_{0},\omega_{\mathfrak{t}})
. Therefore, both
N
and
M-\frac{1}{2}(\operatorname{Id}-N)
are symplectic, and therefore traceless. Hence,

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{ad}_{e}|_{\mathfrak{t}})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}(M)=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{Id}-N)=\frac{\dim\mathfrak{t}_{0}}{2}.\qed

Let
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}
be the complexification of
\mathfrak{g}
. Decompose
\mathfrak{q}_{\mathbb{C}}
into generalized eigenspaces
E_{\lambda}
of
A_{\mathbb{C}}
as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{q}_{\mathbb{C}}=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\operatorname{spec}(A_{\mathbb{C}})}E_{\lambda}.

Since
A
is invertible,
0\notin\operatorname{spec}(A_{\mathbb{C}})
. Set
\Omega_{\mathfrak{q}}:=-\ell\circ\beta
, viewed on
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}
. We omit the symbol
\mathbb{C}
to avoid overloading the notation.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\lambda
,
\mu\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
such that
\lambda+\mu\neq 0
.

(i)

\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(E_{\lambda},E_{\mu})=0
.

(ii)

\Omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(E_{\lambda},E_{\mu})=0
.

Proof.

Let
A=A_{s}+A_{n}
be the Jordan-Chevalley decomposition of

. Recall
A\in\mathfrak{sp}(\mathfrak{q},\omega_{\mathfrak{q}})
from Lemma
3.2
(i). Notice that the inclusion
\mathfrak{sp}(\mathfrak{q},\omega_{\mathfrak{q}})\hookrightarrow\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{q})
and the map

\displaystyle\pi:\mathfrak{sp}(\mathfrak{q},\omega_{\mathfrak{q}})\to\operatorname{End}(\operatorname{Hom}(\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\bigwedge$}^{2}\mathfrak{q},\mathfrak{t}_{0})),\quad(\pi(D)\theta)(x,y):=\theta(Dx,y)+\theta(x,Dy)

define Lie algebra representations of
\mathfrak{sp}(\mathfrak{q},\omega_{\mathfrak{q}})
. Combining Lemma
3.4
(ii) and Proposition
4.1
(i), we get from the definitions of
\Omega
and
\pi
that
\pi(A)\Omega=0
. Applying
[
23

to each representation, we obtain that
A_{s}\in\mathfrak{sp}(\mathfrak{q},\omega_{\mathfrak{q}})
and that
\pi(A_{s})\Omega=0
.

(i)

Since
A_{s}|_{E_{\lambda}}=\lambda\operatorname{Id}
, for
x\in E_{\lambda}
and
y\in E_{\mu}
we get

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(A_{s}x,y)=-\frac{1}{\lambda}\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(x,A_{s}y)=-\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(x,y).

(ii)

For
x\in E_{\lambda}
and
y\in E_{\mu}
, we get

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=(\pi(A_{s})\Omega_{\mathfrak{q}})(x,y)=\Omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(A_{s}x,y)+\Omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(x,A_{s}y)=(\lambda+\mu)\Omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(x,y).\qed

Let
\operatorname{spec}(A_{\mathbb{C}})_{+}
be the subset in
\operatorname{spec}(A_{\mathbb{C}})
of either positive real part or zero real part and positive imaginary part. For the next part, we work with a single
\lambda\in\operatorname{spec}(A_{\mathbb{C}})_{+}
at a time. Since
\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}
is nondegenerate on
\mathfrak{q}
, it induces a nondegenerate pairing
\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}:E_{\lambda}\times E_{-\lambda}\to\mathbb{C}
for each
\lambda\in\operatorname{spec}(A_{\mathbb{C}})
.

Remark 4.5
.

The nondegeneracy of
\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}
on each
E_{\lambda}\times E_{-\lambda}
implies that
\dim E_{\lambda}=\dim E_{-\lambda}
for all
\lambda\in\operatorname{spec}(A_{\mathbb{C}})
. Let’s call
r_{\lambda}
that common dimension. Denote by
\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\bigwedge$}^{2}A:\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\bigwedge$}^{2}\mathfrak{q}^{*}\to\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\bigwedge$}^{2}\mathfrak{q}^{*}
the map given by
(\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\bigwedge$}^{2}A)(u,v):=Au\wedge v+u\wedge Av
. The semisimple part of the complexification of
\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\bigwedge$}^{2}A
acts on
E_{\lambda}\wedge E_{\mu}
by
\lambda+\mu
, and so
\ker\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\bigwedge$}^{2}A
lies on
\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\operatorname{spec}(A_{\mathbb{C}})_{+}}E_{\lambda}\wedge E_{-\lambda}
. In particular,

\displaystyle\dim\ker(\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\bigwedge$}^{2}A)\leq\sum_{\lambda\in\operatorname{spec}(A_{\mathbb{C}})}r_{\lambda}^{2}\leq\left(\frac{\dim\mathfrak{q}}{2}\right)^{2}.

The nondegeneracy of
\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}
on
E_{\lambda}\times E_{-\lambda}
implies that there exists a unique
T_{\lambda}\in E_{\lambda}
such that

\displaystyle\omega(e,\beta(u,v))=\Omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(u,v)=\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(T_{\lambda}u,v)\text{ for all $u\in E_{\lambda}$ and $v\in E_{-\lambda}$}.

(8)

Combining Lemma
3.4
(vi) and Lemma
4.2
, we see that
A
and
\rho(e)=\operatorname{ad}_{e}|_{\mathfrak{q}}
commute; in particular,
\operatorname{ad}_{e}
preserves the generalized eigenspaces of
A
. Hence, the operators

\displaystyle P_{\lambda}:=\operatorname{ad}_{e}|_{E_{\lambda}}\in\operatorname{End}(E_{\lambda}),\quad Q_{\lambda}:=\operatorname{ad}_{e}|_{E_{-\lambda}}\in\operatorname{End}(E_{-\lambda})

are well-defined. Denote by
Q_{\lambda}^{\dagger}\in\operatorname{End}(E_{-\lambda})
the
\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}
-adjoint of
Q_{\lambda}
.

Lemma 4.6
.

.

(i)

T_{\lambda}=P_{\lambda}+Q_{\lambda}^{\dagger}
.

(ii)

T_{\lambda}=T_{\lambda}P_{\lambda}+Q_{\lambda}^{\dagger}T_{\lambda}
.

Proof.

.

(i)

Take
u\in E_{\lambda}
and
v\in E_{-\lambda}
. Recall that
d_{\mathfrak{g}}\omega=0
and
\iota_{\xi}\omega=0
, that
\omega(\gamma(u,v),e)=0
due to Lemma
3.2
(iii), and that
\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(T_{\lambda}u,v)=\omega(e,\beta(u,v))
as observed in equation (
8
). Then,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=-(d_{\mathfrak{g}}\omega)(e,u,v)=\omega([e,u],v)+\omega(u,[e,v])+\omega([u,v],e)

\displaystyle=\omega(P_{\lambda}u,v)+\omega(u,Q_{\lambda}v)+\omega(\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(u,v)\xi+\beta(u,v)+\gamma(u,v),e)

\displaystyle=\omega(P_{\lambda}u,v)+\omega(Q_{\lambda}^{\dagger}u,v)-\omega(e,\beta(u,v))=\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(P_{\lambda}u,v)+\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(Q_{\lambda}^{\dagger}u,v)-\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(T_{\lambda}u,v).

The claim follows from the nondegeneracy of
\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}
on
E_{\lambda}\times E_{-\lambda}
.

(ii)

Take
u\in E_{\lambda}
and
v\in E_{-\lambda}
. Combining Lemma
3.4
(ix) and Lemma
4.2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\beta(P_{\lambda}u,v)+\beta(u,Q_{\lambda}v)=\alpha(e,\beta(u,v)).

Now apply
-\ell
to both sides. The resulting expression can be simplified with the definition of
\Omega_{\mathfrak{q}}
, equation (
8
), Proposition
4.1
(i), and Lemma
4.2
again:

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(T_{\lambda}P_{\lambda}u,v)+\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(Q_{\lambda}^{\dagger}T_{\lambda}u,v)

\displaystyle=\Omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(P_{\lambda}u,v)+\Omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(u,Q_{\lambda}v)=-\ell([e,\beta(u,v)]_{\mathfrak{t}_{0}})=(d_{\mathfrak{t}}\ell)(e,\beta(u,v))

\displaystyle=\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}(e,\beta(u,v))-\sigma(e,\beta(u,v))=\Omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(u,v)=\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(T_{\lambda}u,v).

The claim follows from the nondegeneracy of
\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}
on

. ∎

Lemma

gives valuable information about the spectrum of

.

Proposition 4.7
.

Both
\operatorname{spec}(T_{\lambda})\subseteq\{0,1\}
and
\operatorname{tr}(T_{\lambda})\geq 0
.

Proof.

Substitute the formula in Lemma
4.6
(i) into the formula in Lemma
4.6
(ii) to obtain

\displaystyle T_{\lambda}=T_{\lambda}P_{\lambda}+(T_{\lambda}-P_{\lambda})T_{\lambda}=T_{\lambda}^{2}+[T_{\lambda},P_{\lambda}].

Let
\mu\in\operatorname{spec}(T_{\lambda})
be an eigenvalue of
T_{\lambda}
with generalized eigenspace
W_{\mu}
and spectral projector
\Pi_{\mu}
. Define
T_{\lambda,\mu}:=T_{\lambda}|_{W_{\mu}}
and
P_{\lambda,\mu}:=\Pi_{\mu}P_{\lambda}|_{W_{\mu}}
. Since
\Pi_{\mu}
and

commute, we get
T_{\lambda,\mu}-T_{\lambda,\mu}^{2}=[T_{\lambda,\mu},P_{\lambda,\mu}]
, hence
\operatorname{tr}(T_{\lambda,\mu})=\operatorname{tr}(T_{\lambda,\mu}^{2})
. Since

with

\displaystyle(\dim W_{\mu})\mu=\operatorname{tr}(T_{\lambda,\mu})=\operatorname{tr}(T_{\lambda,\mu}^{2})=(\dim W_{\mu})\mu^{2},

and so

.

Just as
\mathfrak{q}_{\mathbb{C}}
is decomposed as a direct sum of pairs of generalized eigenspaces

, where

, the operator

can be decomposed into a direct sum of blocks of the form

, again with

. Recall that the trace of an operator is preserved under complexification, as well as

.

Proposition 4.8
.

\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{ad}_{e}|_{\mathfrak{q}})\geq 0
.

Proof.

Following Proposition
4.7
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{ad}_{e}|_{\mathfrak{q}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\operatorname{spec}(A_{\mathbb{C}})_{+}}\operatorname{tr}(P_{\lambda})+\operatorname{tr}(Q_{\lambda})=\sum_{\operatorname{spec}(A_{\mathbb{C}})_{+}}\operatorname{tr}(P_{\lambda})+\operatorname{tr}(Q_{\lambda}^{\dagger})=\sum_{\operatorname{spec}(A_{\mathbb{C}})_{+}}\operatorname{tr}(T_{\lambda})\geq 0.\qed

Our main result follows by tying everything up.

Theorem 4.9
.

If
\mathfrak{t}_{0}\neq 0
and
\mathfrak{q}\neq 0
then
\mathfrak{g}
is not transversely unimodular.

Proof.

Regarding equation (
7
), the first term is positive by Proposition
4.3
and the second term is nonnegative by Proposition
4.8
. Thus,
\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{ad}_{e})>0
. Since Lemma
4.2
gives
[\xi,e]=0
, we get
e\in\ker(\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}|_{\mathfrak{h}})
and thus

\displaystyle\operatorname{ad}_{e}=\begin{pmatrix}0&*\\
0&M_{e}\end{pmatrix},\quad M_{e}:=\mathfrak{pr}_{\mathfrak{h}}\circ\operatorname{ad}_{e}|_{\mathfrak{h}}\in\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{h})

with respect to the vector space splitting
\mathfrak{g}=\mathbb{R}\xi\oplus\mathfrak{h}
. Therefore,
\operatorname{tr}(M_{e})=\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{ad}_{e})>0
.
∎

By Proposition
3.1
, the condition
\mathfrak{t}_{0}\neq 0
only excludes the contact Lie algebras
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
and
\mathfrak{su}(2)
. On the other hand, the condition
\operatorname{im}(K_{s})=\mathfrak{q}\neq 0
means that

has nonzero semisimple part. Thus, Theorem
4.9
is equivalent to the fact that transversely unimodular contact Lie algebras not isomorphic to
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
or
\mathfrak{su}(2)
must have nilpotent
\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}
. From both assertions we get the claim in Theorem
1.1
. Conversely, it is not difficult to find unimodular contact Lie algebras with nilpotent
\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}
, as the next example shows.

Example 4.10
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be the Lie algebra with basis
\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3},e_{4},e_{5}\}
and bracket given by

\displaystyle[e_{1},e_{4}]=e_{1},\quad[e_{3},e_{4}]=-e_{3},\quad[e_{2},e_{5}]=e_{2},\quad[e_{3},e_{5}]=-e_{3}.

Here,
\eta:=e^{1}+e^{2}+e^{3}
is the contact form and
\xi:=\frac{1}{3}(e_{1}+e_{2}+e_{3})
. A direct computation shows
\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}^{2}=0
. As pointed out in
[
20
, Proposition 4.4]
, it is solvable and the associated simply connected Lie group has lattices.

As already discussed in Section
2.3
, Theorem
4.9
entails that any contact Lie algebra with both
\mathfrak{t}_{0}\neq 0
and
\mathfrak{q}\neq 0
fails to be
0
-Lefschetz in both the sense of
[
13
]
and the sense of
[
22
]
.

5.
DS-contact Lie algebras

A
DS-contact
Lie algebra is a contact Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},\eta)
for which the following vector-space decomposition holds:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}=\ker\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}\oplus\operatorname{im}\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}.

(9)

The “DS”​ bit stands for “direct sum”​. The validity of such a decomposition is just a constraint on the Jordan form of
\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}
. There are multiple ways to express the DS-condition, namely:

•

\ker\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}\cap\operatorname{im}\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}=\{0\}
.

•

\ker\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}^{2}=\ker\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}
.

•

K_{n}|_{\mathfrak{t}}=0
.

•

C=0
. That is,
\xi
is central in
\mathfrak{t}_{0}
.

•

The characteristic polynomial of
\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}
is divisible by
x
but not by
x^{2}
.

As observed in the first lines in Section
3
, both summands in the vector space decomposition
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{t}\oplus\mathfrak{q}
in equation (
4
) are
K
-invariant, the equality
K|_{\mathfrak{t}}=K_{n}|_{\mathfrak{t}}
holds, and
K|_{\mathfrak{q}}
is invertible. In particular,
\operatorname{im}K=\operatorname{im}K_{s}\oplus\operatorname{im}K_{n}|_{\mathfrak{t}}
and
\mathfrak{q}=\operatorname{im}\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}
if and only if
K_{n}|_{\mathfrak{t}}=0
. Thus, the decomposition in equation (
9
) is just

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}=\ker\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}\oplus\operatorname{im}\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}=\mathfrak{t}\oplus\mathfrak{q},

without any clash of notation whatsoever.

Notice that K-contact and Sasakian Lie algebras are DS-contact, as noted in the discussion leading up to equation (
3
). Certainly, there are strict DS-contact Lie algebras; that is, ones not admitting compatible K-contact metrics: as seen from Proposition
5.10
below, the Lie algebra labeled
\mathfrak{g}_{1,-}
is strict DS-contact. We emphasize once more that, in contrast with the K-contact case, the decomposition in equation (
9
) is not assumed to be neither
g
-orthogonal nor
\Phi
-invariant for any compatible metric
g
or
(1,1)
tensor
\Phi
, although it still is
\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}
-invariant.

It is apparent from the definition that, besides
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
and
\mathfrak{su}(2)
, the only DS-contact Lie algebras having nilpotent
\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}
are those with
\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}=0
, thus having a nontrivial center. In combination with Theorem
1.1
, it follows that centerless unimodular DS-contact Lie algebras are either
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
or
\mathfrak{su}(2)
. Since K-contact Lie algebras are DS-contact, we obtain a generalization of Corollary
1.2
.

5.1.
Elementary properties of DS-contact Lie algebras

The main motivation behind the introduction of DS-contact Lie algebras is the observation that a number of known properties of Sasakian Lie algebras are consequences of equation (
9
) alone, not the compatible metric structure nor its good behavior. We see this already with the discussion in the previous paragraph, itself a partial generalization of
[
1
, Theorem 2.1]
, and with Proposition
1.5
, which generalizes
[
6
, Proposition 8.1]
. Something similar happens with the proof of the next result.

Lemma 5.1
.

Both
[\mathfrak{t},\mathfrak{t}]\subseteq\mathfrak{t}
and
[\mathfrak{t},\mathfrak{q}]\subseteq\mathfrak{q}
hold.

Proof.

While both claims follow from Lemma
3.3
, we point out that now the proof of

in
[
5
, Proposition 8 (2)]
also holds in this setting. We reproduce it here: Take

and

, where

for some

. The Jacobi identity on the triple

and the fact that

ensure that

\displaystyle[x,y]_{\mathfrak{g}}

\displaystyle=[x,[\xi,z]_{\mathfrak{g}}]_{\mathfrak{g}}=[[x,\xi]_{\mathfrak{g}},z]_{\mathfrak{g}}+[\xi,[x,z]_{\mathfrak{g}}]_{\mathfrak{g}}=[\xi,[x,z]_{\mathfrak{g}}]_{\mathfrak{g}}=T([x,z]_{\mathfrak{g}})\in\mathfrak{q}.\qed

Just as in Lemma
3.2
,
\mathfrak{t}_{0}
and
\mathfrak{q}
are
\omega
-orthogonal, from where it follows that the restrictions
\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}
and
\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}
are symplectic on
\mathfrak{t}_{0}
and
\mathfrak{q}
. In particular,
(\mathfrak{t},\eta_{\mathfrak{t}})
is a contact Lie subalgebra of
(\mathfrak{g},\eta)
; moreover, since

, it also has nontrivial center. According to Proposition

,

is the contactization of

, and thus

is a Lie bracket on

. Notice that if further

is K-contact or Sasakian, so is

. This is observed in
[
5
, Proposition 8]
.

The following properties all follow from Lemma
3.4
together with the fact that

is the zero map. For the other identities in the same result we obtain no obvious simplifications in the DS-contact setting.

Lemma 5.2
.

The following identities hold in DS-contact Lie algebras, where

that
\mathfrak{b}:=\operatorname{im}\beta

.

\mathfrak{b}

.

(iv)

\mathfrak{k}

\mathfrak{b}
is an ideal in
\mathfrak{t}_{0}

5.2.
On Frobenius Lie algebras

A Frobenius Lie algebra is just a Lie algebra equipped with an exact symplectic form. It is known that Frobenius Lie algebras are nonunimodular (see

It turns out that the

. Recall

to

Theorem 5.4
.

(\mathfrak{t}_{0},\omega_{\mathfrak{t}})

\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}=d_{\mathfrak{t}_{0}}\ell

Proof.

Proposition

(ii) shows

on

∎

The other half of

Corollary 5.5
.

Neither
\mathfrak{t}_{0}
nor
\mathfrak{t}

\mathfrak{t}_{0}

Theorem

comes with an associated

: which Frobenius Lie algebras can be realized as the

-part of some DS-contact Lie algebra? Corollary

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{a},\omega_{\mathfrak{a}}):=\{d\in 2\mathbb{N}\,|\,(\mathfrak{a},\omega_{\mathfrak{a}})\text{ is realizable with $\dim\mathfrak{q}=d$}\}.

We omit the clarification that the realization is “as the
\mathfrak{t}_{0}
-part of a DS-contact Lie algebra” to save space. We start with two positive results.

Proposition 5.6
.

2\in\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{a},\omega_{\mathfrak{a}})
for any Frobenius Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{a},\omega_{\mathfrak{a}})
.

Proof.

Pick
\alpha\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}
such that
d_{\mathfrak{a}}\alpha=\omega_{\mathfrak{a}}
, a
2
-dimensional vector space
(\mathfrak{q},\omega_{\mathfrak{q}})
, and any invertible
A\in\mathfrak{sp}(\mathfrak{q},\omega_{\mathfrak{q}})
. On the vector space
\mathfrak{g}
given by
\mathbb{R}\xi\oplus\mathfrak{a}\oplus\mathfrak{q}
, let

\displaystyle[\xi,\mathfrak{a}]_{\mathfrak{g}}=0,\quad[\xi,u]_{\mathfrak{g}}=Au,\quad[\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{q}]_{\mathfrak{g}}=0,\quad[x,y]_{\mathfrak{g}}=[x,y]_{\mathfrak{a}},\quad[u,v]_{\mathfrak{g}}=\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(u,v)\,\xi.

define a skew-symmetric bilinear product on
\mathfrak{g}
. A straightforward computation shows that

is indeed a Lie bracket, a fact that can be read off from Lemma
3.4
and Lemma

holds trivially because

by

∎

It is not hard to see that

Proposition 5.7

\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{a},\omega_{\mathfrak{a}})=2\mathbb{N}
for any Frobenius Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{a},\omega_{\mathfrak{a}})
with a one-dimensional ideal.

Proof.

Let

\alpha\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}
such that

, which is readily shown to be closed. The nondegeneracy of
\omega_{\mathfrak{a}}
implies that
\alpha(z)\neq 0

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathfrak{a}}(x,z)=d_{\mathfrak{a}}\alpha(x,z)=-\alpha([x,z]_{\mathfrak{a}})=-\chi(x)\alpha(z).

We may assume
\alpha(z)=-1
, replacing

by a nonzero scalar multiple if necessary. Pick a symplectic vector space

of dimension
2m
, and let

be invertible. Define

\displaystyle\rho:\mathfrak{a}\to\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{q}),\quad\rho(x):=\frac{\chi(x)}{2}\operatorname{Id}+\alpha(x)A.

On the vector space
\mathfrak{g}
given by
\mathbb{R}\xi\oplus\mathfrak{a}\oplus\mathfrak{q}
, let

\displaystyle[\xi,\mathfrak{a}]_{\mathfrak{g}}=0,\quad[\xi,u]=Au,\quad[x,u]_{\mathfrak{g}}=\rho(x)u,

\displaystyle[x,y]_{\mathfrak{g}}=[x,y]_{\mathfrak{a}}+\omega_{\mathfrak{a}}(x,y)\xi,\quad[u,v]_{\mathfrak{g}}=\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(u,v)(\xi+z)

define a skew-symmetric bilinear product on

; here
x
,
y\in\mathfrak{a}
and
u
,

. We now verify that Jacobi identity holds:

•

Since
\mathbb{R}\xi\oplus\mathfrak{a}
is just the contactization of
(\mathfrak{a},\omega_{\mathfrak{a}})
, Jacobi holds for the triples on
\mathbb{R}\xi\oplus\mathfrak{a}
.

•

For
(x,y,u)
with
x
,
y\in\mathfrak{a}
and

, we have

\displaystyle\rho([x,y]_{\mathfrak{a}})+\omega_{\mathfrak{a}}(x,y)A=\frac{\chi([x,y]_{\mathfrak{a}})}{2}\operatorname{Id}+\alpha([x,y]_{\mathfrak{a}})A+\omega_{\mathfrak{a}}(x,y)A=0=[\rho(x),\rho(y)].

This follows from the following facts:
\chi
is a character,

,

and

•

For

and

\displaystyle[x,[u,v]]_{\mathfrak{g}}

\displaystyle=\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(u,v)[x,\xi+z]_{\mathfrak{g}}=\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(u,v)\chi(x)(\xi+z)

\displaystyle=(\rho(x)^{*}\omega_{\mathfrak{q}})(u,v)(\xi+z)=[\rho(x)u,v]_{\mathfrak{g}}+[u,\rho(x)v]_{\mathfrak{g}}.

•

Since

, Jacobi holds for

with

,

.

•

For
(u,v,w)
with

,

\displaystyle[u,\xi+z]_{\mathfrak{g}}=-Au-\rho(z)u=-Au+Au=0,

Let

be dual to

The combination of Corollary

Corollary 5.8

Proof.

Every realization of a given Frobenius Lie algebra

We illustrate Corollary

Let

and let

be the row vector

. Define

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=-\omega_{\mathfrak{a}_{n}}((X,u),(Y,v))=\lambda([(X,u),(Y,v)])

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}(T[X,Y])+b(Xv-Yu)=\operatorname{tr}(([T,X]-ub)Y)+(bX)(v).

Hence,

and

. The diagonal of

is zero, whereas the diagonal of

. Thus

, and therefore

. Since

must be diagonal. The fact that

.

(\mathfrak{a}_{n},\omega_{\mathfrak{a}_{n}})

5.3.
A classification result in dimension five

As remarked below Proposition
2.4
, contact Lie algebras with nontrivial center are in bijective correspondence with symplectic Lie algebras, via
1
-dimensional central extension by a symplectic cocycle. Thus, in view of a classification of DS-contact Lie algebras, it is natural to split the problem according to whether the center is trivial or not. In dimension

4
-dimensional symplectic Lie algebras, which is known by
[
31
]
. Hence only the centerless case is relevant here.

Proposition 5.10
.

A centerless
5
-dimensional DS-contact Lie algebra is isomorphic to exactly one of the following Lie algebras:

(i)

The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}_{0,+}
with basis
\{\xi,x,y,u,v\}
and nonzero brackets given by

\displaystyle[x,y]=\xi+y,\quad[u,v]=\xi,\quad[\xi,u]=v,\quad[\xi,v]=-u,

\displaystyle[y,u]=-v,\quad[y,v]=u.

(ii)

The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}_{1,+}
with basis
\{\xi,x,y,u,v\}
and nonzero brackets given by

\displaystyle[x,y]=\xi+y,\quad[u,v]=\xi,\quad[\xi,u]=u,\quad[\xi,v]=-v,

\displaystyle[y,u]=-u,\quad[y,v]=v.

(iii)

The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}_{0,-}
with basis
\{\xi,x,y,u,v\}
and nonzero brackets given by

\displaystyle[x,y]=\xi+y,\quad[u,v]=\xi+y,\quad[\xi,u]=v,\quad[\xi,v]=-u,

\displaystyle[x,u]=\tfrac{1}{2}u,\quad[x,v]=\tfrac{1}{2}v,\quad[y,u]=-v,\quad[y,v]=u.

The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}_{1,-}

\displaystyle[x,y]=\xi+y,\quad[u,v]=\xi+y,\quad[\xi,u]=u,\quad[\xi,v]=-v,

One has

commutes with
\xi
,
u
, and
v
, one readily checks that
\mathfrak{g}
splits as a direct sum of ideals
\operatorname{span}\{x,z\}
and
\operatorname{span}\{\xi,u,v\}
. Moreover, as discussed during the proof,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}_{0,+}\cong\mathfrak{aff}(\mathbb{R})\oplus\mathfrak{su}(2),\quad\mathfrak{g}_{1,+}\cong\mathfrak{aff}(\mathbb{R})\oplus\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R}).

Likewise, for
\mathfrak{g}_{0,-}
, the basis

\displaystyle e_{1}:=x,\quad e_{2}:=\xi,\quad e_{3}:=-(\xi+y),\quad e_{4}:=u,\quad e_{5}:=v

yields the nonzero brackets

\displaystyle[e_{1},e_{3}]=e_{3},\quad[e_{1},e_{4}]=\tfrac{1}{2}e_{4},\quad[e_{1},e_{5}]=\tfrac{1}{2}e_{5},

\displaystyle[e_{2},e_{4}]=e_{5},\quad[e_{2},e_{5}]=-e_{4},\quad[e_{4},e_{5}]=-e_{3},

so
\mathfrak{g}_{0,-}
is precisely the Lie algebra denoted by
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
in both
[
5
, Theorem 13]
and
[
14
, Theorem 4.7]
. It follows from those classifications that
\mathfrak{g}_{0,+}
,
\mathfrak{g}_{1,+}
, and
\mathfrak{g}_{0,-}
admit compatible Sasakian structures, whereas
\mathfrak{g}_{1,-}
does not. Thus
\mathfrak{g}_{1,-}
is the only strict example in the list (i.e., not admitting K-compact compatible metrics).

Notice that both
\mathfrak{g}_{0,-}
and
\mathfrak{g}_{1,-}
are solvable (indeed, the commutator ideal for both of them is
\operatorname{span}\{\xi+y\}
), while neither
\mathfrak{g}_{0,+}
nor
\mathfrak{g}_{1,+}
are solvable (their derived series stabilizes at
\operatorname{span}\{\xi,u,v\}
). Both
\mathfrak{g}_{0,-}
and
\mathfrak{g}_{1,-}
appear on the list given in
[
18
, Section 4.2]
, being isomorphic to the Lie algebras 22 and 21, respectively.

It seems plausible that similar arguments could be used to classify DS-contact Lie algebras in higher dimensions, provided classifications of lower-dimensional exact and nonexact symplectic Lie algebras are available. It is less clear whether such a classification would be worth pursuing, since the computations appear to grow quickly out of hand.

For the

The map

\mathfrak{g}
, via
\ker F_{\ell}=Z^{1}_{CE}(\mathfrak{g})
. To see this set
\varphi\in\mathfrak{t}^{*}
to be
\varphi=\eta+\ell
, which is closed on

due to Lemma

.

Lemma 5.11
.

A
1
-form
\lambda\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
is closed if and only if
\lambda|_{\mathfrak{q}}=0
and
\lambda|_{\mathfrak{t}}=c\varphi+\sigma
for some
c\in\mathbb{R}
and
\sigma\in Z^{1}_{CE}(\mathfrak{t}_{0})
satisfying
F_{\ell}(c,[\sigma])=0
.

Proof.

Every closed
1
-form vanishes on
\mathfrak{q}=\operatorname{im}(\operatorname{ad}_{\xi})\subseteq[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]
. Write
\lambda|_{\mathfrak{t}}=c\eta|_{\mathfrak{t}}+\tau
for some
\tau\in\mathfrak{t}_{0}^{*}
, and note that

\displaystyle d_{\mathfrak{t}}(\lambda|_{\mathfrak{t}})=-c\omega_{\mathfrak{t}}+d_{\mathfrak{t}_{0}}\tau=cd_{\mathfrak{t}_{0}}(\ell+\tau)

Thus
\lambda|_{\mathfrak{t}}
is closed on
\mathfrak{t}
if and only if there is
\sigma\in Z^{1}_{CE}(\mathfrak{t}_{0})
for which
\tau=c\ell+\sigma
. The remaining conditions on
c
and
\sigma
come from the fact that
[\mathfrak{t},\mathfrak{q}]\subseteq\mathfrak{q}
, since for all
u
,
v\in\mathfrak{q}
we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\lambda([u,v])=c\omega_{\mathfrak{q}}(u,v)+c\ell(\beta(u,v))+(\sigma\circ\beta)(u,v).\qed

The next is a refined version of Lemma
2.16
. Set
b_{1}(\mathfrak{g}):=\dim H^{1}_{CE}(\mathfrak{g})

Proposition 5.12
.

The following conditions are equivalent on any DS-contact Lie algebra:

(i)

\xi\in[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]
.

(ii)

\delta_{\ell}\notin\operatorname{im}(\beta^{*})
.

(iii)

b_{1}(\mathfrak{g})=\dim\ker(\beta^{*})
.

Proof.

.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii): If there exists
\sigma\in Z^{1}_{CE}(\mathfrak{t}_{0})
such that
\beta^{*}[\sigma]=-\delta_{\ell}
then
(1,[\sigma])\in\ker F_{\ell}
, which by Lemma
5.11
gives a closed
1
-form vanishing whose value on
\xi
is
1
, contradicting
\xi\in[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]
.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii): If
(c,[\sigma])\in\ker F_{\ell}
then
c\delta_{\ell}\in\operatorname{im}(\beta^{*})
. By (ii), this forces
c=0
. Thus
\ker F_{\ell}=\ker(\beta^{*})
, and so
b_{1}(\mathfrak{g})=\dim\ker(\beta^{*})
.

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(i): If
\xi\notin[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]
, there exists a closed
1
-form
\lambda
with
\lambda(\xi)=1
. By Lemma
5.11
, this gives an element
(1,[\sigma_{0}])\in\ker F_{\ell}
, and hence we get the identification

\displaystyle\ker F_{\ell}=\ker(\beta^{*})\oplus\mathbb{R}(1,[\sigma_{0}]),

implying
b_{1}(\mathfrak{g})=\dim\ker(\beta^{*})+1
. ∎

The fact that
