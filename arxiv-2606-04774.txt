Weighted centro-affine Poincaré inequalities
Yingxiang Hu, Mohammad N. Ivaki

Abstract
. We obtain weighted centro-affine Bochner formulas on spherical caps associated with smooth strictly convex hypersurfaces. As a consequence, we prove weighted Poincaré inequalities on caps and on intersections of caps for a class of weights depending on the position vector X X of the hypersurface. In the unconditional case, we obtain a centro-affine Poincaré inequality with weight | X | 2 |X|^{2} , which is used to prove a Brunn–Minkowski inequality for the ( n + 2 ) (n+2) -nd dual quermassintegral. We also establish an L 0 L_{0} -Brunn–Minkowski inequality for the q q -th dual quermassintegral for q ∈ ( 0 , n ) q\in(0,n) , with equality only for dilates, and an L p L_{p} -Brunn–Minkowski inequality for q = n + α q=n+\alpha whenever 0 < α ≤ 2 ​ p ​ ( 1 − p ) 2 − p , 0<\alpha\leq\frac{2p(1-p)}{2-p}, which in particular covers the range q ∈ ( n , n + 6 − 4 2 ] q\in(n,n+6-4\sqrt{2}] for suitable p ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) p\in(0,1) . These Brunn–Minkowski inequalities imply weighted centro-affine Poincaré inequalities and uniqueness results for the L p , q L_{p,q} -Minkowski problem in the unconditional class. Our main contribution is the introduction of a flat logarithmic centro-affine geometry on the positive orthant ( 0 , ∞ ) n (0,\infty)^{n} , adapted to the multiplicative structure of the L 0 L_{0} -sum. In this geometry, a Bochner formula yields a sharp Poincaré inequality, as well as a new proof of the centro-affine Poincaré inequality with constant n n due to Kolesnikov–Milman, for unconditional bodies and unconditional functions.



1.
Introduction

Throughout the paper we assume
n\geq 2
. A convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a compact convex set with nonempty interior. Let
k\geq 2
. A convex body
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be
C^{k}_{+}
if its boundary is
C^{k}
-smooth and it has positive Gauss curvature. We always assume that
K
contains the origin in its interior. Let
h=h_{K}
denote the support function of
K
, and define the inverse Gauss map of
K
by

(1.1)

\displaystyle X(x)=Dh(x)=\bar{\nabla}h(x)+h(x)x,\quad x\in S^{n-1},

where
D
denotes the Euclidean gradient in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
S^{n-1}
is the unit sphere equipped with its standard induced round metric
\bar{g}
and Levi-Civita connection
\bar{\nabla}
.

For a smooth function
f
on
S^{n-1}
, we write
\bar{\nabla}f
and
\bar{\nabla}^{2}f
for the corresponding gradient and Hessian with respect to
\bar{g}
. The rescaled cone-volume measure of
K
is defined as

(1.2)

\displaystyle dV_{K}=h\det(\bar{\nabla}^{2}h+hI)\,dx,

where
dx
denotes the standard measure on
S^{n-1}
. The centro-affine metric of
K
on
S^{n-1}
is defined by

(1.3)

\displaystyle g=\frac{1}{h}(\bar{\nabla}^{2}h+hI).

We write
\nabla
for the torsion-free centro-affine connection.

For
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
, define
\ell_{w}=\langle X,w\rangle
. One of our main results is the following weighted centro-affine Poincaré inequality on intersections of caps.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
K
be a
C^{2}_{+}
convex body. Assume that
\omega\in C([0,\infty)^{m})\cap C^{2}((0,\infty)^{m})
and
\omega|_{(0,\infty)^{m}}>0
. Let

(1.4)

\displaystyle\Phi(x)=-\log\omega(\ell_{w_{1}}(x),\dots,\ell_{w_{m}}(x)),\quad d\mu_{\Phi}=e^{-\Phi}\,dV_{K}=\omega(\ell_{w_{1}},\dots,\ell_{w_{m}})\,dV_{K}.

Suppose for some constants
\alpha>0
and
\beta\geq 0
, and for all
t=(t_{1},\dots,t_{m})\in(0,\infty)^{m}

(1.5)

\displaystyle D^{2}\omega^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(t)\leq 0

as a symmetric
m\times m
matrix, and

(1.6)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}t_{i}\partial_{i}\log\omega(t)\geq\beta.

Then for every
F\in C^{1}(S^{n-1})
,

(1.7)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\alpha,\beta}\int_{\Omega}(F-\bar{F}_{\Phi})^{2}\,d\mu_{\Phi}\leq\int_{\Omega}|\nabla F|_{g}^{2}\,d\mu_{\Phi},

where

(1.8)

\displaystyle\Omega=\bigcap_{i=1}^{m}\{\ell_{w_{i}}>0\}\neq\emptyset,\quad\bar{F}_{\Phi}=\frac{\int_{\Omega}F\,d\mu_{\Phi}}{\int_{\Omega}d\mu_{\Phi}},\quad\lambda_{\alpha,\beta}=\frac{n-1+\alpha}{n-2+\alpha}(n-2+\beta).

In the dual Brunn–Minkowski theory, the
q
-th dual quermassintegral is defined by

(1.9)

\displaystyle\widetilde{V}_{q}(K)=\frac{q}{n}\int_{K}|x|^{q-n}\,dx.

As a corollary of recent analytic developments, Sadovsky and Zhang
[
SZ25
]
, building on results of Kolesnikov–Milman
[
KM22
]
, Kolesnikov–Livshyts
[
KL21
]
, and a key ingredient due to Cordero-Erausquin and Rotem
[
CER23
]
, established a Brunn–Minkowski inequality for
\widetilde{V}_{q}
in the range
1<q<n
for origin-symmetric convex bodies. The case
q=n
reduces to the classical Brunn–Minkowski inequality. For related results in the range
q\leq 1
, see
[
XZ22
]
.

Theorem
.

Let
q\in(1,n)
, and let
K,L\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be origin-symmetric convex bodies. Then

(1.10)

\displaystyle\widetilde{V}_{q}(K+L)^{\frac{1}{q}}\geq\widetilde{V}_{q}(K)^{\frac{1}{q}}+\widetilde{V}_{q}(L)^{\frac{1}{q}}.

A function
F:S^{n-1}\to\mathbb{R}
is called unconditional if it is invariant under all coordinate reflections. A convex body
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called unconditional if its support function
h_{K}
is unconditional.

Our cap inequalities have several consequences in symmetric settings. In particular, in the unconditional class we obtain a weighted centro-affine Poincaré inequality with the weight
|X|^{2}
, and use it to prove the following Brunn–Minkowski inequality.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
K,L\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be unconditional convex bodies. Then

(1.11)

\displaystyle\widetilde{V}_{n+2}(K+L)^{\frac{1}{n+2}}\geq\widetilde{V}_{n+2}(K)^{\frac{1}{n+2}}+\widetilde{V}_{n+2}(L)^{\frac{1}{n+2}}.

Moreover, equality holds if and only if
K
and
L
are dilates of each other.

We present two proofs of
Theorem 1.2
. The first is based on the weighted centro-affine Poincaré inequality in
3.22
, which is derived from
Theorem 3.20
with
\alpha=2
and
w=(1,\dots,1)
, namely a single-cap version of
Theorem 1.1
with a power weight. This cap-based approach is essential for our argument: within the weighted centro-affine Bochner framework on the whole sphere, we were not able to obtain effective control of the term involving
-2\nabla^{2}\log|X|
; see
3.23
. The second proof is shorter, relies on the Borell–Brascamp–Lieb inequality, and yields the equality characterization.

Let
K,L\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be unconditional convex bodies. For
0<p\leq 1
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
, define

(1.12)

\displaystyle(1-\lambda)\cdot K+_{p}\lambda\cdot L=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\langle x,y\rangle\leq\left((1-\lambda)h_{K}(y)^{p}+\lambda h_{L}(y)^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\quad\forall y\in S^{n-1}\right\}.

For
p=0
, define

(1.13)

\displaystyle(1-\lambda)\cdot K+_{0}\lambda\cdot L=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\langle x,y\rangle\leq h_{K}(y)^{1-\lambda}h_{L}(y)^{\lambda}\quad\forall y\in S^{n-1}\right\}.

We prove:

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
q\in(0,n)
. If
K,L\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
are unconditional convex bodies, then for every
\lambda\in(0,1)
,

(1.14)

\displaystyle\widetilde{V}_{q}\left((1-\lambda)\cdot K+_{0}\lambda\cdot L\right)\geq\widetilde{V}_{q}(K)^{1-\lambda}\widetilde{V}_{q}(L)^{\lambda}.

Moreover, equality holds if and only if
K
and
L
are dilates of one another.

The proof for
q\leq n
and the second proof for
q=n+2
both rely on the Prékopa–Leindler or Borell–Brascamp–Lieb inequality, but in essentially different coordinate systems. For
q<n
, one uses the well-known trick of logarithmic change of variables on the positive orthant, as in
[
BL95
,
Sar15
]
, which is compatible with the
L_{0}
-sum and turns the radial density
|x|^{q-n}\,dx
into a log-concave density. For
q=n+2
, one instead works in the original Euclidean coordinates on a half-space, where the weight
\langle x,w\rangle^{2}
is directly suited to the Borell–Brascamp–Lieb inequality; when
w=(1,\ldots,1)
, this half-space weight reproduces the radial weight
|x|^{2}
after integration over unconditional sets. Although we do not know of an analogous half-space mechanism for the whole range
q\in(n,n+2)
, these two arguments suggest interpolating between the logarithmic and Euclidean coordinate systems. This leads naturally to
L_{p}
-type changes of variables on the positive orthant
(0,\infty)^{n}
, and yields an extension to the range
q\in(n,n+6-4\sqrt{2}]
:

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
K,L\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be unconditional convex bodies, let
0<p<1
,
q=n+\alpha
, and assume
0<\alpha\leq\frac{2p(1-p)}{2-p}
.
Then

(1.15)

\displaystyle\widetilde{V}_{q}(K+_{p}L)^{\frac{p}{q}}\geq\widetilde{V}_{q}(K)^{\frac{p}{q}}+\widetilde{V}_{q}(L)^{\frac{p}{q}}.

Moreover, equality holds if and only if
K
and
L
are dilates.

See the discussion in
section 6
for alternative perspectives on the distinction between the cases
q<n
and
q>n
through the Reilly approach of
[
KM22
]
, which was successfully used to treat the case
q=n
.

We also prove the following weighted Poincaré inequality as a consequence of the Brunn–Minkowski inequalities for the
q
-th dual quermassintegral for
q\in(0,n)
. The case
q=n
is the local Brunn–Minkowski inequality, which was proved in
[
KM22
,
Mil23
]
.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
q\in(0,n)
. Assume that
K
is a
C^{2}_{+}

(1.16)

\displaystyle(q-1)\int_{S^{n-1}}(F-\bar{F})^{2}|X|^{q-n}\,dV_{K}\leq\int_{S^{n-1}}|\nabla F|_{g}^{2}|X|^{q-n}\,dV_{K},

where

(1.17)

\displaystyle\bar{F}=\frac{\int_{S^{n-1}}F|X|^{q-n}\,dV_{K}}{\int_{S^{n-1}}|X|^{q-n}\,dV_{K}}.

If
K
is unconditional and
F
is unconditional, then the constant
q-1
improves to
q
.

In addition, we prove the following uniqueness results for the
L_{p,q}
-Minkowski problem.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
f\in C(S^{n-1})
be positive and unconditional, and assume either

(i)

q=n+2
and
1\leq p<q
, or

(ii)

q\in(0,n)

.

Then there exists at most one

unconditional convex body whose support function

solves

(1.18)

\displaystyle|Dh|^{q-n}h^{1-p}\det(\bar{\nabla}^{2}h+hI)=f.

The uniqueness statement in
\rm(i)
appears to be new even in the case
f=1
and the pair
(p,q)=(1,n+2)
for
n\geq 3
. For
f=1
,
n=2
, and
(p,q)=(1,4)
, uniqueness without the unconditional symmetry assumption was established in
[
LW26
, Thm. 1.2, Case (3), Subcase (2)]
. We refer the reader to

And99
,

In the last section, we introduce the logarithmic centro-affine geometry. The proof of
Theorem 1.3
, in the range
0<q\leq n
, suggests that centro-affine geometry, although well adapted to the
L_{1}
-structure, does not fully reflect the multiplicative nature of the
L_{0}
-sum. The relevant infinitesimal geometry for unconditional bodies is instead obtained after passing to logarithmic coordinates on the positive orthant. More precisely, on

(1.19)

\displaystyle\Omega_{+}=\{x\in S^{n-1}:\ X_{i}(x)>0,\ i=1,\ldots,n\},

we consider the immersion
Y=\log X=(\log X_{1},\ldots,\log X_{n})
and the constant transversal vector
\bm{\xi}=(1,\ldots,1)
. This induces a flat equiaffine structure
(g_{\log},\nabla^{\log})
whose affine volume is
dV_{\log}=e^{-\eta}dV_{K}
, where
\eta:=\sum_{i}Y_{i}
.

The logarithmic metric is closely related to the centro-affine metric. Indeed,

(1.20)

\displaystyle g_{\log}=g+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{x_{i}X_{i}}{h}d\log X_{i}\otimes d\log X_{i}.

Since
K
is unconditional, we have
x_{i}X_{i}>0
on
\Omega_{+}
, and hence
g_{\log}>g
. Consequently, the Poincaré inequality in the logarithmic geometry with weight
e^{\eta}
and constant
n
yields the centro-affine Poincaré inequality with the same constant
n
. See
8.1
,
8.2
and
8.3
.

The logarithmic geometry also combines the geometry of
K
and its polar body. Let

(1.21)

\displaystyle\bm{\xi}^{*}=\frac{Px}{h}=\left(\frac{x_{1}X_{1}}{h},\ldots,\frac{x_{n}X_{n}}{h}\right)

denote the conormal field. Its components

(1.22)

\displaystyle p_{i}=\frac{x_{i}X_{i}}{h},\quad i=1,\ldots,n,

are the diagonal entries of the matrix
\mathcal{P}=\frac{x}{h}\otimes X
, coupling the coordinate
X_{i}
of the boundary point of
K
with the corresponding coordinate
x_{i}/h
of the polar point. These functions satisfy

(1.23)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{0}^{\log}p_{i}+np_{i}=1,\quad\text{where}\quad\mathcal{L}_{0}^{\log}:=\Delta_{\log}+g_{\log}(\nabla^{\log}\eta,\nabla^{\log}\cdot).

Hence the functions
p_{i}-\frac{1}{n}
are eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{L}_{0}^{\log}
with eigenvalue
-n
.

We note that, in the unconditional setting,
[
KM16
, Sec. 5]
uses a change of metric on the positive orthant in the derivation of functional inequalities. Here the change of metric emerges from the affine geometry induced by a constant transversal vector. Using the Hessian identity
(\nabla^{\log})^{2}\eta+ng_{\log}=0
and a logarithmic Bochner formula, our approach yields

(1.24)

\displaystyle n\int_{\Omega_{+}}(F-\bar{F}_{+})^{2}\,dV_{K}\leq\int_{\Omega_{+}}\left|\nabla^{\log}F\right|_{g_{\log}}^{2}\,dV_{K}\leq\int_{\Omega_{+}}\left|\nabla F\right|_{g}^{2}\,dV_{K},

where the first inequality is sharp in the logarithmic geometry. In particular, equality in the first inequality is attained by the functions
p_{i}-\frac{1}{n}
, which are the analogues of the quadratic spherical harmonics on the round sphere. Moreover, equality in the second inequality is attained only by constant functions. See
Theorem 8.12
and
Theorem 8.13
. This should also be compared with
[
KM22
, Thm. 8.3 and Rem. 8.4]
, where the corresponding unconditional infinitesimal statement is obtained by a Reilly-type method, but without passing through the intermediate inequality above or the equality characterization.

2.
Background

Define the positive definite
(1,1)
-tensor
\tau=\tau[h]=\bar{\nabla}^{2}h+hI
. Then

(2.1)

\displaystyle dX|_{x}(v)=\tau(x)v,\quad v\in T_{x}S^{n-1}.

We write
\tau^{ij}
for the entries of the inverse matrix of
\tau
.

Let
S_{K}
denote the surface area measure of
K
:

(2.2)

\displaystyle dS_{K}=\det(\tau)\,dx=\frac{1}{\mathcal{K}}\,dx=\frac{1}{h}\,dV_{K},

where
\mathcal{K}
denotes the Gauss curvature of
\partial K
at the point
X(x)
.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
HLYZ16
, Lem. 2.9]
)
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a
C^{2}_{+}
convex body containing the origin in its interior. Then for every
q>0
,
\widetilde{V}_{q}(K)=\frac{1}{n}\int_{S^{n-1}}|X|^{q-n}\,dV_{K}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be unconditional and convex. If
x\in K
and
|y_{i}|\leq|x_{i}|
for all
i=1,\dots,n,

then
y\in K
.

Proof.

By unconditionality, all points
(\sigma_{1}x_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n}x_{n})
,
\sigma_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
belong to
K
. Since their convex hull is
\prod_{i=1}^{n}[-|x_{i}|,|x_{i}|]
, this box is contained in
K
.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a
C^{2}_{+}
unconditional convex body. Then, for every
x\in S^{n-1}
and every
i=1,\ldots,n
,

(2.3)

\displaystyle X_{i}(x)>0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x_{i}>0.

In particular,

(2.4)

\displaystyle\{x\in S^{n-1}:X_{i}(x)>0,\ i=1,\ldots,n\}=\{x\in S^{n-1}:x_{i}>0,\ i=1,\ldots,n\}.

Proof.

Let
\{E_{i}\}
be the standard coordinate basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
R_{i}
denote reflection in the coordinate hyperplane
\{x_{i}=0\}
. We first prove that
X_{i}(x)=0
implies
x_{i}=0
. Set
y=X(x)
and suppose that
y_{i}=0
. Since
R_{i}y=y
, both
x
and
R_{i}x
are outer unit normals to the boundary of
K
,
\partial K
, at
y
. Therefore,
R_{i}x=x
.

We now prove that
X_{i}(x)>0
implies
x_{i}>0
. By reflection symmetry, we then also have that
X_{i}(x)<0
implies
x_{i}<0
. By
2.2
, for every sufficiently small
t>0
with
t<y_{i}
, the point
y-tE_{i}
belongs to
K
. If
x_{i}<0
, then

(2.5)

\displaystyle\langle y-tE_{i},x\rangle=\langle y,x\rangle-tx_{i}>\langle y,x\rangle=h_{K}(x),

which contradicts the definition of the support function. If
x_{i}=0
, then

(2.6)

\displaystyle\langle y-tE_{i},x\rangle=\langle y,x\rangle=h_{K}(x).

Thus the line segment
[y-tE_{i},y]
lies on
\partial K
. This contradicts the strict convexity of
K
. Hence
x_{i}>0
.

Putting everything together, the claim follows.
∎

3.
Centro-affine geometry

We now recall some basics from centro-affine geometry; see for details
[
NS94
,
Mil23
]
. The position vector
X
induces a torsion-free connection
\nabla
and a metric
g
on
S^{n-1}
as follows:

(3.1)

\displaystyle D_{U}dX(V)=dX(\nabla_{U}V)-g(U,V)X,\quad U,V\in TS^{n-1}.

If
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n-1}
is a local
\bar{g}
-orthonormal frame diagonalizing
\tau
, say
\tau e_{i}=\lambda_{i}e_{i}
,
then

(3.2)

\displaystyle g(e_{i},e_{j})=\frac{\lambda_{i}}{h}\delta_{ij},\quad g^{-1}(e_{i},e_{j})=\frac{h}{\lambda_{i}}\delta_{ij}.

We also use the conjugate torsion-free connection
\nabla^{*}
defined via:

(3.3)

\displaystyle U(g(V,W))=g(\nabla_{U}V,W)+g(V,\nabla_{U}^{*}W).

For a smooth function
F
on
S^{n-1}
, the centro-affine gradient is defined by

(3.4)

\displaystyle g(\nabla F,V)=dF(V),\quad V\in TS^{n-1}.

We also define

(3.5)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}F(U,V)

\displaystyle=U(VF)-(\nabla_{U}V)F,

\displaystyle(\nabla^{*})^{2}F(U,V)

\displaystyle=U(VF)-(\nabla_{U}^{*}V)F,

\displaystyle\Delta F

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}_{g}\left((\nabla^{*})^{2}F\right).

We record two important identities, which will be used repeatedly:

(3.6)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ric}:=\operatorname{Ric}^{\nabla}=(n-2)g,\quad\nabla^{2}X+gX=0.

The latter identity is a special case of the following useful lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\tilde{f}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
be
k
-homogeneous, and define

(3.7)

\displaystyle f(x)=\tilde{f}(X(x)),\quad x\in S^{n-1}.

Then for every
x\in S^{n-1}
and every
v_{1},v_{2}\in T_{x}S^{n-1}
,

(3.8)

\displaystyle D^{2}\tilde{f}(X(x))[dX(v_{1}),dX(v_{2})]=\nabla^{2}f(v_{1},v_{2})+kfg(v_{1},v_{2}).

In particular, if
\tilde{f}
is concave on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
\nabla^{2}f+kfg\leq 0
.

Proof.

We first take the directional derivative in the
v_{2}
-direction:

(3.9)

\displaystyle v_{2}(f)=D\tilde{f}(X)[dX(v_{2})].

Differentiating once more in the direction
v_{1}
gives

(3.10)

\displaystyle v_{1}(v_{2}f)=D^{2}\tilde{f}(X)[dX(v_{1}),dX(v_{2})]+D\tilde{f}(X)[D_{v_{1}}dX(v_{2})].

Now using
D_{v_{1}}dX(v_{2})=dX(\nabla_{v_{1}}v_{2})-g(v_{1},v_{2})X
, and Euler’s identity we obtain

(3.11)

\displaystyle D\tilde{f}(X)[D_{v_{1}}dX(v_{2})]

\displaystyle=D\tilde{f}(X)[dX(\nabla_{v_{1}}v_{2})]-g(v_{1},v_{2})\,D\tilde{f}(X)[X]

\displaystyle=(\nabla_{v_{1}}v_{2})f-kfg(v_{1},v_{2}).

Rearranging terms gives

(3.12)

\displaystyle D^{2}\tilde{f}(X)[dX(v_{1}),dX(v_{2})]

\displaystyle=v_{1}(v_{2}f)-(\nabla_{v_{1}}v_{2})f+kfg(v_{1},v_{2})

\displaystyle=\nabla^{2}f(v_{1},v_{2})+kfg(v_{1},v_{2}).

∎

Lemma 3.2
.

For every
f\in C^{2}(S^{n-1})
, we have

(3.13)

\displaystyle\Delta f=\frac{1}{h\det(\tau)}\bar{\nabla}_{i}\left(h^{2}\tau^{ij}\det(\tau)\bar{\nabla}_{j}f\right).

Proof.

We recall the following identity; see
[
Mil23
]
:

(3.14)

\displaystyle\Delta f+(n-1)f=\tau^{ij}\left(\bar{\nabla}_{ij}^{2}(hf)+\bar{g}_{ij}hf\right).

Therefore

(3.15)

\displaystyle\Delta f=h\tau^{ij}\bar{\nabla}_{ij}^{2}f+2\tau^{ij}\bar{\nabla}_{i}h\bar{\nabla}_{j}f.

Moreover, due to the identity
\bar{\nabla}_{i}(\tau^{ij}\det(\tau))=0
, a differentiation gives

(3.16)

\displaystyle\bar{\nabla}_{i}\left(h^{2}\tau^{ij}\det(\tau)\bar{\nabla}_{j}f\right)=2h\tau^{ij}\det(\tau)\bar{\nabla}_{i}h\bar{\nabla}_{j}f+h^{2}\tau^{ij}\det(\tau)\bar{\nabla}_{ij}^{2}f.

Dividing this last equation by
h\det(\tau)
proves the claim.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
Z=Z^{i}\partial_{i}
be a smooth vector field on
S^{n-1}
, written in local coordinates with respect to the round metric
\bar{g}
. Then

(3.17)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}_{\nabla}Z=\frac{1}{h\det(\tau)}\bar{\nabla}_{i}\left(h\det(\tau)Z^{i}\right).

Proof.

Let
\phi\in C^{\infty}(S^{n-1})
. By integration-by-parts with respect to
(g,\nabla,V_{K})
,

(3.18)

\displaystyle\int_{S^{n-1}}\phi\operatorname{div}_{\nabla}Z\,dV_{K}=-\int_{S^{n-1}}g(\nabla\phi,Z)\,dV_{K}=-\int_{S^{n-1}}(Z^{i}\bar{\nabla}_{i}\phi)h\det(\tau)\,dx.

Moreover, applying the integration-by-parts with respect to
(\bar{g},\bar{\nabla},dx)
gives

(3.19)

\displaystyle-\int_{S^{n-1}}(Z^{i}\bar{\nabla}_{i}\phi)h\det(\tau)\,dx=\int_{S^{n-1}}\phi\bar{\nabla}_{i}\left(h\det(\tau)Z^{i}\right)\,dx.

Hence

(3.20)

\displaystyle\int_{S^{n-1}}\phi\operatorname{div}_{\nabla}Z\,dV_{K}=\int_{S^{n-1}}\phi\bar{\nabla}_{i}\left(h\det(\tau)Z^{i}\right)\,dx.

Since this holds for all smooth test functions
\phi
, the claim follows.
∎

Let
\Phi,F\in C^{\infty}(S^{n-1})
, and set
d\mu_{\Phi}=e^{-\Phi}\,dV_{K}
. We define the weighted divergence of a smooth vector field
Z
and the weighted centro-affine Laplacian of
F
by

(3.21)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}_{\Phi}Z

\displaystyle=\operatorname{div}_{\nabla}Z-g(\nabla\Phi,Z),

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\Phi}F

\displaystyle=\Delta F-g(\nabla\Phi,\nabla F)=\operatorname{div}_{\Phi}(\nabla F).

Lemma 3.4
.

For every smooth function
u
and smooth vector field
Z
on
S^{n-1}
,

(3.22)

\displaystyle\int_{S^{n-1}}u\operatorname{div}_{\Phi}Z\,d\mu_{\Phi}=-\int_{S^{n-1}}g(\nabla u,Z)\,d\mu_{\Phi}.

In particular,

(3.23)

\displaystyle\int_{S^{n-1}}\operatorname{div}_{\Phi}Z\,d\mu_{\Phi}=0,

and for smooth functions
u,v
,

(3.24)

\displaystyle\int_{S^{n-1}}u\mathcal{L}_{\Phi}v\,d\mu_{\Phi}=-\int_{S^{n-1}}g(\nabla u,\nabla v)\,d\mu_{\Phi}.

Proof.

Let us recall the following unweighted integration-by-parts formula:

(3.25)

\displaystyle\int_{S^{n-1}}f\operatorname{div}_{\nabla}Z\,dV_{K}=-\int_{S^{n-1}}g(\nabla f,Z)\,dV_{K}.

By the definition of the weighted divergence, the left-hand side of (
3.22
) expands as

(3.26)

\displaystyle\int_{S^{n-1}}u\operatorname{div}_{\Phi}Z\,d\mu_{\Phi}=\int_{S^{n-1}}ue^{-\Phi}\operatorname{div}_{\nabla}Z\,dV_{K}-\int_{S^{n-1}}ug(\nabla\Phi,Z)e^{-\Phi}\,dV_{K}.

We now apply (
3.25
) with
f=ue^{-\Phi}
. This yields

(3.27)

\displaystyle\int_{S^{n-1}}ue^{-\Phi}\operatorname{div}_{\nabla}Z\,dV_{K}=-\int_{S^{n-1}}g\left(\nabla(ue^{-\Phi}),Z\right)\,dV_{K}.

On the other hand, we have

(3.28)

\displaystyle g\left(\nabla(ue^{-\Phi}),Z\right)=e^{-\Phi}g(\nabla u,Z)-ue^{-\Phi}g(\nabla\Phi,Z).

Substituting this expansion back yields

(3.29)

\displaystyle\int_{S^{n-1}}ue^{-\Phi}\operatorname{div}_{\nabla}Z\,dV_{K}=-\int_{S^{n-1}}e^{-\Phi}g(\nabla u,Z)\,dV_{K}+\int_{S^{n-1}}u\,e^{-\Phi}g(\nabla\Phi,Z)\,dV_{K}.

Inserting this identity into (
3.26
) and rearranging establishes (
3.22
). Taking the special case
u\equiv 1
in this identity then yields (
3.23
).

Finally, to obtain (
3.24
), it suffices to substitute the vector field
Z=\nabla v
into (
3.22
).
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

For a smooth function
F
and vector fields
U,V
on
S^{n-1}
we have

(3.30)

\displaystyle g(\nabla_{U}\nabla F,V)

\displaystyle=(\nabla^{*})^{2}F(U,V),

(3.31)

\displaystyle g(V,\nabla_{U}^{*}\nabla F)

\displaystyle=\nabla^{2}F(U,V).

Moreover, we have

(3.32)

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}_{g}\left((\nabla Z)\circ(\nabla Z)\right)=\left|(\nabla^{*})^{2}F\right|_{g}^{2}\quad\text{for }Z=\nabla F.

Proof.

The conjugacy relation (
3.3
) states

(3.33)

\displaystyle U(VF)=U\left(g(\nabla F,V)\right)=g(\nabla_{U}\nabla F,V)+g(\nabla F,\nabla_{U}^{*}V).

Hence

(3.34)

\displaystyle g(\nabla_{U}\nabla F,V)=U(VF)-(\nabla_{U}^{*}V)F=(\nabla^{*})^{2}F(U,V),

which is (
3.30
). The proof of the second identity (
3.31
) follows similarly, with the roles of the two connections interchanged.

To establish (
3.32
), we work in a local
g
-orthonormal frame
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n-1}
. By definition of the trace we have

(3.35)

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}_{g}\left((\nabla Z)\circ(\nabla Z)\right)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n-1}g(\nabla_{e_{i}}Z,e_{j})g(\nabla_{e_{j}}Z,e_{i}).

Choosing the vector field
Z=\nabla F
and applying (
3.30
) gives

(3.36)

\displaystyle g(\nabla_{e_{i}}Z,e_{j})=g(\nabla_{e_{i}}\nabla F,e_{j})=(\nabla^{*})^{2}F(e_{i},e_{j}).

Since the Hessian
(\nabla^{*})^{2}F
is symmetric, the double sum on the right-hand side is the squared norm of this Hessian:

(3.37)

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{n-1}g(\nabla_{e_{i}}Z,e_{j})g(\nabla_{e_{j}}Z,e_{i})=\left|(\nabla^{*})^{2}F\right|_{g}^{2}.

∎

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
\Phi,F\in C^{\infty}(S^{n-1})
, and
Z=\nabla F
. Then

(3.38)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}_{\Phi}(\nabla_{Z}Z)=Z(\mathcal{L}_{\Phi}F)+\left(\operatorname{Ric}+\nabla^{2}\Phi\right)(Z,Z)+\left|(\nabla^{*})^{2}F\right|_{g}^{2}.

Moreover, integrating against
d\mu_{\Phi}=e^{-\Phi}\,dV_{K}
yields

(3.39)

\displaystyle\int_{S^{n-1}}(\mathcal{L}_{\Phi}F)^{2}\,d\mu_{\Phi}=\int_{S^{n-1}}\left|(\nabla^{*})^{2}F\right|_{g}^{2}\,d\mu_{\Phi}+\int_{S^{n-1}}(\operatorname{Ric}+\nabla^{2}\Phi)(\nabla F,\nabla F)\,d\mu_{\Phi}.

Proof.

We start from the asymmetric Bochner identity (cf.
[
Mil23
, Prop. 5.1]
):

(3.40)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}_{\nabla}(\nabla_{Z}Z)=Z(\operatorname{div}_{\nabla}Z)+\operatorname{Ric}(Z,Z)+\operatorname{tr}_{g}\left((\nabla Z)\circ(\nabla Z)\right).

For
Z=\nabla F
, we have
\operatorname{div}_{\nabla}Z=\Delta F
and, by (
3.32
),

(3.41)

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}_{g}\left((\nabla Z)\circ(\nabla Z)\right)=\left|(\nabla^{*})^{2}F\right|_{g}^{2}.

Hence

(3.42)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}_{\nabla}(\nabla_{Z}Z)=Z(\Delta F)+\operatorname{Ric}(Z,Z)+\left|(\nabla^{*})^{2}F\right|_{g}^{2}

and

(3.43)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}_{\Phi}(\nabla_{Z}Z)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{div}_{\nabla}(\nabla_{Z}Z)-g(\nabla\Phi,\nabla_{Z}Z)

\displaystyle=Z(\Delta F)-g(\nabla\Phi,\nabla_{Z}Z)+\operatorname{Ric}(Z,Z)+\left|(\nabla^{*})^{2}F\right|_{g}^{2}.

Since
\mathcal{L}_{\Phi}F=\Delta F-g(\nabla\Phi,Z)
, we have

(3.44)

\displaystyle Z(\mathcal{L}_{\Phi}F)=Z(\Delta F)-Z\left(g(\nabla\Phi,Z)\right).

Due to (
3.3
),

(3.45)

\displaystyle Z\left(g(\nabla\Phi,Z)\right)=g(\nabla_{Z}Z,\nabla\Phi)+g(Z,\nabla_{Z}^{*}\nabla\Phi).

Moreover, by the second identity in
3.5
,
g(Z,\nabla_{Z}^{*}\nabla\Phi)=\nabla^{2}\Phi(Z,Z)
. Thus

(3.46)

\displaystyle Z\left(g(\nabla\Phi,Z)\right)=g(\nabla\Phi,\nabla_{Z}Z)+\nabla^{2}\Phi(Z,Z).

Putting (
3.44
) and (
3.46
) together yields

(3.47)

\displaystyle Z(\Delta F)-g(\nabla\Phi,\nabla_{Z}Z)=Z(\mathcal{L}_{\Phi}F)+\nabla^{2}\Phi(Z,Z).

Now substituting (
3.47
) into (
3.43
) proves (
3.38
).

We integrate (
3.38
) against
d\mu_{\Phi}
and then use (
3.23
) to obtain

(3.48)

\displaystyle 0=\int_{S^{n-1}}Z(\mathcal{L}_{\Phi}F)\,d\mu_{\Phi}+\int_{S^{n-1}}(\operatorname{Ric}+\nabla^{2}\Phi)(Z,Z)\,d\mu_{\Phi}+\int_{S^{n-1}}\left|(\nabla^{*})^{2}F\right|_{g}^{2}\,d\mu_{\Phi}.

By (
3.24
) with
u=\mathcal{L}_{\Phi}F
and
v=F
,

(3.49)

\displaystyle\int_{S^{n-1}}Z(\mathcal{L}_{\Phi}F)\,d\mu_{\Phi}=\int_{S^{n-1}}g\left(\nabla(\mathcal{L}_{\Phi}F),\nabla F\right)\,d\mu_{\Phi}=-\int_{S^{n-1}}(\mathcal{L}_{\Phi}F)^{2}\,d\mu_{\Phi}.

Rearranging the terms gives (
3.39
).
∎

Remark 3.7
.

Let
\sigma_{k}=\sigma_{k}(\tau)
denote the
k
-th elementary symmetric function of eigenvalues of
\tau=\tau[h]
. We write

(3.50)

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}^{ij}:=\frac{\partial\sigma_{k}}{\partial\tau_{ij}},\quad\sigma_{k}^{ij,pq}:=\frac{\partial^{2}\sigma_{k}}{\partial\tau_{ij}\partial\tau_{pq}},

and define
\mathrm{H}=(\nabla^{*})^{2}f
.
By
[
CHG17
, Lem. 2.3]
,
\sigma_{k}^{ij,pq}\tau[hf]_{pq}
is
\bar{\nabla}
-divergence-free. Hence

(3.51)

\displaystyle\int_{S^{n-1}}h\sigma_{k}^{ij,pq}\tau[hf]_{pq}\left(\tau[hf]_{ij}-f\tau_{ij}\right)\,dx=\int_{S^{n-1}}\sigma_{k}^{ij,pq}\tau[hf]_{pq}\bar{\nabla}_{i}\left(h^{2}\bar{\nabla}_{j}f\right)\,dx=0.

Due to (
3.14
),
\tau[hf]_{ij}=h\mathrm{H}_{ij}+f\tau_{ij}
. Thus the identity (
3.51
) for
k=n-1
becomes

(3.52)

\displaystyle 0=\int_{S^{n-1}}h^{2}\sigma_{n-1}^{ij,pq}\tau[hf]_{pq}\mathrm{H}_{ij}\,dx.

Substituting
\tau[hf]_{pq}=h\mathrm{H}_{pq}+f\tau_{pq}
yields

(3.53)

\displaystyle 0=\int_{S^{n-1}}\left(h^{3}\sigma_{n-1}^{ij,pq}\mathrm{H}_{ij}\mathrm{H}_{pq}+h^{2}f\sigma_{n-1}^{ij,pq}\tau_{pq}\mathrm{H}_{ij}\right)\,dx.

Now using the identity
\sigma_{n-1}^{ij,pq}\tau_{pq}=(n-2)\sigma_{n-1}^{ij}

we obtain

(3.54)

\displaystyle 0=\int_{S^{n-1}}\left(h^{3}\sigma_{n-1}^{ij,pq}\mathrm{H}_{ij}\mathrm{H}_{pq}+(n-2)h^{2}f\sigma_{n-1}^{ij}\mathrm{H}_{ij}\right)\,dx.

The second term in (
3.54
) is simply
(n-2)\int_{S^{n-1}}f\Delta f\,dV_{K}
. Also note that

(3.55)

\displaystyle\sigma_{n-1}^{ij,pq}=\left(\tau^{ij}\tau^{pq}-\tau^{ip}\tau^{jq}\right)\sigma_{n-1}.

Therefore

(3.56)

\displaystyle h^{3}\sigma_{n-1}^{ij,pq}\mathrm{H}_{ij}\mathrm{H}_{pq}\,dx=\left((\Delta f)^{2}-|(\nabla^{*})^{2}f|_{g}^{2}\right)\,dV_{K}.

Substituting this into (
3.54
), using

h^{2}\sigma_{n-1}^{ij}\mathrm{H}_{ij}\,dx=\Delta f\,dV_{K}
,
and integrating by parts yields

(3.57)

\displaystyle\int_{S^{n-1}}(\Delta f)^{2}\,dV_{K}=\int_{S^{n-1}}|(\nabla^{*})^{2}f|_{g}^{2}+(n-2)|\nabla f|_{g}^{2}\,dV_{K}.

This last identity, the integrated centro-affine Bochner formula, was proved in
[
Opo15
,
Mil23
]
. Hence, (
3.51
) may be regarded as a higher-order integrated Bochner formula.

It is worth noting that the integrated centro-affine Bochner formula may also be recovered variationally from the identity

(3.58)

\displaystyle\int_{S^{n-1}}\Delta_{t}f\,dV_{t}=0,

valid for every
t
with
|t|
sufficiently small, where

(3.59)

\displaystyle h_{t}

\displaystyle=e^{tf}h_{K},\quad K_{t}

\displaystyle=\text{the convex body with support function }h_{t},

\displaystyle\Delta_{t}

\displaystyle=\Delta_{K_{t}},\quad dV_{t}

\displaystyle=dV_{K_{t}}.

Indeed, one computes

(3.60)

\displaystyle\dot{dV}_{t}\big|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=(\Delta_{K}f+nf)\,dV_{K},

\displaystyle\dot{\Delta}_{t}\big|_{t=0}f

\displaystyle=-\left|(\nabla_{K}^{*})^{2}f\right|_{g_{K}}^{2}+2|\nabla_{K}f|_{g_{K}}^{2}.

Then differentiating the identity
\int_{S^{n-1}}\Delta_{t}f\,dV_{t}=0
at
t=0
and integrating by parts yields

(3.61)

\displaystyle\int_{S^{n-1}}(\Delta_{K}f)^{2}\,dV_{K}=\int_{S^{n-1}}\left|(\nabla_{K}^{*})^{2}f\right|_{g_{K}}^{2}\,dV_{K}+(n-2)\int_{S^{n-1}}|\nabla_{K}f|_{g_{K}}^{2}\,dV_{K}.

We leave the details to the interested reader.

3.1.
Poincaré inequalities on caps

Let
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
, and recall that
\ell_{w}=\langle X,w\rangle
. We define the open cap

(3.62)

\displaystyle\Omega_{w}=\{x\in S^{n-1}:\ \ell_{w}(x)>0\}

together with its boundary

(3.63)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{w}=\partial\Omega_{w}=\{x\in S^{n-1}:\ \ell_{w}=0\}.

For every sufficiently small positive number
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
, we also consider the corresponding smooth open set and its boundary

(3.64)

\displaystyle\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}=\{\ell_{w}>\varepsilon\},\quad\Sigma_{w,\varepsilon}=\partial\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}=\{\ell_{w}=\varepsilon\},

where the threshold
\varepsilon_{0}>0
is chosen exactly as in
3.8
.

Note that
\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}
is connected: Let
x_{0},x_{1}\in\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}
and set
p_{i}=X(x_{i})\in\partial K
,
i=0,1
. Then
p_{t}=(1-t)p_{0}+tp_{1}\in K
and
\langle p_{t},w\rangle>\varepsilon
for all
t\in[0,1]
. Let
q_{t}=p_{t}/\|p_{t}\|_{K}
, where
\|\cdot\|_{K}
denotes the Minkowski functional of
K
. Since
p_{t}\in K
, we have
\|p_{t}\|_{K}\leq 1
, and hence
\langle q_{t},w\rangle>\varepsilon
. Therefore, the path
t\mapsto X^{-1}(q_{t})\subset\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}
joins
x_{0}
to
x_{1}
.

Lemma 3.8
.

Suppose
K
is
C^{\infty}_{+}
. There exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that, for every
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
, the level set
\Sigma_{w,\varepsilon}
is a smooth (possibly disconnected) hypersurface of
S^{n-1}
.

Proof.

We show that
0
is a regular value of
\ell_{w}
. Let
x\in\Sigma_{w}
. If
d\ell_{w}|_{x}=0
, then

(3.65)

\displaystyle 0=d\ell_{w}|_{x}(v)=\langle dX|_{x}(v),w\rangle\quad\text{for all }v\in T_{x}S^{n-1}.

That is,
w
is parallel to
x
. Therefore
\ell_{w}(x)=h_{K}(x)\langle x,w\rangle\neq 0
,
which contradicts
\ell_{w}(x)=0
. By continuity, all sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
are also regular values.
∎

The
g
-unit and
\bar{g}
-unit outward normals to the hypersurface
\Sigma_{w,\varepsilon}
are given by

(3.66)

\displaystyle\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}=-\frac{\nabla\ell_{w}}{|\nabla\ell_{w}|_{g}},\quad\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}=-\frac{\bar{\nabla}\ell_{w}}{|\bar{\nabla}\ell_{w}|_{\bar{g}}}.

Here, “outward” means that as
\varepsilon
decreases, the domains
\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}
expand in the outward normal direction. For the limiting case
\varepsilon=0
, we simply write
\mathbf{N}=\mathbf{N}_{0}
and
\mathbf{n}=\mathbf{n}_{0}
.

Note that, in general, the two unit normals
\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}
and
\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}
are not collinear. At each point of
\Sigma_{w,\varepsilon}
we have the
\bar{g}
-orthogonal decomposition

(3.67)

\displaystyle\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}=\frac{|\nabla\ell_{w}|_{g}}{|\bar{\nabla}\ell_{w}|_{\bar{g}}}\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}+\mathbf{T},\quad\mathbf{T}\in T\Sigma_{w,\varepsilon}.

Indeed, this follows from the definitions of the following inner products:

(3.68)

\displaystyle g(Z,\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon})=-\frac{1}{|\nabla\ell_{w}|_{g}}d\ell_{w}(Z),\quad\bar{g}(Z,\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon})=-\frac{1}{|\bar{\nabla}\ell_{w}|_{\bar{g}}}d\ell_{w}(Z).

Hence, for every vector field
Z
, we have the following relations along
\Sigma_{w,\varepsilon}

(3.69)

\displaystyle\bar{g}(Z,\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon})=\frac{|\nabla\ell_{w}|_{g}}{|\bar{\nabla}\ell_{w}|_{\bar{g}}}g(Z,\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}),\quad d\ell_{w}\left(\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}-\frac{|\nabla\ell_{w}|_{g}}{|\bar{\nabla}\ell_{w}|_{\bar{g}}}\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}\right)=0.

Let
\alpha>0
. On
\Omega_{w}
we consider the weighted measure associated with the potential

(3.70)

\displaystyle\Phi=\Phi_{w,\alpha}=-\alpha\log\ell_{w},\quad d\mu_{w,\alpha}=e^{-\Phi_{w,\alpha}}\,dV_{K}.

We also introduce a boundary measure induced by
dV_{K}
on the hypersurface
\Sigma_{w,\varepsilon}
:

(3.71)

\displaystyle d\sigma_{K,\varepsilon}=h\det(\tau)\frac{|\nabla\ell_{w}|_{g}}{|\bar{\nabla}\ell_{w}|_{\bar{g}}}\,d\bar{\sigma}_{\varepsilon},

where
d\bar{\sigma}_{\varepsilon}
denotes the hypersurface measure of
\Sigma_{w,\varepsilon}
with respect to the metric
\bar{g}
. The corresponding weighted boundary measure is then defined by

(3.72)

\displaystyle d\sigma_{w,\alpha,\varepsilon}=e^{-\Phi_{w,\alpha}}\,d\sigma_{K,\varepsilon}.

\Sigma_{w,\varepsilon}
,
Z
is tangent to
\Sigma_{w,\varepsilon}
. Due to the conjugacy identity,

(3.87)

\displaystyle Z\left(g(Z,\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon})\right)=g(\nabla_{Z}Z,\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon})+g(Z,\nabla_{Z}^{*}\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}).

Hence
-g(\nabla_{Z}Z,\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon})=g(Z,\nabla_{Z}^{*}\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon})=\mathrm{II}^{*}_{\varepsilon}(Z,Z)
.
∎

Lemma 3.15
.

For every tangent vector
Z\in T\Sigma_{w,\varepsilon}
, we have
\mathrm{II}^{*}_{\varepsilon}(Z,Z)=\varepsilon\frac{|Z|_{g}^{2}}{|\nabla\ell_{w}|_{g}}
.

Proof.

We have
Z(\ell_{w})=g(\nabla\ell_{w},Z)=0
. Hence

(3.88)

\displaystyle\mathrm{II}^{*}_{\varepsilon}(Z,Z)=g(Z,\nabla_{Z}^{*}\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon})=-\frac{g(Z,\nabla_{Z}^{*}\nabla\ell_{w})}{|\nabla\ell_{w}|_{g}}.

By
3.5
,
g(Z,\nabla_{Z}^{*}\nabla\ell_{w})=\nabla^{2}\ell_{w}(Z,Z)
. Using
\nabla^{2}\ell_{w}=-\ell_{w}g
on
\Sigma_{w,\varepsilon}
, where
\ell_{w}=\varepsilon
, we obtain
g(Z,\nabla_{Z}^{*}\nabla\ell_{w})=-\varepsilon|Z|_{g}^{2}
.
∎

Lemma 3.16
.

On
\Omega_{w}
we have

(3.89)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ric}+\nabla^{2}\Phi_{w,\alpha}-\frac{1}{\alpha}d\Phi_{w,\alpha}\otimes d\Phi_{w,\alpha}=(n-2+\alpha)g.

Proof.

Recall
\nabla^{2}\ell_{w}=-\ell_{w}g
. Therefore

(3.90)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}(\log\ell_{w})=\frac{1}{\ell_{w}}\nabla^{2}\ell_{w}-\frac{1}{\ell_{w}^{2}}d\ell_{w}\otimes d\ell_{w}=-g-d\log\ell_{w}\otimes d\log\ell_{w},

and
\nabla^{2}\Phi_{w,\alpha}=\alpha g+\alpha d\log\ell_{w}\otimes d\log\ell_{w}
.
We also have

(3.91)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha}d\Phi_{w,\alpha}\otimes d\Phi_{w,\alpha}=\alpha d\log\ell_{w}\otimes d\log\ell_{w}.

Since
\operatorname{Ric}=(n-2)g
, identity (
3.89
) follows.
∎

Lemma 3.17
.

For every smooth function
F
on
\Omega_{w}
,

(3.92)

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\left|(\nabla^{*})^{2}F\right|_{g}^{2}+\nabla^{2}\Phi_{w,\alpha}(\nabla F,\nabla F)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{n+\alpha-1}(\mathcal{L}_{\Phi}F)^{2}+\left(\nabla^{2}\Phi_{w,\alpha}-\frac{1}{\alpha}d\Phi_{w,\alpha}\otimes d\Phi_{w,\alpha}\right)(\nabla F,\nabla F).

Proof.

Due to the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,
\left|(\nabla^{*})^{2}F\right|_{g}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{n-1}(\Delta F)^{2}
. Now let
a=\mathcal{L}_{\Phi}F
,
b=g(\nabla\Phi_{w,\alpha},\nabla F)
, and
N=n+\alpha-1
. Then
\Delta F=a+b
and

(3.93)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n-1}(a+b)^{2}\geq\frac{1}{N}a^{2}-\frac{1}{N-n+1}b^{2}=\frac{1}{n+\alpha-1}a^{2}-\frac{1}{\alpha}b^{2}.

Hence

(3.94)

\displaystyle\left|(\nabla^{*})^{2}F\right|_{g}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{n+\alpha-1}(\mathcal{L}_{\Phi}F)^{2}-\frac{1}{\alpha}g(\nabla\Phi_{w,\alpha},\nabla F)^{2}.

∎

Corollary 3.18
.

Let
u\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}})
satisfy
u_{\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}}=0
on
\Sigma_{w,\varepsilon}
. Then

(3.95)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}}(\mathcal{L}_{\Phi}u)^{2}\,d\mu_{w,\alpha}\geq(n+\alpha-1)\int_{\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}}|\nabla u|_{g}^{2}\,d\mu_{w,\alpha}.

Proof.

In view of
Theorem 3.14
and
3.15
,

(3.96)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}}(\mathcal{L}_{\Phi}u)^{2}\,d\mu_{w,\alpha}\geq\int_{\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}}|(\nabla^{*})^{2}u|_{g}^{2}\,d\mu_{w,\alpha}+\int_{\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}}(\operatorname{Ric}+\nabla^{2}\Phi_{w,\alpha})(\nabla u,\nabla u)\,d\mu_{w,\alpha}.

Now we apply
3.17
and
3.16
:

(3.97)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}}(\mathcal{L}_{\Phi}u)^{2}\,d\mu_{w,\alpha}\geq\frac{1}{n+\alpha-1}\int_{\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}}(\mathcal{L}_{\Phi}u)^{2}\,d\mu_{w,\alpha}+(n-2+\alpha)\int_{\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}}|\nabla u|_{g}^{2}\,d\mu_{w,\alpha}.

∎

Lemma 3.19
.

Suppose
K
is
C^{\infty}_{+}
. For every
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
and every
f_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}})
with
\int_{\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}}f_{\varepsilon}\,d\mu_{w,\alpha}=0
, there exists
u_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}})
such that

(3.98)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\Phi}u_{\varepsilon}=-f_{\varepsilon}\quad\text{in }\Omega_{w,\varepsilon},\quad(u_{\varepsilon})_{\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}}=0\quad\text{on }\Sigma_{w,\varepsilon}.

The solution is unique in the class
\int_{\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}}u_{\varepsilon}\,d\mu_{w,\alpha}=0
.

Proof.

Due to
3.2
,

(3.99)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\Phi}u

\displaystyle=\Delta u-g(\nabla\Phi_{w,\alpha},\nabla u)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\Phi_{w,\alpha}}}{h\det(\tau)}\bar{\nabla}_{i}\left(e^{-\Phi_{w,\alpha}}h^{2}\tau^{ij}\det(\tau)\bar{\nabla}_{j}u\right).

Thus
\mathcal{L}_{\Phi}
is uniformly elliptic on
\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}
. Moreover, the boundary condition
(u_{\varepsilon})_{\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}}=0
is uniformly oblique. Indeed, for the outward normals
\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}
and
\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}
we have

(3.100)

\displaystyle\bar{g}(\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon},\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon})=\frac{\bar{g}(\nabla\ell_{w},\bar{\nabla}\ell_{w})}{|\nabla\ell_{w}|_{g}|\bar{\nabla}\ell_{w}|_{\bar{g}}}=\frac{|\nabla\ell_{w}|_{g}}{|\bar{\nabla}\ell_{w}|_{\bar{g}}}>0\quad\text{on }\Sigma_{w,\varepsilon}.

Equivalently, the inward vector
-\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}
is uniformly oblique with respect to the inward normal
-\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}
, which is the convention used in
[
Lie13
, Thm. 2.30]
.

The compatibility condition for the Neumann problem is
\int_{\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}}f_{\varepsilon}\,d\mu_{w,\alpha}=0
, which holds by assumption. For convenience, we reproduce the argument from
[
Lie13
, Thm. 2.30]
using notation adapted to our setting.

Let
L=\mathcal{L}_{\Phi}
and, in the convention of
[
Lie13
, Thm. 2.30]
, define the inward oblique boundary operator by

(3.101)

\displaystyle Mu=-g\left(\nabla u,\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}\right)

on the smooth bounded domain
\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}
. Both
L
and
M
have no zeroth-order term. Hence, for any
\kappa>0
, the shifted operators
L-\kappa
and
M-\kappa
have strictly negative zeroth-order coefficients. Therefore, by
[
Lie13
, Thm. 2.27]
,

(3.102)

\displaystyle(L-\kappa)u=f\quad\text{in }\Omega_{w,\varepsilon},\quad(M-\kappa)u=\phi\quad\text{on }\Sigma_{w,\varepsilon}

has a unique solution
u\in H^{2+\gamma}\left(\overline{\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}}\right)
for any
(f,\phi)\in\mathcal{B}:=H^{\gamma}\left(\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}\right)\times H^{1+\gamma}\left(\Sigma_{w,\varepsilon}\right)
.

Now we define the compact operator
\mathcal{T}:\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{B}
by

(3.103)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(f,\phi)=\left(u,u|_{\Sigma_{w,\varepsilon}}\right).

As in the proof of
[
Lie13
, Thm. 2.30]
, we also define

(3.104)

\displaystyle N_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\left\{(f,\phi)\in\mathcal{B}:\ (f,\phi)=\mu\mathcal{T}(f,\phi)\right\},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}

\displaystyle=\left\{\sigma=-\kappa-\mu:\ \mu\neq 0\text{ and }\dim N_{\mu}>0\right\}.

If
c
is constant and
(f,\phi)=\left(-\kappa c,-\kappa c\right)
, then
\mathcal{T}(f,\phi)=\left(c,c\right)
. Hence
\left(I+\kappa\mathcal{T}\right)(f,\phi)=(0,0)
and
\dim N_{-\kappa}\geq 1
. Therefore,
0=-\kappa-(-\kappa)\in\mathfrak{S}
.

Consider the linear functional

(3.105)

\displaystyle\Lambda(f,\phi)=\int_{\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}}f\,d\mu_{w,\alpha}+\int_{\Sigma_{w,\varepsilon}}\phi\,d\sigma_{w,\alpha,\varepsilon}.

If
(f,\phi)
is solvable, that is, if
Lu=f
in
\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}
and
Mu=\phi
on
\Sigma_{w,\varepsilon}
, then
g\left(\nabla u,\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}\right)=-\phi
, and
3.12
gives

(3.106)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}}f\,d\mu_{w,\alpha}=\int_{\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}}Lu\,d\mu_{w,\alpha}=\int_{\Sigma_{w,\varepsilon}}g\left(\nabla u,\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}\right)\,d\sigma_{w,\alpha,\varepsilon}=-\int_{\Sigma_{w,\varepsilon}}\phi\,d\sigma_{w,\alpha,\varepsilon}.

Thus every solvable datum lies in
\ker\Lambda
. On the other hand, by
3.12
, the only solutions to
Lu=0
in
\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}
and
Mu=0
on
\Sigma_{w,\varepsilon}
are the constant functions, hence
I_{0}=1
. Now
[
Lie13
, Thm. 2.30]
yields a one-dimensional subspace
W_{0}^{*}\subset\mathcal{B}^{*}
such that the problem
Lu=f
in
\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}
,
Mu=\phi
on
\Sigma_{w,\varepsilon}
has a solution if and only if
(f,\phi)\in\left(W_{0}^{*}\right)^{\perp}
. We have
W_{0}^{*}=\operatorname{span}\{\Xi\}
for some nonzero functional
\Xi\in\mathcal{B}^{*}
. Therefore the space of solvable data is exactly
\ker\Xi
and
\ker\Xi\subset\ker\Lambda
. Since
\Lambda\neq 0
, this implies that
\ker\Xi=\ker\Lambda
. Consequently, the space of solvable data is exactly
\ker\Lambda
.

In our oblique problem we have
\phi=0
. Hence, due to
\int_{\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}}f_{\varepsilon}\,d\mu_{w,\alpha}=0
, we have

(3.107)

\displaystyle\Lambda(-f_{\varepsilon},0)=-\int_{\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}}f_{\varepsilon}\,d\mu_{w,\alpha}=0.

Therefore there exists
u_{\varepsilon}\in H^{2+\gamma}\left(\overline{\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}}\right)
such that

(3.108)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\Phi}u_{\varepsilon}=-f_{\varepsilon}\quad\text{in }\Omega_{w,\varepsilon},\quad\left(u_{\varepsilon}\right)_{\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}}=0\quad\text{on }\Sigma_{w,\varepsilon}.

Since the coefficients, the boundary, and
f_{\varepsilon}
are smooth, we have
u_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}\left(\overline{\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}}\right)
. The uniqueness of the solution in the class
\int_{\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}}u_{\varepsilon}\,d\mu_{w,\alpha}=0
follows from integration by parts and connectedness of
\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}
.
∎

Theorem 3.20
.

Let
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
,
\alpha>0
,
F\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega_{w}})
. Suppose
K
is
C^{2}_{+}
. Then

(3.109)

\displaystyle(n+\alpha-1)\int_{\Omega_{w}}(F-\bar{F}_{w})^{2}\langle X,w\rangle^{\alpha}\,dV_{K}\leq\int_{\Omega_{w}}|\nabla F|_{g}^{2}\langle X,w\rangle^{\alpha}\,dV_{K},

where

(3.110)

\displaystyle\bar{F}_{w}=\frac{\int_{\Omega_{w}}F\langle X,w\rangle^{\alpha}\,dV_{K}}{\int_{\Omega_{w}}\langle X,w\rangle^{\alpha}\,dV_{K}}.

Proof.

By approximation, we may assume
F\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega_{w}})
and
K
is
C^{\infty}_{+}
. For each
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
, we define

(3.111)

\displaystyle\bar{F}_{w,\varepsilon}=\frac{\int_{\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}}F\,d\mu_{w,\alpha}}{\int_{\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}}d\mu_{w,\alpha}},\quad f_{\varepsilon}=F-\bar{F}_{w,\varepsilon}.

In view of
3.19
, there exists
u_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}})
such that

(3.112)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\Phi}u_{\varepsilon}=-f_{\varepsilon}\quad\text{in }\Omega_{w,\varepsilon},\quad(u_{\varepsilon})_{\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}}=0\quad\text{on }\Sigma_{w,\varepsilon}.

Since
\nabla f_{\varepsilon}=\nabla F
and
(u_{\varepsilon})_{\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}}=0
, due to
3.12
,

(3.113)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}}f_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,d\mu_{w,\alpha}=\int_{\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}}g(\nabla u_{\varepsilon},\nabla F)\,d\mu_{w,\alpha}.

Hence, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

(3.114)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}}(F-\bar{F}_{w,\varepsilon})^{2}\,d\mu_{w,\alpha}\leq\left(\int_{\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}}|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|_{g}^{2}\,d\mu_{w,\alpha}\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}}|\nabla F|_{g}^{2}\,d\mu_{w,\alpha}\right)^{1/2}.

On the other hand, by
3.18
,

(3.115)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}}(F-\bar{F}_{w,\varepsilon})^{2}\,d\mu_{w,\alpha}=\int_{\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}}(\mathcal{L}_{\Phi}u_{\varepsilon})^{2}\,d\mu_{w,\alpha}\geq(n+\alpha-1)\int_{\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}}|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|_{g}^{2}\,d\mu_{w,\alpha}.

Now substituting into (
3.114
) yields

(3.116)

\displaystyle(n+\alpha-1)\int_{\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}}(F-\bar{F}_{w,\varepsilon})^{2}\,d\mu_{w,\alpha}\leq\int_{\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}}|\nabla F|_{g}^{2}\,d\mu_{w,\alpha}.

Since
\mathbf{1}_{\Omega_{w,\varepsilon}}\to\mathbf{1}_{\Omega_{w}}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, and
d\mu_{w,\alpha}=\ell_{w}^{\alpha}\,dV_{K}
is finite on
\Omega_{w}
, by the dominated convergence theorem we may pass to the limit in (
3.116
).
∎

Corollary 3.21
.

Assume that
K
is a
C^{2}_{+}
origin-symmetric convex body and
F\in C^{1}(S^{n-1})
is even. Then

(3.117)

\displaystyle(n+\alpha-1)\int_{S^{n-1}}(F-\bar{F}_{w})^{2}|\langle X,w\rangle|^{\alpha}\,dV_{K}\leq\int_{S^{n-1}}|\nabla F|_{g}^{2}|\langle X,w\rangle|^{\alpha}\,dV_{K},

where
\bar{F}_{w}=\frac{\int_{S^{n-1}}F|\langle X,w\rangle|^{\alpha}\,dV_{K}}{\int_{S^{n-1}}|\langle X,w\rangle|^{\alpha}\,dV_{K}}
.

Proof.

In view of
Theorem 3.20
, we have

(3.118)

\displaystyle(n+\alpha-1)\left[\int_{\Omega_{w}}(F-\bar{F}_{w})^{2}\ell_{w}^{\alpha}\,dV_{K}+\int_{\Omega_{-w}}(F-\bar{F}_{-w})^{2}\ell_{-w}^{\alpha}\,dV_{K}\right]\leq\int_{S^{n-1}}|\nabla F|_{g}^{2}|\ell_{w}|^{\alpha}\,dV_{K}.

Since
K
is origin-symmetric and
F
is even,
\bar{F}_{w}=\bar{F}_{-w}

and

(3.119)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{w}}(F-\bar{F}_{w})^{2}\ell_{w}^{\alpha}\,dV_{K}+\int_{\Omega_{-w}}(F-\bar{F}_{-w})^{2}\ell_{-w}^{\alpha}\,dV_{K}=\int_{S^{n-1}}(F-\bar{F}_{w})^{2}|\ell_{w}|^{\alpha}\,dV_{K}.

∎

Corollary 3.22
.

Assume that
K
is a
C^{2}_{+}
unconditional convex body and
F\in C^{1}(S^{n-1})
is unconditional. Then

(3.120)

\displaystyle(n+1)\int_{S^{n-1}}(F-\bar{F})^{2}|X|^{2}\,dV_{K}\leq\int_{S^{n-1}}|\nabla F|_{g}^{2}|X|^{2}\,dV_{K},\quad\bar{F}=\frac{\int_{S^{n-1}}F|X|^{2}\,dV_{K}}{\int_{S^{n-1}}|X|^{2}\,dV_{K}}.

Proof.

Let
w=(1,\dots,1)
. By
3.21
,

(3.121)

\displaystyle(n+1)\int_{S^{n-1}}(F-\bar{F}_{w})^{2}\ell_{w}^{2}\,dV_{K}\leq\int_{S^{n-1}}|\nabla F|_{g}^{2}\ell_{w}^{2}\,dV_{K}.

We claim that
\bar{F}_{w}=\bar{F}
. Indeed, expanding
\ell_{w}^{2}
gives

(3.122)

\displaystyle\ell_{w}^{2}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right)^{2}=|X|^{2}+2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}X_{i}X_{j}.

Since both
F
and
K
are unconditional, for every
i\neq j
,

(3.123)

\displaystyle\int_{S^{n-1}}FX_{i}X_{j}\,dV_{K}=0,\quad\int_{S^{n-1}}X_{i}X_{j}\,dV_{K}=0.

Hence

(3.124)

\displaystyle\int_{S^{n-1}}F\ell_{w}^{2}\,dV_{K}=\int_{S^{n-1}}F|X|^{2}\,dV_{K},\quad\int_{S^{n-1}}\ell_{w}^{2}\,dV_{K}=\int_{S^{n-1}}|X|^{2}\,dV_{K},

and

(3.125)

\displaystyle\bar{F}_{w}=\frac{\int_{S^{n-1}}F\ell_{w}^{2}\,dV_{K}}{\int_{S^{n-1}}\ell_{w}^{2}\,dV_{K}}=\frac{\int_{S^{n-1}}F|X|^{2}\,dV_{K}}{\int_{S^{n-1}}|X|^{2}\,dV_{K}}=\bar{F}.

Moreover, both
(F-\bar{F})^{2}
and
|\nabla F|_{g}^{2}
are unconditional, so the same cancellation of mixed terms yields

(3.126)

\displaystyle\int_{S^{n-1}}(F-\bar{F})^{2}\ell_{w}^{2}\,dV_{K}

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{n-1}}(F-\bar{F})^{2}|X|^{2}\,dV_{K},

\displaystyle\int_{S^{n-1}}|\nabla F|_{g}^{2}\ell_{w}^{2}\,dV_{K}

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{n-1}}|\nabla F|_{g}^{2}|X|^{2}\,dV_{K}.

∎

Remark 3.23
.

Let
\Phi=-\alpha\log|X|
with
\alpha>0
. Using the identities
\nabla^{2}X+gX=0
and
dX=\tau
, we compute

(3.127)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\Phi=\alpha g-\frac{\alpha}{|X|^{2}}\bar{g}(\tau(\cdot),\tau(\cdot))+2\alpha\,d\log|X|\otimes d\log|X|.

Hence, for any
N>n-1
,

(3.128)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\Phi-\frac{1}{N-(n-1)}d\Phi\otimes d\Phi=

\displaystyle\alpha g-\frac{\alpha}{|X|^{2}}\bar{g}(\tau(\cdot),\tau(\cdot))

\displaystyle+\left(2\alpha-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{N-(n-1)}\right)d\log|X|\otimes d\log|X|.

Choosing
N=n-1+\frac{\alpha}{2}
cancels the last term and gives

(3.129)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ric}_{\Phi,N}:=\operatorname{Ric}+\nabla^{2}\Phi-\frac{1}{N-(n-1)}d\Phi\otimes d\Phi=(n-2+\alpha)g-\frac{\alpha}{|X|^{2}}\bar{g}(\tau(\cdot),\tau(\cdot)).

The final term has the wrong sign and, in general, cannot be controlled from below by a positive multiple of
g
without an additional pinching assumption. Thus the global weighted Bochner formula does not seem well suited for proving
Theorem 1.2
. This motivates the cap-based argument developed in this section, which yields
3.22
.

3.2.
Poincaré inequalities on intersections of caps

We extend the cap-based Bochner method used above to prove
Theorem 3.20
to intersections of caps and power weights, and then prove
Theorem 1.1
. As we will see, this extension plays a crucial role in deriving Poincaré-type inequalities for unconditional convex bodies here and in
section 8
.

Let

(3.130)

\displaystyle\ell_{w_{i}}=\langle X,w_{i}\rangle,\quad\Omega=\bigcap_{i=1}^{m}\{\ell_{w_{i}}>0\},\quad\Phi=-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}\log\ell_{w_{i}},\quad a=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i},

where
w_{1},\dots,w_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
and
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}>0
.
Since
\nabla^{2}\ell_{w_{i}}=-\ell_{w_{i}}g
,

(3.131)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\Phi-\frac{1}{a}\,d\Phi\otimes d\Phi

\displaystyle=ag+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}\,d\log\ell_{w_{i}}\otimes d\log\ell_{w_{i}}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{a}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}d\log\ell_{w_{i}}\right)\otimes\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}d\log\ell_{w_{i}}\right).

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,
\nabla^{2}\Phi-\frac{1}{a}\,d\Phi\otimes d\Phi\geq ag
.
Therefore

(3.132)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ric}+\nabla^{2}\Phi-\frac{1}{a}\,d\Phi\otimes d\Phi\geq(n-2+a)g.

This suggests a weighted Poincaré inequality on
\Omega
with spectral constant
n+a-1
.

The main additional difficulty, compared to the single-cap case, is that
\Omega
has only piecewise smooth boundary. We therefore approximate
\Omega
from inside by a family of smooth domains
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
. The key point is that this approximation must be chosen so that, in the weighted Bochner argument on
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
, the
\nabla^{*}
-second fundamental form of
\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}
is non-negative definite. In the single-cap case, this sign ultimately comes from the concavity of the defining function
\ell_{w}
, namely
\nabla^{2}\ell_{w}=-\ell_{w}g\leq 0
.

For intersections of several caps, one therefore needs a smooth defining function for the approximation that enjoys the same concavity property.

Theorem 3.24
.

Suppose
K
is
C^{2}_{+}
. With the notation above, assume that
\Omega\neq\emptyset
, and set

(3.133)

\displaystyle d\mu_{\Phi}=e^{-\Phi}\,dV_{K}=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\ell_{w_{i}}^{\alpha_{i}}\,dV_{K}.

Then for every
F\in C^{1}(S^{n-1})
,

(3.134)

\displaystyle(n+a-1)\int_{\Omega}(F-\bar{F}_{\Phi})^{2}\,d\mu_{\Phi}\leq\int_{\Omega}|\nabla F|_{g}^{2}\,d\mu_{\Phi},\quad\bar{F}_{\Phi}=\frac{\int_{\Omega}F\,d\mu_{\Phi}}{\int_{\Omega}d\mu_{\Phi}}.

Proof.

We may assume
F\in C^{\infty}(S^{n-1})
and
K
is
C^{\infty}_{+}
. Let
q(x)=\min_{1\leq i\leq m}\ell_{w_{i}}(x)
. For
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small, on
S^{n-1}
we define

(3.135)

\displaystyle q_{\varepsilon}=-\varepsilon\log\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}e^{-\ell_{w_{i}}/\varepsilon}\right).

Note that we have

(3.136)

\displaystyle q_{\varepsilon}(x)\leq q(x)\leq q_{\varepsilon}(x)+\varepsilon\log m,\quad\text{for all }x\in S^{n-1}.

Due to Sard’s theorem, for each
\varepsilon>0
there exists a regular value
c(\varepsilon)\in(0,\varepsilon)
. We now define smooth open sets
\Omega_{\varepsilon}=\{q_{\varepsilon}>c(\varepsilon)\}
. We next show that they are connected.

Claim 3.25
.

\Omega_{\varepsilon}
is connected.

Proof.

Let
x_{0},x_{1}\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}
be arbitrary. We will construct a continuous curve in
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
connecting
x_{0}
to
x_{1}
. Set
p_{0}=X(x_{0})
and
p_{1}=X(x_{1})
. For
t\in[0,1]
, define
p_{t}=(1-t)p_{0}+tp_{1}
. The points
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
belong to
\partial K
, and therefore the convexity of
K
implies that
p_{t}\in K
for every
t\in[0,1]
.

Since
x_{0},x_{1}\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}
, for
j=0,1
we have

(3.137)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}e^{-\ell_{w_{i}}(x_{j})/\varepsilon}<e^{-c(\varepsilon)/\varepsilon}.

Hence
\ell_{w_{i}}(x_{j})>c(\varepsilon)>0
for every
i=1,\ldots,m
and
j=0,1
. It follows that

(3.138)

\displaystyle\langle p_{t},w_{i}\rangle=(1-t)\langle p_{0},w_{i}\rangle+t\langle p_{1},w_{i}\rangle=(1-t)\ell_{w_{i}}(x_{0})+t\ell_{w_{i}}(x_{1})>c(\varepsilon)>0\quad\forall t\in[0,1].

In particular,
p_{t}\neq 0
for every
t\in[0,1]
. We write

(3.139)

\displaystyle p_{t}=r_{t}\theta_{t},\text{ where }r_{t}=|p_{t}|>0\text{ and }\theta_{t}=p_{t}/|p_{t}|\in S^{n-1}.

Let
\rho_{K}
denote the radial function of
K
. Then
r_{t}\leq\rho_{K}(\theta_{t})
. Hence, if we set

(3.140)

\displaystyle\lambda_{t}=\frac{\rho_{K}(\theta_{t})}{r_{t}}\geq 1,

then
\widehat{p}_{t}=\lambda_{t}p_{t}\in\partial K
. Now we define
x_{t}=X^{-1}(\widehat{p}_{t})
. Since
X
is a diffeomorphism, the curve
t\mapsto x_{t}
is continuous. We prove that this curve stays in
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
.

For each
i
,

(3.141)

\displaystyle e^{-\langle p_{t},w_{i}\rangle/\varepsilon}=e^{-((1-t)\ell_{w_{i}}(x_{0})+t\ell_{w_{i}}(x_{1}))/\varepsilon}\leq(1-t)e^{-\ell_{w_{i}}(x_{0})/\varepsilon}+te^{-\ell_{w_{i}}(x_{1})/\varepsilon}.

Summing this inequality over
i=1,\ldots,m
gives

(3.142)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}e^{-\langle p_{t},w_{i}\rangle/\varepsilon}\leq(1-t)\sum_{i=1}^{m}e^{-\ell_{w_{i}}(x_{0})/\varepsilon}+t\sum_{i=1}^{m}e^{-\ell_{w_{i}}(x_{1})/\varepsilon}<e^{-c(\varepsilon)/\varepsilon}.

Next, since
\langle p_{t},w_{i}\rangle>c(\varepsilon)>0
, and
\lambda_{t}\geq 1
, we have

(3.143)

\displaystyle e^{-\langle\widehat{p}_{t},w_{i}\rangle/\varepsilon}=e^{-\lambda_{t}\langle p_{t},w_{i}\rangle/\varepsilon}\leq e^{-\langle p_{t},w_{i}\rangle/\varepsilon}.

Consequently,

(3.144)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}e^{-\ell_{w_{i}}(x_{t})/\varepsilon}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}e^{-\langle X(x_{t}),w_{i}\rangle/\varepsilon}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}e^{-\langle\widehat{p}_{t},w_{i}\rangle/\varepsilon}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}e^{-\langle p_{t},w_{i}\rangle/\varepsilon}<e^{-c(\varepsilon)/\varepsilon}.

That is,
x_{t}\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}
for every
t\in[0,1]
.
∎

By (
3.136
), we have
\Omega_{\varepsilon}\subset\Omega
. Conversely, let
x\in\Omega
. Then
q(x)>0
. Moreover, for all sufficiently small
\varepsilon
, we have
q_{\varepsilon}(x)\geq q(x)-\varepsilon\log m>\varepsilon>c(\varepsilon)
, and hence
x\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}
. We have shown

(3.145)

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}(x)\to\mathbf{1}_{\Omega}(x)\quad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0.

We next verify that
\nabla^{2}q_{\varepsilon}\leq 0
. For convenience, we set

(3.146)

\displaystyle\theta_{i}=\frac{e^{-\ell_{w_{i}}/\varepsilon}}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}e^{-\ell_{w_{j}}/\varepsilon}},\quad i=1,\ldots,m.

Using
\nabla^{2}\ell_{w_{i}}=-\ell_{w_{i}}g
, we calculate

(3.147)

\displaystyle\nabla q_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\theta_{i}\nabla\ell_{w_{i}},

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}q_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\theta_{i}\ell_{w_{i}}\right)g-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\theta_{i}\,d\ell_{w_{i}}\otimes d\ell_{w_{i}}-\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}\theta_{i}\theta_{j}\,d\ell_{w_{i}}\otimes d\ell_{w_{j}}\right).

Due to
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\theta_{i}=1
and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, the second term is non-positive definite. Therefore

(3.148)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}q_{\varepsilon}\leq 0\quad\text{on }\Omega.

Let
\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}=-\frac{\nabla q_{\varepsilon}}{|\nabla q_{\varepsilon}|_{g}}
be the outward
g
-unit normal to
\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}
. For vector fields
Z
that are tangential along
\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}
, due to (
3.148
), we have

(3.149)

\displaystyle\mathrm{II}^{*}_{\varepsilon}(Z,Z)=g(Z,\nabla_{Z}^{*}\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon})=-\frac{1}{|\nabla q_{\varepsilon}|_{g}}\nabla^{2}q_{\varepsilon}(Z,Z)\geq 0.

Now applying the weighted Bochner formula on the smooth domain
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
, exactly as in the proof of
Theorem 3.14
, we obtain

(3.150)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}(\mathcal{L}_{\Phi}u)^{2}\,d\mu_{\Phi}\geq(n+a-1)\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}|\nabla u|_{g}^{2}\,d\mu_{\Phi}

for every
u\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega_{\varepsilon}})
with
u_{\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}}=0
on
\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}
.

Define
\bar{F}_{\Phi,\varepsilon}=\frac{\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}F\,d\mu_{\Phi}}{\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}d\mu_{\Phi}}
and
f_{\varepsilon}=F-\bar{F}_{\Phi,\varepsilon}
. Then there exists a solution
u_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega_{\varepsilon}})
to

(3.151)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\Phi}u_{\varepsilon}=-f_{\varepsilon}\quad\text{in }\Omega_{\varepsilon},\quad(u_{\varepsilon})_{\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}}=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}.

Applying (
3.150
) to
u_{\varepsilon}
, integrating by parts, and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we obtain

(3.152)

\displaystyle(n+a-1)\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}(F-\bar{F}_{\Phi,\varepsilon})^{2}\,d\mu_{\Phi}\leq\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}|\nabla F|_{g}^{2}\,d\mu_{\Phi}.

Due to (
3.145
) and the dominated convergence theorem, we may let
\varepsilon\to 0
to obtain

(3.153)

\displaystyle(n+a-1)\int_{\Omega}(F-\bar{F}_{\Phi})^{2}\,d\mu_{\Phi}\leq\int_{\Omega}|\nabla F|_{g}^{2}\,d\mu_{\Phi}.

∎

In
Theorem 3.24
, consider the special case that
w_{i}=E_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, where
\{E_{i}\}
is the standard coordinate basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then the Poincaré constant can be improved to
n+a
. Indeed, for an unconditional convex body
Q
, define
|Q|_{\bar{\alpha},+}=\int_{Q\cap(0,\infty)^{n}}x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}\,dx
and

(3.154)

\displaystyle f_{Q,\bar{\alpha}}(z)=\mathbf{1}_{Q\cap(0,\infty)^{n}}(e^{z_{1}},\ldots,e^{z_{n}})e^{(\alpha_{1}+1)z_{1}+\cdots+(\alpha_{n}+1)z_{n}},\quad z\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
\bar{\alpha}:=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
and we additionally allow
\alpha_{i}>-1
. Then, by the change of variables
x_{i}=e^{z_{i}}
, we have
|Q|_{\bar{\alpha},+}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f_{Q,\bar{\alpha}}(z)\,dz
.

Since the density in
f_{Q,\bar{\alpha}}
is log-affine, the argument of
[
Sar15
]
based on the Prékopa–Leindler inequality gives

(3.155)

\displaystyle\big|(1-\lambda)\cdot K+_{0}\lambda\cdot L\big|_{\bar{\alpha},+}\geq|K|_{\bar{\alpha},+}^{1-\lambda}|L|_{\bar{\alpha},+}^{\lambda}.

For
|s|
sufficiently small,
h_{s}=he^{sF}
is the support function of a
C^{2}_{+}
unconditional convex body
K_{s}
, and by the definition of the
L_{0}
-sum,

(3.156)

\displaystyle(1-\lambda)\cdot K_{s}+_{0}\lambda\cdot K_{t}=K_{(1-\lambda)s+\lambda t}.

Hence (
3.155
) implies that

(3.157)

\displaystyle\big|K_{(1-\lambda)s+\lambda t}\big|_{\bar{\alpha},+}\geq|K_{s}|_{\bar{\alpha},+}^{1-\lambda}|K_{t}|_{\bar{\alpha},+}^{\lambda}.

That is,
s\mapsto\log|K_{s}|_{\bar{\alpha},+}
is concave for
|s|
sufficiently small. We may now differentiate at
s=0
to obtain the corresponding weighted Poincaré inequality with the improved constant
n+a
. See also
Theorem 8.14
for an alternative proof that does not rely on the Prékopa–Leindler inequality.

Remark 3.26
.

The inequality of
Theorem 3.24
may be interpreted as an infinitesimal version of a Brunn–Minkowski-type inequality. For
a=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}
, consider the functional

(3.158)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(L)=\int_{L}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\langle x,w_{i}\rangle_{+}^{\alpha_{i}}\,dx.

The Borell–Brascamp–Lieb inequality then gives the concavity of
\mathcal{F}^{1/(n+a)}
. Therefore, formally differentiating this statement along a linear variation
h_{s}=h+s\psi
, with
F=\psi/h
, leads to the same weighted Poincaré constant
n+a-1
as in
Theorem 3.24
.

There is, however, a technical nuisance in this variational interpretation. The Gauss image is the moving intersection

(3.159)

\displaystyle\Omega_{s}=\bigcap_{i=1}^{m}\{\ell_{i,s}>0\},\quad\ell_{i,s}=\langle X_{s},w_{i}\rangle.

Its boundary is generally only piecewise smooth, and differentiating the weight
\prod_{i=1}^{m}(\ell_{i,s})_{+}^{\alpha_{i}}
requires keeping track of weak derivatives near the faces and corners. Although the singular factors
(\ell_{i,s})^{\alpha_{i}-1}
are locally integrable for
\alpha_{i}>0
, and one expects this approach to yield the same result after a suitable approximation or weak differentiation argument, the full details are not clear to us.

The above cap-based proof overcomes these issues conveniently and provides an intrinsic derivation of the preceding Poincaré inequality.

In the capillary setting, this variational viewpoint becomes manageable, because the Gauss image is fixed and the admissible variations are well posed via a Robin boundary condition. We will not pursue this direction here.

Proof of
Theorem 1.1
.

Set
\ell=(\ell_{w_{1}},\dots,\ell_{w_{m}})
. We calculate

(3.160)

\displaystyle\nabla\Phi=-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\partial_{i}(\log\omega)(\ell)\nabla\ell_{w_{i}}

and

(3.161)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\nabla^{2}\Phi-\frac{1}{\alpha}d\Phi\otimes d\Phi=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ell_{w_{i}}\partial_{i}(\log\omega)(\ell)\right)g\\
-\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}\left(\partial_{ij}^{2}(\log\omega)+\frac{1}{\alpha}\partial_{i}(\log\omega)\partial_{j}(\log\omega)\right)(\ell)\,d\ell_{w_{i}}\otimes d\ell_{w_{j}}.\end{multlined}

Note that (
1.5
) says

(3.162)

\displaystyle D^{2}\log\omega+\frac{1}{\alpha}D\log\omega\otimes D\log\omega=\alpha\omega^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}D^{2}\omega^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\leq 0.

Therefore, the second term is non-negative definite, and by (
1.6
),

(3.163)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\Phi-\frac{1}{\alpha}d\Phi\otimes d\Phi\geq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ell_{w_{i}}\partial_{i}(\log\omega)(\ell)\right)g\geq\beta g.

Since
\operatorname{Ric}=(n-2)g
, it follows that
\operatorname{Ric}+\nabla^{2}\Phi-\frac{1}{\alpha}d\Phi\otimes d\Phi\geq(n-2+\beta)g
. Using this and the finite-
N
estimate (see the proof of
3.17
), we obtain

(3.164)

\displaystyle|(\nabla^{*})^{2}u|_{g}^{2}+(\operatorname{Ric}+\nabla^{2}\Phi)(\nabla u,\nabla u)\geq\frac{1}{n-1+\alpha}(\mathcal{L}_{\Phi}u)^{2}+(n-2+\beta)|\nabla u|_{g}^{2}.

The rest of the proof is identical to that of
Theorem 3.24
.
∎

Corollary 3.27
.

Let
0<\alpha\leq 1
. Assume that
K
is a
C^{2}_{+}
unconditional convex body, and that
F\in C^{1}(S^{n-1})
is unconditional. Then

(3.165)

\displaystyle(n+\alpha-1)\int_{S^{n-1}}(F-\bar{F})^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|X_{i}|^{\alpha}\,dV_{K}\leq\int_{S^{n-1}}|\nabla F|_{g}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|X_{i}|^{\alpha}\,dV_{K},

where

(3.166)

\displaystyle\bar{F}=\frac{\int_{S^{n-1}}F\sum_{i=1}^{n}|X_{i}|^{\alpha}\,dV_{K}}{\int_{S^{n-1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|X_{i}|^{\alpha}\,dV_{K}}.

Proof.

We apply
Theorem 1.1
on
\Omega_{+}=\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\{X_{i}>0\}
, with
w_{i}=E_{i}
and the weight
\omega(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}^{\alpha}
. Since
\omega^{1/\alpha}
is concave on
(0,\infty)^{n}
for
0<\alpha\leq 1
, the matrix condition holds. Moreover, since
\omega
is homogeneous of degree
\alpha
,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}\partial_{i}\log\omega(t)=\alpha
. Hence

(3.167)

\displaystyle(n+\alpha-1)\int_{\Omega_{+}}(F-\bar{F}_{\Omega_{+}})^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{\alpha}\,dV_{K}\leq\int_{\Omega_{+}}|\nabla F|_{g}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{\alpha}\,dV_{K},

where
\bar{F}_{\Omega_{+}}=\frac{\int_{\Omega_{+}}F\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{\alpha}\,dV_{K}}{\int_{\Omega_{+}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{\alpha}\,dV_{K}}
. Since
K
is unconditional and
F
is unconditional, all orthants contribute equally.
∎

Remark 3.28
.

Assume that
\tilde{f}\in C^{2}\left((0,\infty)^{n}\right)
is positive, concave, and
k
-homogeneous on
(0,\infty)^{n}
. Define
f(x)=\tilde{f}\left(X(x)\right),\,\Phi(x)=-\alpha\log f(x)
on

, where

. By

,

(3.168)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\Phi-\frac{1}{\alpha}d\Phi\otimes d\Phi=-\frac{\alpha}{f}\nabla^{2}f\geq\alpha kg\quad\text{on }\Omega_{+}.

For example, we may take

on

. Then

),

(3.169)

\displaystyle(n+\alpha-1)\int_{S^{n-1}}(F-\bar{F})^{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}|X_{i}|\right)^{\alpha}\,dV_{K}\leq\int_{S^{n-1}}|\nabla F|_{g}^{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}|X_{i}|\right)^{\alpha}\,dV_{K},

.

Remark 3.29
.

There is a complementary formulation of the cap argument obtained by duality, in which the roles of
\nabla
and
\nabla^{*}
are interchanged and the measure
dV_{K}
is replaced by

. In this setting the exact analogue of the function

, which is dual to

, one can obtain analogous cap inequalities in the dual setting.

4.
Second variation in centro-affine geometry

Let
W:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a smooth function, and consider the associated weighted measure and its total mass on a smooth strictly convex body
K
by

(4.1)

\displaystyle d\mu(x)=e^{-W(x)}\,dx,\quad\mu(K)=\int_{K}e^{-W(x)}\,dx,\quad d\mu_{\partial K}=e^{-W}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\big|_{\partial K}.

Let
\psi\in C^{2}(S^{n-1})
, and for
|s|
sufficiently small let
K_{s}
be the convex body with support function
h_{K_{s}}=h_{K}+s\psi
. We define

(4.2)

\displaystyle\phi=\psi\circ\nu\quad\text{i.e.}\quad\psi=\phi\circ X.

Here
\nu=X^{-1}
denotes the outward unit normal vector field on
\partial K
.

We recall the following second-variation formula (cf.
[
KM18
, Thm. 6.6]
,
[
SZ25
, Eq. (10)]
):

(4.3)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}\mu(K_{s})\right|_{s=0}=\int_{\partial K}\left(H_{W}\phi^{2}-\langle\mathrm{II}^{-1}\nabla_{\partial K}\phi,\nabla_{\partial K}\phi\rangle\right)\,d\mu_{\partial K},

where
H_{W}=H-\langle DW,\nu\rangle
,
\mathrm{II}
denotes the second fundamental form of the boundary
\partial K
,
\nabla_{\partial K}
is the tangential gradient on
\partial K
, and
H
denotes the mean curvature of
\partial K
.

In this section we rewrite the second variation formula (
4.3
) on the sphere
S^{n-1}
and express it in the language of centro-affine geometry. Recall first that the pullback of the Euclidean surface area measure under the inverse Gauss map
X
is
X^{*}\left(d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\big|_{\partial K}\right)=dS_{K}
. Consequently

(4.4)

\displaystyle X^{*}(d\mu_{\partial K})=X^{*}\left(e^{-W}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\big|_{\partial K}\right)=e^{-W(X)}\,dS_{K}=e^{-w}\,dS_{K},

where
w
on the sphere is defined by
w=W\circ X
.

Lemma 4.1
.

We have

(4.5)

\displaystyle\int_{\partial K}\phi\,d\mu_{\partial K}=\int_{S^{n-1}}\psi\frac{e^{-w}}{h}\,dV_{K},

(4.6)

\displaystyle(H_{W}\circ X)(x)=\operatorname{tr}(\tau^{-1})(x)-\langle DW(X(x)),x\rangle,

(4.7)

\displaystyle\left(\langle\mathrm{II}^{-1}\nabla_{\partial K}\phi,\nabla_{\partial K}\phi\rangle\right)\circ X=\frac{1}{h}\left|\nabla\psi\right|_{g}^{2}.

Proof.

The first identity follows from
X^{*}(d\mu_{\partial K})=\frac{e^{-w}}{h}\,dV_{K}
, together with
\psi=\phi\circ X
.

Next we prove (
4.6
). At the point
X(x)\in\partial K
, the outer unit normal is
x
. Let
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n-1}
be a local
\bar{g}
-orthonormal frame diagonalizing
\tau
, say
\tau e_{i}=\lambda_{i}e_{i}
. Then the principal curvatures are
\lambda_{i}^{-1}
, and
H\circ X=\operatorname{tr}(\tau^{-1})
. Since
\nu\circ X(x)=x
, it follows that

(4.8)

\displaystyle H_{W}\circ X=H\circ X-\langle DW(X),x\rangle=\operatorname{tr}(\tau^{-1})-\langle DW(X),x\rangle.

We prove (
4.7
). Let
\hat{e}_{i}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}}dX(e_{i})
. Then
\{\hat{e}_{i}\}
is an orthonormal basis for
T_{X(x)}\partial K
and

(4.9)

\displaystyle\hat{e}_{i}\phi=d\phi(\hat{e}_{i})=\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}}d(\phi\circ X)(e_{i})=\frac{e_{i}\psi}{\lambda_{i}}.

Since the eigenvalues of
\mathrm{II}^{-1}
are
\lambda_{i}
, from (
3.2
) we obtain

(4.10)

\displaystyle\left(\langle\mathrm{II}^{-1}\nabla_{\partial K}\phi,\nabla_{\partial K}\phi\rangle\right)\circ X=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{i}(\hat{e}_{i}\phi)^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\frac{(e_{i}\psi)^{2}}{\lambda_{i}}=\frac{1}{h}\left|\nabla\psi\right|_{g}^{2}.

∎

Proposition 4.2
.

The formula (
4.3
) is equivalent to

(4.11)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}\mu(K_{s})\right|_{s=0}

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{n-1}}\left(\left(\operatorname{tr}(\tau^{-1})-\langle DW(X),x\rangle\right)\psi^{2}\right)\frac{e^{-w}}{h}\,dV_{K}

\displaystyle-\int_{S^{n-1}}\frac{e^{-w}}{h^{2}}\left|\nabla\psi\right|_{g}^{2}\,dV_{K}.

Proof.

Using
4.1
, we compute

(4.12)

\displaystyle\int_{\partial K}H_{W}\phi^{2}\,d\mu_{\partial K}

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{n-1}}\left(\operatorname{tr}(\tau^{-1})-\langle DW(X),x\rangle\right)\psi^{2}\frac{e^{-w}}{h}\,dV_{K},

\displaystyle\int_{\partial K}\langle\mathrm{II}^{-1}\nabla_{\partial K}\phi,\nabla_{\partial K}\phi\rangle\,d\mu_{\partial K}

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{n-1}}\frac{1}{h}\left|\nabla\psi\right|_{g}^{2}\frac{e^{-w}}{h}\,dV_{K}=\int_{S^{n-1}}\frac{e^{-w}}{h^{2}}\left|\nabla\psi\right|_{g}^{2}\,dV_{K}.

Substituting these identities into (
4.3
) yields (
4.11
).
∎

We now specialize to the context of the dual quermassintegrals where

(4.13)

\displaystyle W(x):=(n-q)\log\left|x\right|,\quad w(x)=W\circ X(x)=(n-q)\log\left|X(x)\right|.

Although
W
is singular at the origin, the singularity is irrelevant for the first and second variation calculations. Indeed, for
|s|
sufficiently small, the support functions
h_{s}=h+s\psi
remain uniformly positive. Hence there exists
r>0
such that

(4.14)

\displaystyle B_{r}\subset K_{s}\quad\text{for all sufficiently small }|s|.

We may now choose
\widetilde{W}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that

(4.15)

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}=W\quad\text{on }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{r}.

Then

(4.16)

\displaystyle\int_{K_{s}}e^{-W}\,dx-\int_{K_{s}}e^{-\widetilde{W}}\,dx=\int_{B_{r}}\left(e^{-W}-e^{-\widetilde{W}}\right)\,dx.

Since the right-hand side is independent of
s
,
4.2
applies to
W
.

Define

(4.17)

\displaystyle f=\frac{\psi}{h}e^{-w},\quad F=\frac{\psi}{h}=e^{w}f=\left|X\right|^{n-q}f.

The
L^{q}
cone-volume measure is defined by

(4.18)

\displaystyle dV_{K,q}=\left|X\right|^{q-n}\,dV_{K}=e^{-w}\,dV_{K}.

With the notation above,

(4.19)

\displaystyle\int_{S^{n-1}}\frac{\psi}{h}e^{-w}\,dV_{K}=\int_{S^{n-1}}f\,dV_{K}=\int_{S^{n-1}}F\,dV_{K,q}.

Moreover,

(4.20)

\displaystyle\nabla F=e^{w}\left(\nabla f+f\nabla w\right).

Lemma 4.3
.

We have
g\left(\nabla\log h,\nabla\log\left|X\right|\right)=1-\frac{h^{2}}{\left|X\right|^{2}}
.

Proof.

Choose a local
\bar{g}
-orthonormal frame
\{e_{i}\}
diagonalizing
\tau
, with
\tau e_{i}=\lambda_{i}e_{i}
. By (
3.2
),

(4.21)

\displaystyle g(\nabla a,\nabla b)=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\frac{h}{\lambda_{i}}(e_{i}a)(e_{i}b).

Due to (
2.1
),
e_{i}(\left|X\right|^{2})=2\langle X,dX(e_{i})\rangle=2\lambda_{i}\langle X,e_{i}\rangle
. Therefore

(4.22)

\displaystyle e_{i}(\log\left|X\right|)=\frac{1}{2\left|X\right|^{2}}e_{i}(\left|X\right|^{2})=\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\left|X\right|^{2}}e_{i}h.

Combining the last two identities, we obtain

(4.23)

\displaystyle g\left(\nabla\log h,\nabla\log\left|X\right|\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\frac{(e_{i}h)^{2}}{\left|X\right|^{2}}=\frac{\left|\bar{\nabla}h\right|^{2}}{\left|X\right|^{2}}=1-\frac{h^{2}}{\left|X\right|^{2}}.

∎

Proposition 4.4
.

The formula (
4.11
) can be rewritten as

(4.24)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}\mu(K_{s})\right|_{s=0}

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{n-1}}(q-1)F^{2}-\left|\nabla F\right|_{g}^{2}\,dV_{K,q}.

Proof.

First we substitute
\psi=he^{w}f
into (
4.11
):

(4.25)

\displaystyle\left(\operatorname{tr}(\tau^{-1})-\langle DW(X),x\rangle\right)\psi^{2}\frac{e^{-w}}{h}\,dV_{K}=\left(\operatorname{tr}(\tau^{-1})-\langle DW(X),x\rangle\right)he^{w}f^{2}\,dV_{K}.

Next we rewrite the gradient term:

(4.26)

\displaystyle\nabla\psi=\nabla(he^{w}f)=he^{w}\left(\nabla f+f\nabla\log h+f\nabla w\right),

so

(4.27)

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-w}}{h^{2}}\left|\nabla\psi\right|_{g}^{2}=e^{w}\left|\nabla f+f\nabla\log h+f\nabla w\right|_{g}^{2}.

Note that (see (
3.14
))

(4.28)

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}(\tau^{-1})=\Delta\frac{1}{h}+(n-1)\frac{1}{h}.

Using this and integrating by parts, we obtain

(4.29)

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\int_{S^{n-1}}he^{w}f^{2}\Delta\frac{1}{h}\,dV_{K}

\displaystyle=-\int_{S^{n-1}}g\left(\nabla\frac{1}{h},\nabla(he^{w}f^{2})\right)\,dV_{K}

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{n-1}}e^{w}\left(f^{2}g(\nabla\log h,\nabla w)+2fg(\nabla\log h,\nabla f)+f^{2}\left|\nabla\log h\right|_{g}^{2}\right)\,dV_{K}.

We also rewrite (
4.27
) as follows:

(4.30)

\displaystyle\left|\nabla f+f\nabla\log h+f\nabla w\right|_{g}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left|\nabla f+f\nabla w\right|_{g}^{2}+f^{2}\left|\nabla\log h\right|_{g}^{2}

\displaystyle+2fg(\nabla f,\nabla\log h)+2f^{2}g(\nabla w,\nabla\log h).

Now substituting (
4.28
), (
4.29
), and (
4.30
) into (
4.11
), we obtain

(4.31)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}\mu(K_{s})\right|_{s=0}=\int_{S^{n-1}}e^{w}\left(-\left|\nabla f+f\nabla w\right|_{g}^{2}+\mathcal{R}_{q}f^{2}\right)\,dV_{K},

where
\mathcal{R}_{q}=(n-1)-h\langle DW(X),x\rangle-g(\nabla\log h,\nabla w)
.

We have

(4.32)

\displaystyle DW(x)=(n-q)\frac{x}{\left|x\right|^{2}},\quad\nabla w=(n-q)\nabla\log\left|X\right|

and thus

(4.33)

\displaystyle h\langle DW(X),x\rangle=(n-q)\frac{h^{2}}{\left|X\right|^{2}},\quad g(\nabla\log h,\nabla w)=(n-q)\left(1-\frac{h^{2}}{\left|X\right|^{2}}\right),

where we used
4.3
. Therefore
\mathcal{R}_{q}=q-1
and (
4.31
) becomes

(4.34)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}\mu(K_{s})\right|_{s=0}=\int_{S^{n-1}}e^{w}\left(-\left|\nabla f+f\nabla w\right|_{g}^{2}+(q-1)f^{2}\right)\,dV_{K}.

Finally, by (
4.20
),
\left|\nabla f+f\nabla w\right|_{g}^{2}=e^{-2w}\left|\nabla F\right|_{g}^{2}
. Now recall
F=e^{w}f
and
dV_{K,q}=e^{-w}dV_{K}
, and hence

(4.35)

\displaystyle e^{w}\left|\nabla f+f\nabla w\right|_{g}^{2}\,dV_{K}=\left|\nabla F\right|_{g}^{2}\,dV_{K,q},\quad e^{w}f^{2}\,dV_{K}=F^{2}\,dV_{K,q}.

∎

5.
Applications of BBL and PL inequalities

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\alpha>0
and
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
, and define
\mathcal{H}_{w}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\langle x,w\rangle\geq 0\}
. Suppose
K,L
are convex bodies contained in
\mathcal{H}_{w}
. Then

(5.1)

\displaystyle\left(\int_{K+L}\langle x,w\rangle^{\alpha}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{n+\alpha}}\geq\left(\int_{K}\langle x,w\rangle^{\alpha}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{n+\alpha}}+\left(\int_{L}\langle x,w\rangle^{\alpha}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{n+\alpha}}.

Proof.

Let
\lambda\in(0,1)
and
A,B\subset\mathcal{H}_{w}
be convex bodies:

(5.2)

\displaystyle\left(\int_{(1-\lambda)A+\lambda B}\langle x,w\rangle^{\alpha}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{n+\alpha}}\geq(1-\lambda)\left(\int_{A}\langle x,w\rangle^{\alpha}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{n+\alpha}}+\lambda\left(\int_{B}\langle x,w\rangle^{\alpha}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{n+\alpha}}.

Define

(5.3)

\displaystyle\mathrm{f}(x)=\mathbf{1}_{A}(x)\langle x,w\rangle^{\alpha},\quad\mathrm{g}(y)=\mathbf{1}_{B}(y)\langle y,w\rangle^{\alpha},\quad\mathrm{h}(z)=\mathbf{1}_{(1-\lambda)A+\lambda B}(z)\langle z,w\rangle^{\alpha}.

Then for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

(5.4)

\displaystyle\mathrm{h}((1-\lambda)x+\lambda y)\geq\mathrm{M}_{\frac{1}{\alpha}}^{\lambda}(\mathrm{f}(x),\mathrm{g}(y)),

where, for
s\neq 0
,

(5.5)

\displaystyle\mathrm{M}_{s}^{\lambda}(a,b)=\begin{cases}\left((1-\lambda)a^{s}+\lambda b^{s}\right)^{\frac{1}{s}},&ab>0,\\
0,&ab=0.\end{cases}

Therefore, by the Borell–Brascamp–Lieb inequality with
s=\alpha^{-1}
(see, e.g.
[
ILS25
]
),

(5.6)

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathrm{h}\right)^{\frac{1}{n+\alpha}}\geq(1-\lambda)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathrm{f}\right)^{\frac{1}{n+\alpha}}+\lambda\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathrm{g}\right)^{\frac{1}{n+\alpha}}.

Now we apply (
5.2
) to the dilated sets
A=\frac{1}{1-\lambda}K
and
B=\frac{1}{\lambda}L
:

(5.7)

\displaystyle\left(\int_{K+L}\langle x,w\rangle^{\alpha}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{n+\alpha}}\geq(1-\lambda)\left(\int_{A}\langle x,w\rangle^{\alpha}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{n+\alpha}}+\lambda\left(\int_{B}\langle x,w\rangle^{\alpha}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{n+\alpha}}.

Using the homogeneity of the weighted integral under dilations, the claim follows.
∎

Second proof of
Theorem 1.2
(with equality characterization).

Let
w=(1,\dots,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For every unconditional measurable set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

(5.8)

\displaystyle\int_{A\cap\mathcal{H}_{w}}\langle x,w\rangle^{2}\,dx=\frac{1}{2}\int_{A}|x|^{2}\,dx.

Note that

(5.9)

\displaystyle(K\cap\mathcal{H}_{w})+(L\cap\mathcal{H}_{w})\subset(K+L)\cap\mathcal{H}_{w}.

Applying
5.1
with
\alpha=2
to the convex bodies
K\cap\mathcal{H}_{w}
and
L\cap\mathcal{H}_{w}
, and then using (
5.8
) for
K
,
L
, and
K+L
, we immediately obtain the inequality.

Now assume that equality holds. Then

(5.10)

\displaystyle\left(\int_{(K\cap\mathcal{H}_{w})+(L\cap\mathcal{H}_{w})}\langle x,w\rangle^{2}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{n+2}}=\left(\int_{K\cap\mathcal{H}_{w}}\langle x,w\rangle^{2}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{n+2}}+\left(\int_{L\cap\mathcal{H}_{w}}\langle x,w\rangle^{2}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{n+2}}.

Due to Dubuc’s equality characterization
[
Dub77
]
, there exist

(5.11)

\displaystyle a>0,\quad b>0,\quad y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

such that
\mathrm{g}(x)=a\mathrm{f}(b^{-1}(x-y))
for almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Hence

(5.12)

\displaystyle L\cap\mathcal{H}_{w}=b(K\cap\mathcal{H}_{w})+y.

Since
K
and
L
are origin-symmetric, we also have

(5.13)

\displaystyle L\cap\mathcal{H}_{-w}=b(K\cap\mathcal{H}_{-w})-y.

We next show that
\langle y,w\rangle=0
. The equality relation for the densities implies that, for almost every
z\in K\cap\mathcal{H}_{w}
,
\langle bz+y,w\rangle^{2}=C\langle z,w\rangle^{2}
for some constant
C>0
. Since
\langle z,w\rangle
ranges over a non-trivial interval of positive values, the identity

(5.14)

\displaystyle\left(bt+\langle y,w\rangle\right)^{2}=Ct^{2}

holds for all
t
in a non-trivial interval. Hence
\langle y,w\rangle=0
.

Intersecting with
w^{\perp}
then gives

(5.15)

\displaystyle L\cap w^{\perp}=b(K\cap w^{\perp})+y.

Both sides are centrally symmetric convex bodies in
w^{\perp}
; their centers are respectively
0
and
y
. Hence
y=0
. Therefore
L\cap\mathcal{H}_{w}=b(K\cap\mathcal{H}_{w})
. Together with the reflected identity on
\mathcal{H}_{-w}
, this gives
L=bK
. Thus
K
and
L
are dilates.
∎

Proof of
Theorem 1.3
.

Set

(5.16)

\displaystyle K_{+}=K\cap(0,\infty)^{n},\quad L_{+}=L\cap(0,\infty)^{n},\quad Q=(1-\lambda)\cdot K+_{0}\lambda\cdot L.

By
[
Sar15
, Lem. 4.1]
, we have

(5.17)

\displaystyle\left\{\left(x_{1}^{1-\lambda}y_{1}^{\lambda},\dots,x_{n}^{1-\lambda}y_{n}^{\lambda}\right):x\in K_{+},\ y\in L_{+}\right\}\subset Q\cap(0,\infty)^{n}.

We write

(5.18)

\displaystyle A=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:e^{x}\in K_{+}\},\quad B=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:e^{y}\in L_{+}\}.

Note that
A
and
B
are closed convex sets, and

(5.19)

\displaystyle\{e^{(1-\lambda)x+\lambda y}:x\in A,\ y\in B\}=\left\{\left(x_{1}^{1-\lambda}y_{1}^{\lambda},\dots,x_{n}^{1-\lambda}y_{n}^{\lambda}\right):x\in K_{+},\ y\in L_{+}\right\}.

We also define

(5.20)

\displaystyle\phi_{q}(z)=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{2z_{i}}\right)^{\frac{q-n}{2}}e^{z_{1}+\cdots+z_{n}},\quad z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Since
z\mapsto\log(\sum_{i}e^{2z_{i}})
is convex,
\phi_{q}
is log-concave for
0<q<n
. Hence, for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

(5.21)

\displaystyle\phi_{q}((1-\lambda)x+\lambda y)\geq\phi_{q}(x)^{1-\lambda}\phi_{q}(y)^{\lambda}.

Applying the Prékopa–Leindler inequality to the functions
\mathbf{1}_{A}\phi_{q}
and
\mathbf{1}_{B}\phi_{q}
, we obtain

(5.22)

\displaystyle\int_{(1-\lambda)A+\lambda B}\phi_{q}(z)\,dz\geq\left(\int_{A}\phi_{q}(z)\,dz\right)^{1-\lambda}\left(\int_{B}\phi_{q}(z)\,dz\right)^{\lambda}.

Using the change of variables
x_{i}=e^{z_{i}}
and
dx=e^{z_{1}+\cdots+z_{n}}\,dz
, this becomes

(5.23)

\displaystyle\int_{(1-\lambda)A+\lambda B}\phi_{q}(z)\,dz\geq\left(\int_{K_{+}}|x|^{q-n}\,dx\right)^{1-\lambda}\left(\int_{L_{+}}|x|^{q-n}\,dx\right)^{\lambda}.

On the other hand, in view of (
5.17
), we have

(5.24)

\displaystyle\int_{(1-\lambda)A+\lambda B}\phi_{q}(z)\,dz=\int_{\{e^{(1-\lambda)r+\lambda s}:\ r\in A,\ s\in B\}}|x|^{q-n}\,dx\leq\int_{Q\cap(0,\infty)^{n}}|x|^{q-n}\,dx.

Therefore

(5.25)

\displaystyle\int_{Q\cap(0,\infty)^{n}}|x|^{q-n}\,dx\geq\left(\int_{K_{+}}|x|^{q-n}\,dx\right)^{1-\lambda}\left(\int_{L_{+}}|x|^{q-n}\,dx\right)^{\lambda}.

Since all orthants contribute equally, this is equivalent to

(5.26)

\displaystyle\widetilde{V}_{q}\left((1-\lambda)\cdot K+_{0}\lambda\cdot L\right)\geq\widetilde{V}_{q}(K)^{1-\lambda}\widetilde{V}_{q}(L)^{\lambda}.

Assume now that equality holds in (
1.14
). By Dubuc’s equality characterization
[
Dub77
, Thm. 12]
, there exist
a>0
and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

(5.27)

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{A}(z)\phi_{q}(z)=a^{\lambda}\mathbf{1}_{C}(z-\lambda b)\phi_{q}(z-\lambda b)\quad\text{for a.e. }z\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

and

(5.28)

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{B}(z)\phi_{q}(z)=a^{-(1-\lambda)}\mathbf{1}_{C}(z+(1-\lambda)b)\phi_{q}(z+(1-\lambda)b)\quad\text{for a.e. }z\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
C=(1-\lambda)A+\lambda B
. Therefore, up to null sets,

(5.29)

\displaystyle A=C+\lambda b,\quad B=C-(1-\lambda)b.

In particular,

(5.30)

\displaystyle\phi_{q}(z)=a^{\lambda}\phi_{q}(z-\lambda b)\quad\text{for a.e. }z\in A.

Since
A
is a closed convex set with non-empty interior and both sides of (
5.30
) are real analytic, (
5.30
) holds on
\operatorname{int}(A)
and we may differentiate in
\operatorname{int}(A)
.

Next, we compute

(5.31)

\displaystyle\partial_{i}\log\phi_{q}(z)=1+(q-n)p_{i}(z),\quad p_{i}(z):=\frac{e^{2z_{i}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{2z_{j}}}.

Since
q\neq n
, differentiating (
5.30
) gives

(5.32)

\displaystyle p_{i}(z)=p_{i}(z-\lambda b)\quad\text{for all }i=1,\dots,n,\ \text{and all }z\in\operatorname{int}(A).

Hence, for all distinct
i,j
,

(5.33)

\displaystyle\frac{p_{i}(z-\lambda b)}{p_{j}(z-\lambda b)}=\frac{p_{i}(z)}{p_{j}(z)}\implies 1=e^{2\lambda(b_{j}-b_{i})}.

Thus
b_{i}=b_{j}
for all distinct
i,j
as
\lambda\in(0,1)
. That is,
b=t(1,\dots,1)
for some
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

Since
A
and
B
are closed convex sets and
B=A-b
up to null sets, we have

(5.34)

\displaystyle B=A-t(1,\dots,1).

If
x\in K_{+}
, then
\log x\in A
, and hence

(5.35)

\displaystyle\log x-t(1,\dots,1)\in B.

Thus
e^{-t}K_{+}\subset L_{+}
. Similarly,
L_{+}\subset e^{-t}K_{+}
. Hence
L_{+}=e^{-t}K_{+}
, and since
K
and
L
are unconditional, this implies
L=e^{-t}K
.
∎

6.
Brunn–Minkowski inequalities for
q>n

Let us recall in the context of the dual quermassintegral:

(6.1)

\displaystyle W(x)

\displaystyle=(n-q)\log\left|x\right|,\quad

\displaystyle d\mu(x)=e^{-W(x)}\,dx,

\displaystyle\mu(K)

\displaystyle=\int_{K}e^{-W(x)}\,dx,\quad

\displaystyle d\mu_{\partial K}=e^{-W}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\big|_{\partial K}.

We begin this section with the following discussion. To handle the singularity of
W
at the origin, one may work throughout with a suitable regularization:

(6.2)

\displaystyle W_{\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{2}(n-q)\log(|x|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}),\quad\varepsilon>0.

Let
\alpha=q-n
and consider the elliptic operator

(6.3)

\displaystyle L(\cdot)=\Delta_{\mathrm{euc}}(\cdot)-\langle DW,D(\cdot)\rangle=\Delta_{\mathrm{euc}}(\cdot)+\alpha\langle\frac{x}{|x|^{2}},D(\cdot)\rangle.

According to
[
KM18
,
KM22
]
and
[
KL21
]
, in order to establish the log-concavity of the functional
\widetilde{V}_{q}(\cdot)
along the
L_{1}
-sum, it is sufficient to prove the non-negativity of

(6.4)

\displaystyle I_{q}(u):=\int_{K}\left(\|D^{2}u\|^{2}+D^{2}W(Du,Du)\right)\,d\mu

for every solution
u
of the Neumann problem

(6.5)

\displaystyle Lu=C_{0}\quad\text{in }K,

where
C_{0}
is a constant satisfying the compatibility condition

(6.6)

\displaystyle C_{0}=\frac{\int_{\partial K}D_{\nu}u\,d\mu_{\partial K}}{\mu(K)}.

We begin by decomposing the Euclidean gradient
Du
on
K\setminus\{0\}
into its radial and tangential components (with respect to the origin):

(6.7)

\displaystyle\partial_{r}u=\langle Du,\frac{x}{|x|}\rangle,\quad D_{T}u=Du-\frac{x}{|x|}\partial_{r}u.

We calculate

(6.8)

\displaystyle D^{2}W=-\frac{\alpha}{|x|^{2}}\left(I-2\frac{x\otimes x}{|x|^{2}}\right),

and therefore

(6.9)

\displaystyle D^{2}W(Du,Du)=-\alpha\frac{|D_{T}u|^{2}}{|x|^{2}}+\alpha\frac{|\partial_{r}u|^{2}}{|x|^{2}}.

Substituting this identity into the definition of
I_{q}(u)
yields

(6.10)

\displaystyle I_{q}(u)=\int_{K}\left(\|D^{2}u\|^{2}-\alpha\frac{|D_{T}u|^{2}}{|x|^{2}}+\alpha\frac{|\partial_{r}u|^{2}}{|x|^{2}}\right)\,d\mu.

This shows that the cases
\alpha>0
and
\alpha<0
are structurally different. When
\alpha>0
the tangential term provides the unfavorable contribution, while when
\alpha<0
the tangential contribution becomes favorable and the radial term is the one that may decrease the integrand.

Now suppose that
q<n
, the convex body
K
is origin-symmetric and that the test function
u
is even. In this setting, the inequality from
[
CER23
, Thm. 4]
applies and asserts that

(6.11)

\displaystyle\int_{K}|D\partial_{i}u|^{2}\,d\mu\geq(n-q)\int_{K}\frac{(\partial_{i}u)^{2}}{|x|^{2}}\,d\mu\quad\text{for each $i=1,\dots,n$}.

Summing these
n
inequalities gives a lower bound for the norm of the Hessian term:

(6.12)

\displaystyle\int_{K}\|D^{2}u\|^{2}\,d\mu\geq(n-q)\int_{K}\frac{|Du|^{2}}{|x|^{2}}\,d\mu.

Inserting this estimate into the expression for
I_{q}(u)
gives

(6.13)

\displaystyle I_{q}(u)

\displaystyle\geq(n-q)\int_{K}\frac{|Du|^{2}}{|x|^{2}}\,d\mu+(n-q)\int_{K}\frac{|D_{T}u|^{2}}{|x|^{2}}\,d\mu+(q-n)\int_{K}\frac{|\partial_{r}u|^{2}}{|x|^{2}}\,d\mu

\displaystyle=2(n-q)\int_{K}\frac{|D_{T}u|^{2}}{|x|^{2}}\,d\mu\geq 0,

Next we briefly outline a second proof of
Theorem 1.3
in the class of unconditional bodies for
q\in(0,n)
, which additionally provides a perspective on the difference between the cases
q<n
and
q>n
.

If
K
is unconditional and
u
is unconditional, then, similarly to the proof of
[
KM22
, Thm. 8.3]
, one can show that

(6.14)

\displaystyle\int_{K}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{ii}^{2}+\alpha\frac{|Du|^{2}}{|x|^{2}}\right)|x|^{\alpha}\,dx\geq\int_{\partial K}\frac{u_{\nu}^{2}}{h}|x|^{\alpha}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

Now, since
\|D^{2}u\|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{ii}^{2}+2\sum_{i<j}u_{ij}^{2}
, we have

(6.15)

\displaystyle\|D^{2}u\|^{2}-\alpha\frac{|D_{T}u|^{2}}{|x|^{2}}+\alpha\frac{(\partial_{r}u)^{2}}{|x|^{2}}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i<j}u_{ij}^{2}-2\alpha\frac{|D_{T}u|^{2}}{|x|^{2}}+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{ii}^{2}+\alpha\frac{|Du|^{2}}{|x|^{2}}\right).

In particular, when
\alpha\leq 0
, we obtain

(6.16)

\displaystyle\int_{K}\left(\|D^{2}u\|^{2}-\alpha\frac{|D_{T}u|^{2}}{|x|^{2}}+\alpha\frac{(\partial_{r}u)^{2}}{|x|^{2}}\right)|x|^{q-n}\,dx\geq\int_{\partial K}\frac{u_{\nu}^{2}}{h}|x|^{q-n}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

Hence,
\widetilde{V}_{q}
is log-concave with respect to
L_{0}
-sum for
q\in(0,n)
. In fact, by
4.4
, (
6.16
) is equivalent to

(6.17)

\displaystyle q\int_{S^{n-1}}(F-\bar{F})^{2}|X|^{q-n}\,dV_{K}\leq\int_{S^{n-1}}|\nabla F|_{g}^{2}|X|^{q-n}\,dV_{K},\quad\bar{F}=\frac{\int_{S^{n-1}}F|X|^{q-n}\,dV_{K}}{\int_{S^{n-1}}|X|^{q-n}\,dV_{K}}.

However, for
\alpha=q-n>0
, in order to prove the non-negativity of the left-hand side of (

), one needs to additionally control the tangential term appearing in the following expression with the off-diagonal terms of

:

(6.18)

\displaystyle\sum_{i<j}u_{ij}^{2}-\alpha\frac{|D_{T}u|^{2}}{|x|^{2}}.

Now we prove the log-concavity of
\widetilde{V}_{q}
with respect to
L_{p}
-sum for
q>n
very close to
n
.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
0<p\leq 1
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
. Suppose
K,L\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
are unconditional convex bodies. Then

(6.21)

\displaystyle T_{p}^{-1}\left((1-\lambda)A_{p}(K)+\lambda A_{p}(L)\right)\subset\left((1-\lambda)\cdot K+_{p}\lambda\cdot L\right)\cap(0,\infty)^{n}.

Proof.

The proof is similar to the proof of
p=0
case in
[
Sar15
]
; see also
[
BL95
]
. Let

(6.22)

\displaystyle z\in T_{p}^{-1}\left((1-\lambda)A_{p}(K)+\lambda A_{p}(L)\right).

Then there exist
x\in K_{+}
and
y\in L_{+}
such that

(6.23)

\displaystyle z_{i}^{p}=(1-\lambda)x_{i}^{p}+\lambda y_{i}^{p}.

Note that
z_{i}=\mathrm{M}_{p}(x_{i},y_{i})
, where

(6.24)

\displaystyle\mathrm{M}_{p}(a,b):=\left((1-\lambda)a^{p}+\lambda b^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Since
\mathrm{M}_{p}
is concave and one-homogeneous on
(0,\infty)^{2}
, for
v\in[0,\infty)^{n}

(6.25)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}\mathrm{M}_{p}(x_{i},y_{i})\leq\mathrm{M}_{p}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}x_{i},\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}y_{i}\right).

Therefore

(6.26)

\displaystyle\langle z,v\rangle\leq\left((1-\lambda)\langle x,v\rangle^{p}+\lambda\langle y,v\rangle^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq\left((1-\lambda)h_{K}(v)^{p}+\lambda h_{L}(v)^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

For general
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, if we set
v_{+}=(|v_{1}|,\ldots,|v_{n}|)
, then

(6.27)

\displaystyle\langle z,v\rangle\leq\langle z,v_{+}\rangle,\quad h_{K}(v)=h_{K}(v_{+}),\quad h_{L}(v)=h_{L}(v_{+}).

Thus
z
belongs to the

Lemma 6.4
.

Let

\displaystyle D^{2}\Phi(y)[\xi,\xi]\leq\frac{r(r-1)}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\xi_{i}^{2}}{y_{i}^{2}}.

\displaystyle D^{2}\Phi(y)[\xi,\xi]

\displaystyle=r(r-1)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{i}a_{i}^{2}-r^{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{i}a_{i}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{i}a_{i}^{2}-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{i}a_{i}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\theta_{i}\theta_{j}(a_{i}^{2}+a_{j}^{2})

Corollary 6.8
.

Let
K
be an unconditional convex body,
\alpha\in\left(0,1\right)
, and let

be odd in the

-th coordinate. Then for every

\displaystyle\int_{K}\left|\partial_{i}u\right|^{2}\left(\left|x\right|^{2}+\delta^{2}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\,dx\geq\frac{\left(1-\alpha\right)^{2}}{4}\int_{K}u^{2}\left(\left|x\right|^{2}+\delta^{2}\right)^{\frac{\alpha-2}{2}}\,dx.

Proposition 6.9
.

Let
q\in(n,n+3-2\sqrt{2}]
. Then, in the class of unconditional bodies

(6.61)

\displaystyle\widetilde{V}_{q}(K+L)^{1/q}\geq\widetilde{V}_{q}(K)^{1/q}+\widetilde{V}_{q}(L)^{1/q}.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
\alpha=q-n
. We introduce the regularized weight

(6.62)

\displaystyle W_{\varepsilon}(x)=-\frac{\alpha}{2}\log\left(|x|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\right),

together with the associated weighted measure

(6.63)

\displaystyle d\mu_{\varepsilon}(x)=e^{-W_{\varepsilon}(x)}\,dx=\left(|x|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\,dx.

We also define the differential operator

(6.64)

\displaystyle L_{\varepsilon}u=\Delta_{\mathrm{euc}}u-\langle DW_{\varepsilon},Du\rangle=\Delta_{\mathrm{euc}}u+\alpha\langle\frac{x}{|x|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}},Du\rangle.

We now show that, for every unconditional
C^{2}
function
u
on the unconditional convex body
K
satisfying
L_{\varepsilon}u=C
for some constant
C
, one has

(6.65)

\displaystyle I_{q,\varepsilon}(u):=\int_{K}\left(\|D^{2}u\|^{2}+D^{2}W_{\varepsilon}(Du,Du)\right)\,d\mu_{\varepsilon}\geq 0.

This non-negativity then implies the log-concavity of
\mu_{\varepsilon}
with respect to the
L_{1}
-sum on unconditional convex bodies. Passing to the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
, we deduce the log-concavity of
\widetilde{V}_{q}
with respect to the
L_{1}
-sum on unconditional convex bodies.

By
6.8
, if
v\in C^{1}\left(K\right)
is odd in the
i
-th coordinate, then

(6.66)

\displaystyle\int_{K}|\partial_{i}v|^{2}\,d\mu_{\varepsilon}\geq\frac{(1-\alpha)^{2}}{4}\int_{K}v^{2}\left(|x|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\right)^{\frac{\alpha-2}{2}}\,dx.

Applying this to

and summing over all coordinate directions yields

(6.67)

\displaystyle\int_{K}\|D^{2}u\|^{2}\,d\mu_{\varepsilon}\geq\frac{(1-\alpha)^{2}}{4}\int_{K}|Du|^{2}\left(|x|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\right)^{\frac{\alpha-2}{2}}\,dx.

On the other hand, a direct computation shows that

(6.68)

\displaystyle D^{2}W_{\varepsilon}=-\alpha\left(\frac{I}{|x|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}-2\frac{x\otimes x}{(|x|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})^{2}}\right),

and hence on
K\setminus\{0\}

(6.69)

\displaystyle D^{2}W_{\varepsilon}(Du,Du)

\displaystyle=-\alpha\frac{|D_{T}u|^{2}}{|x|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}+\alpha\frac{|x|^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}}{(|x|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})^{2}}|\partial_{r}u|^{2}.

Combining these estimates, we obtain

(6.70)

\displaystyle I_{q,\varepsilon}(u)

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{(1-\alpha)^{2}}{4}-\alpha\right)\int_{K}|D_{T}u|^{2}\left(|x|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\right)^{\frac{\alpha-2}{2}}\,dx

\displaystyle+\int_{K}\left(\frac{(1-\alpha)^{2}}{4}+\alpha\frac{|x|^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}}{|x|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}\right)|\partial_{r}u|^{2}\left(|x|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\right)^{\frac{\alpha-2}{2}}\,dx.

Since
\frac{|x|^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}}{|x|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}\geq-1
, the coefficient of the radial term is at least
\frac{(1-\alpha)^{2}}{4}-\alpha
as well. Therefore

(6.71)

\displaystyle I_{q,\varepsilon}(u)\geq\left(\frac{(1-\alpha)^{2}}{4}-\alpha\right)\int_{K}|Du|^{2}\left(|x|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\right)^{\frac{\alpha-2}{2}}\,dx\geq 0.

∎

, we get

(7.8)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{S^{n-1}}h_{L}|Dh_{K}|^{2}\,dS_{K}-\widetilde{V}_{n+2}(K)\geq\widetilde{V}_{n+2}(K)^{\frac{n+1}{n+2}}\widetilde{V}_{n+2}(L)^{\frac{1}{n+2}}-\widetilde{V}_{n+2}(K).

If
K
and
L
are not dilates, by the characterization of equality cases in
Theorem 1.2
, then
\phi^{\prime}(0)>\phi(1)-\phi(0)
, and the inequality is strict.

We now prove (ii). For
\lambda\in[0,1]
, set

(7.9)

\displaystyle h_{\lambda}=h_{K}^{1-\lambda}h_{L}^{\lambda},\quad K_{\lambda}=(1-\lambda)\cdot K+_{0}\lambda\cdot L.

By definition,
K_{\lambda}
is the Wulff shape generated by
h_{\lambda}
. In general,
h_{\lambda}
need not be the support function of
K_{\lambda}
pointwise. For the first variation formula we invoke
[
LYZ18
, Thm. 6.4]
, see also
[
XZ22
, Lem. 2.1]
.

By
Theorem 1.3
, the function
\phi(\lambda):=\log\widetilde{V}_{q}(K_{\lambda})

is concave on
[0,1]
. Hence

(7.10)

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}(0)\geq\phi(1)-\phi(0)=\log\frac{\widetilde{V}_{q}(L)}{\widetilde{V}_{q}(K)}.

Since
\log h_{\lambda}=\log h_{K}+\lambda\log\frac{h_{L}}{h_{K}}
, the first variation formula for
\widetilde{V}_{q}
gives

(7.11)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{d\lambda}\right|_{\lambda=0}\widetilde{V}_{q}(K_{\lambda})=\frac{q}{n}\int_{S^{n-1}}h_{K}\log\frac{h_{L}}{h_{K}}|Dh_{K}|^{q-n}\,dS_{K}.

Therefore

(7.12)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\widetilde{V}_{q}(K)}\frac{q}{n}\int_{S^{n-1}}h_{K}\log\frac{h_{L}}{h_{K}}|Dh_{K}|^{q-n}\,dS_{K}\geq\log\frac{\widetilde{V}_{q}(L)}{\widetilde{V}_{q}(K)},

which is (
7.2
). Finally (
7.3
) follows from (
7.2
) and Jensen’s inequality.

Equality in (
7.2
) implies that
\phi
is affine on
[0,1]
, and hence equality holds in
Theorem 1.3
and thus
K
and
L
are dilates. Equality in (
7.3
) implies equality in Jensen’s inequality, so
(h_{L}/h_{K})^{p}
is constant on
S^{n-1}
, and thus again
K
and
L
are dilates.
∎

Proof of
Theorem 1.6
.

Assume that
K
and
L
are
C^{2}_{+}
unconditional convex bodies solving

(7.13)

\displaystyle|Dh|^{q-n}h^{1-p}\det(\bar{\nabla}^{2}h+hI)=f.

We prove that
K=L
.

Consider the case
q=n+2
and
1\leq p<q
. By (
7.13
) for
K
,

(7.14)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{S^{n-1}}h_{L}|Dh_{K}|^{2}\,dS_{K}=\frac{1}{n}\int_{S^{n-1}}h_{L}h_{K}^{p-1}f\,dx.

By (
7.1
) and Hölder’s inequality,

(7.15)

\displaystyle\widetilde{V}_{n+2}(K)^{\frac{n+1}{n+2}}\widetilde{V}_{n+2}(L)^{\frac{1}{n+2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\int_{S^{n-1}}h_{L}h_{K}^{p-1}f\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{n}\int_{S^{n-1}}h_{K}^{p}f\,dx\right)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\left(\frac{1}{n}\int_{S^{n-1}}h_{L}^{p}f\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Now using

(7.16)

\displaystyle\widetilde{V}_{n+2}(K)=\frac{1}{n}\int_{S^{n-1}}h_{K}^{p}f\,dx,\quad\widetilde{V}_{n+2}(L)=\frac{1}{n}\int_{S^{n-1}}h_{L}^{p}f\,dx,

we find

(7.17)

\displaystyle\widetilde{V}_{n+2}(K)^{\frac{n+1}{n+2}}\widetilde{V}_{n+2}(L)^{\frac{1}{n+2}}\leq\widetilde{V}_{n+2}(K)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\widetilde{V}_{n+2}(L)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Interchanging the roles of
K
and
L
shows that
\widetilde{V}_{n+2}(K)=\widetilde{V}_{n+2}(L)
. Hence equality holds in Hölder’s inequality and in
(
7.1
). Thus
K
and
L
are dilates and since
p<q
, the dilation factor must be
1
.

The remaining cases, corresponding to
q\in(0,n)
and
0\leq p<q
, are treated in the same way. For
p=0
we use (
7.2
), while for
0<p<q
we use (
7.3
), together with the corresponding equality cases.
∎

8.
Logarithmic centro-affine geometry

Our main reference for affine geometry in this section is
[
NS94
]
. Throughout this section,
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an unconditional, smooth, strictly convex body. We write

(8.1)

\displaystyle\Omega_{+}=\{x\in S^{n-1}:\ X_{i}(x)>0,\ i=1,\dots,n\}.

On
\Omega_{+}
, we define a smooth immersion

(8.2)

\displaystyle Y:\Omega_{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{n},\quad Y=\log X:=(\log X_{1},\dots,\log X_{n}),

and we set
\bm{\xi}=(1,\dots,1)
,
P=\operatorname{diag}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
and
Q=\operatorname{diag}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
.

Since
dY=P^{-1}dX
, for every
v\in T_{x}S^{n-1}
we have

(8.3)

\displaystyle\langle dY(v),Px\rangle=\langle P^{-1}dX(v),Px\rangle=\langle dX(v),x\rangle=0.

Thus a Euclidean unit normal to
Y(\Omega_{+})
is

(8.4)

\displaystyle\mathbf{n}=\frac{Px}{\sigma},\quad\text{where }\sigma=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{2}x_{i}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

This implies that

(8.5)

\displaystyle\langle\bm{\xi},\mathbf{n}\rangle=\frac{\langle\bm{\xi},Px\rangle}{\sigma}=\frac{h}{\sigma}.

Hence
\bm{\xi}
is transverse to
Y(\Omega_{+})
.

The vector
\bm{\xi}
induces a torsion-free connection
\nabla^{\log}
and a symmetric bilinear form
g_{\log}
:

(8.6)

\displaystyle D_{v}dY(w)=dY(\nabla^{\log}_{v}w)-g_{\log}(v,w)\bm{\xi}.

The choice of the transversal field
\bm{\xi}=(1,\dots,1)
is natural. In centro-affine geometry, the transversal vector field is the position vector
X
, which may be interpreted as the infinitesimal direction of dilations
X\mapsto e^{t}X
. After passing to logarithmic coordinates
Y=\log X
, these dilations become translations
Y\mapsto Y+t\bm{\xi}
. Thus
\bm{\xi}
is precisely the logarithmic representative of the centro-affine transversal vector field.

Lemma 8.1
.

We have

(8.7)

\displaystyle g_{\log}(v,w)=g(v,w)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{x_{i}X_{i}}{h}d\log X_{i}(v)d\log X_{i}(w).

In particular,
g_{\log}>g
on
\Omega_{+}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathrm{II}(v,w)=\langle D_{v}\mathbf{n},dY(w)\rangle
be the Euclidean second fundamental form of
Y(\Omega_{+})
. Since
\langle Px,dY(w)\rangle=0
, we have

(8.8)

\displaystyle\mathrm{II}(v,w)=\frac{1}{\sigma}\left\langle D_{v}(Px),P^{-1}dX(w)\right\rangle.

On the other hand,

(8.9)

\displaystyle D_{v}(Px)=Pv+QdX(v).

Moreover, we have

(8.10)

\displaystyle\left\langle Pv,P^{-1}dX(w)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle v,dX(w)\rangle=\bar{g}(v,\tau w),

\displaystyle\left\langle QdX(v),P^{-1}dX(w)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{x_{i}}{X_{i}}dX_{i}(v)dX_{i}(w).

Hence

(8.11)

\displaystyle\mathrm{II}(v,w)=\frac{1}{\sigma}\left(\bar{g}(v,\tau w)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{x_{i}}{X_{i}}dX_{i}(v)dX_{i}(w)\right).

Taking the Euclidean scalar product of (
8.6
) with
\mathbf{n}
gives

(8.12)

\displaystyle-g_{\log}(v,w)\langle\bm{\xi},\mathbf{n}\rangle=\langle D_{v}dY(w),\mathbf{n}\rangle=-\mathrm{II}(v,w).

Thus

(8.13)

\displaystyle g_{\log}(v,w)=\frac{\mathrm{II}(v,w)}{\langle\bm{\xi},\mathbf{n}\rangle}=\frac{\sigma}{h}\mathrm{II}(v,w).

Due to
\bar{g}(v,\tau w)=hg(v,w)
, (
8.7
) follows.

Since
dX=\tau
is injective, by
2.3
, the second term in (
8.7
) is positive definite. Consequently,
g_{\log}>g
on
\Omega_{+}
.
∎

Proposition 8.2
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a
C^{2}_{+}
unconditional convex body. Put

(8.14)

\displaystyle y=\frac{x}{h(x)},\quad p_{i}=X_{i}y_{i},\quad u_{i}=\log X_{i},\quad v_{i}=\log y_{i}.

Then on
\Omega_{+}
:

(8.15)

\displaystyle\sum_{i}p_{i}du_{i}=0,\quad\sum_{i}p_{i}dv_{i}=0,\quad g=\sum_{i}p_{i}du_{i}dv_{i}.

Define
g_{\log}=g+\sum_{i}p_{i}du_{i}^{2}
and
g_{\log}^{\circ}=g+\sum_{i}p_{i}dv_{i}^{2}
. Then

(8.16)

\displaystyle g_{\log}=\sum_{i}du_{i}dp_{i},\quad g_{\log}^{\circ}=\sum_{i}dv_{i}dp_{i}.

Moreover, if
g_{\mathrm{av}}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(g_{\log}+g_{\log}^{\circ}\right)
, then

(8.17)

\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{av}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\frac{1}{p_{i}}dp_{i}^{2}.

In particular,
g_{\mathrm{av}}\geq 2g
. For a coordinate ellipsoid,

(8.18)

\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{av}}=g_{\log}=g_{\log}^{\circ}=2g.

Proof.

The first set of identities were proved in
[
Mil23
, p. 28]
. From
p_{i}=X_{i}y_{i}
we have

(8.19)

\displaystyle dp_{i}=p_{i}(du_{i}+dv_{i}).

Therefore

(8.20)

\displaystyle\sum_{i}du_{i}dp_{i}=\sum_{i}p_{i}du_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i}p_{i}du_{i}dv_{i}=g+\sum_{i}p_{i}du_{i}^{2}=g_{\log}.

Interchanging
u_{i}
and
v_{i}
gives

(8.21)

\displaystyle\sum_{i}dv_{i}dp_{i}=\sum_{i}p_{i}dv_{i}du_{i}+\sum_{i}p_{i}dv_{i}^{2}=g+\sum_{i}p_{i}dv_{i}^{2}=g_{\log}^{\circ}.

Now using again
dp_{i}=p_{i}(du_{i}+dv_{i})
, we obtain

(8.22)

\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{av}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}(du_{i}+dv_{i})dp_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\frac{1}{p_{i}}dp_{i}^{2}.

Finally,

(8.23)

\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{av}}-2g=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}p_{i}(du_{i}+dv_{i})^{2}-2\sum_{i}p_{i}du_{i}dv_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}p_{i}(du_{i}-dv_{i})^{2}\geq 0.

For a coordinate ellipsoid
\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\sum_{i}z_{i}^{2}/a_{i}^{2}\leq 1\}
we have
y_{i}=X_{i}/a_{i}^{2}
and hence
dv_{i}=du_{i}
. That is,
g_{\log}=g_{\log}^{\circ}=g_{\mathrm{av}}=2g
.
∎

Proposition 8.3
.

Let
\Delta^{n-1}_{+}=\{p=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}):p_{i}>0,\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}=1\}
. On
\Delta^{n-1}_{+}
consider the Riemannian metric
g_{\mathrm{av}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\frac{1}{p_{i}}dp_{i}^{2}
. Let
\tilde{\nabla}
be its Levi-Civita connection. Then
\left(\Delta^{n-1}_{+},g_{\mathrm{av}}\right)
is locally isometric to the round sphere of radius
\sqrt{2}
.

Proof.

Define

(8.24)

\displaystyle f:\Delta^{n-1}_{+}\to S^{n-1}_{\sqrt{2},+},\quad f(p)=\left(\sqrt{2p_{1}},\ldots,\sqrt{2p_{n}}\right),

where

(8.25)

\displaystyle S^{n-1}_{\sqrt{2},+}=\left\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:z_{i}>0,\ \sum_{i}z_{i}^{2}=2\right\}.

Note that
f
is a diffeomorphism from
\Delta^{n-1}_{+}
onto its image. Moreover, we have

(8.26)

\displaystyle f^{\ast}\left(\sum_{i}dz_{i}^{2}\right)=\sum_{i}df_{i}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\frac{1}{p_{i}}dp_{i}^{2}=g_{\mathrm{av}}.

Thus
\left(\Delta^{n-1}_{+},g_{\mathrm{av}}\right)
is locally isometric to the round sphere of radius
\sqrt{2}
.
∎

By the affine Weingarten formula,

(8.27)

\displaystyle D_{v}\bm{\xi}=dY(\mathcal{S}v)-\theta(v)\bm{\xi},

where
\mathcal{S}
is called the affine shape operator and
\theta
is called the transversal connection form. Therefore we have
\mathcal{S}=0
and
\theta=0
. Now the Gauss equation gives
\operatorname{Rm}^{\nabla^{\log}}=0
.

The structure

; see

NS94

.

Let
\nabla^{\log,*}
denote the connection conjugate to
\nabla^{\log}

g_{\log}

(8.28)

\displaystyle U(g_{\log}(V,W))=g_{\log}(\nabla^{\log}_{U}V,W)+g_{\log}(V,\nabla_{U}^{\log,*}W).

The connection
\nabla^{\log,*}
is torsion-free; see
[
NS94

.

For
F\in C^{\infty}(\Omega_{+})
define

(8.29)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\log}F=\operatorname{tr}_{g_{\log}}\left((\nabla^{\log,*})^{2}F\right).

Lemma 8.4
.

We have
dV_{\log}=(X_{1}\cdots X_{n})^{-1}\,dV_{K}
. Equivalently, if we set
\eta:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}
, then

(8.30)

\displaystyle e^{\eta}\,dV_{\log}=dV_{K}.

Proof.

Let
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n-1}
be a local positively oriented
\bar{g}
-orthonormal frame. Then, using
dY=P^{-1}dX
and
P\bm{\xi}=X
, we have

(8.31)

\displaystyle\frac{dV_{\log}}{dx}=\det(dY(e_{1}),\dots,dY(e_{n-1}),\bm{\xi})=\det P^{-1}\cdot\det(dX(e_{1}),\dots,dX(e_{n-1}),X).

The last determinant is the density of the cone-volume measure of
K
.
∎

Lemma 8.5
.

Let
\Psi\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and set
\psi=\Psi\circ Y
. Then

(8.32)

\displaystyle(\nabla^{\log})^{2}\psi(v,w)=D^{2}\Psi(Y)[dY(v),dY(w)]-D\Psi(Y)[\bm{\xi}]\,g_{\log}(v,w).

In particular, we have

(8.33)

\displaystyle(\nabla^{\log})^{2}\eta+ng_{\log}=0.

Moreover,
r(y):=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{2y_{i}}\right)
satisfies

(8.34)

\displaystyle(\nabla^{\log})^{2}(r\circ Y)=D^{2}r(Y)[dY,dY]-g_{\log}.

Proof.

By definition,

(8.35)

\displaystyle(\nabla^{\log})^{2}\psi(v,w)=v(w\psi)-(\nabla^{\log}_{v}w)\psi.

Since
\psi=\Psi\circ Y
, we have

(8.36)

\displaystyle v(w\psi)=D^{2}\Psi(Y)[dY(v),dY(w)]+D\Psi(Y)[D_{v}dY(w)].

Moreover, we have
(\nabla^{\log}_{v}w)\psi=D\Psi(Y)[dY(\nabla^{\log}_{v}w)]
. Hence subtracting these two equations and using (
8.6
) yields (
8.32
).

The identity (
8.33
) follows from (
8.32
) with
\Psi(y)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}
:
D^{2}\Psi=0
and
D\Psi(Y)[\bm{\xi}]=n
.

Finally, since
Dr(Y)[\bm{\xi}]=1
, applying (
8.32
) to
\Psi=r
yields (
8.34
).
∎

For a weight
\Phi\in C^{\infty}(\Omega_{+})

(8.37)

\displaystyle d\mu_{\Phi}=e^{-\Phi}dV_{\log},\quad\mathcal{L}_{\Phi}^{\log}F=\Delta_{\log}F-g_{\log}(\nabla^{\log}\Phi,\nabla^{\log}F).

Moreover, for a vector field
Z
, we define

(8.38)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}^{\log}_{\Phi}Z=\operatorname{div}_{\nabla^{\log}}Z-g_{\log}(\nabla^{\log}\Phi,Z).

Lemma 8.6
.

For every
u\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega_{+})
and every smooth vector field
Z
there holds

(8.39)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{+}}u\,\operatorname{div}^{\log}_{\Phi}Z\,d\mu_{\Phi}=-\int_{\Omega_{+}}g_{\log}(\nabla^{\log}u,Z)\,d\mu_{\Phi}.

In particular, for smooth compactly supported functions
u,v
, we have

(8.40)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{+}}u\,\mathcal{L}_{\Phi}^{\log}v\,d\mu_{\Phi}=-\int_{\Omega_{+}}g_{\log}(\nabla^{\log}u,\nabla^{\log}v)\,d\mu_{\Phi}.

Proof.

By
[
NS94
, Chap. 2, Prop. 1.4]
,
\nabla^{\log}dV_{\log}=0
. Since
\nabla^{\log}
is torsion-free, by the divergence theorem, whenever
W
has compact support in
\Omega_{+}
, we have

(8.41)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{+}}\operatorname{div}_{\nabla^{\log}}W\,dV_{\log}=0.

Therefore, since
\operatorname{div}_{\nabla^{\log}}(e^{-\Phi}Z)=e^{-\Phi}\operatorname{div}_{\Phi}^{\log}Z

for every compactly supported vector field

(8.42)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}_{\Phi}^{\log}(uZ)=u\operatorname{div}_{\Phi}^{\log}Z+g_{\log}(\nabla^{\log}u,Z),

Lemma 8.7
.

Let
\Phi\in C^{\infty}(\Omega_{+})
. Then, for every
F\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega_{+})
,

(8.43)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{+}}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\Phi}^{\log}F\right)^{2}\,d\mu_{\Phi}=\int_{\Omega_{+}}\left|(\nabla^{\log,*})^{2}F\right|_{g_{\log}}^{2}\,d\mu_{\Phi}+\int_{\Omega_{+}}(\nabla^{\log})^{2}\Phi(\nabla^{\log}F,\nabla^{\log}F)\,d\mu_{\Phi}.

Proof.

For readability, write
\nabla=\nabla^{\log}
,
\nabla^{*}=\nabla^{\log,*}
,
g=g_{\log}
,
dV=dV_{\log}
,
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\Phi}^{\log}
, and
Z=\nabla F
. We first prove the asymmetric affine Bochner identity

(8.44)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}_{\Phi}^{\log}(\nabla_{Z}Z)=Z(\mathcal{L}F)+\nabla^{2}\Phi(Z,Z)+\left|(\nabla^{*})^{2}F\right|_{g}^{2}.

Since the logarithmic affine connection
\nabla^{\log}
is flat,
[
Opo15
, Eq. (8)]
gives

(8.45)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}_{\nabla}(\nabla_{Z}Z)=Z(\operatorname{div}_{\nabla}Z)+\operatorname{tr}_{g}\left((\nabla Z)\circ(\nabla Z)\right).

Moreover,
\operatorname{div}_{\nabla}Z=\Delta F
. The conjugacy identity (
8.28
) yields, as in the proof of (
3.32
),

(8.46)

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}_{g}\left((\nabla Z)\circ(\nabla Z)\right)=\left|(\nabla^{*})^{2}F\right|_{g}^{2}.

Consequently,

(8.47)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}_{\nabla}(\nabla_{Z}Z)=Z(\Delta F)+\left|(\nabla^{*})^{2}F\right|_{g}^{2}.

Now using the definition of the weighted divergence, we obtain

(8.48)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}_{\Phi}^{\log}(\nabla_{Z}Z)=Z(\Delta F)-g(\nabla\Phi,\nabla_{Z}Z)+\left|(\nabla^{*})^{2}F\right|_{g}^{2}.

Since
\mathcal{L}F=\Delta F-g(\nabla\Phi,Z)
, we have

(8.49)

\displaystyle Z(\mathcal{L}F)=Z(\Delta F)-Z\left(g(\nabla\Phi,Z)\right).

Moreover, we have

(8.50)

\displaystyle Z\left(g(\nabla\Phi,Z)\right)=g(\nabla_{Z}Z,\nabla\Phi)+g(Z,\nabla_{Z}^{*}\nabla\Phi),\quad g(Z,\nabla_{Z}^{*}\nabla\Phi)=\nabla^{2}\Phi(Z,Z).

Therefore,

(8.51)

\displaystyle Z(\Delta F)-g(\nabla\Phi,\nabla_{Z}Z)=Z(\mathcal{L}F)+\nabla^{2}\Phi(Z,Z).

Substituting this into the expression for
\operatorname{div}_{\Phi}^{\log}(\nabla_{Z}Z)
proves the identity.

The vector field
\nabla_{Z}Z
also has compact support. Hence

(8.52)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{+}}\operatorname{div}_{\Phi}^{\log}(\nabla_{Z}Z)\,d\mu_{\Phi}=0

and

(8.53)

\displaystyle 0=\int_{\Omega_{+}}Z(\mathcal{L}F)\,d\mu_{\Phi}+\int_{\Omega_{+}}\nabla^{2}\Phi(Z,Z)\,d\mu_{\Phi}+\int_{\Omega_{+}}\left|(\nabla^{*})^{2}F\right|_{g}^{2}\,d\mu_{\Phi}.

Moreover, we have

(8.54)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{+}}Z(\mathcal{L}F)\,d\mu_{\Phi}=-\int_{\Omega_{+}}(\mathcal{L}F)^{2}\,d\mu_{\Phi}.

Therefore

(8.55)

\displaystyle 0=-\int_{\Omega_{+}}(\mathcal{L}F)^{2}\,d\mu_{\Phi}+\int_{\Omega_{+}}\nabla^{2}\Phi(\nabla F,\nabla F)\,d\mu_{\Phi}+\int_{\Omega_{+}}\left|(\nabla^{*})^{2}F\right|_{g}^{2}\,d\mu_{\Phi}.

Now we return to logarithmic notation.

For

,

Lemma 8.8
.

For every smooth vector field
Z
on
\Omega_{+}
, one has

(8.56)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}_{\Phi_{0}}^{\log}Z=\operatorname{div}_{\nabla}Z.

Proof.

We work in local coordinates
x^{1},\dots,x^{n-1}
. We write

(8.57)

\displaystyle dV_{\log}=J_{\log}\,dx^{1}\cdots dx^{n-1},\quad dV_{K}=J_{K}\,dx^{1}\cdots dx^{n-1}.

Since
dV_{K}=e^{-\Phi_{0}}dV_{\log}
, we have
J_{K}=e^{-\Phi_{0}}J_{\log}
. Moreover,
\nabla^{\log}dV_{\log}=0=\nabla dV_{K}
and the connections are torsion-free, therefore

(8.58)

\displaystyle(\Gamma^{\log})^{i}_{ik}=\partial_{k}\log J_{\log},\quad\Gamma^{i}_{ik}=\partial_{k}\log J_{K},

where
\nabla^{\log}_{\partial_{j}}\partial_{k}=\left(\Gamma^{\log}\right)^{i}_{jk}\partial_{i}
and
\nabla_{\partial_{j}}\partial_{k}=\Gamma^{i}_{jk}\partial_{i}
.

Now let
Z=Z^{i}\partial_{i}
. By definition,

(8.59)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}_{\Phi_{0}}^{\log}Z

\displaystyle=\partial_{i}Z^{i}+(\Gamma^{\log})^{i}_{ik}Z^{k}-Z^{k}\partial_{k}\Phi_{0}

\displaystyle=\partial_{i}Z^{i}+Z^{k}\partial_{k}\log J_{\log}-Z^{k}\partial_{k}\Phi_{0}

\displaystyle=\partial_{i}Z^{i}+Z^{k}\partial_{k}\log J_{K}

\displaystyle=\partial_{i}Z^{i}+\Gamma^{i}_{ik}Z^{k}=\operatorname{div}_{\nabla}Z.

Lemma 8.9
.

Let
\Omega\Subset\Omega_{+}
be a smooth domain. Let
\mathbf{N}_{\Omega}
be the outward
g_{\log}
-unit normal to
\partial\Omega
and
\mathbf{n}_{\Omega}
be the outward
\bar{g}
-unit normal to
\partial\Omega
. Then

(8.60)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\operatorname{div}_{\Phi_{0}}^{\log}Z\,dV_{K}=\int_{\partial\Omega}g_{\log}(Z,\mathbf{N}_{\Omega})\,d\sigma_{\log},

where
d\sigma_{\log}=h\det(\tau)\,\bar{g}(\mathbf{N}_{\Omega},\mathbf{n}_{\Omega})\,d\bar{\sigma}
.

Proof.

By
8.8
and
3.3
, we have

(8.61)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}_{\nabla}Z\,dV_{K}=\bar{\nabla}_{i}\left(h\det\left(\tau\right)Z^{i}\right)\,dx.

Therefore, applying the standard divergence theorem on
\left(S^{n-1},\bar{g}\right)
gives

(8.62)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\operatorname{div}^{\log}_{\Phi_{0}}Z\,dV_{K}=\int_{\partial\Omega}h\det\left(\tau\right)\bar{g}\left(Z,\mathbf{n}_{\Omega}\right)\,d\bar{\sigma}.

Now the claim follows from the identity

(8.63)

\displaystyle\bar{g}\left(Z,\mathbf{n}_{\Omega}\right)=g_{\log}\left(Z,\mathbf{N}_{\Omega}\right)\bar{g}\left(\mathbf{N}_{\Omega},\mathbf{n}_{\Omega}\right).

∎

We now derive the corresponding Bochner formula on smooth subdomains of
\Omega_{+}
.

Theorem 8.10
.

Let
\Omega\Subset\Omega_{+}
be a smooth domain. For
u\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
, set
Z=\nabla^{\log}u
. Then

(8.64)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left((\mathcal{L}_{0}^{\log}u)^{2}-\left|(\nabla^{\log,*})^{2}u\right|_{g_{\log}}^{2}-n|\nabla^{\log}u|_{g_{\log}}^{2}\right)\,dV_{K}

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\Omega}\left((\mathcal{L}_{0}^{\log}u)u_{\mathbf{N}_{\Omega}}-g_{\log}(\nabla_{Z}^{\log}Z,\mathbf{N}_{\Omega})\right)\,d\sigma_{\log}.

Here
u_{\mathbf{N}_{\Omega}}=g_{\log}(\nabla^{\log}u,\mathbf{N}_{\Omega})
. In particular, if
u_{\mathbf{N}_{\Omega}}=0
on
\partial\Omega
, then

(8.65)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left((\mathcal{L}_{0}^{\log}u)^{2}-\left|(\nabla^{\log,*})^{2}u\right|_{g_{\log}}^{2}-n|\nabla^{\log}u|_{g_{\log}}^{2}\right)\,dV_{K}=\int_{\partial\Omega}\mathrm{II}_{\log}^{*}(\nabla^{\log}u,\nabla^{\log}u)\,d\sigma_{\log},

where
\mathrm{II}_{\log}^{*}(U,V):=g_{\log}(U,\nabla_{V}^{\log,*}\mathbf{N}_{\Omega})
for tangent vector fields
U,V
along
\partial\Omega
.

Proof.

The pointwise weighted Bochner identity from the proof of
8.7
, applied with
\Phi=\Phi_{0}
, gives

(8.66)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}_{\Phi_{0}}^{\log}(\nabla_{Z}^{\log}Z)=Z(\mathcal{L}_{0}^{\log}u)+n|\nabla^{\log}u|_{g_{\log}}^{2}+\left|(\nabla^{\log,*})^{2}u\right|_{g_{\log}}^{2}.

Here we used
(\nabla^{\log})^{2}\Phi_{0}=ng_{\log}
. Integrating over
\Omega
gives

(8.67)

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}g_{\log}(\nabla_{Z}^{\log}Z,\mathbf{N}_{\Omega})\,d\sigma_{\log}=\int_{\Omega}Z(\mathcal{L}_{0}^{\log}u)\,dV_{K}+n\int_{\Omega}|\nabla^{\log}u|_{g_{\log}}^{2}\,dV_{K}+\int_{\Omega}\left|(\nabla^{\log,*})^{2}u\right|_{g_{\log}}^{2}\,dV_{K}.

Integration-by-parts also gives

(8.68)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}Z(\mathcal{L}_{0}^{\log}u)\,dV_{K}=-\int_{\Omega}(\mathcal{L}_{0}^{\log}u)^{2}\,dV_{K}+\int_{\partial\Omega}(\mathcal{L}_{0}^{\log}u)u_{\mathbf{N}_{\Omega}}\,d\sigma_{\log}.

Hence the first formula follows.

If
u_{\mathbf{N}_{\Omega}}=0
on
\partial\Omega
, then
Z=\nabla^{\log}u
is tangent to
\partial\Omega
. Since
g_{\log}(Z,\mathbf{N}_{\Omega})=0
along
\partial\Omega
, differentiating tangentially in the direction
Z
and using (
8.28
) gives

(8.69)

\displaystyle 0=Z(g_{\log}(Z,\mathbf{N}_{\Omega}))=g_{\log}(\nabla_{Z}^{\log}Z,\mathbf{N}_{\Omega})+g_{\log}(Z,\nabla_{Z}^{\log,*}\mathbf{N}_{\Omega}).

Thus

(8.70)

\displaystyle-g_{\log}(\nabla_{Z}^{\log}Z,\mathbf{N}_{\Omega})=g_{\log}(Z,\nabla_{Z}^{\log,*}\mathbf{N}_{\Omega})=\mathrm{II}_{\log}^{*}(Z,Z),

and the second identity follows.
∎

Lemma 8.11
.

For
\varepsilon>0
, suppose
c
is a regular value of

(8.71)

\displaystyle q_{\varepsilon}=-\varepsilon\log\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{-Y_{i}/\varepsilon}\right).

Set
\Omega_{\varepsilon}=\{q_{\varepsilon}>c\}\Subset\Omega_{+}
and let

(8.72)

\displaystyle\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}=-\frac{\nabla^{\log}q_{\varepsilon}}{\left|\nabla^{\log}q_{\varepsilon}\right|_{g_{\log}}}

be the outward
g_{\log}
-unit normal to
\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}
.
Then, for every tangent vector
Z\in T\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}
,

(8.73)

\displaystyle\mathrm{II}_{\log,\varepsilon}^{*}(Z,Z)=-\frac{(\nabla^{\log})^{2}q_{\varepsilon}(Z,Z)}{|\nabla^{\log}q_{\varepsilon}|_{g_{\log}}}\geq 0.

Proof.

Since
(\nabla^{\log})^{2}Y_{i}=-g_{\log}
(see (
8.32
)), direct differentiation gives

(8.74)

\displaystyle dq_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{i}dY_{i},

\displaystyle(\nabla^{\log})^{2}q_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=-g_{\log}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{i}dY_{i}\otimes dY_{i}-\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\theta_{i}\theta_{j}dY_{i}\otimes dY_{j}\right),

where
\theta_{i}=\frac{e^{-Y_{i}/\varepsilon}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{-Y_{j}/\varepsilon}}
. The tensor in parentheses is non-negative definite, and therefore

(8.75)

\displaystyle(\nabla^{\log})^{2}q_{\varepsilon}\leq-g_{\log}\leq 0.

Note that
dq_{\varepsilon}(Z)=0
, that is,
g_{\log}(Z,\nabla^{\log}q_{\varepsilon})=0
. Now using (
8.28
),

(8.76)

\displaystyle\mathrm{II}_{\log,\varepsilon}^{*}(Z,Z)

\displaystyle=g_{\log}(Z,\nabla_{Z}^{\log,*}\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon})=-\frac{g_{\log}(Z,\nabla_{Z}^{\log,*}\nabla^{\log}q_{\varepsilon})}{|\nabla^{\log}q_{\varepsilon}|_{g_{\log}}}=-\frac{(\nabla^{\log})^{2}q_{\varepsilon}(Z,Z)}{|\nabla^{\log}q_{\varepsilon}|_{g_{\log}}}\geq 0.

∎

The proof of
Theorem 8.12
extends verbatim to the weighted measures
|X|^{q-n}dV_{K}
for all
q\in(0,n)
. For simplicity, we present the details only in the case

Theorem 8.12
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a
C^{2}_{+}
unconditional convex body, and let
F\in C^{1}(S^{n-1})
be unconditional. Then

(8.77)

\displaystyle n\int_{\Omega_{+}}(F-\bar{F}_{+})^{2}\,dV_{K}\leq\int_{\Omega_{+}}|\nabla^{\log}F|_{g_{\log}}^{2}\,dV_{K}\leq\int_{\Omega_{+}}|\nabla F|_{g}^{2}\,dV_{K},\quad\bar{F}_{+}=\frac{\int_{\Omega_{+}}F\,dV_{K}}{\int_{\Omega_{+}}dV_{K}}.

In particular

(8.78)

\displaystyle n\int_{S^{n-1}}(F-\bar{F})^{2}\,dV_{K}\leq\int_{S^{n-1}}|\nabla F|_{g}^{2}\,dV_{K},\quad\bar{F}=\frac{\int_{S^{n-1}}F\,dV_{K}}{\int_{S^{n-1}}dV_{K}}.

Moreover, equality in (
8.78
) holds only for constant functions.

Proof.

By approximation, we may assume that
F\in C^{\infty}(S^{n-1})
and
K
is
C^{\infty}_{+}
. On
\Omega_{+}
,

(8.79)

\displaystyle q(x)-\varepsilon\log n\leq q_{\varepsilon}(x)\leq q(x),\quad\text{where }q=\min_{i}Y_{i}.

Let
\varepsilon_{j}\to 0
and fix
x_{0}\in\Omega_{+}
. Since
q_{\varepsilon_{j}}(x_{0})\to q(x_{0})>-\infty
, there exists
j_{0}
such that
q_{\varepsilon_{j}}(x_{0})>-j
for every
j\geq j_{0}
. Hence, for every
j\geq j_{0}
, by Sard’s theorem we can choose a regular value
c_{j}\in(-j-1,-j)
of
q_{\varepsilon_{j}}
. Now set
\Omega_{j}=\{x\in\Omega_{+}:\ q_{\varepsilon_{j}}(x)>c_{j}\}
, which is a non-empty smooth domain. Since
q_{\varepsilon_{j}}\leq q
, the condition
q_{\varepsilon_{j}}>c_{j}
implies
Y_{i}>c_{j}
for every
i
, equivalently
X_{i}>e^{c_{j}}
for every
i
; hence
\Omega_{j}\Subset\Omega_{+}
. Moreover,
\Omega_{j}
is connected; the proof is similar to that of
3.25
. Finally, it is easy to see that
\mathbf{1}_{\Omega_{j}}\to\mathbf{1}_{\Omega_{+}}
pointwise.

Let
\mathbf{N}_{j}
be the outward
g_{\log}
-unit normal to
\partial\Omega_{j}
. For each
j
, we define

(8.80)

\displaystyle\bar{F}_{j}=\frac{\int_{\Omega_{j}}F\,dV_{K}}{\int_{\Omega_{j}}dV_{K}},\quad f_{j}=F-\bar{F}_{j}.

Since
\mathcal{L}_{0}^{\log}
is uniformly elliptic on
\Omega_{j}
, and since the compatibility condition holds, there exists a smooth solution
u_{j}
, unique up to an additive constant, of

(8.81)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{0}^{\log}u_{j}=-f_{j}\quad\text{in }\Omega_{j},\quad(u_{j})_{\mathbf{N}_{j}}=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega_{j}.

See the proof of
3.19
for more details.

The Bochner formula of
Theorem 8.10
, together with
8.11
, gives

(8.82)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{j}}f_{j}^{2}\,dV_{K}=\int_{\Omega_{j}}(\mathcal{L}_{0}^{\log}u_{j})^{2}\,dV_{K}\geq n\int_{\Omega_{j}}|\nabla^{\log}u_{j}|_{g_{\log}}^{2}\,dV_{K}.

On the other hand, integration-by-parts, the Neumann condition, and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality imply

(8.83)

\displaystyle n\int_{\Omega_{j}}(F-\bar{F}_{j})^{2}\,dV_{K}\leq\int_{\Omega_{j}}|\nabla^{\log}F|_{g_{\log}}^{2}\,dV_{K};

see the proof of
Theorem 3.20
for more details.

By
8.1
,
g_{\log}^{-1}\leq g^{-1}
. Therefore

(8.84)

\displaystyle n\int_{\Omega_{j}}(F-\bar{F}_{j})^{2}\,dV_{K}\leq\int_{\Omega_{j}}|\nabla^{\log}F|_{g_{\log}}^{2}\,dV_{K}\leq\int_{\Omega_{j}}|\nabla F|_{g}^{2}\,dV_{K}\leq\int_{\Omega_{+}}|\nabla F|_{g}^{2}\,dV_{K}.

Letting
j\to\infty
and using the dominated convergence theorem, we conclude that

(8.85)

\displaystyle n\int_{\Omega_{+}}(F-\bar{F}_{+})^{2}\,dV_{K}\leq\int_{\Omega_{+}}|\nabla^{\log}F|_{g_{\log}}^{2}\,dV_{K}\leq\int_{\Omega_{+}}|\nabla F|_{g}^{2}\,dV_{K}.

Since
K
and
F
are unconditional, summing over all orthants gives the non-strict version of the inequality on
S^{n-1}
.

Now suppose
F\in C^{1}(S^{n-1})
and
K
is
C^{2}_{+}
. On
\Omega_{+}
, due to
8.1
, we have
g_{\log}>g
, and hence

(8.86)

\displaystyle|\nabla^{\log}F|_{g_{\log}}^{2}<|\nabla F|_{g}^{2}

at every point where
dF\neq 0
. Therefore, if
F
is non-constant on
\Omega_{+}
, then

(8.87)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{+}}|\nabla^{\log}F|_{g_{\log}}^{2}\,dV_{K}<\int_{\Omega_{+}}|\nabla F|_{g}^{2}\,dV_{K}.

Thus equality in the theorem can occur only if
F
is constant on
\Omega_{+}
. By unconditionality and continuity, this implies that
F
is constant on
S^{n-1}
.
∎

Theorem 8.13
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a
C^{\infty}_{+}
unconditional convex body.
The equality in

(8.88)

\displaystyle n\int_{\Omega_{+}}(F-\bar{F}_{+})^{2}\,dV_{K}\leq\int_{\Omega_{+}}|\nabla^{\log}F|_{g_{\log}}^{2}\,dV_{K}

is attained by the functions

(8.89)

\displaystyle F_{i}=\frac{x_{i}X_{i}}{h}-\frac{1}{n},\quad i=1,\ldots,n.

Proof.

Let
\bm{\xi}^{\ast}
be the conormal field defined by

(8.90)

\displaystyle\bm{\xi}^{\ast}=\frac{Px}{h}=\left(\frac{x_{1}X_{1}}{h},\ldots,\frac{x_{n}X_{n}}{h}\right).

Then

(8.91)

\displaystyle\langle\bm{\xi}^{\ast},dY(V)\rangle=0\quad\text{for all }V\in T\Omega_{+},\quad\langle\bm{\xi}^{\ast},\bm{\xi}\rangle=1.

We first prove

(8.92)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\log}_{0}\bm{\xi}^{\ast}+n\bm{\xi}^{\ast}=\bm{\xi}.

Differentiating the relations
\langle\bm{\xi}^{\ast},\bm{\xi}\rangle=1
and
\langle\bm{\xi}^{\ast},dY(V)\rangle=0
in the direction
U
and using the affine Gauss formula

(8.93)

\displaystyle D_{U}dY(V)=dY(\nabla^{\log}_{U}V)-g_{\log}(U,V)\bm{\xi}

yields

(8.94)

\displaystyle\langle d\bm{\xi}^{\ast}(U),\bm{\xi}\rangle=0,\quad\langle d\bm{\xi}^{\ast}(U),dY(V)\rangle=g_{\log}(U,V).

For
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, set
f_{a}=\langle\bm{\xi}^{\ast},a\rangle
.
Note that the vector
a
decomposes uniquely into a tangential part and a transversal part:

(8.95)

\displaystyle a=dY(W)+f_{a}\bm{\xi}.

For every
V\in T\Omega_{+}
, we have

(8.96)

\displaystyle df_{a}(V)=\langle d\bm{\xi}^{\ast}(V),a\rangle.

Hence, using
\langle d\bm{\xi}^{\ast}(V),\bm{\xi}\rangle=0
and
\langle d\bm{\xi}^{\ast}(V),dY(W)\rangle=g_{\log}(V,W)
,

(8.97)

\displaystyle df_{a}(V)=\langle d\bm{\xi}^{\ast}(V),dY(W)\rangle=g_{\log}(V,W).

Thus
W=\nabla^{\log}f_{a}
and

(8.98)

\displaystyle a=dY(\nabla^{\log}f_{a})+f_{a}\bm{\xi}.

Now we differentiate this identity in the direction
U
:

(8.99)

\displaystyle 0=D_{U}dY(\nabla^{\log}f_{a})+df_{a}(U)\bm{\xi}.

Using the affine Gauss formula,

(8.100)

\displaystyle D_{U}dY(Z)=dY(\nabla^{\log}_{U}Z)-g_{\log}(U,Z)\bm{\xi},

we obtain

(8.101)

\displaystyle dY(\nabla^{\log}_{U}\nabla^{\log}f_{a})=0\implies\nabla^{\log}_{U}\nabla^{\log}f_{a}=0.

Moreover, by the conjugacy relation between
\nabla^{\log}
and
\nabla^{\log,*}
,

(8.102)

\displaystyle g_{\log}(\nabla^{\log}_{U}\nabla^{\log}f_{a},V)=(\nabla^{\log,*})^{2}f_{a}(U,V).

Therefore

(8.103)

\displaystyle(\nabla^{\log,*})^{2}f_{a}=0\implies\Delta^{\log}f_{a}=0\implies\mathcal{L}^{\log}_{0}f_{a}=g_{\log}(\nabla^{\log}\eta,\nabla^{\log}f_{a}).

We compute
(\nabla^{\log,*})^{2}f_{a}(U,V)
. Taking the Euclidean inner product of
\bm{\xi}
with
a
yields

(8.104)

\displaystyle\langle dY(\nabla^{\log}f_{a}),\bm{\xi}\rangle=\langle a,\bm{\xi}\rangle-nf_{a}.

On the other hand, we have

(8.105)

\displaystyle\langle dY(\nabla^{\log}f_{a}),\bm{\xi}\rangle=d\eta(\nabla^{\log}f_{a})=g_{\log}(\nabla^{\log}\eta,\nabla^{\log}f_{a}).

Hence

(8.106)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\log}_{0}f_{a}+nf_{a}=\langle a,\bm{\xi}\rangle.

Next, we justify the integrations by parts on
\Omega_{+}
. We recall from the proof of
Theorem 8.12
that
q=\min_{k}Y_{k}
and
q_{\varepsilon}=-\varepsilon\log\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}e^{-Y_{k}/\varepsilon}\right)
. We choose a sequence
\varepsilon_{j}\to 0
, and for simplicity we set
q_{j}=q_{\varepsilon_{j}}
. Let
\chi_{j}=\rho_{j}\circ q_{j}
, where
\rho_{j}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
satisfies
0\leq\rho_{j}\leq 1
,
\rho_{j}=0
on
(-\infty,-j-1]
,
\rho_{j}=1
on
[-j,\infty)
, and
|\rho_{j}^{\prime}|\leq 2
. Then
\chi_{j}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega_{+})
and
\chi_{j}\to 1
pointwise on
\Omega_{+}
.

Recall
p_{i}=x_{i}X_{i}/h
. From
E_{i}=dY(\nabla^{\log}p_{i})+p_{i}\bm{\xi}
, we obtain
dY_{k}(\nabla^{\log}p_{i})=\delta_{ik}-p_{i}
. Moreover, if we set
\theta_{j,k}=e^{-Y_{k}/\varepsilon_{j}}/\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}e^{-Y_{\ell}/\varepsilon_{j}}
, then
dq_{j}=\sum_{k}\theta_{j,k}dY_{k}
. Therefore

(8.107)

\displaystyle dq_{j}(\nabla^{\log}p_{i})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\theta_{j,k}dY_{k}(\nabla^{\log}p_{i})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\theta_{j,k}(\delta_{ik}-p_{i})=\theta_{j,i}-p_{i},

where we used
\sum_{k}\theta_{j,k}=1
. Since
p_{i},\theta_{j,i}\in(0,1)
on
\Omega_{+}
, we have

(8.108)

\displaystyle|dq_{j}(\nabla^{\log}p_{i})|\leq 1.

Due to
\mathcal{L}_{0}^{\log}F_{i}=-nF_{i}
,
\nabla^{\log}F_{i}=\nabla^{\log}p_{i}
, and
8.6
,

(8.109)

\displaystyle n\int_{\Omega_{+}}\chi_{j}F_{i}^{2}\,dV_{K}=\int_{\Omega_{+}}\chi_{j}|\nabla^{\log}F_{i}|_{g_{\log}}^{2}\,dV_{K}+\int_{\Omega_{+}}F_{i}\rho_{j}^{\prime}(q_{j})dq_{j}(\nabla^{\log}p_{i})\,dV_{K}.

Note that
\rho_{j}^{\prime}(q_{j})
is supported in
A_{j}=\{-j-1<q_{j}<-j\}
, and
\mathbf{1}_{A_{j}}\to 0
pointwise. Hence

(8.110)

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega_{+}}F_{i}\rho_{j}^{\prime}(q_{j})dq_{j}(\nabla^{\log}p_{i})\,dV_{K}\right|\leq 2\|F_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{+})}dV_{K}(A_{j})\to 0.

Since
0\leq\chi_{j}\leq 1
and
\chi_{j}\to 1
pointwise, Fatou’s lemma gives

(8.111)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{+}}|\nabla^{\log}F_{i}|_{g_{\log}}^{2}\,dV_{K}\leq n\int_{\Omega_{+}}F_{i}^{2}\,dV_{K}.

Finally, by
[
HI25
, Lem. 4.4]
, we have

(8.112)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V(K)}\int_{S^{n-1}}\frac{x}{h}\otimes X\,dV_{K}=I\implies\int_{\Omega_{+}}F_{i}\,dV_{K}=0.

Consequently, due to
Theorem 8.12
,

(8.113)

\displaystyle n\int_{\Omega_{+}}F_{i}^{2}\,dV_{K}=\int_{\Omega_{+}}|\nabla^{\log}F_{i}|_{g_{\log}}^{2}\,dV_{K}.

∎

Remark 8.15
.

For
p\in(0,1]
, the
L_{p}
version of centro-affine geometry for
C^{\infty}_{+}
unconditional convex bodies is obtained by replacing the logarithmic map by the power map

(8.117)

\displaystyle T_{p}:(0,\infty)^{n}\to(0,\infty)^{n},\quad T_{p}(z)=\frac{1}{p}\left(z_{1}^{p},\dots,z_{n}^{p}\right).

On
\Omega_{+}
, we define

(8.118)

\displaystyle Y_{p}=T_{p}\circ X,\quad(Y_{p})_{i}=\frac{X_{i}^{p}}{p},\quad\bm{\xi}_{p}=pY_{p}.

The induced
L_{p}
-centro-affine structure is given by

(8.119)

\displaystyle D_{v}dY_{p}(w)

\displaystyle=dY_{p}(\nabla^{(p)}_{v}w)-g_{p}(v,w)\bm{\xi}_{p},\quad dV_{p}/dx=\det(dY_{p}(e_{1}),\dots,dY_{p}(e_{n-1}),\bm{\xi}_{p}).

Since
D_{v}\bm{\xi}_{p}=p\,dY_{p}(v)
,

(8.120)

\displaystyle D_{v}\bm{\xi}_{p}=dY_{p}(\mathcal{S}_{p}v)-\theta_{p}(v)\bm{\xi}_{p}\quad\text{with}\quad\mathcal{S}_{p}=pI,\quad\theta_{p}=0.

Thus the structure is equiaffine, and the Gauss equation gives

(8.121)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Rm}^{\nabla^{(p)}}(u,v)w=p\left(g_{p}(v,w)u-g_{p}(u,w)v\right)\implies\operatorname{Ric}^{\nabla^{(p)}}=p(n-2)g_{p}.

The case
p=1
corresponds to the classical centro-affine geometry, which is globally defined on the whole sphere. For
0\leq p<1
, however, the corresponding
L_{p}
-type geometry is confined to the positive orthant
S^{n-1}\cap(0,\infty)^{n}
. Nevertheless, for
p=0
, this restriction is compensated by the fact that the resulting geometry is flat.

By deriving the corresponding Bochner formulas and carrying out the same approximation scheme as in the case
p=0
, together with an appropriate Bakry–Émery argument, one should obtain another proof of
Theorem 6.6
. We leave the details to the interested reader.

It is tempting to view the logarithmic centro-affine geometry as the shadow of a geometry for origin-symmetric
C^{\infty}_{+}
convex bodies, one that would ideally produce the additional
+1
contribution without needing the restriction to
(0,\infty)^{n}
. At present, however, such a geometry remains elusive, and with it the source of the additional
+1
contribution.

We end this remark with a dictionary relating the objects in logarithmic centro-affine geometry in the unconditional class to those in centro-affine geometry. Let us define the
g
-self-adjoint endomorphism
\mathcal{A}:T\Omega_{+}\to T\Omega_{+}
by

(8.122)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}U

\displaystyle=U+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{x_{i}X_{i}}{h}d\log X_{i}(U)\nabla\log X_{i},

where
\nabla\log X_{i}
denotes the
g
-gradient of
\log X_{i}
. Recall
\eta=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log X_{i}
. We have:

\begin{array}[]{|c|c|}\hline\cr\text{Logarithmic centro-affine}&\text{Centro-affine}\\
\hline\cr Y=\log X,\,\text{transversal }(1,\ldots,1)&X,\,\text{transversal }X\\
\hline\cr g_{\log}(U,V)&g(\mathcal{A}U,V)\\
\hline\cr\nabla^{\log}u&\mathcal{A}^{-1}\nabla u\\
\hline\cr e^{\eta}dV_{\log}&dV_{K}\\
\hline\cr\operatorname{div}^{\log}_{-\eta}W&\operatorname{div}_{\nabla}W\\
\hline\cr\operatorname{Rm}^{\nabla^{\log}}=0&\operatorname{Ric}^{\nabla}=(n-2)g\\
\hline\cr(\nabla^{\log})^{2}\eta+ng_{\log}=0&\nabla^{2}X_{i}+X_{i}g=0\\
\hline\cr\mathcal{L}^{\log}_{-\eta}\left(\frac{x_{i}X_{i}}{h}\right)=-n\left(\frac{x_{i}X_{i}}{h}-\frac{1}{n}\right)&\Delta\frac{x_{i}}{h}=-(n-1)\frac{x_{i}}{h}\\
\hline\cr\end{array}
