Proenkephalin neurons in the adBNST mitigate stress-induced anxiety and estrous cycle disruption in female mice
Authors: Wenwen Su, Yongxiu Song, Xia Zhang, Bin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic stress is a major risk factor for anxiety disorders and is often accompanied by disruptions in female reproductive cyclicity, but the stress-responsive neural populations that shape these comorbid behavioral and physiological outcomes remain poorly defined. The bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (BNST) is a major node for sustained anxiety and stress integration, yet the contribution of genetically defined BNST neurons to restraint stress is not fully resolved in females. Here, we identify proenkephalin-expressing neurons in the anterior dorsal BNST (adBNSTPENK) as a restraint-responsive population that constrains anxiety-like behaviors and modulates cytology-based estrous cycle organization. Acute chemogenetic inhibition of adBNSTPENK neurons produced anxiety-like behaviors in females, whereas activation attenuated acute restraint stress (ARS)-induced anxiety-like behaviors. Female mice subjected to chronic restraint stress (CRS) exhibited both anxiety-like avoidance and disrupted estrous-cycle organization, which can be mitigated by sustained activation of adBNSTPENK neurons. These findings suggest that adBNSTPENK neurons form a stress-recruited limbic circuit node that buffers stress-induced anxiety-like behaviors and is associated with improved vaginal cytology-based estrous-cycle organization under chronic stress.

Introduction

Anxiety disorders are among the most prevalent psychiatric conditions worldwide and impose a substantial individual and societal burden (McGrath et al., 2023). Clinically, anxiety disorders are frequently accompanied by somatic complaints, autonomic dysregulation, sleep disturbance, and endocrine abnormalities (Haug et al., 2004; Hoehn-Saric et al., 1989; Mallorquí-Bagué et al., 2016; Stein and Sareen, 2015). Epidemiological studies consistently demonstrate a marked sex bias in anxiety-related disorders, with women exhibiting greater vulnerability to stress (Altemus et al., 2014; Bale and Epperson, 2015). In parallel, psychological stress is associated with menstrual irregularity and altered cycle length in women (Allsworth et al., 2007; Rafique and Al-Sheikh, 2018; Williams et al., 2007; Yamamoto et al., 2009), suggesting potentially shared neurobiological substrates underlying stress-related affective disorders and reproductive dysfunction.

Rodent estrous cyclicity provides a tractable experimental readout of this neuroendocrine regulation. Vaginal cytology enables reliable classification of proestrus, estrus, metestrus, and diestrus based on cellular composition (Ajayi and Akhigbe, 2020; Byers et al., 2012; Caligioni, 2009; Cora et al., 2015). Deviations from the ordered sequence of these stages, including prolonged phase occupancy, abnormal transitions, altered estrus or diestrus duration, and reduced cycle regularity, are widely interpreted as indicators of disrupted estrous cyclicity (Adeniyi and Agoreyo, 2019; Ajayi and Akhigbe, 2020). However, while stress-induced hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis suppression has been extensively studied, the upstream limbic circuits that couple chronic stress exposure to both anxiety-like behaviors and estrous cycle dysfunction remain incompletely defined.

The bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (BNST), a key component of the central extended amygdala, is well positioned to mediate such coupling. The BNST is critically involved in sustained anxiety states, diffuse threat processing, and stress integration (Fox and Shackman, 2019; Lebow and Chen, 2016; Walker and Davis, 2008). Human neuroimaging studies further implicate the BNST in anxiety- and addiction-related disorders (Avery et al., 2016). Importantly, the BNST exhibits marked cellular and functional heterogeneity, with distinct neuronal populations exerting divergent effects on behavior (Gungor and Paré, 2016; Ortiz-Juza et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2020). Circuit studies have shown that specific BNST pathways regulate anxiety-related behaviors in a projection- and neurotransmitter-specific manner (Kim et al., 2013; Namburi et al., 2015; Tye, 2018; Xiao et al., 2021). Moreover, stress-responsive neuropeptide systems, including corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) and neuropeptide Y, modulate BNST synaptic transmission and contribute to anxiety and stress resilience (Heilig, 2004; Kash and Winder, 2006). Recent studies have identified BNST neuronal populations, including CRH- and protein kinase C delta (PKCδ)-expressing neurons, that are selectively recruited by aversive conditions to promote anxiety-like behaviors, particularly in females (Williford et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024). These findings underscore the need to define additional genetically specified BNST populations that may shape anxiety-like behavioral output during stress. Beyond its established role in anxiety, the BNST is also positioned to influence reproductive neuroendocrine regulation. Prior work has shown that BNST projections provide input to gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) cells (Pompolo et al., 2005) and that intra-BNST infusion of CRH can alter pulsatile luteinizing-hormone secretion (Li et al., 2011), suggesting that this extended-amygdala region may provide an upstream limbic route through which stress affects HPG-axis-related outcomes. In the present study, vaginal cytology is used as an integrated readout of estrous-cycle organization.

Proenkephalin (PENK)-expressing neurons represent a compelling candidate population. Enkephalins are endogenous opioid peptides involved in analgesia, reward processing, affective regulation, and stress resilience (Belluzzi and Stein, 1977; Henry et al., 2017; Thompson and Neugebauer, 2017). Beyond these classical functions, opioid signaling also regulates reproductive neuroendocrine function, including GnRH release, pituitary sensitivity, and gonadotropin secretion (Barkan et al., 1985; Böttcher et al., 2017). Nevertheless, the anterior dorsal bed nucleus of the stria terminalis proenkephalin (adBNSTPENK) neuronal responses to restraint stress and their modulation in anxiety-related phenotypes remain unknown.

Here, we investigated whether adBNSTPENK neurons represent a restraint-responsive BNST population that regulates anxiety-like behaviors under basal and stress-challenged conditions and is associated with cytology-based estrous-cycle organization during chronic stress. Combining chronic restraint stress (CRS) with chemogenetic manipulation, we examined how this genetically defined BNST population was recruited by stress and whether its activity was required to constrain anxiety-like behaviors and whether sustained activation improves cytology-based estrous-cycle organization after CRS. This approach reveals adBNSTPENK neurons as a stress-responsive limbic population whose modulation reshapes anxiety-related behavioral output and female estrous cyclicity.

Materials and methods

Animals

Adult C57BL/6J male and female mice (8-12 weeks old) were used in this study. Animals were group-housed under a 12-h light/dark cycle (lights on at 7:00 a.m. - 7:00 p.m.) in a controlled environment maintained at 24 ± 2°C and 50%-60% humidity. Food and water were provided ad libitum. All mice were allowed at least one week of acclimation to the animal facility before any experimental procedure. Female mice were monitored daily for estrous cycle stages by vaginal cytology throughout the experimental period. All experimental protocols were approved by the Qingdao University Animal Ethics Committee (AEC, Approval No. QDU-AEC-2024364). For experiments requiring transgene expression, the following mouse lines were used: Penk-Cre (B6; 129S-Penktm2(cre)Hze/J, JAX: 025112) and Ai14 (B6; 129S6-Gt (ROSA) 26Sortm14(CAG-tdTomato)Hze/J, JAX: 007908). All mice used in this study were bred in-house and genotyped before experiments.

Viral vectors and stereotaxic surgery

Penk-Cre mice were anesthetized with 1% pentobarbital sodium and placed in a stereotaxic frame. A small craniotomy was made above the adBNST (AP +0.28 mm, ML ±0.98 mm, DV -4.00 mm), and 40 nL viral vectors were delivered through a glass micropipette connected to a microsyringe pump at an injection rate of 20 nL/min. For chemogenetic experiments, Cre-dependent adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors encoding excitatory (hM3Dq) or inhibitory (hM4Di) DREADDs were used. For calcium recording, an AAV-hSyn-DIO-GCaMP6m was injected into the adBNST, and an optical fiber was implanted above the injection site (AP +0.28 mm, ML ±0.98 mm, DV -3.80 mm) in Penk-CreAi14 mice. After each injection, the micropipette was left in place for 7 min to allow sufficient diffusion before being slowly withdrawn. Mice were allowed at least three weeks of recovery before behavioral testing. Viral expression and fiber placement were verified histologically after experiments; animals with substantial off-target expression in adjacent striatal regions, missed fiber placement, or insufficient expression within the adBNST were excluded according to predefined criteria.

The following viral vectors were purchased from Taitool (Shanghai, China):

AAV2/9-hSyn-DIO-hM3D(Gq)-mCherry-WPRE-pA (4.75 × 1012 vg/mL), AAV2/9-hSyn-DIO-hM4D(Gi)-mCherry-WPRE-pA (4.32 × 1012 vg/mL), AAV2/9-hEF1α-DIO-mCherry-WPRE-pA (1.99 × 1012 vg/mL), AAV2/9-hSyn-DIO-GCaMP6m-WPRE-pA (3.47 × 1012 vg/mL).

All viruses were aliquoted and stored at −80°C, and repeated freeze-thaw cycles were avoided.

Estrous cycle monitoring and analysis

Estrous cycle stages were monitored daily by vaginal cytology. Vaginal lavage samples were collected each morning at 9:00 a.m. using sterile saline, transferred onto glass slides, air-dried, and examined under a light microscope. Based on the relative proportions of epithelial cells, cornified cells, and leukocytes, stages were classified as proestrus, estrus, metestrus, or diestrus.

Estrous cycle organization was evaluated using the following parameters: the number of complete cycles, aberrant transitions, estrous duration, diestrous duration, and estrous cycle ratio (ECR). A complete cycle was defined as an ordered progression through proestrus, estrus, metestrus, and diestrus before returning to proestrus. Aberrant transitions were defined as deviations from the normal sequential progression, including irregular transitions or prolonged arrest in a given phase. The ECR was calculated as: ECR = DurationEstrus/DurationDiestrus. Cycle trajectories for individual mice were visualized longitudinally and interpreted as indicators of cycle organization rather than HPG-axis normalization.

Chronic and acute restraint stress

Mice were individually placed into ventilated restraint tubes for 2 h per day on 14 consecutive days. Restraint sessions were performed at the same time each day to minimize circadian variability. Control mice were handled daily without being restrained. To avoid the potential confound of repeated vaginal lavage during restraint exposure, estrous cyclicity was monitored for 8 consecutive days after the end of CRS.

For acute stress experiments, mice underwent a single 4-h restraint session using the same apparatus. Immediately after stress exposure, behavioral tests or fiber photometry recordings were performed to assess the acute effects of stress on anxiety-like behaviors and neuronal activity.

In vivo fiber photometry recording and analysis

Fiber photometry was used to record calcium dynamics of adBNSTPENK neurons under basal conditions and during ARS exposure. Female Penk-CreAi14 mice were used for fiber-photometry recordings. After viral expression of GCaMP6m in PENK neurons, an optical fiber was implanted above the adBNST. Following recovery, fluorescence signals were collected using a Thinkertech fiber photometry system. Data were analyzed offline using custom scripts written in MATLAB. Raw fluorescence traces were corrected for baseline drift. The relative change in fluorescence was calculated as:ΔF/F=(F−F0)/F0where F is the raw fluorescence signal and F0 is the baseline fluorescence. For event-aligned fiber photometry analysis, time point 0 was identified as the beginning of the rising phase of the corrected calcium signal and a 2-s window prior to time point 0 was adopted as the baseline control. Calcium traces were aligned to this time point to visualize the temporal dynamics of spontaneous adBNSTPENK neuronal activity. For statistical analysis, calcium-event measures were first averaged within each mouse, and mice were treated as biological replicates.

Chemogenetic manipulation

For acute chemogenetic manipulation, to test the causal role of adBNSTPENK neurons in anxiety regulation, chemogenetic activation or inhibition was performed. Mice expressing hM3Dq (excitatory) or hM4Di (inhibitory) DREADDs in adBNSTPENK neurons received an intraperitoneal injection of clozapine-N-oxide (CNO) prior to behavioral testing. Anxiety-like behaviors were then assessed using the OFT and EPM in both male and female mice.

For chemogenetic activation during acute stress, to evaluate whether adBNSTPENK neurons contribute to stress-induced anxiety, chemogenetic activation was combined with ARS. Mice were injected with CNO (3 mg/kg for hM3Dq-expressing mice; 5 mg/kg for hM4Di-expressing mice) immediately after exposure to ARS, and behavioral tests were performed 45 min later.

For chronic chemogenetic manipulation, hM3Dq-, hM4Di-, or mCherry-expressing Penk-Cre mice received CNO in drinking water daily throughout the manipulation period. As behavioral phenotyping of hM3Dq and hM4Di cohorts was performed in parallel with fully matched animal and procedural variables, a common CNO-administered mCherry control group was included for primary omnibus statistical comparisons. Mice expressing hM3Dq in adBNSTPENK neurons received CNO in their drinking water daily throughout the 14-day CRS paradigm. A working solution was prepared by dissolving 1.5 mg of CNO in 100 mL of double-distilled water and supplied as the sole drinking source. The CNO solution was stored in light-proof containers and replenished with freshly prepared solution every 48 h. Post restraint stress, anxiety-like behaviors were evaluated, and estrous cycle fluctuations were monitored daily over 8 consecutive days.

Behavioral tests

Anxiety-like behavior was assessed using the open field test (OFT) and elevated plus maze (EPM) test. For the OFT, each mouse was placed individually in the center of a square arena (45 × 45 × 45 cm) and allowed to explore freely for 10 min. Movements were recorded using the EthoVision XT video tracking system. The following parameters were analyzed: total distance traveled, time spent in the center zone, and number of entries into the center. For the EPM, each mouse was placed on the central platform (arm dimensions: 30 × 6 × 15 cm; two open arms and two closed arms) facing an open arm and allowed to explore for 5 min. Time spent in open arms, number of open-arm entries, and total locomotor activity were quantified.

Immunohistochemistry and RNAscope

Female Penk-CreAi14 mice were deeply anesthetized and transcardially perfused with phosphate-buffered saline followed by 4% paraformaldehyde. Brains were post-fixed, cryoprotected, and sectioned into 30 μm slices. Coronal sections containing the BNST were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against cFos and mCherry. The following primary antibodies were used: guinea pig anti-cFos (Synaptic Systems, Cat# 226308, 1:4000, RRID: AB_2905595) and rat anti-mCherry (Invitrogen, Cat# M11217, 1:1000, RRID: AB_2536611). The next day, sections were incubated with appropriate Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen, 1:1000) for 2 h at room temperature in the dark. To determine the molecular relationship between PENK neurons and other adBNST populations, adBNST sections of Penk-CreAi14 mice were processed for PKCδ immunofluorescence staining using mouse anti-PKCδ antibody (BD Biosciences, Cat# 610397, 1:2000, RRID: AB_397753) or Crh RNAscope in situ hybridization using Crh RNA probe (ACD, Cat# 25296A).

Bioinformatic analysis

For the bioinformatic analysis, the processed female adBNST single-nucleus RNA-seq expression matrix was used to examine the transcriptomic relationship among Penk-, Prkcd-, and Crh-expressing neuronal populations. Nuclei assigned to adBNST neuronal clusters were retained for analysis. Penk, Prkcd, and Crh expression was visualized on a t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (tSNE) map. Marker-positive nuclei were defined as nuclei with detectable normalized expression of the corresponding transcript. Co-expression was quantified by calculating the percentage of Penk-positive nuclei that also expressed Prkcd or Crh. The reciprocal distribution of Penk expression among Prkcd- or Crh-positive nuclei was also summarized when applicable.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0) and MATLAB. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Non-parametric tests were applied for data deviating from normal distribution or exhibiting unequal variance. For two-group comparisons, t-tests were used for normally distributed data, whereas the Mann-Whitney U test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied for non-normally distributed data. For multiple comparisons, analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used, followed by post hoc corrections. The n value represents biological replicates as indicated in the figure legends. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Acute restraint stress recruits adBNSTPENK neurons

Acute restraint stress (ARS) rapidly engages extended-amygdala circuits (Roozendaal, McEwenChattarji, 2009). Restraint stress induces a rapid shift in behavioral and physiological state. Given the established role of the adBNST in stress integration, we first mapped neuronal activation in this region following ARS to identify restraint-responsive neuronal populations in this region. A single 4-h ARS robustly increased neuronal activation within the BNST, as evidenced by a significant elevation in cFos-positive cells that were spatially confined to the anterior dorsal subdivision (Fig. 1A–C). As we previously reported, PENK-expressing neurons exhibit a sexually dimorphic enrichment in the heterogeneous adBNST, while they are largely non-overlapping with PKCδ- or CRH-expressing populations (sFig. 1A–E). In line with this, independent snRNA-seq data confirmed that Penk+ nuclei exhibited minimal overlap with Prkcd+ (7.49%) or Crh+ (2.25%) nuclei (sFig. 1F–I). We next asked whether this stress-responsive ensemble included PENK-expressing neurons. In Penk-CreAi14 mice, approximately 22.22% of cFos-positive neurons co-expressed PENK, whereas 9.41% of PENK-lineage neurons expressed cFos after stress exposure (Fig. 1D–F), suggesting that PENK neurons were recruited as a defined subset of the ARS-responsive adBNST ensemble. To further assess whether adBNSTPENK neurons were dynamically engaged during restraint stress, we performed fiber photometry recordings in vivo with mice expressing GCaMP6m selectively in PENK neurons. Viral expression and fiber placement were verified histologically, with strong overlap between GCaMP6m and tdTomato signals confirming cell-type specificity (Fig. 1G and H). During ARS, adBNSTPENK neurons exhibited an increase in calcium activity, as reflected by elevated ΔF/F signals across the restraint epoch (Fig. 1I and J). Quantitative measures demonstrated significant increases in the area under the curve (AUC) and an increasing trend in peak amplitude (Fig. 1K and L), indicating enhanced neuronal excitability. Acute inhibition of adBNSTPENK neurons promotes anxiety-like behaviors in female mice

The stress-evoked recruitment of adBNSTPENK neurons suggested that this population may participate in an adaptive response to restraint stress, potentially buffering the expression of anxiety-like behaviors. To determine whether endogenous adBNSTPENK activity is required to constrain anxiety-like behaviors, we acutely inhibited these neurons in naïve female mice using a chemogenetic strategy with Cre-dependent designer receptors exclusively activated by designer drugs (DREADDs). Cre-dependent inhibitory hM4Di was bilaterally infused into the adBNST of female Penk-Cre mice, followed by intraperitoneal CNO administration before behavioral testing (Fig. 2A). Histological examination confirmed hM4Di-mCherry expression in the adBNST (Fig. 2B and I). We additionally delineated the viral expression boundaries for all included mice (10 per viral condition) to show that viral spread was largely confined to the adBNST (sFig. 2). Acute inhibition of adBNSTPENK neurons via hM4Di before behavioral testing significantly decreased exploratory behavior in anxiogenic environments. Specifically, mice displayed reduced time spent in both the center zone during the OFT and the open-arm during the EPM (Fig. 2C–H). Heatmap representations further illustrated a redistribution of spatial occupancy from central to peripheral or closed-arm regions, consistent with increased anxiety-like behaviors. Notably, these effects were consistent across multiple behavioral metrics, including both duration and entry frequency, reinforcing the reliability of the phenotype. In male mice, a similar pattern was observed. Acute inhibition of adBNSTPENK neurons produced robust anxiogenic effects, as evidenced by decreased center time in the OFT and decreased open-arm time in the EPM (Fig. 2J–O). Activation of adBNSTPENK neurons attenuates ARS-induced anxiety-like behaviors

Although acute inhibition of adBNSTPENK neurons promoted anxiety-like behaviors, acute activation of this population under basal conditions produced limited behavioral effects in naïve males (Fig. S3). This asymmetry suggested that the behavioral impact of adBNSTPENK activation may depend on internal state, with its anxiety-constraining function becoming more evident under stress-challenged conditions. To test this possibility, we examined whether their activation could counteract ARS-induced anxiety-like behaviors in females. Female Penk-Cre mice expressing Cre-dependent hM3Dq or mCherry control virus in the adBNST were subjected to a single 4-h restraint session and received intraperitoneal injection of CNO (Fig. 3A and B). ARS induced a robust anxiogenic behavioral profile, characterized by reduced center exploration in the OFT and decreased open-arm engagement in the EPM. When adBNSTPENK neurons were activated via hM3Dq immediately after stress exposure, these behavioral deficits were significantly attenuated. Quantitative analysis showed restoration of both OFT and EPM parameters toward baseline levels, indicating a robust reversal of stress-induced behavioral suppression. Heatmap analysis further demonstrated normalized exploration patterns, with increased occupancy of previously avoided zones (Fig. 3C–F). These findings indicate that activation of adBNSTPENK neurons buffers acute stress-induced anxiety-like behaviors in females. To determine whether the stress-buffering effect was also present in males, we performed the same ARS paradigm in male Penk-Cre mice expressing hM3Dq or mCherry in the adBNST (Fig. 3G and H). Chemogenetic activation of adBNSTPENK neurons after ARS significantly restored center exploration in the OFT and open-arm exploration in the EPM (Fig. 3I–K). Chronic activation of adBNSTPENK neurons reduces anxiety-like behaviors in female mice

Given that adBNSTPENK neurons constrain anxiety-like behaviors in a state-dependent manner and chronic stress induces long-lasting behavioral alterations, we next investigated whether sustained manipulation of adBNSTPENK neurons would reshape anxiety-like behavioral output in female mice. Chronic inhibition of adBNSTPENK neurons via hM4Di was achieved by continuous CNO administration through drinking water during the chronic manipulation period (Fig. 4A). Behavioral trajectories and spatial occupancy patterns remained comparable to control animals in the OFT or EPM, suggesting that prolonged suppression of this circuit does not substantially impact baseline anxiety in females (Fig. 4B–H). In contrast, chronic activation of adBNSTPENK neurons via hM3Dq induced a consistent shift toward anxiolytic profiles. Mice exhibited increased center exploration in the OFT and greater time spent in the open arms of the EPM (Fig. 4I–O), which emerged over the course of repeated activation. Collectively, these results reveal a temporal dissociation in the functional impact of adBNSTPENK manipulation, with chronic activation, but not inhibition, producing anxiolytic outcomes in female mice. Chronic restraint stress induces anxiety-like behaviors and disrupts estrous cycle organization

We next established a 14-day chronic restraint stress (CRS) model to determine whether repeated restraint produces persistent anxiety-like behaviors and estrous-cycle disruption in female mice. Behavioral assays revealed a robust anxiogenic phenotype following CRS exposure. In the OFT, stressed mice displayed a significant reduction in time spent in the center zone, indicative of increased anxiety-like behaviors (Fig. 5A–C). Similarly, in the EPM, CRS-exposed mice exhibited decreased open-arm time and reduced open-arm entries, further supporting increased anxiety-like behaviors (Fig. 5D–F). Importantly, total locomotor activity remained unchanged (Fig. 5C), suggesting that these behavioral alterations were not confounded by motor impairments. In parallel with behavioral changes, CRS profoundly disrupted estrous cyclicity. Vaginal cytology revealed that, instead of progressing through regular 4-5 -day cycles, stressed mice frequently exhibited prolonged occupancy within individual estrous stages, with some phases extending to approximately 2-3 days (Fig. 5G and H). Quantitative analysis over an 8-day observation window demonstrated a higher proportion of diestrus, a reduction in the number of complete cycles, accompanied by an increase in aberrant transitions (Fig. 5I–N). Chronic activation of adBNSTPENK neurons mitigates CRS-induced behavioral and estrous abnormalities

Finally, we tested whether sustained activation of adBNSTPENK neurons could rescue the behavioral and estrous-cycle abnormalities induced by chronic stress. To address this, Cre-dependent hM3Dq was infused into the adBNST of female Penk-Cre mice, which were subjected to the 14-day CRS paradigm while receiving CNO through drinking water during the stress period (Fig. 6A and B). Consistent with the CRS phenotype described above, stressed control mice displayed reduced exploration of anxiogenic zones in both the OFT and EPM. Chronic activation of adBNSTPENK neurons increased center exploration in the OFT and open-arm exploration in the EPM under stressed conditions (Fig. 6C–H), suggesting partial behavioral improvement. More pronounced effects were observed at the level of estrous cyclicity. Chronic activation restored a more balanced distribution of estrous stages, reducing prolonged phase occupancy and improving the regularity of stage transitions (Fig. 6I and J). Time-course analyses indicated a re-establishment of cyclical progression, resembling patterns observed in unstressed controls. Quantitative metrics further supported these observations. Activation of adBNSTPENK neurons increased the number of complete cycles, while reduced aberrant transitions (Fig. 6K–O). Discussion

In this study, we identify adBNSTPENK neurons as a stress-responsive population that regulates anxiety-like behaviors across sexes and contributes to stress-related disruption of female estrous-cycle organization in females. ARS activated adBNSTPENK neurons, of which the acute inhibition promoted anxiety-like behaviors in female mice, with a similar effect observed in males. Conversely, activation of adBNSTPENK neurons attenuated ARS-induced anxiety-like behaviors, indicating that stress-evoked recruitment of this population may function as an adaptive anxiety-buffering response rather than a purely stress-driving signal. Extending this acute framework, CRS produced robust anxiety-like behaviors and disrupted estrous cyclicity, consistent with clinical and preclinical evidence that stress can perturb both affective state and endocrine function (Allsworth et al., 2007; McCosh et al., 2019; Yamamoto et al., 2009). Chronic activation of adBNSTPENK neurons mitigated CRS-induced behavioral avoidance partially restored cytology-based cycle organization. These findings support a model in which adBNSTPENK neurons participate in stress-related affective regulation across sexes, while also engaging female-specific estrous cycle organization. Thus, these data support a model in which adBNSTPENK neurons form part of a limbic interface that constrains stress-induced anxiety and supports female reproductive rhythmicity, while leaving sex-by-treatment interactions, downstream circuits, and endocrine mediators to be resolved by future pathway-specific and endocrine studies.

A central finding of this study is that restraint stress recruits a genetically defined subset within the adBNST. The BNST is a core component of the extended amygdala and has been implicated in sustained anxiety states and stress processing (Fox and Shackman, 2019; Lebow and Chen, 2016; Walker and Davis, 2008). Previous studies have shown that BNST activity modulates anxiety-like behaviors through diverse projection-defined circuits (Kim et al., 2013; Namburi et al., 2015; Tye, 2018; Xiao et al., 2021). Moreover, BNST neuronal populations are highly heterogeneous, with distinct cell types contributing to either anxiogenic or anxiolytic outputs depending on their molecular identity and connectivity (Gungor and Paré, 2016; Ortiz-Juza et al., 2021). Functional studies have demonstrated that BNST activity regulates LH pulsatility and mediates stress-induced suppression of reproductive function (Li et al., 2011; Yamada et al., 2006). Our results support this integrative role by showing that restraint stress recruits a specific subset of adBNST neurons and that manipulating this genetically defined population can influence anxiety-like behaviors and cytology-based estrous-cycle organization. However, the limited overlap between ARS-induced cFos expression and adBNSTPENK neurons indicates that additional molecularly distinct BNST populations, including corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH)-, protein kinase C delta (PKCδ)-related neurons, may also participate in parallel stress-related behavioral and physiological outputs (Gungor and Paré, 2016; Wang et al., 2020; Xiao et al., 2021; Williford et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024).

Earlier optogenetic studies demonstrated that broad manipulation of dorsal BNST or oval BNST (ovBNST) promotes anxiety-like behavioral and physiological responses, whereas recruitment of BLA inputs to the surrounding adBNST and distinct adBNST efferent projections exerts anxiolytic effects (Kim et al., 2013). Thus, an anxiolytic effect of adBNSTPENK activation is not necessarily incompatible with prior work but suggests that the behavioral output of this region depends on which neuronal ensemble and projection are engaged. This interpretation is further supported by cell-type-specific studies showing that CRH- and PKCδ-expressing neurons in the adBNST have distinct anatomical distributions, input-output architectures, and behavioral effects, with CRH-neuron activation promoting anxiety-like behaviors and PKCδ-neuron activation producing context-dependent effects (Wang et al., 2020; Williford et al., 2023). Likewise, ovBNSTCRH neurons exhibit stress- and sex-dependent hyperexcitation, and manipulating their stress-evoked activity pattern can bidirectionally alter susceptibility to anxiety-like avoidance (Zhang et al., 2024). These findings emphasize that adBNST should not be treated as a functionally uniform structure. In this context, the stress-recruited adBNSTPENK population identified here may represent an anxiety-buffering component embedded within a broader stress-responsive adBNST network that also contains anxiogenic CRH-, PKCδ-, and other molecularly defined neurons, which are largely segregated from adBNSTPENK neurons (sFig. 1). Future studies combining multiplex molecular mapping with projection-specific optogenetic manipulation will be required to define how PENK neurons interact with other subpopulations and subregions to regulate anxiety-like behaviors and cytology-based estrous-cycle organization.

The present study demonstrates that acute inhibition of adBNSTPENK neurons produced a robust anxiogenic phenotype in both male and female mice, suggesting that their activity is necessary for maintaining a low anxiety level. Interestingly, acute activation of adBNSTPENK neurons did not reduce anxiety-like behaviors under baseline conditions. This asymmetry suggests that the circuit may operate near a functional floor during unstressed states, or that compensatory network activity may constrain the detection of additional anxiolytic effects. Similar nonlinear effects have been observed in other limbic circuits, where the impact of manipulation depends on baseline network state and synaptic context (Heitz and Schall, 2012). Under ARS, activation of the targeted population attenuated stress-induced behavioral avoidance, supporting a state-dependent anxiety-buffering role in females. Such context-dependent roles have been increasingly recognized in valence-processing circuits, where the same neuronal population can contribute to different behavioral outcomes depending on environmental and internal states (Namburi et al., 2015; Tye, 2018). As male and female ARS activation experiments were performed in separate cohorts, these data support a shared direction of effect but do not constitute a formal sex-by-treatment interaction analysis. Although we used small-volume viral injection, histological verification, and off-target sample exclusion, chemogenetic manipulation cannot fully match the spatial and temporal precision of projection-specific optogenetic approaches. Future studies combining intersectional targeting with pathway-specific manipulation will be required to define downstream outputs that mediate distinct stress-buffering effects.

CRS shifted cytology-based estrous-cycle organization toward fewer complete cycles, more aberrant transitions, and increased diestrus occupancy. These changes are compatible with stress-related disruption of HPA-HPG axis interactions, but the present study did not directly measure corticosterone, gonadotropins, gonadal steroids, or gonadotropin-releasing hormone pulse dynamics. The ability of chronic adBNSTPENK neurons activation to improve estrous cycle organization therefore demonstrates circuit-level modulation of a reproductive-cycle readout rather than mechanistic proof of HPG-axis normalization. This interpretation is consistent with prior evidence that enkephalin and opioid signaling can modulate stress-related affective states and reproductive neuroendocrine function. Enkephalin is widely distributed neuropeptides that modulate pain, reward, and emotional states (Belluzzi and Stein, 1977; Henry et al., 2017). Importantly, opioid signaling has been shown to influence stress resilience and adaptive responses to aversive stimuli (Henry et al., 2017). In addition to their role in affective regulation, opioid receptors modulate GnRH secretion and influence reproductive cyclicity (Barkan et al., 1985; Böttcher et al., 2017). The κ-opioid receptor signaling within hypothalamic circuits can suppress GnRH pulse generation and contribute to stress-induced reproductive inhibition (Grachev et al., 2012; Weems et al., 2016). Our findings are compatible with the broader literature linking opioid signaling to stress adaptation and reproductive neuroendocrine regulation. Activation of adBNSTPENK neurons improved estrous cycle parameters under chronic stress conditions, indicating an association with improved cytology-based estrous-cycle organization under chronic stress. Yet, whether adBNSTPENK neurons influence these endpoints through direct hypothalamic projections, indirect extended-amygdala pathways, or downstream kisspeptin, GnRH, or corticotropin-releasing hormone-related mechanisms remains to be tested.

Sex differences are a defining feature of stress-related disorders, female vulnerability to stress-related disorders is influenced by gonadal steroid state, cycle phase, and stress history (Altemus et al., 2014; Bale and Epperson, 2015). The present findings are relevant to this issue because they show that a defined BNST cell population can affect both anxiety-like behaviors and estrous rhythmicity. Hormonal fluctuations across the estrous or menstrual cycle can modulate neural excitability, stress responsivity, and behavioral output (Handa and Weiser, 2014; Knoedler et al., 2022). Recent studies have shown that estrous cycle stage can dynamically alter circuit function and behavior, including stress responses and social interactions (Shanley et al., 2023). The ability of adBNSTPENK activation to restore estrous cyclicity suggests that targeting specific limbic circuits may have broader implications for treating comorbid affective and endocrine disorders in women. However, the translational relevance of these findings remains to be established. Although rodent estrous cycles cannot be directly equated with human menstrual cycles, the present data highlight the value of studying affective and reproductive-cycle outcomes within the same experimental framework.

In summary, our study identified adBNSTPENK neurons as a restraint-responsive BNST subset that buffers anxiety-like behavioral output. Their inhibition promotes anxiety-like behaviors, whereas activation attenuates anxiety-like behaviors under acute and chronic stress conditions. In female mice, sustained activation of adBNSTPENK neurons improved cytology-based indices of cyclicity during CRS. Our findings highlight the BNST as a stress-responsive anxiety-buffering population, with associated effects on female estrous-cycle organization under chronic stress, which may offer new insight into stress-related affective-neuroendocrine comorbidity.
