Adverse Events Associated with Incretin-Based Therapies: A Narrative Review on Mechanisms, Clinical Management, and Risk Mitigation
Authors: Mansour Tobaiqy, Sulafa Tarek Alqutub
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIncretin-based therapies, including glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs), are established therapies for obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus, providing sustained weight reduction, improved glycaemic control, cardiovascular and renal benefits. Despite these established benefits, safety concerns remain an important clinical issue because adverse events may affect treatment persistence, patient acceptability, and clinical decision-making. This review evaluates a number of safety profiles of GLP-1RAs using clinical, mechanistic, and pharmacovigilance evidence, with attention to both common adverse events and emerging safety signals.MethodsA narrative review was conducted to evaluate the safety profile of GLP-1RAs in obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus. PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and Web of Science were searched for studies published between January 2005 and April 2026. Evidence from randomised controlled trials, observational studies, meta-analyses, pharmacovigilance investigations, and regulatory safety reports was synthesised qualitatively.ResultsFindings from 60 studies and safety reports were included. Common adverse events were predominantly gastrointestinal, particularly nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, and constipation, and represented the most consistently reported adverse effects and the principal cause of treatment discontinuation. Uncommon safety concerns included renal adverse events, which were reported infrequently and often occurred in clinical contexts involving gastrointestinal intolerance, volume depletion, or other predisposing factors. Gallbladder and biliary complications were also infrequent and appeared to be influenced by rapid weight loss and baseline patient risk factors. Acute pancreatitis remained rare in clinical trials, although severe cases have been reported in postmarketing settings. Human evidence did not demonstrate an increased risk of thyroid malignancy. Emerging safety signals, including early worsening of diabetic retinopathy, muscle mass reduction, psychiatric symptoms, and alopecia, were identified mainly through observational and pharmacovigilance evidence.ConclusionGLP-1RA adverse events predominantly reflect predictable pharmacological and physiological effects rather than off-target toxicity. Safety profiles appear agent-specific, dose-dependent, and influenced by underlying mechanisms and patient characteristics. Risk-stratified prescribing should therefore align agent selection, dose escalation, and monitoring intensity with patient-specific vulnerabilities, particularly gastrointestinal tolerability, risk of renal dehydration, gallbladder history, retinopathy risk during rapid glycaemic improvement, and frailty or sarcopenia risk. Continued long-term surveillance and further evidence generation are needed to better define rare, delayed, and emerging safety signals and to optimise safe use across diverse clinical populations.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) often occurs alongside obesity, and together they affect more than one billion people worldwide.1,2 Together, they are major contributors to cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, visual impairment, reduced physical function, and poorer health-related quality of life.1,2 Their incidence and prevalence continue to rise worldwide, with an uneven distribution of impact in low- and middle-income regions and socioeconomically disadvantaged populations.1–3 Type 2 diabetes mellitus constitutes the majority of diabetes cases globally and is closely associated with elevated body mass index and excess adiposity.1,2 The burden is greatest in the Middle East and North Africa and parts of Oceania, with further growth projected by 2050, emphasising the role of obesity management in diabetes control.3–5

A pooled analysis of 1,108 population-based studies shows a considerable global burden of untreated diabetes, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.6 In Saudi Arabia, genetic factors have been linked to increased susceptibility to obesity,7 while ongoing changes in lifestyle and diet continue to challenge the efficacious management of obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus.8

Since their first approval in 2005, glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) have become a key therapeutic class for obesity and type 2 diabetes, providing sustained weight reduction and improved glycaemic control.

GLP-1 receptor agonists are increasingly used to treat obesity and type 2 diabetes, with consistent benefits for weight reduction and glycaemic control.3–5 In obesity, GLP-1 receptor agonists promote weight reduction primarily through central and gastrointestinal mechanisms that reduce energy intake. Activation of GLP-1 receptors in appetite-regulating regions of the brain enhances satiety, reduces hunger, and may alter food reward and preference, while delayed gastric emptying contributes to earlier and more prolonged postprandial fullness. These effects collectively reduce caloric intake and support clinically meaningful weight loss, although the relative contribution of gastric emptying may vary by agent, dose, and duration of treatment.4,9

In type 2 diabetes mellitus, GLP-1 receptor agonists improve glycaemic control by enhancing glucose-dependent insulin secretion and suppressing inappropriate glucagon secretion, thereby reducing both fasting and postprandial hyperglycaemia with a relatively low intrinsic risk of hypoglycaemia when not combined with insulin or sulfonylureas. They also slow gastric emptying, which attenuates postprandial glucose changes, particularly with shorter-acting agents, and may indirectly improve insulin sensitivity through weight reduction. Beyond glucose lowering, some GLP-1 receptor agonists have demonstrated cardiovascular and renal benefits, supporting their broader role in cardiometabolic risk reduction.10,11

Evidence from cardiovascular outcome trials indicates reductions in major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE)12 and favourable renal outcomes in high-risk populations.13 Nevertheless, persistence with therapy in routine clinical practice remains suboptimal.14 Treatment discontinuation reflects a combination of factors, including gastrointestinal tolerability issues and other safety concerns,15 financial cost,16 restricted access,17 unmet expectations regarding weight loss, weight regain after cessation,18 and particularly high early discontinuation rates among individuals treated for obesity without diabetes.17–20 The therapeutic use of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) has expanded, with multiple agents approved or in late-stage development for obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM).21–23 Available therapies include short-acting formulations such as twice-daily exenatide, as well as longer-acting agents such as liraglutide, semaglutide, and dulaglutide.21–23 Current therapeutic agents include short-acting formulations, such as twice-daily exenatide, and longer-acting agents, including liraglutide, semaglutide, and dulaglutide. More recent agents, such as tirzepatide, exert dual agonism at the GLP-1 and glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) receptors. In parallel, orally administered non-peptide GLP-1 receptor agonists, including orforglipron and danuglipron, are undergoing clinical evaluation.21,23

Careful evaluation of the pharmacological and pathophysiological basis of adverse events associated with GLP-1 receptor agonists is important for guiding prescribing, supporting patient understanding, and managing risk. At a population level, poor tolerability may affect adherence to and continuation of treatment, thereby reducing real-world effectiveness and contributing to inequalities in access to cardiometabolic therapies.14,19–21,23,24 There is also increasing evidence that these agents may act on inflammatory pathways linked to insulin resistance, suggesting a broader immunometabolic role with relevance to their overall clinical and safety profile.25

Although several reviews have described the efficacy and general tolerability of GLP-1 receptor agonists, many focus on individual adverse events, specific agents, or single evidence streams. The distinct contribution of the present review is to integrate clinical trial findings, real-world observational evidence, pharmacovigilance signals, and mechanistic data to provide a comparative, clinically oriented interpretation of the safety of GLP-1 receptor agonists. In doing so, this review distinguishes predictable, dose-related pharmacological effects from rarer or less certain safety signals; considers how risks vary according to agent, dose, indication, and patient vulnerability; and translates this evidence into practical strategies for risk stratification, counselling, monitoring, and safe prescribing.

This review aims to synthesise and compare the safety profiles of incretin-based therapies, including GLP-1 receptor agonists for Obesity and Type 2 Diabetes, by integrating clinical and mechanistic evidence and to identify evidence-based strategies for risk mitigation and safe clinical use.

Methods

Study Design

This narrative review evaluated the safety profile of GLP-1RAs used as therapeutics for type 2 diabetes mellitus and obesity. Evidence on adverse events, dose-related effects, mechanisms of toxicity, and clinical safety outcomes was examined using data from clinical trials, observational studies, pharmacovigilance analyses, and regulatory reports.

Although PRISMA reporting was not applied, the review followed a structured process for identifying and appraising the literature to support methodological consistency and minimise bias.

Literature Search Strategy

A structured search of PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and Web of Science was conducted on 18 April 2026 for studies published between January 2005 and April 2026. Search terms incorporated both indexed subject headings and free-text terminology relevant to GLP-1RAs, individual agents, adverse events, tolerability, and major safety outcomes.

The search was designed to identify clinically relevant and representative evidence that addresses the objectives of this narrative review, rather than to capture all potentially eligible publications.

Targeted searches were also undertaken for gastrointestinal complications, pancreatitis, gallbladder disease, acute kidney injury, thyroid neoplasms, psychiatric events, and diabetic retinopathy. Additional evidence was identified through manual screening of reference lists, major clinical trials, systematic reviews, and safety communications issued by the US Food and Drug Administration, European Medicines Agency, and Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency.

Eligibility Criteria

Studies were eligible if they reported clinically relevant safety outcomes associated with GLP-1RA use in adults with obesity or type 2 diabetes mellitus. Included evidence comprised randomised controlled trials, outcome trials, observational studies, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, pharmacovigilance investigations, and regulatory safety reports.

Studies were selected based on their scientific relevance, methodological quality, clinical applicability, and contribution to understanding the mechanisms, frequency, or management of adverse events.

Studies limited to paediatric populations, non-English publications, preclinical investigations without clinical relevance, or reports lacking meaningful safety data were excluded.

Quality Assessment

The included studies were qualitatively evaluated by two authors for methodological quality, clinical relevance, and study design. Greater weight was given to higher-level evidence, while pharmacovigilance and mechanistic studies were used primarily to contextualise emerging safety signals.

Evidence Appraisal and Synthesis

Evidence was synthesised descriptively by integrating findings across study designs, with conclusions based on the consistency, scientific rigour, study relevance, and strength of the available evidence. Where findings differed between studies, greater weight was given to higher-level evidence and consistency across multiple independent sources. The synthesis focused on adverse-event patterns, mechanistic plausibility, and clinically relevant risk-mitigation strategies.

Results

Given the methodological heterogeneity across studies, findings from 60 articles and safety reports were qualitatively synthesised rather than formally quantitatively pooled. Gastrointestinal adverse events were the most extensively investigated safety domain and were consistently evaluated across clinical trials, mechanistic studies, and meta-analyses.18,19,21,26–31 Renal and hepatobiliary outcomes were supported by evidence from outcome trials, observational analyses, pooled RCT data, and regulatory reports.5,12,13,29,32–42 Pancreatic and thyroid safety findings were derived from clinical trials, real-world cohort studies, pharmacovigilance investigations, and multinational observational analyses.5,15,22,27,43–53 Psychiatric, musculoskeletal, ocular, and dermatologic adverse events were reported primarily in observational, mechanistic, and postmarketing studies.27,53–71

Glucagon-Like Peptide-1 Receptor Agonist–Associated Adverse Events

Gastrointestinal Adverse Events

Gastrointestinal adverse effects, most notably nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, and constipation, are the most frequently reported safety events associated with GLP-1 receptor agonists and represent a leading cause of treatment discontinuation in clinical trials of obesity and type 2 diabetes.23

Differences in gastrointestinal tolerability should be interpreted in relation to the therapeutic indication, since the higher doses used for obesity are associated with a greater burden of gastrointestinal adverse events than those typically prescribed for type 2 diabetes, especially during dose escalation.28,29,72

Although incidence varies by agent and study population, nausea is consistently the predominant symptom, while other gastrointestinal events occur at lower but clinically meaningful rates than placebo.28,29,71 Similar gastrointestinal tolerability profiles have been reported in investigational studies of next-generation GLP-1 receptor agonists, including orforglipron and danuglipron, indicating that gastrointestinal effects remain a class-related limitation despite advances in drug design.23,73

A recent systematic review and dose-response network meta-analysis of 39 studies included 33,354 non-diabetic individuals with overweight or obesity. It demonstrated that GLP-1 receptor agonists significantly increase the risk of gastrointestinal adverse events, with nausea being the most frequently reported outcome, and substantial heterogeneity across agents, particularly for orforglipron, exenatide, tirzepatide, semaglutide, and liraglutide.27 Overall, available evidence supports a clear association between GLP-1 receptor agonists and common gastrointestinal adverse events, while data on rarer gastrointestinal complications remain limited and inconclusive.3,29–31

Within this context, bowel obstruction is uncommon but mechanistically plausible. It appears to arise predominantly from GLP-1-mediated suppression of gastrointestinal motility rather than direct intestinal toxicity. Activation of the GLP-1 receptor delays gastric emptying and reduces small-bowel and colonic transit via vagal and enteric neural pathways, which may precipitate ileus-like states or unmask subclinical obstruction.28,29,72 This risk is most relevant during early treatment or rapid dose escalation, and in susceptible individuals, including those with prior abdominal surgery or severe constipation.18,19,72 The peri-anaesthesia concern accelerated after 2023 case reports and endoscopy/anesthesia observations of retained gastric contents in fasted patients; the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA’s) June 2023 consensus advice initially recommended holding daily GLP-1 drugs on the day of surgery and weekly preparations for one week before elective procedures.74 This position has since shifted toward individualised assessment: the October 2024 multi-society guidance states that most low-risk patients undergoing elective surgery can continue GLP-1 therapy, while aspiration-risk mitigation should be targeted to those at higher risk.75 In parallel, FDA labelling for GLP-1 receptor agonists was updated between October and November 2024 to include aspiration during general anaesthesia or deep sedation, and the FDA required a postmarketing trial to study temporary withholding, fasting duration, retained gastric contents and mitigation of pulmonary aspiration risk.76

Mechanistic Basis of Gastrointestinal Adverse Events

Gastrointestinal adverse effects associated with GLP-1 receptor agonists are mediated by both peripheral physiological actions and central neuroendocrine mechanisms, primarily delayed gastrointestinal motility, and activation of central satiety and aversive signalling pathways.18,19 Delayed gastric emptying and prolonged gastric retention under GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy enhance satiety but may also precipitate postprandial fullness, dyspeptic symptoms, gastrooesophageal reflux, and abdominal discomfort. These effects are most evident during treatment initiation and dose escalation, when nausea and vomiting tend to be more pronounced.18,19,21,31,77 In parallel, central brainstem and hypothalamic pathways mediate appetite suppression and nausea, while alterations in intestinal transit and secretion contribute to diarrhoea, bloating, and constipation, with substantial inter-individual variability observed across agents.18,19,21,31,77 The mechanistic pathways are shown in Figure 1. Clinical Management and Risk Mitigation

Advisory statements from the American Diabetes Association (ADA) and the European Association (EASD) indicate that gastrointestinal symptoms associated with GLP-1 receptor agonists represent anticipated, dose-dependent pharmacodynamic effects, largely mediated by delayed gastric emptying, modulation of gastrointestinal motility, and enhanced central satiety signalling.4,43 These events are most commonly observed during treatment initiation and dose escalation and are generally transient and mild to moderate in intensity,30 consistent with findings from major cardiovascular outcome trials and the Semaglutide Treatment Effect in People with Obesity programme.21,31 Current recommendations therefore support gradual, individualised titration, proactive patient counselling, dietary modification, and adequate hydration to optimise tolerability and maintain treatment persistence.31 Most patients undergoing elective procedures can continue therapy, whereas those at higher risk may require additional precautions, such as a liquid diet before surgery, gastric ultrasound when available, or aspiration-risk anaesthetic strategies. Unnecessary discontinuation should be avoided, as it may adversely affect glycaemic control and weight management.76

Renal Adverse Events

Patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus carry a markedly elevated risk of acute kidney injury, reflecting the cumulative effects of diabetic kidney disease, systemic vascular pathology, and associated comorbid conditions. Excess adiposity may further compound renal susceptibility and haemodynamic stress.33,34 Because acute kidney injury is associated with accelerated progression to chronic kidney disease and increased cardiovascular morbidity, careful evaluation of the renal safety of contemporary glucose-lowering and weight management therapies is essential.33,34

Renal adverse events associated with GLP-1 receptor agonists are uncommon but clinically relevant, particularly in older adults, individuals with type 2 diabetes, and patients with pre-existing chronic kidney disease.13,22,33 Spontaneous adverse event reports have described associations between GLP-1 receptor agonist use namely “Exenatide” and acute kidney injury,22,23,71 including rare cases of semaglutide-associated biopsy-proven acute interstitial nephritis.22,34 However, causality has not been established, and these observations are subject to confounding and reporting bias.32

Mechanistic Basis of Renal Adverse Events

Multiple confounding factors contribute to renal disease risk in patients with diabetes and obesity, including hypertension, cardiovascular disease, pre-existing chronic kidney disease, advanced age, and longer duration of diabetes, all of which independently increase renal vulnerability.32,35 Simultaneous exposure to diuretics, renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system inhibitors during hypovolaemia, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory medicines, contrast media, and other nephrotoxic agents may potentiate the risk of renal adverse events.32,35 Additionally, dehydration resulting from acute illness or gastrointestinal adverse effects of other antidiabetic and anti-obesity medications may further complicate the interpretation of causality in observational and pharmacovigilance studies.32,35

In contrast, evidence from randomised controlled trials indicates a protective effect on the kidneys of GLP-1 receptor agonists.33,34 In a meta-analysis of 11 trials, including 85,373 patients with T2DM, GLP-1 receptor agonists significantly reduced clinically important renal outcomes.35 Compared with placebo, treatment was associated with an 18% reduction in the composite kidney outcome, a 16% reduction in kidney failure, and a 13% reduction in major adverse cardiovascular events, with consistent findings following inclusion of the SELECT trial.12,37 Similar renal benefits have been re-ported in another meta-analysis, reinforcing the robustness of these observations.34

Clinical Management and Risk Mitigation

Prevention strategies include early recognition of persistent gastrointestinal symptoms, counselling on adequate fluid intake and oral rehydration, and temporary dose stabilisation or treatment interruption during episodes of acute illness.13,22,35 Failure to recognise dehydration related kidney injury may lead to unnecessary treatment discontinuation, inappropriate specialist referral, increased healthcare utilisation, and interruption of therapies that otherwise confer glycaemic and renal protection.13,22,35

According to the American Diabetes Association and Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes consensus statements; for patients with type 2 diabetes and chronic kidney disease who do not achieve individualized glycaemic targets despite treatment with metformin and/or a sodium–glucose co-transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitor, or in whom these agents are contraindicated or not tolerated, initiation of a GLP-1 receptor agonist with established cardiovascular benefit is recommended as part of comprehensive cardiometabolic risk reduction.33

Hepatobiliary Adverse Events

Gallbladder and biliary complications have been documented in clinical trials evaluating GLP-1 receptor agonists for obesity and type 2 diabetes.18,28,32,45 In weight management programmes, these events have been reported in approximately 1 to 3% of treated participants and occur more frequently than in placebo groups.15,30,78 In the SCALE Obesity and Prediabetes programme, gallbladder-related events were observed in 2.5% of participants receiving liraglutide 3.0 mg com-pared with 1.0% in the placebo group.5

A large meta-analysis of 76 randomised controlled trials reported that GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy was associated with an increased incidence of gallbladder or biliary disease, with higher risk at higher doses, longer treatment duration, and when prescribed for weight reduction.37 Interpretation of these findings is constrained by potential underreporting of biliary outcomes and reliance on aggregate trial-level data rather than individual patient data. In contrast, another systematic review and meta-analysis did not demonstrate a statistically significant association between GLP-1 receptor agonist use and specific biliary outcomes, including cholelithiasis, gallstones, cholecystitis, or acute cholecystitis.27 In that analysis, effect estimates were imprecise, likely reflecting low event rates and limited statistical power.

Mechanistic Basis of Hepatobiliary Adverse Events

The association between GLP-1 receptor agonists and gallbladder disease appears to be largely indirect and mediated by weight loss.38 Individuals receiving GLP-1 receptor agonists frequently have baseline risk factors for gallstone disease, including obesity, T2DM, dyslipidaemia, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, female sex, and older age, which may confound observed associations.40,41 Of note, Accelerated weight loss promotes cholesterol supersaturation of bile and impairs gallbladder contractility, facilitating gallstone formation, a pathophysiological process well recognised following bariatric surgery and during very low-calorie dietary interventions.40,41 A summary of the mechanistic basis of hepatobiliary adverse events is shown in Figure 2. Clinical Management and Risk Mitigation

Risk mitigation for gallbladder and biliary events during GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy should focus on modifiable determinants of rapid weight loss rather than treatment avoidance. Evidence from randomised trials and meta-analysis indicates that gradual weight reduction and avoidance of very low-calorie diets reduce biliary cholesterol supersaturation and gallbladder hypomotility, thereby lowering gallstone risk.3,39,41 Regulatory data and cardiovascular outcome trials further support early assessment of biliary symptoms to prevent progression to clinically significant complications.5,39–41

Pancreatic Adverse Events

Acute pancreatitis is a rare but clinically important adverse event reported with GLP-1 receptor agonists.5,22,71,80 Across obesity and cardiometabolic outcome trials, confirmed pancreatitis generally occurs in approximately 0.1–0.3% of treated participants, with similar or slightly lower rates observed in placebo groups.16,22,28,30

In a large United States cohort of comorbidity-free patients with type 2 diabetes, Exposure to GLP-1 receptor agonists was not linked to a higher incidence of pancreatitis. During short- or long-term follow-up and was associated with a lower lifetime incidence compared with patients not receiving GLP-1 receptor agonists.41 However, the generalisability of these findings is constrained by their derivation from a predominantly low-risk study population.

By contrast, another large United States retrospective propensity score–matched cohort study comparing 137,918 GLP-1 receptor agonist users with 241,561 dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitor (DPP-4) users reported a small but statistically significant increase in acute pancreatitis (OR 1.28; 95% CI, 1.07–1.53) and pancreatic cancer following GLP-1 receptor agonist initiation.44 Absolute risk differences were small: pancreatitis occurred in 0.31% versus 0.24%, and pancreatic cancer in 0.24% versus 0.17%, respectively.45 However, as a retrospective observational study, residual and unmeasured confounding remain inherent limitations of these findings. Furthermore, the observational design precludes causal inference, and reverse causality cannot be ruled out.

Nevertheless, these findings support continued clinical vigilance, particularly in off-label use, without outweighing the established therapeutic benefits of GLP-1 receptor agonists.15,22

Regulatory assessments align with a precautionary interpretation of the current evidence base. The Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency has revised safety communications for GLP-1 receptor agonists and dual GLP-1 and glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide agonists to emphasise the potential risk of rare, severe, necrotising, and fatal pancreatitis.45,46 Cumulative pharmacovigilance data from the Yellow Card Scheme indicate that between 2007 and October 2025, 1,296 suspected cases of pancreatitis were reported, including 19 fatal and 24 necrotising events, in the context of an estimated 25.4 million packs dispensed over the preceding five years.45 These spontaneous reports do not establish causality but support continued clinical vigilance.

Mechanistic Basis of Pancreatic Adverse Events

Proposed mechanisms for pancreatitis during GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy include increased pancreatic enzyme activity, ductal epithelial changes, and local inflammatory processes.22,71 Interpretation is challenging, however, because many affected patients have established risk factors such as gallstones, alcohol use, hypertriglyceridaemia, hypercalcaemia, or exposure to other pancreatotoxic drugs, each of which independently elevates pancreatitis risk and may confound causal assessment.46,47

Pooled analyses suggest that the overall absolute risk of pancreatitis is low. Nonetheless, heterogeneity in case definitions, event reporting, and baseline risk characteristics across studies limits the accuracy of risk estimates and prevents firm conclusions regarding causality.46,47

Clinical Management and Risk Mitigation

Risk mitigation for pancreatitis during treatment with GLP-1 receptor agonists should focus on baseline evaluation of established risk factors and rapid assessment of persistent or severe abdominal pain. While rare cases of severe, necrotising, and fatal pancreatitis have been reported, disproportionate risk perception may result in inappropriate treatment discontinuation and loss of therapies with proven cardiovascular and renal benefit.30,31,46,47,72 Clinicians should maintain vigilance, actively enquire about all prescribed and privately obtained incretin-based therapies, and promptly discontinue treatment if pancreatitis is suspected. Rechallenge is contraindicated following confirmed pancreatitis, and heightened caution is warranted in patients with a previous history of pancreatitis.46,47 Suspected cases should be reported through established pharmacovigilance systems. Patients should be clearly counselled to seek urgent medical attention for severe or persistent abdominal pain and to avoid re-initiation of therapy if pancreatitis is confirmed.30,31,46,47,72

Thyroid Adverse Events

Thyroid adverse effects associated with GLP-1 receptor agonists are rare in humans but clinically relevant because of a class-wide boxed warning for medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC). This warning is based on rodent studies demonstrating dose-dependent thyroid C-cell hyperplasia and MTC following chronic GLP-1 receptor activation, a phenomenon considered species-specific.15,22,47,48 In contrast, randomised controlled trials, cardiovascular and obesity outcome studies, and real-world observational analyses have not demonstrated an increased incidence of medullary, papillary, or follicular thyroid carcinoma in humans, with reported rates comparable to those in background populations.15,22,48,49 Consequently, GLP-1 receptor agonists remain contraindicated in individuals with MTC or multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2 (MEN2), while the absolute risk of thyroid cancer in the general population appears low.15,22,47–51

In a large multinational cohort study with meta-analysis across six population-based databases from 2007 to 2023, use of GLP-1 receptor agonists was not associated with a higher incidence of thyroid cancer compared with DPP-4 inhibitors among patients with type 2 diabetes (pooled hazard ratio 0.81, 95% confidence interval 0.59–1.12) with no evidence of a dose–response relationship.49 Results were consistent across multiple sensitivity analyses, supporting short-term thyroid safety, although longer follow-up is required to fully characterise long-latency risk.49

Mechanistic Basis of Thyroid Adverse Events

The biological rationale for thyroid risk stems primarily from rodent-specific findings showing that sustained GLP-1 receptor stimulation induces C-cell hyperplasia and MTC. This mechanism appears species-dependent, as human thyroid C-cells express markedly lower GLP-1 receptor density and show minimal functional responsiveness to GLP-1 signalling.47,49 Although GLP-1 receptor expression has been identified in papillary thyroid carcinoma tissue, there is no convincing evidence that receptor activation promotes oncogenic transformation or tumour progression in humans.48,49 Alternative explanations for observed epidemiologic signals include confounding by obesity, an established thyroid cancer risk factor, and detection bias related to increased thyroid imaging prompted by safety warnings rather than a causal drug effect.51,52

Clinical Management and Risk Mitigation

Given the well-established cardiometabolic benefits of GLP-1 receptor agonists, their use should not be restricted on the basis of theoretical or limited concerns regarding thyroid cancer risk.51,79 However, GLP-1 receptor agonists should remain contraindicated in individuals with either a documented history or a hereditary predisposition to medullary thyroid carcinoma. Ongoing pharmacovigilance and long-term surveillance remain appropriate to further characterise rare or delayed thyroid outcomes during prolonged therapy.15,51

Musculoskeletal Adverse Effects

Loss of skeletal muscle contributes measurably to overall weight reduction observed with GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, with lean mass typically comprising about 20 to 30% of total weight loss.54,55 This estimation is similar to that reported with calorie restriction and bariatric surgery and aligns with expected physiological adaptation to sustained negative energy balance, rather than indicating a direct myotoxic effect of the drug.54,55

Rapid and substantial weight loss during incretin-based therapy has also raised interest in potential effects on bone health. Although current evidence does not indicate a consistent increase in fracture risk, modest reductions in bone mineral density and changes in bone turnover have been reported, particularly with greater weight loss, supporting continued evaluation of skeletal outcomes with newer high-potency and emerging multi-agonist therapies.81,82

Mechanistic Basis of Muscle Mass Reduction

The extent of skeletal muscle reduction is influenced by factors such as age, baseline cardiometabolic disease, habitual physical activity, nutritional adequacy, and the velocity of weight loss.54,55 Magnetic resonance imaging–based assessments suggest that these alterations are predominantly adaptive, with declines in muscle volume corresponding to ageing, comorbidity burden, and the degree of weight reduction achieved. Of note, reductions in lean mass are often accompanied by enhanced insulin sensitivity and decreased intramuscular lipid content, indicating maintained or potentially improved muscle composition rather than overt functional compromise.54 However, advanced age and pre-frailty states may increase vulnerability to clinically meaningful sarcopenia, highlighting the need for individualised assessment and longitudinal monitoring.55–59

Furthermore, rapid weight loss reduces mechanical loading of the skeleton, increasing bone remodelling and potentially reducing bone mineral density, particularly at weight-bearing sites. Current evidence indicates that these skeletal changes are predominantly secondary to weight loss rather than to direct GLP-1 receptor agonist–mediated effects on bone.54,81

Clinical Management and Risk Mitigation

Muscle loss becomes clinically relevant primarily when accompanied by reduced muscle strength or physical performance, consistent with contemporary definitions of sarcopenia that emphasise functional impairment over absolute lean mass loss.54,81 Similarly, preservation of bone health is increasingly recognised as an important consideration during substantial weight loss, particularly in individuals at increased fracture risk. Accordingly, monitoring during GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy should include functional assessments (eg, handgrip strength, chair-stand performance, and gait speed), along with consideration of bone health in high-risk patients, particularly older adults, those with frailty, or those experiencing rapid weight loss.53–58,81,82 Resistance exercise, adequate protein intake, and gradual dose escalation remain key strategies for preserving musculoskeletal health while maintaining metabolic benefits.53–58,81,82 Standardised assessment of muscle function and body composition, alongside appropriate evaluation of skeletal health where clinically indicated, will be important to optimise the long-term, patient-centered use of incretin-based therapies.

Psychiatric Adverse Events

The first pharmacovigilance signal suggesting a potential association between GLP-1 receptor agonists and psychiatric adverse events emerged from an EudraVigilance analysis initiated following the European Medicines Agency’s regulatory review, in response to reports of mood changes and suicidality.62 Among 31,444 adverse event reports for semaglutide, liraglutide, and tirzepatide, psychiatric events accounted for 1.18%, most commonly depression, anxiety, and suicidal ideation. Although infrequent, a small number of reports described severe outcomes, including fatal and life-threatening events, prompting regulatory attention and reinforcing the importance of continued pharmacovigilance.62

Subsequent evidence from observational studies and meta-analyses has produced heterogeneous findings regarding the association and causality.63–66 Overall, the available literature provides reassurance regarding neuropsychiatric safety, as meta-analyses of large randomised controlled trials and post-marketing surveillance have not demonstrated an increased incidence of psychiatric adverse events among users of GLP-1 receptor agonists.63–66 Nonetheless, under-reporting, publication bias, and limited psychiatric phenotyping in clinical trials may reduce sensitivity for detecting rare or subgroup-specific risks, underscoring the need for ongoing surveillance.65

Mechanistic Basis of Psychiatric Adverse Events

Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain the rare psychiatric signals reported in pharmacovigilance studies; however, these mechanisms remain speculative and have not been clinically validated.59,60

Potential psychiatric adverse events may reflect central nervous system effects of GLP-1 signalling, which modulates mood- and reward-related circuits through effects on mesolimbic dopamine pathways, serotonergic neurotransmission, and hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis regulation.66,67 Additional contributory factors may include rapid weight loss, gastrointestinal intolerance, sleep disturbance, neuroendocrine changes, and increased vulnerability among individuals with pre-existing psychiatric disorders, providing biological plausibility for the rare events reported in post-marketing settings.66,67

Clinical Management and Risk Mitigation

Effective risk mitigation requires assessment of baseline psychiatric history, proactive patient counselling, and ongoing clinical monitoring. Communication should remain balanced and evidence-based. Overstating potential risks can result in unnecessary treatment avoidance or early discontinuation, while structured follow-up and targeted monitoring enable most patients to continue therapy safely and effectively.62,66,67

Ocular and Other Adverse Events

Early worsening of diabetic retinopathy (DR) is a well-recognised, non–agent-specific phenomenon reported across type 1 and type 2 diabetes, pregnancy, bariatric surgery, and multiple glucose-lowering therapies, including insulin and GLP-1 receptor agonists.68 It is characterised by a transient progression of retinal lesions and is most strongly associated with rapid and substantial reductions in glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), typically around 2%, particularly in individuals with advanced baseline retinopathy; poorly controlled hypertension further increases risk.68,69 In the SUSTAIN-6 trial, a higher incidence of DR complications was observed primarily among participants with pre-existing retinopathy and rapid early glycaemic improvement. Post hoc analyses indicated that this excess risk was largely attributable to the magnitude and speed of HbA1c reduction rather than to a direct drug-specific retinal effect.52 Accordingly, reviews and clinical guidelines recommend baseline ophthalmic assessment and closer monitoring when initiating therapies expected to produce substantial early reductions in HbA1c in patients at high risk.69

Emerging pharmacovigilance data suggest a possible association between GLP-1 receptor agonists and alopecia.70 A recent disproportionality analysis using the United States Food and Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) identified increased reporting odds of hair loss with once-weekly semaglutide and tirzepatide, an effect not observed in pivotal clinical trials or with once-daily liraglutide.67 The underlying mechanism remains uncertain. However, rapid treatment-associated weight loss may precipitate telogen effluvium through metabolic or nutritional stress rather than direct follicular toxicity.53,71 Clinically, this signal supports patient counselling, nutritional assessment, and reassurance, as hair shedding is typically transient and reversible.71,80

Discussion

The findings of this review indicate that the safety profile of incretin-based therapies is largely attributable to predictable pharmacological and physiological effects rather than to unexpected organ toxicity. As the therapeutic class has evolved from first-generation GLP-1 receptor agonists to dual and emerging triple agonists, improvements in metabolic efficacy have been accompanied by greater attention to dose-related tolerability, individualised monitoring, and long-term safety surveillance. Future research should focus on clarifying uncommon adverse events, optimising patient stratification, and defining the long-term safety of next-generation multi-agonist therapies.18 Notwithstanding their established therapeutic benefits, rare and potentially serious adverse events necessitate rigorous assessment, ongoing clinical monitoring, and structured risk-mitigation strategies to ensure patient safety. Gastrointestinal intolerance, dehydration-related renal dysfunction, early worsening of diabetic retinopathy, and proportional reductions in lean mass are consistently reported across trials and real-world studies and are mechanistically linked to delayed gastric emptying, altered gastrointestinal motility, central satiety signalling, rapid metabolic improvement, and sustained negative energy balance.3,22,40,79,83

Although gastrointestinal adverse events are the most frequently reported adverse effects of incretin-based therapies, they are generally transient, dose-dependent, and manageable with gradual dose escalation and supportive care. Importantly, treatment discontinuation due to gastrointestinal intolerance has been reported in only 1.0–10.5% of participants receiving tirzepatide, indicating that most patients can successfully continue therapy despite early symptoms.29

Comparative evidence suggests that the safety profile of incretin-based therapies has evolved across generations of therapy. Earlier agents, such as exenatide, were associated mainly with gastrointestinal intolerance and injection-site reactions, whereas later agents demonstrated improved tolerability. High-potency therapies, particularly semaglutide and tirzepatide, achieve greater metabolic efficacy; however, the higher doses used for obesity are associated with more frequent dose-related gastrointestinal adverse events, particularly during dose escalation. Gallbladder events appear to be primarily weight-loss-related, whereas early worsening of retinopathy with semaglutide is likely associated with rapid reductions in HbA1c. Emerging signals, including alopecia and neuropsychiatric events, remain preliminary and largely pharmacovigilance-based. Overall, GLP-1RA safety appears agent-specific and dose-dependent.3,22,27,72,83,84

Although GLP-1 receptor agonists do not appear to increase the risk of acute kidney injury at the population level, caution and close monitoring remain appropriate for patients with advanced chronic kidney disease, particularly during treatment initiation, as gastrointestinal adverse events may compromise volume status.26,27,84

Less frequent but clinically relevant outcomes, including pancreatitis, gallbladder disease, acute kidney injury, psychiatric symptoms, and thyroid-related events, occur predominantly in populations with substantial baseline cardiometabolic risk, complicating causal attribution in observational analyses.21,28–31,38–41,44–47,60,85 While large cardiovascular and renal outcome trials and meta-analyses demonstrate reductions in major adverse cardiovascular events and clinically meaningful kidney outcomes with GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, these benefits do not exclude the possibility of rare or subgroup-specific adverse effects that may be under-represented in randomised trials.15,22 Available human data have not demonstrated an increased risk of thyroid malignancy, supporting the interpretation that rodent C-cell findings are species-specific. However, limited long-term follow-up justifies continued contraindication in medullary thyroid carcinoma and multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2, alongside ongoing surveillance.47–51 Emerging safety signals, including alopecia and rare neuropsychiatric events, are derived primarily from pharmacovigilance data and should be interpreted with caution, given reporting bias, confounding by rapid weight loss, and limited psychiatric characterisation.67,68 Nonetheless, under-reporting, publication bias, and limited psychiatric phenotyping in clinical trials may reduce sensitivity for detecting rare or subgroup-specific risks, underscoring the need for ongoing surveillance.62 Collectively, the evidence supports a mechanism-informed, risk-stratified clinical approach that prioritises gradual dose escalation, anticipatory counselling, functional monitoring, and targeted vigilance in susceptible subgroups, while recognising persistent evidence gaps and the need for long-term, high-quality, real-world safety data to characterise uncommon and delayed adverse outcomes associated with GLP-1 receptor agonists.15,22 In September 2025, the Saudi Food and Drug Administration (SFDA) issued a statement from direct healthcare providers on the safety of GLP-1 RAs and related therapies for use in T2DM and obesity management, with a focus on the risk of acute pancreatitis.85 Moreover, in 2024, SFDA provided a preliminary safety signal of depression associated with Semaglutide and Tirzepatide use.86 This indicates the evolving evidence from national pharmacovigilance. Although based on national pharmacovigilance data, these SFDA communications reinforce similar international regulatory signals and illustrate the importance of ongoing real-world safety surveillance.

Of note, emerging multi-agonists, including the investigational triple agonist retatrutide, appear to share the class-related predominance of dose-dependent gastrointestinal adverse events.86 A dose-related increase in heart rate has also been reported with retatrutide,85 whereas its long-term cardiovascular and overall safety profile remains under evaluation in ongoing Phase III studies.87 Continued long-term clinical trials and post-marketing pharmacovigilance will be essential to fully characterise the safety of these next-generation therapies.87,88

Similarly, dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonism introduces additional mechanistic complexity that may influence the adverse-event profile compared with selective GLP-1 receptor agonism. While GLP-1 receptor activation primarily drives delayed gastric emptying and dose-dependent gastrointestinal adverse events, GIP receptor activation may modulate central emetic pathways. However, these mechanistic effects have not translated into clinically meaningful reductions in gastrointestinal adverse events, and tirzepatide retains a predominantly GLP-1-like, dose-dependent gastrointestinal safety profile in clinical trials. This likely reflects the greater pharmacodynamic potency and integrated physiological effects of dual receptor agonism rather than simple additive receptor activation.87,89,90

From a clinical perspective, the available evidence supports individualised prescribing based on the patient’s risk profile, gradual dose escalation, proactive patient counselling before treatment initiation, regular assessment for clinically relevant adverse events during therapy, and targeted monitoring for renal dysfunction, retinopathy, pancreatitis, gallbladder disease, and musculoskeletal health, where clinically indicated. Prompt evaluation of persistent or severe symptoms may facilitate early recognition of uncommon adverse events, improve treatment adherence, and optimise the safe use of incretin-based therapies.91–94

The use of incretin-based therapies in special populations requires an individualised risk–benefit assessment. In older adults, these agents offer effective glycemic control with a low intrinsic risk of hypoglycemia and additional cardiovascular and renal benefits; however, treatment should consider frailty, sarcopenia, gastrointestinal intolerance, dehydration, and polypharmacy. In patients with chronic renal disease, GLP-1 receptor agonists are increasingly recommended because of their cardiovascular and kidney-protective effects and can be used across a broad range of renal function. Conversely, evidence remains limited in pregnancy, lactation, adolescents, and patients with advanced frailty or severe hepatic impairment, warranting cautious use and individualised clinical decision-making. Strengths and Limitations

The primary strength of this review is its mechanistic framing of adverse events associated with glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists, drawing on evidence from randomised trials, observational research, and regulatory safety reports to facilitate a biologically coherent interpretation across populations with obesity and type 2 diabetes. The analysis seeks to minimise inappropriate causal attribution and inform clinically applicable risk-mitigation approaches across populations with obesity and type 2 diabetes users.

Nonetheless, as a narrative review, it remains vulnerable to selection bias and does not incorporate formal risk-of-bias appraisal or quantitative meta-analytic methods.88,89 In addition, conclusions regarding uncommon or delayed adverse events are derived largely from observational studies and pharmacovigilance datasets, highlighting the need for adequately powered, long-term real-world safety investigations.95,96 Although a structured literature search and predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied, the qualitative synthesis of the evidence may remain subject to selection bias and heterogeneity across the included studies. In addition, variations in study design, populations, follow-up duration, and outcome reporting limited direct comparability between studies and precluded quantitative pooling of the findings.

Conclusions

This review highlights that the safety profile of incretin-based therapies is largely attributable to predictable pharmacological effects and physiological adaptations, with most adverse events being dose-dependent and manageable through appropriate clinical care. Many emerging safety signals are derived primarily from observational studies and pharmacovigilance data and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Overall, the benefit–risk profile remains favourable, with established cardiometabolic benefits generally outweighing uncommon and manageable safety concerns. As newer dual- and triple-agonist therapies expand the therapeutic landscape, individualised risk stratification, appropriate monitoring, clinical action and continued pharmacovigilance will remain essential. Future prospective studies should better characterise rare, delayed, and patient-specific adverse events to support precision-based use of incretin-based therapies.
