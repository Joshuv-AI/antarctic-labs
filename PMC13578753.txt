Socioeconomic and partner-related determinants of different forms of sexual violence against women in Türkiye
Authors: Mehmet Latif Candemir, Kenan Özmen, Hüseyin Doğan, İsmail Dönmez, Ömer Alkan, Ceyhun Serçemeli, Adnan Küçükali, Emine Genç
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sexual violence against women is a major public health and human rights issue with severe psychological, social, and economic consequences. Although previous studies have extensively examined intimate partner violence, most have treated sexual violence as a single homogeneous outcome and have not separately investigated the determinants of different forms of sexual violence within the same analytical framework. In particular, limited evidence exists on whether forced sexual intercourse, sexual intercourse due to fear of the partner, and coercion into degrading sexual acts are associated with distinct sociodemographic and relational risk factors in Türkiye. This study aimed to identify the determinants of these three different forms of sexual violence against women in Türkiye. The study used data from the 2014 National Research on Domestic Violence Against Women in Türkiye conducted by the Hacettepe University Institute of Population Studies. The analysis included 6458 women aged 15–59 who had ever been married or in a relationship. Weighted binary logistic regression models and average marginal effects were employed to examine sociodemographic, household, and partner-related determinants. The findings showed that partner-related violence variables were consistently associated with all forms of sexual violence. The findings showed that partner-related violence factors were consistently associated with all forms of sexual violence. Emotional violence, physical violence, partner infidelity, economic violence, fear of the partner, and prior exposure to sexual violence emerged as significant correlates across the models. Conversely, higher educational attainment and greater household welfare were generally associated with lower risks of sexual victimisation. Conversely, university education and higher household welfare levels were negatively associated with sexual violence. The findings demonstrate that different forms of sexual violence are associated with distinct but overlapping relational and socioeconomic determinants, highlighting the need for multidimensional prevention policies addressing multiple forms of violence simultaneously in Türkiye.

Introduction

Sexual violence is considered a significant public health issue due to its high prevalence rates worldwide and the serious human and economic impacts it entails1. It has been found that 24% of adolescent girls aged 15–19 worldwide have experienced intimate partner sexual violence at least once in their lives, and 16% have experienced it in the past year2. Sexual violence encompasses a broad spectrum of issues that permeate the very fabric of societal structures2. This clearly demonstrates that sexual violence is not only an individual problem but one shaped by social and structural dynamics. Research highlights that economic dependence, patriarchal social norms, traditional gender roles3, pathological illnesses4, and lack of education and awareness5 have been highlighted as factors that increase women’s vulnerability to sexual violence.

In this study, sexual violence is considered as a broad spectrum encompassing non-consensual sexual behaviour. Sexual coercion, meanwhile, is positioned as a specific sub-type within this spectrum. Sexual violence is a broad concept encompassing non-consensual sexual acts, attempts at sexual acts, unwanted sexual contact, and coercive interventions targeting a person’s sexuality6. Sexual coercion, on the other hand, refers to a narrower conceptual framework that describes the act of forcing a person to consent to an unwanted sexual act, primarily through verbal pressure, persistence, manipulation, threats, power imbalances, or exploitation of dependent relationships rather than the use of physical force7. Therefore, sexual coercion can be considered one of the subtypes of sexual violence; in contrast, sexual violence refers to a broader range of behaviours that include physical force, threats, intimidation, unwanted touching, sexual humiliation, and non-contact non-consensual sexual experiences6,8.

In studies that associate sexual violence against women with individual factors, low educational level, child marriage2, the age of adolescents, the age of the sexual partner9, substance use by individuals10, and women’s past experiences of violence11. For example, in a study conducted in Türkiye by Tunc, et al.12, individual factors such as not being officially married, low educational level, and men’s alcohol use were prominent among the determinants of sexual violence against pregnant women. Furthermore, individual vulnerabilities such as mental health issues, disability status, and previous traumatic experiences can influence both exposure risk and help-seeking behaviour13,14.

In studies linking sexual violence against women to social factors, gender inequalities2, gender roles and norms, cultural contexts11, family structure, school type, and educational environment are highlighted9. In a study conducted by Apatinga and Tenkorang10, it was observed that social power dynamics and traditional masculinity norms render women vulnerable to violence. Furthermore, it has been noted that traditional understandings of women’s gender roles and statuses are sustained by social norms that perpetuate and often normalise violence9,11.

In the context of Türkiye, the fact that social structure and social norms increase the risk of sexual violence indicates that the normative order shaped by family, marriage, honour, privacy, and gender roles can be a determining factor in the emergence and perpetuation of sexual violence15–17. The rural-urban settlement pattern, migration, seasonal labour, and extended-family household structures, along with religious/ethnic subcultures, alter the nature of social control and solidarity at the community level, thereby differentiating both risk factors and support mechanisms18. Local studies on early marriages in Türkiye indicate that family pressure, coercion, fear of remaining unmarried, dowry, patriarchal norms, and gender inequality shape girls’ marriage processes, thereby restricting women’s bodily autonomy and sexual rights19.

In Türkiye, the patriarchal family structure, the association of women’s sexuality with family and male control, discourses of “honour” and “shame,” the victim-blaming, and the perception of domestic violence as a private matter can lead to the invisibility of sexual consent and the underreporting of sexual violence, particularly in spousal and partner relationships20,21. Indeed, the Study on Domestic Violence Against Women in Türkiye reveals that 12% of married women have experienced sexual violence at some point in their lives, and 5% have experienced it within the past 12 months at the hands of their spouse or male partner; the most commonly reported form of sexual violence is engaging in sexual intercourse against her will out of fear22. The same study indicates that 89% of women who experienced physical and/or sexual violence did not seek help from any institution or organisation, suggesting that sexual violence in Türkiye must be addressed in conjunction with issues of stigmatisation, pressure to preserve family unity, lack of institutional trust, and insufficient social support22.

Finally, studies linking sexual violence against women to structural factors have identified economic dependence2, poverty10, structural racial and gender inequalities23, lack of legal protection3, traditional masculinity norms10, and low levels of social support24. Studies conducted in the US have indicated that structural racial and gender inequalities are important determinants shaping women’s experiences of sexual violence and that these inequalities limit access to justice23. The study conducted by Dias, et al.24 emphasised that intimate partner violence and low levels of social support in Europe are among the fundamental structural factors that increase sexual violence. Similarly, Masa’Deh, et al.25 found in their study in Jordan that low educational level, low income, and perceived low social support are important structural factors that increase sexual violence against women. Regional inequalities in Türkiye, combined with differences in the spatial distribution of women’s shelters, refuges, forensic medicine units, and specialist support networks, can lead to significant disparities in victims’ access to justice and protection26,27. Shocks such as natural disasters and mass displacement (e.g., the 2023 earthquakes) both increase the risk of violence and exacerbate vulnerability by disrupting service delivery28–30.

The three outcome variables were analysed separately; this is because forced sexual intercourse, engaging in sexual intercourse out of fear, and being forced into sexually degrading or humiliating behaviour represent conceptually distinct manifestations of sexual violence and may be associated with both common and species-specific risk factors.

In the Turkish context, this distinction is even more significant because family privacy, honour, gender roles, victim-blaming, reluctance to seek institutional help, and regional/socioeconomic inequalities influence both the nature of sexual violence and its visibility. Furthermore, studies that use nationally representative data to model different types of sexual violence separately are limited in Türkiye. This study uses data from the 2014 Survey on Domestic Violence Against Women in Türkiye to examine the determinants of three distinct types of sexual violence separately among 6458 women who were married or in a relationship; thereby aiming to go beyond the generalising approach of existing studies to produce Türkiye -specific empirical evidence and contribute to the development of intervention policies that are sensitive to the different forms of violence. In this context, the binary logistic regression method was used to identify the risk factors for different types of sexual violence. The study aims to contribute to policy development processes and protective interventions in this field by identifying the factors associated with different forms of sexual violence against women in Türkiye.

This study makes three main contributions to the literature.

Most studies treat sexual violence as a single outcome. However, this study examines sexual violence against women from three different perspectives (forced intercourse, intercourse out of fear, and coercion into humiliating acts).

First, by analysing these three distinct types of sexual violence as separate dependent variables, this study identifies the common and distinct risk factors associated with these forms of violence.

Second, the study analyses the determinants of sexual violence using a large-scale dataset representative of the national population in Türkiye. In this regard, the research provides comprehensive empirical evidence revealing the relationship between sexual violence and sociodemographic and partner-related factors in the Turkish context.

Third, the study evaluates the relationships between sexual violence and not only women’s individual characteristics but also spousal/partner behaviours, household characteristics, and other forms of violence. This multidimensional approach demonstrates that sexual violence is shaped not only by individual factors but also by relational and structural factors.

In these respects, the study provides new empirical findings to the literature by more thoroughly identifying the determinants of different types of sexual violence against women and offers important implications for policymakers.

Materials and methods

Study design

The National Research on Domestic Violence against Women in Türkiye is one of the most comprehensive studies conducted nationwide to understand the dimension, content, causes, effects and risk factors of domestic violence experienced by women in Türkiye. It was conducted for the first time in 2008 to determine the different aspects and reasons of violence against women and to meet the requirement of collecting data on this issue. The National Research on Domestic Violence against Women in Türkiye, conducted in 2014, is significant in terms of reflecting the change regarding violence against women since the research conducted in 200822.

The research questionnaire was designed by taking into account the questionnaire applied by the WHO in the study titled “Multi-country Study on Women’s Health and Domestic Violence against Women”31. New questions regarding country-specific requirements, particularly targeting the monitoring of legal regulations, were also added to the questionnaire22.

Setting

Within the scope of research on violence, Türkiye has been divided into 30 strata to ensure estimates at the country, urban/rural, and 12- and 5-region levels. Except for the Istanbul region, which is among the 12 regions, the distribution between urban and rural strata was approximately 75% to 25% in the other regions. In Istanbul, approximately 5% of the households were selected from the rural areas. In the research, settlements with a population of 10,000 or more constituted the urban strata, and those with a population of less than 10,000 constituted the rural strata. The research sample was selected using cluster sampling22.

Study size and participants

In the National Research on Domestic Violence against Women in Türkiye, the questionnaires were administered in the form of a household questionnaire (asking about the number of individuals in the household, the number of rooms in the household, indicators of welfare level, etc.) and a woman’s questionnaire (asking about the socio-demographic and other characteristics of the woman and her spouse/partner). These data were sent in two separate Excel files. The Excel file containing household and women’s data was merged into a single data file. After merging, 392 women whose household information could not be accessed were excluded, leaving 7070 women for analysis. Since the study examined the exposure of married women, women who had been in a relationship, or women currently in a relationship to sexual violence by their spouse/partner, data on 612 women who had never been in a relationship at the time of the survey were excluded. As a result, the number of units to be considered was calculated as 645822.

Data sources/measurement

In this study, cross-sectional data from the 2014 National Research on Domestic Violence against Women in Türkiye, conducted by the Institute of Population Studies of Hacettepe University, were used.

The survey questionnaires of The National Research on Domestic Violence against Women in Türkiye were implemented by the research team. Ethical rules developed by the WHO were followed at every stage of the study, and various measures were taken to ensure the safety of both the participating women and the research team. Written consent was obtained from the participants before each interview. The researchers received training on Ethical and Safety Rules and conducted themselves with sensitivity to the subject throughout the interview process. The interviews were conducted with one woman from each household. In the event that more than one woman in the 15–59 age group was in the household, the participating woman was selected at random to avoid asking the same questions to more than one woman in the same household. The researchers were very meticulous in ensuring that the interviews were conducted in an isolated environment. Moreover, all interviewers were trained on the confidentiality of the interviews. During the process of obtaining consent and providing information about the study’s content, participants were informed that their answers would be kept confidential22.

Bias

The data regarding women’s history of exposure to sexual violence were the subjective responses of women. Thus, there was a decided risk that any data obtained by this method could be biased.

Outcome variables

According to the Survey on Domestic Violence Against Women in Türkiye, the following questions were asked to the women participating in the survey regarding their exposure to sexual violence during the period when the survey was conducted: “Has your spouse or any of the people you are with ever forced you to have sexual intercourse using physical force against your will? (SV1)”, “Have you ever had sexual intercourse against your will because you were afraid of what your spouse or any of the people you were with might do? (SV2)”, “Has your spouse or any of the people you were with ever forced you to do something that you found sexually degrading or humiliating? (SV3)”.

The dependent variables of the study are SV1 (forced intercourse), SV2 (intercourse due to fear), and SV3 (coercion into degrading acts), which represent the women’s exposure to sexual violence at the time of the survey. Women who participated in the study and were exposed to sexual violence (SV1, SV2, SV3) were coded as “1,” while those who were not were coded as “0.”

Independent variables

The selection of independent variables was guided by the socioecological framework of violence and prior empirical studies on intimate partner violence and sexual violence against women1,10,24,25,31. According to this perspective, violence against women is shaped not only by individual characteristics but also by relational, household, community, and structural factors10,32. Therefore, the study included demographic, socioeconomic, partner-related, and violence-related variables that have been identified in previous research as potential determinants of women’s exposure to sexual violence12.

Demographic and socioeconomic variables such as age, education level, marital status, health status, employment status, household welfare level, and region were included because previous studies have shown that socioeconomic inequalities, educational attainment, economic vulnerability, and regional disparities may influence both the prevalence and reporting of violence against women10,24,25,33. In particular, the region variable was included to capture the substantial cultural, socioeconomic, and gender-norm differences across regions in Türkiye15,18,21.

Partner-related variables, including partner education, drug use, infidelity, controlling behaviours, physical aggression, and acceptance of wife beating, were incorporated because relational power imbalance, coercive control, and risky partner behaviours are widely recognised predictors of sexual violence in intimate relationships10,12. Furthermore, variables related to women’s exposure to physical, emotional, economic, and sexual violence were included because previous studies indicate that different forms of violence frequently coexist and may reinforce the risk of sexual victimisation8,23,32.

Demographic and socioeconomic variables related to women: region (Istanbul, Western Marmara, Aegean, Eastern Marmara, Western Anatolia, Mediterranean, Central Anatolia, Western Black Sea, Eastern Black Sea, Northeast Anatolia, Central East Anatolia, Southeast)34, age (15–24, 25–34, 35–44, 45–54, and 55+)2, education level (illiterate, elementary school, middle school, high school, university)12, marital status (single, married)12, health status (excellent/good, fair, poor/very poor), whether the woman contributes more to the household income (no, yes), and whether she fears her spouse/partner (no, yes)10.

Household well-being was measured using an asset-based household welfare index. The survey collected information on household ownership of a range of durable goods and assets, including refrigerators, washing machines, televisions, computers, internet access, private cars, and agricultural equipment. The average market prices of these assets in 2014 were obtained from the Turkish Statistical Institute (TurkStat). These prices were not adjusted for inflation because all asset values referred to the same survey year. Since this variable consists of market prices, its values were divided into approximately four equal parts (the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentile values were calculated) and converted into a four-category variable. The categories of these variables were established as “1st welfare level (lowest),” “2nd welfare level,” “3rd welfare level,” and “4th welfare level (highest)”.

Asset-based measures are widely used in demographic, public health, and development research as indicators of long-term household socioeconomic status because ownership of durable goods reflects accumulated economic resources more reliably than current income, which may fluctuate over time and may be subject to reporting bias. Household asset indices have been extensively employed in large-scale population surveys, including the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS), to assess household welfare and socioeconomic position. Accordingly, the present study used ownership of household assets as a proxy measure of household well-being35,36.

Factors related to the woman’s spouse/partner: spouse/partner’s education level (illiterate, elementary school, middle school, high school, university)25, spouse/partner’s drug use (no, yes)10, whether the spouse/partner physically fights with the man (no, yes), whether the spouse/partner cheats (no, yes), whether the man beating his wife is considered acceptable (no, yes)37,38, and whether the spouse/partner is controlling37,39.

Other factors that may be related to violence: exposure to economic violence by spouse/partner (no, yes)40, exposure to physical violence by spouse/partner (no, yes)38,41, exposure to sexual violence by spouse/partner (no, yes), exposure to physical violence by someone other than spouse/partner (no, yes)14, experiencing sexual violence from someone other than a spouse/partner (no, yes)42,43, and experiencing emotional violence from someone other than a spouse/partner (no, yes)40.

Analysis method

Survey statistics in Stata 15 (Stata Corporation) were used to account for the complex sampling design and weights (womenweight). Weighted analysis was performed45. First, the frequency and percentages of sexual violence exposure among women participating in the study and the independent variables were obtained. In this study, the binary logistic regression method was used to investigate the differences in exposure to sexual violence according to SV1, SV2, and SV3.

The coefficients estimated in the binary logistic model indicate the change in the z-score resulting from a unit change in the independent variable. Therefore, the interpretation of the coefficients in the binary logistic model is not meaningful. The sign of the coefficient indicates the direction of the relationship between the independent variable and the probability; the size of the coefficient does not directly indicate the magnitude of the effect.

The marginal effect is the partial derivative of the regression equation with respect to each independent variable. In the linear regression model, the slope coefficient equals the marginal effect for each variable. It represents the change in the dependent variable in response to a unit change in the independent variable. In nonlinear regression models such as the probit model, the slope is not constant across all points; it changes with the observation points, that is, the marginal effect varies with the observation points. In nonlinear regression models, the average marginal effects are interpreted, since interpretations based on any single point are not meaningful46.

Marginal effects can be calculated in two different ways: marginal effects for means and average marginal effects. When calculating marginal effects for the means, the mean of each variable is first computed, and the marginal effects are then obtained for these means. Since the mean for dummy variables is statistically invalid, the marginal effects for the means are not appropriate in models with dummy variables among the independent variables. When calculating the average marginal effects, marginal effects are calculated for all units in the data set, that is, for each observation point, and these marginal effects are averaged. Since the models used contain categorical independent variables, average marginal effects were calculated46.

Marginal effects (ME) were preferred for interpretation because they provide more intuitive probability-based interpretations than logistic regression coefficients or odds ratios, particularly in models containing numerous categorical variables. Furthermore, given the large number of predictors and the estimation of three separate models, reporting odds ratios with confidence intervals for all variables would considerably increase table complexity and reduce readability.

For completeness and transparency, both the estimated regression coefficients and the corresponding average marginal effects are reported. While the coefficients reflect the underlying logistic model estimates, the marginal effects provide a more readily interpretable measure of the magnitude of associations in probability terms.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The data were obtained through the joint teamwork of both the Turkish Statistical Institute (TSI) and the European Union Statistical Office (SOEU). We obtained this data from TSI in return for a contract without needing an ethics committee document and used it in our study.

For this study, secondary data were employed. Official approval was received from the Turkish Statistical Institute to use the microdata set from the National Research on Domestic Violence against Women in Türkiye.

The Turkish Statistical Institute also received a “Letter of Undertaking” authorizing it to use the study’s data. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Table 2 presents the descriptive characteristics of the women included in the study. The majority of the women were married (86.0%), unemployed (69.3%), and had an elementary school education (45.0%), indicating a predominantly low-educated sample. Most participants reported good or fair health status (86.9%). Regarding age distribution, women aged 25–34 years constituted the largest group (30.7%), followed by those aged 35–44 years (27.1%). In terms of household welfare, the distribution across welfare categories was relatively balanced. Most women reported that their spouses/partners did not use drugs (99.4%), gamble (98.0%), or engage in physical fights with other men (89.9%). However, substantial proportions of women reported exposure to emotional violence (41.2%), physical violence (33.0%), and economic violence (27.2%) by their spouse/partner. Additionally, 16.6% of women reported fearing their spouse/partner.

It is stated that a variance inflation factor (VIF) of 5 or higher indicates moderate multicollinearity, while a VIF of 10 or higher indicates high multicollinearity. In this study, no independent variables are multicollinear.

For SV1 (forced sexual intercourse), several relational and socioeconomic factors were significantly associated with the predicted probability of sexual violence. Among the partner-related variables, emotional violence (ME = 0.857), physical violence (ME = 1.235), fear of the partner (ME = 0.714), physical harm (ME = 0.975), partner infidelity (ME = 0.395), economic violence (ME = 0.280), and the partner’s involvement in physical fights (ME = 0.265) were positively associated with SV1. Women who had experienced sexual violence by someone other than their spouse/partner also showed higher predicted probabilities of SV1 (ME = 0.576).

Regional and demographic differences were also observed. Women living in Western Anatolia had higher predicted probabilities of SV1 than those living in Southeastern Anatolia (ME = 0.547). Similarly, women aged 45–54 exhibited higher predicted probabilities than women aged 55 years and older (ME = 0.500).

In contrast, several socioeconomic indicators appeared to protect against SV1. University education was negatively associated with the predicted probability of forced sexual intercourse (ME = −0.984). Likewise, women living in households with 4–5 members had lower predicted probabilities than those living in households with 6 or more members (ME = −0.331). Household welfare level also showed a protective pattern, as women in the 2nd, 3rd, and 4th welfare groups exhibited lower predicted probabilities of SV1 (ME = −0.410, −0.390, and − 0.552, respectively) than women in the lowest welfare category.

For SV2 (sexual intercourse due to fear), several partner-related and violence-related variables were significantly associated with the predicted probability of sexual violence. Emotional violence by the spouse/partner demonstrated the strongest positive association with SV2 (ME = 1.625), followed by physical harm (ME = 1.045), physical violence by the spouse/partner (ME = 0.864), fear of the partner (ME = 0.607), Kurdish native language of the spouse/partner (ME = 0.572), economic violence by the spouse/partner (ME = 0.469), exposure to violence by the spouse/partner (ME = 0.411), partner infidelity (ME = 0.380), the partner’s involvement in physical fights (ME = 0.271), and economic violence from someone other than the spouse/partner (ME = 0.235). Women who had experienced sexual violence from someone other than their spouse/partner also exhibited higher predicted probabilities of SV2 (ME = 0.383). In addition, working women had higher predicted probabilities of SV2 than non-working women (ME = 0.226).

Several contextual and demographic factors were negatively associated with the predicted probability of SV2. Women living in Central Anatolia (ME = −0.672) and Western Black Sea (ME = −0.543) had lower predicted probabilities than those living in Southeastern Anatolia. Similarly, women aged 25–34 years (ME = −0.429), women reporting fair health status (ME = −0.330), and women living in households with three or fewer members (ME = −0.412) exhibited lower predicted probabilities of SV2. Legal knowledge was also negatively associated with SV2 (ME = −0.721). Furthermore, spouse/partner drug use demonstrated a negative association with the predicted probability of SV2 (ME = −1.200).

For SV3 (coercion into degrading sexual acts), substantial regional differences were observed. Women living in Istanbul (ME = 0.986), Aegean (ME = 1.199), Eastern Marmara (ME = 1.285), Western Black Sea (ME = 0.990), and Eastern Black Sea (ME = 1.724) exhibited higher predicted probabilities of SV3 compared with women living in Southeastern Anatolia.

Several partner-related and violence-related variables were also positively associated with the predicted probability of SV3. Emotional violence by the spouse/partner showed one of the strongest associations (ME = 1.429), followed by physical violence by the spouse/partner (ME = 1.010), physical harm (ME = 0.999), educational disparity between partners (ME = 0.831), exposure to sexual violence from someone other than the spouse/partner (ME = 0.800), fear of the partner (ME = 0.757), partner infidelity (ME = 0.657), economic violence by the spouse/partner (ME = 0.518), economic violence from someone other than the spouse/partner (ME = 0.591), and the partner’s involvement in physical fights (ME = 0.446).

In contrast, several demographic and socioeconomic characteristics were negatively associated with the predicted probability of SV3. Married women had lower predicted probabilities than single women (ME = −0.625). Similarly, women reporting fair health status had lower predicted probabilities than those with poor/very poor health (ME = −0.515). Women whose spouse/partner had a middle school (ME = −0.883) or high school education (ME = −0.935) also had lower predicted probabilities of SV3 than women whose spouse/partner had a university education. Furthermore, spouse gambling behaviour was negatively associated with the predicted probability of SV3 (ME = −0.965).

Discussion

This study addresses a significant gap in the literature by examining different forms of sexual violence (SV1, SV2, and SV3) within separate analytical frameworks, rather than treating sexual violence against women in Türkiye as a single, homogeneous outcome variable. A large portion of existing studies assess sexual violence as a combined outcome and overlook the potential heterogeneity among different forms of violence. Indeed, Dartnall and Jewkes32 have highlighted the difficulty of examining the issue in terms of distinct types, despite the existence of many different types of violence. The literature increasingly emphasises that sexual violence is a multidimensional phenomenon and that different forms may be associated with distinct risk factors39. In this regard, the study reached similar findings, concluding that sexual violence is a multi-layered phenomenon shaped at the individual, relational, and structural levels47.

The findings indicate that relational factors such as physical violence, emotional abuse, and fear of one’s partner are strongly associated with sexual violence. This pattern is consistent with coercive control frameworks suggesting that intimate partner violence operates as a continuum of power and control, where emotional, physical, and sexual abuse are interconnected rather than isolated phenomena. Empirical evidence further indicates that different forms of intimate partner violence frequently co-occur, with physical, emotional, and sexual violence reinforcing each other within abusive relationships rather than occurring independently48. This finding suggests that sexual violence is often not an isolated behaviour but rather part of a pattern of power-based intimate partner violence (IPV)37. Empirical studies have shown that sexual intimate partner violence frequently co-occurs with physical violence, coercive control, and psychological abuse, indicating that different forms of IPV are highly interconnected rather than independent phenomena49,50. Moreover, factors such as socioeconomic disadvantage, age differences between partners, coercive control, and contextual characteristics such as rurality have been identified as important predictors of sexual IPV victimization and severity, highlighting the multidimensional nature of IPV risk across settings50,51. These results are consistent with the broader literature, which similarly emphasises the clustering of different forms of IPV within intimate relationships. The literature also notes that while sexual violence and IPV are typically treated as separate research areas, this distinction does not adequately reflect real-life dynamics39. Mechanisms such as power asymmetry, controlling behaviours, and emotional coercion can increase the partner’s control over sexual behaviours, thereby raising the risk of sexual violence. In line with this perspective, the present findings reinforce the argument that relational power structures are central determinants of sexual victimisation. These findings are also consistent with the coercive control literature and demonstrate that sexual violence is a process stemming not only from physical coercion but also from relational power imbalances52. Furthermore, the literature indicates that women exposed to physical violence are more likely to experience sexual violence as well38,41. Overall, the current results extend previous evidence by showing that these relational dynamics operate across different forms of sexual violence rather than being limited to a single subtype.

It is also noted that physical violence can often manifest as mutual conflict or self-defence behaviour53. This finding supports the notion that forms of violence do not occur in isolation but rather in intertwined patterns. Furthermore, the tendency for different forms of violence to co-occur indicates that women may be exposed to economic, emotional, and physical violence simultaneously, and that this situation increases the risk of sexual violence40.

The fact that socioeconomic factors such as educational level and welfare level have a protective effect against the risk of sexual violence points to the protective role of individual resources54–56. This relationship must be explained not merely as a statistical correlation but through multi-layered mechanisms. A higher level of education can enhance women’s awareness of their legal rights, thereby strengthening their ability to recognise and resist violence. Consistent with structural evidence, women’s education has been shown to operate through multiple empowerment pathways, including increased decision-making capacity, delayed marriage, and reduced acceptance of violence, all of which contribute to lower intimate partner violence risk57. Empirical evidence also suggests that empowerment-based approaches that enhance women’s decision-making capacity and problem-solving skills are associated with improved outcomes in intimate partner violence and reduced vulnerability to revictimisation58. Further evidence indicates that women’s educational attainment is positively associated with personal agency, which in turn reduces vulnerability to intimate partner violence by strengthening autonomous decision-making and resistance to coercive relationship dynamics59. Additionally, education can increase economic independence, reduce relational dependency, and weaken power asymmetries. Evidence from large-scale multi-country studies further indicates that women’s decision-making capacity and socioeconomic status are significantly associated with intimate partner violence, although the direction and magnitude of these associations may vary across contexts60. Empirical evidence from different socio-cultural contexts also shows that more egalitarian decision-making processes and balanced household resource distribution are associated with lower acceptance of intimate partner violence, highlighting the importance of intra-household power relations in shaping violence-related outcomes61.

Similarly, the literature indicates that socioeconomic vulnerability increases the risk of both sexual violence and intimate partner violence. Tarzia, et al.62 found that financial insecurity and low social support increase the risk of violence among female university students internationally. These findings support the notion that socioeconomic vulnerability is a structural risk factor.

Overall, these results indicate that women’s access to economic and social resources serves as a protective buffer against violence63–67.

One of the study’s significant contributions is demonstrating that SV1, SV2, and SV3 may be associated with distinct determinants. This finding aligns with the view that sexual violence is not a homogeneous category and can be produced through different mechanisms32. In the literature, studies examining sexual violence through the SV1 forced intercourse dimension68–70, those examining it through the SV2 fear dimension71,72, and those focusing on the SV3 degrading behaviour dimension73–75. In this context, the contribution of the present study lies in examining three forms of sexual violence within the same analytical framework and comparing their determinants. This approach allows both common and form-specific risk factors to be identified.

While forms of sexual violence based on direct physical coercion are typically associated with overt use of force and physical control behaviours73, more covert forms of sexual coercion may manifest through psychological pressure, economic dependence, and relational power imbalances76. This distinction demonstrates that sexual violence is a multi-layered process determined not only by behaviour but also by relational context77,78. This aligns with the literature suggesting that different dimensions of sexual violence may be associated with different risk factors39.

The comparison of the three models further supports this interpretation. While emotional violence, physical violence, partner fear, physical harm, partner infidelity, and partner economic violence emerged as common predictors across all three forms of sexual violence, several factors showed form-specific associations. Forced sexual intercourse (SV1) was more strongly associated with educational attainment and household welfare, suggesting a greater role of socioeconomic resources. Sexual intercourse due to fear (SV2) was additionally associated with employment status, legal knowledge, and certain partner characteristics, indicating the importance of relational vulnerability and perceived consequences within the relationship. In contrast, coercion into degrading sexual acts (SV3) was more strongly associated with educational disparity between partners, marital status, partner education, and regional differences. These findings suggest that although different forms of sexual violence share common relational foundations, they may also operate through distinct social and contextual pathways.

In Türkiye, regions can broadly be grouped into western and eastern parts based on socioeconomic development and cultural characteristics. Western regions (including Istanbul, Western Marmara, Aegean, Eastern Marmara, Western Anatolia, and parts of the Mediterranean region) are generally more urbanised, economically developed, and characterised by comparatively more egalitarian gender norms. In contrast, eastern regions (including Southeastern Anatolia and parts of Eastern and Northeastern Anatolia) are relatively less urbanised and are characterised by lower socioeconomic development and more traditional gender role structures.

In the Turkish context, sexual violence is characterised by significant structural factors that influence both its occurrence and its reporting. In regions where patriarchal norms are more prevalent, reporting rates may be low because violence is viewed as an “internal family matter”79. A study conducted among Muslim women in Türkiye demonstrated that beliefs regarding sinfulness, sexual dysfunction, and experiences of sexual violence are associated with attitudes toward family planning80. Similarly, studies indicate that women living in eastern regions are more likely to experience violence81. A forensic psychiatry study conducted in Türkiye has shown that sexual violence may be associated with various psychiatric outcomes; in particular, it has been reported that violence perpetrated by family members is associated with higher rates of psychiatric diagnoses82. However, these findings may vary by context, and in some cases, the higher risk observed in Western regions can be explained by differences in reporting and levels of social awareness.

In the present study, regional variation was observed across all three forms of sexual violence. Interestingly, higher predicted probabilities for SV3 were observed in several western regions, suggesting that observed differences may reflect not only true variation in risk but also differences in awareness, reporting behaviour, and disclosure patterns across regions.

There is also evidence showing that women’s lifetime experiences of violence increase with age83. This study, however, suggests that the middle-aged group may present a specific risk profile. Therefore, the differences observed in the Turkish context may reflect both the actual distribution of risk and societal norms, stigmatisation, and reporting behaviours.

The study indicates that spousal/partner behaviour is a determining factor in the risk of sexual violence. The association of factors such as economic and emotional violence with sexual violence demonstrates that violence tends to cluster in multiple forms40. The partner’s infidelity was also found to be associated with an increased risk; this finding is consistent with some studies in the literature84, though it is noted that findings are not consistent across all contexts85.

An unexpected finding was the negative association between partner drug use and SV2 (sexual intercourse due to fear). This result should be interpreted with caution. In the present study, reported partner drug use was rare, accounting for only 0.6% of the sample. Therefore, the estimated association may be sensitive to sample-specific variation. In addition, the multivariable model simultaneously controlled for several closely related factors, including emotional violence, physical violence, partner fear, economic violence, and infidelity. Previous evidence generally suggests that substance use is associated with an increased risk of intimate partner violence. For example, a meta-analysis of 285 studies by Cafferky, et al. 48 reported significant positive associations between drug use and both IPV perpetration and victimization, with drug use emerging as a particularly strong correlate of victimization. Therefore, the negative association observed in the present study is unlikely to indicate a protective effect and may instead reflect contextual characteristics of the sample, residual confounding, or statistical instability resulting from the low prevalence of reported partner drug use. Further research is needed to clarify this relationship.

Fear of the partner and the perception of threats are also significant predictors and are consistent with previous studies86. Additionally, it has been shown that childhood sexual abuse increases the risk of revictimisation in adulthood42,43.

This study goes beyond the traditional approach, which treats sexual violence as a single outcome variable. Across all three models, relational power imbalances and exposure to other forms of intimate partner violence emerged as consistent predictors of sexual violence, indicating a shared underlying mechanism of coercive relationship dynamics. However, differences across SV1, SV2, and SV3 suggest that sexual violence operates through distinct pathways. SV1 (forced intercourse) appears more directly linked to overt physical coercion, whereas SV2 (sex due to fear) reflects psychological pressure and threat-based control. In contrast, SV3 (coercion into degrading acts) may be more closely associated with symbolic dominance, humiliation, and entrenched power asymmetries within the relationship. This differentiation is consistent with evidence suggesting that IPV-related behaviours reflect varying expressions of power and agency rather than a single homogeneous phenomenon59. These findings imply that conceptualising sexual violence as a single undifferentiated outcome may obscure important heterogeneity in underlying mechanisms. Therefore, prevention and intervention strategies should be tailored to address different forms of coercion, ranging from physical force to psychological control and relational humiliation. It demonstrates that different forms of sexual violence may have distinct sociodemographic and relational determinants. In this regard, the study challenges the assumption of “homogeneous sexual violence” prevalent in the literature and highlights the need for more nuanced analytical approaches. The findings broadly support a socioecological interpretation of sexual violence, in which risk emerges through interacting individual, relational, and structural mechanisms. Furthermore, consistent with critiques that the literature on sexual violence and intimate partner violence has largely developed separately, the study demonstrates that these two areas should be evaluated together within the same relational context39. Furthermore, findings suggesting that legal awareness and resilience programs can reduce violence support the importance of individual empowerment interventions87,88.

The findings indicate that intervention programs must focus not only on individual behaviours but also on relational power dynamics and socioeconomic vulnerabilities. Policies aimed at increasing economic independence can strengthen women’s ability to break free from the cycle of violence. Additionally, there is a need to increase training on recognising the various forms of sexual violence within health, legal, and social service systems, and to develop multi-layered risk assessment approaches87,88. The findings suggest that treating sexual violence as a single undifferentiated outcome may obscure important variation in the relational and structural mechanisms underlying different forms of victimisation.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that sexual violence against women is not a homogeneous phenomenon. By analysing forced sexual intercourse, sexual intercourse due to fear of a partner, and coercion into sexually degrading acts separately, the findings reveal both common and form-specific determinants of sexual victimisation in Türkiye. While partner-related factors such as emotional, physical, and economic violence emerged as consistent correlates across all forms of sexual violence, the influence of sociodemographic and socioeconomic characteristics varied across outcomes. These findings contribute to the literature by showing that different forms of sexual violence are shaped by overlapping yet distinct relational and structural mechanisms and therefore require more differentiated analytical and policy approaches.

The findings indicate that sexual violence is closely associated with other forms of spousal/partner-perpetrated violence and relationship-related risk factors. In particular, spousal/partner physical, emotional, and economic abuse; fear of the partner; physical harm; the partner’s infidelity; and the partner’s engagement in physically violent behaviour emerge as key variables associated with various types of sexual violence. This result indicates that sexual violence should be considered as a phenomenon that develops in close partnership relationships intertwined with power, control, fear, and multiple forms of violence.

Our study revealed some variations among the types of sexual violence. The influence of sociodemographic and socioeconomic factors such as regional differences, educational level, household welfare level, household size, marital status, health status, legal knowledge, and employment status varies depending on the type of sexual violence. This finding indicates that different risk factors influence sexual violence.

In conclusion, it has been determined that approaches focused solely on individual risk factors are insufficient in combating sexual violence against women in Türkiye. The findings indicate a need for social policies that address physical, emotional, economic, and sexual violence collectively; take into account the dynamics of power and control in intimate relationships; and strengthen women’s legal knowledge, risk awareness, and access to support mechanisms.

These findings suggest that efforts to prevent sexual violence should adopt a multisectoral approach involving health, legal, educational, and social service institutions. Policies should focus on the early identification of violence, strengthening women’s awareness of their legal rights, improving access to support services, and addressing the broader relational and structural factors that contribute to sexual victimisation. Such efforts should not only respond to violence after it occurs but also promote prevention through gender equality, awareness raising, and the strengthening of support and protection mechanisms. In this way, the fight against violence against women can be carried out through a social policy framework that encompasses early detection, prevention, protection, support, and access to justice.

Overall, the findings of this study indicate that sexual violence against women in Türkiye is strongly embedded within broader patterns of intimate partner violence and is shaped by relational power imbalances, emotional coercion, and socioeconomic vulnerability. Rather than occurring as isolated events, different forms of sexual violence appear to emerge from interconnected mechanisms of coercive control within intimate relationships. These results support a socioecological understanding of sexual violence, in which individual, relational, and structural factors interact to shape women’s vulnerability. In particular, partner-related violence, fear, and economic dependence highlight the central role of power asymmetries in sustaining abusive dynamics. Accordingly, prevention strategies should move beyond individual-level interventions and adopt a multi-level approach that addresses relational power structures and socioeconomic inequalities simultaneously. Strengthening women’s access to education, economic resources, and legal awareness may enhance their autonomy and reduce vulnerability to sexual violence. At the institutional level, coordinated efforts across health, legal, and social service systems are essential. Early identification of risk in healthcare settings, improved legal protection mechanisms, and accessible psychosocial support services should be integrated into a comprehensive response framework. Such an approach would enable early detection, prevention, and sustained support for women at risk of multiple forms of violence.

Limitations of the study

Although the present study provides a broad and generalizable context for understanding violence, it is not without limitations. First, the data used in this study are secondary, and the variables required for statistical analysis were drawn from the dataset. Certain variables found in the literature that influence the risk of exposure to violence, such as a woman’s age at marriage and the age difference between a woman and her spouse or partner, could not be included in the model. Furthermore, since the data are cross-sectional, it was not possible to establish a causal relationship between the intimate partner’s different types of violence and the factors influencing these types of violence. Additionally, it should be noted that the data obtained in the study are based on the women’s own responses. Since there is no official data, the results obtained during data collection may be biased.

An unexpected finding of the study was the negative association between spouse/partner drug use and the probability of sexual intercourse due to fear (SV2). This result should be interpreted cautiously because the prevalence of reported partner drug use in the sample was extremely low (0.6%), which may have produced unstable estimates and wide confidence intervals. In addition, women whose partners use drugs may experience other forms of violence or relationship disruption that are not fully captured by the SV2 measure. Previous studies generally identify substance use as a risk factor for intimate partner violence; therefore, the present finding does not necessarily indicate a protective effect of drug use but may reflect sample-specific characteristics, reporting bias, or unobserved confounding factors. Future studies should investigate this relationship using more detailed measures of substance use and larger samples of drug-using partners.
