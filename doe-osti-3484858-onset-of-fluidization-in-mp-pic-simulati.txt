Executive Summary
Fluidized bed reactors are used across a variety of industries, including for energy processes like pyrolysis that result in
low-cost energy products. Design and scale-up of fluidized beds is de-risked by modeling and simulation, utilizing tools
like NETL’s MFIX-Exa High Performance Computing (HPC) code for reacting multiphase flow. This report summarizes
an investigation into the breadth of problems to which MFIX-Exa may be applied, specifically with regard to low fluid
velocities and the onset of fluidization. A simple fluidization study is conducted both experimentally and numerically
for particles of interest, then reactor simulations are compared to cold flow experiments for uniform distributor plates.
Approaches for modeling bubble caps are also presented.

1 Introduction
Fluidized beds have a wide array of applications across a variety of sectors, including energy conversion and production
of associated products. For three-dimensionally resolved Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) simulations of fluidized
(or circulating) beds at the lab scale and beyond, there are three common approaches: Multiphase Particle-In-Cell (PIC),
Discrete Element Method (DEM), including for the purposes of this list Coarse-Grained DEM (CG-DEM), and Two-Fluid
Method (TFM). PIC differs from DEM in that each advected parcel represents many particles, and collisions are modeled
using a pseudo-pressure solved on an Eulerian mesh. However, PIC retains Lagrangian advection and natural handling of
sharp transitions between regions containing solids and regions that do not. PIC methods [16, 17, 3] are inexpensive enough
to remain tractable for modeling pilot and industrial scale systems while retaining the Lagrangian character of CFD-DEM.1
Unlike CG-DEM, while each parcel represents mass and volume equal to some multiple of real particles (called a statistical
weight), PIC should be thought of as solving for the volume fraction field in a Lagrangian frame—parcels should not be
thought of as a literal collections of particles [13]. The Multiphase Flow with Interphase eXchange at Exascale (MFIX-Exa)
code2 [14, 15] is used in this work; it is the successor to Multiphase Flow with interphase eXchanges (MFiX) [3, 12].
MFIX-Exa supports Embedded Boundarys (EBs), which will be used to impose most boundary conditions throughout
this report.
 The primary difficulty in simulating physical systems using PIC is the selection of modeling parameters. While signif-
icant literature exists on this topic [5, 6, 12, 20], a simple, user-friendly, ‘plug and play’ set of PIC parameters, especially
for low to moderate (1.0–4.0) multiples of Umf , are generally unavailable. While it is known PIC does not perform well
at low multiples of Umf , the number of particles in system to be simulated may render it intractable for DEM, leaving
only continuum approaches or CG-DEM, both of which present their own challenges. While it is easy to assert that PIC
parameters should be tuned for each problem, this is an expensive proposition and requires comparison data—in many
preliminary or exploratory cases this is not done. The objective of this work is to evaluate fluidization behavior using a
set of chosen parameters based heavily on the results of [5], a study in which PIC parameters were tuned for use in a dif-
ferent code, without incorporating any a-posteriori or problem-specific knowledge. Fluidization and defluidization curves
are computed and compared to experiment in Sec. 2, then qualitatively and quantitatively compared to experiments in an
approximately 2 inch bed in Sec. 3. Section 4 outlines a small-scale (DEM) and a large-scale (PIC) approach for modeling
bubble caps. Key limitations of this work include:

2 Fluidization Study
In this section, fluidization studies of the type conducted at NETL in MFAL [19] are compared to numerical simulations in
MFIX-Exa, including both PIC and DEM. To do so, inflow velocities are stepped through the range in which fluidization
phenomena are expected to occur, and the steady-state pressure drop over the bed is reported (see Fig. 3a and Fig. 4a).
The experimental curve reported is from a defluidization study. Numerical results are reported for both fluidization and
defluidization. It is well known that dependency upon past fluidization or agitation of the bed results in some hysteresis in
the state of the bed [21, 8]; it is not expected for this phenomenon to be captured numerically.
 The effects of geometry were ignored in this section; simulations were conducted in a doubly periodic domain. (Geom-
etry will be revisited in Sec. 3 and Sec. 4.) All simulations in this section were conducted with air under standard conditions
(1.204 kg m−3 , 101 325 Pa, 1.813 × 10−5 Pa s), and with an assumed density of 2817.2 kg m−3 for the solid phase. All
cases were monodisperse, but used either the Sauter Mean Diameter (SMD) (151.9 µm) or median (164.5 µm) particle
diameter. To limit the scope of this report, only one set of DEM and PIC parameters, shown in Tbl. 1b and Tbl. 1c
respectively, are reported. To reduce ambiguity, parameters are listed by the configuration option in MFIX-Exa. This gives
a rough yardstick of what kind of agreement might be expected without extensive parameter tuning. Studies of parameter

3 Experimental Bed
An experiment in a 63.5 mm diameter bed will now be qualitatively compared to PIC and DEM simulations. This case
differs from that of Sec. 2 in a few ways:
 • In the case of interest, a 6.35 mm cylinder is suspended in the middle of the bed. The rod is deflected somewhat by
 the particles, but this is not modeled. Experiments and simulations were conducted with and without this rod in an
 effort to determine if there was bypass around the wall, constrained bubble size, etc.

 • The distributor is not uniform across the bed. Instead, there is a ‘dead zone’ around the circumference of the bed.
 Because this dead zone is partially due to glue seepage shown in Fig. 5a, the precise dimensions are not known a-
 priori.

4 Modeling Bubble Caps
To both promote bubbling and prevent back-flow of solids, some fluidized bed reactors include small ‘bubble caps’. While
a full discussion of bubble cap design is out of the scope of this exploration, the cap of interest is shown in Fig. 9a;
the feasibility of modeling approaches for both the DEM and PIC will be commented upon (Sec. 4). In both cases,
lab experiments under nearly standard conditions using a plenum containing eight bubble caps (instead of one or nine
respectively) reported a fluidization threshold near 4 L min−1 total flow through the apparatus at room temperature and
atmospheric pressure. In this section, Umf is understood to mean a flow rate of approximately 1 × 10−5 kg s−1 per bubble
cap.
 Resolution was chosen in a manner appropriate for DEM and PIC respectively, necessitating different approaches to
bubble cap modeling (Fig. 9). In both cases the domain was doubly periodic, and the number of bubble caps was chosen
to be the largest tractable domain up to the size of the real device of interest on the available resources (see Tbl. 3).
 For DEM, we use nearly the exact geometry with the approach roughly illustrated in Fig. 9b. The small jets are
approximately 1.2 mm in diameter—approximately 7 particle diameters across. It is generally suggested that a ∆x / dp
ratio of 2–3 be maintained in DEM simulations, but in this subsection a ratio of ∆x / dp ≈ 0.85 was used to allow for fluid
through these small jets to be resolved. There are not obvious ways to bridge these scales with widely-employed methods.
Particle-Resolved Direct Numerical Simulation (PR-DNS), requiring several mesh points per particle, is intractable. In
DEM, there is a gap between the mesh size ideal for resolving the jets and the mesh size ideal for modeling the particles.
 Simulations exhibit slugging behavior at U / Umf ≈ 3 and U / Umf ≈ 5 (Fig. 10), whereas cold flow experiments
(Fig. 11a and Fig. 11b) do not. It is unclear whether this is due to the slightly different domain (the simulation is of one
periodic bubble cap, whereas the cold flow experiments consist of eight bubble caps arranged in a large cylinder). There is
physical evidence to suggest some of the holes in the bubble caps used in the experiment are partially clogged, which may
also be a factor. Further, it is suspected other uses of these bubble caps resulted in channeling/spouting behavior more
similar to the experiment shown in Fig. 11 than simulation.
 For a much larger scale simulation, we make an approximation of the geometry (Fig. 9c), use a coarser mesh, and
employ PIC. Instead of flowing gas through the cap, the inflow condition is placed on an angled annulus on the underside
of the cap, with inflow normal to this surface. The dimension of the cap is approximately sixteen times larger than the
dimension of the jets; the mesh size used to demonstrate this method was approximately 2.7 times larger, resulting in
∼ 19 times fewer cells. This approach could likely be coarsened further, but even at this resolution is approximately 1440
times faster time to solution per H100 Graphical Processing Unit (GPU) than the DEM approach. (In the DEM case
∆tsim /∆treal ≈ 4.3 × 105 when one bubble cap was modeled on 4 GPUs; in the PIC case ∆tsim /∆treal ≈ 1 × 104
on a single GPU with nine bubble caps in the domain.)
 Qualitative results are shown in Fig. 12. The slices (Figs. 12a, 12c, 12e, 12g) are taken at a 30◦ angle to be consistent
with the slices shown for DEM despite the lack of drilled holes in this approximated geometry. Both the numerical method
and the geometry differ between the bubble cap approximation here and the DEM results. It is clear a uniform distributor,

5 Discussion and Conclusion
It is known PIC does not perform well at low velocities multiples of Umf . However, for some problems in which large
numbers of particles need to be modeled at low velocity, effective three-dimensionally resolved modeling strategies are
lacking. The narrow question addressed here is how low a velocity can be modeled reasonably with PIC. Unfortunately, only
a partial answer is found in the present work. Emphasis was placed on matching of cold-flow experiments and approaches
for bubble caps in a reactor of interest (Sec. 4); exploration of the space of PIC parameters was extremely limited. TFMs
may be a better approach for this problem. Both PIC and TFM depend on a continuum pseudo-pressure for the solid
phase, which will require some degree of parameter tuning. Despite shaky theoretical grounding, CG-DEM may be the
most viable approach.
 Pressure comparison (Fig. 7 and Fig. 8) for a small test bed did not match adequately. The inclusion of bubble caps
created some bed dynamics, but not likely the correct bed dynamics. None of this is surprising at these relatively low Umf .
The problem of interest at the beginning of this investigation was a fluidized bed with approximately the bed height of the
setup in Sec. 4 with nine bubble caps, a surrounding cylindrical wall, and an inserted rod. Unfortunately, this problem is
not easy to simulate in three dimensions at this inflow velocity (as characterized by a multiple of Umf ). PIC is well-known to
perform poorly at low Umf , and DEM is computationally intractable for this case. Further, some preliminary experimental
investigations at high temperature suggest that channeling or spouting may be occurring in the bed of interest (for which
this cold-flow investigation was conducted), and that phenomena may be sensitive to a largely unknown Particle Size
Distribution (PSD). It is unlikely that any of the approaches discussed here will provide high-confidence predictions of bed
behavior (e.g. channeling, spouting, slugging, etc) in different geometries and flow regimes of interest without experimental
validation. It is possible, however, that a modeling effort parallel to experimental investigation may provide some insight
and be a useful diagnostic tool.
