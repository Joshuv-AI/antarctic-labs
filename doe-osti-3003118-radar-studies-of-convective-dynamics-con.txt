1.0 Summary
 The U.S. Department of Energy Atmospheric Radiation Measurement (ARM) User Facility’s third ARM
 Mobile Facility (AMF3)-Bankhead National Forest (BNF) field deployment site and surrounding region
 offer a significant advantage for convective cloud studies. The higher mid-tropospheric humidity
 promotes frequent clouds, and when coupled with seasonal variability in convective environments,
 promotes isolated convection and severe thunderstorms, organized convection in the summertime, and
 strongly sheared convection during transitional seasons.

 The CONVECT field experiment was designed to provide unique, high-spatiotemporal-resolution
 microphysical and dynamical observations of severe weather events. The CONVECT scientific objectives
 align well with the AMF3-BNF site science plan.

 The BNF site features two scanning radar systems: the 2nd-generation C-band Scanning ARM
 Precipitation Radar (CSAPR2) and the X/Ka-band Scanning ARM Cloud Radar (SACR). In CONVECT
 we deployed (Figure 1) a small form factor Ku-band radar called Rain, Ocean, Atmosphere Radar System
 (ROARS) capable of performing agile adaptive sampling of convective clouds. The Ku-band radar was
 deployed on May 29, 2025, at the S30 supplemental site (Falkville, Alabama). It is ~30 km from the
 Advanced Radar for Meteorological and Operational Research (ARMOR) and CSAPR2, in the middle of
 what would be the pair’s southern dual-Doppler lobe. Blockage is not an issue if pointing mostly north
 here, towards ARMOR, etc., and we could sample cells that pass through west to east between these
 radars (with CSAPR2 supplemental coverage).

 Figure 1. The ROARS radar mounted on the tower at the S30 supplementary site and the CONVECT
 project radar network layout.

 In addition, the S30 supplemental site provides complementary resources (disdrometer, radar wind
 profiler) that will improve the radar-based precipitation/convection studies. The Ku-band radar will
 perform targeted observations of convective updrafts using a series of range-height indicator (RHI) scans.

2.0 Results
 The first objective of this project is to develop and evaluate radar-based retrievals of convective core
 vertical velocity (WAIR) and turbulence in terms of Eddy Dissipation Rate (EDR) using adaptive
 observations collected by ROARS. These data sets provide high-spatiotemporal-resolution RHI
 observations of convective storms that are uniquely suited for characterizing thermal-scale updrafts,
 downdrafts, and turbulence. Accurate retrievals of WAIR and EDR provide the foundation for identifying
 thermal-scale convective cores, quantifying entrainment and mixing, and tracking their evolution
 throughout the storm life cycle.

 Figure 2. The (a) retrieved vertical air motion field from the ROARS RHI observations is used to
 estimate the (b) coherent updrafts/downdrafts within the convective cell. A threshold of 30
             range gates with absolute vertical velocities greater than |6| ms-1 is used to identify coherent
 structures.

 The phased array radar (PAR) observations collected by ROARS provide a unique opportunity to
 characterize these processes at unprecedented spatial and temporal resolution. Dense networks of closely
 spaced RHI scans (Figure 3) will provide quasi-simultaneous observations of adjacent storm
 cross-sections, reduce temporal ambiguity and allow coherent updrafts, downdrafts, and turbulent
 structures to be tracked throughout their evolution. Together, these observations will provide the first
 comprehensive characterization of the relationships among thermal-scale vertical velocity cores,
 turbulence, and polarimetric signatures in severe convective storms.
