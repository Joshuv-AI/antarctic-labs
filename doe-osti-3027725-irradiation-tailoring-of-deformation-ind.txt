PROJECT OVERVIEW

The objective of this project is to understand how irradiation enables deformation‐induced
phase transformations in fcc metallic alloys. Low‐temperature deformation of face centered cubic
(fcc) metals and alloys can occur through several modes, one of which is martensitic
transformation, wherein fcc γ‐Fe austenite reverts to hexagonal close packed (hcp) ε‐ martensite
or body centered cubic (bcc) α’‐martensite. Irradiation is believed to enhance the tendency for this
transformation to occur, considering that irradiation similarly enhances other low‐temperature
deformation modes such as dislocation channeling and deformation twinning. However, the
mechanisms underlying the irradiation enhancement of martensitic transformations remain
unknown. Most studies on deformation of irradiated fcc alloys have focused on deformation
twinning rather than on the martensitic transformation, and have tended to simply ascribe any
martensites to the twinning associated with irradiation‐induced dislocation loops. Recently,
however, porosity has been shown to enhance the martensitic transformation in shape memory
alloys through surface energy effects. Thus, the role of both interstitial‐type (e.g. loops) and
vacancy‐type (e.g. cavities) irradiation defects on the phase transformation must be understood.
An altered tendency to phase transform can have severe implications on the performance of load‐
bearing materials exposed to irradiation, such as nuclear reactor structural materials. More
scientifically, using irradiation to tailor the austenite‐to‐martensite phase transformation can
facilitate materials design and innovation across numerous energy generation applications, through
microstructure optimization, thermal conductivity, lightweighting, electromagnetics, biocell
control, and geological exploration. Thus, there is a critical need to understand exactly how
irradiation affects and enables the deformation‐induced martensitic phase transformation.

This critical need will be addressed with an overarching hypothesis that the type (i.e. interstitial or
vacancy), morphology, and distributions of irradiation defects enable precise tuning of the
deformation mechanism. This project resolves the hypothesis through three scientific questions:

 1. How does irradiation enable the deformation mechanism to evolve from dislocation‐
 mediated or twinning, to martensitic transformation?
 2. How do the relative populations of irradiation‐induced vacancy‐ and interstitial‐type
 defects modulate the martensitic transformation pathway?
 3. How does the stacking fault energy (SFE) vary locally at irradiation‐induced defects, and
 how does this variation affect the deformation pathway?

The hypothesis will be tested using a multiscale approach that combines novel small‐scale
mechanical testing and in situ electron microscopy investigations with mechanics models that will
help us interpret and expand the parametric space of our experiments. Work will focus on alloys
irradiated with neutrons and/or charged particles intentionally selected to create tailored
microstructures that are dominated either by vacancy‐type or interstitial‐type defects, or some
combination of these extremes. We will utilize small‐scale mechanical testing techniques to
introduce deformation, which will then be characterized using transmission electron microscopy
(TEM). Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations will provide complementary insight into
deformation mechanisms in the proximity of irradiation-induced defects.

1. UNDERSTANDING DEFORMATION IN LOW-SFE MATERIALS

1.1 Identification of Initial Deformation Mechanisms

We chose to focus on the central region of the Fe-Mn phase diagram, fabricating a set of five Fe-
xMn alloys, where x = 34, 36, 39, 41, 46. All of these alloys exhibit single-phase fcc austenite
microstructures prior to indentation and irradiation. However, the lower Mn concentration alloys
more easily nucleate strain-induced martensite upon indentation (prior to irradiation).

All alloys were fabricated at Ames Laboratory. Ingots of Fe-Mn alloys were prepared by arc-
melting mixtures of the constituent elements (i.e., high purity powders of Fe and Mn). The
resulting buttons were re-melted several times to promote chemical homogeneity and were
subsequently drop-cast into a water-cooled copper mould to form rectangular bars. The as-cast
bars were homogenized at 1200 C for 4 h and then cold rolled to 50% reduction in thickness. The
rolled plates were subsequently annealed at either 800C or 1000C for 1 h, followed by water
quenching to obtain a stable and fully recrystallized microstructure. Two different final annealing
temperatures (800C or 1000C for 1 h) were conducted on each alloy to obtain different grain
sizes, since austenite grain size is closely correlated with stacking fault energy (SFE) and therefore
deformation mechanisms.

The two bounding compositions – Fe-34Mn and Fe-46Mn – were selected for initial microstructure
characterization by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Specimens were mechanically ground
on SiC paper, polished using 1 m diamond paste, and then electrochemically polished in a
solution of 1:9 volume ratio of HClO4:C2H6O at a potential of 20 V at room temperature. In order
to clearly reveal the microstructure, the specimen surfaces were further etched using 4% nital.
Both the Fe-34Mn and Fe-46Mn alloys are single-phase fcc austenite after 1 h annealing at 800C
(Figs. 2a and 2c) and 1000C (Figs. 2b and 2d). The average austenite grain sizes were measured
to be 20.1 m (Fe-34Mn, 800C anneal), 75.3 m (Fe-34Mn, 1000C), 24.6 m (Fe-46Mn, 800C),
and 82.2 m (Fe-46Mn, 1000C).

Nanoindentation was carried out on near-(100) oriented grains on the Fe-34Mn (specifically a (012)
grain, Figure 1), and the Fe-46 Mn (specifically a (001) grain, Figure 2). Nanoindentation was
conducted using a Nanomechanics iMicro nanoindentation system. Indents were made in load-
controlled mode at a constant strain rate of 0.5 s−1 up to a maximum load of 8000 μN. A Berkovich
type indenter with a centerline-to-face angle of 65.3° was employed. Transmission electron
microscopy (TEM) lamellae were created from the cross-section of the nanoindent using focused
ion beam (FIB) milling. TEM analysis of the indented specimen was performed to check for
deformation mechanisms in the plastic region directly under the indent.

The Fe-34Mn exhibited extensive deformation twinning, seen by selected area electron diffraction
(SAED) at the mesoscale, Figure 1(c)-(d). High resolution TEM (HR-TEM) revealed the atomic
structure at deformation twin interfaces, and fast Fourier transformation (FFT) confirmed the
twinning reflections, Figure 1(e). Near the indent (i.e., at a higher strain), deformation twins
intersected with strain-induced -hcp martensite needles only a few atomic layers thick, as

deformation twinning and martensitic transformation in favor of dislocation-mediated deformation.

1.2 Understanding of Irradiation Evolution of Deformation Mechanisms

An embedded-atom method (EAM) potential for Fe-Ni-Cr was used to simulate strain-induced
transformations in Fe-50Ni using molecular dynamics (MD). We selected this system rather than
the Fe-Mn system we are studying in parallel experiments, because of a lack of a reasonable
interatomic potential for Fe-Mn that can meaningfully describe the fcc-bcc-hcp transformations.
However, the Bonny Fe-Ni-Cr potential has been proven to effectively describe both the fcc-bcc
and fcc-hcp strain-induced martensite transformations in simulations having a range of Fe, Ni, and
Cr concentrations. We set up four unique simulations based upon an fcc single crystal at room
temperature in a 35.3×35.3×35.3 nm simulation cell:

1. pristine fcc single crystal – i.e. reference simulation
2. fcc single crystal with randomly distributed vacancies at a concentration of 10 -4 (atomic %)
3. fcc single crystal with 1.26 nm radius (5 atoms) spherical void at center of simulation cell
4. fcc single crystal with 2.02 nm radius (8 atoms) spherical void at center of simulation cell

A periodic boundary condition is applied in all three dimensions. The crystal is loaded uniaxially
(in the z-direction), with strain rate of 0.001 ps-1, until a total strain of 0.15, using time steps of
0.002 ps. The x- and y- directions are allowed to relax during the deformation. The open source
MD code LAMMPS is used to perform the simulations, and open-source software OVITO is used
for visualization.

Simulated stress-strain curves (in the direction of applied load) and the percentage of atoms in the
simulation identified as bcc-coordinated (bcc%) are shown in Figure 3. The slope of the stress-
strain curves drops coincident with a rapid increase in bcc%, due to local stress constraints
generated by the fcc-bcc transformation. Stress-strain and bcc%-strain curves of the reference
simulation and the vacancies simulation are similar, but the curves of the voided simulations are
notably different. Specifically, upon the onset of the transformation, the bcc%-strain curves have
a steeper slope and the stress-strain curves have a shallower slope in the voided simulations; the
extent of these trends increases with void size. This suggest that bcc forms more easily when a
void is present, and even easier with increasing void size.

Strain distributions and crystallographic orientation relationships are shown in Figure 4 for the
reference 1.26 nm void simulations. Phase and shear strain maps of a two-dimensional slice taken
parallel to the loading axis reveal that martensite phases exist wherever the shear strain is larger
than ~0.15, confirming that the fcc-bcc transformation is strain-induced. High shear strain around
the void (Figure 4(e)) can explain the concentration of bcc phases around the void (Figure 4(c,f)).
Both the Kurdjumov-Sachs (KS) and Nishiyama-Wassermann (NW) orientation relationships
(ORs) are present and are consistent across all of the simulation cells (though Figure 4(g) shows
only the 1.26 nm void simulation for clarity of illustration); no intermediate hcp martensite phase
is found. Voids become unstable at high strains (Figure 4(h)), leading to loss of material integrity,
which explains the peaks in the stress-strain and bcc%-strain curves in the voided simulations
(recall Figure 3).

Vacancies have no evident effect on the martensitic transformation. Although other authors have
suggested that vacancies can lower SFE, our MD simulations show that a single vacancy can only

 DE-SC0020150 Final Report – pp. 6

affect SFE when it is laying on the stacking fault plane. Even then, the SFE change is relatively
small. Considering that the vacancy concentration in this study is 10 -4 and that the vacancies are
randomly distributed, the chance of a vacancy laying on a stacking fault plane when the stacking
fault forms, is low. Thus, the effect of vacancies on the strain-induced martensite transformation
is not sufficiently significant to resolve. Resolvable effects on the phase transformation may arise
if the simulated vacancy concentration is increased by a few orders of magnitude. However, such
a vacancy concentration is unrealistic, even in the presence of irradiation.

Voids aid in the martensite transformation. In order for the fcc-to-bcc transformation to occur,
local stress must reach a critical value to induce 1/6{111}<211>FCC partial dislocation generation
and movement. This critical value can be achieved in two ways when a void is present. In the
first possible mechanism, a void can cause stress concentration, increasing the likelihood that local
stresses reach the critical value needed for partial dislocation generation and sliding. The second
possible mechanism is strain accumulation at the void, which means the void serves as an obstacle
for dislocation movement. Dislocation analyses of the simulations are shown in Fig. 6. The
dislocations in the reference simulation are randomly distributed, while there are more dislocations
concentrated around the void when a void is present. These results provide evidence that strain
accumulation at voids (i.e. the second aforementioned mechanism) is the mechanism for strain-
induced martensite. This conclusion agrees well with the preliminary results in our proposal for
this award – in which we observed a martensitic transformation in 304 stainless steel when voids
were present, while martensite was absent in the void-free counterpart.

2. UNDERSTANDING DEFORMATION IN HIGH-SFE MATERIALS

2.1 Identification of Initial Deformation Mechanisms

We extended our MD effort on Fe-50Ni (described in the previous reporting period) to the high-
SFE system Ni-20Cr because the same Fe-Ni-Cr interatomic potential could be used. We applied
uniaxial compression to simulation boxes along three crystallographic orientations: <100>, <110>,
and <111>.

Surprisingly, the simulations predicted that strain can destabilize the fcc crystal by forming
stacking faults that grow into -hcp martensite, Figure 5(a)-(c). With increasing strain, -bcc
martensite nucleated at -martensite intersections, Figure 5(d). The deformation mechanisms were
relatively consistent across all three orientations studied, except that the transformations onset at
different strains in each orientation, based on the respective critical resolved shear stress (CRSS)
on the slip and twinning planes, Figure 5(e). In other words, the transformation in <111> lagged
behind that in <011>, which lagged behind that in <001> crystals.

2.2 Role of Grain Orientation

Following upon our molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, which suggested the possibility of
DIMT in Ni-20Cr, we studied Alloy 625 and Alloy 690, nominally ~Ni-20Cr. Alloy 690 has few
alloying additions, Alloy 625 contains >10 wt% oversized Mo and Nb solutes specifically
selected because of their role in hindering dislocation motion. We investigated the role of grain
orientation on deformation mechanisms.

Bright field TEM of the as-received deformation microstructures, Figure 6(a), consistently
exhibited shear planes and twinning within ~1-2 µm of the apex of the nanoindent. We examined
the highest strain rate regions (to more closely resemble the MD simulation strain rates) by HR-
TEM, Figure 6(b)-(c), which showed evidence of strain-induced martensite. In <101> grains,
stacking faults form and evolve into -martensite as they grow beyond a single atomic layer (blue
and red circles in FFT in Figure 6(b)). In <111> grains, direct fcc-to- transformation occurred,
Figure 6(c). These results were relatively consistent with the MD simulations. However, the direct
fcc-to- transformation in <111> grains was not predicted by MD, which actually suggested that
martensitic transformation in <111> would lag behind the other grain orientations. However, <111>
has low CRSS on the slip and twinning planes; it may be possible that higher stress is experienced
along the fcc- orientation relationship planes, enabling the transformation to proceed.

(SBCs). SAED, Figure 7(c), reveals these SBCs include deformation microtwins, hcp -martensite,

2.4 Understanding Deformation Mechanism Evolution Under Irradiation

We conducted three sets of irradiation on the ~Ni-20Cr Alloys 625 and 690, for a total of four sets
of specimens:

 • Set 0: Unirradiated
 • Set 1: Bubble-dominated microstructure: Series of 200-800 keV He+ ions, 500°C,
 cumulative fluence 6.5 x 1015 ions/cm2 (relatively constant over 500-1500 nm depth)
 • Set 2: Loop-dominated microstructure: 2 MeV protons, 500°C, fluence 1 dpa
 • Set 3: Bubbles and loops together: neutrons, 400°C, fluence 1 dpa

On all specimen sets, we conducted SEM EBSD to identify grain orientations of interest. In the
unirradiated Alloy 625, we studied all three primary grain orientations: {100}, {110}, and {111}.
In all other conditions, we selected only the {110} grain orientation for comparison. We conducted
nanoindentation on the identified grains, used FIB to extract lamella, then conducted TEM
characterization of the deformation or irradiation-deformation microstructures.

Loop+Cavity Microstructure (Neutron Irradiation) – Lobes of extreme plasticity exist but are
much smaller than in the unirradiated cases. Outside the lobes, {101} grains in Alloys 625 and 690
exhibit dislocation tangles and SBCs, Figure 10. HRTEM and diffraction pattern superlattices
indicate hcp martensite with some intersecting stacking faults in both alloys.

Loop Microstructure (Proton) – Both alloys are free of SBCs and DIMT, but intersecting stacking
faults appear outside the shrunken lobes of extreme plasticity, Figure 11. For Alloy 625, few-
atomic-layer-thick stacking faults are present as FFT streaks, although in isolated cases, stacking

3. DISTINGUISHING CONTRASTS WITH STRESS-INDUCED TRANSFORMS

We studied stress-induced (i.e., reversible) martensitic transformations to draw contrasts with the
strain-induced transformations studied earlier in this project. We conducted MD simulations of
Ni-50Ti containing various point defects: (a) pristine, no defects; 10 -4 at% concentration of (b)
vacancies, (c) interstitials, and (d) anti-site defects; and (e) vacancy clusters formed by
equilibration of 10-3 at% concentration of vacancies. We have thus created simulation cells of
pristine and void-containing Ti-50Ni to study the fcc-B19’ transformation. We used a modified
embedded-atom method (MEAM) potential for Ti-Ni, and used identical simulation box
dimensions as presented for Fe-50Ni. In the Ti-50Ni simulation, void radii are 1.5 nm and 2.4 nm,
corresponding to 5 and 8 atom voids, respectively. The crystal is compressed uniaxially at a strain
rate of 0.001 ps-1 until a total strain of 0.15 is reached, using 0.002 ps time steps.

In all cases, the equilibrated initial structure was fully B2, Figure 13(a)-(e). Both the forward and
reverse transformations were simulated. At peak stress, all simulations transformed to B19’. But
defects inhibited the transformation relative to the pristine simulation, with the vacancy clusters
having the most pronounced effect on slowing the transformation, Figure 13(f). Vacancy clusters
increase the level of stress at which the forward transformation onsets, Figure 13(g). Notably,
dislocation analysis shows no evidence of dislocation activity in any of the simulations throughout
the entire loading process (Figure 14), indicating that the transformations are a result of stress
concentration instead of dislocation movement (i.e. strain).

These results are notably different from our low SFE results, which suggest that cavities (which
are not unlike the vacancy clusters here) should enhance the transformability by reducing the
critical transformation stress. However, our high SFE results initially indicate that irradiation
defects (note, these are not exclusively cavity-type defects, but a wide variety of neutron
irradiation-induced defects) may suppress transformability to some degree. We are intrigued by
these contrasting behaviors and hope to ascertain a unifying explanation for the role of defects on
martensitic transformations at varying SFEs and for strain- versus stress-induced modes.

4. MECHANISMS INFERRED FROM HIGH SFE MATERIALS

Several key conclusions are obtained: (1) in Alloy 690, irradiation-induced defects enable DIMT,
while in Alloy 625, irradiation appears to suppress DIMT; and (2) the loop-dominated
microstructure has the lowest extent of transformation in both alloys, compared to the cavity-
dominated and cavity+loop microstructures. We consider several mechanistic explanations.

Free Energy – A free energy approach (Han criterion) states that the likelihood of DIMT is high
when Δ𝐺 + 𝑈 (Gibbs free energy + applied mechanical load) exceeds a critical free energy
threshold. We initially believed that cavities would contribute more toward Gibbs free energy than
loops, due to their internal surface energy. However, upon calculating free energy contributions of
loops and cavities (based on the microstructures measured experimentally), loops contribute
significantly toward the Gibbs free energy, even when cavities are also present, Figure 15(a).
Nevertheless, in all alloys and irradiation conditions, these Gibbs free energy contributions of loop
and cavity defects are not alone sufficient to make DIMT energetically favorable in these alloys.

Irradiation Hardening – Loops and cavities (as well as solutes) create stress localization points
by impeding dislocation glide, resulting in pile-ups and stress localization which manifest as
irradiation hardening. The hardening contributions of the defect types are shown for each alloy
and irradiation condition in Figure 15(b). In both alloys, the loop microstructure introduces
substantially less hardening than the cavity and loop+cavity microstructures; this can explain the
less extensive DIMT in the loop microstructure. While hardening alone may not be a definitive
predictor of changes in DIMT behavior, greater irradiation hardening is indicative of more
numerous and/or stronger obstacles to dislocation motion, which necessarily creates more stress
localization sites from which DIMT may initiate.

Obstacle-Driven Mechanism for DIMT – Our results from this project are thus far leading us to
believe that DIMT is governed less by SFE or irradiation defect types (e.g., loop-type vs. cavity-
type), but moreso by the phenomenological model depicted in Figure 16. In this model, the mean
obstacle spacing (obstacles to dislocation motion can be solute atoms or extended irradiation
defects) predicts the critical transformation stress. When there is a lack of obstacles to create stress
localization sites, such as in the unirradiated Alloy 690, higher loads are required to reach the
critical transformation stress than in a material with pre-existing obstacles that create stress
localization, such as the unirradiated Alloy 625 given its Mo and Nb solutes that pin dislocations.
Irradiation, then, can shift the obstacle spacing such that transformations may become either more
or less favorable.

There is a “sweet spot” in which the mean obstacle spacing is sufficiently low that transformations
are energetically practical, yet not small enough to harden the material such that plasticity is
practically prevented. In low SFE materials, this “sweet spot” spans a larger range of obstacle
spacings than in high SFE materials, Figure 16(c). Perhaps, then, for the case of NiTi and other
materials that undergo stress-induced (rather than strain-induced) transformations, this “sweet spot”
may span a larger range of stresses than in materials that undergo strain-induced transformations,
but irradiation can reduce that range of stresses, Figure 16(d).

SOCIETAL IMPACTS

Research accomplishments enabled by this project have shed light on the fundamental nano/micro-
scale mechanisms that enable irradiation defects to activate deformation-induced martensitic
transformations and other novel deformation mechanisms. Scientifically, this knowledge can now
allow researchers to pioneer new directions in irradiation-enabled materials design, i.e., creating
irradiated microstructures that enable precise tuning or tailoring of mechanical properties. Such
irradiation-tailored materials will become evermore critical amidst the growing need to deploy
nuclear fission and fusion energy to support artificial intelligence (AI), advanced computing, and
the energy-water-food-technology nexus. Moreover, results herein are directly relevant to
advanced alloys as well as ubiquitously-used materials such as steels and Ni-based alloys. The
advancements herein provide guidance to enhance the strength, ductility, and mechanical integrity
of such materials, thus having broad societal relevance to extending the safe service lifetime and
efficiency of advanced energy structures.
