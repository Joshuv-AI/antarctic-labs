Executive Summary
This final technical report summarizes the work on the award DE-FG02-07ER41460, Transverse Spin and
Momentum Structure of Hadrons in QCD.
 During the performance period, we addressed fundamental questions in nuclear physics, focusing on
how hadrons—and, by extension, light nuclei—emerge from quark and gluon interactions within Quantum
Chromodynamics (QCD). QCD predicts that the internal structure of hadrons can be probed through deep-
inelastic scattering (DIS) experiments, which reveal hadrons as dynamic systems governed by the running of
the strong coupling constant: at short distances, the coupling weakens due to asymptotic freedom, while at long
distances it strengthens, leading to confinement. Consequently, at the high energies accessed in DIS, quarks
and gluons interact weakly and can be treated perturbatively, whereas at larger distance scales, non-perturbative
effects dominate.
 These properties of the QCD coupling form the basis of QCD factorization theorems, which separates
where the short-distance, process-dependent perturbative contributions from long-distance are separated from
the long distance universal non-perturbative components. The latter are encoded in Quantum Correlation
Functions (QCFs), a broad class of parton distribution functions that capture the dynamics of quarks and gluons
and their intrinsic correlations of spin and momentum with that of their parent hadrons.
 Within this framework, our work has focused on uncovering the quantum correlations between hadron
polarization and parton motion and spin, providing new insights into the transverse momentum and spin structure
of hadrons—phenomena central to transverse momentum–dependent parton distribution functions (TMDs).
Organized in slices of longitudinal momentum (1D) coupled with two dimensions of transverse momentum (2D),
TMDs yield tomographic images of hadron structure. Building on this foundation, we pioneered simultaneous
extractions of collinear and transverse-momentum–dependent distributions, combining theoretical developments
with global QCD analyses to achieve unprecedented precision in mapping the three-dimensional structure
of hadrons. In particular, our studies have established rigorous connections between quark- and gluon-level
dynamics at sub-femtometer scales and the hadronic observables measured in deep-inelastic scattering and
Drell–Yan experiments. These advances have placed our group at the forefront of efforts to deliver the first
truly comprehensive tomographic images of hadron structure through a well-defined set of TMD distribution
and fragmentation functions.
 Our studies have established rigorous connections between quark- and gluon-level dynamics at sub-
femtometer scales and the hadronic observables measured in deep-inelastic scattering and Drell–Yan experiments.
These advances have positioned our group at the forefront of efforts to deliver the first truly comprehensive
tomographic images of hadron structure through a well-defined set of TMD distribution and fragmentation
functions.
 Uncovering the partonic 3-D landscape of hadrons is a major goal of high-energy nuclear physics facilities,
including the Relativistic Heavy Ion Collider (RHIC) at BNL, the Continuous Electron Beam Accelerator
Facility (CEBAF) at JLab, and the future Electron-Ion Collider (EIC). Our research is a high priority in the near
and long-term as described in ”A New Era of Discovery: The 2023 Long Range Plan for Nuclear Science”
as well as in the National Academy’s, ”An Assessment of U.S.-Based Electron-Ion Collider Science”, and
the recently published, ”Science Requirements and Detector Concepts for the Electron-Ion Collider: EIC
Yellow Report”, as it relates to present and future studies on 3-D momentum and spin structure of hadrons:
To explore the internal landscape of hadrons, we will provide multidimensional momentum imaging through
spin-momentum and spin-spin correlations, unveiling their fundamental structure and origins.

1 Introduction
 During the performance period of the award, we had considerable success carrying out and disseminated our
work. This was achieved through publications, scientific talks, organizing conferences, supporting undergraduate
and post graduate researchers, providing service to the U.S. Nuclear Physics community. A summary of
accomplishments and activities have been listed in progress reports during the entire performance period. A list
of major publications is available on the OSTI an ORCID website and all approved progress reports are stored
on the Portfolio Analysis And Management System (PAMs) website.
 Given that hadrons make up the dominant content of visible matter in the universe, a central question
in nuclear physics is understanding how hadrons, and by extension light atomic nuclei, emerge from the
interactions of their constituent quarks and gluons. Quantum Chromodynamics (QCD) predicts that hadrons are
dynamic systems governed by the running of the QCD coupling, which exhibits asymptotic freedom at short
time and distance scales, and strong coupling—and ultimately confinement—at long distances. Thus, when
probed at high energies in deep inelastic scattering experiments, the constituents of hadrons become weakly
coupled. This behavior of asymptotic freedom of QCD interactions enables the use of the theoretical formalism
of QCD factorization to quantify the partonic structure and dynamics of hadrons. QCD factorization theorems
predict that measured scattering cross sections can be decomposed into process-dependent perturbative factors
and universal non-perturbative quantum field theoretic parton correlation functions or Quantum Correlation
Functions. In turn, this allows us to link quarks and gluons at short time and distance scales to hadrons measured
in inelastic scattering experiments.
 While decades of high-energy experiments have provided data for high-resolution mapping of the one-
dimensional (1-D) longitudinal momentum structure of hadrons from collinear PDFs, our understanding of
their transverse momentum structure remains comparatively limited. Achieving a complete three-dimensional
(3-D) map of hadron structure, encompassing light-cone, transverse momentum, and spatial degrees of freedom,
requires sensitivity to both collinear and transverse partonic components. In this context, the Quantum
Correlation Functions (QCFs) of particular relevance are the generalized parton distributions (GPDs) and
transverse momentum distributions (TMDs), which uniquely represent different aspects of the 3-D partonic
structure of hadrons.
 Our research focuses on the quantum correlations between hadron polarization and momentum, as well as
parton transverse momentum and spin, providing insights into the transverse momentum and spin structure of
hadrons—key areas in TMD physics. The objectives of our work are to reveal and quantify the 3-D longitudinal
and transverse momentum, along with the conjugate transverse spatial degrees of freedom, of hadrons in terms
of their partonic substructure—core aspects of TMDs.

This report presents an overview of research highlights from the performance period, with an emphasis on

 hadronic collisions. We point out the Sivers functions in such processes are different from those probed in
 the SIDIS process because of different initial- and final-state interactions. By carefully taking into account
 the process-dependence in the Sivers functions, we derive a new formalism within the framework of the
 generalized parton model (GPM) approach [23–25]. We find this formalism has close connections with the
 collinear twist-3 approach. With our modified GPM formalism, we make predictions for the inclusive 𝜋0
 and direct photon production in pp collisions at RHIC energies. We show that the asymmetries predicted
 from the modified GPM formalism are opposite to those in the conventional GPM approach. This sign
 difference comes from the color gauge interaction, which has the same origin as the sign change for Sivers
 functions between SIDIS and DY processes. Our predictions about the sign are consistent with those
 from the twist-3 collinear factorization approach. We encouraged a global analysis of both SIDIS and pp
 experimental data using this modified GPM formalism.

 proton– proton scattering. We include the contributions from both the direct and fragmentation photons.
 While the asymmetry for direct photon production receives only the Sivers type of contribution, the
 asymmetry for fragmentation photons receives both the Sivers and Collins types of contributions. We
 make a model calculation for quark-to-photon Collins function, which is then used to estimate the Collins
 asymmetry for fragmentation photons. We find that the Collins asymmetry for fragmentation photons is

• In Ref. [26] we introduced the concept of Bessel weighted asymmetries for SIDIS by considering the cross
  section in Fourier space, conjugate to the outgoing hadron’s transverse momentum, where convolutions of
  transverse momentum dependent parton distribution functions and fragmentation functions become simple
  products. Individual asymmetric terms in the cross section can be projected out by means of a generalized set
  of weights involving Bessel functions. Advantages of employing these Bessel weights are that they suppress
  (divergent) contributions from high transverse momentum and that soft factors cancel in (Bessel-)weighted
  asymmetries. Also, the resulting compact expressions immediately connect to previous work on evolution
  equations for transverse momentum dependent parton distribution and fragmentation functions [27, 28]
  and to quantities accessible in lattice QCD [29]. Bessel-weighted asymmetries are thus model independent
  observables that augment the description and our understanding of correlations of spin and momentum in
  nucleon structure.
• We followed the above work on Bessel Weighting with ”Studies of Transverse Momentum Dependent
  Parton Distributions and Bessel Weighting” [30], where we present employ our new technique of Bessel
  weighting [26] to study TMD PDFs based on Bessel weighting. The procedure is applied to studies of the
  double longitudinal spin asymmetry in SIDIS using a new dedicated Monte Carlo generator which includes
  quark intrinsic transverse momentum within the generalized parton model. Using a fully differential cross
  section for the process, the effect of four momentum conservation is analyzed using various input models
  for transverse momentum distributions and fragmentation functions. We observe a few percent systematic
  off-set of the Bessel-weighted asymmetry obtained from Monte Carlo extraction compared to input model
  calculations, which is due to the limitations imposed by the energy and momentum conservation at the given
  energy/Q2. We find that the Bessel weighting technique provides a powerful and reliable tool to study the
  Fourier transform of TMDs with controlled systematics due to experimental acceptances and resolutions with
  different TMD model inputs.

  transverse momentum, especially in applications that require higher precision than a leading power parton
  model can provide.

2.1 Mapping 3-D Hadron Structure TMD and Collinear Factorization
 Mapping the 3D structure of hadrons requires observables sensitive to correlatioins of transverse spin &
momentum, which probe intrinsic parton motion and quark–gluon–quark correlations. Experimentally, such
information comes from single-spin asymmetries (𝐴 𝑁 ) and Collins effects in proton–proton collisions, Sivers
and Collins asymmetries in SIDIS, the Collins effect in 𝑒 + 𝑒 − annihilation, and the Sivers effect in Drell–Yan
and weak boson production.
 Theoretically, two complementary frameworks describe these data. For two-scale processes (Λ𝑄𝐶𝐷 ∼
𝑞 𝑇 ≪ 𝑄), TMD factorization expresses cross sections in terms of perturbative hard parts and universal
TMDs [27, 34–36, 44–49]. For single-scale processes (𝑄 ≫ Λ𝑄𝐶𝐷 ), collinear twist-3 (CT3) factorization
encodes quark–gluon–quark correlations in higher-twist PDFs and FFs [50–61]. These frameworks are
consistent in their overlap region (Λ𝑄𝐶 𝐷 ≪ 𝑞 𝑇 ≪ 𝑄) [62–67].
 In our earlier work, Ref. [4] (JAM3D-20) we demonstrated for the first time that one can perform a
simultaneous QCD global analysis of SSAs in SIDIS (Sivers and Collins effects), SIA (Collins effect), DY
(Sivers effect), and proton-proton collisions (𝐴 𝑁 for pion production), extracting a universal set of non-
perturbative functions and achieving agreement with lattice QCD on tensor charges. This provided strong
evidence that all SSAs share a common dynamical origin.

4 Research Impact
 The Nuclear Theory Group at Penn State Berks directly supports near-term and long-range nuclear physics
priorities outlined in An Assessment of U.S.-Based Electron-Ion Collider Science [43], the EIC Yellow
Report [119], and A New Era of Discovery: The 2023 Long Range Plan for Nuclear Science. Our work advances
studies of the three-dimensional momentum and spin structure of hadrons, providing multidimensional imaging
and insight into the emergence of hadronic matter. It also aligns with the APS Division of Nuclear Physics
recommendation to expand undergraduate research opportunities [42].
 As an undergraduate college within Penn State University, we emphasize undergraduate student involvement
in research, providing training, co-authorship on refereed publications, and opportunities to present at national
workshops and conferences. The group has also successfully trained postdoctoral researchers, including Dr. Eric
Moffat (5/2021–5/2022; continuing at Argonne National Laboratory) and Dr. Daniel Pitonyak (9/2016–9/2018;
now faculty at LVC).
 The Nuclear Theory Group at Penn State Berks benefits from a close collaboration with Dr. Alexey Prokudin,
NSF RUI Grant recipient, hired in 2015 through a tenure-track bridge position with Jefferson Lab (JLAB). We
also leverage the expertise of former Postdoctoral Researcher Dr. Daniel Pitonyak, now Associate Professor of
Physics at Lebanon Valley College (LVC) and an NSF RUI recipient. These collaborations have significantly
strengthened the Nuclear Theory program at Penn State Berks.
 With the DOE’s selection of Brookhaven National Laboratory as the site for the Electron-Ion Collider (EIC),
our research provides essential theoretical support for this highest-priority national initiative in next-generation
nuclear physics.
