Optical
and Photochemical Properties of Suwannee River
Natural Organic Matter Fractions Generated by Preparative Free Flow
Electrophoresis

Garrett McKay, Kai Cheng, András Gáspár, Mourad Harir, Philippe Schmitt-Kopplin, Norbert Hertkorn, Ronald Benner, Karl Kaiser

Abstract

Fractionation of
dissolved organic matter (DOM) has been used for
decades to simplify the challenging task of bulk sample characterization
and to gain insight into the structural features governing DOM reactivity.
Here, we used preparative free-flow electrophoresis (PFFE), which
separates molecules based on their charge-to-size ratio, to separate
Suwannee River natural organic matter (SRNOM) into fractions with
varying electrophoretic mobility. Optical and photochemical data of
the fractions were paired with extensive analytical characterization.
The least electrophoretically mobile fraction had the lowest number-average
molecular weight (M
n), aromaticity, and
specific ultraviolet absorbance (SUVA254), while showing
the largest quantum yields of fluorescence as well as singlet oxygen
and hydroxyl radical formation. Structure–activity relationships
for the fractions, which exhibited substantial differences in mobility,
aromaticity, and M
n did not follow the
conventional trends observed in size-based DOM fractionation studies
(e.g., SUVA254 ∝ aromaticity). These findings highlight
the role of charge and structural differences in driving optical and
photochemical properties apart from bulk aromaticity and M
n. The distribution of absorbance, the sum of vanillyl
and syringyl phenols, and DIOHBA (3,5-dihydroxybenzoic acid) indicates
that the most electrophoretically mobile fractions with the highest
absorbance are dominated by DIOHBA, a phenol monomer derived from
tannins and flavonoids, rather than lignin. DIOHBA was inversely related
to the fluorescence index and β/α. Overall, these results
provide insight into SRNOM photophysics and highlight the utility
of PFFE as a tool for resolving DOM structure–reactivity relationships.

Introduction

Dissolved organic matter (DOM) governs
the mechanisms and rates
of key processes in aquatic systems, such as elemental cycles,
−

 attenuation of contaminants,
−

 and productivity of the Earth’s
oceans.
,
 Many of these processes are initiated by
the absorption of sunlight by chromophoric DOM, which comprises up
to ∼50% of the total DOM pool in terrestrial environments. DOM is an exceedingly complex mixture of molecules,
rich in oxygenated functional groups, and sp2 hybridized
carbon with varying molecular size.
−

 DOM molecular size and composition
are altered during environmental processes, such as adsorption, oxidation,
−

 and coagulation.

DOM structure impacts its reactivity in
natural and engineered
systems. One approach to resolving these structure–reactivity
relationships is to fractionate DOM by size or polarity. It has been extensively demonstrated that molecular
size is an important determinant of DOM optical properties
,
 and photoreactivity
,
 using size-based methods such
as gel permeation chromatography,
,
 field-flow
fractionation,
,
 and ultrafiltration.
,,
 Although molecular weight depends
only on the molecular structure, molecular size is a complex function
of structure and environmental conditions such as pH and ionic strength.
,
 For example, ultrafiltration separates molecules based on size (weight
is calibrated with globular proteins), but the resulting fractions
are nonetheless often described as high- and low-molecular-weight
fractions. It has been shown that lower molecular weight DOM generated
from size-based separations has lower specific absorbance,
,
 larger spectral slope, higher fluorescence
quantum yields (Φ
f
), blue-shifted
emission, and higher quantum yields for the photoproduction of reactive
intermediates.
,,
 These lower molecular weight fractions also have a lower proportion
of sp2-hybridized carbon than higher molecular weight fractions.
Evidence for this comes both from nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR),
which demonstrates a higher abundance of sp2 carbon, and
Fourier transform ion cyclotron resonance mass spectrometry, which
shows lower H/C ratios, higher double bond equivalence, and more formulas
with an aromaticity index >0.5 for high relative to low molecular
weight fractions.
,
 Several recent efforts have also
focused on resolving DOM optical spectra via polarity-based separations
with commercial solid-phase extraction cartridges.
−

In contrast
to separation by molecular size and polarity, DOM separation
by charge density is less common. Capillary electrophoresis with optical
or mass spectrometric detection demonstrated that electropherograms
could act as a DOM fingerprint because the electrophoretic mobility
is directly related to the charge and hydrodynamic friction of molecules
in complex organic mixtures. In addition,
Trubetskoj and coworkers investigated the optical and photochemical
properties of DOM fractions of different electrophoretic mobility,
which were generated by size exclusion chromatography (SEC) in the
presence of 7 M urea.
,,
 The predominant acidic functional groups in DOM are carboxylic acids
and phenols, and the protonation state
of these functional groups has been shown to alter DOM optical properties
and photoreactivity.
,
 Although there are some reports
employing absorbance and fluorescence detectors during capillary electrophoresis, complete spectral and photochemical characterization
of DOM separated by charge-based methods is largely absent in the
literature.

Here, we report the optical and photochemical properties
of Suwannee
River natural organic matter (SRNOM) fractions generated by preparative
free-flow electrophoresis (PFFE) in a previous study. We characterized PFFE fractions and bulk SRNOM by optical
measurements (absorbance and fluorescence), spectral pH titrations,
fluorescence quenching, and measurements of apparent quantum yields
for the photochemical production of singlet oxygen (1O2, ΦΔ) and hydroxyl radical (•OH, ΦOH). Correlations were examined between photochemical
parameters and a wealth of compositional data, including NMR-derived
functional group composition, molecular weight, amino acid content,
and phenol monomers produced after copper oxide oxidation. A primary
goal of this study is to assess the utility of PFFE as a DOM fractionation
technique with respect to the molecular distinctness of its fractions.
In addition, we expect that these data will provide new insight into
the structural and compositional underpinnings of SRNOM optical and
photochemical properties.

Materials and Methods

Preparative
Free-Flow Electrophoresis

PFFE of SRNOM
(1R101N) was performed in Munich using an Octopus PZE device manufactured
by Dr. Weber GmbH (Kirchheim, Germany), as described in earlier studies.
−

 The device has a vertical separation chamber with dimensions of
500 mm (length) × 100 mm (width) × 0.5 mm (thickness), resulting
in a chamber volume of 25 mL. The separation was performed at a temperature
of 15 °C and a separation voltage of 500 V. The separation medium
consisted of 5 mM ammonium carbonate (pH 9.2), while the electrode
buffer consisted of 500 mM ammonium carbonate buffer at the same pH.
The sample (20 mg mL–1 dry weight) was continuously
pumped (2 mL h–1) into the bottom of the chamber
and carried by the separation medium (965 mL h–1) in a laminar flow profile. At the top of the chamber, the separation
medium and sample were collected in a tray containing 96 wells.

Solutions from wells were binned into 11 fractions, and fractions
were lyophilized to obtain solid for further analysis and to remove
ammonium carbonate. The appearance of a dry, fluffy powder after lyophilization
is strong evidence of the removal of ammonium carbonate. Although
11 fractions were collected during PFFE, only five were analyzed in
detail here, as they are the main contributors to the absorbance detected
in the electrophoretogram.

Analytical Methods

The SRNOM fractions
and their analytical
characterization were originally described by Gáspár, which included NMR and amino acid analyses.
A brief description of all analytical methods is provided here (see
complete details in the Supporting Information). Optical measurements, SEC, and photochemical characterization
were performed in 2020 and are original to this work.

Optical
measurements, SEC, and photochemical characterization were completed
at Texas A&M. Briefly, absorbance and fluorescence spectra were
collected with an Aqualog (Horiba). Standard protocols for data treatment
were implemented, such as blank subtraction, inner-filter corrections,
Raman normalization, and Rayleigh masking.
,
 Optical surrogates were calculated according to Korak and McKay and included the specific ultraviolet absorbance
at 254 nm (SUVA254), E2:E3 ratio, spectral slope between
275 and 290 (S
275–290) and 350–400
(S
350–400), the fluorescence index
(FI), and the freshness index (β/α). SEC was completed
with an Ultimate 3000 UHPLC (Thermo Fisher) and Waters Protein-Pak
(15 × 300 mm) column, with a mobile phase consisting of 0.1 M
potassium chloride, 2 mM potassium dihydrogen phosphate, and 2 mM
potassium hydrogen phosphate at pH 6.8. The flow rate was 1.0 mL min–1. Retention time was calibrated to molecular weight
using polystyrenesulfonates (PSS) ranging from 246 to 18 000 Da. SEC
is one of the most common techniques used in the DOM community for
obtaining apparent molecular weights.
,,
 Although PSS do not perfectly represent DOM size,
number-average molecular weights (M
n)
measured by SEC with PSS calibration agree well with values from vapor
pressure osmometry.
,
 Total organic carbon was measured
with a Shimadzu V-SCH organic carbon analyzer on 0.45 μm-filtered
samples using the non-purgeable organic carbon method.

NMR analysis
was performed in Munich, Germany, using a Bruker DMX
spectrometer (B
0 = 11.7 T). We focus here
on 13C data, which were collected for PFFE fractions (5–35
mg) redissolved in up to 135 μL CD3OD (99.96% 3H, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) at 283 K. Shift regions were
integrated to obtain quantitative estimates of functional group distributions
(see Text S1).

Amino acid analysis
was performed in Columbia, South Carolina,
while copper oxide oxidation and phenol monomer analysis were performed
in Galveston, TX. Briefly, amino acid content was measured on hydrolyzed
samples (6 M HCl, 20 h, 110 °C) by HPLC after in-line derivatization
with o-phthaldialdehyde/N-isobutyryl-l-cysteine or o-phthaldialdehyde/N-isobutyryl-d-cysteine. Quantification
of phenol monomers after copper oxide oxidation was performed by GC/MS
following cleanup with solid-phase extraction (Oasis HLB cartridges). p-Hydroxyphenyl (P), vanillyl (V), syringyl (S), and cinnamyl
(C) phenols were quantified, including p-hydroxybenzaldehyde
(PAL), p-hydroxyacetophenone (PON), p-hydroxybenzoic acid (PAD), vanillin (VAL), acetovanillone (VON),
vanillic acid (VAD), syringaldehyde (SAL), acetosyringone (SON), syringic
acid (SAD), p-coumaric acid (CAD), and ferulic acid
(FAD). The sum of V and S phenols (Σ6 = VAL + VON
+ VAD + SAL + SON + SAD) is a strong indicator of lignin content.
3,5-Dihydroxybenzoic acid (DIOHBA) was also measured.

Solution Preparation

Solutions for SEC, optical, and
photochemical analyses were prepared using ultrapure water (>18.0
MΩ cm) from a Barnstead water system (Thermo Fisher). PFFE fractions
and bulk SRNOM were dissolved in ultrapure water. Sample pH was adjusted
to 7.0 with 0.1 M NaOH, and solutions were stirred overnight and filtered
with pre-rinsed 0.45 μm polyether sulfone syringe filters. These
stock solutions were stored at 4 °C until dilution for subsequent
experiments. Dilutions were prepared in either 5 mM potassium phosphate
buffer at pH 7.2 (for optical and photochemical measurements) or,
for fluorescence quenching (see Text S1), in acetic acid (pH 4) or sodium carbonate (pH 9).

Photochemical
Measurements

A more detailed description
of the probe compound methods is provided in Text S2. A Rayonet merry-go-round photoreactor with 12 365 nm bulbs,
equipped with a fan, was employed for all irradiations, which were
conducted in borosilicate glass tubes (effective path length of 0.84
cm). Briefly, 25 μM furfuryl alcohol and 2 mM benzoate were
used for quantifying the formation rates of 1O2 and •OH, respectively. The rate of •OH formation was derived from the rate of salicylate formation using
the yield of 0.204 mol salicylate per mol •OH. Apparent quantum yields were calculated using
the measured 1O2 or •OH formation
rate and the rate of light absorption, the latter calculated using
the photon irradiance (measured with p-nitroanisole/pyridine), solution absorbance, and path length in the
quartz tube. Formation of DOM triplet states was not measured in this
study (e.g., using trimethylphenol) as 1O2 is
considered an indicator of triplet reactivity.

Although commonly used within the aquatic photochemistry
literature, the 365 nm light source in our study likely results in
higher ΦΔ values compared to a solar simulator
due to ΦΔ’s wavelength dependence. However, our objective was to investigate fundamental
structural features governing SRNOM optical properties and photochemistry,
not to accurately represent environmental polychromatic conditions.

Statistical Methods

Correlation analysis was performed
using the Statistics and Machine Learning Toolbox in MATLAB 2024b.
Pearson correlation coefficients and their associated P values were calculated. Correlations with a P-value
of 0.01 (indicated with an *) were classified as significant due to
the small number of points in each correlation (n = 6). Correlations with 0.01 ≤ P < 0.05
are considered suggestive trends, and the ρP
 values are still shown.

Results

Preparative
Free-Flow Electrophoresis Produces SRNOM Fractions
with Varying Optical and Photochemical Properties

PFFE separates
molecules based on size and charge, with a molecule’s effective
electrophoretic mobility being proportional to its charge-to-size
ratio. Higher charge density translates into a higher velocity of
the ions in solution in the electric field and thus effective electrophoretic
mobility (field-strength-normalized ion velocity). The electropherogram demonstrates that the majority of
SRNOM molecules have a narrow range of electrophoretic mobilities,
between −0.03 and −0.005 cm2
V
min
–1 (). Although only five fractions are discussed
below (Fr6–Fr10), the mass of organic carbon (OC) in all 11
fractions (Fr1–Fr11) was measured to assess the mass balance
(Table S2). Approximately 80% of the total
SRNOM mass was recovered, while slightly less of the OC was recovered
(73%), which could be explained by the incomplete removal of ammonium
carbonate. Relative to the mass quantified in the 11 fractions, fractions
6 through 10 comprise more than 95% of the SRNOM and OC mass as well
as most of the absorbance at 254 nm in the electrophoretogram ( and S1). The unrecovered mass may include high-mobility
molecules such as small, aliphatic carboxylic acids. Although we did
not examine the organic carbon concentration in each tube after PFFE,
an early report by Leenheer and Malcolm shows a substantial organic carbon peak that is weakly UV-absorbing
with high electrophoretic mobility. Given the probable chemical nature
of the missing mass, we do not expect that its absence will impact
the optical and photochemical properties observed here.

Fluorescence Quenching
and Spectral pH Titration Reveal Charge-Density
Differences of Chromophores in PFFE Fractions

PFFE does not
discriminate between chromophoric and nonchromophoric molecules. Thus,
one would expect that the chromophores present in Fr6 would have greater
charge-to-size ratios than those of later fractions. Fr6 also has
the largest measured molecular weight (A); thus, it should have a far greater number
of charged sites than other fractions to have the largest charge density
and electrophoretic mobility.

pH titrations and fluorescence
quenching were used to evaluate the charge density of chromophores
in the PFFE fractions. Fluorescence quenching with tempamine (TA)
and tempol (TO) provides an assessment of the surface charge of fluorophores.
,
 The ratio of Stern–Volmer constants for each quencher (K
TA/K
TO), defined
as λ, is related to the apparent attractive electrostatic potential
(Ψ′) via λ = exp­(−Ψ′/k
B
T), where k
B is the Boltzmann constant and T is
the temperature. Another way of conceptualizing λ is as the
ratio of local to bulk quencher, which is unity for neutral tempol
and >1 for cationic tempamine. The measured value of λ is
lower
than the actual value due to dynamic quenching processes. Nonetheless,
λ is a useful measure for assessing Ψ′′.

λ values for all PFFE
fractions are smaller at pH 4 than
at pH 9 (),
consistent with the larger expected charge density at pH 9 due to
a greater fraction of deprotonated carboxylic acids and phenols. λ at pH 4 and 9 decrease with decreasing
electrophoretic mobility, consistent with Fr6 having the highest charge-to-size
ratio and therefore the largest attractive electrostatic potential.
The difference in λ between pH 4 and 9 for each fraction is
largest for Fr6 and bulk SRNOM, whereas λ are more similar at
each pH for Fr8 through Fr10. Overall, the quenching behavior of SRNOM
fractions corroborates the effective separation of fluorophores by
PFFE into molecules with varying charge density.

Spectral pH titrations provide an additional assessment of the
chromophore charge density. The response of absorbance to an increase
in pH has been linked to the roles of carboxyl- and phenol-containing
chromophores.
,,
 The ratio of absorbance at 450 nm as a function of pH normalized
to pH 2 (A
pHX/A
pH2) increases more rapidly with increasing pH for Fr6 relative to Fr9
across the pH 2 to 6 (carboxyl) and pH 8 to 12 (phenol) ranges (B). Thus, the pH-dependent
absorbance behavior of PFFE fractions further corroborates the effective
separation of chromophores within SRNOM.

Discussion

PFFE Produces
Fractions with Distinct Structures but Similar
Optical and Photochemical Properties

A primary finding of
this research is that the molecular structure, composition, and photoactivity
of PFFE fractions appear to differ from size-based fractionation techniques
such as ultrafiltration. Studies employing ultrafiltration typically
show increasing E2:E3 with decreasing molecular weight, as defined
by the nominal molecular weight cutoffs of the ultrafilters. For example,
Maizel and Remucal demonstrate that ultrafiltration permeates of Pony
Lake and Suwannee River fulvic acid have larger E2:E3 values than
both the bulk sample and retentate, and show a concomitant increase
in the abundance of formulas with an aromaticity index >0.5. Likewise, Mostafa et al. show that ultrafiltration
permeates of SRNOM and a wastewater derived DOM have longer SEC retention
times and larger E2:E3 values than their respective bulk and retentate
fractions. In contrast, PFFE fractions
of SRNOM show minimal change in E2:E3 from Fr6 through Fr9, which
vary in molecular weight by nearly two-fold and aromaticity by 1.5-fold.
There is no consistent trend in SUVA254 from Fr6–Fr9;
however, Fr10 has the lowest M
n, aromaticity,
and highest E2:E3 of all fractions. In general, absorbance-based optical
surrogates that characterize spectral slope do not vary considerably
until Fr10. Surprisingly, Φ
f
 displays
a pattern similar to E2:E3. This contrasts with FI and β/α which generally increase with decreasing
electrophoretic mobility and exhibit tight correlations to aromaticity
and M
n.

Trubetskoj and coworkers
investigated the optical properties and photoreactivity of DOM fractions
with varying electrophoretic mobility,
,,
 and some of their observations align with those from
PFFE. In Trubetskoj’s studies, DOM was separated by low-pressure
SEC in the presence of 7 M urea and the electrophoretic mobility of
the fractions was measured post hoc with gel electrophoresis. They observed that the most electrophoretically
mobile fractions (C + D) had higher SUVA280 than the least
mobile fractions (A and B).
,
 They also showed that
the more mobile fractions had similar E2:E3, similar to Fr6–Fr9 in our study. One discrepancy is that,
for PFFE separation, fractions with higher electrophoretic mobility
also have the largest molecular size, whereas Trubetskoj et al. found
that the most mobile fraction had the smallest molecular size. In addition, Trubetskoj et al. observed that
the most electrophoretically mobile fraction had higher photosensitizing
efficiency,
,
 which is opposite to the result
seen here that Fr10 has the largest ΦΔ and
ΦOH values. Although speculative, these discrepancies
could be due to disruption of intermolecular interactions (e.g., H-bonding)
by 7 M urea in the most electrophoretically mobile fractions that
have a high abundance of H-bond donors and acceptors. Such disruptions could decrease the apparent size of the
DOM, as measured by SEC, leading to higher photosensitization efficiency
of the high-mobility fraction. Trubetskoj
et al. dialyzed samples prior to photochemical measurements; thus,
any disaggregation resulting from 7 M urea would need to be preserved
post-dialysis for this explanation to be valid. In addition, because
Trubetskoj and coworkers characterized electrophoretic mobility after
SEC, separation by charge density (i.e., pulling apart molecules with
similar size but different charge) would not have occurred during
SEC. Clearly, more work is needed to evaluate these hypotheses. Regardless,
the agreement between our study and Trubetskoj et al. that high-mobility
fractions may have similar absorbance-based properties further supports
the differences between charge-based separation and ultrafiltration.

We propose two possible explanations for the lack of systematic
relationships between aromaticity, M
n,
and absorbance-based surrogates among PFFE fractions. First, aromaticity
and M
n are coarse descriptors that do
not fully capture the nuances of DOM structure. Separation by charge-density
results in high-mobility fractions having a greater abundance of ionizable
functional groups (Table S3). Spectra of
fractions with hhigher charge density will be impacted by these additional
functional groups in ways not easily captured with bulk measures of
aromaticity and molecular weight. For example, phenol and acetovanillone
have the same number of aromatic carbon atoms but quite different
absorbance and fluorescence spectra due to the electronic nature of
their substituents. The more mobile fractions
exhibit red-shifted spectra relative to lower-mobility fractions (Table S4, Figure S6). This observation is consistent
with the positive correlation between fluorescence asymmetry (a measure

that this relationship is driven by the proportion of oxidized aromatic
compounds with low pK

most likely to be carboxylic acids. These same structures govern separation
of DOM molecules by PFFE and are more abundant in the high-mobility
fractions (Table S3).

A second explanation
is related to the PFFE separation mechanism.
Ultrafiltration separates DOM primarily on the basis of size, whereas
PFFE separates DOM on the basis of size and charge. Thus, molecules
with the same molecular weight but different charge densities will
be separated in PFFE but not by ultrafiltration. This explains why
Fr6 through Fr8 can have varying charge densities but similar M
n. The additional separation dimension helps
to resolve compositional heterogeneity among molecules with overlapping
molecular weights.

In contrast to absorbance-based surrogates,
which do not vary systematically
with electrophoretic mobility and exhibit weaker correlations to aromaticity
and M

electrophoretic mobility and have tight correlations to M

these fluorescence-based surrogates to M

optical surrogates as well as continued inquiry using ultrafiltered

The Importance
of DIOHBA

Another explanation for the
lack of systematic relationship between structure and absorbance-based
surrogates for SRNOM fractions is derived from the observation that
Σ6 and DIOHBA have different distributions in the
PFFE fractions (). DIOHBA is most abundant (i.e., μmol mgOC
–1) in Fr6 and decreases monotonically with decreasing
mobility, whereas Σ6 increases from Fr6 to Fr8 and
decreases thereafter. Importantly, the same mobility pattern holds
when absolute DIOHBA and Σ6 amounts (i.e., μmol)
are compared to the electropherogram (Figure S9). We hypothesize that differences in the distribution of Σ6 and DIOHBA impact the structure of the aromatic chromophores
in each fraction. Lignin phenol concentrations, which do not include
DIOHBA, are often considered the main predictor of chromophoric DOM
in natural waters

highest absorbance in the electropherogram (Fr6) is dominated by DIOHBA.

In addition, DIOHBA abundance has a strong inverse correlation
to FI and β/α. This

between DOM optical surrogates and DIOHBA. In contrast, studies of

that the photophysical processes funneling excited states away from
fluorescence are also funneling excited states away from triplet DOM
(1O2 precursor) and •OH precursors.

Environmental Implications

In this work, we have shown that separation of SRNOM by PFFE results
in structurally distinct fractions with optical and photochemical
properties different from those of apparent molecular weight fractions
generated by ultrafiltration. Moreover, the relationships between
absorbance-based optical surrogates and compositional parameters (i.e.,
molecular weight and aromaticity) in some cases go against classical
interpretations in the literature. The
molecular size and aromaticity of Fr6 through Fr9 vary substantially,
yet the E2:E3 values of these fractions are highly similar and their
SUVA254 values do not vary systematically. Practically,
this suggests that caution should be used when interpreting changes
in optical properties in relation to structure for natural or engineered
processes that fractionate DOM on the basis of charge density. At
a fundamental level, these results provide evidence that additional
structural descriptors (beyond aromaticity and molecular size) are
necessary to fully understand E2:E3 and perhaps SUVA254.

We have also shown that DIOHBA precursors are strongly correlated
with DOM optical properties, especially fluorescence. DIOHBA is believed
to be linked to tannins and flavonoids and not lignin. Although tannins
have been hypothesized to be a source of chromophoric DOM,
−

 there is, to our knowledge, very little direct evidence for their
role, in stark contrast to the multitude of studies showing correlations
between absorbance and lignin phenol concentrations.
,
 Future studies should employ cupric oxide oxidation and DIOHBA quantification
in environmental field studies as well as mechanistic investigations
to further understand the role of DIOHBA precursors in DOM optical
properties.

Despite the atypical behavior of absorbance-based
surrogates for
PFFE fractions, correlations between photochemical quantum yields
and optical surrogates (i.e., E2:E3 and Φ
f
) hold across the fractions in a manner typical of ultrafiltration-generated
fractions. This points to E2:E3 being a predictor of reactivity that
cannot be ascribed to something as simple as molecular weight alone
(e.g., the abundance and nature of interchromophore interactions).

On the basis of its unique results compared to ultrafiltration,
PFFE has shown utility in separating DOM, which remains an outstanding
analytical challenge to characterize. The in-depth analysis of PFFE-generated fractions presented herein
is an exemplary top-down approach for tackling this challenge.
,
 PFFE offers the fundamental advantage of avoiding interactions with
a solid-phase matrix. In addition, electrophoretic separation is based
not only on size but also on charge density, which more directly relates
the separation process to DOM structure and, therefore, the mechanistic
role of DOM in natural and engineered aquatic systems. This is an
advantage over ultrafiltration methods, which function primarily based
on size. Drawbacks of PFFE are that the separation device itself is
an instrument with higher cost than the materials typically used for
DOM fractionation, such as ultrafiltration cells and membranes or
solid-phase extraction cartridges. Other disadvantages are the need
to separate the resulting fractions from the aqueous buffer employed
in the separation medium and the comparatively low throughput compared
with solid-phase extraction or ultrafiltration.

It is worth
considering PFFE for the separation of additional DOM
isolates readily available in high quantities (e.g., IHSS isolates).
For example, it would be of interest to see if the trends observed
for SRNOM are different for fulvic acid extracts (which have higher
carboxyl group abundance than SRNOM). Furthermore, investigating the behavior of PFFE fractions in mineral
sorption, microbial processing, organic pollutant and metal interactions,
and photomineralization is another productive research avenue, given
that DOM size and charge are known to influence each of these processes.
For example, one could quantify the metal-binding constants of PFFE
fractions and compare them to the bulk sample with a conservative
mixing model. Finally, DOM is one of many complex carbon-based mixtures for which structure–reactivity relationships
remain elusive, suggesting that PFFE may also prove to be a powerful
separation tool beyond the DOM field.
