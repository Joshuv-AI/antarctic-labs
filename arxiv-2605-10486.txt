Manipulation, Insider Information, and Regulation in Leveraged Event-Linked Markets
Maksym Nechepurenko

Abstract
The introduction of leverage on prediction-market event contracts raises three structurally distinct questions that the existing literatures on prediction-market design and on derivative-market manipulation have not addressed jointly: how does leverage change manipulation incentives, how does leverage interact with informed-trading rents, and how should regulatory frameworks respond. This paper develops a theoretical framework for the first two questions and a synthesis of the existing regulatory landscape for the third. The paper’s principal analytical move is a two-axis manipulation taxonomy distinguishing market-price manipulation (the manipulator moves the contract price without affecting the underlying event resolution) from real-world outcome manipulation (the manipulator affects the underlying event itself: athletes throwing matches, voters bribed, debate moderators behaving strategically). Continuous-underlying derivative markets generally do not make outcome manipulation a venue-level payoff channel; event-linked markets do, because the underlying terminal value depends on a real-world event whose resolution is in principle subject to influence. Standard manipulation theory adapted from Kyle and Allen-Gale addresses the first channel; the second requires a different cost-benefit framework, developed here. Within this two-axis taxonomy, leverage plays asymmetric roles. For market-price manipulation, leverage acts as a standard amplifier: profit is multiplied by leverage, manipulation cost is leverage-independent. For outcome manipulation, the amplification is qualitatively different: leverage shifts the cost-benefit threshold at which influencing a real-world event becomes profitable in expectation. We develop a partial-equilibrium one-shot cost-benefit framework that makes this threshold explicit and present illustrative class-asymmetric scenarios with parameter sensitivity. The framework is descriptive rather than predictive; the qualitative output is that sports and sub-national political markets are the event classes where leverage materially shifts the cost-benefit, while large-electorate political and macroeconomic markets are bounded below by manipulation cost regardless of leverage cap. For the informed-trading regime, the paper builds on the ForesightFlow research programme’s information-leakage detection methodology rather than re-deriving it. We extend the established framework with leverage analysis: informed-trading rents scale with leverage in three ways simultaneously — direct multiplication, Sharpe-ratio preservation, and detection-cost amortization. The third effect is regulatory-significant: leverage shifts the cost-benefit toward informed trading even before considering manipulation. The paper does not propose new detection methodology; the contribution is the leverage-amplification analysis above the detection layer. The empirical interpretation in Section 7 connects Paper 1’s pre-emption and halt-protocol-scope findings (CC-007b, CC-008) to three structurally distinct manipulation channels the framework’s deployment makes legible: a pre-emption channel introduced by the dynamic-margin engine, a halt-arbitrage channel introduced by the resolution-zone halt protocol, and a strategic bad-debt-shifting channel that no engine in Paper 1’s framework family addresses. The framework’s manipulation-resistance contribution is therefore a re-allocation of attack surface, not a net reduction. The regulatory synthesis covers the principal jurisdictions (United States, European Union, United Kingdom, Singapore, offshore-CLOB jurisdictions) and identifies three regulatory-arbitrage pathways available to event-linked-perpetual operators (CFTC-DCM with leverage extension, offshore deployment with geo-blocking, decentralized deployment without identifiable operator). The paper concludes with 14 recommendations for venue operators, regulatory bodies, and the research community, separated into framework-independent and framework-conditional categories. Empirical-magnitude-specific recommendations are anchored directionally to Paper 1’s CC-007b and CC-008 results.

Introduction

The introduction of leverage on prediction-market event contracts raises three structurally distinct questions that the current literature on prediction markets has not addressed jointly: how does leverage change manipulation incentives, how does leverage interact with informed-trading rents, and how should regulatory frameworks respond. This paper develops a theoretical framework for the first two questions and a synthesis of the existing regulatory landscape for the third, with the goal of providing the design vocabulary that any deployable leveraged event-linked product would need to address.

1.1
Motivating observations

Three observations motivate the analysis.

Observation 1: Event-linked markets make outcome manipulation a venue-level payoff channel in a way continuous-underlying markets generally do not.

The defining property of an event-linked perpetual — that the underlying terminal value depends on a real-world event resolution rather than on a continuous price process — creates a manipulation channel running through the real-world event itself. Athletes can throw matches, voters can be bribed, debate moderators can behave strategically; each affects the eventual terminal value of any event-linked instrument tied to the manipulated event. Continuous-underlying markets generally do not make this a venue-level payoff channel; comparable conduct is usually treated as corporate, commodity-supply, governance, or policy manipulation outside the trading-venue microstructure layer. The introduction of leverage on event-linked markets shifts the cost-benefit of this distinct channel in ways the manipulation literature has not addressed.

Observation 2: Empirically documented insider trading on prediction markets is non-marginal.

Empirical anchor in Paper 1’s findings

Paper 1’s CC-007b and CC-008 counterfactual replay results provide the empirical anchor for the leverage-amplification analysis developed in this paper. Three findings are central. First, the dynamic-margin engine pre-empts approximately six percent more trader positions than the naive crypto-perp baseline on observed Polymarket paths, while reducing insurance-pool drawdown by roughly five percent pooled (the directional welfare improvement is real but does not meet Paper 1’s pre-registered magnitude floor). 3
Manipulation Taxonomy: Market-Price Versus Outcome Manipulation

The defining characteristic of event-linked markets — and the property that distinguishes them from continuous-underlying derivatives such as crypto perpetuals — is that the underlying terminal value is a function of a real-world event rather than of a continuously evolving price process. This property creates two structurally distinct manipulation channels that the manipulation literature on continuous-underlying markets does not treat separately, because in those markets only the first channel is available. We make the distinction central to the paper’s analytical framework.

3.1
Definitions

Definition 1

(Market-price manipulation)
.

A manipulator engages in
market-price manipulation
when their actions affect the venue-quoted index
I_{t}
or mark
q_{t}
for an event-linked instrument without affecting the realization of the underlying real-world event. The manipulation is purely informational or trade-based: the manipulator’s profit derives from the difference between their entry price and the manipulated price at exit (or the difference between the manipulated price and the eventual settlement price for short-horizon manipulation followed by genuine event resolution).

Definition 2

(Outcome manipulation)
.

A manipulator engages in
outcome manipulation
when their actions affect the realization of the underlying real-world event itself, with the goal of profiting from event-linked instrument positions whose payoff depends on that realization. The manipulation channel runs through the real world: an athlete throwing a match, a voter being bribed, a referee being compromised, a debate moderator behaving strategically. The manipulator’s profit derives from the realized terminal payoff conditional on the manipulated outcome.

The two manipulation types differ structurally:

•

Domain of action.
Market-price manipulation operates within the trading venue (placing trades, posting quotes, disseminating information). Outcome manipulation operates outside the trading venue (in the world of the underlying event).

•

Profit channel.
Market-price manipulation profits from price dislocation between manipulator’s transactions and observable price; the underlying event’s realization is irrelevant once the manipulator has exited. Outcome manipulation profits from the realized terminal payoff matching the manipulator’s position; the underlying event’s realization is the entire profit channel.

•

Detection signature.
Market-price manipulation has signatures in transaction patterns (spoof-and-withdraw, momentum ignition, marking-the-close). Outcome manipulation has signatures in the joint distribution of position-taking and event-realization that may be statistically detectable but is observationally similar to genuine informed trading by parties with legitimate event-related information.

•

Cost structure.
Market-price manipulation costs scale with the price-impact-versus-detection-cost trade-off familiar from
18
. Outcome manipulation costs scale with the cost of affecting the real-world event, which is variant-specific (modest for some sports markets, substantial for elections, very large for macroeconomic outcomes).

3.2
Why the distinction matters for event-linked perpetuals

The distinction is novel in market-microstructure literature because traditional financial markets generally do not make outcome manipulation a venue-level payoff channel. Corporate insiders, commodity producers, issuers of debt, and policy actors can in principle influence real cash flows or deliverable supply, and the literatures on corporate-governance manipulation, commodity-supply manipulation, and policy-influence trading address these cases. The structural difference is not "impossible versus possible" but venue-versus-environment: in continuous-underlying derivative markets, comparable conduct is treated as a corporate, commodity, or governance problem occurring outside the trading-venue microstructure layer; the trading venue itself is not the natural site at which the manipulation profit accrues. The price of a stock is determined by trader expectations of future cash flows, and venue-level interaction with the underlying real-world cash flows is structurally indirect.

Event-linked markets collapse this venue-versus-environment separation. The traded instrument’s payoff is directly tied to the realization of a specific real-world event, and the venue’s open access (Polymarket is permissionless; Kalshi is permissioned but with low retail thresholds) means the population of potential traders includes participants with operational influence over that event. Outcome manipulation is therefore structurally available as a venue-level payoff channel in event-linked markets, in a way that it is not in continuous-underlying derivative markets. Any event-linked perpetual specification must address it.

The introduction of leverage further changes the cost-benefit calculation. Without leverage, a manipulator’s outcome-manipulation profit is bounded by their position size, which is bounded by their capital. With leverage, the profit is multiplied by the leverage factor. We develop this in detail in Section
5
; the key observation is that leverage shifts the cost-benefit threshold for outcome manipulation, potentially enabling manipulation on events whose unleveraged manipulation would not have been profitable.

3.3
Subcategories of market-price manipulation in event-linked markets

Within the market-price manipulation category (Definition
1
), event-linked markets admit subcategories that the equity-derived literature has analyzed in continuous-underlying settings. We adapt rather than re-derive:

•

Trade-based manipulation.
The manipulator’s trades alone move the index or mark price. Treated in Section
4.1
.

•

Spoof-and-withdraw manipulation.
The manipulator posts large limit quotes to create the appearance of supply or demand, then withdraws before the quotes are filled. Treated in Section
4.2
.

•

Information-based manipulation.
The manipulator disseminates false information (in the case of event-linked markets, false claims about the underlying event) to affect trader beliefs and therefore prices. Treated in Section
4.3
.

•

Oracle / index manipulation.
The manipulator targets the index estimator or oracle directly, attempting to create dislocation between the index and the true probability. Treated in Section
4.4
.

The first three are familiar from continuous-underlying markets and adapt with bounded-event modifications. The fourth is event-linked-specific: the index and oracle structure of event-linked perpetuals introduces manipulation surfaces that do not exist in spot CLOBs.

3.4
Subcategories of outcome manipulation

Within the outcome manipulation category (Definition
2
), four subcategories distinguish event-class-specific manipulation patterns:

•

Sports-outcome manipulation.
An athlete, team, referee, or other sport participant takes actions that affect the event outcome. Match-fixing literature is the closest analog; the leverage-amplification analysis applies here.

•

Political-outcome manipulation.
Vote-buying, voter suppression, election-administration manipulation, or strategic candidate-behavior changes the event outcome. The cost structure is scale-dependent (small constituencies have lower per-vote manipulation costs).

•

Macroeconomic-outcome manipulation.
Strategic behavior by central bankers, regulators, or other policy-makers affects macroeconomic outcomes used as event-linked-instrument underlyings. The cost is high in conventional terms but the channel exists.

•

Information-release-outcome manipulation.
The release timing or content of public information (corporate earnings, government data releases) is manipulated by parties with access. Distinct from market-price information-based manipulation: here the manipulator changes when the genuine information becomes public, not the information itself.

The four subcategories have qualitatively different cost-benefit profiles. We treat them in Section
5
; the leverage-amplification analysis applies most acutely to subcategories where the unleveraged manipulation cost is comparable to plausible position sizes (sports, sub-national political, narrow information releases) and least to subcategories where the unleveraged cost dominates any plausible position (macroeconomic, large-electorate political).

3.5
Boundary cases

Some manipulation patterns sit at the boundary between market-price and outcome manipulation:

•

Genuine informed trading.
A trader with legitimate non-public information about the event resolution takes positions before the information becomes public. This is informed trading in the standard sense
(
18
)
, not manipulation: the trader does not affect the event or the price beyond the equilibrium price impact of their trades. The detection signature is similar to outcome manipulation, but the conduct is different. We discuss this in Section
6
.

•

Manipulation of public-information timing.
A party with control over when information becomes public chooses release timing strategically. This affects the event-linked instrument’s price (by changing when the price impacts), but does not change the eventual realization. Whether to classify this as market-price or outcome manipulation depends on definitional choice; we treat it under outcome manipulation when the released-information timing is part of the event specification, and under market-price manipulation otherwise.

•

Reflexive manipulation in liquidity / volatility variants.
For Variants F (liquidity-index) and H (funding-only) of Paper 2’s taxonomy
(
23
)
, the trader’s own trading activity affects the underlying. This presents a distinct challenge that does not fit neatly into either market-price or outcome manipulation: the trader’s actions in the contract change the underlying that determines the contract’s payoff, without passing through a separate event-realization step. We identify the case in Section
3.6
but do not develop a separate manipulation analysis here; the empirical evaluation of these variants is constrained by counterfactual-replay invalidity
(
23
)
, and the primary scope of Paper 3 is the single-market binary case.

3.6
Reflexive feedback as a distinct challenge (brief)

For variants whose underlying is sensitive to trading activity in the contract itself (the reflexive variants identified in Paper 2 Section 9 and Section 11), a trader’s actions in the contract affect the underlying that determines the contract’s payoff. This is structurally distinct from both market-price manipulation (which moves price without changing the underlying) and outcome manipulation (which changes the underlying without going through the contract). We note the category for completeness but do not develop a separate manipulation analysis for these variants in Paper 3; the empirical evaluation of reflexive variants is constrained by counterfactual-replay invalidity (Paper 2 Section 12), and the manipulation analysis depends on empirical observations that current data does not support.

The remainder of Paper 3 focuses on the canonical case (Variant A, single-market binary perpetual on a Polymarket-class venue) and on the two principal manipulation categories (market-price and outcome). Multi-leg variants are addressed where their manipulation surfaces differ qualitatively; reflexive variants are mentioned as boundary cases without independent treatment.

Market-Price Manipulation in Bounded-Event Markets

This section adapts standard market-price manipulation models from the equity-derived literature
(
18
;
2
;
3
;
1
)
to the bounded-event setting of event-linked perpetuals. The adaptation is necessary because the bounded-support and terminal-collapse properties of the underlying change the manipulation cost-benefit calculation in identifiable ways. We treat trade-based, spoof-based, information-based, and oracle-based manipulation in turn.

4.1
Trade-based manipulation in bounded-event settings

Standard trade-based manipulation in continuous-underlying markets follows the Kyle-strategic-trader template: a manipulator with no genuine information takes a position, then trades aggressively in the same direction to push the price away from fundamental value, and exits with a profit (or sometimes induces follow-on trading by other participants and exits earlier). The manipulation profit depends on the price impact of the manipulator’s trades, the cost of acquiring and unwinding the position, and the eventual reversion of the price toward fundamental value.

In bounded-event settings, three structural modifications apply.

Bounded fundamental value.

The fundamental value of the bounded-event underlying
I_{t}
is bounded in
[0,1]
. The manipulator cannot push the price arbitrarily far from fundamental: at one boundary the price is already at the limit, and the price-impact-versus-distance trade-off becomes asymmetric. A long-side manipulator near
I_{t}=0.9
has limited room to push the price up; a long-side manipulator near
I_{t}=0.5
has more room. This has empirical implications: if trade-based manipulation is occurring on event-linked markets, we should expect it to concentrate in mid-region (where price impact has the most room) rather than near boundaries.

Asymmetric depth.

Paper 1’s stylized fact SF1 establishes that depth is structurally higher near boundaries than at mid-region (boundary depth ratio
\rho\approx 1.7
in pooled measurement). Manipulation cost is therefore higher near boundaries (more capital required to move the price by a given distance), reinforcing the empirical prediction that trade-based manipulation concentrates in mid-region. The asymmetric depth and the bounded fundamental value have consistent implications: trade-based manipulation in bounded-event markets is structurally easier in mid-region than near boundaries.

Terminal collapse as profit ceiling.

For positions held through to resolution, the manipulator’s profit is capped by the terminal collapse: the index resolves to
\{0,1\}
at
\tau
, so any pre-resolution price dislocation is unwound by terminal collapse. This is the bounded-event analog of the price-reversion that limits manipulation profit in continuous-underlying markets, but it is sharper: the reversion happens at a known time and to a known terminal value (one of two values). Trade-based manipulation in bounded-event markets must therefore commit to exiting before resolution, or accept that the manipulation profit is capped by the resolution-zone closing window.

4.2
Spoof-and-withdraw manipulation

Spoof-and-withdraw manipulation involves posting large limit orders far from mid-price to create the appearance of supply or demand pressure, then withdrawing the orders before they are filled. The mechanism profits if other traders adjust their behavior (move quotes, take positions in the spoofed direction) before the spoof is withdrawn; the manipulator profits from the induced price move, then unwinds.

In bounded-event markets, spoof-and-withdraw has two structural advantages and one structural disadvantage relative to continuous-underlying markets.

Advantage 1: out-of-the-money spoof zones are deep.

Paper 1’s stylized fact SF5 documents the depth profile by distance-from-mid: the bulk of resting depth in Polymarket order books is well outside the near-mid region. Spoofs can be posted in the deep-out-of-the-money zone (200–500bps from mid) where they do not commit the manipulator to actual fills (the depth there is unlikely to clear quickly) but where they appear as “support” to other market participants reading the order book.

Advantage 2: bounded support gives reference points for spoof targeting.

A manipulator can target spoofs at salient round prices (e.g., the boundary itself, the 0.5 mark, the 25%/75% quartiles) where other traders’ attention concentrates. The bounded structure gives natural focal points that continuous-underlying markets do not have.

Disadvantage: oracle settlement is not affected by spoofs.

If the goal is to affect the eventual settlement price, spoofs cannot directly affect oracle resolution (the UMA Optimistic Oracle on Polymarket reads the underlying event, not the venue’s order book). Spoofs can only affect the index price during the trading lifetime of the contract; the eventual outcome is unchanged. This limits spoof-based manipulation to short-horizon profit-taking, before the eventual oracle settlement reveals the manipulated dislocation.

Empirical signatures of spoof-and-withdraw manipulation are observable as quote-and-immediate-withdraw patterns in transaction data. Paper 4
(
27
)
treats this empirically as part of supply-side participant behavioral analysis. Paper 3 identifies the manipulation channel; empirical magnitude is a Paper 4 question.

4.3
Information-based manipulation

Information-based manipulation involves disseminating false information about the underlying event to affect trader beliefs and therefore prices. The manipulator profits from the trade-induced price move and exits before the false information is corrected.

In event-linked markets, information-based manipulation is structurally distinct from continuous-underlying markets in several ways.

Event-specific information versus market-data information.

In continuous-underlying markets, information-based manipulation often involves disseminating false information about the asset (e.g., false analyst reports, false earnings projections). In event-linked markets, the relevant information is about the underlying real-world event itself (false claims that an athlete is injured, false claims that a polling firm has new data, false claims about geopolitical developments). The manipulation channel runs through the same news-aggregation infrastructure that supports genuine information flow.

Higher signal-to-noise on event-specific information.

Traders on event-linked markets are typically attending closely to event-relevant news; the news flow is the principal information channel. False information has a higher signal-to-noise ratio in this attentive environment than false information in continuous-underlying markets where multiple information channels compete. This makes information-based manipulation potentially more effective per unit of effort, conditional on detection.

Detection through retraction infrastructure.

Information-based manipulation has a natural correction channel: the underlying event eventually resolves, and false information that contradicted the eventual resolution can be retrospectively identified. Polymarket markets resolve via UMA Optimistic Oracle, which provides a public settlement record. Manipulators face the prospect that their false information will be visibly contradicted at resolution, with the retrospective evidence supporting subsequent enforcement.

Sophisticated retraction-aware manipulation.

A sophisticated manipulator may release information whose truth value cannot be determined from the eventual resolution (e.g., probabilistic claims, claims about counterfactual states, claims with sufficient hedge language). This evades the retraction-correction channel while still affecting price. The category is closer to “misleading communication” than “false information” and lies at the boundary of regulatory scope.

4.4
Oracle and index manipulation

Oracle and index manipulation targets the index estimator or oracle directly. Three subcategories:

Index-construction manipulation.

The index
I_{t}
in Paper 1’s framework is constructed from order-book observations (mid-price, depth-weighted mid, time-decayed VWAP). A manipulator who can affect the inputs to the index estimator can affect the index. Examples: posting one-sided depth to bias the depth-weighted-mid component; executing wash trades to bias the VWAP component; coordinating timing of trades to affect the time-decay structure. Paper 1’s index estimator design (Definition 3) attempts robustness via multi-input combination, but no estimator is fully manipulation-resistant; the cost-benefit of index-targeted manipulation depends on the estimator specifics.

Oracle-resolution manipulation.

The oracle (UMA Optimistic Oracle for Polymarket) reads the underlying event and reports the resolution outcome. Oracle-resolution manipulation is targeting the resolution determination itself. This is conceptually closer to outcome manipulation than market-price manipulation but operates through the oracle mechanism rather than through the underlying event: the manipulator does not change what happened; the manipulator changes what the oracle reports. UMA’s optimistic-oracle design has dispute mechanisms; sophisticated manipulation requires either (a) economically defeating the dispute mechanism via majority-stake control or (b) exploiting ambiguity in the resolution criteria.

Cross-oracle manipulation in multi-leg variants.

For Paper 2’s variants whose constituents come from different oracle mechanisms (cross-platform conditional probabilities, cross-platform spreads), the manipulation surface includes targeting any of the multiple oracles. This is variant-specific and we identify rather than analyze; full treatment depends on which cross-platform deployments emerge.

The oracle-mechanism finding from Paper 1 (UMA-only de facto on Polymarket) is structurally relevant: with a single oracle mechanism, the manipulation surface concentrates on UMA. With multiple oracle mechanisms, the surface is distributed but the manipulator can choose the weakest. Neither configuration is unambiguously better; the relative manipulation-resistance depends on the specific oracles and dispute mechanisms.

4.5
Cost-benefit of market-price manipulation in bounded-event markets

A manipulator considering market-price manipulation in a bounded-event market faces the following cost-benefit calculation:

•

Capital required
: scales with the desired price impact
\Delta I_{t}
times the depth at the relevant region of the index. With Paper 1’s empirical depth profile, mid-region depth is structurally lower (Empirical Condition 1, refined: near-mid depth is approximately zero throughout market lifecycle), so per-unit price impact in mid-region is high. Capital required is therefore lower in mid-region per unit of price impact, reinforcing the prediction that manipulation concentrates there.

•

Detection probability
: scales with the conspicuousness of the manipulation pattern. Spoof-and-withdraw is detectable from order-book sequence reconstruction; trade-based manipulation is detectable from transaction-volume anomalies; information-based manipulation is detectable from the retrospective comparison of disseminated information against eventual outcome. Detection probability is non-zero for all three; on Polymarket, the on-chain transparency makes detection easier than on equity exchanges, but the pseudonymous-address structure makes attribution harder.

•

Profit per unit of price impact
: scales with the manipulator’s position size, leveraged. Without leverage, position size is bounded by capital. With leverage, position size is multiplied by the leverage factor, increasing profit per unit of capital deployed. This is the central observation underlying the leverage-as-amplifier theme that runs through Paper 3.

•

Profit ceiling at terminal collapse
: positions held to resolution settle at
\{0,1\}
, capping pre-resolution dislocation profit at the difference between manipulator’s entry price and the manipulated price at exit. The bounded structure imposes this ceiling, which continuous-underlying markets do not have.

The net cost-benefit is variant-specific (within Paper 2’s variant taxonomy), market-class-specific (sports vs politics vs crypto), and time-to-resolution-specific (manipulation in the resolution-zone window faces different cost-benefit than manipulation hours or days before resolution). We treat the leverage-amplification specifically in Section
5.4
, where the same amplification logic applies to outcome manipulation but with stronger consequences.

Paper 3 in isolation does not establish empirical magnitude; that will require Paper 4’s planned analysis (which is independent of CC-008 but dependent on Paper 4’s own compute schedule).

5
Outcome Manipulation: Cost-Benefit Under Leverage

This section develops the central theoretical contribution of the paper: a cost-benefit framework for outcome manipulation in event-linked markets, with leverage as the principal cost-benefit-shifting variable. The analysis is partial-equilibrium and one-shot; we identify the conditions under which leverage materially shifts the manipulation incentive without claiming a full game-theoretic equilibrium.

The motivating observation: traditional financial markets do not generally admit outcome manipulation in the sense developed here, because the underlying assets are not directly subject to manipulation by trading-venue participants in feasible ways. Event-linked markets collapse this distinction: the underlying real-world event can be subject to manipulation by parties whose costs are within the leveraged-profit range of position-takers. Whether the introduction of event-linked perpetuals materially changes the incentive landscape is the question.

5.1
Cost-benefit framework

Definition 3

(Outcome-manipulation cost-benefit)
.

Let
E
be a binary event with two possible outcomes (YES, NO). Let
\pi_{\mathrm{YES}},\pi_{\mathrm{NO}}
be the prevailing market-implied probabilities at the time the manipulator establishes their position (with
\pi_{\mathrm{YES}}+\pi_{\mathrm{NO}}=1
in the binary case). Let the manipulator’s position be long YES with notional exposure
N
and leverage
L
, where the trader’s capital at risk is
C=N/L
.

The manipulator’s expected profit from outcome manipulation that flips the eventual outcome from NO (under no manipulation) to YES (under manipulation) is

\mathbb{E}[\Pi_{\mathrm{manip}}]=N\cdot(1-\pi_{\mathrm{YES}})-K_{\mathrm{manip}}-C\cdot\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{detected})\cdot\mathrm{penalty\;factor}

(1)

where
K_{\mathrm{manip}}
is the cost of the manipulation action itself (e.g., bribe paid to the athlete, voter-suppression cost, etc.),
\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{detected})
is the probability of detection and enforcement action, and
\mathrm{penalty\;factor}
scales the consequences of detection (legal fines, asset forfeiture, criminal liability, reputational cost in proportion to capital).

The manipulation is profitable in expectation if
\mathbb{E}[\Pi_{\mathrm{manip}}]>0
, equivalently

N\cdot(1-\pi_{\mathrm{YES}})>K_{\mathrm{manip}}+C\cdot\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{detected})\cdot\mathrm{penalty\;factor}

(2)

The expression makes the leverage role explicit. Since
N=L\cdot C
, the left-hand side scales linearly with leverage given fixed capital. The right-hand side scales with
K_{\mathrm{manip}}
(which is leverage-independent: it is the real-world cost of affecting the event) plus a leverage-independent expected detection cost. As
L
grows, the left-hand side grows; the inequality eventually flips for any
K_{\mathrm{manip}}>0
.

The leverage threshold at which manipulation becomes profitable is

L^{*}=\frac{K_{\mathrm{manip}}+C\cdot\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{detected})\cdot\mathrm{penalty\;factor}}{C\cdot(1-\pi_{\mathrm{YES}})}

(3)

Below
L^{*}
, outcome manipulation is unprofitable; above
L^{*}
, it is profitable in expectation. The threshold depends on the manipulation cost, the prevailing market probability, the detection probability, and the penalty factor. None of these is leverage; leverage is the multiplier on the position size that interacts with all of them.

5.2
Implications of the cost-benefit framework

The framework yields several implications relevant to event-linked perpetual design.

Manipulation profitability is event-class-specific.

The cost
K_{\mathrm{manip}}
varies by orders of magnitude across event classes. For sports markets,
K_{\mathrm{manip}}
may be modest (tens of thousands of USD for individual-athlete bribery; somewhat higher for team-level coordination). For political markets,
K_{\mathrm{manip}}
is highly variable: very low for some narrow-electorate cases (per-vote bribery in small constituencies), very high for large-electorate elections (millions of voters cannot be bribed at scale). For macroeconomic releases,
K_{\mathrm{manip}}
is typically very high (the cost of affecting Federal Reserve policy or non-farm-payrolls outcomes is largely outside any plausible position-taker’s capacity). For information-release-timing manipulation,
K_{\mathrm{manip}}
is variable.

The leverage threshold
L^{*}
is therefore highly event-class-specific. Sports markets have low
L^{*}
; large-electorate political markets have high
L^{*}
; macroeconomic markets have very high
L^{*}
. The empirical observation from Paper 1 SF8 that crypto markets have a 24.6
\times
resolution-time activity surge while politics has 0.68
\times
may partly reflect this asymmetry: crypto markets attract speculative leveraged activity at resolution; politics markets do not in the same way, and the cost-benefit framework predicts that politics markets are less attractive for outcome-manipulation under leverage even though the manipulation channels exist in principle.

Detection probability scales with manipulation conspicuousness.

A manipulator targeting a sports outcome via athlete bribery faces detection probability that depends on the integrity-monitoring infrastructure of the sport, the manipulator’s operational sophistication, and the conspicuousness of any pre-event position-taking. Detection probability is non-zero in all settings and is typically higher for high-profile events (more scrutiny) than for low-profile events. The penalty factor is highly variable: legal fines, criminal liability, and asset forfeiture in serious cases; reputational consequences in less serious cases.

The product
\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{detected})\cdot\mathrm{penalty\;factor}
is the expected per-dollar-capital cost of detection. For position-takers with limited reputational stake (anonymous wallets, offshore residence), this product is low; for position-takers with substantial reputational or operational stake, it is high. Leverage shifts the cost-benefit toward the former group: low-reputational-stake position-takers benefit more from leverage relative to high-stake takers, because the proportional gain scales with leverage but the proportional detection cost does not.

Pre-position price as cost-benefit input.

The expression involves
1-\pi_{\mathrm{YES}}
, the venue-implied probability that the manipulated outcome would not occur absent manipulation. If
\pi_{\mathrm{YES}}
is already high (the event was already likely to resolve YES), the marginal benefit of manipulation is small; the manipulator is paying
K_{\mathrm{manip}}
to push an already-probable outcome to certainty, with limited per-unit-capital return. If
\pi_{\mathrm{YES}}
is low (the event was unlikely to resolve YES absent manipulation), the marginal benefit is large; the manipulator is moving the outcome from improbable to certain.

This implies that manipulation incentives concentrate on the outcomes that the market currently views as unlikely. A long-shot favoring the manipulator’s preferred outcome is structurally more attractive to manipulate than a strong favorite.

Coordination of price and outcome manipulation.

A sophisticated manipulator may combine outcome manipulation with market-price manipulation: take a position when the market-implied probability is low, then push it lower via market-price manipulation to suppress the cost basis, then execute outcome manipulation. The combined operation has lower cost basis (more profit per unit of position) and the same outcome-manipulation cost. The combined detection probability is higher than either alone (more conspicuous activity), but the combined profit is also higher.

5.4
Leverage as amplifier: the venue-design implication

The cost-benefit framework establishes that leverage is the primary cost-benefit-shifting variable for outcome manipulation incentives. Higher leverage caps reduce the leverage threshold
L^{*}
at which manipulation becomes profitable; lower caps preserve manipulation-cost dominance.

This has direct venue-design implications:

•

Class-specific leverage caps.
Venues offering event-linked perpetuals should consider class-specific leverage caps that account for the differential outcome-manipulation cost across classes. Sports markets, where outcome-manipulation cost is lowest, warrant the lowest leverage caps. Macroeconomic markets, where manipulation cost dominates, can support higher caps without materially shifting manipulation incentives.

•

Position-size limits as manipulation-resistance tool.
The cost-benefit expression involves
N
(total position notional). Position-size limits cap
N
regardless of leverage, providing a manipulation-resistance tool independent of leverage cap. Combined position-size and leverage caps interact: small position with high leverage and large position with low leverage may have similar manipulation profitability.

•

Concentration limits.
A manipulator’s profit depends on their position concentration on the manipulated side. Concentration limits (e.g., maximum percentage of open interest per address) reduce the per-address profit potential while preserving market liquidity in aggregate. The cost-benefit calculation per address scales with the concentration limit.

•

Detection-and-enforcement infrastructure.
The detection-cost term in the cost-benefit expression is a venue-and-jurisdiction-level variable. Venues with stronger detection infrastructure (transaction-level surveillance, cross-platform information sharing, integration with regulatory enforcement) raise the detection-cost term and reduce manipulation incentives independently of leverage. ForesightFlow’s ILS framework
(
25
)
provides a partial detection-infrastructure foundation.

5.5
Boundary with informed trading

A trader with legitimate non-public information about the event resolution (e.g., an insider with knowledge that an athlete is injured, a corporate insider with knowledge of an upcoming earnings result) takes positions before the information becomes public. This is informed trading in the sense of
18
, not outcome manipulation: the trader does not affect the event; they trade on information about it.

The detection signature of informed trading is similar to outcome manipulation: both involve pre-event position-taking that correlates with eventual outcome. Distinguishing them requires examining the trader’s relationship to the event (operational influence vs informational access) and the conduct (action-affecting-event vs information-only). On detection-only data, this distinction may be impossible.

The regulatory implication is that informed-trading enforcement (which targets insider trading) and outcome-manipulation enforcement (which targets fraud) have similar detection requirements but different conduct elements. Section
6
addresses informed trading specifically; the cost-benefit framework for outcome manipulation here explicitly excludes informed trading, but the detection infrastructure is shared.

5.6
Empirical scope of the cost-benefit framework

Paper 1’s CC-007b and CC-008 results constrain the empirical scope of the outcome-manipulation cost-benefit framework in three specific ways. First, the framework’s theoretical predictions about leverage-shifted thresholds for outcome manipulation are not directly testable on Paper 1’s empirical sample because Paper 1’s counterfactual replay simulates no manipulator and includes no documented historical outcome-manipulation episode during the empirical week (2026-04-21 to 2026-04-27). The cost-benefit framework above remains theoretically complete and structurally calibrated; specific class-specific empirical thresholds (the levels at which outcome manipulation becomes profitable for sports versus politics versus crypto markets) await either explicit manipulator simulation or post-deployment empirical work. Second, the bad-debt-shifting interpretation developed in
Section

7.3
is consistent with the framework above: an outcome manipulator who can influence the realized event also benefits from the structural insurance-pool absorption Paper 1’s CC-008 Floor 2 fail documents. The combination of outcome-manipulation profit (bounded by the manipulator’s ability to influence the event) and bad-debt-shifting profit (bounded by the counterparty’s collateral relative to terminal-jump magnitude) is leverage-multiplicative, not leverage-additive. Third, the empirical sample’s sports-class dominance (
77.9\%
of three-class total) is the class category in which the framework’s outcome-manipulation analysis is most operationally relevant, since sports outcomes have the lowest cost of real-world influence among the analyzed classes. The framework’s predictions are therefore most stringent on the most-empirically-represented class, but the predictions cannot be empirically validated on the present sample without either documented manipulation episodes or explicit simulation.

6.2
Information rents in unleveraged markets

A trader with non-public information about an event resolution can earn information rents by taking pre-event positions. In an unleveraged spot market, the rent is bounded by the trader’s capital: the maximum profit per trade is bounded by the position size, which is bounded by the capital deployed. The trader’s reward-per-capital-deployed is the difference between the eventual settlement price and the entry price, expressed as a return on capital.

The unleveraged spot-market case is the regime of Paper 1’s empirical observations: Polymarket as it currently exists offers no leverage on event-linked positions, so observed informed-trading patterns reflect unleveraged-rent extraction.
16
estimate aggregate flagged-suspicious profits of approximately USD 143 million across 210,718 wallet–market pairs on Polymarket, which is a population-scale anchor for the magnitude of unleveraged informed-trading activity.

The rent structure depends on:

•

Information lead time
: time between when the informed trader can establish their position and when the public-information event reveals the resolution. Longer lead times allow more capital deployment without crowding price impact.

•

Pre-news price level
: the further the venue-implied probability is from the eventual outcome, the larger the per-position rent. Informed traders favor markets where the outcome is mispriced relative to their information.

•

Market depth
: thin markets allow rapid position establishment but produce larger price impact, partially eroding the rent. Deep markets allow large positions without rapid price impact but require more time to deploy.

•

Detection probability
: as in the manipulation cost-benefit framework (Section
5.1
), detection costs are non-zero and scale with the conspicuousness of the position-taking.

6.3
Leverage and information rents

The introduction of leverage transforms the rent structure. With leverage
L
available, the same capital
C
supports a position of notional
N=L\cdot C
, increasing the rent per unit of capital by factor
L
.

Three distinct effects:

Direct rent multiplication.

The simplest effect is straightforward: the informed trader earns a per-event rent of
L
times what they would earn unleveraged. For a strategy with mean rent-per-event of
r
unleveraged, the leveraged equivalent earns
L\cdot r
, before accounting for any leverage-specific costs (financing, margin maintenance).

Risk-adjusted rent and Sharpe ratio.

Leverage increases the volatility of the position by factor
L
as well, so the Sharpe ratio of the leveraged strategy approximately equals the unleveraged Sharpe (with adjustments for funding and financing costs). The leverage does not improve the strategy’s information content; it amplifies both the return and the variance.

The implication is that informed traders who have positive expected returns unleveraged can earn larger absolute profits through leverage at the same Sharpe ratio. This is attractive only if their capital is the binding constraint on profit (rather than scaling, position-size limits, market impact, or detection probability).

Detection-cost amortization.

The detection-cost term in the cost-benefit expression (Section
5.1
) is leverage-independent: detection-and-enforcement applies to the conduct, not the leverage factor. As leverage increases, the absolute profit increases proportionally while the detection cost remains constant; the per-dollar-profit detection cost decreases. This makes leveraged informed trading more attractive than unleveraged informed trading by an amount that depends on the detection probability and penalty factor.

This is the third leverage-amplification effect specific to the regulatory dimension: leverage shifts the cost-benefit toward informed trading even before considering manipulation. A regulator considering whether to allow leverage on prediction markets must weigh this amplification effect alongside the manipulation-amplification effect.

6.4
The dynamic-margin engine and informed-flow rents

The risk-engine design considered in Paper 1 (PIRAP, Resolution-Aware Risk Engine) introduces dynamic-margin requirements that increase as the index probability moves away from the trader’s entry price and as time-to-resolution shrinks. This is engineered for manipulation-resistance and terminal-jump-risk management, but it has implications for informed-trading rents.

A naive (static-margin) engine like the baseline E0 in Paper 1 allows informed traders to hold their positions through to resolution with margin requirements bounded by entry-price-based static rules. The informed trader’s rent is bounded by their capital times the leverage, capped by the static-margin constraint.

A dynamic-margin engine like Paper 1’s E1/E2 imposes increasing margin requirements as the index moves favorably for the informed trader. If the informed trader is correct and the index is moving toward the eventual resolution, the dynamic margin increases to reflect the accumulated profit; the trader is required to add margin (or cap further position growth) at the same rate. This is correct from a risk-management standpoint — the trader’s leveraged exposure to the eventual terminal jump is increasing — but it may constrain the informed trader’s ability to maximize position-and-profit during the favorable price move.

The dynamic-margin engine
may
therefore compress some informed-trading rents, especially for leveraged positions held through favorable price moves, by pre-empting leveraged exposure as accumulated profit raises maintenance-margin requirements. This is a structural prediction about the engine’s behavior, not an empirically estimated rent reduction: Paper 1 does not simulate informed traders directly; the counterfactual replay observes engine behavior on observed Polymarket paths, which include informed flow but do not isolate it from non-informed flow. A direct estimate of informed-trader rent compression would require either explicit informed-trader simulation in counterfactual replay or empirical decomposition of observed positions into informed and non-informed components, neither of which Paper 1 conducts.

Whether the structural rent-compression channel is net welfare-positive depends on which population of traders it constrains: informed traders with legitimate-information access (whose rents may compensate the capital that supports market liquidity) versus manipulators (whose rents directly reduce uninformed-trader welfare). The two populations are partially distinguishable but not fully so on detection-only data, and Paper 1’s counterfactual replay does not separate them. Whether this trade-off is net welfare-positive in any specific deployment is therefore an empirical question this paper does not answer.

Third, the regulatory-relevance of the rent shift is structural rather than magnitude-specific: the framework’s introduction of leverage on event-linked instruments shifts informed-trading from a market-microstructure question (who profits from existing leakage) to a regulatory question (does the venue’s design shift the cost-benefit toward generation of leakage in the first place). Empirical Interpretation: Paper 1’s Pre-Emption Finding and Halt-Protocol Scope

This section connects the two principal empirical findings of Paper 1’s counterfactual replay
(
28
)
to the manipulation framework of
Sections

3
,
4
,
5
and
6
. The findings are: a
pre-emption trade-off
in dynamic-margin engines, by which the engine pre-empts more trader recoveries than it prevents true terminal-collapse losses on observed Polymarket paths; and a
halt-vs-margin scope distinction
, by which the resolution-zone halt protocol (Paper 1, Definition 7) addresses execution-channel liquidation risk but does not address terminal-jump bad-debt risk, which lives in the margin schedule (Paper 1, Definition 4). Three of five Paper 1 pre-registered floors fail in magnitude, two pass; the design lessons emerge from the failures, not the passes.

This section does not introduce new empirical computation. It interprets Paper 1’s existing numerical results through the manipulation lens, identifies three manipulation channels that the empirical findings make legible, and characterizes the framework’s manipulation-resistance budget at the level of empirically grounded structural argument rather than calibration of leverage thresholds.

7.1
The pre-emption channel: leverage-amplified market-price manipulation

Paper 1’s E2 counterfactual replay reports that the full framework engine
C2
liquidates approximately six percent more trader positions than the naive crypto-perp baseline
C0
on observed Polymarket paths, while reducing insurance-pool drawdown by roughly five percent pooled (eleven percent for the crypto event class). The directional welfare improvement is real but small; the unintended liquidation increase is the binding empirical anchor.

Paper 1 attributes the pattern to engine reaction dominated by market-maker repricing dynamics rather than directional retail flow: the dynamic-margin schedule observes elevated realized volatility, raises maintenance margin, and triggers liquidations on positions that — absent the margin reaction — would have survived through ordinary mid-region price oscillations. Paper 4’s planned supply-side characterization is intended to quantify the share of pre-emption-triggering volatility attributable to MM repricing versus directional flow
(
27
)
; the present section takes the structural property as given and interprets it through the manipulation taxonomy.

The pre-emption trade-off is a structural amplifier of trade-based market-price manipulation in the sense of
Section

4.1
. The mechanism:

1.

A manipulator induces transient elevated realized volatility on the venue’s observable price process. The induction may be a genuine large directional order, a spoof-and-withdraw sequence (
Section

4.2
), a wash-trading pattern, or coordinated cross-market activity. The cost of inducing transient volatility on bounded-event markets is governed by the
18
cost-impact relationship documented in
Section

4.5
, with the additional adaptation that bounded-event price impact is itself bounded by the index’s
[0,1]
range.

2.

The dynamic-margin engine raises the maintenance margin requirement on leveraged positions in response to the elevated volatility signal. Positions whose collateral-to-position ratio was previously above the static maintenance threshold but is now below the dynamic threshold are pre-empted: the engine forces close at the prevailing mid before the manipulator’s transient volatility has dissipated.

3.

The forced-close orders produce predictable directional price impact on the venue’s order book (since the engine liquidates at the prevailing mid into thin near-mid depth, per Paper 1’s refined Empirical Condition 1). The price impact is directional in the same sense as the manipulator’s induced volatility was directional.

4.

The manipulator profits by cross-trading against the forced-liquidation order flow. The profit is leverage-amplified: forced-close volume scales with the leveraged notional of pre-empted positions, not with the manipulator’s own capital exposure.

Three observations distinguish this channel from the trade-based market-price manipulation analyzed in continuous-underlying derivative markets:

Bounded-event amplification asymmetry.

On continuous-underlying markets, dynamic-margin schedules are calibrated against unbounded-volatility processes; transient volatility shocks dissipate without forcing the price toward a structural endpoint. On bounded-event markets, transient volatility near a boundary region (where market depth is structurally asymmetric, per Paper 1’s SF1) propagates into forced liquidations on the boundary side disproportionately. The channel therefore favors manipulators who induce volatility on positions exposed to the boundary, not on positions exposed to the mid.

Detection ambiguity.

The pre-emption channel is observationally similar to legitimate engine response to genuine information arrival. A manipulator inducing transient volatility produces venue-observable signatures that are difficult to distinguish ex post from a trader reacting to genuine news. The detection-cost term in
Section

4.5
is therefore higher for pre-emption-channel manipulation than for spoof-only manipulation, where withdrawal patterns leave a more distinctive signature.

Engine-design dependence.

The channel exists on dynamic-margin engines and not on static-margin baselines. A manipulator targeting venues that have shipped any version of the Paper 1 framework therefore has an attack surface that does not exist on naive crypto-perp ports. This is design-dependent, not jurisdiction-dependent. From a venue operator’s perspective, the framework’s deployment introduces a manipulation channel that the naive baseline did not.

The empirical anchor: Paper 1’s pre-emption is replicated across two independent counterfactual replays (E2 and E3, the latter via the leverage-compression mechanic
M1
, which alone produces approximately five percent more liquidations than
M0
). The mechanism is structural; tightening or loosening the dynamic-margin parameters does not eliminate it, only shifts where the trade-off binds.

7.2
The halt-arbitrage channel

Paper 1’s CC-008 reports that the multi-stage resolution-zone protocol
M3
reduces final-hour liquidations by approximately eighty percent versus
M0
. Paper 1 characterizes this reduction as “mechanical, by halt construction”: the staged trading halt at
\tau-\Delta_{R}
eliminates the window in which final-hour liquidations can mechanically occur. The reduction is real but largely tautological with respect to the halt mechanism itself.

The mechanical character of the reduction has a manipulation implication that does not appear on continuous-underlying derivative venues, because continuous-underlying venues do not have analogous resolution-aware trading halts. We name this the
halt-arbitrage channel
.

•

Under
M3
, the staged halt at
\tau-\Delta_{R}
forces all open positions to close at the prevailing index value
I_{\tau-\Delta_{R}}
.

•

A manipulator who can influence the index estimator in the moments immediately preceding the halt activation locks in a settlement price that is favorable to their position. The manipulation is short-horizon (last minutes before halt) and need not survive longer than the halt activation: once the venue is halted, the manipulated price is the realized settlement price for everyone forced to close at that moment.

•

The manipulator’s profit channel runs through the halt itself: counterparties who would have benefited from the natural pre-resolution price drift toward the eventual oracle outcome are deprived of that drift by the halt and absorb the difference.

This channel is a subcategory of the index-manipulation analysis of
Section

4.4
, specialized to venues that have shipped a halt-bearing resolution-zone protocol. It does not exist on venues without halt protocols (where pre-resolution drift is realized by the mark of all open positions). It is structurally distinct from the pre-emption channel of
Section

7.1
: the pre-emption channel exploits the engine’s reaction
during
ordinary trading; the halt-arbitrage channel exploits the engine’s transition to halted state. Both channels coexist under the full Paper 1 framework
C2+M3
.

The empirical anchor: Paper 1’s CC-008 does not directly measure halt-arbitrage activity (no manipulator was simulated in the counterfactual replay). The empirical evidence is structural: the halt-construction nature of the eighty-percent reduction means that the protocol changes
which
price counterparties settle at without changing the realized terminal payoff distribution; this exact asymmetry is the manipulation surface. Paper 1’s recommendations toward margin-side fixes for bad-debt are consistent with the implication: a margin-side hardening that does not require a halt would not introduce this channel.

7.3
Bad-debt-shifting and adversarial position-taking

Paper 1’s CC-008 Floor 2 fail is the most consequential empirical finding for the manipulation framework. Bad-debt frequency under
M3
is essentially unchanged from
M0
(slight increase of approximately two percent); the staged halt does not prevent terminal-collapse loss because settlement still occurs at the oracle outcome, which differs from the pre-halt price by the full terminal-jump magnitude. Paper 1’s SF2 documents that the median terminal-jump magnitude on the analysis sample is fifty percentage points; the maintenance-margin buffer at typical leverage levels (e.g., five times) is approximately twenty percentage points. In approximately half of resolved markets, leveraged trader positions structurally cannot cover the realized loss from collateral, and the insurance pool absorbs the shortfall.

The manipulation implication: the framework’s terminal-collapse loss-absorption channel is, by design, the insurance pool. A manipulator who knows the halt protocol does not prevent terminal-collapse loss can deliberately position adversarial trades sized against the insurance pool’s capacity, without relying on the manipulator’s own collateral being sufficient to cover the realized loss. We call this
strategic bad-debt shifting
: it is risk-allocation exploitation rather than manipulation in the spoofing/wash-trading/false-information sense, and the conduct is observationally similar to high-conviction directional trading. Throughout the rest of this section we use “bad-debt-shifting” as a shorthand; the qualifier is a reminder that the channel does not require deceptive trading practice and that detection and enforcement against it cannot rely on the indicators that detect traditional manipulation.

1.

The manipulator opens a leveraged position in the direction of the eventual oracle outcome (or at minimum, in the direction the manipulator believes most probable). The position is sized large enough that the realized terminal-jump payoff is substantial.

2.

The manipulator’s counterparty (the trader on the other side of the leveraged position) faces structural bad-debt exposure: by Paper 1’s Floor 2 result, the counterparty’s maintenance margin is empirically insufficient in roughly half of resolved markets to cover the terminal-jump loss.

3.

When the manipulator’s directional bet realizes, the counterparty’s collateral is exhausted; the insurance pool absorbs the shortfall; the manipulator is paid in full.

4.

The bad-debt-shifting channel does not require the manipulator to influence either the oracle outcome (no outcome manipulation, in the sense of
Section

3.2
) or the venue index (no market-price manipulation in the trade-based sense). The channel runs entirely through the venue’s risk-allocation structure.

Three observations on the bad-debt-shifting channel:

Position-taking is not manipulation in the traditional sense.

The manipulator is not engaging in spoofing, wash trading, or marking-the-close behavior. They are taking a directional position consistent with their beliefs and sizing it against the venue’s structural risk-allocation policy. The behavior is observationally indistinguishable from a high-conviction directional trader’s behavior. The manipulation arises from the joint distribution of the venue’s risk-allocation policy and the trader’s strategic position-sizing, not from any deceptive trading practice.

The channel is engine-independent within Paper 1’s framework family.

The bad-debt-shifting channel exists under
C0
,
C1
, and
C2
; it is not specific to the framework. What CC-008 establishes is that the framework’s halt protocol does not close this channel. Closing it requires margin-side interventions: a jump-aware tiered margin schedule (Paper 1’s Definition 4) that imposes substantially higher maintenance margins specifically against terminal-jump exposure, or a position-size cap conditional on the venue’s insurance-pool capacity, or both.

The channel interacts with insider-information rents.

Where the manipulator has informational advantage about the eventual oracle outcome (in the sense of
Section

6
), the directional bet’s expected realization probability is materially higher than the venue’s quoted index. The combination of informational advantage and bad-debt-shifting amplifies both: informed-trading rents scale with leverage (
Section

6.3
); bad-debt-shifting profit equals position size times terminal-jump magnitude minus counterparty collateral. An informed manipulator extracting both rents simultaneously faces no detection-cost trade-off because the position appears as ordinary directional trading.

The empirical anchor: Paper 1’s CC-007b reports the crypto event class shows the largest framework benefit (
-11\%
drawdown) and Paper 1’s SF8 reports the crypto event class shows a 24.6
\times
activity surge in the resolution-time window, far exceeding the politics (
0.7\times
) and sports (
2.5\times
) classes. The crypto class is therefore both the class with the highest aggregate insurance-pool exposure and the class with the highest concentration of late-stage leveraged speculation. The bad-debt-shifting channel is most operationally available in crypto-class markets on observed Polymarket data.

7.4
Net interpretation: the framework’s manipulation-resistance budget

By
manipulation-resistance budget
we mean the set of attack surfaces that a specific design removes, introduces, or leaves unchanged relative to a comparison baseline. The budget is not a single scalar quantity; it is a structural inventory of which channels are open, closed, or newly introduced under a given design. The phrase is descriptive, not normative: a design with a positive budget along one axis (channels closed) and a negative budget along another (channels introduced) does not have a well-defined “net” budget without a weighting across channels.

The combination of
Sections

7.1
,
7.2
and
7.3
characterizes the framework’s manipulation-resistance budget along three axes. We summarize:

Pre-emption channel.

Introduced by the dynamic-margin component of
C2
. Cost-imposed on manipulators is bounded by the price-impact-versus-detection trade-off familiar from continuous-underlying analysis, with the bounded-event amplification asymmetry as a venue-specific adaptation. The channel does not exist on static-margin baselines; deploying the framework introduces it.

Halt-arbitrage channel.

Introduced by the halt component of
M3
. Cost is bounded by the index-manipulation cost during the moments preceding halt activation. The channel does not exist on venues without halt protocols. The substantive implication: the eighty-percent final-hour-liquidation reduction Paper 1 documents is paid for by the introduction of a new short-horizon manipulation surface that did not exist on the unhalted baseline.

Bad-debt-shifting channel.

Existed under all engine and resolution-mechanic configurations Paper 1 evaluates; the framework does not address it. The CC-008 Floor 2 fail is, in the manipulation-framework reading, a documentation that the halt-side fixes do not address the channel. The implied design recommendation is margin-side hardening.

The integrated interpretation: the Paper 1 framework, deployed without further refinement, does not strictly dominate the naive baseline along the manipulation-resistance dimension. The framework eliminates the final-hour in-flight liquidation channel by halt construction (a narrow, mechanical reduction) and provides modest welfare-side improvements (directional, sub-magnitude); in exchange it introduces two channels that did not exist on the naive baseline (pre-emption, halt-arbitrage) and leaves one channel unaddressed (bad-debt-shifting) that could in principle be addressed by margin-side design. From a manipulation-resistance perspective, the framework’s contribution is a re-allocation of attack surface, not a net reduction.

We are careful about the strength of this interpretation. The manipulation-framework reading does not directly contradict Paper 1’s framework specification or its honest empirical reporting; it identifies the shape of the trade-off the empirical findings document. A venue operator weighing deployment of the framework should weigh the welfare-side improvements (Paper 1’s E2c result on median trader PnL; class-specific drawdown reduction) against the introduction of the two new manipulation channels and the persistence of the third. The trade-off may be favorable on some venues, classes, or capital-allocation regimes; on others not. The empirical evidence does not establish which.

7.5
What the empirical evidence does and does not establish

To preserve the pre-registration discipline that Paper 1 applies to its own empirical claims, we are explicit about the scope of the manipulation-framework interpretation in this section.

What the empirical evidence establishes.

Paper 1’s CC-007b and CC-008 results document, on the empirical sample (single-week, 13,298 resolved markets, sports-dominant), that (a) the dynamic-margin engine pre-empts more recoveries than it prevents disasters, replicated across two independent counterfactual replays; (b) the halt-protocol family does not reduce bad-debt frequency relative to the naive baseline; (c) the framework’s overall liquidation rate exceeds the naive baseline by approximately six percent. These findings are pre-registration-disciplined empirical outcomes.

What the manipulation-framework interpretation adds.

The interpretation in
Sections

7.1
,
7.2
and
7.3
identifies three manipulation channels whose existence is structural rather than empirical. The pre-emption channel exists wherever the dynamic-margin engine is deployed; the halt-arbitrage channel exists wherever a halt protocol is deployed; the bad-debt-shifting channel exists wherever the venue’s insurance pool absorbs terminal-jump shortfalls. Paper 1’s empirical findings make these channels legible: they are the structural shape of the empirical trade-offs Paper 1 documents.

What the empirical evidence does not establish.

The interpretation does not establish (i) the magnitude of profit available to a manipulator pursuing any of the three channels on observed Polymarket data; (ii) the detection-cost-versus-profit trade-off curve specifically calibrated to Polymarket’s observed thin-near-mid-depth profile; or (iii) the equilibrium response of liquidity providers and informed flow to the introduction of the framework, which would itself shift the empirical trade-offs the framework was evaluated against. The first requires either explicit manipulator simulation in counterfactual replay (CC-007b and CC-008 simulate no manipulator) or empirical detection of historical manipulation episodes during the empirical window (the analysis week shows no documented major manipulation episodes; absence of evidence is not evidence of absence). The second requires the per-class supply-side characterization Paper 4 is intended to develop
(
27
)
. The third requires multi-week or post-deployment empirical work outside the present programme’s scope.

The interpretation in this section is therefore directional and structural: it characterizes how Paper 1’s empirical findings reshape the manipulation-resistance landscape, identifies the design implications, and establishes the analytical framework against which future empirical work on manipulation incidence in event-linked perpetual venues can be calibrated. It does not provide that calibration directly.

MiCA application to crypto-based prediction markets.

MiCA fully entered into force on December 30, 2024, with the transitional grandfathering period ending July 1, 2026

The MiCA application to prediction markets is indirect: MiCA regulates crypto-assets and service providers, not event contracts specifically. Polymarket-class platforms using crypto-asset settlement (e.g., USDC) fall under MiCA service-provider rules; the event-contract content of those platforms is not specifically regulated by MiCA but by national gambling or financial-instrument regimes.

MiFID II as potential financial-instrument framework.

MiFID II provides a non-exhaustive list of financial instruments including options, futures, swaps, forwards, contracts for differences (CFDs), credit derivatives, and certain commodity contracts in Annex I Section C. Prediction-market contracts on financial or economic outcomes (interest rates, equity prices, commodity values) could plausibly fall under MiFID II as derivatives. Contracts on non-financial events (elections, sports, cultural outcomes) do not fit as cleanly. As of April 2026, no EU member state has formally adopted a MiFID II-based approach to prediction markets

Member-state gambling regulation.

Polymarket is reportedly banned or restricted in seven EU member states as of February 2026 according to industry-survey sources: France, Belgium, Poland, Portugal, Hungary, Romania, and Ukraine
(
34

The pattern reflects the absence of a unified framework: each member state applies its own gambling-law treatment, with substantial variation in outcomes.

The institutional treatment of prediction markets within the EU is therefore fragmented across MiCA (crypto-asset service-provider rules), MiFID II (potential but unrealized financial-instrument carve-in for financially-themed markets), and member-state gambling regulation (active enforcement in some states). Convergence is not anticipated in the near term.

8.3
United Kingdom: Gambling Commission framework

The United Kingdom classifies prediction markets under gambling regulation, supervised by the UK Gambling Commission
(
31
)
. The classification follows the established UK regulatory tradition that distinguishes between gambling activities (subject to Gambling Commission authority) and financial instruments (subject to Financial Conduct Authority authority via MiFID II-derived frameworks).

The UK approach has produced a comparatively clear regulatory environment: prediction markets operating in the UK comply with UK Gambling Commission requirements; financial-instrument-style derivatives comply with FCA rules; the categorization is rarely contested. The clarity has avoided most of the federal-state conflict that characterizes the U.S. regime.

The UK approach is influential in Common Law jurisdictions following UK regulatory traditions. Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, Ireland, and Singapore are more likely to adopt a UK-style gambling treatment than a U.S.-style commodity-derivative treatment. This suggests a likely durable split between the CFTC-led U.S. model and a UK-led gambling model across the Commonwealth and adjacent markets
(

Singapore: MAS authority and prohibition

The Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) has authority over capital-market products under the Securities and Futures Act and related legislation. Singapore reportedly banned Polymarket in 2025
(

The MAS framework treats prediction markets primarily under capital-markets and gambling regulation, similar to the UK approach. Singapore has not developed a bespoke prediction-market regulatory regime; the existing capital-markets and gambling frameworks apply with adverse outcomes for offshore venues seeking Singapore-resident customers.

8.6
The structural regulatory questions for event-linked perpetuals

The cross-jurisdictional review highlights three structural regulatory questions that the introduction of leverage on event-linked markets would amplify.

Question 1: Are event-linked perpetuals derivatives or gambling?

The classification has consequence: derivatives regulation (CFTC, MiFID II) imposes specific requirements on market makers, listing standards, manipulation enforcement, and reporting; gambling regulation imposes different requirements (consumer-protection rules, betting limits, age verification, taxation). The same product may be classified differently across jurisdictions. The leverage dimension complicates the question further: a leveraged event contract has structural similarity to a derivative (margin, mark-to-market, funding) that an unleveraged binary contract does not.

Question 2: How should oracle and resolution be regulated?

Event-linked perpetuals depend on oracle resolution mechanisms (UMA Optimistic Oracle for Polymarket; venue-internal resolution for Kalshi). Regulatory treatment of oracle manipulation, oracle-dispute mechanisms, and oracle-failure remediation varies by jurisdiction. The CFTC’s enforcement infrastructure assumes traditional exchange-resolution mechanisms; the offshore decentralized-oracle infrastructure of Polymarket-class venues does not fit cleanly. The MiCA market-abuse regime addresses crypto-asset price manipulation but does not specifically address oracle manipulation.

Question 3: How does cross-jurisdictional regulatory arbitrage work?

The differential regulatory treatment across jurisdictions creates regulatory arbitrage opportunities: a venue can choose its base jurisdiction to avoid the most onerous rules, while serving customers globally via blocked-but-bypassable geo-blocks (the Polymarket pattern) or via permissioned access (the Kalshi pattern). The leverage dimension shifts the cost-benefit of regulatory arbitrage: a leveraged offshore venue offers larger profit-per-customer than an unleveraged regulated venue, increasing the operator’s incentive to choose offshore deployment despite enforcement risk. Section
9
treats this analytically.

Despite the heterogeneity, three common features appear across jurisdictions where prediction markets have been actively regulated:

1.

Insider-trading enforcement is a common concern.
The CFTC’s two Kalshi-channeled enforcement actions

2.

Sports markets are the focal regulatory dispute.
Approximately 87% of Kalshi’s volume is sports; approximately 38% of Polymarket’s is sports. The state-vs-federal preemption dispute in the U.S. centers on sports event contracts. The gambling-vs-derivative classification question is most acute for sports. Regulatory convergence may emerge first on sports-specific rules even where general prediction-market treatment remains heterogeneous.

3.

Leverage is largely outside current regulatory scope.
The deployed prediction-market platforms (Kalshi, Polymarket) do not currently offer leverage on event contracts. Regulatory frameworks have not addressed leveraged event-linked instruments because they do not exist in deployed form. This is the regulatory gap that Paper 3 identifies and that any deployable event-linked perpetual would need to address.

8.8
Section summary

The cross-jurisdictional regulatory landscape is highly variable. The U.S. regime operates with permissive federal CFTC oversight against contested state-level enforcement, with offshore-venue residual exposure. The EU regime is fragmented across MiCA, MiFID II, and member-state gambling regulation, with no specific prediction-market framework. The UK and Commonwealth-influenced jurisdictions treat prediction markets under gambling regulation. Other jurisdictions apply ad-hoc treatment.

The introduction of leverage on event-linked markets would amplify each regulatory question (derivative-vs-gambling classification, oracle regulation, regulatory arbitrage) and would push the regulatory landscape further toward fragmentation absent a coherent framework. Section
9
analyzes the regulatory-arbitrage pathways available given current jurisdictional variation.

9
Regulatory Arbitrage Pathways

The cross-jurisdictional heterogeneity documented in Section
8
creates structured regulatory-arbitrage opportunities. This section identifies the principal pathways by which an event-linked-perpetual operator could navigate the regulatory landscape and the conditions under which each pathway is available. The analysis is descriptive: we characterize the arbitrage incentives without normative claims about whether any specific arbitrage path should be open.

9.1
The four jurisdictional postures

The cross-jurisdictional review reveals four broad regulatory postures relevant to event-linked-perpetual deployment:

•

Permissive federal-derivative posture (United States, post-2025).
CFTC-regulated DCMs can offer event contracts including sports and political event contracts; state-level resistance exists but is partially federally preempted. Leverage on event contracts would require CFTC rulemaking; current Kalshi DCM authority does not include leveraged event contracts.

•

Crypto-asset-service-provider posture (European Union, post-July 2026).
MiCA-licensed CASPs can serve EU clients with crypto-asset services. Event contracts using crypto settlement fall under MiCA; the event-content side requires national gambling-law compliance for many member states. Leverage on event contracts is not currently addressed.

•

Gambling-regulation posture (United Kingdom, several Commonwealth jurisdictions).
Prediction markets are gambling activities subject to gambling regulators. Leverage in gambling contexts is generally restricted (gambling regulators rarely authorize leveraged betting). Event-linked perpetuals would face significant regulatory headwinds.

•

Restrictive or prohibition posture (some EU member states, Singapore, Australia).
Polymarket-class platforms are banned or geo-restricted. Event-linked perpetuals would not be deployable to residents of these jurisdictions even via offshore deployment.

9.2
Three arbitrage pathways

Given the four postures, three principal arbitrage pathways emerge. Each has distinct cost-benefit characteristics and detection / enforcement profiles.

9.2.1
Pathway A: CFTC-DCM with leverage extension

A regulated U.S. operator could pursue CFTC rulemaking or self-certification to offer leveraged event-linked perpetuals on a DCM. The pathway leverages the post-2025 permissive federal posture but requires:

•

DCM-status maintenance under CFTC rules

•

Self-certification of leveraged event contract specifications under the CFTC Prohibition Rule (which permits review and prohibition for contracts contrary to the public interest)

•

State-level enforcement-defense capability (Pathway A operators must defend against state-level cease-and-desist orders, potentially through federal preemption arguments)

•

Capital, compliance, and reporting infrastructure consistent with DCM standards

The advantage is regulatory clarity: a CFTC-DCM operator has explicit federal authority. The disadvantage is the substantial compliance cost and the risk of Supreme Court resolution against federal preemption of state gambling authority for sports event contracts.

9.2.2
Pathway B: Offshore deployment with geo-blocking

An operator could deploy event-linked perpetuals from an offshore jurisdiction with permissive treatment (e.g., a jurisdiction with no specific prediction-market framework and minimal CFTC-equivalent authority over derivatives). The operator implements geo-blocking against high-enforcement jurisdictions (United States, restrictive EU member states, etc.) while serving accessible jurisdictions.

This is the Polymarket pattern. The current Polymarket offshore exchange operates this way
(
11
)
, with U.S. geo-blocking enforced at the platform level. The pathway requires:

•

Choice of operational jurisdiction (Caribbean financial centers, certain European jurisdictions, certain Asian jurisdictions; specifics depend on local treatment of crypto-derivative offerings)

•

Geo-blocking infrastructure for high-enforcement jurisdictions

•

Compliance with the chosen-jurisdiction rules (which are typically lighter than CFTC-DCM standards but still include AML/KYC obligations, capital adequacy, and consumer-protection rules)

•

Acceptance that VPN-based bypass by users in restricted jurisdictions is partially controllable but not fully preventable

The advantage is lower compliance cost than Pathway A and broader contract-design flexibility. The disadvantage is exposure to enforcement against the operator if jurisdictional posture shifts (the 2022 CFTC Polymarket settlement is the historical precedent), and inability to capture the U.S. retail market without VPN-based participation that exposes individual users to enforcement risk.

9.2.3
Pathway C: Decentralized deployment without identifiable operator

Sufficiently decentralized deployments may not have an identifiable operator at all, in which case existing regulatory frameworks struggle to identify enforcement targets. MiCA explicitly excludes from its scope offerings that “run exclusively via smart contracts and on a decentralised DLT system, with no legal entity acting as a counterparty”
(
19
)
; analogous exclusions exist in some other jurisdictions’ frameworks for fully decentralized operations.

The pathway requires:

•

Genuine decentralization (no central operator, no identifiable governance entity, no central front-end with legal presence)

•

Smart-contract implementation of the event-linked-perpetual mechanics including margin, funding, liquidation, and settlement

•

Permissionless oracle resolution (UMA Optimistic Oracle is the principal example)

•

Acceptance that the deployment cannot be modified or shut down by the original developers, with consequent design-correctness requirements

The advantage is jurisdictional opacity: no identifiable entity to enforce against. The disadvantages are substantial: technical risk of immutable mistakes, limited recourse for users if the system malfunctions, and the increasing regulatory attention on identifiable participants in decentralized protocols (front-end operators, governance-token holders, validators). The question of whether decentralization can be sustained as a regulatory boundary in the face of escalating regulatory pressure is open.

9.4
Implications of leverage for regulatory-arbitrage choice

The introduction of leverage on event-linked markets has specific implications for the arbitrage pathway choice.

Leverage amplifies operator profit per customer.

A leveraged venue captures more economic value per customer than an unleveraged venue, given the funding-rate revenue stream and the higher trading-activity-per-capital. This amplifies the operator’s incentive to absorb compliance costs (Pathway A becomes more economically viable) and also amplifies the operator’s incentive to seek jurisdictional escape (Pathway B becomes more profitable as the per-customer profit grows). The net direction depends on whether the compliance-cost increase under Pathway A grows faster or slower than the per-customer-profit increase from leverage.

Leverage amplifies regulatory concern.

As documented in Sections
4
and
5
, leverage shifts the cost-benefit of both market-price and outcome manipulation. Regulators considering leveraged event-linked products would face stronger arguments for restrictive treatment than they currently face for unleveraged event contracts. This pushes against Pathway A (regulated venues face leverage restrictions) and partially in favor of Pathways B and C (where regulatory restriction is less direct), at the cost of intensified enforcement attention to those pathways.

Leverage interacts with manipulation-resistance design.

Paper 1’s framework includes manipulation-resistance components (jump-aware tiered margin, resolution-zone protocol, dynamic-margin engine) that may make leveraged event-linked products more palatable to regulators than naive leverage on unmodified event contracts. A Pathway A operator could attempt to use the framework’s manipulation-resistance properties as a regulatory argument for permitting leverage; a Pathway B or C operator has no such argument available without abandoning the offshore or decentralized posture. However, as
Section

7
shows, the same framework reallocates attack surface rather than strictly reducing it: it introduces pre-emption and halt-arbitrage channels and leaves bad-debt-shifting unaddressed. Regulatory reliance on framework-style components should therefore be conditional on the manipulation-resistance stress testing recommended in
Section

10.2
(Recommendations 4 and 10), not on the framework’s terminal-jump-risk or welfare-side properties alone.

9.5
Cross-pathway operator strategies

Sophisticated operators may pursue multiple pathways simultaneously:

Dual-exchange structures.

Polymarket has acquired a smaller CFTC-approved exchange while maintaining the offshore principal exchange
(
11
)
. This is a Pathway A + Pathway B combination: the regulated U.S. arm captures U.S. retail customers within CFTC scope; the offshore arm captures international customers and offers product designs that the regulated arm cannot. The dual-exchange strategy hedges against jurisdictional shifts and captures different customer segments.

Decentralization as fallback.

An operator pursuing Pathway A or B can structure the technical implementation to support a decentralized fallback: smart-contract-based operations that continue if the central operator is forced to cease activities. This is closer to the Polymarket pattern (with on-chain settlement on Polygon making the underlying mechanism resilient to centralized shutdowns of the front-end operator).

Jurisdiction-shopping over time.

As regulatory environments evolve, an operator can migrate primary jurisdictional base. The historical example is the 2022 Polymarket CFTC settlement (which shifted Polymarket’s U.S. posture) and the 2025 CFTC permissive-shift (which created new opportunities for U.S.-based deployment). Operators with international operational flexibility can adjust deployment to track favorable jurisdictions.

9.6
Anti-arbitrage regulatory responses

Regulators in major jurisdictions have several tools to limit cross-jurisdictional arbitrage:

•

Extraterritorial enforcement.
The CFTC Maduro case
(
8
)
and the Polymarket CFTC settlement of 2022 demonstrate that U.S. authorities can enforce against offshore operators when U.S. residents or U.S. interests are involved. Similar extraterritorial reach exists in EU AML / MiCA frameworks.

•

Settlement-currency targeting.
If USDC, USDT, or other major stablecoins face MiCA-driven delisting from compliant venues, offshore prediction-market infrastructure narrows significantly
(
34
)
. Regulatory pressure on settlement-currency intermediaries can indirectly constrain offshore venue deployment.

•

Cross-jurisdictional information sharing.
The Crypto-Asset Reporting Framework under DAC8 (effective January 1, 2026 in the EU, with first cross-border information exchanges in 2027)
(
33
)
establishes infrastructure for transaction-level reporting that constrains arbitrage based on jurisdictional opacity.

•

Front-end / governance-entity targeting.
For decentralized deployments, regulatory enforcement against identifiable participants (front-end operators, governance-token holders, validators) can constrain Pathway C operations even if the underlying smart contracts are immutable.

The arbitrage pathways are therefore not stable: each is subject to evolving regulatory pressure that may close it over time. The question is which pathways close first and how operators redistribute across the remaining options.

9.7
Section summary

Three principal regulatory-arbitrage pathways are available for event-linked-perpetual deployment: CFTC-DCM with leverage extension, offshore deployment with geo-blocking, and decentralized deployment without identifiable operator. The pathways differ in compliance cost, market access, enforcement risk, and contract-design flexibility. Leverage amplifies both the per-customer economic value of any chosen pathway and the regulatory pressure against the pathway. Sophisticated operators may combine pathways (dual-exchange structures, decentralization fallbacks, jurisdiction-shopping over time). Regulators have multiple anti-arbitrage tools, and the arbitrage pathways are not stable in the long run.

10
Recommendations

This section synthesizes the analytical framework into specific recommendations for venue operators, regulatory bodies, and the broader research community. The recommendations are at the level of structural design choices and policy questions, not at the level of specific regulatory language or implementation details. Where a recommendation depends on empirical findings from Paper 1’s CC-008 results, we anchor it directly.

10.1
Recommendations for venue operators

Recommendation 1: Class-specific leverage caps.

The cost-benefit framework in Section

Sports and sub-national political markets have low manipulation costs; large-electorate political and macroeconomic markets have high manipulation costs. Venue leverage caps should reflect this asymmetry: lower caps for low-manipulation-cost classes, higher caps for high-manipulation-cost classes.

The structural form of the recommendation is: leverage caps should be set so that

Specific numerical caps should not be inferred from the illustrative scenarios alone.

Recommendation 2: Position-size and concentration limits as manipulation-resistance tools.

Position-size limits (caps on per-address notional) and concentration limits (caps on per-address share of open interest) provide manipulation-resistance independent of the leverage cap. Combined with leverage caps, they bound the per-address profit potential of any manipulation strategy. Specific limits should be venue-design choices balancing manipulation-resistance against market liquidity in aggregate.

Recommendation 3: Detection-and-enforcement infrastructure as design priority.

The cost-benefit framework’s detection-cost term is venue-and-jurisdiction-controlled. Venues that invest in detection infrastructure (transaction-pattern surveillance, on-chain attribution, cross-platform information sharing) raise the detection-cost term and reduce both manipulation and informed-trading rents independent of leverage caps. The ForesightFlow ILS framework

Recommendation 4: Stress-test framework-style components against the manipulation channels they introduce.

Paper 1’s framework includes manipulation-resistance components (jump-aware tiered margin, resolution-zone protocol, dynamic-margin engine). These components serve manipulation-resistance functions in addition to their primary terminal-jump-risk functions, but Paper 1 itself documents that the framework introduces new manipulation channels (pre-emption, halt-arbitrage) that do not exist on static-margin baselines (

Recommendation 5: Disclosure of leverage-amplification effects to retail users.

Retail users of leveraged event-linked products should receive clear disclosure of: (a) the increased detection-cost-amortization effect on informed-trading rents; (b) the amplified manipulation incentives introduced by leverage; and (c) the variant-specific terminal-jump-risk that distinguishes event-linked perpetuals from continuous-underlying perpetuals. Standard derivatives-disclosure language designed for continuous-underlying products does not capture the bounded-event structure; venue-specific disclosure should address this.

10.2
Recommendations for regulatory bodies

Recommendation 6: Distinguish leveraged from unleveraged event-contract regulation.

The current regulatory literature treats event contracts as a unified product category. Leveraged event-linked perpetuals have structural features (margin, mark-to-market, funding, liquidation) that resemble traditional derivatives more closely than unleveraged event contracts do. Regulatory frameworks should distinguish the two product types and apply correspondingly different regulatory regimes. The CFTC’s existing DCM authority for event contracts does not currently include leveraged contracts; rulemaking that addresses leveraged event-linked perpetuals as a distinct product category would clarify the regulatory environment.

Recommendation 7: Coordinate cross-jurisdictional approach to oracle regulation.

Event-linked perpetuals depend on oracle resolution mechanisms that span jurisdictions. The UMA Optimistic Oracle is the principal example for Polymarket-class venues; Kalshi uses venue-internal resolution. Regulatory frameworks across jurisdictions treat oracle manipulation, oracle dispute mechanisms, and oracle failure inconsistently. A coordinated cross-jurisdictional approach — through ESMA, IOSCO, or other multinational forums — would reduce regulatory-arbitrage opportunities specifically targeting oracle-mechanism choice. The MiCA market-abuse regime addresses crypto-asset price manipulation but does not specifically address oracle manipulation; this is a gap a coordinated approach could address.

Recommendation 8: Treat sports event contracts as a regulatory boundary case rather than a typical product.

Approximately 87% of Kalshi’s volume is sports
(
11
)
; sports markets are the focal regulatory dispute in U.S. state-vs-federal preemption litigation. Sports manipulation cost (approximately USD 10
5
for individual-athlete bribery in moderate-profile sports) is the lowest among event classes treated in this paper. Regulatory frameworks that work for political or macroeconomic markets may not transfer to sports. A specific rule-set for sports event contracts (potentially distinct from the general event-contract framework) may produce more stable regulatory treatment than attempting to apply uniform rules.

Recommendation 9: Strengthen detection-and-enforcement infrastructure for cross-platform informed trading.

The Crypto-Asset Reporting Framework under DAC8 in the EU
(
33
)
and analogous initiatives in other jurisdictions provide infrastructure for transaction-level reporting that constrains arbitrage based on jurisdictional opacity. Application to prediction markets specifically would require regulatory attention to the unique attribution challenges of pseudonymous on-chain identifiers and to cross-platform position aggregation. Specific recommendations on data infrastructure, attribution methodology, and cross-platform information sharing protocols are beyond the scope of this paper but are identified as a regulatory priority.

Recommendation 10: Require evidence that framework-style components are stress-tested.

Paper 1’s framework includes specific components (jump-aware margin, resolution-zone protocol, dynamic-margin engine) that may be relevant to regulatory assessment of leveraged event-linked products. Regulators should not treat Paper 1-style components as candidate requirements merely because they are resolution-aware. They should require evidence that any proposed jump-aware margin, dynamic-margin engine, or halt protocol has been stress-tested against the manipulation channels identified in this paper, including the channels Paper 1’s deployment introduces (pre-emption, halt-arbitrage) and the bad-debt-shifting channel that the framework as specified does not address. This is a procedural recommendation: framework-style components must demonstrate manipulation-resistance properties before they carry regulatory weight, not on the basis of welfare-side or terminal-jump-risk properties alone.

10.3
Recommendations for the research community

Recommendation 11: Empirical evaluation of manipulation incentives in deployed regimes.

The cost-benefit framework in Section
5
is theoretical; empirical validation requires deployed leveraged event-linked products. As such products emerge, empirical research should track manipulation magnitude relative to the framework’s predictions, with attention to the class-specific leverage thresholds the framework identifies.

Recommendation 12: Cross-platform informed-trading detection.

The ForesightFlow ILS framework operates on single-platform data (primarily Polymarket). Cross-platform informed-trading detection (e.g., correlated position-taking across Polymarket and Kalshi) would provide stronger detection signal than single-platform analysis. The data-infrastructure requirements for this are substantial; coordinated cross-platform research is a research priority.

Recommendation 13: Manipulation-resistance evaluation of the design framework.

Paper 1’s framework includes manipulation-resistance components but does not explicitly evaluate them against manipulation scenarios. Stress-testing the framework against specific manipulation attack patterns (single-trader manipulation, coordinated manipulation, oracle-targeted manipulation) would strengthen the framework’s regulatory weight.

Recommendation 14: Equilibrium analysis of manipulation under leverage.

The partial-equilibrium framework in Section
5
characterizes the cost-benefit threshold but does not address strategic interaction between manipulators, traders, market-makers, and venues. A full game-theoretic equilibrium analysis with multiple participant types and repeated-game structure would extend the framework substantively. This is identified as a research priority but is beyond Paper 3’s scope.

10.4
Cross-recommendation interactions

The recommendations are not independent. Several interact in important ways.

Class-specific leverage caps and disclosure.

Recommendation 1 (class-specific leverage caps) and Recommendation 5 (disclosure of leverage-amplification effects) work together: the structural reason for class-specific leverage caps is the manipulation-amplification cost-benefit; the corresponding disclosure should communicate why the caps differ across classes. A naive disclosure that treats sports and macroeconomic markets identically would not match the structural reasoning.

Manipulation-resistance stress-testing and regulatory rule-making.

Recommendation 4 (stress-testing framework-style components against the manipulation channels they introduce) and Recommendation 10 (requiring evidence of such stress-testing before regulatory weight is assigned) work together: the engine’s stress-tested manipulation-resistance properties are the substantive content that regulatory rule-making could codify. Without regulatory engagement, the framework’s components remain venue-design choices that competing venues may diverge on; with regulatory engagement conditional on stress-testing evidence, the components can become standardized requirements only after their manipulation-resistance properties have been demonstrated under pre-registered tests.

Oracle regulation and detection infrastructure.

Recommendation 7 (oracle regulation coordination) and Recommendation 9 (detection-and-enforcement infrastructure) work together: oracle manipulation detection requires the same data infrastructure as informed-trading detection. Coordinated investment in detection infrastructure addresses both regulatory priorities simultaneously.

11
Limitations

This section enumerates the principal limitations of the analytical framework and the regulatory synthesis. The paper is theoretical primarily, with empirical anchoring drawn from Paper 1’s CC-007b and CC-008 results. We organize limitations by analytical layer.

11.1
Theoretical scope limitations

Partial-equilibrium and one-shot.

The cost-benefit framework in Section
5
is partial-equilibrium and one-shot. Strategic interaction between manipulators, traders, market-makers, and venues; repeated-game considerations; reputation effects; and equilibrium concepts beyond cost-benefit are beyond scope. The framework characterizes the threshold conditions for individual-trader manipulation profitability but does not characterize equilibrium manipulation behavior in a market with multiple potential manipulators, defenders, and uncertain detection.

A full game-theoretic equilibrium analysis would extend the framework substantively. This is identified as a research priority (Recommendation 14) but is beyond Paper 3’s scope.

Order-of-magnitude leverage thresholds.

The class-specific leverage threshold estimates in Section
5.3
are illustrative orders of magnitude, not calibrated empirical estimates. The threshold
L^{*}
depends on parameters (manipulation cost
K_{\mathrm{manip}}
, detection probability
\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{detected})
, penalty factor) that we estimate informally rather than from systematic empirical data. The qualitative comparison across event classes (sports vs sub-national-political vs macroeconomic) is robust to reasonable parameter choices; the specific quantitative thresholds are not.

Bounded-event manipulation literature is sparse.

The market-price manipulation analysis in Section
4
adapts equity-derived manipulation theory to bounded-event markets. The adaptation is qualitative; specific theoretical results from continuous-underlying manipulation theory (price-impact-versus-detection trade-offs in Kyle’s strategic-trader equilibrium, manipulation-equilibrium concepts in Allen-Gale) do not transfer mechanically. A more rigorous adaptation building specifically on bounded-event-process foundations is future work.

No formal pricing of manipulation-amplified instruments.

The paper does not develop pricing formulae for leveraged event-linked instruments under manipulation risk. Standard derivatives-pricing approaches (risk-neutral pricing under bounded-support process, with manipulation-resistance margin) require substantial development beyond Paper 3’s scope.

Empirical-magnitude calibration.

The framework portions of the paper are theoretically complete and the empirical anchoring grounds the magnitude-specific recommendations in the directional rather than order-of-magnitude calibration the empirical evidence supports.

Multi-week patterns, multi-platform patterns, or non-Polymarket patterns are not directly addressed. 11.5
What the paper does not establish

To make scope precise, the following are explicitly not claims of this paper:

1.

That specific leveraged event-linked products are or are not manipulation-resistant in deployed conditions. The framework provides analytical structure; deployed-product empirical evaluation is required for specific claims.

2.

That specific regulatory frameworks should or should not permit leveraged event-linked products. The recommendations identify structural questions a coherent framework must address; the answer to those questions is jurisdiction-specific and value-laden.

3.

That the leverage-amplification effects on outcome manipulation, in specific event classes, exceed or do not exceed the effects of other amplification factors (operational sophistication of manipulators, detection-infrastructure quality, etc.). The cost-benefit framework characterizes the threshold conditions; relative magnitudes against other factors require empirical study not in scope.

4.

That the regulatory-arbitrage pathways will or will not be available indefinitely. Pathway availability depends on evolving regulatory environments; the synthesis here characterizes the pathways as of mid-2026 without claiming long-term stability.

12
Conclusion

This paper has developed a theoretical framework for the manipulation, informed-trading, and regulatory questions that arise when leverage is introduced on prediction-market event contracts. The framework’s central analytical move is a two-axis manipulation taxonomy distinguishing market-price from real-world outcome manipulation, with leverage playing asymmetric roles in each. The principal claims and contributions are summarized below; specific empirical magnitudes are anchored on Paper 1’s CC-007b and CC-008 counterfactual replay results.

12.1
Summary of the manipulation taxonomy

The two-axis distinction between market-price manipulation and real-world outcome manipulation is the paper’s organizing analytical move. The distinction is structurally available in event-linked markets because the underlying terminal value depends on a real-world event whose resolution is in principle subject to influence; continuous-underlying derivative markets generally admit only the first channel. Standard manipulation theory adapted from Kyle and Allen-Gale addresses market-price manipulation; the cost-benefit framework developed in Section
5
addresses outcome manipulation, identifying the conditions under which Polymarket-class venue leverage materially shifts the cost-benefit.

The framework’s key descriptive result is that outcome-manipulation cost varies by orders of magnitude across event classes. Sports events have manipulation costs at the order of USD 10

12.2
Summary of leverage’s asymmetric roles

For market-price manipulation, leverage acts as a standard amplifier: profit from manipulating the contract price is multiplied by leverage, while the cost of accumulating the manipulating position is largely leverage-independent. The amplification is quantitative and well-understood from the equity-derivative literature.

For outcome manipulation, the amplification is qualitatively different: leverage shifts the cost-benefit threshold at which influencing a real-world event becomes profitable in expectation. Without leverage, the cost-benefit is dominated by the absolute manipulation cost: only manipulators with very large positions face profitable cost-benefit ratios. With leverage, the position required to make manipulation profitable falls in proportion to the leverage multiplier, expanding the population of agents for whom manipulation is in principle profitable.

For informed-trading rents, leverage scales the rents in three ways simultaneously: directly (multiplicative scaling), through Sharpe-ratio preservation (informed-flow profit-to-risk ratio is leverage-invariant for properly hedged positions), and through detection-cost amortization (the cost of running detection infrastructure is leverage-independent while the profit being detected is leverage-multiplied). The third effect is regulatory-significant: it shifts the cost-benefit toward informed trading even before considering manipulation.

12.3
What Paper 3 builds on the ForesightFlow programme

The ForesightFlow research programme provides the informed-flow detection methodology (Information Leakage Score, Signal Credibility Index, per-market information leakage, deadline-resolved extension) that this paper builds on. Paper 3 does not propose new detection methodology; the contribution is the leverage-amplification analysis above the established detection framework.

The integration is bidirectional. Paper 3 takes ForesightFlow’s detection layer as foundation and analyzes how leverage changes the cost-benefit of informed trading on top of that layer. Future work within or alongside the ForesightFlow programme could in turn refine the detection methodology to account for the leverage-amplification effects identified here: a detection threshold appropriate for unleveraged event-contract markets may require recalibration for leveraged event-linked markets where the per-position rents are systematically larger. We identify this as a research direction; we do not pursue it here.

12.4
Cross-jurisdictional regulatory takeaways

The regulatory synthesis identifies that no jurisdiction has developed specific regulatory rules for leveraged event-linked perpetuals, because no such product is deployed. The existing frameworks were designed for unleveraged event contracts (CFTC framework with the post-2024 Kalshi-channeled settlement; UK Gambling Commission framework with the de-facto-derivative test; Singapore MAS public-policy-limited-licenses approach; EU member-state gambling regulation interacting with MiCA / MiFID II), or for unleveraged derivative products (offshore CLOB venues operating outside the perimeter of any jurisdiction’s leveraged-derivative rules).

The three principal regulatory-arbitrage pathways identified — jurisdiction-of-incorporation, product-classification, and participant-classification — are each available to potential event-linked-perpetual operators. The synthesis suggests that coherent regulatory treatment of this instrument class will require coordination across jurisdictions: unilateral regulation by any single jurisdiction is unlikely to be effective given the offshore-decentralized deployment options.

The recommendations in Section

The structural recommendations apply regardless of the specific empirical magnitudes that Paper 1’s CC-008 results provide; the magnitude-specific recommendations are anchored to Paper 1’s results.

12.7
Future research directions

The paper opens four follow-up directions:

Empirical manipulation evaluation.

The cost-benefit framework in Section
5
is descriptive and partial-equilibrium. An empirical evaluation — attempting to measure the manipulation activity in observed markets and compare it to the cost-benefit predictions — would test the framework’s predictive validity. The challenges are substantial: manipulation is by design difficult to detect, and the population of confirmed manipulation cases is small. The ForesightFlow programme’s information-leakage methodology provides one path; a manipulation-specific empirical methodology is research-needed.

Game-theoretic equilibrium for manipulation under leverage.

The cost-benefit framework here is one-shot and partial-equilibrium. A full game-theoretic treatment — with strategic interaction between manipulators, traders, market-makers, and venues, repeated-game considerations, and reputation effects — is research-relevant. The manipulation literature on continuous-underlying markets provides starting points but the bounded-event setting with terminal collapse requires specific adaptations.

Cross-platform manipulation coordination.

As leveraged event-linked products are deployed on multiple venues, manipulation-coordination across venues becomes a research question. Paper 3 treats single-venue manipulation; cross-venue manipulation surfaces (manipulating one venue’s price to affect another venue’s settlement, for example) are out of scope and warrant separate analysis.

Coordinated regulatory frameworks.

The cross-jurisdictional regulatory synthesis here describes existing fragmentation. Designing coordinated regulatory treatment of leveraged event-linked instruments — specifying the structural elements that any coherent jurisdiction-coordinating framework would need to address — is a research-and-policy direction beyond Paper 3’s descriptive synthesis. Specific structural elements include: oracle-mechanism standardization or recognition agreements, leverage-cap coordination, venue-licensing coordination, and informed-trading detection-infrastructure sharing.

12.8
Closing remark

The deployment of leveraged event-linked perpetuals would create a market-microstructure regime that the existing literatures on manipulation, informed trading, and prediction-market regulation have not addressed jointly. This paper has not advocated for or against deployment; the contribution is the analytical vocabulary within which deployment decisions can be made and within which subsequent research — on manipulation theory, equilibrium dynamics, regulatory design, and empirical evaluation — can proceed.

The framework portions of the paper are theoretically complete and the empirical anchoring is integrated through Paper 1’s CC-007b and CC-008 results. The empirical magnitudes the framework relies on are directional rather than order-of-magnitude calibrated: three of five Paper 1 pre-registered floors fail in magnitude, two pass, and the framework’s contribution is the documented design tensions rather than full-magnitude liquidation reduction. Whether the framework’s specific predictions are empirically borne out under deployment, whether the regulatory recommendations are adopted, or whether the variants treated in Paper 2 are deployed at all, the analytical structure provided here is intended as foundation for the discussion that any deployment would require.
