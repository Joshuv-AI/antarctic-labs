An Adjoint Projection Formulation for Enforcing the ∇ ⋅ 𝐁 = 0 \nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}=0 Constraint in Smoothed Particle Magnetohydrodynamics
Yusuke Tsukamoto

Abstract
We present a projection method for controlling numerical ∇ ⋅ 𝑩 \nabla\cdot\bm{B} errors in smoothed particle magnetohydrodynamics (SPMHD). The method corrects the magnetic field after an MHD update by solving an elliptic projection problem constructed from the same discrete divergence operator used to measure the error. A key ingredient is to use the adjoint gradient associated with a volume-weighted metric. With this choice, the projection gives an energy-minimizing correction, does not increase the discrete magnetic energy, and leads to a symmetric positive semidefinite linear system that can be solved by the conjugate-gradient method without explicitly assembling the matrix. We test the method using two-dimensional Dedner-type divergence tests and three-dimensional magnetized collapse calculations. With sufficiently many iterations, the projection reduces the divergence error to the floating-point roundoff level in both test problems. In realistic collapse runs, practical stopping criteria designed to reduce the divergence error generated by the underlying SPMHD update suppress the normalized divergence error well below that obtained in the divergence-cleaning run, with a projection cost of only about 1 1 – 10 % 10\% of the SPMHD update cost. The density and plasma- β \beta structures remain consistent when the projection interval is varied, whereas the divergence-cleaning run shows quantitative differences. These results indicate that the projection method is a robust and attractive alternative to divergence cleaning for controlling ∇ ⋅ 𝑩 \nabla\cdot\bm{B} errors in SPMHD and related particle or meshless MHD schemes.

Introduction

The solenoidal constraint on the magnetic field,

\nabla\cdot\bm{B}=0,

(1)

is a central numerical issue in magnetohydrodynamics (MHD).
In mesh-based MHD codes, constrained transport (CT) has been the most promising and widely used approach because it preserves the discrete divergence through the topological pairing of face-centered magnetic fluxes and edge-centered electromotive forces
[
2
]
.

In meshless methods, however, this CT construction is not straightforward to apply because particles do not define clear control-volume boundaries or a natural face–edge topology on which magnetic fluxes and electromotive forces can be evolved.
Divergence cleaning, which evolves a cell- or particle-centered auxiliary scalar field through hyperbolic propagation and parabolic damping of divergence errors
[
1
]
, is therefore the mainstream approach in smoothed particle magnetohydrodynamics (SPMHD) and closely related meshless MHD schemes.
Modern SPMHD commonly uses constrained hyperbolic/parabolic cleaning
[
6
,
7
,
11
,
12
,
8
]
, and Godunov-type SPMHD (GSPMHD) also uses Dedner-type hyperbolic divergence cleaning
[
4
,
5
]
.
Dedner-type cleaning, however, contains two parameters with physical dimensions: the propagation speed of the auxiliary cleaning field and the damping length scale, or equivalently the damping timescale associated with that length.
These parameters determine how rapidly divergence errors are transported and damped.
In simulations with a large dynamical range, the appropriate values can depend on position and on the evolutionary stage of the system.
The original Dedner formulation assumes these parameters to be spatially and temporally constant; if they are varied without a consistent treatment, their variation can instead become a source term for
\nabla\cdot\bm{B}
.
The choice of cleaning speed and damping timescale can affect astrophysical MHD calculations, as recently pointed out by
9
.

Projection methods instead solve an elliptic problem after a time update and map the magnetic field back to a discrete divergence-free subspace.
Such projection ideas are well established in grid-based MHD divergence control
[
10
, e.g.]
.
In SPMHD,
6
tested a projection approach based on a gravity-like direct
O(N^{2})
summation discretization for the scalar correction.
They found that the method was reasonably effective for divergence errors with wavelengths larger than the smoothing length, but was less effective for short-wavelength,
\sim h
, noise because the SPH operator used to estimate
\nabla\cdot\bm{B}
smooths such noise.
This behavior may be attributed to a discretization-level inconsistency between the divergence operator used to measure the error and the gravity-like operator used to compute the scalar correction.
To our knowledge, however, an SPMHD projection formulation that keeps divergence and gradient operators mutually consistent by constructing the gradient correction as the adjoint of the chosen discrete
\nabla\cdot\bm{B}
operator has not yet been examined.
This gap motivates the projection method developed in this paper.

This paper is organized as follows.
Section 2 presents the formulation and discretization of the projection method: Section 2.1 formulates the residual projection problem, Section 2.2 introduces the SPH divergence operator and its volume-metric adjoint gradient, Section 2.3 summarizes the mathematical properties of the projection, and Section 2.4 describes the numerical implementation and pseudocode.
Section 3 presents numerical tests, and Section 4 summarizes the results and discusses their implications.

2
Formulation and Discretization

2.1
Projection as Residual Reduction

2.1.1
Unprojected magnetic field

Let
\bm{B}(\bm{x},t)
denote the continuum magnetic field, and let
\bm{B}_{i}^{n}
be its value carried by particle
i
at time
t_{n}
.
To distinguish a continuum field from the finite-dimensional particle vector, we write the stacked particle field as

\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{n}=(\bm{B}_{1}^{n},\bm{B}_{2}^{n},\ldots,\bm{B}_{N_{\rm p}}^{n})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{3N_{\rm p}}.

(2)

The bracketed subscript
[N_{\rm p}]
labels the number of particles in the stacked vector and is not a particle index.
We first apply an arbitrary induction update before projection and define the resulting unprojected particle vector as

\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{*}=T_{B}^{n}\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{n}.

(3)

Here
T_{B}^{n}
denotes the induction operator used by the underlying SPMHD code.
It may correspond to the induction update of standard SPMHD
[
6
,
7
]
or Godunov-type SPMHD
[
4
,
5
]
, with or without additional dissipative terms.
The projection method described below does not require specifying
T_{B}^{n}
, except through the unprojected particle values in
\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{*}
.

Let
D
be the discrete SPH divergence operator selected for the solenoidal constraint.
For a fixed particle distribution and fixed SPH derivative coefficients during one projection step,
D
is a linear map
D:\mathbb{R}^{3N_{\rm p}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N_{\rm p}}
from the stacked particle vector field to the particle scalar field.
Its explicit SPH form is given below.
The unprojected divergence residual is the
N_{\rm p}
-dimensional vector
r\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\rm p}}
defined by

r=D\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{*},\qquad r_{i}=(D\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{*})_{i}.

(4)

Equations involving
D
,
\mathcal{G}
,
\pi
, and
r
below are written in this stacked-vector notation unless otherwise stated.
The goal of the projection is to reduce this residual to zero, or to the tolerance of the elliptic solver.

2.1.2
Residual correction and the roles of
\mathcal{G}
and
\pi

We seek the corrected field in the form

\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{n+1}=\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{*}-\mathcal{G}\pi.

(5)

Here
\pi
is the scalar Lagrange-multiplier field associated with the constraint
D\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}=0
.
The stacked vector
\mathcal{G}\pi\in\mathbb{R}^{3N_{\rm p}}
is the projection correction generated from this scalar multiplier.
The operator
\mathcal{G}:\mathbb{R}^{N_{\rm p}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3N_{\rm p}}
will be defined as the adjoint gradient associated with the selected divergence operator
D
.
It should not be replaced by an arbitrary SPH gradient.

The constraint is

D\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{n+1}=0.

(6)

Substituting equation (
5
) gives

D(\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{*}-\mathcal{G}\pi)=0,

(7)

or

D\mathcal{G}\pi=D\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{*}=r.

(8)

Thus the projection step is the solution of the residual equation

L\pi=r,\qquad L\equiv D\mathcal{G}.

(9)

In practice, an iterative method constructs

\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{(m)}=\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{*}-\mathcal{G}\pi^{(m)}

(10)

and reduces
D\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{(m)}
until it is sufficiently small.

2.1.3
Constrained minimization and the volume metric

The constraint
D\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{n+1}=0
alone does not, in general, uniquely determine the projected magnetic field.
This is because it imposes at most
N_{\rm p}
scalar constraints on the
3N_{\rm p}
degrees of freedom of the stacked magnetic-field vector.
We therefore regard the projection as a constrained minimization problem that minimizes the change in the discrete magnetic-energy norm under the constraint
D\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{n+1}=0
.
Through this minimization problem, we determine the appropriate form of the correction operator
\mathcal{G}
.
Using the stacked notation introduced in equation (
2
), this minimization problem is written as

\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{n+1}=\arg\min_{\widetilde{\bm{B}}_{[N_{\rm p}]}}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}V_{i}|\widetilde{\bm{B}}_{i}-\bm{B}_{i}^{*}|^{2}\quad\text{subject to}\quad D\widetilde{\bm{B}}_{[N_{\rm p}]}=0.

(11)

Here the argument of the minimization is the entire stacked trial vector
\widetilde{\bm{B}}_{[N_{\rm p}]}
.
The result is the stacked vector
\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{n+1}
, whose particle-wise components
\bm{B}_{i}^{n+1}
give the minimizing magnetic field over all particles and satisfy
D\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{n+1}=0
.
We use

V_{i}=\frac{m_{i}}{\rho_{i}}

(12)

as the particle volume.
Equations (
11
) and (
12
) therefore define the discrete inner product, or metric, used by the projection:

\langle X,Y\rangle_{V}\equiv\sum_{i}V_{i}\bm{X}_{i}\cdot\bm{Y}_{i},\qquad\|X\|_{V}^{2}\equiv\langle X,X\rangle_{V}.

The objective in equation (
11
) is
\frac{1}{2}\|\widetilde{\bm{B}}_{[N_{\rm p}]}-\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{*}\|_{V}^{2}
.
This is an essential part of the method because it fixes the metric of the projection.
With this choice,

\sum_{i}V_{i}|\Delta\bm{B}_{i}|^{2}\approx\int|\Delta\bm{B}(\bm{x})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}V,

(13)

where
\Delta\bm{B}(\bm{x})
is the corresponding correction field in the continuum description and
\Delta\bm{B}_{i}\equiv\bm{B}_{i}^{n+1}-\bm{B}_{i}^{*}
are its particle values.
Since the magnetic energy is

E_{B}=\frac{1}{8\pi}\int|\bm{B}(\bm{x})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}V,

(14)

the volume metric is the natural metric for minimizing the magnetic-field correction.
By contrast, a mass metric,
\sum_{i}m_{i}|\Delta\bm{B}_{i}|^{2}
, would approximate
\int\rho|\Delta\bm{B}(\bm{x})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}V
, i.e. a density-weighted correction norm.
This may be natural for velocity projection, but it is not the magnetic-energy norm.

Let

\Delta\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}=\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{n+1}-\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{*}.

(15)

The constraint becomes

D(\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{*}+\Delta\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]})=0,

(16)

or

D\Delta\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}=-D\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{*}=-r.

(17)

We introduce a scalar Lagrange multiplier
\pi_{i}
and define the Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}V_{i}|\Delta\bm{B}_{i}|^{2}+\sum_{i}\pi_{i}\left[(D\Delta\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]})_{i}+r_{i}\right].

(18)

In matrix notation this is

\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{T}M_{V}\Delta\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}+\pi^{T}(D\Delta\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}+r),

(19)

where the vector-field mass matrix is

M_{V}={\rm diag}(V_{1}I_{3},V_{2}I_{3},\ldots,V_{N_{\rm p}}I_{3}).

(20)

Here
I_{3}
is the
3\times 3
identity matrix.
Explicitly,

M_{V}=\begin{pmatrix}V_{1}I_{3}&0&0&\cdots\\
0&V_{2}I_{3}&0&\cdots\\
0&0&V_{3}I_{3}&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{pmatrix},

(21)

or, in components,

(M_{V})_{(i,\alpha),(k,\beta)}=V_{i}\delta_{ik}\delta_{\alpha\beta}.

(22)

Here
i
and
k
label particles, while
\alpha
and
\beta
label Cartesian components.
Varying the stacked vector
\Delta\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}
gives, using
M_{V}^{T}=M_{V}
,

\delta\mathcal{L}=\left(M_{V}\Delta\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}+D^{T}\pi\right)^{T}\delta\Delta\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}.

(23)

Because
\delta\Delta\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}
is arbitrary, stationarity gives

M_{V}\Delta\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}+D^{T}\pi=0.

(24)

Thus

\Delta\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}=-M_{V}^{-1}D^{T}\pi.

(25)

This motivates the definition

\mathcal{G}\equiv M_{V}^{-1}D^{T}.

(26)

This identifies the abstract adjoint form of the correction operator.
Then

\Delta\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}=-\mathcal{G}\pi,

(27)

and therefore

\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{n+1}=\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{*}-\mathcal{G}\pi.

(28)

Substituting this into the divergence constraint gives

D\mathcal{G}\pi=D\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{*}=r.

(29)

This is the projection equation.

2.2
SPH Divergence and Adjoint Gradient

The purpose of this subsection is to construct the explicit SPH form of the correction operator
\mathcal{G}
associated with the chosen divergence operator
D
.

2.2.1
Discrete divergence operator

The neighbor list of particle
i
is denoted by
\mathcal{N}_{i}
.
We assume that the neighbor graph is symmetric,

j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}\Longleftrightarrow i\in\mathcal{N}_{j}.

(30)

The discrete operator acting on a stacked particle vector
\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}
is defined by

(D\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]})_{i}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\frac{m_{j}}{\Omega_{i}\rho_{i}}\nabla_{i}W(|\bm{r}_{i}-\bm{r}_{j}|,h_{i})\cdot(\bm{B}_{j}-\bm{B}_{i})\equiv\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{d}_{ij}\cdot(\bm{B}_{j}-\bm{B}_{i}),

(31)

where

\bm{d}_{ij}=\frac{m_{j}}{\Omega_{i}\rho_{i}}\nabla_{i}W(|\bm{r}_{i}-\bm{r}_{j}|,h_{i}).

(32)

The reverse directed coefficient, obtained by exchanging
i
and
j
, is

\bm{d}_{ji}=\frac{m_{i}}{\Omega_{j}\rho_{j}}\nabla_{j}W(|\bm{r}_{j}-\bm{r}_{i}|,h_{j}).

(33)

In general,

\bm{d}_{ij}\neq\bm{d}_{ji},

(34)

because the derivative coefficients are evaluated with particle-dependent smoothing lengths, densities, and
\Omega
-factors.

2.2.2
Component and matrix form

In components,

(D\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]})_{i}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\sum_{\alpha\in\{x,y,z\}}d_{ij}^{\alpha}(B_{j}^{\alpha}-B_{i}^{\alpha}),

(35)

where the Cartesian component index
\alpha
runs over
x,y,z
.

As a matrix,

D\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\rm p}\times 3N_{\rm p}}.

(36)

Using a column index
(k,\alpha)
, corresponding to component
\alpha
of particle
k
, the entries are

D_{i,(k,\alpha)}=\begin{cases}d_{ik}^{\alpha},&k\in\mathcal{N}_{i},\quad k\neq i,\\[4.0pt]
-\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}d_{ij}^{\alpha},&k=i,\\[8.0pt]
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(37)

Thus

(D\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]})_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\rm p}}\sum_{\alpha\in\{x,y,z\}}D_{i,(k,\alpha)}B_{k}^{\alpha}.

(38)

This expression explicitly shows that
D
is a linear operator acting on the stacked vector
\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}
.

2.2.3
Adjoint gradient for the Lagrange multiplier

The Lagrange multiplier
\pi_{i}
was introduced in Section
2.1.3
as the multiplier enforcing the constraint
D\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}=0
.
As found in equation (
26
), the correction operator is

\mathcal{G}=M_{V}^{-1}D^{T}.

(39)

Using the matrix entries above, the
\alpha
-component of this vector at particle
i
is

\displaystyle(\mathcal{G}\pi)_{i}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V_{i}}(D^{T}\pi)_{(i,\alpha)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V_{i}}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\rm p}}D_{k,(i,\alpha)}\pi_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V_{i}}\left[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq i,\ i\in\mathcal{N}_{k}\end{subarray}}^{N_{\rm p}}\pi_{k}d_{ki}^{\alpha}-\pi_{i}\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}d_{i\ell}^{\alpha}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V_{i}}\left[\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\pi_{k}d_{ki}^{\alpha}-\pi_{i}\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}d_{i\ell}^{\alpha}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V_{i}}\left[\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\pi_{j}d_{ji}^{\alpha}-\pi_{i}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}d_{ij}^{\alpha}\right].

(40)

In the second equality after the full matrix sum, the first term collects rows
k
for which
i\in\mathcal{N}_{k}
, while the second term is the diagonal contribution from row
i
.
We then use the symmetry of the neighbor graph,
i\in\mathcal{N}_{k}\Leftrightarrow k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}
, and finally relabel
k
as
j
.
In vector notation, this gives

(\mathcal{G}\pi)_{i}=\frac{1}{V_{i}}\left[\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\pi_{j}\bm{d}_{ji}-\pi_{i}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{d}_{ij}\right].

(41)

Substituting equations (
32
) and (
33
), and using
V_{i}=m_{i}/\rho_{i}
, gives the explicit componentwise particle-sum expression

(\mathcal{G}\pi)_{i}^{\alpha}=-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\left[\pi_{i}\frac{m_{j}}{m_{i}\Omega_{i}}\left[\nabla_{i}W(|\bm{r}_{i}-\bm{r}_{j}|,h_{i})\right]^{\alpha}+\pi_{j}\frac{\rho_{i}}{\Omega_{j}\rho_{j}}\left[\nabla_{i}W(|\bm{r}_{i}-\bm{r}_{j}|,h_{j})\right]^{\alpha}\right].

(42)

Here we used
\nabla_{j}W(|\bm{r}_{j}-\bm{r}_{i}|,h_{j})=-\nabla_{i}W(|\bm{r}_{i}-\bm{r}_{j}|,h_{j})
.
The two terms inside the bracket originate from
\bm{d}_{ij}
and
\bm{d}_{ji}
, respectively.
This distinction is essential for an SPH divergence operator.

Equation (
42
) takes a form consistent with the standard symmetric SPH discretization if we introduce a scalar potential
\psi_{i}
by
\pi_{i}=V_{i}\psi_{i}
:

(\mathcal{G}\pi)_{i}^{\alpha}=-\rho_{i}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}m_{j}\left[\frac{\psi_{i}}{\Omega_{i}\rho_{i}^{2}}\left[\nabla_{i}W(|\bm{r}_{i}-\bm{r}_{j}|,h_{i})\right]^{\alpha}+\frac{\psi_{j}}{\Omega_{j}\rho_{j}^{2}}\left[\nabla_{i}W(|\bm{r}_{i}-\bm{r}_{j}|,h_{j})\right]^{\alpha}\right].

Using the Lagrange multiplier
\pi_{i}
as the primary scalar variable keeps the adjoint relation and the resulting projection operator transparent.

Equation (
39
) is equivalent to the volume-metric adjoint relation for arbitrary particle vector fields.
Indeed, for any stacked particle vector field
X
,

\displaystyle\pi^{T}DX

\displaystyle=(D^{T}\pi)^{T}X

\displaystyle=(M_{V}^{-1}D^{T}\pi)^{T}M_{V}X

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{G}\pi)^{T}M_{V}X,

where we used equation (
39
) and the symmetry of the diagonal matrix
M_{V}
.
Written as particle sums, this gives the adjoint relation below.
Conversely, if the adjoint relation holds for arbitrary
X
, then
(D^{T}\pi)^{T}X=(M_{V}\mathcal{G}\pi)^{T}X
for all
X
.
Therefore
D^{T}\pi=M_{V}\mathcal{G}\pi
, which gives equation (
39
).
The adjoint relation is

\sum_{i}\pi_{i}(D\bm{X})_{i}=\sum_{i}V_{i}(\mathcal{G}\pi)_{i}\cdot\bm{X}_{i}

(43)

for any particle scalar field
\pi_{i}
and any particle vector field
\bm{X}_{i}
.
Equation (
43
) is the volume-metric discrete analogue of integration by parts.
The corresponding vector-calculus identity is

\int_{\Omega}\pi\,\nabla\cdot\bm{X}\,\mathrm{d}V=\int_{\partial\Omega}\pi\,\bm{X}\cdot\bm{n}\,\mathrm{d}S+\int_{\Omega}(-\nabla\pi)\cdot\bm{X}\,\mathrm{d}V.

When the boundary term vanishes, this becomes

\int_{\Omega}\pi\,\nabla\cdot\bm{X}\,\mathrm{d}V=\int_{\Omega}(-\nabla\pi)\cdot\bm{X}\,\mathrm{d}V.

Thus
\mathcal{G}\pi
is the discrete counterpart of
-\nabla\pi
associated with the chosen divergence operator
D
and the volume metric.

2.3
Properties of the Volume-Metric Adjoint Projection

The adjoint relation is essential to the projection: it makes the correction space generated by
\mathcal{G}
orthogonal, with respect to the volume inner product, to the discrete divergence-free space
\ker(D)
.
In this section, we demonstrate this fact through the resulting orthogonal decomposition, the discrete magnetic-energy estimate, and the conjugate-gradient solvability of the scalar projection equation.

2.3.1
Orthogonality of the projection

Let

\ker(D)=\{\bm{X}:D\bm{X}=0\}

(44)

be the discrete divergence-free subspace, and

{\rm range}(\mathcal{G})=\{\mathcal{G}\pi:\pi\ \text{arbitrary}\}

(45)

be the space of projection corrections.
For any
\bm{X}\in\ker(D)
, the adjoint identity gives

\sum_{i}V_{i}(\mathcal{G}\pi)_{i}\cdot\bm{X}_{i}=\sum_{i}\pi_{i}(D\bm{X})_{i}=0.

(46)

Thus

{\rm range}(\mathcal{G})\perp\ker(D)

(47)

with respect to the volume inner product.

The corrected field satisfies

\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{n+1}\in\ker(D),

(48)

while the removed component
\mathcal{G}\pi
lies in
{\rm range}(\mathcal{G})
.
Therefore

\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{*}=\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{n+1}+\mathcal{G}\pi

(49)

is an orthogonal decomposition in the volume metric.
Consequently, for consistent boundary and nullspace treatment,

\|\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{*}\|_{V}^{2}=\|\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{n+1}\|_{V}^{2}+\|\mathcal{G}\pi\|_{V}^{2},

(50)

where

\|\bm{X}\|_{V}^{2}=\sum_{i}V_{i}|\bm{X}_{i}|^{2}.

(51)

Thus the method is not merely a divergence-error removal prescription; it is the volume-metric orthogonal projection of
\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{*}
onto the subspace satisfying
D\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}=0
.
Physically, because the volume metric approximates the magnetic energy of the particle field,
E_{B}=(8\pi)^{-1}\|\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}\|_{V}^{2}=(8\pi)^{-1}\sum_{i}V_{i}|\bm{B}_{i}|^{2}
, this orthogonal decomposition also ensures

E_{B}^{n+1}=E_{B}^{*}-\frac{1}{8\pi}\|\mathcal{G}\pi\|_{V}^{2}\leq E_{B}^{*},

(52)

with equality only when no correction is applied.
Therefore, a numerically important property of the projection is that it does not increase the discrete magnetic energy; divergence removal by this projection does not inject magnetic energy.

2.3.2
Conjugate-gradient solvability

Although the SPH derivative coefficients are directed and generally satisfy
\bm{d}_{ij}\neq\bm{d}_{ji}
, the scalar projection equation,
D\mathcal{G}\pi=r
, i.e.,
DM_{V}^{-1}D^{T}\pi=r
, can be solved by the preconditioned conjugate-gradient (PCG) method because the scalar projection operator is symmetric positive semidefinite.
We introduce this operator as

L\equiv D\mathcal{G}=DM_{V}^{-1}D^{T}.

(53)

Because
M_{V}^{-1}
is symmetric,
L
is also symmetric:

L^{T}=\left(DM_{V}^{-1}D^{T}\right)^{T}=D\left(M_{V}^{-1}\right)^{T}D^{T}=DM_{V}^{-1}D^{T}=L.

(54)

The operator
L
also satisfies, for any vector
\bm{X}
in the multiplier space,

\bm{X}^{T}L\bm{X}=(D^{T}\bm{X})^{T}M_{V}^{-1}(D^{T}\bm{X})\geq 0.

(55)

Thus
L
is symmetric positive semidefinite in the ordinary scalar-product space of the multiplier.
After removing the null space, the residual equation can be solved by PCG.

2.4
Numerical Implementation and Pseudocode

2.4.1
Implementation flow

Below, we describe the concrete implementation flow of the projection step.
We distinguish two particle sets.
The active set
\mathcal{A}
contains particles whose magnetic field is corrected by the projection and whose scalar multiplier
\pi_{i}
is an unknown variable.
The fixed set
\mathcal{F}
contains particles whose magnetic field is held fixed during the projection, such as boundary particles; for these particles we set
\pi_{i}=0
and
\bm{B}_{i}^{n+1}=\bm{B}_{i}^{*}
.
Fixed particles are not used as rows of the projection equation, but they may appear in neighbor lists of active particles as prescribed magnetic-field values.

For each active particle
i\in\mathcal{A}
, the neighbor list
\mathcal{N}_{i}
is built and the directed edge coefficient
\bm{d}_{ij}
is stored or recomputed.
The same coefficient is then used in both the divergence operator and its adjoint.

The unprojected residual is evaluated as

r_{i}=(D\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{*})_{i}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{d}_{ij}\cdot(\bm{B}_{j}^{*}-\bm{B}_{i}^{*}).

(56)

for
i\in\mathcal{A}

The neighbor

is simply a prescribed value in this row.

For a trial scalar multiplier
\pi

\displaystyle(\mathcal{G}\pi)_{i}

\displaystyle=(M_{V}^{-1}D^{T}\pi)_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V_{i}}\left[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathcal{A}\\
k\neq i,\ i\in\mathcal{N}_{k}\end{subarray}}\pi_{k}\bm{d}_{ki}-\pi_{i}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{d}_{ij}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V_{i}}\left[\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\pi_{j}\bm{d}_{ji}-\pi_{i}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{d}_{ij}\right],

(57)

The last equality uses the symmetry of the neighbor graph and the convention
\pi_{j}=0

\pi_{j}\bm{d}_{ji}

Then

(L\pi)_{i}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{d}_{ij}\cdot\left[\bm{g}_{j}-\bm{g}_{i}\right].

(58)

Here

for

\bm{g}_{j}=0

The linear system
L\pi=r
is solved by PCG on the active set
\mathcal{A}

The unprojected particle values
\bm{B}_{i}^{*}
are known on both the active particles
\mathcal{A}
and the fixed particles
\mathcal{F}

\mathcal{A}

We introduce the scalar product of stacked scalar vectors on
\mathcal{A}

(a,b)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{A}}a_{i}b_{i}.

(59)

At iteration
m

s^{(m)}=r-L\pi^{(m)}.

(60)

The diagonal preconditioner

is formed from the diagonal of

\bm{q}_{i}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{d}_{ij},\qquad a_{i}=\frac{|\bm{q}_{i}|^{2}}{V_{i}}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}\cap\mathcal{A}}\frac{|\bm{d}_{ij}|^{2}}{V_{j}},\qquad P_{\pi}={\rm diag}(a_{i}).

(61)

Here
i\in\mathcal{A}
.
The preconditioned residual is then

z_{i}^{(m)}=(P_{\pi}^{-1}s^{(m)})_{i}=\frac{s_{i}^{(m)}}{a_{i}+\epsilon_{a}},

(62)

where
\epsilon_{a}
is a small regularization used only if needed.
The second sum in equation (

For the PCG recurrence, we define

\gamma_{m}=(s^{(m)},z^{(m)}).

(63)

The search direction is initialized and updated as

p^{(0)}=z^{(0)},\qquad p^{(m)}=z^{(m)}+\beta_{m-1}p^{(m-1)}\quad(m\geq 1),\qquad\beta_{m-1}=\frac{\gamma_{m}}{\gamma_{m-1}}.

(64)

Given the search direction
p^{(m)}
, the unfiltered operator action is evaluated as

\bm{g}^{(m)}=\mathcal{G}p^{(m)},\qquad\tilde{w}^{(m)}=D\bm{g}^{(m)}=Lp^{(m)}.

(65)

When mean subtraction is required, for example to remove the constant scalar null mode in periodic calculations, the unfiltered scalar action
\tilde{w}^{(m)}
in equation (
65

w_{i}^{(m)}=(P_{\rm c}\tilde{w}^{(m)})_{i}=\tilde{w}_{i}^{(m)}-\bar{\tilde{w}}^{(m)}_{V},\qquad i\in\mathcal{A}.

(

Here

\bar{\tilde{w}}^{(m)}_{V}=\frac{\sum_{k\in\mathcal{A}}V_{k}\tilde{w}_{k}^{(m)}}{\sum_{k\in\mathcal{A}}V_{k}}.

(66)

For cases where mean subtraction is not required,
w^{(m)}=\tilde{w}^{(m)}
, equivalently

In the code this optional step corresponds to subtracting the volume-weighted mean from the scalar operator-action vector in the periodic case.
In the tests presented in this paper, mean subtraction is used only for the Dedner-type divergence test with periodic boundaries.

2.4.2
Convergence criterion used in this paper

At PCG iteration
m
, the provisional corrected magnetic field is

\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{(m)}=\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{*}-\mathcal{G}\pi^{(m)}.

(67)

Since
r=D\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{*}
and
L=D\mathcal{G}
, the residual of the projection equation is

r-L\pi^{(m)}=D\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{(m)}.

(68)

In a periodic domain, the constant mode of the residual is removed before solving the projection equation.
We therefore write the convergence measures in terms of the mean-free discrete divergence of the provisional field.
For a particle vector
q
, we define this mean-subtraction operator by

(P_{\rm c}q)_{i}=q_{i}-\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm p}}V_{j}q_{j}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm p}}V_{j}}.

(69)

Thus the monitored residual is
P_{\rm c}D\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{(m)}
, not the raw
D\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{(m)}
.
A standard relative solver tolerance may be written as

\|r_{m}\|\equiv\|P_{\rm c}D\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{(m)}\|_{V},\qquad\frac{\|r_{m}\|}{\|P_{\rm c}D\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{*}\|_{V}+\epsilon}<\epsilon_{\rm rel},\qquad\|D\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}\|_{V}^{2}=\sum_{i}V_{i}\left[(D\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]})_{i}\right]^{2}.

(70)

This relative residual criterion is used for the periodic Dedner-type divergence tests presented below.
For non-periodic applications, one may simply set
P_{\rm c}=I
.

On the other hand, for the collapse test in this paper, we use a more practical stopping criterion based on the divergence error accumulated during the interval between two projection steps.
This criterion consists of two conditions.
The first is a top-tail condition, which is satisfied either by reducing the divergence change accumulated since the previous projection or by making the current top-tail divergence sufficiently small.
The second is an all-particle absolute condition.

We define the dimensionless divergence measure

\chi_{i}^{(m)}=\left|\frac{(D\bm{B}_{[N_{\rm p}]}^{(m)})_{i}}{|\bm{B}_{i}^{(m)}|/h_{i}}\right|.

(71)

We store
\chi_{i}^{\rm prev}
, the value just after the previous projection.
The change in the divergence measure during the current projection interval is then measured by

\Delta\chi_{i}^{(m)}=\chi_{i}^{(m)}-\chi_{i}^{\rm prev}.

(72)

Let
{\rm RMS}_{f_{\rm top}}(\Delta\chi)
denote the RMS of the largest fraction
f_{\rm top}
of
|\Delta\chi_{i}|
over particles in
\mathcal{A}
, and define
{\rm RMS}_{f_{\rm top}}(\chi)
analogously.
The notation
{\rm RMS}(x)
, without a subscript, denotes the ordinary RMS over
\mathcal{A}
.
The first condition for the top-tail particles is

{\rm RMS}_{f_{\rm top}}(\Delta\chi^{(m)})\leq f_{\rm red}{\rm RMS}_{f_{\rm top}}(\Delta\chi^{(0)})\quad\text{or}\quad{\rm RMS}_{f_{\rm top}}(\chi^{(m)})<\epsilon_{\rm abs},

(73)

where
f_{\rm top}
specifies the fraction of particles used in the top-tail RMS, and
f_{\rm red}
specifies the required reduction factor.
In the calculations presented here we use
f_{\rm top}=0.01
and
f_{\rm red}=0.1/N_{\rm interval}
, where
N_{\rm interval}
is the number of ordinary MHD updates between two projection steps.
Thus the reduction condition in equation (
73
) continues the iterations until the divergence accumulated by the ordinary MHD update, measured over the largest 1% of particles, is reduced to one tenth of the average amount accumulated in one ordinary MHD update during the preceding projection interval.
The top-tail absolute condition prevents unnecessary iteration when the current top-tail divergence is already below the absolute tolerance.
The second condition is

{\rm RMS}(\chi^{(m)})<\epsilon_{\rm abs}.

(74)

This condition requires the ordinary RMS over all active particles to be below the absolute tolerance.
The projection is stopped only when both equations (
73
) and (
74
) are satisfied.
The strategy for the collapse test is therefore to reduce the divergence that has accumulated since the last projection.
It does not aim to over-solve the projection far below the typical level of
D\bm{B}
generated by the underlying MHD scheme, for example down to machine precision, although such a tighter solve is achievable with sufficiently many iterations.

3.1
Dedner-type divergence test

We first test our projection method with a two-dimensional periodic divergence test based on
1
.
The calculation is performed in a unit square with
64^{2}
equal-mass particles.
Instead of evolving the Dedner cleaning equations, we use their compact magnetic perturbation as the magnetic field
\bm{B}^{*}
and apply the projection once.
The imposed field is centered at
(x,y)=(1/2,1/2)
and is

B_{x}^{*}=q^{8}-2q^{4}+1,\qquad B_{y}^{*}=B_{z}^{*}=0\quad(q\leq 1),

(75)

where
q=r/r_{0}
,
r

r_{0}=0.20
.
For
q>1
, we set
\bm{B}^{*}=0
.
This field is compactly supported and smooth at
q=1

We present results for two particle arrangements.
The first is a Cartesian lattice randomly displaced by 10 per cent of the grid spacing in each coordinate.
We use this perturbed lattice, rather than an exact Cartesian lattice, because in the exact periodic lattice the residual did not decrease cleanly, apparently owing to symmetry and periodic-boundary effects.
The second is a random distribution sampled uniformly over the simulation domain.
In both cases,
\rho_{i}
and

are iterated self-consistently from the SPH density relation.
Thus the imposed Dedner-type magnetic perturbation is tested at the same nominal

This may seem counterintuitive because the random distribution is expected to produce more irregular discrete matrices

Note, however, that the PCG convergence is controlled by the spectrum of

A plausible explanation is that the nearly periodic lattice still retains Fourier-like coherent modes associated with the periodic symmetry, so that part of the residual lies along slowly converging small-eigenvalue directions.
The random distribution breaks this coherence and makes the imposed localized residual less aligned with the slow modes of

to the level of the divergence error generated by the MHD solver during the interval between projections; we show below that substantially fewer iterations are sufficient for this purpose.
The projection also need not be applied every timestep.
As long as

remains small enough not to affect the solution, applying the projection every several timesteps is acceptable.
This flexibility is an advantage of solving a time-independent constraint equation, which can be applied to the accumulated divergence error when needed.

3.2
Collapse calculation: divergence cleaning and projection

We next apply the projection method to a three-dimensional non-ideal MHD gravitational collapse calculation based on the initial condition of
13

The initial cloud is a rotating, magnetized dusty Bonnor–Ebert sphere, and the mass within the sphere is
1\,M_{\odot}
.
The initial cloud has
\alpha=0.4
,
\beta=0.03
, and normalized mass-to-flux ratio
\mu=5
.
The
1\,M_{\odot}

N_{\rm SPH}=3\times 10^{6}

In these test calculations, we do not introduce sink particles.
We omit the remaining details of the thermodynamics, resistivity model, and dust treatment because they are not essential for the present divergence-control test.
We compare a divergence-cleaning calculation and projection calculations that use the same initial condition and MHD update.
The divergence-cleaning calculation uses the hyperbolic cleaning prescription described below, while the projection calculations apply the elliptic projection at prescribed intervals.

For the projection calculation, we perform three projection-interval cases with
N_{\rm interval}=1,10,
and
100
.
In all cases, we fix the monitored top fraction to
f_{\rm top}=0.01
, the increment-reduction factor to
f_{\rm red}=0.1/N_{\rm interval}

\chi\equiv h|\nabla\cdot\bm{B}|/|\bm{B}|
to
\epsilon_{\rm abs}=10^{-5}

This choice is designed to reduce the mean divergence error generated per MHD update to roughly one tenth of its accumulated value, rather than to solve the elliptic equation to roundoff accuracy.
Specifically, the projection requires the top

1\%

Here

is set to

4
Summary and Discussion

In this paper, we have studied a projection method for removing numerical
\nabla\cdot\bm{B}
errors in SPMHD.
In contrast to previous projection approaches, we constructed a gradient operator
\mathcal{G}
that is adjoint to the target discrete divergence operator
D
.
We wrote both operators explicitly so that their linearity as discrete maps is transparent.
With this construction, we showed that the projection operator
L=D\mathcal{G}=DM_{V}^{-1}D^{T}
is symmetric positive semidefinite.
Consequently, the projection equation can be solved by the conjugate-gradient method.
We also showed that the projection is orthogonal with respect to the volume-weighted metric.
As a result, the correction does not increase the discrete magnetic energy, which is a desirable property for MHD calculations.

Using this method, we performed test calculations and showed that, with sufficiently many iterations, the divergence error can be reduced to the floating-point roundoff level.
One may worry that such roundoff-level projections are too expensive for practical simulations.
However, production calculations do not require such a strict tolerance.
It is sufficient to stop the projection once the divergence error has been reduced to the level generated by the underlying MHD update itself.
Our gravitational collapse calculations showed that this practical level of divergence control can be achieved at an affordable computational cost.
Even with this practical stopping criterion, the projection method produced substantially better results than the divergence-cleaning method that is commonly used in SPMHD.

The present method still contains free parameters that control when the projection is terminated and how often it is applied.
The need to choose these parameters is a weakness compared with constrained-transport schemes.
On the other hand, the parameters used here are dimensionless, and within the range examined in this paper, the simulation results are not strongly sensitive to parameters such as the projection interval.
This is an advantage of the present projection scheme over divergence cleaning, especially in view of the recent comparison by
9
, who showed that divergence-cleaning calculations can depend on the adopted wave speed and damping timescale.

Although we have focused on SPMHD, the same operator-based projection idea should also be applicable, with appropriate modifications, to other meshless or moving-mesh MHD schemes such as GIZMO
[
3
]
and AREPO.
We therefore consider this projection scheme to be an attractive alternative to divergence cleaning for controlling numerical
\nabla\cdot\bm{B}
errors in particle and meshless MHD calculations.
