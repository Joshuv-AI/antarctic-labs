Executive Summary
The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) established the Concentrating Solar Power Generation 3
(CSP Gen3) program to promote the development of advanced CSP systems capable of
producing electricity at a levelized cost of energy (LCOE) less than $60/MWh, based on criteria
published in the CSP Gen3 Roadmap [1] and a subsequent funding opportunity announcement
(Gen3 FOA) [2]. The National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) was selected to lead the “Liquid
Pathway” project to meet these objectives. The Liquid Pathway proposed the use of low-cost
molten chloride salts for energy storage, mated with an operationally flexible solar receiver that
employs liquid-metal sodium for heat capture and transfer to the storage salt, as depicted in
Figure 1. The results of project Phases 1 and 2 were documented in a prior report [3].

 Figure 1. “Liquid Pathway” Gen3 system showing charging of thermal storage (left) and
 discharging thermal storage to the sCO2 power cycle (right). HEX = heat exchanger
 Graphic by Besiki Kazaishvili, NLR

A formal risk registry was developed and tracked during Phase 2. The risk registry captured
project risks across over 17 component and subsystem categories. The most significant risks
included the integrity of the tank liner, the performance of the salt valves, the impact of salt
vapor on system components, pressure- and chemical-sensor performance, and risks associated
with liquid-metal sodium use. The U.S. DOE did not select the Liquid Pathway for pilot-scale
demonstration under the Gen3 CSP program. However, a Phase 3 project was authorized to
evaluate the proposed internally lined molten salt tank, which became known as the LIMOSA
project. This report documents the findings under Phase 3 construction and testing of a small-
scale internally insulated molten salt tank.

 ix

LIMOSA was designed to de-risk the feasibility of an internally insulated tank for salt
containment. While a purely refractory internal liner was deemed unable to prevent salt wetting
of the insulation, the LIMOSA project promoted the use of a graphite barrier between the liner
hotface and underlying insulation layers to stop molten salt penetration. The LIMOSA team
constructed a small-scale tank that included a graphite-foil barrier within the refractory liner.
Despite encouraging bench-scale testing, the liner failed shortly after transferring 164 kg of
molten chloride salt into the tank. Transfer was made at 550°C and the failure was detected while
the tank was at 620°C, being heated on its way to the design point temperature of 720°C.

Subsequent analysis of the LIMOSA tank liner showed the graphite adhesive in the liner had
failed. Although the graphite foil itself appeared undamaged, the adhesive was cracked and
brittle, leading to salt intrusion to the underlying insulated layers. Testing of three different
graphite adhesives and two different graphite-foil sheet suppliers in molten chloride salt for 500
hours under controlled conditions showed the same behavior. One of the adhesives, Aremco 551
RN, performed better than the others, but it was still negatively affected impacted by the ~750°C
salt. Nonetheless, the use of graphite remains a promising approach for molten chloride
containment, and recommendations for future work include (1) switching to a high-temperature
adhesive that is resistant to salt attack, (2) moderate vacuum purging to ensure no residual
oxygen in the tank, and (3) introducing better cover gas mixing withing the tank during
preheating.

In other outcomes, two types of hotface mortar were tested, but as expected, the mortar joints
between the hotface bricks were fully permeated by salt, highlighting the importance of an
impermeable barrier.

Prior to failure, the tank heat loss and shell temperature were consistent with the targeted
performance for the design. The heat flux values ranged from about 200 to 300 W/m2 at the lid
and between 110 and 200 W/m2 at the tank bottom, as the temperature of the salt zone was raised
from 550°C to 620°C. The shell wall temperature remained below 60°C. However, the limited
duration of the experiment and the inability to reach the target operating temperature of 720°C
must be taken into consideration.

The LIMOSA project confirmed the presence of salt in cover-gas exhaust tubing, indicating it
will be critical to design containment and transfer systems for the presence of salt and possible
precipitation thereof from the vapor phase. The deposited material included Na, K, Mg, and Cl,
indicated all constituents of the ternary chloride entered the vapor phase; however, specific
crystal form was not determined. Lastly, no salt was detected in the fiber blanket insulation of
the tank lid, implying that the introduction of cover gas within the lid insulation and use of a thin
metal barrier at the face of the insulation successfully prevented salt vapor diffusion into the
insulation.

Background: Phase 1 & 2 Results
Salt Chemistry
The chloride salt purification protocols outlined in Figure 2 were developed in prior work. The
nominal salt composition by weight is 46% MgCl2, 39% KCl, and 15% NaCl. The salt is sourced
from industrial suppliers, notably ICL in Israel and Albemarle Chemicals in USA. Industrial
grade anhydrous carnellite (a mix of MgCl2, KCl, NaCl) is blended with additional halite (NaCl)
to produce the desired salt composition. A small amount of magnesium metal (~0.1 wt%) is
added during the melting process to remove impurities. Details on the salt properties and salt
melting protocols may be found in [4], [5], and [6].

 Figure 2. General salt melting and purification process [4].

Chloride Salt Chemical Sensor
With support from DOE, Argonne National Lab developed electrochemical sensors capable of
monitoring molten salts in a variety of industrial applications [7]. These sensors are comprised of
an array of electrodes immersed in the salt. These sensors can provide measurements of salt
composition, salt redox state, and the salt level in a variety of challenging environments,
including large-scale flow systems. Specific to the ternary chloride salt, Argonne developed
measurement capabilities for the salt potential, corrosion product concentrations (e.g., Cr 2+,
Fe2+), and salt impurity concentrations (O2-, MgOHCl). These measurements are essential to
monitor and control the corrosion potential of the salt with respect to containment alloys.

The Argonne sensors were successfully deployed in a variety of CSP-relevant equipment,
including use in three thermal convection loops and in Oak Ridge National Laboratory’s Facility
to Alleviate Salt Technology Risks (FASTR). The sensor installed in the FASTR purification
system was able to successfully monitor the challenging chemical environment throughout the
salt cleaning procedure and was able to provide positive endpoint determination for the process
[8]. A similar electrode array was acquired for the LIMOSA project.

Commercial Salt Tank Design
A major focus of Phases 1 and 2 work was developing a commercial-scale salt tank design. The
proposed refractory liner was a three-component design, composed of a dense, chemically
resistant brick at the salt interface (hotface), an insulating firebrick (IFB) layer, and a
microporous insulating board adhered to the inner tank shell. In the Phases 1 and 2 work, the
team analyzed both “hot wall” and “cold wall” designs before selecting a cold-wall design that is
consistent with use in the magnesium industry. In the selected cold wall design the refractory
liner must be kept free from salt penetration to allow for a carbon steel tank shell that is kept at
less than 100°C.

The proposed tank is butt-welded construction with a bottom-to-shell junction of conventional
construction, as shown in industry standards such as API Standard 650 Welded Steel Tanks for
Oil Storage (API 650). API 650 requires weld preheating for welds thicker than 1-1/4 inches
(31.7 mm). The tank design utilizes 1-1/4” plate for both the tank bottom and shell and avoids
the weld preheating requirement. Thermal expansion joints are included in the wall and floor to
allow the liner to expand to its hot dimensions without damaging the steel shell, which remains
at near-ambient temperature. Representative commercial tank dimensions are shown in Figure 3.
The mortar joints and thermal-expansion joints are considered the major risks in the tank design.
More detail on the selected design can be found in Turchi et al., 2021 [3].

 Figure 3. Representative commercial-scale tank design
 Image courtesy of JT Thorpe

The material at the interface with the molten salt, known as the “hotface,” is a critical
component. The hotface must be chemically compatible with the salt and prevent molten salt

 2

from wetting the underlying insulating layers. A variety of candidate hotface materials were
screened by the team and three hotface candidates completed refractory cup tests following
ceramic-industry protocols. Testing during Phase 2 selected Duro Type II brick for its superior
resistance to salt intrusion and excellent mechanical strength [9].

While Duro Type II brick demonstrates adequate thermal, chemical, and mechanical stability in
molten salt, the mortar used to join bricks is a point of weakness, as porosity in the hotface
mortar joints could allow salt to reach the underlying insulating firebrick layer. This was a major
risk, not adequately addressed during Phase 2. To address this risk, Phase 2 recommended a
small tank with full-size bricks and mortar joints be tested prior to any integrated system
demonstration.

Risk Registry
At the beginning of the Gen3 Liquid Pathway project, the team established a Risk Registry and
identified the expected areas of risk associated with the overall design. The purpose of the risk
registry was to identify and rank potential risk to the overall project goal of demonstrating a
system capable of achieving the DOE Gen3 goal for low-cost CSP. Two elements of significant
risk are the subject of the LIMOSA project.

Pilot-Scale Demonstration Primary Risks
Salt tanks. Integrity of the salt tanks depends upon the ability of the floor, wall, and roof
insulation to resist penetration by the salt or salt vapors. The hotface brick exhibited excellent
resistance to salt penetration and there was little concern regarding the effectiveness of the
hotface brick. However, the mortar joints and expansion joints remained an element of concern.
Experimental measurement of the brick/mortar composite and associated design work in Phase 2
led to better understanding of the thermomechanical stress in the tank and allowed the team to
significantly reduce the number expansion joints in the tank design. However, leakage through
an expansion or mortar joint could allow salt to reach the underlying insulating fire brick. In this
event, salt would wet the fire brick resulting in increased heat loss through the tank wall and
possible overheating of the steel shell. The resulting heat bloom would be easy to identify on the
surface of the tank but correcting the leakage would be difficult. The best means to address this
risk is to employ a liner that retains its insulating performance when wetted by salt; however, no
suitable material was found during the project’s work.

Salt vapor. Many major system risks were associated with the vapor pressure of the molten salt.
Condensing or freezing salt vapors threaten the bellows and operation of the salt valves and
necessitate complex balancing of the ullage gas pressure and temperature at the valves. Salt
vapor deposition in the ullage gas lines or sensor ports could lead to plugging and errant
operation of the system controls. Salt vapor deposition within the tanks’ roof insulation is a
potential threat to the insulating ability of the ceramic fiber blankets and could lead to excessive
heat loss and/or weight gain that impacts roof integrity.

Phase 3 Objectives
The Gen3 Liquid Pathway work of Phases 1 and 2 is documented in [3]. DOE elected not to
move forward with construction of the integrated-system design in Phase 3 but requested a
smaller project to derisk key elements of the molten salt storage tank. Accordingly, NLR
proposed construction and testing of a lined molten salt (LIMOSA) tank. A small-scale tank test
was designed to provide data on behavior of candidate lining systems during exposure to molten
salts. The objectives of this Phase 3 research included:

 • Fabricate a small (~1 cubic meter) refractory-lined tank following the design from Phase
 2. Charge and hold with molten salt at 720°C,
 • Measure thermal flux through the wall, floor, and roof to test and validate the thermal
 modeling and insulation,
 • Quantify salt vapor composition and concentration,
 • Assess impact of salt vapors on roof insulation,
 • Assess integrity of the liner,
 • Confirm if lining materials can withstand thermal expansion and stresses imposed as
 predicted, and
 • Assess the effects of temperature fluctuations

 4

System Conceptual Design
Liner Downselection
Phase 2 concluded that a simple brick and mortar hotface could not prevent salt from penetrating
to the underlying insulation layers. Hence, Phase 3 required refining the liner design to increase
the potential for success. Several potential solutions were explored at the outset of Phase 3.

Alloy Membrane Liner. In the 1980s a team from Sandia National Laboratories explored using
a metal membrane hotface with underlying insulating firebrick for a nitrate salt tank operating at
565°C [10]. Sandia collaborated with Gaz Transport & Technigaz North America (GTT-NA) to
leverage the design used by GTT-NA in commercial liquified natural gas (LNG) tanks. In 2022,
NLR contacted GTT-NA to revisit this approach for molten salt tanks given current knowledge.
During these discussions GTT-NA explained that they have considered, but been hesitant to use,
their liner design for high-temperature salt storage due to the combined stressors of heat and
corrosion on the metal liner. The alloy membrane design was not pursued due to these corrosion
concerns.

Geopolymer Liner. Under a separate award, NLR researched the use of geopolymers with
closed-cell porosity as wetted insulation for molten salts [11]. Figure 4 shows the conceptual
design of the tank wall and presents a few key considerations for the geopolymer insulation
layer.

 Figure 4. Schematic of the tank wall insulation design. The yellow dashed line indicates the
 molten salt/solid salt interface.
Referring to Figure 4, The “Molten salt saturated GP” layer will be partly saturated by the
molten salt and the “Dry GP buffer” will be free of molten salt. The molten-salt-saturated section
of the geopolymer needs to maintain its closed-cell porosity (formed by imbedded cenospheres)
over time to minimize loss of thermal insulation performance.

The “Dry GP buffer” layer is designed to stay below the salt freeze temperature and serves as a
buffer zone in case the heat transfer of the wall changes and the molten salt/solid salt interface
(the yellow dashed line in Figure 4) moves. The molten salt/solid salt interface is the most
important feature of the design because the frozen solid salt effectively self-contains the molten
salt. If there is enough physical space and temperature drop for the molten salt to freeze in the

 5

buffer region, molten salt will always be contained by its frozen counterpart. The containment
will “self-heal” if a crack develops inside the solid salt region by simply freezing the leaked
molten salt. The Dry GP buffer zone prevents molten salt from permeating into the highly
insulating yet fragile “Other internal insulation” layer (for example, insulating firebrick).

Around the molten/solid salt interface, there will be stresses associated with the volume change
between the molten and solid salt. For the nitrate salt, the volume change is about 4.6% [12]; for
the Gen3 chloride salt, the volume change is estimated to be about 20% [13]. This raised
concerns over the mechanical strength of the geopolymer around the interface especially when
the interface moves back and forth over time due to slight changes of heat transfer through the
tank wall/floor. Furthermore, the entire geopolymer insulation layer will be under thermal
stresses due to the temperature gradient (estimated to be around 200–500 K/m).

A key feature of this design is that when the molten salt permeates into pores of the geopolymer
material, it will still retain a reasonable thermal insulation performance because the salt trapped
inside small pores is stagnant and has a relatively low thermal conductivity of 0.4–0.5 W/m-K.
Unlike a design where salt permeation past the hotface must be avoided, this geopolymer-based
design is fail “safer” if the geopolymer insulation experiences more salt permeation than
expected.

The thermal conductivity of a tested dry geopolymer brick was measured to be around 0.4-0.55
W/m-K in the temperature range between room temperature and 750°C. After the brick was
immersed in molten nitrate salt for >1 hour with about 4% salt permeation (based on mass gain),
its thermal conductivity was measured again using the same ASTM method. The wet thermal
conductivity was very close to the dry thermal conductivity suggesting the impact of 4% salt
permeation is relatively negligible. Considering that the nitrate salt thermal conductivity is about
the same at ~0.5 W/m K, more salt permeation at 15-20 wt.% is not expected to significantly
change the wet thermal conductivity. For chloride salt with slightly lower thermal conductivity
compared to nitrate salt, the result is expected to be similar. Other properties such as mechanical
strength of the geopolymer bricks with molten salt saturation were also tested at high
temperatures. The results did not show significant degradation due to salt immersion. Therefore,
the geopolymer insulation remained a viable technical option for chloride-salt containment in the
LIMOSA tank liner test. However, the estimated cost of such a design was estimated at 1.5 to 2x
the estimated cost of the Phase-2-developed refractory brick liner, and work proceeded with that
concept.

Nonwetting Graphite Liner. To address the risk of salt wetting an insulating layer, researchers
at Purdue University proposed the inclusion of a liquid barrier layer. This concept places a non-
wetting layer between the hotface brick and the insulating firebrick, as illustrated in Figure 5.
This nonwetting layer is composed of nonpolar materials such as graphite powder that are porous
but do not allow salt to pass through. The design is like a concept explored by the Gen3 Liquid
team in Phase 2, when powdered NaCl was proposed as a barrier to salt penetration. The NaCl
layer functioned by creating a high local NaCl salt composition when contacted by the ternary
chloride salt that would create a freeze plug and stop salt penetration. Concern regarding the
longevity of this barrier due to diffusion of NaCl into the bulk salt ended pursuit of this design.
The Purdue concept avoids that issue by using a stable, insoluble graphite barrier. The patent-
pending approach [14] does not require the layer to be powdered graphite, but the Purdue

 6

research team recommended it since powdered graphite is generally cheaper than other forms of
the material.

Molten-chloride salts exhibit poor wettability with graphite and graphite has been used as
barriers in nuclear molten salt reactor applications [15]. This wettability or ability for the fluid to
infiltrate the substrate is determined by measuring the contact angle the fluid makes with the
solid surface, where contact angle >90° indicates poor wetting characteristics while <90°
indicates good wetting characteristics. The contact angle and resulting wettability of the graphite
depends on its surface morphology such as roughness, porosity, distribution, and heterogeneity.
Graphite also benefits from strong resistance to corrosion in molten salt solutions. Chemical
reactions between graphite and chloride salts are not thermodynamically favorable and graphite
maintains high strength at temperatures above 2500°C. However, graphite must be protected
from oxidizing conditions such as exposure to oxygen or nitrates at temperatures exceeding
about 500°C.

Molten salt can still infiltrate the powdered graphite if there is sufficient applied pressure to
initiate capillary suction. Infiltration resistance, and the corresponding threshold pressure
increases with smaller pore sizes and with higher contact angles and surface tension. Techniques
such as sealing, closing, or filling open pores on the graphite’s surface using coatings or
modifying the graphite’s microstructure have been used to improve the infiltration resistance
[15].

LIMOSA Tank Graphite Barrier
Graphite/salt compatibility
Graphite components and containment have been used with molten halides in several DOE
programs [18], where they find common usage as containment crucibles. As noted by [19], the
purity and porosity of graphite can vary significantly and can affect salt resistance.

Sure et al. [20] compared the resistance of different types of carbon materials to LiCl/KCl at
600°C and noted that the penetration and absorption of molten salt into graphitic structures can
proceed in different modes depending on the arrangement of graphite planes in the structure,
further noting that the degradation of graphite depends on the degree of structural order, surface
uniformity, preparation method and density of the material. Salt penetration into pores on the
surface of the graphitic structure can remove carbon from the material. The order of increasing
corrosion resistance to molten salt was reported as low-density graphite < high-density graphite
< glassy carbon < pyrolytic graphite.

Rubenstein and Davis [21] summarized that smaller graphite grain size allows for increased
density and strength due to better packing, lower binder fraction, and less porosity. All likely
contribute to the resistance of the graphite, and the greater mechanical strength of fine grain
graphite materials is thought to be associated with superior erosion resistance.

Perhaps most importantly, Lin et al. [22] demonstrated that the low porosity of graphite foil
(Grafoil™ GTJ) could protect more porous graphite materials from molten salt attack in
fluorides at 700°C. Lin further states that pyrolytic carbon has been shown to effectively seal
more-porous graphite from molten salts in at least three other studies. While Lin reports good
results with graphite foil’s ability to seal against molten salt, they note that the comparison of
sealing effectiveness against molten salts between pyrolytic carbon and the graphite foil used in
their study needs further assessment. Figure 8 shows a micrograph that highlights the lower
porosity of the graphite foil.

Graphite barriers are used in chemical systems and have been examined in nuclear power
research for containment in molten salt reactor systems. Xu et al. [23], suggests that thermal

Salt Melter/Purifier Design
The team leveraged the experience at Oak Ridge National Laboratories (ORNL) by copying the
successful chloride-salt melter deployed as part of ORNL’s FASTR facility [25]. Alloy C276’s
documented good corrosion resistance in chloride salt led to its selection and use in the ORNL
salt melter. The NLR melter will purify salt at temperatures approaching 700°C, and according to
ASME Boiler Pressure Vessel Code (BPVC) values, the thermal mechanical strength of
Hastelloy C276 exhibits a dramatic reduction above about 600-650°C. Mimicking the ORNL
design, NLR’s melter holds the salt in a C276 crucible inside a pressure vessel. However, NLR
opted for a stainless-steel vessel for pressure containment. The stainless vessel is internally
coated to provide corrosion resistance to acid and salt vapors. This change was triggered by a
lack of availability and the high cost of the C276 alloy. While we acknowledge concerns in the
use of C276 above 650°C for commercial applications as well as coated stainless steel for a
chloride-salt melter, we felt that the corrosion resistance and successful implementation by
ORNL for a limited-duty chloride salt melter justify its use in the LIMOSA application.

While patterned after the FASTR melter, the NLR system differed in several ways:

 • The pressure shell was fabricated from 316 stainless steel and coated internally to provide

 in the FASTR melter.
 • The overall internal height of NLR’s pressure vessel was approximately 50” (127 cm)
 versus approximately 88” (223 cm) of the FASTR unit, due to the lesser amount of
 molten salt required for the LIMOSA experiment.
 • The ports and lifting eyes differed between the two units based on the needs of the
 experiment, the main change that the NLR unit included an airlock fill port for salt
 addition based on recommendations from ORNL. The final layout and port assignments

Drain Vessel
The third vessel in the NLR system is a sacrificial drain tank. To minimize cost and complexity,
the LIMOSA experiment included no salt pumps. Salt was transferred to the LIMOSA tank by
pressurizing the melter. The LIMOSA tank was not a pressure vessel, so pressure extraction was
not an option, and a gravity drain port would compromise the internal liner. Accordingly, the
project purchased a vacuum-capable stainless tank to serve as a capture vessel for vacuum
extraction of molten salt from the LIMOSA tank at the conclusion of the experiment, see
Appendix for additional detail.

The drain tank lid was welded in place and ports added for connection to the LIMOSA tank and
a vacuum pump. The bottom port on the tank was also sealed, as the tank is designed for one-
time use. During the draining phase, the 30-gal drain tank is connected to the LIMOSA tank by
heat-traced ¾” (>500°C) tubing. Vacuum is pulled on the drain tank to draw most of the
approximate 27-gal inventory of molten salt from the LIMOSA tank into the drain tank. The
sacrificial tank is intended to be disposed of with the frozen salt.

LIMOSA Tank Salt-Level Sensor
NLR developed a sensor to measure the molten salt level in the LIMOSA tank. The designed salt
level is 1.8” (4.6 cm) below the lid insulation in the tank. Because of the variation in density of
the molten salt, a level sensor is needed to ensure the tank is not over filled. The molten salt level
indication also helps alert if the salt is leaking through the graphite barrier. Our team first
contacted Kevin Robb at ORNL, who referenced a quote for a commercial level sensor that was
out of our budget and long lead time. Further inquiries were unable to find a commercially
available alternative that is corrosive resistant to chloride salt and temperature rated to 700°C.
Additionally, because of the uncertainty in the salt density and the tank needing to be
hermetically sealed, tank weight or liquid level float sensors were deemed unreliable methods of
measuring the molten salt height. Therefore, other methods of measuring molten salt level were
sought, and an electrical resistivity method that has been explored for the nuclear industry was
selected. These sensors use the change in electric resistivity between probes to indicate the
presence or absence of salt at the sensor position. Figure 22 depicts the basic arrangement of a 3-
sensor array to indicate salt position in the LIMOSA tank relative to the target fill line.

 Figure 22. Level Sensor System Diagram
The design was based on Allazadeh et al. [26] and Sanga et al. [27] which used two probes to
determine molten salt presence based on an increase in current caused by a decrease in electrical
resistance as the salt probe is submerged in molten salt. Salt properties were referenced from
Zeng, Wang, and Wu [28] and Wang et al. [29]. A DC-power supply directs current to a voltage
divider between a 22k ohm resistor, and two probes submerged in the molten salt, while a
voltage meter measured the voltage across the probes. When the probes are not submerged in the
salt, the voltage meter reads the voltage of the DC power supply (~0 current). When the probes
are submerged in molten salt, the voltage drops close to zero as current more readily flows
through the conducting salt. A basic circuit diagram is shown in Figure 23.

 23

Figure 23. Circuit diagram of level sensors for determining salt column height in the LIMOSA tank
Each sensor was installed in two holes in a 4-bore alumina rod. The probes occupied two bores,
while the other two were used to bubble nitrogen across the probes. This prevents the buildup of
salt on the probes that could cause the voltage to return to the same as the power supply.
Additionally, when the probes are removed from the molten salt the nitrogen prevents salt from
bridging the gap between the probes.

The initial tests were run at less than 10 mV to reduce the ionization of the probes. The length of
the wires in the LIMOSA experimental setup required the voltage to be increased to 1 volt to
reduce the noise in the signal. To reduce the current across the probe, a 1k resistor was added in
parallel. The level sensors were first tested in molten chloride salt at 450°C. In experimental
testing the voltage was constant when submerged in the salt and increased when the probes were
removed seen in Figure 24. The voltage response after the probe is removed was immediate and
stabilized in a few minutes.

Gas Handling and System Scrubber
As indicated in Figure 2, the melter releases HCl and other gas-phase species during operation.
Two acid-gas scrubber systems were designed for the experiment: (1) the melter vented through
a 4-tank wet scrubber system patterned after the unit used by ORNL [24] and (2) a dry scrubber
bed intended to operate on the LIMOSA tank offgas. Tubing, valves, and quick connects allowed
the scrubbers to operate on the offgas from either tank. A system schematic is provided in the
Appendix.

The wet-scrubber vessels were 20-L Nalgene carboys. The first vessel served as an antisiphon
tank (no dip tube), while the gases were bubbled through each of the final three vessels in series.
The last two vessels were filled to the halfway mark with a saturated sodium bicarbonate
solution. Scrubber 2 was half-filled with deionized (DI) water at the start of the experiment. The
Antisiphon vessel (1st vessel) began empty.

Melting/Purification Phase
Electrode Array Debugging
The electrochemical array was used to monitor and confirm molten salt purity during Mg
addition. Mg is added to reduce the oxidation/reduction (redox) potential of the chloride salt to
less than 850 mV per a procedure pioneered by NLR and Savannah River National Laboratory.
However, during the initial scans of the salt after melting and prior to Mg addition, the
electrochemical sensor was unable to identify impurities in the molten salt. To explore the cause,
the NLR team performed a series of diagnostic tests:

 1. The Autolab potentiostat was carefully calibrated and tested with the calibration cell as
 well as in an aqueous electrolyte made of the carnallite salt dissolved in water. Both
 showed that the potentiostat was functioning properly.
 2. The electrical connections inside the electrochemical sensor as well as the cables
 provided by Argonne were carefully checked to eliminate any potential short-circuit in
 the wiring.
 3. The tungsten rods used for the electrochemical sensor were retrieved for visual
 inspection. The tungsten rods were also cleaned with deionized water and refurbished
 with sandpaper before the sensor was tested in molten salt again. The same scan results
 were obtained, indicating that the issue was not likely related to the quality of the
 tungsten electrodes.

 Figure 33. Cyclic voltammetry data of the unpurified molten salt showing unexplained
  discontinuity and change of sign at a working electrode potential of 0 V vs. tungsten reference.
Figure 33 shows the cyclic voltammetry data of the unpurified molten salt. Both linear sweep
voltammetry and cyclic voltammetry showed a discontinuous current jump with a rough change
of sign at about 0 V versus the voltage of the reference tungsten electrode. No obvious current
peaks representative of impurities peaks could be observed. The discontinuous current change
with a change of sign at 0 V is likely an indicator of certain spontaneous reactions happening at
the working electrode under an applied voltage. Due to the symmetry of the tungsten working
electrode and tungsten counter electrode, when the applied potential changes sign, it is possible
that the current generated from the spontaneous reactions shows the same change of sign.

 32

ORNL’s procedure preloaded some Mg in the melter before melting. However, due to prior
melter gasket damage, Mg was not pre-loaded into the melter with the carnallite salt and halite
salt because NLR Safety prohibited Mg addition (which may generate H2 gas) until the gasket
was proven airtight. It was speculated that the lack of Mg preloading may have led to a very
corrosive molten salt that reacted with the tungsten working electrode, causing the errant
behavior of the electrochemical probing of the salt. Ultimately, NLR removed the sensor from
the melter and proceeded with Mg purification without the sensor. This approach relied on
excess Mg to ensure purification, as described below.

Salt/Mg Fill Port Gasket Failure and Repair
The HCl alarm alerted the team to a leak in the melter, and it was discovered that a weld at the
salt-fill port had failed. It is presumed HCl released during the initial salt melting attacked the
weld, leaving the tube loose inside the top flange. While the salt-containing crucible in the melter
is C276 and the melter vessel is 316 SS with a protective coating, the flanges and some tubing
are uncoated 316 SS, due to a lack of availability of C276 fittings.

Upon removing the flange to repair the tubing weld, it was noted that the copper conflat gasket
used in the flange also showed signs of corrosion and leakage. Leakage in this region would have
provided an outlet for HCl from the melter (i.e., avoiding the scrubber) and brought a regular
flow of HCl to a location that should have been a static dead leg in the melter. The copper
gaskets on the magnesium addition port and the electrochemical sensor were replaced with
graphite gaskets, and a new flange was ordered, tapped, and 1-inch stainless pipe nipple
installed.

Melter Lid Gasket Failure & Repair
Following the fill-port repair, a 10-min positive pressure test was performed with compressed
nitrogen at 125% of the maximum operating pressure; however, a leak persisted. Removing the
insulation on top of the melter revealed it was discolored, suggesting gas leakage at the main
melter flange. Further investigation revealed multiple cracks in the main flange gasket, see
Figure 35.

Figure 35. Discolored insulation from the melter lid (left) and fractures in the melter gasket (right).
Subsequent investigation determined that an improper gasket was installed for the melter lid.
NLR specified a system temperature to 600°C; however, the vendor’s quote included a gasket
rated to a much lower temperature, which was not caught by NLR. Hence, the gasket used in the
melter was made from Garlock Thermoseal C-4401 (Klingersil), which is rated to only 750°F

 34

(~400°C) maximum temperature and then for only limited duration. The melter lid gasket failure
triggered a multi-week repair process.

The team ordered a replacement gasket made of graphite foil with a stainless-steel insert, given
graphite’s excellent chemical resistance to chloride molten salt and HCl. Graphite is thermally
stable above 800°C in a reducing environment, but only about 500°C in air. Given the melter’s
operating temperature approaches 600°C, the flange was to be only lightly insulated and
monitored to stay below 500°C. Replacing the lid gasket required lifting the lid; however,
several dip tubes were frozen in the salt at this time, see Figure 36. A safety review was required
to allow melting the salt with a leaking lid gasket due to the likely release of HCl vapor into the
area around the melter. Additional ventilation and worker protocols were instituted during this
process.

Salt Purification and Second Melter-Gasket Failure
NLR proceeded with Mg purification without the electrochemical sensor or RGA. Salt
purification was conducted similar to ORNL’s first [8] and second batch of purification [30],
where Mg rods and turnings were manually dropped into the melter at a regular intervals during
work hours. At a purification temperature less than 600°C (below magnesium’s melting point of

 38

650°C), the turnings are expected to provide more reactivity and purification power than Mg
rods due to their higher area-to-volume ratio. Based on the total amount of carnallite added to the
melter (about 270 kg) and the prescribed percentage of Mg addition (0.2% to 0.25% of the
carnallite weight)6, the targeted total Mg addition was 540–675 g.

Based on NLR’s lab scale experience, 0.20% to 0.25% of Mg addition always led to excess Mg
at the end of the purification, revealed by visual observation of metallic Mg particles in the
settled sludge. NLR introduced Mg rods and turnings into the salt melt across a span of about
eight days (70-g Mg rods on April 5, 250-g Mg turnings and another 250 g on April 9-10, and a
final 100-g Mg turnings on April 12). Hence ~670 g of Mg (i.e., about 2.5%) was added overall.
The salt settling step commenced at the end of April 12.

On April 15, after three days of settling, the team attempted to transfer salt to the LIMOSA tank
by pressurizing the Melter. This failed. Further inspection revealed that the melter’s main-flange
graphite gasket was not sealing and had largely disappeared due to oxidation in the ambient air.
A review of the preceding operations discovered that the flange had been insulated, resulting in
the gasket exceeding its maximum allowable temperature in air. The necessity of keeping the
flange below 500°C had not been adequately conveyed to the system operators.

Second Melter-Gasket Replacement, Salt Purity Confirmation, and Salt Transfer
In late April, the team received two melter replacement gaskets, one made of graphite as used
previously, and a second made of a phlogopite mica paper impregnated with an inorganic binder
and no carrier (Durlon HT1000 S90). The mica gasket is air-stable at higher temperature than the
graphite. Two options were ordered given uncertainty in delivery time. Both gaskets arrived at
approximately the same date and based on the previous successful installation of a graphite
gasket, the graphite option was selected. As before, the gasket was cut in a single location to
allow it to be maneuvered around the existing tubing and onto the flange face. The cut was
sealed with graphite adhesive and the lid lowered to seal the flange.

Once the melter lid was resealed, the team began the gradual process of remelting the salt. To
avoid a recurrence of oxidation of the graphite gasket, care was taken to monitor and maintain
the melter flange well below the maximum air-stable temperature of the graphite (~500°C).
Removing some insulation from the flange kept the air-exposed gasket edge at less than 200°C.
The salt needed to be liquid before the bubbler and transfer tubing could be installed and
connected. After installation of the tubing, the melter was successfully pressure tested, indicated
a good seal with the lid gasket and tubing fittings.

Finally, the potentiostat array was reinserted and scans initiated on the molten salt. The team
hypothesized that prior issues with the sensor readings may have resulted from the salt being far
from the target purity level and proposed to retest the salt now that it had undergone Mg
addition. A scan between -1.2 and 0.2 V (starting from 0V, to 0.2 V, down to -1.2 V and back to
0 V) gave no indication of peaks in the region above -1.1 V, suggesting the salt purification
process had been successful, see Figure 39 and Figure 40.

Dwell Phase
Salt was transferred to the LIMOSA tank at 550°C to minimize stress on the system tubing and
fittings. Over a span of several weeks, the LIMOSA tank had been preheated to 720°C and
reduced to 550°C for salt transfer. During this preheating, substantial moisture was driven from
the liner.

 41

The day following salt transfer a dipstick measurement of the salt level confirmed a depth of
18.2” of salt in the LIMOSA tank. At the time the dipstick measurement was made, Level Sensor
2 was no longer reading salt. This was not deemed a concern, as it was expected to lose a
fraction of an inch in salt height as the salt permeated into the porous hotface mortar joints,
which would be recovered as the salt expanded upon heating to the target temperature of 720°C.

With a charge of salt transferred to the LIMOSA tank, the team began to gradually increase salt
temperature towards the 720°C target while monitoring the heater power, internal temperatures,
and surface temperatures of the tank. The latter was augmented by daily infra-red (IR) images of
the tank shell. Monitoring of the tank had progressed only a few days when Level Sensor 1
indicated it was no longer reading salt. A dipstick test on May 23 revealed that the salt level
within the hotface chamber was at only 2.5 in. The low salt level meant the graphite barrier had
failed, and most of the salt had leaked into the insulating firebrick. There had been little
indication of this in the thermal images. After confirming the measurements, the team shut down
the heaters and allowed the tank to cool, thus ending the Dwell Phase.

Figure 41 shows the internal temperature readings and the power readings for the LIMOSA tank
during the Dwell period. The thermocouple tree within the LIMOSA tank consisted of three
thermocouples: the top was located within the insulated lid zone, while the center and bottom
thermocouples were in the salt zone. The center and bottom tree temperatures are equivalent and
show changes in salt temperature as the immersion heater setpoint was raised, which is also
apparent from the spike in total heater power. Total power increased from about 1450 W to 1650
W between May 14 and May 20, while the salt temperature increased from approximately 550°C
to 577°C following a 25 K setpoint increase on May 16. The heater setpoint was increased 50 K
on May 21, and further increased by 25 K on May 22. The resulting salt-zone temperature
stabilized at about 620°C, and heater power was approximately 2050 W when the experiment
was terminated.

LIMOSA Tank Liner Deconstruction and Analysis
Leakage of the graphite barrier led to the shutdown of the experiment ten days after salt filling.
The LIMOSA tank cooled to ambient over three weeks. The team pulled the melter vessel and
LIMOSA tanks out of the container, removed the tank lids, and re-inserted the tanks into the
container for further inspection. The critical question at this point was where and why the
graphite liner failed. (See Figure 17 for a reminder of the liner dimensions.)

One hotface brick was chiseled from the cylindrical liner and the remaining bricks were pried out
in sets of two or more to preserve the mortar joints between the bricks. The hotface bricks were
removed from the underlying graphite without destroying the graphite layer. The graphite barrier
was constructed of three overlapping layers of graphite sheet with graphite adhesive between the
layers. The exposed graphite foil sheets appeared intact, while the adhesive between the layers
cracked and flaked off, allowing the graphite sheets to separate.

Figure 42 shows the condition of the tank liner when the lid was removed from the tank. Salt was
found on top of the liner, under the thin layer of insulation that covered the top of the bricks. Salt
in this location indicates that salt vapors were permeating the headspace in the tank and
condensing and solidifying as they moved between the bricks and lid insulation. Corrosion is
also seen on the tank shell, which could be from salt vapors or acid gases. The black coloration
extending radially outward from the salt chamber may also indicate degassing from the graphite
adhesive.

500-h Graphite/Salt Exposure Test
Although literature and short-duration testing indicated good compatibility between graphite and
molten chloride salt, the rapid failure of the graphite barrier motivated a closer examination of
salt/graphite compatibility. NLR fabricated small graphite-foil coupons that were sent to SNL to
be submerged in molten ternary-chloride salt at for 500 hours, using a system developed for
molten-salt exposure testing [31].

Samples consisted of 1”x1”x1/8” flexible graphite coupons provided by Graphite Store. Samples
were differentiated by the adhesive used to create stacked samples of layered and adhered
flexible graphite coupons. Samples without adhesive were also included. If molten salt is
absorbed by the graphite or the graphite or adhesive deteriorates after being exposed to salt, it is
deemed unsuitable to be used as a tank liner.

A complication arose prior to testing when SNL’s two-zone furnace required maintenance, and a
smaller batched corrosion test vessel was employed. This vessel uses a 4.4-kW, single zone
heater, with a capacity of 0.5 gallons. Due to its reduced capacity, coupon testing was split into
two separate test series of 12 samples each, with triplicate samples from four different sample
types.

500-h Graphite/Salt Exposure Summary
In summary, the Aremco 551 RN adhesive performed the best of the three tested adhesives, but
all showed damage and cracking when exposed to the ternary chloride salt at 765°C for 500
hours. In contrast, the graphite foil coupons without adhesive appeared relatively unaffected, at
least on their planar surfaces. Indication of salt intrusion or impact was seen on the edges.
Determining a stable adhesive to bond graphite foil sheets and protect exposed edges is an
essential need for use of graphite foil as a barrier to the molten salt.

Discussion and Conclusions
The goal of the LIMOSA project was to derisk the design of an internally insulted molten
chloride salt tank and gain a better understanding of the challenges associated with the presence
of vapor-phase salt. The specific objectives were:

 1. Fabricate a small (~1 cubic meter) refractory-lined tank following the design from Phase
 2. Charge and hold with molten salt at 720°C,
 2. Measure thermal flux through the wall, floor, and roof to test and validate the thermal
 modeling and insulation,
 3. Quantify salt vapor composition and concentration,
 4. Assess impact of salt vapors on roof insulation,
 5. Assess integrity of the liner,
 6. Confirm if lining materials can withstand thermal expansion and stresses imposed as
 predicted, and assess the effects of temperature fluctuations

LIMOSA Tank Fabrication
The prototype LIMOSA tank was fabricated following the basic design developed under Phase 2
with the key addition of a graphite foil barrier added between the hotface brick and the
underlying IFB. The tank design is described in Sections 0 and 0. The tank was charged with 164
kg of ternary chloride salt at 550°C and heated to 620°C before the liner failed and the
experiment was terminated.

LIMOSA Tank Heat Loss
At equilibrium, the heater input power equals the heat loss from the tank. The 2-kW heat loss
aligns with our pre-experiment estimate of 1.96 kW, based on the expected thermal performance
of the liner calculated during project Phase 2. The close match is likely fortuitous because the
heat loss estimates were based on the wall/floor design and did not account for the different lid
insulation. Nonetheless, the liner appeared to be performing well with respect to insulating
ability. Throughout the dwell period, the external tank wall temperatures generally remained
below the target of 60°C. Tank bottom temperatures reached 73°C, while the hottest location
imaged from the tank was the immersion heater ports on the tank lid. Representative thermal
images of the tank are shown in Figure 66.

Figure 67 displays the sensor readings from the exterior shell of the LIMOSA tank. In addition to
thermocouples mounted on the top, bottom, and wall of the tank, heat flux sensors were mounted

seen to fluctuate with ambient temperature at the site. Temperature and heat flux are higher at the
tank bottom than on the lid. The heat flux values ranged from about 200 to 300 W/m2 at the lid
and between 110 and 200 W/m2 at the tank bottom as the temperature of the salt zone was raised
from 550°C to 620°C (temperatures shown in Figure 41). While these heat flux measurements
seem consistent with the pre-experiment estimate of 270 W/m2 for the liner design, one must
consider the opposing effects that the tank internals only reached 620°C vs. the design point of
720°C, while leakage through the barrier would have reduced insulating ability and increased
heat flux.

Salt Vapor Impact on LIMOSA Tank Lid Insulation
The design of the LIMOSA tank lid introduced the nitrogen cover gas within the ceramic fiber
blanket lid insulation. The gas diffused through the insulation into the main salt chamber of the
tank before exiting through an exhaust port in the salt chamber headspace (refer to Figure 7). The
purpose was to maintain a slight positive pressure of cover gas in the insulation to minimize salt
vapor intrusion and salt deposition.

Figure 68 shows the inverted LIMOSA tank lid with the protective C276 plate removed. Cover
gas entered within the insulation layer at the location marked by a blue flag (right side of image).
The cover gas exited through a tube that extended below the C276 plate into the headspace of the
salt chamber at the location marked by the white flag. Eight samples of insulation were taken
from the surface (immediately under the C276 cover plate) and approximately 1” deep within the
insulation layer. The samples were leached with DI water and water tested for chloride. No salt
was detected within the samples. At least for the short duration of the test, the cover gas flow
appeared to prevent salt vapor intrusion into the lid insulation.

Integrity of the LIMOSA Tank Liner
As described Section 0, shortly after filling the LIMOSA tank with molten salt the salt penetrated
the graphite barrier and wetted the underlying IFB, leading to termination of the experiment.
Examination of the graphite barrier indicated that the graphite foil itself was unwetted by the salt
and was in good condition. In contrast, the graphite adhesive was brittle and fractured throughout
the barrier structure, including zones that were never contacted by molten salt. The appearance of
the graphite adhesive and the rapid failure of the barrier suggests the adhesive was attacked by
residual oxygen or moisture within the LIMOSA tank during the ~4 months when the tank was
held at temperature awaiting transfer of the chloride salt.

The AREMCO 551-RN graphite adhesive used in the LIMOSA barrier is rated to almost 3000°C
in reducing environments—as is the graphite foil itself. However, in air the temperature limit
drops to 510°C for the graphite foil. Per the manufacturer, 551-RN is recommended for reducing
environments only; no temperature limit is given for applications in air, although the cure
temperature is 260°C. An alternative graphite adhesive, Graphi-Bond 669, while not as strong as
551-RN is suitable for oxidizing conditions up to 760°C.

The use of molten chloride salts requires a reducing environment for the salt. Small incursions of
oxygen would lead to the formation of MgO and increased corrosivity in the salt. This can be
addressed by maintaining salt redox potential. However, the presence of oxygen within the tank
can permanently damage the integrity of the graphite barrier if the adhesive or graphite foil is
oxidized. The examination of the failed liner suggests that the graphite adhesive was prone to
oxidation at the preheating temperature of tank due inadequate purging of air. Recommendations
for future include (1) switching to an adhesive that is more resistant to oxidation (e.g., Graphi-
Bond 669), (2) moderate vacuum purging of the tank to extract residual oxygen from the system,
and (3) introducing cover gas directly into the salt chamber during preheating.

The bottom joint of the graphite barrier was particularly vulnerable due to the 90-degree angle
formed by the barrier wall and floor. While a commercial-scale tank would not have such an
abrupt angle in the graphite-foil barrier due to the radial curve applied to the ceramic bricks at

 69

the tank floor, the curvature and need for expansion joints in the commercial tank require their
own verification.

Evaluation of Thermal Expansion and Temperature Fluctuations
Failure of the LIMOSA liner shortly after the tank filling prevented the planned evaluation of the
liner’s ability to withstand salt-temperature fluctuations.

Conclusions
The “LIMOSA” project was Phase 3 of the larger Gen3 Liquid Pathway project to investigate the
potential of ternary chloride salts for ~720°C thermal energy storage. LIMOSA was designed to
explore the feasibility of an internally insulated tank for salt containment, which was identified
as one of the major risk factors in the Gen3 Liquid Pathway concept. While a purely refractory
internal liner was unable to prevent salt wetting of the insulation, the LIMOSA project explored
the use of a graphite-based barrier to stop molten salt penetration. The LIMOSA team
constructed a small-scale tank that included a graphite-foil barrier within the refractory liner.
Despite encouraging bench-top testing, the liner failed shortly after transferring 164 kg of molten
chloride salt into the tank. Transfer was made at 550°C and the failure was detected while the
tank was at 620°C, being heated on its way to the design point temperature of 720°C.

Subsequent analysis of the LIMOSA tank liner showed that, although the graphite foil itself was
undamaged, the adhesive was cracked and brittle, leading to salt intrusion to the underlying
insulated layers. The use of graphite remains a promising approach for molten chloride
containment, and recommendations for future work include (1) identifying a high-temperature
adhesive that is more resistant to salt attack, (2) moderate vacuum purging of the tank to
eliminate residual oxygen from the system, and (3) introducing better cover gas mixing within
the tank during preheating.

Two types of hotface mortar were tested, but as expected, the mortar joints between the hotface
bricks were fully permeated by salt, highlighting the importance of an impermeable barrier.

Prior to failure, the tank heat loss and shell temperature were consistent with the targeted
performance for the design. The heat flux values ranged from about 200 to 300 W/m2 at the lid
and between 110 and 200 W/m2 at the tank bottom as the temperature of the salt zone was raised
from 550°C to 620°C. The shell wall temperature remained below 60°C. However, the limited
duration of the experiment and the inability to reach the target operating temperature of 720°C
must be taken into consideration.

The LIMOSA project confirmed the presence of salt in cover-gas exhaust tubing, indicating it
will be critical to design containment and transfer systems for the presence of salt and possible
precipitation thereof from the vapor phase. The deposited material included Na, K, Mg, and Cl,
indicated all constituents of the ternary chloride entered the vapor phase; however specific
crystal form was not determined. Despite this, no salt was detected in the fiber blanket insulation
of the tank lid, implying that the introduction of cover gas within the lid insulation and use of a
thin metal barrier at the face of the insulation successfully prevented salt vapor diffusion into the
insulation.

MATERIAL SPECIFICATIONS

The general classifications of the selected refractory LIMOSA Tank lining materials are listed and the properties
favorable to chloride salt service and or insulating properties are discussed:

Acid Resistant Brick ASTM C275 Type II: Acid bricks are well known for their compatibility to acidic and severe
corrosive environments. Acid bricks have low porosity and low permeability.

Insulating Fire Brick ASTM C155 Group 23: Insulating fire brick with a maximum service temperature of 2300°F.
This type of brick can provide superior thermal insulation that contributes to significant energy savings at a
relatively low cost. Key features of an insulating fire brick are a reduction in weight, high porosity, very low
thermal conductivity to reduce heat absorption, and high hot compressive strength.

Microporous: Microporous insulation is a material that has an extremely low thermal conductivity coefficient
giving it very good insulating properties. Used generally as the outer most layer in the refractory liner design
since it is used mainly to decrease heat transition as it is designed to have no gaps. A significant temperature
drop can be achieved with a much smaller thickness when compared to other refractory materials.

2300F Ceramic Fiber: Ceramic fiber is a lightweight material that is generally used in areas where mechanical
abuse and contact with the process is not likely. This specific type of blanket has a maximum service
temperature of 2300°F. Ceramic fiber typically is easier to install and is lightweight while still providing very
good thermal insulation and corrosion resistance in high temperature services.

Mortar DURO Type II Powder/STEULER Acid Cement Powder SO mixed with STEULER Silica MH and STEULER
Acid Cement Hardener T: Mortar selection is experimental and is intended to resist salt permeation and loss in
the refractory system.

Flexible Graphite: Flexible graphite is manufactured from mineral graphite (expandable flake graphite). As its
name indicates, flexible graphite is flexible. Because it is compressible and conformable it is an ideal material for
gasketing and fluid sealing applications. It has excellent chemical resistance; specifically repelling chloride salt
intended to create an impermeable barrier and works well to temperatures up to 5432°F in reducing
atmosphere.
