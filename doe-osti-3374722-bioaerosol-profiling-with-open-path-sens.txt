1.0 Summary
 BIOPOP, Bioaerosol Profiling with Open Path Sensors, was designed to characterize coarse and
 super-coarse biological aerosol particles in the atmospheric boundary layer using the U.S. Department of
 Energy (DOE) Atmospheric Radiation Measurement (ARM) User Facility’s tethered balloon system
 (TBS). The campaign period was 2025-05-28 through 2025-08-31 and involved the third ARM Mobile
 Facility (AMF3) operations at Bankhead National Forest (BNF) in Alabama. The analysis summarized
 here focuses on the June and August 2025 BNF intensive operating periods (IOPs).

 The motivating measurement problem of this campaign was that pollen, fungal spores, and related
 biological fragments commonly occur in the coarse aerosol mode (1-10 μm) and super-coarse mode
 (>10 μm). These particles are climatically and ecologically important but are difficult to sample
 quantitatively through traditional aerosol inlets designed primarily for accumulation-mode and sub-10-μm
 aerosol. During the first planned BNF IOP of the related DOE Facilities Integrating Collaborations for
 User Science (FICUS) project, visual evidence confirmed pollen in the lower atmosphere, while
 inlet-based instruments such as the wideband integrated bioaerosol sensor (WIBS) and aerodynamic
 particle sizer (APS) did not identify a corresponding coarse-mode signal. This indicated that inlet
 transmission losses were likely a controlling limitation for large biological particles.

 BIOPOP addressed this limitation by deploying open-path optical sensors on the TBS. The Mesa
 Photonics cloud droplet measurement system (CDMS) and video ice particle sampler (VIPS) were
 selected because they image or size particles directly in ambient air without relying on inlet transmission.
 The Grimm 11-D optical particle counter (OPC) was requested to provide aerosol number size
 distributions from 0.25 to 35 μm with an inlet designed for improved super-micron particle transmission.
 Together, CDMS, WIBS, OPC, and co-located inlet-based measurements were intended to quantify
 particle count, size, and shape and to identify where conventional inlet-based measurements undercount
 the coarse biological population. The campaign objectives were to (1) detect and characterize coarse
 biological particles such as pollen and fungal spores during mid- and late-summer periods when
 bioaerosol concentrations were expected to be still high, (2) assess the vertical distribution of coarse
 bioaerosols in the boundary layer and their possible relevance to clouds, and (3) evaluate the limitations
 of inlet-based aerosol instrumentation by comparing co-located measurements under conditions where
 inlet losses were expected to be most significant.

 Preliminary BNF results indicate that surface WIBS measurements captured fluorescence properties and
 diurnal cycles consistent with bioaerosol-relevant periods. TBS profiles showed that coarse-mode particle
 concentrations were generally low through most of the vertical profile and were often largest near the
 surface. Periods of enhanced coarse aerosol sometimes corresponded to fluorescent-particle signals, but
 coarse fluorescent particles were generally rare aloft. These results support the central BIOPOP premise:
 open-path sensors are necessary for evaluating particles larger than 10 μm and for diagnosing when
 inlet-based bioaerosol measurements miss or distort the largest biological aerosol population.

2.0 Campaign Design and Measurement Approach
 The BIOPOP proposal was structured around three TBS deployment windows: the second IOP of the
 Southeastern U.S. ARM- Environmental Molecular Sciences Laboratory (EMSL) FICUS project at BNF

 1

in June 2025, and the third BNF FICUS IOP in August 2025. The AMF3 BNF periods targeted a forested
Southeastern United States environment. This design allows the same open-path measurement concept to
be evaluated across distinct biological-source and meteorological regimes.

The scientific focus was the vertical distribution of bioaerosols, including bacteria, fungi, viruses, pollen,
spores, and fragments of biological material. Bioaerosols affect cloud formation, disease transmission,
biogeochemical cycling, and ecosystem-atmosphere exchange. Their surface abundance is much better
characterized than their altitude distribution, particularly for coarse particles that are difficult to transport
through inlets and tubing. The BIOPOP payload was therefore designed to measure large particles under
ambient, open-path conditions and to compare those measurements with conventional inlet-based aerosol
instrumentation. The specific campaign objectives were:
1. Detect and characterize coarse biological particles, including pollen and fungal spores, under ambient
 conditions during mid- and late-summer periods when bioaerosol concentrations are expected to peak.
2. Assess the vertical distribution of coarse-mode bioaerosols in the boundary layer and evaluate their
 potential relevance to cloud and climate processes.
3. Evaluate limitations of inlet-based aerosol instrumentation, including APS and WIBS measurements,
 by comparing co-located measurements and identifying conditions where inlet losses are most
 significant.

Instrument Payload

The core BIOPOP payload combined open-path imaging and sizing with inlet-based size distributions.
CDMS and VIPS target approximately 10-1000-μm particles and are therefore directly relevant for pollen
and large fungal-spore aggregates that are not expected to be transmitted efficiently through conventional
aerosol inlets. The Grimm 11-D OPC provides a bridge between conventional aerosol sizing and the
larger particle range by measuring 0.25-35-μm particles. Additionally, WIBS provided fluorescence,
sizing, and continuity with established aerosol measurements. The WIBS was deployed both on the TBS
and the surface to provide context to the airborne measurements.

3.0 Preliminary Results

 3.1 Surface Particle Fluorescence at BNF
 Surface WIBS measurements during the June and August 2025 BNF periods captured intervals with
 elevated fluorescent-particle concentrations and fluorescence-channel behavior consistent with
 bioaerosol-relevant particles. These surface measurements provide the timing context for TBS flights and
 allow vertical profiles to be interpreted relative to near-surface source activity. The fluorescence response
 was multi-channel and variable, consistent with the known complexity of pollen and other biological
 aerosol fluorescence [1]. Fluorescence is therefore treated as a bioaerosol-relevant proxy rather than a
 definitive biological identifier. The campaign interpretation relies on convergence among fluorescence,
 size, open-path detection, morphology, and vertical context.

 Figure 1. Surface WIBS time series during BNF III in June 2025 and BNF IV in August 2025. Surface
 fluorescent-particle periods provide context for TBS profile interpretation.

 The surface WIBS measurements show diurnal cycles in fluorescent-particle abundance during bioaerosol
 periods. This structure is consistent with a combination of biological source timing, surface-atmosphere
 exchange, and boundary-layer evolution. Diurnal timing is operationally important because a strong
 surface bioaerosol signal does not necessarily imply vertical redistribution through the boundary layer and
 TBS profiles are needed to determine whether the particles remain near the surface or are mixed aloft.
 The size and shape distributions associated with fluorescent classes provide additional discrimination
 beyond concentration alone. Pollen and fungal spores are not expected to behave as a single optical or
 size class, so interpretation requires considering fluorescence channel, particle size, and nonsphericity
 together.

3.2 TBS Vertical Profiles at BNF
TBS vertical profiles from June and August indicate that WIBS coarse-mode particle concentrations were
generally low in the boundary-layer profiles, commonly below about 100 particles per cubic centimeter
except very close to the surface. This pattern suggests that, during the sampled conditions, the coarse
aerosol population was often concentrated near the surface rather than efficiently mixed throughout the
lower atmosphere.

Some periods of increased coarse-particle concentration within the profiles corresponded to
fluorescent-particle signals, but the abundance of coarse fluorescent particles aloft was generally low.
This is a critical result for bioaerosol-cloud interpretation: surface bioaerosol activity was detectable, but
vertical export of fluorescent coarse particles was not persistently strong during the observed cases in the
mid- to late summer.

3.3 June 16 Case: Paired WIBS and CDMS Measurements
The June 16 TBS case provides a direct example of the BIOPOP strategy. WIBS and CDMS were
operated together so that fluorescence information from the inlet-based sensor could be compared against
open-path measurements of larger particles. This pairing is the key test of whether particle layers are
visible to conventional fluorescence measurements, open-path sizing, or both.

The case demonstrates the practical value of open-path measurements. For particles larger than
approximately 10 μm, the absence of a strong inlet-based fluorescence signal does not necessarily imply
absence of pollen or other biological particles: it may reflect inlet losses. CDMS helps to address this
ambiguity by observing large particles in ambient air before they encounter inlet-transmission constraints.

4.0 Synthesis and Next Analysis Steps
 BIOPOP directly addresses a measurement gap in vertical bioaerosol observations. Large biological
 particles can be present in the lower atmosphere while remaining poorly represented in inlet-based
 measurements. By combining CDMS, OPC, WIBS, and TBS vertical profiling, the campaign provides a
 framework for determining when the absence of a fluorescent signal represents true atmospheric absence
 and when it reflects size-dependent sampling losses.

 The BNF results support four preliminary findings: (1) surface aerosol during bioaerosol periods exhibits
 fluorescence properties and diurnal cycles consistent with biological particle influence, (2) the vertical
 profiles sampled during June and August generally contained low concentrations of coarse fluorescent
 particles aloft, implying limited vertical redistribution under the observed conditions, (3) elevated
 coarse-particle layers can occur without a strong fluorescence signature, so biological attribution requires
 more than particle size, and (4) open-path sensors are essential for particles larger than 10 μm and should
 be treated as a necessary complement to inlet-based fluorescence and sizing instruments for
 pollen-focused vertical-profile studies.

 Remaining priorities are to complete quantitative CDMS analysis; compare CDMS particle detections
 against WIBS fluorescence classes and OPC size distributions; evaluate inlet losses by size, particle type,
 and flight condition; and integrate the BNF data sets to determine how ecosystem type and
 boundary-layer structure control the vertical distribution of bioaerosol-relevant particles.
