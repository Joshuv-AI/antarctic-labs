Embodied morality in a disembodied world: Dishonesty in real online interactions
Authors: Luca Provenzano, Marina Scattolin, Giuseppina Porciello, Maria Serena Panasiti, Riccardo Domenico Saulle, Giuseppe Ciccarone, Salvatore Maria Aglioti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Increasing evidence suggests that morality is linked to bodily self-consciousness (BSC), yet the factors shaping dishonest behaviour in competitive and ecologically valid interactions remain unclear. To address this, we developed a smartphone dice game app (“Roll & Tell”) in which participants engaged in 60 online matches against either Facebook friends or unknown opponents. Participants could misreport their dice outcomes to increase their chances of winning and obtain a monetary reward based on a global leaderboard. Results showed that participants deviated from the Nash equilibrium, as they did not consistently report the highest possible outcome. Instead, dishonest behaviour was flexibly modulated by contextual factors. Participants lied more when holding a greater scoring advantage, particularly when the game outcome had already been decided, and higher-ranking individuals showed a greater tendency to lie. Moreover, the effect of competitive advantage was amplified when playing against Facebook friends, suggesting that social closeness may increase competition. At the individual level, the relationship between BSC and dishonest behaviour was moderated by perceived sense of power. Higher Sense of Agency was associated with more honest behaviour only at low levels of perceived power, whereas this relationship disappeared at higher levels. No significant effects were observed for Sense of Ownership, reward sensitivity, or moral disengagement. Overall, dishonesty in online competitive environments appears to emerge from the interaction between strategic incentives, social context, and embodied self-processes. These findings highlight the value of ecologically valid paradigms for studying embodied morality in real-world interactions.

Introduction

Bodily self-consciousness (BSC) serves as the cornerstone of self-awareness, stemming from the integration of sensory inputs from within and outside the body (Berlucchi & Aglioti, 2010). BSC encompasses two key components: the sensation of possessing a physical body, referred to as the sense of ownership (Blanke, 2012), and the feeling of controlling one's bodily actions, referred to as the sense of agency (Haggard, 2017). Embodied cognition research (Barsalou, 2008) shows how basic bodily signals (e.g., interoceptive cues such as hunger, thirst, or heartbeat) and higher-order bodily representations (such as SoO and SoA) can influence higher-level mental processes. Evidence that cognition is embodied comes from domains such as visual stimuli processing (Urgesi et al., 2004), action recognition (Moro et al., 2008; Urgesi et al., 2007), and emotional activation (de Gelder, 2006; Tamietto & de Gelder, 2010).

More recently, studies have investigated the role of the body in decisions involving honesty or deception. Lying and dishonesty are common components of daily life (DePaulo et al., 1996) and are widely diffused across various societies and cultures (Mann et al., 2016). Besides affecting individuals’ lives and relationships, dishonest behaviour can have widespread societal consequences. Bribery and corruption, for instance, can produce huge economic damage (Dreher & Herzfeld, 2005; European Court of Auditors., 2019), with implications for public welfare (Welsch, 2008) and healthcare systems (Li et al., 2018). This is particularly relevant today, when dishonest behaviour can often be enacted in technologically mediated, disembodied contexts, ranging from online fraud to anonymous communication or remote business transactions (Button et al., 2025; Cross, 2019; Starke et al., 2016).

Studies indicate that bodily signals may be associated with increased immoral or dishonest behaviour. For example, people tend to make more egoistic offers during the ultimatum game when bodily signals are made more salient by listening to their own heartbeat (Lenggenhager et al., 2013) or by receiving a painful stimulation (Mancini et al., 2011). Similarly, hungry and thirsty individuals are more likely to behave dishonestly to gain a reward that could alleviate their deprived state (Williams et al., 2016). These results show that, as SoO increases due to a heightened relevance of one's own bodily signals, so does the occurrence of dishonest behaviour, possibly because a stronger egocentric bodily anchoring drives individuals' decision-making (Paulus, 2007). However, the relationship between SoO and moral behaviour is not as straightforward as it would appear. A recent study investigating dishonest behaviour found that high SoO was positively correlated with high moral identity, as assessed via questionnaires, suggesting a positive relationship between SoO and morality (Scattolin, Panasiti, & Aglioti, 2022). Furthermore, a second study employing immersive virtual reality to manipulate participants' SoO during the “Temptation to Lie” card game—a task in which individuals can deceive others about card outcomes to increase their monetary gains (Azevedo et al., 2018; Panasiti et al., 2011, 2014, 2016) - showed that, when participants' SoO was reduced, participants produced more egoistic lies particularly when the monetary reward was high (Scattolin, Panasiti, Villa, & Aglioti, 2022).

Interestingly, SoA also positively correlates with moral behaviour, being enhanced for moral actions (Moretto et al., 2011) and reduced for immoral ones (Caspar et al., 2018). Moreover, SoA may interact with broader constructs, such as moral disengagement—defined as the detachment of moral self-sanctions from one's own harmful actions (Bandura et al., 1996) — and sense of power, understood as the perceived ability to influence others (Anderson et al., 2012). These two constructs can act as psychological moderators of the relationship between SoA and dishonest behaviour. While moral disengagement negatively moderates the link between SoA and moral behaviour, sense of power can have adverse or positive effects, as the behavioural disinhibition associated with a high sense of power can either lead to more egocentric behaviour (Lammers et al., 2015) or to an easier adherence to one's moral identity (Scattolin, Panasiti, & Aglioti, 2022).

The present study

Taken together, the existing literature suggests a complex relationship between bodily self-consciousness and moral behaviour, which has been primarily investigated in controlled laboratory settings. In this study, we aimed to investigate the relationship between BSC and dishonest behaviour in a more ecologically valid context. Specifically, our aim was to investigate morally relevant decision-making during online social interactions, spanning multiple sessions and conducted on participants’ own mobile devices. Since most individuals interact with both strangers and acquaintances in online environments—exchanging personal views and opinions as well as engaging in business and economic transactions— we thought it was important to understand whether the relationship between morality and our bodily anchored self endures when the self is digitally extended and disembodied (Galanxhi & Nah, 2007; Sheth & Solomon, 2014).

To this aim, we developed a smartphone-based online application adapted from a previously validated dice game task used to investigate dishonest behaviour (Arbel et al., 2014a, Arbel et al., 2014b; Fischbacher & Föllmi-Heusi, 2013). In the original version of the task, participants were asked to roll a die and report the outcome to the experimenter. The monetary payout was linked to the reported outcome; therefore, participants could lie and report a higher outcome to increase their gains. In our adapted version of the task, the ‘Roll and Tell’ dice game (R&T), participants engaged in 60 anonymous, randomised online matches against other real participants using their own mobile devices. Each match was composed of ten rounds. Crucially, participants reported their outcomes directly to their online opponents, who were labelled as Facebook friends or as unknown participants. To win a round and score a point, participants had to report a higher outcome than their opponent. When reporting the same outcome, neither participant gained points. While there was no direct monetary reward for inflating the outcome of each die roll, the application kept track of all participants' points to create an online global ranking. At the end of the study, only the three top-ranked participants received an additional monetary reward. Overall, this approach allowed us to investigate dishonest behaviour across repeated, interactive, and ecologically valid contexts, capturing strategic decisions as they dynamically unfold. In contrast to traditional single-shot laboratory paradigms, our design makes it possible to examine how dishonesty is shaped by social relationships, game dynamics (e.g., status and competitive advantage), and embodied self-processes, as well as how individuals adapt their behaviour over time in response to changing situational demands.

From a game theory perspective, our game's structure resembles a strategic tournament (Arad & Rubinstein, 2013), in which n players simultaneously compete through repeated dyadic interactions within a competitive ranking system (round robin). Within this game framework, the behaviour of players who strive to outperform others tends to converge toward a Nash Equilibrium (Nash, 1950). In game theory, a Nash equilibrium is a state in which no player can improve their outcome by unilaterally changing their strategy, assuming the other players keep their strategies unchanged. In our game settings, Nash equilibrium analysis suggests that a dominant strategy is to consistently report the highest possible outcome (i.e., “six”), especially when there is no way to verify the true outcome of the dice roll (see section S6 A formal solution of the Roll & Tell dice game of the Supplementary Materials for the detailed mathematical model). Therefore, this tournament-like structure was intended to foster more competitive and, consequently, dishonest behaviour, as participants seek to outperform other players under the uncertainty of how they could behave.

Methods and materials

Participants

Initially, 69 participants agreed to take part in the study. From this initial sample, we excluded 24 participants who did not complete the required 60 matches. Two more participants were later excluded because their number of failed attention checks exceeded the upper Tukey fence (Tukey, 1977; Leys et al., 2013). The final sample included 43 participants: 7 males (16.28%), 34 females (79.07%), and 2 participants who did not disclose their sex (4.65%). Participants' ages ranged from 20 to 42, with a mean of 22.66 and a standard deviation of 4.02. All participants were recruited via the laboratory's participants mailing list, flyers, and word of mouth. Participants' exclusion criteria, clearly stated at the beginning of the online study's consent form, consisted of the diagnosis or history of neurological or psychiatric disorders and use of psychiatric drugs. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Department of Psychology at Sapienza University of Rome and conducted in accordance with the Ethical standards of the 2013 Declaration of Helsinki. Participants were asked to read and sign an online consent form. Participants received monetary compensation for participating in the study and an additional monetary reward based on their performance in the game (see 2.2.2).

Self-report questionnaires

Before the data collection, all participants were asked to complete a series of self-report questionnaires through the online platform PsyToolkit (Stoet, 2010, 2017). The order of presentation of all self-report measures was randomised between participants. To measure participants' bodily self-consciousness in terms of body ownership and body agency, we administered the Private Body subscale of the Body Consciousness Questionnaire (Miller et al., 1981), which measures individuals' awareness of their bodily signals (M = 14.41, SD = 2.97, range = 8 - 19), and the Sense of Agency Scale (Tapal et al., 2017), which measures the personal beliefs of having a core sense of agency. The latter includes two subscales: a positive sense of agency (M = 27.27, SD = 4.11, range = 17 - 35), reflecting the extent to which individuals feel in control of their body, mind, and environment, and a negative sense of agency (M = 11.88, SD = 4.31, range = 6 - 22), reflecting the extent to which these are perceived as not under their control. To investigate participants' moral identity, we administered the Moral Identity Measure (Aquino & Reed II, 2002), a questionnaire measuring two distinct moral identity dimensions: the internalized dimension refers to the subjective experience of having a moral identity (M = 4.35, SD = 0.58, range = 2.80 - 5), whereas the symbolized dimension refers to expressing one's own moral identity in social and public domains (M = 2.80, SD = 0.74, range = 1 - 4.6). Finally, to assess moderating variables of the relationship between the dimensions of BSC and morality, we administered the Sense of Power Scale (M = 5.12, SD = 0.80, range = 3.25 - 6.75) measuring individuals' perceived ability to influence others' behaviours, opinions, decisions, and outcomes within specific social relationships or context (Anderson et al., 2012), the Moral Disengagement Measure (M = 2.07, SD = 0.63, range = 1 - 3.62) assessing the extent to which individuals use cognitive justifications to disengage from their moral standards and rationalize unethical behaviour without feeling guilt or self-condemnation (Moore et al., 2012, 2018), and the Monetary Intelligence Scale (M = 2.77, SD = 0.16, range = 2.45 - 3.14), measuring individual's ability to understand, manage, and use money (Tang et al., 2018).

To better characterise the sample and explore potential individual differences that could influence dishonest behaviour, we measured participants' general usage of Facebook by asking them how often they used the platform and how many Facebook friends they had. Participants were also asked to indicate both the number and the percentage of their Facebook contacts they considered to be real friends. Moreover, we presented participants with three items assessing their attitudes toward different types of games (gambling, online gaming, and competitive games) and three items assessing how frequently they engage in such activities. Finally, we assessed participants' self-reported economic status by asking where they would position themselves along a horizontal line, with the left end of the line representing people with less money (0) and the right end indicating people with the most money (100) (M = 44.1, SD = 21.91, range = 0 - 100) (Adler et al., 2000; Ostrove et al., 2000; Scattolin, Panasiti, & Aglioti, 2022) (for a detailed description of all the questionnaires and items used, please refer to section S1. Self-report questionnaires of the Supplementary Materials).

Measuring moral behaviour in digital social interactions: the ‘Roll & Tell’ application

The ‘Roll and Tell’ (R&T) is a mobile application developed for this study and based on previous dice game experimental paradigms (Arbel et al., 2014a, Arbel et al., 2014b; Fischbacher & Föllmi-Heusi, 2013). The R&T app allowed participants to play an online dice game on their personal smartphones against either Facebook friends or non-friends. The app was developed with Unity 2019.2.18f1 (Unity Technologies, San Francisco) and the Android Studio SDK (Google LLC, Mountain View) for Android devices, and later ported to iOS (iPhones) (app's source code and a short gameplay video are available at https://github.com/Adiell83/Roll-Tell-Mobile). Matchmaking was implemented via the Photon Plugin for Unity, and the games were hosted on Photon Cloud (Exit Games Inc.). Data was stored on a Firebase server (Google LLC, Mountain View). All participants were required to have a previously existing Facebook account, as the app would use their Facebook account details to enable players to log in and access the app's online functionalities (Fig. 1).Fig. 1The Facebook SDK (Facebook Inc., Menlo Park) enabled participants to log in to the app using their Facebook account. Once logged in, participants were presented with the game's instructions (see section S3. ‘Roll & Tell’ App ‘In-Game’ instructions of the Supplementary Materials for the detailed instructions) and could then either search for an opponent or view the current global ranking of all players participating in the study (panel Fig. 1C). If they decided to search for an opponent, the app paired the player with the first available opponent; if no opponent was found after 30 s, the app offered the option to refresh the search for a new opponent or return to the previous screen. The matchmaking system paired players with the first available online opponent. Once an opponent was matched, the app would inform the player whether they were competing against a Facebook friend or a non-friend. While the app could distinguish between Facebook friends and non-friends, we decided to assign this status independently of the actual relationship with the opponent, as we needed the matches to be equally distributed between 'friends' and 'non-friends.' Therefore, the app logic randomly assigned players to compete against either Facebook friends or non-friends, while maintaining an overall balance so that the 60 matches were evenly split between the two conditions. No other information about the opponent's identity (e.g., the name or the sex) was provided.

Once two players were paired, the app would start a 5-s countdown, and after that, the game would begin. A game of ‘Roll & Tell’ consisted of ten turns. Participants would gain a point for each turn they won and two points for winning the entire game. At each turn, participants were prompted to roll a digital die by swiping their smartphone's screen. The actual die roll was not scripted or animated and was entirely physics-based. The force applied and the spin were randomly determined for each turn to ensure the die's unpredictable behaviour. Once the die was cast, participants were instructed to report their die roll's outcome to the other player by pressing a number on the screen. There was no time limit for participants to report their outcome after the die settled, and thus no specific inter-trial interval between turns. The player who reported the highest number would win the turn and earn a point; in the event of a tie, both participants would lose. Importantly, participants could only see the outcome of their own die roll and were thus unable to see the opponent's die roll outcome. Also, at every turn, participants learned about each other's reported outcomes simultaneously, after both had provided their response. This was done to prevent participants from making decisions based on the opponent's reported outcome. Each match consisted of 10 rounds, and to win, a player had to win more rounds than their opponents by the end of the match. Winning a match awarded an extra point, increasing players' total points on the global leaderboard. After each match, participants had a 1-in-2 chance of being presented with an attention check, in which they were asked to report the number of turns they won in the previous match.

Although they were not explicitly told they could lie, participants were free to report any number from 1 to 6. Therefore, participants could spontaneously decide to report the actual outcome of their die roll or a different one. (Fig. 2, Panel A).Fig. 2Panel A. To measure dishonesty, a relative lie index was calculated as the difference between the reported and real dice outcomes for each turn. We opted for a relative lie index to measure the magnitude of deception, accounting for the fact that the opportunity to lie depends on the actual die outcome. The relative lie index was therefore computed to scale each lie by the maximum lie that was possible given the actual die outcome. Let Ri denote the reported die outcome and Ai the actual die outcome in trial i. The relative lie index was defined as:RLIi={Ri−Ai6−AiifRi>Ai(egoisticlie)0ifRi=Ai(truth−telling)Ri−AiAi−1ifRi<Ai(altruisticlie)

Specifically, when participants reported a number higher than the actual outcome (egoistic lie), the discrepancy between the reported and actual outcomes was divided by the largest possible upward lie, i.e., 6−Ai. When participants reported a number lower than the true outcome (altruistic lie), the discrepancy was divided by the largest possible downward lie, i.e., Ai−1. Truthful responses were coded as 0. The formulation also accounts for boundary cases: when Ai=1, downward deviations are not possible, and when Ai=6, upward deviations are not possible. As a result, the index ranges from −1 to 1, with positive values indicating egoistic lies, negative values indicating altruistic lies, and larger absolute values indicating lies closer to the maximum possible lie for a specific trial. For example, if the actual outcome is 5 and the participant reports 6, this represents the largest possible egoistic lie. In this case, the relative lie index is (6−5)/(6−5)=1, indicating a maximal upward deviation. Conversely, if the actual outcome is 5 and the participant reports 2, this represents an altruistic lie. The relative lie index is (2−5)/(5−1)=−3/4=−0.75, indicating a substantial but not maximal downward deviation. The most extreme altruistic lie in this case would be reporting 1, which would yield (1−5)/(5−1)=−1. Therefore, the relative lie index reflects the extent of lying relative to the maximum possible lie in that specific trial, rather than the absolute discrepancy between reported and actual outcomes. Our sample had a mean relative lie index of 0.15 and a standard deviation of 0.36.

For each turn, the application created a local JSON file containing the following data: participant's user name, total amount of games played so far, current position on the global leaderboard, the status of the opponent (Facebook friend vs. Facebook non-friend), current match turn, player's and opponent's current match scores, the real outcome of the die, the participants' reported die outcome, opponent's reported outcome, if the turn was won by the player and the raw lie index (the difference between the reported score and the actual score). At the end of each turn, the JSON file and all its contents were stored in a Firebase Realtime Database and continuously updated thereafter (see section S2 Personal Data Handling of the Supplementary Materials for a detailed description of how the data was handled). For each match, a second JSON file was created and stored online to track participants' performance across matches, recording the following data: username, number of matches played vs friends/non-friends, total number of matches played, matches won, total points scored, and current leaderboard ranking. All this information was displayed on the online leaderboard, accessible to participants from the main menu of the mobile app. Therefore, participants could freely check their position on the global leaderboard and the rankings of other players before and after each game. The viewable data included their leaderboard position, the number of games played, the number of games won, their total score, and their username (Fig. 2, Panel B). While all participants received a compensation of 20 euros for participating in the study, they were incentivised to care about their positioning on the global leaderboard, as at the beginning of the data collection, they were explicitly told that the first three players in the global leaderboard would be awarded a monetary prize of 300, 200, and 100 euros, respectively.

Procedure

The experiment was conducted online, with participants using the R&T app from their smartphones. At the beginning of data collection, participants were asked to sign the online consent form and to complete a series of online questionnaires (see 2.2.1). Then, Android smartphone and iPhone users were sent a link to download the R&T app from the Google Play Store or Apple Store, respectively. To prevent external people from participating in the study, the R&T app was published in a beta state on both storefronts, and a private invite was required to download and install the application. Data collection lasted about 2.5 months. At the start of each week, the experimenter sent an email to all participants, announcing three “play sessions” per week, held on three different days and lasting approximately 30 min each. Participants were asked to use the R&T app only during these play sessions and to play 5 matches per session against other participants. To strictly limit participants' access to the R&T app and control its usage, the experimenter disabled the app's online functionality outside the 30-min window of the play sessions by changing the Facebook Provider Keys value for the app, which are required to enable the login via Facebook account, from the Photon control dashboard, making it impossible to log in to the app outside the planned sessions. In total, participants were asked to play 60 matches of the R&T game, and after that, the app disabled further play. All these measures were taken to prevent participants from making arrangements with each other and fixing their matches to boost their scores and increase their chances of winning one of the additional monetary rewards awarded to the top three players. For instance, playing during off-hours could have enabled them to play against an agreed-upon opponent. Finally, the experimenters organised extra weeks of play sessions to allow all participants to complete the required 60 matches and minimise dropouts.

Results

Statistical analyses

We handled and analysed all data using R (version 4.5.3) (R Core Team, 2024) through RStudio (version 2026.1.1.403) (Posit team, 2025). Function stat.desc of the pastecs package (version 1.4.2) was used to run Shapiro-Wilk tests of normality and check the skewness and kurtosis of data. Linear models and linear mixed-effects models were computed with the lm function of the package stats and with the lmer function in the lmerTest package (version 3.2-1), respectively. An alpha level of 0.05 was set for all analyses.

Strategic behaviour/Game strategy

According to a game strategy based on Nash equilibrium, rational, egoistic players would have reported 6 in each round of each game, regardless of the actual outcome. To test whether participants’ behaviour during the game was in line with the pure-strategy Nash equilibrium, we compared the observed percentage of trials in which participants reported a six with the theorised 100%. Since the distribution of percentages differed significantly from normality (W = 0.739, p < 0.001) and its skewness (Z = 4.667) and kurtosis (Z = 2.689) exceeded the ±1.96 range (Field et al., 2012), we ran a one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Results showed that the observed percentage of reported six was significantly lower than 100% (W = 0, r = 0.871, 95% CI [0.87, 0.87], p < 0.001). To clarify whether playing a pure-strategy game could predict a better outcome for participants (i.e., ranking higher on the final leaderboard) or whether other predictors played a significant role, we applied a forward stepwise model selection. Specifically, we ran a baseline linear model including Ranking Position as our dependent variable, with the percentage of dishonestly reported sixes as predictor. The following models incrementally included the percentage of truthful sixes, the percentage of trials in which participants lied egoistically but reported values lower than six, and the percentage of trials in which participants lied altruistically. The ANOVA model comparison showed that the model with all these predictors performed significantly better than the others, as shown by successive F tests (all p < 0.01; final comparison: F(1, 38) = 12.658, p = 0.001). Model fit was additionally evaluated using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). This model (R2 = 0.750, adjusted R2 = 0.723, AIC = 275.51, BIC = 286.07, F(4, 38) = 28.47, p < 0.001) showed that the percentage of sixes that are reported dishonestly improves participants’ final ranking (β = −0.36, 95% CI [−0.49, −0.24], t(38) = −6.01, p < 0.001; Std. beta = −0.64, 95% CI [−0.86, −0.43]), which accounted for 68% of the unique explained variance (η2 = 0.68, 95% CI [0.53, 1.00]). The same positive association was observed between the final ranking of participants and both the percentage of truthfully reported sixes (β = −1.61, 95% CI [−2.80, −0.43], t(38) = −2.76, p = 0.009; Std. beta = −0.23, 95% CI [−0.40, −0.06]) and the percentage of egoistic lies below six (β = −0.46, 95% CI [−0.74, −0.18], t(38) = −3.35, p = 0.002; Std. beta = −0.35, 95% CI [−0.56, −0.14]), which account for 23% (η2 = 0.23, 95% CI [0.06, 1.00]) and 22% (η2 = 0.22, 95% CI [0.05, 1.00]) of the unique variance, respectively. Discussion

Strategic behaviour and deviation from Nash Equilibrium (H1)

In this study, we investigated how dishonest behaviour emerges from the interplay between situational, social, and individual factors during online interactions, with a specific focus on the role of Bodily Self-Consciousness (BSC), namely the Sense of Body Ownership (SoO) and Sense of Agency (SoA), alongside contextual variables such as social relationship (i.e. lying to Facebook friends vs. non-Facebook friends), competitive dynamics (indexed by ranking position), and game status (i.e. whether participants were in a favourable winning position or not). To this aim, we developed the “Roll & Tell” application, a smartphone app in which participants played against each other in an online dice game. The structure of our game resembled that of a strategic tournament (Arad & Rubinstein, 2013), which led us to hypothesise that competitive behaviour finalised to maximise the chances of winning the game would adhere to a Nash Equilibrium – in the case of our game, consistently reporting the highest possible outcome (6). The results of our study showed a substantial deviation from this prediction: participants reported a 6 in fewer than a third of the total number of trials, and even the highest-performing individuals in the final ranking never reached full adherence to the equilibrium strategy.

This finding is not entirely surprising, as previous studies in behavioural economics literature show that participants’ behaviour in strategic games often diverges from the optimal strategy, i.e., equilibrium behaviour. For instance, a study investigating strategic behaviour in all-pay auctions (where all players pay a fraction of their bid, regardless of the bid outcome) observed systematic overbidding and non-equilibrium behaviour (Gneezy & Smorodinsky, 2006). Similarly, in a study using simple normal-form games, only a minority of individuals followed Nash-predicted strategies (Sutter et al., 2013). Crucially, in a study on lying that adopted a paradigm similar to ours (Fischbacher & Föllmi-Heusi, 2013), only about one-fifth of participants maximised their payoffs by consistently reporting the most profitable dice outcome. The other participants either never lied (about 39% of participants) or lied but did not maximise their payoff. The authors argued that this behaviour could at least find a partial explanation in lying aversion, which is the tendency to avoid dishonesty due to internal moral costs, a desire to maintain a positive self-image, or aversion to norm violations (Fischbacher & Föllmi-Heusi, 2013; Gneezy et al., 2018; Rosenbaum et al., 2014).

However, rather than reflecting a strict aversion to lying, our findings suggest that participants may have engaged in strategic moral self-regulation, balancing the incentive to win with the psychological costs of fully deceptive behaviour. In line with prior work on self-concept maintenance (Mazar et al., 2008), participants may have preferred partial dishonesty over maximal dishonesty, thereby preserving their self-image as not being completely dishonest. This interpretation is consistent with our observation that participants often used mixed strategies and varied their degree of dishonesty across trials. Importantly, regression analyses showed that dishonest behaviour - both in the form of falsely reported sixes or misreporting any other number - significantly predicted higher-ranking outcomes, suggesting that while dishonesty was instrumentally beneficial, participants did not systematically adopt the payoff-maximising strategy (Nash equilibrium).

Friendship, competition, and dishonest behaviour (H2)

Our behavioural data also showed that the larger the players' advantage in a given game, the more likely they were to lie egoistically by reporting a higher dice outcome than the actual one. Furthermore, higher-ranked participants were more likely to lie egoistically. This result indicates that, for the higher-ranked participants, the incentive to win was amplified by the pressure to secure a spot in the top three positions on the global leaderboard and to obtain the additional reward, leading them to behave more dishonestly. These results align with previous literature on the effect of competition on moral behaviour, which suggests that competition has an adverse effect on individual moral behaviour (Huber et al., 2023). In a study by Reall and colleagues (Reall et al., 1998), participants' moral reasoning was evaluated while engaging in two different tasks: a non-competitive and a competitive one, i.e., The Commons Game (Powers, 1986). The Commons Game is a simulation game where players manage a limited renewable resource. They can either choose to cooperate, ensuring the resource's renewal, or compete to maximise individual gains, potentially depleting the resource entirely. Data showed that participants exhibited lower levels of moral reasoning while playing the Commons Game than when engaged in a non-competitive task. The researchers argued that this difference could be explained by participants' momentary suspension of moral reasoning, as they rationalised their actions by framing them as part of the game and therefore acceptable within the boundaries of its rules. The relevance of the game's rules in affecting moral behaviour is also highlighted in a 2019 meta-analysis that reviewed studies using different experimental paradigms to investigate dishonest behaviour (Gerlach et al., 2019). The review concluded that not only do individuals behave more dishonestly as the reward size increases, but dishonest behaviour is also affected by the game's characteristics. Specifically, data showed that participants behaved more dishonestly when playing a dice-rolling game than in other experimental paradigms, such as matrix or coin-flip games (Gerlach et al., 2019). Moreover, a more recent study (Vadera & Pathki, 2021) showed that, as competition increases, so does cheating. This might be caused by impaired moral awareness rather than by the mere desire to win, as competition compromises individuals' ability to evaluate the moral salience of the situation they are in.

Surprisingly, we also found that participants modulated their dishonest behaviour depending on the social relationship with their opponent, i.e., when playing against a friend. Although egoistic lying generally increased as participants' scoring advantage over their opponent grew, regardless of the opponent's status (regardless of whether the opponent was a friend or not), this effect was significantly amplified in the Facebook friend condition, particularly among higher-ranking individuals. While the observed effects were modest in magnitude, they were consistent across repeated interactions, suggesting a meaningful impact on participants' overall strategic behaviour. This pattern is in contrast with our initial hypothesis based on in-group favouritism (H2), which predicted more prosocial and honest behaviour toward familiar others (Balliet et al., 2014; Hewstone et al., 2002). One contributing factor to these results may have been that players were unaware of their opponent's specific identity. Therefore, regardless of whether they were a Facebook friend or an unknown individual, all opponents were equally anonymous to each participant. Anonymity has been shown to increase anti-normative and egoistic behaviour in individuals (Bohnet & Frey, 1999; Burnham, 2003). Moreover, anonymous individuals are also more likely to be on the receiving end of unethical behaviour (Yam & Reynolds, 2016).

However, while anonymity might have dampened the effect of ingroup membership and promoted egoistic behaviour, it does not fully account for the increased dishonesty observed when participants played against friends. This pattern is more consistent with an alternative account in which social closeness enhances competitive motivation. We hypothesise that this behaviour may result from a rivalry mechanism that is present only when playing against Facebook friends. Rivalry can be described as a subset of general competition in which the existing relationship between the competing actors further increases the stakes beyond the objective characteristics of competition (G. Kilduff et al., 2012). In particular, rivalry appears to have a greater effect on promoting dishonest behaviour than general competition, by incentivising a specific mindset focused on pursuing the best possible outcome, outperforming opponents regardless of ethical concerns about the behaviour required to beat them (Kilduff et al., 2012). Previous studies have shown that rivalry is associated with increased unsportsmanlike behaviour, deception, Machiavellianism, and unethical negotiation tactics (G. Kilduff et al., 2012; G. J. Kilduff et al., 2016). As factors like similarity in geographical location, university status, and previous competitive interactions all contribute to fostering rivalry (G. J. Kilduff et al., 2010), we hypothesise that our participants, who were all recruited from the same university, may have acted more unethically while playing against their Facebook friends possibly because of the pre-existing relationship they had with the opponents that were presented to them as being their friends, which may have activated in them the desire to outperform those that they considered to be their rivals. However, it is important to emphasise that we did not directly measure competitive motivation, rivalry, or social comparison during the task. Thus, while our findings are consistent with the literature on these topics, future studies should directly assess rivalry, perceived competitiveness, and social-comparison motives when examining dishonest behaviour in interactions involving familiar others.

Relationship between BSC and dishonest behaviour (H3 & H4)

As previously stated, one of the main aims of the present study was to assess the relationship between bodily self-consciousness (BSC) and dishonest behaviour. Although BSC was not experimentally manipulated, we examined this relationship by asking participants to complete a series of self-report questionnaires and by conducting moderation analyses. Self-reported measures of Sense of Ownership (SoO) and Sense of Agency (SoA) were included as predictors of dishonest behaviour. Moral disengagement and sense of power were tested as moderators of the relationship between SoA and behaviour, while reward sensitivity was examined as a moderator of the relationship between SoO and behaviour.

With respect to SoO (H3), we did not observe a direct effect on dishonest behaviour, nor did we observe a moderating effect of reward sensitivity. This null result contrasts with previous studies suggesting that heightened bodily signals can increase dishonest or egoistic behaviour (Lenggenhager et al., 2013; Mancini et al., 2011; Williams et al., 2016). However, in these studies, bodily self-related signals were made highly salient, either through interoceptive manipulations (e.g., heartbeat perception), induced physiological states (e.g., hunger or pain), or embodiment of virtual avatars (Scattolin, Panasiti, Villa, & Aglioti, 2022). In contrast, the present study was conducted in a naturalistic context in which participants simply operated with their own smartphones. In such a context, bodily signals were not explicitly enhanced and may have played a less prominent role in guiding participants’ decision-making. Therefore, under these conditions, SoO may have shown a weaker association with moral behaviour.

Conversely, the interactive nature of the task, which required participants to continuously execute actions (e.g., swiping to roll the die), may have more strongly engaged participants’ SoA. This might explain why, with respect to SoA (H3), our results revealed a significant interaction with sense of power. Specifically, when participants reported low levels of sense of power, an increase in Positive SoA was associated with increasingly more honest behaviour. In contrast, when sense of power was high, dishonest behaviour remained stable across different levels of Positive SoA. No significant moderating effect of moral disengagement emerged.

These findings are consistent with previous research suggesting a positive association between sense of agency and moral behaviour, with higher SoA being linked to moral actions and lower SoA to immoral ones (Caspar et al., 2018). However, our results also highlight the critical role of sense of power in moderating the relationship between SoA and moral behaviour. Previous research shows that the behavioural disinhibition and the accentuated focus on self associated with high power can negatively affect moral behaviour (Lammers et al., 2015). For instance, a high sense of power can lead an individual to betray their romantic partner more easily (Lammers et al., 2011) or engage in more traffic violations by driving more powerful cars (Piff et al., 2012). In line with this literature, our findings suggest that the association between SoA and moral behaviour may depend on perceived sense of power. Specifically, a negative association between Positive SoA and dishonest behaviour was observed only among participants reporting low sense of power, whereas this association was not evident among those reporting high sense of power. From this perspective, high sense of power may be linked to a weaker association between agency and moral behaviour, possibly because it is associated with reduced sensitivity to social norms and lower perspective-taking toward others (Galinsky et al., 2006; Keltner et al., 2003).

Finally, it is important to note that our ecological design did not allow us to experimentally manipulate' SoO and SoA and verify whether inducing high/low SoO and SoA would have affected moral behaviour while playing online against other players. Mounting evidence indicates that immersive virtual reality (IVR) can be used to effectively change BSC. By experimentally manipulating SoO and SoA, IVR studies have shown that it is possible to modify individuals' body image (Keizer et al., 2016; Normand et al., 2011; Provenzano et al., 2020) and body schema (Provenzano et al., 2024) as well as inducing illusory ownership of bodies vastly different from one's own (Frisanco et al., 2026; Hoort et al., 2011). More importantly, a study by this group (Scattolin et al., 2022a, Scattolin et al., 2022b) demonstrated that reduced SoO leads to increased dishonest behaviour when a higher reward is at stake in an experimental setting. Bringing IVR outside the laboratory appears to be a new challenge that future studies should tackle to further investigate how BSC affects people's moral behaviour during their daily online social interactions.

Conclusions

The findings of the present study highlight the importance of investigating dishonesty in ecological settings as a dynamic and context-dependent behaviour, emerging from the interaction between bodily self-consciousness (BSC), situational factors, and social context. Results showed that participants did not systematically report the highest possible outcome. Instead, dishonest behaviour was flexibly modulated by individual and contextual factors. Specifically, our results suggest that participants were more likely to engage in egoistic lying as their scoring advantage over their opponent increased, particularly when the game's outcome had already been determined. Moreover, higher-ranking individuals tended to lie more, suggesting that competition plays a key role in shaping dishonesty. Importantly, these effects were further amplified in the Facebook friend condition. This pattern indicates that social closeness does not necessarily promote honesty, but may instead enhance rivalry or increase the motivational salience of winning.

Although we found no direct association between BSC components and dishonest behaviour, results showed that the relationship between Positive SoA and dishonest behaviour was moderated by perceived power. Specifically, Positive SoA was associated with more honest behaviour only under conditions of low perceived power, whereas this association was not observed at higher levels of perceived power. Thus, these findings contribute to the literature on embodied morality by suggesting that the relationship between bodily self-consciousness and moral behaviour may depend on interactions with contextual and psychological factors, such as perceived power.

Overall, the present study demonstrates the feasibility and relevance of using naturalistic, socially interactive paradigms to study moral decision-making outside the constraints of the laboratory. Future studies investigating the relationship between BSC and morality in online social contexts should employ novel IVR paradigms to exert experimental control over both SoO and SoA and to evaluate the effects of increasing/decreasing these two components of BSC on individuals’ moral behaviour. As the first ventures in the metaverse face problems related to inappropriate and immoral user behaviour (Wiederhold, 2022), such inquiries could yield valuable insights toward a safe cyberspace and a metaverse populated by responsible, morally engaged actors.

Limitations

A key limitation of the present study concerns the relatively small sample size (N = 43), which should be considered when interpreting the findings. The study, therefore, provides an initial exploratory investigation of the relationship between bodily self-consciousness and dishonest behaviour within an ecologically valid, app-based online paradigm. As such, the limited sample size may have reduced statistical power, increasing the risk of Type II errors and limiting the ability to detect small or moderate effects, particularly in interaction analyses. Data collection for this study was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, which posed a significant challenge for recruiting a diverse array of participants. As a result, all participants were recruited from students of Psychology and Medicine at Sapienza University of Rome, resulting in 81.40% of participants being female. Literature on sex differences and dishonesty seems to point to the fact that men are generally more dishonest than women (Capraro, 2018), women tell more altruistic lies (Erat & Gneezy, 2012), and men appear to tell significantly more egoistic/self-serving lies than women (Grosch & Rau, 2017; Kajackaite & Gneezy, 2017). These factors limit the generalizability of the findings and suggest that future studies should aim to recruit larger, more diverse samples with a more balanced sex distribution to provide a more robust verification of the observed effects. Moreover, as the present design is cross-sectional and based on self-report measures of BSC, the observed relationships should be interpreted as associative rather than causal. Future studies employing experimental manipulations will be necessary to establish causal pathways. Furthermore, psychological constructs such as sense of power, moral disengagement, and moral identity were assessed using self-report questionnaires, which may have introduced common-method bias and social desirability bias.

A further limitation concerns the specific characteristics of the experimental task. The “Roll & Tell” paradigm was designed as a competitive, monetarily incentivised interaction in which participants were motivated to outperform others within a ranking system. While this represents an ecologically valid approximation of many real-world online environments (e.g., competitive gaming or performance-based reward systems), it may limit the generalizability of the findings to other types of online interactions, such as cooperative or non-competitive contexts. Previous research suggests that competition can increase dishonest behaviour by shifting individuals’ focus toward performance outcomes and reducing moral awareness (Gerlach et al., 2019; Huber et al., 2023; Vadera & Pathki, 2021). Therefore, the behavioural patterns observed in the present study may be specific to competitive environments, and future research should examine whether similar effects emerge in other cooperative or non-incentivised settings.

Additionally, we used Facebook to induce the belief that participants were playing against either friends or non-friends. However, although all our participants had a pre-existing Facebook account, 53.66% of our sample reported using it sparingly (on a monthly basis or less), which aligns with the global trend of younger individuals abandoning Facebook for other social media platforms (Eckles, 2018). In light of this, future studies investigating the existing relationship between online users should consider alternative methods, especially when younger populations are the intended target. Also, because our initial hypothesis examined the relationship between opponents' status and moral behaviour exclusively from an ingroup-outgroup perspective, we did not consider alternative perspectives, such as rivalry and social comparison, which may have suggested an increase in dishonest behaviour when interacting with known others in competitive contexts. For this reason, our study lacks a direct measure of rivalry, and thus, its effect is merely inferred. More generally, the present manipulation should be considered relatively minimal, as it likely captures participants’ internal representations or expectations about competing against an unspecified “friend,” rather than the dynamics of an actual, identifiable relationship with concrete social consequences. Future research should incorporate measures of rivalry and competitive orientation when examining dishonest behaviour in interactions involving familiar others, including measures of interpersonal competitiveness (e.g., Houston et al., 2002) or rivalry in achievement contexts (e.g., Kilduff et al., 2010), and verify whether increased dishonesty toward friends reflects rivalry, social comparison processes, or other motivational mechanisms.
