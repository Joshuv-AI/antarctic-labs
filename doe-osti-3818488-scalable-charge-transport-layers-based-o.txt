5. Background:

Recent developments in perovskite solar cell (PSC) research emphasize that the long-term
stability and peak performance of these devices increasingly depend on the rational design of
charge transport layers, including both hole transport layers (HTLs) and electron transport layers
(ETLs).(Afraj et al., 2025; Saliba et al., 2016; Sun et al., 2018) As PSCs approach efficiencies
rivaling commercial photovoltaic technologies, the stability bottlenecks associated with charge
extraction interfaces remain a major focus of the broader research community.(Chowdhury et al.,
2023; H.-S. Kim et al., 2016; Mahmud et al., 2022; Ouedraogo et al., 2020; Saliba et al., 2018;
Wang et al., 2019) The four publications emerging from this project directly address these
challenges through the molecular design of advanced HTLs and ETLs. These efforts align closely
with the current literature while providing new mechanistic insights and design rules that
distinguish the work and accelerate progress beyond the state of the art.

In the area of HTLs, our work connects strongly with contemporary strategies in polymer
electronics that leverage backbone planarization, non-covalent intramolecular interactions, and
side-chain engineering to modulate morphology, electronic structure, and thermal behavior.
Recent peer-reviewed reports in the organic electronics field have shown that introducing
interactions between the sulfur with the nitrogen and oxygen species, or hydrogen-bond
interactions into conjugated polymers enhances planarity, improves π–π stacking, and increases
charge mobility.(Sabury et al., 2024) Paper 1 uses an analogous but more structurally
comprehensive strategy by designing the 1,4-(2-thienyl)-2,5-dialkoxyphenylene thienothiophene
(TPT–TT) polymer, verified through density functional theory, to induce backbone planarization
and multi-level liquid-crystalline (LC) ordering. This provides a level of structural control rarely
implemented in HTL design for PSCs. At the same time, our work aligns with existing studies
demonstrating that solution aggregation and temperature-induced ordering can be harnessed to
create high-performance films, but diverges by providing the first detailed mapping of three distinct
LC phases and correlating these phases with dramatic enhancements in conductivity and mobility
uniformity following thermal annealing. This approach, seldom applied in PSC interface

 Page 3 of 35

engineering, establishes a mechanistic framework for controlling solid-state morphology through
thermotropic behavior.(Sabury et al., 2024)

Our second HTL publication (Paper 2) also draws from existing literature suggesting that the
thermal properties of polymeric HTLs influence device durability. Conventional approaches
typically aim to increase the glass transition temperature or introduce rigid comonomers to
stabilize morphology. However, recent studies have hinted that reducing the number of thermal
transitions within the operating temperature range may be equally important for preventing
cracking or phase rearrangement. Our work expands this concept by designing 1,4-(2-thienyl)-
2,5-dialkoxyphenylene (TPT)-based polymers with controlled side-chain asymmetry, showing that
minimizing or eliminating thermal transitions below 200 °C leads to HTLs that remain crack-free
and mechanically stable under accelerated aging at 65 °C. This diverges from typical strategies
in the literature by demonstrating that the absence of transitions, rather than their elevation, is an
effective pathway for improving PSC stability. By leveraging side-chain engineering principles
established in the broader polymer electronics community, we accelerated identification of a high-
performing HTL that achieves over 15% efficiency and exceeds the thermal stability of spiro-
OMeTAD, a benchmark that anchors much of the current state of the art.(S. Kim et al., 2024)

The project’s ETL work is likewise embedded within and informed by major research trends.
Organic ETLs, particularly those based on naphthalene diimide (NDI), have gained attention as
alternatives to inorganic metal oxides like titanium dioxide (TiO2), which can suffer from UV-
induced instabilities. The literature shows ongoing efforts to incorporate electron-withdrawing
substituents or anchoring groups to facilitate strong adhesion to oxide substrates and tune energy
alignment with perovskite conduction bands. Our third publication (Paper 3) parallels these
themes by comparing two NDI derivatives: one functionalized with phosphonic acid anchoring
groups and one carrying nonpolar bromophenyl substituents. Similar to reports demonstrating
that strong anchoring groups improve film uniformity and durability, 1,3,6,8-tetraoxo-1,3,6,8-
tetrahydrobenzo[lmn][3,8]phenanthroline-2,-diyl)bis(4,1-phenylene))bis(phosphonic acid) (NDI-
PhPA)₂ adheres robustly to fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO), forming solvent-resistant and
thermally stable films. However, our work distinguishes itself through a systematic demonstration
that the absence of anchoring groups (as in 2,7-bis(4-bromphenyl)benzo[lmn][3,8]phenanthroline-
1,3,6,8(2H, 7H)-tetraone or NDI-(PhBr)₂) results in near-complete film loss and dramatic
performance decay, which provides one of the clearest comparative analyses of anchoring-driven
interfacial stabilization to date. The performance parity between phosphonic-acid-functionalized
NDI ETLs and conventional TiO₂ under 200 hours of continuous illumination represents a
significant advancement that directly leverages mechanistic findings from the surface chemistry
literature.(S. Kim, Wagner, Sabury, Lawton, et al., 2025)

The fourth publication (Paper 4) advances the ETL field by introducing an additional design
element: molecular-level electronic tuning to optimize energy-level alignment. Existing research
has shown that halogenation of NDI cores deepens the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital
(LUMO) and improves electron extraction, but these strategies have rarely been paired with
robust anchoring chemistries in a PSC-relevant ETL. Our work integrates these two concepts by
combining benzyl-phosphonic acid anchoring groups with targeted bromination to deepen the
LUMO by 0.29 eV, improving alignment with the perovskite conduction band. This dual-tuning
strategy differs from the majority of the literature, which typically tunes either electronic structure
or interfacial adhesion, but not both simultaneously. Leveraging insights from previous optical
spectroscopy and device hysteresis studies, we hypothesized that deeper LUMO energies

 Page 4 of 35

suppress interfacial charge accumulation and reduce field-driven ion migration. The resulting
devices confirm this, showing reduced hysteresis and higher current density, thus validating
design rules extrapolated from recent mechanistic reports on perovskite-ion dynamics and
interface energetics.(S. Kim, Wagner, Sabury, Gilman, et al., 2025)

Taken together, the four publications generated in this project build directly upon the latest peer-
reviewed literature while also introducing new principles that broaden the design space for both
HTLs and ETLs in PSCs. The project explicitly leveraged prior knowledge in polymer design, side-
chain engineering, NDI functionalization, interfacial chemistry, and energy-level alignment, which
accelerated progress from molecular synthesis to stable device operation. At the same time, the
innovations demonstrated here, including LC-phase–directed ordering in HTLs, elimination of
thermal transitions as a stability strategy, anchoring-driven ETL durability, and LUMO tuning
coupled with robust interfacial bonding, represent contributions beyond the current state of the
art. These results not only contextualize the project within ongoing R&D but also provide
actionable pathways for future DOE-supported materials innovation aimed at improving both
efficiency and long-term stability in perovskite photovoltaics.

6. Project Objectives:
This project was designed to address one of the key barriers to commercialization of perovskite
solar cells: the thermal and environmental instability of charge transport interfaces. Perovskites
are one of the most promising next generation photovoltaic technologies for delivering low cost,
high efficiency solar power. However, to contribute meaningfully to long term targets for reliable,
low levelized cost solar electricity, perovskite modules must meet stringent stability and damp
heat requirements. Our central objective was therefore to develop polymeric hole and electron
transport layers that can be processed from solution, deliver competitive device efficiencies, and
maintain performance under high temperature and damp heat conditions representative of fielded
modules. By replacing or outperforming conventional materials such as TiO₂, C₆₀, spiro-OMeTAD,
and PTAA, these polymeric charge transport layers have the potential to reduce processing cost,
enable scalable coating on large areas, and improve reliability, ultimately supporting domestic
manufacturing and deployment of durable perovskite-based photovoltaics.

The project goals were organized in two budget periods that together provided a pathway from
materials discovery to reliability demonstration and manufacturing feasibility. In the first budget
period, we focused on the design, synthesis, and integration of solution processable conjugated
polymers as HTLs and ETLs that could match state of the art device performance while being
intrinsically more thermally robust. Tasks 1 through 5 established a reproducible perovskite device
platform with average reference efficiencies of at least 18%, developed and characterized a series
of polymers including oligo(ether)-functionalized propylenedioxy-thiophene-based (e.g., Pro
BTTT), 2,5-bis(3-alkylthiophen-2-yl)thieno[2-b]thiophene-based (Ac BTTT),
thiadiazoloquinoxaline-based (Il-TQ), and Benzothiadiazole-based il-bTz, and incorporated them
as HTLs and ETLs in n i p device stacks. Each polymer was synthesized via Stille coupling,
verified structurally by NMR, and subjected to stringent purity and molecular weight criteria to
ensure reproducible electronic properties. Devices using these layers were evaluated under
standard AM1.5G efficiency measurements and subjected to stress testing at 85 °C under one
sun illumination with maximum power point tracking to probe their stability. The go/no go decision
at the end of budget period 1 required that devices incorporating the new polymers maintain high
efficiency under high temperature, constant illumination and show performance that does not
degrade significantly relative to controls, in combination with an initial industry feasibility

 Page 5 of 35

assessment and submission of at least two peer reviewed manuscripts. Meeting this decision
point ensured that only polymers with demonstrated performance and stability potential were
advanced to the cross linking and scaling phase.In the second budget period, the emphasis
shifted to extending lifetimes and bringing the technology closer to conditions relevant for
commercial modules. Building on the best performing HTLs and ETLs from budget period 1, we
pursued two complementary strategies. First, we examined the long term durability of TPT T (MB
C6) in devices at 65 °C for hundreds of hours, establishing whether the design principle of
suppressing thermal transitions translates into stable operation at elevated temperature. Second,
we expanded the ETL design space by synthesizing halogenated Naphthalene diimide (NDI) and
Perylene diimide (PDI)l molecules with phosphonic acid anchoring groups and electron
withdrawing substituents to tune energy levels and interfacial adhesion. Tasks 7 and 8
encompassed the synthesis of these NDI and PDI derivatives, detailed structural and elemental
analysis, electrochemical and optoelectronic characterization, and thermal gravimetric analysis
up to 500 °C to confirm that the new ETLs combine favorable energetics with high thermal stability.
These materials were then integrated into devices using TPT T (MB C6) as the HTL and subjected
to the same AM1.5G performance evaluation and 65 °C stability testing with maximum power
point tracking. The end of project milestones required achieving average efficiencies of 18 to 20%
and maintaining those efficiencies for hundreds of hours at 65 °C in nitrogen, conditions that
deliberately exceed typical literature benchmarks at 30 °C and thus more realistically probe the
robustness required for outdoor operation.

Across both budget periods, the technical work plan included explicit go/no go and end of project
decision points that ensured alignment with DOE’s objectives of funding technologies with a clear
pathway to impact. The budget period 1 go/no go milestone required devices with polymeric HTL
and ETL combinations to retain high efficiency after 500 hours at 85 °C and to pass damp heat
testing at 85 °C and 85% relative humidity for 1,000 hours without catastrophic degradation. It
also required a preliminary manufacturing feasibility analysis with industrial partners such as
Adesis and Phillips 66, focused on assessing synthetic scalability, cost, and compatibility with
large area coating processes, as well as the submission of manuscripts that disseminate the
scientific advances to the broader community. The end of project decision points extended this
logic by requiring not only long term stability at 65 °C with 20% efficiency devices, but also a more
detailed manufacturing feasibility analysis for both HTL and ETL materials in collaboration with
an industrial materials supplier and the submission of at least one additional manuscript on
molecular ETLs. Collectively, these milestones ensured that the project delivered both
fundamental advances in materials design and practical information on scalability and reliability.

By the conclusion of the project, we had demonstrated that carefully designed polymeric HTLs
and molecular ETLs can deliver high efficiency devices while exhibiting significantly improved
thermal stability compared to conventional charge transport layers. The work produced four peer
reviewed publications on both hole and electron transport layers, validated new design rules for
avoiding thermal transitions and leveraging cross linking to suppress morphology changes, and
established that anchored NDI based ETLs can achieve stability comparable to TiO₂ under
continuous illumination..

7. Project Results and Discussion:
The following sections summarize the major technical outcomes of the project and the extent to
which the original milestones were achieved. While the associated peer-reviewed publications
contain additional experimental detail, the discussion below is intended to provide sufficient

 Page 6 of 35

context for readers outside the project team to understand the purpose of each task, the rationale
behind the materials design strategies, and the significance of the resulting device performance
and stability trends.
For Task 1.0 (reference devices), the project exceeded the anticipated efficiency outcomes and
made partial progress on the most aggressive stability targets. We established a perovskite
reference device with phenylethyl ammonium iodide (PEAI) dual passivation that consistently
delivered high performance. Maximum power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 21%% was attained,
with median PCE and median stabilized MPP values of 19.48% and 19.95%, respectively,
measured over many substrates and pixels under AM1.5G one sun illumination at 25 °C with 0.1
cm² masked area. These data provide high confidence in the robustness of the reference
architecture and formed the baseline for HTL and ETL screening.
Milestone 1.1.1: Average efficiency of 18% for the reference devices, evaluated over at least 15
substrates with up to eight pixels each and approximately 240 data points, was exceeded. We
achieved a maximum PCE of 21%% and median PCE of 19.5%%for a perovskite with PEAI dual
passivation, confirming that the reference device platform outperformed the target and met the
intended quality standard for benchmarking new HTLs and ETLs. See Figure 2 for more details
on the device efficiency over time.
These reference devices served as the experimental baseline against which all newly synthesized
HTLs and ETLs were evaluated. Establishing a highly reproducible and efficient reference
architecture was essential because it allowed performance changes observed in later tasks to be
attributed primarily to the newly developed charge transport layers rather than variability in the
perovskite absorber or fabrication process.

and an early failure of the original in situ stability setup. See Figure 3 for more details on the
device stability over time as measured under 75C heating.
These accelerated aging measurements were designed to simulate the elevated temperatures
and continuous illumination conditions experienced by photovoltaic modules during long-term
outdoor operation. Monitoring device performance at the maximum power point under controlled
environmental conditions provides insight into degradation mechanisms associated with
interfaces and charge transport layers.

Tasks 2 and 3 focused on developing conjugated polymer HTLs with improved molecular
ordering and thermal robustness. The central hypothesis was that increasing backbone planarity
and suppressing thermal transitions would improve charge transport while minimizing morphology
changes during prolonged heating.
Task 2.0 focused on developing TPT TT as an HTL, after redirecting from the original Pro BTTT
design due to synthetic challenges. We implemented Stille cross coupling between bis
stannylated thienothiophene and a dialkoxy benzene triad to obtain an electron rich polymer,
and used high temperature GPC, NMR, and elemental analysis to confirm structure, purity, and
molecular weight. Devices incorporating TPT TT as an HTL were then fabricated and tested
under AM1.5G conditions. Synthetic details can be found in Figures 4-8
Milestone 1.2.1: Confirmation of the target electron rich donor polymer by NMR, originally
specified for Pro BTTT, was achieved via the TPT TT redesign. High temperature NMR spectra
of TPT TT were consistent with the repeat unit structure and free of significant side products,
satisfying the structural confirmation requirement with a more stable and synthetically accessible
backbone.

Milestone 1.2.2: Elemental analysis purity within 0.4%% of the theoretical CHNS composition for
the HTL polymer was achieved. TPT TT showed C, H, and S values that deviated by less than
0.4%% from the calculated composition, indicating high chemical purity and meeting the intended
quality metric.

Milestone 1.2.3: Number average molecular weight greater than 10 kDa with dispersity less than
3.0, targeted to ensure a high temperature resistant polymer, was achieved. High temperature
GPC measurements showed that TPT TT had a number average molecular weight of 19.2 kg/mol
with dispersity of 1.73, exceeding the molecular weight target and providing confidence in its film
forming and thermal properties.

The stabilization behavior observed after the initial aging period suggests that the TPT-T (MB/C6)
HTL undergoes less severe morphology evolution than earlier polymer generations. This is
important because morphology changes in HTLs are a major source of efficiency loss in
perovskite solar cells operated at elevated temperature.
The ETL development effort sought to replace conventional inorganic electron transport layers
with solution-processable organic materials that could potentially simplify manufacturing and
reduce processing temperature. A key challenge identified during this task was achieving
sufficient interfacial compatibility between the ETL surface and the subsequently deposited
perovskite layer.
Task 4.0 and Task 5.0 targeted new ETLs. We synthesized il T and il bTz polymers, as well as
polar modified variants of il T, and later shifted to small molecule NDI and PDI based ETLs when
polymeric ETLs showed persistent interfacial issues. Devices and perovskite films were fabricated
following the SOPO protocol, and wettability, reproducibility, and stability were systematically
evaluated.

Given the persistent interfacial instability and poor long-term durability observed for the polymeric
ETLs in Tasks 4.0 and 5.0, the project strategically pivoted toward NDI-based small-molecule
ETLs incorporating phosphonic acid anchoring groups and electron-withdrawing substituents
(Figure 28). This redesign remained fully consistent with the original technical scope of developing
organic ETLs, while addressing key limitations identified during device integration and stability
testing. The small-molecule approach was intended to preserve the advantages of solution-
processable organic ETLs while improving film adhesion to the FTO substrate, optimizing energy-
level alignment with the perovskite absorber, and enhancing thermal and operational stability.
These new ETLs were thoroughly characterized and subsequently integrated into PSC devices
for photovoltaic and stability evaluation.

End of Project Milestones:
Although several of the original numerical targets were intentionally ambitious and were not fully
achieved within the project period, the work successfully identified the dominant limitations
affecting both HTLs and ETLs and established experimentally validated molecular design
principles for improving thermal stability and interfacial robustness in perovskite solar cells. In
several cases, the project also identified more promising alternative materials pathways that
emerged from the mechanistic understanding developed during the award. The specific end-of-
project milestones and outcomes are summarized below.

EOP A: Achieving 18% efficiency after 500 hours at 85 °C in nitrogen and under damp heat
conditions for devices based on the originally proposed polymer HTL/ETL combinations was
partially achieved. The initially targeted Ac-BTTT, Pro-BTTT, iI-TQ, and iI-Tz-TQ-Tz materials did
not ultimately reach the desired combination of efficiency and long-term stability due to synthetic,
interfacial, and morphology-related limitations identified during the project. However, these
findings directly informed the development of alternative materials systems within the project
scope. In particular, the TPT-T (MB/C6) HTL family demonstrated substantially improved thermal
robustness, while the project strategically pivoted from polymeric ETLs to NDI-based small-
molecule ETLs with phosphonic acid anchoring groups to address interfacial instability and poor
adhesion. These redesigned ETLs exhibited significantly improved device stability, reduced

                                           Page 31 of 35

hysteresis, and competitive efficiencies under 65–85 °C stress conditions, providing a viable
pathway toward the intended reliability objectives.

EOP B: A feasibility analysis incorporating industrial input regarding scalability and
manufacturability of the developed HTL and ETL materials was successfully completed. The
chemical company 1 Material performed a preliminary manufacturing and cost assessment of
TPT-T (MB/C6), concluding that the HTL could potentially be produced at lower cost than
incumbent materials such as spiro-OMeTAD at commodity scale, with no fundamental synthetic
or manufacturing barriers identified for scale-up.EOP C: Submission of at least one manuscripts
to peer reviewed journals discussing the role of polymer layers on performance and reliability was
achieved. We published 4 papers in total on the synthesis and applications of new molecules and
polymers to perovskite solar cells. 2 of the papers involved HTLs and 2 others involved ETLs.

8. Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions:
The project met and, in some cases, exceeded the planned reference device performance
milestones, achieved most structural and purity milestones for the new HTL and ETL materials,
and delivered HTLs and ETLs with substantially improved thermal characteristics relative to
standard layers. Several of the most ambitious numerical efficiency and long term stability
milestones were not fully met, particularly for polymeric ETLs and very aggressive high
temperature aging conditions, but the work successfully identified the limitations of the original
designs, pivoted to more promising small molecule ETLs, and established robust methodologies
and design rules. The quantitative results and milestone comparisons demonstrate significant
technical progress toward stable, high performance organic charge transport layers for perovskite
solar cells, and provide a strong foundation for future DOE supported efforts to close the
remaining gap to commercial reliability targets.

9. Path Forward:
Future research and development will build directly on the materials design rules and interface
insights established in this project, with an immediate focus on closing the remaining gap between
demonstrated device stability and commercial qualification standards. For hole transport layers,
the next R&D step is to further optimize the TPT T (MB/C6) polymer family by systematically
tuning side chain architecture, dopant formulations, and cross linking strategies to suppress
residual morphology evolution under prolonged thermal and damp heat stress. The cross linkable
derivatives originally envisioned in the SOPO remain a high priority, as they offer a clear pathway
to immobilizing polymer microstructure during thermal cycling without sacrificing charge transport.
For electron transport layers, the results indicate that small molecule NDI based systems with
phosphonic acid anchoring groups provide a more reliable interfacial platform than fully
conjugated polymer ETLs. Future efforts will therefore focus on refining NDI and PDI derivatives
to deepen the LUMO, improve wettability for perovskite deposition, and further reduce hysteresis
and ion migration. These efforts will be coupled with advanced in situ characterization of
interfaces under bias and temperature to directly link molecular structure to long term operational
stability.

Opportunities for technology transfer and commercialization are strongest for the HTL materials
developed in this project. Preliminary analyses conducted with industry partners, including 1

                                          Page 32 of 35

Material, indicate that the synthesis routes for TPT T (MB/C6) and related polymers are scalable
and potentially lower cost than incumbent HTLs such as spiro OMeTAD. Near term
commercialization pathways include licensing of the polymer formulations to specialty chemical
suppliers for pilot scale production and evaluation in large area coating processes such as slot
die or blade coating. Remaining technical risks that must be addressed prior to commercialization
include demonstrating reproducible performance on large area devices, achieving stability under
full IEC damp heat protocols, and ensuring long term dopant stability or developing fully dopant
free formulations. For ETLs, while the small molecule NDI systems show promise, additional
optimization is required to simultaneously meet efficiency, stability, and manufacturability targets.
Key barriers to immediate commercialization include sensitivity of perovskite ETL interfaces to
surface chemistry, the need for tighter control of interfacial energetics across different perovskite
compositions, and limited lifetime data beyond several hundred hours under realistic stress
conditions. Addressing these risks through continued DOE supported research and close
collaboration with industry will be critical for translating the advances reported here into
deployable perovskite photovoltaic technologies.
