PsychoSafe : Eliciting Psychologically-Informed Refusals in Large Language Models
Gianluca Barmina, Federico Torrielli, Sven Harms, Jacob Nielsen, Felix Mächtle, Stine Lyngsø Beltoft, Peter Schneider-Kamp, Thomas Eisenbarth, Lukas Galke Poech, Anne Lauscher

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) routinely face requests that should be refused, creating a trade-off between helpfulness and harm prevention. However, refusals themselves can be helpful. In high-risk interactions involving crisis, coercion, or escalating intent, blunt non-compliance may prevent direct harm while still failing to support the needs of the person behind the request. We present PsychoSafe , a psychologically-informed refusal framework that reframes refusal as structured supportive communication grounded in evidence-based intervention strategies. To develop PsychoSafe , we construct a corpus of 8,019 prompt–response pairs spanning five psychologically salient risk domains and apply prompting and parameter-efficient fine-tuning to Qwen 3.5 27B . On a balanced validation set of 500 prompts, evaluated with an LLM judge and validated through human ratings, PsychoSafe prompting improves overall refusal quality by 28.1% over a generic baseline, with particularly strong gains in external resource referral (+46.8%) and psychological grounding (+34.8%), while preserving downstream performance on non-refusal tasks. Fine-tuning achieves near-perfect refusal and resource-referral rates but reduces response relevance. Additional evaluations on SORRY-Bench and XSTest show strong in-domain robustness but limited out-of-domain generalization, suggesting that future work should diversify fine-tuning data to help models apply interventions selectively rather than schematically. Content warning: This paper discusses and contains examples of sensitive and potentially distressing content.

1
Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in contexts where they must navigate the tension between being maximally helpful and avoiding harm
3
;
32
. When a model determines that a request should not be fulfilled, it produces a
refusal
: a response that declines to comply with the request. Current refusal techniques, however, largely treat safety as a binary classification problem.
This framing reduces the rich space of opportunities between full compliance and outright denial to a single decision boundary, discarding the opportunity to provide genuine support to users.

A growing body of work thus recognizes that the helpfulness–harmfulness trade-off need not be zero-sum
14
;
21
. Rather than simply blocking unsafe queries, models can acknowledge the underlying need, explain why full compliance is problematic, and redirect users toward constructive alternatives. We refer to such responses as
helpful refusals
. Recent efforts in this direction have begun to decompose refusals into components such as a refusal statement and a rationale
21
, and to frame safety-aware behavior as a form of competence under constraint
14
. However, this line of work has so far lacked a principled framework for
what makes a refusal genuinely helpful
to users in high-risk situations.

We argue that psychological theory offers exactly such a framework. As
6
recently urged, human–AI interaction research that is not grounded in psychological theory risks producing findings that are tied to a particular model version and fail to generalize as technology evolves. Their call for coherence with existing theory, attention to what distinguishes human–AI from human–human interaction, and explicit discussion of generalizability applies directly to the study of refusals. A user who receives a refusal is experiencing a social interaction, in which their autonomy, emotional state, and sense of being understood are all at stake. Decades of research in counseling psychology, motivational interviewing, and crisis communication provide well-validated principles for how to deliver unwelcome messages in ways that preserve the recipient’s agency and well-being
(e.g.,
27
;
39
,
inter alia
)
.

In this work, we operationalize these insights as
psychologically-informed refusals
(
PsychoSafe
). Starting from existing safety datasets, we identify five risk categories suitable for psychologically informed intervention and construct the
PsychoSafe
dataset, consisting of 8,019 prompt–refusal pairs grounded in principles such as empathic acknowledgment and constructive redirection. Using
Qwen3.5-35B-A3B
, we study two complementary elicitation strategies: (1) prompting with a dedicated psychologically-informed system prompt, and (2) fine-tuning on reasoning traces distilled from a larger prompted model. We evaluate both approaches using psychologically grounded criteria (e.g.,
referral to external resources
) on 500 held-out safety-relevant queries.
PsychoSafe
prompting improves overall refusal quality by 28.1% over a generic baseline while preserving downstream performance on non-refusal tasks. Fine-tuning achieves near-perfect refusal and referral rates but reduces response relevance. Additional evaluations show strong in-domain robustness but weaker out-of-domain generalization, suggesting that broader fine-tuning data is needed for more context-sensitive intervention behavior.

Contributions

Our contributions are fourfold. First, we introduce
PsychoSafe
(Figure
1
), a psychologically-informed refusal framework grounded in validated principles of supportive communication and structured around five psychologically salient safety domains, bridging AI alignment research with established intervention strategies from psychology
(
6
)
. Second, we construct a dataset of 8,019 psychologically-informed prompt–refusal pairs together with evaluation criteria targeting qualities such as empathy, psychological grounding, and referral to external resources. Third, we show that psychologically-informed prompting substantially improves refusal quality, yielding responses that are perceived as more helpful, empathetic, and constructive while preserving safety and downstream non-refusal capabilities. Fourth, we demonstrate that these behaviors can be embedded into model weights through fine-tuning, offering a scalable way for psychologically-informed refusals.

Taken together, our results suggest that helpfulness and harm prevention are not necessarily opposing objectives: by drawing on established human intervention practices, language models can refuse unsafe requests while still providing supportive and constructive responses.

2
Psychologically-informed Refusals

To operationalize helpful refusals in safety-critical settings, we first need a shared representation of the risk space and a principled account of what a supportive refusal should contain. We therefore construct
PsychoSafe
in three stages: (1) we harmonize existing safety datasets and taxonomies into broader risk domains, (2) identify evidence-based psychological intervention principles that can be adapted to single-turn model responses, and (3) instantiate these principles as reusable refusal exemplars and couple them with existing unsafe prompts from the benchmarks collected. The resulting collection treats refusal not as a binary endpoint, but as a structured communicative, supportive act.

2.1
Harmonizing risk categories

We aggregate safety taxonomies from public benchmarks and harmonize them through clustering of related risk labels. Based on this unified view, we identify those categories where psychology-informed refusal strategies are most appropriate.

Selection of datasets

Existing safety benchmarks vary substantially in how they define and categorize harmful content. To establish a stable foundation for psychology-informed refusals, we build on the dataset collection curated by
38
. From this collection, we select 16 benchmarks
(
10
;
29
, e.g.,)
that (
i
) are publicly available and licensed for academic use, (
ii
) contain safety-relevant prompts paired with responses and annotated with safety categories, and (
iii
) cover domains of psychological relevance. The complete list of datasets is provided in Appendix
C
. This selection yields a corpus of 54,109 potentially harmful prompts, from which we extract the finest-grained available risk labels. We further enrich these risk labels with labels from three established risk taxonomies
44
;
16
;
20
.

Harmonization via clustering

Because safety benchmarks employ heterogeneous and partially overlapping taxonomies, we harmonize the extracted risk labels through semantic clustering. Specifically, we compute embeddings for all categories using
sentence-t5-large

30
and cluster them with HDBSCAN
9
. Additional details on the clustering procedure are provided in Appendix
A
. The resulting taxonomy abstracts away from dataset-specific nomenclature while preserving distinctions relevant for downstream intervention design.

Selection of risk clusters

Not all risk domains are equally suitable for psychology-informed refusal strategies. We therefore evaluate the consolidated clusters according to five criteria: (
i
) whether the topic is sufficiently specific to support targeted interventions, (
ii
) whether brief intervention strategies exist for the domain, (
iii
) whether the cluster corresponds to an established clinical or psychosocial problem space, (
iv
) whether it reflects acute or escalating risk, and (
v
) whether it disproportionately affects vulnerable populations. An overview of our assessments according to these criteria is provided in Appendix
E
. This process identifies five domains in which refusals should provide more than simple non-compliance by incorporating supportive or de-escalatory intervention strategies. We refer to these domains as
risk clusters
.

Suicide and Self-Harm:
prompts involving suicidal ideation, self-injury, lethal means, self-punishment, or related forms of self-endangerment. We treat this domain as the most acute risk category, requiring de-escalation, grounding, and referral to crisis-support resources.

Sexual Crimes:
prompts involving sexual violence or exploitation, including coercion, assault, child sexual abuse material, grooming, and trafficking-related content. Refusals in this domain must withhold assistance while emphasizing consent, responsibility, and the protection of potential victims.

Substance Use:
prompts involving substance use, misuse, dependency, or requests related to alcohol, drugs, or tobacco. Here, psychology-informed refusals aim not only to block harmful assistance, but also to encourage reflection, self-monitoring, and help-seeking behavior.

Weapons:
prompts concerning weapon acquisition, construction, modification, or use, including both conventional weapons and high-consequence agents such as chemical or biological weapons. Refusals in this domain prioritize interruption of harmful action, reflection on consequences, and redirection toward safer alternatives.

Violence:
prompts involving physical or psychological violence, threats, violent planning, or the glorification of harm. Corresponding refusal strategies aim to reduce escalation, redirect attention to consequences, and encourage safer action.

2.2
Leveraging psychological best practices

Our refusal design draws on brief psychological intervention frameworks appropriate for high-risk, time-constrained interactions
(
39
, e.g.,)
. We restrict our selection to approaches that can be expressed in short responses, are suitable for non-specialists, follow a clear procedural structure, and have been described and evaluated in the psychology literature. Despite targeting different risk domains, these intervention frameworks converge on several common principles: acknowledging the person without endorsing the request, reducing immediate risk, preserving agency, encouraging self-efficacy, suggesting concrete next steps, and facilitating access to professional or social support.
These principles are instantiated differently across the five risk domains. Suicide- and self-harm-related refusals primarily draw on crisis-oriented approaches such as Psychological First Aid
39
, Mental Health First Aid
22
, Question–Persuade–Refer
2
, and safety-planning techniques
42
. Substance-related refusals are informed by brief intervention and motivational approaches that emphasize reflective awareness, motivation for change, and short-horizon self-regulation
(
27
;
4
;
7
)
. Refusals involving violence, weapons, and sexual crimes place greater emphasis on interrupting harmful action, foregrounding responsibility toward others, and redirecting users toward safe, non-escalatory alternatives
(
27
)
. Full mappings are available in Appendix
D
.
Operationally, these principles are distilled into a library of intervention strategies that we later instantiate as 14 ideal-response exemplars. Across all domains, refusals follow the same high-level structure: (1) a clear but supportive refusal, (2) a brief psychologically grounded de-escalation or self-help step, (3) referral to relevant external resources, and (4) a short supportive closing. This shared template ensures consistency across domains while allowing the intervention content itself to remain domain-specific.

2.3
The
PsychoSafe
dataset

Based on this framework, we construct
PsychoSafe
, a dataset of 8,019 prompt–response pairs spanning the five psychologically relevant risk categories. We derive the resource from the subset of collected prompts that can be confidently mapped to the risk clusters. The resulting collection contains entries for suicide and self-harm (2,578), crimes (sexual) (326), substance (1,998), weapon (1,740), and violence (1,377).
Each entry stores the prompt, a psychologically-informed refusal response, the risk cluster, and provenance metadata.

We first write 14 hand-crafted responses following the template from Section
2.2
, each grounded in a specific psychological intervention strategy and formulated as an exemplar of the target refusal style. These responses are intentionally generic at the instance level but specific at the level of psychological mechanism. They can therefore be mapped to large numbers of prompts within the same risk cluster while preserving conceptual consistency. We provide an example response in Appendix
F
.

Finally, these human-crafted responses are reviewed for psychological appropriateness by an expert in psychology, clarity, and non-escalatory tone before they are used in the dataset.
PsychoSafe
should therefore be understood as an alignment resource in which refusal behavior is explicitly grounded in psychologically motivated principles.

3
Elicitation

Our dataset provides cluster-level target responses, but the intervention frameworks support more context-sensitive refusals than a fixed template can capture. We therefore operationalize
PsychoSafe
in two complementary settings. First, we evaluate whether the desired refusal style can be induced at inference time through a dedicated system prompt (
in-context learning
). Second, we evaluate whether the same behavior can be internalized within the model’s parameters by fine-tuning the same model on the human-crafted responses augmented with imputed reasoning traces (
supervised fine-tuning
). For this, reasoning traces were generated by GPT-OSS-120B
31
based on the input and the desired output. Both settings use the powerful open-source model
Qwen-3.5-27B

(
35
)
as the base model, allowing a direct comparison of prompt-driven and fine-tuning-driven behavior change on the same base model architecture.

3.1
In-context learning

We encode the complete
PsychoSafe
framework in a single system prompt (Appendix
B
). The prompt instructs the model to respond to sensitive messages with a fixed four-part structure following the responses in our dataset (Section
2.3
): (1) a warm acknowledgment paired with a gentle refusal, (2) a personalized self-help step grounded in the intervention strategy associated with the detected risk cluster, (3) matched professional resources presented as genuine options, and (4) a brief hopeful closing tied to something the user expressed.
For each of the five risk clusters (Section
2.1
), we suggest designated evidence-based strategy according to our mapping (e.g., Psychological First Aid and Safety Planning for suicidality; Motivational Interviewing for substance use and violence), cluster-specific helplines and two examples with responses.
Personalization is further encouraged through explicit guidelines: the model is instructed to mirror the user’s language register, reference their specific situation and emotional cues, and vary phrasing across interactions.
When a message implicates multiple clusters, the prompt instructs the model to prioritize the most acute risk: suicide and self-harm always first, blend relevant strategies, and provide resources for all applicable clusters in a single coherent response.
A further design goal is to preserve normal assistant behavior outside the targeted domains: the model is instructed to treat educational or research-oriented questions as non-sensitive and to use context to distinguish intent, avoiding over-triggering on benign requests involving sensitive topics.

3.2
Supervised fine-tuning

While in-context learning demonstrates whether psychologically-informed refusals can be induced through prompting alone, deployment benefits from a model that reproduces this behavior without relying on a long system prompt, as it has been shown, that long inputs decrease an LLMs performance
36
;
24
. To mitigate that issue, we experiment with finetuning the desired behavior into the model.

Reasoning trace imputation

Reasoning models emit an explicit reasoning segment before the final answer (the completion). In practice, this means that the raw generations consists of two phases: One with the intermediate reasoning, encapsulated by special-tokens and then the user-facing completion generated as a consequence of the conditional generation from the beginning throughout the reasoning phase.
We therefore impute this boundary by generating a reasoning-trace based on each in the
input
and and yielded
completion
.
This serves two purposes. First, it allows us to analyze elicited behavior at two levels: the model’s
constructed
intermediate rationale and the final refusal shown to the user. Second, it enables the construction of clean supervised training data from teacher-models generations without requiring the student model to reproduce the teacher’s full reasoning trace verbatim.
In the current implementation, the student is supervised on the final answer, while generated reasoning traces are retained as auxiliary artifacts for analysis and future training variants.

Fine-tuning on imputed reasoning traces

Our fine-tuning dataset consists of the 8,019 prompt-response pairs, augmented with reasoning traces as described above. We fine-tune the model with cross-entropy loss calculated on the reasoning traces and the human-crafted responses, but not the user prompts.
Specifically, we fine-tune
Qwen 3.5 27B
using LoRA applied to all attention and feed-forward projection layers with rank
r{=}1
,
\alpha{=}32
, and no dropout (details in Appendix
I
).

Experiments

In-context learning

We evaluate two system prompt configurations applied to
Qwen-3.5-27B
at inference time. A minimal instruction (

Finetuning

In addition to the in-context learning setting, we evaluate the fine-tuned model. We also consider mixed conditions where the fine-tuned model is evaluated with both prompt variants (
v0

v1

Experimental procedure

We evaluate both the starting model
Qwen-3.5-27B
and our fine-tuned variant with both system prompt variants on a held-out validation set of 500 examples stratified over risk clusters (100 per cluster). Evaluation is carried out via LLM-as-a-judge based on 4 criteria. We validate the performance of our LLM judge against human ratings, as will be detailed below.

We additionally evaluate standard refusal benchmarks: SORRY-Bench
49

37

51

Evaluating psychological grounding

Judge selection.

We considered three open-weight candidate judges, namely

Qualitative error analysis

We performed a qualitative analysis of model answers understand why Relevance criterion is lower in the fine-tuned model under
v0
prompting. The main pattern among low scores on this criterion is that the model responses are insufficiently context-sensitive. Many of them rely on a generic crisis-intervention template rather than adapting to the user’s specific situation. Other observed errors are that model sometimes framed factual, ambiguous, or benign prompts as acute crisis scenarios, provides mismatched external resources, and assumes user intent or emotional state without sufficient evidence. In some cases, it also misread victim, witness, or emergency situations as perpetrator-intent scenarios, leading to moral reflection where immediate, domain-specific guidance would be more relevant. These patterns explain why the criterion tends to be lower the other criteria despite otherwise strong safety-oriented behavior.

5
Discussion

The results suggest that refusal quality is more design variable than a fixed model property.
PsychoSafe
prompting achieves the highest overall score, with gains concentrated where the base model is weakest:
Qwen 3.5 27B
already refuses most harmful requests under a generic prompt but rarely pairs refusal with support or redirection, so the largest improvements come in external resource referral and psychological grounding rather than in the refusal rate.

Supervised fine-tuning internalizes much of this behavior, reaching near-perfect refusal and resource-referral rates and remaining nearly indifferent to the prompt version. Relevance drops relative to
PsychoSafe
prompting, however, likely because the SFT targets emphasize human-crafted templates for the five risk clusters. Our qualitative analysis shows the model applies a crisis-intervention schema too broadly: it captures the
PsychoSafe
template but not the pragmatic judgment needed to apply it selectively. A different training strategy may improve both relevance and stability.

The capability trade-off is modest (Section
4.2
) and, in safety-critical settings, outweighed by these gains. SORRY-Bench and XSTest nonetheless delimit them.

Qwen3.5-27B-psysafe
brings SORRY-Bench compliance to near-zero across all prompt conditions and 19 linguistic mutations, and over-refuses less than the base model on benign prompts, ruling out indiscriminate refusal. Its safety rate on out-of-domain adversarial prompts falls well below the base, however, indicating weak generalization beyond the five training domains. Broader coverage will require more diverse fine-tuning data;
PsychoSafe
is therefore best suited for domain-specific deployments, such as mental health or crisis-intervention platforms, where the risks are known and high-quality refusals are crucial.

6
Conclusion

We presented
PsychoSafe
, a psychologically informed refusal framework for helpful refusals through structured supportive communication. We introduced a dataset of 8,019 prompt-response pairs across five psychologically salient risk domains. We proposed eliciting psychologically informed refusals through in-context learning or supervised fine-tuning. Results obtained through a human-validated LLM judge display substantial gains in refusal quality.
These results are complemented with experiments on standard benchmarks measuring safety and general capabilities, indicating generality of our approach.
Future work may broaden the diversity of the responses for fine-tuning.

Limitations

This work has several limitations and points to several directions for improvement.

The intervention strategies used in
PsychoSafe
were originally designed for human–human settings. In the current framework, they are abstracted into short written responses. While this can support de-escalation and redirection, it should not be interpreted as therapy, diagnosis, or crisis management.

Domain coverage is intentionally narrow. The framework focuses on five psychologically salient risk clusters and therefore does not address the full space of unsafe model behavior. Many harmful domains are safety-relevant without being clearly amenable to short psychological intervention.

The released artifact is English-only, and its built-in support resources are primarily tailored to the United States and the United Kingdom.
PsychoSafe
would benefit from broader language and resource coverage to make it more general and accessible.

The
PsychoSafe
fine-tuned model shows limited personalization (as measured by our relevance criterion) in some refusals, likely due to limited variability in the training data. Improving response diversity and context sensitivity may lead to stronger performance and more robustly embed psychological refusal behavior in the model.

Finally, the current results are based on single-turn validation prompts. Multi-turn behavior, adversarial robustness, cross-lingual generalization, and real-user effects remain open.

Ethical Considerations

Our work aims to reduce risks arising from blunt or poorly designed refusals in high-risk interactions. While our experiments show that psychology-informed refusals improve multiple aspects of refusal quality, our approach remains generative rather than rule-based and therefore cannot guarantee appropriate behavior for all possible inputs or conversational contexts. In particular, miscalibrated refusals may still cause harm by providing inadequate support, escalating distress, creating false reassurance, or failing to appropriately respond to crisis situations.
Moreover, psychology-informed refusal strategies introduce dual-use concerns. Supportive and empathetic refusal behavior could potentially be misused to create unwarranted perceptions of emotional competence, therapeutic authority, or interpersonal trust in systems that are neither clinically validated nor capable of genuine psychological care. We therefore emphasize that
PsychoSafe
is not intended as a substitute for professional mental-health intervention, crisis counseling, or medical advice.
Given these risks, we strongly recommend extensive pre-deployment evaluation, including stress-testing under adversarial, emotionally charged, and out-of-distribution scenarios, as well as continuous monitoring and iterative correction of refusal behavior after deployment. In particular, future evaluations should investigate failure modes across diverse cultural contexts, vulnerable populations, and multilingual settings to ensure that supportive refusal strategies remain safe, appropriate in practice.

Appendix A
Details on Harmonizing Risk Categories

To group semantically related risk categories, we perform a clustering analysis over the collected safety datasets. In addition, we include the taxonomies proposed by
44
,
16
, and
20
, as these provide widely used categorizations of harmful content.

Semantic clustering

We first compute embeddings for all risk-category labels using
sentence-t5-large

(
30
)
, which we selected based on stable performance in preliminary experiments. To enable clustering in a lower-dimensional semantic space, we apply UMAP for dimensionality reduction followed by HDBSCAN
9
for density-based clustering.

Hyperparameter optimization

Because both methods are sensitive to parameter selection, we conduct a systematic grid search over the parameter spaces of both algorithms: For UMAP, we vary
n_neighbors

\in\{2,5,10,20,30,40,50\}
,
min_dist

\in\{0.0,0.1\}
, and
n_components

\in\{5,10,20,30\}
. For HDBSCAN, we vary
min_cluster_size

\in\{6,7,8\}
,
min_samples

\in\{1,2\}
, and
cluster_selection_epsilon

\in\{0.0,0.1\}
. This results in 672 parameter combinations.
To identify suitable configurations, we analyze both the resulting number of clusters and the proportion of noise points, i.e., labels that are not assigned to any cluster. We prioritize parameter settings with comparatively low noise rates while aiming for a cluster structure that remains interpretable and aligned with existing safety taxonomies. Guided by the granularity of prior taxonomies, we target approximately 15–20 semantically coherent clusters.
Because UMAP relies on stochastic approximation procedures, the resulting embeddings and cluster assignments are not fully deterministic. We therefore treat the grid search not as a mechanism for identifying a single optimal configuration, but rather as a way to identify a stable parameter region. Final parameter selection is additionally informed by qualitative inspection of the resulting cluster structures, including assessments of cluster-size balance and semantic coherence.
Based on this, we select the following parameters for the final clustering pipeline: for UMAP,
n_neighbors
=30,
min_dist
=0.0, and
n_components
=5; for HDBSCAN,
min_cluster_size
=7,
min_samples
=1, and
cluster_selection_epsilon
=20. These settings are used to generate the final set of clusters.

Labeling of risk clusters

To automatically derive interpretable cluster names, we perform a TF–IDF analysis over the risk-category labels contained in each cluster. Specifically, we compute TF–IDF scores over uni- and bigrams and assign each cluster the highest-scoring aggregated term.

Appendix I
Finetuning Details

The model is trained in full precision on the
PsychoSafe
reasoning split for 5 epochs with a maximum sequence length of 4,096 tokens, a batch size of 4, and no gradient accumulation. Optimization uses AdamW with 8-bit quantization, a peak learning rate of
1{\times}10^{-4}
with cosine decay and 100 warmup steps, and weight decay of 0.01. Loss is computed on responses only, with the reasoning trace delimited by
<think>
…
</think>
markers excluded from the supervision signal, so the model is trained to produce the final user-facing refusal rather than to reproduce intermediate reasoning steps verbatim. All experiments have been carried out on a H100 GPU.

Appendix M
Use of AI Assistants

AI assistants were used only to support coding, grammar and style revisions, and literature search.

Appendix N
Information about Human Annotator

For the judge selection process, annotations were provided by a student assistant studying business informatics and fluent in english. The annotator was informed about how the annotation data would be used, in addition to receiving the main annotation instructions, and was aware of the broader project context. The collected data consisted only of annotation judgments and did not involve sensitive personal information or interventions with human participants; therefore, ethics committee approval was not required.

Matching of Dataset Intended Use

The datasets introduced by
