 ABSTRACT

This study investigates the utility of multimodal, or functional, atomic force microscopy (AFM) for the
characterization of individual particles and particle ensembles. In single-particle analyses, AFM imaging
modes provided orthogonal insights into mechanical and magnetic properties that are not accessible
through conventional electron microscopy. Although the experiments were inherently delicate and time-
intensive, with an observed sample loss rate of approximately 30%, these techniques enabled the
qualitative differentiation of grain structures and magnetic domains, underscoring the challenges of
experimental robustness. For particle ensembles, AFM enabled the extraction of reliable 2D and 3D
particle size distributions. However, efforts to chemically differentiate particles based on mechanical
property contrasts were limited by scaling effects and the qualitative nature of the data.

Overall, multimodal AFM offers valuable complementary information, but its application requires careful
consideration of methodological limitations, particularly for sample integrity, calibration, and scalability
of mechanical and magnetic measurements. This report is organized to first address the application of
AFM to single-particle analysis using various imaging modalities, followed by its potential role in
ensemble-level particle screening and differentiation.

 1. BACKGROUND

1.1 SCOPE AND OBJECTIVES OF AFM-BASED PARTICLE CHARACTERIZATION

The primary goal of this work was to evaluate the efficacy of AFM in providing meaningful orthogonal
information for characterizing metallic particulates. Although conventional techniques offer critical
insights into surface morphology and composition, they do not capture certain mechanical, topographical,
or nanoscale features that AFM can reveal. By leveraging the high spatial resolution and multifunctional
capabilities of AFM, this study aimed to assess the technique’s potential to complement existing
nondestructive methods and enhance the overall understanding of the physical properties of metallic
particulates.

AFM was selected for this study because of its unique ability to provide high-resolution, nanoscale
information about a material’s surface topography, mechanical properties, and local interactions. Unlike
electron-based imaging techniques, AFM operates through physical probe–sample interactions, allowing
it to characterize both conductive and nonconductive materials without the need for extensive sample
preparation or conductive coatings. Its versatility across different operational modes, such as tapping
mode for surface mapping or force modulation for mechanical property analysis, makes it a powerful
complementary tool to traditional nondestructive techniques.

In this study, we aimed to determine the utility of AFM for characterizing metallic particulates, including
individual particles and particle ensembles. We focused on three modalities, based on prior experience
characterizing bulk metals: standard tapping mode, AM-FM (amplitude modulation–frequency
modulation), and MFM (magnetic force microscopy). The three modes are described in the following
section, and their applicability and utility for characterizing individual particles and particle ensembles are
detailed in the subsequent sections.

We emphasize that extracting quantitative information from any functional imaging mode is extremely
challenging. These techniques often rely on indirect observables (e.g., phase shifts, frequency changes, or
amplitude variations) that must be interpreted through complex models and calibration procedures.
Factors such as tip geometry, environmental conditions, and sample heterogeneity can introduce
significant uncertainty. As a result, AFM-based functional imaging can reveal relative contrast and spatial

trends with high fidelity, but translating these signals into absolute material properties requires careful
experimental design, rigorous calibration, and, potentially, complementary measurements. Because of
these challenges, this study focused on qualitative comparisons to assess spatial variations in physical and
functional properties, rather than attempting to extract fully quantitative mechanical or magnetic
parameters.

1.2 AFM MODALITIES EMPLOYED

AFM is a high-resolution scanning probe microscopy technique that maps surface topography and other
nanoscale properties by physically interacting with a sample using a sharp probe attached to a flexible
cantilever. A nanometer-sharp tip mounted on a microfabricated cantilever scans the sample surface.
Scanning electron micrographs of a typical AFM cantilever are shown in Figure 1, and the whole chip
assembly is shown on the left and the sample interaction tip is shown on the right. In operation, tip–
sample interaction forces (van der Waals, electrostatic, magnetic, etc.) cause deflection of the cantilever.
To detect this, a laser beam is focused on the back of the cantilever and reflected into a position-sensitive
photodetector. As the cantilever bends in response to surface features, the change in laser position is
recorded, providing a measure of the tip’s vertical deflection. An integrated electronic feedback loop
functions to maintain a constant interaction force (or height) between the tip and the sample by adjusting
the vertical position (z-axis) of the sample or tip using a piezoelectric scanner. Image generation or
mapping is performed by rastering the tip across the sample. The feedback-controlled z-movement needed
to maintain constant interaction is used to reconstruct the 3D topography of the surface at nanometer or
subnanometer resolution.

1.2.1 Standard Tapping Mode

Standard tapping mode, also known as intermittent contact mode, is one of the most commonly used and
well-established imaging modalities in AFM. In this modality, the cantilever with a sharp tip oscillates at
or near its resonant frequency as it approaches the sample surface and periodically taps the surface of the
sample, producing minimal lateral forces (that can damage soft or delicate samples). Although the
cantilever oscillates, the tip experiences small deflections that are measured using a laser. This is done by
focusing the laser on the back side of the cantilever and using a position-sensitive quadrant photodiode to
track the angle of deflection. The precise deflection angle correlates with the absolute topography of the
sample, enabling the production of high-resolution 3D topographic images for which the technique is
renowned.

1.2.2 Amplitude Modulation-Frequency Modulation

AM-FM imaging represents a significant advancement over conventional phase contrast in tapping mode
by enabling quantitative mapping of mechanical properties at the nanoscale. Although phase contrast
provides qualitative information based on energy dissipation or tip–sample interaction forces, it lacks the
specificity and calibration necessary to extract material parameters such as stiffness or elastic modulus. In
contrast, AM-FM directly probes local mechanical responses by monitoring frequency shifts in a higher-
order cantilever resonance. Through appropriate modeling and calibration, this technique yields spatially
resolved maps of mechanical moduli, offering a more rigorous and interpretable dataset than phase
imaging alone. These capabilities make AM-FM particularly valuable for characterizing heterogeneous or
compositionally complex materials that have subtle but significant mechanical contrast.

AM-FM is a dual-frequency AFM technique in which the cantilever is simultaneously excited at two of
its resonant frequencies. The first, typically the fundamental resonance, is amplitude-modulated and used
in feedback to maintain a constant oscillation amplitude, enabling high-resolution topographic imaging
similar to conventional tapping mode. The second, higher-order resonance is frequency modulated, with
its frequency shifts monitored in response to tip–sample interactions. These shifts are sensitive to local
variations in mechanical properties such as stiffness and elasticity. This dual-resonance approach enables
simultaneous acquisition of surface morphology and mechanical property maps with nanometer-scale
spatial resolution, facilitating direct correlations between topography and variations in stiffness or
elasticity.

The ability to resolve mechanical heterogeneity at the nanoscale is especially important when studying
particles formed under nonequilibrium or process-specific conditions. Local variations in stiffness or
elasticity can reveal the presence of microstructural defects, phase segregation, or residual stress—
features often introduced during rapid cooling, incomplete reactions, or other kinetically constrained
events. By correlating mechanical property maps with topographic and compositional data, AM-FM
imaging offers a powerful means of linking observed particle characteristics to their formation history.
This, in turn, enhances our understanding of how processing conditions influence the mechanical integrity
and performance of particulate materials, which is critical for both forensic analysis and materials
development.

1.2.3 Magnetic Force Microscopy

MFM is a powerful extension of AFM that enables the characterization of magnetic properties at the
nanoscale and simultaneously captures surface topography. It operates using a magnetized cantilever and
a dual-pass scanning technique. In the first pass, the cantilever scans the sample in standard tapping mode
to acquire high-resolution topographic data. In the second pass, the cantilever is lifted to a user-defined
height and retraces the previously mapped topography. At this elevated distance, short-range forces such
as van der Waals interactions are minimized, allowing the cantilever to respond primarily to long-range
magnetic interactions between the tip and the sample.

One of the key advantages of MFM is its non-surface-sensitive nature. Because the magnetic signal is
collected above the sample surface, MFM is far less dependent on surface flatness or cleanliness than
other AFM-based techniques. This makes it particularly well-suited for analyzing rough, irregular, or
delicate samples where achieving a pristine surface is impractical.

MFM can be used to determine general magnetic behavior based on the presence or absence of magnetic
signal and its spatial distribution. In ferromagnetic materials, MFM can resolve domain structures and
quantify domain sizes, providing insight into the magnetic microstructure. These features are often
directly influenced by the material’s formation history, including factors such as cooling rate or

 3

mechanical stress. As such, MFM can serve as a diagnostic tool for linking observed magnetic behavior to
processing conditions or environmental exposure.

 2. RESULTS

2.1 INDIVIDUAL PARTICLE CHARACTERIZATION VIA AFM

2.1.1 Sample Preparation

Focused ion beam (FIB) thinning was performed to prepare individual particles identified through
scanning electron microscopy (SEM) screening as being of interest based on their elemental composition.
The coordinates of the selected particles were recorded, and the particles were subsequently relocated in a
FIB-equipped instrument for targeted sample preparation. The selected particles were coated with a
protective platinum layer, and trenches were milled out of either side of the particle before lifting the
particle cross-section lamellae. The lamellae was then affixed to a transmission electron microscopy
(TEM) grid as shown in Figure 2. The resulting sample is approximately 100–200 nm thick with a flat
enough surface to perform AFM measurements.

zoomed in view of finger B with the mounted particle cross-section lamellae. (Right) An even further zoomed view

After appropriate thinning and mounting onto TEM lift-out grids, the samples underwent a series of
TEM-based characterizations, including high-resolution SEM imaging with energy-dispersive X-ray
spectroscopy for compositional analysis and Kikuchi diffraction mapping via electron diffraction for
structural phase identification. Following TEM analysis, the samples were submitted for further
characterization using AFM. The selected AFM techniques were chosen to provide complementary
information beyond that obtained in prior characterization efforts. An unforeseen challenge in this
workflow was the handling of the fragile sample grids and the precise positioning of the AFM probe head
at an appropriate imaging height. During grid mounting and probe alignment, we observed a sample loss
rate of approximately 33%.

2.1.2 Topographic Imaging Single Particles

Figure 3 shows a scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) image of a tantalum steel particle
alongside corresponding AFM topographic and amplitude-based measurements. STEM imaging,
supported by electron diffraction (not shown), revealed a complex microstructure consisting of spherical
nickel-rich inclusions, iron–chromium oxide phases consistent with a spinel-type structure, and a tantalum

 4

oxide layer capping the particle. After FIB sectioning, AFM topography confirmed that the exposed
surface was nearly planar, as expected from controlled milling.

Although amplitude signal contrast in tapping mode is inherently qualitative, the AFM measurements
successfully resolved individual grains within the sectioned particle. These features correspond well with
the microstructural domains observed in the STEM image, suggesting that AFM can provide
complementary information even in the absence of compositional contrast. Notably, the ability to relocate
and image the same sectioned particle using AFM demonstrates the feasibility of correlative workflows,
where structural features identified in electron microscopy can be revisited and further interrogated using
surface-sensitive or mechanical imaging modes. Although the amplitude channel does not yield
quantitative mechanical data, it offers a rapid, nondestructive means of assessing grain structure and
surface uniformity.

 Figure 3. (Left) STEM image, and AFM (middle) topographic and (right) amplitude measurements of a
sectioned Ta-steel particle. The AFM measurements were acquired on the same particle, demonstrating the ability
 to relocate and correlate structural features across modalities.

2.1.3 AM-FM Single Particles

Having established the ability to reliably relocate sectioned particles for AFM analysis, we next explored
the application of AM-FM as a functional imaging technique. For this demonstration, we focused on a
particle containing large UO2 grains embedded within an Fe–Al–Cr–Si–O matrix. The particle also
featured a prominent internal void, offering an opportunity to examine both bulk and interfacial
mechanical behavior. As with previous measurements, AFM topography and phase imaging reproduced
the grain structure observed in electron microscopy, confirming the consistency of surface features across
modalities. However, the AM-FM indentation response and the resulting Young’s modulus maps
appeared relatively insensitive to individual grains. Instead, the mechanical signal was most responsive to
voids, edges, and other discontinuities, suggesting that the technique is more sensitive to large-scale
structural inhomogeneities than to subtle grain-to-grain variations in this context.

Although AM-FM provides a nominal value for the local Young’s modulus, these measurements should
be interpreted as qualitative indicators rather than absolute values. The lack of in situ calibration standards
and the variability introduced by tip condition, contact geometry, and surface preparation limit the
quantitative reliability of the extracted moduli. Future work should incorporate embedded or co-mounted
reference materials with known mechanical properties to enable per-tip calibration. This would provide a
pathway toward achieving semiquantitative mechanical mapping and improving the interpretability of
AM-FM data in complex, heterogeneous systems.

2.1.4 MFM Single Particles

Figure 5 presents MFM data collected from a sectioned particle previously characterized by STEM.
Structural analysis revealed an Fe–Ni alloy core capped by a Ta–Al–oxide phase. Within the Fe–Ni
region, two distinct morphologies were observed: one resembling a pie slice and the other a “Pac-Man”
shape. Crystallographic analysis suggested that the Pac-Man-shaped region may be consistent with ε-Fe, a
high-pressure phase of Fe known to exhibit suppressed magnetic ordering. Because of the rich magnetic
behavior of Fe-based alloys and the reported nonmagnetic nature of ε-Fe, this particle provided a
compelling test case for evaluating the sensitivity and interpretability of MFM in complex, multiphase
systems.

AFM topography, phase, and amplitude images all successfully distinguished the two regions, consistent
with the grain structure observed in STEM. MFM imaging further revealed distinct magnetic responses
between the two domains. One region exhibited an attractive interaction between the magnetized tip and
the sample, and the other showed a net repulsive interaction. These contrasting signals suggest the
presence of different magnetic domains or magnetic phases, potentially corresponding to the hypothesized
ε-Fe and a more conventional ferromagnetic Fe–Ni phase.

However, because of the inherently qualitative nature of MFM and the challenges associated with tip
magnetization, lift height optimization, and environmental noise, these results should be interpreted
cautiously. Although the observed contrast is promising, it is not sufficient to definitively assign magnetic
phases without additional corroborating data. Future work should incorporate reference materials with
well-characterized magnetic properties and explore variable lift-height or dual-pass protocols to improve
signal fidelity. Nonetheless, this demonstration highlights the potential of MFM to provide spatially
resolved magnetic information in sectioned particles and supports its use as a complementary technique in
correlative microscopy workflows.

2.1.5 Summary of AFM for Single-Particle Analysis

This study successfully demonstrated the ability to relocate sectioned particles onto an atomic force
microscope for correlative characterization. Using a combination of topography, phase, amplitude,
AM-FM, and MFM imaging modes, we evaluated the structural and functional properties of individual
particles. AFM phase imaging reliably reproduced grain structures observed in electron microscopy,
confirming the consistency of surface features across modalities. Functional imaging using AM-FM and
MFM was also successfully implemented. These techniques currently yield only qualitative contrast, but
they offer promising avenues for future development. With the incorporation of in situ calibration
standards, it may become feasible to extract semiquantitative mechanical and magnetic property data from
individual particles.

At present, however, AM-FM and MFM provide contrast that is best interpreted qualitatively. The
specificity and reproducibility required for rigorous analysis remain limited by factors such as tip

 7

variability, environmental sensitivity, and the absence of standardized reference materials. Despite these
limitations, functional imaging modes may still serve a valuable role in screening particle ensembles. For
example, AM-FM could be used to identify particles that are qualitatively harder or softer than others,
whereas MFM could highlight particles with distinct magnetic behavior. This capability could support
rapid triage or selection of particles for more detailed follow-up analysis. To explore this potential, we
extended our investigation to particle ensemble analysis.

2.2 PARTICLE ENSEMBLE CHARACTERIZATION VIA AFM

One of the key benefits of AFM is the ability to extract both 2D and 3D particle size distributions with
nanometer-scale resolution. AFM provides true topographic height data, enabling accurate volume and
aspect ratio measurements that are difficult to obtain from projection-based imaging techniques.

At the outset of this work, we hypothesized that AFM might also enable differentiation between particle
types based on observable physical property contrasts. Specifically, we aimed to explore whether
variations in mechanical stiffness, magnetic response, or surface texture could be used to distinguish
metallic particles from nonmetallic or carbonaceous backgrounds. By leveraging functional imaging
modes such as AM-FM and MFM, we hoped to identify qualitative differences in elasticity or magnetic
behavior that could serve as proxies for composition or formation history.

2.2.1 Sample Preparation

Silicon wafers (3 mm², Ted Pella) were cleaned using aqua regia (3:1 HCl:HNO₃) followed by rinsing
with isopropyl alcohol to remove surface contaminants. A 200 µg/mL dilution of 30 nm citrate-capped
Ag nanoparticles (AgNPs, nanoComposix) was prepared from a 1 mg/mL stock in ultrapure water. This
suspension was centrifuged at 6,000 rpm for 10 min, the supernatant was removed, and the pellet was
resuspended in 500 µL of ultrapure water. A final dilution to 20 µg/mL was prepared, and 2 µL was drop-
cast onto the cleaned Si wafer and dried under Ar gas.

2.2.2 Topographic Imaging Particle Ensembles

AFM topographic scans were acquired, and particle height distributions were extracted from the height
trace (Figure 6a–c). SEM images (Figure 6d) were collected to confirm the presence and distribution of
AgNPs on the wafer surface. The extracted particle sizes agree reasonably well with manufacturer’s
specifications, as reflected in the histogram shown in Figure 6c. These results indicate that topographic
measurements were relatively successful for single-component samples.

MFM was also explored as a means of differentiating particles based on magnetic response. Mixtures of
iron oxide nanoparticles and AgNPs were tested; however, MFM imaging did not yield reliable or
interpretable contrast between the two particle types. These results suggest that under the current
experimental conditions MFM is not yet a viable method for ensemble-level particle differentiation,
though further optimization may improve its utility.

 3. CONCLUSIONS

This study evaluated the capabilities and limitations of multimodal AFM for the characterization of both
individual particles and particle ensembles. Through the integration of topographic, phase, amplitude,
AM-FM, and MFM imaging modes, we demonstrated that AFM can provide orthogonal information that
complements conventional electron microscopy techniques. In single-particle analyses, AFM phase
imaging reliably reproduced grain structures, and AM-FM and MFM offered qualitative insights into
mechanical and magnetic properties, respectively. However, these functional imaging modes remain
inherently qualitative under current conditions, with challenges in calibration, tip variability, and sample
fragility limiting their quantitative utility.

In ensemble-level studies, AFM proved effective for extracting high-resolution 2D and 3D particle size
distributions. However, attempts to differentiate particle types based on mechanical or magnetic contrast
were largely inconclusive. Neither AM-FM nor MFM consistently resolved differences between materials
such as silver, platinum, and iron oxide nanoparticles, highlighting the difficulty of scaling functional
AFM techniques to heterogeneous ensembles.

Despite these limitations, the ability of AFM to nondestructively probe surface morphology and
functional properties at the nanoscale could be a powerful asset. With further development, multimodal
AFM could evolve into a more robust tool for both forensic and materials science applications. Future
work should focus on enhancing measurement reproducibility and extracting semiquantitative information
by incorporation of in situ standards.
