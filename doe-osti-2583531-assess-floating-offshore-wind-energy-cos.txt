Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

In support of the FLOWIN Phase One Winner Voucher Utilization, NREL will assist with
assessing floating offshore wind energy installation logistics and costs for several sites using the
floating substructure design developed by the University of Maine. This will help inform
understanding of floating offshore wind energy technology cost drivers.

Summary of Research Results:

Floating offshore wind energy represents a promising technology for accessing wind resources
over water more than approximately 60 m deep. Areas like this represent over 65% of the total
offshore wind resource in the United States (Lopez et al. 2022). The U.S. Department of Energy
(DOE) announced the Floating Offshore Wind ReadINess (FLOWIN) Prize to facilitate the
development of a cost-effective domestic floating offshore wind energy supply chain (U.S. DOE
2022a, U.S. DOE 2022b). Winning teams from Phase One of the FLOWIN Prize received
technical support from DOE National Laboratories. The National Renewable Energy Laboratory
(NREL) conducted analysis for the University of Maine (UMaine) under this Cooperative
Research and Development Agreement (CRADA). Below, we summarize the work completed
under two tasks in this CRADA effort.

Summary of Research Results:

Task 1 – Excel-Based Floating Wind LCOE Calculator:

Under this task NREL developed an Excel-based levelized cost of energy (LCOE) calculator
using the Offshore Renewables Balance of System and Installation Tool (ORBIT) with standard
inputs for estimating costs of commercial-scale floating wind projects at multiple potential sites
in the United States. NREL used ORBIT to assess installation logistics scenarios and
corresponding cost impacts (see Task 2 below) for hypothetical 1 GW floating offshore wind
energy projects using 20 MW wind turbines, after agreeing on the plant and turbine rating with
the University of Maine team. The University of Maine team provided guidance on procurement
costs and process times associated with the manufacture, assembly, tow-out and installation of
their substructure technology, which NREL incorporated into the installation model. NREL
compared installation times and costs for projects using this technology with a generic steel
semisubmersible included in the ORBIT model.

 2

To calculate LCOE, NREL incorporated standard assumptions for operations and maintenance,
financing terms, turbine capital costs, and balance-of station costs including (but not limited to)
floating array cables, export system, port fees. NREL also developed site-specific annual energy
production and wake loss estimates with FLORIS (FLOW Redirection and Induction in Steady
state) for each of the representative sites. Resulting cost parameters, annual energy yield
estimates, and installation times were included in the Excel-based LCOE calculator so the
University of Maine team could explore cost impacts of design changes or installation strategies
in the future. A screenshot of the LCOE calculator and assumed site parameters for a
representative location in the Gulf of Maine is presented in Figure 1.

Task 2 – Additional Tasks:
Under this task NREL outlined ideas for potential additional work should Task 1 be completed
within time and budget. Those original ideas (which appeared in the CRADA agreement)
included:

 1. Updating numerical tools in the OpenFAST model tools to include physics of motion
 mitigation.
 2. Conducting a global performance assessment of floating offshore wind turbine and
 comparing against with other hull types
 3. Providing a review letter with technical opinion
 4. Repeating the cost analysis from Task 1 for additional potential project sites and
 including results in the Excel Tool/LCOE comparison.

During Task 1, NREL and the University of Maine identified that there was sufficient time and
budget for additional analysis. Both parties agreed to change the focus of the additional tasks to
investigating installation logistics using the ORBIT model, assessing port infrastructure which
might support floating offshore wind energy installation, and repeating the cost analysis for an
additional site. The impacts of the additional analyses were included in the Excel-based LCOE
calculator delivered for Task 1.

In ORBIT’s substructure installation modeling approach, the substructures are produced at port
through substructure assembly lines (SALs). Once the substructures are ready for turbine
integration, they are moved to the turbine assembly lines (TALs), where the substructure and the
turbine components are assembled (turbine integration) and prepared to be towed to the offshore
wind site through vessel groups (VGs). If the turbine-substructure assembly is ready to tow but
the VG is not available at port, the assembly is placed at wet storage (initially assumed capacity
of 5 substructures).

The installation analysis involved identifying the number of SALs, TALs, and VGs required to
install the project in two years. Figure 2 presents the number of substructures installed over time
for 27 installation scenarios (left) and indicative installation schedules for scenarios requiring 2
or 3 installation seasons (right) for a hypothetical Gulf of Maine site.

Once the numbers of SALs, TALs, and VGs per site was established, a subsequent wet storage
sensitivity study was conducted. The objective was to comprehend the potential impact of a
port's wet storage capabilities on the installation schedule. The impact of different wet storage
limits on the installation schedule for the Gulf of Maine site is presented in Figure 3.

 Figure 3: Wet storage sensitivity analysis for the Gulf of Maine

 5

NREL explored the impact on the total installation schedule of seasonal installation windows,
and this is presented in Figure 4. Some installation activities require calm winds and waves for
safety and risk management. Since costs acrue when installation activities are delayed due to
weather, there is a balance between potential delays and remobilization costs. One key takeaway
from the analysis is that the affordability of installation windows may vary the relative difference
between port fees and vessel rates. To minimize the installation cost, one should maximize the
uptime of the most expensive part of the installation process. For example, under scenarios with
lower monthly port fees and higher vessel day rates, installation windows may be more
economically advantageous. Conversely, in situations where monthly port fees are higher and
vessel day rates are lower, completing the project in the shortest timeframe possibly becomes
less costly, irrespective of vessel operational efficiencies.

Figure 4: Substructure installation schedules for different installation windows for the Gulf of Maine site

NREL conducted a literature review of requirements for ports supporting floating offshore wind
energy projects and discussed implications for project costs. The core findings are summarized
below and in Table 1.

Ports must cover their upgrade costs with revenue from lease agreements. There may be different
revenue requirements depending on the business model of the port, source of financing (private
equity or public funds), and expectations around a pipeline of activities driving occupancy over
the financial lifetime of the port infrastructure investment. Across the ports analyzed, final
upgrade costs ranged between $50 million to $1 billion. Because the total cost depends on the
economic drivers, existing infrastructure, available land space, and extent of upgrades, and
uncertainty in the OSW industry, the accuracies most studies provide is +/-50% along with hefty
contingencies. Some lease agreements on the East Coast can provide a rough estimate of what a
developer may expect to pay as their operations ramp up. However, several fixed-bottom
offshore wind projects have been cancelled or renegotiated based on other economic factors
(supply chains, inflation, interest rates). The little information that is available reveals that lease
values could range from $14-20 million per year for fixed bottom WTG operations. Leases for a
floating offshore wind project may carry a premium given their additional technical

requirements. Further, the lease duration may be determined by the use of the space (i.e., staging
and integration or operations and maintenance).

In the end, the investments made for port upgrades need to be recovered through leases and
agreements with customers. Through a sensitivity on LCOE, we demonstrate that even a 100%
increase in our assumed floating wind port rental rates leads to an increase in LCOE of only a
couple percentage points at most. This is because the assumed port rates represent a small
portion of the overall project CapEx. Note values are presented in relative terms (percentage
difference in LCOE compared to Site 1 using the default port rate.

Summary Conclusion

Under this CRADA, NREL developed an Excel-based floating offshore wind energy LCOE
calculator to support the University of Maine’s participation in the FLOWIN prize program. In
the process, NREL investigated installation logistics scenarios and cost impacts for hypothetical
1 GW projects consisting of 20 MW wind turbines on floating substructures. The scenarios
considered different levels of substructure manufacturing infrastructure, available wet storage
space for turbine-substructure assemblies, and the impacts of weather/seasonal installation
windows. Finally, NREL incorporated a literature survey on potential costs for supporting
floating offshore wind port infrastructure and developed this CFR summarizing the work
performed.
