 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Background Information: The DOE-FECM Carbon Capture Program has taken a multi-pronged approach
in reducing capture cost during process scaleup while maintaining capture efficiency. Included in this
approach is process intensification with new equipment manufacturing methods, which shows great
potential in process consolidation, footprint reduction, as well as construction and operating cost reduction.
The conventional solvent-base carbon capture equipment designs are usually based on multistage columns,
which utilize sequentially coupled stages with high surface-area packing for mass and heat transfer.
Problem: Carbon dioxide (CO2) absorption by amine solvents is exothermic, releasing a significant amount
of heat (e.g., 80–100 kJ/mol CO2 for 30 wt% aqueous MEA solvent at 40°C [1]). This heat increases the
temperature of the solvent and the column, leading to a temperature bulge in the absorption column
exceeding 70°C [2, 3]. It is noted that approximately 60% of the absorbed CO2 desorbs at 90°C, and higher
temperatures (100°C or greater) are required for CO2 desorption and solvent regeneration [4]. Although this
temperature-dependent desorption behavior is favorable for solvent regeneration, accumulation of reaction
heat in the solvent can negatively impact the CO2 capture efficiency. Also, the amine solvent absorption
capacity for CO2, defined as moles CO2 absorbed per kg of lean solvent, increases as the solvent temperature
is decreased. Therefore, it is essential to remove the heat released by the reaction of CO2 with the solvent.
Proposed Solution: To achieve this task, an additively manufactured (AM) intensified packing device,
specially designed to allow cooling fluid flow through internal channels, is integrated with the column
packing. This device removes the need of pumping the solvent out of the column through a heat exchanger
for interstage cooling, allowing intrastage cooling instead [5-7] [Figure 1]. The AM device helps control
the solvent temperature, potentially lowering the column height, which leads to reduced capital cost [7].
Intrastage cooling, however, may increase the pressure drop which reduces the maximum possible gas and
solvent flowrates through the column [5, 8]. Therefore, the intrastage cooling device is optimized to provide
maximum cooling capacity at a minimum pressure drop or maximum void fraction of the packing.
Project Goals and Objectives: In previous work funded by the Carbon Capture Program during FY2018-
2019 under FWP-FEAA130 and FY2020 under FWP-FEAA375, the ORNL advanced manufacturing and
testing team has demonstrated that an AM intensified device, i.e., a mass exchange packing with built-in
heat exchange channels [Figure 2], can effectively achieve the mass- and heat-exchange functionalities for
both aqueous solvent and advanced low-aqueous solvent at the desired scale. Since device-scale capture
with different solvents and incoming flue gas composition and temperature relies heavily on the
thermodynamic and kinetic properties of the solvent, the actual capture efficiency with and without a heat
exchanging packing device should be quantified at a larger scale for aqueous and low-aqueous (or low
aqueous) solvents. The main goal of this project was to design, construct, and demonstrate a larger-scale
packing device and CO2 capture column (absorber) than the ones previously tested at ORNL, suitable for
approximately 1 ton-CO2/day). To achieve this goal, it was necessary to (1) design a scaled-up intensified
device and absorber to demonstrate enhanced CO2 capture with intrastage cooling; (2) demonstrate that the
absorber can be modularized; and (3) demonstrate that intrastage cooling can be used to effectively capture
CO2 from different point sources (e.g., coal-fired and natural gas-fired power plants), as well as during
process transients (i.e., capacity ramping up and down anticipating the intermittent nature of fossil fuel
plants working together with renewable energy installations). These project objectives were successfully
accomplished, and collected data were made available to the CCSI2 program for model validation purposes.
Project Overview: DOE-FECM provided a funding level of $2,080K federal funds, while ORNL provided
$220K to help construct a 12-inch absorber. During the period of January 1, 2021, through June 30, 2025,
the project team successfully completed all the construction and experimental tasks. Experimental data
showed that the scalable, heat-exchanging packing device can be used to effectively cool down the solvent
temperature to keep its CO2 capacity high. It was demonstrated that cooling the solvent along the column
leads to a significant increase in the CO2 capture efficiency. The project was completed on June 30, 2025.

The CO2 capture prediction and the improvement in capture from intrastage cooling are summarized in
Figure 4 for both Enhancement factor models compared against previously published CO2 capture results
from Miramontes et al. [9] using aqueous MEA solvent. The parity plot for CO2 capture, Figure 4(a),

relative to Model 1 simulations, ranging from 1-5% reduction in the capture rate. The capture rate
improvement with cooling observed with Model 2 more closely represents the average reported CO2 capture
from Miramontes et al. [9]. The average relative deviation for CO2 capture from Model 1 and Model 2 are
3.6% and 2.3%, respectively. Another method of assessing the deterministic nature of this rate-based model
framework is to compare the CO2 capture improvement for each case as shown in Figure 4(b). In all cases,
there is a decrease in the improvement predicted by Model 2 relative to Model 1 simulations. The largest
decrease in capture improvement was observed from 25% using Model 1 to 15% using Model 2. Overall,
these simulation results suggest Model 2 may more accurately predict the observed CO2 capture in these
pilot plant data.

different solvents and geometric structured packing parameters may reveal if the correlation utilized here
would be accurate in predicting the heat transfer phenomena in the packing.

Subtask 2.3 – Instrumentation of Column B
Summary
The project team developed a data acquisition system for column operation, including temperature and
flowrates monitoring [Figure 10(a)]. This system includes temperature and pressure sensors, flowmeters,
control valves and a computer interface. Also included are the exhaust gas heat exchanger and system
control for the exhaust gas temperature to the column from the natural gas generator set, as shown in Figure
10(b). Temperature control of the flue gas is achieved through an in-series heat-exchanger network with
valves on the cooling water that control the flue gas temperature before it is fed to the column. The transient
temperature, flow, and gas- and liquid-phase compositions in the absorption column are also monitored. In-
line flow measurement devices are shown in Figure 10(c). A custom LabVIEW software-based data
acquisition and control interface is used for monitoring and control. The solvent regeneration column was
also assembled and installed in Cell 5 [Figure 9] along with the solvent feed and receiving tanks, as shown
in Figures 10(d, e).
A schematic of the regeneration column is shown in Figure 1. Rich solvent (e.g., solvent with absorbed
CO2) is sent to the column using a metering pump and is preheated using a recuperating heat exchanger
(HEX-7) before being introduced to the column. That solvent flows through the packing material until it
reaches the bottom of the column, where a liquid level is maintained. The liquid at the bottom of the column
is circulated; a portion of the solvent flows into a steam heat exchanger, which is the primary source of heat
for the column, and the remainder is fed to the outlet metering pump.

Subtask 2.4 – Initial Column Evaluation

Summary

This task was focused on hydraulic testing of the column using air and water. After completion of Column
B construction, the system was tested hydraulically to confirm no leaks observed from either the gas or
liquid side of the system using air and water. In addition, the sensors were evaluated and verified to be
functioning. The air blower located in the Engine Test Cell 5 was used to test the sensors for the gas side.
Figure 14(a) shows the measured gas mass flowrate in kg/hr and volumetric flowrate in LPM against the
blower set point. These results are consistent with previous calibration performed on the air blower. Figure
14(b) shows the measured liquid mass flowrate in kg/hr and volumetric flowrate in LPM against the pump
speed set point. Some hydraulic limitations in increasing the flowrate above 45% required modifications to
the storage tanks to allow adequate venting under these higher flowrate conditions. There was no observed
flowrate below a pump speed set point of 21%, implying the pump requires a certain speed to overcome
the pump head pressure of the piping system. With the modifications made, we could have flowrates to as
high as 40 LPM or 2400 kg/hr if required for experimental campaigns. Lastly, the hydraulics of the
intensified device were tested. Figure 14(c) shows a cycling of opening and closing the control valve and

Task 6.0 – Aqueous Solvent Capture with Simulated Coal-Fired Power Plant Flue Gas
Summary

Several sets of CO2 capture experiments were performed to validate the feasibility of intrastage cooling for
coal-fired flue gas with ~15% CO2. This series of experiments demonstrated improved CO2 capture by an
aqueous amine solvent (30% MEA) from coal-fired flue gas, using intrastage cooling with the intensified
packing device.
Subtask 6.1 – Baseline Experiments with Commercial Packing

Baseline experiments refer to experiments without cooling. Each set of experiments included baseline
conditions until steady state was reached and then, cooling-fluid flow was started without changing the
flowrates of gas and liquid, and the temperature and CO2 concentration in the gas phase were monitored
until a new steady state was established. The genset was first started, and the power load was adjusted to
the desired set point to provide a certain flue gas flowrate. Then, the solvent flowrate pump was turned on
at the desired flowrate. The gas and liquid temperature profile along the column and CO2 concentration in
the gas were monitored using the custom LabVIEW software-based control and data acquisition interface
with specialized sensors. At steady-state conditions in terms of temperature profile and CO2 concentration
in the gas phase, solvent samples were taken from different stages of the column for analysis after the
experiment. Cooling fluid was then introduced in the intensified device, and the experiment continued until
a new steady state was reached. After the new steady state was established, more solvents samples were
taken for analysis.

Subtask 6.2 – Experiments with Intensified MellaPak 250

By adding pure CO2 from a gas cylinder into the natural gas-fired flue-gas stream, the CO2 concentration
was adjusted to ~15% to simulate coal-fired flue gas. The feed flue gas flowrate ranged from 158 kg/hr to
217 kg/hr. The feed solvent temperature was adjusted to 40 C, and the flue gas was cooled down from 300
C at the exit of the natural gas power generator, to 40 C at the entrance of the CO2 capture column.
Intrastage cooling effectively improved the capture efficiency from 93% to ~98%, as shown in Table 6.
The fractional increase in the capture rate ranged from 3.2% to 7.7%, depending on the operating conditions.

efficiency significantly increased after cooling, with a fractional increase of 7.7% at 650 kg/hr and 4.9% at
750 kg/hr. This indicates that intrastage cooling can yield a capture efficiency increase similar to that
produced by increasing the solvent flowrate.

Figures 33(c,d) illustrate the influence of flue gas flowrate on temperature profiles and the associated
capture efficiencies. As the gas flowrate increased, the temperature profiles gradually decreased below a
height of 3 meters, indicating a reduction in exothermic reactions within the column, which led to a lower
capture efficiency. Additionally, as expected, intrastage cooling significantly lowered the column
temperature by up to 26 C, thereby enhancing the capture efficiency. It is important to note, however, that
the cooling effect diminished with increasing flue gas flowrate, indicating that additional cooling was
needed. For instance, at an intermediate gas flowrate of 185 kg/hr, the improvement in fractional capture
efficiency is the highest, reaching up to 6.9%. At a flue gas flowrate of 215 kg/hr, the average temperature
reduction was only 12 C, resulting in a capture efficiency enhancement of just 2.6%. In that scenario, the
bottom temperature bulge reached 65 C, which suggests that additional thermal control (e.g., employing
two intensified packing devices) might be needed to help provide broader thermal management along the
column to further improve the capture efficiency.

Subtask 7.2 – Experiments with Intensified MellaPak 250

A series of experiments with natural-gas flue gas demonstrated improvement in the CO2 capture rates after

depending on operational conditions, as shown in Table 7. The feed flue gas flowrate ranged between 170

from the 300 C output temperature of the natural gas generator. The solvent temperature was adjusted at

gas and the CO2-lean solvent coming from the regeneration column. It is shown in Table 7 that the 30%

Time-dependent CO2 concentration and temperature profiles before and after intrastage cooling by the

the device. This device could be positioned along the absorption column, near areas of expected temperature
bulge, eliminating the need for external heat exchangers and pumps.

The heat removal rate by the intensified device was maximized once the coolant flow was initiated,

an increase in capture efficiency from 90.2% without cooling to 94.0% with cooling.

corresponding to 0.71 mol CO2/kg of additional loading associated with cooling, corresponding to a 22.4%
enhancement in CO2 loading.

corresponding increase in the CO2 capture efficiency, as shown in Table 7.

Subtask 8.2 – Experiments with Intensified MellaPak 250

Transient experiments were performed as shown in Figure 38. In this figure, the feed CO2 concentration
was allowed to change, and corresponding changes were observed in the outlet CO2 concentration both with
and without cooling. With cooling, the CO2 capture efficiency was 93%. After cooling was ended by

The objective of these experiments was to show that the CO2 capture system developed in this project could
be used to capture the dynamic response of the absorber when unexpected abrupt changes occur in important
process parameters, such as gas flowrate due to a sudden reduction or increase in the load of the power
generator. Sensors installed on the absorber were able to capture changes in the performance parameters,
including CO2 concentration in the gas exiting the column and CO2 capture efficiency. Capturing the
dynamics of the absorption column means that the column performance can be controlled if a suitable
feedback controller is designed and installed; however, controlling the column performance is beyond the
objectives of this project. Designing and demonstrating a suitable control system for the column can be a
topic for future research.

Subtask 9.1 – Modify Column B to Demonstrate Modular Design

This subtask was focused on preparing the column and solvent for experiments with the nonaqueous (low-
aqueous, LAS) solvent invented by RTI International. Column B is a modularized column with 9 modules,
one of them being the intensified device. Here, the term modularized column reveals that the number of

comparison with MEA results, it was decided to use the same number of modules and keep the intensified

subtask, the previously used MEA solvent was drained into a mobile storage tank and stored. Both the feed
and recycling tanks as well as the absorption column, regeneration column, and pipes that were in contact
with the solvent were rinsed by recycling clean water through the system. Following this step, pristine RTI
solvent, which was purchased through a third party, was introduced into the feed tank of the system.

Conclusions and Future Work

In conclusion, the project was successfully completed with all 10 tasks carried out effectively. The project
Team designed and constructed a 0.3-m diameter, 4-m height column (Column B) and scaled-up intensified
devices to demonstrate larger-scale intrastage cooling benefits on CO2 capture compared to previous work
with a 0.2-m diameter, 1.6-m height column (Column A). After construction of Column B, it was first
hydraulically tested with temperature, pressure drop, and liquid holdup measurements to make sure it was
working as designed. Pressure drop measurements were higher than predicted by correlations for
commercial packing devices of the same geometry as that of the intensified device. It was determined that
the higher pressure-drop was caused by the intensified device due to its lower void fraction compared to
that of commercial packing. A solution to this issue was proposed for future work. Following hydraulic
testing, mass transfer experiments were conducted with two solvents, a 30% aqueous MEA solution and
the low-aqueous RTI solvent, for natural-gas and coal simulated flue gas, under a range of experimental
conditions. Experimental data led to the conclusion that the intensified device improved the performance
of the column under all experimental conditions. The magnitude of improvement depends on the gas and
solvent flowrates, CO2 concentration in the feed gas, and CO2 loading in the lean solvent. Transient data
indicate that process control can be applied to the column to address potential transient process parameters.

Future work may include the following topics among others:
