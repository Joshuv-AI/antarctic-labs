 SECTION 1.0
 INTRODUCTION

 About 50 million gallons of high-level waste (HLW) is currently stored in underground
tanks at the United States Department of Energy’s (DOE’s) Hanford site in the state of
Washington. The Hanford Tank Waste Treatment and Immobilization Plant (WTP) will provide
DOE’s Office of River Protection (ORP) with a means of treating this waste by vitrification for
subsequent disposal. The tank waste will be separated into low-activity waste (LAW) and HLW
fractions, which will then be vitrified respectively into Immobilized Low Activity Waste
(ILAW) and Immobilized High Level Waste (IHLW) products. The ILAW product will be
disposed in an engineered facility on the Hanford site while the IHLW product is designed for
acceptance into a national deep geological disposal facility for high-level nuclear waste.

 Glass property-composition models will be used to control the vitrification process at the
WTP to ensure that the glass meets product quality and processability requirements. The quality
and the validity ranges of the property-composition models and the supporting database will
greatly influence waste treatment operations, including the waste loading that is achievable in the
glass for each waste composition. The current baseline models for operation of the WTP were
developed under the Bechtel National, Inc. (BNI) WTP contract [1-4]. These models were based
on LAW and HLW glass property data over a range of compositions that were guided primarily
by the WTP contract and the specific set of tanks specified therein. The HLW testing was
directed towards waste streams from the then-planned early feed tanks for the WTP, which are
high in iron (AZ-101, AZ-102 and C-106/AY-102) or thorium (C-104/AY-101). The overall
mission of the WTP will require the immobilization of tank waste compositions that are
dominated by mixtures of aluminum, chromium, bismuth, iron, phosphorous, zirconium, and
sulfur compounds as waste-limiting components. Furthermore, the WTP contract requirements
for minimum waste loadings are generally rather modest, suggesting that loadings significantly
higher than those in the current baseline are likely achievable. However, in order to achieve these
higher waste loadings and to expand the applicable composition regions, new models will be
required to support the optimized operation of the WTP.

 Results of the WTP HLW baseline model development work were presented in 2008 [1].
That report discusses the data, model development, model validation, and model uncertainty
results for each of the glass properties that the WTP plans to control using glass property-
composition models. HLW models were developed for Product Consistency Test (PCT) releases
(of B, Li and Na), spinel crystallization (one-percent crystal fraction temperature, T1%), melt
viscosity, and melt electrical conductivity. A model was also developed for Toxicity
Characteristic Leach Procedure (TCLP) Cd response of HLW glasses to support delisting
requirements [2]. No WTP models were developed to control nepheline formation upon Canister
Centerline Cooling (CCC). It was assumed that nepheline formation upon CCC would be

 8

controlled using the nepheline discriminator [5], which is the current approach employed at the
Defense Waste Processing Facility (DWPF).

 In parallel with and subsequent to the development of the baseline WTP models, ORP
has directed a program of work to examine various approaches for optimization of the
performance of the WTP, including increasing the waste loadings in the LAW and HLW glass
products [6-16]. As a result, a significant amount of new data has been collected that can be used
for improvement of the baseline models. Inclusion of the new data in the development of new
property-composition models will be beneficial in expanding the composition ranges over which
they are valid and improving the quality of the models. Glass property-composition data have
been collected under the ORP program for HLW rich in Al, Al+Na, Cr, Bi, P, S, Sr, Zr, and Fe
(at higher Fe concentrations than that in WTP baseline glasses). To utilize the advantages of
these new higher waste loading glass formulations for HLW processing at the WTP, the HLW
glass property-composition models need to be updated to incorporate the new compositions.
Furthermore, in order to develop HLW models, adequate property-composition data covering the
glass composition regions of interest are required. To address these needs, ORP has initiated a
program to incorporate new HLW glass data to enhance the models. Compilation of glass
property data collected under the ORP program was performed in 2012, 2014, and 2017 [17-20].
Under Phase 1 through Phase 3 of this program, the property-composition data in the WTP-ORP
database were reviewed to identify compositional gaps that need to be filled; new glasses were
then fabricated and characterized to provide new modeling data. New HLW models were
subsequently developed using the combined WTP-ORP data during Phases 1, 2 and 3 to evaluate
the impacts of expanded compositional ranges on the models [18-20].

 Tank retrieval, blending, and treatment projections at Hanford [21] have shown that many
of the waste streams are high in aluminum; on an oxide basis, the Al2O3 concentration in some
sludge batches could be as high as 70 wt%. The increased concentration of Al2O3 in glass can: (i)
promote the crystallization of aluminum-containing crystals such as nepheline (NaAlSiO4)
during slow cooling in the canister, which can increase the leachability of the waste form; (ii)
increase the liquidus temperature of spinel crystals that can interfere with melter operation; and
(iii) potentially decrease feed processing rates due to refractory nature of Al2O3 leading to slow
dissolution into the glass melt. To address these issues, crucible scale tests have been performed
to develop high waste loading HLW glasses with high Al2O3 loading capacity, and scaled melter
runs have been conducted to demonstrate their processability and the ability to achieve high melt
rates [9-11]. Glass development for high-Al HLW has focused on avoiding nepheline
crystallization on CCC heat treatment and spinel crystallization on isothermal heat treatment near
the liquidus temperature. That work was successful in developing high waste loading glasses to
treat Hanford high-Al HLW with glass production rates well above the current WTP requirement
[9-11].

 Nepheline formation during CCC can pose a product quality issue for some high-Al
glasses since it can lead to significant increases in PCT leachate concentrations. For high-Al
silicate glasses, formation of nepheline during CCC often can be rather extensive since all of the

 9

nepheline constituents are typically present in large amounts in the glass. Therefore, suppression
of nepheline crystallization during canister cooling is a significant factor that must be considered
in the development of high-Al HLW glasses. In view of the large volume of high-Al HLW, there
is a significant driver for the development of improved methods for prediction and control of
nepheline formation during the vitrification process. Since the CCC profile for WTP HLW glass
is determined largely by the standard canister size and melter operating temperature, nepheline
formation during CCC is controlled mostly by glass formulation. Consequently, models that are
able to predict nepheline formation based on glass composition are useful both for glass
formulation development and for process control.

 The formation of nepheline during CCC heat treatment is ultimately determined by its
phase stability with respect to the parent glass melt and the time allowed for nucleation and
crystal growth during cooling. More specifically, the crystallization of nepheline is determined
by the chemical composition of the bulk glass in question and, therefore, can be manipulated and
managed by adjustment of the glass formulation. Such a relationship between the glass
composition and nepheline crystallization during CCC heat treatment is the basis for devising a
practical composition-based indicator for nepheline crystallization. In a previous study [22] we
evaluated the performance of available nepheline models using the VSL database of nepheline
crystallization in HLW glasses subjected to CCC heat treatment and explored more sophisticated
statistical models for prediction of nepheline crystallization in these glasses. The results from
that work showed that both a Logistic Regression Model (LRM) and a Support Vector Machine
(SVM) model performed considerably better than other model forms evaluated as judged by
statistics of model training and validation.

 The primary objectives of the present work were, firstly, to expand the presently
available database of nepheline formation in Hanford high-Al HLW glasses when subjected to
prototypical WTP CCC heat treatment. In view of the very large composition range that will be
involved in the vitrification of HLW at the WTP, expansion of this database is essential for the
development of practical and reliable models. Thus, the new data acquired in this work were
added to the existing database that was developed to support analysis and model development. A
second objective of the present work was to further improve the performance of the LRM and
SVM models for nepheline formation in WTP HLW glasses subjected to CCC heat treatment.
This work was performed according to the associated Test Plan [23]. The work included the
design, fabrication, and characterization of 35 HLW glasses and assessment and development of
new models for nepheline formation.

 SECTION 2.0
 DESIGN AND CHARACTERIZATION
 OF NEW GLASSES

 As described in the Test Plan [23], the present work was intended to expand the available
HLW glass property-composition data relating to nepheline formation on CCC heat treatment
and to develop improved models to predict nepheline formation that would allow processing of
high waste loading high-Al HLW glass formulations at the WTP.

 More specifically, the objectives of the present work were to: 1) Expand and improve the
database of HLW glasses subjected to CCC heat treatment and, in particular, to enhance the
crystallization data in the composition regions that are most influential in model development;
and 2) Develop improved models based on this database. In the previous modeling work [22],
two models had particularly low misclassification rates (i.e., the rates of incorrectly predicting
nepheline formation or incorrectly predicting the lack of nepheline formation); these were a
Logistic Regression Model (LRM) and a Support Vector Machine (SVM) model. Thus, in the
present work, these two models were used in the design of 35 new glasses (“HLW17-NP”
glasses) for database enhancement and nepheline model refinement.

2.1 Basic Considerations for Design of HLW17-NP Glasses

 The performance of empirical property-composition models generally depends strongly
on the database available for training and validation. As shown from analysis of SVM modeling
results in the previous work [22], the performance of the SVM model hinges on the set of
influential HLW glass formulations that define the Support Vectors, which, in turn, essentially
define the boundary dividing glasses free of nepheline from glasses with various degrees of
nepheline crystallization. Similarly, results from the LRM showed that the misclassification rate
is essentially zero for HLW glasses predicted to have near-zero probability for nepheline
formation and essentially zero for HLW glasses predicted to have near-100% probability for
nepheline formation. The misclassification rate is, however, much higher for glasses predicted to
have around 50% probability for nepheline formation. Thus, from both perspectives, it is likely
that new glasses added in this transition region would be most influential in improving model
performance. Consequently, that approach was taken for the present design of new glasses for
this work. Based on the forgoing discussion and as explained below, the present glass design
approach differs from the typical D-Optimal Mixture design method that has been used
previously for model development in that the present approach places glasses near the boundary
between glasses that do and do not form nepheline while the latter approach is typically
employed to optimally cover a defined composition region.

2.2 Method for HLW17-NP Glass Development

 The 35 HLW17-NP glasses were designed in two stages. In Stage One, 1,000 candidate
HLW glass formulations within the constraints listed in Tables 2.1 and 2.2 were generated. In
Stage Two, 35 final HLW glass formulations were then selected from the 1,000 candidate points,
using the approach described below.

 2.2.1 Stage One: Generation of 1,000 Candidate Formulations

 In Stage One, a large set (1000) of compositional points uniformly spaced throughout the
design region were created using a Space Filling Mixture Design, subject to multiple composite
linear constraints (Table 2.2) and simple lower and upper bounds for each oxide concentration in
the final glass formulations (Table 2.1).

 The JMP Fast Flexible Filling design tool [24] was used to generate a large number of
random points within the specified design region. For each target run of a total 1000 runs in
Stage One, 50 composition points were generated randomly. These 50 x 1000 points were then
clustered using a Ward algorithm [24] into 1000 clusters. Next, 1000 candidate composition
points were obtained by using the C-MaxPro (maximum projection) optimality criterion [24]
for p factors (p = 18 glass components, as listed in Table 2.1) and n runs (n = 1000 clusters in our
case). Briefly, the C-MaxPro criterion looks for points in the clusters that minimize the following
quantity:

                    𝐶𝑀𝑎𝑥𝑃𝑟𝑜 = ∑ ∑ [1/ ∏(𝑥𝑖𝑘 − 𝑥𝑗𝑘 )2 ]                                      (2.1)
 𝑖 𝑗=𝑖+1 𝑘=1

In other words, the C-MaxPro criterion maximizes the products of the distances between
potential design points in a way that involves all factors.

 2.2.2 Stage Two: Down-Selection of Formulations

 The 1000 HLW glass formulations generated in Stage One were further evaluated for
their influence on the predictions of the SMV and LRM nepheline models. Based on this
evaluation, 35 of the more influential formulations were identified. The evaluation of the 1000
candidate HLW formulations was performed in three steps, as described below.

 12

 2.2.2.1 Evaluation of SVM-Influential Formulations

 The nepheline SVM model developed previously (referred to as “2016SVM”) [22] was
used to evaluate the influence of the 1000 candidate points. The output of a SVM model is a
value indicative of the distance from transition (boundary between nepheline-free and nepheline-
forming). Positive values predict that nepheline formation is likely while negative values predict
that it is not. The magnitude of the prediction value is a measure of its uncertainty; calculated
values between -1 and 1 have varying uncertainty, with a decision value of 0 being the most
uncertain. In the present work, a subset of the set of 1000 candidate points having SVM model
prediction values of between -0.6 and 0.6 were considered to be SVM-influential; these were
then further evaluated for their influence on the LRM, as described below.

 2.2.2.2 Evaluation of SVM-Influential Formulations by LRM

 The nepheline LRM developed previously (referred to as “2016LRM”) [22] was used to
cross check the influence of the SVM-influential formulations selected as described in Section
2.2.2.1. Only those candidate formulations from the SVM-influential group that have 2016LRM
classifier values between 0.01 and 0.99 were advanced to the final selection. As described
previously [22], formulations that have prediction values between 0.01 and 0.99 from the LRM,
and particularly near 0.5, are less certain, and tend to have higher misclassification rates.

 2.2.2.3 Selection of 35 HLW17-NP Glasses

 The candidate formulations identified as described in Section 2.2.2.2 were further down
selected based on conflicting predictions by the 2016SVM and 2016LRM models; that is, when
the predictions from these two models contradicted each other. It is expected that including
formulations of this nature should improve the utility of the data set in improving the
performance of both models.

 2.3.1 Preparation and Characterization of Nepheline Matrix Glasses

 All 35 HLW17-NP glasses were batched and melted according to standard Vitreous State
Laboratory (VSL) procedures; the experimental methods used for glass preparation and
characterization are described in Section 5. The resulting glasses were analyzed by X-ray
fluorescence (XRF) spectroscopy to verify their chemical composition; the target and analyzed
compositions are presented in Tables 2.4. Samples of each of the glasses were subjected to heat
treatment according to the WTP HLW CCC schedule [25]. The heat treated glass samples were
prepared for and examined by scanning electron microscopy coupled with energy dispersive
X-ray spectroscopy (SEM/EDS) to identify and quantify major crystalline phases. In some cases,
quantitative X-ray diffraction (XRD) was used to confirm the SEM/EDS findings. The results are
presented in Table 2.5. For every glass that showed evidence of nepheline crystallization, larger
quantities of glass were subjected to CCC heat treatment in order to provide sufficient material
for leach testing according to the PCT method. For each of those glasses, the PCT was then
performed on both the HLW CCC heat treated glass and the quenched glass. The PCT results are
summarized in Table 2.6.

 2.3.2 Effect of Nepheline Formation on PCT Release

 As listed in Table 2.5, nepheline or nepheline-like phases were found in 24 CCC heat
treated samples out of a total of 35 HLW17-NP glasses investigated in this work. Three samples
showed less than 1 vol% of nepheline, while the CCC heat treated samples of the other 21
formulations displayed variable amounts of nepheline formation, from a few vol% to 72 vol%
(Table 2.5). For those glasses with no nepheline present in the CCC heat treated samples, a few
are essentially amorphous, some show trace amounts of spinel, while others show considerable
amounts of crystallization of spinel, phosphate, and sometimes water-soluble phase(s) observed
as voids after polishing with aqueous media. SEM images of nepheline crystals in CCC heat
treated samples are shown in Figures 2.8.

 As evident from the PCT leach data given in Table 2.6, the crystallization of nepheline
strongly influenced the PCT releases for the HLW17-NP glasses. Comparison of the PCT results
for the quenched and corresponding CCC heat treated samples shows that even as little as 1 vol%
of nepheline present in CCC heat treated samples can increase the leachate concentrations
significantly for B and Li. For HLW17-NP glasses with 1 to 10 vol% nepheline, the normalized
PCT B releases increased by between ~3 to 26 times in comparison to those for the quenched

increases (4 to 5 times after CCC). Overall, the PCT releases of HLW17-NP glasses displayed

the normalized release of Na, a key nepheline component, versus the normalized B release
showed an approximate 2:5 ratio (Figure 2.10b).

 Formation of nepheline in CCC heat treated HLW glasses tend to increase PCT releases;
it is therefore desirable to design glass formulations such that nepheline formation is avoided,
which is the driver to develop models to distinguish nepheline-containing CCC heat treated
glasses from nepheline-free CCC heat treated glasses. We previously [22] employed a criterion
that a CCC heat treated glass with more than 0.1 vol% nepheline should be classified “Yes” for
nepheline model development. PCT leach data from the present work in general are in agreement
with this assessment. Among the five CCC heat treated samples of HLW17-NP with less than 2
vol% nepheline, nepheline crystallization did not result in increased PCT B release for three
glasses (HLW17-NP9 with 0.2% nepheline; HLW17-NP13 with 0.8% nepheline; and HLW17-
NP29 with 0.5% nepheline) whereas PCT B release doubled or tripled for the other two glasses
(HLW17-NP8 with 1% nepheline and HLW17-NP26 with 1.6% nepheline). Although the
minimum vol% of nepheline necessary to substantially impact PCT release is higher than the
0.1 vol% proposed in our previous work, it is clear that crystallization of small amounts of
nepheline can indeed influence the PCT release of HLW glasses. For consistency, therefore,
glasses with more than 0.1 vol% nepheline are classified as nepheline-containing for the
development of nepheline models described in Section 3.

3.2 Evaluation of Existing Nepheline Discriminator Models

 In a previous study [22], several existing nepheline models were evaluated and new
models were developed. Since these models were designed to provide a binary classification for
the presence or absence of nepheline after CCC heat treatment, it is convenient to compare
model performance in terms of their misclassification rate. The misclassification can be further
defined as false positive and false negative, as is often described in the so-called confusion
matrix, which is a 2-by-2 matrix of actual positives and actual negatives versus predicted
positives and predicted negatives (here, “positive” and “negative” refer to samples with- and
without nepheline, respectively). The misclassification rate is equal to the number of false
positives plus false negatives divided by the total number of samples.

 17

The Catholic University of America Nepheline Constraint for Hanford HLW Glass
Vitreous State Laboratory Final Report, VSL-18R4390-1, Rev. 0

 The false negative rate is defined as the proportion of all positives that yield a negative
prediction; thus, it is the conditional probability of a negative prediction given that the actual
result is positive (i.e., a “type II error” usually denoted by β). The false negative rate is also
sometime termed the “miss rate” from the perspective that it measures the probability of missing
a positive given that it is actually there. The false negative rate is equal to the number of false
negatives divided by the number of actual positives. The false positive rate (“type I error,’ α) can
be similarly defined. In addition to the misclassification rate, the false negative rate is reported in
the following sections since it provides a measure of the tendency of a given model to miss
nepheline formation when it actually occurs.

 In the previous work [22], we evaluated existing nepheline models using a database of
HLW glasses subjected to CCC and their performance with respect to misclassification rates in
the prediction of nepheline formation in CCC heat treated HLW glass samples. These models
included two Sub-Lattice models [26, 27] and a Sub-Mixture model [28]. In that work, we also
proposed two alternative statistical models, a LRM (Logistic Regression Model) and SVM
(Support Vector Machine) as nepheline classifiers for HLW glasses. For convenience, the results
from that work are summarized in Table 3.5a, which shows the key performance measures of
those models against the 2016 database of 250 training and 27 validation CCC heat treated HLW
glasses.

 In the present work, the data for the 35 new glasses (HLW17-NP glasses) described in
Section 2 were added to the training set and the performance of the models (without re-fitting)
was reevaluated; the results are shown in Table 3.5b, which summarizes the key performance
measures of those models against the new 285-glass training set and the same 27-glass validation
set. The ND (nepheline discriminator) model [5], which is the current WTP baseline model, still
performs poorly with a 49% misclassification rate for the training dataset. The predictions by the
two Sub-lattice models are considerably better, with misclassification rates of about 20% for

(Table 3.5a), with misclassification rates of about 12-13% for the training sets and 19% for the
validation set, and relatively high false negative rates (29 and 83%, respectively).

 3.3.1 Selection of Main Model Terms for LRM and SVM Models

 The selection of model variables for the LRM and SVM models was performed in two
steps: 1) Identification of the influential linear variables (main terms) by the Partial Least
Squares (PLS) method with regard to nepheline formation (yes or no, regardless of volume %);
2) Identification of the influential 2nd-order variables (cross terms and quadratic terms that are
constructed from the pool of main terms) by a stepwise regression method (α=0.05
forward/backward direction) for the LRM only (SVM does not use 2nd-order terms) with main
terms selected as in (1).

 The PLS method exploits the correlations between independent variables (glass
constituents) and the response (yes/no for nepheline formation) to reveal underlying latent
structures [29, 30]. The PLS method can be particularly useful when potential “independent”
variables are correlated, as sometimes is the case with a subset of glass constituents. The
Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) score generated from the PLS for each potential
independent variable measures the importance of the variable in the modeling of the dependent
variable (yes/no for nepheline formation) as a function of the potential independent variable
(glass constituent concentration). Variables with VIP scores below a predefined threshold are
removed from the model (and included in Others) and only those variables that increase the
predictive ability of a model are retained. The calculated VIP values for the major glass
constituents are shown in Figure 3.2. The threshold value of 0.8 (JMP default value) is marked in
Figure 3.2 to indicate the four most influential glass components for nepheline formation: Al2O3,
B2O3, Na2O, and SiO2. At VIP > 0.4 the main model terms (linear variables) include five
additional glass constituents: CaO, Fe2O3, K2O, Li2O, and Others. These nine glass components
based on VIP scores from the PLS method agreed with the main terms of the 2016LRM model,
except for MgO (Table 3.6). MgO was added as a main term in the present model based on its
significant benefit in reducing the misclassification rate for the validation dataset, as well as to
avoid inflation of the Others term.

 3.3.3 Support Vector Machine Model

 A Support Vector Machine (SVM) is a non-probabilistic classifier [31-34]. The
mathematical background of SVM was described previously [22]. One of the main advantages of
a SVM as a classifier is its flexibility of classification scheme. Classification of data into two
categories requires a scheme that would be flexible enough to account for complex intimate
mixing of the two classes. For example, in classifying data into categories A and B, there may be
islands of class A inside class B in the configuration space. Additionally, the boundary between
A and B might be nonlinear. Also, combinations of both of these are possible as the
dimensionality of the configuration space increases. Without a priori knowledge of the nature of
the classes, a classification scheme should be flexible enough to account for these possibilities.
Even a very mild form of nonlinearity in two dimensions would be difficult for most
non-adaptive classification routines to handle (take, for example, a single island of class A inside
B). Thus, while classification is a difficult problem, SVM provides a robust general-purpose tool
with enough flexibility for complex classification problems.

construct the high-dimensional configuration space for the SVM in the present work. A large

 SECTION 4.0
 SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

 Nepheline formation during canister centerline cooling (CCC) can pose a product quality
issue for some high-Al glasses since it can lead to significant increases in PCT releases. For
high-Al silicate glasses, formation of nepheline during CCC often can be rather extensive since
all of the nepheline constituents are typically present in large amounts in the glass. Therefore,
suppression of nepheline crystallization during canister cooling is a significant factor that must
be considered in the development of high-Al HLW glasses. In view of the large volume of
high-Al HLW, there is a significant driver for the development of improved methods for
prediction and control of nepheline formation during the vitrification process.

 A primary objective of the present work was to further augment the database of nepheline
formation in Hanford high-Al HLW glasses when subjected to prototypical WTP HLW CCC
heat treatment. In view of the very large composition range covered by the database of CCC heat
treated glasses employed in the previous work [22], one objective of the present work was to
design and test new glasses with compositions such that they are strategically placed near the
nepheline/no-nepheline boundary in an effort to improve the discrimination powers of the
models that are developed. A further objective of the present work was to use the expanded data
set to improve the performance of the previously developed models (LRM and SVM) for
nepheline formation. The work included the design, fabrication, and characterization of a matrix
of 35 new high-Al HLW glasses, and assessment and development of new models for nepheline
formation.

 In contrast to many previous test matrices, the present test matrix of 35 HLW glass
formulations (HLW17-NP glasses) was not designed using a mixture model, which typically
emphasizes spanning or coverage of a compositional space. Instead, a different approach was
used that involved selecting a set of 35 HLW17-NP formulations distributed along the
nepheline/no-nepheline boundary where prediction uncertainty is high, as estimated by the 2016
versions of the SVM and LRM models.

 All of the 35 HLW17-NP compositions were batched, melted, and the resulting glasses
were analyzed to verify their chemical composition. Samples of each of the glasses were
subjected to heat treatment according to the WTP HLW CCC schedule. The heat treated glass
samples were prepared, and examined by SEM/EDS to identify and quantify major crystalline
phases. For every glass that showed evidence of nepheline crystallization, larger quantities of
glass were prepared, and subjected to CCC heat treatment in order to provide sufficient material
for leach testing according to the PCT method. For each of those glasses, the PCT was then
performed on both the CCC heat treated glass and the quenched glass. The amount of nepheline
found in the CCC heat treated glasses ranged from 0.2 vol% to about 72 vol%.

 25

 Comparison of the PCT releases for the quenched and corresponding HLW17-NP
samples subjected to CCC showed that about 1 vol% of nepheline present in CCC heat treated
samples can increase the leachate concentrations significantly, in particular for B and Li. For
HLW17-NP glasses with 1 to 10 vol% nepheline, the normalized PCT B releases increased
between 5 to 9 times in comparison to those for the quenched glass samples. For some HLW17-
NP glasses with more than 40 vol% nepheline-like phases, the normalized PCT B releases
increased to about 50 times that of the corresponding quenched glasses. As discussed in Section
2, the impact of nepheline formation on PCT releases of HLW17-NP glasses subjected to CCC is
profound and similar to those observed for the HLW15 glasses [22].

 In addition, as part of the ongoing effort to expand the database available for HLW
property-composition modeling, six HLW17-NP glasses were selected for measurement of their
melt viscosity and electrical conductivity. Only those glasses that were homogeneous by visual
inspection, and free of crystallization after CCC heat treatment were selected.

 The database used for updating the nepheline models in the present work contains a total
of 312 high waste loading Hanford HLW glasses developed for ORP by VSL. The preparation
and characterization of these glasses at VSL was fully compliant with ORP and WTP quality
assurance requirements. In addition, all of these glasses were heat treated according to the same
prototypical WTP CCC profile [25]. Of the 312 glasses in the database, 98 showed nepheline
crystallization in CCC heat treated samples. For the modeling work, the 312 HLW glasses were
divided into a training set of 285 glasses and a validation set of 27 glasses. All of the 35 new
HLW17-NP glasses were included in the training set, while validation set of the 27 HLW glasses
was identical to the validation set used in the previous work [22].

 In the present work, the data for the 35 new glasses (HLW17-NP glasses) were added to
the training set and the performance of the models considered previously [22] (without re-fitting)
was reevaluated against the new 285-glass training set and the same 27-glass validation set. The
ND (nepheline discriminator) model [5], which is the current WTP baseline model, still performs
poorly with a 49% misclassification rate for the training dataset. The predictions by the two
Sub-lattice models [26, 27] are considerably better, with misclassification rates of about 20% for
both the training set and validation set, similar to the results with the original training set. The
performance of the Sub-mixture model [28] is similar to that for the original training set, with
misclassification rates of about 12-13% for the training set and 19% for the validation set, and
relatively high false negative rates (29 and 83%, respectively).

 As would be expected based on the design of the new glasses, which placed the new
glasses near the yes/no boundary of the 2016 SVM and LRM models, both of these models
perform worse with the new training set, which includes the new glasses, as compared to the
2016 training set, which does not. The misclassification rates increased from about 8% to about
13% for both the SVM and LRM models, while the false negative rates increased from about
19% to about 30% for the LRM model and from about 19% to about 24% for the SVM model.

Nevertheless, both models remain very competitive, ranking first (SVM) and third (LRM) in
terms of misclassification rates for the training set and first for the validation set; and of the three
models with the lowest misclassification rates, the SVM and LRM models have the lowest
overall false negative rates.

 In the next stage of the work, the LRM and SVM models were refined by re-training
them on the 285 glass training set, which includes the 35 new glasses (HLW17-NP glasses)
designed and characterized in the present work. The new LRM model (2018LRM) gives a
misclassification rate of 8.4% for the training set and 7.4% for the validation set, and false
negative rates of 13.0% and 16.7%, respectively. The misclassification rates are essentially
identical to those from the 2016LRM for its training set. The false negative rate is slightly lower
than that of 2016LRM for its training set, and identical to that of 2016LRM for the validation set.
For the new SVM model (2018SVM), the misclassification rate for the 285-glass training set is
6.7%; this can be compared to 7.6% for the 2016SVM model with the 250-glass training set on
which it was developed and 12.6% for the 285-glass training set. The misclassification rate for
the validation set was the same for the 2016SVM and 2018SVM models. The false negative rate
for the training set decreased significantly to 12.0% and remained the same at 16.7% for the
validation set.

 In summary, the SVM model developed in the present work is able to classify nepheline-
free from nepheline-bearing HLW CCC glasses with about 7% misclassification rate for both the
training set and the validation set and false negative rates of 12.0 and 16.7%, respectively. The
SVM model from this work is thus recommended as the preferred nepheline classifier for HLW
CCC glasses. At a slightly higher misclassification rate, LRM from the present work can be
almost as effective. Based on this progress, efforts should continue on collecting additional glass
property-composition data for development of models for prediction of nepheline formation.

 SECTION 5.0
 EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES

 The experimental procedures used in the preparation and characterization of the
simulated HLW glasses are presented in this section. The following subsections discuss the
preparation of glass batches, crucible glass melting, glass composition analysis, CCC heat
treatment, and test procedures for PCT, determination of crystal content and type, electrical
conductivity, and viscosity. Nepheline formation on CCC heat treatment was measured on all of
the glasses. The remaining properties were measured on selected glasses based on an assessment
of the phase quality of quenched and CCC glass samples.

5.1 Glass Batching and Preparation

 Thirty-five crucible melts were prepared and characterized following VSL standard
operating procedures. The target glass composition (as weight percent oxides) was used to create
a batching sheet providing information on the required starting materials and their weights for
glass melting. A blender was used to mix and homogenize the starting materials before they were
loaded into platinum-10% gold (Pt-Au) crucibles that were engraved with individual
identification numbers.

 The crucibles were placed inside a Deltech DT-28 or DT-29 furnace with a Eurotherm
2404 temperature controller. Glasses were melted for 120 minutes at 1150°C. Mixing of the melt
(typically for 90 minutes) was accomplished mechanically using a platinum stirrer. The molten
glass was poured onto a graphite plate to cool. The glass samples were collected and analyzed
for composition before distribution for property measurements.

5.2 Analysis of Glass Compositions

 The compositions of the glasses were determined using XRF spectroscopy. Powdered
samples of the fabricated glasses (–200 mesh) were analyzed with a PANalytical AxiosmAX-
Advanced XRF spectrometer. The spectrometer was calibrated over a range of glass
compositions using standard reference materials traceable to the National Institute of Standards
and Technology (NIST), as well as waste glasses including the Argonne National Laboratory-
Low Activity Waste Reference Material (ANL-LRM) [36], the Defense Waste Processing
Facility-Environmental Assessment (DWPF-EA) glass [37], and Hanford WTP glasses.

 28

 The XRF detection limit for most components is about 0.01 wt%. The accuracy of the
analysis is about  10 relative percent for major components (> 3.0 wt% in the glass) or 1.0 wt%
absolute, whichever is smaller.

5.3 Nepheline Formation on CCC

 Samples of all of the 35 HLW glasses were subjected to CCC heat treatment in order to
simulate the slow cooling of glass in the center of a canister. The glass samples were maintained
at a pre-melt temperature of 1200°C in a platinum alloy crucible for 1 hour before initiation of
CCC heat treatment. The samples were then prepared for SEM/EDS examination to determine
the volume fraction of crystalline phases and for identification of the dominant crystalline
phases. The volume percent of crystalline phases found in the heat-treated glasses were
determined by performing image analysis on the SEM images.

 All glasses were subjected to CCC in 5 ml Pt-Au crucibles using about 5 g each of
sample. For those glass samples showing nepheline crystallization after CCC, a second CCC heat
treatment was conducted using ~50 g samples in 100 ml Pt-Au crucibles and prepared for the
7-day PCT.

5.4 Product Consistency Test

 The Product Consistency Test (PCT) was conducted using 4 g of crushed glass (100-200
mesh, 75-149 m) placed in 40 ml of test solution (de-ionized water) inside 304L stainless steel
vessels. These test conditions result in a ratio of the glass surface area to the solution volume of
about 2000 m-1. PCTs were performed at 90ºC for 7 days according to American Society for
Testing and Materials (ASTM) C 1285 [38], in accordance with the current WTP contract
requirement [39]. All tests were conducted in triplicate, in parallel with a standard glass included
in each test set. The leachates were sampled after seven days. One milliliter of sampled leachate
was mixed with 20 ml of 1M HNO3 and the resulting solution analyzed by direct current plasma
atomic emission spectroscopy (DCP-AES). Another 3 ml of sampled leachate was used for pH
measurement. All glasses showing nepheline crystallization on CCC as well as the corresponding
as-melted glasses were subjected to the PCT to determine the effect of nepheline crystallization
on PCT releases.

5.5 Electrical Conductivity

 The electrical conductivity (σ) of selected glasses was determined by measuring the
resistance of the melt as a function of frequency using a calibrated platinum/rhodium electrode
probe attached to an Agilent model 4294A impedance analyzer. Measurements were performed
at temperatures (T) around 950, 1050, 1150 and 1250ºC. The results were analyzed using an

 29

The Catholic University of America Nepheline Constraint for Hanford HLW Glass
Vitreous State Laboratory Final Report, VSL-18R4390-1, Rev. 0

equivalent circuit model to obtain the DC electrical conductivity. To facilitate comparison, the
electrical conductivity data were interpolated to standard temperatures (e.g., 1150ºC) using the
Vogel-Fulcher equation:

                                         ln σ = [A/(T–To)] + B                                 (5.1)

where A, B, and To are fitting parameters. Estimated uncertainties in the electrical conductivity
measurements are ±20 relative%. Six glasses showing no nepheline crystallization were selected
for electrical conductivity measurements.

5.6 Viscosity

 The melt viscosity (η) of selected glasses was measured using a Brookfield viscometer
with a platinum-rhodium spindle and crucible. The relative torque of a rotating spindle immersed
in molten glass was measured as a function of rotational velocity at four temperatures (T) in the
range of 950-1250ºC, similar to those employed for electrical conductivity. To facilitate
comparison, the viscosity data were interpolated to standard temperatures (e.g., 1150ºC) using
the Vogel-Fulcher equation:

                                         ln η = [A/(T–To)] + B,                               (5.2)

where A, B, and To are fitting parameters. The equipment was calibrated at room temperature
using standard oils of known viscosity and then checked in the temperature range of 950 to
1250ºC using a NIST standard reference glass (SRM 711). Both precision and accuracy of the
viscosity measurements are estimated to be within ±20 relative %. Six glasses showing no
nepheline crystallization were selected for viscosity measurements.
