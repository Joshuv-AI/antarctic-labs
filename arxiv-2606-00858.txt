Change-Point Detection for Object-valued Time Series
Yi Zhang a {a}, Changbo Zhu and Xiaofeng Shao c {c}

Abstract
This article is concerned with change point detection for object-valued data that reside in a metric space, which has attracted some recent interests in statistics and econometrics literature. The existing methods either focus on independent data or can only detect change in the Fréchet mean or variance. In this paper, we propose a self-normalization (SN, hereafter) based statistic for detecting a shift in the marginal distribution of object-valued time series. Our test is universally applicable to a wide range of object-valued data, such as distributional and network data, and can accommodate weak serial dependence. In addition the proposed test statistic is almost tuning parameter free, has pivotal limiting null distribution and only uses the pairwise distances. When combined with the Wild Binary Segmentation algorithm (WBS, hereafter), our statistic can be used to estimate the number and locations of multiple change points. Asymptotic results for our SN based statistic are derived under both null and local alternatives in the single change point setting. For the first time, the WBS estimation consistency is shown for a broad class of object-valued time series and in a nonparametric setting, which requires new non-standard theoretical arguments. Extensive numerical experiments and real data analysis are conducted to illustrate the effectiveness and broad applicability of our proposed method.



Keywords:
Binary Segmentation; Functional data; Non-Euclidean Data; Sample Splitting; Self-normalization.

Introduction

Statistical analysis of non-Euclidean data (object-valued data or random objects) that reside in a metric space is gradually emerging as an important branch of functional data analysis in econometrics and statistics, motivated by increasing encounter of such data in many modern applications. Examples include
the analysis of sequences of age-at-death distributions over calendar years
(
38
;
47
)
,

(
19
)
, compositional brain volumes
(
62
;
60
)
and graph Laplacians of networks
(
28
;
53
)
.
One of the main challenges in dealing with such data is that the usual vector/Hilbert space operations, such as
projection and inner product may not be well defined and only the distance between two non-Euclidean
data objects is available. Despite the challenge, the list of papers that propose new statistical techniques to analyze
non-Euclidean data has been growing. See
21
;
22
,
41
,
52
,
61
,
56
,
63
;
64
,
32
;
33
, among others.

In this paper, we shall focus on offline change-point detection for object-valued time series.
Such temporally dependent objects are prevalent in practice and well-known examples include yearly age-at-death distributions for countries in Europe
(
22
)
, annual observations of compositions of energy sources for power generation in the US
(
64
)
, and daily Pearson correlation matrices for five cryptocurrencies
(
33
)
. Also see Section
5
for additional data examples. The literature for offline change-point detection of object-valued data is quite recent. For example,
12
and
15
developed
graph-based scan statistics for both the single change-point and the changed interval alternatives to detect the distributional change for independent object data; see
11
for a recent review on graph-based change point detection methods. Recently,
22
proposed a novel test for a change point in the Fréchet mean and variance
(
24
)
for a sequence of independent random objects and adopted binary segmentation for change point estimation.
20
proposed the concept of distance profiles, which fully characterize the distribution of random objects under conditions that basically require the metric space to be of strong negative type (see proposition 1 therein). Building on distance profile,
23
developed a test which can detect distributional change for serially independent random objects. Note that theoretical results developed for the above-mentioned methods are all for single change point detection. To accommodate serial dependence,

33
developed an self-normalization (SN, hereafter) based test by extending the SN idea
(
49
;
48
;
50
)
from vector time series to object-valued time series. However, the method in
33
is also limited to capture change point in Fréchet mean or variance only and their test may not be powerful when the change is in high-order characteristics of distributions; see numerical evidence in Section
4
.

In contrast to the limited literature for change-point detection of object-valued data, the same problem for functional time series, which resides in a Hilbert space, has been extensively studied. For example, single and multiple change point detection probem for functional data has been studied in
57
;
3
;
51
;
4
;
34
;
13
;
42
;
29
; see Chapter 8 in
30
for a comprehensive review of the main developments in this area. It is worth noting that these methods can not be easily extended to the object-valued time series setting, due to the lack of algebraic operations for metric space valued data.

The main goal of this paper is to fill in the gap and develop new testing and estimation procedures that target the shift in marginal distributions of object-valued time series with serial dependence. On the testing front, we propose a non-parametric method that can detect distributional changes for metric space valued time series based on sample splitting and self-normalization.
To construct our test statistic, the object-valued data are first mapped into a Hilbert space, and then projected along the direction of change. After that, an SN-based test statistic is constructed using the projected one-dimensional sequence. In the single change point setting, the statistic we developed is nearly tuning parameter free (except for a cut-off parameter which restricts the true change point from being too close to the end-points) and has pivotal limiting null distribution.
On the estimation front, we utilize the WBS algorithm
(
25
)
for multiple change point estimation and provide theoretical guarantee in terms of estimation consistency, which is a new contribution to the literature.

As one of the state-of-the-art segmentation algorithm with promising practical performance, WBS has been combined with various single change point detection methods for Euclidean or metric space valued time series to enable multiple change point estimation (see e.g.,
9
,
54
,
58
,
55
and
33
) without theoretical justification. The WBS algorithm was originally developed to detect change points in the mean of univariate i.i.d data, and its performance on temporally dependent data could depend on the estimation of the long run variance (LRV), which was known to be a difficult problem due to the difficulty of choosing the bandwidth in the presence of change-points
(
16
;
48
;
43
)
. By using self-normalization, our method avoids direct estimation of LRV. At the same time, the adoption of a self-normalizer and the usage of projection in our test statistic significantly complicate our proof for estimation consistency, which requires substantially different theoretical arguments from those in
25
. To our knowledge, except for
35
where the WBS estimation consistency was proved for a parametric time series model, we are the first to show the WBS estimation consistency for a broad class of time series (which includes Euclidean valued time series as a special case) and in a nonparametric setting. In our theory, the convergence rates for each change point estimator are adaptive to the heterogeneous change magnitudes (see Remark
5
in Section
3
). A more detailed discussion on WBS and WBS2
(
26
)
is provided at the beginning of Section
3
.

2.1
Preliminary Result

Given
\mathbb{R}
- or
\mathcal{H}
-valued random variables
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
and positive integer
n
, define its
\rho
-mixing coefficient
(
7
)
as
\rho(n)=\sup_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\rho(\mathcal{F}_{-\infty}^{j},\mathcal{F}_{j+n}^{\infty})
, where

\rho(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})=\sup\left\{\frac{|\cov(X,Y)|}{\sqrt{\var(X)\var(Y)}}:X\in L_{2}(\mathcal{A}),Y\in L_{2}(\mathcal{B})\right\}

and
L_{2}(\mathcal{A})
is the set of
\mathbb{R}
-valued
\mathcal{A}
-measurable random variables with finite second moment for any
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
.
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
is said to be
\rho
-mixing if
\lim_{n\to\infty}\rho(n)=0
. As in
8
, define the coefficient
\alpha_{1,1}(n)=\sup_{j\in\mathbb{Z},k\geq n}\alpha(\mathcal{F}_{j}^{j},\mathcal{F}_{j+k}^{j+k})
, where
\alpha(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})=\sup\big\{|P(A\cap B)-P(A)P(B)|:A\in\mathcal{A},B\in\mathcal{B}\big\}
for any
\sigma
-fields
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
.
An operator
Q:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
is said to be a positive, self-adjoint, trace class operator if (i)
\langle Qh,h\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\geq 0
for any
h\in\mathcal{H}
;
(ii)
\langle Qh,x\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\langle h,Qx\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
for any
h,x\in\mathcal{H}
; (iii)
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\langle Qe_{k},e_{k}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}<\infty
for any orthonormal base
\{e_{k}\}
of
\mathcal{H}
.

Based on these concepts, we can define
\mathcal{H}
-valued Gaussian random element as well as
\mathcal{H}
-valued Brownian motion.

Definition 1

(
1
)
.

An
\mathcal{H}
-valued random element
X
is Gaussian with covariance operator
Q
and mean
\mu\in H
(denoted as
X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,Q)
) if for any
h\in\mathcal{H}
,
\langle X,h\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\langle\mu,h\rangle_{\mathcal{H}},\langle Qh,h\rangle_{\mathcal{H}})
.

Definition 2

(
1
)
.

Let
Q
be a positive, self-adjoint, trace class operator on
\mathcal{H}
. An
\mathcal{H}
-valued Brownian motion with covariance operator
Q
is defined as
B_{Q}(t),t\in[0,1]
such that (i)
B_{Q}(0)=0
; (ii)
B_{Q}(t)
has continuous sample path; (iii)
B_{Q}(t)
has independent increment; (iv)
B_{Q}(t)-B_{Q}(s)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,(t-s)Q)
for any
0\leq s<t\leq 1
.

The following FCLT for stationary
\mathcal{H}
-valued random variables is from
8
; see
51
for similar results under different mixing conditions.

.

Let
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a sequence of strictly stationary
\mathcal{H}
-valued random variables with mean

. Assume

is

-mixing and the following condition holds for some

where

is an

-valued Brownian motion with

\displaystyle\langle Qx,y\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\sum_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}\E\big(\langle X_{0}-\mu,x\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\langle X_{t}-\mu,y\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\big)\mbox{ for any }x,y\in\mathcal{H}.

(1)

Furthermore, the series in Equation (
1
) converges absolutely.

2.2
Single Change Point Testing and Estimation

From now on, we assume the time series
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
takes value in a separable metric space
(\mathcal{X},d)
of strong negative type (see Definition 2.2 in
9
or
36
). Let
P_{i}
be the marginal distribution of
X_{i}
. We aim to test

H_{0}:P_{1}=\cdots=P_{n}

versus

H_{1}:P_{1}=\cdots=P_{k^{\ast}}\not=P_{k^{\ast}+1}=\cdots=P_{n}
for some
k^{\ast}=\lfloor nr_{0}\rfloor\in(nb,(1-b)n)
with
b\in(0,1/2)
.
Here
b
is a cutoff parameter and it is typically set to be
0.1
or
0.15
; see
2
. This is a common assumption as the detection of a change point is very challenging when it is close to the boundary; see
31
for some recent work.

By Proposition 3.1 in
9
, for a metric space
(\mathcal{X},d)
of strong negative type, there exists a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and an embedding map
\phi:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
such that
d(x,x^{\prime})=\|\phi(x)-\phi(x^{\prime})\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}
. For
t\in\mathbb{Z}
, let
Y_{t}=\phi(X_{t})
. If
\int\|Y_{t}\|_{\mathcal{H}}dP_{t}<\infty
, define
\mu_{t}=\int Y_{t}dP_{t}
, which is the unique element in
\mathcal{H}
corresponding to the bounded linear functional
\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}:h\to\int\langle h,Y_{t}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}dP_{t}
. Then by Proposition 3.1 in
36
, the null and alternative hypothesis are equivalent to
H_{0}:\mu_{1}=\cdots=\mu_{n}

versus

H_{1}:\mu_{1}=\cdots=\mu_{k^{\ast}}\not=\mu_{k^{\ast}+1}=\cdots=\mu_{n}
.

Remark 1
.

Some possible embedding maps for specific metric spaces are given in Section 3 of
36
. For example, when
(\mathcal{X},d)
is the one dimensional Euclidean space, we can define
\phi(x)=\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)}(y)-\mathbbm{1}_{[x,\infty)}(y)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
where
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is the Hilbert space of square integrable functions on
\mathbb{R}
. A similar embedding into
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
can also be defined when
(\mathcal{X},d)
is the
p
-dimensional Euclidean space for
p\geq 2
. Additionally, define
k:\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}
as

\displaystyle k(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}\big[d(x,x_{0})+d(y,x_{0})-d(x,y)\big]

(2)

for some fixed
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Then we can also define the embedding of the
p
-dimensional Euclidean space as
\phi(x)=k(x,\cdot)
where
k(x,\cdot)
is an element in the reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) induced by the kernel function
k(\cdot,\cdot)
; see Section 4 of
46
for more details.

To detect a change in
\mu_{t}
, we first split the data into three parts:
\mathcal{X}_{1}=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{{\lfloor nb\rfloor}}\}
,
\mathcal{X}_{2}=\{X_{{\lfloor nb\rfloor}+1},\dots,X_{n-{\lfloor nb\rfloor}}\}
and
\mathcal{X}_{3}=\{X_{n-{\lfloor nb\rfloor}+1},\dots,X_{n}\}
. Based on
\mathcal{X}_{1}
and
\mathcal{X}_{3}
, we define

\displaystyle\bar{Y}_{1}-\bar{Y}_{3}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{t=1}^{{\lfloor nb\rfloor}}Y_{t}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{t=n-{\lfloor nb\rfloor}+1}^{n}Y_{t},

(3)

which estimates the mean change
\mu_{1}{-}\mu_{n}
(subject to a rescaling factor) under
H_{1}
. Then we project the transformed observations in
\mathcal{X}_{2}
along the direction
\bar{Y}_{1}-\bar{Y}_{3}
, that is, we define univariate random variables

Z_{t}=\langle\bar{Y}_{1}-\bar{Y}_{3},Y_{t}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
for
t={\lfloor nb\rfloor}{+}1,\cdots,n{-}{\lfloor nb\rfloor}
. Note that the temporal dependence in the projected sequence
\{Z_{t}\}
is very complex due to projection. Nevertheless, we shall apply the SN test statistic in
48
, which is developed for weakly dependent vector time series, to
\{Z_{t}\}
. To be specific, let
S_{a,b}{=}\sum_{t=a}^{b}Z_{t}
, our test statistic is defined as

\displaystyle G_{n}=\max_{k={\lfloor nb\rfloor}+1,{\lfloor nb\rfloor}+2,\dots,n-{\lfloor nb\rfloor}-1}\frac{T_{n}(k)}{\sqrt{V_{n}(k)}},

where
T_{n},V_{n}
are defined as

\displaystyle T_{n}(k)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n-2{\lfloor nb\rfloor}}}\sum_{t={\lfloor nb\rfloor}+1}^{k}\left(Z_{t}-\frac{S_{{\lfloor nb\rfloor}+1,n-{\lfloor nb\rfloor}}}{n-2{\lfloor nb\rfloor}}\right),

(4)

\displaystyle V_{n}(k)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(n-2{\lfloor nb\rfloor})^{2}}\Bigg\{\sum_{t={\lfloor nb\rfloor}+1}^{k}\left\{S_{{\lfloor nb\rfloor}+1,t}-\frac{t-{\lfloor nb\rfloor}}{k-{\lfloor nb\rfloor}}S_{{\lfloor nb\rfloor}+1,k}\right\}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\sum_{t=k+1}^{n-{\lfloor nb\rfloor}}\left\{S_{t,n-{\lfloor nb\rfloor}}-\frac{n-{\lfloor nb\rfloor}-t+1}{n-{\lfloor nb\rfloor}-k}S_{k+1,n-{\lfloor nb\rfloor}}\right\}^{2}\Bigg\}.

(5)

Remark 2
.

It is worth noting that the sequence
\{Y_{t}\}
depends on the embedding map
\phi(\cdot)
which may be unknown or difficult to derive. However, due to the use of sample splitting and self-normalization, our statistic
G_{n}
does not depend on
\phi(\cdot)
in any explicit way and only depends on pairwise distances. To see this, note that

\displaystyle Z_{t}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{{\lfloor nb\rfloor}}\langle Y_{j},Y_{t}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=n-{\lfloor nb\rfloor}+1}^{n}\langle Y_{j},Y_{t}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{{\lfloor nb\rfloor}}\{\|Y_{j}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}+\|Y_{t}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}-d(X_{j},X_{t})\}-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=n-{\lfloor nb\rfloor}+1}^{n}\{\|Y_{j}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}+\|Y_{t}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}-d(X_{j},X_{t})\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{{\lfloor nb\rfloor}}d(X_{j},X_{t})+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=n-{\lfloor nb\rfloor}+1}^{n}d(X_{j},X_{t})+R_{n},

where
R_{n}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{{\lfloor nb\rfloor}}\|Y_{j}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=n-{\lfloor nb\rfloor}+1}^{n}\|Y_{j}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}
is an unknown quantity that depends on
\phi(\cdot)
. The key is that
R_{n}
does not depend on
t
, so it gets canceled out when forming the SN test statistic. To be specific, define
\hat{Z}_{t}=\sum_{j=n-{\lfloor nb\rfloor}+1}^{n}d(X_{j},X_{t})-\sum_{j=1}^{{\lfloor nb\rfloor}}d(X_{j},X_{t})
and
\hat{S}_{a,b}=\sum_{t=a}^{b}\hat{Z}_{t}
, then the statistic
G_{n}
can be calculated by replacing
Z_{t},S_{s,b}
in the definition of
T_{n}(k)
and
V_{n}(k)
with
\hat{Z}_{t}
and
\hat{S}_{a,b}
. In other words, our test statistic
G_{n}
only depends on the pairwise distances
\{d(X_{t},X_{t^{\prime}})\}_{t,t^{\prime}=1}^{n}
.

Remark 3
.

Note that our statistic
G_{n}
only requires one trimming parameter
b
, whereas the SN-based statistics proposed in Section 4 of
33
require two tuning parameters
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
, and target the changes in Fréchet mean and variance only. In their paper,

\boldsymbol{1}(G_{n}>G_{\gamma})

under the null and alternative.

.

Let
\{Y_{t}{-}\mu_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a strictly stationary

-valued time series. Assume

is

-mixing and the following condition hold for some

Remark 4
.

By the definition of

\displaystyle 2^{1+\delta}\big(\E\|Y_{1}\|^{2+\delta}_{\mathcal{H}}+\|\mu_{1}\|^{2+\delta}_{\mathcal{H}}\big)

\sup_{r\in[0,1]}\frac{B^{\prime}(r)-rB^{\prime}(1)}{\big\{\int_{0}^{r}[B^{\prime}(s)-\frac{s}{r}B^{\prime}(r)]^{2}ds+\int_{r}^{1}[B^{\prime}(1)-B^{\prime}(s)-\frac{1-s}{1-r}(B^{\prime}(1)-B^{\prime}(r))]^{2}ds\big\}^{1/2}},

B^{\prime}(r){=}B(r){+}H_{y}((1{-}2b)r{+}b)/\sqrt{1-2b}
,
H_{y}=\langle y,(r{-}r_{0}){c}\mathbf{1}_{r\geq r_{0}}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}/\sqrt{\langle Qy,y\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}}

with
\mathbf{1}_{r\geq r_{0}}=1
if
r\geq r_{0}
and
0
otherwise.

3.

If
\sqrt{n}\|{\Delta}_{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\to 0
, then
G_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\to}}G,
so our test have trivial power asymptotically.

When a single change point is detected, it is of interest to estimate its location. Building upon the test statistic
G_{n}
, it is natural to estimate
k^{\ast}
by

\hat{k}=\argmax_{k={\lfloor nb\rfloor}+1,{\lfloor nb\rfloor}+2,\dots,n-{\lfloor nb\rfloor}-1}\frac{T_{n}(k)}{\sqrt{V_{n}(k)}}.

The following theorem shows the consistency of
\hat{k}
, which is proved in the appendix of the supplement.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose Assumption
1
holds and
\sqrt{n}\|{\Delta}_{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\to\infty
. For a given integer sequence
\epsilon_{n}
which satisfies that
\frac{\epsilon_{n}}{n}\to 0
and
n(\epsilon_{n}\|{\Delta}_{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}})^{-2}\to 0
, we have

P(|\hat{k}-k^{\ast}|<\epsilon_{n})\to 1.

(7)

The proof of Theorem
3
basically follows the same idea as in the proof of Theorem 1 in
59
. Note that the convergence rate
\epsilon_{n}
of the change point estimator
\hat{k}
is the same as the rate derived in Theorem 1 of
59
, which first worked out the consistency and the rate for the SN-based change point location estimator for Euclidean time series. The latter paper also stated that under the fixed alternative setting (i.e.,
\|{\Delta}_{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}}=C>0
), the convergence rate
\epsilon_{n}/n
is at best
1/\sqrt{n}
. In comparison, as shown in
5
, the optimal convergence rate for detecting change point in the mean of a multivariate time series is
1/n
. It seems difficult to improve convergence rate
\epsilon_{n}
due to the complex form of
V_{n}(k)
in the definition of
G_{n}
. We refer to the discussion following Theorem
4
in
59
for more details of a local refinement procedure to achieve the optimal rate.

2.3
Strong negativity of some metric spaces

According to Theorem 3.16 in
36
, every separable Hilbert space, such as the Euclidean space or the space of square integrable functions, is a metric space of strong negative type. Besides that, it is in general not easy to verify that a metric space is of strong negative type. According to part (iv) in
37
, if
(\mathcal{X},d)
is a separable semimetric space of negative type, then
(\mathcal{X},d^{r})
is a metric space of strong negative type for any
r\in(0,1)
. So in order to show
(\mathcal{X},d)
is a metric space of strong negative type, it suffices to show, by verifying the definition,
(\mathcal{X},d^{r})
is of negative type for some
r>1
. For example, by Theorem 3.6 in
39
, it is immediately clear that the space of measurable functions supported on
[0,1]
with metric
d(f,g)=\{\int_{[0,1]}|f(x)-g(x)|^{p}dx\}^{1/p}
is a metric space of strong negative type for any
p\in(1,2)
.

As for metric spaces that are not of strong negative type, it is proved in
18
that
\mathbb{R}^{q}
with the
\ell^{p}
metric is not a metric space of negative type for any
3\leq q\leq\infty
and
2<p\leq\infty
. In addition, as stated in
36
,
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with the
\ell^{1}
metric is a metric space of negative type, but not of strong negative type.

We now show the following metric spaces where object-valued data resides in are of strong negative type.

1.

The space
\Omega_{1}
of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}
, equipped with the 2-Wasserstein metric
d_{W}(x,y)=\{\int_{0}^{1}(F^{-1}_{x}(u)-F^{-1}_{y}(u))^{2}du\}^{1/2}
, where
F^{-1}_{x},F^{-1}_{y}
are the inverse cdf functions of
x
and
y
.

2.

The space
\Omega_{2}
of Graph Laplacians of weighted graphs (see Section 3 in
28
) equipped with Frobenius metric
d_{F}(A,B)=\{\tr[(A-B)^{\top}(A-B)]\}^{1/2}
for
d\times d
symmetric matrices
A
and
B
.

3.

The space
\Omega_{3}
of positive definite covariance matrices, equipped with the log-Euclidean metric
d_{E}(A,B)=d_{F}(\log_{m}(A),\log_{m}(B))
for
d\times d
symmetric matrices
A
and
B
, where
\log_{m}
is the matrix-log function.

According to part (iv) in
37
, it suffice to show
(\Omega_{1},d_{W}^{2})
,
(\Omega_{2},d_{F}^{2})
and
(\Omega_{3},d_{E}^{2})
are separable semimetric spaces of negative type. The separability of
(\Omega_{1},d_{W}^{2})
is implied by the separability of
(\Omega_{1},d_{W})
, which is shown in
6
. For any
n\geq 2
,
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}\in\Omega_{1}
,
a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
with
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}=0
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{i}a_{j}d^{2}_{W}(x_{i},x_{j})=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{i}a_{j}(F^{-1}_{x_{i}}(u)-F^{-1}_{x_{j}}(u))^{2}du

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{i}a_{j}F^{-1}_{x_{i}}(u)F^{-1}_{x_{j}}(u)du

(8)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\int_{0}^{1}\big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}F^{-1}_{x_{i}}(u)\big)^{2}du\leq 0,

so
(\Omega_{1},d_{W}^{2})
is of negative type and the strong negativity of
(\Omega_{1},d_{W})
is shown. For
(\Omega_{2},d_{F}^{2})
, the separability follows from the fact that
\mathbb{R}^{d(d+1)/2}
with the Euclidean metric is separable and every subspace of a separable metric space is separable (see Proposition 26 in Section 9.6 of
44
). Denote
A_{rs}
as the
(r,s)
element of a matrix
A\in\Omega_{2}
, for any
n\geq 2
,
A^{1},A^{2},\dots,A^{n}\in\Omega_{2}
,
a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
with
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}=0
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{i}a_{j}d^{2}_{F}(A^{i},A^{j})=\sum_{r=1}^{n}\sum_{s=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{i}a_{j}(A^{i}_{rs}-A^{j}_{rs})^{2}=-2\sum_{r=1}^{n}\sum_{s=1}^{n}\big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}A^{i}_{rs}\big)^{2}\leq 0,

so
(\Omega_{2},d_{F}^{2})
is of negative type, which implies
(\Omega_{2},d_{F})
is of strong negative type. The strong negativity of
(\Omega_{3},d_{E})
can be shown in a similar way as
(\Omega_{2},d_{F})
and we omit the details.

3
Multiple Change Point Estimation: the WBS-SN Algorithm

The original WBS algorithm was proposed in
25
to detect changes in the mean
f_{t}
for
\mathbb{R}
-valued random variables
X_{t}=f_{t}+\epsilon_{t}
with noise
\epsilon_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
. It operates in an iterative manner as follows: At the beginning,
M
independent random intervals
\{(s_{m},e_{m})\}_{m=1}^{M}
are drawn from
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
and
C_{m}=\max_{k\in\{{s_{m}},\dots,{e_{m}-1}\}}|\tilde{T}_{m}(k)|
are calculated where the CUSUM statistic
\tilde{T}_{m}(k)
(calculated on
\{X_{s_{m}},\dots,X_{e_{m}}\}
) is a rescaled version of
T_{n}(k)
defined in Equation (
4
). If
\max\{C_{m}:m=1,\dots,M\}>K_{n}
for some threshold
K_{n}
, then the first estimated change point is chosen as
k_{1}
where
(m_{1},k_{1})=\argmax_{m\in\{1,2,\dots,M\},k\in\{{s_{m}},\dots,{e_{m}-1}\}}|\tilde{T}_{m}(k)|
. The same procedure is then repeated for
C_{m}
calculated on the intervals inside subsamples
\{X_{1},\dots,X_{k_{1}}\}
and
\{X_{k_{1}+1},\dots,X_{n}\}
, which gives the second and third change point estimator. The splitting and testing continue until no change points are found in each subsample.
That is, the algorithm stops if, after several iterations, the maximum of
C_{m}
calculated in all resulting subsamples are less than
K_{n}
.

As stated in Section 3.4 of
25
, the performance of WBS is sensitive to the choice of the threshold
K_{n}
, which depends on the estimation of the noise level
\sigma^{2}
. Alternatively,
25
proposed another implementation of WBS by setting
K_{n}=0
in the above algorithm and select the change point estimators from the output of WBS using “strengthened Schwarz Information Criterion” (sSIC), which also depends on an estimator of
\sigma^{2}
. The extension of WBS to mean change point estimation in time series data inevitably involves the LRV estimation, which is a difficult task due to the bandwidth selection and the presence of change points. The popularly adopted kernel estimator of LRV tends to incur downward bias
(
10
)
and requires careful choice of bandwidth parameters. So far there seems very little theoretical work on the consistency of WBS in the time series setting.
To the best of our knowledge, the only consistency result for the use of WBS was presented in
35
, which is developed for detecting variance change in a sequence of random scaled
\chi^{2}_{1}
-distributed time series data with piecewise constant variance, where the CUSUM statistic
\tilde{T}_{m}(k)
is scaled by
q_{m}=(e_{m}-s_{m}+1)^{-1}\sum_{t=s_{m}}^{e_{m}}X_{t}^{2}
.

Recently,
26
proposed the Wild Binary Segmentation 2 (WBS2) algorithm for i.i.d data, where instead of generating
M
random intervals at the beginning, a smaller number of random intervals are drawn in each iteration and the algorithm stops after
(n{-}1)
change point candidates are selected (see Theorem 3.1 in
26
). Based on WBS2,
14
proposed the WCM.gSa algorithm for detecting multiple change points in the mean of time series data, where a new application of the Schwarz criterion
(
45
)
is used to select the change point estimators from selected sub-collections in the output of WBS2. Although WBS2 does not impose any model assumption on the data, the estimation consistency result of WCM.gSa requires the demeaned time series coming from an AR(p) model.

In this section, we present an algorithm (WBS-SN) that estimates the locations of unknown but fixed number of change points in
\{\mu_{t}\}
based on WBS. For positive integer
m_{0}
and
r^{\ast}_{0}=0<r^{\ast}_{1}<r^{\ast}_{2}<\cdots<r^{\ast}_{m_{0}}<1=r^{\ast}_{m_{0}+1}
, we assume the change points locate at
k^{\ast}_{i}=\lfloor nr^{\ast}_{i}\rfloor
and have magnitude
\Delta^{\ast}_{i}=\mu_{k^{\ast}_{i}+1}-\mu_{k^{\ast}_{i}}
for
i=1,2,\dots,m_{0}
.
Then the data generating process under the multiple change alternative corresponds to

H_{1}:P_{1}=\cdots=P_{k_{1}^{\ast}}\not=P_{k_{1}^{\ast}+1}=\cdots=P_{k_{2}^{\ast}}\not=P_{k_{2}^{\ast}+1}\cdots P_{k_{m_{0}}^{\ast}}\not=P_{k_{m_{0}}^{\ast}+1}=\cdots=P_{n}.

We generate
M
small intervals such that the length of each interval is proportional to
n
in the following way. Let
\{U_{1m}\}_{m=1}^{M},\{U_{2m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
be two independent sequences of i.i.d
Uniform[0,1]
random variables,
\underline{U}_{m}=\min\{U_{1m},U_{2m}\}
and
\bar{U}_{m}=\max\{U_{1m},U_{2m}\}
, then each interval is represented as
(\underline{U}_{m},\bar{U}_{m})
.
Consider the event that each change point is contained in the middle of at least one interval, which does not contain any other change points. To be specific, define

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=

\displaystyle\Big\{\text{For each }i=1,2,\dots,m_{0},\text{ there exist }m\in\{1,2,\dots,M\},\text{ such that }

\displaystyle\qquad r^{\ast}_{i-1}<\underline{U}_{m}<r^{\ast}_{i}<\bar{U}_{m}<r^{\ast}_{i+1}\text{ and }\frac{r^{\ast}_{i}{-}\underline{U}_{m}}{\bar{U}_{m}{-}\underline{U}_{m}}\in(b,1-b)\Big\}.

(9)

Denote
\underline{r}=\min_{1\leq i\leq m_{0}{+}1}(r^{\ast}_{i}{-}r^{\ast}_{i-1})\leq\frac{1}{m_{0}+1}
, then we have

\displaystyle 1-P(\mathcal{M})\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m_{0}}\big\{1{-}P\big(\{r^{\ast}_{i-1}<\underline{U}_{1}<r^{\ast}_{i}<\bar{U}_{1}<r^{\ast}_{i+1}\text{ and }\frac{r^{\ast}_{i}{-}\underline{U}_{1}}{\bar{U}_{1}{-}\underline{U}_{1}}\in(b,1-b)\}\big)\big\}^{M}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle m_{0}\big\{1{-}\frac{2\underline{r}(1{-}2b)}{1-b}\big\}^{M},

which implies
P(\mathcal{M})\to 1
as
M\to\infty
. Denote
b_{m}=\lfloor(\lfloor n\bar{U}_{m}\rfloor-\lfloor n\underline{U}_{m}\rfloor{+}1)b\rfloor
, and let
T_{nm}(k)
and
V_{nm}(k)
be the numerator and squared denominator of the SS-SN statistic, as defined in Equations (
4
) and (
5
), which are calculated using data
\{X_{\lfloor n\underline{U}_{m}\rfloor},X_{\lfloor n\underline{U}_{m}\rfloor+1},\dots,X_{\lfloor n\bar{U}_{m}\rfloor}\}
for some
k\in\{\lfloor n\underline{U}_{m}\rfloor{+}b_{m},\dots,\lfloor n\bar{U}_{m}\rfloor{-}b_{m}{-}1\}
. Using pseudocode, the WBS-SN algorithm is defined in Algorithm

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose a novel change point detection method for a broad class of object-valued time series that can detect marginal distributional changes. By using Hilbert space embedding, sample splitting and self-normalization, the statistic we developed is almost tuning parameter free, has pivotal limiting null distribution and only uses the distance between two non-Euclidean data objects. For multiple change point estimation, the estimation consistency and convergence rate of change point estimators are established for the proposed WBS-SN algorithm, and the convergence rates for each change point estimator are adaptive to the heterogeneous change magnitudes. To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to show the WBS estimation consistency for a broad class of object-valued time series and in a nonparametric setting. Extensive simulations are conducted to show the accurate size and satisfactory power performance of the proposed method in comparison with existing ones. The data examples further illustrate the versatility of our proposed method in real-world applications.

To conclude, we mention several possible extensions. For multiple change point estimation, this paper only considers unknown but fixed number of change points. It would be helpful to consider the setting where the number of change points can grow with sample size. Also, it may be desirable to relax the abrupt change assumption and develop a test that captures gradual change.

such that
0\leq p+q\leq m_{0}
and
0<r^{\ast}_{1}<\cdots<r^{\ast}_{p}<b<1-b<r^{\ast}_{p+1}<\cdots<r^{\ast}_{p+q}<1
, let
\mathcal{A}_{1n}=\{{\lfloor nb\rfloor}+1\leq k\leq n-{\lfloor nb\rfloor},\,k\in\mathcal{Z}\}
, we show that no change point can be selected inside
\mathcal{A}_{1n}
.

Lemma 1
.

Suppose Assumption
2
holds, then

\displaystyle\max_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}}\frac{T_{n}(k)}{\sqrt{V_{n}(k)}}=O_{p}(1)

(A-10)

Proof.

As defined in Equation (
3
),

\displaystyle\bar{Y}_{1}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{t=1}^{nb}Y^{\prime}_{t}+\frac{nb}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{p+1}a_{i}\mu_{k^{\ast}_{i}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{t=1}^{nb}Y^{\prime}_{t}+\frac{nb}{\sqrt{n}}\big\{\mu_{k^{\ast}_{1}}{+}a_{2}\Delta^{\ast}_{1}{+}a_{3}(\Delta^{\ast}_{1}+\Delta^{\ast}_{2})+\dots+a_{p+1}(\Delta^{\ast}_{1}+\dots+\Delta^{\ast}_{p})\big\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{t=1}^{nb}Y^{\prime}_{t}+\frac{nb}{\sqrt{n}}\big\{\mu_{k^{\ast}_{1}}{+}(1{-}a_{1})\Delta^{\ast}_{1}{+}(1{-}a_{1}{-}a_{2})\Delta^{\ast}_{2}{+}\cdots{+}(1{-}a_{1}{-}\cdots{-}a_{p})\Delta^{\ast}_{p}\big\},

(A-11)

where
a_{1}=\frac{r^{\ast}_{1}}{b},a_{p+1}=\frac{b-r^{\ast}_{p}}{b}
and
a_{i}=\frac{r^{\ast}_{i}-r^{\ast}_{i-1}}{b}
for
i=2,3,\dots,p
. Similarly, let
b_{1}=\frac{r^{\ast}_{p+1}-1+b}{1-b},b_{q+1}=\frac{1-r^{\ast}_{p+q}}{1-b}
and
b_{i}=\frac{r^{\ast}_{p+i}-r^{\ast}_{p+i-1}}{1-b}
for
i=2,3,\dots,q
, we have

\displaystyle\bar{Y}_{3}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{t=n-nb+1}^{n}Y^{\prime}_{t}+\frac{nb}{\sqrt{n}}\big\{\mu_{k^{\ast}_{p+1}}{+}(1{-}b_{1})\Delta^{\ast}_{p+1}{+}(1{-}b_{1}{-}b_{2})\Delta^{\ast}_{p+2}{+}\cdots{+}(1{-}b_{1}{-}\cdots{-}b_{q})\Delta^{\ast}_{p+q}\big\}.

Let
\bar{Y}^{\prime}_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}S_{1,nb}^{Y^{\prime}},\bar{Y}^{\prime}_{3}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}S_{n-nb+1,n}^{Y^{\prime}}
and

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{n}=

\displaystyle a_{1}\Delta^{\ast}_{1}{+}(a_{1}{+}a_{2})\Delta^{\ast}_{2}{+}\cdots{+}({a_{1}{+}\cdots{+}a_{p}})\Delta^{\ast}_{p}

\displaystyle{+}(1{-}b_{1})\Delta^{\ast}_{p+1}{+}(1{-}b_{1}{-}b_{2})\Delta^{\ast}_{p+2}{+}\cdots{+}(1{-}b_{1}{-}\cdots{-}b_{q})\Delta^{\ast}_{p+q},

(A-12)

then
\bar{Y}_{1}-\bar{Y}_{3}=\bar{Y}_{1}^{\prime}-\bar{Y}_{3}^{\prime}-b\sqrt{n}\mathcal{P}_{n}
and for
k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}
, we have

\displaystyle T_{n}(k)=

\displaystyle\big\langle\bar{Y}_{1}^{\prime}{-}\bar{Y}_{3}^{\prime}{-}b\sqrt{n}\mathcal{P}_{n},\frac{1}{\sqrt{n-2nb}}\big[S_{nb+1,k}^{Y^{\prime}}-\frac{k{-}nb}{n{-}2nb}S_{nb+1,n-nb}^{Y^{\prime}}\big]\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}},

(A-13)

\displaystyle V_{n}(k)\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(n{-}2nb)^{2}}\sum_{t=nb+1}^{k}\Big\{S_{nb+1,t}{-}\frac{t{-}nb}{k{-}nb}S_{nb+1,k}\Big\}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(n{-}2nb)^{2}}\sum_{t=nb{+}1}^{k}\big\langle\bar{Y}_{1}^{\prime}{-}\bar{Y}_{3}^{\prime}{-}b\sqrt{n}\mathcal{P}_{n},S^{Y^{\prime}}_{nb+1,t}{-}\frac{t{-}nb}{k{-}nb}S^{Y^{\prime}}_{nb+1,k}\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L_{n}(k).

(A-14)

If
\mathcal{P}_{n}=0
, it is easy to see
\max_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}}\frac{T_{n}(k)}{\sqrt{V_{n}(k)}}=O_{p}(1)
. If
\mathcal{P}_{n}\neq 0
, we can write
\mathcal{P}_{n}=\kappa_{0}f_{0}^{\prime}{+}f^{n}_{0}
for some
f^{\prime}_{0},f^{n}_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
such that
\kappa_{0}\sim n^{-\zeta_{0}^{\prime}}
,
\zeta_{0}^{\prime}\in[0,1/2)
and
\|f^{n}_{0}\|_{\mathcal{H}}=o(\kappa_{0})
. since
\min_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}}L_{n}(k)=O_{p}^{s}(n^{1-2\zeta^{\prime}_{0}})
and
\max_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}}|T_{n}(k)|=O_{p}(n^{1/2-\zeta^{\prime}_{0}})
, we have
\max_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}}V_{n}(k)^{-1}=O_{p}(n^{-1+2\zeta_{0}})
and
\max_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}}\frac{T_{n}(k)}{\sqrt{V_{n}(k)}}=O_{p}(1)
.

∎

C.2
One change point setting

For nonnegative integers
q,p
such that
1\leq p+q\leq m_{0}-1
and
0<r^{\ast}_{1}<\cdots<r^{\ast}_{p}<b<r^{\ast}_{p+1}<1-b<r^{\ast}_{p+2}<\cdots<r^{\ast}_{p+q+1}<1
, let
\mathcal{A}_{1n}=\{k\geq{\lfloor nb\rfloor}+1|k\leq k^{\ast}_{p+1}-\epsilon_{(p+1)n},\,k\in\mathcal{Z}\}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2n}=\{k\leq n-{\lfloor nb\rfloor}|k\geq k^{\ast}_{p+1}+\epsilon_{(p+1)n},\,k\in\mathcal{Z}\}
, we show that no change point can be selected inside
\mathcal{A}_{1n}\cup\mathcal{A}_{2n}
.

Lemma 2
.

Suppose Assumption
2
holds, then

\displaystyle\max_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}\cup\mathcal{A}_{2n}}\frac{T_{n}(k)}{\sqrt{V_{n}(k)}}=o_{p}(n^{1/2-\delta_{p+1}})

(A-15)

Proof.

we only prove
\max_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}}\frac{T_{n}(k)}{\sqrt{V_{n}(k)}}=o_{p}(n^{1/2-\delta_{p+1}})
since the proof for
\max_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{2n}}\frac{T_{n}(k)}{\sqrt{V_{n}(k)}}=o_{p}(n^{1/2-\delta_{p+1}})
is analogous. Note that in this setting,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{n}=

\displaystyle a_{1}\Delta^{\ast}_{1}{+}(a_{1}{+}a_{2})\Delta^{\ast}_{2}{+}\cdots{+}({a_{1}{+}\cdots{+}a_{p}})\Delta^{\ast}_{p}{+}\Delta^{\ast}_{p+1}

\displaystyle{+}(1{-}b_{1})\Delta^{\ast}_{p+2}{+}(1{-}b_{1}{-}b_{2})\Delta^{\ast}_{p+3}{+}\cdots{+}(1{-}b_{1}{-}\cdots{-}b_{q})\Delta^{\ast}_{p+q+1},

(A-16)

where
\{a_{i}\}
is defined in Section
C.1
,
b_{1}=\frac{r^{\ast}_{p+2}-1+b}{1-b},b_{q+1}=\frac{1-r^{\ast}_{p+q+1}}{1-b}
and
b_{i}=\frac{r^{\ast}_{p+i+1}-r^{\ast}_{p+i}}{1-b}
for
i=2,3,\dots,q
. For
k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}
, we have

\displaystyle T_{n}(k)=

\displaystyle{-}\frac{(k{-}nb)(n{-}nb{-}k^{\ast}_{p+1})}{(n-2nb)^{3/2}}\big\langle\bar{Y}_{1}^{\prime}{-}\bar{Y}_{3}^{\prime}{-}b\sqrt{n}\mathcal{P}_{n},\Delta^{\ast}_{p+1}\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle{+}\big\langle\bar{Y}_{1}^{\prime}{-}\bar{Y}_{3}^{\prime}{-}b\sqrt{n}\mathcal{P}_{n},\frac{1}{\sqrt{n-2nb}}\big[S_{nb+1,k}^{Y^{\prime}}-\frac{k{-}nb}{n{-}2nb}S_{nb+1,n-nb}^{Y^{\prime}}\big]\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

(A-17)

\displaystyle V_{n}(k)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(n{-}2nb)^{2}}\sum_{t=k+1}^{n-nb}\Big\{S_{t,n-nb}{-}\frac{n{-}nb{-}t{+}1}{n{-}nb{-}k}S_{k+1,n-nb}\Big\}^{2}{+}\frac{1}{(n{-}2nb)^{2}}\sum_{t=nb+1}^{k}\Big\{S_{nb+1,t}{-}\frac{t{-}nb}{k{-}nb}S_{nb+1,k}\Big\}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R_{n}(k)+L_{n}(k).

(A-18)

Let
R_{1n}(k)=\frac{1}{(n{-}2nb)^{2}}\sum_{t=k^{\ast}_{p+1}{+}1}^{n{-}nb}\Big\{S_{t,n-nb}{-}\frac{n{-}nb{-}t{+}1}{n{-}nb{-}k}S_{k+1,n-nb}\Big\}^{2}
, then

\displaystyle R_{1n}(k)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(n{-}2nb)^{2}}\sum_{t=k^{\ast}_{p+1}{+}1}^{n{-}nb}\Big\{\big\langle\bar{Y}_{1}^{\prime}{-}\bar{Y}_{3}^{\prime}{-}b\sqrt{n}\mathcal{P}_{n},S^{Y^{\prime}}_{t,n-nb}{-}\frac{n{-}nb{-}t{+}1}{n{-}nb{-}k}S^{Y^{\prime}}_{k+1,n-nb}\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad{+}\frac{(n{-}nb{-}t{+}1)(k^{\ast}_{p+1}{-}k)}{n{-}nb{-}k}\big\langle\bar{Y}^{\prime}_{1}{-}\bar{Y}^{\prime}_{3}{-}b\sqrt{n}\mathcal{P}_{n},\Delta^{\ast}_{p+1}\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\Big\}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(n{-}2nb)^{2}}\sum_{t=k^{\ast}_{p+1}{+}1}^{n{-}nb}\Big\{R_{11}(k,t){+}R_{12}(k,t)\Big\}^{2},

(A-19)

\displaystyle L_{n}(k)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(n{-}2nb)^{2}}\sum_{t=nb{+}1}^{k}\big\langle\bar{Y}_{1}^{\prime}{-}\bar{Y}_{3}^{\prime}{-}b\sqrt{n}\mathcal{P}_{n},S^{Y^{\prime}}_{nb+1,t}{-}\frac{t{-}nb}{k{-}nb}S^{Y^{\prime}}_{nb+1,k}\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}

and
R_{n}(k)\geq R_{1n}(k)
. According to the definition of
\mathcal{P}_{n}
in Equation (
A-16
) and Assumption
2
, we know
\mathcal{P}_{n}=\theta_{p+1}f_{p+1}+\kappa_{p+1}f^{\prime}_{p+1}{+}f^{n}_{p+1}
for some
f^{\prime}_{p+1},f^{n}_{p+1}\in\{f_{p+1}\}^{\perp}
such that
\|f^{n}_{p+1}\|_{\mathcal{H}}=o(\kappa_{p+1})
. For
\theta_{p+1}
, it is either
\theta_{p+1}=0
or
\theta_{p+1}\sim n^{-\zeta_{p+1}}
for some
\zeta_{p+1}\in[0,1/2)
. Similarly
\kappa_{p+1}=0
(in this case we have
\|f^{n}_{p+1}\|_{\mathcal{H}}=0
) or
\kappa_{p+1}\sim n^{-\zeta_{p+1}^{\prime}}
for some
\zeta_{p+1}^{\prime}\in[0,1/2)
. We consider six different scenarios.

(1)
\theta_{p+1}=\kappa_{p+1}=0
.

From Equation (
A-17
),
\max_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}}|T_{n}(k)|=O_{p}(n^{1/2-\delta_{p+1}})
. Since

\displaystyle\max_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}}\frac{1}{(n{-}2nb)^{2}}\sum_{t=k^{\ast}_{p+1}{+}1}^{n{-}nb}R_{11}(k,t)^{2}

(A-20)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}}\frac{1}{(n{-}2nb)^{2}}\sum_{t=k^{\ast}_{p+1}{+}1}^{n{-}nb}\big\langle\bar{Y}_{1}^{\prime}{-}\bar{Y}_{3}^{\prime},S^{Y^{\prime}}_{t,n-nb}{-}\frac{n{-}nb{-}t{+}1}{n{-}nb{-}k}S^{Y^{\prime}}_{k+1,n-nb}\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=O_{p}(1)

and

\displaystyle\min_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}}\frac{1}{(n{-}2nb)^{2}}\sum_{t=k^{\ast}_{p+1}{+}1}^{n{-}nb}R_{12}(k,t)^{2}=

\displaystyle\min_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}}\frac{n^{-2\delta_{p+1}}(k^{\ast}_{p+1}{-}k)^{2}}{(n{-}2nb)^{2}(n{-}nb{-}k)^{2}}\langle\bar{Y}^{\prime}_{1}{-}\bar{Y}^{\prime}_{3},f_{p+1}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\sum_{t=k^{\ast}_{p+1}{+}1}^{n{-}nb}(n{-}nb{-}t{+}1)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle Cn^{-1}(\epsilon_{(p+1)n}n^{-\delta_{p+1}})^{2}\langle\bar{Y}^{\prime}_{1}{-}\bar{Y}^{\prime}_{3},f_{p+1}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\to}}\infty,

(A-21)

we have
\max_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}}V_{n}(k)^{-1}=o_{p}(1)
, which implies
\max_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}}\frac{T_{n}(k)}{\sqrt{V_{n}(k)}}=o_{p}(n^{1/2-\delta_{p+1}})
.

(2)
\theta_{p+1}=0
,
\kappa_{p+1}\sim n^{-\zeta_{p+1}^{\prime}}
and
\zeta_{p+1}^{\prime}<\delta_{p+1}
.

From Equation (
A-17
),
\max_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}}|T_{n}(k)|=O_{p}(n^{1/2-\zeta_{p+1}^{\prime}})
. Since

\displaystyle\min_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}}\frac{1}{(n{-}2nb)^{2}}\sum_{t=k^{\ast}_{p+1}{+}1}^{n{-}nb}R_{11}(k,t)^{2}

(A-22)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}}\frac{1}{(n{-}2nb)^{2}}\sum_{t=k^{\ast}_{p+1}{+}1}^{n{-}nb}\big\langle\bar{Y}_{1}^{\prime}{-}\bar{Y}_{3}^{\prime}{-}b\sqrt{n}\mathcal{P}_{n},S^{Y^{\prime}}_{t,n-nb}{-}\frac{n{-}nb{-}t{+}1}{n{-}nb{-}k}S^{Y^{\prime}}_{k+1,n-nb}\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O_{p}^{s}(n^{1-2\zeta_{p+1}^{\prime}})

(A-23)

and

\displaystyle\max_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}}\frac{1}{(n{-}2nb)^{2}}\sum_{t=k^{\ast}_{p+1}{+}1}^{n{-}nb}R_{12}(k,t)^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\max_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}}\frac{Cn^{2-2\delta_{p+1}}}{(n{-}2nb)^{2}(n{-}nb{-}k)^{2}}\langle\bar{Y}^{\prime}_{1}{-}\bar{Y}^{\prime}_{3},f_{p+1}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\sum_{t=k^{\ast}_{p+1}{+}1}^{n{-}nb}(n{-}nb{-}t{+}1)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Cn^{1-2\delta_{p+1}}\langle\bar{Y}^{\prime}_{1}{-}\bar{Y}^{\prime}_{3},f_{p+1}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}^{2},

(A-24)

we have
\max_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}}V_{n}(k)^{-1}=O_{p}(n^{-1+2\zeta_{p+1}^{\prime}})
, which implies
\max_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}}\frac{T_{n}(k)}{\sqrt{V_{n}(k)}}=O_{p}(1)
.

(3)
\theta_{p+1}=0
,
\kappa_{p+1}\sim n^{-\zeta_{p+1}^{\prime}}
and
\zeta_{p+1}^{\prime}>\delta_{p+1}
.

In this case
\max_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}}|T_{n}(k)|=O_{p}(n^{1/2-\delta_{p+1}})
. Let
\mathcal{A}_{1n}^{1}=\mathcal{A}_{1n}\cap\{k\leq n\frac{b+r^{\ast}_{p+1}}{2}\}
and
\mathcal{A}_{1n}^{2}=\mathcal{A}_{1n}\cap\{k>n\frac{b+r^{\ast}_{p+1}}{2}\}
, then we have

\displaystyle\min_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}^{1}}R_{n}(k)\geq

\displaystyle\min_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}^{1}}\frac{1}{(n{-}2nb)^{2}}\sum_{t=k^{\ast}_{p+1}{+}1}^{n{-}nb}R_{12}(k,t)^{2}=O_{p}^{s}(n^{1-2\delta_{p+1}})

(A-25)

and
\min_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}^{2}}V_{n}(k)\geq\min_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}^{2}}L_{n}(k)=O_{p}^{s}(n^{1-2\zeta_{p+1}^{\prime}})
, which implies
\max_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}}V_{n}(k)^{-1}=o_{p}(1)
and
\max_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}}\frac{T_{n}(k)}{\sqrt{V_{n}(k)}}=o_{p}(n^{1/2-\delta_{p+1}})
.

(4)
\theta_{p+1}=0
,
\kappa_{p+1}\sim n^{-\zeta_{p+1}^{\prime}}
and
\zeta_{p+1}^{\prime}=\delta_{p+1}
.

In this case
\max_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}}|T_{n}(k)|=O_{p}(n^{1/2-\delta_{p+1}})
and

\displaystyle\min_{nb+1\leq k\leq k^{\ast}_{p+1}}\frac{n^{-1+2\delta_{p+1}}}{(n{-}2nb)^{2}}\sum_{t=k^{\ast}_{p+1}+1}^{n-nb}\big\{R_{11}(k,t){+}R_{12}(k,t)\big\}^{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\to}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1{-}2b)^{2}}\inf_{r\in[b,r^{\ast}_{p+1}]}\int_{r^{\ast}_{p+1}}^{1-b}\Big\{{-}\big\langle Cbf_{p+1}^{\prime},B_{Q}(1{-}b){-}B_{Q}(s){-}\frac{1{-}b{-}s}{1{-}b{-}r}\big[B_{Q}(1{-}b){-}B_{Q}(r)\big]\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt{+}\big\langle B_{Q}(b){-}B_{Q}(1){+}B_{Q}(1{-}b),\frac{(1{-}b{-}r)(r^{\ast}_{p+1}{-}r)}{1-b-r}f_{p+2}\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\Big\}^{2}ds,

which implies
\max_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}}V_{n}(k)^{-1}=o_{p}(n^{-1+2\delta_{p+1}})
and
\max_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}}\frac{T_{n}(k)}{\sqrt{V_{n}(k)}}=O_{p}(1)
.

(5)
\theta_{p+1}\sim n^{-\zeta_{p+1}}
,
\kappa_{p+1}=0
or
\kappa_{p+1}\sim n^{-\zeta_{p+1}^{\prime}}
with
\zeta_{p+1}^{\prime}\geq\zeta_{p+1}
.

From Equation (
A-17
),
\max_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}}|T_{n}(k)|=O_{p}(n^{1-\zeta_{p+1}-\delta_{p+1}})
. Similar to the proof in part (1), we have

\displaystyle\max_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}}\frac{1}{(n{-}2nb)^{2}}\sum_{t=k^{\ast}_{p+1}{+}1}^{n{-}nb}R_{11}(k,t)^{2}=

\displaystyle O_{p}(n^{1-2\zeta_{p+1}})

\displaystyle\min_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}}\frac{1}{(n{-}2nb)^{2}}\sum_{t=k^{\ast}_{p+1}{+}1}^{n{-}nb}R_{12}(k,t)^{2}\geq

\displaystyle Cn^{-1}(\epsilon_{(p+1)n}n^{-\delta_{p+1}})^{2}n^{1-2\zeta_{p+1}}(1{+}o_{p}(1)),

(A-26)

which implies
\max_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}}V_{n}(k)^{-1}=o_{p}(n^{-1+2\zeta_{p+1}})
and
\max_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}}\frac{T_{n}(k)}{\sqrt{V_{n}(k)}}=o_{p}(n^{1/2-\delta_{p+1}})
.

(6)
\theta_{p+1}\sim n^{-\zeta_{p+1}}
and
\kappa_{p+1}\sim n^{-\zeta_{p+1}^{\prime}}
with
\zeta_{p+1}^{\prime}<\zeta_{p+1}
.

From Equation (
A-17
),
\max_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}}|T_{n}(k)|=O_{p}(n^{1-\zeta_{p+1}-\delta_{p+1}}+n^{1/2-\zeta_{p+1}^{\prime}})
. Similar to the proof in part (4), we have

\displaystyle\min_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}^{1}}\frac{1}{(n{-}2nb)^{2}}\sum_{t=k^{\ast}_{p+1}{+}1}^{n{-}nb}\Big\{R_{11}(k,t){+}R_{12}(k,t)\Big\}^{2}=O_{p}^{s}(n^{2-2\zeta_{p+1}-2\delta_{p+1}}+n^{1-2\zeta_{p+1}^{\prime}}),

and

\max_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}}\frac{T_{n}(k)}{\sqrt{V_{n}(k)}}=O_{p}(1)
. If

\displaystyle{+}(1{-}b_{1})\Delta^{\ast}_{p+3}{+}(1{-}b_{1}{-}b_{2})\Delta^{\ast}_{p+4}{+}\cdots{+}(1{-}b_{1}{-}\cdots{-}b_{q})\Delta^{\ast}_{p+q+2},

(A-28)

where

C.1

Lemma 3
.

Suppose Assumption
2
holds, then

\displaystyle\max_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}\cup\mathcal{A}_{2n}\cup\mathcal{A}_{3n}}\frac{T_{n}(k)}{\sqrt{V_{n}(k)}}=o_{p}(n^{1/2-\min\{\delta_{p+1},\delta_{p+2}\}})

(A-29)

C.3.1
No change point can be selected on
\mathcal{A}_{1n}

For
k\in\mathcal{A}_{1n}
,
