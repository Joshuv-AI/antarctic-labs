Astrocytic mGluR5 Regulation of Synaptic Transmission is Activity‐Dependent in Adult Rats
Authors: Sarah Mountadem, Muna L Hilal, Dylan Pommier, Dorian Arnouil, Valentin Clément Langlais, Vincent Simon, Aurélie Amadio, Marie Miegebielle, Sébastien Marais, Charlène Joséphine, Astrid Cannich, Marjorie Varilh, Julien Bourel, Daniela Cota, Giovanni Marsicano, Alexis‐Pierre Bemelmans, Philippe Ciofi, Stéphane Henri Richard Oliet, Aude Panatier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTData accumulated over the last two decades have demonstrated that astrocytes play key roles in the regulation of synaptic transmission and plasticity. This is due, among other mechanisms, to their capability to detect and regulate synaptic transmission by expressing receptors and releasing gliotransmitters, respectively. Importantly, in juvenile rats, astrocytes are able to detect glutamate release at the level of individual synapses through mGluR5 and consequently up‐regulate excitatory synaptic transmission efficacy through the release of purines. Whether this upregulation is still present in the adult brain is an open question. Using immunohistochemistry and RNAscope on fixed tissue, as well as electrophysiological recordings on acute hippocampal brain slices of adult male rats, we demonstrated that this regulatory pathway also prevails in adult rats. Most surprisingly, such facilitation of glutamate release that is readily observed when a small number of synapses are activated was completely abolished under conditions where a large number of inputs were stimulated. These findings thus suggest that astrocytes integrate the incoming afferent information and adapt their responses depending on the network activity.Keywords: astrocyte, basal synaptic transmission, gliotransmission, mGluR5

Introduction

While synaptic transmission depends on the communication between neuronal elements, accumulating data have shown that glial cells, and in particular astrocytes, play active roles as regulators at central synapses. For a long time, it was assumed that astrocytes were active partners of neurons only under conditions of intense or sustained network activity (Araque et al. 2014; Fellin et al. 2004; Henneberger et al. 2010; Panatier et al. 2006; Pascual et al. 2005; Pasti et al. 1997; Serrano et al. 2006). This view has changed as it has been demonstrated that fast calcium events could be induced locally, in vitro and in vivo, at the level of astrocytic processes (Arizono et al. 2020; Bindocci et al. 2017; Di Castro et al. 2011; Panatier et al. 2011; Stobart et al. 2018).

At hippocampal CA3‐CA1 synapses of 3‐week‐old rats, astrocytes are recruited at individual synapses during basal synaptic activity (Panatier et al. 2011). We have shown that single fiber activation through a single pulse of stimulation could trigger local Ca2+ events in astrocytic compartments. These events, mediated by metabotropic glutamate receptors subtype 5 (mGluR5) activation, cause the release of purines which subsequently upregulate the efficacy of glutamatergic transmission through presynaptic adenosine A2A receptors (A2ARs) activation (Panatier et al. 2011).

The implication of mGluR5 in the detection of the synaptic signal by astrocytes has been described extensively in juvenile rodents (D'Ascenzo et al. 2007; Honsek et al. 2010; Latour et al. 2001; Panatier et al. 2011; Pasti et al. 1997; Porter and McCarthy 1995; Porter and McCarthy 1996; Sun et al. 2014; Wang et al. 2006). Like other astrocytic proteins such as GFAP and vimentin, the expression of mGluR5 is developmentally regulated and is drastically decreased in 4‐week‐old rodents (Cai et al. 2000; Dias et al. 2026; Panatier and Robitaille 2016; Sun et al. 2013). Nevertheless, it has been shown that whisker stimulation in adult mice induced Ca2+ events in astrocytes that were sensitive to MPEP, an antagonist of mGluR5 (Wang et al. 2006). Furthermore, both mGluR5 and its scaffold protein Homer1b/c were shown to be expressed in 30‐day‐old mice (Buscemi et al. 2017). In addition, another group showed that mGluR5 is present in astrocytic processes of adult rats (Lavialle et al. 2011). Altogether, these data indicate that mGluR5 is expressed and functional in astrocytes of adult rodents (Dias et al. 2026; Panatier and Robitaille 2016).

However, whether astrocytes upregulate basal synaptic transmission in adult rats through the same astrocytic mGluR5 presynaptic A2AR pathway described in juvenile rats remains unknown. Here we show that adult astrocytes regulate basal excitatory synaptic transmission at CA3‐CA1 synapses at the level of individual synapses. Unexpectedly, performing input/output experiments revealed that such regulation is activity‐dependent. Indeed, this mechanism is no longer present when numerous neighboring glutamatergic synapses are co‐activated by a single pulse of stimulation. These results suggest that astrocytes have the ability to adapt their responses as a function of the prevailing afferent input activity.

Results

Astrocytes of Adult Rats Express mGluR5

We first aimed at assessing whether astrocytes in the stratum radiatum of the CA1 area of adult rats express mGluR5. To this end, immunohistochemical experiments were performed on fixed sections from 11‐ and 15‐week‐old rats (Figures 1 and S1A–G). Astrocytes were identified using a double immunostaining for GFAP and S100β. Using this co‐labelling, as reported before (Panatier et al. 2011), astrocytes were characterized by a small soma from which emerged GFAP/S100β‐positive principal processes and numerous S100β‐positive spongiform processes (Figure S1A–C). As expected, mGluR5 labeling was dense, punctate and extensively expressed in the stratum radiatum of the CA1 area (Figures 1A and S1A–G; Lujan et al. 1996). Of the total GFAP signal, 34.92% ± 0.54% overlapped with the mGluR5 signal, representing 7.99% ± 0.41% of the total mGluR5 signal (n = 21 tissue sections; N = 3; Figure 1B–D). Likewise, of the total S100β signal, 44.79% ± 0.91% overlapped with the mGluR5 signal, representing 28.23% ± 1.22% of the total mGluR5 signal (n = 21 tissue sections; N = 3; Figure 1B–D). In tissue sections in which the anti‐mGluR5 antibody was omitted from an otherwise complete procedure, only extremely low residual background signal was detected (n = 42 tissue sections; N = 3; Figure 1B–D). Importantly, RNAscope experiments revealed that 80.48% ± 4.98% of S100β‐positive astrocytic cell nuclei were positive for mGluR5 mRNA (n = 584 cells, N = 3; Figure 1E,F). Additional confocal and in situ hybridization experiments (Figure S1) confirmed the expression of mGluR5 in astrocytes.

Astrocytic mGluR5—Presynaptic A2AR Pathway Regulates Glutamate Release in Adulthood

We decided to investigate whether the mGluR5‐mediated regulation of transmission described at CA3‐CA1 synapses in young animals was also prevailing in adults. To this end, patch‐clamp electrophysiological experiments of pyramidal neurons in whole‐cell configuration were performed and miniature excitatory post‐synaptic currents (mEPSCs) were recorded in adult rats (aged 11–15 weeks; Figure 2). After a recording period of 15 min, MPEP (25 μM), an antagonist of mGluR5, was applied in the perfusion bath. Application of MPEP induced an increase of the inter‐event interval (2.29 ± 0.15 s vs. 3.35 ± 0.20 s in MPEP, n = 10, N = 7; p < 0.0001, Paired t‐test) and thus a decrease of mEPSCs frequency. Importantly, mEPSCs amplitude was not changed (−19.58 ± 1.25 pA vs. −20.04 ± 0.94 pA in MPEP; p = 0.58; Figure 2A,B), in agreement with a presynaptic site of action. Since in juvenile rats the mGluR5‐mediating facilitation involved the astrocytic release of purines and the subsequent activation of adenosine A2ARs, we then tested the effect of an antagonist of these receptors (SCH 58261, 100 nM). Similarly, to MPEP, the application of SCH 58261 induced an increase of the inter‐event interval (2.50 ± 0.48 s vs. 3.59 ± 0.55 s in SCH 58261, n = 10, N = 7; p = 0.003), without modifying mEPSCs amplitude (−19.60 ± 0.90 pA vs. −18.69 ± 0.60 pA in SCH 58261; p = 0.19; Figure 2C,D). An intriguing question is whether astrocytes release ATP or adenosine. If astrocytes release ATP, preventing ATP degradation by adding the ectonucleotidase inhibitor (ARL 67156, 50 μM) in the perfusion bath, should prevent the production of adenosine extracellularly and therefore induce a similar effect, as the one induced by SCH 58261. As hypothesized, bath application of ARL 67156 induced an increase of mEPSCs inter‐event interval (2.94 ± 0.27 s vs. 4.01 ± 0.32 s in ARL 67156, n = 9, N = 6; p = 0.0003), without altering their amplitude (−17.51 ± 0.71 pA vs. −17.56 ± 0.87 pA in ARL 67156; p = 0.93; Figure 2E,F). To confirm that both receptors, mGluR5 and A2AR, were part of the same pathway, an occlusion experiment was performed during which the impact of SCH 58261 was tested in the presence of MPEP. Under these conditions, bath‐application of SCH 58261 no longer had an effect on mEPSCs inter‐event interval (3.31 ± 0.50 s in MPEP vs. 3.53 ± 0.51 s in MPEP + SCH 58261, n = 10, N = 8; p = 0.13) or their amplitude (−19.84 ± 0.71 pA in MPEP vs. −19.98 ± 0.64 pA in MPEP + SCH 58261; p = 0.77; Figure 2G,H).

Finally, to confirm that this pathway was dependent on astrocytes (Panatier et al. 2011), whole‐cell patch‐clamp electrophysiological recordings of a CA1 pyramidal neuron and an astrocyte in the stratum radiatum were performed simultaneously (Figure 3). To impair Ca2+ activity of astrocytes, an internal solution containing BAPTA (15 mM) was introduced in the patched‐astrocyte to chelate intracellular Ca2+ into the astrocytic network and consequently block Ca2+‐dependent gliotransmission in the patched astrocyte and connected astrocytes in the syncytium. Importantly, 15 min were allowed for intracellular diffusion before recording neuronal activity. Under these conditions, MPEP had no impact on mEPSCs activity (inter‐event interval: 2.88 ± 0.26 s vs. 3.82 ± 0.62 s in MPEP; p = 0.14; amplitude: −18.58 ± 0.83 pA vs. −17.63 ± 0.79 pA, n = 8, N = 8; p = 0.19; Figure 3C,D). Importantly, when a control internal solution was used to perfuse individual astrocytes, MPEP still induced an increase of mEPSCs inter‐event interval (3.87 ± 0.52 s vs. 6.91 ± 1.30 s in MPEP, n = 9, N = 8; p = 0.004) without changing the amplitude (−18.68 ± 0.70 pA vs. −17.52 ± 0.59 pA; p = 0.09; Figure 3A,B). Taken together, this set of experiments establishes the role of astrocytes in the mGluR5‐mediated upregulation of transmission at CA3‐CA1 synapses in adult rats.

Astrocytic mGluR5—Presynaptic A2AR Regulation Only Occurs When a Small Number of Synapses Are Activated

Our data on mEPSCs indicate that astrocytes up‐regulate glutamate release through the mGluR5‐A2A pathway. We next tested whether we could monitor this regulation on glutamatergic responses evoked by stimulating the Schaffer's collaterals. To this end, the afferent fiber volley and its associated field excitatory post‐synaptic potentials (fEPSPs) were recorded in the hippocampal CA1 stratum radiatum. The fiber volley amplitude reflects the number of stimulated presynaptic fibers, while fEPSP slope reflects the activity of synchronized neighboring synapses. The amplitude of stimulation was increased during the experiment, in order to progressively recruit an increased number of synapses, from a small number (fiber volley amplitude from −0.01 to −0.049 mV; < −0.05 mV) to a higher one (from −0.05 to −0.08 mV; > −0.05 mV). To monitor the presynaptic regulation, the facilitation of glutamate release elicited by 2 synaptic stimulations evoked at 100 ms interval was measured. The associated paired pulse facilitation ratio (PPR) was calculated and expressed as the ratio of the slope of the second fEPSP over the first one. Interestingly, MPEP (25 μM) appears to have a differential effect according to the strength of the stimulation. Indeed, the mGluR5 antagonist only induced a decrease of the first fEPSP slope when the amplitude of the fiber volley was smaller than −0.05 mV (−0.02 ± 0.002 mV/ms vs. −0.01 ± 0.001 mV/ms in MPEP, n = 22, N = 11; p = 0.0049 for fv = −0.01 mV; and −0.06 ± 0.004 mV/ms vs. −0.05 ± 0.003 mV/ms, n = 20, N = 10; p = 0.0013 for fv = −0.03, Figure 4A,B). Accordingly, the associated PPR was significantly increased (1.35 ± 0.13 vs. 2.98 ± 0.52 in MPEP for fv = −0.01 mV; p < 0.0001; Figure 4A,C). Intriguingly, when the fiber volley was > −0.05 mV, MPEP was no longer affecting fEPSPs slope (−0.10 ± 0.01 mV/ms vs. −0.10 ± 0.01 mV/ms in MPEP, n = 19, N = 10; p > 0.999 for fv = −0.05 mV; and −0.16 ± 0.01 mV/ms vs. −0.17 ± 0.01 mV/ms, n = 17, N = 9; p = 0.9998 for fv = −0.07), nor the PPR (1.71 ± 0.08 vs. 1.59 ± 0.06 in MPEP; p > 0.989 for fv = −0.05 mV; and 1.68 ± 0.09 vs. 1.65 ± 0.06; p > 0.999 for fv = −0.07).

In agreement with these results, the application of SCH 58261 (100 nM), the adenosine A2AR antagonist, induced a decrease of the first fEPSP slope (0.09 ± 0.02 vs. 0.07 ± 0.01 in SCH 58261; n = 20, N = 10, p = 0.0009) and an increase of the PPR (1.51 ± 0.09 vs. 1.93 ± 0.11 in SCH 58261; p = 0.0009; Figure 5A,B) only when fiber volley was < −0.05 mV (from −0.01 to −0.049 mV). For fiber volleys > −0.05 mV, while SCH 58261 induced an increase of the first fEPSP slope (0.21 ± 0.03 vs. 0.23 ± 0.02 in SCH 58261; n = 19, N = 10; Wilcoxon test; p = 0.0024), it did not have an effect on the PPR (1.58 ± 0.02 vs. 1.55 ± 0.03 in SCH 58261; n = 19, N = 10; p > 0.999; Figure 5A,C), suggesting an absence of a presynaptic effect.

These data thus indicate that the astrocytic mGluR5‐presynaptic A2AR up‐regulation of glutamatergic transmission only prevails when a small number of synapses are activated. Under conditions where a large number of synapses are stimulated this regulation is no longer observed. We then tested whether presynaptic A2ARs were still functional when a higher number of synapses are recruited. To address this point, we used a specific agonist of A2ARs (CGS 21680, 30 nM) and analyzed its effect on the slope of fEPSPs evoked at different stimulus strengths. Due to the endogenous activation of presynaptic A2ARs, while CGS 21680 bath application induced an increase of the first fEPSPs slope (−0.24 ± 0.02 mV/ms vs. −0.30 ± 0.03 mV/ms in CGS 21680, n = 15, N = 8; p = 0.0026) the PPR was not affected (1.62 ± 0.08 mV/ms vs. 1.54 ± 0.04 mV/ms in CGS 21680; p = 0.23, for a fv amplitude < −0.05 mV, Figure D,E). Interestingly, CGS 21680 had a stronger effect when the fiber volley was > −0.05 mV on the first slope (−0.42 ± 0.03 mV/ms vs. −0.53 ± 0.02 mV/ms in CGS 21680, n = 16, N = 9; p < 0.0001), and the PPR was significantly decreased (1.57 ± 0.04 mV/ms vs. 1.42 ± 0.02 mV/ms in CGS 21680; p < 0.0001; Figure 5D,F). This is in agreement with a stronger prevailing activation of A2ARs under conditions where a small number of synapses are activated. Most importantly, these data demonstrate that A2ARs are functional independently of the number of synapses stimulated (Figure 5F).

To confirm that this mGluR5‐mediated upregulation of transmission was dependent on astrocytes, we repeated the experiments under conditions where astrocyte Ca2+ activity was inhibited by a Ca2+‐clamp solution (Henneberger et al. 2010; Langlais et al. 2025). This time, fEPSPs were recorded through the patch pipette of the recorded astrocyte (afEPSPs) allowing to simultaneously manipulate the intracellular content of an astrocyte and record at the same time fEPSPs evoked at the level of associated synapses (Henneberger and Rusakov 2012; Langlais et al. 2025; Sherwood et al. 2017). Two internal solutions were used, either a control solution, or a Ca2+‐clamp solution. While using control internal solution, MPEP could still induce a decrease of the first fEPSP slope (−0.05 ± 0.01 mV/ms vs. −0.03 ± 0.01 mV/ms in MPEP, n = 8, N = 7; p = 0.0078) and an increase of the PPR when the fiber volley was < −0.05 mV (1.71 ± 0.11 vs. 2.22 ± 0.14 in MPEP; p = 0.0012; Figure 6A,B). Importantly, it was not the case when the Ca2+‐clamp solution was introduced in the recorded astrocyte neither on fEPSPs slope (−0.02 ± 0.003 mV/ms vs. −0.02 ± 0.003 mV/ms in MPEP, n = 8, N = 8; p = 0.24) nor the PPR (2.34 ± 0.22 vs. 2.07 ± 0.16 in MPEP; p = 0.44; Figure 6D,E), thereby confirming the role of astrocyte in this phenomenon. When the fiber volley was > −0.05 mV, neither the first afEPSP, nor the PPR were affected using control internal solution (−0.17 ± 0.03 mV/ms vs. 0.17 ± 0.03 mV/ms in MPEP; n = 10, N = 8; p = 0.69 for the first slope; 1.70 ± 0.09 vs. 1.73 ± 0.17; p = 0.92 for the PPR; Figure 6A,C) or the Ca2 + −clamp solution (−0.06 ± 0.006 mV/ms vs. 0.07 ± 0.004 mV/ms in MPEP; n = 10, N = 9; p = 0.21 for the slope; 1.62 ± 0.11 vs. 1.68 ± 0.09; p = 0.66 for the PPR; Figure 6D,F).

A Mechanism That Depends on Astrocytic mGluR5‐Homer Interactions

It has been reported that the interaction of astrocytic mGluR5 with the scaffold protein Homer1b/c plays a key role in astrocytic mGluR5 function (Buscemi et al. 2017). Therefore, we investigated whether mGluR5‐Homer1b/c interaction was necessary for the mGluR5‐A2AR up regulation in hippocampal adult rats. To this end we took advantage of a C‐tail decoy peptide (PPSPFR; mGluR5pep) that was developed to disrupt mGluR5‐Homer1b/c interaction by mimicking the carboxy‐terminal tail of mGluR5 containing the Homer ligand domain (Mao et al. 2005; O'Riordan et al. 2014; Ronesi et al. 2012; Ronesi and Huber 2008; Tronson et al. 2010). Importantly, the same group developed a control peptide with a dual point mutation in the binding motif rendering it incapable of binding Homer (PLSPRR) (Mao et al. 2005). These peptides were previously fused to a Tat sequence allowing them to be membrane permeable and applied directly in the perfusion bath. As we wanted to assess the function of mGluR5‐Homer1b/c interaction in astrocytes, we took advantage of a viral approach (Figure S2). To this end, a TagRFP sequence was fused to the sequence of the two peptides. Then the genes of interest were inserted in an AAV2 genome under the control of the astrocyte‐selective gfaABC1D promoter, and encapsidated in AAV 9 particles (Ceyzeriat et al. 2018; Lee et al. 2008) (Figure S2). Therefore, two viruses were produced, the AAV9‐TagRFP‐mGluR5pep to express the mGluR5 C‐tail decoy peptide (mGluR5pep) and the AAV9‐TagRFP‐Mutpep to express the control peptide (Mutpep; Figures 7 and 8).

Using these tools, we investigated the impact of mGluR5‐Homer1/c interaction at the level of the single synapse, similarly to our previous work on the single synapse regulation (Panatier et al. 2011). We studied the effect of MPEP on minimal synaptic stimulation, allowing us to stimulate a putative single presynaptic fiber (Panatier et al. 2011). Any modification of the failure rate reflects a presynaptic modification, while a modification of the potency, corresponding to the amplitude of evoked EPSCs excluding failures, reflects a post‐synaptic modification. In acute slices coming from animals injected with the control AAV9‐TagRFP‐Mutpep virus, the application of MPEP induced an increase of the failure rate (from 38.63% ± 5.63% to 64.00% ± 9.07% in MPEP, n = 8, N = 8; p = 0.01; Figure 7A,C) with no modification of the potency (from −18.27 ± 2.73 pA to −12.70 ± 2.03 pA in MPEP; p = 0.19; Figure 7A,D). Importantly, the expression of the mGluR5 C‐tail decoy peptide (mGluR5pep) in astrocytes (AAV9‐TagRFP‐mGluR5pep injected animals) occluded the effect of MPEP as its application had no impact on the failure rate (from 51.68% ± 4.41% to 45.05% ± 8.66% in MPEP, n = 10, N = 8; p = 0.38; Figure 7B,C), nor the potency (from −17.52 ± 1.15 pA to −16.11 ± 2.13 pA; p = 0.56; Figure 7B,D).

Finally, we tested the impact of mGluR5pep on evoked EPSCs induced by a higher amplitude of stimulation. Notably, when a higher number of presynaptic fibers contacting the recording neuron were stimulated, MPEP had no impact neither on the amplitude of the first EPSC (from −103.60 ± 25.68 pA to −112.80 ± 27.46 pA for Mutpep, n = 13, N = 11; p = 0.75 and from −150.60 ± 30.67 pA to −149.10 ± 29.02 pA for mGluR5pep, n = 12, N = 10; p = 0.39; Figure 8A,B) nor on the PPR (from 1.99 ± 0.16 to 1.99 ± 0.16 for Mutpep, p = 0.99; and from 2.28 ± 0.18 to 2.32 ± 0.18 for mGluR5pep, p = 0.74; Figure 8A,C).

Discussion

In this study, we have shown that astrocytes of adult rats up‐regulate glutamate release at individual synapses through a pathway involving astrocytic mGluR5 and presynaptic A2ARs. Importantly, our data indicate for the first time that astrocytes in this hippocampal region are able to integrate synaptic activity and adapt their response as a function of the number of activated synapses: when few excitatory synapses are recruited, glutamate release is upregulated through this astrocytic‐dependent process, whereas when a large number of synapses are activated simultaneously, this regulation is no longer present.

In Adulthood Astrocytes Express mGluR5

Accumulation of data in the literature indicates that astrocytes in adult rodents express functional mGluR5. Lavialle and collaborators have reported that in adult rats, mGluR5 is expressed at the level of astrocytic processes, in close apposition with synapses (Lavialle et al. 2011). While the expression of this receptor decreases during development (Sun et al. 2013), it has been shown that astrocytes, similarly to neurons, are able to synthesize proteins at the level of their processes (Sakers et al. 2017). Ribosomes and mRNA are present in astrocytic processes (Sakers et al. 2017). Our immunohistochemical, RNAscope and FISH data confirm that mGluR5 is expressed in astrocytes and at the level of astrocytic processes. Importantly, as reported in juvenile rats (Panatier et al. 2011), it was possible to visualize mGluR5 in colocalization with GFAP in major processes of astrocytes as well as in S100β‐positive processes that do not express GFAP.

Astrocytes Facilitate Glutamatergic Transmission Only When a Small Number of Synapses Are Activated

To investigate the role of astrocytes on excitatory transmission in the hippocampus of juvenile rats, a protocol of minimal stimulation was used to activate a single or a few fibers (Panatier et al. 2011). In the present study, as in the work done by Di Castro and collaborators (Di Castro et al. 2011), we decided to first study miniature post‐synaptic glutamatergic activity. Like in juvenile rats, quantal release of glutamate at adult CA3‐CA1 synapses was found to be up‐regulated by astrocytes through the mGluR5‐A2AR pathway. It is important to notice that in adult mice it has been shown that astrocytic mGluR5 are also involved in the upregulation of glutamate release (Chipman et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2022).

Interestingly, when we extended this study to evoked‐EPSCs, we noticed that this mGluR5‐A2AR up‐regulation of excitatory transmission only occurred when a small number of synapses were stimulated. When stimulation strength was increased above a given threshold (−0.05 mV), co‐activating a significant number of synapses, we could no longer observe such up‐regulation. Moreover, we found that such a facilitation of excitatory transmission observed at low stimulus intensity allows to maintain the probability of glutamate release at a level equivalent to that observed when a higher number of synapses was stimulated. This astrocytic mechanism could potentially ensure a more reliable transmission at individual synapses. Finally, we show that this mGluR5‐dependent regulation process depends on the interaction between mGluR5 and Homer1/c in astrocytes. Indeed, the expression of a peptide mimicking the carboxy‐terminal tail of mGluR5 containing the Homer ligand domain to disrupt mGluR5‐Homer1b/c interaction in 4‐week‐old animals was sufficient to impair the single synapse regulation observed when few excitatory synapses were recruited in older animals (from 9‐ to 15‐week‐old rats). Interestingly, its presence had no impact on excitatory synaptic transmission when a higher number of synapses was this time recruited. We decided to perform stereotaxic injections of viruses in 4‐week‐old rats as viral infections can induce astrocyte reactivity that can last up to 4 weeks. To avoid this complication that could impact our experiments and the interpretation of our results, as we did before (Langlais et al. 2025), we decided to wait at least 5 to 6 weeks between the stereotaxic injections and the electrophysiological experiments to make sure that such a reactivity would not be present. Importantly, to control for potential modifications induced by the surgery, a control virus allowing the expression of a control peptide in astrocytes was also used for the control group. We would like to emphasize that in 4‐week‐old rats, mGluR5 expression in the hippocampus has already been developmentally reduced to levels comparable to those observed in adult animals (Cai et al. 2000; Dias et al. 2026; Panatier and Robitaille 2016; Sun et al. 2013).

In physiological condition, each astrocyte has its own territory that does not overlap with its neighbors, allowing each glial cell to be in close apposition with more than 100,000 exclusive synapses in the hippocampus and up to 14 pyramidal neurons in the CA1 area (Bushong et al. 2002; Refaeli et al. 2021). Our findings suggest that mGluR5‐dependent astrocytic regulation facilitates glutamate release at individual synaptic inputs, a regulation that no longer prevails when several synapses are recruited together. Whereas the purpose of such regulation is unclear, we can hypothesize that it could be a way for the hippocampal network to boost the signal from a small number of neighboring synapses within individual astrocytic domains, thereby maximizing their ability to impact the post‐synaptic cells' activity. Such a boost would clearly not be necessary when a high number of neighboring synapses are recruited simultaneously as the excitatory post‐synaptic potential summation would likely generate a change in post‐synaptic activity. The physiological purpose of this mGluR5 regulation in the context of the hippocampus functions still remains to be investigated.

Regarding the cellular process by which glutamate release is regulated as a function of the number of recruited synapses, we could speculate that astrocytes modify their response depending on the nature of the incoming signal. One obvious candidate for mediating this adaptive regulation could be intracellular Ca2+ that represents a sophisticated encoding process in astrocytes (Araque et al. 2014). According to the number of synapses stimulated, the spatio‐temporal profile of intracellular Ca2+ is likely to be dramatically different and thus to encode distinct information. Using Ca2+ imaging, it would be possible to assess the impact of synapse stimulation on intracellular Ca2+ signaling in astrocytes and its consequence on the mGluR5‐induced regulation. Through the process we uncovered, it appears that astrocytes behave as integrators of the incoming activity, increasing the probability that weak individual synaptic signals are detected and impact the postsynaptic cells.

Finally, since our original study was performed in juvenile male rats (Panatier et al. 2011), we focused here on adult male adult rats to assess whether the astrocytic mGluR5‐dependent regulation was still present in adulthood. Investigating female rats would be important to evaluate possible sex‐dependent differences in this regulation.

Materials and Methods

Animals

All efforts were made to reduce any distress caused by the procedures in accordance with the European Communities Council Directive of September 22, 2010 (2010/63/UE) and complying with the guidelines of INSERM and the local ethical committee of the University of Bordeaux (APAFIS #22815). Experiments were performed using adult Wistar male rats (4‐ to 17‐week‐old; Janvier, Le Genest Saint Isle, France). Animals were housed in collective cages under a 12 h light/dark cycle and controlled temperature (22°C ± 2°C) and humidity (50% ± 10%) with ad libitum access to water and food.

AAV Vectors

To study the function of astrocytic mGluR5 we took advantage of two peptides, namely the mGluR5 C‐tail decoy peptide (PPSPFR) and a control peptide with a dual point mutation in the binding motif rendering it incapable of binding Homer (PLSPRR) that were developed (Mao et al. 2005) and used previously (Mao et al. 2005; O'Riordan et al. 2014; Ronesi et al. 2012; Ronesi and Huber 2008; Tronson et al. 2010). This time, to prevent the diffusion of the peptide, the Tat sequence was omitted, and the TagRFP ORF was fused to the N‐terminal part of the peptides. The genes of interest were then inserted in an adeno‐associated virus (AAV) shuttle plasmid that bears the gfaABC1D promoter, a synthetic promoter derived from the GFAP promoter (Ceyzeriat et al. 2018; Lee et al. 2008). Recombinant AAV9 particles were generated by transient co‐transfection of HEK‐293 T cells with a three‐plasmid system as previously described (Berger et al. 2015). Briefly, 3 days following transfection, viral particles were extracted from cell supernatant and cell lysate, concentrated by PEG precipitation, purified by ultracentrifugation on an Iodixaniol step‐gradient, and desalted and concentrated by tangential ultrafiltration. The resulting AAV vector batches were stored at 4°C until further use. AAV concentration was estimated by QPCR using a plasmid of known concentration for the standard curve and expressed as genome content by milliliter (gc/mL).

Therefore, two viruses were used: the AAV9‐TagRFP‐mGluR5pep to express the mGluR5 C‐tail decoy peptide mimicking the carboxy‐terminal tail of mGluR5 and the AAV9‐TagRFP‐Mutpep to express the control peptide with a dual point mutation in astrocytes.

Stereotaxic Injection

In vivo stereotaxic injections were performed in the CA1 area of the hippocampus of 4‐week‐old male Wistar rats, an age at which mGluR5 expression has reached a mature level, ensuring reliable targeting without interfering with developmental processes (Sun et al. 2013). Importantly, experiments were performed a minimum of 5 weeks later, in 9 to 15‐week‐old rats. During the entire procedure, each rat was anesthetized with isoflurane (induction at 4% + air 2 L/min, maintenance at 2% + air 0.4 L/min) and placed on a heating pad at 38°C to maintain a stable body temperature. A subcutaneous injection of Meloxicam (1 mg/Kg) was performed. Local anesthesia was performed using a subcutaneous injection of 0.5% lidocaine (maximum 490 μL for a rat of 350 g) before incision of the skull skin. Then a skin incision was performed, and a hole was drilled in the skull to inject in each hemisphere 1 μL of viral suspension with a Hamilton syringe at 0.2 μL/min. Viral vectors were injected in the CA1 region of the hippocampus (coordinates from Bregma in mm: anteroposterior (AP): −2.8; lateral (L): +/−1.9; ventral (V): −2.6 from the dura; or more caudally as it improves viral diffusion AP: −3, L: +/−3; V: −1.5). AAV were diluted in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) (PBS with 1.6 mM KH2PO4, 3 mM Na2HPO4, 2H2O, 154 mM NaCl; pH 7.4) with 0.001% pluronic acid, at a final total concentration of 1 × 1013 viral genome (vg)/mL. The syringe was left in place for 5 min following each injection to minimize back flow of viral solution. After the injection, the skin was sutured using an ETHILON non‐absorbable 5.0 thread mounted on a 16 mm T‐tip needle, and the rat was placed in a post‐operative recovery chamber maintained at 30°C. Rats were monitored until they woke up then put back in a collective cage enriched with lab “nest”.

Acute Slice Preparation

Transverse and coronal hippocampal slices (350 μm) were prepared as described previously (Panatier et al. 2011). Briefly, 9 to 15‐week‐old rats were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane (isoflurane 5%, air 2L/min), decapitated and the brain was quickly collected and placed either in ice‐cold cutting solution or artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF) saturated with 95% O2 and 5% CO2. The cutting solution composition was (in mM): 120 Choline‐Cl, 3 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 26 NaHCO3, 2 MgCl2, 0.6 CaCl2 (pH 7.3–7.4; 296 mosmol/kg). The ACSF contained (in mM): 120 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 26 NaHCO3, 1.3 MgCl2, 2 CaCl2, and 10 glucose (pH 7.3–7.4; 296 mosmol/kg). A block of tissue containing the hippocampus was prepared and acute slices were cut on a vibratome (Microm HM605V or LEICA VT1200S). Slices were maintained at 32°C for 30 min in ACSF (equilibrated with 95% O2 and 5% CO2) and then at room temperature at least 1 h and maximum 4 h before being transferred to a recording chamber where they were perfused (3 mL/min) with ACSF at 31°C–32°C.

For animals that underwent virus injection: rats were anesthetized with intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (IMALGENE 1000; 167 mg/kg) and xylazine (ROMPUN; 20 mg/kg). In order to better preserve tissues, the blood was flushed by transaortic perfusion of ice‐cold sucrose solution. The sucrose solution was composed of (in mM): 180 Sucrose, 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 25 NaHCO3, 20 Glucose, 7 MgCl2, 0.5 CaCl2 (pH 7.3–7.4; 296 mosmol/kg). An 150 mL of sucrose solution was perfused through the feeding needle at a rate of 70 mL/min. Then hippocampal slices were prepared as described above.

Electrophysiology

Hippocampal CA3‐CA1 area was identified using infrared differential interference contrast microscopy (Olympus BX50WI microscope). For transfected brain slices, virus expression was visually assessed by looking at fluorescent cells in the CA1 stratum radiatum using a reflected fluorescence system (Olympus U‐RFL‐T) set on green excitation (illuminated with 120 W mercury vapor arc lamp coupled to U‐MWIG2 filter). Synaptic events were evoked at 0.033 Hz by orthodromic stimulation (100 μs) of Schaffer collaterals using either a glass pipette (2–4 MΩ) pulled from borosilicate glass (1.5 mm o.d., 1.17 mm i.d., Harvard Apparatus) and filled with ACSF or a bipolar tungsten stimulating electrode, placed in the stratum radiatum more than 150 μm away from the recording electrode.

For patch‐clamp experiments of pyramidal neurons, glass pipettes (5–7 MΩ) were filled with an internal solution containing (in mM): 125 CsMeSO3, 5 NaCl, 1 MgCl2, 10 HEPES, 10 Na2CreatinePO4, 0.1 spermine, 2 QX‐314, 2 ATP, 0.4 GTP (pH 7.1–7.3 adjusted with CsOH; 290–292 mosmol/kg). Excitatory post‐synaptic miniature currents (mEPSCs) were recorded at −70 mV in voltage‐clamp mode in the presence of picrotoxin (100 μM) in the perfused ACSF. Spontaneous unitary synaptic events (miniatures) were obtained in the presence of tetrodotoxin (TTX, 0.5 μM). Series resistance (10–25 MΩ) was monitored online and cells were excluded from data analysis if more than 15% change occurred during the course of the experiment.

For minimal synaptic stimulations, EPSCs were evoked at 0.066 Hz (period of 15 s). The stimulus strength was adjusted at the beginning of each experiment to a value that yielded around 50% successes. The failure rate (number of failures as percentage of total number of stimulations), average EPSCs amplitude (including failures), and EPSC potency (amplitude of EPSCs excluding failures) were calculated. Traces containing spontaneous EPSCs not temporally correlated with the stimulus and that interfered with baseline or peak measurements were not included in the analysis. After 10 min of baseline, drug (MPEP) was bath applied and the recording was continued for 20 min. The last 10 min were compared to baseline for quantification. An upward or downward trend in frequency or amplitude during baseline was an exclusion criterion.

For paired pulse stimulation experiments pairs of EPSCs were evoked 50 ms apart, at a frequency of 0.066 Hz. The paired‐pulse ratio (PPR) was calculated as the amplitude of the second EPSC over the amplitude of the first EPSC.

For patch‐clamp experiments of astrocytes, astrocytes in the stratum radiatum were identified using infrared differential interference contrast microscopy based on soma size (≤ 10 μm), shape, and biophysical properties. Astrocytes were characterized by performing a whole‐cell current–voltage profile using a ramp voltage command from −100 to +100 mV (600 ms duration) in voltage‐clamp mode, according to criteria in Panatier et al. (Panatier et al. 2011). Cells included in this study were characterized by a linear profile (in voltage‐clamp mode), without action potentials (depolarizing steps in current‐clamp mode). The resting membrane potential was recorded in current‐clamp configuration during all the experiment. Whole‐cell patch‐clamp recordings were performed using pipettes (5–7 MΩ) filled with a solution containing (in mM): 126 KMeSO3, 4 MgCl2, 10 HEPES, 10 Na2CreatinePO4, 4 ATP, 0.4 GTP (pH 7.2 adjusted with KOH; 292 mosmol/kg). For experiments with BAPTA tetrapotassium (15 mM, Santa Cruz Biotech) the concentration of KMeSO3 was adjusted to 66 mM to maintain the concentration of potassium ions. To constrain free astrocytic Ca2+, Ca2+ concentration was clamped to 50–80 nM, by complementing the internal solution with (in mM): 0.2 BAPTA (Tocris), 0.45 EGTA (Tocris) and 0.14 Ca2+ (Henneberger et al. 2010). Importantly, 15 min were allowed for intracellular diffusion, before starting the recording of neuronal activity.

Field excitatory post‐synaptic potentials (fEPSPs) recording pipettes (3–4 MΩ) pulled from borosilicate glass and filled with ACSF were placed in the stratum radiatum of the CA1 area. fEPSPs were recorded in current‐clamp mode (I = 0). In order to study basal synaptic transmission, fEPSPs were monitored at CA3‐CA1 synapses in response to increasing stimulus strength. The amplitude of stimulation was incremented during the experiment, in order to recruit an increasing number of synapses (from a small number to a higher one). This procedure was used to build input/output (I/O) relationship. Paired fEPSPs were evoked 100 ms apart, at 0.033 Hz. The paired‐pulse ratio was calculated as the slope of the second fEPSP over the slope of the first evoked fEPSP (paired‐pulsed ratio; PPR). A stable baseline was recorded for at least 20 min before starting experiments. For the I/O experiment, the same amplitude of stimulation was applied 3 times (0.033 Hz), before being increased. Once the amplitude of fiber volley reached −0.07 mV, the amplitude of stimulation used for the baseline was again applied during 10 min. If the fEPSP slope came back, the pharmacological drug (either antagonist or agonist) was applied for 15 min before the realization of the second I/O. Importantly, if the fEPSP slope did not return to the baseline level the experiment was stopped. The group 0.01 mV corresponded to fEPSPs evoked when the amplitude of the fiber volley was smaller than −0.02 mV; the group 0.03 mV for fEPSPs evoked when the amplitude of the fiber volley was comprised between −0.02 and −0.039 mV; for the group 0.05 mV the fiber volley was comprised in between −0.04 and −0.059 mV; for the group 0.07 mV, the fiber volley was in between −0.06 and −0.08 mV.

All electrophysiological recordings were performed using a Multiclamp 700B amplifier (Axon Instruments Inc.). Signals were filtered at 2 kHz and digitized at 20 kHz via a Digidata 1440. Data were analyzed offline using clampfit (Axon Instruments Inc) and Igor Pro 8 (Neuromatic, WaveMetrics).

Immunohistochemical Procedures

For the quantification of mGluR5 expression in astrocytes, we followed our standard procedures (Israel et al. 2014), 11 to 15‐week‐old male rats were overdosed i.p. with a mixture of pentobarbital (200 mg/kg) and lidocaine (20 mg/kg) and perfused transaortically over 60 min with 50 mL saline followed by 800 mL of a cold mixture of 4% paraformaldehyde in a 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH = 7.4). The brains were dissected out and post‐fixed overnight in the same fixative before cutting on a VTS1000S vibratome (Leica Biosystems, 92,752 Nanterre, France). Series of 40 μm‐thick coronal sections spanning the rostral third of the dorsal hippocampus were collected and rinsed in 0.9% NaCl PB (PBS; pH 7.4). Sections anatomically matched with the sites of the electrophysiological recordings were processed free‐floating for simultaneous double‐immunolabeling. Primary and secondary antisera were diluted in PBS containing 0.25% Triton X‐100 (Sigma) and applied at room temperature overnight and for 2 h, respectively. The primary antisera solution included 1% normal donkey serum and a polyclonal affinity‐purified rabbit anti‐mGluR5 (1:1000, Millipore no. AB5675) applied in a cocktail with either a mouse monoclonal anti‐S100β (1:500, Sigma clone SH‐B1 no. S2532) or an affinity‐purified chicken polyclonal anti‐GFAP (1:3000, USBiologicals no. G2032‐25F). The secondary antisera (all from Jackson Immunoresearch and diluted 1:1000) included an Alexa 488‐linked donkey anti‐rabbit (no. 711–545‐152) applied in a cocktail with either a TRITC‐linked donkey anti‐mouse (no. 715–025‐151) or a TRITC‐linked donkey anti‐chicken (no. 703–025‐155). Finally, sections were rinsed in PBS, mounted on slides, air‐dried, counterstained with bisbenzimide (Hoechst, Sigma no. B‐1155), and coverslipped under an antifading medium made of 0.1% para‐phenylenediamine (Sigma no. P‐6001) in 3 vol./1 vol. pure bidistilled glycerol and PB, respectively. In control experiments, omission of anyone of, or of all of, primaries in an otherwise normal procedure resulted in an absence of the respective color or in no labelling at all, respectively, indicating neither cross‐reaction nor unspecific binding of the secondaries. The distribution of labelling for mGluR5, S100β, and GFAP was congruent with previous descriptions (Panatier et al. 2011).

To test for the astrocytic specificity of AAV‐mediated transgenes driven by the gfaABC1D promoter, 11‐week‐old male rats were perfused and dissected. The brains were collected and post‐fixed overnight at 4°C. Serial coronal 30 μm‐thick slices were cut and processed free‐floating. Primary and secondary antisera were diluted in PBS containing 0.3% Triton X‐100 (Sigma) and applied at 4°C overnight and at room temperature for 2 h, respectively. Primary antibodies solution contained: 2% normal donkey serum, goat anti‐TdTomato (1:1000, Sicgen no. AB8181‐200), rabbit anti‐Sox9 (1:4000, Merck Millipore no. AB5535), and chicken anti‐NeuN (1:2000, Merck Millipore no. ABN91). The secondary antibodies solution (all from Jackson Immunoresearch and diluted 1:1000) was a cocktail including a TRITC‐linked donkey anti‐goat (no. 705–025‐147), an Alexa 488‐linked donkey anti‐rabbit (no. 711–545‐152) and an Alexa 647‐linked donkey anti‐chicken (no. 703–545‐155).

RNAscope Combined With Immunofluorescence

From 12 to 17‐week‐old male rats were used and their brains were fixed, all as described above for immunohistochemistry. Free‐floating 30 μm‐thick coronal vibratome sections were stored at −20°C in antifreeze solution (30% ethylene glycol and 20% glycerol in phosphate buffer) until used. All reagents were purchased from Bio‐Techne, unless otherwise stated. Opal fluorophores were obtained from Akoya Biosciences. The Grm5 probe (Bio‐Techne no. 471241) comprised 20 oligonucleotide pairs targeting nucleotides 1199–2142 of the rat Grm5 transcript (NM_001081414.2). For control slides, positive and negative control probes (Bio‐Techne nos. 320,891 and 321,831 respectively) were applied to separate sections. For the Grm5 staining, six brain sections containing the CA1 region were selected, based on the Allen Brain Atlas. Sections were mounted on SuperFrost Plus Gold slides (Thermo Fisher Scientific no. J1800AMNZ), air‐dried at room temperature for 1 h, briefly rehydrated in ultrapure (Milli‐Q) water, air‐dried overnight, and baked at 60°C for 1 h the following day. All baking steps were performed in a dry HybEZ Humidity Control Tray (without lid or humidifying paper) placed in a HybEZ oven at 60°C. Tissue pretreatment, probe hybridization, and signal detection and amplification were performed according to the manufacturer's protease‐free protocol for fixed‐frozen samples (Bio‐Techne no. UM 323100/Rev. C) using the RNAscope Fluorescent Multiplex Detection Reagent Kit (Bio‐Techne no. 323110). During pretreatment, sections were incubated in target retrieval solution for 15 min, followed by incubation in Manual PreTreat Pro Reagent for 30 min. Grm5 probes were visualized using tyramide signal amplification with Opal570 (Akoya Biosciences no. FP1488001KT) diluted 1:750 in RNAscope TSA buffer. Following RNAscope, immunofluorescence staining was performed. Sections were first blocked for 30 min in PBS containing 0.3% Triton, 2% Normal Donkey Serum and 50 mM Glycine. Primary antibodies against S100β (1:1000, Sigma no. S2644, rabbit) and GFAP (1:1000, Abcam no. Ab4674, chicken) were applied overnight at room temperature in PBS containing, 0.3% Triton, 0.1% H2O2 and 10 mM Glycine. The following day, sections were washed three times for 5 min in PBS and incubated 2 h with Alexa 488‐linked donkey anti‐rabbit (1:500, Jackson Immunoresearch no. 711–545‐152) and Alexa 647‐linked donkey anti‐chicken (1:500, Jackson Immunoresearch no. 703–606‐155). Sections were counterstained with DAPI (0.25 μg/mL in PBS) for 5 min, washed twice for 5 min in PBS and once in 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH = 7.5). Slides were briefly rinsed in distilled water and mounted with ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant (Thermo Fisher Scientific no. P36930) using glass coverslips (thickness 0.13–0.17 mm). After drying at room temperature, slides were stored in the dark at 4°C until imaging.

Image Acquisition and Analysis

For AAV characterization, image acquisition was performed on a Nanozoomer 2.0 HT (Hamamatsu, Japan) with a 20× objective (numerical aperture = 0.75; additional lens 1.75×). The different fluorescent channels were merged in ImageJ, and the Cell Counter plugin was then used to quantify the phenotype of transfected cells (defined by colocalization of TdTomato with Sox9 for astrocytes or with NeuN for neurons) in the CA1 region across four rostrocaudal planes ranging from −2.8 to −3.3 mm relative to Bregma. Data are expressed as the percentage of labeled cells in each animal (number of transfected cells normalized to total number of cells × 100). Counting was made by an experimenter who was blind to experimental groups.

For double‐labeling immunohistochemistry, epifluorescence was done on a Zeiss Axiophot 1 microscope (Zeiss, 07745 Jena, Germany) equipped with a Leica Flexacam C1 camera (Leica, 35578 Wetzlar, Germany). Confocal imaging was performed on a Leica DM6 CFS TCS SP8 confocal microscope operating at the highest resolution: channels were acquired separately with a 63× oil‐immersion objective (numerical aperture = 1.40) with a pinhole of 20 μm yielding a section thickness of 0.421 μm and at a format and zoom factor generating a pixel size of 70 nm for an image size of 144 × 144 μm. Each acquisition was a single 0.63 μm‐thick optimized stack of 5 images (step = 0.16 μm) appropriate for deconvolution taken at the tissue section plane with brightest fluorescence. For each of the 3 animals and each double‐labeling procedure, including controls, 7 stacks centered at the right CA1 stratum radiatum were obtained, each stack acquired from individual separate tissue sections (n = 21 stacks per procedure). Images were deconvoluted using the Huygens Professional software (Scientific Volume Imaging, 1213 VB Hilversum, The Netherlands) and analyzed with the Fiji Software for pixel colocalization using a custom‐made script including thresholding with the method Li and binarization. Data were expressed as percentages of pixel overlap, quantified as (1) the proportion of mGluR5 signal overlapping with astrocytic markers (GFAP or S100β), (2) the proportion of astrocytic marker signal overlapping with mGluR5, and (3) the percentage of overlapping pixels measured in control conditions without primary mGluR5 antibody.

For RNAscope experiments, imaging was performed on a Leica DM6 CFS TCS SP8 confocal microscope operating in general mode: the four channels (for DAPI, Alexa 488, Opal 570 and Alexa 647) were acquired separately with a 40× oil‐immersion objective (numerical aperture = 1.30) with an Airy 1 pinhole yielding an optical section thickness of 1.039 μm and at a format generating a pixel size of 82 nm and an image size of 168 × 168 μm. Each acquisition was a rectangular 2‐tile‐stack of 5–6 frames for a total tissue thickness of 4.43 ± 0.39 μm. For each of the 3 rats, six sections were processed with the Grm5 probe and two sections with either the negative control probe or the positive control probe, that latter not imaged. The CA1 stratum radiatum was imaged on both hemispheres, for a total of 6–7 stacks per animal per procedure (standard and control). Image analysis was performed using QuPath software (version 0.4.0). Regions of interest corresponding to the CA1 stratum radiatum region of the hippocampus were manually delineated for each image prior to analysis. Astrocytes were identified using the “Positive cell detection” tool. Briefly, nuclei were first detected based on DAPI signal intensity, and astrocytic nuclei were subsequently classified using an intensity threshold applied to S100β immunoreactivity. Detection of mGluR5 mRNA (Grm5) was performed using the “Subcellular detection” tool to identify punctate signals corresponding to RNAscope labeling. Detection parameters were stringently optimized to ensure that no spots were detected in negative control samples. Based on this criterion, astrocytes were classified as Grm5‐positive when at least one intracellular mRNA spot was detected. Data are expressed as numbers of Grm5‐positive astrocytes relative to total numbers of astrocytes.

Chemicals

All drugs were bath‐applied. Appropriate stock solutions were made and diluted with ACSF just before application. Drug used were ARL 67156 (50 μM; Biotechne), CGS 21680 hydrochloride (30 nM; Tocris), MPEP hydrochloride (25 μM; Tocris), SCH 58261 (100 nM; Tocris), Picrotoxin (100 μM; Tocris), Tetrodotoxin Citrate (500 nM; Tocris).

Statistical Analysis

The number of tissue sections, cells or slices (n), and animals (N) used for each experiment are indicated in the text, figure legends, and the Table S1. Each dot in the figures corresponds to the n, as indicated in the figure legend. Statistical analyses were performed using the n. Normality and homogeneity of variance were determined using Agostino‐Pearson test and Fisher‐Snedecor test, respectively. When normality was achieved or in large samples (n > 40), comparison between groups was made using two‐tailed parametric Student's unpaired t‐test, Student's paired t‐test, one‐way ANOVA or the two‐way repeated‐measures ANOVA. When normality failed in small samples (n < 40), comparison between groups was made using the nonparametric Mann–Whitney test, Wilcoxon signed rank test or Kruskal‐Wallis test. For multiple comparisons, p values were calculated using Sidak or Dunn's tests (for parametric and non‐parametric variables respectively) depending on the number of tested comparisons. The statistical significance was established at p < 0.05. Graphpad Prism software was used for statistical analysis. Data were expressed as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses are summarized in Table S1.

Figure S1: Complementary approaches to confirm the localization of mGluR5 in adult hippocampal astrocytes. (A–D) Triple‐labeled histological sections, in the CA1 stratum radiatum (st.rad), viewed in confocal microscopy through channels for GFAP (blue), mGluR5 (green) or S100β (red/magenta; 11‐week‐old rat). (A) Low magnification view (maximum intensity projection, MIP, z = 0.95 μm) indicating the localization of astrocytes that were imaged with a higher magnification in B and C. (B, C) Merged channels of MIPs (z = 0.95 μm) and (C) single planes (z = 0.19 μm) show how mGluR5‐positive patches decorate astrocytes. To confirm their localization in astrocytes, arrows and enlarged insets indicate the co‐localization of mGluR5 with GFAP signals. (D) Surface rendering dissecting the cell marked by an asterisk in (B). (E–G) Quadruple‐labeled histological section, through the CA1 stratum radiatum, (15‐week‐old rat) viewed in confocal (E, F) and STED (G) microscopy through channels for GFAP (blue), mGluR5 (green), S100β (red) or Hoechst (gray). (E) Low magnification view (single optical slice; z = 0.98 μm) of the astrocyte that was imaged with a higher magnification in (F) and (G). (G) In inset 1, arrows point to mGluR5 signal located in astrocytic compartments (overlap between mGluR5 patches and cytoplasmic S100β). In inset 2, arrows point to overlap between mGluR5 and GFAP signals. (H–K) FISH experiments. (H) Example of a 3D reconstruction from confocal sections with Imaris. A mask was generated to isolate Grm5 mRNA (red spots) in neurons identified using NeuN immunolabeling (green). Sense (left) and antisense (right) probes were used. (I) Bar graph representing the density of red spots indicating labeling with either the sense probe or the antisense probe for Grm5 mRNA in the cell body of pyramidal neurons or interneurons in the CA1 stratum radiatum labeled with NeuN antibody. (J) Example of a 3D reconstruction from confocal sections with Imaris. A mask was generated to isolate Grm5 mRNA (red spots) in astrocytes identified using the co‐immunolabeling for GFAP (white) and S100 β (green). Sense (left) and antisense (right) probes were used. (K) Bar graph representing the density of red spots indicating labeling with either the sense probe or the antisense probe for Grm5 in astrocytes in the CA1 stratum radiatum labeled with S100β and GFAP. Data are represented as mean ± SEM. Each dot represents one tissue section. Mann–Whitney test, *p < 0.05. Scale bars in (A), 50 μm; in (B) and (C), 10 μm; in (D), 5 μm; in (E), 5 μm; in (F) and (G), 1 μm; (H) and (I), 10 μm.

Figure S2: AAV strategy. (A) A series of coronal sections through the hippocampus showing the spread of TagRFP immunofluorescence using an anti‐TdTomato antibody in the CA1 hippocampus after stereotaxic injection of AAV9‐gfaABC1D‐TagRFP. (B–D) Immunohistochemical studies confirm the efficacy of the viral approach to target hippocampal astrocytes. The quantification, expressed as a percentage (N = 4 rats), was performed by assessing the number of transduced cells co‐expressing TdTomato with Sox9 (astrocytes) or NeuN (neurons).
