RecourseBench: A Modular Framework for Reproducible Algorithmic Recourse Evaluation
Zahra Khotanlou, Hashir Ahmed, Chenghao Tan, Ahmed Abdelaal, Amir-Hossein Karimi

Abstract
Algorithmic recourse methods provide counterfactual explanations that inform individuals of the actions required to overturn an unfavorable model decision. Despite rapid methodological progress, principled comparison remains elusive; existing frameworks are often difficult to extend and lack both interoperability and systematic verification that integrated methods faithfully reproduce their originally reported results. We introduce RecourseBench , a unified evaluation framework built around three commitments namely, modularity, reproducibility, and interactivity. The framework decomposes the pipeline into five fully decoupled layers—Data, Preprocessing, Model, Recourse Method, and Evaluation—governed by abstract interfaces and a dynamic registry. To address the reproducibility gap in prior benchmarks, we introduce a four-tier classification system in which every integrated method is validated by an automated test suite against its originally reported results. We further provide an interactive web interface for flexible, configuration-driven comparison across methods, datasets, and model architectures. Our framework currently integrates 28 state-of-the-art recourse methods and, to our knowledge, constitutes the first recourse benchmark to explicitly enforce method-level reproducibility through automated, quantitative testing.

Introduction

The use of machine learning models has expanded rapidly in high-stakes domains such as healthcare
(
24
)
, finance
(
9
)
, and criminal justice
(
33
;
26
)
. As these systems increasingly influence consequential decisions, the need for transparency and actionable feedback has become more pronounced. While early Explainable AI (XAI) methods aim to interpret model behavior
(
11
)
, they primarily provide descriptive insights and often fail to offer concrete guidance to affected individuals.

Algorithmic recourse addresses this limitation by generating counterfactual explanations that specify how an individual can modify their features to obtain a desired outcome
(
49
;
22
)
. In contrast to purely descriptive explanations, recourse methods provide prescriptive recommendations, making them particularly relevant in decision-critical settings. Moreover, emerging regulatory frameworks such as the European Union’s AI Act
(
43
)
and GDPR
(
48
)
have increased the demand for meaningful explanations of automated decisions, further motivating the development of actionable recourse methods.

As the diversity of algorithmic recourse methods continues to grow, the need for robust, fair, and scalable evaluation frameworks becomes increasingly critical.
Recourse methods are often optimized for different and sometimes conflicting objectives, such as proximity, sparsity, robustness, and feasibility, making direct comparison non-trivial.
Furthermore, evaluation criteria are inherently context-dependent.
For instance, robustness must be assessed differently depending on whether the focus is data shift
(
45
)
or model multiplicity
(
15
)
.
This variability necessitates flexible benchmarking pipelines that can accommodate evolving metrics and experiment settings while maintaining consistent experimental conditions.
A standardized benchmarking framework is therefore essential for ensuring reproducibility, enabling fair comparisons, and allowing practitioners to identify methods that best align with application-specific constraints.

Although the research community has recognized this need, existing open-source solutions leave critical gaps in structural modularity and rigorous verification of the implemented methods.
Historically, many libraries have positioned recourse merely as a sub-feature within broader explainability toolkits (e.g., Alibi
(
25
)
) or specialized method repositories (e.g., RobustX
(
18
)
, CounterfactualExplanations.jl
(
2
)
).
While useful for standalone generation, these tools lack the unified pipelines and diverse datasets required for systematic benchmarking.
More recently, dedicated recourse benchmarking frameworks have emerged, but they introduce their own architectural limitations.
CARLA
(
34
)
pioneered unified environments for tabular recourse, yet its tightly coupled architecture restricts the seamless integration of new evaluation metrics or model types.
Conversely, ReLax
(
14
)
achieves high computational efficiency via hardware-accelerated JAX
(
6
)
, but this design imposes strict ecosystem constraints, complicating integration of the method using other libraries like PyTorch and scikit-learn. Ultimately, current ecosystems leave a critical void for a framework that is simultaneously modular and strictly reproducible.

Our Contributions:

To address these limitations, we introduce
RecourseBench
, a unified benchmarking framework designed around three core principles: modularity, reproducibility, and interactivity. The framework decomposes the benchmarking pipeline into five decoupled layers—Data, Preprocessing, Model, Recourse Method, and Evaluation which enables seamless integration of new components without modifying existing ones.
The system natively supports widely used machine learning ecosystems, including PyTorch and Scikit-learn.
Also, we introduce a four-tier reproducibility classification system and validate each integrated method through automated testing against reported results, promoting transparency and reliability in benchmarking.
Finally, we develop an interactive system for exploring benchmarking results, allowing users to construct configurable leaderboards, perform multi-metric comparisons, and filter methods based on their capabilities.

2
Preliminaries

To contextualize our framework, we provide a brief overview of post-hoc algorithmic recourse methods and discuss the challenges of reproducibility in machine learning systems. Our framework is designed specifically for post-hoc recourse methods, which operate on trained models without modifying their internal structure.

2.1
Recourse Methods

We organize the recourse methods implemented in our framework into six broad categories.
This taxonomy reflects common methodological paradigms observed in the literature.
However, we acknowledge that this classification scheme is representative of our benchmarking suite rather than an exhaustive list of all possible post-hoc recourse methodologies.

Constraint
\&
Logic-Based Methods:
This family formulates recourse as a formal constraint satisfaction problem. Methods may employ general-purpose solvers like Mixed-Integer Programming (MIP)
(
7
;
46
)
and Satisfiability (SAT)
(
21
)
, or directly exploit the architecture of glass-box models by extracting and satisfying logical root-to-leaf paths in decision trees
(
44
)
. While providing guarantees for optimal solutions and strict adherence to complex feasibility rules, these methods often face exponential scaling bottlenecks on large datasets or deep models
(
30
)
.

Gradient-Based Optimization:
Pioneered by Wachter et al.
(
49
)
and expanded in subsequent works
(
45
;
36
)
, these approaches treat counterfactual generation as a continuous minimization task. They leverage backpropagation to iteratively perturb inputs across decision boundaries
(
31
;
5
)
. Though computationally efficient and highly scalable, they risk converging on practically infeasible “off-manifold” states and require strict regularization to ensure actionability.

Representation Learning:
These methods shift the search from the ambient feature space to a learned, lower-dimensional latent manifold via encoder-decoder architectures
(
10
;
20
;
35
;
29
)
. This generative constraint naturally encourages realistic, distributionally plausible counterfactuals
(
37
;
50
;
40
)
. However, their success is heavily bottlenecked by the quality of the learned representation and the difficulty of decoding discrete features faithfully.

Surrogate-Assisted Optimization:
To bypass the opacity and non-convexity of black-box models, these techniques fit a localized approximation around the factual instance. The surrogate can be a deterministic linear hyperplane
(
23
)
or a probabilistic density landscape
(
32
)
. The counterfactual is then found by optimizing directly over this localized surrogate. While highly scalable, the recourse’s validity depends entirely on the local fidelity of the surrogate approximation.

Derivative-Free
\&
Heuristic Search:
Treating the underlying model as a strict black box, these methods bypass gradients and formal solvers entirely. Instead, they utilize iterative meta-heuristics to perturb discrete feature combinations
(
52
)
or generate random, synthetic observations in the continuous space to find boundary-crossing points
(
27
)
. This paradigm easily handles non-differentiable models, but lacks global optimality guarantees and can suffer from poor sample efficiency due to high query counts.

Instance-Based Search:
Anchoring the search entirely to existing observational data, these methods filter the training dataset for candidate points already in the target class and interpolate geometric paths toward them
(
38
;
28
)
. This data-driven approach naturally avoids unrealistic synthetic states by relying on historical distributions, though its success is strictly limited by the density and diversity of the underlying dataset.

2.2
Reproducibility in Machine Learning and Software Engineering

Reproducibility refers to the ability of an independent research team to obtain equivalent results using the same method, given sufficient documentation
(
12
)
.
It is a fundamental requirement for scientific progress and is distinct from
replicability
, which concerns the ability to reach similar conclusions under different data or experimental conditions
(
41
)
.
While the broader scientific community has long contended with widespread failures of
reproducibility across disciplines
(
4
)
, machine learning presents a uniquely challenging setting due to the multiplicity of factors that can silently invalidate a result.

Despite its importance, reproducibility remains a persistent challenge in machine learning. Empirical studies have shown that a large proportion of published results cannot be reliably reproduced due to incomplete reporting of experimental details
(
12
;
13
)
.
Even when code is available, missing documentation and implicit assumptions can prevent faithful reproduction.

Henderson et al.
(
16
)
provide a particularly instructive
demonstration of the problem: in deep reinforcement learning, non-determinism in
standard benchmark environments, combined with variance intrinsic to the methods
themselves, can render published comparisons statistically uninterpretable.
Seemingly distinct algorithms may exhibit no meaningful performance difference once
experimental variance is properly characterized, a finding that calls into question the validity of entire lines of comparative work.
More broadly, Semmelrock et al.
(
42
)
identify a compounding set of factors
inherent to ML research any of which can
shift not merely individual numerical outcomes but the comparative conclusions
of a study altogether.
These difficulties are further amplified by structural
incentives within the research community
(
8
)
.

Recognition of these failures has prompted several community-level responses.
From the software engineering
perspective, the discipline of regression testing, systematically re-executing
a validated test suite to detect behavioral regressions as a codebase
evolves
(
39
)
, provides a principled and well-established
mechanism for enforcing consistency guarantees in scientific software.
Several recent works have drawn on this tradition explicitly, proposing that the
maintenance of automated, executable test suites is essential for ensuring that
ML systems and benchmarks can be externally verified
(
39
)
.

Despite these advances, the uptake of such practices within specialized
benchmarking repositories remains limited.
In the domain of algorithmic recourse
specifically, existing frameworks such as CARLA
(
34
)
and ReLax
(
14
)
integrate methods in good faith but provide no
systematic mechanism to validate that their implementations remain faithful to
the empirical results originally reported.
To our knowledge, no prior recourse benchmark explicitly enforces method-level reproducibility through automated quantitative testing.
This gap is consequential: a benchmarking framework can
serve as a reliable basis for comparative evaluation only if the fidelity of
each constituent method to its original specification has been established.
How we address this gap in the design of our framework is described in
Section

4
.

3
Implementation Design

Our framework is engineered to address the systemic challenges of benchmarking algorithmic recourse. Because new methods constantly introduce novel concepts, data processing techniques, and evaluation paradigms, a flexible framework is essential to establish common ground while accommodating differences. To support large-scale collaborative development without compromising scientific rigor, the system adheres to four core design principles:

Modularity.
The framework is strictly compartmentalized into independent subsystems (e.g., Dataset, PreProcess, TargetModel, Method, Evaluation) that interact exclusively through predefined public interfaces. Internally, self-contained modules can be modified without inducing systemic failures. To prevent abstraction leaks, messages passed between subsystems is encapsulated in
DatasetObject
s, making tabular data self-contained (see
subsection A.1
for detailed rationals behind this design). This strict modularity facilitates parallel development by minimizing integration friction; adding new methods requires only the addition of isolated components.

Extensibility.
We enforce an additive development paradigm where new functionality is introduced without altering existing components. For instance, integrating a new recourse method requires only an isolated subfolder containing a class that inherits from
MethodObject
(implementing
fit()
and
get_counterfactuals()
), followed by a simple registration. See
Figure 4
. This confines potential code pollution. Consequently, components act as composable, independently substitutable units that researchers can combine using minimal glue code to customize diverse workflows.

Reproducibility.
Randomness is tightly regulated via a dedicated context manager that resets the local seed and pseudo-random sequence, guaranteeing reproducibility even if module execution order changes. Furthermore, the framework allows the integration of distinct reproduction codebases. These isolated, single-file implementations operate with fewer structural constraints, retaining their original architectural idiosyncrasies without compromising the overarching framework design.

Experimental Integrity.
To ensure a level playing field, the framework enforces strict privilege controls on public interfaces. For example,
TargetModel
components are exposed to
Method
components as read-only, preventing unintended mutations and cascading side effects. Orchestrated through a centralized
Experiment
workflow and governed by a YAML configuration, all methods are subjected to identical, immutable settings, including standardized datasets (
DatasetObject
) and target models (
ModelObject
).

Reproducibility Protocol

While the proposed architecture enables flexible and consistent benchmarking, ensuring the reliability of its outputs depends on the faithful reproduction of the integrated methods. In practice, this is often difficult due to incomplete experimental specifications, unavailable code, and sensitivity to implementation details.

To address this issue, we introduce a quantitative, metric-level reproducibility framework that evaluates the fidelity of each implementation against the results reported in the original work. This system provides an objective and comparable measure of reproduction quality across methods, and pairs lower-confidence implementations with explicit limitation logs. Importantly, our evaluation focuses exclusively on the primary quantitative results reported by each paper.

4.1
Reproducibility Criterion

Original works report results across multiple datasets, model architectures, and evaluation metrics. We therefore define a
result
as a tuple
(\text{dataset},\text{model},\text{metric})
, corresponding to a single scalar value reported in the original paper.

Let
m
denote the value reported in the original work, and
\hat{m}
the value obtained from our implementation under the same configuration. We define the symmetric relative error as

\Delta(m,\hat{m})=\frac{|\hat{m}-m|}{\max\{|m|,|\hat{m}|,\epsilon_{0}\}},

where
\epsilon_{0}>0
is a small constant introduced to ensure numerical stability for values close to zero. We set
\epsilon_{0}=1e-12
.

A result is considered
reproduced
if
\Delta(m,\hat{m})\leq\delta
, where
\delta
is a fixed tolerance threshold. In all experiments, we set
\delta=0.35
, corresponding to a
35\%
relative deviation. To display the effects of the delta threshold, we show a sweep in
Figure 2
. More details about the delta selection can be found in
Appendix B
.

:

Appendix C
for details.

4.2
Reproducibility Levels

Based on the criterion above, we evaluate each method over the set of results it reports in the original paper. Let
R
denote the total number of reported results, and
R_{\text{rep}}
the number of reproduced results.

We categorize methods into four reproducibility levels:

Level 0: No Reproduction (
\emptyset
).

R_{\text{rep}}=0
. None of the reported results are reproduced within the tolerance threshold.

Level 1: Minimal Reproduction.

R_{\text{rep}}=1
. Exactly one reported result is reproduced.

Level 2: Partial Reproduction.

1<R_{\text{rep}}<R
. More than one, but not all, reported results are reproduced.

Level 3: Full Reproduction (
\forall
).

R_{\text{rep}}=R
. All reported results are reproduced within the tolerance threshold.

4.3
The Empirical Landscape of Reproducibility

In
Table

1
, we detail the reproduction levels for
all 28 methods. Rather than reflecting an engineering
shortfall, this distribution exposes a structural infrastructure gap in
the recourse literature: the field evolved rapidly under heterogeneous
experimental practices, leaving independent verification systematically
unsupported.

The primary obstacles are missing artifacts—unreleased code, omitted hyperparameters, proprietary datasets, and undocumented configurations.
Even when code exists, reproduction is further complicated by computational constraints (e.g., MACE can require over 70 hours per configuration) and structural incompatibilities with our post-hoc design.
Additional variance arises from execution-order seed divergence, hardware-specific floating-point implementations, and software version differences, all of which are rarely reported in published work yet can produce deviations indistinguishable from implementation errors.

Crucially, the Level 0 to Level 1 transition represents the epistemically significant leap; moving from zero verifiable evidence to independently validating correspondence between a published metric and its observed counterpart.
This transition is qualitatively larger than subsequent advances, which face diminishing barriers as artifact quality improves.
We therefore position
RecourseBench
not as a complete reproduction of the literature, but as the operationalization of reproducibility, transforming it from an implicit expectation into a standardized, measurable, and continuously extensible property. Detailed per-method logs are provided in Appendix
C
.

5
Evaluation and Benchmark Representation

A central objective of this study is to provide the research community with not
only a rigorous evaluation pipeline, but also an accessible and expressive interface
through which benchmark results can be explored and interpreted.
To this end, our framework separates two distinct concerns: the
evaluation protocol
, which defines the metrics, normalization procedure, and aggregation strategy applied uniformly across all methods, and the
benchmark interface
, which exposes those results through an interactive visual environment designed to support flexible, user-driven comparison.

5.1
Evaluation Design

Experimental coverage.

A distinguishing feature of our benchmarking approach is the systematic enumeration
of experimental configurations.
Specifically, we evaluate combinations of recourse methods, datasets, and
predictive model architectures whose compatibility is declared in the per-method
configuration files. Each method explicitly specifies the model types it supports
(e.g., differentiable models such as MLP, or black-box models such as Random Forest);
combinations not declared as compatible are excluded from evaluation. In total, the benchmark currently evaluates 3 datasets, 3 model types, and 22 recourse methods (those achieving a reproduction level of 1 or higher), yielding 137 compatible method–model–dataset configurations after excluding incompatible combinations.
Every reported figure is directly comparable to every other, having been produced under identical preprocessing and model training procedures.

Evaluation metrics.

Algorithmic recourse methods optimize for objectives that are often in tension with
one another, and no single metric is sufficient to characterize method quality.
We evaluate each method across a comprehensive set of metrics drawn from the established recourse literature
(
34
;
22
)
, covering the dimensions of recourse quality.
For each configuration, evaluation is performed over instances drawn from the held-out test set that received an unfavorable prediction (i.e., a prediction differing from the desired class, which is specified per dataset in the configuration).
To balance computational cost against sufficient sample diversity, up to 50 instances are sampled uniformly at random from this pool.
Instances are drawn independently for each method–dataset–model configuration without variance aggregation across runs; this design reflects the framework’s focus on enabling extensible, reproducible evaluation.
Let
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote a factual instance,
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
its generated counterfactual,
f
the target predictive model,
y^{*}
the desired class label, and
\{\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
the set of evaluated factual and counterfactual pairs. We define the evaluation metrics as follows:

Validity
(success rate): the proportion of input instances for which the method produces a counterfactual that achieves the desired class label under the target predictive model.

V=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\bigl[f(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{i})=y^{*}\bigr]

(1)

Proximity
(
\ell_{2}
distance): the mean Euclidean distance between each factual instance and its generated counterfactual, measured in the preprocessed feature space. Proximity captures the cost of the recommended changes and is a primary objective in most recourse formulations.

P=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{i}\|_{2}

(2)

Sparsity
(
\ell_{0}
distance): the mean number of features modified between a factual instance and its counterfactual. Sparse recourse is generally preferred in practice, as interventions on fewer features are cognitively simpler and operationally less burdensome for the affected individual.

S=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{i}\|_{0}

(3)

Plausibility
(YNN): the mean Euclidean distance from each generated counterfactual to its
Y
nearest neighbors in the training set, measured in the preprocessed feature space
34
. A lower value indicates that the counterfactual lies closer to the observed data distribution, and is therefore more likely to represent an achievable real-world state. This metric serves as a lightweight, model-agnostic proxy for distributional plausibility that requires no auxiliary density estimator, where
\mathbf{z}^{(j)}_{i}
denotes the
j
-th nearest neighbor of
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{i}
in the training set.

\text{yNN}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{y}\sum_{j=1}^{y}\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{i}-\mathbf{z}^{(j)}_{i}\|_{2}

(4)

Runtime
: the total time required to generate counterfactuals for the evaluated factual instances, measured on a consistent computational platform.
Runtime is a practical consideration for deployment and is particularly relevant when comparing methods whose computational complexity differs by orders of magnitude, such as constraint-based and gradient-based approaches.

T=\sum_{i=1}^{N}t_{i}

(5)

Score aggregation.

The metrics defined above are heterogeneous in both scale and direction. To enable principled aggregation, we map all metrics to a normalized score in
[0,1]
under a consistent higher-is-better convention. For metrics where lower raw values are preferable, we apply a negative exponential transformation
\tilde{m}=e^{(-m)}
.
All raw metric values m are non-negative by construction, so this transformation always produces a score in (0, 1], also, it is scale-invariant and does not depend on empirical minimum and maximum values across the current method set, which would shift arbitrarily as new methods are integrated into the framework.
For validity, which is already bounded and higher-is-better,
the raw value is used directly. Given a user-specified subset
of metrics
\mathcal{M}
, the overall benchmark score is then:

Score=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}|}\sum_{m\in\mathcal{M}}\tilde{m}.

(6)

This aggregation strategy treats all selected metrics as equally weighted, yielding
a single scalar score that reflects balanced performance across the dimensions the
user deems relevant for their application context. We deliberately avoid imposing
a fixed global weighting scheme, as the relative importance of proximity, sparsity,
plausibility, and runtime is inherently application-dependent; the user-driven
metric selection mechanism described in Section
5.2
allows this
weighting to be determined by the practitioner rather than prescribed by the
benchmark.

6
Conclusion and Future Work

We have presented
RecourseBench
, a modular, reproducible, and interactive framework for the systematic evaluation of algorithmic recourse methods. By decomposing the benchmarking pipeline into five fully decoupled layers, enforcing method-level reproducibility through a four-tier classification system and automated per-method test suites, and exposing results through a configurable interactive interface, RecourseBench enables rigorous auditing of recourse methods. The framework’s design supports easy integration of new datasets, models, and methods, lowering barriers to both reproduction of prior work and development of new approaches. Some reproducibility challenges stem from documentation gaps in the literature, while others reflect computational and verification constraints within our framework — such as the
\delta
= 0.35 reproducibility threshold and the omission of run-level variance aggregation — which balance practical feasibility with evaluation rigor. Future work should address these design choices by incorporating variance estimation and expanding the method registry as new approaches emerge. We invite the community to adopt
RecourseBench
as a unified development and evaluation platform, contributing new methods, datasets, and evaluation criteria to strengthen reproducibility standards in algorithmic recourse research. In doing so, we hope to establish a shared reference that keeps pace with the rapid expansion of the field and provides practitioners with a reliable, continuously updated guide for method selection in real-world deployments.

A.1
Architecture in details

The benchmark is driven by a YAML-configured central
Experiment

Abstraction Layers & Interfaces.

Each component in the five main layers corresponds to a dedicated ABC. Interactions are strictly limited to well-defined public interfaces of the ABCs. Most of the public methods are fixed and only a small part needs to be implemented when introducing new components. By design, the minimal interfaces needed to be implemented are shown in
Figure 4

When adding a new component, the number of interfaces that need to be implemented is reduced to as few as one or two, greatly lowering the burden of understanding and implementation while still supporting a complete method training and inference workflow.

Rationals behind the design of
DatasetObject
: Compared with frameworks for other tasks such as text or image classification, algorithm recourse framework present a unique challenge: lots of recourse papers are working with tabular data, which requires a lot of metadata and many different ways of data processing. This scenario is unlike image or text modality. The former is mainly composed of dense pixels, barely with metadata; the latter is mainly composed of sparse tokens, usually does not need extra metadata as well. That’s the reason we designed DatasetObject as the self-contained message among components.

Except for
ModelObject
embedded inside
MethodObject
,
DatasetObject
is the only thing to be passed between components. Input is a processed DatasetObject and output is a new DatasetObject as well. They contain all the metadata needed for following operations and are automatically packed or unpacked by pre-built functions inside components. This is beneficial for compatibility check and keeps each component independent. To prevent undesirable changes to DatasetObject,
freeze()
function is introduced, after which, no changes can be applied to the object, ensuring re-usability and overall experiment reproducibility.

To avoid other components being altered undesirably, vital internal states are protected. These internal members are copied before returning. All these privilege control features and pre-defined in abstract class and do not need developers to intervene, while all core methods to be implemented are marked as abstract, enforcing a rigid yet clear development guideline for subclass implementations.

Pluggability & No Hard Coupling.

As shown in the interface design, the system operates with minimal assumptions about the underlying algorithms. What’s more, components are dynamically plugged into the pipeline using a centralized
@register
decorator. The
Experiment
workflow queries this global registry at runtime to instantiate classes defined in the YAML file. Consequently, there is zero hard-coded catalogs, enabling developers to change only one place when adding new components. This greatly reduces omissions and lowers the likelihood of merge conflicts. As a bonus, faulty components can be modified or purged cleanly without affecting anything else.

Compatibility check is achieved by flags inside components. And auxiliary data can be all put into DatasetObject to avoid workflow breach. So there’s no hard-coded dependency between two components.

Scalability Proof.

The architecture’s viability is empirically demonstrated by its ability to effortlessly scale. By adhering to the isolated design and dynamic registry, the framework successfully supports 28 recourse methods.

Appendix B
Reproduction Delta

As defined in
section 4
, we calculate the symmetric relative error by averaging the percentage difference between the reproduced metrics and the originally reported ones.
Because of this, the chosen tolerance threshold,
\delta
, directly determines how strict our reproducibility claims are.
A threshold of
\delta=0
means the results perfectly match the original paper, while increasing the
\delta
allows for more variance between the reported and reproduced values.
To show how sensitive this metric is and to transparently report the current state of reproducibility, we have ran a sweep across different values of
\delta
, as shown in
Figure 2
.

For our main evaluations, we establish a baseline threshold of
\delta=0.35
.
We select this broader tolerance specifically to account for the mathematical vulnerability of unweighted averages to extreme outliers.
Because our current evaluation treats all metrics equally, a severe mismatch in a single metric can significantly skew the overall average.
Rather than discarding these outliers to artificially lower the threshold, we maintain a higher
\delta
to ensure all empirical deviations are transparently included.
While future frameworks might adopt a weighted average that prioritizes core algorithmic goals over secondary metrics, we avoid this approach for now.
Manually deciding which metrics matter most introduces subjective bias, and defining a universally "fair" weighting system across so many different recourse methods is a significant challenge.
Until a clear consensus emerges from the community, retaining an unweighted average with a broader, inclusive threshold best preserves the objective neutrality of our benchmark.

Appendix C
Per-Method Reproducibility Logs

Reproducing published results in algorithmic recourse is subject to a compounding set of barriers. The most common ones are proprietary or non-restorable artifacts and unclear documentation. And even in the best scenario that an official implementation exists and hyperparameters are specified, exact numerical reproduction may remain elusive due to sources of non-determinism that are rarely reported in published work.
Specifically, pseudo-random number sequences are sensitive to execution order; even with an identical seed, changes in the order in which modules are executed can produce divergent trajectories through the random state, leading to materially different outputs.
Hardware heterogeneity introduces a further confound, as floating-point operator implementations differ across CPUs, GPUs, and other accelerators, yielding numerically distinct results from identical code.
Finally, software stack versions, particularly deep learning frameworks such as PyTorch, can alter low-level operator behavior across releases, making results obtained under one environment non-transferable to another.
These factors, individually subtle and collectively compounding, constitute a structural reproducibility challenge.
The reproducibility level assigned to each method is summarized in
Table

1
and the following notes document the specific barriers encountered during the methods’ reproduction, which generally categorized in three levels.

Impossible to reproduce.

Full reproduction is impossible when essential artifacts are proprietary, unavailable, or non-restorable, including datasets, indispensable implementation details and explanations and so on. There are also some works that do not provide reproducible metrics.

Substantially harder, but not impossible.

Reproduction becomes considerably more difficult when artifacts are missing or incomplete, experimental configurations are undocumented, or key details are only partially specified.
These include incomplete hyperparameters, missing model weights, absent or ambiguous official code, vague descriptions in the paper, unspecified hardware type, and unreported software versions.
And even when the seeds are reported, they doe not guarantee identical results, since pseudo-random sequences might not match execution order.
In addition, when results are reported only in plots without tables or numerical values, reproduction can only rely on approximate visual estimation.

Limitations on our side.

Some barriers arise from practical constraints on our side, including commercial software dependencies, prohibitive computational cost due to limited hardware resources, and framework incompatibilities that remain to be addressed in future work.
