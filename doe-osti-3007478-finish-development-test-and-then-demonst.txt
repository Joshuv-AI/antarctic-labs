1. Executive Summary

Uranium mononitride (UN) has emerged as a promising candidate for advanced nuclear reactor
fuels, particularly for high-temperature and high-power applications such as sodium-cooled fast
reactors [1, 2], space power systems, and TRISO particle fuel designs. Its higher uranium den-
sity increases reactivity while the superior thermal conductivity compared to UO2 helps reduce
centerline temperature and mitigate the risk of fuel melting under both normal and accidental op-
erating conditions. These advantages offer the potential for increased fuel efficiency, improved
safety margins, and greater reactor design flexibility. However, the deployment of UN has been
limited by gaps in the mechanistic understanding of its in-reactor behavior, particularly regard-
ing fission gas release (FGR) and swelling. This is especially true for transient conditions, as
well as understanding of the impact of chemistry on performance of the fuel: Early irradiations
indicated a strong dependence on chemistry for the swelling and fission gas release response
in nitride (and the very similar carbide) fuels [3–5], with the so-called “European” fuel which
typically had > 2000 ppm O2 swelling and release , vs. the cleaner “US” fuels which had on the
order of 500 ppm O2 [5].

Recent multiscale modeling efforts have begun to unravel the mechanisms underpinning fission
gas swelling in UN. Notably, Rizk et al. [6] developed a physics-based swelling and gas release
model for UN (UNSIFGRS, based on the SIFGRS model for UO2 ) within the BISON fuel per-
formance code [7], incorporating inputs from lower-length-scale simulations (density functional
theory and molecular dynamics), cluster dynamics (Centipede code), and continuum-scale mod-
els (BISON). This work revealed that the “breakaway swelling” transition is driven by a shift in
the dominant self-diffusion mechanism (from a low-temperature irradiation-induced regime to a
high-temperature, equilibrium regime). This transition enables more rapid growth of dislocation-
associated bubbles, accelerating swelling rates and elevating the risk of fuel-cladding mechanical
interaction (FCMI). The model also demonstrated the importance of vacancy diffusion, particu-
larly at grain boundaries, for predicting the onset of gas release through bubble interconnection.
The results obtained are in very good agreement with experimental measurements.

In the last year, significant upgrades have been made to the full-suite of multi-scale, mechanistic
modeling tools that are used to predict fuel performance of UN. Through the use of this mech-
anistic, multi-scale approach, we have been able to perform chemistry-aware, steady-state and
transient UN fuel performance simulations. This required extensive updates to all of the steps of
the simulation, each of which is co-dependent on each other.

Starting with the lower-length-scale information, we have implemented newly available density

functional theory (DFT) calculations for defect behavior in UN. This refined model provides
significant updates to the previous parameters that were derived using empirical potentials. In
addition, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations on UN were completed to generate fracture
energies of nitride fuel to use in the microcracking transient fission gas release model.

The two primary codes used for fuel behavior under irradiation, Centipede and BISON, received
updates to support more advanced modeling of UN fuel to capture chemistry dependence and
transient response. Enabled by the new lower-length-scale data and code improvements, new
predictions of UN fuel performance were executed using the same assessments developed last
year. In particular, the stoichiometry (i.e., chemistry) dependence of UN swelling was deter-
mined to be due to enhanced diffusion of defects in hyper-stoichiometric fuel. In addition,
transient behavior fission gas release of UN was explored using the microcracking model, show-
ing that for similar reactor conditions, UN releases more transient fission gas than UO2 relative
to it’s steady state release, but with still much less total release.

Ultimately, the advancements made here have enabled, for the first time, oxygen dependence to
be mechanistically linked to performance, and transient behavior of UN to be estimated. While
this type of information is important to predict the behavior of uranium nitride fuel under irra-
diation, there are a number of missing factors that should be explored next. First, the solubility
of oxygen in UN should be explored both computationally and experimentally, especially given
its importance in keeping the swelling and fission gas release in UN to a minimum. Second,
with the ability now to couple BISON and Centipede directly, the Centipede dislocation model
should be added to the BISON simulations to capture the behavior of dislocations at high tem-
peratures and high burnups [8]. Finally, the BISON simulations are enabled by the quasi-static
approximation forced by the SIFGRS model [6]. This approximation holds for most steady state
conditions, but will ultimately hinder high temperature, high burnup, and transient simulations.
Methods of relaxing the quasi-static approximation, including potential SIFGRS replacement,
should be explored.

2. Introduction

Uranium mononitride (UN) has emerged as a promising candidate for advanced nuclear reactor
fuels, particularly for high-temperature and high-power applications such as sodium-cooled fast
reactors [1, 2], space power systems, and TRISO particle fuel designs. Its higher uranium den-
sity increases reactivity while the superior thermal conductivity compared to UO2 helps reduce
centerline temperature and mitigate the risk of fuel melting under both normal and accidental
operating conditions. These advantages offer the potential for increased fuel efficiency, im-
proved safety margins, and greater reactor design flexibility. However, the deployment of UN
has been limited by gaps in the mechanistic understanding of its in-reactor behavior, particularly
regarding fission gas release (FGR) and swelling.

Fission gas behavior represents one of the most critical, yet least predictable, aspects of nuclear
fuel performance. It is established that after fission gas atoms are produced within the bulk
lattice by fission events, their low solubility in the fuel drives them to precipitate and form intra-
granular gas bubbles [9–11]. A portion of these trapped fission gas atoms is re-solved into the
lattice by irradiation [12, 13]. That repeated trapping-resolution cycle allows a growing frac-
tion of gas atoms to reach grain-boundaries but with an effective diffusivity that is modulated
by the relative rate of trapping and resolution. Upon reaching the grain boundary they form
inter-granular bubbles [12,14]. These bubbles exhibit an important growth due to the absorption
of grain-boundary vacancies, driven by the over-pressure induced by gas atoms [14, 15]. Both
types of bubbles are responsible for swelling [11, 16], although the relative importance for the
different bubble types is sensitive to the fuel type and arises from the different diffusion coeffi-
cients and resolution rates for different materials [6]. As inter-granular bubbles keep growing,
they ultimately form a percolation network that facilitates the release of fission gas out of the
fuel [9,16,17]. In UN, swelling due to bubbles within the grain tends to be more pronounced than
in UO2 , in part due to faster gas diffusion rates and the formation of larger dislocation-associated
bubble populations [6]. A particularly concerning feature observed in UN is the so-called break-
away swelling—a rapid increase in intra-granular bubble growth above a critical temperature,
which has been experimentally observed but is not yet fully understood [6].

Early irradiations indicated a strong dependence on chemistry for the swelling and fission gas
release response in nitride fuels, as well as the very similar carbide fuels [3–5]. In fact, the
more extensive database of results for carbide fuels showed fission gas release has a very strong
dependence from the so-called “European” fuel which typically had > 2000 ppm O2 , vs. the
cleaner “US” fuels which had on the order of 500 ppm O2 (Fig. 2.1) [5]. In addition, there
is evidence that hypo-stoichiometric UN experienced extensive swelling, behavior that is often

attributed to free uranium. While there are qualitative descriptions of how oxygen changes the
defect behavior in nitride (and carbide) fuel, a fundamental explanation between the performance
of the fuel and oxygen content has not been established beyond a lower limit of 500-650 ppm
oxygen during fabrication.

Recent multiscale modeling efforts have begun to unravel the mechanisms underpinning fission
gas swelling in UN. Notably, Rizk et al. [6] developed a physics-based swelling and gas release
model for UN (UNSIFGRS, based on the SIFGRS model for UO2 ) within the BISON fuel per-
formance code [7], incorporating inputs from lower-length-scale simulations (density functional
theory and molecular dynamics), cluster dynamics (Centipede code), and continuum-scale mod-
els (BISON). This work revealed that the breakaway swelling transition is driven by a shift in
the dominant self-diffusion mechanism (from a low-temperature irradiation-induced regime to a
high-temperature, equilibrium regime). This transition enables more rapid growth of dislocation-
associated bubbles, accelerating swelling rates and elevating the risk of fuel-cladding mechanical
interaction (FCMI). The model also demonstrated the importance of vacancy diffusion, particu-
larly at grain boundaries, for predicting the onset of gas release through bubble interconnection.
The results obtained are in very good agreement with experimental measurements.

While this initial work indicated that the swelling response of UN could be captured using
multiscale, mechanistic modeling, there were several key deficiencies. First, through hand-
calibration of the model which used separate-effects swelling data as a guide, it was determined
that the fuel had to be in a state along the extreme nitrogen-rich edge of the phase diagram. While
this allowed for good comparison to data, it left no room to explore the chemistry dependence
of the fuel that was known to exist due to the presence of O2 and deviations in stoichiometry. In
particular, since the fuel material can not accommodate any more nitrogen defects, any additional
oxygen would drive the formation of sesqui-nitride fuel, rather than defective UN. While this
would have it’s own consequences, the exceedingly small amounts of a U2 N3 phase would have
a much smaller impact than could be accounted for.

 6

In addition to the difficulty in capturing the chemistry dependence of UN fuel, the state-of-the-
art of BISON for UN as of last year did not address the behavior of UN fuel under some of the
expected transient conditions. Fortunately, advancements in micro-cracking of the UO2 can be
applied here: a microcracking model was developed for UO2 that improves the mechanistic de-
scription of microcracking at grain boundaries due to intergranular bubbles [18, 19]. The model
implemented several lower length scale models into BISON: i) an improved fission gas equa-
tion of state, ii) a microcracking pressure threshold, and iii) a model to account for the number
of vacancies that assist fission gas diffusion. These models were implemented such that dur-
ing temperature transients high pressure bubbles can drive grain boundary microcracking - the
mechanism thought to be responsible for transient fission gas release [20]. This improved the
description of bubble pressure and size evolution in UO2 , particularly for cooler regions of the
pellet. The better accuracy of the equation of state at high bubble pressures and the use of the
microcracking threshold allowed the creation of microcracks in response to temperature tran-
sients to be predicted in a way that accounts for the underlying microstructural state associated
with grain boundary bubbles. Conversely, previous models for transient gas release associated
with microcracking during transients were empirical in nature [20], relying on extensive UO2
fission gas release data. Recent work, by Cooper et al., extended the mechanistic microcracking
model to predict transient gas release in UO2 [21] which can readily be applied to other fuels
where a baseline mechanistic fission gas model already exists, such as UN.

In this work, we report on significant upgrades to the full-suite of multi-scale, mechanistic mod-
eling tools that are used to predict fuel performance of UN. Starting with the lower-length-scale
information, we have implemented newly available density functional theory (DFT) calculations
for defect behavior in UN. This refined model provides significant updates to the previous pa-
rameters that were derived using empirical potentials. In addition, molecular dynamics (MD)
simulations on UN were completed to generate fracture energies of nitride fuel to use in the
microcracking transient fission gas release model.

Along with the updates to the data inputs, the two codes Centipede [22] and BISON [7] received
updates to support more advanced modeling of UN fuel to capture chemistry dependence and
transient response. Extensive changes to the cluster dynamics code Centipede were completed
this year, including a new model to capture stoichiometry dependence of the fuel while respect-
ing phase boundaries, inclusion of the new lower-length-scale UN defect database, and internal
algorithmic changes to make Centipede more robust. In BISON, The UO2 microcracking model
was adapted to UN, along with some general improvements to the BISON repository to sup-
port UN calculations. Lastly, several BISON/Centipede coupling schemes were explored, both
to support the work here (especially the chemistry dependence) as well as to inform coupling
techniques that should be adopted for future uranium nitride development.

Enabled by the new lower-length-scale data and code improvements, new predictions of UN
fuel performance were executed using the same assessments developed last year. In particular,
the stoichiometry (i.e., chemistry) dependence of UN swelling was determined to be due to
enhanced diffusion of defects in hyper-stoichiometric fuel. This behavior can be connected
directly to the solubility of oxygen in UN, i.e., to the 500 ppm limit typically set by modern

manufacturers of UN (see Fig. 2.1). In addition, transient behavior fission gas release of UN
was explored using the microcracking model, showing that for similar reactor conditions, UN
releases more transient fission gas than UO2 relative to it’s steady state release, but with still
much less total release.

3.1. Lower-Length Scale Data Improvements

3.1.1. Updated UN data

The model developed as part of last year’s milestone, as reported in the UN-BISON paper [6],
utilized a data set from Cooper et al. [23]. It included six xenon clusters and nine self-defect
clusters that were described using the Kocevski empirical potential (EP) [24]. This initial study
allowed for a rapid parameterization of the relevant defects, enabling a BISON model to be
developed informed by these lower-length-scale parameters. However, since EP’s in general
have a hard time describing defect energies, the rapid parameterization was always considered an
initial estimate of the actual defect behavior. Since then, a more detailed parameter set describing
UN defects has been developed by Schneider et al., independent of NEAMS [25]. This new
dataset reparameterized the same xenon defects, but included nine more vacancy and interstitial
clusters and four more xenon clusters.

While some of the new parameters were similar between the Schneider and Cooper datasets, if
not exactly the same, there were key differences, especially in the xenon defect behavior: the
activation barrier of the xenon interstitial cluster was found by Schneider et al. to be much higher
than the value calculated previously (1.38 vs. 0.4 eV). Since this defect was found to result in an
irradiation enhancement in the diffusion (the so-called D2 region), by suppressing its mobility,
this region of enhanced diffusivity occurred well below the athermal D3 region, and thus did not
impact the net xenon diffusivity. Consequently, the net xenon diffusivity can be described using
the thermal equilibrium, D1 , diffusivity along with the D3 diffusivity.

One of the concerns with the original work by Cooper et al. was that by not including larger
clusters in the dataset, the true irradiation behavior of U-, N-, and Xe-diffusion could not be
captured. The need for larger clusters was supported by similar work in UO2 , that showed larger
clusters, especially xenon clusters, were necessary to capture the behavior of defects [22, 26].
By including more self defects, Schneider et al. were able to conclude that the original set of
defects studied by Cooper et al. was sufficient.

3.1.2. UN Fracture Strength Calculations

To support the transient microcracking BISON simulation, the model needs to be parametrized
for UN fuel. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were used to predict the fracture energy
of grain boundaries in UN. The fracture energy describes the energetic cost of cleaving a grain
boundary to form two free surfaces and is calculated as the difference in total energy between
the minimized grain boundary configuration and the corresponding open surface configuration,
normalized by the grain boundary area (A):

Here, EGB, open is the total energy of the system after introducing a cleavage plane (creating two
free surfaces), and EGB is the energy of the minimized grain boundary structure. The factor of 2
accounts for the creation of two surfaces upon fracture.

The grain boundary fracture energy is a critical input for higher-length-scale models, yet it is
often difficult to obtain experimentally due to challenges in isolating individual grain boundaries.
Consequently, lower-length-scale modeling approaches such as MD simulations play a key role
in predicting this property. Notably, the fracture energies reported here are not directly known
from experiment and are typically inferred or approximated through computational or empirical
means.

All simulations were carried out using the Large-Scale Atomic/Molecular Massively Parallel
Simulator (LAMMPS) [27] package. Interatomic forces between the N and U atoms were de-
scribed using the Kocevski potential set [24], which consists of a pairwise Buckingham poten-
tial, combined with the Finnis-Sinclair many-body Embedded Atom Method (EAM) potential.
In this work, the fracture energies were computed at 0 K using static energy minimization,
thereby neglecting temperature effects. These aspects will be addressed in future work as well
as investigating other grain boundary types, e.g., twist and asymmetrical boundaries.

Two symmetric tilt grain boundary structures are considered in this work: the Σ5 (001) [310]
grain boundary, in which one grain is rotated by 36.87◦ about the [310] axis relative to the other
and the interface lies in the (001) crystallographic plane (Fig. 3.1a); and the Σ9 (110) [11̄4] grain
boundary, formed by a relative rotation of the two grains about the [11̄4] axis, with the boundary
plane oriented along (110).

To identify the most stable atomic configuration for each grain boundary, a γ-surface search
is performed. This involves systematically translating one grain relative to the other along the

be 0.95 J/m2 [19], also using MD simulations. This is likely due to the strength of the Coulom-

electronic structure.

3.2.1. Chemistry Impact

Previous implementations of Centipede simulations of defect concentrations in uranium nitride
have captured the impact of chemistry by providing an analytical expression for a nitrogen gas
partial pressure that traces the UN-U2 N3 phase boundary, or the UN-U phase boundary (see

3.2.2. Log Transform

As the usefulness of Centipede continues to grow, a natural increase in complexity of the cluster
dynamics simulation follows. This can either be due to increasing the total set of defects tracked
(as was the case in moving from the Cooper to Schneider datasets, Section 3.1.1) or by tracking
the state of defects through a temperature and fission rate profile that varies with time. As a
consequence of using Newton’s nonlinear solving techniques, it is possible to push the interme-
diate solve state into nonphysical regions, for example, pushing one of the concentrations into a
negative value. Due to the natural log contributions in the free energy formulation, this leads to
NaN’s (non a number) errors introduced into the residual, and consequently, cascading timestep
reductions that leads to either termination of the simulation as it reaches below the minimum
timestep, or a simulation wall time that is prohibitively long. In order to combat this, Centipede
was modified behind to transform all free variables into natural log values of the actual concen-
trations, ci . To do this, we defined a new variable, ui = ln(ci ) which guarantees ci > 0 for all
ui . The governing equations must then be reformulated in terms of ui : Given a system of ODEs
over a set of concentrations, c:

While the kernel-level code implementation is relatively easy, the entire Centipede code-base

While mathematically such concentrations are compatible for the residual formulations, the
std::exp(u i) method provided by c++ will return 0 for ui values much lower than -500. These
zeros can then be passed into the residual formulations, resulting in NaN’s, and failure of the
simulations. To prevent this, a new “protected” exp formulation was implemented in Centipede
that limits the return value of the exponential to exp(-400). This ensures that zero’s do not
make their way into the residual formulation, ensuring smooth convergence of the simulation.

3.2.3. Time dependence

As discussed in a previous milestone [8], we implemented a new convergence method into
MOOSE to more accurately capture a “relative” convergence tolerance. This method was im-
plemented in the ReferenceResidualProblem, and utilizes the sum of the absolute value of
the residuals to determine a “reference” residual to which each nonlinear residual is compared.
Since this initial implementation, this method has been adapted in more Centipede problems, as
well as other MOOSE-based simulations (e.g., BISON). In particular, we have refined the PETSc
options to more effectively utilize this new convergence method, such as the use of MUMPS
(MUltifrontal Massively Parallel sparse direct Solver [30]), variables scaling, and appropriately
defined tolerances.

We have found that this method can accurately capture the time evolution of defects in a given
simulation, providing insight into more realistic behavior of the net diffusivity of fission gas.
For example, in UO2 , it was found that at lower temperatures (i.e., fuel surface temperatures)
the defects in the fuel reached a pseudo-steady-state behavior within the first month of opera-
tion that only transitioned to the true steady-state defect concentrations after 100’s of years of
operation.

To ensure physical values of diffusivities are passed to BISON, the time-evolution of a Centipede
at high and low temperatures are plotted as a function of time in Fig. 3.4. Due to the faster
mobility of the defects, it’s clear to see that even at relatively low temperatures (600◦ C), the
defects rapidly reach steady state behavior (∼10 days). This proves that the steady-state defect
concentrations are the appropriate formulations to pass from Centipede to BISON.

3.3. BISON Improvements

3.3.1. UNSIFGRS Updates

Last year’s milestone (and subsequent publication [6]) saw the completion of a UN-version of
the SIFGRS fission gas release and swelling model. A large portion of this work was centered
on reviving a dislocation swelling model introduced some years before [31] that had since been
underutilized. In the year that followed, changes to the SIFGRS base classes (which all fuel
types inherit from) led to an inadvertent miscalculation of dislocation swelling. Once identified,
a fix was employed that combined the two separate methods that were utilized to compute intra-
granular bubble radius sizes (i.e., bulk and dislocation bubbles) into a single coherent method.
While the technical details of this work will be omitted here, it is worth mentioning that the
utilization of a streamlined assessment database may have prevented introduction of the bug.
It also highlights that careful code review, maintenance, and background improvements are a
necessary supporting part of each milestone.

3.4. Centipede/BISON Coupling

The log transform and transient Centipede modifications discussed in Section 3.2 primarily en-
able implementation and testing of several new ways to couple Centipede and BISON. Tradition-
ally, Centipede has been used to calculate the steady-state behavior of defects under irradiation
using single-point calculations at a variety of temperatures and fission rates. These results are
then compiled in approximate Arrhenius-like analytical correlations for implementation into BI-
SON simulations, both for fission gas (i.e., xenon) diffusivity and self-diffusion. These values
are utilized in the SIFGRS algorithm in BISON to determine the growth of intra- and inter-
granular bubbles, and ultimately, fission gas release and swelling.

While this workflow proved useful to incorporate the lower-length-scale informed diffusivity in
engineering scale calculations, it overlooked several complications. First, the utilization of an
analytical fit worked well at high and low temperatures where the diffusivity is dominated by
either the thermal equilibrium concentration or athermal diffusivity of defects, but it missed the
finer details of the irradiation enhanced diffusion region. As will be discussed in Section 4.1,

 18

the temperature dependence on the diffusivity becomes even more complicated as specific non-
stoichiometry chemistry is considered.

Second, as mechanistic multiscale uncertainty quantification [33] becomes more prevalent in
BISON simulations, the ability to generate new correlations for use in BISON becomes essential
to enable uncertainty propagation from the lower-length-scale parameters up to engineering scale
measurements.

In order to prepare for a more streamlined integration of Centipede results into BISON, two
new methods of incorporating the two codes have been explored here, table lookup and direct
coupling. These new methods are compared to the previous method of providing an analytical
expression for BISON simulations, as well as compared against potential upcoming methods
such as machine-learning surrogate creation and integration.

3.4.1. Table Lookup

The first new method implemented this year to improve Centipede and BISON coupling was
table lookup. This method first required running Centipede in a variety of conditions, temper-
atures, and fission rates to collect the requisite data. For example, running Centipede with five
different stoichiometries, for 20 different temperatures, and three different fission rates. Then, a
single comma-separated value file (csv) for each stoichiometry is generated with column names
corresponding to the fission rate, and rows corresponding to the inverse temperature (here, typi-
cally 104 / temperature). The respective cells are filled with the natural log of the diffusivity. This
ensures that when the values are linearly interpolated in BISON, the natural Arrhenius behavior
is captured. This is repeated for both xenon- and self-diffusion, and for any other deviations in
chemistry or lower-length-scale information.

Once the necessary Centipede simulations are run and diffusivity csv files compiled, they are in-
corporated into a BISON simulation through the use of a new method, PiecewiseDiffusivity.
This “function” utilizes much of capabilities in the MOOSE class PiecewiseBilinear, along
with some custom modifications to handle the temperature and diffusivity value transforms cor-
rectly. Once implemented, the diffusivity is then passed to SIFGRS via the gas diffusivity func-
tion options implemented last year.

While this method allows for a much more nuanced diffusivity to be incorporated in BISON,
it still requires a priori compilation of the relevant Centipede output in order to be useful. This
method works for most applications, but will be limited if the lower-length scale parameters need
to be sampled more extensively, as required during calibration or surrogate creation. In addition,
this method only allows for one-way coupling, e.g., time-dependence is inherently absent in
the Centipede formulations. This may become important as the evolution of sinks – primarily
bubbles and dislocations – may significantly impact the diffusivity of defects, especially in the
case of transient simulations.

 19

3.4.2. Direct Coupling

The most flexible coupling technique available for Centipede and BISON is direct coupling dur-
ing execution of BISON. This is enabled by the MultiApps system already in place in MOOSE,
and requires only that both Centipede and BISON are compiled using the exact same local
MOOSE distribution. By using Transfers, fission rate and temperature are passed from BI-
SON to Centipede, Centipede is executed, and then the self- and xenon-diffusivities are passed
back to BISON. This communication can be done either at the beginning of the time step or it-
eratively during the simulation. While this ensures that the most accurate diffusivities are being
computed for BISON, it does make the simulation more computationally expensive.

Through the enabling work discussed in Section 3.2, we were able to run simulations with direct
coupling. Ultimately, such complications and computational expense was not required to explore
the chemistry behavior and transient response for this work. Clearly, as BISON becomes more
sophisticated with its use of defect populations – as will be the case when bubble sizes (and thus
pressures) will eventually be computed solely on the net flux of vacancies and interstitials to
the bubbles – this direct coupling technique will become more relevant. It should be noted that
no optimization of Centipede was performed here, which could help speed up the simulation
significantly through the omission of irrelevant cluster tracking or smarter computational and
memory optimization.

3.4.3. Coupling via Centipede Surrogate

The advancements of machine-learning (M.L.) and artificial intelligence (A.I.) generated surro-
gates of advanced models is opening up the possibility of using complex, mechanistic, multiscale
simulations within reasonable simulation times [33–35]. Such surrogates have been built already
for Centipede simulations with varying complexity from state-variable surrogates to models with
sensitivity to the underlying lower-length-scale parameters [35]. Centipede surrogates for UN
are already under development to calibrate lower-length-scale parameters using BISON simula-
tion results [34]. The built-in “stochastic tools” module in MOOSE [36] may help enable this
even further. While each surrogate is extremely computationally expensive to set up and train,
once the surrogate exists, sampling during a BISON simulation is nearly as fast as an analytical
expression. This is especially helpful when performing uncertainty quantification, for which
creation of a surrogate would result in far fewer total model evaluations than utilizing the native
Centipede model.

4.1. Centipede Results

The new uranium nitride defect dataset was utilized in the typical Centipede simulation used
for UN fuel at 1019 fsn/m3 /s and typical UN defect sink concentrations. Some comparisons for

show that while the U and N diffusivities are almost exactly the same, the xenon diffusivity

cussion in Section 3.1.1. In fact, for N-rich conditions, Xe diffusivity can be treated as just the
thermal-equilibrium diffusivity added to the athermal diffusivity, a completely analytically de-
rived value. The elimination of the D2 region was supported in last year’s milestone that showed
the irradiation enhancement for xenon diffusivity led to poor comparison with the swelling data.
Although the Schneider dataset was not complete in time for last year’s milestone, preliminary
results provided justification in removing the irradiation enhancement. See Appendix A for a
more complete comparison of defects.

4.2.2. Transient gas model

Fig. 4.8 shows the fission gas release for UN and UO2 . The results consist of two contributions
to fission gas release: i) diffusional release that dominates at high temperature and at constant
temperature (smooth release), and ii) microcrack-driven release that contributes only during the
temperature ramps (sharp release). Several trends are qualitatively the same for both fuels:

 • Steady-state gas release is higher at high temperatures, as expected due to it being domi-
 nated by diffusion processes that follow Arrhenius laws.

 • In both fuels, gas release during transients is significant only if the fuel was irradiated at
 low temperatures (1000-1100 K) preceding the transient. This is expected from previous
 work [6,18] that showed high pressure bubbles only form at low temperatures. Conversely,
 high temperatures facilitate pressure relief through grain boundary vacancy diffusion dur-
 ing steady state. This means that, unless the fuel was irradiated at low temperatures, the
 thermal expansion of the gas is insufficient during the transient for bubble pressures to
 exceed the microcracking threshold.

 • Transient gas release is highly dependent on burnup. Both fuel types exhibit no tFGR
 during the ramp initiated at 1.1% burnup, but have significant tFGR release during the
 transients at 3.1% and 5.7% burnup.

Despite a number of qualitative similarities between the fuel types, several quantitative differ-
ences are identified that result from the different microstructures of UO2 and UN:

 31

 • Steady state fission gas release is far higher in UO2 than in UN. In UN, significant trapping
 of gas at dislocation bubbles within the grain interior prevents gas from arriving at grain
 boundaries and limits the growth of intergranular bubbles that contribute to fission gas
 release through percolation.

 • For the transient at 3.1% burnup with a base irradiation temperature of 1100 K, around
 8% gas release occurs for both UN and UO2 . However, this represents a more significant
 transient contribution to overall fission gas release for UN, due to the lower diffusional
 release in UN.

 • For the transient at 5.7% burnup and with low base irradiation temperatures, UO2 has
 a dramatically higher transient fission gas release compared to UN. As was the case for
 steady-state release, this is attributed to the importance of dislocation bubbles in UN that
 limit the availability of gas at grain boundaries to contribute to transient gas release.

 • Interestingly, for a base irradiation of 1200 K in UN, there is no transient gas release for
 any burnup. This indicates that grain boundary diffusivity is sufficient to suppress the
 bubble pressure making it difficult to reach the microcracking threshold; however, it is
 insufficient to promote enough intergranular bubble growth for interconnection to occur
 during steady state.

Further coupling of the transient fission gas release model with advancements in a dislocation
Centipede model could help refine the temperature dependence identified here. Ultimately, an
idealized operational temperature could be defined that results in minimal fission gas release for
both steady state and transient operational conditions.

5. Discussion

5.1. Impacts of Chemistry

By combining the new DFT lower-length-scale defect information, with the ability to track sto-
ichiometry enabled in Centipede, and the streamlined table-lookup coupling enabled in BISON,
we can now perform stoichiometry-sensitive fission gas release and swelling calculations for
uranium nitride. Furthermore, by virtue of the mechanistic modeling nature enabled by BISON,
we can now show that the strong variation in swelling and fission gas in the historical irradiation
database can most likely be traced back to the orders of magnitude difference in diffusivities due
to very small variations in stoichiometry, at least for the hyper-stoichiometric cases since we are
not capturing the impact of free uranium on the total swelling in hypo-stoichiometric fuel.

Fig. 5.1 shows the impact of stoichiometry on the total gaseous swelling for the ANP6 3.2%
FIMA irradiation. Since this plots the total swelling, it includes contributions from bulk and
grain boundary bubbles, as well as dislocation bubbles. While this plot shows swelling due to
hyper- and hypo-stoichiometric conditions, it’s clear that the hyper-stoichiometric fuels result
in higher fission-gas-driven swelling as non-stoichiometry is increased. This is especially rel-
evant at higher temperatures above 1,400 K where swelling becomes markedly more prevalent
as the fuel approaches the N-rich conditions, a consequence of the two-phase boundary between
uranium mononitride and the sesqui-nitride phases (see Fig. 3.2). This can be more clearly ob-
served by plotting the transition temperature of the UN/U2 N3 phase boundary as a function of
non-stoichiometry in Fig. 5.2, which shows that y traces the N-rich boundary at low tempera-
tures, and only eventually maintains the prescribed overall stoichiometry at higher temperatures,
representing a transition to the single phase.

Note, the total swelling is not shown above 30% (i.e., at higher temperatures), for which dis-
locations will likely anneal out at these higher temperatures, and thus the dislocation bubble
swelling will be smaller than the predictions provided here. Indications of a temperature in-
crease and drop in dislocations was explored in previous milestones [8], but was not included
here for simplicity.

Given this dependence of swelling on stoichiometry, we can now extend this correlation to the
chemistry state of the fabricated uranium nitride fuel. In particular, there has been clear evidence
that oxygen impurities in the fuel have a negative impact on swelling (see Section 2). There is
some evidence that oxygen dissolves in small amounts in the lattice (Blank cites approximately

top x-axis of Fig. 5.1.

In addition to oxygen, there are often limits placed on the total amount of carbon impurities.
Unlike oxygen, carbon has a high solubility in the uranium nitride, and will readily make carbo-
nitride fuels, a material that was often irradiated in the past [5]. While there is a potential for
fuel/cladding chemical interaction (FCCI), carbon impurities in the fuel will likely not result in
a direct change in stoichiometry, and thus swelling, although a full thermo-chemistry analysis of
the U-N-C-O system may provide a more definitive conclusion on this claim.

Fig. 5.1 indicates that in order to achieve the least amount of swelling, the oxygen impurity
content should be kept below the solubility of O2 in UN. However, the operational regime of
the fuel is also important. For example, if the fuel is expected to be irradiated at relatively cold
temperatures, it is unlikely that the samples need to be extremely pure since swelling is not high
regardless of oxygen effects. At relatively medium temperatures, an increase in swelling can be
expected for as more oxygen impurities are introduced (i.e., N-rich conditions). At high temper-
atures, the oxygen solubility may be high enough to accommodate any oxygen impurities, thus
not placing the fuel in an N-rich condition through the scavenging of U. In addition, a more care-
ful accounting of the dislocation structure must be considered – both from a computational and
experimental point of view – in order to determine the impact of dislocation bubble swelling.

Ultimately, a lower-length-scale-informed prediction with experimental validation is a key pa-
rameter to understanding the swelling behavior of uranium nitride. This new metric should
replace the standard standard 500 wppm requirement for all irradiations above 1100 K. No such
requirement on oxygen impurities are needed below 1100 K due to thermally activated gaseous
swelling. Similar to carbon, FCCI interactions may be possible due to increased oxygen.

5.2. Centipede/BISON coupling

By exploring some of coupling methods described in Section 3.4, some conclusions can be
drawn on the methods to be utilized going forward:

Method 1 – Analytical:
 First run Centipede for different temperatures and fission rates. Formulate analytical math-
 ematical expression for the diffusivity. Implement diffusivity either as hard-coded into
 BISON, or as input-file option.
 • Pros:

 35

 – Fast execution of BISON.
 – Extremely portable since correlations remain either in BISON repository or in-
 put file.
 – Does not require Centipede to run BISON.
 • Cons:
 – Requires Centipede to be evaluated at the relevant conditions a priori.
 – Analytical expression needs to be reformulated by hand for each new Centipede
 dataset.
 – Ability to reproduce true behavior of diffusivity is limited to analytical, and
 often simple expressions.
 – Limits free variable dependence, ultimately preventing uncertainty quantifica-
 tion.
 – Does not enable time-evolution due to BISON-level features such as sink strengths.
Method 2 – Lookup Table:
 First run Centipede for different temperatures and fission rates. Compile diffusivities into
 two-dimensional lookup tables as a function of temperature and fission rate. Import into
 BISON via the input file and perform linear interpolation between table values.
 • Pros:
 – Fast execution of BISON.
 – Does not require Centipede to run BISON.
 – Captures transition regions more accurately due to interpolation between data
 points.
 – Does not require hand-formulation of analytical expressions
 • Cons:
 – Requires Centipede to be evaluated at the relevant conditions a priori.
 – Does not enable time-evolution due to BISON-level features such as sink strengths.
 – Requires companion lookup tables with associated diffusivity values to be com-
 bined with input file.
Method 3 – Fully coupled:
 Use MultiApps to fully couple a Centipede and BISON simulation by passing fission rate
 and temperature (or any other variable) from BISON to Centipede, and self- and xenon-
 diffusivity from Centipede to BISON.
 • Pros:
 – Requires no a priori decision on the necessary Centipede conditions
 – Allows for more sophisticated coupling between BISON and Centipede calcu-
 lated values
 • Cons:
 – Computationally expensive, some of which can be optimized
Method 4 – Surrogate:

 36

 Perform thousands to millions different Centipede simulations to span the full parameter
 space. Develop M.L. or A.I. surrogate to sample directly from BISON.
 • Pros:
 – Very rapid sampling of mechanistically informed Centipede surrogate.
 • Cons:
 – Extensive Centipede simulation database to train models on.

In this year’s work, the need to capture the non-Arrhenius behavior introduced by the stoichiom-
etry enforcement in Centipede has already necessitated moving away from analytical fits to
lookup tables. Although Centipede and BISON were successfully coupled, the models were not
fully sensitive to time evolution of defects to necessitate the need for direct coupling. As a need
for time-evolved defects, – such as might be the case when treating over-pressurized bubbles
that may suppress interstitial defect absorption, or in cases where dislocation content is evolv-
ing due to point-defect absorption – direct coupling will be an enabling method to capture the
multi-scale behavior of fission gas.

Ultimately, surrogate development of the Centipede, or even the grain-level fission gas behav-
ior would truly enable rapid fuel performance simulations that utilize a truly multi-scale ap-
proach.

5.3. Uncertainty Quantification

It is critical from a regulation standpoint to quantify the uncertainty of the underlying ab-initio
results to attribute meaningful confidence intervals to the diffusivities [33]. To do so, a Bayesian
calibration strategy, using experimental measurements from separate effects testing and post-
irradiation examinations, can be used to compute the probability distributions of various mean-
ingful parameters, including DFT energetics and microstructure properties, such as the disloca-
tion line density. Finally, the forward propagation of these distributions leads to a mechanistic,
data-informed, and uncertainty-aware prediction of fission gas swelling in UN fuel.

Since this workflow is computationally expensive (and intractable for uncertainty quantifica-
tion purposes), surrogate models are necessary to replace the full modeling chain (from DFT to
BISON). Coupling strategies, such as those discussed in Section 3.4 can help provide a mean-
ingful model to generate data for the surrogates, but ultimately a rapid model is required for the
(sometimes) millions of simulations needed to cover the entire parameter phase-space.

Despite the computational challenges, the multiscale, mechanistic approach developed by NEAMS
makes uranium nitride fuel a natural candidate for exploration of uncertainty quantification to
help support future experiments or simply help design calculations of the fuel.

 37

5.4. Capturing Transient Behavior

One of the primary challenges in exploring this transient for nuclear fuels is the lack of extensive
transient irradiation data. This is even more difficult for nitride fuels for which transient data is
nearly absent. Fortunately, we can follow some of the model development for the more familiar
UO2 fuel, particularly in how the grain boundary fracture can impact transient fission gas release.
The motivation behind understanding grain boundary fracture behavior stems from the critical
role such processes play during transient scenarios in nuclear fuels such as during loss of coolant
accidents (LOCAs) and at high burnup structures (HBS) in the fuel pellet. In these regimes, fuel
fragmentation and micro-cracking at grain boundaries driven by over-pressurized fission gas
bubbles can lead to rapid fission gas release (FGR).

In UO2 , recent modeling efforts to capture transient fission gas release rely on accurate grain
boundary fracture energy predictions which are then used in pressure-threshold models predict-
ing bubble-induced micro-cracking. These predictions were shown to depend sensitively on
bubble size, temperature, and grain boundary coverage all variables influenced by the intrinsic
fracture toughness of the boundary. Given that the fracture energies for UN are significantly
lower compared to UO2 , a correspondingly lower threshold pressure for bubble induced fracture
under similar conditions is expected.

Ultimately, the transient simulations performed in this work show that, like chemistry depen-
dence, transient fission gas release will be a temperature dependent phenomena. For exam-
ple, if the fuel is expected to be irradiated at relatively cold temperatures, the pressurization
of grain-boundary bubbles places UN at a higher risk for transient fission gas release, with a
corresponding decrease in total fission gas release due to slower thermal diffusion. Conversely,
transient fission gas release will not be as prevalent at high irradiation temperatures due to more
mobile vacancies, keeping the grain-boundary bubble close to an equilibrium state. The temper-
ature dependence of transient fission gas release, which comes from the diffusivities, indicates a
chemistry sensitivity could also be expected, which will be explored in future work. The mecha-
nistic formulation of the diffusivities and transient fission gas release behavior provides a strong
tool for exploring the tradeoff space between normal and transient behavior of ceramic nuclear
fuel.

6. Conclusions

Through the use of a mechanistic, multi-scale approach, we have been able to perform chemistry-
aware, steady-state and transient UN fuel performance simulations. This required extensive
updates to each step of the work-flow: First, the lower-length-scale data-base, on which this work
stands upon, was updated to the latest DFT results on defect behavior in uranium nitride. While
the atomistic calculations were performed outside of NEAMS, the existing Centipede database
provided a framework for direct application of the new values. In particular, the utilization of
DFT allowed for a much more accurate accounting of defect energies and mobilities, resulting
in a much more accurate description of the uranium nitride system, and in particular, greater
confidence in the database to predict sensitivity to chemistry.

Next, several Centipede updates were performed. While some of this upgrade work is more
hidden, such as refactoring of the code to accept log-transformed variables and transient simula-
tions upgrades, these improvements were enabling factors for the extensive new dataset, as well
as supportive of future efforts in coupled BISON simulations in support of uncertainty quantifi-
cation. Most notably, a new stoichiometry model was introduced into Centipede which allowed
for a much more accurate tracking of a specified chemical state, rather than being forced to
two-phase boundaries.

Several improvements were performed at the engineering scale as well, most importantly, the
adaptation of the transient fission gas release model from UO2 to UN fuel. In addition, many
small improvements to the code base were performed as the work developed, both fixing of
bugs as well as small cleanups. While these updates are not always easily reportable, they are
necessary steps in achieving the outcomes of simulation work.

Finally, given the need for a more robust coupling between Centipede and BISON, several new
techniques were implemented and explored. This work utilized a new lookup-table method that
allowed for a much more accurate incorporation of Centipede-calculated diffusivities into BI-
SON. This was especially important to capture the non-Arrhenius behavior of the stoichiometry-
specific diffusivities needed to capture UN behavior as a function of temperature. In addition
to one-way coupling techniques, direct coupling between Centipede and BISON was performed
via built in MOOSE MultiApp methods. While this method proved easy to implement and pro-
vided the most flexibility, it also came with an unnecessary computational cost given the scope of
work performed here. This full coupling should be explored further, especially in the context of
optimization of Centipede, as it will become necessary as time-dependent Centipede simulations
become more relevant.

 39

These new features enabled several new BISON simulations. First, the change a strong de-
pendence of diffusivities on stoichiometry was shown to impact swelling significantly at higher
temperatures and higher non-stoichiometries. By making a couple assumptions, the connec-
tion to oxygen impurities can also be established, showing that only slight amounts of oxygen
above the solubility limit leads to much greater swelling. This type of behavior is a historically
observed, but previously proven trend.

Transient simulations were performed to show the comparative behavior of UN to UO2 . In
particular, it was shown that while UN has a lower total fission gas release, it does suffer more
transient fission gas release at colder irradiation temperatures. Interestingly, UN does not exhibit
transient fission gas release at high temperatures where the high mobility of point defects helps
maintain grain boundary bubbles at equilibrium pressures.

Ultimately, the advancements made here have enabled, for the first time, oxygen dependence to
be mechanistically linked to performance, and transient behavior of UN to be captured. While
this type of information is important to predict the behavior of uranium nitride fuel under irradi-
ation, there are a number of missing factors that should be explored next. First, the solubility of
oxygen in UN should be explored both computationally and experimentally, especially given its
importance in keeping the swelling and fission gas release in UN to a minimum. Second, with
the ability to now couple BISON and Centipede directly, the Centipede dislocation model should
be added to the BISON simulations to capture the behavior of dislocations at high temperatures
and high burnups [8]. Third, application of the UN BISON models to the TRISO fuel form
should be explored, especially with regards to the lower initial porosity that TRISO fuel ker-
nels are expected. Finally, the BISON simulations are enabled by the quasi-static approximation
forced by the SIFGRS model [6]. This approximation holds for most steady state conditions, but
will ultimately hinder high temperature, high burnup, and transient simulations. Exploration of
a SIFGRS replacement should be addressed in the coming years to provide an option when the
quasi-static approximation breaks down.
