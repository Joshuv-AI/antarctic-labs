3. Executive Summary:
Efficient utilization of CO2 is amongst the most critical cost drivers in algal biomass
production in open pond systems. CO2 delivery costs represent approximately 20% of the
final biomass selling price in algal mass cultivation systems. Technologies that enable
direct air capture (DAC) of atmospheric CO2 to decouple algae cultivation from CO2 point
sources thus present an opportunity to improve the economics and resource potential of
algal biomass. Current DAC technologies typically employ amine- or caustic-based
absorption, demanding significant water and/or energy inputs and incurring substantial
capital expenditures. Conversely, bio-based approaches to DAC offer a means to bypass
conventional technoeconomic and sustainability hurdles.
We integrate recent advances in computational metabolic modeling, algal genetic
engineering, algal cultivation, and algal biomass upgrading to enable directed localization
and self-assembly of carbonic anhydrase molecular films to gas-liquid interfaces for
enhanced CO2 capture and conversion. We established a predictive genome-scale
metabolic model for a top-candidate algal chassis, Picochlorum renovo. The genome-
scale model, iPicre1184, contains 10 compartments, 4,673 reactions, 3,586 metabolites,
and 1,184 genes. We used this model to gain novel insights into the biology of this
halophilic algae and to predict interventions that improve its growth and carbonic
anhydrase activity. We engineered P. renovo for CA production and tested new strains in
combination with media optimization.
Next we implemented model-driven interventions of P. renovo strains into a revolving
algal biofilm system (RAB). The RAB system was optimized to operate with different P.
renovo strains using different belt materials for best performance. Highest aerial
productivity of P. renovo in the RAB system was 116gDW/m2/d. Long term performance

over several weeks of a CA-engineered P. renovo strain resulted in production of 71
gDW/m2/d – independent of CO2 supplementation.
Using RAB system data we performed a technoeconomical analysis and showed that
higher biomass productivities were associated with more lucrative economics (indicated
by lower minimum fuel selling price, MFSPs) and improved sustainability (indicated by
lower fuel carbon intensity). Several cases result in a negative MFSP, indicating that a
10% internal rate of return could be achieved even without fuel revenues.
Our results demonstrate that genome-scale model informed strain engineering of P.
renovo integrated with RAB system growth performance results in technoeconomically
favorable biomass productivity. Furthermore we demonstrated that biomass of P. renovo
is suitable for plastic composite production. Overalll, the system developed here is
suitable for algal biomass production for biocomposite production at economic scale.

5. Background:
Efficient utilization of CO2 is amongst the most critical cost drivers in algal biomass
production in open pond systems. Current TEA and LCA open pond farm models assume
CO2 costs to be $45 per tonne, which includes all associated costs for off-site power plant
carbon capture/purification, compression, and transportation to the algae farm. This cost
represents approximately 20% of the final algal biomass selling price (ABSP) for
economically-viable biomass production. Thus, improvements in CO2 utilization can
significantly decrease the cost of algal biomass, and the downstream price of biofuels
and co-products. Additionally, recent TEA published by DOE and our team have indicated
current baseline strains utilized to establish the BETO SOT fall far short of target
productivity metrics for economically viable biomass production. These metrics indicate
that nearly a >2X enhancement in annual average biomass productivity is required from
the current state of technology baseline to achieve viable economics for algal biofuels.
Compounding issues associated with CO2 cost is the poor dissolution of CO2 into water,
limited by the high surface tension of water and relatively low CO 2 content of the
atmosphere. This combination can lead to carbon limitation in pond systems and
represents a major difference between open ponds and photobioreactors (Van Den
Hende, Vervaeren, and Boon 2012; He, Subramanian, and Tang 2012). The addition of
supplementary CO2 can dramatically increase productivity, yet the high cost of CO 2
represents a barrier to economically viable algal biofuels and negatively impacts energy
return on investment. Additionally, CO2 supplementation generally requires co-location
with a CO2 point source emitter, limiting the regional distribution of mass cultivation
facilities. Enabling enhanced CO2 transfer and bioavailability in pond systems represents
a major technical hurdle and is the key driver for our proposed research objectives.
Successful implementation of the proposed project scope offers a means to concurrently
enhance DAC, carbon conversion efficiency, and biomass productivity, targeting key
sustainability and economic drivers in algal biofuel production. Current DAC technologies
typically employ amine- or caustic-based absorption, demanding significant water and/or
energy inputs and incurring substantial capital expenditures. Conversely, bio-based
approaches to DAC offer a means to bypass conventional technoeconomic and
sustainability hurdles. To this end, we proposed to enhance atmospheric CO 2
bioavailability via the employ of algal-secreted, CO2-sequestering molecular films to
enhance dissolution of atmospheric CO2 into water and co-localize CA to gas-water
interfaces for improved algal carbon sequestration and conversion in biofilm-based algal
culture systems. The resultant technology presents a first-in-class, no capital expenditure,
renewable, bio-based approach to DAC and will ultimately increase the delivery and
utilization of CO2 by >20% relative to the current state of technology.
This proposal leveraged the power of genome-scale metabolic model-informed strain
engineering (Feist et al. 2009; Li et al. 2023; Zuñiga et al. 2018) to genetically modify an
alga to secrete an optimized CA into algal cultivation media for enhanced productivity and
conversion yield. The work leverages a high productivity, halotolerant alga, with proven
outdoor viability at DOE testbeds. We have generated an extensive genomic
knowledgebase for this strain, Picochlorrum renovo, enabling the development of facile,
reproducible, and efficient genetic tools for chloroplast and nuclear gene expression, as
well as extracellular protein secretion. This strain was integrated with GWT’s advanced

RAB system to demonstrate scalable improvements to DAC-mediated cultivation and
resultant biomass will be valorized via the demonstrated production of bioplastics.

6. Project Objectives:
We proposed to integrate recent advances in computational metabolic modeling, algal
genetic engineering, algal cultivation, and algal biomass upgrading to enable directed
localization and self-assembly of carbonic anhydrase molecular films to gas-liquid
interfaces for enhanced CO2 capture and conversion. This technology was integrated with
GWT’s best in class, RAB cultivation system to enhance algal productivity and provide
simple harvest. Algal protein will be upgraded to bioplastics using Algix’s cutting edge
technology; residual biomass carbohydrates and lipids will be used for the production of
fuel and platform chemical intermediates. Combined, the resultant technology presents a
paradigm shifting, bio-based approach to DAC and will ultimately facilitate enhanced algal
biomass revenue potential by enabling efficient atmospheric CO2 capture and conversion.
The project goals were set up in different Tasks: Task 1 Verification of project methods.
Task 2) Establishment of a predictive genome-scale metabolic model for NREL’s top-
candidate algal chassis, Picochlorum renovo; Task 3) algal genetic engineering for in situ
photoproduction, Task 4) large-scale deployment and testing in GWT’s RAB system, Task
5) biomass upgrading to fuel and value-added products using established NREL and Algix
technology, and Task 6 TEA/LCA analysis to define an economically-viable path to
sustainable commercialization.

Task 1: Verification

Task 2: Computational Modeling
This task reconstructed a genome-scale metabolic model (GEM) of Picochlorum renovo
for the design of improved protein production and secretion to achieve maximal
productivity.
Microalgae are a sustainable source of biofuels and high-value products. The recently
discovered Picochlorum renovo attains outstanding lipid productivities, growth rates, and
titers in diverse and adverse environments, making it a promising candidate for the
bioproduction of biofuels, nutraceuticals, and carbon sequestration. However, optimizing
these applications requires a deeper understanding of P. renovo's metabolic capabilities.
Genome-scale metabolic models (GEMs) have been at the forefront of metabolic
engineering. GEMs have been instrumental in the biotechnological application of
numerous organisms, spanning bacteria, fungi, and microalgae. In microalgae, GEMs
have identified optimal growth conditions for fast-growing microalgae such as
Chlamydomonas reinhardtii, and Chlorella vulgaris, and have elucidated how resources
are allocated across organelles. However, modeling efforts are lacking for P. renovo.
Here we manually curated a metabolic model for P. renovo, iPicre1184, reconstructed
using a combination of bioinformatics tools, and metabolomics data (Figure 1a). We

under the supplementation of 14 different organic carbon sources (Figure 1b). Then, we
measured the transcriptome of P. renovo in the presence and absence of zinc

dataset to elucidate the resource allocation that drives the maximization of growth of this
microalga.

Reconstruction and properties of iPicre1184
The draft model contained 6,374 reactions, out of which 2,583 were orphan reactions.
The model contained 1,618 P. renovo genes and 426 exogenous genes. The model was
manually curated iteratively by mapping exogenous genes to P. renovo gene homologs,
and removing reactions not associated with any P. renovo gene. After the final manual
curation of P. renovo genes increased to 4,221.
The final model, iPicre1184, contains 10 compartments, 4,673 reactions, 3,586
metabolites, and 1,184 genes (Figure 2a). The compartments in the model represent the
cytosol (c), Golgi apparatus (g), glyoxysome (x), chloroplast (h), mitochondria (m),
thylakoid membrane (u), endoplasmic reticulum (r), lysosome (l), nucleus (n), and
extracellular environment (e). Of the reactions, 63% are involved in lipid metabolism,
which is a result of the detailed manual curation on the synthesis of lipids, as well as their
degradation pathways. The high number of compartments and resolution in the
compartmentalization of enzymes in P. renovo led to 10% of reactions involved in

metabolic transport. Furthermore, metabolic pathways accounted for the remaining 27%.
Our carbon substrate utilization analysis (Figure 2a) performed on 14 organic carbon
substrates in light and dark conditions showed a prediction accuracy of 75%, precision of
86%, and recall of 71% (Figure 2b).

Figure 2. Properties of iPicre1184 and comparison with template models. a, Agreement between predicted

We used previously published high-quality GEMs of photosynthetic organisms as
templates to reconstruct iPicre1184. A majority of iPicre1184-unique reactions are
involved in lipid metabolism (63%), as they were manually curated and added to the
model after draft reconstruction from templates (Figure 2c). A similar result was obtained
for the GEM of P. tricornutum by Levering et al. (2019), where their high-resolution
curation of lipid metabolism led to over 3,000 reactions in this subsystem, accounting for
75% of its model. Approximately 10% of these unique reactions correspond to transport,
exchange, and nucleotide metabolism, with minor contributions from all other
subsystems. However, some template-specific lipid metabolism reactions were inherited
in iPicre1184.
The majority of reactions inherited from other templates correspond to metabolic
processes other than lipids. Out of the 2,734 reactions associated with fatty acid and lipid
metabolism, 2,174 reactions corresponding to different lipid and fatty acid metabolites,
such as, fatty acyls (399), fatty acids (244), glycerolipids (493), glycerophospholipids
(764), sphingolipids (47), and glycosyldiacylglycerols (90) were added through manual
curation to represent the lipid metabolism of P. renovo. Every lipid metabolite is
represented by the class of lipid, number of carbon atoms, and saturation of fatty acyl
chains.

P. renovo attains a unique phenotype in comparison with templates
We showcased the metabolic capabilities of P. renovo in the context of other highly-
curated metabolic models for microalgae, namely C. vulgaris, C. reinhardtii, and P.
tricornutum, (Figure 3a). Notably, the positive correlation between P. renovo metabolism
and C. vulgaris and C. reinhardtii is in sync with their phylogenetic closeness (R2 of 0.78
and 0.79, respectively). On the other hand, the correlation between P. tricornutum and all
the other models was only moderate, as it was the only non-green microalgae in the
included pool of highly curated template models. While the two green microalgae
templates, C. vulgaris and C. reinhardtii, showed very high correlation (R2 = 0.94), P.
renovo showed a more distant metabolic phenotype with significantly lower correlation.
Simulations showed that while P. renovo has similar trends in pathway activities (Figure
3b), several pathways are significantly more active, such as nucleotide and amino acid
biosynthesis, and oxidative phosphorylation. Interestingly, the high resolution in the
reconstruction of the lipid biosynthetic pathways in P. renovo and P. tricornutum, yielded
a significantly higher activity of these pathways in both models, showcasing the
importance and effect of their detailed reconstruction.

Figure 3. Comparison of metabolic phenotypes across GEM templates of microalgae. a, Correlation of
metabolic fluxes at the pathway level of iPicre1184 and the metabolic model templates. b, Heatmap of the
metabolic fluxes at the pathway level in iPicre1184 and the template models.
Higher zinc concentrations (microM/L and 1 microM/L) did not confer additional growth
benefits, with growth rates comparable to control conditions. These findings established
0.1 microM/L zinc as the optimal concentration, and this condition was subsequently

 Page 8 of 26

 Zengler

selected for transcriptomic profiling to elucidate the molecular basis of the observed
growth enhancement.
Transcriptomic analysis of cells grown under 0.1 microM/L zinc identified a selective set
of differentially expressed genes, comprising 21 upregulated and 20 downregulated
genes. Functional enrichment analysis revealed that upregulated genes were
predominantly associated with membrane transport functions, including ion transport
(GO:0006833), transmembrane transport (GO:0055085), and oxidoreductase activity
(GO:0016491). Several of these genes map to KEGG pathways related to transporters
(ko02000) and redox metabolism (ko00190), implicating active zinc uptake, homeostasis,
and mitigation of oxidative stress. Notably, genes involved in zinc ion transmembrane
transport (GO:0005385) and metal ion binding (GO:0046873) were upregulated,
suggesting that the improved growth phenotype at 0.1 microM/L zinc is supported by
enhanced metal handling capacity. Furthermore, genes associated with energy
metabolism and photosynthetic structures, such as ATP binding (GO:0005524) and
thylakoid components (GO:0009579), were also induced, indicating metabolic activation
to sustain higher growth rates. In contrast, downregulated genes were strongly enriched
in ribosomal components (GO:0005840), translation machinery (GO:0006412), and RNA
binding proteins (GO:0003723), mapping prominently to the KEGG ribosome pathway
(ko03010). This repression suggests a tightly regulated balance between promoting metal
homeostasis and moderating protein synthesis, potentially as an energy-conserving
strategy under modified metabolic demands.
Overall, these results demonstrate that P. renovo responds to moderate zinc
supplementation not by a generalized stress response, but rather through precise
transcriptional adjustments that enhance transport systems and oxidative defenses while
modulating energy expenditure on growth-related processes. The integration of
physiological growth data with transcriptomic and metabolic pathway insights provides a
comprehensive view of zinc-induced metabolic reprogramming in this microalga.

Task 3: Hy-CA Production and Strain Engineering
Prior NREL data established carbonic anhydrase (CA)-mediated growth enhancement in
Picochlorum renovo. To screen and generate optimized protein variants for exogenous
supplementation to P. renovo cultures, a matrix of twelve hydrophobin-carbonic
anhydrase (HyCA) fusion proteins were generated and heterologously expressed in a
Pichia pastoris expression system. All fusion proteins were able to reduce water surface
tension (based upon drop shape analyses), suggesting that the hydrophobins were
functional when tethered to carbonic anhydrase. However, subsequent growth assays
indicated that CA-only controls conferred superior growth enhancement to P. renovo;
thus, subsequent engineering efforts focused exclusively upon CA secretion. Ultimately,
Bovine CA demonstrated the greatest growth enhancement of all CA assessed and was
thus targeted for genetic incorporation and secretion.

 Page 9 of 26

 Zengler

To enable effective secretion of CA, P. renovo secretomic data and genome scale model
secretomic data were used to empirically identify and computationally predict the most
abundant secreted proteins in P. renovo, respectively. As shown in Figure 4,
computational analysis of the putative signal peptides (SP) driving protein secretion
reveal high sequence conservation, including a leucine rich core and an AQ termination
sequence at the cleavage site. The consensus sequence was thus synthesized and
secretion efficiency generated therefrom was assessed, ultimately conferring ~2X
enhancement to protein secretion using fluorescent protein in culture supernatant as a
direct readout (Figure 4, right). Bovine Carbonic Anhydrase tethered to the synthetic P.
renovo SP was successfully integrated into P. renovo cells and transferred to partner
GWT for RAB cultivation enhancement analysis.

Finally, media optimization was informed by genome-scale modeling, which indicated Zn
as a critical driver of P. renovo growth. A series of Zn concentrations were evaluated to
assess potential growth enhancement. Zn supplementation proved to significantly
enhance biomass accumulation; 0.1uM Zn supplementation led to approximately 2X
enhancement in final culture density following 120hrs of cultivation (Figure 5, left). To
gain insight into potential impact of Zn upon carbonic anhydrase (CA) activity we deployed
colorimetric analyses of culture supernatants in wild-type (WT) P. renovo and our top
candidate CA secretion strain (numbered 58). No significant CA activity enhancement
was observed in wild-type cultivars following Zn supplementation. However, CA secreting
strain 58 showed approximately 20% enhancement in CA activity following Zn
supplementation, suggesting a potential dual-pronged enhancement may be achieved in
engineered cultivars. Experimental designs to evaluate Zn growth enhancement in RAB
were transferred to GWT for further evaluation (Task 4). To determine if other divalent
metal trace elements could impart a similar phenotype, iron and manganese were
evaluated for potential growth enhancement, as both metals have a multitude of essential
functions in photosynthetic organisms. Supplementation of these metals did not confer
any enhancement of growth, suggesting a unique, Zn 2+-specific mechanism is
responsible for the observed growth enhancement in prior studies.

To assess the potential mechanism of Zn2+-mediated growth enhancement, we
sequenced RNA from P. renovo cells grown with and without 0.1 µM Zn2+
supplementation and performed comparative transcriptomics to determine which genes
are differentially upregulated in the presence of elevated zinc. Notably, multiple zinc
transporters were upregulated. Additionally, Zinc-regulated GTPase activator proteins
were highly upregulated. GTPase are involved in regulatory cascades, which suggests
the Zinc may be responsible for initiating global cellular responses. Perhaps most
interesting was the upregulation of chlorophyll-a-b binding protein CP29. This could
suggest that Zinc is directly affecting photosynthesis in P. renovo. These findings were
integrated with GEM efforts (Task 2) to update and refine genome scale models.

Task 4: Deployment & Compositional Analysis
Cultivation equipment to perform P. renovo growth on site was set up at GWT. GWT’s
existing research laboratory was reconfigured for this work. This included installing new
CO2 incubators, flask shaker tables, and lighting systems. GWT’s algae cultivation
greenhouse was reconfigured as well and three pilot-scale RAB systems were installed.
P. renovo was attained from NREL partners. P. renovo was scaled-up from growth on
agar plates to 250 mL flasks. This suspension-based culture was used to inoculate lab-
RAB systems as well as inoculate 20 L flat panels and 100 L flat panels, which were used
to inoculate the pilot-scale RAB systems. Five distinct substrates were tested with varying
degrees of effectiveness. Two substrates were identified that promoted attachment of P.
renovo to the RAB-system. Algae samples from these RAB-systems were collected to
establish baseline productivity.

RAB+CA-Secreting P. renovo Baselining (Subtask 4.3a)
In this baseline trial, GWT compared the performance of CA-Secreting P. renovo with the
wild type P. renovo by evaluating six defined conditions alongside two additional control
lab scale RABs with no supplementation (Figure 12) to define a clean baseline as
biological replicates to the specific growth conditions. The RABs are exposed to a light
intensity of 500uE and a 12/12 light/dark cycle. The liquid in the reservoir is half
suspended cell culture and half f/2 medium, with urea as the nitrogen source with 100 nM
of Zn2+ (sans buffer) maintained at 33°C in the reservoir. All CO2 conditions supplemented
with CO2 are mediated by a pH of 7.5. Reservoir procedures involved compensating for
evaporation and refreshing the media bi-weekly. RAB belt harvests were divided into
three time points on the belts. One third of the belt was harvested every five days, the
second third section of the belt every ten days, and the final third every 15 days for 8
weeks post initial biofilm attachment to quantify surface area productivity. Biomass
samples from the RAB biofilm and reservoir were sent to NREL for compositional
analysis.

Chimeric Belt Implementation (Revisiting Subtasks 4.3a,b)
GWT evaluated the productivity of CA-Secreting P. renovo under various nutrient
conditions. In addition to working on Subtask 4.3b, GWT reran the entire Subtask 4.3a
study. 4.3a was repeated due to inconsistencies in the productivity data that was
collected during the initial attempt at executing this work. This trial featured RABs using
a chimeric belt composed of cellulose, polyester, and PVC, the change aimed at
enhancing the reliability of the data collected and to give more comprehensive insights
on how belt material affects productivity.

After conducting the experiments outlined in Subtasks 4.3a, 4.3b, and 4.3x, GWT has
concluded that cotton is the most effective belt material for achieving the highest mean
productivity across all solid retention times (SRTs) ranging from 3.5, 7, and 14 days, with
an average belt productivity of approximately 3.16 gDW/m2 (Figure 14). The study also
revealed that SRTs of 3.5 and 7 days exhibit similar mean belt productivity of around 2
gDW/m2/d across all tested belt materials, including cotton, PVC, and polyester. Among
all the conditions investigated, the combination of a cotton belt and a 3.5-day SRT
demonstrated the highest mean belt productivity, reaching approximately 3.5 gDW/m2/d.
Furthermore, the CA-secreting strain grown with supplemental CO2 at a pH of 7.5 on a
cotton belt with a 3.5-day SRT achieved a mean belt productivity of about 5.3 gDW/m2/d.
When evaluating the impact of nutritional conditions, the CA-secreting strain grown on
centrate wastewater with a 6-hour hydraulic retention time (HRT) and a cotton belt with a
3.5-day SRT proved to be the most favorable, resulting in the highest mean belt
productivity of approximately 5.3 gDW/m2/d. Notably, the combination of the CA-
secreting strain, supplemental CO2 at pH 7.5, a cotton belt, and a 3.5-day SRT yielded
an estimated mean commercial-scale aerial productivity of around 71 gDW/m2/d. These
results are summarized in Table 2.

Task 5: Technoeconomic Analyses and Life Cycle Assessment
Algix conducted compositional testing of P. renovo biomass and showed that the algae
from the RAB’s is suitable for plastic composite production. The algae contained only 6
compounds associated with degradation in the odor testing. This is below current
thresholds for deeming algae biomass significantly degraded and qualifies the RAB algae
material for use in commercial applications. Additionally protein, ash, and water contents
met minimum requirements.

We conducted TEA and LCA on a process for treating secondary effluent wastewater
using the revolving algal biofilm (RAB) technology and subsequently converting the algal
biomass into fuels and products. The results of the analysis indicated that utilizing an
organism engineered to secrete carbonic anhydrase (CA) would show economic and
sustainability advantages compared to a control organism. Additionally, including
supplemental CO2 in the system could result additional cost and sustainability
improvements. These results were obtained by mapping a range of typical surface area
removal rates (SARRs) for N and P to the biomass productivities observed
experimentally; a sensitivity analysis indicated that the SARR values had significant
influence on economics and sustainability, suggesting that future work should focus on
further investigating the relationship between biomass productivity and SARR.
Methodology: The methodology used in this analysis is largely consistent with that
described previously in the 2023 TEA & LCA milestone report and will only be described
briefly here. The previous milestone report compared TEA and LCA results for four
scenarios (algae produced via dedicated cultivation or wastewater treatment, using an
RAB approach or an open pond approach). Findings from that work indicated that using
RABs in conjunction with wastewater treatment was the most economical and sustainable
approach; accordingly, we focus on this approach here and further investigate the
implications of using a microalgae strain engineered with Hy-CA secretion, with or without
supplemental CO2.
A process flow diagram of the facility, encompassing both wastewater treatment and
biomass conversion, is shown in Figure 15. The facility consists of algae production
ponds overlayed with RAB units and enclosed in a greenhouse structure, and CO 2 is
captured from ambient air with optional supplemental CO2 (assumed to be sourced from
a nearby power plant). An inoculum system (consisting of photobioreactors, covered
ponds, and open fully lined ponds) is used to supply the culture to the RABs. Secondary
wastewater effluent is fed to the ponds along with a recycled aqueous stream from the
conversion facility; no additional water or nutrients are supplied, with clean water
discharged to the environment. Biomass is harvested from the RAB units at 5-10% solids
and is subsequently dewatered to 20% solids via centrifuge. Following a wet anaerobic
storage step to maintain constant flow despite seasonal variations, biomass proceeds to
the conversion process. At a high level, the conversion approach involved pretreatment
with dilute acid, fermentation of carbohydrates to ethanol, extraction of lipids for co-
processing to fuels, and drying and sale of residual solids for bioplastic production (sold
at $900/tonne [$817.5/ton]). Detailed parameters for the wastewater treatment and
biomass conversion processes are shown in Table 3 and Table 4. Parameters are
equivalent to those used in the 2023 milestone report with the exception the surface area
removal rates (SARR) of N and P, described below.
SARR removal rates are a key design parameter for sizing the number of RABs required,
but were not independently measured for each scenario considered in this report. To
address this data gap, we mapped a range of typical SARR values measured by GWT
for tertiary treatment (0.5-2.5 g/m2 belt area/day for N and 0.1-0.5 g/m2 belt area/day for
P) to the range of algae biomass productivities observed in this work (0.96-6.48 g/m2 belt
area/day). Due to the uncertainty associated with this assumption, we also further

investigate the sensitivity of minimum fuel selling price (MFSP) and carbon intensity (CI)
to SARR.

Material and energy flow rates and pond/equipment sizing were based on outputs from
process models for algae cultivation (developed in Excel and based on a modified version
of a public model and conversion (developed in Aspen Plus) and were used to inform the
TEA and LCA. The TEA used a discounted cash flow rate of return financial analysis
which modeled the construction and operation of a combined algae biomass cultivation
and conversion facility, while LCA focused on greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions only and
considered several different allocation approaches, including a fuel-focused displacement
method and mass- and market-based allocation methods. The primary metrics solved for
are the MFSP (representing the cost that the ethanol and algal lipids would have to be
sold at to meet a 10% internal rate of return) and the fuel CI (representing the life cycle
greenhouse gas emissions with producing the combined fuel products). The minimum
biomass selling price (MBSP), representing the intermediate “cost” of the algae biomass
to the conversion facility, is also reported. Algae lipids were discounted to reflect the
additional fuel processing steps required downstream but otherwise outside the scope of
the TEA model boundaries, as is described in our previous work (Wendt et al. 2019).

Finally, it should be reiterated that the results presented here were obtained by estimating
SARR values for each scenario based on the biomass productivity, and that SARR values
were not directly measured. SARR values may be influenced by external factors such as
temperature, pH, or TN and TP in the incoming wastewater, and may not scale linearly
with biomass productivity. Accordingly, we considered a sensitivity analysis where SARR
values of N and P are varied in concert over the range of typical values observed for
tertiary treatment (Figure 17). It was shown that the SARR value had a significant impact
on the overall MFSP, with economics ranging from highly unfeasibly (>$50/GGE) to highly
profitable (<$0/GGE) depending on the SARR value. Additionally, an unintuitive
phenomenon is observed where economics for the CA Secretion case without CO2
supplementation outperform economics of the case with supplementation at higher SARR
rates, despite a lower biomass productivity. This is a result of the water treatment credits
being applied over a smaller amount of biomass (and, accordingly, fuel) – thus, while the
overall MFSP appears to be lower, there would be less overall revenue from fuels. This
is largely an artifact of the dominating role that the water treatment credits and associated

8. Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions:
The project achieved several key accomplishments. First, we generated the first genome-
scale metabolic model for a Picochlorum species. We used this model to gain novel
insights into the biology of this halophilic algae and to predict interventions that would
improve its growth and carbonic anhydrase activity. These modifications were deployed
in the RAB system. The RAB system was optimized to operate with different P. renovo
strains using different belt material. Highest aerial productivity was achieved at
71gDW/m2/d. Using RAB system data to inform our technoeconomical analysis showed
higher biomass productivity were associated with more lucrative economics (indicated by
lower MFSPs) and improved sustainability (indicated by lower fuel CI). Several cases
result in a negative MFSP, indicating that a 10% internal rate of return could be achieved
even without fuel revenues. Addition of supplemental CO2 increased productivity (and,
accordingly, the modeled SARR for TN and TP), resulting in improved economics even
after accounting for additional costs associated with CO2 supplementation. Overall our
results demonstrate that genome-scale model informed strain engineering of P. renovo
integrated with RAB system growth results in technoeconomically favorable biomass
productivity.

9. Path Forward:
The system developed here is suitable for algal biomass production for biocomposite
production. While the system performs well, further improvements could be implemented
that are focused advanced strain engineering and deployment of these strains in complex
nutrient source, such as wastewater.
