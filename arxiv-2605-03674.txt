Statistical Inference via T-Posterior Randomised Estimators
Yannick BARAUD

Abstract
. Given a statistical model, we propose a novel estimation method that yields randomised estimators for the unknown distribution of an observed random variable. We establish non-asymptotic bounds for the performance of these estimators and demonstrate their robustness to potential model misspecification. Notably, these properties are established by circumventing the use of concentration inequalities and empirical process theory. We provide an illustration of this approach to the problem of estimating the intensity of a Poisson process.

1.
Introduction

The aim of this paper is to propose a new approach for estimating the distribution
{\mathbf{P}}^{\star}
of an observed random variable
{\boldsymbol{X}}
. Given a parameterised family
{\mathscrbf{M}}=\left\{{{\mathbf{P}}_{\theta},\;\theta\in\Theta}\right\}
of candidate probability distributions for
{\mathbf{P}}^{\star}
, our aim is to propose an estimation strategy that yields an estimator
\widehat{\theta}
whose values belong to
\Theta
and for which
{\mathbf{P}}^{\star}
and
{\mathbf{P}}_{\smash{\widehat{\theta}}}
are as close as possible, in a sense that we shall specify later. Unlike the classical frequentist approach, in which the estimator is a deterministic function of the data
{\boldsymbol{X}}
, ours is drawn over the parameter space
\Theta
with a distribution from
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}
that depends on
{\boldsymbol{X}}
. From this perspective, our approach can be interpreted as a randomised estimation procedure, or a Bayes-like one, and we shall see that it offers certain advantages over the frequentist approach.

To design our random estimator, or equivalently the distribution
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}
that generates it, we need three main elements.

Firstly, we need a loss function
\ell
on the parameter space
\Theta
as well as a test statistic
{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta,\theta^{\prime})
between
\theta
and
\theta^{\prime}
that allows us to compare two candidate parameters
\theta,\theta^{\prime}
in
\Theta
. When
{\mathbf{P}}^{\star}={\mathbf{P}}_{\theta^{\star}}
belongs to our model
{\mathscrbf{M}}
and
\theta
is much closer to
\theta^{\star}
than to
\theta^{\prime}
(relatively to our loss
\ell
), we expect our test statistic
{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta,\theta^{\prime})
to take very negative values. Since we are considering test statistics that satisfy
{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta,\theta^{\prime})=-{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta^{\prime},\theta)
for all
(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\in\Theta^{2}
,
{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta,\theta^{\prime})
is automatically large in the opposite situation where
\theta^{\prime}
is much closer to
\theta^{\star}
than to
\theta
. When these parameters are either both close to or both far from
\theta^{\star}
, the choice between one or the other becomes unimportant, as does the value of
{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta,\theta^{\prime})
. The statistic
{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta,\theta^{\prime})
can be regarded as an estimator of the difference
\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta)-\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta^{\prime})
and we shall sometimes refer to this interpretation when describing certain heuristics.

Our second ingredient is a prior distribution over the parameter space
\Theta
. This prior (for short) will prove to be a practical tool for assessing the complexity of the parameter space, and more specifically, of an element
\theta
within it.
We shall use it to replace other notions of complexity such as VC-dimensions, entropies, Rademacher complexities, amongst others. Even though the latter notions are commonly used in the frequentist paradigm, they may also be difficult to evaluate in general.

To explain the heuristic that underlies our approach, let us assume for a moment that our test statistic
{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta,\theta^{\prime})
is the ideal quantity
\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta)-\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta^{\prime})
. For
\theta\in\Theta
, let us introduce

{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta)=\int_{\Theta}{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta,\theta^{\prime})\;d\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}},\theta}(\theta^{\prime})\quad\text{where}\quad\frac{d\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}},\theta}}{d{\boldsymbol{\pi}}}(\theta^{\prime})=\frac{\exp\left({\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta,\theta^{\prime})\right)}{\int_{\Theta}\exp\left({\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta,\theta^{\prime})\right)\cdot d{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\theta^{\prime})}

and define the distribution
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}
as

(1)

\frac{d{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}}{d{\boldsymbol{\pi}}}(\theta)=\frac{\exp\left[{-{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta)}\right]}{\int_{\Theta}\exp\left[{-{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta)}\right]d{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\theta)}.

For
\theta\in\Theta
, we note that
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}},\theta}
is a Gibbs measure associated with the function
\theta^{\prime}\mapsto{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta,\theta^{\prime})
. Our interest in this Gibbs measure lies in the fact that it concentrates its mass around the values of
\theta^{\prime}
for which
{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta,\theta^{\prime})
is large. Since in our case
{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta,\theta^{\prime})=\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta)-\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta^{\prime})
,
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}},\theta}
concentrates its mass in a neighbourhood of
\theta^{\star}
.
Furthermore, for this specific test statistic, the density of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}},\theta}
takes the form

\frac{d\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}},\theta}}{d{\boldsymbol{\pi}}}(\theta^{\prime})=\frac{\exp\left[{-\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta^{\prime})}\right]}{\int_{\Theta}\exp\left[{-\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta^{\prime})}\right]d{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\theta^{\prime})}

and is therefore independent of
\theta
. As a consequence, the statistic

\displaystyle{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta)=\int_{\Theta}{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta,\theta^{\prime})d\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}},\theta}(\theta^{\prime})=\int_{\Theta}\left({\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta)-\ell\left({\theta^{\star},\theta^{\prime}}\right)}\right)d\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}},\theta}(\theta^{\prime})

is of the form
{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta)=\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta)+C
for some constant
C\in{\mathbb{R}}
and we observe that
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}
is also a Gibbs measure, but associated with
-{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta)=-\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta)-C
since its density takes the form

\frac{d{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}}{d{\boldsymbol{\pi}}}(\theta)=\frac{\exp\left[{-\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta)-C}\right]}{\int_{\Theta}\exp\left[{-\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta^{\prime})-C}\right]d{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\theta^{\prime})}=\frac{\exp\left[{-\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta)}\right]}{\int_{\Theta}\exp\left[{-\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta^{\prime})}\right]d{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\theta^{\prime})}.

The distribution
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}
therefore concentrates its mass around these
\theta
for which
\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta)
is small and a random variable
\widehat{\theta}
with distribution
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}
is likely to lie close to
\theta^{\star}
. In order to be more precise and derive a risk bound, at least in the simple situation where the parameter space
\Theta
is finite or countable, let us first observe that

(2)

\int_{\Theta}\exp\left[{\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta)}\right]d{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}(\theta)=\frac{{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\Theta)}{\int_{\Theta}\exp\left[{-\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta^{\prime})}\right]d{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\theta^{\prime})}=\frac{1}{\int_{\Theta}\exp\left[{-\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta^{\prime})}\right]d{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\theta^{\prime})}.

Using (
2
) and the concavity property of the logarithm, we obtain that

\displaystyle\int_{\Theta}\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta)d{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Theta}\log\exp\left[{\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta)}\right]d{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}(\theta)\leqslant\log\int_{\Theta}\exp\left[{\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta)}\right]d{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=-\log\int_{\Theta}\exp\left[{-\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta)}\right]d{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\theta)=-\log\left[{\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\exp\left[{-\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta)}\right]{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\theta)}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\left[{\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta)+\log\left({\frac{1}{{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\theta)}}\right)}\right].

This bound can be interpreted as

{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\ell(\theta^{\star},\widehat{\theta})|{\boldsymbol{X}}}\right]\leqslant\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\left[{\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta)+\log\frac{1}{{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\theta)}}\right]

when
\widehat{\theta}
has a distribution
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}
conditionally on
{\boldsymbol{X}}
. Taking the expectation on both sides with respect to
{\boldsymbol{X}}
, we obtain the risk bound

(3)

{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\ell(\theta^{\star},\widehat{\theta})}\right]\leqslant\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\left[{\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta)+\log\left({\frac{1}{{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\theta)}}\right)}\right].

We observe that it decomposes into two terms. The second term
\log\left({1/{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\theta)}\right)
depends solely on our choice of the prior distribution.
If the parameter space
\Theta
is finite and has cardinality
N
, we can take
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\theta)=1/N
, in which case the risk depends logarithmically on
N
and the quantity
\log N
can be interpreted as the complexity of our parameter space. Nevertheless, it may sometimes be more sensible to choose a prior distribution
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
that favours certain parameters over others, and
\log\left({1/{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\theta)}\right)
then corresponds to a complexity value associated with the parameter
\theta
. The first term on the right-hand side of (
3
) is an approximation term. It measures the quality of the approximation of
\theta^{\star}
by an element with complexity
\log\left({1/{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\theta)}\right)
. The risk bound provides the best compromise between approximation and complexity amongst the elements of the parameter space.

Since
\theta^{\star}
is unknown, it is unfortunately impossible to take
{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta,\theta^{\prime})=\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta)-\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta^{\prime})
as we did in the heuristic argument that we presented above. The main idea is therefore to replace
\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta)-\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta^{\prime})
with a suitable estimator of it.

In this paper, we shall see how it is possible to establish risk bounds under an appropriate control of the Laplace transform of a certain linear combinaison of the test statistics
{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta,\theta^{\prime})
, namely that of the random variable
\Delta_{\kappa}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta,\theta^{\prime},\theta^{\prime\prime})=\kappa{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta^{\prime\prime},\theta^{\prime})-{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta^{\prime\prime},\theta)
where
\kappa
denotes of positive number. Interestingly, it is sufficient to establish this control for fixed values of
\theta,\theta^{\prime},\theta^{\prime\prime}
in
\Theta
. In contrast, a frequentist approach based on minimising a criterion
{\rm crit}({\boldsymbol{X}},\cdot)
over the parameter space would generally require a uniform control of the difference
{\rm crit}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta)-{\mathbb{E}}\left[{{\rm crit}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta)}\right]
over
\Theta
to establish such risk bounds. These controls generally rely on concentration inequalities, which can be quite difficult to establish when the data are not independent. We refer the reader to Koltchinskii
(
Koltchinskii, 2011
)
and Massart
(
Massart, 2000
)
, amongst other references, for the crucial roles that play concentration inequalities in statistical estimation and statistical learning in the frequentist paradigm. Our approach entirely circumvents these difficulties.

The test statistics we consider here have the property of yielding robust tests between two probabilities. This means that the initial assumption that
{\mathbf{P}}^{\star}={\mathbf{P}}_{\theta^{\star}}
belongs to
{\mathscrbf{M}}
can be relaxed.
The value of
{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta,\theta^{\prime})
will in fact depend on the proximity (in a sense to be specified) of
{\mathbf{P}}^{\star}
relative to
{\mathbf{P}}_{\theta}
and
{\mathbf{P}}_{\theta^{\prime}}
. The robustness of these statistics, combined with the fact that we only need to control the Laplace transform of
\Delta_{\kappa}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta,\theta^{\prime},\theta^{\prime\prime})
, allows us to establish these risk bounds under weak assumptions on
{\mathbf{P}}^{\star}
, in particular not only in situations where
{\boldsymbol{X}}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
consists of independent data.

1.1.
Randomised versus non-randomised estimators

Constructing a distribution over the parameter space from data for estimation purposes is not a new idea.
It has been used successfully for decades in Bayesian statistics. Indeed, in the special case where
{\mathscrbf{M}}=\{{\mathbf{p}}_{\theta}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\;\theta\in\Theta\}
is a dominated parametric model and

{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta,\theta^{\prime})=\log{\mathbf{p}}_{\theta^{\prime}}({\boldsymbol{X}})-\log{\mathbf{p}}_{\theta}({\boldsymbol{X}})

is the difference between the log-likelihood functions, the distribution
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}
given by (
1
) coincides with the classical Bayesian posterior distribution
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}^{B}
. From this perspective, our approach can be regarded as a generalisation of the Bayesian approach. In this paradigm, the authors primarily study the contraction properties of the posterior distribution when the model is well-specified or, at least, when the Kullback-Leibler divergence between
{\mathbf{P}}^{\star}
and a suitable element
{\mathbf{P}}_{\theta^{\star}}
in the statistical model
{\mathscrbf{M}}
is sufficiently small. We refer readers to
Ghosal and van der Vaart
(
Ghosal et al., 2000
;
Ghosal and van der Vaart, 2017
)
, Birgé
(
Birgé, 2015
)
, Castillo
(
Castillo, 2024
)
amongst other references. It follows that the Bayesian approach has been shown to possess good estimation properties when the true data distribution
{\mathbf{P}}^{\star}={\mathbf{P}}_{\theta^{\star}}
belongs to the statistical model
{\mathscrbf{M}}
. The authors mentioned above show that, under appropriate assumptions on
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
, the posterior distribution
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}^{B}
concentrates most of its mass on small neighbourhoods of
\theta^{\star}
, except perhaps when
{\boldsymbol{X}}
belongs to an unlikely set
\Omega^{\prime}
of configurations. In other words, except on a set
\Omega^{\prime}
of low probability, a random variable
\widehat{\theta}
drawn from the distribution
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}^{B}
is close to
\theta^{\star}
with a probability close to 1.

Modifications of the Bayesian posterior distribution have also been proposed in the literature. We mention only a few of them. Chernozhukov and Hong
(
Chernozhukov and Hong, 2003
)
studied Laplace-type estimators. The results established therein are mainly asymptotic. This is not the case for Atchadé
(
Atchadé, 2017
)
, who studied the contraction properties of quasi-likelihoods for the estimation problem in sparse parameter spaces.

Under appropriate assumptions regarding the statistical model and
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
, the Bernstein-von Mises theorem bridges the gap between the Bayesian approach and the well-known frequentist one based on maximum likelihood. Nevertheless, this result does not imply that there are no fundamental differences between the Bayesian and frequentist approaches.
For example, if we observe
n
i.i.d. data points drawn from a shifted density
p(\cdot-\theta^{\star})
that is positive and unbounded on
{\mathbb{R}}
, the maximum likelihood estimator will not exist, whereas it is always possible to estimate the location parameter
\theta^{\star}
using a Bayesian approach. This simple example already illustrates the fact that there may be certain advantages to using randomised estimators rather than designing one by optimising a criterion.

Randomised estimators have also been introduced into statistical learning, notably thanks to the pioneering work of Olivier Catoni. The theory we develop here is closely related to his. In particular, Catoni was, to our knowledge, the first to introduce Gibbs estimators as an alternative to the classical empirical risk minimiser. His idea is to replace the minimisation of an empirical risk
\theta\mapsto{\rm crit}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta)
over the parameter space
\Theta
with the random selection
\widehat{\theta}
of a parameter
\theta\in\Theta
with a Gibbs distribution

\frac{dP_{\beta}}{d{\boldsymbol{\pi}}}(\theta)=\frac{\exp\left[{-\beta{\rm crit}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta)}\right]}{\int_{\Theta}\exp\left[{-\beta{\rm crit}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta)}\right]d{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\theta)}\quad\text{with $\beta>0$.}

Catoni obtained oracle-type inequalities for
\widehat{\theta}
for appropriate values of
\beta
. We refer the reader to Catoni
(
Catoni, 2004
)
. Gibbs posterior distributions were also used by Jiang and Tanner
(
Jiang and Tanner, 2008
)
to address the problem of variable selection from a Bayesian perspective.

Catoni also studied the risk of more general randomised estimators for which he established PAC Bayesian bounds. His approach was used in Catoni
(
Catoni, 2007
)
for classification purposes, in Audibert and Catoni
(
Audibert and Catoni, 2011
)
to estimate a regression function and some generalisations of Catoni’s approach are available in Alquier
(
Alquier, 2008
)
. We also mention that Bhattacharya et al.
(
Bhattacharya et al., 2019
)
studied the properties of the posterior distribution based on fractional likelihood. Certain PAC Bayesian bounds were established there for the
\alpha
-Rényi divergence loss.

1.2.
Robustness

A common undesirable feature of the Bayesian approach and some frequentist ones, typically based on the minimisation of a contrast function like the least-squares or the likelihood, is their lack of stability under misspecification. The resulting estimators may perform well when the true distribution
{\mathbf{P}}^{\star}
of the data belongs to the statistical model but may also perform very poorly when this condition is not met, even when
{\mathbf{P}}^{\star}
is very close to
{\mathscrbf{M}}
. The problem of robustness in statistics is a major one as statistician often use statistical models which are approximation of reality.

In the frequentist paradigm, this problem has been known for a long time and the search for robust estimators has led to numerous papers especially in the 1960s and 1970s. The reader may find an account of this research in Hubert
(
Huber, 1981
)
. It was tackled in Birgé
(
Birgé, 1979
;
Birgé, 1982
;
Birgé, 1983
)
with
T
-estimators and more recently in a series of papers based on
\rho
-estimation by Baraud et al
(
Baraud et al., 2017
)
, Baraud and Birgé
(
Baraud and Birgé, 2016
;
Baraud and Birgé, 2018
)
, Baraud and Chen
(
Baraud and Chen, 2024
)
, Chen
(
Chen, 2024b
;
Chen, 2024a
;
Chen, 2025
)
, Sart
(
Sart, 2016
;
Sart, 2021
)
.

We are not aware of many examples of robust procedures in the Bayesian paradigm, at least as soon as the distance between
{\mathbf{P}}^{\star}
and
{\mathscrbf{M}}
is small enough in the Hellinger or total variation distance. The only exceptions we are aware of are Baraud and Birgé
(
Baraud and Birgé, 2020
)
and Baraud
(
Baraud, 2024
)
which are, however, limited to density estimation.

1.3.
What is new here?

The aim of this article is to propose an alternative approach to
\rho
-estimation in order to construct optimal and robust estimators in statistics. The randomised approach we develop here offers certain theoretical and computational advantages over
\rho
-estimators. We believe they may be easier to implement, at least when the parameter space is not too complex, as they require the simulation of random variables following a given distribution
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}
, whereas the calculation of
\rho
-estimators requires the optimisation of a criterion. As already mentioned, another motivation for these randomised estimators lies in the fact that the complexity of a parameter space equipped with a prior can be much easier to assess in the Bayesian paradigm than in the frequentist one. This property facilitates the analysis of their risks.

The statistical method we describe here is intended to be applied across various statistical frameworks in subsequent work. In the present paper, we illustrate it by considering the problem of estimating the intensity of a Poisson process, possibly in the presence of covariates.

Without covariates, this problem was studied by Reynaud-Bouret
(
Reynaud-Bouret, 2003
)
under the
{\mathbb{L}}_{2}
-loss, and by Birgé
(
Birgé, 2007
)
using a Hellinger-type loss. Sart
(
Sart, 2015
)
extended Birgé’s work to the setting where the intensity depends on covariates. Assuming that the target intensity is square-integrable, Reynaud-Bouret analysed an estimator defined as the minimiser of a penalised
{\mathbb{L}}_{2}
-criterion, and derived risk bounds using suitable concentration inequalities. Birgé
(
Birgé, 2007
)
and Sart
(
Sart, 2015
)
proposed an alternative approach based on
T
-estimators. These
T
-estimators are constructed from robust tests comparing the elements of a suitable discretisation of the parameter space.

A common feature of all these papers is the assumption that the observed random variable
{\boldsymbol{X}}
is an exact realisation of a Poisson process. We do not make this assumption here, as it is rather strong and cannot be verified in practice. Instead, we assume that
{\boldsymbol{X}}
coincides with an unobserved Poisson process
{\boldsymbol{X}}^{\star}
, up to the addition or removal of a small number of points. In this sense, the observed process
{\boldsymbol{X}}
may be viewed as a corrupted version of the ideal dataset
{\boldsymbol{X}}^{\star}
.

2.
The statistical setting

As previously mentioned, our goal is to estimate the unknown distribution
{\mathbf{P}}^{\star}
of an observed random variable
{\boldsymbol{X}}
defined on a measurable space
(\Omega,{\mathcal{C}})
. To this end, we introduce certain assumptions regarding
{\mathbf{P}}^{\star}
and design a parameterised statistical model
{\mathscrbf{M}}=\left\{{{\mathbf{P}}_{\theta},\;\theta\in\Theta}\right\}
which is intended to approximate
{\mathbf{P}}^{\star}
. Proceeding as if
{\mathbf{P}}^{\star}={\mathbf{P}}_{\theta^{\star}}
belonged to
{\mathscrbf{M}}
, we focus on estimating the parameter
\theta^{\star}
. To evaluate the accuracy of our estimator, we equip
\Theta
with a loss function
\ell
acting as a distance; that is, an estimator
\widehat{\theta}
is considered to perform well if and only if
\ell(\theta^{\star},\widehat{\theta})
is sufficiently small. In cases where the model is misspecified,
\theta^{\star}
should be interpreted as the parameter associated with the distribution
{\mathbf{P}}_{\theta^{\star}}\in{\mathscrbf{M}}
that best approximates
{\mathbf{P}}^{\star}
or provides the best compromise between approximation and complexity as in the right-hand side of (
3
).

The estimators proposed herein are randomised, meaning they are drawn from a distribution
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}
on
\Theta
that depends upon the data. We call
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}
as the
T
-posterior distribution
(or
T
-posterior
for short) to emphasise its analogy with the classical Bayesian framework. The letter
T
refers to
Test (statistic)
, as in the
T
-estimators introduced by Birgé within the frequentist paradigm.

We are primarily interested in evaluating the mass of
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}
on sets of the form
{\mathscr{B}}(\theta,r)=\{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta,\;\ell(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\leqslant r\}
for
\theta\in\Theta
and
r\geqslant 0
, which we call
\ell
-balls, with special interest on those centred at
\theta^{\star}
. We treat the parameter space
\Theta
as a measurable space by equipping it with a
\sigma
-algebra
{\mathfrak{B}}
that ensures all such balls are measurable.

It is convenient to establish our results on a set
B
with positive probability, typically close to 1. This set corresponds to favourable configurations of
{\boldsymbol{X}}
under which our assumptions on
{\mathbf{P}}^{\star}
would hold. For example, if
{\boldsymbol{X}}
is an
n
-tuple
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
the coordinates of which are presumed to be i.i.d.,
B
might represent the set of
\omega\in\Omega
for which the value of
{\boldsymbol{X}}(\omega)
coincides with that of an
n
-sample. It is then of interest to examine how the risk of our estimator depends on
{\mathbb{P}}(B)
and, consequently, on the validity of our assumption on the
X_{i}
. We denote by
{\mathbb{P}}_{B}
and
{\mathbb{E}}_{B}
the conditional probability and expectation given
B
. If
\widehat{\theta}
is a randomised estimator with distribution
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}
, then for every measurable subset
A
of
\Theta
:

(6)

{\mathbb{P}}_{B}\left[{\widehat{\theta}\in A}\right]={\mathbb{E}}_{B}\left[{{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\widehat{\theta}\in A|{\boldsymbol{X}}}\right]}\right]={\mathbb{E}}_{B}\left[{{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}(A)}\right].

We shall repeatedly use this equality to link the behaviour of
\widehat{\theta}
as an estimator of
\theta^{\star}
to the properties of
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}
.

3.
The
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
-complexity of the parameter space at a point

Throughout this paper, we consider a prior distribution
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
on
\Theta
. As previously noted in the introduction, this distribution is used to quantify the complexity of the parameter space
\Theta
at a point
\theta\in\Theta
.

3.1.
Definition

Given a parameter space
\Theta
equipped with a prior
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
and a positive number
\gamma
, we define
the
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
-complexity of
\Theta
at
\theta\in\Theta
as the smallest positive number
{r}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},\theta)
which satisfies

(7)

\displaystyle 0<{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\left({{\mathscr{B}}(\theta,2r)}\right)\leqslant\exp\left({\gamma r}\right){\boldsymbol{\pi}}\left({{\mathscr{B}}(\theta,r)}\right)\quad\text{for every }r\geqslant{r}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},\theta).

This notion of complexity is not new and has been introduced before in the Bayesian setting for balls based on the Kullback-Leibler divergence (see for example Ghosal et al
(
Ghosal et al., 2000
)
). However, our definition is close to that given in Birgé
(
Birgé, 2015
)
for analysing the risk of Bayesian estimators.

The quantity
{r}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},\theta)
depends upon the value of
\gamma>0
; however, as
\gamma
remains a numerical constant throughout our results, we shall henceforth suppress this dependency in our notation.

Note that the number
{r}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},\theta)
always exists since

\frac{{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\left({{\mathscr{B}}(\theta,2r)}\right)}{{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\left({{\mathscr{B}}(\theta,r)}\right)}e^{-\gamma r}\,\displaystyle{\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{r\to+\infty}}\,0

and it is not difficult to check that

(8)

{r}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},\theta)=\sup\left\{{r>0,\;\frac{{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\left({{\mathscr{B}}(\theta,2r)}\right)}{{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\left({{\mathscr{B}}(\theta,r)}\right)}e^{-\gamma r}>1}\right\}.

We relate the value of
{r}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},\theta)
to other classical notions of “complexity” in the following examples.

3.2.
The parametric case

Let us first start with the simple situation where
\Theta
is a bounded convex subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{D}
,
D\geqslant 1
, on which the prior
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
is equivalent to Lebesgue measure
\nu
. Also assume that the loss
\ell
is equivalent to some power of a norm
\left|{\cdot}\right|
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{D}
. More precisely, we assume that there exist positive numbers
\underline{a},\overline{a},\underline{b},\overline{b},\alpha
such that for every measurable subset
A
of
\Theta
and
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta

(9)

\underline{a}\left|{\theta-\theta^{\prime}}\right|^{\alpha}\leqslant\ell(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\leqslant\overline{a}\left|{\theta-\theta^{\prime}}\right|^{\alpha}\quad\text{and}\quad\underline{b}\nu(A)\leqslant{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(A)\leqslant\overline{b}\nu(A).

The following result holds.

Proposition 1
.

Assume that
\Theta
is a convex subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{D}
and that
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
and
\ell
satisfy (
9
) for some norm
\left|{\cdot}\right|
. Then, for every
\theta\in\Theta

(10)

{r}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},\theta)\leqslant\overline{\kappa}_{D}D\leqslant\overline{\kappa}_{1}D\quad\text{with}\quad\overline{\kappa}_{D}=\frac{\log\left[{2(\overline{b}/\underline{b})^{1/D}}\right]}{\gamma}\left[\frac{\log(2\overline{a}/\underline{a})}{\alpha\log 2}+1\right].

We omit the proof of this proposition as it follows the same line as that of Proposition 10 in Baraud and Birgé
(
Baraud and Birgé, 2020
)
.

In other words, when the loss function is equivalent to the power of a norm on
\Theta\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{D}
and
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
has a density with respect to the Lebesgue measure which is bounded away from 0 and infinity, the
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
-complexity
{r}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},\theta)
of
\Theta
at
\theta
is bounded (independently of
\theta
) by the dimension
D
of the ambient space
{\mathbb{R}}^{D}
.

Refinements of Proposition
1
can also be inferred from Proposition 10 in Baraud and Birgé
(
Baraud and Birgé, 2020
)
and we therefore refer the reader to this proposition, especially when
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
is not bounded away from 0 on
\Theta
.

3.3.
A bound based on a critical radius

Given
\theta\in\Theta
, a convenient way to control the size of the quantity
r({\boldsymbol{\pi}},\theta)
is to assume that the prior
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
puts “enough mass” on an
\ell
-ball centred at
\theta
. We measure it by means of
\overline{r}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},\theta)
which is the smallest positive number that satisfies
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\left({{\mathscr{B}}(\theta,r)}\right)\geqslant e^{-\gamma r}
, hence

(11)

\overline{r}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},\theta)=\inf\left\{{r\geqslant 0,\;e^{\gamma r}{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\left({{\mathscr{B}}(\theta,r)}\right)\geqslant 1}\right\}.

The quantity
\overline{r}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},\theta)
satisfies the inequality
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\left({{\mathscr{B}}(\theta,r)}\right)\geqslant e^{-\gamma r}
and all the values of
r\geqslant\overline{r}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},\theta)
as well. We shall call it
the
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
-critical radius at
\theta
.
Since for every
r\geqslant\overline{r}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},\theta)
,

\frac{{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\left({{\mathscr{B}}(\theta,2r)}\right)}{{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\left({{\mathscr{B}}(\theta,r)}\right)}\leqslant\frac{1}{{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\left({{\mathscr{B}}(\theta,r)}\right)}\leqslant\exp\left({\gamma r}\right),

we obtain that

(12)

r({\boldsymbol{\pi}},\theta)\leqslant\overline{r}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},\theta)\quad\text{for every $\theta\in\Theta$.}

The
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
-complexity of
\Theta
at
\theta
is therefore not larger than the
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
-critical radius at
\theta
.

3.4.
Parameter space with an entropy

Let us now turn to the situation where for every
\epsilon>0
,
\Theta
can be covered by a finite number of
\ell
-balls with radius
\epsilon
. This implies that for every
\epsilon>0
there exists a finite subset of
\Theta[\epsilon]
of
\Theta
for which every
\ell
-ball centred at
\theta\in\Theta
with radius
r\geqslant\epsilon
contains at least one element of
\Theta[\epsilon]
. If
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}={\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{\epsilon}
is the uniform distribution on
\Theta[\epsilon]
, we derive that

{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{\epsilon}\left({{\mathscr{B}}(\theta,r)}\right)\geqslant\frac{1}{\left|{\Theta[\epsilon]}\right|}\quad\text{for every $\theta\in\Theta$ and $r\geqslant\epsilon$}.

This implies that

(13)

r({\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{\epsilon},\theta)\leqslant\overline{r}({\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{\epsilon},\theta)\leqslant\max\left\{{\epsilon,\frac{\log\left|{\Theta[\epsilon]}\right|}{\gamma}}\right\}\quad\text{for every $\theta\in\Theta$.}

Assuming, with no loss of generality, that
\epsilon\mapsto\left|{\Theta[\epsilon]}\right|
is non-increasing on
(0,+\infty)
, we may minimise the right-hand side of this inequality by choosing
\epsilon^{\star}=\inf\{\epsilon>0,\;\log\left|{\Theta[\epsilon]}\right|\leqslant\gamma\epsilon\}
. In this case,
r({\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{\epsilon^{\star}},\theta)\leqslant\epsilon^{\star}
.

3.5.
Hierachical priors

Let us now consider the situation where
\Theta=\bigcup_{m\in{\mathcal{M}}}\Theta_{m}
is an at most countable union of parameter spaces
\Theta_{m}
. This framework typically arises when we wish to select a suitable model for
\theta^{\star}
from a collection
\{\Theta_{m},\;m\in{\mathcal{M}}\}
of candidates. We therefore assume that each model
\Theta_{m}
is equipped with its own prior distribution
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{m}
, and we additionally consider a prior on the index set
{\mathcal{M}}
that serves to favour certain models over others. Since
{\mathcal{M}}
is assumed to be at most countable, it suffices to assign a probability of the form
\exp(-L_{m})
to each
m\in{\mathcal{M}}
. Here,
L_{m}
may be interpreted as a positive weight associated with the model
\Theta_{m}
with the additional constraint that

\sum_{m\in{\mathcal{M}}}e^{-L_{m}}=1.

This results in a prior
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
on
\Theta
defined by
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}=\sum_{m\in{\mathcal{M}}}e^{-L_{m}}{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{m}
that is called
a hierachical prior
. We note that for every
m\in{\mathcal{M}}
and
r>0
,

\displaystyle\frac{{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\left({{\mathscr{B}}(\theta,2r)}\right)}{{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\left({{\mathscr{B}}(\theta,r)}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{m^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{M}}}e^{-L_{m^{\prime}}}{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{m^{\prime}}\left({{\mathscr{B}}(\theta,2r)}\right)}{\sum_{m^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{M}}}e^{-L_{m^{\prime}}}{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{m^{\prime}}\left({{\mathscr{B}}(\theta,r)}\right)}\leqslant\frac{1}{e^{-L_{m}}{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{m}\left({{\mathscr{B}}(\theta,r)}\right)}.

In particular, for
r\geqslant\overline{r}({\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{m},\theta)+L_{m}/\gamma,

\displaystyle\frac{{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\left({{\mathscr{B}}(\theta,2r)}\right)}{{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\left({{\mathscr{B}}(\theta,r)}\right)}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{e^{-L_{m}}{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{m}\left({{\mathscr{B}}(\theta,r)}\right)}\leqslant\frac{1}{e^{-L_{m}}{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{m}\left({{\mathscr{B}}(\theta,\overline{r}_{m}(\gamma,\theta))}\right)}

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\left[{\gamma\left({\overline{r}({\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{m},\theta)+\frac{L_{m}}{\gamma}}\right)}\right]\leqslant\exp\left({\gamma r}\right).

Since the index
m
can be chosen arbitrarily in
{\mathcal{M}}
, we obtain that

(14)

r({\boldsymbol{\pi}},\theta)\leqslant\overline{r}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},\theta)\leqslant\inf_{m\in{\mathcal{M}}}\left[{\overline{r}({\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{m},\theta)+\frac{L_{m}}{\gamma}}\right].

4.
The posterior distribution and its main properties

4.1.
The posterior distribution

Our estimation strategy is based on the choice of a prior
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
on
\Theta
and a suitable test statistic
{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta,\theta^{\prime})
that allows one to compare two parameters
\theta\neq\theta^{\prime}
in
\Theta
. Given two numbers
\lambda>0
and
\beta\in(0,1)
, we define the density with respect to
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
of our posterior distribution
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}
on
\Theta
by

(15)

\frac{d{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}}{d{\boldsymbol{\pi}}}(\theta)=\frac{\exp\left[{-\lambda(1-\beta){\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta)}\right]}{\int_{\Theta}\exp\left[{-\lambda(1-\beta){\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta)}\right]d{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\theta)}\quad\text{for $\theta\in\Theta$}

where

(16)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Theta}{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta,\theta^{\prime})\frac{\exp\left[{\lambda{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta,\theta^{\prime})}\right]}{\int_{\Theta}\exp\left[{\lambda{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta,\theta^{\prime})}\right]d{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\theta^{\prime})}d{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\theta^{\prime}).

Heuristically, this posterior distribution puts most of its mass around parameters
\theta
which minimise over
\Theta
the mapping
\theta\mapsto\sup_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta,\theta^{\prime})
. By using Gibbs-like density measures, our randomised estimator mimics the estimator we could get in the frequentist paradigm by considering

\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\theta\in\Theta}\sup_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta,\theta^{\prime}).

Throughout the paper, we assume that the mapping
({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta,\theta^{\prime})\mapsto{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta,\theta^{\prime})
is measurable on
(\Omega\times\Theta\times\Theta,{\mathcal{C}}\otimes{\mathfrak{B}}\otimes{\mathfrak{B}})
. Under this assumption, our posterior is well-defined and the mapping
({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta)\mapsto d{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}(\theta)/d{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
a measurable function of
({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta)
. We shall not mention this assumption hereafter.

4.2.
The main assumption

For
\kappa>0
and
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3})
in
\Theta^{3}
, we denote by
\Delta_{\kappa}({\boldsymbol{X}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
the random variable

(17)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\kappa}({\boldsymbol{X}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\kappa{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta_{3},\theta_{2})-{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta_{3},\theta_{1})

and by
{\mathscr{L}}_{\kappa}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},\lambda|B)
its Laplace transform at
\lambda>0
conditionally on a set
B
with positive probability. That is,

{\mathscr{L}}_{\kappa}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},\lambda|B)={\mathbb{E}}_{B}\left[{\exp\left({\lambda\Delta_{\kappa}({\boldsymbol{X}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}\right)}\right]=\frac{1}{{\mathbb{P}}(B)}{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\exp\left({\lambda\Delta_{\kappa}({\boldsymbol{X}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}\right)1\hskip-2.6pt{\rm l}_{B}}\right].

The following condition links the test statistic to the loss
\ell
.

Assumption 1
.

There exist numerical constants
\lambda>0
,
\beta\in(0,1)
,
\overline{\beta}=2-\beta\in(1,2)
, and an event
B
with positive probability such that for a given
\theta^{\star}\in\Theta
and every
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3})\in\Theta^{3}
and
\kappa\in\{\beta,\overline{\beta}\}
,

(18)

\displaystyle{\mathscr{L}}_{\kappa}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},\lambda|B)\leqslant\exp\left[{A_{0}(\lambda,\kappa,{\mathbf{P}}^{\star},B)+\sum_{i=1}^{3}(-1)^{i-1}{\mathfrak{c}}_{i}(\lambda,\kappa)\ell\left({\theta^{\star},\theta_{i}}\right)}\right]

where
A_{0}(\lambda,\kappa,{\mathbf{P}}^{\star},B)
is a nonnegative number and the functions
{\mathfrak{c}}_{1},{\mathfrak{c}}_{2}
and
{\mathfrak{c}}_{3}
satisfy the constraints:

(19)

{\mathfrak{c}}_{1}(\lambda,\beta)\wedge{\mathfrak{c}}_{1}(\lambda,\overline{\beta})\geqslant 0,\quad{\mathfrak{c}}_{2}(\lambda,\beta)\wedge{\mathfrak{c}}_{2}(\lambda,\overline{\beta})>0,\quad\text{${\mathfrak{c}}_{3}(\lambda,\overline{\beta})\geqslant 0$ and ${\mathfrak{c}}_{3}(\lambda,\beta)<0$}.

In our applications,
\lambda
and
\beta
are numerical constants that are tuned to satisfy the constraints given by (
19
). Given the values of
\lambda
and
\beta
, the quantities
{\mathfrak{c}}_{1}(\lambda,\kappa),{\mathfrak{c}}_{2}(\lambda,\kappa)
and
{\mathfrak{c}}_{3}(\lambda,\kappa)
with
\kappa\in\{\beta,\overline{\beta}\}
are therefore numerical constants as well.

We provide a heuristic explanation below to offer a better insight into our Assumption
1
. Let us first assume that
{\mathbf{P}}^{\star}={\mathbf{P}}_{\theta^{\star}}
belongs to
{\mathscrbf{M}}
and
B=\Omega
for the sake of simplicity. For small enough values of
\lambda
, the Laplace transform of
\Delta_{\kappa}({\boldsymbol{X}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
at
\lambda
is then of order

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[{1+\lambda\Delta_{\kappa}({\boldsymbol{X}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}\right]

\displaystyle=1+\lambda\left({\kappa{\mathbb{E}}\left[{{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta_{3},\theta_{2})}\right]-{\mathbb{E}}\left[{{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta_{3},\theta_{1})}\right]}\right).

If
{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta,\theta^{\prime})
were an unbiased estimator of
\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta)-\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta^{\prime})
, we would then get

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[{1+\lambda\Delta_{\kappa}({\boldsymbol{X}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}\right]

\displaystyle=1+\lambda\left[{\kappa\left({\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta_{3})-\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta_{2})}\right)-\left({\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta_{3})-\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta_{1})}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle=1+\lambda\ell\left({\theta^{\star},\theta_{1}}\right))-\lambda\kappa\ell\left({\theta^{\star},\theta_{2}}\right)+\lambda\left({\kappa-1}\right)\ell\left({\theta^{\star},\theta_{3}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\left[{\lambda\ell\left({\theta^{\star},\theta_{1}}\right))-\lambda\kappa\ell\left({\theta^{\star},\theta_{2}}\right)+\lambda\left({\kappa-1}\right)\ell\left({\theta^{\star},\theta_{3}}\right)}\right].

As a consequence, at least for these small enough values of
\lambda
, our Assumption
1
on the Laplace transform of
\Delta_{\kappa}({\boldsymbol{X}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
are satisfied with
A_{0}(\lambda,\kappa,{\mathbf{P}}_{\theta^{\star}},\Omega)=0
,
{\mathfrak{c}}_{1}(\lambda,\kappa)=\lambda
,
{\mathfrak{c}}_{2}(\lambda,\kappa)=\lambda\kappa
and
{\mathfrak{c}}_{3}(\lambda,\kappa)=\lambda\left({\kappa-1}\right)
for
\kappa\in\{\beta,\overline{\beta}\}
. Note that the constraints in (
19
) are then automatically satisfied since
\beta\in(0,1)
and
\overline{\beta}=2-\beta\in(1,2)
. In fact, Assumption
1
would also be met for
\lambda
close enough to 0 even if
{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta,\theta^{\prime})
were not an unbiased estimator of
\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta)-\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta^{\prime})
provided that it satisfied for every
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta

c\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta)-C\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta^{\prime})\leqslant{\mathbb{E}}\left[{{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta,\theta^{\prime})}\right]\leqslant C\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta)-c\ell(\theta^{\star},\theta^{\prime})

for constants
0<c\leqslant C
.

When
{\mathbf{P}}^{\star}
does not belong to
{\mathscrbf{M}}
and
B\neq\Omega
, we shall see in our examples that the logarithm of
{\mathscr{L}}_{\kappa}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},\lambda|B)
inflates by some quantity
A_{0}(\lambda,\kappa,{\mathbf{P}}^{\star},B)>0
. It measures the impact of a possible misspecification of the model and the fact that our observation
{\boldsymbol{X}}
may take unfavourable configurations.

In view of providing non-asymptotic results on the performance of our randomised estimator, we wish to make the numerical constants which are involved in our main results as explicit as possible. Hereafter, we provide a list of two constants which can be calculated from the values of
\lambda
and
\beta
:

(20)

\displaystyle\gamma=\gamma(\lambda,\beta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\left({{\mathfrak{c}}_{2}(\lambda,\beta)\wedge{\mathfrak{c}}_{2}(\lambda,\overline{\beta})\wedge|{\mathfrak{c}}_{3}(\lambda,\beta)|}\right)>0;

(21)

\displaystyle J=J(\lambda,\beta)

\displaystyle=2+\left\lceil\log_{2}\left({1+\frac{{\mathfrak{c}}_{1}(\lambda,\overline{\beta})+{\mathfrak{c}}_{1}(\lambda,\beta)+{\mathfrak{c}}_{3}(\lambda,\overline{\beta})}{2\gamma}}\right)\right\rceil.

These constants are probably not sharp and only aim at making our statement more precise. The value of
\gamma
that results from these calculations is that which is used throughout this paper in the definitions of the quantities
r({\boldsymbol{\pi}},\theta)
and
\overline{r}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},\theta)
that are given in Section
3
.

We also use the notation

(22)

A_{0}({\mathbf{P}}^{\star},B)=\frac{A_{0}(\lambda,\beta,{\mathbf{P}}^{\star},B)+A_{0}(\lambda,\overline{\beta},{\mathbf{P}}^{\star},B)}{2},

hence dropping the dependency with respect to the numerical constants
\lambda
and
\beta
.

We illustrate our assumption in the density framework.

Example 1

(The density framework)
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{X}}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
be an
n
-tuple of random variables taking values in a measurable space
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{A}})
. Although this may not be true, we assume that these random variables are i.i.d. with a distribution belonging to a parametrised dominated model
\{P_{\theta}=p_{\theta}\cdot\mu,\;\theta\in\Theta\}
. We wish to analyse the performance of our random estimator in a scenario where reality deviates from our model. The observed data
\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}
may be a corrupted version of an ideal dataset
\{X_{1}^{\star},\ldots,X_{n}^{\star}\}
consisting of genuine independent random variables. In particular,
\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}
may contain a small proportion of dependent data, some of which correspond to repetitions of
X_{i}^{{}^{\star}}
or a function thereof.

We denote by
I
the set of indices for which
X_{i}
and
X_{i}^{{}^{\star}}
do not coincide, that is

(23)

I=\left\{i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\colon X_{i}\neq X_{i}^{{}^{\star}}\right\}.

This set
I
may be random, and we denote by
t_{\xi}
the
(1-e^{-\xi})
-quantile of
|I|
. The marginal distribution of
X_{i}^{\star}
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
is denoted by
P_{i}^{\star}
, and we denote their average by
P^{\star}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}P_{i}^{\star}
. We expect that our statistical model provides a reasonable approximation to most of the marginals
P_{1}^{\star},\ldots,P_{n}^{\star}
, and more specifically to their average
P^{\star}
, with respect to the Hellinger distance.

We recall that the Hellinger distance
h
between two probability measures
P=p\cdot\nu
and
Q=q\cdot\nu
on
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{A}})
, both dominated by
\nu
, is

h(P,Q)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}\left(\sqrt{p}-\sqrt{q}\right)^{2}\,d\nu}

the result being independent of the choice of
\nu
. For this problem, the test statistic

(24)

{\mathbf{T}}_{1}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta,\theta^{\prime})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi\left({\sqrt{\frac{p_{\theta^{\prime}}(X_{i})}{p_{\theta}(X_{i})}}}\right)\quad\text{for $\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta$}

satisfies our Assumption
1
with
\psi
defined in (
4
). More precisely, the following holds.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\xi>0
and
\theta^{\star}
be an arbitrary point in
\Theta
. Assumption
1
is satisfied with
{\mathbf{T}}={\mathbf{T}}_{1}
,
B=\{|I|\leqslant t_{\xi}\}
,

\ell(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=nh^{2}(P_{\theta},P_{\theta^{\prime}})
for
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
provided that
(\lambda,\beta)\in(0,+\infty)\times(0,1)
satisfies the constraints

(25)

\lambda\overline{\beta}\phi(\lambda(1+\overline{\beta}))<\frac{1}{8\sqrt{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{32\beta}{24\sqrt{2}}+\lambda(\beta^{2}+1)\phi(\lambda(1+\beta))<\frac{3}{24\sqrt{2}},

with
\phi
defined in (
4
). The values
\lambda=0.1
and
\beta=0.01
suits. Then, one may take the numerical constants
{\mathfrak{c}}_{i}(\lambda,\kappa)
for
i\in\{0,1,2,3\}
and
\kappa\in\{\beta,\overline{\beta}\}
which are provided in the proof of this proposition in Section
7.1
and
A_{0}(\lambda,\kappa,{\mathbf{P}}^{\star},B)=\lambda(1+\kappa)t_{\xi}-\log(1-e^{-\xi})+{\mathfrak{c}}_{0}(\lambda,\kappa)nh^{2}(P^{\star},P_{\theta^{\star}})
.

The density framework was studied in Baraud
(
Baraud, 2024
)
under the assumption that
X_{i}=X_{i}^{\star}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, hence, under the assumption that the
X_{i}
are truly independent.

4.3.
The main result

The aim of this section is to provide a bound on the loss
\ell(\theta^{\star},\widehat{\theta})
when
\widehat{\theta}
has distribution
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}
. Interestingly, this bound can directly be inferred from the control of the Laplace transform of the random variable
\Delta_{\kappa}({\boldsymbol{X}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
given by (
17
).

Theorem 1
.

Let
\theta^{\star}\in\Theta
and
\xi>0
. Assume that
\lambda>0
,
\beta\in(0,1)
and the event
B
satisfy Assumption
1
. Then our posterior
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}
defined by (
15
) satisfies,

(26)

{\mathbb{E}}_{B}\left[{{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}\left({{\vphantom{\!{{\mathscr{B}}}}}{}^{\mathsf{c}}\!{{\mathscr{B}}}\left({\theta^{\star},2^{J}r}\right)}\right)}\right]\leqslant 2\exp(-\xi)\quad\text{with}\quad r={r}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},\theta^{\star})+\frac{A_{0}({\mathbf{P}}^{\star},B)+1+\xi}{\gamma}

where
\gamma
and
J
are the constants given (
20
) and (
21
) respectively while
A_{0}({\mathbf{P}}^{\star},B)
is given by (
22
). This means that a randomised estimator
\widehat{\theta}
with distribution
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}
satisfies

(27)

{\mathbb{P}}_{B}\left[{2^{-J}\ell\left({\theta^{\star},\widehat{\theta}}\right)>{r}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},\theta^{\star})+\gamma^{-1}\left({A_{0}({\mathbf{P}}^{\star},B)+1+\xi}\right)}\right]\leqslant 2\exp(-\xi).

In particular, if
{\mathbb{P}}(B)\geqslant 1-e^{-\xi}
then
\widehat{\theta}
satisfies

(28)

{\mathbb{P}}\left[{2^{-J}\ell\left({\theta^{\star},\widehat{\theta}}\right)>{r}({\boldsymbol{\pi}},\theta^{\star})+\gamma^{-1}\left({A_{0}({\mathbf{P}}^{\star},B)+1+\xi}\right)}\right]\leqslant 3\exp(-\xi).

The proof of Theorem
1
is postponed to Section
6
.

Example 2

(The density framework (continued))
.

We have seen in our Proposition
2
that the test statistic
{\mathbf{T}}_{1}
given by (
24
) satisfies our Assumption
1
for the choice
(\lambda,\beta)=(0.1,0.01)
. We may therefore infer from Theorem
1
the following result. For every
\xi>\log 3
and
\theta^{\star}\in\Theta
, a randomised estimator
\widehat{\theta}
drawn with the distribution
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}
defined by (
15
) with
{\mathbf{T}}={\mathbf{T}}_{1}
possesses the property that with a probability at least
1-3e^{-\xi}

(29)

Ch^{2}\left({P_{\theta^{\star}},P_{\widehat{\theta}}}\right)\leqslant h^{2}(P^{\star},P_{\theta^{\star}})+\frac{r({\boldsymbol{\pi}},\theta^{\star})+t_{\xi}+\xi}{n}

where
C
is a positive numerical constant. In particular, it follows from the triangle inequality that with a probability at least
1-3e^{-\xi}

(30)

Ch^{2}\left({P^{\star},P_{\widehat{\theta}}}\right)\leqslant\inf_{\theta^{\star}\in\Theta}\left[{h^{2}(P^{\star},P_{\theta^{\star}})+\frac{r({\boldsymbol{\pi}},\theta^{\star})}{n}}\right]+\frac{t_{\xi}+\xi}{n}.

Some comments are in order:

It follows from (
29
) that
r({\boldsymbol{\pi}},\theta^{\star})/n
is the bound we would get if the data were truely i.i.d. with distribution
P_{\theta^{\star}}\in{\mathscr{M}}
.

When the data are independent by not i.i.d., hence
X_{i}=X_{i}^{\star}\sim P_{i}^{\star}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, we may take
t_{\xi}=0
and this risk bound inflates by the additional term
h^{2}(P^{\star},P_{\theta^{\star}})
where we recall that
P^{\star}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}P_{i}^{\star}
. In particular,
h^{2}(P^{\star},P_{\theta^{\star}})
may be 0 even when none of the marginal distributions
P_{i}^{\star}
belong to
{\mathscr{M}}
provided that their average does.

When
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are independent, an integration of (
30
) with respect to
\xi>0
yields the risk bound

C{\mathbb{E}}\left[{h^{2}\left({P^{\star},P_{\widehat{\theta}}}\right)}\right]\leqslant\inf_{\theta^{\star}\in\Theta}\left[{h^{2}(P^{\star},P_{\theta^{\star}})+\frac{1+r({\boldsymbol{\pi}},\theta^{\star})}{n}}\right].

The risk of
\widehat{\theta}
therefore achieves the best trade-off between the approximation of
P^{\star}
by an element of the form
P_{\theta^{\star}}\in{\mathscr{M}}
and the
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
-complexity of the model
\Theta
at
\theta^{\star}
.

When the dataset
\{X_{1}^{{}^{\star}},\ldots,X_{n}^{{}^{\star}}\}
is corrupted in the sense that some of the
X_{i}^{{}^{\star}}
are replaced by arbitrary (possibly dependent) data,
t_{\xi}
may be positive. Inequality (
30
) then shows that the accuracy of our estimator remains of the same order as in the previous situation, provided that the number of such corrupted data points remains sufficiently small compared to
r({\boldsymbol{\pi}},\theta^{{}^{\star}})
, at least with a probability close to 1.

5.
Estimating the distribution of a Poisson process with covariates

Throughout this section, we shall see a point process
X
on a measurable space
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{A}})
both as a finite random subset of
{\mathcal{X}}
and a discrete measure on
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{A}})
putting a mass one at each element of
X
. For example, for a measurable subset
A
of
{\mathcal{X}}
, we shall use the notation
X(A)
when
X
is viewed as a measure and the notation
|X\cap A|
when it is viewed as a subset.

Given a finite measure
\nu
on
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{A}})
, we recall that
X
is a Poisson process with finite intensity measure
\nu

if for every bounded measurable function
f
on
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{A}})
,

(31)

{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\exp\left({\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}fdX}\right)}\right]=\exp\left[{\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}\left({e^{f}-1}\right)d\nu}\right].

In particular, by applying this equality to
sf
, with
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
, and performing a series expansion around 0, we obtain the equality

{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}fdX}\right]=\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}fd\nu.

A more classical way of defining a Poisson process is by mean of this important property. For every disjoint measurable subsets
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}
of
{\mathcal{X}}
,
X(A_{1}),\ldots,X(A_{n})
are independent Poisson random variables with means
\nu(A_{1}),\ldots,\nu(A_{n})
respectively. By convention, a Poisson distribution with mean 0 is the Dirac mass at 0.

5.1.
The statistical framework

We observe
n\geqslant 1
pairs
(w_{1},X_{1}),\ldots,(w_{n},X_{n})
of random variables where the
w_{i}
are deterministic covariates with values in
{\mathscr{W}}
and
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are (almost surely) finite point processes on a measured space
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
.

In order to model our data, we assume that there exist
n
independent Poisson point processes
X^{\star}_{1},\ldots,X^{\star}_{n}
with finite intensity measures
\nu^{\star}_{1},\ldots,\nu^{\star}_{n}
respectively, such that the cardinality of the set
X_{i}\ominus X_{i}^{\star}=(X_{i}\setminus X_{i}^{\star})\cup(X_{i}^{\star}\setminus X_{i})
is small enough, at least for most of these indices
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. This means that each
X_{i}
is regarded as a corrupted version of a genuine Poisson random variable
X_{i}^{\star}
from which some points have possibly been removed or added. The misspecification of our model will be partly measured by the random variable

N=\sum_{i=1}^{n}N_{i}\quad\text{with}\quad N_{i}=\left|{X_{i}\ominus X_{i}^{{}^{\star}}}\right|\quad\text{for }i\in\{1,\ldots,n\},

which counts how many points lie in the symmetric difference between the subsets
X_{i}^{\star}
and
X_{i}
. The random variable
N
can also account for the fact that a subset of our dataset may consist of dependent random variables that could be a source of contamination. We denote by
t_{\xi}
the
(1-e^{-\xi})
-quantile of
N
for
\xi>0
.

To model the intensities
\nu^{\star}_{1},\ldots,\nu^{\star}_{n}
, we consider a set
\Theta
of functions
\theta
on
{\mathscr{W}}\times{\mathcal{X}}
for which
\theta_{i}:x\mapsto\theta(w_{i},x)
is nonnegative and integrable on
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. For
\theta\in\Theta
and
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
\nu_{\theta,i}=\theta_{i}\cdot\mu=\theta(w_{i},\cdot)\cdot\mu
defines thus a finite measure on
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{A}})
and we shall do as if there existed
\theta^{\star}\in\Theta
such that
\nu_{i}^{\star}=\nu_{\theta^{\star},i}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Our goal is therefore to estimate
\theta^{\star}
from the observation
{\boldsymbol{X}}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
and the knowledge of
w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}
.

Given two finite measures
\nu
and
\nu^{\prime}
which are dominated by
\overline{\nu}
on
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{A}})
, we set

H^{2}(\nu,\nu^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}\left({\sqrt{\frac{d\nu}{d\overline{\nu}}}-\sqrt{\frac{d\nu^{\prime}}{d\overline{\nu}}}}\right)^{2}d\overline{\nu},

the result being independent of the choice of
\overline{\nu}
. For an
n
-tuple
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}=(\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{n})
of finite measures on
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{A}})
and
\theta\in\Theta
, we write

{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\theta}=(\nu_{\theta,1},\ldots,\nu_{\theta,n})\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathbf{H}}^{2}({\boldsymbol{\nu}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\theta})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}H^{2}(\nu_{i},\nu_{\theta,i}).

We equip our parameter space
\Theta
with the loss
\ell(\theta,\theta^{\prime})={\mathbf{H}}^{2}({\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\theta},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\theta^{\prime}})
for
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
and a
\sigma
-algebra that makes the corresponding balls measurable. This turns
\Theta
into a measurable space that can be equipped with a prior
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
.

5.2.
The test statistic and the property of the posterior

We use the test statistic defined for
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
by

(32)

{\mathbf{T}}_{2}({\boldsymbol{X}},\theta,\theta^{\prime})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[{\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}\psi\left({\sqrt{\frac{\theta_{i}^{\prime}}{\theta_{i}}}}\right)dX_{i}+\frac{1}{4}\left({\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}\theta_{i}d\mu-\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}\theta_{i}^{\prime}d\mu}\right)}\right].

A different one was proposed in Sart
(

)
in the frequentist setting to define

-estimators. The following result shows that ours possesses the required properties.

Proposition 3

The values

5.3.
An example

In this example
{\mathscr{W}}
denotes the unit sphere of
{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
, where the dimension
k\geqslant 2
is typically large, and
{\mathcal{X}}
a measurable subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
d\geqslant 1
, equipped with the Lebesgue measure
\mu
. We observe
n
point processes
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
on
{\mathcal{X}}
and assume that
X_{i}
is close to a Poisson process
X_{i}^{\star}
on
{\mathcal{X}}
in the sense
N_{i}=|X_{i}\ominus X_{i}^{\star}|
is small enough, at least for most of the indices
i
. The
X_{i}^{\star}
are assumed to be independent and when
X_{i}
is associated with a covariate
w_{i}\in{\mathscr{W}}
, we assume that the intensity
\nu_{i}^{\star}
of
X_{i}^{\star}
admits a density
\theta_{i}^{\star}=\theta^{\star}(w_{i},\cdot)
with respect to
\mu
which is of the form
\theta_{i}^{\star}:x\mapsto\left({\rho^{\star}\left\langle w_{i},a^{\star}\right\rangle s^{\star}(x)}\right)^{2}
where
\rho^{\star}
is a positive number,
s^{\star}
belongs to the unit sphere of the Hilbert space
{\mathbb{H}}={\mathbb{L}}_{2}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
whilst
a^{\star}
belongs to
{\mathscr{W}}
. Note that the average cardinality of
X_{i}^{\star}
is
\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}\left({\rho^{\star}\left\langle w_{i},a^{\star}\right\rangle s^{\star}(x)}\right)^{2}d\mu(x)=\left({\rho^{\star}\left\langle w_{i},a^{\star}\right\rangle}\right)^{2}
. It therefore ranges in
[0,(\rho^{\star})^{2}]
and depends on the angle between the covariate
w_{i}
and the direction
a^{\star}
. The Hilbert norm of
{\mathbb{H}}
will be denoted
\left\|{\cdot}\right\|
throughout this section.

Since
k
is assumed to be large, we wish to reduce the dimensionality of the problem by assuming some sparcity on the parameter
a^{\star}
. This means that the parameter
a^{\star}
belongs to a set of the form
A_{J}=\{a\in{\mathscr{W}}|\;a_{j}=0,\text{ for }j\not\in J\}
for a certain nonempty subset
J=J^{\star}
of
\{1,\ldots,k\}
the cardinality of which is expected to be small compared to
k
. We denote by
{\mathcal{J}}
the class of these nonempty subsets
\{1,\ldots,k\}
.

To approximate
s^{\star}
, we consider an at most countable collection of finite dimensional linear subspaces
\{V_{m},\;m\in{\mathcal{M}}\}
of
{\mathbb{H}}
with
V_{m}\neq\{0\}
for every
m\in{\mathcal{M}}
. We denote by
D_{m}
the dimension of
V_{m}
and by
S_{m}
its unit sphere for
m\in{\mathcal{M}}
.

Our parameter space
\Theta
therefore consists of these functions
\theta
on
{\mathscr{W}}\times{\mathcal{X}}
which are of the form
\theta_{\rho,a,s}:(w,x)\mapsto\left({\rho\left\langle a,w\right\rangle s(x)}\right)^{2}
where the parameter
(\rho,a,s)
belongs to one the sets
\Gamma_{J,m}=(0,+\infty)\times A_{J}\times S_{m}
with
(J,m)\in{\mathcal{J}}\times{\mathcal{M}}
, that is,

\Theta=\left\{{\theta_{\rho,a,s},\;(\rho,a,s)\in\bigcup_{(J,m)\in{\mathcal{J}}\times{\mathcal{M}}}\Gamma_{J,m}}\right\}.

To keep our notation as simple as possible, we denote by
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\rho,a,s}
for
n
-tuple of intensities
(\nu_{\theta,1},\ldots,\nu_{\theta,n})
when
\theta=\theta_{\rho,a,s}
. For
(\rho,a,s)
and
(\varrho,b,t)
in
(0,+\infty)\times{\mathscr{W}}\times{\mathbb{H}}
, we note that

\displaystyle{\mathbf{H}}^{2}\left({{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\rho,a,s},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\varrho,a,t}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left({\rho-\varrho}\right)^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}\left({\sqrt{\left({\left\langle a,w_{i}\right\rangle s(x)}\right)^{2}}}\right)^{2}d\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\left({\rho-\varrho}\right)^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|{\left\langle a,w_{i}\right\rangle}\right|^{2}\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}s^{2}(x)d\mu(x)\leqslant n\left({\rho-\varrho}\right)^{2}.

We obtain similarly that

\displaystyle{\mathbf{H}}^{2}\left({{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\rho,a,s},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\rho,a,t}}\right)

\displaystyle=\rho^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}\left({\sqrt{\left({\left\langle a,w_{i}\right\rangle s(x)}\right)^{2}}-\sqrt{\left({\left\langle a,w_{i}\right\rangle t(x)}\right)^{2}}}\right)^{2}d\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\rho^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|{\left\langle a,w_{i}\right\rangle}\right|^{2}\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}\left({\left|{s(x)}\right|-\left|{t(x)}\right|}\right)^{2}d\mu

(36)

\displaystyle\leqslant\rho^{2}\left\|{s-t}\right\|^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|{\left\langle a,w_{i}\right\rangle}\right|^{2}\leqslant n\rho^{2}\left\|{s-t}\right\|^{2},

whilst, since
\left\|{s}\right\|=1
,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{H}}^{2}\left({{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\rho,a,s},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\rho,b,s}}\right)

\displaystyle=\rho^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}\left({\sqrt{\left({\left\langle a,w_{i}\right\rangle s(x)}\right)^{2}}-\sqrt{\left({\left\langle b,w_{i}\right\rangle s(x)}\right)^{2}}}\right)d\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\rho^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left({\left|{\left\langle a,w_{i}\right\rangle}\right|-\left|{\left\langle b,w_{i}\right\rangle}\right|}\right)^{2}\leqslant\rho^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|{\left\langle a-b,w_{i}\right\rangle}\right|^{2}\leqslant n\rho^{2}\left|{a-b}\right|^{2}.

Consequently, for every
(\rho,a,s)
and
(\varrho,b,t)
in
(0,+\infty)\times{\mathscr{W}}\times{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle{\mathbf{H}}^{2}\left({{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\rho,a,s},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\varrho,b,t}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\left({{\mathbf{H}}\left({{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\rho,a,s},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\rho,b,s}}\right)+{\mathbf{H}}\left({{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\rho,b,s},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\rho,b,t}}\right)+{\mathbf{H}}\left({{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\rho,b,t},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\varrho,b,t}}\right)}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant 3\left[{{\mathbf{H}}^{2}\left({{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\rho,a,s},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\rho,b,s}}\right)+{\mathbf{H}}^{2}\left({{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\rho,b,s},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\rho,b,t}}\right)+{\mathbf{H}}^{2}\left({{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\rho,b,t},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\varrho,b,t}}\right)}\right]

(37)

\displaystyle\leqslant 3n\left[{\rho^{2}\left|{a-b}\right|^{2}+\rho^{2}\left\|{s-t}\right\|^{2}+\left|{\rho-\varrho}\right|^{2}}\right].

Let us now define a prior on
\Theta
.
Given
(J,m)\in{\mathcal{J}}\times{\mathcal{M}}
, we consider an orthonormal basis
\phi_{1,m},\ldots,\phi_{D_{m},m}
of
V_{m}
and three independent random variables
R
,
{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{\!\!J}=(Y_{1,J},\ldots,Y_{k,J})
and
{\boldsymbol{Z}}_{m}=(Z_{1,m},\ldots Z_{D_{m},m})
with values in
(0,+\infty)
,
{\mathscr{W}}
and the unit sphere of
{\mathbb{R}}^{D_{m}}
respectively. The random variable
R
has density
x\mapsto(2/\pi)(1+x^{2})^{-1}
on
(0,+\infty)
,
{\boldsymbol{Z}}_{m}
and
(Y_{j,J})_{j\in J}
are uniformly distributed on the unit spheres of
{\mathbb{R}}^{D_{m}}
and
{\mathbb{R}}^{|J|}
respectively whilst
Y_{j,J}=0
for
j\not\in J
. We denote by
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{J,m}
the image of the distribution of the random variable
(R,Y_{\!\!J},\sum_{j=1}^{D_{m}}Z_{j,m}\phi_{j,m})\in\Gamma_{J,m}
by the mapping
(\rho,a,s)\mapsto\theta_{\rho,a,s}
which yields a probability measure on
\Theta
. Given a collection
\{L_{J,m},\;(J,m)\in{\mathcal{J}}\times{\mathcal{M}}\}
of positive weights that satisfy
\sum_{(J,m)\in{\mathcal{J}}\times{\mathcal{M}}}e^{-L_{J,m}}=1
, we define our prior on
\Theta
as

(38)

{\boldsymbol{\pi}}=\sum_{(J,m)\in{\mathcal{J}}\times{\mathcal{M}}}e^{-L_{J,m}}{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{J,m}.

The following proposition holds.

Proposition 4
.

Let
(J,m)\in{\mathcal{J}}\times{\mathcal{M}}
and
(\rho,a,s)\in\Gamma_{J,m}
. Then,

(39)

\displaystyle\overline{r}\left({{\boldsymbol{\pi}},\theta_{\rho,a,s}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{|J|+D_{m}}{\gamma}\log\left({e+\sqrt{\frac{9\gamma n(1+\rho)^{2}}{(|J|+D_{m})\vee L_{J,m}}}}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\frac{1}{\gamma}\log\left({\frac{\pi^{2}(1+\rho)\sqrt{|J|D_{m}}}{2}}\right)+\frac{L_{J,m}}{\gamma}.

Besides, if
s^{\star}
is an arbitrary point on the unit sphere of
{\mathbb{H}}
,

(40)

\inf_{(\rho,a,s)\in\Gamma_{m}}{\mathbf{H}}^{2}\left({\theta_{\rho,a,s^{\star}},\theta_{\rho,a,s}}\right)\leqslant 2n\rho^{2}\inf_{v\in V_{m}}\left\|{s^{\star}-v}\right\|^{2}=2n\inf_{v\in V_{m}}\left\|{\rho s^{\star}-v}\right\|^{2}.

We deduce from Theorem
2
the following result.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\xi>\ln 3
,
(\lambda,\beta)\in(0,+\infty)\times(0,1)
that satisfies the constraints given by (
33
). An estimator
\widehat{\theta}
drawn with the posterior distribution
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}
defined by (
15
) with
{\mathbf{T}}={\mathbf{T}}_{2}
given in (
32
), possesses the following property. With a probability at least
1-3e^{-\xi}

\displaystyle Cn^{-1}{\mathbf{H}}^{2}\left({{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\theta_{\rho^{\star},a^{\star},s^{\star}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\widehat{\theta}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\inf_{m\in{\mathcal{M}}}\left[{\inf_{v\in V_{m}}\left\|{\rho^{\star}s^{\star}-v_{m}}\right\|^{2}+\frac{|J^{\star}|+D_{m}}{n}\log_{+}\left[{\frac{n(1+\rho^{\star})}{(|J^{\star}|+D_{m})\vee L_{J^{\star},m}}}\right]+\frac{L_{J^{\star},m}}{n}}\right]+\frac{t_{\xi}+\xi}{n},

where
C=C(\lambda,\beta)>0
is a numerical constant that only depends on the chosen values of
\lambda
and
\beta
.

For
m\in{\mathcal{M}}
, the quantity
\inf_{v\in V_{m}}\left\|{\rho^{\star}s^{\star}-v_{m}}\right\|^{2}
corresponds to the approximation of
\rho^{\star}s^{\star}
by an element of our linear space
V_{m}
. Up to the logarithmic term,
(|J^{\star}|+D_{m})/n
is the statistical error we would get for estimating the parameter
(\rho^{\star},a^{\star},s^{\star})
if it belonged to
\Gamma_{J^{\star},m}
. The quantity
t_{\xi}
accounts for the robustness of the procedure with respect to a possible departure from the assumption that our data
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are truly independent Poisson random variables. Finally, the term
L_{J^{\star},m}
depends on our choice of the weights. For example, denoting by
K_{D}
the quantity
\left|{\{m\in{\mathcal{M}},\;D_{m}=D\}}\right|
for
D\geqslant 1
, one may take for every
(J,m)\in{\mathcal{J}}\times{\mathcal{M}}
,
L_{J,m}=L_{1,|J|}+L_{2,D_{m}}
with, for
j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
and
D\geqslant 1
,

L_{1,j}=\log\binom{k}{j}+\log k\;\text{ and }\;L_{2,D}=\log\left({K_{D}\vee 1}\right)+D+\log\left({\sum_{D^{\prime}\geqslant 1}(K_{D^{\prime}}\wedge 1)e^{-D^{\prime}}}\right).

Since
K_{D}/(K_{D}\vee 1)=K_{D}\wedge 1
for every
D\geqslant 1
, we can check that

\displaystyle\sum_{(J,m)\in{\mathcal{J}}\times{\mathcal{M}}}e^{-L_{J,m}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\binom{k}{j}e^{-L_{1,j}}\sum_{D\geqslant 1}K_{D}e^{-L_{2,D}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{\binom{k}{j}}{\binom{k}{j}k}\sum_{D\geqslant 1}\frac{K_{D}}{K_{D}\vee 1}\frac{e^{-D}}{\sum_{D^{\prime}\geqslant 1}(K_{D^{\prime}}\wedge 1)e^{-D^{\prime}}}=1.

If there exists
c>0
such that
K_{D}\leqslant\exp(cD)
for every
D\geqslant 1
, it follows from the inequality
\binom{k}{j}\leqslant k^{j}
for
j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
that
L_{J,m}\leqslant(|J|+1)\log k+(c+1)D_{m}
for every
(J,m)\in{\mathcal{J}}\times{\mathcal{M}}
. For such a choice, our previous bound
n^{-1}{\mathbf{H}}^{2}\left({{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\theta_{\rho^{\star},a^{\star},s^{\star}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\widehat{\theta}}}\right)
remains then of the same order up to a possible additional term of order
(|J^{\star}|+1)\log k
.

5.4.
The case of a Poisson process with no covariates

Let us now model our observations
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
by making the assumption that they are simply
n
i.i.d. Poisson processes and that their common intensity
\nu_{\theta^{\star}}
is of the form
\theta^{\star}\cdot\mu
where
\theta^{\star}
belongs to a given class
\Theta
of nonnegative integrable fonctions on
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
. If
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
were genuine i.i.d. Poisson processes with the same intensity
\nu_{\theta^{\star}}
, their sum
S=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
would also be a Poisson process but with intensity
n\nu_{\theta^{\star}}=(n\theta^{\star})\cdot\mu
. In place of the
n
-tuple
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
