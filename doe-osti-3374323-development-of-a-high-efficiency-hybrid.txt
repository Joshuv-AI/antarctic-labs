3. Executive Summary:
The SunShot Initiative, announced in 2011, launched multiple technology development
programs to substantially reduce the cost of large-scale solar energy in order to bring
parity with other forms of energy production and promote utility market penetration and
adoption. These development efforts identified merit in concentrating solar power (CSP)
installations to support both baseload and peak-demand power, with particular interest
identified in supercritical CO2 (sCO2) power cycles.
Prior to this research program, very little data existed in the literature for pre-cooler
performance in an sCO2 recompression Brayton cycle. Since the initiation of the SunShot
program, significant strides have been made in the areas of sCO2 power cycles,
turbomachinery, recuperators, and primary heaters. Comparatively, the pre-cooler had
received significantly less attention because it is less technically challenging than other
components in the power block. The referenced literature studies utilized a highly
simplified pre-cooler model when simulating the power cycle performance, even at off-
design; simplified models most often assumed a constant CO2 outlet temperature at the
compressor design point. Unless the CSP plant is installed near a large body of water or
other large constant-temperature heat sink, this assumption is not appropriate as it will
not be physically realized. The cooler performance and thus the compressor inlet
temperature will both be impacted by the ambient temperature. This in turn impacts the
cycle efficiency, output power, and ultimately the economics of the plant.
During this program, the project team demonstrated, published, and presented the impact
that the primary pre-cooler has on the cycle and plant performance. The team

                                        Page 2 of 113

demonstrated the importance of air-side performance, the impact of air-side power
consumption, and the consequences of a poorly designed cooler.
In addition to modeling parametric cycle performance, the project team approached the
challenge of optimizing dry cooling in CSP applications. Dry cooling is practically
mandatory for CSP applications, as many geographical locations with high irradiance also
tend to be arid with limited access to water; additionally, water consumption has been
identified as a key metric for the technical and economic feasibility of CSP plants.
Reducing water consumption through the integration of a directly coupled dry cooler
provides several benefits; however, traditional dry cooler arrays are very large in
comparison to the comparatively compact size of the other components in an sCO2 power
block. Targeting the 2030 SunShot program goal of 5 ¢/kWh for baseload CSP
installations, this team proposed to design, fabricate, and test a compact dry cooler that
could meet SunShot power cycle performance targets, reduce installation footprint, and
reduce the dry cooler contribution to CSP plant LCOE.
This program investigated the application of a compact dry cooler composed of
brazed/diffusion bonded microchannel passages on the CO2 side stacked against formed
fins on the air side. The compact heat exchanger was mated to centrifugal fans and
transition ducting on the air side to reduce installation footprint and evenly distribute the
air across the fins. At the onset of this program, the technology was considered to be
TRL-2.
The designed compact dry cooler system is expected to decrease installation footprint by
approximately 50% for a 10+ MWth installation, as compared to a commercial finned-
tube, crossflow fan cooler. This compact heat exchanger design also shows improved
heat transfer performance with economically feasible approach temperatures down to
7°C (CO2 approach to ambient temperature at the design point of 25°C). At this reduced
footprint and improved heat transfer performance, the commercialized unit has a
predicted capital cost in the range of 92.2-115.3 $/kWth. Comparing to commercially
sourced fin-fan dry coolers, this compact dry cooler is expected to reduce plant LCOE
(calculated over a 30-year life) from 6.04 ¢/kWh to 5.85-5.94 ¢/kWh; note that this LCOE
is calculated using the SunShot baseline values for all components outside the power
block. Off-design power cycle performance was modeled for the baseline and compact
dry cooler using vendor-supplied turbomachinery maps and discretized heat exchanger
models over a wide range of ambient temperatures.
It is noted that a compact dry cooler sacrifices air-side pressure drop for installation size
(footprint). The fan power consumption on the compact dry cooler is higher than in
traditional fin-fan coolers; however, the improvement in power cycle performance with
reduced approach temperature outweighs the penalty of the increased parasitic power
loss [P1]. It was found that the compact dry cooler increased plant capacity during
summer months where revenue per produced power is higher. It was also found that
annual energy production and annual revenue both increased when utilizing the compact
dry cooler as compared to the baseline fin-fan cooler.
Fabrication trials and manufacture of a MW-scale unit were pursued in this program. A
demonstration unit was designed and fabricated using a fully aluminum heat exchanger
core; this choice of material showed promise to reduce cost relative to a more

                                        Page 3 of 113

conventional diffusion bonded stainless heat exchanger. During construction, it was found
that chemical etching of aluminum plates was challenging, and this is the primary
technical challenge that persists. Successful trials were completed for short stacks of CO2
plates and formed fins (double-banked to reduce air-side pressure drop) with manifolding
headers welded on, and burst pressure tests on the aluminum 6061 high-pressure side
produced favorable results that exceeded the required values for a sCO2 dry cooler.
However, the lessons learned on the small scale prototypes did not perform as expected
at the MW scale. Slight deformations in the fin layers (at brazing temperatures) increased
the gap between plates, thus the braze foils did not fully seal at all joints. The internal leak
paths in the heat exchanger cores proved detrimental.
A subsequent 1 MWth compact heat exchanger was designed, fabricated, and delivered
to a commercial client, directly leveraging the lessons learned from this program. The
commercial unit has been installed in the Sandia Gen3 Particle Pilot Plant (G3P3), and
the unit will be tested as part of a full sCO2 recompression Brayton cycle with
concentrated solar thermal input. At current, this technology is considered TRL-7.
Pending successful commissioning and testing of the commercial unit, the compact dry
cooler will bridge to TRL-8.

6. Background:
Application Context & Baseline Research
The SunShot Initiative, announced in 2011, launched multiple technology development
programs to substantially reduce the cost of large-scale solar energy in order to bring
parity with other forms of energy production and promote utility market penetration and
adoption. These development efforts identified merit in concentrating solar power (CSP)
installations to support both baseload and peak-demand power, with particular interest
identified in supercritical CO2 (sCO2) power cycles. See a reference CSP installation
schematic presented in Figure 1.

 Figure 1. CSP Plant Schematic Summarizing SETO Program Technical Targets (2019) [1]; this Project Focused on
 Enhancements to the Power Block Section.

In support of these findings and goals, the 2018 SETO FOA 1840 [3] solicited (among
other topics) component innovations for the sCO2 recompression Brayton cycle (RCBC)
(reference Figure 2) to further reduce the levelized cost of electricity (LCOE) of CSP
plants toward the 2030 SunShot targets; the key metrics targeted were 5¢/kWh for
baseload CSP plants (including ≥ 12 hours of thermal energy storage) and 10¢/kWh for
peaker CSP plants (including ≤ 6 hours of thermal energy storage). Other specific metrics
of interest for the power cycle included thermal efficiency > 50%, power cycle capital cost
of less than $900/kW (including heat exchange into the power cycle), compatibility with
dry cooling, and ≥ 30-year plant operating life. In response to this solicitation, the team of
Southwest Research Institute (SwRI) and Vacuum Process Engineering, Inc. (VPE)
proposed a dry cooler research and development project aiming to (1) reduce the dry
                                                 Page 8 of 113

cooler contribution to LCOE, (2) improve cooler performance, and (3) reduce overall
installation footprint.

 Figure 2. Schematic of a sCO2 Recompression Brayton Cycle (RCBC) [2]; this Project Focused on Enhancements to the Air-
 Cooled Pre-Cooler.

Over the past decade, research in CSP coupled with sCO2 power cycles has received
funding globally; programs include those funded by the US DOE (i.e., SunShot and
APOLLO), SCARABEUS (Supercritical CARbon dioxide/Alternative fluids Blends for
Efficiency Upgrade of Solar power plants) funded by the European Commission,
SOLARSCO2OL funded by EU H2020, Australian Solar Thermal Research Initiative
(ASTRI), and National Science Foundation of China, among others. A summary of
relevant published work is provided here as background and context for the present
research program.
As noted above, the cost analyses reported by SETO (2021) and the National Renewable
Energy Laboratory (NREL) (2019) defined the LCOE target for CSP to competitively enter
the market for grid support is 5¢/kWh for baseload CSP plants [4,5]. This LCOE target
follows the prior goal set by SETO, launched as the SunShot initiative in 2011, to reduce
the utility-scale LCOE to 6¢/kWh by 2020 [4]. Efforts within the industry have significantly
reduced the LCOE, driving down from 21¢/kWh in 2010 (not including energy storage) to
9.8¢/kWh in 2019 (with ≥12 hours of energy storage) and continued development efforts
driving toward the 2030 target of 5¢/kWh [4]. Relevant development work performed over
this period (prior to the present research program) includes:
 • 2010-2014: sCO2 cycle studies demonstrating the advantages of recompression
 [6,7],
 • 2017: cycle studies considering the off-design and dynamic performance by
 varying the hot-side and cold-side temperatures of the cycle [8,9],
 • 2017: LCOE studies demonstrating reduction strategies for CSP installations with
 a central tower and an sCO2 power cycle [10],
    •    2014-2018: sCO2 turbomachinery design studies [11-14],

                                                   Page 9 of 113

 • 2018: minimizing cost of electricity for specific applications [15],
 • 2018-2019: studies quantifying the impact of the high-side pressure and
 temperature on cycle performance [14,16], and
 • 2019: additional cycle studies focused on comparing various cycle configurations
 [16,17].
At the commencement of this project, the overarching goal was to incrementally reduce
the LCOE contribution from all systems within the CSP plant, including all sub-systems
within the power block. From the U.S. Department of Energy sCO2 Workshop in 2019 [1],
LCOE contributions from the power block required a 2¢/kWh decrease (from the 2017
baseline) in order to meet the 2030 goal (Figure 3).

  Figure 3. U.S Department of Energy Research and Development on sCO2 Power Cycles, Pathway to 5 Cents per kWh for
 Baseload CSP [1]

It was expected that the power block cooler cost and performance could impact the plant-
level LCOE, but that its individual contribution would be small, perhaps needing to be
reported as a fraction of the power block contribution. Research into the power block
cooler prior to 2019 was minimal, because this was considered to be a commercially
available component with high TRL. The cooler is on the low-temperature and low-
pressure side of the power cycle, and multiple commercial coolers exist that can operate
in this pressure and temperature range; therefore, it is expected that a cooler can be
designed to meet the target conditions. Power cycle research studies up through 2019
[7-10, 14-17] have largely simplified the sCO2 power cycle model by defining a constant
CO2 temperature at the cooler outlet, even though studies have shown that the main
compressor inlet temperature (CO2 temperature exiting the cooler) has a significant
impact on cycle efficiency [8,18]. At the 2019 SETO sCO2 workshop [2], it was highlighted
that the power cycle performance can be impacted by daily and seasonal changes in
ambient temperatures, but the off-design performance impacts for CSP installations had
not yet been documented in the literature.
The baseline dry cooler literature referenced at inception of this project consisted of the
research results presented by Moisseytsev et al. (2017) [19] and Weiland (2019) [18].

                                                 Page 10 of 113

The study by Moisseytsev et al. [19] considered multiple dry cooled heat exchanger
configurations for sCO2 cycles, highlighting the feasibility of dry cooling as one of the
major advantages of sCO2 power cycles because it significantly expands the siting
options for power plants if water access is not required. This study considered the
baseline to be commercial dry coolers with large axial fans blowing upward across
horizontal arrays of finned tubes (fin-fan coolers). This study then considered diffusion-
bonded heat exchangers with air in counter flow and cross flow. This paper presents
results for various diffusion bonded heat exchanger configurations, varying physical size,
number of units, and channel design (zigzag angle and number of passes). Results show
that a crossflow configuration is superior for reducing air-side pressure loss. Generally,
the diffusion-bonded heat exchangers were predicted to significantly reduce the size of
the heat exchanger but significantly increase the heat exchanger cost (7X cost increase
or greater compared to commercial fin-fan units).
Proposed Research Program
Based on the prior research and the current state of technology at the time of proposal,
the project team proposed to investigate a compact hybrid dry cooler design composed
of stainless-steel diffusion bonded microchannels on the CO2 side, alternated with brazed
aluminum formed fins on the air side. The proposed heat exchanger was described as a
‘hybrid’ dry cooler due to the dissimilar materials and the combination of bonding
processes. The selection of aluminum was expected to improve heat transfer and reduce
overall capital cost.
The proposed heat exchanger core would be coupled with a high efficiency centrifugal
blower and transition duct; inclusion of the duct provides the benefits of (i) controlling
airflow to reduce the inefficiencies caused by air-maldistribution across the fins, (ii)
improving environmental protection to reduce the risk of corrosion and fouling on the fins,
and (iii) minimizing the amount of downtime needed to service the dry cooler. Reducing
fouling risk and maintenance cycles will reduce operational costs and improve on-line
time; both are expected to reduce LCOE.
Commercial dry cooling technologies exist, but a reduction in footprint could hold merit
for sCO2 power cycles where the piping, recuperators, and turbomachinery skids are
compact. If utilizing current state of the art technology, the dry cooler installation would
dominate the footprint for the power block installation. Reducing the dry cooler size and
footprint also presents capital cost savings in the associated transport/logistics and the
infrastructure and foundation (civil work). Based on past experience, installed cost is
nearly three times the hardware cost once structural, electrical, and process connections
are completed. Reducing the size and weight of the dry cooler is expected to present
installation cost savings as well.
The initial goal of the program was to maximize heat transfer performance while targeting
a 50% reduction in capital cost, as compared to commercial finned-tube, crossflow fan
coolers. Cost reductions would primarily be accomplished by using a compact heat
exchanger, minimizing the material and fabrication cost.

Parallel and Supporting Research
Weiland (2019) [18] presented technoeconomic and cost modeling for sCO2 power block
components for a natural gas fired plant, including some analysis of the pre-cooler. This
research demonstrates the importance of CO2 cooler outlet temperature and the
approach temperature selection for the cooler at the design point. However, no
consideration is given to the specific cooler geometry and its off-design performance;
again, it is assumed that the cooler can be designed to satisfy the optimal cycle study
specifications.
In research results presented by Pidaparti et al. [20], an extension of the cooler study
data presented by Weiland, an sCO2 RCBC was considered and different pre-cooler
configurations were applied to assess cycle performance and LCOE; this study evaluated
a conventional evaporative cooling tower, a direct dry cooler, an indirect dry cooler with
intermediate water loop, and an adiabatic cooler. From this study, it was found that the
direct dry cooler and adiabatic coolers provided the most desirable results for plant
efficiency and LCOE. The direct dry cooler provided the lowest LCOE for CO2 outlet
temperatures above 26°C; at lower CO2 temperatures, adiabatic and cooling towers
provide some reduction in LCOE. It was also found that direct dry coolers produce plant
efficiencies that are comparable with evaporative cooling towers and adiabatic coolers,
especially above the critical temperature. These results further validated the efforts of this
project, demonstrating that direct dry cooling may be an optimal solution even in areas
where water consumption does not prevent the use of wet coolers. Pidaparti et al. did
expand upon the level of definition around the pre-cooler; however, it is noted that a
simplified heat exchanger model was still utilized. A similar research study was presented
in 2020 for the direct-fired Allam cycle [21] using similar methods; the results here were
also favorable for direct dry cooling.
Parallel and supporting work included the study published by Neises (2020), which
detailed cycle performance of a dry-cooled RCBC, including off-design performance
modeling with variation in ambient temperature and quantification of the cooler fan power
consumption [22]. The study utilized System Advisor Model (SAM), considering a range
of off-design values for ambient temperature, heat transfer fluid (HTF) mass flow, and
HTF inlet temperature. The cycle and cooler trade studies in the current work (DE-
EE0008739) also leveraged SAM to quantify plant LCOE. Due to the similarity between
the current work and Neises, Neises (2020) served as a peer reviewed benchmark to
compare against and build from. More detailed comparison between results is included
in the Results and Discussion section below.
Additional parallel dry cooler research efforts that were referenced later in the project
included the worked presented by Lock et al. (2019) [24], Yu and Li (2022) [25], and Illyes
et al. (2022) [26]; each study showing much more depth in dry cooler modeling. Lock et
al. [24] consider a large diameter finned-tube heat exchanger with multiple passes; both
forced draft and natural draft coolers are analyzed. The study describes the detailed
numerical model utilized, which included discretized two-dimensional cells and fluid
properties analyzed locally to capture the dramatic change in CO2 thermo-physical
properties near the critical point. Conclusions from this study were as follows: (i) constant-
property heat transfer correlations are inaccurate for sCO2, showing up to 27% error; (ii)
forced draft coolers performed marginally better than natural draft coolers at elevated

                                        Page 12 of 113

ambient temperatures, but they performed comparably near design point temperatures;
and (iii) uncertainty still exists around buoyancy in sCO2 heat exchangers and if local
buoyant behavior will impact heat transfer.
Yu and Li [25] compared direct dry and indirect dry cooling for sCO2 Brayton cycles. This
study considered a finned-tube array with mechanical fans for the direct dry-cooled
system; the indirect dry-cooled system was composed of a shell and tube heat exchanger
cooled by an intermediate water loop and a natural-draft dry cooling tower. At elevated
ambient temperatures, this study predicted the net power generation to drop off more
quickly for the direct dry cooled cycle as compared to the indirect cooled cycle; ultimately,
the indirect dry cooler configuration has a lower LCOE.
The study by Illyes et al. [26] presented results for a finned-tube air-cooled condenser
with heat transfer enhancing features applied to a blended-refrigerant sCO2 power cycle.
This heat exchanger included wavy fins on the exterior of the CO2 tubes and internal
microfins to improve heat exchange between fluids. This study also leveraged discretized
heat transfer calculations on the CO2 side and air side to improve accuracy, then validated
the heat transfer correlations through lab-scale testing.
Outside of the literature, several CSP demonstration efforts were progressing in parallel
with the current research program, namely Heliogen TESTBED, Sandia Gen3 Particle
Pilot Plant (G3P3), and Supercritical Transformational Electric Power (STEP). Each
program was considering a sCO2 RCBC with a commercially sourced pre-cooler. The
cooler selections are noted here for reference for comparison with the information
presented on this program; the Heliogen TESTBED project selected an adiabatic cooler
for the packaged power block, the STEP project selected an indirect cooler with a water
to CO2 heat exchanger and an evaporative cooling tower, and the Sandia G3P3 project
selected a stainless steel compact dry cooler (of the type designed in this program).
Finally, this project team also supported a research team developing an optimization tool
for sizing fin-fan type dry coolers for CSP applications. The tool selects cooler parameters
such as tube size, fin size, fin spacing, number of tubes, number of passes, air flowrate,
etc. to optimize based on geographic location, cooler performance, and cooler cost. Initial
results for this optimizer tool are published in [27]. The team is currently pursuing a journal
publication to archive subsequent results.

7. Project Objectives:
The primary objectives of this project were to develop a SunShot-compatible power cycle
that incorporates dry cooling by (1) identifying key areas for CAPEX reduction and (2)
designing and validating a dry cooling system that will realize these improvements. The
SunShot targets include baseload plant LCOE at or below 5¢/kWh, power cycle thermal
efficiency > 50%, a power block capital cost at or below $900/kW, and ≥ 30-year plant
operating life. Demonstrating a CSP cycle that can meet these goals will further build
industry confidence to accelerate market adoption. Identifying, demonstrating, and
deploying low-carbon baseload power is paramount in meeting the national and global
emissions reduction targets, namely net zero by 2050 to limit global temperature rise to
1.5°C.

                                        Page 13 of 113

The goals of the current project were to better understand the cost impact of the dry cooler
portion of the sCO2 power cycle, determine the best path for reducing dry cooler
contribution to plant LCOE, and to design, fabricate, and test a novel hybrid dry-cooled
heat exchanger. Demonstration of an efficient, dry-cooled cycle reduces siting restrictions
for CSP installations using sCO2 power cycles, which may allow for more pilot
demonstrations and shortened market adoption timeline.
The proposed program consisted of two budget periods. The first period focused on cycle
modeling efforts, sensitivity studies, hybrid dry cooler design, and fabrication,
qualification, and testing of prototype scale hybrid heat exchangers. The second budget
period focused on fabrication of the novel MW-scale heat exchanger design, qualification,
packaging, and performance testing using sCO2. The detailed scope of work and
proposed project milestones are summarized below.

Task 1.1 – sCO2 Cycle and CSP Baseline System Modeling
Power Cycle Model Setup
The dry cooler in this study was designed for an RCBC, to be installed upstream of the
first stage main compressor. A fully defined power cycle model was evaluated using
Numerical Propulsion System Simulation (NPSS). The NPSS model calculated on-design
and off-design performance for all turbomachines and heat exchangers. The flow
networks considered in this study include the closed-loop sCO2 cycle, the pre-cooler air-
side stream, and the heat transfer fluid (HTF) stream through the primary heater. For
reference, NIST REFPROP equations of state were used to calculate the fluid properties
for CO2 and air; a representative molten salt fluid property table from Python CoolProp
was utilized for the HTF (here a chloride salt or similar would be required to support the
elevated operating temperatures). See a power cycle schematic with the design-point
operating conditions presented in Figure 4.

                                      Page 17 of 113

 Figure 4. Schematic of Recompression Brayton Cycle, including Design Point Definition for Turbomachines and Heat
 Exchangers

This cycle study leveraged the existing definitions for the turbomachinery, recuperators,
and primary heater from Schmitt et al. [10] and Bennet et al. [8], including cost estimations
for all components [10]; note these studies were performed under DOE Award number
DE-EE0007114 by SwRI and Hanwha Power Systems Americas (Hanwha) to develop an
integrally geared sCO2 compander. Fixed turbomachinery maps were programmed into
NPSS for the two-stage main compressor, two-stage recompressor, and four-stage
turbine; maps for isentropic head and isentropic efficiency for each stage were supplied
by Hanwha. The turbomachines were modeled as fixed speed. Turbomachines were
designed for a wide operating range, but acceptable operation was maintained through
inlet guide vanes installed on the first stage recompressor and inventory control on the
cycle (as needed). The power cycle considered in NPSS produced 9.98 MW net
(calculated as the turbine power less the compressor power) at the design point with a
cycle efficiency of 48.8%. Note that the power output for this cycle was dictated by the
turbomachinery maps utilized. Also note that the cycle thermal efficiency was predicted
to be higher when the compressor inlet temperature is reduced; however, a design-point
compressor inlet temperature of 40.7°C was selected by Hanwha to accommodate the
wide operating range and the large sweep of ambient temperatures with minimal
inventory control changes.

                                                   Page 18 of 113

All heat exchangers in the power cycle were modeled using discretized flow paths
(following the methodology presented by Hoopes et al. [23]), to accurately predict the
pressure drop and heat transfer performance at off-design conditions, capturing the
impact of any internal pinch points. The heat exchanger element in NPSS (1) updates the
fluid properties for each stream at each node and (2) utilizes an internal solver to match
heat transfer to/from each stream at each node throughout the heat exchanger. The
primary heater was modeled with 100 nodes, the low-temperature and high-temperature
recuperators were modeled with 150 nodes, and the pre-cooler was modeled with 150
nodes.
For this project, the simplified pre-cooler model that was used in the previous studies
[8,10] (modelled as a constant CO2 outlet temperature) was replaced with a fully-defined
dry cooler model, to include the discretized flow paths for the air stream and the CO2
stream as well as fan performance curves (as supplied by commercial fan vendors). The
cooler design was varied (including pressure drop values, approach temperature, and fan
selection) by changing the design-point values and performance maps in the NPSS cycle;
this created a slightly different cycle design point for each case (fixing the heat exchanger
geometries with different values [23]) and significantly impacted the off-design cycle
performance. Including the fully defined dry cooler model allowed for the actual
compressor inlet conditions, air-side flowrate, and fan power consumption to be quantified
as they change with the ambient conditions throughout the operating year. This provides
a more accurate estimation of year-round power cycle performance, yielding a more
accurate LCOE value.
To calculate the LCOE for the various dry cooler designs, a large off-design sweep was
conducted within NPSS. The ambient temperature was varied from 0-55°C, the HTF
temperature was varied from 705-735°C, and the HTF mass flow rate was varied from
50-105% of design point value to simulate a variety of ambient conditions along with full-
load and part-load conditions for the power cycle. The combination of these property
sweeps provided 243 off-design performance points which were fed into SAM for CSP
plant performance prediction and calculation of LCOE. LCOE predictions in SAM were
made for a nominal 100 MWe plant (mass flows were multiplied by 10 from the NPSS
cycle), utilizing solar resource and weather data for Daggett, California and default CSP
plant component definitions outside the power cycle. A baseline power block cost of
$754/kWe was used, with a baseline cooler contribution of $25/kWe [8,10]. An
independent NPSS model and SAM model were solved for each pre-cooler design case
to quantify the impact on off-design performance and LCOE based solely on changes in
the dry cooler. For reference, further definition of the NPSS cycle model setup and SAM
model setup can be found in the peer reviewed products of this project [P2] and [P3].

Impact of Simplified Dry Cooler Model
As part of the cycle modeling efforts, the simplified cooler models were compared to the
fully defined cooler model. It can be seen in Figure 7 that, away from the design point
(shown by the yellow circle), the constant CO2 outlet temperature and constant approach
temperature assumptions cannot accurately predict the power cycle performance.
 • At elevated ambient temperatures, the constant outlet temperature assumption
 does not account for the significant reduction in cycle output power.
 • At lower ambient temperatures, the constant approach temperature assumption
 forces the CO2 into liquid phase prior to entering the first stage compressor.
 • Furthermore, in cold ambient conditions, the CO2 inlet temperature to the main
 compressor can be lower than 40.7°C (the design point in this case), which
 improves overall thermal efficiency and net power output; this is shown by the fully
 defined cooler trace in blue (Figure 7). This effect is not captured by the constant
 outlet temperature assumption.

Methods
Throughout the trade studies described in this section, the following methods were used
(also summarized in Figure 8). VPE provided information for various heat exchanger

ambient temperature). For each heat exchanger/fan configuration, the off-design
performance data was calculated independently in NPSS, and the LCOE was
recalculated in SAM.

High-Level Parametric Sensitivity Study
An initial high level sensitivity study was performed to quantify the relative impact of cooler
approach temperature (or effectiveness), fan power consumption, pressure loss, and
capital cost on the plant LCOE. As a first pass, each parameter was varied independently
by +/- 20% from the baseline value. Nominal values considered here were approach
temperature of 12°C, air side pressure drop of 3.65 kPa, CO2 side pressure drop at 1%
of inlet line pressure, and dry cooler capital cost of 139 $/kWth. Approach temperature,
fan power, and pressure losses were altered at the design point in the NPSS cycle, then
results were fed into SAM. Change in capital cost was applied within SAM using the
baseline cycle performance. The relative results are presented in Figure 9. It was found
that changing the fan power consumption has a similar impact to changing the cooler
approach temperature and that both performance factors have a significantly greater
impact than a change in cooler capital cost. The impact of changing the CO2 side pressure
drop had negligible impact on plant LCOE.

 Figure 9. Relative Impact on LCOE of Changing Cooler Approach Temperature, Fan Power, and Capital Cost

In this initial comparison, the capital cost and design parameters were varied
independently. Realistically, the capital cost is driven by the selected design parameters
such as approach temperature and pressure drop, which drive heat exchanger size and
geometry. As a second high-level comparison, the heat exchanger approach
temperature, air-side pressure drop, and CO2 side pressure drop were varied by +/-20%,
and the associated change in capital cost was estimated for the heat exchanger and fan
array for each build. These cycle performance data and associated cooler costs were
then fed into SAM to predict the impact on LCOE. These results are shown below in

Cooler Turndown Scheme
When operating over a large range of ambient conditions, having an adequate cooler
control scheme is critical to maintaining a cycle operating point within the operability range
of the main compressor. At low ambient temperatures, cooler turndown control is required
to maintain the inlet temperature to the main compressor and avoid liquid ingestion.
Multiple cooler control schemes were considered, namely fan on/off control, CO2 bypass
control, fan speed control, and fan inlet damper/guide vane control. It was found that fan
on/off control did not produce favorable compressor inlet temperature control; this method
produced a saw-tooth compressor inlet temperature profile with change in ambient
temperature and was deemed impractical. The remaining three cooler turndown control
schemes were able to maintain a constant compressor inlet temperature at ambient
temperatures below the design point. Each control scheme was exercised over the full
sweep of off-design conditions (0-55°C ambient temperature, 705-735°C HTF
temperature, and 50-105% of design point HTF mass flow). The precooler design point
utilized for this comparison was based on the initial design of the hybrid heat exchanger:
 • Precooler approach temperature: 12°C
 • Air-side pressure drop: 3.65 kPa (14.7 inches of water)
 • CO2-side pressure drop: 1% of inlet pressure

Commercial Air-Side Selections
A wide range of fan quotes were procured to map the space of commercially available
fans for supporting a compact dry cooler design. It was found that the cost per capacity
is higher for larger fans as compared to smaller fans. See this demonstrated in Figure 20.
It was found that unit cost goes up considerably with capacity and power, and the
relationship is non-linear. For this reason, it is more economical to supply air from several
small fans as opposed to fewer large fans; this will also save cost in high-voltage electrical
connections and power supply required by larger fans. Additionally, it was found that for
this application, there were no significant cost or performance benefits favoring axial fans
or centrifugal fans. It was found that centrifugal fans can reach higher flowrates and static
pressures in a single unit, but this design space is not of interest due to the cost and
power consumption implications. In the fan sizes of interest, the quoted operating
efficiencies for centrifugal and axial fans span a similar range. For this reason, the fan
that best couples to the selected heat exchanger geometry with the highest operating
efficiency should be used.

Based on these findings, it is best practice to make the final fan selection first, and then
finalize the heat exchanger core design. Small shifts in the design point on the fan curve
can sacrifice operating efficiency; but small changes can be accommodated easily in the
heat exchanger core by making slight changes to the plate width and length or by
adding/subtracting layers.
In addition, the fan motor size and the fan speed should be considered carefully when
sizing and selecting the air side equipment. Electric motors sized at 250 hp or larger
typically require higher voltage, which can carry significant capital cost for upgrading the
electrical system; this impact is not captured in the fan costs presented above.
Furthermore, running at higher fan speeds may cause the impeller tip speed to become
supersonic, which would greatly reduce fan efficiency; these operating ranges should be
avoided.
Dry Cooler Module Size
Further reduction in LCOE can be achieved by utilizing the optimal cooler module size.
The cost per duty of the hybrid heat exchanger is expected to decrease with increased
module size up to about 8 MWth scale. When utilizing many small heat exchanger
modules, there is a larger packaging cost associated with the headers and nozzles and
the fabrication cost is more labor-dependent. When using fewer large heat exchanger
modules, the cost becomes more material-dependent and the specific packaging cost
decreases. See the dashed traces in Figure 23a for the predicted heat exchanger block
cost considering three different design points; cost data provided by VPE.
Regarding the fan cost, smaller fans are more economical. See the yellow markers and
yellow dashed lines in Figure 23 for the total fan cost per unit. Assuming each heat
exchanger module is coupled to a single fan, optimal module scale is predicted to be
approximately 6.3 MWth (Figure 23a). Further capital cost reduction can be achieved by

coupling multiple fans to a single heat exchanger module (see the various traces in Figure
23b). This allows the advantage of a large heat exchanger module while leveraging the

Design-Point Ambient Temperature Selection
As noted above, cycles producing the highest net power and revenue in the summer
months also produced the lowest LCOE, demonstrating the importance of maintaining the
rated cycle power output during the summer months. To further investigate this impact, a
trade study was conducted considering the design-point ambient temperature for the dry
cooler, namely the ambient temperature up to which the power cycle can produce its full
rated power. At higher ambient temperatures, the CO2 outlet temperature (compressor
inlet temperature) increases necessarily as it is approaching the higher air temperature
(the cooler pinches at the CO2 exit). As the main compressor inlet temperature rises, the
cycle efficiency decreases and the net power output decreases. The power output can be
maintained at the rated value if the mass flow through the loop is increased, which
requires a larger cooler. This larger cooler allows for better performance in hot ambient
conditions but is oversized for majority of the annual operating conditions.

were re-evaluated with a design-point ambient temperature of 50°C. The annual energy
production and revenue are presented for each of the baseline coolers as well as the
hybrid compact dry cooler in Figure 31. The results show that the compact dry cooler
outperforms the fin-fan coolers, producing more energy annually and higher revenue. The
updated LCOE values are presented in Table 9; again, it is shown that the compact cooler
is meeting the baseline LCOE value, indicating that the capital cost for the hybrid dry
cooler system must be reduced to further drive LCOE down.

Impact of Transition Ducting
As indicated in the previous section, the impact of inlet and transition ducting were
considered for the compact dry cooler. One advantage of the compact cooler is the ability
to control and distribute the air evenly among the fins, as well as reduce exposure to the
elements, fouling, and blockage; however, the sensitivity of the fan power consumption
means that losses associated with inlet filtration, inlet ducting, and transition ducting may
not be trivial.
During the project, a full package design was requested for the centrifugal fans. SwRI
worked with Knape Associates (distributor for Northern Blower and Howden) to complete
a design for the 5 MWth heat exchanger module. The fan package quoted by Knape
Associates included inlet filtration, ducting, and an inlet silencer. Per the fan vendor, the
inlet filtration and ducting are generally expected to take a total static pressure drop of
approximately 1.0-1.5 inches of water (0.25-0.37 kPa). For the centrifugal fan sourced
from Northern Blower the potential fan package design is shown in Figure 33. For this
design, the expected losses were quoted at 0.25 in wg. (0.06 kPa) for the filter and 1.05
in wg. (0.26 kPa) for the silencer. The quoted cost for the system was as follows:

centrifugal fans if site-specific contamination concerns exist. Furthermore, fan silencers

The topic of total pressure versus static pressure has been discussed previously. Most
fan curves are presented in terms of static pressure. In general, the dynamic pressure (or
velocity pressure, as fan vendors designate) at the fan outlet is significantly less than the
static pressure gain but becomes more significant at the highest flow rates. See the
velocity pressure plotted on the Northern blower map in Figure 34. For the selected
operating point, the velocity pressure is predicted to be 1.134 in wg. (0.28 kPa). This
difference in total pressure is similar in magnitude to the filtration and ducting losses,
offsetting each other. It is also noted that fan capacities are often quoted based on
stagnation pressure at the fan inlet. Due to the low absolute pressure values across these
air movers, designating and using the correct pressure values is paramount.

                                             Page 48 of 113

 Figure 34. Northern Blower Fan Map Showing Velocity Pressure Along with Static Pressure

A detailed analysis of the filtration and ducting losses were not included in the NPSS
model prior to this study because the frictional losses are heavily dependent on the
geometry of the components, which were not well-defined. Instead, the model took a
static-to-static approach, relying on the assumption that the cross-sectional area of the
air flow would remain relatively constant throughout. However, as the commercial-scale
dry cooler design is fine-tuned, it becomes more critical to capture the true losses through
the air system at off-design conditions, as this was shown to have a large impact on
LCOE. Loss estimations through the inlet screen, inlet duct, and transitional duct

performance can be a costly oversight.

Induced Flow vs. Forced Flow
All compact dry cooler configurations presented above were considering a forced draft
installation, where the fan is producing positive pressure to overcome the pressure loss
through the heat exchanger core. For completeness, induced draft was also considered
here.
It was found that there is some thermodynamic benefit to using induced draft because the
air stream is slightly cooler, removing the slight temperature rise across the fan. It was
found that the inlet volume flow (and power) to the fan was about the same as the forced-
draft case, meaning that the mass flow of air through the cooler was reduced due to the
increased air temperature at the fan inlet (Figure 35). Therefore, a heat exchanger
designed for the induced-draft design would need to be slightly larger with a higher UA to
accommodate the reduced air mass flow.

Prototype-scale Test Loop
A test loop was assembled to supply air and water to the fin-fin prototype heat
exchangers. See a process and instrumentation diagram (P&ID) presented in Figure 39.
The air is supplied from a shop air compressor at approximately 9-10 bar-g. The air is
metered using an orifice flow meter, then blown down through a globe valve. The globe
valve is used to control the flow rate; the majority of the pressure drop is taken at this
valve. Downstream of the globe valve, the air enters a wind tunnel where it passes
through a mesh flow conditioner, a straight flow section, and then a reducer connected to
the prototype heat exchanger inlet. Air static pressure and temperature are measured at
the heat exchanger inlet. Differential pressure is measured across the heat exchanger,
and temperature is measured downstream. See a summary of instrumentation presented
in Table 12.
The water supply is run in an open-loop configuration with a large, insulated water tank.
Water is pulled from the tank, pumped through the resistive-element heater, then it is
supplied to the prototype heat exchanger. A globe valve on the main supply line and the
recycle line downstream of the heater are used to control the water flow rate to the heat
exchanger (see Figure 39). The water supply passes through a mesh filter and a turbine
flow meter before entering the heat exchanger. Static pressure and temperature are
measured at the heat exchanger inlet, and temperature is measured at the outlet.
Differential pressure is measured across two locations: (i) between the inlet and exit

connections, and (ii) between the inlet and exit headers using internal connections
provided by VPE. See instrumentation summarized in Table 12.

Prototype-scale Performance Testing
To validate the test setup, an energy balance calculation was performed to compare the
heat loss from the water to the heat gain by the air. These results for one heat exchanger
are shown in Figure 42. The slight error between the calculated heat transfer and 1:1
(which indicates a perfect energy balance) is due to the small error in the water-side
temperature measurements. The thermocouples were calibrated, and the error is within
the specification for the thermocouple type; however, the actual water temperature
change across the heat exchanger is very small (a maximum of was measured 1.78˚C),
so even a small error in the temperature measurements results in a non-trivial error in the
water side heat loss calculation. This measurement error is also why the delta-enthalpy
error appears larger at small heat transfer values, where the temperature change is nearly
zero. Based on these findings, SwRI and VPE were satisfied with the measurement
accuracy, and the collected measurements were used to quantify the Fanning friction (f)
factor and Colburn (j) heat transfer factor for each fin configuration.

cooler footprint to remain compact, the transition duct must be much shorter. This project

The thermal loading on the aluminum heat exchanger nozzles was of some concern to
the project team. As a precaution, SwRI performed a thermal analysis of the dry cooler
piping, pipe supports, and heat exchanger structure. Based on these results, the CO2
lines into and out of the heat exchanger were supported locally on spring cans in order to
reduce nozzle loading during hot and cold conditions. Thermal growth, nozzle loads, and
spring can sizing calculations were completed using Caesar II software. See an image of
the model presented in Figure 62. The calculated equivalent force and moment loads on
each nozzle are also shown in the figure; these loads were confirmed as acceptable by
VPE. It was also decided to reinforce the welded joints on the heat exchanger nozzles to
ensure joint strength well-exceeds the expected thermal loading. For completeness, the
nozzle loading was also confirmed on the CO2-water separator; the predicted loads are
well below the vendor specified allowables.
Furthermore, the CO2 headers were designed as an extension of the nozzles to further
reduce risk of crack or failure at the aluminum nozzle welds in the case that piping loads
exceed the predicted values. Therefore, any piping loads on the heat exchanger will not
load up the aluminum weld joints in a pure bending mode. Having the extended nozzles
will increase the joint area, and should provide more stiffness as compared to nozzles
with butt-end welded joints. See the schematic presented in Figure 63.

The air mass flowrate was the most difficult parameter to quantify for the dry cooler test.
Based on PTC-19.5, it is recommended to have 10 to 20 diameters upstream of the air-
side flow measurement to allow for the flow to fully develop downstream of a disturbance.
This was not possible in the compact heat transition duct. Because the flow is not fully
developed in the transition duct, a large pitot tube array would be needed to accurately
quantify the mass flow. It was also considered to install a pitot tube array downstream of
the heat exchanger, as the fins act as a flow straightener. Based on the Dwyer sensor
specifications, pitot tubes should be installed 8-10 diameters downstream of a flow
straightener (heat exchanger core), measuring at 16-64 locations. The team ultimately
decided that this was not a worthy use of project resources for the amount of value gained.
Alternatively, it was considered that the air flowrate could be calculated from other
parameters with higher measurement accuracy and the vendor-supplied fan performance
curves. To support this method, a fan power measurement was required. The team
considered a torque meter to directly measure shaft power, but it was not practical to
install the torque meter on the fan shaft because no access was available. Alternatively,
it was investigated if a torque meter could be installed between the fan motor and the belt
drive connection. However, it was found that the belt pull force was in excess of the force
allowed on the sourced torque meter. It was possible to install a bearing-supported torque

                                           Page 88 of 113

meter, but this would require the fan motor to be shifted over a significant distance and
require a redesign of the fan skid. The torque meter would also need to be installed on
the slide plate with the fan motor in order to properly tension the belt. The associated cost
of modifying the fan skid design along with the torque meter was considered too high for
the increase in shaft power measurement accuracy.
For this reason, it was decided to measure fan power electrically instead of mechanically.
An electrical power meter was sourced to measure fan power between the VFD and the
motor. The efficiency and power factor of the motor were accounted for when calculating
the air mass flow. Northern blower was able to provide information on both motor
efficiency and power factor; both should be relatively constant at the higher fan speeds.
However, the values are less certain at the lower end of the fan speed range. More details
on the air flow calculation method are presented under Task 2.5 below.
Additionally, the electrical power measurement provides quantification of a key variable
of interest in dry coolers, namely the parasitic power consumption. Directly measuring the
electrical power will allow a trend to be plotted for power requirement versus cooling
capacity (or CO2 outlet temperature) at various ambient temperatures.

number on the CO2 side, and Reynolds number on the air side. It was expected that the
heat duty would be calculated on the CO2 side and on the air side. SwRI expected to plot

be useful in scaling the cooler design to the full commercial module sizes.

9. Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions:
Cooler’s Impact on Plant Performance
At the beginning of this program, it was expected that the dry cooler’s performance would
have minimal impact on the plant LCOE, contributing only a small fraction of the power
cycle’s LCOE impact.
Through this program, it was demonstrated that the cooler significantly impacts the main
compressor performance, which in turn impacts the power cycle performance and plant
performance. The magnitude of the cooler’s impact on compressor performance was
known, but the impact of the air side was not well quantified. In this program, it was also
demonstrated that the air-side performance has a significant impact; air temperature and
air-side pressure drop are critical variables that cannot be ignored. Here, it was shown
that using a fully defined cooler model results in power cycle efficiency variation from

                                             Page 101 of 113

approximately 44% to 49% with change in ambient temperature, referencing a power
cycle with a design point efficiency of 48%. Furthermore, parasitic power consumption of
the cooler fans is non-negligible; and as such, the fan performance and efficiency of the
sourced fans should not be discounted. It is noted that this finding is relevant for fin-fan
coolers as well as the compact cooler investigated here.
It was found that variable fan speed control was the most effective and most economical
method for cooler turndown control and compressor inlet temperature control. The
associated parasitic power savings at lower ambient temperatures was significant and
helped drive the plant LCOE values down during periods of lower heat rejection.
It was also demonstrated that proper selection of the cooler design point is not trivial and
can have a significant impact on LCOE. Dry coolers are vulnerable to ambient
temperature swings, and power cycle performance can deteriorate in hot ambient
temperatures; furthermore, the ability to generate electricity during hot ambient conditions
directly impacts annual revenue and plant LCOE. Sizing the dry cooler for hot ambient
conditions can increase power production during the summer months and increase
revenue, but selecting the right design-point temperature is a trade between initial
installation cost and increased revenue; in this study, optimal LCOE was found at a
design-point ambient temperature of 42°C (somewhere between the average ambient
temperature and the hottest day temperature).
It is also noted from the cycle analyses that a poorly designed cooler can be detrimental
to plant performance. Elevated air-side pressure drop, fan power consumption, and/or
approach temperature to ambient can make a plant unprofitable and unsustainable.
Compact Dry Cooler Design
At the conclusion of this program, it was found that commercialized compact dry coolers
can be fabricated and marketed at a comparable cost to the commercially available fin-
fan dry coolers. The cost point also falls within the desired range collected through the
industry questionnaire.
At this cooler cost point, the compact dry cooler makes a more efficient use of space. The
heat transfer in this system is still generally governed by the air side however, the specific
air-side heat transfer is superior because the air is driven more evenly across the fin array.
In traditional fin-fan coolers, the distributed axial fans produce zones of high air velocity
and stagnant zones, where the finned tubes are transferring much less heat. In the
compact dry cooler design, air-side pressure drop is traded for excess material.
The heat transfer effectiveness (or minimum pinch-point temperature difference) is also
superior in the compact dry cooler as compared to traditional finned-tube dry coolers. The
microchannel passes, distributed among the fin layers allows for very high effectiveness
values, and design-point approach temperatures below 5°C. This study found that driving
to higher effectiveness also drove up the cooler cost and consequently the LCOE.
However, even at approach temperatures of 7°C to 9°C, the heat transfer performance
exceeded that of commercially quoted systems.
In this program, it was found that capital cost was not the primary driver of LCOE; this
was influenced most by the heat exchanger performance (effectiveness and air-side
parasitic power). This being said, a balance exists. Based on the design studies

                                       Page 102 of 113

performed here, the compact dry cooler design point for optimal plant LCOE falls within
these ranges for the key performance indicators:
 • approach temperature of 7-9˚C,
 • air-side pressure drop of 1-1.5 kPa,
 • CO2-side pressure drop of 1-2% inlet pressure (less impactful),
 • nominal fan efficiency greater than 70%,
 • VFD for turndown control, and
 • resulting cost less than approximately 200 $/kWth.
Compact Heat Exchanger Fabrication
At the initiation of this project, it was expected that the LCOE could be driven down
primarily by reducing heat exchanger size to reduce materials cost as compared to fin-
fan coolers. It was quickly found that reducing size (and consequently increasing air-side
pressure drop) proved to be an impractical solution. The team pivoted to larger heat
exchanger cores with lower pressure loss.
It was expected that capital cost could also be reduced, and heat transfer could be
improved by building a heat exchanger core that utilized stainless steel diffusion bonded
plates for the CO2 side, then brazed to aluminum formed fins. Fabrication of such a heat
exchanger core was novel and demonstrated for the first time during this program. The
hybrid fabrication was completed successfully, and the joint strength passed qualification
testing; however, joining aluminum fins to stainless steel plates proved to be materials-
heavy and labor-intensive. Managing the oxide layer growth along with the difference in
thermal expansion between materials was also challenging. For these reasons, VPE
concluded that it was not feasible to scale up this process. From here, the design team
pivoted to a heat exchanger core fabricated from a single material.
A fully stainless steel core was contemplated, but it was expected that the cost would be
high. It was found that aluminum 6061 was an allowable material in the ASME B&PV
Code, with material strength values that exceeded those needed for an sCO2 pre-cooler.
It was also expected that fabrication from aluminum would substantially save on bulk
material cost. For these reasons, it was decided to pursue a fully aluminum, brazed heat
exchanger core.
Fabrication trials and fin performance testing produced positive results. The MW-scale
heat exchanger core was approached with several trial builds and qualification steps to
derisk the process. Sourcing chemically etched aluminum plates proved challenging, but
acceptable plates were eventually delivered; the team considered CNC machining the
plates instead, but this proved to be cost prohibitive. The single-step brazing and header
welding trials also produced favorable results for a short stack of plates; however,
significant issues were experienced at the full core scale. Deformation of the fin layers at
brazing temperatures caused the joint tolerances to exceed allowables at various
locations in the core, meaning joints were not fully sealed by the braze foil. Closing leak
paths in the aluminum heat exchanger cores consumed a large amount of the project

                                       Page 103 of 113

resources, and ultimately the heat exchanger fabrication was abandoned. A detailed list
of lessons learned from this fabrication effort were documented.
Based on the results of the aluminum heat exchanger fabrication, the second compact
dry cooler unit was fabricated using stainless steel plates for the CO2 side and stainless
steel formed fins. This fabrication effort was completed in substantially less time and with
significantly fewer issues. The completed and qualified heat exchanger (~1 MWth scale)
was delivered for testing in full pressure and temperature conditions. Here, VPE
leveraged proven technologies in chemical etching of stainless steel and diffusion
bonding to seal the CO2 passages. The stainless steel formed fins were then brazed to
the CO2 plates. Even though bulk material costs in stainless steel were much higher than
aluminum, the eventual heat exchanger fabrication cost was comparable or less because
strong supply chains exist here. It is expected that the aluminum heat exchanger core
could realize cost savings, but further development work is required.
Based on the fabrication of the stainless steel compact dry cooler, it is expected that
commercial module scales up to 5 MWth are feasible. A 5 MWth unit would be constructed
of six to eight cores, seam welded together. The commercialized cost estimated based
on these lessons learned is favorable at 92.2-115.3 $/kWth.
It is also noted here, that in the current commercial design with the stainless steel heat
exchanger core, atomized water could feasibly be injected into the transition duct to
operate as an adiabatic cooler in periods where greater cooling duty is desired. LCOE
was not analyzed for this configuration, but it is expected that the compact adiabatic dry
cooler could present a competitive option as well.
Overall Advantages and Disadvantages of Developed Compact Dry Cooler
At the proposal stage of this program, it was expected that the compact dry cooler could
significantly improve on LCOE when compared to the current state of the art fin-fan
coolers; however, early project results show that the LCOE is comparable to traditional
fin-fan coolers. Throughout this project, it was demonstrated that the compact dry cooler
design significantly improves the thermal performance compared to the baseline, but the
air-side pressure drop and power consumption were necessarily higher to maintain a
manufacturable heat exchanger core size. At the selected KPI values, these factors
balance. Throughout this project, the anticipated capital cost of the compact dry cooler
was also significantly reduced. Lessons learned through the fabrication trials and the
subsequent commercial build have significantly reduced uncertainty and provided a clear
path toward a commercial product line. Using cost estimates for the nth commercial unit,
it is expected that the compact dry cooler can drive the LCOE down by 0.10 to 0.19 ¢/kWh
when compared to a commercially sourced fin-fan cooler at the same design point. It is
noted that this LCOE prediction does not take into account the potential transport and
infrastructure savings associated with the smaller size and weight; these factors could
present further LCOE savings.
Based on the results found in this project, the compact dry cooler provides multiple
advantages:
 • The commercialized cost per duty is expected to be comparable to existing fin-fan
 dry coolers.

                                      Page 104 of 113

 • The heat transfer effectiveness is expected to be superior, improving power cycle
 and plant performance in hot ambient conditions.
 • The installed footprint is significantly smaller than commercial fin-fan installations,
 predicting >50% reduction.
 • Coupling with a centrifugal fan provides configuration flexibility; this may prove
 advantageous when packaging sCO2 power cycles on compact skids or power
 cycle towers.
 • The compact heat exchanger core, coupled with the fan through a transition duct,
 provides a more effective use of space.
 • The transition duct may offer some fin protection, to limit fin blockage and fouling,
 potentially reducing maintenance requirements.
 • It is expected that the compact dry cooler can reduce power cycle LCOE
 contribution by a measurable margin when compared to a fin-fan cooler installation
 (predicting >0.10 ¢/kWh improvement toward the 2030 SunShot target of 5 ¢/kWh).
The compact dry cooler design also has some disadvantages when comparing to fin-fan
dry coolers:
 • The module scale is limited, to some extent, by the furnace size and feasible heat
 exchanger core size. For the largest plant installations, economies of scale may
 not favor this cooler design.
 • The air-side pressure drop on this design are inherently higher than for fin-fan
 coolers (due to the achievable stack geometry and size). In the sCO2 power cycles
 analyzed here, the performance benefits outweighed the increased air-side power
 consumption. However, off-design performance should be considered carefully
 when selecting the pre-cooler. In cycles highly sensitive to parasitic losses, the
 compact dry cooler may not be economical.

It is expected that (i) the successful production of the stainless steel compact dry cooler
and (ii) the planned G3P3 testing campaign in an sCO2 power cycle present a positive
path forward for this novel technology. The shortcomings of the current program are
notable, but the lessons learned, pivots, and growth of the technology through this
program are preeminent. The novel compact dry cooler was considered TRL-2 at the
onset of this program; it is currently approaching TRL-7 and will achieve TRL-8 following
successful demonstration in the Sandia G3P3 program.

 depending on fan selection).
