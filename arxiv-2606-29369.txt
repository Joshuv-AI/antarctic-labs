. 1.
Introduction

A point
(x,y)\in\mathbb{A}^{2}(\mathbb{C})
is a
special point
if
x
and
y
are both
singular moduli
, i.e.
j
-invariants of elliptic curves with complex multiplication.
Denote by
\Delta_{x}
(respectively
\Delta_{y}
) the discriminant of the endomorphism order of an elliptic curve with
j
-invariant
x
(respectively
y
).

An irreducible algebraic curve
V\subset\mathbb{A}^{2}(\mathbb{C})
is a
special curve
if
V
is one of:

(1)

a horizontal line
\mathbb{A}^{1}(\mathbb{C})\times\{y\}
for some singular modulus
y
,

(2)

a vertical line
\{x\}\times\mathbb{A}^{1}(\mathbb{C})
for some singular modulus
x
,

(3)

a modular curve
\mathbb{V}(\Phi_{N}(X,Y))
for some
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, where
\Phi_{N}\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]
is the modular polynomial of level
N
, see
[
Lan87
, p. 55]
.

The curve
V
is
non-special
if it is not a special curve.

André
[
And98
]
proved that every non-special algebraic curve
V\subset\mathbb{A}^{2}(\mathbb{C})
contains only finitely many special points.
This is the simplest non-trivial case of the André–Oort conjecture for
\mathbb{A}^{n}(\mathbb{C})
; Pila
[
Pil11
]
subsequently proved the full André–Oort conjecture for
\mathbb{A}^{n}(\mathbb{C})
.
These proofs were ineffective: in particular, they do not give an effectively computable bound on the height (or even the number) of special points on a non-special curve
V
.

The main unconditional result of this paper is an effective version of André’s theorem for a certain class of non-special curves.
In particular, we will prove that the height of special points on such curves
V
may be bounded linearly in terms of the height of the curve.
See §
2.1
for the definitions of the different notions of heights that appear in this paper.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
V\subset\mathbb{A}^{2}(\mathbb{C})
be a geometrically irreducible, non-special algebraic curve defined over a number field
L
. Write
X,Y
for the coordinate functions on
\mathbb{A}^{2}(\mathbb{C})
restricted to
V
.
Let
d_{1}=\deg X
and
d_{2}=\deg Y
.
Suppose that
d_{1}\neq d_{2}
.
Then there exist an effectively computable constant
c_{1}([L:\mathbb{Q}],\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\})
and, for every
\delta>0
, an effectively computable constant
c_{2}([L:\mathbb{Q}],\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\},\delta)
with the following properties: for every special point
(x,y)\in V
,

(1.1)

\displaystyle\max\{h(x),h(y)\}\leq c_{1}\left([L:\mathbb{Q}],\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\}\right)+26\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\}^{3}h(V)

and

(1.2)

\displaystyle\max\{\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert,\lvert\Delta_{y}\rvert\}\leq c_{2}([L:\mathbb{Q}],\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\},\delta)\max\{1,h(V)\}^{2+\delta}.

The condition in Theorem
1.1
that
d_{1}\neq d_{2}
implies, but is strictly stronger than, the condition that
V
is not a modular curve
\mathbb{V}(\Phi_{N}(X,Y))
.
The condition that
V
is a non-special curve is necessary, because every special curve contains infinitely many special points.
For non-special curves in general, Kühne
[
Küh12
]
and, independently, Bilu, Masser, and Zannier
[
BMZ13
]
proved effective versions of André’s theorem.

Compared to these previous effective versions of André’s theorem, the novel aspect of Theorem
1.1
is that, when applicable, it gives a significantly better dependence on the height of the curve
V
.
Kühne’s result
[
Küh12
, Theorem 2]
gives a bound of the form of (
1.2
), but with exponent
8+\delta
in place of
2+\delta
.
Wüstholz
[
Wüs14
, Theorem 1.1]
subsequently improved this exponent to
8
.
The result of Bilu, Masser, and Zannier
[
BMZ13
]
does not give an explicit dependence on the height of the curve.
Kühne
[
Küh12
, p. 654]
notes that a bound as strong as (
1.2
) for general non-special curves would follow via his method from a conjectural elliptic analogue
[
DHK09
, Conjecture 1.7]
of the Lang–Waldschmidt conjecture.
Such conjectures seem out of reach at present however.

More recently, Binyamini
[
Bin20
, Corollary 3]
and Papas
[
Pap26
, Corollary 1.7]
have also given effective proofs of André’s theorem for certain classes of curves, but again without obtaining an explicit dependence on the height of the curve.
Their proofs both follow the Pila–Zannier strategy, which was originally applied to André–Oort for
\mathbb{A}^{n}(\mathbb{C})
by Pila
[
Pil09b
,
Pil11
]
.
Instead of the ineffective Pila–Wilkie theorem
[
PW06
,
Pil09a
]
, Binyamini and Papas apply effective point counting results also due to Binyamini
[
Bin19
,
Bin24
]
.

1.1.
A conditional result and uniformity in André’s theorem

Our proof of Theorem
1.1
will use a result of Habegger
[
Hab10
, Theorem 1.1]
, that gives a height bound for points lying in the intersection of a modular curve
\mathbb{V}(\Phi_{N}(X,Y))
and a curve
V
satisfying the hypotheses of Theorem
1.1
.
This is the only part of the proof of Theorem
1.1
that requires the hypothesis that
d_{1}\neq d_{2}
, rather than just the condition that
V
is non-special.

Habegger conjectures
[
Hab10
, p. 44]
that an analogous height bound to
[
Hab10
, Theorem 1.1]
should hold solely under the hypothesis that
V
is a non-special curve.
Assuming such a conjecture, we can extend the proof of Theorem
1.1
to all non-special curves.
In fact, a qualitatively weaker height bound than the one in
[
Hab10
, Theorem 1.1]
would suffice.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
\eta\in(0,1/2)
and
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
We say that the property
\mathrm{WBH}(\eta,\kappa)
holds if the following property is satisfied.
Let
V\subset\mathbb{A}^{2}(\mathbb{C})
be a geometrically irreducible, non-special algebraic curve defined over a number field
L
.
Write
X,Y
for the coordinate functions on
\mathbb{A}^{2}(\mathbb{C})
restricted to
V
.
Let
d_{1}=\deg X
and
d_{2}=\deg Y
.
There exists a constant
c_{*}=c_{*}(\eta,\kappa,[L:\mathbb{Q}],\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\})
such that, for every special point
(x,y)\in V
and every
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, if
\Phi_{N}(x,y)=0
, then

\max\{h(x),h(y)\}\leq c_{*}\max\{1,h(V)\}^{\kappa}N^{1/2-\eta}.

Similar height bounds have been formulated in
[
Hab17a
, Appendix B]
and
[
Pil22
, Conjecture 21.24]
.
The key aspect to note in the formulation of
\mathrm{WBH}(\eta,\kappa)
is that it records the dependence on
h(V)
explicitly.

The second main result of this paper is the following conditional result.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\eta\in(0,1/2)
and
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
Suppose that
\mathrm{WBH}(\eta,\kappa)
holds with a given
c_{*}
.
Let
V\subset\mathbb{A}^{2}(\mathbb{C})
be a geometrically irreducible, non-special algebraic curve defined over a number field
L
.
Write
X,Y
for the coordinate functions on
\mathbb{A}^{2}(\mathbb{C})
restricted to
V
.
Let
d_{1}=\deg X
and
d_{2}=\deg Y
.

(1)

For every
\delta>0
, there exists an effectively computable constant
c_{1}([L:\mathbb{Q}],\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\},c_{*},\delta)
such that

\quad\quad\quad\max\{h(x),h(y)\}\leq c_{1}([L:\mathbb{Q}],\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\},c_{*},\delta)\max\{1,h(V)\}^{\kappa/2\eta+\delta}

for every special point
(x,y)\in V
.

(2)

If
\kappa<2\eta
, then there exists an effectively computable constant
c_{2}([L:\mathbb{Q}],\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\},c_{*})
such that

\max\{\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert,\lvert\Delta_{y}\rvert\}\leq c_{2}([L:\mathbb{Q}],\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\},c_{*})

for every special point
(x,y)\in V
.

The motivation for part (2) of this theorem is the following ineffective result of Pila
[
Pil09b
]
.
This is a uniform version of André’s theorem, in which there is no dependence on the height of the curve
V
.
A different proof was given, again ineffectively, by Kühne
[
Küh13
, Theorem 2]
.
Edixhoven
[
Edi98
, Remark 7.1]
had earlier obtained the same result, conditional on GRH.

Theorem 1.4

(
[
Pil09b
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.

Let
V\subset\mathbb{A}^{2}(\mathbb{C})
be a geometrically irreducible, non-special algebraic curve defined over a number field
L
.
Write
X,Y
for the coordinate functions on
\mathbb{A}^{2}(\mathbb{C})
restricted to
V
.
Let
d_{1}=\deg X
and
d_{2}=\deg Y
.
There exists a constant
c([L:\mathbb{Q}],\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\})
such that

(1.3)

\displaystyle\max\{\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert,\lvert\Delta_{y}\rvert\}\leq c([L:\mathbb{Q}],\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\})

for every special point
(x,y)\in V
.

No effective version of Theorem
1.4
is known in general.
In the special case that
V\subset\mathbb{A}^{2}(\mathbb{C})
has Zariski-closure in
(\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1})(\mathbb{C})
equal to
V\cup\{(\infty,\infty)\}
, Kühne
[
Küh13
, Theorem 4]
proved an effective version of Theorem
1.4
.
Note that the class of such curves neither contains, nor is contained in, the class of curves considered in Theorem
1.1
.
Kühne’s proof depends crucially on the fact that
\{(\infty,\infty)\}
is the only point at infinity of
V
, and so does not seem generalisable to other non-special curves.

Theorem
1.3
provides a strategy to prove Theorem
1.4
effectively for general non-special curves.
Indeed, it reduces the problem to proving, for some suitable
\eta
and
\kappa
, the height bound
\mathrm{WBH}(\eta,\kappa)
with an effectively computable constant
c_{*}
.
In Proposition
3.3
, we will show, by explicating the constant in
[
Hab10
, Theorem 1.1]
, that, for the class of curves appearing in Theorem
1.1
, the statement
\mathrm{WBH}(\eta,1)
holds for every
\eta\in(0,1/2)
.
Unfortunately, this falls just short of the threshold in part (2) of Theorem
1.3
that is required to prove an effective version of Theorem
1.4
for these curves.

1.2.
The point counting approach to André’s theorem

The proofs of Theorems
1.1
and
1.3
will use an effective form of the Pila–Zannier strategy of o-minimal point counting (see
[
Pil22
]
for an overview of this approach to unlikely intersections problems).
An ineffective version of this strategy was used by Pila
[
Pil09b
]
to prove Theorem
1.4
.
The point counting part of the Pila–Zannier strategy can, in applications to André–Oort for
\mathbb{A}^{n}(\mathbb{C})
, be made effective by using effective point counting results of Binyamini
[
Bin19
,
Bin24
]
, in place of the ineffective Pila–Wilkie theorem
[
PW06
]
.

The Pila–Zannier strategy also requires some arithmetic inputs.
In particular, to give a proof of André’s theorem, the strategy requires a lower bound for the Galois orbit of a special point
(x,y)\in V
of the form

(1.4)

\displaystyle[\mathbb{Q}(x,y):\mathbb{Q}]\gg\max\{\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert,\lvert\Delta_{y}\rvert\}^{\theta}

for some
\theta>0
.
Pila
[
Pil09b
, p. 2497]
deduces this from the Landau–Siegel bound
[
Lan35
,
Sie35
]
for the class number of an imaginary quadratic field:

(1.5)

\displaystyle\cl(D)\gg\lvert D\rvert^{1/2-\epsilon},

where
\cl(D)
denotes the class number of the imaginary quadratic field of discriminant
D
.
Unfortunately, the bound (
1.5
) is ineffective.
The only known effective bounds for the class number, due to Goldfeld
[
Gol76
]
and Gross and Zagier
[
GZ86
]
, are not strong enough to imply a bound (
1.4
).

Tatuzawa
[
Tat51
]
proved an effective version of (
1.5
) that holds for the discriminant of every imaginary quadratic field, apart from at most one exceptional field
K_{*}
(depending on
\epsilon
).
Binyamini applied this to prove a result
[
Bin20
, Corollary 1]
which has as a special case an effective (and uniform) version of André’s theorem for special points
(x,y)
with
(\sqrt{\Delta_{x}},\sqrt{\Delta_{y}})\notin K_{*}^{2}
.

Unfortunately, Tatuzawa’s result does not give any information about the field
K_{*}
.
It is therefore necessary to find a different way of controlling special points with
(\sqrt{\Delta_{x}},\sqrt{\Delta_{y}})\in K_{*}^{2}
.
In the special case that
V
is defined by a polynomial
F\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[X,Y]
with
\deg_{X}F=\deg_{Y}F=\deg F
, Binyamini
[
Bin20
, Corollary 3]
was able to do this via Archimedean estimates for special points, but in a way that depends essentially on the particular form of
F
.

For some curves
V
, one may prove an effective version of (
1.4
) directly.
Papas
[
Pap26
, Theorem 1.5]
did this for a certain class of curves by using André’s
G
-functions method, which was originally applied to unlikely intersections in
\mathbb{A}^{n}(\mathbb{C})
by Daw and Orr
[
DO25
]
.
Papas proved
[
Pap26
, Theorem 2.17]
that if
V
satisfies a suitable boundary condition, then there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}
such that every special point
(x,y)\in V
satisfies

(1.6)

\displaystyle\max\{h(x),h(y)\}\leq c_{1}[\mathbb{Q}(x,y):\mathbb{Q}]^{c_{2}}.

The constants
c_{1},c_{2}
are effectively computable in terms of some quantities related to the
G
-functions used
[
Pap26
, Remark 6.1]
.
From (
1.6
), Papas then deduces a bound of the form (
1.4
) by using endomorphism estimates of Masser and Wüstholz
[
MW94
]
.
This Galois bound is then used to prove
[
Pap26
, Corollary 1.7]
via an effective version of the Pila–Zannier strategy.

A drawback of the
G
-functions approach is that it requires the curve
V
to satisfy a suitable boundary condition.
(Papas was also able to obtain a partial result
[
Pap25
, Theorem 6.4]
without a boundary condition, but with a restriction on the special points considered instead.)
It is also not completely straightforward to calculate the constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
in (
1.6
).

1.3.
Strategy of proof

To prove Theorems
1.1
and
1.3
, we will use a new way of controlling special points
(x,y)\in V
with
(\sqrt{\Delta_{x}},\sqrt{\Delta_{y}})\in K_{*}^{2}
.
The advantages of this new approach are that it leads to a good dependence on the height of the curve
V
and requires only that
V
satisfies a height bound along the lines of the one formulated in Definition
1.2
.
It therefore seems to offer a more promising approach than
[
Pap26
, Corollary 1.7]
and
[
Bin20
, Corollary 3]
to proving the uniform Theorem
1.4
effectively.

Moreover, our method also gives a way of deducing André–Oort results directly from height bounds for special points of the form of (
1.6
).
This method differs from the usual approach of applying Masser–Wüstholz estimates and point counting.
We explain this in Section
6.1
.

We now give an outline of the proof of Theorem
1.1
. A special point
(x,y)\in V
with
(\sqrt{\Delta_{x}},\sqrt{\Delta_{y}})\in K_{*}^{2}
necessarily lies in the intersection of
V
with a modular curve
\mathbb{V}(\Phi_{N}(X,Y))
.
We take
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
to be the least such
N
.
Under the assumptions on
V
in Theorem
1.1
, a result of Habegger
[
Hab10
, Theorem 1.1]

implies that

\max\{h(x),h(y)\}\ll\log N.

Isogeny estimates
of Gaudron and Rémond
[
GR14
]
then give that

[\mathbb{Q}(x,y):\mathbb{Q}]\gg N^{1/3}.

On the other hand, if the special point
(x,y)
is such that

[\mathbb{Q}(x):\mathbb{Q}]\ll\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert^{1/2-\epsilon}\mbox{ and }[\mathbb{Q}(y):\mathbb{Q}]\ll\lvert\Delta_{y}\rvert^{1/2-\epsilon},

then, via Archimedean estimates for the
j
-function (Proposition
2.3
),

\max\{h(x),h(y)\}\gg\max\{\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert,\lvert\Delta_{y}\rvert\}^{\epsilon}.

We now have upper and lower bounds for
\max\{h(x),h(y)\}
and
[\mathbb{Q}(x,y):\mathbb{Q}]
, which are incompatible with each other if
\max\{\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert,\lvert\Delta_{y}\rvert\}
is sufficiently large.
The bound on
\max\{\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert,\lvert\Delta_{y}\rvert\}
we thereby obtain (Theorem
6.3
) is completely explicit and sharp enough to allow one to rule out the existence of any such special points on many curves
V
, see Remark
6.4
.

The proof of part (1) in Theorem
1.3
is similar, but using the assumption of
\mathrm{WBH}(\eta,\kappa)
in place of Habegger’s result.
Part (2) then follows from (1) via uniform bounds on the number of points of small height on an affine curve, as used by Kühne
[
Küh13
, §3]
.

There is a subtlety to note in the above outline.
Tatuzawa’s result implies that if
(x,y)
is a special point such that
x
and
y
have abnormally small degree, then
(\sqrt{\Delta_{x}},\sqrt{\Delta_{y}})\in K_{*}^{2}
.
It does not imply though that all special points with
(\sqrt{\Delta_{x}},\sqrt{\Delta_{y}})\in K_{*}^{2}
have
x
and
y
of abnormally small degree.
Those special points where
x
and
y
do not both have abnormally small degree though may be bounded by effective point counting.

Kühne’s
[
Küh12
]
effective proof of André’s theorem does not use point counting.
He too was able, by using Tatuzawa’s version of (
1.5
) with
\epsilon<1/10
, to prove a uniform, effective bound
[
Küh13
, Corollary 2]
on special points
(x,y)\in V
with
(\sqrt{\Delta_{x}},\sqrt{\Delta_{y}})\notin K_{*}^{2}
.

We will use this result of Kühne to give a second proof of the bound (
1.1
) in Theorem
1.1
.
In particular, one still obtains from this proof the linear dependence on
h(V)
in (
1.1
).
This second proof has the advantage that it does not use any point counting and is therefore more conducive to computing the constant
c_{1}
in (
1.1
) explicitly.
The disadvantage is that the version of the bound (
1.2
) one obtains in this way has the exponent
24+\delta
rather than
2+\delta
, and so is weaker than
[
Küh12
, Theorem 2]
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Heights

Denote by
h(x)
the absolute logarithmic height of an algebraic number
x
, as defined in
[
BG06
, §1.5]
.
When we refer to the “height”, we mean this height.

Given a geometrically irreducible curve
V\subset\mathbb{A}^{2}(\mathbb{C})
defined over a number field
L
, we write
h(V)
for the height of the curve
V
.
This is defined to be the projective logarithmic height of the vector whose entries are the non-zero coefficients of a minimal defining polynomial for
V
.

Since
x,y
will always denote algebraic numbers and
V
will always denote an algebraic curve, we trust that there will be no confusion between the notation
h(x)
(respectively
h(y)
) and
h(V)
.

Given an elliptic curve
E/\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
, we write
h_{\mathrm{F}}(E)
for the stable Faltings height of
E
.
We adopt the same normalisation for
h_{\mathrm{F}}(E)
as in
[
GR14
,
Paz19
]
, so that we may quote results from those papers without changing the constants.

2.2.
Singular moduli

Let
j\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{C}
denote the modular
j
-function, where
\mathbb{H}
denotes the complex upper half-plane.
The
j
-function is surjective and invariant under the action of
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
on
\mathbb{H}
.
A complex number
x=j(\tau)
is a singular modulus if and only if
[\mathbb{Q}(\tau):\mathbb{Q}]=2
.

Let
\tau\in\mathbb{H}
be such that

a\tau^{2}+b\tau+c=0

for some
a,b,c\in\mathbb{Z}
, not all zero, such that
\gcd(a,b,c)=1
.
Then
x=j(\tau)
is a singular modulus, and the discriminant
\Delta_{x}
of
x
satisfies

\Delta_{x}=b^{2}-4ac.

The
fundamental discriminant

D_{x}
of
x=j(\tau)
is the discriminant of the imaginary quadratic field
\mathbb{Q}(\tau)
.
One has that
\Delta_{x}=f^{2}D_{x}
for some
f\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
.
The integer
f
is the conductor of the endomorphism order of an elliptic curve
E_{x}
with
j
-invariant
x
, i.e
\mathrm{End}(E_{x})\cong\mathbb{Z}+f\mathcal{O}_{K}
, where
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{\Delta_{x}})
.

The singular moduli of a given discriminant form a complete set of Galois conjugates over
\mathbb{Q}
, see e.g.
[
Cox22
, Proposition 13.2]
.
Hence, if
x
is a singular modulus, then

[\mathbb{Q}(x):\mathbb{Q}]=\cl(\Delta_{x}),

where
\cl(\Delta_{x})
denotes
1

h(\cdot)
.
the class number of the imaginary quadratic order of discriminant
\Delta_{x}
.
The results of Goldfeld
[
Gol76
]
and Gross–Zagier
[
GZ86

give an effective lower bound for for
\cl(\Delta_{x})
, which is however qualitatively much weaker than the one given by Landau and Siegel
[
Lan35
,
Sie35
]
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
x
be a singular modulus.
Then

[\mathbb{Q}(x):\mathbb{Q}]\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{168000}}(\log\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert)^{1/2}

Proof.

Let
D_{x}
be the fundamental discriminant of
x
and let
f\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
be such that
\Delta_{x}=f^{2}D_{x}
.
It follows from Oesterlé’s explicit version
[
Oes85
, Théorème 1 & §5.1]
of the result of Goldfeld and Gross–Zagier that

\cl(D_{x})^{2}\geq\frac{\log\lvert D_{x}\rvert}{42000},

see
[
Fow26
, Proposition 2.7]
for the details.
Let
\varphi(\cdot)
denote Euler’s totient function.
Then
[
Cox22
, Theorem 7.24]
implies that

\cl(\Delta)^{2}\geq\varphi(f)^{2}\cl(D)^{2}.

Hence, the elementary bound
\varphi(f)\geq\sqrt{f/2}
yields that

\displaystyle\cl(\Delta)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{f\log\lvert D\rvert}{84000}\geq\frac{\log\lvert D\rvert+(f-1)\log\lvert D\rvert}{84000}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\log\lvert D\rvert+2\log f}{168000},

since
\lvert D\rvert\geq 3
implies
(f-1)\log\lvert D\rvert\geq f-1\geq\log f
for every
f\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
.
∎

Weaker versions of the following bound are well-known, see e.g.
[
FP87
, Lemme 1(iii)]
,
[
BMZ13
, Lemma 1]
, and
[
Paz19
, Lemma 2.5]
.
The constant
744
is sharp, as may be seen by taking
z=-1/2+iy
and letting
y\to\infty
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
z\in\mathbb{H}
. Then

\lvert j(z)\rvert>e^{2\pi\im z}-744.

Proof.

Since
j(z)
is invariant under
z\mapsto z+1
and
e^{\sqrt{3}\pi}<744
, we may assume that
-1/2\leq\re z\leq 1/2
and
\im z\geq\sqrt{3}/2
.
Hence, by a result of Diaz and Philibert
[
DP89
, Theorem (i)]
,

\lvert j(z)\rvert\geq\left\lvert j\left(-\frac{1}{2}+i\im z\right)\right\rvert=-j\left(-\frac{1}{2}+i\im z\right),

where the equality follows from the fact that the
j
-function takes non-positive real values on the line segment
\{-1/2+iy:y\geq\sqrt{3}/2\}
.
The
q
-expansion of the
j
-function then gives that

\lvert j(z)\rvert-e^{2\pi\im z}\geq-744-\sum_{n\geq 1}c_{n}(-e^{-2\pi\im z})^{n},

where the
c_{n}
are the coefficients in the
q
-expansion of the
j
-function.

It therefore suffices to show that the sum

(2.1)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}(-1)^{n-1}c_{n}(e^{-2\pi\im z})^{n}

is positive.
For every
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, it follows from
[
BP05
, Theorem 1.1]
that

0.91\frac{e^{4\pi\sqrt{n}}}{\sqrt{2}n^{3/4}}\leq c_{n}\leq 1.03\frac{e^{4\pi\sqrt{n}}}{\sqrt{2}n^{3/4}}.

These bounds and and the fact
\im z\geq\sqrt{3}/2
together imply that

\displaystyle\frac{c_{n+1}(e^{-2\pi\im z})^{n+1}}{c_{n}(e^{-2\pi\im z})^{n}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1.03}{0.91}\frac{e^{4\pi(\sqrt{n+1}-\sqrt{n})}}{(1+\frac{1}{n})^{3/4}}e^{-\pi\sqrt{3}}

\displaystyle\leq 1.14e^{4\pi(\sqrt{2}-1)}e^{-\pi\sqrt{3}}.

Since
1.14e^{4\pi(\sqrt{2}-1)}e^{-\pi\sqrt{3}}<0.91
, the sum in (
2.1
) is positive as required.
∎

This result implies a lower bound for the height of a singular modulus.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
x
be a non-zero singular modulus. Then

h(x)\geq\frac{3\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert^{1/2}}{[\mathbb{Q}(x):\mathbb{Q}]}.

Proof.

Let
k\in\{0,1\}
be such that
k\equiv\Delta_{x}\bmod 2
and set

\tau=\frac{-k+\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert^{1/2}i}{2}.

Then
j(\tau)
is a singular modulus of discriminant
\Delta_{x}
.
So
x
is conjugate over
\mathbb{Q}
to
j(\tau)
.
Thus, by basic properties of the height (see e.g.
[
BG06
, (1.8)]
),

h(x)\geq\frac{\log\lvert j(\tau)\rvert}{[\mathbb{Q}(x):\mathbb{Q}]}.

By Lemma
2.2
,

\lvert j(\tau)\rvert\geq e^{\pi\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert^{1/2}}-744.

Since
x\neq 0
, we have that
\Delta_{x}\neq-3
.
For
\Delta_{x}\in\{-4,-7\}
, the inequality is checked directly.
Assume then that
\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert\geq 8
.
It now suffices to observe that

\log(1-744e^{-\pi\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert^{1/2}})\geq\log(1-744e^{-\pi\sqrt{8}})>-0.11.\qed

2.3.
Modular polynomials

We recall some basic facts about modular polynomials and their relation to singular moduli and isogenies.

Proposition 2.4

(
[
Lan87
, Ch.5 §3]
)
.

Let
x,y\in\mathbb{C}
.

(1)

Let
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
and
E_{x},E_{y}
be elliptic curves with
j
-invariants
x,y
respectively.
Then
\Phi_{N}(x,y)=0
if and only if there exists an isogeny
\varphi\colon E_{x}\to E_{y}
such that
\ker\varphi\cong\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}
, i.e.
\varphi
is a cyclic
N
-isogeny.

(2)

Suppose that
\Phi_{N}(x,y)=0
for some
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
. Then
x
is a singular modulus if and only if
y
is a singular modulus.

(3)

Suppose that
x,y
are singular moduli with fundamental discriminants
D_{x},D_{y}
respectively. Then
\Phi_{N}(x,y)=0
for some
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
if and only if
D_{x}=D_{y}
.

The following isogeny estimate allows us to obtain lower bounds for the Galois orbit of a special point from an upper bound for its height.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
x,y\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
and set
d=[\mathbb{Q}(x,y):\mathbb{Q}]
. Let
E_{x},E_{y}/\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
be elliptic curves with
j
-invariants
x,y
respectively. If
\Phi_{M}(x,y)=0
for some
M\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, then there exists
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
such that
\Phi_{N}(x,y)=0
and

N\leq 10^{13}\max\{1,h_{\mathrm{F}}(E_{x}),\log d\}^{2}d^{2}.

Proof.

This follows immediately from the explicit isogeny estimate of Gaudron and Rémond
[
GR14
, Théorème 1.4]
, since every isogeny of minimal degree is cyclic
[
GR14
, Lemma 7.2]
.
∎

3.
Weakly bounded height

To prove Theorem
1.1
, we will make use of a result of Habegger
[
Hab10
, Theorem 1.1]
that gives a height bound for points in the intersection between a curve
satisfying the hypotheses of Theorem
1.1
and a modular curve.
Our aim in this section is to make explicit how the constant in Habegger’s result depends on the height of the curve in question.
We start by recalling another result of Habegger
[
Hab17b
]
.

Proposition 3.1

(
[
Hab17b
, Theorem 1]
)
.

Let
V\subset\mathbb{A}^{2}(\mathbb{C})
be an irreducible algebraic curve defined over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
.
Write
X,Y
for the coordinate functions on
\mathbb{A}^{2}(\mathbb{C})
restricted to
V
.
Suppose that
d_{1}=\deg X>0
and
d_{2}=\deg V>0
.
Let
L_{d_{1},d_{2}}=\log(2^{\min\{d_{1},d_{2}\}}(d_{1}+1)(d_{2}+1))
.
If
(x,y)\in V(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
, then

(3.1)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\frac{h(x)}{d_{1}}-\frac{h(y)}{d_{2}}\right\rvert\leq 5(L_{d_{1},d_{2}}+h(V))^{1/2}\max\left\{\frac{h(x)}{d_{1}},\frac{h(y)}{d_{2}}\right\}^{1/2}.

Remark 3.2
.

The constant
5(L_{d_{1},d_{2}}+h(V))^{1/2}
in (
3.1
) cannot in general be replaced by a constant which is
o(h(V)^{1/2})
.
To see this, let
d_{1},d_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
with
(d_{1},d_{2})=1
and consider, for
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, the curve
V_{n}
defined by
F_{n}(X,Y)=X^{d_{2}}-nY^{d_{1}}
with the point
(n^{1/d_{2}},1)\in V_{n}
.

We may now state the following result, which is the requisite explicit form of
[
Hab10
, Theorem 1.1]
. The proof is exactly that of Habegger.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
V\subset\mathbb{A}^{2}(\mathbb{C})
be an irreducible algebraic curve defined over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
.
Write
X,Y
for the coordinate functions on
\mathbb{A}^{2}(\mathbb{C})
restricted to
V
.
Suppose that
d_{1}=\deg X>0
and
d_{2}=\deg V>0
and, further,
d_{1}\neq d_{2}
.
Let
L_{d_{1},d_{2}}
be as in Proposition
3.1
.
If
(x,y)\in V(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
is such that

\max\left\{\frac{h(x)}{d_{1}},\frac{h(y)}{d_{2}}\right\}\geq 26\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\}^{2}(L_{d_{1},d_{2}}+h(V))

and
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
is such that
\Phi_{N}(x,y)=0
, then

\max\left\{\frac{h(x)}{d_{1}},\frac{h(y)}{d_{2}}\right\}\leq 474+618\log N.

Proof.

Let

B=\max\left\{\frac{h(x)}{d_{1}},\frac{h(y)}{d_{2}}\right\},

and suppose that

(3.2)

\displaystyle B\geq 26\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\}^{2}(L_{d_{1},d_{2}}+h(V)).

Since
(x,y)\in V(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
, Proposition
3.1
implies that

\left\lvert\frac{h(x)}{d_{1}}-\frac{h(y)}{d_{2}}\right\rvert\leq 5(L_{d_{1},d_{2}}+h(V))^{1/2}B^{1/2}.

Hence, the assumption (
3.2
) yields that

(3.3)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\frac{h(x)}{d_{1}}-\frac{h(y)}{d_{2}}\right\rvert\leq\frac{5B}{\sqrt{26}\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\}}.

Now observe that, since
d_{1}\neq d_{2}
,

\displaystyle\frac{h(x)}{d_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{h(x)}{d_{1}}\lvert d_{1}-d_{2}\rvert

(3.4)

\displaystyle\leq d_{2}\left\lvert\frac{h(x)}{d_{1}}-\frac{h(y)}{d_{2}}\right\rvert+\lvert h(y)-h(x)\rvert,

and, similarly,

(3.5)

\displaystyle\frac{h(y)}{d_{2}}\leq d_{1}\left\lvert\frac{h(y)}{d_{2}}-\frac{h(x)}{d_{1}}\right\rvert+\lvert h(x)-h(y)\rvert.

From (
3.3
), (
3
), and (
3.5
), we thus obtain that

\left(1-\frac{5}{\sqrt{26}}\right)B\leq\lvert h(x)-h(y)\rvert.

Finally, note that if
\Phi_{N}(x,y)=0
, then
[
Paz19
, Theorem 1.1]
implies that

\lvert h(x)-h(y)\rvert\leq 9.204+12\log N.\qed

Proposition
3.3
implies that special points
(x,y)\in V\cap\mathbb{V}(\Phi_{N})
satisfy

\max\{h(x),h(y)\}\leq c_{1}h(V)+c_{2}\log N,

where
c_{1},c_{2}
depend only on
\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\}
and
[L:\mathbb{Q}]
.
This is much stronger in its dependence on
N
than the condition
\mathrm{WBH}(\eta,\kappa)
in Theorem
1.3
.
Nonetheless, Proposition
3.3
is still not sufficient to prove a uniform version of André’s theorem, because of the linear dependence on
h(V)
.
Remark
3.2
shows that obtaining a better than linear dependence on
h(V)
will require a different approach than using Proposition
3.1
alone.

4.
Tatuzawa’s theorem and
\epsilon
-exceptional special points

4.1.
Tatuzawa’s theorem

Tatuzawa
[
Tat51
]
proved that Siegel’s
[
Sie35
]
ineffective lower bound for the class number of an imaginary quadratic field can be made effective, apart from a single possible exceptional field.

Proposition 4.1

(
[
Tat51
, Theorem 1]
)
.

Let
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
. Then

(4.1)

\displaystyle\cl(D)\geq\frac{\epsilon}{10\pi}\lvert D\rvert^{1/2-\epsilon}

for every fundamental discriminant
D
of an imaginary quadratic field, apart from at most one exception.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
. Denote by
D_{\epsilon}
the unique (for the given
\epsilon
)
D
for which (
4.1
) fails, if it exists.

Tatuzawa’s theorem extends to non-fundamental discriminants via classical bounds for the totient function
\varphi(\cdot)
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
. Then
\varphi(n)\geq 2^{-1/\delta}n^{1-\delta}
for every
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
.

Proof.

Observe that

\displaystyle\varphi(n)

\displaystyle=n\prod_{p\mid n}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\geq n\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\geq 2\\
k^{\delta}\leq 2\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\mid n\\
p^{\delta}>2\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\geq n2^{-1/\delta}\prod_{p\mid n}p^{-\delta},

since
p^{\delta}>2
implies that
p^{-\delta}<1/2<1-1/p
.
∎

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
. Let
D\neq D_{\epsilon}
be a fundamental discriminant of an imaginary quadratic field. Let
f\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
and
\Delta=f^{2}D
. Then

\cl(\Delta)\geq\frac{\epsilon}{30\pi(\sqrt{2})^{1/\epsilon}}\lvert\Delta\rvert^{1/2-\epsilon}.

Proof.

Let
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
and denote by
\mathcal{O}_{K}
its ring of integers. Let
\mathcal{O}_{f}
denote the order of conductor
f
in
\mathcal{O}_{K}
. Then, by
[
Cox22
, Theorem 7.24]
,

\cl(\Delta)=\frac{\cl(D)}{[\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}:\mathcal{O}_{f}^{\times}]}f\prod_{p\mid f}\left(1-\left(\frac{D}{p}\right)\frac{1}{p}\right),

where here and hereafter
(D/p)
denotes the Kronecker symbol. Since
[\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}:\mathcal{O}_{f}^{\times}]\leq 3
and
(D/p)\in\{-1,0,1\}
, we have that

\cl(\Delta)\geq\frac{\cl(D)}{3}\varphi(f).

The result thus follows from Proposition
4.1
and Lemma
4.3
.
∎

4.2.
\epsilon
-exceptional special points

Definition 4.5
.

Let
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
. Let
x,y
be singular moduli of respective fundamental discriminants
D_{x},D_{y}
.
Let
f,g\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
be such that
\Delta_{x}=f^{2}D_{x}
and
\Delta_{y}=g^{2}D_{y}
.
Let
l=\mathrm{lcm}(f,g)
.
The special point
(x,y)
is called
\epsilon
-
exceptional
if
D_{x}=D_{y}
and

\cl(l^{2}D_{x})<\frac{\epsilon}{30\pi(\sqrt{2})^{1/\epsilon}}\lvert l^{2}D_{x}\rvert^{1/2-\epsilon}.

Remark 4.6
.

Observe that if the special point
(x,y)
is
\epsilon
-exceptional, then
D_{x}=D_{y}=D_{\epsilon}
by Proposition
4.4
.
Note however that a special point
(x,y)
with
D_{x}=D_{y}=D_{\epsilon}
is not necessarily
\epsilon
-exceptional.
Indeed, if
p
is prime, then
[
Cox22
, Theorem 7.24]
implies that

\cl(p^{2}D)\geq\frac{p-1}{3}\cl(D),

but
(p-1)/p^{1-2\epsilon}\to\infty
as
p\to\infty
.

The key property of
\epsilon
-exceptional special points
(x,y)
is that both
\Delta_{x}
and
\Delta_{y}
have small class numbers.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
.
Let
(x,y)
be an
\epsilon
-exceptional special point.
Then

\cl(\Delta_{x})<\frac{\epsilon}{10\pi}\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert^{1/2-\epsilon}\mbox{ and }\cl(\Delta_{y})<\frac{\epsilon}{10\pi}\lvert\Delta_{y}\rvert^{1/2-\epsilon}.

Proof.

Denote by
D
the common fundamental discriminant of
x
and
y
.
Let
f,g\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
be such that
\Delta_{x}=f^{2}D
and
\Delta_{y}=g^{2}D
.
Let
l=\mathrm{lcm}(f,g)
.
Since the special point
(x,y)
is
\epsilon
-exceptional,

(4.2)

\displaystyle\cl(l^{2}D)<\frac{\epsilon}{30\pi(\sqrt{2})^{1/\epsilon}}\lvert l^{2}D\rvert^{1/2-\epsilon}.

Let
\mathcal{O}_{f},\mathcal{O}_{l}
denote the imaginary quadratic orders of discriminant
f^{2}D
and
l^{2}D
respectively.
By
[
Cox22
, Corollary 7.28]
,

\cl(l^{2}D)=\frac{\cl(\Delta_{x})}{[\mathcal{O}_{f}^{\times}:\mathcal{O}_{l}^{\times}]}\frac{l}{f}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\mid\frac{l}{f}\end{subarray}}\left(1-\left(\frac{f^{2}D}{p}\right)\frac{1}{p}\right).

Since
[\mathcal{O}_{f}^{\times}:\mathcal{O}_{l}^{\times}]\leq 3
and the Kronecker symbol takes values in
\{-1,0,1\}
, we obtain that

\frac{1}{3}\cl(f^{2}D)\varphi\left(\frac{l}{f}\right)\leq\cl(l^{2}D).

Therefore, Lemma
4.3
and (
4.2
) together imply that

\cl(\Delta_{x})<\frac{\epsilon}{10\pi}\lvert f^{2}D\rvert^{1/2-\epsilon}.

The situation for
\cl(\Delta_{y})
is analogous.
∎

Proposition
4.7
gives an upper bound for the degrees of
x
and
y
. This yields a lower bound for the heights of
x
and
y
, thanks to Proposition
2.3
.

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
.
Let
(x,y)
be an
\epsilon
-exceptional special point.
Then

h(x)\geq\frac{30\pi}{\epsilon}\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert^{\epsilon}\mbox{ and }h(y)\geq\frac{30\pi}{\epsilon}\lvert\Delta_{y}\rvert^{\epsilon}.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of Propositions
2.3
and
4.7
.
∎

4.3.
Non-
\epsilon
-exceptional special points

If a special point
(x,y)
with
D_{x}=D_{y}
is not
\epsilon
-exceptional, then its Galois orbit is “large” (i.e. it satisfies (
1.4
)).
This is true even if
D_{x}=D_{y}=D_{\epsilon}
.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
.
Let
x,y
be singular moduli of the same fundamental discriminant
D
.
If
(x,y)
is not
\epsilon
-exceptional, then

[\mathbb{Q}(x,y):\mathbb{Q}]\geq\frac{\epsilon}{90\pi(\sqrt{2})^{1/\epsilon}}\max\{\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert,\lvert\Delta_{y}\rvert\}^{1/2-\epsilon}.

Proof.

Let
f,g\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
be such that
\Delta_{x}=f^{2}D
and
\Delta_{y}=g^{2}D
.
Let
l=\mathrm{lcm}(f,g)
.
Let
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
.
By
[
ABPM15
, Proposition 3.1]
, the field
K(x,y)
is a subfield of degree at most
3
of the ring class field of discriminant
l^{2}D
.
The ring class field of discriminant
l^{2}D
is itself an extension of
K
of degree
\cl(l^{2}D)
. Hence,

[K(x,y):K]\geq\frac{\cl(l^{2}D)}{3}.

Since the special point
(x,y)
is not
\epsilon
-exceptional, by definition we have that

\cl(l^{2}D)\geq\frac{\epsilon}{30\pi(\sqrt{2})^{1/\epsilon}}\lvert l^{2}D\rvert^{1/2-\epsilon}.

Note that
\max\{\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert,\lvert\Delta_{y}\rvert\}\leq\lvert l^{2}D\rvert
. We thus obtain that

\displaystyle[\mathbb{Q}(x,y):\mathbb{Q}]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\epsilon}{90\pi(\sqrt{2})^{1/\epsilon}}\max\{\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert,\lvert\Delta_{y}\rvert\}^{1/2-\epsilon}.\qed

In fact, we may prove a similar lower bound for the Galois orbit of one of
x
and
y
, provided
\epsilon
is sufficiently small. This requires an easy lemma about the Dedekind
\psi
-function
\psi(\cdot)
.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
\delta>0
.
Then
\psi(n)\leq n^{1+\delta}/\delta
for every
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 2}
.

Proof.

Let
f_{n}(t)=n^{1+t}/t
.
Then, for every
\delta>0
,

f_{n}(\delta)\geq f_{n}\left(\frac{1}{\log n}\right)=en\log n.

In particular,
f_{n}(\delta)\geq\psi(n)
for
2\leq n\leq 6
.
For
n\geq 7
, note that

\displaystyle\frac{\psi(n)}{n}\leq\prod_{p\mid n}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{-1}=\frac{n}{\varphi(n)}

\displaystyle\leq e^{\gamma}\log\log n+\frac{2.51}{\log\log n},

by
[
RS62
, Theorem 15]
, and the right-hand side is
\leq e\log n
for
n\geq 7
.
∎

Proposition 4.11
.

Let
\epsilon\in(0,1/5)
.
Let
x,y
be singular moduli of the same fundamental discriminant
D
.
Let
f,g\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
be such that
\Delta_{x}=f^{2}D
and
\Delta_{y}=g^{2}D
.
Suppose that the special point
(x,y)
is not
\epsilon
-exceptional.
Then

\cl\left(\max\{f,g\}^{2}D\right)\geq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{30\pi(\sqrt{2})^{1/\epsilon}}\left\lvert\max\{f,g\}^{2}D\right\rvert^{1/2-5\epsilon/2}.

Proof.

Let
l=\mathrm{lcm}(f,g)
.
Without loss of generality, assume that
f\geq g
.
In particular,
l\leq f^{2}
and
\max\{f,g\}^{2}D=\Delta_{x}
.
Let
\mathcal{O}_{f},\mathcal{O}_{l}
denote the imaginary quadratic orders of discriminant
f^{2}D
and
l^{2}D
respectively.
By
[
Cox22
, Corollary 7.28]
,

\cl(l^{2}D)=\frac{\cl(f^{2}D)}{[\mathcal{O}_{f}^{\times}:\mathcal{O}_{l}^{\times}]}\frac{l}{f}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\mid\frac{l}{f}\end{subarray}}\left(1-\left(\frac{f^{2}D}{p}\right)\frac{1}{p}\right).

Since the Kronecker symbol takes values in
\{-1,0,1\}
, we obtain that

\cl(l^{2}D)\leq\cl(f^{2}D)\psi\left(\frac{l}{f}\right),

where
\psi(\cdot)
denotes the Dedekind
\psi
-function. Lemma
4.10
implies that

\cl(l^{2}D)\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(\frac{l}{f}\right)^{1+\epsilon}\cl(f^{2}D).

Since the special point
(x,y)
is not
\epsilon
-exceptional,

\cl(l^{2}D)\geq\frac{\epsilon}{30\pi(\sqrt{2})^{1/\epsilon}}\lvert l^{2}D\rvert^{1/2-\epsilon}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\cl(f^{2}D)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{30\pi(\sqrt{2})^{1/\epsilon}}\left(\frac{f}{l}\right)^{1+\epsilon}\lvert l^{2}D\rvert^{1/2-\epsilon}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{30\pi(\sqrt{2})^{1/\epsilon}}\frac{f^{1+\epsilon}}{l^{3\epsilon}}\lvert D\rvert^{1/2-\epsilon}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{30\pi(\sqrt{2})^{1/\epsilon}}f^{1-5\epsilon}\lvert D\rvert^{1/2-\epsilon}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{30\pi(\sqrt{2})^{1/\epsilon}}\lvert f^{2}D\rvert^{1/2-5\epsilon/2},

where we use that
f^{2}\geq l

5.

The following result was proved by Kühne

In general, it is a special case of a result proved using effective point counting by Binyamini
[

Strictly speaking, Binyamini
[

A simplified proof of
[

Theorem 5.1

(
[
Bin20
, Corollary 1]
)
.

Let
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
.
Let
V\subset\mathbb{A}^{2}(\mathbb{C})
be a geometrically irreducible, non-special algebraic curve defined over a number field
L
. Write
X,Y
for the coordinate functions on
\mathbb{A}^{2}(\mathbb{C})
restricted to
V
.
Let
d_{1}=\deg X
and
d_{2}=\deg Y
.
There exists an effectively computable constant
c(\epsilon,[L:\mathbb{Q}],\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\})
with the following property:
if
(x,y)\in V
is a special point with fundamental discriminants
(D_{x},D_{y})\neq(D_{\epsilon},D_{\epsilon})
, then

\displaystyle\max\{\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert,\lvert\Delta_{y}\rvert\}\leq c(\epsilon,[L:\mathbb{Q}],\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\}).

To prove Theorem
1.1
, we therefore only need to consider special points
(x,y)
with fundamental discriminants

.
Such special points
(x,y)
are either
\epsilon
-exceptional or not, and we treat the two cases separately.

In this section, we will strengthen Theorem
5.1
by proving the following theorem, which covers all special points
(x,y)
that are not
\epsilon
-exceptional.
Note that this theorem holds for all non-special curves, and does not require the additional hypothesis of Theorem
1.1
that
d_{1}\neq d_{2}

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
.

V\subset\mathbb{A}^{2}(\mathbb{C})
be a geometrically irreducible, non-special algebraic curve defined over a number field
L

X,Y
for the coordinate functions on
\mathbb{A}^{2}(\mathbb{C})
restricted to
V
.
Let
d_{1}=\deg X
and
d_{2}=\deg Y
.
There exists an effectively computable constant
c(\epsilon,[L:\mathbb{Q}],\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\})
with the following property:
if a special point
(x,y)\in V
is not
\epsilon
-exceptional, then

\displaystyle\max\{\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert,\lvert\Delta_{y}\rvert\}\leq c(\epsilon,[L:\mathbb{Q}],\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\}).

The first proof uses effective point counting results of Binyamini
[

5.1.
Effective Pila–Zannier

In

The Galois bound in Proposition

First proof of Theorem

.

.

Let

be singular moduli of the same fundamental discriminant

such that the special point

is not

Since

and

is defined over

[

6.
Handling

To complete the proof of Theorem

6.1.
From height bounds to finiteness

Before proving Theorem
1.1
, we will show how one may deduce a version of André’s theorem directly from Theorem
5.2
and a height bound for special points of the form of (
1.6
).

Let
V\subset\mathbb{A}^{2}(\mathbb{C})
be an irreducible, non-special algebraic curve defined over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
.
Suppose that constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
are such that

(6.1)

\displaystyle\max\{h(x),h(y)\}\leq c_{1}[\mathbb{Q}(x,y):\mathbb{Q}]^{c_{2}}

for every special point
(x,y)\in V
.
Such a bound follows, for example, from a result of Papas
[
Pap26
, Theorem 2.17]
in the case that
V
satisfies a suitable boundary condition.
A conjecture of Habegger
[
Hab10
, Conjecture]
would imply, for every non-special curve
V
, a stronger bound

\max\{h(x),h(y)\}\leq c_{3}\log(1+[\mathbb{Q}(x,y):\mathbb{Q}]).

Let
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
be such that
\epsilon>c_{2}/(2c_{2}+1)
.
Let
(x,y)\in V
be an
\epsilon
-exceptional special point.
Then Propositions
4.7
and
4.8
, together with (
6.1
), imply that

\frac{30\pi}{\epsilon}\max\{\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert,\lvert\Delta_{y}\rvert\}^{\epsilon}\leq c_{1}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{10\pi}\max\{\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert,\lvert\Delta_{y}\rvert\}^{(1/2-\epsilon)}\right)^{2c_{2}}.

Note that
\epsilon-(1-2\epsilon)c_{2}>0
.
Hence,

\max\{\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert,\lvert\Delta_{y}\rvert\}\leq\left(\frac{c_{1}}{3}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{10\pi}\right)^{2c_{2}+1}\right)^{1/(\epsilon-(1-2\epsilon)c_{2})}.

On the other hand, if a special point
(x,y)\in V
is not
\epsilon
-exceptional, then
\max\{\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert,\lvert\Delta_{y}\rvert\}
is bounded by Theorem
5.2
.

Remark 6.1
.

Of course, since André
[
And98
]
showed that a non-special curve contains only finitely many special points, there will always exist, for a given curve
V
, constants
c_{1},c_{2}
such that (
6.1
) holds.
The case where the above argument is interesting is therefore when
c_{1},c_{2}
either are of relatively modest size or are uniform in certain data associated to
V
.
Then we may deduce, by the above argument, a very strong bound on the discriminants of
\epsilon
-exceptional special points that lie on
V
, without appealing to Masser–Wüstholz endomorphism estimates or point counting.
Together with Theorem
5.2
, this establishes an explicit version of André’s theorem for such curves
V
.
In particular, it gives a different way to deduce
[
Pap26
, Corollary 1.7]
from
[
Pap26
, Theorem 2.17]
than the one used by Papas.

6.2.
Bounds for
\epsilon
-exceptional special points

We now start the proof of Theorem
1.1
.
The following lemma is a convenience to ease notation later.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
.
Let
(x,y)
be an
\epsilon
-exceptional special point.
Assume that
\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert\geq\lvert\Delta_{y}\rvert
.
Let
E_{x}
be an elliptic curve with
j
-invariant
x
.
If

h_{\mathrm{F}}(E_{x})\leq\log[\mathbb{Q}(x,y):\mathbb{Q}],

then

\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert\leq\left(\frac{1}{12\epsilon}\right)^{2/\epsilon}.

Proof.

Since
(x,y)
is
\epsilon
-exceptional, Proposition
4.7
implies that

\displaystyle[\mathbb{Q}(x,y):\mathbb{Q}]\leq\cl(\Delta_{x})\cl(\Delta_{y})\leq\left(\frac{\epsilon}{10\pi}\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert^{1/2-\epsilon}\right)^{2}.

Pazuki’s explicit version
[
Paz19
, (3.19)]
of Silverman’s
[
Sil86
, Proposition 2.1]
height comparison gives that

h_{\mathrm{F}}(E_{x})\geq\frac{1}{12}h(x)-\frac{1}{2}\log(1+h(x))-2.08.

Note that

\frac{1}{12}t-\frac{1}{2}\log(1+t)-2.08\geq\frac{1}{20}t-3

for every
t\geq 0
.
Proposition
4.8
therefore implies that

h_{\mathrm{F}}(E_{x})\geq\frac{3\pi}{2\epsilon}\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert^{\epsilon}-3.

Suppose then that

h_{\mathrm{F}}(E_{x})\leq\log[\mathbb{Q}(x,y):\mathbb{Q}].

Then

\frac{3\pi}{2\epsilon}\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert^{\epsilon}-3\leq 2\log\left(\frac{\epsilon}{10\pi}\right)+(1-2\epsilon)\log(\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert).

Since
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
, we obtain that

3\pi\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert^{\epsilon}-\log(\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert)\leq 3-2\log\left(20\pi\right)\leq 0.

Recall that if
\delta>0
, then
\log(t)\leq t^{\delta}/(e\delta)
for every

. Therefore,

3\pi\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert^{\epsilon}\leq\frac{2}{e\epsilon}\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert^{\epsilon/2}.\qed

The proof of Theorem
1.1
rests on the following theorem.
The hypothesis that

is required in the proof only for the appeal to Proposition
3.3
.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
.
Let
V\subset\mathbb{A}^{2}(\mathbb{C})
be a geometrically irreducible, non-special algebraic curve defined over a number field

X,Y
for the coordinate functions on

\epsilon

\max\{\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert,\lvert\Delta_{y}\}\rvert\leq\max\left\{\frac{1}{12\epsilon},37+3\log\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\},6\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\}\right\}^{2/\epsilon}.

Proof.

Denote by
D_{x},D_{y}
the fundamental discriminants of

respectively.
Note that
D_{x}=D_{y}
, since
(x,y)
is
\epsilon
-exceptional. Hence, Proposition
2.4
implies that
\Phi_{N}(x,y)=0
for some

with this property. Without loss of generality, assume that
\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert\geq\lvert\Delta_{y}\rvert

Suppose that

(6.2)

\displaystyle\max\{h(x),h(y)\}\geq 26\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\}^{3}(L_{d_{1},d_{2}}+h(V)).

Since
(x,y)\in V
and
\Phi_{N}(x,y)=0
, Proposition
3.3
therefore implies that

(6.3)

\displaystyle h(x),h(y)\leq\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\}(474+618\log N).

Since
(x,y)
is
\epsilon
-exceptional, Proposition
4.8
implies that

(6.4)

\displaystyle h(x)\geq\frac{30\pi}{\epsilon}\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert^{\epsilon}\mbox{ and }h(y)\geq\frac{30\pi}{\epsilon}\lvert\Delta_{y}\rvert^{\epsilon}.

From (
6.3

), we obtain that

(6.5)

\displaystyle\log N

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{0.15}{\epsilon\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\}}\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert^{\epsilon}-0.8\right).

E_{x}/\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
be an elliptic curve with

-invariant

.

would imply that

(6.6)

\displaystyle\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert\leq\left(\frac{1}{12\epsilon}\right)^{2/\epsilon}.

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle[\mathbb{Q}(x,y):\mathbb{Q}]

Remark 6.4
.

The constants in Theorem
6.3
are completely explicit.
Moreover, they are independent of
[L:\mathbb{Q}]
.
If
\epsilon
is not too small, then the constants are also of relatively modest size in terms of
\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\}
and
h(V)
.
Theorem
6.3
therefore offers a practical way to rule out the existence of
\epsilon
-exceptional special points on many curves of interest.

For example, fix
\epsilon=2/5
.
Let
V
be an irreducible algebraic curve satisfying the hypotheses of Theorem
6.3
and such that
\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\}\leq 10
and
h(V)\leq 10
.
Then Theorem
6.3
and Proposition
4.8
together imply that any

\epsilon
-exceptional special point
(x,y)\in V
satisfies

\max\{\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert,\lvert\Delta_{y}\rvert\}<8\times 10^{8}.

It is easy to verify computationally (e.g. in PARI
[
PAR24
]
) that every fundamental discriminant
D
with
\lvert D\rvert<8\times 10^{8}
satisfies the bound (
4.1
) with
\epsilon=2/5
, i.e. none of the fundamental discriminants
D
with
\lvert D\rvert<8\times 10^{8}
is the single possible exceptional fundamental discriminant in Tatuzawa’s theorem applied with
\epsilon=2/5
.
In particular, there are therefore no
\epsilon
-exceptional special points
(x,y)
with
\max\{\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert,\lvert\Delta_{y}\rvert\}<8\times 10^{8}
.
Hence, there are no
\epsilon
-exceptional special points
(x,y)\in V
.
(Of course, this may not surprise the reader who believes in the Generalised Riemann Hypothesis.)

-exceptional.
Hence, for every special point
(x,y)\in V
that is not
\epsilon
-exceptional,

\max\{h(x),h(y)\}\leq\frac{9c_{1}([L:\mathbb{Q}],\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\},\epsilon)^{1/2}}{2}

by
[
Rif19
, Lemma 2.10]
.

If a special point
(x,y)\in V
is
\epsilon
-exceptional, then Theorem
6.3
implies that either

\max\{h(x),h(y)\}\leq 26\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\}^{3}(L_{d_{1},d_{2}}+h(V))

where
L_{d_{1},d_{2}}=\log(2^{\min\{d_{1},d_{2}\}}(d_{1}+1)(d_{2}+1))
, or

\max\{\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert,\lvert\Delta_{y}\rvert\}\leq\max\left\{\frac{1}{12\epsilon},37+3\log\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\},6\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\}\right\}^{2/\epsilon}.

In the latter case,

\max\{h(x),h(y)\}\leq\frac{9}{2}\max\left\{\frac{1}{12\epsilon},37+3\log\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\},6\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\}\right\}^{1+\epsilon/2}

by
[
Rif19
, Lemma 2.10]
again.

So there is an effectively computable constant
c_{2}([L:\mathbb{Q}],\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\},\epsilon)
such that

\max\{h(x),h(y)\}\leq c_{2}([L:\mathbb{Q}],\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\},\epsilon)+26\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\}^{3}h(V)

for every special point
(x,y)\in V
.
The bound (
1.1
) in Theorem
1.1
is then just the specialisation to some arbitrary
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
.

Now we prove the bound (
1.2
). Let some
\delta>0
be given.
Fix

\epsilon=\frac{1}{2+\delta/2},

so that
\epsilon\in(1/(2+\delta),1/2)
.
By the above proof, there exist effectively computable constants
c_{3}([L:\mathbb{Q}],\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\},\delta)
and
c_{4}([L:\mathbb{Q}],\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\},\delta)
with the following property:
for every special point
(x,y)\in V
,

\max\{\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert,\lvert\Delta_{y}\rvert\}\leq c_{3}([L:\mathbb{Q}],\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\},\delta)

if
(x,y)
is not
\epsilon
-exceptional, and

\max\{h(x),h(y)\}\leq c_{4}([L:\mathbb{Q}],\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\},\delta)\max\{1,h(V)\}

if
(x,y)
is
\epsilon
-exceptional.

If a special point
(x,y)
is
\epsilon
-exceptional, then

h(x)\geq\frac{30\pi}{\epsilon}\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert^{\epsilon}\mbox{ and }h(y)\geq\frac{30\pi}{\epsilon}\lvert\Delta_{y}\rvert^{\epsilon}

by Proposition
4.8
.
Therefore, since
\epsilon>1/(2+\delta)
, there exists an effectively computable constant
c_{5}([L:\mathbb{Q}],\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\},\delta)
such that

\max\{\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert,\lvert\Delta_{y}\rvert\}\leq c_{5}([L:\mathbb{Q}],\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\},\delta)\max\{1,h(V)\}^{2+\delta}

for every
\epsilon
-exceptional special point
(x,y)\in V
.
This proves the bound (
1.2
).
Note that, to prove this for every
\delta>0
, it was crucial that Theorem
5.2
holds for every
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
, and not just for
\epsilon\in(0,1/24)
as in Proposition
5.3
.
∎

7.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

Finally, we come to the proof of Theorem
1.3
.
The first part follows the same approach as Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Suppose that
\mathrm{WBH}(\eta,\kappa)
holds for some
\eta\in(0,1/2)
and
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
with a given constant
c_{*}
.
Let
\delta>0
.
Fix some
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
for which

(7.1)

\displaystyle 0<\frac{\kappa}{4\eta}\frac{1}{\epsilon-(1-2\epsilon)(1/2\eta-1)}\leq\frac{\kappa}{2\eta}+\delta,

which we may always achieve by taking
\epsilon
sufficiently close to
1/2
.
To limit the proliferation of constants, we will use Vinogradov’s asymptotic notation
\ll
, where the implied constants will always be effectively computable in terms of
[L:\mathbb{Q}]
,
\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\}
,
c_{*}
, and
\delta
.

Thanks to Theorem
5.2
, it is enough to consider only special points
(x,y)\in V
that are
\epsilon
-exceptional.
Let
(x,y)\in V
be an
\epsilon
-exceptional special point.
Assume, without loss of generality, that
\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert\geq\lvert\Delta_{y}\rvert
.
Let

d=[\mathbb{Q}(x,y):\mathbb{Q}].

Let
E_{x}
be an elliptic curve with
j
-invariant
x
.
By Lemma
6.2
, we may assume that
h_{\mathrm{F}}(E_{x})\geq\log d
.
Hence, by
[
Paz19
, (3.19)]
,

(7.2)

\displaystyle h(x)\gg\log d.

Since
(x,y)
is
\epsilon
-exceptional, we have that
D_{x}=D_{y}
.
Proposition
2.4
then implies that
\Phi_{N}(x,y)=0
for some
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, which we take to be the least such
N
.
Since we are assuming that
\mathrm{WBH}(\eta,\kappa)
holds, we have that

(7.3)

\displaystyle\max\{h(x),h(y)\}\ll\max\{1,h(V)\}^{\kappa}N^{1/2-\eta}.

Proposition
2.5
together with (
7.2
) and (
7.3
) imply that

N\ll\left(\max\{1,h(V)\}^{\kappa}N^{1/2-\eta}d\right)^{2}.

So

(7.4)

\displaystyle N^{\eta}\ll\max\{1,h(V)\}^{\kappa}d.

Since
(x,y)
is
\epsilon
-exceptional, Proposition
4.8
implies that

(7.5)

\displaystyle h(x)\gg\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert^{\epsilon},

and Proposition
4.7
implies, since
\lvert\Delta_{y}\rvert\leq\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert
, that

(7.6)

\displaystyle d\ll\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert^{1-2\epsilon}.

Now put these inequalities together.
From (
7.3
) and (
7.5
), we obtain that

\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert^{\epsilon}\ll\max\{1,h(V)\}^{\kappa}N^{1/2-\eta}.

So, by (
7.4
),

\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert^{\epsilon}\ll\max\{1,h(V)\}^{\kappa}\left(\left(\max\{1,h(V)\}^{\kappa}d\right)^{1/\eta}\right)^{1/2-\eta}.

Hence, by (
7.6
),

\displaystyle\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert^{\epsilon}

\displaystyle\ll\max\{1,h(V)\}^{\kappa}\left(\max\{1,h(V)\}^{\kappa}\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert^{1-2\epsilon}\right)^{1/2\eta-1}

\displaystyle\ll\max\{1,h(V)\}^{\kappa/2\eta}\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert^{(1-2\epsilon)(1/2\eta-1)}.

So

\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert^{\epsilon-(1-2\epsilon)(1/2\eta-1)}\ll\max\{1,h(V)\}^{\kappa/2\eta}.

By
[
Rif19
, Lemma 2.9]
,

\max\{h(x),h(y)\}\ll\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert^{1/2}.

Therefore,

\max\{h(x),h(y)\}\ll(\max\{1,h(V)\}^{\kappa/4\eta})^{1/(\epsilon-(1-2\epsilon)(1/2\eta-1))}.

This completes the proof of (1) in Theorem
1.3
, thanks to inequality (
7.1
).

Now we prove (2) of Theorem
1.3
.
Suppose additionally that
\kappa<2\eta
. Let

\delta=\frac{1-\frac{\kappa}{2\eta}}{2}.

By (1) of Theorem
1.3
, there exists an effectively computable constant
c_{0}=c_{0}([L:\mathbb{Q}],\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\},c_{*})
such that, for every special point
(x,y)\in V
,

(7.7)

\displaystyle\max\{h(x),h(y)\}\leq c_{0}\max\{1,h(V)\}^{\kappa/2\eta+\delta}.

Note that
\kappa/2\eta+\delta<1
.
We may therefore follow the argument of Kühne
[
Küh13
, §5]
.
By
[
Küh13
, Lemma 2]
and
[
Küh13
, (2.1)]
, there exist explicit constants
c_{1}=c_{1}(\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\})>0
and
c_{2}=c_{2}(\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\})
such that the number of points
(x,y)\in V(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
for which

(7.8)

\displaystyle\max\{h(x),h(y)\}\leq c_{1}h(V)+c_{2}

is bounded by an explicit constant
c_{3}=c_{3}(\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\})
.

If
h(V)
is larger than some constant
c_{4}([L:\mathbb{Q}],\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\},c_{*})
, then (
7.7
) implies (
7.8
).
In this case, there are therefore at most
c_{3}
many special points
(x,y)\in V
.
Hence, for every special point
(x,y)\in V
,

\max\{\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert,\lvert\Delta_{y}\rvert\}\leq c_{5}([L:\mathbb{Q}],\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\})

thanks to Proposition
2.1
.
If
h(V)\leq c_{4}([L:\mathbb{Q}],\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\},c_{*})
, then
[
Küh12
, Theorem 2]
implies directly that

\max\{\lvert\Delta_{x}\rvert,\lvert\Delta_{y}\rvert\}\leq c_{6}([L:\mathbb{Q}],\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\},c_{*})

for every special point
(x,y)\in V
.
In either case, we are done.
∎
