Monitoring changes in mixed venous oxygen saturation using photoplethysmography on the sternum – a proof of concept study
Authors: Erik Näslund, Gunnar Strandberg, Jacob Karlsson, Stephanie Franzén, Robert Frithiof
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMixed venous oxygen saturation (SvO2) is a valuable indicator of the balance between oxygen delivery and consumption in the body. However, the invasiveness and risk of complications limit its use. This study aimed to test the feasibility of continuously monitoring variations in SvO2 from photoplethysmography (PPG) measurements of sternal intramedullary oxygen saturation. In an animal model, including anesthetized pigs (n = 15, mean 33.1 (SD 2.0) kg), alterations in SvO2 were instituted by separate sessions: incremental decrease in arterial oxygen saturation using hypoxic gas mixtures (N2/air), and hemorrhage of 20% plus 20% of estimated blood volume. Estimations of non-invasive sternal oxygen saturation (nSsO2) were derived from calibrating PPG measurements against SvO2 sampled from the pulmonary artery. Individual linear correlations were found between SvO2 and PPG measurements. nSsO2 and SvO2 co-varied, except in severe hypovolemia. A concordance rate between nSsO2 and SvO2 was determined to 87% (95% CI 79.1–95.0). In this study, we demonstrated linear correlations between SvO2 and PPG-measurements on the sternum, enabling estimation of nSsO2, which trends with changes in SvO2. Introduction

Mixed venous oxygen saturation (SvO2) is measured from the pulmonary artery and depends on whole-body oxygen delivery and consumption. SvO2 is therefore of clinical interest to monitor in certain situations, such as circulatory or septic shock, guidance for optimization of fluid balance or treatment with vasoactive drugs, or after major heart surgery. However, SvO2 is infrequently used due to its inaccessibility and need for advanced cannulation of intrathoracic vessels, increasing the risk for complications affecting morbidity and sometimes even mortality [1, 2]. In addition, the equipment necessary for continuous SvO2-monitoring is not always available at standard intensive care units. Therefore, a possibility of non- or semi-invasive means of measuring or estimating SvO2 is of great interest. Unfortunately, currently available alternative methods for the continuous assessment of SvO2 are either not representative of a true SvO2 value or not yet fully validated in the clinical context (e.g., near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) and capnodynamics) [3–8].

The oxygen saturation in the blood is defined as the proportion of occupied hemoglobin binding sites by oxygen molecules, expressed as a percentage of the total hemoglobin binding capacity [9].

There is a distinction between how the arterial versus venous oxygen saturation is formed. The arterial oxygen saturation is primarily dependent on the partial pressure of oxygen. In contrast, the venous oxygen saturation is dependent on the balance of oxygen supply and demand, which may vary among locations as different tissue beds have varying metabolic needs [9].

We have previously proposed a novel method for non-invasive measurement of sternal intramedullary oxygen saturation using a wireless photoplethysmography (PPG) sternal prototype probe [10, 11]. The sternum has a mixed arteriovenous blood flow [12, 13]. To investigate how sternal intramedullary oxygen saturation (SsO2) responds to different causes of decreased oxygen delivery, we previously conducted a study using a porcine model of both hypoxemia and hypovolemia. From that study, we concluded that SsO2, resembled SvO2 more closely than arterial oxygen saturation. Additionally, non-invasive PPG measurements over the sternum exhibited the same pattern of changes as SsO2 [11]. However, measurements in that study were obtained only at baseline and during a defined provocation, while the changes occurring between these time points were not assessed. Even though SsO2 differs from SvO2, based on previous results, we hypothesized that changes in SsO2 co-varies with changes in SvO2. Such a relationship could enable a simple method for detecting a change in the body's utilization of oxygen.

The aim of the current study was to determine whether non-invasive PPG measurements of SsO2 could enable continuous monitoring of variations in SvO2. To achieve this, our first objective was to calibrate non-invasive PPG measurements from the sternum against blood gas measurements obtained from mixed venous blood in the pulmonary artery. From this calibration process, we estimated a variable, we refer to as non-invasive SsO2 (nSsO2). The second objective was to assess how well nSsO2 could detect concomitant changes in SvO2 as assessed by the reference method—blood gas analysis from the pulmonary artery.

Methods

Study subjects

The Uppsala Animal Ethics Committee, Uppsala, Sweden, approved the study (5.8.18–02325/2019, date of approval 2019–03–29). All animals were treated according to the Swedish Board of Agriculture guidelines and complied with the European Convention for the Protection of Vertebrate Animals used for Experimental and other Scientific Purposes (Council of Europe No 123, Strasbourg 1985) and the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE 2.0) guidelines.

The study included 15 Norwegian Landrace breed/Hampshire/Yorkshire pigs of both sexes aged 3–4 months. All pigs were acquired from a local breeder, where they were housed in groups and raised according to the farmer’s standard protocol.

Anesthesia and preparation

The pigs arrived at the laboratory at 8.00 am local time. Upon arrival, all animals were calm and normothermic. One animal per day was included in the study. All animals were anesthetized with an intramuscular injection of 6 mg kg−1 tilétamine-zolezepam (Zoletil, Virbac, Denmark) and 2.2 mg kg−1 xylazine (Rompun, Elanco, Denmark). A bolus dose of fentanyl 100 µg was administered intravenously after the establishment of peripheral venous access in an ear vein. The animals were placed in a supine position, tracheotomized, and mechanically ventilated in a volume-controlled mode to reach tidal volume 7 mL kg−1, respiratory rate 25 min−1, fraction of inspired oxygen (FIO2) 0.4, and positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) 5 cmH2O (Servo-I ventilator, Maquet Critical Care, Solna, Sweden) intending to maintain normocapnia.

Maintenance of general anesthesia was performed with an intravenous infusion of propofol 10 mg mL −1 (10 mg kg−1 h−1) and fentanyl (30 μg kg−1 h−1). Rocuronium bromide (Esmeron, Merck & Co. Inc., Kenilworth, NJ, USA) was given 0.25 mg kg−1 h−1 to reduce motion artifacts. All animals were given a fluid bolus of 20 mL kg−1 during the first hour, and after that, the infusion rate was lowered to 10 mL kg−1 h−1 (1:1 ratio of Ringer's Acetate + buffered solution of Glucose 2.5 mg mL−1).

The animals were surgically catheterized with a 3-lumen central venous catheter inserted through a right-sided cervical vein, and forwarded to the central vena cava, along with a balloon-tipped pulmonary artery catheter (7.5F Swan-Ganz, Edwards Lifesciences, Irvine, CA). On the left side of the neck, a central 7.5F PA-introducer was inserted in a cervical vein, enabling hemorrhage. An arterial catheter was placed in the femoral artery using an ultrasound-guided Seldinger technique.

After thoroughly cleansing the skin, the sternal probe was attached to the caudal and flat surface of the sternum using double adhesive tape. All animals were given 30 min to recover before the experimental protocol commenced.

Protocol

The protocol consisted of two sections investigating the performance of nSsO2 (Fig. 1). The first part was progressing systemic hypoxemia, and the second part, controlled hemorrhage.

Schematic illustration of study protocol. For the hypoxic provocation, the percentages refer to intended oxygen saturation levels guided by peripheral pulse oximetry. The stars denote the time points for gathering sternal probe readings along with mixed venous blood gas sampling

Hypoxemia was induced by incrementally adding N2 to the inspired gas mixture in the ventilator. Peripheral pulse oximetry aided in producing stable oxygen saturation levels of 100–95%, 90–85%, 85–80% and 80–70%, respectively. Blood samples were simultaneously collected from both arterial and pulmonary artery catheters, on two separate occasions for each oxygen saturation level. The samplings were separated by a period of at least 30 s. At the end of the hypoxic maneuver, ventilator settings were reset, and approximately 15 min of rest ensued.

Hypovolemia was instituted by hemorrhage of 20% of the estimated circulating blood volume (70 mL kg−1). After approximately five minutes, a second bleeding of another 20% was done. Sampling of arterial and mixed venous blood, and measurement of circulatory parameters, including determination of cardiac output using thermal dilution, were performed at baseline, directly after completion of the bleeding, and again after five minutes for both steps of the bleedings.

All blood samples were analyzed on an ABL800 blood gas analyzer (Radiometer AS, Copenhagen, Denmark) calibrated for porcine blood. The equipment was located within the laboratory, which ensured a minimal time delay before analysis. Basic physiological parameters, such as heart rate and blood pressure were continuously monitored throughout the experiment.

Sufficient anesthetic depth and analgesic level were tested regularly throughout the experiment by monitoring circulatory status in combination with any present motor reflexes from a pain stimulus to the hoof. The experiments were conducted under general anesthesia. At the end of the study protocol, the animals were euthanized with a 20 mL bolus dose of KCl (150 mg mL−1), administered in a central vein, under continuous ECG monitoring to ensure the onset of asystole. Included ethical endpoints for cessation of the study protocol were hyperthermia (> 40 °C) at arrival to the laboratory, mean arterial pressure (MAP) < 40 mmHg despite treatment, pulmonary artery pressure > arterial pressure despite treatment, and arterial oxygen saturation < 85% despite treatment (besides hypoxic provocation).

Sternal probe data

The sternal probe was developed by RespiHeart AB and was loaned to the investigators for the purpose of this study. The company had no involvement in study design, data collection, analysis, interpretation of results, or manuscript preparation. The methodological background of the sternum probe has been described in detail previously [10, 11, 14, 15]. In short, the sternum probe utilizes red (660 nm) and infrared (810 nm) lights in reflectance mode PPG. The separation distance of the LED lights and photodiode (22.5 mm) allows deep penetration of light [16]. The ratio of the DC-components from the PPG-signal is used to form the sternal probe quotient value (SQV), which is used to estimate oxygen saturation. As opposed to previously described [10, 11], this study utilized an updated version of the sternum prototype probe, which included new LED and photodiode components. Also, on startup, light intensity and gain were automatically adjusted to achieve the best possible signal quality. However, the signal enhancements hampered comparisons between individuals since SQV-constructs became specific, hence were data analyzed individually. The 100 Hz raw signal from the sternal probe was transferred via Bluetooth communication to an Android tablet using a signal acquisition program. Matlab (R2022b, Mathworks, Massachusetts, USA) was used to post-process data. The DC-component of the acquired PPG-signal was extracted by applying a second-order Butterworth filter with a cutoff frequency of 0.4 Hz. As opposed to previously described [10], in this study, we choose to form the SQV by dividing DC-components of red light by infrared to better conform to standard PPG-methodology. The SQV values, which were determined for all corresponding time points when blood gases were sampled, were calculated as a four-second SQV-mean, including approximately 400 data points.

Descriptive statistics (Table 1) are presented as median (IQR), using the Wilcoxon signed rank test to analyze for differences, regardless of statistical distribution.

The relationship between SQV/nSsO2 and SvO2

Estimating nSsO2 was performed using a regression model that included SvO2 and SQV. To account for repeated measures within subjects and clustered data (sampling from the different provocations), a linear mixed effects model was used [17].

To determine individual nSsO2 values, the model included SQV and SvO2 from both provocations as fixed effects, along with slope and intercept as random effects. Any violation of model assumptions was tested with residuals vs fitted plots, and normal quantile–quantile (QQ)-plots.

Measures of the goodness of fit, such as the coefficient of determination (R2), describe the amount of variation explained by a model and are well established for linear regression models, but are not as easily adopted regarding linear mixed effects models, including random effect structure. For the mixed-effects model, we report both the conditional R2 (including fixed and random effects) and the marginal R2 (fixed effects only) [18]. Because the analysis was performed at the individual subject level, the random effects contributed substantially to the total variance. Therefore, our interpretation focuses on the model’s explanatory capacity, as captured by the conditional R2.

There is no straightforward method for obtaining subject-specific R2 estimates in a linear mixed-effects model. As a surrogate measure of the strength of the individual-level fit, R2 was calculated using ordinary linear regression on paired raw data of SvO2 and SQV for each subject.

Trend analysis between nSsO2 and SvO2

The ability of SQV to track changes in global level of oxygen balance compared to the reference method SvO2 was assessed by calculating the concordance rate, i.e., the percentage of data points moving in the same direction when comparing two different techniques [19]. Previously published data has shown an inherent precision of ± 7% for the reference method CO-oximetry SvO2 when using the same blood gas machine calibrated for porcine hemoglobin in the same laboratory [20]. This precision was then used to calculate the least significant change (LSC), i.e., the minimum change measured and recognized as a true change for the reference method [21]. LSC was used to determine the exclusion zone in the concordance analysis as previously described [8]. The LSC for CO-oximetry was found to be 9.5%. Based on this, changes in SvO2 of less than 9.5% (rounded up to 10%) of the mean of all SvO2 CO-oximetry recordings (corresponding to a minimal change in CO-oximetry SvO2 of 6 percentage points) were not regarded as true changes and excluded from the concordance analysis. A 95% CI for the concordance rate was also calculated as previously described [22].

A concordance rate of > 92% was considered a good result, based on clinical judgment and investigation of related monitoring methods [19, 20].

All statistical analysis and graphics were done using R Statistical Software (v4.3.1; R Core Team 2021). The mixed models were constructed using the lme4-package (v1.1.34; Bates et al., 2015) together with the lmerTest-package (v3.1.3; Kuznetsova, Brockhoff, and Christensen 2017) to approximate p-values using Satterwaith's method. A two-sided p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

The included animals (7 male/8 female) had a mean weight of 33.1 (SD 2.0) kg. One subject could not complete the protocol due to severe hemodynamic instability. The remaining 14 animals produced 182 paired observations between SvO2 and SQV. Five observations were excluded due to malfunction of the blood gas analyzer, thus generating a total of n = 177 paired measurements.

According to measured vital parameters, all pigs were in a stable physiological state before the hypoxic and hypovolemic provocations were commenced (Table 1). FIO2 required to reach the predefined oxygen saturation levels, measured by pulse oximetry, ranged between 0.4–0.12 (the latter was the lowest value the oxygen sensor in the ventilator could measure). Hypoxemia induced an expected marked drop in systemic arterial saturation, SvO2, and nSsO2. Additional recorded data is displayed in Table 1.

During hemorrhage, the mean bleeding times were 5 min 1 s (SD 1 min 12 s) and 4 min 47 s (SD 1 min 43 s) for the first and second bleeding, respectively. SaO2 remained constant throughout the hypovolemic provocation, but SvO2 and nSsO2 decreased. The significantly reduced cardiac output (CO), MAP, and hemoglobin suggested an established low circulatory state (Table 1).

The conditional R2 for the mixed-effects model was 0.71, indicating that 71% of the variance in SvO2 was accounted for when including the random subject effects. The marginal R2 was 0.37, corresponding to the variance attributable to SQV independent of between-subject differences. Visual inspections of individual raw data, together with R2 advocated a linear relationship between changes in SQV and SvO2.

Trend analysis between nSsO2 and SvO2

A total of 144 complete data pairs from the different provocations were available for concordance analysis. Of those, 76 data pairs had a consecutive difference of less than 6 oxygen percentage points, hence were within the exclusion zone. The remaining 68 data points (31 originating from the hypoxemic provocation, and 37 from the hypovolemic provocation, respectively) were available to assess trending (Fig. 4). From the four-quadrant plot the pooled concordance rate, for the two provocations, was 87% (95% CI 79.1–95.0). The concordance rates for the hypoxic and hypovolemic provocations were 90.6% (95% CI 80.5–100) and 83.8 (95% CI 71.9–95.7) respectively.

A post hoc sensitivity analysis including all 144 data points for the pooled concordance rate was performed, which demonstrated a concordance rate of 75.4% (95% CI 68.2–82.6). When studying Figs. 2, and 4, respectively it is evident that the data points originating from the 40% bleeding level often deviates from the rest. Therefore, an exploratory post hoc sensitivity analysis was performed excluding those data from the pooled concordance analysis. The resulting 51 data pairs demonstrated a pooled concordance rate of 92.2% (95% CI 84.8–99.6).

Discussion

In this porcine model, the main findings are that SQV, measured non-invasively on the sternum, and SvO2, determined by CO-oximetry, are linearly correlated on an individual subject level. This correlation can be used to estimate nSsO2, which co-varies with changes in SvO2 during hypoxemia, and mild to moderate hypovolemia.

The current study is a progression of a previous study, in which we demonstrated that SsO2 has more similarities with SvO2 than arterial oxygen saturation [11]. Our first objective in this study was to calibrate SQV measurements against SvO2. This calibration procedure is similar to the ones undertaken when developing new pulse oximeters, in the sense that a calibration curve is empirically obtained by measuring absorption quotients between red and infrared lights at a spectrum of varied oxygen saturations [23]. Usually, the pulse oximeter quotient formed is a ratio of AC- and DC-components to isolate the pulsatile component [23]. Our proposed method only utilizes the DC-components of red and infrared lights, thus determines the total change in absorbance when illuminating a tissue volume [10, 11]. To only account for DC-component alterations is an approach towards the NIRS-methodology. The feasibility of using DC-PPG in a NIRS-like manner has been shown by others [24].

Our rationale for assessing PPG on sternal blood flow are based on the facts that intramedullary PPG measurements are stable and less susceptible to external influence such as temperature or pressure [25, 26]. The sternum has a central anatomical location, supporting rapid detection of changes in oxygen saturation [10, 27, 28]. There are several arteries perfusing the bone, rendering PPG measurements stable [29, 30]. Also, the sternum is easily accessible.

Sternal PPG does not yield a direct measure of SvO2 but instead reflects regional sternal tissue oxygenation. Similarly, NIRS quantifies regional tissue oxygen saturation rather than true mixed venous oxygen saturation. Multiple anatomical measurement sites have been evaluated with NIRS to improve its agreement with SvO2 [3–7]. Since both PPG and NIRS are optically based, they share common sources for artifacts, such as shunting of light and susceptibility to motion. One distinction between the two, is that sternal PPG signals contain both the AC- and DC-components. Although, measurement of oxygenation is based on the DC-components, the preserved AC-components can be used for determination of pulse or respiratory rate [14, 15]. Unlike PPG, and NIRS, capnodynamics estimates SvO2 by analyzing the expiratory CO2 content, and has demonstrated promising agreement to measured SvO2. However, the technique is semi-invasive necessitating a mechanically ventilated patient [8].

A consequence of the automatic adjustment of signal quality was that the starting SQV became close to 1, regardless of SvO2. Hence, creating a population-level calibration curve was not feasible in this study since all pigs had slightly different SvO2 when the experiments commenced. Individual scatter plots of SQV and SvO2 demonstrated strong individual linear relationships, supported both by visual inspection, individual R2, and the conditional R2 of 0.71. Also, the slopes between individuals were found to be similar (Table 2, Fig. 2), except for a few individuals (subjects 2 and 5). For subject 2, all SQV-values for the hypovolemic provocation were high (Fig. 2). The recording from the sternal probe showed that the signal intensity of red light was higher than infrared, opposite to the hypoxic provocation in that animal, and the rest of the population (data not shown). The reason for this is unknown. For subject 5, obtaining a peripheral pulse oximeter reading was difficult due to peripheral vasoconstriction. The baseline cardiac output measurement was considerably lower than the rest of the pigs, and a proper separation of red and infrared light could not be seen on the sternal probe recordings from that individual.

During the hypoxic provocation (Fig. 3a), SvO2 and nSsO2 declined in parallel. However, during hypovolemia (Fig. 3b), SvO2 and nSsO2 only co-varied up to a moderate degree of hemorrhage (approximately 20%) [31], after which nSsO2 began to deviate from SvO2. During 40% volume loss, measured SQV, and consequently nSsO2 was higher than expected.

The divergence between nSsO2 and SvO2 observed during severe hypovolemia, has not been studied in detail, but may reflect fundamental differences between global and regional oxygenation measurements.

SvO2 represents the weighted average of all venous beds and is therefore highly sensitive to increases in systemic oxygen extraction. Experimental data demonstrate that rapid hemorrhage induces profound hemodynamic alterations and neurohumoral activation, resulting in marked increases in global oxygen extraction compared with graded bleeding [32]. As our model represents rapid hemorrhage, the pronounced decline in SvO2 (Fig. 3b) is consistent with such a systemic response.

In contrast, the sternal measurement reflects oxygenation within a localized bone marrow compartment. Since SaO2 remained constant throughout hypovolemia, the arteriovenous admixture may have attenuated the decline of nSsO2 compared with SvO2. Also, the sternum is highly vascularized and contains a sinusoidal microcirculation with relatively slow transit time and substantial intravascular volume, which might have reduced rapid fluctuations in oxygen saturation. Furthermore, due to its central anatomical location and crucial role in erythropoiesis, regional perfusion to the sternum may be relatively preserved during sympathetic redistribution of blood flow.

Methodological aspects of the DC-based optical approach may also be considered. Because nSsO2 is derived from non-pulsatile light attenuation components, its sensitivity depends on the fractional blood volume within the sampled tissue. During severe hypovolemia, a reduction in local blood volume may decrease the relative contribution of hemoglobin to the overall optical signal. As a consequence, further decreases in venous oxygen saturation may produce proportionally smaller changes in the DC ratio, resulting in a plateau-like behavior despite continued systemic desaturation [33].

Because different tissues have distinct metabolic rates and oxygen extraction patterns, the resulting venous oxygen saturation can vary between vascular beds. Consequently, a venous oxygen saturation measured in one compartment cannot be directly substituted for that from another site. This is particularly true, when comparing a local to global level of oxygen balance. Thus, SsO2/nSsO2 and SvO2 cannot be considered directly exchangeable. However, the measured SQV/SvO2-slopes between the individuals were similar. This suggests a uniform change in SQV for every alteration in SvO2 supporting the feasibility of continuous trend monitoring of SvO2 variations.

The pooled concordance from both provocations did not reach the desired threshold of 92%, although the result was within the 95% CI. The lower degree of concordance between nSsO2 and SvO2 during hypovolemia likely reduced the overall concordance rate. Thresholds for determining reliable trending have been proposed by studying different methods of cardiac output monitoring [19]. Whether those thresholds are the best for our proposed method is unknown. Despite that, we chose to adhere to the most described ones. Since the device is primarily intended as a trend monitor, and not to detect instant changes, we believe that a concordance level of 87% is sufficient for clinical purposes.

At current, the sternum probe has potential for continuous intra-individual monitoring of either increases or decreases of SvO2, possibly also with an indication of the magnitude change by analyzing the rate of change from the SQV/SvO2-slope. An individual single-point calibration of SvO2 from a blood sample is not regarded feasible. Future possibilities of creating a population level calibration curve would probably require modifying or removing the automatic calibration in its current form. It is also possible that, if the starting SQV could have been set freely, the marginal R2 might have increased substantially, since the different individuals shared a common slope. Due to different volumes of subcutaneous tissue, the between-subject variability may also have been influenced by variations in the amount of light reaching the sternal marrow. Increasing the penetration depth of light by moving the probe light source and photodetector even further apart, may help mitigate this effect.

Pulse oximeters sense the arterial oxygen saturation, which decreases when there is a reduction, or a mismatch in ventilation with pulmonary perfusion. Sternal PPG measurements could therefore have potential as a parallel complement to standard pulse oximetry. A decrease in both modalities would be consistent with hypoxic hypoxemia. However, if pulse oximetry remains stable while nSsO2 decreases, this may indicate increasing tissue oxygen extraction, for example due to hypovolemia.

This study failed to demonstrate a co-variation between nSsO2 and SvO2 for a 40% volume loss level. But such severe hypovolemia would have been clearly evident from basic circulatory monitoring. Early detection of disturbances in oxygen balance may be clinically relevant, which nSsO2 monitoring might provide.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that need to be addressed. The study utilized pigs as subjects, and the results cannot be directly translated for human use. Despite the similarities in physiology between man and pigs [34], it is unknown how the oxygen balance in the human sternum varies with hypovolemia. Also, a mean weight of 33.1 kg implies young individuals with an uncertain ossification of the sternum as well as a high metabolic turnover and high baseline oxygen consumption, as seen from the relatively low baseline SvO2-values (Table 1).

The sternal probe is not explicitly designed for porcine use. The differences between pigs and humans regarding sternal width and segmentation, introduced potential inaccuracies in measuring intramedullary blood flow, and consequently, nSsO2-estimation. Moreover, some subjects had a variation of their surface anatomy, resembling Pectus excavatum. Although the LED-light and photodetector geometry entails deep light penetration, the increased amount of subcutaneous tissue in those individuals might have affected the measurements and increased the between-subject variability. Individual anatomical differences, such as skin thickness, pigmentation, sternal variations and perfusion, has the greatest impact on SQV values from the automatic calibration of the sternal probe. However, since the sternal probe had to be restarted between the provocations, an intra-individual discrepancy in SQV-calibration cannot be ruled out.

Muscle relaxation was used in order to reduce motion artifacts, still possible motion artifacts from the handling of the animal cannot be ruled out. Since the probe analyzes light reflection, it is dependent upon a good skin contact. Despite thorough skin preparation, the possibility for probe-skin interface issues with shunting of light and measurement interferences cannot be excluded.

Statistical limitations likely reflect both the modest sample size, especially after excluding the participant unable to complete the protocol, and the predefined exclusion zone in the concordance analysis, which eliminated a large proportion of observations. This may have resulted in an inflation of the concordance rate but also yielding wider confidence intervals and diminished power to identify genuine changes.

The current study has only evaluated the relationship between nSsO2 and SvO2 during hypoxemia and hypovolemia. However, other conditions affecting SvO2 exist, such as early sepsis, fever, pain/stress, and thyroid hormones [35]. How these situations affect SsO2 has not yet been clarified. Also, general anesthesia and neuromuscular blockade may affect SvO2 by altering oxygen consumption and cardiac output. How the current results of calibrating SQV to SvO2 can be translated to the awake individual, has not yet been investigated.

Conclusions

This study has demonstrated the feasibility of calibrating non-invasive sternal PPG measurements against SvO2, with the intent to continuously monitor changes in global oxygen utilization.

Alterations in SvO2 during hypoxemia or mild/moderate hypovolemia may be tracked by sternal PPG-measurements, with high temporal resolution. However, further technical development of the current prototype probe is necessary to allow for inter-individual comparisons of nSsO2.
