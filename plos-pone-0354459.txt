English-Persian cross-language plagiarism detection: Multilingual deep learning approach

Sara Ansar, Marzieh Zarinbal, Azadeh Mohebi

ABSTRACT
Cross-lingual plagiarism detection (CLPD) remains a critical challenge in Natural Language Processing, particularly for low-resource language pairs where translation and paraphrasing obscure source materials. This study introduces a robust framework for Persian-English CLPD by integrating state-of-the-art sentence representations with optimized machine learning classifiers. We utilized the BAAI/bge-m3 model to generate high-dimensional embeddings, employing cosine similarity to measure contrastive linguistic distances. To evaluate classification performance, we compared various architectures, including XGBoost, LSTM, and Logistic Regression (LR), across three categories: Exact, Semi-Exact, and Different. While deep learning and ensemble methods were initially considered, experimental results on an expanded dataset demonstrated that a fine-tuned LR model provided superior stability and generalization. The optimized LR approach significantly increased classification accuracy from 89% to 96% (p < 0.05). These findings suggest that high-quality embeddings can enhance detection performance in low-resource contexts without the computational overhead of complex architectures. To ensure transparency and reproducibility, our dataset and source code are available on GitHub.

Introduction The rapid advancement of machine translation technologies and multilingual large language models (LLMs) has significantly increased the risk of cross-lingual plagiarism, where translated text is presented as original without proper attribution [ 1 ]. Compared with monolingual plagiarism detection, cross-lingual detection is more challenging because it requires identifying semantic equivalence across languages with different lexical, syntactic, and morphological structures. As a result, it has become an important area of research for maintaining academic integrity in multilingual environments. Over the past decade, various approaches have been proposed to address this problem. Early methods relied on machine translation (MT) to convert texts into a single language, followed by traditional monolingual plagiarism detection. More recent studies have focused on cross-lingual alignment techniques, multilingual embeddings, and transformer-based models, which improve semantic matching across languages. However, these methods still depend heavily on high-quality resources and benchmark datasets, and their performance varies across language pairs. Despite these advances, important challenges remain, especially for low-resource languages such as Persian. The lack of large benchmark datasets and the presence of complex morphological and syntactic structures make reliable detection difficult. In addition, AI-assisted translation tools now generate highly fluent texts that are harder to distinguish from original writing, further increasing the difficulty of detection. To address these challenges, this study proposes a deep learning framework for Persian–English cross-lingual plagiarism detection. The framework combines multilingual sentence embeddings with supervised classifiers to improve semantic discrimination across plagiarism levels. An AI-assisted benchmark dataset is constructed to reflect modern translation characteristics and varying degrees of similarity. The proposed method is evaluated through comprehensive experiments and statistical analysis. Literature review This section reviews previous studies in detecting plagiarism and categorizes them to identify research gaps. Machine translation-based analysis and cross-lingual alignment-based similarity analysis (CL-ASA) is the most widely used methods in this field. Machine translation-based analysis The most straightforward approach to cross-lingual plagiarism detection involves leveraging Machine Translation (MT) to reduce the problem to a monolingual one. While the accessibility of translation tools makes this method popular, its reliability is inherently tied to the performance of the underlying MT system. Early implementations, such as the work by Kasprzak and Brandejs [ 2 ] and Pataki [ 3 , 4 ], demonstrated that while large-scale detection is feasible, issues like poor translation quality in complex academic texts and reliance on public APIs often limit scalability and accuracy. The literature also highlights a recurring struggle with computational costs and language-specific nuances. For instance, while Kent and Salim [ 5 ] utilized Google’s infrastructure for English-Arabic detection, their reliance on k-gram matching proved computationally expensive. To mitigate these efficiency and accuracy gaps, researchers have integrated more sophisticated layers; Alzahrani and Aljuaid [ 6 ] and Safi-Esfahani et al. [ 7 ] successfully employed deep learning and semantic analysis to filter out false positives particularly in Persian and Arabic contexts. However, as noted by Ferrero et al. [ 8 ] and Pinto et al. [ 9 ], MT-based models still face a “performance ceiling” when dealing with low-resource languages or complex syntactic structures [ 10 , 11 ], where translation errors directly degrade the recall of the entire detection system. Cross-lingual alignment-based similarity analysis Beyond translation-based methods, CL-ASA offers a more direct route by aligning equivalent sentences or phrases across different languages. This technique, pioneered by Barrón-Cedeño et al. [ 12 ], minimizes the dependency on machine translation quality by focusing on structural and feature-based alignments. To tackle the nuances of heavily paraphrased content, Franco-Salvador et al. [ 13 ] later integrated knowledge graphs into this framework, significantly sharpening the model's semantic depth. Practical applications, such as those explored by Gupta et al. [ 10 ], confirm that this approach is particularly adept at spotting translated plagiarism in diverse pairs like Hindi and English. Ultimately, as noted by Potthast et al. [ 14 ], the true strength of CL-ASA lies in its capacity to detect long-range reuse within dense academic texts a task where traditional word-for-word methods often fail. Advanced approaches To overcome the inherent limitations of machine translation (MT) and capture complex linguistic variations, recent research has shifted toward deep learning (DL), word embeddings, and sophisticated semantic analyses. The primary objective is to enhance detection accuracy while optimizing processing time for languages with significant structural disparities. Persian and multilingual contexts In the context of Persian plagiarism detection, Gharavi et al. [ 15 , 16 ] and Pakniat and Mohebi [ 1 ] have demonstrated that converting text into numerical vectors and employing DL frameworks can effectively identify word-order changes and synonym substitutions, achieving accuracies up to 95.9%. These methods often utilize sentence-level comparisons and cosine similarity, although some trade-offs exist regarding the retention of word-order information. To further refine this, Roostaee et al. [ 17 ] introduced a two-level alignment approach integrating syntactic and semantic features via graph-based word representations, ensuring stable performance across diverse datasets. Arabic and cross-lingual benchmarks Arabic-English plagiarism detection has also seen significant advancement. Ezzikouri et al. [ 18 ] and Alotaibi et al. [ 19 ] evaluated various methods, including cross-lingual character n-grams (CL-CNG) and multilingual encoders, finding that while MT-based analysis remains accurate, embedding-based techniques provide a more efficient alternative for distant language pairs. Furthermore, the development of benchmark datasets, such as the JUPlag system [ 20 ], and the Urdu-English dataset by Haneef et al. [ 21 ], has provided essential resources for training and testing these sophisticated models. Hybrid and scalable systems Hybrid systems that combine statistical methods with DL have proven particularly effective for large-scale detection. Bakhteev et al. [ 22 ] and Gang et al. [ 23 ] proposed multi-stage processes identifying candidates first, then analyzing fragments, while utilizing knowledge distillation to improve efficiency. Similarly, Corezola et al. [ 24 ] and Al-Jibory et al. [ 25 ] designed comprehensive systems incorporating sliding-window techniques and hybrid feature extraction (textual and non-textual) to generate detailed similarity reports. Despite these technical strides, systematic reviews by Botto-Tobar et al. [ 26 ] and Tlitova et al. [ 27 ] highlight a persistent lack of specialized tools and standardized methodologies across many studies. These researchers emphasize the need for more robust detection methods as global academic publishing continues to expand across diverse language pairs (e.g., Russian-English), necessitating systems that can navigate complex ethical concerns and varied representation types Research gap Table 1 summarizes prior studies. Materials & methods This study follows a methodological framework that outlines the key steps undertaken in the research process. The dataset consists of the abstracts of Persian scientific documents along with their English translations, and to enhance the diversity of the dataset, two versions, semi‑exact and different, were generated by applying semantic and structural transformations to the original abstracts. For text representation and similarity measurement, the BAAI/BGE‑M3 model is utilized to convert abstracts into vector representations, after which the cosine similarity between Persian and English abstracts is calculated to assess their resemblance. Based on the obtained similarity scores, the abstracts are labeled into predefined categories, and the dataset is subsequently divided into training and testing subsets. In the classification phase, various classification algorithms are applied and evaluated using accuracy, recall, and F1‑score to determine their effectiveness. Finally, the best‑performing models undergo fine‑tuning and further evaluation to enhance their performance. By following this methodology, we ensure a robust approach to detecting cross‑lingual plagiarism between Persian and English texts. Dataset The empirical foundation of this study comprises a curated dataset of 1,000 academic abstracts sourced from the Iranian Research Institute for Information Science and Technology (IranDoc). To facilitate the investigation of cross-lingual plagiarism detection between Persian and English, the original Persian abstracts underwent a systematic translation and modification pipeline. While traditional methodologies have frequently utilized conventional machine translation (MT) engines, this research intentionally employed Microsoft Copilot for the translation phase. This selection was motivated by two primary factors: first, the advanced capability of large language models (LLMs) to preserve nuanced semantic structures in technical discourse [ 29 ]; and second, the necessity for the detection model to remain robust against modern, AI-mediated translation artifacts that researchers may encounter in contemporary academic environments. First, synonym substitution was conducted by replacing specific words with their appropriate synonyms utilizing the NLTK library and the WordNet database [ 30 ]. Second, sentence reordering was applied to alter sentence structures while carefully preserving the overall contextual meaning. Two human annotators independently labeled all paraphrased texts, and manual supervision ensured high agreement. Finally, basic preprocessing steps, including tokenization, lowercasing, and punctuation removal, were applied to all texts to standardize the dataset before embedding generation. Embedding method In this step, the textual dataset is converted into numerical vector representations. These vectors provide a numerical representation of sentences, enabling models to capture the semantic and syntactic features of the text. Unlike word-level representations, a sentence-level approach allows for a more precise evaluation of meaning and structural variations. This is particularly important in cross-lingual plagiarism detection, where minor changes in wording can significantly alter the interpretation of a sentence. By focusing on entire sentences rather than individual words, this method enhances the accuracy of similarity detection and ensures a more reliable assessment of content transformation. This method facilitates the extraction of similarities between Persian and English texts through vector-based computations. Common sentence representation techniques include Word2Vec, GloVe, FastText, and BERT [ 32 ]. In this study, we used two embedding models, BERT and BGE-M3-Embeddings, to generate sentence vectors, enabling numerical representation of sentences for similarity measurement. BERT is widely recognized for its deep contextual understanding, while BGE-M3-Embeddings is specifically designed for tasks involving cross-lingual similarity. By leveraging both models, we aimed to obtain rich vector representations that capture semantic and syntactic variations between Persian and English abstracts. The Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) model, introduced by Vaswani et al. [ 32 ], is a widely used method for generating contextualized embeddings. Built on the transformer architecture, BERT employs multi-head self-attention mechanisms to process input sequences bidirectionally, ensuring a deeper understanding of linguistic structures. The input text undergoes tokenization using a specialized word piece tokenizer, which splits words into sub-word units. These tokens are then mapped to high-dimensional vector representations through multiple transformer layers. The final output provides contextual embeddings that enhance natural language processing tasks, particularly in cross-lingual and semantic understanding applications. The M3-Embedding model was introduced by Chen et al. [ 33 ] in 2024, featuring multilingual, multi-functional, and multi-granular capabilities. It supports semantic retrieval in over 100 languages and performs dense, multi-vector, and sparse retrieval tasks. The model handles input sequences from short sentences to documents up to 8192 tokens. The BGE-M3 model is built on the transformer architecture. Input tokens pass through layers of self-attention and feed-forward networks, mapping tokens to high-dimensional spaces. Final representations are aggregated into 1024-dimensional dense vectors. This embedding model optimizes multilingual text representation and maximizes GPU utilization, crucial for high-performance retrieval tasks [ 33 ]. To determine the most suitable embedding model for this study, we employed Cosine similarity to analyze the semantic alignment of Persian-English text pairs. Unlike distance-based metrics such as Euclidean distance, Cosine similarity measures the angle between vectors, making it well-suited for high-dimensional text representations. By leveraging this method, the semantic similarity between two texts can be quantified based on the angle between their corresponding vectors (a smaller angle (closer to 0 degrees) indicates a higher degree of similarity [ 34 ]. Contrastive Distance in the context of Large Language Models (LLMs) and representation learning is a metric used to evaluate the proximity of semantic embeddings within a high-dimensional vector space. It operates on the principle of distance-based discrimination, where the model is tasked with minimizing the distance between ‘positive’ pairs (e.g., a Persian abstract and its accurate English translation) while simultaneously maximizing the gap for ‘negative’ or dissimilar pairs (e.g., unrelated or heavily manipulated texts) [35]. Experimental setup and classification framework In the first stage of the experiments, the classification models were evaluated on a dataset containing 500 samples, with three predefined categories: Exact, Semi-Exact, and Different. The objective was to identify the most effective classifier for cross-lingual plagiarism detection. To this end, seven widely used machine learning and deep learning models, including Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), Feedforward Neural Network (FNN), Support Vector Machine (SVM), XGBoost, Recurrent Neural Network (RNN), Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), and Logistic Regression (LR), were trained and evaluated. In this study, performance metrics are interpreted based on standard NLP benchmarks, where scores above 90% are considered strong, 80–90% as good, and 70–80% as moderate In the second stage, the experimental analysis was extended by increasing the dataset size from 500 to 1,000 samples in order to evaluate the impact of data scale on classification performance. Using the same three predefined categories (Exact, Semi-Exact, and Different), five representative classifiers were reassessed, including Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), Support Vector Machine (SVM), XGBoost, Recurrent Neural Network (RNN), and Logistic Regression (LR). The models were evaluated using precision, recall, and F1-score to provide a comprehensive comparison. In the context of academic integrity, minimizing “False Negatives” (missed plagiarism) is paramount, making Recall a prioritized metric. To optimize detection across varying degrees of similarity, the framework establishes distinct coverage boundaries using specialized classifiers. For lower-bound coverage targeting exact categories, XGBoost was selected due to its superior ability to capture direct translations, achieving the highest recall of 0.84 and ensuring that verbatim theft is identified with high sensitivity. Conversely, for upper-bound coverage across different categories, Logistic Regression was utilized because of its peak performance in the most complex classes, yielding a maximum recall of 0.91 and providing a robust defense against sophisticated, multi-source plagiarism. By focusing on these two complementary frameworks, one based on ensemble gradient boosting and the other on statistical linear weighting the study ensures a balanced and transparent detection system. This approach not only optimizes accuracy across all levels of plagiarism but also maintains computational efficiency, making it the most viable solution for the finalized detection framework. Fine tune and statistical validation strategies To enhance model sensitivity to subtle cross-lingual semantic variations and improve class separation between highly similar plagiarism categories, a systematic hyperparameter fine-tuning phase was executed. For the baseline dataset configuration consisting of 500 samples, XGBoost and LSTM were selected for optimization due to their complementary structural properties. The fine-tuning configuration for the XGBoost model focused on regularization adjustments, settling on a subsample rate of 0.4 and a colsample_bytree ratio of 0.6 to minimize potential over-parameterization. For the baseline LSTM model, structural complexity was increased by implementing a three-layer architecture with 128 hidden units per layer to capture deeper semantic dependencies. To ensure training stability and prevent overfitting at this scale, a dropout rate of 0.5 was applied along with gradient clipping, a refined learning rate of 0.0005, and an early stopping criterion. For the scaled-up experiments utilizing 1,000 samples, XGBoost and Logistic Regression (LR) were chosen for deeper refinement. Automated hyperparameter tuning for the scaled-up XGBoost architecture was executed via the Optuna framework to systematically search the algorithmic configuration space. For the Logistic Regression classifier, optimization focused on refining the regularization parameters to better map decision boundaries within the dense, high-dimensional embedding spaces. To ensure that the observed performance variations between the baseline configurations and the fine-tuned models were not the result of stochastic variation or random chance, a McNemar’s test for statistical significance was performed. This non-parametric statistical test is specifically suited for comparing the paired performance of two classifiers evaluated on the same empirical dataset by analyzing the discordance between their respective correct and incorrect predictions. Discussion From an algorithmic perspective, the superior performance of Logistic Regression (LR) over non-linear models like XGBoost and LSTM suggests that the semantic embedding space generated by BGE-M3 may exhibit a relatively high degree of linear separability for this task. The BGE-M3 model projects text into a dense, 1024-dimensional semantic space, where semantically similar documents are positioned closer together. Within this embedding space, LR appears to construct an effective decision boundary that generalizes well while maintaining low model complexity. In contrast, XGBoost may derive less benefit from recursive feature partitioning in dense continuous embedding spaces, leading to limited performance gains over simpler linear classifiers. Similarly, because BGE-M3 compresses contextual and sequential information into a single fixed-length semantic representation, the sequential modeling capability of LSTM may provide limited additional discriminative information. These findings suggest that, for low-resource cross-lingual plagiarism detection, the quality of semantic representations may play a more important role than increasing downstream classifier complexity. Linguistically, these findings suggest that the embedding model effectively mitigates syntactic disparities between Persian (Subject–Object–Verb) and English (Subject–Verb–Object). While conventional classifiers often struggle to bridge these cross-lingual differences with limited training data, BGE-M3 appears to map semantically equivalent concepts into a shared representation space during pre-training, regardless of surface-level linguistic structure. As a result, downstream classification becomes largely dependent on semantic proximity within the embedding space, enabling LR to effectively discriminate between plagiarism categories without relying on complex non-linear feature interactions. Furthermore, expanding the dataset from 500 to 1,000 samples improved model stability and reduced ambiguity between the Exact and Semi-Exact plagiarism classes. However, the persistent confusion between these categories underscores the ongoing technical challenge of distinguishing literal translations from highly faithful paraphrases. These findings are consistent with previous studies highlighting the advantages of semantic embedding models over conventional translation-then-monolingual pipelines. Moreover, the results suggest that carefully optimized conventional machine learning classifiers can achieve performance comparable to more complex deep learning architectures when supported by high-quality multilingual semantic representations, while offering lower computational complexity and greater interpretability. Conclusions This study investigated cross-lingual plagiarism detection in Persian–English academic abstracts, specifically addressing the challenges posed by machine translation and complex paraphrasing. By leveraging the BGE-M3 embedding model and expanding the dataset to 1,000 samples, the empirical results demonstrated that downstream classifier selection becomes increasingly important as dataset size increases. A key finding of this research is the superior generalization performance of the optimized Logistic Regression (LR) model on the expanded dataset. While XGBoost and LSTM showed strong performance on the initial 500-sample dataset, the optimized LR model achieved the best overall performance on the larger dataset. Ultimately, the proposed framework provides a computationally efficient and scalable solution for cross-lingual plagiarism detection in academic publishing systems. However, the consistent difficulty in identifying the Exact plagiarism category across all models, attributed to the extreme semantic proximity between verbatim and near-verbatim translations, remains a key technical challenge that warrants further methodological investigation. To further enhance the effectiveness and reliability of cross-lingual plagiarism detection, future research should explore the integration of syntactic information, Named Entity Recognition (NER), and authorship-related metadata to improve plagiarism detection performance. Developing Explainable AI (XAI) techniques could provide transparent justifications for plagiarism decisions, increase user trust, and reduce the impact of false-positive detections. From a practical perspective, applying model pruning and quantization may improve computational efficiency and facilitate real-time, large-scale deployment in academic publishing workflows. In addition, incorporating continual learning mechanisms would enable plagiarism detection systems to adapt to evolving language patterns and emerging AI-assisted plagiarism strategies Finally, future studies should investigate class imbalance mitigation techniques, including oversampling and class-weighted loss functions, to improve model reliability in real-world environments where genuine documents substantially outnumber plagiarized ones. Expanding the proposed framework to larger and more diverse datasets spanning multiple scientific disciplines and additional cross-lingual settings, particularly low-resource language pairs, would further improve the generalizability, robustness, and practical applicability of the proposed methodology.
