 Abstract
 The volume of space between Earth’s geosynchronous orbit out to the Moon’s sphere
 of influence, including the lunar Lagrange points, is crucial for the successful planning
 and execution of space missions, but not fully understood dynamically. This region
 is a part of cislunar space. Trajectories through cislunar space are influenced by the
 gravitational forces of the Sun, Earth, Moon, and other Solar System planets leading
 to typically unpredictable and chaotic trajectory behavior. It is therefore difficult
 to predict the stability of an trajectory through cislunar space from a set of initial
 conditions or orbital elements. We simulate one million cislunar orbits to train a self-
 organizing map (SOM) to cluster the trajectories and orbits into families based on how
 long they remain stable within the cislunar space. Using the trained SOM, we are
 able to predict the stable lifetime of a trajectory through cislunar space from a set of
 initial orbital elements to within an accuracy of 10% for 8% of simulated trajectories
 and within 50% for 43% of the simulated trajectories. Clustering in the SOM suggests
 that a variety of trajectory morphologies have similar lifetimes. Once trained, the
 SOM can predict the stable lifetime of a given cislunar trajectory within milliseconds.
 The methods developed in this work enable the rapid identification of stable cislunar
 orbits and trajectories that could be used for future space exploration. Moreover, the
 developed SOM method can generate orbital and trajectory lifetime estimates from
 minimal observational data, such as a single two line element, making it useful for
 early warning systems and large-scale sensor network operations.

 Keywords: Cislunar, Machine Learning, Self-Organizing Maps

1 Introduction
The volume between Earth’s geosynchronous orbit and beyond the Moon, including the
lunar Lagrange points is becoming increasingly important due to the growing interest in
lunar exploration, satellite deployment, and space traffic management [2]. This is driving
advances in space domain awareness (SDA) techniques to ensure the safety and efficiency
of cislunar operations [3]. Commercial cislunar interests include plans for combusting the

hydrogen encased within the lunar surface with oxygen for efficient fuel production for space
exploration [17]. NASA’s Lunar Gateway [12] plans to create a communication network
orbiting the Moon to support the Artemis mission [1]. Alongside U.S. interests in cislunar
space, the Outer Space Treaty [16] ensures global participation in outer space exploration,
including the exploration of cislunar space. Therefore, the number of satellites in cislunar
space is likely to increase significantly over the coming decade.
 Critical to planning any cislunar activity or mission is an understanding of unaided
cislunar orbits and trajectories. Unaided orbits in cislunar space are difficult to characterize
due to gravitational forces from the Sun, Moon, and planets all playing significant roles in
this regime. This complicated force landscape leads to chaotic and typically unpredictable
orbital dynamics [4]. This makes space traffic management in cislunar space challenging. In
particular, trying to accurately predict a satellite’s unaided end-of-life trajectory in cislunar
space is difficult [13]. Understanding and mapping unaided trajectories through cislunar
space will lead to better end-of-life outcomes for satellites [6].
 The trajectory lifetime of a particular satellite in cislunar space can be estimated by
propagation of the trajectory into the future based on measurements of the satellite’s posi-
tion. While this is computationally feasible to compute for a small number of satellites or
trajectories (approximately 3 central-processing unit-hours to accurately propagate a trajec-
tory over 6 years in cislunar space) it will not scale larger numbers of satellites that will be
detected in modern data streams from wide-field astronomical surveys such as the Vera C.
Rubin Observatory Legacy Survey of Space and Time [10]. LSST is going to detect satellites
in at least 40,000 of its images over its 10-year survey [9]. With at least one satellite expect
to be detected in each LSST images, a technique that can rapidly characterize objects in
cislunar space based off of single or a few observations to determine end-of-life outcomes and
stability is necessary.
 Although trajectories through n-body gravitational spaces are typically chaotic and un-
predictable, with high performance compute systems (HPC) we can simulate many of them
through and train machine-learning (ML) algorithms to predict trends in their behavior [30].
In recent years ML has been applied to create a constellation of sensors in cislunar space
to detect and track objects [7] as well as Moon-based sensors to track families of orbits,
which can inform mission planning and collision avoidance strategies [11]. However, using
ML algorithms to determine orbit lifetime from data-sparse trajectories, a critical issue for
SDA applications, has not been well studied.
 This paper investigates the application of an unsupervised ML algorithm called a self-
organizing maps [14] in determining the lifetime of orbits within cislunar space from an initial
measurement of its orbital trajectory. Recent studies have demonstrated the potential of
SOMs to effectively cluster high-dimensional astronomical data. For example, SOMs have
been sued to categorize estimate galaxy parameters [26], estimate photometric redshifts [20],
cluster X-ray sources [18], and for sorting survey images based on source morphology [28]
revealing underlying patterns that may not be immediately apparent through conventional
methods relying on human intervention [5]. In this paper, we train a SOM on a simulated
data set of one million cislunar trajectories to cluster trajectories and predict their stable
lifetime in cislunar space given an initial orbital trajectory. By using SOMs, we improve our
understanding of possible stable trajectories in cislunar space in this critical paving the way
for more efficient early warning systems and improved space traffic management.

2 Methods
2.1 Simulating Orbits
We simulated one million trajectories over six years in cislunar space using the Space Situa-
tional Awareness for Python package [21]. The force model used in the simulation includes
an Earth [23], Lunar [19], and Solar gravity model. The force model also includes solar
radiation pressure with coefficient of 1.3 [22] assumed to act over a fiducial satellite area of
0.25m2 . Trajectory propagation was performed using a 7/8 Runge–Kutta integrator using a
dynamic time step with a maximum of 10 seconds and a converge tolerance of 10−6 which
corresponds to a target positional propagation accuracy of ∼ 102 over ∼day timescales. Ini-
tial Keplerian orbital elements were uniformly sampled from the subsets listed in Table 1,
ensuring thorough coverage of the cislunar phase space.
 Each orbit in the dataset was assigned a lifetime, defined as the duration it remains within
the volume of cislunar space. To calculate when a trajectory left cislunar space, orbits were
evaluated at hourly intervals. We define an trajectory to have left cislunar space if any
of the following criteria were met: the trajectory passed within the geosynchronous radius
(4.216 × 105 km) of Earth or beyond two lunar distances (LD) from Earth (7.688 × 106
km), or if the trajectory intersected with the Moon. Lunar intersection was calculated based
on whether the trajectory’s current position and velocity would allow it to pass within two
lunar radii within the next time step of the simulation. This results in intersection distances
ranging from two to five lunar radii. This approach avoids propagating the trajectory down
to the lunar surface, significantly reducing computational requirements while ensuring an
accurate prediction of Lunar intersection. The two LD threshold is close to the limit of the
sphere of influence of the Earth-Moon system; beyond this, other celestial bodies dominate
orbital dynamics and the orbital period increases to too long of a timescale for a six year
propagation. Two LD also encloses the important volume for space mission planning. This
procedure produced 902,402 orbits with lifetimes between 0 and 6 years lifetimes as our
sample (see Figure 1).

2.2 Self-Organizing Maps
Self-organizing maps [14] can visualize high-dimensional data and identify clusters without
prior labeling. A SOM is an unsupervised ML technique that combines dimensionality
reduction and clustering into a resulting 2-D map. The SOM organizes input data using
synaptic connections (or cell weights) onto a lattice of postsynaptic neurons (or cells). The
algorithm consists of three stages: competition, collaboration, and adjustment [15].
 In the competition phase, each neuron competes to represent the input data. The winning
neuron, referred to as the best matching unit (BMU), is the neuron that is most similar to
the input data by its randomly initialized weights. It has the shortest euclidean distance to
the input data in feature space. Here i is the winning neuron, x is the input data, j is a
neuron, and wj is the associated weight of the neuron:

 The side length is given by taking the square root of m for a square map, which was
39 units for our dataset. Choosing a side length larger than this can unintentionally bias
the SOM results, as it has more choices for less data. To judge the quality of the output
map, there are two metrics available: the topographic error and the quantization error [27].
The topographic error is a measure of how many data points have first and second BMUs
in different neighborhoods. The quantization error measures the average difference between
the weights of the BMU and the input data. Minimizing both of these errors yields a SOM
that is most representative of the input data in feature space.
 We use the MiniSOM Python implementation [29]. This SOM model is based on NumPy
[8]. The best SOM is chosen through a compromise of good sampling and high resolution,
leveraging the topographic and quantization errors. We choose lifetime to be the label for
the SOM. We adapted MiniSOM’s accuracy to be a median absolute deviation between the
test data’s lifetime and the median lifetime of the SOM node to which the test data was
assigned. We tested the SOM on a 20% subset of our data, 180,480 orbits. Each test orbit’s
lifetime recovery was computed in 2.72 milliseconds. We took the absolute deviation between
the lifetime (Ltest ) label of each orbit with the median value of the cell (Lcell ) it was assigned
to. This is the median absolute deviation (MAD),

a feature space that can be inputted into the SOM. The initial two line element is used as the

normalize the value of each element across the sample of simulated trajectories to mitigate the

3 Results
3.1 SOM Construction
The best performing SOM, with a quantization error of 0.321 and a topographic error of
0.163, was made using MinMax in its pre-processing with σ0 = 10, µ0 = 5, and n = 106 .
This SOM is visualized in Figure 2. Note that the color map in this plot refers to the lifetime
of the orbit, measured in years. The lighter the cell, the longer the orbit lasted, and vice
versa for the darker cells. Empty SOM cells are colored black.
 The density plots in Figure 2 show how data clusters beyond the labels associated with
the input. The SOM shows rounded valleys of cells across the map containing fewer than
1,000 orbits, as well as single-cell peaks around the periphery of the map containing more
than 5,000 orbits. These valleys strongly correlated with regions of high average lifetime,
suggesting that the SOM effectively clustered the orbits as into distinct regions based of
patterns within the higher-dimensional dataset.
 The lifetime label variance within cells in the SOM is shown in Figure 2. High variances
indicate that trajectories with different lifetimes are sorted into the same cell, whereas a low
variance indicates that trajectories with similar lifetimes are grouped together. However,
lifetime is not the only parameter that determines whether trajectories are physically similar,
so high variance values within the SOM do not necessarily suggest ineffective clustering. Only
5.4% of the trajectories have a lifetime exceeding 4 years. Therefore, the high variance in cells
containing high-lifetime orbits will be greater than in the rest of the data could be explained
given their higher likelihood of being paired with an orbit with a much lower lifetime.

3.2 Lifetime Recovery
The relationships between test orbit lifetime and both M AD and M ADfrac are visualized in
Figure 3, showing a density plot showing a count of orbits in a region, with the deviation
value on the x-axis and the raw lifetime value on the y-axis.
 There was a prominence at 1 for fractional lifetime recovery in Figure 3, suggesting that
many orbits are recovered within 100% of their lifetime value. For low lifetime values below
2 years, predominantly for those under 1 year, there was a positive skew to the fractional

absolute deviations values, suggesting that the lifetimes of these orbits are systematically
overestimated. Orbits that survive less time in cislunar space are typically chaotic, and their
initial TLE is not always deterministic of this chaos, so this overestimation was reflective
of this nature of cislunar space. Further study on fractional median absolute deviation in
Figure 4 indicated that that majority of the 180,480 test orbits are recovered within 100%
of their lifetime, amounting to 135,673.
 Indeed, 78,467 orbit’s lifetimes were recovered within 50%, and 15,905 were recovered
within 10%. This indicates that less than 25% of the orbit’s lifetimes were overestimated.
The tail of the distribution represents orbits with lifetimes below 2 years, with the majority
below 1 year. Beyond the test dataset as a whole, recovery as a function of lifetime was
of interest. Monthly lifetime bins between 1 and 72 were created to test the SOM and
recoup the associated lifetime recovery with a 68% confidence interval, shown in Figure 5.
As lifetime increased, the predicted lifetime value plateaued at 1 year and the confidence
interval widened.

4 Limitations of SOMs
We constructed six additional SOMs from evenly sampled data, ensuring that all lifetimes
in month increments are equally represented. Each SOM was trained on a year window of
data, then tested on data of all lifetimes. Figure 6 showcases the lifetime predictions for
monthly windows of data for each pre-trained SOM.
 The SOM accurately recognized the test data and predicted lifetimes within the training
set window, recovering lifetimes of the test orbits within 2.385 months on average. A wave-
like pattern was observed across the test dataset within the lifetime bin of the pre-trained
SOM. For lifetimes outside the training window, there was no significant overestimation or
underestimation which indicates that the SOM was ineffective at classifying data beyond this
range. Figure 7 reiterates Figure 6, but this time through the median absolute deviation be-
tween the predicted lifetime and the true test orbit lifetime. The wave-like pattern observed
in Figure 6 aligns with the minimum of each curve in Figure 7. Lifetimes near the central
value of each training set exhibit the minimal error in predicted lifetimes. The downward
curve at the beginning of each training window, followed by an upward trend after the center
point, indicates that the SOM is predicting lifetimes in the correct “directions.” This means
that, for test data included in the training set, the SOM is statistically identifying the orbits
correctly in a meaningful way.
 The lowest MAD values for the test data corresponded with the window of data that the
SOM was trained on. As a result of this study, we conclude that the SOMs are not useful
outside their training sets.

5 Conclusion
In this study, we aimed to classify unaided orbits in cislunar space using SOMs based on their
initial TLE. This research demonstrated the potential of SOMs to improve our understanding
of orbital dynamics in the cislunar region, which is becoming increasingly important for future
space exploration and satellite operations.

 7

 The results of our analysis revealed several key findings.
 1. The SOM provided a nearly instant guess toward whether an orbit would be stable for
 months or years for 75% of the orbits based on their initial Keplerian orbital elements.
 2. The SOM produced lifetime estimations in an average of 2.72 milliseconds, leaving the
 door open for applications to real-time observing pipelines.
 3. The SOM included multi-modal lifetime distributions, with additional texture revealed
 through the density plot.
 4. The algorithm discerned patterns within the data that were independent of lifetime.
 5. The lifetime-restricted SOMs were ineffective when applied to data outside their train-
 ing window, highlighting the limitations of SOMs in predicting anomalous test data.
 By characterizing cislunar orbits from single-event data, we inform early warning systems
and satellite deployment strategies, ultimately contributing to more efficient use of resources
in space exploration. While the SOM was not highly specific in its results regarding lifetime,
it demonstrated potential for identifying stable regions and families of orbits. The variability
observed in results based on different sampling methods highlights the need for caution when
generalizing findings.
 Looking ahead, there are several promising directions for future research. Exploring alter-
native ML techniques could enable real-time capability and improve classification accuracy.
Longitudinal studies assessing the stability of classifications over time could further validate
our findings and their applicability. Of particular interest is the diffusion rate of cislunar
families over time.
