 ABSTRACT

Artificial intelligence (AI), and more specifically large language models (LLMs) have the potential for
use in penetration testing (“pentesting”) against devices, networks, and computer systems in information
technology (IT). We explore the possibility of extending pentesting from IT systems to operational
technology (OT) systems, which are more obscure than IT systems in their protocols and design. A
challenge therefore exists when applying pretrained LLMs to OT systems as corpora are likely to
underrepresent OT systems in comparison to other more prevalent systems. We evaluate augmentations of
LLMs with various methods, especially retrieval augmented generation (RAG), to improve performance
of the LLMs in the OT domain. In addition to pentesting, some of the testing of these OT devices may
include commissioning to ensure that the newly installed devices work correctly. Our framework may
also be applied in such cases.

 1. INTRODUCTION

Major cyberattacks, which have already occurred against critical infrastructure, can greatly hamper
society’s ability to function, endanger public safety, and cause costly damage. These attacks have
infiltrated and affected supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA) systems, networks, and
computerized assets, such as programmable logic controllers (PLCs) and other intelligent electronic
devices (IEDs). Examples of attacks include the BlackEnergy3 malware power utilities attack [1],
ransomware infecting the computers of the Colonial Pipeline [2] and others [3]. These attacks had a
substantial effect and were complex in their software and operation, indicating that the creators of the
attacks performed extensive research and development to tailor the attacks to their specific targets.
 To ensure the security of all these systems, engineers and cyber security professionals perform
pentesting, an approach that is offensive in nature and defensive in its ultimate purpose. These
professionals seek to find vulnerabilities or weaknesses in the underlying system so that they can patch or
defend them with various security controls. Performing these pentests manually is often tedious and may
fail to be comprehensive as the process is highly human dependent. Therefore, methods of automation
and artificial intelligence are of interest.
 Recent breakthroughs that have emerged in Artificial Intelligence (AI) have significant potential
for both benefits and malfeasance. This is especially true for cybersecurity, and the possibility exists for
even more sophisticated cyberattacks, as AI agents assist in preparation and execution of these attacks,
ranging from fashioning vulnerability exploits and generating realistic phishing attacks to empowering
advanced persistent threats with natural language reasoning to readily adapt to changing conditions [43],
[44]. The AI, and specifically an LLM-based agent, could improvise in such scenarios. We will
investigate how to prepare for these potential attacks and their implications so that we can defend critical
infrastructure against them.
 We have created a framework which leverages LLMs for pentesting critical infrastructure, dubbed
the Unconventional Cyber LLM Executor, or UnCLE. In terms of contributions, with this new capability,
we believe that we can more readily pentest both information technology (IT) and operational technology
(OT) systems. To do so, the framework uses Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG) and iteratively
interacts with target devices. Furthermore, as a contribution, this framework may also be used in the
commissioning of OT devices by testing some of their core functions.
 The remaining sections of this report are as follows: Section 2 discusses the concept of agents
empowered by AI and the literature associated with them. Section 3 covers background, including
methods for augmenting LLMs and pentesting OT systems. Section 4 discusses the design of the AI-
based pentesting framework for OT systems. Section 5 discusses the environment in which the agents of
the framework operates. In this work, the environment is a power systems testbed along with its
associated industrial control system (ICS) networks and devices. Section 6 describes the experiments

 2. LITERATURE REVIEW

The concept of autonomous agents has been an important focus of research in AI for decades but defining
them can be challenging. Modern definitions of agents, particularly those related to large language models
(LLMs), have become more nuanced. This also has led to the same issue of a consensus LLM agent
definition being elusive. For example, many applications that combine LLMs with tool usage or prompt
an LLM in a loop have been considered agents, as noted by open-source developer Simon Willison [19].
These definitions, however, often fail to capture the element of autonomy because they do not necessarily
reflect the ability to sense and act on the environment. Anthropic’s “Building Effective Agents” [20].
provides a clear definition of how agents “dynamically direct their own processes and tool usage,
maintaining control over how they accomplish tasks,” which echoes earlier definitions while emphasizing
the role of LLMs as responsible for autonomous decision-making Based on this definition, they also
distinguish agents from the basic LLM workflow patterns upon which they are built, but do not—when
implemented alone—constitute an agent application.

2.1 LLM-Based Agents

In the research literature, LLM-based agents have been used in a variety of ways. The authors of [4] used
LLMs for pentesting IT systems. In [5], LLMs were shown to be able to hack websites through means
such as SQL injection. In [4], LLMs were used to find vulnerabilities for zero-day attacks.
LLMs were shown to be effective in each of these applications described. However, one of the major
findings in the literature is that LLMs may hallucinate when attempting to create code or commands
[4][40], which can lead to strange, ineffective behavior for the agent. Therefore, it is necessary to use
approaches found in the literature to improve the LLM agent’s performance in producing valid code and
commands.

2.3 Agent Benefits and Risks

LLM-based AI agents are growing in popularity because of their ability to handle complex tasks
autonomously [28]. Although agents have demonstrated the potential to free time spent by humans to
focus on higher-level tasks, significant drawbacks still must be addressed. The validation of LLM output
is critical owing to its nondeterministic nature, especially when combined with increased autonomy [29].

 2

Access to certain tools and data can have serious consequences, especially without humans in the loop.
For example, Anthropic’s computer use of an API feature provides the ability to have its models
manipulate a computer desktop environment using an agent loop [30]. This application is an example of
both exciting automation possibilities and associated risks. This is highlighted by the developers, who
describe examples ranging from an agent deviating from its assigned coding task to browse the web for
unrelated content, to more serious dangers like prompt injection attacks that could hijack the agent [31].

2.4 Related Works

We have compared other pentesting technologies incorporating LLMs in the research literature. Laney
developed one of the early tools for pentesting IT systems with LLMs, which involved multiple steps and
a hierarchical approach. Laney discussed some of the limitations of LLM and approaches to improving
performance, such as the use of memory [8]. However, an approach that Laney did not investigate was
RAG. On the other hand, Isozaki did use RAG by scraping pertinent websites for information on hacking
to improve overall performance [9] and built on the work of PentestGPT [10]. Pentest-AI is a framework
that uses multiple LLM-based agents [10]. HackSynth is another recently developed LLM-based
pentesting tool, with prominent features including a planner and a summarizer to create commands and
receive and process feedback [11]. What these frameworks or tools have in common is that they were
tested only on IT systems. Therefore, they are unlikely to perform adequately for OT systems. Table 1

 3. BACKGROUND

For this work, the focus is on pretrained LLMs due to their availability, but future work may explore
pretraining and finetuning as possible approaches. This work augments pretrained LLMs for pentesting
with available information from OT systems, such as manuals and other information, which are added to
the LLM’s context through retrieval augmented generation (RAG).

3.1 Approaches to Augment LLMs

LLMs possess both great potential for code generation and the ability to generate commands that may be
executed. However, as mentioned, LLMs may be prone to hallucinations, or at least the LLMs are not
precise in creating correct commands or code based on factual information in cases where no examples
exist in the original data set on which the model is trained. Methods are available to improve the
performance of the LLMs in these cases, including prompt engineering, chain of thought, feedback,
retrieval-augmented generation, fine tuning, and test-driven development (in the case of LLMs used for
software development). Given the scope of this effort for OT systems, this article focuses on the first two
main methods.

3.1.1 Context or Prompt Engineering

Context or prompt engineering is a useful methodology in which certain information (e.g., examples,
instructions, or contextual information) may be used to enhance the performance of the LLM for a given
task [12]. Prompt engineering has some limitations, however, in that the size of the prompt window is
finite. Various ways of performing prompt engineering include chain of thought and feedback. Chain of
thought involves designing a prompt with instructions on what the LLM must perform, as well as steps or
examples of steps carried out to achieve the requirements of instructions [13]. In the case of feedback,
results or output from receiving information from the environment are given as part of the prompt (i.e., a
sub-prompt) in a later prompt to the LLM. An example of feedback is incorporating the result of using a
tool or output of the tool, such as Nmap, in a later prompt to the LLM after running the tool. This
example is seen in certain works involving pentesting [8], [10].

3.1.2 Retrieval Augmented Generation and other External Information

An approach described in [14] uses other forms of information besides the information embedded in the
parameters to improve the LLM’s performance when queried. LLMs have difficulty accurately retrieving
specific facts from their training data [14]. Also, data inherent in the LLM model are only as current as
the last time the model was trained. Creating a foundation model is quite expensive computationally [15],
so creating a more updated foundation model may be impractical. In contrast, RAG provides a way to
bring in relevant, up-to-date information that the LLM can use to produce better answers without having
to go through extensive training. Essentially, RAG involves enhancing the prompt context by augmenting
it with information from the external source. A common approach is to incorporate vector search, which
uses vector embeddings that capture semantic relationships. The retrieved data are combined in the
overall input to the LLM. The external source may include databases, files, and other forms of stored data
as text included directly in the context window.

3.2 Penetration Testing in Operational Technology (OT) Environment

Penetration testing, or pentesting, is an active form of cybersecurity that involves executing cyberattacks
on systems with the aim of finding and remedying vulnerabilities before malicious actors exploit them.

 5

The primary goal is to determine the security condition of the system and provide actionable intelligence
for the sake of enhancing defenses.
 In OT systems, pentesting is especially critical because the systems are directly engaging with
mission-critical physical processes, such as electricity supply, water supply, and industrial manufacturing.
Because OT systems are directly connected to these processes through actuators and sensors, the exposure
could have severe real-world consequences, such as risk to human life, economic loss, and destruction of
critical infrastructure. Some pentesters will, in fact, conduct certain activities on a replica of the OT
system rather than on the production system to avoid adversely affecting the production system, which
may only allow Nmap activities that are less problematic [16]. Well known frameworks, which can
indicate the various stages of an attack, can also be used for pentesting. UnCLE in its current form uses an
abridged version of a kill chain focusing on more benign activities. The Lockheed Martin Cyber Kill
Chain (CKC) [17] is a linear, methodical model of cybersecurity developed to illustrate the sequential
nature of cyberattacks. The CKC model consists of the following seven stages: (1) reconnaissance, (2)
weaponization, (3) delivery, (4) exploitation, (5) installation, (6) command and control, and (7) actions on
objectives.
 The MITRE ATT&CK framework is a comprehensive, rich knowledge base of adversary tactics,
techniques, and procedures. Unlike the linear nature of the CKC, MITRE ATT&CK provides a structured
but flexible matrix that categorizes various methods attackers employ in various stages of cyberattacks
[18], as follows:

Tactics: Adversary high-level objectives within an attack, such as initial access, execution, persistence,
privilege escalation, and impact.

Techniques: Attackers’ techniques used to achieve their tactical objectives. Each technique has a specific
identifier (e.g., T1595—Active Scanning).

Procedures: Specific deployments or actual instances of techniques employed by documented attackers.

 4. EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN OF AI-BASED PENTESTING FRAMEWORK

UnCLE implements a cybersecurity agent that performs network reconnaissance on a local subnet,
following the first stage of the Lockheed Martin Cyber Kill Chain cybersecurity model [17]. It uses Nmap
to identify hosts, open ports, and running services. The system also includes a Telnet agent that is
integrated with a RAG system to attempt connections to identified devices, particularly focusing on OT
devices, such as protective relays and meters, found in an electric utility substation environment.

4.1 First Design Phase (Proof of Concept)

 The first prototype of the system comprises an agent component, which uses memory to persist state and
supplementary context, and a ReAct (Reasoning and Action) algorithm Error! Reference source not found.
to prompt an LLM, and the prototype interacts with the environment through tools (part [a] of Figure 1). The

 6

agent interacts with the network environment, which consists of various IT and OT devices running software
that can be accessed by different communication protocols (part [b] of Figure 1).
The application is orchestrated using the Burr framework Error! Reference source not found., which
provides a state machine management system and structured transitions between different actions. The
agent steps are separated into stages. The workflow begins with a target subnet as input and includes core
components (e.g., main application using Burr workflow, Nmap agent stage, and Telnet agent stage)
alongside supporting services (e.g., LLM provider accessed via LiteLLM, SQLite database, and a simple
RAG component).

4.1.1 ReAct Implementation—Core Loop Architecture

The foundation of both the Nmap and Telnet agents is a sophisticated implementation of the ReAct
pattern. This pattern enables the LLM to reason about the environment, take actions, observe results, and
incorporate those observations into its reasoning process. The implementation consists of the following
carefully designed components:

4.1.1.1 System Prompts

Each agent has a detailed ReAct system prompt that provides

  a clear definition of the agent’s role and objectives,
  specific guidelines for decision-making,
  examples of ideal reasoning and action patterns,
  constraints and boundaries for the agent’s operations, and
  exact JSON schema specifications for structured outputs.
These system prompts are critical for constraining the LLM’s outputs to be consistent, parsable, and aligned
with the agent’s purpose. For example, the Telnet agent’s system prompt specifically instructs it to attempt
anonymous login, identify devices, and try to gain higher access privileges, whereas the Nmap agent
focuses on discovering hosts, scanning ports, and identifying services.

4.1.1.2 ReAct Loop Execution

The core ReAct loop is responsible for reasoning about how to complete tasks and select actions using
tools.

4.1.1.3 System Prompts for Goal Setting and Output Structuring

Both agent stages employ carefully crafted system prompts that serve as the foundation for the LLM’s
behavior throughout the ReAct loop. A system prompt is a set of instructions provided to an LLM before
any user interaction. It helps establish the “rules of engagement” that shape the model’s overall behavior,
such as its role, response format, and constraints.

4.1.1.4 Nmap Agent System Prompt

The Nmap agent’s system prompt establishes its identity as a “network scanning agent that uses Nmap to
survey networks” with three clear objectives:
 1. Discover live hosts on the exact target subnet.
 2. Scan open ports on discovered hosts.
 3. Identify services running on open ports and OS details where possible.

 7

The prompt emphasizes efficiency through parallel scanning approaches and provides concrete examples
of effective scanning workflows. This is an important optimization because testing indicated that Nmap
scans can take up to several minutes per host. Combined with model inference times on local hardware, this
can cause an extremely long execution time for completing this stage. The prompt also sets expectations
for the agent to reason about scan results and make intelligent decisions about follow-up commands. Critical
guidance includes avoiding redundant scans and using space-separated lists based on the expected Nmap
parameter format to scan multiple hosts simultaneously.
The system prompt also strictly defines the expected JSON output format for thoughts, actions, and
observations, ensuring the LLM produces parsable structured output with fields that match the Pydantic
models used for validation with the Instructor Error! Reference source not found. library. Validation
failures result in retries, automatically handled by Instructor, which resubmits the query to the model with
error details.

4.1.1.5 Telnet Agent System Prompt

The Telnet agent’s system prompt defines a more complex role as an agent that uses Telnet to access devices
for evaluation and network assessment with specific goals: (1) attempt anonymous login to devices on port
23, (2) identify the device if login succeeds, (3) try to gain higher access privileges, and (4) report findings.
This prompt provides a detailed command set (connect, send, disconnect, query, complete) to help map
actions output by the LLM to specific tools and function calls. Specific guidelines for the assessment
process are also provided, including explicit limits on identification and privilege escalation attempts. This
helps prevent the LLM from getting stuck in an endless loop of trying different usernames or passwords,
for example. This agent stage also integrates RAG capabilities by providing instructions in the system
prompt for the agent to “use the ‘query’ command to look up information about the device” before
attempting privilege escalation. Like the Nmap agent, it enforces strict JSON formatting requirements.

4.1.2 ReAct Process

The ReAct process as used by the UnCLE framework involves multiple activities including context
assembly, LLM reasoning and action generation, structured output parsing, command execution, database
persistence, observation generation, and loop continuation decision.

4.1.2.1 Context Assembly

Before each LLM query, a comprehensive context is assembled, including the following:
  Target information (subnet for Nmap, host details for Telnet)
  Complete history of previous commands and their outputs
  All previous observations made by the LLM
  For the Telnet agent, additional context information from RAG queries

This step is essential, because without enough context, the LLM will not be able to select the appropriate
action for the current state of its operating environment. Additionally, the results of previous actions must
be included for the LLM to make progress toward its goals without getting “stuck.” For example, during
the network reconnaissance stage using Nmap, if the LLM is not prompted with the results of its attempt
to discover hosts, it could get caught in a loop of rediscovering the same host(s).

4.1.2.2 LLM Reasoning and Action Generation

The LLM is prompted using the “ReAct Step” user prompt template with the assembled context, generating
  a “thought” representing its reasoning about the current state and objectives,
  an “action” specifying exactly what command to execute, and

 8

  a “purpose” explaining the intent behind the action.

4.1.2.3 Structured Output Parsing

The LLM is prompted using Instructor and LiteLLM [35] as the client. The call specifies the Pydantic
model to validate and parse the LLM’s JSON output, ensuring strict adherence to the required format and
preventing malformed actions.

4.1.2.4 Command Execution

The parsed action is executed by the appropriate tool, as follows:
  For Nmap: The specified Nmap command is run via the Nmap tool implementation class, which
 uses the Pexpect library Error! Reference source not found. to invoke Nmap.
  For Telnet: Commands are routed to the appropriate method (connect, send, disconnect) of the
 Telnet tool implementation class based on the command type. This class also uses Pexpect to
 invoke the Telnet client command.
  For RAG: The “query” action is used to invoke RAG queries; this is done through a separate
 module of the application that provides a vector search method.

4.1.2.5 Database Persistence

All commands, their raw outputs, and metadata are stored in the SQLite database with appropriate
relations: (1) command text and purpose; (2) execution timestamp; (3) raw command output; (4) exit
code; and (5) stage identifier (Nmap or Telnet).

4.1.2.6 Observation Generation

The LLM is prompted again using the “ReAct Response” user prompt template with the command output,
generating a structured observation that analyzes and interprets the results.

4.1.2.7 Loop Continuation Decision

The loop continues until one of the following occurs: (1) The LLM determines the assessment is complete
and issues a “complete” action; (2) the maximum iteration limit is reached (configurable, default 20–50
iterations); or (3) an unrecoverable error occurs. This architecture ensures that the LLM maintains
continuity of reasoning across iterations while accumulating knowledge about the network or device
being assessed.

 5. ENVIRONMENT AND EXPERIMENTAL SETUP

This research employed a cyber-physical testbed environment, incorporating networks that facilitate
communication between electrical grid devices for different electrical utilities. We connect the UnCLE
tool to one of the main networks to allow the tool to start performing its main steps of the pentest. The
devices connected to the network include protective relays and power meters, which generically may be
referred to as intelligent electronic devices (IED). These IEDs have a microprocessor, and they maintain
the ability to communicate on the network. They interact with the electrical grid through current and
voltage sensors and three-phase breakers located in power lines and substation feeders. Protective relays
and power meters have protection, control, measurement, and communication functions that can measure

and control the power flow of the electrical grid. The protective relays and power meters are wired to
sensors for measuring the phase currents and voltages on different electrical grid locations. Protective
relays can also control the breakers that can open or close the three-phase AC circuits of the electrical
grid. In this testbed, our goal is to create an environment that closely matches the behavior of a real
electrical power grid. To do so, we decided to use a real-time simulator with hardware-in-the-loop, real-
life electrical control system equipment that includes protective relays and power meters. Also, we
include a Programable Logic Controller (PLC) that can communicate in Modbus on the network to
represent the controller at a power plant. The testbed has several features that make it useful for this work,
including a synchronized time-source system for all devices on the test bed: the Center for Alternate
Synchronization and Timing system [39]. Certain protocols, specifically the precision time protocol
(PTP) and Inter-Range Instrumentation Group Time Code B (IRIG-B) protocol, facilitate synchronization
between the time source and the devices. The testbed also possesses a synchronized trigger event signal
system that permits the testbed to send a signal to power meters and relays for recording their events at a
specific time. The electrical substation grid testbed was created in ORNL’s Advanced Protection
Laboratory and first commissioned there [37]. A one-line diagram of the system is shown in Figure 3. In
depth information on the testbed is available in [39]; for this work we focus on the red dashed area. This
one-line diagram power system represents a cyber-physical environment and is modeled as an RT-LAB
project using MATLAB Simulink with the OPAL-RT simulator and protective relays/power meters in the
loop.

 6. EXPERIMENTS

For this work, we devised two main sets of experiments that allowed us to make comparisons with and
without the use of RAG. Our main hypothesis is that RAG will improve the performance of the agents in
the experiments. The first set of two experiments—one without RAG and one with RAG for
comparison—involves a single SEL device networked with the tool. The second set of experiments
involves multiple devices in which we perform some experiments with RAG and some without. We also
compare between a local model (Llama) and a non-local model (Gemini). Therefore, there are four
possible situations in the second set.

 8. CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK

Although the smolagents framework enabled rapid deployment of code agent functionality, the ability to
perform structured output validation on the final result is limited when compared with approaches such as
the Instructor library using Pydantic models, and it also is not as reliable. Analysis and reporting stages
would be improved by taking the raw results from code agents and using an analysis/reporting stage with
better structured output validation for better consistency and quality. This would address missing report
sections, inconsistent host/target numbers, and other issues. Optimizing context in agent applications such
as this one (and especially multi-agent approaches) remain one of the biggest challenges encountered.
This challenge is called “context engineering” because it involves related concepts to the “context” you
feed an LLM, such as prompt engineering, RAG, or memory. Context is a challenge owing to the need to
balance providing sufficient instructions and detail to accomplish tasks while avoiding ballooning token
counts and the associated costs and model limitations. In general, though, we managed to show that these
augmenting methods and a good model could improve the performance of the agent.

 14

 Future work could involve exploring more comprehensive evaluation of instruction/system
prompts and techniques for managing and compressing context. Future work may also be to develop a
more in-depth pentesting tool that will cover many more steps than what is currently found in the MITRE
ATT&CK framework.
