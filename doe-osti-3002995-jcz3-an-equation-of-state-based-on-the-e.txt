ABSTRACT
This report presents a comprehensive examination of the thermodynamic functions utilized in the
JCZ3 equation of state, which is framed within the context of Helmholtz free energy (𝐴). The
independent variables of the equation are defined as temperature (𝑇), extrinsic gas volume (𝑉), and
the mole numbers of the gas phase constituents. A critical distinction is made between extrinsic
and intrinsic variables, with extrinsic values being homogeneous functions of the first degree in
mole numbers, adhering to the partial molar properties law. Key extrinsic functions, including
entropy, internal energy, Helmholtz free energy, system volume, and heat capacity, are discussed
alongside intrinsic values such as temperature and pressure. The report emphasizes the importance
of equilibrated thermodynamic properties in high-temperature and pressure conditions, particu-
larly within the framework of the Tiger thermal equilibrium code. It delineates the methodologies
for calculating conventional and equilibrated thermal expansion coefficients and isothermal com-
pressibility, while also addressing the formulation of partial molar expressions and heat capacities.
The foundational equations for the JCZ3 equation of state are derived, highlighting the interplay
between the ideal gas Helmholtz free energy and the extensive nature of the equation. This work
lays the groundwork for further thermodynamic relations derived from the Helmholtz free energy,
ultimately contributing to a deeper understanding of gas phase behavior in multiphase systems.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The Jacobs, Cowperthwaite, Zwisler equation of state (JCZ3-EOS) originated around 1969 from
Sigmund Jacobs’ dissatisfaction with the existing methods for calculating detonation states. At
that time, the prevailing equations of state (EOS) were primarily based on either solid-like models,
such as the Mie-Grüneisen equation, or gas-like models. Jacobs proposed a novel approach that
combined both solid and gas-like EOS, utilizing Monte Carlo analysis alongside Lennard-Jones
and Devonshire (LJD) calculations.
This report presents a comprehensive analysis of the JCZ3 equation of state as implemented in
the TIGER code, focusing on its thermodynamic framework, mathematical formulations, and
implications for modeling gas phase behavior under varying conditions.
The JCZ3 EOS is grounded in the Helmholtz free energy, which serves as the central function
for calculating essential thermodynamic properties, including entropy, Gibbs free energy, internal
energy, enthalpy, and heat capacity. The report emphasizes the critical distinction between extrinsic
variables, which depend on the amount of substance, and intrinsic variables, which remain constant
regardless of quantity. This distinction is vital for accurately modeling chemical equilibrium,
particularly in high-temperature and pressure regimes where species concentrations fluctuate.
The TIGER code simplifies the modeling of condensed phases by focusing exclusively on stoichio-
metric phases. While this design choice enhances computational efficiency, it may limit accuracy
in systems with variable compositions, such as alloys or mixtures. The report details the derivation
of key thermodynamic properties from the Helmholtz free energy, ensuring internal consistency
and highlighting the interrelationships among various thermodynamic quantities.
A significant focus is placed on the imperfection factor often called the compressibility factor with
the symbol 𝑍, which quantifies deviations from ideal gas behavior. The report confirms that the
chemical potential derived from the EOS adheres to the Gibbs-Duhem relation, reinforcing the
model’s thermodynamic validity. Additionally, the flexibility of the TIGER code in calculating
ideal gas reference state properties is demonstrated through various polynomial forms and the
Einstein oscillator model, allowing for accurate representation across a range of conditions.
An appendix provides a catalog of mathematical expressions used in the TIGER code, facilitating
validation and enhancing understanding of the computational framework. The consistent use of
established equations bolsters the report’s reliability as a reference for users of the TIGER code.
In conclusion, the JCZ3 EOS, as implemented in the TIGER code, offers a robust framework for
modeling thermodynamic properties in gas mixtures, particularly under extreme conditions. While
the simplification of phase modeling to stoichiometric compounds may introduce limitations in
predictive accuracy, the report underscores the foundational robustness of the TIGER code and its
potential as a valuable tool for simulating high-pressure, high-temperature gas mixtures. Continued

1. CONVENTIONS AND USEFUL THERMODYNAMIC RELATIONS

During the development of the JCZ3-EOS [1], Cowperthwaite investigated two other EOSs referred
to as the JCZ1-EOS and JCZ2-EOS. The JCZ1-EOS was based on a Lennard-Jones potential and
the JCZ2-EOS was based on a Lennard-Jones n:6 potential. The more accurate EOS was based on
the Exponential 6,13 potential and is known as the JCZ3-EOS discussed in the current work.
In this document, we describe the thermodynamic functions employed for the JCZ3 equation of
state. The JCZ3-EOS is not introduced until chapter 3, and the first two chapters are provided as
background material. Because the JCZ3-EOS is a Helmholtz equation of state, the independent
variables are defined as the temperature (𝑇), the extrinsic gas volume (𝑉), and the mole numbers
of the species (𝑛𝑖 ) that comprise the gas phase.
This section establishes the fundamental thermodynamic principles and definitions used in the
TIGER code’s implementation of gas phase models [2], which are based on the JCZ3 Helmholtz
EOS. The primary conclusion is that the calculations for thermodynamic properties in high-
temperature and pressure regimes must explicitly account for chemical equilibrium, as species
concentrations are not constant but rather change instantaneously with conditions. This is the dis-
tinction between "conventional" and "equilibrated" properties. This section also demonstrates how
to derive key thermodynamic relationships—e.g., the relationship between 𝐶 𝑝 and 𝐶𝑣 —using the
independent variables of a Helmholtz EOS. This indicates a strong emphasis on internal consistency
and a rigorous mathematical foundation for the model, ensuring that the computed properties are
thermodynamically sound and reflect the system’s true equilibrium state. The detailed derivations
and the focus on consistent variable representation highlight the robustness of the JCZ3 model for
its intended applications.

1.1. Extrinsic vs Intrinsic Variables

Within derivations for thermodynamic relations, there is an important distinction to be made
between intrinsic values and extrinsic values. Intrinsic properties are independent of the amount of
material, e.g., density, melting point, boiling point, specific heat capacity, etc. Extrinsic properties
depend on the amount of material, mass, volume, total heat capacity, etc. Extrinsic values are
homogeneous functions of the first degree in the mole number of the constituents and therefore,
they must obey the partial molar properties law. The partial molar properties law states that an
extensive thermodynamic property of a mixture can be expressed as a sum of its partial molar
values for each component.

All equations of state within TIGER’s gas phase models are complete Helmholtz free energy
equations of state. The equations of state are separated into a gas phase, which is assumed to be
always present and a set of condensed phases.

1.2. Conventional Thermodynamic Properties Versus Equilibrated
 Thermodynamic Properties

There are two different mixtures possible depending on whether or not the species in the mixture
are in mobile chemical equilibrium with each other at all densities and temperatures. In this case,
the composition will change if the density or temperature changes. There are also non-reactive
mixtures, where the species may interact physically by intermolecular forces but do not interact
chemically, and the composition of the material is fixed, independent of density or temperatures.
Mixtures in TIGER are typically reactive and change depending on the state of the mixture.
However, TIGER can also freeze the composition and not react chemically. We do this sometimes
during the expansion along an isentrope, where the composition is "frozen" when it reaches a
sufficiently cool temperature that the reactions are slow. This "freeze out" temperature calculated
during a detonation isentrope has typically been chosen to be about 1800 K.
TIGER is an equilibrium code meant to represent high temperature and pressure conditions,
where equilibrium between species is believed to exist, except in situations where the gas is
cooling via isentropic expansion processes. Therefore, equilibrium between species exists almost
everywhere. Here, we make the distinction that the thermodynamic processes that occur as the
volume, temperature, or pressure changes will occur through states that are at equilibrium with
respect to the species concentrations even condensed phase species. This means that common
properties such as the thermal expansion coefficient, or isothermal compressibility will occur via
states that are at equilibrium with respect to changes in the speciation. Conventional properties for
thermal expansion coefficients and isothermal compressibility are all calculated assuming species
moles are held constant.
We will identify and distinguish thermodynamic states where the species concentrations are allowed
to vary according to thermodynamic equilibrium with a superscript E, standing for “equilibrated”,
and we will precede the identity of those states with the word, equilibrated, meaning that the mole
numbers are varied according to equilibrium conditions. Note, condensed states represented by
condensed phase species included in the equilibrated states may also be included. The representation
of condensed states is outside the scope of this chapter, which focuses on the JCZ3 equation of
state.

2. LAYOUT OF THE THERMODYNAMICS PROBLEM

Solid or liquid solutions exhibit different characteristics and behaviors than stoichiometric phases.
TIGER only uses stoichiometric solid or liquid phases. TIGER does not handle solid or liquid
solutions. Therefore, every liquid or solid phase within TIGER is a stoichiometric phase.
Solid and liquid solutions are homogeneous mixtures where one or more solutes are dissolved in a
solvent. In solid solutions, the solute atoms or molecules occupy interstitial or substitutional sites
within the crystal lattice of the solvent, leading to a single-phase material with varying compositions.
The properties of solid solutions can change continuously with composition, allowing for a range
of physical and chemical characteristics. For example, in metallic alloys, the addition of different
elements can enhance strength, ductility, or corrosion resistance. In liquid solutions, solutes
are uniformly distributed within the solvent, resulting in a single-phase liquid. The interactions
between solute and solvent molecules can significantly affect properties such as boiling point,
freezing point, and viscosity. Liquid solutions can also exhibit a wide range of compositions and
behaviors, influenced by factors such as temperature and pressure.
In contrast, stoichiometric phases refer to materials that have a fixed ratio of constituent elements
or compounds, defined by their chemical formula. These phases are characterized by a specific
composition and distinct properties that arise from the precise arrangement of atoms or molecules.
Stoichiometric phases typically form under equilibrium conditions and can be represented by
well-defined chemical formulas, such as NaCl for sodium chloride.
The key difference between stoichiometric phases and solid or liquid solutions lies in their compo-
sitional flexibility. While solid and liquid solutions can accommodate a range of compositions and
exhibit continuous variations in properties, stoichiometric phases are rigid in their composition and
properties. Deviations from the stoichiometric ratio can lead to the formation of non-stoichiometric
phases, which may exhibit different physical and chemical behaviors.
TIGER’s approach to condensed phases is a significant simplification. By only using stoichiometric
solid and liquid phases, the model avoids the complexities of handling compositional variability
and the continuous range of properties found in solutions. This design choice implies that TIGER
is best suited for systems where solid and liquid phases are assumed to exist as pure, well-defined
compounds with fixed compositions. This limitation could affect the accuracy of the model for
systems containing alloys, ceramics, or other solid/liquid solutions.
TIGER assumes that the gas phase exists within the system. TIGER uses various equations of
state for the gas phase. In the next section we will fully describe the JCZ3 equation of state, the
current workhorse EOS. It should be noted that these equations of state are Helmholtz free energy
equations of state with the possibility of multiple simultaneous solutions and with the possibility
of the existence of a forbidden region. TIGER does not consider any of this behavior. Therefore,
TIGER must be forced to stay out of the forbidden region by pre-calculating the gas phase spinodal

 21

curve, and then ensuring that the nonlinear iteration process stays out of the forbidden region as it
progresses.
The gas phase spinodal curve is a boundary in the pressure-temperature (𝑃-𝑇) space that separates
stable and unstable regions of a gas. Inside the spinodal region, any small fluctuation in density or
composition can lead to phase separation (e.g., the gas can spontaneously condense into a liquid).
Outside this region, the gas is stable and small fluctuations will not lead to phase separation.
The forbidden region refers to areas in the 𝑃-𝑇 space where the physical behavior of the gas in not
well-defined or is unstable. This region typically lies between the spinodal curve and the critical
point of the mixture. In the forbidden region, the equations of state may yield multiple simultaneous
solutions where the system could theoretically exist in more than one state at the same conditions.
This can lead to non-physical results or undefined behavior in TIGER calculations. Since TIGER
does not account for the complex behavior that can occur in the forbidden region, the calculations
must remain outside this area. This is done by pre-calculating the gas phase spinodal curve and
monitoring the iterative process to avoid entering the forbidden region.
The need to manually prevent TIGER from entering a "forbidden region" for its gas phase EOS
highlights a potential vulnerability in the code’s robustness. Rather than natively handling the full
range of mathematical behavior of the JCZ3 EOS, TIGER relies on a pre-calculated boundary and
forced iteration. This suggests that while the model is designed to be efficient, it may not fully
explore or account for all possible thermodynamic states predicted by the underlying equations,
which could be a source of potential inaccuracies or limitations in its predictive capabilities.

3.3. Summary

This section establishes that the TIGER algorithm’s core is a rigorous and self-consistent thermody-
namic model based on the Helmholtz free energy. By defining all thermodynamic properties from
a single, fundamental equation, the model ensures internal consistency. The use of a semiempirical
approach to define the EOS parameters suggests a balance between theoretical rigor and practical,
data-driven validation.
The takeaway is the transition from a single-phase to a multiphase perspective. TIGER must handle
the complex interplay between phases in equilibrium. This requires a sophisticated approach to
calculate system-wide properties, such as compressibility, by solving a system of coupled differential
equations that relate changes in total volume to changes in the gas phase volume and the extent of
chemical reactions. This highlights the advanced nature of TIGER’s computational framework and
its ability to simulate complex, multi-component systems under changing conditions.
In summary, this section has established the primary pressure relation for the TIGER EOS in its
gas phase (Eqn. 3.1) and the corresponding extensive relationship for the Helmholtz free energy
(Eqn. 3.2), which aligns with the pressure relationship. This is a significant advancement, as all
thermodynamic relations can ultimately be derived from the derivative of this extensive Helmholtz
free energy relationship (Eqn. 3.16). Additionally, we have explored the principles governing the
relationship between total volume and phase volumes during system perturbations, recognizing
that any change in total volume involves a perturbation of the extents of the entire system of
reactions. This understanding can only be achieved through equations that account for changes in
total pressure, total temperature, or other system-wide thermodynamic variables. Moving forward,
the next step is to further develop the underlying relationship involving 𝐸 and 𝑓 .

thus obeys the Gibbs-Duhem relation. The proof is broken down into two parts, showing that both
the lattice-potential term (𝐸 𝑜 ) and the intermolecular-force term (𝐼 or ln 𝑓 ) independently satisfy
the necessary mathematical conditions. This rigorous verification ensures the internal consistency

4.4. Rigor and Consistency

This section demonstrates the thermodynamic rigor and internal consistency of the JCZ3 EOS
as implemented in the TIGER code. The detailed derivations of the imperfection factor and its
derivatives, along with the proof that the chemical potential is a partial molar quantity, show that
the model is built on a solid theoretical foundation.
The most critical conclusion is the verification of the Gibbs-Duhem relationship. By proving that
the chemical potential is a partial molar property, the report validates the entire framework for
calculating multi-component, multi-phase equilibrium. This ensures that the model’s predictions
for chemical potential and related properties are consistent and thermodynamically sound. While
the document notes a potential discrepancy in Cowperthwaite’s definition of the activity coefficient,
the core framework is shown to be robust, confirming that the TIGER code’s calculations are based
on valid and internally consistent thermodynamic principles.

We have found that the Exp 6,13 potential fits detonation velocities to within about 2% for ideal
explosives. However, we realize that slight modifications in the stiffness parameter 𝛼 could fit
data better as discussed by Stiel et al. [10]. Stiel determined that a large stiffness 𝛼 − 18.2) was
necessary to match the polytetrafluoroethylene (Teflon𝑇 𝑀 ) shock Hugoniot. However, we have
found that stiffness can be enhanced by keeping 𝛼 constant and decreasing 𝑟 ∗ and increasing 𝜖/𝑘.
This "potential compensation" effect is similar to the "kinetic compensation effect" described by
Brill et al. [11] where a relationship exists between the pre-exponential factor and the activation
energy, where one effect is compensated for by another. Hallstadius [13, 14] pointed out that the
"potential compensation" effect may be less accurate when individual gases are considered such as
the critical temperature predictions for carbon tetrafluoride (228 K) which is outside of the range
of typical calculations for explosives. In fact, specific heat fits for most of the equilibrium products
usually start at 298.15 K, and polynomial effects such as the Runge phenomena would make critical
temperature predictions with any EOS problematic.
There have been some criticisms of the JCZ3-EOS, specifically by Byers Brown and Amaee [12]
who emphasized that a good EOS needed to pass the sine qua non (absolutely necessary) and acid
test. The sine qua non test was the necessity to replicate Monte Carlo data. We addressed this
requirement in the 16𝑡ℎ Detonation Symposium [15] where we compared Monte Carlo data with
JCZ3 predictions and concluded that the JCZ3 EOS was as good as integral theory and better than
variational perturbation theory in matching Monte Carlo results using the Exp-6 potential. For the
"acid" test, we showed excellent agreement with data such as liquid Hugoniot matches, overdriven
Hugoniot matches, and detonation predictions which were as good or better than other models.
Although the EOS calculations were valid over a wide range of conditions, the calculations also
used a large number of species, radicals, and ions in the database. We conclude that an individual
species’ parameters should be fit using available Hugoniot data where that species dominates.

This section provides a comprehensive and transparent look at the specific implementation of
the JCZ3 EOS. The detailed equations and definitions, including the explicit forms of 𝑓𝑔 and 𝑓𝑠 ,

nature. It acknowledges that while the EOS is based on theoretical potentials, its predictive power
relies on carefully fitted parameters, particularly when applied to complex systems like explosives.

sets of parameters that yield similar results, emphasizing the need to validate parameters against

6. CALCULATION OF THERMODYNAMIC QUANTITIES

This section of the report derives the complete set of thermodynamic quantities for the JCZ3
equation of state (EOS). Building upon the Helmholtz free energy (𝐴) as the central function, it
provides explicit calculations for entropy (𝑆), Gibbs free energy (𝐺), internal energy (𝑈), enthalpy
(𝐻), and constant-volume heat capacity (𝐶𝑣 ). The derivations use partial derivatives of 𝐴 with
respect to (wrt) its independent variables: temperature (𝑇), volume (𝑉), and mole numbers (𝑛𝑖 ).
Key derivations include:

 • Entropy (S): Calculated from the temperature derivative of 𝐴, with separate derivations for
 the gas ( 𝑓𝑔 ) and solid ( 𝑓𝑠 ) contributions to the imperfection factor.
 • Gibbs Free Energy (G): Defined as 𝐺 = 𝐴 + 𝑃𝑉, with its derivation confirming consistency
 with a previously established formula derived from an integral.
 • Chemical Potential (𝜇𝑖 ): Derived from the partial derivative of 𝐴 wrt the mole number of
 species 𝑖. The document highlights a key intermediate quantity, Γ𝑖 , which simplifies the final
 expression.
 • Enthalpy (H) and Internal Energy (U): Calculated using the fundamental thermodynamic
 relationships 𝐻 = 𝐺 + 𝑇 𝑆 and 𝑈 = 𝐴 + 𝑇 𝑆. The section also provides a detailed transfor-
 mation for calculating partial molar enthalpy at constant pressure, a more complex task for a
 Helmholtz EOS, by converting it into a derivative at constant volume.
 • Pressure (P) and Heat Capacity (𝐶𝑣 ): These are also derived from the first and second
 derivatives of the Helmholtz free energy wrt volume and temperature, respectively. The
 section presents explicit formulas for the first and second derivatives of the imperfection
 factor’s components.

Overall, this chapter functions as a comprehensive reference guide for the thermodynamic calcula-
tions performed within the TIGER code, showing the inter-relationships between all key thermo-
dynamic quantities.

6.15. Key Takeaways

This chapter demonstrates the thermodynamic consistency and computational completeness of
the JCZ3 EOS implementation. The internal consistency of the EOS is shown by systematically
deriving all major thermodynamic properties from a single fundamental equation (the Helmholtz
free energy). The derived expressions for entropy, enthalpy, and other properties are directly related
to the parameters of the intermolecular potential, such as the well depth and excluded volume.
A key takeaway is the derivation of quantities defined at constant pressure (like partial molar
enthalpy), which are not natural for a constant-volume EOS. The provided transformation from
volume-based to pressure-based derivatives shows that the model can handle complex thermody-
namic calculations correctly.
Furthermore, the document serves as a validation tool, with explicit formulas that can be used to
verify the implementation of the EOS into TIGER. This is particularly important for second-order
derivatives, such as those for heat capacity, where the potential for error increases. The detailed
breakdown of the derivatives for the gas and solid parts of the imperfection factor ( 𝑓𝑔 and 𝑓𝑠 ) and
their dependence on volume and temperature parameters (𝑦 and 𝑧) provides a roadmap for anyone
seeking to understand or debug the TIGER code’s implementation of the JCZ3 EOS.

7. SPECIFICATION OF THE SPECIES REFERENCE STATE

This section details the methods used in the TIGER code to calculate the ideal gas reference
state thermodynamic properties (𝐶 𝑝0 , 𝐻 0 , 𝑆 0 , and 𝜇0 ). These properties are required inputs for the
JCZ3 EOS, as the total energy is partitioned into ideal and non-ideal contributions. The report
outlines three primary polynomial forms used to represent the temperature dependence of these
properties:

 • TIGER Polynomial: A seven-term polynomial in powers of 𝜃 = 𝑇/1000. Equations are
 provided for calculating heat capacity (𝐶 𝑝0 /𝑅), enthalpy (𝐻 0 /𝑅𝑇), and entropy (𝑆 0 /𝑅) by
 integrating the heat capacity expression. Integration constants are determined using standard
 enthalpy and entropy of formation at 298.15 K.
 • NASA Polynomial: A seven-term polynomial in powers of temperature 𝑇 with coefficients
 represented by 𝑎𝑖 . This format is a common standard in thermochemical databases. The
 NASA polynomials are implemented into TIGER using piecewise intervals with the interval
 temperature specified. The implementation was done using C0 continuity at the splice or tie
 points.
 • Einstein Oscillator Model: This model, based on quantum statistical mechanics, represents
 the heat capacity as a sum of contributions from harmonic oscillators, plus a constant term.
 It accounts for the difference between constant pressure and constant volume heat capacities
 (𝐶 𝑝0 and 𝐶𝑉0 ) using the thermal expansion coefficient and isothermal compressibility.

The section also mentions a linear extrapolation method for heat capacity at very high temperatures
and provides a general equation for calculating the reference chemical potential (𝜇 𝑜 /𝑅𝑇) from
the derived enthalpy and entropy. In future work, we plan to implement Chebyshev polynomial
fits for the reference states. We believe that a single Chebyshev polynomial fit will be capable
of covering the entire temperature range of interest, from the reference temperature up to 20,000
K. Discontinuities caused by phase transitions should be addressed separately by using distinct
Chebyshev polynomials for each phase.

7.4. Key Points

This chapter demonstrates that the TIGER code is flexible and robust in its handling of the ideal
gas reference state, supporting multiple well-established polynomial forms and even a physically-
based Einstein oscillator model. The inclusion of both empirical (TIGER, NASA) and theoretical
(Einstein) models suggests a sophisticated approach to data fitting, allowing the user to select
the most appropriate representation for a given species. The method for linear extrapolation to
high temperatures is a pragmatic solution for handling extreme conditions, which are common in
detonation simulations, while acknowledging the limitations of standard polynomial fits.

9. ADDRESSING CRITICISMS

The earliest criticism of the Jacobs equation of state (EOS) can be traced back to a question posed
by Charles Mader during the 12𝑡ℎ International Combustion Symposium in 1969 [6]. Mader
challenged Jacobs with the following statement:

 "It will not be possible to obtain agreement with the experimental data for the state
 parameters of explosives with any equation of state using the steady-state and chemical
 equilibrium assumptions. The observed time-dependent behavior of explosives will not
 be reproduced by any such theory. Fickett’s studies with the Lennard-Jones-Devonshire
 equation of state indicate that it is not possible to improve the agreement, already
 obtained using the more empirical BKW equation of state, between the computed and
 experimental detonation properties by improving the form of the equation of state. The
 idealizing assumptions of steady-state behavior and of chemical and thermodynamic
 equilibrium prevent one from reproducing the observed detonation behavior with any
 equation of state. If one wishes to obtain improved calculations and understanding
 of the detonation properties of explosives, it will be necessary to include the time-
 dependent behavior of the hydrodynamic flow, the chemical kinetics, and the time-
 dependent deposition of carbon into the calculations."

Jacobs summarized his response,

 I feel that it is important for the solution of detonation and shock problems to obtain the
 best equation of state that our physical insight can provide. The state of our knowledge
 on molecular systems at high density is sufficiently advanced to do this now. Despite
 the prophesy of failure, the steady-state detonation calculations and computations of
 isentropes with assumptions of thermodynamic equilibrium must be given a fair try. I
 think the effort will be productive. If and when the time dependent problems involving
 kinetics of reaction need to be solved, their solution will be better obtained with an
 improved equation of state. The time-dependent hydrodynamic flow problems can
 probably be treated with the less exact equations of state already available.

Twenty-two years later, in 1992, Byers Brown and Amaee [12] also provided a critical review of
the Jacobs’ EOS, stating that the

 "Jacobs EOS is a rather complicated semi-empirical equation, and its agreement with
 MC computer simulations results is poor. Were it not for the fact that the mixture
 version (JCZ3) has been incorporated into the well-known TIGER ideal detonation
 code, the Jacobs EOS would only be of historic interest."

 91

In 2018 [15] at the 16𝑡ℎ International Detonation Symposium, we presented a paper on JCZS3
database to show the predictions of the Monte Carlo simulations. We showed excellent agreement
with Monte Carlo data as well as agreement with liquid Hugoniot test, overdriven detonation of
condensed explosives, and detonation performance for a variety of explosives. The new JCZS3
database fits both Hugoniot data as well as detonation performance data. The remaining sections
show some of the results from our 2018 paper [15].

9.1. Monte Carlo Simulations

Ross and Adler [17] performed Monte Carlo (MC) calculations for shock compression of Argon
using an Exp-6, 13.5 potential. The method generated a sequence of configurations through a
Markov process, with averages corresponding to a canonical ensemble, utilizing up to 300,000
configurations to determine the average pressure and energy for the given potential. Fried et
al. [18] extended Ross and Adler’s MC calculations by using an Exp-6, 11.5 and an Exp-6, 15.5
potential.

9.5. Key Points

We have also addressed several criticisms of the JCZ3-EOS. Specifically, we have shown that Byers
Brown and Amaee’s [12] comments regarding the poor agreement of the JCZ3-EOS with Monte
Carlo data, which they refer to as the sine qua non test, are not accurate. In fact, the JCZ3-EOS
matches both historic [17] and recent [20] Monte Carlo data, as well as other Exp-6 EOS models
based on integral theory and variational perturbation theory.
We have demonstrated that the JCZ3-EOS, when combined with the extensive database of Exp-6, 13
parameters, can accurately match pure liquid Hugoniots for CH4 , CO, CO2 , C6 H6 , H2 , H2 O, NH3 ,
N2 , NO, and O2 . Additionally, it effectively correlates with overdriven detonation data for PETN,
PBX 9501, and the HMX/TATB/Estane mixture. We have also shown that the EOS can predict
detonation performance from low-density to high-density explosives. We believe the JCZ3-EOS
is an excellent EOS as long as the parameters such as exponential 6,13 parameters are accurate
and the reference thermodynamic data–such as entropy of formation, reference heat capacity (𝐶 𝑝 ),
and entropy 𝑆 𝑜 –are known. Also, accurate predictions depend on having an adequate number of
products in the final equilibrium mixture.

10. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

This report presents a comprehensive analysis of the JCZ3 equation of state (EOS) as implemented
in the TIGER code, focusing on its thermodynamic framework, mathematical formulations, and
implications for modeling gas phase behavior under varying conditions.

Summary of Key Findings

 • Thermodynamic Framework: The JCZ3 EOS is grounded in Helmholtz free energy,
 distinguishing between extrinsic and intrinsic thermodynamic variables. Extrinsic variables
 (e.g., volume, entropy) depend on the amount of substance, while intrinsic variables (e.g.,
 temperature, pressure) remain constant regardless of quantity. The report emphasizes the
 importance of chemical equilibrium in high-temperature and pressure regimes, where species
 concentrations fluctuate.

 • Modeling Phases: The TIGER code simplifies the modeling of condensed phases by focusing
 solely on stoichiometric phases, avoiding the complexities associated with liquid and solid
 solutions. This design choice enhances computational efficiency but may limit accuracy in
 systems with variable compositions, such as alloys or mixtures.

 • Mathematical Derivations: The report details the derivation of key thermodynamic prop-
 erties, including entropy, Gibbs free energy, and heat capacities, from the Helmholtz free
 energy. The rigorous approach ensures internal consistency and highlights the interrelation-
 ships among various thermodynamic quantities.

 • Imperfection Factor: The imperfection factor, which quantifies deviations from ideal gas
 behavior, is thoroughly analyzed. The report confirms that the chemical potential derived
 from the EOS adheres to the Gibbs-Duhem relation, reinforcing the model’s thermodynamic
 validity.

 • Ideal Gas Reference State: The TIGER code’s flexibility in calculating ideal gas reference
 state properties is demonstrated through various polynomial forms and the Einstein oscillator
 model. This adaptability allows for accurate representation of thermodynamic properties
 across a range of conditions.

 • Mathematical Appendix: An appendix provides a catalog of mathematical expressions used
 in the TIGER code, facilitating validation and understanding of the computational framework.
 The normalization of equations enhances readability and streamlines calculations.

 99

Conclusions

The JCZ3 EOS, as implemented in the TIGER code, offers a robust and internally consistent
framework for modeling thermodynamic properties in gas mixtures, particularly under extreme
conditions. The emphasis on rigorous mathematical derivations and the validation of key thermo-
dynamic relationships underscore the model’s reliability.
However, the simplification of phase modeling to stoichiometric compounds may introduce limita-
tions in predictive accuracy for systems with compositional variability. Future work should consider
enhancing the model’s capabilities to address these complexities, ensuring broader applicability
across diverse chemical systems.
Overall, the report affirms the TIGER code’s foundational robustness and its potential as a valuable
tool for simulating high-pressure, high-temperature gas mixtures, while also highlighting areas for
further refinement and validation.
