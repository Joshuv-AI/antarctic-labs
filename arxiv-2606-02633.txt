1.
Introduction and Results

For a nonnegative integer
m
and
k\geq 3
, the
m
th

k
-gonal number
p_{k}(m)
is the number of dots used to arrange a regular
k
-gon with a side length of
m
dots. From this definition, it is straightforward to see that

\displaystyle p_{k}(m)=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}((k-2)i+1)=\frac{(k-2)m^{2}-(k-4)m}{2}.

From this polynomial formula, we say that
N
is a generalized
k
-gonal number if
p_{k}(x)=N
for some
x\in\mathbb{Z}
. As we will frequently need to make the distinction between polygonal numbers and generalized polygonal numbers, we will often refer to
k
-gonal numbers as ordinary
k
-gonal numbers for emphasis and clarity.

The study of polygonal numbers as additive bases began upon Fermat conjecturing that every positive integer is the sum of
k
ordinary
k
-gonal numbers
[
16
]
, which is now known as the Fermat–Cauchy polygonal number theorem, following Cauchy’s proof
[
8
]
(the cases
k=3
and
k=4
are known as Gauss’ Eureka theorem
[
12
]
and Lagrange’s four-square theorem
[
25
]
, respectively, and were proven earlier). Some years after Cauchy’s proof, Legendre
[
27
]
refined the Fermat–Cauchy polygonal number theorem, showing that if
k\not\equiv 0\pmod{4}
, every sufficiently large integer is the sum of four ordinary
k
-gonal numbers, and if
k\equiv 0\pmod{4}
, every sufficiently large integer is the sum of five ordinary
k
-gonal numbers and at least one of these five is
0
or
1
. These works of Cauchy and Legendre have been wonderfully exposited and proven more efficiently in papers
[
33
]
and
[
34
]
of Nathanson.

To this day, the study of polygonal numbers as additive bases is a rich and active area of research. In
[
31
]
, Meng and Sun furthered Legendre’s refinement by showing that if
k\geq 8
is divisible by four, then every integer of the form
4r^{2}(4^{N\phi(2r+1)}-1)/(2r+1)
is not the sum of four ordinary
k
-gonal numbers, where
r=(k-4)/4
and
\phi
is Euler’s phi function. In
[
13
]
, Guy provided an abundance of open problems on representing integers as sums of polygonal numbers, such as determining the minimal number
\ell_{k}
of generalized
k
-gonal summands needed to obtain every positive integer, which, for
k\geq 8
, is easily shown to satisfy
\ell_{k}\geq k-4
. Ultimately, it was shown by Sun
[
38
]
that
\ell_{8}=4
and by Banerjee et al.
[
1
]
that
\ell_{k}=k-4
for all
k\geq 10
. In
[
14
]
, Haensch and Kane handled the delicate ternary case, showing that almost every positive integer is the sum of three generalized
k
-gonal numbers if
k\not\equiv 0\pmod{4}
and
k\not\equiv 2\pmod{3}
. A very general result due to Kane and Yang
[
23
]
shows that, to determine whether every positive integer is represented by a sum of (potentially various) generalized polygonal numbers, one only needs to check a finite subset of the positive integers (the maximum of this subset is ineffective as of writing, but can be made effective upon completing the classification of ternary sums of generalized polygonal numbers that represent every positive integer). Recently, much work has been done on determining the existence of representations of integers as sums of generalized polygonal numbers with parameters with a bounded number of prime divisors; see
[
2
,
35
,
3
]
.

While the existence of representations of integers as sums of polygonal numbers has been and continues to be studied extensively, the problem of counting such representations has received relatively less attention, though interest in polygonal number representation functions has been growing recently. We define
r_{n}^{{k}}(N)
to be the number of representations of
N
as the sum of
n
generalized
k
-gonal numbers, and
r_{n,+}^{{k}}(N)
to be the number of representations of
N
as the sum of
n
ordinary
k
-gonal numbers.

In
[
36
]
, Ono, Robins, and Wahl give exact formulas for the ordinary triangular number representation function
r_{n,+}^{{3}}(N)
for
n\in\{2,3,4,6,8,10,12,24\}
, recovering for the
n=4
case the formula
r_{4,+}^{{3}}(N)=\sigma(2N+1)
known previously to Legendre
[
26
]
, where
\sigma
is the sum-of-divisors function. In the case of four variables, Li and Wang
[
28
]
give an exact formula for
r_{4}^{{k}}(N)
for all
k\geq 3
in terms of sums of Hurwitz class numbers.

Since
r_{n}^{{k}}(N)
and
r_{n,+}^{{k}}(N)
count solutions to the Diophantine equation
\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{k}(x_{i})=N
over
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
, respectively, based on the available machinery, handling
r_{n}^{{k}}(N)
is easier than handling
r_{n,+}^{{k}}(N)
. As remarked in
[
7
, pg.4]
, we expect each
x_{i}
over the set of solutions
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
to be asymptotically equally distributed between the negative and positive integers, giving the heuristic that
r_{n,+}^{{k}}(N)=2^{-n}r_{n}^{{k}}(N)+o(N^{n/2-1})
. This is challenging to show, but Bringmann, Jang, Kane, and Tse
[
7
]
recently succeeded in proving the
n=4
case, which we will see gives some nice corollaries to our main result.

While showing a relationship between
r_{n}^{{k}}(N)
and
r_{n,+}^{{k}}(N)
is certainly difficult when
k\geq 5
, the
k=3
and
k=4
cases are much easier. For
k=3
, since
p_{3}(x)=p_{3}(-x-1)
, we in fact have
r_{n,+}^{{3}}(N)=2^{-n}r_{n}^{{3}}(N)
for all
n
. The
k=4
case is not quite this simple, but we still have a nice exact relationship. Indeed, for all
n
, we have

\displaystyle r_{n}^{{4}}(N)=[q^{N}]\left(\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}q^{m^{2}}\right)^{n}=[q^{N}]\left(-1+2\sum_{m\geq 0}q^{m^{2}}\right)^{n}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}{n\choose\ell}(-1)^{n-\ell}2^{\ell}r_{\ell,+}^{{4}}(N),

where
[q^{N}]f(q)
denotes the
N
th
coefficient of
f(q)
. Binomial inversion gives a similar formula for
r_{n,+}^{{4}}(N)
in terms of
r_{\ell}^{{4}}(N)
. Since
r_{n}^{{4}}(N)\ll N^{n/2-1+\varepsilon}
for all
n\geq 2
, it follows that
r_{n,+}^{{4}}(N)=2^{-n}r_{n}^{{4}}(N)+O(N^{(n-3)/2+\varepsilon})
for all
n\geq 3
. These nice correspondences exist for
k=3
and
k=4
since
p_{3}
and
p_{4}
have nice symmetry between negative and positive integer arguments; however,
p_{k}
does not possess such symmetry for
k\geq 5
, thereby making proving
r_{n,+}^{{k}}(N)=2^{-n}r_{n}^{{k}}(N)+o(N^{n/2-1})
difficult.

The
k=4
case of
r_{n}^{{k}}(N)
is, of course, just the sum of squares function, counting the number of solutions to
\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}=N
over
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, and is a classical object of study. Because of the significance of this case, we give it the special notation
r_{n}^{\square}(N)
. The sum of
n
squares function has been studied considerably, and exact formulas exist for several
n
. For example, Jacobi
[
22
]
gave exact divisor-sum formulas for
r_{n}^{\square}(N)
for
n\in\{2,4,6,8\}
, and exact formulas for
r_{10}^{\square}(N)
and
r_{12}^{\square}(N)
were given by Liouville
[
30
,
29
]
. For
n\geq 4
, we also have the asymptotic formula

\displaystyle r_{n}^{\square}(N)=\frac{\pi^{n/2}}{\Gamma(n/2)}\mathfrak{S}_{n}^{\square}(N)N^{n/2-1}+O(N^{(n-1)/4+\varepsilon}),

(1)

where
\mathfrak{S}_{n}^{\square}(N)
is the singular series of
r_{n}^{\square}(N)
. An embryonic version of this result arises as a special case of the analysis done on Waring’s problem by Hardy and Littlewood
[
15
]
, which is what prompted the development of the Hardy–Littlewood circle method. The exact version of (
1
) is these days a standard result, and can be seen, for example, by taking
F^{(0)}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}
in
[
17
, Corollary 1]
.

In
[
34
, pgs.305–306]
, Nathanson remarks through quotes of Uspensky and Heaslet
[
39
, pg.380]
and Weil
[
41
, pg.102]
that directly from Gauss’ Eureka theorem, one can obtain the Fermat–Cauchy polygonal number theorem and Legendre’s refinement thereof. In this sense, the triangular numbers are the most fundamental of the polygonal numbers. However, when moving from studying the existence of polygonal number representations to the polygonal number representation functions, it is natural to instead consider the squares as the most fundamental due to the number of clean results on the sum of squares function, motivating the desire to obtain a closed-form asymptotic relation between
r_{n}^{{k}}
and
r_{n}^{\square}
. Our main result is our obtaining such a relation, provided that we have at least four variables.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
n\geq 4
and
k\geq 3
. For
N\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, let
X_{N}=8(k-2)N+n(k-4)^{2}
. Then the number
r_{n}^{{k}}(N)
of representations of
N
as the sum of
n
generalized
k
-gonal numbers satisfies

\displaystyle r_{n}^{{k}}(N)=\frac{1+(-1)^{\lfloor n/4\rfloor}4^{n-2}T(X_{N},n,k)}{2^{n-3}(k-2)^{n-1}\mathcal{N}_{n}(X_{N},k-2)}r_{n}^{\square}(X_{N})+O(N^{(n-1)/4+\varepsilon}),

where
T(X_{N},n,k)
is given in closed form in Proposition
3.6
and where

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{n}(N,L)=\left(1+(-1)^{\lfloor n/4\rfloor}f_{2}(N,n)\right)\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\mid L\\
p\text{~odd}\end{subarray}}\left(1+p^{-n/2}f_{p}(N,n)\right),

(2)

and
f_{p}
and
f_{2}
are given in closed form in Propositions
3.1
and
3.3
, respectively.

We note that
\mathcal{N}_{n}(N,L)
is simply the product of
p
-adic densities of the sum of
n
squares equation
\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}=N
over all
p\mid 2L
.

The factor affixed to
r_{n}^{\square}(X_{N})
in Theorem
1.1
will often simplify greatly upon a specific choice of
n
and
k
. For example, we have

\displaystyle r_{4}^{{6}}(N)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{24}r_{4}^{\square}(32N+16)+O(N^{3/4+\varepsilon}),

\displaystyle r_{8}^{{5}}(N)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{78705}r_{8}^{\square}(24N+8)+O(N^{7/4+\varepsilon}).

The first relation above is particularly special because it turns out that its error is actually zero (this is not true for the second relation); that is, we have the exact identity
r_{4}^{{6}}(N)={(24)}^{-1}r_{4}^{\square}(32N+16)
. We defer the proof and further discussion of such exact identities to Section
5
.

By Theorem
1.1
, for
n\geq 4
, the problem of estimating the
m
th
moment of
r_{n}^{{k}}(N)
is reduced to estimating the
m
th
moment of
r_{n}^{\square}(X_{N})
possibly twisted by a weight such as
(-1)^{N}
. We have, as an example, estimated the second moment of
r_{4}^{{k}}
for all
k\geq 3
using this reduction. We stress that there is no barrier in particular for estimating the moments of
r_{n}^{{k}}
for
n>4
, and that we have merely chosen our example to have
n=4
because of the relative simplicity and because it generalizes the known asymptotics of
\sum_{N\leq x}r_{4}^{\square}(N)^{2}
.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
k\geq 3
and
p_{1},\dots,p_{r}
be the odd prime divisors of
k-2
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{N\leq x}r_{4}^{{k}}(N)^{2}=\frac{C_{k}\zeta(3)}{(k-2)^{4}}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{1-p_{i}^{-3}}{1-p_{i}^{-4}}x^{3}+O(x^{11/4+\varepsilon}),

(3)

where
C_{k}=448
if
k\not\equiv 0\pmod{4}
and
C_{k}=512
if
k\equiv 0\pmod{4}
.

We suspect that the error in (
3
) can be improved, and suggest it as a possible direction for future work. We have opted to compute a second moment (as opposed to a first moment) for our example since the estimation of the first moments is essentially a generalized Gauss circle problem, which is simpler than using Theorem
1.1
. Indeed, for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, we have that
\sum_{N\leq x}r_{n}^{{k}}(N)
equals

\displaystyle\#\left\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{k}(y_{i})\leq x\right\}=\#\left\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_{i}-\frac{k-4}{2(k-2)}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{2x}{k-2}+\frac{n(k-4)^{2}}{4(k-2)^{2}}\right\},

which is equal to
(\pi^{n/2}/\Gamma(n/2+1))(2x/(k-2))^{n/2}+O(x^{(n-1)/2})
. To see that this asymptotic formula indeed holds, see, for example,
[
42
, Theorem 2.4 and Theorem 2.6]
and take the lattice
\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and the set

\displaystyle S=\left\{\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(t_{i}-\frac{k-4}{2(k-2)}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{2x}{k-2}+\frac{n(k-4)^{2}}{4(k-2)^{2}}\right\}.

Of course, if one follows the same strategy as the proof of Corollary
1.2
, one will obtain
\sum_{N\leq x}r_{4}^{{k}}(N)=({2\pi^{2}}/{(k-2)^{2}})x^{2}+O(x^{7/4+\varepsilon})
, in accordance with the above, but with a worse error estimate. However, when estimating the
m
th
moment of
r_{n}^{{k}}(N)
where
m\geq 2
, one loses the simpler generalized Gauss circle problem interpretation, hence our choice to exemplify the use of Theorem
1.1
to obtain (
3
).

A result of Bringmann, Jang, Kane, and Tse
[
7
, Theorem 1.1]
asserts that
r_{4,+}^{{k}}(N)=2^{-4}r_{4}^{{k}}(N)+O(N^{15/16+\varepsilon})
. By this result, we obtain the following corollary of Theorem
1.1
.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
k\geq 3
and
p_{1},\dots,p_{r}
be the odd prime divisors of
k-2
. Then the number
r_{4,+}^{{k}}(N)
of representations of
N
as the sum of four ordinary
k
-gonal numbers satisfies

\displaystyle r_{4,+}^{{k}}(N)=\frac{A_{N,k}}{24(k-2)^{3}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{p_{i}^{2}}{p_{i}^{2}-1}\right)r_{4}^{\square}(8(k-2)N+4(k-4)^{2})+O(N^{15/16+\varepsilon}),

where
A_{N,k}=1
if
k
is odd,
A_{N,k}=4
if
k\equiv 2\pmod{4}
, and
A_{N,k}=8+4(-1)^{N}
if
k\equiv 0\pmod{4}
.

From Corollary
1.3
, we recover as special cases
[
7
, Corollary 1.2]
and
[
7
, Corollary 1.4]
, which assert respectively that

\displaystyle r_{4,+}^{{6}}(N)=\frac{1}{16}\sigma(2N+1)+O(N^{15/16+\varepsilon}),

\displaystyle r_{4,+}^{{5}}(N)=\frac{1}{24}\sigma(6N+1)+O(N^{15/16+\varepsilon}).

These formulas follow immediately from Corollary
1.3
upon noting the identity
r_{4}^{\square}(2N)=8\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}4\nmid d\mid 2N\end{subarray}}d=8\sum_{d\mid N_{2}}(d+2d)=24\sigma(N_{2})
, where
N_{2}=2^{-v_{2}(N)}N
is the odd part of
N
.

In addition to Corollary
1.3
, we note that, in view of Corollary
1.2
, the relation
r_{4,+}^{{k}}(N)=2^{-4}r_{4}^{{k}}(N)+O(N^{15/16+\varepsilon})
gives the formula

\displaystyle\sum_{N\leq x}r_{4,+}^{{k}}(N)^{2}=\frac{C_{k}\zeta(3)}{256(k-2)^{4}}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{1-p_{i}^{-3}}{1-p_{i}^{-4}}x^{3}+O(x^{47/16+\varepsilon}),

where we have the error of
O(x^{47/16+\varepsilon})
since
\sum_{N\leq x}r_{4}^{k}(N)N^{15/16+\varepsilon}\ll x^{47/16+\varepsilon}
, which follows by summation by parts since
\sum_{N\leq x}r_{4}^{k}(N)\ll x^{2}
. We do remark, however, that we do not need
[
7
, Theorem 1.1]
to estimate the first moment
\sum_{N\leq x}r_{4,+}^{{k}}(N)
. In fact, it is not too difficult to see that by modifying the generalized Gauss circle problem methodology outlined earlier, one can show that for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{N\leq x}r_{n,+}^{{k}}(N)=\frac{\pi^{n/2}}{\Gamma(n/2+1)}\left(\frac{x}{2(k-2)}\right)^{n/2}+O(x^{(n-1)/2}).

Through direct analysis of the main term from Corollary
1.3
, we obtain the following result governing which subsequences of positive integers
r_{4,+}^{{k}}
may be bounded on (if any).

Corollary 1.4
.

If
k\equiv 0\pmod{4}
, then any strictly increasing infinite subsequence of positive integers on which
r_{4,+}^{{k}}
is bounded converges
2
-adically to
(k-4)^{2}/(4-2k)\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. If
k\not\equiv 0\pmod{4}
, then there is no strictly increasing infinite subsequence of positive integers on which
r_{4,+}^{{k}}
is bounded.

For
k\equiv 0\pmod{4}
and
r=(k-4)/4>0
, the aforementioned result of Meng and Sun
[
31
]
asserts that for all
N\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
,

\displaystyle r_{4,+}^{{k}}\left(4r^{2}\frac{4^{N\phi(2r+1)}-1}{2r+1}\right)=0.

(4)

The
k\equiv 0\pmod{4}
case of Corollary
1.4
can thus be viewed as a supplement to (
4
) upon observing that the strictly increasing infinite subsequence
4r^{2}(4^{N\phi(2r+1)}-1)/(2r+1)
does indeed converge
2
-adically to
(k-4)^{2}/(4-2k)
. It would be interesting to obtain more results on the subsequences on which
r_{4,+}^{{k}}
is bounded, and we suggest it as a possible direction for future work.

In a landmark paper, Heath-Brown
[
17
]
developed a variant of the circle method for problems on quadratic forms (and in a later paper
[
18
]
, cubic forms). We obtain Theorem
1.1
by modifying the Heath-Brown circle method to count solutions to the sum of
n
squares equation satisfying a fixed set of congruence equations. Setting notation, for
\mathbf{k}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and
M\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, we define
r_{n}^{\equiv}(N)
to be the number of solutions
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
to
\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}=N
such that each
x_{i}\equiv k_{i}\bmod{M}
. We use the symbol
\mathbf{k}
because we later take
\mathbf{k}=(k,\dots,k)
in the pursuit of obtaining Theorem
1.1
, but for now, we leave
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
arbitrary. We are interested in
r_{n}^{\equiv}(N)
because it allows us to count solutions to quadratic Diophantine equations of shape
\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q(x_{i})=N
, where
Q(x)=ax^{2}+bx+c
is integral-valued on
\mathbb{Z}
. Indeed, beginning with the equation
\sum_{i=1}^{n}(ax_{i}^{2}+bx_{i}+c)=N
and completing the square gives
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(4ax_{i}+2b\right)^{2}=16aN+4nb^{2}-16anc
, whence we have Remark
1.5
.

Remark 1.5
.

Let
Q(x)=ax^{2}+bx+c
be integral-valued on
\mathbb{Z}
. Let
\mathbf{k}=(2b,\dots,2b)
and
M=4a
. Then the number
r_{n}^{Q}(N)
of solutions to
\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q(x_{i})=N
over
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
equals
r_{n}^{\equiv}(16aN+4nb^{2}-16anc)
.

In Theorem
2.14
, we relate
r_{n}^{\equiv}(N)
to
r_{n}^{\square}(N)
for arbitrary
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and
M\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. So by Remark
1.5
, our methods succeed in proving an asymptotic relation between
r_{n}^{Q}(N)
and
r_{n}^{\square}(N)
, which is in closed form up to evaluating the
p
-adic densities of the Diophantine equation
\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q(x_{i})=N
for
p
dividing
4a
. Theorem
1.1
then results from the special case of taking
Q(x)=p_{k}(x)
and computing the polygonal
p
-adic densities. In particular, we have the following more general result due to Theorem
2.14
and Remark
1.5
.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
Q(x)=ax^{2}+bx+c
be integral-valued on
\mathbb{Z}
. Let
n\geq 4
and for
N\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, let
q_{N}=16aN+4nb^{2}-16anc
. Then the number
r_{n}^{Q}(N)
of solutions over
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
to
\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q(x_{i})=N
satisfies

\displaystyle r_{n}^{Q}(N)=\frac{(4a)^{-n}}{\mathcal{N}_{n}(q_{N},2a)}\left(\prod_{p\mid 4a}\delta_{p}^{Q}(N,n)\right)r_{n}^{\square}(q_{N})+O(N^{(n-1)/4+\varepsilon}),

where
\mathcal{N}_{n}
is as in (
2
), and
\delta_{p}^{Q}(N,n)
is the
p
-adic density

\displaystyle\delta_{p}^{Q}(N,n)=\lim_{d\to\infty}p^{-d(n-1)}{\#\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\left(\mathbb{Z}/p^{d+v_{p}(4a)}\mathbb{Z}\right)^{n}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}\equiv q_{N}\bmod p^{d},~x_{i}\equiv 2b\bmod p^{v_{p}(4a)}\right\}}.

Once one calculates each
\delta_{p}^{Q}(N,n)
for
p\mid 4a
, one can use Theorem
1.6
to estimate the moments of
r_{n}^{Q}
if
n\geq 4
, as we did in Corollary
1.2
for the second moment of
r_{4}^{{k}}
using Theorem
1.1
. However, we remark that, once again, the first moment can be handled by the same generalized Gauss circle problem methodology as before to obtain
\sum_{N\leq x}r_{n}^{Q}(N)=(\pi^{n/2}/\Gamma(n/2+1))(x/a)^{n/2}+O(x^{(n-1)/2})
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
.

In Section
2
, we modify the Heath-Brown circle method to obtain Theorem
2.14
, relating
r_{n}^{\equiv}(N)
to
r_{n}^{\square}(N)
. In Section
3
, we compute the square
p
-adic densities (which we note may be of independent interest) and the polygonal
p
-adic densities. Then, in Section
4
, we prove Theorems
1.1
and
1.6
and Corollaries
1.2
,
1.3
, and

2.
Modifying the Heath-Brown Circle Method

We proceed by merging the ideas of works
[
17
]
and
[
19
]
of Heath-Brown. Letting
F
be a positive-definite quadratic form in
n\geq 4
variables,
[
17
, Corollary 1]
states that the number
r_{F}(N)
of solutions to
F(\mathbf{x})=N
satisfies

\displaystyle r_{F}(N)=\left(\sigma_{\infty}\prod_{p}\sigma_{p}^{F}(N)\right)N^{n/2-1}+O(N^{(n-1)/4+\varepsilon}),

(5)

where
\sigma_{\infty}
is the singular integral and
\sigma_{p}^{F}(N)
is the
p
-adic density of the Diophantine equation
F(\mathbf{x})=N
. This is essentially the same result (albeit with better error) as Kloosterman
[
24
]
gave in his refinement of the Hardy–Littlewood circle method, prompting the introduction of the Kloosterman sum.

By Remark
1.5
, we are interested in counting solutions to the sum of
n
squares problem that satisfy a fixed set of congruence equations. The principal upside of Heath-Brown’s circle method, as it pertains to our work, is that the methods used are conducive to modification by imposing congruence conditions upon the solution set of the quadratic Diophantine equation in question. The essential question is then: for
F(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}
, how does the congruence modification affect formula (
5
)? This is where Heath-Brown’s other work,
[
19
]
, comes into play. In
[
19
]
, the number of solutions
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
to
F(\mathbf{x})=0
is explored, where
\mathcal{B}
is a box of size depending on a variable
B
and whose elements all satisfy a fixed
n
-dimensional congruence equation. However,
[
19
]
uses a traditional form of the circle method without a Kloosterman refinement, thus requiring
n\geq 5
.

By using some of the methods from
[
19
]
, we modify formula (
5
) from
[
17
]
to count solutions satisfying a fixed
n
-dimensional congruence equation for the form
F(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}
, yielding an asymptotic formula for
r_{n}^{\equiv}(N)
. We find, under the congruence modification, that the singular integral is invariant and that the Euler product of
p
-adic densities changes only at the prime factors of the modulus of the congruence (the asymptotics, including the error up to a constant, are otherwise unaffected; see Theorem
2.14
). From here, to obtain Theorems
1.1
and
1.6
, it remains to compute the
p
-adic densities of
\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}=N
with and without the congruence restriction, which is the purpose of Section
3
.

By Remark

, the problem of asymptotically relating
r_{n}^{Q}
and

reduces to determining the proportion of solutions
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
to the sum of
n

for all

. To understand how we can handle calculating this proportion, it is prudent to understand the core ideas of the Heath-Brown circle method, which can be synopsized as a weighted-approximation version of
the circle method driven by the
\delta
-method of Duke, Friedlander, and Iwaniec
[
9
]
.

If we wish to approximate some counting function
\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{1}_{S}(\mathbf{x})
for some set
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, it is typical to instead consider the weighted counting function
\sum_{\mathbf{x}}W(\mathbf{x})
, where

is some weight function
1
1

1

For our purposes, weight functions will be infinitely differentiable bounded functions with compact support in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. However, to obtain the desired asymptotic results derived from Proposition
2.1
, one must further require that the weight function is in the class
\mathscr{C}(S)
defined in
[
17
, §2]
.
that approximates the characteristic function
\mathbf{1}_{S}
of
S
. In this vein, for
F(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
, instead of approximating
\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\mathbf{y}:F(\mathbf{y})=0\}}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\mathbf{x}:F(\mathbf{x})=0}1
as done by other forms of the circle method, Heath-Brown approximates
\sum_{\mathbf{x}:F(\mathbf{x})=0}W(P^{-1}\mathbf{x})
, where

is a variable that we are later interested in taking asymptotically.

In
[
9
]
, Duke, Friedlander, and Iwaniec provide a series formula for the Kronecker delta
\delta_{m,0}
, developed as an alternative to the circle method. We refer the interested reader to
[
10
]
and
[
11
]
for some further applications of the
\delta
-method. After some minor tweaks to this formula for
\delta_{m,0}
and writing
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}:F(\mathbf{x})=0\end{subarray}}W(P^{-1}\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\end{subarray}}W(P^{-1}\mathbf{x})\delta_{F(\mathbf{x}),0}
, Heath-Brown
[
17
]
obtains Proposition
2.1
, which is the basis for all the results of the Heath-Brown circle method.

Proposition 2.1

(
[
17
, Theorem 2]
)
.

Let
W
be an infinitely differentiable bounded function with compact support in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
F(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
. Let
Q>1
and
q\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and

\displaystyle S_{q}(\mathbf{c})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\bmod q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{b}\bmod{q}\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{aF_{N}(\mathbf{b})+\mathbf{c\cdot b}}{q}\right)

and

\displaystyle J_{q}(\mathbf{c})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}W\left(P^{-1}{\mathbf{x}}{}\right)h(Q^{-1}q,Q^{-2}F(\mathbf{x}))~e\left(-\frac{\mathbf{c\cdot x}}{q}\right)d\mathbf{x}.

Then

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\\
F(\mathbf{x})=0\end{subarray}}W(P^{-1}\mathbf{x})=c_{Q}Q^{-2}\sum_{\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\sum_{q\geq 1}q^{-n}S_{q}(\mathbf{c})J_{q}(\mathbf{c}),

where
c_{Q}
satisfies
c_{Q}=1+O_{A}(Q^{-A})
for any
A>0
and
h\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathbb{R})
is as defined in
[
17
, pg.166]
.

We will not need to modify the function
h
whatsoever, so we omit its discussion and analysis. It is essential to point out that for a particular choice of weight function
W
, the weighted counting function
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}:F(\mathbf{x})=0\end{subarray}}W(P^{-1}\mathbf{x})
is equal to the unweighted counting function
\sum_{\mathbf{x}:F(\mathbf{x})=0}1
. This is how Heath-Brown recovers formula (
5
), which was previously attained by standard versions of the circle method. Imposing the condition
\mathbf{x}\equiv\mathbf{k}\pmod{M}
on these sums with the same weight function will lead us to the desired analog of Proposition
2.1
, giving a formula for
r_{n}^{\equiv}(N)
. Since we are not handling weighted counting functions, the reason we opt for the Heath-Brown circle method is that modifying Proposition
2.1
by imposing the condition
\mathbf{x}\equiv\mathbf{k}\pmod{M}
is straightforward and essentially all of the resulting estimates hold similarly to the unmodified case. It is the purpose of this section, starting with Subsection
2.2
, to verify this. Before stating the analog of Proposition
2.1
, we set important notation.

Let
F_{N}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}-N
and
w_{0}(x)
be defined by
w_{0}(x)=\exp(-(1-x^{2})^{-1})
if
|x|<1
and
w_{0}(x)=0
if
|x|\geq 1
. Then define
w(\mathbf{x})=e\cdot w_{0}(2(F_{0}(\mathbf{x})-1))
. This is the weight function that makes our weighted counting function equal to the unweighted counting function, and is given in
[
17
, pg.203]
. Set
P=\sqrt{N}
. For our purposes, we will take
Q=P
but continue to use the symbol
Q
as a relic of the more general setting of dealing with forms of degree
d
(wherein one should take
Q=P^{d/2}
).

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
Q>1
and
q\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and

\displaystyle S_{q}^{\equiv}(\mathbf{c})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\bmod q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{b}\bmod{Mq}\\
\mathbf{b}\equiv\mathbf{k}\bmod M\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{aF_{N}(\mathbf{b})}{q}+\frac{\mathbf{c\cdot b}}{Mq}\right)

and

\displaystyle I_{q}^{\equiv}(\mathbf{c})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}w\left(P^{-1}{\mathbf{x}}{}\right)h(Q^{-1}q,Q^{-2}F_{N}(\mathbf{x}))~e\left(-\frac{\mathbf{c\cdot x}}{Mq}\right)d\mathbf{x}.

Then

\displaystyle r_{n}^{\equiv}(N)=c_{Q}Q^{-2}\sum_{\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\sum_{q\geq 1}(Mq)^{-n}S_{q}^{\equiv}(\mathbf{c})I_{q}^{\equiv}(\mathbf{c}),

(6)

where
c_{Q}
satisfies
c_{Q}=1+O_{A}(Q^{-A})
for any
A>0
and
h\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathbb{R})
is as defined in
[
17
, pg.166]
.

Proof.

The proof of this theorem is a straightforward generalization of the proof of
[
17
, Theorem 2]
for our choice of weight function. Let
\delta_{m,0}
be the Kronecker delta. Note that
w(P^{-1}\mathbf{x})=e\cdot w_{0}(2(N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}-1))
equals
1
if and only if
F_{N}(\mathbf{x})=0
. Thus,

\displaystyle r_{n}^{\equiv}(N)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\\
F_{N}(\mathbf{x})=0\\
\mathbf{x}\equiv\mathbf{k}\bmod M\end{subarray}}w(P^{-1}\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\\
\mathbf{x}\equiv\mathbf{k}\bmod M\end{subarray}}w(P^{-1}\mathbf{x})\delta_{F_{N}(\mathbf{x}),0}.

Invoking
[
17
, Theorem 1]
, we obtain

\displaystyle r_{n}^{\equiv}(N)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\\
\mathbf{x}\equiv\mathbf{k}\bmod M\end{subarray}}w(P^{-1}\mathbf{x})\left(c_{Q}Q^{-2}\sum_{q\geq 1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\bmod q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{aF_{N}(\mathbf{x})}{q}\right)h(Q^{-1}q,Q^{-2}F_{N}(\mathbf{x}))\right)

\displaystyle=c_{Q}Q^{-2}\sum_{q\geq 1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\bmod q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\\
\mathbf{x}\equiv\mathbf{k}\bmod M\end{subarray}}w(P^{-1}\mathbf{x})e\left(\frac{aF_{N}(\mathbf{x})}{q}\right)h(Q^{-1}q,Q^{-2}F_{N}(\mathbf{x}))

\displaystyle=c_{Q}Q^{-2}\sum_{q\geq 1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\bmod q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{b}\bmod Mq\\
\mathbf{b}\equiv\mathbf{k}\bmod M\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{aF_{N}(\mathbf{b})}{q}\right)\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}f(\mathbf{y}),

where
f(\mathbf{y})=w(\mathbf{b}+Mq\mathbf{y})h(Q^{-1}q,Q^{-2}F_{N}(\mathbf{b}+Mq\mathbf{y}))
. The Poisson summation formula yields
\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}f(\mathbf{y})=\sum_{\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\hat{f}(\mathbf{c})
, where

\displaystyle\hat{f}(\mathbf{c})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(\mathbf{y})e(-\mathbf{c\cdot b})d\mathbf{y}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}w(\mathbf{b}+Mq\mathbf{y})h(Q^{-1}q,Q^{-2}F_{N}(\mathbf{b}+Mq\mathbf{y}))e(-\mathbf{c\cdot b})d\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=e\left(\frac{\mathbf{c\cdot b}}{Mq}\right)\frac{1}{(Mq)^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}w(P^{-1}\mathbf{x})h(Q^{-1}q,Q^{-2}F_{N}(\mathbf{x}))e\left(-\frac{\mathbf{c\cdot x}}{Mq}\right)d\mathbf{x}.

Substituting back into our original equation then gives the desired formula.
∎

To get an analogous form of (
5
) for

, we directly estimate (

). The leading-order term comes from the terms of (
6
) with

and

. In this regime,

grows like

times the singular integral, and as expected, the sum of

may be approximated by the singular series (the error of this approximation by the singular series will be absorbed into the

error). When

, the contribution is negligible for all

, as is the contribution when

(for all

still hold.

2.2.
Estimating
I^{\equiv}_{q}(\mathbf{c})

Let
I_{q}(\mathbf{c})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}w\left(P^{-1}{\mathbf{x}}{}\right)h(Q^{-1}q,Q^{-2}F_{N}(\mathbf{x}))e\left(-{\mathbf{c\cdot x}}/q\right)d\mathbf{x}
, where
q\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, as defined in
[
17
]
. By the relation

given in
[
17

hold immediately for

I_{q}
being integral is not necessary for the analysis, and it is thus acceptable to apply Heath-Brown’s results to the argument
\mathbf{c}/M
. Since the leading-order term of (

The following lemma allows us to reduce the series over all

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
q\geq P
and
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. Then
I_{q}^{\equiv}(\mathbf{c})=0
.

Proof.

Since
q\geq P
, note that
q/Q=q/P\geq 1
. Suppose that
|N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}-1|\leq 1/2
. Then

\displaystyle|Q^{-2}F_{N}(\mathbf{x})|=\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}-1\right|\leq 1/2\leq\frac{q/Q}{2},

whence
h(Q^{-1}q,Q^{-2}F_{N}(\mathbf{x}))=0
by
[
17
, Lemma 4]
. On the other hand, if
|N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}-1|>1/2
, then
w(P^{-1}\mathbf{x})=e\cdot w_{0}(2(N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}-1))=0
. So
I^{\equiv}_{q}(\mathbf{c})=0
.
∎

Let
G(\mathbf{x})=F_{0}(\mathbf{x})-1
. As noted by Heath-Brown
[
17
, pg.204]
, we remark that since
w(\mathbf{x})=1+O(\varepsilon)
for

\displaystyle\sigma_{\infty}(G,w)\vcentcolon=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\int_{|G(\mathbf{x})|\leq\varepsilon}w(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\int_{|G(\mathbf{x})|\leq\varepsilon}d\mathbf{x}=\vcentcolon\sigma_{\infty}(G).

This is the singular integral. By the equality of
\sigma_{\infty}(G,w)
and
\sigma_{\infty}(G)
, we have the following estimate by
[
17
, Lemma 13]
.

Lemma 2.4

(
[
17
, Lemma 13]
)
.

Suppose that
q\ll Q
. Then for any
A>0
,

\displaystyle I^{\equiv}_{q}(\mathbf{0})=I_{q}(\mathbf{0})=P^{n}\left(\sigma_{\infty}(G)+O_{A}\left(\left(q/Q\right)^{A}\right)\right).

We digress that we can compute
\sigma_{\infty}(G)
explicitly, though we do not need to for our purposes. Indeed, the integral in the definition of
\sigma_{\infty}(G)
is simply the difference of the volumes of two
n
-dimensional balls of radii
\sqrt{1+\varepsilon}
and
\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}
. Since
(2\varepsilon)^{-1}((1+\varepsilon)^{n/2}-(1-\varepsilon)^{n/2})
tends to

as
\varepsilon\to 0
, we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{\infty}(G)=\frac{n}{2}\frac{\pi^{n/2}}{\Gamma(n/2+1)}=\frac{\pi^{n/2}}{\Gamma(n/2)},

recovering the constant given in (

) and as noted by Bateman
[
4
]
, for example. This differs from the constant
\Gamma(1+1/2)^{n}/\Gamma(n/2)
seen in Hua’s result
[
20
]
on Waring’s problem (regarding squares in this case) by a factor of
2^{n}
, which comes from the fact that we are counting solutions over
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
rather than
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
. A more recent illustration of Hua’s result may be seen in
[
21
, §20.2]
.

When
q\ll Q
, the coefficient of the leading-order term of
I_{q}^{\equiv}(\mathbf{0})
ultimately corresponds to the coefficient of the leading-order term of (
6
), so determining it to be exactly the singular integral was important. However, terms with

outside this range will be absorbed into the error, so it is sufficient to leave the asymptotics of
I_{q}^{\equiv}(\mathbf{0})
up to a constant in this case.

Lemma 2.5

(
[
17
, Lemma 16]
)
.

For any
q
, we have the bound

\displaystyle I^{\equiv}_{q}(\mathbf{0})=I_{q}(\mathbf{0})\ll P^{n}.

The next two lemmas handle bounding
I_{q}^{\equiv}(\mathbf{c})
when
\mathbf{c}\neq\mathbf{0}
(with no restriction on
q
). The first will be used when
|\mathbf{c}|>P^{\varepsilon}
, wherein having the savings factor

for all

is essential.

Lemma 2.6

(
[
17
, Lemma 19]
)
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{c}\neq 0
. Then for any
A>0
,

\displaystyle I^{\equiv}_{q}(\mathbf{c})=I_{q}(\mathbf{c}/M)\ll_{A}P^{n+1}q^{-1}|\mathbf{c}/M|^{-A}\ll P^{n+1}q^{-1}|\mathbf{c}|^{-A}.

For
\mathbf{c}\ll P^{\varepsilon}
, we will use the following lemma. Since the bound is independent of
\mathbf{c}
, the estimation of (
6
) is essentially reduced (using a dyadic summation argument, see
[
17
, pg.200]
) to estimating
\sum_{q\leq X}|S_{q}^{\equiv}(\mathbf{c})|
, which is handled by Lemma

.

Lemma 2.7
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{c}\neq 0
and
\mathbf{c}\ll P^{\varepsilon}
. Then

\displaystyle I_{q}^{\equiv}(\mathbf{c})\ll P^{n/2+1+\varepsilon}q^{n/2-1}.

Proof.

By
[
17
, Lemma 22]
,

\displaystyle I^{\equiv}_{q}(\mathbf{c})=I_{q}(\mathbf{c}/M)\ll P^{n}\left(\frac{PQ|\mathbf{c}/M|}{q^{2}}\right)^{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{P|\mathbf{c}/M|}{q}\right)^{1-n/2}\ll P^{n}\left(\frac{PQ|\mathbf{c}|}{q^{2}}\right)^{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{P|\mathbf{c}|}{q}\right)^{1-n/2}.

Recalling that
Q=P
, since
\mathbf{c}\ll P^{\varepsilon}
, we have
(PQ|\mathbf{c}|/q^{2})^{\varepsilon}\ll(P^{2+\varepsilon}/q^{2})^{\varepsilon}
, which is

, we have

∎

2.3.
Estimating

We now move on to estimating

The multiplicative property of

Definition 2.8

Let

\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. Then we define

\displaystyle S_{q}^{\equiv}(T,\mathbf{c})\vcentcolon=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\bmod q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{b}\bmod{Tq}\\
\mathbf{b}\equiv\mathbf{k}\bmod T\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{aF_{N}(\mathbf{b})}{q}+\frac{\mathbf{c\cdot b}}{Tq}\right).

When
T=M
, we write
S_{q}^{\equiv}(T,\mathbf{c})=S_{q}^{\equiv}(\mathbf{c})
, in agreement with the notation in Proposition
2.2
.

We will use the following lemma to bound (
6
) when
|c|>P^{\varepsilon}
and to prove Lemma
2.12
. The proof follows by an application of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to the dot product.

Lemma 2.9
.

For any
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and
T\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, we have
S_{q}^{\equiv}(T,\mathbf{c})\ll q^{1+n/2}
.

Proof.

This result and its proof are direct modifications of
[
17
, Lemma 25]
. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle|S_{q}^{\equiv}(T,\mathbf{c})|^{2}=\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\bmod q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{b}\bmod{Tq}\\
\mathbf{b}\equiv\mathbf{k}\bmod T\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{aF_{N}(\mathbf{b})}{q}+\frac{\mathbf{c\cdot b}}{Tq}\right)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\bmod q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}1\right)\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\bmod q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{b}\bmod{Tq}\\
\mathbf{b}\equiv\mathbf{k}\bmod T\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{aF_{N}(\mathbf{b})}{q}+\frac{\mathbf{c\cdot b}}{Tq}\right)\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\phi(q)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\bmod q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{u}\bmod{Tq}\\
\mathbf{u}\equiv\mathbf{k}\bmod T\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{aF_{N}(\mathbf{u})}{q}+\frac{\mathbf{c\cdot u}}{Tq}\right)\right)\overline{\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{v}\bmod{Tq}\\
\mathbf{v}\equiv\mathbf{k}\bmod T\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{aF_{N}(\mathbf{v})}{q}+\frac{\mathbf{c\cdot v}}{Tq}\right)\right)}

\displaystyle=\phi(q)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\bmod q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\bmod{Tq}\\
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\equiv\mathbf{k}\bmod{T}\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{a(F_{N}(\mathbf{u})-F_{N}(\mathbf{v}))}{q}+\frac{\mathbf{c\cdot\phantom{}}(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v})}{Tq}\right).

By the substitution
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{d}+\mathbf{v}
, the above is equal to

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\phi(q)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\bmod q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{d},\mathbf{v}\bmod{Tq}\\
\mathbf{v}\equiv\mathbf{k}\bmod{T}\\
T\mid\mathbf{d}\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{a(F_{N}(\mathbf{\mathbf{d}+\mathbf{v}})-F_{N}(\mathbf{v}))}{q}+\frac{\mathbf{c\cdot d}}{Tq}\right)

\displaystyle=\phi(q)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\bmod q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{d}\bmod{Tq}\\
T\mid\mathbf{d}\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{aF_{0}(\mathbf{d})}{q}+\frac{\mathbf{c\cdot d}}{Tq}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{v}\bmod{Tq}\\
\mathbf{v}\equiv\mathbf{k}\bmod{T}\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{2a\mathbf{v\cdot d}}{q}\right)

\displaystyle=\phi(q)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\bmod q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{d}\bmod{Tq}\\
T\mid\mathbf{d}\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{aF_{0}(\mathbf{d})}{q}+\frac{\mathbf{c\cdot d}}{Tq}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{v}\bmod{q}\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{2a(T\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{k})\mathbf{\phantom{}\cdot d}}{q}\right)

\displaystyle=\phi(q)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\bmod q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{d^{\prime}}\bmod{q}\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{a(F_{0}(T\mathbf{d^{\prime}})+2T\mathbf{k\cdot d^{\prime}})+\mathbf{c\cdot d^{\prime}}}{q}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{v}\bmod{q}\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{2aT^{2}\mathbf{v}\mathbf{\phantom{}\cdot d^{\prime}}}{q}\right)

Since
(a,q)=1
, the inner summation over
\mathbf{v}
equals
q^{n}
when
q\mid 2T^{2}\mathbf{d^{\prime}}
, and is zero otherwise. Since the number of
\mathbf{d^{\prime}}\pmod{q}
satisfying this property is bounded in
q
, there exists some
K
that is constant in
q
such that the above equation is at most

\displaystyle\phi(q)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\bmod q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}Kq^{n}=K\phi(q)^{2}q^{n}\ll q^{2+n}.

So
S_{q}^{\equiv}(T,\mathbf{c})\ll q^{1+n/2}
.
∎

The following lemma demonstrates the multiplicative property of
S_{q}^{\equiv}(T,\mathbf{c})
, generalizing
[
19
, Lemma 2.1]
, which only considers
\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{0}
and
N=0
, and
[
17
, Lemma 23]
, which only considers
T=1
. The sole purpose of Lemma
2.10
is to ultimately improve Lemma
2.9
for use in Lemma
2.12
. Typically, we would also need the multiplicative property to prove that
\sum_{q\geq 1}q^{-n}S_{q}^{\equiv}(\mathbf{0})
admits a representation as an Euler product of the appropriate
p
-adic densities; however, since the singular series will come from the terms of (
6
) with
\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{0}
,
[
19
, Lemma 2.2]
essentially already handles this (the non-homogeneous case where
N\neq 0
is a minor modification).

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
q=rs
and
T=j\ell
such that
(rj,s\ell)=1
. Let
\bar{r}
and
\bar{j}
be the multiplicative inverses of
r
and
j
modulo
s\ell
, respectively. Let
\bar{s}
and
\bar{\ell}
be the multiplicative inverses of
s
and
\ell
modulo
rj
, respectively. Then

\displaystyle S_{q}^{\equiv}(T,\mathbf{c})=S_{r}^{\equiv}(j,\bar{s}\bar{\ell}\mathbf{c})S_{s}^{\equiv}(\ell,\bar{r}\bar{j}\mathbf{c}).

Proof.

We closely follow the proof of
[
19
, Lemma 2.1]
. Let

\displaystyle S_{q}(a,T,\mathbf{c})\vcentcolon=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{b}\bmod{Tq}\\
\mathbf{b}\equiv\mathbf{k}\bmod T\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{aF_{N}(\mathbf{b})}{q}+\frac{\mathbf{c\cdot b}}{Tq}\right).

We proceed by first proving that
S_{q}(a,T,\mathbf{c})=S_{r}(\bar{s}a,j,\bar{s}\bar{\ell}\mathbf{c})S_{s}(\bar{r}a,\ell,\bar{r}\bar{j}\mathbf{c})
. Since the relationship is delicate, we provide a great amount of detail.

Let
U=\{\mathbf{u}\in(\mathbb{Z}/rj\mathbb{Z})^{n}:\mathbf{u}\equiv\mathbf{k}\bmod j\}
and
V=\{\mathbf{v}\in(\mathbb{Z}/s\ell\mathbb{Z})^{n}:\mathbf{v}\equiv\mathbf{k}\bmod\ell\}
and
B=\{\mathbf{b}\in(\mathbb{Z}/Tq\mathbb{Z})^{n}:\mathbf{b}\equiv\mathbf{k}\bmod{T}\}
. We claim that
\rho\colon U\times V\to B
defined by
\rho(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})=s\ell\bar{s}\bar{\ell}\mathbf{u}+rj\bar{r}\bar{j}\mathbf{v}
is a bijection. We first confirm that
\rho
maps to
B
. Indeed, by the congruences
s\ell\bar{s}\bar{\ell}\equiv 1\pmod{j}
and
s\ell\bar{s}\bar{\ell}\equiv 0\pmod{\ell}
and
rj\bar{r}\bar{j}\equiv 0\pmod{j}
and
rj\bar{r}\bar{j}\equiv 1\pmod{\ell}
, we see that
\rho(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})
is congruent to
\mathbf{k}
modulo
j
and modulo
\ell
, whence it follows that
\rho(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})\equiv\mathbf{k}\pmod{T}
.

Suppose that
s\ell\bar{s}\bar{\ell}\mathbf{u}_{1}+rj\bar{r}\bar{j}\mathbf{v}_{1}\equiv s\ell\bar{s}\bar{\ell}\mathbf{u}_{2}+rj\bar{r}\bar{j}\mathbf{v}_{2}\pmod{Tq}
. Then
s\ell\bar{s}\bar{\ell}(\mathbf{u}_{1}-\mathbf{u}_{2})\equiv rj\bar{r}\bar{j}(\mathbf{v}_{2}-\mathbf{v}_{1})\pmod{Tq}
, from which it follows by reducing modulo
rj
and
s\ell
that
\mathbf{u}_{1}-\mathbf{u}_{2}\equiv\mathbf{0}\pmod{rj}
and
\mathbf{v}_{2}-\mathbf{v}_{1}\equiv\mathbf{0}\pmod{s\ell}
. So
\rho
is injective. Noting that
|U|\cdot|V|=(rj/j)\cdot(s\ell/\ell)=q=Tq/T=|B|
, it follows that
\rho
is also bijective.

Let
\mathbf{b}=\rho(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})
. Observe that

\displaystyle F_{N}(\mathbf{b})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(s\ell\bar{s}\bar{\ell}u_{i}+rj\bar{r}\bar{j}v_{i}\right)^{2}-N=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left((s\ell\bar{s}\bar{\ell}u_{i})^{2}+2Tq\bar{s}\bar{\ell}\bar{r}\bar{j}u_{i}v_{i}+(rj\bar{r}\bar{j}v_{i})^{2}\right)-N

\displaystyle\equiv(s\ell\bar{s}\bar{\ell})^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{2}+(rj\bar{r}\bar{j})^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}^{2}-N\pmod{q}.

Now since
\ell\bar{\ell}
and
s\bar{s}
are both congruent to
1
modulo
r
, we have
(s\ell\bar{s}\bar{\ell})^{2}\equiv(s\bar{s})^{2}\equiv s\bar{s}\pmod{r}
. Similarly, since
j\bar{j}
and
r\bar{r}
are both congruent to
1
modulo
s
, we have
(rj\bar{r}\bar{j})^{2}\equiv(r\bar{r})^{2}\equiv r\bar{r}\pmod{s}
. So since
(s\ell\bar{s}\bar{\ell})^{2}\equiv 0\equiv s\bar{s}\pmod{s}
and
(rj\bar{r}\bar{j})^{2}\equiv 0\equiv r\bar{r}\pmod{r}
and
(r,s)=1
, we have
(s\ell\bar{s}\bar{\ell})^{2}\equiv s\bar{s}\pmod{q}
and
(rj\bar{r}\bar{j})^{2}\equiv r\bar{r}\pmod{q}
.

Furthermore, we have
s\bar{s}+r\bar{r}\equiv s\bar{s}\equiv 1\pmod{r}
and
s\bar{s}+r\bar{r}\equiv r\bar{r}\equiv 1\pmod{s}
. So
s\bar{s}+r\bar{r}\equiv 1\pmod{rs}
. This, in particular, implies that
N\equiv s\bar{s}N+r\bar{r}N\pmod{q}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle F_{N}(\mathbf{b})\equiv s\bar{s}\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{2}+r\bar{r}\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}^{2}-N\equiv s\bar{s}F_{N}(\mathbf{u})+r\bar{r}F_{N}(\mathbf{v})\pmod{q},

giving that

\displaystyle e\left(\frac{aF_{N}(\mathbf{b})}{q}\right)=e\left(\frac{\bar{s}aF_{N}(\mathbf{u})}{r}\right)e\left(\frac{\bar{r}aF_{N}(\mathbf{v})}{s}\right).

Lastly, we have

\displaystyle e\left(\frac{\mathbf{c\cdot\mathbf{b}}}{Tq}\right)=e\left(\frac{(\bar{s}\bar{\ell}\mathbf{c})\cdot\mathbf{u}}{rj}\right)e\left(\frac{(\bar{r}\bar{j}\mathbf{c})\cdot\mathbf{v}}{s\ell}\right).

Thus,
S_{q}(a,T,\mathbf{c})
equals

\displaystyle\sum_{(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})\in U\times V}e\left(\frac{\bar{s}aF_{N}(\mathbf{u})}{r}+\frac{(\bar{s}\bar{\ell}\mathbf{c})\cdot\mathbf{u}}{rj}\right)e\left(\frac{\bar{r}aF_{N}(\mathbf{v})}{s}+\frac{(\bar{r}\bar{j}\mathbf{c})\cdot\mathbf{v}}{s\ell}\right)=S_{r}(\bar{s}a,j,\bar{s}\bar{\ell}\mathbf{c})S_{s}(\bar{r}a,\ell,\bar{r}\bar{j}\mathbf{c}).

We now proceed to proving the multiplicative property for
S_{q}^{\equiv}(T,\mathbf{c})
. First, we establish that
\eta\colon(\mathbb{Z}/r\mathbb{Z})^{\times}\times(\mathbb{Z}/s\mathbb{Z})^{\times}\to(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
defined by
\eta(t_{1},t_{2})=st_{1}+rt_{2}
is a bijection. Confirming that
\eta
indeed maps to
(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
, by the congruences
st_{1}+rt_{2}\equiv st_{1}\pmod{r}
and
st_{1}+rt_{2}\equiv rt_{2}\pmod{s}
and the fact that
(st_{1},r)=(rt_{2},s)=1
, we have that
(st_{1}+rt_{2},q)=1
.

Suppose that
st_{1}+rt_{2}\equiv st_{1}^{\prime}+rt_{2}^{\prime}\pmod{q}
. Then
s(t_{1}-t_{1}^{\prime})\equiv r(t_{2}^{\prime}-t_{2})\pmod{q}
, from which it follows by reducing modulo
r
and
s
that
t_{1}-t_{1}^{\prime}\equiv 0\pmod{r}
and
t_{2}^{\prime}-t_{2}\equiv{0}\pmod{s}
. So
\eta
is injective. Since
|(\mathbb{Z}/r\mathbb{Z})^{\times}\times(\mathbb{Z}/s\mathbb{Z})^{\times}|=|(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{\times}|
, it follows that
\eta
is also bijective. So, by the multiplicative property for
S_{q}(a,T,\mathbf{c})
together with the congruences
\bar{s}(st_{1}+rt_{2})\equiv t_{1}\pmod{r}
and
\bar{r}(st_{1}+rt_{2})\equiv t_{2}\pmod{s}
,

\displaystyle S_{q}^{\equiv}(T,\mathbf{c})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq t_{1}<r\\
(t_{1},r)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq t_{2}<s\\
(t_{2},s)=1\end{subarray}}S_{q}(st_{1}+rt_{2},T,\mathbf{c})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq t_{1}<r\\
(t_{1},r)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq t_{2}<s\\
(t_{2},s)=1\end{subarray}}S_{r}(\bar{s}(st_{1}+rt_{2}),j,\bar{s}\bar{\ell}\mathbf{c})S_{s}(\bar{r}(st_{1}+rt_{2}),\ell,\bar{r}\bar{j}\mathbf{c})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq t_{1}<r\\
(t_{1},r)=1\end{subarray}}S_{r}(t_{1},j,\bar{s}\bar{\ell}\mathbf{c})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq t_{2}<s\\
(t_{2},s)=1\end{subarray}}S_{s}(t_{2},\ell,\bar{r}\bar{j}\mathbf{c})=S_{r}^{\equiv}(j,\bar{s}\bar{\ell}\mathbf{c})S_{s}^{\equiv}(\ell,\bar{r}\bar{j}\mathbf{c}),

giving the multiplicative property desired.
∎

By Lemma
2.10
, we can write
S_{q}^{\equiv}
as a product of the form
\prod_{p\mid q}S_{p^{v_{p}(q)}}^{\equiv}
. So if
q
is square-free, the problem of bounding
S_{q}^{\equiv}
is reduced to bounding
S_{p}^{\equiv}
for
p
a prime divisor of
q
. We will see later that this reduction is quite useful. We show in the following lemma that bounding
S_{p}^{\equiv}
can be reduced to bounding Kloosterman sums (when
n
is even) and Salié sums (when
n
is odd), for which the Weil bound suffices. Since
[
17
, Lemma 26]
does not handle a congruence restriction, the only stipulation on
p
(for the form
F_{0}
) is that
p
must be odd. However, to reduce the evaluation of
S_{p}^{\equiv}
to a Kloosterman or Salié sum, we will need to complete the square modulo
p
, for which we require our modulus
T
to have a multiplicative inverse modulo
p
. Hence, our condition that
p\nmid T
.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
p
be an odd prime not dividing
T\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. Then

\displaystyle|S_{p}^{\equiv}(T,\mathbf{c})|\leq 2p^{(n+1)/2}(N,\mathbf{c},p)^{1/2}.

We also note the trivial bound
|S^{\equiv}_{p}(T,\mathbf{c})|\leq(p-1)p^{n}
, which holds for all primes
p
.

Proof.

We closely follow the proof of
[
17
, Lemma 26]
. We have

\displaystyle S_{p}^{\equiv}(T,\mathbf{c})

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=1}^{p-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{b}\bmod Tp\\
\mathbf{b}\equiv\mathbf{k}\bmod T\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{aF_{N}(\mathbf{b})}{p}+\frac{\mathbf{c\cdot b}}{Tp}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=1}^{p-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{b}\bmod p\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{aF_{N}(T\mathbf{b}+\mathbf{k})}{p}+\frac{\mathbf{c\cdot(Tb+\mathbf{k})}}{Tp}\right)

\displaystyle=e\left(\frac{\mathbf{c\cdot k}}{Tp}\right)\sum_{a=1}^{p-1}\sum_{\mathbf{b}\bmod p}e\left(\frac{aF_{N}(T\mathbf{b}+\mathbf{k})+\mathbf{c\cdot b}}{p}\right)

\displaystyle=e\left(\frac{\mathbf{c\cdot k}}{Tp}\right)\sum_{a=1}^{p-1}\sum_{\mathbf{b}\bmod p}e\left(\frac{a\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(T^{2}b_{i}^{2}+2Tk_{i}b_{i})+|\mathbf{k}|^{2}-N\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}b_{i}}{p}\right)

\displaystyle=e\left(\frac{\mathbf{c\cdot k}}{Tp}\right)\sum_{a=1}^{p-1}e\left(\frac{a(|\mathbf{k}|^{2}-N)}{p}\right)\sum_{\mathbf{b}\bmod p}e\left(\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(aT^{2}b_{i}^{2}+(2Tk_{i}a+c_{i})b_{i})}{p}\right)

\displaystyle=e\left(\frac{\mathbf{c\cdot k}}{Tp}\right)\sum_{a=1}^{p-1}e\left(\frac{a(|\mathbf{k}|^{2}-N)}{p}\right)\prod_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{{b}\bmod p}e\left(\frac{aT^{2}b^{2}+(2Tk_{i}a+c_{i})b}{p}\right).

Let
A_{a}=aT^{2}
and
B_{i,a}=2Tk_{i}a+c_{i}
. We henceforth use an overline upon a quantity to denote its multiplicative inverse modulo
p
. Since
p\nmid T
, we complete the square modulo
p
by

\displaystyle aT^{2}b^{2}+(2Tk_{i}a+c_{i})b\equiv A_{a}(b+\overline{2A_{a}}B_{i,a})^{2}-\overline{4A_{a}}B_{i,a}^{2}\pmod{p}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{b\bmod p}e\left(\frac{aT^{2}b^{2}+(2Tk_{i}a+c_{i})b}{p}\right)=e\left(-\frac{\overline{4A_{a}}B_{i,a}^{2}}{p}\right)\sum_{b\bmod p}e\left(\frac{A_{a}(b+\overline{2A_{a}}B_{i,a})^{2}}{p}\right)

\displaystyle=e\left(-\frac{\overline{4A_{a}}B_{i,a}^{2}}{p}\right)\sum_{b\bmod p}e\left(\frac{A_{a}b^{2}}{p}\right)=e\left(-\frac{\overline{4A_{a}}B_{i,a}^{2}}{p}\right)\left(\frac{A_{a}}{p}\right)\tau_{p}=e\left(-\frac{\overline{4A_{a}}B_{i,a}^{2}}{p}\right)\left(\frac{a}{p}\right)\tau_{p},

where
\tau_{p}=i^{(p-1)^{2}/4}\sqrt{p}
. By considering the equality

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}e\left(-\frac{\overline{4A_{a}}B_{i,a}^{2}}{p}\right)=e\left(\frac{-\overline{T}\mathbf{c\cdot k}}{p}\right)e\left(\frac{-a|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}{p}\right)e\left(\frac{-\overline{a}(\overline{4T^{2}}|\mathbf{c}|^{2})}{p}\right),

we obtain

\displaystyle S^{\equiv}_{p}(T,\mathbf{c})=e\left(\frac{(T^{-1}-\overline{T})(\mathbf{c\cdot k})}{p}\right)\tau_{p}^{n}\sum_{a=1}^{p-1}\left(\frac{a}{p}\right)^{n}e\left(\frac{a(-N)+\overline{a}(-\overline{4T^{2}}|\mathbf{c}|^{2})}{p}\right).

The remaining sum is then a Kloosterman sum when
n
is even, and is a Salié sum when
n
is odd. Using Weil’s bound
[
40
]
and Salié’s evaluation
[
37
]
, we finally have

\displaystyle|S_{p}^{\equiv}(T,\mathbf{c})|\leq p^{n/2}\cdot 2p^{1/2}(-N,-\overline{4T^{2}}|\mathbf{c}|^{2},p)^{1/2}=2p^{(n+1)/2}(N,\mathbf{c},p)^{1/2},

as desired.
∎

We now arrive at the punchline set up by Lemmas
2.10
and
2.11
: writing
S_{q}^{\equiv}
as
S_{u}^{\equiv}\cdot S_{v}^{\equiv}
with
u
square-free and
v
square-full, we can apply Lemma
2.9
to
S_{v}^{\equiv}
and Lemma
2.11
to almost all factors
S_{p}^{\equiv}
in the prime product decomposition of
S_{u}^{\equiv}
to yield a refined estimate for
S_{q}^{\equiv}
. Then, parameterizing
q\leq X
by
u
square-free and
v
square-full yields an almost-square-root improvement to our estimate of
\sum_{q\leq X}|S_{q}^{\equiv}(\mathbf{c})|
over simply applying Lemma
2.9
. This is the biggest hurdle toward obtaining an error as good as
O(N^{(n-1)/4+\varepsilon})
.

We note that approximation (
8
) also holds for
0<|\mathbf{c}|\leq P
, but we are only interested in the
\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{0}
case, since that corresponds to the leading-order term of
r_{n}^{\equiv}(N)
.

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
|\mathbf{c}|\leq P
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{q\leq X}|S_{q}^{\equiv}(\mathbf{c})|\ll X^{(3+n)/2+\varepsilon}P^{\varepsilon}.

(7)

Consequently,

\displaystyle\sum_{q\leq X}(Mq)^{-n}S_{q}^{\equiv}(\mathbf{0})=\sum_{q\geq 1}(Mq)^{-n}S_{q}^{\equiv}(\mathbf{0})+O(X^{(3-n)/2+\varepsilon}P^{\varepsilon}),

(8)

and the infinite sum converges absolutely.

Proof.

This result and its proof are direct modifications of
[
17
, Lemma 28]
. We begin by refining Lemma
2.9
to obtain an almost-square-root improvement by leveraging the multiplicative property exhibited by Lemma
2.10
. For
q\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, let
u=\prod_{p:v_{p}(q)=1}p
and
v=\prod_{p:v_{p}(q)\geq 2}p^{v_{p}(q)}
be the square-free and square-full parts of
q=uv
. Letting
j=\prod_{p:v_{p}(q)=1}p^{v_{p}(M)}
and
\ell=\prod_{p:v_{p}(q)\geq 2}p^{v_{p}(M)}
so that
M=j\ell
, by Lemmas
2.10
and
2.9
we have

\displaystyle S_{q}^{\equiv}(\mathbf{c})=S_{u}^{\equiv}(j,\bar{v}\bar{\ell}\mathbf{c})S_{v}^{\equiv}(\ell,\bar{u}\bar{j}\mathbf{c})\ll v^{1+n/2}\left|S_{u}^{\equiv}(j,\bar{v}\bar{\ell}\mathbf{c})\right|.

Since
u
is square-free, by Lemma
2.10
,

\displaystyle\left|S_{u}^{\equiv}(j,\bar{v}\bar{\ell}\mathbf{c})\right|=\prod_{p\mid u}\left|S_{p}^{\equiv}(p^{v_{p}(j)},B_{p}\mathbf{c})\right|,

where each
B_{p}
is an integer coprime to
p
. So by Lemma
2.11
,
\left|S_{u}^{\equiv}(j,\bar{v}\bar{\ell}\mathbf{c})\right|
is at most

\displaystyle 2^{n}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\mid u\\
p\mid j\end{subarray}}(p-1)p^{n}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\mid u\\
p\nmid j\end{subarray}}2p^{\frac{n+1}{2}}(p,N,B_{p}\mathbf{c})^{1/2}\ll\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\mid u\end{subarray}}2p^{\frac{n+1}{2}}(p,N,B_{p}\mathbf{c})^{1/2}=2^{\omega(u)}u^{\frac{n+1}{2}}(u,N,\mathbf{c})^{1/2}.

Following the rest of the proof of
[
17
, Lemma 28]
, mutatis mutandis, yields (
7
). Finally, we see by summation by parts that (
7
) implies approximation (
8
) and the absolute convergence of the infinite series therein.
∎

2.4.
Relating
r_{n}^{\equiv}(N)
to
r_{n}^{\square}(N)

By our estimates on
I_{q}^{\equiv}(\mathbf{c})
and
S_{q}^{\equiv}(\mathbf{c})
, the error of
O(N^{(n-1)/4+\varepsilon})
follows by
[
17
, pg.200]
, mutatis mutandis. Thus, we turn to considering the leading order term of
r_{n}^{\equiv}(N)
. Lemma
2.4
handles the contribution from

, so it remains to note the Euler product representation of
p
-adic densities for
\sum_{q\geq 1}(Mq)^{-n}S_{q}^{\equiv}(\mathbf{0})
. This follows upon using
[
19
, Lemma 2.2]
and replacing
F(\mathbf{x})
with
F_{N}(\mathbf{x})
since
S_{q}^{\equiv}(\mathbf{0})
is already directly handled by
[
19
]
(with the notation

instead). Furthermore, the convergence of the Euler product is assured by Lemma
2.12
by asserting that

is indeed absolutely convergent.

Lemma 2.13

(
[
19
, pgs.152–154]
)
.

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{q\geq 1}(Mq)^{-n}S_{q}^{\equiv}(\mathbf{0})=M^{-n}\prod_{p}\sigma_{p}^{\equiv}(N,n),

where
\sigma_{p}^{\equiv}(N,n)
is the
p
-adic density

\displaystyle\sigma_{p}^{\equiv}(N,n)\vcentcolon=\lim_{d\to\infty}p^{-d(n-1)}{\#\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\left(\mathbb{Z}/p^{d+v_{p}(M)}\mathbb{Z}\right)^{n}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}\equiv N\bmod p^{d},~x_{i}\equiv k_{i}\bmod p^{v_{p}(M)}\right\}}.

We are now able to prove the theorem that is this section’s purpose, successfully modifying Heath-Brown’s circle method to obtain an explicit asymptotic relation between
r_{n}^{\equiv}(N)
and
r_{n}^{\square}(N)
in terms of their
p
-adic densities, which we evaluate in the next section. The
p
-adic density
\delta_{p}^{\square}(N,n)
of
r_{n}^{\square}(N)
is

\displaystyle\delta_{p}^{\square}(N,n)\vcentcolon=\lim_{d\to\infty}p^{-d(n-1)}{\#\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\left(\mathbb{Z}/p^{d}\mathbb{Z}\right)^{n}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}\equiv N\bmod p^{d}\right\}}.

(9)

With this notation, we may now state the theorem sought.

Theorem 2.14
.

Let
n\geq 4
and
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and
M\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. Then the number
r_{n}^{\equiv}(N)
of solutions over
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
to
\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}=N
such that each
x_{i}\equiv k_{i}\bmod{M}
satisfies

\displaystyle r_{n}^{\equiv}(N)=M^{-n}\left(\prod_{p\mid M}\frac{\sigma_{p}^{\equiv}(N,n)}{\delta_{p}^{\square}(N,n)}\right)r_{n}^{\square}(N)+O(N^{(n-1)/4+\varepsilon}).

Proof.

We have shown that
I_{q}^{\equiv}(\mathbf{c})=0
holds for
q\geq P
as it does for
I_{q}(\mathbf{c})
, and have sufficiently modified or generalized
[
17
, Lemmas 13, 16, 19, 22, 25, 23, 26, 28]
and the relevant portion of
[
17
, Lemma 31]
. Thus, following the proof in
[
17
, pg.200]
, mutatis mutandis, yields

\displaystyle r_{n}^{\equiv}(N)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\infty}(G)M^{-n}\left(\prod_{p}\sigma_{p}^{\equiv}(N,n)\right)N^{n/2-1}+O(N^{(n-1)/4+\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\left(M^{-n}\prod_{p\mid M}\sigma_{p}^{\equiv}(N,n)\right)\left(\sigma_{\infty}(G)\prod_{p\nmid M}\delta^{\square}_{p}(N,n)N^{n/2-1}\right)+O(N^{(n-1)/4+\varepsilon})

upon noting that
\sigma_{p}^{\equiv}(N,n)=\delta_{p}^{\square}(N,n)
when
p\nmid M
. By
[
17
, Corollary 1]
, we see that
\sigma_{\infty}(G)\prod_{p}\delta^{\square}_{p}(N,n)N^{n/2-1}=r_{n}^{\square}(N)+O(N^{(n-1)/4+\varepsilon})
, yielding the desired asymptotic relation.
∎

Evaluating the

Having proven Theorem

\displaystyle\delta_{p}^{{k}}(N,n)

\displaystyle=\lim_{d\to\infty}p^{-d(n-1)}\left(p^{-d}\sum_{a\bmod p^{d}}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\bmod p^{d+v}\\

for

, as recalled in (

We will use the characteristic function notation
\mathbf{1}_{S}
throughout this section in our results and proofs thereof: for some statement

, we set

if

if

3.1.
The square
p
-adic densities

We first compute the square
p
-adic densities, which, once done, suffice for Theorem
1.6
once paired with Theorem
2.14
. The closed formulas for
\delta_{p}^{\square}(N,n)
are complicated, and their proofs are incredibly tedious (especially for
p=2
).

For
(A,C)=1
, we recall (see
[
5
, Chapter 1]
, for example) the evaluation of the quadratic Gauss sum

\displaystyle\sum_{r\bmod C}e\left(\frac{Ar^{2}}{C}\right)=\begin{cases}(1+i)\varepsilon_{A}^{-1}\sqrt{C}\left(\frac{C}{A}\right)&\text{if~}c\equiv 0\pmod{4},\\
\varepsilon_{C}\sqrt{C}\left(\frac{A}{C}\right)&\text{if~}c\equiv 1\pmod{2},\\
0&\text{if~}c\equiv 2\pmod{4},\end{cases}

(10)

where
\left(\frac{\cdot}{\cdot}\right)
is the Jacobi symbol and
\varepsilon_{m}\vcentcolon=i^{(m-1)^{2}/4}
. We first evaluate
\delta_{p}^{\square}(N,n)
for odd
p
, which we can state by a single closed form, albeit somewhat unnaturally.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
p
be an odd prime and
v=v_{p}(N)
and
N_{p}=p^{-v}N
be the
p
-free part of
N
. Then
\delta_{p}^{\square}(N,n)=1+p^{-n/2}f_{p}(N,n)
, where

\displaystyle f_{p}(N,n)=(p

\displaystyle-1)\left(\cos\left(\frac{\pi n}{2}\right)(-1)^{n(p+1)/4}\frac{1-p^{2\lceil v/2\rceil(1-n/2)}}{1-p^{2-n}}+p^{1-n/2}\frac{1-p^{2\lceil(v-1)/2\rceil(1-n/2)}}{1-p^{2-n}}\right)

\displaystyle-(-1)^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor(v+1)(p-1)/2}\left(-\left(\frac{N_{p}}{p}\right)\right)^{n(v+1)}p^{v(1-n/2)+(1-(-1)^{n})(1+(-1)^{v})/8}.

Proof.

Let
a\neq 0
be modulo
p^{d}
and
a_{p}=ap^{-v_{p}(a)}
be its
p
-free part. We then have

\displaystyle\sum_{r\bmod p^{d}}e\left(\frac{ar^{2}}{p^{d}}\right)=p^{v_{p}(a)}\sum_{t\bmod p^{d-v_{p}(a)}}e\left(\frac{a_{p}t^{2}}{p^{d-v_{p}(a)}}\right)=p^{v_{p}(a)}\varepsilon_{p^{d-v_{p}(a)}}\left(\frac{a_{p}}{p^{d-v_{p}(a)}}\right)p^{(d-v_{p}(a))/2},

(11)

where the first equality follows from the substitution
r\mapsto t+bp^{d-v_{p}(a)}
with
b,t
modulo
p^{v_{p}(a)}
and the second equality follows from Gauss’ formula (
10
). For a function
f
, we note that we can partition the sum
\sum_{a\bmod p^{d}}f(a)
via the
p
-adic valuation of the variable
a
by

\displaystyle\sum_{a\bmod p^{d}}f(a)=f(0)+\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\sum_{c=1}^{d}\sum_{t\bmod p^{d-c}}f(jp^{c-1}+tp^{c}).

Using this formula on the exponential sum formula for
\delta_{p}^{\square}(N,n)
, we have that
\delta_{p}^{\square}(N,n)
is the limit as
d\to\infty
of

\displaystyle 1+p^{-nd}\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\sum_{c=1}^{d}\sum_{t\bmod p^{d-c}}\left(\sum_{r\bmod p^{d}}e\left(\frac{(jp^{c-1}+tp^{c})r^{2}}{p^{d}}\right)\right)^{n}e\left(-\frac{(jp^{c-1}+tp^{c})N}{p^{d}}\right).

(12)

By (
11
),

\displaystyle\sum_{r\bmod p^{d}}e\left(\frac{(jp^{c-1}+tp^{c})r^{2}}{p^{d}}\right)=p^{c-1}\varepsilon_{p^{d-c+1}}\left(\frac{j+tp}{p^{d-c+1}}\right)p^{(d-c+1)/2}=\left(\varepsilon_{p}\left(\frac{j}{p}\right)\right)^{\mathbf{1}_{c\equiv d\bmod 2}}p^{(d+c-1)/2}.

So the sum over
t
modulo
p^{d-c}
in (
12
) equals

\displaystyle e\left(\frac{-jN}{p^{d-c+1}}\right)\left(\left(\varepsilon_{p}\left(\frac{j}{p}\right)\right)^{\mathbf{1}_{c\equiv d\bmod 2}}p^{(d+c-1)/2}\right)^{n}\sum_{t\bmod p^{d-c}}e\left(\frac{-tN}{p^{d-c}}\right)

\displaystyle=p^{n(d+c-1)/2+d-c}e\left(\frac{-jN}{p^{d-c+1}}\right)\left(\varepsilon_{p}\left(\frac{j}{p}\right)\right)^{n(\mathbf{1}_{c\equiv d\bmod 2})}\mathbf{1}_{v_{p}(N)\geq d-c}.

So the sum over
1\leq c\leq d
in (
12
) equals

\displaystyle~~~~~\sum_{c=d-v_{p}(N)}^{d}p^{n(d+c-1)/2+d-c}e\left(\frac{-jN}{p^{d-c+1}}\right)\left(\varepsilon_{p}\left(\frac{j}{p}\right)\right)^{n(\mathbf{1}_{c\equiv d\bmod 2})}

\displaystyle=p^{nd-n/2}\sum_{c=0}^{v_{p}(N)}p^{c(1-n/2)}e\left(\frac{-jN}{p^{c+1}}\right)\left(\varepsilon_{p}\left(\frac{j}{p}\right)\right)^{n(\mathbf{1}_{c\text{~even}})}

\displaystyle=p^{nd-n/2}\left(\sum_{c=0}^{\lfloor v_{p}(N)/2\rfloor}p^{2c(1-n/2)}\left(\varepsilon_{p}\left(\frac{j}{p}\right)\right)^{n}e\left(\frac{-jN}{p^{2c+1}}\right)+\sum_{c=0}^{\lfloor(v_{p}(N)-1)/2\rfloor}p^{(2c+1)(1-n/2)}e\left(\frac{-jN}{p^{2c+2}}\right)\right).

(13)

Passing the sum over
1\leq j\leq p-1
inside, we are interested in evaluating

\displaystyle\sum_{c=0}^{\lfloor v_{p}(N)/2\rfloor}p^{2c(1-n/2)}\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\left(\varepsilon_{p}\left(\frac{j}{p}\right)\right)^{n}e\left(\frac{-jN}{p^{2c+1}}\right)

(14)

and

\displaystyle\sum_{c=0}^{\lfloor(v_{p}(N)-1)/2\rfloor}p^{(2c+1)(1-n/2)}\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}e\left(\frac{-jN}{p^{2c+2}}\right).

(15)

We begin by handling (
14
). If
c<v_{p}(N)/2
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\left(\varepsilon_{p}\left(\frac{j}{p}\right)\right)^{n}e\left(\frac{-jN}{p^{2c+1}}\right)=\varepsilon_{p}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\left(\frac{j}{p}\right)^{n}=\varepsilon_{p}^{n}(p-1)\mathbf{1}_{n\text{~even}}.

(16)

If
c=v_{p}(N)/2
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\left(\varepsilon_{p}\left(\frac{j}{p}\right)\right)^{n}e\left(\frac{-jN}{p^{2c+1}}\right)=\varepsilon_{p}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\left(\frac{j}{p}\right)^{n}e\left(\frac{-jN_{p}}{p}\right)=\begin{cases}-\varepsilon_{p}^{n}&n\text{~even,}\\
\varepsilon_{p}^{n}\left(\frac{-N_{p}}{p}\right)\varepsilon_{p}p^{1/2}&n\text{~odd,}\end{cases}

(17)

by the standard Gauss sum evaluation. We note the simple identity
\varepsilon_{p}^{n}\left(\frac{-N_{p}}{p}\right)\varepsilon_{p}p^{1/2}=(-1)^{(n+1)(p-1)/4}\left(\frac{-N_{p}}{p}\right)p^{1/2}=(-1)^{(n-1)(p-1)/4}\left(\frac{N_{p}}{p}\right)p^{1/2}
for
n
odd, which we will use instead as it allows us to nicely unify our final expressions that depend on the parity on
n
.

Suppose that
v_{p}(N)
is even. Then if
n
is even, by (
16
) and (
17
),

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\sum_{c=0}^{\lfloor v_{p}(N)/2\rfloor}p^{2c(1-n/2)}\left(\varepsilon_{p}\left(\frac{j}{p}\right)\right)^{n}e\left(\frac{-jN}{p^{2c+1}}\right)=\sum_{c=0}^{v_{p}(N)/2-1}p^{2c(1-n/2)}\varepsilon_{p}^{n}(p-1)-\varepsilon_{p}^{n}p^{v_{p}(N)(1-n/2)}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{p}^{n}(p-1)\frac{1-p^{v_{p}(N)(1-n/2)}}{1-p^{2-n}}-(-1)^{{n(p-1)}/{4}}p^{v_{p}(N)(1-n/2)}.

If
n
is odd, then by (
16
) and (
17
),

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\sum_{c=0}^{\lfloor v_{p}(N)/2\rfloor}p^{2c(1-n/2)}\left(\varepsilon_{p}\left(\frac{j}{p}\right)\right)^{n}e\left(\frac{-jN}{p^{2c+1}}\right)=(-1)^{(n-1)(p-1)/4}\left(\frac{N_{p}}{p}\right)p^{v_{p}(N)(1-n/2)+1/2}.

Now suppose that
v_{p}(N)
is odd. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\sum_{c=0}^{\lfloor v_{p}(N)/2\rfloor}p^{2c(1-n/2)}\left(\varepsilon_{p}\left(\frac{j}{p}\right)\right)^{n}e\left(\frac{-jN}{p^{2c+1}}\right)=\varepsilon_{p}^{n}(p-1)\frac{1-p^{(v_{p}(N)+1)(1-n/2)}}{1-p^{2-n}}\mathbf{1}_{n~\text{even}},

by (
16
) since we avoid the edge case where
c=v_{p}(N)/2
. This completes the evaluation of (
14
).

We now handle the evaluation of (
15
). If
c<(v_{p}(N)-1)/2
, then
\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}e(-jN/p^{2c+2})=p-1
, and if
c=(v_{p}(N)-1)/2
, then
\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}e(-jN/p^{2c+2})=-1
. Thus, if
v_{p}(N)
is even, then

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\sum_{c=0}^{\lfloor(v_{p}(N)-1)/2\rfloor}p^{(2c+1)(1-n/2)}e\left(\frac{-jN}{p^{2c+2}}\right)=(p-1)\sum_{c=0}^{(v_{p}(N)-2)/2}p^{(2c+1)(1-n/2)}

\displaystyle=(p-1)p^{1-n/2}\frac{1-p^{v_{p}(N)(1-n/2)}}{1-p^{2-n}}.

If
v_{p}(N)
is odd, then

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\sum_{c=0}^{\lfloor(v_{p}(N)-1)/2\rfloor}p^{(2c+1)(1-n/2)}e\left(\frac{-jN}{p^{2c+2}}\right)=(p-1)\sum_{c=0}^{(v_{p}(N)-1)/2-1}p^{(2c+1)(1-n/2)}-p^{v_{p}(N)(1-n/2)}

\displaystyle=(p-1)p^{1-n/2}\frac{1-p^{(v_{p}(N)-1)(1-n/2)}}{1-p^{2-n}}-p^{v_{p}(N)(1-n/2)}.

Combining our evaluations of (

) and (

) with (

) and (

) gives that, for

odd,
\delta_{p}^{\square}(N,n)
equals

\displaystyle 1+p^{-n/2}\left((p-1)\left(\varepsilon_{p}^{n}\frac{1-p^{(v_{p}(N)+1)(1-n/2)}}{1-p^{2-n}}\mathbf{1}_{n~\text{even}}+p^{1-n/2}\frac{1-p^{(v_{p}(N)-1)(1-n/2)}}{1-p^{2-n}}\right)-p^{v_{p}(N)(1-n/2)}\right).

Turning our attention to the case where
v_{p}(N)
is even, note that

\displaystyle-(-1)^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor(p-1)/2}\left(-\left(\frac{N_{p}}{p}\right)\right)^{n}p^{v_{p}(N)(1-n/2)+(1-(-1)^{n})/4}

equals
-(-1)^{n(p-1)/4}p^{v_{p}(N)(1-n/2)}
if
n
is even, and equals
(-1)^{(n-1)(p-1)/4}\left(\frac{N_{p}}{p}\right)p^{v_{p}(N)(1-n/2)+1/2}
if
n
is odd. So by our evaluations of (
14
) and (

) together with (

) and (

), we have that if

is even,
\delta_{p}^{\square}(N,n)
equals

\displaystyle 1+p^{-n/2}\Bigg((p-1)\left(\varepsilon_{p}^{n}\frac{1-p^{v_{p}(N)(1-n/2)}}{1-p^{2-n}}\mathbf{1}_{n\text{~even}}+p^{1-n/2}\frac{1-p^{v_{p}(N)(1-n/2)}}{1-p^{2-n}}\right)

\displaystyle-(-1)^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor(p-1)/2}\left(-\left(\frac{N_{p}}{p}\right)\right)^{n}p^{v_{p}(N)(1-n/2)+(1-(-1)^{n})/4}\Bigg).

Upon noting that
\varepsilon_{p}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{n~\text{even}}=\cos(\pi n/2)(-1)^{n(p+1)/4}
, we see that

Finally, we move to computing
\delta_{2}^{\square}(N,n)
, which is the most difficult of the density computations. In our evaluation of
\delta_{2}^{\square}(N,n)
, we encounter a geometric sum twisted by a sum of cosines, which merely forces us to break our formula into cases, but nevertheless makes the computations quite tedious. This twisted geometric sum runs over the positive integers up to
v_{2}(N)+2
, so we are interested in values of our sum of cosines over that range.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
n,N\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and
1\leq c\leq v+2
, where
v=v_{2}(N)
. Set
N_{2}=2^{-v}N
to be the odd part of
N
. Define
C_{n}(c,N)=\cos\left({\pi n}/{4}-{\pi N}/{2^{c}}\right)+(-1)^{nc}\cos\left({3\pi n}/{4}-{3\pi N}/{2^{c}}\right)
. Since
C_{n+4}(c,N)=-C_{n}(c,N)
, it suffices to evaluate
C_{n}(c,N)
only for
n\in\{0,1,2,3\}
. For these cases, we have the evaluations

\displaystyle C_{0}(c,N)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}2&\text{if~}c<v,\\
-2&\text{if~}c=v,\\
0&\text{if~}c>v.\end{cases}

\displaystyle C_{1}(c,N)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}2^{1/2}\mathbf{1}_{c~\text{odd}}&\text{if~}c<v,\\
-2^{1/2}\mathbf{1}_{c~\text{odd}}&\text{if~}c=v,\\
2^{1/2}(-1)^{(N_{2}-1)/2}\mathbf{1}_{c\text{~odd}}&\text{if~}c=v+1,\\
2^{1/2}\left(\sin(N_{2}\pi/4)+\cos(N_{2}\pi/4)\right)\mathbf{1}_{c\text{~even}}&\text{if~}c=v+2.\end{cases}

\displaystyle C_{2}(c,N)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&\text{if~}c\neq v+1,\\
2(-1)^{(N_{2}-1)/2}&\text{if~}c=v+1.\end{cases}

\displaystyle C_{3}(c,N)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}-2^{1/2}\mathbf{1}_{c~\text{odd}}&\text{if~}c<v,\\
2^{1/2}\mathbf{1}_{c~\text{odd}}&\text{if~}c=v,\\
2^{1/2}(-1)^{(N_{2}-1)/2}\mathbf{1}_{c\text{~odd}}&\text{if~}c=v+1,\\
2^{1/2}\left(\sin(N_{2}\pi/4)-\cos(N_{2}\pi/4)\right)\mathbf{1}_{c\text{~even}}&\text{if~}c=v+2.\end{cases}

Proof.

We first note the
n=0,2
cases since they are simpler. If
n=0
, then

\displaystyle C_{n}(c,N)=\cos\left(\frac{\pi N}{2^{c}}\right)+\cos\left(\frac{3\pi N}{2^{c}}\right)=\begin{cases}2&\text{if~}1\leq c\leq v-1,\\
-2&\text{if~}c=v,\\
0&\text{if~}c>v.\end{cases}

If
n=2
, then

\displaystyle C_{n}(c,N)=\sin\left(\frac{\pi N}{2^{c}}\right)-\sin\left(\frac{3\pi N}{2^{c}}\right)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if~}c\neq v+1,\\

3.2.
The polygonal
p
-adic densities

We now move to computing the polygonal
p
-adic densities. Let
G(A,B,C)\vcentcolon=\sum_{r\bmod C}e((Ar^{2}+Br)/C)
be the generalized quadratic Gauss sum. Recall that
G(A,B,C)=0
if
(A,C)>1
, unless
(A,C)\mid B
, wherein we have

\displaystyle G(A,B,C)=(A,C)\cdot G(A/(A,C),B/(A,C),C/(A,C)).

(23)

This is, in fact, the only result that we will need to evaluate
\delta_{p}^{{k}}(N,n)
when
p
is an odd prime divisor of
k-2
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
p
be an odd prime divisor of
k-2
. Then
\delta_{p}^{{k}}(N,n)=p^{v_{p}(k-2)}
for any
n
and
N
.

Proof.

Let
v=v_{p}(k-2)
. We first evaluate the exponential sum
\sum_{r=0}^{p^{d}-1}e\left({a(rp^{v}+k)^{2}}/{p^{d}}\right)
. We have

\displaystyle\sum_{r\bmod p^{d}}e\left(\frac{a(rp^{v}+k)^{2}}{p^{d}}\right)=e(ak^{2}/p^{d})G(ap^{2v},2akp^{v},p^{d}),

which is zero unless
p^{\min(v_{p}(a)+2v,d)}=(ap^{2v},p^{d})\mid 2akp^{v}
. This holds if and only if
v_{p}(2akp^{v})=v_{p}(a)+v\geq\min(v_{p}(a)+2v,d)
, which holds only if
v_{p}(a)\geq d-v
since
v>0
. If
v_{p}(a)\geq d-v
, then
ap^{2v}\equiv 2akp^{v}\equiv 0\pmod{p^{d}}
so that
G(ap^{2v},2akp^{v},p^{d})=p^{d}
. So

\displaystyle\sum_{r\bmod p^{d}}e\left(\frac{a(rp^{v}+k)^{2}}{p^{d}}\right)=\begin{cases}e({ak^{2}}/p^{d})p^{d}&\text{if~}p^{d-v}\mid a,\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

By utilizing this evaluation, we see that

\displaystyle\delta_{p}^{{k}}(N,n)

\displaystyle=\lim_{d\to\infty}p^{-nd}\sum_{a\bmod p^{d}}\left(\sum_{r\bmod p^{d}}e\left(\frac{a(rp^{v}+k)^{2}}{p^{d}}\right)\right)^{n}e\left(\frac{-aX_{N}}{p^{d}}\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{d\to\infty}p^{-nd}\sum_{a\bmod p^{v}}\left(e\left(\frac{ap^{p-v}k^{2}}{p^{d}}\right)p^{d}\right)^{n}e\left(\frac{-ap^{d-v}X_{N}}{p^{d}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\bmod p^{v}}e\left(\frac{ank^{2}}{p^{v}}\right)e\left(\frac{-aX_{N}}{p^{v}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\bmod p^{v}}e\left(\frac{-a\left(8(k-2)N+n((k-4)^{2}-k^{2})\right)}{p^{v}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\bmod p^{v}}e\left(\frac{-8a(k-2)(N-n)}{p^{v}}\right)=\sum_{a\bmod p^{v}}1=p^{v}.

∎

The method of evaluating
\delta_{2}^{{k}}(N,n)
is only slightly more involved than Proposition
3.4
when
k\not\equiv 0\pmod{4}
, but is as involved as Proposition
3.3
when
k\equiv 0\pmod{4}
. In fact, the evaluation of
\delta_{2}^{{k}}(N,n)
is quite similar to
\delta_{2}^{\square}(N,n)
when
k\equiv 0\pmod{4}
. Before moving on to this evaluation, we first note a simple, standard result on
2
-adic generalized quadratic Gauss sums, which we will need to use in conjunction with (
23
) when
k
is odd.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
L\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and
A,B
be positive odd integers. Then
G(A,B,2^{L})=\mathbf{1}_{L=1}\cdot 2
.

Proof.

First note for
L=1
that
G(A,B,2^{L})=1+e((A+B)/2)=2
. Now suppose that
L\geq 2
. Let
g(x)=Ax^{2}+Bx
. Since
L\geq 2
, we have
A\cdot 2^{2L-2}\equiv 0\pmod{2^{L}}
. So since
2Ax+B
is odd,
g(x+2^{L-1})=Ax^{2}+Bx+2^{L-1}(2Ax+B)+A\cdot 2^{2L-2}\equiv g(x)+2^{L-1}
. So

\mathcal{A}_{4}(s,\chi)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{4}(s,\chi)

\displaystyle=576\sum_{N\geq 1}\frac{(-1)^{N}\sigma(N_{2})^{2}\chi(N)}{N^{s}}=576\left(-\sigma(1)^{2}+\sum_{v\geq 1}\sigma(1)^{2}z_{2}^{v}\right)\prod_{p\text{~odd}}\sum_{v\geq 0}\sigma(p^{v})^{2}z_{p}^{v}

\displaystyle=\frac{2z_{2}-1}{1-z_{2}}\left(\frac{1}{1-z_{2}}\right)^{-1}\mathcal{R}_{4}(s,\chi)=(\chi(2)2^{1-s}-1)\mathcal{R}_{4}(s,\chi),

as desired.
∎

Proof of Corollary
1.2
.

Let
p_{1},\dots,p_{r}
be the odd prime divisors of
k-2
. We first handle the case where
k
is odd. By Theorem
1.1
and (
28
),

\displaystyle\sum_{N\leq x}r_{4}^{{k}}(N)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{N\leq x}\left(\frac{2}{3(k-2)^{3}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{p_{i}^{2}}{p_{i}^{2}-1}\right)r_{4}^{\square}(8(k-2)N+4(k-4)^{2})+O(N^{3/4+\varepsilon})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{9(k-2)^{6}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{p_{i}^{2}}{p_{i}^{2}-1}\right)^{2}\sum_{N\leq x}r_{4}^{\square}(8(k-2)N+4(k-4)^{2})^{2}+O(x^{11/4+\varepsilon}),

where we have the error of
O(x^{11/4+\varepsilon})
since
\sum_{N\leq x}r_{4}^{\square}(N)N^{3/4+\varepsilon}\ll x^{11/4+\varepsilon}
, which follows by summation by parts since
\sum_{N\leq x}r_{4}^{\square}(N)\ll x^{2}
. Since
(4(k-4)^{2},8(k-2))=4
, we have the orthogonality relation

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{N\equiv 4(k-4)^{2}\bmod{8(k-2)}}=\frac{\mathbf{1}_{4\mid N}}{\phi(2(k-2))}\sum_{\chi}\overline{\chi((k-4)^{2})}\chi(N/4),

where the sum is taken over the Dirichlet characters modulo
2(k-2)
. Letting
y=2(k-2)x+(k-4)^{2}
, we then have for some constant
B_{k}
that
\sum_{N\leq x}r_{4}^{\square}(8(k-2)N+4(k-4)^{2})^{2}+B_{k}
equals

\displaystyle\sum_{N\leq 4y}r_{4}^{\square}(N)^{2}\mathbf{1}_{N\equiv 4(k-4)^{2}\bmod{8(k-2)}}=\frac{1}{\phi(2(k-2))}\sum_{\chi}\overline{\chi((k-4)^{2})}\sum_{N\leq y}r_{4}^{\square}(4N)^{2}\chi(N).

We proceed by estimating
\sum_{N\leq y}r_{4}^{\square}(4N)^{2}\chi(N)
. By Lemma
4.1
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{4}(s,\chi)\vcentcolon=576\frac{(1-\chi(2)2^{1-s})^{2}(1-\chi(2)2^{2-s})}{1-\chi(2)^{2}2^{2-2s}}\cdot\frac{L(s,\chi)L(s-1,\chi)^{2}L(s-2,\chi)}{L(2s-2,\chi^{2})}

is the meromorphic extension of
\sum_{N\geq 1}{r_{4}^{\square}(4N)^{2}\chi(N)}/{N^{s}}
to
\mathbb{C}
. By Perron’s formula (see
[
32
, Corollary 5.3]
), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{N\leq y}r_{4}^{\square}(4N)^{2}\chi(N)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\sigma_{0}-iT}^{\sigma_{0}+iT}\mathcal{R}_{4}(s,\chi)\frac{y^{s}}{s}ds+O(y^{3+\varepsilon}/T)

for
\sigma_{0}>3
. Let
G(s)=\mathcal{R}_{4}(s,\chi){y^{s}}/{s}
and let
\delta,\delta^{\prime}>0
be arbitrary. Taking
\sigma_{0}=2+\delta
, we write by the residue theorem the vertical integral
\int_{\sigma_{0}-iT}^{\sigma_{0}+iT}G(s)ds
as

\displaystyle 2\pi i\text{Res}(G(s),3)-\int_{(3+\delta)+iT}^{(2+\delta^{\prime})+iT}G(s)ds-\int_{(2+\delta^{\prime})+iT}^{(2+\delta^{\prime})-iT}G(s)ds-\int_{(2+\delta^{\prime})-iT}^{(3+\delta)-iT}G(s)ds.

We proceed by bounding the three integrals above, which we henceforth denote as
E_{1}
,
E_{2}
, and
E_{3}
, respectively. We note that for
\Re(s)\geq 2+\delta^{\prime}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{(1-\chi(2)2^{1-s})^{2}(1-\chi(2)2^{2-s})}{1-\chi(2)^{2}2^{2-2s}},L(s,\chi),L(s-1,\chi),L(2s-2,\chi)^{-1}\ll 1.

Thus, it suffices to estimate
L(s-2,\chi)
along the contours of
E_{1}
,
E_{2}
, and
E_{3}
. For this, we use the standard convexity bound

\displaystyle L(\sigma+it,\chi)\ll(1+|t|)^{\max(0,(1-\sigma)/2)+\varepsilon}

(32)

for
\sigma\geq 0
. The estimation of the horizontal integrals
E_{1}
and
E_{3}
is identical, so we only consider
E_{3}
. By (
32
), we have

\displaystyle E_{3}

\displaystyle=\int_{2+\delta^{\prime}}^{3+\delta}G(u-iT)du=\int_{2+\delta^{\prime}}^{3}G(u-iT)du+\int_{3}^{3+\delta}G(u-iT)du

\displaystyle\ll\int_{2+\delta^{\prime}}^{3}T^{(1-(u-2))/2+\varepsilon}\frac{y^{u}}{T}du+\int_{3}^{3+\delta}T^{\varepsilon}\frac{y^{u}}{T}du\ll y^{3+\delta}/T^{1-\varepsilon}.

Following in identical fashion, we have
E_{1}\ll y^{3+\delta}/T^{1-\varepsilon}
. Now for the vertical integral
E_{2}
, we have by (
32
) that

\displaystyle E_{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{-T}^{T}G(2+\delta^{\prime}-it)dt\ll\int_{-T}^{T}(1+|t|)^{(1-\delta^{\prime})/2+\varepsilon}\frac{y^{2+\delta^{\prime}}}{1+|t|}dt

\displaystyle=y^{2+\delta^{\prime}}\int_{-T}^{T}(1+|t|)^{-(1+\delta^{\prime})/2+\varepsilon}dt\ll y^{2+\delta^{\prime}}T^{1/2+\varepsilon}.

Since
\delta,\delta^{\prime}>0
were arbitrary, by taking
T=y^{2/3}
we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{N\leq y}r_{4}^{\square}(4N)^{2}\chi(N)=\text{Res}(\mathcal{R}_{4}(s,\chi)y^{s}/s,3)+O(y^{7/3+\varepsilon}).

The error estimate of
O(y^{7/3+\varepsilon})
will suffice for our purposes. Let
\chi_{0}
be the principal character modulo
2(k-2)
and

\displaystyle\Pi\vcentcolon=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{(1-p_{i}^{-3})(1-p_{i}^{-2})^{2}(1-p_{i}^{-1})}{(1-p_{i}^{-4})}.

Upon noting that
\chi_{0}(2)=0
, we have the evaluation

\displaystyle\text{Res}(\mathcal{R}_{4}(s,\chi_{0})y^{s}/s,3)=576\frac{\zeta(3)\zeta(2)^{2}}{\zeta(4)}\prod_{p\mid 2(k-2)}\frac{(1-p^{-3})(1-p^{-2})^{2}(1-p^{-1})}{(1-p^{-4})}\frac{y^{3}}{3}=126\zeta(3)\Pi y^{3}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\phi(2(k-2))}\sum_{\chi}\overline{\chi((k-4)^{2})}\sum_{N\leq y}r_{4}^{\square}(4N)^{2}\chi(N)=\frac{126\zeta(3)\Pi}{\phi(2(k-2))}y^{3}+O(y^{7/3+\varepsilon}).

Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{N\leq x}r_{4}^{{k}}(N)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{9(k-2)^{6}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{p_{i}^{2}}{p_{i}^{2}-1}\right)^{2}\frac{126\zeta(3)\Pi}{\phi(2(k-2))}(2(k-2)x)^{3}+O(x^{11/4+\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\frac{56\zeta(3)}{(k-2)^{7}}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{1-p_{i}^{-3}}{1-p_{i}^{-4}}(2(k-2)x)^{3}+O(x^{11/4+\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\frac{448\zeta(3)}{(k-2)^{4}}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{1-p_{i}^{-3}}{1-p_{i}^{-4}}x^{3}+O(x^{11/4+\varepsilon}).

We now handle the case where
k\equiv 2\pmod{4}
, for which we may borrow much of the computation from the
k
odd case. By Theorem
1.1
and (
29
),

\displaystyle\sum_{N\leq x}r_{4}^{{k}}(N)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{N\leq x}\left(\frac{8}{3(k-2)^{3}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{p_{i}^{2}}{p_{i}^{2}-1}\right)r_{4}^{\square}(8(k-2)N+4(k-4)^{2})+O(N^{3/4+\varepsilon})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{64}{9(k-2)^{6}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{p_{i}^{2}}{p_{i}^{2}-1}\right)^{2}\sum_{N\leq x}r_{4}^{\square}(8(k-2)N+4(k-4)^{2})^{2}+O(x^{11/4+\varepsilon}).

Since
(4(k-4)^{2},8(k-2))=16
, we have the orthogonality relation

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{N\equiv 4(k-4)^{2}\bmod{8(k-2)}}=\frac{\mathbf{1}_{16\mid N}}{\phi((k-2)/2)}\sum_{\chi}\overline{\chi((k-4)^{2}/4)}\chi(N/16),

where the sum is taken over the Dirichlet characters modulo
(k-2)/2
. Letting
y=((k-2)/2)x+(k-4)^{2}/4
, we then have for some constant
B_{k}
that
\sum_{N\leq x}r_{4}^{\square}(8(k-2)N+4(k-4)^{2})^{2}+B_{k}
equals

\displaystyle\sum_{N\leq 16y}r_{4}^{\square}(N)^{2}\mathbf{1}_{N\equiv 4(k-4)^{2}\bmod{8(k-2)}}=\frac{1}{\phi((k-2)/2)}\sum_{\chi}\overline{\chi((k-4)^{2}/4)}\sum_{N\leq y}r_{4}^{\square}(16N)^{2}\chi(N).

Since
r_{4}^{\square}(16N)=r_{4}^{\square}(4N)
, the estimation is identical to that in the
k
odd case. Since
{2\mid(k-2)/2}
, we still have
\chi_{0}(2)=0
, where
\chi_{0}
is the principal character modulo
(k-2)/2
. So

\displaystyle\sum_{N\leq y}r_{4}^{\square}(16N)^{2}\chi(N)=\mathbf{1}_{\chi=\chi_{0}}126\zeta(3)\Pi y^{3}+O(y^{7/3+\varepsilon})

and hence

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\phi((k-2)/2)}\sum_{\chi}\overline{\chi((k-4)^{2}/4)}\sum_{N\leq y}r_{4}^{\square}(16N)^{2}\chi(N)=\frac{126\zeta(3)\Pi}{\phi((k-2)/2)}y^{3}+O(y^{7/3+\varepsilon}).

Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{N\leq x}r_{4}^{{k}}(N)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{64}{9(k-2)^{6}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{p_{i}^{2}}{p_{i}^{2}-1}\right)^{2}\frac{126\zeta(3)\Pi}{\phi\left(\frac{k-2}{2}\right)}\left(\frac{(k-2)x}{2}\right)^{3}+O(x^{11/4+\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\frac{3584\zeta(3)}{(k-2)^{7}}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{1-p_{i}^{-3}}{1-p_{i}^{-4}}\left(\frac{(k-2)x}{2}\right)^{3}+O(x^{11/4+\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\frac{448\zeta(3)}{(k-2)^{4}}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{1-p_{i}^{-3}}{1-p_{i}^{-4}}x^{3}+O(x^{11/4+\varepsilon}).

We finally handle the case where
k\equiv 0\pmod{4}
, which is more involved. By Theorem
1.1
, (
30
), and (
31
),

\displaystyle\sum_{N\leq x}r_{4}^{{k}}(N)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{N\leq x}\left(\frac{8(2+(-1)^{N})}{3(k-2)^{3}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{p_{i}^{2}}{p_{i}^{2}-1}\right)r_{4}^{\square}(8(k-2)N+4(k-4)^{2})+O(N^{3/4+\varepsilon})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{N\leq x}\frac{320}{9(k-2)^{6}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{p_{i}^{2}}{p_{i}^{2}-1}\right)^{2}r_{4}^{\square}(8(k-2)N+4(k-4)^{2})^{2}

\displaystyle+\sum_{N\leq x}\frac{256}{9(k-2)^{6}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{p_{i}^{2}}{p_{i}^{2}-1}\right)^{2}(-1)^{N}r_{4}^{\square}(8(k-2)N+4(k-4)^{2})^{2}+O(x^{11/4+\varepsilon}).

We first handle
\sum_{N\leq x}r_{4}^{\square}(8(k-2)N+4(k-4)^{2})^{2}
. Let
y=((k-2)/2)x+(k-4)^{2}/4
. Since
(4(k-4)^{2},8(k-2))=16
, we have for some constant
B_{k}
that
\sum_{N\leq x}r_{4}^{\square}(8(k-2)N+4(k-4)^{2})^{2}+B_{k}
equals

\displaystyle\sum_{N\leq 16y}r_{4}^{\square}(N)^{2}\mathbf{1}_{N\equiv 4(k-4)^{2}\bmod{8(k-2)}}=\frac{1}{\phi((k-2)/2)}\sum_{\chi}\overline{\chi((k-4)^{2}/4)}\sum_{N\leq y}r_{4}^{\square}(16N)^{2}\chi(N),

where the sum over
\chi
is over the Dirichlet characters modulo
(k-2)/2
. Since
r_{4}^{\square}(16N)=r_{4}^{\square}(4N)
, the estimation is nearly identical to that in the
k
odd case. However, since
(k-2)/2
is odd, we now have
\chi_{0}(2)=1
, where
\chi_{0}
is the principal character modulo
(k-2)/2
. So

\displaystyle\sum_{N\leq y}r_{4}^{\square}(16N)^{2}\chi(N)

\displaystyle=\text{Res}\left(\mathcal{R}_{4}(s,\chi_{0})\frac{y^{s}}{s},3\right)+O(y^{7/3+\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}_{\chi=\chi_{0}}576\frac{(1-2^{-2})^{2}(1-2^{-1})}{1-2^{-4}}\frac{\zeta(3)\zeta(2)^{2}}{\zeta(4)}\Pi\frac{y^{3}}{3}+O(y^{7/3+\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}_{\chi=\chi_{0}}144\zeta(3)\Pi y^{3}+O(y^{7/3+\varepsilon}).

Hence

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\phi(\frac{k-2}{2})}\sum_{\chi}\overline{\chi\left(\frac{(k-4)^{2}}{4}\right)}\sum_{N\leq y}r_{4}^{\square}(16N)^{2}\chi(N)=\frac{144\zeta(3)\Pi}{\phi(\frac{k-2}{2})}y^{3}+O(y^{7/3+\varepsilon}).

We now handle
\sum_{N\leq x}(-1)^{N}r_{4}^{\square}(8(k-2)N+4(k-4)^{2})^{2}
. For some constant
B_{k}^{\prime}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{N\leq x}(-1)^{N}r_{4}^{\square}(8(k-2)N+4(k-4)^{2})^{2}+B_{k}^{\prime}=\sum_{N\leq 16y}(-1)^{\frac{N-4(k-4)^{2}}{8(k-2)}}r_{4}^{\square}(N)^{2}\mathbf{1}_{N\equiv 4(k-4)^{2}\bmod{8(k-2)}}.

Now for
N\equiv 4(k-4)^{2}\pmod{8(k-2)}
,

\displaystyle\frac{N-4(k-4)^{2}}{8(k-2)}=\frac{(N/16)-((k-4)^{2}/4)}{(k-2)/2}\equiv(N/16)-((k-4)^{2}/4)\equiv N/16\pmod{2}.

So
\sum_{N\leq x}(-1)^{N}r_{4}^{\square}(8(k-2)N+4(k-4)^{2})^{2}+B_{k}^{\prime}
equals

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\phi((k-2)/2)}\sum_{\chi}\overline{\chi((k-4)^{2}/4)}\sum_{N\leq y}(-1)^{N}r_{4}^{\square}(16N)^{2}\chi(N).

By Lemma
4.1
, we have that
\mathcal{A}_{4}(s,\chi)\vcentcolon=(\chi(2)2^{1-s}-1)\mathcal{R}_{4}(s,\chi)
is the meromorphic extension of
\sum_{N\geq 1}(-1)^{N}r_{4}^{\square}(4N)\chi(N)/N^{s}=\sum_{N\geq 1}(-1)^{N}r_{4}^{\square}(16N)\chi(N)/N^{s}
to
\mathbb{C}
. Since
\chi(2)2^{1-s}-1
is uniformly bounded on
\Re(s)\geq 2+\delta^{\prime}
for any
\delta^{\prime}>0
, the same Perron’s formula argument from earlier yields

\displaystyle\sum_{N\leq y}(-1)^{N}r_{4}^{\square}(16N)^{2}\chi(N)

\displaystyle=\text{Res}(\mathcal{A}_{4}(s,\chi)y^{s}/s,3)+O(y^{7/3+\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}_{\chi=\chi_{0}}(-108\zeta(3)\Pi)y^{3}+O(y^{7/3+\varepsilon}).

Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\phi(\frac{k-2}{2})}\sum_{\chi}\overline{\chi\left(\frac{(k-4)^{2}}{4}\right)}\sum_{N\leq y}(-1)^{N}r_{4}^{\square}(16N)^{2}\chi(N)=-\frac{108\zeta(3)\Pi}{\phi(\frac{k-2}{2})}y^{3}+O(y^{7/3+\varepsilon}).

Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{N\leq x}r_{4}^{{k}}(N)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{320}{9(k-2)^{6}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{p_{i}^{2}}{p_{i}^{2}-1}\right)^{2}\frac{144\zeta(3)\Pi}{\phi(\frac{k-2}{2})}\left(\frac{(k-2)x}{2}\right)^{3}

\displaystyle-\frac{256}{9(k-2)^{6}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{p_{i}^{2}}{p_{i}^{2}-1}\right)^{2}\frac{108\zeta(3)\Pi}{\phi(\frac{k-2}{2})}\left(\frac{(k-2)x}{2}\right)^{3}+O(x^{11/4+\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\left(320\cdot 288-256\cdot 216\right)\frac{\zeta(3)}{9(k-2)^{7}}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{1-p_{i}^{-3}}{1-p_{i}^{-4}}\left(\frac{(k-2)x}{2}\right)^{3}+O(x^{11/4+\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\frac{512\zeta(3)}{(k-2)^{4}}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{1-p_{i}^{-3}}{1-p_{i}^{-4}}x^{3}+O(x^{11/4+\varepsilon}).

∎

Finally, we turn to Corollary
1.4
. Before giving its proof, we first note a simple lemma, which follows from Jacobi’s four-square theorem
[
22
]
.

Lemma 4.2
.

If a strictly increasing infinite subsequence
a_{N}
of positive integers does not converge
2
-adically to
0
, then
r_{4}^{\square}(a_{N})\geq\varepsilon\cdot N
infinitely often for some
\varepsilon>0
.

Proof.

Suppose that
a_{N}
is a strictly increasing infinite subsequence that does not converge
2
-adically to
0
. Then there exists an
\varepsilon>0
such that
2^{-v_{2}(a_{N})}\geq\varepsilon
for infinitely many
N
. So

\displaystyle r_{4}^{\square}(a_{N})=8\sum_{4\nmid d\mid a_{N}}d\geq 8\sigma(2^{-v_{2}(a_{N})}a_{N})>2^{-v_{2}(a_{N})}a_{N}\geq\varepsilon\cdot a_{N}\geq\varepsilon\cdot N

infinitely often.
∎

We now prove Corollary
1.4
by using Corollary
1.3
and Lemma
4.2
.

Proof of Corollary
1.4
.

Suppose that
k\equiv 0\pmod{4}
. Suppose that
a_{N}
is a strictly increasing infinite subsequence of positive integers that does not converge
2
-adically to
(k-4)^{2}/(4-2k)
. We note that
(k-4)^{2}/(4-2k)
is indeed a
2
-adic integer, which is easy to see by writing
(k-4)^{2}/(4-2k)=4(r-1)^{2}/(1-2r)
, where
r=k/4
. Since
a_{N}
does not converge
2
-adically to
(k-4)^{2}/(4-2k)
, we have that
8(k-2)a_{N}+4(k-4)^{2}
does not converge
2
-adically to
0
. Thus, by Lemma
4.2
,
r_{4}^{\square}(8(k-2)a_{N}+4(k-4)^{2})\geq\varepsilon\cdot N
infinitely often for some
\varepsilon>0
. So by Corollary
1.3
,
r_{4,+}^{{k}}(a_{N})
is unbounded.

Suppose that
k\not\equiv 0\pmod{4}
. Let
b_{N}
be an arbitrary strictly increasing infinite subsequence of positive integers. If
k
is odd, then
v_{2}(8(k-2)b_{N}+4(k-4)^{2})=2
, and if
k\equiv 2\pmod{4}
, then
v_{2}(8(k-2)b_{N}+4(k-4)^{2})=4
. In either case,
8(k-2)b_{N}+4(k-4)^{2}
does not converge
2
-adically to
0
. So by Lemma
4.2
and Corollary
1.3
,
r_{4,+}^{{k}}(b_{N})
is unbounded.
∎

5.
Directions for Future Work

A natural continuation of our work is to attempt to improve the
O(N^{(n-1)/4+\varepsilon})
error in Theorem
1.1
. Since we are restricting to the class of quadratics
p_{k}
, it is reasonable for one to hope for improvements to the error, at least in some cases. Curiously, we have verified this to be the case in the extreme when
n=4
and
k=6
, in that the error is in fact zero. Indeed, as stated earlier, we have the exact identity

\displaystyle r_{4}^{{6}}(N)=\frac{1}{24}r_{4}^{\square}(32N+16).

(33)

This is not too difficult to prove elementarily. We show that the solutions to
\sum_{i=1}^{4}p_{6}(x_{i})=N
over
\mathbb{Z}^{4}
are equinumerous to the solutions to
\sum_{i=1}^{4}p_{3}(m_{i})=N
over
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{4}
. Completing the square to
\sum_{i=1}^{4}p_{6}(x_{i})=N
gives the equation
\sum_{i=1}^{4}x_{i}^{2}=32N+16
with the congruence restriction
x_{i}\equiv 6\pmod{8}
. Since
x_{i}\equiv 6\pmod{8}
is equivalent to
x_{i}/2=2y_{i}+1
for some odd integer
y_{i}
, the equation becomes
\sum_{i=1}^{4}(2y_{i}+1)^{2}=8N+4
, where each
y_{i}
is odd. Since
(2y_{i}+1)^{2}=8p_{3}(y_{i})+1
, we obtain the equation
\sum_{i=1}^{4}p_{3}(y_{i})=N
where each
y_{i}
is odd. Since
p_{3}(y_{i})=p_{3}(-y_{i}-1)
, we then obtain the equation
\sum_{i=1}^{4}p_{3}(m_{i})=N
, where each
m_{i}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Finally, we obtain (
33
) by noting that
r_{4,+}^{{3}}(N)=\sigma(2N+1)=(24)^{-1}r_{4}^{\square}(32N+16)
, where the first equality is a classical result of Legendre
[
26
]
and the second equality follows from the identity
r_{4}^{\square}(2N)=24\sigma(N_{2})
, where
N_{2}=2^{-v_{2}(N)}N
is the odd part of
N
.

The case where
n=4
and
k=6
is obviously an extreme example in error improvement, but it does prompt the following question: for which values of
n
and
k
do we have that
r_{n}^{{k}}(N)
is exactly equal to the main term in Theorem
1.1
?

A more straightforward yet still interesting direction for future work is to estimate more moments of
r_{n}^{{k}}
using Theorem
1.1
. In addition to the proof of Corollary
1.2
, we refer the reader interested in doing so to
[
6
]
. We also suggest trying to improve the error of (
3
), though this would likely have to be done with other methods that circumvent the use of Theorem
1.1
.

For what we anticipate to be a more substantial undertaking, we also suggest generalizing Corollary
1.3
to all
n\geq 4
. This generalization of Corollary
1.3
is essentially equivalent to proving the heuristic that
r_{n,+}^{{k}}(N)=2^{-n}r_{n}^{{k}}(N)+o(N^{n/2-1})
. Hence, we expect this direction to be quite challenging.

Lastly, we suggest furthering the
k\equiv 0\pmod{4}
case of Corollary
1.4
in the sense of determining what other conditions the subsequences on which
r_{4,+}^{{k}}
is bounded must satisfy. Determining sufficient conditions for boundedness would also be very interesting, though these may be more elusive.
