1. Project Overview and Objectives
The objective of the proposed research is to investigate the physical mechanisms and processes
underlying the formation of structures and patterns in systems with plasma-surface interactions.
In the past decades, there have been extensive studies on the interaction of glow discharges,
dielectric barrier discharges, and arc discharges with confining or intervening surfaces. The
advancement of the understanding of these phenomena is not only of fundamental scientific
interest and relevance to the knowledge of the plasma state, but also with profound implications
in various technological applications. The research will integrate theoretical, computational, and
experimental work within an innovative framework of data assimilation, i.e., optimally combining
model predictions with measurements. The scientific merit of this research has three aspects.
Firstly, it extends the studies of plasma-surface interactions to systems with insulator surfaces
and multi-layer systems, while existing studies are predominantly on electrode surfaces.
Secondly, it expects to develop a novel data-driven modeling approach based on data
assimilation to enhance the predictive and control capabilities, which could make transformative
contributions to basic plasma research. Thirdly, it will shed new light on outstanding problems
related to formation of patterns interfacing plasmas.
This project also aims to launch an education and outreach initiative at Texas A&M University-
Kingsville, a non-R1, minority-serving institution in South Texas. The initiative is structured as a
four-tier pyramid. Tier one will be a webinar series for culture and capacity building to inform
broader audience in the region about the research fields of plasma science and engineering. Tier
two will be the creation and offering of an upper-level undergraduate course on introductory
plasma physics, which will help with the recruitment for the upper tiers. On tier three, we will
engage and mentor senior design students to conduct work toward the research goal of this
project. There will also be a certificate program on general plasma science for undergrad and
graduate students, part of which will be lab training at Princeton University. Tier four will be the
supervision and mentoring of Ph.D. students. Therefore, this project will systematically expand
the talent pipeline, broaden participation from communities historically and geographically
underrepresented in DOE SC research portfolio, significantly improve the research and education
capacity at the PI’s institution, and contribute to developing a diverse workforce in plasma
science and engineering.

2. Education and Outreach Activities and Accomplishments
2.1 Tier One
The planning and preparation of the proposed webinar series took longer than expected. So far,
we have offered 4 series: Fall 2024 (4 lectures), Spring 2025 (8 lectures), Fall 2025 (12 lectures),
and Spring 2026 (10 lectures). In Fall 2024, the lectures (by the senior personnel of the project)

audience, and some students even presented their own research or assigned reading. There is
not an accurate headcount; but it is estimated to be 20 per lecture.
To advertise the webinar series to the broader community, the PI reached out to 5 school districts
in South Texas in Fall 2023 and Fall 2025. We invited high school students and teachers to visit
the PI’s lab and learn more details about our research. About 20 high school students participated
in STEM competitions (named Holiday Cup) organized by the PI. Two high school students signed
up for a semester-long research apprenticeship program and experienced different phases of
authentic research. One of them presented her work on data assimilated simulation of chaotic
dynamics of power grid at one of the seminars in Fall 2025.

2.2 Tier Two
The curriculum development effort as part of this project yielded 4 new courses. At the upper

were offered in Fall 2023 as stacked undergraduate-master’s degree courses. There were 18

3. Research Activities and Results
3.1 Summary of Research Activities
To kick off the proposed research, the PI first placed orders for the two pieces of key equipment
to be used. It took over 18 months for the PI to receive the delivery, which was the primary cause
of the requested 12-month no-cost extension of this project. In the meantime, the project team
started building the research capabilities in the following aspects:
  Physics-based models. Working with collaborators at Princeton, the PI identified two
 systems with (broad-sense) plasma-surface interactions. The first one is the dynamic
 modeling of plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition, which would be the
 continuation of a previous collaborative research project. The model involved here is
 relatively simple (ODE-based). The second system with plasma-liquid interface consists of
 DC (glow and/or corona) discharge plasma on top of a thin liquid (insulating oil) layer. The
 aim is to understand how charges penetrate the layer to deposit on the bottom and how
 the stability of the interface is changed in the process.
  Multiphysics modeling software. In order to model the coupled electrical, mechanical and
 chemical processes, the project team built the capacity to develop numerical models in
 OpenFOAM, an open-source finite-volume-method based software package. We also
 used Python, MATLAB, and COMSOL for comparison.
  Data-driven modeling software. The algorithms for our research belong to the general
 class of Kalman filtering based data assimilation. The project team explored a few open-
 source codes such as OpenDA, developed our own codes in Python and MATLAB, and
 eventually decided to use MATLAB.
During the next phase (Fall 2024 – Summer 2025), to establish a baseline, we implemented data
assimilation for improving the accuracy of a simulated double pendulum. By starting with this
simpler system, the data assimilation methods can be validated before scaling the research to

3.2 Calibration and Validation of Data Assimilation
For real-world applications, the reliability of the data assimilation system is critical. It has four
sub-systems: data acquisition (sensors, transmitters, etc.), data preprocessing, physics-based
modeling, and data assimilation. While the latter two are readily there, the first two usually
present challenges and create uncertainties. Therefore, we devoted a lot of project time to the
calibration and validation of the data assimilation system, with special interest in observing how
the errors and noises in raw data would affect the performance of a data assimilation algorithm
and figuring out methods to mitigate the negative effects.
As the initial test, a physical double pendulum was created and paired with a digital twin. This
framework served as the primary testbed for parameter estimation. The parameter estimation
process was first validated using a twin-experiment approach involving two distinct simulated
double pendulums. In this phase, one simulation was performed with known parameters, while
the other was assigned parameters initialized with proximal values. Through this, various
optimization and search methods were evaluated within the MATLAB environment used for the
simulations. The methods which have been selected currently are Levenberg-Marquardt least-
squares search, a steepest-descent least-squares search, and a gradient-based nonlinear
optimizer. After validating these methods, the parameter estimation framework was transitioned
to utilize empirical data which was recorded directly from the physical system via a combination
of Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs) and a rotary encoder. This step aimed to address real-world
noise and other systemic discrepancies, such as unmodeled dynamics like aerodynamic friction.
Following the completion of this step, data assimilation techniques such as Ensemble Kalman
Filters and Particle Filters were implemented to correct the simulated system, ensuring the digital
twin can track the physical system.
The next phase of this research will expand upon the validated framework by adapting it to more
complex systems. This will entail a generalized setup where the simulated model and relevant
experimental observations can be specified by the user. To demonstrate this, the framework will
be tested with other available physical systems, notably a charge plate with complex electrostatic
interactions. With this research, a scalable, generalizable framework for data assimilation in non-
linear dynamics will be developed. If successful, the resulting software will be applicable to
various complex systems, improving predictive capabilities in domains where analytic models are
insufficient.

3.3 Development of Multiphysics Model of Plasma-Surface Interactions
The numerical modeling part of Study #1 and #2 outlined in the proposal has been completed.
We numerically implemented a corona discharge model, based on a simple experimental setting
(starting with 2-D axisymmetric model). We then ran the simulations under different applied
voltages; it is important to observe the changes in the flow pattern with increasing voltage. Our
model included energy and radiation transport and photoionization. However, the introduction
of energy equation also brought in many new questions regarding boundary condition settings
as well as uncertainty in plasma parameters (e.g., ion energy, electron temperature). This is
exactly why a strategy is called for to estimate these properties of plasma via a carefully designed
“merging” of numerical and experimental results. Currently the fully coupled model with corona
discharge, ionic wind, and charge transport at surfaces have been built and tested in COMSOL
and OpenFOAM. The application of data assimilation to this system requires a careful design of
the experimental system. On this front, we have made progress in separately collecting data from
charge plate (measuring the amount of charge deposited on the plate) and corona current and
voltage at electrode. The measurements are not sufficiently resolved spatially; we need to
interpolate the measured data to feed to the data assimilation algorithms.

3.4 Challenges and Paths Ahead
To use data assimilation in non-trivial ways in plasma discharge related systems, the main
challenge is the incompatibility between the short violent nature of the physical systems, the
unavailability of detailed measurement data, and the much longer computational time. First of
all, this makes online data assimilation impossible, unless there are sufficient computational
resources. Second, even for slow processes such as PECVD, the lack of spatiotemporally resolved
measurements could downgrade data assimilation into a purely data analysis/prediction tool.
Finally, in the presence of plasma, the accuracy and precision of measurements would also bring
uncertainties to the performance of data assimilation.
Nevertheless, there are a few directions that the PI and his team have made advancements. First,
starting with a relatively simple system such as double pendulum, we could determine the
necessary conditions for online data assimilation, including the trade-off between computational
power and frequency of data assimilation. Second, for slow processes such as PECVD, we could
use interpolated data from literature to improve the predictive power of the dynamic model
when accurate knowledge of nanostructure dimension is needed. Finally, for the more complex
PDE based Multiphysics model, we could first seek reduced-order models or surrogate models
which will be subject to data assimilation. It should be admitted that there is still huge gap in the
data assimilation approach to plasma modeling. But it is a promising technique and worth further
investigating.
