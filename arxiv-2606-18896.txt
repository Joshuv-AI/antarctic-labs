Thermodynamics of photonic nonlinear Aharonov-Bohm cages
Stefano Iubini, Carlo Danieli

Abstract
We investigate equilibrium and non-equilibrium thermodynamics of one-dimensional photonic diamond lattices with Kerr nonlinearity. The equilibrium phase diagram is obtained as a function of the synthetic magnetic flux acting on each plaquette. In the linear regime, the magnetic flux can induce Aharonov-Bohm caging, flattening all Bloch bands and suppressing particle and energy currents. In this caging regime, non-vanishing currents are enabled by nonlinearity. By imposing stationary temperature- and chemical potential- imbalances at the system boundaries, we show that at weak nonlinearity fine tuning the flux at the Aharonov-Bohm caging transforms the system from a conductor to an insulator. For intermediate nonlinear strength, the system remains conducting for all magnetic fluxes; however, the caging condition significantly enhances the Seebeck coefficient and thermoelectric figure of merit, improving the thermoelectric features of the system. Our results give evidence of a novel route towards optimization of coupled transport devices, based on the control of linear versus nonlinear conduction channels via a synthetic magnetic flux.

Discussion and perspectives–

We have studied one of the most notable examples of AB cages with Kerr nonlinearity from a thermodynamical standpoint and revealed that nonlinearity enables non-zero stationary currents, whose features can be controlled via the magnetic field
\phi
. In particular, we demonstrated that fine-tuning AB caging
(i) turns the lattice from a conductor to an insulator for
\gamma\ll 1
, and
(ii) significantly improves the thermoelectric efficiency of the system for
\gamma\approx 1
, while only mildly lowering both heat and norm conductivities by less that one order of magnitude.
Given the
uniform
magnetic field, this effect is expected to be accessible within standard Onsager reciprocal relations in place of Onsager-Casimir ones
42
.
Our results open a new intriguing venue in the rapidly growing field of optical thermodynamics,
i.e.
exploit nonlinear AB cages to engineer materials with enhanced thermoelectric properties, in both one and higher dimensions
12
. This includes exploring the negative temperature regions of nonlinear AB caging lattices (the area above the red dashed line in Fig.
2
), where unconventional non-equilibrium and dynamical freezing phenomena emerge already for the 1D DNLS
27
, as well as entering the realm of quantum thermodynamics
6
.

reads

\begin{split}H_{0}&=\sum_{n}\left[a_{n}^{*}(c_{n}+c_{n+1})+b_{n}^{*}(c_{n}-c_{n+1})+\text{H.c.}\right]\\
&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{n}\left[(p_{n}+f_{n})^{*}(c_{n}+c_{n+1})\right.\\
&\qquad\quad\left.+(p_{n}-f_{n})^{*}(c_{n}-c_{n+1})+\text{H.c.}\right]\\
&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{n}\left[p_{n}^{*}c_{n}+p_{n}^{*}c_{n+1}+f_{n}^{*}c_{n}+f_{n}^{*}c_{n+1}\right.\\
&\qquad\quad\left.+p_{n}^{*}c_{n}-p_{n}^{*}c_{n+1}-f_{n}^{*}c_{n}+f_{n}^{*}c_{n+1}\ +\text{H.c.}\right]\\
&=\sqrt{2}\sum_{n}\left[p_{n}^{*}c_{n}+f_{n}^{*}c_{n+1}+\text{H.c.}\right]\\
&=\sqrt{2}\sum_{n}\left[c_{n}(p_{n}^{*}+f_{n-1}^{*})+\text{H.c.}\right]\end{split}

(12)

Let us expand the terms
|a_{n}|^{4}
and
|b_{n}|^{4}
of the interaction Hamiltonian in the new coordinates

\begin{split}|a_{n}|^{4}&=\frac{1}{4}[(p_{n}+f_{n})(p_{n}^{*}+f_{n}^{*})]^{2}\\
&=\frac{1}{4}(|p_{n}|^{2}+p_{n}^{*}f_{n}+p_{n}f_{n}^{*}+|f_{n}|^{2})^{2}\\
&=\frac{1}{4}(|p_{n}|^{4}+(p_{n}^{*}f_{n})^{2}+(p_{n}f_{n}^{*})^{2}+|f_{n}|^{4}\\
&\qquad+2|p_{n}|^{2}p_{n}^{*}f_{n}+2|p_{n}|^{2}p_{n}f_{n}^{*}+2|p_{n}|^{2}|f_{n}|^{2}\\
&\qquad+|p_{n}|^{2}|f_{n}|^{2}+p_{n}^{*}f_{n}|f_{n}|^{2}+p_{n}f_{n}^{*}|f_{n}|^{2})\\
|b_{n}|^{4}&=\frac{1}{4}[(p_{n}-f_{n})(p_{n}^{*}-f_{n}^{*})]^{2}\\
&=\frac{1}{4}(|p_{n}|^{2}-p_{n}^{*}f_{n}-p_{n}f_{n}^{*}+|f_{n}|^{2})^{2}\\
&=\frac{1}{4}(|p_{n}|^{4}+(p_{n}^{*}f_{n})^{2}+(p_{n}f_{n}^{*})^{2}+|f_{n}|^{4}\\
&\qquad-2|p_{n}|^{2}p_{n}^{*}f_{n}-2|p_{n}|^{2}p_{n}f_{n}^{*}-2|p_{n}|^{2}|f_{n}|^{2}\\
&\qquad-|p_{n}|^{2}|f_{n}|^{2}-p_{n}^{*}f_{n}|f_{n}|^{2}-p_{n}f_{n}^{*}|f_{n}|^{2})\end{split}

(13)

The sum of these two expanded terms
|a_{n}|^{4}+|b_{n}|^{4}
in Eq. (
13
) cancels many addends.
This yields a rotated form of the interaction Hamiltonian

\begin{split}H_{1}&=\frac{\gamma}{2}\sum_{n}\left[|a_{n}|^{4}+|b_{n}|^{4}+|c_{n}|^{4}\right]\\
&=\frac{\gamma}{4}\sum_{n}\left[|p_{n}|^{4}+(p_{n}^{*}f_{n})^{2}+(p_{n}f_{n}^{*})^{2}\right.\\
&\qquad\quad\left.+|f_{n}|^{4}+4|p_{n}|^{2}|f_{n}|^{2}+2|c_{n}|^{4}\right]\end{split}

(14)

III
Thermodynamics of the nonlinear system

In this section, we detail the calculation of the ground state line and the infinite temperature line, respectively obtained for
\beta=\infty
and
\beta=0
in the probability distribution

\mathcal{P}=\frac{1}{Z}\exp[-\beta(H-\mu A)]

(15)

where
Z
is the the grand canonical partition function

Z=\int\prod_{n=1}^{N}d\psi_{n}d\psi_{n}^{*}\exp[-\beta(H-\mu A)]

(16)

defined for the Hamiltonian
H
, the total norm
A=\sum_{n=1}^{N}[|a_{n}|^{2}+|b_{n}|^{2}+|c_{n}|^{2}]
, the inverse temperature
\beta=\frac{1}{T}
and the chemical potential
\mu
.

III.1
The
\beta=\infty
line

Let us compute the ground state line for the nonlinear Aharonov-Bohm lattice.
To do this, we consider the following ansatz

\begin{split}p_{n}=\sqrt{P}e^{i\phi_{n}}\quad c_{n}=\sqrt{C}e^{i\theta_{n}}\quad f_{n}=\sqrt{F}e^{i\rho_{n}}\end{split}

(17)

defined for the amplitudes
P,C,F
with
\Gamma=P+F+C
, and the phases
\phi_{n},\theta_{n},\rho_{n}
.
This results in a norm density
a=\frac{A}{3N}=\frac{\Gamma}{3}
, since

\begin{split}A=\sum_{n=1}^{N}|p_{n}|^{2}+|c_{n}|^{2}+|f_{n}|^{2}=L(P+F+C)=N\Gamma\end{split}

(18)

The rotated Hamiltonian – here recapped

\begin{split}H&=\sqrt{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left[c_{n}(p_{n}^{*}+f_{n-1}^{*})+\text{H.c.}\right]\\
&+\frac{\gamma}{4}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left[|p_{n}|^{4}+(p_{n}^{*}f_{n})^{2}+(p_{n}f_{n}^{*})^{2}\right.\\
&\qquad\quad\left.+|f_{n}|^{4}+4|p_{n}|^{2}|f_{n}|^{2}+2|c_{n}|^{4}\right]\end{split}

(19)

turns to

\begin{split}H&=2\sqrt{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sqrt{C}\left[\sqrt{P}\cos(\phi_{n}-\theta_{n})+\sqrt{F}\cos(\rho_{n-1}-\theta_{n})\right]\\
&+\frac{\gamma}{4}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left[P^{2}+2PF(2+\cos 2(\phi_{n}-\rho_{n}))+F^{2}+2C^{2}\right]\\
\end{split}

(20)

To minimize this Hamiltonian, we assume the following phase relations which ensure all
\cos
terms to be
-1

\begin{split}\begin{cases}\phi_{n}-\theta_{n}=\pi\\
\rho_{n-1}-\theta_{n}=\pi\\
2(\phi_{n}-\rho_{n})=\pi\end{cases}\,\Leftrightarrow\qquad&\begin{cases}\phi_{n}=\theta_{n}+\pi\\
\rho_{n}=\phi_{n}+\frac{\pi}{2}\\
\theta_{n}=\rho_{n-1}+\pi\\
\end{cases}\end{split}

(21)

For
C=\Gamma-P-F
, the phase relations yield the energy density
h=\frac{H}{3N}

\begin{split}h&=\frac{H}{3N}=\frac{H_{0}}{3N}+\frac{\gamma}{2}\frac{H_{1}}{3N}\\
&=-\frac{2}{3}\sqrt{2(\Gamma-P-F)}\left[\sqrt{P}+\sqrt{F}\right]\\
&+\frac{\gamma}{12}\left[\frac{1}{2}[P+F]^{2}+(\Gamma-P-F)^{2}\right]\end{split}

(22)

The partial derivatives of
h
over
P
and
F
respectively are

\small\begin{split}\frac{\partial h}{\partial P}&=\frac{1}{6}\left\{-2\sqrt{2}\frac{2P+F+\sqrt{PF}-\Gamma}{\sqrt{P(\Gamma-P-F)}}+\frac{\gamma}{2}(3P+3F-2\Gamma)\right\}\\
\frac{\partial h}{\partial F}&=\frac{1}{6}\left\{-2\sqrt{2}\frac{2F+P+\sqrt{PF}-\Gamma}{\sqrt{F(\Gamma-P-F)}}+\frac{\gamma}{2}(3P+3F-2\Gamma)\right\}\end{split}

(23)

Setting the minimum implies search for
\frac{\partial h}{\partial P}=0=\frac{\partial h}{\partial F}
– an identity that implies that
P=F
.
Following this, the energy density
h
and its derivative
\frac{\partial h}{\partial P}
in Eqs. (
22
,
23
) reduce to

\begin{split}h&=-\frac{4}{3}\sqrt{2P(\Gamma-2P)}+\frac{\gamma}{4}\frac{1}{3}\left[2P^{2}+(\Gamma-2P)^{2}\right]\\
\frac{\partial h}{\partial P}&=\frac{2}{3}\left\{-\sqrt{2}\frac{\Gamma-4P}{\sqrt{P(\Gamma-2P)}}+\frac{\gamma}{2}(3P-\Gamma)\right\}\end{split}

(24)

Setting the condition for the minumum
\frac{\partial h}{\partial P}=0
implies

\small\begin{split}&\sqrt{2}(\Gamma-4P)=\frac{\gamma}{2}(3P-\Gamma)\sqrt{P(\Gamma-2P)}\\
&2\Gamma^{2}+32P^{2}-16\Gamma P=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4}(P\Gamma^{3}-8P^{2}\Gamma^{2}+21P^{2}\Gamma-18P^{4})\end{split}

(25)

which ultimately yields a
4^{th}
-order polynomial in
P

\begin{split}f(P,\Gamma,\gamma)&=18\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4}P^{4}-21\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4}\Gamma P^{3}+8(4+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4}\Gamma^{2})P^{2}\\
&-(16\Gamma+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4}\Gamma^{3})P+2\Gamma^{2}\end{split}

(26)

for given norm density
a=\frac{\Gamma}{3}
and nonlinear strength
\gamma
.
Among the four roots of the polynomial
f
in Eq. (
26
) for given
\gamma,\Gamma
, only one is real and positive. This root, for a given pair
\{\gamma,\Gamma\}
, defines the profile of the ground state in Eq. (
17
) with the phase relations in Eq. (
21
) and its energy.

We plot the numerically computed root as function of the nonlinear strength
\gamma
in Fig.
5
for three different values
\Gamma=1,2,5
.
We observe how, in all three cases, the rescaled root
\frac{P}{\Gamma}
ranges from
\frac{P}{\Gamma}=\frac{1}{4}
for
\gamma=0
(linear limit) to
\frac{P}{\Gamma}=\frac{1}{3}
for
\gamma\longmapsto+\infty
(purely nonlinear limit).

Figure 5:
Positive real root
P
of the polynomial
f(P,\Gamma,\gamma)
rescaled by constant
\Gamma
as function the nonlinear strength
\gamma
for
\Gamma=1
(blue),
\Gamma=2
(magenta), and
\Gamma=5
(green).

III.2
The
\beta=0
line

In the infinite temperature limit
\beta=0
, the probability distribution
\mathcal{P}
in Eq. (
15
) reduces to
\mathcal{P}\sim\exp(mA)
where
m=\beta\mu
is supposed to be finite and negative –
i.e.

-\infty<m<0
. Notice that, since
A
does not depend on the phases of the variables
a_{n},b_{n},c_{n}
, the partition function
P
is uniform in phases.
For our convenience, let us rewrite the distribution as
\mathcal{P}\sim\exp(-\alpha A)
for positive
\alpha=-m=-\beta\mu
.

Let us compute the average norm and energy densities in this limit, specifically

a=\frac{\langle A\rangle}{3N}\qquad\qquad h=\frac{\langle H\rangle}{3N}

(27)

where
\langle\cdot\rangle
is the average over
P
.
It is convenient to express
P
as a function of local masses
x_{n}=|a_{n}|^{2}
,
y_{n}=|b_{n}|^{2}
,
z_{n}=|c_{n}|^{2}
. This yields

\mathcal{P}=\alpha^{3N}\exp[-\alpha\sum_{n=1}^{N}(x_{n}+y_{n}+z_{n})]

(28)

The average norm density
a
reads

a=\frac{1}{3N}(3N)\left(\alpha\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\exp(-\alpha x)x\right)=\frac{1}{\alpha}

(29)

which implies that
\alpha=\frac{1}{a}
.

Since all hopping terms of
H_{0}
vanish due to the flat distribution of phases, the average energy density
h
reads

h=\frac{1}{3N}(3N)\left(\frac{\alpha\gamma}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\exp(-\alpha x)x^{2}\right)=\frac{\gamma}{\alpha^{2}}

(30)

Therefore, for
\beta=0
we obtain the relation
h=\gamma a^{2}
.

IV
Details on numerical simulations

To sample equilibrium and nonequilibrium thermodynamic states, lattice equations (
2
) have been numerically integrated
according to a standard 4th-order Runge-Kutta algorithm
54
with a time step not larger than
10^{-3}
units.
In addition to the Hamiltonian bulk dynamics,
thermal boundary reservoir have been implemented according to stochastic Monte Carlo rules, see next paragraph. Averages of observables in stationary states
have been computed for time periods of at least
5\times 10^{6}
and up to
5\times 10^{7}
units after suitable relaxation transients of at least
10^{5}
units.

Definition of heat baths at the chain edges –

Grand-canonical Monte Carlo reservoirs are implemented at the chain edges according to
28
. Here we focus on the left reservoir,
imposing an inverse temperature
\beta_{L}
and chemical potential
\mu_{L}
. An analogous definition applies to the reservoir at the right edge.

The reservoir introduces stochastic perturbations of the field variables
\psi_{n}=(a_{n},b_{n},c_{n})
at lattice site
n=1
, namely
\psi_{1}\to\psi_{1}+\delta\psi_{1}
,
where
\delta\psi_{1}=(\delta p_{1}+i\delta q_{1})/\sqrt{2}
. In between two successive stochastic moves, the evolution of the system is deterministic
and governed by the Hamiltonian equations in (
2
). Moves are implemented with a time period
t_{b}
.
Perturbed variables are accepted or rejected according to a Metropolis cost function

\exp\{-\beta_{L}(\Delta H-\mu\Delta A)\}
, where
\Delta H
and
\Delta A
are respectively the energy and norm variations produced by the
perturbation
\delta\psi_{1}
. Perturbations
\delta p_{1}
and
\delta q_{1}
are independent random variables extracted from an uniform distribution
in the interval
[-\epsilon,\epsilon]
. The parameters
\epsilon
and
t_{b}
determine effective system-bath coupling.

We have verified that different prescriptions on the bath dynamics, as for example a Poissonian distribution of interaction times in place of the
constant-pace rule here adopted do not significantly modify the statistical properties of the model. Simulations are performed with
\epsilon=2

and
t_{b}=0.1
time units. We have verified that this choice of parameters allows to minimize boundary resistances in the whole range of parameter
explored in this study.

Definition of norm and energy currents –

Currents are measured at the edge of the system by monitoring the exchanges of norm and energy between the system and the bath.
For the left edge,

\begin{split}j_{a}^{(L)}&=\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{t_{k}<\tau}\delta a_{1}(t_{k})\\
j_{h}^{(L)}&=\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{t_{k}<\tau}\delta h_{1}(t_{k})\end{split}

(31)

where
\delta a_{1}(t_{k})
and
\delta h_{1}(t_{k})
are respectively the norm and energy variations at the first lattice site produced by a reservoir update occurring at time
t_{k}
. An average over a sufficiently long time
\tau\gg 1
is performed. An analogous expression for the currents at the right edge,
-j_{a}^{(R)}
and

-j_{h}^{(R)}
can be obtained form Eq. (
31
) upon replacing
a_{1}(t_{k})\to a_{N}(t_{k})
and
h_{1}(t_{k})\to h_{N}(t_{k})
.

Onsager matrix elements–

For the numerical estimation of the Onsager coefficients
L_{xy}
in Eq. (
10
) the following procedure is employed, see
28
;
29
for details. A reference working point
(\beta,m)
is fixed and two independent simulations are performed imposing either a pure temperature gradient
\beta_{y}
(with
m_{y}=0
) or a pure ‘chemical’ gradient
m_{y}
(with
\beta_{y}=0
). From the measure of stationary currents
j_{a}
and
j_{j}
one obtains

\begin{split}L_{ah}&=j_{a}/\beta_{y}\quad(m_{y}=0)\\
L_{hh}&=j_{h}/\beta_{y}\quad(m_{y}=0)\\
L_{aa}&=-j_{a}/m_{y}\quad(\beta_{y}=0)\\
L_{ha}&=-j_{h}/m_{y}\quad(\beta_{y}=0)\end{split}

(32)

Nonequilibrium simulations are performed on a chain with
N=100
evolved for
5\times 10^{7}
units.

V
Equilibrium thermodynamics of the linear chain
H_{0}

In general, for a lattice with three bands
\omega_{i}(k)
with
i=0,\pm
represented with normal modes
\varphi_{i}(k)
, the norm and Hamiltonian energy read

\displaystyle\small A

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k}|\varphi_{0}(k)|^{2}+|\varphi_{+}(k)|^{2}+|\varphi_{-}(k)|^{2}

(33)

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\omega_{0}(k)|\varphi_{0}(k)|^{2}+\omega_{+}(k)|\varphi_{+}(k)|^{2}+\omega_{-}(k)|\varphi_{-}(k)|^{2}

The partition function
Z
in Eq. (
16
)

Z=\int\prod_{k}\prod_{i=0,\pm}d\varphi_{i}(k)\,d\varphi_{i}^{*}(k)e^{-\beta H+mA}

(34)

expressed in the normal modes for
m=\beta\mu
reduces to a product of Gaussian integrals

\small\begin{split}&Z=\prod_{k}\prod_{i=0,\pm}\frac{2\pi}{(\beta\omega_{i}(k)-m)}\\
=&(2\pi)^{3N}\left(\frac{1}{\beta\omega_{0}-m}\right)^{N}\left(\frac{1}{\beta\omega_{+}-m}\right)^{N}\left(\frac{1}{\beta\omega_{-}-m}\right)^{N}\end{split}

(35)

The average norm density is

\begin{split}a&=\frac{1}{3N}\frac{\partial\log Z}{\partial m}=\frac{1}{3N}\frac{\sum_{k,i}\log(\frac{2\pi}{\beta\omega_{i}(k)-m})}{\partial m}\\
&=\frac{1}{3N}\sum_{k,i}\frac{1}{\beta\omega_{i}(k)-m}\end{split}

(36)

Let us recall that in our system, the central band is in general independent on the wave vector
k
–
i.e.

\omega_{0}=0
– and the two dispersive bands are symmetric –
i.e.

\omega_{+}(k)=-\omega_{-}(k)
for all
\phi
.
This implies that

\begin{split}\frac{1}{\beta\omega_{+}(k)-m}+\frac{1}{\beta\omega_{-}(k)-m}=\frac{2m}{\beta^{2}\omega^{2}_{+}(k)-m^{2}}\end{split}

(37)

Then, Eq.(
36
) reduces to

\small a=\frac{1}{3}\left[\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{2m}{4\beta^{2}(1+\cos(\phi/2)\cos(k))-m^{2}}\,dk-\frac{1}{m}\right]

(38)

Setting
q=[m/(2\beta)]^{2}=\mu^{2}/4
yields the solution of the integral

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{1}{(1+\cos(\phi/2)\cos(k))-q}\,dk=-\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{K-2q+q^{2}}}

(39)

where we set
q>1+\sqrt{1-K}
with
K=1/2(1-\cos\phi)
to get real solutions.
This implies the relation

a=-\frac{1}{3m}\left(\frac{2q}{\sqrt{K-2q+q^{2}}}+1\right)

(40)

The condition
q>1+\sqrt{1-K}
is equivalent to
|\mu|>2\sqrt{1+\cos(\phi/2)}
.
Furthermore, along a similar approach, it follows that

\begin{split}h&=-\frac{1}{3N}\frac{\partial\log Z}{\partial\beta}=\frac{1}{3N}\sum_{k,i}\frac{\omega_{i}(k)}{\beta\omega_{i}(k)-m}\\
&=\frac{1}{3N\beta}\sum_{k,i}\frac{\beta\omega_{i}(k)-m+m}{\beta\omega_{i}(k)-m}=\frac{1}{\beta}+\mu a\end{split}

(41)

i.e.
the relation between the norm and the energy density is
h=\frac{1}{\beta}+\mu a
.

VI
Landauer theory for linear transport

In the linear limit
\gamma=0
, we employ Landauer theory to describe stationary transport
60
.
The stationary norm current
j_{a}
and energy current
j_{h}
measured at the edges of the chain connected to the baths are defined as

\displaystyle j_{a}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{D}_{\omega}}d\omega\,t(\omega)[f_{L}(\omega)-f_{R}(\omega)]

(42)

\displaystyle j_{h}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{D}_{\omega}}d\omega\,\omega t(\omega)[f_{L}(\omega)-f_{R}(\omega)]

(43)

where
f_{L,R}
is the Rayleigh-Jeans (RJ) distribution,
f_{L,R}=\frac{1}{\beta(\omega-\mu)}
,
\mathcal{D}_{\omega}
is the available frequency spectrum, and
t(\omega)
is the transmission coefficient.
The specific form of
t(\omega)
depends on the system-bath coupling, and in this case we assume it constant
t=1
.

The norm flux
j_{a}
in Eq. (
42
) reads

\begin{split}j_{a}&=\Phi(\beta_{L},\mu_{L},\phi)-\Phi(\beta_{R},\mu_{R},\phi)\\
\end{split}

(44)

where

\begin{split}\Phi(\beta,\mu,\phi)&=\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{\mathcal{D}_{\omega}}d\omega\,\frac{1}{\omega-\mu}\end{split}

(45)

The available frequency spectrum
\mathcal{D}_{\omega}
is the union of the three bands:
two dispersive
\omega_{+}\in[\omega_{l},\omega_{u}]
and
\omega_{-}\in[-\omega_{u},-\omega_{l}]
for
\omega_{l}=2\sqrt{1-\cos{\phi/2}}
and
\omega_{u}=2\sqrt{1+\cos{\phi/2}}
, and a flat band
\omega_{0}=0
. Over these two symmetric intervals, the integral of RJ distribution reads

\begin{split}\Phi(\beta,\mu,\phi)&=\frac{1}{\beta}\log\left[\frac{(\omega_{u}-\mu)}{(\omega_{u}+\mu)}\frac{(\omega_{l}+\mu)}{(\omega_{l}-\mu)}\right]\end{split}

(46)

The expression for
j_{a}
in Eq. (
44
) for
\mu_{L}=\mu_{R}\equiv\mu
then reduces to

\begin{split}j_{a}&=\log\left[\frac{(\omega_{u}-\mu)}{(\omega_{u}+\mu)}\frac{(\omega_{l}+\mu)}{(\omega_{l}-\mu)}\right]\left[\frac{1}{\beta_{L}}-\frac{1}{\beta_{R}}\right]\end{split}

(47)

The energy flux
j_{h}
in Eq. (
43
) reads

j_{h}=\Lambda(\beta_{L},\mu_{L},\phi)-\Lambda(\beta_{R},\mu_{R},\phi)

(48)

where

\begin{split}\Lambda(\beta,\mu,\phi)&=\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{\mathcal{D}_{\omega}}d\omega\,\frac{\omega}{\omega-\mu}\\
&=\frac{2}{\beta}(\omega_{u}-\omega_{l})+\mu\Phi(\beta,\mu,\phi)\end{split}

(49)

For
\mu_{L}=\mu_{R}\equiv\mu
, Eq. (
48
) reduces to

\small\begin{split}j_{h}&=\left\{2(\omega_{u}-\omega_{l})+\mu\log\left[\frac{(\omega_{u}-\mu)}{(\omega_{u}+\mu)}\frac{(\omega_{l}+\mu)}{(\omega_{l}-\mu)}\right]\right\}\left[\frac{1}{\beta_{L}}-\frac{1}{\beta_{R}}\right]\end{split}

(50)

Ultimately, the fluxes
j_{a}
and
j_{h}
can be written as

\begin{split}j_{a}&=C_{\phi}\left[\frac{1}{\beta_{L}}-\frac{1}{\beta_{R}}\right]\\
j_{h}&=\left\{2(\omega_{u}-\omega_{l})+\mu C_{\phi}\right\}\left[\frac{1}{\beta_{L}}-\frac{1}{\beta_{R}}\right]\end{split}

(51)

for the coefficient
C_{\phi}=\log\left[\frac{(\omega_{u}-\mu)}{(\omega_{u}+\mu)}\frac{(\omega_{l}+\mu)}{(\omega_{l}-\mu)}\right]
.
Notice that, for
\phi=\pi
where all bands turn flat, since
\omega_{l}=\omega_{u}
both fluxes
j_{a}
and
j_{h}
in Eqs. (
44
,
48
) vanish for any choices of
\{\beta_{L},\mu_{L}\}
and
\{\beta_{R},\mu_{R}\}
.
