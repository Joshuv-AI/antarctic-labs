AID self-assembly and multistranded DNA binding drive synapsis in class switch recombination
Authors: Di Liu, Chenyang Zhang, Yuhang He, Hongtao Zhu, Keyin Qi, Yanying Dong, Bailin Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractActivation-induced deaminase (AID) initiates immunoglobulin class switch recombination (CSR) by deaminating cytosines within transcription-generated single-stranded DNA in switch regions. R-loops formed during switch-region transcription are thought to expose AID substrates; however, how AID engages and organizes these complex nucleic acid structures remain unclear. Here, combining ensemble biochemistry with single-molecule colocalization and fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) analyses, we uncover an unexpected role for AID as a DNA synapsis factor. AID preferentially promotes synapsis between multistranded DNA substrates, including R-loops and tailed D-loops, and stabilizes these higher-order synaptic complexes. Mutational analyses reveal that two distinct nucleic acid-binding pockets cooperate to drive efficient synapsis. Single-molecule FRET further reveals that AID promotes intramolecular synapsis of tailed D-loops that mimic key CSR intermediates. Moreover, three-color single-molecule analyses indicate that DNA binding-associated AID self-assembly, consistent with AID assemblies observed in cells, accompanies with synaptic complex formation. Notably, a catalytically active AID mutant with impaired AID–AID interactions shows severely compromised DNA synapsis, indicating that higher-order AID organization is essential for synaptic complex formation and synapsis is mechanistically separable from cytosine deamination. Together, our findings establish AID as a DNA synapsis factor and support a model in which AID self-assembly and multistranded DNA binding drive higher-order synapsis during CSR.

Introduction

Class switch recombination (CSR) is a programmed intrachromosomal recombination process that changes the immunoglobulin (Ig) heavy-chain constant (C) region, thereby altering antibody isotype and tailoring effector functions during adaptive immune responses [1–3]. CSR occurs within repetitive switch (S) regions located upstream of each C regions and is highly transcription-dependent [2, 4]. Impaired CSR results in immunodeficiency diseases such as hyper IgM syndrome, whereas aberrant recombination promotes oncogenic chromosomal translocations [5–8]. Thus, productive CSR requires not only the generation of DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) within donor and acceptor S regions, but also precise spatial coordination of these distal DNA segments for timely end joining to ensure accurate intrachromosomal recombination.

Immunoglobulin S regions possess several unusual sequence features, including extended length (2–12 kb), high repetitiveness (10–80 bp per repeat unit), and marked G-richness (GC-skew) on the nontemplate strand (NTS), which strongly favor R-loop formation during transcription [2, 3, 9–15]. R-loops are three-stranded nucleic acid structures in which nascent RNA hybridizes with the template DNA strand, leaving the NTS exposed as single-stranded DNA (ssDNA). The exposed ssDNA within R-loop is a preferred substrate for the downstream activation-induced deaminase (AID) enzymatic activity, which initiates CSR by deaminating cytosines in ssDNA generated during the transcription of S regions [8, 16–19]. These AID-induced lesions are subsequently processed into DSBs and repaired primarily through nonhomologous end joining (NHEJ) pathway [20–22]. Although the mechanisms underlying AID-mediated DNA break formation have been extensively studied, how distant S regions are physically coordinated and stabilized prior to ligation remains less well understood.

Higher-order chromatin organization contributes to CSR by bringing distant S regions into spatial proximity and creating a permissive environment for recombination [23, 24]. However, chromatin-scale proximity alone cannot fully explain how broken S regions are physically paired and locally stabilized at the molecular scale. Several protein cofactors, including Spt5, ELOF1, PTBP2, and 14-3-3, have been reported to play roles in AID recruitment during CSR [25–29]. Nucleic acid structural features, including RNA G-quadruplexes, have been implicated in directing AID targeting during CSR [30–32]. In addition, earlier chromatin conformation and genetic studies have also suggested possible contributions of AID or its C-terminal region to S region organization [33–36], although the magnitude and physiological interpretation of AID-dependent S region synapsis remain debated. These observations raise the possibility that AID may possess structural or organizational properties in addition to its canonical cytosine deamination activity.

Structural studies reveal that AID contains two positively charged nucleic acid binding surfaces, the substrate channel and the assistant patch, which contribute to nucleic acid engagement and preferential recognition of branched nucleic acid substrates [37, 38]. These properties suggest that AID may associate with transcription-associated nucleic acid structures such as R-loops and influence their spatial organization through multivalent protein–DNA interactions.

Here, we tested the hypothesis that AID possesses an intrinsic ability to organize multistranded nucleic acid structures related to recombination-associated DNA architectures. Using ensemble biochemical approaches together with single-molecule colocalization (smCL) assays, we show that AID preferentially promotes synapsis of tailed D-loops and R-loops, forming stable synaptic complexes with substantially enhanced synaptic lifetimes compared with other DNA substrates, including ssDNA, overhang DNA, Y-DNA, bubble DNA, and canonical D-loops. Mutational analysis of AID nucleic acid-binding surfaces reveals their requirement for efficient synapsis formation. Single-molecule fluorescence resonance energy transfer (smFRET) experiments further reveal that AID promotes intramolecular synapsis between recombination-like DNA substrates within a single DNA molecule. Three-color smCL imaging additionally reveals that AID self-assembly accompanies synaptic complex formation, consistent with oligomeric behavior observed in cellular contexts. Importantly, disruption of AID–AID interactions generates a separation-of-function mutant that retains catalytic activity yet is severely compromised in DNA synapsis, supporting that AID-mediated DNA synapsis depends on higher-order AID assembly and can be uncoupled from catalytic deamination. Together, these results suggest that AID can organize multistranded DNA substrates into higher-order synaptic assemblies, providing a structural framework that contributes to the spatial coordination of recombination-associated DNA substrates during CSR.

Materials and methods

DNA probes and substrates

DNA oligonucleotides (oligos) DL133, DL134, 121-BZ16, 121-BZ42, 121-BZ44, 121-BZ54, 121-BZ91, 121-BZ101, and 121-BZ109 were synthesized and fluorescently labeled by Integrated DNA Technologies (Coralville, USA), while all other oligos were synthesized and fluorescently labeled by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Sequences and nucleotide modifications of these oligonucleotides are summarized in Supplementary Table S1. The oligos were purified by high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) orpolyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) as specified in the table, and were directly used without further purification.

DNA substrates of different configurations were annealed by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) machine (Bio-Rad, Hercules, USA) at 95°C for 5 min and cooled to 4°C at a ramp rate of 0.1°C/s with appropriate oligo ratios. All substrates with <5% annealing byproducts were directly used after quality check by native PAGE. The oligos used to assemble different substrates are summarized in Supplementary Table S2. The general structure of each substrate is shown in Fig. 2A with fluorescent labels and lengths of single- and double-stranded regions annotated.

Protein purification

GST–AID, AID mutants, and GST. The pAcG2T-AID plasmid carrying human AID gene fused with a GST tag was kindly provided by Dr Myron Goodman at University of Southern California. AID mutants with mutations at catalytic channel and assistant patch were generated in the lab, including mut1 with K22E/R24S/R25E mutations, mut2 with R50E/K52E mutations, mut3 with R171E mutation, mut4 with R171E/R174E mutations, and mut6 with K22E/R24S/R25E/R50E/K52E/R171E/R174E mutations. GST-fAID.crystal (mentioned as fAID.crystal hereafter in main text) was generated in the lab based on a previously reported AID.crystal variant that disrupts AID–AID interactions [38]. fAID.crystal consists of full-length AID harboring the mutations F42E, H130A, R131E, F141Y, and Y145E. The GST gene, GST–AID gene, fAID.crystal gene, and five other mutant genes were independently cloned into the pFastBac1 vector to make bacmids and further generate baculoviruses using Sf21 insect cells. GST–AID and mutants were expressed and purified in the same way as reported before [19]. GST was expressed and purified in the same way as GST–AID. Specifically, infected Sf9 cells were resuspended in ice-cold lysis buffer containing 10 mM Tris (pH 7.5), 500 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 10 mM NaF, 10 mM NaHPO4, 10 mM sodium pyrophosphate, 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), and 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) supplemented with proteinase inhibitors (Sangon Biotech). After incubation on ice for 30 min, the cells were lysed by sonication followed by centrifugation at 10 000 revolutions per minute (rpm) for 30 min. The supernatant containing GST–AID or mutants was incubated with glutathione beads (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) at 4°C for 2 h, followed by extensive washing with lysis buffer. GST–AID or mutants bound to glutathione beads were activated by RNase A (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) at a final concentration of 50 µg/ml in a 30°C water bath with gently shaking for 2 min. RNase A was removed by extensive washing with cold lysis buffer. GST–AID, mutants, or GST were then eluted from the beads using an elution buffer of 50 mM Tris (pH 7.5), 500 mM NaCl, and 20 mM reduced glutathione. Eluted protein was dialyzed overnight against 20 mM Tris (pH 7.5), 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT, and 20% glycerol, then stored at −80°C in small aliquots. Purified AID mutants exhibited reduced deamination activity compared with wild-type AID (WT) (Supplementary Fig. S1) as previously reported [38]. All GST–AID used in this study was pre-activated by RNase A, except the one in Supplementary Fig. S14 which specifically mentioned as no RNase A treated.

AID–Cy5. The sulfo–Cy5–CoA was prepared as described previously [39]. Briefly, sulfo–Cy5 Mal potassium (AmBeed, Buffalo Grove, USA) was incubated with coenzyme A trilithium salt (Millipore Sigma, Burlington, USA) at 1:1.5 ratio in 100 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.1) at room temperature for 1 h, followed by addition of 5 mM cysteine to terminate the reaction. The reaction mixture was desalted with a gravity column (Yeasen, Shanghai, China). The target product sulfo–Cy5–CoA in the fractions was characterized by a HPLC equipped with 260 and 640 nm ultraviolet detectors.

Sfp phosphopantetheinyl transferase (mentioned as Sfp hereafter) was purified from Escherichia coli cells as reported previously [39]. Briefly, a vector carrying the C-terminal His6-tagged sfp gene was transformed to BL21(DE3) cells. Protein expression was induced with 1 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) until OD600 reaches 0.6 followed by shaking at 220 rpm at 25°C overnight. The harvested cells were resuspended in buffer containing 5 mM imidazole, 500 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0) and lysed with sonication. Sfp was purified with Ni-NTA beads (union-biotech, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The protein was eluted with buffer containing 250 mM imidazole, dialyzed against buffer containing 10 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 1 mM EDTA, and 10% glycerol, and stored at −80°C in small aliquots.

A carboxyl (C)-terminal ybbR-tagged GST–AID variant was generated in the lab and expressed in Sf9-infected insect cells as described above. Infected Sf9 cells were lysed, incubated with GST resin, and RNase A activated, followed by incubation with the above prepared sulfo–Cy5–CoA (1:3 relative to AID amount) and Sfp (1:1 relative to AID amount) at room temperature for 20 min and then transferred to 4°C for overnight labeling in buffer containing 50 mM 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES), pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, and 10 mM MgCl2. Unbound Cy5 dye was removed through thorough wash with lysis buffer. The GST–AID-ybbR-Cy5 (mentioned as AID–Cy5 hereafter) was eluted in the same way as above. The labeling efficiency of AID–Cy5 was 72% calculated from an sodium dodecyl sulphate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) gel with bovine serum albumin (BSA) and Cy5 dye standards.

AID.mono. Plasmid carrying AID monomer gene (AID.mono) on pFastBac1 vector was a kind gift from Dr Hao Wu at Harvard Medical School. AID.mono was purified in a similar way as reported before [38]. The detailed information on AID.mono purification can be found in Supplementary Materials.

GST-HMGB1. The detailed information on GST-HMGB1 purification can be found in Supplementary Materials.

AID deamination activity and nuclease activity test

The AID deamination assay is shown schematically in Supplementary Fig. S1B. Briefly, deamination reaction (20 µl) containing 3 pmol of DL134, 7 pmol of AID or mutants, and 5 units of uracil DNA glycosylase (Beyotime Biotechnology) in 1 × transcription buffer (40 mM Tris, 6 mM MgCl2, 2 mM spermidine, and 10 mM NaCl, pH 8.0) was incubated at 37°C for 1 h. The reaction was terminated by adding 0.4 µl of freshly prepared 10 M NaOH and heated at 95°C for 5 min. An equal volume of formamide was added and the samples were heated again at 95°C for 5 min. Reaction products were characterized by a 12% urea–PAGE (8 M). The AID–Cy5, fAID.crystal, and AID activity in buffers with different salt concentration were tested in similar condition except the NaCl concentration varied as indicated in the figures. The deamination reactions were incubated with 0.2 unit of USER enzyme (New England Biolabs, Massachusetts, USA) at 37°C for 1 h and resolved with a 12% native PAGE gel. All gels were imaged using ENFAN gel image analysis system (FIAS360F, Xi’an, China). The deamination percentage was calculated by dividing the product band intensity by the intensity of the whole lane of the same reaction. Three independent replicates were performed for AID and its mutants. No obvious nuclease activity was detected in AID and its mutants (see Supplementary Fig. S2).

Ensemble pull-down assay

Four reaction conditions were tested in the ensemble pull-down assay as illustrated in Fig. 1A: I) streptavidin beads and free solution DNA; II) streptavidin beads, free solution DNA, and AID; III) streptavidin beads with immobilized DNA and free solution DNA; IV) streptavidin beads with immobilized DNA, free solution DNA, and AID. In the pull-down assay with tailed D-loops, 5 µl of Dynabeads MyOne Streptavidin C1 beads (Invitrogen, Waltham, USA) was resuspended in 50 µl of 1 × binding buffer (5 mM Tris, 1 M NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA, and 0.05% Tween 20, pH 7.5) for each reaction. When indicated, 0.3 pmol of biotin and Cy3 labeled tailed D-loop substrate was immobilized on streptavidin beads through biotin–streptavidin interaction followed by thorough wash with 1 × binding buffer and 1 × transcription buffer supplemented with 0.05% Tween 20 to remove any unbound Cy3-tailed D-loop DNA. An equal amount of Cy5-tailed D-loop substrate was then added and mixed well with the beads. When specified, 5 pmol of AID was added to the reaction. The reactions were incubated at room temperature for 15 min with constant rotating, followed by thorough wash with 1 × transcription buffer containing 0.05% Tween 20. The captured Cy5 labeled DNA on beads was then eluted with 20 µl of 1 × transcription buffer containing 0.05% Tween 20 and 1.5% sodium dodecyl sulphate by incubating at room temperature for 5 min. The remaining beads were resuspended with 20 µl of 1 × transcription buffer containing 0.05% Tween 20 and boiled for 5 min. The eluent and the supernatant of the boiled beads were all loaded to a 12% urea–PAGE (8 M) to characterize the captured Cy5-tailed D-loop and the immobilized Cy3-tailed D-loop on the beads, respectively. The pull-down assay with R-loop substrates was performed in the same way as above except that the biotin and Cy5 labeled R-loop was immobilized on streptavidin beads and the Cy3-R-loop was free in solution. The gels were imaged for both Cy3 and Cy5 signals using ENFAN gel image analysis system. The relative fold change of the captured band intensity was calculated by dividing the captured band intensity in each reaction by that in reaction II (streptavidin beads, free solution DNA, and AID). At least three independent replicates were performed for each substrate.

Single-molecule setup

The imaging coverslips were modified in the same way as previously reported [40–42]. The smCL assays were performed on the Olympus IX83P2ZF inverted microscope equipped with the 532 nm (COHERENT, OBIS 532 nm LS 50 mW), 640 nm (COHERENT, OBIS 640 nM LX 40 mW), and 488 nm (Changchun New Industries Optoelectronics Technology, XS-488) lasers. The output lasers were collimated and reflected by a mirror (Chroma ZT405/488/532/640rpc-XT) into an oil immersion objective (Olympus, 100×, NA = 1.5) to achieve the TIRF illumination. The laser powers were set at 1.5 and 1.2 mW for 532 and 640 nm lasers, and 80% output for 488 nm laser, before the objective lens, respectively. A trigger box, which is based on the UNO board from Arduino and is provided by FluoCa Scientific (Shanghai, China), was applied to synchronize the laser excitation and the camera exposure, and also to achieve the alternative laser excitation mode during imaging. The Alexa Fluor 488 (AF488), Cy3, and Cy5 emission wavelengths passed through a bandpass filter (Chroma ZET405/488/532/642m) mounted in the microscope, and were further separated by a dichroic beam splitter (Semrock FF640-FDi01-25 × 36) mounted in the OptoSplit II (CAIRN RESEARCH). The AF488/Cy3 and Cy5 emissions were finally imaged on the two halves of a back-illuminated sCMOS camera (Teledyne Photometrics, Prime 95B) after passing the bandpass filters of ET600/50m (Chroma) and ET700/75m (Chroma), respectively.

smCL experiments

All reactions were performed at room temperature (about 22°C) in 1 × transcription buffer except where specifically indicated as no spermidine or different NaCl concentrations. Two millimolar Trolox (Millipore Sigma, Burlington, USA) and an oxygen scavenger system containing 0.8% (w/v) D-glucose (RHAWN, Shanghai, China), 165 U/ml glucose oxidase (Millipore Sigma), and 2170 U/ml catalase (Millipore Sigma) were supplemented to all reactions. Both the two-color and three-color smCL experiments in this study were performed in open chambers to allow sequential addition of the free solution Cy5 labeled DNA substrate and AID. Eight substrates of different configurations, as schematically illustrated in Fig. 2A, were tested in the two-color smCL experiments. For each reaction, 20 pM of biotin and Cy3 labeled DNA substrate was immobilized on the coverslip via the biotin–neutravidin–biotin strategy, and 5 nM of free Cy5 labeled DNA was used in the solution. The free Cy5–DNA in reaction buffer with oxygen scavenger reagents supplemented with 3% glycerol (v/v) and 1 mg/ml BSA was added into the imaging chamber which was composed of a coverslip and a silicone isolator. Images were captured immediately after sample injection. The 532 and 640 nm lasers were applied alternatively to excite the coverslip. The appearance of both Cy3 and Cy5 signals indicated the occurrence of synapsis between the immobilized and solution DNA substrates. AID, mutants, GST, or GST-HMGB1 was added to the same chamber at 100 nM and mixed well by gently pipetting up and down. Images were captured immediately after protein injection. The camera exposure time was set at 30 ms. Three movies each containing 3000 frames were captured before and after AID injection. For the three-color smCL experiments using AID–Cy5, the reactions were set up in the same way as described above except that 20 nM of AID–Cy5 was used and the free DNA was AF488 labeled. The 532, 640, and 488 nm lasers were applied alternatively to excite the coverslip. Images were captured before and after AID–Cy5 injection with 50 ms exposure time for a total of 1998 frames. At least three independent samples were analyzed for each condition.

smCL data analysis

Synapsis efficiency in two-color smCL experiments. All synapsis pairs and their corresponding single-molecule trajectories were identified and extracted using the published iSMS v2.01 software [43, 44] built-in MATLAB. The data analysis method was similar to what has been described in our previous studies [40–42]. Briefly, the Cy3 and Cy5 channels were first registered by mapping a Cy3- and Cy5-tagged duplex (121-BZ42/121-BZ44) using the iSMS software, and a mapping function was then obtained. The slide surface immobilized Cy3–molecules were then identified and quantified (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${{N}_{{\mathrm{ immobilization}}}}$\end{document}) by searching the first 100 frames of each region of interest (ROI) using the iSMS software. The colocalized Cy3 and Cy5 signal pairs, which represent the synapsis between the immobilized Cy3–molecule and the solution Cy5–molecule, were then identified by searching the Cy5 signal locating within 3-pixel range of the Cy3 spot using the above obtained mapping function through the whole video. The trajectories with colocalized Cy3 and Cy5 signal pairs were then quantified (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${{N}_{{\mathrm{ colocalization}}}}$\end{document}) after further visual inspection of each Cy3 and Cy5 signal pair. The synapsis efficiency was calculated as the ratio of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${{N}_{{\mathrm{ colocalization}}}}$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${{N}_{{\mathrm{ immobilization}}}}$\end{document}. All identified trajectories with colocalized Cy3 and Cy5 signals were exported and further used for analysis of synaptic times. The percentage of certain type of synapsis events was calculated by dividing the number of synapsis events of each type (transient, long-lived, or persistent) by the total number of synapsis events. The total number of trajectories visually checked and the final synapsis numbers for each substrate can be found in Supplementary Table S3.

Synapsis time in two-color smCL experiments. The synapsis time was calculated based on the durations of Cy5 signals within the above identified and exported trajectories. Briefly, the synapsis events were manually truncated from the trajectories, and the dwell time of each synapsis event was then calculated by multiplying the lasting frames of the synapsis event and the camera exposure time. The dwell times of all the synapsis events in each experiment were then binned for histogram plotting and exponential fitting. Note that all the dwell time histograms and the corresponding exponential fits only corresponded to the synapsis events with both beginning and end time points within the detection time window. Since two different types of synapsis events (transient and long-lived events) were included in the binned histograms, we applied the bi-exponential functions to fit the dwell histograms to obtain the fitted dwell times for both types of events. The dwell fittings of three replicates for each substrate were presented in Supplementary Fig. S10. The corresponding residuals and R-squared values of the dwell time fitting were presented in Supplementary Fig. S18 and Supplementary Table S4, which indicate good agreements of bi-exponential fits for all substrates.

Three-color smCL analysis. The data obtained from three-color smCL experiments was analyzed using the imscroll software, a MATLAB-based software package, as previously described [45–48] (https://github.com/gelles-brandeis/CoSMoS_Analysis). Channel registration was achieved through a mapping experiment using a DNA duplex (121-BZ54/121-BZ109/121-BZ42/Zhaolab_BZ27) labeled with three fluorophores (AF488, Cy3, and Cy5). Immobilized DNA molecules were first automatically detected in the 532 nm laser-excitation channel. Overlapping spots (distance < 2 pixels) were removed, and spot centers were determined by two-dimensional Gaussian fitting. Fluorescence emission at the predefined DNA locations was integrated over a 0.16 µm2 region (3 × 3 pixels) using linear interpolation to obtain Cy3 intensity time traces. The same coordinates were then mapped onto the 640 and 488 nm excitation channels to extract the corresponding Cy5 and AF488 intensity traces, respectively.

Simultaneous detection of Cy5 and AF488 signals at Cy3-defined DNA position was scored as a three-color colocalization event, indicating colocalization of Cy3–DNA, AF488–DNA, and AID–Cy5. All three-color events were manually screened from the full set of immobilized DNA molecules. Colocalization efficiency was calculated as the percentage of immobilized Cy3–DNA molecules exhibiting concurrent Cy5 and AF488 signals.

Events corresponding to AID–Cy5 binding to the immobilized Cy3–DNA and corresponding to synapsis of immobilized Cy3–DNA and solution AF488–DNA by AID–Cy5 were manually extracted from the intensity traces, respectively. The Cy5 intensities from these events were then compiled to generate histograms for binding stoichiometry analysis. A hexa-histidine (his)-tagged 19-amino-acid peptide (pep2DL-1: Biotin-PEG6-GSG-PLTQGPAK-GG-HHHHHH-COOH), which was labeled with HIS Lite™ Cy5 Tris NTA-Ni Complex (AAT Bioquest, Sunnyvale, USA), was applied as the reference for Cy5 intensity to estimate the number of AID–Cy5 binding (Supplementary Fig. S16A). The average intensity of a single Cy5 dye, determined from the reference peptide imaged in the same field of view, was used to estimate the number of AID–Cy5 molecules in each binding or colocalization event after correcting for spatial variations in Cy5 fluorescence intensity (see Supplementary Materials for details). At least three independent experiments were performed for binding stoichiometry estimation.

smFRET experiments

Two-kilobase substrate preparation. The 2-kb substrate with Cy3 and Cy5 labeled tailed D-loops at each end and a biotin label in the middle was used in the smFRET assays to test the performance of AID in mediating synapsis of two tailed D-loops within the same molecule. The detailed process for substrate preparation is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S12. DL270 and DL271 were used to amplify a 2.3-kb amplicon from the pDL64 plasmid. The 2.3-kb product contained two incompatible SapI sites at each of the two ends and two Nb.BbvCI sites in the middle which was 25 bp apart. The PCR product was purified with HighPrep PCR kit (MAGBIO, Maryland, USA) followed by incubation with SapI (New England Biolabs) and Nb.BbvCI (New England Biolabs) at 37°C for 2 h. The restriction enzymes were inactivated by incubation at 80°C for 20 min. The doubly nicked DNA was annealed with around 50-fold molar excess of DL265, which contained an internal biotin label and shared the same sequence with the DNA between the two Nb.BbvCI sites. The annealing reaction was performed with a PCR machine by heating at 95°C for 5 min followed by cooling to room temperature with a ramp rate of 0.1°C/s. The DL265 annealed DNA was then purified with HighPrep PCR kit before ligation.

The tailed D-loop substrates with Cy3 and Cy5 labeled at the tail portion were assembled as mentioned before. The oligo compositions for assembling the tailed D-loop substrates were shown in Supplementary Table S2. Assembled tailed D-loops were characterized by native PAGE to verify their quality before being used in ligation reaction. The Cy3- and Cy5-tailed D-loops each contained a 3 nt 5′ overhang that was compatible to one of the two ends of the 2-kb substrate resulted from SapI digestion.

The ligation reaction with 3 pmol of DL265 annealed DNA, 20 pmol of each tailed D-loop with Cy3 or Cy5 label, 2 units of ligase (Roche, Basel, Switzerland), and 1 mM ATP in 1 × ligase buffer (Roche) was incubated at 15°C overnight followed by inactivation at 65°C for 20 min. The ligated product was directly immobilized on the coverslip in smFRET assay without further purification.

smFRET imaging. The smFRET reactions were set up in the same way as smCL reactions as mentioned above. Briefly, reaction with the oxygen scavenger reagents in 1 × transcription buffer supplemented with 3% glycerol (v/v) and 1 mg/ml BSA was added into the open chamber. Both 532 and 640 nm lasers were applied alternatively to excite the coverslip. The appearance of both Cy3 and Cy5 signals indicated the successful ligation of the tailed D-loops to the substrate, and the appearance of FRET signal between Cy3 and Cy5 dyes (FRET pair) indicated the synapsis of the two tailed D-loops within the circular substrate. The camera exposure time was set at 30 ms with 30 ms interval time. Three movies each containing 500 frames were captured to measure the background FRET pair efficiency. Five picomoles of GST–AID or AID.mono was added to the same chamber followed by immediate capturing of another three movies. The total number of trajectories visually checked and the final FRET pair numbers for each protein can be found in Supplementary Table S3.

Results

AID enhances proximity between tailed D-loops and between R-loops in bulk solution

Previous studies have reported the messenger RNA (mRNA) tether model in which AID simultaneously engages two nucleic acid strands within a transcription bubble [49,50]. The binding of AID to the new mRNA transcript through the assistant patch can stabilize its binding to the substrate and thus facilitate its catalytic deamination activity on the NTS. In addition, biochemical and cellular studies have suggested that AID can exist in dimeric or oligomeric states under certain conditions [38, 51, 52]. These observations raised the possibility that AID, through its dimer or oligomer state, may interact with two individual multistrand nucleic acid molecules simultaneously and bring them into close proximity, resulting in the synapsis of the two DNA molecules via this AID protein bridge.

AID was purified after RNase A treatment and assayed for activity as previously described [19] (Supplementary Figs S1 and S2). To examine whether AID can mediate synapsis of multistrand substrates, we first performed an ensemble pull-down assay to test the capability of AID in mediating the capture of the solution Cy5-tailed D-loop substrate, which mimic key structural features of R-loop-associated DNA intermediates, by the magnetic bead-bound Cy3-tailed D-loop (Fig. 1A). Control reactions without AID showed only a background level of nonspecific absorbance of Cy5–DNA on the beads (reactions I and III; Fig. 1B). Comparable Cy3 signals in the boiled-bead fractions of reactions III and IV were observed, verifying the same amount of immobilized Cy3-tailed D-loop on the beads in these two reactions (Fig. 1B, right). AID in the reaction caused some nonspecific absorbance of Cy5-tailed D-loop to the beads lacking immobilized Cy3–DNA (reaction II). Importantly, when both AID and immobilized Cy3–DNA were present, a reproducible ∼2-fold increase in Cy5 capture was observed compared with control conditions (reactions IV versus II; Fig. 1B and C). These results indicate that AID can enhance the proximity of two tailed D-loop molecules in solution.

Considering the chemistry difference of ssDNA and single-stranded RNA in tailed D-loop and R-loop substrates, we further performed the ensemble pull-down experiment using the R-loop substrates. The same experimental design was used except the solution R-loop contains a Cy3 label and the beads-bound R-loop is labeled with Cy5 dye. Comparable Cy5 signals in the boiled-bead fractions indicate similar amounts of immobilized Cy5-R-loop in reactions III and IV (Fig. 1D, right). As observed for tailed D-loops, minimal Cy3 signal was detected in the absence of AID (reactions I and III), whereas AID alone produced a modest background level of capture (reaction II; Fig. 1D). The combined presence of AID and immobilized R-loops resulted in more than a twofold increase in Cy3 signal relative to AID-only control (reactions IV versus II; Fig. 1D and E). Taken together, these data indicate that AID can enhance proximity between multistranded nucleic acid substrates in bulk solution, including both tailed D-loops and R-loops.

R-loop and tailed D-loop substrates exhibit enhanced AID-mediated synapsis in single-molecule assays

We further developed an smCL assay to explore the details and mechanisms of the AID-mediated synapsis. Eight DNA substrates in different configurations were prepared and analyzed in the smCL assays (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Fig. S3A). For each substrate, Cy3-labeled DNA molecules were immobilized on passivated imaging surfaces through biotin-neutravidin interactions, whereas Cy5-labeled DNA molecules were introduced in solution. Colocalized Cy3 and Cy5 signals were scored as synapsis events.

Minimal Cy5 signal was detected in the absence of immobilized Cy3–DNA (Supplementary Fig. S4), indicating low nonspecific adsorption of solution-phase DNA to the imaging surface. In addition, reactions lacking AID exhibited only background levels of Cy3-Cy5 colocalization across all substrate types (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Fig. S3B), suggesting negligible spontaneous association between immobilized and solution-phase DNA molecules under these conditions. Moreover, double-stranded DNA substrates exhibited negligible levels of synapsis in the presence of AID (Fig. 2B and C, and Supplementary Fig. S3B), consistent with limited nonspecific interactions between AID and fully duplex DNA. For the other seven substrates, addition of AID increased synapsis in a substrate-dependent manner (Supplementary Fig. S3B). Quantified synapsis efficiencies for ssDNA, overhang DNA, Y-DNA, bubble DNA, D-loop, tailed D-loop, and R-loop substrates were 6.4%, 3.8%, 7.4%, 8.3%, 9.9%, 10.9%, and 12.0%, respectively (Fig. 2C). Notably, the highest synapsis efficiencies were achieved from tailed D-loop and R-loop substrates, indicating that multistranded nucleic acid architectures strongly favor AID-mediated synaptic interactions. Because the assay reports only colocalization events detected within the observation window, the measured efficiencies likely underestimate the total extent of synapsis formation. Nevertheless, the pronounced substrate dependence demonstrates that AID preferentially promotes synapsis on multistranded DNA substrates.

Several control experiments further supported the specificity of these observations. Spermidine exerted only minimal effects on AID-mediated synapsis of ssDNA and tailed D-loop substrates (Supplementary Fig. S5), excluding substantial contributions from buffer components. Moreover, purified GST alone produced negligible synapsis (Supplementary Fig. S6). Similarly, GST-HMGB1, despite retaining detectable binding to R-loop substrates, did not efficiently promote synapsis (Supplementary Fig. S7). Together, these controls confirm that the observed synapsis is specifically mediated by AID rather than by nonspecific protein aggregation or GST-mediated bridging effects.

The AID-mediated synapsis was further examined under elevated salt conditions using ssDNA and tailed D-loop substrates (Supplementary Fig. S8). Although the observed efficiency decreased with increasing ionic strength, AID-mediated synapsis remained detectable at salt concentrations up to 100 mM. Consistent with this observation, biochemical activity assays showed reduced AID catalytic activity under elevated salt conditions (Supplementary Fig. S8E). Notably, tailed D-loop substrates continued to exhibit increased synapsis relative to ssDNA across the tested conditions (Supplementary Fig. S8A and C). These results indicate that AID-mediated synapsis of multistranded substrates can be maintained over a range of ionic strengths.

Collectively, these single-molecule results indicate that AID preferentially promotes structure-dependent synapsis. In particular, bifurcated DNA architectures within multistranded substrates substantially enhance synapsis efficiency. The increased synapsis observed for R-loop and tailed D-loop substrates likely reflects the ability of AID to simultaneously engage the single-stranded NTS and an adjacent nucleic acid tail through its two nucleic acid binding surfaces, thereby stabilizing the synaptic complex.

The bifurcated configuration in the multistranded substrates helps stabilize the AID-mediated synaptic complexes

We next characterized the stability of the formed synaptic complexes composed of substrates of different configurations by analyzing single-molecule fluorescence trajectories and synapsis dwell times. Three general classes of AID-mediated synapsis events were observed across the seven substrate types (Fig. 3A). Transient synapsis events persisted for <1 s, long-lived synapsis events persisted between 1 and 90 s, and persistent synapsis events remained stable for longer than 90 s. The transient synapsis likely reflects rapid and dynamic encounters in which solution Cy5–molecule is briefly brought into proximity with immobilized Cy3–molecule by AID followed by rapid dissociation. In contrast, long-lived synapsis events represent more stable complexes that persist for several seconds before dissociation of the Cy5–DNA and associated AID molecules. Persistent synapsis events correspond to highly stable complexes in which Cy5–DNA and AID remain associated with immobilized Cy3–DNA throughout the observation window. The strong dependence of these events on both AID and substrate architecture supports their assignment as bona fide synaptic complexes. Representative fluorescence trajectories illustrating these three classes of synapsis events are shown for all seven substrates (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Fig. S9).

We then analyzed the percentages of the three types of synapsis events for each substrate since this can be used to reflect the stability of the synapsis for different substrates. The larger percentage of long-lived and persistent synapsed molecules represents a more stable synapsis of the substrate, which might provide a longer time window for AID to perform its deamination activity as well as for the subsequent end-joining. Synaptic complexes formed on ssDNA, overhang DNA, and Y-DNA were dominated by transient events, which accounted for >65% of total synapsis events (Fig. 3B). In contrast, bubble DNA, D-loop, tailed D-loop, and R-loop substrates exhibited substantially increased fractions of long-lived and persistent events. Notably, these substrates all contain bifurcated multistranded architectures that could enable simultaneous engagement of multiple nucleic acid elements by the distinct nucleic acid-binding surfaces of AID, thereby stabilizing the formed synaptic complexes.

We further calculated the dwell time of the formed transient and long-lived synapsis events for all the substrates to directly evaluate the stability of formed synaptic complexes (Fig. 3C and D, and Supplementary Fig. S10). The characteristic dwell times of transient events (t1) were relatively similar across all substrates, suggesting that the initial encounter dynamics are largely substrate-independent. In contrast, the dwell times associated with long-lived events (t2) varied substantially depending on substrate architecture. Multistranded substrates containing bifurcated structures, including bubble DNA, D-loop, tailed D-loop, and R-loop substrates, exhibited consistently increased t2 values compared with ssDNA (Fig. 3D), indicating formation of more stable synaptic complexes.

Together, these results indicate that branched multistranded nucleic acid architectures promote formation of more stable AID-mediated synaptic complexes.

Nucleic acid-binding surfaces of AID are required for efficient synapsis

Structural study reveals AID has two nucleic acid-binding surfaces, the catalytic channel and assistant patch. The two binding surfaces are critical for AID to perform its deamination activities [38]. We examined the direct role of these interaction surfaces in synapsis. Five AID mutants with mutations in nucleic acid-binding pockets were generated based on a previous study [38]: mut1 (K22E/R24S/R25E) and mut2 (R50E/K52E) with mutations in the catalytic channel, mut3 (R171E) and mut4 (R171E/R174E) with assistant patch mutated, and mut6 (K22E/R24S/R25E/R50E/K52E/R171E/R174E) containing mutations in both interaction surfaces (Fig. 4A).

We tested the capability of these mutants in mediating synapsis of ssDNA and R-loop substrates. Compared with WT, all five AID mutants exhibited significantly reduced synapsis efficiencies on ssDNA (Fig. 4B and C, and Supplementary Fig. S11A and B). Mut6, with mutations at both interaction surfaces, displayed the most pronounced defect in synapsis efficiency compared with other mutants. Because flexible ssDNA substrates may simultaneously engage both nucleic acid-binding surfaces of AID, mutations within a single interaction pocket likely partially impair substrate engagement, whereas simultaneous disruption of both pockets substantially disrupts nucleic acid binding and consequently impairs synapsis formation. Similar decreases in synapsis efficiency were observed for R-loop substrates (Fig. 4D and E, and Supplementary Fig. S11C and D). Consistent with these observations, electrophoretic mobility shift assays demonstrated decreased substrate binding by the AID mutants (Supplementary Fig. S11E, shifted probe percentage was calculated from the reduction in the free probe signal), suggesting that impaired synapsis primarily results from reduced nucleic acid engagement. Notably, mut6 did not exhibit a substantially greater reduction in R-loop synapsis than mut3 or mut4, in contrast to the stronger additive effect observed on ssDNA substrates. This observation suggests that disruption of the assistant patch exerts a particularly strong effect on R-loop recognition and stabilization, consistent with a specialized role for this interaction surface in engaging structurally complex multistranded nucleic acid architectures.

To further assess the functional relevance of these interaction surfaces, CSR complementation assays were performed in CH12F3 Aicda−/− cells reconstituted with wild-type or mutant AID constructs. Wild-type AID restored measurable CSR activity above background levels, whereas all mutant constructs exhibited substantially reduced CSR efficiencies (Fig. 4F). Although these cellular assays do not directly measure synapsis, the results may indicate an important contribution of AID nucleic acid interaction surfaces to CSR-associated activities.

Together, these results suggest the important roles of the two nucleic acid binding surfaces in mediating specific synapsis events, especially for R-loop substrate. These results also support a model in which multivalent nucleic acid interactions mediated by distinct AID interaction surfaces stabilize higher-order synaptic complexes.

AID promotes intramolecular synapsis of multistranded DNA substrates

We next examined whether AID can facilitate synaptic interactions between two DNA ends within the same molecule, a configuration that mimics the intramolecular nature of CSR. An smFRET assay was developed using a 2-kb DNA substrate containing Cy3- and Cy5-labeled tailed D-loop structures positioned at opposing termini (Fig. 5A). The occurrence of FRET signals between Cy3 and Cy5 fluorophores within the same DNA molecule was used as a readout for intramolecular synapsis.

In the absence of AID, low levels of FRET signals were detected. Addition of AID increased the fraction of FRET-positive molecules, resulting in an apparent FRET-associated population of ∼20% (Fig. 5B), representing a ∼5-fold increase relative to control conditions. These results indicate that AID can mediate synapsis between two tailed D-loops within the same molecule. Single-molecule trajectory analysis further revealed heterogeneous synaptic behaviors, including transient, long-lived, and persistent synapsis events, and the distribution of those events is consistent with the synaptic patterns observed in the smCL assays (Fig. 5C and Supplementary Fig. S12). The agreement between the smCL and smFRET assays strongly supports that the AID-mediated synapsis events are reliable and based on specific DNA–AID interactions.

We further examined a monomeric variant of AID (AID.mono), generated by truncations and mutations that reduce oligomerization propensity [38]. AID.mono was purified as previously described and verified by size exclusion chromatography and SDS–PAGE (Supplementary Fig. S13A and B). In the presence of AID.mono, FRET-positive events were also detected, albeit significantly reduced (∼10%, representing an approximately threefold decrease after background subtraction) compared with wild-type AID (Supplementary Fig. S13D and E). Single-molecule trajectory analyses further revealed transient, long-lived, and persistent synapsis events for AID.mono (Supplementary Fig. S13C and F). Compared with wild-type AID, the reduced FRET-associated population observed for AID.mono suggests that higher-order AID organization likely contributes to the efficiency of intramolecular DNA synapsis. Because AID.mono lacks the C-terminal region in addition to exhibiting reduced oligomerization propensity, its phenotype should be interpreted with appropriate caution, as additional effects beyond impaired self-association cannot be excluded [34–36].

Higher-order AID assembly is associated with synaptic complex formation

We further explore how AID mediates synapsis by examining the assembly state of AID using TEM. RNase A-activated AID, which is catalytically active [19] and was used throughout this study, predominantly exhibited particle sizes consistent with a monomeric form comparable to AID.mono (12.0 versus 15.0 nm), whereas inactive AID (no RNase A activation) displayed larger heterogeneous assemblies (∼49.4 nm) (Supplementary Fig. S14). Upon addition of tailed D-loop substrates, activated AID showed a shift toward larger particle sizes (∼39.8 nm), suggesting the formation of higher-order assemblies in the presence of structured DNA substrates. AID.mono also formed detectable higher-order species in the presence of the tailed D-loop substrates, although with reduced average particle sizes (∼24.2 nm), likely reflecting its engineered constraints on multimerization. These observations suggest that AID promotes tailed D-loop synapsis likely through DNA-triggered AID assembly into higher-order states. These results further suggest that the long-recognized aggregation of purified AID may arise from its association with abundant cellular RNA, thereby limiting its accessibility to other nucleic acid substrates.

We next quantified AID stoichiometry using a three-color smCL assay (Fig. 6A). AID–Cy5 was generated by Sfp-catalyzed transfer of Cy5–CoA to the ybbR tag fused to GST–AID at C-terminal. The labeled protein retained deamination activity, although some reduction compared with unlabeled AID (Supplementary Fig. S15A and B). A Cy5-labeled reference peptide (Cy5-peptide) was used to calibrate fluorescence intensity for estimation of AID copy number (Supplementary Fig. S16A). With this reference, we studied the binding stoichiometry of AID–Cy5 to the immobilized Cy3-tailed D-loop in the absence of synapsis. Gaussian-fitting revealed one major intensity peak for AID–Cy5 binding, which exhibit intensity level comparable to those of the reference peptide (Fig. 6B and Supplementary Fig. S16B), indicating that one AID molecule predominately binds to individual tailed D-loop substrate.

We next analyzed three-color colocalization events (Cy3, Cy5, and AF488), which report synapsis between immobilized Cy3-tailed D-loop and solution-phase AF488-tailed D-loop mediated by AID–Cy5. Two classes of synapsis events were observed (Fig. 6C). Type I events showed simultaneous appearance of AF488 and Cy5 signals (Fig. 6C and Supplementary Fig. S16C), consistent with binding of a pre-assembled AID–DNA complex to the immobilized substrate, indicative of a protein–DNA-mediated synapsis pathway. Type II events displayed sequential Cy5 binding followed by AF488 recruitment and a further increased Cy5 intensity (Fig. 6C and Supplementary Fig. S16D), consistent with interactions between pre-assembled AID–DNA complexes and suggesting a protein–protein-mediated synapsis pathway. Type I events accounted for the majority (>80%) of observed synaptic events (Fig. 6D), suggesting that AID–DNA interactions represent a primary route for synaptic complex formation.

Analysis of AID stoichiometry within synaptic events revealed a broad distribution of AID copy numbers, with a substantial fraction (>50%) involving two or more AID molecules (Fig. 6E). This fraction was higher than that observed in binding-only events, where predominantly one to two AID molecules were detected. These results suggest that multimeric AID assemblies are enriched in synaptic complexes and contribute to synapsis.

To directly examine the contribution of AID self-assembly to DNA synapsis, we generated a full-length AID mutant (fAID.crystal) harboring the same amino acids substitutions as the previously described AID.crystal variant, which was reported with disrupted AID–AID interactions [38]. The purified fAID.crystal retained robust deamination activity and exhibited even higher catalytic activity than wild-type AID (Supplementary Fig. S15C and D). In striking contrast, fAID.crystal exhibited a profound defect in synapsis of tailed D-loop substrate (Fig. 6F and G). Thus, disruption of AID self-association selectively impairs DNA synapsis despite enhanced catalytic activity, demonstrating that higher-order AID assembly is required for efficient synaptic complex formation. These findings identify fAID.crystal as a separation-of-function mutant and establish DNA synapsis as a mechanistically distinct functional output of AID beyond cytosine deamination.

Finally, cellular imaging of co-expressed AID-mApple and AID-EGFP in U2OS cells revealed punctate nuclear foci, in contrast to diffuse distribution of control fluorophores (Fig. 7A and Supplementary Fig. S17). These observations are consistent with the ability of AID to form higher-order assemblies in a cellular context, although the exact molecular nature of these structures remains to be further defined. The cellular imaging experiments were performed in U2OS cells because their large nuclear area facilitate visualization of subnuclear AID assemblies (B cells have much smaller nuclei).

Collectively, these results indicate that higher-order AID assembly is closely associated with formation of DNA synaptic complexes and support a model in which DNA-induced AID organization promotes recombination-associated DNA–DNA interactions.

Discussion

Bringing switch (S) regions into spatial proximity is essential for efficient CSR and for limiting aberrant chromosomal translocations. Previous studies suggested the possibility that AID contributes to stabilization of interactions between S regions in B cells, but this suggestion remained undefined in all molecular aspects [33, 36].

Here, we find that AID promotes synapsis between multistranded DNA substrates, including R-loops and tailed D-loops, as supported by ensemble biochemical assays and smCL analyses (Figs 1 and 2). The efficiency and stability of AID-mediated synapsis are influenced by DNA structural context and require intact nucleic acid binding pockets of AID (Figs 3 and 4). In addition, AID is capable of promoting intramolecular synapsis between two tailed D-loops within a single DNA molecule (Fig. 5), suggesting that it can support proximity between distal DNA regions in a constrained DNA context. Negative-stain TEM reveals substrate-induced formation of higher-order AID assemblies associated with synaptic substrates, and three-color single-molecule analyses further indicate that AID assembly accompanies synapsis formation (Fig. 6). AID exhibits punctate nuclear localization patterns in cells (Fig. 7A), which are consistent with the ability of AID to form higher-order assemblies.

Importantly, we identified a separation-of-function AID mutant (fAID.crystal) that retains, and in our assays exceeds, the deamination activity of wild-type AID yet is profoundly defective in DNA synapsis (Fig. 6). Because this mutant disrupts AID–AID interactions [38] while preserving catalytic competence, the synapsis defect cannot be explained by loss of deaminase activity. Instead, these results demonstrate that higher-order AID assembly is specifically required for efficient synaptic complex formation. The uncoupling of synapsis from catalysis therefore reveals that AID possesses at least two mechanistically separable functional outputs: cytosine deamination and structural organization of recombination-associated DNA substrates. This distinction has important implications for understanding how AID contributes to CSR beyond lesion generation. Notably, the same mutant has previously been reported to exhibit severe CSR defects in cells, providing independent genetic support for the physiological relevance of the synapsis activity [38].

Multiple control experiments further support the specificity of these observations. GST alone did not promote efficient synapsis, and GST-HMGB1, although capable of binding R-loop substrates, did not recapitulate the characteristic AID-dependent bridging behavior (Supplementary Fig. S6 and S7). In addition, RNase A-activated AID appeared predominantly monomeric by negative-stain TEM before DNA addition, whereas larger assemblies were observed after addition of tailed D-loop substrates (Supplementary Fig. S14). Finally, mutations disrupting the nucleic acid-binding pockets of AID impaired both substrate binding and synapsis (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. S11). Together, these controls argue that AID-mediated DNA synapsis arises from specific AID–DNA interactions coupled to DNA-induced higher-order assembly, rather than from nonspecific aggregation, GST-driven dimerization, or generic DNA-binding activity.

Based on these observations, we propose a working model in which AID engages multistranded DNA structures through its catalytic channel and assistant patch, while DNA binding promotes assembly of AID into higher-order oligomeric structures. Within regions of transcription-dependent chromatin proximity, these higher-order AID assemblies may in turn stabilize local DNA–DNA proximity within recombination-associated S regions (Fig. 7B). In this model, R-loop formation at actively transcribed S regions generates structurally distinct DNA substrates that can be preferentially recognized by AID. Such interactions could increase the local concentration of AID at S regions and facilitate multivalent nucleic acid engagement, thereby stabilizing synaptic interactions. The dual roles of AID as both a mutator enzyme and a structural factor could increase the likelihood that AID-induced DNA lesions are resolved as productive CSR events, while also facilitating subsequent repair by NHEJ factors.

Although our data do not directly address chromosomal-scale organization of S regions, they support a model in which AID contributes to nanoscale organization of recombination-associated nucleic acid structures. This model is compatible with previous studies showing that chromatin loop extrusion, cohesin-mediated chromatin organization, and locus contraction bring donor and acceptor S regions into spatial proximity during CSR [23, 24]. These chromosomal-scale processes establish a permissive chromatin microenvironment in which distal S regions are positioned within micrometer-scale proximity [23, 24]. Within this environment, we propose that AID functions as a local molecular organizer that engages exposed multistranded DNA structures and further stabilize switch-region interactions at the molecular scale. Notably, S regions are highly enriched in repetitive, G-rich sequences that favor R-loop formation and AID targeting [9, 10, 13–15], suggesting that DNA structure itself contributes to localized AID accumulation and assembly of AID. Our model is also consistent with the reported cell-cycle regulation of AID, which predominantly functions during G1 phase [53–55]. During this stage, actively transcribed switch regions and higher-order chromatin organization may provide a favorable structural environment for AID-mediated synapsis.

Beyond CSR, AID-mediated interactions with multistranded DNA structures may also be relevant to genome instability events such as chromosomal translocations involving the immunoglobulin heavy chain locus and oncogenes such as MYC, where both AID hotspot motifs and R-loop formation have been implicated [6, 7, 56–59]. Moreover, our findings suggest that AID, in addition to its established catalytic role, possesses intrinsic architectural properties on structured nucleic acid substrates.

Our study provides evidence that purified AID possesses an intrinsic capacity to bridge nucleic acid molecules; however, contributions from nonspecific or nonproductive higher-order AID–nucleic acid assemblies cannot be completely excluded. Several limitations of this study should be considered. Our single-molecule and biochemical assays used purified AID and defined nucleic acid substrates, and thus do not fully recapitulate the chromatin environment or regulatory complexity of endogenous immunoglobulin switch regions during CSR. Although multiple lines of evidence, including substrate-dependent synapsis efficiencies and kinetics, reduced synapsis by AID variants impaired in nucleic acid-binding or oligomerization, and AID–Cy5 stoichiometry analyses, support a model in which AID promotes nucleic acid bridging through regulated assembly, these experiments cannot fully distinguish productive bridging from nonspecific nucleic acid-induced AID assemblies. Moreover, our cellular imaging experiments demonstrate AID colocalization but do not directly establish AID oligomerization or DNA synapsis at endogenous Ig loci. Finally, while R-loops and tailed D-loops display enhanced synapsis properties compared with simpler substrates, the moderate magnitude of these differences suggests that substrate recognition alone is unlikely to determine CSR specificity. It is likely that intrinsic AID-mediated nucleic acid bridging cooperates with chromatin context, transcription-associated structures, and B-cell-specific repair factors to promote productive CSR. Future studies combining endogenous AID labeling [32], live-cell imaging, and chromatin-based approaches will be important to determine whether the AID-mediated nucleic acid bridging activity observed in vitro also occurs at endogenous immunoglobulin loci and how it is coordinated with transcription-associated structures and DNA repair pathways during CSR. Structural studies will further help define the architecture of AID-mediated synaptic complexes, including the molecular organization of AID and the spatial arrangement of bridged nucleic acid substrates such as R-loops and tailed D-loops.
