Bronchoscopic recognition of tracheobronchial lesions in severe varicella pneumonia after resolution of the characteristic rash: A case report
Authors: Lei Xu, Shuang Peng, Zhongjun Huo, Zhixiong Fang, Yuqing Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVaricella pneumonia is a serious complication of primary varicella-zoster virus (VZV) infection in adults and may be associated with substantial morbidity and mortality, despite being vaccine-preventable. Poor outcomes are often related to rapid disease progression and delayed initiation of antiviral therapy. Here, we report a case of severe varicella pneumonia with tracheobronchial involvement in a 35-year-old man with diabetes mellitus. The patient developed progressive dyspnea five days after rash onset, by which time most cutaneous lesions had already crusted. Bronchoscopy revealed scattered pustular lesions involving the distal trachea, carina, and main bronchi, providing direct visual evidence of airway involvement. Targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS) of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) detected VZV without evidence of other pathogens. The patient improved rapidly after intravenous acyclovir and respiratory support, and follow-up chest computed tomography five days later showed marked resolution of pulmonary infiltrates. This case demonstrates that bronchoscopic recognition of characteristic airway lesions may aid diagnosis when cutaneous manifestations have crusted or are absent and may provide information about the extent of respiratory tract involvement. BALF molecular testing can provide complementary etiological confirmation and assist in evaluating possible co-pathogens.Keywords: Varicella-zoster virus, Varicella pneumonia, Tracheobronchial involvement, Bronchoscopy, Targeted next-generation sequencing

Introduction

Varicella-zoster virus (VZV) causes primary varicella, a disease that is usually self-limiting in children but may lead to severe complications in adults [1], [2]. Varicella pneumonia is among the most serious complications of primary VZV infection in adults and can progress rapidly to respiratory failure, particularly when antiviral therapy is delayed [1], [2]. Although the diagnosis is often suggested by the presence of a characteristic vesicular rash, this clinical clue may be less apparent in patients who present after the skin lesions have crusted or begun to resolve [1], [2]. In such cases, the association between recent varicella and progressive pulmonary infiltrates may be overlooked, especially in patients with underlying risk factors or possible co-infections.

Tracheobronchial involvement should be considered part of the spectrum of varicella respiratory disease rather than a distinct rare entity. Bronchoscopic recognition of airway lesions may provide important diagnostic evidence when respiratory symptoms predominate and cutaneous manifestations are resolving or absent. Previous reports have also suggested that the extent and depth of airway lesions may have potential prognostic relevance [3], [4], [5].

In addition, molecular testing of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) may help establish the etiological diagnosis and evaluate for concurrent pathogens [6]. Such testing should be regarded as complementary evidence for confirming VZV infection and assessing possible co-pathogens.

Here, we report a case of severe varicella pneumonia with tracheobronchial lesions in an adult man with diabetes mellitus, in whom bronchoscopic recognition of characteristic airway lesions aided diagnosis after the cutaneous eruption had largely resolved, while BALF tNGS provided complementary etiological confirmation.

Case presentation

A 35-year-old man presented to a local hospital with a two-day history of fever and rash. After three days of oral acyclovir therapy, his fever resolved, but he subsequently developed a progressively worsening dry cough and dyspnea. The temporal progression from improving cutaneous symptoms to worsening respiratory manifestations raised concern for ongoing visceral VZV involvement.

On admission to our hospital, his oxygen saturation was 92% while breathing room air. He was afebrile, with a respiratory rate of 24 breaths/min. Chest auscultation revealed coarse crackles over both lower lung fields. Cutaneous examination revealed widespread crusted maculopapular lesions on erythematous bases involving the face, neck, trunk, and upper and lower extremities. Representative lesions on the face, trunk, and neck are shown in Fig. 1. The classic “dew drops on a rose petal” appearance of varicella was no longer evident. He reported no known sick contacts or recent travel. His medical history was significant for diabetes mellitus. He was not taking regular medications and had no known drug allergies.

Chest computed tomography (CT) performed on admission showed multiple bilateral pulmonary nodules of variable size, many of which were surrounded by ground-glass opacities, consistent with halo signs (Fig. 2). These radiological findings, together with the recent history of varicella infection, raised suspicion of varicella pneumonia; however, alternative or concurrent pulmonary infections could not be excluded. A diagnosis of bilateral pneumonia was made, and the patient was admitted for further management. Intravenous piperacillin-tazobactam and acyclovir were started empirically. Within hours of admission, his dyspnea worsened and signs of respiratory distress developed, with oxygen saturation decreasing to 85% while breathing room air. Chest auscultation demonstrated worsening bilateral crackles, with diffuse polyphonic expiratory wheezes throughout both lung fields, and his respiratory rate increased to at least 30 breaths/min. Supplemental oxygen was immediately administered via a standard face mask at 10 L/min. Arterial blood gas analysis while receiving this oxygen regimen showed hypoxemic respiratory failure: pH 7.42, PaO2 48.8 mmHg, PaCO2 30.2 mmHg, and HCO3- 20.9 mmol/L. Because of increasing oxygen requirements, the patient was transferred to a high-dependency unit and started on humidified high-flow oxygen therapy.

Initial chest computed tomography findings. Axial chest CT images on admission showing multiple bilateral pulmonary nodules and patchy infiltrates, several with surrounding ground-glass opacity consistent with a halo sign.

Given the progressive hypoxemia, prominent respiratory symptoms, and the need to evaluate possible alternative or co-pathogens, flexible bronchoscopy was performed on hospital day 2 under appropriate infection-control precautions. Bronchoscopy revealed multiple 2–5 mm pustular lesions on the mucosa of the distal trachea, carina, and both main bronchi, similar in appearance to cutaneous varicella lesions (Fig. 3). These characteristic endobronchial lesions provided additional evidence supporting respiratory tract involvement by VZV [4], [5]. Purulent secretions were also noted in the right middle lobe bronchus.

The patient was weaned off high-flow oxygen therapy after 72 h. His clinical status and laboratory parameters improved: dyspnea resolved, and his oxygen requirement progressively decreased. After completing a course of intravenous acyclovir, he was discharged on hospital day 7. At discharge, his oxygen saturation had improved to 95% on room air, and repeat chest CT performed before discharge showed substantial resolution of the bilateral pulmonary infiltrates (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Adult varicella pneumonia is a serious complication of primary varicella-zoster virus (VZV) infection and may progress rapidly to hypoxemic respiratory failure [1], [2]. Early recognition and timely antiviral therapy are important; however, diagnosis may be challenging when the characteristic vesicular rash is atypical, resolving, or no longer evident. In adults, pulmonary involvement may progress after cutaneous lesions have crusted, potentially obscuring the relationship between recent varicella infection and respiratory deterioration. Therefore, the resolution of skin lesions should not be considered evidence that visceral VZV involvement has resolved.

The present case illustrates this diagnostic challenge. The patient developed progressive hypoxemia after most cutaneous lesions had already crusted. Although the recent history of varicella infection and chest CT findings of bilateral pulmonary nodules with surrounding ground-glass opacities (halo signs) supported the diagnosis of varicella pneumonia [7], the absence of active vesicular lesions created diagnostic uncertainty. Bronchoscopy subsequently demonstrated multiple pustular lesions involving the distal trachea, carina, and main bronchi. Similar bronchoscopic appearances of airway lesions associated with VZV infection have been previously described, supporting the interpretation that these lesions represent direct respiratory tract involvement rather than an incidental finding [4], [5]. These endobronchial lesions closely resembled the patient’s cutaneous lesions and provided direct evidence of airway involvement by VZV.

Tracheobronchial involvement should be interpreted as part of the spectrum of varicella respiratory disease rather than as a distinct rare clinical entity. Previous reports have described bronchial mucosal involvement in varicella pneumonia, including vesicular, white-coated, and ulcerative lesions of the airway mucosa [3], [4], [5]. Importantly, airway lesions are not merely descriptive findings. A systematic review of varicella pneumonia cases with bronchoscopic evaluation demonstrated that the extent and depth of bronchial ulceration were associated with clinical outcomes, with extensive and deep ulcerations observed in patients with poor outcomes [3]. These findings suggest that bronchoscopic assessment may provide information regarding the severity of respiratory tract involvement. However, the prognostic significance of airway lesions requires further validation, as conclusions cannot be drawn from a single case.

Bronchoscopy is not routinely performed in patients with suspected varicella pneumonia because of procedural risks, particularly in patients with hypoxemic respiratory failure, and because of the potential risk of airborne transmission [8]. Nevertheless, bronchoscopy may be considered in selected patients with progressive respiratory symptoms, resolving or absent cutaneous manifestations, and persistent diagnostic uncertainty. In this case, bronchoscopy contributed to diagnostic clarification by demonstrating characteristic airway lesions. Targeted next-generation sequencing of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid further supported the diagnosis by confirming VZV infection and identifying no additional pathogens [4]. Therefore, molecular testing should be considered a complementary diagnostic approach for confirming etiology and assessing possible co-pathogens.

This case highlights two clinically important lessons. First, clinicians should maintain suspicion for varicella pneumonia in adults with recent varicella infection who develop progressive respiratory symptoms, even when cutaneous lesions have already crusted. Second, bronchoscopic recognition of airway lesions may provide valuable diagnostic information when the typical skin manifestations are no longer evident and may also offer clues regarding the extent of respiratory tract involvement, while molecular testing of BALF can provide complementary etiological confirmation and help evaluate for co-pathogens.

The main limitation of this report is that it describes a single patient; therefore, the diagnostic and prognostic implications of bronchoscopic findings and BALF molecular testing cannot be generalized. Further studies are required to better define the role of these approaches in varicella pneumonia.
