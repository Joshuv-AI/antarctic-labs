Convection injects labile particulate organic carbon to the deep ocean
Authors: Martí Galí, María Andrea Orihuela-García, Yohan Ruprich-Robert, Vladimir Lapin, María Sánchez-Urrea, Marcos Fontela, Joan Llort, Valentina Sicardi, Raffaele Bernardello
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Vertical fluxes of particulate organic carbon (POC) are central to the oceanic carbon cycle, yet the role and interannual variability of convective POC transport remain poorly constrained. Autonomous profilers drifting in the Labrador and Irminger Seas detected recurrent winter-spring chlorophyll a fluorescence peaks at 1000 meters during intense convection in 2014–2017. The bio-optical properties of the particles indicate deep mixing of surface POC rich in labile phytoplanktonic material, distinct from both background suspended particles and sinking aggregates. Model simulations show that the mixing-driven POC supply to the deep ocean (500 to 2000 meters) scales with convection volume (R2 ≈ 0.88) and is typically dominated by planktonic biomass. According to the model, vertical mixing regionally contributes ∼33 to 44% of the combined POC supply from mixing and sinking to the 500 to 2000–meter layer during strong convection years. These convective injection episodes likely reshape deep-ocean carbon budgets by enhancing metabolism and driving lateral exports, linking upper-ocean dynamics to deep-ocean water masses and ecosystems.

INTRODUCTION

In the North Atlantic subpolar gyre (NASPG), intense winter atmospheric forcing makes surface water dense enough to sink violently, mixing the water column below 1000 m (1). This process, known as open-ocean deep convection, plays a vital role in global climate (2–6) and ocean biogeochemistry (7–10). During the past decades, considerable effort has been devoted to understanding the coupled physical-biogeochemical pathways through which deep convection draws down and stores atmospheric carbon dioxide as dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC) (9, 10) and dissolved organic carbon (DOC) (11–14). However, the vertical transport of particulate organic carbon (POC) into deep waters (>1000 m) via convective injection remains unquantified. This omission, combined with the uncertainty about the future evolution of NASPG deep convection during the current century (3–5), hampers both present and future ocean carbon uptake assessments.

Convective particle injection (15), also termed particle detrainment or mixed-layer particle pumping (16), occurs when a previously stratified water column rapidly homogenizes through turbulent mixing. This process enables the rapid transport of planktonic and detrital particles from the POC-enriched euphotic layer into the POC-depleted ocean interior. Unlike gravitational particle settling, which is driven by large and fast-sinking detrital particles, convection preferentially injects suspended and slow-sinking particles that dominate the POC stock (17–19). Once the water column restratifies, convection-injected material becomes isolated from the atmosphere over timescales ranging from subseasonal (20, 21) to centennial (10, 22). The impact of convection on carbon storage ultimately depends on how detrained POC and its degradation products (DOC and DIC) are routed through competing pathways, i.e., re-entrainment into the surface layer and eventual atmospheric exchange of remineralized carbon, or transport and storage in the ocean interior (22, 23). Current observational estimates of convective POC injection, primarily based on indirect proxies measured from satellites and autonomous profiling floats (20, 24, 25), are limited to the mesopelagic layer (above 1000 m depth). Thus, POC detrainment is associated with shallow injection depths and short turnover timescales (26). Convection-driven POC fluxes have also been assessed using coupled physical-biogeochemical models (13, 14, 27, 28), but these estimates likely suffer from the tendency of classical parameterizations to underestimate POC concentration below the euphotic zone (29). Furthermore, an assessment of deep convective POC fluxes over interannual-to-decadal scales is lacking, despite the widely recognized role of ocean-atmosphere variability in driving convection in the NASPG and other deep convection sites (1–3, 10, 30). Reflecting these knowledge gaps, convective POC injection is not routinely assessed in the simulations that inform the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (31).

Here, we use still unexplored bio-optical observations from Argo floats to reveal widespread injection of POC at 1000 m during a recent multiannual period of strong convection in the NASPG (2). We further use a model with an improved representation of vertical POC profiles (19, 29) to assess convective POC injection across seasonal to decadal scales and evaluate its contribution to regional POC budgets between 500 and 2000 m.

RESULTS

Convective events observed by drifting Biogeochemical-Argo floats

A swarm of 22 instrumented robots known as Biogeochemical-Argo (BGC-Argo) floats was deployed in the NASPG in 2013 (32, 33). Several floats drifted in the region for up to 4 years, together collecting over 3300 vertical profiles at 1- to 10-day intervals. Some of these floats documented the intense convection that occurred after 2014 (2) in unprecedented detail (Fig. 1 and sections S1 to S3). Examining temperature and conductivity profiles, we identified the signature of recent deep convection by a seawater density (σθ) difference of less than 0.03 kg m−3 across the top 1000 m (Fig. 1A) (1, 34). Although the mixed-layer depth (MLD) computed with a density threshold (e.g., MLD0.03) does not imply that convection is ongoing, the density difference was often smaller than 0.005 kg m−3, likely indicating active convection (Fig. 1, fig. S1, and table S1) (35). The vertical homogeneity of bio-optical variables—chlorophyll a fluorescence (FChla, in milligrams per cubic meter) and particulate backscattering at 700-nm wavelength (bbp,700, in per meter)—revealed the transport of phytoplankton (microscopic algae) and nonfluorescing particles to 1000 m and beyond (fig. S2).

To better understand the temporal dynamics of convected particles, we examined the near-continuous measurements taken by the floats while drifting at 1000 m (figs. S3 to S9), a large (N ≈ 106) and previously unexplored dataset. Robust statistics were obtained by binning 10-min resolution bio-optical data into daily periods. In addition, FChla was baseline-corrected by subtracting from the time series the signal from the preconvection phase, when no FChla is expected at 1000 m (36). Baseline-corrected FChla at 1000, expressed in milligrams of Chla per cubic meter, is referred to hereafter as ΔFChla(1000). As shown in Fig. 1B, the onset of deep convection was evidenced by a sharp increase in ΔFChla(1000) above background noise levels (table S1), concurrent with a relatively smaller increase in bbp,700 and an abrupt temperature change (Fig. 1, C and D). The temporal trace at 1000 m was coherent with the signature of convection in vertical profiles (Fig. 1, A to D).

Between 2014 and 2017, 12 periods with above-noise ΔFChla(1000) were recorded by five different BGC-Argo floats (Fig. 1, F to H, and figs. S3 to S7). These periods constitute the main observational basis for subsequent analyses. High ΔFChla(1000) periods started between late January and early April, most frequently in February. Taking the last detection of MLD0.03 > 1000 m as the termination date, the average duration of the convection-driven ΔFChla(1000) signal was 45 days, ranging from 9 to 80 days (table S2). The time-weighted mean ΔFChla(1000) during convection was 0.042 mg m−3, with peak daily-mean values ranging from 0.05 to 0.17 mg m−3 (Fig. 1F), which are exceptional for the deep ocean. Peak ΔFChla(1000) was consistent with sea-surface Chla retrieved from satellites, typically within ∼30% (Fig. 2, table S3, and section S3.5). Once convection stopped, ΔFChla(1000) decayed to background levels, although with variable abruptness and oscillations, likely reflecting the degradation and physical dilution of phytoplanktonic material (figs. S3 to S7; see below).

Convection periods defined on the basis of single-float observations often showed successive peaks and valleys in ΔFChla(1000) (Fig. 1B and figs. S3 to S7). In some cases, the dates of convection onset and peak ΔFChla(1000) were synchronous in various floats, especially in 2015 (Figs. 1F and 2 and table S1). Inspection of MLD0.03 fields from reanalysis indicates that convection patches expanded (eventually coalescing) and contracted (eventually fragmenting), reflecting the episodic nature of atmospheric forcing and deep convection (1, 37). As a result, the same convection areas were sometimes sampled by more than one float, as illustrated in Fig. 2 and its animated version covering February to April 2015 (movie S1).

Our analysis purposefully focuses on the high-ΔFChla(1000) signal that can be robustly attributed to convection. We exclude four smaller ΔFChla(1000) features observed during the convection season because they were not associated with σθ differences of <0.03 kg m−3 (table S2 and section S3.4). The 0.03 kg m−3 stratification threshold also excludes ΔFChla(1000) peaks that occurred shortly after prolonged convection, generally matching sudden decreases in 1000-m temperature (figs. S3 to S7). While ΔFChla(1000) excursions may simply reflect floats drifting in and out of mixed patches of variable age, the observed patterns are also consistent with (sub)mesoscale advective processes associated with the late convection phase (38), during which vertically mixed waters spread horizontally (1). Last, some ΔFChla(1000) features occurred near boundary currents, highlighting that their interaction with convection may also contribute to particle transport (7).

Carbon content, freshness, and size of convected particles

The largest ΔFChla(1000) peaks occurred during the late convection season (Fig. 1F), when deep mixing alternates with periods of arrested convection. These calm intervals promoted phytoplankton growth (fig. S2), likely driven by high nutrient availability, low grazing pressure, and transient relief from light limitation (32, 33). When convection reintensified (Fig. 2), the accumulated phytoplankton stock was redistributed vertically, usually resulting in greater ΔFChla(1000) than in the early convection period (Fig. 1).

To assess the biogeochemical impact of convective inputs, we converted the maximum ΔFChla(1000) of each period to carbon biomass, ΔPOCphy(1000), using a photoacclimation model (39). Prescribed photoacclimation parameters (section S4.2) are characteristic of phytoplankton under extremely low light (Fig. 3A) associated with deep mixing, resulting in a median C:Chla ratio of 12 g C (g Chl)−1 [range: 10 to 18 g C (g Chl)−1]. Across the 12 convection periods, the maximum ΔPOCphy(1000) had a median (range) of 1.2 (0.5 to 1.8) mg C m−3 (Fig. 3A). Because ΔPOCphy(1000) only accounts for chlorophyll-containing material, it provides a lower-bound estimate of convective inputs. In parallel, we computed the total POC change for each convection period, ΔPOC(1000), based on the change in particulate backscatter, Δbbp,700(1000), and an empirical conversion factor (which is admittedly uncertain; see table S3 and section S4.4). Best estimates of phytoplanktonic and total ΔPOC at 1000 suggest that the phytoplanktonic fraction ranged from 13 to 83% (median of 58%, interquartile range of 46 to 68%). This fraction increased as the season progressed (Pearson’s r = 0.72, P = 0.009), confirming the interplay of mixing intermittence and phytoplankton growth.

We further contextualize the magnitude of convective inputs by estimating POC concentrations at 1000 m from particulate backscatter prior to the onset of convection (table S4). Comparison between preconvection POC(1000) (median ≈ 2.0 mg C m−3) and the maximum increase during convection, ΔPOC(1000) (median ≈ 2.3 mg C m−3), implies total concentrations of ≈4.3 mg C m−3 (range of 3.4 to 6.0), approximately a doubling relative to preconvection conditions. However, the biogeochemical impact of turbulent fluxes in meso- and bathypelagic waters, where aged suspended particles usually prevail (40), likely goes beyond their contribution in carbon currency. Below, we analyze bio-optical signals showing that convection-injected particles are fresher—hence, more labile—and smaller than the detrital aggregates supplied later in the season by gravitational sinking, following phytoplankton bloom collapse (41).

To estimate the lability of convected particles, we analyzed the FChla/bbp,700 ratio, a proxy for particle freshness (20, 41, 42). In the vertical profiles, this ratio exhibited the smallest vertical variability during the convection period (Fig. 1E), especially under active convection (σθ difference of <0.005 kg m−3). Values fell between those typical of aged detrital matter found in the lower mesopelagic (<30 mg m−2) and those of freshly produced particles in the euphotic layer (>300 mg m−2) under stratified conditions (Fig. 1E). In the 1000-m drift data, the FChla/bbp,700 ratio varied substantially across events (Fig. 1G), suggesting that high-frequency sampling (21, 43) allowed capturing a broader range of particle freshness than vertical profiles. Overall, these patterns illustrate how convection mixed fresh and aged materials in variable proportions.

Analysis of bio-optical spikes provides insights into particle size (41, 44). During convection, reduced spikiness, especially in ΔFChla(1000) (figs. S10 and S11), indicates dominance of small particles. Vertical profiles confirmed prevailing particle sizes of <30 μm (fig. S12). As the ocean restratified, ΔFChla(1000) returned to baseline, while bbp,700(1000) increased into midsummer (Fig. 1C), driven mainly by spikes (fig. S11). During this period, sinking particle plumes originating from the euphotic zone (41) were conspicuous in bio-optical spike signals (Fig. 1A and figs. S3 to S8), and particle size became more variable and increased with depth to reach 50 to 70 μm in the lower mesopelagic (fig. S12). However, most sinking plumes left little or no trace in ΔFChla(1000) (Fig. 1 and figs. S3 to S8), likely because the fluorescent and more labile component of aggregate sinking fluxes was degraded at shallow depths (41).

Modeled POC fluxes from seasonal to interannual scales

We used the ocean physics and biogeochemistry model NEMO4-PISCESv2 to quantify convection-driven POC injection and elucidate the underlying mechanisms (section S5). As a first step, we used a water-column (one-dimensional) configuration with prescribed vertical mixing (19) to examine POC dynamics at daily resolution in the central Labrador Sea. The model reproduced, to first order, key observed features at 1000 m (fig. S13), such as (i) the range of preconvection POC concentrations, (ii) the abrupt POC increase when winter convection deepened beyond 1000 m (simulated ΔPOC ≈ 1.5 mg C m−3, with phytoplankton contributing 30 to 70%), and (iii) the gradual midsummer POC increase driven by large sinking detritus.

Building on this agreement, we extended the analysis using a coupled three-dimensional configuration of NEMO4-PISCESv2 (section S5). Global ocean-only simulations (∼1° horizontal resolution and 75 vertical levels) were forced by atmospheric reanalysis, thus capturing the influence of atmospheric forcing variability on deep convection (Fig. 4A and figs. S14 and S15). Positive phases of the North Atlantic Oscillation (NAO) index are associated with stronger westerly winds and enhanced sea-surface buoyancy loss over the NASPG, favoring deeper convection (1, 2, 45). Multiannual periods of enhanced convection emerge from the interaction between atmospheric forcing variability (chiefly positive NAO phases), water-column preconditioning, and subpolar gyre circulation (2, 10, 30). Here, we first report results from our reference simulation “REF” (1958–2019), focusing on the Labrador and Irminger seas over bathymetries of >2000 m (see Fig. 4B). To isolate the influence of convection intensity, we focus on the recent transition from weak (Wk3, 2009–2013) to strong (Str3, 2014–2018) convection (Fig. 4A).

Spatial and temporal variability in model-diagnosed POC transport by mixing, advection, and gravitational sinking (29, 46) is illustrated in Fig. 4. Vertical mixing-driven transports encompass two regimes (25): vigorous, turbulent mixing during deep convection, and weaker mixing under stratified conditions. These regimes align with a seasonal shift in the composition of exported material at 1000 m (Fig. 4C). At the peak of the convection season and immediately after, during March to May, plankton (including phytoplankton and heterotrophs) dominates mixing-driven fluxes. As convection subsides, and well into the stratified season, detritus become dominant. These mixing-driven fluxes of detritus reflect the degradation of convected plankton (20, 46) and, later in the season, increasing inputs of detrital POC via gravitational sinking. This seasonal shift is amplified during periods of strong convection.

On interannual timescales, the simulation reveals wide variability in mixing-driven POC fluxes at 1000 m linked to convection intensity, alongside a dominant but less variable gravitational component and a smaller opposing advective flux (Fig. 4A). The relative importance of mixing and sinking fluxes varies spatially (Fig. 4B), reflecting interannual shifts in convection patterns (30). Integrated regionally and annually, vertical POC mixing contributed an additional 9 ± 5% relative to gravitational export at 1000 m during 2009–2013, increasing to 36 ± 7% during 2014–2018 (Fig. 4A). As a result, strong convection enhanced mesopelagic (100 to 1000 m) POC transfer efficiency from 16 ± 2% to 21 ± 2% (table S5). Analogous patterns occurred in earlier convection cycles, with convective injection peaking during the exceptional deep-water formation events of the early 1990s (Fig. 4A) (10, 30).

Modeled ocean-interior POC budgets

While analysis of model simulations at 1000 m allows for comparison to BGC-Argo observations, calculation of volumetric budgets is needed to evaluate the biogeochemical impact of convection-driven fluxes. Thus, we extracted from our simulations the annual POC budgets between 500 and 2000 m (Fig. 5, table S5, and section S5.2; Methods) (46). The choice of this layer is based on previous assessments (22, 47) and on the vertical penetration of mixing-driven POC fluxes during strong convection (Fig. 4D). Particle fluxes above 500 m are not considered because much of the NASPG is mixed deeper than 500 m every winter (fig. S14), partly re-entraining to the surface the materials detrained during the prior convection season (22).

The budgets portray POC inputs dominated by gravitational fluxes and outputs dominated by biological degradation, with superimposed interannual variability driven by convection intensity. Net convective POC inputs between 500 and 2000 m diagnosed by the model are proportional to the deep convection volume (DCV) (adjusted R2 = 0.88; Fig. 5A and Methods). As a result, mixing-driven POC supply doubled during the recent transition from weak to strong convection, increasing from 1.7 ± 0.6 to 3.5 ± 0.3 Tg C year−1 in the REF experiment (Fig. 5B). In parallel, net gravitational supply decreased from 7.6 ± 0.3 to 7.2 ± 0.3 Tg C year−1, primarily because convectively injected particles enhanced subsequent sinking fluxes at 2000 m (table S5). Overall, the net additional POC input (∼1.4 Tg C year−1) was partitioned evenly between biological degradation and lateral export, both of which increased by ∼0.6 Tg C year−1 between Wk3 and Str3 (Fig. 5B). Strong convection was also associated with comparatively smaller increases in POC inventories and vertical advective losses. Enhanced vertical redistribution and lateral transport translated into positive POC concentration anomalies within the deep convection region (up to +30% between 1500 to 2000 m) and further south, to about 50°N (fig. S16).

Sparse biogeochemical observations in the NASPG lend empirical support to model-derived inferences. In the mesopelagic Labrador Sea, exceptional particle remineralization rates and abundance of heterotrophic prokaryotes were observed in June 2014 (47). This high remineralization signature reached at least 1500 m, and propagated with Labrador Sea Water into the Irminger Sea at 500 to 1000 m depth, consistent with horizontal transport of the newly formed water mass. Additional evidence for lateral exchange during the postconvection phase comes from the 1000-m temperatures recorded by floats, particularly those that remained in the Labrador Sea over extended periods (Fig. 1, A to D and H; figs. S3, S4, and S6; and section S3.4). The gradual postconvection temperature increase, with superimposed oscillations, likely reflects a combination of lateral advection and mixing with neighboring water masses (1, 37). Last, early-spring shipboard observations from the NASPG (18) support the notion that, after convection, injected particles can augment gravitational fluxes in deeper waters, possibly through subsequent aggregation and sinking.

Constraining uncertainty: Convection strength and phytoplankton stocks

Convection plays a dual role in regulating POC fluxes because, while directly transporting particles to depth, it dilutes actively growing phytoplankton in the productive surface layer. Dilution of the seed population prevents biomass accumulation (Fig. 2, B and D) (20, 28, 37), eventually limiting mixing-driven fluxes during subsequent convection events. The net outcome of the mixing-growth interplay is hard to constrain, owing, in large part, to the exponential nature of phytoplankton growth and structural model limitations. In this section, we evaluate biases in modeled convection strength and phytoplankton stocks, and use satellite observations to constrain their potential effects on modeled POC fluxes.

Comparison with the GLORYS12v1 ocean reanalysis suggests too vigorous mixing below 1000 m in our model (figs. S14 and S15). Conversely, comparison between in situ float profiles and reanalysis-derived MLD0.03 suggests that the deep convection area (DCA) diagnosed from the reanalysis is somewhat conservative (Fig. 2 and movie S1; discussed in section S5.4). Thus, the actual DCV might well lie between these two estimates.

Too vigorous convection in our model, combined with an additional growth reduction imposed by PISCES under deep mixing conditions, likely suppresses phytoplankton biomass and subsequent vertical export. Consistent with this assessment, the REF simulation underestimates satellite-derived POCphy during strong convection years (figs. S17 to S19), particularly in March (−40%). Likewise, the REF simulation underestimates ΔPOCphy(1000) inferred from BGC-Argo observations (Fig. 3). These negative biases across the upper 1000 m suggest that, overall, the REF simulation provides a conservative estimate of convective POC supply to the 500 to 2000–m layer, primarily driven by suppressed phytoplankton growth in late winter. Model-diagnosed fluxes in different layers (table S5) and POC concentration anomalies (fig. S16) further indicate that supply below 1000 m occurs at the expense of shallower layers. Thus, it is likely that negative/positive biases in POC supply above/below 1000 m partially offset when integrated over 500 to 2000 m. In other words, 500 to 2000 m integral estimates are more robust to mixing biases than layer-specific estimates.

To explore the consequences of the POCphy bias, we performed an additional simulation (SAT) whereby modeled POCphy was restored toward satellite observations (1998–2019), while keeping mixing unchanged (Methods). This sensitivity experiment isolates the effect of upper-ocean phytoplankton biases on POC fluxes. Satellite restoring effectively reduced sea-surface POCphy bias (figs. S17 to S19) and improved agreement with observed ΔPOCphy(1000) during convection, capturing the observed increase toward the late convection period (Fig. 3). During strong convection (Str3), the upward correction of surface POCphy increased the mixing-driven POC supply to 500 to 2000 m from 3.5 ± 0.3 to 4.9 ± 0.8 Tg C year−1 (P < 0.05, Student’s t test; Fig. 5B), primarily above 1500 m (Fig. 4D). POCphy restoring also increased the contribution of plankton to total mixing-driven fluxes from 56% in REF to 65% in SAT (Fig. 4C). This result confirms that the labile nature of convection-injected POC is a robust feature both in observations and simulations.

The influence of satellite data assimilation goes beyond mixing-driven fluxes. During May to July, at the bloom peak, satellite restoring corrected an overestimation of diatom biomass—common to weak and strong convection years. In turn, this reduced gravitational POC supply to 500 to 2000 m by ∼1 Tg C year−1, or ∼15% (Fig. 5B). As a result, the contribution of vertical mixing to the combined (mixing + sinking) POC supply in this layer increased from 33 ± 2% (REF) to 44 ± 4% (SAT) during Str3. In SAT, this combined supply increased from 8.4 ± 0.6 to 11.2 ± 0.7 Tg C year−1 between Wk3 and Str3, implying a greater interperiod difference than in REF. The additional convective supply translated primarily into enhanced POC degradation, but also favored POC accumulation and gravitational export at 2000 m (table S5). These responses indicate that correcting upper-ocean phytoplankton biases substantially alters the magnitude, composition, and fate of POC supply to the deep ocean.

Overall, these results confirm the hypothesized links between convection strength and POC injection, while highlighting associated modeling biases. The SAT experiment should be regarded as a sensitivity test that likely provides an upper bound on convective POC supply, given the combination of satellite-derived POCphy with overly vigorous mixing fields. Together, the REF and SAT simulations likely bracket the actual magnitude of convective POC supply as a function of DCV, as reflected by the similar intercepts but contrasting slopes of the DCV-POC supply relationships (Fig. 5A). This emergent relationship could help constrain mixing-driven POC supply across models with different physical representations of convection (45, 48), even without coupled biogeochemical models. Despite uncertainties in convection intensity and upper-ocean phytoplankton biomass, the main conclusions remain robust across simulations (Fig. 5).

DISCUSSION

Climate change threatens the integrity of the ocean carbon sink (49), and deep convection regions are particularly susceptible because of their tight physical-biogeochemical coupling. However, the future evolution of NASPG deep convection (3–5) and its relationship with biogenic carbon storage (22, 23) remain debated. Our results improve quantification of convection-driven POC transport in the global carbon cycle (12, 26, 31), emphasizing that interannual variability must be considered. Given the increasing interest in purposeful marine carbon dioxide removal (22, 23), this effort is critical for defining a robust baseline against which additional carbon uptake and sequestration can be claimed.

Despite the NASPG covering less than 1% of the global ocean, the model suggests that it contributes 10 to 20% of global mixing-driven POC fluxes to the 500 to 2000–m layer (table S6). While microbial degradation is likely the dominant fate of injected POC, lateral transport increases during strong convection periods. Transport of POC away from the deep convection region, where atmospheric exchange timescales are short, would effectively promote longer-term carbon storage (22). In addition to convective POC injection reported here, shipboard observations from the NASPG suggest enhanced vertical redistribution of DOC after strong convection in 2016, relative to preceding years with weak convection (fig. S20). Together, these observations highlight that coupled transport and biodegradation of POC and DOC in deep convection regions merit future investigation (13, 25, 50).

Our simulations rely on a coarse-resolution (∼1°) ocean model with a simplified convection parameterization (1), constrained by atmospheric forcing and sea-surface salinity observations (section S5.1). Although such models can reproduce key observed features (Figs. 1 and 3 and fig. S13), yielding mechanistic insights into convective POC injection and its decadal variability, they are prone to convection biases (30, 45, 48) and cannot represent (sub)mesoscale dynamics (1, 30, 37). Float observations reveal pronounced fine-scale heterogeneity during the postconvective phase and near convection patches, highlighting the need to better quantify the role of boundary currents (2, 7, 45) and (sub)mesoscale transports (38, 45) in the spreading of recently mixed waters. In summary, the estimates presented here provide first-order constraints on convection-driven POC transport. Future refinements will require higher-resolution observations and models (21, 37).

Beyond carbon flux magnitude and penetration depth (22, 26), the variable reactivity and energy content (51) of convection-injected particles deserve further attention. Since current models do not represent the full range of particle transformations and their kinetics (29, 46), the variable fates and residence times of injected POC remain uncertain (22). Efficient injection of energy-rich POC during a period of low primary production may play a crucial role in sustaining deep-ocean microbes and detritivorous animals. Ultimately, episodic particle injection may reshape deep-ocean ecology, from microbial dispersal and metabolism to food-web interactions (52, 53). The remineralization of labile convected particles likely alters widely used metrics of deep-ocean respiration, such as apparent oxygen utilization (AOU) (11), as well as tracers of organic matter decay rates (47). Because convection in the NASPG feeds the global overturning circulation (2, 10, 45), these biogeochemical signatures may propagate with newly formed deep water masses far beyond the convective region.

METHODS

Study domain

Our study focuses on the western NASPG, where wintertime deep convection occurs recurrently (1, 30). Quantitative analyses are restricted to the region within the 2000-m isobath of the Labrador and Irminger Seas, bounded to the west by the Reykjanes Ridge and to the south by the 50°N parallel (Fig. 4B).

MLD and deep convection criteria

Deep convection is defined as an MLD0.03 exceeding 1000 m. MLD0.03 is the shallowest depth at which potential density (σθ) exceeds its near-surface reference value by at least 0.03 kg m−3 (34). The subscript distinguishes this metric from other vertical mixing diagnostics used here (table S1). The same definition is applied to conductivity-temperature-depth (CTD) profiles from BGC-Argo floats and to vertical profiles simulated by the NEMO model.

BGC-Argo dataset selection

BGC-Argo data were obtained from Ifremer’s Global Data Assembly Center and included all profiles collected between 2013 and 2019 in the NASPG (40° to 70°N, 15° to 65°W) over bathymetry deeper than 1200 m (section S1). Quality-controlled and calibrated data (19, 33) from 42 floats (6157 profiles) were screened, leading to selection of 22 PROVOR CTS-4 floats deployed through the RemOcean project. These floats were equipped, at least, with CTD, photosynthetically available radiation (PAR), and bio-optical (chlorophyll fluorescence and particle backscattering) sensors. Vertical profile and trajectory (parking-depth) data were preprocessed separately and subsequently merged to evaluate sensor stability and detect deep convection signatures. Based on data quality and clear convection signals, five floats (6901480, 6901486, 6901523, 6901524, and 6901527) were retained for detailed analysis (table S2 and figs. S3 to S7). Two additional floats outside the deep convection region are shown for comparison (figs. S8 and S9). Measurements taken by the floats while drifting at the parking depth (nominally 1000 m; in practice, 973 ± 3 m for the selected floats) are hereafter referred to as 1000-m measurements.

Processing of BGC-Argo vertical profiles

Ascending BGC-Argo profiles (1- to 10-day resolution) acquired at local solar noon ±2.5 hours were processed following (19). Variables were calculated from native-resolution profiles and from derived, vertically binned profiles (section S2). MLD was defined as the shallowest depth where σθ exceeded the near-surface reference (4 to 6 m average) by 0.005, 0.01, or 0.03 kg m−3 (table S1). MLD0.005 is used to diagnose active vertical mixing (35). Additional MLD estimates based on a temperature criterion (20) are shown in figs. S3 to S9. The diffuse attenuation coefficient of downwelling PAR (Kd,PAR) was obtained from the exponential fit of instantaneous PAR (iPAR) profiles against depth (z). Daily irradiance profiles were reconstructed as PAR(z) = PAR(0)·exp.(−z Kd,PAR) using satellite-derived surface PAR and in situ Kd,PAR. The euphotic layer depth (Zeu) corresponded to the 0.1 mol photons m−2 day−1 isolume (32). Bio-optical data—FChla and bbp,700—were decomposed into spike and baseline components to separate large particles from the bulk assemblage (41), following (19). Profiles and derived variables were binned onto a common vertical grid (19). Within each bin, equivalent spherical diameter (ESD) was estimated from bbp,700 data using the variance-to-mean ratio approach (44), assuming a backscattering attenuation efficiency Qbb = 0.024.

Processing of 1000-m trajectory data

Daily binning and spike detection

While drifting at parking depth, floats recorded bio-optical data every 10 min (section S3). Owing to low signal-to-noise ratios at depth (fig. S10), trajectory data were temporally aggregated to obtain robust daily statistics. Spike filters designed for profiles (41) were not fully appropriate for the statistical characteristics of 1000-m time series (fig. S10). Therefore, we applied a Tukey outlier criterion to daily distributions, classifying as spikes the measurements exceeding the third quartile plus 1.5 times the interquartile range. Daily means of spike and baseline (despiked) components were then calculated. Nevertheless, conclusions are not sensitive to the spike criterion used (fig. S11).

Baseline adjustment and definition of ΔFChla(1000)

Despiked daily-mean FChla(1000) exhibited small interannual drifts (section S3.3 and table S2). To define a stable baseline, we (i) identified low-variability periods using a 31-day running standard deviation, (ii) fitted a linear trend to this subset, and (iii) removed significant trends. Baseline noise, σb,FChla, was defined from the lower quartile of the running standard deviation during stable periods. The detrended, baseline-adjusted anomaly, hereafter ΔFChla(1000), which excludes nonphytoplanktonic fluorescence (36), forms the basis of subsequent analyses. An analogous correction was not applied to bbp,700, for which a persistent deep background signal is expected.

Detection of convection periods

For each float and year, convection onset was defined as the first day when ΔFChla(1000) exceeded 10σb,FChla, provided that the preceding vertical profile indicated weak stratification (density difference of <0.03 kg m−3 in the upper 1000 m). Convection termination corresponded to the last occurrence of MLD0.03 of >1000 m prior to restratification (January to May window). Observation periods satisfying these criteria are listed in table S2 along with discarded ones (section S3.4).

Uncertainty assessment

Trajectory FChla data (CHLA_ADJUSTED) were used as provided by the Global Data Assembly Center. In the absence of in situ chlorophyll measurements at 1000 m, uncertainty was assessed through satellite matchups. Agreement was strongest at peak ΔFChla(1000), and differences were within typical satellite retrieval uncertainties for comparable Chla concentrations (section S3.5 and table S3) (54).

Convection-driven changes in POC at 1000 m

Preconvection bbp,700 and POC

For each convection event, preconvection POC at 1000 m was estimated from the minimum daily mean bbp,700 during the preconvection period. This was converted to POC using a deep-ocean POC/bbp,700 ratio of 12,000 mg C m−3 (m−1) (19), consistent with available mesopelagic chemical measurements (section S4.1 and table S4).

Total POC change (ΔPOC) and phytoplanktonic fraction

To estimate ΔPOC, we first computed the change in particulate backscattering (Δbbp,700) between the day of maximum ΔFChla(1000) and preconvection bbp,700. This allowed for simultaneous estimates of ΔPOC (from Δbbp,700) and ΔPOCphy (from ΔFChla). Δbbp,700 was converted to POC using two empirical conversion factors: 12,000 mg C m−3 (m−1) for deep stratified waters and 40,000 mg C m−3 (m−1) for upper mixed-layer properties (19). Only the latter ensures ΔPOC ≥ ΔPOCphy in all events and is therefore retained (section S4.4 and table S3).

Model simulations

Model configuration and reference (REF) simulation

We used the NEMO4.0.4 ocean circulation model (∼1° resolution, 75 vertical levels) coupled to the PISCESv2_RC biogeochemical model (29). Vertical mixing follows a turbulent kinetic energy scheme, with deep convection represented by enhanced vertical diffusivity under unstable stratification; mixed-layer eddies are parameterized. The reference (REF) simulation follows the Ocean Model Intercomparison Project phase 2 (OMIP-2) protocol with repeated atmospheric forcing to equilibrate circulation and biogeochemistry (section S5.1). This model configuration has been extensively evaluated in the North Atlantic and reproduces observational estimates of POC gravitational fluxes, POC vertical mixing, and remineralization rates (46). Analyses use the 1958–2019 period (final forcing cycle) at monthly resolution, with additional high-frequency output for selected years.

POC fluxes and budgets

In PISCES, POC is represented by six prognostic tracers: living plankton (two phytoplankton and two zooplankton groups) and by small and large detrital particles that sink at different speeds. POC budgets (section S5.2) were diagnosed by decomposing tracer tendencies into transport (advection, diffusion, and gravitational sinking) and biogeochemical source-minus-sink terms. Vertical and horizontal mixing fluxes are diagnosed as diffusion terms in the model. Fluxes were integrated over space and time to quantify regional budgets, with a focus on the 500 to 2000–m layer. Internal exchanges among POC compartments were aggregated, and all biogeochemical transformations of POC to DOC were combined into a single net degradation term. This framework isolates the relative roles of physical transport pathways and biological decay in regulating deep POC variability.

Definition of weak and strong convection periods

Convection strength was quantified using two diagnostics based on the annual maximum MLD0.03: DCA, defined as the area where MLD0.03 exceeded 1000 m, and DCV, integrated over the 500 to 2000–m layer (section S5.3). Both metrics capture interannual variability in deep convection and are strongly correlated with each other and with the winter NAO index (r ≈ 0.6 to 0.7 at interannual scales). To compare contrasting regimes, we defined weak and strong convection periods as consecutive 5-year intervals identified from the smoothed DCA time series (Fig. 4A). Strong periods correspond to sustained maxima in convection strength, while weak periods precede them and represent background conditions. Three weak-strong pairs were analyzed, focusing on the most recent transition (Wk3-Str3). Results are robust to the choice of diagnostic (DCA or DCV) and diagnostic threshold values.

Evaluation of deep convection against reanalysis

To assess simulated convection, we compared the ORCA1 configuration with the eddy-resolving GLORYS12v1 reanalysis over 1993–2019. Using the annual maximum MLD0.03, the simulated DCA agrees well with reanalysis estimates (r = 0.88). The model reproduces the timing of major convection events, including the recent intensification phase. Despite biases described in the main text and section S5.4, the magnitude and variability of simulated deep convection are comparable to reanalysis, supporting its use for regional POC budget analysis.

Evaluation of phytoplankton biomass (REF simulation)

Simulated phytoplankton carbon biomass (POCphy) was evaluated against the Ocean Color Climate Change Initiative (OC-CCI v5) product, which provides size-resolved surface biomass estimates (section S5.5). Comparison focused on March to August, spanning deep convection and the subsequent bloom. Overall, the model captures the timing of seasonal biomass evolution but exhibits biases in small phytoplankton and diatom biomass (figs. S17 to S19) linked to convection depth and phytoplankton growth parameterization.

Assimilation of satellite-derived phytoplankton biomass (SAT simulation)

To reduce surface biomass biases, we implemented a restoring approach (section S5.6) that nudges simulated phytoplankton carbon toward time-varying satellite estimates from OC-CCI v5. Small phytoplankton and diatoms were restored independently, using observation-based monthly targets over 1998–2019. Restoring effectively constrains POCphy within the model’s turbulent mixing layer, substantially reducing surface POCphy biases (figs. S17 to S19), particularly during the convection season, and largely corrects the excessive diatom bloom simulated in REF.
