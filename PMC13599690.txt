Adaptive Functioning, Sensory Processing, and Behavioral Problems in Autistic Children: The Moderating Role of Gender
Authors: Cory Shulman, Danielle Bitton, Zili Nir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChildren with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) frequently experience elevated emotional and behavioral difficulties, yet factors associated with variability remain incompletely understood. Adaptive functioning and sensory processing differences have been linked to behavioral problems, but little is known about whether associations differ between boys and girls. This study examined relationships between adaptive functioning, sensory processing, and behavioral problems in children with ASD, focusing on gender as a moderator. The purposive sampling included 110 autistic children (M age = 85.61 months, SD = 31.51; 50 boys and 60 girls) and their primary caregivers. Adaptive functioning was assessed using the Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales–Third Edition, sensory processing using the Short Sensory Profile, and behavioral problems using the C hild Behavior Checklist. Gender differences were examined with Mann–Whitney tests, associations with Spearman's correlations, and moderation analyses using PROCESS with bootstrap resampling. Girls demonstrated higher internalizing and externalizing behavioral problems than boys, whereas boys showed greater sensory processing differences. Correlational analyses revealed distinct patterns: adaptive functioning showed stronger associations with behavioral difficulties among girls, whereas sensory processing differences were associated with behavioral problems among boys. Moderation analyses indicated that gender moderated the relationship between adaptive functioning and behavioral problems, such that higher adaptive functioning was associated with fewer behavioral difficulties among girls but not boys. These findings highlight gender‐specific patterns in associations between functional abilities and behavior profiles in autism, underscoring the importance of considering gender in developmental pathways and intervention approaches.

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental condition characterized by persistent differences in social communication and interaction, alongside restricted and repetitive patterns of behavior, interests, or activities (American Psychiatric Association 2022). Beyond these core characteristics, many children with autism experience substantial emotional and behavioral difficulties, including internalizing symptoms such as anxiety and depression as well as externalizing behaviors such as aggression, hyperactivity, and emotional dysregulation (Kanne et al. 2011; Simonoff et al. 2008). These behavioral challenges represent a significant clinical concern, as they are associated with poorer everyday functioning, increased caregiver stress, and reduced quality of life for both children and their families (Lecavalier et al. 2006; Totsika et al. 2011). However, autistic children vary widely in their behavioral profiles, and the factors associated with this variability remain incompletely understood. Identifying characteristics that may be linked to emotional and behavioral difficulties in autism is therefore an important step toward improving both conceptual understanding and clinical support.

Among possible explanations for the variability in behavioral profiles among autistic children, adaptive functioning has received considerable attention. Adaptive functioning refers to the practical, social, and communication skills required for effective participation in everyday life, including communication abilities, socialization skills, and activities of daily living skills (Sparrow et al. 2016). Individuals with autism frequently demonstrate adaptive functioning levels lower than would be expected based on their cognitive abilities (Kanne et al. 2011; Klin et al. 2007). The relationship between adaptive functioning and behavioral difficulties appears complex and likely reflects multiple interacting developmental processes. Previous research suggests that differences in adaptive skills are associated with variability in emotional and behavioral functioning among autistic children (Colombi et al. 2021; Pugliese et al. 2015). For example, children who experience greater challenges in communication, social interaction, or daily living skills may encounter increased difficulty navigating everyday social and environmental demands (Saulnier et al. 2022). Consequently, adaptive functioning is often examined alongside emotional and behavioral challenges in order to better understand differences in behavioral profiles among children with autism. Although adaptive functioning is conceptually distinct from behavioral difficulties, the two constructs are not entirely independent. For example, adaptive functioning encompasses communication, socialization, and daily living skills, domains that may be influenced by autism characteristics as well as emotional and behavioral functioning. Consequently, researchers have increasingly emphasized the importance of examining adaptive functioning and behavioral difficulties simultaneously, while recognizing that they represent related but distinct aspects of children's development and everyday functioning (Kanne et al. 2011; Tillmann et al. 2019).

Sensory processing differences constitute another important dimension of everyday functioning in autism. Sensory processing refers to the ways in which individuals perceive, interpret, and respond to sensory information from the environment. Many autistic individuals experience atypical sensory responses, including hyper‐responsivity or hyporesponsivity to sensory stimuli, and these differences are now recognized as part of the diagnostic characteristics of autism (American Psychiatric Association 2022). A growing body of research indicates that sensory processing differences are frequently associated with emotional and behavioral challenges in autistic children, including elevated internalizing and externalizing symptoms (Dellapiazza et al. 2024; Green et al. 2021; Little et al. 2022; Kirby et al. 2022) as well as anxiety and emotional dysregulation (Ben‐Sasson et al. 2009; Robertson and Baron‐Cohen 2017). For instance, children who experience overwhelming sensory input may have difficulty regulating emotional responses or coping with environmental demands, which may be reflected in behavioral withdrawal, irritability, or disruptive behavior (Ashburner et al. 2008).

Emerging evidence suggests that sensory processing differences are associated with adaptive functioning in autism, as variations in sensory responsiveness can shape children's engagement in everyday activities and social contexts (Dellapiazza et al. 2018; Neufeld et al. 2021; Shulman et al. 2026). Adaptive functioning and sensory processing are two aspects of children's everyday experiences that may be closely linked to emotional and behavioral difficulties in autism. Examining these domains simultaneously may therefore provide a more comprehensive understanding of variability in emotional and behavioral profiles among children with autism. Importantly, emerging research suggests that developmental characteristics associated with autism, including sensory processing patterns, social communication profiles, and emotional difficulties, may not manifest in the same way across boys and girls on the autism spectrum (Hull et al. 2020; Wood‐Downie et al. 2021; Osório et al. 2021). These findings highlight the importance of considering gender when examining factors associated with behavioral profiles in autism.

Increasing attention has been directed toward gender differences in autism, as emerging evidence suggests that developmental pathways and behavioral profiles may differ between boys and girls on the autism spectrum. Although autism has historically been diagnosed more frequently in males than females, with estimated male‐to‐female ratios of approximately 3–4:1 (Loomes et al. 2017), growing evidence indicates that girls with autism may exhibit somewhat different emotional and behavioral characteristics. For example, several studies have reported that girls with autism experience higher levels of internalizing symptoms, including anxiety and depression, compared to boys (Kreiser and White 2014; Hull et al. 2020). Recent meta‐analytic evidence further suggests that autistic girls demonstrate elevated levels of anxiety symptoms across development (Suominen et al. 2025). Importantly, accumulating research indicates that autistic girls may also present with higher overall levels of emotional and behavioral difficulties, including both internalizing and externalizing challenges, compared to boys (Horwitz et al. 2023; Mandy et al. 2018; Prosperi et al. 2021).

One possible explanation for this pattern is that girls may need to exhibit greater levels of difficulties before meeting diagnostic criteria for autism, a phenomenon sometimes referred to as the female protective effect (Happé et al. 2016; Lai et al. 2015). According to this perspective, girls may require a higher threshold of autistic traits or associated difficulties to receive a clinical diagnosis. This may contribute to the observation that diagnosed girls often present with more pronounced emotional or behavioral challenges. These findings suggest that gender differences in autism may extend beyond core diagnostic characteristics to broader behavioral profiles and patterns of emotional adjustment (Napolitano et al. 2022). Such differences underscore the importance of examining gender when investigating factors associated with behavioral difficulties in autism.

Beyond differences in emotional symptoms, research also suggests that patterns of everyday functioning and behavioral challenges may vary across genders in autism. For instance, some studies have reported gender‐related differences in adaptive functioning profiles and social functioning among autistic children and adolescents (Hull et al. 2020; Wood‐Downie et al. 2021). These findings raise the possibility that factors associated with behavioral profiles in autism, such as adaptive skills or sensory processing differences, may not operate in the same way in boys and girls.

Although interest in sex and gender differences in autism has increased substantially over the past decade, progress in this area has been limited by methodological challenges. Many autism studies have included predominantly male samples or relatively small numbers of girls, making it difficult to draw firm conclusions regarding gender‐related differences (Hull et al. 2020; Wood‐Downie et al. 2021). Furthermore, observed gender differences may be influenced by developmental and cognitive factors, as age and intellectual functioning are known to be associated with adaptive functioning, sensory processing, and behavioral presentation in autism (Kanne et al. 2011; Pugliese et al. 2015). Consequently, studies that include comparable groups of boys and girls and account for key developmental characteristics are needed to better understand whether behavioral profiles and their associated correlates differ across genders.

Despite advances in recognizing gender‐related variability in autism, relatively little research has examined whether the relationships between adaptive functioning, sensory processing, and behavioral difficulties differ between boys and girls. Most previous studies have focused primarily on documenting gender differences in symptom presentation or emotional functioning. In contrast, fewer investigations have examined whether gender may shape the associations between everyday functional abilities and behavioral profiles in autistic children. Understanding these relationships is important because adaptive functioning and sensory processing represent key aspects of children's daily experiences that may be linked to emotional and behavioral challenges in different ways across genders.

A moderation framework allows researchers to examine whether the strength or direction of associations between variables differs across groups, thereby identifying potential gender‐specific pathways underlying behavioral challenges. Examining these relationships may help clarify whether adaptive functioning and sensory processing contribute differently to behavioral profiles among boys and girls with autism.

The present study aimed to elucidate the complex interplay between adaptive functioning, sensory processing, and behavioral difficulties in children with autism, with a specific focus on gender‐related differences. To facilitate meaningful examination of gender effects, the study included comparable groups of boys and girls matched on chronological age and cognitive ability. Specifically, the study had three primary objectives. First, we examined gender differences in adaptive functioning, sensory processing, and behavioral difficulties. Second, we explored the associations between adaptive functioning, sensory processing, and behavioral difficulties within each gender. Third, we examined whether child gender moderates the association between adaptive functioning and behavioral difficulties after accounting for autism‐related behaviors and relevant background characteristics.

Based on prior literature, several hypotheses were proposed. First, we expected that girls with autism would demonstrate higher levels of internalizing behavioral difficulties compared to boys, whereas gender differences in adaptive functioning would be smaller or inconsistent. Second, lower adaptive functioning and greater sensory processing difficulties were expected to be associated with higher levels of behavioral difficulties. Third, child gender was expected to moderate the relationship between adaptive functioning and behavioral difficulties, such that lower adaptive functioning would be more strongly associated with emotional and behavioral challenges among girls than among boys.

By examining these relationships, the present study aims to provide a more nuanced understanding of gender‐related variability in behavioral profiles among autistic children.

Method

Participants

Because the investigation of gender differences was a primary aim of the study, boys and girls were selected to create groups that were comparable with respect to chronological age and cognitive ability. This matching procedure was implemented to reduce the potential confounding effects of developmental level and intellectual functioning when examining gender‐related differences in adaptive functioning, sensory processing, and behavioral difficulties. The study included 110 children diagnosed with autism and their primary caregivers. The sample comprised 50 boys (45.5%) and 60 girls (54.5%). Child gender was coded as a dichotomous variable (0 = male, 1 = female). No other gender categories were reported (e.g., nonbinary). Participants were recruited through dissemination of flyers in relevant clinical facilities, posts on social media platforms, and word‐of‐mouth referrals. All data were collected at the time of the initial diagnostic evaluation. Autism diagnoses were established using a comprehensive standardized assessment battery. Developmental history and autism‐specific behaviors were evaluated using the Autism Diagnostic Interview–Revised (ADI‐R; Rutter et al. 2003), administered by trained clinicians with established research reliability. Direct behavioral assessment was conducted using the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule, Second Edition (ADOS‐2; Lord et al. 2012), administered by experienced clinicians with established research reliability.

There were no significant gender differences in parental educational level, marital status, primary home language, family lifestyle, or average monthly family income. Marital status was included as a background variable because family structure has been associated with child behavioral outcomes, and children from divorced or separated families may be at increased risk for behavioral and emotional difficulties (Weaver and Schofield 2015). In addition, because divorce rates in Israel are relatively lower than those reported in many Western countries (Weinreb 2022), we considered it important to describe the marital composition of our sample and examine its potential relationship with study variables. One mother of a girl reported being a single parent; therefore, paternal data were unavailable for one participant in the girls' group (n = 59 for father‐related variables). Measures

Autism symptom expression was assessed using the Autism Diagnositic Obsetrvation Schedule‐Second Edition (ADOS‐2 Lord et al. 2012). The ADOS‐2 is a standardized, semi‐structured observational assessment used in the diagnostic evaluation of autism. A trained clinician interacts with the child and evaluates social communication, social interaction, and restricted/repetitive behaviors during standardized activities. Participants completed the module appropriate to their chronological age and expressive language level, and all examiners met research reliability standards. Analyses were based on the CSS (range 0–10), which adjusts symptom ratings for age and language level and was therefore treated as a continuous variable. The CSS demonstrates moderate to high test–retest reliability across modules (ICC ≈0.71–0.87), supporting its consistency as an index of autism‐related behaviors. Internal consistency is not typically reported due to the observational nature of the measure (Janvier et al. 2022).

Adaptive functioning was assessed using the Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales–Third Edition Comprehensive Interview Form (VABS‐3: Sparrow et al. 2016). Standard scores (M = 100, SD = 15) were used for Communication, Daily Living Skills, Socialization domains, and the adaptive behavior composite (ABC). Scores were treated as continuous variables. The VABS‐3 demonstrates excellent internal consistency (α ≈0.86–0.99) and moderate to high test–retest reliability (r ≈0.62–0.94).

Sensory processing was calculated by means of the Short Sensory Profile (SSP: McIntosh et al. 1999), which is a standardized caregiver‐report questionnaire derived from the original Sensory Profile. The SSP consists of 38 items that evaluate children's behavioral responses to everyday sensory experiences across seven domains: (1) tactile sensitivity; (2) taste/smell sensitivity; (3) movement sensitivity; (4) under responsive/seeks sensation; (5) auditory filtering; (6) low energy/weak; and (7) visual/auditory sensitivity. These domains reflect patterns of hyper‐responsivity, hyporesponsivity, and sensory seeking behaviors as conceptualized in Dunn's model of sensory processing (Dunn 1997). Items are rated on a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from “Always” to “Never,” indicating the frequency with which the child demonstrates specific sensory‐related behaviors. Raw scores are summed to yield section scores and a total score. Scores are interpreted using normative data and classified into three performance categories: Typical Performance, Probable Difference, and Definite Difference. Scores falling within the typical range reflect sensory processing comparable to same‐aged peers, whereas scores in the probable or definite difference ranges indicate atypical sensory processing patterns that occur more frequently than expected relative to normative samples. Higher scores correspond to greater sensory processing differences. In the present study, total SSP scores were included in analyses. The internal consistency was high in the current study α = 0.94.

Behavioral functioning was assessed using the age‐appropriate Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL/1½–5 and CBCL/6–18; Achenbach and Rescorla 2000, 2001). Caregivers rated behaviors observed over the past 2 onths on a 3‐point scale (0 = Not True, 1 = Sometimes True, 2 = Usually/Very True). Raw scores were converted to age and gender normed T‐scores (M = 50, SD = 10), yielding comparable Internalizing, Externalizing, and Total Problems scores across forms. Higher scores indicate greater behavioral problems. The Hebrew CBCL, translated and culturally adapted for use in Israel, was employed. T‐scores were calculated using Israeli normative data to ensure cultural validity. These versions have demonstrated satisfactory reliability and validity in Israeli samples (Zilber et al. 1994; Auerbach et al. 1996). The internal consistency was high in the current study α = 0.97.

Procedure

All data were collected during the children's initial diagnostic evaluation. After obtaining informed consent from parents or legal guardians, trained clinicians administered a comprehensive standardized assessment battery to confirm ASD diagnoses and evaluate developmental and behavioral functioning. Diagnostic decisions were based on a best‐estimate clinical process that integrated information obtained from developmental history, parent interview, clinical observation, and standardized assessments, including ADOS‐2 (Lord et al. 2012) and the ADI‐R (Rutter et al. 2003). Assessments were conducted by licensed clinicians with specialized expertise in autism evaluation. All clinicians had attained research reliability in administration and scoring of the ADOS‐2 and ADI‐R. Although standardized cutoff scores from the ADOS‐2 and ADI‐R informed clinical decision‐making, final diagnostic determinations were based on clinician judgment and consensus in accordance with DSM‐5 criteria. Parent questionnaires were completed in‐person or online, and caregivers were instructed to report on typical behavior.

Data Analysis

All analyses were conducted using SPSS Version 29, following the study's three main aims. First, given deviations from normality, Mann–Whitney U tests examined gender differences in VABS, SSP, and CBCL, with effect sizes calculated as r = Z/√N. Second, Spearman's correlations were computed separately for boys and girls to explore gender‐specific associations among adaptive functioning, sensory processing, and behavioral problems. Third, multiple regression analyses identified explanatory variables of CBCL scores. A set of participant background, educational, co‐occurring conditions, and diagnostic characteristics were entered stepwise to assess unique contributions and to identify potential covariates for subsequent moderation analyses. Recognizing the limitations of stepwise procedures, such as overfitting and sample sensitivity (Babyak 2004; Field 2018), regression models were interpreted cautiously, and only variables demonstrating consistent explanatory power were entered into the regression model and carried forward into moderation analyses, ensuring that the variables were theoretically and empirically justified while accounting for participant heterogeneity. Finally, moderation analyses were performed using PROCESS (Model 1, Hayes 2018) to examine whether child gender moderated associations between adaptive functioning and behavioral outcomes. Bootstrap resampling (5000 samples) addressed non‐normality, providing robust confidence intervals (Preacher and Hayes 2008). Covariates included only those variables identified as significant explanatory factors in the preliminary regression analyses (e.g., ADOS‐2 CSS controlling for the manifestation of autism‐related behaviors; the presence of emotional/behavioral co‐occurring conditions (0 = no, 1 = yes), controlling for co‐occurring conditions; and child's age).

Skewness and kurtosis values for all variables were within the conventional range of −1 to 1 (Skewness ranged from 0.01 to 0.71 and kurtosis ranged from 0.00 to 0.37), suggesting approximate normality (George and Mallery 2019). However, Shapiro–Wilk tests indicated significant deviations from normality for several variables (p < 0.05), justifying the use of nonparametric analyses.

Boys scored higher than girls on the VABS Communication domain (p = 0.005, r = 0.27), reflecting better adaptive communication skills, and on total SSP scores (p = 0.045, r = 0.19), indicating greater sensory processing difficulties. Conversely, girls scored higher than boys on CBCL Total (p = 0.008, r = 0.25), Internalizing Problems (p = 0.013, r = 0.24), and Externalizing Problems (p = 0.021, r = 0.22), reflecting more behavioral and emotional problems. Moderation Analyses

Moderation analyses examined whether child gender moderated the associations between VABS ABC and CBCL measures, controlling for ADOS‐2 CSS, child age, and presence of emotional/behavioral co‐occurring conditions. The moderation models were designed to test whether the strength of the association between adaptive functioning (VABS) and behavioral difficulties (CBCL) differed between boys and girls. Bootstrap resampling (5000 samples) was applied to account for nonnormality (Preacher and Hayes 2008). In the moderation analyses we chose to use the VABS composite score rather than individual domains, as our primary interest was in overall adaptive functioning rather than domain‐specific variability. The decision not to conduct separate moderation analyses for the three VABS domain scores (Communication, Daily Living Skills, and Socialization) was theoretically and statistically driven. First, the VABS ABC is a validated global index that integrates across domains and is specifically designed to provide a stable and reliable overall estimate of adaptive functioning (Sparrow et al. 2016). In contrast, the domain scores are highly intercorrelated and partially derived from overlapping item content, which limits their independence and increases the risk of multicollinearity and redundancy when used simultaneously in interaction models. Second, given the modest sample size, conducting separate moderation models for each domain would have substantially increased the number of statistical tests and inflated the risk of Type I error (Bender and Lange 2001). This concern is particularly relevant in interaction analyses, which are already statistically less powerful and more sensitive to sample fluctuations (Aiken and West 1991). Finally, the correlations indicated that the three VABS domains showed highly similar patterns of association with CBCL scores across boys and girls, suggesting a shared underlying factor rather than domain‐specific effects. This pattern was also observed for the Socialization domain, despite its potential conceptual overlap with social‐communication characteristics and behavioral functioning. The similarity of findings across all three domains suggested that the observed associations reflected overall adaptive functioning rather than effects specific to a particular adaptive domain. On this basis, the composite score was considered the most parsimonious and theoretically meaningful representation for testing moderation effects.

In contrast, the CBCL outcomes were examined separately for Total, Internalizing, and Externalizing Problems to capture specific patterns of behavioral and emotional difficulties, which may be differentially associated with adaptive functioning and sensory processing. Moderation analyses were not conducted with SSP subdomain scores because correlations between sensory processing and CBCL measures were highly similar across boys and girls. Child age, ADOS‐2 CSS, and co‐occurring conditions were included as covariates in all moderation models even if they were not always significant. This strategy was used to ensure consistency across models, to control for theoretically important confounds, and to maintain comparability of effect estimates. Age, autism‐related behaviors, and co‐occurring emotional/behavioral conditions are well‐established explanatory variables of behavioral problems (Chen et al. 2025), and controlling for them a priori allows for clearer interpretation of the moderating role of gender.

Sensitivity Analyses

To evaluate the statistical power of the study, a sensitivity analysis was performed using G*Power 3.1. With a total sample of N = 110 and α = 0.05, the study is powered (0.80) to detect effect sizes of r > 0.27. According to Cohen's (1988) criteria, this corresponds to a medium effect size (equivalent to d = 0.55). While some observed effects were smaller, their clinical significance and the fact that they reached the p < 0.05 threshold support their inclusion, although they should be interpreted with caution as they may be underpowered.

Regarding the moderation analyses, the sample size (N = 110) exceeds the requirements for multiple regression based on Green's (1991) criteria for detecting medium‐sized effects. The requirement is calculated as N > 50 + 8 m, where m represents the number of explanatory variables. With six explanatory variables in the current model (including the independent variable, moderator, interaction, and three covariates), the minimum requirement is thus 98 participants (50 + [8 × 6] = 98). Furthermore, the use of 5000 bootstrap resamples in the PROCESS macro provides robust standard errors and CIs, which further mitigates potential issues arising from a modest sample size or non‐normal distributions.

Discussion

The present study examined relationships between adaptive functioning, sensory processing, and behavioral difficulties in children with autism, with particular attention to the moderating role of child gender. Three key findings emerged. First, gender differences were observed in specific domains of adaptive functioning, sensory processing, and behavioral difficulties. Second, the associations between these domains differed between boys and girls. Third, and most notably, child gender significantly moderated the relationship between adaptive functioning and behavioral problems. Together, these findings suggest that the links between everyday functional abilities, including sensory processing, and emotional and behavioral difficulties in autism vary by gender, highlighting the importance of considering gender‐specific pathways in research and clinical practice. A notable strength of the present study is that the examination of gender differences was incorporated into the study design. By including comparable groups of boys and girls matched on age and cognitive ability, we were able to examine gender‐related differences while minimizing the influence of two factors known to affect adaptive functioning, sensory processing, and behavioral presentation in autism.

Gender Differences in Behavioral and Adaptive Functioning Profiles

It is important to note that adaptive functioning and behavioral profiles are conceptually related but distinct constructs. Although aspects of adaptive functioning, particularly, socialization skills, may overlap with domains affected by autism characteristics and emotional functioning, adaptive functioning reflects the performance of everyday skills required for independent functioning rather than the presence of behavioral symptoms. Consequently, the associations observed in the present study should be interpreted as relationships between related developmental domains rather than evidence of construct equivalence.

Consistent with previous research, girls with autism in the present study demonstrated higher levels of behavioral difficulties, including both internalizing and externalizing symptoms, compared to boys. The finding that girls exhibited higher externalizing difficulties than boys was somewhat unexpected, as previous studies have often reported either comparable levels of externalizing problems across sexes or higher levels among autistic boys. One possible explanation relates to the recruitment characteristics of the present sample. All participants were assessed during their initial diagnostic evaluation, a context in which referral processes may influence the clinical characteristics of those ultimately identified. Emerging evidence suggests that autistic girls often face a higher threshold for referral and diagnosis and may therefore come to clinical attention only when they exhibit additional emotional, behavioral, or functional difficulties beyond core autistic characteristics (Dworzynski et al. 2012; Giarelli et al. 2010; Russell et al. 2011). Within this framework, the elevated externalizing scores observed among girls may reflect a subgroup of clinically referred girls with greater overall levels of impairment rather than a generalizable sex/gender difference within the broader autistic population. Future studies using community‐based samples will be important for determining whether these findings reflect referral‐related processes or true phenotypic differences.

At the same time, boys in the present sample demonstrated greater sensory processing differences. The finding that boys exhibited greater sensory processing differences than girls contrasts with some previous reports suggesting elevated sensory sensitivities among autistic girls. However, the literature regarding sex/gender differences in sensory processing in autism remains inconsistent. For example, Osório et al. (2021) reported greater sensory difficulties among autistic girls in specific sensory domains, whereas other studies and recent reviews have found limited or inconsistent evidence for robust sex/gender differences in sensory processing profiles. In a recent review, Asaridou et al. (2024) concluded that sensory differences in autism may not reliably distinguish between males and females and noted that sex/gender has not consistently emerged as a significant moderator of sensory features across studies.

Several factors may account for the discrepancy between the present findings and findings from previous literature. First, in the present study, sensory processing was assessed using the SSP, which may capture somewhat different sensory constructs than measures used in previous studies which used other instruments. Second, the present sample consisted of children undergoing initial diagnostic evaluation and therefore may reflect referral‐related characteristics rather than broader population patterns. As discussed previously, autistic girls may come to clinical attention primarily when emotional, behavioral, or functional difficulties become more pronounced. Consequently, girls in the present sample may have been referred primarily due to behavioral and adaptive functioning concerns, whereas sensory processing differences may have been a more salient referral feature among boys. Taken together, the present findings highlight the heterogeneity of sensory presentations in autism and suggest that sex/gender differences in sensory processing may be context‐ and sample‐dependent rather than universal.

Previous studies have reported mixed findings regarding gender differences in adaptive functioning in autism (Hull et al. 2020; Wood‐Downie et al. 2021). The present results suggest that gender differences may emerge in specific adaptive domains rather than in overall adaptive functioning. Importantly, the observed gender differences were relatively modest in magnitude, suggesting that boys and girls in the present sample were broadly comparable across many developmental domains. These differences are clinically and developmentally relevant, as they may inform targeted assessment and intervention strategies tailored to gender‐specific behavioral and functional profiles.

The finding that girls demonstrated lower scores in the Communication domain should be interpreted cautiously in light of previous studies reporting comparable or relatively stronger language abilities among autistic girls. Importantly, the Vineland‐3 Communication domain assesses adaptive communication—that is, the effective use of communication skills in everyday contexts—rather than language competence alone. Consequently, lower Communication scores may not necessarily reflect deficits in structural language abilities such as vocabulary, syntax, or verbal reasoning. Instead, they may indicate greater difficulty translating communication abilities into successful day‐to‐day functioning across social, educational, and family environments.

This distinction may be particularly relevant for autistic girls. Previous research has suggested that some girls develop compensatory strategies that can mask underlying social‐communication difficulties despite apparently age‐appropriate language abilities. Within this framework, girls may possess language skills comparable to or exceeding those of boys while simultaneously experiencing challenges in the functional application of these skills in real‐world settings. Thus, the present findings may reflect differences in adaptive communication demands rather than contradictions to the broader literature on language development in autism.

An alternative explanation relates to the clinical nature of the sample. Because all participants were recruited during an initial diagnostic evaluation, girls referred for assessment may represent a subgroup with greater functional impairment than autistic girls in the broader population. Future research incorporating direct measures of language ability alongside adaptive communication measures would help clarify whether observed sex/gender differences reflect language skills, functional communication, referral biases, or a combination of these factors.

Divergent Associations Between Functional Domains and Behavioral Difficulties

A central finding of the present study is the distinct pattern of associations between adaptive functioning, sensory processing, and behavioral difficulties across genders. Among boys, sensory processing differences were strongly associated with behavioral difficulties, whereas adaptive functioning showed relatively weak, non‐significant associations. In contrast, among girls, adaptive functioning demonstrated consistent negative associations with behavioral problems, indicating that higher adaptive functioning was linked to fewer emotional and behavioral difficulties. As predicted, these gender‐specific patterns align with our hypotheses regarding differential functional correlates of behavioral difficulties.

These findings suggest that different domains of functioning may be more closely linked to behavioral difficulties in boys and girls with autism. Sensory processing differences have long been associated with emotional regulation difficulties and behavioral responses in autism (Ben‐Sasson et al. 2009; Robertson and Baron‐Cohen 2017). Children with heightened sensory sensitivities may experience increased stress in everyday environments, manifesting as irritability, withdrawal, or behavioral dysregulation (Ashburner et al. 2008; Green et al. 2021). The strong associations between sensory processing differences and behavioral difficulties among boys in the present study are therefore consistent with prior research highlighting the behavioral impact of sensory reactivity in autism.

In contrast, the stronger associations between adaptive functioning and behavioral difficulties among girls suggest that everyday functional skills may be particularly relevant to emotional and behavioral adjustment. Adaptive functioning reflects the capacity to manage everyday social and environmental demands (Sparrow et al. 2016), and variability in these skills may shape children's experiences in school, family, and peer contexts. These gender‐specific associations suggest that developmental mechanisms contributing to behavioral difficulties may operate differently in boys and girls, potentially reflecting differences in environmental sensitivity or social expectations. It is important to note that interpretations regarding social expectations are speculative, as these were not directly measured.

An alternative interpretation of the stronger association between adaptive functioning and behavioral difficulties observed among girls relates to emerging evidence regarding sex differences in pathways to autism identification. Several studies have suggested that autistic girls may be referred for diagnostic evaluation only when they exhibit a greater overall level of impairment, including more pronounced emotional, behavioral, or functional difficulties, compared to boys with similar levels of autistic characteristics (Dworzynski et al. 2012; Ratto et al. 2018; Russell et al. 2011). Within this framework, the stronger relationship between adaptive functioning and behavioral difficulties observed among girls may reflect a subgroup of girls whose daily functioning has become sufficiently compromised to prompt clinical referral. Consequently, adaptive skill deficits and behavioral difficulties may be more tightly linked in clinically referred girls than boys. At the same time, because both adaptive functioning and behavioral difficulties were assessed via parent report, shared method variance and parental perceptions may have contributed to the observed associations. Future studies incorporating multiple informants and performance‐based measures of adaptive functioning will be important for disentangling true phenotypic differences from potential reporting effects. This interpretation is particularly relevant given that all participants were assessed during their initial diagnostic evaluation, a stage at which referral biases associated with sex/gender differences in autism recognition are likely to be most pronounced.

Gender as a Moderator of the Adaptive–Behavioral Relationship

A primary contribution of this study is the identification of child gender as a significant moderator of the associations between adaptive functioning and behavioral difficulties. Among girls, higher adaptive functioning was associated with fewer behavioral problems, suggesting that adaptive skills may serve as a potential protective or buffering factor. In contrast, this association was not observed among boys, highlighting the relevance of adaptive functioning for understanding behavioral profiles among girls with autism.

One possible explanation is that the development of daily living and socialization skills may help girls navigate everyday social and school expectations. Girls with stronger adaptive skills may be better equipped to meet these demands, which could be linked to fewer behavioral difficulties, whereas girls with lower adaptive functioning may experience a greater mismatch between environmental expectations and their capacities, contributing to increased frustration, stress, or behavioral difficulties. These gender‐specific patterns suggest that developmental mechanisms contributing to behavioral difficulties may operate differently in boys and girls, potentially reflecting differences in environmental sensitivity or social expectations.

Multiple Pathways to Behavioral Difficulties

The correlational patterns observed in the present study further suggest that the mechanisms associated with behavioral difficulties may differ across genders. Among boys, sensory processing differences emerged as the predominant correlate of behavioral problems, whereas adaptive functioning showed relatively little association. In contrast, for girls, both sensory sensitivities and adaptive functioning were associated with behavioral problems. This gender‐specific pattern suggests a more multidetermined pathway to behavioral difficulties among girls, highlighting that different developmental mechanisms may operate across genders.

From a developmental psychopathology perspective, emotional and behavioral difficulties are understood as emerging from complex interactions among multiple developmental processes (Cicchetti and Rogosch 2002; Beauchaine and Hinshaw 2020). The present findings suggest that these processes may operate differently across genders, with behavioral difficulties among boys more strongly associated with sensory reactivity, whereas behavioral difficulties among girls appear to be linked to a broader constellation of functional domains (Mandy et al. 2018; Napolitano et al. 2022).

Clinical Implications

The present findings have several implications for clinical assessment and intervention. First, the elevated behavioral difficulties observed among girls highlight the importance of careful screening for emotional and behavioral problems in autistic girls. Previous research suggests that emotional difficulties may sometimes be under‐recognized in girls on the autism spectrum (Lai et al. 2015; Hull et al. 2020), supporting the need for gender‐sensitive awareness among clinicians, as well as the development of gender‐sensitive screening and assessment instruments.

Second, the differential patterns of association observed across genders suggest that interventions targeting behavioral difficulties may benefit from considering multiple domains of functioning. Sensory processing differences were strongly associated with behavioral difficulties among boys, suggesting that sensory‐informed interventions may be particularly relevant. Simultaneously, the stronger link between adaptive functioning and behavioral difficulties among girls indicates that interventions aimed at strengthening everyday functional skills could enhance behavioral adjustment, emphasizing a gender‐tailored approach.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the present findings. First, the cross‐sectional design limits conclusions regarding the directionality of the associations observed among adaptive functioning, sensory processing, and behavioral difficulties. Longitudinal research will be necessary to clarify how these domains interact across development. Second, the study relied primarily on caregiver‐reported measures. Although these instruments demonstrate strong psychometric properties, shared method variance may have influenced some associations. Thus, although there is emerging evidence that the ADOS‐2 may be less sensitive to some female presentations, we elected to use the ADOS‐2 CSSs because they provide a standardized clinician‐observed measure of autism‐related symptomatology that is relatively independent of age, language level, and parent‐report biases. Third, although the sample included both boys and girls with autism, the sample size was modest for detecting small effects. While sensitivity analyses indicated sufficient power to detect medium‐sized effects, smaller associations should be interpreted cautiously. Finally, gender was assessed using a binary classification, as no participants identified outside male–female categories. Future research should incorporate broader conceptualizations of gender identity to better capture developmental variability.

Future Directions

Future research should continue to investigate gender‐related variability in developmental pathways associated with behavioral difficulties in autism. Longitudinal studies may clarify how adaptive functioning, sensory processing, and behavioral challenges interact across development. Examining contextual influences, including school environments, peer relationships, and family dynamics, could further illuminate mechanisms underlying gender differences in behavioral profiles.

Conclusion

The present study contributes to a growing body of research highlighting gender differences in autism by demonstrating that the associations between adaptive functioning and behavioral difficulties differ between boys and girls. While sensory processing differences were strongly associated with behavioral difficulties among boys, adaptive functioning showed stronger associations among girls. Furthermore, child gender moderated the relationship between adaptive functioning and behavioral problems, underscoring the importance of considering gender when examining developmental pathways in autism. Overall, these findings provide both theoretical and clinical insights into gender‐specific mechanisms, emphasizing the need for tailored approaches in research and intervention.
