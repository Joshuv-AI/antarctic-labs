Formally Verifying Analog Neural Networks Under Process Variations Using Polynomial Zonotopes
Yasmine Abu-Haeyeh †1 , Tobias Ladner †2 , Matthias Althoff 2 , Lars Hedrich 1

Abstract
Analog neural networks are gaining attention due to their efficiency in terms of power consumption and processing speed. However, since analog neural networks are implemented as physical circuits, they are highly sensitive to manufacturing process variations, which can cause large deviations from the nominal model. We present a polynomial-based model that resembles the performance of the neuron circuit under process variations. Then, we formally verify the behavior of the circuit-level model using reachability analysis with polynomial zonotopes, thus, avoiding conventional, time-consuming Monte Carlo simulations. We evaluate our proposed verification approach on three different datasets, verifying both fully-connected and convolutional analog neural networks. Our experimental results confirm the effectiveness of our verification approach by reducing the verification time from days to seconds while enclosing 99 % 99\% of the variation samples.

I
Introduction

The growing demand for artificial neural networks has driven the need for low-power hardware solutions, such as FPGA accelerators, in-memory computing, and CMOS-based inference circuits
[
8
,
7
]
. This facilitates the deployment of neural networks in Internet of Things (IoT) devices, such as sensors, mobile devices, and wearable technology, where power consumption is a critical concern
[
42
,
10
,
39
]
. Analog inference hardware demonstrates efficiency in terms of processing speed, energy, and chip area. However, the design and verification of analog devices is challenging due to their vulnerability to perturbations such as noise, mismatch, and process variations
[
28
,
43
,
41
]
. To verify the functionality of analog neural networks, they must be fully validated under these variations to ensure safety, which is usually performed by running time-consuming Monte Carlo simulations
[
12
]
. In this work, we focus on analyzing and verifying the characteristics of the analog neural network influenced by varying process parameters.

I-A

Related Work

Modeling and analysis of the influence of varying process parameters on analog circuits have been investigated extensively
[
36
,
34
,
32
,
22
]
.
However, recent advances in the design and implementation of analog neural networks have opened up new challenges that need to be further addressed:
During semiconductor device fabrication, variations, such as gate width, threshold voltage, channel length, and oxide thickness, can occur due to process variations
[
17
]
.
It is well known that perturbations can significantly impact the accuracy of conventionally implemented neural networks
[
14
]
,
and similar effects have also been observed for process variations on analog neural networks
[
43
,
41
,
2
,
18
]
. Hence, modeling the behavior of analog inference circuits under these variations is crucial to ensure the safety of these circuits and improve their design and robustness
[
1
]
. The emerging modeling methodologies either rely on device physics or machine learning engines
[
29
]
. Since developing accurate physics-based models is a challenging and time-consuming task, many works adopt an analytical approach to quantify the parameter variations. For example, machine learning has been utilized for modeling analog circuits under process variations
[
32
]
. While the proposed model provides the capability of identifying the sources of variability in the circuit, it uses a single-transistor model, which is insufficient to reflect the full system behavior and therefore cannot be extended to a system-level modeling approach.
Other available analog neural network implementations employ Monte Carlo simulations to analyze the behavior of the network under process variations
[
12
,
33
]
,
while being incapable of providing a system-level verification approach that is adaptive to different network architectures.

Recently, it was shown that system-level verification of analog neural networks under device mismatch can be achieved by utilizing formal neural network verification
[
1
]
.
Neural network verification can provide formal guarantees of the prediction of a neural network under perturbations, where usually conventionally implemented neural networks are considered
[
21
,
30
]
.
These guarantees are obtained by rigorous mathematical proofs that enable reasoning about the safety of a neural network under perturbation
[
16
]
.
Unfortunately, these proofs are usually difficult to obtain for large neural networks as it has been shown that verifying certain properties is NP-hard
[
19
]
.
Thus, modern verifiers introduce relaxations to remain computationally feasible,
where both optimization-based approaches
[
44
,
20
,
15
,
38
]
and approaches based on reachability analysis
[
11
,
37
,
31
,
25
]
are used.

I-B

Contributions

This work presents a verification method for analog neural networks.
To summarize, our contributions are:

•

An optimized polynomial-based parametric model that precisely abstracts the analog neuron performance under process variations.

•

The system-level verification of a circuit-level model under process variations using reachability analysis.

•

The proposed modeling approach is scalable to various neural network architectures, including fully-connected and convolutional architectures.

•

The effectiveness of our variation-aware model and verification approach is demonstrated
on benchmark classification tasks, such as the MNIST dataset.

Neural Networks

We initially consider fully-connected neural networks with rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation, and later extend our approach to convolutional neural networks
[

.

Definition 1

(ReLU Neural Network
[
5
, Sec. 5.1]
)
.

Given an input
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}
and an output
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\kappa}}
,
a neural network
y=\Phi(x)
with
\kappa\in\mathbb{N}
layers can be formulated as

\displaystyle\begin{split}h_{0}&=x\text{,}\\
h_{k}&=L_{k}^{\text{}}\left(h_{k-1}\right)=\text{ReLU}(W_{k}h_{k-1}+b_{k}),\quad k\in[\kappa],\\

with outputs of hidden layers
h_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}}
, layer
L_{k}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n_{k-1}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}}
, weights
W_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}\times n_{k-1}}
, and bias
b_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}}
.

II-C

Set-Based Computing

We bound variations using sets.
To efficiently represent them in high-dimensional spaces,
we use (matrix) polynomial zonotopes
[
23
,
26
]
as a set representation.

Definition 2

(Matrix Polynomial Zonotope
[
26
, Sec. 3]
)
.

Given an offset
C\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
,
dependent generators
G\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m\times h}
,
independent generators
G_{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m\times q}
,
and an exponent matrix
E\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{p\times h}
,
a matrix polynomial zonotope
\mathcal{PZ}=\left<C,G,G_{I},E\right>_{PZ\,}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{PZ}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{C+\sum_{i=1}^{h}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{p}\alpha_{k}^{E_{(k,i)}}\right)G_{(\cdot,\cdot,i)}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}\beta_{j}G_{I(\cdot,\cdot,j)}\ \right|

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\alpha_{k},\beta_{j}\in\left[-1,1\right]\bigg\}.

Many of the required operations to verify analog neural networks can be computed efficiently using polynomial zonotopes.
We discuss them only briefly here due to space limitations, and refer the reader to the example in
[
26
, Appendix A]
for further intuition on polynomial zonotopes.
Given a polynomial zonotope
\mathcal{PZ}_{1}=\left<C_{1},G_{1},G_{I,1},E_{1}\right>_{PZ\,}
, a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}
, and a vector
B\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times m}
,
an affine map
[
26
, Eq. (6a)]
is computed by

\displaystyle\begin{split}A\mathcal{PZ}_{1}+b&=\left\{AX_{1}+B\ \middle|\ X_{1}\in\mathcal{PZ}_{1}\right\}\\
&=\left<AC_{1}+B,AG_{1},AG_{I,1},E_{1}\right>_{PZ\,}.\end{split}

(1)

Without going into details,
given a second polynomial zonotope
\mathcal{PZ}_{2}
with matching dimensions,
one can also efficiently compute their sum
[
26
, Eq. (5)]

\mathcal{PZ}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{PZ}_{2}=\left\{X_{1}+X_{2}\ \middle|\ X_{1}\in\mathcal{PZ}_{1},\,X_{2}\in\mathcal{PZ}_{2}\right\},

(2)

and their multiplication
[
26
, Lemma 1]

\mathcal{PZ}_{1}\mathcal{PZ}_{2}=\left\{X_{1}X_{2}\ \middle|\ X_{1}\in\mathcal{PZ}_{1},\,X_{2}\in\mathcal{PZ}_{2}\right\}.

(3)

II-D

Formal Verification of Neural Networks

Set-based computing can be used to formally verify neural networks.
Here, we try to enclose the possible outputs of a neural network for a given set of inputs.
More formally, given an input set
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}
(e.g., a maximal perturbation
\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
around an input
x
can be modeled by
\mathcal{X}=\left<x,\epsilon I_{n_{0}},[\ ],I_{n_{0}}\right>_{PZ\,}
), the corresponding output set is computed by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{H}^{*}_{0}&=\mathcal{X},\\
\mathcal{H}^{*}_{k}&=L_{k}^{\text{}}\left(\mathcal{H}^{*}_{k-1}\right),\quad\ k\in[\kappa],\\
\mathcal{Y}^{*}&=\mathcal{H}^{*}_{\kappa}.\end{split}

(4)

Unfortunately, it is generally computationally infeasible to obtain these true sets (indicated by
\square^{*}
)
[
19
]
.
Thus, the output of each layer is enclosed:

Proposition 1

(Neural Network Enclosure
[
37
, Sec. 3]
)
.

Given an input set
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}
to a neural network
\Phi
,
we obtain an enclosure of the output set
\mathcal{Y}=\text{\small{$\mathtt{enclose}$}}\left(\Phi,\mathcal{X}\right)\supseteq\mathcal{Y}^{*}
by iteratively enclosing the output of each layer:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{k}=\text{\small{$\mathtt{enclose}$}}\left(L_{k},\mathcal{H}_{k-1}\right)\supseteq\mathcal{H}^{*}_{k}\text{,}\quad k\in[\kappa]\text{,}

with
\mathcal{H}_{0}=\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}=\mathcal{H}_{\kappa}
.

After an output set enclosure
\mathcal{Y}\supseteq\mathcal{Y}^{*}
is obtained,
we can use
\mathcal{Y}
to verify given specifications. For example,
let
\mathcal{S}
be the set of unsafe outputs,
it follows that

\mathcal{Y}\cap\mathcal{S}=\emptyset\implies\mathcal{Y}^{*}\cap\mathcal{S}=\emptyset,

(5)

which verifies the safety of the neural network.
Many common verification tasks such as image classification can be formulated this way.

Fig. 1
:

Verification of neural networks: (a) input to neural network, (b) output of hidden layer, and (c) output of neural network.
As the enclosure does not intersect with the unsafe set, we can also conclude that the unobserved true output set does not intersect with the unsafe set.

An example of this procedure is given in
fig.

1
.
How tightly the obtained enclosures fit the true output sets heavily depends on the chosen set representation.
It has been shown that zonotopic enclosures work very well in practice as they obtain tight enclosures and are fast to compute
[
37
,
25
,
24
]
.

III-B

Sensitivity Modeling

To better understand the impact of varying process parameters on our analog inference circuit,
we run a sensitivity analysis with an underlying real process based on the PSP transistor model to identify the parameters that have the most significant impact on the circuit performance.
As shown in
fig.

2
, the neuron circuit contains both NMOS and PMOS transistors operating in the subthreshold region,
which are subject to more than
50
variable model parameters depending on the used technology.

As modeling the circuit behavior using such a large number of parameters is infeasible,
we need to limit our modeling approach to reduce the model complexity and make it scalable for larger inference circuits.
We run Monte Carlo sampling with process variations on the neuron netlist (
fig.

2
), and analyze the output current’s variance contribution from all the process parameters.
Afterwards, we choose the parameters with the highest contribution as the dominant parameters.
For different neuron topologies, this step is repeated to identify the new dominant parameters.
For the current neuron topology, the behavior can be well described using two dominant parameters,
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
,
which represent two parameters influencing the surface potential of the NMOS transistor, which affects the threshold voltage and the overall transistor behavior.
While
\phi_{1},\,\phi_{2}
greatly influence NMOS transistors, they only have a negligible effect on PMOS transistors in the upper current mirror in
fig.

2
, since the current mirror topology mitigates the impact of surface potential variations.
Moreover, we notice in
fig.

4
that the neuron output current is highly sensitive to the variations in
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
.

where
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}
are sampled from a normal distribution.

IV
Bounding Process Variations Using Set-Based Computing

In this section, we first establish a model describing the effect of process variations on the output of an analog neural network, followed by a description of how these variations can be bounded using set-based computing.

IV-A

Modeling Process Variations

By analyzing the variance-based sensitivity results in
section

III
, we noticed that out of the more than
50
process parameters influencing the circuit,
only two process parameters,
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
, contribute significantly to process variations.
This enables us to model these variations as a neural network (
definition

1
) with respect to
\phi_{1}
,
\phi_{2}
and account for all error terms:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widetilde{h}_{0}&=x,\\
\widetilde{h}_{1}&=\widetilde{W}_{1}\widetilde{h}_{0}+\widetilde{b}_{1},\\
\widetilde{h}_{k^{\prime}}&=\widetilde{W}_{k^{\prime}}\text{ReLU}(\widetilde{h}_{k^{\prime}-1})+\widetilde{b}_{k^{\prime}},\quad k^{\prime}\in\{2,\ldots,\kappa\},\\
\widetilde{y}&=\text{ReLU}(\widetilde{h}_{\kappa}),\\
\end{split}

(6)

where the weights
\widetilde{W}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}\times n_{k-1}}
,
k\in[\kappa]
, are influenced polynomially by
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
:

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{3}\sum_{j=0}^{3}A_{k,i,j}{\phi_{1}}^{i}{\phi_{2}}^{j}+d_{3}\mathbf{1},\quad i+j\leq 3.

(7)

The influence on the bias term is negligible.
For subsequent layers and negative inputs
h_{k^{\prime}-1}<0
, the output of the respective neuron is reset due to the ReLU activation:

\displaystyle h_{k^{\prime}}=\sum_{i=0}^{3}\sum_{j=0}^{3}b_{k,i,j}{\phi_{1}}^{i}{\phi_{2}}^{j},\quad i+j\leq 3.

(8)

The coefficients
A_{k,i,j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}\times n_{k-1}}
,
b_{k,i,j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}}
depend on the respective quantized weight and are obtained through polynomial regression.
Please note that this was done for each of the
64
quantized weights individually, and the entries in
A_{k,i,j},\,b_{k,i,j}
are given by the respective entries in
W_{k}
of the nominal model (
definition

1
).
As mentioned before, we deploy a different circuit for the first layer to deal with negative inputs,
thus, the coefficients for the respective weights also differ slightly from the ones in all other layers (
fig.

2
).
The polynomial model is both simple and scalable, as determining the coefficients for each weight only needs to be done once and can be reused for all architectures.

Following the analysis conducted in
section

III
,
we can account for the amount of process variations by introducing noise variables around the nominal value
1
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\phi_{1}=1+d_{1},\quad d_{1}\in\mathcal{D}_{1}\subset\mathbb{R},\\
\phi_{2}=1+d_{2},\quad d_{2}\in\mathcal{D}_{2}\subset\mathbb{R},\end{split}

(9)

where the domains
\mathcal{D}_{1},\mathcal{D}_{2}
are chosen to enclose a desired number of Monte Carlo simulations (
fig.

4
).
In particular, the process parameters
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}
are sampled from a normal distribution with standard deviations
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
, respectively,
and we choose, e.g.,
\mathcal{D}_{1}=[-3\sigma_{1},+3\sigma_{1}]
to enclose
\approx 99\%
of the simulations.
The subtle variation produced by the rest of the varying process parameters is captured with a single third noise symbol
d_{3}\in\mathcal{D}_{3}\subset\mathbb{R}
(
7
).
By choosing the domains large enough such that all measurements are contained, we say that the model is reachset conformant
[
35
]
.

We also analyze the influence of process variations on convolutional neural networks
[
5
, Sec. 5.5.6]
,
and we find that our model also generalizes to convolutional neural networks and only requires updating the coefficients
A_{k,i,j},\,b_{k,i,j}
.
Please note that, as a convolution is applied instead of a matrix multiplication in (
6
), which has far fewer weights,
the overall variation is typically much smaller than for feed-forward neural networks.
Thus, convolutional neural networks are more robust against process variations.

IV-B

Bounding Process Variations

Process variations can be bounded by following the general verification procedure described in
section

II-D
.
We start the verification by propagating the input set
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{0}=\mathcal{X}
through the first layer according to (
6
).
In contrast to the general procedure,
the weights
\widetilde{W}_{1}
of linear layers (
7
) are no longer matrices but sets
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}_{1}
due to the uncertainties introduced in (
9
).
This polynomial model (
7
) enables us to represent
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}_{1}
as polynomial zonotopes,
where the noise symbols
d_{1},\,d_{2},\,d_{3}
become (scaled) dependent factors
\alpha_{1},\,\alpha_{2},\,\alpha_{3}
, as given in
definition

2
.

Fig. 5
:

Polynomial zonotope enclosure of the neuron with the maximal value of the first hidden layer according to the nominal value with respect to the main process parameters
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}
.

Given
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}_{1}
, their multiplication can be computed using (
3
).
An example enclosure of the first linear layer is visualized in
fig.

5
,
where the enclosure is shown with respect to the main process parameters
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}
.
As these two parameters describe the behavior well, i.e., low variance for each (
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}
)-pair,
the enclosure barely has to be enlarged in the z-direction to enclose all points.
Thus, the remaining process variations barely influence the final outcome.
Please note that the enclosure is represented using a single set
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
and is only split for visualization purposes.

As we proceed to propagate the set through the entire network (
6
),
we repeatedly multiply
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}_{k}
onto the current set
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{k-1}
,
k\in[\kappa]
,
and enclose the ReLU nonlinearities using standard techniques (
proposition

1
),
until we obtain an enclosure of the output set
\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}
.
We can then use
\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}
to verify given specifications (
5
).

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we present a scalable modeling approach for analog neural networks that incorporates process variations.
We have developed a polynomial-based model by analyzing the neuron circuit under varying process parameters on the transistor level.
The model is then deployed in a system-level formal verification approach that encloses the variations in the output of each layer using reachability analysis.
We evaluated our approach on three classification benchmarks with different network architectures, including convolutional neural networks, encompassing most of the output variations.
In contrast to Monte Carlo simulations, which are not feasible for deeper neural networks (already up to a day for MNIST CNN), our verification approach runs in a few seconds on a normal home computer.
Thus, it can be utilized to optimize the inference circuit design and robustness towards process variations, significantly speeding up the design cycle.
In future work, we plan to integrate other sources of variation, such as device mismatch and noise, and extend our approach to larger inference circuits.
