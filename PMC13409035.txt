Safety of Anaerobutyricum soehngenii CH106 as a novel food pursuant to Regulation (EU) 2015/2283
Authors: EFSA Panel on Nutrition, Novel Foods and Food Allergens (NDA), Dominique Turck, Torsten Bohn, Montaña Cámara, Jacqueline Castenmiller, Stefaan De Henauw, Karen Ildico Hirsch‐Ernst, Ángeles Jos, Alexandre Maciuk, Inge Mangelsdorf, Breige McNulty, Androniki Naska, Kristina Pentieva, Alfonso Siani, Frank Thies, Margarita Aguilera‐Gómez, Francesca Marcon, Monika Neuhäuser‐Berthold, Morten Poulsen, Miguel Prieto Maradona, Reinhard Ackerl, Maria Glymenaki, Thomas Frenzel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFollowing a request from the European Commission, the EFSA Panel on Nutrition, Novel Foods and Food Allergens (NDA) was asked to deliver an opinion on the safety of freeze‐dried viable Anaerobutyricum (A.) soehngenii CH106 as a novel food (NF) pursuant to Regulation (EU) 2015/2283. The applicant intends to market the NF in food supplements for adults at a maximum dose of 2.0 × 109 active fluorescent units (AFU)/day, corresponding to 2.9 × 107 AFU/kg body weight per day. The information on the identity, production process, composition, stability and specifications of the NF is sufficient and does not raise safety concerns. There are no concerns regarding genotoxicity of the NF. A human intervention study with the NF, in which half of the proposed dose was given to the participants for 3 months, did not reveal any safety concerns. One 90‐day oral toxicity study in Wistar rats was provided from which the Panel identified the highest dose tested in males, i.e. 4.7 × 1010 AFU/kg bw per day, as the NOAEL. The margin of exposure between the NOAEL and the maximum proposed daily dose of the NF is 1621, which the Panel considers to be sufficient. Considering that appropriate initial bacteria colonisation of the gastrointestinal tract in humans profoundly affects health during infancy and childhood, that little is known on how the maternal microbiota shapes the maternal–fetal immune system during pregnancy and that disruptions to the gut microbiota early in life can have lasting health effects into adulthood, the Panel considers that pregnant and lactating women should be excluded from the target population. The Panel concludes that the NF, freeze‐dried viable A. soehngenii CH106, is safe at 2.0 × 109 AFU/day for the general adult population, excluding pregnant and lactating women.

INTRODUCTION

Background and Terms of Reference as provided by the requestor

On 6 July 2022, the company Caelus pharmaceuticals BV 1 submitted an application to the European Commission in accordance with Article 10 of Regulation (EU) 2015/2283 to authorise the placing on the Union market of Anaerobutyricum (A.) soehngenii CH106 as a NF.

The applicant requests to authorise the use of A. soehngenii CH106 as a NF in food supplements as defined in Directive 2002/46/EC, excluding food supplements for infants and younger children. 2

The applicant has also requested data protection under Article 26 of Regulation (EU) 2015/2283.

In accordance with Article 29(1)(a) of Regulation (EC) No 178/2002, the European Commission asks EFSA to provide a scientific opinion on A. soehngenii CH106 as a NF in accordance with Article 10(3) of Regulation (EU) 2015/2283.

The Commission also asks EFSA to evaluate and inform the Commission as to whether and if so, to what extent, the requirements of Article 26(2)(c) of Regulation (EU) 2015/2283 are fulfilled in elaborating its opinion on A. soehngenii CH106 as a NF regarding the proprietary data for which the applicant is requesting data protection.

Additional information

In 2023 and 2026, the EFSA Panel on Biological Hazards (BIOHAZ) reviewed the available literature for A. soehngenii in the context of its update of the list of qualified presumption of safety (QPS) recommended microbiological agents intentionally added to food or feed. Although no safety concerns were identified, due to the limited body of knowledge on its use in the food and feed chains, A. soehngenii was not included in the QPS list (EFSA BIOHAZ Panel, 2023, 2026).

ASSESSMENT

Introduction

The NF which is the subject of the application is freeze‐dried viable Anaerobutyricum (A.) soehngenii CH106.

The NF falls under Article 3(2)(a)(ii) of Regulation (EU) 2015/2283, i.e. foods consisting of, isolated from or produced from microorganisms, fungi or algae.

The NF, which is produced by anaerobic fermentation and subsequent freeze‐drying, is proposed to be used in food supplements for adults at a maximum dose of up to 2.0 × 109 active fluorescent units (AFU) per day.

Identity of the NF

The NF is the powder formulation of freeze‐dried viable A. soehngenii CH106 bacteria. A. soehngenii CH106 is a commensal bacterium that is Gram‐positive, obligate anaerobic and non‐spore forming (Louis & Flint, 2017).

A. soehngenii CH106 is derived from the parental strain Eubacterium hallii L2‐7 which was originally isolated from infant faeces (Barcenilla et al., 2000). Microorganisms previously classified as Eubacterium hallii were reclassified within the newly established genus Anaerobutyricum. Within this genus, strain L2‐7 was assigned to the newly described species Anaerobutyricum soehngenii, whereas other former Eubacterium hallii strains were classified as Anaerobutyricum hallii (Shetty et al., 2018). The parental strain was subjected to random mutagenesis, yielding a tetracycline‐sensitive mutant, which was classified as A. soehngenii CH106. Upon EFSA's request, the applicant provided a description of additional mutations incurred by the random mutagenesis and provided genomic sequence analysis data that verified that these mutations do not pose any safety concern.

The strain is deposited at Westerdijk Fungal Biodiversity Institute (Utrecht, The Netherlands) under the accession number CBS 145175, which follows the Budapest Treaty on the International Recognition of the Deposit of Microorganisms. The certificate of deposit was provided by the applicant.

The full taxonomic classification of the employed strain is defined as follows. Domain: Bacteria; Phylum: Bacillota; Class: Clostridia; Order: Clostridiales; Family: Lachnospiraceae; Genus: Anaerobutyricum, Species: Anaerobutyricum soehngenii; Strain: Anaerobutyricum soehngenii CH106.

In 2023, A. soehngenii was evaluated by the EFSA Panel on Biological Hazards (BIOHAZ) for its inclusion into the list of qualified presumption of safety (QPS) recommended microbiological agents. No safety concerns were identified in the scientific literature. However, the BIOHAZ Panel did not recommend A. soehngenii for QPS status due to the limited knowledge available regarding its use in the food and feed chains (EFSA BIOHAZ Panel, 2023, 2026).

The identity of A. soehngenii CH106 was determined based on whole genome sequence (WGS) data in accordance with the ‘EFSA statement on the requirements for whole genome sequence analysis of microorganisms intentionally used in the food chain’ (EFSA, 2024). The sequencing was performed using Illumina MiSeq and the resulting sequencing reads were aligned to a reference genome (A. soehngenii L2‐7T).

The taxonomic identification of A. soehngenii CH106 was further established by using alignment and non‐alignment tools for the WGS data. The phylogenetic analysis based on the core gene alignment grouped the test strain together with A. soehngenii L2‐7T. The whole genome average nucleotide identity (ANI) assigned the strain to the type A. soehngenii L2‐7T, with an ANI score of 99.99. Upon EFSA's request, the applicant provided additional ANI scores to closely related taxa to facilitate the taxonomic assignment of the strain. Based on these results, the taxonomic identification of the strain was confirmed to belong to the species A. soehngenii.

To characterise the capacity of the strain to express genes of potential concern such as acquired antimicrobial resistance (AMR) and virulence factors, WGS data were screened for matching with AMR gene libraries (Resfinder and PATRIC databases), and virulence profiles (virulence factor database (VFDB)). Standard thresholds (≥ 80% identity and ≥ 70% coverage) were applied. The query in the two AMR profiling databases identified one hit for the A. soehngenii CH106 genome, which met the defined threshold criteria and corresponded to the tetO gene. The parental strain Anaerobutyricum soehngenii L2‐7T carries a functional tetO gene, conferring resistance to tetracycline. However, through random mutagenesis resulting in a frameshift mutation, this gene was rendered inactive in the test strain (i.e. A. soehngenii CH106). Further to this, PATRIC annotation database revealed a homology with one gene involved in antibiotic resistance meeting the coverage thresholds matching it to tufA (Elongation Factor Tu). Nonetheless, tufA encodes a common bacterial protein as demonstrated by the significant (≥ 90% identity) and high number of matches (> 100 Clostridia organisms and other bacteria) in a BLAST search using the NCBI database.

The antimicrobial resistance of the test strain was assessed in vitro according to the EFSA cut‐off values for Corynebacterium and other Gram‐positive bacteria (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2018). The measurement of antimicrobial susceptibility showed that the strain is resistant to the aminoglycosides gentamicin, kanamycin, streptomycin and neomycin. The minimum inhibitory concentration for gentamicin was 32 mg/L, for kanamycin 512 mg/L and for streptomycin > 256 mg/L compared to the cut‐off values defined by the EFSA FEEDAP Panel in 2018 (4 mg/L for gentamicin, 16 mg/L for kanamycin and 8 mg/L for streptomycin, respectively). However, this phenotypic resistance is intrinsic to these species and anaerobes in general (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2009, 2014; Krause et al., 2016), and no horizontal gene transfer is expected. In addition to that, the strain was also more resistant to trimethoprim and ciprofloxacin relative to the parental strain, and this resistance could be explained, as it is intrinsic to anaerobes and A. soehngenii strains (Appelbaum, 1995; Brook et al., 2013). On the contrary, the strain was sensitive to other antibiotic groups such as β‐lactams including ampicillin and penicillin, glycopeptides such as vancomycin and amphenicols such as chloramphenicol.

The genome of A. soehngenii CH106 was investigated for the presence of prophage‐like regions. Three prophage‐like regions were identified using PhiSpy, which is based on de novo and sequence‐homology prophage identification. To complement the output of PhiSpy, a similarity search in the PHASTER database was run to assign function to prophage genes. However, the three PhiSpy‐identified regions did not contain antibiotic resistance genes, and their presence was not confirmed with PHASTER.

Taken together, the results from the genetic analyses and the in vitro assay indicate that horizontal transfer of antibiotic resistance genes is of no concern, and the Panel therefore considers that the novel food (NF) is sufficiently characterised and does not raise safety concerns.

Production process

According to the information provided, the NF is produced in line with good manufacturing practice (GMP) and Hazard Analysis Critical Control Points (HACCP) principles.

Flow charts of the production process, detailed information of the culture conditions and media together with the quality control methods involved at each step were provided (confidential information).

The first step of the process consists of the cultivation and propagation of A. soehngenii CH106. Initially, the A. soehngenii CH106 inoculum is prepared from a culture aliquot stored at −80°C. Successive subculture steps in liquid media of increasing volumes are performed to prepare the inoculum of the fermentation step in the bioreactor. The fermentation is performed under strict anaerobic conditions at specified temperature and pH (confidential information) and the morphology of the cells, as well as the absence of microbial contaminants is confirmed. An antifoaming agent (i.e. dimethyl polysiloxane (E 900)) is used during the fermentation. Biomass is harvested by centrifugation followed by resuspension in a cryoprotectant, freezing and freeze‐drying (confidential information). The freeze‐dried powder is further milled, packaged and stored (confidential information). That powder, which constitutes the NF, is finally mixed with excipients for the formulation of food supplements.

The Panel considers that the production process is sufficiently described and does not raise safety concerns.

Analyses on pesticides in the NF were also provided, and they were found to be < LOD (i.e. 0.005 mg/kg).

Upon EFSA's request, silicon (Si) analysis was performed in order to detect any silicon‐based residues originating from the antifoaming agent (i.e. dimethyl polysiloxane (E 900)) used during the production process. Free extractable polydimethylsiloxane was detected in the NF at concentrations ranging from 22.6 mg/kg to 38.1 mg/kg in the NF. Considering the maximum proposed use levels of the NF, which could reach up to 4 g/day, the daily intake of dimethyl polysiloxane would be 0.1524 mg (equivalent to 0.0022 mg/kg bw per day for adults). Taking into account the acceptable daily intake (ADI) for dimethyl polysiloxane of 17 mg/kg bw per day established in 2020 (EFSA FAF Panel, 2020), the Panel considers that the concentrations of dimethyl polysiloxane in the NF do not raise a safety concern.

Information was provided on the accreditation of the laboratories that conducted the analyses presented in the application.

The Panel notes deviations in the production process for two batches. Despite the inconsistencies identified, they had no impact on the identity and the safety‐relevant characteristics of the NF. Thus, the Panel considers these deviations of no safety concern.

The Panel considers that the information provided on the composition is sufficient for characterising the NF.

Stability

The applicant performed stability tests with three independently produced batches of the NF. The tests were carried out at normal storage conditions at 25°C and at 60% RH for a period of 2 years. Stability test measurements were conducted at 0, 3, 6, 12 and 24 months for one batch and at the same time intervals without the time points at 3 or 6 months for the two other batches, respectively. The batches were analysed for appearance, moisture and microbiological parameters. No significant changes were observed in any of the parameters over the period tested in either of the studies. Further to this, five batches including the three batches used in the stability tests were tested for the total number of viable A. soehngenii cells (expressed as AFUs) and for the total number of A. soehngenii cells (expressed as TFUs) under normal conditions at 0, 30, 60, 180, 270 and 360 days except for two batches which were not tested at 30 days. One of the batches with no testing of A. soehngenii cells at 30 days was also not tested at 60 days, but instead a time point at 90 days was included. No deviation from the specifications in the number of AFUs was reported up to 360 days in two batches, while in two other batches, the number of AFUs was below the specification limit at 270 days, and for another batch, this happened at 30 days. No deviation from the specifications in the number of TFUs was reported at any of the time points. The applicant proposed a shelf‐life for the NF of up to 2 years under normal conditions (i.e. < 25°C and < 60% humidity).

The Panel considers that the data provided are sufficient to establish the stability of the NF.

History of use of the NF and/or of its source

In 2023, the NF received a generally recognised as safe (GRAS) status in the US for use as an ingredient in sports drinks and fluid replacement drinks; ready‐to‐eat breakfast cereals; cereal and granola bars, energy bars, protein bars and meal replacement bars; ice cream; plain fermented milks; yogurts; nut spreads; and chocolate at a maximum level of 1.0 × 1010 total fluorescent units/serving (GRN No. 1065).

Proposed uses and use levels and anticipated intake

Target population

Following an EFSA request, the applicant clarified that the proposed target population is the general adult population.

Proposed uses and use levels

The applicant intends to market the NF for use in food supplements, at a maximum dose of 2.0 × 109 AFU/day.

On a body weight (bw) basis of 70 kg for adults (EFSA Scientific Committee, 2012), this amount corresponds to 2.9 × 107 AFU/kg bw per day.

Absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion (ADME)

In a multi‐centre, randomised, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled parallel study with the NF, 100 prediabetic insulin‐resistant and hyperglycaemic adults were randomised to receive for 3 months 1 × 109 AFU/day of A. soehngenii CH106 or a placebo (Attaye et al., 2025). The relative abundance of A. soehngenii CH106, as determined in faecal samples by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), was increased (close to a 100‐fold) in the A. soehngenii‐treated group compared to baseline (from week 0 to week 12). Similarly, the absolute amount of A. soehngenii CH106, as copies per gram of faeces (as determined by qPCR in both groups before and after the intervention), was significantly increased in the A. soehngenii‐treated group. However, no significant differences between the global microbiota composition assessed by principal coordinate analysis (PCoA based on β‐diversity using Bray–Curtis dissimilarity) were observed at baseline and end of the trial in either the A. soehngenii‐treated or placebo group. No differences in α‐diversity metrics (richness, evenness) were observed.

The applicant also referred to a human study in which cells of the parental strain, i.e. A. soehngenii L2‐7, of the NF were administered daily for 1 month to 24 men with overweight/obesity (Gilijamse et al., 2020). In this study, the bacteria were shown to survive passage through the digestive tract and cell counts were elevated from baseline during administration, as determined by qPCR analysis of extracted faecal DNA. However, within 2 weeks of treatment cessation, the concentration of A. soehngenii L2‐7 cells returned to pre‐treatment levels (as measured via faecal excretion).

Nutritional information

According to the information provided, the NF contains 62.0%–86.5% carbohydrates, 3.2%–24.3% protein, 6.0%–8.1% ash and minor amounts of fat (< 0.5%).

The Panel considers that, taking into account the composition of the NF and the proposed conditions of use, the consumption of the NF is not nutritionally disadvantageous.

Genotoxicity

In the bacterial reverse mutation test (Eurofins/Caelus Pharmaceuticals B.V., 2020a, unpublished (study report); Seegers et al., 2021), the NF was investigated for its potential to induce gene mutations according to the plate incorporation test (experiment I) and the pre‐incubation test (experiment II), using Salmonella Typhimurium strains TA98, TA100, TA1535, TA1537 and Escherichia coli WP2 uvrA (pKM101). The test item (batch No. 112761.004; 5 × 109 CFU/g) was diluted in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS), including 0.5 g/L L‐cysteine. The test item was processed by sonication for 5 min at 37°C. The following concentrations (tested in triplicate) were used in the experiments: 31.6, 100, 316, 1000, 2500 and 5000 μg/plate. No toxicity was noted in any of the five tester strains used, up to the highest dose tested, with and without metabolic activation (S9 mix). No biologically relevant increases in revertant colony numbers of any of the five tester strains were observed following treatment with the NF at any concentration, neither in the presence nor absence of metabolic activation.

The applicant also provided an in vitro mammalian cell micronucleus test (Eurofins/Caelus Pharmaceuticals B.V., 2020b, unpublished (study report); Seegers et al., 2021), which was conducted in human lymphocytes. The NF (batch No. 112761.004; 5 × 109 CFU/g) was dissolved in PBS, including 0.5 g/L L‐cysteine. Based on data from the pre‐experiment, in the main experiment, 5000 μg/mL test item was selected as the highest concentration. Cell cultures were exposed to the NF and incubated for 4 h in the absence or presence of metabolic activation (S9 mix) or for 44 h in the absence of metabolic activation. Following the 4‐ and 44‐h exposure periods, the NF did not induce any statistically significant or dose‐related increases in the frequency of micronucleated binucleated cells, compared with controls.

Following an EFSA request for additional genotoxicity testing for both the lysate of the NF and the culture supernatant (in separate tests), the applicant submitted the studies as described below.

In order to produce the lysate, A. soehngenii cells were harvested by centrifugation, washed with sterile water to remove traces of the culture medium and resuspended in sterile water at a concentration of 2.7 × 1010 TFU/mL. Sonication was applied for 15 min with 60% amplitude and 30″ on/off cycle. Efficiency of the lysis was confirmed by microscopic analysis and by flow cytometry (which showed almost complete loss of intact cells after sonication).

In the bacterial reverse mutation test (2024a, unpublished study report), the cell lysate was tested with the plate incorporation method (experiment I) and the pre‐incubation method (experiment II), using Salmonella Typhimurium strains TA98, TA100, TA1535, TA1537 and Escherichia coli WP2 uvrA. The cell lysate (batch No. GTS202401) was diluted in distilled water. Based on the results of a preliminary experiment, the following concentrations (tested in triplicate) were used in the main experiments: 15.8, 50, 158.1, 500, 1581 and 5000 μg/plate. No toxicity was noted in any of the five tester strains used, up to the highest dose tested, with and without metabolic activation (S9 mix). No biologically relevant increases in revertant colony numbers of any of the five tester strains were observed following treatment with the cell lysate at any concentration, neither in the presence nor absence of metabolic activation.

The cell lysate was also tested in an in vitro mammalian cell micronucleus test (2024b, unpublished study report), which was conducted in mouse lymphoma L5178Y TK+/− 3.7.2 C cells. The lysate (batch No. GTS202401), which was diluted in distilled water, was tested at concentrations up to 5000 μg/mL. Cell cultures were exposed to the lysate and incubated for 3 h in the absence or presence of metabolic activation (S9 mix) or for 24 h in the absence of metabolic activation. No cytotoxicity was observed under any of the conditions. Following the 3‐ and 24‐h exposure periods, the cell lysate did not induce any statistically significant or dose‐related increases in the frequency of micronuclei.

The culture supernatant of the NF was tested in a bacterial reverse mutation test (2025a, unpublished study report) following the treat and wash method (Thompson et al., 2005), in order to avoid overgrowth of the bacterial background owing to the nutrients in the culture medium. The test item was the pooled supernatant of three production batches of the NF. The same bacteria strains as for the testing of the cell lysate were used, i.e. Salmonella Typhimurium strains TA98, TA100, TA1535, TA1537 and Escherichia coli WP2 uvrA. The culture supernatant, formulated with distilled water, was tested at concentrations of 15.8, 50, 158.1, 500, 1581 and 5000 μg/plate. No toxicity was noted in any of the five tester strains used, up to the highest dose tested, with and without metabolic activation (S9 mix). No biologically relevant increases in revertant colony numbers of any of the five tester strains were observed following treatment with the culture supernatant at any concentration, neither in the presence nor absence of metabolic activation.

The culture supernatant of the NF was also tested in an in vitro mammalian cell micronucleus test (2025b, unpublished study report), conducted in mouse lymphoma L5178Y TK+/− 3.7.2 C cells. The test item, formulated in distilled water, was tested at concentrations up to 5000 μg/mL. Cell cultures were exposed to the lysate and incubated for 3 h in the absence or presence of metabolic activation (S9 mix) or for 24 h in the absence of metabolic activation. No cytotoxicity was observed under any of the conditions. Following the 3‐ and 24‐h exposure periods, the culture supernatant did not induce any statistically significant or dose‐related increases in the frequency of micronuclei.

Taking into account the test results provided, the Panel considers that there are no concerns regarding the genotoxicity of the NF.

Subchronic toxicity

The applicant provided a repeated dose 90‐day oral toxicity study with the NF (Eurofins/Caelus Pharmaceuticals B.V., 2021, unpublished (study report); Seegers et al., 2021).

Wistar rats (10 per sex and group) received for 90 days by gavage 0 (control group), 8 × 109 CFU/animal per day (low dose), 4 × 1010 CFU/animal per day (medium dose) or 2 × 1011 CFU/animal per day (high dose) of the NF. On a body weight basis, these doses translated to 1.9 × 1010, 9.5 × 1010 and 4.7 × 1011 CFU/kg bw per day in males, and 3.4 × 1010, 1.7 × 1011 and 8.5 × 1011 CFU/kg bw per day in females (at the end of the trial). On an mg/kg bw basis, at the end of the trial, the administered amounts corresponded to 95, 450 and 2350 mg/kg per day in males and 170, 850 and 4250 mg/kg per day in females.

While in the full study report and the publication by Seegers et al. (2021), the number of viable A. soehngenii cells is expressed as CFU based on most probable number (MPN) measurements, in the dossier, the applicant provided the amount of viable cells expressed as AFU, which was lower than the CFU by one order of magnitude (i.e. a factor of 10), determined by flow cytometry. Information on the analyses and comparison of the methods was provided.

As vehicle (and control substance) PBS supplemented with 0.05% L‐cysteine was used. The test item was prepared freshly on each day of administration. Viability of A. soehngenii cells was assessed at weeks 1, 5, 9 and during the last week of the study.

There were two mortalities during the treatment phase. One female in the mid‐dose group was found dead on day 4 while another female from the high‐dose group was euthanised for animal welfare reasons on day 9 of the study. The findings from necropsy and histopathology suggested that both deaths were most likely related to a traumatic injury during gavage and were, thus, deemed to be incidental.

There were no clinical signs of systemic toxicity. No test‐item related effects were observed in the functional observation battery for all parameters in both male and female groups at the end of the treatment when compared to observations before the treatment.

Body weights did not differ in the groups that received the NF as compared to the control groups. No differences were observed for food consumption.

No differences in blood coagulation parameters or hormones (T3, T4, TSH) were found between groups receiving the NF and the control group.

In females, statistically significant differences compared to the control group were observed for the following haematological parameters: a lower (35.1%) white blood cell count in the high‐dose group and a higher red blood cell count in all dose groups (low‐dose 6.7%, mid‐dose 7.8%, high‐dose 6.5% higher than control group, respectively). In addition, the haemoglobin content (low‐dose 5.7%, mid‐dose 7.5%, high‐dose 5.4% above control, respectively) and the haematocrit value (low‐dose 5.6%, mid‐dose 7.3%, high‐dose 5.4% above control, respectively) were statistically significantly higher in all dose groups compared to controls. However, these small statistically significant differences could be explained by low values in the concurrent control group when compared to historical controls.

Statistically significantly lower total serum bile acid concentrations in males in the mid‐dose and high‐dose groups were observed compared to the controls (45.9% and 56.4% lower, respectively). In females, compared to controls, serum creatinine concentrations were statistically significantly lower in the mid‐dose group (28.3% below controls) and fasting blood glucose concentrations were lower in the high‐dose group (27.3% below controls).

For urinalysis, no differences of toxicological relevance were observed between the dose groups compared to the control group.

Regarding organ weights, statistically significant differences were found for adrenal glands in males in the mid‐dose and high‐dose groups as compared to controls (16.5% and 16.7% lower than the control group, respectively). The findings observed in necroscopy and histopathology were consistent with changes commonly found in rats of this age and strain and were thus considered not treatment‐related.

The Panel notes that the observed findings in the subchronic toxicity study did not show a clear dose–response relationship, occurred in one sex only and could in part (for haematology) be explained by the low values in the control group. The Panel considers these findings as incidental.

Thus, the Panel considers the highest dose tested, i.e. 4.7 × 1011 CFU/kg bw per day (the highest dose in males) corresponding to 4.7 × 1010 AFU/kg bw per day, as the no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) of this study.

Human data

Human data with the NF

The applicant provided one human study with the NF (Attaye et al., 2025; see also section 3.8 ADME). For this study, the applicant also submitted the study protocol and the draft manuscript. In this multi‐centre, randomised, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled parallel study, 100 prediabetic insulin‐resistant and hyperglycaemic adults were randomised to receive for 3 months a placebo or 1 × 109 AFU of A. soehngenii CH106 per day (both administered in not distinguishable capsule form). Primary outcomes of the study were changes in 2‐h blood glucose incremental area under the curve (AUC), 2‐h blood insulin incremental AUC, post‐prandial insulin sensitivity and glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) concentrations. Secondary outcomes were glycaemic variability (GV) over a 24‐h period, glycaemic control over a 24‐h period, glycaemic variability during sleeping hours, fasting blood glucose and blood pressure. Other outcome measures included blood profile (haematology and clinical chemistry), vitals and recording of adverse events. Four subjects dropped out from the study, two in the control group and two in the intervention group. Daily oral supplementation of the NF was well tolerated, with no serious adverse events observed. All reported adverse events were of mild or moderate intensity, with similar incidence in placebo and intervention group. No relevant differences were observed in haematology or clinical chemistry. There were no clinically meaningful changes in participants' anthropometrics or vital signs detected during the study.

The Panel notes that this human intervention study does not reveal any safety concerns but is of limited value, given that only half of the proposed dose for the NF was administered to the participants.

Human data with the progenitor strain

In addition to the above study with the NF, the applicant provided three human studies with the progenitor strain, i.e. A. soehngenii L2‐7 (Attaye et al., 2024; Gilijamse et al., 2020; Koopen et al., 2021).

In the randomised, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled, crossover study by Koopen et al. (2021), 12 male subjects with metabolic syndrome received a single dose (i.e. 1011 CFU) of A. soehngenii L2‐7 by duodenal infusion, which did not cause any adverse effects.

The study by Gilijamse et al. (2020) was a single‐centre, double‐blind, dose‐finding, pilot study in 24 male subjects with metabolic syndrome, who received for 4 weeks either 1 × 107, 1 × 109 or 1 × 1011 CFU of A. soehngenii L2‐7 per day. Outcome measures were levels of A. soehngenii in faeces, fasting plasma bile acids, insulin sensitivity and faecal SCFA concentrations. Dietary and bowel habits were also measured. According to the study authors, the administration of A. soehngenii L2‐7 was safe up to the highest dose tested.

In the randomised, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled trial by Attaye et al. (2024), 25 males with type 2 diabetes (T2D) on stable metformin therapy received daily for 14 days 1 × 1010 AFU A. soehngenii L2‐7 or placebo. The primary endpoint was the effect of A. soehngenii L2‐7 vs. placebo on glucose concentrations and variability as determined by continuous glucose monitoring. Secondary endpoints included changes in ambulatory 24‐h blood pressure, as well as systemic concentrations of incretins, short‐chain fatty acids (SCFAs), bile acids and other metabolites upon a standardised meal. The intervention was well tolerated, and no adverse events occurred during the study. The safety laboratory parameters (inflammatory markers and liver parameters) and anthropometric and biochemical parameters were similar in both groups after the intervention.

The Panel notes that these studies did not raise concerns but are of limited relevance as compared to the study with the NF (Attaye et al., 2025) described above.

Allergenicity

As described in Section 3.2, the progenitor strain from which the NF was obtained (via chemical mutagenesis) was isolated from human faeces. Genome sequence analysis of the newly derived strain revealed that, besides the desired mutation (i.e. to knock out tetracycline resistance), the mutagenesis resulted in an additional 37 point mutations, 20 of which resulted in an amino acid substitution. Further information on the affected genes was provided.

The applicant performed a genomics and bioinformatics assisted prediction of putative allergens by comparing protein sequences derived from annotated gene coding sequences (CDS) in the NF with known allergenic proteins. To predict overall structural similarities between the 3316 protein coding sequences in the genome of the NF and known allergenic proteins, the applicant used the database search tools Allermatch, Allergome, AllerBase and Immune Epitope Database (IEDB), setting (i) a minimum of 35% sequence similarity over a window of 80 amino acids or (ii) at least six contiguous amino acids that are exact matches.

The various analyses resulted in 19 unique protein queries with a minimum of 35% identical amino acids matches in an 80 amino acid segment. The full sequence of these proteins was used for more detailed analyses. IgE‐binding epitope similarity was examined using the IEDB, which did not result in any hit.

According to the applicant, regarding the matches obtained by searching 80 amino acid segments with more than 35% identity or small perfect matches of six or more contiguous amino acids, the high degree of conservation across diverse species and kingdoms combined with the mode of exposure makes it unlikely that these query proteins are allergenic. In addition, no IgE‐epitope data were obtained for these proteins.

The Panel considers that the risk of allergic reactions to the NF for the general population is unknown but is expected to be low.

DISCUSSION

The NF, which is the subject of the application, is freeze‐dried viable A. soehngenii CH106.

The applicant intends to market the NF for use in food supplements for adults at a maximum dose of 2.0 × 109 AFU/day. On a body weight basis, this amount corresponds to 2.9 × 107 AFU/kg bw per day.

The NF is sufficiently characterised. The information provided on the production process, composition, stability and specifications of the NF is sufficient and does not raise safety concerns. There are no concerns regarding genotoxicity of the NF.

A human intervention study with the NF, in which half of the proposed dose was given to the participants, did not reveal any safety concerns. In addition, human studies with the progenitor strain of the NF did not raise safety concerns.

One 90‐day oral toxicity study in Wistar rats was provided, in which the NF was tested at doses up to 4.7 × 1010 AFU/kg bw per day in males and up to 8.5 × 1010 AFU/kg bw per day in females. The Panel identified the highest dose tested in males, i.e. 4.7 × 1010 AFU/kg bw per day, as the NOAEL of this study. The margin of exposure between the NOAEL and the maximum proposed daily dose of the NF is 1621, which the Panel considers to be sufficient.

Considering that appropriate initial bacteria colonisation of the gastrointestinal tract in humans profoundly affects health during infancy and childhood (Romano‐Keeler & Sun, 2022; Taddei & Neu, 2023), that little is known on how the maternal microbiota shapes the maternal–fetal immune system during pregnancy (Koren et al., 2024) and that disruptions to the gut microbiota early in life can have lasting health effects into adulthood, the Panel considers that pregnant and lactating women should be excluded from the target population.

CONCLUSIONS

The Panel concludes that the NF, A. soehngenii CH106, is safe at 2.0 × 109 AFU/day for the general adult population, excluding pregnant and lactating women.
