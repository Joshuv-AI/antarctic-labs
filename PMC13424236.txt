Breathing retraining for paediatric dysfunctional breathing with objective improvements in breathing patterns
Authors: Nicki Barker, Lowri A Thomas, Mark L Everard, Heather E Elphick
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDysfunctional breathing is an important cause of breathlessness, characterised by alterations in breathing patterns. Breathing retraining (BRtr) is believed to aid patients re-establish a normal diaphragmatic breathing pattern. Structured light plethysmography was used to assess the breathing pattern of 23 children and adolescents before and after attending a specialist BRtr clinic. It showed that a 3-month physiotherapist-led BRtr programme was associated with a significant reduction in the contribution of rib cage movement to the total change in thoracic volume and respiratory rate, which was maintained when reassessed 3 months later, despite having persisted for the 3 months prior to intervention. This study provides the first objective evidence that BRtr may result in sustained improvements in breathing patterns in children and adolescents with dysfunctional breathing.Trial registration: ClinicalTrials.gov, TRN: NCT04215341. Introduction

Dysfunctional breathing (DB) is an important and common cause of intermittent and/or chronic respiratory and non-respiratory symptoms in children and adolescents. It affects 7–8% of the general adolescent population and the incidence is much higher than this in those with co-morbidities, such as asthma, and athletes [1, 2]. Dysfunctional breathing is characterised by an alteration in the biomechanical patterns of breathing resulting in shortness of breath and chest discomfort. Most cases are due to an acquired functional alteration in respiratory mechanics, leading to excessive use of intercostal and accessory breathing muscles and underutilisation of the diaphragm [3]. The condition can occur alone or as a co-morbidity alongside respiratory conditions, with persisting DB often being responsible for patients being mislabelled as having ‘difficult’ asthma [4].

The diagnosis of DB is challenging, due to the similarity of presentation and co-existence with other respiratory disorders such as asthma and is based on a patient’s history and the identification of an abnormal breathing pattern. Breathing pattern changes are typically subtle and therefore difficult to identify. Breathing retraining (BRtr) is the treatment of choice for DB and is believed to re-establish normal breathing patterns [5]. There are several studies highlighting the clinical benefits of this approach including a previous study from our group which found that 93% of children attending a specialist physiotherapy clinic reported marked improvement in symptoms such as feeling ‘short of breath’ and ‘unable to breathe deeply’, with 85% reporting a clinically significant improvement in health-related quality of life (HRQoL) as measured using the Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL) Generic Core Scales [6]. Similar significant improvements in HRQoL, sustained over 12 months, were reported in a randomised controlled trial in adults with asthma [7]. However, no studies have objectively assessed the impact of BRtr on the breathing patterns of these patients.

Structured light plethysmography (SLP) is a non-invasive tool used to measure breathing patterns [8] by projecting structured light, in the form of a chequerboard, onto the chest. Analysis of breathing using SLP is based on the Konno-Mead method [9], where computer tracking of the deformation of the chequerboard pattern is translated into various breathing parameters. Specialist physiotherapists use SLP with patients with suspected DB to identify the nature of the altered breathing pattern [10], explain the condition to patients and families using the visual displays generated by the SLP software, and plan treatment programmes.

In this study, SLP was used to objectively measure specific breathing pattern parameters to determine whether they altered in response to breathing retraining.

Methods

Participants, aged 8–16 years with a possible diagnosis of DB, were recruited from specialist tertiary clinics [respiratory (17), ENT (5) and allergy (1)] at a UK Children’s Hospital. Diagnosis was not confirmed due to the lack of a diagnostic tool but considered based on detailed clinical history and lung function testing, no co-morbidities or well controlled co-morbidities, as per current clinical practice. Patients with symptoms assessed as being predominantly due to uncontrolled co-morbidities, such as poorly controlled asthma, were excluded.

This study was performed in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Approval was granted by NRES Committee West Midlands – South Birmingham (14/WM/1008) alongside local NHS Trust approvals. Written consent was obtained from a parent/guardian and written assent from the participant.

Patients consented to 4 SLP assessments over a 9-month period in addition to their clinical BRtr (typically 4 visits over a 3-month period). During SLP scanning, participants wore a snug-fitting white T-shirt whilst sitting on a chair in front of the Thora3Di scanner (PneumaCare, Cambridgeshire, England). Participants were asked to maintain a relaxed sitting position and breathe naturally for three minutes. Structured light plethysmography assessments were obtained, where possible, 3 months before the first visit to the clinic, at the first clinic visit, at discharge from the clinic and 3 months after discharge (Fig. 1). Timepoints were chosen pragmatically in line with current waiting times for treatment and average duration of the clinical intervention. Other than the scan at the first clinic visit, which formed part of routine care, the other scans were all assessed by a blinded reviewer (NB) after completion of the study.

The BRtr intervention was delivered by a specialist physiotherapist on a one-to-one basis with the active involvement of a parent, carer or other member of the family. The BRtr method involved education and teaching diaphragmatic breathing, aimed at achieving nasal breathing, a normal respiratory rate, tidal volume, inspiratory/expiratory ratio, a reduction in upper chest and accessory muscle use, and a feeling of control. The techniques were adjusted for children with the use of age-appropriate language and activities. Treatment programmes were also individualised based on the child’s initial and ongoing assessment, and, where appropriate, included one or more of the following other interventions: postural correction, thoracic mobility exercises, core stability exercises, shortness of breath management strategies and exercise advice.

Given the small sample size, an assumption was made that data would not be normally distributed. Wilcoxon’s signed rank test was therefore used to analyse non-parametric paired data with a significance level of p < 0.05. Sample sizes were different for the 3 analyses due to missing data. Imputation methods were not performed. T2 (Fig. 1) was used as the pre-intervention datapoint and T3 was used as the post-intervention datapoint where available. For participants for whom these datapoints were not available, T1 was used as an alternative pre-intervention value and T4 was used as an alternative post-intervention value.

Data were analysed using the median of the entire 3-min capture for rib cage contribution to total thoracic volume change (rib cage: total), respiratory rate (RR) and inspiratory: expiratory ratio (Ti/Te), which were automatically generated by the Thora3Di scanner software. Scan data were considered unusable if the capture was less than 3 min or the software was unable to convert the raw data into numerical and visual representations.

Results

Thirty CYP were approached; 3 were not recruited following non-attendance at visit 1. Following recruitment, 2 were excluded as they no longer met the inclusion criteria, 1 was lost to follow-up and 1 did not attend at the 3rd and 4th timepoints. Structured light plethysmography data were therefore obtained prior to and after breathing retraining for 23 participants (14 females (61%), median age 14.2 years, IQR 13.5–15.5 years). Data were available at all four SLP timepoints for 6 participants. The one or two data points missing for the other 17 participants were due either to non-attendance (affecting 5 data sets) or unusable scan data due to technical faults with the scanner or scanning process during data acquisition (affecting 19 data sets).

For the 14 participants in whom SLP data were available from 3 months prior to the first clinic appointment (T1) and at the first clinic appointment (T2), there was no significant change in any of the parameters. The median difference in RR was − 0.29 BPM (IQR − 2.85 to 4.1; p = 0.83); the median difference in Ti/Te was 0.03 (IQR − 0.02 to 0.09; p = 0.27); the median difference in rib cage: total was 5.57% (IQR − 2.0 to 10.84; p = 0.10).

This was also the case for data collected at the end of the intervention sessions (T3) and 3 months later (T4) in the 10 participants for whom both sets of data were available. The median difference in RR was 0.31 BPM (IQR − 1.65 to 0.94; p = 0.88); the median difference in Ti/Te was − 0.07 (IQR − 0.13 to 0.07; p = 0.24); the median difference in rib cage: total was 4.54% (IQR − 9.85 to 10.53; p = 0.72).

When comparing data pre-breathing retraining (T2 or T1) to that obtained after (T3 or T4), the median difference in RR was − 2.32 BPM (IQR − 8.52 to − 1.15; p = 0.047); the median difference in Ti/Te was − 0.11 (IQR − 0.13 to 0.01; p = 0.11); and the median difference in rib cage: total was − 9.51% (IQR − 26.26 to − 4.51; p = 0.01).

Discussion

Breathing retraining for dysfunctional breathing is now widely accepted as the therapeutic intervention of choice due to the clinical improvements experienced by patients in symptoms and the ability to return to normal function. In this study using SLP, we have, for the first time, provided objective evidence indicating that BRtr may significantly reduce the thoracic contribution to the breathing cycle in patients with DB. This is accompanied by a small reduction in respiratory rate. Importantly, too, the data indicate that, without BRtr, the patient’s breathing pattern persists until the intervention is initiated and that, following the intervention, a reduction in the thoracic contribution may be maintained in line with the persistence of clinical benefits, e.g. reduction in shortness of breath and chest discomfort, as previously reported [6]. The data also identify altered breathing parameters at rest, e.g. an increased contribution of rib cage movement to total change in thoracic volume. These alterations, seen in clinical practice even when symptoms are not being experienced, indicate that DB could be considered a chronic condition which is exacerbated by triggers such as exercise or anxiety.

Structured light plethysmography is currently used at first visits in our specialist clinic to assist clinicians in understanding the characteristics of the breathing pattern abnormality and to explain, using images of the breathing pattern generated by the software, the nature of the condition to patients and their families. However, it is not possible to pre-determine the relative contribution of the thorax and diaphragm to breathing as the range of normality is wide [11]. Body size, lung capacity, metabolic rate and hormonal profiles all affect breathing patterns [12], and these variations will be particularly notable in the age range studied, although relative thoracic contribution is ~ 6.7% higher in females than males across the whole life cycle [11]. There is currently no minimal clinically important difference for SLP parameters but, whilst not all parameters have been studied, preliminary work has shown that SLP parameters are repeatable, reproducible and operator independent [13]. The creation of an SLP reference range calculator by Motamedi-Fakhr et al. [11] supports the interpretation of SLP data but the relatively small numbers and wide age range of participants involved in the development of the calculator means that detailed history taking and clinical expertise remain valuable components in the assessment and management of DB alongside tools such as SLP.

A limitation of this study, and all DB studies, is the difficulty in identifying the population to be studied due to the lack of a diagnostic tool for DB. Work is ongoing to better define and characterise DB [14, 15] which will support development of a diagnostic tool and enhance the reliability and reproducibility of future study findings.

The generalisability of this study is also limited due to the initial small sample size which further decreased through the duration of the study, with natural attrition associated with attendance for clinical intervention as well as unusable scan data due to technical faults with the scanner, poor scan conditions (e.g. unacceptable levels of ambient light) and 2 participants excluded as they no longer met the inclusion criteria. The attrition rate may have influenced the fact that a statistically significant difference was seen pre-post treatment but not in the T1/2 and T3/4 periods as the sample size was larger for the former analysis. Missing datapoints are also likely to have affected the validity of these preliminary results. This study also lacked a control group as it was not considered ethical to withhold routine treatment. However, participants functioned as their own control during the 3 months between T1 and T2 (Waiting-list control phase) where no intervention was received.

In summary, breathing retraining in patients with dysfunctional breathing may result in objective improvements in breathing mechanics. A multi-centre study is needed to understand whether these findings are supported in larger populations (enabling sub-analyses for participant characteristics such as age, sex, weight and height) and under different clinical circumstances, including administration of the breathing retraining intervention by physiotherapists with various levels of expertise.
