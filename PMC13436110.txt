Prediction models for highly scalable technology-assisted differential diagnostics of autism spectrum disorder
Authors: Irene Sophia Plank, Jana C Koehler, Jonathan Eckelmann, Afton M Bierlich, Richard Musil, Nikolaos Koutsouleris, Christine M Falter-Wagner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiagnosing autism spectrum disorder (ASD) in adulthood is time-consuming and markedly complicated by the requirement to distinguish between ASD and differential diagnoses also associated with social interaction difficulties, such as borderline personality disorder (BPD)—a distinction for which currently no valid screening or diagnostic tool exists. While technology-assisted diagnostics (TAD) has emerged, existing algorithms have focused on classifying between ASD and no diagnosis, not fully addressing clinical reality.ObjectiveTherefore, we assessed the feasibility of TAD for differential diagnostics by classifying between ASD and BPD in this proof-of-concept study.MethodsWe collected a rich multimodal dataset of reciprocal interactions, specifically dyadic conversations (n=120 interaction partners). From this data, we extracted more than 800 features, allowing us to capture the core area of defining symptoms for both conditions: social interactions. These features include speech patterns, facial expressions, movement quantity and interpersonal synchrony. We used these features to train and stack linear support vector machines to classify between ASD-involved, BPD-involved and comparison interaction partners.FindingsBase models capturing facial expressions during speaking and listening, speech patterns, synchronisation of facial expressions and movement quantity all performed above chance when differentiating between ASD-involved and BPD-involved interaction partners. Stacking all base models containing conceptually related features further increased accuracy, with our algorithm achieving nearly 82% of balanced accuracy, solely based on 20 min of conversation.ConclusionsOur proof-of-concept study shows the immense potential of TAD for differential diagnostics: data collection only requires microphones and webcams while feature-extraction is automated, making this approach highly objective, scalable and user-friendly.Clinical implicationsOur TAD algorithm shows the potential of multimodal, behavioural data for differential diagnostics. On the basis of clinical validation such an algorithm has the potential to streamline differential diagnoses of ASD in the future, enabling faster and more accurate diagnostic assessment and ultimately reducing patient distress by shortening the wait for an appropriate treatment plan.Keywords: Borderline Personality Disorder, Machine Learning, Neurodevelopmental Disorders

Background

Diagnostic assessment for autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is time consuming and resource consuming and requires a trained expert.1 Outpatient clinics for ASD have very long waiting times affected by rising referral numbers and by the complex challenge of differential diagnostics.1 In the last years, machine learning has been used to develop algorithms to assist the diagnostic process.1 These algorithms for technology-assisted diagnostics (TAD) have shown great promise, specifically classifying between autistic individuals and non-clinical controls.2

Previous work has demonstrated the feasibility of TAD using neuroimaging, electrophysiological, behavioural and genetic data as well as physical or metabolic biomarkers.1 The usage of behavioural data has important advantages. First, collection of behavioural data is non-invasive, user-friendly and cost-efficient. Second, ASD is defined based on behavioural symptoms in both Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth edition and International Classification of Diseases 11th revision,1 specifically regarding social communication and reciprocal social interactions as well as repetitive and inflexible patterns of behaviour or interests. Consequently, TAD trained on behavioural measurements aligns directly with the reference standard, whereas models trained on neuroimaging or genetics target correlates whose relation to the diagnostic construct is indirect and often unclear. Freud and colleagues3 collected grasping kinematics of autistic and non-autistic adults and found high accuracies ranging from 84% to 89% across different classifier types, including linear support vector classifiers. Ochi and colleagues4 focused on recorded speech and found that a support vector machine (SVM) was able to classify between autistic and non-autistic adults with above 90% accuracy.

In our previous studies comparing ASD to non-clinical controls, SVM were able to classify between autistic and non-autistic adults based on interpersonal synchrony of speech5 and facial expressions6 as well as intrapersonal synchrony of movement.7 These features were chosen in a theory-driven and symptom-driven manner to capture the diagnostic criteria of ASD: interpersonal synchrony and turn-taking capture reciprocal social interactions, speech features extracted from conversations capture social communication and intrapersonal synchrony captures repetitive behaviours localised to one part of the body. Previous studies reported reduced interpersonal synchrony8 as well as reduced intrapersonal synchrony9 in ASD, possibly leading to less favourable impressions of autistic adults and ASD-involved interactions.10 11 The dyadic nature of these interactions is also reflected in the classification labels: we distinguish between interactions which include a person with ASD (ASD-involved dyads) and those that do not. This approach reflects the inherently reciprocal nature of social interactions and social interaction difficulties as emergent features of dyads.

Despite these promising results, clinical reality does not usually entail differentiating between presence of ASD and absence of any diagnosis. Instead, the clinical challenge is the differentiation between different conditions associated with social interaction difficulties. Currently no screening tool or diagnostic tool exists that is recommendable for differential diagnostics.12

Some recent work has begun investigating the potential of TAD for differential diagnostics. For instance, Demetriou and colleagues13 performed a classification on the basis of questionnaire data and could distinguish between ASD versus social anxiety disorder with an accuracy of 72.3% and with psychosis patients with an accuracy of 69.3%. Although the diagnosis of these conditions is mainly based on behaviour, a quantification of naturalistic behaviour in social interactions was not included in the study by Demetriou and colleagues.13

One differential diagnostics for which TAD has not been developed yet is the distinction between ASD and borderline personality disorder (BPD). This differentiation is challenging and highly relevant, particularly in women.14 BPD is a diagnosis which significantly affects social interactions and behaviour, but in a different manner from ASD.15 In order to meet the increasing demand for assessment, TAD could support diagnosticians in differentiating between ASD and BPD, make the diagnostic pathway more reliable and objective, and free up valuable resources to meet more patients’ needs.16

Objective

Thus, the goal of this proof-of-concept study was to focus on clinically relevant behaviour from which features were extracted in an automated manner to differentiate between ASD-involved and BPD-involved social interactions. We focus on SVMs which perform well for high-dimensional feature spaces17 and apply careful cross-validation taking into account the dyadic nature of our reciprocal data.5 6 This project is a continuation of two previous studies,5 6 additionally including participants with BPD. In these previous studies, we developed theory-driven and symptom-driven algorithms to capture the core symptoms of autism: (1) reciprocal social interaction by computing interpersonal synchrony of movement, facial expressions and speech as well as turn-taking behaviour, (2) speech features to capture social communication and (3) intrapersonal synchrony and overall movement to capture repetitive behaviours. In addition to these previously extracted features, the updated approach presented in this manuscript goes beyond measuring single modalities. To reflect the complex, concerted coordination happening between interaction partners during the dynamics of live interactions, we included cross-modal features, allowing us to capture the non-verbal reaction of one interaction partner towards speech of the other interaction partner. For instance, one might smile in reaction to someone’s joke or nod to acknowledge their gestures. We used webcam recordings of body movements and facial expressions as well as speech recordings during two 10-minute conversations between two strangers. This combination of modalities offers complementary information, allowing us to capture the conversations on multiple behavioural levels. The data were easy to collect non-invasively, the feature extraction pipeline is automated and the approach avoids circularity. Thus, we argue that this approach could have enormous potential for TAD of ASD.

Methods

Throughout the manuscript, we use a threshold of α=0.05 to assess significance. All machine learning analyses were computed using NeuroMiner V.1.3 curufin.18 All scripts and preprocessed, anonymised data are available on https://github.com/IreneSophia/NEVIA_ASD-BPD/tree/main/ML_BPD-ASD, while full SVM models are provided on https://osf.io/y8xbp.19 This study was not registered, no study protocol was prepared and there was no patient or public involvement.

Participants

This project reused data from a previous project which developed machine learning models to classify between ASD-involved (ie, one autistic and one individual without psychiatric diagnoses) and comparison (COMP, ie, two individuals without psychiatric diagnoses) dyads.5 6 To extend this development to the differential diagnostics considering BPD, we recruited 41 adults with BPD and 75 adults without any psychiatric diagnoses between 18 and 60 years of age. ASD participants were recruited from our outpatient clinic and its database. Most BPD participants were recruited from the inpatient ward of the Department of Psychiatry and Psychotherapy, LMU University Hospital. Some BPD participants were recruited with flyers outside of the LMU University Hospital, in which case their diagnosis was confirmed by assessing their medical report. Adults without any psychiatric diagnoses were recruited with flyers distributed online and by contacting previous participants. Dyads were paired based on availability. All participants gave their written informed consent, and all data was collected between February 2020 and September 2024 in Munich, Germany, but no information on race or ethnicity was collected. Gender information but no information on assigned sex at birth was collected.

We only included clinical participants with either a BPD or an ASD diagnosis but excluded anyone who was given both diagnoses. We excluded 17% of successfully collected data after inspecting the audio recordings, mostly due to equipment failure (for a complete description of the exclusion of data see online supplemental materials section S1). After exclusion, we included the data of 17 ASD-involved, 21 BPD-involved and 22 COMP dyads, resulting in data from 120 participants (see online supplemental material section S3 for group comparisons). Since our algorithm performed its classification on the level of the interaction partner, the final input consisted of 34 ASD-involved interaction partners (17 with ASD and 17 without psychiatric diagnoses), 42 BPD-involved interaction partners (21 with BPD and 21 without psychiatric diagnoses) and 44 COMP interaction partners (44 without psychiatric diagnoses).

Participants were comparable in age and estimated IQ (p values >0.05). However, both clinical groups reported higher alexithymia and depressive symptoms than COMP participants (all p values <0.001). Furthermore, BPD participants reported higher BPD symptoms than COMP participants (p<0.001), while both clinical groups had higher scores on the autism quotient than COMP participants with ASD participants also having higher scores than BPD participants (p values <0.001; for all comparisons see online supplemental table S1).

Procedure

Participants were scheduled based on their availability and were paired in ASD-involved, BPD-involved and COMP dyads with each dyad including at least one comparison person without any psychiatric diagnosis. All participants were unaware of the diagnostic status of their interaction partner, unless they chose to disclose it themselves during testing. They had two 11-minute conversations in these dyads, one where they were prompted to talk about their hobbies and one where they were asked to plan a meal with foods and drinks they both dislike (see figure 1). During the conversations, they were seated at a table approximately 190 cm apart and asked not to move their chairs. In front of them, we placed two webcams on tripods with which we video recorded their facial expressions (Logitech C922, 640×480 pixels, 30 Hz). We also placed another webcam at a 240 cm distance from the table to capture the whole scene from a side angle (Logitech C922, 640×480 pixels, 30 Hz). Furthermore, we captured their speech using shotgun microphones (t.bone HeadmiKe, D AKG) connected to the same recorder (Zoom H4N Pro, 44 100 Hz). Participants were also wearing wristbands to capture heart rate and electrodermal activity on their left hand (Empatica E4 or EmbracePlus), which will be reported elsewhere. For a visualisation of the full process, please refer to the online supplemental materials section S2.

Data preprocessing and feature extraction

We computed 266 features measuring interpersonal synchrony based on movement and facial expressions to capture reciprocal social interactions, 14 features measuring intrapersonal synchrony capturing localised, repetitive behaviours, 6 features measuring movement quantity and facial expressiveness to capture overall behaviour and 30 features measuring speech patterns, turn-taking and interpersonal synchrony of speech to capture reciprocal social interactions and social communication. Furthermore, we computed 532 features capturing cross-modal features, for example, smiling while listening to the interaction partner (see online supplemental materials section S4).

Support vector machines for classification

We used SVMs to leverage our features to distinguish interaction partners from two interaction pairings at a time, resulting in three comparisons: ASD-involved versus BPD-involved, ASD-involved versus COMP interactions as well as BPD-involved versus COMP interactions. Our classification labels capture the dyad composition rather than the diagnostic label of the individual. For example, both the autistic and the comparison interaction partner of the same dyad would be labelled as having taken part in an ASD-involved interaction. This approach resulted in a largely balanced dataset, with 42 BPD-involved interaction partners, 34 ASD-involved interaction partners and 44 COMP interaction partners. We explored multigroup classification by combining the binary decisions to assign a final label. Each dyad type is represented by a unique binary pattern, and the final prediction is based on how closely a set of binary classification results matches that pattern, measured by the Hamming distance. Thus, the performance of the multigroup classifier depends on all pairwise comparisons.

We conceptually grouped features for the separate base models. INTRAsync unites all features capturing intrapersonal synchrony within one interaction partner. BODYsync, HEADsync and FACEsync focus on interpersonal synchronisation of the motion quantity of the body, the head and of the strength of activation of facial action units, respectively. CROSSsync includes features measuring synchronisation of motion quantity of the body of one interaction partner with the head of the other. MovEx contains features describing movement quantity and facial expressiveness of each single interaction partner. Speech includes all features describing speech patterns and turn-taking features extracted from the audio recordings. Last, CROSSturn captures cross-modal interactional behaviour, specifically facial expressiveness and motion quantity while the interaction partner is listening or speaking. All models contain features of both conversations, meal planning and hobbies. The decision scores of all base models were used in a stacking model to leverage information from all modalities.20

The Speech model mirrors the model used for classification between interaction partners in ASD-involved and COMP interactions in a previous study.5 Similarly, five of our eight base models, namely BODYsync, FACEsync, HEADsync, INTRAsync and MovEx, as well as our stacking model mirror the models in another previous study.6 All models were preprocessed and analysed separately. Preprocessing for the base models included scaling the data from −1 to 1 as recommended by NeuroMiner to improve the fairness of feature comparisons and pruning non-informative features to increase efficiency and generalisation by allowing the model to focus on the most informative patterns in the data. Additionally, for all base models with more than 50 features, we performed a principal component analysis, retaining the principal components explaining at least 80% of the variance before applying pruning again. Furthermore, we used ensemble learning to choose the models performing in the top 50%.

We used a repeated, nested, stratified cross-validation structure where the inner loop allows for hyperparameter tuning to be separate from the model evaluation in the outer loop.21 Cross-validation is a procedure in which the dataset is repeatedly split into training and testing subsets, that is, folds, allowing model performance to be estimated on data that were not used for model training (for a visualisation see online supplemental materials S2). Such a separation of hyperparameter tuning from model evaluation helps reduce the risk of overly optimistic performance estimates and provides a more conservative and reliable assessment of how well the model is expected to generalise to new data. We implemented 10 folds with 10 permutations in the outer loop and 9 folds with 1 permutation in the inner loop. Repeating the procedure across multiple permutations reduces the influence of any data split on the final performance estimate. Due to the dyadic nature of our data, we specifically constructed the cross-validation structure such that both interaction partners of one dyad are always in the same fold. This conservative approach prevents information from one member of a dyad appearing in the training data while the other member appears in the test data, which could otherwise lead to artificially inflated performance estimates. Base models were trained using LIBLINEAR Support Vector L2-regularised L2-loss classification algorithms with balanced accuracy which considers both sensitivity and specificity equally as the performance criterion and using hyperplane weighing for uneven group sizes. L2 regularisation helps reduce overfitting by discouraging overly complex models. The features of the stacking model, which are the decision scores of the base models, were entered into an L1-loss LIBSVM algorithm with a Gaussian kernel and used single node learning. A Gaussian kernel allows the model to capture complex, non-linear relationships between predictors and outcomes.

We assessed whether our base models performed above chance with a permutation-based approach while preserving the dyadic structure of the data.5 6 Furthermore, we compared our models based on the best balanced accuracies of each of the 10 permutations in the 10 folds of the outer loop using a Quade test.22 For all models, we report balanced accuracy including 95% CIs, the recall measures sensitivity and specificity as well as precision.

Since previous research has reported gender differences for social communication features in ASD,23 we explored whether decision scores differed by gender in the STACK or the Speech model. The comparisons yielded no significant differences between male and female participants suggesting a comparable performance for data of male and female participants (see online supplemental materials section S5.5).

Distinguishing ASD-involved from BPD-involved interactions

Four of our eight base models were able to distinguish between ASD-involved and BPD-involved interactions based on permutation testing, with the exception of the BODYsync, CROSSsync, HEADsync and INTRAsync models (BODYsync: pFDR=1; CROSSsync: pFDR=0.136; HEADsync: pFDR=0.936; INTRAsync: pFDR=1; all other models pFDR<0.05). Of the base models, the CROSSturn model performed best, with a balanced accuracy of 76.7% [39.8%, 100%], weighing sensitivity and specificity equally, which were 67.6% and 85.7%, respectively, and a precision of 79.3%. The other base models performing above chance were the Speech model with a balanced accuracy of 70.4% [36.6%, 96.1%] (64.7% sensitivity; 76.2% specificity; 68.8% precision), followed by the MovEx model with 68.7% [38.3%, 100%] (58.8% sensitivity; 78.6% specificity; 69% precision) and the FACEsync model with 68% [30.9%, 100%] (76.5% sensitivity; 59.5% specificity; 60.5% precision). The stacking model outperformed all base models, although the difference between the stacking model and the CROSSturn model was not significant (see table 1). The stacking model achieved a balanced accuracy of 81.7% [37.5%, 100%] (70.6% sensitivity; 92.9% specificity; 88.9% precision; see figure 2), meaning that only 2 interaction partners from BPD-involved interactions were misclassified as ASD-involved, and 10 interaction partners were misclassified as BPD-involved despite being ASD-involved. The other 64 interaction partners were classified correctly by the stacking model (see figure 3). Exploring multigroup classification

Arguably, the above results already show that distinguishing between the two clinical labels involves a different pattern of models compared with distinguishing between the ASD-involved and the COMP interactions, which also reflects clinical decision making (a clinical decision between clinical labels is derived on different information than the decision between the presence and absence of a clinical label). Nevertheless, we also explored the use of a multigroup classification which combines the output of the three one-versus-one classifiers. All base models performed significantly above chance except for the BODYsync model (pFDR=1). The best base model was the Speech model which achieved a balanced accuracy of 63.9% [41.7%, 82.3%], followed by the MovEx model which reached 60.8% [43.8%, 78.1%] balanced accuracy. The stacking model performed comparable to those two base models with 63.2% [42.9%, 81.2%] balanced accuracy, while outperforming all other base models (see table 1). The confusion matrix of the stacking model (see online supplemental materials S5.4) revealed that ASD-involved interactions were detected accurately in 61.8% of the cases and COMP interactions in 52.3% of the cases, arguably reflecting our focus on ASD-associated features capturing diagnostic criteria formulated in comparison to behaviour of people without any psychiatric diagnoses. However, BPD-involved interactions were most often misclassified, suggesting a need for BPD-associated features to be added to classification algorithms aimed at BPD-related diagnostics or to improve multigroup classification.

Discussion

Theory-driven selected features capturing clinically relevant behaviour showed an enormous potential of TAD for differential diagnostics of ASD in this proof-of-concept study. A stacking SVM leveraging decision scores from base models including information from video and audio recordings of dyadic conversations distinguished between interaction partners from ASD-involved and BPD-involved interactions with approximately 82% balanced accuracy. This model only misclassified 12 of 76 interaction partners.

At the same time a stacking model classifying between interaction partners from ASD-involved and COMP dyads reached a balanced accuracy of 80%. These features were specifically chosen to capture social interaction behaviour associated with ASD, including multiple measures of behavioural synchrony, demonstrating the efficacy of theory-driven and definition-driven feature selection to develop algorithms for TAD.

Despite the aim of the current study to distinguish between ASD-involved and BPD-involved interactions or between ASD-involved and COMP interactions, and high performance in both, for completeness reasons, we explored performance of the multigroup classifier, which was lower at 63% balanced accuracy. This result suggests that broader differential diagnostics, covering several diagnostic populations, remains challenging and might not be an ideal target for TAD, especially for a classifier trained on ASD-associated features. This focus on ASD-associated features expectedly led to a high rate of misclassification for BPD-involved interactions in the multigroup classifier. As such, the multigroup classifier may have underperformed because its approach depends on the combined accuracy of all pairwise classifiers, so weakness in one comparison can propagate and reduce overall group assignment accuracy. However, it is important to note that a simultaneous three-way classification between ASD, BPD and comparison interactions does not reflect a typical clinical decision-making scenario. In practice, diagnostic assessments are guided by specific differential diagnostic questions rather than by an unconstrained multiclass selection. The multigroup analysis was therefore included primarily to explore the limits of the algorithm under a more complex classification setting, rather than to represent the intended clinical application.

Most importantly, the algorithms tackle the so-far overlooked but highly relevant differential diagnostics for autistic adults and potentially adolescents. In the clinical setting, the diagnostic decision, particularly in adolescence and adulthood, is usually not one of absence/presence of ASD but the clinical decision between ASD and differential diagnoses, such as BPD. One of the most common differential diagnoses for adults indeed are personality disorders.24 Thus, the focus of this project was to evaluate the use of digital technology for assisting differential diagnostics, specifically distinguishing between BPD and ASD, a distinction particularly relevant for late and female diagnostics. Differential diagnostics is a resource-intensive and time-intensive process; however, our algorithm was able to differentiate between those two diagnoses with high precision based only on two 10-minute conversations. Thus, our algorithm could be a valuable tool for clinicians assessing ASD versus differential diagnoses in adults, freeing up resources, shortening waiting times and reducing suffering.

An important advantage of our approach is its scalability and efficiency. The presently used tools, webcams and microphones, are cheap and user-friendly. Feature extraction algorithms are mainly automated, requiring little working power. Most projects developing TAD have focused on neuroimaging which is not only expensive to acquire and analyse, but also excludes individuals not able to lie still in the scanner or to handle the noise.2 As hyperreactivity to sensory aspects, including sounds, is common in autistic individuals, autistic people might be less likely to attend diagnostic evaluations that include neuroimaging.25 Especially people in rural areas or the global south may not have easy access to these methods. Our approach could be extended to other cultures without the need of expensive equipment. While some features are certainly going to differ in their expression between cultures and languages, the same process could be applied to collect data, extract features and develop machine learning algorithms. We successfully demonstrated this potential in a proof-of-concept study focusing on classifying autism in a South Korean sample.26

While most base models focusing on conceptually relevant features performed above chance when distinguishing between interaction partners from ASD-involved and BPD-involved interactions, the balanced accuracy of those models varied, ranging from 40% for the model focusing on synchronisation of body movements to 77% in the model focusing on movement and facial expressiveness while talking or listening. While some of the base models were comparably powerful when distinguishing between ASD-involved and BPD-involved or between ASD-involved or COMP interactions, there were some differences. The model focusing on synchronisation of body movements only successfully distinguished between ASD-involved and COMP interactions, possibly indicating that adults with ASD and BPD are similar in this regard. Similarly, the model including features capturing synchronisation of head with body movements and vice versa was able to distinguish between both clinical labels and the comparison label, but not between ASD-involved and BPD-involved interactions. Other models were able to distinguish between all combinations, in particular: the model focusing on speech and turn-taking features, the model focusing on synchronisation of facial expressions as well as the model focusing on facial expressiveness and movement while talking or listening. Thus, these models seem to be especially promising for differential diagnostics and could be assessed including people with other psychiatric diagnoses than BPD or ASD.

Previous research developing TAD of ASD based on behavioural data have focused on the distinction of autistic and non-clinical individuals. These classification algorithms have shown great promise with classification accuracies of 79.5% based on facial expressions,6 81.1% based on a visual-orienting task,27 85.5% based on photos taken by autistic individuals,28 89% based on grasping kinematics3 and 90.4% based on speech features.4 TAD for differentiating between psychiatric diagnoses is scarcer, with many studies focusing on the differentiation of ASD and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder in children and young adults often based on neuroimaging data.2 However, there are fewer approaches targeting adults for differentiating between psychiatric diagnoses. Demetriou and colleagues used measures of cognitive and executive function to distinguish between adults with ASD, early psychosis or social anxiety disorder.13 Their approach reached accuracies of almost 70% distinguishing between early psychosis and ASD as well as 73% distinguishing between social anxiety disorder and ASD. To our knowledge, there is currently no TAD approach published targeting the differentiation between ASD and personality disorders like BPD, despite the clinical relevance especially for women.29 Thus, the high balanced accuracy of 82% distinguishing between ASD-involved and BPD-involved interaction partners based on behavioural data is very promising and should be validated in a clinical setting.

Although the dyadic approach we chose comes with challenges, both for constructing the cross-validation and for the interpretation, we chose it to be able to assess social interactions. Simply classifying between ASD and non-clinical controls would disregard the mutual influence the two interaction partners pose on each other’s behaviour. Instead, only a dyadic approach can fully capture the reciprocal nature of social interactions, the core of ASD symptoms. However, a dyadic approach comes with specific constraints for analysis. For example, since the interaction partners are influencing each other’s behaviour, one must ensure that the model does not merely learn which interaction partner formed a dyad together. Thus, we carefully designed a cross-validation structure where interaction partners belonging together are always in the same fold, both for the outer and the inner loop, to reflect their dependence. We used the same cross-validation structure for all models and included this constraint for our permutation testing. Therefore, we can rule out that the model merely learnt dyad membership. We argue that it is worthwhile considering and incorporating a dyad structure, because dyadic conversations provide researchers with insights into the dynamic nature of social interactions that cannot be accessed on an individual basis.

We selected features for our base models in a theory-driven way, focusing on aspects of social interaction behaviour which are reflective of the core symptoms of ASD and for which reliable differences between autistic and non-autistic people have been documented. Furthermore, the conversation prompts were also chosen with the core symptoms of ASD in mind: autistic people often monologue about their hobbies due to restricted interests but struggle with conversations requiring higher levels of reciprocity with smooth turn-taking which is important during collaborative planning.5 This autism-focused feature selection process could explain why the comparison between BPD-involved and COMP interactions did not perform as well as the other models, although the stacking model still reached a respectable balanced accuracy of 65% showing great promise. If future studies were to consider developing further diagnostic algorithms specifically to classify BPD, the following considerations might be helpful. BPD is associated with differences in trust behaviour and hypersensitivity to social rejection.30 Furthermore, people with BPD are prone to misinterpreting neutral situations, experiencing feelings of social rejection in typical inclusion scenarios and demonstrating difficulties in repairing cooperative efforts following experiences of disappointment.15 These differences could be leveraged by including more competitive conversation prompts or possibly extending the conversations to include more than two people, thus increasing the possibility of misinterpreting neutrality for social rejection.

The predictive performance of multivariate machine learning models arises from the combined pattern of multiple features rather than from the isolated contribution of any single feature. This combined pattern reflects the importance of considering the overall clinical picture during the differential diagnostic process to avoid misdiagnosis.14 Nonetheless, while caution is warranted when interpreting individual features, their interpretation may still be compared with existing differential knowledge. Using the eyelid tightener during speaking and listening in both conversations was more indicative of a BPD-involved interaction. This pattern could indicate that BPD-involved interactions were characterised by stronger display of negative emotions, potentially reflecting affective instability and difficulty controlling anger, which are both core symptoms of BPD but not ASD.14 Furthermore, a higher weight of features capturing the lip corner puller during the hobbies conversation was associated with ASD-involved interactions, possibly indicating stronger display of positive emotions. These positive emotions could be explained by autistic participants talking about their special interests, a core symptom of ASD but not a primary feature of BPD.14 This pattern is also reflected in the feature weights of the Speech model, where features capturing the hobbies conversation were especially indicative of ASD-involved conversations.

Our proof-of-concept study has important limitations. First, the models currently require spoken conversational data, which excludes minimally verbal and non-speaking autistic people. Importantly, challenging differential diagnostic questions, such as distinguishing ASD from BPD, most commonly arise in verbally fluent adults. In these cases, autistic characteristics may be more subtle and primarily observable in conversational patterns, making spoken language data particularly relevant for computational approaches such as TAD. Second, the extraction of turn-taking features led to the exclusion of 17% of the dyads due to insufficient audio quality, mostly caused by equipment failure. Third, interactive study paradigms can pose participation barriers for some speaking autistic individuals who prefer not to engage in such settings. We used standard prompts to facilitate two conversational contexts, with the meal-planning being collaborative and requiring a high degree of turn-taking while the hobbies prompt allows for monologuing. Due to conversation features being sensitive to the interaction design and the specific interaction partner, the model may be driven by disorder-specific markers of the interaction partner with BPD or ASD, but it may also be driven by interactional adaptation of the non-clinical interaction partner. Nonetheless, the approach adopted here to focus on the dyad as the unit of analysis matches the nature of reciprocal interaction as an emergent feature of dyads (or groups). Fourth, while our results are encouraging and show the feasibility of our approach, the sample size consisted of 120 participants in three groups and the algorithms presented in this proof-of-concept study need to be validated in a clinical setting to assess their generality and robustness. Last, the algorithms should be assessed in comparison to other differential diagnoses to ensure their validity and generalisability.

Clinical implications

This proof-of-concept study shows the enormous potential of using scalable recording technology to assist differential diagnostics. After independent validation with larger and more diverse samples, this approach could streamline the diagnostic process and free up resources. Specifically, this algorithm could be applied to conversations in the clinical context, for example, diagnostic interviews. These conversations would be recorded with microphones and webcams, preferably as time-locked data streams to ease data preparation. Data would be collected from both clinician and patient to allow for the computation of features capturing the reciprocal social interaction and communication. After the audio and video data are cut, the open-source preprocessing pipeline is applied, and the extracted features representing the patient are used as input for the machine learning algorithm. Since the preprocessing pipeline and the algorithm already exist, this process is fast and user-friendly, requiring minimal technical skills. For each patient, one would receive a classification label as well as the decision score, capturing the model’s confidence in this classification.

The complexity of differential diagnoses, specifically for adults, has resulted in increased waiting times and prolonged suffering. One problem hampering an improvement of the situation is that current screening and diagnostic tools are not recommended for diagnostics in adulthood due to low differential diagnostic specificity.12 Thus, developing solutions able to support differential diagnostics that are faster and more objective is urgently needed. Our approach has several important advantages. First, the features capture clinically relevant behaviour, tapping into the context in which symptoms of autism are most pronounced: social interactions. These interactions are captured in live dyadic conversations. Second, we pair user-friendly devices, namely webcams and microphones, with automated feature extraction, making our approach highly scalable as there is no need for manual annotation. Third, we compute features not only within the individual and between the interaction partners, but also between modalities. These cross-modal features are highly relevant in real life, where reactions to an interaction partner are rarely confined by modality. Last, data collection can be integrated into the diagnostic process without risking circularity. Thus, we propose this approach for improving the diagnostic process using TAD to support differential diagnostics, ultimately alleviating suffering caused by long waiting times.
