 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 The Contractor will analyze data from sensors and equipment provided by Participant in order to
 provide the basis for understanding of the processes and rates for the formation of ‘persistent’
 soiling (soiling not removed by rain). The resulting, data, technical report, and publications will
 provide the basis for Participant to improve it's predictive soiling model.

 Summary of Research Results:

 The eastern United States was the primary focus area for the study. The original hypothesis was
 that locations with a heavy spring pollen season would be correlated with the worst photovoltaic
 (PV) bio-soiling losses. A soiling station was deployed with an adjacent microscope capturing
 daily images of surface deposits on glass. While soiling losses did increase during pollen season
 the losses were not sustained through rain events and into the summer. Solar Unsoiled provided
 thousands of microscopic images of the surface of solar panels that had sustained losses in the
 5% to 15% levels. Careful analysis of these images showed that pollen was present in the spring
 months but not at other times of year. Furthermore, regardless of the time of the year, fungus of
 varying degrees of coverage was found in the majority of images. A draft publication is currently
 in process to share the findings regarding fungus versus pollen soiling. The existing images were
 not sufficient to answer why certain sites have very severe coverage and others only minor
 coverage, but additional investigations are ongoing to attempt to better understand the fungal
 soiling problem.

 Task 1.2:

 The Participant will provide the following Information to the Contractor: operational data, daily
 microscopy images of deposited particulate matter (PM), meteorological data, ambient pollen
 count, and soiling station data. Contractor will quantify and document within the report: airborne
 pollen levels and relations to the associated short- term soiling losses; rainfall threshold for
 pollen removal; particle size distribution; persistent soiling rates.

 Task 1.2 Results:

 The Participant provided NLR with access to thousands of microscopic images of the surfaces of
 solar panels from the eastern U.S. Although the next step was intended to be analysis of airborne
 pollen levels and meteorological conditions in comparison to the pollen losses, the finding that
 fungus was the primary biological agent switched the research direction. Bi-weekly meetings and
 information sharing continued but the focus was directed towards understanding types or stages
 of fungal growth on the solar panels.

 Task 2:

 The Contractor will analyze 5,000 microscopy images provided by the Participant. Where
 possible quantify: fungal growth coverage; rate of fungal growth; meteorological factors
 accelerating growth; efficiency of fungal removal by site cleaning.

 Task 2 Results:

 The Contractor analyzed the images providing: classification of the degree of fungal coverage,
 presence of pollen, presence of filamentous fungi, presence of other unique characteristics, and
 investigated possible classes of fungi to explain variance across sites. reported to the participant
 that pollen was only found in images from springtime months, where Figure 1 shows an image
 with pollen versus and image without pollen.

 Fig. 1. Left image shows pollen grains in white with other soiling in black. Right image shows
 fungal soiling with some minor mineral deposits along interconnecting filaments (while no pollen
 is present).

 Many images were ambiguous due to image quality and color representation resulting from the
 low-cost microscope which allowed for thousands of images to be captured autonomously
 outdoors. For example, both images in Figure 1 show a significant amount of black objects on
 the surface that are where makeup is difficult to identify. Alternatively, Figure 2 shows a
 network of interconnected filaments that are almost certainly filamentous fungi based on well-
 established images and behavior of fungus. NLR, through past soiling analysis, suspected that
 black material such as what is shown in Figure 1 was also fungal growth rather than mineral or
 dust soiling.

 4

 Fig. 2. A microscopic image of what is highly likely to be filamentous fungi on the surface of a
 solar panel.

 To understand if the assumption of fungal soiling was correct, NLR captured images with the
 same microscope used by Solar Unsoiled on known samples at NLR. Additionally, Solar
 Unsoiled sent NLR shards of glass from a PV site in North Carolina that were hypothesized to
 have fungus present based on low quality images. When NLR compared in-house (low-quality)
 images of pre-identified fungus and mineral soiling, the results continued to support that dark
 black clumps in Figure 1 are fungus. Furthermore, the samples subjected to SEM shown in
 Figure 3 show that what appears to be black clumps in low resolution images actually consist
 primarily of spherical fungal material with a few shards of mineral material intermixed.

 Fig. 3. SEM results show black lumps that are actually primarily spherical fungal material with a
 smaller amount of minerals intermixed (smaller random shards).

 Task 3:

 The Contractor and the Participant will develop a joint journal article from the findings
 associated with Tasks 1 and 2.

 Task 3 Results:

 In 2025, this task was accomplished through a jointly written conference presentation and poster,
 given at the 2025 PV Reliability Workshop, March 4-6, Lakewood CO. The poster was
 developed in consultation with, and coauthored by sponsor teammates Alexandra Rivera and
 Michael Valerino (Solar Unsoiled). The title of the poster was “Updates on PV Bio-Soiling in the
 Southeast U.S.” The problem statement is given that “current PV sawtooth soiling models have
 been proven to be incorrect for the Southeast United States. Whereas previous models predict
 negligible soiling losses in the Southeast, the authors have demonstrated a number of sites in the
 region with sustained soiling losses greater than 10%. It is currently unclear what factors drive
 the most extreme losses and if there are alternatives to mechanical cleaning to prevent theses
 losses.” However, based on observation, a hypothesis is given that “bio-soiling losses from
 fungus and bacteria build up over months and years to create sustained soiling losses. Fungus
 and bacteria build up requires mechanical cleaning to remove.”

 These hypotheses and initial conference results were largely upheld at the conclusion of the
 CRADA, however the clarification has been made that heat- and radiation-adapted epiphytic
 fungi that form complex sub-aerial biofilms (SAB) are the primary bio-soiling problem for solar
 panels. In more general terms, black microcolonial fungi originally adapted to thrive on rock
 surfaces in various harsh climates and now have been able to use these same adaptions to thrive
 on solar panel surface, forming dark growths on rock surfaces.

 Task 4.1: Soiling Station

 A soiling station was designed and built for future deployment with the ability to capture daily
 microscopic images on several glass types. The intent is to capture fungal growth while also
 determining if the surface coating of the glass has a significant impact on the rate of fungal
 growth.

 This specialized soiling station with five microscopes has been constructed with the intent of
 gathering time series progression of fungal growth on different glass types and surface coatings.
 This station is expected to be deployed in FY25 Q4 with the intent to continue to help the PV
 industry de-risk soiling from bio-based contaminants. Figure 5 show an early prototype of an
 NLR soiling station with two microscopes for capturing daily images of biofilm growth on glass.

 Fig. 5. Soiling station deployed in North Carolina with two microscopes for measuring biofilm
 growth.

 Task 4.2: Outside expert consultation

 NLR held meetings with Dr Anna A. Gorbushina and her team, experts in fungal biology, to
 enhance paper quality and determine next steps in research. A total of 3 meetings were
 conducted over email and via conference call to help identify the sources of the bio-soiling and
 the species of the fungus captured. These meetings were critical to bring in needed expertise in
 biology and mold morphology which resulted in a positive identification of the species, and
 greater awareness and understanding of the contributors to bio-soiling proliferation.

 Task 4.3: Enhanced imaging

 The initial images of our bio-soiling were taken with a low-power microscope which gave
 general structure and morphology of the species being studied. However, to get a much closer
 image for identification and local environment understanding, NLR conducted Scanning
 Electron Microscope (SEM) images. This analysis was conducted to verify that our previous
 low-resolution images were in fact of fungus. Figure 4 shows a zoomed-in view of a fungal
 filament where small shards of mineral deposits are stuck to the filaments. These minerals, likely
 provide needed nutrients for growth of the filamentous fungi.

 Fig. 6. SEM zoom in of a fungal filament where small shards of mineral dust are seen attached
 along the filament path.

 The SEM images helped to positively identify filamentous fungus as the likely source of our bio-
 soiling.
