A theranostic wearable laser-induced graphene-hydrogel dressing for synergistic treatment and real-time monitoring of dermatophytosis
Authors: Luojia Chen, Zihao Li, Chao Shen, Si-Ling Chen, Le Cheng, Weiran Niu, Yuncong Yuan, Yidan Zhou, Jie Li, Jiansheng Su, Nan Lin, Shi Chen, ZhongMeng Lai, Ruquan Ye, Meijia Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe complexity, high recurrence rate, and need for long-term monitoring in dermatophytosis treatment underscore the demand for high-performance antifungal strategies. Here, we present a theranostic wearable hydrogel dressing (LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel) that integrates laser-induced graphene (LIG) with co-encapsulated itraconazole (ITZ) for synergistic therapy and real-time monitoring. This platform features a cationic LIG+ surface (+43.1 mV) for targeted fungal capture. Sunlight exposure induces a mild photothermal effect (∼45°C), which enables the controlled release of ITZ. The combined action of photothermal therapy (PTT) and ITZ inflicts multimodal injury on Trichophyton rubrum (T. rubrum), inducing ferroptosis along with other cellular damage mechanisms. This mechanism achieves T. rubrum eradication and biofilm disruption without recurrence in murine models. The conductive LIG network facilitates real-time, smartphone-based tracking of fungal load through voltage signals. Furthermore, LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel exert a powerful modulates immune microenvironment effect. This intelligent system, which integrates low-temperature phototherapy, immunotherapy, and real-time sensing, offers a comprehensive platform for dermatophytosis management.Keywords: Smart wearable, Laser-induced graphene, Trichophyton rubrum, Ferroptosis, Digital monitoring

Introduction

The population aging and global pandemics highlight the need for more accessible and affordable health monitoring solutions. Recent advances in stretchable and wearable technologies [1] have led to more affordable and functional wearable and digital monitoring devices [2,3]. By utilizing ultrathin and soft materials, wearable devices can interact with human body to monitor physiological signals with high fidelity [4]. Current reported composites show potential in sensing physiological conditions such as pH, temperature, oxygenation, impedance and motion changes in wounds [5]. Composites have been applied as a variety of wound patches depending on the forming structure, such as microneedles (MNs), hydrogels, microcapsules, microspheres, and fibers [6]. Besides, the utilization of specialized carrier systems, in which topical antifungal agents are conjugated with carriers such as liposomes and nanostructured lipids, enhance their skin permeability and bioavailability. Such strategies hold promises for the development of novel formulations of topical antifungal agents, thereby improving therapeutic efficacy and enhancing patient compliance [7,8] (see Scheme 1).

Schematic illustration of the LIG + ITZ@PCS hydrogel for theranostic management of dermatophytosis. The cationic LIG + surface captures T. rubrum, while sunlight triggers mild photothermal heating (∼45°C) and controlled ITZ release, inducing ferroptosis and multimodal fungal killing. The conductive LIG network enables real-time smartphone-based monitoring of fungal load, and the dressing further modulates the immune microenvironment to promote tissue repair.

Dermatophytosis is a pervasive global fungal infection, which affects over 70% of people irrespective of age or sex. Tinea pedis, represents at least 50% of foot abnormalities, is a contagious fungal skin infection of feet caused by dermatophytes. Its prevalence has surged among aging and immunocompromised populations, emerging as a widespread public health challenge [9]. Onychomycosis (nail fungus), which occurs in approximately 30–59% of tinea pedis cases, causes nail to become thick, yellow and show white spots and streak [10]. Even though it is not life-threatening, approximately 50% of individuals with tinea pedis report experiencing pain or other forms of discomfort, leading to a diminished patients' quality of life [11].

The most prevalent and predominant causative pathogen of tinea pedis is Trichophyton rubrum (T. rubrum), which can invade keratinized tissues in immunocompetent individuals. Despite great efforts on the development of novel antifungal agents, effective antifungal drugs are still lagging behind [12,13]. This is because fungi share similar enzymes and biochemical pathways with humans, resulting in a very limited number of unique targets that can be effectively utilized for antifungal development [14]. Currently, treatments for tinea pedis mainly include topical antifungals, oral antifungals, or a combination of both [15]. Nevertheless, treatment with these pharmacological agents often requires long-term administration [16]. This may result in adverse effects such as drug resistance, gastrointestinal upsets, hepatotoxicity and congestive heart failure [16]. These potential side effects can lead to psychological concerns among patients, thereby affecting the use of oral drugs [17].

Laser-induced graphene (LIG) has emerged as a versatile platform for monolithically integrated, multifunctional, and stretchable bioelectronics, owing to its tunable physicochemical properties and unique 3D porous architecture [18]. This architecture combines high conductivity and large specific surface area, optimizing mass transport and charge transfer efficiency [19]. These attributes are indispensable for advanced sensor systems capable of simultaneous physical, chemical, and electrophysiological signal monitoring [20,21]. In addition to its conductivity, mechanical flexibility and biocompatibility further position LIG as a pivotal material for next-generation wearable biosensors [22]. However, most fabrication routes rely on flexible polyimide (PI) substrates or thick elastic films, owing to the mechanical constraints associated with transferring LIG onto soft elastomers [23]. The development of LIG-based dressing that combines ultrathin structural characteristics with robust stretchability remains a significant challenge [23]. To address these limitations, strategies integrating LIG with stimuli-responsive hydrogels have emerged. Hydrogels, characterized by their hydrated 3D crosslinked networks, combine softness with excellent tissue compatibility, rendering them ideal candidates for flexible and wearable bioelectronic interfaces [24]. Their intrinsic properties, nontoxicity, biodegradability, biocompatibility, and antimicrobial activity, further extend their utility for applications such as wound healing, hemostatic materials, sealants, wearable devices, and drug carriers [[24], [25], [26], [27]]. Recent advances underscore this potential. Graphene-polyacrylic acid composite hydrogels demonstrate enhanced compressive strength and conductivity without compromising biocompatibility [28]. Poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAAm)-based hydrogels enable temperature-controlled drug release [29,30]. Further functionalization with chitosan (CS), a polysaccharide bearing protonated amino groups that disrupt microbial membranes, enables antimicrobial activity and enhanced mechanical stability through hydrophobic modification of d-glucosamine units [31,32]. Such hybrid systems synergize LIG's electronic capabilities with hydrogels' biocompatibility and controlled drug delivery, advancing multifunctional wearable platforms [33,34].

Here, we have developed an intelligent multifunctional wearable cutaneous fungal dermatosis treating/monitoring system, defined as LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel. The hydrogel contains LIG functionalized with quaternary pyridinium cations (denoted as LIG+) [35], which helps T. rubrum capture through electrostatic interactions. By further leveraging the broad solar spectrum absorption property of LIG, sunlight can activate photothermal therapy (PTT) and trigger controlled ITZ release, which induces T. rubrum ferroptosis and biofilm disruption. In addition, the voltage signals across the LIG surface can reflect the fungal concentrations at the nidus of foot. By transmitting voltage signals to smartphones, it enables real-time monitoring of treatment. Furthermore, the LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel can reshape the immune microenvironment, reduce inflammation, and avoid the occurrence of pain-related depressive behaviors. The easy-to-use and stable antifungal performance of LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel dressings show great potential for advantages in home health care, self-monitoring, and sustainability.

Results

Mechanical, adhesive properties, and T. rubrum capture performance

The multifunctional LIG+ ITZ@PCS hydrogel was fabricated through a sequential strategy integrating surface engineering, drug loading, and hydrogel assembly (Fig. 1A). The process began with the quaternization of the LIG surface, which transformed the originally negatively charged surface potential into a highly positive state. Zeta potential measurements (n = 5) confirmed this modification, showing a shift from −14.5 ± 0.5 mV for pristine LIG to +43.1 ± 1.9 mV for LIG+ (Fig. 1B). This quantitative shift from negative to positive confirms the successful introduction of cationic groups onto the LIG surface. Subsequently, ITZ was co-encapsulated using a eutectic fatty acid mixture of lauric acid (LA) and stearic acid (SA). Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) analysis revealed that the eutectic system markedly lowered the melting point of the fatty acids, thereby reducing the release threshold for ITZ and enabling sunlight-triggered photothermal therapy under mild conditions (Fig. 1C). The positively charged LIG+ITZ was subsequently delaminated from the PI substrate and transferred onto the surface of the polyacrylamide/chitosan (PAAm/CS) hydrogel, yielding the final composite structure, LIG+ITZ@PCS (Fig. 1D). Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was further performed to characterize the chemical composition of the PAAm/CS hydrogel (Fig. S1). The characteristic absorption bands confirmed the successful incorporation of PAAm and chitosan components [36], supporting the formation of the hydrogel matrix used for integration. This configuration endowed the LIG+ layer surface to have the ability to treat and efficiently capture negatively charged fungal structures, while the hydrogel provided a soft, biocompatible support.

Both of LIGITZ@PCS and LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel dressings demonstrated a remarkable stretchability, extending up to 4.5 times their original length (Fig. S2). Beyond tensile deformation, compressive testing was conducted to further evaluate their mechanical robustness. The stress–strain profiles revealed that the incorporation of the LIG+ITZ layer exerted negligible influence on the intrinsic elasticity of the hydrogel (Fig. 1E). Moreover, cyclic compression tests confirmed excellent fatigue resistance and self-recovery, as the curves remained nearly overlapping over ten successive loading–unloading cycles without noticeable stress decay (Fig. 1F). To further evaluate the mechanical suitability of the hydrogel for wearable applications, adhesion and cyclic deformation tests were conducted (Fig. S3). The lap shear and peel tests showed stable interfacial adhesion and resistance to peeling-induced detachment, while cyclic tensile loading–unloading tests indicated reproducible tensile behavior under repeated deformation. These results indicate that the hydrogel maintains stable adhesion, mechanical compliance, and deformation tolerance, supporting its use as a wearable sensing/treatment interface.

We utilized scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to observe that the LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel forms a highly conformal biointerface with porcine skin (Fig. S4, SEM image of pristine hydrogel shown in Fig. S5). Additionally, we performed a series of external force tests to further evaluate the stability of its adhesion performance. (Fig. 1G and Fig. S6). These tests include stretching, twisting and bending, washing, and turning, aiming to evaluate the tensile strength, fracture toughness, elastic recovery ability, durability, and stability of hydrogels in simulating human motion. Throughout these manipulations, the hydrogel and LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel remained firmly adhered to the pig skin without any signs of separation. This indicates that the LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel can maintain stable adhesion even under complex mechanical stress in practical applications, laying a solid foundation for the development of high-performance wearable devices. Additionally, the robust mechanical properties of LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel allowed it to be bent into various angles (Fig. 1H and Fig. S7). Notably, the LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel achieves conformal adhesion to dynamically bending plantar skin and toe surfaces, synchronizing with epidermal strain gradients during foot motion (Fig. 1I). This flexibility is particularly significant as it fulfills the requirement of wound dressings in dynamic human feet skin locations that experience high-frequency movements. The ability of this hydrogel to maintain its structural integrity while accommodating these movements ensures that it can provide effective protection for wounds without causing discomfort or restricting mobility.

The high water-absorbing capacity of hydrogels is particularly important. Hydrogels can rapidly absorb excess tissue exudate, reducing the local moist environment, thereby inhibiting the growth of fungi and bacteria. To this end, we have studied the swelling ratio of the hydrogels (Fig. 1J). The swelling ratios of the three types of hydrogels are essentially consistent, which proves that the loading of LIG does not affect the water-absorbing property of the hydrogels. In the first 30 min, the hydrogels absorb water rapidly, with swelling rates of approximately 625%, 509%, and 494%, respectively. After 25 h, the swelling ratio stabilizes. It is evident that LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogels possess excellent and sustainable hygroscopic properties, capable of absorbing the excess tissue exudate caused by Tinea pedis.

To evaluate the capture capacity of the hydrogel, we incubated the hydrogel with T. rubrum for 1 h and monitored the OD600 of T. rubrum in the supernatant at different time (0 min, 30 min, 60 min). As shown in Fig. 1K, L and Fig. S8A, the OD600 values of all groups decreased with the extension of incubation time. In particular, the LIG+@PCS hydrogel and LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel had OD600 values of only 0.16 and 0.12 in the supernatant at 60 min, while the control groups LIG@PCS hydrogel and LIGITZ@PCS hydrogel had OD600 values of 0.58 and 0.54, respectively. Importantly, this efficient capture capability was maintained under conditions simulating the acidic skin microenvironment (pH: 5.5) (Fig. S9). This indicates that the cationic hydrogel has a significant capture effect on T. rubrum. Furthermore, we performed SEM observations on the surface of the hydrogel after 60 min of incubation. As shown in Fig. 1M and Fig. S8B, the LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel adsorbed a significantly higher number of T. rubrum. Comparative analysis revealed a more dispersed distribution pattern of T. rubrum colonies in the ITZ-containing group compared to the LIG+@PCS hydrogel. This phenomenon may be attributed to the antifungal mechanism of ITZ, which specifically inhibits the biosynthesis of ergosterol [37]. The resulting membrane destabilization significantly reduces extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) secretion, thereby compromising the structural integrity of fungal aggregates and establishing a critical precondition for enhanced antimicrobial efficacy. The strong capture capacity of the LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel provides a solid foundation for the subsequent effective exertion of antifungal effects.

LIG + ITZ@PCS hydrogel eradicates T. rubrum via ferroptosis

LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel integrates temperature-responsive microgels for controlled ITZ release. Under 1.5 sunlight, the surface reaches approximately 33°C within 5 min and become stabilized at 45°C over 20 min (Fig. S10). This “comfort heating” program can maximize the patient's compliance. Crucially, the temperature-sensitive microgels initiate ITZ release at 37°C (near physiological temperature), enabling precise drug liberation at the infection site (Fig. S11). Furthermore, the controlled-release characteristics of ITZ remained relatively stable with increasing temperature and no significant degradation of the hydrogel was observed in the simulated acidic skin microenvironment (pH 5.5), demonstrating the robustness of the drug delivery system and its suitability for the physiological environment (Fig. S12).

We evaluated the antifungal effect of LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel dressing on planktonic T. rubrum with a live & dead viability/cytotoxicity assay kit. Compared to the control and hydrogel without irradiation, the LIGs (LIG@PCS, LIG+@PCS, LIGITZ@PCS, LIG+ITZ@PCS) hydrogel showed much redder staining, indicating that more T. rubrum were killed by the LIGs (Fig. S13). Notably, the antifungal efficacy of the LIG+ITZ@PCS reached as high as 100% with sunlight exposure, highlighting the synergy of photothermal effect and electrostatic interactions. However, there was only a small portion of red fluorescence from the single treatment of 2.0 μg mL−1 ITZ group irrespective of irradiation. This may be attributed to the time-dependent pharmacological properties of ITZ [38]. Current research indicates that accurate evaluation of its minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) in vitro typically requires sustained action for over 7 days, whereas a brief 30 min exposure period is far from sufficient [39]. As shown in Fig. S14, the antifungal efficacy of the LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel dressings was further determined by evaluating its capacity to inhibit the growth of T. rubrum using the plate counting method. Under sunlight irradiation, neither the control group nor the hydrogel group showed any observable antifungal effect. While the LIG@PCS group and the ITZ group exhibited an inhibitory effect on the growth of T. rubrum, the antifungal efficacy of the LIG+ITZ@PCS group was significantly stronger in comparison. Besides, SEM was also employed to investigate the synergistic killing effects of LIG+ITZ@PCS (Fig. S15). Without light irradiation, T. rubrum showed oval in shape with a dense and homogeneous cytoplasm and an intact cell wall in the dark groups of control, hydrogel and LIG@PCS. After the application of sunlight irradiation, T. rubrum treated with LIG+@PCS, LIGITZ@PCS and LIG+ITZ@PCS presented irregular and shrunken shapes, with cracks and cellular contents leaking as observed. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) further elucidated the impact of LIG on the internal structures of T. rubrum. As shown in Fig. 2A, the T. rubrum in the light-control group were oval in shape with a dense and homogeneous cytoplasm and an intact cell wall (186.10 mm), and the mitochondria have normal morphology. However, for T. rubrum treated with LIG + ITZ@PCS group, T. rubrum conidia exhibited a characteristic circular morphology, with their cell walls displaying abnormal thickening (reaching 359.80 mm) accompanied by localized rupture (indicated by red circles), resulting in the leakage of intracellular contents. For filamentous fungi, excessive thickening of the conidial cell wall significantly increases rigidity, thereby compromising the elastic deformation capacity of the wall, ultimately leading to diminished budding and hyphal extension capabilities [40]. Furthermore, the LIG+ITZ@PCS group showed a marked increase in the secretion of Extracellular Vesicles (ECVs). This phenomenon correlates with the cell wall damage-repair mechanism [41]. The DiOC2(3) (3,3′-Diethyloxacarbocyanine iodide) fluorescent probe was used to assess membrane potential changes across treatment groups [42]. The LIG+ITZ@PCS group under sunlight irradiation exhibited the lowest red/green fluorescence ratio (0.27), indicating the most significant reduction in membrane potential (Fig. S16).

To further investigate the antifungal mechanism of LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel against T. rubrum, we performed gene transcriptome sequencing to analyze the global gene expression profile of T. rubrum following treatment with LIG + ITZ@PCS hydrogel under sunlight irradiation (Fig. S19 and S20). Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and a sample correlation clustering heatmap demonstrated a significant separation between the LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel treatment and the control group, alongside high intra-group correlation among biological replicates (Fig. S21 and 22).

Hierarchical clustering analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across treatment groups is presented in Fig. S23. Volcano plot analysis identified 95 significantly upregulated genes and 305 significantly downregulated genes (|log2FoldChange|>1, P-value<0.05) in T. rubrum treated with LIG + ITZ@PCS hydrogel under sunlight irradiation compared to the control group (Fig. 2B). These findings demonstrate that LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel exerts substantial multi-target regulatory effects on genes associated with T. rubrum pathogenicity progression (including biofilm formation, host immune evasion, and morphological transition) and drug resistance mechanisms.

Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) pathway enrichment analysis of DEGs revealed that LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel significantly modulated several metabolic pathways in T. rubrum, including glutathione metabolism, ABC transporters, aspartate and glutamate metabolism (Fig. 2C and Fig. S24). Notably, these pathways have been critically implicated in ferroptosis mechanisms [47,48]. Gene Ontology (GO) annotation analysis indicated that the antifungal effect of LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel under sunlight irradiation primarily involved key biological processes, including response to chemical stress, sulfite reductase activity and lipid peroxidation (Fig. 2D).

Ferroptosis is a form of regulated cell death, characterized by iron overload, accumulation of lipid reactive oxygen species (ROS), and depletion of glutathione (GSH) [49]. The accumulation of Fe2+ is one of the cardinal indicators of ferroptosis. Among iron metabolism-related proteins, expression of the iron/copper reductase transmembrane component B (FreB) is enhanced. Upregulation of the FreB gene accelerates the reduction of Fe3+ to reactive Fe2+, thereby promoting intracellular accumulation of labile iron. Concurrently, expression of the iron transport oxidase gene (FET3) is suppressed (Fig. 2E and F and Fig. S25). This collectively disrupts the redox balance of intracellular iron and impairs normal iron metabolism [50]. The accumulation of Fe2+ is one of the cardinal indicators of ferroptosis. During this process, Fe2+catalyze the Fenton reaction, generating hydroxyl radicals that markedly elevate the levels of oxidative stress within the cell, thereby precipitating cellular damage. We monitored the intracellular levels of Fe2+ within T. rubrum. As illustrated in Fig. 2G and Fig. S26), a significant accumulation of Fe2+ in T. rubrum cells treated with LIG + ITZ@PCS. Another critical factor triggering ferroptosis is the depletion of glutathione (GSH) levels. During the process of ferroptosis, the cellular antioxidant system is compromised, leading to a decrease in GSH levels and a concomitant increase in intracellular oxidative stress [51]. As expected, genes associated with glutathione synthesis (gedE/GST3/gstk-1/gsa1/MGST) were uniformly downregulated. This indicates partial oxidation of intracellular GSH to GSSG in T. rubrum, which may trigger ferroptosis. We conducted assays to measure the intracellular GSH levels in T. rubrum. The results indicated that the GSH levels in T. rubrum treated with LIGs were significantly reduced, with the LIG+ITZ@PCS treatment group exhibiting the lowest GSH levels (only 0.21) (Fig. 2H and Fig. S27). This suggests that LIG+ITZ@PCS can effectively synergize the actions of PTT and ITZ to substantially deplete GSH, thereby enhancing T. rubrum oxidative damage. Reduced GSH levels lead to accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and promote lipid peroxidation. We observed significantly elevated expression of the NADPH oxidase (NOX) gene NOX1. In eukaryotic organisms, NOX is involved in a multitude of physiological metabolic processes and cellular differentiation events [52]. NOX can be activated by lipid peroxidation products that accelerates the depletion of GSH and enhances cellular sensitivity to ferroptosis [53]. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) validated the RNA-seq results, LIG+ITZ@PCS significantly upregulated NOX expression by 12.95-fold (Fig. 2I, Fig. S28 and Table S1). Mitochondria, as the primary site for iron utilization, can have their function impaired by abnormalities in iron metabolism. Expression of the iron-sulfur cluster binding gene (sreA) and mitochondrial respiration-related genes (RCF2/ccp1/mcfO/MDM35) was suppressed. Impaired iron-sulfur cluster synthesis consequently induces mitochondrial dysfunction, reducing protein levels of mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes and impairing respiratory function, which correlates with mitochondrial structural damage observed via TEM.

To further validate whether ferroptosis occurred in T. rubrum, the fungal conidia were pre-incubated with the Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) solution, followed by treatment with different hydrogel groups (LIG+ITZ@PCS, LIGITZ@PCS, LIG+@PCS, LIG@PCS, ITZ@PCS) under both light and dark conditions. The rescue experiment demonstrated that the ferroptosis inhibitor Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) could significantly inhibit the cell death of T. rubrum induced by the LIG + ITZ@PCS hydrogel treatment. The potent antifungal efficacy of the LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel under sunlight irradiation was attenuated in the Fer-1 pre-treated group, with the viability rate increasing by approximately 42% compared to the non-inhibited control (Fig. S29). This partial rescue of cell death upon specific ferroptosis inhibition provides direct evidence confirming the occurrence of ferroptosis in T. rubrum. Crucially, the LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel with light group retained substantial antifungal activity even with Fer-1, consistent with its designed multimodal action combining photothermal therapy and ITZ release.

We further observed that LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel significantly downregulated heat shock factor (HSF) HSF2 and heat shock protein (HSP) genes (HSP90/HSP60/HSP70). This phenomenon is likely mediated by LIG's exceptional photothermal conversion efficiency. In eukaryotes, thermoadaptive responses are HSF-dependent [54]. Specifically, HSP inhibition may compromise conidiation, development, hyphal growth, attenuates stress adaptation and disrupts mitochondrial chaperone function [[55], [56], [57]]. These alterations impair T. rubrum's self-repair capacity, thereby affecting the functions of the chitin synthase genes (CHS2/CHS7). Given that the LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel contains ITZ, we found that key ergosterol biosynthesis genes were significantly downregulated. This includes ERG26, which encodes C-3 sterol dehydrogenase/C-4 decarboxylase (a crucial enzyme for the C-4 demethylation step), and ERG10A/ERG10B, encoding cytoplasmic acetyl-CoA acetyltransferase that catalyzes the pathway's initial reaction. This transcriptional repression directly aligns with the pharmacological action of ITZ and potently disrupts fungal cell membrane integrity and function [58,59]. Suppressed expression of these genes impairs sporulation integrity, sporangium development, and hyphal elongation, ultimately attenuating fungal virulence [60]. This observation aligns with experimentally documented fungal plasma membrane fragmentation. Furthermore, key genes regulating cytoskeletal organization and polarity (arc3/ARC15/PAN1) were significantly suppressed. Their functional impairment directly obstructs actin polymerization and polarized growth at hyphal tips, triggering aberrant budding and cytoskeletal disorganization [61]. Downregulation of these genes induced pseudohyphal regulatory dysfunction (reduced stuA and increased hat1 expression), inhibiting hyphal extension. Consequently, biofilm-associated genes (ace2/SNF1/snf21) are downregulated, diminishing biofilm formation capacity. The development of hyphal facilitates rapid immune evasion [62], LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel treatment significantly suppressed host escape-related genes (yap1/SUB2/SSD1). Additionally, efflux pump genes (patC/MFS2/aurT/dotC/atnC/crf2) were suppressed, including drug resistance genes including multidrug resistance transporters (MDR1/MDR2/MDR3) and ABC transporters (abcB/abcI).

In conclusion, our research has demonstrated that LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel exerts potent antifungal activity against T.rubrum synergized by photothermal therapy and ITZ interactions (Fig. 2J). LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel orchestrates iron dyshomeostasis, triggering Fe2+ accumulation and Fenton reactions, which leads to ferroptosis. Concurrent depletion of GSH and downregulation of glutathione synthesis genes disrupt redox balance, while NOX1 overexpression amplifies ROS generation and lipid peroxidation. Mitochondrial dysfunction is evidenced by cristae fragmentation, ATP depletion, and impaired respiration chain gene expression. The hydrogel further compromises fungal virulence by suppressing HSP, cytoskeletal organizers, and biofilm formation genes. Crucially, it overcomes resistance by inhibiting efflux pumps and multidrug transporters, while blocking immune evasion.

LIG + ITZ@PCS hydrogel in eliminating T. rubrum biofilms and destroying hyphae

Emerging evidence of the role of biofilm in diseases may account for antifungal drug resistance and increased virulence [63]. Consequently, efficacious antifungal agents must not only suppress the proliferation of planktonic fungi but also effectively eliminating mature biofilm [64,65]. We validate the RNA-seq results showing downregulation of biofilm formation-related genes and inhibition of hyphal growth. When T. rubrum was pre-treated with LIG+ITZ@PCS under sunlight, the formation of biofilm was significantly inhibited. In contrast, biofilm formation still occurred when T. rubrum was treated with LIGs alone or ITZ alone (Fig. S30). The crystal violet staining results provided further confirmation of this result (Fig. 3A, B and Fig. S31).

To investigate how LIG+ITZ@PCS reduces the formation of biofilms in T. rubrum, we employed lactophenol cotton blue (LPCB) staining to observe the inhibitory effects of LIGs and ITZ on the hyphal growth and intercrossing in T. rubrum. This approach allowed us to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the observed biofilm inhibition by targeting the critical stages of fungal biofilm development. The development of filamentous fungal biofilms progresses from spore attachment to the formation of small colonies. Subsequently, through hyphal extension and branching, a monolayer extracellular matrix is formed. Eventually, a three-dimensional structure is established via the formation of a hyphal network. Therefore, the interwoven growth of hyphae is one of the primary reasons for the formation of robust biofilms in filamentous fungi. As shown in Fig. 3C, D and Fig. S32, the number of hyphal crossings significantly increased and substantial degree of hyphal showed remarkable elongation and extensive branching, with some even formed dense clusters, was observed in Control and Hydrogel group. In contrast, T. rubrum hyphae crossings in the LIG + ITZ@PCS group was significantly reduced, with only 7 intersection points observed. Additionally, LPCB staining revealed a substantial presence of distressed hyphae (indicated by red arrows) in the LIG+ITZ@PCS group [66]. Distressed hyphae were defined as follows: (i) darkened hyphal walls; (ii) granulated alterations in cytoplasmic structures [66]. These results demonstrate that LIG+ITZ@PCS effectively disrupts T. rubrum spores and suppresses hyphal developmental capacity, with a significant increase in distressed hyphae, through synergistic interactions of inherent electrostatic attraction, PTT, and ITZ. This action consequently impedes hyphal network formation, ultimately exhibiting remarkable efficacy in inhibiting biofilm maturation. This finding further corroborates the RNA-seq results.

Compared with the inhibition of planktonic T. rubrum, the elimination of mature biofilms is much more challenging. Conventional antifungal drugs often fail to eliminate biofilms, increasing pathogenicity and drug resistance. This is because fungi in biofilms slow their metabolism and form spores or persister cells due to uneven nutrient distribution, reducing their antibiotic susceptibility. Therefore, the effect of LIG+ITZ@PCS on eradicating mature biofilms was studied. A biofilm model was created by growing T. rubrum on glass slides, EPSs are electronegative due to their components, such as polysaccharides, proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids. Since both of LIG+@PCS and LIG+ITZ@PCS has multiple positive charges, they can bind with EPSs and T. rubrum, thus promoting its adsorption into the biofilm. The 3D images are shown in Fig. 3E and F and Fig. S33. ITZ treatment alone showed negligible killing effect, with strong green and only dim red fluorescence observed. In contrast, the combination of LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel with irradiation demonstrated a superior synergistic effect, evidenced by strong red fluorescence and visible holes in the biofilm. The quantitative assessment of biofilm biomass, average thickness, and fungal survival rate demonstrated that the irradiated LIG+ITZ@PCS treatment achieved the lowest values (Fig. 3G–I and Fig. S34). SEM analysis revealed distinct morphological changes in T. rubrum biofilms after various treatments. Control group showed intact hyphae with smooth surfaces, while ITZ treatment caused only minor surface wrinkling without compromising structural integrity. In stark contrast, biofilms treated with irradiated LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel exhibited severe deformation, appearing shrunken and collapsed with extensive surface damage, including perforations and fractures (Fig. 3J and Fig. S35), indicating superior biofilm eradication efficacy.

We have demonstrated that LIG+ITZ@PCS induces ferroptosis in T. rubrum by exacerbating oxidative stress and depleting GSH, potentially leading to a critical accumulation of intracellular ROS. We employed the fluorescent probe dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH) to detect ROS levels in biofilms (Fig. 3K and Fig. S36). Compared to untreated biofilms or those treated with ITZ, biofilms treated with LIG+ITZ@PCS under light irradiation exhibited massive endogenous ROS accumulation, with fluorescence intensity up to 2.1-fold higher than the untreated group. Unlike the ROS signals produced by fungi under heat stress, which may ultimately activate protective mechanisms, the ROS storm induced by LIG+ITZ@PCS exceeds cellular tolerance thresholds. It disrupts mitochondrial function, further triggering additional ROS generation and forming a vicious cycle. Consequently, it synergistically achieves efficient eradication of planktonic cells and thorough disruption of stubborn mature biofilms.

Immune Microenvironment Remodeling by LIG + ITZ@PCS hydrogel

Tinea pedis, as a common fungal skin infection, is closely related to the imbalance of the immune microenvironment in its recurrent attacks. However, traditional antifungal drugs, such as ITZ, although capable of inhibiting pathogens by disrupting the stability of fungal cell membranes through the inhibition of ergosterol synthesis, have limited effects on modulating the immune microenvironment. This limitation may be a significant reason for the development of treatment resistance.

The LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel captures T. rubrum on the mouse foot through cationic interactions and generates a photothermal effect under sunlight. In this process, LIG can efficiently convert light energy into heat energy. Meanwhile, the microgels in the hydrogel release ITZ, which works synergistically to exert antifungal effects, destroy and lyse T. rubrum. The efficient capture and subsequent destruction of fungi by the combined therapy lead to the direct release and exposure of fungal antigens, which may promote the release of pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and activate innate immune cells such as macrophage [77]. This provides the initial immunological signal that drives the subsequent changes in immune cell populations. INOS/MMR Immunofluorescence staining revealed that the treatment significantly altered macrophage polarization, shifting the balance from a pro-inflammatory state towards a reparative state. Specifically, the proportion of M1 macrophages (iNOS) decreased from 6.2% in the w/o treatment group to 1.0% in the w/treatment group. Conversely, the proportion of M2 macrophages (MMR) increased to 5.6% following treatment (Fig. 6A and Fig. S45). This shift in macrophage polarization was further corroborated by flow cytometric analysis (Fig. S46). Macrophage polarization is mechanistically linked to the clearance of the fungal burden and the reduction of pro-inflammatory stimuli by the therapy, creating a microenvironment conducive to healing.

The polarization shift of macrophages not only signifies the re-establishment of the inflammation-repair balance but, more crucially, this reshaping of the immune phenotype may form a positive regulatory loop through the maturation of dendritic cells (DCs), thereby activating adaptive immunity. The altered cytokine milieu and the availability of fungal antigens from cleared pathogens, resulting directly from the effective treatment, provide the necessary signals for DC activation and maturation [78]. Flow cytometry data showed that the maturation rate of DCs in the w/treatment group significantly increased to 39.5% (compared to 23% in the w/o treatment group and 28.6% in the ITZ group) (Fig. 6B and Fig. S47). This suggests that the LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel, by promoting the maturation of DCs, may accelerate the initiation of a fungus antigen-specific immune response. Mature DCs then migrate to lymphoid tissues, where they present the processed fungal antigens to T cells, establishing a pathway from treatment efficacy to adaptive immune activation[79]. This phenomenon was further validated in subsequent analyses of T-cell subsets: the w/treatment group exhibited significantly higher proportions of CD4+ T cells (47.2%) and CD8+ T cells (30.9%) compared to other groups, with a CD4/CD8 ratio of 1.53 (compared to 1.02 in the w/o treatment group, 1.35 in the ITZ group, and 1.28 in the Control group) (Fig. 6C–G).

Grocott-Gomori methenamine silver (GMS) staining results intuitively show that the skin T. rubrum load in the w/treatment group approached that of the Control group, with a significantly higher clearance efficiency than the ITZ group (Fig. 6H and I). In the hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining results, the w/treatment group exhibited pathological features such as inflammation, epidermal thickness, and hyperkeratosis that were restored to near-normal levels compared to the healthy control group. In contrast, the w/o treatment group showed markedly thickened epidermis with increased numbers of spinous cells (black arrows) and hyperkeratosis in the stratum corneum (blue arrows). The dermis displayed extensive granulation tissue proliferation composed of fibroblasts (yellow arrows) and fibrocytes, with massive infiltration of granulocytes (red arrows). Although the ITZ group showed some improvement in the degree of abnormality in the stratum corneum, it still exhibited epidermal thickening, increased numbers of spinous cells, and massive infiltration of granulocytes (Fig. 6J and K).

The presence of tissue exudate and necrotic tissue from tinea pedis creates a favorable environment for the growth of T. rubrum. The proliferating T. rubrum can lead to secondary infections in the foot, and persistent infection can destabilize the collagen matrix of the dermis, creating a vicious cycle that further delays the tissue repair process. The presence of tissue exudate and necrotic tissue from tinea pedis creates a favorable environment for the growth of T. rubrum. Quantitative results from Masson staining showed that the collagen content in the w/treatment group (40%) was significantly higher than that in the w/o treatment group (17.83%) and the ITZ group (23.83%), indicating that the hydrogel inhibits fungal degradation of collagen and reconstructs the three-dimensional scaffold structure of the skin tissue (Fig. 6L and M). Moreover, we demonstrate the complete resolution of epidermal thickening and hyperkeratosis in the treatment group, as well as the reduction of inflammatory cell infiltration in the dermis observed in the HE staining, were highly consistent with the restoration of collagen content.

Throughout this process, the LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel reshapes the immune microenvironment of tinea pedis by modulating the three types of different immune cells: (1) regulation of macrophage polarization to balance inflammation and repair processes, (2) promotion of DC maturation and (3) modulation of the ratio of T cell subsets. Consequently, this approach reduces inflammatory responses, breaks the infection-damage cycle, and supports the recovery of the foot skin barrier by repairing the collagen network (Fig. 6N).

Among the 37 patients, 24 of them are diagnosed as fugus dermatosis, leaving 13 were not detected any fungal related feature structures.

From the SEM image of LIG+ITZ, it exhibits high specific surface area with porous structure. LIG possesses advantageous microporous structure and robust adsorption capacity for organic molecules, widely utilized in water purification processes [80]. Meanwhile, in clinical tooth-whitening, peroxide, such as H2O2 is often employed for oxidizing the stains. Since H2O2 is one of the ROS in PDT, it could be envisioned that LIG+ITZ@PCS with abundant porous structures may be applicable to clearance of toenail and foot skin. Collectively, LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel dressing can clear toenail while disintegrating the fungal biofilm efficiently, making it a potential therapeutic agent for clinical usage.

Encouraged by the outstanding performance of LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel dressing to treat tinea pedis in vitro and in vivo, we further investigated its biocompatibility for potential application in the clinic. These collective results confirm the good biocompatibility of the LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel dressing.

Conclusions

We developed an intelligent wearable hydrogel dressing (LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel) that integrates flexible sensing with precise therapeutic functions, offering an innovative solution for the dynamic management of skin fungal infections. The system couples the cationic properties of LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel for targeted fungal capture with sunlight-triggered photothermal effects that enable controlled release of ITZ from PNIPAAm microgels at 37°C. This cascade induces mitochondrial damage, Fe2+ overload, ROS accumulation, and GSH depletion in T. rubrum, triggering ferroptosis through lipid peroxidation. As a result, nearly 100% fungal eradication is achieved while concurrently suppressing immune evasion, disrupting biofilm formation, and overcoming drug resistance by inhibiting efflux pumps and MDR transporters. The conductive LIG network further enables real-time and high-precision in situ monitoring by transducing physiological signals into electrical signals via a smartphone with only two electrodes. Additionally, the LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel reshapes the immune microenvironment of tinea pedis lesions by modulating the three types of different immune cells: (1) polarizing macrophages to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype to suppress excessive inflammation; (2) promoting DCs maturation to enhance antigen presentation; and (3) optimizing the CD4+/CD8+ T cell ratio to restore immune homeostasis, ultimately accelerating skin barrier repair and alleviating pain-related behavioral disorders.

This intelligent system, integrating low-temperature therapy, immune modulation, and real-time monitoring, establishes a new paradigm for dermatological diagnosis and treatment. Beyond fungal infections, the platform shows great potential in addressing multidrug-resistant pathogens and promoting the healing of chronic wounds, thereby extending its utility to broader dermatological and wound-care applications. Future directions include incorporating wireless communication and artificial intelligence to construct autonomous and personalized diagnostic–therapeutic systems that function outside conventional medical institutions. By uniting LIG–hydrogel engineering, immune regulation, and wearable electronics, this work opens new avenues for precision and intelligent dermatological care.

Preparation of intelligent LIG dressing

PI film with a thickness of 120 μm (Zeman Tape Material Technology, China) was directly irradiated by a 10.6 μm CO2 laser marking machine (Minsheng Laser #MSDB-FM60 CO2 Laser Marker, 60W) to obtain LIG. The laser was operated in vector mode under ambient air atmosphere, and the laser power, speed, frequency, line spacing, and pulses were set as 1.8 W, 1000 mm/s, 10 kHz, 0.03 mm, and 5, respectively.

To introduce positive charges onto the LIG surface, LIG films were used as substrates. A solution of 0.6368 g 4-aminopyridine dissolved in 10 mL of dimethyl sulfoxide was prepared and sonicated until fully transparent, after which 1 mL of isopentyl nitrite was added. The LIG films were placed on a hot plate preheated to 70°C, and approximately 200 μL cm−2 of the reaction mixture was added dropwise to the surface. The films were maintained at 70°C for 1 h to allow anchoring of pyridine groups, then rinsed several times with dimethylformamide (DMF) and dried at 60°C. For quaternization, a mixture of 3 mL 2,6-lutidine, 3 mL iodomethane, and 3.6 mL DMF was prepared. 800 μL of this solution was carefully dropped onto the LIG surface at 60°C, ensuring uniform coverage and controlled addition speed. After reaction, the films were thoroughly washed with DMF and ethanol and dried at 60°C to yield positively charged LIG (LIG+).

To prepare the eutectic carrier for ITZ loading, 2 g of lauric acid (LA) and 0.5 g of stearic acid (SA) were heated together at 70°C with stirring for 1 h until fully melted, followed by cooling to room temperature to obtain a solid eutectic. Subsequently, 0.4 g of this eutectic mixture and 20 mg of ITZ were dissolved in 2 mL dichloromethane (DCM). Separately, 0.8 g dopamine (DA) was dissolved in 40 mL Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.5), and 0.6 g sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) was added as a stabilizer. A portion (1.6 mL) of the eutectic–ITZ solution in DCM was then added to the DA/SDS aqueous solution by shaking at 100 Hz for 30 min. LIG+ films were immersed in the resulting solution for 24 h, with gentle shaking every 6 h to ensure uniform exposure. The samples were then rinsed with deionized water to remove free components and dried at 60°C, yielding LIG+ films loaded with ITZ.

Polyacrylamide/chitosan (PAAm/CS) hydrogel was synthesized by UV-initiated free radical polymerization. A precursor solution was prepared by mixing 1.76 g acrylamide, 8.8 mg PEGDA crosslinker, and 17.6 mg Irgacure 2959 photoinitiator into 10 mL of 1 wt% chitosan in 0.2 M acetic acid, followed by sonication in an ice bath for 30 min until homogeneous. The precursor solution was then cast directly onto the surface of LIG+ITZ on PI substrates placed at the bottom of a glass mold, with thickness adjusted by varying the solution volume. The assembly was exposed to 365 nm UV light for 30 min to achieve gelation. After curing, the hydrogel–LIG+ITZ–PI composite was demolded and placed at −18°C for 3 h. The PI substrate was then carefully peeled away, leaving the LIG+ITZ layer adhered to the surface of the PAAm/CS hydrogel. The resulting LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel was rinsed with PBS or deionized water and dried at room temperature. The LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel recommended storage condition is at 4°C in a controlled environment with approximately 52% relative humidity.

Cells and fungus strains and growth conditions

T. rubrum microconidia were grown in PDB culture medium at 28°C for at least 10 days. Subsequently, a sterile cotton tip applicator was used to transfer the microconidia from the surface of the hyphal colonies into sterile PBS. The microconidia were separated by filtration of the suspension through sterile cotton gauze and diluted to a density of 1 × 108 conidia mL−1 in PBS with a haemocytometer.

For biofilm culturing, the T. rubrum was grown on PDB and incubated at 28°C for seven days or until sporulation. The inoculum was prepared by covering the cultures with 5 ml of PBS, and adjusting to a final concentration of 1 × 108 conidia mL−1. Then, 1000 μL of inoculum was added to 24-well plates containing previously sterilized coverslips. The plates were then incubated without agitation at 37°C for 3 h for biofilm pre-adhesion. After this time, the supernatant was gently removed from the wells, the cells were washed three times with PBS for removal of non-adherent cells, 1000 μL of RPMI 1640 medium was added and the plates were incubated at 37°C for 72 h.

Human embryonic lung fibroblasts (MRC-5 cells) were obtained from the China Center for Type Culture Collection and cultured in MEM supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution (penicillin and streptomycin) in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator at 37°C.

To study the adhesion ability of hydrogel and LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel, a series of external stresses was introduced, including stretching, distorting, bending, washing and turning. Then, adhesive ability of the hydrogel and LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel on fresh porcine skin (bonding area, 10 × 40 mm) was evaluated.

Construction of the mouse tinea pedis model and treatment

All animal procedures were carried out under the guidelines set by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Hubei Province, and the overall project protocols were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Wuhan University (NO. WP20220020).

12-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were used for mouse models of tinea pedis. T. rubrum inoculation was performed on the hind feet of the mouse. Inject 20 μL of the conidial suspension (approximately 1.0 × 108 CFU mL−1) into the hind foot of the mouse according to the previous method. Moisten a portion of gauze with 500 μL of the conidial suspension and fix it on the mouse's hind foot with adhesive tape to infect the mouse. Seven days post-infection, remove the adhesive tape, cease the injection of T. rubrum suspension, and commence different treatment. The mice with tinea pedis were randomly divided into four groups (n = 8): 1) no T. rubrum infection or treatment (Control); 2) no treatment after successful modeling (Without Treatment); 3) affix a 5 mm × 8 mm piece of LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel to the sole of the mouse's foot and subject it to sunlight exposure three times a week for 30 min each session. (With Treatment); 4) apply ITZ ointment to the soles of the mice's feet three times a week (ITZ).

Behavioral testing in tinea pedis animals

The mice were allowed to freely explore their surroundings. A video camera placed above the OFT arena was used to record the entire OFT trial. Individual mice were transferred to the OFT arena and placed at the estimated central point. After an acclimation period (2 min), behavior was recorded for 5 min. After each test, the apparatus was cleaned with 75% ethanol to remove olfactory cues. Four behavioral traits were measured (distance moved, velocity, mobility state and distance moved in center). The recorded videos were stored on a computer, and behavior was analyzed using EthoVision XT software (Noldus Information Technology, the Netherlands).

Gait analysis: Gait properties were assessed by footprint analysis as described previously. Briefly, fore- and hind paws were colored with non-toxic dye and the animals allowed crossing a white sheet of paper, 70 cm in length, in a straight line. Stride, sway, and stance length, as well as paw distance.

The red blood cells were collected by centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 10 min and then resuspended in PBS (20%, v/v). 50 μL red blood cell suspension was mixed with LIG@PCS hydrogel, LIG+@PCS hydrogel, LIG+ITZ@PCS hydrogel and ITZ. Each mixture was incubated at 37°C for 2 h. After centrifugation at 2000 rpm for 10 min, the OD540 of the supernatant was measured with a microplate reader. Long-term toxicity study

Soles from male C57BL/6 mice (12 weeks, n = 8) were treated with LIG+@PCS hydrogel or PBS. Euthanasia was performed after 30 days. Blood samples were collected for hematological and clinical biochemical analyses. Moreover, the soles were removed from each group. Paraffin sections of these organs were prepared and subjected to H&E staining for histological examination.

Statistical methods

All the experiments in this study were performed with at least three replicates. All the data were statistically analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.5 statistical software. The 2D images and fluorescence intensities were processed or analyzed by ImageJ. The 3D images were processed using NIS-Elements AR software (Nikon) and COMSTAT 2.0. All the data are presented as the means±standard deviations (SDs), except where indicated otherwise. The statistical significance of differences was determined by an unpaired t-test or analysis of variance (ANOVA) using GraphPad Prism 9.5 software and indicated as follows: p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), p < 0.001 (***), p < 0.0001 (****) and n.s. = not significant.
