Studying the Natural History of the Knee Joint
Authors: Kellen L. Mulford, Marisa Ulrich, Sami Saniei, Cody C. Wyles
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
By the time the orthopaedic surgeon sees a patient with symptomatic knee osteoarthritis, the disease has already progressed to an advanced stage. As a result, studying the full natural history of the knee is especially difficult. A few large longitudinal cohorts, the Osteoarthritis Initiative and Multicenter Osteoarthritis Study in particular, provide the bulk of the data for most extant studies. In this review, we report on the current state of knowledge regarding the study of the natural history of the knee, including the difficulties of natural history research, the tools used to assess knee osteoarthritis, the major longitudinal studies in knee osteoarthritis, and the ability of early interventions to modify natural history. In addition, we will preview what is possible using artificial intelligence and newer large longitudinal cohorts.

Osteoarthritis of the knee (KOA) is among the most common hallmarks of aging. Commensurate with an aging population, the volume of total knee arthroplasty (TKA) in the United States is projected to surpass one million procedures per year.1 Although TKA is known to be a safe and effective procedure, it presents a substantial burden both for the patient and for the healthcare system.2,3 Preservation procedures and nonsurgical interventions may alter the natural history of the knee joint, prolonging the time a patient has with a functional joint before definitive treatment with arthroplasty to lessen pain and restore function. Because the processes resulting in end-stage KOA occur over a long time span, robust research on the natural history of the knee joint is exceptionally difficult. In this narrative review, we identified many of the major longitudinal studies in KOA research. Using these landmark studies, we will explore how researchers have studied the natural history of the knee joint by examining the tools, metrics, and populations that make up the landmark studies in the field. We will conclude with a discussion on the future of longitudinal KOA research, and what kind of studies are possible through innovative thinking and careful planning.

Current Understanding of Knee Osteoarthritis

The etiology of KOA is complex, and the factors contributing to its progression are myriad. Figure 1 displays some of the known risk factors associated with KOA. Heritability of KOA is known to be 40%, with increasing quantities of genetic loci identified as contributory.4 The genetic milieu is further complicated with the addition of systemic comorbidities such as diabetes and inflammatory arthritides which markedly increase risk of early arthritis development.5 The presence of congenital or acquired abnormalities such as discoid meniscus or trochlear dysplasia has also been implicated in premature KOA. Trauma similarly contributes markedly, with more than half of patients developing posttraumatic arthritis after ACL rupture alone.6 These genetic and comorbid conditions potentiate the known effects of aging, characterized by cellular senescence and increased sensitivity to oxidative damage. This complexity, combined with the long time horizon of disease progression complicates high-quality study of the natural history of the knee.

Longitudinal Assessments of Knee Osteoarthritis

One of the reasons KOA is difficult to study is that because the presentation of KOA is quite variable, the methods for assessing it are as well. Most assessments fall into one of the three categories: (1) patient-reported functional scores, (2) practitioner-assessed categorical characterizations, and 3. quantitative measurements. We will discuss each in turn.

Patient-reported Functional Scores

Patient-reported functional scores can perhaps provide the most complete assessment of a patient's lifestyle effect from KOA. These instruments are designed to assess pain, disability, function, sleep, work, mobility, and other facets of a patient's life that may be affected by KOA.7 The Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) is one of the most widely used tools for KOA assessment, with measures for pain, stiffness, and function as well as questions regarding specific activities.8 Other scoring systems, such as the Intermittent and Constant Osteoarthritis Pain Measure (ICOAP), further characterize the pain experience on mood, sleep, and quality of life independent of physical function.9 Both of these systems exhibit excellent reliability and validity in capturing the clinical status of the patient. Other validated measures include the Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS), the Oxford Knee Score (OKS), and Knee Society Scores (KSS), which have wide adoption and acceptance. These are often complemented by the more global assessment of a patient by the Patient-reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) measures. These tools are helpful for monitoring postoperative progress, capturing the patient experience, and facilitating quality benchmarking standards. However, their widespread use is complicated by administrative burdens, uncertain clinical significance thresholds, and measurement difficulties that inhibit optimal data collection and evaluation.

Practitioner-assessed Categorical Characterizations

Kellgren-Lawrence System: The Kellgren-Lawrence (KL) system was proposed in 1957 based on the examination of radiographs of 11 joints from 85 patients.10 The KL system ranks the severity of radiographic OA on a 5-point scale from zero (no OA) to 4 (severe OA). While not specific to the knee, countless knee studies have since relied on the KL system. Subjective categorization schemes have the benefit of simplicity, yet the downsides are substantial. Practitioners determine KL grades through a qualitative examination, which introduces interobserver variability. There are many studies on the interobserver reliability of KL grades, and one can find studies reporting agreement ranging from poor to excellent.11,12 Nonetheless, owing to its simplicity and convenience, KL grades have achieved widespread usage, both in clinical practice and in longitudinal studies. Recently, many groups have published artificial intelligence algorithms for automatically determining KL grade from radiographs.13,14 Brejnbøl et al15 found that junior clinicians using AI assistance to determine KL grade performed more consistently in line with senior radiologists than without. However, since the underlying ground-truth labels for these algorithms are subject to substantial interobserver variability, the utility of these algorithms is questionable.

Although Kellgren-Lawrence is the most widely used system, it is not the only one. The International Knee Documentation Committee (IKDC), Fairbank, and Ahlbäck classifications offer alternatives. The Ahlbäck16 classification comes from a 1968 study and uses six grades, focusing on joint space narrowing and increasing amounts of bone defect. The IKDC is a four-grade system focused almost exclusively on varying amounts of joint space narrowing.17 The Fairbank18 system, from 1948, assesses the presence of four characteristics: spurring of the tibial spines, osteophytes, femur/tibia flattening, and joint space narrowing. Eckersley et al19 reported higher interobserver reliability for the joint space focused IKDC and Ahlbäck systems compared with the KL and Fairbank assessments.

Quantitative Measurements

The severity of KOA exists on a continuum, and so any categorical representation of severity discards within-group information. The most severe KL grade 3 can look quite different than the least severe KL grade 3. Quantitative measurements attempt to retain that information by assessing KOA on a continuous scale. One difficulty with quantitative measurement is choosing an assessment, or group of assessments, that accurately model the severity of KOA. One radiographic quantitative continuous variable assessment is minimum joint space width (mJSW), which is a surrogate marker for cartilage degeneration. Often acquired in both the medial and lateral compartments, mJSW has the benefit of being relatively quick to measure, and the Japan-based ROAD study found that patient-reported pain was markedly associated with medial mJSW and that the observed effect was mediated by the body mass index.20 Although no meta-analysis comparison between the reliabilities of mJSW and KL grade exists, interobserver reliability of mJSW measurements are usually high.21 Reliability in quantitative radiographic measurements can also be affected by radiographic technique, positioning, and implant.

Quantitative measurements can usually be defined in multiple ways. Cheung et al22 found that using several joint space measurements along the knee joint, which they called multiple joint space, resulted in higher predictive performance when used to predict KOA progression. Other measurements may also serve as surrogates for KOA progression. Hip-knee-ankle angle may become more varus or valgus as joint degeneration progresses if that degeneration primarily occurs in either the medial or lateral compartment. The CHECK study, described below, also included osteophyte area, subchondral bone density, joint angle, and tibial eminence height in addition to mJSW in their analysis.23

Studies From the Osteoarthritis Initiative and the MOST

The two largest and most comprehensive longitudinal observational cohort studies for KOA are the Osteoarthritis Initiative (OAI) and the Multicenter Osteoarthritis Study (MOST). Both studies recruited thousands of participants who were followed for at least 7 years with a battery of clinical, laboratory, and imaging assessments. Both studies have resulted in hundreds of publications and insights into the exacerbating factors for KOA.

The Osteoarthritis Initiative (OAI) was a longitudinal study primarily examining KOA.24 Roughly 4800 patients with or at risk for KOA were followed for 10 years with assessments every 12 months, including radiography and MRI at six of those follow-ups. Patients were collected into three cohorts: (1) a progression cohort of patients with existing KOA symptoms (N = 1389), (2) an incidence cohort of participants at higher risk for developing KOA but without existing symptoms (N = 3285), and (3) a control cohort of participants without symptoms or risk factors (N = 122). Data collected for each participant included blood; urine; standard clinical assessments; radiography including knee, hip, hand, and full lower-limb studies; knee and thigh MRI; and DEXA. Data from the OAI are publicly available and have been used in hundreds of publications to date.

Two recent studies using OAI data used interesting trajectory-analysis techniques. Collins et al25 used a clustering method called latent class growth analysis on the longitudinal mJSW measurements from OAI to group participants into trajectories of disease progression. They identified three subgroups: participants with no joint space narrowing (86% of participants), those with stable mJSW followed by delayed joint space narrowing (6%), and those with early joint space narrowing (8%). In a similar study using OAI data, Chang et al26 constructed trajectories of patient-reported pain scores using the intermittent and constant osteoarthritis pain instrument (ICOAP). They identified four pain trajectories and found that advanced radiographic disease was associated with the more severe pain trajectories.

The Multicenter Osteoarthritis Study (MOST) was a two-part study that looked specifically at Americans with current KOA or who were at higher risk of developing KOA. An initial cohort of 3026 participants was recruited in 2003, and a second cohort of 1525 with mild KOA symptoms and radiographic KOA was recruited between 2016 and 2018.27 All participants were assessed for KOA risk factors, KOA symptoms and severity, measures of physical function and physical activity, and strength, gait, and sensory testing. In addition, the study collected radiography and MRI from each participant at each clinical visit. Clinic visits occurred at 15, 30, 60, and 84 months for the original cohort. Both the original and new cohort were asked to come in for assessments at 144, 152, 160, and 168 months (timed relative to the beginning of the original cohort). Like OAI, this study has resulted in hundreds of downstream analyses.

Longitudinal Studies in Knee OA

CHECK = Cohort Hip and Cohort Knee study, JoCoOA = Johnston County Osteoarthritis project, OAI = Osteoarthritis Initiative, MOST = Multicenter Osteoarthritis Study.

The Johnston County Osteoarthritis Project (JoCoOA) was designed to address gaps in the epidemiologic understanding of hip and knee OA in Black Americans.28 Around 3000 men and women aged 45 years and older were recruited in the early 1990s. An additional 1100 participants were added in the early 2000s. The study collected clinical assessments and radiography in each participant. Knee radiographs were scored according to the KL grade. The study found that Black Americans were 36% more likely to develop KOA than White Americans.29 Similar to other studies discussed in this review, older participants and women were also more likely to develop KOA.

In the Cohort Hip and Cohort Knee (CHECK) study, 1002 Dutch patients aged 45 to 65 years who reported hip or knee pain to their general practitioner were recruited for annual (severe initial symptoms) or semiannual (mild initial symptoms) visits to a research center.30 At each visit, data from self-reported questionnaires, physical examinations, KL grade (with agreement kappa values of 0.60 for knee), and blood and urine samples were acquired from each patient. After 7 years, 897 patients remained. The major strength of the CHECK study is that patients were recruited at the first report of relevant symptoms. Holla et al31 performed a similar trajectory analysis to the two OAI papers described above while using activity limitations (through the WOMAC score) as their primary outcome. They identified three subgroups with increasingly worse activity limitations. Patients in the moderate and poor outcome tracks tended to be younger with higher BMI, more pain, and reduced knee flexion.

The Chingford Women's Study was a 20-year study of 1003 women between the ages of 45 and 64 years. Each participant was assessed at baseline and then every 5 years with clinical examinations, questionnaires, blood tests, and radiologic examinations consisting of bone mineral density scans and radiography. High BMI and older age were associated with the development of KOA, as well as the IL-6 and C-reactive protein blood markers. Leyland et al32 used data from the Chingford Women's Study to assess the cumulative incidence of radiographic KOA. The annual cumulative incidence was 2.3%, and the annual rate of progression was 2.8% in the 561 patients with complete imaging data. They also noted an association between existing radiographic KOA as defined as KL grade of greater than or equal to one at baseline and progression.

The Fels Longitudinal Study is the longest running study on this list. Beginning in 1929, the Fels study has collected data on growth, skeletal maturity, and body composition.33 Interestingly, the structure of the study has allowed for multiple generations of the same nuclear families to be studied. While not specifically focused on KOA, the information collected in Fels participants over their lifetimes has allowed for the study of how developmental and compositional factors early in life may lead to KOA. One such study found a notable genetic effect on joint space width by comparing radiographic data between successive generations.34

The Framingham Knee Osteoarthritis Study was an add-on study to a 35-year cardiac study in a population of adults from Framingham, Massachusetts.35 In addition to the many risk factors for KOA that had been tracked over the course of the cardiac study, knee radiographs and questionnaires were acquired from roughly 1800 remaining participants at the 1983 to 1985 examination (the 18th biennial examination). They found associations between KOA and obesity, prior knee injury, and occupations requiring heavy joint use. They also observed an interesting protective effect of smoking on the development of KOA. On re-examining 1051 elderly patients from the study in 1992 to 1993, the investigators found that women were 1.7 times more likely to develop KOA than men, but that elderly age itself was not predictive of KOA incidence.36

The Matsudai Knee OA Survey is the longest running study focusing specifically on KOA on this list, but also one of the smallest. The study group followed 78 knees from 39 patients for 40 years. At the end of the study, the patient ages ranged from 80 to 94 years old. All patients exhibited some form of radiologic KOA at the final follow-up, with 84.9% experiencing joint space narrowing.37

Difficulties in Knee Natural History Research

There are two main difficulties in modern knee natural history research: (1) the long time horizon of KOA progression and (2) the variety of factors contributing to KOA progression. First, the long time horizon of KOA progression inhibits research because for a study to adequately cover the complete natural history of the joint it must span decades. As a result, most longitudinal studies either focus on specific at-risk patient populations (as in OAI, MOST, CHECK, etc), or KOA is a secondary outcome for the study (as in the Fels longitudinal study). Second, there are a large number of potential factors that can be studied, and including all of them would be cost-prohibitive for most studies or too onerous for participants.

Future Directions

We see three main areas of growth for natural history research in orthopaedics. First, the utilization of existing data acquired in the course of clinical care. Second, the widening of data collected in ongoing and future studies through automation. Third, the study of how the natural history of the knee can be altered through intervention.

There are millions of people in the United States alone who have undergone TKA whose data could be analyzed both in the replaced knee and in the contralateral side. The contralateral knee is an intriguing target for study because most patients will adhere to a regular postoperative follow-up schedule for their knee, providing a study-like accumulation of radiographic data. The downside of this is there may be a correlation between ipsilateral KOA and the development and progression of KOA in the contralateral knee that would be difficult to correct for.38

One substantial difficulty in determining the breadth of data collected in a longitudinal study is the manual labor involved in curating that data. Each additional measurement required on an imaging study increases the amount of labor that must be spent annotating data. The rapid adoption of artificial intelligence (AI) for radiographic interpretation has led to many published algorithms that can automate measurements previously performed by hand, including mJSW of the knee.39 The algorithms could be used to retrospectively analyze existing data sets of images (especially in cases where the original primary outcome was KL grade) and applied prospectively to increase the breadth of measurements acquired. A single well-trained deep learning model with appropriate postprocessing steps can produce (1) categorical classifications, (2) continuous measurements, and (3) abstract features that may be even more predictive than outputs 1 and 2 (Figure 2). Furthermore, speech-to-text and chatbot utilities could be used to increase the efficiency of study follow-up procedures. In a similar fashion, new techniques in bioinformatics, particularly in next-generation sequencing and wearable sensors, could expand the breadth of data collected in a study. There are several important considerations when implementing AI in research related to model overfitting, validation strategy, and model bias that must be considered as well.

A relatively unexplored area in knee natural history research is the study of how different interventions delay the onset of end-stage KOA. These interventions comprise surgical options, such as osteotomies and unicompartmental arthroplasties, and nonsurgical options, such as lifestyle changes and injections. Studying these is a more difficult research problem than studying unaltered natural histories because it requires a population followed for a long time, a portion of which underwent the intervention and a portion that did not (Figure 3). Furthermore, the development and adoption of new preservation techniques have limited the time horizon that can be studied. One systematic review of high tibial osteotomies suggests that the procedure can delay arthroplasty by up to 15 years.40 However, the overall level of evidence was low due to lack of appropriate studies. Furthermore, unicompartmental arthroplasties have become a more commonly applied technique to medial-compartment KOA. Much of the research in this area takes the form of prospective randomized trials—which are expensive. This is an area where the reanalysis of existing patient data with follow-up information could yield new evidence about the effectiveness of surgical and nonsurgical interventions.

Conclusion

In this review article, we outlined the difficulties in knee natural history research, including the long time horizon of disease development and the complex and indeterminate etiologies involved in KOA. We then discussed the ways in which KOA is assessed and many of the large longitudinal studies that have examined the natural history of the knee joint—including some novel techniques for shape analysis and trajectory clustering. Finally, we discussed techniques that could enhance natural history research for future efforts, from adoption of AI to new retrospective analyses of existing data. We believe that the era of big data and automation we are entering will provide new opportunities to study the natural history of the knee joint in meaningful ways.
