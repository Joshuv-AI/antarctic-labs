ABSTRACT
Regional impacts from sea ice loss can be challenging to separate from internal climate variability,
potentially requiring thousands of ensemble members. East Asian wintertime cooling has been
linked to sea ice loss from present day conditions in the Polar Amplification Model Intercomparison
Project with these large ensemble counts. This cooling is theorized to arise from a strengthened
Siberian High and East Asian Jet response. The strengthened Siberian High can be detected with
one fifth the ensemble members needed for the East Asian wintertime cooling in a single model.
We thus hypothesize that leveraging relationships between multiple variables in a conditional
pathways-based approach would reduce the number of required ensemble members to conclusively
attribute East Asian wintertime cooling to future sea ice concentrations. In all analyzed cases,
confidence was increased when evaluating sea ice loss’s responsibility for the joint effects of East
Asian cooling, East Asian Jet strengthening, and Siberian High strengthening over just East Asian
cooling.
However, we were not able to confidently attribute future East Asian wintertime cooling to sea ice
loss in a single model. We found that significant intra-ensemble variability within single Earth
System Models (ESMs) produced highly uncertain forcing response models upon which attribution
results were undermined.
We were able to show that ensemble mean seasonally averaged metrics from multiple ESMs greatly
improved the accuracy of the forcing response linear models and exposed the necessity of all three
steps in the pathway (sea ice area, Siberian High pressure, and East Asian Jet speed) for accurate
prediction of East Asian wintertime cooling. Although all three steps were necessary, East Asian
wintertime cooling possesses a large dependence on the Siberian High pressure, which weakens
the confidence associated with overall strong joint-attribution comparing present day and future
scenarios. We believe transitioning the pathway nodes to relative changes between the Siberian
High and Aleutian Low as well as between the midlatitude westerlies and subtropical jet in the East
Asianj Jet region may be able to produce significant attribution more fully dependent upon all three
steps. Ultimately, this research demonstrates the simple extensibility of conditional pathways-based
attribution to sea ice loss forcing on the Earth system.

1. INTRODUCTION

The Arctic is warming at four times the global average (Rantanen et al., 2022) and harbors a
majority of potential climate tipping events (Armstrong McKay et al., 2022). A tipping event is
defined as a period when a climate subsystem undergoes significant, large-scale, often irreversible
changes when a critical threshold is passed (Wunderling et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023). Impacts
from a tipping event such as sea ice loss (which has shown more than 75% loss over the satellite era)
are uncertain and difficult to attribute due to internal climate variability. In particular, midlatitude
weather consequences of Arctic sea ice loss have been studied for a decade, yet these changes
and their drivers are still extensively debated (Ye et al., 2024). Previous research has identified
potential atmospheric drivers of midlatitude changes that include the North Atlantic Oscillation
(NAO), jet stream waviness/strength, and the stratospheric polar vortex (PV) that are affected by
sea ice loss and may contribute to regional climate impacts like Asian wintertime cooling. Recent
studies suggest that thousands of simulation ensemble members are required to reliably detect these
regional impacts (Ye et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024).

In this project we investigate the application of a novel multi-step and multi-variable attribution
approach to this problem. Our goal is to understand the tractability of our attribution approach for
smaller data sets.

 • QUESTION: Can regional impacts from sea ice loss be found with typical ensemble sizes?

 • HYPOTHESIS: We can reduce the number of required ensemble members by leveraging
 relationships between multiple variables in a pathways-based attribution approach.

 • SUCCESS: Achieving attribution with half the ensemble members as Ye et al. (2024).

The conditional pathways-based attribution approach was developed under the LDRD Grand Chal-
lenge Project CLDERA and leverages relationships between variables in a conditional framework
to increase confidence in attribution of climate impacts (Wentland et al., 2025). A connected series
of climate consequences, which can be treated as intermediate steps of a causal pathway, is used to
characterize the forcing impact.

For the Arctic sea ice loss application, we utilized openly available simulations ensembles with
three sea ice concentration states (pre-industrial control, present day, and future) from the Polar
Amplification Model Intercomparison Project (PAMIP) (Smith et al., 2019). Utilizing this data, we
developed a model of the relationships between variables and forcing magnitude for the connected
series of climate consequences. In particular, we used metrics associated with the following
drivers and impacts identified in a 2,200 ensemble member simulation by Ye et al. (2024): sea ice
loss leading to a strengthening of the Siberian High (aspect of NAO), an equatorward shift and
strengthening of the East Asian Jet Stream, resulting in a detectable cooling of wintertime Asian

temperatures. This initial application of the conditional pathways-based approach uses scalar
metrics and a linear model of the relationships.

1.0.1. Background

Arctic sea ice loss could directly affect lower latitudes through southward advection of warmer
Arctic air, or it could result in large-scale circulation changes with indirect impacts on lower
latitudes (Screen et al., 2018). These large-scale circulation changes and the mechanisms by which
they arise have been identified as the genesis of the much contested winter-cooling in midlatitudes,
as demonstrated by an intercomparison project (PAMIP (Smith et al., 2019)) and much research
(Screen, 2017; Screen et al., 2018; Chripko et al., 2021; Smith et al., 2022; Ye et al., 2024).
This research indicates that Arctic sea ice loss produces a decrease and equatorward shift of the
midlatitude westerly winds accompanied with a strengthening of the subtropical jet stream, an
intensification of the Siberian high and Aleutian low, and a weakening of the Icelandic low (Screen,
2017; Screen et al., 2018; Chripko et al., 2021; Smith et al., 2022; Ye et al., 2024). The proposed
mechanism for a decrease in midlatitude westerlies includes first a reduction in the meridional
temperature gradient, consistent with an increase of the polar cap geopotential height, that would
slow down the polar jet stream situated in midlatitudes (∼55–65°N), consistent with thermal wind
balance (Screen, 2017; Smith et al., 2022). This Arctic surface warming is a direct effect of heating
of the atmosphere, consistent with the loss of sea ice (Smith et al., 2022). Smith et al. (2022)
additionally found dynamical responses to be significant in the warming signature by observing
that the warming in the lower stratosphere could not be from direct energy transport from the
surface.
These midlatitude westerlies are associated with the polar jet, which forms at the intersection of
the Polar and Ferrel Cells around 60° latitude. Atmospheric jets are positioned near the top of the
troposphere (200–300 hPa) and are coherent air currents that simultaneously embody large-scale
circulation characteristics and synoptic-scale weather (Christenson et al., 2017). The polar jet is an
eddy-driven jet arising from midlatitude storms that derive their energy from the strong meridional
temperature gradient in midlatitudes (Liu and Alexander, 2007). Hence as that temperature gradient
decreases, so does the strength of the polar jet, causing this decrease in midlatitude westerlies.
The relationship between the polar and subtropical jets, even in idealized formulations, is nonlinear
and complex (Lee and Kim, 2003). However, a robust weakening and equatorward shift of the
midlatitude westerly winds in response to sea ice loss has consistently been accompanied by a
strengthening of the subtropical jet stream (Screen, 2017; Screen et al., 2018; Chripko et al.,
2021; Smith et al., 2022; Ye et al., 2024). The subtropical jet is driven by angular momentum
transport forced by differential heating in the subtropical to equatorial zone and is thus located at
the intersection of the Ferrel and Hadley Cells (at ∼30° latitude), with a maximum speed at a higher
∼200 hPa pressure.
The wintertime Pacific subtropical jet extends from East Asia to the date line and is a center of
maximum intensity (as well as over the western Atlantic) (Martin, 2021). When there are strong
westerly winds at 200 hPa between 120E–150W, 30N–45N in this wintertime Pacific subtropical
jet, the East Asian Trough (EAT) is mainly oriented N/S with a low tilt axis index (Wang et al.,

 14

2009). Deep EAT’s with N/S orientations often co-occur with strong East Asian Winter Monsoons
(EAWM) as a result of a strengthened Siberian High meeting an Aleutian low (Wang et al., 2009;
He et al., 2017; You et al., 2022). A negative Arctic Oscillation (AO) pattern (the primary mode
of variability in the sea-level pressure range over the entire Northern Hemisphere, i.e. with centers
of action over the Atlantic, Arctic, and Pacific oceans (Thompson and Wallace, 2000)) produces
the needed Aleutian low to create this strong EAWM. Strong EAWM with EAT’s possessing N/S
orientations correlate with negative surface air temperatures in Asia and East Asia (Wang et al.,
2009) thus enabling this wintertime cooling when standard advection may have suggested warming
would be expected.
Indications of this set of dynamical changes hypothesized to result in East Asian wintertime cooling
from increased subtropical jet speeds, to the co-occurring strengthening of the Siberian High and
decreasing Icelandic Low have been identified (Chripko et al., 2021; Smith et al., 2022; Ye et al.,
2024). However, only Ye et al. (2024) has been able to conclusively detect East Asian wintertime
cooling with thousands of ensemble members. The strengthened Siberian High, however, was
detected with one fifth the ensemble members needed for the East Asian wintertime cooling (Ye
et al., 2024). We thus hypothesize that leveraging relationships between these variables (forcing
magnitude, Siberian High, and East Asian Jet speed) in a conditional pathways-based approach
could reduce the number of required ensemble members to conclusively attribute East Asian
wintertime cooling to future sea ice concentrations with less ensemble members than Ye et al.
(2024) possessed.

2. POLAR AMPLIFICATION MODELING INTERCOMPARISON
 PROJECT (PAMIP) DATA

The Polar Amplification Model Intercomparison Project (PAMIP) is an initiative aimed at improving
the understanding of the mechanisms and consequences of polar amplification, whereby regions
of high latitudes experience larger changes in surface temperature due to external radiative forcing
(Smith et al., 2019). To facilitate model intercomparison, a set of PAMIP simulation protocols
have been designed that specify model configuration and external forcing for atmosphere and
coupled Earth system simulations. The majority of the PAMIP experiments are atmosphere-only
with prescribed sea surface temperature (SST) and sea ice concentration (SIC). Multiple centers
from around the world have released simulation data for these experiments for use in comparative
analysis. Based on available open data from the Earth System Grid Federation (ESGF), we utilized
data from nine models listed in Table 2-1.
In our analysis we utilized data from three different atmosphere-only experiments: present day
SST with present data SIC (pdSST-pdSIC), present day SST with pre-industrial control Arctic
SIC (pdSST-piArcSIC), and present day SST with future Arctic SIC (pdSST-futArcSIC). Each
simulation was run for just over one year from 1 April 2000 to 20 May 2001. The simulations
were forced by present-day climatological monthly mean SST and climatological monthly mean
SIC for three configurations: present day, pre-industrial control, and 2-degree warming future
conditions. Initial conditions for the atmosphere simulations were taken from 1 April 2000 from a
corresponding Atmospheric Model Intercomparison Project (AMIP) run for each model.
All models utilized provided surface temperature (tas), sea level pressure (psl, or SLP), and
zonal wind speeds (ua) on a monthly frequency. The number of monthly data ensemble members
associated with each model and variable is outlined in Table 2-2. A subset of these models provided
data at daily temporal resolution, as noted in Table 2-3. The number of available ensemble members
is listed in the table for each of the simulation configurations pdSST-pdSIC, pdSST-piArcSIC, and
pdSST-futArcSIC.

3. DETECTING CHANGES

This section will presents results analyzing the 300 HadGem simulations submitted to PAMIP.
These analyses establish differences in detection between the 2,200 analyzed simulations in Ye
et al. (2024) and those submitted to PAMIP.
Ye et al. (2024) detected significant changes through Student’s 𝑡-tests comparing differences in
gridded December-January-February (DJF) averaged values in particular regions. We calculate
a similar 𝑡-statistic but expand the analysis to present day versus both pre-industrial and future
conditions with the null hypothesis that the pre-industrial and future conditions are indistinguishable
to the present day conditions. We stipple results for which we have 95% confidence that the mean
magnitudes are distinguishable in each grid cell. Similar to Ye et al. (2024), we do not apply a
family-wise error rate or false discovery rate correction which can account for multiple hypotheses.
Approaches such as a Bonferroni correction or the Benjamini–Hochberg correction (Haynes, 2013)
could be applied to the gridded results, and should be considered in future work to account for
spatial correlation. As described more fully below, like Ye et al. (2024) we are able to detect
significant changes in Siberian High and zonal mean zonal wind response in the subtropics and
midlatitudes between present day and future conditions. Although we do detect some grid-points
with significant differences between present day and future surface temperatures, many fewer grid
cells are found which supports Ye et al. (2024)’s analysis to show 1,000 members are needed. We
are unable to detect significant changes between present day and pre-industrial conditions for all
three variables.
We also calculated a similar Kolmogorov-Smirnov (K-S) test statistics to determine distinguishabil-
ity between distributions of DJF daily values for all grid cells in a region. We additionally expanded
the analysis to present day versus both pre-industrial and future conditions. As shown below, we
were able to show that the future distributions are distinguishable from the present day distributions
with 95% confidence for: Siberian High, the East Asian Jet, and the temperature in East Asia and
Asia. We are only able to distinguish the pre-industrial Asia temperature distribution from present
day, for the rest of the variables, the distributions are not distinguishable.

4. FORCING RESPONSE MODELS

To evaluate the distinct conditional probabilities outlined in Figure 1-1, forcing response models
for the various steps in each pathway (Figure 1-1) must be defined. These models are built using
a scalar metric for each pathway element identified in Chapter 3. Although we explored multiple
distinct representations of the 𝐸 𝐴𝐽 we mainly present Ye et al. (2024)’s version in the work below
unless otherwise noted.
Below we present seasonally averaged and cold extreme regressions for both a single model and
multiple models. Intra-ensemble variability from HadGEM, as presented below, was too large
swamping the signal across the experiments. Hence individual models do not appear to possess
sufficient signal-to-noise to establish predictive regressions. We additionally explored the pathways
by focusing only on the coldest days in an attempt to improve the signal-to-noise of the nodes.
However, as presented below, this did not improve the signal-to-noise.
The only analysis yielding regressions with reasonable inferential utility were seasonally averaged
multi-model regressions. However, the high variance and low correlation for all of these regressions
cast significant uncertainty on their predictive power.

4.1. Seasonally averaged forcing response models

4.1.1.      HadGEM

To begin, forcing response models are constructed with scalar metrics from each of the 300
HadGEM ensemble members. Here the scalar metrics are the time average over DJF of the area-
weighted means for each element and region in the pathway. From these time-averaged summaries,
we assess the relationship between the scalar measure of a downstream impact and those of any
variables which are immediately upstream in the proposed pathway, as a result of varying the
forcing magnitude. This upstream-downstream relationship is denoted by a “parent set” function
P (·). For example, the forcing quantity 𝑓 is upstream of the surface temperature 𝑇 in Figure 1-1a,
formalized as P (𝑇) = { 𝑓 }.
Here the intra-ensemble variability of the scalar metrics encodes the internal variability of the
system. The ensemble members capture and represent different climate states evolving over time,
making their variability a good representation of internal variability. To ensure that the intra-
ensemble variability for HadGEM was not unusual, we repeated this analysis (not shown) for the
MIROC and IPSL ensembles and found similarly large intra-ensemble variability.

5. CONDITIONAL PATHWAYS-BASED ATTRIBUTION

Having estimated functional relationships for the multi-model seasonally averaged variables in
our proposed causal pathways (see Figure 1-1), we are now ready to apply these models to the
task of attributing observed impacts to specific forcing levels. The best regressions were obtained
for the multi-model seasonally averaged forcing response models. We thus employed these when
pursuing the conditional pathways-based attribution method developed by Wentland et al. (2025).
We combine the functional forms estimated previously into a single joint likelihood, which we then
use to develop rigorous statistical tests as outlined in Wentland et al. (2025). In order to assess
the effectiveness of our approach, we select the future sea ice condition as the “attribution” forcing
level and use the associated values of 𝑇 𝑂 , 𝐸 𝐴𝐽 𝑂 , and 𝑆𝐻 𝑂 as our “pseudo-observations”. That is,
we will assess whether “observed” future regional surface temperatures, wind speeds, and sea level
pressure can be attributed to future changes in SIA.

The final step to assess attribution involves comparing the likelihoods for each forcing, as computed
from the joint probability above. We propose the null hypotheses that the observed future values
are consistent with either the pre-industrial or present day values and an alternative hypothesis
that the observed values are more consistent with the future values. Through hypothesis testing in
which we establish a statistical test to reject the null hypothesis, we can then attribute the observed
values to the alternative forcing. Following Wentland et al. (2025) we adopt a likelihood ratio
testing framework, as it provides a flexible and intuitive approach to building powerful statistical
tests under arbitrarily complex multivariate pathways.

6. CONCLUSION

In this research we evaluated the extensibility of the conditional pathways-based attribution method
developed under the CLDERA Grand Challenge LDRD and detailed in Wentland et al. (2025). We
found that we were able to develop linear forcing response models of scalar metrics for changing
sea ice concentration, thus demonstrating extensibility in the approach.
The pathway to East Asian wintertime cooling, as laid out by Ye et al. (2024), posits a link from the
extent of sea ice area, through the Siberian High sea level pressure and East Asian Jet speed, and
finally to surface temperatures. Ye et al. (2024) had demonstrated that 1,000 ensemble members
were needed to detect this wintertime cooling in East Asia but only 200 were needed to detect the
changes in the Siberian High. We were thus motivated to understand whether regional consequences
from sea ice loss could be found with typical ensemble sizes.
We had hypothesized that leveraging relationships between multiple variables in a conditional
pathways-based approach would reduce the number of required ensemble members to conclusively
attribute East Asian wintertime cooling to future sea ice concentrations, i.e. that we would be able
to attribute East Asian wintertime cooling with fewer than 1,000 ensemble members. However, we
were not able to attribute East Asian wintertime cooling in this research using the pathway identified
by Ye et al. (2024) with a single model. We found that significant intra-ensemble variability within
single Earth System Models (ESMs) produced highly uncertain forcing response models which
severely undermined attribution results.
We were able to show that ensemble mean seasonally averaged metrics from multiple ESMs greatly
improved the accuracy of the forcing response linear models and exposed the necessity of all three
steps in the pathway (sea ice area, Siberian High pressure, and East Asian Jet speed) for accurate
prediction of East Asian wintertime cooling. Although all three steps were necessary, East Asian
wintertime cooling does possess the largest dependence on Siberian High pressure, which weakens
the confidence associated with overall strong joint-attribution between present day and future.
The inclusion of relationships between variables in the attribution formalism did improve the
outcome. For instance, the certainty that the East Asian temperature in the future was distinct from
the pre-industrial East Asian temperatures increased from ∼95% to ∼99.9% between a single-step
( 𝑓 → 𝑇, Figure 1-1a) and three-step pathway ( 𝑓 → 𝑆𝐻 → 𝐸 𝐴𝐽 → 𝑇, Figure 1-1b); future
East Asian temperatures showed similar improvements in comparison to present day East Asian
temperatures from ∼84% to ∼96% between a single-step and three-step pathway.
Future work should expand our last exploratory work to evaluate different measures of the Siberian
High and East Asian Jet. We believe transitioning the pathway to relative changes between the
Siberian High and Aleutian Low as well as between the midlatitude westerlies and subtropical
jet in the EAJ region may increase the signal in these steps, producing a multivariate regression
model in which each variable is correlated with a significant portion of the variability in 𝑇𝐸 𝐴

 43

(greater than what we see for 𝑆𝐻 or 𝐸 𝐴𝐽 presented herein). Exploratory analysis substituting
𝐸 𝐴𝐽 with the Zonal Wind Response Index (𝑍𝑊 𝑅𝐼) inspired by Smith et al. (2022), but limiting
our zonal average to the East Asian region (120–180°E) reveals a strengthening of the predictive
ability of the regressions and improvement in attribution confidence. Attribution of future East
Asian temperatures in comparison to present day East Asian temperatures improves from ∼84% to
∼99.6% confidence between a single-step and three-step 𝑍𝑊 𝑅𝐼 𝐸 𝐴𝐽 pathway. Research to further
alter the Siberian High step in order to identify even more conclusive attribution statements should
be pursued.
