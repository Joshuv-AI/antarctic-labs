Controlling Radiative Phonons in the van der Waals Ferroelectric NbOI2
Authors: Baolong Zhang, Ruihuan Duan, Sobhan Subhra Mishra, Thomas Tan Caiwei, Zheng Liu, Ranjan Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe optical properties of quantum systems are fundamentally governed by their electromagnetic environment through the local density of optical states (LDOS), enabling powerful control over atomic, excitonic, and electronic transitions. However, the radiative properties of phonons have remained largely unexplored within this framework of environmental engineering. Here we demonstrate LDOS control of terahertz (THz) phonon emission in the van der Waals ferroelectric NbOI2 using a simple mirror geometry. By modifying the photonic environment, we achieve pronounced and reversible tuning of the phonon Q‐factor, peak frequency, linewidth, and emission intensity, with modulation approaching an order of magnitude. The observed behavior arises from Purcell‐like enhancement and suppression of the phonon radiative rate, revealing a near‐unity radiative quantum efficiency in NbOI2. Our results establish radiative phonons as tunable quasiparticles governed by photonic boundary conditions, extending LDOS engineering from electronic to lattice excitations. This framework enables deterministic control of phonon lifetimes and emission spectra, opening new opportunities for compact tunable THz sources and phonon‐based quantum and photonic technologies.

Introduction

The local density of optical states (LDOS) quantifies the availability of electromagnetic modes at a given point in space and therefore sets the rate at which an excited system relaxes to the ground state through radiative decay [1]. Engineering the LDOS provides a general route to tailor a material's optical response, including emission, reflection, and absorption. This concept has been widely validated across diverse emitters, spanning trapped ions [2, 3], molecules [3, 4], magnons [5, 6], and excitons [7, 8, 9, 10, 11].

A classical demonstration of LDOS manipulation is the Drexhage effect reported in 1970 [3]. When a fluorescing molecule is placed in front of a mirror, its fluorescence lifetime varies periodically with the emitter to mirror separation, with a period of approximately half a wavelength. The mirror partially reflects the spontaneous emission of the emitter back to itself, and the returned field interferes with radiation from the still decaying dipole. This electromagnetic feedback reshapes subsequent emissions into destructive and constructive emission states. Destructive interference reduces the LDOS and prolongs the dipole lifetime, whereas constructive interference increases the LDOS and shortens the lifetime. Mirror‐based LDOS control has since become a standard strategy for manipulating radiative properties in many optical systems. Related feedback and interference ideas have also been explored in acoustic settings, for example, in controlling macroscopic oscillations of a Chinese gong [12]. Despite this broad impact, extending LDOS control to phonon‐based radiation remains largely unexplored. The limited exploration of LDOS control in phononic systems mainly arises from two factors. First, the lack of material platforms exhibiting sufficiently strong phonon radiation has hindered systematic studies and device‐level manipulation of phonon emission. Second, phonons have traditionally been viewed as nonradiative lattice excitations rather than electromagnetic emitters, which has largely excluded them from LDOS‐based control schemes. Compared with exciton‐based systems, LDOS control of phonon radiation is experimentally more challenging because phonons typically operate in the terahertz (THz) frequency range. Excitonic emissions in the visible and infrared regimes can be characterized using mature and highly sensitive optical spectrometers. By contrast, THz spectroscopy generally relies on time‐domain detection techniques based on nonlinear optical processes such as the Pockels effect, which are substantially less sensitive than conventional optical spectrometers. These constraints impose stringent requirements on both the radiation efficiency and coherence of the phonon emitter. Nevertheless, LDOS engineering of THz phonon radiation also offers several important advantages. In particular, the long wavelength of THz radiation, which is typically two orders of magnitude larger than that of visible or infrared light, significantly relaxes the requirements on displacement precision and sample‐mirror parallelism. This feature makes LDOS‐based manipulation in the THz regime experimentally accessible using relatively simple geometries.

Recent discovery of highly efficient THz phonon emission from a new class of van der Waals ferroelectrics, NbOX2 (X = Cl, Br, I), under 800 nm femtosecond optical pumping has opened up a route to tailor radiative properties of phonons [13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. More recent studies further report strong coupling between the THz‐emitting phonon modes and the ferroelectric order, referring to these coupled excitations as ferrons, an electric analog of magnons [18, 19]. Their high efficiency and long‐lived THz radiation make NbOX2 an attractive platform for bringing LDOS concepts into the phonon emission regime. At the same time, conventional methods that are often used to tune phonons, including temperature [13, 15, 20, 21], strain [22], pressure [23], and pump fluence [13], have been reported to have limited influence. For instance, in ultrafast optical pump probe experiments on NbOI2 within 298 to 573 K, the peak frequency shifts by ∼0.025 THz and the linewidth changes by ∼0.02 THz, which is negligible compared to the peak frequency 3.12 THz [20]. In THz emission measurements between 20 and 480 K, the peak frequency changes by only 0.030 THz, while there are insignificant changes to the linewidth and amplitude. Increasing pump fluence further produces minor influence [13]. Other works involving strain tuning also show a similar trend. Even at 0.4% strain, no obvious modification was observed for the phonon mode within the emission window near 104 cm−1 [22]. For pressure control, Raman peak position shifts to a slightly lower wavenumber with a pressure up to 11.2 GPa [23]. Together, these results point to a high phase transition temperature and unusually robust optical properties of NbOI2. Although the coupling to ferroelectric polarization enables in‐plane electric fields to modulate the emission intensity and polarity [13], implementing temperature control, strain or pressure loading, or strong electric fields typically require complex instrumentation. This makes flexible control of the THz phonon emission properties particularly challenging.

Previous work has already shown that THz emission from thin NbOI2 flakes is bidirectional and can be viewed as radiation from a 2D dipole sheet [24]. This picture immediately motivates a simple LDOS engineering approach. A mirror placed on one side can reflect the THz radiation back to the emitter and create controlled electromagnetic feedback and interference. Here, we implement single‐mirror‐based LDOS engineering to control THz phonon emission from NbOI2. This work constitutes the first demonstration that LDOS engineering can be extended to radiation from lattice excitations, rather than being limited to electronic or magnetic emitters. In the following sections, we describe the experimental implementation and quantify the resulting modulation of the emission linewidth, frequency, and intensity.

Results

Illustration of Experimental Schematic and Basic Characterization of the NbOI2

Figure 1a illustrates the experimental setup. A sapphire substrate coated with a 200 nm thick indium tin oxide (ITO) layer, with lateral dimensions of 1 cm × 1 cm and a thickness of 2 mm, serves as a highly reflective mirror in the THz range while remaining highly transparent at optical wavelengths. The THz reflectivity of the ITO‐coated sapphire is characterized in Figures S3 and S4. The NbOI2 sample was exfoliated from a bulk crystal and transferred onto a ∼3‐µm‐thick Kapton film using a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) support tape with a thickness of ∼167 µm. The NbOI2 flake, Kapton film, and PDMS tape form a sandwich structure with NbOI2 flake in the middle. The NbOI2 flake has a thickness of approximately 1 µm, as confirmed by thickness measurements. The results and optical images are shown in Figure S2. This layered NbOI2 structure was positioned parallel to the ITO‐coated mirror. Parallel alignment was achieved by monitoring interference fringes in reflection mode using a continuous‐wave visible laser, a detailed schematic is shown in Figure S1.

A femtosecond laser pulse centered at 800 nm was incident from the backside of the ITO‐coated sapphire at normal incidence and illuminated the NbOI2 flake. Upon excitation, the NbOI2 flake emitted THz radiation in both forward and backward directions [24]. The ITO‐coated mirror was mounted on an electrically controlled translation stage, enabling precise adjustment of the separation distance z between the mirror and the NbOI2 flake. The initial effective separation distance was determined by measuring the time delay Δt between the forward‐emitted and reflected THz pulses, following the relation z=Δt×c2, where c is the velocity of light in vacuum. The uncertainty of δ z 0 = 6.8 µm is determined by the transform‐limited pulse duration of the probe laser, 45 fs. After determining the initial position, the measurement data were acquired through a continuous, unidirectional scan of the mirror position, which avoids uncertainties associated with bidirectional repeatability and backlash of the translation stage. For this unidirectional scan, the effective step‐to‐step positioning accuracy is governed by the minimum step size of 0.8 µm, which is less than λP3/100. The pump laser spot size on the NbOI2 flake was approximately 0.5 mm, much larger than λ P3, and the pump fluence was set to ∼1 mJ cm−2. The pump polarization was aligned parallel to the polar axis of the NbOI2 flake along the y‐direction to maximize the THz output. The thin NbOI2 sample, large pump spot, thin and highly transparent spacer layer, and highly reflective ITO mirror are chosen to ensure that the modulation process closely follows the idealized model of a 2D dipole sheet near a mirror. All measurements were performed at room temperature in a dry nitrogen‐purged environment with a relative humidity of approximately 5%.

The NbOI2 crystal has in‐plane dipoles. When such a dipole is placed parallel to a reflective interface, the boundary condition imposed by the mirror creates an inverse image of the dipole with opposite orientation located symmetrically behind the mirror plane, as shown in Figure 1a [1]. As a result, the backward‐emitted THz phonon radiation and the reflected wave interfere to form a standing wave, with the mirror surface corresponding to a node. The Kapton film and PDMS support are highly transparent in the THz range and therefore do not significantly perturb the electromagnetic boundary conditions or the LDOS experienced by the dipole, as confirmed by the transmission measurements shown in Figure S5. The resulting distance‐dependent THz phonon radiation is collected and detected using free‐space electro‐optic sampling, with details of the detection scheme provided in Figure S1.

NbOI2 is a van der Waals ferroelectric material in which the spontaneous polarization arises from the displacement of Nb atoms away from the center of the NbO2X4 octahedra toward one of the bridging oxygen atoms. This displacement generates a spontaneous polarization along the crystallographic b‐axis, as illustrated in Figure 1b [21]. Besides, NbOI2 exhibits two pronounced Raman‐active modes at wavenumbers 104.10 cm−1 and 144.21 cm−1, corresponding to frequencies of 3.13 THz and 4.33 THz respectively. These values are in good agreement with previous studies of this material [21, 25].

Characterization of THz Phonon Radiation of NbOI2 Without Mirror

We first characterize the THz emission properties of NbOI2 in the absence of ITO mirror. To ensure a sufficiently broad detection bandwidth, a 500 µm thick GaP crystal is employed as the electro‐optic detector. The measured THz time‐domain waveform is shown in Figure 2a. Consistent with previous reports, the signal consists of a dominant main pulse followed by damped, long‐lived oscillations [13, 15]. The corresponding Fourier‐transform spectrum is presented in Figure 2b. Multiple narrow peaks are superimposed on a weak and broadband background extending up to 8 THz, with the full spectrum provided in Figure S6. Four prominent peaks, labeled P1‐P4, are clearly resolved. Among them, the frequencies of P3 and P4 agree well with the Raman‐active phonon modes identified in Raman spectroscopy, indicating that these features originate from infrared‐active phonons. Notably, the P3 mode also shows the highest emission efficiency among the observed phonons and highest signal‐to‐noise ratio. For this reason, we select the P3 phonon as the primary focus of the following analysis. To investigate the temporal evolution of the phonon modes, a short‐time‐window Fourier‐transform analysis was applied to the time‐domain signal in Figure 2a. The resulting phonon dynamics are shown in Figure 2c. The P1 mode exhibits the longest lifetime, followed by P3, P4, and P2, in good agreement with previous studies [13]. In Figure 2d, the emission line of the P3 phonon is analyzed by truncating the main pulse and fitting the residual oscillation with a Lorentzian profile. A peak frequency of 3.125 THz and full width at half maximum (FWHM) of 0.103 THz are obtained. The power spectrum is obtained by taking the squared magnitude of the amplitude spectrum in Figure 2d. A Lorentzian fit yields a FWHM of 0.063 THz, corresponding to a quality factor (Q‐factor) of approximately 50. These parameters serve as references for comparison with the subsequent LDOS‐modulated emission results.

LDOS Modulation of THz Phonon Radiation With a Mirror

When an ITO mirror is placed in close proximity to the NbOI2 sample, as illustrated in Figure 1a, the THz time‐domain signal exhibits a pronounced and periodic modulation as a function of the separation distance, as shown in Figure 3a. For clarity, the absolute amplitude of the time‐domain signal is plotted. The horizontal axis represents the separation distance normalized to the wavelength of the P3 phonon mode, λ P3 = 96 µm. In this representation, positions at integer multiples of half the wavelength, z=n×λP32, correspond to nodes, while positions at z=(n+12)×λP32 correspond to antinodes. A clear modulation of the phonon decay dynamics is observed. Specifically, the lifetime of the P3 phonon mode is significantly prolonged near the node positions and shortened near the antinodes. This behavior is in excellent agreement with the distance‐dependent modulation of the LDOS predicted for an in‐plane dipole near a mirror [1, 3]. The Fourier‐transform spectra obtained after truncating the main emission pulse are shown in Figure 3b, revealing modulation of all phonon modes with different periods. The P3 phonon is the focus of the following analysis because its amplitude exhibits the highest signal‐to‐noise ratio. The modulation of the other phonon modes is discussed in Note 3. Three key characteristics of the P3 phonon mode vary systematically with separation distance: the linewidth, the peak frequency, and the peak amplitude. To quantify these variations, Lorentzian fitting was applied to extract the linewidth γ(z) and the peak frequency at each separation distance, as shown in Figure 3c,d. The LDOS determines the energy decay rate of a radiative mode, rather than the field‐amplitude decay rate. Therefore, the linewidth extracted from the power spectrum is used, which is given by the squared amplitude spectrum. For a Lorentzian oscillator, the power spectrum is intrinsically narrower than the amplitude spectrum, with their FWHM linewidths related by γpower=γamp/3. However, when the linewidth approaches the Fourier resolution imposed by the finite time window, a direct Lorentzian fit to the power spectrum becomes more sensitive to spectral sampling and window‐limited resolution because the power spectrum is narrower. Therefore, we estimate the power‐spectrum linewidth from the fitted amplitude‐spectrum linewidth using γpower=γamp/3. This procedure is expected to provide a more robust estimate of the intrinsic linewidth in the resolution‐limited regime than directly fitting the power spectrum. Figure 3c displays the FWHM power spectrum linewidth together with its normalization to the intrinsic value measured in the absence of the mirror. The measured linewidth modulation closely follows the LDOS prediction. Notably, the intrinsic linewidth of the power spectrum without a mirror, γ0 = 0.063 THz can be broadened to approximately 0.12 THz at the antinode position and compressed to as low as 0.011 THz at the node position. This corresponds to an overall modulation of the linewidth close to an order of magnitude, which also signifies the change of the Q‐factor from 26 to 275, as shown in Figure 3e.

For a 2D dipole sheet, the total linewidth can be described by the following model [7]: (1) γtotzγtot,0=1−η0cos4πzλP3 where γ tot (z) represents the linewidth at a separation z, γ tot,0 is the intrinsic total linewidth in the absence of a mirror. The parameter η0 represents the intrinsic coherent quantum efficiency in vacuum and is defined as η0=γradγrad+γnr+γib, where γ rad , γ nr , γ ib are the radiative, nonradiative and inhomogeneous broadening, respectively. This quantity describes the fraction of energy dissipated through radiation. According to Equation (1), the experimentally observed twofold linewidth broadening relative to the intrinsic linewidth implies a quantum efficiency η0 approaching unity, indicating that the measured linewidth is dominated by radiative decay rather than nonradiative loss channels. We fitted η0 as a free parameter using the full dataset and obtained a η0 = 0.804 ± 0.161. The fitting details are provided in Note S1. Using the fitted value of η0 = 0.804, the calculated linewidth modulation is plotted as the solid blue curve in Figure 3c. We note that the fitting uncertainty is relatively large and that a clear discrepancy remains between the calculated and experimentally achieved maximum linewidth, which can be mainly attributed to the instrument‐limited linewidth floor. In the limit of unity quantum efficiency, the model predicts that the linewidth can be compressed to values approaching zero at the node position. Experimentally, however, the narrowest amplitude spectrum linewidth observed is approximately 0.02 THz. We attribute this difference to two primary factors. First, the radiative decay rate is strongly suppressed near the node position, leading to a substantially reduced emission amplitude that is difficult to detect experimentally. Second, the finite temporal window of the measured time‐domain signal limits the achievable spectral resolution. For example, resolving a linewidth of 0.01 THz requires a time‐domain signal duration of at least 100 ps. The η0 is expected to decrease slightly as the pump fluence increases and to increase with sample thickness, as discussed in detail in Note S2. For these reasons, the Gaussian spatial profile of the pump laser and possible thickness non‐uniformity of the sample may lead to inhomogeneous broadening, which could also partly account for the relatively large fitting uncertainty of η0.

Notably, efficient LDOS modulation persists even when the separation distance increases to approximately twice the emission wavelength. This behavior is enabled by the high transverse coherence of the 2D dipole sheet and is ultimately limited by the finite lifetime of the THz emission. At very large separations, the reflected THz wave can no longer efficiently interfere with the forward‐emitted field. Nevertheless, the ability to achieve strong LDOS modulation over such large distances greatly simplifies device implementation compared with schemes requiring nanometer‐scale control of emitter‐mirror separation [7, 8].

In addition, the peak frequency f 0 of the emission also depends on the mirror position, as shown in Figure 3d. When the reflected THz wave is exactly in phase or out of phase with the forward‐emitted wave, the emission peak remains at its vacuum frequency f 0 = 3.125 THz. Away from these positions, dispersion across the resonance leads to spectrally asymmetric interference, resulting in a shift of the effective line center [7]. In this work, a total peak frequency shift of ∼50 GHz is observed. The observed peak‐frequency modulation can also be rationalized using a Green‐function back‐action model. As detailed in Note S4, the real part of the reflected Green function gives the resonance‐frequency shift, whereas its imaginary part governs the LDOS‐induced linewidth modulation. This model predicts that the peak‐frequency modulation is approximately in quadrature with the linewidth modulation. Using the fitted radiative efficiency η0 = 0.804 and the power‐spectrum Lorentzian FWHM γ0power=0.063 THz, the calculated f peak(d) shows good agreement with the measured distance dependence, with the small residual discrepancy possibly arising from finite spectral resolution.

To further illustrate this effect, three representative time‐domain waveforms and their normalized spectra are compared in Figure 3f and Figure 3g. Clear changes in both the temporal decay and spectral linewidth are observed at different mirror positions. These results demonstrate that LDOS engineering provides a high degree of flexibility in controlling the THz radiation process.

Amplitude Modulation of THz Phonon Radiation With a Mirror

The peak amplitude at the resonance frequency represents the amplitude of the THz field at the peak frequency of the P3 mode and is extracted for each separation distance, as shown in Figure 4a. Notably, the maximum amplitude does not occur at the constructive interference position. Instead, it appears at two mirror positions located on either side of the antinode. A qualitatively similar behavior has been reported in absorption spectra measurements of 2D MoSe2, where it was attributed to the interplay between radiative and nonradiative coupling rates [7]. In that case, excessive radiative coupling at the constructive interference position leads to strong reflection rather than absorption, while maximum absorption occurs at the critical‐coupling positions on either side [7]. Although the observed trends appear similar, the underlying mechanisms are fundamentally different. In the emission case considered here, the total emitted energy is governed by the position‐dependent quantum efficiency η(z)=Γrad(z)Γrad(z)+Γnr, which scale with the radiative decay rate Γ rad . As shown in Figure 4b, the spectrally integrated intensity represents total radiated energy of the P3 phonon and exhibits excellent agreement with the LDOS prediction, with the maximum total energy occurring at the constructive interference position and the minimum at the destructive interference position. By contrast, the reduction of the peak amplitude at the constructive interference position is primarily caused by significant spectral broadening. The enhanced LDOS accelerates the radiative decay process, thereby shortening the lifetime of the emitting mode and redistributing the emission over a broader frequency range. In our experiment, the integrated THz emission reaches a maximum enhancement factor of approximately 2.55 and a minimum of 0.044, corresponding to an overall modulation factor of about 58.

We further examine the modulation of the broadband THz transient pulse under LDOS control. A zoomed‐in view of the time‐domain signal is shown in Figure 4c. The peak‐to‐peak amplitude of this main pulse represents the peak field strength and exhibits a clear periodic enhancement as the separation distance decreases, as summarized in Figure 4d. This modulation follows a trend similar to that of the resonance peak amplitude, with the largest enhancement occurring near the antinode position and the smallest near the node position. Notably, when the separation distance becomes smaller than half the wavelength, the forward‐and backward‐emitted THz waves begin to interfere directly, leading to a pronounced enhancement of up to 1.6 times. A similar enhancement trend has recently been reported for a spintronic THz emitter coupled to a mirror [26].

Conclusion

In conclusion, we demonstrate that LDOS engineering can be extended to the phonon regime. By coupling a thin NbOI2 flake to a single mirror, we realize nearly one order of magnitude modulation of both the linewidth and amplitude of THz emission through simple control of the emitter‐mirror separation. This approach provides direct experimental access to the radiative dynamics of infrared‐active phonons and reveals, for the first time, a high quantum emission efficiency of nearly unity in NbOI2. Beyond spectral control, reducing the mirror separation leads to a pronounced enhancement of the main THz emission pulse, reaching a factor of 1.6. These results establish single‐mirror‐based LDOS engineering as a simple, contact‐free, and highly effective means of manipulating THz emission without altering the intrinsic properties of the material. Compared with conventional strategies that rely on temperature, strain, or pressure to tune phonon behavior, the present method offers large modulation depth and straightforward implementation. More broadly, this work suggests a general route toward controlling the radiative properties of infrared‐active phonons and related quasiparticles using photonic environments. Such LDOS‐based control provides a versatile platform for tunable THz emitters and absorbers and lays the groundwork for extending cavity‐free radiation engineering to a wide range of low‐dimensional and van der Waals materials.

Materials and Methods

Materials Growth

NbOI2 crystals are grown by the chemical vapor transport (CVT) method. The stoichiometric ratio (Nb: O: I = 1: 1: 2) of Nb, Nb2O5 with a halogen source (I2) are sealed in silica tubes under high vacuum (10−2 Pa). The sealed tubes are put in a two‐zone furnace, which is heated to 650°C and 640°C within 10 h, and soaked for 72 h. After 15 days, rectangular crystals (NbOX2) are obtained in the cold zone.
