Revealing Sex Disparities in the Burden of Diarrheal Diseases Among Children Under 5: Global and Asian Trends and Projections, 1990–2050 – A Population‐Based Observational Analysis Using GBD 2021 Data
Authors: Maedeh Amini, Paria Dehesh, Melina Farshbafnadi, Elaheh Dehghani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAlthough the global burden of diarrheal diseases (DDs) among children under 5 years of age has declined substantially over recent decades, sex‐specific disparities and regional heterogeneity across Asia—particularly regarding non‐fatal outcomes—remain insufficiently characterized. This study investigated long‐term global and Asian trends in the burden of DDs by sex and projected future patterns to inform equitable child health strategies.MethodsSex‐disaggregated data on incidence, prevalence, mortality, and disability‐adjusted life years (DALYs) among children under 5 years of age were obtained from the Global Burden of Disease 2021 Study. Temporal trends were evaluated using estimated annual percentage changes. For Asian countries, three‐level hierarchical linear mixed‐effects models were used to identify factors associated with disease burden. In addition, autoregressive integrated moving average models were applied to forecast sex‐specific trends over the next 30 years.ResultsBetween 1990 and 2021, the burden of DDs declined substantially among both males and females across all outcome measures, although males consistently experienced higher DALYs and mortality. Across Asia, considerable regional heterogeneity was observed. The largest declines occurred in Southeast Asia and South Asia, whereas the smallest declines were observed in High‐income Asia Pacific. Incidence and prevalence rates decreased in most regions but plateaued or increased slightly in East Asia and High‐income Asia Pacific. Multilevel analyses demonstrated significantly higher DALYs and mortality among males and revealed strong inverse associations between the Sociodemographic Index and the burden of DDs. Projections to 2050 indicate continued declines globally and across most Asian subregions; however, the greatest absolute burden is expected to remain in South and Southeast Asia, with relatively limited progress projected for Central Asia and High‐income Asia Pacific.ConclusionsDespite substantial progress in reducing the burden of DDs among children under 5 years of age, marked sex disparities and regional inequalities persist across Asia. Targeted, sex‐specific interventions—particularly in South Asia and Southeast Asia—remain essential to accelerate progress and promote equitable child health outcomes.Keywords: burden of disease, child, diarrheal disease, multilevel analysis, projection, sex

Introduction

Childhood diarrhea is defined as the passage of three or more loose or watery stools within a 24‐h period, or a change in stool frequency or consistency that a caregiver considers abnormal [1, 2]. Despite substantial reductions in global under‐5 mortality over recent decades, diarrheal diseases (DDs) remain a major public health concern among children younger than 5 years [3]. In 2021, DDs ranked as the eleventh leading cause of disability‐adjusted life years (DALYs) and the sixth leading cause of death globally [3, 4]. During the same year, DDs accounted for approximately 30.9 million DALYs and 340,429 deaths among children under 5 worldwide. Sex‐disaggregated estimates indicated that males experienced approximately 185,000 deaths (54.5 deaths per 100,000 population), whereas females experienced approximately 155,000 deaths (48.7 deaths per 100,000 population). Compared with 1990, these figures represent reductions of 78.6% and 79.9% in the number of deaths among males and females, respectively, with corresponding declines of 79.9% and 81.0% in mortality rates [3, 5].

Over the past three decades, the burden of DDs has remained disproportionately high in low‐income countries, where limited resources and inadequate healthcare infrastructure continue to hinder effective prevention, diagnosis, and treatment [6]. In these settings, mortality among children under 5 remains nearly 10 times higher than that observed in high‐income countries, highlighting persistent global inequalities in child health [7]. Asia illustrates both remarkable progress and continuing challenges in reducing the burden of childhood DDs [5, 8]. Since 1990, many countries across the region have achieved substantial reductions in disease burden. Southeast Asia (SEA) experienced the greatest decline in DALYs among both females (8.36%) and males (8.29%), whereas East Asia (EA) recorded the largest reductions in mortality among females (11.37%) and males (10.71%) [5, 9]. Similarly, the greatest reductions in incidence were observed in Central Asia (CA), while prevalence declined most markedly in South Asia (SA) and CA [5, 10]. Nevertheless, incidence and prevalence have remained stable or increased slightly in several higher‐income subregions, particularly High‐income Asia Pacific (HIAP) [5, 9, 11].

In addition to biological differences between the sexes, socioeconomic development is an important determinant of the burden of DDs [10, 12]. Countries with higher levels of development, reflected by a higher Sociodemographic Index (SDI), Human Development Index, and related composite indicators, consistently report lower incidence, mortality, and DALY rates, likely owing to improved sanitation, greater access to healthcare, higher educational attainment, and better nutritional status [9, 13]. In contrast, low‐SDI regions continue to face structural barriers that increase the risk of adverse diarrheal outcomes, particularly among young children [8]. Previous studies have also suggested that developmental conditions may interact with sex‐specific patterns: boys may be biologically more susceptible to severe diarrheal outcomes, whereas girls in certain sociocultural settings may experience reduced access to food and healthcare services [5, 14]. Although earlier studies have reported sex‐disaggregated estimates in selected settings, comprehensive analyses across Asian subregions remain limited [5]. Therefore, a better understanding of the combined influence of sex and socioeconomic development is essential for designing equitable and context‐specific public health interventions.

The existing evidence has improved understanding of the burden of DDs in children; however, there remain significant knowledge gaps, especially concerning non‐fatal outcomes and sex‐specific analyses across Asia [8]. Noticeably, recent Global Burden of Disease (GBD) assessments, such as the 2021 iteration, have accounted for sex differences in their estimates for incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs; however, such analyses have not uniformly disaggregated results by Asian subregions nor explored potential interactions by sex, SDI, and time in a formal manner through multilevel modeling approaches [5]. In addition, projections for 2050 that combine long‐term trend analysis with sex and region disaggregation are limited, which hampers foresight of the future burdens to best funnel limited resources for intervention in varied socio‐economic settings [12, 13]. Our study addresses both these gaps and produces findings with novelty, such as slower rates of decline in higher‐SDI quintiles and predicted reversals in regions including CA and HIAP, that contradict notions of universal progress.

Therefore, this study had three primary objectives. First, we described temporal trends in the incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs of DDs among children younger than 5 years worldwide and across the six Asian subregions between 1990 and 2021, with particular emphasis on sex‐specific patterns. Second, we evaluated the effects of calendar year, sex, and SDI on these outcomes in Asian countries using three‐level hierarchical longitudinal models. Third, we projected sex‐specific trends in both rates and absolute numbers through 2050 using autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) models. By integrating descriptive trend analyses, multilevel modeling, and long‐term forecasting, this study provides a comprehensive assessment of historical trends, current disparities, and future trajectories in the burden of childhood DDs globally and across Asia.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This population‐based longitudinal observational study used data from the GBD 2021 Study. The GBD Study provides comprehensive estimates for 369 diseases and injuries across 204 countries and territories from 1990 to 2021 [15]. For DDs, the GBD framework integrates multiple sources of information, including vital registration systems, demographic surveillance systems, verbal autopsy studies, household surveys, and published scientific literature, to generate internally consistent estimates [9]. Uncertainty is quantified throughout the modeling process, and final estimates are reported as 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) based on 1000 posterior draws [16]. The GBD 2021 Study applies a standardized analytical framework that enables comparable, transparent, and reproducible estimation of disease burden trends, thereby supporting interpretation of global health patterns and informing evidence‐based health policy and resource allocation [17].

Data Sources

Sex‐specific estimates of the absolute numbers and rates of incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs attributable to DDs among children under 5 years of age were obtained from the GBD 2021 database [15]. Within the GBD framework, sex refers to biological classification (male or female) and does not represent gender‐related social roles, behaviors, or identities, as such information is not available in the dataset. Data were extracted for all 204 countries and territories worldwide, including 55 Asian countries classified into six geographic regions: North Africa and Middle East (NAME), SEA, SA, EA, CA, and HIAP [9]. DDs were defined according to the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) codes A00–A09 [18]. Estimates were stratified by sex (male, female, and both sexes), geographic region, and SDI quintiles. SDI is a composite indicator based on income per capita, educational attainment, and fertility rate, and was used to facilitate comparisons across different levels of sociodemographic development [19]. DALYs were calculated as the sum of years of life lost due to premature mortality and years lived with disability [20]. The data used in this study were accessed on July 4, 2025. Because this analysis relied exclusively on aggregated and de‐identified secondary data, ethical approval and informed consent were not required. This study was reported in accordance with the STROBE Statement, and the completed STROBE checklist is provided as Supporting Information (Table S1).

Bias

Potential sources of bias in the GBD 2021 estimates include selection bias resulting from differences in data availability across locations, information bias arising from variation in data quality, and residual uncertainty related to model assumptions. Within the GBD framework, these challenges are addressed through standardized data processing procedures, advanced statistical modeling approaches, and validation using multiple independent data sources. Nevertheless, some degree of uncertainty may remain, particularly in settings with limited primary data availability, potential misclassification, or underreporting. These limitations should therefore be considered when interpreting the findings of this study.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics, including means and standard deviations (SDs), were calculated to summarize the incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs rates in 1990 and 2021 by sex and geographic region. Temporal trends during 1990–2021 were assessed by calculating the estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) using a log‐linear regression model, in which the natural logarithm of the rate was regressed against calendar year (y=α+βx+ε), where y = ln(rate), α is the intercept, β is the regression coefficient representing the temporal trend, x denotes calendar year, and ε is the random error term. The EAPC reflects the average annual percentage change in disease burden metrics, with positive values indicating upward trends and negative values indicating downward trends. Corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated from the standard error of the regression coefficient [21].

Sex differences were evaluated by calculating the mean differences between females and males, with corresponding 95% CIs derived from the uncertainty estimates provided by the GBD study. Welch's two‐sample t‐test was used to compare mean values between sexes because it is robust to unequal variances. For each combination of region, health indicator, and year, mean estimates and their associated UIs were obtained directly from the GBD database. Missing data were handled within the GBD 2021 analytical framework; therefore, no additional imputation procedures were performed. All analyses incorporated the reported GBD UIs to appropriately reflect estimation uncertainty. Trends and annualized rates of change were visualized separately by sex, geographic region, and SDI category (low, low‐middle, middle, high‐middle, and high).

To evaluate the associations of sex and SDI with the burden of DDs, hierarchical linear mixed‐effects models were fitted separately for each outcome using the lme4 package in R. Model development followed a prespecified hierarchical strategy. Initially, intercept‐only (null) models were fitted to estimate variance components at the country and regional levels. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were subsequently calculated to quantify clustering and determine whether multilevel modeling was warranted. Because meaningful between‐country and between‐region variation was observed, a three‐level hierarchical model was retained, with repeated annual observations (Level 1) nested within 55 Asian countries (Level 2), which were further nested within six Asian regions (Level 3: NAME, CA, EA, SA, SEA, and HIAP). Fixed effects for calendar year, sex, SDI, and the SDI × time interaction were included, while random intercepts for countries and regions were maintained to preserve model parsimony and interpretability. Future trends from 2022 to 2050 were projected using ARIMA models implemented with the forecast package in R. ARIMA models are widely used for epidemiological forecasting when the primary objective is to extrapolate historical trends rather than estimate causal effects, particularly when long‐term projections of explanatory variables are unavailable [24, 25]. Separate ARIMA models were developed for each health indicator, sex, and geographic unit using annual observations from 1990 to 2021. Stationarity was achieved through differencing when required, and a Box–Cox transformation (λ = 0) was applied to stabilize the variance [26, 27]. The autoregressive (p), differencing (d), and moving‐average (q) parameters were selected automatically using the auto.arima() function based on the corrected Akaike Information Criterion with maximum likelihood estimation. A value of zero for any parameter indicated that the corresponding model component was not included.

Model adequacy was assessed by examining residual autocorrelation functions and conducting Ljung–Box tests to confirm that residuals approximated white noise before forecasting [28, 29]. Sensitivity analyses were performed by fitting alternative ARIMA specifications with similar autoregressive and moving‐average structures. The consistency of forecast trajectories across alternative models supported the robustness of the projections. ARIMA models were selected because they are well‐suited to modeling long‐term temporal patterns in population‐based disease indicators measured at regular annual intervals. Unlike conventional regression models that impose predefined functional relationships, ARIMA models flexibly capture temporal autocorrelation and stochastic variation observed in historical data. Forecasts were generated under the assumption that the temporal patterns observed during 1990–2021—including epidemiological trends and reporting practices—would continue throughout the projection period in the absence of major structural changes or large‐scale interventions. All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.5.1 (https://www.R-project.org/). All statistical tests were two‐sided, and a p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Global and Regional Trends, 1990–2021

Between 1990 and 2021, all four indicators of the burden of DDs among children younger than 5 years—DALYs, mortality, incidence, and prevalence—declined globally (Table 1; Figure 1). The greatest reductions were observed for DALYs and mortality in both females and males throughout the study period. These declines were most pronounced during the early 1990s and gradually slowed thereafter, although downward trends continued through 2021.

Across Asia, all regions experienced declines in DALYs and mortality; however, the magnitude and rate of decline varied considerably (Table 1; Figure 1). SA showed the largest absolute reductions, reflecting its initially high disease burden, whereas the HIAP region experienced comparatively smaller improvements. EA demonstrated the fastest annual declines, particularly in mortality, while incidence and prevalence approached a plateau toward the end of the study period.

Overall, Asian regions experienced substantial reductions in DALYs and mortality, whereas trends in incidence and prevalence were more heterogeneous. HIAP was an exception, showing relatively small reductions in DALYs and mortality, together with minimal changes in incidence. Age‐standardized incidence and prevalence rates declined in nearly all Asian regions, although these decreases were less pronounced than those observed for mortality. Similar temporal patterns were observed in females and males, with only modest differences in the magnitude of change (Figure 1).

Collectively, these findings indicate substantial progress in reducing the fatal and disabling burden of DDs globally and across Asia since 1990. However, reductions in disease prevalence have been slower and more variable, particularly in higher‐income settings.

Female–Male Mean Difference for Each Indicator by Region and Year

Globally, females had significantly lower DALYs and mortality than males in 1990 (both p = 0.002). By 2021, these differences had diminished and were no longer statistically significant. No significant global sex differences were observed for incidence or prevalence in either year.

In the NAME region, females consistently experienced a lower burden of DDs than males. DALYs and mortality were significantly lower among females in 1990 (both p < 0.001). Although these differences became smaller by 2021, they remained statistically significant (DALYs: p = 0.013; mortality: p = 0.007). Incidence did not differ significantly between the sexes in 1990 but was significantly lower among females in 2021 (p = 0.031). No significant sex differences were observed in prevalence in either year.

Large and highly significant sex differences were observed in the SEA region. In both 1990 and 2021, females had significantly lower DALYs and mortality than males (all p < 0.001). In contrast, incidence and prevalence showed changing patterns over time. In 1990, both indicators were lower among females (both p < 0.001); however, by 2021, females had significantly higher incidence (p < 0.001) and prevalence (p = 0.002), suggesting a temporal shift in disease distribution.

In the SA region, the opposite pattern was observed throughout the study period, with females consistently showing higher DALYs and mortality than males (all p < 0.001). Incidence was significantly higher among females in 1990 (p = 0.023) and increased further by 2021 (p < 0.001). Prevalence did not differ significantly between the sexes in 1990 but was significantly higher among females in 2021 (p < 0.001). In contrast, no statistically significant sex differences were observed in the EA region for any indicator in either 1990 or 2021 (all p > 0.05).

In the CA region, no significant sex differences were observed in 1990. By 2021, however, females had significantly higher incidence and prevalence than males (both p < 0.001), whereas DALYs and mortality remained comparable between the sexes. In HIAP, females had significantly lower DALYs, mortality, incidence, and prevalence than males in 1990 (all p < 0.05, most p < 0.001). Although these differences narrowed by 2021, they remained statistically significant for DALYs (p = 0.018), incidence (p = 0.012), and prevalence (p = 0.021), with females continuing to experience a lower disease burden. No significant sex difference in mortality was observed in 2021 (Figure 2).

Discussion

Our analysis demonstrated a substantial decline in the burden of DDs among children under 5 years of age globally between 1990 and 2021. Despite these encouraging improvements, DDs remain an important cause of mortality and disability in this population, underscoring the continued need for effective preventive and therapeutic interventions. It is worth noting that in this study, estimates are stratified by biological sex (male/female), as defined in the GBD framework. Although potential biological and socially mediated mechanisms are discussed to aid interpretation, these factors were not directly measured in the GBD database. Consequently, the observed sex differences should be interpreted as reflecting the combined influence of biological susceptibility and population‐level social processes rather than direct evidence of causal mechanisms.

A substantial regional variation was observed in the sex differences of the burden of DDs. In NAME, SEA, and HIAP, females consistently experienced lower DALYs and mortality burdens. Conversely, SA had consistently higher burden estimates among females for all measures, pointing to persistent sex‐related disparities. CA experienced a nascent female disadvantage in incidence and prevalence by 2021, while EA exhibited no significant sex differentials. At the global level, females experienced lower DALYs and mortality in 1990; however, these gaps closed by 2021, demonstrating convergence across time. These results emphasize the context‐specific impact of sex on the DDs burden, as influenced by regional sociocultural, economic, and health‐system characteristics [30, 31].

Substantial differences between countries and regions were observed throughout Asia. SA and EA experienced the greatest reductions in the burden of DDs among children under 5 years of age, whereas progress was slower in the HIAP region. These regional differences may reflect variations in socioeconomic development, public health policies, healthcare infrastructure, and implementation of preventive interventions. From 1990 to 2021, advances in oral rehydration therapy, zinc supplementation, rotavirus vaccination, nutrition, and sanitation led to significant declines in the burden of disease in SA and EA [9]. For instance, India and Bangladesh have raised rates of exclusive breastfeeding, broadened vitamin A coverage, and curtailed childhood stunting, all critical defenses against diarrheal mortality [32]. Rapid economic development in several countries has also improved access to healthcare, safe drinking water, sanitation, and child nutrition, thereby contributing to the observed reductions in disease burden. In the HIAP region, the slower rate of decline may be due to a low initial mortality rate and shifting health priorities toward non‐communicable diseases and towards a diminished focus on diarrhea‐specific interventions [8]. These findings highlight the need for continued prevention, early diagnosis, and proper management of DDs, even in high‐income countries, if gains in reducing morbidity and mortality among children aged under 5 years are to be sustained. In addition, as the analyses are based on aggregated regional estimates, the findings do not allow inference regarding individual‐level risk or underlying causal mechanisms.

The largest reductions in the DDs burden were observed in the low‐middle, middle, and high‐middle SDI regions; however, the rates of decline in high SDI for both incidence and prevalence were slower or not statistically significant, suggesting a possible plateau in progress. Males had somewhat greater declines in rates than females in all SDI groups. While the burden of DDs is generally higher in lower‐SDI countries, some have experienced rapid declines in it in the face of limited socioeconomic progress. Both Niger and Liberia, for example, have reduced diarrhea mortality substantially with less than average improvement in SDI, indicating that targeted interventions can yield outsized effects. Changes in nutritional status and specifically reductions in childhood underweight and stunting, combined with initiatives such as disease surveillance, health education, oral rehydration therapy, and rotavirus vaccination, have been correlated with decreases seemingly too large to be attributed to SDI trends alone [33, 34]. These observations indicate that targeted public health and nutrition interventions can produce substantial reductions in the burden of DDs that are not fully explained by broader socioeconomic development.

Sex differences in DDs burden remain, with boys bearing a greater burden than girls. Our results align with previous research; however, the mechanisms underlying these differences are likely multifactorial. Biological factors such as increased vulnerability of boys to wasting, stunting, and underweight, may predispose them to diarrheal infection and associated complications [35]. This heightened risk seems to be largely unrelated to socioeconomic/nutritional factors and may signify increased dehydration sensitivity and possibly higher rates of hospitalization after infection among these infants [36, 37]. Results from the Bangladesh study demonstrated that boys were more commonly admitted with diarrhea, whereas girls who were admitted tended to have more severe dehydration at presentation, potentially reflecting sex differences in care‐seeking at the household level rather than at the level of clinical management [38]. Similarly, research in The Gambia suggested that boys may have a greater biological susceptibility to diarrheal pathogens at the community level [39]. Taken together, these findings highlight the importance of taking every step to prevent DDs in children under 5 based on sex‐sensitive policies encompassing biological, social, and clinical aspects. However, these proposed biological and social mechanisms remain speculative because individual‐level clinical, behavioral, and healthcare utilization data are not available within the GBD framework.

A further objective of this study was to examine temporal trends in the main indicators and to project these trends through 2050. Our findings indicated a decreasing trend in all indicators across all regions and in both sexes. Only minor changes were observed in the differences between regions and between sexes. The largest decreases were observed in SA and SEA, whereas in HIAP, prevalence and incidence rates remained relatively stable. Consistent with our findings, Troeger [40] reported a decline in pediatric diarrhea‐related mortality between 1990 and 2016. This reduction in mortality may be attributable to increased use of oral rehydration solutions, the introduction and expansion of rotavirus vaccination programs, and improved access to clean water and sanitation. Although all regions are projected to experience continued declines, important regional differences are expected to persist through 2050. Additionally, these forecasts assume that historical temporal dynamics will continue into the future. Because ARIMA does not include causal determinants (e.g., risk factor trends), the projections should be interpreted as trend continuations under historical conditions rather than as causal forecasts.

Generally, our findings highlight the need for region‐ and sex‐specific interventions. Additional investments in water and sanitation infrastructure, immunization programs, nutrition, and provision of an equitable health system will be necessary, particularly in low‐SDI regions. Continued surveillance, timely diagnosis, and effective management of non‐fatal DD are also essential in higher‐income regions to prevent stagnation or reversal of recent gains.

Because the GBD database classifies individuals according to biological sex (male/female) rather than gender identity or gender‐related social characteristics, the present findings should be interpreted as sex‐specific population estimates. Future studies incorporating individual‐level biological, behavioral, and sociocultural data would help clarify the mechanisms underlying the observed sex differences.

This study has two notable strengths. First, the use of hierarchical modeling enables a more detailed assessment of sex‐specific differences and SDI in relation to disease burden, while accounting for variation at both regional and country levels. Second, the ARIMA model‐based projections provide quantitative predictions with uncertainty, and this allows quantitative assessment to evaluate disease trends in the future. Taken together, these methods offer comprehensive insights into male‐female and regional differences, help identify emerging health challenges, and generate evidence that may inform health policy or long‐term strategic planning.

Nevertheless, this study is not without limitations. First, the analyses utilized the point estimates from GBD 2021 for DDs indicators. Although the GBD framework provides UIs based on posterior simulations, these uncertainties were not propagated within the multilevel regression/time‐series forecasting framework. Consequently, uncertainty surrounding the estimated associations and long‐term projections may be underestimated. Second, ARIMA‐based predictions are predicated on the premise that historical temporal patterns will continue in the future and are not influenced by potential structural change, policy interventions, or unexpected public health shocks. In addition, since uncertainty is expected to grow on the projection horizon, this may limit forecast accuracy for estimates towards 2050. Hence, long‐term projections should therefore be interpreted as indicative of general trends rather than precise forecasts, especially in the case of abrupt changes in the epidemiological patterns or health system conditions. Third, although the SDI was considered a measure of developmental status, other important environmental factors, such as climate change and antimicrobial resistance, were not included due to data constraints. Fourth, differences in risk exposure or access to health services between males and females were not further examined, despite the fact that data were sex‐disaggregated. Lastly, since this work is designed as an ecological study and is dependent on modeled estimates, interpretations of causality of observed associations cannot be drawn.

Conclusion

Using GBD 2021 data, this study demonstrates that the burden of DDs among children under 5 has declined substantially worldwide and across Asia since 1990, with the most rapid reductions observed in EA and middle to high‐middle SDI regions, while SA and SEA continue to bear the highest burden. Sex differences were generally modest at the global level, but there were regional variations, with males typically having higher DALYs and mortality in high‐burden settings. The hierarchical longitudinal mixed‐effects models demonstrated that all diarrheal disease indicators were significantly associated with time, sex, and SDI, and that considerable heterogeneity existed both among countries and regions. Projections through 2050 suggest further overall declines but heterogeneous regional progress with high burden persisting in parts of Asia and stagnation or small increases in incidence and prevalence in some higher‐SDI regions, highlighting the importance of sustained, regionally tailored, and equity‐focused public health interventions to further reduce preventable diarrheal diseases in early childhood.
