Facial Characteristics.?In describing the bones of the
skull the outstanding features of the peculiarities of facial
development have been indicated. In the face, as in the
cranium, there is universal synostosis. The flat vertical forehead
has already been described. The shallowness of the orbits needs
no further mention. The want of depth is not primarily due to
synostosis, though all sutures have been obliterated, but to
yielding of the pars orbitalis of the frontal and the great wing
?f the sphenoid under pressure of the growing brain.
The maxillae are under-developed and they have no sinuses.
The frontal sinuses are merely indicated which accounts for the
smallness of the glabella and of the superciliary arches. These
sinuses bear a functional relation to each other, but in oxycephaly
the absence of frontal sinuses is probably due to intracranial
Pressure preventing their formation, just as it prevents the
formation of diploe, whereas absence of the maxillary sinuses
seems more likely to result from maldevelopment of the palate,
Pressure by the nasal septum, and want of use of the nasal
Passages in respiration. The sphenoidal sinuses are restricted
and shallow. Oxycephalic children are mouth-breathers and
that too interferes with a full development of the accessory
sinuses. The synostosis of the palate may take place early and,
unable to expand laterally, it remains narrow while the alveolar
Processes growing downwards increase its depth. Through
diminution in height of the nasal cavity thus brought about, the
nasal septum in the sagittal plane is compressed vertically and
yields by bending laterally, at the same time bringing about a
deflection of the nose. That the nose is well developed and
stands out prominently in the adult oxycephalic is due to the
growth of the septum which forces itself in the line of least
resistance. In this case the nasal development is not a criterion
?f respiration.
These defects in development are mechanical results which
necessarily follow premature synostosis. The narrow palate
and the small basilar process entail a pharynx of small size.
This leads to difficulty in respiration especially during breast-

If, with Bertolotti,89 we regard oxycephaly as a mere
component of a " pathological cranio-synostosis" group we
maintain a heterogeneous collection of innumerable varieties,
and if from such a confused and varied mass any consistent
type can be separated, surely such separation is desirable. It
is the conformity of its essential characteristics that justifies the
isolation of oxycephaly. True oxycephaly forms a definite
entity in its causation, its course and in its symptoms both
primary and secondary. It may be more or less imitated but
cannot be reproduced by other cranial deformities the more
admissible of which I refer to as false oxycephaly.
False oxycephaly is an example of localised premature
syostosis occasionally congenital. It is a fortuitous production
and is never hereditary. Jt is very unusual to find the deformity
ascribed to an injury, yet Atgier124 has reported a false
oxycephaly in a man aged 22 which gradually followed a crush
to the head between two leaves of a heavy farm door blown
together by the wind. There was a scalp wound and later
bilateral otorrhcea. The skull ultimately came to slope upwards
and backwards and the occiput was flattened and the parietals
appeared to be reduced in volume. There was no exophthalmos
and no defect of vision and the author ascribes the deformity to
premature synostosis consecutive to the injury.
The species of false oxycephaly are varieties of the two
types which I have described, in one of which the skull runs to
height anteriorly, in the other, posteriorly. Height makes the
skull hypsocephalic but it does not constitute oxycephaly; and
Schiiller90 records that the highest grade of high head which
he has observed is of a skull in the Pathological Museum in
Vienna in which the only suture obliterated was the sagittal.
Bertolotti 91 figures in right lateral orientation as an oxycephalic
skull, the counterpart of my second type of false oxycephaly,
and in it as in mine the coronal and sagittal sutures are
obliterated but the lambdoid, the squamous, and the facial

full powers of sight. Mental capacity in true oxycephaly
varies through a comparatively slight range.
In false oxycephaly there are met with all extremes of
mentality from idiocy or insanity to the highest genius.

convolutions were found imperfectly developed. The cranial
capacity was small but the head as voluminous as that of an
adult on account of the great thickness of the skull-cap. In
spite of their thickness the bones were light and very porous
and the hyperostosis was due almost entirely to excess of the
diploe. These bony conditions are incompatible with true
oxycephaly but characteristic enough of inherited syphilis.
The other case is that of Bourneville and Paul-Boncour110 and
often referred to as the first case of oxycephaly submitted to
autopsy. The patient was a mentally deficient epileptic who
died at the age of 33 years. It was observed at birth that he
had no forehead, that the head seemed compressed laterally and
that his eyes were prominent. Post-mortem the calvarium
was found compact and hard, asymmetrical, and its sutures
synostosed; the parietal foramina were unusually large; the
anterior cerebral lobes were separated by an osseous crest rising
from the crista galli; the frontal lobes of the brain were
undeveloped so that the anterior extremities of the temporo-
sphenoidal lobes projected beneath them. The skull is described
as trigonocephalic and elevated posteriorly into acrocephaly.
No mention is made of the basilar sutures, the palate, or of the
facial bones. A condition assuredly with no relation whatever
to true oxycephaly.
