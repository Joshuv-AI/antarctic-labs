On the Lévy concentration function of Gaussian quadratic forms with applications to second order U-statistics
Abhimanyu Choudhary, Arun Kumar Kuchibhotla

Abstract
We provide an upper-bound for the Lévy concentration function: Q S ​ ( ε ) := sup x ∈ ℝ ℙ ⁡ ( x < S ≤ x + ε ) Q_{S}(\varepsilon):=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}(x<S\leq x+\varepsilon) where S S is a weighted sum of noncentral chi-square random variables: S := ∑ k = 1 ∞ λ k ​ ( Z k 2 − 1 ) + μ k ​ Z k S:=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}(Z_{k}^{2}-1)+\mu_{k}Z_{k} Here, Z 1 , ⋯ , Z n Z_{1},\cdots,Z_{n} are independent standard Gaussian random variables and { λ k } k = 1 ∞ , { μ k } k = 1 ∞ \{\lambda_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty},\{\mu_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty} are real valued, square summable sequences. Random variables of this type often appear as limiting distributions of second order U U -statistics. Our bound is adaptive, in that it recovers (up to constant factors) Gaussian type concentration function estimates if ‖ λ ‖ 2 \|\lambda\|_{2} is negligible compared to ‖ μ ‖ 2 \left\|\mu\right\|_{2} and chi-square estimates if ‖ μ ‖ 2 \left\|\mu\right\|_{2} is negligible compared to ‖ λ ‖ 2 \left\|\lambda\right\|_{2} . Our bound generalizes existing bounds in various ways. In particular, we make no assumptions regarding the number of nonzero | λ k | |\lambda_{k}| or the size of the minimal | λ k | |\lambda_{k}| , nor do we make any assumptions on the signs of λ k \lambda_{k} . Finally, we apply our bound to some examples of interest, specifically quadratic forms that arise in limit theorems for second-order U-statistics.



1
Introduction

Given independent and identically distributed random variables
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
taking values in some measurable space
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A})
and a measurable function
h:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, consider the second order
U
-statistic

U_{n}=\frac{1}{{n\choose 2}}\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq n}\,h(X_{i},X_{j}).

This is an unbiased estimator of
\theta=\mathbb{E}[U_{n}]=\mathbb{E}[h(X_{1},X_{2})].
In fact,
U_{n}
is the UMVUE (uniformly minimum variance unbiased estimator) for
\theta
.
U
-Statistics are a natural first extension of sums of independent random variables. In the context of hypothesis testing,
U
-statistics appear as test statistics. The sample variance is one of the most commonly used second order
U
-statistics (in the context of inference for the population mean). Additionally, the Hoeffding decomposition (a form of ANOVA decomposition) implies that any function of independent random variables can be approximated by a
U
-statistics of sufficiently high order
(
3
)
. For these reasons, understanding the limiting distributions of
U
-statistics is a fundamental problem. The limit theory of
U
-statistics is much richer compared to that of IID sums. The limiting distribution can be a Gaussian or a Gaussian chaos (related to the chi-square distribution) depending the degeneracy of
h(\cdot,\cdot)
. The
kernel

h(\cdot,\cdot)
is called degenerate or canonical if

\mathbb{E}[h(X_{1},X_{2})|X_{2}]=\mathbb{E}[h(X_{1},X_{2})|X_{1}]=C\quad\mbox{almost surely.}

From now on, we assume that the kernels of the
U
-statistics are symmetric, i.e.,
h(x_{1},x_{2})=h(x_{2},x_{1})
and we set
H_{1}(x)=\mathbb{E}[h(x,X_{1})]
. With this notation, the kernel is degenerate if
H_{1}(X)=C
almost surely.

To illustrate the difference in the limiting distributions, consider the example of estimating
\mu^{2}=(\mathbb{E}[X_{1}])^{2}
using

\widehat{\theta}_{n}=\frac{1}{\binom{n}{2}}\sum_{i<j}X_{i}X_{j}~=~\frac{n}{n-1}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{n-1}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{2}\right).

If
\mu=\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]=0
(degenerate case), then both terms are of order
1/n
and if
\mu=\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]\neq 0
(non-degenerate case), then the first term is of order
1/\sqrt{n}
and the second term is of order
1/n
. This implies that the rate of convergence and the limiting distribution depend heavily on the value of
\mu
. Considering the cases at higher granularity, there is a phase transition from Gaussian to a more complicated limiting distribution as
|\mu|
varies from a constant to zero. Such a precise phase transition can be fleshed out explicitly by considering a Berry–Esseen bound in the Kolmogorov distance for
U_{n}
which should remain valid uniformly over all values of
|\mu|
.

Unfortunately, such a uniform Berry–Esseen bound for
U
-statistics is unavailable. Most existing BE bounds either assume explicitly a non-degenerate or degenerate kernel or do not imply convergence in distribution under degeneracy. For example, the BE bounds of
2
are of the order
n^{-1/2}\mathbb{E}[|H_{1}(X_{1})-\theta|^{3}]/(\mbox{Var}(H_{1}(X)))^{3/2}
, which becomes order 1 if
\mbox{Var}(H_{1}(X))
is close to zero. On the other hand, the BE bounds of
18
and
13
do not assume explicit non-degeneracy but their bounds only converge to zero at a rate of
n^{-1/12}
or
n^{-1/14}
, with some dependence on the eigenvalues of the kernel. The BE and Edgeworth expansions bounds of
4
could imply an
n^{-1/2}
rate but requires too many eigenvalues of the kernel to be non-zero (e.g., their result would not apply for the estimation of
\mu^{2}
). Currently, there is no BE bound that provides a “smooth” phase transition between Gaussian and non-Gaussian limiting distributions. Here we also mention the works
5
, where a BE bound of order
n^{-1}
is obtained for quadratic forms for
\mathbb{R}^{d}
- or Hilbert-space valued random variables. These quadratic forms can be viewed as degenerate second-order
U
-statistics, and the results of
5
can be contrasted with the rates obtained in
2
and
18
. We consider this paper as a first step in deriving a BE bound that gets the best of these settings and exhibits a smooth transition. We mention here the recent article
14
that provides lower bounds on the BE bound for
U
-statistics. It should, however, be clarified that these are worst case lower bounds and do not contradict out goal of BE bounds that exhibit smooth transitions.

The derivation of BE bounds in the Kolmogorov distance involves two key steps: (1) a smoothing inequality; and (2) a Lévy concentration function estimate. The smooth part of the smoothing inequalities revolve around the estimation of the characteristic function or thrice-differentiable functions of the
U
-statistic, and has been well-studied. The anti-concentration inequality is the subject of the current paper. The concentration function estimate from
2
would depend on
\mbox{Var}(H_{1}(X))
and the anti-concentration inequality from
18
and
13
does not depend on this variance, but on the eigenvalues of the kernel. However, if one combines the concentration function estimate of of
18
or
13
with the techniques of
2
, then one obtains a sub-optimal rate of at most
n^{-1/4}.
We note here that the anti-concentration inequality is needed for the supposed limiting distribution and not the
U
-statistic itself. The goal of the current paper is to thus derive a concentration function estimate that smoothly transitions between Gaussian and chi-square regimes. The next section discusses the overall organization of the article.

2
Distributional approximations for
U
-statistics

2.1
Distributional approximations and the concentration function

Let
X,X^{\prime},X_{1},\cdots,X_{n}
be random IID variables taking values in a measurable space
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A})
with distribution
P_{n}
(allowing for a triangular array setting.) Let
h:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be a symmetric, square integrable, mean zero function with respect to
P_{n}\otimes P_{n}
, that is,

h(x,x^{\prime})=h(x^{\prime},x),\quad\mathbb{E}[h(X,X^{\prime})^{2}]<+\infty,\quad\mathbb{E}[h(X,X^{\prime})]=0

The random variable

U_{n}=\frac{1}{\binom{n}{2}}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}h(X_{i},X_{j}),

is known as the
U-Statistic
associated to the
kernel

h
.
Lemma 5.1.5A of
17
(known more generally as the
Hoeffding Decomposition
) implies that we may decompose
U_{n}
into two parts:

U_{n}=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}H_{1}(X_{i})+\frac{1}{\binom{n}{2}}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}H_{2}(X_{i},X_{j}),

(1)

where

H_{1}(x)=\mathbb{E}[h(X_{1},X_{2})|X_{1}=x],\quad H_{2}(x,y)=h(x,y)-H_{1}(x)-H_{1}(y)

The first term in (
1
) is a sum of independent mean-zero random variables and is often known as the
linear
part. The second can be thought of as an interaction term that remains after removing first order effects. The function
H_{2}(\cdot,\cdot)
defines a self-adjoint Hilbert-Schmidt Operator
T:L^{2}(\mathcal{X},P_{n})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{X},P_{n})
given by:

Tf(x):=\int_{\mathcal{X}}H_{2}(x,y)f(y)\mathrm{d}P_{n}(y)=\mathbb{E}[H_{2}(x,X)f(X)]

Consequently, the spectral theory of compact operators on Hilbert Spaces gives us that
H_{2}(\cdot,\cdot)
may be written in the form:

H_{2}(x,y)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}b_{k}\phi_{k}(x)\phi_{k}(y),\quad\text{(In the $L^{2}(P_{n}\otimes P_{n})$ sense)}

(2)

where
\{\phi_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
,
\phi_{k}:X\to\mathbb{R}
is a sequence of
orthonormal eigenfunctions
of
T
i.e.,

\mathbb{E}[\phi_{j}(X_{i})\phi_{k}(X_{i})]=\begin{cases}1,&\mbox{if }j=k,\\
0,&\mbox{if }j\neq k.\end{cases}

and the functions
\{\phi_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
and
\{b_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
satisfy

b_{k}\phi_{k}(x)=(T\phi_{k})(x)=\mathbb{E}[H_{2}(x,X)\phi_{k}(X)],

and
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}b_{k}^{2}<\infty
. We may, without loss of generality, assume that
|b_{1}|\geq|b_{2}|\geq|b_{3}|\geq\cdots
after appropriate rearrangement. An important fact to notice is:

(T\mathbbm{1})(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}H_{2}(x,y)\mathrm{d}P_{n}(y)=\mathbb{E}[H_{2}(x,X^{\prime})]=0.

This means that
\mathbbm{1}
is always an eigenfunction of
T
with eigenvalue
0
. Thus, a corollary of orthonormality is that
\mathbb{E}[\phi_{k}(X)]=\mathbb{E}[\phi_{k}(X)\mathbbm{1}]=0
for any eigenfunction with
1\leq k<+\infty
. Note that in our previous terminology this states that the
U
-statistic associated to the kernel
H_{2}
is always
degenerate
.

With all of this in mind, let us jointly consider
H_{1},H_{2}
. If needed, let
\phi_{0}(\cdot)
be a function orthonormal to
\{\phi_{k}(\cdot):1\leq k\leq\infty\}
such that
\{\phi_{k}(\cdot):\,0\leq k\leq+\infty\}
forms an orthonormal basis of the linear span of
\{H_{1}(\cdot),E[H_{2}(\cdot,X)g(X)]:g\in L_{2}\}
. In words: this set spans the image of the operator
T
(which is spanned by
\phi_{k}
with indices
1\leq k<+\infty
) along with the one-dimensional subspace spanned by
H_{1}
. We thus obtain the decompositions:

H_{1}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\phi_{k}(x),\quad\mbox{and}\quad H_{2}(x,y)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{k}\phi_{k}(x)\phi_{k}(y).

for some square summable coefficients
a_{k}
, determined by the standard
L^{2}(P_{n})
inner product, i.e.
a_{k}=E[H_{1}(X)\phi_{k}(X)]
and
b_{0}=0
. Substituting this into the Hoeffding decomposition (
1
),
U_{n}
can be represented as:

\displaystyle U_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\phi_{k}(X_{i})\right)+\binom{n}{2}^{-1}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{k}\phi_{k}(X_{i})\phi_{k}(X_{j})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left[\frac{2a_{k}}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{k}(X_{i})+\frac{2b_{k}}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\phi_{k}(X_{i})\phi_{k}(X_{j})\right].

Using the identity

\frac{2}{n}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\xi_{i}\xi_{j}=(n-1)\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}^{2}-\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}\right)^{2}\right),

and setting

\overline{\phi}_{k}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{k}(X_{i})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\overline{S}_{\phi_{k}}^{2}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{k}^{2}(X_{i})-\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{k}(X_{i})\right)^{2},\quad k\geq 0,

we obtain

\displaystyle U_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left[2a_{k}\overline{\phi}_{k}+\frac{b_{k}}{n-1}\left\{(n-1)\overline{\phi}_{k}^{2}-\overline{S}_{\phi_{k}}^{2}\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left[\frac{2a_{k}}{\sqrt{n}}(n^{1/2}\overline{\phi}_{k})+\frac{b_{k}}{n}(n^{1/2}\overline{\phi}_{k})^{2}-\frac{b_{k}}{n-1}\overline{S}_{\phi_{k}}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left[\frac{2a_{k}}{\sqrt{n}}(n^{1/2}\overline{\phi}_{k})+\frac{b_{k}}{n}\left\{(n^{1/2}\overline{\phi}_{k})^{2}-1\right\}-\frac{b_{k}}{n-1}\left\{\overline{S}_{\phi_{k}}^{2}-1\right\}-\frac{b_{k}}{n(n-1)}\right].

Heuristically, we have that the sequence
(n^{1/2}\overline{\phi}_{k})_{k\geq 0}
converges in distribution to an infinite-dimensional Gaussian random variable with mean zero and identity covariance, because
\mathbb{E}[\phi_{k}(X)]=0
,
\mathbb{E}[\phi_{k}^{2}(X)]=1
, and
E[\phi_{k}(X)\phi_{k^{\prime}}(X)]=0
for
k\neq k^{\prime}
. This, in turn, suggests that the distribution of
U_{n}
should be approximately one where
n^{1/2}\overline{\phi}_{k},k\geq 0
are replaced with an independent sequence of standard Gaussian random variables. More formally, consider
\{G_{i,k},1\leq i\leq n,k\geq 0\}
, a set of iid
{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)
random variables. Define

Z_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}G_{i,k},\quad\mbox{and}\quad S_{k}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}G_{i,k}^{2}-\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}G_{i,k}\right)^{2}.

This gives the approximating random variable as:

W_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left[\frac{2a_{k}}{\sqrt{n}}Z_{k}+\frac{b_{k}}{n}\left\{Z_{k}^{2}-1\right\}-\frac{b_{k}}{n-1}\left\{S_{k}^{2}-1\right\}-\frac{b_{k}}{n(n-1)}\right].

One can make this argument rigorous by considering a truncated series in the definition of
U_{n}
to get
U_{n,K}
defined as

U_{n,K}~:=~\sum_{k=0}^{K}\left[\frac{2a_{k}}{\sqrt{n}}(n^{1/2}\overline{\phi}_{k})+\frac{b_{k}}{n}\left\{(n^{1/2}\overline{\phi}_{k})^{2}-1\right\}-\frac{b_{k}}{n-1}\left\{\overline{S}_{\phi_{k}}^{2}-1\right\}-\frac{b_{k}}{n(n-1)}\right].

Then approximating
U_{n,K}
with
W_{n,K}
(the approximate version of
W_{n}
) and then letting
K
diverge slowly enough, one can prove that that the Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance between
U_{n}
and
W_{n}
converges to zero; see, for example, Section 5.5.2 of
17
for details.

To quantify the accuracy of this approximation, we seek an estimate of
the Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance:

d_{\mathrm{KS}}(U_{n},W_{n})=\sup_{t}|\mathbb{P}(U_{n}\leq t)-\mathbb{P}(W_{n}\leq t)|.

Note that for any choice of smooth functions
g_{t,\pm\varepsilon}(\cdot)
satisfying

\mathbf{1}\{x\leq t-\varepsilon\}\leq g_{t,-\varepsilon}(x)\leq\mathbf{1}\{x\leq t\}\leq g_{t,\varepsilon}(x)\leq\mathbf{1}\{x\leq t+\varepsilon\},

we have

d_{\mathrm{KS}}(U_{n},W_{n})\leq\sup_{t,\eta\in\{\pm\varepsilon\}}\,|\mathbb{E}[g_{t,\eta}(U_{n})]-\mathbb{E}[g_{t,\eta}(W_{n})]|~+~\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\,\mathbb{P}(t-\varepsilon\leq W_{n}\leq t+\varepsilon).

(3)

The second term here is related to the Lévy concentration function of
W_{n}
: for any random variable
Y
, define

Q_{Y}(l)~:=~\sup_{a,b\in\mathbb{R}:\,b-a\leq l}\,\mathbb{P}(a<Y\leq b).

It should be mentioned here that the smoothing inequality (
3
) could be replaced with the Esseen smoothing inequality
(
16
, Theorem 5.3)
, where the first term of (
3
) is replaced with a term involving the difference of characteristic functions of
U_{n}
, and
W_{n}
.
5
,
18
, and others provide bounds on the first part of these smoothing inequalities. The focus in the current paper is the concentration function.

By standard results on Gaussian random variables, we know that
S_{k}^{2}
,
Z_{k}
are independent, and moreover, if
X+Y
are independent, then
Q_{X+Y}(\varepsilon)\leq Q_{X}(\varepsilon)

(
16
, Lemma 1.11)
. Thus, if we define:

\widetilde{W}_{n}:=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left[\frac{2a_{k}}{\sqrt{n}}Z_{k}+\frac{b_{k}}{n}\left\{Z_{k}^{2}-1\right\}\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left[\frac{2a_{k-1}}{\sqrt{n}}Z_{k}+\frac{b_{k}}{n}(Z_{k}^{2}-1)\right],

(4)

then
Q_{W_{n}}(\varepsilon)\leq Q_{\widetilde{W}_{n}}(\varepsilon)
. Thus it suffices to estimate
Q_{\widetilde{W}_{n}}(\varepsilon)
. In particular
Q_{\widetilde{W_{n}}}
is exactly of the form of the statistic
S
mentioned in the abstract, with
\mu_{k}=\frac{2a_{k-1}}{\sqrt{n}}
and
\lambda_{k}=\frac{b_{k}}{n}
for
k\geq 1
.

While we motivated the problem of bounding the concentration function from
U
-statistics, there are other applications that involve similar concentration functions; see, for example,
12
. Before proceeding to present our results on the concentration function, we review existing results and their limitations.

2.2
Literature Review

Let
\{Z_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
be an infinite sequence of independent standard Gaussian random variables on some common probability space, and let
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty},\{\mu_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
be real-valued, square-summable sequences with
|\lambda_{k}|\geq|\lambda_{k+1}|
. We study the concentration function of

S:=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left[\lambda_{k}(Z_{k}^{2}-1)+\mu_{k}Z_{k}\right].

Here the infinite series is to be interpreted as an almost-sure limit, which by the Kolmogorov three-series theorem is well-defined if
\left\|\lambda\right\|_{2}+\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}<\infty
. We derive upper bounds on the concentration function:

Q_{S}(\varepsilon)=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}\left(x<S\leq x+\varepsilon\right).

The behavior of
Q_{S}(\varepsilon)
was studied in
4
, corresponding to their Theorem 1.2. They provide a rate for statistics of a more general form, but their bound is finite only when
\lambda_{9}\neq 0
. It was further studied in
1
, who obtain a bound for more general statistics. However, their bound does not tend to
0
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
as they work in a very general case in which the random variables involved may not be continuous. Finally,
Q_{S}(\varepsilon)
was also studied in
6
under the assumption that
\lambda_{k}\geq 0
and
\mu_{k}=0
for all
k\geq 1
. Note that, in the context of
U
-statistics, there are no constraints on the signs of
\lambda_{k},\,k\geq 0
.
While the theory of quadratic forms could be considered the “first-step” beyond that of sums of independent random variables, concentration function bounds for such objects can be rather subtle. This is because they are inherently “mixed” objects: they combine quadratic Gaussian (chi-square) and Gaussian behavior in one statistic and exhibit properties of both simultaneously. For the simplest (and perhaps most extreme) example, if
Z
is a Gaussian random variable and
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, then for
X_{a,b}:=aZ^{2}+bZ
, we have
Q_{X_{0,b}}(\varepsilon)\asymp{\varepsilon}/{|b|}
while
Q_{X_{a,0}}(\varepsilon)\asymp{\varepsilon^{1/2}}/{|a|}
. Thus, the individual coefficients on each term in the sum are of importance: e.g. if
|a|
is small, but nonzero, and
|b|
is large, one should expect a rate “close” to
\varepsilon
, and vice-versa for the opposite case. The bound used in
18
is of the form
\varepsilon^{1/2}/|a|
for all cases, whereas
13
use the Carberry Wright inequality which provides an estimate of the form
\varepsilon^{1/2}/\mathrm{Var}(S)
. The upshot here is that these bounds
always
yield a rate of order
\varepsilon^{1/2}
, even in cases when the linear part of
S
is large compared to its quadratic part.

Currently known concentration function bounds for quadratic forms, while elegant and sharp in certain cases, often make some restrictive assumptions regarding the coefficients
\lambda_{k}
and
\mu_{k}
, mostly to avoid working with degenerate cases as exhibited above. Matching, two-sided bounds are available when
\mu\equiv 0
and
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}>0

(
6
, Theorem 1)
. However, the presence of nonzero
\mu
and differently signed
\lambda_{k}

significantly
complicates the analysis. Theorem 2 of
6
bounds
Q_{S}(\cdot)
when
\mu\neq 0
, but only when all
\lambda_{k}
are positive and satisfy a specific balancing condition. This condition makes quantitative the qualitative idea that better anti-concentration occurs when one summand does not dominate the others. Such ideas are closely related to the theory of majorization; see
9
and
15
for a further discussion of this topic in the context of concentration function estimates.

Theorem 2 of
6
is sharp in the sense that under the assumed balancing and non-negativity conditions of the theorem statement, the bound provided is an upper and lower bound up to universal constant factors. However, in the context of
U
-Statistics, the coefficients
\lambda_{k}
correspond to the eigenvalues of a Hilbert-Schmidt operator on a Hilbert Space, while the
\mu_{k}
have a similar interpretation. In particular
\lambda_{k},\mu_{k}
may be
any
square-summable sequences. They need not satisfy any non-negativity or balancing conditions, as will be illustrated in Section
6
. This makes the existing concentration function bounds are either impossible to apply or sub-optimal.

In summary, current results in the literature have obtained concentration function estimates in the following three settings:

1.

Purely Gaussian random variables.

2.

Weighted sums of central chi-square random variables.

3.

Weighted sums of non-central chi-square random variables under a set of balancing and non-negativity assumptions.

We bound the concentration function
Q_{S}(\cdot)
under minimal assumptions on the coefficients
\lambda_{k}
and
\mu_{k}
. In particular, we make no assumptions on
\lambda_{k}
,
\mu_{k}
beyond square-summability. Furthermore, our bounds are adaptive, in that they recover the right behavior as
\mu
tends to
0
(purely chi-square) and as
\lambda
tends to
0
(purely Gaussian). Our bounds extend currently existing bounds to new coefficient regimes, while recovering previously known bounds in already studied cases (except one).

3
Notation

Throughout, we let
\lambda:=(\lambda_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
and
\mu:=(\mu_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
denote elements of
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N},\mathbb{R})
with decreasing absolute value, e.g.
|\lambda_{1}|\geq|\lambda_{2}|\geq|\lambda_{3}|\geq\cdots
. For a sequence
x\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
, we say
x\geq 0
if
x_{j}\geq 0
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\not\geq 0
if this is not the case. For any
j\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote:

\Lambda_{j}^{2}:=\sum_{k=j}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}^{2}.

A special case is of course that
\Lambda_{1}=\left\|\lambda\right\|_{2}
. The symbols
p_{j},q_{j}
denote real valued sequences, satisfying the relations:

\frac{1}{p_{j}}:=\frac{\lambda_{j}^{2}}{\Lambda_{1}^{2}},\quad\mbox{for}\quad j\geq 1\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{q_{j}}:=\frac{\lambda_{j}^{2}}{\Lambda_{2}^{2}}\quad\mbox{for}\quad j\geq 2.

In particular, note that
\sum_{j\geq 1}{1}/{p_{j}}=\sum_{j\geq 2}{1}/{q_{j}}=1
. The sequence
\{Z_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
is an infinite sequence of independent standard Gaussian random variables defined on some common probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, while
\Phi
denotes the standard Gaussian cumulative distribution function. Finally,
\phi_{X}(t)
denotes the characteristic function of a random variable, given by
\phi_{X}(t):=\mathbb{E}[e^{itX}]
whereas
f_{X}(x)
denotes the density of a random variable
X
whose distribution is absolutely continuous (with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
).

4
The Main Estimate and its Properties

In this section, we present our main results and compare them to existing results.

Theorem 1

(General Concentration Function Estimate)
.

Let
S:=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}(Z_{k}^{2}-1)+\mu_{k}Z_{k}
.
Then, the series defining
S
converges almost surely and

\displaystyle Q_{S}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\leq 35\varepsilon\left[\left(\frac{1}{\Lambda_{1}^{2}+\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}}\right)^{1/2}+\mathbbm{1}{\{\varepsilon\leq 2\Lambda_{1}\}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4}\frac{\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\Lambda_{1}^{2}}\right)\mathcal{I}(\varepsilon,\lambda)\right],

(5)

where
\mathcal{I}(\varepsilon,\lambda)
is given by

\mathcal{I}(\varepsilon,\lambda)~=~\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\Lambda_{1}}A_{p,1}^{1/p_{1}}A_{p,2}^{1/p_{2}},&p_{1}>2,\\
\frac{1}{\Lambda_{1}}\log\left(\frac{2\Lambda_{1}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{p_{2}}},&p_{1}=2,\\
\frac{1}{\Lambda_{1}}B_{p,1}^{1/p_{1}}A_{p,2}^{1/p_{2}},&1\leq p_{1}<2,\\
\frac{1}{(\Lambda_{1}\Lambda_{2})^{1/2}}\left(1+\mathbbm{1}{\{\varepsilon\leq 2\Lambda_{2}\}C_{q,2}}\right),&1\leq p_{1}<2,\ q_{2}>1,\end{cases}

(6)

with

\displaystyle A_{p,i}=\left(\frac{1}{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p_{i}}}\right)\left[1-\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2\Lambda_{1}}\right)^{\frac{p_{i}}{2}-1}\right],

(7)

\displaystyle B_{p,i}=\left(\frac{1}{\frac{1}{p_{i}}-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\left[\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2\Lambda_{1}}\right)^{\frac{p_{i}}{2}-1}-1\right],

(8)

\displaystyle C_{q,i}=\left(\frac{1}{1-\frac{1}{q_{i}}}\right)\left[1-\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2\Lambda_{2}}\right)^{\frac{q_{i}}{2}-\frac{1}{2}}\right].

(9)

Theorem 2

(User-friendly Concentration Function Estimate)
.

Given the same setup as Theorem
1
, we have the estimate:

\displaystyle Q_{S}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\leq 35\varepsilon\left[\left(\frac{1}{\Lambda_{1}^{2}+\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}}\right)^{1/2}+\mathbbm{1}{\{\varepsilon\leq 2\Lambda_{1}\}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4}\frac{\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\Lambda_{1}^{2}}\right)\mathcal{J}(\varepsilon,\lambda)\right],

(10)

where
\mathcal{J}(\varepsilon,\lambda)
is given by

\mathcal{J}(\varepsilon,\lambda)~:=~\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\Lambda_{1}}\frac{1}{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p_{1}}}&p_{1}>2,\\
\frac{1}{\Lambda_{1}}\log\left(\frac{2\Lambda_{1}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{p_{2}}}&p_{1}=2,\\
\frac{1}{\Lambda_{1}^{\frac{3}{2}-\frac{1}{p_{1}}}}\frac{1}{\frac{1}{p_{1}}-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{p_{1}}-\frac{1}{2}}}&1\leq p_{1}<2,\\
\frac{1}{(\Lambda_{1}\Lambda_{2})^{1/2}}\frac{1}{1-\frac{1}{q_{2}}}&1\leq p_{1}<2,\ q_{2}>1.\end{cases}

(11)

The only difference between Theorem
2
and Theorem
1
is the replacement of the simpler, easier to understand
\mathcal{J}(\varepsilon,\lambda)
with the more complicated but ultimately more accurate, and sometimes better-behaved
\mathcal{I}(\varepsilon,\lambda)
. It is worth noting that our piecewise expressions are not disjoint; we obtain a bound for the case
1\leq p_{1}<2
(valid for all sub-cases) as well as the case
1\leq p_{1}<2
and
q_{2}>1
. These bounds are different, but both are valid, and so one may take the minimum of the two in applications. Finally, the constant 35 is by no means optimal and can definitely be reduced if one tracks constants more carefully through the course of the proof.

The difference between
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}
is most pronounced in certain critical/edge cases, as we will see in the next section. The proof of Theorem
1
follows from a classical result connecting the concentration function to the characteristic function: Lemma 1.16 of
16
states that for any random variable
Y
,

Q_{Y}(\varepsilon)\leq\left(\frac{96}{95}\right)^{2}\max\{\varepsilon,1/a\}\int_{-a}^{a}|\phi_{Y}(t)|dt,\quad\mbox{for all}\quad\varepsilon\geq 0,a\geq 0.

(12)

Independence of the
Z_{k},k\geq 1
implies a product structure for
\phi_{S}(t)
, but controlling the integral is subtle depending on the cases listed in Theorem
1
. The full proof can be found in Section
7
, with an outline in Section
5
. It should be noted that the proof approaches of
6
and
12
are similar to ours.
The proof that Theorem
1
implies Theorem
2
is brief and may be found in the appendix; see Section
S.6
.

Given that
S
can be viewed as a sum of infinitely many independent random variables and also as a quadratic form of independent standard Gaussian random variables, it is possible to apply general tools such as one of the elegant Kolmogorov-Rogozin Carbery-Wright inequalities. However, neither of them yield a bound with the correct behavior in all cases and they sometimes provide a bound with significant slack; we defer the details to Sections
S.1
and
S.2
. It is worth noting that the Kolmogorov-Rogozin inequality can be derived from (
12
), which is the main result we use directly in our proof - thus the slack in the bound it provides is not entirely unexpected.

Before comparing our results to the existing ones, we first consider how random variable
S
behaves for different choices of
(\lambda_{k},\,\mu_{k}),\,k\geq 1
.

(S1)

If
\Lambda_{1}=\|\lambda\|_{2}=0
(i.e., pure Gaussian), then
S\sim N(0,\|\mu\|_{2}^{2})
which implies that the maximum value of density of
S
is
(2\pi)^{-1/2}/\|\mu\|_{2}
, i.e.,
Q_{S}(\varepsilon)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}\|\mu\|_{2}^{-1}\varepsilon
.

(S2)

If
\|\mu\|_{2}=0
and
\Lambda_{2}=0
(i.e., chi-square), then
S\sim\lambda_{1}(\chi_{1}^{2}-1)
, the density of which is unbounded and
Q_{S}(\varepsilon)\asymp\varepsilon^{1/2}/\lambda_{1}
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
.

(S3)

If
\|\mu\|_{2}=0
and
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=1
with
\Lambda_{3}=0
, then
S
is a sum of two independent centered chi-square random variables which has a bounded density (because Exp(1) has a bounded density) and one should expect
Q_{S}(\varepsilon)\asymp\varepsilon
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
(with constants depending on
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
).

(S4)

If
\|\mu\|_{2}=0
and
\lambda_{1}=-\lambda_{2}=1
with
\Lambda_{3}=0
, then
S
is a difference of two independent chi-square random variables which does
not
have a bounded density and it can be proved that
Q_{S}(\varepsilon)\asymp\varepsilon\ln(1/\varepsilon)
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
; see Section
S.3.2
for a proof.

Comparing settings
(S3)
and
(S4)
, it is interesting to note that the mismatch in the signs of
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
plays a significant role in the behavior of the concentration function. In relation, the results of
6
apply for
(S3)
but not
(S4)
. Our bound applies in all cases because our bound is sign agnostic. Given that lower bounds are in the settings discussed above, we expect a good bound on
Q_{S}(\varepsilon)
to at least match the orders in these settings. Also, note that comparing settings
(S2)
and
(S3)
, we expect the behavior of the concentration function changes from
\varepsilon
to
\varepsilon^{1/2}
as
\lambda_{2}\downarrow 0
. Capturing this behavior is one of the reasons why
\mathcal{I}(\cdot,\cdot)
and
\mathcal{J}(\cdot,\cdot)
depend on the size of
p_{1}
and
q_{2}
; see Section
4.1
for more details. This relates to balancing of the
\lambda
coefficients. The importance of balancing of coefficients has been studied elegantly using the framework of majorization and Schur-concavity/monotonicty (cf.
15
). This viewpoint is explored thoroughly in
9
who obtain various results, in particular the maximum of the density, for weighted sums of independent gamma random variables.

Our bound in Theorems
1
,
2
can be written as a sum of two terms,
Q_{S}(\varepsilon)\leq 35\left[Q_{G}(\varepsilon)+\mathbbm{1}\{\varepsilon\leq 2\Lambda_{1}\}Q_{C}(\varepsilon)\right]
where

Q_{G}(\varepsilon)~:=~\varepsilon\left(\frac{1}{\Lambda_{1}^{2}+\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}}\right)^{1/2},\quad\mbox{and}\quad Q_{C}(\varepsilon)~:=~\varepsilon\mathcal{I}(\varepsilon,\lambda)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4}\frac{\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\Lambda_{1}^{2}}\right).

(13)

which can be interpreted as Gaussian and chi-square “components” of the concentration function. In Setting
(S1)
,
Q_{C}(\varepsilon)=0
and
Q_{G}(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon/\|\mu\|_{2}
matching the right behavior. In Setting
(S2)
,
p_{1}=1
,
p_{2}=\Lambda_{1}^{2}/\lambda_{2}^{2}=\infty
and hence,
\mathcal{J}(\varepsilon,\lambda)=2\Lambda_{1}^{-1}/\varepsilon^{1/2}
matching the right behavior. In Settings
(S3)
and
(S4)
,
\mathcal{J}(\varepsilon,\lambda)=\Lambda_{1}^{-1}(\log(2\Lambda_{1}/\varepsilon))
by taking
p_{1}=2
. Interestingly, our bound cannot recover the right behavior for Setting
(S3)
because our proof technique cannot take advantage of matching signs of
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
. In particular,
|\phi_{S}|
depends only on the absolute values of the
\lambda_{k}
.

Comparison with the bounds of
6
and
12
.

Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 of

of

covers the case when

4.1
Behavior of the Estimates at Boundaries

As our estimates for
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}
are piecewise, it is natural to understand their behavior at the respective boundaries in their definition. For example, at
p_{1}=2
, we witness a logarithmic transition - can this be achieved by taking
p_{1}\to 2
in either of the bounds we obtain for
p_{1}>2
or
p_{1}<2
. The answer is
yes
, with the caveat that we must consider the more complicated
\mathcal{I}
from Theorem
1
.
However, such contiguity does not hold for the bound in Theorem
2
, which is one of its main weaknesses. We provide details in the analysis below.

1.

p_{1}>2
and
p_{1}\to 2^{+}
: For
p_{1}>2
, Theorem
2
yields a linear rate of the form:

\frac{\varepsilon}{\Lambda_{1}}\frac{1}{1-\frac{2}{p_{1}}}.

(17)

If
p_{1}\to 2^{+}
, however, this simplified bound diverges. In such cases, it is important to use the bound in Theorem
1
, as it recovers the appropriate logarithmic rate given in the case
p_{1}=2
. Indeed, we have:

\frac{1}{\Lambda_{1}}\frac{1}{1-\frac{2}{p_{1}}}\left[1-\left(\frac{2\Lambda_{1}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\left(1-\frac{p_{1}}{2}\right)}\right]=\frac{1}{\Lambda_{1}}\frac{p_{1}/2}{p_{1}/2-1}\left[1-\left(\frac{2\Lambda_{1}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\left(1-\frac{p_{1}}{2}\right)}\right]\underset{p_{1}\to 2^{+}}{\to}\frac{1}{\Lambda_{1}}\log\left(\frac{2\Lambda_{1}}{\varepsilon}\right).

2.

p_{1}=2
: In this case, we obtain a rate of the form

\varepsilon(\log\left(2\Lambda_{1}/\varepsilon\right))^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{p_{2}}}.

(18)

This rate is suboptimal in the special case where
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
have the same sign, but is otherwise optimal; see Section
S.3.2
. In some cases, for example, when
p_{1}
is very close to
2
, this bound is better than the simplified bound obtained in the prior case (from Theorem
2
). However, some calculus shows it is always inferior to the bound we obtain in
p_{1}>2
in Theorem
1
.

3.

1\leq p_{1}\leq 2
: In this case, the bound is the minimum of two other bounds; each of which is preferable in a distinct scenario:

(a)

The first is of the following form:

\varepsilon\left(\frac{1}{\Lambda_{1}^{\frac{3}{2}-\frac{1}{p_{1}}}}\frac{1}{\frac{2}{p_{1}}-1}\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{p_{1}}-\frac{1}{2}}}\right)=\frac{1}{\frac{2}{p_{1}}-1}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\Lambda_{1}}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}-\frac{1}{p_{1}}}.

and it holds regardless of the values of
q_{2}
and
\Lambda_{2}
. This bound has no coefficient of the form
{\Lambda_{2}}^{-1}
(which in this case is large due to the assumption of
p_{1}
being small), so it does not explode as
\Lambda_{2}\to 0^{+}
. However, it always yields sublinear concentration as
3/2-{1}/{p_{1}}<1
. Thus, we sacrifice decay in
\varepsilon
but remove the dependence on
\Lambda_{2}
. Notice also in the definition of
\mathcal{I}(\cdot,\cdot)
that as
p_{1}\to 2^{-}
, we obtain a bound matching the
p_{1}=2
case, which can be seen much like in the case
p_{1}>2
by computing limits.

(b)

The second bound behaves in the opposite manner and is of the form:

\frac{\varepsilon}{(\Lambda_{1}\Lambda_{2})^{1/2}}\frac{1}{1-\frac{1}{q_{2}}}.

(19)

It requires
q_{2}>1
which is equivalent to assuming
\lambda_{3}\neq 0
(but is weaker than assuming
p_{1}\geq 3
). We obtain a linear rate in
\varepsilon
but at the cost of a term
{\Lambda_{2}}^{-1}
so that the bound diverges when
\Lambda_{2}\to 0
, or when
q_{2}\to 1
.

Thus, for small
\Lambda_{2}
and large
\varepsilon
, the first bound is preferable. For small
\varepsilon
and large
\Lambda_{2}
, then the second is preferable.

5
Sketch of the Proof

As mentioned, our proof begins by invoking Lemma 1.16 of
16
as in equation (
12
). Fixing
\varepsilon>0
and setting
a=\varepsilon
in this bound gives:

Q_{X}(\varepsilon)\leq\left(\frac{96}{95}\right)^{2}\varepsilon\int_{-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}|\phi_{X}(t)|\mathrm{d}t.

(20)

If
X=\sum X_{i}
is a sum of independent random variables, the right hand side is straightforward to compute as
\phi_{X}
is simply the product of the individual
\phi_{X_{i}}
. In our case, a brief computation shows that:

|\phi_{\lambda_{k}(Z^{2}-1)+\mu_{k}Z}(t)|=\ \frac{\exp\left(-\frac{2\mu_{k}^{2}t^{2}}{1+4\lambda_{k}^{2}t^{2}}\right)}{(1+4\lambda_{k}^{2}t^{2})^{1/4}},

so that:

|\phi_{S}(t)|=\frac{\exp\left(-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{2\mu_{k}^{2}t^{2}}{1+4\lambda_{k}^{2}t^{2}}\right)}{\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}(1+4\lambda_{k}^{2}t^{2})^{1/4}}.

To estimate this, we apply the following heuristics:

1.

For
t
such that
4\lambda_{k}^{2}t^{2}
is of constant order, we have:

|\phi_{S}(t)|\approx\exp\left(-t^{2}\left[2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mu_{k}^{2}\right]\right).

The right hand side of the above is the characteristic function of a Gaussian with variance
\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}
. Thus, for small
t
, the characteristic function is essentially that of a Gaussian. In the notation of equation (
13
), this gives us the
Q_{G}
term.

2.

On the other hand, if
t
is taken ”large”:

|\phi_{S}(t)|\approx\frac{\exp\left(-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu_{k}^{2}}{4\lambda_{k}^{2}}\right)}{\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}(1+4\lambda_{k}^{2}t^{2})^{1/4}}=C(\mu,\lambda)\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1+4\lambda_{k}^{2}t^{2})^{1/4}}

since
\frac{t^{2}}{1+4\lambda_{k}^{2}t^{2}}\to\frac{1}{4\lambda_{k}^{2}}
as
t\to\infty
.
In this regime, the integrand behaves like that of the characteristic function of a weighted sum of independent chi-square random-variables, multiplied by a prefactor
C(\mu,\lambda)
that decays exponentially as a function of the ratio
\frac{\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\Lambda_{1}^{2}}
. In the notation of equation (
13
), this gives us the term
Q_{C}
.

Adding these two terms and applying (
20
) gives

Q_{X}(\varepsilon)\lesssim\varepsilon\int_{-1/\varepsilon}^{1/\varepsilon}|\phi_{X}(t)|\mathrm{d}t\approx\varepsilon\left[\underbrace{\int_{t\text{ small}}\exp\left(-t^{2}\left[2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mu_{k}^{2}\right]\right)\mathrm{d}t}_{\text{Gaussian Part}}+C(\mu,\lambda)\underbrace{\int_{t\text{ large}}\frac{1}{\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}(1+4\lambda_{k}^{2}t^{2})^{1/4}}\mathrm{d}t}_{\text{Chi Square Part}}\right].

(21)

The first integral is straightforward to compute, being the integral of a Gaussian function over some interval. Estimating the second integral is more involved and its behavior is highly dependent on the relative size of the
\lambda_{k}
. For example, if all
\lambda_{k}
but
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
are
0
, then the integral over the whole real line (corresponding to
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
) will diverge, and we cannot obtain a linear in
\varepsilon
with this technique. On the other hand, if
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}\neq 0
then said integral will converge and give us a linear rate. The estimation of this integral is the content of Lemma
1
:

Lemma 1
.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be defined as in Theorem
1
. Then:

\int_{\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{1}}}^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1+4\lambda_{k}^{2}t^{2})^{1/4}}\mathrm{d}t\leq 6\mathcal{I}(\varepsilon,\lambda).

(22)

Proof.

See the computations in Section
S.5
.
∎

The proof of this estimate is lengthy but straightforward. Our approach follows that of Lemma A2 from
12
, which hinges upon an application of Hölder’s inequality with an intelligent choice of weights. However, their proof only covers the case when
p_{1}\geq 3
or
\frac{{\lambda_{1}}^{2}}{\Lambda^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{3}
. Our proof provides estimates for the remaining cases, based on casework depending on the relative sizes of
p_{k}
. The most involved case is showing we can obtain a linear rate when
\frac{1}{p_{1}}>\frac{1}{2}
but
\lambda_{3}\neq 0
, e.g. the final case in the definitions of
\mathcal{J},\mathcal{I}
. This case represents coefficients that are poorly balanced yet still numerous enough to provide linear concentration in
\varepsilon
. The proof technique in this case is essentially a one-step recursion and was inspired by the proof of Case 2 in Theorem 1 of
6
.

6.1
Square of the Mean and General Rank 1 - U-statistics

Let
X,X_{1},\cdots,X_{n}
denote an IID sample from some probability distribution
P_{n}
with
\mathbb{E}[X]=\theta_{n},\mathrm{Var}(X)=\sigma_{n}^{2}
. We wish to obtain an unbiased estimate of
\theta_{n}^{2}
. The corresponding U-Statistic is:

\widetilde{U}_{n}=\frac{1}{{n\choose 2}}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}X_{i}X_{j},

(23)

and its centered version (which we consider hereafter):

U_{n}=\frac{1}{{n\choose 2}}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(X_{i}X_{j}-\theta_{n}^{2}),

(24)

i.e., according to the setup in Section
2.1
, we have kernel function
h(x,y)=xy-\theta_{n}^{2}
.

The statistic
U_{n}
above may seem artificial - the square of the mean is not necessarily an everyday quantity of interest. However, after applying the diagonalization procedure discussed in
2.1
,
every
U-statistic with a rank
1
kernel can be written in such a form after a deterministic transformation (diagonalization). Thus, it is a simple yet broadly applicable starting point for any analysis. In the appendix, we show explicitly how
U_{n}
exhibits a phase transition in its distribution, based on the parameters
\theta_{n},\sigma_{n}
- see - The calculation is essentially identical to one done in Section
2.1
but is more transparent.

Following the notation of Section
2
, we obtain by calculations in Appendix
S.8
that:

H_{1}(x)=x\theta_{n}-\theta_{n}^{2},\quad H_{2}(x,y)=xy-\theta_{n}(x+y)

with a single non-constant eigenfunction

\phi_{1}(x)=\frac{x-\theta_{n}}{\sigma_{n}},

and coefficients:

a_{1}=\theta_{n}\sigma_{n},\quad b_{1}=\sigma_{n}^{2}.

So in summary:

H_{1}(x)=a_{1}\phi_{1}(x),\quad H_{2}(x,y)=b_{1}\phi_{1}(x)\phi_{1}(y).

This represents the simplest possible limiting behavior for a
U
-statistic -
H_{2}
has one nontrivial eigenfunction and eigenvalue, and is a rank-one operator. Thus, as derived in
2
the corresponding Gaussian approximation is

W_{n}=\frac{2a_{1}}{\sqrt{n}}Z_{1}+\frac{b_{1}}{n}(Z_{1}^{2}-1)-\frac{b_{1}^{2}}{n-1}(S^{2}-1)-\frac{b_{1}}{n(n-1)}.

As mentioned in equation (
4
) of Section
2
, it suffices to estimate the concentration function of:

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}_{n}=\frac{2a_{1}}{\sqrt{n}}Z_{1}+\frac{b_{1}}{n}(Z_{1}^{2}-1)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\theta_{n}\sigma_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}Z_{1}+\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}}{n}(Z_{1}^{2}-1).

This implies

\frac{n^{1/2}}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}\widetilde{W}_{n}=\frac{2\theta_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}Z_{1}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(Z_{1}^{2}-1),\quad\frac{n}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}\widetilde{W}_{n}=\frac{2n^{1/2}\theta_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}Z_{1}+(Z_{1}^{2}-1),

whose behavior depends only on that of the ratio
\frac{\theta_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}
.
Let us take a moment to understand the limiting behavior of the statistic
\widetilde{W_{n}}
and its dependence on
\theta_{n},\sigma_{n}
.

1.

If

6.2
Student’s T-Statistic Kernel

The following example is of particular interest due to the appearance of both positive and negative
\lambda_{k}
.

Let
X,X_{1},\cdots,X_{n}
be IID with
\mathbb{E}[X]=0,\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]=1
,
\mathbb{E}[X^{3}]=\gamma^{3}
,
\mathbb{E}[X^{4}]=\kappa^{4}<\infty
and let
h(x,y)=xy^{2}+x^{2}y
. Then:

U_{n}=\frac{1}{{n\choose 2}}\sum_{i<j}X_{i}X_{j}^{2}+X_{i}^{2}X_{j}.

This statistic is closely related to Student’s T-statistic and self-normalized sums in general and is one of the main examples discussed in
2
. Without loss of generality, we will assume that
\gamma^{3}\geq 0
for the remainder of the analysis. The case
\gamma^{3}<0
is entirely analogous.

By the calculations of Section
S.8
in the appendix, we obtain:

H_{1}(x)=\mathbb{E}[xX^{2}+x^{2}X]=x,

and:

H_{2}(x,y)=h(x,y)-H_{1}(x)-H_{1}(y)=xy^{2}+x^{2}y-x-y=x(y^{2}-1)+y(x^{2}-1).

For notational brevity, let us define:

\xi=\kappa^{4}-1.

In this new notation, the coefficients
a_{k}
are given by:

a_{1}=\frac{\left(2\left(1+\frac{2\gamma^{3}}{\xi^{1/2}}\right)\right)^{1/2}}{2},\quad a_{2}=\frac{\left(2\left(1-\frac{2\gamma^{3}}{\xi^{1/2}}\right)\right)^{1/2}}{2}.

Note that
a_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2}=1
.

The coefficients
b_{k}
are given by:

b_{1}=\gamma^{3}+\xi^{1/2},\quad b_{2}=\gamma^{3}-\xi^{1/2}.

Thus, the corresponding Gaussian approximant is:

\displaystyle\widetilde{W_{n}}=\frac{2a_{1}}{\sqrt{n}}Z_{1}+\frac{2a_{2}}{\sqrt{n}}Z_{2}+\frac{b_{1}}{n}(Z_{1}^{2}-1)+\frac{b_{2}}{n}(Z_{2}^{2}-1).

As before, we examine the normalized statistic
n^{1/2}\widetilde{W_{n}}
, which is given by

n^{1/2}\widetilde{W_{n}}=2a_{1}Z_{1}+2a_{2}Z_{2}+\frac{b_{1}}{n^{1/2}}(Z_{1}^{2}-1)+\frac{b_{2}}{n^{1/2}}(Z_{2}^{2}-1).

Note that the statistic with normalization by
n

n\widetilde{W_{n}}=\frac{2a_{1}}{\sqrt{n}}Z_{1}+\frac{2a_{2}}{\sqrt{n}}Z_{2}+b_{1}(Z_{1}^{2}-1)+b_{2}(Z_{2}^{2}-1)

cannot have a nontrivial distributional limit as
a_{1},a_{2}
are fixed nonzero constants. Thus, we do not consider its asymptotic behavior.

Moving to the notation of Theorem
1
, we have:

\mu_{1}=2a_{1},\quad\mu_{2}=2a_{2},\quad\lambda_{1}=\frac{b_{1}}{n^{1/2}},\quad\lambda_{2}=\frac{b_{2}}{n^{1/2}}.

We compute:

\Lambda_{1}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}(b_{1}^{2}+b_{2}^{2})=\frac{1}{n}\left[\left(\gamma^{3}+\xi^{1/2}\right)^{2}+\left(\gamma^{3}-\xi^{1/2}\right)^{2}\right]=\frac{2(\gamma^{6}+\xi)}{n},

and

\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}=\left((2a_{1})^{2}+(2a_{2})^{2}\right)=4.

Finally:

\frac{1}{p_{1}}=\frac{\lambda_{1}^{2}}{\Lambda_{1}^{2}}=\frac{b_{2}^{2}}{2(\gamma^{6}+\xi)}=\frac{(\gamma^{3}+\xi^{1/2})^{2}}{2(\gamma^{6}+\xi)}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\gamma^{3}\xi^{1/2}}{2(\gamma^{6}+\xi)},

\frac{1}{p_{2}}=\frac{\lambda_{2}^{2}}{\Lambda_{1}^{2}}=\frac{b_{2}^{2}}{2(\gamma^{6}+\xi)}=\frac{(\gamma^{3}-\xi^{1/2})^{2}}{2(\gamma^{6}+\xi)}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\gamma^{3}\xi^{1/2}}{2(\gamma^{6}+\xi)}.

Proof.

The proof breaks naturally into several steps.

1.

The Convergence of and Characteristic Function of
S

We begin by observing that we may make sense of the random series

S(\omega)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}(Z_{k}^{2}(\omega)-1)+\mu_{k}Z_{k}(\omega)

in the pointwise (a.s.) sense, as it converges for
\mathbb{P}

by Kolmogorov’s two-series theorem. Indeed:

\mathbb{E}\left[\lambda_{k}(Z_{k}^{2}-1)+\mu_{k}Z_{k}\right]=0

And:

\mathrm{Var}(\lambda_{k}(Z_{k}^{2}-1)+\mu_{k}Z_{k})=\mathbb{E}[(\lambda_{k}Z_{k}^{2}+\mu_{k}Z_{k})^{2}]-\lambda_{k}^{2}=2\lambda_{k}^{2}+\mu_{k}^{2}

which are both summable by our assumption.

Turning towards the estimation of
Q_{S}
, we apply Lemma 1.16 of
16
: for any real-valued random variable
X
with characteristic function
\phi_{X}(t):=\mathbb{E}[\exp(itX)]
we

Q_{X}(\varepsilon)\leq\left(\frac{96}{95}\right)^{2}\varepsilon\int_{-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}|\phi_{X}(t)|\mathrm{d}t\leq 2.2\varepsilon\int_{-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}|\phi_{X}(t)|\mathrm{d}t.

In our case, we have with
X=S
that:

\displaystyle|\phi_{S}(t)|

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(it\left[\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}Z_{k}^{2}+\mu_{k}Z_{k}\right]\right)\right]\right|

(31)

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\lim_{n\to\infty}\exp\left(it\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}Z_{k}^{2}+\mu_{k}Z_{k}\right)\right]\right|

(32)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(it\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}Z_{k}^{2}+\mu_{k}Z_{k}\right)\right]\right|

(33)

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{+\infty}|\phi_{\lambda_{k}(Z^{2}-1)+\mu Z}(t)|

(34)

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\left(-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{2\mu_{k}^{2}t^{2}}{1+4\lambda_{k}^{2}t^{2}}\right)}{\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}(1+4\lambda_{k}^{2}t^{2})^{1/4}}.

(35)

Here, we used the dominated convergence theorem to interchange the limit and expectation, and the fact that the characteristic function of an independent sum is the product of those of the summands. Finally, in the last equality, we used that:

|\phi_{\lambda(Z^{2}-1)+\mu Z}(t)|=\frac{\exp\left(\frac{-2\mu^{2}t^{2}}{1+4\lambda^{2}t^{2}}\right)}{(1+4\lambda^{2}t^{2})^{1/4}},

as can be verified by a direct calculation.

We thus must estimate:

\int_{-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}|\phi_{S}(t)|\mathrm{d}t=\int_{-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\frac{\exp\left(-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{2\mu_{k}^{2}t^{2}}{1+4\lambda_{k}^{2}t^{2}}\right)}{\prod_{k=1}^{n}(1+4\lambda_{k}^{2}t^{2})^{1/4}}\mathrm{d}t=2\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\frac{\exp\left(-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{2\mu_{k}^{2}t^{2}}{1+4\lambda_{k}^{2}t^{2}}\right)}{\prod_{k=1}^{n}(1+4\lambda_{k}^{2}t^{2})^{1/4}}\mathrm{d}t.

we obtain:

\exp\left(-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\frac{2\mu_{k}^{2}}{4\Lambda_{1}^{2}}}{1+\frac{\lambda_{k}^{2}}{\Lambda_{1}^{2}}}\right)=\exp\left(-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\frac{1}{2}\mu_{k}^{2}}{\Lambda_{1}^{2}+\lambda_{k}^{2}}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4}\frac{\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\Lambda_{1}^{2}}\right).

Hence for
\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{1}}\leq t\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
:

|\phi_{S}(t)|\leq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4}\frac{\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\Lambda_{1}^{2}}\right)\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1+4\lambda_{k}^{2}t^{2})^{1/4}}.

So that:

\mathrm{II}\leq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4}\frac{\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\Lambda_{1}^{2}}\right)\int_{\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{1}}}^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{(1+4\lambda_{k}^{2}t^{2})^{1/4}}\mathrm{d}t.

Applying Lemma
1
to the integral on the right hand side gives

\mathrm{II}\leq 6\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4}\frac{\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\Lambda_{1}^{2}}\right)\mathcal{I}(\varepsilon,\lambda);

(43)

which completes the estimation of
\mathrm{II}
.

5.

Putting Everything Together:

Plugging in the bounds (
42
) and (
43
) respectively for
\mathrm{I},\mathrm{II}
into (
40
) and replacing different universal constants by their maxima gives

\displaystyle Q_{S}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\leq 4.4\varepsilon\left[\left(\frac{\pi}{2\Lambda_{1}^{2}+\frac{\left\|\mu\right\|_{2^{2}}}{12}}\right)^{1/2}+6\mathbbm{1}{\{\varepsilon\leq 2\Lambda_{1}\}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4}\frac{\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\Lambda_{1}^{2}}\right){\mathcal{I}(\varepsilon,\lambda)}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 4.4\varepsilon\left[\frac{7}{(24\Lambda_{1}^{2}+\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2})^{1/2}}+6\mathbbm{1}{\{\varepsilon\leq 2\Lambda_{1}\}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4}\frac{\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\Lambda_{1}^{2}}\right){6\mathcal{I}(\varepsilon,\lambda)}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 35\varepsilon\left[\frac{1}{(\Lambda_{1}^{2}+\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2})^{1/2}}+\mathbbm{1}{\{\varepsilon\leq 2\Lambda_{1}\}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4}\frac{\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\Lambda_{1}^{2}}\right)\mathcal{I}(\varepsilon,\lambda)\right].

which is the desired bound of Theorem
1
.

∎

8
Conclusion and Future Work

In this note, we obtained estimates for the concentration function of a Gaussian quadratic form, improving upon and generalizing the existing estimates in the literature. We also provided applications of our bounds to the limiting distributions of certain U-statistics. Continuing in this direction, several natural avenues for future work arise, which we describe below:

1.

Simplified bounds and analysis
:
Handling the chi-square portion of our bound seems to involve a large amount of casework, and it would be interesting to see if a more unified approach exists to obtain such estimates. In addition, our bounds do not recover optimal rates in a special case where
\lambda_{k}\geq 0
for all
k\geq 1
. It would be interesting to see if this can be taken into account without any cumbersome casework.

2.

Berry-Esseen bounds for second order U-statistics
: Current proofs of Berry-Esseen Bounds for U-statistics use simpler, but ultimately, suboptimal bounds for the concentration function of quadratic forms. For example,
13
proves an estimate for the Kolmogorov metric using the celebrated Carbery-Wright inequality. Although powerful and general, it suffers from the drawback that it always provides a rate of
\varepsilon^{1/2}
for quadratic forms.
18
provides rates for degenerate U-statistics by estimating the concentration function of the sum with simply that of the largest term. Thus, to our knowledge, no work has used a concentration function estimate with a rate other than
\varepsilon^{1/2}
, and we plan to explore how our new estimate can provide improved convergence rates in the Kolmogorov metric.

3.

Extension to higher order U-statistics
: Our current estimates are for Gaussian quadratic forms, and these are precisely the limiting distributions for second order U-statistics. In general, for an
m
-th order
U
-statistic, the appropriate limit distribution is a Gaussian polynomial of order
m
. Such random variables do not admit closed-form expressions for the characteristic function; making it difficult for us to derive such estimates directly. However, in this regard, the technique of
symmetrization
, introduced in
11
for quadratic forms in independent random variables and later extended by (for example)
19
for higher order polynomials, can be helpful in estimating such quantities. In particular, when estimating the characteristic function, such results allow us to replace a nonlinear expression in the Gaussian random variables by a linear one, after appropriately symmetrizing. Thus, a further line of work could consist of proving analogous estimates for the concentration function to what we have shown here using Fourier-Analytic techniques, and subsequently using these to obtain sharper Berry-Esseen bounds for higher order
U
-statistics.

4.

A Complete Set of Lower Bounds
: All of our estimates are currently upper bounds, and in certain cases, we obtain matching lower bounds. For example, in the case
{1}/{p_{1}}\leq{1}/{3}
, we obtain rates that match those of Theorem
2
of
6
, which are in turn two sided bounds. Our computations in section
S.3.2
also show that in the special case of
\lambda_{1}=1,\lambda_{2}=-1
and
\mu=0
, we obtain a matching lower bound for
\varepsilon
taken sufficiently small. To obtain lower bounds in all regimes of
\mu,\lambda
would be an interesting line of future work.

Appendix to “On the Lévy concentration function of Gaussian quadratic forms with applications to second order U-statistics”

S.1
The Kolmogorov-Rogozin Inequality

Our initial approach for obtaining such estimates was to use the Kolmogorov-Rogozin inequality. It bounds the concentration function of a sum of independent random variables in terms of the concentration function of the summands. A version given in
10
states that for
\{X_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
independent and
S=\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}X_{k}
(the series interpreted
\mathbb{P}
a.s.), we have that:

\displaystyle Q_{S}(\varepsilon)\lesssim\varepsilon\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\inf_{0\leq\varepsilon_{k}\leq\varepsilon}\varepsilon_{k}^{2}D(\widetilde{X_{k}},\varepsilon_{k})\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sup_{0\leq\varepsilon_{k}\leq\varepsilon}\varepsilon_{k}^{2}(1-Q_{\widetilde{X_{k}}}(\varepsilon))}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sup_{0\leq\varepsilon_{k}\leq\varepsilon}\varepsilon_{k}^{2}(1-Q_{{{X_{k}}}}(\varepsilon))}};

where here
D
denotes the following truncated second moment:

D(X,c)=\mathbb{E}\left[|X|^{2}\mathbbm{1}{\{|X|\leq c\}}\right]+c^{2}\mathbb{P}(|X|>c),

and
\widetilde{X}
is a symmetrized version of
X
, e.g.
\widetilde{X}=X-X^{\prime}
where
X^{\prime}
is an independent copy of
X
. In general, the easiest estimates to apply are the two rightmost, which require only knowledge of
Q_{\widetilde{X_{k}}}
or
Q_{X_{k}}
for individual random variables
X_{k}
. We always have that
Q_{\widetilde{X}}\leq Q_{X}
, which gives us the second inequality above. However, sometimes
Q_{\widetilde{X}}
may be difficult to estimate compared to
Q_{X}
and so the second inequality may be used. Prior to applying the inequality, let us also note that if we have an upper bound for the concentration function, e.g.:

Q_{X_{k}}(\varepsilon)\leq M_{k}(\varepsilon)

then the inequality holds with
M_{k}
in place of
Q_{X_{k}}
, that is:

Q_{S}(\varepsilon)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sup_{0\leq\varepsilon_{k}\leq\varepsilon}\varepsilon_{k}^{2}(1-M_{k}(\varepsilon))}};

which is how the inequality would be applied in practice, as the exact form
Q_{X_{K}}(\varepsilon)
is almost never known but can usually be bounded from above without difficulty.

To demonstrate the deficiency of the inequality for our applications, we show it yields poor bounds in the case of sums of central chi-square random variables, i.e. we consider:

S=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}(Z_{k}^{2}-1).

Although matching two sided bounds have already been proven in this case by
6
, it is instructive and straightforward to apply the inequality in this case as
Q_{X_{k}}(\varepsilon)
has a simple upper bound. Indeed,
X_{k}=\lambda_{k}(Z_{k}^{2}-1)
, and by translation invariance,
Q_{X_{k}}(\varepsilon)=Q_{\lambda_{k}Z_{k}^{2}}(\varepsilon)=Q_{\mathbb{Z}_{k}^{2}}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{|\lambda_{k}|}\right)
. For any
x\in\mathbb{R}
we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(x\leq Z_{k}^{2}<x+\frac{\varepsilon}{|\lambda_{k}|}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{x}^{x+\frac{\varepsilon}{|\lambda_{k}|}}\frac{1}{z^{1/2}}\exp(-z/2)\mathbf{1}\left\{x\geq 0\right\}\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\frac{\varepsilon}{|\lambda_{k}|}}\frac{1}{z^{1/2}}\exp(-z/2)\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{1/2}}{|\lambda_{k}|^{1/2}}\right).

Hence, we have the estimate:

Q_{X_{k}}(\varepsilon)\leq\min\left\{1,2\frac{\varepsilon^{1/2}}{|\lambda_{k}|^{1/2}}\right\}.

Thus, for each
k
we must compute:

\sup_{0\leq\varepsilon_{k}\leq\varepsilon}\varepsilon_{k}^{2}\left(1-\min\left\{1,2\frac{\varepsilon_{k}^{1/2}}{|\lambda_{k}|^{1/2}}\right\}\right).

For the bound to not trivialize (e.g. for the supremum to be nonzero), we would need for all
k
that:

2\frac{\varepsilon_{k}^{1/2}}{|\lambda_{k}|^{1/2}}<1\implies\varepsilon_{k}<\frac{|\lambda_{k}|}{4}.

Note that we also have the constraint
\varepsilon_{k}\leq\varepsilon
.
Thus, we must compute:

\sup_{0\leq\varepsilon_{k}\leq\min\left\{\varepsilon,\frac{|\lambda_{k}|}{4}\right\}}\varepsilon_{k}^{2}\left(1-2\frac{\varepsilon_{k}^{1/2}}{|\lambda_{k}|^{1/2}}\right).

We consider the function
x^{2}\left(1-2\frac{x^{1/2}}{|\lambda_{k}|^{1/2}}\right)
. The function is
0
for
x>0
and positive for
x
small, so any maxima be identified by computing critical points. We differentiate and find the critical point:

x^{*}=\frac{4}{25}|\lambda_{k}|.

Note that
\frac{4}{25}|\lambda_{k}|<\frac{|\lambda_{k}|}{4}
, so that all that remains to be seen is whether
\frac{4}{25}|\lambda_{k}|<\varepsilon
. Assuming that
\varepsilon>\frac{4}{25}|\lambda_{k}|
, we obtain the value:

\left(\frac{4}{25}|\lambda_{k}|\right)^{2}\frac{1}{5}=\frac{16}{3125}|\lambda_{k}|^{2}.

On the other hand, if
\varepsilon
is smaller than
\frac{4}{25}|\lambda_{k}|
this value, then the maximum is achieved at
\varepsilon
as this is an increasing function to the left of the critical point. Thus, we obtain:

\sup_{0\leq\varepsilon_{k}\leq\min\left\{\varepsilon,\frac{|\lambda_{k}|}{4}\right\}}\varepsilon_{k}^{2}\left(1-2\frac{\varepsilon_{k}^{1/2}}{|\lambda_{k}|^{1/2}}\right)=\begin{cases}\frac{16}{3125}|\lambda_{k}^{2}|&\varepsilon>\frac{4}{25}|\lambda_{k}|,\\
\varepsilon^{2}\left(1-2\frac{\varepsilon^{1/2}}{|\lambda_{k}|^{1/2}}\right)&\varepsilon\leq\frac{4}{25}|\lambda_{k}|.\end{cases}

The sequence
|\lambda_{k}|\to 0
and is monotone, so there exists some
K_{\varepsilon}
for which
\varepsilon\leq\frac{4}{25}|\lambda_{k}|
for
k\leq K_{\varepsilon}
and
\varepsilon>\frac{4}{25}|\lambda_{k}|
for
k>K_{\varepsilon}
. Hence, we obtain a bound of the form:

\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{K_{\varepsilon}}\varepsilon^{2}\left(1-2\frac{\varepsilon^{1/2}}{|\lambda_{k}|^{1/2}}\right)+\frac{16}{3125}\Lambda_{K_{\varepsilon}+1}^{2}}}.

To see the issues with this bound, we consider the case when presented a sum with finitely many terms, say
N
terms so that
\lambda_{k}=0
for all
k>N+1
and
\lambda_{k}\neq 0
for
1\leq k\leq N
. Then, for any
\varepsilon\leq\frac{4}{25}|\lambda_{N}|
we obtain the lower bound:

\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(1-2\frac{\varepsilon^{1/2}}{|\lambda_{k}|^{1/2}}\right)}}\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}.

This is simply because
1-2\frac{e^{1/2}}{|\lambda_{k}|^{1/2}}\leq 1
for this value of
\varepsilon
. Thus, in these cases, the Kolmogorov-Rogozin inequality provides the correct rate in
N
, the number of summands, but does not provide the correct rate in terms of
\varepsilon
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
for fixed
N
. Indeed, all terms in the sum are absolutely continuous random variables and so as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
we should also have
Q_{S}(\varepsilon)\to 0
. However, the Kolmogorov Rogozin inequality gives us a bound which is constant order as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
.

S.2
The Carbery-Wright Inequality

Theorem 8 of
8
is a concentration function estimate for polynomials of a log-concave random variable. Specializing the result in our case to Gaussian quadratic forms, e.g. as is done in
13
, it states that

Q_{S}(\varepsilon)\leq\frac{C\varepsilon^{1/2}}{\mathrm{Var}(S)^{1/4}}

(E.1)

for some universal constant
C>0
.
The Carbery-Wright inequality is powerful due to its impressive generality and ease of use – it provides a bound for polynomials of
any
degree and with
any
log-concave random variable as input. This generality can also lead to suboptimal bounds in simple cases. In our setting, we have that

\mbox{Var}(S)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left[\lambda_{k}^{2}\mathbb{E}[(Z_{k}^{2}-1)^{2}]+\mu_{k}^{2}\mathbb{E}[Z_{k}^{2}]\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(\mu_{k}^{2}+3\lambda_{k}^{2})=\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}+3\|\lambda\|_{2}^{2}.

Hence, recalling the notation of (
13
) this gives a bound of the form
Q_{S}(\varepsilon)\leq CQ_{G}^{1/2}(\varepsilon)
. In particular, such a bound always gives a rate of
\varepsilon^{1/2}
when it is possible in many cases to obtain the faster rate of
\varepsilon
.

S.3
Density and Concentration Function of a Symmetrized chi-square Random Variable

Here, we compute the density of
Z_{1}^{2}-Z_{2}^{2}
where
Z_{1},Z_{2}
are independent,
{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)
random variables. Although this is a straightforward computation, we provide it here for future reference as we could not find it elsewhere. We also provide a lower bound for the concentration function, showing that it is of order
\varepsilon\log(1/\varepsilon)
for
\varepsilon
sufficiently small. This provides evidence for the optimality of our bounds in Theorems
1
and
2
in the
p_{1}=2
case.

S.3.1
Density Computation

We have:

f_{Z^{2}}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{1/2}}\frac{1}{x^{1/2}}\exp(-x/2)\mathbf{1}_{x>0}.

Thus:

\displaystyle f_{Z_{1}^{2}-Z_{2}^{2}}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{Z^{2}}(x-z)f_{Z^{2}}(-z)dz

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\min(0,x)}\frac{1}{(x-z)^{1/2}(-z)^{1/2}}\exp(-(x-z)/2)\exp(z/2)\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp(-x/2)}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\min(0,x)}\frac{1}{(x-z)^{1/2}(-z)^{1/2}}\exp(z)\mathrm{d}z.

As this density must be symmetric, without loss of generality we take
x>0
so that the
\min(0,x)=0
. We change variables
z=xu
and then
v=-u
to obtain:

\displaystyle\frac{\exp(-x/2)}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\frac{1}{(x-z)^{1/2}(-z)^{1/2}}\exp(z)\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp(-x/2)}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\frac{1}{(1-u)^{1/2}u^{1/2}}\exp(xu)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp(-x/2)}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{(1+v)^{1/2}v^{1/2}}\exp(-xv)\mathrm{d}v.

This integral is finite for all non-zero
x
. However, as
x\to 0^{+}
, the monotone convergence theorem gives:

\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}f_{Z_{1}^{2}-Z_{2}^{2}}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{(1+u)^{1/2}u^{1/2}}\mathrm{d}u=+\infty.

Showing that the density is unbounded and that the concentration function cannot behave linearly as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
.

S.3.2
Concentration Function Lower Bound

With this formula in hand, we provide a lower bound for the concentration function by lower bounding
\mathbb{P}(0<Z_{1}^{2}-Z_{2}^{2}\leq\varepsilon)
. More concretely, we will show that for
\varepsilon<\exp\left(-\frac{\exp(1)}{2}\right)
:

\mathbb{P}(0<Z_{1}^{2}-Z_{2}^{2}\leq\varepsilon)\geq\frac{1}{4\pi}\varepsilon\log\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right).

For
\varepsilon>0
, we compute:

\displaystyle(2\pi)\mathbb{P}(0<Z_{1}^{2}-Z_{2}^{2}\leq\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\left[\exp\left(-x/2\right)\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{\exp(-xv)}{(1+v)^{1/2}v^{1/2}}\mathrm{d}v\right]\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left[\frac{1}{(1+v)^{1/2}v^{1/2}}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\exp\left(-x/2-xv\right)\mathrm{d}x\right]\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{(v^{2}+v)^{1/2}}\frac{1-\exp\left(\left(-v+\frac{1}{2}\right)\varepsilon\right)}{v+\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}v.

with the interchange of integrals being justified by Tonelli’s theorem. Completing the square in the denominator gives that the above is:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{(v^{2}+v)}\frac{1-\exp\left(\left(-v+\frac{1}{2}\right)\varepsilon\right)}{v+\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{\left[\left(v+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\right]^{1/2}}\frac{1-\exp\left(\left(-v+\frac{1}{2}\right)\varepsilon\right)}{v+\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=\int_{1/2}^{+\infty}\frac{1-\exp\left(-u\varepsilon\right)}{u\left[u^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\right]^{1/2}}\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\int_{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}^{+\infty}\frac{1-\exp(-z)}{z\left[\frac{z^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}-\frac{1}{4}\right]^{1/2}}\mathrm{d}z.

By Taylor’s theorem, for
z
in the interval
[0,c]
, we have the estimate:

\exp(-z)\leq 1-z+\exp(c)\frac{z^{2}}{2}.

Take
c
such that
\frac{c}{\varepsilon}>1/2
e.g.
c>\varepsilon/2
.
We may truncate the integral to obtain:

\displaystyle\int_{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}^{+\infty}\frac{1-\exp(-z)}{z\left[\frac{z^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}-\frac{1}{4}\right]^{1/2}}\mathrm{d}z\geq\int_{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}^{c}\frac{z-\exp(c)\frac{z^{2}}{2}}{z\left[\frac{z^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}-\frac{1}{4}\right]^{1/2}}\mathrm{d}z=\int_{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}^{c}\frac{1}{\left[\frac{z^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}-\frac{1}{4}\right]^{1/2}}\mathrm{d}z-\exp(c)\int_{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}^{c}\frac{\frac{z^{2}}{2}}{z\left[\frac{z^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}-\frac{1}{4}\right]^{1/2}}\mathrm{d}z.

For the first term:

\int_{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}^{c}\frac{1}{\left[\frac{z^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}-\frac{1}{4}\right]^{1/2}}\mathrm{d}z=\varepsilon\int_{1/2}^{c/\varepsilon}\frac{1}{\left(u^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\right)^{1/2}}\mathrm{d}u=\varepsilon\log\left(2\left[\frac{c}{\varepsilon}+\left(\frac{c^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}-\frac{1}{4}\right)^{1/2}\right]\right).

For the second term, we compute:

\displaystyle\int_{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}^{c}\frac{\frac{z^{2}}{2}}{z\left[\frac{z^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}-\frac{1}{4}\right]^{1/2}}\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\int_{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}^{c}\frac{z}{2\left[\frac{z^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}-\frac{1}{4}\right]^{1/2}}\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{2}\int_{1/2}^{c/\varepsilon}\frac{u}{2\left(u^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\right)^{1/2}}\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{2}\int_{1/4}^{c^{2}/\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{1}{4\left(z-\frac{1}{4}\right)^{1/2}}\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\left[\left(\frac{c^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}-\frac{1}{4}\right)^{1/2}\right].

Adding the results, we obtain the estimate:

\varepsilon\log\left(2\left[\frac{c}{\varepsilon}+\left(\frac{c^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}-\frac{1}{4}\right)^{1/2}\right]\right)-\exp(c)\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\left[\left(\frac{c^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}-\frac{1}{4}\right)^{1/2}\right].

To simplify this estimate a bit, we have:

\varepsilon\log\left(2\left[\frac{c}{\varepsilon}+\left(\frac{c^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}-\frac{1}{4}\right)^{1/2}\right]\right)\geq\varepsilon\log\left(\frac{c}{\varepsilon}\right),

and:

\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\left[\left(\frac{c^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}-\frac{1}{4}\right)^{1/2}\right]\leq c\frac{\varepsilon}{4}.

Assume without loss of generality that
\varepsilon<1
. Then, we may take
c=1
(for example) and obtain the estimate:

\displaystyle\varepsilon\log\left(2\left[\frac{c}{\varepsilon}+\left(\frac{c^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}-\frac{1}{4}\right)^{1/2}\right]\right)-\exp(c)\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\left[\left(\frac{c^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}-\frac{1}{4}\right)^{1/2}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\varepsilon\log\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)-\varepsilon\frac{\exp(1)}{4}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\left[\log\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)-\frac{\exp(1)}{4}\right].

Hereafter, we restrict to
\varepsilon
such that:

\varepsilon<\exp\left(-\frac{\exp(1)}{2}\right).

For such
\varepsilon
, we have:

\log\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)-\frac{\exp(1)}{4}>\frac{1}{2}\log\left(`\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right).

Hence:

\varepsilon\left[\log\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)-\frac{\exp(1)}{4}\right]\geq\frac{1}{2}\left[\varepsilon\log\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)\right].

Dividing both sides by the normalizing constant
2\pi
provides the desired estimate.

S.4
Recovering the Bounds of Theorem 2 of
6

In this section, we show that our bounds imply the concentration function estimate obtained from Theorem 2 of
6
, up to universal constant factors. As we recover their estimate under weaker assumptions, we can consider our result a strengthening of theirs, modulo worse constants.

Proof.

Strictly speaking, to apply Theorem 2 of
6
exactly as stated, we must assume that we have
\lambda_{1}^{2}\leq\frac{\Lambda_{1}^{2}}{3}
, i.e.
\frac{1}{p_{1}}\leq\frac{1}{3}
or
p_{1}\geq 3
and that
\lambda\geq 0
. Furthermore, their result applies only to finite sums. However, we emphasize that these assumptions are not necessary to apply our result.

To apply their estimate to our case, we use their notation and set
a_{k}=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mu_{k}}{\lambda_{k}}
. If we define:

W_{a}:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}(Z_{k}-a_{k})^{2},

(E.2)

then we have:

\displaystyle W_{a}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}Z_{k}^{2}+\mu_{k}Z_{k}+\frac{1}{4}\frac{\mu_{k}^{2}}{\lambda_{k}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}(Z_{k}^{2}-1)+\mu_{k}Z_{k}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}+\frac{1}{4}\frac{\mu_{k}^{2}}{\lambda_{k}^{2}}

\displaystyle=S+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}+\frac{1}{4}\frac{\mu_{k}^{2}}{\lambda_{k}^{2}}.

Theorem
2
of
6
states that:

\left\|f_{W_{a}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\frac{2}{\left(\Lambda_{1}^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}^{2}a_{k}^{2}\right)^{1/2}}.

(E.3)

Because
W_{a}=S+C
where
C
is a constant, necessarily
\left\|f_{Y}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}=\left\|f_{W_{a}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}
. Applying this fact gives an estimate

Q_{S}(\varepsilon)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{\left(\Lambda_{1}^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}^{2}a_{k}^{2}\right)^{1/2}}.

Substituting for
a_{k}
gives:

Q_{S}(\varepsilon)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{\left(\Lambda_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mu_{k}^{2}\right)^{1/2}}=\frac{2\varepsilon}{\left(4\Lambda_{1}^{2}+\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}}:=B(\varepsilon).

(E.4)

Let
A(\varepsilon)
denote our bound from Theorem (
2
). To show that our bound recovers theirs, we must show that there exists a universal constant
m>0
for which:

B(\varepsilon)\geq mA(\varepsilon).

First, we observe:

B(\varepsilon)\geq\frac{2\varepsilon}{\left(4\Lambda_{1}^{2}+4\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}}=\frac{\varepsilon}{\left(\Lambda_{1}^{2}+\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}}.

Recalling Theorem
2
, our bound is of the form:

35\varepsilon\left[\left(\frac{1}{\Lambda_{1}^{2}+\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}}\right)^{1/2}+\mathbbm{1}{\{\varepsilon\leq 2\Lambda_{1}\}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4}\frac{\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\Lambda_{1}^{2}}\right)\mathcal{J}(\varepsilon,\lambda)\right].

(E.5)

The first term in square brackets is exactly in the form of the bound given in (
E.4
). For the second term,
\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4}\frac{\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\Lambda_{1}^{2}}\right)\mathcal{J}(\varepsilon,\lambda)
, we have
\frac{1}{p_{1}}\leq\frac{1}{3}
, which means that we may apply the bound corresponding to
p_{1}>2
and obtain:

\mathcal{J}(\varepsilon,\lambda)\leq\frac{1}{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p_{1}}}\frac{1}{\Lambda_{1}}\leq\frac{6}{\Lambda_{1}}.

(E.6)

Thus, the second term is bounded by:

6\cdot\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4}\frac{\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\Lambda_{1}^{2}}\right)}{\Lambda_{1}}.

(E.7)

To complete the proof, we will show that there exists a universal constant
C>0
for which:

\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4}\frac{\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\Lambda_{1}^{2}}\right)}{\Lambda_{1}}\leq\frac{C}{\left(\Lambda_{1}^{2}+\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}}.

By nonnegativity of our expressions, we have the equivalence:

\displaystyle\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4}\frac{\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\Lambda_{1}^{2}}\right)}{\Lambda_{1}}\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{\Lambda_{1}^{2}+\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}}}

\displaystyle\iff\left(\Lambda_{1}^{2}+\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\Lambda_{1}^{2}}\right)}{\Lambda_{1}^{2}}\leq C^{2}.

Simplifying gives that
C
must satisfy:

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\Lambda_{1}^{2}}\right)+\frac{\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\Lambda_{1}^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\Lambda_{1}^{2}}\right)\leq C^{2}.

The first term in the sum on the left hand side is bounded by
1
, being the exponential of a negative number. For the second term, the function
x\mapsto x\exp(-x/2)
achieves its maximum value
2\exp(-1)\leq 1
at
x=2
. Thus, the above bound holds for
C^{2}=1+2\exp(-1)<2
. Substituting this into (
E.7
) gives:

\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4}\frac{\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\Lambda_{1}^{2}}\right)\mathcal{J}(\varepsilon,\lambda)\leq\frac{6\cdot 2^{1/2}}{\sqrt{\Lambda_{1}^{2}+\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}}}.

(E.8)

Totaling everything in (
E.5
) gives:

A(\varepsilon)\leq\frac{(35\cdot 6\cdot 2^{1/2})\varepsilon}{\left(\Lambda_{1}^{2}+\left\|\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}}\leq(35\cdot 6\cdot 2^{1/2})B(\varepsilon).

(E.9)

Thus, we obtain the desired estimate with
m=\frac{1}{(35\cdot 6\cdot 2^{1/2})}
.
∎

S.5
Proof of Lemma 1

Proof.

We wish to estimate the integral:

\int_{\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{1}}}^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1+4\lambda_{k}^{2}t^{2})^{1/4}}\mathrm{d}t.

(E.10)

As defined in Section
3
, we set:

\frac{1}{p_{k}}:=\frac{\lambda_{k}^{2}}{\Lambda_{1}^{2}},\quad k\geq 1.

Each
1/p_{k}
describes the total contribution of the
\lambda_{k}
term to the total variance of the sum. We have the following easy to verify properties:

1.

\sum_{k}\frac{1}{p_{k}}=1,

2.

\frac{1}{p_{k}}\geq\frac{1}{p_{k+1}}
or
p_{k}\leq p_{k+1}
(by the fact that
|\lambda_{k}|
are decreasing),

3.

p_{k}\geq k
for all
k
.

Hölder’s inequality with exponents
p_{k}
gives us that:

\int_{\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{1}}}^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1+4\lambda_{k}^{2}t^{2})^{1/4}}\mathrm{d}t\leq\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\int_{\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{1}}}^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\frac{1}{(1+4\lambda_{k}^{2}t^{2})^{p_{k}/4}}\mathrm{d}t\right)^{1/p_{k}}.

For the integral inside parentheses, we make the substitution:

u=1+4\lambda_{k}^{2}t^{2},\implies t=\frac{(u-1)^{1/2}}{2|\lambda_{k}|},\mathrm{d}t=\frac{1}{4|\lambda_{k}|(u-1)^{1/2}}\mathrm{d}u.

(E.11)

Hence, the integral becomes:

\int_{\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{1}}}^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\frac{1}{(1+4\lambda_{k}^{2}t^{2})^{p_{k}/4}}\mathrm{d}t=\frac{1}{4|\lambda_{k}|}\int_{1+\frac{\lambda_{k}^{2}}{\Lambda_{1}^{2}}}^{1+\frac{4\lambda_{k}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}}u^{-p_{k}/4}(u-1)^{-1/2}\mathrm{d}u=\frac{1}{4|\lambda_{k}|}\int_{1+\frac{1}{p_{k}}}^{1+\frac{4\lambda_{k}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}}u^{-p_{k}/4}(u-1)^{-1/2}\mathrm{d}u.

(E.12)

We next search for an estimate of the kind:

(u-1)^{-1/2}\leq Cu^{-1/2},\quad u\in\left[1+\frac{1}{p_{k}},1+\frac{4\lambda_{k}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right],

(E.13)

so that (
E.12
) can be bounded by the integral of
u^{-p_{k}/4-1/2}
times a constant. We observe that the function

\frac{u}{u-1}

is decreasing for
u\geq 1
. Hence, on our interval of integration, we must have:

\frac{u}{u-1}\leq\frac{1+\frac{1}{p_{k}}}{\frac{1}{p_{k}}}\implies(u-1)^{-1/2}\leq\left(\frac{1+\frac{1}{p_{k}}}{\frac{1}{p_{k}}}\right)^{1/2}u^{-1/2}=(p_{k}+1)^{1/2}u^{-1/2}.

Hence:

\frac{1}{4|\lambda_{k}|}\int_{1+\frac{1}{p_{k}}}^{1+\frac{4\lambda_{k}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}}u^{-p_{k}/4}(u-1)^{-1/2}\mathrm{d}u\leq\frac{(p_{k}+1)^{1/2}}{4|\lambda_{k}|}\int_{1+\frac{1}{p_{k}}}^{1+\frac{4\lambda_{k}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}}u^{-(p_{k}/4+1/2)}\mathrm{d}u.

(E.14)

This is equal to:

I_{k}(\varepsilon):=\begin{cases}\frac{(p_{k}+1)^{1/2}}{4|\lambda_{k}|}\frac{1}{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{p_{k}}{4}}\left[\left(1+\frac{4\lambda_{k}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{p_{k}}{4}}-\left(1+\frac{1}{p_{k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{p_{k}}{4}}\right]&\text{if }p_{k}\neq 2,\\
\frac{3^{1/2}}{4|\lambda_{k}|}\left[\log\left(1+\frac{4\lambda_{k}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)-\log\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\right)\right]&\text{if }p_{k}=2.\end{cases}

(E.15)

For later analysis, we write all expressions in terms of
p_{k}
and
\Lambda_{1}
as these are the natural units of the problem. This gives:

I_{k}(\varepsilon)=\begin{cases}\frac{(p_{k}^{2}+p_{k})^{1/2}}{2\Lambda_{1}}\frac{1}{1-\frac{p_{k}}{2}}\left[\left(1+\frac{4\Lambda_{1}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{1}{p_{k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{p_{k}}{2}\right)}-\left(1+\frac{1}{p_{k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{p_{k}}{2}\right)}\right]&\text{if }p_{k}\neq 2,\\
\frac{3^{1/2}2^{1/2}}{4\Lambda_{1}}\left[\log\left(1+\frac{4\Lambda_{1}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)-\log\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\right)\right]&\text{if }p_{k}=2.\end{cases}

(E.16)

Our final bound is thus:

\int_{\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{1}}}^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1+4\lambda_{k}^{2}t^{2})^{1/4}}\mathrm{d}t\leq\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}I_{k}(\varepsilon)^{1/p_{k}}.

(E.17)

To analyze this product, we consider cases (in order of simplicity of analysis), depending on the size of
p_{1}
. We consider the following 3 cases, and show that they provide the respective rates in
\varepsilon
:

1.

p_{1}>2
(Linear in
\varepsilon
)

2.

1\leq p_{1}\leq 2
(Sublinear in
\varepsilon
or Linear in
\varepsilon
if
q_{1}>0
)

3.

p_{1}=2
(Log-Linear in
\varepsilon
or Linear in
\varepsilon
if
q_{1}>0
)

Case 1: (
p_{1}>2
)

In this case,
\frac{\lambda_{1}^{2}}{\Lambda_{1}^{2}}=\frac{1}{p_{1}}<\frac{1}{2}
, e.g. the first term in the sum does not ”dominate” the others. In this case, we obtain the best possible rate in
\varepsilon
(linear), provided
p_{1}
is bounded away from
2
.

By monotonicity, we know that
p_{k}\geq 2
for all
k
as well.
In such a case,
1-p_{k}/2<1
and so
I_{k}
may be written more suggestively:

\frac{(p_{k}^{2}+p_{k})^{1/2}}{2\Lambda_{1}}\frac{1}{\frac{p_{k}}{2}-1}\left[\left(1+\frac{1}{p_{k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{p_{k}}{2}\right)}-\left(1+\frac{4\Lambda_{1}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{1}{p_{k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{p_{k}}{2}\right)}\right].

Using the fact that
1-\frac{p_{k}}{2}<0
and
\frac{4\Lambda_{1}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}>1
, we may estimate the second term in parentheses above as:

\left(1+\frac{4\Lambda_{1}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{1}{p_{k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{p_{k}}{2}\right)}\geq\left(\frac{4\Lambda_{1}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{4\Lambda_{1}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{1}{p_{k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{p_{k}}{2}\right)}=\left(1+\frac{1}{p_{k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{p_{k}}{2}\right)}\left(\frac{4\Lambda_{1}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{p_{k}}{2}\right)}

(E.18)

Applying this inequality and factoring, we obtain the estimate:

\displaystyle\left[\left(1+\frac{1}{p_{k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{p_{k}}{2}\right)}-\left(1+\frac{4\Lambda_{1}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{1}{p_{k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{p_{k}}{2}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{1}{p_{k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{p_{k}}{2}\right)}\left[1-\left(\frac{4\Lambda_{1}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{p_{k}}{2}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left[1-\left(\frac{2\Lambda_{1}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\left(1-\frac{p_{k}}{2}\right)}\right].

On the other hand:

\displaystyle\frac{(p_{k}^{2}+p_{k})^{1/2}}{2\Lambda_{1}}\frac{1}{\frac{p_{k}}{2}-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(1+\frac{1}{p_{k}}\right)^{1/2}}{\Lambda_{1}}\frac{p_{k}}{p_{k}-2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{3/2}\frac{1}{\Lambda_{1}}\frac{p_{k}}{p_{k}-2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{3/2}\frac{1}{\Lambda_{1}}\frac{1}{1-\frac{2}{p_{k}}}

We obtained the term
3/2
simply by substituting
p_{k}=2
into the decreasing function
1+\frac{1}{p_{k}}
.
Thus far, we have shown:

I_{k}(\varepsilon)\leq\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{3/2}\frac{1}{\Lambda_{1}}\frac{1}{1-\frac{2}{p_{k}}}\left[1-\left(\frac{2\Lambda_{1}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\left(1-\frac{p_{k}}{2}\right)}\right].

(E.19)

Taking products and powers to the
1/p_{k}
gives an estimate:

\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}I_{k}(\varepsilon)^{1/p_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{3/2}\frac{1}{\Lambda_{1}}\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{1-\frac{2}{p_{k}}}\right)^{1/p_{k}}\left[1-\left(\frac{2\Lambda_{1}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\left(1-\frac{p_{k}}{2}\right)}\right]^{1/p_{k}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{3/2}\frac{1}{\Lambda_{1}}\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}J_{k}(\varepsilon)^{1/p_{k}},

where we have set:

J_{k}(\varepsilon):=\frac{1}{1-\frac{2}{p_{k}}}\left[1-\left(\frac{2\Lambda_{1}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\left(1-\frac{p_{k}}{2}\right)}\right].

(E.20)

Using that the term in square braces is always less than
1
, we have for
k\geq 3
:

\displaystyle\prod_{k=3}^{\infty}J_{k}(\varepsilon)^{1/p_{k}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{1-\frac{2}{p_{k}}}\right)^{1/p_{k}}\left[1-\left(\frac{2\Lambda_{1}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\left(1-\frac{p_{k}}{2}\right)}\right]^{1/p_{k}}

(E.21)

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{k=3}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{1-\frac{2}{p_{k}}}\right)^{1/p_{k}}

(E.22)

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{k=3}^{+\infty}\left(\frac{1}{1-\frac{2}{p_{3}}}\right)^{1/p_{k}}

(E.23)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{1-\frac{2}{p_{3}}}\right)^{1-\left(\frac{1}{p_{1}}+\frac{1}{p_{2}}\right)}.

(E.24)

We obtained the penultimate inequality by plugging
p_{3}
into the decreasing function
\frac{1}{1-\frac{2}{x}}
and then multiplying over
k
.
As
\frac{1}{p_{3}}\leq\frac{1}{3}
, the above is bounded by:

\left(\frac{1}{1-\frac{2}{3}}\right)^{1-\left(\frac{1}{p_{1}}+\frac{1}{p_{2}}\right)}=3^{1-\left(\frac{1}{p_{1}}+\frac{1}{p_{2}}\right)}\leq 3.

Thus:

\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}I_{k}(\varepsilon)^{1/p_{k}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{3/2}\frac{1}{\Lambda_{1}}\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}J_{k}(\varepsilon)^{1/p_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{3/2}\left[J_{1}(\varepsilon)^{1/p_{1}}J_{2}(\varepsilon)^{1/p_{2}}\right]\prod_{k=3}^{+\infty}J_{k}(\varepsilon)^{1/p_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq 3\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{3/2}\frac{1}{\Lambda_{1}}\left[J_{1}(\varepsilon)^{1/p_{1}}J_{2}(\varepsilon)^{1/p_{2}}\right].

Recalling the definition of
J_{k}(\varepsilon)
from (
E.20
)
gives that the entire product can be bounded by:

\displaystyle 3\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{3/2}\frac{1}{\Lambda_{1}}\left(\frac{1}{1-\frac{2}{p_{1}}}\right)^{1/p_{1}}\left(\frac{1}{1-\frac{2}{p_{2}}}\right)^{1/p_{2}}\left[1-\left(\frac{2\Lambda_{1}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\left(1-\frac{p_{1}}{2}\right)}\right]^{1/p_{1}}\left[1-\left(\frac{2\Lambda_{1}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\left(1-\frac{p_{2}}{2}\right)}\right]^{1/p_{2}}.

Recalling the definitions
A_{p,i}
from the statement of Theorem
1
gives the final estimate:

\displaystyle\frac{3^{5/2}}{2^{3/2}}\frac{1}{\Lambda_{1}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{p_{1}}+\frac{1}{p_{2}}}A_{p,1}^{1/p_{1}}A_{p,2}^{1/p_{2}}\leq\frac{6}{\Lambda_{1}}A_{p,1}^{1/p_{1}}A_{p,2}^{1/p_{2}},

which is the desired estimate.

Case 2: (
1\leq p_{1}<2
)

In such a case, the term corresponding to
\lambda_{1}
”dominates” the remaining terms in the sum. Heuristically, we should expect slower rates in this case. As described in Section 4, we can obtain both sublinear and linear rates in
\varepsilon
in this case. The linear rate is inversely proportional to
\left(\Lambda_{1}\Lambda_{2}\right)^{1/2}
, whereas the sublinear rate is inversely proportional to
\Lambda_{1}
.

Finally, notice that in this case
p_{1}<2
means that
p_{k}\geq 2
for all
k
, as if this were not true we would have
1\geq\frac{1}{p_{1}}+\frac{1}{p_{k}}>\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}=1
for some
k
.

Case 2.1: (Sublinear Concentration)

As
p_{1}<2
, we may write:

I_{1}(\varepsilon)=\frac{(p_{1}^{2}+p_{1})^{1/2}}{2\Lambda_{1}}\frac{1}{1-\frac{p_{1}}{2}}\left[\left(1+\frac{4\Lambda_{1}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{1}{p_{1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{p_{1}}{2}\right)}-\left(1+\frac{1}{p_{1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{p_{1}}{2}\right)}\right].

For the term in square braces, we reason similarly (but essentially in the reverse fashion as
1-\frac{p_{1}}{2}>0
) as we did in the
p_{1}\geq 2
case (see equation (
E.18
)) and obtain:

\displaystyle\left[\left(1+\frac{4\Lambda_{1}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{1}{p_{1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{p_{1}}{2}\right)}-\left(1+\frac{1}{p_{1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{p_{1}}{2}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{1}{p_{1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{p_{1}}{2}\right)}\left[\left(\frac{4\Lambda_{1}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{p_{1}}{2}\right)}-1\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2^{1/2}\left[\left(\frac{2\Lambda_{1}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{1-\frac{p_{1}}{2}}-1\right].

And as before:

\frac{(p_{1}^{2}+p_{1})^{1/2}}{2\Lambda_{1}}\frac{1}{1-\frac{p_{1}}{2}}=\frac{\left(1+\frac{1}{p_{1}}\right)^{1/2}}{\Lambda_{1}}\frac{p_{1}}{2-p_{1}}\leq\frac{2^{1/2}}{\Lambda_{1}}\frac{1}{\frac{2}{p_{1}}-1}.

So in total:

I_{1}(\varepsilon)\leq\frac{2}{\Lambda_{1}}\frac{1}{\frac{2}{p_{1}}-1}\left[\left(\frac{2\Lambda_{1}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{1-\frac{p_{1}}{2}}-1\right]\leq\frac{4}{\Lambda_{1}}\frac{1}{\frac{2}{p_{1}}-1}\left[\left(\frac{2\Lambda_{1}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{1-\frac{p_{1}}{2}}-1\right].

On the other hand, for the remaining
I_{k}(\varepsilon)
, using the fact that
p_{k}\geq 2
and mimicking the approach of the previous case gives:

\displaystyle I_{k}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{3/2}\frac{1}{\Lambda_{1}}\frac{1}{1-\frac{2}{p_{k}}}\left[1-\left(\frac{2\Lambda_{1}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\left(1-\frac{p_{k}}{2}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle:=\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{3/2}\frac{1}{\Lambda_{1}}J_{k}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{6}{\Lambda_{1}}J_{k}(\varepsilon).

By the same argument as in the first case as carried out in equation (
E.21
) we obtain:

\prod_{k=2}^{\infty}I_{k}(\varepsilon)^{1/p_{k}}\leq\left(\frac{6}{\Lambda_{1}}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{p_{1}}}J_{2}(\varepsilon)^{1/p_{2}}.

Putting this together and again recalling the definition of
J
from (
E.20
) gives:

\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}I_{k}(\varepsilon)^{1/p_{k}}

\displaystyle=I_{1}(\varepsilon)^{1/p_{1}}\prod_{k=2}^{\infty}I_{k}(\varepsilon)^{1/p_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{6}{\Lambda_{1}}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{p_{1}}}\left(\frac{6}{\Lambda_{1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p_{1}}}\left[J_{1}(\varepsilon)^{1/p_{1}}J_{2}(\varepsilon)^{1/p_{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{6}{\Lambda_{1}}\left(\frac{1}{\frac{2}{p_{1}}-1}\right)^{1/p_{1}}\left(\frac{1}{1-\frac{2}{p_{2}}}\right)^{1/p_{2}}\left[\left(\frac{2\Lambda_{1}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{1-\frac{p_{1}}{2}}-1\right]\left[1-\left(\frac{2\Lambda_{1}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\left(1-\frac{p_{2}}{2}\right)}\right]^{1/p_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{6}{\Lambda_{1}}B_{p,1}^{1/p_{1}}A_{p,2}^{1/p_{2}}.

which is the desired result.

Case 2.2: (Linear Concentration,
q_{2}>1
i.e.
\lambda_{3}\neq 0
)

In such a case, the
\lambda_{1}
term still dominates the others, however there are enough nonzero terms in the sum to obtain a linear rate in
\varepsilon
. Thus, we need to derive a bound that simultaneously exploits the largeness of
\lambda_{1}
while also including the contribution of smaller
\lambda_{k}
.

To estimate the integral, we have
\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{1}}<\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{2}}\leq\min\left\{\frac{1}{\varepsilon},\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{2}}\right\}
, so similarly to the proof of Theorem
1
(in particular, Step 2 in Section
7
), we divide the region of integration and obtain for any
f
nonnegative and measurable:

\int_{\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{1}}}^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}f(t)\mathrm{d}t\leq\int_{\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{1}}}^{\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{2}}}f(t)\mathrm{d}t+\mathbbm{1}{\{\varepsilon\leq 2\Lambda_{2}\}}\int_{\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{2}}}^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}f(t)\mathrm{d}t.

We define:

\mathrm{I}:=\int_{\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{1}}}^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1+4\lambda_{k}^{2}t^{2})^{1/4}}\mathrm{d}t,\quad\mathrm{II}:=\int_{\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{2}}}^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1+4\lambda_{k}^{2}t^{2})^{1/4}}\mathrm{d}t.

For
\mathrm{I}
, we estimate:

\displaystyle\int_{\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{1}}}^{\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{2}}}\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1+4\lambda_{k}^{2}t^{2})^{1/4}}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{1}}}^{\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{2}}}\frac{1}{(1+4\lambda_{1}^{2}t^{2})^{1/4}}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4|\lambda_{1}|}\int_{1+\frac{1}{p_{1}}}^{1+\frac{\lambda_{1}^{2}}{\Lambda_{2}^{2}}}\frac{1}{u^{1/4}(u-1)^{1/2}}\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(p_{1}^{2}+p_{1})^{1/2}}{4\Lambda_{1}}\int_{1+\frac{1}{p_{1}}}^{1+\frac{\lambda_{1}^{2}}{\Lambda_{2}^{2}}}u^{-3/4}\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\frac{(p_{1}^{2}+p_{1})^{1/2}}{\Lambda_{1}}\left[\left(1+\frac{\lambda_{1}^{2}}{\Lambda_{2}^{2}}\right)^{1/4}-\left(1+\frac{1}{p_{1}}\right)^{1/4}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(p_{1}^{2}+p_{1})^{1/2}}{\Lambda_{1}}\left(1+\frac{1}{p_{1}}\right)^{1/4}\left[\frac{\Lambda_{1}^{1/2}}{\Lambda_{2}^{1/2}}-1\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{6^{1/2}2^{1/4}}{\Lambda_{1}}\left[\frac{\Lambda_{1}^{1/2}}{\Lambda_{2}^{1/2}}-1\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{\Lambda_{1}}\left[\frac{\Lambda_{1}^{1/2}}{\Lambda_{2}^{1/2}}-1\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{(\Lambda_{1}\Lambda_{2})^{1/2}}.

For
\mathrm{II}
, we begin with the estimate:

\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1+4\lambda_{k}^{2}t^{2})^{1/4}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4^{1/4}|\lambda_{1}|^{1/2}t^{1/2}}\prod_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1+4\lambda_{k}^{2}t^{2})^{1/4}}

(E.25)

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=2}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{(1+4\lambda_{k}^{2}t^{2})^{1/4}}\frac{1}{(4^{1/4}|\lambda_{1}|^{1/2}t^{1/2})^{1/q_{k}}}\right).

(E.26)

Applying Hölder’s inequality to the final expression with weights
q_{k}
,
k\geq 2
gives:

\displaystyle\mathrm{II}

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{k=2}^{\infty}\left(\int_{\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{2}}}^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\frac{1}{4^{1/4}|\lambda_{1}|^{1/2}t^{1/2}(1+4\lambda_{k}^{2}t^{2})^{q_{k}/4}}\mathrm{d}t\right)^{1/q_{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4^{1/4}|\lambda_{1}|^{1/2}}\prod_{k=2}^{\infty}\left(\int_{\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{2}}}^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\frac{1}{t^{1/2}(1+4\lambda_{k}^{2}t^{2})^{q_{k}/4}}\mathrm{d}t\right)^{1/q_{k}}.

For the inner integrals, we proceed as we did in the
p_{1}>2
case (see equations (
E.11
), (
E.13
)) and thereafter), making the usual substitution
u=1+4\lambda_{k}^{2}t^{2}
and obtain:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4|\lambda_{k}|}\int_{1+\frac{1}{q_{k}}}^{1+\frac{4\lambda_{k}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}}\frac{1}{\frac{(u-1)^{1/4}}{(2|\lambda_{k}|)^{1/2}}u^{q_{k}/4}}\frac{1}{(u-1)^{1/2}}\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{3/2}|\lambda_{k}|^{1/2}}\int_{1+\frac{1}{q_{k}}}^{1+\frac{4\lambda_{k}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}}u^{-q_{k}/4}(u-1)^{-3/4}\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(q_{k}+1)^{3/4}}{2^{3/2}|\lambda_{k}|^{1/2}}\int_{1+\frac{1}{q_{k}}}^{1+\frac{4\lambda_{k}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}}u^{-q_{k}/4-3/4}\mathrm{d}u.

Rewriting the constant in front in terms of
q_{k}
,
\Lambda_{2}

\frac{(q_{k}+1)^{3/4}}{2^{3/2}\frac{\Lambda_{2}^{1/2}}{q_{k}^{1/4}}}=\frac{(q_{k}^{4/3}+q_{k}^{1/3})^{3/4}}{2^{3/2}\Lambda_{2}^{1/2}}.

Because

q_{k}/4+3/4>1
and so the integrals above can be written as:

\frac{1}{\frac{q_{k}}{4}-\frac{1}{4}}\left[\left(1+\frac{1}{q_{k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}-\frac{q_{k}}{4}}-\left(1+\frac{4\lambda_{k}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}-\frac{q_{k}}{4}}\right]=\frac{4}{q_{k}-1}\left[\left(1+\frac{1}{q_{k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}-\frac{q_{k}}{4}}-\left(1+\frac{4\Lambda_{2}^{2}\frac{1}{q_{k}}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}-\frac{q_{k}}{4}}\right].

Setting

\displaystyle J_{k}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle:=\frac{(q_{k}+1)^{3/4}}{2^{3/2}|\lambda_{k}|^{1/2}}\int_{1+\frac{1}{q_{k}}}^{1+\frac{4\lambda_{k}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}}u^{-q_{k}/4-3/4}\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\frac{4(q_{k}^{4/3}+q_{k}^{1/3})^{3/4}}{2^{3/2}\Lambda_{2}^{1/2}}\frac{1}{1-q_{k}}\left[\left(1+\frac{1}{q_{k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}-\frac{q_{k}}{4}}-\left(1+\frac{4\Lambda_{2}^{2}\frac{1}{q_{k}}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}-\frac{q_{k}}{4}}\right].

we obtain:

\mathrm{II}\leq\frac{1}{4^{1/4}|\lambda_{1}|}\prod_{k=2}^{\infty}J_{k}(\varepsilon)^{1/q_{k}}=\frac{p_{1}^{1/2}}{4^{1/4}\Lambda_{1}}\prod_{k=2}^{\infty}J_{k}(\varepsilon)^{1/q_{k}}.

) and onwards. For the term in square brackets:

So, it follows:

\displaystyle\mathrm{II}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{p_{1}^{1/2}}{4^{1/4}\Lambda_{1}^{1/2}}\prod_{k=2}^{\infty}I_{k}(\varepsilon)^{1/q_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4^{3/4}}{2^{3/4}}\frac{p_{1}^{1/2}}{\Lambda_{1}^{1/2}}\frac{1}{\Lambda_{2}^{1/2}}\frac{1}{1-\frac{1}{q_{2}}}\left[1-\left(\frac{2\Lambda_{2}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{q_{2}}{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{(\Lambda_{1}\Lambda_{2})^{1/2}}\frac{1}{1-\frac{1}{q_{2}}}\left[1-\left(\frac{2\Lambda_{2}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{q_{2}}{2}}\right].

Adding the bounds for

together and replacing universal constants by their maxima/nicer numbers gives:

\frac{3}{(\Lambda_{1}\Lambda_{2})^{1/2}}\left(1+\mathbbm{1}{\{\varepsilon\leq 2\Lambda_{2}\}}\frac{1}{1-\frac{1}{q_{2}}}\left[1-\left(\frac{2\Lambda_{2}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{q_{2}}{2}}\right]\right).

as we desire.

Case 3:(Linear-times-Logarithmic Concentration,

\displaystyle I_{1}(\varepsilon)^{1/p_{1}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{3^{1/2}2^{1/2}}{4\Lambda_{1}}\left[\log\left(1+\frac{4\Lambda_{1}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{1}{2}\right)-\log\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\right)\right]\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{3^{1/2}2^{1/2}}{4\Lambda_{1}}\left[\log\left(\frac{4\Lambda_{1}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left[1+\frac{1}{2}\right]\right)-\log\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\right)\right]\right)^{1/2}

Next, we know that for the remaining
p_{k}
we must have
p_{k}\geq 2
. We briefly break into two short subcases, the first being nearly trivial.

(p_{2}=2)

and
\lambda_{3}

S.7
Phase Transition for the Square of the Mean Statistic

Let
X,X_{1},\cdots,X_{n}\overset{IID}{\sim}P_{n}
with
\mathbb{E}[X]=\theta_{n}
and
\mathbb{E}[(X-\theta_{n})]=\sigma^{2}_{n}
. We consider the
U
-statistic with kernel
h=x-\theta_{n}
, e.g.:

U_{n}:=\frac{1}{{n\choose 2}}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}h(X_{i},X_{j})=\frac{1}{{n\choose 2}}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(X_{i}-\theta_{n})(X_{j}-\theta_{n})

(E.27)

To see where and why a phase-transition in the limiting behavior of this statistic occurs, we decompose
U_{n}
as follows - we first write:

X_{i}X_{j}-\theta_{n}^{2}=(X_{i}-\theta_{n})(X_{j}-\theta_{n})+\theta_{n}(X_{j}-\theta_{n})+(X_{i}-\theta_{n})\theta_{n}+\theta_{n}^{2}-\theta_{n}^{2}

Summing gives:

\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(X_{i}-\theta_{n})(X_{j}-\theta_{n})+\theta_{n}(X_{j}-\theta_{n})+(X_{i}-\theta_{n})\theta_{n}

For the second term:

\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\theta_{n}(X_{j}-\theta_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}\theta_{n}(X_{j}-\theta_{n})=\sum_{j=2}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\theta_{n}(X_{j}-\theta_{n})=\theta_{n}\sum_{j=2}^{n}(j-1)(X_{j}-\theta_{n})

Even more simply, for the third:

\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(X_{i}-\theta_{n})\theta_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}(X_{i}-\theta_{n})\theta_{n}=\theta_{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(n-(i+1))(X_{i}-\theta_{n})=\theta_{n}\sum_{i=2}^{n}(n-i)(X_{i}-\theta_{n})

So:

\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}X_{i}X_{j}=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(X_{i}-\theta_{n})(X_{j}-\theta_{n})+\theta_{n}(n-1)\sum_{k=2}^{n}(X_{k}-\theta_{n})

and finally arrive at:

U_{n}=\underbrace{\frac{1}{{n\choose 2}}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(X_{i}-\theta_{n})(X_{j}-\theta_{n})}_{:=U^{C}_{n}}+\underbrace{\frac{2\theta_{n}}{n}\sum_{k=2}^{n}(X_{k}-\theta_{n})}_{:=U_{n}^{G}}

(E.28)

The second term, being the sum of IID random variables, can be thought of as the Gaussian part of the statistic, while the first term can be thought of as the quadratic/chi-square part.

S.7.0.1
Analysis of Limiting Behavior

Let us examine this bound in some typical scenarios and use some heuristics to understand which regimes lead to Gaussian behavior and which regimes lead to chi-square behavior as
n\to\infty
. Throughout, we make the following two assumptions on the distribution
P_{n}
:

Assumption 1 (CLT):

\frac{1}{n^{1/2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{X_{k}-\theta_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}\underset{D}{\to}{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)

(E.29)

Assumption 2 (LLN):

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{(X_{k}-\theta_{n})^{2}}{\sigma_{n}}\underset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}1

(E.30)

Note that these assumptions both hold true in the classical case when
\theta_{n}=\theta
,
\sigma_{n}=\sigma>0
, e.g. both quantities are fixed.

With these assumptions in hand, we can show that the statistics
n^{1/2}U_{n}
,
nU_{n}
have tractable limits in a variety of cases, much like what occurs in the classical CLT for U-statistics.

Case 1:
\theta_{n}\sigma_{n}\to C_{1}\neq 0
and
\sigma_{n}=o(n^{1/4})

In this case, we will show that
n^{1/2}U_{n}\underset{D}{\to}2C_{1}Z
.

We first show that
n^{1/2}U_{n}^{C}
converges to
0
in probability. We have:

\displaystyle n^{1/2}U_{n}^{C}=\frac{2}{n^{1/2}(n-1)}\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}-\theta_{n}\right)^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\theta_{n})^{2}\right]

(E.31)

For the first term, we write:

\frac{2}{n^{1/2}(n-1)}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}-\theta_{n}\right)^{2}=\frac{2\sigma_{n}^{2}}{n^{1/2}}\left(\frac{1}{(n-1)^{1/2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{X_{i}-\theta_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}\right)^{2}\underset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}0

by the growth rate assumption on
\sigma_{n}
and the fact that the term in parentheses converges in distribution to a Gaussian. For the second term:

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{2}{n^{1/2}(n-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\theta_{n})^{2}\right]=\frac{n\sigma_{n}^{2}}{n^{1/2}(n-1)}\to 0

by the assumption on
\sigma_{n}
. Thus,
n^{1/2}U_{n}^{C}
converges to
0
in probability. For the second term, we have:

n^{1/2}U_{n}^{G}=2\theta_{n}\sigma_{n}\cdot\frac{1}{n^{1/2}}\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{(X_{k}-\theta_{n})}{\sigma_{n}}\underset{D}{\to}2C_{1}Z

as desired.

Case 2:
\theta_{n}\sigma_{n}\to C_{1}\neq 0
and
\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}}{n^{1/2}}\to C_{2}>0

In this case, we will show that
n^{1/2}U_{n}
has a limit of the form:

2C_{1}Z_{1}+2C_{2}(Z_{1}^{2}-1)

e.g. a non-central chi-square random variable.
Repeating the same calculations as above gives us that:

n^{1/2}U_{n}^{C}=\frac{2}{n^{1/2}(n-1)}\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}-\theta_{n}\right)^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\theta_{n})^{2}\right]

Again for the first term:

\frac{2}{n^{1/2}(n-1)}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}-\theta_{n}\right)^{2}=\frac{2\sigma_{n}^{2}}{n^{1/2}}\left(\frac{1}{(n-1)^{1/2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{X_{i}-\theta_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}\right)^{2}\underset{D}{\to}2C_{2}Z^{2}

By assumption and the continuous mapping theorem. For the second term:

\frac{2}{n^{1/2}(n-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\theta_{n})^{2}=\frac{2\sigma_{n}^{2}}{n^{1/2}}\frac{1}{(n-1)}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{(X_{i}-\theta_{n})}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}\underset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}2C_{2}

Finally, for
n^{1/2}U_{n}^{G}
:

n^{1/2}U_{n}^{G}=2\theta_{n}\sigma_{n}\cdot\frac{1}{n^{1/2}}\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{(X_{k}-\theta_{n})}{\sigma_{n}}\underset{D}{\to}2C_{1}Z

Adding these two limits gives us our desired result.

Case 3:
\theta_{n}\sigma_{n}\to 0
and
\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}}{n^{1/2}}\to C_{2}>0

In this case, we will show that
n^{1/2}U_{n}
has a limit of the form:

2C_{2}(Z_{1}^{2}-1)

Repeating the same calculations as above gives us that:

n^{1/2}U_{n}^{C}=\frac{2}{n^{1/2}(n-1)}\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}-\theta_{n}\right)^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\theta_{n})^{2}\right]

Again for the first term:

\frac{2}{n^{1/2}(n-1)}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}-\theta_{n}\right)^{2}=\frac{2\sigma_{n}^{2}}{n^{1/2}}\left(\frac{1}{(n-1)^{1/2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{X_{i}-\theta_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}\right)^{2}\underset{D}{\to}2C_{2}Z^{2}

By assumption and the continuous mapping theorem. For the second term:

\frac{2}{n^{1/2}(n-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\theta_{n})^{2}=\frac{2\sigma_{n}^{2}}{n^{1/2}}\frac{1}{(n-1)}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{(X_{i}-\theta_{n})}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}\underset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}2C_{2}

Finally, for
n^{1/2}U_{n}^{G}
:

n^{1/2}U_{n}^{G}=2\theta_{n}\sigma_{n}\cdot\frac{1}{n^{1/2}}\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{(X_{k}-\theta_{n})}{\sigma_{n}}\underset{D}{\to}0

Thus, we obtain a distributional limit of the form:

2C_{2}(Z^{2}-1)

which is a central chi-square random variable.

S.8
Eigenfunction and Eigenvalue Computations

In this section, we provide diagonalization computations for the two examples of U-Statistics we mention and discuss in our applications section
6
.

S.8.1
Square of the Mean and General Rank-1 U-Statistic

Following the notation of section
6.1
we write

\mathbb{E}[X]:=\theta_{n}:=\theta

and

\mathrm{Var}(X):=\sigma_{n}:=\sigma

i.e. we suppress the dependence on
n
to simplify notation throughout.

Let us compute the approximating Gaussian quadratic form
W_{n}
associated to
U_{n}
. For this, we need to compute the functions
H_{1},H_{2}
, and then compute the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the Hilbert Schmidt operator corresponding to
H_{2}
. In this case:

h(x,y)=xy-\theta^{2}

So that:

H_{1}(x)=\mathbb{E}[h(X_{1},X_{2})|X_{1}=x]=\mathbb{E}[xX_{2}-\theta^{2}]=x\theta-\theta^{2}

and:

H_{2}(x,y)=h(x,y)-H_{1}(x)-H_{1}(y)=xy-\theta(x+y)-\theta^{2}

Next, we compute the eigenfunctions of
H_{2}
with nonzero eigenvalue. Let
\psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R},P_{n})
be arbitrary, then the eigenfunction equation is:

c\psi(x)=\mathbb{E}[H_{2}(x,X)\psi(X)]

We compute:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[H_{2}(x,X)\psi(X)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[(xX-\theta(x+X)-\theta^{2})\psi(X)]

\displaystyle=x\mathbb{E}[X\psi(X)]-\theta x\mathbb{E}[\psi(X)]-\theta\mathbb{E}[X\psi(X)]-\theta^{2}\mathbb{E}[\psi(X)]

\displaystyle=x\mathbb{E}[X\psi(X)]-\theta\mathbb{E}[X\psi(X)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[X\psi(X)](x-\theta)

Where here we used the fact that
\mathbb{E}[\psi(X)]=0
. The final expression is a linear function in
x
, thus the image of
H_{2}
is a subset of the linear functions, and we may set
\psi(x)=ax+b
for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
. Using orthonormality, we obtain:

\mathbb{E}[aX+b]=0\implies a\theta+b=0\text{ and }\mathbb{E}[(aX+b)^{2}]=1\implies a^{2}(\theta^{2}+\sigma^{2})+2ab\theta+b^{2}=1

Using these equations gives:

a=\frac{1}{\sigma},b=-\frac{\theta}{\sigma}

So that

\phi_{1}(x)=\psi(x)=\frac{(x-\theta)}{\sigma}

Thus, the image of
H_{2}
is one-dimensional, and spanned by
\phi_{1}
. Having found the sole nontrivial eigenfunction, it remains to find the eigenvalue
c
. The eigenvalue equation is:

\displaystyle c\frac{(x-\theta)}{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[H_{2}(x,X)\psi(X)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[X\psi(X)](x-\theta)

So:

\frac{c}{\sigma}=\mathbb{E}[X\psi(X)]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{X(X-\theta)}{\sigma}\right]\implies c=\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]-\theta^{2}=\sigma^{2}

This completes the process of finding eigenvalues and eigenfunctions. In summary, using the notation of Section
2
, we obtain:

\phi_{1}(x)=\left(\frac{x-\theta}{\sigma}\right),\quad b_{1}=\sigma

(E.32)

The final step in the analysis is to represent
H_{1}(x),H_{2}(x,y)
in terms of these functions and add another eigenfunction if needed. For
H_{2}
, we obtain the representation:
Thus:

H_{2}(x,y)=b_{1}\phi_{1}(x)\phi_{1}(y)=\sigma^{2}\left(\frac{x-\theta}{\sigma}\right)\left(\frac{y-\theta}{\sigma}\right)

For
H_{1}
, we seek a representation of the form:

H_{1}(x)=x\theta=a_{0}\phi_{0}(x)+a_{1}\phi_{1}(x)

where
\phi_{0}
is a function to be chosen orthonormal to
\phi_{1}
. We observe:

H_{1}(x)=x\theta-\theta^{2}=\theta(x-\theta)=\theta\sigma\frac{x-\theta}{\sigma}=\theta\sigma\phi_{1}(x)

Thus, again with notation of section
2
, we obtain:

\phi_{1}(x)=\frac{x-\theta}{\sigma},\quad a_{1}=\theta\sigma

(E.33)

This completes the analysis.

S.8.2
Student’s T Statistic Kernel

We have:

H_{1}(x)=\mathbb{E}[h(x,X)]=\mathbb{E}[xX^{2}+x^{2}X]=x

So:

H_{2}(x,y)=h(x,y)-H_{1}(x)-H_{1}(y)=xy^{2}+x^{2}y-x-y=x(y^{2}-1)+y(x^{2}-1)

For any function
\phi
, the integral operator gives:

\mathbb{E}[H_{2}(x,X)\phi(X)]=x\mathbb{E}[(X^{2}-1)\phi(X)]+(x^{2}-1)\mathbb{E}[X\phi(X)]

(E.34)

Thus, the image of
H_{2}
is contained in
\mathrm{span}\{x,x^{2}-1\}
(which is a linearly independent set of functions), and it is at most a rank
2
operator. In particular, any eigenfunction must lie in this set as well. Restricted to this set, the operator can be written in matrix form with respect to the basis as:

A=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma^{3}&\kappa^{4}-1\\
1&\gamma^{3}\end{pmatrix}

which can be obtained by plugging
\phi(x)=x
and
\phi(x)=x^{2}-1
into the operator equation (
E.34
) above.
Thus, computing the eigenvalues is a matter of finding the zeroes of the characteristic polynomial. We set:

\det(A-cI)=(\gamma^{3}-c)^{2}-(\kappa^{4}-1)=0\implies c=\gamma^{3}\pm(\kappa^{4}-1)^{1/2}

This gives us the coefficients
b_{1},b_{2}
as the following - if
\gamma^{3}>0
, then:

b_{1}=\gamma^{3}+(\kappa^{4}-1)^{1/2},\quad b_{2}=\gamma^{3}-(\kappa^{4}-1)^{1/2}

and if
\gamma^{3}<0
then:

b_{1}=\gamma^{3}-(\kappa^{4}-1)^{1/2},\quad b_{2}=\gamma^{3}+(\kappa^{4}-1)^{1/2}

The reason for this is that we label our eigenvalues in order of absolute value. With the definitions above, we are guaranteed
|b_{1}|\geq|b_{2}|
. The number
\kappa^{4}-1
is always nonnegative
by Jensen’s inequality, but equals zero whenever
\mathbb{E}[X^{4}]=\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]=1
. This occurs if and only if
|X|
is almost surely equal to
1
, which in turn occurs if and only if
X
is a random variable supported on the two point set
\left\{-1,1\right\}
.

Next, we need to find the corresponding eigenfunctions, so that we can ultimately determine
\mu_{k}
.
Next, we solve for the eigenfunctions. For any coefficients
(p,q)
we have:

\begin{pmatrix}\gamma^{3}&\kappa^{4}-1\\
1&\gamma^{3}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}p\\
q\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}p\gamma^{3}+q(\kappa^{4}-1)\\
p+q\gamma^{3}\end{pmatrix}

Letting
c
be any eigenvalue, the eigenfunction equations are thus:

p\gamma^{3}+q(\kappa^{4}-1)=cp,\quad p+q\gamma^{3}=cq

We can get one selection by setting
p=1
. Then, looking at the second equation gives:

1+q\gamma^{3}=cq\implies q=\frac{1}{c-\gamma^{3}}

Plugging in the two possible values for
c
gives::

\psi_{1}(x)=x+\frac{1}{(\kappa^{4}-1)^{1/2}}(x^{2}-1),\quad\psi_{2}(x)=x-\frac{1}{(\kappa^{4}-1)^{1/2}}(x^{2}-1)

Next, we need to normalize these so that they have
L^{2}
norm
1
. In general we have:

\mathbb{E}[(pX+q(X^{2}-1))^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[p^{2}X^{2}+2pq(X^{3}-X)+q^{2}(X^{2}-1)^{2}]=p^{2}+2pq\gamma^{3}+q^{2}(\kappa^{4}-1)

Hence:

\mathbb{E}[\psi_{1}(X)^{2}]=1+\frac{2\gamma^{6}}{(\kappa^{4}-1)^{1/2}}+\frac{\kappa^{4}-1}{(\kappa^{4}-1)}=2\left(1+\frac{2\gamma^{3}}{(\kappa^{4}-1)^{1/2}}\right)

And:

\mathbb{E}[\psi_{2}(X)^{2}]=2\left(1-\frac{2\gamma^{3}}{(\kappa^{4}-1)^{1/2}}\right)

So that the normalized eigen-functions are:

\phi_{1}(x)=\frac{x+\frac{1}{(\kappa^{4}-1)^{1/2}}(x^{2}-1)}{\left(2\left(1+\frac{2\gamma^{3}}{(\kappa^{4}-1)^{1/2}}\right)\right)^{1/2}}

\phi_{2}(x)=\frac{x+\frac{1}{(\kappa^{4}-1)^{1/2}}(x^{2}-1)}{\left(2\left(1-\frac{2\gamma^{3}}{(\kappa^{4}-1)^{1/2}}\right)\right)^{1/2}}

Finally, we need to obtain
a_{k}
, to represent
H_{1}(x)
in terms of the eigen-functions
\phi_{1}(x),\phi_{2}(x)
and the constant eigenfunction
\phi_{0}(x)
. Notice:

H_{1}(x)=x=\frac{1}{2}\psi_{1}(x)+\frac{1}{2}\psi_{2}(x)=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\psi_{1}(X)^{2}]^{1/2}}{2}\phi_{1}(x)+\frac{\mathbb{E}[\psi_{2}(X)^{2}]^{1/2}}{2}\phi_{2}(x)

Thus, by definition, we have:

a_{1}=\frac{\left(2\left(1+\frac{2\gamma^{3}}{(\kappa^{4}-1)^{1/2}}\right)\right)^{1/2}}{2},\quad a_{2}=\frac{\left(2\left(1-\frac{2\gamma^{3}}{(\kappa^{4}-1)^{1/2}}\right)\right)^{1/2}}{2}

S.8.3
Student’s T-Statistic Kernel - Bound Analysis

In this section we provide the computations behind the asymptotics discussed in section
6.2
, in particular in the case when
\gamma>0
, i.e.
p_{1}<2
.
We begin with the bound from Theorem
1
. We first compute some intermediate quantities. In this case, we exactly two eigenvalues and hence:

\frac{1}{p_{1}}+\frac{1}{p_{2}}=1\implies\frac{1}{p_{1}}-\frac{1}{2}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p_{2}}.

We have:

\frac{1}{p_{1}}-\frac{1}{2}=\frac{\gamma^{3}\xi^{1/2}}{2(\gamma^{6}+\xi)},\quad\frac{p_{1}}{2}-1=\frac{\gamma^{6}+\xi}{(\gamma^{3}+\xi^{1/2})^{2}}-1=\frac{-2\gamma^{3}\xi}{(\gamma^{3}+\xi^{1/2})^{2}},

and

\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p_{2}}=\frac{\gamma^{3}\xi^{1/2}}{2(\gamma^{6}+\xi)},\quad\frac{p_{2}}{2}-1=\frac{\gamma^{6}+\xi}{(\gamma^{3}-\xi^{1/2})^{2}}-1=\frac{2\gamma^{3}\xi}{(\gamma^{3}+\xi^{1/2})^{2}}.

Using the notation of Theorem 1, we obtain the quantities:

\displaystyle B_{p,1}^{1/p_{1}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{\frac{1}{p_{1}}-\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{1/p_{1}}\left[\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2\Lambda_{1}}\right)^{\frac{p_{1}}{2}-1}-1\right]^{\frac{1}{p_{1}}}

(E.35)

\displaystyle=\left[\left(\frac{2(\gamma^{6}+\xi)}{\gamma^{3}\xi^{1/2}}\right)\left[\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2\Lambda_{1}}\right)^{\frac{-2\gamma^{3}\xi}{(\gamma^{3}+\xi^{1/2})^{2}}}-1\right]\right]^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\gamma^{3}\xi^{1/2}}{2(\gamma^{6}+\xi)}}

(E.36)

\displaystyle A_{p,2}^{1/p_{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p_{2}}}\right)^{1/p_{2}}\left[1-\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2\Lambda_{1}}\right)^{\frac{p_{2}}{2}-1}\right]^{\frac{1}{p_{2}}}

(E.37)

\displaystyle=\left[\left(\frac{2(\gamma^{6}+\xi)}{\gamma^{3}\xi^{1/2}}\right)\left[1-\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2\Lambda_{1}}\right)^{\frac{-2\gamma^{3}\xi}{(\gamma^{3}+\xi^{1/2})^{2}}}\right]\right]^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\gamma^{3}\xi^{1/2}}{2(\gamma^{6}+\xi)}}

(E.38)

Multiplying together and substituting the expression for
\Lambda_{1}^{2}
gives we obtain:

\displaystyle B_{p,1}^{1/p_{1}}A_{p,2}^{1/p_{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{2(\gamma^{6}+\xi)}{\gamma^{3}\xi^{1/2}}\right)\left[\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2\Lambda_{1}}\right)^{{\frac{-2\gamma^{3}\xi}{(\gamma^{3}+\xi^{1/2})^{2}}}}-1\right]^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\gamma^{3}\xi^{1/2}}{2(\gamma^{6}+\xi)}}\left[1-\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2\Lambda_{1}}\right)^{\frac{2\gamma^{3}\xi}{(\gamma^{3}+\xi^{1/2})^{2}}}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\gamma^{3}\xi^{1/2}}{2(\gamma^{6}+\xi)}}

\Lambda_{1}

\frac{n^{1/2}}{[2(\gamma^{6}+\xi)]^{1/2}}\left(\frac{2(\gamma^{6}+\xi)}{\gamma^{3}\xi^{1/2}}\right)\left[\left(\frac{n\varepsilon^{2}}{4(\gamma^{6}+\xi)}\right)^{{\frac{-\gamma^{3}\xi}{(\gamma^{3}+\xi^{1/2})^{2}}}}-1\right]^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\gamma^{3}\xi^{1/2}}{2(\gamma^{6}+\xi)}}\left[1-\left(\frac{n\varepsilon^{2}}{4(\gamma^{6}+\xi)}\right)^{\frac{\gamma^{3}\xi}{(\gamma^{3}+\xi^{1/2})^{2}}}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\gamma^{3}\xi^{1/2}}{2(\gamma^{6}+\xi)}}

,
2

\mathbbm{1}\left\{\varepsilon\leq 2\Lambda_{1}\right\}=\mathbbm{1}\left\{\varepsilon\leq\frac{2(\gamma^{6}+\xi)}{n}\right\}.

Thus, it only makes sense to analyze the bound in these cases.

Assume now that:

\frac{\gamma^{6}+\xi}{n}\to c^{2},

for some

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2}-\frac{1}{p_{1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{p_{1}}+\frac{1}{p_{2}}-\frac{1}{p_{1}}
