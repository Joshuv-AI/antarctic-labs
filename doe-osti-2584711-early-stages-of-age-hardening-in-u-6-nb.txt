 Early Stages of Age Hardening in U-6%Nb:
 An Energy Barrier Hypothesis for Twin Reorientation
 Kenneth H. Eckelmeyer
                                  December 24, 2024

1.0 Background:
It is well known that age hardening occurs in U-6%Nb (Ref. 1-9). Figure 1 shows that
increases in hardness occur at aging temperatures up to ~400°C, and overaging occurs
above ~450°C. Overaging occurs by cellular decomposition of the ʺb martensite, similar
to overaging reactions in other uranium alloys. But the mechanism of age hardening
remains elusive, particularly in its early stages.

A detailed APFIM study (Ref. 7) showed that phase separation occurs during the later
stages of age hardening. Nb-enriched precipitates spaced at ~6-8 nm were observed in
material aged at 300°C for 1 x 105 minutes – an aging treatment that results in hardening
to ~89% of peak hardness, as shown in Figure 2. But samples aged at 200°C for 1 x 105
minutes, which corresponds to ~50% of peak hardness, exhibited APFIM results virtually
identical to those of as-quenched (unaged) samples. It was concluded that no precipitates
were present in these 200°C aged samples, at least down to the resolution limit of 3 nm.

Further considerations concluded that the 6-8 nm spacing observed in the 300°C/105
minute sample would be consistent with the reported 136.4 KJ/mol activation energy for
diffusion of Nb in -U if the pre-exponential value of D0 was decreased by several orders
of magnitude from D0 = 1.38 x 10-8 m2/sec (the previously published value) to 7.2 x 10-12
m2/sec (Ref. 7). Using this “adjusted” value of D0, it was calculated that precipitate
spacings corresponding to 200°C/105 minute aging would be ~0.39 nm (comparable to
atomic dimensions in uranium), and that for 200°C/120 minute aging (a treatment that is
frequently used to obtain a moderate increase in yield stress with little or no loss of
ductility) precipitate spacing could be only ~0.013 nm (dramatically less that atomic
dimensions). The fact that these calculated precipitate spacings were equal to or less than
atomic dimensions was taken to be evidence that classical precipitate formation could not
be responsible for the considerable age hardening that occurs at 200°C, and that some
other mechanism involving little or no Nb-atom mobility must be responsible for the
early stages of age hardening. However, the credibility of this assessment was somewhat
compromised by the requirement to adjust the previously determined value of D0 by
several orders of magnitude, as well as the assumption that the reported value of
activation energy, which was experimentally determined between 977°C and 777°C, is
applicable at the much lower temperatures at which early age hardening occurs.
Fortunately, there is an alternative approach that circumvents these difficulties.

2.0 An Alternative Approach to Estimating Precipitate Spacings
It is possible to estimate precipitate spacings at various times and temperatures
independent of specific values of D0 or activation energy. Diffusion theory indicates that

 1

spacings should vary linearly with (Dt)0.5. Since D will be constant for all aging
treatments at 300°C, the spacings for other aging times at 300°C will be proportional to
(t/105)0.5. 300°C/120 minute aging, then, should result in a factor of (120/105)0.5 = 0.0346
change in spacing, i.e., spacings in the range 0.21 to 0.28 nm.

Figure 2 also shows that 300°C/120 minute aging results in a hardness of VHN 287,
which would also be produced by 8500 minutes at 200°C. So, it is reasonable to assume
that 200°C/8500 minute aging would also result in 0.21 to 0.28 nm spacings. 120 minute
aging at 200°C would then result in an additional factor of (120/8500)0.5 = 0.119 change
in spacing, i.e., spacings in the range 0.025 to 0.033 nm.

These calculated spacings vary somewhat from those calculated in Ref. 7, but not enough
to change their conclusion that classical precipitation cannot account for the early age
hardening that occurs at 200°C. Particle spacings of at least several times atomic spacings
would seem to be a realistic lower limit for formation of discrete second phases, i.e., a
spacing of ~1.0 nm. Using the approach outlined above, this would require aging times in
the vicinity of 105 minutes at 200°C and would correspond to a fractional hardening of
~0.5. No phase separation was experimentally observed in such samples (Ref. 7),
however the resolution limit was ~3 nm, so particles this fine would not have been
detected.

The apparent activation energy for aging can also be estimated from the data in Figure 2.
As noted above, 300°C/120 minute aging results in identical hardness as 200°C/8500
minute aging. Q/R can be determined from the slope of a plot of ln t vs. 1/T, where Q =
activation energy, R = gas constant, t = aging time to reach some particular value of
hardness, T = aging temperature (K). So, for the two equivalent aging conditions above,
both resulting in VHN 287 (f = 0.37), Q/R = (ln 8500 – ln 120)/(0.002114 - 0.001745) =
4.260/0.000369 = 11,545. So, the apparent activation energy is 8.314 x 11,545 = 96.0
kJ/mol. This is significantly lower than the 136.4 kJ/mol value for Nb diffusion in -U
which was assumed to be applicable in Ref. 7.

In addition to this, the fact that the lines in Figure 2 are not parallel to one another
suggests that the value of apparent activation energy changes as age hardening proceeds.
For example, equivalent hardness values of VHN 400 (f = 0.66) can be achieved by aging
at 300°/5345 minutes or 200°C/1,848,000 minutes. Using the same approach as above
gives an apparent activation energy of 131.7 kJ/mol.

It is apparent that there is no single value of activation energy that is characteristic of the
entire age hardening process. Perhaps the activation energy approaches the 136.4 kJ/mol
value for Nb diffusion in -U in the later stages of age hardening, but this is definitely no
true of the early stages of aging.

3.0 The Increase in ʺb → 0 Transformation Temperature During Aging:
It has been shown that very little hysteresis occurs between the 0 → ʺb martensitic
transformation (on cooling) and the reverse ʺb → 0 transformation (on heating)
provided heating rates are high (~100°C/sec) (Refs. 6, 10). So, rapidly heated material
would be expected to have totally transformed to 0 prior to aging at 200°C.

But several researchers have noted that the temperature at which the reverse ʺb → 0
transformation occurs increases with decreasing heating rate (Refs. 9, 10).
Transformation temperature was shown to be above 200°C at a heating rate in the vicinity
of 0.1°C/sec (Ref. 9). Heating rates in most production operations are in this vicinity, so
the ʺb-phase is typically retained during 200°C aging.

Bob Hackenberg also showed how holding for various times at 200°C also increases the
ʺb → 0 transformation temperature on subsequent rapid heating (Ref. 6). The results are
shown in Table 1.

The regular increase in reverse transformation shown in Table 1 indicates that the ʺb
martensite becomes increasingly stable as age hardening progresses, i.e., that the energy
of ʺb decreases relative to that of 0 during 200°C exposure. The magnitude of the
decrease in energy of the ʺb is presumably proportional to the increase in transformation
temperature.

Comparing this with the increase in strength that occurs on age hardening shows a
striking correlation. Figure 3 shows a plot of the measured yield strengths reported in
Ref. 6 for samples age hardened for various times at 200°C vs. the corresponding
increases in ʺb → 0 transformation temperature. The strong relationship strongly
suggests that the increases in yield strength are directly related to the increasing
difference in energy between the ʺb and 0 phases.

So, a question is, “why would the difference in energy between the ʺb and 0 phases
have anything to do with yield strength?” And if it does, a follow-up question is, “why

 3

does 200°C aging result in stabilization of the ʺb phase?” The first question will be dealt
with in Sections 4 and 5. The second question will be considered in Section 6.

4.0 An Energy Barrier Hypothesis for Twin Boundary Mobility and Yielding:
One possibility is that the ease with which twin reorientation occurs is controlled by this
difference in energy between the ʺb and 0 phases. The following paragraph presents a
rationale for why this might be the case.

It is well known that the stress at which the first few percent of plastic strain occurs
increases with increasing 200°C aging time, as shown in Figure 4 (Refs. 6, 8). It is also
known that the ʺb martensite laths contain an abundance of adjacent ( 30) twins, as
shown in Figure 5 (Ref. 11). It has also been shown that the first few percent of strain
occurs by the preferred variants of these twins growing into and consuming their less-
preferred adjacent neighbors (Refs. 9, 11, 12). This indicates that yield strength is
controlled by the mobility of these ( 30) twin boundaries – anything that decreases
( 30) twin boundary mobility will increase yield strength. If, in the process of one ʺb
twin variant growing into the adjacent variant, atoms move thorough configurations
similar to those characteristic of 0, an increasing energy difference between ʺb and 0
could impede twin boundary mobility. In other words, the energy difference between the
ʺb and 0 phases would constitute an energy barrier to twin reorientation. Anything that
stabilized the ʺb (decreased its energy) would increase the size of this energy barrier,
thus making twin boundary mobility more difficult, thus increasing yield strength.

This is a substantial departure from traditional thinking about the mechanism of age
hardening. Metallurgists typically think about age hardening occurring by microstructural
interactions, specifically as finely divided precipitates, or their precursors known as G-P
zones, form and provide barriers to dislocation movement and plastic deformation by
slip. In U-6%Nb, however, initial deformation occurs by twin reorientation, rather that
slip, so the pertinent issue is, how might twin boundary mobility be impeded, especially
if limited atomic mobility precludes the formation of precipitates? One microstructural
interaction possibility that has been proposed is that Nb atoms diffuse to the twin
boundaries and stabilize them (Ref. 7). This could conceivably occur with only limited
movement of Nb atoms, but no APFIM evidence for this was found in Ref. 7. Another
possibility is the energy barrier hypothesis described above.

A key question for assessing the viability of the energy barrier hypothesis is, do atoms, in
fact, move through -like positions in the process of twin boundary movement, i.e.,
when preferred variants of ( 30) twins grow into and consume adjacent non-preferred
variants? That is the topic of the following section. (Spoiler alert – the answer is YES –
not exactly 0 positions, but closely related ones.)

5.0 Crystallographic Relationship Between -like and -like Phases:
It is widely recognized that the orthorhombic structure of -uranium can be derived from
body-centered-cubic -uranium by a 10.025-degree shear parallel to the {112} plane, as

 4

illustrated in Figure 6. Note that the rectangular coordination of atoms on the (110)
plane, a defining characteristic of the parent -phase.

In U-6%Nb the parent 0-phase is distorted slightly from cubic and the ʺb-product phase
is a slightly monoclinic variant of orthorhombic . But the transformation can occur by a
very similar shear, as illustrated in Figure 7. In U-6%Nb a shear of ~4.6° is required to
convert the tetragonal parent phase to monoclinic ʺb with an included angle of ~93°.

It should be noted that Figure 7 represents the 0 structure without the slight (~0.06)
<001>0 direction displacements of half the atoms (some in the + direction and others in
the – direction) (Ref. 13). It will be shown in Appendix 1 that these displacements are
eliminated in the process of the 0 → ʺb transformation. Figure 7 represents the shear
that occurs after these <001>0 direction displacements have been eliminated. So, the
parent phase in Figure 7 and all subsequent illustrations might be more appropriately
referred to as “-like”, rather than strictly “0-phase”.

Figure 7 also shows that the ( 30) twinning plane in ʺb is essentially parallel to the
(001) plane in the parent 0-phase. The precision of this parallelism varies with both the
c/a ratio of the 0-phase and the monoclinic angle in the ʺb-phase, both of which vary
with Nb content. Calculations indicate that this parallelism is perfect at ~5.5 wt.%Nb
composition, as is shown in Appendix 2. All subsequent figures correspond to this
condition.

Figure 8 shows how twin-related unit cells of ʺb can be formed by crystallographically
equivalent shears in a single orientation of the parent 0-phase. The adjacent regions of
ʺb-phase share a common ( 30) plane, exactly like the fine twins that are
characteristically observed in U-6%Nb (see Figure 5). Figure 9 shows the atom positions
in the resulting twin-related variants of ʺb-phase.

Figure 10 is similar to Figure 9 but is useful for illustrating how a “preferred variant”
can grow into and consume the adjacent “non-preferred variant” – the process known as
“twin-reorientation”. The atoms in the variant being consumed (open circles in variant 2)
move left-to-right to occupy positions characteristic of the preferred variant that is
consuming it (filled circles corresponding to variant 1). Note that the magnitudes of these
atomic movements are directly proportional to the distance from the original twin plane.
So, taken together, they represent shear deformation. When this shear deformation
occurs, it helps accommodate applied shear stresses, thus providing the reason why twin
reorientation occurs under the influence of applied stress.

Measurements indicate that the angle of shear involved in ( 30) twin reorientation is
~9.2° – two times the shear angle inherent in the 0 → ʺb transformation. Assuming an
initial microstructure consisting of 50% variant 1 and 50% variant 2, the maximum shear
strain attributable to twin reorientation would be ~ 9.2/2 = 4.6%, roughly consistent with
experimental observations of the amount of strain that occurs by twin reorientation.

 5

Figure 11 is identical to Figure 10, but with the midpoints of the variant 2 to variant 1
atom movements marked by small triangles, and these triangles connected with dashed
lines. The resulting rectangular arrangements are virtually identical to those characteristic
of atoms on the (110) plane of the -phase in unalloyed uranium (see Figure 6), or the
corresponding parent 0-phase in U-6%Nb (see Figure 7). This indicates that the atomic
arrangements midway in the twin reorientation process are essentially the same as those
in the parent 0-phase (not including the <001> direction displacements of the body-
centered atoms).

This confirms the hypothesis that the process of favorably oriented variants of ( 30)ʺ
twins growing into and consuming adjacent unfavorably oriented variants does, in fact,
involve atoms moving through positions very similar to those characteristic of the 0-
phase. So, any energy difference between the ʺb and 0 phases would constitute an
energy barrier to twin reorientation. The required atomic movements would be expected
to occur relatively easily if the ʺb and 0 phases had similar energies, as is the case in
as-quenched ʺb in the ~6%Nb alloy – here the “energy barrier” to twin reorientation
would be low. But if the ʺb had been stabilized during aging, its energy would be
significantly lower than that of 0, thus increasing the ”energy barrier” and making it
more difficult for favorably oriented variants to grow into unfavorably oriented ones.
This would result in the increased yield strength that is known to occur during age
hardening.

The key concept is that twin reorientation requires the atoms to move through positions
similar to those characteristic of the 0 phase. So as the energy of the 0-phase becomes
progressively higher than that of the ʺb-phase, as Table 1 indicates occurs during 200°C
aging, there will be an ever-increasing energy barrier to twin reorientation occurring. This
would result in an increase in yield strength proportional to the increase in ʺb → 0
transformation temperature, consistent with that shown in Figure 3.

6.0 Potential Mechanisms for ʺb-Phase Stabilization:
If this energy barrier hypothesis is correct, the next question is, why does the energy of
the ʺb-phase decrease during low temperature aging? The following are several
possibilities worthy of consideration.

6.1 Anomalous Lattice Expansion During Heating and Subsequent Relaxation at 200°C:
As noted earlier, the ʺb → 0 transformation is completed below 200°C during rapid
heating (heating rates in the vicinity of 100°C/sec) (Refs. 6, 10). But this transformation
temperature increases on slower heating, resulting in the ʺb- phase being retained during
typical aging at 200°C (Refs. 9, 10). Aging for longer times at 200°C also results in
progressive increases in transformation temperature on subsequent heating (Ref. 6).

Don Brown and his coworkers have shown that abnormally large anisotropic changes in
lattice parameters occur during gradual heating (rates in the vicinity of ~0.1°C/sec) of ʺb
to 200°C (Ref. 9). These changes dramatically exceed typical thermal expansion behavior

 6

and suggest that the material is “anticipating” the occurrence of the of ʺb → 0
transformation as temperature continues to increase. Then, when held at 200°C, these
accentuated ʺb lattice parameters gradually “relax”. Finally, when subsequently cooled
to room temperature, the lattice parameters revert to very near to their original (pre-
elevated temperature exposure) values.

The large anisotropic changes that occur in the individual microstructural units during
heating are somewhat constrained or resisted by the overall surrounding material,
presumably giving rise to complex local stresses. The relaxation that occurs during
subsequent holds at 200°C is discussed in terms of a stress relaxation phenomenon (Ref.
9). This stress relaxation is presumed to result in stabilization of the ʺb-phase, resulting
in an increase in the ʺb → 0 transformation temperature during subsequent heating,
which was described in Section 3.0. However, the details of how stress relaxation at the
200°C aging temperature occurs are not well understood, nor is it entirely clear how this
affects the energy of the ʺb-phase at room temperature.

Significant residual stresses and the associated elastic strain energy would certainly
contribute to the overall energy of the ʺb-phase, and a reduction of these could result in
an increase in the energy barrier for twin reorientation. However, there is little evidence
of any change in room temperature residual stresses before and after aging. Room
temperature lattice parameters are nearly identical before and after 2500 minutes at
200°C, and peak widths appear to be identical.

The overall appearance is that the “driving force” for ʺb → 0 transformation is partially
“used up” as relaxation occurs, thus increasing temperature required for the ʺb → 0
transformation to later occur. It would be interesting to determine in future experiments
whether the magnitude of the anomalous changes in lattice parameters decrease on
subsequent heating after extended prior exposures during which relaxation had occurred,
as this proposal suggests.

Despite the lack of complete understanding, the correlation between elevated temperature
lattice parameter relaxation and increases in yield strength during aging is compelling. It
is particularly noteworthy that relaxation at 100°C was almost non-existent compared
with that at 200°C (see Ref. 9 figure 5). This parallels a similar yield strength observation
in Ref. 6 – much less strengthening was associated with 100°C aging than might be
expected based on traditional kinetic comparisons with the increases in strength that
occurred at higher aging temperatures.

Perhaps the most that can be said at present is that anomalous lattice changes occur
during slow heating suggesting that the ʺb-phase is anticipating the transformation to 0,
and that their isothermal relaxation during aging appears to partially stabilize (lower the
energy of) the ʺb-phase. The progressively lower energy of the ʺb-phase results in
increases in the temperature required for transformation to 0 likely increasing the energy
barrier for twin reorientation.

6.2 Formation & Ripening of Embryonic U-Nb Clusters By Short-Range Nb Atom
Movement:
Another possibility is that embryonic U-Nb clusters already exist in the as-quenched
alloy, or that they can form and subsequently ripen by Nb-atom movements involving as
little as a single jump – much shorter than the longer-range diffusional movements
required to form the 6-8 nm spaced precipitates seen after aging to near-peak hardness in
Ref. 7.

Amy Clarke’s APT results suggested that the second phase that eventually forms in peak
aged material contains ~25 at.% Nb (Ref. 7). This is the same concentration that AEM
work found in the Nb-enriched -phase in slowly cooled material (Ref. 4). Other
investigators, as far back as Ross Jackson in the late 1960s, have observed that a transient
variant of -phase with an Nb concentration in this vicinity commonly occurs as a
precursor to the higher-Nb 2-phase shown on the U-Nb equilibrium phase diagram,
which only forms after very long times at temperatures in the vicinity of 500°C (Ref. 2).

The number of U atoms that might, strictly by chance, have multiple Nb atoms as nearest
neighbors can be readily calculated. ʺb in as-quenched U-6 wt.%Nb is a substitutional
solid solution in which the Nb atoms are presumably randomly distributed on sites
typically occupied by U atoms. Each U-atom is surrounded by 12 nearly equally
distanced “nearest neighbors” (interatomic distances ranging from ~2.76 to ~3.32 Å). The
probability of each U-atom having one or more Nb atoms as nearest neighbors is given
by:

               P(n) = C12,n (pn) (q12-n) = [12!/n!(12-n)!] (pn) (q12-n),
 where:
 n = number of Nb atom nearest neighbors,
 p = atom fraction Nb atoms = at.%Nb/100,
 q = atom fraction U atoms = 1-p.

U-6 wt.%Nb is U-14 at.% Nb, so this reduces to:

               P(n) = [12!/n!(12-n)!] (0.14n) (.8612-n).

Working through this equation for one, two, three, four, and more Nb atoms as nearest
neighbors yields the results shown in Table 2.

As can be seen, more that 15% of the U atoms have 3 Nb atoms as nearest neighbors,
thus constituting embryonic clusters consisting of 10 U-atoms and 3 Nb-atoms – very
near the preferred concentration of 25 at.% Nb. An even larger percentage of sub-
embryonic clusters consisting of 11 U-atoms and 2 Nb-atoms (15 at.%Nb) would also be
present. The thermodynamic driving force pushing things toward equilibrium would
favor Nb atom movements that add a Nb atom to the sub-embryonic clusters, as well as
increasing the sizes of embryonic clusters that already exist. Such changes could occur
with as little as a single jump. The overall outcome would be a decrease in the energy of
the ʺb-phase as clusters of this preferred composition form and gradually mature over
time. This decrease in energy would result in an increasing energy difference between the
ʺb-phase and the of -phase, thus increasing the energy barrier for twin reorientation
and increasing yield strength. Given sufficient time and Nb mobility, these clusters would
eventually “evolve” into the second phase particles observed by Clarke (Ref. 7), but only
after aging at much long times and higher temperatures (~300°C/105 minutes).

While this may be a credible mechanism for reducing the energy of the ʺb-phase, it
seems unlikely that it would cause the observed lattice distortions described in Section
6.2. This detracts from the credibility of this possibility being the primary reason why the
energy of the ʺb-phase decreases during early aging.

6.3 Twin Boundary Modification:
As was noted in Section 5.0 and will be described in more detail in Appendix 2, the ( 30)
twinning plane in ʺb is very nearly parallel to the (001) plane in the parent 0-phase. But
this relationship is only perfectly true when the angle between ( 30)ʺ and (010)ʺ is
exactly equal to the equivalent angle between the precursor planes in the parent phase.
The former varies with the lattice parameters of the ʺb-phase, particularly the
monoclinic angle. The latter varies with the lattice parameters of the parent phase,
particularly the c/a ratio. Both of these vary with Nb content. So, the ( 30)ʺ twinning
plane is perfectly parallel to (001)0 only at a particular niobium content, in the vicinity of
5.5 wt.% Nb. (Note that this is based on lattice parameter measurements from early
studies (Refs. 14, 15). More precise measurements from later studies, such as Ref. 9, may
alter this composition slightly.)

When niobium content differs from this ideal composition, imperfections and lattice
strains are likely to occur on and near the twin boundaries. Imperfections may also form
and be “frozen in” during the rapid quench that follows gamma solution heat treatment.
All this will contribute to the overall energy of the as-quenched ʺb-phase. Amy Clarke
proposed that these interfacial structures may rearrange to lower energy configurations by
processes such as dislocation glide, climb, of ledge/step migration during low
temperature aging (Ref. 7). For example, twin boundaries could become “segmented” and
develop occasional “jogs”. These “modified twin boundaries” could be inherently less
mobile. Or the reduction in energy of the ʺb-phase could simply increase the energy
barrier to twin reorientation, as was described in Section 5.0.

High resolution TEM studies are currently being conducted in an effort to characterize
any changes that occur in twin boundaries during low temperature aging.

7.0 Summary:
Initial age hardening (up to fractional hardening of at least f = 0.5) cannot occur by a
process that requires significant niobium atom mobility, such as classical precipitate
formation. Slow heating followed by holding at 200°C, as occurs during typical age
hardening treatments, results in progressive increases in the ʺb → 0 transformation
temperature, indicative of partial stabilization of the ʺb phase and increasing energy
differences between the ʺb and 0 phases. The increase in yield strength during 200°C
aging is directly proportional to this increase in transformation temperature. Initial
deformation occurs by twin reorientation in which preferred variants of ( 30) twins grow
into and consume adjacent non-preferred variants. In the process of twin reorientation
atoms move through configurations similar to those in the 0 phase. So, the energy
difference between the ʺb and 0 phases may constitute an energy barrier to twin
reorientation which increases with 200°C aging time, thus being responsible for the
increase in yield strength that occurs during early aging. Several possible causes for
stabilization of the ʺb phase during 200°C exposure are presented and discussed. The
most likely cause may be associated with time-dependent relaxation of the anomalous
lattice parameter changes that occur on slow heating to 200°C (Ref. 9). But the details of
how this results in stabilization of the ʺb phase are not yet clear.

Appendix 1. Elimination of <001> Direction Atomic Displacements in 0 During the
  0 → ʺb Transformation and Consequences Regarding the Energy Barrier for
  Twin Reorientation

When the martensitic transformation occurs on quenching of U-6%Nb following solution
heat treatment, the parent phase is no longer perfectly cubic – it is now a tetragonal
distortion of the body-centered-cubic -phase, designated 0. The  → 0 transformation
occurs between ~375° and 325°C during the quench (Ref. 6). The body centered atoms in
the BCC -phase are displaced very slightly in the <001> direction (~6% of the BCC a-
parameter), and these displacements occur in opposite senses in adjacent BCC unit cells –
upward in half of them, and downward in the other half (Ref. 13). As a result, the
tetragonal unit cell of 0 is made up of 4 former BCC unit cells, with the tetragonal “a”-
parameter being twice that of the former BCC -phase. The (001) planes also become
slightly more closely spaced, making the c/a ratio slightly less than 0.5. The c/a ratio
varies with composition and is ~0.48 for U-6%Nb (Refs. 9, 10).

As was noted in the main text, Figure 7 and all subsequent figures presented the 0-phase
in U-6%Nb as being tetragonal in the sense that the (001) planes were slightly more
closely spaced than they would have been in body-centered-cubic -phase but ignored the
<001> displacements of half the atoms. This appendix outlines evidence that these
displacements are reversed (undone) during the 0 → ʺb transformation. Since these
displacements are not carried over into the ʺb-phase, there is no need to include them in
considering how favorably oriented variants of ( 30)ʺ twins grow into unfavorably
oriented variants. So, the simplified version of 0 shown in Figures 7-11 is appropriate for
this purpose. The following is more detail.

Figure A1 compares the atom positions on the (110) plane of BCC -phase with those on
the equivalent plane in tetragonal 0. Similar rectangular atomic arrangements exist, but
the body-centered-cubic atoms in 0 are slightly displaced in the <001> direction –
downward for those in the plane of view, but upward for those in the planes above and
below the plane of view. The spacing of the (001) planes are also decreased slightly,
making the c/a ratio ~0.48 (Refs. 9, 14).

Based on the above reasoning, it seems clear that the small <001> diection displacements
of half the atoms in the 0 -phase reverse themselves during the 0 → ʺb transformation.
If this were not true the ʺb-phase could not form by pure shear, and the unit cell of ʺb
would be much larger than is experimentally observed. So, the simplified versions of 0
shown in Figures 7-11 of the main text, are appropriate for describing the  → ʺb
transformation, as well as the atom movements that occur as favorably oriented variants
of ( 30)ʺ twins grow into unfavorably oriented variants during twin reorientation.

The difference between 0 and 0 without the <001> displacements is not problematic for
the energy barrier hypothesis. Since the atom movements involved in twin reorientation
take them through positions characteristic of  minus the <001> direction displacements,
the energy barrier is not quantitatively equal to the difference in energy between ʺb and
0. The energy of  without the <001> direction displacements will be somewhat higher
than that of  adding an increment to the energy barrier. But the stabilization of ʺb that
occurs during 200°C aging will result in an increase in the magnitude of the energy
barrier regardless of the additional increment corresponding to the difference between 0
and  without the <001> direction displacements. This makes energy barrier hypothesis
reasonable, regardless of the fact that twin reorientation takes atoms through positions
similar to 0 without the <001> direction displacements, rather than 0, per se.

Appendix 2: Effect of Niobium Content on Parallelism of ( 30)ʺ and (001)

Figure 7 shows that the characteristic ( 30)ʺ twinning plane in ʺb is nominally
coincident with the (001) plane in the parent 0. Note that the line representing the
profile of the ( 30)ʺ plane intersects the ʺb a-axis one unit to the left of the lower
lefthand corner of the ʺb unit cell, and intersects the ʺb b-axis 1/3 of the way up from
that corner of the ʺb unit cell, thus confirming its Miller indices as ( 30)ʺ

However, this relationship is not exact for all Nb contents. The interplanar angles in both
phases vary with Nb content – due primarily to varying degrees of tetragonality in 0, and
varying monoclinic -angles in ʺb. In order for the ( 30)ʺ - (001) equivalency to be
exact, the angle between ( 30)ʺ and (010)ʺ in the ʺb phase must be equal to the angle
between the corresponding planes in the parent 0 phase.

Figure A2-1 shows how these interplanar angles vary with Nb content. Note that these
angles become equal at 33.5° at 13 at.% (5.5 wt.%) Nb. This defines where there is
minimum distortion involved in the formation of ( 30) twins during the 0 → ʺb
transformation, and presumably where the ( 30) twin boundaries would be most readily
mobile during subsequent straining.
