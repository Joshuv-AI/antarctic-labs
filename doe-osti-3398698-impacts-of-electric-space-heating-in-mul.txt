 Impacts of Electric Space Heating in Multifamily
 Apartments in Juneau
• Objective: To understand the change in electrical consumption and cost after the addition of a
  ductless mini-split air-source heat pump (ASHP) across rental apartments heated by electrical
  resistance heating systems in multifamily buildings in Juneau, Alaska.
• Methods: Compare electrical use (total and specific to heating appliances), adjusted for weather
  and apartment size, and electrical demand in the year before and after installation of an ASHP
  across study units.
 ◦ Alaska Electric Light and Power Company (AELP), the utility in Juneau, Alaska, funded installation of 19
 heat pumps across four buildings.
 ◦ Using electrical meter interval data, including the total apartment consumption (unless otherwise noted)
 and submeter data for each heating appliance (electric resistance baseboard and heat pump), the National
 Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) calculated energy use metrics before and after heat pump installation and
 visualized results.
• Impact: Inform residents of documented energy and cost savings from transitioning building
  heating systems to a higher-efficiency electricity-based appliance, inform building energy
  modeling across other multifamily housing dwellings, and enable estimates of potential
  electricity savings to AELP grid under widespread adoption scenarios.

 Background: Nineteen Study Units Selected by AELP
• Nineteen units were located across four multifamily buildings in Juneau; not all units of a
  building were necessarily study units. Units are referred to by the neighborhood of the
  building location; numbers assigned to reference units within a building do not necessarily
  align with true building apartment number. All units are served by baseboard electric
  resistance heating.
 - Four apartments, each 950 square feet (ft2), in Auke Bay (“Auke Bay” 1–4)
 - Four apartments, three 800 ft2 units, one 500 ft2, in Lemon Creek (“Lemon Creek” 1–4)
 - Four apartments, each 740 ft2, in Mendenhall Valley (“Valley” 1–4)
 - Seven apartments, ranging from 374 ft2 to 536 ft2, in Douglas (“Douglas” 1–7)
• Each unit in the study has a dwelling unit-level electrical meter. For this study, AELP
  installed submeters on dedicated electrical circuits for both the electric resistance
  baseboards and the ASHP. All meters were two-way automatic communication system
  (TWACS) meters. Total electrical usage was available only for the entire Douglas building,
  not individual units.

 Methods: Analysis of Electrical Meter Data
Key Study Dates:
• Whole-unit and baseboard-specific TWACS meters (pre-heat pump) installed: Auke Bay, Lemon Creek, and Mendenhall Valley study units in
 December 2022; Douglas units in August 2023.
• Ductless mini-split air-source heat pump and ASHP-specific TWACS meters installed: Auke Bay, Lemon Creek, and Mendenhall Valley study
 units from March to May 2024; Douglas units in October 2024.

Data Analysis:
AELP electrical consumption is recorded at two frequencies: (1) interval data (15 or 60 minutes), primarily used for this analysis, and (2) register
data (daily values), used when interval data were not available or reliable, as detailed in metadata (available on request). Register data for the
Douglas whole-unit and all unit baseboard meters require a x40 multiplier.
Interval data were summarized into hourly (when applicable), daily, weekly, monthly, and yearly values.
One year of data before and after installation of the heat pump for each unit were compared.
Metrics from before and after heat pump installation were calculated for total electricity and specifically electricity from heating appliances.
Daily electrical demand data were provided by AELP to evaluate daily peak demand as a function of weather.
Time series of consumption and comparisons of consumption and demand before and after heat pump installation were visualized: Baseboard
(blue), Heat pump (orange), Other plug loads/Non-heat electrical (green), Demand (purple). Slide 13 includes a visual key for easy reference.

 Methods: Definitions and Metrics
Kilowatt (kW): The rate of power used by a load or device—the higher the number of kilowatts required, the more power is needed to
run it. These data from AELP represent peak demand, reported as the highest average power measured over 15 minutes each day,
irrespective of time of day.
Kilowatt-Hour (kWh): Unit of electricity consumption, measure of energy used over time, reported by the utility. These data from AELP
are the base data of all analysis.
Energy Cost Index​ (ECI): Annual cost per square foot ($/ft2) of conditioned space. This is calculated from kilowatt-hours recorded in meter
data using AELP electricity rates by season not customer bills. Rates were provided directly by AELP through their rates explainer.
 • Auke Bay, Lemon Creek, and Valley rates: winter (November–May) = $0.1229/kWh and summer (June–October) = $0.101/kWh
 • Douglas rates: winter (November–May) = $0.0622/kWh and summer (June–October) = $0.0585/kWh. An additional demand-based
 charge is calculated based on the building’s status as a large commercial (rate 24) building and is based on the highest peak demand
 for the month: winter (November–May) = $14.56/kW and summer (June–October) = $9.27/kW.
Energy Use Intensity​ (EUI): Energy consumption per unit area of conditioned space. Consumption in kilowatt-hours is converted to kilo-
British thermal units (kBtu) (1 kWh = 3.412 kBtu) and divided by square foot (ft2) to align with standard reporting convention (kBtu/ft2).
Heating Degree Days​​ (HDDxx): The cumulative difference between the mean daily temperature and a set temperature baseline, commonly
65°F. A heating degree day implies colder outdoor temperatures that are likely to influence the demand for indoor heating (HDDxx).
Energy Use Intensity per Heating Degree Day (EUI/HDDXX): Energy use per square foot of building space normalized by the daily heating
requirement.

 Methods: Definitions and Metrics (cont’d)
ASHRAE Guideline 14-2014: An industry-standard building energy model (BEM) calibration method
for energy use that uses daily weather data gathered from the National Oceanic and Atmospheric
Administration’s (NOAA’s) Online Weather Data (NOWData) service. Applied here with Juneau
Airport (PAJN) weather and AELP meter data.
Projected Weather-Normalized kWh: Electrical use projected for a time period based on prior
electrical consumption and adjusted for the actual weather (ambient temperature) specific to that
period of time,* according to ASHRAE Guideline 14-2014. Baseboard meter data of year prior to
heat pump installation were used to calculate the expected kilowatt-hours in the year after heat
pump installation had there been no change to the heating system or occupancy/behavioral habits.
Weather-Normalized Difference in kWh: Represents the difference between the electrical
consumption that occurred over a period of time compared to the projected weather-normalized
energy in kilowatt-hours. This is discussed as energy saved compared to if there had been no
change to the heating system (heat pump install) or occupancy/behavioral habits.

 *Base temperature HDD was calculated for each unit based on actual energy use.

 Typical Usage, Typical Occupancy
The Douglas units (seven) are treated separately when the addition of a whole-building energy meter
would impact the analysis. Titles and notes indicate when Douglas data were omitted or included.
After evaluating patterns of tenancy and use of dedicated (sub-metered) heating appliances (baseboard or
heat pump) in the Auke Bay, Lemon Creek, and Valley buildings (12 total units), AELP identified units that
had “typical” occupancy/usage before and after heat pump installation. Results aggregate trends across
nine “typical” units and report total and average change in energy metrics.
The following units have been excluded from “typical” occupancy/usage analysis:
 - Auke Bay 3 – did not use baseboard heat in year before heat pump installation
 - Lemon Creek 1 – prolonged vacancy and tenancy change
 - Lemon Creek 4 – did not use baseboard heat before heat pump installation; did not use heat pump after
 installation.
In graphics marked “typical,” these three units were omitted. “Typical + Douglas” results represent 16 units.
To understand the impact of the omitted units, analysis and aggregated energy metrics including all units – a
total of 19 across Auke Bay, Lemon Creek, Valley, and Douglas – is also presented and labeled “full study.”

 Early Takeaways
• Considering consumption before weather normalization, typical and Douglas units,
 together, saw a 41% decrease (25,267 kWh) in consumption from heating appliances
 compared to a 36% decrease (23,295 kWh) across all 19 AELP heat pump study units.
• Occupancy and tenant behavior, including use (or not) of dedicated heating appliances
 (baseboards or heat pump), varied between study units, impacting energy use patterns.
• While units with atypical occupancy or heating patterns dampened the overall effect,
 heat pump installations led to reductions in total and heating-only electricity
 consumption across multifamily apartments in Juneau.
• Despite an increase in “other” electricity in the Douglas building from pre- to post-heat
 pump installation, the total drop in heating kWh was extreme (53%) such that the total
 building electricity use declined (16%).
• Summing usage of Auke Bay, Lemon Creek, and Mendenhall Valley units, there was a
 greater decline in heating-specific energy use (30%) compared to total (including other
 non-heat and plug loads) energy use (21%). When atypical apartments were removed,
 those values increased to 35% (heating-specific) and 28% (total) reductions in usage.

 Seasonal Trends in Juneau

• Seasonal trends are examined to understand change in heating demand and potential impact on electricity use.
• Monthly HDD65 over the study period (2023–2025) rarely exceeded the 30-year average (1991–2020) HDD65 for Juneau.
• The winter after heat pump installation was slightly colder, with 2.3% greater HDD 65 than the year prior to installation (using
  March 2024 as the break). Douglas units, with a later installation date in October 2024, saw no difference between years (<1%).
• The difference in HDD65 between periods before/after installation was not statistically significant (paired t-test with p-value
  0.05), so by and large the inter-annual temperatures do not explain changes in electrical consumption.

 Daily Peak Electrical Demand Across Study
Data: AELP meters measure demand by identifying the 15-minute window during which the
greatest amount of energy was consumed. One value is reported for each day. (Note: Because
the demand peak records the highest average power over 15 minutes, the instantaneous peak
power might be higher than the billed demand peak. That instantaneous peak is not measured or
evaluated.)
Analysis:
• Summarize daily peak demand (kW) across rental apartments in the study.
• Perform regression analysis for daily peak demand in response to weather; weather
  metrics included heating degree days, daily average temperature, daily minimum
  temperature, and daily change in temperature.
 ◦ Compare model results, evaluate statistical significance pre/post heat pump installation.
• Examine demand-weather relationship during a 2025 three-week “cold snap” compared to
  “normal” winter weather pre and post heat pump installation.

 Key Takeaways: Demand-Weather Relationship
 • Further regression analysis by unit or building considering interaction effects or temporal autocorrelation, among other methods,
 is warranted to better understand and plan for any impact of new technology adoption on energy system demand.

 • No or limited relationship exists (coefficient p-val >0.5; R2<0.2) in simple linear regression models of daily peak electrical demand
 and weather (HDD57) for each unit and each building. Across all units and buildings, no pattern was observed in direction or
 strength of kW-HDD trend. There was no evidence that heat pump installation resulted in higher peak demand.
 ◦ Scatterplots and preliminary regression results are delivered in supplemental materials.
 ◦ HDD57 was used in exploratory plots after it was determined to be the practical setpoint most common for Juneau MMFH residents
 based on consumption trends.

 • For all study apartments, the max daily demand occurred in the upper quartile of HDD days, but hypothesis testing was
 inappropriate to characterize difference in mean kilowatts between groups given temporal autocorrelation and deviation from
 normalcy.
 • Data resolution, time of day or week, and weather characteristics beyond temperature such as daylight, precipitation, wind, and
 humidity influence how the demand-weather relationship may be characterized. Further, resident behavior, socioeconomic
 status, and technology adoption may confound the relationship as they drive demand patterns (Sivanendran, Han, & Woodward,
        2022; Kang & Reiner, 2022).

 Key Takeaways
• Across all study units, there was a 38% decrease in EUI/HDD65 from heating appliances in the
 year before compared to after heat pump installation; this compares to a 36% decrease in
 heating-specific energy use (in kWh) if conditioned area and weather are not accounted for.
• In total, all buildings experienced reductions in energy intensity and cost after heat pump
 installation. Douglas units, followed by Valley units then Lemon Creek units, saw greatest
 reductions.
• Considering only study units with typical occupancy and heating system use before and after
 the heat pump installation (typical group + Douglas), heating-associated EUI/HDD65 declined by
 42% and associated annual average savings on heating was nearly $120 for each unit.
• In total, the savings in electrical heating costs across all study units was $2,220.
• No clear relationship was observed between daily peak electrical demand and weather, and no
 evidence that heat pump installation resulted in higher demand. More analysis is warranted.
• While units with atypical occupancy or heating use patterns dampened the overall effect, heat
 pump installations led to total consumption and cost reductions across multifamily apartments.
