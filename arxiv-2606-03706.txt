Abstract
. We use recent results, among which modular inequalities for curves, to determine the Alexander polynomials for some classes of pencil-type conic-line arrangements. For these classes of curves we prove that the Alexander polynomial is (at least partially) combinatorial. To this end, we exemplify new techniques that are suitable for broader use, lending themselves to more general classes of curves.

1.
Introduction

Seen as a natural generalization of arrangements of projective lines in the field of projective plane curves,
conic-line arrangements
(or, equivalently,
conic-line curves
) are defined as curves whose irreducible components are lines and smooth conics. There are numerous recent articles that explore algebraic, geometric, topological and combinatorial properties of conic-line arrangements, see for instance
[
4
,
19
,
18
,
26
,
28
,
33
,
36
]
.

Given a complex projective plane curve
\mathcal{C}
defined by a homogeneous degree
d
polynomial
f_{\mathcal{C}}\in\mathbb{C}[x,y,z]
, we consider the Milnor fiber associated to
\mathcal{C}
, i.e. the hypersurface in
\mathbb{C}^{3}
defined by

\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{C}):f_{\mathcal{C}}=1.

The multiplication by a primitive root of unity of order
d
defines an action on
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{C})
. This induces an action on the homology group
H_{1}(\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{C}),\mathbb{Q})
, called algebraic monodromy, which makes
H_{1}(\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{C}),\mathbb{Q})
a
\mathbb{Q}[t^{\pm 1}]
-module, where
t
acts by the algebraic monodromy.
Since the monodromy is of finite order
d
,
H_{1}(\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{C}),\mathbb{Q})
is a torsion module.
The Alexander polynomial of a curve,
\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}(t)
, is defined as the characteristic polynomial of the monodromy action on
H_{1}(\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{C}),\mathbb{Q})
and, in light of the above discussion, it satisfies the formula

(1)

\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}(t)=\Pi_{k|d}\phi_{k}^{b_{k}},

where
\phi_{k}
is the
k
-th cyclotomic polynomial.

Computing the monodromy action on the homology of the Milnor fiber is a very difficult problem, even in the case of degree
1
homology (meaning, computing the Alexander polynomial), and even if we narrow down the problem to the case of arrangements of projective lines. To name just a few papers on the subject, see
[
6
,
8
,
10
,
15
,
17
,
14
,
21
,
23
,
24
,
27
]
. The study of the Alexander polynomial of a curve is also relevant in connection to the fundamental group of the complement of the curve in the complex projective plane, see for instance
[
2
]
.

In
[
11
]
, based on a result of Papadima-Suciu from
[
34
]
, the authors introduce modular inequalities for (complements of) complex projective plane curves, inequalities that give upper bounds for the multiplicities of roots of the associated Alexander polynomial, provided that those roots are powers of primes. In the same paper
[
11
]
, lower bounds are given for the multiplicities of roots of the associated Alexander polynomial, for quasi fiber-type curves.
In this note we will use these results to compute Alexander polynomials for pencil-type conic-line arrangements, but the techniques employed for this purpose are likely to be of use in the computation of the Alexander polynomial of other general classes of curves.

By
pencil of conic-line arrangements
, or, equivalently,
pencil of conic-line curves
, we mean a primitive pencil
{\mathcal{P}}
of degree
d\geq 3
curves having at least three reducible and reduced members whose irreducible components are lines and smooth conics. We will call these members
conic-line fibers
. A
pencil-type conic-line arrangement
is a reduced curve that can be realized as an union of conic-line fibers of a pencil of conic-line arrangements.

An intensely studied and still open problem concerning arrangements of hyperplanes, related to the Milnor fiber monodromy problem, is whether the Alexander polynomial is determined by the combinatorics of the intersection lattice of the arrangement, i.e. if it is
combinatorial
. A comprehensive presentation of the problem and various approaches to solve it can be found in
[
32
, Subsection 1.8]
.
The general case of this problem can be reduced to the case of arrangements of projective lines.
On the other hand, we know that this conjecture does not hold for plane projective curves in general. In the context of curves, by
combinatorial
we mean determined by the weak combinatorial type of the curve, see Section §
2
for the definition. Counterexamples of the combinatorial determination of the Alexander polynomial for curves can be found among
Zariski pairs
(briefly, Zariski pairs are pairs of curves with the same weak combinatorial type but different embedded in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
topology, see for instance
[
2
]
for a detailed exposition on the subject).
One may consider for instance the pair of irreducible sextics that Zariski introduced in
[
39
]
.
The two curves are combinatorially equivalent but it turns out they have distinct Alexander polynomials.
Examples involving reducible curves that are combinatorially equivalent but have distinct Alexander polynomials
have bee constructed as well, see for instance Artal Bartolo’s example from
[
3
]
of a pair of degree
6
curves, each consisting of a union of a smooth cubic and three lines, which is also a Zariski pair. Or, more generally, we refer to the Zariski pairs of sextics from Oka’s papers
[
30
,
31
]
, emerging from sextics classification results.

However, we were able to detect a distinctive phenomenon that happens for a subclass of pencil-type conic-line arrangements. Specifically, for this subclass, for which we give formulas for the Alexander polynomials, in Theorems
2
and
3
, we prove that the multiplicities of the roots of the Alexander polynomials that we compute are
combinatorial
, see Theorem
1
. These results are reminiscent of similar results in the theory of arrangements of hyperplanes, see
[
25
,
32
]
.

In
[
9
]
Cogolludo-Libgober prove that in a pencil-type conic-line arrangement having only ordinary singular points as base points and which is realized as the union of
m
conic-line fibers of a conic-line pencil of curves of degree
d\geq 3
, necessarily
m\leq 6
. We should point out that this statement, recalled in Theorem
12
, involves two cases different in nature, see
[
9
, Proposition 4.2]
for the case
d\geq 4
and
[
9
, Corollary 4.4]
&
[
35
]
for the case
d=3
. The bound on
m
in the case
d=3
is sharp, as illustrated by an example of Ruppert (see
[
35
]
) of a a pencil-type conic-line arrangement which is the union of
6
reducible conic-line fibers, each fiber being the union of a line and a smooth conic. The bound on
m
in the case
d=4
is also known to be sharp, see
[
9
, Proposition 4.5]
.

We study here the class of pencil-type conic-line arrangements with transversal base points coming from pencils of degree
3
curves.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a pencil-type conic-line arrangement arising from a pencil of degree
3
curves such that the singularities of
\mathcal{C}
at the base points of the pencil are transversal.Then the multiplicity of primitive roots of unity of odd order as roots of the Alexander polynomial
\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}(t)
is combinatorial. Moreover, if there are at most two non-ordinary singular points in
\Sing(\mathcal{C})
, then the multiplicity of primitive roots of unity of order powers of
2
as roots of the Alexander polynomial
\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}(t)
is combinatorial as well.

Furthermore, we determine the Alexander polynomial of the curves
\mathcal{C}
described in the above theorem.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a pencil-type conic-line arrangement which is realized as the union of
m\geq 3
conic-line fibers of a conic-line pencil of degree
3
curves. Assume that the singularities of
\mathcal{C}
at the base points of the pencil are ordinary.
Denote
s:=|\Irr(\mathcal{C})|
. Then:

(1)

If
m=3
, then
\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}(t)=(t-1)^{s-1}(t^{2}+t+1)
, except if
\mathcal{C}
is the Ceva arrangement, in which case
\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}(t)=(t-1)^{s-1}(t^{2}+t+1)^{2}
.

(2)

If
m=4
, then
\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}(t)=(t-1)^{s-1}(t+1)^{b_{2}}(t^{2}+1)^{b_{4}}
and
b_{2},b_{4}\geq 2
.

(3)

If
m=5
, then
\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}(t)=(t-1)^{s-1}(t^{4}+t^{3}+t^{2}+t+1)^{3}
.

(4)

If
m=6
, then
\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}(t)=(t-1)^{s-1}(t+1)^{b_{2}}(t^{2}+t+1)^{4}(t^{2}-t+1)^{b_{6}}
and
b_{2},b_{6}\geq 4
.

Moreover, if there are at most two non-ordinary singularities in
\Sing(\mathcal{C})
, then

(1)

If
m=4
, then
b_{2}=b_{4}=2
.

(2)

If
m=6
, then
b_{2}=4
.

When
m=4
we can ease the restrictions on the number of non-ordinary singularities. We prove the following result, see Theorem
20
for a rephrasing.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a pencil-type conic-line arrangement as in Theorem
2
and assume
m=4
.
If either one of the two conditions hold:

(1)

There exist at most three non-ordinary singularities in
\Sing(\mathcal{C})
,

(2)

There exist a base point
P
of the pencil such that an odd number of lines from
\Irr(\mathcal{C})
pass through
P
,

then
\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}(t)=(t-1)^{s-1}(t+1)^{2}(t^{2}+1)^{2}
.

We give in Subsection §
4.1
an abstract curve configuration that illustrates a situation when none of the two conditions from Theorem
3
are met. A curve that realizes this configuration would potentially give an example of Alexander polynomial with the root multiplicities
b_{2},b_{4}
satisfying
2<b_{2},b_{4}
. In any case, we are able to show that, for this combinatorial type,
b_{2},b_{4}\leq 3
.
Moreover, we show that this configuration is the unique weak combinatorial type of a pencil-type conic-line arrangement
\mathcal{C}
which is the union of the
m=4
conic-line fibers of a pencil of degree
3
curves such that conditions (1) and (2) do not hold, see Theorem
22
. We end Section §
4
with a couple of open questions regarding this abstract curve configuration.

2.
Preliminaries

Let
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{1}\cup\dots\cup\mathcal{C}_{r}
be the decomposition into irreducible components of the reduced plane projective curve
\mathcal{C}
and denote
d_{i}:=\deg(\mathcal{C}_{i})
.

The data that consists of:

•

\Sing(\mathcal{C})
, the set of singularities of
\mathcal{C}
;

•

the set of labels
\mathbf{r}:=\{1,\dots,r\}
of the irreducible components;

•

for each
P\in\Sing(\mathcal{C})
the set of local branches
\Delta_{P}
of
\mathcal{C}
at
P
;

•

the map
\phi_{P}
that assigns to each branch at
P
the label of the irreducible component it belongs to;

•

the map that assigns to each pair of branches (from distinct components) at
P
the intersection number of the two;

is called the
weak combinatorial type
of the curve
\mathcal{C}
, see
[
7
]
. We will denote it by
W_{\mathcal{C}}
.
The weak combinatorial type can be seen as playing the same role for curves as the notion of intersection lattice for arrangements of hyperplanes. Furthermore, the notion of
abstract curve combinatorics
was developed in
[
13
]
to capture formally the defining characteristics of a weak combinatorial type, see
[
13
]
for a precise definition. Taking further the parallelism to the theory of hyperplane arrangements, an abstract curve combinatorics is reminiscent of the notion of matroid.
We will get back to this in Subsection §
4.1
.

Keeping in mind the analogy to arrangements of hyperplanes, we will call an invariant or property of a curve
\mathcal{C}

combinatorial
if it depends only on the weak combinatorial type of
\mathcal{C}
. For instance, Cogolludo-Matei prove that the cohomology algebra of the complement of a plane curve is combinatorial, building on a similar result by Cogolludo for rational curves, see
[
7
,
12
]
.
This mirrors Orlik-Solomon’s emblematic result on the combinatoriality of the cohomology of the complement of an arrangement of hyperplanes in a complex vector space.

To the weak combinatorial type of a curve
\mathcal{C}
one associates a graded algebra,
(A^{*}_{W_{\mathcal{C}}},\wedge)
over
\Bbbk=\mathbb{Z}_{p}
,
p
prime, defined as follows (see
[
11
]
). The
\Bbbk
-module structure is given by

•

A_{W_{\mathcal{C}}}^{0}:=\Bbbk
,

•

A_{W_{\mathcal{C}}}^{1}:=\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbf{r}}\sigma_{i}\Bbbk
,

•

A_{W_{\mathcal{C}}}^{2}:=A_{W_{\mathcal{C}}}^{2,0}\oplus A_{W_{\mathcal{C}}}^{2,\infty}
, where

A_{W_{\mathcal{C}}}^{2,\infty}:=\langle\bar{\psi}^{i}_{\infty}:i\in\mathbf{r}\rangle_{\Bbbk}/\langle\bar{\psi}^{i}_{\infty}:p\nmid d_{i}\rangle_{\Bbbk},\quad A_{W_{\mathcal{C}}}^{2,0}:=\bigoplus_{P\in S}\frac{A_{P}\wedge A_{P}}{I_{P}},\quad A_{P}:=\langle\psi_{P}^{\delta}:\delta\in\Delta_{P}\rangle_{\Bbbk},

I_{P}:=\{\psi_{P}^{\delta_{1}}\wedge\psi_{P}^{\delta_{2}}+\psi_{P}^{\delta_{2}}\wedge\psi_{P}^{\delta_{3}}+\psi_{P}^{\delta_{3}}\wedge\psi_{P}^{\delta_{1}}\}+\{\psi_{P}^{\delta_{1}}\wedge\psi_{P}^{\delta_{2}}\mid\#\phi_{P}(\Delta_{P})=1\}\subset A_{P}\wedge A_{P}.

•

A_{W_{\mathcal{C}}}^{i}=0
(
i\geq 3
).

while the product, defined on the degree
1
generators as described below, makes
A^{*}_{W_{\mathcal{C}}}
an algebra

\sigma_{i}\wedge\sigma_{j}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in S,\\
\delta_{1}\in\phi_{P}^{-1}(i),\\
\delta_{2}\in\phi_{P}^{-1}(j)\end{subarray}}\mu_{P}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})\ \psi_{P}^{\delta_{1}}\wedge\psi_{P}^{\delta_{2}}\wedge+d_{j}\bar{\psi}_{\infty}^{i}-d_{i}\bar{\psi}_{\infty}^{j}.

The multiplication by an arbitrary
1
-form
\omega\in A^{1}_{W_{\mathcal{C}}}
defines a complex structure
(A^{*}_{W_{\mathcal{C}}},\wedge\omega)
on the algebra
A^{*}_{W_{\mathcal{C}}}
, called the
Combinatorial Aomoto complex
. Accordingly we define the
k
-th resonance varieties associated to these complexes:

\mathcal{R}_{k}(W_{\mathcal{C}},\Bbbk):=\{\omega\in A^{1}_{W_{\mathcal{C}}}|\dim_{\Bbbk}(H^{1}(A^{*}_{W_{\mathcal{C}}},\wedge\omega)\geq k)\}

With this, let us recall a result from
[
11
]
that gives an upper bound for the exponents

from (

Theorem 4
.

Let

and
\omega_{1}:=\sum_{i\in\mathbf{r}}\sigma_{i}

b_{k}\leq\dim_{\Bbbk}H^{1}(A^{*}_{W_{\mathcal{C}}},\wedge\omega_{1}).

Notice that the right hand side of the inequality in Theorem

is combinatorial. Moreover, we have a result that allows the computation of this combinatorial invariant (compare to
[

(

3.1.
Proof of Theorem
2

Since the fibers are assumed to be reduced, each fiber is either a union of three lines (either a pencil of three lines or three lines in general position) or a union of a smooth conic and a line (that intersect either transversely or the line is tangent to the smooth conic).
By Theorem
12
and Proposition
13
,
m\leq 6
and
n\leq 3
. We will make a case by case discussion on the possible values of
m
and
n
.

Recall that, by the hypothesis, any point
P\in\Sing(\mathcal{C})
is either an ordinary singularity of multiplicity
2,3
or
m
, or a simple singularity of type
A_{3}
. Moreover, the
A_{3}
type singularity can only appear as a non-base point type singularity, i.e. a singularity in one of the conic-line fibers.

Case m = 6.

If
m=6
, then none of the conic-line members of the pencil is a pencil of lines, i.e.
n=0
. By Theorem
8
,
b_{k}\geq 4
for
k\in\{2,3,6\}
.We will show that
\beta_{p}\leq 4
for
p\in\{2,3\}
- for the case
p=2
we will use the additional ’moreover’ hypothesis. This will imply, by Theorem
4
(that is, inequality (
7
)), that
b_{2}=b_{3}=4
.
To compute
\beta_{p}
, by Proposition
5
(see also Remark
7
), one has to solve a linear system
S
of equations in
|\Irr(\mathcal{C})|

(a_{q}^{i}),\;q\in\overline{1,6},\;1\leq i\leq|\Irr(\mathcal{C}_{q})|
over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. The system consists of the equations of type (
2
) associated to each singularity of
\mathcal{C}
and equations of type (
3
) for each irreducible component of degree divisible by
p
. This system translates the resonance condition
\omega\wedge\omega_{1}=0
, where
\omega=\sum_{q,i}a^{i}_{q}\sigma^{i}_{q}
.
Each ordinary multiple point
P\in\Sing(\mathcal{C})
produces either an equation of type (
5
) or a series of equations, of type (
4
). A singularity
P
of type
A_{3}
produces a non-trivial equation in

, of type (

), only if
p=3

Each singular point which is not a base point type is either an ordinary singular point of multiplicity

, or a singularity of type

A base point type singularity is an ordinary singularity of multiplicity

.

The case

of type

. These singularities are necessarily non-base point type singular points. Assume without loss of generality that

and

, hence

.

Case m = 5.

If

, then

. Non-base point type singularities are ordinary singularities of multiplicity

or of type

and the base point type singularities are ordinary singularities of multiplicity

a_{q}:=a_{q}^{i}=a_{q}^{j},\forall i\neq j

Assume

Case m = 4.

By Theorem

4.
On the Alexander polynomial of a pencil-type curve with

Throughout this section, unless otherwise specified,
\mathcal{C}
is a pencil-type conic-line arrangement defined by a pencil of conic-line arrangements of degree
3
having
m=4
conic-line fibers, denoted
\mathcal{C}_{1},\cdots,\mathcal{C}_{4}
. As a general rule, we denote the irreducible components of
\mathcal{C}_{i}
by

, i.e.
\mathcal{C}_{i}=\cup_{j}C_{i}^{j}

However, when the irreducible decomposition of a fiber

\mathcal{C}_{i}
is the union of three lines, we denote those lines by

, and if
\mathcal{C}_{i}

the line and by

the smooth conic.

We will give a more precise formula for the Alexander polynomial of such a curve
\mathcal{C}
, without imposing any restrictions on the type of singularities that are non-ordinary, as was the case in Theorem
2
. Base point type singularities are assumed to be transversal, so any non-ordinary type singularity in
\Sing(\mathcal{C})
must be the intersection of some irreducible components of a conic-line fiber
\mathcal{C}_{i}
. This means that we can have at most four non-ordinary singularities, and they must be
A_{3}
type singularities.
Denote

{\mathcal{N}}:=\{P\in\Sing(\mathcal{C})\;|\;P\;\text{non-ordinary}\}.

Since Theorem
2
covers the case when there are at most two non-ordinary singularities in
\Sing(\mathcal{C})
, we are left to deal with the cases
|{\mathcal{N}}|\in\{3,4\}
.

Basically, given such a curve
\mathcal{C}
, we will compute
\beta_{2}(\mathcal{C})
, in other words we will estimate the dimension of the space of solutions over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
of the linear system
S
described in Remark
7
.
Via inequality (
7
) and Theorem
8
, this will enable us to compute the exponents
b_{2}
and
b_{4}
from the formula (
1
) of the Alexander polynomial of the curve
\mathcal{C}
.

Lemma 16
.

|C_{q}\cap C_{r}\cap C_{s}|<4
for any
q\neq r\neq s
.

Proof.

Assume the contrary, that there exist three conics
C_{q},C_{r},C_{s}
in
\Irr(\mathcal{C})
that intersect at four base point type singularities. Consider the two base point type singularities
L_{q}\cap C_{r}
. The irreducible component of the pencil member
\mathcal{C}_{s}
that passes through such a point must be in both instances the line
L_{s}
, otherwise we would have
|C_{r}\cap C_{s}|>4
, contradiction. But this means that
|L_{q}\cap L_{s}|>1
, contradiction.
∎

Proposition 17
.

Assume
\mathcal{C}
has four lines and four smooth conics as irreducible components, i.e.
\mathcal{C}_{q}=C_{q}\cup L_{q},\;q\in\overline{1,4}
.
If
L_{1},L_{2},L_{3},L_{4}
are concurrent lines, then
\beta_{2}(\mathcal{C})=2
.

Proof.

Since
L_{1},L_{2},L_{3},L_{4}
are concurrent lines, we have in the associated system
S
with
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
coefficients the equation

(*)\;a_{1}^{1}+a_{2}^{1}+a_{3}^{1}+a_{4}^{1}=0.

Consider the base point type singularities
L_{i}\cap C_{j}
and
L_{j}\cap C_{i}
,
i\neq j
. They are ordinary singularities of multiplicity
4
of the curve
\mathcal{C}
, and necessarily the conics
C_{k}
and
C_{l}
,
i\neq j\neq k\neq l
, pass through these points. The equations in
S
corresponding to these points are

(**)

a_{i}^{1}+a_{j}^{2}+a_{k}^{2}+a_{l}^{2}=0
and
a_{i}^{2}+a_{j}^{1}+a_{k}^{2}+a_{l}^{2}=0
.

It follows
a_{i}^{1}+a_{j}^{2}=a_{i}^{2}+a_{j}^{1}
for any
i\neq j\in\overline{1,4}
, or equivalently
a^{2}_{i}-a^{1}_{i}=a^{2}_{j}-a^{1}_{j}
for any
i\neq j\in\overline{1,4}
. Denote
a:=a^{2}_{i}-a^{1}_{i}
. By
(*)
and
(**)
,
a=0
. This implies
a^{2}_{i}=a^{1}_{i}
for all
i\in\overline{1,4}
. Hence there are
3
independent parameters that define the solution space for the system
S
, which implies
\beta_{2}(\mathcal{C})=2
. ∎

Proposition 18
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be such that
|{\mathcal{N}}|=3
. Then
\beta_{2}(\mathcal{C})=2
.

Proof.

|{\mathcal{N}}|=3
means there are precisely three singularities of type

in
\Sing(\mathcal{C})
, so we may assume that
\mathcal{C}_{1}
has only ordinary singularities.
If
\mathcal{C}_{1}

. If
\mathcal{C}_{1}
is a union of a line and a smooth conic intersecting transversely, we get

.

Consider the four base point type singularities
C_{2}\cap C_{3}
. By Lemma
16
,
L_{4}
passes through at least one of these points. On the other hand, since

a_{1}+a^{2}_{2}+a_{3}^{2}+a_{4}^{1}=0

The equations corresponding to a base point type quadruple point

∎

|{\mathcal{N}}|=4
, minus the situation when all base point type singularities involve an even number of lines. In this situation, it can happen (at least theoretically) that
\beta_{2}(\mathcal{C})>2
. More precisely, we show next that there exists an
’abstract’ weak combinatorial type

W
such that
\beta_{2}(W)=3
.
This will be an instance of the concept of
abstract curve combinatorics
, introduced by Cogolludo-Agustín & Marco-Buzunáriz in
[
13
]
. This notion, in relation to curves, can be seen through analogy to the notion of matroid in relation to arrangements of hyperplanes. The intersection lattice of an arrangement of hyperplanes is in particular a matroid, but the class of matroids is strictly larger than the class of intersection lattices of hyperplane arrangements. A matroid that is isomorphic to the intersection lattice of a hyperplane arrangement is called
realizable
.
Likewise, to a curve one associates the weak combinatorial type, which is in particular an abstract curve combinatorics.

Recall that the Combinatorial Aomoto complex defined in Section
2
depends only on the weak combinatorial type of the curve
\mathcal{C}
. So one can extend seamlessly this notion to an abstract weak combinatorial type.

Let us describe in detail the abstract weak combinatorial type
W
mentioned earlier.
A curve
\mathcal{C}
that would realize
W
should be the union of four smooth conics
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3},C_{4}
and four lines
L_{1},L_{2},L_{3},L_{4}
such that a line
L_{i}
and a smooth conic
C_{i}
intersect in an
A_{3}
type singularity and the rest of the singularities of
\mathcal{C}
would be these
9
ordinary singularities of multiplicity
4
:

(1)

C_{1}\cap C_{2}\cap L_{3}\cap L_{4}

(2)

C_{1}\cap C_{2}\cap C_{3}\cap C_{4}

(3)

C_{1}\cap C_{2}\cap C_{3}\cap C_{4}

(4)

C_{1}\cap C_{2}\cap C_{3}\cap C_{4}

(5)

L_{1}\cap L_{2}\cap C_{3}\cap C_{4}

(6)

C_{1}\cap L_{2}\cap L_{3}\cap C_{4}

(7)

C_{1}\cap L_{2}\cap C_{3}\cap L_{4}

(8)

L_{1}\cap C_{2}\cap L_{3}\cap C_{4}

(9)

L_{1}\cap C_{2}\cap C_{3}\cap L_{4}

Proposition 21
.

A curve
\mathcal{C}
that realizes the abstract weak combinatorial type
W
described above has
\beta_{2}(\mathcal{C})=3
.

Proof.

We need to compute the dimension of the space of solutions of the linear system
S
with
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
coefficients. In the notations from the beginning of this section, the variables, corresponding to the irreducible components of
\mathcal{C}
, are
(a_{q}^{i})_{1\leq q\leq 4,\;1\leq i\leq 2}
.
There are
9
equations of type (
2
), more precisely of type (
5
), since they are induced by multiplicity
4
ordinary singularities. These
9
equations boil down to the following system

\begin{cases}a_{q}^{2}-a_{q}^{1}=a_{s}^{2}-a_{s}^{1}\textrm{, for any }q,s\in\overline{1,4}\\
a_{1}^{1}+a_{2}^{1}+a_{3}^{1}+a_{4}^{1}=0.\end{cases}

The equations of type (
3
) spell
a_{1}^{1}+a_{2}^{1}+a_{3}^{1}+a_{4}^{1}=0
. So a solution to
S
depends on
4
independent parameters, which implies
\beta_{2}(\mathcal{C})=3
.
∎

Theorem 22
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be such that
|\mathcal{N}|=4
and through each base point-type singularity passes an even number of lines from
\Irr(\mathcal{C})
. Then the weak combinatorial type of
\mathcal{C}
is
W
.

Proof.

Consider the four ordinary quadruple base point type singularities
C_{1}\cap C_{2}
. By Lemma
16
,
L_{3}
passes through at least one of those four points.
L_{4}
must pass through the same point, by the hypothesis on the number of lines through base point singularities. We have then the singular point
C_{1}\cap C_{2}\cap L_{3}\cap L_{4}
. By at least two of the points
C_{1}\cap C_{2}
passes the conic
C_{3}
(otherwise the the line
L_{3}
would intersect the conics
C_{1},C_{2}
in more than 2 distinct points, contradiction). Again by the hypothesis, those two points must be of type
C_{1}\cap C_{2}\cap C_{3}\cap C_{4}
. Since an even number of lines must pass through the fourth quadruple point
C_{1}\cap C_{2}
as well, this point is either of type
C_{1}\cap C_{2}\cap L_{3}\cap L_{4}
or of type
C_{1}\cap C_{2}\cap C_{3}\cap C_{4}
. But if it is of type
C_{1}\cap C_{2}\cap L_{3}\cap L_{4}
we would have two distinct points in the intersection
L_{3}\cap L_{4}
, contradiction. So the fourth intersection point
C_{1}\cap C_{2}
must be of type
C_{1}\cap C_{2}\cap C_{3}\cap C_{4}
.
To recap, up to now we have found one base point-type singularity of type

C_{1}\cap C_{2}\cap L_{3}\cap L_{4}

and three base point-type singularities of type

C_{1}\cap C_{2}\cap C_{3}\cap C_{4}

This inventory already contains three of the four distinct intersection points of type
C_{1}\cap C_{3}
. By Lemma
16
, the fourth singularity
C_{1}\cap C_{3}
is necessarily
C_{1}\cap L_{2}\cap C_{3}\cap L_{4}
. Likewise, the fourth singularity
C_{2}\cap C_{3}
is necessarily
L_{1}\cap C_{2}\cap C_{3}\cap L_{4}
, the fourth singularity
C_{2}\cap C_{4}
is necessarily
L_{1}\cap C_{2}\cap L_{3}\cap C_{4}
, the fourth singularity
C_{1}\cap C_{4}
is necessarily
C_{1}\cap L_{2}\cap L_{3}\cap C_{4}
and the fourth singularity
C_{3}\cap C_{4}
is necessarily
L_{1}\cap L_{2}\cap C_{3}\cap C_{4}
.
This proves our claim.
∎

Corollary 23
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be such that
|\mathcal{N}|=4

The following are equivalent:

(1)

\mathcal{C}
is such that through each base point-type singularity passes an even number of lines from
\Irr(\mathcal{C})

(2)

W_{\mathcal{C}}=W

(3)

\beta_{2}(\mathcal{C})=3

Proof.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2) follows from Theorem
22
, (2)
\Rightarrow
(3) follows from Proposition
21
and (3)
\Rightarrow
(1) follows from Proposition
19
.
∎
