A c-di-AMP receptor mediates epithelial adhesion of Lactiplantibacillus plantarum
Authors: Quan Guo, Yujing Liao, Zeng Zhang, Lei Zhao, Ruimin Wang, Jiachao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cyclic di-adenosine monophosphate (c-di-AMP) is a crucial second messenger used by bacteria to mediate bacterial-host interactions. However, its roles in Lactiplantibacillus plantarum remain poorly understood. Here, we identify RwpL, which consists solely of an HTH domain, as a c-di-AMP receptor in L. plantarum WCFS1. RwpL mediates epithelial adhesion of L. plantarum WCFS1 and functions as both a transcription inhibitor and activator, with transcriptional output determined by the position of its binding site relative to the transcription start site (TSS) of target genes. Specifically, RwpL inhibits wxlA transcription by binding downstream of its TSS, thus reducing WxlA interaction with fibronectin, a matrix protein on the surface of intestinal epithelia. In contrast, RwpL activates transcription of the matrix-degrading metalloprotease gene mmpL by recruiting the sigma factor RpoA upstream of its TSS. Notably, elevated c-di-AMP levels diminish RwpL binding affinity for both DNA and RpoA, thereby reversing its regulatory functions and enhancing L. plantarum adhesion to host epithelial cells. Together, our findings uncover a dual and reversible regulatory mechanism through which c-di-AMP modulates bacterial-host interactions in L. plantarum.

Introduction

Oral administration of Lactiplantibacillus plantarum is a potential strategy to improve human and animal health, including managing chronic diarrhea1, ameliorating ulcerative colitis2, and alleviating high-fat diet-induced hyperlipidemia3. However, its efficacy is frequently limited by the harsh gastrointestinal environments that compromise probiotic adhesion and activity4–6. To establish interactions with the host within the intestine, many bacteria produce adhesins, such as exopolysaccharides7, fimbriae8, and mucin-binding proteins9, to allow themselves to bind to the intestinal surface. The WxL domain-containing proteins, which have recently been discovered as cell wall-binding proteins in several Gram-positive bacterial species, are another kind of adhesins10. The WxL domain is characterized by two conserved motifs with the sequence Trp-X-Leu in 160 to 190 amino acids11. Research has shown that WxL proteins from Enterococcus faecium are involved in bile salt stress and bind extracellular matrix proteins, including collagen, fibrinogen, and fibronectin11. Bioinformatics analysis predicted that the WxL proteins in L. plantarum are involved in stress survival adaptation12, while the predicted role and its upstream regulatory pathways still need further confirmation and elucidation.

Nucleotide secondary messengers are crucial for all forms of life to respond to environmental changes and to adapt13. As a relative latecomer, bis-(3′,5′)-cyclic diadenosine monophosphate (c-di-AMP) was initially identified in the crystal structure of DisA from Thermotoga maritima and Bacillus subtilis14. This signal molecule is synthesized by DisA_N domain-containing diadenylate cyclase (DAC) and hydrolyzed by c-di-AMP-specific phosphodiesterase (PDE)15,16. In many Firmicutes, c-di-AMP is thought to be an essential secondary messenger, and one of its most important roles is the regulation of potassium homeostasis17–19. Accordingly, the intracellular c-di-AMP levels induced by a high extracellular potassium concentration limit potassium uptake and promote potassium export19. Another notable role of c-di-AMP is its ability to bind the stimulator of interferon genes (STING) and activate a host type I interferon response20,21. A decade of research has revealed that c-di-AMP is also involved in diverse essential processes, including DNA damage repair, biofilm formation, virulence, stress responses, and cell membrane homeostasis22–25. However, the regulatory role of c-di-AMP in L. plantarum remains vague.

Upon receiving environmental stimulation, bacteria carefully control the intracellular c-di-AMP concentrations by regulating the levels or activities of specific DACs or PDEs26. While the DACs and PDEs harbor well-conserved domains, the c-di-AMP sensors are structurally diverse. Multiple classes of c-di-AMP-binding proteins and riboswitches have been identified27. For example, c-di-AMP binds to the central metabolic enzyme pyruvate carboxylase (LmPC) at the dimer interface of two CT domains28, potassium transporter-gating component KtrA at the RCK_C domain29, dedicated signal transduction protein DarB, which consists only of two tandem CBS (cystathionine-beta synthase) domains30, and sensor kinase KdpD via the universal stress protein (USP) domain31. The identified c-di-AMP-binding motifs show no significant conservation. This makes it quite challenging to identify previously uncharacterized c-di-AMP effectors and regulatory mechanisms in bacteria, especially in L. plantarum.

In this work, we identify RwpL, which contains only a Helix-Turn-Helix (HTH) domain, as a receptor of c-di-AMP. This receptor links c-di-AMP signaling and bacterial-host interactions in L. plantarum by binding globally to the genome and acts as a bifunctional transcriptional regulator. On one hand, RwpL binds downstream of the transcription start sites (TSSs) and blocks the transcription of target genes, such as wxlA (LP_RS15420), a gene mediating epithelial cell adhesion of L. plantarum. On the other hand, this protein binds upstream of TSSs and recruits sigma factor RpoA to activate transcription of target genes, such as mmpL (LP_RS02585), a gene encoding matrix-degrading metalloprotease. Binding by c-di-AMP represses both the affinity of RwpL for target DNA and its recruitment of RpoA, thus reversing the functions of RwpL. Our results show a dual and antagonistic regulatory mechanism through which c-di-AMP modulates probiotic activity in c-di-AMP-producing L. plantarum.

Results

WxlA mediates epithelial adhesion of L. plantarum in a c-di-AMP-dependent manner

GdpP is a c-di-AMP phosphodiesterase in L. plantarum WCFS125. Deletion of its coding gene increased the intracellular c-di-AMP levels (Supplementary Fig. 1). Moreover, the ΔgdpP mutant exhibited enhanced self-aggregation and adhesion to the human epithelial cell line Caco-2 compared to the wild-type strain (Fig. 1a, b). To gain insight into the biological functions regulated by c-di-AMP in L. plantarum, the transcriptomes of the wild-type strain and ΔgdpP strain were compared using RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq). The transcriptional levels of 148 genes were increased and those of 47 genes were decreased ( | log2 fold-change | >1.5 and P-value < 0.05 by the two-sided t-test adjusted with the Benjamini-Hochberg method) by in-frame deletion of gdpP (Fig. 1c). These differentially expressed genes were associated with a variety of biological processes, including polysaccharide production, carbohydrate transport and metabolism, and cell attachment (Supplementary Data 1). The RNA-Seq results were validated by quantitative reverse-transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis of selected genes (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Notably, we found that several genes encoding WxL proteins (Fig. 1d), including LP_RS15420 (wxlA), LP_RS12530 (wxlB), LP_RS12920 (wxlC), LP_RS14265 (wxlD), LP_RS14255 (wxlE), LP_RS14430 (wxlF), LP_RS14425 (wxlG), and LP_RS09195 (wxlH), are highly upregulated in the ΔgdpP strain (Supplementary Data 1). To determine whether these WxL proteins mediate the enhanced aggregation and adhesion observed in the ΔgdpP mutant, we individually deleted each corresponding gene. In-frame deletion of wxlA impaired self-aggregation and adhesion of L. plantarum WCFS1 (Fig. 1e, f), whereas deletion of other WxL protein-coding genes did not (Supplementary Fig. 2b, c). A double mutant was also constructed by deleting wxlA from the ΔgdpP mutant. Compared to the gdpP single-deletion mutant, the resulting ΔgdpPΔwxlA mutant exhibited reduced aggregation and epithelial adhesion (Fig. 1a, b). These results suggest that WxlA is one downstream effector of c-di-AMP signaling. Previous research has shown that WxL proteins from E. faecium bind to extracellular matrix proteins, including collagen and fibronectin, which are widely distributed on the surface of intestinal epithelial cells11,32,33. To test whether WxlA interacts with host matrix proteins, we expressed and purified WxlA. Cross-linking experiments followed by sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) analysis revealed that the purified WxlA forms oligomers in solution (Fig. 1g). Size exclusion chromatography (SEC) further confirmed that it is present as octamers (Supplementary Fig. 3). Using microscale thermophoresis (MST), we found that WxlA binds to fibronectin and collagen, with calculated dissociation constant (KD) values of 0.13 μM and 1.04 μM, respectively (Fig. 1h). These results indicate that WxlA promotes L. plantarum adhesion by interacting with host extracellular matrix proteins.

RwpL is a transcription inhibitor of wxlA

To identify the regulator of wxlA, a DNA pulldown assay was performed to enrich the proteins binding to the wxlA promoter from the cell lysate of L. plantarum WCFS1. A distinct protein band between 17 and 25 kDa was identified after separation by SDS-PAGE (Fig. 2a). Mass spectrometry analysis indicated that this protein matches LP_RS14275 with a molecular weight (MW) of 19.7 kDa (Supplementary Data 2). Domain structure analysis reveals that LP_RS14275 contains only a MarR-type HTH domain (Fig. 2b, c). We designated this protein RwpL (regulator of WxL protein in L. plantarum), as it likely regulates wxlA expression. Transcriptome analysis revealed that 674 genes were upregulated and 112 genes were downregulated in the ΔrwpL mutant compared to the wild-type strain ( | log2 fold-change | >1.5 and P-value < 0.05 by the t-test adjusted with the Benjamini-Hochberg method) (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Data 3). Comparative analysis of the transcriptomes of ΔgdpP and ΔrwpL mutants showed an overlap of target genes, including 107 upregulated and 20 downregulated genes (Fig. 2e), among which were wxlA and other WxL protein-encoding genes (Supplementary Data 4). Deletion of rwpL significantly increased the self-aggregation and adhesive ability of L. plantarum WCFS1 (Fig. 2f, g). These phenotypes were rescued by in trans expression of rwpL. To determine whether transcriptional regulation of wxlA is achieved by direct binding of RwpL to the wxlA promoter, we purified RwpL to perform electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSAs). Cross-linking experiments followed by SDS-PAGE analysis (Fig. 2h) and SEC (Supplementary Fig. 3) revealed that RwpL tends to form dimers. EMSA showed that RwpL binds the wxlA promoter, forming a stable DNA-protein complex with retarded electrophoretic mobility relative to free DNA (Fig. 2i). Moreover, the amount of DNA-RwpL complex increased in a RwpL-concentration-dependent manner. RwpL binds the wxlA promoter sequence specifically

Chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-Seq) was performed to determine the regulatory scope of RwpL by using the ΔrwpL(P23-rwpL) mutant in which FLAG-tagged RwpL was overexpressed. ChIP-Seq analysis of RwpL-FLAG revealed broad chromosomal occupancy across the L. plantarum WCFS1 genome, with enrichment signals sharply peaking at translation initiation sites (TISs) (Fig. 3a, b). We detected 235 peaks with more than 2-fold enrichment over input for RwpL, including peak_506, which contains the wxlA promoter (Supplementary Data 5). To identify the RwpL binding motif, we used the MEME-ChIP online tool (https://meme-suite.org/meme/doc/meme-chip.html) to examine the 300-bp sequences centered around each peak summit. A motif containing two inverted complementary sequences separated by five bases was identified (Fig. 3c). The motif implies that RwpL binds DNA as a dimer. To confirm the importance of the motif, EMSAs were performed with the wild-type wxlA promoter or variants with one or both mutated sites. While RwpL bound the wild-type promoter with a KD value of ~0.5 μM and the single-site mutant with a KD of ~2 μM, the DNA probe with double-mutated sites lost affinity to RwpL (Fig. 2i and Fig. 3d-f). We continued to construct a reporter plasmid (pPtac-rwpL-lacZ) in which expression of rwpL is controlled by a tac promoter (Ptac) followed by a lac operator, and β-galactosidase (LacZ) expression is controlled by a separate tac promoter followed by an RwpL binding sequence (Fig. 3g). The plasmid was transformed into Escherichia coli DH5α. If our hypothesis that RwpL binding suppresses transcription of target genes is correct, the introduction of isopropyl β-D-thiogalactoside (IPTG) should lead to a reduction in LacZ expression. c-di-AMP is a ligand of RwpL

Both gdpP and rwpL disruption upregulated wxlA transcription, and complementation of rwpL restored the phenotypes of the ΔgdpP mutant (Fig. 2f, g). These results suggest that c-di-AMP may affect RwpL functions through ligand-protein interaction. This possibility was proven by isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) analysis, which revealed a 1:1 binding stoichiometry between c-di-AMP and RwpL with a KD value of 6.45 μM (Fig. 4a). However, the related secondary messenger c-di-GMP or 3′,5′-cAMP failed to bind RwpL (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b). To identify the potential ligand binding sites, we generated the three-dimensional structure of RwpL using the AlphaFold Server (https://alphafoldserver.com/) and performed molecular docking analysis with AutoDock Vina 1.1.2. The best conformation of the RwpL-c-di-AMP complex has the lowest docking score of −9.6 kcal mol−1, suggesting that c-di-AMP makes close contact with RwpL. The dimeric RwpL structure contains two c-di-AMP binding pockets (Fig. 4b). Specifically, c-di-AMP is stabilized by two π-stacking interactions with H13 (chain A), a salt bridge with D45 (chain B), and several hydrogen bonds with H13 (chain A), D45 (chain B), D57 (chain B), and S61 (chain B), as analyzed by Protein-Ligand Interaction Profiler (Fig. 4c). Four single-point mutants were generated, and their affinities to c-di-AMP were measured using MST. c-di-AMP reduces the binding affinity of RwpL to target DNA

The impact of c-di-AMP on RwpL binding to the wxlA promoter was determined by using EMSA. Increasing c-di-AMP concentrations reduced RwpL binding to the DNA probe (Fig. 5a). However, mutation of the c-di-AMP-binding site (H13A) abolished the impact of c-di-AMP (Fig. 5b). Similarly, c-di-AMP decreased the RwpL binding to other tested DNA probes (Supplementary Fig. 7a-d). These results support the conclusion that c-di-AMP enhances wxlA expression by preventing RwpL binding to its promoter. To further validate this point, we constructed another reporter plasmid (pPtac-cdaA-lacZ) in which the cdaA encoding c-di-AMP synthase, rwpL, and lacZ are each expressed from separate tac promoters (Fig. 5c). The construct includes a lac operator before cdaA and an RwpL binding sequence before lacZ. If c-di-AMP indeed inhibits RwpL binding, IPTG-induced expression of CdaA should enhance LacZ expression in the c-di-AMP-null E. coli DH5α. As expected, both LacZ expression (Fig. 5d) and intracellular c-di-AMP levels (Supplementary Fig. 8a) increased in a dose-dependent manner with IPTG, while RwpL expression was consistent across different IPTG concentrations (Supplementary Fig. 8b). Those results support our model that c-di-AMP antagonizes RwpL-mediated repression. We also modeled the complex of RwpL with wxlA promoter and found that the DNA-binding site of RwpL is distinct from its c-di-AMP-binding site (Supplementary Fig. 9a, b). RwpL recruits sigma factor RpoA to activate mmpL transcription

Although RwpL acts as a transcriptional inhibitor of 694 genes, we also found that 112 genes were downregulated in the rwpL deletion mutant (Supplementary Data 3). Moreover, 55 of them contain a putative RwpL binding sequence. Those genes include LP_RS01255, which encodes a protein containing a peptidoglycan-binding domain, and LP_RS02585, which encodes a matrix-degrading metalloprotease (designated as MmpL). EMSAs revealed that RwpL binds the promoters of LP_RS01255 (Supplementary Fig. 5d) and mmpL (Supplementary Fig. 10a, b), corresponding to peak_67 and peak_126, respectively. Mutation of the RwpL-binding sequences completely abrogated RwpL binding to those DNA probes (Supplementary Fig. 5f and Supplementary Fig. 10c). The results suggest that RwpL also binds the promoters of its activated genes.

To investigate the activation mechanism, we performed a GST pull-down assay using GST-tagged RwpL as bait to capture RwpL-interacting proteins from the L. plantarum cell lysate. An additional band with an MW of 17 to 25 kDa was observed in the experimental sample compared to the control (Fig. 6a). Subsequent mass spectrometry identified the protein as LP_RS02645 (Supplementary Data 6). LP_RS02645 has an MW of 21.8 kDa and contains a sigma70_r2 domain (Fig. 6b). The protein is annotated as sigma-70 family RNA polymerase sigma factor and is therefore named RpoA. A bacterial two-hybrid assay based on 3′, 5′-cAMP synthesis in E. coli BTH101 further confirmed the interaction between RwpL and RpoA (Fig. 6c). CdaA overexpression in the c-di-AMP-null BTH101 strain demonstrated that c-di-AMP reduces the interaction between RwpL and RpoA (Fig. 6d), despite c-di-AMP not binding RpoA (Supplementary Fig. 11). Notably, overexpression of CdaA failed to disrupt the interaction between RpoA and RwpLH13A (Fig. 6d), confirming the specificity of c-di-AMP in modulating the RwpL-RpoA interaction. We noticed that the RwpL-binding sequences in the mmpL and LP_RS01255 promoters lie upstream of their TSSs (Fig. 6e, f). Meanwhile, the RpoA-binding regions are located downstream of the RwpL-binding site (Fig. 6g, h). To further determine the RpoA-binding site, we introduced mutations in the -10 and -35 regions of the mmpL and LP_RS01255 promoters. Discussion

It is believed that c-di-AMP regulates bacterial functions by binding to various receptor proteins via diverse domains29,34. The identified domains that sense this signaling molecule include CT domains, RCK_C domain, tandem CBS domains, and USP domain28–31. In this study, we identified RwpL, which contains only an HTH domain, as a c-di-AMP receptor protein in L. plantarum WCFS1 (Figs. 2c and 4a). To our knowledge, there is no established evidence that HTH domains directly bind c-di-AMP. With the identification of RwpL, we have linked c-di-AMP signaling to bacterial-host interaction of L. plantarum. Interestingly, this protein distinguishes c-di-AMP from c-di-GMP (Supplementary Fig. 6a), which regulates the mucin-binding activity of L. plantarum35. The observed functional difference provides a basis for the precise and complex regulatory network of signaling nucleotides. Moreover, the genes regulated by GdpP and RwpL do not entirely overlap (Fig. 2e), indicating the existence of other as yet unidentified c-di-AMP-dependent transcription factors in L. plantarum WCFS1.

Activation or inhibition of gene transcription is precisely controlled by various transcription factors. In this study, we reveal that RwpL functions as both a transcription inhibitor and activator (Supplementary Data 3). The inhibitory effect of transcription factors could result from steric hindrance, which is applied in CRISPR interference36. In contrast, the impacts of the transcriptional activator are more complex. Many MerR-family transcription factors activate gene transcription by binding the promoter to alter DNA topology or to adapt RNA polymerase (RNAP) through remodeling or bridging contacts37,38. The transcription factors Crl and TraR interact only with RNAP rather than promoter DNA to activate transcription39,40. The transcription activators PafBC and DdaA bind to the promoter region and recruit RNAP41,42. Disruption of PafBC and DdaA also results in widespread upregulation of gene transcription42,43, indicating that promoter-binding transcription factors exert dual roles in regulating target gene expression. However, the underlying mechanism has remained elusive. Our findings reveal that the transcriptional outcome of a DNA-binding transcription factor is dictated by its binding position relative to the TSS (Fig. 7). Specifically, when RwpL binds downstream of the TSS of its target genes, it functions as a transcriptional repressor. In contrast, binding upstream of the TSS enables RwpL to recruit the sigma factor, thereby activating transcription.

Surface proteins, which are expressed on the bacterial cell surface, play critical roles in cell wall synthesis, maintenance of cell morphology, and signaling with the host44. Many of those proteins have been implicated in facilitating L. plantarum adhesion to mucosal and epithelial surfaces. For example, glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) has been shown to bind mucus components and extracellular matrices45,46. WxL proteins are recently identified surface proteins of many commensal gut bacteria and are involved in diverse functions such as cell wall binding, stress tolerance, and adherence10,11. Our results demonstrated that WxlA binds to fibronectin with high affinity and, by doing so, mediates L. plantarum attachment to Caco‑2 cells (Fig. 1f, h). However, the protein did not impact intracellular c-di-AMP levels (Supplementary Fig. 1). Although eight proteins in L. plantarum WCFS1 contain a conserved WxL domain, wxlA is the only one whose deletion affects cell aggregation (Fig. 1e). We speculate that the other WxL domain-containing proteins may regulate distinct phenotypes, given that the WxL domain can also bind alternative ligands such as peptidoglycan and other proteins10,47. This speculation is further supported by the presence of additional functional domains in the WxlE and WxlH (Fig. 1d).

MarR transcriptional regulators are widely distributed among bacteria, leading us to presume that the RwpL homologs might be highly conserved. Interestingly, however, no high-similarity homologs of RwpL were identified in Limosilactobacillus rhamnosus and Limosilactobacillus fermentum through BLAST searches against the NCBI Reference Proteins database. Instead, moderately conserved homologs (with an identity of only about 35% to RwpL) were found in other Lactobacillaceae, such as Limosilactobacillus frumenti, Limosilactobacillus pontis, and Limosilactobacillus reuteri (Supplementary Data 7). However, these homologs lack the conserved c-di-AMP-binding residue H13 (Supplementary Fig. 15). We also performed an intraspecies homology analysis of RwpL across different L. plantarum strains. Through BLAST searches against the NCBI Reference Proteins database, we identified 58 RwpL homologs in sequenced L. plantarum strains (Supplementary Fig. 16). Further examination revealed that 23 of these homologs contain conserved c-di-AMP-binding sites. Notably, these 23 homologs are found across 1377 out of 1394 L. plantarum strains (Supplementary Data 8), suggesting that RwpL is highly conserved at the intraspecies level. These results suggest that c-di-AMP-mediated regulation may exhibit species-specific or strain-specific functional adaptations.

The human intestinal lumen is lined with a mucus layer secreted by goblet cells, which produce rod-shaped mucins48. Ranging from 50 to 800 μm in thickness, this gel-like barrier serves as the primary interface between intestinal bacteria and the epithelia, shielding the host from potentially harmful antigens and molecules49. L. plantarum could exert beneficial effects through indirect interaction with the host, such as modulating the microbial community and producing metabolites like indole-3-lactic acid and lactate. These metabolites respectively regulate CD8+ T cell immunity and accelerate intestinal stem-cell-mediated epithelial development50,51. Meanwhile, mucus integrity is compromised in specific populations. Patients with ulcerative colitis and the elderly exhibit reduced mucus thickness, particularly at sites of active inflammation52–54. Various pathogens, such as Helicobacter pylori, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Candida albicans, have evolved strategies to degrade mucus via reduction of mucin disulfide bonds, protease activity, and glycosidase activity55–57. Consequently, direct interaction with the epithelium represents a critical alternative strategy for probiotics to confer benefits, such as competing with pathogens and potentiating the tumor suppressive effect of anti-PD-149,58. Beyond WxlA, several L. plantarum proteins have been identified as mediators of epithelial adhesion, including elongation factor Tu, triosephosphate isomerase, and EnoA159–61.

Methods

Bacterial strain and growth conditions

All mutants used in this study are listed in Supplementary Data 9. E. coli DH5α and BTH101 were maintained in Lysogeny Broth (LB) at 37 °C and 30 °C, respectively. L. plantarum WCFS1 wild-type strain and its mutants were cultured in standard DeMan, Rogosa and Sharpe (MRS) broth at 37 °C. When needed, ampicillin (100 μg/ml), kanamycin (50 μg/ml), chloramphenicol (20 μg/ml for E. coli, 10 μg/ml for L. plantarum), or erythromycin (10 μg/ml) were added.

Plasmid constructions

All plasmids used are listed in Supplementary Data 10. Plasmids for this study were created through In-Fusion cloning. Plasmids were transformed into E. coli via heat shock and verified by sequencing. Primers used in this study were synthesized by GENEWIZ and are listed in Supplementary Data 11. All plasmids generated in this study are available upon request from the corresponding author.

To generate pHSP02 derivatives, a guide RNA framework was amplified from the pHSP01 plasmid62, and two 1000 bp fragments flanking the target genes were amplified using L. plantarum WCFS1 genomic DNA. The abovementioned fragments were cloned into the ApaI and XbaI sites of pHSP02.

To construct pPtac-rwpL-lacZ, the pMAL-c5x expression plasmid was digested with EcoRI and HindIII restriction enzymes. A DNA fragment, which contains a tac promoter, an RwpL binding sequence of wxlA, and lacZ, was amplified from plasmid pME2-lacZ. The PCR product was cloned into the digested pMAL-c5x. Following sequencing, the generated pMAL-lacZ plasmid was digested with MfeI and SalI. A fragment encoding RwpL with a C‑terminal FLAG tag was amplified and cloned into the digested pMAL-lacZ plasmid.

The pPtac-cdaA-lacZ reporter construct was generated by cloning two DNA fragments into the MfeI and SalI sites of the pMAL-lacZ plasmid: one containing the rwpL in frame with a FLAG epitope tag and a tac promoter, and the other encompassing the CadA coding sequence.

The pPmmpL-egfp reporting plasmid was constructed by cloning the mmpL promoter (PmmpL) and egfp into the EcoRI sites of the pLH01. The resulting plasmid was digested with NheI and ligated with the fusion of orfX promoter and rwpL, rpoA, or cdaA.

To generate complement plasmids, the coding sequences of target genes with their promoter were amplified by PCR and cloned into the NheI site of pLH01.

Preparation of electrocompetent cells and transformation

Bacteria were sub-cultured in sgMRS (MRS supplemented with 1% glycine and 0.2 M sucrose) and grown to an optimal density of 600 nm (OD600) = 0.4–0.6. Following washing twice with 10 mM MgCl2, cells were resuspended in 1 mL of electroporation buffer (3.5 mM MgCl2, 952 mM sucrose). The plasmid (0.5 μg) was mixed with electrocompetent cells (60 μL) and transferred into a 2 mm cuvette (Bio-Rad). Electroporation was carried out at 2.1 kV (Eporator, Eppendorf, Germany). The cells were recovered with 1 mL recovery buffer (MRS supplemented with 0.1 M MgCl2 and 0.5 M sucrose), and cultured for 3 h at 37 °C. After that, the cells were disseminated on chloramphenicol (10 μg/mL) containing MRS agar plates.

Genome editing

For the generation of all mutants, the transformant carrying the pLH01 was used to prepare electrocompetent cells62. The SppIP inducible peptide (sequence: MAGNSSNFIHKIKQIFTHR), which was synthesized by GenScript (Nanjing, China), at a final concentration of 100 ng/mL, was added when the OD600 reached 0.3. Then, the cells were collected when OD600 reached 0.4–0.5 and prepared to be competent as described above. The electrocompetent cells were transformed with pHSP02 derivatives (1 μg), followed by 3 h recovery at 37 °C. The mutants were plated on the MRS agar medium containing erythromycin (10 μg/mL) and chloramphenicol (10 μg/mL). After verification by PCR and sequencing, the plasmids in the mutants were cured.

Protein expression and purification

Target sequences were amplified by PCR using L. plantarum WCFS1 genomic DNA as templates. Mutant constructs were generated via overlapping-based mutagenesis. The purified PCR products were inserted into the HindIII and XhoI sites of the pET-SUMO vector (Miaoling Biotechnology, Inc.). The generated plasmids were then transformed into E. coli strain BL21(DE3). Cells were grown at 37 °C to an OD600 of 0.5, induced with 1 mM isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactoside (IPTG), and incubated at 16 °C overnight. Harvested cells were resuspended in Lysis buffer (100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl) and subjected to sonication. Proteins were purified using Ni-NTA resin (Thermal), followed by cleavage with SUMO protease (P2312S, Beyotime, China).

c-di-AMP extraction and detection

WCFS1 and its mutants were grown in MRS broth at 37 °C for 16 h. Harvested cells were washed twice with cooled water and resuspended in 0.6 M perchloric acid (Sigma). The soluble fraction was neutralized with 4 M KHCO3. After centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 5 min, c-di-AMP concentration in the supernatant was quantified using the c-di-AMP ELISA kit (Cayman Chemical, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Self-aggregation assay

WCFS1 and its derivative mutants were cultured in MRS broth at 37 °C for 16 h. Cells were harvested, washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and resuspended in PBS to an OD600 of 0.6. The suspensions were then incubated at 37 °C for 6 h. Following incubation, 200 μL of supernatant was carefully collected, and its absorbance at 600 nm was measured. The self-aggregation rate was calculated as follows: Self−aggregation (%) = (1− ODt /OD0) × 100, where OD0 is the initial optical density, and ODt is the optical density at the time of measurement.

Cell adhesion experiments

Human colorectal adenocarcinoma cell line Caco-2 cells (CBP60025) were obtained from Nanjing Cobioer Biosciences Co. Ltd. The cells were incubated at 37 °C under 5% CO2. The logarithmic L. plantarum cells were harvested by centrifugation, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in DMEM at a final concentration of 108 CFU/mL. 1 × 105 Caco-2 cells were seeded in 96-well culture plates until reaching full confluence. The supernatant was replaced by 100 μL of bacterial suspension. Following incubation for 4 h, unattached bacteria were rinsed. The adhered L. plantarum was released by incubating the Caco-2 cells with Triton X-100 (1%, v/v) for 20 min at 4 °C, and quantified by plating serial dilutions of the lysate onto MRS agar.

Western blotting

Briefly, the logarithmic cells (OD600 = 1.0) were collected and washed three times with PBS. Cell pellets were resuspended in 100 μL of SDS-PAGE loading buffer, followed by boiling for 10 min. The samples were separated on 10% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Millipore). After blocking with Blocking Buffer (BP0222, Beyotime, China), the membranes were incubated with the Flag Tag Mouse Monoclonal Antibody (HRP Conjugated) (AF2855, Beyotime, China) in Blocking buffer at 4 °C. Chemiluminescent signals were detected using SignalFire ECL Reagent (Cell Signaling Technology, USA).

Microscale thermophoresis (MST) assay

RwpL, fibronectin (Thermo Fisher), and Collagen IV (Merck) were labeled using the L001 Monolith NT.115 Protein Labeling Kit (NanoTemper Technologies). Unlabeled c-di-AMP or protein was serially diluted in equal-fold steps and mixed with the labeled protein. The resulting mixtures were loaded into standard-treated silicon capillaries (Monolith NT.115 Capillaries, Type 19) for microscale thermophoresis (MST) analysis. The fluorescence intensity was determined by a Monolith NT.115 instrument (NanoTemper Technologies).

DNA pull-down assay

The wild-type strain of L. plantarum WCFS1 was grown to OD600 = 0.6 at 37 °C in 1 L of sgMRS. The cells were then collected, resuspended with 10 mL Binding buffer [100 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.5), 300 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mM DTT, 0.5% (v/v) Triton X-100, 1 × protease inhibitor], and then subjected to ultrasonication. One milliliter of supernatant was collected and mixed with biotin-labelled WxlA promoter, which was amplified by PCR using Biotin-labelled primer. Following incubation at 4 °C overnight, Streptavidin Dynabeads were added to the mixture and incubated for 30 min. The Dynabeads were washed three times with binding buffer and treated with 95% formamide (10 mM EDTA, pH 8.2). The supernatant was separated by SDS-PAGE. The gel containing the indicated band was cut. The gel particles underwent sequential washing with a mixture of ethanol, acetic acid, and water (4:1:5, by volume), followed by dehydration using acetonitrile and subsequent digestion with trypsin at a concentration of 0.01 μg/μL trypsin. The digests were then subjected to LC‑MS/MS analysis35. Peptide separation was achieved on an Easy-nLC 1200 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) equipped with a home-packed C18 column (75 μm internal diameter, 1.9 μm column size, 25 cm column length). The flow rate was 200 nL/min, and the separation employed a gradient with mobile phase A consisting of 0.1% formic acid in water and mobile phase B consisting of 80% acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid. The gradient program was set as follows: 0–3 min at 8% B; 3 -45 min, B increased linearly from 8% to 44%; at 45–50 min, B was raised to 60%; at 50–53 min, B was further increased to 100%; and finally from 53 to 60 min, B was maintained at 80%. The outlet of the nanoLC column was directly coupled to a nano-electrospray ionization (nanoESI) source and subsequently to an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), operating in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode. The spray voltage was set to 2.1 kV. MS1 full scans were performed with a resolution of 60,000 over an m/z range of 350–1,600. Precursor ions with charge states between 2+ and 7+ and intensity exceeding 50,000 were selected for MS2 fragmentation using higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD). The dynamic exclusion window was 30 seconds. Automatic gain control (AGC) targets were configured at 1 × 106 for MS1 and 1 × 105 for MS2. Acquired MS/MS spectra were searched against the UniProt protein database with Mascot (version 2.3.02). To enhance identification accuracy, search outputs were pre-processed and re-scored with Percolator (version 2.04), followed by filtering at a 1% false discovery rate at the peptide-spectrum match level (PSM-level FDR ≤ 0.01). Proteins supported by three or more unique peptides were considered positive identifications. The LC-MS run was carried out in singlicate.

Protein pull-down assay

The RwpL CDS was amplified by using L. plantarum WCFS1 genomic DNA as the template and cloned into the pGEX-6p-1 vector. The generated construct was then transformed into E. coli strain BL21(DE3). Protein was expressed as described above. Then, the cells were collected and resuspended in binding buffer (100 mM Tirs, pH 8.0, 200 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 5% glycerol).

The wild-type strain of L. plantarum WCFS1 was grown to an optical density of 0.6 in sgMRS. The cells were then collected and resuspended in binding buffer.

The E. coli and L. plantarum cells were mixed and subjected to ultrasonication. The mixture was then centrifuged at 4 °C. The GST-tagged RwpL with its interaction proteins was purified using BeyoMagTM Magnetic Agarose Beads for GST-Tag Protein Purification and was separated using SDS-PAGE. The RwpL-interacting proteins were analyzed by LC‑MS/MS analysis as described above.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA)

Briefly, the DNA fragments were amplified by PCR using FAM-labelled primers and purified using the EZNA Cycle-Pure Kit (Omega Bio-tek Inc.). Indicated concentrations of protein, DNA probes, and c-di-AMP were mixed and incubated in EMSA buffer (100 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 10% glycerol, 300 mM NaCl, and 50 mM MgCl2) for 30 min at 37 °C. After incubation, the mixture was loaded onto a 5% polyacrylamide gel and separated by electrophoresis in 0.5 × TBE (Tris-Borate-EDTA) buffer at 4 °C. The gel was pre-run in 0.5 × TBE for 0.5 h. Gels were imaged by Image Lab software (Bio-Rad).

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from L. plantarum WCFS1 cultures harvested at the mid-exponential phase (OD600 = 1.0) using the RNeasy Protect Bacteria Mini Kit (Qiagen, Denmark). Sequencing libraries were prepared from double-stranded cDNA and subjected to high-throughput sequencing. The raw reads were quality-trimmed and aligned to the L. plantarum WCFS1 reference genome using Bowtie2 (v2.2.3). Gene expression levels were quantified as normalized read counts with HTSeq (v0.6.1) and FPKM values. Differential expression analysis was performed with a stringent threshold, identifying genes with an absolute log2 (fold change) ≥ 1.5 and a false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted p-value < 0.05 as statistically significant.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-Seq)

A pLH01 derivative was generated by inserting a p23 promoter, FLAG tag, and rwpL into the NheI site of pLH01. The construct was transformed into the ΔrwpL mutant, which was then grown in MRS liquid until it reached logarithmic growth (6 h). Cross-linking was initiated by formaldehyde at a final concentration of 1% (v/v) and stopped by glycine at a final concentration of 125 mM, respectively. ChIP assays were performed by Wuhan IGENEBOOK Biotechnology Co., Ltd, using Anti-DDDDK tag (Binds to FLAG® tag sequence) antibody [EPR20018-251] (Abcam, ab205606). Immunoprecipitated DNA was sequenced using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 with the PE 150 method. Low-quality reads were filtered out using Trimmomatic (version 0.38). The clean reads were mapped to the L. plantarum WCFS1 genome by BWA (version 0.7.15). Potential PCR duplicates were removed using SAMtools (version 1.3.1). Peaks were called using MACS2 software (version 2.1.1.20160309) with default parameters (bandwidth, 300 bp; model fold, 5, 50; q value, 0.05). MEME-ChIP (version 3) was used to predict motif occurrence within peaks with default settings for a maximum motif length of 20 base pairs.

Reverse-transcriptase quantitative PCR

L. plantarum cells were cultured to an OD600 = 1.0 and then harvested. RNA was isolated using TRIZOL Reagent (Thermol). cDNA was synthesized using ChamQTM Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). RT-qPCR analysis was performed with a qTOWER 3 G (Analytic Jena, Jena, Germany). Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with 16S RNA serving as the internal control and the wild-type (WT) strain as the reference condition.

Size exclusion chromatography (SEC)

SEC analysis of RwpL and WxlA was performed on a HITACHI Chromaster high-performance liquid chromatography system (Hitachi, Japan) equipped with a UV/Vis detector (280 nm) at 30 °C. Separation was achieved using a Nanochrom Biocore SEC-150 column (4.6 × 300 mm). The column was equilibrated with PBS at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. After equilibration, 10 μL of protein sample (0.5 mg/mL) was directly injected into the column. Thyroglobulin (669 kDa), Conalbumin (78 kDa), and Ribonuclease-A (13.7 kDa) (MCE) were run as markers.

Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) assay

ITC assays were performed using MicroCal PEAQ-ITC microcalorimeter (Malvern Panalytical GmbH, Germany). Both RwpL and c-di-AMP (MCE) were dissolved in Tris buffer (100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl). Titrations were carried out at 25 °C by injection of c-di-AMP solution (400 μM) into the calorimeter cell containing RwpL solution (30 μM). The first injection was 0.4 μL, followed by twelve injections of 3 μL each.

Molecular docking analysis

The RwpL structure was built by Alphafold (https://alphafold.com/). The three-dimensional structure of c-di-AMP was extracted from the complex of KhtT-c-di-AMP (PDB: 7AGV). To identify the potential binding sites, a docking grid encompassing the entire RwpL dimer was generated. The docking was performed with AutoDock Vina 1.1.2 using an exhaustiveness value of 50. The conformation with the lowest binding energy was selected, and putative binding interactions were visualized and analyzed using PyMOL and Protein-Ligand Interaction Profiler (PLIP, https://plip-tool.biotec.tu-dresden.de/plip-web/plip/index).

Bacterial two-hybrid assay (BTHA)

BTHA is based on 3′, 5′-cAMP synthesis in E. coli BTH10163. The encoding regions of RwpL and RpoA were amplified using L. plantarum WCFS1 genomic DNA and inserted into the HindIII and EcoRI sites of pUT18 or pKNT25, respectively. The resulting plasmid pair was co-transformed into E. coli BTH101 competent cells. Transformants were spotted on LB agar plates containing ampicillin (100 μg/mL), kanamycin (50 μg/mL), IPTG (0.5 mM), and X-gal (40 μg/L) and incubated at 30 °C. Protein-protein interaction between RwpL and RpoA was assessed by blue clone formation or the determination of the β-galactosidase activity64.

Fluorescence assays

Strains transformed with reporter plasmids were grown in MRS supplemented with 10 μg/mL chloramphenicol at 37 °C to an OD600 of 0.5. Following centrifugation and three PBS washes, the fluorescence signal was detected with a Synergy H1 reader (BioTek, USA). Fluorescence was blank-subtracted and then normalized with the OD600 value obtained at the same sampling time.

β-galactosidase assays

E. coli DH5α and BTH101 were cultured in Luria–Bertani (LB) medium at 37 °C or 30 °C, respectively, with shaking until the OD600 reached approximately 1.0. 1 mL aliquots were harvested for β-galactosidase activity assays following a standard protocol64. In brief, cell pellets were resuspended in 1 mL of Z buffer (40 mM NaH2PO4, 60 mM Na2HPO4, 1 mM MgSO4, 10 mM KCl, and 50 mM 2-Mercaptoethanol). A 250 μL portion of this suspension was mixed with 750 μL of Z buffer containing lysozyme (0.25 mg/mL). Following incubation at 37 °C for 15 min, the reaction was initiated by adding 200 μL of 2-nitrophenyl-β-D-galactopyranoside (4 mg/mL in Z buffer) and terminated by the addition of 500 μL of 1 M Na2CO3. The reaction time, along with absorbance readings at 420 nm (A420) and 550 nm (OD550), was recorded. β-Galactosidase activity was expressed in Miller units and calculated using the formula: [A420-1.75 × (OD550)] / (time [min] × OD600) × dilution factor × 1000.

Capping-dependent rapid amplification of cDNA ends (Capping-RACE)

Capping-RACE was performed to detect the transcription start sites of genes65. Briefly, a total of 10 μg RNA was treated with 1 μL of Vaccinia Capping System (M2080S, NEB) and purified by ethanol precipitation. The capped RNA (0.5 μg) and GSP1 (2 pmol) were mixed and incubated at 65 °C for 5 min, followed by chilling on ice. The reverse transcription reaction (10 μL total volume) contained 0.5 μL SuperScript III Reverse Transcriptase (18080093, Invitrogen), 1 μL of 10 mM dNTPs, 0.5 μL of 2 mM DTT, and 2 μL of 5 × buffer. After reaction at 50 °C for 60 min, a second mixture comprising 2 μL of 0.1% bovine serum albumin (BSA), 0.8 μL of 50 mM MnCl2, 1 μL of template switching oligonucleotide (TOS), 2 μL of 5 × buffer, 0.5 μL of DTT, 0.5 μL of reverse transcriptase, and 3.2 μL DEPC-treated water was added. The combined reaction was then incubated at 42 °C for 90 min, followed by inactivation at 70 °C for 10 min. The resulting DNA was used as a template for the first-round PCR with the outer primer and GSP1. The amplicon was diluted and subjected to a second-round PCR using the inner primer and GSP2. Final PCR products were purified and cloned into the pET28a vector for sequencing.

Phylogenetic analysis

The sequences of RwpL homologues from different strains of L. plantarum or other bacteria were collected using BLAST against the NCBI Reference Proteins database with the RwpL amino acid sequence as query. Multiple sequence alignment was performed using ClustalW in MEGA (version 12) with default parameters, followed by trimming of positions with > 50% gaps. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the neighbor-joining method with the Poisson model for amino acid substitutions. Branch support was assessed with 1000 bootstrap replicates. The tree was visualized using iTOL.

Statistics and reproducibility

The data are presented as the means ± standard deviations (SDs) from at least three independent experiments and analyzed using Prism 8 software (GraphPad). Statistical significance was performed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparison test
