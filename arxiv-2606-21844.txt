Inverse Turing Bench: Evaluating Language Models as Judges of Human vs. AI Dialogue
William Hager, Ishika Rathi, Masum Hasan, Cameron Jones

Abstract
As AI systems integrate into online spaces, differentiating them from humans in conversations is increasingly important. We present Inverse Turing Bench , a benchmark that evaluates LLMs and other models on their ability to differentiate humans and AI in multi-turn text. The benchmark provides a collection of paired dialogue transcripts, wherein one dialogue is between two humans and the other is between a human and an AI. The task is to correctly identify which dialogue is human-only vs. human-AI. We evaluated a preliminary set of models against this benchmark, and found that GPTZero, Claude Opus-4.6, and GPT-5.5 achieve the highest accuracy: 89.41%, 77.92%, and 75.94% respectively. Our results suggest that statistical approaches to detection have semantic blind spots, but semantic approaches are susceptible to persona-prompting. Our work speaks to the Inverse Turing Test as a probe of LLM theory of mind, and motivates human-AI differentiation as a critical capability for AI systems. Our live benchmark can be found at https://huggingface.co/spaces/roc-hci/Inverse-Turing-Bench-Leaderboard (anonymity preserved).

The internet is shifting from a space of human interaction to a mixed ecosystem of humans and AI agents

Current AI detection can be categorized as: i) classical methods that use statistical metrics such as likelihood to classify text, ii) supervised classifiers fine-tuned on labeled data, and iii) zero- and few-shot LLM-as-judge techniques

2
Inverse Turing Benchmark

2.1
Dataset

Our dataset is drawn from
13
, filtered for dialogues of length 50 or more to ensure sufficient conversational depth. It features 557 pairs of dialogues as part of a 3-party Turing Test, in which a human
Interrogator
engages in dialogue with two
Witnesses
: one human and one AI. In the 3-party test, the interrogator must determine which of these witnesses is human. Figure
2
and Table
2
in the Appendix show the distribution of dialogue length and a sample dialogue pair.

The AI witnesses include four different AI systems: Eliza
29
, GPT-4.5
20
, GPT-4o
19
, and LLaMA-405B
14
. The dataset also includes
Persona
variants of GPT-4.5 and LLaMA-405B, which contain an additional system prompt of a college student persona. Appendix Table
3
shows Witness model distribution.

2.2
Task

Each item in the task consists of a pair of dialogues, labeled "A" and "B", as input. Each dialogue is a conversation between interrogator ("I") and witness ("W"). The interrogator is always human. Of the two dialogues, exactly one features a human witness, while the other features an AI witness. The classifier must determine which label corresponds to the human–human conversation. Given dialogue transcripts
A
,
B
, the judge predicts a binary label:

who\_is\_human\in\{\text{A},\text{B}\}.

2.3
Live Benchmark

Inverse Turing Bench
is publicly available at
https://huggingface.co/spaces/roc-hci/Inverse-Turing-Bench-Leaderboard
. It includes a live leaderboard displaying submissions, provides an explanation of the task, and offers a submission portal through which users can submit their own classification labels for evaluation. For submission, users either download the raw dataset containing only the conversation pairs or obtain the provided “starter code," which includes additional framework and infrastructure for evaluation, including the prompt used in this study.

2.4
Learnable patterns in dataset

To check for learnable superficial patterns in the dataset, we trained TF-IDF and DistilBERT
22
classifiers using ten-fold cross-validation on labeled data (Table
1
). TF-IDF (witness turns only) achieved 47.04% and DistilBERT (full-dialogue) achieved 78.46% accuracy. DistilBERT’s accuracy indicates that there are clearly syntactic and semantic patterns across dialogues that can be learned. However, this pattern cannot be captured by word frequency alone, as implied by the poor performance of TF-IDF. Existence of such patterns suggests that the benchmark is effective on a zero-shot basis, and may not be as effective if the models are specifically trained with this data.

We evaluated a suite of models on the benchmark task. The models fall into two main categories: token-probability-based judges and LLM-as-a-judge (Table
1
).

3.1
Token Probability Models

Token-probability-based models calculate how likely a token is to be generated by a language model given a previous string of tokens. High likelihood implies LLM-generated text, and low likelihood implies human-written text
17
;
31
. We evaluated two token-probability-based models: GPTZero
2
, a commercial model, and DetectGPT
17
, an open source model.

GPTZero
is an AI-detection tool that classifies a text string as human-generated, AI-generated, or mixed, and provides probability scores for each label. Using this label as a binary classification task, GPTZero achieved 77.38% classification accuracy on the Witness turns of the individual dialogues.

To adapt GPTZero for the Inverse 3-party Turing Test, we pass both dialogues and choose the dialogue with a higher probability score for “human” as prediction. We evaluated GPTZero in two different settings: Witness-only and full dialogue.

DetectGPT
measures how much a model’s log probability for a passage decreases under small meaning-preserving perturbations. We used OPT-125M
33
as the scoring model. For each dialogue, we generated perturbations, computed perturbation discrepancy, and classified the one with larger discrepancy as AI-generated.

GPTZero achieved an accuracy of 89.41% for witness-only dialogues and 86.16% for full dialogues. DetectGPT was 46.32% accurate.

3.2
LLM-as-a-Judge

We evaluated a suite of LLMs on this benchmark. For each model, we passed each pair of dialogues as a "zero-shot" classification task. We prompted the LLM to output in JSON format which dialogue is human/human, and to ignore the interrogator turns and focus only on the witness turns (Appendix
4
). The model providers used were OpenAI, Anthropic, Qwen, and Moonshot AI. Qwen 3.6 was run on a local Ollama server with 2 Nvidia A6000 GPUs; all other models were accessed via API.

Top performing LLMs included Anthropic’s Claude Opus 4.6
4
("Max" reasoning 77.92%, "No" reasoning 77.56%), and OpenAI’s GPT 5.5
21
("High" reasoning 75.94%, "Low" reasoning 74.87%). This performance is consistent with the growing model intelligence over time – showing that, in general, recent models perform better than older models, larger models outperform smaller models, and higher reasoning effort outperforms non-reasoning models. However, all these patterns have clear and notable exceptions, showing that identifying human dialogue is more than just model intelligence.

4
Discussion

Figure 1:
Accuracy of top performing models compared to humans, by AI witness.
GPT-5.5 and Opus-4.6 accuracy dropped for persona-prompted witnesses. GPTZero was successful for all witnesses except Eliza.

Of the preliminary models tested, GPTZero performed best, at 86-89%. This implies that contemporary statistical detection methods can be effective at mixed multi-turn AI-detection, with appropriate scaffolding. If statistical tools like GPTZero are successful, why do we need to benchmark this capability in LLMs?

Semantic vs. Statistical Detection
In Figure
1
, we break down the top-performing judge models thusfar by Witness models, and an interesting pattern emerges. Though GPTZero is a relatively successful detector for most LLM witnesses, its accuracy dropped to 59.02% for Eliza
1
1

1

Eliza is natural language processing computer program developed in the 1960s. Unlike LLMs, it uses simple pattern- matching and keyword-substitution rules to simulate conversations
29
.
. As a token-probability-based model, GPTZero is trained to extract statistical patterns from popular LLMs rather than semantic qualities of text. However, when it is presented with a chatbot that is obviously AI, but different from its training data, it fails notably. By contrast, Opus-4.6 and GPT-5.5 identified Eliza as AI 100% of the time. This suggests that GPTZero is more of an LLM detector than an AI detector. It may not be as effective with a different architecture or data distribution.

Persona Prompt Attack
Opus-4.6, GPT-5.5, and the human judge’s performance dropped when the AI witness was created with a persona prompt, as opposed to no persona (Opus: 95.80% vs. 59.04%; GPT-5.5: 93.36% vs. 57.56%; Human: 68.53% vs 39.85%), while GPTZero’s performance remains consistent. In fact, this is the only scenario where GPTZero outperforms Opus-4.6, and human judges perform below chance. This implies that both LLM and human judges are susceptible to carefully crafted prompt personas, while token-based models are more resilient to this attack.

The semantic approach considers deeper parts of the text, such as coherence, pragmatics, conversational dynamics, reasoning, and contextual consistency across turns. This approach seems to be more susceptible to prompt-engineering attacks, however, in theory, may be less susceptible to statistical attacks. Future studies should empirically explore the differences between these two approaches, including their efficacy across domains, susceptibility to attacks, and whether a combined approach improves accuracy and resilience.

Inverse Turing Test
The Inverse Turing test, implemented here in the context of AI detection benchmarking, is primarily a differentiation task. The ITT was said to be passed if a machine judge performs on-par with human judges, as this would demonstrate a latent model of human vs. machine behavior that is similar and equal to that of humans
28
. In our setting, however, several LLM judge models performed higher than human judges. What does this imply about their naive psychology?

Watt theorized that higher-than-human performance could indicate dissimilar intelligence with superior discriminative ability. However, the best-performing judge models were released later than the witness models they evaluated. Superior performance may partly stem from exposure to outputs from these witness models during training. Future work should examine whether newer models are equally effective at detecting content from contemporary systems within and across model families.

AI Agents & Theory of Mind
The Inverse test probes LLM theory of mind. Understanding and benchmarking this capability in LLMs is increasingly important. As AI applications become more agentic, (M)LLMs autonomously interact with humans and other AI systems. In order to be effective in multi-agent interaction, autonomous agents should have the ability to discriminate human and AI behavior. Recognizing LLM content will enable these agents to become active partners in protecting human spaces and, eventually, protect themselves from adversarial AI agents.

5
Conclusion

Human-AI differentiation in multi-turn settings is both a challenging detection task and a critical capability for future LLMs. Rapid advances in model and agentic capabilities make live benchmarking essential for evaluating these systems over time. We built
Inverse Turing Bench
, a benchmark for measuring how well models can discriminate between multi-turn human/human and human/AI dialogue. Preliminary results found that statistical and semantic approaches leave blind spots. Our initial findings call for further evaluation of different tools, such as agentic tools or models that combine statistical-semantic approaches.

6
Limitations

This benchmark uses transcripts from interactive Turing tests, which built witness personas using GPT-4.5, GPT-4o, and LLaMA-405B. The benchmark may not account for detection of more recent models, or other model families. The human judges in the original Turing test often use adversarial questioning to detect AI, so transcripts may contain more relevant information for detection than unsuspecting Human-AI conversations. Another important limitation of the benchmark is the lack of diversity in the AI witness persona. Risks of this study include use of the benchmark or its dataset for training LLMs to be less detectable; to adapt the benchmark for such future challenges, we would re-release with more complex dialogues. Future work could test longer conversations where neither agent is suspecting AI, add more diverse personas, or conduct interactive Inverted tests.
