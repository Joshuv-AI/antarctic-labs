 3. Executive Summary: In this project we developed a very sensitive magnetic
 resonance technique – electrically-detected magnetic resonance (EDMR) – to identify
 atomic origins of performance- and reliability-limiting defects in photovoltaic (PV)
 devices. The result and impact is a new, ultrasensitive characterization technique for
 PV modules, cells, bulk materials, and interfaces, widely applicable to industrially
 relevant, rapidly advancing PV technologies. This may facilitate the faster design of
 more efficient, cost-effective, and degradation-resistant cells, with the levelized cost of
 electricity (LCOE) reduction estimated at least 8% of the 2025 utility LCOE goal gained
 from module efficiency improvements. In the course of this project, the National
 Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR)team collaborated and consulted with world-renowned
 experts in EDMR: Penn State University (Prof. Patrick Lenahan, also a founder of the
 related BlueSpin startup) and HZB Berlin (Dr. Klaus Lips). U.S. PV industry partner
 Maxeon provided us with a state-of-the-art silicon (Si) absorber material for this study.
 NLR state-of-the-art cell processing facilities were used to make miniature cell replicas
 for the EDMR. NLR Characterization (related to Module Reliability) and Cadmium
 Telluride (CdTe) PV teams were involved in the project as well.

 This project resulted in the following achievements:
 1. A new EDMR capability at NLR, including:
 • Minicell technology development (a miniature 2 mm x 2 cm replica of a n-type
 Si-based cell with tunneling oxide on polysilicon contacts [TOPCon]).
 • Novel low-noise, no-solder minicell contacting method for electron
 paramagnetic resonance (EPR) cavity.
 • Modified Bruker E-500 EPR spectrometer with integrated EDMR
 electronics.
 2. Using the new EDMR capability, performed research and published:
 • Identified defect EDMR signatures in passivated contact two-sided TOPCon
 cells (TOPCon2) as interface defects (silicon dangling bond defects Pb0, Pb1
 associated with (100) and (111) Si surface orientation, respectively, and
 boron-oxygen (B-O) defects).
 • Identified with EDMR and EPR, novel dopants and dopant clustering in the
 newest generation of n-type Czochralski (Cz) high-performance wafers:
 antimony (Sb) (TaiRay wafers) and arsenic (As). These dopants are now
 replacing conventional phosphorus (P) doping in Si PV.
 • Developed minicell and module fragment edge passivation technique based
 on Nafion superacid field-effect passivation. Showed the limitations of the
 technique (aging effects and the need for wet chemistry after laser cutting).

 4. Background:

 Determining the microscopic nature of performance-limiting defects is becoming
 increasingly important for all solar cell technologies as they are advancing to record
 efficiencies and reducing degradation rates to enable 50-year performance lifetimes.
 Basic research is needed to advance cell efficiencies through surface passivation,
 increasing wafer bulk lifetime, and mitigating degradation, both during cell processing,
 and in the field for 50-year reliability [1,2]. As the mainstream cell efficiencies advance (p-
 Cz Si p-type passivated emitter and rear cell (PERC) cells over 24% efficiency [3], n-Cz
 Si tunnel oxide passivated contacts (TOPCon) cells at 25% [4-6], with records over 27%
 [7]), defects affecting performance and reliability at interfaces and in the Si bulk are low,
 yet are increasingly important for high efficiency (and ultimately, LCOE [8]) and are harder
 to detect. Impurity levels as low as ~1010 cm-3 still affect the performance of these cells.
 Understanding the atomic character and origin of these defects (e.g., from wafer,
 passivation, cell processing, or field degradation) can enable the targeted development
 of mitigation strategies. While such atomic-level detail may not be required to resolve all
 industry-relevant issues, this type of analysis provides a diagnostic tool that can be
 applied when deeper insight is needed. This approach has been explored in the current
 project.
 In the Si PV field, defects are typically detected by photocarrier lifetime spectroscopy.
 Cells and specially-designed lifetime structures are used to identify defects in a specific

 5

 38526
 NLR

 cell component (surface, passivated contact interface, bulk). Lifetime spectroscopies
 such as quasi-steady state photoconductivity (QSSPC) and photoluminescence (PL) are
 very sensitive and allow high throughput, yet they offer limited insights into microscopic
 defect mechanisms. PL offers imaging capability of defective regions with spatial
 resolution on the order of the photocarrier diffusion length [9 - 11] and could be applied
 to assess, nondestructively, even modules in the field [10]. To identify defect structure,
 however, PL requires sub-bandgap detection at cryogenic temperatures; sensitivity is
 relatively low. Advanced photocarrier lifetime spectroscopies (QSSPC) detect, with
 limitations, the ratios of electron-hole capture coefficients and energy level positions
 [12,13]. Also, this requires special sample preparation and measurement techniques: a
 wafer thickness series combined with temperature-dependent spectroscopies [14]. Deep-
 level transient spectroscopy (DLTS) and capacitance measurements can detect absolute
 defect energy level positions but require special sample preparation and only detect
 defects in the bias-limited depletion region [15 - 17]. Finally, synchrotron-based
 techniques such as X-ray fluorescence can detect low impurity levels, but identify only
 atomically different species [18,19]. Microscopic techniques such as transmission
 electron microscopy (TEM), electron beam induced current (EBIC), and secondary ion
 mass spectroscopy (SIMS) are insensitive to the miniscule amounts of performance-
 limiting defects (<1012 cm-3).
 Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) is a sensitive technique bridging this gap, as
 it can detect ~ 1011 cm-3 defects and their atomistic and structural origin by electron spin
 g-value. From 2020 to 2025, the NLR team has demonstrated detection of light-induced
 degradation (LID) and light-and elevated temperature-induced degradation (LeTID)
 defects in p-type Cz-Si known to be undetectable by classical microscopies [20, 21].
 During this time period, we have not been aware of any other groups in Si PV field using
 EPR and EDMR to identify performance-limiting defects. However, conventional EPR
 measurements are affected by surface defects. In cells, EPR detects defect spins in the
 whole device stack, including inactive layers. Moreover, cryogenic temperatures need to
 be used to boost the cavity-EPR sensitivity to detect ~ 1011 cm-3 defects. All of these
 factors complicate the use of the otherwise promising cavity EPR as an emerging
 widespread characterization technique for PV cells and modules.

 In this project, starting in 2022, we developed electrically-detected magnetic resonance
 (EDMR). This technique, according to the theory and earlier semiconductor device
 literature, is three to six orders more sensitive than conventional EPR [22 - 24]. About a
 decade ago, PV-adapted EDMR was developed at Helmholtz Center Berlin (HZB) by
 Klaus Lips and his coworkers, and is used to identify defects in Si heterojunction and thin-
 film microcrystalline Si cells [25, 26], but were not so successful on perovskite cells [27].
 Since then, to our knowledge, the technique has not been reported in Si PV literature or
 at conferences. Rapid development of other cell types in Si PV industry (especially
 TOPCon) prompted us to establish this capability at NLR, apply it to cell replicas with
 TOPCon contacts, and explore its potential and limitations. The apparent significant
 advantage of EDMR is that it can be performed (at least, theoretically) on the whole
 device, a cell fragment from a degraded module, or on a passivated test structure with
 contacts. Monitoring the device current makes this technique sensitive only to lifetime-
 limiting defects, which affect performance, and ignores electrically passive defects in the
 contact or dielectric film stack. EDMR can be performed at room temperature, and can
 identify defects by their g-value and line shape.
 In this work, we revealed the potential of EDMR including its limitations. As a result of
 the EDMR work, we have also combined it with the cavity EPR on the very same device,
 thus taking advantage of both methods. Using this approach, besides purely EDMR
 measurements, we have identified new dopants (As and Sb) now being used in Si PV
 industry, and their clustering. In the realm of EDMR specifically, we have for the first time

 7

 38526
 NLR

 identified atomic origins of defects at the tunnel oxide/wafer interface in TOPCon
 passivated contacts. NLR is now likely the only research institution internationally capable
 of both manufacturing the TOPCon passivated contact minicells and measuring them by
 EDMR/EPR in-house. This capability has been appreciated by awards from the
 international Si PV community, especially in the leading SiliconPV conference (see one
 of those in the Project Products list). However, during the project, we have also
 established limitations of the EDMR technique. These include difficulty of edge
 passivation in minicells, especially cell and module fragments, limitations in signal
 strength, and the selective sensitivity to the defects inside or near the depletion region of
 a p-n junction (as opposed to bulk of the absorber).

 5. Project Objectives:

 The impact of this project to the U.S. economy is ultimately related to achieving record
 cell/module efficiencies in the growing U.S. production and reducing panel degradation
 rates to enable 50-year performance lifetimes. The mainstream PV by the end of 2024
 was 2.2 terawatts (TW) globally and 239 gigawatts (GW) in the U.S., with Si PV in the
 absolute lead, constituting > 95% of the PV globally and about 80% in the U.S. Si PV
 module efficiencies improved considerably over the past five years, leading to low LCOE
 [1, 2, 8]. Thin film PV is rapidly advancing as well, especially the CdTe industry based in
 the U.S. High efficiencies require increasingly more sensitive and sophisticated methods
 of determining the origins of performance-limiting defects and ways to mitigate them. This
 EDMR project facilitates this goal by innovative, atomic-level defect characterization for
 higher PV efficiency and better reliability. The impact addresses PV modules, cells, bulk
 materials, and interfaces, widely applicable to industrially-relevant, rapidly advancing PV
 technologies. This project bridges the gap between microscopic and carrier lifetime defect
 characterization techniques, as EDMR is both very sensitive and identifies atomic origins.
 This technique may facilitate faster design of more efficient, cost-effective, and
 degradation-resistant cells. In this project, as the main examples for the impact, we have
 identified two types of atomic defect environments in TOPCon-type solar cells [35];
 identified and explored new generation of n-type dopants in n-Cz Si adopted by the PV
 industry in the last two to five years (As [37, 39] and Sb [38,40]) and quantified their
 clustering – a potential performance issue. The full list of publications from this project is
 given in References [28-47]. We have also detected EDMR responses from the module
 and cell fragments and developed techniques [44] for their passivation/extraction with
 minimal loss of edge passivation.

 dark forward bias, electrons and holes are entering the cell from an external source and
 recombining within the cell. The cell’s dark J(V) is essentially the recombination rate

 Task 2: EDMR on module fragments.
 The overall goal of this task was to capitalize on the development of the EDMR
 technique by extending it to the analysis of materials within actual field deployed PV
 modules. The high sensitivity of EDMR has great potential for exposing more details
 about degradation mechanisms in industrial PV modules. Their degradation modes (such
 as LID and LeTID) are thought to be influenced by small concentrations of defect
 complexes. For example, those could be lattice vacancies containing H that are far below
 most analytical characterization methods.
 While this activity holds great promise, it also presented a significant challenge in terms
 of sample preparation. The process of removing a fragment of a PV device from a fielded
 module was developed in detail under a previous SETO funded project, “Modules to
 Atoms” led by Steve Johnston at NLR [49]. The process consists of defining a region of
 the module, then mechanically removing a circular piece on the order of 20 mm diameter
 using a circular saw coated with diamond paste, then the remaining glass/encapsulant is
 removed by soaking and peeling away from the underlying PV cell. The damage occurring
 during this process is mechanical in nature and generally contained around the perimeter
 of the device fragment. For EDMR measurements we need a specific geometry, primarily
 constrained by the diameter of the EPR measurement tube, and so the module fragment
 size must be further reduced to be no larger than 2 - 3 mm in one dimension. Our initial
 approach was to produce these pieces via laser scribing and then to apply gentle
 passivation to the exposed edges to reduce their effect on the EDMR signal. This is shown
 schematically in Figure 15.

 Lessons Learned – Task 2

 Producing module fragments for EDMR measurements within the context of the LID or
 LeTID degradation modes relevant for Si PV modules requires further work. Like recent
 findings for similar efforts in III-V mesa isolation work [51], some variety of chemical
 treatment or layer deposition is needed to mitigate the laser scribing damage such that
 true material properties can be observed with the EDMR technique. This is an example
 of a limitation of the EDMR technique as researchers must choose a problem that is both
 relevant and a material system or device architecture that lends itself to the geometry
 requirements without treatments that would alter the material state intended to be studied.
 Here we list several other lessons learned over the project duration.
 1) Laser conditions should be tuned such that extreme damage (melting, cracking, etc.)
 do not occur when removing module fragments, but scribing alone is insufficient.

 2) Post-scribing treatment is necessary to negate laser damage regardless of how
 “gentle” the scribing conditions are.

 a) Quick fix → Nafion (or other super acid treatments) can temporarily passivate laser
 induces damage, but a permanent fix is more challenging
 b) For real module core samples, the post-scribe etch must be tailored to the device
 architecture (PERC, TOPCon, HJT, etc.) to account for different dielectric material
 etching

 3) Post-scribing treatments present a challenge when trying to study degradation modes
 that occur at relatively low temperatures or are related to subtle chemical differences
 (e.g., hydrogen). Researchers need to consider preparation of specimen geometry
 and what degree of passivation is required when approaching EDMR experiments. An
 ideal laser cutting with post-treatment would produce fragments with the same PL
 brightness and same open-circuit voltage at 1-sun, as before coring/cutting.

 Finally, the EDMR technique development at NLR included significant minicell
 technology development. The basic approach (Generation 1, or Gen-1) is already
 discussed previously. The lesson learned here is to aim for the most robust, simple
 sample structures and contacting schemes based on mechanical contacts. We also
 developed more advanced Generation 2 (Gen-2) minicells with a smaller active area and
 long metal fingers running on top of the isolating nitride dielectric layer, from contact pads,
 down to the cell’s active area (Fig. 24). These minicells also featured perpendicular
 gridlines, to minimize EDMR signal due to charge transport, in the charge-selective
 contact layers. However, Gen-2 minicells featured significantly lower EDMR signal, likely
 due to their small active area, and we used mostly Gen-1 cells for the experiments
 reported. Developing a reliable cabling and contacting scheme was another challenge.
 The delicate minicells got frequently damaged by soldering. Wire-bonding was also found
 to be not fully applicable, especially in minicells featuring Al contacts. Our Penn State
 collaborators shared their contacting method involving long circuit board strips with open
 metal connectors and soldered contacts. To avoid soldering and to maximize the S/N
 ratio, we developed our own mechanical contacting technique. It employs two flexible
 phosphorus-bronze strips that clamp metal pads on the opposite sides of the minicell.
 This avoids soldering or wire-bonding and allows minicells with Al contacts be used in
 EDMR experiments. To improve S/N further, we opted to use a single Twinax cable
 instead of two thin coaxial cables. This allows much better EPR sample tube sealing as
 well.
 7. Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions:

 The first significant accomplishment is the establishment at NLR, a fully in-house based
 EDMR capability together with special minicell sample preparation and contacting
 techniques. Minicell technique using only mechanically-aligned masks with 10 micron
 precision and resulting in two-sided TOPCon2 miniature cell devices, is unique to NLR.
 Besides EDMR, we have successfully applied minicell technology to create tandem
 planar luminescent concentrators (with the California Institute of Technology) and 40%
 efficient power beaming receiver. Within this EDMR project, the minicell technology
 inspired us to create two NLR Records of Invention, one of which resulted in non-
 provisional patent application (use of minicells in space-deployable, compact solar
 panels). The custom-made electrical contacting socket, though seemingly simple,
 efficiently solved an ongoing S/N problem is EDMR, that other leading groups (including
 Penn State and HZB) has been struggling with.
 The second group of achievements relates to the EDMR measurements on n-Cz
 TOPCon2 minicells. This type of cell (two-sided, n+ and p+ poly-Si contact cell) is gaining
 increasingly more interest in the perovskite tandem field. We established, for the first time,

 30

 38526
 NLR

 atomic origins of the defects at the Si absorber/tunneling SiO2 interface. This work
 attracted significant interest from the Si PV community, as evidenced by the “10-best
 presentations” award at the field-leading SiliconPV conference in 2024. As a follow up
 development, we have also applied this technique to specifically reveal defects related to
 the Boron-induced deterioration of the tunneling oxide interface in these cells.
 Finally, the EDMR technique enabled us to reveal, ahead of other researchers, new n-
 type dopant families that Asian-based companies have recently started to use for high
 quality, uniformly-doped n-Cz Si ingots – arsenic and antimony. Since the magnetic
 resonance study of these dopants required cryogenic temperatures for dopant freeze-out,
 we ended up using cavity EPR after discovering dopant signatures by EDMR. These
 experiments allowed us to realize the new directions that Si PV mass production has
 undertaken and reveal the dopant clustering effect as possible concern for high-
 performance wafers doped with these impurities. This research direction has also
 attracted significant interest, evidenced by a joint paper on Sb doping of TaiRay wafers
 with Australian National University (ANU) [40]. These experiments resemble a certain
 degree of pivot from the original plan (broadly defined “process-induced defects in n-Cz”).
 This is because in these new wafers, besides using As and Sb dopants, the crystal growth
 industry has achieved low oxygen content. Because of this, we believe the oxygen
 precipitation is of much lesser concern than in wafers produced ten years ago. In fact, we
 had difficulties observing the expected drop in carrier lifetime even after running these
 wafers through our robust O-precipitation treatment (nucleation and growth sequences of
 the precipitates).
 Yet another significant accomplishment relates to development of superacid Nafion
 passivation for Si wafers and laser-cut edges, and deeper understanding of the field-effect
 passivation mechanisms. Previously, we had adapted the liquid hydrofluoric/hydrochloric
 (HF/HCl) passivation of wafers for high-throughput, highly sensitive assessment of large
 numbers of wafers from industry and academic collaborators. However, this technique
 requires in-situ, transient carrier lifetime measurement of the immersed wafer. After
 adapting the Nafion passivation technique from Ali Javey’s group in University of
 California Berkeley, we are now able to passivate wafers and edges semi-permanently
 for hours or days, allowing for PL mapping. In the course of these experiments, we
 discovered the EPR-detected free electron signal to strongly correlate with field effect
 passivation by the dielectric on Si. The initial findings were reported in our paper [42]. The
 passivation studies with EPR and cyclotron resonance, detecting 2D electron and hole
 gas induced by the filed-effect passivation of Si by dielectric layer stacks, will be pursued
 further within the Si Core project.
 Three main challenges were encountered within this project. The first challenge relates
 to the experimental technique itself. The Si TOPCon2 minicell technology is relatively
 complex and time consuming, yet leads to reliable, reproducible EDMR signals (Task 1).
 The cell and module fragment approach (Task 2), though seemingly much simpler, is not
 representative because of the quite significant EDMR cell fragment performance
 deterioration due to laser cutting of cleaving. In most cases, the observed EDMR signals
 are due to the edge defects, as confirmed also by the DLTS studies reported above. The
 possible future refinement of the EDMR should solve the cored module edge passivation
 problem. In addition, testing EDMR on other cell technologies proved rather challenging:

 31

 38526
 NLR

 both in CdTe and III-V cases, proper preparation of EDMR minicells, though possible,
 requires significant effort, and is not easy to realize in the lab (laser isolation, masking,
 metallization, contact designs and soldering).
 Second, LeTID defect studies in p-Cz minicells proved most challenging, due to the
 absence of in-house tube PECVD, industrial-grade SiNx capabilities. Moreover, unlike the
 p-PERC cell with doped P-emitter, the TOPCon2 minicell requires different process
 sequences (tunnel oxide, PECVD a-Si, thermal crystallization, ALD AlOx with subsequent
 activation at 400°C). Most importantly, our then-only alternative low-T deposited PECVD
 nitride did not result in LeTID degradation after firing. Only close to the end of the project,
 in 2024, we observed LeTID in commercial PERC cell precursors, however, to convert
 those to minicells would require T > 900°C which would erase LeTID precursors. A new
 tube PECVD nitride capability was established at NLR only after the end of the EDMR
 project, in September 2025.
 Third, the deeper origin of the observed EDMR bias dependence remains unsolved.
 The peak of the EDMR response near 0.4V seems to suggest that it is sensitive only to
 the defects in the p-n depletion region, as explained in our APL Materials 2025 paper [35].
 Yet, it is known that the recombination increases exponentially with forward bias to the
 cell–in fact, this is the very origin for the “diode-like” dark JV characteristic. At forward
 bias above the MPP point, the dominant recombination mechanisms shift from the
 depletion region (“J02 behavior”) to defects at doped passivated surfaces and interfaces
 (“J01 behavior”) and ultimately, to the Auger-Meitner recombination mechanism. Defects
 in the bulk contribute to the recombination as well, at all forward biases. These
 recombination channels are not observed in the EDMR experiments. Our interviews with
 leading EDMR experts (Lenahan and Lips) did not significantly add to the bias
 dependence understanding at the cell level. This would be an interesting future project
 aimed at basic science of spin coherence time and recombination.
 8. Path Forward:

 Once established as an in-house technique at NLR, the EDMR will be used alongside
 cavity EPR to characterize performance-limiting defects in our Core Si project. The
 method and newly developed NLR capability is not limited to Si PV only, but we will
 attempt to apply it to other cell technologies, notably CdTe and III-V. Note: magnetic
 resonance experiments of perovskite materials have not been proven successful so far,
 apparently due to very strong spin-lattice coupling.
 Beyond PV, it can be envisioned that EDMR can be also applied to
 electrochemical/catalytic devices and quantum computing. We have already acquired
 small GaAs cell fragments from the III-V team (Ryan France) and tested them in our
 EDMR setup. The initial tests showed a cavity tuning problem, due to a thick, very
 conductive GaAs substrate with full-area back contact. A way forward would be a)
 selective, one-sided chemical thinning of the substrate; b) reducing the size of the cell
 fragment several times; c) using thin III-V cells with a handle, separated from the growth
 substrate by chemical liftoff. CdTe EDMR cell fragmenting would require laser cutting or
 chemical edge separation through the thick TCO layers, metal fingers, and junctions,
 without shunting the cell. Both III-V and CdTe cells therefore appear measurable by our

 32

 38526
 NLR

 EDMR, some work is required to optimize the cell fragment and ensure no cavity loading.
 This would be a promising forward direction with additional funding.
 Applications outside PV could include quantum computing. Recently, it has been
 shown that electron spins associated with dopant atoms in isotopically pure 28Si epitaxial
 films can have very long spin coherence times (> 1 ms) and thus, present interest for
 quantum computing. Clusters of dopants, especially those with higher nuclear spin, have
 similar properties and could potentially be used for qudit modes of quantum computing,
 using multiple spin states instead of 0 and 1, to increase the information encoding density
 and reduce errors in computing and quantum key distribution. A promising qudit system
 is 123Sb dopant spin system in 28Si epitaxial film, that has been recently explored by the
 University of New South Wales/Sandia/QuAIL, et al. team [48]. Isotopically pure 28Si
 substrates epitaxial films are becoming available, through ultracentrifugation of SiF4 and
 subsequent conversion into isotopically pure SiH4. From the silane, 28Si films could be
 grown at NLR, e.g., by low-T PECVD epitaxy, or PECVD of a-Si:H followed by extensive
 dehydrogenation and sold phase epitaxy at > 600oC, or MBE. We have expertise in these
 processes from our past c-Si film work. In addition, our Si cleanroom and process
 capabilities allow most of the device fabrication steps outlined in [48], and the spin
 manipulation pulse sequences can be realized with our E-580 Bruker spectrometer.

 As a more detailed example, below is one of EDMR-inspired paths forward using
 EPR/EDMR detection to reveal surface passivation mechanisms for Si PV. As part of this
 EDMR project, we observed the presence of free electrons, or conduction-band electron
 signals, in our measurement data when samples were coated with certain dielectric and
 Si layers. However, this signal was absent in some cases. The presence of these free-
 electron signals were found to be correlated with surface passivation, as only specific
 dielectric or Si passivation layers exhibited the signal [44]. In particular, when using silicon
 nitride (SiNx) to passivate our Si samples (Figure 25), we detected a strong free-electron
 signal with a g-value of ~2.000. In a wide magnetic field scan, a clear cyclotron resonance
 peak was also observed in the spectrum. The cyclotron signal is related to free electrons
 and holes, and is expected to correlate with free electron EPR signal. Importantly,
 substrate polarity plays a role: these signals were observed only in n-type substrates and
 not in p-type substrates.
 Using the simulation program AMPS, we demonstrated that at a low temperature of 20
 K, the band diagram reveals the formation of a two-dimensional electron gas (2DEG)
 layer at the interface between SiNx and the n-Si surface. This occurs because, at low
 temperature, the Fermi level shifts upward toward the conduction band, combined with
 surface inversion induced by the positive fixed charge of the SiNx layer. As a result, the
 Fermi level in the Si bulk lies higher than the bottom of the inversion layer. These excess
 electrons injected into the wafer by charge exchange with SiNx, are not attached to the
 frozen-out As dopants, as those are already saturated. Instead, they form 2DEG in the
 wafer near the SiNx/Si interface.
 The free-electron signal at g ≈ 2.000 observed in the narrow scan was not present in
 samples coated with AlOx, due to its opposite fixed charge polarity (negative).
 Correspondingly, cyclotron resonance was also absent (Figure 28). This can be explained
 by the negative built-in charge injecting holes in the wafer, which cannot be detected by
 the EPR under typical experimental conditions.
 Interestingly, a free-electron signal was also detected in samples coated with an n-
 poly-Si/SiO₂ stack on n-type substrates. This is consistent with the fact that the poly-
 Si/SiO₂ stack can also induce the formation of a 2DEG layer at the interface.
 Based on these observations, we plan to correlate the EPR signals obtained from
 different passivation layers (SiNx, AlOx, poly-Si/SiO₂, etc.) with their corresponding
 surface passivation mechanisms. To the best of our knowledge, this represents the first
 demonstration of magnetic resonance studies on Si surface passivation in solar materials
 and devices. In future work, we will extend this research within our Core Si program to
 further investigate free electrons and correlate their behavior with solar cell degradation
 mechanisms such as LETID and UVID, which are currently critical topics in Si PV.
