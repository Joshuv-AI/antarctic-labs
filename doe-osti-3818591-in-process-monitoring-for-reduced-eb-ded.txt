Executive Summary:

 This final technical report summarizes work performed under Award DE-EE0009399 to
 develop a process-monitoring-informed NDT methodology for EB-DED titanium
 components. The project ended early due to contractual delays that impacted schedule
 execution and subcontractor staffing; therefore, this report documents completed
 accomplishments as well as notes for completion of the remaining Budget Period (BP) 3
 milestones.

 This project, led by Edison Welding Institute (EWI) with GE Research (GER), Oak Ridge
 National Laboratory (ORNL), and Lockheed Martin Corporation (LMC), addresses a key
 barrier to adoption of electron beam directed energy deposition (EB-DED) for large,
 fracture-critical titanium aerostructures: the high cost and long cycle time of post-process
 nondestructive testing (NDT). The project’s goal was to develop and demonstrate a
 process-monitoring-informed NDT methodology that fuses registered in-process
 monitoring data (thermal camera, melt pool camera, and dual-wavelength pyrometry) with
 artificial intelligence/machine learning (AI/ML) to set zone-based process-monitoring-
 informed post-process NDT requirements. This approach targeted a reduction in inspection
 cost of 25%–50% and reduction in NDT cycle time by 12%–25%.

 Work completed before closure established the core technical foundation needed for the
 planned demonstration. EWI implemented and calibrated a multi-modal process
 monitoring suite and developed geometric and thermal calibration approaches to support
 data registration. A production-relevant training dataset was generated using coupon builds
 designed to match the thermal scenarios measured on a production component. Defects
 were characterized by micro X-ray computed tomography (µCT) and registered to the
 monitoring data resulting in a dataset including 6098 defects larger than 0.010 in. Using
 these data, the team developed a defect prediction approach that classifies nominal versus
 non-nominal quality scenarios from multimodal monitoring data and achieved the BP 2

 Go/No-Go performance criteria. In parallel, fan beam computed tomography (FCT) was
 identified as the most suitable post-process NDT method for thick-section EB-DED Ti-
 6Al-4V at the project’s 0.010-in. defect threshold.

 The project ended early due to contractual delays that affected schedule execution and the
 ability to maintain subcontractor staffing. As a result, the planned BP 3 activities, including
 the translation of model outputs and reliability into zone-based informed NDT
 requirements (Milestone 8.1), application of informed requirements to full-scale drop-link
 components (Milestone 9.2.1), and the End of Project Goal demonstration of inspection
 cost and cycle-time reduction, were not completed. The results reported here document the
 completed technical foundation (process monitoring and data registration, production-
 relevant dataset generation, model development and validation, and post-process NDT
 assessment) and define the remaining integration and demonstration work needed to realize
 the originally proposed production-part impact.

1. Introduction

 This project was undertaken to address a major barrier to broader industrial use of electron
 beam directed energy deposition (EB-DED) for large, fracture-critical titanium
 components: the cost and time burden of post-process nondestructive testing (NDT). Led
 by Edison Welding Institute (EWI) with GE Research, Oak Ridge National Laboratory,
 and Lockheed Martin Corporation, the project focused on developing a process-
 monitoring-informed inspection methodology that combines in-process sensing with
 artificial intelligence and machine learning to better target post-process inspection
 requirements.

 The technology developed in this project integrates a multi-modal monitoring suite—
 including a thermal camera, melt pool camera, and dual-wavelength pyrometry—with
 calibrated data registration and defect characterization workflows. These data streams were
 aligned with micro X-ray computed tomography defect maps to create production-relevant
 datasets that could be used to train models to distinguish nominal from defect-prone quality
 conditions during deposition. The long-term intent was to use those model outputs together
 with part criticality to establish zone-based, process-monitoring-informed NDT
 requirements rather than relying on uniformly intensive inspection across the entire part.

 The project specifically targeted improved manufacturing efficiency by reducing
 unnecessary inspection burden while maintaining defect detection for structurally critical
 regions. For the selected aerospace use case—a Lockheed Martin Ti-6Al-4V drop-link
 component—the planned approach was intended to reduce NDT cost by 25% to 50% and
 reduce inspection cycle time by 12% to 25%. By enabling more efficient qualification of
 additively manufactured titanium parts, the technology also supports broader deployment
 of advanced manufacturing methods that can reduce material waste, shorten production
 lead times, and improve the competitiveness of domestic manufacturing.

 Potential applications are strongest in aerospace and other industries that require inspection
 of large, high-value, fracture-critical metal components produced by directed energy
 deposition or similar additive processes. If commercialized, the methodology could
 provide economic benefit through lower inspection cost, faster throughput, and more

  focused use of high-cost CT resources, while also supporting environmental and energy
  benefits associated with more efficient production workflows and increased confidence in
  qualifying additively manufactured parts.

  From a commercialization perspective, the project established the technical foundation
  needed for future transition to a production-part inspection workflow. Major building
  blocks were completed, including sensor integration and calibration, production-relevant
  dataset generation, defect-model development that met the BP2 Go/No-Go criteria, and
  selection of fan beam computed tomography as the most suitable post-process NDT
  technique for the target defect threshold. Although the project ended before the final BP3
  demonstration could be completed, the work substantially reduced technical risk for future
  demonstration and deployment efforts with end users.

  Overall, the project demonstrates a credible path toward smarter, risk-informed inspection
  of EB-DED titanium components by linking in-process data, defect analytics, and post-
  process NDT planning. Even though the final integrated demonstration was not completed
  because of contractual delays and subcontractor staffing impacts, the completed work
  provides a strong basis for follow-on development, industrial demonstration, and
  commercialization planning.

2. Background

  The project was initiated to address a key technical and commercialization barrier to
  broader use of electron beam directed energy deposition (EB-DED) for large, fracture-
  critical titanium components: conventional post-process nondestructive testing is
  expensive, time intensive, and difficult to scale efficiently across complex, thick-section
  parts. For aerospace applications such as the selected Lockheed Martin Ti-6Al-4V drop-
  link component, these inspection burdens can limit the economic and production
  advantages that additive manufacturing would otherwise provide.

  At the start of the project, the relevant state of the art suggested that process monitoring
  could generate rich information about build quality, but a practical pathway for translating
  that information into inspection requirements for production parts had not yet been
  demonstrated for thick-section EB-DED Ti-6Al-4V. Existing options presented tradeoffs.
  Direct post-process methods such as phased array ultrasonic testing and computed
  tomography could detect defects, but they imposed cost, access, image-quality, and
  throughput constraints. At the same time, purely data-driven monitoring approaches
  depended on production-representative datasets and reliable registration between process
  signatures and actual defects. The project therefore set out to bridge sensing, defect
  characterization, and inspection planning in a single workflow.

  The project objectives were to implement and calibrate a multi-modal monitoring suite on
  the EB-DED platform, generate defect-labeled datasets representative of production
  thermal conditions, develop machine-learning models that distinguish nominal from
  defect-prone quality scenarios, and use those results together with component criticality to
  support process-monitoring-informed post-process NDT requirements. The planned
  commercialization relevance was clear: if successful, the methodology would enable more

 targeted inspection of high-value components and reduce the need for uniformly intensive
 full-part inspection.

 The planned technical approach reflected those objectives. EWI led integration of thermal,
 imaging, and pyrometry-based monitoring on the Sciaky EB-DED system and deployed
 the digital-thread infrastructure needed to capture and contextualize sensor data. Oak Ridge
 National Laboratory supported thermal modeling used to design production-relevant
 coupon builds. GE Research performed micro X-ray computed tomography
 characterization needed to identify and register defects. Lockheed Martin provided the
 production use case, structural context, and transition target through the drop-link
 application. This team structure gave the project both the technical depth and end-user
 relevance needed to pursue an inspection methodology with practical manufacturing value.

 Commercially, the project addressed the challenge of qualifying additively manufactured
 titanium parts for demanding service environments while preserving the cost and lead-time
 advantages of additive production. Technically, it also confronted key issues that are
 common in real manufacturing settings, including sensor calibration, defect registration,
 stochastic defect formation, model generalizability, and the need to match training data to
 production conditions. Those issues shaped both the progress achieved during BP1 and
 BP2 and the follow-on work identified after the project ended before completion of the
 BP3 demonstration.

3.1 Process Monitoring:

 Several sensors were added to the DED-EB platform to generate rich datasets for machine
 learning, as shown in Figure 4. Sciaky provides several built-in monitoring capabilities:

 • Co-axial melt pool camera providing real-time images of the wire feedstock and
 melt pool.

 • Logging of machine operating parameters (e.g., voltage, current, vacuum
 pressure).
 EWI complemented these built-in sensors with additional thermal sensing modalities:

 • Co-axial, dual-wavelength optical pyrometer to measure melt pool temperature
 (fiber-coupled into the system).
 • Deposition-head-mounted optical pyrometer (side view) targeted ahead of the
 melt pool to measure interpass temperature.
 • Deposition-head-mounted thermal camera (side view) to measure cooling
 behavior behind the melt pool.
 Integrating these sensors was complicated by the physical vapor deposition that is a
 byproduct of the EB-DED process. The melt pool pyrometer was protected by an air knife.
 The side-mounted sensors were protected by a continuously unrolling Kevlar tape. EWI
 also attempted to integrate a height sensor; however, the time-of-flight sensor could not be
 fiber-coupled, and the integration was not successful.

Figure 4: Illustration of the process monitoring used to generate rich data for the models.

 All sensors were calibrated geometrically and thermally. Geometric calibration of the
 thermal camera and melt pool camera was performed periodically using an OpenCV dot-
 grid approach to estimate distortion, focal length, and camera pose. To register the camera
 coordinate frame to the build coordinate frame, the reference dot pattern was written using
 the Sciaky electron beam. Geometric calibration of the pyrometers was performed by
 placing a tee bracket on a hot plate that was rastered beneath the sensors using the Sciaky
 motion platform. Thermal calibration of the thermal camera and both pyrometers was
 performed using a thin Ti-6Al-4V specimen excised from an EB-DED build produced with
 the nominal process parameters (Figure 5), to match the material and surface condition of
 interest. The specimen was made thin and elevated on ceramic pillars to enable rapid
 heating and cooling. The specimen was resistively heated, and temperature was
 independently measured using a calibrated thermocouple.

Figure 5: Thermal calibration specimen.

To streamline process-monitoring data acquisition, EWI deployed its Smart Manufacturing
framework to the Sciaky system. The framework uses an Industrial Internet of Things
(IIoT) architecture based on the Sparkplug B standard (MQTT topic namespaces and
payload structure). Metrics are grounded in a manufacturing ontology (Common Core
Ontology and Basic Formal Ontologies as upper ontologies). The resulting digital thread
persists data in a MongoDB database using json-ld triples aligned to the ontology, enabling
contextualized data storage and supporting findable, accessible, interoperable, and reusable
(FAIR) data practices. EWI extended this infrastructure to the Sciaky system as shown in
Figure 6 and built edge-of-network gateways for the model TIM640 thermal camera, model
CTR melt pool pyrometer, model CTL inter-pass pyrometer, Sciaky log files, Sciaky melt
pool camera images, and the Kapton tape motor.

 Figure 6: Smart Manufacturing infrastructure deployed to capture Sciaky data.

 The resulting platform met all data collection milestones including thermal measurement
 accuracy and geometric accuracy. Additional implementation and verification details are
 provided in the milestone reports for Milestones 2.1.1 (thermal camera installation and
 calibration), 2.2.1 (pyrometer integration and calibration), and 2.4.1 (automated multi-
 sensor data acquisition and registration).

 With registered sensing modalities and calibrated data streams in place, the next step was
 to generate defect-labeled training datasets that were representative of production thermal
 and quality scenarios.

3.2 Dataset Development:

 The effectiveness of the machine-learning approach in a production environment depends
 strongly on how representative the training data are of production conditions. A key
 advancement of this program was the development of an efficient methodology for
 generating production-relevant, defect-labeled datasets using coupon builds. In all cases,
 coupons were fabricated using nominal production process parameters. Dataset
 representativeness was achieved by systematically varying two primary axes: thermal
 scenarios and quality scenarios.

To establish a production thermal baseline, an initial full-scale drop-link component was
built and monitored. The distribution of interpass temperatures shown in Figure 7 was used
to define the production-relevant range of thermal scenarios.

Figure 7: Histogram of interpass temperatures observed during a full-scale drop link build.

Coupons were designed to replicate the thermal scenarios observed in the full-scale
component. Inter-pass temperature was controlled by adjusting inter-pass dwell time, while
coupon geometry was selected to match the size scale of relevant drop-link features (Figure
8). Finite-element thermal simulations performed by Oak Ridge National Laboratory were
used to guide coupon design and ensure thermal similarity to the full-scale build. Defects
within the coupons were characterized by µCT performed by GE Research. Defect size and
location information from CT were registered to the corresponding in-process monitoring
data, enabling the creation of spatially aligned training datasets.

Figure 8: Example coupon used for training data development. Fiduciary marks are used to register
the monitoring data to the X-ray CT data.

To efficiently introduce defects suitable for model training, a quality-scenario-based
approach was developed. In additive manufacturing real-time defect detection programs,
two general strategies are commonly used to obtain defects: (1) introducing surrogate
defects (e.g., designed voids) directly into the part geometry, which offers high
experimental control but does not represent production defect formation mechanisms; and

(2) collecting large datasets without intentional defect induction, which preserves
production realism but is often cost-prohibitive. The approach adopted in this program
balances these tradeoffs by selectively replicating production-relevant defect formation
mechanisms.

Figure 9: Decision tree for selection of methods to generate defects.

The decision tree shown in Figure 9 summarizes the methodology. If a sensing modality
directly measures defects (e.g., an ultrasonic or eddy-current sensor trailing the melt pool),
surrogate defects may be appropriate. This is similar to the common NDT practice of using
test indicators such as blind-bottom holes to validate performance. However, when sensors
measure indicators of defect formation rather than defects themselves, surrogate defects
may produce non-representative signatures. In these cases, defects were induced by
replicating their underlying causes, referred to here as quality scenarios. For example,
oxygen concentration can influence defect formation and can be replicated by introducing
controlled, production-relevant oxygen ingress. When neither direct measurement nor
realistic replication of defect causes is feasible, large datasets without intentional defect
induction are required.

3.3 Defect Detection Model:

 This training data was used to develop a classification model that distinguished nominal
 from defect-prone quality scenarios. Separate deep learning classifiers were trained for
 each data modality to predict the quality scenario associated with a given observation.
 Quality scenario 0 represents the nominal condition, while quality scenarios 1–7 represent
 production-relevant process disturbances expected to increase defect formation. The
 models were trained using data from Test Block 7 (nominal), a subset of Block 10 to
 account for build-to-build variability in the nominal case, and Test Blocks 11–17
 representing the non-nominal quality scenarios. Classification performance across the full
 set of quality scenarios is summarized by the confusion matrices in Figure 10.

The modality-specific classifiers were combined by segmenting each block into
overlapping slices along its length (Figure 11). The slice width and overlap were selected
to emulate the inspection regions expected for fan-beam X-ray CT. For each slice, a
continuous defect score in the range [0, 1] was computed from the fraction of
measurements within the slice predicted to be nominal (quality scenario 0) for each
modality, followed by aggregation across modalities. The overall scoring workflow is
summarized in Figure 12.

Figure 11: Segmenting block into overlapping segments along the length of the block.

Figure 12: The overall process for estimating the multi-modal defect score for the entire block.
Model validation was performed using Test Block 10, which was produced under the
nominal quality scenario but contained 28 defects and therefore provided a production-

 relevant test case. To convert the continuous defect score into a binary defect-detection
 decision, a defect-score threshold was selected to balance the following metrics:

 • Defect detection rate (DDR): Fraction of defects in the block that fall within a
 segment classified as defect-prone (defect score below the threshold); corresponds
 to the first part of the go/no-go criterion.
 • Normal detection rate (NDR): Fraction of defect-free regions that fall within a
 segment classified as defect-free (defect score above the threshold); equivalent to
 the true negative rate (TNR).
 • True positive rate (TPR): Fraction of defect-containing regions that fall within a
 segment classified as defect-prone (defect score below the threshold).
 Performance characteristic curves for candidate thresholds are shown in Figure 13. Based
 on these curves, a threshold of 0.945 was selected as an operating point that provides a
 favorable trade-off among DDR, NDR, and TPR and meets the DDR go/no-go
 requirement.

 Figure 13: Performance characteristic curve for the approach.

 Additional model development and validation details are documented in the BP2
 continuation application and the milestone deliverables for Milestones 5.1.1 and 7.1.1.

 To translate model outputs into an inspection decision-support workflow, candidate post-
 process NDT methods and inspection cost baselines were evaluated.

3.4 Post-Process Nondestructive Examination:

 Two candidate post-process NDT techniques were evaluated: phased array ultrasonic
 testing (PAUT) and FCT. These methods were selected based on prior collaborative work
 between EWI and Lockheed Martin [2] and their potential applicability to thick-section

EB-DED Ti-6Al-4V components. The objective was to assess whether each method could
reliably support the project’s defect detection criterion of 0.010 in. while remaining
compatible with production-relevant inspection workflows.

To guide down-selection of post-process NDT approaches, two inspection cost models
were developed: (1) a baseline model and (2) a process-monitoring-informed model. The
models account for the costs of process-monitoring sensors, post-process inspection, and
post-process machining, and they also capture the increased cost impact of scrapping a part
after machining. In the baseline flow (Figure 14a), the component is built, fully inspected
using FCT, and machine if acceptable. In the process-monitoring-informed flow (Figure
14b), process monitoring and AI/ML predictions are used to create a part-specific
inspection plan: prior to machining, only a specified fraction of the part is inspected using
FCT, and a different fraction is inspected using scatter-free radiography; after machining,
PAUT is applied to a final fraction of the part. These models were used to compare
inspection burden and risk across candidate techniques.

PAUT Results: PAUT was considered because it can rapidly scan selected regions of
interest. However, prior work by EWI under an America Makes program [2] demonstrated
that the relatively coarse grain structure typical of EB-DED Ti-6Al-4V increases ultrasonic
scattering and noise, complicating defect detection and sizing. Building on those findings,

this program evaluated transducer configurations intended to improve signal quality and
sizing capability.

PAUT performance is sensitive to surface condition and geometry. As-built EB-DED
surfaces introduce coupling variability, and thin-wall features can produce edge reflections
that complicate interpretation. Inspection of components in the as-built condition is
generally preferred because machining parts that are later scrapped increases the value of
the scrap. However, to enable meaningful PAUT evaluation, Block 10 was surface-
machined to reduce waviness and roughness. Cone beam CT was used as the reference for
defect location and size, and the prepared reference artifact is shown in Figure 15.

Figure 15: Reference artifact for PAUT.

Two linear array probes were evaluated:

 1. 5L60: 5MHz Linear array probe with 60 elements each of 0.036-in. size.
 2. 10L128: 10MHz Linear array with 128 elements each of 0.0078-in. size.
Theoretical sizing capability is approximately half the ultrasonic wavelength (≈0.012 in. at
10 MHz and ≈0.024 in. at 5 MHz). All scans used full matrix capture/total focusing method
(FMC/TFM) to reduce noise and improve focusing. Defects were sized using the 6 dB drop
method.

The 10L128 array demonstrated sensitivity to defects near the 0.010-in. threshold, but
defect sizing was not reliable. Defects were consistently oversized, and the degree of
oversizing depended on defect depth due to beam spread. As a result, this configuration
could not robustly distinguish defects above versus below the 0.010-in. threshold.

The 5L60 array provided improved sizing accuracy for larger defects but did not
demonstrate sufficient sensitivity near the 0.010-in. target. In practice, this approach was
generally capable of detecting and sizing defects larger than approximately 1 mm, well
above the defect threshold for this project.

Based on these results, PAUT was not selected as the primary post-process inspection
method for this program.

FCT Results: FCT was selected over cone beam CT because the full-scale drop-link
includes Ti64 cross-sections up to approximately 4 in. thick in the as-built condition. This
increases scatter and degrades cone beam CT image quality. FCT provides geometric
rejection of scattered radiation, improving image quality for thick components. A practical
limitation is that FCT acquires a two-dimensional slice per rotation; even at fast acquisition
rates (< 1 minute/rotation), full-part inspection would require multiple days. This limitation
is mitigated within a process-monitoring-informed workflow, where FCT is applied only
to selected regions of interest.

As an initial assessment of image quality, a full-scale drop-link in the as-built condition
was scanned to represent a production-relevant inspection scenario. A representative cross-
section from the thickest region of the part is shown in Figure 16 and includes a ~0.010-in.
pore. Streaking artifacts (associated with material anisotropy) and ring artifacts (associated
with detector response non-uniformity) were observed but were largely confined to the
build plate region where defects are not expected. FCT operators rated the ~0.010-in. pore
as readily identifiable in this configuration.

Figure 16: Characteristic full scale drop link FCT image showing a 0.010 inch pore.

ASTM E1695-20 [3] was used to quantify FCT spatial resolution using Ti-6Al-4V disk
phantoms representative of the drop-link’s maximum thickness. A 4-in. diameter disk was
selected to represent the largest component width and a 3-in. diameter disk to represent the
largest thickness; both represent conservative, worst-case conditions relative to thinner
regions on the component. The disks were scanned using the same X-ray filtering and
acquisition parameters used for the drop-link. Figure 17 summarizes the ASTM E1695-20
analysis sequence: disk center/diameter identification (a), edge response function (b), line

spread function (c), and modulation transfer function (MTF). Using the MTF at 10%, the
measured spatial resolution was 0.0057 inch for the 3-in. disk and 0.0061 inch for the 4-in.
disk.

A common rule of thumb for ideal detectability is that spatial resolution should be on the
order of one-third of the defect size of interest (≈0.0033 in. for a .010-in. defect). While the
measured resolution (~150 µm) does not meet this conservative criterion, it is smaller than
the defect size threshold and was established under worst-case attenuation conditions.
Combined with the full-scale component inspection results, these findings indicate that
FCT provides sufficient image quality and resolution for this study.

In summary, fan beam CT provided the most suitable post-process NDT capability for
thick-section EB-DED Ti-6Al-4V in this program, particularly when paired with a process-
monitoring-informed inspection strategy. PAUT did not demonstrate reliable detection and
sizing performance at the 0.010-in. threshold under production-relevant conditions and was
not pursued further as the primary inspection method.

 These post-process NDT findings and the associated inspection cost-model framework
 were intended to support the SOPO-defined informed NDT requirements activities
 (Milestone 8.1) and the subsequent application of informed inspection requirements to full-
 scale drop-link components (Milestone 9.2.1), which together underpin the End of Project
 Goal demonstration. Because the project ended early, these milestones were not completed;
 however, the NDT down-selection and performance characterization completed here
 establish the inspection baseline and capability assumptions needed to execute the planned
 informed inspection methodology.

 Accordingly, the discussion above should be interpreted as the completed inspection
 capability and baseline assessment, rather than a completed BP3 integrated demonstration.

4. Key Achievements

 Major project achievements included successful implementation and calibration of a multi-
 modal process monitoring suite for the EB-DED platform, including the thermal camera,
 melt pool camera, and pyrometry systems, together with data-registration methods that
 enabled spatial alignment of process signatures to part geometry and defect locations.

 A second key achievement was development of a production-relevant defect-labeled
 dataset using coupon builds designed to match the thermal conditions of a full-scale drop-
 link component. Using registered in-process data and micro X-ray computed tomography,
 the team created a dataset containing 6,098 defects larger than 0.010 in., far exceeding the
 minimum dataset thresholds established in the project milestones.

 The project also achieved its BP2 model-development objectives. The multi-modal
 machine-learning approach met the BP2 Go/No-Go requirement by predicting 85.58% of
 defects larger than 0.010 in. and achieving average nominal classification accuracy of
 90.2% with a 96.7% detection rate. In parallel, the team completed an important NDT
 down-selection and showed that fan beam computed tomography was the most suitable
 post-process inspection method for thick-section EB-DED Ti-6Al-4V at the target defect
 threshold, while phased array ultrasonic testing did not demonstrate sufficient
 performance. Additional accomplishments included deployment of a digital-thread data
 infrastructure, development of an enhanced probabilistic modeling concept during BP3,
 and generation of practical lessons learned regarding defect registration, stochastic defect
 formation, and model generalizability. A significant negative finding was that the final BP3
 integrated demonstration and full end-of-project validation were not completed because
 contractual delays and subcontractor staffing limitations ended the project early.

5. Benefits Assessment

 The anticipated benefit of commercial implementation is a more efficient inspection
 workflow for high-value additive-manufactured titanium components. The project was
 originally structured to demonstrate a 25% to 50% reduction in NDT cost and a 12% to
 25% reduction in inspection cycle time by using process-monitoring and AI/ML outputs to
 focus the most intensive post-process inspection only where it is most needed. Even though

 that final demonstration was not completed, the technical results achieved in BP1 and BP2
 support the plausibility of those expected gains.

 Potential energy and environmental benefits are tied to broader adoption of advanced
 additive manufacturing workflows that reduce unnecessary processing, inspection time,
 and manufacturing inefficiency for fracture-critical parts. By increasing confidence in EB-
 DED process quality and enabling more targeted NDT, the approach can support faster
 qualification and greater industrial use of near-net-shape titanium additive manufacturing,
 which in turn can reduce wasted effort, improve resource utilization, and strengthen U.S.
 manufacturing competitiveness. Additional economic benefits include more effective use
 of expensive CT assets, reduced production bottlenecks, and a clearer technical pathway
 for integrating smart manufacturing, process monitoring, and inspection planning in future
 production environments.

6. Lessons Learned

 This section summarizes key technical lessons learned that affect data labeling, model
 robustness, and the use of process-monitoring predictions for inspection decision support.
 These lessons also highlight remaining technical considerations that would need to be
 addressed to complete the originally planned BP3 integration and demonstration.

6.1 Defect Registration:

 Using µCT to establish ground-truth defect locations for model development requires
 careful interpretation for EB-DED builds for two primary reasons:

 1. Defects can migrate between adjacent beads and layers due to partial remelting
 during subsequent depositions.
 2. Defect formation and persistence are stochastic, including both defect nucleation
 and potential healing during remelting.
 These effects motivate modified registration and modeling approaches, as summarized
 below.

 Figure 18 compares µCT cross-section used for labeling with an etched photomicrograph
 taken at the same location. The melt pool boundaries visible in the micrograph illustrate
 the defect migration challenge. Comparing the upper beads to the beads in the layer below
 shows substantial remelting during subsequent deposition; as a result, defects can migrate
 upward over multiple remelt cycles. In addition, the substantial overlap of beads within a
 layer enables lateral migration of defects between adjacent beads. To account for this
 behavior, an initial “super-voxel” approach was used. Super-voxels were sized to
 accommodate migration of approximately one bead left/right and one bead up/down. µCT
 defect locations were binned into these super-voxels, and classification was performed at
 the super-voxel level.

 µCT Cross-section Etched Photo-Micrograph

Figure 18: Comparison of micro-CT cross-section to an etched photomicrograph of the same block at
the same position.

While the super-voxel approach mitigates defect migration, it does not fully address the
stochastic nature of defect formation and persistence. Two sources of stochasticity are
particularly important:
 1. Defect nucleation is stochastic; identical thermal and quality scenarios may not
 always produce a defect.
 2. Subsequent remelting during deposition can partially heal or eliminate defects that
 formed in prior layers.
This stochastic behavior should be acknowledged and accounted for when using µCT-
based labels for model development and validation.

During BP3, an enhanced model concept was developed to explicitly incorporate stochastic
defect formation while providing additional benefits:
 • Provide uncertainty-quantified defect predictions
 • Provide actionable insight to the system operator to support real-time operator
 corrections
 • Map model outputs more directly to manufacturing quality control decisions and
 downstream inspection planning

This enhanced model was motivated by the BP2 model’s ability to accurately classify
specific quality scenarios. In BP2, the defect prediction workflow used a simplified binary
interpretation of the process state (“nominal” versus “not nominal”) rather than leveraging
the full multi-class quality scenario classification. As illustrated in Figure 19, the enhanced
approach first predicts the specific quality scenario and then combines (i) the probability
of that quality scenario occurring with (ii) the defect formation propensity associated with
that scenario to estimate defect likelihood. The predicted quality scenario can be reported

to the operator to support real-time corrective actions, and probabilistic defect likelihood
can be used to better inform process-monitoring-informed post-process NDT planning.

BP2 Blocks 10–17, which were each built using a single quality scenario, can be used to
estimate defect formation propensity for each scenario without concern for defect
migration. The spatially binned defect counts were histogrammed to generate frequentist
estimates of defect formation probability. The defect distribution was modeled using a
Poisson distribution (consistent with binary events and an assumption of spatial
independence), and Bayesian inference was used to estimate the mean and standard
deviation of the rate parameter. Results are shown in Figure 20.

Figure 20: The probabilistic defect formation propensity for each quality scenario.

Two approaches can be used to estimate the probability of a quality scenario occurring
based on process monitoring data:

First, a frequentist approach based on the confusion matrix can be used to estimate the
probability of each predicted class. Second, an entropy-based approach can be used to
identify low-confidence regions that warrant increased inspection or operator attention.

 This enhanced modeling approach decouples prediction of quality scenario occurrence
 from prediction of defect formation given a quality scenario occurrence. The latter can be
 estimated directly by building a block using a single quality scenario and counting defects
 in that block. The former can be trained by inducing quality scenarios and predicting their
 occurrence from process monitoring data. This decoupling helps address defect migration
 and defect healing, while also providing actionable insight to the operator regarding which
 quality scenarios are occurring.

 Beyond defect labeling and registration challenges, the work also highlighted the
 importance of model generalizability across builds and across variations in how quality
 scenarios manifest in production.

6.2 Neural Network Generalizability:

 During BP 3, the team evaluated whether the models developed to support the BP 2
 continuation application could generalize to additional coupons and build conditions.
 Initial results indicated limited generalizability. The BP 2 validation relied on eight blocks,
 where each block represented a single realization of each quality scenario. The following
 questions were identified in a risk assessment:

 1. Did the model learn signatures specific to individual block builds, or did it learn
 transferable signatures of the underlying quality scenarios?
 2. Did the model learn a narrow implementation of a scenario (e.g., wire off-axis by
 exactly 0.015 in.), or did it learn a broader, production-relevant definition of that
 quality scenario?
 To test these questions, the BP2 model was applied to BP1 data. These data were generated
 from different blocks and included slightly different instantiations of the quality scenarios,
 providing a practical test of both block-to-block transferability and scenario generalization.

 Representative results are shown in Figure 21 for Block 1. Each layer in Block 1 was
 produced under a single quality scenario, and the quality scenario changed layer-by-layer.
 If the BP2 model generalized well, the predicted labels would form distinct horizontal
 bands corresponding to the applied quality scenarios (e.g., Scenarios 3, 4, and 5). While
 some banding is visible, the overall result indicates that additional work would be required
 to improve generalizability.

Figure 21: Results of applying BP2 model to BP1 data.

To improve generalizability, the following mitigation strategies were explored:
 • Reduced the number of classes by combining similar quality scenarios (e.g.,
 multiple wire off-center conditions).
 • Applied geometric calibration to reduce image-registration differences between
 BP1 and BP2 datasets.
 • Augmented training by mixing BP1 and BP2 data to expand coverage of quality-
 scenario variability.

These steps produced modest improvement; however, performance on BP1 data remained
insufficient for reliable cross-build deployment.

As a sanity check, the team performed descriptive analytics across the quality scenarios.
The following observations were made:
 • The melt pool pyrometer captured clear signatures associated with power outages.
 • The melt pool pyrometer was expected to capture power-surge signatures, but
 these were not clearly observed; this may be related to down-sampling in the
 current processing pipeline.
 • Thermal camera imagery provided clear evidence of wire position.
These observations suggest that additional feature engineering, improved data
conditioning, and expanded training coverage could improve model transferability across
builds and quality-scenario variations.

 In summary, the BP2 results demonstrate that high within-dataset performance does not
 guarantee cross-build transferability when training coverage is limited to a single
 realization of each quality scenario. Future work should emphasize multi-build training
 datasets, explicit coverage of scenario variability, and data conditioning steps that reduce
 inter-build differences, with validation designed to reflect the intended deployment
 conditions.

 Collectively, these lessons inform how the completed BP 1/BP 2 foundation could be
 extended toward deployment and underscore the importance of maintaining strong end-
 user engagement during technical maturation.

7. Technical Transfer Activities

 Lockheed Martin was engaged throughout the program as a funded partner, providing
 direct input on requirements, cost constraints, and production considerations. This
 engagement helped ensure that the technical approach and deliverables remained aligned
 with a production-relevant use case.

 As a result, Lockheed Martin was positioned to support technical transition of the process-
 monitoring-informed inspection methodology at the conclusion of the project. Because the
 project ended early, full transition activities and production demonstration steps planned
 for BP3 were not completed; however, the partnership structure and ongoing requirements
 feedback established a clear pathway for future continuation and transfer.

8. Conclusions

 This project established the technical foundation for a process-monitoring-informed NDT
 methodology for EB-DED titanium components and showed that the core concept is
 technically viable. The team successfully implemented calibrated multi-modal sensing,
 generated a large production-relevant defect dataset, achieved BP2 model-performance
 requirements, and identified fan beam CT as the most appropriate post-process inspection
 method for the target defect size. Together, these results represent meaningful progress
 toward a smarter inspection strategy for large, fracture-critical additive-manufactured
 parts.

 At the same time, the report concludes that the project stopped short of full
 commercialization readiness because the BP3 integration and full-scale demonstration
 were not completed. As a result, the expected inspection cost and cycle-time reductions
 were not experimentally demonstrated on production components within this project. Even
 so, the completed work reduced key technical risks and clarified what follow-on work is
 required, including improved model generalizability across builds, translation of model
 outputs into inspection requirements, and validation on full-scale parts.

 The most important lesson from the project is that strong early technical progress in
 sensing, data generation, and model development must be coupled with sustained
 integration and end-user validation to achieve deployment. In that sense, the project’s
 outcome is best understood as a successful technical foundation effort with an incomplete
 final demonstration phase. The work leaves behind a credible pathway for future R&D,

 demonstration, and commercialization of risk-informed inspection methods for additive
 manufacturing.

9. Future Work/Recommendations

 Future work should focus first on completing the BP3 activities that were not finished
 before project closeout. The highest-priority step is to translate process-monitoring model
 outputs and their reliability into zone-based informed NDT requirements and then apply
 and validate those requirements on full-scale drop-link components. This follow-on effort
 should include end-to-end demonstration of the inspection workflow against the original
 project objectives for NDT cost reduction, scan-time reduction, and defect detection in
 structurally critical regions.

 A second priority is to strengthen model robustness for deployment. The report shows that
 strong within-dataset performance did not automatically translate to cross-build
 generalizability, so future R&D should emphasize multi-build training datasets, broader
 coverage of quality-scenario variability, improved data conditioning and geometric
 alignment, and validation protocols that reflect intended production conditions. Additional
 feature engineering and continued fusion of the thermal, imaging, scalar, and digital-thread
 data streams should also be pursued to improve model transferability and operational
 reliability.

 Future development should also continue the enhanced probabilistic modeling approach
 initiated during BP3. That approach offers a practical path to uncertainty-quantified defect
 predictions, clearer identification of specific quality scenarios, more actionable feedback
 to machine operators, and more direct translation of process-monitoring outputs into
 inspection planning. Advancing this framework would improve both manufacturing quality
 control and the technical basis for risk-informed post-process inspection decisions.

 From a commercialization perspective, the recommended path is a staged demonstration
 with an end user beginning from the established technical baseline created in this project.
 Fan beam CT should remain the primary post-process NDT method for thick-section EB-
 DED Ti-6Al-4V, while process monitoring and AI/ML are refined to reduce inspection
 burden in low-risk zones. A follow-on program should therefore focus on full-scale
 validation, quantification of business-case benefits, and transition of the sensing, analytics,
 and inspection-planning workflow into a production-relevant environment with sustained
 end-user participation.
