On (Non-)Isomorphism of Self-Dual Lattices and Codes
Huck Bennett, Kyle Fridberg

Abstract
A recent line of work motivated by cryptographic applications has studied the complexity of the Lattice Isomorphism Problem ( LIP \mathrm{LIP} ). In this work, we study LIP \mathrm{LIP} on self-dual lattices ℒ ⊂ ℝ n \mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n} , which appear naturally in many applications. Our main results are a 2 n / 2 + o ⁡ ( n ) 2^{n/2+o(n)} -time randomized algorithm for LIP \mathrm{LIP} and a 𝖼𝗈𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{coNP} protocol for LIP \mathrm{LIP} on a broad class of self-dual lattices. These results extend recent work on ℤ ​ LIP \mathbb{Z}\mathrm{LIP} , the problem of deciding whether a lattice is isomorphic to ℤ n \mathbb{Z}^{n} . In particular, the former result extends the 2 n / 2 + o ⁡ ( n ) 2^{n/2+o(n)} -time algorithms for ℤ ​ LIP \mathbb{Z}\mathrm{LIP} of Bennett, Ganju, Peetathawachai, and Stephens-Davidowitz (Eurocrypt, 2023) and of Ducas (Des. Codes Cryptogr., 2024). The latter result extends the ℤ ​ LIP ∈ 𝖼𝗈𝖭𝖯 \mathbb{Z}\mathrm{LIP}\in\mathsf{coNP} result of Hunkenschröder (Math. Prog. Series A, 2024). Our results leverage two key structural properties of self-dual lattices ℒ ⊂ ℝ n \mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n} : (1) every such lattice ℒ \mathcal{L} is isomorphic to ℒ 0 ⊕ ℤ r \mathcal{L}_{0}\oplus\mathbb{Z}^{r} for some self-dual lattice ℒ 0 \mathcal{L}_{0} with λ 1 ​ ( ℒ 0 ) 2 ≥ 2 \lambda_{1}(\mathcal{L}_{0})^{2}\geq 2 , and (2) every such lattice ℒ \mathcal{L} has characteristic vectors , i.e., there exist vectors 𝒘 ∈ ℒ \boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{L} such that for every 𝒗 ∈ ℒ \boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{L} , ⟨ 𝒗 , 𝒘 ⟩ ≡ ⟨ 𝒗 , 𝒗 ⟩ ​ ( mod ​ 2 ) \langle\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}\rangle\equiv\langle\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2) . Our results use a line of work by Elkies and Gaulter on lattices with long shortest characteristic vectors, and can be strengthened assuming a positive answer to a related question of Elkies (Math. Res. Lett., 1995). We also study Permutation Code Equivalence ( PCE \mathrm{PCE} ) on self-dual codes, and we observe that similar structural properties imply a polynomial-time algorithm for PCE \mathrm{PCE} on certain such codes. This gives a natural class of codes with large hull for which PCE \mathrm{PCE} is easy.

Introduction

A lattice
\mathcal{L}
is a discrete, additive subgroup of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The decisional
Lattice Isomorphism Problem
(LIP) asks, roughly speaking, whether two lattices
\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
are rotations of one another.

\mathrm{LIP}
has received substantial attention from an algorithmic and complexity-theoretic perspective (see, e.g.,
[
Szy03
,
HR14
,
LS17
,
CGG17
]
).
Moreover, a thriving line of recent work initiated by
[
DvW22
,
BGPS23
]
has developed cryptosystems that are inspired by
\mathrm{LIP}
and whose security relies on the hardness of
\mathrm{LIP}
.
This work has been quite fruitful already. In particular, it has led to the development of advanced cryptographic primitives based on LIP (e.g.,
[
BMM25
,
LRvW25
,
BLS26
]
) and to the practical digital signature algorithm HAWK
[
DPPvW22
]
.
1
1

1

HAWK is the sole lattice-based cryptosystem to appear as a second-round candidate in the “Additional Digital Signature Schemes” standardization process run by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST)
[
Nat25
]
.

Many of these works concern
\mathrm{LIP}
on certain nice classes of lattices such as module lattices in
[
DPPvW22
]
and the integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
in
[
DvW22
,
BGPS23
]
.

The main goal of this work is to better understand the complexity of
\mathrm{LIP}
on
self-dual lattices
, i.e., lattices
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}^{*}
, where
\mathcal{L}^{*}:=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\forall\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{L},\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle\in\mathbb{Z}\}
is the dual lattice of
\mathcal{L}
.
2
2

2

Self-dual lattices are also called
unimodular lattices
since Gram matrices of their bases are necessarily unimodular matrices. In this work, we choose to call these lattices self-dual since we also work with self-dual codes. Throughout this work we assume by default that lattices are full-rank, i.e., we assume that if
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
then
\lspan(\mathcal{L})=\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Self-dual lattices are heavily studied in number theory
[
CS98
]
, and they appear in topology (e.g.,
[
Fre82
,
Don83
]
) and sphere packing.
In particular, optimal sphere packings in
8
and
24
dimensions are achieved by the famous self-dual lattices
E_{8}
and
\Lambda_{24}
, respectively
[
Via17
,
CKM+17
]
.
Moreover, the list of LIP challenges given in
[
DvW22
, Table 1]
includes several self-dual lattices.
The integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is a self-dual lattice, and so
\mathbb{Z}\mathrm{LIP}
, the problem of deciding whether a given input lattice is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, is a very special case of
\mathrm{LIP}
on self-dual lattices.

The fastest known algorithm for (general)
\mathrm{LIP}
runs in
n^{O(n)}
time
[
HR14
]
.
Faster algorithms are known on so-called Construction-A lattices
[
CGG17
,
DG23
]
and on rank-
2
module lattices over certain number fields
[
MPPW24
,
APvW25
]
.
Furthermore, the fastest known algorithms for
\mathbb{Z}\mathrm{LIP}
run in
2^{n/2+o(n)}
time
[
BGPS23
,
Duc24
]
, and to the best of our knowledge very little is known about
\mathrm{LIP}
on broader classes of self-dual lattices than rotations of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.

We also consider the complexity of
\mathrm{LIP}
more broadly.
Intuitively, it is straightforward to certify that two lattices
\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L}^{\prime}

are
isomorphic by providing an orthogonal transformation
O
such that
O(\mathcal{L})=\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
as a certificate. This essentially shows that
\mathrm{LIP}\in\mathsf{NP}
.
3
3

3

In fact, we will formalize
\mathrm{LIP}
in terms of quadratic forms rather than lattices in the sequel, in which case the witness is a bit different.

However, it is a priori unclear how to certify that two lattices are not isomorphic, and it is an open question whether
\mathrm{LIP}\in\mathsf{coNP}
.

In fact, showing that
\mathrm{LIP}
is in
\mathsf{coNP}
would imply that the Graph Isomorphism Problem (
\mathrm{GI}
) is in
\mathsf{coNP}
by a known polynomial-time reduction from
\mathrm{GI}
to
\mathrm{LIP}

[
SSV09
]
, thus resolving another open problem. (This reduction also shows that
\mathrm{LIP}
is
\mathrm{GI}
-hard, i.e., that
\mathrm{LIP}
has a polynomial-time algorithm only if
\mathrm{GI}
does.)
Additionally,
[
HR14
]
showed that
\mathrm{LIP}
is in
\mathsf{SZK}
and therefore that it is in
\mathsf{coAM}
, i.e., that there is an Arthur-Merlin protocol for certifying that two lattices are not isomorphic. This implies that
\mathrm{LIP}
is unlikely to be
\mathsf{NP}
-complete since then the polynomial hierarchy would collapse.
Recently, Hunkenschröder
[
Hun24
]
used a result of Elkies
[
Elk95a
]
to give a simple, elegant proof that
\mathbb{Z}\mathrm{LIP}\in\mathsf{coNP}
. To the best of our knowledge, nothing else is known about the complexity of
\mathrm{LIP}
on self-dual lattices.

We also study the analog of
\mathrm{LIP}
on self-dual codes.
A (binary, linear)
code

\mathcal{C}
is a linear subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
.
As with lattices, the question of whether two codes are “essentially the same”—formalized in terms of variants of the
Code Equivalence Problem
—has garnered substantial attention in the cryptographic literature, including as the basis of the LESS digital signature scheme
[
BMPS20
,
BBPS21
]
.
4
4

4

Like HAWK, LESS is a second-round candidate in NIST’s “Additional Digital Signature Schemes” standardization process
[
Nat25
]
. LESS uses codes over large finite fields
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Specifically, the parameter sets submitted to
[
Nat25
]
take
q=127
. Its security also relies on the hardness of the
Linear Code Equivalence Problem
, which consider a more general notion of equivalence allowing for both permuting and scaling coordinates.

In particular, the
Permutation
Code Equivalence Problem (
\mathrm{PCE}
) asks whether two codes
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
are the same up to a permutation of coordinates.
A code
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
is
self-dual
if
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
, where
\mathcal{C}^{\perp}:=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}:\forall\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{C},\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle=0\}
is the dual code of
\mathcal{C}
.
5
5

5

Here and in general we emphasize that all arithmetic over codes in
\mathbb{F}_{2}
is mod
2
.

1.1
Reduced Lattices and Characteristic Vectors

We write
\mathcal{L}\cong\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
if lattices
\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
are isomorphic (i.e., if there exists an orthogonal transformation
O
such that
O(\mathcal{L})=\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
). We call a self-dual lattice
\mathcal{L}_{0}
a
reduced lattice
if its squared minimum distance is at least
2
(i.e., if
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{L}_{0})^{2}\geq 2
).
To describe our results, we first introduce and discuss two key properties of self-dual lattices, which are as follows.

1.

Every self-dual lattice
\mathcal{L}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{L}_{0}\oplus\mathbb{Z}^{r}
for a unique (up to isomorphism) reduced lattice
\mathcal{L}_{0}
and some
r\geq 0
.
6
6

6

We note that either
\mathcal{L}_{0}
or
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
may be trivial. I.e., it may be the case that
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{0}
or
\mathcal{L}=\mathbb{Z}^{r}
.

We define
R_{0}(\mathcal{L}):=\rank(\mathcal{L}_{0})
, and we call a reduced lattice
\mathcal{L}_{0}
such that
\mathcal{L}\cong\mathcal{L}_{0}\oplus\mathbb{Z}^{r}
a
reduction
of
\mathcal{L}
.

2.

Every self-dual lattice
\mathcal{L}
has
characteristic vectors
, i.e., there exist vectors
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{L}
such that for every
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{L}
,
\langle\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}\rangle\equiv\langle\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)
. Moreover,
\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}
is a characteristic vector of
\mathcal{L}
if and only if
\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}\in 2\mathcal{L}+\boldsymbol{w}
for some fixed characteristic vector
\boldsymbol{w}
. Following the notation of Gaulter
[
Gau07
]
, we define
s(\mathcal{L}):=\min\{\lVert\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}\rVert^{2}:\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}\in 2\mathcal{L}+\boldsymbol{w}\}
to be the minimum squared Euclidean norm of a characteristic vector of
\mathcal{L}
.

The coset
\mathcal{L}+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{w}
of
\mathcal{L}
consisting of all
\frac{1}{2}
-scaled characteristic vectors of
\mathcal{L}
is called the
shadow
of
\mathcal{L}
in the literature
(see, e.g.,
[
CS98
, p. xxxiv]
and
[
Elk95b
]
). We note that the parameters
R_{0}
and
s
are both invariant under isomorphism.
Analogous properties also hold for self-dual codes
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, although in the introduction we focus on lattices.
(See
Section

2
for formal statements and corresponding references or proofs for all of the facts claimed here.)

We will crucially use a line of work, initiated by Elkies
[
Elk95a
]
and continuing with works by Elkies and Gaulter
[
Elk95b
,
Gau98a
,
Gau07
]
, on characterizing which self-dual lattices
\mathcal{L}
have large
s(\mathcal{L})
.
One can show that if
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a self-dual lattice then
s(\mathcal{L})=n-8K
for some
K\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
(see
2.2
,
Item

3
), and in fact the work of Elkies and Gaulter is framed in terms of characterizing the self-dual lattices
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
s(\mathcal{L})=n-8K
for small values of
K\geq 0
.
(
[
Elk95b
]
also studies the analogous question for codes.)

Starting with
K=0
,
[
Elk95a
]
showed that
s(\mathcal{L})=n
if and only if
\mathcal{L}\cong\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.
This led to Hunkenschröder’s
[
Hun24
]

\mathsf{coNP}
protocol for
\mathbb{Z}\mathrm{LIP}
.
He noted that to certify that a self-dual lattice
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is not isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, it suffices to provide a characteristic vector
\boldsymbol{w}
of
\mathcal{L}
with
\lVert\boldsymbol{w}\rVert^{2}<n
as a witness. (Although we are continuing to present our overview in terms of lattices,
[
Hun24
]
like us formally works with quadratic forms
Q=B^{T}B
equal to the Gram matrix of a basis
B
of
\mathcal{L}
. Accordingly, the
\mathsf{coNP}
protocol in
[
Hun24
]
in fact provides the
coefficient vector

\boldsymbol{z}
of such a characteristic vector
\boldsymbol{w}=B\boldsymbol{z}
. It checks that
B\boldsymbol{z}
is a characteristic vector of
\mathcal{L}
and that
\boldsymbol{z}^{T}Q\boldsymbol{z}<n
.)

Following
[
Elk95b
]
, we define the quantity
N_{L}(K):=\sup\{R_{0}(\mathcal{L}):\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}^{*},s(\mathcal{L})\geq\rank(\mathcal{L})-8K\}
.
7
7

7

We note that if
\mathcal{L}\cong\mathcal{L}_{0}\oplus\mathbb{Z}^{r}
then
s(\mathcal{L})=s(\mathcal{L}_{0})+r

(see
2.3
,
Item

3
), and so equivalently one can take the supremum over reduced lattices
\mathcal{L}_{0}
as opposed to arbitrary self-dual lattices
\mathcal{L}
.

So, by definition, if
s(\mathcal{L})\geq n-8K
then
R_{0}(\mathcal{L})\leq N_{L}(K)
.

In
[
Elk95b
]
, Elkies showed that
N_{L}(1)=23
and asked whether
N_{L}(K)
is finite for every
K
. (In fact,
[
Elk95b
]
showed that, up to isomorphism, there are precisely
14
reduced lattices
\mathcal{L}_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
s(\mathcal{L}_{0})=n-8
. We include a list of these lattices in
Table

1
.)
Gaulter
[
Gau98a
]
subsequently gave a positive answer to this question for
K=2
and
K=3
and even gave explicit upper bounds on
N_{L}(2)
and
N_{L}(3)
. Gaulter also gave an improved upper bound on
N_{L}(2)
in
[
Gau07
]
.
These bounds are important for our work, and we summarize them in
Theorem

2.7
.
To the best of our knowledge, nothing is known in general for
K\geq 4
, although there has been substantial progress in bounding the rank of reduced lattices
\mathcal{L}_{0}
that simultaneously have large
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{L}_{0})
and large
s(\mathcal{L}_{0})

[
Gau98b
,
NV03
,
Gab07
,
NS07
]
.

1.2
Results and Technical Overview

We now state our results. To do this formally for lattices, we use quadratic forms. A
quadratic form

Q
corresponding to a lattice
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the Gram matrix
Q:=B^{T}B
of a basis
B
of
\mathcal{L}
.
8
8

8

Formally, a quadratic form is the scalar-valued function
\boldsymbol{z}\mapsto\boldsymbol{z}^{T}Q\boldsymbol{z}
. For simplicity, we directly refer to the matrix
Q
as a quadratic form.

We write
\mathcal{L}\cong\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
if two lattices
\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
are isomorphic (i.e., if there exists an orthogonal transformation
O
such that
O(\mathcal{L})=\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
).

An algorithm for
\mathrm{LIP}
on self-dual lattices.

Our first result is an algorithm for search
\mathrm{LIP}
on self-dual lattices
\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
(i.e., when
\mathcal{L}\cong\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
, and the goal is to
find
an isomorphism between the lattices) with relatively low-rank reductions
\mathcal{L}_{0},\mathcal{L}_{0}^{\prime}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

There is an algorithm for search
\mathrm{LIP}
on quadratic forms
Q,Q^{\prime}
corresponding to self-dual lattices
\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
that runs in
2^{n/2+o(n)}+n_{0}^{O(n_{0})}
time, where
n_{0}:=R_{0}(\mathcal{L})=R_{0}(\mathcal{L}^{\prime})
.
In particular, if
n_{0}=o(n/\log n)
then the algorithm runs in
2^{n/2+o(n)}
time.

We emphasize again that the fastest known algorithm for
\mathrm{LIP}
on general lattices takes
n^{O(n)}
time
[
HR14
]
(see also
Theorem

2.15
), and that
Theorem

1.1
with
n_{0}=o(n/\log n)
matches the running time of the algorithms for
\mathbb{Z}\mathrm{LIP}
in
[
BGPS23
,
Duc24
]
, but applies to a much larger class of self-dual lattices (note that
R_{0}(\mathcal{L})=0
for
\mathcal{L}\cong\mathbb{Z}^{n}
).

The algorithm works by reducing
\mathrm{LIP}
on
\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
to
\mathrm{LIP}
on their reductions
\mathcal{L}_{0},\mathcal{L}_{0}^{\prime}
, and then using the algorithm in
[
HR14
]
to solve
\mathrm{LIP}
on
\mathcal{L}_{0},\mathcal{L}_{0}^{\prime}
.
9
9

9

We note the unfortunate clash in terminology between a reduction in the standard algorithmic sense and a reduction
\mathcal{L}_{0}
of a self-dual lattice
\mathcal{L}
.

The reduction works by finding all unit-length vectors in
\mathcal{L}\cong\mathcal{L}_{0}\oplus\mathbb{Z}^{r},\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\cong\mathcal{L}_{0}^{\prime}\oplus\mathbb{Z}^{r}
—i.e., by finding the sublattices of
\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
—and then projecting orthogonally to obtain
\mathcal{L}_{0},\mathcal{L}_{0}^{\prime}
.
To implement this reduction, we show that the algorithm from
[
BGPS23
]
for finding unit-length vectors in rotations of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
also works for finding unit-length vectors in arbitrary self-dual lattices (if any exist).

By definition of
N_{L}(K)
, we get that if
N_{L}(K)=o(n/\log n)
for
K=K(n)
, then
Theorem

1.1
solves
\mathrm{LIP}
on lattices
\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
s(\mathcal{L}),s(\mathcal{L}^{\prime})\geq n-8K
in
2^{n/2+o(n)}
time.
In particular, by the work of Elkies and Gaulter,
Theorem

1.1
applies when
s(\mathcal{L}),s(\mathcal{L}^{\prime})\geq n-8K
for
K=3
unconditionally. It applies for arbitrary constant
K
assuming a positive answer to Elkies’s question about
N_{L}(K)
being bounded.
Finally, we note that although in this work we focus on self-dual lattices,
Theorem

1.1
actually applies to
integral lattices
—lattices
\mathcal{L}
that satisfy the condition
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathcal{L}^{*}
but not necessarily the stronger condition
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}^{*}
required to be self-dual.

An
\mathsf{NP}\cap\mathsf{coNP}
protocol for
\mathrm{LIP}
on self-dual lattices.

Our next result is a
\mathsf{coNP}
protocol for
\mathrm{LIP}
on certain self-dual lattices
\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
.
That is, we give a protocol for certifying that
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
are
not
isomorphic when at least one of
s(\mathcal{L})
and
s(\mathcal{L}^{\prime})
is sufficiently large.
We formalize this by introducing and studying a decisional promise problem,
\mathrm{SDLIP}_{K}
(“self-dual
\mathrm{LIP}
”), parameterized by an integer
K\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. See
Definition

2.10
.

An instance of
\mathrm{SDLIP}_{K}
consists of quadratic forms corresponding to two self-dual lattices
\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. It is a YES instance if
s(\mathcal{L})\geq n-8K
and
\mathcal{L}\cong\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
(these conditions additionally imply that
s(\mathcal{L}^{\prime})\geq n-8K
), and it is a NO instance if
\mathcal{L}\not\cong\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
and either
s(\mathcal{L})\geq n-8K
or
s(\mathcal{L}^{\prime})\geq n-8K
(or both).
(Notice that the requirement that
s(\mathcal{L})\geq n-8K
in the YES case makes showing that
\mathrm{SDLIP}_{K}\in\mathsf{NP}
non-trivial, and we prove this in addition to
\mathrm{SDLIP}_{K}\in\mathsf{coNP}
for certain
K
.)

We motivate the definition of
\mathrm{SDLIP}_{K}
by noting several useful points about it. First,
\mathrm{SDLIP}_{K}
trivially reduces to
\mathrm{SDLIP}_{K+1}
for every
K\geq 0
.
10
10

10

Moreover,
\mathrm{SDLIP}_{\infty}
is simply the problem of deciding whether two self-dual lattices (represented by quadratic forms) are isomorphic.
Second, the problem of deciding whether a single input lattice
\mathcal{L}
is isomorphic to a fixed self-dual lattice
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
with
s(\mathcal{L}^{\prime})\geq n-8K
trivially reduces to
\mathrm{SDLIP}_{K}
.
11
11

11

More properly, one can consider a fixed family
\{\mathcal{L}_{n}^{\prime}\}
of self-dual lattices with
\mathcal{L}_{n}^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying
s(\mathcal{L}_{n}^{\prime})\geq n-8K
.
In particular,
\mathbb{Z}\mathrm{LIP}
reduces to
\mathrm{SDLIP}_{0}
by fixing
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}:=\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.
Third, YES instances
\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
of
\mathrm{SDLIP}_{K}
for
K
such that
N_{L}(K)\leq n_{0}
have
R_{0}(\mathcal{L})=R_{0}(\mathcal{L}^{\prime})\leq n_{0}
, and so
\mathrm{SDLIP}_{K}
for such
K
reduces to search
\mathrm{LIP}
on lattices with low
R_{0}
. It is therefore solvable in
2^{n/2}+n_{0}^{O(n_{0})}
time using the algorithm in
Theorem

1.1
.

Specifically, we show the following.

Theorem 1.2
.

For any
K=K(n)
such that
N_{L}(K)\leq O(\log n/\log\log n)
,
\mathrm{SDLIP}_{K}
is in
\mathsf{NP}\cap\mathsf{coNP}
.

As a corollary of
[
Elk95a
,
Elk95b
,
Gau98b
,
Gau07
]
, we then get that
\mathrm{SDLIP}_{3}\in\mathsf{NP}\cap\mathsf{coNP}
unconditionally, and we get that
\mathrm{SDLIP}_{K}\in\mathsf{NP}\cap\mathsf{coNP}
for any constant
K
assuming a positive answer to Elkies’s question about
N_{L}(K)
being bounded for every
K
.

The protocol works roughly as follows. To certify either that
\mathcal{L}\cong\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
or
\mathcal{L}\not\cong\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
in the case where
s(\mathcal{L}),s(\mathcal{L}^{\prime})\geq n-8K
, the protocol uses a witness consisting of orthogonal transformations
O,O^{\prime}
such that
O(\mathcal{L})=\mathcal{L}_{0}\oplus\mathbb{Z}^{r}
and
O^{\prime}(\mathcal{L}^{\prime})=\mathcal{L}_{0}^{\prime}\oplus\mathbb{Z}^{r^{\prime}}
for some reduced, low-rank lattices
\mathcal{L}_{0},\mathcal{L}_{0}^{\prime}
and some
r,r^{\prime}\geq 0
. Because
\mathcal{L}_{0},\mathcal{L}_{0}^{\prime}
each have rank at most
O(\log n/\log\log n)
, the verifier can check whether they are in fact reduced lattices using the
\mathrm{SVP}
algorithm from
[
ADRS15
]
(to check that
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{L}_{0})^{2}\geq 2
and
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{L}_{0}^{\prime})^{2}\geq 2
), and can then decide whether
\mathcal{L}_{0}\cong\mathcal{L}_{0}^{\prime}
using the
\mathrm{LIP}
algorithm from
[
HR14
]
in
O(\log n/\log\log n)^{O(\log n/\log\log n)}=\poly(n)
time.
To certify
\mathcal{L}\not\cong\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
when
s(\mathcal{L})<n-8K
but
s(\mathcal{L}^{\prime})\geq n-8K
, the protocol uses a short characteristic vector
\boldsymbol{w}
for
\mathcal{L}
together with an orthogonal transformation
O^{\prime}
such that
O^{\prime}(\mathcal{L}^{\prime})=\mathcal{L}_{0}^{\prime}\oplus\mathbb{Z}^{r^{\prime}}
for a reduced lattice
\mathcal{L}_{0}^{\prime}
as before. After verifying that
\mathcal{L}_{0}^{\prime}
is in fact a reduced lattice, the verifier can efficiently compute
s(\mathcal{L}_{0}^{\prime})
and therefore
s(\mathcal{L}^{\prime})=s(\mathcal{L}_{0}^{\prime})+r^{\prime}
(see
Corollaries

2.14
and
2.3
). From this, the verifier can check that
s(\mathcal{L})<s(\mathcal{L}^{\prime})
, and if so conclude that
\mathcal{L}\not\cong\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
.

An algorithm for
\mathrm{PCE}
on self-dual codes.

We then turn to studying Permutational Code Equivalence (
\mathrm{PCE}
), the code analog of
\mathrm{LIP}
, on self-dual codes
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
.
Specifically, we observe that an analog of
Theorem

1.1
holds for
\mathrm{PCE}
.

We define a
reduced code
to be a self-dual code
\mathcal{C}_{0}
with minimum distance at least
4
, and we define
\mathcal{Z}:=\{(0,0)^{T},(1,1)^{T}\}\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2}
to be the code generated by the vector
(1,1)^{T}
.
The
2
-fold repetition code
\mathcal{Z}^{r}=\mathcal{Z}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathcal{Z}\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2r}
is the analog of the lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
for codes.
By analogy with
Item

1
for self-dual lattices, every self-dual code
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
is permutationally equivalent to
\mathcal{C}_{0}\oplus\mathcal{Z}^{r}
for a unique (up to permutational equivalence) reduced code
\mathcal{C}_{0}\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n_{0}}
and some
r\geq 0
. We call such a code
\mathcal{C}_{0}
a
reduction
of
\mathcal{C}
, and we define
R_{0}(\mathcal{C})
to be the block length
n_{0}
of
\mathcal{C}_{0}
.
We show the following.

Theorem 1.3
.

There is an algorithm for search
\mathrm{PCE}
on self-dual codes
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
that runs in
2^{n_{0}+o(n_{0})}+\poly(n)
time, where
n_{0}:=R_{0}(\mathcal{C})=R_{0}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})
. In particular, if
n_{0}=O(\log n)
then the algorithm runs in
\poly(n)
time.

Elkies’s proof in
[
Elk95b
]
that
\mathcal{C}
is permutationally equivalent to
\mathcal{C}_{0}\oplus\mathcal{Z}^{r}
for some reduced code
\mathcal{C}_{0}
is constructive, and it implicitly gives a polynomial-time algorithm for computing
\mathcal{C}_{0}
. The algorithm is essentially to find all weight-
2
codewords in
\mathcal{C}
and then to take
\mathcal{C}_{0}
to be the punctured code obtained by restricting
\mathcal{C}
to coordinates not in the support of any weight-
2
codeword.
That allows us to reduce
\mathrm{PCE}
on
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
to
\mathrm{PCE}
on their reductions
\mathcal{C}_{0},\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\prime}
, which is analogous to the proof of
Theorem

1.1
. (However, unlike the analogous situation for lattices, this reduction to solving
\mathrm{PCE}
on
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
is efficient.)

We remark that the
2^{n_{0}+o(n_{0})}
term in the running time of
Theorem

1.3
comes from calling the
\mathrm{PCE}
algorithm of Babai
[
BCGQ11
]
on the reductions
\mathcal{C}_{0},\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
.
12
12

12

One can improve this running time to
2^{n_{0}/2+o(n_{0})}
using the randomized algorithm (resp., to
2^{n_{0}/3+o(n_{0})}
using the quantum algorithm) from
[
BBB+26
]
.

In fact, one can use
[
BCGQ11
]
to achieve a similar running time for search
\mathrm{PCE}
on direct sum codes
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\cong\mathcal{C}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathcal{C}_{m}
where each code
\mathcal{C}_{i}
has block length
O(\log n)
.
Theorem

1.3
is an interesting special case of this because of its connection to structural properties of self-dual codes.

Again by analogy with
Item

2
for self-dual lattices, every self-dual code
\mathcal{C}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
has characteristic vectors
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
(note that characteristic vectors of
\mathcal{C}
are
not
contained in
\mathcal{C}
).
Such a vector
\boldsymbol{w}
is such that for every
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{L}
,
\langle\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}\rangle\equiv\frac{1}{2}\wt(\boldsymbol{v})\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)
, where
\wt(\boldsymbol{v})
denotes the Hamming weight of
\boldsymbol{v}
. Similarly, one can define
s(\mathcal{C})
to be the minimum Hamming weight of a characteristic vector of
\mathcal{C}
, and one can show that
s(\mathcal{C})=(n-8K)/2
for some
K\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
Furthermore, one can define
N_{C}(K):=\sup\{R_{0}(\mathcal{C}):\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}^{\perp},s(\mathcal{C})\geq(n-8K)/2\}
. Again it follows by definition that if
s(\mathcal{C})\geq(n-8K)/2
then
R_{0}(\mathcal{C})\leq N_{C}(K)
.

As observed in
[
Elk95b
]
,
N_{C}(K)\leq N_{L}(K)
for every
K
(see
2.6
).
So, by the line of work by Elkies and Gaulter on bounding
N_{L}(K)
,
Theorem

1.3
gives a polynomial-time algorithm for
\mathrm{PCE}
on codes with
s(\mathcal{C})\geq(n-8K)/K
for
K=3
unconditionally, and, assuming that
N_{L}(K)
is bounded for every
K
, for any constant
K
. (Recall that
[
Elk95b
]
asked whether this is true for
N_{L}(K)
. In fact, it also separately asked whether
N_{C}(K)
is bounded for every
K
.)

We conclude by noting a consequence of
Theorem

1.3
.
The
hull
of a code
\mathcal{C}
is its intersection with its dual code (i.e., the hull of
\mathcal{C}
is
\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
), and it is well known that
\mathrm{PCE}
is easy to solve on codes with small hulls both in theory and in practice
[
Sen00
,
BOS19
]
.
That is, hard instances of
\mathrm{PCE}
necessarily consist of codes with large hulls.
Theorem

1.3
is interesting because it gives a natural class of codes
\mathcal{C}
(ones for which
s(\mathcal{C})
is large) that have maximally large hulls but on which
\mathrm{PCE}
is still easy.

1.3
Open Questions

A primary open question is whether one can find an algorithm for
\mathrm{LIP}
on
arbitrary
self-dual lattices that is faster than the
n^{O(n)}
-time algorithm for general
\mathrm{LIP}
in
[
HR14
]
. Indeed, even a
2^{100n}
-time algorithm would be interesting.
One approach for this would be to compute shortest characteristic vectors for the input lattices
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
(and therefore
s(\mathcal{L})
and
s(\mathcal{L}^{\prime})
).
Indeed, one can think of
s(\mathcal{L})
as a refinement of the genus of
\mathcal{L}
(the genus is certain efficiently computable data about
\mathcal{L}
; see
Section

2.2.2
). And,
s(\mathcal{L})
is computable in
2^{O(n)}
time using known algorithms
[
MV13
,
ADS15
]
for the Closest Vector Problem (
\mathrm{CVP}
), since it amounts to finding a shortest vector in a coset of
\mathcal{L}
, which is equivalent to
\mathrm{CVP}
.
However, unfortunately, it is not clear how to use
s(\mathcal{L}),s(\mathcal{L}^{\prime})
to decide whether
\mathcal{L}\cong\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
. Indeed, there are non-isomorphic odd self-dual lattices
\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
with
s(\mathcal{L})=s(\mathcal{L}^{\prime})
(see the pairs of such lattices of rank
n=18
and rank
n=20
in
Table

1
), and it is easy to see that all even self-dual lattices have the all-zeroes vector
\boldsymbol{0}
as their shortest characteristic vector (so that, e.g., shortest characteristic vectors cannot be used to distinguish between
E_{8}^{3k}
and
\Lambda_{24}^{k}
).

Similarly, it is an interesting open question whether one can find faster algorithms for
\mathrm{PCE}
on arbitrary self-dual codes than those in
[
BCGQ11
,
BBB+26
]
for arbitrary codes.

Preliminaries

We use
\mathsf{O}_{n}:=\{O\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}:O^{T}O=I_{n}\}
to denote the set of
n\times n

orthogonal matrices
. In particular,
\mathsf{O}_{n}
consists of matrices
O\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
such that
O^{T}O=I_{n}
, hence left-multiplication by
O
does not change the Euclidean norm of any vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
is the set of
unimodular matrices
. That is, every matrix
U\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
is an
n\times n
integer matrix with
\det(U)=\pm 1
. We use
S^{>0}_{n}(R)
to denote the set of symmetric positive definite matrices over a ring
R\subseteq\mathbb{R}
(we will take
R=\mathbb{Z}
or
R=\mathbb{R}
).

We use
\boldsymbol{e}_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
(or
\boldsymbol{e}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
) for
i\in[n]
to denote the
i
th standard normal basis vector.

2.1
Lattices

A (full-rank)
lattice

\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the set of all integer linear combinations of some
n
linearly independent vectors
\boldsymbol{b}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{b}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
That is,

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(B):=\Big\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\boldsymbol{b}_{i}:a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}\Big\}

is the lattice generated by the
basis

B:=(\boldsymbol{b}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{b}_{n})\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
.
We define the
direct sum
of two lattices
\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
to be the lattice obtained by concatenating vectors from
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
:

\mathcal{L}\oplus\mathcal{L}^{\prime}:=\{(\boldsymbol{v}^{T},(\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime})^{T})^{T}:\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{L},\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\}\ \text{.}

The
Gram matrix
of a basis
B
is defined as
B^{T}B
,
and we call
Q:=B^{T}B
the
quadratic form
associated with the lattice
\mathcal{L}(B)
.

The
minimum distance
of a lattice
\mathcal{L}
is defined as

\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{L}):=\min_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{L}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}}\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert\ \text{.}

The
determinant
of a lattice
\mathcal{L}
with basis
B
is equal to
\det(\mathcal{L}):=\det(B^{T}B)^{1/2}
.
Two bases
B,B^{\prime}\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
generate the same lattice if and only if
B^{\prime}=BU
for some
U\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
, and so
\det(\mathcal{L})
is well-defined. Minkowski’s Theorem asserts that for any lattice
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{L})\leq\sqrt{n}\cdot\det(\mathcal{L})^{1/n}\ \text{.}

(1)

The
dual lattice

\mathcal{L}^{*}
of a lattice
\mathcal{L}
is defined as

\mathcal{L}^{*}:=\{\boldsymbol{y}\in\lspan(\mathcal{L}):\forall\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{L},\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle\in\mathbb{Z}\}\ \text{.}

It is straightforward to show that
\mathcal{L}^{*}
is indeed a lattice,
(\mathcal{L}^{*})^{*}=\mathcal{L}
, and
\det(\mathcal{L}^{*})=1/\det(\mathcal{L})
.

A lattice
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called
integral
if
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathcal{L}^{*}
, i.e., if the inner product of any two vectors in
\mathcal{L}
is an integer.
Lattices that satisfy
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}^{*}
are called
self-dual
, and one can show that
\mathcal{L}
is self-dual if and only if it is integral and
\det(\mathcal{L})=1
.
Self-dual lattices are often referred to as
unimodular lattices
because a lattice
\mathcal{L}(B)
generated by a basis
B
is self-dual if and only if its Gram matrix
B^{T}B
is unimodular (see, e.g.,
[
Hun24
, Lemma 2]
).

A self-dual lattice
\mathcal{L}
is called
odd
if there exists
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{L}
such that
\lVert\boldsymbol{x}\rVert^{2}
is odd, and it is called
even
otherwise
[
CS98
, p. 48]
. Even self-dual lattices only exist in dimensions that are multiples of
8

[
CS98
, Ch. 7, Corollary 18]
.

2.2
Lattice Isomorphism and Quadratic Form Equivalence

Given bases
B,B^{\prime}\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
, the lattices
\mathcal{L}(B)
and
\mathcal{L}(B^{\prime})
are
isomorphic
if there exist an orthogonal matrix
O\in\mathsf{O}_{n}
and a unimodular matrix
U\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
such that
B^{\prime}=OBU
. Equivalently, two lattices
\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
are isomorphic if there exists
O\in\mathsf{O}_{n}
such that
O(\mathcal{L})=\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
. We define the equivalence class
[\mathcal{L}]:=\{O(\mathcal{L}):O\in\mathsf{O}_{n}\}
of lattices isomorphic to
\mathcal{L}
.

We also follow the notation of
[
DvW22
]
and denote the
R
-
equivalence class
of a quadratic form
Q\in S^{>0}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
with respect to a ring
R
as

[Q]_{R}=\{U^{T}QU:U\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(R)\}\ \text{.}

We are mainly interested in the case where
R=\mathbb{Z}
, and in this case we sometimes omit the subscript and simply write
[Q]
.

2.2.1
The Correspondence Between Lattices and Quadratic Forms

Two bases
B,B^{\prime}
generate isomorphic lattices if and only if their corresponding quadratic forms
Q:=B^{T}B,~Q^{\prime}:=(B^{\prime})^{T}B^{\prime}\in S^{>0}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
are
\mathbb{Z}
-equivalent. Indeed, if
OBU=B^{\prime}
for
O\in\mathsf{O}_{n}
and
U\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
, then

Q^{\prime}=(B^{\prime})^{T}B^{\prime}=U^{T}B^{T}O^{T}OBU=U^{T}B^{T}BU=U^{T}QU\ \text{.}

Moreover, if
Q^{\prime}=U^{T}QU
for
U\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
then it must be the case that
B^{\prime}=OBU
for some
O\in\mathsf{O}_{n}
. Indeed,
O:=B^{\prime}(BU)^{-1}
satisfies
OBU=B^{\prime}
, and
13
13

13

We use the notation
A^{-T}
for a matrix
A
to mean its inverse transpose
(A^{-1})^{T}=(A^{T})^{-1}
.

O^{T}O=(BU)^{-T}(B^{\prime})^{T}B^{\prime}(BU)^{-1}=B^{-T}U^{-T}Q^{\prime}U^{-1}B^{-1}=B^{-T}QB^{-1}=I_{n}\ \text{.}

Quadratic forms
Q
corresponding to integral lattices are useful to work with from a computational perspective since they contain all integer entries. So, we formalize
\mathrm{LIP}
in terms of quadratic forms in the sequel.

We also note that the above discussion shows that the map
[\mathcal{L}]\mapsto[Q]
defined by taking
B
to be a basis of
\mathcal{L}
and setting
Q:=B^{T}B
is a well-defined, bijective map from equivalence classes of lattices to equivalence classes of quadratic forms.
We say that a quadratic form
Q
(respectively, quadratic form equivalence class
[Q]
)
corresponds to
a lattice
\mathcal{L}
with basis
B
(respectively, lattice equivalence class
[\mathcal{L}]
) if
Q=B^{T}B
(respectively, if
[Q]=[B^{T}B]
).

2.2.2
Lattice Genus

Taking
R=\mathbb{Q}
and
R=\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, where
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
denotes the
p
-adic integers, affords a coarser notion of equivalence.
Specifically,
[Q]_{\mathbb{Z}}=[Q^{\prime}]_{\mathbb{Z}}
implies that
[Q]_{\mathbb{Q}}=[Q^{\prime}]_{\mathbb{Q}}
and
[Q]_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}}=[Q^{\prime}]_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}}
for all primes
p
, where
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
denotes the
p
-adic integers.
The data
([Q]_{\mathbb{Q}},([Q]_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}})_{p})
, where
p
ranges over primes dividing
2\det(Q)
, is called the
genus
of
Q
.
Moreover, the genus of
Q
is efficient to compute given a prime factorization of
\det(Q)
; see, e.g.,
[
vW24
,
DvW22
]
.

So, quadratic forms
Q,Q^{\prime}
in distinct genera cannot be
\mathbb{Z}
-equivalent, and therefore
\mathrm{LIP}
is essentially only a hard problem on quadratic forms in the same genus.
There are at most two distinct genera that contain self-dual lattices in each dimension. In particular, the genera of full-rank, self-dual lattices of fixed dimension
n
are the odd self-dual lattices, and, for
n\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 8)
, the even self-dual lattices
[
CS98
]
.

2.3
Codes and Code Equivalence

A
binary, linear code

\mathcal{C}
(or simply
code
) of length
n
is a linear subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, and we call a code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
of dimension
k
an
[n,k]
code. We write
\clen(\mathcal{C})
and
\dim(\mathcal{C})
for the length and dimension of a code
\mathcal{C}
, respectively.
As with lattices, one can represent
[n,k]
codes via a basis (i.e.,
generator matrix
)
G\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times k}
.
14
14

14

In this work, we use
column
bases both for codes and lattices.

Specifically, we define the code
\mathcal{C}(G)
generated by
G
to be

\mathcal{C}(G):=\{G\boldsymbol{x}:\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}\}\ \text{.}

Furthermore, we define the orthogonal subspace of a code
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
to be the
dual code
of
\mathcal{C}
, which we denote by
\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
. That is,

\mathcal{C}^{\perp}:=\{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}:\forall\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathcal{C},\langle\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle=0\}\ \text{.}

By the rank-nullity theorem, if
\mathcal{C}
is an
[n,k]
code then
\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
is an
[n,n-k]
code.

We call a code
\mathcal{C}

self-orthogonal
if
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
, which is equivalent to the condition that
\langle\boldsymbol{c}_{1},\boldsymbol{c}_{2}\rangle=0
for all codewords
\boldsymbol{c}_{1},\boldsymbol{c}_{2}\in\mathcal{C}
.
If
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
, then we call
\mathcal{C}

self-dual
.
Note that a self-dual code
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
must have dimension
k=n/2
, which in particular implies that self-dual codes only exist for even lengths
n
.

We define the
direct sum
of two codes
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
to be the code

\mathcal{C}\oplus\mathcal{C}^{\prime}:=\{(\boldsymbol{c}^{T},(\boldsymbol{c}^{\prime})^{T})^{T}:\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathcal{C},\boldsymbol{c}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\}\ \text{.}

We define the
minimum distance
of a code
\mathcal{C}
as

d(\mathcal{C}):=\min_{\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathcal{C}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}}\wt(\boldsymbol{c})\ \text{,}

where
\wt(\cdot)
denotes Hamming weight.

2.3.1
Code Equivalence

Let
\mathcal{P}_{n}
denote the set of
n\times n
permutation matrices.
We say that two codes
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
are
permutationally equivalent
, denoted by
\mathcal{C}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\approx}}\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
, if there exists
P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
such that
P(\mathcal{C})=\{P\boldsymbol{c}:\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathcal{C}\}=\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
. Recall that
\mathcal{C}(GU)=\mathcal{C}(G)
for any generator matrix
G\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times k}
and
U\in\mathrm{GL}_{k}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
. So, we have that two codes
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
with respective generator matrices
G,G^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times k}
are permutationally equivalent if and only if there exist a permutation matrix
P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
and a full-rank matrix
U\in\mathrm{GL}_{k}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
such that
PGU=G^{\prime}
(equivalently, if there exists
P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
such that
P(\mathcal{C})=\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
).

2.3.2
Construction A

Codes and lattices are closely related objects.
In particular, one can construct lattices from binary, linear codes via
Construction A
.

Definition 2.1

(Construction-A lattice)
.

Given a linear code
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, we define
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{C}}:=\mathcal{C}+2\mathbb{Z}^{n}
as the
Construction-A lattice
obtained from
\mathcal{C}
.

Here we define arithmetic mixing elements of
\mathbb{F}_{2}
and
\mathbb{Z}
by implicitly lifting elements of
\mathbb{F}_{2}
to their canonical representatives over the integers (i.e., to
\{0,1\}\subset\mathbb{Z}
).
It is straightforward to show that
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{C}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a full-rank lattice.
We note that

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{C}}=\{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}:\boldsymbol{y}\bmod 2\in\mathcal{C}\}\ \text{,}

where the
\bmod~2
operation is applied component-wise to each vector.

The simplest self-dual code is the double repetition code

\mathcal{Z}:=\{(0,0)^{T},(1,1)^{T}\}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2}\ \text{,}

2.4
Characteristic Vectors of Self-Dual Lattices and Codes

We now introduce characteristic vectors of self-dual lattices and codes, which is the main technical tool that we leverage in this work.
If
\mathcal{L}
is a self-dual lattice, a vector
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{L}
is called a
characteristic vector
(also called a
parity vector
in
[
CS98
]
) if for all
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{L}
,

\langle\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}\rangle\equiv\langle\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)\ \text{.}

Let
\wt(\boldsymbol{y})
denote the Hamming weight of a vector
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
.
A characteristic vector of a self-dual binary code
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
is a vector
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
that satisfies

\langle\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{w}\rangle\equiv\frac{1}{2}\wt(\boldsymbol{c})\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)

for all
\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathcal{C}
.
We note that
\wt(\boldsymbol{c})
is necessarily even for every codeword
\boldsymbol{c}
in a self-dual code
\mathcal{C}
, and so
\frac{1}{2}\wt(\boldsymbol{c})
is always an integer.
Unlike characteristic vectors of lattices, the characteristic vectors of a self-dual code are not themselves codewords.

We next present several important properties of characteristic vectors for self-dual lattices and codes.

Lemma 2.4

Given a code

For the “furthermore” part of the lemma, it is sufficient to show that

We next give several algorithms for lattice and coding problems.

\mathcal{L}

\boldsymbol{v}

\mathcal{L}

\boldsymbol{t}
(i.e., a vector
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{L}
with
\lVert\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{t}\rVert=\min_{\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}}\lVert\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{t}\rVert
).

(
[

In this section, we study

3.1
An Algorithm for
\mathrm{LIP}
on Self-Dual Lattices

We first argue that there is a roughly
2^{n/2}
-time algorithm for computing the decomposition of a self-dual lattice
\mathcal{L}
into the direct sum of
\mathcal{L}_{0}
and a rotation of
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
.
The algorithm works by noting that the problem reduces to finding unit-length vectors in
\mathcal{L}
, and so we start with the following theorem. Recall that an
integral lattice

\mathcal{L}
is one that satisfies
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathcal{L}^{*}
(and therefore that every self-dual lattice is integral).

Lemma 3.1
.

There is a
2^{n/2+o(n)}
-time randomized algorithm for
\mathrm{SVP}
on integral lattices
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{L})\leq o(\sqrt{n}/\log n)
.

Proof.

A lattice
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called
semi-stable
if for every sublattice
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
,
\det(\mathcal{L}^{\prime})\geq 1
. And,
[
BGPS23
, Corollary 5.5]
gives a
2^{n/2+o(n)}
-time randomized algorithm for
\mathrm{SVP}
on semi-stable lattices
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{L})=o(\sqrt{n}/\log n)
. So, to prove the lemma it suffices to show that an arbitrary integral lattice
\mathcal{L}
is semi-stable.

For this, consider any arbitrary sublattice
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
of
\mathcal{L}
, and let
B^{\prime}
be a basis of
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
. We have that
\det(\mathcal{L}^{\prime})^{2}=\det((B^{\prime})^{T}B^{\prime})
by definition, and, because
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
is integral,
(B^{\prime})^{T}B^{\prime}
is integer-valued. Furthermore, the Gram matrix of a basis is positive definite and so
\det((B^{\prime})^{T}B^{\prime})>0
. It follows that
\det(\mathcal{L}^{\prime})^{2}=\det((B^{\prime})^{T}B^{\prime})
is an integer greater than
0
, which implies that
\det(\mathcal{L}^{\prime})\geq 1
, as needed.
∎

We then get the following theorem.

Theorem 3.2
.

There is a randomized algorithm that, on input a quadratic form

\mathcal{L}_{0}

Proof.
