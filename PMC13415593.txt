AGGF1 delays endothelial cellular senescence through the TGFB3-TAK1-AMPK signaling axis
Authors: Xiaojuan Zhong, Weixin Lv, Xueer Li, Limei Wang, Minhong Zhang, Kang Liu, Jiayi Dong, Qiang Yuan, Shilin Zhang, Andong Wu, Xueting Gong, Jiankun Liu, Bingbing Zhou, Shihui Ye, Qiquan Wang, Yang Xiang, Xiao-Li Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEndothelial senescence is a critical contributor to vascular aging and age-related vasculopathies. Our previous work identified AGGF1 as a regulator of cell cycle progression and anti-inflammatory signaling. However, whether AGGF1 modulates endothelial senescence remains unclear. Here, we demonstrate that AGGF1 is downregulated in both replicative and DOX-induced senescent HUVECs. AGGF1 knockdown accelerated cellular senescence as evidenced by senescence markers, including increased SA-β-gal activity, enhanced formation of γH2AX foci, elevated IL-6 levels, and impaired proliferation, whereas AGGF1 overexpression prevented DOX- and MMC-induced senescence using adenovirus and lentivirus-mediated gene manipulation. To investigate the underlying mechanisms, we performed RNA sequencing, small-molecule drug intervention, transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and other imaging techniques in subsequent experiments. Transcriptomic and functional analyses revealed that AGGF1 transcriptionally upregulates TGFB3, which is associated with TAK1 activation and AMPK phosphorylation, ultimately inhibiting excessive mitochondrial fragmentation and suppressing cellular senescence. This signaling module reduced DRP1 expression and attenuated its activating phosphorylation at Ser616. TEM results further confirmed that AGGF1 overexpression significantly reduced mitochondrial fragmentation in cells. Additionally, this proposed association was supported by pharmacological inhibition (Takinib, Compound C) and TGFB3 knockdown, which abrogated AGGF1-mediated protection. Collectively, we identified that AGGF1 is critical for regulating a proposed TGFB3-TAK1-AMPK regulatory module, which delays endothelial senescence partially through maintenance of mitochondrial morphology. Our study provides evidence that AGGF1 plays an important role in metabolic control and cellular senescence.Keywords: Senescence, AGGF1, AMPK, TAK1, TGFB3

Introduction

Global population aging has become one of the most pressing public health challenges. The World Health Organization projects that individuals aged 60 years and older will account for 38% of the global population by 2050 (Rudnicka et al. 2020; Wu et al. 2024). Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) remain the leading cause of mortality in the aging population, with conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, and cerebral infarction posing serious health threats. Vascular aging, the primary driver of many CVDs, contributes significantly to the development and progression of numerous age-related disorders (Li et al. 2025). Endothelial cells (ECs), which form the innermost monolayer of the blood vessel lumen, play a central role in vascular aging (Assar et al. 2022; Hwang et al. 2022). In response to various stressors, ECs undergo proliferation arrest, sustain DNA damage, and promote a state of chronic systemic inflammation by secreting numerous inflammatory factors, such as Interleukin-6(IL-6), thereby exacerbating CVD progression (Bektas et al. 2018; Long et al. 2022). Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms underlying endothelial cellular senescence and developing therapeutic strategies to delay this process are crucial for the prevention and treatment of age-related vascular CVDs.

Mitochondria possess dynamic structures that undergo fission and fusion (Burtscher et al. 2023; Guo et al. 2023). Perturbations in mitochondrial morphological dynamics contribute to cellular senescence through several mechanisms, including the following: excessive mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) generation (Dan Dunn et al. 2015; Li et al. 2023), imbalance of mitochondrial dynamics, dysregulated activation of 5′ adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) (Wang et al. 2021), alterations in mitochondrial metabolome, and disrupted dysregulation of mitochondrial Ca2+ handling. Mitochondria continuously remodel their architecture through fission and fusion cycles, processes that regulate organelle number, morphology, and intracellular distribution (Tábara et al. 2025). Emerging evidence suggests that AMPK is a sentinel kinase that regulates various aspects of mitochondrial dynamic remodeling (Wang et al. 2024; Zanfardino et al. 2025). Under energetic stress, AMPK promptly remodels the mitochondrial reticulum by phosphorylating the mitochondrial fission factor, facilitating the recruitment of Dynamin-related protein 1 (DRP1) to the outer mitochondrial membrane (Li et al. 2022). Furthermore, AMPK interacts directly with mitofusin 2 (MFN2) in human osteosarcoma cells (Ashraf & Kumar 2022; Hu et al. 2021). However, the upstream signaling regulators governing AMPK activity are complex and require further investigation.

The angiogenic factor with G-patch and FHA domain 1 (AGGF1), also known as VG5Q, is a potent regulator of angiogenesis and endothelial cell proliferation (Cheng et al. 2025; Tian et al. 2004). AGGF1 is widely expressed in various human tissues and is particularly abundant in vascular ECs, smooth muscle cells, and osteoblasts (He et al. 2023; Yu et al. 2022). Increasing evidence indicates that AGGF1 is a key regulator of endothelial cell function by modulating cellular proliferation, migration, and apoptosis. Consequently, AGGF1 plays a critical role in vascular development, angiogenesis, and the maintenance of vascular integrity (Hu et al. 2013; Lv et al. 2025; Takahashi et al. 2024). AGGF1 knockdown inhibits tube formation in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) on Matrigel, and AGGF1 regulates the cell cycle to promote endothelial cell proliferation (Zhang et al. 2021). Additionally, AGGF1 exerts anti-inflammatory effects in HUVECs. For instance, it protects ECs by inhibiting the ERK/NF-κB pathway, thereby alleviating TNF-α-induced inflammatory response (Zhu et al. 2018). Inflammation is a characteristic of senescent cells (Cai et al. 2024). Collectively, these findings suggest a potential role for AGGF1 in cellular senescence. However, the involvement of AGGF1 in endothelial cellular senescence remains unclear.

In this study, we employed replicative and inducible senescence models to investigate the role of AGGF1 in endothelial cellular senescence. We found that AGGF1 delays endothelial cellular senescence and suppresses excessive fission through modulating AMPK activation. Collectively, we propose that TGFB3/TAK1/AMPK form a new regulatory module that restores mitochondrial morphology and delays endothelial cellular senescence.

Methods

Cell culture and treatment

Primary HUVECs were acquired from the Chinese Academy of Sciences cell bank and cultured in endothelial cell medium (ECM, ScienCell) at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee (Approval No. NCULAE-20221130018), and written informed consent was obtained from all donors. To generate senescent cells, HUVECs were serially cultured from passages 10 to 30 using the population doublings (PDL) method. Additionally, to establish the senescence phenotypes, HUVECs were seeded into 6-well plates and treated with doxorubicin (DOX; #C6557, BMASSAY; 50 nM) or mitomycin C (MMC; #H0324, BMASSAY; 1 mg/mL) for 24 h, and then treated with Takinib (#HY-103490, MedChemExpress; 10 μM) or Dorsomorphin (Compound C; #HY-13418A, MedChemExpress; 5 μM) for 24 h. The cells were then subsequently incubated in medium for an additional 72 h. Control groups were treated with an equal volume of DMSO. For transfection, the shAGGF1-1 (5′-GGTTTACAGAATACAGAATAC-3′), shAGGF1-2 sequences (5′-GAACCAGAGGAAGGTGAAATT-3′) and siTGFB3(5′-GUCCAUGUCACACCUUUCATT-3′) were acquired from the DSIRI (http://biodev.extra.cea.fr/DSIR/DSIR.html) website. HUVECs were maintained in a 6-well plate at 2 × 105 cells per well until the cell density reached 70%–90%. HUVECs were transfected with lentivirus containing either shAGGF1-1, shAGGF1-2, or control (shNC) for 24 h. For GFP and AGGF1 overexpression, the coding sequences were cloned into adenovirus plasmids. Adenoviral particles were produced by co-transfecting HEK293A cells into 6-well plates with GFP and AGGF1 overexpression adenovirus plasmids. Viral supernatants were harvested and used to infect the HUVECs for 24 h.

Colony formation assay

HUVECs (200 cells per well) were seeded in a 6-well plate and incubated in a 5% CO2 incubator for 14 days. When visible clones appeared, the cells were fixed with 1 mL of absolute methanol (#80080418, Sinopharm) and stained with crystal violet (#C0121-100 mL, Beyotime). Finally, the results were photographed after the excess dye was removed.

Senescence-associated β-galactosidase assay

The Senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) staining kit (#C0602, Beyotime) was employed to measure senescent cells. HUVECs were seeded into 6-well culture plates and incubated overnight with a staining solution at 37 ℃ and 5% CO2 to assess SA-β-gal activity, at pH 6. The percentage of SA-β-gal-positive cells with a blue stain was captured under a bright-field microscope. The percentage of positive cells was calculated.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was acquired following lysis with lysis buffer (#P0013B, Beyotime). Subsequently, the proteins were uncoupled by sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (#FFP24, Beyotime). The blocked membranes were probed with primary antibodies against AGGF1 (1:1000; #11889-1-AP, proteintech), γH2AX (1:1000; #ab26350, Abcam), DRP1 (1:1000; #12957-1-AP, proteintech), MFN2 (1:2000;#12186-1-AP, proteintech), AMPK (1:1000; #10929-2-AP, proteintech), p-AMPK (1:1000; #2535, cell signaling), p-DRP1 (Ser637) (1:1000; #4867, cell signaling), p-DRP1 (Ser616) (1:1000; #4494, cell signaling), β-actin (1:5000; #66009-1-Ig, proteintech) at 4℃ overnight incubation with secondary antibody goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:5000; #SA00001-2, proteintech) or goat anti-mouse IgG (1:5000; #SA00001-1, proteintech) for 60 min. Membranes were observed using an electrochemical luminescence reagent (#P0018S, Beyotime).

Real-time quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction

HUVECs were lysed with Trizol reagent (#15596026, Ambion), then 5 × FastKing RT SuperMix (#KR118, TIANGEN) was employed for cDNA synthesis. Then, real-time quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) was conducted by SYBR GREEN Real-time PCR Master Mix (#QPK-201, toyobo). Primer sequences contained:

AGGF1 (F) 5′-GCATCACACAGAACGGCTGTA-3′

(R) 5′-TCATTTCTCCCACGTTGGAGTAT-3′

IL-6(F)5′-GTCAGGGGTGGTTATTGCAT-3′

(R) 5′-AGTGAGGAACAAGCCAGAGC-3′

TGFB3 (F) 5′-GAAGAGGGTGGAAGCCATTAGG-3′

(R) 5′-TAGGGCAGACAGCCAGTTCGT-3′

GAPDH (F)5′-TGTGGGCATCAATGGATTTGG-3′

(R) 5′-ACACCATGTATTCCGGGTCAAT-3′

Immunofluorescence

The cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and treated with 1% Triton-X 100 (#T8200; Solarbio) for 5 min. Following blocking with 3% BSA for 30 min, cells were incubated with primary antibodies against γH2AX (1:200; #ab26350, Abcam) and Ki-67 (1:200; #ab15580, Abcam) at 4 ℃ overnight. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:200; #ab150077, Abcam) secondary antibodies for 1 h. After Hoechst (#C1017, Beyotime) staining for 10 min, the cells were observed under a confocal laser.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

HUVECs were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 2 h. After the preparation, the samples were examined under an electron microscope (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). Target images were captured with an AMT-600 image acquisition engine and processed in Adobe Illustrator software.

Ribonucleic acid extraction, complementary DNA (cDNA) library construction, and transcriptome analysis

After AGGF1 overexpression in HUVECs, cells were harvested following DOX treatment, and total Ribonucleic acid (RNA) was extracted using TRIzol. Sequencing libraries were constructed and subjected to PE150 sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform (GeneChem, Shanghai, China). Raw reads were filtered to remove adapters, ambiguous N bases, and low-quality reads (Qphred ≤ 20 in > 50% bases), generating clean data. Clean reads were mapped to the hg38 genome using HISAT2 software (version 2.0.5), and gene expression was quantified with feature Counts software (version 1.5.0-p3). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 software (version 1.20.0) with thresholds of |fold change|> 2 and Padj < 0.05. Gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed using clusterProfiler software (version 0.3.8.1; Kim et al. 2015; Liao et al. 2014; Mortazavi et al. 2008).

The “DESeq2” package software (version: 1.48.1; Wu et al. 2021) was employed to identify DEGs, and < 0.05 and |log2FC|≥ 1 were utilized as the screening thresholds. Additionally, enrichment analysis was conducted using the clusterProfiler (Wang et al. 2021) software (version 4.9.0.002), followed by visualization using the ggplot2 software (version 3.5.1).

Statistical analysis

All data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. For comparisons between two independent groups, the t-test was used. For comparisons across three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used and Dunnett’s post-hoc test was applied to compare each experimental group against the control group to control the familywise Type I error rate. GraphPad Prism 6.0 was utilized for all analyses. Statistical significance was deemed when P < 0.05.

Results

AGGF1 expression is decreased with senescence

To investigate the relationship between AGGF1 and cellular senescence, two senescence models were established in HUVECs: Replicative senescence and DOX-induced senescence. In the replicative senescence model, HUVECs at PDL30 exhibited a significant increase in SA-β-gal–positive cells (P < 0.0001) and a notable decrease in Ki-67-positive proliferating cells (P < 0.01, a prominent proliferation marker) when compared to HUVECs at PDL10 (Fig. 1a–d, respectively). Concurrently, AGGF1 mRNA (P < 0.001) and protein (P < 0.05) levels were significantly reduced in PDL30 HUVECs (Fig. 1e–g). Similarly, in the DOX-induced senescence model, treatment with 50 nM DOX led to a significant increase in SA-β-gal-positive cells (P < 0.001; Fig. 1h, i) and a reduction in Ki-67-positive cells (P < 0.01; Fig. 1j, k). Furthermore, AGGF1 expression was downregulated at mRNA (P < 0.01, P < 0.001) and protein levels (P < 0.01) in a dose-dependent manner after DOX treatment (Fig. 1l–n).

Collectively, these results consistently demonstrated a pronounced decrease in AGGF1 expression at the transcriptional and translational levels in replicative and stress-induced senescence models, establishing a strong inverse correlation between AGGF1 and endothelial cellular senescence.

AGGF1 knockdown promotes endothelial cellular senescence

To elucidate the regulatory role of AGGF1 in endothelial cellular senescence, HUVECs were transfected with two independent AGGF1-targeting short hairpin RNAs (shAGGF1-1 and shAGGF1-2) to suppress AGGF1 expression, while a non-targeting control shRNA (shNC) was used as the negative control. Cellular senescence was assessed by SA-β-gal staining. Our findings revealed a remarkable elevation in the percentage of SA-β-gal-positive cells in the shAGGF1-1 and shAGGF1-2 groups compared to the shNC (P < 0.001; Fig. 2a, b). This observation indicates that AGGF1 knockdown significantly exacerbates endothelial cellular senescence.

AGGF1 knockdown promotes endothelial cellular senescence. a, b SA-β-gal staining and quantification of SA-β-gal-positive cells in HUVECs after AGGF1 downregulation. c, f Immunofluorescence images of Ki-67 (c, d) and γH2AX (e, f) expression in HUVECs after AGGF1 downregulation. g, h Colony formation assay detected cell proliferation following knockdown of AGGF1. i mRNA expression levels of IL-6 detected in HUVECs following knockdown of AGGF1, (n = 3). *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001

Besides, DNA damage is a central hallmark of cellular senescence, we investigated γH2AX expression. Our results demonstrated a significant enhancement in γH2AX (foci per cell, with ≥ 5 foci considered positive) following AGGF1 knockdown (P < 0.01; Fig. 2e, f). Conversely, Ki-67 expression was markedly decreased in AGGF1-knockdown HUVECs when compared to the shNC group (P < 0.001; Fig. 2c, d). To further evaluate its effect on the proliferative capacity, a colony formation assay was performed. This assay, known for its utility in assessing cellular proliferation, demonstrated a substantial decrease in the number of large colonies after AGGF1 downregulation (P < 0.05, P < 0.01; Fig. 2g, h). Moreover, IL-6 mRNA expression in HUVECs was significantly increased after AGGF1 downregulation (P < 0.01; Fig. 2i). Collectively, these results strongly suggest that AGGF1 knockdown promotes endothelial cellular senescence.

AGGF1 prevents endothelial cellular senescence

To clarify the role of AGGF1 in cellular senescence, HUVECs were infected with Adv-AGGF1. As indicated in Fig. 3a–c, under DOX and MMC treatments, the percentage of SA-β-gal-positive cells was significantly reduced in the AGGF1 overexpression group than in the control group (P < 0.01, P < 0.05). Correspondingly, immunofluorescence analysis revealed that AGGF1 overexpression significantly decreased the number of γH2AX in DOX- and MMC-induced senescence models (P < 0.01, P < 0.05; Fig. 3h–j). Consistent with this, Western blot results demonstrated that AGGF1 overexpression markedly reduced the protein level of γH2AX (P < 0.05; Fig. 3f, g). Furthermore, the colony formation assay revealed a significant increase in the number of large colonies upon AGGF1 overexpression (P < 0.05; Fig. 3d, e). Additionally, the percentage of Ki-67-positive cells was significantly higher in the AGGF1-overexpressing group compared to the Adv-GFP control group (P < 0.05; Figs. 3k–m). IL-6 mRNA expression in DOX-treated HUVECs was significantly decreased following AGGF1 overexpression (P < 0.01; Fig. 3n). Collectively, these results suggested that AGGF1 plays a critical role in mitigating DOX-induced senescence.

AMPK is a key modulatory node linking AGGF1 to senescence regulation

To investigate the mechanism underlying AGGF1-mediated delay of cellular senescence, RNA sequencing was conducted in AGGF1-overexpressing cells treated with DOX. Following differential expression analysis, 467 DEGs were screened, including 312 upregulated and 155 downregulated DEGs (Fig. 4a). The heatmap shows the expression patterns (Fig. 4b). Moreover, enrichment analysis revealed that these 467 DEGs were significantly enriched in GO terms related to cell cycle checkpoint signaling, response to tumor necrosis factor, and response to oxygen levels (Fig. 4c), as well as in KEGG pathways associated with cell cycle and cellular senescence (Fig. 4d). To identify potential core genes within these pathways, we performed a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis of senescence-associated genes (Fig. 4e). The PPI network was predominantly composed of cell-cycle-related genes. Notably, AMPK emerged as a key modulatory node linking cell cycle regulation with mitochondrial function-related genes, including TGFB3, IGFBP3, and others. Numerous studies have reported that AMPK is critically involved in mitochondrial function and cellular senescence (El-Dessouki et al. 2024; Zhu et al. 2018) and that AMPK is localized to the mitochondria, suggesting its potential role in mediating the anti-senescence effects of AGGF1.

AGGF1 inhibits endothelial cellular senescence and reduces mitochondrial fragmentation by modulating AMPK

We determined whether the AGGF1-mediated inhibition of endothelial cellular senescence and mitochondrial fragmentation is linked to AMPK. Subsequently, the findings related to AGGF1-AMPK-mitochondrial dynamics were presented. Notably, in shRNA-mediated AGGF1 knockdown groups (shAGGF1-1 and shAGGF1-2), the levels of phospho-AMPK (p-AMPK) protein expression were significantly reduced (P < 0.001; Fig. 5a, b). Concurrently, AGGF1 depletion led to extensive mitochondrial fragmentation, reflected by shortened and disconnected organelles, as indicated in Fig. 5c. To quantitatively characterize these morphological changes, we conducted image analysis, which demonstrated that AGGF1 knockdown reduced both the mean and median mitochondrial branch length per cell compared to controls, confirming enhanced mitochondrial fission (P < 0.01, P < 0.001, P < 0.0001; Fig. 5d, e). While the levels of p-AMPK were markedly increased, both mean and median mitochondrial branch lengths per cell were significantly increased following AGGF1 overexpression (P < 0.05; Fig. 5f–j). Based on the results, we further employed TEM to examine mitochondrial morphology alterations in DOX-treated cells following AGGF1 overexpression. The results demonstrated that, compared with the GFP group, AGGF1 overexpression resulted in elongated mitochondrial structures. (Fig. 5k). We finally performed Western blot analysis to evaluate the expression of key regulatory proteins. Expression of the fission-related protein DRP1 was downregulated upon AGGF1 overexpression (P < 0.05), accompanied by a marked reduction in DRP1 phosphorylation at Ser616 (P < 0.01). Conversely, AGGF1 overexpression substantially increased the protein level of MFN2 (P < 0.05), a key mediator of mitochondrial fusion (P < 0.05; Fig. 5l–o). These data suggest that AGGF1 is associated with AMPK activation during HUVEC senescence.

To further delineate the contribution of AMPK to AGGF1-mediated inhibition of endothelial cellular senescence, the AMPK inhibitor Compound C was used. In DOX-treated cells, Compound C administration effectively counteracted the AGGF1-mediated reduction in the percentage of SA-β-gal-positive cells (P < 0.05; Fig. 5p, q). Crucially, the inhibitory effect of AGGF1 on the expression of γH2AX and Ki-67 in DOX-treated HUVECs was abrogated by Compound C administration (P < 0.05; Fig. 5r–u). These findings suggested that AMPK activation is functionally significant in the mechanism by which AGGF1 attenuates cellular senescence and mitochondrial fragmentation.

Transforming growth factor-β-activated kinase 1(TAK1) is functionally required for AGGF1-associated AMPK activation

Finally, to investigate whether AGGF1 inhibited endothelial cellular senescence by modulating AMPK, we analyzed its downstream signaling pathways. RNA-seq data and validation experiments (P < 0.05; Fig. 6b) revealed that AGGF1 overexpression significantly upregulated TGFβ3 transcription. While TGFB1 and TGFB2 have been established as upstream activators of TAK1 through TRAF6-mediated mechanisms, direct evidence for TGFB3-mediated TAK1 activation remains to be established. TAK1 has been reported as a kinase capable of phosphorylating AMPK in certain cellular contexts (Fig. 6a). Based on these observations and our transcriptomic data indicating TGFB3 upregulation upon AGGF1 overexpression, we hypothesized that the TGFβ3-TAK1 axis might serve as a candidate upstream module through which AGGF1 modulates AMPK. This hypothesis is supported by the observation that Takinib, a TAK1 inhibitor, attenuates AGGF1’s anti-senescent effects. Compared with the control group, the proportion of SA-β-gal positive cells was significantly increased in the Takinib-treated group following DOX exposure (P < 0.05, Fig. 6c, d). Besides, Takinib treatment effectively reversed the effects of AGGF1 on the expression levels of γH2AX (P < 0.05, Fig. 6e, f) and the proliferation marker Ki-67 (P < 0.05, Fig. 6g, h) in DOX-induced HUVECs. To clarify whether AGGF1 directly regulates TAK1 through TGFB3, we designed siTGFB3 to downregulate the expression of TGFB3. Knockdown of TGFB3 effectively counteracted the AGGF1-mediated reduction in the percentage of SA-β-gal-positive cells in DOX-treated cells (P < 0.05, Fig. 6i, j). Meanwhile, the rescue of AGGF1 on the expression of Ki-67 in DOX-treated HUVECs was abrogated by TGFB3 downregulation (P < 0.01; Fig. 6k, l). Collectively, these results indicate that TGFB3/TAK1 is functionally involved in AGGF1-mediated AMPK activation. We propose that AGGF1 upregulates TGFB3, thereby contributing to TAK1 activation and subsequent AMPK phosphorylation, ultimately delaying endothelial cellular senescence. However, direct biochemical evidence demonstrating TGFB3-induced TAK1 activation remains to be established.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that AGGF1 delays endothelial cellular senescence and is associated with changed mitochondrial morphology. Our findings suggest that AGGF1 involved in a proposed TGFB3-TAK1-AMPK regulatory module that appears to influence DRP1 and MFN2 expression and contribute to the maintenance of mitochondrial morphology, potentially attenuating endothelial cellular senescence.

Age-related endothelial dysfunction is a well-established contributor to CVD. In this study, AGGF1 expression was significantly reduced in replicative senescent HUVECs and DOX-induced senescent HUVECs, paralleled by a significant reduction in proliferation marker Ki-67. The decline in AGGF1 contribute to endothelial dysfunction. This finding aligns with our previous work demonstrating the role of AGGF1 in cell-cycle regulation and anti-inflammatory signaling. Additionally, previous studies have demonstrated that AGGF1 overexpression enhances the proliferation of human pulmonary artery endothelial cells (HPAECs) (Cheng et al. 2025). Moreover, AGGF1 overexpression promotes the proliferation of human retinal microvascular endothelial cells (HRMECs). However, the direct evidence supporting the role of AGGF1 in delaying cellular senescence has remained limited. In the present study, loss-of-function experiments using lentiviral-mediated knockdown demonstrated that AGGF1 depletion accelerated multiple hallmarks of endothelial senescence, including increased SA-β-gal activity, enhanced γH2AX foci formation, reduced Ki-67 expression, and elevated levels of senescence-associated secretory phenotype factors such as IL-6. Conversely, gain-of-function experiments demonstrated that AGGF1 overexpression attenuated DOX- and MMC-induced senescence in vitro, suggesting its protective role against stress-induced premature senescence. DOX- and MMC-induced senescence are well-established models for studying therapy-induced aging and have been widely used to explore the mechanisms of cellular senescence (Devrukhkar et al. 2025; Kciuk et al. 2023; Matveeva et al. 2024). Collectively, our study is the first to indicate that AGGF1 delays cellular senescence.

Morphological changes and functional impairments of mitochondria are among the characteristic features of cellular senescence. Excessive mitochondrial fission, mediated primarily by DRP1, has been implicated in multiple age-related pathologies (Li et al. 2019; Tang et al. 2024; Yan et al. 2024). Conversely, impaired MFN2 activity leads to mitochondrial fusion defects, which in turn induce severe metabolic dysfunctions, including inhibition of oxidative phosphorylation and reduced ATP production (Mas-Bargues 2023). In this study, our morphometric analyses using MitoTracker staining and TEM revealed that AGGF1 knockdown promoted mitochondrial fragmentation, whereas AGGF1 overexpression was associated with elongated mitochondrial networks. These morphological alterations were accompanied by corresponding changes in DRP1 and MFN2 protein levels, suggesting that AGGF1 may modulate the molecular machinery governing mitochondrial morphology. AGGF1 help maintain mitochondrial quality control ultimately delaying the onset of senescence. This aligns with emerging evidence that mitochondrial morphology plays a critical role in regulating cellular senescence.

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying AGGF1’s effects on mitochondrial dynamics and senescence, we performed transcriptomic profiling of DOX-treated senescent HUVECs. Our analysis identified 467 DEGs following AGGF1 overexpression, with substantial enrichment in cell cycle and cellular senescence pathways. Notably, many of these DEGs were associated with mitochondrial function, and approximately 53.3% of the genes in the senescence pathway were linked to mitochondrial biology, aligned with mitochondria’s central role in senescence regulation. Furthermore, PPI network analysis highlighted AMPK as a central node that connects various mitochondria-related genes. Additionally, our experimental findings revealed that AGGF1 overexpression enhanced its phosphorylation. AMPK is a master regulator of cellular energy metabolism and has been implicated in lifespan extension and promotion of health span in multiple species (Langer et al. 2024; Penugurti et al. 2024). AMPK activation can delay cellular senescence through multiple mechanisms, including enhancing mitochondrial biogenesis, promoting mitophagy, and modulating mitochondrial dynamics (Hu et al. 2021; Jiang et al. 2024). Our findings indicate that AGGF1-mediated AMPK activation may contribute to the suppression of DRP1 and the upregulation of MFN2, ultimately modulating mitochondrial structural balance.

AMPK activation is primarily mediated by phosphorylation of its catalytic α-subunit at the Thr(172) residue, a process regulated by three canonical upstream kinases: Calcium and Calmodulin-Dependent Protein Kinase Kinase Beta (CaMKKβ1), Liver Kinase B1 (LKB1), and TAK1 (Neumann 2018; Sun et al. 2025; Xu et al. 2018; Yu et al. 2014). In this study, transcriptomic analysis revealed that TGFB3 was among the upregulated genes after AGGF1 overexpression, suggesting a potential link between AGGF1 and TGFB3 expression. Functional experiments demonstrated that treatment with Takinib, Compound C or siTGFB3 attenuated the anti-senescent effects of AGGF1 overexpression, as evidenced by increased SA-β-gal positivity, increased γH2AX foci, or decreased Ki-67 expression. These findings are aligned with the hypothesis that AGGF1-mediated TGFB3 upregulation may be functionally associated with TAK1 and AMPK activation.

However, this study has several limitations. First, DOX- and MMC-induced senescence models differ from physiological aging in several aspects, which may limit their clinical relevance to natural human aging. Second, although our results indicate that AGGF1 promotes TGFB3 transcription, the precise molecular mechanism by which AGGF1 regulates TGFB3 transcriptional activation remains unclear and requires further in-depth investigation. Third, the proposed TGFB3-TAK1-AMPK regulatory network is based primarily on transcriptomic correlation, pharmacological inhibition studies, and genetic evidence from TGFB3 knockdown. Although we have validated the pathway using siTGFB3, direct biochemical evidence demonstrating TGFB3-induced TAK1 phosphorylation and the direct phosphorylation events between TAK1 and AMPK in our experimental system remain elusive. Moreover, the reliance on pharmacological inhibitors (Takinib and Compound C) alone limits causal interpretation due to potential off-target effects; therefore, further genetic validation targeting TAK1 and AMPK will be necessary to establish definitive causality. Finally, our mitochondrial analyses are primarily morphological; functional readouts, including mitochondrial membrane potential, ATP production, and mtROS levels, remain to be elucidated in future research.

In summary, this study demonstrates that AGGF1 delays endothelial cellular senescence and is associated with activation of a proposed TGFB3-TAK1-AMPK regulatory module. Our findings establish a potential role for AGGF1 in cellular senescence, providing the foundation for its translational potential in treating age-related vascular diseases.
