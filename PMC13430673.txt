Online Abortion Information for Unintended Pregnancy in Australia: An Evaluation
Authors: Lucy Lewis, Jennifer L Marino, Yifan Gao, Anne‐Marie La Salle, Marlena Leslie, Yvonne Hauck
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundOnline information in Australia about abortion has not been assessed, despite its capacity to impact the trajectory of care without face‐to‐face medical expertise.AimTo evaluate the online information that women in Australia can access for an unintended pregnancy.Study DesignMixed methods were employed in four phases. A search identified abortion portals (defined as a webpage under a single domain, providing access to other abortion websites). DISCERN scores evaluated portals quality standard. A Student's t‐test was employed to assess the difference in quality by type of organisation (portals ending in .gov.au vs. .org.au). A one‐way ANOVA compared portals according to the population size they targeted: ≤ 3 million, > 3 million to 8.2 million and > 8.2 million. Summative content analysis explored the framework of written words within portals associated with decision‐making.ResultsTwo hundred and thirty‐seven results were identified, with 24 selected. Portals were generally high quality (61.25 out of a possible 80). Quality standard did not significantly differ by type of organisation (.gov.au: 64.9, .org.au: 59.1; p = 0.28), nor by population size targeted (≤ 3 million: 64.2, > 3 million to 8.2 million: 58.3, > 8.2 million: 61.25; p = 0.59). Summative content analysis identified five categories associated with decision‐making: I am pregnant now what?, emotional support, facts that impact choices, practicalities and procedural knowledge.ConclusionThe rigorous methods employed to review the online abortion sites provided women with the information required to better prepare for and navigate abortion services and make decisions expeditiously. We now need to assess if women are using these resources to make decisions about their care.

Introduction

The World Health Organization [1] defines abortion as an essential component of women's sexual and reproductive health. Globally, there are an estimated 73.3 million abortions annually [2], with estimates suggesting that 1 in 4 Australian women will have an abortion in their lifetime [3]. However, Australia has no nationally reported data on private or government funded abortion services [3, 4], which has the capacity to challenge service provision [5, 6].

The decision to proceed with an abortion is a private matter [7]. A global scoping review of 50 qualitative studies explored how stigma shapes the abortion environment. Results revealed shame, denial and secrecy formed one of four overarching themes [8]. These sentiments are echoed by women globally [8, 9]. A contemporary study [9] exploring what constitutes quality abortion care interviewed 98 women who obtained an abortion. Confidentiality and privacy were one key theme identified. Therefore, being able to search the internet privately might be particularly important for those seeking an abortion.

Moreover, Generation Z (born 1995–2010) are more likely (995/1862, 53.44%) to use the internet to learn about health‐related topics than earlier generations [10]. Additionally, women are increasingly technologically educated at sourcing health information online [10, 11, 12]. Limited evidence exists in relation to using the internet to procure abortion services. However, a recent American study interviewed 45 women to explore women's pathways to abortion, in abortion‐restrictive South Carolina, finding they located abortion clinics through online sites [13].

The quality of online abortion information Australian women can access for an unintended pregnancy has not been assessed. This is important as women may be using these resources to make decisions about care without the input of face‐to‐face medical expertise. Therefore, we aimed to evaluate the quality standard and structure within online portals for women seeking an abortion for an unintended pregnancy in Australia. We also sought to identify factors within those portals associated with decision‐making.

Materials and Methods

Prior to commencing the research, our methodology was presented to the ‘Working Group’ within SPHERE (Australia's Centre of Research Excellence in Sexual and Reproductive Health for Women in Primary Care and an Australian National Health and Medical Research Council funded organisation). Undergoing this consultative process enabled us to refine our methods through brainstorming each phase of the research, with a team of experts. Mixed methods, in four phases, were employed. The examination was undertaken between December 2022 and November 2023.

Ethics Approval

As the research utilised information that was in the public domain, freely available and did not directly involve individual human participants, ethics approval was deemed unnecessary. Furthermore, although the research was not strictly a synthesis of the literature, we followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systemic Reviews and Meta‐Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines [14].

Search Strategy

Similar to a prior study undertaken by members of the research team [15], a portal was defined as a web page, under a single domain, produced by a single organisation. Portals also had to provide access to other websites and web pages concerning abortion.

An internet search was performed in English using ‘Google’. ‘Incognito mode’ was activated to enable the research team to search the internet privately. For consistency, only portals identified in the first 10 pages of each search were considered, as it was noted that the majority of searches resulted in 10 pages.

The terms ‘women’ and either ‘termination of pregnancy’ or ‘abortion’ were searched in conjunction with the name of each Australian state and territory (Australian Capital Territory, New South Wales, Northern Territory, Queensland, South Australia, Tasmania, Victoria or Western Australia). Two types of portal were eligible for investigation: government portals ending with ‘.gov.au’ and those operated by the Public Interest Registry and commonly used by non‐profit organisations such as hospitals ending with ‘.org.au’.

To minimise the risk of missing a portal, recording an incorrect portal title or ‘Uniform Resource Locator’ (URL), every state and territory was searched independently by two researchers [16]. The resulting 16 data sets (two for each of the eight states and territories) were uploaded into a spreadsheet (Microsoft Excel 2016, Redmond WA USA) and the duplicates deleted. The research team met every fortnight over 4 months to discuss and reach consensus in relation to the exclusions. Finally, eligible portals were saved in a ‘Portable Document Format’ (PDF) to ensure the same data was used throughout the entire study.

Quality Standard

DISCERN [17] was utilised to evaluate the quality of written information within eligible portals. This validated tool comprises 16 questions, scored 1–5, divided into three sections: the reliability of the publication (eight questions), the quality of information on treatment choices (seven questions) and an overall rating (one question). DISCERN does not explicitly assess the factual accuracy of assessed websites but requires users to have sufficient expertise in website topics to consider the quality of information. The three senior team members are experts in women's reproductive health. Scores range from 16 to 80, with a higher quality standard aligned with a higher score [18].

When DISCERN was initially validated, consumer information was provided in a paper format. The tool continues to be employed to assess online consumer information [15, 19], with contemporary research comparing other validated tools to DISCERN, evidencing the tool's reliability [19].

To reduce bias and increase inter‐coder reliability, each portal was scored independently by two team members [16]. Differences in scoring were resolved by discussion between paired researchers. Agreed scores for each portal were uploaded into Excel for analysis.

Portal Structure

To evaluate the structure, online links out and other visual content within portals, a data extraction tool was developed in Excel. Piloting of this tool using a Canadian and English termination of pregnancy portal identified the need to record the number of online links out (including to online counselling services) and whether portals addressed counselling.

The final data extraction tool collected information relating to: portal safety (the presence of a quick exit button and the ability to hide search history), access to portal content in a language other than English, the option to have written content read aloud, links to an online thesaurus and the date the portal was last updated.

Additionally, the tool collected information on: levels of information within portals; number of links out from each portal; links from selected portals to each other; links to counselling services; whether counselling was discussed; number of PDFs and finally, the number of videos.

For uniformity, the initial page of a portal was the URL of its entry, with the first four levels [20] considered for enumeration. Error was minimised [16] as team members assessed portals independently. Paired researchers re‐examined differences until a resolution was reached. Resulting data were uploaded into Excel for analysis.

Qualitative Content Analysis

To assess the framework of written words within eligible portals associated with decision‐making, subjective interpretation of the text was undertaken using qualitative content analysis. This inductive method goes beyond the enumeration of words used in traditional quantitative analysis, systematically extracting the text to identify concepts and categories [21].

Two teams of three researchers reviewed 12 portals each. Heading topics were extracted. Weekly meetings for 6 weeks were required to meet consensus in relation to confirming the categories and their content. During this process, factual accuracy of the portals was informally evaluated by the team.

Statistics

Categorical data were analysed using frequency distributions, with continuous data summarised as means, standard deviations (SD), interquartile ranges (IQR) and ranges. Mean DISCERN scores were utilised to evaluate quality standard. To assess whether there was a difference in quality by the type of organisation providing information, quality standard was compared between portals with a URL ending in .gov.au and those ending in .org.au using Student's t‐test [22]. Additionally, a one‐way ANOVA [23] was undertaken to compare portals according to the population size they targeted: ≤ 3 million (Australian Capital Territory, Northern Territory, South Australia, Tasmania and Western Australia), > 3 million to 8.2 million (New South Wales, Queensland and Victoria), and individual portals which catered for more than one state and territory where the combined total population was more than > 8.2 million. To examine significant differences between groups and check homogeneity of variances, Levine's test for equality of variances was utilised [22]. Two‐sided p‐values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Summative content analysis was utilised to explore the framework of written words [21] within portals with information to aid decision‐making. Concepts were derived from headings within the selected portals. These were revisited and condensed further into categories and sub‐categories. Two processes were central to trustworthiness. Having a clearly defined coding system and de‐contextualisation and contextualisation of the concepts into categories [24].

Results

Search Strategy

Searching identified 773 results. The eight Australian states and territories were searched independently, with duplicates in each domain removed. Results were pooled, enabling the identification of portals that catered for more than one state or territory. In total 536 duplicates were removed (Figure 1). The remaining 237 results were examined for eligibility. A further 213 exclusions were identified, leaving 24 portals (Table 1) for analysis. The majority of portals were developed in Victoria, with Western Australia and the Northern Australia each having one portal.

Quality Standard

Portals were generally of high quality (61.25 out of a possible 80; Data S1). The IQR was 53.7; 64.5−72. Quality standard (77: SD 14.63) did not significantly differ between portals with URLs ending in .gov.au and those ending in .org.au (.gov.au: 64.9, .org.au: 59.1; p = 0.28).

Quality standard did not differ significantly by population size targeted (pop size ≤ 3 million: 64.2, > 3 million to 8.2 million: 58.3, portals which catered for more than one state and territory where the combined total population was more than 8.2 million: 61.25, p = 0.59).

Portal Structure

Just under half (45.8%, n = 11) of the portals had a ‘quick exit icon’, with a third (33.3%, n = 8) having a ‘hide history icon’. The option to have the content read aloud was available in some portals (16.6%, n = 4), whilst just under a third (29.2%, n = 7) had an online thesaurus to decipher medical terminology. One‐fifth (20.8%, n = 5) had the option to have information presented in a language other than English (Table 1). One portal accounted for 44% (n = 48) of the PDFs, with all PDFs providing identical information in a different language. Portals had a mean of 2.75 information levels. The date the portal was last updated was provided by 58.3% (n = 14).

Portals had a total of n = 359 online links out (IQR 4.75; 9−13). Just under a fifth (17.5%, n = 63) of the links out were to another selected portal in this analysis. The majority (79.2%, n = 19) of portals addressed counselling, with most (58.3%, n = 14) providing an online link to a counselling service. Portals also contained 109 PDFs; almost a third of portals (29.2%, n = 7) included a video, all of which presented information on medication and/or aspiration abortion. All links were active.

Qualitative Content Analysis

Initial review of portal content identified 98 concepts. After duplicate removal the remaining 76 concepts were de‐contextualised and condensed into 11 categories, which were de‐contextualised and contextualised into five final categories and their accompanying sub‐themes (recorded in italics): I am pregnant now what? (Confirming pregnancy, Implications of gestation); emotional support (Reducing psychological issues, Counselling options); facts that impact choice (Legal facts, Languages other than English); practicalities (Private/government funded abortion, Identification of a support person); and procedural knowledge (Medical abortion, Surgical abortion, Post‐abortion) (Table 2). Information within portals was clear, unbiased and non‐judgmental.

Discussion

This world‐first study identified 24 Australian portals for women seeking an abortion for an unintended pregnancy. Abortion portals were identified in every Australian state and territory. As portals were freely available for those women with access to the internet, we surmise women may be using these portals to access information about abortion and make decisions about care, without the input of face‐to‐face medical expertise. Therefore, gaining insight into the information associated with decision‐making within these portals is important.

Whilst studies have employed DISCERN to examine the quality of online health information [15, 19, 25], none has used this tool to assess the quality of online portals for women seeking an abortion. Whilst this makes our study unique, it also makes it difficult to discuss these findings within the context of the current literature. Whilst we did not formally evaluate the factual accuracy of the portals by comparison to national or international guidelines, portals were generally of high quality (61.25 out of a possible 80), and our qualitative content analysis concluded that information was clear, current and unbiased. Government portals did not significantly differ in quality from other types of portals, nor did the size of the targeted population. Portals had 359 online links out and 63 of the links out from portals were to each other. This had the potential to homogenise information within portals and could have impacted our finding that portals were similar in quality.

The decision to proceed with an abortion is often a private [8], secret [8], confidential [8, 9] matter. Being able to exit abortion portals quickly and hide one's browser history helps ensure privacy. Most (54.2%) selected portals did not contain a ‘quick exit icon’, and only a third (33.3%) had a ‘hide history icon’. In addition, only five (16.6%) portals provided information in a language other than English. Greater diversity in language options is warranted in a multicultural country like Australia. We suggest these aspects warrant further attention.

Despite differences in Australian abortion law in each state and territory [6, 26], we found five categories that described information to assist decision‐making woven throughout the portals. A contemporary global study [27] identified nine abortion decision‐making trajectories. Some of our categories aligned with this research such as: ‘I'm pregnant now what?’ and ‘Pregnancy awareness’; ‘Emotional support’ and ‘Seeking support’; and ‘Procedural knowledge’, and ‘Abortion procedure’. Others did not, such as ‘Initial abortion decision’ and ‘Negotiations’. We suggest the reasoning behind these differences may be jurisdiction, as our focus was from an Australian, not a global perspective.

Moreover, alongside our category ‘emotional support’ the majority (79.2%) of selected portals discussed the importance of counselling, with most (58.3%) providing an online link to a counselling service. A recent contemporary study [28] by two of the services linked to the portals included in this analysis, contacted 5107 individuals who had sought counselling for their current and/or past pregnancy over a period of 20 months. Whilst the study did not establish the proportion of Australian women requesting abortion counselling, it illustrates a need for supportive services.

As we necessarily restricted our search to one time point, this study did not reflect subsequent changes in abortion legislation and new online innovations such as Google AI and ChatGPT. It is not yet clear how the integration of generative AI into search functions will affect the accuracy of search results and the privacy of users [29, 30]. We suggest it may be valuable to regularly evaluate the online abortion information Australian women can access for unintended pregnancy at regular intervals to capture changes.

Whilst we undertook strategies to minimise the risk of missing a portal, we may not have identified all portals that met our inclusion criteria. Similar research in other jurisdictions may generate different results. We did not investigate the content of links out of the selected portals or quantitative data relating to resources for the prevention of unintended pregnancy post abortion. This would have enhanced our knowledge in relation to the information being conveyed to women. We did not evaluate the relative frequency of specific results.

We did not consider whether women with an unintended pregnancy are accessing information from these online abortion portals to make decisions about their care. This information is needed. Future research could survey women through abortion providers. Alternatively, monitoring internet usage could be considered. A recent cross‐sectional study [31] provided evidence that following the US Supreme Court ruling on Dobbs v Jackson Women's Health Organization, Google queries about abortion and contraception increased from 16 302 to 75 746 per 10 million.

Finally, Google search results are personalised to the user [32]. Whilst we attempted to overcome this by duplicating searches across the team and by using incognito/private browsing mode, it is possible that our searches differed from those of the average Australian woman seeking an abortion for an unintended pregnancy.

Our study is unique in that it is the first to employ a number of rigorous methods to examine the quality standard, structure and decision‐making categories within online Australian abortion portals. It was encouraging to find abortion portals in every state and territory. We now need to ascertain whether women with an unintended pregnancy are accessing this information to make decisions about their care.
