Optimization and hardware evaluation of a modified reconfigurable ring oscillator PUF
Authors: Kaveri Hatti, C Paramasivam, Ramesh Chinthala, Jitendra Bahadur, Dong-Won Kang, Sree Ranjani Rajendran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhysical Unclonable Functions (PUFs) are emerging hardware security primitives that generate device-specific secret keys by exploiting inherent manufacturing variations. Among the various PUF architectures, Ring Oscillator PUFs (RO-PUFs) are notable for their simplicity and high performance. However, conventional RO-PUFs suffer from a limited number of challenge–response pairs (CRPs), making them susceptible to machine learning (ML) attacks, and scaling them to handle more CRPs often incurs significant hardware overheads. This paper presents a novel Configurable Delay Unit based Modified Reconfigurable Ring Oscillator PUF (MRROPUF) that integrates Exclusive-OR (XOR) and Exclusive-NOR (XNOR) gates to dynamically configure delay paths using challenge bits. This architecture substantially increases the CRP space and entropy while reducing resource utilization. Additional security is achieved through challenge obfuscation via permutation and enhanced reliability via Temporal Majority Voting (TMV). The proposed design was implemented on Xilinx Virtex-6 and Zynq-7000 FPGAs. The experimental results demonstrated high uniqueness (49.76% and 49.0%), near-ideal uniformity (49.3% and 49.0%), and excellent reliability (99.72% and 99.0%) across the two platforms. Compared to existing Configurable RO-PUF and Reconfigurable RO-PUF designs, the MRROPUF achieves superior efficiency and robustness, making it a strong candidate for secure and lightweight hardware authentication in resource-constrained applications.Keywords: Ring oscillator, Delay unit, Temporal majority voting (TMV), Hard macros, Unit resource costSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Physical Unclonable Functions (PUFs) have emerged in the past two decades as a widely studied hardware security primitive, offering lightweight and intrinsic protection against cloning and counterfeiting in integrated circuits (ICs)1,2. PUFs can generate device-unique responses to input challenges by exploiting unavoidable manufacturing process variations, thereby enabling applications such as authentication, key generation, and protection of intellectual property (IP)3. Unlike secret keys stored in non-volatile memory (NVM), which are vulnerable to invasive readout and tampering4, PUF responses are generated on demand and do not require permanent storage.

In a PUF, a one-way mapping from an m-bit challenge C to an n-bit response R is implemented;

where P is determined by the physical characteristics of the device. The uniqueness of this mapping across devices is measured using the inter-chip Hamming distance, Tfinter, and its reliability under environmental variations is measured using the intra-chip Hamming distance, Hintra.

PUFs are typically classified as weak or strong5,6. Weak PUFs, with a limited number of challenge-response pairs (CRPs), are suitable for applications such as key derivation or random number generation, wherein the responses remain internal to the device. Strong PUFs offer a much larger CRP space, supporting challenge response-based authentication without additional cryptographic processors. Prominent strong PUF implementations include arbiter PUFs and bistable ring PUFs7.

Among strong PUF designs, the Ring Oscillator PUF (RO-PUF) has gained particular attention due to its simple architecture, compatibility with standard digital flows, and good statistical performance. A hybrid logic gate-based configurable RO-PUF architecture was proposed in8 to eliminate the area and power overhead of multiplexer-based designs, and by using compact delay-configurable units, it improves hardware efficiency while preserving PUF properties such as uniformity, uniqueness, and robustness against process and environmental variations. As an extension of8, an ultra-lightweight configurable RO-PUF structure was proposed in9 that significantly enhances the CRP space without incurring a proportional silicon area increase and thus can be used in resource-constrained IoT applications. A more robust configurable RO-PUF architecture was proposed in10 to improve the reliability of PUF- based key generation in embedded systems under environmental variations through oscillator selection optimization. An efficient configurable RO-based module that can be used as both a PUF and a tr random number generator (TRNG) was proposed in11, which achieved higher hardware efficiency than traditional methods by exploiting logic block configurability, and the statistical evaluation demonstrated strong reliability and uniqueness for the use of such a PUF in embedded and IoT applications. A crossover ring oscillator PUF architecture was proposed in12 to resist machine learning and differential power analysis attacks, and the proposed architecture increased the CRP space through intercrossed oscillator paths and optimal key selection, thus providing strong attack resilience with low hardware overhead. In13, a reliability-aware complementary cascode current mirror PUF is presented in 65 nm technology, which, through circuit-level optimizations, demonstrates zero native bit flips at various temperatures and voltages, proving that high reliability is possible without the aid of additional error correction mechanisms. In a conventional RO-PUF, pairs of nominally identical ring oscillators are compared to generate response bits. However, such designs suffer from an inherent limitation: the number of unique CRPs is directly tied to the number of available oscillator pairs, which is bounded by the silicon area. This restriction makes traditional RO-PUFs susceptible to modelling attacks, especially those using machine learning, because the reduced CRP space can be exhaustively profiled.

In contrast to traditional CRO-PUFs, which employ multiplexers to select multiple parallel inverter paths, our architecture directly integrates configurability into the delay element. This method enhances the variability within each stage rather than between different paths. Conventional CROs/RROs alternate between a limited number of pre-established delay paths, resulting in increased routing complexity and additional hardware overhead. However, our approach leverages the dual functionality of XOR/XNOR gates to reconfigure the delay without replicating the entire stages. Instead of selecting among multiple paths, we adjust the delay at the gate level, thereby shifting the configurability from path selection to the behavior of the logic gate itself, which increases the variability within each stage. While classical designs predominantly depend on delay differences between nominally identical oscillator paths, our method introduces configuration-dependent delay dispersion within the XOR/XNOR stages, thereby expanding the challenge space without necessitating additional oscillators. This approach also enhances hardware efficiency by eliminating multiplexers and redundant inverter branches, allowing for more configurations per CLB and yielding more CRPS per unit silicon area. From a security perspective, embedding configurability within logic gates enhances resistance to modeling attacks, as the mapping between configuration bits and oscillation frequency becomes more non-linear and challenging to decipher. Finally, because the architecture is defined at the primitive gate level, it is inherently adaptable across technologies, enabling its application in both FPGA and ASIC flows, making it particularly suitable for resource-constrained security applications.

To overcome these limitation, the Configurable and Reconfigurable RO-PUFs architecture was proposed3. These approaches introduce additional delay paths, multiplexer-based selection, or reconfiguration logic to expand the CRP space and enhance resistance to modelling. However, they often require significantly more hardware resources, which may not be suitable for lightweight or cost-sensitive applications, such as Internet of Things (IoT) edge devices.

In this study, we propose a configurable delay unit-based modified reconfigurable ring oscillator PUF (MRROPUF) that addresses the CRP scalability problem with minimal hardware overhead and improved modelling resistance and reliability. The proposed design integrates configurable delay elements implemented using XOR and XNOR gates, the configuration of which is dynamically controlled by challenge bits. This approach increases entropy and expands the effective CRP set without proportionally increasing the number of physical oscillators. Security is further enhanced through obfuscated challenge permutations and temporal majority voting, which improve both modelling resistance and reliability.

The proposed MRROPUF was implemented and experimentally validated on Xilinx Virtex-6 and Zynq-7000 FPGAs. The results indicate improvements in uniqueness, uniformity, and reliability compared with existing configurable and reconfigurable RO-PUF designs, while maintaining a low hardware cost. These enhancements demonstrate that the proposed approach provides a practical and efficient means of strengthening hardware security in resource-constrained environments.The results show uniqueness rates of 49.76% and 49%, uniformity rates of 49.3% and 49%, and reliability rates of 99.729c and 99%, respectively outperforming existing configurable and reconfigurable RO-PUF designs while maintaining low hardware cost. These findings demonstrate that the proposed approach offers a practical and efficient route for enhancing hardware security in resource-constrained environments.

Related work

Three frequently used forms of FPGA-based PUFs are as follows:The arbiter-based PUF or APUF compares the timing or delay information of two identical pathways to select the output14. One of the oldest and most thoroughly researched PUF designs is the A-PUF. However, because of the arbiter circuit’s (D-Flip Flop) metastability and the delay routes’ intricate routing requirements, it has a subpar FPGA implementation15.The starting value of static random access memory cells (SRAM-PUF) was initially used to introduce memory-based PUF16. The primary drawback of these PUFs is that not all FPGA boards have SRAM modules that have not been initialized16. All types of FPGA boards may be used to build Butterfly PUF, an alternative memory-based PUF structure7.In comparison to the A-PUF and Butterfly PUF, the RO- PUF is easy to implement on FPGA. It makes use of the difference in frequency between a pair of identically constructed ring oscillators (ROs). The primary drawback of this type of PUF is that a large number of RO pairs must be included in the design of the RO PUF to provide numerous reliable output bits, which consumes a large number of hardware resources17. Due to its need for substantial hardware resources for design, implementing such an RO PUF design poses challenges in many lightweight IoT and embedded system applications. In earlier work, many RO PUF configurations have been presented18 to reduce the amount of hardware required. In19, a prediction test screening method that uses fewer hardware resources is suggested as a way to increase the reliability of the ring oscillator PUF. The configurable ring oscillator(CRO) PUF was used in the proposed authentication protocol and key renewal strategy for clustered sensor networks to increase security20. Kursawe et a1. pioneered the development of the Reconfigurable PUF (RPUF) circuit18, which enhances the number of Challenge- Response Pairs (CRPs) by incorporating various operations through circuit reconfiguration. The reconfiguration signals can modify the PUF behavior either partially or entirely, resulting in the creation of a new PUF. RPUFs can be categorized into physical and logical RPUFs21. A notable example of a physical RPUF is the reconfigurable optical PUF, which utilizes a powerful laser to rapidly melt the optical material. This process causes the optical scatter to rearrange, thereby generating new random CRPs. Such a transformation alters the PUF at the molecular level and is irreversible. In contrast, logical RPUFs expand the number of CRPs without changing the original design of the PUF, thereby allowing for reversible modifications.

To reduce resource utilization, an FPGA-based configurable EXOR gate-based reconfigurable RO-PUF was designed. Additionally, the permutation and temporal majority voting (TMV) technique was used to generate the CRPs, which increased the performance of the design. Comparing the proposed PUF architecture to configurable and reconfigurable RO PUF to produce the single-bit response, it is intended to increase CRPs with the same amount of CLB.

The proposed MRROPUF architecture is based on an XOR-configurable Ring Oscillator(RO) that increases entropy at the cost of minimal hardware overhead. In traditional RO PUFs, each oscillator is based on a fixed delay path, but the proposed design introduces structural configurability at the gate level. XOR gates are used as configurable delay elements, and each RO can operate in multiple structural configurations without additional multiplexers, thus avoiding the additional routing asymmetry and deterministic delay components introduced by multiplexers. In contrast to CRO and RRO PUFs, where configurability is implemented by external path selection, such flexibility is moved inside the LUTs, and by perturbing delays at the LUT leve the design can finely tune the internal propagation paths, making the circuit more sensitive to manufacturing variations and increasing the structural diversity within a single CLB.

MRRO PUF Algorithm.

According to Fig. 10, the RO-PUF is initially implemented by initiating a 64-bit master challenge from a user PC (Matlab) over a UART interface, which is then applied to a permutation function to produce a permutated challenge. The data encryption standard (DES) cryptographic technique is used by the permutation function to reorder the words in a series. The MRROPUF implementation uses only the fixed, public DES Initial Permutation (IP) table a keyless, combinational 64 bit remapping, requiring just FPGA LUT6 primitives, not full DES encryption with rounds, S-boxes, or key management. The 64-bit input challenge undergoes static rewiring (e.g., bit 58 position 1, bit 50 position 2) before feeding three PUFs, rotating the feature space from f(C_in) to f(DES_IP(C_in)) to disrupt linear ML modeling. No key exists the permutation is hardcoded at synthesis across all 10 FPGAs, with zero key-related signals in the Vivado netlist. Our adversary model assumes full attacker knowledge: they know/invert the public DES IP table and attack using actual permuted challenges collected from the 100000.

The internal challenge bits are basically concealed by this permutation strategy. Then, two distinct ROs are selected for comparison from each of these sub-challenges (permutation output). The least significant bits (LSBs) in the challenges choose one of the eight ROs in group 1 and group 2 using multiplexers 1 (MUXI) and 2. (MUX2). The PUF is provided the whole challenge sequence in order to set the EOR to the specified number of NOT gates and select the path through the MUX. The frequency of the chosen ROs from groups 1 and 2 is then acquired and supplied into the corresponding 9-bit counters.

The 9-bit reference counter is powered by a crystal clock signal produced by an on-board oscillator at 100 MHz (controller). The reference counter, counter 1, and counter 2 begin counting simultaneously and are compelled to stop when the reference counter reaches its maximum value. The response bit for this RO pair is then generated by comparing the values of counters 1 and 2, depending on which counter has the greater value. In this study, Temporal Majority Voting (TMV) was used before the EOR operation to enhance attack complexity and decrease response instability. The inputs for delay control are applied 64 times to each level. The raw responses are stored in a shift register with 64 bits.

The “golden responses” is then determined for them using the TMV principle. If more than 50% of the raw replies generated are 1s, the golden response is deemed 1, else it is considered 0. A 1-bit “final response” is produced by EXORing the 64 1-bit generated golden replies (TMV output), which are stored in a 64-bit shift register. Each 64-bit master challenge (challenge from UART) generates a total of 64-bit final response bits, which are then placed in a 64-bit shift register. The final response bits are sent to the PC through a UART 8-bit connection in order to be analysed for PUF performance. The ROs are chosen, the oscillations are counted, and responses are returned based on comparisons by the controller circuitry. It utilizes the enable input to start and stop the ROs.

By controlling the “En” input, only selected ROs oscillate, while unselected ones stay still, significantly reducing overall power use.

The proposed design better utilizes the FPGA’s LUT resources and it incorporates configurability directly into the XOR- based logic. It does not rely on additional multiplexers, because unlike CRO PUFs, it does not require dedicated MUXs to choose between different delay paths. Rather, the XOR gate serves as both an inverter and a configurable delay, thus there is no additional selection logic. Furthermore, the design exploits the "don’t-care" inputs of FPGA LUTs, so it can be optimized for better performance. The inputs can be configured accordingly, and the internal signal routing within the LUTs is slightly altered. This provides very fine-grained delay control at sub-gate granularity, because a large number of oscillator configurations can be realized within the same physical implementation. This expands the CRP space without additional area overhead, making the architecture more efficient. Finally, the architecture enables selection of RO pairs with adequate frequency separation, and if the oscillators have large frequency margins when paired, the design will perform better. The responses will be more robust against variations in temperature and voltage; therefore, they will be more reliable.

The goal of the vulnerability analysis is to assess the robustness of the diiferent RO-PUF designs against machine learning- based modeling attacks, and the results of this study are expected to provide insights into designing more robust PUFs in the future. For the assumed threat model, the attacker has access to a subset of the MRRO-PUF CRPs, namely 25% of the CRPs, and for calculating the predictive accuracy of the machine learning models, the standard split is used, i.e., 75% of the data is used for training and 25% of the data is kept aside for testing. Each MRRO-PUF CRP covers 75% of the total CRP space, and 25% of the MRRO-PUF CRPs are included in each randomly chosen set of 100,000 CRPs used to train the different machine learning models, which allows five of the most commonly used machine learning algorithms to be used to evaluate the susceptibility of the RO-PUF design to modeling attacks in a comprehensive manner and thus allows for a broad comparison of how well an adversary can learn and predict the behavior of the PUF.

Conclusion

This paper reviews PUF architectures that emphasize hardware efficiency and reliability, aiming at resource-constrained devices, and highlights how architectural configurability and circuit-level optimization can improve key PUF metrics, such as uniqueness, uniformity, reliability, and robustness against environmental variability. The proposed approaches reduce the area and power consumption without compromising security because the conventional multiplexers in a PUF design are replaced by compact delay-configurable units or optimized analog structures. However, current methods also present certain limitations, as most evaluations are conducted in limited voltage and temperature ranges and are based on simulations or short-term experimental data. Although resilience to machine learning and side-channel attacks has been demonstrated, the increasing complexity of the threat landscape demands validation against more sophisticated and diverse attack models. Conversely, the results demonstrate that configurable and reliability-aware PUF architectures can be considered a reliable solution for secure key generation and device authentication in hardware security applications; therefore, the MRRO PUF replaces the inverters in the RRO PUF with XOR gates. By controlling one input of each XOR gate, it acts as a configurable inverter, and the MRRO PUF allows more responses to pass through configurable ring oscillators with different signal configurations. The MRRO PUF is implemented on Zynq-7000 and Virtex-6 FPGAs, where the PUFs are built with CLB resources to enhance uniqueness, and the proposed design leverages structural elements as well as routing resources in the FPGA fabric; thus, it has shown good scalability across FPGA platforms. The unit resource cost is reduced by using the same CLBs to generate more responses, and the experimental results show promising uniqueness, uniformity and reliability with a difference of only 0.76%, 0.3% and 0.72%, respectively, between the two FPGA families. Consequently, these properties demonstrate that the MRRO PUF is a promising candidate as a security primitive for chip authentication. The MRROPUF providing robust ML resistance (prediction rates 0.63–0.78 across DT/RF/SVM) and 20–37% error gains over prior configurable RO-PUFs, at 25–40% lower unit resource cost (URC), positioning MRROPUF as a practical choice for lightweight FPGA based secure key generation. Future work will focus on further hardware optimization, evaluating the performance on more FPGA families, and conducting a thorough security analysis against state-of-the-art machine learning attacks.

Discussion and future research directions

Although significant progress has been made in the design of PUFs, as demonstrated in RO-based studies, there are still many open challenges and opportunities for future research. One major limitation of a large number of RO- and CRO-based PUF architectures is their susceptibility to long-term aging effects, such as BTI and hot-carrier degradation. Aging aware modeling and accelerated stress testing should therefore be part of future research to evaluate the long-term stability of PUF responses, especially for applications requiring long device lifetimes. Another major research direction should be the improvement of security against emerging attacks. Although recent CRO-PUF designs show strong resistance against machine learning-based modeling and Differential Power Analysis(DPA), attackers are working on increasingly sophisticated hybrid and adaptive strategies. Therefore, future PUF architectures can leverage dynamic configurability or runtime reconfiguration to continuously change the challenge-response behavior, thereby increasing the unpredictability and resistance against learning-based attacks. From an architectural perspective, hybrid PUF designs that combine delay-based mechanisms with analog entropy sources are a promising research direction. These hybrids can leverage the scalability and simplicity of digital delay-based PUFs while profiting from the high reliability and low bit-flip rates achieved by optimized analog PUFs. In addition, extending the PUF implementations to advanced technology nodes and emerging semiconductor processes can evaluate the scalability and robustness under higher variability. Future research can include the system-level integration of PUFs in secure hardware roots of trust, including tight integration with lightweight error correction codes, secure key management schemes, and cryptographic accelerators, particularly for IoT and edge-computing platforms.
