A replication-defective tick-borne encephalitis virus generated by NS1 trans-complementation confers robust protective efficacy
Authors: Ji Zhang, Qiu-Yan Zhang, Zhe-Rui Zhang, Ya-Nan Zhang, Hong-Qing Zhang, Cheng-Lin Deng, Bo Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTick-borne encephalitis virus (TBEV) poses a severe threat to public health, causing neurological disorders with high morbidity and mortality in endemic regions. Therefore, developing safe and effective strategies for TBEV vaccines has long been a focus of attention. Herein, leveraging our well-established NS1 trans-complementation platform, we successfully constructed a high-titer replication-defective TBEV (TBEV-ΔNS1) using the BHK-21 cell line stably expressing Omsk hemorrhagic fever virus (OHFV) NS1 (designated BHKNS1). Retention of the NS1 deletion in TBEV-ΔNS1 was confirmed by continuous passaging in BHKNS1 cells, as no replicative virus was detected in naive BHK-21 cells. The safety profile of TBEV-ΔNS1 was further validated, as administration of a high dose of TBEV-ΔNS1 to ICR mice did not induce any clinical symptoms. Notably, TBEV-ΔNS1 conferred complete protection against lethal wild-type (WT) TBEV challenge in ICR mice, whereas non-immunized control mice exhibited 100% mortality, significant weight loss, and viremia. This protective efficacy is tightly correlated with robust humoral and cellular immune responses, as demonstrated by the induction of high titers of TBEV-specific IgG, neutralizing antibodies, and IFN-γ-secreting CD8+ T cells following a single immunization. Collectively, our findings provide a proof-of-concept for the NS1 trans-complementation platform in TBEV vaccine development.Keywords: Tick-borne encephalitis virus (TBEV), NS1, Trans-complementation strategy, Vaccine

Introduction

Tick-borne encephalitis (TBE) is a severe infectious disease caused by the tick-borne encephalitis virus (TBEV), which can induce fatal encephalitis and poses a life-threatening risk to humans (Sui et al., 2024). The endemic regions span parts of Europe and Asia, including northern China, and the burden of TBEV infections is increasing (Pustijanac et al., 2023). Currently, TBE is preventable through vaccination (Srinivasan et al., 2025). However, existing inactivated vaccines still have certain limitations, including low neutralizing antibody titers, the requirement for multiple vaccination doses, and incomplete protection (particularly in older individuals) (Hainz et al., 2005; Venturi et al., 2009; Lotrič-Furlan et al., 2017; Pugh et al., 2022; Kulatay et al., 2025). Therefore, the development of novel strategies for TBEV vaccines with high immunogenicity and reliable safety is urgently needed.

TBEV belongs to the genus Orthoflavivirus within the family Flaviviridae, which encompasses numerous other critical human pathogenic viruses, including Omsk hemorrhagic fever virus (OHFV), Zika virus (ZIKV), West Nile virus (WNV), and Japanese encephalitis virus (JEV). The flavivirus genome (approximately 11,000 nucleotides) encodes a polyprotein cleaved into three structural proteins [capsid (C), envelope (E), pre-membrane (prM)] and seven non-structural proteins (NS1, NS2A, NS2B, NS3, NS4A, NS4B, NS5), with conserved processing by host/viral proteases (Li et al., 2019; Hehner et al., 2024). Among flaviviral proteins, NS1 exhibits functional versatility and high interspecies sequence conservation (≥ 75% among TBE serocomplex members). It acts as a major virulence factor in immune evasion and pathogenesis, including the induction of vascular leakage, thrombocytopenia, and complement subversion (Avirutnan et al., 2011; Zhang H. L. et al., 2017; Chao et al., 2019; Biering et al., 2021; Khlusevich et al., 2025). Moreover, NS1 is critical for viral replication complex assembly (Carpio and Barrett, 2021), and its deletion renders the virus replication-defective, a property that has been extensively exploited in the trans-complementation system.

In our previous studies, we successfully employed this platform for vaccine design, generating replication-defective flaviviruses with NS1 deletion that elicited effective immune protection (Li et al., 2019, 2020, 2023). In this system, viral replication is supported in trans by a helper cell line expressing NS1. Moreover, we found that compared with the homologous complementation strategy, the use of heterologous NS1 confers two key advantages. First, it reduces the risk of homologous recombination and subsequent reversion to wild-type virus, as sequence divergence between the helper NS1 and the viral genome lowers the probability of such events. Second, heterologous NS1 maintains functional compatibility across flaviviruses due to high sequence conservation in key functional domains, enabling cross-virus complementation and providing a versatile platform for vaccine development. Notably, within this heterologous framework, complementation efficiency is higher when the helper NS1 and the target virus belong to the same serocomplex (Youn et al., 2013).

Given these benefits, we reasoned that this heterologous trans-complementation strategy could be applied to generate a safe and effective vaccine candidate against TBEV, a tick-borne flavivirus with significant public health burden. Extending this approach from mosquito-borne flaviviruses (e.g., WNV, ZIKV and JEV) to TBEV is particularly relevant given the limitations of current inactivated vaccines. Accordingly, high-titer replication-defective TBEV-ΔNS1 (with NS1 gene deletion) was generated by employing the previously established BHK-21 stable cell line that expresses OHFV NS1 protein (designated BHKNS1) in trans. Specifically, TBEV-ΔNS1 replicated efficiently only in BHKNS1 cell line, while it could not replicate in naive BHK-21 cells. Additionally, through in vitro serial passage in BHKNS1 cells, we confirmed that TBEV-ΔNS1 exhibited no reversion to WT virus, which further demonstrated its safety. Collectively, these key characteristics conferred the highly attenuated phenotype of TBEV-ΔNS1. Notably, in vivo evaluation revealed that a single immunization with the TBEV-ΔNS1 vaccine was sufficient to elicit robust neutralizing antibody responses and could protect ICR mice against TBEV challenge. Our findings demonstrate that TBEV-ΔNS1 holds promise as a safe and effective alternative vaccine candidate for TBEV prevention.

Results

NS1 trans-complementation for high-titer TBEV-ΔNS1

Based on the efficient trans-complementation platform (Li et al., 2019), we examined the feasibility of this system as a strategy for TBEV vaccine development. Using the similar strategy for establishing a replication-defective virus above, a cDNA clone of TBEV with a large in-frame deletion of NS1 (aa 4–298) was first prepared in a background of the full-length cDNA clone of TBEV. The BHKNS1 cell line that stably expressed heterologous OHFV NS1 protein was used to recover TBEV-ΔNS1 (Fig. 1A). This cell line was previously established using a retroviral system by transfecting BHK-21 cells with a plasmid encoding OHFV NS1, together with helper plasmids, followed by puromycin selection (Zhang Q. et al., 2019). Notably, the heterologous NS1 trans-complementation platform has been validated as reliable and highly efficient, and its safety is expected to be enhanced, primarily owing to its capacity to further reduce the possibility of viral reversion to the WT virus (Li et al., 2023).

As expected, the BHKNS1 cell line could rescue the replication defect of TBEV-ΔNS1, as an increasing number of IFA-positive cells (detected using an anti-TBEV NS3 antibody) were observed from 24 to 72 hpt. In contrast, no IFA-positive signals were detected in parental BHK-21 cells, indicating that the replication-defective virus was unable to be generated in the absence of NS1 (Fig. 1B). Subsequently, to evaluate the growth kinetics of the rescued TBEV-ΔNS1, the supernatants harvested from transfected BHKNS1 cells at 72 hpt were titrated and used to infect the BHKNS1 cells at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.1. The results demonstrated that the titer of the rescued TBEV-ΔNS1 exhibited a gradual increase, reaching a peak titer of approximately 108 IU/mL at 72 hpi (Fig. 1C). The results indicated that the defective virus of TBEV-ΔNS1 could be successfully generated and propagated in the cell line expressing heterologous NS1 protein.

Maintenance of NS1 deletion after serial passaging

Furthermore, to rule out the potential risk of generating replication-competent virus via recombination during passaging, we subjected TBEV-ΔNS1 to serial passages (P1−P20) in BHKNS1 cell line and the obtained viruses were then used to infect BHKNS1 and the parental BHK-21 cells, respectively (Fig. 2A). As shown in Fig. 2B, when propagated in BHKNS1 cells, passaged TBEV-ΔNS1 elicited robust IFA-positive signals, whereas it could not replicate in parental BHK-21 cells.

Confirmation of NS1 deletion after serial passaging. A Schematic diagram of the serial passage experimental protocol. The generated TBEV-ΔNS1 (P0) through transfection of BHKNS1 was passaged independently for 20 rounds in BHKNS1 cells and then infected parental BHK-21 cells. The infection status was examined by IFA with anti-NS3 antibody. Scale bar, 400 μm (B). Total RNA was extracted from the TBEV-ΔNS1-infected cell samples (P5, P10, and P20) and subsequently amplified by RT-PCR using specific primers containing the NS1 gene fragment (C). Then the whole genome of P20 was further analyzed by sequencing (D, E).

Besides, we designed a specific primer pair spanning the E to NS3 genomic region, which encompasses the entire NS1 coding sequence. RT-PCR amplification revealed the expected 3.0-kb product in TBEV-WT samples and 2.2-kb product in TBEV-ΔNS1 samples (Fig. 2C). Concurrently, whole-genome sequencing results provided corroborative evidence that the passaged TBEV-ΔNS1 strains retained the designed 295-amino-acid deletion (aa 4–298) in the NS1 protein (Fig. 2D). In addition, a deletion spanning residues 84 to 158 was identified in the variable region of the 3' UTR (Fig. 2E). Notably, deletions in this variable region have been previously documented in serial passage studies and confirmed to exert no detectable impact on viral replication or virulence (Mandl et al., 1998; Hoenninger et al., 2008). Furthermore, two additional amino acid mutations were detected in E (E34G) and NS4A (E84K) (Fig. 2E). The NS4A E84K mutation is more likely an adaptive change associated with the trans-complementation system (Li et al., 2023). On the other hand, the E34G mutation in the E protein is likely a cell culture-adaptive mutation as previously reported (Mandl et al., 2001). This residue is not located within the core antigenic region (domain III) and is thus unlikely to alter viral antigenicity. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that TBEV-ΔNS1 stably maintains the NS1 deletion throughout serial passaging, without recombination or reversion into replication competent wild-type (WT) viruses.

TBEV-ΔNS1 is highly attenuated in vivo

To further evaluate the biosafety of TBEV-ΔNS1 as possible vaccine candidate, we assessed its virulence in vivo. Groups of 4-week-old female ICR mice were inoculated intraperitoneally (i.p.) with 106 IU of WT or 6 × 106/6 × 107 IU of TBEV-ΔNS1. Viremia was detected in the infected mice at 2 dpi, and the animals were subsequently monitored daily for 16 consecutive days to assess clinical signs of disease, survival rate, and body weight changes (Fig. 3A). Obvious clinical manifestations including ruffled fur, lethargy and limb paralysis were observed in the WT virus-infected group, and all mice ultimately died within 10 dpi. By contrast, the mice infected with TBEV-ΔNS1 displayed no clinical symptoms or mortality during the entire observation period (Fig. 3B). In terms of body weight changes, mice infected with WT virus exhibited slight weight fluctuations as early as 2 dpi and developed sustained significant weight loss from 5 dpi onwards. In contrast, the body weight of mice in the TBEV-ΔNS1 infected group showed a similar trend to that in the mock-infected group, with no weight loss observed throughout the entire period (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, the WT virus infected mice showed distinct viremia, whereas no viremia was detected in the sera from mice infected with the higher titers of replication-defective virus (Fig. 3D).

Furthermore, given that TBEV infection primarily induces encephalitis and targets brain as the principal tissue tropism, we evaluated viral loads and pathological alterations in brain tissues at 6 dpi. As shown in Fig. 3E and F, high viral loads were detected in the WT virus challenge group, and histopathological examination via H&E staining revealed vacuolar degeneration in nerve fibers, which displayed disorganized and loose arrangement (indicated by black arrows) (quantitative histopathological score: 2/4). Correspondingly, viral antigens were detected by immunohistochemistry (IHC) (Fig. 3G). In contrast, no viral presence or pathological lesions were observed in TBEV-ΔNS1 or mock infected mice (quantitative histopathological score: 0/4). Collectively, these in vivo results demonstrate that TBEV-ΔNS1 is highly attenuated in mice and sufficiently safe as a vaccine candidate.

TBEV-specific immune responses induced by TBEV-ΔNS1 in ICR mice

To systematically evaluate the potential of the replication-defective TBEV-ΔNS1 as a promising vaccine candidate, we detected TBEV-specific immune responses it induced using the immunocompetent ICR mice. Specifically, 4-week-old female ICR mice were immunized i.p. with a single dose of 6 × 104 / 6 × 105 / 6 × 106 / 6 × 107 IU of TBEV-ΔNS1 (Fig. 4A). After 14 and 28 days post-immunization, ELISA and PRNT were performed to assess the humoral immune responses by quantifying TBEV-specific IgG and neutralizing antibodies, respectively. A strong TBEV-specific IgG response was elicited by TBEV-ΔNS1, with the antibody titers reaching 1:140, 1:1280, 1:12800, and 1:51200 in 6 × 104, 6 × 105, 6 × 106, 6 × 107 IU immunized groups by day 28, respectively (Fig. 4B). Corroborating the IgG titers, neutralizing antibody levels remained low on day 14 but increased significantly by day 28, with all immunized groups developing detectable neutralizing antibodies at titers ranging from 1:14 to 1:76 (Fig. 4C). In addition, to characterize the T helper (Th) 1/2 polarization profile induced by TBEV-ΔNS1, we analyzed IgG isotype ratios. Quantification of specific IgG2a and IgG1 antibody isotypes revealed an IgG2a/IgG1 ratio exceeding 1 (Fig. 4D and E), indicating a Th1-biased immune response. Since IgG antibodies reflect the quantity of humoral responses, whereas IgG avidity represents the quality and functional affinity of these antibodies, we assessed antibody avidity maturation following vaccination. The results revealed a high avidity index (approximately 70%), indicating that TBEV-ΔNS1 effectively induced the production of mature, high-avidity antibodies (Fig. 4F).

Next, T-cell responses were assessed via ELISpot assay at 7 days post-immunization. Splenocytes were harvested from the spleens of immunized mice and subjected to stimulation with peptides of TBEV C and E. Phorbol myristate acetate (PMA) combined with ionomycin (IONO) was used for nonspecific stimulation as a positive control, whereas medium alone was employed as a negative control. On average, approximately 150 IFN-γ-secreting CD8+ T cells per 1 × 106 splenocytes were identified in the TBEV-ΔNS1-immunized group, in contrast to minimal responses observed in the mock group (Fig. 4G). However, no IL-4 production was detected after immunization (Fig. 4H), possibly due to the Th1-biased immune response induced by TBEV-ΔNS1, which is characterized by IFN-γ secretion, a profile consistent with our antibody isotyping results and known to be favorable for antiviral protection. Collectively, these findings indicate that TBEV-ΔNS1, as a vaccine candidate, is capable of eliciting potent humoral and cellular immune responses following a single immunization.

TBEV-ΔNS1 produces complete protection against TBEV challenge

To further explore whether a single vaccination with TBEV-ΔNS1 was sufficient to elicit protective immunity against TBEV, female ICR mice (4-week-old) were first immunized i.p. with 6 × 104/ 6 × 105 / 6 × 106 / 6 × 107 IU of TBEV-ΔNS1. At 30 days post-immunization, these mice were challenged with a high dose of 3 × 107 IU of TBEV-WT via the i.p. route (Fig. 5A). As shown in Fig. 5B, all unimmunized control mice developed overt disease symptoms and succumbed to lethal TBEV-WT challenge, resulting in a 100% mortality rate. In contrast, mice immunized with TBEV-ΔNS1 exhibited dose-dependent protection. Specifically, immunization with 6 × 106 or 6 × 107 IU provided complete protection (100% survival), whereas lower doses of 6 × 104 and 6 × 105 IU conferred partial protection, with survival rates of 40% and 60%, respectively. Furthermore, compared with the TBEV-ΔNS1 immunized groups, unimmunized mice exhibited approximately 20% weight loss and significant viremia after TBEV attack (Fig. 5C, D). In mice immunized with lower doses (6 × 104 and 6 × 105 IU), detectable body weight loss and viremia were observed in a subset of animals, which correlated with the partial protection conferred. In contrast, mice immunized with higher doses achieved complete immune protection, comparable to that of the mock group, with no detectable body weight loss or viremia. In addition, histopathological examination revealed that mice with complete immune protection exhibited no detectable lesions in brain tissues, similar to the mock group, whereas unimmunized control mice developed severe pathological lesions, including extensive neuronal degeneration and shrinkage, characterized by reduced cell size and increased basophilia (blue arrows). The nerve fibers exhibited a disorganized and loose arrangement, accompanied by marked vascular congestion and increased perivascular microglia (black arrows) (quantitative histopathological score: 3/4) (Fig. 5E). In summary, using the trans-complementation platform, we successfully constructed TBEV-ΔNS1, which exhibited high immunogenicity and protective efficacy in murine models, thereby serving as a promising candidate for TBEV vaccine development.

Discussion

The Orthoflavivirus NS1 protein is a multifunctional virulence factor that orchestrates viral replication, pathogenesis, and immune evasion (Chew et al., 2024; Perera et al., 2024), rendering it a prime target for antiviral interventions and vaccine development (Carpio and Barrett, 2021). In our previous studies, the NS1 trans-complementation platform has been successfully utilized for vaccine design against most mosquito-borne flaviviruses (Li et al., 2019, 2020, 2023), which provides a solid theoretical basis for the cross-flavivirus application. Here, we further extended this strategy to the tick-borne flavivirus, and rescued NS1-deficient TBEV (TBEV-ΔNS1) in BHK-21 cells stably expressing OHFV NS1. Subsequent experiments demonstrated that a single-dose immunization with TBEV-ΔNS1 confers complete immune protection against lethal TBEV challenge in mice. This approach represents a feasible strategy for TBEV vaccine development and further suggests the potential for extending the application of this trans-complementation platform across orthoflaviviruses.

At present, the inactivated TBEV vaccines have been licensed for clinical use (Srinivasan et al., 2025). However, they still have certain drawbacks, including safety concerns (Lotrič-Furlan et al., 2017), poor immunogenicity (especially for the elderly) (Hainz et al., 2005; Venturi et al., 2009; Andersson et al., 2010) and the requirement for multiple injections (Loew-Baselli et al., 2011; Pugh et al., 2022). To address these issues, we introduced a replication-defective virus construction platform. Compared with traditional vaccines, our engineered vaccine candidate for TBEV has achieved further improvements, with its advantages primarily reflected in the following aspects.

(I)High safety: Given that TBEV is a biosafety level 3 (BSL-3) pathogen, safety is a primary concern in vaccine development. To address it, we constructed a replication-defective TBEV-ΔNS1 using the NS1 trans-complementation strategy. The improved safety of this vaccine candidate can be attributed to its replication-defective nature, namely that TBEV-ΔNS1 is only capable of viral entry and initial translation but cannot complete the replication cycle in naive cells. Owing to this advantage, even a high-dose challenge (6 × 107 IU) failed to induce any disease symptoms. Moreover, extended passaging (20 passages) of TBEV-ΔNS1 in NS1-complementing cells confirmed the stable maintenance of the NS1 deletion and the strict replication dependence on NS1 complementation. In addition, the large deletion of NS1 (aa 4–298) and the use of heterologous NS1 (OHFV) in trans-complementation system further reduce the possibility of homologous recombination and reversion to wild-type virus.

(II)Potent immunogenicity: The generated TBEV-ΔNS1 induces strong humoral and cellular immune responses. Regarding humoral immunity, although the neutralizing antibody titer (PRNT50) after a single dose appears moderate, with a peak titer of 1:76, it clearly exceeded the seropositivity cutoff for licensed inactivated TBEV vaccines (e.g., NT ≥ 10 after primary immunization) (Berens-Riha et al., 2024). More importantly, inactivated vaccines such as FSME-IMMUN® typically elicit only weak cellular immunity (Kubinski et al., 2023), whereas TBEV-ΔNS1 induces a strong counterpart. Together with humoral immunity, these responses drive rapid viral clearance. Thus, a single immunization with TBEV-ΔNS1 confers complete protection against lethal TBEV challenge in mice.

(III)Simplified production process: Manufacturing traditional inactivated vaccines demands viral amplification in BSL-3 laboratories, followed by rigorous inactivation procedures and validation of inactivation efficacy. These steps elevate the biosafety risks of vaccine production while markedly increasing process complexity. By comparison, owing to the replication defect of TBEV-ΔNS1 in normal cells, the TBEV-ΔNS1 vaccine candidate can be produced exclusively in BSL-2 laboratories. This not only greatly streamlines the production workflow but also holds the potential to reduce production costs.

(IV)Predicted mitigation of NS1-mediated pathogenic effects: The NS1 protein has been identified as the key pathogenic factor of flaviviruses, capable of inducing a series of pathological responses including vascular leakage and thrombocyte aggregation (Perera et al., 2024; Puerta-Guardo et al., 2025). It also serves as a critical regulatory modulator that mediates viral replication (Glasner et al., 2018). Given these properties, the trans-complementation strategy established in this study achieves the dual effect of abolishing viral replication capacity while also eliminating the expression of NS1, a key flavivirus virulence factor. Although functional endpoints of NS1-mediated pathogenesis (e.g., vascular leakage or endothelial activation) were not directly assessed in this study, the large in-frame deletion of NS1 (aa 4–298) from the vaccine genome suggests that this platform may offer an additional safety advantage by avoiding NS1-associated pathogenic effects. Furthermore, the absence of NS1-specific antibodies does not compromise vaccine efficacy, as protection can be achieved through robust immune responses targeting the E protein, which has been widely demonstrated in other vaccine platforms (e.g., MVA-prME and VLP-based vaccines) (Kubinski et al., 2023; Tang et al., 2023).

(V)Potential broad cross-protection: The E protein is the major antigen and is highly conserved among TBEV subtypes (Central European, Siberian, and Far Eastern), with amino acid sequence identities exceeding 94% (Ecker et al., 1999). Moreover, cross-protective immunity has been observed in animals vaccinated with the Central European subtype vaccine (Holzmann et al., 1992; McAuley et al., 2017). Our replication-deficient TBEV-ΔNS1 candidate is derived from the same serotype (Neudoerfl strain) as the licensed FSME-IMMUN® vaccine and retains the intact E protein, which further supports its potential for cross-protection.

Notably, we also found that TBEV-ΔNS1-immunized sera exhibited strong cross-reactive antibody activity against OHFV (data not shown), which is phylogenetically closely related to TBEV within the TBE serocomplex (Chidumayo et al., 2014). The OHFV cross-neutralization assay used in this study employed a replication-defective OHFV-ΔNS1-Gluc reporter virus propagated in NS1-complementing BHKNS1 cells. This assay measures inhibition of reporter virus entry and luciferase expression rather than plaque reduction of authentic OHFV. Therefore, the results reflect cross-reactive antibody activity rather than confirmed neutralization of wild-type OHFV. Although direct confirmation against live OHFV or other TBEV subtypes awaits future studies, the current evidence supports the cross-reactive potential of TBEV-ΔNS1.

Although our findings show great promise, certain limitations remain to be addressed in subsequent studies. First, we only evaluated the intraperitoneal route of immunization. While this route is widely used in preclinical studies, other routes of administration (e.g., subcutaneous or intramuscular) may affect antigen distribution and immune response quality and warrant further exploration. Second, long-term immune memory induced by vaccination is critical for practical application. However, the durability of protection elicited by TBEV-ΔNS1 has not been assessed currently. Third, the immunological evaluation was not comprehensive: cellular immunity was limited to IFN-γ and IL-4 responses without broader epitope coverage or cytokine profiling (e.g., IL-2, TNF-α), and other potential protective mechanisms such as antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC) were not examined. Additionally, due to the scope and experimental constraints of the current study, a direct head-to-head comparison with licensed inactivated TBEV vaccines (FSME-IMMUN®, Encepur®) was not included. Finally, although our serial passaging data have demonstrated that the NS1 deletion of TBEV-ΔNS1 was stably maintained in the NS1-complementing cell system, additional studies, including in vivo passaging, recombination studies, or co-infection experiments with wild-type TBEV will be necessary to further assess its stability under more complex biological conditions. Such studies are warranted and will be pursued in our future work. These limitations define clear directions for future research to comprehensively evaluate the advantages of the TBEV-ΔNS1 vaccine and the replication-deficient platform.

In summary, using the trans-complementation platform, we successfully constructed a replication-deficient TBEV-ΔNS1 vaccine candidate with favorable safety and potent immune efficacy. Our findings demonstrate that the NS1 trans-complementation system holds great potential for the development of tick-borne flavivirus vaccines. The successful application of this platform thus encourages us to further assess the clinical potential of this vaccine candidate, yet we acknowledge that substantial additional work is required.

Conclusions

In this study, we generated a replication-defective TBEV-ΔNS1 using a heterologous NS1 trans-complementation system, extending this effective strategy to tick-borne flaviviruses. Immunization with TBEV-ΔNS1 induces robust neutralizing antibody as well as T-cell responses and protects mice against lethal TBEV challenge. Thus, this replication-deficient virus is a safe and effective TBEV vaccine candidate that warrants further development.

Materials and methods

Animals

Four-week-old female ICR mice, an outbred mouse strain widely used in flavivirus vaccine evaluation (Chang et al., 2000; Arroyo et al., 2004; Baldwin et al., 2021), were obtained from the Animal Center of Wuhan Institute of Virology and were randomly assigned to experimental groups. The experiments were conducted in a single-blind manner (the investigator was blinded to group allocation).

Cells, viruses and antibodies

BHK-21 (Baby Hamster Syrian Kidney, ATCC: CCL 10) cell line was maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Biological Industries, Kibbutz Beit HaEmek, Israel) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) and 1 × penicillin-streptomycin (100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin) (Solarbio, Beijing, China) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The BHK-21 cell line stably expressing NS1 protein (designated BHKNS1), constructed in our previous study (Zhang Q. et al., 2019), was cultured in DMEM supplemented with 0.8 μg/mL puromycin (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) as described above. The WT and mutant viruses were prepared from the infectious cDNA clone of TBEV strain Neudoerfl (European subtype/GenBank: U27495) and stored as aliquots at −80 °C. Polyclonal antibody against TBEV NS3 protein was previously produced by our laboratory, and FITC-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG was purchased from Proteintech (Wuhan, China).

Viral titers quantification via immunofluorescence assay (IFA)

BHKNS1 cells were plated on glass coverslips in 24-well plates and inoculated with serial 10-fold dilutions of TBEV-ΔNS1 for 1 h at 37 °C. Cells were then overlaid with 2% methylcellulose, and at 48 h post-infection (hpi), were washed three times with PBS and fixed in cold 5% acetone in methanol for 15 min at room temperature. After fixation, cells were incubated with anti-TBEV NS3 primary antibody for 1 h, followed by incubation with FITC-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG polyclonal secondary antibody for an additional 1 h. Images were taken by fluorescence microscopy (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). The infectious titer was determined manually as IU/mL, with 1 IU corresponding to one viral focus.

Serial passages of TBEV-ΔNS1 in BHKNS1 cells

The genomic RNA of TBEV-ΔNS1 was transfected into BHKNS1 cells. At 72 h post-transfection (hpt), the supernatants were harvested and designated as the P0 virus for subsequent passages. The P0 virus was then inoculated into BHKNS1 cells cultured in 35 mm dishes. At 72 hpi, the cell supernatants were harvested and designated as the P1 virus. This passage procedure was repeated continuously with 10 μL of supernatants from the preceding passage used to inoculate BHKNS1 cells each time.

Virus growth kinetics detection

BHKNS1 cells were infected with TBEV-ΔNS1 (MOI = 0.1), and cell culture supernatants were collected at indicated time points post-infection. Then viral titers were quantified as described above.

RNA isolation, RT-PCR and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Takara, Dalian, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the obtained RNA was identified by RT-PCR using the PrimeScript One Step RT-PCR Kit (Takara, Dalian, China).

Viral loads in brain tissues were measured by qRT-PCR using One Step TB Green® PrimeScript™ PLUS RT-PCR Kit (Perfect Real Time) (Takara, Dalian, China) on a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Viral loads were expressed as copy numbers per 1 μg total RNA.

Primers used above are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Animal experiments

To confirm the attenuation efficacy of TBEV-ΔNS1, 4-week-old female ICR mice (n = 5 per group) were randomly assigned to experimental groups and injected intraperitoneally (i.p.) with 106 IU of TBEV-WT (Neudoerfl strain/European subtype), 6 × 106/6 × 107 IU of TBEV-ΔNS1, or PBS (negative control) in a final volume of 200 μL. The infected mice were monitored for survival rate, viremia levels, and body weight changes. At 6 dpi, the mouse brain tissues were harvested, fixed, and processed into paraffin-embedded sections, which were then subjected to H&E staining and immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis. Pathological lesions were scored on a 5-point scale: 0 (none), 1 (minimal), 2 (mild), 3 (moderate), and 4 (severe). In addition, brain tissues were collected for viral loads detection by qRT-PCR.

For immunization and subsequent challenge experiments, 4-week-old female ICR mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups and immunized i.p. with 6 × 104/6 × 105/6 × 106/6 × 107 IU of TBEV-ΔNS1 or PBS as a negative control. At 7 days post-immunization, the mice (n = 5 per group) were humanely euthanized, and cellular immune responses were measured by IFN-γ and IL-4 ELISpot kits (Dakewe, Shenzhen, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. At 14 and 28 days post-immunization, total anti-TBEV IgG antibodies titers, avidity maturation and neutralizing antibody titers in mice sera (n = 5 per group) were determined by ELISA and plaque reduction neutralization test (PRNT), respectively, as previously described (Li et al., 2023; Lustig et al., 2025). At 30 days post-immunization, these immunized mice (n = 5 per group) were challenged i.p. with a lethal dose of 3 × 107 IU TBEV-WT (Neudoerfl strain / European subtype). Survival rates and body weight changes were monitored daily for 16 consecutive days post-challenge. Viral titers in peripheral blood (viremia) were quantified at 2 dpi. At 6 dpi, mouse brain tissues were collected, fixed, and then embedded in paraffin. Sections were subsequently stained with hematoxylin and eosin.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS Statistics (Version 24, Armonk, NY, USA). Comparisons between two groups were performed via unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. For comparisons involving three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s test was applied. ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001 and no significance (n.s.).
