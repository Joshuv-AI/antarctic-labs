RIOK2 Promotes Clear Cell Renal Cell Carcinoma Progression Through Cell Cycle Regulation
Authors: Hiroyuki Shikuma, Keisuke Goto, Ayako Shimo, Tomoya Hatayama, Kazuma Yukihiro, Kyosuke Iwane, Ryoken Yamanaka, Ryo Tasaka, Yuki Kohada, Kenshiro Takemoto, Kohei Kobatake, Yohei Sekino, Hiroyuki Kitano, Akihiro Goriki, Keisuke Hieda, Osamu Kaminuma, Nobuyuki Hinata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTClear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) is the most prevalent subtype of kidney cancer and is frequently associated with poor clinical outcomes. Ribosome biogenesis, which is upregulated in many malignancies, plays a key role in tumor proliferation. Right open reading frame kinase 2 (RIOK2) is an atypical kinase essential for 40S ribosomal subunit maturation, but its role in ccRCC remains unexplored. We investigated the clinical and biological significance of RIOK2 in ccRCC. Immunohistochemical analysis of 166 ccRCC specimens revealed that high RIOK2 expression was significantly associated with advanced pathological stage, higher tumor grade, and shorter cancer‐specific and progression‐free survival. Public datasets confirmed the elevated RIOK2 mRNA expression in ccRCC, and its association with poor prognosis. Functional studies demonstrated that RIOK2 knockdown suppressed the proliferation, invasion, and migration of ccRCC cells. RNA sequencing, followed by gene set enrichment analysis, revealed that RIOK2 knockdown led to the downregulation of cell cycle‐related pathways, including E2F targets. Analyses of public datasets further highlighted suppression of the G2/M checkpoint. Flow cytometry revealed G1 cell cycle arrest accompanied by reduced CDK2 protein expression in RIOK2‐knockdown cells. Furthermore, the small‐molecule RIOK2 inhibitor, NSC139021, suppressed ccRCC cell proliferation in a dose‐dependent manner, with reduced efficiency in RIOK2‐knockdown cells. Collectively, these findings identify RIOK2 as a clinically relevant factor associated with aggressive disease behavior and cell‐cycle regulation in ccRCC.Keywords: biomarker, cell cycle checkpoints, renal cell carcinoma, ribosome biogenesis, RIOK2

Introduction

Clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) is the most common type of kidney cancer and is associated with poor prognosis in advanced stages [1, 2]. Even with the latest progress in targeted treatments and immune checkpoint inhibitors [3], therapeutic resistance and tumor progression remain significant challenges. Therefore, the identification of novel molecular targets is urgently needed to improve the clinical outcomes of patients with ccRCC.

Ribosome biogenesis, a fundamental cellular process essential for protein synthesis, is frequently upregulated in malignant tumors and has been implicated in cancer progression [4, 5]. In cancer cells, nucleolar size and number—key indicators of ribosome biogenesis—are frequently increased [6, 7, 8]. This phenomenon is prominent in ccRCC, in which nucleolar alterations are key determinants of tumor grade [9]. Recent studies have suggested a potential link between the ribosome production process and the progression of ccRCC [10, 11], indicating that ribosome‐associated factors may play a critical role in the pathogenesis of ccRCC.

Right open reading frame kinase 2 (RIOK2) is an unusual serine/threonine kinase essential for ribosome biogenesis, as it oversees the ultimate maturation of the pre‐40S ribosomal subunit [12, 13, 14]. RIOK2 is found to be overexpressed in various solid tumors, such as those in the oral cavity, brain, and lungs, and this overexpression is linked to aggressive clinical features and an unfavorable prognosis [15, 16, 17]. Recently, a small‐molecule RIOK2 inhibitor has been developed demonstrating anti‐tumor activity in glioblastoma and prostate cancer [18, 19]. However, the expression patterns and functional significance of RIOK2 in ccRCC have not been systematically investigated.

Given the critical role of RIOK2 in ribosome assembly and its reported association with tumor progression in other cancers, we hypothesized that RIOK2 contributes to the malignant phenotype of ccRCC. In this study, we explored the clinical relevance and biological function of RIOK2 in ccRCC. We analyzed its expression in clinical samples, assessed its association with clinicopathological features, validated its significance using public databases, and investigated its functional role through in vitro knockdown and pharmacological inhibition. Our findings suggest that RIOK2 contributes to the malignant phenotype of ccRCC and may represent a potential therapeutic target.

Materials and Methods

Tissue Samples

A total of 166 primary tumor samples were collected from patients diagnosed with renal cancer who underwent nephrectomy at the Hiroshima University Hospital between 2002 and 2018. Pathological diagnosis was performed based on the 2016 World Health Organization classification. Clinicopathological data, including age, sex, pathological TNM stage, tumor grade, and blood biochemical parameters, were retrospectively collected from the medical records. Tumor staging was performed according to the 2010 American Joint Committee on Cancer TNM Staging System. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Good Clinical Practice guidelines. All experimental procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Hiroshima University Hospital (approval no. E‐588‐2). Informed consent was obtained from all patients either through a written document or an opt‐out approach.

Immunohistochemical Staining

Immunohistochemical staining was performed on tumor sections obtained through nephrectomy, as described previously [20]. Briefly, the sections were incubated with anti‐RIOK2 (1:200; HPA005681; Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. After washing with PBS containing 0.05% Tween‐20, the sections were incubated with anti‐rabbit HRP polymer (Code No. 458; Medical & Biological Laboratories Co. Ltd., Minato‐ku, Tokyo, Japan) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with 3,3′‐diaminobenzidine substrate buffer (W1018; Promega Corporation, Madison, WI, USA) for 1 min. After a blinded examination by two independent uropathologists, specimens in which ≥ 50% of tumor cells exhibited cytoplasmic staining were determined to be positive for RIOK2, whereas specimens with staining in < 50% of tumor cells were considered negative.

Public Database Analysis

Patient data and gene expression datasets from the GSE40435 and Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) cohorts were obtained from cBioPortal (https://www.cbioportal.org/) and Gene Expression Omnibus (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Preprocessed RIOK2 expression data for non‐neoplastic kidney and renal cell carcinoma (RCC) tissues were normalized using variance stabilizing transformation and quantile normalization, implemented with the lumi package (v2.6.0). For patients with ccRCC in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) Firehose Legacy dataset, diploid samples were stratified into high and low RIOK2 expression groups based on the median of mRNA expression z‐scores (RNA‐Seq V2 RSEM), and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted following a previously reported method [21]. GSEA software (version 4.3.3) was downloaded from the Broad Institute website (http://software.broadinstitute.org/gsea/downloads.jsp).

Cell Culture

Human RCC cell lines 786‐O (RRID: CVCL_1051), 769‐P (RRID: CVCL_1056), Caki‐1 (RRID: CVCL_0234), Caki‐2 (RRID: CVCL_0233), A704 (RRID: CVCL_1052), and ACHN (RRID: CVCL_1067) were purchased from the Japanese Collection of Research Bioresources (JCRB) Cell Bank. All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat profiling by the provider prior to distribution. Mycoplasma contamination was regularly monitored in our laboratory and confirmed to be negative. Cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 in RPMI‐1640 medium (Sigma‐Aldrich) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and 1% penicillin‐streptomycin (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan).

Transfection and RNA Interference

To knock down RIOK2 expression in 786‐O and Caki‐1 cells, we used siRNA‐based RNA interference technology. Cells were independently transfected with two different siRNAs against human RIOK2 (siRIOK2‐1 [SI03649940], target sequence: 5′‐ATGAAACGTTTCAGCTACGAA‐3′; siRIOK2‐2 [SI03649933], target sequence: 5′‐TAGGAAGAACCTCGTTTCGAA‐3′; QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) or control siRNA (siCtrl; Silencer, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at a final concentration of 10 nM using the Lipofectamine RNAiMAX reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The cells were harvested at 48 h for RNA extraction and at 72 h for protein extraction.

Quantitative RT‐PCR

Total RNA was isolated using NucleoSpin RNA (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan), and 500 ng of total RNA was reverse‐transcribed to cDNA using PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara). Quantitative PCR analysis was performed on the cDNA with SYBR Select Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) using the Step One Plus Real‐time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) as described previously [22]. The cycle threshold values were normalized to the expression of the endogenous housekeeping gene, HPRT, and 2(−ΔΔCt) values were calculated for relative quantification. The reactions were performed in triplicate using the quantitative PCR primers listed in Table S1.

Western Blotting

Western blotting was conducted following previously established protocols [23]. Briefly, the protein samples were electrophoresed on 5%–20% precast polyacrylamide gels (SuperSep Ace; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation) and transferred to nitrocellulose blotting membranes (GE Healthcare Life Sciences, Chicago, IL, USA) via electroblotting. The membranes were blocked for 30 min with 5% nonfat dry milk in Tris‐buffered saline with Tween‐20 (10 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, and 0.05% Tween‐20) and incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight. The primary antibodies used in these assays were as follows: anti‐human RIOK2 (1:500, HPA005681; Sigma Aldrich), anti‐p21 (1:1000, #2947; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), anti‐CDK2 (1:1000, #18048; Cell Signaling), anti‐E‐Cadherin (1:1000, #14472; Cell Signaling), anti‐N‐Cadherin (1:2000, #22018‐1‐AP; Proteintech), anti‐Vimentin (1:1000, #5741; Cell Signaling), and anti‐β‐actin (1:5000, #A2228; Sigma‐Aldrich).

Cell Proliferation Assay

Cells (786‐O: 1.0 × 103 cells/well; Caki‐1: 3.0 × 103 cells/well) were incubated in a 96‐well cell culture plate for 24 h. Cell proliferation was assessed using the Premix WST‐1 Cell Proliferation Assay System (Takara) and expressed as an absorbance value measured using a microplate reader at 450 nm. For each measurement, wells with untreated cells and media without cells were used as controls and blanks, respectively.

Transwell Invasion Assay

For the Transwell invasion assay, 24‐well ThinCert inserts with 8.0 μm pores (Greiner Bio‐One, Frickenhausen, Germany) were used. Cells suspended in serum‐free medium were seeded in the top chamber after adding serum‐containing medium (10% FBS) to the bottom chamber. After 24 h, noninvaded cells were removed from the upper surface; the membranes were then fixed, stained with Diff‐Quik, and the invaded cells on the lower surface were counted.

Wound Healing Assay

We performed a wound healing assay to detect cell migration. Cells were seeded in a culture insert (ibidi culture‐insert 2 well; ibidi GmbH, Gräfelfing, Germany) at a density of 4.0 × 105 cells/mL. After allowing the cells to adhere overnight, the culture insert was removed and the cells were washed with PBS to remove non‐adherent cells. The cells were then cultured in fresh medium for 6 h. After imaging the plate at the start and end of the culture period, the number of cells that migrated into the wound space was manually counted in three fields per well under a light microscope at ×50 magnification. Cell migration areas were quantified using ImageJ version 1.54p (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA; https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/).

RNA Sequencing (RNA‐Seq) and GSEA

Total RNA was extracted from siRIOK2‐2‐ or siCtrl‐introduced 786‐O and Caki‐1 cells using the NucleoSpin RNA Kit (Takara). RNA‐seq and primary data analysis were outsourced to Macrogen Inc. (Japan). Library preparation was performed using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA LT Sample Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), and sequencing was conducted on a NovaSeq X Plus platform (Illumina) with 101‐bp paired‐end reads. Raw sequencing reads were quality‐trimmed using Trimmomatic and aligned to the GRCh38 reference genome using HISAT2 (v2.1.0). Transcript assembly and quantification of expression levels (fragments per kilobase of transcript per million fragments mapped and transcripts per million) were conducted using StringTie (v2.1.3b). Differential gene expression analysis was performed using edgeR. GSEA was independently performed by the authors using the MSigDB Hallmark gene sets, based on the expression profiles and following a previously described protocol [24].

Cell Cycle Assay

Cell cycle progression was assessed using Cell Cycle Assay Solution Blue (C549, Dojindo Molecular Technologies, Kumamoto, Japan), according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, the treated cells were harvested, resuspended at 5 × 105 cells/mL, washed with PBS, and incubated with 5 μL of assay solution for 15 min at 37°C. DNA content was measured based on the staining intensity using a BD LSRFortessa X‐20 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) to determine the cell cycle phase distribution.

RIOK2 Inhibitor

NSC139021 (CAS: 1147‐56‐4; Tokyo Chemical Industry Co. Ltd. [TCI], Tokyo, Japan) was dissolved in DMSO (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation) to prepare a 10 mM stock solution. The stock was diluted to the designated concentrations using the culture medium immediately before use. An equivalent volume of DMSO was used as the vehicle control in all experiments.

Statistical Analysis

All in vitro experiments were performed in at least three independent replicates, and the data are reported as means ± standard error of the mean. The two groups were compared using Student's t‐test. For comparison among multiple groups, one‐way analysis of variance, followed by Dunnett's or Tukey's multiple comparison tests, was applied. Survival analysis was performed using the log‐rank test. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with progression‐free survival (PFS). IC50 values were calculated and graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA; RRID: SCR_002798), version 10.4.2. Statistical analyses were performed using JMP version 18.0.1 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA; RRID: SCR_008567). A p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Normalized enrichment scores, normalized p‐values, nominal p‐values, and false discovery rate‐adjusted p‐values were calculated.

Results

RIOK2 Expression Is Associated With Aggressive Clinicopathological Features and Unfavorable Outcomes in ccRCC

To investigate the relationship between RIOK2 expression and the clinicopathological features of ccRCC, we performed immunohistochemical analysis of tumor specimens from patients who underwent nephrectomy. In nonneoplastic kidney tissue, RIOK2 expression was localized to the proximal tubules, whereas ccRCC tissue exhibited stronger and more widespread RIOK2 staining than adjacent normal parenchyma (Figure 1A). In ccRCC tumor regions, RIOK2 expression was predominantly cytoplasmic. Among the 166 samples analyzed, 87 (52.4%) showed positive RIOK2 staining and 79 (47.6%) were negative (Figure 1B). Comparative analysis revealed that RIOK2‐positive samples were significantly associated with advanced pathological T stage, higher tumor grade, presence of vascular invasion, and greater incidence of metastasis compared to RIOK2‐negative samples (Table 1). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed that patients with RIOK2‐positive tumors had significantly worse cancer‐specific survival and PFS than those with RIOK2‐negative tumors (p = 0.001 and p = 0.04, respectively; Figure 1C). Although RIOK2 positivity was associated with poorer outcomes in Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, it was not identified as an independent predictor of PFS in multivariable Cox regression analysis (Hazard ratio 0.86, p = 0.63) (Table S1). To validate these findings, publicly available databases from the GSE40435 cohort were analyzed to compare RIOK2 expression between non‐neoplastic kidney and RCC tissues in patients with ccRCC. Consistent with the immunohistochemical findings, RIOK2 mRNA expression levels were significantly higher in tumor tissues than in normal kidney tissues (Figure 1D). Moreover, analysis of the CPTAC cohort revealed that patients with ccRCC and high RIOK2 expression exhibited significantly poorer overall survival (p = 0.016; Figure 1E). These findings suggest that elevated RIOK2 expression is associated with adverse clinicopathological characteristics and unfavorable clinical outcomes in patients with ccRCC.

RIOK2 Promotes Cell Proliferation, Invasion, and Migration In Vitro

To investigate the biological role of RIOK2 in RCC, we performed functional analyses using RCC cell lines. First, we performed western blot analysis to evaluate RIOK2 expression levels in several RCC cell lines (Figure 2A). Among these, 786‐O and Caki‐1 cells exhibited relatively high RIOK2 expression and were selected for subsequent experiments. RIOK2 knockdown was achieved using two independent siRNAs, and the knockdown efficiency was confirmed using quantitative RT‐PCR and western blotting (Figure 2B). To investigate the contribution of RIOK2 to cell proliferation, WST‐1 assays were performed every 24 h for up to 96 h after siRNA transfection. RIOK2 knockdown significantly suppressed cell proliferation in both 786‐O and Caki‐1 cells compared to that in control siRNA‐transfected cells (Figure 2C). To further explore the role of RIOK2, we conducted cell invasion and migration assays. RIOK2 knockdown significantly inhibited cell invasion (Figure 2D) and migration (Figure 2E), suggesting that RIOK2 is essential for the proliferation, invasion, and migration of RCC cells in vitro. To investigate whether the reduced migratory and invasive capacities induced by RIOK2 knockdown were associated with epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), we evaluated representative EMT‐related proteins, including E‐cadherin, N‐cadherin, and vimentin, by western blot analysis. However, no consistent alterations were observed in the expression of these canonical EMT markers following RIOK2 knockdown in 786‐O and Caki‐1 cells (Figure S1).

RIOK2 Upregulates Cell Cycle‐Related Pathways

To investigate the molecular pathways regulated by RIOK2 in ccRCC, we performed RNA‐seq on RIOK2‐knockdown 786‐O and Caki‐1 cells. Differentially expressed genes were visualized using volcano plots, which revealed distinct expression profiles between the control and RIOK2‐knockdown cells (Figure 3A, Table S2–, S5). GSEA was conducted using RNA‐seq data. Among the top 10 downregulated hallmark gene sets (Figure 3B), cell cycle‐related pathways, such as those related to E2F targets, were prominently enriched in control cells compared to RIOK2‐knockdown cells (Figure 3C). This suggests that RIOK2 positively regulates the genes involved in cell cycle progression. To validate these findings in clinical samples, GSEA was performed using the TCGA‐Kidney Renal Clear Cell Carcinoma dataset. Consistently, higher RIOK2 expression in tumors was significantly associated with the enrichment of E2F target and G2/M checkpoint pathways (Figure 3D). These results suggest that RIOK2 facilitates tumor growth in ccRCC by modulating key cell cycle‐related pathways.

RIOK2 Knockdown Induces G1 Cell Cycle Arrest in ccRCC Cells

Given the association between RIOK2 and cell cycle‐related pathways identified by RNA‐seq and GSEA, we next investigated the effects of RIOK2 knockdown on cell cycle progression in ccRCC cells using flow cytometry. In both 786‐O and Caki‐1 cells, RIOK2 knockdown led to a significant increase in the proportion of cells in the G1 phase and a corresponding decrease in the S phase, compared to the control siRNA‐transfected cells (Figures 4A,B). Western blot analysis demonstrated reduced CDK2 expression following RIOK2 knockdown. p21 expression was increased in RIOK2‐knockdown Caki‐1 cells, but this upregulation was not consistently observed in 786‐O cells (Figure 4C). These findings indicate that RIOK2 knockdown induces G1 cell cycle arrest accompanied by reduced CDK2 expression, supporting a role for RIOK2 in G1/S cell cycle progression.

Pharmacological Inhibition of RIOK2 Suppresses ccRCC Cell Proliferation In Vitro

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of targeting RIOK2 in ccRCC, we used a previously reported small‐molecule RIOK2 inhibitor (Figure 5A; 1‐[2‐(2‐thiazolyl)diazenyl]‐2‐naphthalenol, NSC139021). NSC139021 inhibited the proliferation of ccRCC cell lines in a dose‐dependent manner, with IC50 values of 0.9–1.2 μM (Figure 5B). The inhibitory effect of NSC139021 was diminished in RIOK2‐knockdown cells (Figure 5C), suggesting that its action is at least partly dependent on RIOK2. In addition, NSC139021 treatment increased p21 expression in both cell lines and reduced CDK2 expression in 786‐O cells, with a modest decrease also observed in Caki‐1 cells, consistent with the cell‐cycle regulatory effects observed following RIOK2 knockdown (Figure 5D). These findings suggest that pharmacological inhibition of RIOK2 suppresses ccRCC cell proliferation and alters the expression of cell cycle‐associated proteins.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that RIOK2 expression is associated with adverse clinicopathological features and poorer clinical outcomes in patients with ccRCC, based on immunohistochemical and validation analyses using public databases. These findings are supported by previous reports showing that elevated RIOK2 expression is linked to unfavorable outcomes in various solid tumors, including oral cancer, brain tumors, and lung cancer [15, 16, 17, 19], and in public omics datasets [25]. Ribosome biogenesis is upregulated in cancer cells [4, 26] and further enhanced in high‐grade malignancies [27]. Given the role of RIOK2 in ribosome biogenesis, its upregulation may contribute to the elevated ribosomal output required to sustain rapid proliferation in aggressive tumors. This biological function may partly explain the observed association between high RIOK2 expression and adverse clinicopathological features in ccRCC, supporting its potential relevance as a marker of tumor aggressiveness.

The functional significance of RIOK2 in ccRCC progression was evidenced by the reduced proliferation and cell cycle arrest observed in RIOK2‐knockdown ccRCC cells. These findings align with previous studies demonstrating the role of RIOK2 in promoting proliferation across various cancer cell types [15, 16, 17, 19]. Furthermore, RNA‐seq and database analyses revealed a positive correlation between RIOK2 expression and cell cycle‐related pathways, including E2F targets and the G2/M checkpoint. E2F transcription factors are known to bind to the CDK2 promoter and upregulate its transcription [28, 29], consistent with the observed decrease in CDK2 expression following RIOK2 knockdown. CDK2, activated through its association with cyclins A and E, plays a critical role in regulating the G1/S phase transition [30, 31]. Together with the suppression of E2F target pathways identified by GSEA, the reduction of CDK2 expression and induction of G1 arrest following RIOK2 knockdown suggest that RIOK2 contributes to cell‐cycle progression in ccRCC.

We observed notable changes in the expression of p21, a key cell cycle regulator. p21 binds to cyclin‐CDK complexes, inhibits their kinase activity, and thereby induces G1/S phase arrest [32]. This mechanism is consistent with the increased p21 expression observed in RIOK2‐knockdown Caki‐1 cells, but not in 786‐O cells. One possible explanation for this discrepancy is the difference in p53 status between these cell lines, as Caki‐1 cells reportedly harbor wild‐type p53 whereas 786‐O cells carry mutant p53 [33, 34]. Ribosomal stress is known to stabilize p53 through inhibition of MDM2 by ribosomal proteins, leading to transcriptional activation of p21 [35, 36]. Therefore, p53‐dependent signaling may contribute to p21 induction following RIOK2 knockdown in Caki‐1 cells. The absence of p21 upregulation in 786‐O cells suggests that alternative, p21‐independent mechanisms may also contribute to the growth suppression and G1 arrest induced by RIOK2 depletion. Further studies are required to clarify the involvement of the p53–p21 axis in this process.

RIOK2 regulates proliferation, which represents cell cycle progression, migration, and invasion of ccRCC cells. These cellular processes are controlled by distinct regulatory mechanisms. Currently, no direct evidence exists linking RIOK2 to cell migration and invasion. However, Song et al. reported that RIOK2 knockdown suppressed migration and invasion in glioma cells, which was accompanied by the downregulation of matrix metalloproteinases and mesenchymal markers [16]. In the present study, transcriptomic analyses primarily identified alterations in cell cycle‐related pathways, and evaluation of representative EMT‐related markers did not reveal consistent changes following RIOK2 knockdown. These findings suggest that the reduced migratory and invasive phenotypes observed in RIOK2‐depleted ccRCC cells may be mediated through mechanisms other than canonical EMT programs, although the underlying mechanism remains unclear.

The therapeutic potential of RIOK2‐targeted intervention in ccRCC was further investigated using the small‐molecule compound NSC139021, which directly binds to RIOK2 and induces a ribosomal stress signature [18]. Consistent with previous reports demonstrating anti‐tumor activity in prostate cancer and glioblastoma [18, 19], NSC139021 significantly suppressed the proliferation of ccRCC cells. In addition, NSC139021 treatment decreased CDK2 expression and increased p21 expression, consistent with the changes observed following RIOK2 knockdown. Although Yu et al. reported that this compound modulates the Skp2‐p27/p21‐cyclin E/CDK2‐pRb signaling pathway independently of RIOK2 inhibition, these effects were primarily observed at concentrations exceeding 10 μM [19]. Given the compound's IC50 of approximately 1 μM for suppressing cell proliferation and the reduced efficacy following RIOK2 knockdown, it is likely that NSC139021 exerts its effects on ccRCC cells through a RIOK2‐dependent mechanism.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that RIOK2 contributes to the malignant phenotype of ccRCC by promoting cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, potentially through the regulation of cell cycle‐related pathways. This association warrants further validation through prospective studies involving larger cohorts. Although several chemotherapeutic agents targeting ribosome biogenesis or protein synthesis via RIOK2‐independent mechanisms have been developed, their clinical application has been hindered principally by substantial systemic toxicity [37, 38, 39]. Future studies investigating the role of RIOK2 in ribosome biogenesis and evaluating the therapeutic efficacy of RIOK2 inhibition in vivo will further clarify its biological and therapeutic significance in ccRCC.
