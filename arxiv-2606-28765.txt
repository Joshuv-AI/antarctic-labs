Low cosmic-ray ionisation at parsec scales in G035.39-00.33
A. Socci, A. Barnes, J. Pineda, O. Sipilä, C. Gieser, P. Caselli, A. Hacar, G. Cosentino, C. Y. Law, M. T. Owen, P. Gorai, F. Fontani, I. Jiménez-Serra, J. C. Tan

Abstract
Context. Cosmic rays (CRs) play a key role in the interstellar medium (ISM) by regulating the chemical evolution of the gas and its coupling to the magnetic field in the densest and coldest regions of molecular clouds. However, the CR ionisation rate of H 2 ( ζ 2 \zeta_{2} ) is one of the most debated parameters characterising molecular clouds due to the uncertainties in its estimation. Aims. We aim to derive a new and reliable indirect analytical method to probe the electron fraction, x ⁡ ( e ) x(e) , and ζ 2 \zeta_{2} of the gas across multiple density regimes. We further apply this novel framework to a parsec-scale filament to homogeneously map a significant sample of fields with different physical conditions and test their impact on x ⁡ ( e ) x(e) and ζ 2 \zeta_{2} . Methods. Recent estimates of x ⁡ ( e ) x(e) and ζ 2 \zeta_{2} suffer from limitations, mostly driven by observational restrictions. We thus developed a new analytical framework based on the chemistry of N 2 H + , N 2 D + and DCO + . We mapped the emission from their ground transition towards the parsec-scale filament of the infrared dark cloud (IRDC) G035.39-00.33 with new observations from the NOrthern Extended Array (NOEMA) at a resolution of 3 ′′ 3^{\prime\prime} (or ∼ 9000 \sim 9000 au at the cloud distance). By combining this novel survey with ancillary observations of C 18 O ( 1 − 0 1-0 ), we determined the CO depletion factor, the N 2 H + deuterium fraction and, ultimately, x ⁡ ( e ) x(e) and ζ 2 \zeta_{2} in G035.39-00.33. Results. The CO depletion is significant and widespread in G035.39-00.33. Its depletion factor, f D f_{\mathrm{D}} , shows a positive correlation with column and number densities of H 2 in the cloud, with the highest intensities in both N 2 H + and N 2 D + , and with the sites displaying an enhanced deuterium fraction ( R D R_{\mathrm{D}} ). The electron fraction varies by two orders of magnitude within ∼ 10 − 9 − 10 − 7 \sim 10^{-9}-10^{-7} and shows a functional dependence on the number density of H 2 in the region. This correlation is consistent with that determined in low-mass cores with similar degrees of CO depletion, but now extended to parsec scales, and with the predictions from chemical models. ζ 2 \zeta_{2} varies by three orders of magnitude within ∼ 10 − 18 − 10 − 15 \sim 10^{-18}-10^{-15} s -1 , and most fields show values of ∼ 2.3 × 10 − 18 \sim 2.3\times 10^{-18} s -1 on average. ζ 2 \zeta_{2} shows a functional dependence on N ⁡ ( H 2 ) N(\mathrm{H_{2}}) , as predicted by theoretical models, but its scatter and its values, on average lower than the typical ζ 2 \zeta_{2} for the ISM, suggest the presence of local enhancement and overall attenuation of the CR flux taking place in G035.39-00.33. Conclusions. f D f_{\mathrm{D}} and R D R_{\mathrm{D}} show values consistent with theoretical predictions and previous independent studies. On the other hand, x ⁡ ( e ) x(e) and ζ 2 \zeta_{2} show values at the lower end compared to independent estimates in filaments and cores. The values of ζ 2 \zeta_{2} are consistent with those reported for other IRDCs and giant filaments, but lower compared to theoretical predictions for the column density regime sampled by G035.39-00.33. CR attenuation can come from the change in magnetic field strength and morphology previously reported in G035.39-00.33, which explains the reduced values of x ⁡ ( e ) x(e) and ζ 2 \zeta_{2} . Fields with ionisation rates of ζ 2 ≳ 10 − 16 \zeta_{2}\gtrsim 10^{-16} s -1 may be affected by the local acceleration of CRs or by the magnetic pockets formed from the uneven magnetic field instead.

Introduction

Cosmic rays (CRs) are charged accelerated particles that play a key role in the evolution of the interstellar medium (ISM)
(
102
)
. At densities
n\gtrsim 10^{3}
cm
-3
, low-energy CRs (
E<10
GeV) mostly interact with H

, causing its ionisation at a rate
\zeta_{2}

{}_{2}^{+}
rapidly forms H
{}_{3}^{+}

(
59

(
60

. Recombination of these ions with electrons and neutrals is the main source of heating in molecular clouds and filaments
(
52
)
, thus setting the thermal pressure that confines the dense cores embedded within them
(
54

. The balance between ions and neutrals, usually expressed via the electron fraction
x(e)=n(e)/n
(with
n(e)
and

(
82

with the resulting magnetic pressure potentially slowing the gravitational collapse of individual cores
(
128

\zeta_{2}
.

Measuring
\zeta_{2}
is however complex. Its most direct probe is the abundance of H

(
93

(
94

lacks rotational transitions in the millimetre regime (mm), where emission from the cold gas of molecular clouds peaks instead. The elusiveness of H

in these regions has so far prevented direct and accurate measurements of
\zeta_{2}
towards the sites of star formation.

Proxies were therefore used over the years to determine
\zeta_{2}
in the ISM. Early studies using radicals
(

(

\sim 10^{-17}

(

(

Recently, two methods proved reliable in mapping

Despite connecting the large dynamic range in

C
18

\tau\sim 1

T_{\mathrm{B}}=(J_{\mathrm{ex}}-J_{\mathrm{bg}})(1-e^{-\tau}),

(11)

where

\sim 7.5

\sim 10^{3}

T_{\mathrm{ex}}

T_{\mathrm{ex}}=T_{\mathrm{K}}\bigg[1+\frac{k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{\mathrm{K}}}{h\nu}\mathrm{ln}(1+A_{10}/C_{10})\bigg]^{-1},

(12)

where
h
is the Planck constant,
A_{10}
,
C_{10}=c_{10}n(\mathrm{H_{2}})
are the Einstein coefficient for spontaneous emission and the collisional rate for the ground transition, respectively. The latter depends on the collisional coefficient (
c_{10}
, taken together with

, from the LAMDA database
2
2

2

https://home.strw.leidenuniv.nl/~moldata/

The excitation temperature determined through Eq.

is close to thermalisation with values within
\sim 7-20

\sim 12

\sim 1

\sim 0.3

\sim 0.3

\sim 14^{\prime\prime}

\tau\sim 0.3

\sim 15\%

\sim 3.5-6

5.3
Large-scale CO depletion at high resolution

The freeze-out of CO onto dust grain surfaces is the main cause of its disappearance from the gas phase. The degree to which this effect influences its observed abundance is estimated with the depletion factor
f_{\mathrm{D}}
. The latter can be evaluated as the ratio between the expected abundance of CO (
x_{0}
) and its observed abundance (
x_{\mathrm{obs}}

f_{\mathrm{D}}=x_{0}/x_{\mathrm{obs}}
. We determined
x_{0}
via the Galactic scaling relation for C

O
(
49

x_{0}=\frac{9.5\times 10^{-5}\times 10^{\alpha(D_{\mathrm{GC}}-D_{\mathrm{GC,\odot}})}}{{}^{16}\mathrm{O}/^{18}\mathrm{O}},

(13)

where
D_{\mathrm{GC,\odot}}=8.15\pm 0.15
kpc is the Galactocentric distance of the Sun
(
119
)
,
\alpha=-0.077\pm 0.019
dex kpc
-1
is the scaling of the C/H Galactic gradient
(
83
)
,
{}^{16}\mathrm{O}/^{18}\mathrm{O}=(58.8\pm 11.8)D_{\mathrm{GC}}+(37.1\pm 82.6)
is the O isotopic ratio across the Milky Way
(
163
)
. This scaling and Eq.
13
were calculated taking
D_{\mathrm{GC}}=6.27
kpc, Galactocentric distance of Cloud H
(see
113
, for a discussion)
. We determined
x_{\mathrm{obs}}
taking the ratio of
N(\mathrm{C^{18}O})
, estimated as discussed in Sect.
5.2
, over
N(\mathrm{H_{2}})
(Fig.
9
, left panel).

The map of CO depletion factors shows values

in the majority of Cloud H (see Fig.

, panel a). This widespread depletion, already reported by

\sim 7

(

In the last section, we obtained all the parameters required to determine

in Cloud H. Now, we will discuss their estimated values as follows: first, we will present the typical values retrieved in Cloud H and their corresponding degree of accuracy (Sect.
6.1

x(e)
(Sect.
6.2

\zeta_{2}
(Sect.
6.3

(Sect.
6.4

with

in comparison with the theoretical predictions for the propagation of CRs in the cloud (Sect.

6.1
Analytical estimates and error budget

Equations
3
and
8
allow us to estimate
x(e)
and
\zeta_{2}
with a nominal accuracy of a factor of 3 associated to the method. Many of the parameters embedded in this calculation, however, bear uncertainties: the flux calibration in our observations (Sect.
4.1
); the systematics in the methods to derive
N(\mathrm{H_{2}})
and
n(\mathrm{H_{2}})
(Sect.
4.2
); the errors in Galactocentric distance of the Sun and in the Galactic gradients (Sect.
5.3
). The propagation of these uncertainties can easily grow above the measured values for
x(e)
and
\zeta_{2}
. Given that both quantities are definite positive (i.e. with 0 as a lower bound), this propagation is not reasonable nor accurate as it assumed an underlying Gaussian distribution for the two parameters. We therefore assume
x(e)
and
\zeta_{2}
to follow a log-normal distribution, propagate their errors accordingly and report their median values and prediction intervals.

We determined the median and first prediction interval for the full sample of
x(e)
and
\zeta_{2}
measured in Cloud H as
\langle a\rangle=e^{\mu\pm\sigma}
, where
\mu
and
\sigma
are the mean and standard deviation, respectively, of the logarithms for the two parameters. Their values correspond to
\langle x(e)\rangle=1.0^{+2.2}_{-0.7}\times 10^{-8}
and
\langle\zeta_{2}\rangle=2.3^{+5.5}_{-1.6}\times 10^{-18}
. Both prediction intervals range around a factor of 3 around their median, thus demonstrating that our data allows us to retrieve average properties for
x(e)
and
\zeta_{2}
with an accuracy consistent with that of the method. Beyond the typical values in Cloud H, we are also interested in studying the local variations of electron fraction and ionisation rate across the filament (Sects.
6.4
and
6.5
). We therefore propagated the uncertainties in Eqs.
3
and
8
as sum of Gaussian errors and took them as a pixel-by-pixel deviation (
\sigma
) around each estimate. These errors vary across Cloud H, but their typical magnitude corresponds to a factor of
\sim 2
for
x(e)
and a factor of
\sim 9
for
\zeta_{2}
.

Our estimates return values mostly consistent with the uncertainty associated with the analytical method. Local values can, however, exceed this error, especially in the case of
\zeta_{2}
. This increase in uncertainty is not surprising given the higher number of independent parameters that contribute to the estimate of
\zeta_{2}
, and to its error budget, compared to that of
x(e)
(see Eqs.
3
and
8
). These high errors, however, do not detract from the quality of the method (if anything, they prompt less uncertain estimates of the parameters fed into
\zeta_{2}
) and will have only a marginal impact on the conclusions of our work (Sects.
6.5
and
7
).

Low electron fractions across the filament

The electron fraction,

Cosmic-ray ionisation rate in Cloud H

An ionisation rate of

The same wide range of ionisation conditions applies within Cloud H. The CRIR shows values of

around its median of

6.4
Density dependence of the electron fraction

Cosmic rays dominate the ionisation of the gas in clouds with densities above a few visual extinctions
(
63
)
. Therefore, the value of the electron fraction is directly driven by
\zeta_{2}
in this gas regime, which is also the framework for this study. From this premise and a few assumptions,
82
demonstrated that the electron fraction and the gas density can be linked via a simple analytical relation:

x(e)\sim 1.3\times 10^{-5}n(\mathrm{H_{2}})^{-0.5}.

(14)

Later studies based on chemical and physical models
(
8
;
23
;
24
)
demonstrated that additional effects, such as the freeze-out of metals, alter both the pre-factor and the scaling of Eq.
14
. Nevertheless, all relations agree on a net decrease of
x(e)
for an increase in
n(\mathrm{H_{2}})
due to the progressive recombination of ions.

This anti-correlation between
x(e)
and
n(\mathrm{H_{2}})
applies in Cloud H as well. We explored this dependence through the KDE distribution of the two parameters taken from Fig.
4
, panels (a) and (c). Figure
6
shows the KDE distribution along with the scaling relations for
x(e)
from
82
,
30
and
24
. The electron fractions throughout Cloud H show a clear dependence on the number density. When compared to the scaling relations, patterns start to emerge: the relations from
82
and
30
are consistent only with ionisation fractions above
\gtrsim 5\times 10^{-8}
for the density regime of Cloud H. These ionisation fractions correspond to those sub-regions in the filament potentially affected by local sources of radiation (see Sect.
6.2
), thus suggesting the two relations to be more suited for active regions in the cloud. The peak of the distribution is instead consistent, within errors, with the relation from
24
. The latter was determined in the pre-stellar core L1544 by modelling the emission from several ions and for a range of depletion factors within
f_{\mathrm{D}}\sim 3-9
. Our results in Cloud H confirm that this relation is best suited to describe the ionisation budget in regions affected by high CO depletion, even when considering parsec-scale objects.

As a final test, we compared the electron fraction obtained throughout Cloud H with that from
pyRate
(see Sect.
3
). The electron fraction derived from the models depends on the input CRIR per proton
\zeta
. We therefore run the physical and chemical models with the same setup, but with an ionisation rate
\zeta=\langle\zeta_{2}\rangle/2.3\sim 1\times 10^{-18}
s
-1
, where the relation is discussed by
51
and given the median value of
\zeta_{2}
in Cloud H (see Sect.

). Figure

x(e)
for the density regime of Cloud H. This result confirms once more the reliability of our analytical methods in the determination of

\zeta_{2}

7
Conclusions

We studied the electron fraction and CRIR at parsec scales across the filamentary IRDC G035.39-00.33 (Cloud H) using a newly developed analytical framework. We derived these two parameters using a new set of NOEMA+30m observations for our main suite of tracers, N
2
H
+
, N
2
D
+
, and DCO
+
(1
-
0). These were paired with ancillary observations of C
18
O (1
-
0), plus estimates of the kinetic temperature of the gas, its column and number density of H
2
from several facilities, all re-gridded to a common resolution of
3^{\prime\prime}
(or
\sim 0.04
pc) and pixel size of
1^{\prime\prime}
. Our analysis comprised the following steps: we tested our analytical framework against a chemical model to explore its limits of application; then, we estimated the column densities of our molecular tracers; next, we determined the CO depletion factor from the column densities of C
18
O and H
2

2
D
+
and N
2
H
+
. Finally, we combined these estimates to compute the electron fraction and CRIR in Cloud H. The main results of the study are listed below.

1.

Our analytical framework can accurately reproduce the electron fraction and CRIR from the models within a factor of 3. The formulae to compute
x(e)
and
\zeta_{2}
are most accurate in the number density regime within
\sim 10^{4}-10^{5}
cm
-3
and for cloud ages
\gtrsim 10^{5}
yr (see Sect.
3
,
A.4
and Fig.
2
).

2.

CO depletion factors above
f_{\mathrm{D}}\gtrsim 3
are widespread throughout Cloud H with values up to
f_{\mathrm{D}}\sim 12
towards its densest regions. These depletion factors are higher than those reported in previous studies of Cloud H at lower resolution and show a positive correlation with the number density in the region.

3.

Deuterium fractions above
R_{\mathrm{D}}\gtrsim 0.05
are similarly widespread in the cloud and its highest values (
\gtrsim 0.1
) show a positive correlation with the sites of severe depletion. Deuterium fractions below
R_{\mathrm{D}}\lesssim 0.05
are found adjacent to evolved compact objects where higher temperatures may reduce the abundance of N
2
D
+
and N
2
H
+
in the gas phase.

4.

The electron fraction varies by two orders of magnitude in Cloud H with values within
\sim 10^{-9}-10^{-7}
and a median of
1\times 10^{-8}
.

shows a functional dependence on

that is consistent with the scaling relation determined for low-mass cores with depletion factors similar to those of Cloud H and is well-reproduced by the chemical models.

5.

The CRIR in Cloud H varies within
\sim(0.004-4)\times 10^{-16}
s

around its median of

s

10^{-17}
s

6.

Values of

nor with

Our new analytical framework proved robust in the determination of the electron fraction and CRIR through the wide range of physical properties offered by Cloud H. It challenges other analytical methods
(

14

\zeta_{2}

While these errors do not undermine the correlations identified in the work, future applications of the method should focus on obtaining better estimates for its most uncertain parameters.

x(\mathrm{N_{2}})=7.56\times 10^{-5}

(
125

. For atomic O, the choice is complex, as this species is progressively locked in CO and H

x(\mathrm{CO})\sim 10^{-4}
of this oxygen, while H

\sim(0.001-1)\times 10^{-4}

(

. The corresponding variation in the abundance of atomic O is
x(\mathrm{O})=(2-4)\times 10^{-4}
. We adopt a value of
x(\mathrm{O})=2\times 10^{-4}
, although a variation of up to a factor of 2 in
x(\mathrm{O})
accounts for only a variation of
\sim 30\%
in
\zeta_{2}
. The abundance of grains available for recombination reactions (i.e. negatively-charged grains) can be computed as follows
(
82
)
:

x(g^{-})=6\times 10^{-11}\bigg(\frac{10^{-6}\penalty\ \mathrm{cm}}{a_{\mathrm{min}}}\bigg)^{2.5},

(29)

where
a
is the minimum grain size in the distribution. Assuming a constant grain size of
0.1\penalty\ \mu
m, as we did to run the models (see Sect.
3
), the typical abundance of the negatively-charged grains is
x(g^{-})\sim 2\times 10^{-13}
. We therefore include the grain contribution in Eqs.
3
and
8
, although its influence is limited compared to the other species for number densities below
\lesssim 10^{6}
cm
-3
.

The atomic and molecular abundances of some of these species may be altered by the freeze-out of these species onto dust grains. However, the only depletion factor that we can directly measure is the CO depletion factor (
f_{\mathrm{D}}
). This leaves the degree of depletion for O (
f_{\mathrm{O}}
) and N

(
f_{\mathrm{N_{2}}}
) unchecked. The easiest way to treat the latter two factors is to assume that
f_{\mathrm{N_{2}}}=1
and
f_{\mathrm{O}}=f_{\mathrm{D}}
. The first assumption is rooted in the slow formation of N
2

(
1

, for which we consider its abundance unaltered in Cloud H while that of CO is already reduced by a factor
f_{\mathrm{D}}

(see discussions in
9
;
114

2
in two different density regime)
. The second assumption is rooted in the freeze-out of oxygen onto dust grains under the same conditions and timescales as its main molecular compound, CO
(
23

. Despite the abundance of oxygen in the ISM being still debated
(see
152

, this is the simplest approach for a dense filament such as Cloud H.
