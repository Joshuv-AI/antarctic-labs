Weighted partitions with interval restrictions: exact formulas and a bivariate master identity Thanks: The first author is partially supported by Simons Foundation Grant 633284.
George E. Andrews, Mohamed El Bachraoui, Aritram Dhar, Ankush Goswami, Runqiao Li

Abstract
. Let a 2 ′′ ​ ( n ) a_{2}^{\prime\prime}(n) and b 2 ′′ ​ ( n ) b_{2}^{\prime\prime}(n) be the signed partition functions introduced by Andrews and El Bachraoui for interval-restricted partitions whose parts greater than 1 1 are controlled by the smallest even part and by the number of ones. We prove two conjectures for these functions. The first gives the generating function for a 2 ′′ ​ ( n ) a_{2}^{\prime\prime}(n) as an elementary rational term plus a false theta series with periodic signs; the second asserts that the companion coefficients b 2 ′′ ​ ( n ) b_{2}^{\prime\prime}(n) take only the values − 1 , 0 , 1 , 2 -1,0,1,2 . The central structural result introduces an auxiliary variable z z recording the number of non-compulsory parts greater than 1 1 . We obtain closed forms for the two resulting generating functions and prove the master identity ( 1 + q 2 ) ℬ ( z , q ) − ( 1 + q ) 𝒜 ( z , q ) = − q 4 / ( 1 − q 3 ) (1+q^{2})\mathcal{B}(z,q)-(1+q)\mathcal{A}(z,q)=-q^{4}/(1-q^{3}) using both analytic and combinatorial techniques. At z = − 1 z=-1 , this identity, together with a Rogers–Fine evaluation, gives the false theta formula for a 2 ′′ ​ ( n ) a_{2}^{\prime\prime}(n) and an explicit generating function for b 2 ′′ ​ ( n ) b_{2}^{\prime\prime}(n) . The latter formula implies the asserted coefficient range and leads to an exact coefficient description of b 2 ′′ ​ ( n ) b_{2}^{\prime\prime}(n) . We also include a direct Heine–Rogers–Fine proof of the false theta formula, ordinary and fixed-refinement consequences of the master identity, and the resulting quantum modular interpretation.

1.
Introduction

Partitions in which the parts are constrained to lie in a short interval often lead to generating functions with a rich basic hypergeometric structure. In
[
3
]
, the first two authors studied several such families and introduced signed refinements obtained by weighting each partition by
(-1)^{j+1}
, where
j
is the number of parts greater than
1
. For two of these signed partition functions, denoted
a_{2}^{\prime\prime}(n)
and
b_{2}^{\prime\prime}(n)
, computations suggested unexpectedly rigid behavior: the first was conjectured to have a triangular false theta description, while the second appeared to be confined to the set
\{-1,0,1,2\}
. The purpose of the present paper is to prove these conjectures by combining two complementary viewpoints. The main structural viewpoint is that both conjectures are consequences of a bivariate identity involving an additional variable
z
. This is the natural level of generality: the weighted identities arise from the specialization
z=-1
, while the same identity also controls the ordinary and fixed-refinement generating functions. We also give a direct Heine–Rogers–Fine derivation of the false theta formula for
a_{2}^{\prime\prime}(n)
, which makes the false theta and quantum modular structure especially transparent.

We use the standard notation
[
1
,
6
]

(z;q)_{0}=1,\quad(z;q)_{n}=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}(1-zq^{j}),\quad(z;q)_{\infty}=\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}(1-zq^{j}).

All generating-function identities below are interpreted as identities of formal power series in
q
.

We begin with the family
a_{2}(n)
from
[
3
, Definition 2]
. It counts partitions of
n
such that there are
l-1
ones, the smallest even part is
2k
, every part greater than
1
lies in the interval
[2k,2l-2]
, and the odd parts greater than
1
are distinct. If
j
denotes the number of parts greater than
1
, then
a_{2}^{\prime\prime}(n)
is the corresponding signed count in which each partition is counted with weight
(-1)^{j+1}
. The generating functions of the sequences
a_{2}(n)
and
a_{2}^{\prime\prime}(n)
are

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{2}(n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\geq 2\\
1\leq k\leq l-1\end{subarray}}\frac{(-q^{2k+1};q^{2})_{l-k-1}}{(q^{2k};q^{2})_{l-k}}q^{2k+l-1}

\displaystyle=q^{3}+q^{4}+2q^{5}+3q^{6}+5q^{7}+6q^{8}+\cdots,

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{2}^{\prime\prime}(n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\geq 2\\
1\leq k\leq l-1\end{subarray}}\frac{(q^{2k+1};q^{2})_{l-k-1}}{(-q^{2k};q^{2})_{l-k}}q^{2k+l-1}

\displaystyle=q^{3}+q^{4}+q^{6}+q^{7}+q^{9}+q^{12}+\cdots.

The following conjecture regarding
a_{2}^{\prime\prime}(n)
was stated in the earlier paper
[
3
, Conjecture 1]
.

Conjecture 1
.

There holds

\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{2}^{\prime\prime}(n)q^{n}=\frac{q}{1-q^{2}}+\frac{1}{1+q}\sum_{n\geq 0}\chi(n)q^{\binom{n+1}{2}+1},

(1.1)

where

\chi(n)=\begin{cases}-1&\ \text{if\ }n\equiv 0,1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 4),\\
1&\ \text{if\ }n\equiv 2,3\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 4).\end{cases}

We now turn to the companion family
b_{2}(n)
, where the interval of allowed parts is enlarged from
[2k,2l-2]
to
[2k,2l-1]
. This apparently small change produces a more complicated signed generating function, but it again leads to a strikingly simple coefficient pattern.

The companion function
b_{2}(n)
is defined analogously: there are
l-1
ones, the smallest even part is
2k
, every part greater than
1
lies in the enlarged interval
[2k,2l-1]
, and the odd parts greater than
1
are distinct. We let
b_{2}^{\prime\prime}(n)
be the corresponding signed count, again with weight
(-1)^{j+1}
where
j
is the number of parts greater than
1
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}b_{2}(n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\geq 2\\
1\leq k\leq l-1\end{subarray}}\frac{(-q^{2k+1};q^{2})_{l-k}}{(q^{2k};q^{2})_{l-k}}q^{2k+l-1}

\displaystyle=q^{3}+q^{4}+2q^{5}+4q^{6}+5q^{7}+7q^{8}+10q^{9}+\cdots,

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}b_{2}^{\prime\prime}(n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\geq 2\\
1\leq k\leq l-1\end{subarray}}\frac{(q^{2k+1};q^{2})_{l-k}}{(-q^{2k};q^{2})_{l-k}}q^{2k+l-1}

\displaystyle=q^{3}+q^{4}+q^{7}+q^{8}-q^{10}+2q^{12}-q^{14}+q^{15}\cdots.

The original second conjecture was the following range assertion
[
3
, Conjecture 2]
.

Conjecture 2
.

For any positive integer
n
, we have
b_{2}^{\prime\prime}(n)\in\{-1,0,1,2\}
.

The central point is that the two families should first be treated together. For a partition counted by
a_{2}(n)
or
b_{2}(n)
, let
r
denote the number of parts greater than
1
other than the compulsory smallest even part
2k
. We write
a_{2}(n;r)
and
b_{2}(n;r)
for the corresponding refined counts and define

\mathcal{A}(z,q):=\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{r\geq 0}a_{2}(n;r)z^{r}q^{n},\qquad\mathcal{B}(z,q):=\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{r\geq 0}b_{2}(n;r)z^{r}q^{n}.

Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(z,q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\geq 2\\
1\leq k\leq l-1\end{subarray}}\frac{(-zq^{2k+1};q^{2})_{l-k-1}}{(zq^{2k};q^{2})_{l-k}}q^{2k+l-1},

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(z,q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\geq 2\\
1\leq k\leq l-1\end{subarray}}\frac{(-zq^{2k+1};q^{2})_{l-k}}{(zq^{2k};q^{2})_{l-k}}q^{2k+l-1}.

(1.3)

By construction,

\mathcal{A}(1,q)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{2}(n)q^{n},\qquad\mathcal{B}(1,q)=\sum_{n\geq 0}b_{2}(n)q^{n}.

Moreover, if
j
is the number of parts greater than
1
, then
j=r+1
; hence
(-1)^{r}=(-1)^{j+1}
. Therefore

\mathcal{A}(-1,q)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{2}^{\prime\prime}(n)q^{n},\qquad\mathcal{B}(-1,q)=\sum_{n\geq 0}b_{2}^{\prime\prime}(n)q^{n}.

Let

F(z,q):=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(-zq;q^{2})_{n}q^{n}}{(zq^{2};q^{2})_{n}},\qquad H(z,q):=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(-zq;q^{2})_{n}q^{n}}{(z;q^{2})_{n}}.

Only the product
(1-z)H(z,q)
occurs in the closed form for
\mathcal{B}(z,q)
; equivalently,

(1-z)H(z,q)=\sum_{n\geq 0}(1-zq^{2n})\frac{(-zq;q^{2})_{n}q^{n}}{(zq^{2};q^{2})_{n}},

so the expression is regular at
z=1
in the combinations used below.
The following theorem gives the bivariate closed forms and the master identity relating them.

Main Theorem
.

One has

\displaystyle z\mathcal{A}(z,q)

\displaystyle=\frac{q}{1+q}F(z,q)-\frac{q}{1-q^{2}},

(1.4)

\displaystyle z\mathcal{B}(z,q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-z}{1+q}H(z,q)-\frac{1}{1-q^{2}}+\frac{z}{(1+q)(1-q^{3})}-\frac{zq^{2}}{1-q^{3}},

(1.5)

and consequently

(1+q^{2})\mathcal{B}(z,q)-(1+q)\mathcal{A}(z,q)=-\frac{q^{4}}{1-q^{3}}.

(1.6)

Writing

A(q):=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{2}^{\prime\prime}(n)q^{n},\qquad B(q):=\sum_{n\geq 0}b_{2}^{\prime\prime}(n)q^{n},

the specialization
z=-1
of (
1.6
) gives

(1+q^{2})B(q)-(1+q)A(q)=-\frac{q^{4}}{1-q^{3}}.

(1.7)

Equivalently, for every integer
n\geq 0
,

b_{2}^{\prime\prime}(n)+b_{2}^{\prime\prime}(n-2)=a_{2}^{\prime\prime}(n)+a_{2}^{\prime\prime}(n-1)-\mathbf{1}_{\{n\geq 4,\ n\equiv 1\pmod{3}\}},

(1.8)

where
a_{2}^{\prime\prime}(m)=b_{2}^{\prime\prime}(m)=0
for
m<0
, and
\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{P}}
denotes the indicator of the property
\mathcal{P}
.

The first main consequence is the proof of Conjecture
1
.

Theorem 1
.

Conjecture
1
is true; that is,

A(q)=\frac{q}{1-q^{2}}+\frac{1}{1+q}\sum_{n\geq 0}\chi(n)q^{\binom{n+1}{2}+1}.

As a consequence,
a_{2}^{\prime\prime}(n)
is completely determined by a simple parity rule on triangular intervals, recovering
[
3
, Proposition 1]
.

Corollary 1
.

Set
\beta(1)=\beta(2)=0
. For
n\geq 3
, define
\beta(n)
as follows:

(a) If
\binom{2k}{2}<n\leq\binom{2k+1}{2}
for some nonnegative integer
k
, then

\beta(n)=\begin{cases}1&\ \text{if $n$ is odd},\\
0&\ \text{if $n$ is even}.\end{cases}

(b) If
\binom{2k+1}{2}<n\leq\binom{2k+2}{2}
for some nonnegative integer
k
, then

\beta(n)=\begin{cases}1&\ \text{if $n$ is even},\\
0&\ \text{if $n$ is odd}.\end{cases}

Then for any positive integer
n
, we have
a_{2}^{\prime\prime}(n)=\beta(n)
.

The second main consequence is the corresponding formula for
b_{2}^{\prime\prime}(n)
; the conjectured range follows from this formula.

Theorem 2
.

There holds

B(q)=\frac{q+q^{2}+q^{3}-q^{4}}{(1-q)(1+q^{2})(1+q+q^{2})}+\frac{q}{1+q^{2}}\sum_{n\geq 0}\chi(n)q^{\binom{n+1}{2}}.

(1.9)

Consequently, for any positive integer
n
, we have

b_{2}^{\prime\prime}(n)\in\{-1,0,1,2\}.

Thus Theorem
2
proves Conjecture
2
, and in fact gives a sharper generating-function statement than the original range assertion.

Two further consequences come directly from specializations of the bivariate identity.
Putting
z=1
in (
1.6
) immediately gives the ordinary specialization.

Corollary 2
.

There holds

(1+q^{2})\sum_{n\geq 0}b_{2}(n)q^{n}-(1+q)\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{2}(n)q^{n}=-\frac{q^{4}}{1-q^{3}}.

(1.10)

The fixed-refinement specialization gives a coefficient-wise relation for each positive power of
z
; its proof is given in Section
5
.

Corollary 3
.

Let

A_{r}(q):=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{2}(n;r)q^{n},\qquad B_{r}(q):=\sum_{n\geq 0}b_{2}(n;r)q^{n}.

Then

A_{0}(q)=B_{0}(q)=\frac{q^{3}}{(1-q)(1-q^{3})},

and for every integer
r\geq 1
,

(1+q^{2})B_{r}(q)=(1+q)A_{r}(q).

(1.11)

Equivalently, for every
r\geq 1
and every integer
n\geq 0
,

b_{2}(n;r)+b_{2}(n-2;r)=a_{2}(n;r)+a_{2}(n-1;r),

(1.12)

where
a_{2}(m;r)=b_{2}(m;r)=0
for
m<0
.

The range assertion in Theorem
2
can be sharpened to the following exact coefficient formula. Its proof is also given in Section
5
.

Corollary 4
.

Let
\rho(n)
be the
12
-periodic function defined by

\rho(n)=\begin{cases}1,&n\equiv 1,2,5,10\pmod{12},\\
2,&n\equiv 6,9\pmod{12},\\
-1,&n\equiv 4,7,8,11\pmod{12},\\
0,&n\equiv 0,3\pmod{12}.\end{cases}

For
1\leq u\leq 8
, set

\mu_{u}(n)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1-(-1)^{n}}{2},&u=1,7,\\[6.0pt]
1,&u=2,6,\\[4.0pt]
1+\dfrac{1+(-1)^{n}}{2},&u=3,5,\\[6.0pt]
2,&u=4,\\[4.0pt]
0,&u=8.\end{cases}

(1.13)

Let
T_{s}=\binom{s+1}{2}
for
s\geq 0
. Then
b_{2}^{\prime\prime}(0)=0
, and for every
n\geq 1
with

T_{8m+u-1}<n\leq T_{8m+u}\qquad(m\geq 0,\ 1\leq u\leq 8),

we have

b_{2}^{\prime\prime}(n)=\rho(n)+\mu_{u}(n)\,\chi(n-1).

(1.14)

2.
Proof of the Main Theorem

We shall use the identity of Andrews, Subbarao, and Vidyasagar
[
4
]

\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(x;q)_{n}}{(y;q)_{n}}q^{n}=\frac{q(x;q)_{\infty}}{y(y;q)_{\infty}\big(1-\frac{xq}{y}\big)}+\frac{1-\frac{q}{y}}{1-\frac{xq}{y}}.

(2.1)

Remark
1
.

The identity (
2.1
) is also the
\gamma=0
specialization of the following three-parameter identity of Dixit and Goswami
[
5
, Theorem 1.3]
:

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(\alpha,\gamma;q)_{n}}{(\beta,\alpha\gamma q^{2}/\beta;q)_{n}}q^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta^{-1}q}{(1-\alpha q/\beta)(1-\gamma q/\beta)}\frac{(\alpha,\gamma;q)_{\infty}}{(\beta,\alpha\gamma q^{2}/\beta;q)_{\infty}}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{(1-q/\beta)(1-\alpha\gamma q/\beta)}{(1-\gamma q/\beta)(1-\alpha q/\beta)}.

(2.2)

Indeed, taking
\alpha=x
,
\beta=y
, and
\gamma=0
gives (
2.1
). This observation suggests a natural route toward parameter-dependent variants of the bivariate identities below. We do not pursue those extensions here.

Proof of the Main Theorem.

We first record two simple shift formulas. For every integer
k\geq 1
, a direct index shift gives

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(-zq^{2k+1};q^{2})_{n}q^{n}}{(zq^{2k+2};q^{2})_{n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-zq^{2k}}{q(1+zq^{2k-1})}\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(-zq^{2k-1};q^{2})_{n}q^{n}}{(zq^{2k};q^{2})_{n}}-\frac{1-zq^{2k}}{q(1+zq^{2k-1})},

(2.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(-zq^{2k+1};q^{2})_{n}q^{n}}{(zq^{2k};q^{2})_{n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-zq^{2k-2}}{q(1+zq^{2k-1})}\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(-zq^{2k-1};q^{2})_{n}q^{n}}{(zq^{2k-2};q^{2})_{n}}-\frac{1-zq^{2k-2}}{q(1+zq^{2k-1})}.

(2.4)

Indeed, for (
2.3
),

\displaystyle q\frac{1+zq^{2k-1}}{1-zq^{2k}}\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(-zq^{2k+1};q^{2})_{n}q^{n}}{(zq^{2k+2};q^{2})_{n}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(-zq^{2k-1};q^{2})_{n+1}q^{n+1}}{(zq^{2k};q^{2})_{n+1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{(-zq^{2k-1};q^{2})_{n}q^{n}}{(zq^{2k};q^{2})_{n}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(-zq^{2k-1};q^{2})_{n}q^{n}}{(zq^{2k};q^{2})_{n}}-1,

which proves (
2.3
). The proof of (
2.4
) is identical.

Iterating these identities gives

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(-zq^{2k+1};q^{2})_{n}q^{n}}{(zq^{2k+2};q^{2})_{n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q^{k}}\frac{(zq^{2};q^{2})_{k}}{(-zq;q^{2})_{k}}F(z,q)-\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{q^{k-j+1}}\frac{(zq^{2j};q^{2})_{k-j+1}}{(-zq^{2j-1};q^{2})_{k-j+1}},

(2.5)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(-zq^{2k+1};q^{2})_{n}q^{n}}{(zq^{2k};q^{2})_{n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q^{k}}\frac{(z;q^{2})_{k}}{(-zq;q^{2})_{k}}H(z,q)-\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{q^{k-j+1}}\frac{(zq^{2j-2};q^{2})_{k-j+1}}{(-zq^{2j-1};q^{2})_{k-j+1}}.

(2.6)

We now find closed forms for
\mathcal{A}(z,q)
and
\mathcal{B}(z,q)
. Starting from (
1.2
) and using (
2.5
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(z,q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 1}q^{2k}\sum_{l\geq k+1}\frac{(-zq^{2k+1};q^{2})_{l-k-1}}{(zq^{2k};q^{2})_{l-k}}q^{l-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{q^{3k}}{1-zq^{2k}}\sum_{l\geq 0}\frac{(-zq^{2k+1};q^{2})_{l}q^{l}}{(zq^{2k+2};q^{2})_{l}}

\displaystyle=F(z,q)\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{q^{2k}(zq^{2};q^{2})_{k-1}}{(-zq;q^{2})_{k}}-\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{q^{3j-1}}{1+zq^{2j-1}}\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{(zq^{2j};q^{2})_{k}q^{2k}}{(-zq^{2j+1};q^{2})_{k}}.

Applying (
2.1
) with
q\to q^{2}
, the first sum becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{q^{2k}(zq^{2};q^{2})_{k-1}}{(-zq;q^{2})_{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+zq}\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{(zq^{2};q^{2})_{k}q^{2k+2}}{(-zq^{3};q^{2})_{k}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{q(zq^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}{z(1+q)(-zq;q^{2})_{\infty}}+\frac{q}{z(1+q)}.

Similarly,

\displaystyle\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{q^{3j-1}}{1+zq^{2j-1}}\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{(zq^{2j};q^{2})_{k}q^{2k}}{(-zq^{2j+1};q^{2})_{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{q^{3j-1}}{1+zq^{2j-1}}\left(-\frac{(zq^{2j};q^{2})_{\infty}}{zq^{2j-1}(1+q)(-zq^{2j+1};q^{2})_{\infty}}+\frac{1+q^{1-2j}/z}{1+q}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{z(1+q)}\sum_{j\geq 1}q^{j}\frac{(zq^{2j};q^{2})_{\infty}}{(-zq^{2j-1};q^{2})_{\infty}}+\frac{q}{z(1-q^{2})}

\displaystyle=-\frac{q(zq^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}{z(1+q)(-zq;q^{2})_{\infty}}F(z,q)+\frac{q}{z(1-q^{2})}.

Therefore

z\mathcal{A}(z,q)=\frac{q}{1+q}F(z,q)-\frac{q}{1-q^{2}},

which is (
1.4
).

The same method applied to (
1.3
) and (
2.6
) gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(z,q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 1}q^{2k}\sum_{l\geq k+1}\frac{(-zq^{2k+1};q^{2})_{l-k}}{(zq^{2k};q^{2})_{l-k}}q^{l-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 1}q^{3k-1}\sum_{l\geq 0}\frac{(-zq^{2k+1};q^{2})_{l}q^{l}}{(zq^{2k};q^{2})_{l}}-\frac{q^{2}}{1-q^{3}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q}H(z,q)\sum_{k\geq 1}q^{2k}\frac{(z;q^{2})_{k}}{(-zq;q^{2})_{k}}-D(z,q)-\frac{q^{2}}{1-q^{3}},

where

D(z,q):=\sum_{k\geq 1}q^{3k-1}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{q^{k-j+1}}\frac{(zq^{2j-2};q^{2})_{k-j+1}}{(-zq^{2j-1};q^{2})_{k-j+1}}.

Again using (
2.1
) with
q\to q^{2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q}\sum_{k\geq 1}q^{2k}\frac{(z;q^{2})_{k}}{(-zq;q^{2})_{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-z}{q(1+zq)}\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{(zq^{2};q^{2})_{k}q^{2k+2}}{(-zq^{3};q^{2})_{k}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{(z;q^{2})_{\infty}}{z(1+q)(-zq;q^{2})_{\infty}}+\frac{1-z}{z(1+q)},

and

\displaystyle D(z,q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\geq 0}q^{3j-1}\sum_{k\geq 0}q^{2k}\frac{(zq^{2j};q^{2})_{k}}{(-zq^{2j+1};q^{2})_{k}}-\frac{1}{q(1-q^{3})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\geq 0}q^{3j-1}\left(-\frac{(zq^{2j};q^{2})_{\infty}}{zq^{2j-1}(1+q)(-zq^{2j+1};q^{2})_{\infty}}+\frac{1+q^{1-2j}/z}{1+q}\right)-\frac{1}{q(1-q^{3})}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{z(1+q)}\sum_{j\geq 0}q^{j}\frac{(zq^{2j};q^{2})_{\infty}}{(-zq^{2j+1};q^{2})_{\infty}}+\frac{1}{z(1-q^{2})}-\frac{1}{(1+q)(1-q^{3})}

\displaystyle=-\frac{(z;q^{2})_{\infty}}{z(1+q)(-zq;q^{2})_{\infty}}H(z,q)+\frac{1}{z(1-q^{2})}-\frac{1}{(1+q)(1-q^{3})}.

Thus

z\mathcal{B}(z,q)=\frac{1-z}{1+q}H(z,q)-\frac{1}{1-q^{2}}+\frac{z}{(1+q)(1-q^{3})}-\frac{zq^{2}}{1-q^{3}},

which is (
1.5
).

It remains to derive the master identity. Let

\nu_{n}(z,q):=\frac{(-zq;q^{2})_{n}q^{n}}{(zq^{2};q^{2})_{n}}.

Then

F(z,q)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\nu_{n}(z,q).

Also, since

(z;q^{2})_{n}=\frac{1-z}{1-zq^{2n}}(zq^{2};q^{2})_{n},

we have

(1-z)H(z,q)=\sum_{n\geq 0}(1-zq^{2n})\nu_{n}(z,q).

Furthermore,

(1-zq^{2n+2})\nu_{n+1}(z,q)=q(1+zq^{2n+1})\nu_{n}(z,q),

so

\displaystyle(1-zq^{2n})\nu_{n}(z,q)-(1-zq^{2n+2})\nu_{n+1}(z,q)

\displaystyle=\big((1-q)-z(1+q^{2})q^{2n}\big)\nu_{n}(z,q)

\displaystyle=\big((1+q^{2})(1-zq^{2n})-q(1+q)\big)\nu_{n}(z,q).

Summing over
n\geq 0
yields the telescoping identity

(1+q^{2})(1-z)H(z,q)-q(1+q)F(z,q)=1-z.

(2.7)

Now multiplying the desired identity by
z
and using (
1.4
) and (
1.5
), we find

\displaystyle z\big((1+q^{2})\mathcal{B}(z,q)-(1+q)\mathcal{A}(z,q)\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1+q^{2}}{1+q}(1-z)H(z,q)-qF(z,q)-\frac{1}{1+q}

\displaystyle\quad+z\left(\frac{1+q^{2}}{(1+q)(1-q^{3})}-\frac{q^{2}(1+q^{2})}{1-q^{3}}\right).

By (
2.7
), the first two terms equal
(1-z)/(1+q)
, and hence

\displaystyle z\big((1+q^{2})\mathcal{B}(z,q)-(1+q)\mathcal{A}(z,q)\big)

\displaystyle=-\frac{z}{1+q}+z\left(\frac{1+q^{2}}{(1+q)(1-q^{3})}-\frac{q^{2}(1+q^{2})}{1-q^{3}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{zq^{4}}{1-q^{3}}.

Cancelling the common factor
z
proves (
1.6
) and completes the proof of the Main Theorem.
∎

3.3.
Involutive proof of (
1.6
)

We prove (
1.6
) using a sign-reversing involution. The point of the proof is that the two partition families counted by

\mathcal{A}(z,q)
and
\mathcal{B}(z,q)
are almost the same. The only
difference is that the
\mathcal{B}
-family allows one additional largest odd part than the
\mathcal{A}
-family.
After the two sides are modified by the factors
1+q^{2}
and
1+q
, this
extra freedom is cancelled by a sign-reversing involution. The only objects
which cannot be paired form a simple boundary family, and this family gives
the correction term on the right-hand side of (1.6).

We first recall the combinatorial meaning of the two generating functions.
An object counted by
\mathcal{A}(z,q)
and
\mathcal{B}(z,q)
is determined by integers
l\geq 2,1\leq k\leq l-1
, together with a choice of optional parts. They have the following structure:

•

contains the compulsory part
2k
;

•

contains
l-1
compulsory parts equal to
1
;

•

may contain optional even parts from
2k,\,2k+2,\,2k+4,\ldots,2l-2
, with repetitions allowed;

•

may contain optional odd parts from
2k+1,\,2k+3,\ldots,2l-3+2i
, each at most once, where
i
is
0
or
1
, according as partitions are enumerated by
\mathcal{A}(z,q)
or
\mathcal{B}(z,q)
, respectively.

The exponent of
q
records the size of the partition, while the exponent
of
z
records the number of optional parts greater than
1
. Thus every
optional part contributes one factor of
z
, in addition to its usual weight, accounted for by the exponent of
q
. It is convenient to put
m=l-k
. Then
m\geq 1
, and the largest optional even part for both sets is

E_{m}=2l-2=2k+2m-2,

while the largest odd part available only in the
\mathcal{B}
-family is

O_{m}=2l-1=2k+2m-1.

Thus
O_{m}=E_{m}+1
. We now interpret the left-hand side of (1.6) as a signed generating function. The positive objects are the
\mathcal{B}
-objects,
together with an auxiliary marker of weight either
1
or
q^{2}
. These
are counted by
(1+q^{2})\mathcal{B}(z,q)
. The negative objects are the
\mathcal{A}
-objects, together with an auxiliary marker
of weight either
1
or
q
. These are counted by
(1+q)\mathcal{A}(z,q)
, and are assigned negative signs.

We shall define a weight-preserving, sign-reversing involution on all augmented
objects except for one boundary family. Here is a flow-chart on how the involution pairs off partitions on both the sets:

\begin{array}[]{rclcl}\overline{\mathcal{B}}(1,\;O_{m}\notin\lambda)&\longleftrightarrow&\overline{\mathcal{A}}(1)&&\text{common cancellation},\\[5.69054pt]
\overline{\mathcal{A}}(q,\;E_{m}\in\lambda)&\longleftrightarrow&\overline{\mathcal{B}}(1,\;O_{m}\in\lambda)&&q+E_{m}\leftrightarrow O_{m},\\[5.69054pt]
\overline{\mathcal{A}}(q,\;E_{m}\notin\lambda,\;m\geq 2)&\longleftrightarrow&\overline{\mathcal{B}}(q^{2},\;m-1)&&m\mapsto m-1,\\[5.69054pt]
\overline{\mathcal{A}}(q,\;E_{1}\notin\lambda,\;m=1)&\longmapsto&\text{unpaired}&&(2k,1^{k})\text{ with marker }q\end{array}

where we used the notations
\overline{\mathcal{A}}(\text{marker},\text{conditions})
and
\overline{\mathcal{B}}(\text{marker},\text{conditions})
to represent a partition enumerated by
(1+q)\mathcal{A}(z,q)
and
(1+q^{2})\mathcal{B}(z,q)
with the markers and conditions on the parts. We omit the conditions from the notation when there are no conditions on the parts. We now describe the involution in detail.

First, there is an immediate cancellation. If a positive
\mathcal{B}
-object carries the marker of weight
1
and does not contain the extra top odd part
O_{m}=2l-1
, then it is already
an
\mathcal{A}
-object with the same parameters and the same optional parts. We pair
it with the corresponding negative
\mathcal{A}
-object carrying the marker of weight

1
. This pairing is clearly sign-reversing and weight-preserving. It remains to pair three types of objects:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{negative:}&\mathcal{A}\text{-objects with marker }q\\
\text{positive:}&\mathcal{B}\text{-objects with marker }q^{2},\\
\text{positive:}&\mathcal{B}\text{-objects with marker }1\text{ containing }O_{m}.\end{array}

Take a negative
\mathcal{A}
-object with marker
q
, and fix its parameters
k
and
m
. There are two possibilities.

Case 1
(
E_{m}
is in
\mathcal{A}
): Suppose first that the object contains at least one copy of the largest
optional even part
E_{m}
. Then we remove the marker
q
and remove one copy of
E_{m}
. In their place we insert the top odd part
O_{m}
. The resulting object is a positive
\mathcal{B}
-object with marker
1
. The weight is unchanged, because

q\cdot zq^{E_{m}}=zq^{E_{m}+1}=zq^{O_{m}}.

The operation is reversible: starting with a positive
\mathcal{B}
-object with marker

1
which contains
O_{m}
, we remove
O_{m}
, insert one copy of
E_{m}
, and restore the negative marker
q
. Thus these two classes cancel
perfectly.

Case 2
(
E_{m}
is not in
\mathcal{A}
): Now suppose that the negative
\mathcal{A}
-object with marker
q
contains no copy
of its largest optional even part
E_{m}
. If
m\geq 2
, then the same
optional parts are still legal after lowering the interval length from
m

to
m-1
. Indeed, the even parts now lie among

2k,2k+2,\ldots,2k+2m-4,

and the odd parts lie among

2k+1,2k+3,\ldots,2k+2m-3.

But these are precisely the optional even and odd parts allowed for a

\mathcal{B}
-object with parameters
k
and
m-1
. We need to check if the weight of the partition coming from the compulsory parts changes. The compulsory contribution for the
\mathcal{A}
object is
q^{2k+l-1}=q^{3k+m-1}
, and the marker contributes an additional factor
q
. Hence the compulsory marker contribution is
q^{3k+m}
. After lowering
m
to
m-1
, the compulsory contribution becomes
q^{3k+(m-1)-1}=q^{3k+m-2}
, and the
q^{2}
marker in
\mathcal{B}
contributes
q^{2}
. Thus the new compulsory-marker contribution is again
q^{3k+m-2}q^{2}=q^{3k+m}
. Since the optional parts were not changed, the total weight is unchanged. This operation is also sign-reversing and reversible.
Indeed, starting with a positive
\mathcal{B}
-object
with marker
q^{2}
and parameters
k,m-1
(
m\geq 2
), we raise the interval length to

m
, keep the optional parts unchanged, and replace the marker
q^{2}
by
the negative marker
q
. The resulting
\mathcal{A}
-object has no copy of the new
largest optional even part
E_{m}
, exactly as required.

We have therefore paired all objects except those for which the second
operation cannot be performed. This happens precisely when
m=1
. In that
case the largest optional even part is
E_{1}=2k
, and if the negative
\mathcal{A}
-object contains no copy of
E_{1}
, then it contains no optional parts at all (since the smallest odd part for
\mathcal{A}
is
2k+1
). Since
m=1
, we have
l=k+1
. The unpaired
\mathcal{A}
object with negative marker
q
is therefore
the partition

(2k,\underbrace{1,1,\cdots,1}_{k-\text{ones}}),

Its weight is
q^{2k+k}q=q^{3k+1}
. Consequently the total contribution of the unpaired objects is

-\sum_{k\geq 1}q^{3k+1}=-\frac{q^{4}}{1-q^{3}}.

All the remaining objects cancel in pairs under the weight-preserving
sign-reversing involution. Hence the signed generating function is exactly
the contribution of the unpaired boundary family, and we obtain

(1+q^{2})\mathcal{B}(z,q)-(1+q)\mathcal{A}(z,q)=-\frac{q^{4}}{1-q^{3}}.

This proves (
1.6
).∎

4.
Proofs of Theorems
1
and
2

We first prove Theorem
1
from the bivariate formula. We then give an independent direct proof based on Heine’s transformation and the Rogers–Fine identity. Finally, we prove Theorem
2
.

4.1.
Proof of Theorem
1
from the master identity

Set
z=-1
in (
1.4
). Since

F(-1,q)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(q;q^{2})_{n}q^{n}}{(-q^{2};q^{2})_{n}},

we obtain

A(q)=\frac{q}{1-q^{2}}-\frac{q}{1+q}F(-1,q).

(4.1)

Thus it remains only to evaluate this single basic hypergeometric series.

Recall the Rogers–Fine identity
[
2
, p.223, Eq. (9.1.1)]

\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(\alpha;q)_{n}}{(\beta;q)_{n}}\tau^{n}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(\alpha;q)_{n}(\alpha\tau q/\beta;q)_{n}(1-\alpha\tau q^{2n})\beta^{n}\tau^{n}q^{n^{2}-n}}{(\beta;q)_{n}(\tau;q)_{n+1}}.

(4.2)

Applying (
4.2
) with
q\to q^{2}
and
(\alpha,\beta,\tau)=(q,-q^{2},q)
, then simplifying, gives

\displaystyle F(-1,q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(q;q^{2})_{n}q^{n}}{(-q^{2};q^{2})_{n}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(q;q^{2})_{n}(-q^{2};q^{2})_{n}(1-q^{4n+2})(-1)^{n}q^{2n^{2}+n}}{(-q^{2};q^{2})_{n}(q;q^{2})_{n+1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}(-1)^{n}q^{2n^{2}+n}(1+q^{2n+1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\Big(q^{8n^{2}+2n}(1+q^{4n+1})-q^{8n^{2}+10n+3}(1+q^{4n+3})\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\Big(q^{\binom{4n+1}{2}}+q^{\binom{4n+2}{2}}-q^{\binom{4n+3}{2}}-q^{\binom{4n+4}{2}}\Big).

Hence

F(-1,q)=-\sum_{n\geq 0}\chi(n)q^{\binom{n+1}{2}}.

(4.3)

Substituting (
4.3
) into (
4.1
) yields

A(q)=\frac{q}{1-q^{2}}+\frac{1}{1+q}\sum_{n\geq 0}\chi(n)q^{\binom{n+1}{2}+1},

which is exactly (
1.1
). This proves Theorem
1
.

4.2.
A direct Heine–Rogers–Fine proof of Theorem
1

For completeness, and also to record the complementary method, we give a direct proof of Theorem
1
which does not use the bivariate master identity. Starting from the defining double series and writing
r=l-k
, we have

A(q)=\sum_{k,r\geq 1}\frac{(q^{2k+1};q^{2})_{r-1}}{(-q^{2k};q^{2})_{r}}q^{3k+r-1}.

(4.4)

Putting
j=r-1
gives

A(q)=\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{q^{3k}}{1+q^{2k}}I_{k}(q),\qquad I_{k}(q):=\sum_{j\geq 0}\frac{(q^{2k+1};q^{2})_{j}}{(-q^{2k+2};q^{2})_{j}}q^{j}.

(4.5)

We regard
I_{k}(q)
as

{}_{2}\phi_{1}\left(\begin{matrix}q^{2k+1},q^{2}\\
-q^{2k+2}\end{matrix};q^{2},q\right).

We use Heine’s transformation in the form

{}_{2}\phi_{1}\left(\begin{matrix}a,b\\
c\end{matrix};Q,z\right)=\frac{(abz/c;Q)_{\infty}}{(z;Q)_{\infty}}{}_{2}\phi_{1}\left(\begin{matrix}c/a,c/b\\
c\end{matrix};Q,abz/c\right),

where

{}_{2}\phi_{1}\left(\begin{matrix}a,b\\
c\end{matrix};Q,z\right):=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(a,b;Q)_{n}}{(c,Q;Q)_{n}}z^{n}.

With base
q^{2}
and parameters

a=q^{2k+1},\qquad b=q^{2},\qquad c=-q^{2k+2},\qquad z=q,

yields

\displaystyle I_{k}(q)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}{(q;q^{2})_{\infty}}\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(-q;q^{2})_{n}(-q^{2k};q^{2})_{n}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{n}(-q^{2k+2};q^{2})_{n}}(-q^{2})^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}{(q;q^{2})_{\infty}}\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{1+q^{2k}}{1+q^{2k+2n}}\frac{(-q;q^{2})_{n}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{n}}(-q^{2})^{n}.

(4.6)

Substituting (
4.6
) into (
4.5
), expanding

(1+q^{2k+2n})^{-1}
as a geometric series, and interchanging sums gives

\displaystyle A(q)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}{(q;q^{2})_{\infty}}\sum_{h\geq 0}(-1)^{h}\frac{q^{2h+3}}{1-q^{2h+3}}\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(-q;q^{2})_{n}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{n}}(-q^{2h+2})^{n}.

(4.7)

The
q
-binomial theorem evaluates the inner sum as

\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(-q;q^{2})_{n}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{n}}(-q^{2h+2})^{n}=\frac{(q^{2h+3};q^{2})_{\infty}}{(-q^{2h+2};q^{2})_{\infty}}.

Hence

\displaystyle A(q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{h\geq 0}(-1)^{h}\frac{q^{2h+3}(-q^{2};q^{2})_{h}}{(1-q^{2h+3})(q;q^{2})_{h+1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{3}}{(1-q)(1-q^{3})}\sum_{h\geq 0}\frac{(-q^{2};q^{2})_{h}(-q^{2})^{h}}{(q^{5};q^{2})_{h}}.

(4.8)

Applying the Rogers–Fine identity (
4.2
) with base
q^{2}
and

\alpha=-q^{2},\qquad\beta=q^{5},\qquad\tau=-q^{2},

we obtain from (
4.8
)

A(q)=q^{3}\sum_{n\geq 0}(-1)^{n}q^{2n^{2}+5n}\frac{1-q^{2n+2}}{(1-q^{2n+1})(1-q^{2n+3})}.

(4.9)

Using

\frac{1-q^{2n+2}}{(1-q^{2n+1})(1-q^{2n+3})}=\frac{1}{(1+q)(1-q^{2n+1})}+\frac{q}{(1+q)(1-q^{2n+3})},

then shifting the second sum in the resulting expression by
n\mapsto n-1
, gives

\displaystyle A(q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+q}\left(\frac{q^{3}}{1-q}-\sum_{n\geq 1}(-1)^{n}q^{2n^{2}+n+1}(1+q^{2n+1})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{q}{1-q^{2}}-\frac{q}{1+q}\sum_{n\geq 0}(-1)^{n}q^{2n^{2}+n}(1+q^{2n+1}).

Finally,

\sum_{s\geq 0}\chi(s)q^{\binom{s+1}{2}}=-\sum_{n\geq 0}(-1)^{n}q^{2n^{2}+n}(1+q^{2n+1}),

which again gives

A(q)=\frac{q}{1-q^{2}}+\frac{1}{1+q}\sum_{s\geq 0}\chi(s)q^{\binom{s+1}{2}+1}.

This is Theorem
1
.

4.3.
Proof of Theorem
2

The proof separates the derivation of the generating function from the coefficient estimate needed for the range assertion. We first isolate the false theta contribution.

Lemma 1
.

Let

C(q):=\frac{q}{1+q^{2}}\sum_{n\geq 0}\chi(n)q^{\binom{n+1}{2}}=\sum_{n\geq 0}c(n)q^{n}.

Also let
T_{r}:=\binom{r+1}{2}
for
r\geq 0
. Fix
m\geq 0
and
u\in\{1,2,\dots,8\}
. If

T_{8m+u-1}<n\leq T_{8m+u},

then

c(n)=\mu_{u}(n)\,\chi(n-1),

(4.10)

where
\mu_{u}(n)
is defined in (
1.13
). In particular,
c(n)=0
whenever
T_{8m+7}<n\leq T_{8m+8}
.

Proof.

For
m\geq 0
, set

t(m):=\frac{1}{1+q^{2}}\sum_{j=8m}^{8m+7}\chi(j)q^{\binom{j+1}{2}+1}.

Then
C(q)=\sum_{m\geq 0}t(m)
. Pairing the terms with indices
j
and
j+4
gives

t(m)=\sum_{j=8m}^{8m+3}\chi(j)q^{\binom{j+1}{2}+1}(1-q^{2}+q^{4}-\cdots+q^{4j+8}).

(4.11)

Indeed, since
\chi(j+4)=\chi(j)
and

\binom{j+5}{2}-\binom{j+1}{2}=4j+10,

we have

\frac{q^{\binom{j+1}{2}+1}+q^{\binom{j+5}{2}+1}}{1+q^{2}}=q^{\binom{j+1}{2}+1}(1-q^{2}+q^{4}-\cdots+q^{4j+8}).

Thus

\displaystyle t(m)

\displaystyle=-q^{T_{8m}+1}(1-q^{2}+q^{4}-\cdots+q^{32m+8})

\displaystyle\quad-q^{T_{8m+1}+1}(1-q^{2}+q^{4}-\cdots+q^{32m+12})

\displaystyle\quad+q^{T_{8m+2}+1}(1-q^{2}+q^{4}-\cdots+q^{32m+16})

\displaystyle\quad+q^{T_{8m+3}+1}(1-q^{2}+q^{4}-\cdots+q^{32m+20}).

The lowest power in
t(m)
is
T_{8m}+1
and the highest power is
T_{8m+7}-1=\binom{8m+8}{2}-1
, so the supports of the polynomials
t(m)
are pairwise disjoint. Hence
c(n)=[q^{n}]t(m)
whenever
T_{8m}<n\leq T_{8m+8}
.

Now assume
T_{8m+u-1}<n\leq T_{8m+u}
. The summand with index
s\in\{0,1,2,3\}
has sign
\chi(8m+s)
and contributes only to exponents

T_{8m+s}<n<T_{8m+s+4},\qquad n\equiv T_{8m+s}+1\pmod{2}.

Thus, before imposing the parity condition, the possible indices
s
are

s\in\begin{cases}\{0\},&u=1,\\
\{0,1\},&u=2,\\
\{0,1,2\},&u=3,\\
\{0,1,2,3\},&u=4,\\
\{1,2,3\},&u=5,\\
\{2,3\},&u=6,\\
\{3\},&u=7,\\
\varnothing,&u=8.\end{cases}

At the upper endpoints for the lines
u=5,6,7
, the extra boundary index listed above has the wrong parity and therefore contributes zero; thus the displayed list is harmless once the parity condition is imposed. Since
\chi(8m)=\chi(8m+1)=-1
and
\chi(8m+2)=\chi(8m+3)=1
, each actual contribution has sign
\chi(n-1)
: the summands with
s=0,3
contribute when
n
is odd, while those with
s=1,2
contribute when
n
is even. Therefore
c(n)
equals
\chi(n-1)
multiplied by the number of possible indices whose parity matches
n
. Counting these indices gives exactly the factor
\mu_{u}(n)
in (
4.10
).
∎

Proof of Theorem
2
.

First we prove the generating-function formula. By (
1.7
) and Theorem
1
,

\displaystyle(1+q^{2})B(q)

\displaystyle=(1+q)A(q)-\frac{q^{4}}{1-q^{3}}

\displaystyle=(1+q)\left(\frac{q}{1-q^{2}}+\frac{1}{1+q}\sum_{n\geq 0}\chi(n)q^{\binom{n+1}{2}+1}\right)-\frac{q^{4}}{1-q^{3}}

\displaystyle=\frac{q}{1-q}+q\sum_{n\geq 0}\chi(n)q^{\binom{n+1}{2}}-\frac{q^{4}}{1-q^{3}}.

Dividing by
1+q^{2}
and simplifying the rational part,

\frac{q}{(1-q)(1+q^{2})}-\frac{q^{4}}{(1-q^{3})(1+q^{2})}=\frac{q+q^{2}+q^{3}-q^{4}}{(1-q)(1+q^{2})(1+q+q^{2})},

gives (
1.9
).

It remains to prove the range assertion. Let

R(q):=\frac{q+q^{2}+q^{3}-q^{4}}{(1-q)(1+q^{2})(1+q+q^{2})}.

Then

\displaystyle R(q)

\displaystyle=\frac{q+q^{2}-q^{4}+q^{5}+2q^{6}-q^{7}-q^{8}+2q^{9}+q^{10}-q^{11}}{1-q^{12}},

so

[q^{N}]R(q)\in\begin{cases}\{0,-1\}&\ \text{if\ }N\equiv 0,3\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 4),\\
\{1,2\}&\ \text{if\ }N\equiv 1,2\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 4).\end{cases}

(4.12)

On the other hand, Lemma
1
implies

[q^{N}]C(q)\in\begin{cases}\{0,1,2\}&\ \text{if\ }N\equiv 0,3\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 4),\\
\{0,-1,-2\}&\ \text{if\ }N\equiv 1,2\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 4).\end{cases}

(4.13)

Since
B(q)=R(q)+C(q)
, equations (
4.12
) and (
4.13
) give

[q^{N}]B(q)\in\begin{cases}\{0,-1\}+\{0,1,2\},&N\equiv 0,3\pmod{4},\\
\{1,2\}+\{0,-1,-2\},&N\equiv 1,2\pmod{4},\end{cases}

and both displayed sumsets are contained in
\{-1,0,1,2\}
. This completes the proof of Theorem
2
.
∎

.

Setting
z=0
in (

) and (

from (

) for every

.

.

By Theorem

,

6.
A quantum modular consequence

The direct false theta form above also gives the quantum modular interpretation of the first signed generating function. Let
q=e^{2\pi i\tau}
and define

\Theta(\tau):=\frac{q^{1/8}}{1-q}-q^{-7/8}(1+q)A(q).

By Theorem
1
, or equivalently by the last displayed formula in the direct derivation,

\Theta(\tau)=q^{1/8}F(-1,q),\qquad F(-1,q)=\sum_{n\geq 0}(-1)^{n}q^{2n^{2}+n}(1+q^{2n+1}).

(6.1)

Let
\psi
be the odd periodic function modulo
8
defined by

\psi(r)=\begin{cases}1,&r\equiv 1,3\pmod{8},\\
-1,&r\equiv 5,7\pmod{8},\\
0,&r\equiv 0,2,4,6\pmod{8}.\end{cases}

Then

F(-1,q)=\sum_{r\geq 1}\psi(r)q^{(r^{2}-1)/8}.

(6.2)

Indeed, the substitutions
r=4n+1
and
r=4n+3
in the two summands of
F(-1,q)
give exactly the four nonzero residue classes of
\psi
modulo
8
.

We now place
\Theta
in the framework of partial theta series with periodic
coefficients. Let

\vartheta_{\psi}(z):=\sum_{r\geq 0}\psi(r)e^{2\pi izr^{2}/16}.

This is the partial theta series
\theta_{f}(z)
of
[
7
]
with
period
M=8
and coefficient function
f=\psi
. Since
\psi
is odd and
periodic modulo
8
, their theorem implies that
\vartheta_{\psi}(z)
is a
strong quantum modular form (see
[
7
, pp 453]
) of weight
1/2
on
\mathbb{Q}
, with respect to
the group
\Gamma_{8}=\Gamma_{1}(16)
. Moreover,
\Theta(\tau)=\vartheta_{\psi}(2\tau)
. Consequently,
\Theta
is also a strong quantum modular form of weight

1/2
on
\mathbb{Q}
, with respect to the pullback of
\Gamma_{1}(16)
under
the dilation
\tau\mapsto 2\tau
, where for
N\geq 1

\Gamma_{1}(N):=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z}):c\equiv 0\!\!\pmod{N},\,a\equiv d\equiv 1\!\!\pmod{N}\right\}.

The associated weight
3/2
unary theta series, or shadow, is

\Theta_{\psi}(z):=\sum_{r\in\mathbb{Z}}r\psi(r)e^{2\pi izr^{2}/16}.

(6.3)

In particular, the quantum modularity of
\Theta(\tau)
is governed by the
Eichler integral attached to the unary theta function

\Theta_{\psi}(z)
. More explicitly,
it is shown in
[
7
]
that the obstruction function for

\vartheta_{\psi}
is given by an Eichler integral of
\Theta_{\psi}
. Define the non-holomorphic Eichler integral associated with

\Theta_{\psi}

\widehat{\Theta}_{\psi}(\tau):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{8i}}\int_{-\overline{\tau}}^{i\infty}\Theta_{\psi}(w)(w+\tau)^{-1/2}\,dw

then we have

\vartheta_{\psi}(\alpha)-\left(\vartheta_{\psi}|_{1/2,\nu}\gamma\right)(\alpha)=r_{\gamma,\psi}(\alpha),

(6.4)

where
\nu
is a multiplier,
f|_{k,\chi}\gamma
is the usual slash operator, and
the modular obstruction is

r_{\gamma,\psi}(\tau)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{8i}}\int_{\gamma^{-1}(i\infty)}^{i\infty}\Theta_{\psi}(w)(w-\tau)^{-1/2}\,dw,\quad\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}.

(6.5)

The function
r_{\gamma,\psi}
is holomorphic on the lower half-plane,
extends smoothly to the real line, and is real-analytic away from the singular
point
\gamma^{-1}(i\infty)
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
D=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
and set
\Gamma_{\Theta}=D^{-1}\Gamma_{1}(16)D\cap SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. Then
\Theta(\tau)
is a strong
quantum modular form of weight
1/2
on
\mathbb{Q}
with respect to

\Gamma_{\Theta}
. More precisely, we have

\Theta(\alpha)-(\Theta|_{1/2,\nu_{\Theta}}\delta)(\alpha)=R_{\delta,\psi}(\alpha),\quad\delta\in\Gamma_{\Theta},\quad\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}

where
\nu_{\Theta}(\delta)=\nu_{\psi}(D\delta D^{-1})
and
\,R_{\delta,\psi}=r_{D\delta D^{-1},\psi}(2\tau)
. The function
R_{\delta,\psi}
extends smoothly to the real line and is
real-analytic on
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{\delta^{-1}(i\infty)\}
.

Proof.

Let all notations be as in Theorem
3
. For
\delta\in\Gamma_{\Theta}
, since
\Theta(\tau)=\vartheta_{\psi}(2\tau)
and
\gamma(2\tau)=2\delta\tau
, the slash actions are compatible:

\left(\vartheta_{\psi}\big|_{\frac{1}{2},\nu_{\psi}}\gamma\right)(2\tau)=\left(\Theta\big|_{\frac{1}{2},\nu_{\Theta}}\delta\right)(\tau).

(6.6)

Thus (
6.4
) and (
6.6
) gives

\Theta(\alpha)-\left(\Theta\big|_{\frac{1}{2},\nu_{\Theta}}\delta\right)(\alpha)=r_{\gamma,\psi}(2\alpha)=:R_{\delta,\psi}(\alpha).

Since
r_{\gamma,\psi}
is real-analytic away from
\gamma^{-1}(i\infty)
,

R_{\delta,\psi}
is real-analytic away from
\delta^{-1}(i\infty)
. This
proves the result.
∎
