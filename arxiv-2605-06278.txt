Pace : Prune-And-Compress Ensemble Models

Abstract
Ensemble models achieve state-of-the-art performance on prediction tasks, but usually require aggregating a large number of weak learners. This can hinder deployment, interpretability, and downstream tasks such as robustness verification. Remedies to this issue fall into two main camps: pruning, which discards redundant learners, and compression, which generates new ones from scratch. We introduce Pace , a framework that interleaves these paradigms in a two-phase strategy. First, new learners are actively generated via a theoretically grounded procedure to enhance the diversity of the initial ensemble. When no more relevant learners can be found, a second phase of pruning is performed on this enriched ensemble. During both operations, Pace allows fine control on the faithfulness to the original ensemble. Experiments show that our method outperforms prior pruning and compression methods while offering principled control of faithfulness guarantees.

Introduction

Ensemble modeling is a widely used machine learning technique where several weak learners are aggregated into a single strong predictor
[
55
]
. It has achieved state-of-the-art performance across many applications, especially on tabular data
[
44
]
, capturing complex relationships while remaining robust to overfitting. Formally, an ensemble consists of a collection
\boldsymbol{h}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
of learners from a prescribed family, such as decision trees. Each learner
h\in\mathcal{H}
encodes functions
h_{y}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
assigning a score to possible prediction labels
y\in\mathcal{Y}
over a sample space
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, larger values indicating stronger support. The ensemble assigns a prediction label to any sample
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
queried via a majority vote parameterized by weights
\boldsymbol{w}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{+}
as

\textstyle V_{\boldsymbol{h}}(\boldsymbol{w},\mathbf{x})\in\argmax_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\sum_{h\in\boldsymbol{h}}w_{h}\cdot h_{y}(\mathbf{x}).

(1)

Recent machine learning competitions have shown that strong predictive performance with ensemble models often requires a large number of learners
[
44
]
. At the same time, popular training methods such as random forests and boosting often produce redundant learners due to their heuristic nature
[
24
]
. This redundancy increases memory footprint and inference time, which are critical limitations in latency-sensitive and resource-constrained deployment settings
[
13
,
30
,
34
]
. These issues are amplified at scale, where even small per-query overheads can translate into substantial computational and energy costs. Large ensembles also complicate downstream audit and explanation tasks, including the computation of optimal counterfactual explanations
[
48
]
and robustness verification
[
10
]
. They may further reduce interpretability by increasing decision complexity, measured as the number of conditions evaluated to produce a prediction
[
33
]
. Finally, they can leak more information to adversaries: for example, trained random forests can be used to reconstruct much of their training data, with attack success strongly depending on the number and depth of trees
[
20
]
.

Ensemble Pruning and Compression.

To address these issues, several studies have proposed
pruning
strategies that assign a new set of sparse weights to a trained ensemble model, aiming to discard redundant learners
[
64
]
. A central requirement in this process is to preserve the predictive behavior of the original ensemble, either empirically on a finite set of samples, or formally over all samples in a region of interest. We refer to the latter requirement as
faithfulness
, and focus on methods that provide such guarantees. Although recent pruning approaches can certify faithfulness
[
19
]
, their pruning potential is limited by the fact that they can only reweight or discard existing learners. Another line of research instead focuses on
compressing
the initial ensemble by the active generation of new learners. Some approaches in this direction can also preserve faithfulness, but typically require producing learners that are more complex than the original ones and no longer belong to the prescribed family
\mathcal{H}

[
61
]
. Other approaches allow sticking to the original family of learners
[
66
]
, but achieve compression without faithfulness guarantees, thereby potentially altering prediction and generalization capabilities. A thorough review of these techniques is provided in Appendix
A
.

Contributions.

This paper introduces
Pace
(Prune-And-Compress Ensemble), a novel framework that unifies pruning and compression techniques for ensemble models while providing flexible, yet rigorous, control over faithfulness enforcement. Our method is built upon the following contributions:

1.

We show that pruning and compression are complementary rather than competing paradigms. Based on this observation, we propose a unified framework that first actively generates new learners to help compress the ensemble, and then prunes the redundant ones generated along the way.

2.

We leverage column generation to provide a principled strategy for generating new learners along with a stopping criterion. Thanks to this design, the enriched ensemble has greater potential for subsequent pruning.

3.

Within this unified framework, we extend prior notions of faithfulness to target only regions that are meaningful in practice. This enables a trade-off between ad hoc approximations of the initial decision boundary with no faithfulness guarantees, and exact imitation, which can otherwise limit compression.

4.

Numerical experiments suggest that
Pace
achieves stronger compression and pruning performance than prior methods, while providing finer control over faithfulness.

Our exposition is organized as follows.
Section

2
gives a global overview of
Pace
, with the core methodologies and definitions. Technical details are presented in
Sections

3
and
4
. The performance of our method on practical problems is then assessed in
Section

5
. Finally,
Section

6
discusses limitations and future research directions. Appendices gather additional technical details, material to reproduce our experiments, and further numerical analyzes.

2
Prune-and-Compress Framework for Ensemble Models

The gold standard for obtaining a compact and faithful representation of a weighted ensemble
(\boldsymbol{h}^{o},\boldsymbol{w}^{o})
is to identify a new pair
(\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{w})
achieving the optimal value in the following problem:

\textstyle\min_{\boldsymbol{w}\geq\mathbf{0}}\ \|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{0}\ \ \text{s.t.}\ \ V_{\boldsymbol{h}}(\boldsymbol{w},\mathbf{x})=V_{\boldsymbol{h}^{o}}(\boldsymbol{w}^{o},\mathbf{x})\ \ \forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}^{o},

(
P
)

where the
\ell_{0}
-norm counts the number of nonzero weights, and the constraints enforce faithfulness over a region
\mathcal{X}^{o}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
of interest. This ideal approach raises two key difficulties. First, it requires searching over possible subsets of learners in
\mathcal{H}
, which itself can be very large or even infinite. Second, even for a fixed subset of learners, Problem (

P

) remains challenging because of the combinatorial nature of the
\ell_{0}
-norm objective and the large, potentially infinite, set of faithfulness constraints.

Pruning and compression techniques can both be viewed as surrogate strategies for approximately addressing this ideal task. On the one hand, pruning restricts its focus to the original ensemble
\boldsymbol{h}=\boldsymbol{h}^{o}
and computes a one-shot solution of Problem (

P

) to obtain a sparser set of weights. However, fixing the ensemble inherently limits pruning capabilities. On the other hand, compression actively generates a new ensemble
\boldsymbol{h}
, but disregards sparsification of the associated weights.
Both methods usually enforce faithfulness over the whole region
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{X}^{o}
, which can lead to stringent constraints that degrade pruning and compression performances. Motivated by these observations,
Pace
combines the strengths of pruning and compression techniques, together with a finer control of faithfulness. Its main ingredients are outlined below and in
Figure

1
, with further details given in
Sections

3
and
4
.

Method backbone.

Pace
is structured in two phases. First, it actively generates new learners to enrich the original ensemble, aiming to improve the ensemble’s potential for downstream pruning task in a second phase, where a one-shot solution to Problem (

P

) is computed using the augmented ensemble. Specifically,
Pace
starts with
\boldsymbol{h}=\boldsymbol{h}^{o}
and searches for any
improving learner
in the set

\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{h})=\{h\in\mathcal{H}:\ell(\boldsymbol{h}\cup\{h\})<\ell(\boldsymbol{h})\},

(2)

where a larger value of the measure
\smash{\ell:2^{\mathcal{H}}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}}
indicates stronger potential for the downstream pruning operation. Instead of directly relying on the optimal value of the pruning Problem (

P

) to quantify
\ell(\boldsymbol{h})
, this measure is rather defined as the optimal value to its relaxation

\textstyle\min_{\boldsymbol{w}\geq\mathbf{0}}\ \|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{1}\ \ \text{s.t.}\ \ V_{\boldsymbol{h}}(\boldsymbol{w},\mathbf{x})=V_{\boldsymbol{h}^{o}}(\boldsymbol{w}^{o},\mathbf{x})\ \ \forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}^{o},

(
R
)

involving an
\ell_{1}
-norm surrogate. Stated otherwise, any
h\in\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{h})
can be added to the current ensemble
\boldsymbol{h}
to improve the optimal value achieved by Problem (

R

). The main advantage of this definition is that this relaxation can be cast as a linear program. Building on column generation techniques
[
16
]
, we provide a principled and efficient strategy to retrieve improving learners, together with a theoretically-grounded criterion to verify that
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{h})=\emptyset
, leading to the following guarantee.

Pruning guarantee:
When
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{h})=\emptyset
, no new learner in
\mathcal{H}
can be added to the current ensemble
\boldsymbol{h}
to improve the optimal value achieved by Problem (

R

).

Once this condition is met, the enriched ensemble
\boldsymbol{h}
is guaranteed to contain all necessary learners to achieve the best possible optimal value in (

R

), and is thus expected to offer strong pruning capabilities via Problem (

P

).
Pace
then carries out a pruning phase through a one-shot solution of Problem (

P

) to assign the sparsest possible set of weights in this enriched ensemble. Overall,
Pace
benefits from more flexibility in the choice of learners than pruning techniques, and also accounts for sparsification of their weights, which is not considered in compression techniques.

Faithfulness management.

Instead of enforcing faithfulness over an empirical set of samples
\mathcal{X}^{o}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
when solving Problems (

P

) and (

R

),
Pace
enforces more global faithfulness guarantees. After solving the problem at hand, the method searches for
separating samples
in the region

\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{h}}(\boldsymbol{w})=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{\eta}\cap\mathcal{X}_{\delta}:V_{\boldsymbol{h}}(\boldsymbol{w},\mathbf{x})\neq V_{\boldsymbol{h}^{o}}(\boldsymbol{w}^{o},\mathbf{x})\},

(3)

where the predictions of the current ensemble
(\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{w})
and the initial one
(\boldsymbol{h}^{o},\boldsymbol{w}^{o})
disagree. Any separating sample found is added to
\mathcal{X}^{o}
, and the problem is solved again to update the weights in the current pair
(\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{w})
. This process is repeated until no more separating sample can be found. Unlike prior approaches
[
19
]
retrieving separating samples over the entire space
\mathcal{X}
,
Pace
controls faithfulness guarantees over a region of practical interest, defined from the intersection of the region

\textstyle\mathcal{X}_{\eta}=\big\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}\mid\textstyle\sum_{h\in\boldsymbol{h}^{o}}\ w^{o}_{h}\cdot(h_{y^{o}}(\mathbf{x})-h_{\tilde{y}}(\mathbf{x}))>\eta\ \ \forall\tilde{y}\neq y^{o}\big\}

(4)

capturing samples where the initial ensemble takes a prediction
y^{o}=V_{\boldsymbol{h}^{o}}(\boldsymbol{w}^{o},\mathbf{x})
with a
confidence
margin
\eta\geq 0
compared to alternative classes, and the region

\textstyle\mathcal{X}_{\delta}=\big\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}\mid\sum_{h\in\mathcal{T}}b_{h}(\mathbf{x})\geq\delta\big\}

(5)

where
b_{h}(\mathbf{x})\geq 0
denotes
plausibility
score of a sample
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
with respect to the data-distribution. This score is computed through trees
h\in\mathcal{T}
of an isolation forest that is broadly employed in the literature to identify out-of-distribution samples
[
39
,
40
]
. Intuitively, outliers are typically isolated within sparse leaves and after only a few splits in a decision tree, therefore receiving low plausibility scores.
This faithfulness control is motivated by two complementary considerations. On the one hand, models with comparable accuracy may still disagree on individual predictions, so exact faithfulness of the original ensemble in low-confidence regions can preserve an arbitrary choice among similarly valid alternatives. On the other hand, plausibility restriction prevents faithfulness constraints from being driven by out-of-distribution samples, where preserving the original ensemble’s predictions is of limited practical relevance. In this way, relaxing faithfulness over irrelevant regions allows for potentially stronger pruning capabilities during
Pace
, with the following guarantee:

Faithfulness guarantee:
When
\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{h}}(\boldsymbol{w})=\emptyset
, the solution
(\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{w})
of the Problem (

P

) or (

R

) at hand is faithful to the initial ensemble
(\boldsymbol{h}^{o},\boldsymbol{w}^{o})
over the region
\mathcal{X}_{\eta}\cap\mathcal{X}_{\delta}
.

This novel definition provides finer control over the region where faithfulness is enforced, potentially achieving stronger pruning capabilities during
Pace
, based on two meaningful parameters,
\eta\geq 0
and
\delta\geq 0
. Setting
\eta=\delta=0
recovers the case where faithfulness is enforced over the whole space
\mathcal{X}
. In practice,
Pace
opts for a constraint programming formulation of the region
\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{h}}(\boldsymbol{w})
, allowing to retrieve separating samples or prove that no more exist with enhanced computational performance compared state-of-the-art formulations previously proposed in the literature for similar tasks.

3
Building Improving Learners via Column Generation

A key feature of
Pace
, which distinguishes it from previous approaches, is that it first augments the initial ensemble
\boldsymbol{h}^{o}
with improving learners before performing the final pruning step. The goal is to produce an enriched ensemble with learners that make stronger compression possible later on. We measure this potential through the optimal value of Problem (

R

), and use column generation to guide the selection of new learners in a principled way.

Improving learners detection.

From Definition (
2
), an improving learner is any new
h\in\mathcal{H}
that can be added to the current ensemble
\boldsymbol{h}
being augmented during the first phase of
Pace
to reduce the optimal value achieved by Problem (

R

). To identify them, we observe that the latter problem can be seen as a restricted version of the extended formulation

\textstyle\min_{\boldsymbol{w}\geq\mathbf{0}}\ \|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{1}\ \ \text{s.t.}\ \ V_{\mathcal{H}}(\boldsymbol{w},\mathbf{x})=V_{\boldsymbol{h}^{o}}(\boldsymbol{w}^{o},\mathbf{x})\ \ \forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}^{o},

(
R_{\text{ext}}
)

involving all possible learners from the family
\mathcal{H}
.
1
1

1

Our method accommodates scenarios where
\mathcal{H}
is of infinite cardinality, as the theoretical results from the semi-infinite linear programming literature allow to adapt column generation to such infinite-dimensional settings
[
42
]
.
By design, this extended formulation achieves a better optimal value than Problem (

R

) since it benefits from more flexibility in the choice of learners. Hence, generating an improving learner amounts to checking whether there exists some
h\in\mathcal{H}
such that the inequalities
\ell(\mathcal{H})\leq\ell(\boldsymbol{h}\cup h)<\ell(\boldsymbol{h})
hold, reminding that
\ell(\cdot)
denotes the optimal value of Problem (

R

) for a given ensemble of learners. No more improving learners exist once
\ell(\mathcal{H})=\ell(\boldsymbol{h})
. Interestingly, Problem (

R

) can be cast into a linear program as

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\min_{\boldsymbol{w}\geq\mathbf{0}}&\sum_{h\in\boldsymbol{h}}w_{h}\\
\text{s.t.}&\sum_{h\in\boldsymbol{h}}w_{h}\big(h_{y^{o}}(\mathbf{x})-h_{y}(\mathbf{x})\big)\geq 1&\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}^{o},\ \forall y\neq y^{o},\end{array}\right.

(
R
)

where
y^{o}=V_{\boldsymbol{h}^{o}}(\boldsymbol{w}^{o},\mathbf{x})
denotes the class predicted by the original ensemble. Each constraint enforces faithfulness for a sample
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}^{o}
through the expanded expression of the majority vote
V_{\boldsymbol{h}}(\boldsymbol{w},\mathbf{x})=V_{\boldsymbol{h}^{o}}(\boldsymbol{w}^{o},\mathbf{x})
in (
1
). Note that the right-hand side in the constraints can be set arbitrarily to one since the problem is scale-invariant with respect to the variable
\boldsymbol{w}\geq\mathbf{0}
. A similar linear program reformulation holds for the extended formulation (

R
ext

). Building on column generation theory, we then establish the following result.

Proposition 1
.

Consider Problem (

R

) constructed for some ensemble
\boldsymbol{h}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
, and define

c^{\star}=\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\ 1-\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}^{o}}\sum_{y\neq y^{o}}v_{\mathbf{x},y}\cdot\big(h_{y^{o}}(\mathbf{x})-h_{y}(\mathbf{x})\big),

(6)

where
v_{\mathbf{x},y}\geq 0
is the optimal solution in the dual of Problem (

R

) associated with the constraint defined for some pair
(\mathbf{x},y)\in\mathcal{X}^{o}\times\mathcal{Y}
. If
c^{\star}<0
, any minimizer to Problem (
6
) is an improving learner verifying
h\in\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{h})
. Otherwise, we have
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{h})=\emptyset
.

Proof of this property is provided in Appendix
B

pricing
Problem (
6
) determines the marginal modification in the measure
\ell(\cdot)
. If this quantity is strictly negative, any solution
h\in\mathcal{H}
ensures a strict decrease
\ell(\boldsymbol{h}\cup h)<\ell(\boldsymbol{h})
, whereas when it is non-negative, no new improving learner can be generated and we have
\ell(\mathcal{H})=\ell(\boldsymbol{h})
, i.e., the optimal value of Problem (

R

) coincides with that of the extended formulation (

R

). Solving this pricing problem is then sufficient to identify improving learners during
Pace
or to detect that no more remain, meaning that the pruning guarantee stated in
Section

2
is met.

Pricing problem as a learning task.

At first glance, the pricing Problem (
6
) may appear challenging, as it involves optimizing over all possible learners in the family
\mathcal{H}
. However, borrowing a shift in perspective from the LP-boosting literature
[
14
]
, we observe that it can be reformulated as

\max_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\ \sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}^{o}}\sum_{y\neq y^{o}}v_{\mathbf{x},y}\cdot\big(h_{y^{o}}(\mathbf{x})-h_{y}(\mathbf{x})\big),

(7)

by switching the objective direction and discarding the constant term. This reformulation can be interpreted as a search for a new learner
h\in\mathcal{H}
that maximizes the margin between the score assigned to the class
y^{o}=V_{\boldsymbol{h}^{o}}(\boldsymbol{w}^{o},\mathbf{x})
predicted by the original ensemble and that assigned to any alternative class
y\neq y^{o}
across all samples in
\mathcal{X}^{o}
, weighted by the value of solutions in the dual of Problem (

R

). Consequently, evaluating the condition in
Proposition

1
can be equivalently performed by training a new learner
h\in\mathcal{H}
on a re-weighted version of
\mathcal{X}^{o}
with a particular loss function. This interpretation is particularly clear for binary classification where
\mathcal{Y}=\{-1,+1\}
. In this case, the pricing problem can be equivalently expressed as

\max_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}^{o}}v_{\mathbf{x},-y^{o}}\cdot h_{y^{o}}(\mathbf{x}),

(8)

and corresponds to a classical weighted prediction score maximization for a target label
[
14
, Sec. 4]
.

The pricing Problem (
6
) must be solved optimally to guarantee that the condition
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{h})=\emptyset
terminating the generation of improving learners is correctly evaluated via
Proposition

1
during
Pace
. For some families of learners, exact algorithmic methods
[
15
]
exist, but they might be computationally intensive. Alternatively, it is possible to replace the exact pricing with a heuristic, such as CART
[
5
]
, yielding an approximate solution to the pricing Problem (
7
) at a low computational cost. Note that the heuristic or exact nature of the solution to this pricing problem solely drives the enrichment of the ensemble performed during the first phase of
Pace
, but does not impact its faithfulness guarantees.

4
Retrieving Separating Samples to Achieve Faithfulness

Iteratively, after each solution of Problem (

P

) or (

R

),

Pace
searches for separating samples using constraint programming (CP) techniques. For each pair
(y^{o},y)\in\mathcal{Y}^{2}
of distinct labels, it searches for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{h}}(\boldsymbol{w})
such that
V_{\boldsymbol{h}^{o}}(\boldsymbol{w}^{o},\mathbf{x})=y^{o}
and
V_{\boldsymbol{h}}(\boldsymbol{w},\mathbf{x})=y
. It either returns one such separating sample per pair, or certifies that none exists, i.e.,
\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{h}}(\boldsymbol{w})=\emptyset
and the algorithmic cycle stops. More precisely, this CP-based separation approach builds on the mixed-integer formulation introduced by
19
for a similar task for families
\mathcal{H}
of decision tree learners. However, it differs in two important ways: (i) it uses a constraint-based separation enforcement and avoids cumbersome linearization of logical constraints to get the most out of CP primitives, accelerating separating sample retrieval by several orders of magnitude, and (ii) it accommodates our novel notion of faithfulness that incorporates the notion of confidence of the original ensemble and the plausibility of the separating samples. We present the CP formulation below and refer to Appendix
C
for further technical details.

Modeling ensemble predictions.

A key ingredient in our CP-based formulation of the region
\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{h}}(\boldsymbol{w})
is a new mathematical model for the prediction score
h_{y}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
of learners in the family
\mathcal{H}
of decision trees. For such learners, denote by
\mathcal{V}_{h}
the set of leaves of a decision tree
h\in\mathcal{H}
, by
\smash{\Gamma_{hv}^{+}}
(resp.
\smash{\Gamma_{hv}^{-}}
) the set of index-scalar pairs
(i,a)
such that the
i
-th entry of some
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
must be greater (resp. smaller) than
a\in\mathbb{R}
to reach leaf
v\in\mathcal{V}_{h}
, and by
v_{y}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
the leaf prediction score for each label
y\in\mathcal{Y}
.
Then, a relation
r_{hy}=h_{y}(\mathbf{x})
can be modeled for any sample
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
as

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{h}}v_{y}z_{hv}

\displaystyle=r_{hy}

(9a)

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{h}}z_{hv}

\displaystyle=1

(9b)

\displaystyle\textstyle z_{hv}

\displaystyle=1\textstyle\implies\big(\bigwedge_{(i,a)\in\Gamma_{hv}^{+}}x_{i}>a\big)\land\big(\bigwedge_{(i,a)\in\Gamma_{hv}^{-}}x_{i}\leq a\big)

\displaystyle\forall v\in\mathcal{V}_{h}

(9c)

using a set of binary variables
z_{hv}\in\{0,1\}
indicating whether the sample falls into leaf
v\in\mathcal{V}_{h}
of the tree
h\in\mathcal{H}
. Constraints (

Constraint-based separation.

Building on the mathematical model (
9
) for the prediction score of tree-based learners, we propose to enforce separation via the constraints

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{h\in\boldsymbol{h}^{o}}\ w^{o}_{h}\cdot(r_{hy^{o}}-r_{h\tilde{y}})

\displaystyle>0\quad\forall\tilde{y}\neq y^{o}

(10)

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{h\in\boldsymbol{h}}\ w_{h}\cdot(r_{hy}-r_{hy^{o}})

\displaystyle>0

(11)

defined for a pair
(y^{o},y)\in\mathcal{Y}^{2}
of distinct prediction labels. While constraint (
10
) enforces the prediction relation
y^{o}=V_{\boldsymbol{h}^{o}}(\boldsymbol{w}^{o},\mathbf{x})
for the initial ensemble, constraint (
11
) promotes an alternate class
y=V_{\boldsymbol{h}}(\boldsymbol{w},\mathbf{x})
for the current ensemble processed by
Pace
. Hence, any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
feasible for this problem is a separating sample. Prior state-of-the-art formulations of separation
[
19
]
rather opted for maximizing the left-hand side of constraint (
11

This usually increases runtime because both separation and faithfulness require solving the optimization problem to optimality: first to find the maximal disagreement sample, and then to prove that no sample with positive objective exists. In contrast, we opt for a constraint-based separation returning
any
separating sample. This paradigm reduces the search of separation samples to a feasibility problem: any feasible solution is a separating sample, and infeasibility certifies faithfulness for the considered pair of distinct labels.

Confidence and plausibility regions.

With constraints (
10
)-(
11

\mathcal{X}

\mathcal{X}_{\eta}
involved in the definition of

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{h\in\boldsymbol{h}^{o}}\ w^{o}_{h}\cdot(r_{hy^{o}}-r_{h\tilde{y}})

\displaystyle>\eta\quad\forall\tilde{y}\neq y^{o}

(12)

\mathcal{X}_{\delta}
involved in the definition of
\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{h}}(\boldsymbol{w})
by introducing analogous constraints to (
9

\mathcal{T}
capturing the plausibility scores, together with the additional constraint

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{h\in\mathcal{T}}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{h}}b_{hv}\cdot z_{hv}\geq\delta,

(13)

where the coefficients
b_{hv}\geq 0
encode the corrected path length associated with each leaf
v\in\mathcal{V}_{h}
of each tree
h\in\mathcal{T}
in the isolation forest. They can be pre-computed since
\mathcal{T}
is fixed throughout
Pace
.

Our experimental setup and the datasets used are described in

6
Conclusion

This paper introduced
Pace
, a framework that unifies compression and pruning techniques to provide a compact and faithful representation of trained ensemble models. In contrast to prior methodologies from the literature, the proposed method combines the active generation of new learners with a strong pruning operation based on the
\ell_{0}
-norm. In addition,
Pace
incorporates a flexible notion of faithfulness, allowing one to range from exact replication of the initial ensemble’s predictions to more relaxed requirements to obtain enhanced pruning capabilities. Numerical results demonstrate that
Pace
consistently achieves more effective pruning and compression than existing approaches, and that further improvements can be obtained through its finer control of faithfulness enforcement.

Limitations.

A first limitation of
Pace
lies in its computational cost, which is higher than that of previously proposed pruning-only or compression-only methods. This overhead is primarily driven by the combinatorial nature of the
\ell_{0}
-norm pruning problem solved after the active generation of new learners. Nevertheless, we have observed that the flexible control of faithfulness can help mitigate this cost. Second, the current implementation of
Pace
is restricted to ensembles composed of tree-based learners. However, the proposed strategy could be naturally extended to other families of learners, provided that an appropriate mathematical model of their prediction functions can be established. Finally, the proposed characterization of faithfulness in
Pace
could be further enriched by incorporating additional considerations into the generation of separating samples. Such extensions could be readily integrated into the current implementation, provided that appropriate mathematical models of these new considerations are available.

Perspectives.

This work opens several directions for future research. A first promising avenue is the study of methods for generating improving learners during the first phase of
Pace
. In particular, it would be valuable to investigate the impact of solving the underlying pricing problem either exactly or heuristically. Prior work on optimal decision tree design could be leveraged to explore this direction. Another way to further enhance
Pace
would be to refine the notion of ensemble complexity beyond simply counting the number of base learners. For instance, for tree-based ensembles, one could instead consider the total number of nodes or leaves the trees, which more accurately reflects the memory footprint of the learner. This notion could be readily incorporated into the current framework through an appropriate scaling of the learners’ weights. Finally, a natural extension of
Pace
would be to generate improving learners directly with respect to the
\ell_{0}
-norm pruning objective, rather than relying on its
\ell_{1}
-norm surrogate. In this context, branch-and-cut-and-price techniques, generalizing column generation to mixed-integer linear programs, could be adapted for this purpose. Although such an approach would likely increase the computational burden of the method, it would provide an optimal strategy for compressing a trained ensemble model with faithfulness guarantees.

Appendix A
Related Works

This appendix provides a review of related works on ensemble training, pruning, and compression techniques, as well as connections to other machine learning models beyond ensemble models.

A.1
Ensemble Model Training

Training an ensemble model amounts to building a collection of learners and weights using a given dataset. Historically, bagging techniques
[
6
]
were proposed for this task, in which learners are trained simultaneously on variations of the training data generated via bootstrap sampling to reduce prediction variance
[
29
]
. This paradigm has been largely replaced by more recent boosting methods, in which learners are built sequentially with a focus on reducing prediction error
[
22
]
. Two landmark algorithms in this vein are AdaBoost
[
56
]
and Extreme Gradient Boosting
[
24
]
, the latter being most commonly known as XGBoost. Both interpret the boosting task as functional gradient descent in the predictor space, pointing towards new learners that reduce prediction errors
[
23
]
.

While these strategies apply to abstract learners, particular effort has been devoted to tree-based learners, leading to random forest ensemble models
[
53
]
. Their structure can be leveraged within training methods to obtain strong aggregated predictors. For instance, combining bootstrap resampling with randomized feature selection at each tree split has been shown to decorrelate trees and reduce variance
[
7
]
. Related methods further increase randomization by selecting split thresholds at random, often improving computational efficiency and robustness
[
25
]
.

Another ensemble training technique comes from the linear programming literature, where the training task has been identified as a column generation procedure
[
14
]
. With this approach, training a new learner can be viewed as solving a pricing problem
[
62
]
, focusing on reducing the prediction error of previously generated learners. The parametrization of this pricing problem has led to different variants of LP-boosting methods, focusing on margin maximization
[
26
]
, nominal improvement from Lagrange duals
[
57
]
, or gradient values
[
12
]
. A unified view of these methods is provided in
[
1
]
.

A.2
Ensemble Model Pruning

Training methods for ensemble models typically generate a large number of learners that may be redundant. Various studies have proposed pruning techniques to reduce the number of learners while maintaining, or even improving, generalization performance. Early approaches relied on ordering and ranking strategies, in which individual learners are scored based on standalone accuracy or diversity, and only the top candidates are retained
[
8
,
46
]
. These methods can also be seen as a subset selection problem, explicitly searching for a collection of models whose joint predictions maximize a validation criterion
[
45
]
. Practical approaches rely on greedy forward-backward selection, local search, or metaheuristics
[
49
]
. More recently, optimization-based pruning methods have been proposed, trading this discrete subset selection problem for continuous weighting schemes. Typical examples include
\ell_{1}
-penalized weighting
[
36
]
, convex margin maximization
[
64
]
, or constrained optimization formulations that jointly optimize predictive accuracy and ensemble size
[
52
]
. Recently, studies have investigated the possibility of faithful ensemble pruning. While some have focused on achieving a suitable faithful-pruning tradeoff
[
2
,
38
]
, others rather focus on exact faithfulness guarantees via a separation problem
[
19
]
.

A.3
Ensemble Model Compression

Beyond pruning, a complementary line of work focuses on compression techniques, aiming to generate a new, compact ensemble model from scratch that approximates the behavior of a larger one. In particular, a few works have attempted to compress existing tree ensembles into a single decision tree. Some approximate approaches train a surrogate tree from synthetic data obtained by sampling pseudo-covariates and labeling them with predictions from the original ensemble, using a custom split-selection procedure to stabilize the resulting tree
[
66
]
. While they can yield substantial compression, they do not guarantee exact faithfulness to the original model. In contrast, a dynamic-programming algorithm can be devised to construct a single decision tree that is certifiably faithful to the original ensemble
[
61
]
. While this guarantees exact faithfulness, the objective is no longer to learn a simplified surrogate, but to recover an exact representation, which may remain significantly large in practice.

A.4
Beyond Ensemble Models

Apart from ensembles of learners, pruning and compression techniques have been proposed for more general machine learning models. Recently, a significant amount of research has focused on techniques to reduce the memory footprint of deep neural networks via impact-based
[
18
,
37
]
, optimization-based
[
4
,
65
]
, quantization-based
[
35
]
, or structural-based
[
47
]
methods. These approaches can be mainly categorized into unstructured pruning, where individual weights are removed based on their importance, and structured pruning, where entire neurons or filters are removed
[
41
]
. We refer to
[
11
]
for a broader overview of these connected works.

Appendix B
Technical Specifications on the Learner Generation

This appendix presents the main ingredients from the column generation literature relevant to the creation of new learners in
Pace
, along with a proof of
Proposition

1
. We refer the reader to
[
17
, Chapiter 2]
for a complete description of these techniques.

B.1
Column Generation Ingredients

Column generation is a technique for solving linear programs of the form

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lrl}\min&\mathbf{d}^{\top}\mathbf{w}\\
\text{s.t.}&\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w}&\geq\mathbf{b}\\
&\mathbf{w}&\geq\mathbf{0}.\end{array}\right.

(14)

Instead of directly addressing this formulation with the entire variable
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, column generation considers a restricted version of the problem defined as

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lrl}\min&\mathbf{d}_{I}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{I}\\
\text{s.t.}&\mathbf{A}_{I}\mathbf{w}_{I}&\geq\mathbf{b}\\
&\mathbf{w}_{I}&\geq\mathbf{0}\end{array}\right.

(15)

over a subset
I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
of the variable indices, where
\mathbf{d}_{I}
and
\mathbf{w}_{I}
(resp.
\mathbf{A}_{I}
) denote the restriction to the indices (resp. columns) indexed by
I
. The column generation procedure iteratively solves the restricted problem (
15
), and seeks a new index allowing to improve its optimal value when added to
I
. The operation is carried out by solving a so-called pricing problem of the form

\textstyle c^{\star}=\min_{i\in\{1,\dots,n\}}\ d_{i}-\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{v}^{\star},

(16)

where
\mathbf{v}^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
denotes the optimal solution to the dual of problem (
15
), given by

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lrl}\max&\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{v}\\
\text{s.t.}&\mathbf{A}^{\top}_{I}\mathbf{v}&\geq\mathbf{d}_{I}\\
&\mathbf{v}&\geq\mathbf{0}.\end{array}\right.

(17)

The quantity
c_{i}=d_{i}-\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{v}^{\star}
is referred to as
reduced cost
of the index
i
, and represents the marginal improvement in the optimal value of the restricted problem (
15
) when this index is added to the subset
I
. We have
c_{i}=0
whenever
i\in I
.
If
c^{\star}<0
, then the index that achieves the minimum value in (
16
) is added to
I
to improve the optimal value of the restricted problem (
15
), and the operation is repeated. Ultimately, one obtains
c^{\star}=0
, and no more indices can improve this optimal value. At this point, the solution to problem (
14
) can be retrieved from that of its restriction (
15
), and the column generation procedure terminates. This can occur even (and hopefully) if not all indices have been included in
I
.

B.2
Proof of
Proposition

1

Similarly to (

R

), problem (

R
ext

) can be expressed by instantiating the linear program (
14
) using
\mathbf{d}=\mathbf{1}
and
\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{1}
, together with a matrix
\mathbf{A}
with
|\mathcal{H}|
columns and
|\mathcal{X}^{o}|\cdot(|\mathcal{Y}|-1)
rows, where the entry corresponding to some
h\in\mathcal{H}
and
(\mathbf{x},y)\in\mathcal{X}^{o}\times\mathcal{Y}
is defined as

\mathbf{A}_{h,(\mathbf{x},y)}=h_{y^{o}}(\mathbf{x})-h_{y}(\mathbf{x})

(18)

with
y^{o}=V_{\boldsymbol{h}^{o}}(\boldsymbol{w}^{o},\mathbf{x})
. Note that the optimization variable in this problem is the vector of the base learner weights only, while the sample subset
\mathcal{X}^{o}
, the label classes
\mathcal{Y}
, and the set of base learners
\mathcal{H}
are fixed. In a similar fashion to how problem (
15
) is a restricted version of (
14
), problem (

R

) is a restricted version of (

R
ext

) where only a subset of base learners in the whole family
\mathcal{H}
is involved. It involves a reduced version of the vectors
\mathbf{d}
and
\mathbf{w}
(resp. matrix
\mathbf{A}
), where only the entries (resp. columns) of the base learners in the current ensemble
\boldsymbol{h}
are considered. Translating (
16
) to our setting, we obtain the pricing problem

c^{\star}=\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\ 1-\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{y\neq y^{o}}v_{\mathbf{x},y}\cdot\big(h_{y^{o}}(\mathbf{x})-h_{y}(\mathbf{x})\big)

(19)

by expanding the definitions of
\mathbf{d}
and
\mathbf{A}
as in
Proposition

1
. The conclusion on whether there exists an improving base learner is then obtained from the quantity
c^{\star}
as in the column generation procedure.

Appendix C
Technical Specifications on the Separation Problem

This appendix provides supplementary details on the formulation used in
Pace
to generate separating samples while solving Problems (

R

) and (

P

). A complete formulation of the separation problem is also provided, gathering all the ingredients described in
Section

4
.

C.1
Plausibility Score Computation

The plausibility score is modeled in
Pace
through an isolation forest
\mathcal{T}
as
A(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{h\in\mathcal{T}}b_{h}(\mathbf{x})
where
b_{h}(\mathbf{x})\geq 0
denotes the path length of a sample
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
through isolation tree
h
, i.e., the depth of the leaf reached, corrected using an adjustment factor based on the support of this leaf. Specifically, denote by
d_{h}(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{N}
the depth of the leaf reached by the sample and by
s_{h}(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{N}
the number of training samples that reached this leaf during the isolation forest building. Then, this corrected path length is given by

b_{h}(\mathbf{x})\;=\;\begin{cases}d_{h}(\mathbf{x})&s_{h}(\mathbf{x})\leq 1\\
d_{h}(\mathbf{x})+c(s_{h}(\mathbf{x})),&s_{h}(\mathbf{x})>1\end{cases}

(20)

where
c:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is the expected additional path length that would have been necessary to further isolate the sample
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
from the other training samples that reached the leaf. In practice, isolation forests express this quantity as

c(s)\;=\;\begin{cases}0,&s\leq 1\\
1,&s=2\\
2(\ln(s-1)+\gamma)-\tfrac{2(s-1)}{s},&s>2\end{cases}

(21)

where
\gamma\approx 0.57721
is the Euler-Mascheroni constant. Note that a scaled version

\textstyle\mathcal{X}_{\delta_{\text{scaled}}}=\big\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}\mid 2^{-A(\mathbf{x})/c_{\text{max}}}\leq\delta_{\text{scaled}}\big\}

(22)

of the plausibility region with some threshold
\delta_{\text{scaled}}\in[0,1]
is sometimes used in the literature where
c_{\max}=c(s_{\max})
accounts for the maximum number of training samples
s_{\max}\in\mathbb{N}
used to build each tree in the isolation forest. A simple transformation allows to recover our formulation since

2^{-A(\mathbf{x})/c(s_{\text{max}})}\leq\delta_{\text{scaled}}\iff A(\mathbf{x})\geq\delta

(23)

with
\delta=-c(s_{\text{max}})\cdot\log_{2}(\delta_{\text{scaled}})
.

C.2
Encoding Continuous Features

Each feature of a sample
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
may take values in a continuous domain, i.e.,
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
. Although CP solvers can only handle discrete variables, this technical limitation can be readily overcome. Indeed, tree-based learners only contain finitely many nodes, and therefore only involve finitely many split levels
\mathcal{X}_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
for the feature
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
. Consequently, one can pre-compute, for every feature
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, the finite set of possible split levels
\mathcal{X}_{i}
induced by the initial ensemble
(\boldsymbol{h}^{o},\boldsymbol{w}^{o})
given to
Pace
. A discrete variable
k_{i}\in\{1,\dots,|\mathcal{X}_{i}|\}
can then be introduced to encode the interval between consecutive elements of
\mathcal{X}_{i}
in which the feature value lies. In the special case of binary features, we have
\mathcal{X}_{i}=\{0,1\}
, while for categorical features, the number of split levels matches the number of categories. With this encoding, the CP solver becomes fully agnostic to the actual value of continuous features, and instead bases its search for separating points on the intervals defined by the split levels of the initial ensemble
(\boldsymbol{h}^{o},\boldsymbol{w}^{o})
and of the isolation forest
\mathcal{T}
. This induces no loss of accuracy, since any feature value
x_{i}\in[a,a^{\prime})
lying between two consecutive split levels
(a,a^{\prime})
of
\mathcal{X}_{i}
belongs to the same decision region of the tree, and is therefore indistinguishable from its perspective.

C.3
Encoding Continuous Coefficients

In a similar fashion to the encoding of continuous feature values, a continuous value can be taken by the entries of weights
(\boldsymbol{w}^{o},\boldsymbol{w})
from the initial and current ensemble involved in
Pace
, as well as by coefficients
c(s)
appearing in the definition of the plausibility score computation discussed in
Section

C.1
. Although they appear as constants in the separation problem, CP solvers only support discrete coefficients. These coefficients are used in the expression of the per-label confidence scores, which are themselves decision variables and must therefore be represented as integer-valued quantities. To overcome this issue, we rescale any continuous coefficient
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
appearing in the separation problem as

\alpha_{\text{scaled}}=\operatorname{round}\!\left(\alpha\cdot 10^{d}\right).

(24)

where
d\in\mathbb{N}
determines the decimal precision: the coefficient is first scaled by
10^{d}
and then rounded to the nearest integer. By default,
Pace
uses
d=9
. Note that the separation problem is invariant with respect to positive rescaling of the weights
(\boldsymbol{w}^{o},\boldsymbol{w})
and of the coefficients
c(s)
. Hence, this is done without loss of generality, provided that a similar rescaling of the parameters
\eta
and
\delta
involved in the definition of the regions
\mathcal{X}_{\eta}
and
\mathcal{X}_{\delta}
is performed. Note that such scaling has usually little impact on the numerical efficiency and stability of CP solvers used to tackle the separation problem.

C.4
Complete Separation Problem Formulation

With all the ingredients introduced in
Section

4
, the complete formulation of the separation problem used in
Pace
for some pair
(y^{o}{},y)\in\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}
of distinct labels reads

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rll}\sum_{h\in\boldsymbol{h}^{o}}w^{o}_{h}(h_{y^{o}}(\mathbf{x})-h_{\tilde{y}}(\mathbf{x}))>\eta&\forall\tilde{y}\neq y^{o}\\
\sum_{h\in\boldsymbol{h}\phantom{{}^{o}}}w_{h}(h_{y}(\mathbf{x})-h_{y^{o}}(\mathbf{x}))>0\\
\sum_{h\in\mathcal{T}}b_{h}(\mathbf{x})\geq\delta\\
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}\end{array}\right.

(25)

for fixed parameters
\eta\geq 0
and
\delta\geq 0
. Upon the model decision tree learner prediction functions as described by (
9
), the extended and practical formulation of this separation problem reads

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\sum_{h\in\boldsymbol{h}^{o}}w^{o}_{h}(r_{hy^{o}}-r_{h\tilde{y}})&>\eta&\forall\tilde{y}\neq y^{o}\\
\sum_{h\in\boldsymbol{h}\phantom{{}^{o}}}w_{h}(r_{hy}-r_{hy^{o}})&>0\\
\sum_{h\in\mathcal{T}}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{h}}b_{hv}\cdot z_{hv}&\geq\delta\\
\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{h}}v_{y}\cdot z_{hv}-r_{hy}&=0&\forall h\in\boldsymbol{h}^{o}\cup\boldsymbol{h}\cup\mathcal{T}\\
\textstyle\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{h}}z_{hv}&=1&\forall h\in\boldsymbol{h}^{o}\cup\boldsymbol{h}\cup\mathcal{T}\\
\lx@intercol z_{hv}=1\textstyle\implies\big(\bigwedge_{(i,a)\in\Gamma_{hv}^{+}}x_{i}>a\big)\land\big(\bigwedge_{(i,a)\in\Gamma_{hv}^{-}}x_{i}\leq a\big)\hfil\lx@intercol&\forall h\in\boldsymbol{h}^{o}\cup\boldsymbol{h}\cup\mathcal{T},\ \forall v\in\mathcal{V}_{h}\\
\lx@intercol\hfil r_{hy}\lx@intercol&\in\mathbb{N}&\forall h\in\boldsymbol{h}^{o}\cup\boldsymbol{h}\cup\mathcal{T},\ \forall v\in\mathcal{V}_{h}\\
\lx@intercol\hfil z_{hv}\lx@intercol&\in\{0,1\}&\forall h\in\boldsymbol{h}^{o}\cup\boldsymbol{h}\cup\mathcal{T},\ \forall v\in\mathcal{V}_{h}\\
\lx@intercol\hfil x_{i}\lx@intercol&\in\mathcal{X}_{i}&\forall i\in\{1,\dots,n\}\end{array}\right.

(26)

for a fixed pair
(y^{o}{},y)\in\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}
of distinct prediction labels. In this formulation, the discrete sets
\{\mathcal{X}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{X}_{n}\}
are obtained in pre-processing as described in
Section

C.2
from the split levels appearing in the original ensemble
(\boldsymbol{h}^{o},\boldsymbol{w}^{o})
. Similarly, all coefficients
(r_{hv},v_{y},b_{hv})
and sets
(\Gamma_{hv}^{-},\Gamma_{hv}^{+})
are obtained in pre-processing from the structure of the initial ensemble
\boldsymbol{h}^{o}
, the current ensemble
\boldsymbol{h}
, and the isolation forest
\mathcal{T}
. Continuous coefficients are rescaled as described in
Section

C.3
if necessary. Any addition of a new improving learner
h\in\mathcal{H}
to the current ensemble
\boldsymbol{h}
during
Pace
can be done online by adding the corresponding sets of variables
\{z_{hv}\}_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{h}}
and
\{r_{hy}\}_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{h}}
together with their associated constraints in the model, without re-creating it from scratch.

E.3
Finer Analysis on the Generation of Separating Samples

In this paragraph, we further analyze the constraint-based formulation (
11
) used in
Pace
to retrieve separating samples. We compare it to an objective-based separation problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\textstyle\max_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}&\textstyle\sum_{h\in\boldsymbol{h}\phantom{{}^{o}}}\ w_{h}\cdot(r_{hy}-r_{hy^{o}})\\
\text{s.t.}&\textstyle\sum_{h\in\boldsymbol{h}^{o}}\ w^{o}_{h}\cdot(r_{hy^{o}}-r_{h\tilde{y}})&>0\quad\forall\tilde{y}\neq y^{o}\end{array}\right.

(27)

which corresponds to prior separation formulations proposed in the literature
[
19
]
.
These formulations opt for different trade-offs:

•

Objective-based separation seeks maximal disagreement in prediction, typically yielding fewer but higher-quality separating samples, at the expense of higher computational cost.

•

Constraint-based separation seeks any separating sample, often resulting in more separating samples generated, but at a lower computational cost since only feasibility is required.

This trade-off is investigated in
Figures

8
and
9
where we report the overall solving time and number of samples generated when solving Problem (

R

) until faithfulness can be certified globally over
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, setting
\boldsymbol{h}=\boldsymbol{h}^{o}
and initializing
\mathcal{X}^{o}
with the dataset used to train the initial ensemble
(\boldsymbol{h}^{o},\boldsymbol{w}^{o})
.
Results show that constraint-based separation consistently achieves the lowest computational time, although it requires generating more separating samples than its objective-based counterpart. Combining these approaches by enforcing separation with both an objective and a constraint does not yield meaningful improvements in runtime, even if the number of generated samples is reduced compared to the constraint-based separation. The constraint-based approach appears to be the best choice overall.

trees of depth

. Results aggregate all the 11 datasets listed in

D
.

E.4
Finer Analysis on the Generation of Improving Learners

In this paragraph, we further analyze the first phase where improving learners are generated by
Pace
. We conduct experiments on the
Ionosphere
dataset, compressing random forest and AdaBoost ensembles with
n_{\text{est}}\in\{50,100,200,500\}
trees of depths
d\in\{5,7\}
.
Figures

and
11
report the evolution of the reduced cost in
Proposition

1
, the optimal value of Problem (

R

), the cumulative number of separation problems solved, and the cumulative number of separating samples generated, as a function of the number of improving learners generated by
Pace
.

First, the top-left plots in
Figures

10
and
11
show that the reduced cost consistently converges toward zero across all tested configurations, indicating diminishing opportunities for improving the optimal value of Problem (

R

) as new improving learners are added. This trend is reflected in the top-right plots, where the optimal value of Problem (

R

) progressively decreases. Convergence appears slower for more complex ensembles with deeper, more numerous trees. In some cases, the reduced cost does not reach zero due to the 20-hour time limit, which interrupts the learner generation phase of
Pace
.

The bottom plots in
Figures

and
11
show that most separating samples are generated during the very first iteration of the learner generation phase in
Pace
. In subsequent iterations, both the number of separation problems solved and the number of newly generated samples per iteration stabilize, with roughly linear growth as the number of improving learners increases. This suggests that relevant separating samples to enforce faithfulness are largely captured when pruning the initial ensemble given to
Pace
. Subsequent iterations mainly perform incremental improvements with new learners incorporated into the ensemble, and only minor corrections to ensure faithfulness are performed.
