Introduction

Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) is a rare, life‐threatening thrombotic microangiopathy characterized by thrombocytopenia, microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, and organ ischemia, caused by severe deficiency of the metalloprotease ADAMTS13 [1, 2]. Congenital TTP (cTTP, Upshaw–Schulman syndrome) results from biallelic ADAMTS13 (A Distegrin And Metalloproteinase with Thrombospondin type1 motif member13) mutations and represents < 5% of all TTP cases, while the acquired form is due to anti‐ADAMTS13 autoantibodies [2]. The disorder is extremely rare, with an estimated prevalence of 0.5–2.0 per million worldwide [3]. Pregnancy is a recognized trigger, as increased von Willebrand factor and reduced ADAMTS13 activity may precipitate acute microangiopathy in susceptible women [4, 5]. Without treatment, maternal and fetal mortality rates may reach 10% and 40%–50%, respectively, whereas prophylactic plasma therapy greatly improves outcomes [5, 6]. We report a successful full‐term pregnancy in a woman with cTTP managed with regular plasma infusions, followed by a brief literature review.

Case Presentation

A 32‐year‐old woman with a clinically diagnosed case highly consistent with cTTP had been followed since the age of 3, when she presented with hematuria, anemia, and thrombocytopenia. Genetic sequencing of the ADAMTS13 gene was not available at our center. However, the combination of severe ADAMTS13 deficiency without detectable inhibitor, childhood‐onset recurrent thrombotic microangiopathy shown on renal biopsy, and long‐term responsiveness to plasma prophylaxis strongly supported a diagnosis of cTTP. Following her diagnosis, she was initiated on prophylactic fresh frozen plasma (FFP) infusions (2 units monthly), which maintained her in clinical remission without relapses until the age of 25.

Thereafter, she developed recurrent TTP episodes (three within 1 year), necessitating intensification of her regimen to 2 units every 2 weeks, which successfully stabilized her disease course.

At age 29, she attempted pregnancy. Approximately 5–6 weeks after a positive β‐hCG, she experienced an acute relapse requiring plasmapheresis. Despite stabilization and continuation of routine FFP prophylaxis, this pregnancy resulted in spontaneous abortion. A second attempt also ended in miscarriage at 10 weeks, accompanied by passage of clots. After 6 months, she tried for another pregnancy, and her prophylactic regimen was therefore escalated to FFP every 10 days.

Subsequently, her ADAMTS13 activity was measured and found to be within the normal range. ADAMTS13 activity and anti‐ADAMTS13 antibody levels were measured using enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). The reference range for ADAMTS13 activity was 40%–130%. Anti‐ADAMTS13 antibody levels were interpreted as negative (< 12 U/mL), borderline (12–15 U/mL), or positive (> 15 U/mL). After multidisciplinary consultation, she was counseled to proceed with another pregnancy. Although guidelines recommend ADAMTS13 monitoring every month during pregnancy, limited availability and high cost in Iran precluded regular testing. Instead, she was monitored with complete blood counts (CBC) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), while continuing FFP every 10 days.

During the third trimester, repeat ADAMTS13 testing revealed activity of 2.4%, prompting intensification of her prophylaxis to weekly FFP infusions. She subsequently carried the pregnancy to term without maternal complications such as hypertension or preeclampsia. Gestational diabetes mellitus developed but was effectively managed with dietary modification.

At 37 weeks + 4 days, she underwent elective cesarean delivery of a healthy male infant with Apgar scores of 8/10.

In the postpartum period, FFP was maintained every 10 days for 6 weeks, followed by tapering to every 14 days as part of her long‐term maintenance regimen. The patient also had a history of hypothyroidism, managed with levothyroxine 100 mg daily throughout pregnancy.

Discussion

cTTP is a rare autosomal recessive microangiopathy resulting from biallelic ADAMTS13 mutations, leading to severe deficiency of the von Willebrand factor‐cleaving protease [1, 2]. The estimated prevalence is 0.5–2.0 per million worldwide, with congenital cases comprising fewer than 5% of all TTP cases [1, 3]. East Asian and Finnish populations show the highest carrier rates, while Middle Eastern populations, including Iranians, have much lower prevalence, around 1 per million [3]. In Iran, national data are limited, but an existing registry of 114 patients, predominantly with acquired TTP, reported a remission rate of 75.5% with plasma exchange, relapses in 23.6%, and mortality in 26.3% [7]. ADAMTS13 testing was not routinely performed, preventing distinction between congenital and acquired forms.

Pregnancy is a recognized trigger of TTP, accounting for 10%–36% of cases, and up to 30% of pregnancy‐associated episodes occur in women with congenital disease [4, 5]. Physiologic increases in VWF levels and decreased ADAMTS13 activity during gestation contribute to microangiopathic exacerbation in susceptible patients [5, 6]. The disease most often presents in the second or third trimester or postpartum, when hemostatic stress is greatest [5, 8]. Clinical manifestations such as thrombocytopenia, hemolytic anemia, and hypertension may mimic preeclampsia or HELLP syndrome, complicating timely diagnosis. Without treatment, maternal mortality may approach 10% and fetal loss 40%–50% [4, 5]. Early recognition and prompt plasma replacement dramatically improve outcomes, with maternal survival exceeding 90% and fetal survival nearing 100% in adequately treated pregnancies [4, 8].

Management of cTTP focuses on ADAMTS13 replacement rather than immunosuppression. Regular infusions of FFP or plasma exchange replenish the deficient enzyme [4]. Recombinant ADAMTS13, recently approved, represents a promising targeted alternative, particularly for patients intolerant of plasma [4]. The International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH) recommends prophylactic plasma therapy throughout pregnancy in asymptomatic women with cTTP to maintain platelet counts and ADAMTS13 activity above relapse thresholds [9]. FFP at 10–15 mL/kg every 1–2 weeks is generally sufficient, but frequency should be individualized. Case reports and small series confirm that such prophylaxis prevents relapses and recurrent miscarriages [8].

In contrast, acquired TTP, driven by inhibitory antibodies, requires plasma exchange, corticosteroids, rituximab, and caplacizumab to suppress immune activity [9]. Women with prior acquired TTP may also require prophylactic plasma or caplacizumab if ADAMTS13 remains persistently < 10% before conception [6, 9]. Although both forms can relapse during pregnancy, cTTP tends to relapse predictably with physiologic stress, while acquired disease relapses variably. Thus, identifying congenital versus acquired etiology through ADAMTS13 testing and inhibitor assays is essential for individualized care [2].

Several cohort studies have characterized pregnancy‐associated cTTP and demonstrated the importance of early recognition and prophylactic plasma therapy. Fujimura et al. described 15 pregnancies in nine women with genetically confirmed Upshaw–Schulman syndrome, in whom thrombocytopenia typically developed during the second or third trimester and fetal loss occurred in 8 of 16 pregnancies, emphasizing both the severe obstetric risk and the importance of ADAMTS13 testing during pregnancy and childhood thrombocytopenia [10]. Similarly, Miodownik et al. reported a familial Bedouin cohort in which pregnancy frequently precipitated acute TTP episodes, while prophylactic plasma replacement substantially improved maternal and fetal outcomes [8]. Data from the UK TTP Registry further demonstrated that pregnancy may represent the first presentation of either congenital or acquired TTP; among 47 women with pregnancy‐associated TTP, fetal loss occurred in 16 of 38 pregnancies before cTTP diagnosis was made, whereas no fetal losses occurred in subsequent managed pregnancies after diagnosis and prophylactic treatment were established [11]. More recently, a Japanese registry study likewise demonstrated higher live‐birth rates following regular FFP prophylaxis during pregnancy [12].

Our case shares several features reported in these cohorts, including childhood‐onset disease, pregnancy‐triggered relapses, recurrent fetal loss prior to intensified prophylaxis, and successful full‐term pregnancy after closer surveillance and escalation of plasma therapy. Unlike many published cohorts with routine genetic confirmation and serial ADAMTS13 monitoring, our report additionally highlights the practical challenges of managing suspected cTTP in a resource‐limited setting.

Although ADAMTS13 gene sequencing remains the diagnostic gold standard, current diagnostic frameworks recognize that cTTP is often initially identified through integrated clinical and laboratory findings rather than immediate molecular confirmation [13]. Severe ADAMTS13 deficiency, absence of detectable inhibitors, recurrent thrombotic microangiopathy beginning in childhood, pregnancy‐triggered episodes, and sustained responsiveness to plasma therapy collectively support a strong presumptive diagnosis of cTTP [13, 14]. However, negative inhibitor testing alone is insufficient because false‐negative and non‐neutralizing antibodies have been reported; therefore, longitudinal phenotype, serial assessment, and treatment‐response patterns remain important complementary discriminators when genetic testing is unavailable [13].

In resource‐limited settings, longitudinal clinical monitoring becomes particularly important. Recommended surrogate strategies include serial platelet counts, LDH, hemoglobin, peripheral smear evaluation, renal function assessment, and ADAMTS13 activity testing when feasible [14, 15, 16]. Current guidelines recommend regular hematologic assessment and serial ADAMTS13 monitoring during pregnancy whenever available [9]. Pregnancy represents a high‐risk physiologic trigger requiring intensified surveillance. In our patient, limited access to serial ADAMTS13 assays necessitated a pragmatic monitoring strategy based mainly on CBC and LDH trends, clinical assessment, and adjustment of prophylactic plasma intervals. Similar pragmatic reliance on hematologic and clinical parameters has also been described in international experience when assay availability or turnaround time is limited [6, 17]. We do not propose these measures as substitutes for molecular confirmation or ADAMTS13 monitoring, but rather as practical adjunctive tools when advanced testing is inaccessible.

Our patient's management demonstrates the effectiveness of individualized plasma‐based prophylaxis and clinical monitoring in the absence of frequent ADAMTS13 testing. She received FFP every 10 days, intensified to weekly in late pregnancy, resulting in a successful term delivery without maternal complications. This schedule is consistent with prior reports showing that prophylaxis at 7–10‐day intervals is sufficient to prevent relapses when laboratory monitoring is limited [8, 9].

The postpartum period remains a high‐risk interval for relapse due to abrupt hemostatic changes. Continuing prophylactic plasma for at least 6 weeks postpartum, as in this case, is recommended [9]. Future availability of recombinant ADAMTS13 may simplify management by providing standardized dosing and avoiding plasma‐associated complications, such as volume overload and allergic reactions [4].

Conclusion

cTTP poses significant risks during pregnancy but can be safely managed with consistent plasma replacement and vigilant monitoring. Prophylactic plasma infusion throughout gestation is the cornerstone of care, preventing maternal crises and recurrent pregnancy loss. Where ADAMTS13 testing is limited, careful interpretation of platelet and LDH trends offers a viable alternative for guiding therapy. Multidisciplinary coordination between hematology and obstetrics is essential to optimize maternal and neonatal outcomes in this rare but high‐stakes condition.
