Optimal Confidence Band for Kernel Gradient Flow Estimator
Yuqian Cheng yuqian.cheng.1999@gmail.com, Zhuo Chen chenzhuo@tsinghua.edu.cn, Qian Lin qianlin@tsinghua.edu.cn

Abstract
In this paper, we investigate the supremum-norm generalization error and the uniform inference for a specific class of kernel regression methods, namely the kernel gradient flows. Under the widely adopted capacity-source condition framework in the kernel regression literature, we first establish convergence rates for the supremum norm generalization error of both continuous and discrete kernel gradient flows under the source condition s > α 0 s>\alpha_{0} , where α 0 ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \alpha_{0}\in(0,1) denotes the embedding index of the kernel function. Moreover, we show that these rates match the minimax optimal rates. Building on this result, we then construct simultaneous confidence bands for both continuous and discrete kernel gradient flows. Notably, the widths of the proposed confidence bands are also optimal, in the sense that their shrinkage rates are greater than, while can be arbitrarily close to, the minimax optimal rates.

Introduction

In recent years, a family of non-parametric regression methods, known as the kernel regression methods, has attracted considerable attention. The central idea of these methods is to estimate the target function within the reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) associated with a preselected kernel function. In order to accommodate the diverse characteristics of real-world data, various forms of kernel regression have been developed, including kernel ridge regression
(
22
)
, kernel gradient flow
(
55
)
, and kernel spectral cut-off methods
(
56
)
. These approaches can be further tailored through different choices of kernels, such as Gaussian kernels
(
42
)
, Matérn kernels
(
30
)
, neural tangent kernels
(
29
)
, and even data-dependent kernels
(
46
)
. This flexibility highlights the potential of kernel regression as a powerful and versatile framework for modeling complex input–output relationships.

In this paper, we focus on a particular class of kernel regression methods known as kernel gradient flow, which have attracted considerable attention in recent years due to their strong theoretical properties and wide applicability. This method can be viewed as a natural extension of gradient flow methods in convex optimization
(
4
)
. Moreover, in the context of artificial intelligence and deep learning, the neural tangent kernel theory
(
29
)
provides an interpretation of kernel gradient flow as the training dynamics of sufficiently wide neural networks.

The existing literature on kernel gradient flow has primarily focused on the mean squared generalization error. In contrast, the supremum-norm generalization error and the statistical inference theory for kernel gradient flow remains relatively limited. Among the most closely related works,
39
derived an estimate of the supremum-norm generalization error for the stochastic kernel gradient descent estimator, a common variant of kernel gradient flow, and employed an online multiplier bootstrap procedure to construct asymptotically exact confidence bands for the proposed estimator. However, several important questions still remaining to be solved. First,
39
only established a loose upper bound for the supremum-norm generalization error while did not prove the minimax optimality, hence they did not prove that the proposed confidence band attains the optimal band width as well; Second, they only considers the source condition
s=1
(that is, the target function lies in the RKHS); Moreover, they only focused on RKHS with uniformly bounded and Lipschitz eigenfunctions, which limits the range of kernel functions to which their theory applies.

We list our contributions as follows:

(I) Convergence rate of the supremum-norm generalization error of kernel gradient flow.

Under the capacity-source condition framework (see Assumptions
2
and
4
) and other additional mild conditions, we derive a convergence rate of the supremum-norm generalization error for both continuous and discrete kernel gradient flow estimators (defined in Definitions
1
and
2
) under general source conditions
s>\alpha_{0}
, where
\alpha_{0}<1
is the embedding index of the kernel function (see Assumption
5
).

Moreover, for the optimal selection of the training time of the kernel gradient flow estimator, the best convergence rate in fact (nearly) matches the minimax optimal rate (Corollary
6
and Theorem
7
), which demonstrates the minimax optimality of kernel gradient flow estimators.

(II) Simultaneous confidence band for kernel gradient flow.

Based on the results of the supremum-norm generalization error convergence rate above, we construct simultaneous confidence bands for both continuous and discrete kernel gradient flow estimators, and establish Theorem
11
which guarantees the asymptotic exactness of the proposed confidence bands.

It is worth noting that the confidence bands we construct are optimal, in the sense that their widths shrinks at nearly minimax optimal rate (see Remark
15
).

The construction of the confidence bands is technically built on the theory of Gaussian approximation (see Section
4.3
and Appendix
C
for details).

Background

2.1
The regression problem

Assume that the input space
\mathcal{X}
is a compact subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the output space is
\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}
. Let
\rho
be an unknown probability distribution on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, and suppose that we have
n
i.i.d. samples
\mathbb{D}_{n}=\{(x_{i},y_{i}):\,i=1,\dots,n\}
drawn from
\rho
. Our goal is to find a function
\hat{f}
such that

\mathcal{E}(\hat{f})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}(\hat{f}(x)-y)^{2}d\rho

(1)

is sufficiently small. By elementary computation, it is equivalent to searching for a function
\hat{f}
such that the generalization error

\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{X}}(\hat{f}(x)-f^{*}(x))^{2}d\mu(x)

(2)

is sufficiently small, where
\mu
is the marginal distribution of
\rho
on
\mathcal{X}
, and the function
f^{*}
is defined by

f^{*}(x)=\mathrm{E}_{\rho}(y|x)=\int_{\mathcal{Y}}yd\rho(y|x)

(3)

It is easy to see that
f^{*}
is the minimizer of (
2
). The function
f^{*}
is called the regression function or the true function, and
\hat{f}
can be viewed as an estimator of
f^{*}
.

Basically, we assume the following assumption on the noise
\varepsilon=y-f^{*}(x)
of the model:

Assumption 1
.

(noise moment bound) Assume that the noise
\varepsilon
satisfies: for a.s.
x\in\mathcal{X}
, we have
\mathrm{E}[\varepsilon|x]=0
,
\mathrm{E}[\varepsilon^{2}|x]\leq\sigma^{2}<\infty
, and

\mathrm{E}[|\varepsilon|^{m}|x]\leq\frac{1}{2}m!\sigma^{2}L^{m-2}

(4)

for any integer
m>2
.

This is a standard assumption on noise. Gaussian noise and sub-Gaussian noise are common examples that satisfy this assumption.

2.2
Reproducing kernel Hilbert space

In the kernel gradient flow algorithm, we search for the estimator of the regression function
f^{*}
in the reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) of a positive kernel function
k:\,\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
. In this subsection, we recall some critical details about kernel functions and their RKHS.

Suppose that the kernel function
k
is bounded in the following sense:

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}k(x,x)\leq\kappa^{2}

(5)

for some universal constant
\kappa>0
. The positiveness of the kernel
k
means that the integral operator

T:\,L^{2}(\mathcal{X})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{X}),\quad Tf(x)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}k(x,\xi)f(\xi)d\xi

(6)

has eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\dots>0
. Let
e_{i}
be the corresponding eigenfunction of
\lambda_{i}
such that
e_{1},e_{2},\dots
form an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathcal{X})
. Then, by Mercer decomposition
(
48
)
, we have

k(x,x^{\prime})=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}e_{i}(x)e_{i}(x^{\prime}),

(7)

and this expansion converges absolutely and uniformly.

Denote
k_{x}(\cdot)=k(x,\cdot)
, and define an inner product on the linear space
\mathrm{span}\{k_{x}:\,x\in\mathcal{X}\}
by
\langle k_{x},k_{x^{\prime}}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=k(x,x^{\prime})
. The closure of
\mathrm{span}\{k_{x}:\,x\in\mathcal{X}\}
under this inner product is called the reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) of
k
, denoted as
\mathcal{H}
. Accordingly,
k
is called the reproducing kernel of
\mathcal{H}
. The names “RKHS” and “reproducing kernel” come from the following property, known as the reproducing property:

\langle k_{x},f\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=f(x),\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{X},\,f\in\mathcal{H}.

(8)

For any function
f\in\mathcal{H}
, it has a unique RKHS expansion:

f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}f_{i}\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}e_{i}(x),\quad\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}\in l^{2},

(9)

and the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
can be computed as
\langle f,g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}f_{i}g_{i}
for
f=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}f_{i}\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}e_{i}\in\mathcal{H}
and
g=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}g_{i}\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}e_{i}\in\mathcal{H}
(see
(
22
)
for example). Thus,
\{\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
forms an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}
.

We introduce the following basic assumptions on the kernel function:

Assumption 2
.

(Eigenvalue decay rate) Assume that

ci^{-\beta}\leq\lambda_{i}\leq Ci^{-\beta}

(10)

for some positive constants
c
and
C
.

This assumption is also called the capacity condition or the effective dimension condition
(
9
)
. It is satisfied by various kinds of kernels such as Sobolev kernels
(
25
)
, Matérn kernels on spheres
(
28
)
, neural tangent kernels
(
35
)
, etc.

Assumption 3
.

(Hölder continuity) Assume that the kernel function
k
is
2h
-Hölder for some
h\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
. In other words, there exists a constant
L_{k}>0
such that

|k(x_{1},x_{2})-k(x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime})|\leq L_{k}|(x_{1},x_{2})-(x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime})|^{2h},\quad\forall x_{1},x_{2},x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}.

(11)

The Hölder continuity of kernel function is technically required in the proofs of our main theorems, especially in those parts where we extend pointwise estimations to uniform estimations.

2.3
Interpolation spaces

In order to characterize the relative smoothness of the regression function
f^{*}
with respect to
\mathcal{H}
, many prior works
(
9
;
38
;
32
;
57
)
usually assume that
f^{*}
lies in
[\mathcal{H}]^{s}
, the interpolation space of
\mathcal{H}
of order
s
, which is defined by

[\mathcal{H}]^{s}=\mathrm{Ran}T^{\frac{s}{2}}=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}f_{i}\lambda_{i}^{\frac{s}{2}}e_{i}:\,\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}\in l^{2}\right\},

(12)

where
T^{\frac{s}{2}}
is the
s
-power of the integral operator
T
defined by

T^{\frac{s}{2}}:\,L^{2}(\mathcal{X})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{X}),\quad T^{\frac{s}{2}}f=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}^{\frac{s}{2}}\langle f,e_{i}\rangle_{L^{2}}e_{i}.

(13)

[\mathcal{H}]^{s}
is naturally equipped with an inner product:
\langle f,g\rangle_{[\mathcal{H}]^{s}}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}f_{i}g_{i}
for
f=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}f_{i}\lambda_{i}^{\frac{s}{2}}e_{i}\in[\mathcal{H}]^{s}
and
g=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}g_{i}\lambda_{i}^{\frac{s}{2}}e_{i}\in[\mathcal{H}]^{s}
. Note that
[\mathcal{H}]^{0}
coincides with
L^{2}(\mathcal{X})
, and
[\mathcal{H}]^{1}
coincides with
\mathcal{H}
. For
0<s_{1}<s_{2}
, the embeddings
[\mathcal{H}]^{s_{2}}\hookrightarrow[\mathcal{H}]^{s_{1}}\hookrightarrow[\mathcal{H}]^{0}
exist and are compact.

Assumption 4
.

(Source condition) Assume that for some
s>\alpha_{0}
, there exists a constant
R>0
such that
f^{*}\in[\mathcal{H}]^{s}
and

\|f^{*}\|_{[\mathcal{H}]^{s}}\leq R.

(14)

Here,
s
is called the source condition of
f^{*}
. Assumptions
2
and
4
are jointly referred to as the capacity–source condition framework, which constitutes a standard setting in the study of kernel regression methods
(
9
;
38
;
2
;
57
;
56
)
. The source condition
s
is commonly interpreted as the relative smoothness of the true function
f^{*}
with respect to the RKHS
\mathcal{H}
.

One of the important characterizations of RKHS is the embedding property of its interpolation spaces. For any
\alpha>0
, define the embedding coefficient
M_{\alpha}
of order
\alpha
by

M_{\alpha}^{2}=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}^{\alpha}e_{i}(x)^{2}\in[0,\infty].

(15)

It is clear that
M_{\alpha}
is nonincreasing in
\alpha
. If
M_{\alpha}<\infty
, then by Cauchy’s inequality, it follows that for any
f\in[\mathcal{H}]^{\alpha}
,
f\in C^{0}(\mathcal{X})
and

\|f\|_{\infty}=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|f(x)|\leq M_{\alpha}\|f\|_{[\mathcal{H}]^{\alpha}},

(16)

which implies that
[\mathcal{H}]^{\alpha}
is naturally embedded into
C^{0}(\mathcal{X})
. Therfore, we say that the RKHS
\mathcal{H}
satisfies the embedding property of order
\alpha
if
M_{\alpha}<\infty
. Finally, we define the
embedding index
of
\mathcal{H}
by

\alpha_{0}=\inf\left\{\alpha\in[\frac{1}{\beta},1]:\,M_{\alpha}<\infty\right\}.

(17)

We make the following assumption on the embedding index of the kernel function:

Assumption 5
.

(Embedding index) Assume that the embedding index of
\mathcal{H}
is
\alpha_{0}=\frac{1}{\beta}
, where
\beta
is the eigenvalue decay rate in assumption
2
, and the embedding index
\alpha_{0}
is defined in (
17
).

This assumption is satisfied by most commonly used kernels, including Sobolev kernels, inner-product kernels on spheres, and periodic translation-invariant kernels
(
56
)
.

For more details about RKHS and its interpolation spaces, we refer to
48
and
22
for example.

2.4
Kernel gradient flow estimators

Recall that the mean square generalization error
\mathcal{E}(\hat{f})
is defined by (
2
). The corresponding empirical mean square error function is

\hat{\mathcal{E}}(\hat{f})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\hat{f}(x_{j})-y_{j})^{2},

(18)

In the kernel gradient flow algorithm, the estimator
\hat{f}
is set to be in the following form:
\hat{f}=\hat{f}_{t}
(with parameter
t\in[0,\infty)
) lies in the RKHS
\mathcal{H}
with coefficients
\{a_{i}(t)\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
; in other words,

\hat{f}(x)=\hat{f}_{t}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}(t)\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}e_{i}(x).

(19)

The parameter
t\geq 0
is called the training time or stopping time of the kernel gradient flow estimator.

In this paper, we consider two kinds of kernel gradient flow estimator: the continuous kernel gradient flow estimator
\hat{f}_{t}=\hat{f}_{t}^{con}
and the discrete kernel gradient flow estimator
\hat{f}_{t}=\hat{f}_{t}^{dis}
.

2.4.1
The continuous kernel gradient flow

In this case, each parameter
a_{i}(t)
is initialized as
a_{i}(0)=0
, and is then set to evolve along the following gradient flow:

\frac{d}{dt}a_{i}(t)=-\frac{\partial}{\partial a_{i}(t)}\hat{\mathcal{E}}(\hat{f}_{t})=-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\partial\hat{f}_{t}(x_{i})}{\partial a_{i}(t)}\cdot(\hat{f}_{t}(x_{j})-y_{j}).

(20)

Equivalently, the evolution equation of the estimator
\hat{f}_{t}
is given by

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\hat{f}_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\partial\hat{f}_{t}(x)}{\partial a_{i}(t)}\cdot\frac{da_{i}(t)}{dt}=-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\partial\hat{f}_{t}(x)}{\partial a_{i}(t)}\cdot\frac{\partial\hat{f}_{t}(x_{j})}{\partial a_{i}(t)}\cdot(\hat{f}_{t}(x_{j})-y_{j})

(21)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{n}k(x,x_{j})(\hat{f}_{t}(x_{j})-y_{j}).

Definition 1
.

(Continuous kernel gradient flow estimator)
Let
\hat{f}_{t}^{con}(x)
be the solution to the equation (
21
) with initial condition
\hat{f}_{0}^{con}(x)\equiv 0
.
\hat{f}_{t}^{con}(x)
is called the continuous kernel gradient flow estimator.

Note that the continuous kernel gradient estimator
\hat{f}_{t}^{con}
admits an explicit expression:

\hat{f}_{t}^{con}(x)=\mathbb{K}(x,X)\mathbb{K}(X,X)^{-1}\left(I_{n}-\exp(-\frac{t}{n}\mathbb{K}(X,X))\right)Y,

(22)

where
\mathbb{K}(x,X)=(k(x,x_{1}),\dots,k(x,x_{n}))
,
\mathbb{K}(X,X)=(k(x_{i},x_{j}))_{n\times n}
and
X=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})^{T}
,
Y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})^{T}
, providing that
\mathbb{K}(X,X)
is invertible.

2.4.2
The discrete kernel gradient flow

We also consider the discretized counterpart of the evolution equation (
21
):

\hat{f}_{t_{m+1}}(x)=\hat{f}_{t_{m}}(x)-\eta\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}k(x,x_{j})(\hat{f}_{t_{m}}(x_{j})-y_{j}),

(23)

where
t_{0}=0
,
t_{m}=t_{m-1}+\eta
for
m\geq 0
, and
\eta>0
is a preselected parameter.

Definition 2
.

(Discrete gradient flow estimator)
Let
\hat{f}_{t}^{dis}(x)
(
t=t_{m}=m\eta
,
m=0,1,\dots
) be the solution to (
23
) with initial condition
\hat{f}_{0}^{dis}(x)\equiv 0
.
\hat{f}_{t}^{dis}(x)
is called the discrete kernel gradient flow estimator or the kernel gradient descent estimator, and the parameter
\eta
is called the learning rate of
\hat{f}_{t}^{dis}(x)
.

Both the continuous kernel gradient flow estimator
\hat{f}_{t}^{con}
and the discrete kernel gradient flow estimator
\hat{f}_{t}^{dis}
can be expressed in the form of spectral algorithm
(
1
;
27
)
. Consider the following empirical version of the integral operator
T
defined in (
6
):

T_{X}:\,\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H},\quad T_{X}f(\cdot)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}f(x_{j})k(x_{j},\cdot),

(24)

and define the sample basis function as

\hat{g}(\cdot)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}y_{j}k(x_{j},\cdot).

(25)

Then,
\hat{f}_{t}^{con}
and
\hat{f}_{t}^{dis}
can be represented by

\hat{f}_{t}^{con}=\varphi_{t}^{con}(T_{X})\hat{g},\quad\hat{f}_{t}^{dis}=\varphi_{t}^{dis}(T_{X})\hat{g},

(26)

(we refer to
56
;
33
for the details), where the functions
\varphi_{t}^{con}
and
\varphi_{t}^{dis}
are the filter functions of continuous and discrete kernel gradient flows, respectively, and are defined as follows:

Definition 3
.

We define the filter function
\varphi_{t}(z)
and the remainder function
\psi_{t}(z)=1-z\varphi_{t}(z)
of the kernel gradient flow as follows:

(I) For the continuous kernel gradient flow, we define

\varphi_{t}(z)=\varphi_{t}^{con}(z):=\frac{1-e^{-tz}}{z},

(27)

\psi_{t}(z)=\psi_{t}^{con}(z)=1-z\varphi_{t}^{con}(z):=e^{-tz};

(28)

(II) For the discrete kernel gradient flow, we define

\varphi_{t}(z)=\varphi_{t}^{dis}(z):=\frac{1-(1-\eta z)^{t/\eta}}{z},

(29)

\psi_{t}(z)=\psi_{t}^{dis}(z)=1-z\varphi_{t}^{dis}(z):=(1-\eta z)^{t/\eta}.

(30)

For the discrete kernel gradient flow, we need an additional assumption on its learning rate
\eta
:

Assumption 6
.

The learning rate
\eta
satisfies
0<\eta<\frac{1}{2\kappa^{2}}
, where
\kappa^{2}
is the bound of the kernel function described in (
5
).

It is a technical assumption required in the proofs. In particular, it guarantees that the filter function
\varphi_{t}^{dis}
of the discrete kernel gradient flow admits an analytic extension to a larger domain in the complex plane (see Appendix
D.3
for detailed discussions)

3
Exact Convergence Rate of the Supremum-Norm Generalization Error

In this section, we present our results on the convergence rate of the supremum-norm generalization error of kernel gradient flow. We further show that the proposed convergence rate is in fact minimax optimal.

Theorem 4
.

(Upper bound of the supremum-norm generalization error)
Suppose that Assumptions
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
and
5
are satisfied. Let
t=n^{\theta}
for
\theta\in(0,\beta)
. Then for any
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small such that
0<\varepsilon<\min\{s-\frac{1}{\beta},\frac{1}{\theta}-\frac{1}{\beta}\}
and for any
p>1
, the following estimations hold:

(I) For the continuous kernel gradient flow estimator
\hat{f}_{t}=\hat{f}_{t}^{con}
, when
n
is sufficiently great, we have

\|\hat{f}_{t}^{con}-f^{*}\|_{\infty}\leq Ct^{-\frac{s-\alpha}{2}}+C\sqrt{\frac{p\log n}{n}}t^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}

(31)

with probability
1-\mathcal{O}(n^{-p})
, where
\alpha=\frac{1}{\beta}+\varepsilon
, and the constant
C>0
depends only on
\varepsilon
,
d
,
\beta
,
L_{k}
,
h
,
s
,
R
,
\sigma
and
L
;

(II) For the discrete kernel gradient flow estimator
\hat{f}_{t}=\hat{f}_{t}^{dis}
, if we additionally assume that Assumption
6
holds as well , then

\|\hat{f}_{t}^{dis}-f^{*}\|_{\infty}\leq Ct^{-\frac{s-\alpha}{2}}+C\sqrt{\frac{p\log n}{n}}t^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}

(32)

with probability
1-\mathcal{O}(n^{-p})
when
n
is sufficiently great, where the constant
C>0
depends only on
\varepsilon
,
d
,
\beta
,
\kappa
,
L_{k}
,
h
,
s
,
R
,
\sigma
,
L
and
\eta
.

The proof of this theorem can be found in Appendix
A
.

Remark 5
.

For brevity, here and throughout the paper, “depending on
\varepsilon
” refers to dependence on both
\varepsilon
and
M_{\alpha_{0}+\varepsilon}
.

Based on Theorem
4
, by balancing the terms on the right-hand sides of (

4
Inference for Kernel Gradient Flow

In this section, we aim to construct simultaneous confidence bands for kernel gradient flow estimators in the following form:

Definition 10
.

A simultaneous confidence band of the (continuous or discrete) kernel gradient flow estimator
\hat{f}_{t}(x)
is a subset of
\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R}
in the following form:

\mathrm{CB}=\{(x,y):\,x\in\mathcal{X},\,y\in[\hat{f}_{t}(x)-\hat{\lambda}(x),\hat{f}_{t}(x)+\hat{\upsilon}(x)]\},

(38)

where
\hat{\lambda}
and
\hat{\upsilon}
are non-negative bounded measurable functions on
\mathcal{X}
depending on the training time
t
and the data set
\mathbb{D}_{n}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
. Given a constant
\delta\in(0,1]
,
\mathrm{CB}
is asymptotically exact with coverage level
1-\delta
, if

\mathbb{P}(f^{*}\in\mathrm{CB})\to 1-\delta

(39)

in probability as
n\to\infty
.

4.1
Crucial quantities

We first introduce the key quantities involved in the construction of the confidence bands. For any
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
, define

\displaystyle C_{t}(x,x^{\prime})=\sigma^{2}\cdot\mathrm{E}_{z\sim\mu}(\varphi_{t}(T)k_{x}(z)\cdot\varphi_{t}(T)k_{x^{\prime}}(z))

(40)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\sigma^{2}\cdot\mathrm{E}_{z\sim\mu}(\varphi_{t}^{con}(T)k_{x}(z)\cdot\varphi_{t}^{con}(T)k_{x^{\prime}}(z))\quad&&\mbox{for continuous kernel gradient flow};\\
&\sigma^{2}\cdot\mathrm{E}_{z\sim\mu}(\varphi_{t}^{dis}(T)k_{x}(z)\cdot\varphi_{t}^{dis}(T)k_{x^{\prime}}(z))\quad&&\mbox{for discrete kernel gradient flow},\end{aligned}\right.

where
T:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
is the integral operator defined in (
6
), and
\varphi_{t}
is the filter function defined in Definition
3
. Define the Gaussian process
W_{t}(x)
,
x\in\mathcal{X}
as

W_{n}(x)\sim\mathrm{GP}\left(0,\frac{C_{t}(x,x^{\prime})}{C_{t}(x,x)^{\frac{1}{2}}C_{t}(x^{\prime},x^{\prime})^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right),

(41)

and let
Z_{t}=\|W_{t}(x)\|_{\infty}
.

The function
C_{t}(x,x^{\prime})
is intractable in practice. When
x=x^{\prime}
, we introduce the following empirical estimator of
C_{t}(x,x)
:

\displaystyle\widehat{C}_{n,t}(x,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\varphi_{t}(T_{X})k_{x}(x_{i})\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}|^{2},

(42)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\varphi_{t}^{con}(T_{X})k_{x}(x_{i})\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}|^{2}\quad&&\mbox{for continuous kernel gradient flow};\\
&\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\varphi_{t}^{dis}(T_{X})k_{x}(x_{i})\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}|^{2}\quad&&\mbox{for discrete kernel gradient flow},\end{aligned}\right.

where
\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}=y_{i}-\hat{f}_{t}(x_{i})
,
i=1,\dots,n
.

For both the continuous kernel gradient flow and the discrete kernel gradient flow, the function
\widehat{C}_{n,t}(x,x)
can be explicit computed:

(I) For the continuous kernel gradient flow, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{C}_{n,t}(x,x)

\displaystyle=\widehat{C}_{n,t}^{con}(x,x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\varphi^{con}_{t}(T_{X})k_{x}(x_{i})\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}|^{2}

(43)

\displaystyle=n\left|\mathbb{K}(x,X)\mathbb{K}(X,X)^{-1}\left(I_{n}-\exp(-\frac{t}{n}\mathbb{K}(X,X))\right)\cdot\mathrm{diag}(\hat{\varepsilon})\right|^{2},

where
\hat{\varepsilon}=(\hat{\varepsilon}_{1},\dots,\hat{\varepsilon}_{n})
,
\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}=y_{i}-\hat{f}_{t}(x_{i})
;

(II) For the discrete kernel gradient flow, the function
\widehat{C}_{n,t}(x,x^{\prime})=\widehat{C}_{n,t}^{dis}(x,x^{\prime})
can be computed via the following iterative procedure: For any
t_{m}=m\eta
,
m=0,1,2\dots
, let
\widehat{F}_{t_{m}}(x)=(\widehat{F}^{1}_{t_{m}}(x),\dots,\widehat{F}^{n}_{t_{m}}(x))
be a mapping from
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined by

\displaystyle\widehat{F}_{0}(x)=0;

(44)

\displaystyle\widehat{F}_{t_{m}}(x)=\widehat{F}_{t_{m-1}}(x)-\eta\cdot\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{K}(x,X)\cdot(\widehat{\mathbb{F}}_{t_{m-1}}(X)-\mathrm{diag}(\hat{\varepsilon})),\quad m=1,2,\dots

where

\widehat{\mathbb{F}}_{t_{m}}(X)=(\widehat{F}_{t_{m}}^{1}(X),\dots,\widehat{F}_{t_{m}}^{n}(X)),\quad\widehat{F}_{t_{m}}^{i}(X)=(\widehat{F}_{t_{m}}^{i}(x_{1}),\dots,\widehat{F}_{t_{m}}^{i}(x_{n}))^{T},

(45)

then for the discrete kernel gradient flow and
t=t_{m}=m\eta
,
m=1,2,\dots
, the function
\widehat{C}_{n,t}^{dis}(x,x)
is computed as

\widehat{C}_{n,t_{m}}^{dis}(x,x)=n|\widehat{F}_{t_{m}}(x)|^{2}.

(46)

Next, define

\widetilde{W}_{n,t}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{C_{t}(x,x)}}\cdot\sqrt{n}(\hat{f}_{t}(x)-f_{t}(x)),\quad\widetilde{Z}_{n,t}=\|\widetilde{W}_{n,t}\|_{\infty},

(47)

and

\widehat{W}_{n,t}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\widehat{C}_{n,t}(x,x)}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\varphi_{t}(T_{X})k_{x}(x_{j})\hat{\varepsilon}_{j}g_{j},\quad\widehat{Z}_{n,t}=\|\widehat{W}_{n,t}\|_{\infty}

(48)

where
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}
are i.i.d. one-dimensional standard Gaussian random variables.
\widehat{Z}_{n,t}
is called the multiplier bootstrap variable
(
18
)
.

Similarly with (
43
) and (
46
), for both continuous and discrete kernel gradient flows, we can compute
\widehat{W}_{n,t}(x)
and
\widehat{Z}_{n,t}
explicitly:

(I) For the continuous kernel gradient flow, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{W}_{n,t}(x)

\displaystyle=\widehat{W}_{n,t}^{con}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\widehat{C}_{n,t}^{con}(x,x)}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\varphi^{con}_{t}(T_{X})k_{x}(x_{j})\hat{\varepsilon_{j}}g_{j}

(49)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\widehat{C}^{con}_{n,t}(x,x)}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbb{K}(x,X)\mathbb{K}(X,X)^{-1}(1-e^{-\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{K}(X,X)t})\cdot\mathrm{diag}(\hat{\varepsilon})\cdot g,

where
g=(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})^{T}\sim N(0,I_{n})
is a standard
n
-dimensional Gaussian random variable;

(II) For the discrete kernel gradient flow and
t=t_{m}=m\eta
,
m=1,2,\dots
, we compute
\widehat{W}_{n,t_{m}}(x)=\widehat{W}_{n,t_{m}}^{dis}(x)
via the following iterative procedure: Let
\widehat{G}_{t_{m}}(x)
be a mapping from
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathbb{R}
defined by

\displaystyle\widehat{G}_{0}(x)=0;

(50)

\displaystyle\widehat{G}_{t_{m}}(x)=\widehat{G}_{t_{m-1}}(x)-\eta\cdot\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{K}(x,X)\cdot(\widehat{G}_{t_{m-1}}(X)-\mathrm{diag}(\hat{\varepsilon})\cdot g),\quad m=1,2,\dots

where

\quad\widehat{G}_{t_{m}}(X)=(\widehat{G}_{t_{m}}(x_{1}),\dots,\widehat{G}_{t_{m}}(x_{n}))^{T},

(51)

then for the discrete kernel gradient flow and
t=t_{m}=m\eta
,
m=1,2,\dots
, the function
\widehat{W}_{n,t}(x)
is computed as

\widehat{W}_{n,t_{m}}=\widehat{W}^{dis}_{n,t_{m}}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\widehat{C}_{n,t_{m}}^{dis}(x,x)}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\widehat{G}_{t_{m}}(x).

(52)

4.2
Simultaneous confidence band

In this section, we make the following additional assumption:

Assumption 7
.

There exists a universal constant
c>0
such that

C_{t}(x,x)\geq c\sigma^{2}t^{\frac{1}{\beta}}.

(53)

This assumption is introduced to control the covariance of the Gaussian process
W_{t}
defined in (
41
). It is reasonable in view of Lemma
61
and
34
, which imply that (i)
\mathrm{E}_{x}C_{t}(x,x)\gtrsim\sigma^{2}t^{\frac{1}{\beta}}
, and (ii)
\sup_{x}C_{t}(x,x)\lesssim\sigma^{2}t^{\alpha}<<\sigma^{2}t^{1/\beta}
, where
\alpha=\alpha_{0}+\varepsilon>1/\beta
. This assumption is satisfied by a broad class of kernel functions. For instance, since
\sin^{2}\langle\mu,x\rangle+\cos^{2}\langle\mu,x\rangle=1
for any
m,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have
C_{t}(x,x)\asymp\mathrm{E}_{x}C_{t}(x,x)\gtrsim\sigma^{2}t^{1/\beta}
for kernel functions that satisfy Assumption
2
and has Fourier basis. In particular, the shift-invariant periodic kernels (see Section 4.3 of
56
) satisfy this assumption. Furthermore, we will show in Lemma
64
that the inner-product kernels satisfy this assumption as well.

We now state our main result of this section:

Theorem 11
.

For both the continuous kernel gradient flow estimator
\hat{f}_{t}=\hat{f}_{t}^{con}
and the discrete kernel gradient flow estimator
\hat{f}_{t}=\hat{f}_{t}^{dis}
, the following statement holds: Suppose that Assumption
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
and
7
are all satisfied, and we additionally assume that
\mathrm{E}[\varepsilon^{2}|x]=\sigma^{2}
for a.s.
x\in\mathcal{X}
. If we set
t\asymp n^{\theta}
for
\theta\in(\frac{1}{s},\beta)
, then with probability at least
1-p_{n}
, we have

\sup_{a\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\left(\sqrt{n}\left\|\frac{\hat{f}_{t}(x)-f^{*}(x)}{\sqrt{\widehat{C}_{n,t}(x,x)}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq a\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\widehat{Z}_{n}\leq a\right|\mathbb{D}_{n}\right)\right|\leq q_{n},

(54)

where
p_{n}=c_{1}n^{-c_{2}}
,
q_{n}=c_{3}n^{-c_{4}}
for some
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},c_{4}>0
depending only on
\theta
,
d
,
\kappa
,
\beta
,
L_{k}
,
h
,
s
,
R
,
\sigma
,
L
(and
\eta
additionally for discrete kernel gradient flow).

The probability
1-p_{n}
arises from the randomness of the samples
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}
. As implied by Theorem
4
and its proof, the condition
\theta\in(\frac{1}{s},\beta)
ensures that the variance term is greater than the bias term, which is a standard assumption in constructing asymptotically exact confidence bands.

Theorem
11
implies that the quantity
\sqrt{n}\|(\hat{f}_{t}(x)-f^{*}(x))/\widehat{C}_{n,t}(x,x)^{1/2}\|_{\infty}
is approximately distributed as the multiplier Gaussian bootstrap variable
\widehat{Z}_{n}|\mathbb{D}_{n}
, as long as the sample size
n
is sufficiently great. This observation motivates the construction of the following confidence bands for both continuous and discrete kernel gradient flows:

Definition 12
.

(Simultaneous confidence band for continuous kernel gradient flow)
For preselected sample size
n
, training time
t
and coverage level
\delta\in(0,1)
, we construct the simultaneous confidence band
\mathrm{CB}^{con}(\delta)
for the continuous kernel gradient flow by the following steps:

(1)

Compute the continuous kernel gradient flow estimator
\hat{f}^{con}_{t}(x)
by formula (
22
);

(2)

Compute the function
\widehat{C}_{n,t}^{con}(x,x)
by formula (
43
);

(3)

For each bootstrap iteration, draw a vector
g=(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
from the standard
n
-dimensional normal distribution
N(0,I_{n})
, and use it to compute the multiplier bootstrap variable
\widehat{Z}_{n,t}^{con}=\|\widehat{W}_{n,t}^{con}(x)\|_{\infty}
by formula (
49
);

(4)

Across bootstrap iterations, use the samples of
\widehat{Z}_{n,t_{k}}^{con}
obtained in step (3) to compute the
\delta
-quantile of
\widehat{Z}_{n,t}^{con}
conditioning on
\mathbb{D}_{n}
, denoted as
r(\delta)
;

(5)

The confidence band is computed by

\mathrm{CB}^{con}(\delta)=\left\{(x,y):\,x\in\mathcal{X},\,y\in\left[\hat{f}^{con}_{t}(x)-\Delta^{con}(t,x,\delta),\hat{f}_{t}^{con}(x)+\Delta^{con}(t,x,\delta)\right]\right\},

(55)

where

\Delta^{con}(t,x,\delta)=r(\delta)\cdot n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\widehat{C}^{con}_{n,t}(x,x)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(56)

Definition 13
.

(Simultaneous confidence band for continuous kernel gradient flow)
For preselected sample size
n
, sample data
\mathbb{D}_{n}=\{(x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{n},y_{n})\}
, training time
t_{m}=m\eta
,
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and coverage level
\delta\in(0,1)
, we construct the simultaneous confidence band
\mathrm{CB}^{dis}(\delta)
for the continuous kernel gradient flow by the following steps:

\hat{f}^{dis}_{t_{m}}(x)
by Definition

;

(2)

\widehat{C}_{n,t_{m}}^{dis}(x,x)

(3)

n

\widehat{Z}_{n,t_{m}}^{dis}=\|\widehat{W}_{n,t_{m}}^{dis}(x)\|_{\infty}

52
);

(4)

\widehat{Z}_{n,t_{k}}^{dis}
obtained in step (3) to compute the
\delta
-quantile of
\widehat{Z}_{n,t_{m}}^{dis}

\mathbb{D}_{n}

\Delta^{dis}(t_{m},x,\delta)=r(\delta)\cdot n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\widehat{C}^{dis}_{n,t_{m}}(x,x)^{\frac{1}{2}}

(58)

5
Experiments

In this section, we present numerical experiments to illustrate our results on supremum-norm convergence rates and simultaneous confidence bands for kernel gradient flow estimators.

5.1
Supremum-norm convergence rate

In the following experiment, we set the input space to be the
1
-dimensional interval
\mathcal{X}=[0,1]
equipped with uniform distribution
\mu=U([0,1])
. The kernel function is set to be the Min kernel

k_{min}(x,x^{\prime})=\min(x,x^{\prime}),\quad x,x^{\prime}\in[0,1].

(62)

The RKHS of
k_{min}
on
[0,1]
is characterized by
(
53
)
:

\mathcal{H}_{min}=\left\{f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}:\,f\mbox{ is absolutely continuous, }f(0)=0,\,\int_{0}^{1}(f^{\prime}(x))^{2}dx<\infty\right\}.

(63)

Moreover, the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of
k_{min}
is computed by

\lambda_{j}=\left(\frac{2j-1}{2}\pi\right)^{-2},\quad e_{j}(x)=\sqrt{2}\sin\left(\frac{2j-1}{2}\pi x\right),\quad j=1,2,\dots

(64)

Thus, it is easy to verify that the eigenvalue decay rate of
k_{min}
is
\beta=2
.

Consider the function
f_{1}(x)=\sqrt{2}\sin(2\pi x)
, whose source condition with respect to
k_{\min}
is
s=1.5
. Taking
f_{1}(x)
as the ground-truth function, we generate
n
i.i.d. samples
{(x_{i},y_{i})}{i=1}^{n}
from the model
y=f_{1}(x)+\varepsilon
, where
\varepsilon\sim N(0,\sigma^{2})
with

6
Discussions and Conclusion

In this paper, we construct simultaneous confidence band for kernel gradient flow based on the estimation of its supremum-norm generalization error convergence rate. In contrast to prior work, the convergence rate established in this paper nearly attains the minimax optimal rate. Moreover, the proposed confidence band is also optimal, in the sense that its width shrinks at nearly optimal rate.

We conclude by outlining several open problems that remain unresolved:

(1)

From a technical perspective, this paper relies on the embedding index condition
\alpha_{0}=\frac{1}{\beta}
(Assumption
5
). To the best of our knowledge, no example of an RKHS violating Assumption
5
has been identified so far. We conjecture that this assumption holds for a broad class of RKHS with eigenvalue decay rate
\beta>1
.

(2)

This paper focuses on kernel gradient flow, while other closely related methods, such as kernel ridge regression and neural networks, are beyond the scope of the present paper. Extending the inference framework developed here to more general statistical and machine learning methods remains an important direction for future research.

(3)

To achieve the optimal convergence rate of the generalization error and the optimal shrinkage rate of the confidence band width, the selection of the training time in kernel gradient flow relies heavily on prior information about the target function, such as the source condition
s
. Developing data-driven methods for selecting the optimal early stopping time would significantly improve the practical utility of the proposed methods.

We hope that our contributions will inspire further research on regression algorithms, inference theory, and other related areas in statistics and machine learning.

Appendix B
Proof of Theorem
7

We first introduce the basic concepts and results in the duality framework introduced by
12
, which will be used in the proof of Theorem
7
later.

Suppose that there are three Banach spaces:
\mathcal{F}
,
\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{M}
, such that
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{Q}\cap\mathcal{M}
. The space
\mathcal{F}
is called the model space, and the norms
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{Q}}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{M}}
are used for model training and model evaluation, respectively.

In particular, in this paper, we set

\mathcal{F}=[\mathcal{H}]^{s},\quad\mathcal{Q}=L^{2}(\mathcal{X}),\quad\mathcal{M}=C^{0}(\mathcal{X}).

(200)

For a Banach space
V
, we denote
V(1)=\{w\in V:\,\|w\|_{V}\leq 1\}
.

Definition 36
.

(I-complexity)
For
\varepsilon\geq 0
, we define the I-complexity as

\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{M}}(\mathcal{F}(1),\varepsilon)=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}(1),\,\|f\|_{\mathcal{Q}}\leq\varepsilon}\|f\|_{\mathcal{M}}.

(201)

The I-complexity can provide a minimax lower bound for the regression problem in
\mathcal{F}(1)
with independent Gaussian noises:

Theorem 37
.

(Proposition 3.6 of
12
)
Consider the classical regression model

y_{j}=f^{*}(x_{j})+\varepsilon_{j},\quad f^{*}\in\mathcal{F}(1),\quad j=1,\dots,n

(202)

where
x_{j}
are independent samples drawn from
(\mathcal{X},\mu)
, and
\varepsilon_{j}
are independent Gaussian noises,
\varepsilon_{j}|x_{j}\sim N(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})
for some
\sigma>0
(
I_{d}
is the
d
-dimensional unit matrix). Then we have

\inf_{\hat{f}}\sup_{f^{*}\in\mathcal{F}(1)}\left\|\hat{f}-f^{*}\right\|_{\mathcal{M}}\gtrsim\mathbb{I}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}),\mathcal{M}}(\mathcal{F}(1),\sigma/\sqrt{n}).

(203)

The following theorem shows that we can compute the I-complexity by solving an approximation problem in the dual spaces:

Theorem 38
.

(Theorem 3.3 of
12
)
The following equality holds:

\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{M}}(\mathcal{F}(1),\varepsilon)=\sup_{\|g^{*}\|_{\mathcal{M}^{*}\leq 1}}\inf_{h^{*}\in\mathcal{Q}^{*}}\left[\|g^{*}-h^{*}\|_{\mathcal{F}^{*}}+\varepsilon\|h^{*}\|_{\mathcal{Q}^{*}}\right].

(204)

Now we begin to prove Theorem
7
.

Lemma 39
.

There exists a feature map
\phi:\,\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{R}
, where
\mathcal{V}
is the weight domain of the feature map equipped with a probability distribution
\pi
, such that

[\mathcal{H}]^{s}=\left\{f(x)=\int_{\mathcal{V}}a(v)\phi(x,v)d\pi(v):\,a\in L^{2}(\mathcal{V},\pi)\right\},

(205)

and

\|f\|_{[\mathcal{H}]^{s}}=c_{s}\|a\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{V},\pi)}\quad\mbox{for }f(x)=\int_{\mathcal{V}}a(v)\phi(x,v)d\pi(v).

(206)

for some constant
c_{s}>0
.

Proof.

We take
\mathcal{V}=\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, and define
\pi
as
\pi(i)=c_{s}\lambda_{i}^{s}
, where
c_{s}
is a rescaling constant to make
\pi
indeed a probability distribution:

\pi(\mathcal{V})=c_{s}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}^{s}=1.

(207)

Note that by Assumption
2
and
5
,

\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}^{s}\lesssim\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}i^{-\beta s}<\infty,

(208)

hence
c_{s}
is well-defined. Note that

\|a\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{V},\pi)}^{2}=c_{s}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}^{s}a(i)^{2},\quad\forall a\in L^{2}(\mathcal{V},\pi).

(209)

Next, define the feature map as

\phi(x,v)=e_{i}(x),\quad x\in\mathcal{X},\,i\in\mathcal{V}.

(210)

Now we define the space
\mathcal{W}
to be

\mathcal{W}=\left\{f(x)=\int_{\mathcal{V}}a(v)\phi(x,v)d\pi(v):\,a\in L^{2}(\mathcal{V},\pi)\right\},

(211)

which is equipped with the norm

\|f\|_{\mathcal{W}}=\|a\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{V},\pi)}\quad\mbox{for }f(x)=\int_{\mathcal{V}}a(v)\phi(x,v)d\pi(v).

(212)

On the one hand, any
f\in[\mathcal{H}]^{s}
can be represented as

f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}f_{i}\lambda_{i}^{s/2}e_{i}(x),\quad\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}\in l^{2},

(213)

and we notice that

f(x)=c_{s}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}^{s}a(i)e_{i}(x)=\int_{\mathcal{V}}a(i)\phi(x,i)d\pi(i),

(214)

where

a(i)=\frac{f_{i}}{c_{s}\lambda_{i}^{s/2}},

(215)

hence
f\in\mathcal{W}
, and

\|f\|_{\mathcal{W}}^{2}=\|a(i)\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{V},\pi)}^{2}=c_{s}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}^{s}a(i)^{2}=\frac{1}{c_{s}}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}f_{i}^{2}=\frac{1}{c_{s}}\|f\|_{[\mathcal{H}]^{s}}^{2}.

(216)

On the other hand, by a similar procedure, we can prove that for any
f\in\mathcal{W}
, we have
f\in[\mathcal{H}]^{s}
and
\|f\|_{[\mathcal{H}]^{s}}=c_{s}\|f\|_{\mathcal{W}}
.

This completes the proof of this lemma.
∎

Lemma 40
.

The space

\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}=\left\{a(i)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}h(x)\phi(x,i)d\mu(x):\,h\in L^{2}(\mathcal{X})\right\}

(217)

equipped with a norm

\|a\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}}=\|h\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X})}\quad\mbox{for }a(i)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}h(x)\phi(x,i)d\mu(x)

(218)

is an RKHS. Its reproducing kernel is
\tilde{k}(i,j)=\delta_{ij}
, where
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker delta. Moreover, its eigenvalues (with respect to
L^{2}(\mathcal{V},\pi)
) are
c_{s}\lambda_{i}^{s}
,
i=1,2,\dots
, whose corresponding eigenfunctions are
\tilde{e}_{i}(j)=\delta_{ij}/\sqrt{c_{s}\lambda_{i}^{s}}

Proof.

Let

\tilde{k}(i,j)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\phi(x,i)\phi(x,j)d\mu(x)=\delta_{ij}.

(219)

Then its corresponding integral operator in
L^{2}(\mathcal{V},\pi)
is

\tilde{T}a(i)=\int_{\mathcal{V}}\tilde{k}(i,j)a(j)d\pi(j)=c_{s}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}^{s}\delta_{ij}a(j)=c_{s}\lambda_{i}^{s}a(i).

(220)

Thus, the eigenvalues and orthonormal eigenfunctions of
\tilde{T}
are
\tilde{\lambda}_{i}=c_{s}\lambda_{i}^{s}
and
\tilde{e}_{i}(j)=\delta_{ij}/\sqrt{c_{s}\lambda_{i}^{s}}
,respectively.

Furthermore, its RKHS is

\left\{a(i)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}h_{j}\sqrt{\tilde{\lambda}_{i}}\tilde{e}_{j}(i):\,\{h_{i}\}\in l^{2}\right\}=\left\{a(i)=h_{i}:\,\{h_{i}\}\in l^{2}\right\},

(221)

which is exactly the space
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
.

Lemma 41
.

Suppose that
a\in\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
. Then for any

, we have

\inf_{r_{1},\dots,r_{m}\in\mathbb{R}}\left\|a-\sum_{j=1}^{m}r_{j}\tilde{e}_{j}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{V},\pi)}\leq\sqrt{\tilde{\lambda}_{m+1}}\|h\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}}.

(223)

whose minimum is reached if and only if

\inf_{r_{1},\dots,r_{m}\in\mathbb{R}}\left\|a-\sum_{j=1}^{m}r_{j}\tilde{e}_{j}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{V},\pi)}^{2}=\sum_{j=m+1}^{\infty}a(j)^{2}=\tilde{\lambda}_{m+1}\sum_{j=m+1}^{\infty}\tilde{\lambda}_{j}^{-1}a(j)^{2}\leq\tilde{\lambda}_{m+1}\|a\|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}}^{2}.

(224)

\displaystyle\sup_{\|\gamma\|_{TV}\leq 1}\inf_{r_{1},\dots,r_{m}\in\mathbb{R}}\left\|\int_{\mathcal{X}}\phi(x,\cdot)d\gamma(x)-\sum_{j=1}^{m}r_{j}\tilde{e}_{j}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{V},\pi)}

(319)

Combining (

(423)

∎

Lemma 73
.

We have the following estimation for the filter function
\varphi_{t}(z)
defined in Definition

\oint_{\Gamma_{t}}|\varphi_{t}(z)dz|\leq C\log t

for some constant

(depending only on

Proof.

Note that by Lemma

, we have

|\varphi_{t}(z)|\leq\frac{C}{|z+\lambda|},\quad\lambda=\frac{1}{t}

\kappa
and

\oint_{\Gamma_{t}}|\varphi_{t}(z)dz|\leq C\oint_{\Gamma_{t}}\frac{1}{|z+\lambda|}|dz|=C\oint_{\Gamma_{t,1}}\frac{1}{|z+\lambda|}|dz|+C\oint_{\Gamma_{t,2}}\frac{1}{|z+\lambda|}|dz|.

Lemma 74
.

We have the following estimation:

\oint_{\Gamma_{t}}\left|e^{-tz}dz\right|\leq\frac{C}{t}

(429)

for some constant
C>0
depending only on
\kappa
, and

\oint_{\Gamma_{t}}\left|(1-\eta z)^{t}dz\right|\leq\frac{C}{t}

for some constant

\eta
and
\kappa
.

Proof.

For the first estimation, we first note that

\oint_{\Gamma_{\lambda}}|e^{-tz}dz|\leq\oint_{\Gamma_{t,1}}|e^{-tz}|\cdot|dz|+\oint_{\Gamma_{t,2}}|e^{-tz}|\cdot|dz|.

(431)

When
z\in\Gamma_{t,1}

\oint_{\Gamma_{t,1}}|e^{-tz}|\cdot|dz|=C\int_{-\frac{1}{2t}}^{\kappa^{2}}e^{-ts}ds\leq\frac{C}{t}(e^{\frac{1}{2}}-e^{-\kappa^{2}t})\leq\frac{C}{t}.

\oint_{\Gamma_{\lambda}}|e^{-tz}dz|\leq\frac{C}{t}.

(434)

The proof for the second estimation is similar.

D.4
Gaussian approximation

Definition 75
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a class of measurable functions on a measurable space
(S,\mathcal{S})
. Let
F
be an envelop of
\mathcal{F}
. In other words,
F
is a measurable function such that
|f(x)|\leq|F(x)|
for any
f\in\mathcal{F}
and
x\in S
.

\mathcal{F}
is called a VC-type class with envelop
F
if there exist constants
A,v>0
such that

\sup_{Q}\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{F},e_{Q},\varepsilon\|F\|_{L^{2}(Q)})\leq\left(\frac{A}{\varepsilon}\right)^{v},\quad\forall\varepsilon\in(0,1],

(435)

where the supremum is taken over all finite probability measures
Q
on
S
,
e_{Q}(f,g)=\|f-g\|_{L^{2}(Q)}
, and
mathcal{N}(\mathcal{F},e_{Q},\varepsilon\|F\|_{L^{2}(Q)})
denotes the
\varepsilon\|F\|_{L^{2}(Q)}
-covering number of
\mathcal{F}
under the distance
e_{Q}
.

The following theorem and its proof can be found in
14
;
15
;
16
;
17
; See Lemma A.1 of
15
for example.

Theorem 76
.

(Anti-concentration inequality)
Let
(S,\mathcal{S},P)
be a probability space, and let
\mathcal{F}\subset L^{2}(S,\mathcal{S},P)
be a
P
-pre-Gaussian class. Suppose that
G_{P}
is a tight Gaussian random variable in
l^{\infty}(\mathcal{F})
with zero mean and covariance function
\mathrm{Cov}_{P}(f,g)=\mathrm{E}(G_{P}(f)G_{P}(g))
,
f,g\in\mathcal{F}
. Assume that there exists some constants
\bar{\sigma}
,
\underline{\sigma}
such that
\underline{\sigma}^{2}\leq\mathrm{Var}_{P}(f)\leq\bar{\sigma}^{2}
, where
\mathrm{Var}_{P}(f)=\mathrm{Cov}_{P}(f,f)
. Then, for any
\varepsilon>0
, we have

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}G_{P}f-x\right|\leq\varepsilon\right)\leq C_{\sigma}\varepsilon\left(\mathrm{E}\left(\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}G_{P}f\right)+\sqrt{1\vee\log(\underline{\sigma}/\varepsilon)}\right),

(436)

where
C_{\sigma}\leq C(\bar{\sigma}/\underline{\sigma})^{3}
and
C>0
is a universal constant.

We note that the VC-type class satisfies the requirements of the anti-concentration inequality above (see Lemma 4.1 of
16
and Lemma 2.1 of
15
). We also note that there is a slight difference in the statement of this theorem, where we explicitly determine the dependence of the constant
C_{\sigma}
on
\bar{\sigma}
and
\underline{\sigma}
; See the proof in
17
.
