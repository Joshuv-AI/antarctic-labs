 Key Findings for Island Grids
• Without a minimum inertia requirement, in this island grid, system inertia can reach zero about 10% of the year
 based on the current scheduling strategy. This implies that these low/zero inertia cases could cause dynamic
 instability of the grid during the events without additional measures.

• With the fixed minimum inertia requirement, the island grid will experience a large amount of PV curtailment
 (13.7%). This reduces the economic benefits of PV and PV + storage systems. And it cannot ensure frequency
 stability either.

• Compared to the fixed minimum inertia requirement (Iner. 350 case), the proposed linear regression-based Freq.
 Constr. method can save about $13 million/year and reduce the probabilities of triggering the UFLS to 0.20%.

• Battery energy storage systems (BESSs) play an important role in frequency stability. When enabled to provide
 fast frequency support—such as primary frequency response (PFR)—BESSs can significantly enhance system
 performance. In particular, when BESSs are charging, they offer “double” headroom, which is highly benefit.

• However, new challenges can arise during non-PV hours when BESSs are fully discharging. In low-inertia systems
 with limited headroom from synchronous generators, a discharging BESS can potentially trip and behave like a
 generator outage. As a result, this creates the largest N-1 contingency, posing additional operational risks for
 islanded power systems.

Lessons learned from Island Grids
General:
• Scheduling results couldn’t guarantee the stability of a future low-inertia grid
  without considering stability constraints. This requests a detailed
  dynamic/contingency check for simulating a grid with high RE penetration.

• Enforcing an inertia constraint could improve the frequency response of the grid
  but can take away the economic benefits of installing new PV plants by curtailing
  more renewables.

• Adding adaptive stability constraints/requirements into the scheduling model
 can be one of the effective ways to improve grid stability from the scheduling
 perspective. However, this is an emerging field that will require further research,
 innovation, and validation.
Note: We haven’t considered using synchronous machines as synchronous condensers.
