Executive Summary
The Glen Canyon Dam (GCD) is a Colorado River Storage Project (CRSP) power resource that
is a component of the Salt Lake City Area Integrated Projects (SLCA/IP). The 2016 record
of decision (ROD) for the GCD long-term experimental and management plan (LTEMP) final
Environmental Impact Statement (EIS) specified criteria for GCD monthly water releases, daily
and hourly operating limits, and experimental releases. This report examines the financial
implications of the high flow experiment (HFE) conducted at GCD during the spring of Water
Year (WY) 2023 as required by the LTEMP HFE Protocol. This report is part of a series of
reports that describe the financial costs of LTEMP experimental releases since the 2016 ROD
was adopted in January 2017 [1]. Previous reports analyzed the impact of several past HFEs
and Bug Flow Experiments [2–4].
 This report focuses on the HFE conducted in April 2023. For this experimental release,
financial costs of approximately $1.33 million were incurred because the HFE required sustained
water releases exceeding the power plant’s maximum turbine flow rate. In addition, during the
experiment, operators were not allowed to shape GCD power production, either to follow Firm
Electric Service (FES) customer day-ahead energy deliveries or to respond to market prices.
 This study identifies the main factors contributing to the HFE costs and examines the
interdependencies among these factors. It applies an integrated set of tools to estimate Western
Area Power Administration (WAPA) financial impacts by simulating GCD under two types of
cases; namely, (1) a “With Experiment” case that mimics the operations that actually occurred
and (2) a “Without Experiment” case that simulates operations under the assumption that the
HFE did not occur. The “With Experiment” case mimics operations during the HFE and the
entire month the HFE occurred. It complies with LTEMP hourly and daily operating criteria.
The “Without Experiment” case assumes that the HFE did not occur. The monthly water
release volume is assumed to be identical under both cases.
 The Colorado River Storage Project Python-based model (CRiSPPy) model [5] was the main
modeling tool used to simulate the dispatch of the GCD hydropower plant and associated water
releases from Lake Powell. In the modeling process, the research team used extensive data sets
and historical information on SLCA/IP power plant characteristics, hydrologic conditions, and
WAPA’s power purchases and sales prices. In addition to estimating the financial impact of
the HFE, the team used the CRiSPPy model to gain insights into the interplay among ROD
operating criteria, exceptions made to criteria to accommodate the HFE, and WAPA operating
practices.

 VI

1 Introduction
The GCD Powerplant (referred to as the Powerplant in this report) consists of eight generating
units with a continuous operating capacity of 1,320 megawatts (MW) at unity power factor. It is
the largest component of a system of coordinated resources known as the SLCA/IP that serves
the demand of 5.8 million consumers in 10 western states located in the Western Interconnection
(WI). In the early days of its operation, the Powerplant had few restrictions. Except for a
minimum water release requirement, the daily and hourly operations of the Powerplant were
initially constrained only by the physical limitations of the dam structures, the Powerplant,
and the water storage level in Lake Powell, the reservoir formed by the GCD. The Powerplant
dispatch was principally driven by CRSP loads and market price signals, which often resulted
in large fluctuations in the Powerplant output and associated reservoir water releases.
 Concerns about the impact of GCD operations on downstream ecosystems and endangered
species, including those in Grand Canyon National Park, prompted the U.S. Bureau of Recla-
mation (USBR) to conduct a series of research releases from June 1990 to July 1991 as part of
an environmental studies program. Based on impact analyses of these releases, USBR imposed
operational flow constraints on August 1, 1991. These constraints were implemented through
February 1997, when new operational rules and management goals specified in the GCD EIS
ROD were adopted [6]. The 1996-ROD operating criteria, which implement the monthly low
fluctuating flow regime, limited hourly maximum and minimum water release volumes from the
dam. The 1996 ROD criteria also constrain the change in the water release between consecutive
hours and implemented a daily change limit that restrict the range of hourly releases on a rolling
24-hour basis.
 1996 ROD restrictions were eventually replaced by criteria specified under the LTEMP ROD
that was phased in during calendar year 2017 [1]. It required changes to (1) the allocation of
monthly Lake Powell water releases, (2) daily and hourly operating criteria, and (3) experiments
that are periodically conducted at GCD. These restrictions do not allow Powerplant operators
to fully utilize GCD water and hydropower resources, resulting in grid costs in terms of both
firm capacity and energy production.
 In the first phase that began on January 1, 2017, LTEMP altered monthly Lake Powell wa-
ter release volumes. Hourly and daily operating criteria were implement in a second phase that
began on October 1, 2017 (i.e., the first day of WY 2018). The GCD Adaptive Management
Program, established by the 1996 GCD EIS ROD [6], conducts scientific studies on the relation-
ship between Powerplant operations and downstream resources. Experimental water releases are
performed periodically to monitor river conditions, conduct specific studies, enhance native fish
habitat, and conserve fine sediment in the Colorado River corridor in Grand Canyon National
Park. Such experimental water releases include HFEs and Bug Flow Experiments.
 This report follows several other financial analyses of GCD experiments that began in 1997
[2–4, 7, 8]. This report focuses on the HFE that was conducted in April 2023. The purpose of
HFEs is to release high volumes of water to mimic natural floods and redistribute sediment,
enhancing ecological and physical conditions along the Colorado River downstream of the dam
[9]. The 2023 spring HFE prescribed a fixed pattern of GCD water releases over a 4-day period.
During 83 hours, the prescribed releases exceeded the Powerplant’s maximum turbine flow rate
by up to 14,000 cubic feet per second (cfs). Water release in excess of the Powerplant’s maximum
turbine flow rate was routed through GCD bypass tubes. This “non-power” water release did
not produce energy and thus resulted in a financial cost to WAPA; that is, the opportunity to

route non-power water releases through the Powerplant at a later time to produce energy was
lost. Non-power water releases also increase the tailwater elevation below the dam thus reducing
the hydraulic head and water-to-power conversion efficiency. This report describes the method
that was used to model the operation of SLCA/IP hydropower resources, which includes GCD,
and discusses the estimated financial costs of conducting this experiment. During the 2023 HFE
period, operational flexibility was essentially eliminated. Water had to be released according to a
fixed/prespecified schedule, and this prevented operators from responding to market price signal
and customer demand by fully utilizing GCD hydropower resources. The CRiSPPy model [5]
was used to estimate the financial impacts of the HFE by simulating GCD operations under two
types of cases; namely, (1) a “With Experiment” case that mimics the operations that actually
occurred and (2) a “Without Experiment” case that simulated operations under the assumption
that the HFE did not occur. The financial cost of the HFE is calculated as the difference in
WAPA’s financial position between the two cases. GCD monthly water release volume decisions
are assumed to be identical under both cases. As a result, the study period was limited to April
2023.
 The “With Experiment” case mimics historical operations under the HFE and during the
month of April 2023. The HFE event occurred from April 24 through April 27, 2023. During
the event, the turbine and bypass water release followed a specific HFE pattern [9]. Outside of
this time period, GCD was governed by the daily and hourly operating rules specified by the
2016 LTEMP ROD. The “Without Experiment” case simulates the water and power operations
that would have occurred without HFE. These operations also comply with LTEMP hourly and
daily operating criteria. It is assumed that the total volume of water released in April 2023
would have been the same with or without HFE.

2 Glen Canyon Dam operating rules
The 2016 LTEMP ROD describes the operating constraints at GCD. These include (1) the
allocation of monthly Lake Powell water releases, (2) daily and hourly operating criteria, and
(3) experiments that are periodically conducted at GCD.

2.1 Hourly and Daily Operating Criteria and Exceptions
Operating criteria specified in the 2016 ROD are intended to temper the rate of change in
hourly and daily water releases. The criteria selected were based on the LTEMP Alternative as
described in the final ROD [1]. These criteria were put into practice by WAPA from October
2017.
 Flow restrictions under the 2016 ROD are shown in Table 2.1. For comparison purposes,
they are represented along with operational limits in effect between February 1997 and October
2017 that were complying with the 1996 ROD, and operational limits prior to June 1, 1991. The
2016 ROD criteria require water release rates to be 8,000 cfs or greater between the hours of
7:00 a.m. and 7:00 p.m. and at least 5,000 cfs at night. The criteria also limit how quickly the
release rate can increase and decrease in consecutive hours. The maximum hourly increase (i.e.,
the up-ramp rate) is 4,000 cfs/hour (hr), and the maximum hourly decrease (i.e., the down-ramp
rate) is 2,500 cfs/hr.
 The 2016 ROD operating criteria also restrict the range of release rates during rolling 24-
hr periods. This change constraint depends on the monthly volume of water releases. Daily
fluctuation (in cfs/24-hr) is limited to 10 times the monthly release specified in thousands of
acre-feet (TAF) from June to August. From September to May, it is limited to 9 times the
monthly release volume. The daily range can never exceed 8,000 cfs. In April 2023, the total
volume of water released was 910 TAF, resulting in a daily fluctuation of 8,000 cfs/hr.
 The maximum flow rate is limited to 25,000 cfs under the 2016 ROD operating criteria. It is,
however, allowed to exceed this limit in order to avoid spills or flood releases during high-runoff
periods. Under very wet hydrological conditions, defined as when the average monthly release
rate is greater than 25,000 cfs, the flow rate may also be exceeded; however, water must be
released at a constant rate. Exceptions to the operating criteria are also made to accommodate
experimental releases. For the experiment discussed in this report, maximum flow rates above
25,000 cfs were allowed during the 4 days of the HFE.
 In addition to the 2016 ROD operating rules, WAPA follows other scheduling practices.
These practices fall within ROD operational boundaries but are not ROD requirements. One
practice involves releasing the same volume of water during weekdays. It also requires weekend
and holiday release volumes to be no larger than weekday release volumes but not smaller than
85 percent of weekday release volumes.

2.3 Scheduling guidelines
The hourly scheduling of SLCA/IP hydropower plant operations is performed by WAPA’s En-
ergy Management and Marketing Office (EMMO) located in Montrose, Colorado. Schedulers
make decisions based on a set of scheduling priorities and guidelines, including a directive to
comply with several reservoir-specific environmental operating criteria.
 As operational constraints were imposed on SLCA/IP resources, scheduling guidelines and
goals shifted from objectives driven primarily by market prices to objectives driven by customer
loads. Within the boundaries of these operating constraints, SLCA/IP power resources are
used to serve firm load. Because of operational limitations, WAPA staff may need either to
purchase or sell varying amounts of energy on an hourly basis in the day-ahead and/or real-time
market. In the day-ahead market, such transactions are scheduled in 16-hr, on-peak blocks,
and 8-hr, off-peak blocks. In the past, the volume of these variable market purchases and sales
used to be relatively small. However, evolving energy market and dry conditions led to more
significant transactions. As a result, in 2022, WAPA implemented the Deliverable Sales Amount
(DSA) [16]. The DSA is the amount of hydropower capacity and energy, determined quarterly,
based on the forecasted SLCA/IP generation. Under DSA rules, each customer receives its
proportionate share of the marketable hydropower capacity and energy.
 The load-following objective and DSA rules create a strong link between WAPA’s contrac-
tual obligations and SLCA/IP hydropower plant operations, requiring dispatch among the hy-
dropower plants to be closely coordinated. This interdependency exists because loads and
hydropower resources are balanced whenever feasible. WAPA is able to affect the shape of its
FES customer load requests indirectly through specifications in its contract amendments. In
turn, these customer loads affect both SLCA/IP powerplant operations and hourly reservoir
releases. The load-following dispatch directive minimizes scheduling problems and helps WAPA
avoid noncompliant water releases.
 An unwritten scheduling practice followed by WAPA involves maintaining a number of ramp-
ing cycles that is consistent with customer load patterns but not greater than two. This practice
applies to all CRSP plants and is mainly followed to minimize turbine wear and tear. In addi-
tion, GCD operators generally follow the same 24-hr release pattern for all weekdays of a given
month. The same practice applies to all Saturday 24-hr patterns, and to Sunday 24-hr patterns.

3 Experimental release description
In April 2023, a HFE was conducted at GCD to mobilize sediment and rebuild sandbars along
the Colorado River downstream of the dam. This event marked the first time an HFE was
implemented in the spring since the 2016 ROD, taking advantage of favorable hydrologic condi-
tions and full Powerplant capacity. The primary objective of the HFE was to redistribute sand
inputs from the Paria River to enhance sandbar and beach formation in Marble Canyon and
Grand Canyon, which are critical for recreational use, ecological habitats, and archaeological site
protection [9]. This spring HFE was implemented under the guidelines of the LTEMP ROD and
demonstrated adaptive management in response to sediment availability and system flexibility.

 Figure 3.1: Hydrograph of the April 2023 HFE [17]

 The experiment was conducted from April 24 to 27, 2023, featuring a 72-hr sustained peak
release (nearly four days including ramp-up and ramp-down phases) at approximately 39,500
cfs, combining 25,500 cfs through the turbines and an additional 14,000 cfs via bypass tubes [17].
Starting at 2:00 a.m. Mountain Standard Time (MST) on April 24, releases were ramped up
from baseflows (10,500 cfs) toward full Powerplant capacity (25,500 cfs) at a rate of up to 4,000
cfs/hr, followed by the opening of bypass tubes at 6:00 a.m. MST, reaching full combined
discharge (Powerplant and bypass) by 9:00 a.m. MST. Following the 72-hour peak release,
ramp-down started on April 27 at 9:00 a.m. MST, first reducing flow at approximately 1,750
cfs/hr down to powerhouse-only operations (25,500 cfs), and then at about 2,500 cfs/hr back
toward pre-event baseflow levels (14,000 cfs), ending flow adjustments later that evening. This
operational profile, including controlled ramp rates and peak duration, adhered to LTEMP
constraints (non-experimental maximum ramp-up and ramp-down of 4,000 and 2,500 cfs/hr,
respectively), while maximizing capacity by using all eight generating units and full bypass tube
capability. The hydrograph of the 2023 HFE is depicted in Figure 3.1.

4 Methodology
4.1 Modeling tool
The 2023 HFE analysis was performed using the CRiSPPy model. The CRiSPPy model is
a recent Python-based hydropower scheduling model specifically designed and developed for
WAPA CRSP and their needs. It uses advanced machine learning (ML) and optimization
techniques to accurately represent the hydropower operations of the SLCA/IP system [5]. The
model computes optimal hourly water release schedules driven by customer loads and energy
prices while taking into account monthly release targets and physical and operational rules.
Users can also manually fix hourly power and non-power releases of modeled plants. The model
operates at an hourly level, simulating power and non-power release schedules, power generation,
reservoir storage volume, and forebay elevation, as well as the system’s day-ahead and real-time
energy transactions.

4.2 Study scope and modeling cases
Unlike previous HFEs, the 2023 HFE did not involve a reallocation of monthly release volumes
at GCD. This means that the monthly release volumes at GCD during WY 2023 are assumed
to remain unchanged regardless of whether the HFE took place. Consequently, the financial
impact of the 2023 HFE is confined to the month in which the event occurred (April 2023),
and the analysis time scope is limited to that month. Other SLCA/IP resources were required
to compensate for the reduced operational flexibility of the GCD during the HFE event. As a
result, all SLCA/IP resources (including Flaming Gorge, Blue Mesa, Morrow Point, and Crystal)
were being modeled in the analysis.
 For the 2023 HFE analysis, financial impacts were calculated by comparing simulated results
between two cases: (1) a “With Experiment” case that mimics the operations that actually
occurred and (2) a “Without Experiment” case that simulated operations under the assumption
that the HFE did not occur.
 In the “Without Experiment” case, it is assumed that the HFE did not occur, and the release
schedule of all SLCA/IP resources, including GCD, is optimized for the entire month of April
2023. Releases at GCD are optimized by the CRiSPPy model following the operating rules
outlined in section 2. Releases at the other SLCA/IP resources are optimized according to their
respective RODs; for example, Flaming Gorge adheres to its minimum flow requirements and
downstream flow constraints, both of which are embedded in the CRiSPPy model.
 In contrast, the “With Experiment” case assumes the HFE did occur. The hourly power
and non-power release rates at GCD during the days of the HFE event (April 24 to April 27,
2023) are fixed and set equal to their historical values, reflecting the actual HFE pattern. For
the remainder of April, GCD releases are optimized following the operating rules outlined in
section 2. To isolate the effect of the HFE on GCD, the release schedules of all other SLCA/IP
plants are held fixed to the values obtained in the “Without Experiment” case.
 A summary of the modeling assumptions made in both cases is shown in Table 4.1. For
the sake of simplicity, it is assumed that the HFE had no impact on energy prices. This
assumption is justified by the relatively limited market power of the GCD Powerplant within
the broader Western Electricity Coordinating Council (WECC) system. Accordingly, energy
prices are considered identical under both the “With Experiment” and “Without Experiment”

4.3 Input data
Hydrology data for the reservoirs and power plants of the SLCA/IP system are based on his-
torical monthly data from the USBR hydrologic database [15]. This information is used by the
CRiSPPy model to calculate model parameters such as water release volumes, water-to-power
conversion factor (PCF), power capacities, and maximum daily flow fluctuations. Parameters
such as PCFs are calculated by CRiSPPy using embedded pre-trained ML models. More details
can be found in the CRiSPPy report [5].
 Hourly power and non-power water release data at GCD during HFE days are imported from
the USBR hydrologic database [15] and held fixed in the “With Experiment” case.
 Outage data are based on planned outage operations files called “CRSP and LAP Unit
Availability” and provided by EMMO. This information is used by the CRiSPPy model to
calculate adjusted hourly plant capacity.
 Load data are based on pre-scheduled operations files called “Load and Resource” and pro-
vided by EMMO. For the sake of simplicity, customer load is aggregated with other types of
loads to represent the total amount of energy withdrawn from the system (Customer AHP load,
Miscellaneous load, Pump operations at Deer Creek, Transmission losses, and SLCA/IP system
energy exchanges into and out of the system).
 Although the power generation of the main hydropower plants (GCD, Flaming Gorge, As-
pinall, Fontenelle) is optimized by the CRiSPPy model, generation from smaller units within
the CRSP footprint is considered fixed and imported from the pre-scheduled operations files
provided by EMMO. These smaller units include Upper and Lower Molina, Deer Creek, and
Energy interchange into the SLCA/IP system.
 Energy price data are based on historical hourly energy transactions records called “Daily
Deals Reports” and provided by EMMO. For a given hour, the energy price is calculated as the
energy-weighted average of pre-scheduled and real-time energy transaction price.

4.4 Calculation of financial costs
The financial cost of the HFE is calculated as the difference in WAPA’s financial position between
the “With Experiment” and “Without Experiment” cases. For each case, the financial position
in a given hour is computed as the product of the total CRSP generation during that hour and
the corresponding energy price. Note that, based on the modeling assumptions, the energy price
remains the same under both cases. In addition, the generation profile of all power plants except
GCD is also identical under both cases. As a result, the financial cost in a given hour can be
expressed as the product of the difference in generation at GCD between the two cases and the
corresponding energy price. In other words, the financial cost of the experimental release at
GCD can be expressed as:

 H represents the set of hours in the study period. π(h) is the energy price in hour h.
P N oExp (GC, h) and P Exp (GC, h) are the generation at GCD in hour h under the “Without
Experiment” and “With Experiment”, respectively.

5.2 Financial results
Financial costs are derived from the generation outputs of the “With Experiment” and “Without
Experiment” cases, using the methodology outlined in Section 4.4. The total financial impact
of the April 2023 HFE is estimated at $1.33 million.
 Figure 5.5 illustrates the hourly distribution of financial costs. Due to the reduced volume
of water available in the “With Experiment” case, hours outside the HFE period are associated
with a net financial cost. In contrast, the high turbine release rate and corresponding generation
during the HFE event lead to negative financial costs, i.e., a positive financial impact, in most
hours of that period.
 A substantial portion of water was bypassed during the event, resulting in lost generation
potential. Additionally, the constrained operational flexibility during the HFE limited the Pow-
erplant’s ability to respond to energy price fluctuations. As a consequence, the negative financial
costs incurred during the HFE days were insufficient to offset the positive financial costs accrued
outside of the event period. Specifically, the net financial cost incurred outside of the HFE days
totals $2.03 million (Figure 5.6). This net financial cost is partially offset by a negative financial
cost of $0.70 million realized during the HFE event. Overall, the total net financial cost of the
April 2023 HFE is estimated at $1.33 million, representing a cost of $14.9 per acre-feet (AF) of
bypassed water.
