Integrating Spiritual Care Teaching Within the Australian Medical Curriculum
Authors: John Wenham, Megan C. Best, David W. Kissane AC
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundSpiritual care has been shown to be an important component of holistic patient care. However, students have reported it missing from current Australian medical school curricula. The aim of this study was to evaluate the impact of a three‐hour spiritual care workshop designed to enable final year medical students to take a spiritual history from their patients.MethodsWe used a prospective pilot study design to evaluate a novel half‐day workshop designed to equip final year medical students to assess the spiritual wellbeing of their patients. The impact of the spiritual care workshop was evaluated using video analysis of a formative objective structured clinical examination (OSCE) of a spiritual consultation using a standardised patient prior to and after the teaching episode. Students self‐assessed confidence scales pre‐ and post‐workshop. Student characteristics that might facilitate learning in this domain were assessed.ResultsThirty‐two final year medical students from four universities participated at two training sites. Video analysis by four independent assessors showed satisfactory inter‐rater reliability and demonstrated a statistically significant increase (p < 0.001) in the use of spirituality questions. The students' self‐assessed confidence scales pre‐ and post‐workshop demonstrated statistically significant increases in assessing all domains except empathic responsiveness; the greatest improvement was in the spiritual domain.ConclusionWhilst this study was confined to medical students, we believe that the objective and subjective effectiveness demonstrated in this spiritual care workshop will be readily translatable into multidisciplinary holistic communication skills training.

Background

In the United States of America (USA), extensive work on developing spiritual care teaching within the medical curricula, now present in 90% of their medical schools [1], has been led by a number of researchers including Puchalski [2]. In order to deliver quality spiritual care, a clear definition of spirituality is required. Consensus was sought amongst American experts, and they agreed on a definition of spirituality as, “the aspect of humanity that refers to the way individuals seek and express meaning and purpose, and the way they experience their connectedness to the moment, to self, to others, to nature, and to the significant or sacred.” ([3], p.887) Spiritual care is a vital component of holistic medical care [4]. Svalastog and colleagues defined holistic health as, ‘a relative state in which one is able to function well physically, mentally, socially, and spiritually to express the full range of one's unique potentialities within the environment in which one lives’ ([5], p.432). All graduating doctors should be equipped to appraise effectively all four aspects of their patients' holistic care needs.

Spiritual care teaching and training in medical schools worldwide has involved didactic lectures [6, 7, 8], small group discussions [6, 8], shadowing hospital chaplains [9], writing in reflective journals [10], literature appraisal [10], and use of role play [10, 11]. Integration of these learning and teaching methods into the medical curricula included both a one‐off module and a vertically integrated program across clinical teaching years for 6–16 h [9, 11]. Evaluation suggests evidence of reinforcement of participants' motivation and ability to learn. Assessment methods include journaling [10], essays [6] and questionnaires [10], with a recent shift towards role play with immediate feedback [11, 12, 13]. Reported outcomes include an appreciation of whole‐person directedness [10], attitudinal change towards spirituality [7, 8], skill development in history taking [11, 12] and growth in personal meaning and purpose that nurtured spirituality [9].

Ledford et al. (2014) used a teaching OSCE to prompt learners to engage with patients who want to talk about religion and/or spirituality (R/S). Overall, this innovation provided a model for physicians to engage willingly in discussion of spirituality with patients [12]. However, there was no assessment reported. Similarly, a third year OSCE circuit that had a case with a spiritual component inserted into it identified that only approximately 2% of students could explore the topic without specific teaching [14].

A randomised control trial of a theoretical/practical course run over 4 months, with a total of 24 h duration, delivered at a medical school in Brazil, showed a significant improvement in performance of spiritual history taking by the intervention group when compared with the control group [15]. However, the dropout rate of 43% suggests that perhaps a shorter intervention would be more broadly received. In 2024 at the University of Notre Dame, Australia, we designed a half‐day workshop to enable medical students to understand the evidence base for, and to develop communication skills in, spiritual history‐taking. We were guided on content by eight studies over the past 20 years which we reported in our previously published systematic review [16].

A survey of 147 General Practitioners (GPs) practicing in Australia regarding holistic consultations identified a lack of knowledge and skills; they desired further training, especially in spiritual history‐taking. There was strong support for this being included in Australian medical education [4]. We set out to address this gap in the medical school curriculum by designing and delivering a workshop to enable medical students to include spiritual assessment in the holistic care of their patients. Our own survey of students' views confirmed their desire for diverse instructional approaches that both challenge attitudes and develop skills [17]; this informed our pedagogy. The aim of this study was to evaluate the impact of a three‐hour spiritual care workshop designed to enable final year medical students to take a spiritual history from their patients.

Methods

Study Design

We used a prospective pilot study design [18] to assess the impact of our teaching intervention, using subjective confidence scales and objective formative OSCE assessments with simulated patients, prior to and following the workshop.

Participants

Medical students attending clinical placements at two Australian medical school rural sites were eligible to take part in the study. Medical School Education Officers sent eligible students an e‐mail providing information about the project. They were invited to an information session with the lead researcher who clarified the components of the project. Participant Information Sheets and written consent forms were provided. Those who voluntarily consented were briefed on the timetable for the project.

Workshop Design

Details on the design and content of the workshop have previously been published [19]. Evidence for spiritual care as part of holistic patient management was presented and two models of eliciting a spiritual history were taught. The core beliefs of four major world religions that may impact on healthcare decision making were summarised. Opportunities were included for students to practice spiritual history taking from a colleague; later they were assigned patient vignettes to role play belief‐driven healthcare decisions (Data S1). The workshop was run four times between April 2024 and April 2025 by the same facilitator.

OSCE Station Design and Setting

The OSCE stations were designed by the first author who has 12 years' experience of examining final year medical student OSCEs. Spiritual issues and concerns were woven into the simulated patients' scripts in the settings of end‐of‐life care and termination of pregnancy. Drafts were reviewed and modified by colleagues experienced in setting final exams at the Universities of Adelaide and Wollongong.

OSCEs were conducted both before and after the workshop and were recorded for video analysis by independent examiners. The first OSCE station (Box 1) took place in the week prior to the workshop, serving two purposes; firstly to benchmark each student's baseline ability in the spiritual care domain; it also allowed the student to identify their learning needs and thus act as a driver of engagement with the workshop itself. This OSCE sensitised learners to the organic experience of a challenging communication context, in this case R/S [12]. The post‐workshop OSCE (Box 2) was designed to reassess each student's ability following the learning intervention and allow any change in performance to be measured. It was scheduled between 1–14 days following the workshop depending on student availability.

Pre‐workshop OSCE instructions.

‘A 50‐year‐old lady comes to see you and breaks down in tears. Her 25‐year‐old daughter has just had a termination of pregnancy. She has found herself overcome with guilt as she had the same procedure herself 20 years ago. You have 12 minutes to explore her physical, emotional and spiritual needs.’

(Presented to the students outside the consultation room).

Post‐workshop OSCE instructions.

‘David is a 42‐year‐old gentleman who has come to see you today in the General Practice setting. His 66‐year‐old mother has a diagnosis of stage 4 bowel cancer and has decided that she would like to end her life through Voluntary Assisted Dying before she has to deal with the unbearable suffering that she believes will eventuate in the latter stages of the disease. She lives in a remote country town and she wants her three children to be with her. David is struggling with this for a number of reasons. You have 12 minutes to elicit a history, including a holistic exploration of David's beliefs, and to discuss an appropriate management plan.’

(Presented to the students outside the consultation room).

Preparation of Standardised Patients (SP)

Two simulated patients were recruited voluntarily at each site. Both had previous experience in acting and, after reading the full SP instructions (Appendices S1 and S2), were willing to play the required role for the number of student participants. Simulated patients rehearsed the role play scenarios with the facilitator until uniformity of presentation was achieved.

Development of Checklist

Two of the authors (JW and DK) developed the checklist of tasks to be demonstrated once the OSCE design had been completed. In particular students' abilities in taking a mental health history including risk assessment as well as their competence in taking a spiritual history were assessed. A checklist was developed for each of the OSCEs (Appendices S3 and S4). This defined the parameters of each score. Examiners were briefed individually to ensure understanding of each rating.

Real Time Feedback to Students

The first author (J.W.) was present for all the OSCE encounters; notes were taken and feedback was given to students during the workshop after the first OSCE: key tasks that were done well, those which were missed and areas for improvement for the group as a whole. Feedback on their communication skills was offered immediately following the post‐workshop OSCE. The first author's observations did not contribute to their assessment scores.

Student Demographics and Attitudes

At the start of the half‐day spiritual care training session, students were asked to complete a validated 20‐item ‘Survey of attitudes to whole person medicine and spirituality’. Researchers at Queen's University, Belfast, United Kingdom, used it to assess views of staff and students at the medical school [20], including demographic data, attitudes to holistic care and training in spiritual care (Appendix S5). Post‐workshop, these responses were mapped against changes in consultation skill performance.

Video Marking

Each OSCE was recorded. Thirty‐two pairs of videos were assessed using four examiners; two General Practitioners and two psychologists participated. Each video was marked by at least three examiners. Quantitative analysis of the simulated patient interviews was carried out using the checklist data from the first and second OSCEs, and mapped to psychological and spiritual domains. The frequency of each checklist item being displayed by the cohort of students was assessed. Student performance pre‐ and post‐training was compared. Inter‐rater reliability was assessed using Fleiss's Kappa coefficient.

Confidence Scales

At the end of the training, the students were asked to complete a questionnaire to assess their attitudes, knowledge and skills towards the subject and what they had learnt from the content of the session. Pre‐ and post‐workshop questionnaires contained a visual analogue scale to allow the students to self‐rate their current performance confidence from 1–10 in each domain: this included physical, psychological and spiritual history taking (Appendix S6).

Statistical analysis

Baseline sociodemographic characteristics of the student participants (including age group; gender; country/continent of birth; University, campus group, graduate entry, and year of training; previous experience of spiritual care training; and self‐identified spiritual worldview) were summarised using counts (percentages) for categorical variables. Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 29, Version 29.0.0.0 (241).

Pre‐ and post‐workshop subjective self‐rated confidence scales and objective OSCE performance scores were summarised using median (1st and 3rd quartiles, and interquartile range [IQR]) for non‐parametric skewed continuous numerical variables. Pre‐ and post‐workshop differences in confidence scales and OSCE scores were analysed using the paired Wilcoxon signed‐rank test, to identify subjective and objective change in performance and assess outcomes of the spiritual care workshop. Pre‐ and post‐workshop differences in confidence scales and OSCE scores were also analysed for multiple independent comparisons within each group of baseline sociodemographic characteristics using the Kruskal–Wallis test.

Normality was assessed by visual inspection of box plots. Missing data (< 5% for all variables) were analysed using complete‐case analysis. Statistical significance was defined as a two‐sided p < 0.05. Magnitude of effect for the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test was defined as r ≈0.10 as small, ≈0.30 as medium and ≈0.50 large [21].

Video marking

Two examiners marked 32 video pairs; a third examiner marked 12 pairs and a fourth 20 pairs, aiming to achieve consistency to the standard of assessment. Fleiss's kappa [22] for the three examiners who marked the first 12 students' video pairs as 0.488. For the three who marked the second 20 video pairs, Fleiss's kappa was 0.462.

The baseline OSCE scores prior to the training were compared with OSCE scores following the training (Table 2). A statistically significant increase (p < 0.001) in the students' use of spirituality questions was demonstrated. Participants had a higher median difference in their OSCE scores after the spiritual workshop compared with baseline in all three domains—psychological, spiritual and holistic assessment where holistic is the combined score of mental and spiritual health assessments. Students whose pre‐workshop attitude was either neutral towards the importance of spiritual care in patient management, or neutral on spiritual history being as important as physical history, had a greater increase in OSCE scores for spiritual/holistic care.

Confidence scales

Students' self‐assessed confidence scales pre‐ and post‐workshop (Table 3) demonstrate statistically significant increases in assessing all domains except their ability to show empathic responsiveness. The lowest self‐rating score for assessing spiritual symptoms prior to the workshop was 2 with a median of 3; following the workshop the lowest score was 7 with a median of 7. There was an increased confidence in spiritual assessment in those who disagreed that health workers should share their own spiritual beliefs with their patients. Students who agreed that health workers could share their beliefs if invited by a patient showed a greater improvement in empathic response. Students who reported a religious worldview were found to have a significant increase in empathic responsiveness when compared with agnostic or a spiritual worldview. The R/S grouping had a greater change in confidence score for psychological assessment than the agnostic/atheist grouping.

Discussion

Previous studies evaluating spiritual care training have relied on post‐intervention subjective feedback from students. Researchers have enquired about students' understanding of R/S [6, 9, 12], their perceived competence [13] and their willingness to apply this knowledge in future practice [6, 7, 9, 10]. Whilst a significant increase in student confidence has been shown in other research studies [8], demonstration of a higher level of clinical skills assessment, as described in Miller's pyramid [23], has not been included in any previous spiritual care curriculum evaluation. Recent research has shown that self‐assessed confidence or comfort in providing spiritual care is not related to competence [24]. In order to make a strong case for inclusion of such training in Australasian medical school programs, we subjected our workshop to more rigorous evaluation. We believe our study is the first to have used objective analysis of spiritual care training using video consultations with simulated patients combined with the subjective assessments of students' confidence scores utilised by other researchers [8, 11].

Enhanced Holistic Care

Following spiritual care training, student confidence increased significantly in all domains apart from one; the greatest subjective improvement was demonstrated in the spiritual domain. Scores remained unchanged for empathic response post‐workshop; this could reasonably be expected at this latter stage in medical school training [25]. Students demonstrated enhanced clinical skills with objective improvement in their ability to take an effective spiritual history.

Students demonstrated enhanced clinical skills with objective improvement in their ability to take an effective spiritual history.

Improvement in the quality of students' psychological assessments was also observed in this study. Despite the spiritual care workshop not specifically addressing the psychological domain in consultation skills, a positive change in student performance was demonstrated in this domain. We propose that in addressing spiritual history taking we have enhanced students' understanding of the necessity of holistic care for all patients [26]; as a consequence both spiritual and psychological exploration of patient histories improved. It is equally possible that, by providing feedback on the performance in the pre‐workshop OSCE, students would have reflected on their approach to psychological history taking and modified their communication skills in the subsequent OSCE [27].

Participant Characteristics

Selection of participants for this study was limited to a rural setting and relied on students who were on longer‐term placements and were willing to volunteer 5 h of their time. Participants are often self‐selecting depending on whether they have a personal interest in the subject being investigated [28]. The sample in this study showed a broad range of demographics and a wide variety of S/R beliefs (Table 1). We believe that our conclusions will be relevant to medical student curricula in both rural and urban medical schools in Australia and further afield no matter the student demographic or existing R/S beliefs. In our multiple independent comparisons we found that age, country of birth, university of origin, study site, being undergraduate rather than postgraduate, year of study and previous spiritual care training had no impact on the change in OSCE scores pre‐ and post‐ workshop. The only demographic variable that showed a statistically significant increase in median change in OSCE score was being female, a pattern often found in assessment of female medical students' history taking due to naturally increased aptitude to communication skills [29].

Calibration of examiner scoring

Marking of student performance in summative assessment requires substantial work on the examiners' checklist to ensure consistency so that students and academics can be confident in the validity and fairness of the process. The aim is for a Kappa coefficient above 0.7 for a pair of assessors [30]. This can be challenging to achieve as each examiner's observations are impacted by their own bias; they tend to focus on different aspects when making their judgements and this has proven hard to correct [31]. Final year medical schools' barrier exams use pairs of examiners and analyse scoring by individual assessors to gauge their marker type from hawk to dove. Fairness is achieved using up to 12 OSCE stations in a circuit with criteria clearly delineated [32]. In our project we used formative assessment in our OSCEs to measure change in performance post‐intervention. We believe that using the same three examiners for each student video before and after the workshop helped reduce the impact of any variance of inter‐rater reliability. However, our moderate kappa scores suggest room for improvement in definitions of grading assessment items on our checklists.

Limitations of This Study

Our pilot was a single arm study and included 32 subjects, using a pre‐ and post‐assessment design, to confirm feasibility and to calculate effect sizes; these in turn will power a future randomised controlled trial. Although the participant number may appear small, it proved sufficient to reveal statistically significant change and satisfactory effect sizes on change measures. The sample may have been skewed towards students who had an interest in developing a better understanding of spiritual and holistic care. It is already documented that students willing to study medicine in a rural setting have an aptitude inclined towards generalist practice [33]. The 32 students came from four universities who all have different baseline curricula and not all the students were at the same stage in final year. To ensure our pilot study size was sufficient for statistical power, a small number of third year students were recruited. This variation and inter‐rater reliability were mitigated by measuring improvement following training with comparison with baseline, both in confidence expressed by the students, and by independent examiners' video assessments.

Conclusion

Overall improvements in both confidence and OSCE scores demonstrate this spiritual care workshop to be both a feasible and worthwhile communication skills training intervention. In this paper we have shown both the subjective and objective benefit of embedding this training in medical school curricula. We commend our findings in this pilot study to educators from all health disciplines, noting that both nurses and chaplains have been involved in earlier curriculum work. We believe there is great translatability of this work to a multidisciplinary approach in holistic patient care. In the future, we aim to conduct a randomised controlled trial involving a broad range of healthcare students and properly powered, based on the effect sizes we found in this pilot study, to demonstrate the efficacy of this educational intervention.
