Immunotherapy and the novel therapeutic development for small-cell lung cancer
Authors: Midori Matsuo, Hirokazu Taniguchi, Shinnosuke Takemoto, Keisuke Mine, Takahito Fukuda, Fumiko Hayashi, Sawana Ono, Kazumasa Akagi, Hiromi Tomono, Noritaka Honda, Yosuke Dotsu, Kazuto Ashizawa, Hiroshi Mukae
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSmall-cell lung cancer (SCLC) is broadly classified into limited- (LS) and extensive-stage (ES) disease, with distinct therapeutic approaches and outcomes. Despite high initial response rates, most patients eventually experience disease progression, particularly in the ES setting, where effective treatment options remain limited. For several decades, platinum–etoposide-based chemotherapy has remained the cornerstone of treatment, with modest improvements in overall survival. Recent advances in cancer immunology have led to the incorporation of immune checkpoint inhibitors as the first-line treatment for many cancers, representing the first major therapeutic breakthrough for SCLC. In ES-SCLC, the addition of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) inhibitors to platinum–etoposide chemotherapy has become the standard first-line approach based on the survival benefits demonstrated in phase III trials. In LS-SCLC, a consolidation PD-L1 inhibitor following definitive chemoradiotherapy has recently emerged as a new standard, as highlighted by the ADRIATIC trial, which demonstrated a significant survival advantage with durvalumab. Despite these advances, the prognosis of ES-SCLC remains poor with limited response durability and lack of robust predictive biomarkers. Tumor heterogeneity, dynamic phenotypic plasticity, and the absence of actionable genomic alterations continue to hinder precision treatment strategies. In relapse settings, novel antibody-based therapies have begun to reshape the therapeutic landscape. Delta-like ligand 3 (DLL3)-targeted T-cell engagers and antibody–drug conjugates directed against DLL3 and B7-H3 have shown encouraging antitumor activity in heavily pretreated patients, with some agents advancing to late-phase clinical evaluations. This review summarizes the systemic therapies for SCLC, focusing on recent advances in immunotherapy and emerging antibody-based strategies. This review discusses current challenges, including treatment resistance, toxicity, and biomarker development, and highlights future perspectives aimed at enabling more personalized and effective therapeutic approaches for patients with this historically aggressive disease.Keywords: antibody–drug conjugates, biomarkers, chemoimmunotherapy, DLL3, small-cell lung cancer

Introduction

Small-cell lung cancer (SCLC) accounts for approximately 15% of all lung cancer cases worldwide and is an aggressive thoracic malignancy characterized by rapid tumor growth, early metastatic potential, and extremely poor prognosis.1–4 SCLC is commonly classified not only by the tumor, nodes, and metastasis system but also by disease extent as either limited stage (LS) or extensive stage (ES). LS-SCLC is a disease confined to one hemithorax that can be encompassed within a tolerable radiation field and is typically treated with concurrent chemoradiotherapy with curative intent. By contrast, ES-SCLC is characterized by the presence of the disease beyond a single radiation field, including distant metastases, and is primarily managed with systemic therapy, usually with palliative intent. These stages differ substantially in terms of the treatment strategies, response patterns, and clinical outcomes. Despite its initial sensitivity to treatment, SCLC is associated with a high risk of relapse and poor long-term outcomes, highlighting the need for improved therapeutic strategies and more precise approaches for patient selection.5–10

From a biological perspective, analyses of tumor immune profiles have demonstrated that programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression in SCLC tumor cells is generally lower than that observed in non-SCLC (NSCLC). This finding has consistently been reported in large clinical and translational studies.11–13 Furthermore, classical immunological studies have shown that SCLC exhibits markedly reduced expression of major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I molecules compared with NSCLC.14,15 This deficiency limits effective tumor immune recognition because MHC class I is essential for antigen presentation to cytotoxic T lymphocytes. Together, low PD-L1 expression and impaired antigen presentation suggest that SCLC represents an “immune-cold” phenotype and is therefore intrinsically less responsive to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) monotherapy than NSCLC.11–13,16

This review outlines the current treatment strategies for both LS and ES-SCLC. We then examined recent developments in immunotherapy and emerging antibody-based approaches, followed by a discussion on translational insights, including DNA damage response (DDR) targeting and biomarker research. Finally, we address the key challenges and future perspectives for optimizing treatment strategies for SCLC.

Methods

PubMed was searched to identify relevant literature published in English until March 31, 2026. The search terms included “small cell lung cancer,” “treatment,” “chemotherapy,” “immunotherapy,” “antibody–drug conjugates,” “bispecific antibodies,” and “DNA damage response.” Titles and abstracts were screened for relevance, and full texts were reviewed when appropriate. Original research and review articles relevant to SCLC were included, whereas non-English language articles and studies not directly related to SCLC were excluded. In addition, relevant clinical guidelines (e.g., those from the National Comprehensive Cancer Network, European Society for Medical Oncology, and other professional societies) were reviewed. Clinical trial registries, including ClinicalTrials.gov and the Japan Registry of Clinical Trials (jRCT), were searched up to March 31, 2026, to identify ongoing or unpublished clinical trials relevant to SCLC.

Advances in treatment of LS-SCLC

For LS-SCLC, surgical resection followed by adjuvant chemotherapy is recommended or considered in selected patients with clinical stage I or IIA disease without lymph node involvement, although chemoradiotherapy remains the standard treatment for most LS cases.17–20 Importantly, evidence from retrospective analyses and clinical guidelines indicates that adjuvant systemic therapy, typically platinum-based chemotherapy, such as cisplatin or carboplatin combined with etoposide, is associated with improved survival outcomes after resection in early stage SCLC; therefore, the use of postoperative platinum-based systemic therapy is recommended in this setting.17,21,22

For patients with LS-SCLC with lymph node metastases at stage IIA or higher, the long-standing standard of care has been to administer cisplatin plus etoposide chemotherapy combined with accelerated hyperfractionated thoracic radiotherapy (45 Gy in 30 fractions over 3 weeks) in patients with a good performance status. 23 Patients who achieve complete response to prophylactic cranial irradiation (PCI) should be considered. 23 In LS-SCLC, PCI has been associated with a reduced risk of brain metastasis and improved survival in selected patients, although the potential neurocognitive toxicity should be carefully considered. However, its role in ES-SCLC remains controversial in the era of routine MRI surveillance, given the lack of consistent survival benefits and concerns regarding neurotoxicity.

Although the prognosis of LS-SCLC remains poor, recent advances in the understanding of its biological features have provided important insights that may inform the development of novel therapeutic strategies to improve outcomes. Recently, the introduction of ICIs has advanced the treatment landscape of LS-SCLC, particularly in light of the demonstrated survival benefit when ICIs are incorporated as a consolidation therapy following definitive chemoradiotherapy. Several clinical trials have evaluated, or are currently evaluating, the role of ICIs in LS-SCLC using different strategies, including concurrent administration with chemoradiotherapy and consolidation therapy after definitive treatment (Table 1). Although most early phase and ongoing studies have demonstrated feasibility, robust phase III evidence remained limited until recently. The ADRIATIC trial represents a pivotal milestone in this field, demonstrating a significant survival benefit with consolidation durvalumab after chemoradiotherapy and redefining the standard treatment paradigm for LS-SCLC. 24 This evolution in the treatment paradigm for LS-SCLC, from chemoradiotherapy alone to consolidation immunotherapy following definitive treatment, is schematically illustrated in Figure 1. The ADRIATIC trial was a phase III, randomized, double-blind study that enrolled patients with LS-SCLC who had not experienced disease progression after definitive concurrent chemoradiotherapy and who had a good Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS 0–1). The patients were randomized to receive maintenance therapy with durvalumab, durvalumab plus tremelimumab, or placebo. To date, results comparing durvalumab with a placebo have been reported. 25 Durvalumab significantly improved both progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) compared with placebo, with a median PFS of 16.6 versus 9.2 months (hazard ratio (HR), 0.76; p = 0.02) and a median OS of 55.9 versus 33.4 months (HR, 0.73; p = 0.01), thereby meeting both primary endpoints. Importantly, beyond the improvement in median survival, some patients achieved prolonged survival, suggesting the potential for long-term disease control and possible curability in selected cases. Subgroup analysis suggested particularly favorable outcomes among patients who received PCI; however, these findings are exploratory and should be interpreted with caution. These observations support the integration of consolidation durvalumab into routine clinical practice, while also highlighting the unmet need for refined biomarkers to further personalize treatment strategies in LS-SCLC.

However, the ADRIATIC trial has some limitations that must be considered when interpreting these findings. The study population was highly selected, including only patients with a good performance status (ECOG PS 0–1) who did not experience disease progression after concurrent chemoradiotherapy, which may limit generalizability to the broader LS-SCLC population encountered in routine clinical practice. Although the survival benefit was statistically significant, the subgroup analysis results should be carefully interpreted as these were exploratory and not powered for definitive conclusions. The optimal integration of consolidation immunotherapy with other modalities, such as PCI, and its role in patients with comorbidities or less favorable clinical conditions remain unclear.

By contrast, the recent phase III NRG Oncology/Alliance LU005 trial, which evaluated the addition of atezolizumab concurrently with chemoradiotherapy, did not demonstrate a significant OS benefit compared to chemoradiotherapy alone. 29 These findings underscore the ongoing uncertainty regarding the optimal timing and integration of immunotherapy in LS-SCLC, and suggest that treatment sequencing may be critically important.

In parallel with the establishment of consolidation immunotherapy as a new standard of care following definitive chemoradiotherapy, growing interest has been focused on identifying biomarkers that may further refine patient selection and optimize therapeutic benefits in LS-SCLC. Recent studies employing serial circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) analyses during and after definitive chemoradiotherapy in patients with LS-SCLC have demonstrated that patients with persistent or insufficient ctDNA clearance, which reflects molecular residual disease, are at a substantially higher risk of disease progression. 30 In these analyses, ctDNA was typically assessed at predefined time points after chemoradiotherapy completion and prior to consolidation treatment. Importantly, patients with detectable ctDNA levels after chemoradiotherapy derived greater PFS benefits from consolidation immunotherapy than those who achieved early and deep molecular clearance. Collectively, these findings suggest that ctDNA-based dynamic risk stratification may serve as a complementary tool to the current clinical criteria, enabling a more personalized application of consolidation ICI therapy, while reinforcing the survival benefit established by the ADRIATIC trial. However, ctDNA remains an investigational biomarker, and its clinical utility requires validation in prospective trials before it can be incorporated into routine practice.

Advances in first-line treatment for ES-SCLC

For several decades, platinum-based chemotherapy combined with etoposide has remained the standard first-line treatment for ES-SCLC.31–33 However, despite high initial response rates, disease relapse is inevitable, and long-term survival outcomes remain poor. Recently, treatment paradigms have evolved with the introduction of ICIs. Although SCLC appears to be biologically less immunogenic than NSCLC, the addition of PD-L1 inhibitors to platinum–etoposide chemotherapy has demonstrated consistent survival benefits in randomized phase III trials. The phase III CASPIAN trial demonstrated that the addition of the PD-L1 inhibitor durvalumab to platinum–etoposide chemotherapy, followed by durvalumab maintenance, significantly prolonged OS compared with chemotherapy alone in patients with ES-SCLC (median OS, 12.9 vs 10.5 months; HR, 0.75; p = 0.0032). 34 Similarly, the pivotal IMpower133 phase III trial showed that atezolizumab combined with carboplatin and etoposide, followed by atezolizumab maintenance, resulted in a significant OS benefit over chemotherapy alone (median OS, 12.3 vs 10.3 months; HR, 0.70; p = 0.007). 35 Based on the consistent survival benefits observed in these two pivotal trials, platinum–etoposide-based chemoimmunotherapy has become the standard first-line treatment for patients with ES-SCLC. Notably, these findings suggest that, while SCLC is intrinsically less responsive to ICI monotherapy owing to low PD-L1 and MHC class I expression, chemotherapy may enhance tumor immunogenicity by promoting antigen release and modulating the tumor microenvironment, thereby enabling a clinically meaningful synergy with PD-L1 inhibition in the first-line setting. In addition to the improvement in median OS, a subset of patients derived durable long-term benefits, as reflected by the sustained separation of survival curves over time. These trials demonstrated statistically significant survival benefits. However, the absolute improvement in the median OS was modest. Furthermore, the study populations were selected, and the benefits in certain subgroups (e.g., patients with poor performance status or untreated brain metastases) remain unclear, warranting cautious interpretation in real-world settings, particularly when extrapolating these findings to broader patient populations.

To further improve outcomes beyond the current standard of chemoimmunotherapy followed by maintenance of PD-L1 inhibition, intensified maintenance strategies are being actively investigated. The phase III IMforte trial evaluated the addition of lurbinectedin, a selective transcription inhibitor, to maintenance immunotherapy. The phase III DeLLphi-303 trial assessed tarlatamab, a DLL3-targeted T-cell engager, in a first-line maintenance setting.36,37 If these approaches demonstrate clinical benefit, they may substantially reshape the treatment paradigm of ES-SCLC. In particular, the introduction of these active agents into the earlier lines of therapy could shift their use away from second-line or later settings, thereby altering the current treatment sequence. This may reduce the availability of effective therapeutic options for relapses and necessitate the development of new post-progression strategies. In addition, earlier exposure to T-cell-engaging therapies may influence subsequent treatment responses through the emergence of resistance mechanisms, including antigen loss and T-cell dysfunction. These considerations highlight the importance of optimizing treatment sequencing and identifying predictive biomarkers to guide patient selection in an era of intensified first-line maintenance therapies.

In this evolving therapeutic landscape, no robust predictive biomarkers have been established to identify patients most likely to benefit from chemoimmunotherapy. Recent studies have proposed a molecular classification of SCLC into four distinct subtypes based on transcriptional profiles, characterized by high expression of ASCL1, NEUROD1, and POU2F3, as well as an inflamed subtype defined by immune-related gene signatures, which may provide a framework for biomarker development; however, this approach has not yet been validated for routine clinical use.38–40 This molecular taxonomy, initially established through RNA sequencing analyses, has been associated with differential sensitivity to ICIs and other therapeutic strategies. Importantly, emerging evidence suggests that these subtypes can be assessed using immunohistochemistry, offering a more practical approach for clinical implementation, although this strategy has not yet been incorporated into routine practice. Comprehensive integrative analyses have highlighted that SCLC subtypes are not fixed entities, but rather represent dynamic cellular states with substantial phenotypic plasticity. SCLC cells can transition between neuroendocrine-high and non-neuroendocrine or inflamed states in response to intrinsic factors and therapeutic pressures, a process governed by epigenetic and transcriptional regulators. This plasticity may contribute to treatment resistance and intratumoral heterogeneity while also creating context-dependent therapeutic vulnerabilities. 41 Simultaneously, analyses of the tumor immune microenvironment have indicated that lower densities of tumor-associated macrophages and higher infiltration of effector T cells are associated with more favorable clinical outcomes, further supporting the relevance of the immune context, together with subtype identity and plasticity, as a potential predictive framework for immunotherapy responsiveness in SCLC. 42 However, at present, these approaches are primarily hypothesis-generating and are not yet ready for clinical decision-making. Among emerging strategies, BNT327 (pumitamig), a bispecific antibody targeting PD-L1 and VEGF-A, has recently attracted attention for ES-SCLC treatment. Early phase studies combining BNT327 with platinum–etoposide chemotherapy have demonstrated encouraging preliminary antitumor activity, and phase III evaluations are ongoing.

Advances in subsequent-line treatment for ES-SCLC

Despite these advances in first-line treatment, disease progression remains inevitable in most patients with ES-SCLC.8–10 Outcomes after relapse remain poor, as effective subsequent-line treatment options are limited. Consequently, the management of relapsed ES-SCLC has long been a major unmet clinical need. Treatment outcomes are strongly influenced by the pattern of relapse, with patients experiencing platinum-sensitive relapse receiving greater benefits from subsequent chemotherapy than those with refractory disease.43,44 The subsequent-line therapy for ES-SCLC relies almost exclusively on cytotoxic chemotherapy. Topotecan is the only approved agent based on randomized clinical trials and has remained the standard second-line treatment for many years.45–47 However, its clinical benefits are modest, with limited response rates and considerable hematological toxicity, particularly in patients with chemotherapy-refractory disease. Collectively, these approaches failed to meaningfully alter the natural history of relapsed ES-SCLC, underscoring the need for more effective biomarker-driven therapies, such as delta-like ligand 3 (DLL3)-targeted agents.

Parallel to this unmet need, rapid advances in antibody-based drug technologies have led to the development of novel therapeutic platforms, including antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) and bispecific antibodies capable of simultaneously engaging two distinct molecular targets.48,49 Among these emerging approaches, tarlatamab is a DLL3-targeted T-cell engager designed to redirect cytotoxic T cells toward tumor cells. Tarlatamab is a bispecific antibody that binds to DLL3 expressed on SCLC tumor cells and CD3 on T cells, thereby physically bridging the two cell types and inducing T-cell activation, proliferation, and subsequent tumor cell lysis. In the United States, tarlatamab received regulatory approval in 2024 for the treatment of patients with ES-SCLC who experience disease progression after platinum-based chemotherapy. In the phase II DeLLphi-301 trial, tarlatamab demonstrated robust antitumor activity in heavily pretreated patients, with an objective response rate of 40%, median duration of response of 9.7 months, and median OS of 14.3 months in a third-line or later setting. 50 Furthermore, the phase III DeLLphi-304 trial met its primary endpoint by demonstrating a statistically significant improvement in OS compared with cytotoxic chemotherapy in the second-line setting (median OS, 13.6 vs 8.3 months; HR, 0.60; p < 0.001). 51 PFS was also significantly prolonged (median PFS, 4.2 vs 3.7 months; HR, 0.71; p < 0.001), establishing tarlatamab as the first biomarker-driven therapy to show a survival benefit over chemotherapy for relapsed ES-SCLC. Subsequent analyses demonstrated that this survival benefit is maintained over time and is generally consistent across clinically relevant subgroups, regardless of age, sex, or prior exposure to ICIs. 51 Favorable outcomes were also observed in patients with brain metastases, whereas the benefit was less pronounced in patients with liver metastases.

Notably, tarlatamab treatment is associated with characteristic immune-related toxicities, including cytokine release syndrome and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome, both of which can be severe and require careful monitoring and management.50,51 Other commonly observed adverse events include pyrexia, fatigue, decreased appetite, myelosuppression, and hepatic dysfunction, highlighting the importance of proactive toxicity management in clinical practice. Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests multiple mechanisms of resistance to T-cell-engaging therapies, including loss of target antigen expression, upregulation of immune checkpoint inhibitory ligands, alterations in intracellular signaling pathways that confer resistance to immune-mediated cytotoxicity, dysfunction of effector T cells, and the presence of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment.52,53

Although DLL3-targeted T-cell-engaging therapies represent a major therapeutic advance in the subsequent-line treatment of ES-SCLC, their unique toxicity profiles, emerging resistance mechanisms, and lack of predictive biomarkers highlight important challenges that must be addressed. These issues form the basis for future research aimed at optimizing patient selection, improving patient safety, and developing next-generation therapeutic strategies, as discussed in the following section.

DDR and immune modulation in SCLC

SCLC frequently harbors loss-of-function alterations in RB1 and TP53, resulting in defects in cell cycle checkpoints and increased susceptibility to DNA damage. These molecular vulnerabilities provide a rationale for targeting DDR pathways as a therapeutic strategy (Figure 3). 54 Inhibition of DDR proteins, such as PARP, CHK1, WEE1, and ATR, has been shown to increase replication stress and unrepaired DNA damage in preclinical SCLC models, leading to the accumulation of cytosolic DNA and activation of the cGAS–STING innate immune pathway. Activation of the cGAS–STING pathway results in increased expression of chemokines such as CCL5 and CXCL10, thereby promoting the recruitment of cytotoxic T cells into the tumor microenvironment (Figure 4).4,55–57

In addition to DDR inhibition strategies, DNA-damaging agents such as temozolomide have been explored in SCLC. 58 Temozolomide induces DNA damage leading to replication stress and cell death. Its efficacy, particularly in relapsed settings, is closely associated with MGMT expression, with low MGMT expression or promoter methylation linked to improved responses. Furthermore, PARP sensitivity is associated with high SLFN11 expression, which impairs replication in response to DNA damage.59,60 These biomarkers highlight the importance of patient stratification in optimizing DDR-targeted therapies in SCLC.

However, a substantial proportion of these findings have been derived from preclinical studies, and clinical evidence supporting this therapeutic strategy remains limited. Several clinical trials evaluating PARP inhibitors in combination with anti-PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies have demonstrated modest and inconsistent clinical activity with no clear improvement in survival outcomes. Early phase studies have reported low response rates and short PFS, indicating limited and inconsistent clinical activity.61,62 This discrepancy between preclinical and clinical findings may be attributed to several factors, including the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment of SCLC, the limited and potentially transient immunogenic effects of DDR inhibition, and the lack of robust predictive biomarkers for patient selection. Therefore, although DDR-targeted strategies remain promising, their clinical utility in combination with ICIs will likely depend on improved patient stratification and a deeper understanding of resistance mechanisms.

Translational efforts have led to the development of the DDR-immune fitness score, which integrates homologous recombination deficiency, tumor mutational burden, and STING pathway activity to predict which patients are most likely to benefit from DDR-targeted immunotherapy combinations. 63 This approach remains investigational and requires prospective clinical validation. Several clinical trials are currently evaluating PARP, CHK1, and WEE1 inhibitors in combination with ICIs for the treatment of SCLC (NCT04538378, NCT04728230, NCT05718323, and NCT05815160), and their clinical efficacy and safety have been actively investigated.

Challenges and future perspectives

Despite recent advances in first-line chemoimmunotherapy and subsequent-line treatments, the overall prognosis of ES-SCLC remains poor, and several critical challenges remain unresolved. Disease relapse is almost inevitable in most patients, and therapeutic options after disease progression continue to provide limited and short-lived clinical benefits. Emerging evidence indicates that some patients may experience durable responses and long-term survival after chemoimmunotherapy, suggesting that ES-SCLC may not be uniformly associated with poor outcomes in the immunotherapy era. The increasing use of ICIs has highlighted the clinical importance of immune-related adverse events, underscoring the urgent need to identify patients who are most likely to benefit from immunotherapy.

Genomic analyses of SCLC have demonstrated that approximately 90% of tumors harbor inactivating mutations in the tumor suppressor genes TP53 and RB1. 64 However, these genomic alterations are currently not actionable therapeutic targets and do not predict responsiveness to platinum–etoposide chemotherapy combined with PD-L1 inhibitors. Moreover, established biomarkers widely used in other malignancies, such as PD-L1 expression and tumor mutational burden, have shown limited utility in predicting treatment outcomes in SCLC, reflecting the unique biology and profound intratumoral heterogeneity of this disease.

In parallel with immunotherapeutic approaches, ADCs have emerged as a promising and novel therapeutic strategy for SCLC. Besides DLL3, several tumor-associated antigens, including B7-H3, SEZ6, and TROP2, have been actively investigated as ADC targets. ADCs consist of a monoclonal antibody directed against a tumor-specific or tumor-associated antigen, a potent cytotoxic payload, and a linker connecting these components. Upon binding to the target antigen, ADCs are internalized into tumor cells, where lysosomal cleavage of the linker releases the cytotoxic payload and induces tumor cell death through mechanisms such as DNA damage or microtubule disruption. Furthermore, some ADCs exert a bystander effect, enabling cytotoxic activity against neighboring tumor cells with low or heterogeneous antigen expression. Multiple ongoing and recently reported clinical trials have evaluated ADCs and other novel therapeutic agents in patients with SCLC. Several of these agents have demonstrated encouraging antitumor activity (Table 2). Earlier ADC approaches, such as rovalpituzumab tesirine (Rova-T), failed to demonstrate a survival benefit and have been associated with significant toxicity in randomized clinical trials. 65 By contrast, newer ADCs targeting DLL3, B7-H3, and other tumor-associated antigens have shown encouraging clinical activity with manageable safety profiles in early phase studies. 66 Among these investigational agents, DLL3- and B7-H3-directed ADCs appear to be the most clinically advanced, with several compounds demonstrating encouraging response rates in heavily pretreated patients. Some agents are currently being evaluated in phase III trials.

In addition to ADC-based strategies, novel immunotherapeutic approaches that target alternative immune regulatory pathways are being actively investigated. Bispecific antibodies that simultaneously engage T cells and tumor-associated antigens or immune checkpoints represent promising strategies for enhancing antitumor immunity. Although bispecific antibodies targeting PD-L1 and CD3 have shown encouraging activity against other malignancies in preclinical studies, their clinical application in SCLC remains unclear. 73 Another emerging target is the T-cell immunoreceptor with Ig and ITIM domains (TIGIT), an inhibitory receptor that contributes to immune evasion. While anti-TIGIT antibodies in combination with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors have demonstrated promising results in NSCLC, clinical evidence in SCLC remains limited, with recent phase III trials not demonstrating clear survival benefits, and their therapeutic roles remain to be defined. 74

From a clinical perspective, the optimal integration of ADCs into the current treatment algorithms remains unclear. Currently, these agents are most likely to be administered in a relapsed or refractory setting, particularly in patients who have exhausted standard second-line options, where effective treatment options are limited. Further clinical trials are needed to clarify whether ADCs can be incorporated earlier into the treatment course or combined with existing regimens. In addition, patient selection based on target antigen expression and prior treatment exposure is critical for maximizing clinical benefits. Therefore, although ADCs are a promising therapeutic option, their optimal clinical positioning, including patient selection and sequencing with existing therapies, remains unclear.

Although these advances represent important progress, several challenges remain before these approaches can be fully integrated into routine clinical practice. As the number of available therapeutic options increases, optimizing the treatment selection for individual patients will become increasingly complex. Accordingly, the development of reliable and clinically feasible predictive biomarkers that integrate tumor-intrinsic molecular features, tumor immune microenvironment, and dynamic changes during treatment is essential. Future strategies that incorporate simplified molecular subtyping, liquid biopsies, and rational combination therapies may ultimately enable more personalized and effective treatment paradigms for patients with ES-SCLC.

Discussion and conclusions

Despite the incremental improvements achieved with first-line chemoimmunotherapy, ES-SCLC remains characterized by relapse, limited response durability, and a persistent lack of clinically actionable predictive biomarkers. The biological heterogeneity and dynamic phenotypic plasticity of SCLC continue to hinder precise treatment approaches, particularly in post-progression settings.

Emerging therapeutic strategies, particularly ADC targeting DLL3 and B7-H3, represent promising avenues for improving the outcomes of patients with relapsed ES-SCLC. Early phase clinical trials have demonstrated encouraging response rates even in heavily pretreated populations, and the ongoing phase III evaluation of selected ADCs marks an important step toward potential clinical integration. However, the optimal positioning of these agents within the existing treatment algorithms remains unclear.

In LS-SCLC, the ADRIATIC trial represents a major step forward in demonstrating the survival benefits of durvalumab consolidation after concurrent chemoradiotherapy. However, several unresolved issues remain, including the optimal duration of immunotherapy and lack of validated predictive biomarkers, highlighting the need for refined patient selection.

Altogether, the recent progress in both ES- and LS-SCLC highlights a gradual shift toward more biologically informed treatment strategies. Future studies should focus on developing clinically feasible biomarkers and rational treatment strategies to translate these advances into long-term clinical benefits.
