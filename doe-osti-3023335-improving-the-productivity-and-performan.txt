Executive Summary:
 This project addressed one of the high priority research areas within biofuel technologies:
Cultivation Intensiﬁcation Processes for Algae (CIPA). The overarching goal of this project was to
develop and demonstrate an algal biofuel production system that can produce fuel at a cost
below the BETO minimum fuel selling price (MFSP) trajectory target of approximately $3.00 per
gallon of gasoline equivalent (gge). For this project, we provided experimental data and techno-
economic analysis showing the ability to produce drop-in biofuels from wastewater derived
algal biomass at a cost of $3.32 and identiﬁed methods to further reduce costs as noted below.
This goal for cost-competitive biofuels was pursued through four main methods:
 • Utilize a commercially available algal wastewater treatment (WWT) system
 Algaewheel®, which provides an additional revenue stream for treated wastewater,
 eliminates the need for costly fertilizer inputs and requires less land footprint than
 using algal raceway ponds to produce the same amount of biomass.
 • Optimize the Algaewheel system for biofuel production by improving the biomass
 quality and productivity from 20 g/m2/day, to at least 30 g/m2/day (>50%) through
 operational adjustments and improvements, and experimental treatments including
 endoreduplication, bioaugmentation, adsorbents, and heating.
 • Improve the hydrothermal liquefaction (HTL) conversion yield of the wet Algaewheel
 biomass to bio-oil via nanoﬁltration membrane ﬁltration of the HTL aqueous product
 and recycling the concentrated organics back into the HTL process.
 • Optimize the HTL process to increase conversion yields by 20% and address aqueous
 byproduct disposal by treating it with nanoﬁltration.
 This project achieved these research objectives and demonstrated at large scale that this
wastewater treatment and biofuel conversion technology is not only economically feasible, but
lower in cost than conventional activated sludge (CAS) wastewater treatment. We exceeded the
productivity metrics at demonstration scale throughout the project with these methods,
achieving a typical productivity of 44-64 g/m2/day at the northern site (project goal of 30
g/m2/day), and upwards of 130 g/m2/day at the southern ﬁeld site. We also exceeded the bio-oil
conversion yield goal, achieving a yield increase of 37-43% (project goal of a 20% increase).
 Wastewater treatment via the Algaewheel can reduce wastewater treatment costs by 11%
compared to CAS treatment. When accounting for this cost savings relative to CAS, integrating
Algaewheel based wastewater treatment with biofuel conversion by HTL and nanoﬁltration can
provide a negative minimum fuel selling price (MFSP) as low as –$9.45/gge compared to prior
established estimates of +$2.78 to +3.11/gge. All Algaewheel treatment scenarios resulted in a
lower Global Warming Potential (GWP, kg of CO2eq) compared to CAS, except for a wastewater
heating scenario that had a high electricity input. Up to a 53% reduction in GWP per MG of
wastewater treated was estimated for the southern site condition with higher loading. Our algal
raceway scenario resulted in the lowest GWP due to greater fossil fuel avoidance; however, this
option comes with an increase in WWT cost and signiﬁcantly larger land area requirements.

Background:
 In recent years, U.S. government agencies and research institutions have emphasized the
importance of developing algae-based, low-energy, highly effective wastewater treatment
technologies, along with the need of developing low-cost, reliable feedstock supply systems to
provide renewable carbon resources (BETO 2023). Algae-based wastewater treatment
technologies are gaining increasing attention for their ability to remove and recover nutrients
while generating algal biomass that have various applications such as biofuels (Peter et al.,
2021; Mohsenpour et al., 2021; Li et al., 2019). The Algaewheel system is an example of a robust
and cost-effective algal wastewater treatment process that has been pilot tested and now
implemented in several full-scale installations (Johnson et al., 2016, 2018). It uses rotating
wheels to promote mixed species algal and bacterial bioﬁlms via alternating sunlight and CO₂
exposure (Johnson et al., 2016, 2018). The resulting bioﬁlm sloughs off and can then be used as
a fertilizer or biofuel feedstock (Kuo, 2017; Johnson, 2019). This approach represents a shift
from conventional, energy-intensive treatment toward integrated biomass and nutrient
recovery. The Algaewheel wastewater treatment system provides a unique approach to algal
biomass production with dual-use infrastructure that lowers the net cost of biofuel production
because the typical fees collected for wastewater treatment can also be used for producing
biofuel feedstocks. However, its broader adoption is challenged by limited understanding of
how to ensure stable effluent quality and consistent biomass production under variable
environmental and wastewater inﬂuent conditions (Johnson et al., 2019; Molitor et al., 2024).
 The Algaewheel technology, while primarily a wastewater treatment system has signiﬁcant
potential for biomass production. It grows a mixed native algal-bacterial biomass in a ﬁxed ﬁlm
utilizing wastewater as a growth media, versus a pure culture grown with specialized media,
raceway ponds, and it is also different than traditional wastewater treatment by conventional
activated sludge (CAS), which grows cultures of suspended bacterial cells that consume excess
organics. The primary advantages are lower net energy input, and less land requirements than
most biofuel feedstocks. This research was focused on addressing BETO’s barrier Aft-B
(Sustainable Algae Production) by bringing this technology from wastewater treatment only, to a
dual use of sustainable wastewater treatment and biofuel production. In addition, we have
investigated how to boost biomass yields with a variety of approaches that improve the overall
economic feasibility of using it as an algae cultivation system. Speciﬁcally, bioaugmentation
and endoreduplication activities targeted Aft-C (Biomass Genetics and Development) aiming to
improve productivity and robustness of algal strains. Finally, Aft-J (Resource Recapture and
Recycle) was addressed through nanoﬁltration experiments on the aqueous by-product of
hydrothermal liquefaction (HTL) biofuel conversion processes, where residual organics were
separated from nitrogen compounds to achieve higher oil yields, and reclaim nutrients for a
fertilizer co-product. Our research ﬁnished with full-scale demonstration of algae cultivation
operations that further progress towards BETO programmatic goals.

 Hydrothermal liquefaction (HTL) is an emerging thermochemical technology that converts
wet biomass into a biocrude oil, which can be further reﬁned into drop-in liquid fuels with
characteristics similar to their fossil fuel counterparts. These biofuels require minimal to no
modiﬁcations to existing vehicle ﬂeets, while achieving signiﬁcantly lower life cycle greenhouse
gas (GHG) emissions. Because of its versatility and capability to handle high-moisture biomass,
HTL is one of the most promising pathways for converting algal biomass into fuels and value-
added chemicals. Due to elevated cultivation costs and competing demands of single-species
microalgae cultivation, mixed algal-bacterial biomass from wastewater treatment systems has
emerged as an attractive feedstock for HTL. Despite its great potential, HTL also produces a
nutrient- and carbon-rich waste stream known as HTL aqueous phase (HTL-AP), which requires
proper treatment and/or disposal. Addressing the treatment and valorization of this byproduct
remains one of the key challenges impeding the widespread, full-scale implementation of HTL.
Therefore, this study investigated the integration of membrane ﬁltration as a valorization
method for the HTL-AP of algal-bacterial biomass.

Project Objectives:
 The overarching goal of this project was to develop and demonstrate an algal biofuel system
that can produce fuels at or below the DOE’s minimum fuel selling price target ($2.50-
$3.50/gge). The goal was achieved through integrating a commercially available algal
wastewater treatment system, Algaewheel®, with hydrothermal biofuel conversion technology.
The Algaewheel® wastewater treatment system was designed based on meeting typical
wastewater effluent quality goals, and thus, there were opportunities to further improve algal
biomass productivity of the system. In this project, we proposed to develop and apply a suite of
novel biological and engineering tools to enhance algal biomass productivity, quality and
system robustness. The end of project goal was to improve the annual biomass productivity
from a baseline of 20 g/m2/day to 30 g/m2/day, a 50% improvement. The biomass quality and
biocrude oil yield was also to be improved by 20% (from 30% to 37.5%). Figure 1 compares the
2015 DOE baseline state of the technology (SOT) for algal biofuels with the proposed approach
using the Algaewheel system, HTL conversion technology, and several system enhancements to
be demonstrated on this project. The proposed system with improvements has a projected
areal biofuel productivity is 4700 gal/ac-yr, which more than 5 times the current DOE techno-
economic baseline (900 gal/ac-yr). Table 1 summarizes the project milestones that were
proposed and Table 2 shows the timing of Project Tasks and Milestones in a Gantt schedule
format including a 1-yr no cost extension during Budget Period 2 (BP2).

 Introduction
 Bacterial co-cultivation has been shown to enhance algal tolerance to environmental
stresses through mutualistic interactions (Saravanan et al., 2021; Ramanan et al., 2016; Amin et
al., 2009). For instance, the heterotrophic bacterium Dinoroseobacter shibae supplies essential
micronutrients like vitamins B1 and B12 to its dinoﬂagellate host, supporting growth under
nutrient-limited conditions (Yang et al., 2024; Cooper et al., 2019). Similarly, the cobalamin-
producing bacterium, Sinorhizobium meliloti, activates the B12-dependent methionine synthase
in algae under heat stress, preventing chlorosis and algal death (Xie et al., 2013). Some bacteria
also produce extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) or antibiotic compounds that protect
algae from environmental ﬂuctuations and harmful pathogens (Wang et al., 2016). These
synergistic relationships highlight the potential of bioaugmentation with functional bacteria to
enhance algal productivity and resilience, particularly in systems like the Algaewheel, which are
designed to promote symbiotic relationships between algae and bacteria. The Bioaugmentation
tasks in this project were designed to address BETO target area Aft-C (Biomass Genetics and
Development) investigating how to boost biomass yields with a variety of approaches that
improve productivity and robustness of algal strains, thereby improving the overall economic
feasibility and resilience of algae cultivation systems.
 Indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), an auxin hormone, has exhibited capacity to promote growth in a
variety of plants, including algae (Dao et al., 2018; De-Bashan et al., 2008; Jusoh et al., 2015;
Lambrecht et al., 2000; Peng et al., 2020). This growth-promoting effect has been demonstrated
across multiple algal strains, including Chlamydomonas, Chlorella pyrenoidosa, Chlorella
vulgaris, Haematococcus pluvialis, Nannochloropsis oceanica, Scenedesmus quadricauda, C.
sorokiniana (De-Bashan et al., 2004; Lee et al., 2019; Lin et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2016; Touliabah
et al., 2021), with improvements observed in biomass dry weight and cell density. The impact of
IAA typically becomes evident within a week of application (Chung et al., 2018; Zhang et al.,
2021; Peng et al., 2020) but is highly dependent on the dosage and algal species. While low
concentrations of IAA can promote growth, higher concentrations may have the opposite effect,
leading to inhibition (Chung et al., 2018; Peng et al., 2020). The optimal IAA concentration was
found to be 10 nM for Scenedesmus quadricauda (Kozlova et al., 2017), and it was 230 µM for
Chlorella pyrenoidosa and 100 µM for Desmodesmus komarekii (Chung et al., 2018; Liu et al.,
2016). Recent studies report IAA can enhance algal biomass, secondary metabolite production,
starch and lipid content simultaneously under stress conditions, offering a solution for
producing high-value algal products without compromising biomass yields (Lin et al., 2022;
                                            Page 10 of 175

Babu et al., 2017; Pagnussat et al., 2023; Palacios et al., 2016). The IAA’s low effective
concentrations (in the µM or nM range) and rapid impact make it a promising candidate for
large-scale algal cultivation. Furthermore, utilizing symbiotic bacteria capable of synthesizing
IAA offers long-term growth stimulation, providing a viable strategy for enhancing algal
productivity in commercial applications. In a previous study of algae-bacteria symbiosis,
sixteen out of forty isolated bacterial strains from algal culture were found to produce IAA, with
which the algal biomass productivity increased by more than 70% (Zhang et al., 2021). Similarly,
Dao et al. found ten out of twenty-six bacteria isolated from Scenedesmus sp. LX1 cultivated in
the domestic wastewater can produce IAA (Dao et al., 2018). Series studies have demonstrated
that IAA produced by Azospirillum brasilense can enhance CO₂ ﬁxation and promote
intracellular ammonium and polyphosphate accumulation in Chlorella vulgaris, highlighting its
potential in wastewater treatment applications (Choix et al., 2018; Meza et al., 2015a, 2015b).
However, the symbiotic relationships between algae and IAA-producing bacteria have been
mainly studied to individual algae cultures on a benchtop scale. No implementation methods
for bioaugmenting IAA-producing bacteria at industry-scale have been developed.
 The overall goal of this study was therefore to develop a bioaugmentation strategy using
IAA-producing bacteria to improve algal productivity in an up-scale study of Algaewheel
wastewater treatment system. The main bacterial species tested in our experiments was
Azospirillum brasilense (ATCC 29729). A. brasilense is a producer of indole-3-acetic acid (IAA),
a plant growth-promoting phytohormone auxin. After our bench-scale algal assays co-culturing
A. brasilense with mixed algae and pure algae showed increased algal growth, it was
determined that A. brasilense and its associated chemical IAA would be the best treatment
options moving forward into pilot-scale and demo-scale testing. Pseudomonas denitriﬁcans
and Phaeobacter inhibens were also tested in the lab for potential bioaugmenting effects, but
neither were selected for testing beyond the pilot-scale and bench-scale respectively. P.
denitriﬁcans is known to produce Vitamin B-12 and Phaeobacter inhibens produces an
antibiotic to protect algae from pathogens.
 A. brasilense, a well-studied plant growth-promoting bacteria, was selected for
bioaugmentation treatment due to its capacity of secreting a variety of metabolites and
hormones, including IAA, to boost plant and algae development (Zaheer et al., 2022; Bible et al.,
2015; Ramos-Ibarra et al., 2019). Chemical/bacterial dosage, dosing frequency, and
bioaugmenting conditions were investigated in benchtop experiments leading to a pilot study
using Algaewheel tanks. With the determined methods, the bioaugmentation treatments were
implemented to an industrial-scale Algaewheel system operating at a wastewater treatment
plant for validation and optimization. The ﬁndings could provide valuable insights into the
practical application of bioaugmentation in enhancing algal productivity and wastewater
treatment efficiency, promoting sustainable and cost-effective environmental management
solutions.

  Bench-Scale Experiments – Task 5
 Background & Methods
 Bench-scale laboratory experimentation provided a small-scale, short-term demonstration
of potential algae bioaugmentation concepts. Over the course of 50+ experiments spread over 4
years, our team tested potential algal treatment options for subsequent pilot-scale and
demonstration-scale algae cultivation. At the end of these experiments, we were able to identify
an effective bioaugmenting bacterial species that improved algal productivity, the algal growth-
promoting chemical, and their respective dosing information.
 Our results from benchtop algae experiments gave insight into how best to apply both
bacterial and chemical treatments in scaled-up experiments moving forward. Typical
experimental testing involved algal growth assays with various treatment options, conducted in
triplicate or quadruplicate. Reﬁnement of treatment conditions after several trials resulted in
our most successful bacterial species option (A. brasilense), chemical treatment options (IAA),
dosage concentrations, and dosage frequency. Assays utilized either mixed algae or pure algae
cultures. Mixed algae culture was obtained from our pilot-scale Algaewheel system, grown in
synthetic wastewater (dog food-soaked water), which was a mixture of various ﬁlamentous and
microalgae species as well as other normally occurring microorganisms (bacteria, rotifers). This
type of assay was particularly helpful in determining treatment effectiveness under wastewater-
based Algaewheel-type conditions. Growth was measured with ash-free dry weight (volatile
suspended solids) biomass analysis. Pure algae cultures consisted of freshwater axenic
cultures (singular species) in speciﬁc medium including Chlorella vulgaris or Chlamydomonas
reinhardtii, with growth measured via optical density at 680nm. A. brasilense bacterial cultures
were maintained with sterile procedures and were grown in their own medium containing
sodium malate as a carbon source and tryptophan (an amino acid and precursor to IAA) to
facilitate IAA production. Our experiments made it clear that the presence of sodium malate
and tryptophan in our A. brasilense cultures were instrumental in quick culture growth and
sufficient IAA production. Additional information about our bioaugmentation methods can be
found in the Supplemental Information (SI) document.
 Results & Conclusions
 IAA chemicals were directly tested in a set of experiments to determine if the synthesized
chemical alone could produce enhancement effects. After high doses (>500µM) of IAA
chemical were dosed, an inhibitory effect on algal growth was seen, whereas lower doses (20-
100 µM) demonstrated an enhancement effect (Figure 2A). IAA is quickly absorbed by algal
bioﬁlm and other microorganisms in wastewater conditions, so it was apparent that IAA
chemical treatment would need to be continuously supplemented to remain at effective levels.
Notably, under conditions resembling wastewater treatment, IAA is absorbed by both algae and
bacteria, including its producer A. brasilense (Figure 2B). The IAA uptake rate by the algal
bioﬁlm was approximately 4–10 µM per day. This suggests that enhanced algal growth is
transient, as the effective IAA concentration is depleted within a few days without continuous
                                          Page 12 of 175

supplementation. For large-scale implementation, it would be ideal to ensure continuous IAA
addition or co-culture bacteria capable of producing IAA at an effective rate, rather than relying
on periodic spiking of IAA at inhibitory concentrations.
 Effects of the IAA producing bacteria on C. vulgaris algal growth were further examined at
different culturing conditions (Figure 2C), which all show the enhancement effects. When C.
vulgaris was co-cultured with A. brasilense at 106 and 108 cells/L, the medium was amended
with tryptophan, an amino acid and a precursor of IAA, to ensure an immediate extracellular IAA
production up to 50 µM. As a result, the algal biomass in A. brasilense treatments at both cell
densities had 50-270% increased growth than the bacteria-free control group after 3-7 days
growth. Furthermore, growth of C. vulgaris was promoted when being treated by the spent
medium of A. brasilense at 106 cells/L while incubated in the presence of tryptophan, showing
40-300% more biomass than the control during day 3-10. The observed enhanced algal growth
can be attributed to the uptake of IAA produced by A. brasilense, as the spent medium
contained secretions from A. brasilense, with IAA being the primary component, as measured
with IAA concentration testing. Finally, when the C. vulgaris was co-cultured with A. brasilense
at 106 cells/L without tryptophan (dark blue bar), the promoted algal growth did not show until
day 10, likely because of a delayed and reduced IAA production resulting from lacking an
immediate presence of IAA precursor. As mentioned above, the one-time addition of A.
brasilense bacteria or spent medium seems to only promote the algal growth initially, as the
extended observation of this experiment showed decreased biomass in all treatment groups.
This temporary effect can be attributed to the depletion of nutrients and IAA as a stimulator.

 The impact of continuous A. brasilense dosing on algal growth was evaluated by varying
dosing frequency under realistic conditions using mixed algal cultures in synthetic wastewater.
All groups received an initial high dose of 10⁸ cells/L, followed by smaller additions at different
frequencies but equal total dosage. As shown in Figure 3A, groups with only an initial dose or
one dose per week showed no signiﬁcant increase in biomass compared to the control after
three weeks. Only groups with twice and three times per week dosing showed a signiﬁcantly
increased biomass over control at this point. The reduced enhancement effect in the bacterial
treatment groups was likely due to depletion of IAA and its precursor. Although A. brasilense can
still produce IAA without added tryptophan (which can be synthesized as an amino acid),
limited access to tryptophan can negatively impact IAA production rates and levels.
Additionally, the experiment demonstrated a reduction in the ash content of algae following

                                             Page 14 of 175

bioaugmentation (Figure 3B). Overall, to sustain the enhancement effects, continuous dosing is
required with higher dosing frequencies providing better results, as A. brasilense cells may be
outcompeted by other species in the co-culture. A. brasilense exhibited slower growth when co-
cultured with wastewater-relevant bacterial species, such as P. denitriﬁcans and native bacteria
in synthetic wastewater (SWW), compared to its growth in pure culture (Figure SI1). Therefore,
in the practical ﬂow-through wastewater treatment system, continuous bioaugmentation is
necessary for A. brasilense to maintain their role in the indigenous microbial community.

 Our A. brasilense culture preparation for bioaugmentation requires the maintenance of a
pure culture, a task typically carried out under sterile lab conditions. However, such
maintenance practices and lab conditions are not always feasible in wastewater treatment
                                             Page 15 of 175

plants or other industrial projects. To bridge this practicality gap, we explored the application of
a commercial product: a commercial rooting powder product, Big Foot® Root Boost, which
claims to contain 1x107 CFU/g Azospirillum brasilense. A powder mixture was plated and
positively identiﬁed with a high degree of conﬁdence as Azospirillum brasilense with Matrix
Assisted Laser Desorption Ionization Time of Flight (MALDI-TOF) bacterial colony identiﬁcation
at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign College of Veterinary Medicine Diagnostic Lab
(Figure SI2). In addition to pure culture dosing, this rooting product was utilized for scaling up A.
brasilense microbial treatment dosing at both demonstration sites, Task 10 and 11, as
described in their respective sections below.
 A set of experiments were conducted to determine if the chemicals associated with A.
brasilense growth, sodium malate and tryptophan, would be able to facilitate enhanced algal
growth without the A. brasilense present. In these experiments, algae with added sodium
malate experienced a spike in growth compared to the control after about a week, but any
enhancement effects evened out after 3 weeks (Figure SI3). From this result, we attempted to
use these chemicals at the pilot Algaewheel system as a treatment solution with plans of
eventually utilizing the treatments at the full-scale system, depending on the results.

 Pilot-Scale Experiments – Tasks 3 and 5
 Background
 Experiments performed at the pilot Algaewheel system served the purpose to demonstrate
and reﬁne various experimental treatments before implementing at the full-scale wastewater
Algaewheel sites in Task 10 and 11. Pilot-scale experimentation involved the use of four 200-
gallon (757L) Algaewheel pilot tanks housed at a greenhouse on the UIUC campus. Each tank
had six Algaewheels and was continuously fed with synthetic wastewater (see SI for more
information) to simulate full-scale wastewater treatment. By adjusting harvesting frequency,
water retention time, and swapping wheels between the tanks, the performance of all pilot
systems was standardized, achieving a biomass productivity benchmark of 15-22 g/m²/day and
soluble chemical oxygen demand (sCOD) removal efficiency of 90% within 24 hours. Each
week, the suspended biomass from the test tanks was harvested, concentrated using a clariﬁer,
and analyzed for ash-free dry weight to determine algal biomass productivity.
 Building on the results from benchtop experiments, the effects of A. brasilense
bioaugmentation and IAA chemical treatments were further examined using the Algaewheel
pilot system. The purpose of these pilot experiments was to reﬁne bioaugmentation treatment
viability, dosage, and practical application before implementation to the full-scale Algaewheel
systems. Three main rounds of microbial and chemical bioaugmentation experiments were
conducted during the summer and early fall months at the pilot site in 2022 and 2023. Our
results indicated that the addition of some chemicals, including sodium malate and
tryptophan, alone did not signiﬁcantly inﬂuence algal productivity on the pilot scale system.

                                             Page 16 of 175

This led us to continue exploring the addition of IAA chemical solution and IAA-producing A.
brasilense culture directly to the system as treatment methods. These two methods, chemical
IAA treatment and microbial A. brasilense treatment, led to increased productivity in the pilot
and were subsequently scaled up for use in the Task 10 and Task 11 Algaewheel full-scale ﬁeld
sites.

 Methods and Results
 Sodium malate and tryptophan chemicals, which are both important to the growth of
microbes and production of IAA respectively, were tested in a pilot Algaewheel tank to improve
algal productivity of the system. In any pilot experiment using sodium malate in an Algaewheel
tank, glycerol was used in any comparable tank since both chemicals increase the sCOD of the
wastewater. Initially, neither of these additions showed improvement in growth alone in the pilot
experiments. This contrasted with bench-scale experiments demonstrating increased growth
after adding sodium malate to mixed algae (Figure SI3). We also attempted to include sodium
malate as a supplement in the Algaewheel SWW feedstock, but high doses caused a severe
midge larvae outbreak. After these results at the pilot, sodium malate was not directly used in
the full-scale Algaewheel experiments.
 A supplementary experiment, in which tryptophan was consistently added to the
Algaewheel tank containing microbial communities capable of converting tryptophan to IAA,
showed no discernable enhancement effects until A. brasilense cells were added (Figure SI5).
The lack of enhancement using tryptophan alone suggests the conversion from the added
tryptophan to IAA did not occur, because tryptophan was competitively utilized by other
organisms. As an amino acid, tryptophan can be preferentially taken up by various species
before it can be used for IAA production. These ﬁndings suggest the addition of IAA precursor is
unnecessary for implementing bioaugmentation in large scale cultivation. Both chemicals,
sodium malate and tryptophan, continued to be used in the cell culture of A. brasilense to
facilitate its IAA production.
 P. denitriﬁcans was also tested during these periods by dosing cultures several times per
week into a designated treatment tank. At the bench-scale, P. denitriﬁcans was shown to
improve algal biomass, but no productivity improvements were observed in pilot-scale
experiments. High frequency and high cell density dosages of P. denitriﬁcans may have
contributed to these results, as larger amounts of P. denitriﬁcans present may consume the
algae’s nutrient resources, such as nitrates. Less frequent dosing (once per week) of P.
denitriﬁcans was shown to inﬂuence algal growth in bench experiments as opposed to more
frequent doses. Due to these suboptimal results, P. denitriﬁcans was not used as a treatment
beyond the pilot-scale experiments.
 This set of pilot results mentioned previously (Figure SI5) was signiﬁcant, but not
conclusive enough as to the potential efficacy of A. brasilense and IAA treatments speciﬁcally.
                                            Page 17 of 175

The DOE BP2 interim veriﬁcation team recommended that A. brasilense microbial and IAA
chemical treatments be re-tested at the pilot-scale before moving on to full-scale to determine
their respective dosing and treatment viability. Two treatments, microbial A. brasilense and
chemical IAA, were conducted in two pilot Algaewheel tanks over the summer months. A.
brasilense cultures were added to reach a cell density of 1-4×10⁹ cells/L on a weekly or biweekly
basis. Each dose consisted of 10-20L of well grown (7-14 days growth) culture with IAA
produced microbially (see SI for more details). Previous batch experiments indicated that an
effective bacterial dosage ranges from 1×10⁶ to 1×10⁹ cells/L (Figure 2 and Figure 3), and that
A. brasilense could be outcompeted by the wastewater relevant species (Figure SI1). Therefore,
the higher end of this dosage range was selected to account for the partial loss of
bioaugmented cells in the ﬂow-through system and the complex microbial communities.
Additionally, tryptophan was added as an IAA precursor at varying dosing frequencies to again
test its role in enhancing bioaugmentation in the pilot system. As for the biomass productivity,
the bioaugmented Algaewheel system had the similar yields with control group (26.3±3.7 vs.
27.1±3.0 g/m2/day) when A. brasilense culture was added on biweekly basis (Figure 4A). The
dosing frequency was thus increased to once per week. Correspondingly, the average biomass
productivity of bioaugmented Algaewheel system increased by an average of 11.8% g/m2/day
(p<0.05) compared to the control system, yielding 26.1±5.7 g/m2/day (Figure 4B). This
accomplishment further demonstrated the goal for Task 5, where bioaugmentation can improve
biomass productivity by at least 10% in pilot-scale testing.

 Finally, to simplify the bioaugmentation process, chemically synthesized IAA was added to

IAA was dosed three to ﬁve times per week at the inﬂuent area at a concentration sufficient to

the control (Figure 5B-D). This indicates that the chemical IAA treatment was effective, initially
promoting bioﬁlm growth on the wheels. Bioﬁlm grows slowly over the long term in the
Algaewheel fans, so this may have explained why an immediate productivity enhancement was
not seen. The increased suspended biomass could be detected when the bioﬁlm sloughed off.
Interestingly, although IAA chemical was added at a higher dosing frequency than that of adding
bacteria, the latter led to a greater enhancement effect likely because the colonized
bioaugmenting bacteria could continuously produce IAA. To achieve more pronounced growth
promotion, higher dosing frequency of chemical IAA addition is required.

 Conclusions
 After the set of experiments conducted at the request of the BP2 veriﬁcation team, we
obtained results demonstrating the bioaugmenting effects of both the A. brasilense and IAA
treatments at the pilot, as described above. Sodium malate and tryptophan alone as treatments
were not effective in improving productivity at the pilot and were not selected as treatments at
the full-scale sites. Besides algal biomass and productivity enhancement, other effects to the
                                            Page 19 of 175

Algaewheel system were observed during the pilot experiments, including stable biodiversity
and improved nutrient removal.
 Notably, high-throughput sequencing of 18S rRNA genes revealed that the algal community
in the bioaugmented tank exhibited more stable biodiversity than the control (Figure SI4.
Despite signiﬁcant variations in the Chao1 and Shannon indices in the control, which indicated
ﬂuctuating biodiversity, the bioaugmented tank maintained a consistent diversity proﬁle with
Oedogonium dominating 80-91% of the community throughout the study period. This
consistency supports ﬁndings from recent studies (Alam et al., 2024) suggesting that stable
system performance, achieving stringent nutrient removal and steady biomass yield, is
associated with a low-diversity microbial community dominated by a single algal species.
 During two months of operation, the bioaugmented Algaewheel tank demonstrated
superior nutrients removal performance, with slightly higher average sCOD and NH3-N removal
efficiencies than the control system (Figure SI7). The inﬂuent for all Algaewheel tanks was
synthetic wastewater typically containing 888-1451 mg/L sCOD and 9.6-17.5 mg/L NH3-N.
Throughout the experimental period, the control system’s effluent averaged 48.9±24.0 mg/L
sCOD and 0.2±0.2 mg/L NH3-N, demonstrating removal rates of over 92.7% sCOD and 96.1% for
NH3-N.
 Bioaugmentation PCR Genetic Analysis
 A PCR DNA assay was developed to detect A. brasilense cells in wastewater. The results
from these PCR experiments were helpful in further determination of effective dosage
frequency and amount of A. brasilense cultures in our treatments. Experiments were performed
at the full-, pilot-, and bench-scales to detect the number of cells required to increase algal
biomass and determine how long dosed A. brasilense cells were detectable in the Algaewheel
system. The pilot- and bench-scale work determined that the cells were detectable up to
several weeks (12-21 days) dependent on conditions. A. brasilense did not last as long in the
tank at the full-scale site (Bookwalter) as it did in a pilot Algaewheel system in central Illinois,
but a follow up laboratory experiment showed the opposite result using wastewater and
biomass sampled from these two sites. Colonization seems highly dependent on the
wastewater matrix and the available substrates/nutrients. Thus, environmental conditions
seem to play an important role in microbial colonization, rather than biological competition
alone.
 A conventional PCR method was used to amplify a section (1299 bp) of the A. brasilense
Indole-3-Pyruvate Decarboxylase (IPDC) gene (from the NCBI genome assembly ASM131501v1)
spanning the gene’s exon into the terminator sequence (2497546 - 2498844 bp) to test for A.
brasilense genomic DNA in the operating Algaewheel pilot system. A. brasilense gDNA
extracted from cells suspended in active wastewater yielded similar results compared to gDNA
extracted from cells in pure culture (Figure 6 A&B). In addition to the PCR method, Matrix-
assisted laser desorption/ionization time of ﬂight (MALDI-TOF) analysis, Scanning Electronic

                                            Page 20 of 175

Microscope (SEM), and light microscopes were utilized to monitor the bioaugmenting bacteria
in pure and mixed algal culture during the benchtop experiments, with their detailed method
descriptions in SI and previous studies (Zhao et al., 2013).
 To conﬁrm its persistence, a PCR method targeting the ipdC gene was used and detected A.
brasilense in wastewater at levels as low as 10⁷ cells/L (Figure 6B). Since enhancement effects
are limited below 10⁸–10⁹ cells/L (Figure 3 & Figure 4), densities ≤10⁷ cells/L are insufficient for

cells/L, bacterial density in the water samples dropped to the threshold of 107 cells/L after more
than six days of effective operation (Figure 6C), supporting a weekly dosing schedule. The
gradual decline in the proportion of exogenous bacteria and the enhanced biomass yield
suggest that the addition of A. brasilense did not adversely affect the microbial community. The
PCR assay was able to detect A. brasilense cells at a previously determined effective
concentration, and we veriﬁed that A. brasilense cells were present in an amount that has the
ability to signiﬁcantly increase algal biomass.

  Northern Field Site Demonstration (Full-Scale) at Bookwalter Woods Wastewater Treatment
  Plant in Gardner, IL – Task 10
 Background
 With the two effective treatment methods to increase algal productivity utilized in the
previous experiments, A. brasilense bioaugmentation and IAA chemical treatments were
implemented in a covered Algaewheel system operating at Bookwalter Woods wastewater
treatment plant located in Gardner, IL. Following the results of benchtop and pilot studies, the
bacterial and chemical dosage targeted at the ﬁnal cell density of 109 cells/L and IAA
concentration of 20-50 µM, respectively. No IAA precursor tryptophan treatment was added to
the tank since tryptophan alone did not affect algal productivity in the bench-scale and pilot-
scale testing, as shown above.
 The bioaugmentation and IAA treatments were implemented to two of the six paralleled
secondary Algaewheel tanks. In two of the three parallel Algaewheel tanks, pre-incubated IAA-
producing A. brasilense cultures were added weekly to biweekly in one tank, and IAA chemical
solution was consistently added near the inﬂuent with a peristaltic pump in another. The third
tank, Tank 8, was maintained as the control throughout all the experiments. This experimental
design was chosen to determine which of the two treatments provided the best algal
enhancement effects on a full-scale Algaewheel WWTP system. Suspended biomass in the
inﬂuent and effluent water was collected from each tank by several refrigerated autosamplers
and measured for ash-free dry weight to monitor the productivity and water quality of
Algaewheel system.
  Methods and Results
 Task 10: IAA Chemical Treatment
 There were two phases of these experiments set up in the same timelines as the A.
brasilense microbial treatment described in the following section. The ﬁrst IAA experiment
involved using IAA chemicals in Tank 7 from June 25, 2023 to April 3, 2024. Though experimental
treatment started in June, due to operational issues at the treatment plant and autosampler
equipment issues, reliable samples from the treatment tanks were not available until Nov 1,
2023. An in-depth analysis of productivity results from this experimental period can be seen in
Figure 7. Through November 2023 to March 2024, the algal productivity of the control tank
ranged from 8.5-56.6 g/m2/day (Figure 7A), with an averaged algal biomass yield of 23.4
g/m2/day (Figure 7B). This yield of algal biomass is in alignment with the productivity of other
algal based wastewater treatment systems (Molitor et al., 2024; Gross & Wen, 2014) as well as
our long-term pilot data. Additionally, the algal productivity in the control Algaewheel system
exhibited a clear seasonal pattern, with productivity remaining steady in the low range from
December 2023 to February 2024 and gradually increased from 18.5 to 51.6 g/m²/day in March
and April 2024.

 For the Algaewheel tank treated with IAA chemicals, the tank received an IAA solution for 8
hours per day. Its productivity over time showed large variations within 35.1-102.8 g/m2/day
through the tested period of Nov 2023 – April 2024, however, with most datapoints above the
productivity of control tank. The box and whisker plots (Figure 7B and Figure 8A) show that the
IAA treated Algaewheel tank had an average yield of 61.2(±17.7) g/m2/day through the tested
period, demonstrating 160% improvement compared to the control group (23.4 ±11.8 g/m2/day;
p<0.01). Consistent to previous studies (Peng et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2020) and our batch
experiment (Figure 2A), the observed IAA enhancement effects are concentration dependent.
 Based on the IAA level in the chemical treated Algaewheel tank, the experiment can be
grouped into three periods, i.e. the period of high IAA level (40 µM, Nov 2023 – Jan 2024),
medium IAA level (30 µM, Jan 2024 – Feb 2024), and low IAA level (20 µM, Feb 2024 – April 2024)
(Figure 7A and Figure 8B). The average increased percentage in algal productivity during the
three periods of high, medium, and low IAA level were approximately 237%, 143%, and 74%
g/m2/day, respectively, clearly showing a decreasing IAA enhancement effects as the IAA
dosage reduced (Figure 7C). The average increased IAA treatment productivity over the control
during this period was 161.2% g/m2/day (Figure 8A). This promoted algal growth by IAA
chemical addition is more distinguishable than the previous benchtop and pilot scale
experiments in which about only 10% (Figure 2A) and 25% (Figure 5D) enhancement effects
were observed, respectively. This is because the IAA concentration of the full-scale Algaewheel
system was maintained at a relatively constant level as it was fed continuously, while IAA levels
in the previous periodic dosing experiments could drop quickly due to algae consumption. As a
result, the exposure time to IAA at an effective level is reduced in the previous batch and pilot
experiments, leading to lower enhancement effects. These data ﬁrst conﬁrm the mechanism of
using A. brasilense for producing IAA and together suggest the temporary effect of using IAA
chemicals and the importance of continuous dosing to achieve an observable positive effect.

 Bookwalter WWTP Demo Conclusions
 The monitored Algaewheel system at Bookwalter wastewater treatment plant (IL, 41° N
latitude) achieved areal productivity of 23.4 g/m2/day during winter (-9 to 16°C temperature
range) while treating approximately 38,000 gallons per day municipal wastewater. The
bioaugmentation and indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) treatment strategies developed in this study
signiﬁcantly improved winter areal productivity, raising it to 47 and 63 g/m²/day, respectively, a
101% and 169% increase in productivity. These results further support the completion of Task
10 (ﬁeld demo showing >25% productivity increase) and the Go/No-Go Milestone 3 (ﬁeld scale
test showing >50% biomass productivity increase).
 To our knowledge, this study is the ﬁrst to demonstrate a successful, gradual scale-up of
bioaugmentation from lab-scale to full-scale application in an algae-based wastewater
treatment system. Scaling up bioaugmentation poses signiﬁcant uncertainties and challenges,
as strains with promising lab-scale performance often fail to replicate their effectiveness within
the complexities of natural systems (Ma et al., 2022; Herrero and Stuckey, 2015; Wu et al.,
2020). For example, while Pseudomonas denitriﬁcans showed potential for promoting algal
growth in our lab-scale experiments, it did not achieve comparable success during scale-up.
Despite these challenges, this study identiﬁed a valid bioaugmentation strategy for A.
brasilense at the full-scale, involving repeated direct dosing every two weeks, with bacterial
inoculum supplied via the process that is equivalent to using side-stream reactors. For an
Algaewheel system, achieving a ﬁnal A. brasilense bacterial density of 0.2–4 × 10⁹ cells/L
effectively doubled algal growth (Figure 7B). It is believed the Algaewheel’s design facilitates the
colonization of added bacteria by providing optimal surfaces for bioﬁlm development (Johnson,
2019), which helps retain introduced microbes and protects them from washout or predation
(Ma et al., 2022; Li et al., 2013). Successful bioaugmentation at full-scale also requires either
high dosing volumes or frequent applications (Pei et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2018; Hong et al.,
2020), owing to the complex, nonlinear interactions among inﬂuent characteristics, microbial
dynamics, and operational conditions (Ma et al., 2022). These ﬁndings are consistent with our
observations that high bacterial dosages (3–4 × 10⁹ cells/L) provide greater enhancement
compared to lower dosages (0.2–1 × 10⁹ cells/L) (Figure 7B). In general, higher dosages extend
the survival and stability of the introduced strains, supporting more sustained beneﬁts (Wen, et
al., 2018). The necessity of continuous bioaugmentation was evident in this study, as the
enhanced growth and productivity levels reverted to control conditions following the cessation
of bioaugmentation on April 1, 2024. Productivity levels in the treatment tanks went back in line
with the control in the months after treatment ended (Figure SI8). However, the two-week
dosing frequency identiﬁed here is notably less frequent than the 2-5-day intervals reported in
previous studies (Ikeda-Ohtsubo et al., 2013; Sforza et al., 2018), making it more practical for
long-term application. It does not require an in-depth analysis to illustrate that the effects of A.

                                            Page 29 of 175

brasilense bioaugmentation at a two-week dosing interval were comparable to those achieved
through the continuous addition of indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) (Figure 7B-C). Chemical addition
could prove valuable for targeted short-term objectives, such as jump-starting systems, rapidly
enhancing performance, or boosting resilience under sudden and extreme conditions.
 The whole system exhibited improved synergistic effects with the treatments; enhanced
algal growth resulted in improved wastewater treatment performance, achieving 100%
ammonia removal in both treatment tanks and consistently met discharge limits throughout the
winter (Figure SI9). While previous studies have highlighted the challenges of bioaugmentation
at low temperatures, including the potential failure of inoculated strains to proliferate or
maintain biological activity (Wu et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2018b; Guo et al., 2010), our ﬁndings
overcome this risk. This success may be attributed to the unclear resilience of A. brasilense
bacteria to ﬂuctuating environmental conditions or the inherent advantages conferred by the
bioﬁlm-based design of the Algaewheel system (Johnson et al., 2018; Johnson, 2019). This
ﬁnding aligns with previous research suggesting that IAA treatment can optimize the
biochemical composition of algae (Chung et al., 2018). It suggests that the developed
bioaugmentation and chemical methods can serve as selective operational tools, like solids
retention time, pH, and dissolved oxygen, to reduce nutrient management costs while
improving yield, biochemical proﬁles, and biofuel production potential. The bioaugmentation
approach offers further adaptability using commercially available freeze-dried bacterial
products, simplifying application across diverse algae-based systems. This scalability
enhances its potential for broader adoption.
 Bioaugmentation Overall Conclusions
 Algae-based wastewater treatment technologies can recover nutrients while generating
algal biomass with diverse application potentials. While numerous bacteria have demonstrated
the ability to enhance algal growth through symbiotic relationships in laboratory settings,
scaling this beneﬁt to larger applications remains challenging due to complex wastewater
conditions and microbe-algae dynamics. This section of the project explored the effectiveness
of bioaugmentation with Azospirillum brasilense, a plant growth-promoting bacterium known
for producing the phytohormone indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), in boosting algal productivity within
an Algaewheel wastewater treatment system. Through a series of experiments conducted at
lab-, pilot-, and full-scale levels, we evaluated the impact of bioaugmentation on algal growth
and developed a bioaugmentation strategy for an Algaewheel system.
 By leveraging the bacterium Azospirillum brasilense and its production of IAA, we achieved
consistent improvements in algal growth, biodiversity stability, and system performance,
particularly under challenging winter conditions. Overall, from Nov 2023 to Nov 2024, our
Northern Field Site (Bookwalter) experienced an average productivity increase (across both sets
of treatment experiments) of 62.9% and 68.6% g/m2/day from A. brasilense and IAA treatments,
respectively. The results indicate that a biweekly dosing, achieving a ﬁnal bioaugmenting cell
density of 0.2–4 × 10⁹ cells/L, effectively doubles the biomass productivity in a residential
                                            Page 30 of 175

subdivision wastewater treatment plant in Northern Illinois. The bacterial production of IAA was
the primary mechanism driving this enhancement, as demonstrated by the similar growth and
yield improvements observed with chemically supplemented IAA and bioaugmentation across
all experimental scales.
 High-throughput sequencing of 18S rRNA genes revealed that the algal community in the
bioaugmented tank exhibited more stable biodiversity than the control. Additionally,
bioaugmentation improved nutrient removal efficiency during winter, highlighting its potential to
enhance the overall performance and sustainability of algae-based wastewater systems. The
developed bioaugmentation and chemical treatment methods offer further operational
solutions for managing yield, optimizing biochemical proﬁles, and enhancing biofuel production
potential. Additionally, the dual approach of microbial and chemical enhancement offers
ﬂexible tools for optimizing system performance and improving the viability of algal biomass for
downstream applications such as biofuels and bioproducts.

Adsorbents
 Bench-Scale
 Algae require nitrogen to grow. In the wastewater treatment Algaewheel tanks, nitrogen is
supplied by ammonia extracted from the wastewater. Due to the nature of typical household
usage, wastewater concentration can ﬂuctuate, possibly leading to higher than desired
concentrations of ammonia in the effluent during these peak usages through the plant. Since
ammonia levels can have large variance over time, this can lead to periods of nitrogen being
wasted when it could be captured and recycled back to the inﬂuent, giving the algae additional
time to take up the nitrogen. To smooth periods of high ammonia, we introduce a Zeolite media
ammonia buffer with a 20-minute contact time into the system. The buffer will load and store
ammonia when ammonia levels are high and then release the ammonia back into the system
when ammonia levels fall. This leads to more stable ammonia levels over time, which is better
for algae growth.

Endoreduplication
 Plant have evolved ways to cope with herbivory, and one method is through
overcompensation, where plants exhibit increased growth following non-lethal damage or
stress. One mechanism for this phenomenon is endoreduplication (Figure 28 top), where a cell
does not go through cytokinesis (ﬁnal stage of cell division), which leads to an increase in the
cell’s DNA content, genome, ploidy (chromosome number), and size. About 90% of all
herbaceous plants employ endoreduplication as an adaptive, plastic response to mitigate the
effects of stress (Scholes & Paige, 2015). Due to the increase in ploidy, endoreduplication
allows the upregulation of genes that facilitate processes such as protein synthesis and
photosynthesis, which are essential for rapid regrowth. This response is driven by the ILP1 gene.
In a study by Mesa et. al. 2017, enhancing a plant’s ability to endoreduplicate during regrowth
led to the complete mitigation of the detrimental effects of damage on its ﬁtness, and in some
cases led to enhanced ﬁtness (seed, seed weight, biomass etc.) in some plant varieties.

Figure 28. Mechanism of salt-induced lipid accumulation in halotolerant microalgae (adapted
from Arora et al. 2017) and its application. Schematic representation (top) of metabolic
adjustments in Scenedesmus sp. IITRIND2 under high salinity (30 g/L sea salts including NaCl,
MgCl₂, and CaCl₂). (Bottom) an example of biochemical composition and cell size of microalgae
under increasing salinity stress.

 As shown in Figure 28, salt stress can trigger an ion imbalance (Na⁺ inﬂux, K⁺ efflux) and
reactive oxygen species (ROS) formation, which activates antioxidant enzymes and osmolyte
production (e.g., proline, glycine betaine). These stress responses divert carbon toward glycerol
and neutral lipid synthesis, resulting in ~3.2-fold higher lipid content under salinity stress. Lipid
accumulation enhances the potential of halotolerant algae for biodiesel production. The
bottom part of Figure 28 shows the effects of artiﬁcial seawater (ASW) concentration (0–100%)
compared to standard Bold’s Basal Medium (BBM) on cellular biochemical composition and
morphology. With increasing salinity, lipid content (orange) progressively increased, while
carbohydrate (red) and protein (blue) contents decreased. Concurrently, average cell size (green
line) increased with higher ASW concentrations. At 100% ASW, lipid content peaked while
protein content was lowest, indicating a strong metabolic shift favoring lipid accumulation
under high salinity stress.
 This mechanism, if present in algae, could provide a means to improve desirable
characteristics of algae such as increased protein or lipids, which could then improve biofuel
production made from algae. The increase in the lipid content was balanced by a sharp
decrease in its protein and carbohydrate content. Thus, this could result in a negligible increase
in biomass but signiﬁcantly enhance lipid production (Figure 28 bottom), which is desirable for
biofuel production. The primary goals of this work were to identify if green microalgae
endoreduplicate, and if so, what stress mechanisms provide the best results for biofuels.
  Objectives and Summary of Endoreduplication Results
 1. Do algae endoreduplicate (increase chromosome numbers) when under stress?
 a. Yes, algae shifted from predominantly haploidy with low levels of polyploidy
 to signiﬁcantly higher levels of polyploidy (tetraploids and octaploids) and
 signiﬁcantly lower levels of haploidy.
 2. Do algae increase in biomass and cellular lipid concentration following stress
 induced endoreduplication?
 a. Yes, algal cell size increases when stressed. However, endoreduplication arrests
 the cell cycle from further division, thus there are tradeoffs between increasing
 overall biomass and decreasing the overall number of cells in a population.
 b. Yes, in spite of a comparative reduction in cellular production due to the fact that
 endoreduplication arrests the cell from further division, lipid production showed a
 6.9-fold increase in triglycerides and a 2.27-fold increase in neutral cholesterol
 esters. The increase in the lipid content was balanced by a sharp decrease in its
 protein and carbohydrate content which likely results in a negligible increase in
 biomass – while enhancing lipid production (Arora et al. 2017).
 3. What is the mechanism for endoreduplication in algae? Do algae carry an analogous
 mechanism such as the ILP1 gene found in plants?
 a. Yes, the ILP1 gene was with a signiﬁcant proportion of their two functional motifs
 being shared in common between algae and plants.
                                           Page 45 of 175

 4. Which stress treatment is most effective?
 a. Salt treatment with NaCl was most effective and replicable to induce
 endoreduplication. UV-B treatment was variable in results sometimes
 resulting in endoreduplication, and sometimes showing little effect.
 Desiccation resulted in no effect and was discontinued early on.
 b. Does waste water alone lead to endoreduplication in algae?
 i. No, experiments show that algae are not stressed by waste water alone but
 need an added stressor such as salt.

  Endoreduplication Treatments, Results and Accomplishments
 Salt treatments
 Two approaches were used to test the effects of salt stress on green algae: a. Grow and
then stress and b. Cell cycling. For Grow and stress, mature cultures were established by
inoculating approximately 20 million cells per ﬂask for Ankistrodesmus and 200 million cells per
ﬂask for Chlorella. Cultures were grown for 1.5 months, after which 0.1 M NaCl was added as
the stressor. Cultures were then maintained for ~6 days post-treatment. As for the cell cycling, 5
million cells were inoculated into fresh medium and allowed to incubate for ~1 week to examine
whether alternating stress and recovery phases inﬂuence endoreduplication. Cultures were
then exposed to NaCl stress for ~5 days, followed by a 5-day recovery period without stress, and
subsequently re-stressed for another ~5 days. This cycle was repeated twice.
 Exposure of Ankistrodesmus to 0.1 M NaCl resulted in distinct morphological and
physiological changes consistent with endoreduplication. Microscopy revealed that cells in the
control maintained normal nuclear size, while NaCl-treated cells displayed visibly enlarged
nuclei (Figure 29), a hallmark of endoreduplication commonly observed in higher plants.
 Growth and biomass analyses further supported this observation (Figure 30). Cell
proliferation was signiﬁcantly inhibited under NaCl treatment compared to controls (*p < 0.05),
resulting in lower overall cell densities. Despite reduced cell division, average cell size
increased signiﬁcantly across multiple days of salt exposure (*p < 0.05), indicating a stress-
induced enlargement of individual cells. Biomass measurements showed that total culture
biomass increased by 37% under NaCl treatment relative to controls, and biomass per cell
increased even more substantially (*p < 0.05). These ﬁndings demonstrate that although fewer
cells were produced under salt stress, each cell accumulated more biomass.

 Wastewater and NaCl Treatment
 Another important question was whether wastewater alone, from the algal wheel system,
serves as a stressor to induce endoreduplication? We found that wastewater alone was not
sufficient to induce endoreduplication, as algal cells remained predominantly haploid in a
control with wastewater as the cultivation media (Figure 39). In contrast, this ﬁgure also shows
that when NaCl stress was applied in wastewater-grown cultures, endoreduplication occurred,
which demonstrates that stress-induced polyploidy is effective even outside controlled
laboratory conditions. These results show that while wastewater alone does not induce stress
responses, NaCl effectively triggers endoreduplication and lipid remodeling in both controlled
and wastewater media. Consistent with this, Figure 40 shows that NaCl stress at 0.1 M led to
larger algal cell sizes with both a typical algae cultivation media (Bold’s) and a wastewater
media. Importantly, this increase in cell size was accompanied by a pronounced rise in neutral
lipids. Together, these ﬁndings conﬁrm the NaCl treatment supports the same stress-induced
endoreduplication and lipid remodeling responses, both under controlled laboratory conditions
and wastewater conditions.

Water Quality
 We tracked several water quality metrics at various stages of the project, including
ammonia, chemical oxygen demand (COD), and ash content. Our aim was to increase algal

biomass dies and decays. At the pilot scale, we were able to signiﬁcantly reduce the ash
content of biomass using two methods. First, we increased the harvesting frequency from once
per week to 2-3 times per week. This reduced the amount of time solids spent in the system,
giving them less time to decay. This effect is shown in Figure 44. We also implemented shear
enhanced sloughing by doubling the airﬂow to the Algaewheels for 30 minutes prior to
harvesting, allowing us to more precisely harvest biomass.

Hydrothermal Liquefaction and Nanoﬁltration
 Introduction
 Hydrothermal liquefaction (HTL) is an emerging thermochemical technology that converts
wet biomass into a biocrude, which can be reﬁned into drop-in liquid fuels with characteristics
similar to those of their fossil counterparts. These biofuels require minimal to no modiﬁcations
to existing ﬂeets, while achieving signiﬁcantly lower life cycle greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions.
Because of its versatility and capability to handle high-moisture biomass, HTL is one of the most
promising pathways for converting algal biomass into fuels and value-added chemicals. Due to
higher net cultivation costs and contamination issues with single-species microalgae
cultivation, mixed algal-bacterial biomass from wastewater treatment systems has emerged as
an attractive feedstock for HTL.
 Despite its great potential, HTL also produces a nutrient- and carbon-rich waste stream
known as HTL aqueous phase (HTL-AP), which requires proper treatment and disposal.
Addressing the treatment and valorization of this byproduct remains one of the key challenges
impeding the widespread, full-scale implementation of HTL. Therefore, this study investigated
the integration of membrane ﬁltration as a valorization method for the HTL-AP of algal-bacterial
biomass.
 In the proposed process conﬁguration, nanoﬁltration concentrates organic compounds and
separates nutrients from HTL-AP. The concentrated organics can be recirculated back into HTL
as process water to improve biocrude conversion efficiency, while the nutrients can serve as a
growth supplement in biomass cultivation.
 The research on HTL and nanoﬁltration of the HTL aqueous product was structured into
three main studies. In the ﬁrst study, the effects of ﬁltration operational conditions (membrane
type, pH, and ﬁltration temperature) on separation efficiency and permeate ﬂux were evaluated
for two types of biomass: one from an algal wastewater treatment system and the other from
harmful algal blooms (HABs). The impact of recirculating the concentrated HTL-AP back into
HTL was assessed in terms of biocrude yield and quality over one recirculation cycle.
 In the second study, humic acid precipitation was investigated as an HTL-AP pretreatment
method to improve the efficiency and robustness of HTL-AP nanoﬁltration. Its effects on
ﬁltration performance and HTL efficiency parameters were evaluated over ﬁve consecutive
recirculation cycles, and the nanoﬁltration permeate was tested as a growth supplement in
algal-bacterial biomass cultivation.
 In the third study, techno-economic analysis (TEA) and life cycle analysis (LCA) were
conducted to assess the process's economic feasibility and environmental sustainability,
comparing it with other HTL-AP valorization strategies (catalytic hydrothermal gasiﬁcation and
untreated HTL-AP recirculation).

                                           Page 68 of 175

 HTL Research objectives
 This research aims to evaluate the technical, economic, and sustainability implications of
integrating membrane ﬁltration into the HTL of algal-bacterial biomass. In this approach, also
called nanoﬁltration-enhanced HTL, membrane ﬁltration is used to separate organics from
nutrients in the HTL-AP before it is recirculated back into HTL as process water. As shown in
Figure 53, the concentrated fraction (retentate) can be recirculated back to HTL, and the
nutrients recovered in the permeate can be used as a nutrient source in biomass cultivation.
The primary analysis will focus on the HTL of algal-bacterial biomass from the Algaewheel®
system (One Water Ltd., Cary, NC), which is a commercially available algal wastewater
treatment system that has been installed at several wastewater treatment facilities.

treatments were prepared for each biomass: (i) a baseline with only biomass (no HTL-AP
addition) and (ii) a direct recirculation treatment, in which untreated HTL-AP (as-is) was mixed
at the same ratio that it is produced (0.94 g of HTL-AP as-is per g of biomass).
 The HTL reactions were performed in duplicate using 10-mL stainless-steel pipe reactors,
assembled using 316 stainless-steel tubing and double ferrule caps on both ends. Each reactor
was loaded with approximately 3 g of the prepared feedstock mixture, and the reactions were
conducted at a constant temperature of 300 °C for a 60-min duration. The reactors were placed
in a convection oven set to 300 °C. Upon reaching thermal equilibrium, a 60-min countdown
was initiated. Following the HTL reaction, the reactors were placed in a container with water
and rapidly cooled down to room temperature. Product separation involved ﬁltration and
solvent extraction with dichloromethane (DCM), selected due to its high biocrude recovery
efficiency and comparatively low energy requirements for HTL of algal feedstocks (Watson et
al., 2019). The contents of the reactors were mixed with approximately 6 mL of DCM and ﬁltered
using 1.5 µm pore size glass microﬁber ﬁlters. This procedure was repeated for an additional
two times to ensure that the contents of the reactor were fully recovered. The ﬁltered mixture of
HTL products and DCM was then transferred to a separating funnel to isolate the biocrude
phase from the HTL-AP.

 Rejection of solutes and separation factor
 Figure 54 shows the rejection of NH3-N, TN, and COD for the treatments applied to each
HTL-AP. The average COD rejection was 95.9 ± 1.2%, and no signiﬁcant difference was observed
among the treatment levels. The minor differences in the rejection of COD were mainly a
function of HTL-AP characteristics and membrane properties. Fluorescence EEM analysis of the
HTL-AP revealed a signiﬁcant presence of humic-like dissolved organic matter (Figure 55).
Humic substances are complex, high-molecular-weight organic compounds that can be formed
during HTL through a series of reactions involving the condensation and polymerization of
cellulose, hemicellulose, starch, and proteins (Shao et al., 2024). Zhang et al. (2020) reported
that even in small quantities, these substances can cause signiﬁcant fouling and are a major
contributor to ﬂux decline in membrane ﬁltration processes such as the one used in this study.
 Overall, the same trends were observed for the rejection of NH3-N and TN. While no
signiﬁcant difference was observed for treatments with pH 5 and 8, the lowest rejections were
observed for treatments with pH 11 (T3, T5, T7). The lowest rejection of NH3-N and TN were
achieved for treatment T3, which were 7.1 ± 4.2% and 42.5 ± 1.0%, respectively. This treatment
also provided the highest separation factors (Figure 54b), reﬂecting a higher ratio of permeation
of NH3-N and TN per permeation of COD. The main effects plot for the separation factor (Figure
56a) shows a signiﬁcant increase in separation when pH is increased from 8 to 11, which
suggests that pH was the dominant effect on the separation factor. Additionally, the maximum
delta values (Δmax) observed for pH (Table 5) were consistently higher for the rejection of
solutes and separation factors, and it ranked highest among all variables.

 The relationship between pH and permeation of ammonia has also been observed in
previous nanoﬁltration studies (Ray et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2020), which concluded that at
higher pH values, above the pKa of ammonia, the nonionized form of ammonia (NH3) is
prevalent and has a higher permeability than the ionized one (NH4+). Since a signiﬁcant fraction
of the TN of these HTL-APs was composed of ammonia species (Table 5), the treatment effects
observed on TN were likely dominated by the pH effect on NH3-N.
 Due to the thermal expansion of pores and changes in the solubility of solutes, an increase
in ﬁltration temperature is expected to increase the mass transfer through composite
polyamide membranes (Nilsson et al., 2008). Although COD rejection was not signiﬁcantly
affected by temperature, increasing temperature increased the separation of NH3-N in relation
to COD (Figure 56a). According to Huang and Shang (2006), the speciation and concentration of
NH3 in solution relative to NH4+ increases with temperature. Therefore, similar to the effect of
pH, the inﬂuence of temperature on the NH3 and NH4+ equilibrium contributed to a higher
permeability of NH3-N, which ultimately increased the separation factors (Figure 56a) without
decreasing permeate ﬂux (Figure 56b), as observed for the other cases in which there is an
apparent inverse relationship between separation factor and ﬂux. Given the beneﬁts of higher
ﬁltration temperature, it is worth mentioning that in a continuous process, the HTL products
leave the reactor at elevated temperatures, which can be conserved to eliminate or reduce the
need for additional energy input for heating the HTL-AP during nanoﬁltration.

 The effectiveness of the nanoﬁltration process as an HTL-AP treatment method was
 analyzed through the levels of COD (Figure 57a) and NH3-N (Figure 57b) in the permeate. The
 mass permeation (Figure 57c) was calculated as the percentage of the mass of these solutes in
 the HTL-AP that permeates through the membrane during nanoﬁltration. All treatments
 achieved low mass permeation of COD. Overall, between 99.1% and 96.3% of the COD mass in
 the HTL-AP remained in the retentate, which was the fraction recirculated into the HTL process.
 For some treatments (T9, T2, T5, T8), the COD in the permeate was reduced to levels similar to
 untreated domestic wastewater, which typically varies from 339 - 1,016 mg/L (Tchobanoglous et
 al., 2014). The mass permeation of NH3-N varied in a broader range since the rejection of this
 solute was more responsive to the treatment variables. In all cases, the permeate is expected to
 require additional treatment before environmental discharge. However, nanoﬁltration
 drastically reduced the strength of the HTL waste stream, which allows it to be further valorized
 by biological treatment processes such as anaerobic digestion or algal biomass cultivation
 without the heavy dilutions of up to1000 times used in previous studies (Tommaso et al., 2015).
 In the case of high NH3-N permeability (T3), the permeate contains ammonia levels that can be
 toxic for some microorganisms. Previous studies achieved signiﬁcant success in reducing HTL-
 AP toxicity by integrating treatment with activated carbon and zeolites (Shanmugam et al.,
 2017b; Wang et al., 2019; Zheng et al., 2017) or struvite precipitation (McGinn et al., 2019;
 Shanmugam et al., 2017a). Struvite is an attractive option because the nitrogen is concentrated
 into a form that could be a valuable fertilizer product. Algal treatment processes remove
 ammoniacal nitrogen through biomass assimilation and can typically tolerate higher levels,
 especially rotating contactors like the one that generated the AWS biomass, which provides
 additional N removal mechanisms in anoxic parts of the bioﬁlm (Johnson et al., 2020, 2018).

 Average permeate ﬂux
 Average permeate ﬂux is an essential parameter for investigating the performance of
membrane ﬁltration processes. In Figure 59, the average permeate ﬂux for each treatment is
presented as a function of permeate recovery. The highest mean ﬂux was achieved for T4 (42.9 ±
1.5 liters per square meter per hour) (LMH) and the lowest for T9 (10.0 ± 0.1 LMH). The source of
HTL-AP had the highest inﬂuence on permeate ﬂux (highest Δmax), followed by pH, temperature,
and membrane used, in order of decreasing Δmax.
 The relative effect of each variable level can be visually observed on the main effects plot
for permeate ﬂux (Figure 56b). The mean ﬂux for HAB-1 (32.9 LMH) was higher than that of HAB-
2 (29.1 LMH), which, in turn, was higher than AWS (11.8 LMH). The mass transport through thin-
ﬁlm membranes such as the ones used in this study is highly dependent on the composition of
the feed solution, especially the presence of multivalent and high molecular weight organic
solutes (Nagy, 2019). With ﬂuorescent emissions varying between 387.61 nm and 392.20 nm
and excitation between 314 and 320 nm, the ﬂuorescence EEM peaks expressed in quinine
sulfate units (QSU) for AWS, HAB-1, and HAB-2 were 245.6, 26.3, and 119.5, respectively (Figure
55). Peak intensity values in this EEM region are directly related to the solution's dissolved
organic matter (DOM) concentration and the presence of humic-like compounds (Chen et al.,
2003). Given the general membrane fouling effects of DOM, there is an inverse relationship
between the concentration of DOM and permeate ﬂux.

                                            Page 80 of 175

 Figure 59. Average permeate flux for each treatment during nanofiltration.

 There was a positive correlation between ﬁltration temperature and ﬂux (Figure 56b).
Speciﬁcally, treatments with temperatures of 35 ºC and 45 ºC presented a respective increase
of 61.3% and 87.9% in mean ﬂux compared to the baseline temperature of 25 ºC. Higher
temperatures are known to affect membrane ﬁltration processes by decreasing the viscosity of
water and causing thermal expansion of pores, which contributes to increased ﬂux and higher
diffusion rate of speciﬁc solutes through the membrane (Xu et al., 2023).
 Flux decline was observed across all treatments during nanoﬁltration, with values ranging
from 0.8 LMH (T9) to 24.4 LMH (T8). Multiple factors can contribute to declining ﬂux, such as
fouling, the interaction between solute and membrane, and increasing osmotic pressure.
Fouling was visually detected by the presence of particles on the membrane surface and
conﬁrmed through the comparison of DI water ﬂux before and after HTL-AP ﬁltration. The DI
water ﬂux of the new membrane for CR100, XLE, and BW30 at 250 PSI was 59.5 ± 6.6 LMH,
113.4 ± 8.6 LMH, and 66.2 ± 9.9 LMH. Their respective average decline in DI water ﬂux after HTL-
AP ﬁltration was 8.2%, 26.1%, and 12.9%. Since excessive ﬂux decline can impact membrane
ﬁltration’s cost-effectiveness, more investigation is needed to determine its root cause and
whether chemical treatment or backwashing can reverse it.

 Effect of recirculation on biocrude yield
 Figure 60 shows the biocrude yield (Yoil) for the various HTL experiments. Recirculating HTL-
AP, after nanoﬁltration treatment or untreated as is (AI), signiﬁcantly increased the biocrude
yield of both AWS and HAB-2. The highest biocrude yield for AWS was achieved for T6 (39.5%
ash free dry weight basis, afdw), which represented a 72.7% increase in relation to the AWS
baseline and a 26.3% increase in relation to recirculating the HTL-AP as is material. For the
HAB-2, the highest biocrude yield was achieved for T1, representing a 116.2% increase in
relation to its baseline. The T1 treatment also exhibited a 10.1% higher biocrude yield in relation
to recirculating the HTL-AP as is material, but the difference was not statistically signiﬁcant
(P=.461). The composition of their HTL-AP products can largely explain the effect of
recirculation on the biocrude yield for each biomass type. The HTL-AP used in this study were
obtained from demonstration HTL runs of three sources of algal-bacterial biomass: one from a
pilot algal wastewater treatment system (AWS), and two obtained from harmful algal blooms at
two separate lakes in Florida (HAB-1 and HAB-2). The biomasses consisted of naturally
occurring algal and bacterial species speciﬁc to their respective environments, with no further
species characterization performed. The HAB biomasses were harvested with a dissolved air
ﬂotation system and concentrated through a combination of coagulation and screw press
dewatering before being processed at 300 ˚C and a 60-min retention time in a continuous-ﬂow
HTL pilot reactor at the Department of Agricultural and Biological Engineering at the University
of Illinois Urbana-Champaign. The AWS biomass was dewatered by freeze-thawing and
processed under the same temperature and retention conditions in a 1-liter stirred stainless
steel batch reactor (PARR, Model 4571). These runs were conducted to demonstrate HTL
operations, not for experimental purposes. Therefore, only the HTL-AP and biomass were
accessible for this study, and no other process materials were available. Table 3 shows the
characteristics of the three biomass feedstocks subjected to HTL and their respective HTL-AP.
Before nanoﬁltration, the HTL-AP was ﬁltered with 0.45 µm pore size ﬁlters to remove
suspended solids, followed by pH adjustment with NaOH pellets or concentrated H2SO4.
 As seen in Table 3 and Figure 60, recirculation of the HTL-AP with the highest COD and
lowest ash content (HAB-2) also resulted in the highest biocrude yield increase. The
recirculation of HTL-AP for HAB-1 did not signiﬁcantly affect biocrude yield, which can be
explained by it having the lowest COD, and its dry weight was dominated by ash. Relatively high
ash contents can negatively impact the HTL process, reducing both the yield and quality of the
biocrude (Chen et al., 2014a, 2014c, 2014b).

 Overall, nanoﬁltration was highly advantageous for HTL-AP recirculation of AWS and HAB-2.
In addition to achieving the highest biocrude yields for the feedstocks (T6 and T1, respectively),
concentrating the HTL-AP before recirculation also signiﬁcantly reduces the size of reactors.
Based on the recirculation ratios used in this study (0.94 g HTL-AP per g biomass and 0.61 g
retentate per g biomass), at a 35% permeate recovery, nanoﬁltration reduced the required HTL
reactor size by 17% relative to direct HTL-AP recirculation for processing the same amount of
fresh biomass. This reduction in reactor size can have signiﬁcant impacts on the process’s cost-
effectiveness and sustainability and deserves further investigation.
 The organic composition of the HTL-AP plays an essential role during recirculation. Previous
studies have shown that the increase in biocrude yield when recirculating HTL-AP can be
explained by the reaction of short-chain organic acids with intermediate HTL products (Chen et
al., 2019; Zhu et al., 2015). GC-MS analysis conﬁrmed the presence of organic acids, such as
glycolic, succinic, and lactic acids, in relatively high abundance in the AWS and HAB-2 HTL-APs
(Table 6). The abundance of organic acids was much lower in the HTL-AP of HAB-1, which was
primarily dominated by mannitol (54%), a sugar alcohol. The decomposition of organic acids
during the HTL process is believed to form in situ H2, which can serve as a hydrogen donor in
reactions that favor the formation of biocrude and enhance its quality (Hu et al., 2017).

 Regarding the baseline for each biomass, the highest yields were obtained for AWS,
followed by HAB-1 and HAB-2. Since they were processed in the same conditions at similar

Previous studies have found that higher fat and protein content in the HTL feedstock generally

approximately 4.5 times higher than the HABs and a signiﬁcantly higher protein content (Table

maximum biocrude yield for this type of biomass is typically achieved between 15%-30% solids
content (Watson et al., 2020). Recirculation of HTL-AP inevitably increases the feedstock
moisture. However, nanoﬁltration can minimize feedstock dilution by dewatering and
concentrating the HTL-AP before recirculation.

 Effect of recirculation on biocrude oil characteristics
 Table 7 shows the elemental characteristics of biocrude and their respective HHV, carbon
recovery, and energy recovery. As shown, the baseline for AWS had a higher crude N content
than the HAB-1 and HAB-2. This is due to its higher protein content (Table 3). A meta-analysis
study by Leng et al. (2020) found a strong positive correlation between biomass proteinaceous
nitrogen and biocrude N content among various types of feedstock, including microalgae and
sludge. Common nitrogenous compounds found in biocrude are amine, amides, nitriles, and N-
heterocycles, which are formed during HTL through various reaction pathways (Obeid et al.
2020; Yang et al. 2017; Matayeva et al. 2019).
 The effect of HTL-AP recirculation on the elemental composition of the biocrude (Table 7)
varied for each biomass type. A common outcome was that recirculating the HTL-AP without
nanoﬁltration (AI) increased heteroatom content (N+O) in the biocrude for all three biomasses.
Ranging from 3% to 11%, this increase in N content was not as severe as reported in previous
studies using HTL-AP recirculation without treatment. For example, when recirculating the HTL-
AP from Spirulina platensis, Chen et al. (2019) found an increase of approximately 57% in
biocrude N content. Another study by Chen et al. (2020) found a 15% increase in biocrude N
content after four consecutive cycles of HTL-AP recirculation of Chlorella pyrenoidosa. Previous
experimental evidence showed that during HTL, the presence of polar glycerol promotes the
denitrogenation of biocrude by facilitating the decomposition of highly polar N-containing
compounds into water-soluble chemicals (Hu et al., 2017; Lu et al., 2017). Hu et al. (2017)
reported a decrease of up to 38% in biocrude N content when glycerol was used as a cosolvent
in the HTL of algae. Therefore, the relatively high abundance of glycerol in the HTL-AP of AWS
and HAB-2 (Table 6) likely contributed to the lower increase (3.5%) in biocrude N content
observed for the recirculation of these HTL-AP as-is products (Table 7) (Hu et al., 2017; Lu et
al., 2017).

 As for the nanoﬁltration treatments, T3 displayed an 11.8% reduction in nitrogen content

ammonia, which can react with fatty acids and cellulose derivatives to form biocrude
nitrogenates such as amines, amides, nitriles, indoles, pyridines, and pyrazines (Inoue et al.,

reduction in biocrude HHV for the HAB biomass sources. That was likely due to the increase in
heteroatom content, which negatively affects the HHV of the biocrude. Following the same
reasoning, an increase in HHV was observed for the AWS treatments, which presented a
reduction in heteroatom content (T3 and T6). Concentrating the HTL-AP positively affected
energy recovery (ER) for both AWS and HAB-2 as they showed a maximum increase of 74.3%
(T6) and 112.1% (T1) with respect to their baselines. These treatments also presented a 54.5%
and 113.9% carbon recovery increase, respectively.

nanoﬁltration successfully mitigates the impact of HTL-AP recirculation on biocrude quality.

was the variable that correlated with the smallest change in these parameters.

 Overall, the combination of pH 11 and a ﬁltration temperature of 45 °C (T3) provided the
most favorable outcomes for biocrude yield and quality. This treatment achieved the second-
highest biocrude yield (Figure 60), the highest HHV (Table 7), signiﬁcant reductions in

separation efficiency (Figure 56a) and permeate ﬂux during nanoﬁltration (Figure 56b) in
comparison to HTL-AP of the same biomass (AWS).
 In this study, humic substances were identiﬁed as the primary cause of fouling. In addition
to drastically reducing membrane permeability, fouling also shortens membrane lifespan and
lowers separation selectivity (Van der Bruggen et al., 2008), posing signiﬁcant challenges to the
scalability and cost-effectiveness of this process. Future studies should explore integrating
fouling mitigation strategies, such as adsorption or chemical precipitation, to address these
issues and enhance the efficiency and robustness of HTL-AP nanoﬁltration. Additionally, while
discussed brieﬂy here, a full assessment of nanoﬁltration’s impact on overall cost-effectiveness
and sustainability should be conducted through techno-economic and life cycle analyses.

 HTL-Nanoﬁltration Study 1- Conclusions
 This study demonstrated that nanoﬁltration can improve the yield and quality of biocrude
produced via the recirculation of concentrated HTL-AP. Nanoﬁltration of HTL-AP also drastically
reduces the volume and strength of the HTL aqueous waste, allowing further valorization of this
byproduct. The separation efficiency of nitrogenous compounds from organics was maximum
for pH 11 and a ﬁltration temperature of 45 °C. Elevating ﬁltration temperature also signiﬁcantly
improved permeate ﬂux. The highest biocrude yield (39.5% afdw) was achieved for recirculating
the nanoﬁltered HTL-AP for AWS, which was the feedstock with the highest crude fat and
protein content.

 HTL-Nanoﬁltration Study 2- Overview of HTL-AP pretreatment to optimize nanoﬁltration
 conditions.
 In addition to determining the optimal nanoﬁltration conditions, experiments revealed that
humic substances present in the HTL-AP can heavily impact ﬁltration efficiency. Therefore, a
series of experiments aimed at identifying pretreatment strategies to remove humic acids from
the HTL-AP before nanoﬁltration. The effectiveness of the pretreatment method was analyzed
over ﬁve consecutive HTL-AP recirculation cycles, and the effect of recirculating the
nanoﬁltration retentate was compared to that of recirculating untreated HTL-AP. Additionally, a
preliminary study was conducted to assess the feasibility of using the nanoﬁltration permeate
as a growth supplement for the cultivation of algal-bacterial biomass.

  HTL-Nanoﬁltration Study 2- Materials and methods
 Hydrothermal liquefaction conditions
 The mixed algal-bacterial biomass was obtained from a pilot-scale algal wastewater
treatment system (Algaewheel®, OneWater Inc., Cary, NC) housed at the Illinois Sustainable
Technology Center at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign. The feedstock was
dewatered to approximately 15.4% dry solids via freeze-thaw and ﬁltration with 35 µm pore-size
nylon ﬁlter bags. Approximately 200g of sample was loaded into a 1L stirred high-pressure high-
temperature reactor (4571 HP/HT, Parr Instrument Co, Moline, IL), which was pre-pressurized to
100 psi using nitrogen gas. The HTL reaction was performed at 300 ± 2 °C and a reaction time of
30 minutes at the target temperature. After each run, the HTL solids were separated through
ﬁltration with 1.5 µm pore-size glass microﬁber ﬁlters, and the biocrude was recovered by
solvent extraction with dichloromethane (DCM). The biocrude yield (wt%) was reported on an
ash-free dry weight (afdw) basis, calculated by dividing the mass of biocrude recovered in each
run by the mass of ash-free dry biomass loaded into the reactor. The masses of recirculated
retentate and untreated HTL-AP were excluded from the biocrude yield calculations since they
are internal to the process.
 The baseline, which consisted of HTL of biomass with no HTL-AP recirculation, was
compared to two methods of HTL-AP recirculation: one in which the HTL-AP was recirculated
untreated (UNT), and the other in which the HTL-AP was treated with nanoﬁltration (NF) before
being recirculated. These experiments were performed in duplicate for ﬁve recirculation cycles
for each method. The feedstock mixture for the UNT experiments was prepared at a ratio of 0.82
g of untreated HTL-AP per g of biomass and the NF at 0.18 g of treated HTL-AP per g of biomass.
These ratios were determined based on the assumption that HTL-AP is generated at a constant
rate of 0.82 g per g of feedstock and a consistent permeate recovery of 70% for NF. An
approximate 5% of untreated HTL-AP and retentate from each run was saved for analysis. The
characteristics of the biomass and its baseline HTL-AP are shown in Table 9.

 Nanoﬁltration of HTL aqueous phase
 The nanoﬁltration experiments were performed with the NF270 thin ﬁlm membrane (DuPont
FilmTec©) in a dead-end ﬁltration system (HP4750, Sterlitech, Kent, WA) housed in a water bath
for temperature control. The membrane was cut into coupons of approximately 5 cm in
diameter and soaked in deionized water for at least 24 hours. Throughout the experiment, the
system was maintained at 45 °C and 500 psi, and the HTL-AP was stirred at a constant speed of
240 rpm. These ﬁltration conditions have previously been shown to increase the ﬂux and
separation efficiency during nanoﬁltration of HTL-AP of algal biomass.
 Separation and removal of humic acids can be achieved with hydrochloric acid treatment
followed by centrifugation (Skripkina et al., 2023), which was found to signiﬁcantly reduce
membrane fouling. Thus, before each nanoﬁltration run, the HTL-AP underwent an acid
pretreatment step. It consisted of adjusting the pH of HTL-AP to 1.5 using concentrated
hydrochloric acid (HCl) (ACS reagent grade), followed by centrifugation at 4,000 rpm for 10
                                             Page 92 of 175

minutes. The recovered precipitate was mixed with concentrated HTL-AP and utilized in the NF
HTL recirculation experiments. The supernatant was ﬁltered with a 0.45 µm pore-size ﬁlter, and
its pH was adjusted to 11.0 using NaOH pellets. This pH value was chosen because previous
studies showed that the permeation of total ammonia nitrogen (NH3-N) is maximized at pH 11
(Zhang et al., 2020). Approximately 150 g of pretreated HTL-AP was used in each run, and the
system was operated until approximately 70% of this mass was recovered as permeate. The
nanoﬁltration process separation efficiency was evaluated in terms of solute mass permeation,
which was calculated by dividing the mass of solute that permeated through the membrane by
the mass of solute in the HTL-AP before nanoﬁltration.

 Algae cultivation in nanoﬁltration-treated HTL-AP
 A batch-feed cultivation experiment was conducted to assess the effect of nanoﬁltration
permeate on biomass growth in algal-bacterial systems, using a mixed microalgae culture from
the same system that supplied feedstock for the HTL experiments. The collected fresh biomass
was cultivated in synthetic wastewater for two weeks before the start of the experiments. The
synthetic wastewater was prepared by dissolving ground dry dog food in water and ﬁltering it
through 0.45 µm pore-size ﬁlters. The resulting synthetic wastewater presented a COD of 504
mg/L and an NH₃-N of 12 mg/L, comparable to the level found in medium-strength domestic
wastewater (Tchobanoglous et al., 2014).
 The experiment consisted of three duplicate groups: a group receiving only synthetic
wastewater (Control) and two experimental groups with the addition of permeate at low and
high concentrations, respectively. The low concentration group (Perm (low)) was based on a
modeled closed-loop biocrude production system, in which all the permeate produced would
be recycled back to the algal cultivation system. Mass balance calculations were based on a
wastewater treatment plant with a nominal capacity of 1 million gallons per day of domestic
wastewater, with an annual biomass production of 112 metric tons (ash-free dry weight). The
calculations resulted in a yield of 0.25 g of permeate per g of biomass (15% dw), which, when
recycled back to the wastewater treatment system, would be diluted at a ratio of 1:2900 for
permeate to synthetic wastewater. As for the high concentration group (Perm (high)), previous
algal growth studies using HTL-AP of similar characteristics tested dilutions between 20x and
600x (Biller et al., 2012; Watson et al., 2020). To line up with the higher end of COD and NH₃-N
levels from these previous studies, a 1:30 ratio of permeate to synthetic wastewater was
selected. The permeate used for preparing the cultivation medium was a composite sample
containing an equal amount of permeate collected from each nanoﬁltration run. The composite
permeate sample had a COD of 40,895 mg/L and an NH₃-N of 5,559 mg/L. Table 10 presents
the characteristics of the resulting cultivation medium for each group.

  HTL-Nanoﬁltration Study 2- Results and discussion
 HTL aqueous phase quality analysis
 Figure 63 displays the contents of COD, NH3-N, and the EEM peaks of the HTL-AP for the
baseline and each HTL-AP recirculation experiment (R1 to R5). On average, recirculation
decreased COD levels. However, only NF had a statistically signiﬁcant (P=.026) lower COD than
the baseline. No signiﬁcant differences were found for NH3-N levels (P=.323) (Table 11).
Compounds of a humic nature are foulants known to drastically reduce the efficiency of many
membrane ﬁltration processes (Alomar et al., 2023). Analysis of peak intensity in the humic
substances’ region of the ﬂuorescence spectroscopy EEM revealed that HTL-AP recirculation
substantially increases its humic substance content (Figure 63c). Pretreatment of the HTL-AP
with HCl before NF effectively reduced the aqueous concentrations of humic acids (HAs) to
similar levels to the baseline algae biomass feedstock. Initial NF tests without this pretreatment
resulted in near-zero permeate ﬂux after recirculation cycle number 2 (R2), indicating that NF
                                            Page 95 of 175

can promote a possible gradual accumulation of these foulants in the HTL-AP when a removal
method is not in place. With molecular weights ranging from 2kDa to 1300 kDa, humic acids are
very complex and can contain various functional groups, resulting in pH-dependent properties
(de Melo et al., 2016). They are typically soluble in neutral to alkaline conditions and tend to
precipitate at pH < 2.0 after protonation of certain functional groups (Alomar et al., 2023; de
Melo et al., 2016). The diversity of humic acids, their size, and other compositional factors may
be the reason why higher removals were not achieved in this study. Nonetheless, the HA
pretreatment implemented in this study demonstrated satisfactory efficiency in removing HAs
from the HTL-AP, and the precipitated HAs were recirculated back to HTL processing to increase
the net biocrude yield. This HA pretreatment also facilitated multiple cycles of NF with
recirculation of the retentate. Since the HA EEM peak and the COD levels were not
accumulating in the HTL-AP feed to the NF process over ﬁve recirculation cycles, the buildup of
HAs and other organics is not expected to be a signiﬁcant limit on the use of NF for valorizing
HTL-AP.

  Nanoﬁltration efficiency parameters
 The mass permeation of solutes for each NF recirculation cycle is shown in Figure 64. Mass
permeation is an important efficiency parameter that measures how much a certain solute
                                          Page 97 of 175

permeates the membrane. The average permeation of COD was approximately 51%, and the
NH3-N was 72%. These parameters varied in a relatively narrow range throughout the
experiment, and the effect of the number of recirculation cycles on their permeation was not
statistically signiﬁcant (P=.466 and P=.356 for COD and NH3-N, respectively). For the purposes
of recirculating HTL-AP, a low mass permeation of COD is desirable since it indirectly represents
the amount of organic compounds lost to the permeate. The permeation of NH3-N, on the other
hand, should be maximized to limit its amount in the concentrated fraction. This form of
nitrogen is believed to be a precursor to amidation and cyclization reactions, which can
increase the content of N-containing compounds in biocrude (SundarRajan et al., 2021). The
rejection selectivity of NF membranes, which directly inﬂuences solute permeability, depends
on complex mechanisms involving both size exclusion and charge effects. These effects result
from ﬁxed charges on the membrane surface and the adsorption of ions from the feed solution
(Guo et al., 2023). The NF270 membranes are known to have a molecular weight cut-off of 155
Da and are negatively charged above its isoelectric point, pH 3.5 (Boussu et al., 2006).
Therefore, the permeate from this experiment is largely composed of water and low molecular
weight uncharged compounds.

Figure 64. Average permeate flux at 70% recovery and mass permeation of COD and NH3-N

 The ﬂux through the membrane is also a design parameter of great relevance in membrane
ﬁltration processes. As seen in Figure 64, the average ﬂux at 70% permeate recovery ranged
from 35 LMH to 51 LMH (liters per square meter per hour). A considerable drop in ﬂux was
observed between the nanoﬁltration of the HTL-AP from the baseline biomass and up to the

                                            Page 98 of 175

second recirculation cycle (R2), but when comparing all the recirculation cycle ﬂux data, the
overall effect of the number of cycles on membrane ﬂux was not statistically signiﬁcant
(P=.291). Organic foulants of a humic nature have high fouling potential and it is one of the
greatest challenges for nanoﬁltration of solutions containing these compounds (Song et al.,
2011). Since the pretreatment of the HTL-AP with HCl effectively reduced the concentration of
humic substances in the nanoﬁltration feed (Figure 63), the differences found in ﬂux between
runs were likely caused by other factors. Pore blockage by adhesive substances, ion adsorption
on the membrane surface, and concentration polarization are some of the main mechanisms
that restrict the transport of both water and solutes through nanoﬁltration membranes (Bellona
et al., 2010; Guo et al., 2023). An increased formation of compounds associated with these
mechanisms during HTL may help explain the decline in ﬂux observed between the baseline
and the recirculation cycles (Figure 64).

 Effect of HTL aqueous phase recirculation on biocrude oil yield
 The yield, HHV, and elemental characteristics of the HTL biocrude are shown in Table 12.
HTL-AP recirculation signiﬁcantly increases biocrude yield, especially when NF is used. Over
ﬁve cycles, NF presented a mean biocrude yield of 42.4 wt% (daf, dry ash free basis), which
represents a 28% increase compared to the baseline (33.1 wt% daf) and a 14% increase
compared to UNT (37.1 wt% daf). All three mean values were statistically different from each
other (P<.001) (Table 13). Increases in biocrude yield during recirculation of HTL-AP are mainly
attributed to the increased presence of compounds from the recirculated HTL-AP that react
with intermediate HTL products to form biocrude oil and the catalytic effect of certain salts and
organic acids (Watson et al., 2020). Since nanoﬁltration concentrates these compounds, the
effect of recirculation on oil yield is expected to surpass that of the untreated form, as
experimentally conﬁrmed in this study. In previous HTL-AP recirculation studies, the biocrude
yield gradually increased with the number of cycles (Biller et al., 2016; Ramos-Tercero et al.,
2015). This was not the case in this study as the effect of the number of cycles on biocrude yield
was not statistically signiﬁcant (P=.170). However, recirculation of both the UNT and NF treated
HTL-AP facilitated consistently higher biocrude yields than the baseline throughout all ﬁve
recirculation cycles.

 Effect of HTL aqueous phase recirculation on biocrude oil characteristics
 As seen in Table 12, the NF biocrude presented the highest average C and H contents and
the lowest O content. Its N content was slightly higher than the baseline, but the difference was
not statistically signiﬁcant (P=.306). The UNT biocrude, on the other hand, had a statistically
signiﬁcantly higher N content than both the NF and the baseline (P=.002). This is consistent
with the ﬁndings of previous studies that found that recirculation of untreated HTL-AP from algal
biomass can signiﬁcantly increase biocrude N content (Chen et al., 2019, 2020). Thus, the
proposed NF treatment can mitigate this key drawback of recirculation of HTL-AP. Experimental
evidence suggests that the recirculation of certain compounds in the HTL-AP impacts the
elemental migration during HTL (Watson et al., 2020). For example, glycerol is believed to
facilitate the decomposition of highly polar N-containing compounds into water-soluble
substances, promoting the denitrogenation of biocrude (Hu et al., 2017; Lu et al., 2017a).
Glycerol was the second most abundant organic compound identiﬁed in the baseline HTL-AP ().
Although both NF and UNT initially contained glycerol, its capture and concentration via
nanoﬁltration may have been enough to counterbalance the increase in N content associated
with HTL-AP recirculation, as observed for UNT. Also, NF reduces the ratio of nitrogen to
organics in the recirculated HTL-AP material because of the preferential retention of organics
versus ammonia.

 Since elemental composition directly impacts the HHV calculation, the effects of

higher than that of the UNT, which in turn was higher than the baseline. These differences were
statistically different from each other (P<.001). Figure 65 shows the Van Krevelen diagram of
atomic H:C versus O:C of the biocrudes and compares these elemental ratios with a typical
petroleum crude oil. The O:C and H:C ratios for the biocrude from the baseline algal biomass
were 0.14 and 1.51, respectively. The mean O:C of UNT and NF were 0.10 and 0.08, respectively,
and the mean H:C ratios were 1.51 and 1.55, respectively. The higher H:C and lower O:C for the

biocrude that would be better suited as a petroleum substitute compared to the baseline and

 Energy and carbon balance
 Figure 66 shows the energy and carbon transference from the biomass to the different HTL
products. NF signiﬁcantly increases the recovery of both carbon and energy to the biocrude,
consequently decreasing losses to the solid, aqueous, and gaseous phases. The effect of
various HTL-AP treatments had a statistically signiﬁcant impact on energy recovery into the
biocrude (P<.001) (Table 15). Speciﬁcally, the average energy recovery in the biocrude was
increased from the baseline of 51.6 ± 0.5 %, to the UNT recirculation case (58.1 ± 1.7 %), and
further increased with NF recirculation case (68.4 ± 3.0 %). A notable decrease in energy
transference to the solids fraction was observed for the recirculation experiments, which also
presented a decrease in solids formation. The baseline had a 16.2 ± 0.2 % (dw) solids yield,
whereas the UNT and NF experiments averaged 13.3 ± 0.5 % (dw) and 13.6 ± 1.8 % (dw),
respectively. The water-soluble organic chemicals in the HTL-AP participate in reactions that
stabilize intermediate HTL products and decrease the formation of solids (Leng et al., 2020).
The relatively low energy content of the HTL solids fraction in both UNT and NF suggests that the
transfer of C and H to this fraction during HTL is limited, which indicates that the mass of
recovered HTL solids is likely dominated by ash from the feedstock.
 The averages for the recovery of carbon into the biocrude for the baseline (46.1 ± 0.3 %),
UNT (51.5 ± 1.7 %), and NF (59.8 ± 2.5 %) were also statistically different from each other
(P<.001) (Table 13). Besides its impact on the biocrude’s energy content, the recovery of carbon
into the biocrude fraction also impacts the process’s overall environmental sustainability since
less carbon is transferred to the gaseous and aqueous phases in the form of gases and organic
pollutants. The HTL gaseous phase is typically composed primarily of CO2, which can account
for more than 90% of its constituents (Lu et al., 2017b). The number of cycles was found to only
have a marginally signiﬁcant effect on the transference of carbon (P=.021) and energy (P=.037)
into the NF biocrude fraction, suggesting that the use of nanoﬁltration gradually increases the
recovery of energy and carbon into the biocrude.

 GC-MS analysis of biocrude oil
 GC-MS analysis was performed on the biocrude of the baseline case and after the ﬁfth
recirculation cycle (R5) of NF and UNT cases. In total, over 200 organic compounds were
detected in the three samples. Table 16 shows the major compounds identiﬁed and their
associated chemical class based on the hierarchical chemical taxonomy developed by
Djoumbou-Feunang et al. (2016). It is important to note that because the GC-MS analysis had a
ﬁnal temperature of 280 °C, only compounds with boiling points below this temperature were
detected. TGA analysis (Figure 68) showed that this fraction corresponds to 27.6% of the
weight of the baseline biocrude, 35.7% of the UNT, and 39.1% of the NF biocrude. Some low
boiling point compounds may have been lost during the biocrude extraction procedure due to
evaporation. For comparison, distillates with boiling points below 280 °C make up 59% of
typical petroleum crude oils in U.S. reﬁneries (US EIA, 2024).
                                           Page 105 of 175

 The composition of the biocrude fraction detected by GC-MS was dominated by lipids and
lipid-like molecules, mainly fatty acids and their derivatives. This class amounted to 23% to
28% of the relative abundance. These compounds are produced via the hydrolyzation of lipid
molecules during HTL and signiﬁcantly contribute to the O content of the biocrude (Biller and
Ross, 2011). They can be deoxygenated and upgraded to hydrocarbons through catalytic
decarboxylation, improving the fuel properties of the biocrude (Xu et al., 2018). Other organic
acids and organoheterocyclic compounds were also found in relatively high abundance,
corresponding to 33% to 41% of the biocrude composition. HTL-AP recirculation seemed to
decrease the abundance of these classes of compounds.
 The most common compounds identiﬁed varied among the different types of biocrude. In
the baseline case, amino acids, ketones, and fatty amides were the main compound classes
and made up 31% of the compounds. In the UNT biocrude, amino acids, fatty amides, and
phenols comprised 35% of the compounds. The NF biocrude had a more spread composition,
with its most common compounds (fatty acyls, fatty amides, and ketones) making up
approximately 25% of its composition. The notable abundance of N-containing compounds
reﬂects the composition of the original biomass feedstock, which is dominated by protein.
These results are consistent with previous studies that characterized biocrude from HTL of algal
biomass (Biller and Ross, 2011; Brown et al., 2010) and other protein-rich feedstocks from
wastewater treatment (Basar et al., 2023; Shah et al., 2021). Catalytic upgrading, including
hydrogenation, deoxygenation, decarboxylation, and denitrogenation, can address the relatively
high N and O contents of the biocrude and increase hydrocarbon content (Xu et al., 2018). The
relatively higher presence of hydrocarbons in the NF suggests that this approach beneﬁts the
formation of these compounds, potentially decreasing the cost of subsequent upgrading
processes.

 FTIR analysis of biocrude oil
 FT-IR spectra of the biocrude were obtained for the baseline case and the ﬁfth recirculation
cycle (R5) of UNT and NF cases (Figure 67). The spectra of the three samples were almost
identical, indicating similar qualitative compositional characteristics. The functional group
characterization was done based on the band assignment described by Chen et al. (2019). The
broad peak between 3240 and 3140 cm-1 was attributed to O–H and N–H stretching vibrations
from amino groups and hydroxyl compounds such as alcohols and phenols (Chen et al., 2019).
Peaks between 3000 and 2800 cm-1 were attributed to C–H stretching vibrations of methyl and
methylene groups, which are related to the presence of aliphatic groups. The peak between
1700 and 1640 cm-1 was attributed to C=O stretching bands, indicating the presence of esters,
acids, ketones, and aldehydes (Yuan et al., 2011). The peaks between 1460 and 1330 cm-1 can
also be attributed to C–H stretching and the presence of fats and esters. The 1330 and 1200 cm-
1 peaks were assigned to C–O stretching and related to acids and alcohols. The peak at 1106
cm-1 was also attributed to C–O stretching but related to the presence of alcohol and phenolic
compounds. This peak was only clearly present in the UNT biocrude. Lastly, the 830 and 690
cm-1 peaks were attributed to C–H bending and related to aromatic compounds and their
substituted derivatives. The presence of ketones, phenols, and heterocyclic compounds was
conﬁrmed in the light fraction (< 280 °C) of all three biocrude samples, as shown in Table 16.

 Boiling point distribution of biocrude oil
 The biocrude of the baseline and the ﬁfth recirculation cycle (R5) for UNT and NF were also
analyzed using TGA in a nitrogen atmosphere, which simulates a crude oil fractional distillation
process (Chen et al., 2020). All three biocrude samples presented two major weight loss peaks
in the derivative thermogravimetric (DTG) curve (Figure 68), one between 250-300 °C and the
other between 400-450 °C. The NF also presented two local maxima, one at around 87 °C and
one at approximately 195 °C. Another noticeable difference is that the TG curves of UNT and NF
had a higher weight loss ratio than the baseline, especially at temperatures below 343 °C,
indicating a higher presence of lighter compounds.
 Following the temperature ranges described by Speight (2014), the biocrude's boiling point
distribution was partitioned into six distillate range fractions, as presented in Table 17. For all
three samples, the highest weight loss occurred in the diesel range (232-343 °C). Both NF and
UNT presented considerable increases in the diesel, gasoline, and kerosene ranges, accounting
for a total of 57% and 59% of their weights, thus displaying a greater potential for producing
drop-in fuels. The baseline only had 48% in the same boiling point range. NF and UNT had fewer
heavier compounds (343-566 °C). This directly affects the cost-effectiveness of the entire
process, as they contain proportionally less high boiling point material and will require lower
processing temperatures for subsequent upgrading (Biller et al., 2016, 2015). At 9.8% of its
weight, the NF biocrude presented a signiﬁcantly lower amount of residue (>566 °C). Previous
studies showed that this fraction could be as high as 30% for certain algal feedstocks (Shakya et
al., 2017).

 Algae cultivation in nanoﬁltration-treated HTL-AP
 Figure 69 shows the results from the algal growth experiment with synthetic wastewater
and permeate from nanoﬁltration experiments at low concentration, Perm (low), and high
concentration, Perm (high). The initial VSS concentration for all treatments was approximately
203 mg/L at the start of the experiment (Figure 69a). The Perm (high) treatment presented a
growth pattern different from the one observed for the other treatments. Its VSS peaked at 863
mg/L at week 5, while the control and Perm (low) peaked at 534 mg/L and 511 mg/L,
respectively. The higher amount of biomass growth for Perm (high) was also conﬁrmed visually.
This treatment presented a much denser dark green culture, indicating a predominance of green
microalgae species. In addition to having a higher concentration of nutrients, the Perm (high)
cultivation medium also had a higher concentration of growth-promoting compounds. GC-MS
analysis of the permeate from a nanoﬁltration experiment using the same HTL-AP from this
study showed that its composition is dominated by glycerol and organic acids (Table 18). Short-
chain organic acids have been shown to increase the growth of mixotrophic algal species (Leng
et al., 2018). Similarly, in addition to also promoting mixotrophic algal growth, glycerol can
increase the removal of NH3-N from wastewater and increase the content of fatty acids in the
resulting biomass (Tan et al., 2020).
 Throughout the experiment, Perm (high) had an average growth rate of 36.8 VSS mg/L/day,
which was statistically signiﬁcantly higher (P=.008) than both the control and Perm (low). At an
average of 19.5 and 22.2 VSS mg/L/day, respectively, the growth rate for the control and Perm
(low) was not statistically different from each other. The growth rate for Perm (high) displayed a
reasonably steady increase between weeks 1 and 4 (Figure 69b). The decline observed in the
                                             Page 111 of 175

following weeks was likely due to the high density of cells in the ﬂask, which can affect
photosynthetic algal growth by generating competition for CO2 for autotrophic and decreasing
light penetration (Abdur Razzak et al., 2023). Additionally, the accumulation of toxic compounds
may also have contributed to the observed decline. Aromatic compounds and nitrogen-
containing organics are found in HTL-AP of algal biomass and can have toxic effects on
microorganisms even at very low concentrations (Watson et al., 2020).

 HTL-Nanoﬁltration Study 2- Conclusions
 Humic acid precipitation proved an effective and essential pretreatment for the
nanoﬁltration of high-strength HTL-AP. Integrating nanoﬁltration with HTL signiﬁcantly improves
the HTL conversion efficiency and the potential for producing drop-in fuels from algal-bacterial
biomass. Results from this study showed a greater than 30% increase in both energy and
carbon recovery. Additionally, nanoﬁltration of HTL-AP allows for nutrient recovery by producing
a nutrient-rich permeate that enhances biomass growth rate by 89% in mixed algal-bacterial
cultivation systems.

 HTL-Nanoﬁltration Study 3- HTL and Nanoﬁltration TEA & LCA Overview
 The economic and sustainability implications of integrating nanoﬁltration into the HTL of
algal-bacterial biomass are assessed through techno-economic analysis (TEA) and life-cycle
analysis (LCA) was evaluated. Three scenarios are compared: a) baseline: which consists of
HTL with no HTL-AP recirculation and catalytic hydrothermal gasiﬁcation (CHG) is used for HTL-
AP treatment; b) direct HTL-AP recirculation: in this scenario, 50% of the HTL-AP is recirculated
(no treatment) and the remaining 50% is treated via CHG; and c) nanoﬁltration-enhanced HTL:
in this scenario, CHG is replaced with nanoﬁltration, which is used to concentrate the HTL-AP
before it is recirculated back to HTL and the permeate is recycled back to the cultivation
system.

Additionally, the nanoﬁltration parameters are optimized to maximize the permeation of
nitrogenous compounds, effectively reducing the amount of nitrogen recirculated back to HTL.

conditions and results reported by (Jones et al., 2014a). In their study, HTL-AP from HTL of algae
was processed at 350 °C and 2,980 psi using a 7.8% Ru/C catalyst and achieved a 57% carbon-
to-syngas conversion, with CH4 accounting for 71% of the syngas composition. The CHG
reactors were sized based on a weight hourly space velocity (WHSV) of 3.99, and a lifespan of
one year was assumed for the Ru/C catalyst.
 In the NF scenario, humic acids were removed from HTL-AP through HCl treatment followed
by centrifugation, as described by Skripkina et al. (2023). This pretreatment involves ﬁrst
lowering the HTL-AP pH to 1.5, then centrifuging it at 4,000 rpm in a continuous bowl centrifuge
with a residence time of 10 minutes. The precipitate is sent to HTL, while the supernatant is ﬁrst
fed to nanoﬁltration after its pH is raised to 11 using NaOH pellets. At this pH, the permeation of
total ammonia nitrogen is maximized (Zhang et al., 2020).
 The nanoﬁltration process is carried out in a cross-ﬂow ﬁltration unit equipped with an
NF270 thin-ﬁlm membrane (DuPont FilmTec©), operating at a ﬁltration temperature of 45 °C and
a pressure of 500 psi. Under these operational conditions and a 70% permeate recovery, a
permeate ﬂow of 41 liters per square meter per hour (LMH) is achieved for the HTL-AP generated
from the feedstock used in this study (Chapter 4).

 Biocrude upgrading
 The HTL facility in all three scenarios featured catalytic hydrotreating and hydrocracking
units for in-situ biocrude upgrading. The process conditions were based on studies by
Snowden-Swan et al. (2022) and (Jones et al., 2014a), which assumed that HTL biocrude
hydrotreated at 403 °C and a pressure of 1,515 psi using a CoMo/alumina catalyst results in a
fuel blendstock composed of 16% light gas, 13% naphtha, 17% jet fuel, 48% heavy diesel, and
11% heavy fraction. The heavy fraction is then hydrocracked at 370 °C and 1,035 psi. The
hydrotreater was sized based on a WHSV of 0.625 and a catalyst lifespan of two years, while the
hydrocracker was sized with a WHSV of 0.5 and a catalyst lifespan of ﬁve years.
 The hydrogen gas was assumed to be sourced from steam methane reforming (SMR).
Snowden-Swan et al. (2022) assumed a chemical H2 consumption of 0.0430 (wt/wt HTL
biocrude) for a biocrude with a higher heating value (HHV) of 38.5 MJ/kg. Based on an inverse
relationship with HHV, the H2 consumption for the Baseline, UNT, and NF scenarios were
estimated to be 0.0478, 0.0462, and 0.0445 (wt/wt), respectively.

 The installed equipment costs were sourced from various studies in the literature (Jones et
al., 2014a; Knorr et al., 2013; Samhaber and Nguyen, 2014; Snowden-Swan et al., 2022) and

costs, including site development, additional piping, project contingency, and other associated
expenses, were estimated as a function of total installed cost using cost factors from Snowden-
Swan et al. (2022).

 Annual operating costs varied across the scenarios and primarily consisted of utilities,
catalysts, chemicals, membranes for nanoﬁltration, and labor. The costs of upgrading and CHG
catalysts, as well as labor, were referenced from Jones et al. (2014a). Electricity was assumed

respectively. The price of the NF270 (DuPont FilmTec©) ﬂat sheet membrane was assumed to

 Life cycle analysis
 The LCA conducted in this study followed a system boundary similar to that of the TEA.
Except that the boundary was expanded to include a well-to-wheel assessment of greenhouse
gas (GHG) emissions associated with the fuel produced in each scenario. The primary focus of
the analysis was to quantify the life cycle GHG emissions of the fuel, expressed on both a
gasoline gallon equivalent (gge) and a per-ton-of-biomass basis.
 The emission estimates for materials and energy sources were obtained from the GREET®
2023 (version 1.3.0.14168) database (ANL, 2023). The major sources of emissions included in
the analysis were associated with the production of NaOH, HCl, blue hydrogen gas, thin-ﬁlm
membranes, energy generation, and catalysts. This study did not account for land use or
emissions related to plant construction and equipment manufacturing.
 Life cycle emissions of materials not available in the GREET database, such as the CHG
catalyst and ﬁltration membranes, were estimated based on the emissions from producing the
input materials required for their manufacturing. For the nanoﬁltration membranes, the
structural composition was modeled based on data reported by Boussouga and Lhassani
(2017), which characterized NF270 (DuPont FilmTec©) thin-ﬁlm ﬁltration membranes as
multilayer structures with a ~1.65 µm thick polyamide active layer supported by a ~160 µm
thick microporous polysulfone layer. The mass of these materials per unit of membrane area
was estimated by multiplying their thicknesses by their respective densities, as Veit (2022) and
Ismail and Mansourizadeh (2010) reported. The CHG catalyst was assumed to consist of 7.8%
ruthenium and 92.2% activated carbon by weight (Jones et al., 2014). These material-speciﬁc
considerations ensured a comprehensive estimation of emissions across all analyzed
scenarios.

 Sensitivity analysis
 Based on MFSP (Figure 72), the NF scenario presents the most cost-effective pathway for
producing drop-in sustainable fuels through the HTL of algal-bacterial biomass. Accordingly,
this scenario was selected for sensitivity analysis, in which key input parameters affecting the
MFSP were varied one at a time. The resulting relative changes to its MFSP of $3.32/gge were
calculated and are displayed in Figure 73. Internal rate of return, feedstock dry solids content,
plant size, avoided sludge disposal costs, and biocrude yield are the ﬁve model inputs that most
affect MFSP. Interestingly, the internal rate of return was the parameter with the largest effect on
MFSP. Increasing its value to 20% caused the MFSP to increase to $4.63/gge (39% increase).
While reducing the internal rate of return to 0% caused the MFSP to decrease signiﬁcantly, it
may not be a viable route for reducing the MFSP since it would make this pathway a less
attractive investment for investors.

Figure 73. Sensitivity analysis of key inputs affecting the minimum fuel selling price (MFSP) for
the NF scenario ($3.32/gasoline gallon equivalent).

 Since sludge dewatering is a standard part of the wastewater treatment process, it was not
considered in this cost analysis. However, because feedstock solids content has a signiﬁcant
effect on MFSP (Figure 73), identifying cost-effective ways to increase the solids content from
25% to 35% could reduce the MFSP by 27%, from $3.32 to $2.41/gge. It is important to note that
increasing the solids content may pose substantial technical challenges, including reduced
feedstock pumpability and the production of a much more concentrated HTL-AP, which could
signiﬁcantly impact the nanoﬁltration process. However, the potential for a signiﬁcant reduction
in MFSP shows that optimization of this parameter is worth further investigation.

 As previously mentioned, the credit associated with the avoided sludge disposal costs may
vary depending on the strategies adopted by different wastewater treatment plants. The model
showed relatively high sensitivity to this parameter as reducing the avoided sludge disposal
credit to $92.29 per dry ton of biomass increases the MFSP to $4.19/gge (26% increase).
Contrarily, increasing this credit to $276.86 per dry ton of biomass leads to an MFSP of
$2.44/gge (27% decrease).
 Feedstock compositional parameters can signiﬁcantly impact biocrude yield during HTL,
and biomass from algal wastewater treatment systems such as the one used in this study are
particularly susceptible to compositional changes due to seasonal, environmental, and
operational variations (Chen et al., 2017, 2014). This introduces a considerable level of
uncertainty to the TEA since a reduction in biocrude yield from 42% to 30% (afdw) leads to an
MFSP of $3.74/gge (13% increase). Acid biomass pretreatments and catalyst addition to HTL
can signiﬁcantly improve biocrude yield while requiring minimal process and operational
changes (Hu et al., 2019). They also may help mitigate MFSP increases resulting from reduced
biocrude yields. However, their techno-economic and life cycle implications must be carefully
evaluated to determine their overall impact on the process.
 The model has shown relatively low sensitivity to parameters related to the nanoﬁltration of
HTL-AP, such as the price of membrane and the price of NaOH and HCl used in the
pretreatment prior to nanoﬁltration to remove humic substances. As previously mentioned,
increasing the solids content of the feedstock may lead to a much more concentrated HTL-AP,
requiring adjustments in the nanoﬁltration process, which may increase the effect of
nanoﬁltration-related parameters on the process cost. Although these parameters may not
affect the MFSP as much as other process parameters, the production of these chemicals and
the fabrication process of the membrane may result in signiﬁcant added life cycle CO2eq
emissions. These effects were analyzed and compared in the LCA section of this study.

 HTL-Nanoﬁltration Study 3- HTL and Nanoﬁltration TEA/ LCA Conclusions
 The techno-economic and sustainability implications of integrating nanoﬁltration (NF) into
the recirculation of HTL-AP from algal-bacterial biomass were evaluated through TEA and LCA.
Compared to the other two HTL-AP handling strategies analyzed in this study, namely CHG
(baseline scenario) and a combination of CHG and untreated HTL-AP recirculation (UNT
scenario), nanoﬁltration signiﬁcantly reduces the capital and operating costs of fuel production,
                                          Page 129 of 175

resulting in an MFSP of $3.32/gge, which is 44% lower than the Baseline and 50% lower than
UNT. Notable differences were also found in the process’s overall energy balance, with the NF
scenario producing signiﬁcantly more energy than it consumes. The comparative LCA indicated
that all three scenarios achieve net negative life-cycle CO2eq emissions on both a dry ton of
biomass and gge basis. Normalizing the MFSP by the amount of carbon offset in each scenario
showed that NF is a comparatively more cost-effective strategy for reducing GHG emissions
from the transportation sector.
 HTL-Nanoﬁltration Studies Overall Summary
 The results of this research showed that nanoﬁltration is an effective strategy for valorizing
the HTL-AP of algal-bacterial biomass. When optimized for maximum separation efficiency of
organics from nutrients (pH 11 and ﬁltration temperature of 45 °C), 99% of the organics were
recovered in the retentate. Recirculating the retentate back to HTL increased biocrude yield by
70% to 116% while reducing biocrude nitrogen content by up to 12%. The carbon and energy
capture in the biocrude also increased by 66% and 68%, respectively.
 Humic acid precipitation proved an effective and essential pretreatment for the
nanoﬁltration of high-strength HTL-AP, allowing the process to sustain reasonable permeate
ﬂuxes over ﬁve consecutive recirculation cycles. Thermogravimetric analysis of the biocrude
showed that 83% of its mass falls in the distillate range of sustainable transportation fuels.
Additionally, biomass cultivation experiments showed that adding the permeate from the
nanoﬁltration of HTL-AP to the cultivation medium at approximately 3 wt% increased the
biomass growth rate by 89%.
 The TEA and LCA revealed that, compared to the other valorization strategies, integration of
nanoﬁltration improves both the cost-effectiveness and sustainability of biofuels produced via
HTL of algal-bacterial biomass. The analysis showed that this HTL conﬁguration could produce
net carbon-negative fuels at a minimum fuel selling price (MFSP) of $3.32 per gasoline gallon
equivalent (gge).

Techno-Economic Analysis and Life Cycle Assessment
 Overview
 Techno-economic analysis (TEA) and life cycle assessment (LCA) were conducted to
evaluate the cost and potential carbon emissions reduction of the proposed Algaewheel plus
hydrothermal liquefaction (HTL) process at scale. Full-scale wastewater treatment models were
developed using the commercially available software, CapdetWorks. User-deﬁned process
models were developed for the various Algaewheel operational scenarios that were
demonstrated in this study. Comparison process models for wastewater treatment via
conventional activated sludge (CAS) and traditional algal raceway pond (Raceway) were also
established for comparison to the Algaewheel scenarios. The wastewater treatment process
models developed for TEA and LCA were combined with the HTL process models and cost data
that were generated from this study, along with data from published HTL literature, to evaluate
the total cost and carbon emissions of the integrated wastewater treatment and HTL biofuel
production process.

 Initial Baseline Model Development
 Process models for full-scale wastewater treatment were established using the software
CadpetWorks (Hydromantics ESS, Inc.). CapdetWorks is a commercially available wastewater
treatment design modeling software that allows the user to drag-and-drop unit processes to
build a plant schematic and estimate the costs to build, operate and maintain the facility. It
utilizes industry-accepted algorithms to derive the required unit process designs and estimate
the cost of all treatment processes.

 All process models that were developed in CapdetWorks included the following unit
processes as illustrated in Figure 78: preliminary treatment (i.e., screening and grit removal),
primary clariﬁcation, secondary treatment with nitrogen removal (via either CAS, Algaewheel, or
traditional algal raceway pond), secondary clariﬁcation, centrifugation of primary and
secondary sludge up to 20% solids content, and effluent discharge. In all scenarios, a medium
strength inﬂuent wastewater was assumed (220 mg/L BOD), with 40% nutrient removal (N&P) to
meet discharge standards. Plant lifetime was set to 30 years, with an 8% interest rate, and land
cost assumed to be $50,000 per acre. Initial model development was carried out during Budget
Period 2 (BP2) of the project using 2022 USD values, modelling treatment capacities of 1, 10,
and 50 MGD. This initial modeling assumed a cost of $4 per ft2 for a simple plastic tank cover,
for climates that experience freezing temperatures. In BP3, the 1 MGD model was reﬁned and
ﬁnalized to reﬂect 2025 USD values, and the cover costs were updated to $75 per ft2. A
summary of these foundational model assumptions is provided in Table 21.

 Initial TEA/LCA Baseline Model Validation
 TEA results from the initial CadpetWorks models developed in BP2 using 2022 USD values,
were compared to TEA results from an NREL report (Clippinger and Davis, 2021) for wastewater
treatment via a traditional algal raceway pond. This comparison served to validate the accuracy
of the CapdetWorks models. Results from this comparison are shown in Figure 79. The NREL
models were established for 10 and 50 MGD wastewater treatment capacity and assumed that
100% of wastewater nutrients would be removed during algal cultivation, with supplemental
CO2 addition, at an algal productivity of 25 g/m2/day. The NREL report assumed land costs to be
$3,000 per acre. The NREL report also assumed that conventional wastewater treatment costs
ranged from $2,400 to $4500 per MG (treatment plant capacity was not speciﬁed).

 When the same assumptions (10 or 50 MGD, 100% nutrient removal, and $3,000 per acre,

updated and ﬁnalized in BP3, as described next.

 Baseline TEA/LCA Model Finalization
 Having established a valid initial baseline TEA model for the three different full-scale
wastewater treatment scenarios (CAS, Algaewheel, and Raceway Pond), further reﬁnements
were made focusing on just the 1 MGD plant capacity. 1 MGD represents the current upper limit
for the Algaewheel at scale. Most full-scale Algaewheel systems that are currently in place
operate at less than 1 MGD average daily ﬂow.

 Reﬁnements to the initial 1 MGD baseline process models included updating the dollar
value to reﬂect 2025 USD, and updating the cost for inclusion of a greenhouse/cover in the
Algaewheel and Raceway Pond scenarios from $4 per ft2 to $75 per ft2 . This value was
determined based on conversations with One Water. It represents the cost for implementing a
permanent greenhouse structure in which the algal systems would be housed and operated in
climates that experience freezing temperatures. This kind of greenhouse encloser would be
necessary to keep the algae alive and functioning during the cold season. An example of this is
the Bookwalter, IL site. In a warm climate like Okeechobee, FL no greenhouse cover would be
needed since the air temperature rarely drops below freezing.

 Algaewheel Treatment Scenarios – Process Model Development
 Using the established baseline wastewater treatment model, productivity and cost values
for the Algae Whell unit process were varied, based on experimental work that was carried out
in this study. Productivity values for each experimental treatment that was carried out in the
Bookwalter, IL and Okeechobee, FL demonstration systems are shown in Figure 81. A different
Algaewheel system was used at either site: 0.45 m wheel diameter in IL, and 0.9 m wheel
diameter in FL. Based on the land area needed to treat 1 MGD of wastewater in each system
(3573 m2 for the 0.45 m wheel system, and 1787 m2 for the 0.9 m wheel system) the estimated
daily biomass production for each treatment scenario was calculated and is also shown in
Figure 81. As presented and discussed in earlier sections of this report, all treatments resulted
in increased biomass productivity compared to the baseline (no treatment) at each
demonstration site. IAA treatment at the northern site resulted in the greatest improvement
(74%) to biomass productivity, followed by bioaugmentation (52%), higher loading (1.5x) (48%)
and heating (20%). Higher loading (1.3x) at the southern site, resulted in an approximate 30%
increase in productivity.

 Life Cycle Assessment
 LCA was conducted to compare the different treatment scenarios in terms of global
warming potential (GWP). A system boundary diagram is provided as . Figure 86 The functional
unit for both scenarios was established as 1 MG of wastewater treated. Foreground processes
included all wastewater treatment unit processes, as established in the CapdetWorks models
and Algaewheel scenario models, as well as HTL conversion with nanoﬁltration, and HTL
upgrading as established in this project. Background processes in this LCA included production
of polymer for sludge thickening, plastic for the Algaewheel wheels and Raceway pond liner,
electricity, concrete, glass, acetic acid which was used as a proxy for IAA and sodium malate,
NaOH, HCl, catalyst and blue H2 for HTL upgrading, and polymer for the nanoﬁltration
membrane. Life cycle inventories for background processes related to the wastewater
treatment component of the system were obtained from EcoInvent 3.0 LCI database, accessed
via SimaPro (version 9.1.0.8 multi-user). The TRACI 2.1 V1.05/US-Canadian 2008 life cycle
impact assessment method was used to determine the Global Warming Potential (GWP)
associated with one unit of these background processes. Life cycle inventories and CO2
emissions for background processes related to HTL conversion and upgrading proceses were
obtained from the GREET database as described previously. All resource inputs and kg of CO2
equivalent emissions per functional unit (MG of wastewater) are summarized in Table 27.

 LCA results are shown in Figure 87 and broken down by either (a) unit process or (b)
resource input. Net GWP (as kg CO2 eq) per MG wastewater and per ton of biomass produced is
presented. For all Algaewheel scenarios representative of the northern (Illinois) site, inclusion of
a glass cover was considered. All Algaewheel scenarios resulted in a lower net GWP per MG
wastewater treated compared to CAS, except for the higher heating condition. The baseline and
higher loading Algaewheel northern climate scenarios resulted in a 44% and 48% reduction in
net kg CO2 eq/MG compared to CAS, while the baseline and higher loading Algaewheel southern
climate (Florida) scenarios resulted in a 46% and 53% reduction in net kg CO2 eq/MG compared
to CAS. The raceway pond resulted in the lowest net GWP in terms of both wastewater
treatment and biomass production due to the large amount of biomass and corresponding
fossil fuel offset that can be achieved in that system. Electricity input for wastewater treatment
represented the largest contribution to CO2 emissions in all scenarios.

 Overall TEA/LCA Summary
 The TEA and LCA indicate that the Algaewheel can reduce the cost of wastewater treatment
(WWT) compared to treatment via CAS or a traditional raceway pond. Raceway treatment
results in the highest WWT cost due to greater biomass dewatering infrastructure and

                                        Page 148 of 175

operational needs. Raceway WWT with a cover is not economically viable due to the large land
area that a raceway would occupy. The higher loading scenario in the Algaewheel resulted in the
most favorable combination of cost and biomass productivity, compared to other Algaewheel
operating scenarios. The southern site (Okeechobee, FL) demonstrated potentially higher
productivity relative to the northern site (Bookwalter, IL) when the difference in wheel size was
accounted for. This could potentially be due to site variability or the different Algaewheel
systems employed. Utilizing nanoﬁltration instead of catalytic hydrothermal gasiﬁcation to treat
HTL-AP can signiﬁcantly reduce biofuel costs. Wastewater treatment + HTL with nanoﬁltration
can provide a lower cost for WWT than CAS, resulting in an MFSP as low as –9.45 $/gge
compared to established PNNL estimates of 2.78 – 3.11 $/gge. All Algaewheel treatment
scenarios resulted in lower GWP compared to CAS, except for addition of heating due to the
high electricity input required. Up to 53% reduction in GWP per MG of wastewater treated was
estimated for the southern site condition with higher loading. The raceway scenario results in
the lowest GWP due to greater fossil fuel avoidance; however, this option comes with an
increase in WWT cost and signiﬁcant land area requirements.

Signiﬁcant Accomplishments and Conclusions:
Signiﬁcant Accomplishments

 • Demonstrated at full-scale a dual-purpose algal cultivation and wastewater treatment
 system capable of achieving a typical productivity of 44-64 g/m2/day, which is well
 above other reported cultivation systems and the project goal of 30 g/m2/day.
 • Demonstrated at full-scale the signiﬁcant beneﬁts of bioaugmentation of the
 Algaewheel to improve algal productivity and wastewater treatment.
 • Demonstrated at full-scale the improvement of algal productivity by increasing loading
 of the system and heating.
 • Demonstrated at full-scale that this wastewater treatment and biofuel conversion
 technology is not only economically feasible, but lower in cost than conventional
 activated sludge wastewater treatment at a capacity of 1 MGD.
 • Wastewater treatment via the Algaewheel can reduced wastewater treatment costs by
 close to 11% and 22% compared to treatment via CAS or traditional raceway pond,
 respectively.
 • Considering this cost savings relative to CAS, integrating Algaewheel based
 wastewater treatment with HTL and nanoﬁltration can provide a negative MFSP of as
 low as –$9.45/gge compared to established PNNL estimates of +$2.78 to $3.11/gge.
 • All Algaewheel treatment scenarios resulted in a lower Global Warming Potential (kg of
 CO2 eq) compared to CAS, except for addition of heating due to the high electricity
 input required. Up to 53% reduction in GWP per MG of wastewater treated was
 estimated for the southern site condition with higher loading.
 • Bench-scale results showed salt stress induced cell cycle arrest and increased cell
 size in baseline algal cultures, with a shift from haploidy to polyploidy. Neutral lipid
 accumulation occurred in C. vulgaris by approximately 3x under salt stress.
 • Discovered that green algae species C. reinhardtii and C. vulgaris possess potential
 homologs of the ILP1 gene, which is responsible for endopolyploidy in plants.

Challenges and Lessons Learned
The greatest challenges in this project were:
 COVID delays and resulting NCE- more expensive
 Logistics- distance was an impediment to monitoring and managing system performance.
 Equipment failures resulted in lost data and time. Programming delays.
 Field scale/real world issues: weather, hurricane, seasonal variability (sunlight and temp),
 maintenance or break down of equipment, plant operations. Some resulted in weeks to
 months of lost time.
 Pilot HTL system breakdown caused delays.
                                           Page 150 of 175

It took longer to grow algae and collect data than originally planned. Seasonality was an
important effect. Needed to implement recovery periods between treatments.
IAA was not replicable as expected at southern site. Adsorbent treatment worked best
when system was not operating under normal conditions, resulting in difficult to interpret
data.
Treatment to induce endopolyploidy increased cell size and lipids, but arrested growth
resulting in lower overall biomass.

Path Forward:
 This research demonstrated a lower cost approach for wastewater treatment in smaller
systems in most US climates (N & S). Algaewheel utilizes less land area and has lower
greenhouse gas emissions than CAS, and typical wastewater treatment costs can cover
cultivation with cover and some post processing. The proposed treatments to the Algaewheel
system can increase productivity >50%, and the proposed conversion technology of
hydrothermal liquefaction and nanoﬁltration improved oil yield by > 30%. While treatments
greatly increased the productivity of biomass, raceway ponds still produce more biomass per
amount of wastewater.
 The path forward indicates a two-stage system integrating a ﬁxed ﬁlm wastewater treatment
such as Algaewheel with a raceway pond to combine the best of both systems for the most
cost-efficient wastewater treatment and highest biomass productivity. The Algaewheel with the
proposed improvements, provides cost efficient wastewater treatment and biofuels, with a
raceway pond downstream using recycled nutrients from the nanoﬁltration treated
hydrothermal liquefaction aqueous product.
