The Role of Precision Medicine in Neuroblastoma: Targeted Therapies and Personalized Approaches—A Narrative Review
Authors: Mossamat Jannatul Mawa, Mohammad Shahangir Biswas, Maksudul Haque, Munna Kumar Podder
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsNeuroblastoma is the most common extracranial solid tumor and contributes the most to pediatric oncology deaths worldwide. Its clinical manifestations are heterogeneous, including relapses, and the prognosis remains poor, especially for high‐risk patients. The principal interventions—surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy—exacerbate the prognosis due to high morbidity and poor long‐term remission rates. This study attempts to integrate available information on the genetic and molecular characteristics of neuroblastoma, focusing on how the potential of precision medicine is improving neuroblastoma diagnosis and treatment.MethodsThis review aims to compile recent literature on the genomic, transcriptomic and epigenetic studies of neuroblastoma and its treatment. The review included the most recent molecular markers for the relevant pathway, the therapeutic targets that arise from them and precision medicine approaches that incorporate AI, CRISPR, and neoantigen vaccines at various stages of clinical trials.ResultsTargeted medicines such MYCN amplification, ALK mutations, and chromosomal deletions including 1p36 and 11q have revealed important genetic drivers of neuroblastoma. Early‐phase studies have shown encouraging reductions in tumor progression with MYCN modulators, ALK inhibitors and immunotherapies (anti‐GD2 antibodies, B7‐H3 CAR‐T cells). Integrating omics‐based markers and AI‐directed therapy prediction more precisely classifies risk and customizes therapy.ConclusionPrecision medicine has transformed neuroblastoma treatment by improving diagnostic specificity and therapeutic effectiveness while reducing toxicity. Among emerging strategies, ALK‐targeted therapies and anti‐GD2 immunotherapies show strong clinical potential, although further validation is still required to improve survival and quality of life in affected children.Keywords: genetic mutation, patient‐derived organoid, personalized approaches, precision medicine, targeted therapy, tumor heterogeneity

Introduction

Neuroblastoma (NB) is among the most prominent extracranial solid tumor in children, it is also responsible for 8%–10% of pediatric cancers and around 15% deaths associated with pediatric cancers [1]. Primarily, neuroblastoma originates from neural crest cells and which also develop in the territories of the sympathetic nervous system and in the medulla of the adrenal cortex, it can manifest different kind of clinical symptoms from rapid remission to severe metastatic disease [2]. According to studies, global incidence is 11–13 per million children under 15 years, ranging from 65 per million in infants to 1 per million in adolescents with over 90% of cases diagnosed before 10 years of age [3]. Children under 5 years old are mainly affected by neuroblastoma with a slight male predominance [4]. The location and size of tumor usually affect the clinical symptoms of neuroblastoma, which may include abdominal mass, fever, weight loss, bone pain and periorbital ecchymosis in metastatic disease [5]. Orbital involvement is a well‐known manifestation of metastatic neuroblastoma, especially in high‐risk cases and may appear with proptosis and bilateral periorbital ecchymosis, commonly known as “raccoon eyes” due to metastatic spread to the soft tissues and orbital bones. These ocular symptoms may help with early clinical suspicion of metastatic neuroblastoma and are regarded as significant clinical signs of advanced disease. Recent studies in precision‐based immunotherapy have also improved the management of metastatic neuroblastoma affecting craniofacial and orbital regions. Compared with conventional approaches such as extensive radiation or surgical intervention, targeted therapy may help reduce metastatic burden while limiting treatment‐associated morbidity in sensitive anatomical regions such as the orbital and craniofacial structures [6, 7, 8, 9]. Recent risk‐adapted treatment approaches used for neuroblastoma are radiation therapy, chemotherapy, surgery and autologous stem cell transplantation in high‐risk conditions [10]. Even with multimodal therapy treatment, the prognosis for high‐risk neuroblastoma is still poor with an estimated number of long‐term survival rate less than 40% [11]. In standard treatments of neuroblastoma have some major challenges, tumor heterogeneity, relapse, treatment resistance and significant toxicities that harm the quality of life for survivors [12].

Precision medicine (PM) utilizes genomic, molecular and phenotypic data to tailor treatment according to individual patient characteristics [13]. Advances in pediatric oncology have accelerated the integration of PM into clinical trials and therapeutic decision‐making and enabled more personalized and effective treatment strategies [14, 15].

Neuroblastoma is particularly suitable for precision medicine because of its marked clinical and molecular heterogeneity. Key alterations such as MYCN amplification and ALK mutations serve as prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets and facilitate risk stratification and the development of targeted therapies [16, 17, 18, 19].

This review aims to present a thorough overview of the role of precision medicine in neuroblastoma by exploring the terrain of genetic alterations, emerging personalized treatment approaches and targeted therapies. In this review, the integration of molecular testing in healthcare and therapeutic impacts of biomarkers identification have also been discussed. The primary goal is to evaluate the current advancement and future directions of precision medicine in enhancing the prognosis and survival of neuroblastoma patients.

Genetic and Molecular Landscape of Neuroblastoma

Understanding the complex genomic alterations and molecular mechanisms of neuroblastoma are important to determine the effective targeted therapies [20]. Amplification of MYCN oncogene is one of the most significant prognostic markers in neuroblastoma, leads to the activation of transcription genes that associates in metastasis, angiogenesis, cellular proliferation and also suppresses gene expression, which develops differentiation and immune surveillance [21]. Anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) gene mutation, which occurs in both sporadic and familial neuroblastoma cases and serves as a potential targeted therapy [22]. Paired‐like homeobox 2B (PHOX2B) gene mutation are rare but acts as a reliable predictor of genetic predisposition in neuroblastoma [23]. Besides MYCN amplification, the pathogenesis of neuroblastoma is also significantly influenced by alterations in ALK and PHOX2B. Activating mutations in the ALK gene enhance tumor development via dysregulated receptor tyrosine kinase signaling that causes elevated cellular proliferation and survival. Somatic and germline ALK alterations are specifically related with familial and high‐risk neuroblastoma cases. PHOX2B, a transcription factor critical for autonomic nervous system development has also been implicated in neuroblastoma susceptibility and tumorigenesis. Studies show that PHOX2B alterations may disrupt developmental pathways and normal neuronal differentiation involved in sympathetic nervous system maturation that can lead to malignant transformation. Altered PHOX2B activity has been associated with interactions between neural crest cell development and ALK signaling that show its functional significance in the biology of neuroblastoma [24].

Genome‐scale sequencing method have shown the association between distinct gene expression and DNA abnormalities, which are used for better understanding of neuroblastoma heterogeneity to prioritizing effective therapeutic targets for pragmatic drug development [31]. High expression of genes that regulate neuronal growth is associated with effective outcomes, while undifferentiated sequence represents susceptibility and relapse [32]. Advanced method for cancer treatment and diagnosis have identified proteins involved in progression and resistance of neuroblastoma, elevated levels of survivin and Bcl‐2 proteins are associated with poor prognosis and therapeutic resistance and targeting these proteins contains potential for therapeutic approaches [33]. Epigenetic dysregulation, DNA methylation, greatly contributes to neuroblastoma pathogenesis. Hypermethylation of tumor suppressive genes have been observing to promote effective therapeutic approaches [34]. Histone deacetylase inhibitors, vorinostat (Suberanilohydroxamic Acid – SAHA) have shown their promising effectiveness to reverse epigenetic silencing [35].

Targeted Therapies in Neuroblastoma

Precision medicine has introduced targeted therapies as promising approach to enhance outcomes in high‐risk neuroblastoma by targeting on selective oncogenic drivers, cell surface antigen and dysregulated signaling pathways [36]. Table 2 lists the various targeted therapy approaches for neuroblastoma at different developmental stages. The presence of activating mutations in anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) gene is one of the well‐characterized genetic alterations in neuroblastoma. ALK is receptor tyrosine kinase involved in nervous system development. Activating ALK mutations promote neuroblastoma growth and make ALK an important drug target. ALk inhibitors are mainly used in patients with ALK‐mutated high‐risk or relapsed neuroblastoma, although treatment response can vary due to resistance and mutation type [45, 46, 47] These mutations, particularly F1174L and R1275Q, enhance tumor growth and survival by constitutively activating ALK signaling pathways [48]. Crizotinib, a first‐generation ALK inhibitor, first showed action in preclinical models and early‐phase trials but it also showed limited efficacy for certain ALK mutations due to its lower affinity [49]. Ceritinib, a next‐generation ALK inhibitor, it has shown better potency and preclinical efficacy in neuroblastoma models with ALK mutations [50]. Recently, a third‐generation ALK inhibitor, lorlatinib has shown positive results in early clinical studies and it also has superior penetration into the central nervous system and efficacy against resistant mutations, F1174L and G1202R [51]. Immunotherapy targeting disialoganglioside GD2 is a cell surface antigen, which is abundantly expressed on neuroblastoma cells but minimally available on normal cells and represents important advancement. An anti‐GD2 monoclonal antibody, Dinutuximab, was the first immunotherapy for high‐risk neuroblastoma, when mixed with granulocyte‐macrophage colony‐stimulating factor (GM‐CSF), isotretinoin and interleukin‐2 (IL‐2) then it shows increased event‐free and longevity [52]. GD2 expression on peripheral nerves causes severe neuropathic pain after taking the medication. To overcome limitations and increase effectiveness, next‐generation strategies, GD2‐specific bispecific antibodies and chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T‐cell therapies are under clinical development [53]. About 20%–25% of neuroblastoma cases contain MYCN amplification, this is also a robust marker of poor prognosis and resistance to therapies. The MYCN oncoprotein's inherently disordered structure and lack of a suitable binding pocket have made direct targeting difficult, that's why the indirect strategies have been occupied. Bromodomain and extraterminal (BET) inhibitors, such as JQ1, which suppress MYCN transcription by blocking BRD4 (a key regulator of MYCN expression) [54]. Another promising strategy includes targeting Aurora kinase A, which stabilizes the MYCN protein and inhibits its degradation. Inhibitors such as alisertib (MLN8237) block this interaction, leading to reduction of MYCN levels and tumor growth suppression in preclinical models and early‐phase trials [26, 55]. However, most MYCN‐targeting methods remain experimental and are currently supported mainly by preclinical studies and early‐phase clinical trials with limited long‐term clinical validation in neuroblastoma patients. Resistance mechanisms and treatment‐related toxicities also remain important challenges for successful clinical translation [54, 56]. Emerging immune checkpoint sites, B7‐H3 (CD276), it belongs to the same family as PD‐L1, are also being studied for neuroblastoma. Overexpression of B7‐H3 in neuroblastoma has been linked with poor outcomes and tumor immune evasion mechanism. Monoclonal antibodies, antibody‐drug conjugates (ADCs) and CAR T‐cell therapies targeting B7‐H3 have demonstrated promising antitumor activity in preclinical research and now under clinical trials. Enoblituzumab, which is an anti‐B7‐H3 monoclonal antibody, is currently undergoing evaluation in pediatric solid tumors with improved early results [8].

Types of Targeted Therapies in Different Developmental Stages of Neuroblastoma.

Cell cycle dysregulation is an important feature of cancer and cyclin‐dependent kinase (CDK) 4/6 inhibitors have shown effectiveness in MYCN‐amplified neuroblastoma. Agents block CDK4/6‐mediated phosphorylation of retinoblastoma (Rb) protein to enter G1 phase arrest and induce apoptosis in neuroblastoma cells [41]. Neuroblastomas with mutations that activate the RAS/MAPK pathway and are frequently observed in relapsed or refractory disease, may benefit from MEK inhibition. MEK inhibitors trametinib have shown tumor suppressive efficacy in neuroblastoma models with activated MAPK signaling, particularly those with KRAS, NRAS or BRAF mutations. Currently, clinical trials are ongoing to identify their effectiveness as monotherapy or in combination regimen [42]. MEK inhibitors such as trametinib are associated with characteristic ocular toxicities such as MEK inhibitor‐associated retinopathy (MEKAR), serous retinal detachment and transient visual disturbances, which are generally reversible but require regular ophthalmologic monitoring in pediatric patients [57].

However, neuroblastoma cells with defective DNA damage repair pathways or MYCN overexpression show vulnerability to poly (ADP‐ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors. Agents such as olaparib and talazoparib impair single‐strand break repair, causing genomic instability and cell death. Preclinical studies have shown increased effectiveness when combined with DNA‐damaging chemotherapeutics, indicating a rational combination approach in high‐risk NB [58].

Genome Profile and Risk Stratification

The advent of whole‐exome sequencing (WES) and next‐generation sequencing (NGS) has allowed for a detailed understanding of neuroblastoma genetics [59]. Several somatic and genetic mutations, alterations in the number of copies and gene fusion can be immediately detected by NGS and many of those have therapeutic and prognostic implications [71]. Various recurring alterations in neuroblastoma, ARID1A, ATRX, ALK and TP53 have been detected by using WES, which allows for risk assessment and tailored treatment plans [72, 73].

In neuroblastoma, ATRX mutations promote tumor progression and therapeutic resistance through alternative lengthening of telomeres (ALT) [74, 75] and altered DNA damage repair pathways [75]. TP53 mutations are less frequent at diagnosis (~2%–5%), but it's become increasingly prevalent in relapsed disease and are also linked to therapy resistance and poor prognosis [76, 77]. Subunits of the SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complex, ARID1B and ARID1A are essential for maintaining gene transcription and DNA accessibility. Alterations or deletions in these genes disrupt chromatin remodeling and are associated with aggressive behavior [77]. Preclinical studies have shown that neuroblastoma cells with ARID1B/A loss are especially vulnerable to inhibitors that target the histone methyltransferase EZH2, which supports the use of synthetic lethality‐based treatment approaches [78]. In clinical settings, treatment is increasingly directed by risk stratification that is driven by molecular factors. The Children's Oncology Group (COG) study ANBL1531 implements a response and biology‐based grouping strategy that incorporates genomic modification, including MYCN amplification and segmental chromosomal abnormalities with traditional clinical factors [79]. Risk stratification is also improved by DNA methylation profiling and this can also delineate epigenetic subgroups with different kinds of prognoses [64].

Diagnostic and Prognostic Biomarkers

To detect treatment response, detect minimal residual disease (MRD) and early relapse, ctDNA evolution allows a minimally invasive method such as ultra‐deep NGS. Besides ultra‐deep NGS, the droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) method also provides high specificity for mutation detection [80]. ctDNA profiling has shown tumor heterogeneity and tumor clonal assessment in real‐time. Instead of imaging and bone marrow biopsy, serial monitoring has shown earlier detection of relapse. MicroRNAs (miRNAs) can regulate gene expression post‐transcriptionally and also are dysregulated in many malignancies, including neuroblastoma. Particular circulating and extracellular vesicle‐associated miRNAs, such as miR‐375, miR‐21 and miR‐204, derived from tumor cells and the tumor microenvironment, have been associtated with aggressive disease and poor outcomes in neuroblastoma [81, 82]. miR‐375 has been associated with increased cell proliferation and metastasis in neuroblastoma. miR‐375 could serve as a non‐invasive biomarker and also predictive for poor prognosis [82]. Additional biomarkers, such as serum neuron‐specific enolase (NSE), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and ferritin are routinely accessible in current clinical practice and elevated levels are generally associated with higher tumor burden, aggressive disease and poorer prognosis in neuroblastoma patients [83]. Several molecular and circulating indicators have potential for the early detection of neuroblastoma patients in addition to their functions in risk stratification and treatment monitoring [84]. Emerging liquid biopsy techniques such as exosomal biomarkers, microRNAs and circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) have shown potential as less invasive methods for neuroblastoma detection and long‐term monitoring [85]. In pediatric neuroblastoma patients, ctDNA is detectable both at diagnosis and during post‐treatment monitoring, where levels often decrease after effective therapy but enhance during relapse. ctDNA profiling also closely reflects the genomic landscape and clonal evolution of primary tumors, which supports its value as a reliable real‐time biomarker [86]. Similarly, the use of exosome‐based liquid biopsy methods in biomarker‐driven diagnosis and early cancer screening is becoming more widely recognized [87]. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)‐derived ctDNA has also been shown to reliably reflect tumor genomic alterations in central nervous system associated disease and is increasingly used for compartment‐specific monitoring in neuro‐oncology [85]. Despite the fact that these methods are not yet proven for routine clinical screening, studies found that prompt monitoring and intervention could lead to an earlier diagnosis and better prognosis [88].

Liquid Biopsies and Functional Drug Testing

Liquid biopsy approaches such as exosomes and circulating tumor cells (CTCs) provide real‐time monitoring of disease progression and treatment response [62]. In neuroblastoma, patient‐derived organoids have been shown to preserve tumor heterogeneity and are useful for functional drug screening and assessment of therapy response and resistance patterns [60, 65].

Challenges and Limitations

One of the major obstacles in neuroblastoma precision medicine is intra‐ and inter‐tumoral heterogeneity. Neuroblastoma displays significant variation not only between patients but also within the same tumor, which can contribute to unpredictable reactions to targeted therapies. Heterogeneous epigenetic and genetic landscapes can lead to subclonal individuals with differential sensitivity to treatment and make therapeutic approaches more complicated [89]. Even within a single tumor, different clonal individuals can emerge, particularly when pressure is applied and which could result in disease relapse [90]. Another major drawback is the development of resistance to targeted treatments. Although precision medicine aims to focus on certain oncogenic drivers, tumors often adapt by reactivating pathways, compensating signaling mechanisms or secondary mutations [91]. A critical obstacle to the application of precision medicine for neuroblastoma is the lack of clinical trials on pediatric‐specific. Most of the targeted therapies are primarily developed and tested in adult populations, where pediatric patients are frequently excluded from early‐phase trials [92]. Precision medicine in pediatrics, particularly when it about genomic testing then it raises complicated ethical and practical concerns. Important factors to take into consideration, guardians' informed consent, handling incidental findings and the psychological effects of germline alteration outcomes on families [93]. Equitable access to precision medicine is impeded by the high expense of genome sequencing and related evaluation. Whole‐genome, whole‐exome or transcriptome sequencing are costly, particularly in low and middle‐income countries with inadequate healthcare systems [94]. Another challenge is that certain juvenile neuroblastoma patients may lack actionable alterations despite genomic testing. Pediatric malignancies such as neuroblastoma may be driven by intricate gene regulatory patterns or epigenetic mutations that are complicated for current therapies to target, in addition to adult tumors, which often harbor mutations in well‐characterized oncogenes [95]. Interpreting variants of unknown significance (VUS) and incorporating multi‐omic data into clinical choices remain major hurdles. Clinical decision systems and bioinformatics tools for pediatric oncology are still evolving and need to be standardized to ensure reproducibility and reliability [96]. While MYCN amplification and ALK mutations are important oncogenic drivers, disease progression often involves multiple dysregulated pathways such as RAS/MAPK and PI3K/AKT signaling, which complicates precision therapy development. Despite these limitations, precision therapy remains highly promising for neuroblastoma, especially for patients with high‐risk, relapsed or treatment‐resistent disease. Nearly 50% of high‐risk neuroblastoma patients still experience poor outcomes with conventional therapies, highlighting the urgent need for more effective and personalized treatment strategies. Advances in targeted therapies and immunotherapy continue to improve survival and provide new therapeutic options for patients who previously had very limited treatment possibilities [97, 98].

Future Directions and Emerging Technologies

The combination of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) in oncology is a valuable tool for predicting treatment outcomes and aiding in drugs selection. These technologies can analyze clinical, molecular and imaging datasets to predict tumor behavior, therapeutic response and treatment selection [97]. Deep learning models can classify patients according to risk and predict therapeutic responsiveness [98]. Functional genomics has been revolutionized by the advent of CRISPR‐Cas9 and related gene editing techniques, which also hold prospects for precision‐targeted therapies in neuroblastoma. CRISPR allows precise gene knockout, gene correction and gene activation to help detect key drug resistance mechanisms and oncogenic drivers [99]. Functional genome‐wide CRISPR methods have already detected MYCN‐amplified neuroblastoma defects and provide novel therapeutic targets [100]. Additionally, CAR‐T cell engineering is being evaluated for immunotherapy against neuroblastoma. These genetically tailored T cells can be altered to generate chimeric antigen receptors (CARs) specific to neuroblastoma‐associated antigens [101]. Early clinical studies have evaluated GD2‐targeted CAR‐T cells in relapsed neuroblastoma. In a phase I trial, 3 of 11 patients achieved complete remission, including one durable remission lasting over 8 years and showed long‐term feasibility of CAR‐T therapy in neuroblastoma [102]. Multi‐omics integration can detect therapeutic vulnerabilities, biomarkers and resistance mechanisms beyond those detectable by single‐omics techniques [103]. Digital twins (DTs) are being developed to mimic tumor evolution and treatment outcomes based on multi‐omics data. These models can assist clinicians to predict disease progression, improve combination therapies and assess adverse effects before commencing treatment [104]. Although personalized neoantigen vaccines remain largely unexplored in neuroblastoma because of its low tumor mutational burden, several NB‐specific preclinical vaccine approaches have shown promise. An IL‐21‐secreting whole‐cell neuroblastoma vaccine showed therapeutic effectiveness in murine metastatic NB models and increased survival and achieved cure rates of up to 33%, partly through survivin‐specific CD8+ T‐cell responses. Ongoing preclinical studies are detecting novel NB‐specific immunogenic targets that may support future personalized vaccine development [105, 106]. Due to its immunologically “cold” tumor microenvironment, neuroblastoma usually shows low tumor mutational burden, limited neoantigen expression, decreased MHC class I expression and poor T‐cell infiltration that collectively decrease responsiveness to immune checkpoint inhibitors targeting PD‐1 and CTLA‐4. Future studies should prioritize combination strategies such as checkpoint blockade with GD2‐targeted therapies, kinase inhibitors or CAR‐T/NK‐cell approaches to improve immune infiltration and overcome therapeutic resistance. However, emerging agents that inhibit new immune checkpoints (such as B7‐H3) are under research to make tumors more vulnerable to immune attack [8, 107]. Cytokine‐engineered CAR‐T cells, which are designed to secrete immunostimulatory cytokines to develop persistence and tumor infiltration [106]. Additionally, oncolytic viruses, which are genetically modified that specifically infect and lyse tumor cells while promoting systemic anti‐tumor immunity [107].

Conclusion

Precision medicine (PM) has ushered in a new era in the treatment and understanding of neuroblastoma, which offers promise against one of the most diverse and aggressive pediatric malignancies. By going beyond the traditional “one‐size‐fits‐all” strategy, PM makes it possible to develop treatments for individual genomic, transcriptomic and molecular profiles of each patient. Over the past decade, the detection of critical oncogenic drivers MYCN amplification, ALK alterations and epigenetic changes have revolutionized diagnostic accuracy and therapeutic approaches to create less toxic and more tailored therapy routes. Despite these improvements, the clinical use of Pm in neuroblastoma still faces several hurdles and chief among them are tumor heterogeneity, treatment resistance, lack of actionable targets and limited reliable pediatric‐specific clinical trials. However, the combination therapy holds tremendous potential for addressing the dynamic landscape of tumor assessment. Such combination frameworks can reveal latent therapeutic defects, novel biomarkers and allow for real‐time treatment approaches in response to disease progression. Moreover, developing technologies CRISPR‐based gene editing, AI‐powered treatment prediction algorithms, digital twins and personalized neoantigen vaccines are changing the neuroblastoma therapeutic landscape. These methods allow not only for increased precision in target detection but also for stimulation‐based prediction of treatment outcomes, which can guide treatment choices prior to clinical implementation. However, overcoming logistical, ethical and regulatory barriers is important for their successful use in clinical settings, especially in youngsters. Collaborative interdisciplinary efforts that bridge basic science, clinical oncology, data science and bioethics are vital to move forward. International collaboration among pediatric oncology centers, precision medicine consortia and biobanks can expedite the validation of novel biomarkers, simplify data‐sharing infrastructures and standardize PM procedures globally. Additionally, democratizing the advantages of precision medicine across high‐income and resource‐limited settings requires ensuring equitable access to next‐generation and high‐throughput diagnostics.
