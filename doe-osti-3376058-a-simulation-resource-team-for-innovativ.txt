Public Executive Summary

This project created a shared simulation resource for innovative fusion concepts at the University
of Rochester, built around the FLASH, TriForce, OSIRIS, and OSHUN codes. Many promising
fusion approaches are too complex to evaluate by experiment alone yet building a custom
simulation capability from scratch for each new concept is expensive and slow. The BETHE
Simulation Resource Team addressed this problem by developing and validating a flexible set of
numerical tools, which were then applied across three major concept areas: plasma-jet-driven
magneto-inertial fusion, staged Z pinches, and field-reversed configurations. The team assisted
three concept teams and carried out independent analyses of all three concepts.

The research by the team added to fusion energy understanding and knowhow in several important
ways. First, the project improved the underlying physics models used in fusion simulations. The
team produced a set of transport coefficients, derived from the OSHUN Fokker–Planck code,
which were subsequently published and incorporated into FLASH. The new implementation was
tested using magnetic-diffusion benchmarks as well as in integrated problems involving plasma-
liner experiments and staged-Z-pinch concepts. TriForce also gained multiple transport models,
allowing more realistic comparisons across fusion conditions. These advances matter because
transport models control how heat, current, and magnetic fields move through plasmas, which
strongly affects predicted fusion performance.

Second, the project strengthened confidence in the technical effectiveness of the simulation
methods themselves. For the staged Z-pinch effort, FLASH was extended with implicit anisotropic
resistivity and a circuit model, and the new circuit capability was verified by reproducing the same
load current as MACH2 in several benchmarks. For plasma-liner studies, the team explicitly used
particle-in-cell calculations to check whether a simpler fluid model was valid, because effects such
as jet interpenetration and micro-instabilities can make fluid-only predictions unreliable. The
FLASH code was then used to model all stages of a plasma-jet-driven implosion, informed by the
kinetic simulation results. In other words, the project did not just run simulations; it worked to
show when the simulations should be trusted.

The concept studies also produced useful findings about fusion-system feasibility. In the staged Z-
pinch area, the team published one- and two-dimensional FLASH–MACH2 studies and carried
out an independent TriForce assessment. That assessment concluded that the original staged-Z-
pinch design was too optimistic and, as originally conceived, would not work as a practical fusion-
energy source. It also revealed limitations in prior MACH2 calculations which resulted in the
experimental team adopting FLASH as a tool for their R&D. At the same time, the work identified
a more promising updated nested gas-puff design, based on later work by LLNL’s Dr. D.
Higginson and co-workers, and TriForce successfully reproduced the best reported case,
supporting the conclusion that the revised concept has real merit in one-dimensional studies. This
is a valuable outcome: it shows that the project helped rule out weaker assumptions while
identifying more credible directions for future research.

For field-reversed configurations, the project improved the TriForce kinetic code with collision
models, charge- and energy-conserving algorithms, better geometry handling, and other tools
needed for long-timescale simulations. The team verified a Monte Carlo binary-collision model
and included simulations of startup and the formation of a field-reversed magnetic profile. The
TriForce code was updated to version 2.0 and became more capable, performant, and flexible, with
continued application to PFRC-2 and PFRC-3 heating and sustainment studies. These
developments deepen understanding of whether compact magnetic-fusion systems can be heated
and sustained in the way their designers propose.

The project’s methods were economically sensible. Rather than requiring each fusion concept team
to develop its own software stack, validation tools, and expert staff, the award supported a shared,
reusable modeling capability. The team’s work overcame a major “entry barrier” in a cost-effective
way by providing a multi-purpose, multi-physics simulation resource that can be reused across
concepts. That strategy reduced duplication of effort, accelerated technical learning, and made it
possible to compare different fusion ideas using a common framework. The project’s value is
further reflected in follow-on activity: its work led directly to multiple government-funded awards,
additional proposals to government funding agencies, and discussions of longer-term research
agreements with industry.

The broader public benefit of this project is that it reduced technical uncertainty in fusion-energy
research while building lasting national capability. It produced validated tools, trained students
and researchers, supported collaborations among universities, national laboratories, and private
companies, and helped move advanced modeling methods into wider use through efforts such as
SimNet. For the public, that means better use of research dollars: weak ideas can be identified
earlier, promising ideas can be improved faster, and future experiments can be designed with better
scientific guidance. In short, this project did not simply simulate fusion plasmas; it created a
durable simulation infrastructure that makes fusion research more rigorous, more efficient, and
more likely to yield technologies of long-term public value.

 The project’s computer-modeling effort relied on a coordinated suite of complementary plasma
 simulation tools: FLASH, a radiation-magnetohydrodynamics code for fluid-scale modeling;
 TriForce, a particle-in-cell platform for problems requiring both collective plasma behavior and
 particle effects; OSIRIS, a fully kinetic particle-in-cell code used where collisionless and weakly
 collisional effects are important; and OSHUN, a Fokker–Planck code used to calculate transport
 coefficients for magnetized plasma modeling. Together, these tools were used to study plasma-jet-
 driven magneto-inertial fusion (PJMIF), staged Z-pinches (SZP), and field-reversed configurations
 (FRC), with the choice of model matched to the dominant physics and spatial/temporal scales of
 each problem.

 Key assumptions depended on the application. For PJMIF and PLX target-compression studies,
 FLASH treated the plasma as a single conducting fluid with radiation and magnetic-field transport,
 which is appropriate only if kinetic effects such as jet interpenetration, species separation, and
 micro-instabilities do not invalidate the fluid approximation. For that reason, OSIRIS particle-in-
 cell calculations were used alongside FLASH to test whether the fluid description remained
 credible in the relevant regimes. For SZP studies, simplified one-dimensional models were first
 used to isolate magnetic diffusion, circuit coupling, and geometry effects before progressively
 adding radiation and realistic equation-of-state physics. For transport modeling, OSHUN-derived

The models were validated in several ways. The OSHUN transport coefficients were benchmarked
through magnetic-diffusion test problems and then exercised in PLX and SZP simulations after
incorporation into FLASH. The new FLASH circuit model was checked against the McBride
formulation and against MACH2 calculations, reproducing the same load-current behavior in test
problems. In PJMIF target-formation studies, two-dimensional OSIRIS simulations and FLASH
simulations produced similar Hall parameters and plasma beta, supporting use of the fluid model
in that regime; OSIRIS also identified the modified two-stream instability as the main mechanism
limiting jet interpenetration. In SZP, FLASH was systematically compared with MACH2 on
simplified and then more complete implosion problems, achieving very good agreement in
magnetic-field profiles and stagnation timing. In FRC studies, TriForce was benchmarked against
previously published PFRC particle-in-cell results, and its Monte Carlo binary-collision model
was independently verified.

The modeling methods and many of the results were presented in peer-reviewed publications.
Published examples include the OSHUN transport-coefficient paper by Davies et al. in Physics of
Plasmas (2021); the staged-Z-pinch validation and performance study, “Feasibility and
Performance of the Staged Z-Pinch: A One-dimensional Study with FLASH and MACH2,” in
Physics of Plasmas (2024); the TriForce collision-model verification paper in Physics of Plasmas
(2024), which also included FRC startup and field-reversed-profile simulations; and the transport-
sensitivity study for magnetized liner inertial fusion in Physics of Plasmas (2024). A second SZP
paper was published in Physical Review E in 2024. Some late-stage PLX and TriForce studies are
under preparation for publication, but the project as a whole generated a substantial peer-reviewed
modeling record.

Project Activities
This project focused on building a shared, multi-physics simulation resource for innovative fusion
concepts so that promising ideas could be tested, improved, and compared more rigorously than
any one concept team could typically do on its own. The major approach combined targeted code
development with coordinated application to three concept areas (plasma-jet-driven magneto-
inertial fusion, staged Z pinches, and field-reversed configurations) using the complementary
capabilities of FLASH, TriForce, OSIRIS, and OSHUN to span fluid and kinetic plasma modeling.
The central hypothesis was that a common suite of validated simulation tools could lower the entry
barrier for emerging fusion concepts, provide more reliable guidance for experiment and design,
and reveal when simplified models remain adequate and when kinetic, transport, or geometric
effects materially change the outcome. Over the course of the project, the team developed and
improved key modeling capabilities, including transport-coefficient integration, implicit
anisotropic resistivity, circuit and boundary-handling methods, and upgraded TriForce algorithms;
it also carried out extensive benchmarking and code-to-code comparisons to build confidence in
the models. At a high level, the project delivered sustained simulation support to external concept
teams, produced independent assessments that sharpened understanding of concept feasibility and
generated follow-on publications and additional government-funded work that extended the
impact of the BETHE effort beyond the original award period.
