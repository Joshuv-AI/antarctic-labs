CD36 is a receptor for human sapovirus
Authors: Kei Haga, Miyabi Arai, Reiko Takai-Todaka, Yuya Fukuda, Ryoka Ishiyama, Kazuhiko Katayama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Human sapovirus (HuSaV) is a major cause of gastroenteritis but has long lacked a defined receptor. Here, we identify the scavenger receptor CD36 as essential for infection. Comparative transcriptomics identified CD36 as the key susceptibility factor. CRISPR knockout abolished replication, while ectopic expression restored permissiveness across diverse cells. Binding assays showed nanomolar affinity between CD36 and HuSaV particles. Infection also required the bile acid glycocholic acid, which promoted CD36 internalization and viral entry. In human intestinal organoids, CD36 knockout abrogated infection, confirming physiological relevance. Our findings establish CD36 as the long-sought HuSaV receptor, uncover a bile acid–assisted entry mechanism that confines infection to the intestine and point to opportunities for antiviral and vaccine development.

INTRODUCTION

Acute viral gastroenteritis is a major public health problem worldwide, causing substantial morbidity in children and adults alike. Among the etiological agents, human noroviruses (HuNoVs) are the leading cause of outbreaks, while human sapoviruses (HuSaVs) are increasingly recognized as the second most prevalent cause, accounting for roughly one-third of nonbacterial gastroenteritis cases (1). Taxonomically, within the family Caliciviridae, the genus Norovirus includes the species Norovirus Norwalkense, which currently comprises 10 recognized genogroups (2, 3). Among these, GI, GII, GIV, GVIII, and GIX contain viruses that infect humans and are collectively referred to as HuNoVs. Similarly, the genus Sapovirus includes the species Sapovirus Sapporoense, which currently comprises 19 recognized genogroups (4, 5). Of these, GI, GII, GIV, and GV include viruses that infect humans and are designated as HuSaVs. Despite their epidemiological significance, research into HuSaV pathogenesis and immunity has lagged far behind that of HuNoVs.

Progress in HuSaV research has long been hampered by the lack of a robust cell culture system. In 2020, the successful cultivation of HuSaV using HuTu80 cells was reported (6). However, this system relies on HuTu80 cells that were adapted through extensive serial passage (7). Our group subsequently developed a more robust HuSaV cultivation system based on the colorectal adenocarcinoma–derived cell line, Caco-2, which we designated Caco-2MC (8). This cell line efficiently supports HuSaV replication, in contrast to most commonly used human cell lines that remain nonpermissive. Furthermore, bile acids, including glycocholic acid (GCA), have been reported as essential cofactors that facilitate HuSaV replication in both systems. However, bile acids alone are insufficient to establish infection in many cell types, implying the requirement for additional host determinants. Thus, the precise identity and function of host factors—particularly the cellular receptor(s) mediating viral entry—remain unclear. Defining the cellular receptor is crucial not only for understanding the initial steps of HuSaV infection but also for advancing our understanding of viral pathogenesis and developing antiviral strategies.

For enteric viruses, receptor identification has been a cornerstone in understanding host specificity and pathogenesis. NoVs were long thought to rely exclusively on histo-blood group antigens as attachment factors (9). However, studies on murine norovirus (MNV) fundamentally changed this view: The immunoglobulin superfamily member CD300lf was identified as an essential receptor, a finding independently supported by our group’s demonstration of CD300lf as the functional MNV receptor (10, 11). In addition, feline calicivirus, another member of the Caliciviridae, was shown to use junctional adhesion molecule A as its functional receptor (12). Similarly, we recently identified the neonatal Fc receptor as a receptor for classical human astrovirus (13), which was later confirmed by others (14, 15). Together, these studies establish that caliciviruses can engage proteinaceous receptors in addition to glycans, emphasizing the importance of defining the host molecules that mediate susceptibility. Yet, the identity of a functional receptor for HuSaV has remained unknown.

Here, we report the discovery of CD36, a multifunctional scavenger receptor, as an essential entry factor for HuSaV. We demonstrate that CD36 is both necessary and sufficient for HuSaV infection across diverse cell types and that bile acids facilitate CD36-mediated viral internalization. This work establishes CD36 as the long-sought receptor for HuSaV and provides a mechanistic framework for understanding sapovirus tropism while also identifying a potential target for antiviral strategies.

RESULTS

CD36 is required for HuSaV infection

To identify host determinants underlying cellular susceptibility to HuSaV, we leveraged our previously established cell culture system comprising two highly permissive cell lines (Caco-2MC and ME) and two low-permissive cell lines (PG and PJ) (8). Caco-2MC and ME are single cell–derived subclones of parental cells that exhibit enhanced HuSaV replication capacity. In contrast, PG and PJ cells are subclones derived from the same parental background but display limited viral replication efficiency. Transcriptome-wide comparison of these cell lines revealed notable differences (Fig. 1A and dataset S1). Among membrane-associated genes, CD36 emerged as the most prominent candidate, showing a 147-fold higher expression in susceptible cells compared to nonsusceptible cells (P value < 1.23 × 10−8). CD36 encodes a multifunctional class B scavenger receptor involved in fatty acid uptake, lipid metabolism, and immune regulation (16, 17), raising the possibility that it may serve as a viral entry factor.

We next examined CD36 protein levels by both immunofluorescence microscopy and flow cytometry. In line with the transcriptomic data, robust surface expression of CD36 was detected in Caco-2MC cells, whereas little or no staining was observed in Caco-2PG cells (fig. S1A). Consistently, flow cytometry analysis revealed a markedly higher population of CD36-positive cells in Caco-2MC compared to Caco-2PG (fig. S1B). This pronounced difference prompted us to test the functional requirement of CD36. Using CRISPR-Cas9–mediated gene editing, we generated CD36-knockout (KO) Caco-2MC cells and confirmed loss of expression by genomic sequencing, Western blotting (fig. S1, C to E), immunofluorescence staining, and flow cytometry (Fig. 1, B and C). Upon infection with HuSaV GI.1, viral replication was almost completely abolished in CD36-KO cells relative to parental Caco-2MC cells, whereas susceptibility was restored by reexpression of CD36 (Fig. 1, D and E). A low-level increase in viral RNA was occasionally observed in CD36-KO cells over the course of infection; however, the signal remained near background levels and was substantially lower than that in parental MC or CD36-complemented cells. This signal likely reflects residual or nonproductive viral RNA as efficient replication was not supported in the absence of CD36. The level of infection in CD36-complemented cells did not fully reach that of parental MC cells, indicating partial rather than complete rescue. This incomplete restoration is consistent with flow cytometry analysis showing a lower expression and proportion of CD36-positive cells in complemented cells (∼36%) compared to parental MC cells (∼57%) (fig. S1E and Fig. 1C), suggesting that infection efficiency correlates with CD36 expression levels. Flow cytometric analysis further confirmed VP1 antigen expression exclusively in CD36-positive cells but not in CD36-deficient cells (Fig. 1F). These findings establish a direct causal role of CD36 in HuSaV replication.

Given that Caco-2MC cells are susceptible to HuSaV genogroups GI, GII, and GV, but not GIV (8), we next assessed whether CD36 was broadly required across these genogroups. In CD36-KO cells, replication of GI, GII, and GV HuSaV was completely abolished, in sharp contrast to parental Caco-2MC cells. Reintroduction of CD36 restored susceptibility to all three genogroups (Fig. 1G). In contrast, GIV did not show any detectable increase in viral RNA in any of the tested conditions, including parental Caco-2MC, CD36-KO, and CD36-complemented cells (fig. S2). Thus, CD36 is indispensable for HuSaV infection across multiple genogroups that replicate in Caco-2MC cells.

CD36 permits HuSaV infection in nonpermissive cells

To directly test whether CD36 can confer susceptibility to HuSaV, we generated CD36 knock-in (KI) lines from 293T, American Type Culture Collection (ATCC)–derived HuTu80, and HCT116 cells, which normally display little or no replication (8). Stable expression of CD36 was confirmed on the cell surface (fig. S3, A and B). Following HuSaV infection, viral RNA levels increased significantly relative to parental controls (Fig. 2A), and VP1 protein expression was readily detected in CD36-positive cells (Fig. 2B and table S1).

Because HuSaV particles are known to remain largely intracellular (8), we compared viral RNA distribution between intracellular and extracellular fractions. In CD36-expressing 293T and HCT 116 cells, RNA was predominantly cell associated (fig. S4). Infectivity assays demonstrated that lysates from these cells transmitted infection to Caco-2MC and HCT 116/CD36 cells but not to CD36-KO Caco-2MC cells or parental HCT 116 cells (fig. S5), confirming that productive infection had occurred.

We next tested cross-species applicability by introducing human CD36 into hamster [Chinese hamster ovary–K1 (CHO-K1)], monkey (Vero E6), and mouse (NIH/3T3) cells. CD36 was expressed on all lines (fig. S6, A and B). Productive replication was evident in CHO-K1/CD36 and Vero E6/CD36 cells, as shown by RNA amplification and VP1 protein detection (Fig. 2, C and D, and table S2). In Vero E6/CD36 cells, surface CD36 expression was heterogeneous, and only a subset of cells showed detectable CD36 expression under our experimental conditions. Nevertheless, VP1 expression was predominantly observed in CD36-positive cells. In NIH/3T3/CD36 cells, viral RNA did not increase substantially, but a small fraction of VP1-positive cells (approximately 6%) was detected by immunostaining (Fig. 2D and fig. S7), suggesting that CD36 is necessary but not always sufficient, with additional host factors required for full replication.

Despite heterogeneous CD36 expression in the Vero E6/CD36 population, Vero/CD36 cells supported robust replication (Fig. 2C) and exhibited infection-induced detachment, characteristic of a cytopathic effect (CPE). Clonal selection yielded highly susceptible populations, among which clone VeroC16 showed strong CD36 expression and pronounced cytotoxicity upon infection (figs. S6A and S8, A and B). Viral titration demonstrated that median tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) values obtained using VeroC16 cells (Fig. 2E) were comparable to those determined with Caco-2MC cells (Fig. 1E). Moreover, VeroC16 supported serial passaging of diverse HuSaV genotypes (Fig. 2F) and provided a convenient platform for TCID50 determination. However, similar to Caco-2MC cells, GIV HuSaV did not show detectable replication in VeroC16 cells under the conditions tested (fig. S9), suggesting that this genotype may use a distinct entry mechanism independent of CD36.

Last, we quantified infectious progeny production by TCID50 assay. CD36-expressing 293T, HuTu80, HCT 116, CHO, and Vero cells produced >106 TCID50/ml of HuSaV, in sharp contrast to parental lines, which yielded <103 TCID50/ml (Fig. 2G). Together, these results establish CD36 as an essential determinant of HuSaV infection and highlight the practical utility of the VeroC16 line as a complementary assay system.

CD36 is essential for HuSaV susceptibility in human intestinal organoids

Because HuSaV primarily targets the human intestine, and both our group and others have shown limited replication in human intestinal organoids and induced pluripotent stem cell–derived intestinal cells (18, 19), we sought to determine whether CD36 mediates susceptibility in these physiologically relevant models. We used ileum-1 cells derived from human ileal tissue, which exhibited lower CD36 expression than Caco-2MC cells and showed little detectable signal by immunofluorescence, whereas CD36 expression became clearly detectable in ileum-1 cells following CD36 expression (fig. S10, A and B). CRISPR-Cas9–mediated KO of CD36 was confirmed by genomic sequencing (fig. S10C), and the low but detectable CD36 expression in parental ileum-1 cells was completely abolished in KO clones (fig. S10A).

When challenged with HuSaV GI.1, parental ileum-1 cells supported robust replication, with viral RNA increasing from ∼103 at 0 days postinfection to >105 at 3 days postinfection. In contrast, CD36-deficient ileum-1 cells showed no detectable RNA increase (Fig. 3A), and no progeny virus production was observed (Fig. 3B). Notably, CD36 KO had no effect on replication of HuNoV GII.4 (Fig. 3C), indicating that CD36 is specifically required for HuSaV but not HuNoV infection.

Although the Human Protein Atlas reports relatively high CD36 expression in small intestinal tissue, ileum-1 cells displayed lower protein levels. To reconcile this discrepancy, we analyzed human ileal tissue sections and confirmed CD36 expression in epithelial cells (Fig. 3D and fig. S11). To directly test whether expression levels influence susceptibility, we ectopically expressed CD36 in ileum-1 cells. This markedly elevated CD36 protein expression (fig. S10A) and strongly enhanced HuSaV replication (Fig. 3, E and F). Likewise, reintroduction of CD36 into ileum-1/CD36 KO cells restored high CD36 expression and increased susceptibility relative to parental ileum-1 cells (fig. S10A and Fig. 3, E and F). Together, these findings demonstrate that CD36 is a critical determinant of HuSaV infection in human intestinal cells and further highlight the importance of understanding the mechanisms regulating CD36 expression in vivo.

CD36 directly binds HuSaV particles

Given that CD36 is a multifunctional surface receptor, we examined whether it directly binds HuSaV particles. Using biolayer interferometry (BLI), recombinant human CD36 (r-hCD36) was immobilized on the sensor surface and exposed to virus-like particles (VLPs) derived from the HuSaV I22-124 strain (GII.3). Robust binding was detected, with kinetic analysis revealing high-affinity interactions (Kd = 7.78 × 10−9 M) (Fig. 4A). In contrast, HuNoV VLPs failed to show detectable binding (Fig. 4B), indicating that CD36 specifically recognizes HuSaV.

Consistent with these findings, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) independently confirmed direct binding between immobilized HuSaV and soluble r-hCD36-Fc in a concentration-dependent manner, whereas no binding was observed with recombinant Fc (r-Fc) (Fig. 4C). These results validate the specificity of the CD36-HuSaV interaction across distinct experimental platforms.

To test the functional consequences of this interaction, we assessed whether soluble CD36 could block infection. HuSaV was preincubated with increasing concentrations of Fc-tagged extracellular domain of CD36 before inoculation. A clear dose-dependent inhibition of infection was observed, as shown by both quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) quantification of viral RNA and detection of VP1 protein in target cells (Fig. 4, D and E). By contrast, treatment with r-Fc alone had no effect. These data demonstrate that soluble CD36 competitively interferes with virus entry, underscoring the biological relevance of the CD36-HuSaV interaction.

We further evaluated whether blocking cell-surface CD36 with antibodies could affect infection. Preincubation of target cells with anti-CD36 polyclonal antibody markedly reduced HuSaV replication, whereas an isotype-matched control antibody did not (Fig. 4F). These findings indicate that cell-surface CD36 is essential for HuSaV entry and provide strong evidence that CD36 functions as a bona fide receptor for the virus.

Bile acids facilitate CD36-mediated viral entry

Although CD36 directly binds HuSaV and functions as a receptor, productive infection remains strictly dependent on GCA, prompting investigation into its mechanistic role. To determine whether GCA acts on the virus or on host cells, we compared infection under various conditions (Fig. 5, A and B). In the presence of GCA throughout infection, robust replication was observed, whereas infection was abolished in its absence (Fig. 5B, conditions 3 versus 6). Pretreatment of HuSaV with GCA before inoculation reduced infectivity to background levels comparable to those observed in CD36 KO cells (Fig. 5B, condition 1), whereas addition of GCA postinfection markedly enhanced replication (Fig. 5B, condition 2). Notably, infection proceeded efficiently when GCA was added at 1 hour postinfection or present only during the first hour of inoculation (Fig. 5B, conditions 4 and 5). These results indicate that HuSaV attachment to cells occurs independently of GCA, but GCA promotes a postattachment step, consistent with a role in viral internalization. We next assessed cell-associated viral RNA at 1 hour postinfection. CD36-expressing cell lines displayed substantially higher levels of bound/associated virus in the presence of GCA compared to its absence (Fig. 5C).

Because CD36 is a known receptor for long-chain fatty acids (LCFAs) (20, 21), we tested whether GCA could directly interact with CD36. Oleic acid, a canonical CD36 ligand, induced only a modest increase in viral RNA levels (<10-fold), comparable to basal replication observed in the absence of GCA (fig. S12). In contrast, GCA treatment resulted in an approximately 100-fold enhancement of HuSaV replication.

BLI analysis revealed weak but measurable binding between GCA and CD36 (Kd = 9.77 × 10−4 M) (fig. S13), suggesting that any direct interaction is likely of low affinity. To determine whether this interaction influences virus-receptor binding, we performed ELISA-based binding assays in the presence or absence of GCA. The addition of GCA resulted in a modest increase in CD36-HuSaV binding (fig. S14). However, CD36 binding was clearly observed even in the absence of GCA, and the magnitude of this increase was limited. These results indicate that GCA is not required for virus-receptor binding and is unlikely to account for the substantial enhancement of infection observed. Instead, these findings support a model in which CD36 mediates initial virus attachment, whereas GCA acts at a postattachment step, likely facilitating viral internalization rather than substantially increasing initial binding.

Last, to test whether CD36 or GCA are required after entry, purified HuSaV genomic RNA was transfected into Caco-2MC or CD36-KO cells, and progeny virus production was measured in the absence of GCA. Comparable titers were obtained from both cell types (Fig. 5D), demonstrating that CD36 and GCA are dispensable after RNA delivery. Together, these results reveal that CD36 is essential for HuSaV infection, and that GCA acts primarily on host cells to facilitate CD36-mediated internalization, likely through a weak interaction with CD36, thereby enabling productive viral replication.

DISCUSSION

Our study identifies CD36 as a previously unrecognized cellular receptor for HuSaV. By demonstrating that CD36 expression confers susceptibility in otherwise nonpermissive cells, we establish CD36 as a critical determinant of viral entry. Direct binding assays further confirm that CD36 acts as a bona fide receptor. However, CD36 alone was insufficient for productive replication; GCA was also required. Because both CD36 and bile acids are abundant in the intestinal epithelium—the primary site of HuSaV infection—our findings provide a molecular explanation for the strict enteric tropism of HuSaV and represent a major step forward in understanding its pathogenesis. During the revision of this manuscript, an independent study reported that CD36 is required for efficient HuSaV propagation (22). While that study identified CD36 as a critical host factor for HuSaV replication, our results extend this observation by demonstrating that CD36 functions as a cellular receptor that directly binds HuSaV and mediates viral entry.

CD36 is a multifunctional scavenger receptor with two transmembrane domains and a large extracellular loop that binds diverse ligands, including LCFAs, oxidized low-density lipoprotein, and thrombospondin (16, 17). In the gut, CD36 is localized to the apical membrane of enterocytes and facilitates uptake of dietary lipids, cholesterol, and carotenoids (23, 24). Internalization of LCFAs is regulated by posttranslational palmitoylation of the CD36 cytoplasmic tail (20). These features suggest that CD36 may provide both a binding site and an internalization pathway that HuSaV has co-opted for infection.

The requirement for bile acids further refines this model. Several enteric viruses exploit bile acids during infection. In MNV, bile acids enhance receptor binding and induce conformational changes in the capsid P domain (25, 26). Certain HuNoV genotypes (e.g., GII.3) also require bile acids for infection (27), although bile acid binding is genotype specific (28). Bile acids can additionally modulate host cells through receptors such as S1PR2, promoting endocytosis of some norovirus strains (29, 30). For porcine sapovirus, bile acids suppress innate responses and disrupt tight junctions to facilitate replication (31–33). Consistent with these precedents, we found that GCA alone did not mediate attachment but instead promoted CD36 internalization, thereby enabling efficient viral entry. Weak but measurable binding between GCA and CD36, as observed by BLI, suggests a multicomponent entry mechanism in which CD36 provides the primary docking site, while bile acids act as cofactors to enhance uptake (Fig. 6 and fig. S11).

CD36-mediated HuSaV infection model in intestine.

Notably, CD36 expression alone did not universally permit productive replication. For example, NIH3T3/CD36 cells expressed CD36 and supported initial VP1 detection but failed to generate progeny virus, implying that additional human-specific cofactors are required. These results highlight species barriers that may explain the narrow host range of HuSaV and underscore the complexity of virus-host interactions. Identification of such cofactors will be critical for elucidating the molecular basis of HuSaV host specificity.

Last, our findings were obtained in vitro, and validation in primary human tissues or animal models remains an important future direction. While CD36 and bile acids are necessary, they are not sufficient to fully recapitulate HuSaV replication across diverse cell types, particularly in non-human cells. This points to additional host determinants that remain to be found.

Although our results demonstrate that CD36 mediates HuSaV infection in human intestinal organoids, the present study used a single ileum-derived organoid line. While these findings support a role for CD36 in a physiologically relevant intestinal model, future studies using additional organoid lines derived from different donors and intestinal regions will be necessary to further evaluate the generalizability of CD36-mediated HuSaV entry.

In conclusion, we define CD36 as a key receptor for HuSaV and demonstrate that bile acids function as cofactors facilitating CD36-mediated viral entry. Together, these findings provide mechanistic insight into HuSaV tropism, establish a foundation for developing in vitro and in vivo models, and highlight the virus-receptor interface as a promising target for antiviral strategies.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from Caco-2MC, ME, PG, and PJ cells using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA samples were submitted to Azenta Life Sciences for library preparation, sequencing, and analysis. One microgram of total RNA was used for the following library preparation. The polyadenylate mRNA isolation was performed using Oligo(dT) beads. The mRNA fragmentation was performed using divalent cations and high temperature. Priming was performed using Random Primers. First strand cDNA and the second-strand cDNA were synthesized. The purified double-stranded cDNA was then treated to repair both ends and add a dA-tailing in one reaction, followed by a T-A ligation to add adaptors to both ends. Size selection of adaptor-ligated DNA was then performed using DNA Clean Beads. Each sample was then amplified by PCR using P5 and P7 primers, and the PCR products were validated. Then, libraries with different indexs were multiplexed and loaded on an Illumina HiSeq/Illumina Novaseq/MGI2000 instrument for sequencing using a 2 × 150 paired-end configuration according to manufacturer’s instructions.

Differential expression analysis was performed by Azenta using their standard RNA sequencing pipeline. Genes with an absolute log2 fold change >1 and adjusted P value <0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed.

Cell culture

Caco2-derived clones, HuTu80 (ATCC) and Vero E6 (JCRB) were cultured in minimum essential medium (Nacalai Tesque) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% nonessential amino acids (Nacalai Tesque), 1% sodium pyruvate (Nacalai Tesque), and 1× penicillin-streptomycin (PS) (Nacalai Tesque). HCT 116 (ATCC) were cultured in McCoy’s 5a medium (Gibco) containing 10% FBS and 1× PS. NIH/3T3 (ATCC) and 293T (ATCC) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Nacalai Tesque) containing 10% FBS and 1× PS. CHO-K1 (ECACC) cells were cultured in Ham’s F12 medium (Nacalai Tesque) containing 10% FBS and 1× PS. Ileum-1 was prepared as previously described (34) and was established at the Keio University School of Medicine. Ileum-1 was maintained as described in (35) with the following modifications: Ileum-1 was embedded in Matrigel matrix (Corning) and cultured in IntestiCult Organoid Growth Medium (Veritas).

Flow cytometric analysis

The surface CD36 expression was analyzed using flow cytometric analysis. Cells were detached from the plate with TrypLE Express Enzyme (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and blocked with Block Ace solution (Waken B Tech). The anti-CD36 antibody (BioLegend, 5-271) was incubated with the cells. The cells were then washed and incubated with Alexa Fluor 647 donkey anti-mouse immunoglobulin G (IgG, Thermo Fisher Scientific). For costaining of CD36 and HuSaV VP1, fixed cells were blocked with Block Ace solution containing 0.05% Triton X-100 solution for permeabilization. After blocking, the anti-CD36 antibody and a rabbit antiserum for HuSaV VLP (8) was incubated with the cells. The cells were then washed and incubated with Alexa Fluor 647 donkey anti-mouse IgG and Alexa Fluor 488 donkey anti-rabbit IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The antibodies were diluted in Block Ace solution (Waken B Tech), and the washing steps were carried out in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) without magnesium or calcium salts [PBS (−)]. The cells were analyzed using a FACSMelody cell sorter (BD Biosciences) and FlowJo software (BD Biosciences).

Production of sapovirus VLPs

The VP1 coding region of HuSaV GII.3 (EH-37 strain; GenBank accession no. LC504413) was codon-optimized for expression in High Five insect cells, synthesized (GenScript), and cloned into the pFastBac1 vector (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Recombinant bacmid DNA was generated using the Bac-to-Bac Baculovirus Expression System. Sf9 and High Five cells were maintained in Sf-900 III SFM and Express Five SFM, respectively. Bacmid DNA was transfected into Sf9 cells using Lipofectamine 2000 to produce P1 baculovirus, which was sequentially amplified to P3 stock in Sf9 cells. Viral titers were determined by plaque assay, and P3 stocks (≥3 × 107 plaque-forming units/ml) were used for VLP production. For large-scale production, High Five cells were infected with P3 virus [multiplicity of infection (MOI) 5] and cultured at 26.5°C. After 8 days, culture supernatants were collected and clarified by centrifugation, and VLPs were pelleted through a 30% sucrose cushion. Further purification was performed by CsCl density-gradient ultracentrifugation. The VLP band was collected, diluted with PBS, and recovered by ultracentrifugation.

CD36 KO lines

Human CD36 single guide RNA (sgRNA) (5′-aagcaattgtaccttcttcg-3′) was inserted in lentiCRISPRv2 hygro which was a gift from B. Stringer (36) (Addgene plasmid # 98291). This plasmid was transfected to MC cells by Lipofectamin 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). A day after transfection, the cells were selected with hygromycin (500 μg/ml). After selection, the KO cells were isolated through single-cell cloning. The successful KO was confirmed by sequencing the targeted region within the CD36 genomic locus. Genomic DNA was isolated from each clone, and the sgRNA-targeted region of the CD36 locus was amplified by PCR. The PCR products were cloned into the pUC19 vector, and individual colonies were sequenced to confirm the presence of the desired KO mutations by Sanger sequencing.

CD36 KI lines

The CD36 cDNA was isolated from MC cells and was cloned into pLVSIN-CMV-IRES-puro (10) or pLVSIN-EF1a-IRES-bsd vector. Human CD36 cDNA was transduced into each cell line by lentiviral vector. A day after infection, each cell line was selected with puromycin.

HuSaV infection

HuSaV was inoculated onto each cell line at the indicated MOI. The virus inoculum containing 1 mM GCA was incubated for 1 hour at 37°C. After infection, the cells were washed and further incubated for the indicated periods. HuSaV RNA copy numbers in the infected cells were quantified at 1 hour postinfection (immediately after medium replacement) and at the indicated time points.

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR

HuSaV RNA quantification by RT-qPCR was described in the previous report (8).

TCID50 assay using Caco-2MC cells

Determination of TCID50 using Caco-2MC cells was described in (8). Caco-2MC cells were seeded into 96-well plates at 2 × 104 cells per well and inoculated with a fivefold serial dilution of the virus. The plates were incubated at 37°C for 5 days. The infection was considered successful when even minimal fluorescence of VP1 was detected through immunostaining. TCID50 values were calculated using the Reed and Muench method (37).

TCID50 assay using Vero E6/CD36KI clone 16

Vero E6/CD36KI clone 16 (VeroC16) cells were seeded into 384-well plates at 1 × 104 cells per well and inoculated with a fivefold serial dilution of the virus. The plates were incubated at 37°C for 7 days. The wells showing CPEs were counted, and TCID50 values were calculated using the Reed and Muench method (37).

Immunofluorescence image

The cells were infected with HuSaV (MOI: 0.5 to 1) for 24 hours. Then, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and, in some experiments, were permeabilized with 0.05% Triton X-100. Then, the cells were treated with Block Ace solution (Waken B Tech) for blocking. Rabbit anti-serum for HuSaV VLP or mouse anti-human CD36 antibody (BioLegend, 5-271) was used as first antibodies. After washing, Alexa Fluor 568–conjugated anti-mouse IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were added as secondary antibodies and treated with Hoechst 33342 stain (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Images were captured using a BZ-X 800 fluorescence microscope (Keyence).

Western blotting

Whole-cell lysates were prepared by adding Laemmli’s SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis sample buffer after washing the cells with PBS (−) solution. The lysates were separated on a 5 to 20% polyacrylamide gel (ATTO) and transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane (Bio-Rad) using Trans blot turbo (Bio-Rad). The primary antibodies used were rabbit anti-CD36 antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, #14347), and rhodamine-conjugated anti-actin FAb (Bio-Rad). Secondary antibodies used were horseradish peroxidase (HRP)–conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (Cell Signaling Technology). The ChemiDoc Touch (Bio-Rad) was used to detect proteins visualized by Chemi-Lumi One L Solution A and Chemi-Lumi One L Solution B (Nacalai Tesque).

Blocking assay with soluble recombinant CD36

HuSaV (1250 TCID50) was incubated with 1 or 0.1 μg of soluble recombinant CD36-Fc (Acro Biosystems, #CD6-H5258) or recombinant Human IgG1 Fc (Thr106-Lys330) (BioLegend) for 2 hours at 37°C and was then incubated with Caco-2MC (MOI 0.1) for 1 hour with GCA. After infection, the cells were washed and were cultured for 1 day. HuSaV genomic RNA copies in the cells at 1 day postinfection were quantified by qPCR.

For immunofluorescence image, HuSaV (12,500 TCID50) was incubated with 1 or 0.1 μg of soluble recombinant CD36-Fc or recombinant human IgG1 Fc for 2 hours at 37°C and was then incubated with Caco-2MC (MOI 1) for 1 day with GCA. Infected cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and were permeabilized with 0.05% Triton X-100. Then, the cells were treated with Block Ace solution (Waken B Tech) for blocking. Rabbit anti-serum for HuSaV VLP was used as first antibodies. After washing, Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were added as secondary antibodies and treated with Hoechst 33342 stain (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Images were captured using a BZ-X800 fluorescence microscope (Keyence). VP1-positive cells were quantified using BZ-X Analyzer software by calculating the percentage of VP1-positive cells relative to the total number of nuclei in multiple randomly selected fields.

Blocking assay with anti-CD36 antibody

Caco-2MC cells were incubated with 0.1 μg per well of anti-CD36 antibody (HPA002018, Sigma-Aldrich) or rabbit isotype control IgG (0.1 μg/ml; ab37415, Abcam) for 1 hour before infection. A mixture of HuSaV (MOI 0.1) and GCA was added to the culture, and the cells were incubated for 1 day. HuSaV genomic RNA copies in the cells at 1 day postinfection were quantified by qPCR.

Establishment of CD36 KO Ileum-1

Cas9 gene and CD36 sgRNA were transduced into Ileum-1 using a lentiviral vector (lentiCRISPRv2 hygro), and transduced cells were selected with hygromycin (200 μg/ml). After selection, KO cells were isolated via single-cell cloning. The successful KO was confirmed by sequencing the targeted region within the CD36 genomic locus.

Biolayer interferometry

Binding affinities between HuSaV VLP and CD36 were analyzed by BLI using an Octet R2 system (Sartorius). Each measurement was performed in 200 μl of solution in a black 96-well plate (#655209, Greiner). Biotinylated human CD36 (CD6-H82E9, Acro Biosystems) was immobilized onto streptavidin (SA) biosensors (Sartorius) to loading level approximately 4 nm. The biosensors were then blocked with biocytin (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After equilibration in 1× kinetic buffer (Sartorius) for 60 s, the sensors were exposed to twofold serial dilutions of HuSaV GII.3 (I22-24) VLP (starting at 37.6 nM) to measure association for 120 s, followed by transfer into kinetic buffer for 120 s to monitor dissociation. All data were processed and analyzed using the Octet BLI Analysis software (Sartorius).

Immunohistochemistry of human intestinal tissue

Human intestinal paraffin serial sections were purchased from BioChain (catalog no. T2234226). After deparaffinization, antigen retrieval was performed by heating the sections in 1 mM EDTA (pH 8.0) at 95°C for 20 min. The sections were then blocked with BlockAid Blocking Solution (Molecular Probes) at room temperature for 1 hour. Subsequently, the samples were incubated overnight at room temperature with rabbit anti-CD36 (HPA002018, Sigma-Aldrich) and rabbit anti-Villin antibody (GTX109940, GeneTex) as primary antibodies. After washing, the sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor 594–conjugated anti-rabbit IgG and Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated anti-rabbit IgG as secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at room temperature for 1 hour. Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst33342 stain (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Images were acquired using a BZ-X800 fluorescence microscope (Keyence).

Time-of-addition assay of GCA treatment

HuSaV (GI.1) was incubated with or without 1 mM GCA for 1 hour at 37°C, followed by ultracentrifugation (55,000 rpm, 90 min, 4°C; Optima MAX-TL, Beckman) to remove residual GCA in the medium. The virus was then used to infect cells for 1 hour in the presence or absence of GCA. After infection, the cells were washed twice with PBS and subsequently incubated for 3 days with or without GCA.

Viral RNA transfection

HuSaV was infected Caco-2MC cells cultured in T75 flask (MOI 1). After 3 days postinfection, culture supernatant was replaced to distilled water and freezing-thawing cycle was repeated three times. After removing debris from the solution by centrifugation (10,000g for 10 min), the collected culture supernatant was centrifuged through a 30% (w/v) sucrose cushion for 2 hours at 124,000g using a Beckman SW32 Ti rotor. Viral genomic RNA was isolated from the concentrated virus by QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kits (Qiagen). Viral RNA was transduced into Caco-2MC or CD36 KO cells by Super Electroporator NEPA21 Type II (NEPAGENE) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

ELISA for CD36 and HuSaV binding

Purified HuSaV GI.1 (AH20) virion (0.025 μg/ml) was immobilized on MaxiSorp plate (464718, Thermo Fisher Scientific). After coating, the wells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and subsequently blocked with 1% Block Ace (Waken B Tech) in PBS. Serial dilutions of recombinant human CD36 (human CD36/SR-B3 Protein, Fc Tag; Acro biosystems) or recombinant control human IgG1-Fc (BioLegend) (1 to 0.125 μg per well) were added to the wells and incubated. CD36 bound to immobilized HuSaV was detected using goat anti-human IgG-HRP antibody (Southern Biotech). Between each incubation, the wells were washed with PBST. KPL SureBlue Reserve TMB Microwell Peroxidase Substrate (Sera Care) was used as a substrate of HRP, and the reaction was stopped by 1 M H2SO4. Absorbance was measured using by Ensight plate reader (PerkinElmer).
