Final Scientific/Technical Report
 Precision spectroscopy of nuclear decays using quantum
 optomechanical sensors

 DOE Award Number: DE-SC0023672
 Sponsoring Office: U.S. Department of Energy, Office of Science, Office of Nuclear Physics
 (Quantum Horizons: QIS for Nuclear Physics)
 Recipient: Yale University, New Haven, CT
 Principal Investigator: David C. Moore
 Project Period:               02/01/2023 – 01/31/2026

Summary
This project developed and demonstrated a fundamentally new way to detect individual nuclear decays:
rather than capturing the energy that decay products deposit in a detector, we measure the tiny mechan-
ical recoil of the entire micron- or nanometer-sized particle in which the decaying nucleus is embedded.
Because momentum is conserved, this approach is sensitive even to neutral, weakly interacting particles,
including neutrinos, that escape conventional detectors. During the award, the Yale group reported the
first-ever mechanical detection of single nuclear decays, a result featured widely in the scientific press,
and pushed the sensitivity of smaller levitated nanoparticles into the quantum measurement regime,
reaching an impulse resolution within a factor of five of the fundamental Standard Quantum Limit
(SQL), good enough in principle to detect the momentum kicked to the particle by a single emitted
neutrino. In parallel, the LBNL group developed the theory of quantum-enhanced (sub-SQL) readout
tailored to this experiment, showing how squeezed light can push the sensitivity below the SQL. To-
gether these results establish levitated optomechanical sensors as a new tool for precision nuclear-decay
spectroscopy, with further applications in neutrino physics, quantum metrology, and nuclear forensics
and safeguards.

Project Goals and Objectives
The project combined experimental work at Yale with theory at LBNL, aimed at detecting single nuclear
decays using levitated optomechanical sensors operated in the regime where quantum measurement
noise is non-negligible. On the experimental side, the central goals were to demonstrate detection of the
recoil of a micron-scale sphere following α decays of implanted 220 Rn daughters, in coincidence with the
emitted α and with high signal-to-noise ratio, and to bring smaller (∼100 nm) nanoparticle sensors into
the quantum measurement regime, reaching impulse sensitivity within a small factor of the Standard
Quantum Limit (SQL). On the theory side, the goal was to develop detailed, experimentally realistic
protocols for surpassing the SQL in the Yale impulse sensor, in particular by tailoring squeezing and
back-action-evasion techniques to that system.

Accomplishments
First mechanical detection of single nuclear decays. We reported the detection of individual
nuclear α decays through the mechanical recoil of the entire ∼3 µm diameter (1.5 µm radius) silica sphere
in which the decaying nuclei were embedded, after implanting 212 Pb (220 Rn daughters) into optically
levitated spheres. This is, to our knowledge, the first demonstration that a single sub-atomic particle
can be detected through the force it imparts to an object more than 1012 times more massive. Observing
the coincident change in the sphere’s net charge allows decays to be identified with backgrounds at the
micro-becquerel level. These results were published as Mechanical Detection of Nuclear Decays, Phys.
Rev. Lett. 133, 023602 (2024), selected as an Editors’ Suggestion and Featured in Physics, highlighted
by the DOE Office of Nuclear Physics, and covered by Physics Today, Physics World, Discover Magazine,
Chemistry World, and others.
Quantum-limited nanoparticle sensing. We built a high-numerical-aperture trap for ∼150 nm
nanoparticles and implemented homodyne detection of the backscattered light, which optimally reads
out the photons entangled  √ with the particle’s center-of-mass motion. This achieved a displacement
sensitivity near 10−13 m/ Hz, corresponding to an information-collection efficiency > 0.15. Direct
calibration using single-electron charge √and pulsed electric fields gave a measured impulse resolution
of ∼220 keV/c, within 5× of the SQL ( h̄mΩ ≈ 48 keV/c) and already slightly exceeding the original
proposal goal. This sensitivity is sufficient for SNR ≫ 10 detection of the ∼4 MeV momentum imparted
by the neutrino and de-excitation γ in 208 Tl β decay. We also designed and installed a custom position-
sensitive α/β detector (a 3 × 3 SiPM array behind a scintillator, vacuum-compatible and tolerant of
scattered laser light) providing ∼1 mm position resolution for coincident tagging of decay products.
Decay-resolved charge changes. Employing the position-sensitive SiPM detector described above
together with 212 Pb-loaded spheres, we measured event-by-event discrete changes in the sphere’s net
charge arising from individual decays, correlated with the emitted α and β particles. These measure-
ments characterize the backgrounds achievable with the technique and identify showers of low-energy
electrons from α-decaying radon daughters near surfaces; they are reported in Decay-Resolved Charge
Changes from Radioactive Decays in Levitated Microparticles, arXiv:2603.14979 (2026). The results are
also relevant to nuclear safeguards and forensics applications.
Radioisotope loading of quantum-limited nanoparticles. To extend these techniques toward the
measurement of single-neutrino recoils, we developed two methods to load neutrino-emitting isotopes
into the quantum-limited nanospheres: (i) a Paul trap, co-aligned with the optical trap, that drifts
charged 216 Po+ ions onto a nanosphere so that the subsequent α decay embeds the 212 Pb chain (whose
212 Pb and 208 Tl β decays are detectable at the demonstrated sensitivity); and (ii) a microfluidic droplet

loader that delivers a single radioisotope-doped nanoparticle into the trap and supports a wider range
of isotopes. Both loading systems were designed and built, with commissioning underway during the
project, establishing the platform for future neutrino-recoil measurements.
Sub-SQL readout theory (LBNL). The LBNL group developed a detailed microscopic input–output
theory for levitated dielectric sensors and used it to design quantum-enhanced readout tailored to the
Yale system. They quantified how squeezed readout light reduces measurement noise below the SQL in
impulse sensing, derived the optimal frequency-dependent squeezing spectrum and the impact of realistic
photon loss, and showed that n dB of injected squeezing lowers the impulse threshold by n/2 dB (Impulse
measurements enhanced with squeezed readout light, Phys. Rev. Research 7, 033041 [2025]). A follow-
up extended these protocols to simultaneous squeezing in all three spatial dimensions, important for
sensing impulses along an a priori unknown direction (Three-dimensional squeezing of optically levitated
nanospheres, arXiv:2601.22283 [2026]). These provide a concrete path to push the Yale sensors below
the SQL.

Outcomes and Impact
The project established a new class of detector for individual nuclear decays and demonstrated, for the
first time, that the recoil of a massive levitated object can be used to detect the forces from single sub-
atomic particles. It also demonstrated calibrated impulse measurements with levitated nanoparticle
sensors that are within a small factor of the SQL and developed the theory needed to surpass the
SQL in future work. These capabilities can enable future work to realize kinematic reconstruction of
β and electron-capture decays, including the direct detection of the forces imparted by recoils from
the emission of individual neutrinos. It also establishes technologies that may provide methods for
assaying single nuclear decays in dust-sized particles, with possible applications in nuclear safeguards or
environmental monitoring. Beyond nuclear physics, this work has broader relevance to high-frequency
gravitational-wave detection, dark matter searches, and quantum-limited force sensing and metrology.
The award supported the training of graduate students and postdocs at Yale and LBNL in quantum
measurement and nuclear-physics techniques, including two PhD students who completed their degrees
under the project, both of whom moved on to postdoctoral positions in quantum information science.
