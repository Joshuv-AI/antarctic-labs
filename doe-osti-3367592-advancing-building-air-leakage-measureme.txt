 Executive Summary
 Building air leakage has a direct impact on energy consumption, indoor air quality, and
 moisture accumulation. It is therefore crucial to measure air leakage in multizone
 buildings, particularly multifamily residential buildings, both through the exterior
 envelope and between different zones inside the building (interior leakage). While
 blower door testing methods to measure air leaks to the outdoors are well established
 and have been used for more than 50 years, current tests cannot directly measure the
 total interior boundary leakage of a zone, which can impact indoor air quality and
 energy.

 This report (1) compares existing measurement methods, based on field testing in a
 wide range of buildings, to identify information provided and workload; (2) presents the
 development of two new methods to directly measure exterior and interior leakage of an
 attached unit, and compares to existing methods; and (3) experimentally evaluates the
 behavior of leak flow for specific crack structures and measurement conditions, which
 has important implications for measuring and modeling building leaks.

 The team developed a new guarded interior test that directly measures the total interior
 boundary leakage of an attached unit using common blower door equipment. They also
 developed a new zonal multipoint pressure testing method that simultaneously
 measures both exterior and interior boundary leaks to identify leak parameters of the
 tested zone. The team evaluated the performance of these two new tests in a wide
 range of multifamily buildings, including newly constructed apartment buildings, old
 brownstones, and existing dormitories. Results from the new testing methods where
 compared to existing techniques. A comparison of single-point versus multipoint testing
 is presented in terms of testing errors, information provided, and cost. The team also
 developed a new closed-unit flow-balance test to evaluate the consistency of results for
 both conventional and new testing methods, as well as the importance of testing
 conditions.

 To better understand the differences among leak types, the team conducted an
 experimental study to measure airflow through engineered leak pathways of different
 geometries for a wide range of pressures. These experiments revealed that both the
 pressure exponent and normalized leak coefficient scale with the crack geometry and
 testing flow conditions. Similarly, the team observed that blower door test results
 obtained for several buildings also depended on the tested pressure-flow conditions.

 These results demonstrate that measured results are dependent on testing conditions,
 which therefore should be accounted for. The identified leak parameters will allow
 modeling to predict different building energy use, indoor air quality, and moisture
 impacts due to the difference between testing and use conditions. In addition, a
 correlation between the pressure exponent and normalized leak coefficient is also
 observed in extensive whole-building field measurements in multifamily buildings. This
 implies that the distribution of pressure exponent values for a portfolio of buildings can
 indicate the relative leakiness of building boundaries.

U.S. Department of Energy | Office of Critical Minerals and Energy Innovation                    vii

 By using the test methods developed in this study, energy raters can more broadly
 evaluate the air leakage in multifamily buildings, providing measurements of both the
 individual unit leakage to the outside and leakage between dwelling units. With this
 actionable information, targeted air sealing can not only reduce energy use, but also
 improve indoor air quality, reduce odors and noises, control moisture transmission, and
 improve fire safety. In addition, information is provided to help raters identify the nature
 of the leaks, which can be useful for identifying appropriate air-sealing methods. Finally,
 this work also makes important contributions to the understanding of airflow leakage
 physics and modeling.

 1 Introduction
 Building air leakage has a direct impact on energy consumption, indoor air quality (IAQ),
 and moisture accumulation. It is therefore crucial to measure air leakage in multizone
 buildings, both through the exterior envelope and between different zones inside the
 building (interior leakage). While blower door testing methods to measure air leaks are
 well established and have been used for more than 50 years, current tests lack the
 ability to directly measure the total interior boundary leakage of a zone. In addition,
 while power law models have long been used to express the building pressure-flow
 relationships used for code compliance and understand building leakage behavior, it
 has not been clearly established how model parameters relate to the structure of leak
 paths, testing conditions, and the distribution of model parameters in relation to leak
 behavior.

 This document reports our recent research results (Jayarathne 2023; Jayarathne,
 Browne, and Gevelber 2021, 2022, 2024) 1 on air leakage measurement in terms of:

 1. Comparing existing measurement methods, based on field testing in a wide
 range of buildings, to identify information provided and workload.

 2. Developing two new methods to directly measure exterior and interior leakage of
 an attached unit and comparing them to existing methods.

 3. Experimentally evaluating leak flow behavior for specific crack structures and
 measurement conditions, which is found to have important implications for
 measuring and modeling building leaks.

 1.1 Air Leaks in Buildings
 Buildings are three-dimensional structures with exterior and interior boundaries, and a
 pressure field develops inside and outside them because of external and internal factors
 such as stack effect (thermal buoyancy), wind, and air distribution systems. This
 pressure field induces air leakage through unintentional gaps and cracks such as poorly
 sealed joints between different building materials in both exterior and interior
 boundaries, where the driving force is the pressure difference across the boundary. The
 leakage flow rate depends on the driving pressure force and the physical characteristics
 of the leak paths present in the boundary.

 Air leakage measurement and control of common-entry multifamily buildings have been
 examined in previous studies including Harrje, Bohac, and Feuerman (1988); Tuluca
 and Sherman (1995); Moffat, Theaker, and Wray, (1998); Francisco and Palmiter
   (1994); DePani and Fazio (2001); Wray (2002); Price et al. (2006); Genge (2007);
 Finch, Straube, and Genge (2009); and Griffiths (2012). The new testing methods
 presented here were developed for such buildings; however, it is possible to extend the

 proposed testing methods to other types of multizone buildings, such as garden-style
 multifamily buildings and multizone commercial buildings, as long as the specific
 complexities are considered and accounted for in testing and formulation.

 Two types of leakages are identified: (1) exterior leakage through exterior boundaries
 such as walls, roofs, and floors or foundations that are part of the building envelope,
 and (2) interior leakage, which includes leakage through interior boundaries such as
 walls shared by different spaces inside the building, interior ceilings, interior floors, and
 interstitial spaces such as chases, shafts, and dropped ceilings.

 Energy consumption is primarily affected by exterior leakage because it demands
 additional energy from the heating and cooling system to condition outside air that
 infiltrates the building, while the already conditioned air inside the building exfiltrates.
 Interior leakage can also contribute to energy loss by allowing air to move inside the
 building freely, thus increasing stack-driven infiltration and exfiltration, as well as
 causing leakage between conditioned and unconditioned spaces inside the building,
 such as garages.

 In the United States, the residential building sector accounts for about 21% of total
 primary U.S. energy consumption. Of this, space heating and cooling are the largest
 uses of energy, accounting for about 43% (U.S. Energy Information Administration
 2022, 2023). Up to a third of this is estimated to be caused by unintentional air leakage
 through the exterior envelope (Sherman and Walker 2012).

 In addition to the energy impact, air leakage—especially through interior boundaries—
 can lead to:

 • Poor IAQ and occupant comfort through transmission of odors, pollutants, and
 moisture.
 • Reduced sound and fire separation.
 • Building material degradation through moisture accumulation.
 Previous studies have found large variability in the natural airflow coming into units in
 multifamily buildings; approximately 35% could be from other zones in the building
 (Harrje et al. 1988; Francisco and Palmiter 1994; Bohac et al. 2007; Ricketts 2014).
 Lozinsky and Touchie (2020) review the impact of interzonal airflow on IAQ, comfort,
 and moisture in buildings, highlighting the importance of improving tightness of interior
 boundaries in addition to exterior boundaries to reduce issues with contaminant
 transport, comfort, and fire safety.

 The new insights from the experiments conducted as part of this research are the
 relationship between power law model parameters and physical characteristics of leaks
 and tested conditions, as well as the implications of this relationship on blower door
 testing results. Typically, blower door testing is performed at elevated pressure
 differences (typically ±10–75 Pa) and reported at either a pressure differential of 50 Pa

 • Can the blower door testing results of buildings obtained at high pressures

 • Can the testing results be used to identify the type of dominant leak paths that

 1.3 Air Leakage Measurement Methods
 One key precursor for improving airtightness in buildings is the ability to effectively
 measure leakage. Blower door testing (Kronvall 1980) has been the most widely used
 method for several decades to measure the air leakage rate in buildings, compared to
 other methods such as tracer gas techniques (Hitchin and Wilson 1967). Leakage
 through a building’s exterior envelope or a dwelling unit boundary is measured at
 elevated pressure differences across the boundary, such as ±10–75 Pa. The leak flow
 is then normalized by either the envelope area (CFM50/ft2) or the zone volume,
 expressed as air changes per hour at 50 Pa (ACH50), to use as a compliance metric.

 Historically, reducing the leaks through the exterior boundaries of a building has been
 given more focus because it directly affects energy consumption. Blower door testing of
 the whole building can measure the leakage from the entire exterior envelope, but it
 does not provide information on interior leakage or which areas in the exterior envelope
 have the biggest leaks. In addition, conducting a whole-building test could be difficult,
 especially for multiunit buildings, because of either a lack of equipment to test large
 buildings or partial occupancy. This has led to allowing unit leak-testing methods such
 as compartmentalization (also called unguarded testing) to act as a surrogate. Energy
 codes such as the International Energy Conservation Code (IECC) (International Code
 Council 2021) recommend compartmentalization testing as a unit test, which measures
 the total leakage of the unit including both the exterior and interior boundary leakage
 without distinguishing between the two. This leads to several questions in terms of
 testing and using the information from test results:

 • Does the compartmentalization test give enough information about the exterior
 boundary of the unit?
 • How important and useful is it to know the exterior and interior boundary leaks
 separately?
 • Can compartmentalization be used as a marker to estimate and improve
 airtightness and energy impact?

U.S. Department of Energy | Office of Critical Minerals and Energy Innovation                    4

 It is also appropriate to compare this method with other testing methods such as the
 conventional guarded test (Shaw 1980), which directly measures the exterior boundary
 leakage of an attached unit. This method gives information as to which areas in the
 exterior boundary have the largest leaks; however, similar to the whole-building test, it
 does not give information on interior leakage. Is there a similar method that can directly
 measure the interior boundary leakage?

 This report compares the information, time and effort requirement, and limitations of
 these testing methods based on field testing in a wide range of buildings and introduces
 two new testing methods that can directly measure exterior and interior leakage of
 dwelling units.

 The basic idea of blower door testing is to measure the leakage flow rate through a
 selected boundary of the building at an elevated pressure difference across the
 boundary (e.g., ±50 Pa). At natural conditions, building boundaries could have steady
 pressure differences across them because of stack (thermal buoyancy) and steady wind
 conditions, or they could have unsteady pressure differences because of wind
 fluctuations. Using elevated pressures makes it less noisy and easier to measure
 pressures and leakage flow rates compared to measuring in natural conditions. A series
 of blower doors mounted on entrances to the building or dwelling unit is typically used to
 achieve this elevated pressure. Depending on the boundary of the building to be tested,
 several blower door testing methods can be used. Figure 1 shows the leak flows
 through different boundaries of an attached unit of a common-entry multifamily building
 and what is measured by different tests. Qext represents the exterior leakage from
 different zones, and Qint represents interior leakage between zones. The guarded
 exterior test (GText) measures the exterior leakage of the unit, while
 compartmentalization measures the total (both interior and exterior) leakage of the unit.

 The two common blower door test methods used in multifamily buildings are (1) a
 whole-building test to measure the leakage of the entire exterior boundary of the
 building, and (2) a compartmentalization method (also called an unguarded test) to
 measure the total leakage of an attached dwelling unit within the building. The two tests
 have several pros and cons (Jayarathne 2023; Jayarathne, Browne, and Gevelber
 2024). The whole-building test is a single test that gives the leakage of the whole
 exterior boundary of the building, including common areas, and therefore directly relates
 to energy use. However, it requires access to the entire building, needs more equipment
 and training to perform depending on the size and complexity of the building, and does
 not give any information regarding the interior leaks that affect IAQ and occupant
 comfort. Compartmentalization measures leaks in attached dwelling units in multifamily
 buildings, which is especially important in a large building where performing a whole-
 building test is difficult because of the scale or partial occupancy. In addition, identifying
 problematic locations could be harder using only a whole-building test, whereas
 compartmentalization results can indicate units that may have leakier exterior boundary
 portions. A compartmentalization test gives the total boundary leakage of a unit,
 including exterior and interior boundaries. Despite being easier and relatively fast to
 perform, it is often only performed in a sample of units. If all units are tested, or if
 sampling is done carefully so as to include all different types of units (e.g., corner units,
 middle units, top floor), it can give a better understanding of where major leaks could be

U.S. Department of Energy | Office of Critical Minerals and Energy Innovation                                            6

 located. However, the key limitation is that a compartmentalization test does not
 distinguish between exterior and interior boundary leaks. Field testing in buildings
 shows that two units could have the same compartmentalization leakage but very
 different exterior and interior leaks that lead to different impacts on energy and IAQ
 (Bohac et al. 2020; Jayarathne, Browne, and Gevelber 2022).

 To directly measure the exterior leakage of an attached unit, GText can be used (Shaw
 1980; Canadian General Standards Board 2019). This method, when used in a
 common-entry multifamily building, involves adjusting the unit pressure and the
 pressure in the rest of the building simultaneously to isolate the exterior boundary
 leakage of the unit by nullifying the pressure difference across the interior boundary of
 the unit.

 In addition to these well-known methods, many researchers have developed methods to
 measure leaks between zones inside the building (Nylund 1981; Herrlin and Modera
 1988; Feustel 1990; Emmerich, Gorfain, and Howard-Reed 2003; Proskiw 2003; Hult,
 Sherman, and Walker 2014; Bohac et al. 2020). These methods cannot directly
 measure the total interior boundary leakage of an attached dwelling unit, and that
 limitation motivated the development of the new guarded interior test (GTint) (see
 Section 2.1). In addition, no test can directly measure both exterior and interior leakages
 and separately quantify them, which motivated the development of the zonal multipoint
 pressure test (ZMPT) (see Section 2.2).

 1.4 Airtightness Metrics and Single-Point Versus Multipoint Testing
 Air leakage can be reported using several different metrics based on flow at different
 pressures. The two most common elevated pressures used to report airtightness
 measurement for energy code compliance purposes are 75 Pa (commercial) and 50 Pa
 (residential). They are typically reported as normalized terms (based on shell area or
 floor area) and denoted as CFM50/ft2 or CFM75/ft2. Leakage flow at 50 Pa can also be
 converted to ACH50 using the infiltration volume. In addition, leak flow at 4 Pa (typically
 calculated from high-pressure flow data) is another commonly used metric that converts
 to the effective leakage area. The effective leakage area can be defined as the area of a
 nozzle-shaped hole that has the same airflow rate as the combined leaks in the tested
 building when both are subjected to a pressure difference of 4 Pa. This low pressure is
 selected because it is representative of the typical pressure buildings have because of
 weather conditions and is relevant for flow rates used to develop energy models.

 Neither the compartmentalization test nor GText directly measures the interior boundary

 In general, guarded testing takes more time to perform because of the need to control
 multiple fans, prepare the entire building, and balance the pressure difference across
 one of the boundaries. External factors such as wind can cause pressure fluctuations in
 the building that will make this balancing more time-consuming. In contrast, the whole-
 building, compartmentalization, and ZMPT methods do not need pressure balancing.

 1.6 Errors and Uncertainty in Testing Results and Numerical
 Considerations
 In multipoint testing, leakage airflow is measured at a range of pressures across the
 tested boundary, and the results are fitted to the linearized power law equation shown in
 Eq. 7 to calculate power law leakage parameters: the slope (n) and y-axis intercept (log
 C to find C).

 For typical testing, wind is the biggest factor that results in measurement errors of both
 precision and bias. Previous studies (Modera and Wilson 1990; Walker et al. 2013;
 Carrié and Leprince 2016; Carrié and Mélois 2020; Delmotte 2021) have looked at the
 effects of wind on the uncertainty of measurements and how it affects testing results.
 Wind effects are the biggest source of error for pressure measurement, but
 measurement time averaging can be used to reduce this error. For testing performed in
 this study, flow and pressure measurements for each pressure station (i.e., a value of
 set pressure at which the flow is measured out of a range of such pressures) is
 measured at 1-second intervals for a total number of measurements between 30 and
 120 seconds depending on testing type and outdoor conditions. An average of these
 measurements is taken as the measured value. In multipoint testing, measured
 pressure and flow conditions are fitted to Eq. 7 using the ordinary least square method.
 Here, the sum of the square of residuals between the data and prediction is minimized
 (Taylor 1982). In addition to wind, inside temperature, outside temperature, and
 elevation all affect measurement accuracy because of the density difference in air
 against the device calibration. This should be corrected according to manufacturer
 specifications for each measurement device.

U.S. Department of Energy | Office of Critical Minerals and Energy Innovation                               12

 Another source of error from model fit is the deviation of actual pressure-flow results
 from the assumed power law model. Previous theoretical and experimental work
 (Langhaar 1942; Kreith and Eisenstadt 1957; Honma 1975; Sherman 1992) and results
 under this research have shown that this may not be the case. In other words, actual
 results do not behave linearly as in Eq. 7, and the variations could be caused by a
 range of different factors including change of flow conditions (Reynolds number) with
 different driving pressures, valving effect changing the crack geometry under different
 pressures, presence of series and parallel leaks, and measurement errors.

 The error because of the deviation of actual behavior from the assumed model can
 increase if a wider range of pressures is used to perform the linearized fit, and this
 research and previous work (Sherman 1992) suggest that using a narrow pressure
 range around the target pressure can produce more accurate results.

 1.7 Research Objectives and Results
 The major research objectives addressed in this report include:

 • Comparing air leakage measurement methods in buildings, identifying limitations,
 and testing methods that address these limitations.
 • Experimentally evaluating the behavior of leakage flow through different types of
 crack structures to understand the relationship between building leakage
 measurement results and physical characteristics of cracks and flow conditions.
 The major research results are to:

 • Identify the different types of leaks in buildings (exterior and interior), their
 impact, and what is important to measure.
 • Develop new testing methods to directly measure the interior boundary leakage.
 • Evaluate the common and new leak measurement methods (including single-
 point and multipoint methods) and how they compare in terms of information,
 limitations, accuracy, time and effort requirement, and testing requirements.
 • Experimentally evaluate the relationship between the leakage flow and
 characteristics and the crack geometry and flow measurement conditions for
 specific crack types and the implications for leak measurement procedures and
 interpreting results.
 • Evaluate whether leakage characteristics of buildings also depend on tested flow
 conditions and what the implications are for conducting tests, calculating and
 interpreting results, comparing different buildings, and using the test results
 performed at elevated pressures to estimate leakage at natural low-pressure
 conditions.
 • Examine the relation of leak model parameters (n and C) using multipoint
 measurements for a large number of buildings and determine the relations
 between the parameters and leak types.

 2 Development of New Air Leakage Test Methods
 2.1 Guarded Interior Test To Measure Interior Leakage of an Attached
 Dwelling Unit
 Having outlined existing test methods and their limitations in the previous section, we
 now present details of the new GTint method that can be used to directly measure the
 leakage through the entire interior boundary of an attached unit in a multifamily building.
 The testing procedure is described for a common-entry-style building but can also be
 adapted for a garden-style building. The setup for a garden-style building is similar to
 the GText setup for the same type of building with the tested unit fan flow direction
 reversed. This is named the guarded interior test because it guards the exterior
 boundary to measure leakage through the interior boundary and is the opposite of the
 conventional GText (Shaw 1980; Canadian General Standards Board 2019), which
 guards the interior boundary to measure the leakage through the exterior boundary.

 When compared to other interzonal testing methods that measure leaks through only
 parts of the interior boundary, this method can measure the leakage through the entire
 interior boundary, which is useful when separately identifying exterior and interior leaks.
 In terms of the test setup, the key novelty is the direction of the flow of the fan setup on
 the unit door. It is opposite of the direction of the flow in the building main door fans
 used in the GText. This allows guarding of the exterior boundary.

 In terms of time and effort required, GTint is similar to the conventional GText (Table 1).
 Both guarded tests were found to be more time-consuming to perform (both for single-
 point and multipoint) based on time stamp data from our testing done in multifamily
 apartment buildings.

 neighboring and connected spaces of the tested unit, including in floors above and

 2.1.2 Single-Point Versus Multipoint Testing
 The testing procedures described above can be used for both single-point and
 multipoint testing. In single-point testing, a target pressure difference of 50 or 75 Pa is
 typically used (or the highest possible), and interior leakage at that pressure can be
 reported. If the target pressure cannot be reached, the highest pressure is tested, and

U.S. Department of Energy | Office of Critical Minerals and Energy Innovation                    16

 results are extrapolated to find the leakage at target pressure using an assumed
 exponent value of 0.65 in Eq. 1.

 In multipoint testing, multiple building pressures and ∆𝑃𝑃𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖 (unit to hallway and
 neighboring pressures) should be set, and fan flow rates equal to interior boundary
 leaks should be measured for each case. To be consistent with existing standards
 (IECC 2021), at least five pressures stations ±10–60 Pa should be measured.

 2.1.3 Difference Between GTint and Existing Testing Methods
 The underlying concept of GTint is similar to the conventional GText, which is to nullify the
 pressure difference across one boundary to measures the leakage flow through the
 other boundary.

 When compared to the conventional GText, the important difference in the test setup of
 GTint is the direction of the flow of the fan on the tested unit door. It is opposite of the
 direction of the flow in the building main door fans, and this is one of the key novelties
 that allows the balancing of the exterior boundary pressure difference that isolates the
 leakage flow through only the interior boundary.

 In terms of what is measured, GTint gives interior leakage between two zones in the
 building: the tested zone and the combined rest of the building. Over the years, many
 techniques have been developed to measure leakage between attached neighboring
 buildings (such as a row of townhouses) and between internal zones of a multiunit
 building, such as Nylund (1981); Love and Passmore (1987); Herrlin and Moderra
 (1988); Hult, Sherman, and Walker (2014); and Bohac et al. (2020). However, none of
 these techniques measure the total interior boundary leakage of an attached unit the
 way GTint can, and they require multiple measurements and calculations to obtain it.

 In addition, using GTint with GText allows the measurement of typical natural unit airflow
 direction using only two tests, as opposed to three tests when using conventional
 compartmentalization and GText. For a unit that is at a lower pressure than outside
 pressure, flow directions are exterior boundary leakage into the unit and interior
 boundary leakage out of the unit because air coming into the unit from outside has to
 leave through the interior boundary. To find leakage coming into the unit from the
 exterior boundary and leaving the unit from the interior boundary using conventional
 methods, three tests are needed: guarded exterior depressurization to find exterior
 leakage into the unit, and compartmentalization pressurization with guarded exterior
 pressurization (deduction method) to find interior leakage out of the unit. When using
 the guarded test combination, only two tests are needed, both GText and interior tests in
 depressurization.

 2.2 Zonal Multipoint Pressure Test To Simultaneously Measure Exterior
 and Interior Leaks of an Attached Dwelling Unit
 The goal of the ZMPT method developed under this research study is to measure and
 characterize both the exterior and interior boundaries of an attached unit using a single
 test. This is done by setting the tested zone and the rest of the building to different
 pressures using a flow-modulating and measurement device mounted either on a
 window or a door of the unit and using mass and flow balance of the unit to generate
 and solve a system of nonlinear equations with four unknowns (leak coefficients and
 pressure exponents of the exterior and interior boundaries).

 Figure 6 shows a generalized schematic for the two setups of ZMPT in a multiunit
 building in depressurization form (pressurization form can also be used with the change
 of flow direction in each boundary and device). Exterior boundaries are the walls,
 ceilings, and floors that separate the tested zone or unit from the outside. This is the
 same boundary leakage measured in GText. Interior boundaries are the walls, ceilings,
 and floors shared with other zones in the rest of the building, including the wall to the
 common hallway, partition walls to neighboring units, and floor and ceiling to units
 above and below, if available. All interior doors of the building are open to ensure that
 when depressurized, the entire building is at the same pressure (which is similar to
 GText described in Section 2.1.4 of Jayarathne [2023]). In Figure 6, two ZMPT devices
 are mounted on a window and the door of the unit (in practice only one is needed).
 These could be either passive calibrated flow-modulating devices (such as a calibrated
 damper) or active flow-modulating and measurement devices (such as a calibrated fan).
 For testing under this study, we used both the window device and door device
 configuration, and the door device configuration is preferable because of the ease of
 setup (setting up a blower door on the unit door) and the lack of wind-induced pressure
 fluctuations across the interior boundary.

 effect in which some leak paths get restricted when one side of the boundary is at a

 2.3.3.2 GTint Results Comparison Implications
 The testing performed in the three buildings and the comparison of results among
 conventional tests, ZMPT, and the new GTint show that the new GTint can be used to
 measure the interior leakage of an attached unit directly without doing any calculations
 (such as the deduction method used by many researchers and practitioners). This direct
 measurement minimizes error propagation compared to performing multiple
 measurements and using them to calculate interior leakage, such as the deduction
 method and other interzonal methods discussed in Sections 1.3 and 2.3.3.1. It can also
 be used for both the single-point method to measure the flow rate through the interior
 boundary at a specific pressure (such as 50 or 75 Pa) and the multipoint method to
 measure flow rates at a range of pressures to calculate power law leak parameters. It
 complies with the current standard and code requirements that allow both types of
 testing. In addition, the new GTint results are compatible with the conventional
 compartmentalization and GText results.

 These features make the new GTint a valuable tool that practitioners can use to
 identify major areas of leaks in interior boundaries of attached units. It can also
 be used as an additional diagnostic test to aid sealing efforts for
 compartmentalization compliance required by codes and standards. In terms of
 research efforts for leakage measurement methods (both blower door and other types),
 as well as for energy and IAQ modeling studies based on measurement data, this test
 to directly measure interior leakage can be used with the conventional GText to

 separately identify, compare, and model leakage flows in units. The different levels of
 effort required to make such measurements should be balanced against the potential
 reduction in air leaks that impact energy use and IAQ. These issues are discussed in
 Section 2.4.

 Questions have been raised as to whether it is worthwhile to use the methods
 developed in this study to explicitly measure exterior and interior leak rates and use
 multipoint measurements when they require substantially more time than measurement
 of compartmentalized air leakage.

 One consideration is how prevalent the cases are in which the compartmentalized test
 does not reveal whether there are relatively large levels of either exterior or interior
 leaks. One study that helps inform the need to further examine this issue was
 conducted by Bohac et al. (2020). Interpreting the data presented in Figure 75 of that
 work, it is observed that for 68% of the buildings, there is either significantly higher
 exterior leakage rates (37% of the cases), or that interior leakage rates are significantly
 larger than the compartmentalized result (32% of the cases). (These results are similar
 to the result shown in Figure 3 of this work.) These exterior and interior leak rate
 variations were on average double the variation in the measured compartmentalized
 leak rate. In only 32% of the buildings evaluated did the compartmentalized test
 accurately represent both the exterior and interior leak rate levels. In Bohac et al.’s
 study, there were more than 200 units in 20 buildings that were built in the 2012–2015
 time frame in colder climates of Illinois, Minnesota, Iowa, Washington, Oregon, and
 Michigan. While these tests are limited both geographically and in number, they do
 suggest that under current building practices, using more detailed exterior and interior
 leakage rate measurements could play a significant role in minimizing energy loss and
 improving IAQ.

 The Center for Energy and Environment field measurements suggest that there is a
 significant opportunity to use the advanced tests presented in this report to help enable
 builders to significantly improve leak performance by providing better direction about
 which units have leak problems and what type of leak they have. Thus, there is a need
 to evaluate whether the additional labor cost (which might require approximately a day
 more labor relative to the current level of effort of 4 hours) is a reasonable cost to pay
 relative to the better building performance that can be achieved with the insight from
 more detailed testing.

 There are several reasons why multipoint testing may be preferred over single-point
 testing:

 • Most practically, a multipoint test allows calculation of a flow exponent in the
 power law flow equation, and there is an expected range of flow exponents for
 tests that are performed with good test setup and in good conditions. Theoretical
 limits on the flow exponent value are 0.5 for a sharp-edged orifice and 1.0 for a
 perfect nozzle. A typical range is between 0.55 and 0.75. If a flow exponent is
 outside the typical range, a practitioner should look for problems with the building
 setup, test setup, and/or test conditions.
 • The general understanding is that lower flow exponents indicate more sharp-
 edged openings such as open windows, while higher flow exponents indicate
 more long leakage paths such as plumbing vents and conduits or just long
 narrow paths through interstitial spaces. Knowing the flow exponent can help

U.S. Department of Energy | Office of Critical Minerals and Energy Innovation                    34

 identify the types of leaks that are occurring, which can expedite additional air
 sealing to improve test results. In addition, some types of air leakage paths can
 potentially cause a step change in the leakage result. Examples could be an
 unlatched window or door suddenly opening when a certain pressure is reached,
 or a mechanical system damper that opens above a certain pressure. (See
 Section 4 for our research results that relate the leak coefficient to pressure
 exponential n and type of leak.)
 Though rarely put into practice currently, more advanced predictions of building airflow
 performance with software such as CONTAM can take advantage of knowing the power
 law flow exponent coefficients and exponents to more accurately predict airflow under
 typical building operating conditions.

 2.5 Value of Multipoint Tests in Comparison to Single-Point Measurements
 Using a multipoint test to determine a building zone’s values of pressure exponent, n,
 and leakage coefficient C, also reflect whether the measurement is reasonably accurate
 and if the testing conditions were reasonable, because the relationship between flow
 rate and pressure is expected to follow a power law, and the measured pressure
 exponents should be in the range of 0.5 < n < 1.0 to be physically reasonable. Poor
 testing procedure is indicated in the plot of log Q versus log P if there is a poor linear fit
 between the two, characterized by a poor R2 coefficient. In addition, the value of
 determining the value of n is that it also reflects the type of leak, which is useful in
 remediation efforts as shown in Section 4.1 and Section 4.2).

 For single-point tests, although there is no explicit metric to infer measurement
 reasonableness, the measurement error can be minimized if repeated measurements of
 single-point tests are made to reduce the chance of error. We estimate that the
 additional work for repeated single-point tests is 2 minutes for repeating the test 10
 times.

 Using a multipoint test and tests for both exterior and newly developed interior leaks
 provides deeper insight into the nature of the leaks (see Section 4) as well as where the
 leaks are located (see Figure 1). This information is particularly valuable if the
 measured building leaks are not meeting the desired air leakage standard (e.g., passive
 house or state code requirement, outlined in Sections 1.2 through 1.4).

 In addition, using these tools to identify leaks and addressing them can result in
 significantly better performance in terms of energy, comfort, and air quality. Although
 Table 1 shows that multipoint and interior/exterior tests can involve greater time, labor,
 and cost (ranging from 1.7 to 3.3 times greater), in many cases the additional
 measurement cost and remediation can have paybacks on the order of 1‒2 years. Cost
 for the range of options shown in Table 1, range from the single-point whole-building
 test for 30 units at $225, to the 11 units measuring the guarded exterior and interior
   leaks at $1,500.

 2.6 Summary of New Test Methods
 Two new leak measurement methods were developed and field tested. GTint was
 developed to directly measure the leakage through the entire interior boundary of an
 attached dwelling unit in a multifamily building. This test can be conducted using
 commonly available blower door test equipment and can be performed for both single-
 point and multipoint methods to find compliance leak flow rates and power law leakage
 characteristics of interior boundaries. While conventional methods require two different
 tests (compartmentalized and GText) to calculate the interior leakage, this direct
 measurement method reduces the workload and uncertainty associated with
 propagation of testing errors for two tests. Together with the conventional GText, this
 gives the means to directly measure both exterior and interior leakage of attached
 dwelling units, which is important in understanding the leakage impacts on energy, IAQ,
 comfort, safety, and durability.

 In addition, the ZMPT method was developed to directly measure and separately
 identify the power law leak characteristics of both exterior and interior boundaries of an
 attached dwelling unit of a multifamily building using a single test. Conventional
 methods require two tests to identify them separately. ZMPT takes less time and needs
 fewer measurements but can still provide power law leakage characteristics that can be
 used to extrapolate measurement results to find leaks at natural low pressures in
 buildings.

 The team conducted testing in 65 units in different residential buildings including old
 brownstone buildings, existing university dormitory buildings, and newly constructed
 apartment buildings. These test results were compared against each other in terms of
 information provided, limitations, time requirement, and the number of measurements
 required. Conventional whole-building test and GText can measure exterior boundary
 leakage of a building and an attached unit, respectively, but cannot measure the interior
 leakage. While the compartmentalization method can measure the combined exterior
 and interior leakage, it cannot separately identify them. The newly developed GTint can
 directly measure interior leakage, and the ZMPT can separately identify both exterior
 and interior leakages simultaneously. Time and effort required for the new GTint is
 similar to the conventional GText but more than the compartmentalization test. The
 ZMPT time requirement and number of measurements needed is about 30% less than
 performing two multipoint tests to identify exterior and interior leakages.

 Testing also revealed the importance of consistently treating the flow direction when
 conducting tests, calculating leak flows (such as in the deduction method to calculate
 interior leakage), and comparing results between different tests. Results show
 inconsistencies up to 14% when incorrect flow directions are used for comparison and
 flow-balance test.

 3 Error Analysis
 Section 2 introduced two new air leakage test methods, their setup, and preliminary
 experimental results. We next evaluate their accuracy and reliability using a focused
 error analysis, including the trade-offs of using single-point versus multipoint testing; the
 error between scaling low-pressure single-point tests and desired test pressure; and an
 analysis of errors between different testing methods based on flow balance.

 3.1 Single-Point Versus Multipoint Testing: Errors When Assuming n =
 0.65
 In current energy codes such as IECC 2021, as well as standards such as from Passive
 House Institute US, Inc. (PHIUS), the requirement for leak test measurement is based
 on a CFM50 value, 0.3 CFM50/ft2 for code and 0.06 CFM50/ft2 for passive house based
 on enclosure area. This gives testers the option of performing a single-point test in the
 building or dwelling unit to check compliance rather than performing the more detailed
 but also more time-consuming and labor-intensive multipoint testing. However,
 ANSI/RESNET/ICC 380 and 301 standards (2019) for measuring and modeling
 airtightness encourage multipoint testing by making the single-point test results carry a
 10% penalty or safety buffer when used in energy calculations.

 In this context, it is important to discuss the use of single-point versus multipoint testing
 and the implications on errors when using the single-point data and an assumed
 pressure exponent of n = 0.65 to extrapolate to find natural pressure leakage. Is it worth
 doing multipoint testing with additional time and effort to improve the accuracy of
 results? What additional benefits would multipoint testing have?

 As discussed in Section 2.1.2, when single-point testing is used, a pressure exponent of
 0.65 is assumed when using the measured high-pressure flow rate to extrapolate to find
 low-pressure flow rates using Eq. 4. Previous studies have found that the average value
 of n is close to 0.65 (Walker et al. 2013) and justifies this assumption. Figure 8 and
 Figure 10 show the distribution of the value of n for old and new buildings for both
 exterior and interior boundaries (as well as Figures 20 and 21). These data support the
 assumption of an average value of n = 0.65; however, the difference this can make
 when calculating low-pressure leaks should be evaluated.

 Figure 15 shows the calculated percentage for low-pressure flow calculation using
 assumed n = 0.65 against several actual n values for different extrapolated pressures
 using Eq. 4 to calculate the low-pressure flow rates. If the actual value of n is also 0.65
 (or close to 0.65), the extrapolation difference is zero (green line). The difference

 airflow through building cracks behaves under different pressure conditions and
 geometries, with implications for both measurement accuracy and modeling.

 4.2 Experimental Evaluation of Airflow Through Crack Types: Implications

 Our testing results for old and new buildings show that the power law exponent values
 from multipoint testing vary between 0.55 and 0.75 with an average value around 0.67.
 This raises the question of what causes these variations in building testing and what the
 relationship is between the exponent and the type of leak paths. In addition, what is the
 relationship of the flow coefficient because both the coefficient, C, and the exponent, n,
 are highly correlated? Is it reasonable to use the power law results to identify the
 type of leak paths to use in air-sealing efforts? In typical airflow modeling in

   1975; Sherman 1992).

 4.2.1 Flow Model Dependency on Testing Conditions and Crack Geometry
 One objective of this research study was to understand the connection between power
 law results and the characteristics of the leak path. For the different crack types tested,
 the power law exponent and coefficient were calculated in a similar way to a typical
 building testing by fitting a least square power law fit (linearized version using logarithm
 scale). This critical dimension is useful when deciding which air-sealing methods are
 most appropriate, especially new technologies such as aerosol sealing where the
 method’s effectiveness depends on the size of the crack.

 Smaller cross-sectional diameters/slot gaps lead to higher values for the
 exponent. However, the depth of the crack also affects the value of n. Longer
 depths lead to more wetted surface area, leading to higher friction flow resistance and
 higher values for n for the same cross-sectional dimensions. Therefore, the behavior of
 n depends on both the cross-sectional dimensions and the depth that can be captured
 by the aspect ratio of depth divided by the diameter or slot gap, as seen from Figure 23.

 4.2.2 Are Power Law Characteristics (n and C) Constants for a Specific
 Structure?
 Multipoint testing is typically done for elevated pressures, and ASTM standard
 recommends measuring flow rates between 10 and 60 Pa for residential buildings. The
 results are then used to extrapolate to find low-pressure leaks and parameters such as
 effective leakage area at 4 Pa. This raises the question of whether the measured
 leakage parameters at high pressures are valid for low pressures. In other words, are
 the measured leak parameters, power law pressure exponent, and flow coefficient
 constant for any pressure?

 To evaluate this, leak flow rate through panels was measured for a wide range of
 pressures (between 5 and 90 Pa), and the results were fitted with a power law model
 using a linearized logarithm scale. Figure 24 shows the measured pressure-flow data
 and power law fit for the 1/16-in. gap and 2-in.-deep slots, which includes the results for
 the three different pressure subranges chosen. Separate linear fits were performed for
 each range to show that the values of C and n change for different pressure ranges. In
 terms of the exponent, larger pressures are associated with lower n values, and this is
 consistent with the expected flow conditions, with a higher Reynolds number pushing
 the flow regime to be less laminar and thus driving the n value close to that of orifice or
 turbulent flow of 0.5. Similar behavior is seen for cracks with different dimensions, as
 well as for two panels in parallel (Jayarathne, Browne, and Gevelber 2024).

 This means that the measured C and n values are not constant but depend on the
 flow conditions tested. This is an important finding, and if present in building testing, it
 would mean that results measured at high pressures are different from the actual
 building conditions at low pressures. However, this is only a single type of crack.
 Buildings have many different cracks both in series and in parallel, and the combination
 of different leaks could behave differently from a single type of crack.

 4.2.3 How Do Power Law Results Change With Tested Flow Conditions?

 pressure-flow conditions. Flow conditions can be represented using the Reynolds
 number, where higher Reynolds numbers (>10,000) typically relate to turbulent flow,
 lower Reynolds numbers (<2,500) relate to laminar flow, and the values in between
 relate to mixed flow. For the tested panels, the Reynolds number can be calculated
 using the measured flow rate, total area of the cracks (to find the flow velocity), and the
 hydraulic diameter (diameter for holes, two times the gap for slots) for each pressure-
 flow pair measured, where the Reynolds number = (V·D/ν), where V is the characteristic
 flow velocity, D is the diameter, and ν is the kinematic viscosity.

 4.3 Summary of Leak Flow Behavior
 Field testing results showed a variety of values for power law leakage parameters for
 different buildings and motivated the experimental study of measuring leak flow through
 different engineered cracks to analyze how the behavior of leak flow and leakage
 parameters varied with crack geometry and flow conditions. Leakage experiments for a
 wide range of crack structures showed that power law parameters (pressure exponent
 and leakage coefficient) can be scaled depending on both the crack geometry (crack
 area and depth of the crack impacts the resistance to flow) and tested flow conditions
 (Reynolds number). This behavior was previously observed for short capillary tubes
 (Kreith and Eisenstadt 1957). However, the research presented here significantly
 expands the types of flow paths tested for a wider range of geometries with a range of
 aspect ratios between 0.13 and 344 and different shapes including straight holes,
 double-bend holes, and slots.

U.S. Department of Energy | Office of Critical Minerals and Energy Innovation                            54

 These results imply that power law C and n values are not constant but depend
 on the tested pressure conditions for a given crack structure. When presenting
 testing results, the tested pressure conditions should be specified because
 results would be different for other pressure conditions.

 The experimental study of engineered cracks was supplemented by testing in two units
 in two buildings where leak flows were measured at a wide range of pressures (see
 Sections 3.8.2 and 6.5 of Jayarathne [2023]). It was found that power law leakage
 parameters for exterior and interior boundaries depend on the tested pressure
 conditions, similar to what was seen in single crack types. This implies that results
 from testing conducted at elevated pressures could be different from leaks in
 buildings at natural low-pressure conditions and that they should be
 distinguished when reporting and using results for flow and energy modeling.
 The error in flow rate calculation can be substantial, on the order of 24%–48% (see
   Section 6.5.2 of Jayarathne [2023]).

 Multipoint testing, including low-pressure measurements, is recommended over single-
 point testing to provide calculation of power law C and n values that allows for a more
 accurate extrapolation to find leaks at low pressures. However, these C and n values
 were found to vary with different pressure-flow conditions in the limited field testing
 performed.

 Results from a database of 63 building tests were analyzed (see Sections 3.6 and 6.6 in
 Jayarathne [2023]) in terms of the values of normalized leakage coefficient (𝐶𝐶̃ ), pressure
 exponent (n), and the normalized leak flow rate (CFM/ft2), which showed that lower n
 values are associated with leaky boundaries. Buildings and boundaries with the same
 normalized leakage (CFM50/ft2) at 50 Pa can have a significant difference (up to 30%)
 in the flow rate at low pressures (CFM5/ft2) because of having different 𝐶𝐶̃ and n values.

 Both single-point and multipoint testing are allowed for code compliance, with the
 results from single-point testing being subjected to an adjustment factor according to
 ANSI/RESNET/ICC 380. However, many software applications, such as CBECC used
 for code compliance in California through the performance path, use only the CFM50
 value and use an assumed pressure exponent to calculate leakage at natural low-
 pressure conditions. Energy modeling software such as EnergyPlus® and air and
 contaminant transfer modeling software such as CONTAM can use leakage coefficient
 and pressure exponent values to more accurately model air leakage in natural pressure
 conditions. Improving energy and flow modeling software to use C and n values is
 important to promote the use of multipoint testing that estimates low-pressure leakage
 more accurately.

 5 Summary and Conclusions
 This research focused on evaluating methods of measuring air leakage in buildings,
 measurement methods, understanding the behavior of leak flow through cracks, and
 how these inform building testing and interpreting results. Air leaks in buildings, both
 through exterior boundaries and among different zones in the building (interior leakage)
 affect energy consumption, IAQ, occupant comfort (odor and sound), fire safety, and
 building durability (because of moisture transfer and deposition). Field testing revealed
 that often the leakage characteristics of exterior and interior boundaries are different
 within the same building. Thus, it is important to separately measure and understand
 leakage through both exterior and interior boundaries. Under this study, 65 different
 dwelling units in 20 buildings—including newly constructed apartment buildings, old
 brownstone buildings, and existing university dormitory buildings—were tested to
 understand and compare different conventional and newly developed pressurization
 testing methods used to measure air leakage in buildings. This gave a better
 understanding of typical airtightness levels, time, effort, information, and limitations for
 different testing methods—and motivated the development of two new test methods.

 5.1 Conventional Versus New Air Leakage Measurement Methods
 A key limitation in conventional testing methods is the lack of a measurement method to
 directly measure interior boundary air leakage in an attached zone.
 Compartmentalization tests are fast and easy to implement but cannot distinguish
 between interior and exterior leaks. Guarded tests offer greater specificity but require
 multiple steps and careful pressure balancing. The newly developed GTint can directly
 measure interior boundary leakage using existing blower door equipment, reducing the
 need for indirect calculations. The newly developed ZMPT method goes further by
 directly measuring exterior and interior leakages simultaneously from a single test.
 Compared to both GText and GTint, ZMPT reduced time and effort by about 30% while
 delivering comparable accuracy and added insight into leakage characteristics.

 Both GTint and ZMPT methods were successfully implemented in a range of buildings,
 including new apartment complexes, brownstones, and dormitories. These methods
 were adaptable to different layouts and construction styles, including common-entry and
 garden-style configurations. Field tests showed that both new methods performed
 reliably and produced results consistent with conventional methods, validating their
 practical feasibility for real-world scenarios.

 5.2 Leak Flow Behavior
 To develop a deeper understanding of the relation between crack structure and the
 power law model 𝑄𝑄 = 𝐶𝐶∆𝑃𝑃𝑛𝑛 leak characteristics, an experimental study was conducted
 for different crack types under different flow conditions. These experiments showed that
 normalized flow coefficient 𝐶𝐶̃ and pressure exponent n are not constants but depend on
 both the crack geometry and tested flow conditions. Analysis of results for 63 whole-
 building tests performed in low-rise and midrise apartment buildings revealed a
 correlation between the normalized leakage coefficients and the corresponding

 pressure exponents, where leakier boundaries were correlated with small exponent
 values.

 This variability has important implications for interpreting test results and underscores
 the need to control and document test conditions carefully, especially when comparing
 results across buildings or estimating performance under natural pressure conditions.

 5.3 Real-World Implications
 By using the test methods developed in this study, energy raters can more broadly
 evaluate the air leakage in multifamily buildings, providing measurements of both the
 individual unit leakage to the outside and leakage between dwelling units. With this
 actionable information, targeted air sealing can not only reduce energy use, but also
 improve IAQ, reduce odors and noises, control moisture transmission, and improve fire
 safety. In addition, information is provided to help raters identify the nature of the leaks,
 which can be useful for identifying appropriate air-sealing methods. This work also
 makes important contributions to the understanding of airflow leakage physics and
 modeling. Testing of various buildings over a wide range of pressures also revealed that
 the model parameters―leak coefficients and pressure exponents―vary with pressure.
 Thus, model estimates of energy loss for actual operating conditions (typically low
 pressure) might differ from those predicted by high-pressure tests.
