Microscopic Theory of the Phonon Thermal Hall Effect in Chiral Mott Insulators
Junha Kang, Taekoo Oh

Abstract
The thermal Hall effect (THE) probes charge-neutral excitations in insulators, where the charge gap blocks electronic transport. Recently, phonons have been shown to induce a THE comparable in magnitude to the spin contribution, underscoring their critical role in thermal transport. Here, we develop a microscopic theory of the phonon thermal Hall effect (PTHE) in chiral Mott insulators. First, we derive the analytic form of the effective Raman interaction in half-filled Mott insulators, showing that its strength is directly proportional to the scalar spin chirality. Next, we demonstrate the intrinsic PTHE explicitly on the kagome lattice. Crucially, our formulation reveals a temperature-dependent crossover in the transport behavior under isotopic substitution. Using this result, we establish a scaling law that quantitatively separates the phonon contribution to the THE from other background signals. Our results not only provide the first fully microscopic derivation of the PTHE, but also establish a definitive experimental standard for isolating microscopic heat carriers in chiral Mott insulators.

In this work, we present a microscopic theory of the PTHE in general chiral Mott insulators.
First, we derive the analytic form of the emergent gauge field in half-filled Mott insulators for arbitrary lattices, which acts as an effective Raman spin-lattice interaction.

Our study provides the first microscopic derivation of the effective Raman interaction in chiral Mott insulators, and establishes a comprehensive experimental roadmap for identifying the microscopic origin of the THE in these systems.

Isotope effect and scaling law.—

In chiral Mott insulators, the total transverse thermal transport
\kappa_{\mu\nu}^{tot}=\kappa_{\mu\nu}^{ph}+\kappa_{\mu\nu}^{rest}~(\mu\neq\nu)
is a sum of the phonon contribution and other signals, primarily from magnons
37
.
To isolate the pure phonon response, we turn to isotopic substitution.
This probe alters the lattice dynamics without affecting mass-independent backgrounds.

Isotopic substitution (
M_{0}\rightarrow M_{r}M_{0}
, with relative mass
M_{r}>1
) modifies the phonon properties in two competing ways.
First, the kinematic softening of the dynamical matrix lowers the phonon frequencies (
\omega\sim M_{r}^{-1/2}
).
Second, the emergent gauge field is suppressed by the heavier mass, as it is rescaled by the mass of the kinematic momentum (
F^{em}\propto M_{r}^{-1}
) [see Eq. (
3
)], thereby reducing the phonon Berry curvature.

This dichotomy causes an opposite isotopic mass dependence of the phonon thermal Hall conductivity
|\kappa_{\mu\nu}^{ph}|
depending on the temperature regime.
We demonstrate this in Fig.
2

|\kappa_{xy}^{ph}|
obtained at low (

) temperatures as a function of
M_{r}

At low temperatures, where the small thermal occupation bottlenecks the transport, the softened phonon dispersion increases the thermal population of the Berry curvature hotspots, thereby enhancing
|\kappa_{xy}^{ph}|
.
Conversely, at high temperatures where these modes are sufficiently populated, the dominant effect is the suppression of the phonon Berry curvature, causing a reduction in
|\kappa_{xy}^{ph}|
.

Discussion.—

In summary, we have rigorously established that the scalar spin chirality governs the microscopic spin-lattice coupling in half-filled Mott insulators.
Beyond the quantitative microscopic prediction of the intrinsic PTHE, the isotopic scaling law [Eq. (
15
)] delivers a practical diagnostic toolkit for experimentally isolating the phononic heat carriers.
Although the scaling describes the intermediate-to-high temperature regime, a phononic response can still be isolated from the magnon contribution in the low temperature regime.
When a strong magnetic field is applied, the
SU(2)
symmetry is broken and a Goldstone mass gap is introduced in the magnon spectrum.
Consequently, the magnon thermal Hall conductivity is suppressed via the thermal depletion of low-energy modes
50
, while the phonon signal mostly remains.

Crucially, our microscopic derivation reveals qualitative differences from conventional phenomenological Raman interactions
\alpha_{R}{\mathbf{M}}\cdot({\mathbf{u}}\times{\mathbf{P}})
.
First, the conventional and emergent field-induced Raman interactions exhibit opposite high-field behaviors.
Because the conventional Raman interaction couples to the bulk magnetization, the resulting PTHE saturates at extreme magnetic fields.
In contrast, when a massive external field forces the spins into a collinear polarized state, the scalar spin chirality vanishes.
Consequently, the emergent gauge field vanishes, and the associated PTHE collapses.
Second, the isotope effects for the two Raman interactions are different.
While the emergent field is suppressed by heavier isotopic masses (
\propto M_{r}^{-1}
), the mass scaling of the conventional Raman interaction is governed by the mass dependence of the material-specific phenomenological coupling constant
\alpha_{R}
.
These distinct scaling behaviors could serve as a discriminator between the mechanisms.
Despite these differences, however, the two mechanisms share an identical form
1
;
42
;
74
;
53
of the transport integral [Eq. (
10
)].
Hence, the isotopic scaling ansatz [Eq. (
15
)] remains a ubiquitous tool to separate the phonon signal from other contributions beyond Mott insulators.

Next, we suggest the material candidates.
For immediate 2D experimental realization, we propose iron jarosites
18
;
37
due to their kagome geometry, large canting angles, and high Néel temperatures.
Extending this framework to 3D, we anticipate a massive intrinsic PTHE in pyrochlore antiferromagnets, where the non-coplanar geometry naturally guarantees a macroscopic emergent gauge field
36
.
We also note that
[F^{em}_{ab}]^{(3)}
in Eq. (
8
) can be realized in the loop-current physics historically explored in cuprate Mott insulators
26
;
65
;
10
;
61
;
62
;
16
;
66
;
3
;

;
8
;
9
.

Finally, our microscopic formulation quantifies and generalizes the insights of recent studies
47
;
48
, which identified the scalar spin chirality as the driver of the extrinsic PTHE via skew-scattering in frustrated magnets.
Because the analytic formulation of the emergent field derived here does not require translational symmetry and is dimension independent, it provides a universal platform for evaluating both intrinsic and extrinsic PTHE across arbitrary lattice geometries.
Consequently, our work opens a compelling theoretical direction for thermal transport studies in strongly correlated phases.

S1
Born-Oppenheimer approximation

In this Appendix, we provide the derivation of the effective Born-Oppenheimer (BO) Hamiltonian presented in the main text.
Let
{\mathbf{r}}_{nu}=({\mathbf{r}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{r}}_{N_{nu}})
denote the collection of all nuclear positions in
d
spatial dimensions.
A general Hamiltonian describing the coupled system of interacting electrons and the nuclei is given by

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{nu}}\frac{(\hat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{i}-Z_{i}e{\mathbf{A}}^{ext}_{i})^{2}}{2M_{i}}+\hat{H}_{el}({\mathbf{r}}_{nu})=\hat{K}_{nu}+\hat{H}_{el}({\mathbf{r}}_{nu}),

(S1)

where
\hat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{i}^{\mu}=\frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial r_{i}^{\mu}}~(\mu=x,y,\dots)
is the canonical nuclear momentum operator,
M_{i}
and
Z_{i}e
are the mass and effective charge of the
i
-th nucleus
(Z_{i}\in\mathbb{Z})
, respectively, and
{\mathbf{A}}^{ext}_{i}={\mathbf{A}}^{ext}({\mathbf{r}}_{i})
is the external electromagnetic vector potential evaluated at
{\mathbf{r}}_{i}
.
The electronic Hamiltonian,
\hat{H}_{el}({\mathbf{r}}_{nu})
, is parametrically dependent on the nuclear coordinates
{\mathbf{r}}_{nu}
.
It incorporates the single-particle electronic kinetic energy, electron-electron interactions, and the Coulomb interactions between all the electrons and the nuclei.
Note that we do not require the single-particle electronic energy to be a strictly quadratic function of the electronic momentum, thereby allowing quite general forms including relativistic corrections.

The Born-Oppenheimer approximation assumes that the substantial mass difference between electrons and nuclei allows their dynamics to be adiabatically separated.
Thus, we assume an ansatz for the total system wave function

\displaystyle|\Psi_{BO}({\mathbf{r}}_{nu})\rangle=\Psi_{nu}({\mathbf{r}}_{nu})|\Psi_{el}({\mathbf{r}}_{nu})\rangle.

(S2)

Here,
|\Psi_{el}({\mathbf{r}}_{nu})\rangle
is defined as the instantaneous ground state of the electronic Hamiltonian at a fixed nuclear configuration, satisfying the eigenvalue equation

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{el}({\mathbf{r}}_{nu})|\Psi_{el}({\mathbf{r}}_{nu})\rangle=U_{el}({\mathbf{r}}_{nu})|\Psi_{el}({\mathbf{r}}_{nu})\rangle.

(S3)

The Born-Oppenheimer wave function is then obtained by minimizing the variational energy
\langle\Psi_{BO}({\mathbf{r}}_{nu})|\hat{H}|\Psi_{BO}({\mathbf{r}}_{nu})\rangle
.

The effective Hamiltonian governing the nuclear dynamics is obtained by integrating out the electronic degrees of freedom as
\hat{H}_{BO}\Psi_{nu}({\mathbf{r}}_{nu})=\langle\Psi_{el}({\mathbf{r}}_{nu})|\hat{H}|\Psi_{BO}({\mathbf{r}}_{nu})\rangle
, where the nuclear momentum operates on the total wave function.
Upon direct evaluation, one finds that the derivatives acting on
|\Psi_{el}({\mathbf{r}}_{nu})\rangle
shift the nuclear momentum, resulting in

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{BO}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{nu}}\frac{(\hat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{i}-{\mathbf{A}}_{i})^{2}}{2M_{i}}+\hat{V}_{BO}({\mathbf{r}}_{nu}),\quad{\mathbf{A}}_{i}=Z_{i}e{\mathbf{A}}_{i}^{ext}+\hbar{\mathbf{A}}^{em}_{i}

(S4)

Here, the emergent gauge (Berry) potential is a real-valued connection given by

\displaystyle A^{em}_{i\mu}\equiv i\langle\Psi_{el}({\mathbf{r}}_{nu})|\frac{\partial\Psi_{el}({\mathbf{r}}_{nu})}{\partial r_{i}^{\mu}}\rangle.

(S5)

The corresponding Born-Oppenheimer potential energy,
\hat{V}_{BO}({\mathbf{r}}_{nu})
, includes both the electronic ground state energy and an emergent geometric scalar potential arising from the second-order derivatives of the electronic wave function

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{BO}({\mathbf{r}}_{nu})=U_{el}({\mathbf{r}}_{nu})+\sum_{i=1}^{N_{nu}}\sum_{\mu=1}^{d}\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2M_{i}}\Big(\langle\frac{\partial\Psi_{el}({\mathbf{r}}_{nu})}{\partial r_{i}^{\mu}}|\frac{\partial\Psi_{el}({\mathbf{r}}_{nu})}{\partial r_{i}^{\mu}}\rangle+\langle\Psi_{el}({\mathbf{r}}_{nu})|\frac{\partial\Psi_{el}({\mathbf{r}}_{nu})}{\partial r_{i}^{\mu}}\rangle^{2}\Big).

(S6)

In the following sections, we adopt natural units by setting
\hbar=c=1
.
We will explicitly restore these constants only when required for quantitative, material-specific calculations.

S2
Analytic form of the emergent gauge field

A central object that governs the phonon dynamics is the emergent gauge field
F^{em}
.
In this Appendix, we present the detailed derivation of the analytic form presented in the main text.
In Sec.
S2.1
, we introduce the double exchange model and express the emergent gauge field in terms of the electronic ground state projector.
Next, in Sec.
S2.2
, we introduce a perturbative expansion for the projectors.
In Sec.
S2.3
, we apply this projector perturbation theory to prove the formula for
F^{em}
utilized in the main text.

S2.1
Definition and projector formula

We begin by defining the electronic environment.
We consider a double exchange model at half-filling on a general lattice of
N_{nu}
sites in the strong Hund’s coupling regime.
The electronic Hamiltonian is given by

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{el}({\mathbf{r}}_{nu})=-J\sum_{i}{\mathbf{n}}_{i}\cdot\hat{{\mathbf{S}}}_{i}+\sum_{ij\sigma}t_{ij}({\mathbf{r}}_{nu})\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i\sigma}\hat{c}_{j\sigma},\quad\hat{S}_{i}^{\mu}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ss^{\prime}}\sigma_{ss^{\prime}}^{\mu}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{is}\hat{c}_{is^{\prime}},

(S7)

where
\hat{c}_{i\sigma}
is the electron annihilation operator at site
i
with spin
\sigma
,
{\mathbf{n}}_{i}
is a unit vector representing the local magnetization axis, and
J>0
is the Hund coupling.
We set
t_{ii}=0
and allow for complex hopping amplitudes
t_{ij}
.
This model is completely general regarding spatial dimension and lattice geometry.
Note that
t_{ij}({\mathbf{r}}_{nu})
depends parametrically on the nuclear positions
{\mathbf{r}}_{nu}
; this label will henceforth be omitted for notational simplicity.

Let us denote the single-particle matrix representation of
\hat{H}_{el}
as
H_{el}
.
This
2N_{nu}\times 2N_{nu}
matrix is characterized by the eigenvalue equation
H_{el}{\mathbf{v}}_{n}=E_{n}{\mathbf{v}}_{n}
, with ordered eigenvalues
E_{1}\leq\dots\leq E_{2N_{nu}}
.
At half-filling, the many-body electronic ground state is formed by occupying the lowest
N_{nu}
single-particle states:

\displaystyle|\Psi_{el}\rangle=\prod_{n=1}^{N_{nu}}\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{n}|0\rangle,\quad\Big(\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{n}=\sum_{i\sigma}[{\mathbf{v}}_{n}]_{i\sigma}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i\sigma}\Big),

(S8)

where
|0\rangle
is the fermion vacuum.

Using the emergent gauge potential defined in Eq. (
S5
), we define the corresponding emergent gauge field as its geometric curvature.
Introducing a composite index
a=(i,\mu)
to accommodate both site and spatial indices (
\partial_{a}=\partial/\partial r_{i}^{\mu}
), the emergent gauge field reads

\displaystyle F^{em}_{ab}\equiv\partial_{a}A^{em}_{b}-\partial_{b}A^{em}_{a}=i\Big(\langle\partial_{a}\Psi_{el}|\partial_{b}\Psi_{el}\rangle-\langle\partial_{b}\Psi_{el}|\partial_{a}\Psi_{el}\rangle\Big).

(S9)

Note that the emergent gauge field possesses both intra- and inter-site contributions, contrary to the external gauge field which only has intra-site components.
Furthermore, from
A^{em}_{a}\in\mathbb{R}
and
F^{em}_{ab}=-F^{em}_{ba}
, we readily find that
iF^{em}
is a Hermitian matrix, a property that is highly advantageous when diagonalizing the effective phonon Hamiltonian in Sec.
S3.2
.

Substituting the Slater determinant ground state into Eq. (
S9
), it is straightforward to verify that the emergent gauge potential and curvature reduce to traces over the occupied single-particle states:

\displaystyle A^{em}_{a}

\displaystyle=i\sum_{n=1}^{N_{nu}}{\mathbf{v}}^{\dagger}_{n}\partial_{a}{\mathbf{v}}_{n}=i~Tr[\mathcal{V}^{\dagger}\partial_{a}\mathcal{V}],

\displaystyle F^{em}_{ab}

\displaystyle=i~Tr[\partial_{a}\mathcal{V}^{\dagger}\partial_{b}\mathcal{V}-\partial_{b}\mathcal{V}^{\dagger}\partial_{a}\mathcal{V}]=-2Im[Tr[\partial_{a}\mathcal{V}^{\dagger}\partial_{b}\mathcal{V}]],

(S10)

where
\mathcal{V}=({\mathbf{v}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{v}}_{N_{nu}})
is the
2N_{nu}\times N_{nu}
matrix formed by the single-particle eigenvectors of the occupied states.

To facilitate a perturbative expansion in the strong coupling regime (
|t_{ij}|/J\ll 1
), we prove the following projector formula for the emergent gauge field as presented in the main text:

\displaystyle F^{em}_{ab}=-2Im[Tr[P\partial_{a}P\partial_{b}P]],

(S11)

where
P=\mathcal{V}\mathcal{V}^{\dagger}
is the ground state projector.
We begin by inserting the identity
\mathds{1}=P+(\mathds{1}-P)
into Eq. (
S10
):

\displaystyle F^{em}_{ab}=-2Im[Tr[\partial_{a}\mathcal{V}^{\dagger}P\partial_{b}\mathcal{V}]]-2Im[Tr[\partial_{a}\mathcal{V}^{\dagger}(\mathds{1}-P)\partial_{b}\mathcal{V}]].

(S12)

We can show that the first term vanishes identically.
By substituting
P=\mathcal{V}\mathcal{V}^{\dagger}
, applying the cyclic property of the trace, and using the identity
\mathcal{V}^{\dagger}\mathcal{V}=\mathds{1}_{N_{nu}\times N_{nu}}
(which implies
\partial_{a}(\mathcal{V}^{\dagger}\mathcal{V})=0
), we find

\displaystyle Im[Tr[\partial_{a}\mathcal{V}^{\dagger}P\partial_{b}\mathcal{V}]]

\displaystyle=Im[Tr[\partial_{a}\mathcal{V}^{\dagger}\mathcal{V}\mathcal{V}^{\dagger}\partial_{b}\mathcal{V}]]

\displaystyle=Im[Tr[\mathcal{V}^{\dagger}\partial_{a}\mathcal{V}\partial_{b}\mathcal{V}^{\dagger}\mathcal{V}]]

\displaystyle=Im[Tr[\partial_{b}\mathcal{V}^{\dagger}P\partial_{a}\mathcal{V}]]

\displaystyle=-Im[Tr[\partial_{a}\mathcal{V}^{\dagger}P\partial_{b}\mathcal{V}]]=0.

(S13)

In the last line, we used
Im[Tr[X]]=-Im[Tr[X^{\dagger}]]
.
For the remaining term, we utilize the identities
\mathcal{V}^{\dagger}(\mathds{1}-P)=0
and
(\mathds{1}-P)\mathcal{V}=0
to express the derivatives entirely in terms of the projector:

\displaystyle F^{em}_{ab}

\displaystyle=-2Im[Tr[(\mathcal{V}^{\dagger}\mathcal{V})\partial_{a}\mathcal{V}^{\dagger}(\mathds{1}-P)\partial_{b}\mathcal{V}]]

\displaystyle=-2Im[Tr[\mathcal{V}\partial_{a}\mathcal{V}^{\dagger}(\mathds{1}-P)\partial_{b}\mathcal{V}\mathcal{V}^{\dagger}]]

\displaystyle=-2Im[Tr[(\partial_{a}P-\partial_{a}\mathcal{V}\mathcal{V}^{\dagger})(\mathds{1}-P)(\partial_{b}P-\mathcal{V}\partial_{b}\mathcal{V}^{\dagger})]]

\displaystyle=-2Im[Tr[\partial_{a}P(\mathds{1}-P)\partial_{b}P]].

(S14)

Finally, by utilizing the idempotence of
P
(
P^{2}=P
), we note that

\displaystyle\partial_{a}PP+P\partial_{a}P=\partial_{a}P\Leftrightarrow\partial_{a}P(\mathds{1}-P)=P\partial_{a}P.

(S15)

Substituting this relation into the trace yields Eq. (
S11
).
We note in passing that while we have derived this identity explicitly for the half-filled double exchange model, Eq. (
S11
) is a general property of the Berry curvature for any noninteracting fermionic system described by a single Slater determinant ground state
23
.
Consequently, extracting the analytic form of
F^{em}
is formally reduced to obtaining an analytic expression for the ground state projector
P
.

S2.2
Projector perturbation theory

In this Appendix, we derive a perturbative expansion for the ground state projector by utilizing the Schrieffer-Wolff (SW) transformation.
While we ultimately apply this formalism to the double exchange model, we emphasize that the following approach is completely general.
It can be utilized to track the evolution of any unperturbed eigen-subspace within an arbitrary Hamiltonian, provided that the target subspace remains energetically isolated from the rest of the spectrum upon the introduction of a perturbation.

Consider a general Hamiltonian parametrized by a dimensionless parameter
\lambda\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\lambda}=\mathcal{H}_{0}+\lambda V=\mathcal{H}_{0}+\lambda(V_{d}+V_{o}),

(S16)

where the matrix has been decomposed into its unperturbed part
\mathcal{H}_{0}
and a perturbation
V
.
We further separate the perturbation into block-diagonal (
V_{d}
) and block-off-diagonal (
V_{o}
) components with respect to the unperturbed basis.
In this basis, the matrices take the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{\parallel}&0\\
0&\varepsilon_{\perp}\end{pmatrix},\quad V_{d}=\begin{pmatrix}V_{\parallel}&0\\
0&V_{\perp}\end{pmatrix},\quad V_{o}=\begin{pmatrix}0&W\\
W^{\dagger}&0\end{pmatrix}.

(S17)

Here,
\varepsilon_{\parallel}
and
\varepsilon_{\perp}
are diagonal matrices representing the unperturbed energies of the target subspace and the remainder of the Hilbert space, respectively.
We assume the gap between these two sectors is sufficiently large such that their perturbed eigenvalue spectra remain disjoint for all
\lambda\in[0,1]
.

The goal of the SW transformation is to find an skew-Hermitian generator
S(\lambda)=-S(\lambda)^{\dagger}
that unitarily rotates the Hamiltonian into a block-diagonal form, thereby decoupling the two subspaces:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}=e^{-S(\lambda)}\mathcal{H}_{\lambda}e^{S(\lambda)}=\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{eff}_{\lambda}&0\\
0&*\end{pmatrix},\quad S(\lambda)=\begin{pmatrix}0&T(\lambda)\\
-T(\lambda)^{\dagger}&0\end{pmatrix}.

(S18)

Here,
S(\lambda)
is a smooth function of
\lambda
satisfying
S(0)=0
.
Since this transformation is continuous with respect to the tuning parameter
\lambda
, the spectrum of the upper-left block
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{eff}_{\lambda}
is adiabatically connected to the unperturbed target spectrum
\varepsilon_{\parallel}
.
This explicitly guarantees that
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{eff}_{\lambda}
captures the true physical evolution of the target subspace, preventing the two distinct energetic sectors from mixing as the perturbation is introduced.

Let the eigenvectors of the block-diagonalized Hamiltonian in the target subspace be denoted by
\widetilde{{\mathbf{v}}}_{n}(\lambda)
, satisfying
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}\widetilde{{\mathbf{v}}}_{n}(\lambda)=E_{n}(\lambda)\widetilde{{\mathbf{v}}}_{n}(\lambda)
.
Because
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}
is perfectly block-diagonal, the projector onto this subspace in the rotated frame is simply the unperturbed projector

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}(\lambda)=\sum_{n}\widetilde{{\mathbf{v}}}_{n}(\lambda)\widetilde{{\mathbf{v}}}_{n}^{\dagger}(\lambda)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathds{1}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\equiv P_{0}.

(S19)

The true physical eigenvectors of the original Hamiltonian are given by applying the inverse rotation,
{\mathbf{v}}_{n}(\lambda)=e^{S(\lambda)}\widetilde{{\mathbf{v}}}_{n}(\lambda)
.
Consequently, the true ground state projector evolves according to an inverse SW transformation,

\displaystyle P(\lambda)=e^{S(\lambda)}P_{0}e^{-S(\lambda)}.

(S20)

Thus, determining the generator
S(\lambda)
immediately yields the perturbative expansion for the projector.

To find
S(\lambda)
, we apply the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff (BCH) lemma to expand
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}=\mathcal{H}_{\lambda}+[\mathcal{H}_{\lambda},S(\lambda)]+\frac{1}{2!}[[\mathcal{H}_{\lambda},S(\lambda)],S(\lambda)]+\frac{1}{3!}[[[\mathcal{H}_{\lambda},S(\lambda)],S(\lambda)],S(\lambda)]+\dots.

(S21)

We require
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}
to be block-diagonal, meaning all off-diagonal terms in this expansion must vanish at every order of
\lambda
.
Expanding
S(\lambda)=\lambda S_{1}+\lambda^{2}S_{2}+\lambda^{3}S_{3}+\dots
, substituting
\mathcal{H}_{\lambda}=\mathcal{H}_{0}+\lambda V_{d}+\lambda V_{o}
, and collecting the off-diagonal terms order by order yields the fundamental conditions for the SW generators

\displaystyle[\mathcal{H}_{0},S_{1}]+V_{o}=0,

\displaystyle[\mathcal{H}_{0},S_{2}]+[V_{d},S_{1}]=0,

\displaystyle[\mathcal{H}_{0},S_{3}]+[V_{d},S_{2}]+\frac{1}{2}[[V_{o},S_{1}],S_{1}]+\frac{1}{6}[[[\mathcal{H}_{0},S_{1}],S_{1}],S_{1}]=0.

(S22)

These conditions define
S(\lambda)
up to third order in the perturbation.
By utilizing the analogous BCH expansion for the projector in Eq. (
S20
),

\displaystyle P(\lambda)=P_{0}+[S(\lambda),P_{0}]+\frac{1}{2}[[S(\lambda),P_{0}],S(\lambda)]+\frac{1}{6}[[[S(\lambda),P_{0}],S(\lambda)],S(\lambda)]+\dots,

(S23)

and parameterizing the block-off-diagonal generator as
T(\lambda)=\lambda T_{1}+\lambda^{2}T_{2}+\lambda^{3}T_{3}+\dots
[see Eq. (
S18
)], we obtain the explicit perturbation series
P(\lambda)=P_{0}+\lambda P_{1}+\lambda^{2}P_{2}+\lambda^{3}P_{3}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{4})
.
Evaluating the commutators yields the matrix elements in block form

\displaystyle P_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathds{1}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad P_{1}=-\begin{pmatrix}0&T_{1}\\
T_{1}^{\dagger}&0\end{pmatrix},\quad P_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}-T_{1}T_{1}^{\dagger}&-T_{2}\\
-T_{2}^{\dagger}&T_{1}^{\dagger}T_{1}\end{pmatrix},\quad P_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}-T_{1}T_{2}^{\dagger}-T_{2}T_{1}^{\dagger}&\frac{2}{3}T_{1}T_{1}^{\dagger}T_{1}-T_{3}\\
\frac{2}{3}T_{1}^{\dagger}T_{1}T_{1}^{\dagger}-T_{3}^{\dagger}&T_{1}^{\dagger}T_{2}+T_{2}^{\dagger}T_{1}\end{pmatrix}.

(S24)

We now apply this general formalism to the double exchange model.
We identify the unperturbed Hamiltonian as the local Hund’s coupling,
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}=-J\sum_{i}{\mathbf{n}}_{i}\cdot\hat{{\mathbf{S}}}_{i}
, and the perturbation as the inter-site hopping,
\lambda\hat{V}=\lambda\sum_{ij\sigma}t_{ij}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i\sigma}\hat{c}_{j\sigma}
.
Since a global energy shift does not alter the eigenstates or the projectors, we subtract the single-particle ground state energy
-J/2
to set the unperturbed energy of the half-filled target subspace to zero.
Thus, the unperturbed Hamiltonian matrix takes the simplified form
\mathcal{H}_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&J\mathds{1}\end{pmatrix}
.
Solving the equations in Eq. (
S22
) with this
\mathcal{H}_{0}
yields the generators

\displaystyle T_{1}=\frac{1}{J}W,\quad T_{2}=\frac{1}{J^{2}}(V_{\parallel}W-WV_{\perp}),\quad T_{3}=\frac{1}{J^{3}}\Big(V_{\parallel}^{2}W+WV_{\perp}^{2}-2V_{\parallel}WV_{\perp}-\frac{4}{3}WW^{\dagger}W\Big).

(S25)

Finally, inserting these
T_{n}
matrices into Eq. (
S24
), we obtain the analytic perturbative expansion for the ground state projectors in the strong-coupling limit

\displaystyle P_{0}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mathds{1}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad P_{1}=-\frac{1}{J}\begin{pmatrix}0&W\\
W^{\dagger}&0\end{pmatrix},\quad P_{2}=\frac{1}{J^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}-WW^{\dagger}&WV_{\perp}-V_{\parallel}W\\
V_{\perp}W^{\dagger}-W^{\dagger}V_{\parallel}&W^{\dagger}W\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle P_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{J^{3}}\begin{pmatrix}2WV_{\perp}W^{\dagger}-WW^{\dagger}V_{\parallel}-V_{\parallel}WW^{\dagger}&2WW^{\dagger}W-V_{\parallel}^{2}W-WV_{\perp}^{2}+2V_{\parallel}WV_{\perp}\\
2W^{\dagger}WW^{\dagger}-W^{\dagger}V_{\parallel}^{2}-V_{\perp}^{2}W^{\dagger}+2V_{\perp}W^{\dagger}V_{\parallel}&2W^{\dagger}V_{\parallel}W-W^{\dagger}WV_{\perp}-V_{\perp}W^{\dagger}W\end{pmatrix}.

(S26)

In the next section, we will utilize these explicit projector representations to evaluate the analytic form of the emergent gauge field order by order.

S2.3
Calculation of the emergent gauge field

To obtain the perturbative expansion for the emergent gauge field, we insert the projector series
P(\lambda)=P_{0}+\lambda P_{1}+\lambda^{2}P_{2}+\lambda^{3}P_{3}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{4})
into the geometric formula
F^{em}_{ab}=-2Im[Tr[P\partial_{a}P\partial_{b}P]]
derived in Eq. (
S11
).
Collecting the terms in powers of
\lambda

S3
Dynamical and geometric properties of the phonon system

In this Appendix, we provide a rigorous mathematical formulation of the phonon system in the presence of the emergent gauge field.
In Sec.
S3.1
, we establish the lattice notation, define the coupled phonon Hamiltonian, and explore its underlying momentum space algebraic structure.
Next, in Sec.
S3.2
, we detail the diagonalization procedure for this system, specifically addressing the non-Hermitian nature of the coupled equations of motion.
Finally, in Sec.
S3.3
, we construct the phonon Berry curvature using a generalized multiband projector formalism.
This approach naturally accommodates band degeneracies and provides an efficient framework for numerical evaluation.

S3.1
General definition and algebraic structure

To define the phonon Hamiltonian, we consider a
d
-dimensional (
d
-D) lattice with discrete translational symmetry, subject to periodic boundary conditions (PBC).
We index the unit cells by the lattice vectors
{\mathbf{R}}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}{\mathbf{a}}_{i}~(n_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{N_{i}})
, where
{\mathbf{a}}_{i}
are the primitive lattice vectors, and the integers
N_{i}
define the periodic boundaries of the crystal.
The total number of unit cells is
N_{c}=\prod_{i=1}^{d}N_{i}
.
This structure allows us to uniquely specify any nuclear site using the composite index
({\mathbf{R}},\alpha)
, where
\alpha=1,\dots,N_{s}
denotes the sublattice index within the unit cell.
The equilibrium position of a nucleus is given by
{\mathbf{R}}+\bm{\tau}_{\alpha}
, where
\bm{\tau}_{\alpha}
is the vector connecting the unit cell origin to the sublattice site.

Starting from the effective Born-Oppenheimer dynamics in Eq. (
S4
), the phonon Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{p}
is obtained by expanding the potential energy
\hat{V}_{BO}({\mathbf{r}}_{nu})
up to quadratic order in the nuclear displacements
{\mathbf{u}}_{{\mathbf{R}}\alpha}
around their equilibrium positions, and subtracting the constant equilibrium energy
\hat{V}_{BO}({\mathbf{r}}_{nu}^{eq})
.
To simplify the resulting expressions, we absorb the nuclear masses
M_{\alpha}
by rescaling the operators.
We redefine the displacement
\hat{{\mathbf{u}}}_{{\mathbf{R}}\alpha}\to\hat{{\mathbf{u}}}_{{\mathbf{R}}\alpha}/\sqrt{M_{\alpha}}
, the kinematic momentum
\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{{\mathbf{R}}\alpha}\to\sqrt{M_{\alpha}}\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{{\mathbf{R}}\alpha}
, the gauge connection
{\mathbf{A}}_{{\mathbf{R}}\alpha}\to\sqrt{M_{\alpha}}{\mathbf{A}}_{{\mathbf{R}}\alpha}
, and the gauge field
F_{{\mathbf{R}}\alpha\mu,{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\beta\nu}\to\sqrt{M_{\alpha}M_{\beta}}F_{{\mathbf{R}}\alpha\mu,{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\beta\nu}
.
Upon such transformation, the harmonic phonon Hamiltonian takes the quadratic form

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{p}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\mathbf{R}}\alpha}\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{{\mathbf{R}}\alpha}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\mathbf{R}}{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\alpha\beta\mu\nu}\hat{u}_{{\mathbf{R}}\alpha\mu}D({\mathbf{R}}-{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime})_{\alpha\mu,\beta\nu}\hat{u}_{{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\beta\nu}.

(S62)

The matrix
D
is the dynamical matrix, representing the mass-rescaled second-order spatial derivatives of the Born-Oppenheimer potential.
By definition, the dynamical matrix obeys the symmetries
D({\mathbf{R}})_{\alpha\mu,\beta\nu}=D({\mathbf{R}})_{\alpha\nu,\beta\mu}=D(-{\mathbf{R}})_{\beta\mu,\alpha\nu}
.

The inclusion of the gauge potential fundamentally alters the underlying quantum algebra of the lattice operators.
Since the gauge potential depends on the nuclear coordinates, the kinematic momenta do not commute.
Specifically, the operators obey the following modified commutation relations

\displaystyle[\hat{u}_{{\mathbf{R}}\alpha\mu},\hat{u}_{{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\beta\nu}]=0,\quad[\hat{\pi}_{{\mathbf{R}}\alpha\mu},\hat{u}_{{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\beta\nu}]=-i\delta_{{\mathbf{R}}{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}}\delta_{\alpha\beta}\delta_{\mu\nu},\quad[\hat{\pi}_{{\mathbf{R}}\alpha\mu},\hat{\pi}_{{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\beta\nu}]=iF_{{\mathbf{R}}\alpha\mu,{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\beta\nu}.

(S63)

By definition, the gauge field is antisymmetric upon simultaneously interchanging all the indices
F_{{\mathbf{R}}\alpha\mu,{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\beta\nu}=-F_{{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\beta\nu,{\mathbf{R}}\alpha\mu}
.
Since we consider spatially uniform external magnetic fields and a periodic single-particle electronic Hamiltonian obeying Eq. (
S35
), the total gauge field inherently respects discrete lattice translational symmetry at the equilibrium position [see Sec.
S4.1
for a proof].
Consequently,
F
depends only on the relative distance between unit cells:
F_{{\mathbf{R}}\alpha\mu,{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\beta\nu}=F({\mathbf{R}}-{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime})_{\alpha\mu,\beta\nu}
.

To leverage this discrete translational symmetry, we express the Hamiltonian in the momentum space basis.
We define the reciprocal lattice vectors
{\mathbf{b}}_{j}
such that
{\mathbf{a}}_{i}\cdot{\mathbf{b}}_{j}=2\pi\delta_{ij}
.
The crystal momentum is defined as
{\mathbf{k}}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}k_{i}{\mathbf{b}}_{i}=\sum_{\mu}k_{\mu}\hat{{\mathbf{e}}}_{\mu}
, with
k_{i}=n_{i}/N_{i}~(n_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{N_{i}})
restricted to the first unit cell.
The discrete spatial Fourier transforms of the operators are defined as

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{{\mathbf{R}}\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{c}}}\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}}e^{i{\mathbf{k}}\cdot({\mathbf{R}}+\bm{\tau}_{\alpha})}\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{{\mathbf{k}}\alpha},\quad\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{{\mathbf{k}}\alpha}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{c}}}\sum_{{\mathbf{R}}}e^{-i{\mathbf{k}}\cdot({\mathbf{R}}+\bm{\tau}_{\alpha})}\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{{\mathbf{R}}\alpha},

\displaystyle\hat{{\mathbf{u}}}_{{\mathbf{R}}\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{c}}}\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}}e^{i{\mathbf{k}}\cdot({\mathbf{R}}+\bm{\tau}_{\alpha})}\hat{{\mathbf{u}}}_{{\mathbf{k}}\alpha},\quad\hat{{\mathbf{u}}}_{{\mathbf{k}}\alpha}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{c}}}\sum_{{\mathbf{R}}}e^{-i{\mathbf{k}}\cdot({\mathbf{R}}+\bm{\tau}_{\alpha})}\hat{{\mathbf{u}}}_{{\mathbf{R}}\alpha},

(S64)

where the phase factors explicitly account for the intra-cell sublattice positions
\bm{\tau}_{\alpha}
.

Substituting these Fourier expansions into the real-space equations yields

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{p}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}}\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{-{\mathbf{k}}}h({\mathbf{k}})\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{{\mathbf{k}}},\quad h({\mathbf{k}})=\begin{pmatrix}\mathds{1}&0\\
0&D({\mathbf{k}})\end{pmatrix},

(S65)

where we have grouped the operators into a single multi-component vector
\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{{\mathbf{k}}}=(\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{{\mathbf{k}},1},\dots,\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{{\mathbf{k}},N_{s}},\hat{{\mathbf{u}}}_{{\mathbf{k}},1},\dots,\hat{{\mathbf{u}}}_{{\mathbf{k}},N_{s}})
, which inherently satisfies
\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{-{\mathbf{k}}}=\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{{\mathbf{k}}}^{\dagger}
.
The momentum-space dynamical matrix is defined as
D({\mathbf{k}})_{\alpha\mu,\beta\nu}=\sum_{{\mathbf{R}}}e^{-i{\mathbf{k}}\cdot({\mathbf{R}}+\bm{\tau}_{\alpha}-\bm{\tau}_{\beta})}D({\mathbf{R}})_{\alpha\mu,\beta\nu}
.

By applying the Fourier transformations to the real-space commutation relations and utilizing the translational invariance of
F
, we derive the corresponding momentum-space commutation relations.
These operators obey the compact matrix equation presented in the main text:

\displaystyle[\hat{x}_{{\mathbf{k}}p},\hat{x}_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}q}]=i\delta_{{\mathbf{k}},-{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}}\mathcal{F}({\mathbf{k}})_{pq},\quad\mathcal{F}({\mathbf{k}})=\begin{pmatrix}F({\mathbf{k}})&-\mathds{1}\\
\mathds{1}&0\end{pmatrix},\quad F({\mathbf{k}})_{\alpha\mu,\beta\nu}=\sum_{{\mathbf{R}}}e^{-i{\mathbf{k}}\cdot({\mathbf{R}}+\bm{\tau}_{\alpha}-\bm{\tau}_{\beta})}F({\mathbf{R}})_{\alpha\mu,\beta\nu}

(S66)

where we have introduced a composite index
p
for
\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{{\mathbf{k}}}
.
Note that one must multiply
\hbar/\sqrt{M_{\alpha}M_{\beta}}
to the gauge field expression in Eq. (
S36
) to recover the standard units when performing numerical calculations.

From the underlying real-space symmetries of
D
and
F
, it directly follows that the momentum-space matrices satisfy:

\displaystyle h(-{\mathbf{k}})^{T}=h({\mathbf{k}}),\quad\mathcal{F}(-{\mathbf{k}})^{T}=-\mathcal{F}({\mathbf{k}}).

(S67)

Furthermore, we note that the full real-space dynamical matrix
D
—the massive
N_{c}N_{s}d\times N_{c}N_{s}d
matrix with entries
D_{{\mathbf{R}}\alpha\mu,{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\beta\nu}
—is positive semidefinite due to the lattice stability at equilibrium.
Due to the translational invariance of the lattice, this large matrix can be block-diagonalized into distinct momentum sectors, rendering it unitarily equivalent to the direct sum
\oplus_{{\mathbf{k}}}D({\mathbf{k}})
.
Consequently, the spectrum of the full real-space matrix is simply the union of the spectra of
D({\mathbf{k}})
across all discrete momentum points.
Since the parent real-space matrix is positive semidefinite, it strictly follows that
D({\mathbf{k}})
must be positive semidefinite for every momentum
{\mathbf{k}}
.
These symmetry properties and structural features of
\mathcal{F}({\mathbf{k}})
and
h({\mathbf{k}})
form the mathematical foundation required to diagonalize the phonon Hamiltonian, which we address in the following section.

S3.2
Diagonalization of the phonon Hamiltonian

We diagonalize the phonon Hamiltonian by decomposing the momentum-space operator vector
\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{{\mathbf{k}}}
into normal modes.
These normal modes are defined as the eigensolutions of the Heisenberg equation of motion (EOM),
\partial_{t}\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{\mathbf{k}}=i[\hat{H}_{p},\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{\mathbf{k}}]
.
Utilizing the Hamiltonian definition and the commutation relations derived in Eq. (
S66
), we evaluate the EOM component by component

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\hat{x}_{{\mathbf{k}}p}

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2}\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}p^{\prime}q^{\prime}}h({\mathbf{k}}^{\prime})_{p^{\prime}q^{\prime}}[\hat{x}_{-{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}p^{\prime}}\hat{x}_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}q^{\prime}},\hat{x}_{{\mathbf{k}}p}]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}p^{\prime}q^{\prime}}h({\mathbf{k}}^{\prime})_{p^{\prime}q^{\prime}}(\hat{x}_{-{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}p^{\prime}}\delta_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime},-{\mathbf{k}}}\mathcal{F}({\mathbf{k}}^{\prime})_{q^{\prime}p}+\delta_{{\mathbf{k}}{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}}\mathcal{F}(-{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime})_{p^{\prime}p}\hat{x}_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}q^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p^{\prime}q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{F}(-{\mathbf{k}})_{q^{\prime}p}h(-{\mathbf{k}})_{p^{\prime}q^{\prime}}\hat{x}_{{\mathbf{k}}p^{\prime}}+\mathcal{F}(-{\mathbf{k}})_{p^{\prime}p}h({\mathbf{k}})_{p^{\prime}q^{\prime}}\hat{x}_{{\mathbf{k}}q^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p^{\prime}q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{F}({\mathbf{k}})_{pq^{\prime}}h({\mathbf{k}})_{q^{\prime}p^{\prime}}\hat{x}_{{\mathbf{k}}p^{\prime}}+\mathcal{F}({\mathbf{k}})_{pp^{\prime}}h({\mathbf{k}})_{p^{\prime}q^{\prime}}\hat{x}_{{\mathbf{k}}q^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=[\mathcal{F}({\mathbf{k}})h({\mathbf{k}})\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{\mathbf{k}}]_{p},

(S68)

where we used
h(-{\mathbf{k}})^{T}=h({\mathbf{k}})
and
\mathcal{F}(-{\mathbf{k}})^{T}=-\mathcal{F}({\mathbf{k}})
from Eq. (
S67
).
This reduces the EOM to the compact matrix form
\partial_{t}\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{\mathbf{k}}=\mathcal{F}({\mathbf{k}})h({\mathbf{k}})\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{\mathbf{k}}
.
Assuming a harmonic time dependence
\bm{\zeta}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}(t)=\bm{\zeta}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}e^{-i\omega_{n{\mathbf{k}}}t}
, the normal modes are obtained by solving the eigenvalue equation

\displaystyle\omega_{n{\mathbf{k}}}\bm{\zeta}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}=i\mathcal{F}({\mathbf{k}})h({\mathbf{k}})\bm{\zeta}_{n{\mathbf{k}}},\quad(n=\pm 1,\dots,\pm dN_{s}).

(S69)

A straightforward calculation shows that both
i\mathcal{F}({\mathbf{k}})
and
h({\mathbf{k}})
are Hermitian matrices.
However, their product
i\mathcal{F}({\mathbf{k}})h({\mathbf{k}})
is generally non-Hermitian.
Thus, solving for the normal modes requires diagonalizing a non-Hermitian matrix, which is not guaranteed to be diagonalizable by a unitary transformation and could, in principle, possess complex eigenvalues.

Existence of real eigenvalues.—

We first prove that despite the non-Hermiticity of
i\mathcal{F}({\mathbf{k}})h({\mathbf{k}})
, there exist exactly
2dN_{s}
distinct solutions to Eq. (
S69
) with purely real frequencies
\omega_{n{\mathbf{k}}}\in\mathbb{R}
at any given momentum
{\mathbf{k}}
.
Temporarily suppressing the
(n,{\mathbf{k}})
indices, note that the matrix
i\mathcal{F}h
has dimension
2dN_{s}\times 2dN_{s}
, meaning there are at most
2dN_{s}
real eigenvalues.
By partitioning the eigenvector into momentum and displacement components
\bm{\zeta}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\mu}\\
\bm{\varepsilon}\end{pmatrix}
, the eigenvalue equation expands to

\displaystyle\omega\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\mu}\\
\bm{\varepsilon}\end{pmatrix}=i\begin{pmatrix}F&-D\\
\mathds{1}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\mu}\\
\bm{\varepsilon}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}iF\bm{\mu}-iD\bm{\varepsilon}\\
i\bm{\mu}\end{pmatrix}.

(S70)

This coupled system is algebraically equivalent to the conditions

\displaystyle\omega^{2}\bm{\varepsilon}=(i\omega F+D)\bm{\varepsilon},\quad\bm{\mu}=-i\omega\bm{\varepsilon}.

(S71)

For any purely real frequency
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
, the matrix
X(\omega)=i\omega F+D
is explicitly Hermitian.
Therefore, it possesses a complete set of real eigenvalues
\lambda_{m}(\omega)\in\mathbb{R}
and orthonormal eigenvectors
\bm{\varepsilon}_{m}(\omega)
, satisfying
X(\omega)\bm{\varepsilon}_{m}(\omega)=\lambda_{m}(\omega)\bm{\varepsilon}_{m}(\omega)
for
m=1,\dots,dN_{s}
.
Finding the real-frequency normal modes of Eq. (
S71
) is thus equivalent to finding the roots of the implicit equation
\omega^{2}=\lambda_{m}(\omega)
.
At strictly zero frequency,
X(0)=D\succeq 0
, which implies
\lambda_{m}(0)\geq 0
.
We categorize these modes into two cases.
If
\lambda_{m}(0)=0
, the equation
\omega^{2}=\lambda_{m}(\omega)
possesses a double root at
\omega=0
.
These correspond to Goldstone modes (eigenmodes with strictly zero frequency at the specified momentum
{\mathbf{k}}
), indicating that the emergent gauge field cannot gap out these fundamental zero-energy modes.
We denote the number of such Goldstone modes at
{\mathbf{k}}
as
N_{G}
.
Conversely, if
\lambda_{m}(0)>0
, then at
\omega=0
, we clearly have
\omega^{2}<\lambda_{m}(\omega)
.
However, in the limit of large
|\omega|
, the matrix
X(\omega)
is dominated by the linear term
i\omega F
.
Because
\lambda_{m}(\omega)
scales at most linearly with
\omega
at large frequencies, the quadratic
\omega^{2}
term must eventually overtake it, meaning
\omega^{2}>\lambda_{m}(\omega)
for sufficiently large
|\omega|
.
By continuity, there must exist at least one crossing where
\omega^{2}=\lambda_{m}(\omega)
for
\omega>0
, and another for
\omega<0
.

Counting these solutions at a fixed momentum
{\mathbf{k}}
, there are
2N_{G}
solutions at
\omega=0
(counting the double roots as separate eigenmodes), plus at least
dN_{s}-N_{G}
strictly positive and strictly negative frequency solutions.
Summing these yields a minimum of
2dN_{s}
real-frequency eigenmodes.
Since the total matrix dimension is
2dN_{s}
, this proves that all eigenvalues of Eq. (
S69
) must be purely real.
We adopt the convention of indexing the propagating modes with positive frequencies (
\omega\geq 0
) as
n=1,\dots,dN_{s}
, and the counterpropagating modes with negative frequencies (
\omega\leq 0
) as
n=-1,\dots,-dN_{s}
.
Applying complex conjugation to Eq. (
S69
) and utilizing the real-space conditions
F({\mathbf{R}}),D({\mathbf{R}})\in\mathbb{R}_{dN_{s}\times dN_{s}}
yields

\displaystyle\omega_{n{\mathbf{k}}}\bm{\zeta}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}^{*}

\displaystyle=-i\mathcal{F}({\mathbf{k}})^{*}h({\mathbf{k}})^{*}\bm{\zeta}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}^{*}

\displaystyle=-i\mathcal{F}(-{\mathbf{k}})h(-{\mathbf{k}})\bm{\zeta}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}^{*}.

(S72)

Thus, we enforce the effective particle-hole symmetry constraints on the normal modes

\displaystyle\omega_{-n-{\mathbf{k}}}=-\omega_{n{\mathbf{k}}},\quad\bm{\zeta}_{-n-{\mathbf{k}}}=\bm{\zeta}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}^{*}.

(S73)

This specific gauge choice for the particle-hole related eigenvectors is required to properly define the canonical phonon creation and annihilation operators later in this section.

Numerical diagonalization and normalization.—

Having established that the eigenvalues are purely real, we now present a systematic method for numerically obtaining the normal modes
\bm{\zeta}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}
and enforcing their generalized normalization condition.
Before proceeding, we invoke a standard linear algebraic identity: for any two general square matrices
A
and
B
, the products
AB
and
BA
share the exact same spectrum (
spec[AB]=spec[BA]
).
This is easily proven by noting that the block matrices
\mathfrak{M}_{1}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}AB&0\\
B&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
and
\mathfrak{M}_{2}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&0\\
B&BA\end{smallmatrix}\right)
are related by the similarity transformation
\mathfrak{M}_{2}=\mathcal{S}^{-1}\mathfrak{M}_{1}\mathcal{S}
via the
\mathcal{S}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\mathds{1}&A\\
0&\mathds{1}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, whose inverse is given by
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\mathds{1}&-A\\
0&\mathds{1}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.

From
h({\mathbf{k}})\succeq 0
, we can uniquely define its principal square root and rewrite the EOM matrix as
i\mathcal{F}({\mathbf{k}})h({\mathbf{k}})=[i\mathcal{F}({\mathbf{k}})\sqrt{h({\mathbf{k}})}]\sqrt{h({\mathbf{k}})}
.
Using the spectral identity
spec[AB]=spec[BA]
, we deduce that this non-Hermitian matrix shares the same eigenvalues as the reversed product

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}({\mathbf{k}})=i\sqrt{h({\mathbf{k}})}\mathcal{F}({\mathbf{k}})\sqrt{h({\mathbf{k}})}=i\begin{pmatrix}F({\mathbf{k}})&-\sqrt{D({\mathbf{k}})}\\
\sqrt{D({\mathbf{k}})}&0\end{pmatrix}.

(S74)

Crucially, the effective phonon Hamiltonian matrix
\mathcal{M}({\mathbf{k}})
is explicitly Hermitian.
Thus, the real frequencies
\omega_{n{\mathbf{k}}}
can be robustly obtained through standard unitary diagonalization

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}{\mathbf{y}}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}=\omega_{n{\mathbf{k}}}{\mathbf{y}}_{n{\mathbf{k}}},\quad{\mathbf{y}}_{m{\mathbf{k}}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{y}}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}=\delta_{mn}.

(S75)

To relate
{\mathbf{y}}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}
back to the normal modes
\bm{\zeta}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}
, we multiply the original EOM (Eq. (
S69
)) from the left by
\sqrt{h({\mathbf{k}})}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}({\mathbf{k}})\big(\sqrt{h({\mathbf{k}})}\bm{\zeta}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}\big)=\omega_{n{\mathbf{k}}}\big(\sqrt{h({\mathbf{k}})}\bm{\zeta}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}\big).

(S76)

This structural similarity motivates defining the physical normal modes as

\displaystyle\bm{\zeta}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}\equiv\sqrt{h({\mathbf{k}})^{+}}{\mathbf{y}}_{n{\mathbf{k}}},

(S77)

where
h({\mathbf{k}})^{+}
denotes the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse.
The pseudoinverse safely handles the non-invertibility introduced by the presence of zero-frequency Goldstone modes in the null space of
h({\mathbf{k}})
.
This definition naturally enforces the generalized normalization condition

\displaystyle\bm{\zeta}_{m{\mathbf{k}}}^{\dagger}h({\mathbf{k}})\bm{\zeta}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}=\delta_{mn}.

(S78)

Strictly speaking, the explicit eigenvectors for the zero-frequency Goldstone modes at a given momentum
{\mathbf{k}}
cannot be constructed via this pseudoinverse relation.
However, because zero-frequency modes carry no energy, they do not contribute to the thermal Hall conductivity (as detailed in Sec.
S5.2
). Consequently, their precise analytic form is safely ignored in practical numerical evaluations.

Phonon operators.—

Using these properly normalized modes, we map the displacement vectors to canonical phonon creation and annihilation operators
\hat{b}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}
, defined by the standard commutation relations

\displaystyle[\hat{b}_{m{\mathbf{k}}},\hat{b}_{n{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}}]=\delta_{m,-n}^{z}\delta_{{\mathbf{k}},-{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}},\quad\hat{b}_{-n-{\mathbf{k}}}=\hat{b}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}^{\dagger},

(S79)

where we have introduced the signed Kronecker delta
\delta^{z}_{m,-n}=\text{sign}(m)\delta_{m,-n}
.
Utilizing the completeness relation
\sum_{n}[{\mathbf{y}}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}]_{p}[{\mathbf{y}}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}^{*}]_{q}=\delta_{pq}
(which follows directly from the unitarity of the matrix
Y_{\mathbf{k}}=({\mathbf{y}}_{-dN_{s}{\mathbf{k}}},\dots,{\mathbf{y}}_{dN_{s}{\mathbf{k}}})
), we construct the transformation between the physical coordinates and the canonical operators

\displaystyle\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{\mathbf{k}}=\sum_{n}\sqrt{|\omega_{n{\mathbf{k}}}|}\bm{\zeta}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}\hat{b}_{n{\mathbf{k}}},\quad\hat{b}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\omega_{n{\mathbf{k}}}|}}\bm{\zeta}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}^{\dagger}h({\mathbf{k}})\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{\mathbf{k}},

(S80)

where we implicitly exclude any
\omega_{n{\mathbf{k}}}=0
Goldstone mode at a given momentum
{\mathbf{k}}
.
Substituting this transformation back into the phonon Hamiltonian yields a completely decoupled, diagonal basis

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{p}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}}\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{{\mathbf{k}}}^{\dagger}h({\mathbf{k}})\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{mn{\mathbf{k}}}\sqrt{|\omega_{m{\mathbf{k}}}\omega_{n{\mathbf{k}}}|}\bm{\zeta}_{m{\mathbf{k}}}^{\dagger}h({\mathbf{k}})\bm{\zeta}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}\hat{b}_{m{\mathbf{k}}}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n{\mathbf{k}}}|\omega_{n{\mathbf{k}}}|\hat{b}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n>0}\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}}(\omega_{n{\mathbf{k}}}\hat{b}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}-\omega_{-n-{\mathbf{k}}}\hat{b}_{-n-{\mathbf{k}}}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{-n-{\mathbf{k}}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n>0}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\omega_{n{\mathbf{k}}}(\hat{b}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}+\hat{b}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}\hat{b}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}^{\dagger})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n>0}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}(\hat{b}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}+\frac{1}{2}),

(S81)

This final result confirms that the complex coupled dynamics induced by the emergent gauge field have been successfully reduced to a collection of independent quantum harmonic oscillators.

S3.3
Projector formalism for multiband phonon Berry curvature

In this Appendix, we present a projector formula for the Abelian phonon Berry curvature, which is highly efficient for numerical calculations and naturally handles band degeneracies.
The phonon Berry curvature differs from its electronic counterpart primarily due to the non-unitary nature of the eigenvectors.
To properly account for bosonic orthonormality, we introduce the metric
h({\mathbf{k}})
.
Away from degenerate points, the phonon Berry connection for a single isolated band is defined as
54
;
55

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{n}({\mathbf{k}})_{\mu}=-Im[\bm{\xi}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}^{\dagger}\partial_{\mu}\bm{\zeta}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}],\quad\text{where}\quad\bm{\xi}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}\equiv h({\mathbf{k}})\bm{\zeta}_{n{\mathbf{k}}},

(S82)

where
\partial_{\mu}\bm{\zeta}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}\equiv\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}(\bm{\zeta}_{n,{\mathbf{k}}+\varepsilon\hat{{\mathbf{e}}}_{\mu}}-\bm{\zeta}_{n{\mathbf{k}}})/\varepsilon
.
Unlike electronic eigenstates, the inner product
\bm{\xi}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}^{\dagger}\partial_{\mu}\bm{\zeta}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}
is not automatically purely imaginary; thus, we must explicitly extract the imaginary part to define a real-valued connection.
The corresponding single band Berry curvature is given by

\displaystyle\Omega_{n}({\mathbf{k}})_{\mu\nu}=\partial_{\mu}\mathcal{A}_{n}({\mathbf{k}})_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}\mathcal{A}_{n}({\mathbf{k}})_{\mu}=-Im[\partial_{\mu}\bm{\xi}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}^{\dagger}\partial_{\nu}\bm{\zeta}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}-\partial_{\nu}\bm{\xi}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}^{\dagger}\partial_{\mu}\bm{\zeta}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}].

(S83)

In generic phonon systems, the single band Berry curvature
\Omega_{n}({\mathbf{k}})
exhibits singular divergences at band crossings.
These divergences pose a significant challenge when evaluating physical observables, such as the phonon thermal Hall conductivity
\kappa_{\mu\nu}^{ph}\propto\sum_{{\mathbf{k}},n>0}\big[c_{2}(g(\hbar\omega_{n{\mathbf{k}}}))-\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}\big]\Omega_{n}({\mathbf{k}})_{\mu\nu}
[see Eq. (
S115
)].
However, when multiple bands are (nearly) degenerate at a given momentum, their energy-dependent weighting factors converge to a common value.
The transport integral locally reduces to this common weight multiplied by the sum of the individual Berry curvatures of the constituent bands.
Crucially, even if the individual curvatures diverge, their sum across the degenerate subspace remains entirely smooth and well-defined.
We formalize this by introducing a multiband Abelian generalization.
We define a multiplet matrix
\mathcal{Z}_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}}
formed by grouping the eigenvectors of these (nearly) degenerate bands.
The multiband Abelian Berry connection and curvature are then defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{[n]}({\mathbf{k}})_{\mu}=-Im[Tr[\Xi_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}}^{\dagger}\partial_{\mu}\mathcal{Z}_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}}]],\quad\Omega_{[n]}({\mathbf{k}})_{\mu\nu}=-Im[Tr[\partial_{\mu}\Xi_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}}^{\dagger}\partial_{\nu}\mathcal{Z}_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}}-\partial_{\nu}\Xi_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}}^{\dagger}\partial_{\mu}\mathcal{Z}_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}}]],

(S84)

where
\Xi_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}}=h({\mathbf{k}})\mathcal{Z}_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}}
.

To implement these formulas robustly in numerical calculations—where derivatives like
\partial_{\mu}\mathcal{Z}_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}}
are evaluated using a finite-difference stencil (e.g., at
{\mathbf{k}}
and
{\mathbf{k}}\pm\varepsilon\hat{{\mathbf{e}}}_{\mu}
)—we must ensure that the column dimension of
\mathcal{Z}_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}}
is consistent across the local neighborhood.
To elaborate, the strict degeneracy structure often fluctuates between adjacent grid points.
For example, a four-band system might exhibit degeneracies grouped as
\{1\},\{2,3\},\{4\}
exactly at
{\mathbf{k}}
, but split into
\{1,2\},\{3\},\{4\}
at
{\mathbf{k}}+\varepsilon\hat{{\mathbf{e}}}_{\mu}
.
If we were to define
\mathcal{Z}_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}}
using the exact degeneracies at each independent grid point, the matrix dimension would inconsistently change across the stencil, rendering the finite-difference derivative ill-defined.
To resolve this, we uniformly define the multiplet matrix
\mathcal{Z}_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}}
by grouping all bands that cross or come within a specified small energy tolerance of one another
anywhere
within the local finite-difference neighborhood.
In the previous example, we would uniformly group the bands across the entire stencil as
\mathcal{Z}_{[1]{\mathbf{k}}}=(\bm{\zeta}_{1{\mathbf{k}}},\bm{\zeta}_{2{\mathbf{k}}},\bm{\zeta}_{3{\mathbf{k}}})
and
\mathcal{Z}_{[2]{\mathbf{k}}}=(\bm{\zeta}_{4{\mathbf{k}}})
.
Provided the energy tolerance and the momentum grid spacing are sufficiently small, this multiband curvature naturally allows us to generalize transport formulas for nondegenerate bands to arbitrary phonon systems with complex band crossings.

To compute this multiband curvature efficiently, we define non-Hermitian generalized phonon projector for the multiplet
[n]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}}=\mathcal{Z}_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}}\Xi_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}}^{\dagger}.

(S85)

This quantity satisfies all the fundamental properties of a projector.
Specifically, it is idempotent and acts as the identity within its subspace

\displaystyle\Xi_{[m]{\mathbf{k}}}^{\dagger}\mathcal{P}_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}}=\delta_{[m],[n]}\Xi_{[m]{\mathbf{k}}}^{\dagger},\quad\mathcal{P}_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}}\mathcal{Z}_{[m]{\mathbf{k}}}=\delta_{[m],[n]}\mathcal{Z}_{[m]{\mathbf{k}}}.

(S86)

Furthermore,
\mathcal{P}_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}}
is manifestly invariant under any unitary band mixing
\mathcal{Z}_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}}\to\mathcal{Z}_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}}U_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}}
within the degenerate subspace, and its trace accurately returns the dimension of the degenerate subspace.

We now show that the Abelian phonon Berry curvature in Eq. (
S84
) can be elegantly recast entirely in terms of these generalized projectors and their derivatives.
Inserting the identity
\mathds{1}=\mathcal{P}_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}}+(\mathds{1}-\mathcal{P}_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}})
into Eq. (
S84
) and temporarily suppressing the
([n],{\mathbf{k}})
indices, we separate the curvature into two traces:

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mu\nu}=-Im[Tr[\partial_{\mu}\Xi^{\dagger}\mathcal{P}\partial_{\nu}\mathcal{Z}-\partial_{\nu}\Xi^{\dagger}\mathcal{P}\partial_{\mu}\mathcal{Z}]]-Im[Tr[\partial_{\mu}\Xi^{\dagger}(\mathds{1}-\mathcal{P})\partial_{\nu}\mathcal{Z}-\partial_{\nu}\Xi^{\dagger}(\mathds{1}-\mathcal{P})\partial_{\mu}\mathcal{Z}]].

(S87)

By applying the relation
\Xi^{\dagger}\mathcal{Z}=\mathds{1}
(which implies
\partial_{\mu}\Xi^{\dagger}\mathcal{Z}=-\Xi^{\dagger}\partial_{\mu}\mathcal{Z}
) and utilizing the cyclicity of the trace, the first term identically vanishes:

\displaystyle-Im[Tr[\partial_{\mu}\Xi^{\dagger}(\mathcal{Z}\Xi^{\dagger})\partial_{\nu}\mathcal{Z}-\partial_{\nu}\Xi^{\dagger}(\mathcal{Z}\Xi^{\dagger})\partial_{\mu}\mathcal{Z}]]

\displaystyle=-Im[Tr[\partial_{\mu}\Xi^{\dagger}\mathcal{Z}\Xi^{\dagger}\partial_{\nu}\mathcal{Z}-\Xi^{\dagger}\partial_{\nu}\mathcal{Z}\partial_{\mu}\Xi^{\dagger}\mathcal{Z}]]

\displaystyle=-Im[Tr[\partial_{\mu}\Xi^{\dagger}\mathcal{Z}\Xi^{\dagger}\partial_{\nu}\mathcal{Z}-\partial_{\mu}\Xi^{\dagger}\mathcal{Z}\Xi^{\dagger}\partial_{\nu}\mathcal{Z}]]

\displaystyle=0.

(S88)

To evaluate the remaining term, we insert the identity matrix
\mathds{1}=\Xi^{\dagger}\mathcal{Z}
into the trace and again use cyclicity to pull
\Xi^{\dagger}
to the right

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=-Im[Tr[(\Xi^{\dagger}\mathcal{Z})\partial_{\mu}\Xi^{\dagger}(\mathds{1}-\mathcal{P})\partial_{\nu}\mathcal{Z}-(\mu\leftrightarrow\nu)]]

\displaystyle=-Im[Tr[\mathcal{Z}\partial_{\mu}\Xi^{\dagger}(\mathds{1}-\mathcal{P})\partial_{\nu}\mathcal{Z}\Xi^{\dagger}-(\mu\leftrightarrow\nu)]].

(S89)

Substituting
\partial_{\mu}\mathcal{P}=\partial_{\mu}\mathcal{Z}\Xi^{\dagger}+\mathcal{Z}\partial_{\mu}\Xi^{\dagger}
into the trace yields

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=-Im[Tr[(\partial_{\mu}\mathcal{P}-\partial_{\mu}\mathcal{Z}\Xi^{\dagger})(\mathds{1}-\mathcal{P})(\partial_{\nu}\mathcal{P}-\mathcal{Z}\partial_{\nu}\Xi^{\dagger})-(\mu\leftrightarrow\nu)]].

(S90)

Utilizing
\Xi^{\dagger}(\mathds{1}-\mathcal{P})=\Xi^{\dagger}-\Xi^{\dagger}\mathcal{P}=0
and
(\mathds{1}-\mathcal{P})\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{Z}-\mathcal{P}\mathcal{Z}=0
[see Eq. (
S86
)], the curvature reduces to

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=-Im[Tr[\partial_{\mu}\mathcal{P}(\mathds{1}-\mathcal{P})\partial_{\nu}\mathcal{P}-(\mu\leftrightarrow\nu)]].

(S91)

Finally, taking the derivative of the idempotence relation
\mathcal{P}^{2}=\mathcal{P}
yields
\partial_{\mu}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{P}+\mathcal{P}\partial_{\mu}\mathcal{P}=\partial_{\mu}\mathcal{P}
, which rearranges to
\partial_{\mu}\mathcal{P}(\mathds{1}-\mathcal{P})=\mathcal{P}\partial_{\mu}\mathcal{P}
.
Substituting this identity into our expression, we arrive at the projector formula for the multiband phonon Berry curvature

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mu\nu}=-Im[Tr[\mathcal{P}\partial_{\mu}\mathcal{P}\partial_{\nu}\mathcal{P}-\mathcal{P}\partial_{\nu}\mathcal{P}\partial_{\mu}\mathcal{P}]]=-Im[Tr[\mathcal{P}[\partial_{\mu}\mathcal{P},\partial_{\nu}\mathcal{P}]]].

(S92)

S4
Symmetries of the electron and phonon systems

In this Appendix, we formally define the relevant symmetry groups of the system and examine how they constrain the phonon dynamics coupled to the emergent gauge field.
In Sec.
S4.1
, we establish the electronic symmetries and derive the resulting transformation rules for the emergent gauge field
F^{em}
.
Next, in Sec.
S4.2
, we deduce the symmetry properties of the coupled phonon system.
Establishing these geometric transformation rules is a necessary prerequisite for determining the symmetry constraints on the macroscopic phonon thermal Hall conductivity.
Closely following the notation of Ref.
27
, we denote a general spin space group symmetry element
11
;
70
;
15
;
30
as
g=(\mathcal{T})^{\phi_{g}}\{\mathcal{U}_{g}|O_{g}|\bm{\delta}_{g}\}
, where
\mathcal{T}
is the time-reversal operator,
\mathcal{U}_{g}
is the
SU(2)
action of
g
[see Eq. (
S96
)], and
\phi_{g}=0
(
\phi_{g}=1
) for unitary (antiunitary) symmetries.
As we consider general spin space groups,
O_{g}
does not restrict the form of
\mathcal{U}_{g}
.
The symmetry
g
acts on real and reciprocal space coordinates as

\displaystyle g{\mathbf{r}}=O_{g}{\mathbf{r}}+\bm{\delta}_{g},\quad g{\mathbf{k}}=(-1)^{\phi_{g}}O_{g}{\mathbf{k}},\quad(O_{g}\in O(d)).

(S93)

Its action on a general complex number (as opposed to an operator) is simply complex conjugation for antiunitary operations

\displaystyle gxg^{-1}=\overline{x}^{g}=\begin{cases}x&(\phi_{g}=0)\\
x^{*}&(\phi_{g}=1)\end{cases}.

(S94)

S4.1
Electronic symmetries

A critical subtlety in our formalism is that we define the electronic space group
\mathcal{G}_{el}
based strictly on the equilibrium nuclear configuration.
We say
g\in\mathcal{G}_{el}
if it leaves the electronic Hamiltonian [see Eq. (
S7
)] invariant at the
equilibrium
configuration.
Consequently, at finite displacements,
g
enforces a covariant symmetry transformation between the symmetry-related displaced configurations

\displaystyle g\hat{H}_{el}({\mathbf{0}})g^{-1}=\hat{H}_{el}({\mathbf{0}}),\quad g\hat{H}_{el}({\mathbf{u}}_{ion})g^{-1}=\hat{H}_{el}(g{\mathbf{u}}_{ion}),

(S95)

where
{\mathbf{u}}_{ion}=({\mathbf{u}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{u}}_{N_{nu}})
denotes the nuclear displacements.
We denote the action of
g
on the localized electron creation operators as

\displaystyle g\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i\sigma}g^{-1}=\sum_{j\sigma^{\prime}}[U_{g}]_{j\sigma^{\prime},i\sigma}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j\sigma^{\prime}}=\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}e^{i\theta_{g}(i)}[\mathcal{U}_{g}]_{\sigma^{\prime}\sigma},

(S96)

where
e^{i\theta_{g}(i)}\in U(1)
captures an on-site phase, and
\mathcal{U}_{g}
is the
SU(2)
action of
g
.
Note that the primed site index
i^{\prime}
denotes the spatial coordinate mapped by the symmetry (
g:i\mapsto i^{\prime}
).
Eq. (
S96
) implies that the single-particle matrix representation of the electronic Hamiltonian transforms covariantly as

\displaystyle H_{el}(g{\mathbf{u}}_{ion})=U_{g}\overline{H_{el}({\mathbf{u}}_{ion})}^{g}U_{g}^{\dagger}.

(S97)

To determine how this constrains the real-space emergent gauge field
F^{em}_{i\mu,j\nu}=-2Im[Tr[P\partial_{i\mu}P\partial_{j\nu}P]]
[see Eq. (
S11
)], we note that Eq. (
S97
) forces the electronic ground state projector to obey the identical transformation rule

\displaystyle P(g{\mathbf{u}}_{ion})=U_{g}\overline{P({\mathbf{u}}_{ion})}^{g}U_{g}^{\dagger}.

(S98)

By explicitly evaluating the derivative of the projector with respect to a displacement at the mapped site
i^{\prime}
, and letting
\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{i\mu}
denote the basis vectors of the
dN_{nu}
-dimensional displacement space, we find

\displaystyle\partial_{i^{\prime}\mu^{\prime}}P({\mathbf{0}})

\displaystyle\equiv\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{P(\varepsilon\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{i^{\prime}\mu^{\prime}})-P({\mathbf{0}})}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}U_{g}\frac{\sum_{\mu}\overline{P(\varepsilon[O_{g}]_{\mu^{\prime}\mu}\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{i\mu})}^{g}-\overline{P({\mathbf{0}})}^{g}}{\varepsilon}U_{g}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu}U_{g}\partial_{i\mu}\overline{P({\mathbf{0}})}^{g}U_{g}^{\dagger}[O_{g}]_{\mu^{\prime}\mu}.

(S99)

Substituting this into the definition of the gauge field and utilizing the cyclicity of the trace, the unitary matrices strictly cancel, revealing that the equilibrium emergent gauge field rotates as a tensor

\displaystyle F^{em}_{i^{\prime}\mu^{\prime},j^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}=(-1)^{\phi_{g}}\sum_{\mu\nu}[O_{g}]_{\mu^{\prime}\mu}F^{em}_{i\mu,j\nu}[O_{g}^{T}]_{\nu\nu^{\prime}}.

(S100)

An immediate consequence is that for a crystal with discrete translational symmetry, the gauge field depends only on the relative distance between unit cells.
Separating the site index into unit cell and sublattice components
i=({\mathbf{R}},\alpha)
, we can write
F^{em}_{{\mathbf{R}}_{1}\alpha\mu,{\mathbf{R}}_{2}\beta\nu}=F^{em}({\mathbf{R}}_{1}-{\mathbf{R}}_{2})_{\alpha\mu,\beta\nu}
.
Furthermore, we can apply a spatial Fourier transform to determine the transformation of the momentum-space gauge field
F^{em}({\mathbf{k}})_{\alpha\mu,\beta\nu}=\sum_{{\mathbf{R}}}e^{-i{\mathbf{k}}\cdot({\mathbf{R}}+\bm{\tau}_{\alpha}-\bm{\tau}_{\beta})}F^{em}({\mathbf{R}})_{\alpha\mu,\beta\nu}
.
Tracking the mapped indices
g:\alpha\mapsto\alpha^{\prime}
and relative distances
{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}+\bm{\tau}_{\alpha^{\prime}}-\bm{\tau}_{\beta^{\prime}}=O_{g}({\mathbf{R}}+\bm{\tau}_{\alpha}-\bm{\tau}_{\beta})
yields

\displaystyle F^{em}(g{\mathbf{k}})_{\alpha^{\prime}\mu^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}}e^{-i(g{\mathbf{k}})\cdot({\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}+\bm{\tau}_{\alpha^{\prime}}-\bm{\tau}_{\beta^{\prime}})}F^{em}({\mathbf{R}}^{\prime})_{\alpha^{\prime}\mu^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\phi_{g}}\sum_{{\mathbf{R}}\mu\nu}[O_{g}]_{\mu^{\prime}\mu}e^{-i(-1)^{\phi_{g}}{\mathbf{k}}\cdot({\mathbf{R}}+\bm{\tau}_{\alpha}-\bm{\tau}_{\beta})}F^{em}({\mathbf{R}})_{\alpha\mu,\beta\nu}[O_{g}^{T}]_{\nu\nu^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\phi_{g}}\sum_{\mu\nu}[O_{g}]_{\mu^{\prime}\mu}\overline{F^{em}({\mathbf{k}})_{\alpha\mu,\beta\nu}}^{g}[O_{g}^{T}]_{\nu\nu^{\prime}}.

(S101)

By introducing the sublattice permutation matrix
[P_{g}]_{\beta\alpha}=\delta_{\beta\alpha^{\prime}}
(not to be confused with the electronic projector
P
), this momentum-space constraint can be compactly expressed in matrix form as

\displaystyle F^{em}(g{\mathbf{k}})=(-1)^{\phi_{g}}\Gamma_{g}\overline{F^{em}({\mathbf{k}})}^{g}\Gamma_{g}^{T},\quad\text{where}\quad\Gamma_{g}=P_{g}\otimes O_{g}.

(S102)

S4.2
Phonon symmetries

Defining symmetry in the phonon sector requires care due to the presence of the gauge field.
Because the kinematic momentum
\hat{\bm{\pi}}
completely absorbs the gauge potential, the standard kinetic energy term
\hat{\bm{\pi}}^{2}/2
is formally invariant regardless of the underlying gauge field structure [see Eq. (
S65
)].
Consequently, declaring a symmetry based on the invariance of the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{p}
is insufficient.
Instead, we define the phonon space group
\mathcal{G}_{p}
based on the invariance of the Heisenberg equations of motion,
i\partial_{t}\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{{\mathbf{k}}}=i\mathcal{F}({\mathbf{k}})h({\mathbf{k}})\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{{\mathbf{k}}}
[see Eq. (
S68
)].
In the following, we assume the total effective gauge field is given entirely by the electronic emergent gauge field,
F=F^{em}
.

Let us denote the action of
g
on the lattice operators as

\displaystyle g\hat{\pi}_{{\mathbf{R}}\alpha\mu}g^{-1}=(-1)^{\phi_{g}}\sum_{\beta\nu}\hat{\pi}_{{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\beta\nu}[\Gamma_{g}]_{\beta\nu,\alpha\mu},\quad g\hat{u}_{{\mathbf{R}}\alpha\mu}g^{-1}=\sum_{\beta\nu}\hat{u}_{{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\beta\nu}[\Gamma_{g}]_{\beta\nu,\alpha\mu},

(S103)

where
\Gamma_{g}=P_{g}\otimes O_{g}
and
{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}+{\mathbf{r}}_{\beta}=O_{g}({\mathbf{R}}+{\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha})+\bm{\delta}_{g}
.
Transforming to momentum space, this becomes

\displaystyle g\hat{\pi}_{{\mathbf{k}}\alpha\mu}g^{-1}=(-1)^{\phi_{g}}\sum_{\beta\nu}\hat{\pi}_{g{\mathbf{k}},\beta\nu}e^{ig{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\bm{\delta}_{g}}[\Gamma_{g}]_{\beta\nu,\alpha\mu},\quad g\hat{u}_{{\mathbf{k}}\alpha\mu}g^{-1}=\sum_{\beta\nu}\hat{u}_{g{\mathbf{k}},\beta\nu}e^{ig{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\bm{\delta}_{g}}[\Gamma_{g}]_{\beta\nu,\alpha\mu},

(S104)

which can be written compactly in terms of the composite operator vector
\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{\mathbf{k}}
as

\displaystyle g\hat{x}_{{\mathbf{k}}p}g^{-1}=\sum_{q}\hat{x}_{g{\mathbf{k}},q}e^{ig{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\bm{\delta}_{g}}[\widetilde{\Gamma}_{g}]_{qp},\quad\text{where}\quad\widetilde{\Gamma}_{g}=\begin{pmatrix}(-1)^{\phi_{g}}\Gamma_{g}&0\\
0&\Gamma_{g}\end{pmatrix}.

(S105)

Noting that time derivatives transform as
gi\partial_{t}g^{-1}=(-1)^{2\phi_{g}}i\partial_{t}=i\partial_{t}
, the Heisenberg EOM is invariant under
g\in\mathcal{G}_{p}
if and only if the EOM matrix transforms as

\displaystyle i\mathcal{F}(g{\mathbf{k}})h(g{\mathbf{k}})=\widetilde{\Gamma}_{g}\big(\overline{i\mathcal{F}({\mathbf{k}})h({\mathbf{k}})}^{g}\big)\widetilde{\Gamma}_{g}^{T}.

(S106)

This single matrix equation strictly dictates two independent conditions for the block matrices

\displaystyle F^{em}(g{\mathbf{k}})=(-1)^{\phi_{g}}\Gamma_{g}\overline{F^{em}({\mathbf{k}})}^{g}\Gamma_{g}^{T}\quad\text{and}\quad D(g{\mathbf{k}})=\Gamma_{g}\overline{D({\mathbf{k}})}^{g}\Gamma_{g}^{T}.

(S107)

The first condition demands that either
g\in\mathcal{G}_{el}
(satisfying Eq. (
S102
)) or
F^{em}({\mathbf{k}})=0
.
The second condition dictates that
g
must be a valid symmetry of the bare phonon system (i.e., the standard lattice dynamical matrix without the gauge field).
Thus, one can diagnose whether a specific operation is a valid symmetry of the fully coupled system simply by checking if it is a joint symmetry of the electronic space group and the bare lattice structure.

With these transformation rules established, we can deduce the symmetry properties of the phonon Berry curvature,
\Omega_{[n]}({\mathbf{k}})_{\mu\nu}=-Im[Tr[\mathcal{P}_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}}[\partial_{\mu}\mathcal{P}_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}},\partial_{\nu}\mathcal{P}_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}}]]]
[see Eq. (
S92
)].
From the EOM eigenvalue problem
i\mathcal{F}({\mathbf{k}})h({\mathbf{k}})\bm{\zeta}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}=\omega_{n{\mathbf{k}}}\bm{\zeta}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}
and the transformation rule in Eq. (
S106
), it immediately follows that
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{g}\overline{\bm{\zeta}}^{g}_{n{\mathbf{k}}}
is a valid normal mode with frequency
\omega_{n{\mathbf{k}}}
at the transformed momentum
g{\mathbf{k}}
.
Combined with Eq. (
S107
), the generalized non-Hermitian phonon projector
\mathcal{P}_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}}
must therefore transform as
\mathcal{P}_{[n]g{\mathbf{k}}}=\widetilde{\Gamma}_{g}\overline{\mathcal{P}}^{g}_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}}\widetilde{\Gamma}_{g}^{T}
.
Evaluating the momentum derivative of the projector under this mapping yields

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}\mathcal{P}_{[n],g{\mathbf{k}}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\mathcal{P}_{[n]g{\mathbf{k}}+\varepsilon\hat{{\mathbf{e}}}_{\mu}}-\mathcal{P}_{[n]g{\mathbf{k}}}}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\widetilde{\Gamma}_{g}\big(\overline{\mathcal{P}}^{g}_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}+\varepsilon O_{g}^{-1}\hat{{\mathbf{e}}}_{\mu}}-\overline{\mathcal{P}_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}}}^{g}\big)\widetilde{\Gamma}_{g}^{T}}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\nu}[O_{g}]_{\mu\nu}\widetilde{\Gamma}_{g}\partial_{\nu}\overline{\mathcal{P}}^{g}_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}}\widetilde{\Gamma}_{g}^{T}.

(S108)

Inserting this into the trace formula for the curvature, the internal
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{g}
matrices cancel, leaving the fundamental symmetry transformation rule for the phonon Berry curvature

\displaystyle\Omega_{[n]}(g{\mathbf{k}})=(-1)^{\phi_{g}}O_{g}\Omega_{[n]}({\mathbf{k}})O_{g}^{T}.

(S109)

The general transformation rule in Eq. (
S109
) manifests differently depending on the dimensionality of the system.
In purely two-dimensional systems confined to the
xy
-plane, the Berry curvature possesses only a single independent component,
\Omega_{[n]}({\mathbf{k}})_{xy}
. For spatial symmetries confined to this plane, the transformation rule strictly simplifies to a scalar equation

\displaystyle\Omega_{[n]}(g{\mathbf{k}})_{xy}=(-1)^{\phi_{g}}\det(O_{g})\Omega_{[n]}({\mathbf{k}})_{xy}.

(S110)

This immediately provides powerful local constraints.
Under in-plane unitary and antiunitary rotations,
\det(O_{g})=1
and the curvature obeys

\displaystyle\Omega_{[n]}(C_{nz}{\mathbf{k}})_{xy}=\Omega_{[n]}({\mathbf{k}})_{xy},\quad\Omega_{[n]}(\mathcal{T}C_{nz}{\mathbf{k}})_{xy}=-\Omega_{[n]}({\mathbf{k}})_{xy}.

(S111)

Conversely, under unitary and antiunitary mirror reflections (including glide mirrors),
\det(O_{g})=-1
, and the curvature is constrained as

\displaystyle\Omega_{[n]}(m_{2D}{\mathbf{k}})_{xy}=-\Omega_{[n]}({\mathbf{k}})_{xy},\quad\Omega_{[n]}(\mathcal{T}m_{2D}{\mathbf{k}})_{xy}=\Omega_{[n]}({\mathbf{k}})_{xy}.

(S112)

In three-dimensional systems, the curvature tensor is completely determined by three independent components.
It is therefore highly convenient to map the tensor to a dual vector, defined as
\mathcal{B}_{[n]}^{\rho}({\mathbf{k}})\equiv\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mu\nu}\varepsilon_{\mu\nu\rho}\Omega_{[n]}({\mathbf{k}})_{\mu\nu}
.
In terms of this dual vector, Eq. (
S109
) is mathematically equivalent to

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{B}}_{[n]}(g{\mathbf{k}})=(-1)^{\phi_{g}}\det(O_{g})O_{g}\bm{\mathcal{B}}_{[n]}({\mathbf{k}}).

(S113)

This elegantly demonstrates that the 3D Berry curvature transforms precisely as a time-reversal odd pseudovector.
This pseudovector nature easily resolves standard 3D symmetry constraints.
For instance, under spatial inversion
\mathcal{I}
(
\phi_{g}=0
,
O_{g}=-\mathds{1}
,
|O_{g}|=-1
), the dual vector maps as
\bm{\mathcal{B}}_{[n]}(-{\mathbf{k}})=(1)(-1)(-\mathds{1})\bm{\mathcal{B}}_{[n]}({\mathbf{k}})=\bm{\mathcal{B}}_{[n]}({\mathbf{k}})
, demonstrating that inversion preserves the local Berry curvature.

Emergent time-reversal symmetry at
F^{em}=0
.—

To conclude this section, we examine the limit where the emergent gauge field vanishes.
Because the system hosts local magnetic moments, the true time-reversal operator
\mathcal{T}
is intrinsically broken (
\mathcal{T}\notin\mathcal{G}_{el}
).
However, if the underlying magnetic configuration is strictly collinear, or in the limit
t/J\to 0
, the electron wave is independent of the nuclear displacements, causing the emergent gauge field to strictly vanish (
F^{em}=0
).
Crucially, the bare dynamical matrix inherently respects time-reversal symmetry (
D(-{\mathbf{k}})=D({\mathbf{k}})^{*}
).
Therefore, turning off the emergent gauge field restores an
effective
antiunitary time-reversal symmetry to the lattice dynamics.
Under this emergent symmetry operation (
\phi_{g}=1
,
O_{g}=\mathds{1}
), the Berry curvature transformation rule strictly enforces

\displaystyle\Omega_{[n]}(-{\mathbf{k}})=-\Omega_{[n]}({\mathbf{k}})\quad\text{if}\quad F^{em}=0.

(S114)

The form of the intrinsic phonon thermal Hall conductivity readily shows that this antisymmetry constraint dictates that the intrinsic phonon thermal Hall effect must strictly vanish when
F^{em}=0
[see Eq. (
S115
)].
This confirms the central physical premise of our formalism: the emergent gauge field is the fundamental required mechanism for generating a finite intrinsic phonon thermal Hall effect in these systems.

S5

In this Appendix, we derive the intrinsic phonon thermal Hall conductivity

In Sec.

In Sec.

Finally, in Sec.

S5.1
Multiband linear response formula

We begin by deriving the explicit form of the thermal Hall conductivity introduced in the main text

\displaystyle\kappa_{\mu\nu}^{ph}=-\frac{k_{B}^{2}T}{V\hbar}\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}}\sum_{[n]>0}\big[c_{2}(g(\hbar\omega_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}}))-\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}\big]\Omega_{[n]}({\mathbf{k}})_{\mu\nu}\quad(\mu\neq\nu),

(S115)

where
V
is the system volume,
g(\varepsilon)=\frac{1}{e^{\beta\varepsilon}-1}
is the Bose-Einstein distribution function with
\beta=\frac{1}{k_{B}T}
, and

\displaystyle c_{2}(x)=\int_{0}^{x}dt~\Big(\ln\frac{1+t}{t}\Big)^{2}=(1+x)\Big(\ln\frac{1+x}{x}\Big)^{2}-(\ln x)^{2}-2\text{Li}_{2}(-x),

(S116)

where
\text{Li}_{2}(x)
is the dilogarithm (or second polylogarithm) function
42
.
To obtain this expression, we build upon the foundational derivation by Qin
et al.

54

Correcting a minor sign error in the first term of Eq. (37) in their original manuscript, the macroscopic phonon Hall conductivity is given by

\displaystyle\kappa_{\mu\nu}^{ph}=\frac{(\pi k_{B})^{2}}{3h}Z_{\mu\nu}T-\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\varepsilon~\varepsilon^{2}\sigma_{\mu\nu}(\varepsilon)\frac{dg(\varepsilon)}{d\varepsilon},

(S117)

where the auxiliary variables are defined as sums over the particle bands (
n>0

\displaystyle Z_{\mu\nu}=\frac{2\pi}{V}\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}}\sum_{n>0}\Omega_{n}({\mathbf{k}})_{\mu\nu},\quad\sigma_{\mu\nu}(\varepsilon)=-\frac{1}{V\hbar}\sum_{\hbar\omega_{n{\mathbf{k}}}\leq\varepsilon}\Omega_{n}({\mathbf{k}})_{\mu\nu}.

(S118)

Substituting these definitions into Eq. (
S117
) yields

\displaystyle\kappa_{\mu\nu}^{ph}=\frac{k_{B}^{2}T}{V\hbar}\Big(\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}}\sum_{n>0}\Omega_{n}({\mathbf{k}})_{\mu\nu}+\int_{0}^{\infty}d\varepsilon~(\beta\varepsilon)^{2}\frac{dg(\varepsilon)}{d\varepsilon}\sum_{\hbar\omega_{n{\mathbf{k}}}\leq\varepsilon}\Omega_{n}({\mathbf{k}})_{\mu\nu}\Big).

(S119)

To evaluate the energy integral, we track the integration boundaries for each individual eigenstate.
Introducing the dimensionless variable
x=\beta\varepsilon

\displaystyle\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}}\sum_{n>0}\int_{\beta\hbar\omega_{n{\mathbf{k}}}}^{\infty}dx~x^{2}\frac{d}{dx}\Big(\frac{1}{e^{x}-1}\Big)\Omega_{n}({\mathbf{k}})_{\mu\nu}=-\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}}\sum_{n>0}\int_{0}^{g(\hbar\omega_{n{\mathbf{k}}})}dt~\Big(\ln\frac{1+t}{t}\Big)^{2}\Omega_{n}({\mathbf{k}})_{\mu\nu},

(S120)

where we have applied the integration substitution
t=\frac{1}{e^{x}-1}
, which implies
x=\ln\frac{1+t}{t}
.
Substituting this analytically evaluated integral back into the total conductivity expression yields exactly

\displaystyle\kappa_{\mu\nu}^{ph}=-\frac{k_{B}^{2}T}{V\hbar}\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}}\sum_{n>0}\Big[c_{2}(g(\hbar\omega_{n{\mathbf{k}}}))-\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}\Big]\Omega_{n}({\mathbf{k}})_{\mu\nu}.

(S121)

To make this formula robust against band degeneracies and numerical singularities, we elevate the single-band summation to the multiband Abelian generalization detailed in Sec.
S3.3
, implementing the substitution
n\to[n]
.
This formally yields the expression in Eq. (
S115
).
Crucially, this sign-corrected linear response derivation exactly reproduces the thermal Hall formulas derived independently via semiclassical wave-packet dynamics
74
, as well as formulas derived for canonical phonon Hamiltonians (such as those governing conventional Raman spin-lattice interactions
53
), demonstrating universal agreement across distinct theoretical frameworks.

S5.2
High-temperature series expansion

To obtain a physically transparent expansion of Eq. (
S115
) at high temperatures, we analyze the analytic structure of the dimensionless weighting factor

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(y)\equiv c_{2}\Big(\frac{1}{e^{y}-1}\Big)-\frac{\pi^{2}}{3},\quad\text{where}\quad y=\frac{\hbar\omega_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}}}{k_{B}T}.

(S122)

In the high-temperature limit
T\to\infty
, or equivalently the low-energy limit
\omega_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}}\to 0^{+}
, the variable
y\to 0^{+}
.
In this limit, the integral evaluates to
c_{2}(\infty)-\pi^{2}/3=0

42
.
To determine the higher-order behavior, we differentiate
\mathcal{W}(y)
to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathcal{W}}{dy}=-\Big(\frac{y}{e^{y/2}-e^{-y/2}}\Big)^{2}=-\Big(\frac{(y/2)}{\sinh(y/2)}\Big)^{2}.

(S123)

As this derivative is strictly an even function of
y
, its integral
\mathcal{W}(y)
must consist exclusively of odd powers of
y
in its polynomial expansion around
y=0
.
Crucially, the analytic structure of this derivative precisely dictates the radius of convergence for the resulting series.
In the complex plane,
\frac{d\mathcal{W}}{dy}
possesses a removable singularity at
y=0
and true poles where
\sinh(y/2)=0
for
y\neq 0
, which occur at
y=\pm 2\pi i,\pm 4\pi i,\dots
.
According to complex analysis, the radius of convergence for a Taylor series is strictly bounded by the distance to the nearest non-removable singularity, which is given by
|2\pi i|=2\pi
in this case.
Therefore, the Taylor series for
\mathcal{W}(y)
converges absolutely for
|y|<2\pi
, and strictly diverges for
|y|>2\pi
.
Translated to our physical parameters, this guarantees that the polynomial expansion is mathematically exact so long as
k_{B}T>\max(\hbar\omega_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}})/2\pi
.
Evaluating the successive derivatives analytically, we construct the convergent high-temperature series expansion for the intrinsic thermal Hall conductivity

\displaystyle\kappa_{xy}^{ph}=\frac{k_{B}}{V}\sum_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}}\omega_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}}\Omega_{[n]}({\mathbf{k}})_{xy}\Big[1-\frac{1}{36}\Big(\frac{\hbar\omega_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}}}{k_{B}T}\Big)^{2}+\frac{1}{1200}\Big(\frac{\hbar\omega_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}}}{k_{B}T}\Big)^{4}-\frac{1}{42336}\Big(\frac{\hbar\omega_{[n]{\mathbf{k}}}}{k_{B}T}\Big)^{6}+\dots\Big].

(S124)

As the prefactor
T
in the original
\kappa_{xy}
formula cancels with the
T^{-1}
scaling of the leading
y^{1}
term, the leading-order thermal Hall conductivity approaches a constant value at high temperatures.
Note that this series expansion is a universal property of the geometric function
\mathcal{W}(y)
and thus applies generically to the intrinsic thermal Hall conductivity of various bosonic excitations, including both phonons and magnons
42
.
