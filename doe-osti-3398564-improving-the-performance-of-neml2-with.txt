Abstract
NEML2 vectorizes constitutive-model evaluation for large-scale multiphysics simulation, using
PyTorch as its tensor backend so that a batch of material-point updates runs on CPU or GPU
through a single implementation. In the two prior reports in this series it was a C++-native
library, deployed through TorchScript tracing and just-in-time (JIT) compilation; it has since
been rewritten from the ground up into a Python-native library deployed through Ahead-of-Time
Inductor (AOTInductor), a modern PyTorch graph-compilation backend. The rewrite is driven by
a persistent tension, not a language preference: NEML2 composes constitutive models at runtime
from a registry of small, independently-authored pieces, and that flexibility is difficult to reconcile
with the compile-time knowledge an efficient GPU kernel needs. This report documents the
rewrite and the investment that accompanied it: the AOTInductor export pipeline that turns a
Python-authored model into a portable, Python-free compiled artifact loadable from pure C++;
the eager and compiled runtimes and the new implicit solver layer built on them; a head-to-head
benchmark of legacy JIT against AOTInductor; the physics-model catalog and its worked examples;
the developer tooling; and the corresponding overhaul of MOOSE’s NEML2 integration that lets
MOOSE consume it. A central objective is to examine whether modern PyTorch graph-compilation
backends are effective for MOOSE GPU integration. The benchmark answers directly: AOTInductor
outperforms legacy JIT on every GPU scenario measured, by 1.0–4.5×. Modern graph-compilation
backends are effective for MOOSE GPU integration, and AOTInductor specifically – not compilation
in the abstract – is why.

1 Introduction
NEML2 vectorizes constitutive-model evaluation for large-scale multiphysics simulation, using
PyTorch as its tensor backend so that a batch of material-point updates can run on CPU or GPU
through a single implementation. ANL-24/43 [2] introduced this design and the vectorization-
efficiency model this report’s benchmarking (Chapter 5) still uses; ANL-25/22 [3] added a CPU/GPU
work dispatcher so a batch could be spread across heterogeneous devices. Both reports describe
NEML2 as a C++-native library, compiled for deployment through TorchScript tracing and just-
in-time (JIT) compilation. Since then, NEML2 has been rewritten from the ground up, and this
report documents the result: a Python-native library deployed through Ahead-of-Time Inductor
(AOTInductor), a modern PyTorch graph-compilation backend, together with the numerical-
methods and tooling investment that came with the rewrite and the corresponding overhaul of
MOOSE’s NEML2 integration that lets MOOSE consume it. The rewrite was driven by a genuine tension,
not by a preference for one programming language over another: NEML2 composes constitutive
models at runtime from a registry of small, independently-authored pieces – the same pattern
MOOSE uses for its own physics kernels – and that runtime flexibility is difficult to reconcile with
the compile-time knowledge an efficient GPU kernel needs. Chapter 2 explains that tension in
depth and how each of NEML2’s two major versions resolved it.
 A central objective of this report is to examine the effectiveness of modern PyTorch graph-
compilation backends for MOOSE GPU integration, and to document the resulting tensor-operation
function-graph-tracing capability. AOTInductor is the answer this report examines in depth: how
a Python-authored model becomes a portable, Python-free compiled artifact; how that artifact is
loaded and called from pure C++; and how effective the result actually is, measured against the
legacy JIT path it replaces. That technical core – Chapters 2 through 5 – is this report’s primary
contribution, and Chapter 8 shows it working end to end inside MOOSE.
 NEML2 closed out its v2.1 release line in the months after ANL-25/22 appeared, then in June
2026 completed the rewrite to v3, with point releases continuing through the time of writing. The
rewrite changed both how models are authored – from C++ to Python – and how they are deployed
– from legacy TorchScript-JIT to AOTInductor; the former, as Chapter 2 shows, followed from
the latter.
 The chapters that follow trace this story in three parts. Chapters 2 through 5 cover the rewrite
itself: the architectural break from the C++ tower, the AOTInductor compilation pipeline in depth,
the eager and compiled runtimes with the solver layer and adaptive Newton substepping built on
top of them, and a head-to-head benchmark of legacy JIT against AOTInductor. Chapters 6 and 7
then survey the physics model catalog and the worked examples that exercise it. Chapter 8 closes
the loop on everything that grew alongside the rewrite: the developer-experience and tooling
investment (logging, the nmhit input-file dialect, dependency management, PyPI packaging, and
Claude Code developer skills) and the downstream migration of MOOSE’s NEML2 integration to
consume all of it. Chapter 9 concludes.

2 From a C++ Tower to a Python-Native Library
2.1 Modularity, community development, and the compilation wall

NEML2’s design borrows directly from MOOSE’s own answer to a problem every large, collaboratively-
developed multiphysics code eventually faces: how do you let a large, decoupled community
contribute to a shared code base without every contributor needing to understand the whole of it?
MOOSE answers this with a registry-factory pattern [6]: a developer writes one small, self-contained
piece of physics – a Kernel, a Material, a boundary condition – and registers it under a name; an
analyst then assembles a specific problem by naming those pieces in a hierarchical input file, never
touching the C++ that implements them. This decouples two roles that would otherwise have to
be the same person – the developer who authors a term, and the analyst who composes terms into
a problem – and it decomposes a PDE into small, independently-authored, interchangeable pieces,
maximizing modularity and code reuse while keeping any one contributor’s surface area small.
 NEML2 adopts the identical pattern for constitutive models: a small equation is authored once,
registered into a factory, and a specific material model is assembled at runtime by composing
registered pieces from a HIT input file – the same input-file format MOOSE uses. This is not a
stylistic choice; it is the mechanism that lets a large community contribute constitutive-model
building blocks independently, exactly as MOOSE’s registry-factory pattern lets a large community
contribute physics kernels independently.
 That pattern runs into a real wall on GPU. An efficient device kernel is a compiled artifact,
and compiling a kernel well means knowing, ahead of running it, exactly which operations will
execute and in what order, so that a compiler can fuse them, plan memory traffic, and choose
device-specific code. Registry-factory composition is built to guarantee the opposite: which pieces
compose into a given model, and how, is not known until an analyst’s input file is parsed, at
runtime. Left alone, this forces a choice between the two properties this report cares about – eager,
per-piece dispatch preserves full runtime composability but launches one kernel (or more) per
registered piece, with no fusion across the boundaries between them and full interpreter overhead
in between; a model compiled ahead of the fact buys fusion and device-native code but only for
one fixed configuration, defeating the reason the registry-factory pattern exists in the first place.
 There is a spectrum of ways to resolve this tension, and it is worth surveying briefly, because
NEML2’s two major versions occupy two different points on it. At one end, a host-language,
compile-time metaprogramming approach – C++ expression templates, in the style of libraries
such as Eigen – can fuse a composed expression into a single loop with zero runtime overhead,
but only because the composition must be known at the host application’s compile time; this is
fundamentally incompatible with resolving that composition from an input file at runtime, and
was never an option once a registry-factory design was chosen. At the other end, plain eager
dispatch against a library of pre-compiled generic kernels – ordinary PyTorch eager execution,
or NEML2’s own py-eager route – keeps full runtime flexibility and adds no compile step at all, at
the cost of exactly the cross-piece fusion a GPU benefits most from. In between sits a family of
two-stage designs (Figure 2.1): resolve the runtime composition into one concrete computational
graph first, as a dynamic, lightweight step, and only then hand that frozen, fully-resolved graph
to a separate, static compilation stage. This pattern predates GPU deep learning – finite-element

only in when the second stage runs. v2 compiled just-in-time: libtorch’s JIT executor profiled

2.2 The v2 architecture and its limits
NEML2 v2, as documented in ANL-24/43 [2], was a C++-native library: every constitutive-model leaf
was a C++ class deriving from a common Model base, registered through a register_NEML2_object
macro, with its schema declared through an OptionSet interface and its forward operator imple-
mented in a virtual set_value method. Its path to fast execution was lazy TorchScript tracing:
Model::forward_maybe_jit traced a model the first time it ran against a given input schema –
batch shape, sub-batch shape, and dispatch key – and cached the traced graph under libtorch’s
profiling JIT executor. That executor interprets the graph for its first several calls, records the
shapes and types it sees, and fuses eligible regions into compiled kernels once it has profiled
enough of them – an adaptive process internal to libtorch, not a discrete step NEML2 triggers.
 This is a genuine instance of Section 2.1’s two-stage pattern: composition resolves into a graph,
which is then compiled. Tracing flattens the composed model into one flat sequence of tensor

                                                                             4                                                              ANL-26/33

operations – the boundaries between registered Model classes leave no seam a compiler must cross
– so what limits performance is not composition but coverage. libtorch’s profiling executor fuses
only narrow elementwise and reduction chains, and only after enough calls to profile them; every
other operation runs through the JIT interpreter, one hand-written ATen kernel per call, for the
life of the process. v2’s JIT thus delivered compiled kernels in a few narrow islands and left the
rest of the graph paying the per-operation dispatch overhead Section 2.1 described.
 Coverage, then, is the limit v3 sets out to lift: how much of the resolved graph becomes
device-native code rather than running op-by-op through the JIT interpreter (Figure 2.2). That is
what motivates the rewrite documented in the rest of this chapter.

2.3 The v3 rewrite: one authoring surface, six evaluation routes
v3 closes that coverage gap directly. Compiling the whole resolved graph ahead of time, instead
of fusing a slice of it at runtime, has two consequences. First, the artifact – compiled once, offline,
shipped as a fixed binary – loads into a host with no Python interpreter and no compiler toolchain,
exactly what embedding NEML2 in MOOSE requires (Chapter 8) and exactly what a TorchScript
artifact, still needing libtorch’s JIT interpreter at every call, cannot offer. Second, AOTInductor’s
Inductor backend lowers essentially the entire graph into fused Triton (GPU) or vectorized C++

ANL-26/33                                                                 5

(CPU) kernels, and can search harder for each kernel because that search is paid once, offline, not
on every run.
 NEML2 is not the only scientific-computing framework to have reached this conclusion by
hitting the same wall. NequIP and Allegro, a family of equivariant machine-learning interatomic
potentials built on PyTorch, faced the identical problem deploying modular, composable models
into LAMMPS – a C++ molecular-dynamics host with no Python interpreter of its own – and made
the same move, from TorchScript to AOTInductor [5]. Holding the underlying compiler fixed
(their training path continues to use in-process torch.compile, NEML2’s own py-jit route), they
report that moving inference deployment specifically from TorchScript to AOTInductor (paired
with a hand-written kernel for one operation Inductor could not fuse on its own) yielded 4–18×
speedups on single-node runs, averaged 6.6–6.9× across multi-node strong-scaling tests on two
different supercomputers (peaking at 10.9×), and increased the largest simulatable system size
from roughly 100 atoms (out of memory under TorchScript) to over 5,000 atoms on a single GPU.
They describe embedding a Python interpreter directly in the host application – the alternative
NEML2’s own cpp-eager route takes only for lightweight C++ unit tests (Chapter 4), never for
production deployment – as “a complicated, high-overhead solution.” The pattern is structural to
deploying compiled, composable models into non-Python hosts, not specific to NEML2.
 This compilation strategy is also why authoring moved to Python. torch.export’s tracer,
built on PyTorch’s Dynamo, records a graph by instrumenting live Python execution: each tensor
operation a Python forward performs is dispatched through PyTorch’s operator registry, and
Dynamo hooks that dispatch to emit one graph node per operation. A forward pass written in C++,
calling libtorch’s tensor API directly as v2’s set_value did, never returns to Python between
those calls, so Dynamo sees a single opaque call rather than the operations inside it – and Inductor
is left with nothing to fuse. Wrapping the C++ in a per-operation Python binding would restore
that visibility only by paying a Python call per elementary operation, defeating the point of the
C++. Authoring in Python is, in practice, the only way to hand torch.export a graph worth
compiling.
 The Python dependency runs deeper than the tracer. Inductor emits each GPU kernel as a
Triton kernel – itself a Python function, decorated with @triton.jit and compiled to PTX by
Triton’s Python-hosted compiler. None of this survives into the shipped .pt2: what ships is
compiled binary, loaded with no interpreter present (Chapter 3). But every stage of producing the
artifact runs in Python, so authoring the model in Python crosses the language boundary once,
when the artifact ships, where a C++-authored model would cross it twice – into Python to trace
and compile, back to C++ to deploy.
 A model is therefore authored once, in Python, as an ordinary torch.nn.Module subclass,
and evaluated through one of six runtimes, each carrying a short “host-mode” codename used

2.4 Repository reorganization
The repository reflects this split directly. The Python authoring surface lives under neml2/ –
models/ (the physics catalog, Chapter 6), types/ (the typed-tensor system), solvers/ (Chapter 4),
and es/ (assembled vectors/matrices for coupled systems) among others. The surviving C++ lives
under neml2/csrc/: the AOTInductor runtime (csrc/aoti/), the work scheduler and multi-device
dispatcher (csrc/dispatchers/), and the embedded-Python eager runtime (csrc/eager/). The first
two compile into a single shared library, libneml2.so, built once as part of the wheel and invisible
to most contributors; the third compiles into a separate library, libneml2_eager.so, kept apart
specifically so that libneml2.so – the library every production deployment links against – stays
free of any Python dependency.
 Retiring the C++ tower also retired the compiler as an enforcement mechanism for NEML2’s in-
ternal invariants. In its place, the project relies on a small set of Python-level conventions enforced
by lint and a runtime guard module (neml2/models/_guard.py), rather than by the type system:
every internal function must return a typed tensor wrapper rather than a raw torch.Tensor, and
a leaf’s forward must stay in typed-wrapper algebra rather than reaching into a wrapper’s under-
lying .data. This is a real trade – compile-time enforcement for lint- and runtime-enforcement –
and Chapter 8’s discussion of the project’s Claude Code developer skills (Section 8.1) describes
the tooling built to keep that trade from becoming a liability.
 The build system was simplified in the same spirit: CMakePresets.json is gone, and
pip install -e . is the single build entry point for an editable install, with build-type and
sanitizer/coverage flavors passed as --config-settings=cmake.build-type=... rather than a
preset name. Three CMake options that mattered when the C++ tower had thousands of translation
units – NEML2_PCH (precompiled headers), NEML2_JSON, and NEML2_CSV – were removed outright;
the current codebase has too few translation units for precompiled headers to pay for themselves,
and JSON/CSV support is simply always on.
 This closes a real coupling problem the earlier CMake-based install flow had, independent of
anything about compilation scope: it tied every consuming project to a specific, locally-built
libtorch, which is exactly the kind of binary-compatibility surface that made embedding NEML2
in a large, independently-versioned host like MOOSE brittle. The C++ tower was also, simply, a
large maintenance surface for code that did not need to be compiled at all to run correctly, only to
run fast; retiring it removed that surface along with the coupling problem.

2.5 Custom model authoring: then and now
Authoring a custom model changed correspondingly. Where v2 required a C++ subclass of
Model, a register_NEML2_object macro invocation, and an OptionSet-based schema declared
in the class’s static configuration method, a v3 leaf is a plain Python class with three load-
bearing pieces: a @register_neml2_object("TypeName") decorator, a HitSchema built from the
input/output/parameter helpers in neml2.schema, and a forward method written entirely in typed-
wrapper algebra. A minimal example, reproduced from NEML2’s own model-authoring guidance:

 The optional v keyword and the dictionary of chain-rule actions it enables are how NEML2
propagates first-order tangents through a composed model without ever forming a Jacobian explic-
itly: the action lambda V: K * V describes the local action of 𝜕ℎ/𝜕(equivalent_plastic_strain) on
an incoming tangent 𝑉 , and the framework accumulates these actions across the full dependency
graph. A leaf that needs no custom construction logic – most do not – carries no __init__ at all;
from_hit auto-declares every field named in a parameter(...) call directly from the corresponding
HIT option.
 This authoring change also relocates where a custom model can live. In v2, register_
NEML2_object was a C++ macro that fired at link time, so a user’s custom model had to be compiled
into the NEML2 library or a C++ extension linked against it – adding a model meant building NEML2.
In v3, registration fires when the Python module defining the class is imported, and the CLI tools
expose that directly through a repeatable --load flag: --load takes a path to a .py file, a package
directory, or a dotted module already on sys.path, imports it before the normal workflow runs,
and thereby registers whatever @register_neml2_object classes it defines. A custom constitutive
model can therefore be developed, versioned, and distributed as an ordinary Python package
entirely outside the NEML2 repository, then named in a HIT input file exactly as a built-in type
would be – no fork, no rebuild.

how neml2-compile turns a Python-authored model into a portable artifact, what that artifact

3.1 The neml2-compile export pipeline
neml2-compile is the command-line entry point to this pipeline:

 Internally, exporting a model proceeds through several stages (Figure 3.1). The HIT file is
parsed and the live model instantiated exactly as the eager runtime would. Any parameter named
with -p/--parameter is promoted: re-routed so it becomes a graph input rather than a baked-in
constant, and every other nn.Parameter left in the model is deep-copied into a plain buffer first,
because torch.export treats a live nn.Parameter as a graph input by default and a buffer as a
constant – NEML2 wants everything baked except what was explicitly promoted. Sub-batch shapes
(the per-slip, per-grain structure crystal-plasticity models carry) are seeded from an example
batch, either a built-in default or one supplied via --example-batch-shape, because that structure
must be committed before tracing can begin. The model is then partitioned into segments at every
implicit-solve boundary (Section 3.2), and each segment is traced and lowered independently.

3.2.1 Derivative graphs are opt-in
With no -d/--derivative flag, neml2-compile emits only the forward graph, and the runtime’s
jvp/jacobian calls raise if invoked. Each -d OUT:IN request compiles the Jacobian or JVP for that
specific output–input pair (omitting a side selects every pair on it), keeping artifacts for models
that only need a forward pass – most deployment scenarios – free of unused derivative graphs.
Two limitations of this opt-in derivative compilation matter to a MOOSE user:

 • For an implicit model with sub-batched (per-grain) state, such as crystal plasticity, a deriva-
 tive between two non-sub-batched variables (a global stress with respect to a global strain,
 say) is fully supported – the implicit-function-theorem solve internally handles the per-grain
 coupling and the returned block carries no grain axis. A derivative that touches a sub-batched
 variable is not yet implemented and fails fast at compile time with an explicit error; per-grain
 sensitivities currently require the eager runtime and torch.autograd instead.
 • Parameter-derivative graphs are compiled by reverse-mode autodifferentiation, the only
 mode that currently lowers through AOTInductor. An upstream PyTorch limitation (py-
 torch/pytorch#187907) prevents lowering a reverse-mode graph through an elementwise
 operation whose backward pass saves its own output – sqrt, exp, tanh, reciprocal, and
 division by a differentiated value, which covers common cases such as a von Mises norm
 or an Arrhenius rate law. NEML2 works around the common cases transparently by rout-
 ing these operations, inside its typed-tensor function library, through input-recomputing
 autograd.Functions on the differentiated path while leaving the plain instruction on the
 value path; a model whose differentiated path reaches an unwrapped saved-output operation
 still fails fast with an actionable message.

3.4 The C++ loader and a stable ABI

neml2::aoti::Model is the C++ class that loads an artifact folder and presents it to a host application.
It follows the PImpl idiom deliberately: every implementation detail – per-segment graph state,
the Newton/Krylov machinery, the metadata layout – lives behind an opaque, unexported Impl,
and the shared library is built with -fvisibility=hidden so that only the public surface below is
ever exported. NEML2’s own header comment states the intent plainly: “downstream consumers
compile against a stable ABI that does not change when the internals are reorganised.” For a host
like MOOSE that tracks NEML2 as a fast-moving submodule (Chapter 8), that stability guarantee is
what keeps a NEML2-internal refactor from forcing a MOOSE rebuild against a new interface.

 Two design choices in this interface answer questions a MOOSE integrator would otherwise have
to discover the hard way. First, the “bake by default” contract: every parameter and buffer in the
source model is folded into the compiled graph as a constant unless it was explicitly promoted with
-p at compile time; a model compiled with no promotions has an empty named_parameters() and is,
in effect, a frozen inference artifact. Second, device pinning is permanent: an artifact is compiled
for a specific device and dtype and cannot be migrated after loading. There is intentionally no to()
method – the header comment notes that offering one would either be a misleading no-op (the
underlying graph does not move) or a contract-breaking half-move (only the parameters would).

Retargeting a compiled artifact means recompiling it.
 Every entry point on Model funnels errors through one of three exception types, distinguished

4 Runtimes, Solvers, and Substepping
Chapter 3 covered how a model is compiled. This chapter covers how it is run: the evaluation
routes a model is reached through, the multi-device dispatch layer that spreads a batch across
accelerators, the linear- and nonlinear-solver layer those routes invoke for an implicit update, and
the adaptive substepping that wraps a failing Newton solve. The solver layer and substepping are,
together, among the most consequential additions documented in this report – they determine
whether NEML2 scales gracefully to the large, tightly-coupled implicit problems that dominate
MOOSE’s use case – and both are recent enough to still be actively evolving.

4.1 Eager and compiled routes
Chapter 2 covered the compiled-deployment path (the AOTInductor routes) in depth; the remain-
ing routes of Table 2.1 are the eager ones. py-eager – a model loaded with neml2.load_model and
evaluated directly – is the reference implementation every other route is checked against. It sup-
ports the full PyTorch autograd machinery and every model type NEML2 can express, including the
sub-batched (per-grain) models that cpp-eager (below) cannot handle. It pays for that generality
with per-call Python and dispatcher overhead, which is why it is not the recommended path for a
production deployment (Chapter 5), but it is the only route where a model author debugs with an
ordinary Python debugger and an unmodified stack trace.
 py-jit sits between eager execution and full ahead-of-time compilation: neml2.compile(model)
wraps torch.compile around the model’s forward pass (or, for a pyzag-driven training loop,
around the residual of a ModelNonlinearSystem) with dynamic=False, so PyTorch’s Dynamo/In-
ductor stack compiles and caches a kernel for each distinct input shape it sees, in-process. It
produces no artifact and is not meant to – it exists specifically to accelerate the training loops
pyzag drives, where a model is evaluated thousands of times against a small number of distinct
shapes within one running process, and the cost of an artifact one could deploy elsewhere is
unnecessary overhead.
 The remaining eager route, cpp-eager, answers a narrower need: a C++ test or application
that wants to evaluate a model straight from its HIT input file, at eager-Python speed, without
paying for a multi-minute neml2-compile first. neml2::eager::Model does this by embedding a full
CPython interpreter directly inside the C++ process and driving neml2.factory.load_model from
the original input file. It is intentionally shipped as its own library, libneml2_eager.so, separate
from libneml2.so – precisely so that the library every production deployment links against never
carries a Python dependency, while C++ unit tests that want the convenience of loading a model
from source can still have it. cpp-eager is plain-batch only: a sub-batched model – crystal plasticity
chief among them – is rejected rather than silently mishandled. The two C++ routes, cpp-eager
and cpp-aoti, share a single Newton-solver implementation, following an internal reorganization
that split the AOTInductor runtime into focused translation units and pointed both routes at the
same Newton code; parity between them (Table 2.1) is, for the Newton iteration itself, structural
rather than merely tested.
 In practice the six routes collapse to three decisions. For developing or training a model –
iterating on its Python source, or fitting parameters with pyzag – py-eager and py-jit are the only

ANL-26/33                                       21

routes that make sense; nothing else offers a debugger or tolerates a model whose shape changes
between calls. For deployment, AOTInductor is the only route this report recommends without
qualification (Chapter 5), reached as py-aoti, cpp-aoti, or cpp-dispatch depending on the host.
For a C++ test or tool that needs to load a model from its input file quickly and needs neither GPU
throughput nor sub-batch support, cpp-eager is the intended answer. The decision is low-stakes
to defer, because a consumer such as neml2-run or NEML2’s own TransientDriver does not know
which route backs the model it was handed: name an ordinary registered model type in the HIT
input and the driver runs on py-eager; name type = AOTIModel pointing at a compiled artifact
(Chapter 3) and the identical driver code runs on py-aoti. Switching from eager development to
compiled deployment is a change to the input file, not to the code that consumes it.

4.2 Multi-device dispatch: cpp-dispatch
A compiled artifact runs on the one device its .pt2 binary was built for, and a single cpp-aoti
call evaluates its whole input batch there. Two situations break that: a batch too large to fit in
one device’s memory, and a node with several accelerators that a single-device call leaves idle.
cpp-dispatch addresses both. It is not a different compiled model – it loads the same artifact
cpp-aoti does – but a thin wrapper, neml2::aoti::DispatchedModel, that spreads one batched
call across one or more devices behind a pluggable scheduler. This is the v3 form of the CPU/GPU
work dispatcher introduced in ANL-25/22 [3]: the same need to spread a batch of constitutive
updates across heterogeneous hardware, now built into NEML2’s own C++ runtime rather than
layered on from the embedding host.
 DispatchedModel re-exposes the exact call surface of a single-device Model – forward, jvp,
jacobian, and the parameter-derivative variants – so a consumer cannot tell from the interface
whether one device or several back it. It is deliberately a distinct same-shaped type rather than a
subclass (a Model’s methods are non-virtual by design): a caller substitutes one for the other at the
source level. Each call slices the input batch along its leading dynamic axis into scheduler-sized
chunks, moves each chunk to its target device, runs that device’s copy of the Model, moves the
result back to the device the inputs arrived on, and concatenates – all transparently. When no
scheduler is supplied, or when the whole batch fits in one chunk on the input’s own device, it
short-circuits to a direct Model call and adds no overhead, so the dispatched handle is safe to use
as the universal runnable type even in the single-device case.
 The scheduler is the dispatch policy, injected by composition and swappable. It is a C++-only
opt-in, never read from the input file – the HIT [Schedulers] block v2 exposed is gone in v3
(Chapter 2). NEML2 ships three, in two families distinguished by how the chunk loop runs:

 • SimpleScheduler sends the whole workload to a single device, one fixed-size chunk at a
 time. Its uses are memory tiling – processing a batch that does not fit in device memory
 in pieces – and empirically tuning the per-call chunk size; it is also the natural per-rank
 scheduler when a host pins one device per process by hand.
 • MPISimpleScheduler assigns devices to MPI ranks, round-robin within each node: ranks are
 grouped by host, then local_rank mod 𝑛 indexes a device list of length 𝑛. One device per

                                                  22                                      ANL-26/33

 rank when the counts match, a device shared across ranks when ranks outnumber devices,
 and an error when devices outnumber ranks – idle devices are disallowed. Within each
 rank it then chunks exactly like SimpleScheduler. This is the scheduler MOOSE builds for a
 compiled artifact (Chapter 8); it requires NEML2 built with MPI support.
 • StaticHybridScheduler spreads a single batch across several devices at once – a CPU
 together with one or more GPUs. Assignment is greedy: each chunk goes to the highest-
 priority device that still has spare in-flight capacity, so faster devices are kept filled and the
 next chunk’s host-to-device copy overlaps the current chunk’s compute. The pool admits
 at most one CPU entry, because each device’s AOTI graph already saturates PyTorch’s
 intra-op thread pool and two CPU workers would oversubscribe the same cores.

 The first two are synchronous: DispatchedModel runs their chunk loop on the calling thread,
against the one device the scheduler names. StaticHybridScheduler is asynchronous: Dispatche
dModel drives a thread-per-device pool, asking the scheduler for the next (device, chunk) pair,
enqueuing it, and running one worker per device concurrently while reassembling results in
dispatch order.

dispatch opt-in; the resulting handle has the same call surface as a single-device Model.
auto sched = std :: make_shared < StaticHybridScheduler >(
 StaticHybridScheduler :: Config {

neml2 :: aoti :: DispatchedModel model ( artifact_root , sched ) ;

 Two details make the multi-device case behave like the single-device one. Because a hybrid
dispatch runs several devices at once, a Newton solve can fail on more than one of them within
the same call; those failures are collected into a single AggregateError (Chapter 3) rather than
surfaced one at a time. And promoted parameters are held as one master map on the handle,
re-broadcast to every device copy before each dispatch, so the ordinary single-device idiom of
mutating a parameter in place keeps working unchanged under multi-device dispatch.

4.3 The pluggable solver layer
At the baseline this report measures from, NEML2 had exactly one nonlinear-solver family – Newton,
NewtonWithLineSearch, NewtonWithTrustRegion – and no pluggable linear-solver concept at all:
an implicit update’s Newton step formed and factored a dense Jacobian, unconditionally. It has
since grown a real solver layer, and none of it was possible until the AOTInductor export pipeline

DenseLU is the straightforward case: it assembles the (possibly block-structured) system into a

4.3.2 Matrix-free Krylov solvers: GMRES and BiCGStab

A direct solver assembles the Jacobian 𝐴 and factorizes it, at 𝑂 (𝑁 3 ) cost in the size 𝑁 of the
nonlinear system plus 𝑂 (𝑁 2 ) storage for the factors. NEML2’s newest solvers, GMRES and BiCGStab,
avoid both: they are matrix-free and never assemble 𝐴 at all. A Krylov method needs only the
action of the operator on a vector, 𝐴𝑣, and for a Newton step that operator is the residual Jacobian,
so 𝐴𝑣 is exactly a Jacobian–vector product 𝐽 𝑣 = (𝜕𝑟 /𝜕𝑢) 𝑣.
 This is where NEML2’s first-class jvp operation pays off. A Jacobian–vector product is a
directional derivative of the residual, computed at a cost comparable to a single residual evaluation
and without ever forming or storing the 𝑁 × 𝑁 Jacobian; the compiled _matvec graph from
Chapter 3’s segment table is precisely this product, lowered to device code alongside the residual
it differentiates. A Krylov solve therefore costs on the order of its iteration count times one such
product, which for a well-conditioned system that converges in few iterations sits far below the
𝑂 (𝑁 3 ) a dense factorization pays – and the gap widens as 𝑁 grows. NEML2’s own documentation
makes the same point for GMRES: it is “best for the well-conditioned near-identity systems of
implicit backward-Euler updates, where a handful of matvecs beat an 𝑂 (𝑁 3 ) dense factorization.”
This is what makes the matrix-free path the natural choice for the large nonlinear systems NEML2
increasingly has to solve – many-slip-system crystal plasticity, and the coupled systems the
Schur-complement solver above also targets – where the direct solve’s factorization cost and
dense-factor storage become the bottleneck.
 Both solvers are configured as an ordinary Newton linear_solver dependency, indistinguish-
able at the HIT level from DenseLU except for the options they expose: a restart width for GMRES, an
iteration cap for both, and an optional preconditioner (NoPreconditioner, JacobiPreconditioner,
BlockJacobiPreconditioner, or FullPreconditioner) with a caching strategy that lets an expen-
sive preconditioner persist across Newton iterations rather than being rebuilt each step. BiCGStab
trades GMRES’s growing Krylov subspace, and the memory to store it, for a fixed-footprint two-
matvec-per-iteration recurrence, at some cost in robustness on harder systems.
 We measured the crossover directly, under the same setup as the Schur study above. The
nonlinear-system size was varied through the Chaboche back-stress count 𝐾: with 𝐾 back stresses
the implicit system carries 𝑈 = 7 + 6𝐾 unknowns, giving 𝑈 ∈ {19, 31, 43, 79} for 𝐾 ∈ {2, 4, 6, 12}.
For each 𝑈 , three otherwise-identical models differing only in their [Solvers] block – a direct
DenseLU, an unpreconditioned GMRES, and an unpreconditioned BiCGStab – were compiled and
timed, all converging to the same root (agreement ∼ 10−8 ). Here the batch size 𝐵 (number of
material points solved at once) is the dynamic axis, and the crossover moves with it, so each
configuration was swept at three batches spanning the host-bound and compute-bound regimes.
 The crossover is real and batch-dependent. At a small, host-bound batch (𝐵 = 256) the per-call
overhead of the Krylov solvers’ repeated matvec launches dominates and DenseLU wins at every
𝑈 . At an intermediate batch (𝐵 = 4096) the crossover sits near 𝑈 ≈ 31, with GMRES 2.2× faster by
𝑈 = 43 and 3.8× by 𝑈 = 79. At the compute-bound batch (𝐵 = 65536) that the production route
targets, GMRES wins at every tested size, its margin over the direct solve widening with the system

inverse is applied each iteration to cluster the operator’s spectrum and cut the iteration count;
NEML2 ships four, spanning a cost–fidelity range. NoPreconditioner is the identity, 𝑀 = 𝐼 , leaving

FullPreconditioner takes 𝑀 = 𝐴 exactly, an LU factorization of the assembled Jacobian; on its
own this reintroduces the very factorization a matrix-free solve set out to avoid, but paired with
the caching strategy above – factor once, reuse across Newton iterations or steps – it becomes a
modified-Newton scheme in which one factorization amortizes over many cheap applications. Each
preconditioner is authored as an ordinary pair of PyTorch modules – a setup that builds 𝑀 −1 from
the model and an apply that maps a residual through it – compiled to the _precond_setup and
_precond_apply graphs of Chapter 3’s segment table, so a user can add a custom preconditioner
by subclassing without touching C++.
 We document these rather than benchmark them here, deliberately. A preconditioner’s value
is entirely a function of a system’s conditioning: it earns its keep only when the reduced iteration
count outweighs the per-iteration cost of applying 𝑀 −1 , and that trade turns on how clustered
the operator’s spectrum already is. The near-identity backward-Euler systems in the comparison
above are well-conditioned enough that no preconditioner earns its keep on them – reporting
numbers from such a scenario would understate what preconditioning is for, not measure it. A
fair study needs a genuinely stiff, ill-conditioned problem, and identifying a representative one
from NEML2’s model catalog is itself the open question; we leave that characterization as follow-up

4.4 Adaptive Newton substepping
Adaptive substepping is the newest capability documented in this report – its C++ implementation
landed in a single two-week window, 2026-07-12 through 2026-07-16, immediately before this
report’s baseline checkout, with refinements continuing through the point of writing. It is
presented here as current, actively evolving work, not as a settled design that has had time to be
exercised at production scale.
 The problem it solves is a restatement, one level up, of the segment-partitioning argument from
Chapter 3: a compiled graph cannot contain a data-dependent decision such as “if this increment’s
Newton solve fails to converge, retry it as two half-size increments.” NEML2’s own source comment
for the substepping driver states the resulting division of labor plainly:

 The single sub-step solve / IFT stays the compiled unit; the bisection-on-failure, force
 interpolation, increment scaling, old-state chaining, and consistent-tangent accumulation
 are host control flow here – exactly how the Newton iteration itself is orchestrated. Data-
 dependent (which sub-steps fail) control flow cannot live in a compiled graph, so it lives
 here.

 Substepping is not a driver-level or CLI-level setting; it is configured on the ImplicitUpdate
itself, as two HIT options, and is exclusively a compiled-route feature:

 max_substepping_level (an integer, default 0, which disables substepping entirely) caps how
deep the compiled solve may recursively bisect a failing increment: level 𝐿 allows up to 2𝐿 sub-
steps, applied independently per element of the batch, so that only the rows that actually fail
to converge pay for extra sub-steps. A sub-step still failing at the maximum depth raises the
same recoverable ConvergenceError described in Chapter 3, so that an outer time-stepper – MOOSE
among them (Chapter 8) – can cut the step at that level instead. incremental_variables names
which driving-force inputs should be ramped linearly across each sub-step rather than applied
in full immediately; a force that already carries an old-value (~1) counterpart, as most do, is
ramped automatically without needing to be listed. NEML2’s own documentation of the option
is direct about the restriction that matters most to a prospective user: “The eager runtime does
NOT sub-increment: evaluating an ImplicitUpdate eagerly with this set > 0 raises (substepping is

ANL-26/33                                        29

an AOTI-only feature).” Enabling substepping is therefore a decision made at neml2-compile time,
baked into the artifact’s metadata, not a runtime toggle available from every route.
 Substepping’s dependence on the solver layer of the preceding section is direct: each sub-step
re-invokes whichever linear solver – direct or Krylov – the artifact was compiled with, and the
two features were developed in the same short window for a reason – a substepping strategy that
must re-solve a macro-step’s Newton problem many times, at recursively finer sub-increments,
needs a linear solve cheap enough to repeat that often.
 Taken together, the additions in this chapter move NEML2 from “one dense Newton solve,
always” to a solver layer with a real choice between a direct factorization and a matrix-free Krylov
method – trading assembly cost and memory against iteration count and conditioning sensitivity
– with adaptive substepping layered on top. None of these choices is free, and while the scaling
measurements above establish how the direct and matrix-free paths trade off in isolation, none
has yet been exercised at the scale a production MOOSE simulation will demand – the gap between
“the mechanism exists, is documented, and scales as designed on a single node” and “we know how
it behaves under production load” is, in our view, the highest-value follow-up work this report
identifies.

5.3 Memory
Peak memory tells a simpler story than throughput. Figure 5.3 shows it for elasticity, and the
pattern is representative of the whole suite (Appendix A): memory is set by the batch, not the
backend. On CUDA, legacy JIT and AOTInductor occupy essentially identical device memory – for
elasticity, 653 MB versus 652 MB at the largest batch – both scaling linearly with 𝑁 above a small
fixed floor. The two curves overlap almost exactly, which is the important result: AOTInductor’s
throughput gains are not bought with extra device memory. The CPU curves report process
resident set size rather than a device allocator’s high-water mark, so they carry a larger fixed
footprint – the Python interpreter, libtorch, and the loaded runtime libraries – that reflects the
process baseline, not the per-evaluation working set, and AOTInductor’s baseline is the higher of
the two. The linear, batch-driven scaling that matters for sizing a GPU run is device memory, and
there the two backends are interchangeable.

5.4 Discussion
The pattern is exactly the question at the center of this report: on GPU, AOTInductor is an
unambiguous win over legacy JIT, on every scenario measured, at no memory cost – direct
evidence that a modern PyTorch graph-compilation backend is effective for the kind of GPU
deployment MOOSE integration needs (Chapter 8). On CPU, the same backend wins broadly but
regresses for crystal plasticity, a useful corrective against overclaiming: a compiler backend’s
effectiveness is not device-neutral, and a codegen strategy tuned for one device’s kernel model
can lose ground on another until the equivalent tuning work is done there too.

7 The New Physics Modules in Action
This chapter presents four end-to-end walkthroughs of the physics modules introduced in Chap-
ter 6. Each one composes the catalog primitives into a complete model, runs it at a realistic
parametric scale, and examines the output. The first two exercise the KWN precipitation module;
the second two exercise the crystal-plasticity module and its texture-analysis tooling. All four are
adapted from NEML2’s own pre-executed documentation notebooks, and the figures reproduced
here are the output of those runs. They are demonstrations that the modules compose and run
correctly at scale, not calibrated materials predictions, and we are explicit throughout about which
is which.

7.1 KWN precipitation kinetics

7.1.1 An Al–Cu time–temperature–precipitation diagram

A time–temperature–precipitation (TTP) diagram is a contour plot of precipitate volume fraction
over temperature and time; its characteristic “nose” marks the temperature at which a given
volume fraction forms fastest. This first example computes one for the 𝜃 -phase Al2 Cu precipitate
forming from a dilute Al–4 wt%Cu matrix, using the KWN catalog from Chapter 6.

Thermodynamic input. The KWN driving force needs the matrix and precipitate equilibrium
compositions and the matrix chemical potential as functions of temperature and matrix copper
concentration. These come from an offline CALPHAD calculation with pycalphad against the
Liang and Schmid-Fetzer (2015) database. That step is shown in the notebook but commented out
– it requires a proprietary thermodynamic database – because its output is baked directly into
the model input file as [Tensors] tables. At run time, a ScalarBilinearInterpolation reads the
chemical driving force off that tabulated grid against temperature and matrix Cu concentration,
and the rest of the model is the standard KWN nucleation-and-SFFK-growth chain wrapped in an
ImplicitUpdate. The evaluation half needs no CALPHAD tooling at all.

The sweep. A TTP diagram is many KWN runs: one per temperature, each integrated over the
full time history. The model is swept across 50 temperatures from 300–450 K and 500 log-spaced
time points from 10−4 to 102 hours, on a 300-cell radius grid, starting from a tiny seed number
density. Running all 50 temperatures at once is memory-hungry, so the sweep steps through them
five at a time, keeping the temperatures within a chunk close together – on this stiff population
balance the shared Newton convergence test behaves best when the batched temperatures are at
similar stages of precipitation.

Result. Figure 7.1a is the resulting TTP diagram, and Figure 7.1b the final precipitate size distri-
butions. The qualitative C-curve is reproduced, with its nose near the middle of the temperature
window. The notebook is candid about accuracy: without calibration against experimental Al–Cu
data, the predicted nose lands somewhat cooler than the measured value. This is a demonstration
that the model composes and runs correctly at a realistic parametric scale, not a validated materials
prediction.

7.1.2 Two-phase precipitation in 316H stainless steel
The second KWN example is substantially more elaborate. Two precipitate populations compete
for the same matrix solute reservoir in 316H austenitic stainless steel: Cr23 C6 carbide, rate-limited
by chromium, and the Fe2 Mo Laves phase, rate-limited by molybdenum. Rather than tracking
a single mean radius per phase, the model solves the full population-balance equation for each
phase’s size distribution 𝑛(𝑅, 𝑡) on a shared radius grid, so that nucleation, growth, and coarsening
all emerge from the same upwinded advection in radius space. Material data and composition
follow Hu et al. [10].
Composition. The input file wires together the KWN primitives – volume fraction, mass-balance
concentration, projected diffusivity, ideal-solution driving force, the classical-nucleation chain,
and the rate-limited growth rate – once per phase, couples them to the finite-volume module’s
upwinded advection (Chapter 6, Section 6.8) over a shared 200-cell logarithmic radius grid spanning
0.1 nm to 1 µm, and wraps the whole population balance in an ImplicitUpdate Newton solve. The
equilibrium matrix and precipitate compositions at each temperature come from ThermoCalc
values interpolated with ScalarLinearInterpolation.
The sweep and a graceful failure. The model is swept across four service temperatures (500–
650 °C) over roughly four decades of time (0.1–1000 hours), the four temperatures forming a plain
batch axis so all four histories advance in a single vectorized call per step. The carbide saturates
first, and fastest at the highest temperature – which is also where the backward-Euler/Newton
step becomes stiffest. When a step fails to converge, the sweep stops gracefully and keeps the
history collected so far: the run catches NEML2’s recoverable ConvergenceError, reporting that

crashing. This is the same exception taxonomy Chapter 8 shows MOOSE relying on to cut a time

7.2 Crystal plasticity and texture
The two crystal-plasticity examples share one scenario: 500 randomly oriented FCC single crystals
subjected to isochoric plane-strain rolling – extension along one axis, contraction along another,
no spin – to 50% reduction strain over 2500 time steps at a strain rate of 10−4 . The 500 initial
orientations are sampled uniformly on 𝑆𝑂 (3). Both are integrated with pyzag rather than NEML2’s
built-in transient driver: the NEML2PyzagModel wrapper packs the typed force tensors into the
model’s flat state layout, seeds the initial orientation, and integrates the history through pyzag’s
solve_adjoint with a ChunkNewtonRaphson solver (relative tolerance 10−6 , absolute 10−8 ) in chunks
of 20 steps. Both are heavy enough (500 crystals × 2500 steps) that the notebooks are committed
pre-executed and run on the GPU when one is available.

8 Developer Tooling and MOOSE Integration
The rewrite chronicled in the preceding chapters was accompanied by investment on two fronts
that are neither physics nor the compilation pipeline itself: the developer tooling that keeps a
large, partly AI-assisted contributor base productive and this report’s own claims trustworthy,
and the downstream integration that lets MOOSE – NEML2’s primary consumer – actually use the
compiled runtime. This chapter covers both.

8.1 Developer experience and tooling
Unified logging via NEML2_LOGS. Every route in Table 2.1 now reports through one logging system
rather than each managing its own verbosity. Six channels – newton, linear, substep, model, tensor,
and driver, plus a wildcard all – each carry an independent level from silent through warning,
info, and debug, defaulting to warning everywhere. Configuration is a single environment variable,
in the same comma-separated channel=level syntax PyTorch’s own TORCH_LOGS uses:

 Listing 8.1: Configuring NEML2’s logging via NEML2_LOGS.
NEML2_LOGS =" newton = info , substep = debug " neml2 - run input . i driver

A Newton solve logged at info or above brackets its own begin/end markers with nested indenta-
tion for line-search steps, inner linear solves, and substeps, so a single run can show exactly where
time and iterations went without instrumenting the model itself. The same configuration surface
is available programmatically (neml2.log.set_default_level, set_sink, and related functions
on the Python side) and from C++ (neml2::aoti::log), with precedence running environment
variable, then programmatic default, then the built-in warning floor. This replaces what v2 offered
only as a single, global boolean verbose flag on a driver or solver – on or off, with no channel and
no level in between.
The nmhit input dialect. nmhit is the parser that reads every HIT input file in this report,
and it is worth being precise about what it is: a separately maintained, separately versioned
package (github.com/applied-material-modeling/neml2-hit), pulled into NEML2 as an ordinary
PyPI dependency rather than vendored in the repository. Its own README is the authoritative
grammar reference; we do not attempt to reproduce it here. nmhit replaced two things at once:
the WASP/idaholab-hit C++ dependency NEML2 previously relied on for parsing, and, before that,
an even more ad hoc regular-expression-based HIT reader that had grown inside NEML2 itself.
It is a dependency in both directions – imported at runtime by every from_hit constructor in
neml2/models/, and linked at build time into libneml2.so, since the C++ AOTInductor loader
(Chapter 3) statically links against it – and a companion nmhit-driven formatter now runs as a
pre-commit hook against every HIT file in the repository, keeping the [Tensors] syntax from
Chapter 2 and the rest of this report’s input-file examples in one consistent style.
A dependency manager. NEML2 did not have a single source of truth for its own dependency
versions until a dedicated dependency manager was added; before that, each pinned version lived
wherever it happened to be written – a workflow file, a pyproject.toml entry, a hardcoded constant

ANL-26/33                                       47

– with nothing to notice if two of them drifted apart. scripts/dependencies.yaml is now that source
of truth, and scripts/dep_manager.py is the tool that enforces it, with five subcommands: check
(verify every annotated file matches the registry), list, get DEP.FIELD, bump DEP.FIELD VALUE
(propagate a version change to every file that embeds it), and sync (adopt a version edited directly
in one of those files back into the registry). At the time of writing, the registry pins eleven
entries – PyTorch (2.12.1, with a supported range down to 2.10.0), pyzag, nmhit, clang-format,
ruff, pyright, pybind11_stubgen, questionary (which backs neml2-compile’s interactive wizard),
CMake, the minimum Python version, and NEML2’s own version – plus one entry that is not a
package at all: the AOTInductor artifact metadata schema_version. That last entry is the highest-
value one in the registry, because it is what keeps the Python exporter (neml2/cli/aoti_export.py),
the C++ loader’s hardcoded kSupportedSchemaVersion constant (Chapter 3), and the schema’s own
documentation page in lockstep – a mismatch here is exactly the kind of silent artifact-format
incompatibility a compiled-deployment system cannot afford, and dep_manager.py check runs on
every push and pull request specifically to catch it before it ships.

PyPI wheels and the compatibility matrix. pip install neml2 has published to PyPI for some
time, but for most of this report’s baseline period it published a source distribution only – the
wheel-building job was disabled outright, pending larger CI runners, so installing NEML2 meant
a local CMake/compiler/PyTorch build. Binary wheels are now built for real, via cibuildwheel
across Linux and macOS, and validated by a dedicated compatibility-matrix workflow that is
more thorough than most libraries attempt: every combination of four PyTorch versions (2.10.0
through the pinned 2.12.1) and five Python versions (3.10 through 3.14) on two operating systems
– forty combinations in total – is asserted, in doc/content/installation/compatibility.yaml, to
pass NEML2’s full Python test suite, with the complete matrix run on every push to main and a
lean extremes-only subset run on pull requests for a faster signal. This matrix is CPU-only; GPU
coverage is exercised separately, on self-hosted runners, and does not feed wheel publishing.

Claude Code developer skills. NEML2’s repository carries six Claude Code skills under
.claude/skills/, and they are worth documenting on their own terms rather than as a curiosity:
they are executable encodings of the same conventions NEML2’s human contributors are expected
to follow, available to both. add-model scaffolds a new model leaf and states the wrapper-discipline
rules a leaf’s forward must obey – stay in typed-wrapper algebra, never reach into a wrapper’s
.data, push a chain-rule tangent through the local map rather than materializing a coefficient
and multiplying – with a table of canonical patterns (linear/reduction, rotation, sub-batch
coupling, time integration, and so on) pointing at a reference leaf for each. add-regression and
add-verification scaffold the two flavors of test NEML2 distinguishes sharply: a regression test
pins a model’s output against its own previously-generated gold/result.pt, to catch unintended
drift, while a verification test compares against an external ground truth – an analytical solution,
or another code’s output – to catch algorithmic error; each skill states plainly when to reach for
the other instead. add-doc and add-tutorial cover the two documentation page types – static
MyST reference pages versus self-contained, Colab-runnable notebooks – and the tutorial skill’s
guidance on voice is worth quoting directly, since it doubles as a statement of how NEML2 wants
to present itself to a new user: “lead with running code, then explain,” and prefer the physics’s

                                                 48                                      ANL-26/33

own notation over “meta-abstraction” about NEML2’s internals, since “the reader showed up to do
something, not to learn how NEML2 is built.” build-docs documents the Sphinx/MyST-NB build
pipeline itself, including a table of the specific failure modes a contributor is likely to hit. These
connect back to a detail from Chapter 2: NEML2’s own major commits are routinely co-authored
by an AI coding agent, and the skills are the other half of that fact – the checked-in mechanism
that keeps an AI-assisted contributor, and a human one, converging on the same conventions
rather than each inventing their own.

The input Jacobian – the more expensive of the two things jacobian() can return – is computed
only when MOOSE is actually assembling a Jacobian, or when a mesh change has invalidated a
previously cached one; a plain residual evaluation, such as a line-search trial or a JFNK matrix-
free residual, takes the cheaper value-only path instead. Parameter derivatives are recomputed
on every pass once requested at all, because they are typically consumed directly as inverse-
optimization sensitivities and must always be current rather than lagged. Errors from this call are
handled through exactly the taxonomy Chapter 3 described: if (!e.recoverable()) distinguishes
a Newton-divergence ConvergenceError, which lets MOOSE cut the time step and retry, from a hard
mooseError for anything else. On the way out, the CPU output_device default (above) pays off
directly: NEML2ToMOOSEMaterialProperty reads a single contiguous data_ptr<Real>() and copies
it to each quadrature point with a plain memcpy-equivalent, rather than allocating and copying an
individual tensor view per quadrature point – the generic per-point path is kept only as a fallback
for non-CPU outputs or a batch-growth race.
Work scheduling and dispatching. The [NEML2] block’s device option is now a list, and when the
underlying model is an AOTInductor artifact, MOOSE dispatches batches across it through NEML2’s
own MPISimpleScheduler (Chapter 3): devices are round-robined across MPI ranks on a node, one
device per rank when the counts match, shared when ranks outnumber devices, and rejected as a
configuration error when devices outnumber ranks. A single cpu entry simply runs each rank’s
local batch on the CPU, and device_batch sets an optional chunk size. An eager-backed model
does not dispatch at all – it is pinned to the first entry of the device list. This is a direct continuation
of the CPU/GPU dispatcher work documented in ANL-25/22 [3]: the same underlying need to
spread a batch of constitutive updates across heterogeneous devices, now driven by NEML2’s own
C++ scheduler rather than a MOOSE-side one, and no longer configured through the input-file
[Schedulers] block v2 exposed – that HIT-level surface is gone in v3, and scheduler configuration
for a MOOSE user now happens entirely through device/device_batch on the [NEML2] block itself.

9 Conclusion and Future Work
NEML2 composes constitutive models at runtime from a registry of small, independently-authored
pieces, the same pattern MOOSE uses for its own physics kernels, so that a large community can
contribute to either without any one contributor needing to understand the whole. That pattern is
difficult to reconcile with the compile-time knowledge an efficient GPU kernel needs, and NEML2’s
two major versions represent two different resolutions of the same tension: v2 resolved a runtime
composition into a graph and compiled it just-in-time, through TorchScript; v3 resolves it the
same way but compiles ahead of time, through AOTInductor, before the model is ever deployed.
Chapters 2 through 5 documented that shift in depth – the rewrite itself, the AOTInductor export
pipeline, the eager and compiled runtimes, a new solver layer, and a head-to-head benchmark of the
two compiled paths – and Chapters 6 through 8 documented everything else that grew alongside it:
the physics catalog, its worked examples, the developer tooling, and the corresponding overhaul
of MOOSE’s own NEML2 integration.
 The benchmark results in Chapter 5 answer the question this report set out to examine
directly: AOTInductor beats legacy JIT on every GPU scenario measured, by 1.0–4.5×, and
independent, quantified evidence from an unrelated framework facing the identical deployment
problem – NequIP and Allegro, moving their own composable, GPU-targeted models into a non-
Python host [5] – shows speedups of similar or greater magnitude from the same architectural
choice. Modern PyTorch graph-compilation backends are effective for MOOSE GPU integration, and
AOTInductor specifically, not compilation in the abstract, is why. That result is not unqualified:
AOTInductor is 3–30× slower than legacy JIT for crystal plasticity on CPU, because the compiler
optimizations behind AOTInductor’s GPU wins target Inductor’s Triton codegen specifically
and have not yet been matched in its CPU codegen for the same reduction pattern. We think
this qualification matters more, not less, for a report making a case for a compilation backend’s
effectiveness – a backend that is effective everywhere we happened to look and silent about where
it is not would be a weaker basis for the recommendation Chapter 8 ultimately rests on it.
 We want to highlight one result outside the compilation story itself: the solver layer of Chapter 4
– matrix-free Krylov methods, Schur-complement elimination, and the adaptive substepping
layered on top – is, in our assessment, the single most consequential addition documented in this
report for MOOSE-scale implicit problems, more so than any individual compilation-pipeline detail.
It is also the least battle-tested: most of it was built in the final weeks before this report’s baseline
checkout, and none of it has yet been exercised at the scale a production MOOSE simulation will
demand.
 Several other limitations are worth stating plainly rather than leaving implicit. NEML2’s own
artifact schema does not yet support spilling a large promoted-parameter set to an external state-
dict file – every promoted value is inlined in metadata.json. The torch version pin discussed
in Chapter 3 is stated as deliberate in NEML2’s own source comments, but the design-decision
record those comments point to is not part of the public checkout, so we could not independently
verify the reasoning behind the specific version chosen. AOTInductor’s Windows support carries
a documented temporary-directory collision workaround that is a real portability wrinkle, not
a resolved issue. And the worked-example coverage in Chapter 7 is uneven: four of the six
catalogued physics areas – porous flow, phase-field fracture, chemical reactions, and finite volume

ANL-26/33                                         53

– have no parametric worked example at all, only a static unit test standing in for one.
 Some of the quantitative gaps this report first flagged have since been closed: Chapter 4 now
measures the SchurComplement and matrix-free Krylov scaling directly, confirming the linear-
versus-cubic and batch-dependent crossover behavior the solver layer was designed for. The gaps
that remain are equally concrete and actionable: a substepping demonstration with a real conver-
gence failure and recovery, a preconditioning study on a genuinely ill-conditioned system rather
than the well-conditioned backward-Euler cases already measured, an independent reproduction
of the legacy-JIT-versus-AOTInductor benchmark rather than reliance on NEML2’s own migration
guide for that comparison, and a decision on whether this report should contribute the missing
worked examples itself or leave that as follow-up work. We see closing the crystal-plasticity CPU
regression, extending the new solver layer’s validation to MOOSE-representative problem sizes, and
closing the worked-example coverage gap as the highest-value next steps – ahead of, for instance,
extending AOTInductor to additional accelerator vendors, which is a reasonable long-term goal
but a lower-priority one than making sure what has already been built is validated at the scale it
is meant to serve.

9.1 Beyond the migration: strategic alignment and a domain-specific
 backend
The next steps above are all consolidation – validating and completing what this migration has
already built. Two developments the migration enables are larger in ambition and longer in
horizon, and we close by setting them out. Both follow from a single structural fact: v3 no longer
owns its path to fast execution the way v2 did. v2’s speed came from TorchScript, a tracing-
and-JIT stack internal to that one library; v3’s comes from torch.export and AOTInductor, the
general-purpose export-and-compile stack that PyTorch itself now positions as its supported
deployment path. That change of foundation is what the rest of this section is about.

9.1.1 Alignment with PyTorch’s development direction

The workflow v3 adopts – author the model in Python, export it to a serialized graph, and
deploy that graph into a C++ host with no Python present – is not a NEML2 invention; it is the
direction PyTorch is steering its entire ecosystem toward. As of PyTorch 2.10, TorchScript – the
foundation of v2’s JIT path – is formally deprecated, with torch.export named as its replacement
for the JIT trace and script APIs [11]. The migration this report documents therefore does more
than win a benchmark: it moves NEML2 off a foundation its upstream has stopped developing and
onto the one that upstream is actively investing in. The performance results of Chapter 5 are, in
this light, the near-term payoff of a change whose larger value is sustainability – NEML2’s fast path
now rides the stack PyTorch maintains, rather than one it has abandoned.
 This reframes a limitation stated earlier in this chapter. The crystal-plasticity CPU regression
– AOTInductor running several times slower than legacy JIT for that one workload on CPU –
is a shortfall in Inductor’s CPU code generation, not in NEML2. Because that code generation
is exactly what PyTorch is actively improving, the most likely path to closing the regression is

                                                  54                                      ANL-26/33

an upstream one: the actionable task on the NEML2 side is to track successive PyTorch releases
and re-benchmark, rather than to hand-write a replacement kernel. The same alignment that
makes the GPU wins durable makes this particular weakness a temporary one, on someone else’s
roadmap.

9.1.2 A domain-specific compilation backend
The second opportunity is more speculative and, we think, more interesting. Routing through
torch.export places NEML2 at the seam that torch.compile exposes for extension: export
produces a graph on PyTorch’s core ATen intermediate representation, which is then handed
to a compilation backend – Inductor, by default. PyTorch documents this seam as a supported
extension point, including how to register a custom backend, how to write graph transformations
directly on the ATen IR, and how those compose with ahead-of-time compilation [12]. Today
NEML2 uses the default backend; nothing about the architecture requires that it always does.
 A general-purpose backend optimizes general-purpose tensor programs. Constitutive modeling
for implicit finite-element solves has structure a general-purpose compiler cannot be expected to
know: small dense systems solved once per quadrature point, block-diagonal batched Jacobians,
the residual–Jacobian–solve triple at the heart of every Newton step, and the algebraic identities
of the typed tensor system (SR2, R2, WR2, and their kin) that a compiler sees only as opaque strided
arithmetic. These are precisely the patterns the solver layer of Chapter 4 was built around – the
Schur-complement elimination and matrix-free Krylov methods that already exploit this block
structure at the linear-algebra level. A set of NEML2-authored ATen-IR passes, or in the limit a
purpose-built backend, could carry that structural knowledge all the way down to code generation,
fusing patterns that the generic path lowers piecemeal. We raise this as a research direction rather
than a plan: it sits well above the near-term validation work in ambition and well below it in
immediacy, and it should be scoped only once the foundation this report documents is validated
at production scale. But it is a direction that simply did not exist under v2’s self-contained JIT,
and it is, in our view, the most consequential possibility the migration opens.
