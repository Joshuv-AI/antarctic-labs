Microbial Dynamics
during the Removal of Linear Alkylbenzenesulfonate
(LAS) under Anaerobic and Microaerobic Conditions with Different Co-substrates

Maurício Guimarães de Oliveira, Vicente Elício Porfiro Sales Gonçalves da Silva, Amanda de Sousa e Silva, Fabricio Motteran, André Bezerra dos Santos

Abstract

Linear alkylbenzenesulfonate
(LAS) is the most widely
used anionic
surfactant and is commonly found in wastewater due to its extensive
use in detergents and cleaning products. Although LAS is biodegradable,
its degradation in anaerobic environments remains a challenge, often
requiring the addition of co-substrates and optimized operating conditions.
This pioneering study evaluates the microbiological pathways and metabolisms
underlying LAS biodegradation in UASB reactors operated under microaerated
conditions, compared with strictly anaerobic systems. Furthermore,
the effects of different co-substrates (ethanol and sucrose) on LAS
removal efficiency and biogas production were also evaluated. The
results indicate that microaeration, combined with sucrose as a co-substrate,
significantly improves LAS removal, increasing efficiency by 34.3%.
The study also revealed that microaeration influenced methane production,
reducing its purity but increasing overall volumetric biogas production.
Microaeration significantly influenced the microbial structure and
function of the systems, depending on the co-substrate used. In the
presence of ethanol, both systems showed a predominance of fermentative
and methanogenic communities, with no significant differences in LAS
removal. In contrast, with sucrose, microaeration promoted greater
removal efficiency, associated with the selection of a specialized
consortium composed of Pleomorphomonas and Desulfovibrio, capable of partially oxidizing the benzene
ring and desulfonating the surfactant. This condition activated oxidative
and sulfur metabolism pathways, while maintaining functional methanogenesis,
resulting in a less diverse but metabolically more targeted and efficient
community in LAS degradation. These findings highlight the potential
of microaeration and co-substrate selection to optimize anaerobic
treatment of LAS-containing wastewater while maintaining stable reactor
performance.

Introduction

Upflow Anaerobic Sludge
Blanket (UASB) reactors are widely applied
for domestic wastewater treatment and have increasingly been investigated
for the removal of emerging contaminants (ECs), which are of concern
due to their persistence and ecotoxicological effects.
−

 Among them, Linear Alkylbenzenesulfonate (LAS) is the most extensively
used anionic surfactant worldwide and is frequently detected in domestic
wastewater because of its widespread use in detergents, cleaning products,
and personal care formulations.
,
 LAS concentrations in
wastewater treatment plant influents typically range from 0.8 to 21
mg·L–1, and its
occurrence in aquatic environments has been associated with foam formation,
reduced oxygen transfer, and diminished light penetration.
,

Although LAS can be removed under anaerobic conditions, with
efficiencies
in UASB reactors ranging between 30% and 91%, its biodegradation remains
highly dependent on electron donor availability and the activity of
specialized microbial consortia, which partly explains the large variability
in removal efficiencies reported for UASB reactors.
,−

 In this context, readily fermentable co-substrates
such as ethanol and sucrose can enhance LAS removal by supplying reducing
equivalents and metabolic intermediates that sustain syntrophic interactions
and support co-metabolic degradation processes.
,

The importance of these interactions stems from the fact that anaerobic
LAS biodegradation is inherently more complex than its aerobic counterpart,
as it relies on energetically less favorable pathways involving fumarate
addition to the alkyl chain, followed by β-oxidation, aromatic
ring cleavage, and desulfonation. Consequently,
the complete transformation of LAS depends on the coordinated activity
of multiple microbial populations, including members of the Synergistetes
phylum as well as genera such as Pseudomonas and Desulfovibrio, which
have frequently been associated with the degradation of aromatic and
sulfonated intermediates in anaerobic environments.
,,
 As a result, limitations in electron transfer,
syntrophic cooperation, and the activation of key degradation steps
often restrict LAS biodegradation under strictly anaerobic conditions.

Despite the growing number of studies investigating LAS degradation
in anaerobic reactors, most efforts have focused on reactor configuration,
hydraulic retention time, biomass adaptation, and the use of supplementary
carbon sources. These studies have demonstrated that LAS removal can
be achieved under anaerobic conditions, but the reported efficiencies
remain highly variable and frequently limited by the slow degradation
of aromatic intermediates, insufficient electron transfer, and the
accumulation of partially transformed metabolites. Consequently, strategies
capable of overcoming these biochemical bottlenecks while preserving
the energetic advantages of anaerobic treatment remain an important
research challenge.

Given that LAS degradation is typically
more efficient under aerobic
conditions, the strategic introduction of low-oxygen doses into anaerobic
systems via microaeration emerges as a promising approach to enhance
surfactant removal. Controlled microaerobic inputs can stimulate facultative
and microaerotolerant microbial taxa, promote the partial oxidation
of xenobiotic intermediates, and facilitate subsequent anaerobic transformations,
all while preserving the redox conditions required for stable methanogenesis
and biogas production.

Microaeration
has consistently enhanced the removal of diverse
recalcitrant pollutants. For BTEX compounds, microaeration rates of
0.5–2.0 mL air·min–1 increased overall
removal to above 83%, with benzene elimination rising by approximately
30% relative to anaerobic conditions. In the case of pharmaceuticals, applying microaeration in an Anaerobic
Biofilm Blanket – Membrane Bioreactor system increased antibiotic
degradation to 78% for ciprofloxacin and 88–91% for sulfamethoxazole,
clearly surpassing the non-aerated reactor. For dyes, microaeration enabled the complete removal of aromatic
amines from the dye DB22 in a UASB reactor, producing an effluent
16 times less toxic than that from the anaerobic control. Similarly, for hormones and pharmaceuticals,
an airflow of 4 mL·min–1 increased removal
to 90–94%, compared to less than 30% under strictly anaerobic
operation. Microaeration is also widely
applied for hydrogen sulfide oxidation in biogas treatment.

Collectively, these studies indicate that
microaeration acts as
a process intensification strategy capable of selectively activating
oxygen-dependent reactions that are inaccessible under strictly anaerobic
conditions. By modifying local redox conditions, controlled oxygen
supply can influence microbial community structure, extracellular
polymeric substances production, and the distribution of metabolic
functions responsible for contaminant transformation.
−

 Consequently, oxygen availability not only affects the overall extent
of LAS removal, but also determines the dominant biodegradation routes
involved in its mineralization. Recent studies have demonstrated that
variations in oxygen supply can shift the balance between aerobic
and anaerobic metabolic pathways, alter the abundance of key functional
microorganisms, and modify the fate of LAS transformation intermediates.
,
 Together, these findings highlight that oxygen acts not merely as
an electron acceptor, but as a key regulator of the biological processes
governing LAS degradation. Nevertheless, despite these advances, the
application of controlled oxygen supplementation for LAS removal in
anaerobic reactors remains unexplored.

In addition, previous
studies have shown that LAS degradation is
strongly influenced by operational parameters that regulate microbial
metabolism, including oxygen availability, nutrient balance, electron
donor supply, and microbial community composition.
,,
 Variations in these factors can substantially
modify degradation pathways, extracellular polymeric substances accumulation,
nitrogen transformation routes, and the abundance of key microbial
populations involved in LAS mineralization.
,
 Although co-substrate supplementation and microaeration have independently
been shown to enhance the biodegradation of recalcitrant compounds,
their combined effects on anaerobic LAS removal remain unknown. This
knowledge gap is particularly relevant because oxygen supplementation
and co-substrate availability simultaneously affect electron transfer,
syntrophic interactions, oxidative activation reactions, and microbial
adaptation to surfactant stress, all of which are key processes governing
LAS transformation.

Therefore, this study evaluated the combined
influence of controlled
microaeration and readily degradable co-substrates on LAS removal
in UASB reactors treating synthetic domestic wastewater. Particular
attention was given to understanding whether oxygen supplementation
could overcome metabolic bottlenecks traditionally associated with
anaerobic LAS degradation and whether the nature of the co-substrate
influences this response. In addition to reactor performance, the
metabolic pathways potentially involved in LAS transformation were
investigated to provide a mechanistic interpretation of the observed
behavior.

Materials and Methods

The present study
was structured into two distinct experiments.
The first experiment involved the continuous operation of two reactors
to evaluate the effects of microaeration and the use of different
co-substrates (ethanol and sucrose) on LAS removal. The second experiment
consisted of a batch assay designed to preliminarily assess the role
of primary fermentative microorganisms (represented by sucrose), secondary
fermentative microorganisms (represented by ethanol), and acetoclastic
methanogenic archaea (represented by acetate), as well as abiotic
and endogenous processes, in relation to LAS removal efficiencies
in continuous-flow experiments under the tested operational conditions.

Continuous Operation Experiment Setup

Two cylindrical
acrylic UASB reactors (Figure S1) were operated in parallel at the laboratory scale, with
a working volume of 3.25 L. Both reactors were inoculated with 1.6
L of sludge and operated under the same conditions, differing only
in the microaeration insert: R1, traditional UASB, R2, microaerated
UASB (renamed to UMSB – Upflow Microaerobic Sludge Blanket).

The inoculum was sludge from a UASB reactor treating sewage in
Caucaia, Brazil. Total solids, total volatile solids and total fixed
solids in the inoculum sludge were 48 ± 3, 25 ± 1, and 23
± 1 g·L–1, respectively.

Microaeration
in R2 was performed with synthetic air (80% N2:20% O2, White Martins, Brazil) at its base using
a mass flow controller (GFC17, Cole-Parmer, USA). The microaeration
air dose used in R2 was approximately 0.7 Lair·min–1, corresponding to 0.1 LO2. Lfeed–1.d–1, which was kept constant during the experimental
period. The microaeration dose was selected based on the best results
reported by Nascimento et al. (2021), who evaluated the removal of
emerging sulfonated components. The reactors were fed by two peristaltic
pumps (ColeParmer MasterFlex L/S 7522-30, USA) at a flow rate of 6.77
mL·min–1, resulting in an HRT of 8 h, a value
similar to that used in sewage treatment plants.

The experiment was conducted in two sequential phases,
each lasting
45 days, with a 45-day stabilization interval in between. In the first
phase, ethanol was used as the co-substrate, and in the second phase,
sucrose was introduced to evaluate its impact on system performance.
During the stabilization interval, the reactors were fed with ethanol
in the absence of LAS to reset microbial activity and ensure a clear
transition between phases. This structured approach allowed for a
systematic comparison of the effects of different co-substrates on
organic matter degradation and LAS removal under both anaerobic and
microaerobic conditions.

Batch Experiment

At the end of the
first operational phase, sludge samples from both reactors were collected,
homogenized, and used in the presence of acetate, ethanol, and sucrose
to assess LAS degradation and methanogenesis. The experiment was conducted
in triplicate, following the methodology described by Angelidaki et
al. Anaerobic reactors consisted of 300
mL borosilicate glass flasks with a working volume of 200 mL and a
100 mL headspace, maintained under continuous agitation at 150 rpm
and 35 ± 1 °C.

A substrate-to-microorganism ratio
(S/M) of 0.5 gCOD·gVS–1 was adopted, based
on a final concentration of 1 gCOD·L–1 for
the substrate and 2.0 gVS·L–1 for the inoculated
sludge. The basal medium included LAS (15 mg·L–1), macro- and micronutrients, and sodium bicarbonate (1 g·L–1) as a buffering agent. Two control groups were established:
(i) endogenous control (containing LAS, sludge, and basal medium,
but no additional carbon source), and (ii) chemical control (containing
only LAS and distilled water), as illustrated in Figure S2.

All reactors were sealed with butyl rubber
stoppers and purged
with nitrogen (N2) for approximately 1 min to ensure anaerobic
conditions. The experiment was conducted over 174 h, a period determined
based on the cessation of CH4 production, indicating the
completion of the anaerobic digestion process in batch mode.

Synthetic Wastewater

The synthetic
wastewater consisted of an aqueous solution containing LAS (15 mg·L–1), ethanol (1 gCOD.L–1) (Phase I),
sucrose (1 gCOD.L–1) (Phase II), basal medium (macro
and micronutrients) prepared according to Firmino et al. and sodium bicarbonate (1 g·L–1) to maintain the pH close to 7.0. All reagents were used as purchased
without further purification. The synthetic influent was kept refrigerated
at 4 °C to prevent natural biodegradation of organic matter.

Chemical Analysis

Chemical oxygen
demand (COD), pH, total suspended solids, and total solids content
were determined according to APHA. The
quantification of CH4, CO2, H2, and
H2S in the biogas was determined by gas chromatography
with ionization detection by dielectric barrier discharge (GC BID-2010
Plus, Shimadzu Corporation, Japan), equipped with GS-GASPRO column
(60 m × 0.32 mm) (Agilent Technologies Inc., USA). Helium gas
was used as the carrier gas (White Martins LTDA, Brazil) at a flow
rate of 2 mL·min–1, with a run time of 9 min.
Oven, injector, and detector temperatures were 50, 100, and 250 °C,
respectively. O2 and N2 were quantified by gas
chromatography with thermal conductivity detection (GC-TCD) (GC-17A,
Shimadzu Corporation, Japan). The biogas sample (1.0 mL) was injected
in splitless mode, and chromatographic separation was performed on
a Mol Sieve 5A PLOT column (30 m, 0.32 mm ID) (Restek Corporation,
USA). Oven, injector, and detector temperatures were 35, 40, and 230
°C, respectively. Helium (White Martins LTDA, Brazil) was used
as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 7 mL·min–1, and the run time was 5 min.

LAS concentrations were determined
according to the methodology adapted from Silva et al. by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)
using a Shimadzu system (CBM-20A, SPD-20A, CTO-20A, SIL-20A, and LC-20AT)
equipped with a reversed-phase C18 column from Shimadzu. The mobile
phase was water with 5 mmol·L–1 ammonium acetate
(solvent A) and acetonitrile (solvent B); the mobile phase flow rate
was 1 mL·min–1, the sample volume was 50 μL,
the temperature of the thermostated column compartment was 40 °C,
and UV-Vis detection at 230 nm. A gradient was applied to the mobile
phase to separate the LAS constituents. It started with 45% solvent
B, and its concentration began to rise after 5 min, reaching 60% in
20 min. The concentration then returned to 45% after 1 min and remained
constant for 6 min.

Microbiological Analysis

At the beginning
of the experiment and the end of each phase, sludge samples from both
reactors were collected to analyze the microbial changes. For this
purpose, sludge samples were collected from different reactors’
heights, characterizing a biomass composite sample. 0.25 g of fresh
sample (total wet weight) was used to extract DNA from microorganisms
using the PowerSoil® DNA Isolation Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Alemanha),
following the manufacturer’s instructions. Based on the V4
region of the 16S rRNA gene, amplicon sequencing was performed in
triplicate according to the Illumina protocol, using specific primers
(515F/806R).
,

After indexing,
the PCR products (amplicons) were purified using Agencourt AMPure
XP-PCR purification beads (Beckman Coulter, Fisher Scientific, USA),
according to the product manual, and quantified using the dsDNA BR
assay kit (Invitrogen, ThermoFisher Scientific, USA) on a Qubit 2.0
fluorimeter (Invitrogen, ThermoFisher Scientific, USA). The amplicons
were sequenced using the MiSeq 300-cycle chemistry reagent kit (Illumina,
USA) on the MiSeq desktop sequencer (Illumina, USA) at the Genomics
and Bioinformatics Center (CeGenBio) at UFC.

The sequencing
data were analyzed using bioinformatics tools. The
DADA2 package v1.28.0 was used to filter
and assemble reads into amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). The obtained
sequences were taxonomically classified using version 5.1 of the MiDAS
database, which catalogs microorganisms identified in anaerobic digesters,
with the assign Taxonomy method of the DADA2 package. Alpha diversity
indices were estimated with the estimate_richness method of the Phyloseq
package v1.44.0. The raw sequence data obtained in this study have
been deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information
(NCBI) BioProject database, ID PRJNA836880.

To infer the metabolic
capabilities of the microbial communities
present in the reactors samples, the ASV-based 16S rRNA abundance
data were analyzed using PICRUSt2 (Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities
by Reconstruction of Unobserved States). Functional predictions were
generated based on KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes)
pathways, using EC (Enzyme Commission) numbers and KO (KEGG Orthology)
identifiers to assign putative gene functions.
−

Statistical
Methods

The Mann–Whitney
nonparametric test, which do not require specific distributional assumptions,
were used to compare reactor performance across the different experimental
periods at the 5% significance level (p < 0.05).

Results and Discussion

Linear
Alkylbenzenesulfonate (LAS) Removal

To evaluate the effects
of co-substrate type and microaeration
on reactor performance, LAS removal was monitored during both the
acclimatization and stability phases (). During acclimatization, all reactors exhibited
considerable variability, with standard deviations exceeding 10% of
the mean removal efficiency, indicating that the microbial communities
were still adapting to the operational conditions. In contrast, LAS
removal became more stable after reactor stabilization, particularly
in the sucrose-fed systems, reflecting the establishment of a more
robust microbial consortium.

During the stability period under ethanol feeding,
LAS removal
averaged 19.2 ± 3.2% in the control reactor (R1) and 20.8 ±
4.1% in the microaerated reactor (R2), with no statistically significant
difference between reactors (p = 0.32). When sucrose replaced ethanol
as the co-substrate, LAS removal increased significantly in both systems.
In R1, removal efficiency increased from 19.2 to 25.8% (p = 0.034),
whereas in R2 it increased from 20.8 to 34.3% (p < 0.001). The
highest removal efficiency was therefore achieved by the combination
of microaeration and sucrose supplementation, suggesting a synergistic
effect between oxygen availability and co-substrate composition.

These findings indicate that co-substrate selection exerted a greater
influence on LAS degradation than microaeration alone. Sucrose, as
a readily fermentable carbohydrate, likely promoted faster production
of volatile fatty acids and supported a broader range of fermentative,
facultative, and syntrophic microorganisms, creating metabolic conditions
more favorable for surfactant biodegradation than those established
with ethanol.
,
 The effect was particularly evident
under microaerated conditions, where the enhanced metabolic flexibility
may have facilitated the coexistence of oxidative and anaerobic pathways
potentially involved in LAS degradation.

The LAS removal efficiencies
obtained under ethanol feeding were
higher than those reported by Okada et al., who achieved 9.0 ± 2.4% removal in UASB reactors operated under
comparable LAS concentrations using a co-substrate mixture of ethanol,
methanol, and yeast extract. Moreover, although the same authors observed
that increasing co-substrate complexity enhanced LAS removal, reaching
up to 50% with yeast extract supplementation, the present study demonstrates
that sucrose alone was sufficient to significantly improve LAS degradation
under shorter hydraulic retention time conditions (8 h). These observations
reinforce the importance of co-substrate characteristics in determining
the metabolic potential of anaerobic communities involved in LAS degradation.

In contrast, Macedo et al. reported
higher LAS removal with ethanol than with a sucrose–ethanol
mixture in a fluidized-bed reactor. This discrepancy is likely related
to differences in reactor configuration and operating conditions,
highlighting that the effect of co-substrate selection depends on
the operational environment.

From a practical perspective, the
superior performance achieved
by the microaerated reactor supplemented with sucrose demonstrates
the potential of combining low-intensity oxygen supply with an appropriate
co-substrate as a process intensification strategy for UASB reactors
treating LAS-containing domestic wastewater. Because the improvement
was obtained under an HRT of 8 h, a hydraulic condition compatible
with full-scale domestic wastewater treatment plants, this approach
may enhance LAS removal without requiring larger reactor volumes or
major modifications to conventional UASB operation.

Volumetric Biogas Production

To investigate
the effects of co-substrate type and microaeration on methane generation,
volumetric biogas production and methane content were evaluated. shows volumetric
biogas and methane production in reactors operated with sucrose and
ethanol as substrates. Monitoring data show that both reactors achieved
higher volumetric methane production with sucrose. Under ethanol feeding,
R1 (conventional UASB reactor) produced an average of 1.8 ± 0.4
L·day–1, whereas the microaerated reactor (R2)
reached 2.7 ± 0.5 L·day–1. With sucrose,
both reactors showed similar performance: R1 yielded 2.7 ± 0.3
L·day–1, and R2 achieved 2.7 ± 0.4 L·day–1. These findings indicate that sucrose, as a more
complex substrate, supported more uniform methane production across
configurations.

Sucrose, a disaccharide composed of glucose and
fructose, is hydrolyzed
by hydrolytic bacteria into readily fermentable monosaccharides. These
are processed through glycolysis into organic acids, which are subsequently
transformed into acetate and methane by acetoclastic methanogens. Additionally, monosaccharides are converted
into hydrogen (H2) and carbon dioxide (CO2),
which hydrogenotrophic methanogens further reduce to methane. Ethanol,
in contrast, undergoes a narrower metabolic route, being directly
oxidized into acetate or H2 + CO2 by acetogenic
bacteria prior to methanogenesis.

Average methane concentration in biogas produced by R1 reached
73.9% during ethanol and 73.4% during sucrose operation, values exceeding
the typical range for conventional anaerobic digestion (50–70%), reflecting the absence of oxygen and the dominance
of methanogenic activity.

In contrast, microaerated systems
showed lower methane content,
averaging 36.6% (ethanol) and 32.7% (sucrose). For biogas to be considered
a suitable energy source, the methane concentration must exceed 45%. This reduction is primarily due to dilution
from air injection, along with possible shifts in microbial community
structure induced by oxygen.

These trends were consistent with
findings by Nascimento et al. and Oliveira
et al., who reported that microaeration
enhances organic matter removal
but compromises methane purity. Because the lower methane concentration
mainly resulted from biogas dilution rather than inhibition of methanogenic
activity, strategies such as controlled oxygen dosing, intermittent
microaeration, or oxygen-enriched streams may reduce this effect while
preserving the oxidative microzones required for LAS transformation
and the energetic advantage of anaerobic treatment.

Batch Experiment

A batch experiment
was conducted under anaerobic conditions to preliminarily assess the
role of primary fermentative microorganisms (represented by sucrose),
secondary fermentative microorganisms (represented by ethanol), and
acetoclastic methanogenic archaea (represented by acetate), as well
as abiotic and endogenous processes, in relation to LAS removal efficiencies
in continuous-flow experiments under the tested redox conditions.

Endogenous controls revealed that 20% of LAS was adsorbed onto the
biomass under all tested conditions, whereas the chemical control
showed no abiotic LAS removal. Therefore, LAS removal was attributed
to biological processes, whose efficiency depended on the type of
co-substrate present.

LAS removal efficiencies from the liquid
phase after 174 h of reactor
operation were 80.7 ± 4.2%, 57.6 ± 2.9%, 52.2 ± 2.5%,
and 59.1 ± 3.2% for the sucrose, ethanol, acetate, and endogenous
series, respectively (Figure S3). In contrast,
no LAS removal was observed in the chemical control series, which
lacked both co-substrate and biomass, indicating that LAS is not self-biodegradable
under the conditions tested.

LAS removal rates were 0.26 ±
0.4 mg·L–1·h–1 (sucrose),
0.16 ± 0.2 mg·L–1·h–1 (ethanol), 0.18 ±
0.3 mg·L–1·h–1 (acetate),
and 0.16 ± 0.2 mg·L–1·h–1 (endogenous). These results suggest that co-substrates influence
the assimilation rate of organic matter, with sucrose supporting higher
LAS degradation rates than the other conditions, including the endogenous
control.

Among the tested co-substrates, the acetate series
exhibited the
lowest LAS removal efficiency. This may be attributed to the metabolic
limitations or sensitivity of microorganisms, particularly acetoclastic
methanogens, to the anionic surfactant. Granatto et al. also noted that acetoclastic
methanogens are more susceptible to surfactant toxicity, whereas hydrogenotrophic
archaea are generally more resilient.

An important point to
note was the high LAS removal observed in
the endogenous control, indicating that in the absence of more efficient
substrates for co-metabolic processes, such as sucrose or ethanol,
LAS may be directly utilized by the microorganisms present or degraded
through alternative co-metabolic pathways involving exopolysaccharides
or substrates derived from endogenous metabolism.

Molecular Biology

Microbial Community Richness
and Diversity

To evaluate how co-substrate type and microaeration
affected microbial
richness and diversity, alpha-diversity indices and ASV distribution
were analyzed. In general, although richness and diversity indices
were higher in the Bacteria domain, they showed distinct behaviors
within the Archaea (a) and Bacteria (b) domains, modulated by the nature of the co-substrate
and the presence of residual oxygen introduced in R2.

For the Archaea domain,
a consistent pattern was
observed across
all co-substrates. The conventional reactor (R1) showed greater richness
and diversity, suggesting a more complex and balanced methanogenic
community under strictly anaerobic conditions. The greater diversity
of archaea in R1 may be associated with greater functional redundancy
and trophic stability typical of consolidated anaerobic systems, but
did not necessarily reflect greater efficiency in LAS degradation.
This is because methanogenic archaea, although essential in the final
stage of anaerobic digestion, predominantly convert simple intermediates
into methane and do not directly participate in the oxidative reactions
involved in LAS breakdown.
,

In the bacterial
domain, however, the response was more sensitive
to the type of co-substrate. When ethanol was used, the microaerated
reactor (R2) showed greater bacterial richness and diversity, indicating
that the controlled oxygen availability favored the growth of facultative
and microaerophilic groups that could coexist with strict anaerobes.
This increased diversity may have broadened the system’s metabolic
spectrum, although, in this case, LAS removal did not show a statistically
significant difference between the reactors. This result suggests
that, under ethanol, LAS degradation occurred mainly through anaerobic
mechanisms, dependent on specific consortia already well adapted to
the surfactant, and that the presence of residual oxygen did not represent
a significant metabolic advantage. Furthermore, ethanol, being an
easily assimilated substrate, tends to direct metabolism towards rapid
fermentative and acetogenic pathways, reducing the residence time
of aromatic intermediates derived from LAS and, therefore, limiting
the potential benefit of microaeration in the partial oxidation of
these compounds.
,

In the presence of sucrose,
a pronounced shift was observed in
both the microbial community structure and system performance. In
the microaerated reactor, richness and diversity indices decreased
markedly for both Archaea and Bacteria, indicating the emergence of
a more specialized community. This pattern is consistent with the
fact that sucrose (hydrolyzed into glucose and fructose) is rapidly
assimilated by bacteria equipped with highly efficient metabolic machinery
for sugar utilization. As these sugars provide a high energetic yield
and enable rapid ATP and biomass production, microorganisms with higher
growth rates tend to outcompete slower-growing taxa, leading to reductions
in diversity and richness and, in some cases, increased dominance
within the community. Importantly, this community streamlining coincided
with a substantial increase in LAS removal, particularly in R2, suggesting
that the selective advantage conferred by sucrose assimilation favored
microbial groups potentially associated with surfactant degradation.

This inverse relationship between diversity and performance suggests
a process of functional selection, in which microaeration, associated
with a more complex substrate such as sucrose, favored the enrichment
of populations potentially associated with aromatic compound degradation.
Limited oxygenation may have stimulated the activity of facultative
bacteria involved in the initial steps of benzene ring oxidation,
promoting the conversion of recalcitrant intermediates into molecules
more assimilable by subsequent anaerobes, as already observed by other
authors.
,
 Thus, the reduction in diversity reflected
a more targeted and functionally efficient community, adapted to the
degradation of LAS under controlled microaeration conditions.

In the microaerated reactor, this gradient may have intensified,
creating complementary niches between microaerobic and anaerobic zones,
which favor syntrophic pathways between partial oxidants and residual
sulfate or nitrate reducers, as well as oxygen-tolerant methanogens.
Thus, the greater removal of LAS observed in R2 with sucrose results
not only from the presence of oxygen, but also from the interaction
between the type of co-substrate and the consequent restructuring
of the microbial community towards a more specialized and synergistic
consortium.

The Venn diagram analysis corroborated the diversity
patterns observed
in the richness and diversity indices (). R1 operated with sucrose exhibited the
highest number of unique ASVs (1762), indicating a more heterogeneous
microbial community under strictly anaerobic conditions. In contrast,
R2 with sucrose presented only 91 unique ASVs and no ASVs shared with
the inoculum, reflecting a strong microbial selection driven by microaeration
and complete community restructuring. This specialized community coincided
with the highest LAS removal observed in the study. Moreover, the
presence of only 16 ASVs shared between the inoculum and all reactors
indicates that both co-substrate type and microaeration acted as major
selective forces shaping the microbial community. Overall, ethanol
maintained more similar communities, whereas sucrose under microaeration
promoted a less diverse but functionally more specialized consortium.

Microbial Community Structure

To
determine how co-substrate type and microaeration shaped the microbial
community, the taxonomic composition of each reactor was analyzed
().

In systems with ethanol, the phylum Bacillota was
the most dominant, especially in R1, and was mainly represented by
the classes Clostridia and Bacilli and the families Eubacteriaceae and Carnobacteriaceae. In the anaerobic LAS removal experiments by Costa et al., a predominance of the phylum Bacillota was also observed. Delforno et al. also
found a greater abundance of Bacillota in the biomass
of the bed of an expanded granular bed sludge reactor treating effluent
with 14 mg·L–1 of LAS, ethanol, and methanol.

The predominance of the Clostridium and Carnobacterium genera in these reactors indicates the prevalence
of typical fermentative pathways focused on the conversion of simple
substrates into volatile fatty acids, H2, and CO2. These groups play a significant role in the acidogenic stage by
oxidizing organic acids such as acetate, butyrate, and propionate,
but are inefficient at degrading recalcitrant molecules such as LAS.
,

The slight reduction of Clostridium with
microaeration
suggests that residual oxygen exerted a selective pressure against
strict anaerobes, without, however, introducing groups capable of
degrading LAS more effectively. This explains the absence of a statistical
difference in LAS removal between R1 and R2 with ethanol, as the metabolism
was dominated by redundant fermentative pathways, with a low contribution
from initial oxidative pathways.

Upon replacing the co-substrate
with sucrose, a significant microbiological
transition occurred. The phylum Pseudomonadota became
dominant, mainly in the microaerated R2, accompanied by an increase
in the Alpha-, Gamma-, and Desulfovibrionia classes. This change is directly associated
with the increase in LAS removal, as these groups harbor genera with
documented capacity to oxidize aromatic compounds and surfactants. Pseudomonadota has been the most abundant phylum commonly
found in anaerobic systems treating effluents with surfactants, especially
with sucrose as a co-substrate.
,,

In R2_S, the increase in the genera Pleomorphomonas (family Pleomorphomonadaceae, Alphaproteobacteria) and Desulfovibrio (family Desulfovibrionaceae, Desulfovibrionia) evidences the formation of a
specialized microaerophilic-syntrophic consortium. Pleomorphomonas includes species capable of metabolizing aromatic compounds under
microaerobic conditions. At the same time, Desulfovibrio is known to act in sulfate reduction processes and degradation of
sulfonated intermediates, being considered one of the most abundant
genera associated with LAS degradation.
,,
 The co-occurrence of these genera under microaeration
suggests a potential sequential transformation of LAS, in which Pleomorphomonas may contribute to the initial oxidation
of aromatic structures, whereas Desulfovibrio may
participate in subsequent sulfonate transformation. This synergy explains
the superior performance of R2_S in removing LAS, compared with R1_S,
where the absence of oxygen limited the formation of more biodegradable
intermediates.

Furthermore, the stimulation of Pseudomonadaceae
and the pattern
observed within the genus Pseudomonas reinforce the
importance of microaeration for establishing populations with facultative
aerobic metabolism. Pseudomonas was detected only
in R2 with ethanol, indicating that, despite the potential capacity
to degrade LAS, the low complexity of the substrate (ethanol) and
the lower availability of aromatic structural carbon did not favor
its activity. However, the inhibition of this genus in R2 with sucrose
may be due to competition with other microorganisms adapted to the
microaerobic regime and to the accumulation of metabolites that inhibit Pseudomonas growth.

The predominance of Synergistaceae and Anaerolineaceae, associated with the phyla Synergistota and Chloroflexi, respectively,
in R1_S suggests
the involvement of fermentative and syntrophic pathways during intermediate
stages of anaerobic degradation. Although these groups participate
in the fermentation of amino acids, organic acids, and other complex
compounds, they are not directly involved in the initial degradation
of LAS. Synergistota has been frequently reported
in anaerobic systems treating LAS-containing effluents and as an efficient
degrader of toxic compounds, although
the corresponding enzymatic pathways may not always be active. Together
with the enrichment of Bacteroidota, which degrades
polymers and high-molecular-weight compounds but showed similar abundances in R1 and R2, these results indicate
that sucrose favored a more diverse but functionally less specialized
community for surfactant degradation. In contrast, the inhibition
of Actinomycetota, with Atopobiaceae detected only in the inoculum, suggests sensitivity to LAS and microaeration.

The predominance of Methanobacterium (family Methanobacteriaceae, class Methanobacteria, phylum Methanobacteriota) across all systems reflects
the maintenance of hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis, which is essential
for H2 consumption and redox balance. However, the abundance of this genus was reduced in relation
to the inoculum, especially with sucrose, suggesting that the accumulation
of oxygenated or sulfonated intermediates from the partial degradation
of LAS exerted competitive inhibition on the archaea.

Nevertheless,
the greater presence of Methanobacterium in the microaerated
reactors (R2) indicates that microaeration did
not compromise methanogenesis but allowed functional coexistence between
methanogenic populations and oxidative bacteria. This interaction
is consistent with greater LAS removal in R2 with sucrose, where archaea
likely participated in the final mineralization of carbon from the
initial oxidative degradation of the surfactant.

Overall, the
observed taxonomic pattern indicates that microaeration
and sucrose acted as complementary selective forces, reducing diversity
but promoting a functionally specialized community for LAS degradation.
While R1 maintained predominantly fermentative populations (Clostridium, Carnobacterium, Synergistaceae, Anaerolineaceae), R2, with microaeration and sucrose,
favored the establishment of oxidative and syntrophic consortia (Pleomorphomonas, Desulfovibrio, Methanobacterium), potentially associated with partial surfactant
transformation and subsequent mineralization. The coexistence of these
groups suggests a potential metabolic sequence, in which the initial
transformation of LAS under microaerobic conditions may be followed
by the anaerobic conversion of intermediates, resulting in the greater
efficiency observed in R2_S.

Before discussing the microbial
and functional responses observed
in each reactor, it is important to note that LAS removal was evaluated
based on dissolved-phase concentrations. Therefore, the observed decrease
in LAS may result from a combination of biodegradation and biosorption
mechanisms, since surfactants can be partially retained within the
reactor through adsorption onto sludge particles and extracellular
polymeric substances. Although the relative contribution of these
mechanisms was not directly quantified in the present study, the microbiological
and functional analyses revealed substantial shifts in microbial composition
and metabolic potential associated with LAS transformation. These
findings suggest that biological conversion likely contributed most
significantly to the observed LAS removal, although a complete mass
balance between soluble, adsorbed, and transformed fractions was beyond
the scope of this work. Future studies should quantify the LAS associated
with the sludge phase to better distinguish the relative contributions
of biosorption and biodegradation.

Intracellular
Metabolism from Different
Substrates Used under Strictly Anaerobic and Microaerobic Conditions

Anaerobic LAS biodegradation has been associated with a sequence
of metabolic processes involving fumarate addition to the alkyl chain,
β-oxidation, aromatic ring cleavage, and desulfonation. To investigate how the operational conditions
may have affected these processes, the functional potential of the
microbial communities was inferred from the 16S rRNA gene data using
PICRUSt2. The relative abundance of predicted KEGG Orthologs (KOs)
was then compared among the inoculum and the biomass collected from
each reactor ().

The z-score normalization used in the heatmap represents
the relative
abundance of each predicted pathway across the samples. Positive values
indicate that a pathway was comparatively more abundant in a given
sample, whereas negative values indicate a lower relative abundance.
Therefore, these results represent differences in predicted functional
potential rather than direct measurements of gene expression or enzymatic
activity.

The inoculum exhibited a broad functional profile,
including pathways
associated with sucrose metabolism, acetoclastic and hydrogenotrophic
methanogenesis, methylotrophic metabolism, and Acetyl-CoA conversion.
This pattern is consistent with a metabolically diverse anaerobic
community not yet subjected to the selective pressures imposed by
LAS, co-substrate type, and microaeration.

Under ethanol feeding, both reactors were primarily characterized
by pathways associated with methanogenesis and central carbon metabolism.
R1_E showed a comparatively higher representation of the Acetyl-CoA
pathway, whereas R2_E exhibited a stronger relative contribution of
β-oxidation and hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis. These differences
suggest that microaeration modified the predicted functional organization
of the community, although this change did not result in a statistically
significant improvement in LAS removal. Ethanol metabolism may have
preferentially directed carbon and electron flow toward acetogenic
and methanogenic processes, limiting the contribution of additional
oxidative reactions to surfactant transformation.

A more pronounced functional shift was observed after
sucrose addition.
R1_S showed a higher relative representation of sulfur metabolism,
ω-oxidation, ethanol metabolism, and the tricarboxylic acid
cycle. In R2_S, pathways associated with the tricarboxylic acid cycle,
benzene ring cleavage, ω-oxidation, desulfurization, and ethanol
metabolism were simultaneously enriched. This broader distribution
of pathways potentially associated with the transformation of the
alkyl chain, aromatic ring, and sulfonate group coincided with the
highest LAS removal observed in the study.
,,

Overall, the predicted profiles indicate
that ethanol maintained
a functional structure more strongly associated with acetogenic and
methanogenic metabolism, whereas sucrose promoted pathways potentially
associated with the transformation of LAS and its intermediates. This
effect was most evident under microaeration, suggesting that the combination
of sucrose and limited oxygen availability favored a more integrated
functional potential for LAS degradation. The specific microbial groups and KOs associated with these pathways
are examined in the following subsections.

Methanogenic Acetoclastic,
Hydrogenotrophic,
and Methylotrophic Pathways

The predicted acetoclastic, hydrogenotrophic,
and methylotrophic methanogenic pathways were examined to evaluate
how co-substrate type and microaeration influenced carbon conversion
during LAS degradation (). Across all reactors, the majority of predicted methanogenic
functions were associated with the genus Methanobacterium, indicating that hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis represented the
predominant methane-producing route under the evaluated conditions.
This predominance was more evident in the ethanol-fed reactors, consistent
with the availability of reducing equivalents generated during ethanol
oxidation.

In the acetoclastic pathway, the main predicted
KOs were K00197,
K01895, K00925, and K03388 (Figure S4),
which are associated with enzymes involved in acetate activation and
conversion to methane.
,
 Ethanol-fed reactors exhibited
a greater overall abundance of these predicted functions, particularly
R2_E, where K01895 became more abundant than K00197. In contrast,
sucrose-fed reactors showed lower representation of acetoclastic KOs,
although K01895 remained the predominant ortholog, followed by K00925
and K03388. This shift suggests that sucrose supplementation reduced
the relative contribution of acetoclastic methanogenesis while favoring
alternative metabolic processes involved in carbon transformation.

A similar trend was observed for the hydrogenotrophic pathway.
Ethanol-fed reactors showed a higher abundance of predicted hydrogenotrophic
KOs, with K11261, encoding a ferredoxin-dependent hydrogenase, predominating in all systems except R2_S. In
the microaerated sucrose reactor, K03388, K03389, and K03390 became
the dominant orthologs, indicating a redistribution of the predicted
methanogenic functions under conditions that also favored oxidative
pathways associated with LAS transformation.

The methylotrophic
pathway represented the smallest proportion
of the predicted methanogenic functions in all reactors. Nevertheless,
K03388, K03389, and K03390, which encode subunits of methyl-coenzyme
M reductase,
,
 were consistently detected. These
orthologs were comparatively more abundant under ethanol feeding,
whereas sucrose promoted a more balanced distribution, indicating
that methylotrophic methanogenesis remained active but represented
a smaller fraction of the overall methanogenic potential.

Besides Methanobacterium, the predicted methanogenic
pathways were also associated with Methanobrevibacter, Methanosaeta, and Methanosarcina. Methanobrevibacter was mainly related to hydrogenotrophic
methanogenesis, whereas Methanosaeta and Methanosarcina are classical acetoclastic methanogens, with
the latter also capable of methylotrophic metabolism. The coexistence of these genera indicates that multiple
methanogenic routes remained potentially active regardless of the
operational condition.

Overall, ethanol promoted a greater predicted
abundance of methanogenic
functions, whereas sucrose, particularly under microaeration, shifted
the predicted metabolic profile toward a lower relative contribution
of methanogenesis. This pattern is consistent with the broader redistribution
of predicted metabolic functions observed in R2_S, where pathways
associated with LAS transformation became proportionally more represented.
The specific oxidative pathways potentially responsible for this functional
shift are discussed in the following sections.

TCA and Acetyl-CoA Pathways and Benzene
Ring Cleavage Metabolism

To evaluate how co-substrate type
and microaeration influenced central carbon metabolism and aromatic
compound transformation, the predicted TCA cycle, Acetyl-CoA, and
benzene ring cleavage pathways were analyzed (). The systems fed with ethanol showed a
predominance of Methanobacterium and Pseudomonas, with the former being more abundant in the conventional UASB and
the latter in the microaerated system, suggesting that the controlled
presence of oxygen favored microorganisms with more oxidative metabolism.
With sucrose, the behavior was distinct between the systems. In R1, Tolumonas and Pleomorphomonas stood out,
while in R2 Aeromonas and Desulfovibrio prevailed, indicating greater variation in microbial structure under
the different operating conditions.

In general, the microaerated systems presented
a greater number
of KO in this pathway, suggesting greater metabolic activity associated
with the intermediate oxidation of organic compounds (Figure S5). In terms of KO abundance, reactors
with ethanol showed higher values than those with sucrose, with K00247
being the most representative, followed by K00174 and K00175. In systems
with sucrose, the pattern was reversed, with K00175 being the most
abundant, followed by K00174 and K00247.

In the benzene ring
cleavage pathway, reactors fed with sucrose
showed higher KO abundance, indicating greater potential for the degradation
of aromatic compounds under this condition. Among ethanol-associated
genera, the most common was Methanobacterium. At
the same time, with sucrose, Pleomorphomonas stood
out, both of which have been associated with aromatic compound transformation
and the processing of recalcitrant byproducts, possibly derived from
LAS. In all systems, the main KO identified
were K03388 and K07104, with the R2_E reactor standing out, highlighting
the importance of these routes and suggesting a relevant role for
microaeration combined with ethanol in activating oxidative pathways
complementary to surfactant degradation.

In the Acetyl-CoA pathway,
similar behavior was observed across
the systems, with Methanobacterium as the main genus,
although the reactors with sucrose showed greater total abundance,
especially R2_S, which also showed an increase in Methanobrevibacter. This predominance reinforces the importance of these archaea in
the final conversion of intermediates into methane, especially via
the acetoclastic pathway. In all reactors except R2_S, K00197 was
the most abundant KO, followed by K00194, which represent central
steps in acetate activation.
,
 Among the systems,
R1_E showed the highest KO abundance. At the same time, R2_S had the
lowest, suggesting that ethanol favored greater metabolic flux via
Acetyl-CoA and, consequently, greater energy efficiency and LAS degradation.
In contrast, sucrose partially limited this route, possibly by promoting
the accumulation of fermentative intermediates.

Desulfur Metabolism

To investigate
the potential role of sulfur metabolism in LAS degradation under the
different operational conditions, the predicted sulfur metabolism
pathway was analyzed (). In taxonomic and functional terms, the systems with sucrose
showed a profile that favors the reduction of the sulfonate group. Desulfovibrio and Propionivibrio (R1_S
and R2_S) were the dominant genera, with R2_S showing the highest
overall abundance of microorganisms associated with this route. The
high abundance of Desulfovibrio is mechanistically
relevant, as these sulfate-reducing bacteria perform the final steps
of sulfite/sulfonate reduction to sulfide, releasing the organic skeleton
for subsequent anaerobic mineralization.
,
 This may help explain why the sucrose-containing systems, particularly
R2_S, exhibited greater LAS removal. That is, active desulfonation
makes LAS intermediates more accessible to subsequent catabolism.

As for KO, K00390 (ferredoxin-dependent sulfite
reductase) stood
out in all systems, especially in the sucrose-containing reactors
(Figure S6). The high level of K00390 in
R2_S and R1_S suggests a greater predicted potential to convert sulfite
into sulfide, a reaction potentially involved in LAS desulfonation
and corroborates the functional importance of this pathway for surfactant
removal. Furthermore, R1_S showed the
highest total abundance of sulfur metabolism KO genes, suggesting
a strong overall enzymatic potential for desulfonation even in the
absence of microaeration.

However, the high identification and/or
inference of metabolic
functionality based on the number of KO does not guarantee the removal
efficiency of LAS. The difference between R1_S and R2_S assumes that
desulfonation needs to be coupled with initial oxidative steps for
LAS mineralization to be effective. Wei et al. and Yadav et al. point out
that alkyl chain cleavage and aromatic ring opening precede or coincide
with desulfonation.

In R1_S, despite many KO inferred and identified
(including K00390),
the absence of oxidative support limited by strict anaerobiosis restricts
the generation of intermediates that sulfate-reducing microorganisms
can fully transform, explaining why R1_S had lower removal than R2_S.
In contrast, R2_S combines a high presence of sulfur-reducing microorganisms
with oxidative microzones induced by microaeration, which promotes
both the necessary partial oxidation and subsequent desulfonation,
resulting in the highest removal observed. In the ethanol systems,
the profile identified was different. There was a greater balance
between K00299 (relating to the enzyme Adenylyl-sulfate reductase),
K00390, and K04091 (related to the enzyme cysteine desulfurase), and
the communities included Pseudomonas, Mycobacterium, and Desulfomonile (R2_E). This enzymatic composition
indicates that both sulfate activation (K00299), sulfite reduction
(K00390), and sulfur transfer (K04091) were present. Even so, LAS removal did not show a statistically significant
difference between R1_E and R2_E. This likely occurred due to carbon
competition and metabolic prioritization, which limit the impact on
LAS removal.

LAS Degradation by
ω- and β-Oxidation
Metabolism

To evaluate the influence of co-substrate type
and microaeration on LAS side-chain degradation, the predicted ω-oxidation
and β-oxidation pathways were analyzed (), complementing the ring cleavage and desulfonation
routes discussed previously, where the former is responsible for activation
and the latter for sequential degradation.
,

β-oxidation showed higher predicted abundance
than ω-oxidation
in all systems, consistent with the progressive shortening of the
LAS alkyl chain by successive removal of two-carbon units.
,
 The greatest abundance of both pathways occurred in R2_S, coinciding
with the highest LAS removal and suggesting that microaeration favored
the coupling between initial activation and subsequent side-chain
degradation.

In the ethanol-fed reactors, ω-oxidation
was mainly associated
with Aeromonas and Rhodococcus in R1_E, whereas Pseudomonas became
predominant in R2_E, consistent with its recognized ability to initiate
the oxidation of hydrocarbons and anionic surfactants. The predominance of K00128 and K00121 (Figure S7), encoding NAD+-dependent
alcohol dehydrogenase and aldehyde dehydrogenase, suggests activation of the terminal alkyl chain. However,
ethanol likely acted as the preferred electron donor, limiting the
coupling of these initial reactions with downstream degradation and
explaining the absence of significant differences in LAS removal between
R1_E and R2_E.

Under sucrose, the oxidative profile shifted
from K00128 toward
K00239, K00240, and K00241, encoding acyl-CoA dehydrogenases and enoyl-CoA
hydratases involved in β-oxidation,
,
 suggesting a greater predicted potential for side-chain degradation
following the initial oxidation step. This metabolic shift was accompanied
by changes in the dominant genera, with Tolumonas and Pleomorphomonas
prevailing in R1_S and Aeromonas and Tolumonas in R2_S, consistent
with the enrichment of microorganisms associated with long-chain hydrocarbon
oxidation and aromatic compound degradation.

The β-oxidation
profile supported this interpretation. In
the ethanol-fed reactors, Pleomorphomonas and Pseudomonas predominated
together with K00074 (acetyl-CoA acetyltransferase) and K01897 (3-hydroxyacyl-CoA
dehydrogenase), although the metabolic preference for ethanol probably
limited the conversion of this functional potential into higher LAS
removal. In contrast, sucrose favored the enrichment of Smithella
and Aeromonas, while K01897 was particularly abundant in R1_S, indicating
active conversion of 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA to β-ketoacyl-CoA. Despite the higher abundance of this KO in R1_S,
R2_S achieved the greatest LAS removal, suggesting that microaeration
enhanced the integration of ω- and β-oxidation and promoted
more efficient degradation of partially oxidized intermediates.

Ethanol and Sucrose Metabolism

The
predicted pathways associated with ethanol and sucrose metabolism
were examined to determine how each co-substrate shaped carbon conversion
and its potential interaction with LAS degradation (). In the ethanol-fed reactors, Pseudomonas and Methanobacterium were the
predominant genera associated with ethanol metabolism, indicating
the coexistence of bacteria potentially involved in ethanol oxidation
and hydrogenotrophic methanogens capable of consuming the resulting
intermediates.

The main predicted KOs associated
with this pathway
were K01895,
related to acetaldehyde dehydrogenase and the conversion of acetaldehyde
into Acetyl-CoA, and K01897, related to 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase
and β-oxidation (Figure S8). The
greater relative abundance of K01897 in the ethanol-fed reactors suggests
that carbon metabolism was strongly directed toward the conversion
of ethanol and its intermediates. Because ethanol is readily metabolized,
its preferential consumption may have supported microbial growth and
methanogenic metabolism while limiting the relative contribution of
LAS as a carbon or electron source.

Predicted functions associated
with ethanol metabolism were also
detected in the sucrose-fed reactors, indicating metabolic connectivity
between sugar fermentation and pathways involving ethanol-derived
intermediates. Under these conditions, Pleomorphomonas predominated in R1_S, whereas Aeromonas was more strongly associated
with R2_S. The relative abundance pattern of the KOs was also reversed,
with K01895 exceeding K01897, suggesting a greater potential for the
conversion of fermentative intermediates into Acetyl-CoA and a comparatively
lower representation of β-oxidation-related functions within
this specific pathway. Although R1_S exhibited the highest total abundance
of KOs associated with ethanol metabolism, R2_S achieved greater LAS
removal, indicating that the abundance of an individual pathway alone
was insufficient to explain reactor performance.

The sucrose
metabolism pathway further distinguished the microbial
responses to the two co-substrates. Even in R1_E and R2_E, where sucrose
was not supplied, functions related to sucrose metabolism were detected
at a basal level, possibly reflecting the broad carbohydrate-processing
potential of the microbial community. Clostridium was the predominant genus in these reactors, accompanied by Atopobium
in R1_E and Carnobacterium in R2_E. These fermentative
taxa may contribute to the production of organic acids that subsequently
support acetogenic and methanogenic populations. Consistently, K01193,
associated with sucrose phosphorylase, was comparatively more abundant
under ethanol feeding, indicating the predicted occurrence of carbohydrate–conversion
routes capable of generating glucose-1-phosphate under reducing conditions.

When sucrose was directly supplied, the functional and taxonomic
profiles changed markedly. Pleomorphomonas and Clostridium predominated in R1_S, whereas their relative
contributions varied in R2_S, evidencing a restructuring of the community
in response to the carbohydrate co-substrate. In both sucrose-fed
reactors, K01187, associated with invertase or β-fructofuranosidase,
became the most abundant predicted KO in this pathway. This enzyme
catalyzes the initial hydrolysis of sucrose into glucose and fructose,
thereby supplying intermediates to glycolysis, the TCA cycle, and
Acetyl-CoA formation.

Therefore, the predominance of K01187
indicates a greater predicted
capacity for the direct fermentation of sucrose and the generation
of central metabolic intermediates. These intermediates may have supported
methanogenic carbon conversion while also sustaining microbial populations
potentially involved in LAS transformation. However, because R2_S
achieved the highest LAS removal despite not consistently exhibiting
the greatest abundance of every co-substrate-related KO, the results
indicate that reactor performance was more closely associated with
the integration of fermentative and oxidative metabolic potentials
than with the isolated abundance of the ethanol or sucrose pathways.

Impact of Microaeration and Co-substrate
on LAS Metabolic Pathways

To integrate the microbiological
and functional findings obtained throughout this study, the combined
effects of microaeration and co-substrate on the predicted LAS degradation
pathways are discussed below. The results indicate that the influence
of microaeration on LAS degradation was strongly associated with the
metabolic environment established by the co-substrate. Although oxygen
supplementation was applied under the same operational conditions,
its effects differed substantially between ethanol- and sucrose-fed
reactors, suggesting that the response to microaeration was governed
not only by oxygen availability but also by the structure and functionality
of the microbial consortium supported by each carbon source.

Under ethanol supplementation, LAS degradation appeared to be primarily
driven by conventional anaerobic processes. The predominance of fermentative
and methanogenic microorganisms, together with the enrichment of pathways
associated with carbon fermentation and methane production, suggests
that most of the electron flow was likely directed toward the conversion
of readily biodegradable substrates. Consequently, microaeration produced
only limited changes in LAS removal, indicating that the oxygen supplied
was insufficient to substantially redirect the dominant metabolic
routes established in the reactor.

In contrast, sucrose supplementation
resulted in a markedly different
metabolic response. The greater availability of fermentative intermediates
likely created conditions that favored syntrophic interactions and
increased metabolic flexibility, allowing the microbial community
to more effectively exploit the oxygen introduced through microaeration.
This shift was accompanied by the enrichment of predicted functional
pathways potentially associated with aromatic compound transformation,
side-chain oxidation, sulfur metabolism, and the subsequent conversion
of degradation intermediates. Collectively, these predictions are
consistent with a greater potential for LAS transformation and further
processing of its degradation products.

These findings suggest
that microaeration may not have simply increased
the availability of an additional electron acceptor, but may also
have influenced the sequence of metabolic reactions potentially involved
in LAS degradation. Under sucrose supplementation, oxygen may have
facilitated the initial activation of the surfactant molecule and
the transformation of aromatic intermediates that are typically degraded
slowly under strictly anaerobic conditions. The resulting intermediates
could subsequently be utilized by anaerobic microorganisms through
pathways potentially involving β-oxidation, sulfur transformation,
and methanogenesis, thereby establishing a complementary interaction
between oxidative and reductive processes.

Therefore, the combined
application of microaeration and sucrose
appears to have favored a hybrid degradation pathway in which oxygen-dependent
reactions may have enhanced the initial transformation of LAS, while
anaerobic metabolism likely contributed to the subsequent conversion
of intermediates. This metabolic coupling provides a plausible mechanistic
explanation for the superior LAS removal observed in the microaerated
sucrose-fed reactor and highlights the importance of simultaneously
considering redox conditions and co-substrate selection when designing
strategies to improve surfactant removal in anaerobic wastewater treatment
systems.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated
the potential of microaeration as a strategy
to improve LAS degradation under anaerobic conditions. However, the
response to oxygen supplementation differed according to the co-substrate
employed, suggesting that the metabolic environment established within
the reactor may influence the extent to which microaeration stimulates
surfactant transformation. In this regard, the most pronounced improvement
in LAS removal was observed when sucrose was used as the co-substrate.

The enhanced performance achieved under sucrose supplementation
was associated with a substantial restructuring of the microbial community
and its metabolic functions. Microaeration favored the establishment
of a more specialized consortium capable of coupling oxidative and
anaerobic degradation pathways, thereby facilitating the conversion
of aromatic and sulfonated intermediates while preserving the overall
functionality of the anaerobic process. These findings suggest that
the benefits of microaeration extend beyond direct oxygen supply and
are closely linked to its ability to reshape microbial metabolism
and promote complementary degradation routes.

From a practical
standpoint, the results suggest that microaeration
can be implemented as a low-cost process intensification strategy
to improve LAS removal in UASB reactors treating domestic wastewater.
The positive response observed under sucrose supplementation indicates
that oxygen supply and co-substrate selection may act synergistically
to overcome metabolic limitations associated with anaerobic LAS degradation.
Because the improvement was achieved under an HRT typical of full-scale
UASB reactors, the proposed approach may represent a feasible alternative
for enhancing surfactant removal in existing treatment plants without
substantial infrastructural modifications. Future studies should investigate
the effects of different microaeration rates, redox mediator supplementation,
alternative electron acceptors such as nitrate and nitrite, evaluate
the integration of bioaugmentation or co-metabolic strategies, and
assess the long-term impacts of microaeration on effluent quality
under real-scale operating conditions.
