Genome editing of OsSBEIIb rewires starch architecture to lower predicted glycemic index and enhance protein quality in rice endosperm
Authors: Sakshi Kor, Rhowell Tiozon, Sung-Ryul Kim, Reuben James Q Buenafe, Ana Rose Ramos-Castrosanto, Vipin Pratap, Edward Wake, Paul D Fraser, Alexander Erban, Joachim Kopka, Vito Butardo Jr, Alisdair R Fernie, Nese Sreenivasulu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStarch branching enzyme IIb (OsSBEIIb) plays a key role in determining amylopectin structure and starch digestibility. In this study, CRISPR/Cas9 was used for splice site disruption of OsSBEIIb in the elite indica cultivar IR64, generating a truncated protein with impaired function and reduced relative expression levels. The result showed extensive remodeling of starch fine structure, including reduced short-chain amylopectin chains, increased long-chain glucan fractions, higher apparent amylose content up to 39% and elevated resistant starch (RS) levels up to 10%. These modifications were accompanied with substantial 23-unit decreased predicted glycemic index (pGI), from 80 ± 1 in SBE2b_WT to 57 ± 1 in sbe2b mutant lines. Biophysical analyses showed reduced relative crystallinity but higher gelatinization temperatures, indicating more thermally stable starch structures. Beyond starch, sbe2b mutants displayed improved nutritional profiles, with crude protein content increasing by up to 10% and elevated levels of several essential amino acids, including lysine, methionine, threonine and histidine. Metabolite and lipid profiling revealed broader endosperm metabolic shifts affecting carbohydrates, amino acids, phenylpropanoids, fatty acids and triacylglycerols. However, these nutritional benefits were offset by grain quality and agronomic trade-offs, notably increased chalkiness and reduced seed weight. Overall, OsSBEIIb disruption effectively lowers glycemic properties while enhancing protein quality through coordinated metabolic changes in rice endosperm.Keywords: SBEIIb, Glycemic index, Rice protein, Essential amino acids, Metabolomics

Introduction

Rice (Oryza sativa L.) sustains more than half of the world's population, with polished grains composed predominantly of starch (∼90%), followed by protein (6–8%) and lipids (0.5–1%) (Kasote et al., 2022). Despite its central role in meeting caloric requirements, rice is characterized by a rapid starch digestion leading to elevated postprandial hyperglycemia. Increased starch digestibility is commonly quantified by the high glycemic index (GI) and lower RS content of milled rice. GI reflects the postprandial blood glucose response elicited within 2 h of consuming a defined amount of available carbohydrate relative to a reference food, whereas RS denotes the fraction of starch that escapes digestion in the small intestine and undergoes microbial fermentation in the colon, yielding health-promoting short-chain fatty acids and dietary fiber–like effects (de Guzman et al., 2017; Butardo and Sreenivasulu, 2016). Growing evidence indicates that lowering the GI along with increasing the RS and essential amino acids (EAAs) of staple foods offers significant health benefits, including improved glycemic control and reduced risk of metabolic disorders such as dyslipidemia, cardiovascular disease, and certain cancers (Eleazu et al., 2016). With increasingly sedentary lifestyles and a rising burden of diet-related diseases, the development of rice varieties with low GI and high-quality protein property has emerged as a critical nutritional priority, particularly in low- and middle-income countries in Asia where rice constitutes the primary caloric intake with the highest per capita consumption ranging from 100 to 150 kg/person annually. Indeed, recent estimates suggest that replacing high-GI white rice with low-GI alternatives could reduce diabetes incidence by at least ∼11% (Tiozon et al., 2025a).

IR64 is an elite indica mega-variety that has been widely cultivated for decades across South and Southeast Asia and remains an important parent in rice breeding programs. It combines high yield potential, early maturity, strong resistance to major diseases and pests, and preferred grain quality attributes such as long slender grains, strong milling recovery with intermediate amylose content that result in non-sticky cooked texture (Mackill and Khush, 2018). Its strong genetic alleles make IR64 an ideal target for genome editing where the focus would be improving the nutritional quality of rice which is widely accepted and consumed, thus having potential for precision-breeding application and product development through genome editing.

Among starch biosynthetic enzymes, SBEIIb (LOC_Os02g32660) strongly influence amylopectin architecture and starch digestibility (Nakamura, 2018). Loss of SBEIIb activity in cereals leads to pronounced remodeling of amylopectin, characterized by depletion of short-chain amylopectin (SCAP) and a redistribution toward longer glucan chains, which promotes more ordered crystalline structures and increases RS accumulation (Baysal et al., 2016; Butardo et al., 2011; Guo et al., 2020; Li et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2017; Yang et al., 2012; Tappiban et al., 2022). Previous studies have demonstrated that OsSBEIIb mutations increase RS and reduce in vitro digestibility proxies; however, the detailed mechanistic relationship between OsSBEIIb-driven changes in amylopectin fine architecture and predicted glycemic index has not been fully elucidated at multiple structural and physicochemical levels.

While SBEIIb modifications have focused on starch and RS, protein quality also requires attention, as most of the milled rice has lesser protein content and deficient in EAAs. Badoni et al. (2024) identified multiple QTLs across chromosomes 2, 3, 4, 5, and 8 using a RIL population of High Amylose High Protein lines, underscoring the complex genetic architecture underlying protein accumulation in rice. In rice, emerging evidence indicates that starch and protein accumulation are not independently regulated but are coordinated through interconnected carbon–nitrogen (C–N) networks (Tiozon et al., 2026). Amino acid transporters such as OsAAP6 enhance grain protein content while concomitantly reducing starch accumulation (Peng et al., 2014), whereas transcriptional regulators, including OsNAC20/26 (Wang et al., 2020) and NF-Y family members (Chen et al., 2026), co-regulate storage protein genes and starch biosynthetic pathways (Chen et al., 2022). In parallel, central metabolic enzymes such as OsGAPC3 (Peng et al., 2024) and OsPPDKB (Yang et al., 2024; Bowen et al., 2026) integrate carbon flux with nitrogen assimilation, collectively shaping endosperm composition. Despite these advances, it remains unclear whether targeted perturbation of OsSBEIIb, can induce coordinated changes in both starch structure and protein–amino acid metabolism through shifts in C–N allocation.

Despite extensive research on the role of OsSBEIIb in amylopectin fine structure and resistant starch accumulation, previous studies have primarily focused on starch-related traits. It remains unclear whether targeted disruption of OsSBEIIb can simultaneously remodel starch architecture to lower the glycemic index and enhance protein content and essential amino acid profiles in the polished endosperm through coordinated shifts in C–N metabolism. In this study, we aimed to determine whether Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats–CRISPR-associated protein (Cas) CRISPR/Cas9-mediated splice-site disruption of OsSBEIIb in the elite indica cultivar IR64 can generate dual nutritional benefits, of reduced predicted glycemic index and improved protein quality, while elucidating the underlying endosperm metabolic reprogramming.

Materials and methods

CRISPR/cas-9-based gene editing

A 20-bp single guide RNA (sgRNA) targeting the 15th exon of the OsSBEIIb gene was designed using the CRISPR RGEN tool (http://www.rgenome.net/about/). The vector was constructed per (Badoni et al., 2024) and transformed into IR64 high-yielding, superior grain quality indica rice variety via Agrobacterium-mediated immature embryo transformation (Slamet-Loedin et al., 2014). Through this plant transformation, 65 T0 plants were obtained. Oligonucleotides details are in Table S1.

Mutant identification through PCR and Sanger sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from leaf tissue of T0 plants using a simplified extraction protocol (Kim et al., 2016). The genomic region spanning the CRISPR/Cas9 target site was subsequently amplified with primers SBEIIb_TS1_F1/R1 (Table S1). Amplicons were resolved on 1.2% agarose gel, and selected PCR products were Sanger-sequenced on an ABI 3730 platform (Macrogen Inc., South Korea). Chromatograms were inspected in BioEdit and mutations at the exon-15 donor splice site were identified by manual alignment against the wild-type OsSBEIIb sequence.

Plant growth conditions for genome-edited plants

The tissue-culture obtained plants were grown in glasshouse conditions. The seeds of the individual plants were harvested upon maturity and stored at a transgenic seed storage facility at 18°C. In this study, T3–derived generation homozygous milled/polished grains were used for all biochemical analyses.

RNA extraction, RT-PCR and qRT-PCR

Mutant lines carrying single-nucleotide indels or large deletions that disrupted the exon 15 donor splice site were selected for transcript analysis through RT-PCR and Sanger sequencing. Developing grains from the sbe2b mutants and SBE2b_WT plants were collected at 14 days after flowering (DAF). Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer's instructions. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using the GoScript™ Reverse Transcription System (Promega).

To assess the consequences of donor-site disruption on pre-mRNA splicing, RT-PCR was performed with primers flanking the CRISPR target site in exon 15 (Table S1). Reaction of total 20 μl contained 2 μl of 1:5 diluted cDNA, 1 μM of each primer, 10 mM dNTPs and 10X PCR buffer. The RT-PCR products were resolved on 1.2% agarose gels to detect alternative splicing events. Remaining amplicons were subjected to Sanger sequencing (Macrogen Inc., South Korea) and chromatograms were visualized and analyzed manually using BioEdit software to confirm the precise alterations at the splice junctions.

Quantitative expression of OsSEIIb was measured by qRT-PCR with three technical replicates. Reaction volume of 10 μl contained 2 μl of 1:10 diluted cDNA, 0.3 μM of each primer (Table S1 ;Baysal et al., 2020) and 2X SYBR Select Master Mix (Applied Biosystems), with OsActin1 as the internal control gene. Amplification was performed on a 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) following the program: 50 °C for 2 min and 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 0.03 s and 60 °C for 30 s, and a final melting curve stage (Tappiban et al., 2022, with minor modifications). Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^{−ΔΔCt} method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001). The statistical analysis and data visualization were performed in RStudio using Student's t-test.

Protein structural modeling

The OsSBEIIb coding sequence (CDS) of non-edited intact SBEIIb in IR64 (SBE2b_WT) and putative mutant CDS were translated using the Expasy translate tool (https://web.expasy.org/translate/). The 3D homology-based structure was generated with the Expasy Swiss-Model server. (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/interactive). They were visualized and structurally annotated using UCSF ChimeraX (https://www.cgl.ucsf.edu/chimerax/).

In vitro predicted glycemic index (pGI) and resistant starch phenotyping

The in vitro pGI of milled rice samples from sbe2b mutants and it's SBE2b_WT control was determined using the NutraScan GI20 Artificial Gut Analyzer, an automated system that simulates oral, gastric, pancreatic, and intestinal digestion. The assay was performed following the protocol of Pautong et al. (2026). Glucose release was quantified using the Glucose oxidase (GO) and Peroxidase (POD) (GOPOD) reagent from Megazyme, with absorbance measured at 510 nm. RS and digestible carbohydrate (DC)were quantified using the Resistant Starch assay kit (K-RSTAR) from Megazyme following the method by Pautong et al. (2026) with modification. Cooked rice flour was incubated for 4 h of enzymatic digestion followed by separation of the RS and DC fractions through centrifugation. The glucose released from both fractions reacted with the GOPOD reagent and measured spectrophotometrically at 510 nm. The total carbohydrate (TC) was calculated as sum of RS and DC. The analyses were measured with three biological and technical replicates per sample. Each biological replicate (n = 3) data is presented as average of three technical replicates in Table S2.

For all the above analyses, statistical significance among samples were determined by Student's t-test and one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan's Multiple Range Test (DMRT) at p < 0.05 using R software.

Chain length distribution (CLD) analysis by size exclusion chromatography

For size-exclusion chromatography (SEC), milled rice flour (55 mg) was gelatinized and debranched with isoamylase as previously described in Ward et al., 2006, using glass scintillation vials containing 400 ml of 95% ethanol and 1 ml of 0.25 M NaOH. The debranched sample was desalted in a 2-ml microtube containing approximately 320 mg ion-exchange resin [Bio-Rad AG 501-X8 (D)] and incubated for 30 min at 50°C with mixing every 10 min. The solution was then pipetted into a 150-ml mandrel insert and placed in a sample vial with a septum with a slit. An aliquot of the debranched solution (40 μl) was analyzed using Agilent 1260 Infinity II HPLC, fitted with an Ultrahydrogel 250 column (Waters) and Refractive Index detector, using 0.05 M ammonium acetate buffer, pH 4.75, with 0.02% sodium azide as mobile phase. The starch molecular weights were determined using the Mark-Houwink-Sakaruda equation based on the calibration of the individually injected pullulan standards (P800, P400, P200, P100, P50, P20, P10, and P5; P-82 Shodex; ShowaDenko). The SEC analysis was performed with two technical replicates per sample, and the results were averaged (Table S3).

Amino acid quantification using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and protein content via the Kjeldahl method

The amino acids were quantified using the method reported by (Sekhar and Reddy, 1982) with slight modifications. For amino acid quantification, seeds from all samples were ground into a fine powder for analysis. Approximately 0.5-1.0 g of each sample were weighed and transferred to a hydrolysis tube, followed by the addition of 3 ml of 6 N HCl. The tubes were sealed and placed in an oven at 110 °C for 24 h for hydrolysis. After hydrolysis, the samples were diluted to 15 ml with borate buffer to adjust the pH to 6.5-7.5. The resulting solution was heated in a water bath at 37°C for 1 h, then filtered through a 0.22 μm filter into a 1.5 ml HPLC vial for subsequent analysis. Values are presented as the mean ± standard deviation of the three replicates, and statistical significance was assessed by Student's t-test (Table S6).

The protein content was determined by the Kjeldahl method as described in AOAC Official Method 2001.11 (AOAC, 2001), using a Kjeltec™ 8200 system (FOSS, Sweden). 0.5 g of milled rice flour was taken in a digestion tube and 15 ml of concentrated sulphuric acid and 2 catalyst tablets (Kjeltec™ 8200, Kjeltec, Foss, Sweden) were added to it. The samples, along with blanks, were digested using a Kjeldahl digester unit (Labtec DT220 from FOSS) for 75 min at 420 °C. It was then cooled for approximately 30 min to bring it to room temperature, and then the distillation of each of the samples and the blank was done using a Kjeltec 8200 distillation unit from FOSS. During the distillation process, 80 ml of deionized water, 60 ml of 40% sodium hydroxide solution, and 30 ml of 4% boric acid solution were used. After distillation, the samples were titrated against a 0.1 N HCl solution, and the blank titration value was subtracted from that of each of the samples. A nitrogen-to-protein conversion factor of 5.95 was used to calculate percent protein for rice samples (Juliano et al., 1971).

The Kjeldahl analysis was performed from duplicates. Data is presented as mean ± standard deviation, and statistical significance was determined using Student's t-test (Table S5).

Metabolite and lipid extraction and measurement

Metabolites were extracted following (Tiozon et al., 2023a) with minor adjustments. Briefly, 1 ml of pre-chilled methyl tert-butyl ether:methanol (3:1, v/v) was added to homogenized tissues, vortexed for 1 min, shaken at 4 °C for 15 min, and sonicated for another 15 min. Phase separation was induced by adding 500 μL of water:methanol (3:1, v/v), followed by centrifugation at 13,300 × g for 5 min. The semi-polar phase was used for primary and specialized metabolite profiling, while the organic phase was analyzed for lipids.

Semi-polar metabolites were reconstituted in water:methanol (1:1, v/v) and analyzed using a Thermo Q Exactive Focus Liquid Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (LC–MS) system with a C18 column (40 °C, 400 μL min−1) under a water/acetonitrile gradient containing 0.1% formic acid. Mass spectra were acquired in full-scan mode (m/z 100–1500) with data-independent acquisition (HCD = 30 eV, positive ion mode). Pooled samples were analyzed under both DIA and DDA modes for metabolite annotation.

For primary metabolites, derivatization followed (Lisec et al., 2006) using methoxyamine hydrochloride in pyridine and MSTFA prior to Gas Chromatography–Time-of-Flight Mass Spectrometry (GC–TOF–MS) analysis (LECO Pegasus HT) on a DB-35 column. For the lipidomic analysis, dried extracts were re-dissolved in acetonitrile:isopropanol (7:3, v/v) and analyzed on an Orbitrap LC–MS system (Exactive, Thermo Fisher Scientific) as described by (Tiozon et al., 2025b). Separation was performed on a C18 column (60 °C, 400 μL min−1) using water and acetonitrile:2-propanol (7:3, v/v) with 0.1% acetic acid. Spectra were collected in positive ion mode (m/z 150–1500), with additional runs in negative mode for free fatty acid profiling. For both the metabolomics and lipidomics studies was conducted using three replicates of T1-derived homozygous milled seeds.

Pasting properties of the gene-edited lines

The milled rice flour of the mutants was subjected to rapid viscosity analysis (RVA) to characterize the visco-elastic properties of flour using RVA-4 (Newport Scientific Warriewood, Australia), following the AACC Method 61-02 (Butardo et al., 2017; Buenafe et al., 2021). Exactly 3.5 g of flour was placed in an RVA canister and mixed with 25.0 g of distilled deionized water, which was weighed using an analytical balance. The prepared canister with paddle was inserted into the RVA machine and ran immediately using a slightly modified high temperature profile of 120°C, designed to mimic the old Brabender Amylograph. The analysis was performed on single biological and technical replicates for each sbe2b mutant and SBE2b_WT.

X-ray diffraction analysis of starch crystalline structure

The starch crystalline structure of the selected sbe2b_069 mutant and SBE2b_WT was evaluated by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Miniflex 600C X-ray diffractometer (Rigaku) equipped with Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å). Diffraction data were collected at a resolution of 0.01°. Differential scanning calorimetry of sbe2b and SBE2b_WT starch

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) analysis was performed following the protocol described previously (Tiozon et al., 2024). Flour samples (5.0 mg) were combined with distilled water (10.0 μL) and sealed in hermetic aluminum pans. Thermal analysis was conducted using a TA Instruments DSC 25 equipped with a cooling system. Samples were heated from 25 to 120 °C at a rate of 10 °C min−1. An empty sealed pan was used as the reference.

Thermal transition parameters, including onset temperature (To), peak temperature (Tp), conclusion temperature (Tc), and enthalpy of gelatinization (ΔH), as well as higher-temperature transitions associated with amylose–lipid complex melting, were determined using TRIOS software (TA Instruments). All measurements were performed in two replicates, and results are presented as mean ± standard deviation.

Results

CRISPR/Cas9-mediated disruption of OsSBEIIb generates truncated loss-of-function alleles in IR64 background

The 15th exon of the OsSBEIIb gene was targeted using the CRISPR/Cas9 system to generate knockout lines and structural variants (Fig. 1A). A total of 115 independent transgenic events were obtained from CRISPR/Cas9-transformed immature embryos of IR64. Of these, 20 events were subjected to Sanger sequencing of genomic DNA to identify the mutations at the genomic level. This analysis revealed a range of mutations, from single base pair insertions/deletions to large deletions exceeding 100 bp. Specifically, T3–derived generation homozygous grains mutants with independent events sbe2b_002, sbe2b_003, and sbe2b_008 carried single base pair indels, whereas sbe2b_062 and sbe2b_065 harbored large deletions (Fig. 1B).

To confirm the change at mRNA transcript level, RT-PCR was performed using cDNA with primers flanking the gRNA target site in exon 15. Clear differences in amplicon size were observed: mutants sbe2b_003, sbe2b_062, and sbe2b_069 produced smaller bands compared with the single-insertion mutants sbe2b_002 and sbe2b_008, which showed the same band size as the SBE2b_WT controls (Fig. 1C, Fig. S1). Sequencing of these amplicons revealed that mutants sbe2b_003, sbe2b_062, and sbe2b_069 exhibited complete deletion of exon 15, upon disruption of the alternative splicing site on the same exon 15 due to disruption of the donor splice site, whereas sbe2b_002 and sbe2b_008 retained a single-base-pair insertion at the mRNA level (Fig. S2).

The OsSBEIIb gene comprises 22 exons and encodes a protein of 825 amino acids (aa) in the IR64 SBE2b_WT. In silico translation of the mutant transcripts predicted frameshifts that introduced premature stop codons, resulting in truncated proteins of 566 aa (sbe2b_002 and sbe2b_008) and 531 aa (sbe2b_003, sbe2b_062, and sbe2b_069) (Figs. S2 and S3).

The N-terminal 65 amino acids constitute a plastid transit peptide (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/TargetP-2.0/), essential for targeting SBEIIb to the amyloplast, the site of starch biosynthesis (Ke et al., 2025). The functional OsSBEIIb protein is organized into three domains: an N-terminal domain (aa 66–292), a central catalytic (α/β) domain (aa 293–727), and a C-terminal domain (aa 728–825). The catalytic domain contains seven highly conserved residues—Asp402, His407, Arg471, Asp473, Glu528, His595, and Asp596, of which Asp473 (nucleophile), Glu528 (proton donor), and Asp596 (transition-state stabilizer) form the catalytic triad essential for α-1,6-branch formation (Fig. 1E and F).

Homology-based structural modeling predicted pronounced disruption of the catalytic core in the sbe2b mutants’ proteins. In the sbe2b mutants, His595 and Asp596 were consistently absent, indicating near-complete loss of catalytic functionality. Notably, residues Arg471, Asp473, and Glu528, which stabilize the catalytic center and mediate glycosidic bond cleavage, were preserved in these sbe2b mutants (Fig. 1G and H). Structural features involved in substrate recognition were differentially retained in the comparative models (Fig. S4).

To further examine the expression of OsSBEIIb and other genes influencing starch structure and composition, including starch branching enzymes (SBEs) and granule-bound starch synthase I (GBSSI), qRT-PCR analysis was performed. Expression of SBEIIb transcripts was strongly reduced in the sbe2b mutants compared with the SBE2b_WT (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1D). OsGBSSI also showed a significant reduction in the sbe2b mutants (p < 0.05 and p < 0.01) relative to the SBE2b_WT (Fig. S5A). Among the other SBEs, OsSBEI expression did not differ significantly from the SBE2b_WT (Fig. S5B). Interestingly, OsSBEIIa, which is primarily expressed in leaf tissue, showed increased relative expression in all mutants (p < 0.05), except sbe2b_003 (Fig. S5C). Collectively, the presence of frameshift mutations leading to premature termination at the mRNA level, the consequent loss of the catalytic domain, and the strongly reduced transcript levels demonstrate that the alleles show loss-of-function of SBEIIb gene in the mutants.

OsSBEIIb disruption leads to reduced glycemic index, increased resistant starch with altered chain length distributions in the rice endosperm

All sbe2b knockout mutants exhibited a marked reduction in pGI (57 ± 1), with an average decrease of approximately 23 units relative to the SBE2b_WT in IR64 (80 ± 1, p < 0.05) (Fig. 2A), indicating a shift from a high-GI phenotype toward an intermediate/low-GI range. Complement to this shift, the RS fraction increased from 0.56 ± 0.08% in the SBE2b_WT to 7.84-10.94% across sbe2b mutant lines (Fig. 2B). The mutants showed decrease in amount digestible carbohydrates (DC) as compared to the SBE2b_WT (55.3% to 67.14%, respectively. p < 0.05) (Fig. 2C). These results indicate that making sbe2b non-functional produced starch that is less rapidly digested and has a larger digestion-resistant fraction. In contrast, total carbohydrate content remained largely unchanged between sbe2b mutant and SBE2b_WT lines (Fig. 2D).

Because OsSBEIIb gene is involved in α-1,6 branching formation of amylopectin, especially in formation of short branch chains, our working hypothesis is that inactivity of SBEIIb would alter the branch-chain architecture of amylopectin through a reduction in formation of short chain amylopectin (SCAP) fractions and an increase in the relative abundance of longer amylose-like glucan chains, which affects enzyme accessibility. To investigate this structural basis of reduced pGI, CLD analysis was performed on debranched starch using SEC. Distinct clustering between SBE2b_WT and sbe2b mutant lines was observed, indicating substantial divergence in glucan chain composition (Fig. 2E). Across all sbe2b lines, long-chain fractions corresponding to amylose (AM1, DP 1001–4000) and long amylopectin chains (AM2, DP 121–1000) were markedly increased relative to the SBE2b_WT. The sbe2b_003 and sbe2b_062 exhibited more than a 100% increase in AM1, while sbe2b_003, sbe2b_008, and sbe2b_062 showed 130–160% increases in AM2, which reflect reduced branching activity and the accumulation of extended glucan chains. This resulted in elevated AAC, achieving 27–39% in the mutants compared with 17% in SBE2b_WT (Table S3). A comparable trend was observed in the medium-chain amylopectin (MCAP) fractions: MCAP1 (DP 104–120), MCAP2 (DP 71–103), and MCAP3 (DP 61–70) increased by approximately 130–150%, 70–90%, and 30–50%, respectively, across mutants compared to WT. Moderate increases (28–30%) were also noted in MCAP4 (DP 50–60) for sbe2b_002 and sbe2b_069, whereas MCAP5 (DP 37–49) remained largely unchanged. In contrast, the SCAP fractions, particularly SCAP4 (DP 13–17) and SCAP5 (DP 6–12)—were substantially reduced by 30–50% in the edited lines. SCAP3 (DP 18–27) also decreased in most mutants, except for sbe2b_002 and sbe2b_069, which showed minimal change. Overall, the results clearly demonstrate that sbe2b disruption shifts the starch profile toward longer chain amylopectin, reduces SCAP synthesis, and enhances amylose accumulation (Fig. 2E and Fig. S6A). Long-chain fractions, including AM1 and AM2, showed strong negative associations with pGI sbe2b mutant, whereas SCAP4 and SCAP5 fractions were positively associated with higher pGI values in the SBE2b_WT (Fig. S6B).

OsSBEIIb disruption alters starch structure, pasting behavior and functional properties

To further assess whether these chain-length changes altered starch supramolecular organization and thermal behavior, XRD and DSC analyses were performed. XRD profiles, including an enlarged view of the 12–24° 2θ region, showed broadly similar diffraction patterns between the control and sbe2b_069 mutant starches, indicating that the major crystalline polymorph was retained (Fig. 3A). However, relative crystallinity was reduced in the mutant compared with the control, decreasing from approximately 35% to 29% (Fig. 3C). This reduction in crystallinity is consistent with disruption of regular amylopectin branch organization and reduced formation of ordered short-chain double helices. The broader diffraction feature near 20° 2θ in the mutant (Fig. 3A) further supports the presence of V-type amylose-associated ordering. The mutants showed higher amylose-lipid complex enthalpy (ΔHALC = 0.119-0.211 J/g) than the SBE2b_WT (ΔHALC = 0.025 J/g) (Table 1). The formation of amylose-lipid complex restricts water penetration, further reducing the swelling capacity of starch granules, decreasing surface area available for the enzyme.

DSC analysis further revealed altered starch gelatinization properties in the mutant starch. The first gelatinization transition shifted to higher temperatures in the mutant, with onset, peak, and endset temperatures increasing from approximately 65.43°C, 77.96°C, and 84.19°C in the control to median values of 72.6°C, 82.2°C, and 90.4°C in the mutants, respectively. This is supported by the observed decrease in gelatinization enthalpy in the mutants (ΔHgel = 0.892–2.513 J/g) relative to the SBE2b_WT (ΔHgel = 3.156 J/g) (Table 1). During gelatinization, energy is required to break the hydrogen bonds and van der Waals forces that stabilize the double-helical structures of amylopectin (Cooke and Gidley, 1992). Fewer double helices mean fewer bonds to break, requiring less energy input, and therefore resulting in lower measured enthalpy values. Although the mutants showed higher ΔHALC than the SBE2b_WT, it remained much lower than the ΔHgel, which indicates that amylose–lipid complexation represents a relatively small thermally detectable fraction of the starch structure.

To evaluate the functional consequences of altered starch structure, pasting properties of starch from sbe2b mutants and SBE2b_WT were analyzed using high-temperature RVA (120 °C) (Table S4). Compared with the SBE2b_WT, sbe2b mutant starches exhibited a significant reduction in peak viscosity (PV), decreasing from 5078 cP in SBE2b_WT to 501-3791 cP across mutant lines. This decrease indicates reduced granule swelling capacity and increased resistance to gelatinization (Fig. 3C).

Breakdown viscosity (BD), which reflects the stability of swollen starch granules under heat and shear, was also markedly reduced in the mutants (WT: 4109 cP; mutants: 80-2364 cP), suggesting enhanced structural stability and reduced susceptibility to disintegration during heating. In contrast, final viscosity (FV) increased in several mutant lines has high as 3861 cP as compared to 2303 cP in SBE2b_WT starch, accompanied by a substantial increase in setback viscosity (SB), which rose by 67% relative to the SBE2b_WT. Elevated setback values indicate enhanced retrogradation, reflecting stronger reassociation of starch chains during cooling. Pasting temperature (PsT) was also shifted upward in the mutants (WT: 79.5 °C vs mutants: 84.3-94.7 °C). These functional changes are consistent with the altered CLD of the increased abundance of long glucan chains and reduced SCAP fractions. The reduction in PV and BD reflects limited granule swelling and enhanced internal cohesion, while increased FV and SB indicate stronger post-gelatinization reassociation of starch chains. RVA parameters showed strong positive correlations with PV and BD, while strong negative correlations with trough viscosity (TV), FV, and SB (Fig. S6C). Such changes in pasting behavior are expected to reduce starch digestibility by limiting enzyme accessibility and promoting retrogradation, thereby contributing to the observed reduction in predicted glycemic index in sbe2b mutants.

OsSBEIIb disruption increases protein content and remodels amino acid composition in milled rice

Total protein content was quantified in milled grains of SBE2b_WT and sbe2b mutant lines. All mutant lines exhibited higher protein content values ranging from 9.54% to 10.13% across independent edited lines, compared with the 8.78% of SBE2b_WT. The highest protein level was observed in sbe2b_002 (10.13%). Intermediate increases were detected in sbe2b_003 (9.62%) and sbe2b_008 (9.55%) (Fig. 4C, Table S5). Overall, the sbe2b mutants showed an increase of approximately 8–15% relative to the SBE2b_WT, indicating a consistent elevation in protein accumulation associated with OsSBEIIb disruption (p < 0.001). HPLC-based amino acid profiling further revealed increased accumulation of several EAAs in the mutant lines, except for valine, although the magnitude of change varied across genotypes (Fig. 4A). Notably, lysine, methionine, threonine, and histidine increased by approximately 19.6%, 97.8%, 49.1% and 21.2%, respectively, compared with the SBE2b_WT. Isoleucine, leucine, and phenylalanine had a modest but significant increase across the mutants. Several mutants, including sbe2b_008, sbe2b_062, and sbe2b_069, consistently accumulated higher levels of non-essential amino acids (NEAAs) such as alanine, arginine, aspartic acid, cysteine, glutamic acid, glycine, and proline, whereas serine enrichment was more pronounced in sbe2b_002 and sbe2b_003 (Fig. 4B). Collectively, these results indicate enhanced accumulation of amino acids and protein content in the endosperm of sbe2b mutants.

Metabolomic reprogramming and compositional shifts in secondary and lipid metabolism of sbe2b knockout mutants

To investigate the metabolic changes associated with OsSBEIIb disruption, GC–MS-based profiling of polar metabolites was performed. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed clear separation between SBE2b_WT and sbe2b mutant lines along the first principal component, indicating substantial metabolic divergence (Fig. 5A) (F-value: 108.31; R2: 0.97832; p-value <0.001). Hierarchical clustering of 127 polar metabolites further confirmed this separation (Fig. 5B). Analysis of central carbon metabolites revealed significant shifts in glycolysis and tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle intermediates. Further, comparison of carbon-rich metabolites (soluble sugars and organic acids) with nitrogen-containing compounds showed a clear shift in carbon-nitrogen partitioning. In the mutants, soluble sugars and sugar phosphates, including sucrose-6-phosphate (log2FC = −5.63, p < 0.001) and raffinose (log2FC = −2.08, p < 0.001) were significantly reduced. Sugar alcohols such as maltitol (log2FC = −8.90), ribitol, sorbitol (p < 0.001), and sugar acids like gluconic acid (log2FC = −8.52), arabinoic acid, and galactaric acid also decreased markedly (p < 0.001). Several TCA cycle-related metabolites, such as 4-hydroxybutanoic acid, were similarly reduced (log2FC = −7.69). Consistent with targeted amino acid profiling, several amino acids contributed to the separation between SBE2b_WT and sbe2b mutant lines, with an increase of leucine (log2FC = 8.33), lysine (log2FC = 1.63), threonine (log2FC = 3.00), glutamic acid (log2FC = 1.81), along with other amino acids, amides, and amines. Metabolites associated with phenylpropanoid and fatty acid/lipid pathways were also significantly altered (p < 0.001). (Fig. 6C, Table S8).

To capture broader metabolic changes resulting from sbe2b knockout, we performed pathway-level enrichment analysis of significantly altered polar metabolites detected by LC-MS. Beyond primary metabolism, several secondary metabolic pathways were significantly enriched. These included anthocyanin biosynthesis (log2FC = 12.74), flavonoid pigment metabolism (log2FC = 3.07), phenolic acid metabolism (log2FC = 3.11), and vitamin metabolism (log2FC = 2.82) (p-value <0.01). In contrast, a subset of organic acid pathways, including citrate metabolism (log2FC = −5.48), showed strong downregulation (Fig. 5C). In this study, we found that catechins were consistently reduced across all sbe2b mutants, with an average log2 fold change of −9.87 (Table S9). In contrast, procyanidin B1, an oligomeric product of catechin condensation, was significantly upregulated (log2FC = 1.01), indicating a potential redirection of flavonoid metabolism toward oligomer formation in the absence of OsSBEIIb. Furthermore, several glycosylated flavonoids, including isorhamnetin-3-O-glucoside, myricetin glucoside, cyanidin-3-O-glucoside, and methylquercetin glucoside, were significantly upregulated in the mutants. Compounds including ferulic acid, peonidin-3,5-diglucoside, and kaempferol also showed increased accumulation, supporting a broader shift in phenylpropanoid metabolism. On the other hand, intermediates such as p-coumaroyl malate and N-coumaroyl-aspartate were consistently downregulated (Fig. 6A).

Moreover, differences in fatty acid profiles were observed among the milled rice of mutants (Fig. 6B, Fig. S7A). The SBE2b_WT samples clustered closely and consistently exhibited higher levels of long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acids, including docosadienoic acid (22:2), eicosatrienoic acid (20:3), and eicosenoic acid (20:1). In contrast, specific mutants such as sbe2b_062 and sbe2b_069 showed elevated levels of medium-chain saturated and monounsaturated fatty acids, including palmitic acid (16:0), stearic acid (18:0), oleic acid (18:1), and arachidic acid (20:0). The heatmap also revealed that sbe2b_002 and sbe2b_003 shared similar fatty acid signatures, with moderate increases in behenic acid (22:0) and linolenic acid (18:3) compared to SBE2b_WT (Fig. 6B). These patterns correspond with the preferential accumulation of triglycerides enriched in monounsaturated fatty acids, such as TG 55:2, TG 54:1, TG 52:1, and TG 53:1 (Fig. S7B). However, triglycerides with higher degrees of unsaturation were also present in both SBE2b_WT and sbe2b_mutant lines (Table S10).

Grain morphology and agronomic performance of sbe2b mutants

Disruption of OsSBEIIb resulted in pronounced alterations in grain appearance and endosperm structure. Milled grains from sbe2b_002, sbe2b_003, and sbe2b_062 exhibited heterogeneous phenotypes, ranging from partially translucent to fully chalky endosperms, whereas sbe2b_008 and sbe2b_069 displayed predominantly opaque grains compared with the translucent (SBE2b_WT)(Fig. 4F). These observations indicate that loss of SBEIIb function disrupts normal starch deposition and endosperm organization.

Grain yield components were adversely affected across all mutant lines (Table S11). Seed weight per plant was significantly reduced, with severe reductions of 40–70% observed in sbe2b_002 and sbe2b_003 (p < 0.001), moderate reductions of 20–30% in sbe2b_008 and sbe2b_062 (p < 0.01), and a smaller decrease of approximately 10% in sbe2b_069 (p < 0.05) relative to the SBE2b_WT (Fig. S8D).

Under controlled growth conditions, most mutant lines showed no significant difference in plant height compared with the SBE2b_WT (Fig. 4E), except for sbe2b_002 and sbe2b_069, which exhibited modest reductions of approximately 9% and 5%, respectively (Fig. S8A; p < 0.05). Tiller number varied among the mutants, with sbe2b_003 and sbe2b_062 showing slight reductions (p < 0.05) and sbe2b_008 exhibiting a more pronounced decrease (p < 0.01), while sbe2b_002 and sbe2b_069 remained comparable to the SBE2b_WT (Fig. S8C).

Discussion

OsSBEIIb disruption remodels starch architecture, crystallinity, thermal behavior, and predicted glycemic response

OsSBEIIb plays a central role in amylopectin biosynthesis by introducing α-1,6 branch points into glucan chains. Its disruption therefore produces a characteristic restructuring of starch architecture, marked by depletion of SCAP and enrichment of longer glucans chains. In the present study, this remodeling was associated with a marked increased RS and reduction in pGI, shifting polished IR64 rice from a high-GI phenotype toward an intermediate/low-GI range.

The SCAP normally contribute to the regular crystalline lamellae of rice starch and create branch-rich regions that are readily accessed by hydrolytic enzymes (Pautong et al., 2026). In normal starch, cooking disrupts crystalline lamellae through melting of amylopectin double helices (Cooke and Gidley, 1992; Liu et al., 2022), enabling granule swelling and exposure of α-1,4-glycosidic bonds to digestive enzymes (Lauro et al., 1993; Ratnayake and Jackson, 2006; Slack and Wainwright, 1980). Amylose, by contrast, restricts swelling, stabilizes granular structure, and forms amylose-lipid complexes, thereby limiting enzymatic access (Jane et al., 2003; Jia et al., 2023; Tester and Morrison, 1990; Vamadevan and Bertoft, 2020; Zhao et al., 2022).

The lower GI and higher RS in the mutants are attributed to a multi-barrier mechanism involving altered amylose/amylopectin distribution, restricted granule swelling, and amylose-lipid complexation, which collectively reduce enzyme accessibility and slow starch hydrolysis (Fig. 7).

Proposed multi-barrier mechanism for reduced starch digestibility in sbe2b mutants.

sbe2b inactivity shifts starch architecture by reducing SCAP fractions and increasing AM1 and AM2. This altered structure weakens normal A-type lamellar organization, restricts swelling and chain leaching during gelatinization, and reduces exposure of α-1,4 glycosidic bonds to hydrolytic enzymes. Increased amylose-lipid complexation provides an additional molecular barrier. A possible SGAP-related barrier is shown as a future-testable hypothesis.

The evidence of sbe2b inactivity was observed in the significant reduction of SCAP fractions, particularly, SCAP3-SCAP5. These short chains are critical for forming the double-helical crystalline lamellae that are disrupted during gelatinization (Nakamura and Kainuma, 2022; Vermeylen et al., 2004; Zhong et al., 2021). Their reduction led to decreased molecular order, as shown by lower gelatinization enthalpy in the sbe2b mutants vs SBE2b_WT control and, in sbe2b_069, reduced relative crystallinity with weaker A-type diffraction patterns by XRD (Fig. 3C).

Although the reduction of SCAP fractions is supporting sbe2b inactivity, however, it does not directly explain the observed decrease in pGI of the mutants. While decreased crystallinity would be expected to potentially make starch more accessible to enzymes, but this is not the case. Thus, the observed lower GI indicates the presence of additional structural barriers in the gelatinized state (Lei et al., 2024).

The primary compensatory barrier is the marked increase in longer glucan chains. Apparent amylose content (AAC) rose from 17% in SBE2b_WT to 27–39% in mutants, with enrichment of AM1 and AM2 chains, as well MCAP1 and MCAP2. These longer, more linear chains restrict granule swelling through molecular entanglement and partial helix formation with amylopectin, maintaining a more compact granular structure (Jia et al., 2023; Vamadevan and Bertoft, 2020). This was functionally confirmed by HT-RVA analysis, which showed lower PV and higher PsT in the mutants as compared to control, together with elevated gelatinization temperature in DSC. So, when granule swelling is limited, starch remains in a more compact and less accessible state. Limited swelling reduces the total surface area of starch granules available for the digestive enzymes to bind. Further, restricted swelling reduces the pore size that prevents enzymes from diffusing into the granules. In this condition, the productive enzyme-binding sites are reduced (Jia et al., 2023; Tian et al., 2023). Therefore, the amylose-enriched and long-chain glucan matrix may contribute to RS2-like formation by preserving compact or insufficiently swollen starch domains that remain less accessible to α-amylase.

A secondary contributory mechanism is amylose-lipid complexation. The increase in linear glucan chains, combined with available lipids, resulted in higher amylose-lipid complex enthalpy (ΔHALC = 0.119–0.211 J/g in sbe2b mutants vs. 0.025 J/g in SBE2b_WT) and elevated V-type crystallinity signals in XRD for sbe2b_069. These complexes further restrict water penetration and swelling, contributing to RS5-like resistance, although they represent a relatively minor fraction compared to the overall structural changes (Nebesny et al., 2002; Ye et al., 2018).

These observations align with previous reports showing that the severity of SBEIIb perturbation determines the extent of starch physicochemical remodeling. In cis-regulatory edited SBEIIb lines, moderate reductions in expression retained broadly similar XRD profiles but reduced relative crystallinity and increased gelatinization temperature, closely resembling the pattern observed in this study (Tiozon et al., 2025b). By contrast, stronger SBEIIb deficiency during seed development (5 to 15 DAF) has been associated with more pronounced crystalline transitions from A-type toward C- or B-type patterns, together with reduced crystallinity and elevated gelatinization temperatures (Ying et al., 2022). Similarly, RNAi-mediated suppression of SBEIIb showed a dosage-dependent effect, in which stronger amiRNA lines shifted toward B-type crystallinity and exhibited more pronounced increases in gelatinization temperature, whereas weaker hp-RNA lines retained A-type characteristics with more moderate structural changes (Butardo et al., 2011).

A possible starch granule-associated protein (SGAP)-related mechanism may also be considered, but only as a future-testable contributor rather than a confirmed mechanism in the present study. SGAPs are proteins located on the starch granule surface, within granule channels, or within the internal granule matrix, and may influence starch swelling, gelatinization, and enzymatic hydrolysis. Although total protein content and several amino acids were elevated in the sbe2b mutants, direct evidence linking reduced digestibility changes to SGAPs is lacking. Nevertheless, surface-associated SGAPs may act as a physical boundary that reduces enzyme contact with the starch surface, while channel-associated SGAPs may influence the movement of water and hydrolytic enzymes into internal granule pores which may result in fewer exposed glucan chains and fewer accessible α-1,4-glycosidic bonds (Wu et al., 2026). Thus, an altered SGAP environment could theoretically lower digestibility by creating an additional surface- or channel-level accessibility barrier. Future studies involving SGAP extraction and proteomic analysis are required to assess whether surface- or channel-localized proteins contribute additional barriers to enzyme access or water penetration.

OsSBEIIb disruption links starch remodeling with enhanced protein and essential amino acid accumulation

To reposition milled rice as healthier diet it requires strategies that moderate starch digestibility while enhancing protein quantity and quality. In this study, total starch content remained largely unchanged despite the pronounced restructuring of starch fine structure, suggesting that the mutation primarily altered starch architecture rather than strongly suppressing starch accumulation (Butardo et al., 2012; Yang et al., 2025). This distinction is important. Previous studies reported severe SBEIIb perturbation in starch deposition and more pronounced physicochemical changes, whereas the present lines showed increased RS levels alongside substantial gains in protein and essential amino acids. This suggests that the specific allele and degree of functional impairment in the elite genetic background, may influence whether carbon is retained within starch, redirected toward other storage reserves, or redistributed across endosperm metabolic pathways, due to its superior genetic architecture for nitrogen remobilization and starch-protein coordination. Despite the modest absolute increase in the total protein content in the polished rice endosperm, the change is statistically significant and is accompanied by a more favorable free amino-acid profile. Much of the redirected nitrogen accumulates as free amino acids rather than being fully incorporated into storage proteins. Consequently, while free amino-acid pools increase substantially, the absolute increase in total crude protein remains relatively small, with similar observations made by Baysal et al., 2020) (Baysal et al., 2020) and Chen et al., 2022 (Chen et al., 2022), but in whole rice grains.

The metabolite data support this interpretation by revealing a shift in carbohydrate and nitrogen-associated metabolism. Several soluble carbohydrate-related metabolites, including sucrose-6-phosphate and raffinose, together with sugar alcohols such as maltitol, erythritol, and sorbitol, and sugar acid/uronic acid-related compounds, were reduced in the mutants. These compounds are associated with starch and sucrose, raffinose-family oligosaccharide, polyol metabolisms, and pentose/glucuronate interconversions. Their reduction suggests that OsSBEIIb disruption did not simply increase free soluble sugars as a passive consequence of impaired starch branching. Instead, it appears to have depleted specific soluble carbon pools, consistent with a broader reorganization of carbohydrate metabolism following altered amylopectin assembly. This depletion of soluble carbohydrate pools provides a plausible entry point for explaining the observed shift in C:N partitioning. Impaired amylopectin branching may alter carbon use within the developing endosperm by changing the demand for highly branched amylopectin synthesis, thereby affecting carbon skeleton availability, energy status, and nitrogen assimilation or retention. This model is consistent with the accumulation of nitrogen-containing compounds, the increase in crude protein content, and the enrichment of several essential amino acids in the mutants, probably through closely interlinked pathways of carbohydrate metabolism, intermediates of glycolysis, TCA and pentose phosphate pathway (Chen et al., 2022).

Loss of SBEIIb activity primarily alters starch structure, the resulting “spillover” of carbon could be re-directed towards organic acids, free amino acids and lipids via glycolysis-related protein, TCA cycle (Chen et al., 2022). This shift in C:N partitioning has important nutritional implications. The sbe2b mutants showed an increase in crude protein content in polished grains. Based on an assumed rice intake of 300 g day−1 in Asia (FAO, 2007), this protein concentration would provide approximately 28.6–30.4 g of protein, corresponding to about 57–61% of a 50 g day−1 adult reference intake. In addition to total protein, the mutants showed improved essential amino acid composition, including higher lysine, methionine, threonine, and histidine in polished rice. When benchmarked against Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA) for adult essential amino acid requirement (Joint WHO, 2007), the amino acid scores for histidine (∼268%) and lysine (∼105%) suggest improved protein quality relative to SBE2b_WT polished rice (Fig. 4D; Table S7). Concurrently, Badoni et al., 2024 reported RIL-derived lines carrying a recessive sbe2b allele with both reduced pGI and elevated protein content, while Baysal et al., 2020 (Baysal et al., 2020) showed that sbe2b knockout altered primary metabolism. In sorghum, genome-wide association studies have also linked SBEIIb to amino acid profiles, although functional validation remains limited (Kimani et al., 2020). The present work extends these observations by functionally validating, in a genome-edited sbe2b mutants, that perturbing amylopectin branching can coincide with improved starch digestibility and enhanced protein quality. These findings indicate that OsSBEIIb should not be viewed solely as a starch-branching enzyme affecting digestibility. Rather, its disruption appears to influence a broader endosperm metabolic regulation in which starch architecture, protein accumulation, and amino acid composition are interconnected processes altered by sbe2b mutation. This positions OsSBEIIb as a promising target for nutritional biofortification, with future optimization requiring fine-tuned alleles that balance lower glycemic impact, improved protein quality, grain quality, and agronomic performance.

Secondary metabolite and lipid remodeling reveal broader consequences of OsSBEIIb disruption in polished rice

Beyond changes in starch structure, digestibility, and amino acid metabolism, OsSBEIIb disruption was associated with shifts in specialized secondary metabolites and lipid composition, suggesting that altered amylopectin biosynthesis has wider consequences for endosperm metabolic organization. The accumulation of several flavonoid glycosides, phenolic acid derivatives, and procyanidin B1, together with the reduction of catechin/epicatechin-like features, suggests a rebalancing of phenylpropanoid-derived metabolites rather than uniform activation of the entire pathway. The apparent shift from catechin/epicatechin-like compounds toward procyanidin B1 may indicate altered branch-point allocation within the flavonoid pathway (Tiozon et al., 2023a, 2023b). Concurrently. Badoni et al. (2024) , reported broader metabolic changes, including enhanced flavonoid-related metabolites.

Lipid remodeling was also evident in the mutant endosperm, with changes in fatty acid composition and preferential accumulation of selected triacyl glycerides (TAG) species enriched in saturated and monounsaturated fatty acids. These lipid shifts in starch accumulating endosperm tissue is highly notable because fatty acids and glycerolipids can interact with amylose and long-chain glucans, potentially influencing starch organization, retrogradation behavior, and enzyme accessibility (Tiozon et al., 2024; Shen et al., 2023). This interpretation is compatible with the higher-temperature DSC transition observed in both SBE2b_WT and sbe2b_069 mutant starches. The lipid changes observed here also align with previous reports indicating that sbe2b perturbation affects lipid and fatty acid metabolism (Badoni et al., 2024; Baysal et al., 2020). Together, these studies support the view that SBEIIb disruption does not simply alter starch chain-length distribution but can reshape multiple storage-related metabolic pools in the endosperm.

Overall, the present findings extend these observations by showing that OsSBEIIb perturbation is associated not only with altered starch architecture and improved protein quality, but also with shifts in phenylpropanoid-related metabolites, flavonoid derivatives, fatty acids, and TAG species. These changes support the broader interpretation that OsSBEIIb disruption perturbs endosperm metabolic homeostasis across starch, protein, secondary metabolite, and lipid pathways (Fig. 8). However, targeted metabolite validation, lipid-complex assays, and flux-based analyses will be required to determine whether these secondary and lipid changes arise directly from altered carbon allocation or indirectly from changes in grain filling and endosperm development.

Agronomic trade-offs and deployment considerations for nutritionally enhanced sbe2b rice

Although OsSBEIIb disruption improved starch digestibility, protein content, and essential amino acid composition, these nutritional gains were accompanied by grain-quality and agronomic trade-offs. The increased chalkiness or opacity and reduced seed weight observed in the mutant lines are consistent with the broader behavior of starch-structure mutants, where disruption of normal amylopectin organization often compromises endosperm packing, grain translucency, and yield-related traits. Similar trade-offs have been noted across several starch-related rice mutants, including flo2, ssIIIa/Wx, sbe3-rs, and wx/ae which are frequently associated with chalky grain appearance, reduced yield, and harder cooked texture. This reinforces the idea that starch digestibility cannot be optimized independently of grain physical quality and eating properties.

These penalties likely arise from extensive remodeling of amylopectin chain-length distribution and increased AAC, which together disturb crystalline lamellae organization and produce heterogeneous starch granule structures. Sang et al. (2025) showed that targeted editing of SBEIIb regulatory elements produced graded reductions in gene expression and increased RS, providing proof-of-concept that regulatory editing can generate useful starch-quality variation without necessarily imposing the full penalties associated with complete knockouts. However, previous work has shown that yield penalties associated with severe starch-structure mutants can be mitigated through backcrossing or stacking with favorable agronomic alleles (Jukanti et al., 2020). Miura et al. (2022) demonstrated that backcrossing a double ss3a/sbe2b mutant with the high-yielding cultivar Akita-63 restored seed weight from 16.6 mg to 25.1 mg per seed by the BC3 generation while retaining high RS levels. These findings suggest that future improvement should prioritize promoter-edited or cis-regulatory OsSBEIIb alleles, recurrent backcrossing into elite backgrounds, and stacking with grain-quality or yield-stabilizing loci to retain nutritional benefits while reducing penalties in grain appearance and yield. Achieving measurable gains in the protein content and essential amino acids in IR64 elite background, rather increases the direct application potential of the edited alleles in the pre-breeding programs. Thus, starting with elite genetic background provides a strong foundation for subsequent recovery breeding to mitigate the agronomic trade-offs.

Consumer acceptance represents an equally important deployment constraint. Low-GI and high-amylose rice often exhibit firmer, less sticky texture, which may be acceptable in some markets but undesirable in others where soft and cohesive cooked rice is preferred. The value of the mutants lies in demonstrating that OsSBEIIb is a powerful entry point for simultaneously modifying glycemic potential and nutritional quality. Optimized alleles will be needed to balance reduced starch digestibility, improved protein quality, acceptable texture, grain appearance, and yield stability. Successfully achieving simultaneous reductions in glycemic index and enhancements in protein content and essential amino acids within this elite, widely adapted IR64 variety therefore carries greater direct application potential for precision breeding and deployment. Regulatory developments in several Asian countries increasingly distinguish certain genome-edited crops, particularly SDN-1 and in some cases SDN-2 edits, from transgenic GMOs, which may facilitate future varietal deployment exhibiting low GI with high RS, high protein with elevated EAA.

Rice varieties combining lower pGI with improved protein and amino acid composition could contribute to healthier staple-based diets, especially in populations with high rice intake and limited dietary diversity. For example, the ICMR–INDIAB Survey reported low overall protein intake, and found that substituting 5% of total daily energy from carbohydrates with protein was associated with lower likelihood of type 2 diabetes and prediabetes (Anjana et al., 2025). These findings support the broader nutritional rationale for rice with both slower starch digestibility and improved protein quality with EAA, although clinical feeding studies will be required to determine whether optimized sbe2b lines directly improve postprandial glycemia, protein adequacy, or metabolic outcomes in humans.

Beyond direct table rice consumption, sbe2b-derived starches can also be useful for functional food and industrial applications. Their higher AAC, enhanced retrogradation tendency, and altered starch–lipid interactions may be valuable for low-GI formulations, processed foods, biodegradable packaging, or starch-based delivery systems (Tiozon et al., 2025a; Tiozon et al., 2021). However, these applications require targeted evaluation of processing behavior, texture stability, digestibility after cooking and storage, and consumer acceptability. Overall, the present findings position OsSBEIIb as a promising target to enrich nutritional targets within staples.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that CRISPR/Cas9-mediated disruption of OsSBEIIb in IR64 fundamentally remodels starch architecture and triggers coordinated changes in endosperm metabolism. Loss of sbe2b function in genome edited lines shifts glucan chain distribution toward longer chains, reduces short amylopectin clusters, and alters crystallinity and thermal stability, collectively slowing starch digestibility, increasing RS and lowering predicted glycemic response. These structural modifications are associated with increased protein accumulation, improved EAA profiles, and reprogramming of carbohydrate, phenylpropanoid, and lipid pathways, revealing OsSBEIIb as a key node connecting amylopectin biosynthesis to broader metabolic partitioning to enrich multiple nutrients in rice endosperm. While these changes offer a promising route for enhancing nutritional quality, they are accompanied by trade-offs including grain chalkiness and reduced seed weight, highlighting the pleiotropic effects of complete loss-of-function alleles.

Future efforts should prioritize fine-tuned regulatory edits, recurrent backcrossing into elite backgrounds, and stacking with yield and grain-quality loci to balance nutritional gains with agronomic performance. When supported by sensory testing and human validation studies, optimized OsSBEIIb variants to be developed through genome editing can be positioned as precision breeding product in the target countries matching consumer preferences with appropriate acceptance of SDN1 and SDN2 technologies to disseminate low GI, high protein with enriched EAAs to populations facing diabetes and protein malnutrition. This work provides a mechanistic foundation for precision starch engineering aimed at improving both glycemic properties and elevate protein, EAA values in milled rice and other cereals.
