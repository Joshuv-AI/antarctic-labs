Abstract
. Let 𝔤 \mathfrak{g} be a symmetrizable Kac-Moody algebra over ℂ \mathbb{C} and let L ⁡ ( ρ ) L(\rho) be the irreducible integrable 𝔤 \mathfrak{g} -module with highest weight ρ \rho . Let I I be a subgraph of the Dynkin diagram of 𝔤 \mathfrak{g} which has only simple bonds and no cycle of length ≥ 3 \geq 3 . For every subset D D of I I , denote by β D \beta_{D} the sum of the simple roots corresponding to D D . To every D ⊂ I D\subset I such that λ D , I = 2 ​ ρ − β I − β D \lambda_{D,I}=2\rho-\beta_{I}-\beta_{D} is dominant, we associate certain elements π D , I \pi_{D,I} of weight λ D , I − ρ \lambda_{D,I}{-}\rho in the crystal B ⁡ ( ρ ) B(\rho) , which depend on the choice of a root vertex in each connected component of I I . Then we prove that our elements are ρ \rho -dominant elements of B ⁡ ( ρ ) B(\rho) , hence provide new families of components of the tensor product L ⁡ ( ρ ) ⊗ L ⁡ ( ρ ) L(\rho)\otimes L(\rho) .

1.
Introduction

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a symmetrizable Kac-Moody algebra over
\mathbb{C}
, with Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}
.
Let
A=(a_{st})_{s,t\in\Delta}
be its Cartan matrix, where
\Delta
is a finite set. We identify
\Delta

with the set of simple roots
\{\alpha_{s}\}
and denote the corresponding simple coroots by
\alpha_{s}^{\vee}
.
Let
\leq
denote the usual partial order on
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
, defined by
\lambda\geq\mu
if

\lambda-\mu=\sum_{s\in\Delta}m_{s}\alpha_{s}
, with
m_{s}\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
P^{+}
denote the set of dominant integral weights, i.e.

(1.1)

P^{+}=\{\lambda\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}\mid\langle\lambda,\alpha_{s}^{\vee}\rangle\in\mathbb{N},\;\forall s\in\Delta\}.

For each
\lambda\in P^{+}
, there exists a unique (up to isomorphism) integrable
\mathfrak{g}
-module
L(\lambda)
with
highest weight
\lambda
, and
L(\lambda)
is irreducible, see
[
Kc90
]
, Cor. 10.4. Further, by
loc. cit.
, Cor. 10.7,
the category of integrable
\mathfrak{g}
-modules is completely reducible, and for
\lambda,\mu\in P^{+}

one has

(1.2)

L(\lambda)\otimes L(\mu)=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\in P^{+}\\[3.0pt]
\nu\leq\lambda+\mu\end{subarray}}L(\nu)^{\oplus c_{\lambda,\mu}^{\nu}}

for some integers
c_{\lambda,\mu}^{\nu}\geq 0
. Let
\rho\in P^{+}
such that

\langle\rho,\alpha_{s}^{\vee}\rangle=1
for all
s\in\Delta
.

Let
I
be a subset of
\Delta
such that
a_{ij}\in\{0,-1\}
for
i\not=j
in
I
and such that

I
, regarded as a subgraph of the Dynkin diagram
\mathcal{D}
of
\mathfrak{g}
, has no cycle of length
\geq 3
.
These hypotheses imply that every connected component of
I
is a tree. Set
N=|I|
.

We introduce the following additional data
\mathcal{R}
: we choose in each such tree
T

a vertex called the root of
T
; this makes
T
into a rooted tree.
We orient all the edges of
T
towards the root; then if we have an edge
x\to y
we say that

x
covers
y
.

Given this data
\mathcal{R}
, we consider a numbering
i_{1},\dots,i_{N}
of the elements of
I

which has the property that
i_{k}\to i_{\ell}
implies
k>\ell
, and we consider the following
product of lowering operators:

(1.3)

f_{I}^{\scriptscriptstyle\searrow}(\mathcal{R})=f_{i_{N}}\cdots f_{i_{1}}

where the symbol
{\scriptscriptstyle\searrow}
indicates that the product is taken in decreasing order of the indices. Further, we number arbitrarily
v_{N+1},\dots,v_{|\Delta|}

the elements of
\Delta-I
.

Then, to every subset
D
of
I
we associate the integral weight

(1.4)

\lambda_{D,I}=2\rho-\sum_{i\in I}\alpha_{i}-\sum_{d\in D}\alpha_{d}

and the following product of lowering operators:

(1.5)

f_{D}^{\scriptscriptstyle\nearrow}(\mathcal{R})=\prod_{d\in D}f_{d}

where this time the product is taken in increasing order of the indices (whence the symbol
{\scriptscriptstyle\nearrow}
).
The products
f_{I}^{\scriptscriptstyle\searrow}(\mathcal{R})
and
f_{D}^{\scriptscriptstyle\nearrow}(\mathcal{R})
depend only on
\mathcal{R}

(see Remark
2.9
and Lemma
3.6
).

Let
B(\rho)
denote the crystal of
\mathfrak{g}
with highest weight
\rho
and let
\pi_{\rho}
be its element of
weight
\rho
. Recall that a non-zero element
x\in B(\rho)
is called
\rho
-dominant if

\mathrm{wt}(x)+\rho
is dominant and
\varepsilon_{s}(x)\leq 1
for all
s\in\Delta
.
Our main result is the following theorem, which is a partial extension of Proposition 2.3 of
[
BG25
]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
D
be a subset of
I
such that
\lambda_{D,I}
is dominant.
Then the element

\pi_{D,I}(\mathcal{R})=f_{D}^{\scriptscriptstyle\nearrow}(\mathcal{R})f_{I}^{\scriptscriptstyle\searrow}(\mathcal{R})\pi_{\rho}

is non-zero, of weight
\lambda_{D,I}
, and is
\rho
-dominant.

Since the
\rho
-dominant elements of
B(\rho)
are in bijection with the components of the tensor product

L(\rho)\otimes L(\rho)
(see
[
Ka95
]
, Prop. 4.2 or
[
Ja96
]
, Prop. 9.27), this gives the following corollary.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
D
be a subset of
I
such that
\lambda_{D,I}
is dominant.
Then
L(\lambda_{D,I})
occurs with multiplicity
\geq 1
in
L(\rho)\otimes L(\rho)
. More precisely,
a lower bound for this multiplicity is the number

n(D,I)
of distinct elements
\pi_{D,I}(\mathcal{R})
obtained as
\mathcal{R}
ranges over all possible choices
of root vertices.

We do not know how to give in general an explicit lower bound for
n(D,I)
. However we worked out several examples in the last section of the paper.

Our proof combines crystal theory with combinatorics of rooted trees and proceeds in two steps.
In more details, for
k=1,\dots,|\Delta|
, let
B_{k}
be the crystal with
highest weight the fundamental weight
\omega_{k}
and let
\pi_{k}
denote the element of
B_{k}
of weight

\omega_{k}
. We consider
B(\rho)
as the connected component of
the crystal

B_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes B_{|\Delta|}
containing the element
\pi_{\rho}=\pi_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\pi_{|\Delta|}
.

Firstly, in Proposition
3.17
,
we describe explicitly, in terms of some decomposition of
D
into
“
stems with dead leaves
”, the element
\pi_{D,I}
, showing in particular that it
is non-zero.

Secondly, in Proposition
4.1
, we prove,
using the previous description and Prop. 2.3 of
[
BG25
]
,
that
\varepsilon_{s}(\pi_{D,I})\leq 1
for all
s\in\Delta
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Tensor product of crystals

For the tensor product of crystals, we follow the convention of
[
BS17
]
, §2.3 (which is opposite to that in
Kashiwara’s original papers). In particular, we will use Lemma 2.33 of
loc. cit.
(see also
[
KN94
]
, Prop. 2.1.1 or

[
Ka93
]
, Lemma 1.3.6),
which we recall below for the
convenience of the reader. Firstly, let us introduce the relevant notation.

Notation 2.1
.

(a) If
x
is an element of a crystal
B
, then for each
s\in\Delta
we set
\mathrm{wt}_{s}(x)=\langle\mathrm{wt}(x),\alpha_{s}^{\vee}\rangle
.

(b) Let
x_{j}\in B_{j}
, for crystals
B_{1},\dots,B_{n}
.
For all
s\in\Delta
and
j=1,\dots,n
, we set:

(2.1)

\Sigma_{s}(x,j)=\sum_{h=1}^{j-1}\mathrm{wt}_{s}(x_{h}),\qquad\quad\Sigma^{\prime}_{s}(x,j)=-\sum_{h=j+1}^{n}\mathrm{wt}_{s}(x_{h}).

Then Lemma 2.33 of
[
BS17
]
is the following:

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
x_{j}\in B_{j}
, for crystals
B_{1},\dots,B_{n}
. Consider the element
x=x_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes x_{n}
of

B_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes B_{n}
. Then, for all
s\in\Delta
,

(a)

\varphi_{s}(x)
is given by

\varphi_{s}(x)=\max_{1\leq j\leq n}\Big(\varphi_{s}(x_{j})+\Sigma_{s}(x,j)\Big)

and if
j
is the first index where the maximum is attained, then

f_{s}(x)=x_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes f_{s}(x_{j})\otimes\cdots\otimes x_{n}.

(b)

\varepsilon_{s}(x)
is given by

\varepsilon_{s}(x)=\max_{1\leq j\leq n}\Big(\varepsilon_{s}(x_{j})+\Sigma^{\prime}_{s}(x,j)\Big)

and if
j
is the last index where the maximum is attained, then

e_{s}(x)=x_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{s}(x_{j})\otimes\cdots\otimes x_{n}.

Notation 2.3
.

For the sake of brevity, we will denote
\varphi_{s}(x_{j})+\Sigma_{s}(x,j)
by
\Phi_{s}(x,j)
and

\varepsilon_{s}(x_{j})+\Sigma^{\prime}_{s}(x,j)
by
\Upsilon_{s}(x,j)
.

2.2.
Recollections about trees

Let us recall a few facts about trees and rooted trees.

Notation 2.4
.

If
\Gamma
is a graph (unoriented) and
v
a vertex of
\Gamma
, the
valency
of
v
,
denoted by
\mathrm{val}_{\Gamma}(v)
or simply
\mathrm{val}(v)
, is the number of its neighbours.

Notation 2.5
.

Let
T
be a tree. If we choose, once for all, a vertex
v_{0}
of
T
called the
root
, and
orient all the edges towards the root, then
T
becomes a
rooted tree
.
For each vertex
v
, let

\mathcal{G}_{v}
be the unique geodesic joining
v
to
v_{0}
; then

\mathcal{G}_{v}
has the form

\textstyle{v=v_{h}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{v_{h-1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\cdots\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{v_{0}}

for some
h
and we define the
height
of
v
as
\mathrm{ht}(v)=h=\#\mathcal{G}_{v}-1
.
We set
\mathrm{ht}(T)=\max_{v}\mathrm{ht}(v)
.

If
v\not=v_{0}
,
i.e. if
h\geq 1
, we denote the vertex
v_{h-1}
by
v^{-}
and call it the
predecessor
of
v
.
We will also write
x\to y
to mean that
y=x^{-}
, and say in this case that
x

covers

y
.

The vertices
v
such that
v\not=v_{0}
and
\mathrm{val}(v)=1
are called the
leaves
of
T
.
For any vertex
v
, we define its
depth
, denoted by
\mathrm{dep}_{T}(v)
or simply
\mathrm{dep}(v)
, as the maximum
cardinality of a geodesic from a leaf
\ell
of
T
to
v
.
Thus, in particular,
\mathrm{dep}(v)=0
if and only if
v
is a leaf, and one has
\mathrm{dep}(v_{0})=\mathrm{ht}(T)
.

The previous notions extend immediately to the case of a forest of rooted trees: if
I
is such a forest,
and
v
is a vertex of
I
, one has
\mathrm{ht}_{I}(v)=\mathrm{ht}_{T}(v)
and
\mathrm{dep}_{I}(v)=\mathrm{dep}_{T}(v)
, where
T
is the connected component of
I
containing
v
.

2.3.
A certain numbering of
\mathcal{D}

Let us come back to the Dynkin diagram
\mathcal{D}
of
\mathfrak{g}
.
Let
I
be a subset of
\Delta
such that
a_{ij}\in\{0,-1\}
for
i\not=j
in
I
and such that

I
, regarded as a subgraph of
\mathcal{D}
, has no cycle of length
\geq 3
.
These hypotheses imply that every connected component of
I
is a tree.

Definition 2.6
.

In each connected component
T
of
I
, we choose a vertex as the root of
T
. This makes
I

into a forest of rooted trees; in particular,
I
has a height function
\mathrm{ht}_{I}
.
We fix then a numbering
v_{0},\dots,v_{N}
of the vertices of
I
, where
N=|I|
, which satisfies the following two conditions:

(1)

It is compatible with the height function, i.e.
\mathrm{ht}_{I}(v_{j})>\mathrm{ht}_{I}(v_{i})
implies
j>i
.

(2)

For each given height, the elements of
D
come last, i.e. if
v_{j}\in D
and
v_{i}\in I-D

have the same height
h
, then
i<j
.

This can be obtained by numbering
v_{1},\dots,v_{d}
the elements of
I-D
of height
0
(if any),
then
v_{d+1},\dots,v_{n}
the elements of
D
of height
0
(if any), then

v_{n+1},\dots,v_{n+r}
the elements of
T-D
of height
1
(if any), and so on.

Finally, we number arbitrarily
v_{N+1},\dots,v_{|\Delta|}
the elements of

\Delta-I
.

Definition 2.7
.

(a) For
k=1,\dots,|\Delta|
, let
B_{k}
be the crystal with
highest weight the fundamental weight
\omega_{k}
and let
\pi_{k}
denote the element of
B_{k}
of weight

\omega_{k}
.

(b)
Let
\pi=\pi_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\pi_{|\Delta|}
; this is the element of highest weight
\rho
in the crystal

B_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes B_{|\Delta|}
and it belongs to the subcrystal
B(\rho)
.

(c) Let

\pi_{I}=f_{I}^{\scriptscriptstyle\searrow}\,\pi=\Big(\prod_{i\in I}f_{i}\Big)\pi
,
where the product is taken in
decreasing
order of the indices, e.g. if
I=\{1,3,4\}
then

\pi_{I}=f_{4}f_{3}f_{1}\pi
. (This explains the notation
f_{I}^{\scriptscriptstyle\searrow}
.)

Lemma 2.8
.

One has
\pi_{I}=\Big(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}\pi_{i}\Big)\otimes\Big(\bigotimes_{j>N}\pi_{j}\Big)
, recalling that

N=|I|
.

Proof.

By induction on
|I|
. There is nothing to prove if
I=\varnothing
, so we may assume

I\not=\varnothing

and the result proved for
I^{\prime}=I-\{N\}
, where
N
is the largest element of
I
.
Set
X^{\prime}=\pi_{I^{\prime}}
. We want to compute
f_{N}X^{\prime}
.

By the inductive hypothesis, one has
x_{j}=\pi_{j}
if
j\geq N
and
x_{j}=f_{j}\pi_{j}
if
j<N
.
From this it follows that
\varphi_{N}(x_{j})=\delta_{N,j}=\mathrm{wt}_{N}(x_{j})
for
j\geq N
, while for
j<N
one has:

\varphi_{N}(x_{j})=1=\mathrm{wt}_{N}(x_{j})
if
j=N^{-}
and
\varphi_{N}(x_{j})=0=\mathrm{wt}_{N}(x_{j})
else.

Thus, if
N
has a predecessor
N^{-}
one has:

\Phi_{N}(x,j)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }j<N^{-},\\
1&\text{if }N^{-}\leq j<N,\\
2&\text{if }j\geq N,\end{cases}

and otherwise one has
\Phi_{N}(x,j)=0
if
j<N
and
\Phi_{N}(x,j)=1
if
j\geq N
.
In both cases,
N
is the first index where the maximum of
\Phi_{N}(x,j)
is attained. Hence, by Lemma
2.2
(a),
one obtains, with obvious notation:

f_{N}X^{\prime}=X^{\prime}_{\leq N-1}\otimes f_{N}\pi_{N}\otimes X^{\prime}_{>N}.

This proves the lemma.
∎

Remark 2.9
.

In the proof, we used only the fact that
j\to i
implies
i<j
. This shows that

\pi_{I}
depends only on the data
\mathcal{R}
.

3.
Description of the element
\pi_{D,I}

3.1.
Preliminaries

Definition 3.1
.

Let
f_{D}^{\scriptscriptstyle\nearrow}=\prod_{d\in D}f_{d}
, where now the product is taken in
increasing
order
of the indices, e.g. if
D=\{1,3\}
then
f_{D}^{\scriptscriptstyle\nearrow}=f_{1}f_{3}
. (This explains the notation
f_{D}^{\scriptscriptstyle\nearrow}
.)

Set
\pi_{D,I}=f_{D}^{\scriptscriptstyle\nearrow}\,\pi_{I}
and note that, if
\pi_{D,I}
is non-zero, then
\mathrm{wt}(\pi_{D,I})+\rho
is the weight

(3.1)

\lambda_{D,I}=2\rho-\sum_{i\in I}\alpha_{i}-\sum_{d\in D}\alpha_{d}

considered in the Introduction. Then, regarding
D
as a subgraph of
I
, one has the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

\lambda_{D,I}
is dominant if and only if for each
d\in D
one of the following conditions is satisfied:

(a)

d
has at least two neighbours in
I
, or

(b)

d
has a unique neighbour
i
in
I
, and
i\in D
.

Proof.

One sees immediately that for any simple root
\alpha_{j}\in\Delta
, one has

\begin{cases}\langle\lambda_{D,I},\alpha_{j}^{\vee}\rangle\geq 2&\text{ if }j\not\in I,\\
\langle\lambda_{D,I},\alpha_{j}^{\vee}\rangle\geq 0&\text{ if }j\in I-D,\\
\langle\lambda_{D,I},\alpha_{j}^{\vee}\rangle=\mathrm{val}_{I}(j)+\mathrm{val}_{D}(j)-2&\text{ if }j\in D,\end{cases}

where
\mathrm{val}_{I}(j)
, resp.
\mathrm{val}_{D}(j)
, denote the number of neighbours of
j
in
I
, resp. in
D
.
Thus,
\lambda_{D,I}
is dominant if and only if for every
d\in D
one has

[formula]

\mathrm{val}_{I}(d)+\mathrm{val}_{D}(d)\geq 2.

This condition is automatically satisfied if
\mathrm{val}_{I}(d)\geq 2
. Otherwise, since
\mathrm{val}_{D}(d)\leq\mathrm{val}_{I}(d)
,
one must have
\mathrm{val}_{I}(d)=1=\mathrm{val}_{D}(d)
, i.e. has a unique neighbour
i
in
I
, and
i\in D
.
This proves the lemma.
∎

Note that the lemma implies, in particular, that an isolated point of
I
(i.e. a connected component
of cardinality
1
), cannot belong to
D
.

Remark 3.3
.

We shall repeatedly use the following commutativity property:
for
s\not=t
in
\Delta
which are not neighbours in the Dynkin diagram,
the action of the lowering operators
f_{s}
and
f_{t}
on any highest weight crystal commute
(and similarly for the raising operators
e_{s}
and
e_{t}
).
This is evident in Kashiwara’s definition in terms of quantized enveloping algebras.

On the other hand, Stembridge has taken this property, together
with crystal analogues of the Serre relations in the case where
s
and
t
are linked by a simple bond,
to define the class of what is now called Stembridge crystals, and he proved that any highest weight crystal

B(\lambda)
satisfies these properties, see
[
St03
]
, Def. 1.1 and Th. 2.4 (see also
[
BS17
]
, §§4.2
and 5.7).

Note that
D
, as a (full) subgraph of the forest
I
, is also a forest, i.e. its connected components

D_{1},\dots,D_{u}
are trees. Further, the total order on
I
, compatible with the covering relation
x\to y
,
induces on each
D_{t}
a structure of rooted tree.
In particular,
D
has a height
h=\mathrm{ht}(D)
and a depth function
\mathrm{dep}_{D}
.

Definition 3.4
.

Recall the notation
f_{D}^{\scriptscriptstyle\nearrow}
from
3.1
. For each connected component

D_{t}
of
D
, set
f_{D_{t}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\nearrow}=\prod_{d\in D_{t}}f_{d}
,
where the product is taken in increasing order of the indices. Note that
f_{d}
and
f_{d^{\prime}}
commute if

d
and
d^{\prime}
belong to different components, by Remark
3.3
.
Thus, one obtains that:

(3.2)

f_{D}^{\scriptscriptstyle\nearrow}=\prod_{t=1}^{u}f_{D_{t}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\nearrow}

where the product on the right can be taken in any order.

Definition 3.5
.

For
k=0,\dots,h
, set
D[k]=\{d\in D\mid\mathrm{dep}_{D}(d)=k\}
and consider
the operator

(3.3)

f_{D[k]}=\prod_{d\in D[k]}f_{d}.

In this product the
f_{d}
’s are pairwise non-linked, hence they commute (see Remark
3.3
),
so that the order of the product does not matter.

Lemma 3.6
.

One has
f_{D}^{\scriptscriptstyle\nearrow}=f_{D[h]}\cdots f_{D[0]}
.

Proof.

Suppose first that
D
is a rooted tree, of height
h
, and let us prove the result by induction on
h
.
The result is true for
h=0
since the factors of

f_{D[0]}
commute with each other. So we may assume
h\geq 1
. Let
d_{0}
be the root of

D
and set
D^{\prime}=D-\{d_{0}\}
. One has
f_{D}^{\scriptscriptstyle\nearrow}=f_{d_{0}}f_{D^{\prime}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\nearrow}
and, since

\mathrm{ht}(D^{\prime})=h-1
and
\mathrm{dep}_{D^{\prime}}(d)=\mathrm{dep}_{D}(d)
for every
d\in D^{\prime}
, one obtains by the inductive hypothesis
that

f_{D^{\prime}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\nearrow}=f_{D^{\prime}[h-1]}\cdots f_{D^{\prime}[0]}=f_{D[h-1]}\cdots f_{D[0]}.

This proves the result in the case of a rooted tree. Thus, in the general case, the result is true for each connected
component
D_{t}
of
D
, i.e. for every
t
one has

f_{D_{t}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\nearrow}=f_{D_{t}[h]}\cdots f_{D_{t}[0]}

with the convention that
f_{D_{t}[k]}=1
if
k
is greater than the height of
D_{t}
. Again, since
f_{d}
and
f_{d^{\prime}}
commute if
d
and
d^{\prime}
belong to different components, the above equality combined with (
3.2
)
gives that

f_{D}^{\scriptscriptstyle\nearrow}=\prod_{k=h}^{0}\Big(\prod_{t=1}^{u}f_{D_{t}[k]}\Big)=f_{D[h]}\cdots f_{D[0]}.

This proves the lemma.
∎

3.2.
More notation about
D

In order to describe conveniently the element
\pi_{D,I}
, we need to introduce more definitions.

Definition 3.7
.

For every
d\in D
, let
C_{I}(d)=\{i\in I\mid i\to d\}

be the set of elements of
I
which cover
d
, and let
c_{I}(d)=|C_{I}(d)|
. We also set
C_{D}(d)=D\cap C_{I}(d)
.

Note that if
d
is a leaf of
D
then either
C_{I}(d)=\varnothing

such that
\mathrm{dep}_{D}(d_{s})

=u-1
. Then

\mu(d_{s})
has height
\mathrm{ht}(d)+u
if it is an anchor dead leaf, and
\mathrm{ht}(d)+u+1
if it is an anchor point.
If one had
\mathrm{dep}_{D}(d_{t})\leq u-2
, then
\mu(d_{t})
would have height
\leq\mathrm{ht}(d)+u
, with equality only
if
\mu(d_{t})
is an anchor point. But by our choice of ordering
2.6
, the anchor points of a given height
are smaller than the anchors dead leaves of the same height. This proves that
\mathrm{dep}_{D}(d_{t})=u-1
.

Similarly, each
\mu(v_{j})
, for
j=1,\dots,p
, is an anchor point of height
\mathrm{ht}(d)+2
, hence is smaller
than
\mu(d_{t})
. Therefore,
\mu(d)=\mu(d_{t})
.
∎

Then, by the inductive hypothesis, we have

(3.12)

\begin{cases}\phi_{d}(x_{d^{-}}^{u})=1=\mathrm{wt}_{d}(x_{d^{-}}^{u})&\text{if $d^{-}$ exists and is in }I,\\
\phi_{d}(x_{d}^{u})=(q{-}1)_{+},\;\mathrm{wt}_{d}(x_{d})=q{-}1&\\
\phi_{d}(x_{k}^{u})=1=\mathrm{wt}_{d}(x_{k})&\text{if }k\in C_{I}(d),\\
\phi_{d}(x_{k}^{u})=1=\mathrm{wt}_{d}(x_{k})&\text{if }k\in\mu\big(\{v_{1},\dots,d_{t}\}\big),\\
\phi_{d}(x_{k}^{u})=0=\mathrm{wt}_{d}(x_{k}^{u})&\text{else}.\end{cases}

Note that for
j
running through the ordered set
R(d)=\{i,d\}\cup C_{I}(d)\cup\{\mu(v_{1}),\dots,\mu(v_{p})\}\cup\{\mu(d_{1}),\dots,\mu(d_{t})\}
, the function
\Phi_{d}
can fail to increase only for
j=i
. Then
each element
k>d
of
R(d)
satisfies
\varphi_{d}(x^{u}_{k})=1=\mathrm{wt}_{d}(x^{u}_{k})
, hence adds
1
to the sum

\Sigma_{d}
.

Since
t\geq 1
, one deduces from this that
\Phi_{d}(X^{u},k)
attains its maximum value for the first time at the place

\mu(d)
(and remains constant for
k\geq\mu(d)
). Thus,
it follows from Lemma
2.2
that when one applies

f_{d}
to
X^{u}
, the factor
f_{d}
is attached at the place corresponding to
the anchor point or anchor dead leaf
\mu(d)
. This completes the proof of Prop.
3.17
.

\square

4.
Completion of the proof of Theorem
1.1

In this section we will prove the following:

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
D
be a subset of
I
such that
\lambda_{D,I}
is dominant.
Then
\pi_{D,I}
is a
\rho
-dominant element of the crystal
B(\rho)
, i.e. one has
\varepsilon_{s}(\pi_{D,I})\leq 1
for all
s\in\Delta
.

The proof will occupy the rest of this section.
Firstly, note that if
j\not\in D
, then
2\alpha_{j}+\mathrm{wt}(\pi_{D,I})
is not
\leq\rho
, hence not a weight of
B(\rho)
. Therefore,
e_{j}^{2}\,\pi_{D,I}=0
, whence
\varepsilon_{j}(\pi_{D,I})\leq 1
. Thus, it suffices to prove that
\varepsilon_{d}(\pi_{D,I})\leq 1
for every
d\in D
.

Recall the notation
\pi_{D,I}=y_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes y_{|\Delta|}
. Let
d\in D
. One deduces from
Prop.
3.17
that
\mathrm{wt}_{d}(y_{k})<0
if and only if:

(1)

k=d
and
y_{d}=f_{d}\pi_{d}
, or

(2)

d=\ell_{r}
for some
r=1,\dots,t
and
k=\mu(d)
, or

(3)

d\in\mathrm{DL}(k)
,

and, in these cases,
\mathrm{wt}_{d}(y_{k})=-1
. Moreover, using
[
BG25
]
, Prop. 2.3, one sees that

\varepsilon_{d}(y_{k})=1
in these cases, while
\varepsilon_{d}(y_{k})=0
in all other cases.
Note further that cases (2) and (3) cannot occur together, since in case (3)
d
is a dead leaf, while it is not
in case (2).
One deduces from this that:

(1)

\Sigma^{\prime}_{d}(\pi_{D,I},k)
is always
\leq 1
.

(2)

The set
S(d)
of indices
k
for which
\varepsilon_{d}(y_{k})
equals
1
has cardinality at most
2
.

(3)

If
S(d)\not=\varnothing
and if
j
is its largest element, one has
\Sigma^{\prime}_{d}(\pi_{D,I},j)=0
and hence

\Upsilon_{d}(\pi_{D,I},j)=1
.
Thus, the proposition is true for
d
if
|S(d)|\leq 1
.

(4)

If
S(d)
contains two elements
i<j
, then
for
k\leq j
, the value of
\Upsilon_{d}(\pi_{D,I},k)
cannot increase until
k
reaches
i
.

Therefore, using Lemma
2.2
, it suffices to prove that if
S(d)
contains two elements
i<j
,
then
\Sigma^{\prime}_{d}(\pi_{D,I},i)\leq 0
, because this will imply that
\Upsilon_{d}(\pi_{D,I},k)\leq 1
for
k\leq i
.

4.1.

Suppose that
d
is a dead leaf such that
y_{d}=f_{d}\pi_{d}
.
Then, by Lemma
3.2
,
i=d^{-}
exists and belongs to
I
.
There are two subcases.

4.1.1.

Suppose that
i\not\in D
. Then necessarily
C_{I}(d)
is non-empty, hence contains a unique element

j
. Then, the elements
y_{k}
for
k=i,d,j
and the values of
\varepsilon_{d},\mathrm{wt}_{d},\Sigma^{\prime}_{d}
and
\Upsilon_{d}

are given by the following table:

\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|}\hline\cr&f_{d}f_{i}\pi_{i}&f_{d}\pi_{d}&f_{j}\pi_{j}\\
\hline\cr\varepsilon_{d}&1&1&0\\
\hline\cr\mathrm{wt}_{d}&-1&-1&1\\
\hline\cr\Sigma^{\prime}_{d}&0&1&0\\
\hline\cr\Upsilon_{d}&1&1&0\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

This proves the result in this case.

4.1.2.

Suppose now that
i\in D
. Since
y_{d}=f_{d}\pi_{d}
, one sees that
d
is not an anchor dead leaf, hence

\sigma(i)>d
and
m_{r}=\mu(i)
is
>d
. Since
f_{i}
appears in the operator
f_{L_{r}}
, one has

\mathrm{wt}_{d}(y_{m_{r}})=1
.
Hence one obtains the following table:

\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|c|}\hline\cr&f_{\mathrm{DL}(i)}f_{i}\pi_{i}&f_{d}\pi_{d}&y_{m_{r}}\\
\hline\cr\varepsilon_{d}&1&1&0\\
\hline\cr\mathrm{wt}_{d}&-1&-1&1\\
\hline\cr\Sigma^{\prime}_{d}&0&-1&0\\
\hline\cr\Upsilon_{d}&1&0&0\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

This proves the result in this case.

4.2.

Suppose that
d
is a vivid leaf of
D
and
d=\ell_{r}
for some
r=1,\dots,t
.
This means that
C_{I}(d)
has cardinality
\geq 2
, that its largest element
m
is
some anchor point
m_{r}
, and that
L_{r}^{*}=\{d\}
. (Thus, if
d^{-}
exists, it does not belong to

D
). Then
y_{m}=f_{d}f_{m}\pi_{m}
.

Let
i_{1}<\cdots<i_{q}
be the others elements of
C_{I}(d)
. For
s=1,\dots,q
,
one has
y_{i_{s}}=f_{i_{s}}\pi_{i_{s}}
, hence
\varepsilon_{d}(y_{i_{s}})=0
while
\mathrm{wt}_{d}(y_{i_{s}})=1
.
Therefore, one obtains the following table:

\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|}\hline\cr&f_{d}\pi_{d}&f_{i_{1}}\pi_{i_{1}}&\cdots&f_{i_{q}}\pi_{i_{q}}&f_{d}f_{m}\pi_{m}\\
\hline\cr\varepsilon_{d}&1&0&\cdots&0&1\\
\hline\cr\mathrm{wt}_{d}&-1&1&\cdots&1&-1\\
\hline\cr\Sigma^{\prime}_{d}&1{-}q&2{-}q&\cdots&1&0\\
\hline\cr\Upsilon_{d}&2{-}q&2{-}q&\cdots&1&1\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

Since
q\geq 1
, this proves the result in this case.

4.3.

Now, let
d\in D
such that
\mathrm{dep}_{D}(d)\geq 1
and
|S(d)|=2
.
The hypothesis
|S(d)|=2
implies, firstly, that
y_{d}=f_{d}\pi_{d}
, i.e.
d
is not a receiving node.
It follows that
C_{I}(d)
contains no dead leaf; in particular
\sigma(d)
either is a vivid leaf or satisfies

\mathrm{dep}_{D}\,\sigma(d)\geq 1
. In both cases, setting
m_{r}=\mu(d)
, one has
m_{r}>\sigma(d)
.
Secondly, the hypothesis
|S(d)|=2
implies that
d=\ell_{r}
.

Finally, note that
y_{\sigma(d)}
equals
f_{\mathrm{DL}(\sigma(d))}f_{\sigma(d)}\pi_{\sigma(d)}
(where

\mathrm{DL}(\sigma(d))
can be empty), so that
\mathrm{wt}_{d}(y_{\sigma(d)})=1
. And, as mentioned earlier,
one has
\varepsilon_{d}(y_{\sigma(d)})=0
, by
[
BG25
]
, Prop. 2.3. Therefore, one obtains the following table:

\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|}\hline\cr&f_{d}\pi_{d}&\cdots&y_{\sigma(d)}&\cdots&y_{m_{r}}\\
\hline\cr\varepsilon_{d}&1&\cdots&0&\cdots&1\\
\hline\cr\mathrm{wt}_{d}&-1&\cdots&1&\cdots&-1\\
\hline\cr\Sigma^{\prime}_{d}&\leq 0&\cdots&1&\cdots&0\\
\hline\cr\Upsilon_{d}&\leq 1&\cdots&1&\cdots&1\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

This proves the result in this case. (Note that the exact values of
\Sigma^{\prime}_{d}(\pi_{D,I},d)
and

\Upsilon_{d}(\pi_{D,I},d)
are
1{-}q
and
2{-}q
, where
q=c_{I}(d)
.)
This completes the proof of Prop.
4.1
and, therefore, of Th.
1.1
.

5.
Examples and concluding remarks

Let us conclude with some examples and remarks. Suppose for simplicity that

For integers

(i,i{+}1)

Let

in the crystal

5.1.

Suppose that

and

. Set
\lambda_{3}=\lambda_{I,I}=2\rho-2\alpha_{[1,3]}=2\omega_{2}

Taking the vertex

(5.1)

f_{1}f_{2}f_{3}^{2}f_{2}f_{1}\pi=f_{1}\pi_{1}\otimes f_{3}f_{2}\pi_{2}\otimes f_{1}f_{2}f_{3}\pi_{3}.
