Semiclassical Gravity Efficiently Solves 𝖭𝖯 {\mathsf{NP}} -Complete Problems
Matthew Fox, Chaitanya Karamchedu, Sotirios Mygdalas

Abstract
Assuming the gravitational field is classical and that it couples to quantum fields via the semiclassical Einstein field equations, we show that the weak-field dynamics of a massive and non-relativistic qubit can in principle be used to solve an 𝖭𝖯 {\mathsf{NP}} -complete problem in polynomial time. We attribute this vast computational power to the non-linear dynamics afforded by the semiclassical Einstein field equations. Consequently, the above two assumptions entail a violation of the Physical Extended Church–Turing Thesis, which we regard as evidence for the quantization of gravity.



I
Introduction

The road to quantum gravity is not easy. Stuck in our armchairs with little to no experimental guidance, it is prudent to ponder why we think gravity is quantum-mechanical at all. Could it be that the contrary is true, that gravity is not fundamentally quantum but classical? Of course, this question is hardly new. It has a long and rich history, and traces back to the seminal works of Møller
Møller, C. “The Energy-Momentum Complex in
General Relativity and Related Problems”, in: Lichnerowicz, M. A. and
Tonnelat, M. A. 1962
and Rosenfeld
Rosenfeld 1963
in the 1960s.

Treating gravity classically but matter quantum-mechanically embodies
semiclassical gravity
, which naturally divides into two parts. The first part is the effect of curved spacetimes on quantum fields. This part is well-understood (full books like
Wald 1995
;
Birrell and Davies 1982
;
Mukhanov and Winitzki 2007
;
Parker and Toms 2009
have been written on it) and it has proven to be invaluable because of Hawking’s discovery that gravitational fields can create particles
Hawking 1975
. The second part is the effect of quantum fields on curved spacetimes. This part is not nearly as understood as the first, largely because it is not clear what it means to superpose spacetimes, which appears to be an inevitability
Wald 1994
.

Within the quantum gravity community, semiclassical gravity is generally regarded as an approximation to an as yet undiscovered theory of quantum gravity
Wald 1995
;
Birrell and Davies 1982
;
Mukhanov and Winitzki 2007
;
Parker and Toms 2009
. However, despite many ongoing experiments (e.g.,
Marletto and Vedral 2017
;
Berglund et al. 2023
;
Namdar et al. 2023
;
Tobar et al. 2024
), there is no definitive experimental evidence that gravity is quantized. As a result, quantum gravity is supported primarily by theoretical arguments, such as those by Eppley and Hannah
Eppley and Hannah 1977
and Page and Geilker
Page and Geilker 1981
. However, many of these arguments are considered inconclusive
Mattingly 2005
;
Mattingly 2006
;
Carlip 2008
;
Großardt 2022
;
Bahrami et al. 2014
;
Kent 2018
, highlighting the need for a new case for the quantization of gravity.

In this paper, we provide such an argument by exploring the computational consequences of a fundamentally semiclassical world. In particular, we show that if semiclassical gravity (as described by the semiclassical Einstein field equations) is correct, then it is possible to solve
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete problems in polynomial time. As this challenges commonly held beliefs about what is and what is not efficiently computable by physical devices (beliefs which are encapsulated in a strong and physical version of the Church–Turing Thesis), our results suggest that semiclassical gravity cannot be a correct description of physical reality.

Our main result hinges on an algorithm due to Bao, Bouland, and Jordan
Bao et al. 2016
, which itself is based on an earlier algorithm by Abrams and Lloyd
Abrams and Lloyd 1998
. Essentially, the Abrams–Lloyd algorithm showed how to use Weinberg’s non-linear theory of quantum mechanics
Weinberg 1989
to efficiently solve hard computational problems (e.g.,
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete problems). Several years later, the Bao–Bouland–Jordan algorithm extended this result and showed that it is, in fact, a more general phenomenon: basically
any
non-linear theory of quantum mechanics can be used to efficiently solve hard computational problems. Our conclusions follow from combining the Bao–Bouland–Jordan algorithm with arguments due to Bahrami et al.
Bahrami et al. 2014
and Giulini et al.
Giulini et al. 2023
that the weak-field evolution of a massive qubit in semiclassical gravity is necessarily non-linear. Incidentally, our work is consistent with related work by Kent et al.
Kent 2005
;
Kent 2021
;
Fedida and Kent 2026
, which we discuss in Section
V
.

II
{\mathsf{NP}}
and the Physical Extended Church–Turing Thesis

Computational complexity theory seeks to classify problems according to their intrinsic difficulty, often by quantifying the resources (e.g., time or memory) required to solve them. In this paper, we are primarily concerned with how the difficulty of a problem changes when a candidate solution, or
proof
, is provided. Indeed, many problems exhibit an asymmetry in the time required to
find
a solution versus the time required to
verify
a solution. Consider, for example, a Sudoku puzzle: checking a completed grid is far easier than constructing one from scratch. However, it is not known if this distinction reflects a genuine difference in computational difficulty. In computational complexity theory, this question is formalized in the famous
{\mathsf{P}}
versus
{\mathsf{NP}}
problem, which we now discuss.

Intuitively, the complexity class
{\mathsf{P}}
(“polynomial time”) consists of all the computational problems that can be efficiently solved (a.k.a.
decided
) by a classical computer.
1
1

1

In fact, the set of computational problems that efficient classical computers can solve is not
{\mathsf{P}}
, but
{\mathsf{BPP}}
(“bounded-error probabilistic polynomial time”), which is the probabilistic analogue of
{\mathsf{P}}
. However, it is generally believed that
{\mathsf{P}}={\mathsf{BPP}}

Arora and Barak 2009
, so in this discussion we have chosen to only talk about
{\mathsf{P}}
.
For example, deciding if a binary string is palindromic or not is in
{\mathsf{P}}
. More formally, let
\{0,1\}^{n}
be the set of all
n
-bit binary strings,
\{0,1\}^{*}
the set of all finite-length binary strings (i.e.,
\{0,1\}^{*}=\bigcup_{n\geq 1}\{0,1\}^{n}
), and
|x|
the length of a string
x
. Then,
{\mathsf{P}}
consists of all the functions
L:\{0,1\}^{*}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
(a.k.a.
languages
) for which there exists a deterministic and polynomial time classical algorithm
M
(i.e., a
deterministic and polynomial time Turing machine
) such that for all inputs
x\in\{0,1\}^{*}
,
L(x)=1
if and only if
M(x)=1
.

On the other hand, the complexity class
{\mathsf{NP}}
(“non-deterministic polynomial time”) consists of all of the computational problems for which a valid proof can be efficiently checked by a classical computer. For example, deciding if a logical Boolean formula like
(x_{1}\lor x_{2})\land(x_{3}\lor\neg x_{2})
, where
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\in\{0,1\}
, has a valid assignment is in
{\mathsf{NP}}
.
2
2

2

This problem is closely related to the Boolean Satisfiability Problem
{\mathsf{SAT}}

Arora and Barak 2009
.
In particular,
L\in{\mathsf{NP}}
if and only if there exists a deterministic and polynomial time algorithm
M
and a polynomial
p
such that for all inputs
x\in\{0,1\}^{*}
,
L(x)=1
if and only if there exists

(usually called a
proof
,
certificate
, or
witness
) such that
M(x,y)=1
. In other words,
L\in{\mathsf{NP}}
if and only if for all
x\in\{0,1\}^{*}
, if
L(x)=1
, then there exists a proof that will cause
M
to accept
x
, and if
L(x)=0
, then there is no proof that will cause
M
to accept
x
.

Evidently,
{\mathsf{P}}\subseteq{\mathsf{NP}}
, and it is a major open question (in fact, a Millennium Prize Problem
Carlson et al. 2023
) whether
{\mathsf{P}}={\mathsf{NP}}
. The general consensus is that
{\mathsf{P}}\neq{\mathsf{NP}}
because the alternative suggests that proving a mathematical statement is just as easy as verifying that a given proof is correct, which is something that mathematical experience says is false
Impagliazzo 1995
.

Now, the entire premise of quantum computation is that it changes the narrative of what can and cannot be computed efficiently in the physical world. In particular, there exist problems widely believed to be in
{\mathsf{NP}}\backslash{\mathsf{P}}
that can be solved efficiently on a quantum computer. The most famous such problem is integer factorization, for which Shor exhibited his eponymous polynomial-time quantum algorithm
Shor 1997
.

That said, it is generally expected that the hardest problems in
{\mathsf{NP}}
—the so-called
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete
problems—do not admit efficient quantum algorithms
Nielsen and Chuang 2010
;
Aaronson 2005
. In part, this belief stems from the idea that
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete problems appear to lack the requisite structure for there to be an exponential quantum speed-up
Aaronson 2005
. Of course, this is not to say that quantum computers offer no advantage for these problems. Grover’s algorithm, for example, solves any
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete problem quadratically faster than any classical brute-force algorithm
Grover 1998
. However, this advantage is modest and does not provide the exponential speed-up required to solve an
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete problem in polynomial time on a quantum computer. It is also worth noting that if quantum computers cannot solve
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete problems in polynomial time, then
{\mathsf{P}}\neq{\mathsf{NP}}

Nielsen and Chuang 2010
.

More generally, it is a tenet of quantum information theory, and of the theory of computation itself, that no physically reasonable model of computation (perhaps even more powerful than quantum computation) can efficiently solve an
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete problem. This expectation is stipulated in the
Physical Extended Church–Turing Thesis
, which was originally proposed by Freedman, Kitaev, Larsen, and Wang in the early 2000s
Freedman et al. 2002
and extended an earlier “physical” version of the Church–Turing Thesis by Deutsch in the 1980s
Deutsch 1985
.

Physical Extended Church–Turing Thesis

(PECTT)
.

No physical procedure can decide an
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete problem in polynomially many steps.

While motivated from computer science, this thesis is largely a postulate about the physical world as it articulates what can and cannot be computed efficiently by physical devices. Thus, in the context of demarcating different physical theories, the PECTT dictates that a candidate theory should be deemed implausible if it can be exploited to efficiently solve an
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete problem. Ultimately, we will show that semiclassical gravity is such a theory.

Semiclassical Gravity and its Weak-Field Limit

Historically, the most popular theory of semiclassical gravity is described by the
semiclassical Einstein field equations (semiclassical EFEs)

3

(1)

III.1
The Weak-Field Limit of Semiclassical Gravity

As shown in
Bahrami et al. 2014
;
Giulini et al. 2023

g_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{\mu\nu}+h_{\mu\nu}

i\,\partial_{t}\Psi(r,t)=\left(-\frac{1}{2m}\nabla^{2}+V_{G}(\Psi)\right)\Psi(r,t),

(2)

V_{G}(\Psi)=-m^{2}\int\frac{|\Psi(r^{\prime},t)|^{2}}{|r-r^{\prime}|}\ \mathrm{d}^{3}r^{\prime}

(3)

Bahrami et al. 2014

Despite the non-linear nature of the SN equation, the standard probabilistic interpretation of the wavefunction persists. Indeed, the potential due to the gravitational self-interaction (
3

;
Giulini et al. 2023

The SN dynamics yield a diversity of novel phenomena that have been extensively studied in the literature

;
Penrose and Marcer 1998
;
Penrose 2014
;
Großardt 2024
;
Hatifi and Durt 2023
;
Sahoo et al. 2023
;
Delgado and Plávala 2025

{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete problems (e.g., Weinberg’s
Weinberg 1989
;
Abrams and Lloyd 1998

III.2
Qubit Dynamics in Weak-Field Semiclassical Gravity

We will now specialize the SN dynamics to the case of a massive and non-relativistic qubit. In particular, consider a non-relativistic spin-1/2 particle of mass
m

|\Psi(t)\rangle
. In the position basis, the state of the particle is a two-component wavefunction
\Psi(r,t)=\langle r|\Psi(t)\rangle

|0\rangle
) and spin-down (
|1\rangle
) states,

\Psi(r,t)=\psi_{\uparrow}(r,t)\otimes\alpha|0\rangle+\psi_{\downarrow}(r,t)\otimes\beta|1\rangle,

(4)

|\alpha|^{2}+|\beta|^{2}=1

4
4

4

Note, it is generally unclear how entangled states evolve in a non-linear quantum theory because the superposition principle no longer holds. To mend this issue, we follow the prescription of Weinberg

In the presence of an additional external potential

(5)

Here, the momentum term causes the wavefunction to spread out spatially in time, while the self-gravitational potential

introduces a competing effect that inhibits the rate of spreading.
5
5

5

Incidentally, this gravitational effect was previously conjectured by Diosi and Penrose to be the mechanism behind wavefunction collapse

;
Penrose and Marcer 1998
;
Penrose 2014
;
Moroz et al. 1998

To see this, note that since

(6)

Therefore, as

Ultimately, these gravitational effects manifest in the dynamics as a non-trivial, initial conditions-dependent phase difference

IV
Solving
{\mathsf{NP}}
-Complete Problems with Semiclassical Gravity

In this section, we show how the SN dynamics can be exploited to efficiently solve
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete problems. Our idea is largely a SN-rebranding of Abrams and Lloyd’s argument that Weinberg’s non-linear theory of quantum mechanics can efficiently solve
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete problems
Abrams and Lloyd 1998
, as well as Bao, Bouland, and Jordan’s
Bao et al. 2016
generalization of Abrams and Lloyd’s work to other non-linear theories. To apply their ideas, however, we first need to rephrase a given
{\mathsf{NP}}
problem as a state discrimination task, which can then be solved using the SN dynamics.

IV.1
Encoding an
{\mathsf{NP}}
Problem into a Qubit

Given
L\in{\mathsf{NP}}
, its corresponding algorithm
M
, its corresponding polynomial
p
, and an input
x\in\{0,1\}^{*}
, let

P_{x}=\left|\left\{y\in\{0,1\}^{p(|x|)}:M(x,y)=1\right\}\right|

(7)

be the number of proofs that cause
M
to accept. Evidently, to decide whether
P_{x}=0
or
P_{x}>0
is sufficient to decide
L
. In
Abrams and Lloyd 1998
, Abrams and Lloyd give a quantum algorithm that can efficiently transform this question to the problem of deciding whether a particular quantum state is
|0\rangle
or not. Specifically, the Abrams–Lloyd algorithm takes any
L\in{\mathsf{NP}}
and prepares the single-qubit quantum state (up to normalization)

\frac{2^{p(|x|)}-P_{x}}{2^{p(|x|)}}|0\rangle+\frac{P_{x}}{2^{p(|x|)}}|1\rangle.

(8)

Therefore, the Abrams–Lloyd algorithm proves that to decide
L
on a quantum computer, it suffices to be able to reliably decide if Eq. (
8
) is
|0\rangle
or not. Intuitively, this state discrimination task is hardest when
P_{x}=1
, i.e., when the language
L
not only has a proof, but has a
unique
proof. Interestingly, as mentioned in
Bao et al. 2016
, by using ideas from computational complexity theory (namely, the Valiant–Vazirani theorem

), one can formalize this intuition and refine the Abrams–Lloyd algorithm so that with sufficiently high probability, deciding
L
does indeed reduce to this hardest case.

Theorem 1

(Corollary of Abrams and Lloyd
Abrams and Lloyd 1998
and Valiant and Vazirani
Valiant and Vazirani 1986
; see also Bao, Bouland, and Jordan
Bao et al. 2016
)
.

For all
L\in{\mathsf{NP}}
, there exists a polynomial time quantum algorithm
Q_{L}
such that for all inputs
x\in\{0,1\}^{*}
,
Q_{L}(x)
outputs the state (up to normalization)

\frac{2^{p(|x|)}-L(x)}{2^{p(|x|)}}|0\rangle+\frac{L(x)}{2^{p(|x|)}}|1\rangle

(9)

with constant probability.

In other words, to decide
L\in{\mathsf{NP}}
, it suffices to be able to reliably distinguish between the single-qubit states
|\varphi_{0}\rangle=|0\rangle
and
|\varphi_{1}\rangle=N\big(\frac{2^{p(|x|)}-1}{2^{p(|x|)}}|0\rangle+\frac{1}{2^{p(|x|)}}|1\rangle\big)
, where

is the normalization factor. Notice that
|\varphi_{1}\rangle
is indeed Eq. (
8
) with
P_{x}=1
. Now, as
|\varphi_{0}\rangle
and
|\varphi_{1}\rangle
are exponentially close in
|x|
, one cannot efficiently distinguish them using standard quantum mechanics

. However, as we show in the next subsection, one can efficiently distinguish them using the SN dynamics.
7
7

7

Note that
|\varphi_{1}\rangle
is a highly non-uniform superposition state. Thus, per our discussion in Section
III.2
, the gravitational effects of the SN equation are large for this state, at least in a SN Stern–Gerlach experiment.

IV.2
The SN Dynamics and the Bao–Bouland–Jordan Algorithm

Let
M(\mathbb{C}^{N})
be the manifold of normalized pure states on
\mathbb{C}^{N}
,

8

8

M(\mathbb{C}^{2})/\mathrm{U}(1)\cong\mathrm{CP}(1)

Theorem 2

\frac{d(S(x),S(y))}{d(x,y)}\geq r

To see how this works for the SN equation and
{\mathsf{NP}}

S_{\mathrm{SN}}
be the map that encodes the net SN evolution of a massive qubit for some fixed amount of time, as discussed in Section

\Delta_{\mathrm{SN}}=\min_{x,y\in\mathrm{Im}(\ell_{\mathrm{SN}})}\frac{d(S_{\mathrm{SN}}(x),S_{\mathrm{SN}}(y))}{d(x,y)}\geq r_{\mathrm{SN}}>1,

V
Discussion

In this paper, we showed that the weak-field dynamics of the semiclassical EFEs can efficiently solve
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete problems. In consequence, if gravity is fundamentally classical and if the semiclassical EFEs describe the matter-gravity coupling, then one can efficiently solve
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete problems. As this violates the PECTT, we are led to the conclusion that either gravity is not classical, or gravity is classical, but the semiclassical EFEs do not correctly describe the matter-gravity coupling.

However, given the incredibly general nature of Theorem
2
, our results suggest that, in fact,
any
consistent, semiclassical, and non-linear matter-gravity coupling will entail an efficient algorithm to solve
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete problems and thus violate the PECTT. We therefore take our results not as an argument for an alternative theory of semiclassical gravity, but rather as an argument for the quantization of gravity itself.

Of course, there are many ways that one may try to evade this conclusion, such as by modifying the non-linear structure of the matter-gravity coupling or by simply rejecting the PECTT.
11
11

11

For example, if the dynamics are such that the density matrix of a quantum state evolves linearly and stochastically (as in Oppenheim’s post-quantum theory
Oppenheim 2023
), then our conclusions do not hold.
Yet each such move comes at a conceptual cost and requires considerable justification. By contrast, quantizing gravity restores linearity and thus avoids our argument altogether. For example, in the specific context of the weak-field limit of the semiclassical EFEs, quantizing the spacetime metric causes the self-gravitational potential in the SN equation to drop out, thus linearizing the dynamics
Anastopoulos and Hu 2014
;
Giulini et al. 2023
.
12
12

12

In particular, if the metric perturbation
h_{\mu\nu}
is quantized, then the matter-gravity coupling
\hat{h}_{\mu\nu}\hat{T}^{\mu\nu}
in the interaction Hamiltonian leads to linear weak-field dynamics.
Consequently, at least in this case, our argument in Section
IV.2
does not apply, and the PECTT is not obviously violated.

While preparing this manuscript, we became aware of the series of works
Kent 2005
;
Kent 2021
;
Fedida and Kent 2026
by Kent et al. concerning hypothetical “readout devices” capable of extracting classical information from a quantum system without disturbing it. In
Kent 2021
, it was argued that semiclassical gravity may provide a physically realizable readout mechanism and, moreover, that such devices could in principle implement the original Abrams–Lloyd algorithm in
Abrams and Lloyd 1998
for solving
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete problems. In this respect, their framework is closely related to the gravitational mechanism studied here, but our conclusions differ primarily in their interpretation. Specifically, whereas Kent et al. emphasize the possibility that such dynamics could lead to experimentally accessible computational advantages beyond standard quantum theory, we emphasize that there is a profound tension between such vast computational consequences and the PECTT. Thus, insofar as one regards the PECTT as a fundamental principle of physics, such readout devices cannot exist.

Ultimately, we hope our results inspire a more computational way of thinking about demarcating fundamental physical theories. While gravitational physics and computational complexity theory are rather disparate fields, here we have demonstrated an intriguing consistency check between the two. Specifically, if the PECTT is taken seriously as a principle of physics, then any candidate theory of gravity must respect it. In this way, the PECTT, and computational complexity more broadly, provides not just a constraint, but a guiding principle in the search for a consistent theory of quantum gravity.
