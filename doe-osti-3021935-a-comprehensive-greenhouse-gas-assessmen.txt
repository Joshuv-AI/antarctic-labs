Abstract
Biomass with Carbon Removal and Sequestration (BiCRS) is a suite of technological pathways
that provide a relatively affordable and high-potential way to remove carbon dioxide from the
atmosphere and simultaneously re-purpose residual biomass. BiCRS technologies are
individually optimized to different biomass feedstocks, temperatures and oxygen, with the goal
of capturing a high percentage of biomass-derived carbon dioxide and storing it in permanent
below-ground storage (i.e. geologic storage). The current standard for BiCRS life cycle
assessments does not account for carbon dioxide, methane and nitrous oxide (CO2, CH4, and
N2O) fluxes from surface soil amendment of BiCRS byproducts (e.g. char, ash) and their
alternate fates. This is partly due to a paucity of empirical data due to the novelty of BiCRS
conversion technologies, the range in feedstocks, and the heterogeneity of soils to which they
might be amended. To fill this gap in in the life-cycle assessment (LCA) and provide a realistic
range of parameters for a soil amendment component of the BiCRS LCA, we conducted an
incubation experiment to measure soil carbon changes, microbial respiration, methane fluxes,
and nitrous oxide fluxes from two different soils amended with char- and ash- byproducts from
biomass after gasification to hydrogen, fast pyrolysis to bio-oil, and torrefaction.
Our results suggest that N2O and CH4 emission or consumption from BiCRS char amendments to
soil are context dependent. Nitrous oxide emissions from amendments are higher in soils with
higher pH, whereas some char amendments to low pH soil can reduce N2O emissions relative to
control. In contrast, CH4 is emitted from BiCRS char amended to low pH soil, but consumed or
neutral relative to control when amended to high pH soil. We present carbon and nitrogen
mass balance throughout the experiment to help understand short-term durability of BiCRS
chars and their counterfactuals, and preliminary suggestions for revisions to the broader BiCRS
Measurement, Reporting, and Verification framework.

Background and Research Objectives
To limit damages from climate warming, international experts and treaty agreements have
established that we must thoroughly decarbonize our energy systems (i.e., convert fully from
fossil to renewable energy sources), but also purposefully remove greenhouse gases from the
atmosphere1, a process called carbon dioxide removal (CDR). CDR approaches range from
encouraging more forest growth and bioenergy crops to techniques like ‘direct air capture’
where carbon dioxide (CO2) is drawn directly from ambient air. In the USA, the CDR pathway
with the highest potential to economically scale is termed ‘Biomass-based Carbon Removal and
Storage’ (BiCRS)2,3, a process where waste biomass (agricultural, forest, trash and food waste)
or purpose-grown bioenergy crops are used to produce fuels or electricity, while CO2 emissions
are simultaneously captured. LLNL’s national assessment of CO2 removal technologies indicates
that BiCRS has the capacity to provide 500 million tons of CO2 removal per year, using multiple
different conversion technologies for various bioproducts4. There are over ten different BiCRS
technologies (Figure 1), including gasification to hydrogen, hydrothermal processing to
electricity, and anaerobic digestion. For all BiCRS technologies, the fundamental goal is to
produce bioenergy, capture CO2 and store it in liquid, solid, or gaseous forms.
Waste biomass can stem from wildfire mitigation in forests (e.g. softwood), municipal
greenwaste from cities (e.g. mixed wood), or from agricultural sources (e.g. corn stover).
Repurposing waste biomass for BiCRS has two major benefits beyond decarbonized energy
production and climate-change mitigation: 1st, using waste biomass feedstocks for BiCRS would
reduce the risk of wildfires in fire-prone forest regions, and 2nd could provide municipalities and
agricultural producers with an additional, diversified revenue stream for their biomass.
As BiCRS pathways scale to meet our urgent climate and energy needs, it is imperative to
understand the full life cycle climate impacts of the process to ensure the overall process has a
true net negative effect on the climate. Current BiCRS Life Cycle Assessment tools, such as
GREET6, account for the greenhouse gas emissions from transporting feedstocks (e.g. waste
biomass) to the BiCRS facility, the energy required to run the BiCRS process (i.e. combustion
and carbon capture), and the fossil emissions avoided due to use of biofuels or bioelectricity.
In converting biomass to energy, BiCRS also produces byproducts—, dry-ash-, char-, wet-
viscous-or oil- like substances that are not well characterized7 and must be disposed of
sustainably. Storage or disposal of these byproducts are not included within GREET Life Cycle
Assessment. One storage option for solid bioproducts like biochar and ash is to spread them on
nearby marginal or agricultural soils. Biochar has been shown to improve soil ecosystem
services in some situations, but the overall effects dependent on site and bioproduct
characteristics8 Surface application of these solid BiCRS products to soils is an economical
storage method, but the net CO2 (and CO2 equivalent) removal depends on the biogeochemical
response of soil to the amendment. Amending BiCRS byproducts to soil could stimulate
microbial activity and increase fluxes of CO2 and potent greenhouse gases nitrous oxide,
methane (N2O, CH4) and CO2, which might reduce or cancel out the net-negative effect of the
overall BiCRS process. For example, organic wastes may cause additional denitrification
(microbial reduction of nitrate) and to nitrous oxide (N2O) gas, which has climate forcing effect
273 times more potent than CO29. Thus, even relatively small emissions of N2O could drastically

reduce the net CDR outcome. This study aims to quantify greenhouse gas emissions (N2O, CH4,
and CO2) from BiCRS byproducts and the alternative fates of their waste-inputs, which are a
critical but missing piece of current BiCRS life cycle assessments (Figure 2), and could affect
the realized climate-benefit of BiCRS pathways as a CDR strategy. The net greenhouse gas fluxes
from amendment of BiCRS products will depend on both the soil environment, (climate, soil
texture, pH, and soil carbon and nitrogen availability), and on the biochemical characteristics of
the BiCRS product (moisture, nitrogen content, structural

Scientific Approach and Accomplishments
This study quantified greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions (CO₂, CH₄, N₂O) from soils amended with
byproducts of three biomass carbon removal and storage (BiCRS) technology pathways, using
three distinct feedstocks: softwood forest residual woodchips, mixed woodchips from urban
greenwaste, and corn stover agricultural residual pellets. The experiment assessed two storage

fates for each feedstock: (1) pre-conversion raw biomass amended to surface soil, and (2) post-
BiCRS byproduct (ash or biochar) amended to soil. The GHG fluxes were measured under
controlled moisture and temperature conditions, and results will be used to inform Life Cycle
Assessment (LCA) tools within a BiCRS Measurement, Reporting, and Verification framework.
Materials and Treatments
  Feedstocks: softwood forest residual biomass (0.13 %N), hardwood urban greenwaste
 biomass (0.20 %N) and agricultural corn stover pellets (0.64 % N), were obtained from
 BiCRS industry partners.
  BiCRS Technologies: (i) Torrefaction to biochar (400-500 ˚C), (ii) Fast pyrolysis to bio-oil
 (500-700 ˚C, produces biochar) , (iii) Gasification to hydrogen (>800 ˚C, produces ash).
  Byproducts: 500 g each of ash and biochar derived from the above processes.
  Soils: Surface soils (0–10 cm) from grazed annual grassland from two locations in
 California: Hopland (low pH of 5.7) and Livermore (high pH of 9.0)
  Controls: Unamended soils disturbed to same time and degree as amended soils
  Replicates: 5 replicates per treatment with the exception of 3 treatments which had 4
 replicates due to measurement capacity constraints.
Material Characterization
All feedstocks, byproducts, and soils were characterized for moisture content, pH, and total C
and N concentrations. Detailed biomass chemistry or both pre- and post- conversion biomass
was characterized by the NREL Catalytic Carbon Transformation facility prior to incubation.
Moisture was determined gravimetrically by drying subsamples at 105°C for 24 hours. We
measured pH in a 1:2 soil:water suspension. Total C and N were analyzed using an elemental
analyzer.
Soil and Amendment Preparation and incubation conditions
Soils were air-dried, homogenized, and sieved to 2 mm. Large roots and rocks were removed by
hand. For each incubation jar, 352.4 g air-dried soil (equivalent to 351 g oven-dried) was
weighed into acid-washed mason jars. For amendment treatments, 2% (w/w) of the relevant
BiCRS byproduct or pre-conversion biomass was thoroughly mixed into the top 5 cm of soil. Soil
was compacted or fluffed to a controlled 1.17 g/cm³ bulk density at the 300 mL mark. Water
was added to bring soils to 60% of WHC, determined for each soil type (Hopland: 0.72 g/g,
Livermore: 0.68 g/g)
Jars were sealed except for a quick release inflow and outflow valve and incubated in the dark
at 22°C in an incubation chamber. Moisture was maintained by weighing and rewetting to the
starting moisture level approximately every four days. Jars were stored in the climate
controlled incubation chamber and connected to the LiCOR multiplexer system for gas flux
measurements according to a rotating schedule.
Greenhouse Gas Flux Measurement

Headspace GHG fluxes were measured using an automated LiCOR system (LiCOR 7810, 7820
analyzers, with a Li-8250 multiplexer and two extension manifolds). Soils were amended and
wet up and immediately measured continuously for gas fluxes for 48 hours following
amendment. After all soils were amended, three batches of 21 samples were rotated on 24-69
hour increments, with each batch of samples analyzed continuously for an average of at least
51 hours per week, including at least two separate measurement periods per week.
Observation times were set to 300 seconds for the very first measurement after amendment,
and 120 seconds per measurement thereafter, with a 30-second purge period between
measurements to purge sample gases from tubing. Faw flux data were imported into
SoilFluxPro software. Fluxes and concentrations for CO₂, CH₄, and N₂O were calculated using
linear fits applied to CO₂ and exponential fits to CH₄ and N₂O. Deadband times were optimized
using R² values for each gas and observation using SoilFluxPro software, resulting in a 20
second deadband based on both algorithmic and visual assessment of concentration time
series. Instrument diagnostics were monitored throughout, with regular inspection for moisture
in tubing and periodic instrument downtime to dry sampling lines. All data are checked for
errors using diagnostic mean variables in SoilFluxPro.
Post-Incubation Soil Processing
After incubation, amended soils were subsampled for final mass, soil moisture content and pH,
then samples were air-dried. All soils were measured for final %C and %N content on the
elemental analyzer.
Measurement Reporting and Verification framework integration
Model outputs inform the development of a storage module for a BiCRS Measurement,
Reporting, and Verification tool developed in collaboration with industry partners and the
National Renewable Energy Laboratory. Results will be used to identify optimal storage and
amendment strategies to maximize CO2 removal potential.
Results
We found that raw feedstock (softwood chips, mixed wood chips, and corn stover pellets)
amendments to soil resulted in 4.8 times greater CO2 respiration (11.04 mmol CO2/kg/day over
60 days) than BiCRS postproduct amendments (2.30 mmol CO2/kg/day) relative to control soils.
In contrast, BiCRS postproduct amendments had higher nitrous oxide emissions that raw
feedstock amendments, especially after the first week of incubation. Nitrous oxide emissions
were reduced when byproducts were amended to the low pH soil, and in low pH soil the higher
temperature conversion technologies (e.g. gasification) resulted in lower nitrous oxide
emissions than controls, whereas higher temperature conversion technology (e.g. torrefaction)
resulted in higher or similar nitrous oxide emissions relative to controls.
We found that methane emissions were higher when wood-derived amendments were applied
to high pH soil. While the conversion technology did not have a strong impact on methane
fluxes, soil pH had a very strong effect, with methane emissions of 2500-5000 nmol CH4/kg/day
over 60 days relative to control from all postproduct amendments to the low pH soil, and

methane consumption of 0 to -2500 nmol CH4/kg/day from postproduct amendments to high
pH soil.

Mission Impact
This study supports the LLNL Mission of Energy Security within the Focus Area in Earth System
Resilience by contributing to both renewable energy sources and climate mitigation (carbon
dioxide removal) efforts. We created a new cross-division partnership between NACSD (A.
Mayer and A. Chew) and CED (A. Aui), and a new collaboration with the National Renewable
Energy Laboratory, who provided byproduct material for the incubation experiment and
continue to collaborate with us on a parallel study of the biogeochemistry of BiCRS bioproducts.
The results of this project are being integrated into Measurement, Reporting, and Verification
frameworks commissioned by the Department of Energy, to improve the actual net carbon
removal calculations for BiCRS pathways throughout their entire life cycle. This outcome will
influence the choices about which technologies to scale, and will more accurately represent the
climate benefit for crediting in carbon markets. Finally, this research has supported career
development of two postdoctoral and their conversion to LLNL research scientists (A. Mayer, A.
Aui).

Conclusion
The results from this study are helping to fill the gap in in existing life cycle assessment for
BiCRS climate assessments, and provides a realistic range of parameters for greenhouse gas
fluxes and short-term carbon durability for a soil storage component of the BiCRS life-cycle. In
collaboration with four BiCRS industry partners, a complimentary field study still in-progress,
and technoeconomic analysis experts from both LLNL and NREL, we are in the process of
developing a new byproduct-soil module for an existing industry standard Measurement,
Reporting, and Verification decision tree tool, which will enable stakeholders to identify the
true CDR potential of the entire life cycle of waste-biomass and allow a more nuanced
comparison for carbon market investors interested in high-quality carbon credits.
