Chenodeoxycholic acid restrains tumor growth via TGR5-dependent type 1 dendritic cells cross-priming
Authors: Fanlian Zeng, Fulei Zhao, Pei Zhou, Ya Li, Xiaoyan Wang, Yawen Hu, Jiadong Yu, Guolin Li, Chengcheng Yue, Yuting Feng, Nongyu Huang, Xiaochi Sun, Xinmeng Wang, Shishi Huang, Mingxiang He, Wenling Wu, Kaijun Cui, Jiong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractConventional type 1 dendritic cells (cDC1s) play a pivotal role in initiating CD8+ T-cell-mediated antitumor immunity, and their intratumoral abundance strongly correlates with immunotherapy efficacy. Here, we demonstrate that orally administered chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA), a clinically approved metabolic modulator, accumulates in the tumor interstitial fluid (TIF) of tumor-bearing mice as its metabolite taurochenodeoxycholic acid (TCDCA), thereby potentiating the antitumor functions of cDC1s and CD8+ T cells through a gut microbiota-independent mechanism. Mechanistic studies revealed that CDCA mediates this immunomodulatory effect via TGR5 signaling, which enhances cDC1 functionality and subsequently promotes the proliferation and activation of tumor-specific CD8+ T cells. Clinical correlation analysis of human tumor specimens revealed a positive association between Takeda G protein-coupled receptor 5 (TGR5) expression and HLA-A/B/C/E/F/H levels, with elevated TGR5 expression significantly predicting improved overall patient survival. Moreover, CDCA exhibits synergistic antitumor effects when combined with anti-PD-1 immunotherapy and a poly I:C adjuvant, leading to robust tumor growth suppression and enhanced antitumor-immune responses. Overall, our study elucidates a novel immunoregulatory mechanism through which CDCA modulates tumor progression, highlighting CDCA and its bile acid derivatives as promising therapeutic candidates for cancer immunotherapy, providing a potential strategy to overcome resistance in cold tumors by revitalizing the cDC1-CD8+ T-cell axis.Subject terms: Tumour immunology, Tumour immunology

Introduction

The tumor microenvironment (TME) is a specialized ecosystem consisting of cancer cells, infiltrating immune cells, blood vessels, fibroblasts, and other stromal cells. It is shaped by tumor cells to facilitate tumor growth and metastasis and has profound implications for cancer therapy. Studies have shown that the antitumor activity of dendritic cells (DCs) and T cells is modulated by cytokines, chemokines, and stromal components present within the TME.1,2 Notably, cDC1s are capable of capturing and presenting tumor antigens, and “cross-presentation” of exogenous tumor-derived antigens onto MHC class I molecules, thereby cross-priming antitumor CD8+ T cells.3–5 Activated CD8+ T cells secrete cytokines such as interferon gamma (IFN-γ), granzyme B (GzmB), and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα) to kill tumor cells. Extensive clinical and transcriptomic studies have consistently demonstrated that a high density of intratumoral cDC1s serves as a robust prognostic indicator for favorable outcomes in various malignancies,1,4,6. Despite their pivotal role, the TME often evolves to suppress cDC1 recruitment and maturation through the secretion of inhibitory metabolites and cytokines, effectively “blinding” the immune system to the presence of the tumor. Consequently, understanding the metabolic and signaling cues that govern cDC1 abundance and functionality has become a paramount objective in the field of molecular oncology.

In recent years, the clinical success of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and adoptive cell therapies has firmly established immunotherapy as a cornerstone of modern cancer treatment. These approaches have demonstrated remarkable efficacy in a subset of patients by reinvigorating antitumor-immune responses and overcoming immune evasion mechanisms. However, despite these advances, overall response rates remain limited across many cancer types. One of the major contributing factors is the inefficiency of antigen presentation and subsequent T-cell priming, which are critical steps for initiating robust and durable antitumor immunity.7,8 To address these limitations, dendritic cell (DC) vaccines have been developed as a strategy to enhance antigen presentation and stimulate tumor-specific immune responses. DCs are professional antigen-presenting cells with the unique ability to activate naïve T cells, making them an attractive platform for cancer immunotherapy. Nevertheless, DC-based vaccines still face several challenges, including suboptimal MHC class I antigen presentation and insufficient maturation of DCs, both of which are essential for effective T-cell activation.9,10 Given that cross-presentation via MHC class I molecules is crucial for the activation of tumor-specific CD8+ T cells,11,12 strategies aimed at enhancing this process are of particular importance. In particular, approaches that upregulate MHC I/peptide complex expression and increase costimulatory molecule levels on DCs may significantly improve the efficacy of DC-based immunotherapies.

CDCA is a primary bile acid synthesized from cholesterol in the liver and has long been recognized for its essential role in the emulsification and absorption of dietary lipids. Beyond its surfactant properties, CDCA acts as a potent signaling molecule with high affinity for various nuclear receptors and G protein-coupled receptors. Historically, CDCA has been utilized in clinical practice to treat cholesterol gallstones by reducing biliary cholesterol saturation, and it remains a standard therapy for certain inborn errors of bile acid synthesis and hypertriglyceridemia.13 Recent studies have indicated that CDCA suppresses the progression of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) by activating the ROS/p38 MAPK/DGAT1 pathway in leukemia cells.14 Additionally, CDCA inhibits the progression of lung adenocarcinoma through the integrin α5β1/FAK/p53 signaling pathway15 and promotes angiogenesis via the COX-2 pathway.16 A recent study developed BileMet to distinguish malignant from benign biliary diseases and highlighted that glycochenodeoxycholic acid (GCDCA) is protective.17 Despite this growing body of evidence regarding the cell-intrinsic effects of CDCA on tumor self-proliferation and survival, its broader influence on the tumor-immune microenvironment remains largely unexplored. The possibility that bile acids, which circulate systemically and are influenced by diet and metabolism, could serve as natural ligands to “tune” the immune response presents a fascinating new frontier in immunometabolism. Whether CDCA can bridge the gap between systemic metabolic health and localized antitumor immunity is a question of significant therapeutic importance.

In this study, we discovered that orally administered CDCA inhibits the growth of highly immunogenic transplanted tumors in mice and promotes the infiltration of cDC1s and CD8+ T cells through a gut microbiota-independent mechanism. Untargeted metabolomics revealed that CDCA administration led to an increase in TCDCA within the tumor interstitial fluid. TCDCA enhances antigen presentation by cDC1s in a TGR5-dependent manner, thereby promoting the proliferation and cytotoxic activity of CD8+ T cells. Analysis of TCGA data revealed a positive correlation between HLA-A/B/C/E/F/H expression and TGR5 gene expression, which is associated with improved overall patient survival. Furthermore, TCDCA significantly improved the therapeutic efficacy of cDC1 vaccine treatment against B16-OVA melanoma in mice and enhanced tumor suppression when combined with poly I:C or anti-PD-1 antibodies. This study primarily employs the B16-OVA melanoma model to investigate the mechanisms of antigen-specific immune responses within the tumor-immune microenvironment. Additionally, we incorporated the MC38 colorectal cancer model in the CDCA and TCDCA treatment experiments. The selection of these models not only aids in verifying the broad applicability of our findings but also enhances the scientific robustness of the study. Collectively, our findings reveal a novel role and mechanism of CDCA in modulating tumor immunity, providing valuable insights into the clinical potential of bile acid pathway metabolites.

Results

CDCA-mediated tumor growth inhibition is driven by immune responses

To investigate the effects of CDCA on tumor growth, C57BL/6J mice aged 6 weeks were randomized to receive either vehicle or 5 mg/kg CDCA via oral gavage to establish tumor models in mice. (Fig. 1a). Two weeks after gavage administration, no significant difference in body weight was observed between the CDCA-treated and vehicle control mice (Supplementary Fig. 1a–e). Subcutaneous tumor xenograft models were established in mice via syngeneic MC38 colon cancer cells, B16‒F10 melanoma cells, B16‒OVA melanoma cells, 4T1 breast cancer cells, or LLC Lewis lung cancer cells. Interestingly, MC38 and B16-OVA tumors, which are characterized by high immunogenicity, exhibited significantly slower growth in CDCA-treated mice than in vehicle-treated mice (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 1b, c). Similarly, moderately immunogenic B16-F10 tumors exhibited a reduced growth rate with CDCA treatment (P < 0.01) (Fig. 1d). In contrast, the growth of poorly immunogenic 4T1 and LLC tumors did not significantly differ between the CDCA-treated and vehicle groups (Fig. 1e, f). Survival analysis revealed that compared with vehicle treatment, CDCA treatment significantly prolonged survival in mice bearing MC38 and B16-OVA tumors (Fig. 1g, h). However, no significant differences in survival were observed between the CDCA-treated and vehicle groups in mice bearing B16-F10, 4T1, or LLC tumors (Supplementary Fig. 1f–h).

To determine whether CDCA-mediated tumor inhibition is immune dependent, nude mice were injected with B16-OVA cells. The results revealed that CDCA had no effect on the body weights of the two groups of nude mice (Supplementary Fig. 1i) and did not affect tumor growth or survival (Fig. 1i, j). Additionally, to investigate whether CDCA suppresses tumor growth through T-cell-mediated immune responses, we systematically employed T-cell subset depletion strategies in a B16-OVA tumor-bearing mouse model (Fig. 1k). The experimental results revealed no CDCA-dependent changes in B16-OVA tumor growth in mice after CD8+ T-cell depletion (Fig. 1l, m, Supplementary Fig. 1j–l). CD4+ T-cell depletion reduced B16-OVA tumor growth under both conditions (Supplementary Fig. 1m–o); however, it did not eliminate the tumor growth inhibition caused by CDCA treatment (Fig. 1n, o).

Taken together, these results suggest that the impact of CDCA on tumors is linked to immune regulation. especially CD8+ T cells.

CDCA reshapes the immune microenvironment of tumors in mice

These results suggest that CDCA may play a regulatory role in tumor immunity. To understand how CDCA gavage alters the immune microenvironment of B16-OVA tumors, tumor-draining lymph nodes (tdLNs) and tumor-infiltrating immune cell populations were investigated by flow cytometry (Fig. 2a). In CDCA-treated B16-OVA tumor-bearing mice, an increase in CD45+ leukocyte infiltration within the tumor was observed (Fig. 2b). We employed the following protocol to analyze T lymphocytes (Supplementary Fig. 2a). The results of the T lymphocyte analysis revealed an increased ratio of CD8+ T cells to live cells, as well as an increase in CD3+ T cells, in both the tumor and tumor-draining lymph nodes of the CDCA-treated mice (Fig. 2c, d, Supplementary Fig. 2b, c). The ratio of CD4+ T cells to regulatory CD4+CD25+ Foxp3+ (Tregs) cells did not significantly change (Supplementary Fig. 2d, e).

To investigate the effect of CDCA on CD8+ T cells in B16-OVA tumor-bearing mice, we analyzed markers indicative of CD8+ T-cell function and activity. CDCA treatment increased the levels of interferon gamma (IFN-γ), granzyme B (GzmB), tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα), CD69, and Ki67 in CD8+ T cells (Fig. 2e–i, Supplementary Fig. 2f). Confocal microscopy analysis revealed that CDCA treatment significantly increased the infiltration of Ki67+CD8+ T cells in tumor tissues (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b), suggesting that CDCA may promote the intratumoral recruitment of proliferative CD8+ T cells. The expression of PD-1, LAG-3, and CD39 on CD8+ T cells in tumors and tdLNs was comparable between the CDCA and vehicle-treated groups (Supplementary Fig. 3c). Consistent with the increase in CD8+ T cells, we further detected an elevated percentage of tetramer-SIINFEKL-specific CD8+ T cells in both the tumor and tdLN of the CDCA-treated group (Fig. 2j, k). In line with the results of the flow cytometric analysis, the immunohistochemical staining results revealed that CDCA treatment promoted massive CD8 + T-cell infiltration throughout the tumor, including both the edges and the center (Supplementary Fig. 3d, e). This finding suggested that the increased presence of CD8+ T cells within the tumor following CDCA treatment may be due to either the proliferation of cells within the tumor or an increase in the migration of peripheral cells. To validate the generalizability of the immunoregulatory effects of CDCA on CD8+ T cells, we established an MC38 tumor xenograft model for assessment. We also observed an increased percentage of CD8+ T cells (Fig. 2l) and tetramer-Adpgk-specific CD8+ T cells (Fig. 2m) following CDCA treatment, along with elevated levels of IFN-γ, GzmB, and TNFα in CD8+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 3f–h). These results suggest that CDCA treatment leads to the activation of CD8+ T cells.

In addition to CD8+ T cells, we investigated the effects of CDCA on other immune cell populations in B16-OVA tumors, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 4a. We found that CDCA had no effect on CD3-NK1.1+ NK cells, F4/80+CD11b+ macrophages, or CD45+CD11b+Gr1+ MDSCs (Supplementary Fig. 4b–d). However, the numbers of CD11c+ cells and CD11c+MHC II+ cells were increased in tumors after CDCA treatment (Fig. 2n, Supplementary Fig. 4e). Recent studies have shown that cDC1s primarily activate CD8+ T cells, whereas cDC2s primarily promote CD4+ T cells.3,4 Therefore, we evaluated CD103+ CD11b- DCs (cDC1s) and CD103-CD11b+ DCs (cDC2s) (Supplementary Fig. 4a). The results indicated that the proportion of cDC1 cells increased in tumors following treatment with CDCA, whereas the proportion of cDC2 cells in the tumors decreased. (Fig. 2o, Supplementary Fig. 4f). We also examined DC activation markers and found that the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of the costimulatory molecules CD40 and MHC I (H-2Kb) was increased in tumors and tdLNs treated with CDCA (Fig. 2p, q). However, the expression levels of CD86, CD80, C-C motif chemokine receptor 7 (CCR7), and MHC II did not significantly change (Supplementary Fig. 4g–j). To refine the flow cytometry sorting strategy for the cDC1 and cDC2 subsets, we systematically analyzed DC subpopulations within the CD45+Lineage-CD11c+MHC II+ cell population (Supplementary Fig. 4k). The results demonstrated that CDCA treatment significantly increased the proportion of CD45+Lineage-CD11c+MHC II+ DCs (Supplementary Fig. 4l) and increased the frequency of cDC1s, whereas cDC2s tended to decrease in the two sorting strategies (Supplementary Fig. 4m).

These findings suggest that CD8+ T cells and cDC1s are the immune cell types most dramatically impacted by CDCA in the B16-OVA tumor microenvironment (TME).

Single-cell RNA-seq reveals CDCA modifications within tumor-infiltrating immune cell subsets

To further investigate the alterations in intertumoral immune cell populations, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on purified CD45+ immune cells isolated from B16-OVA tumors in mice fed either vehicle control or CDCA-supplemented diets. This approach provides unbiased and comprehensive profiling of the tumor-immune transcriptome (Fig. 3a). To delineate major cell populations, we conducted unsupervised clustering analysis on the integrated single-cell datasets from both treatment conditions, identifying 11 distinct cell clusters, all of which contained cells from both dietary groups (Fig. 3b). On the basis of the expression patterns of known genetic markers (Supplementary Fig. 5a) and immune cell signatures (Supplementary Fig. 5b), these clusters were annotated into 11 immune cell types. Comparative analysis revealed that CDCA-treated animals presented significantly increased proportions of T lymphocytes, circulating T cells, and natural killer (NK) cells, whereas the relative abundance of dendritic cell (DC) populations remained unchanged (Fig. 3c, d).

Transcriptomic analysis of T-cell and circulating T-cell populations further demonstrated that CDCA-treated mice presented upregulated CD8a expression without significant changes in CD4 levels. Notably, tumor-infiltrating T cells from CDCA-treated mice exhibited marked upregulation of genes encoding T-cell activation markers (e.g., Cd69 and ICOS), effector molecules (GzmB, FasL, and Prf1), and chemokine receptors (CXCR6) (Fig. 3e, f). Pathway enrichment analysis revealed that genes upregulated in CDCA-treated tumor-infiltrating T cells were involved primarily in transcriptional regulation, mRNA splicing and processing, and T-cell differentiation, all of which are associated with innate and adaptive immunity (Fig. 3g). These findings suggest that CDCA treatment promotes the accumulation of a more antitumorigenic CD8+ T-cell population within the TME. To characterize the CDCA-induced CD8+ T-cell landscape at single-cell resolution, we performed scRNA-seq on CD8+ T cells. UMAP visualization revealed distinct clustering patterns between the vehicle- and CDCA-treated groups for both lineages (Fig. 3h). Within the CD8+ T-cell compartment, CDCA treatment led to a marked expansion of the proliferative CD8+ T-cell population and a contraction in the relative proportion of the TEML (T effector memory-like) population following CDCA treatment (Fig. 3i). These data suggest that CDCA may drive the mobilization of memory-like T cells, facilitating their differentiation into active effector states characterized by enhanced proliferative capacity and cytolytic function.

Subsequent transcriptomic profiling of DC populations (Fig. 3j) and circulating DC subsets (Fig. 3k) revealed that CDCA-treated mice presented increased expression of transcription factors linked to cDC1 development (Clec9a, IRF8) but reduced expression of those associated with cDC2 differentiation (IRF4). Additionally, the expression of chemokines that recruit CD8+ T cells (CXCL9 and CXCL10), the chemokine receptor XCR1 (critical for DC-CD8+ T-cell interactions), antigen presentation-related genes (H2-K1), and cytokines that enhance CD8+ T-cell activation (IL-2) was upregulated in CDCA-treated mice (Fig. 3j, k). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that the upregulated genes in DCs were predominantly involved in the interferon response and antigen presentation pathways (Fig. 3l). Similarly, the DC landscape underwent significant remodeling (Fig. 3m). Notably, the proportion of classic cDC1s, which are essential for antigen cross-presentation, substantially increased following CDCA administration, whereas the relative frequencies of MregDCs and cDC2s were altered (Fig. 3n). These findings suggest that CDCA promotes a transition toward a more activated and immunogenic microenvironment characterized by enhanced cDC1 recruitment and CD8+ T-cell proliferation.

To address this, we first sought to determine whether CDCA treatment enhances the recruitment of CD8+ T cells into tumors from lymphoid organs. We used fingolimod (FTY720), a functional antagonist of the S1P1 receptor,18 to block the egress of T cells from lymphoid organs (Fig. 3o). As expected, FTY720 treatment dramatically reduced the number of CD45+ and CD8+ T cells in the peripheral blood (Supplementary Fig. 5c, d). The B16-OVA tumor progression results showed that FTY720 administration promoted tumor growth but did not completely abrogate the inhibitory effect of CDCA treatment (Fig. 3p). Although FTY720 treatment reduced the infiltration of CD45+ cells, CD8+ T cells, and CD11c+MHC II+ DCs in tumors, it did not affect the CDCA-induced increase in these immune cells (Supplementary Fig. 5e–g). Confocal images of cDC1s were consistent with the results of the flow cytometric analysis (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b). These results indicate that the inhibition of tumor growth by CDCA treatment is associated with both the egress of immune cells from lymphoid organs and the presence of immune cells in tumors.

To investigate whether the reduced growth rates of B16-OVA tumors in CDCA-treated mice were due to DC cells, vehicle- and CDCA-treated mice were subcutaneously inoculated with B16-OVA cells and administered either an anti-CD11c antibody or an isotype-matched control antibody (IgG) (Supplementary Fig. 6c, d). Although the growth rate of B16-OVA tumors remained reduced in CDCA-treated mice injected with anti-IgG, CDCA treatment failed to affect tumor growth rates when anti-CD11c antibodies were administered (Fig. 3q). Accordingly, CDCA did not affect the ratio of CD3+CD8+ T cells or tetramer-SIINFEKL+CD8+ T cells to live cells after anti-CD11c antibody treatment (Supplementary Fig. 6e, f). Similarly, the levels of IFN-γ, GzmB, and TNF-α in CD8+ T cells followed the same trend (Supplementary Fig. 6g). Moreover, to elucidate the critical role of cDC1s in CDCA-mediated antitumor immunity, we generated Batf3−/− mice lacking cDC1s19 (Supplementary Fig. 6h–j). Consistent with previous findings, compared with vehicle treatment, CDCA treatment decelerated the growth of B16-OVA tumors in WT mice. However, this antitumor effect was completely abrogated in Batf3−/− mice (Fig. 3r). Furthermore, the increase in the proportions of SIINFEKL-H-2Kb complex-specific CD8+ T cells and effector CD8⁺ T cells observed after CDCA treatment in WT mice was not detected in Batf3−/− mice (Supplementary Fig. 6k‒m). These findings conclusively demonstrate that the antitumor effect of CDCA strongly depends on the presence of cDC1s.

Taken together, these data provide evidence that CDCA decreases B16-OVA tumor growth by limiting antitumor CD8+ T-cell and cDC1 cell responses.

CDCA regulates tumors independently of the gut microbiota

Upon entering the intestine, CDCA can be metabolized by the gut microbiota into other secondary bile acids, which may mediate its effects.20,21 To investigate whether CDCA exerts its biological effects through modulation of the gut microbiota, we initially performed 16S rRNA gene sequencing analysis on fecal samples collected from CDCA-gavaged and vehicle control mice prior to tumor inoculation. Alpha diversity analysis, including the number of operational taxonomic units and the inverse Simpson index, revealed that CDCA treatment did not significantly alter microbial richness or diversity (Supplementary Fig. 7a). However, principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated that CDCA treatment significantly modified the β diversity of the microbial community (Fig. 4a), suggesting that CDCA may influence the overall structural composition of the gut microbiota. Despite these structural changes, CDCA treatment did not significantly affect the relative abundance of major phyla, including Bacillota, Bacteroidota, and Verrucomicrobiota (Supplementary Fig. 7b, c). Genus-level analysis revealed that CDCA treatment primarily increased the abundance of Akkermansia (Fig. 4b, c).

To determine whether the gut microbiota mediates the antitumor effects of CDCA, we established pseudogerm-free mice through broad-spectrum antibiotic mixture (ABX) treatment followed by B16-OVA melanoma cell inoculation (Fig. 4d). Microbiological culture and 16S rRNA sequencing of fecal samples collected before and after ABX treatment confirmed successful microbiota depletion: plate culture completely eliminated cultivable bacteria (Supplementary Fig. 7d); sequencing analysis revealed significantly reduced microbial richness and diversity (Supplementary Fig. 7e); PCA demonstrated a complete loss of microbiota structural features (Fig. 4e); and there was a marked reduction in the relative abundance of dominant taxa, including norank_f__Muribaculaceae, Allobaculum, and Akkermansia (Fig. 4f, Supplementary Fig. 7f-g). Importantly, under complete microbiota depletion conditions, CDCA still significantly inhibited tumor growth (Fig. 4g) and promoted the infiltration of CD3+ CD8+ T cells and SIINFEKL-H-2Kb complex-specific CD8+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 7h). These results strongly suggest that the antitumor effects of CDCA may not depend on gut microbiota modulation.

On the basis of these findings, we hypothesized that CDCA might directly target tumors. To test this hypothesis, we administered CDCA treatment after tumor implantation (Fig. 4h). The results showed that CDCA significantly suppressed the growth of both B16-OVA melanoma (Fig. 4i) and MC38 colon carcinoma (Fig. 4j) cells. Flow cytometry analysis further demonstrated that CDCA treatment significantly increased the number of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 7i), including both the Tetramer-SIINFEKL-specific and the Tetramer-Adpgk-specific CD8+ T-cell populations (Supplementary Fig. 7j). These results collectively indicate that the antitumor effects of CDCA in mouse transplantation tumor models are mediated primarily through direct biological actions rather than through indirect modulation of the gut microbiota.

TCDCA is enriched within the tumor interstitial fluid and directly inhibits tumor growth

Tumor interstitial fluid (TIF) constitutes the local perfusate of the tumor microenvironment (TME), exerting a significant influence on immune cell function and behavior within tumors.22,23 To investigate whether CDCA treatment regulates antitumor immunity by affecting the composition of metabolites in mice, plasma and TIF were collected 24 h after treatment with vehicle or CDCA for untargeted metabolomics analysis. Partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) revealed significant differences in the plasma and TIF metabolite profiles between the vehicle and CDCA treatment groups (Fig. 5a, b). Bile acids (BAs) are classified into primary and secondary types. Primary BAs include cholic acid (CA) and chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA) in humans and CA and muricholic acid (MCA) in mice.21,24 These BAs are conjugated with glycine or taurine (taurine predominates in mice). Approximately 90–95% of BAs are reabsorbed via enterohepatic circulation, whereas the remaining 5% are metabolized by gut bacteria into secondary BAs.25–27 Previous studies have shown that oral CDCA is absorbed by the intestine, extracted by the liver, conjugated with glycine and taurine, secreted into bile, and undergoes enterohepatic circulation with endogenous bile acids28 (Fig. 5c). Metabolic analyses revealed no significant differences in plasma primary BAs between the vehicle- and CDCA-treated groups (Supplementary Fig. 8a). The conjugated bile acid TCDCA significantly increased the TIF after CDCA treatment for 24 h (Fig. 5d). There were no differences in the composition of secondary bile acids in both the plasma and TIF between the vehicle- and CDCA-treated groups (Supplementary Fig. 8b, c). Additional analyses revealed elevated levels of pyruvate, D-sphingosine, and cortisol in the CDCA group, indicating that glucose and lipid metabolism pathways in plasma were impacted (Supplementary Fig. 8d–f). An increase in purine metabolites, such as xanthine, was also observed, suggesting alterations in the purine metabolism pathway in TIFs (Supplementary Fig. 8g–i). The observed increase in these metabolites may be attributed to the effects of CDCA following oral administration, which affects both brown and white adipose tissues through the TGR5 or farnesoid X receptor (FXR) signaling pathways. This interaction subsequently facilitates lipolysis, enhances mitochondrial function, and elevates energy expenditure, thereby significantly augmenting systemic glucose and lipid metabolism and amino acid metabolism.29–32

To comprehensively delineate the pharmacokinetics of orally administered chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA) and its enrichment within tumors, we conducted a stable-isotope tracing study in B16-OVA tumor-bearing mice 14 days after implantation, administering a single gavage dose of CDCA-d4 (10 mg/kg) and collecting plasma, liver, tumor, and fecal samples at 1, 12, and 24 h. (Fig. 5e). Prior work has shown that oral CDCA remodels the hepatic bile acid pool in mice—including CDCA, TCDCA, lithocholic acid (LCA), taurolithocholic acid (TLCA), ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA), tauroursodeoxycholic acid (TUDCA), α-muricholic acid (α-MCA), and tauro-α-muricholic acid (Tα-MCA)33–35—we propose an in vivo metabolic map for CDCA (Fig. 5f) and establish a quantitative LC–MS/MS (MRM) assay using authentic standards to define characteristic spectra (Supplementary Fig. 9a), retention times, ionization modes, m/z transitions (Fig. 5g) and calibration curves (Supplementary Fig. 9b) for each analyte.

Following dosing, CDCA-d4 was rapidly absorbed in plasma (1 h: 3.09 ± 1.01 ng/mL), maintained for a period of time (12 h: 4.02 ± 1.64 ng/mL), and decreased to below the limit of quantification by 24 h. Hepatic amounts were modest (1 h: 3.10 ± 0.87 ng/mL; 12 h: 1.40 ± 0.41 ng/mL; 24 h: 1.60 ± 0.42 ng/mL), whereas fecal amounts increased consistently with hepatobiliary elimination and enterohepatic recirculation (12 h: 110.95 ± 41.77 ng/mL; 24 h: 3.90 ± 1.36 ng/mL). In the TIF, CDCA-d4 was detectable at 1 h (1.26 ± 0.10 ng/mL) but became undetectable thereafter (Fig. 5h). In contrast, the principal conjugate TCDCA-d4—formed via hepatic taurine conjugation—showed high and sustained hepatic exposure (1 h: 72.53 ± 65.17 ng/mL; 12 h: 96.72 ± 41.54 ng/mL; 24 h: 75.87 ± 19.45 ng/mL), low-to-moderate plasma levels (1 h: 1.49 ± 0.88 ng/mL; 12 h: 1.87 ± 1.51 ng/mL; 24 h: 2.48 ± 2.32 ng/mL), measurable fecal excretion (1 h: 12.83 ± 8.50 ng/mL; 24 h: 16.84 ± 2.25 ng/mL), and notably sustained accumulation within the TIF (1 h: 2.69 ± 0.88 ng/mL; 12 h: 5.56 ± 1.32 ng/mL; 24 h: 1.42 ± 0.20 ng/mL), indicating prolonged interstitial residency relative to systemic compartments (Fig. 5i).

For minor metabolites, UDCA-d4 (epimerization product) was detectable in the liver (1 h: 1.15 ± 0.83 ng/mL; 12 h: 3.58 ± 1.00 ng/mL; 24 h: 4.58 ± 0.37 ng/mL) and feces (1 h: 2.16 ± 0.47 ng/mL; 12 h: 10.76 ± 2.28 ng/mL; 24 h: 16.84 ± 6.75 ng/mL) but not in the plasma or TIF. The taurine conjugate TUDCA-d4 was present at relatively low concentrations, with liver concentrations of 14.26 ± 12.36 ng/mL (1 h), 11.39 ± 6.83 ng/mL (12 h), and 5.42 ± 0.29 ng/mL (24 h); plasma concentrations of 0.94 ± 0.37 ng/mL (1 h), 0.55 ± 0.42 ng/mL (12 h), and 1.60 ± 0.82 ng/mL (24 h); feces concentrations of 2.19 ± 1.00 ng/mL (1 h), 4.67 ± 2.14 ng/mL (12 h), 5.20 ± 2.60 ng/mL (24 h); and TIF concentrations of 0.74 ± 0.43 ng/mL (1 h), 0.75 ± 0.40 ng/mL (12 h), and 0.10 ± 0.08 ng/mL (24 h); in contrast, LCA-d4, TLCA-d4, α-MCA-d4, and Tα-MCA-d4 were below the limit of quantification under our conditions (Supplementary Fig. 10a–f). Collectively, these data establish that CDCA-d4 is rapidly absorbed, extensively metabolized in the liver, and eliminated primarily via the fecal route, whereas its major metabolite TCDCA-d4 is persistently exposed in the tumor interstitium, supporting a model in which hepatobiliary cycling and tumor-specific transport/clearance constraints permit local retention with potential implications for immune modulation within the tumor microenvironment.

Consistent with this finding, oral TCDCA (10 mg/kg) reduced tumor growth in both the B16-OVA and MC38 models (Fig. 5j–l) and was associated with increased infiltration of total and antigen-specific intratumoral CD8⁺ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 10g, h), suggesting that sustained interstitial TCDCA-level exposure may contribute to enhanced antitumor immunity.

CDCA suppresses tumor growth via the antigen-specific immunity of cDCs

The results demonstrated that TCDCA levels were elevated in TIFs, along with increases in CD8+ T cells and cDC1s in vivo, prompting us to further explore the role of TCDCA in subsequent analyses. Reports in the literature indicate that the physiological concentration of bile acid (BA) in the portal vein of humans and mice ranges from 10 to 80 μM and from 2 to 10 μM in the peripheral circulation.36 BA concentrations can reach approximately 100 μM in the tumor interstitial fluid of liver cancer patients.37 Therefore, we used concentrations up to 100 μM for our study. First, we investigated whether TCDCA affects the activation of naive splenic CD8+ T cells ex vivo following stimulation with anti-CD3/anti-CD28 antibodies. Our findings revealed no significant differences in proliferation, IFN-γ expression, GzmB expression, or TNFα expression in CD8+ T cells after TCDCA treatment (Supplementary Fig. 11a, b). Next, we examined whether TCDCA altered the activation of OT-I naive splenic CD8+ T cells ex vivo after stimulation with 5 mg/ml or 0.5 mg/ml OVA257–264 peptide. The results indicated that TCDCA treatment did not affect the proliferation or cytotoxicity of OT-I naive splenic CD8+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 11c, f). Overall, these results suggest that TCDCA does not modulate the function of CD8+ T cells.

We further induced cDC1s from bone marrow-derived cDC1s (Supplementary Fig. 12a) to examine their differentiation after TCDCA treatment. Cell viability assays and LDH release tests indicated that TCDCA had no cytotoxic effects at concentrations less than 100 μM ex vivo (Supplementary Fig. 12b–e). Consequently, we investigated the effects of TCDCA at 0 μM, 5 μM, 10 μM, and 50 μM. The results revealed no significant differences in the expression of immune costimulatory molecules (CD80, CD86, and CD40), MHC II, MHC I (H-2Kb), or CCR7 between the TCDCA-treated cells and the control (0 μM) cells after 6 h of treatment (Supplementary Fig. 12f–h). cDC1s were cocultured with OVA-FITC antigen for 6 h to assess antigen uptake, and we found that 50 μM TCDCA treatment ex vivo significantly increased OVA-FITC uptake (Fig. 6a, Supplementary Fig. 12i). Additionally, antigen presentation by SIINFEKL was also enhanced by 50 μM TCDCA treatment (Fig. 6b, Supplementary Fig. 12j). This phenomenon may be attributed to the enhanced endocytic activity of cDC1s subsequent to treatment with TCDCA, which consequently augments their capacity for antigen uptake and presentation.38 When cDC1s were treated with TCDCA for 24 h ex vivo, MHC I (H-2Kb) expression was upregulated with increasing TCDCA concentrations (Fig. 6c), whereas CD80, CD86, CD40, MHC II, and CCR7 expression remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 12k–m). Antigen uptake and antigen presentation by SIINFEKL were also enhanced with increasing concentrations of TCDCA (Fig. 6d, e). Next, we investigated whether cDC1s treated with TCDCA could activate CD8+ T cells. cDC1s were treated with TCDCA for 24 h and cocultured with OT-I naive splenic CD8+ T cells ex vivo. The proliferation of OT-I CD8+ T cells (Fig. 6f), as well as the production of IFN-γ, GzmB, and TNFα, was greater than that of the corresponding control (0 μM) T cells (Fig. 6g). These results indicated that TCDCA treatment enhanced MHC I (H-2Kb) expression, along with antigen uptake and the cross-presentation ability of cDC1s, thereby promoting the proliferation and effector functions of CD8+ T cells when they were coincubated with cDC1s.

Since TCDCA enhances MHC I (H-2Kb) expression, antigen uptake, and antigen presentation, we hypothesized that the reduced growth rates of tumors in CDCA-treated mice are mediated by MHC I (H-2Kb). To test this hypothesis, we used an anti-MHC I (H-2Kb) antibody or an isotype-matched control antibody (IgG2a) to block MHC I in B16-OVA tumor-bearing mice (Fig. 6h). The results showed that depletion of MHC I (H-2Kb) eliminated tumor growth suppression in CDCA-treated mice (Fig. 6i). The proportions of CD8+ T cells, tetramer-SIINFEKL+CD8+ T cells (Fig. 6j), and cytokines produced by CD8+ T cells (Fig. 6k) were significantly lower in MHC I (H-2Kb)-blocked mice than in IgG2a-injected control mice. However, no significant differences in these metrics were detected between the CDCA- and vehicle-treated groups of MHC I (H-2Kb)-blocked mice. Additionally, we used an antibody specific to the SIINFEKL peptide (OVA residues 257–264) (MHC I-SIINFEKL) along with an isotype-matched control antibody (IgG1) in B16-OVA tumor-bearing mice treated with CDCA and vehicle (Fig. 6h). Consistent with the anti-MHC I (H-2Kb) antibody results, blocking MHC I-SIINFEKL abolished the differences in tumor growth (Fig. 6l), the ratio of CD8+ T cells to tetramer-SIINFEKL+CD8+ T cells (Fig. 6m), and the levels of IFN-γ, GzmB, and TNFα in CD8+ T cells (Fig. 6n) between CDCA- and vehicle-treated mice. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the CDCA-induced suppression of B16-OVA tumor growth is dependent on the antigen-specific immunity of DCs.

TCDCA promotes MHC I expression in a TGR5-dependent manner

MHC class I expression is an essential function, and its modulation may involve multiple mechanisms. To elucidate the mechanism by which TCDCA induces MHC I (H-2Kb) expression in cDC1s, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on cDC1s treated with TCDCA or vehicle. CDC1 cells treated with TCDCA in vitro exhibited extensive changes in signal transduction and immune system-related signaling pathways (Supplementary Fig. 13a). KEGG enrichment analysis was performed on these two types of gene sets, selecting pathways with P-values < 0.05 and Padjust < 0.05. The analysis revealed significant differences between vehicle- and TCDCA-treated cDC1s. Treatment of cDC1 cells with TCDCA primarily altered the expression of genes associated with Th1 (CD4+IFNγ+), Th2 (CD4+IL4+), and Th17 (CD4+IL17A+) cell differentiation (Fig. 7a). However, oral administration of CDCA or TCDCA did not significantly change the proportions of Th1, Th2, or Th17 cells within tumor tissues (Supplementary Fig. 13b, c). Furthermore, when we cocultured bone marrow-derived cDC1s from Batf3−/− and WT mice with OT-I naive CD8+ T cells, Batf3−/− cDC1s, compared with WT cDC1s, failed to efficiently stimulate OT-I naive CD8+ T-cell proliferation (Supplementary Fig. 13d), indicating that Batf3−/− cDC1s were defective in an assay for the cross-presentation of cellular antigens to CD8+ T cells. Treatment of Batf3−/− cDC1s with varying concentrations of the TCDCA OT-I did not alter CD8+ T‑cell proliferation (Fig. 7b). These findings suggest that the observed effects may be linked to the activation of antigen processing and presentation pathways (in the immune system) (Fig. 7a). Additionally, pathways associated with MHC I regulation, such as the JAK-STAT and NF-kappa B signaling pathways, were altered (in signal transduction)39–41 (Fig. 7c). RNA sequencing confirmed the upregulation of MHC class I genes (H-2K2, H-2D1, H-2M2, H-2T24) and MHC I-related protein 2 (Mill2) and the change in transcription factors (Nfya, Ciita) (Fig. 7d). We also observed an enrichment of lysosomes (Supplementary Fig. 13e) and changes in proteasome subunits (Supplementary Fig. 13f) associated with antigen processing at the RNA level following TCDCA treatment, which is consistent with the flow cytometry results previously reported (Fig. 6b–e). To validate the RNA-sequencing results, we further investigated the expression of the MHC I gene (H-2K1) in cDC1s treated with or without TCDCA. Notably, treatment with TCDCA for 24 h resulted in the upregulation of H-2K1 mRNA expression (Supplementary Fig. 13g), which was confirmed at the protein level via Western blotting (Fig. 7e). However, no changes were observed in the antigen-processing machinery (APM) (e.g., B2M, TAP1, TAP2, TAPBP, or NLRC5). These results indicate that TCDCA upregulates MHC I expression in cDC1s and affects antigen processing and presentation through a TAP-independent mechanism.42,43

TGR5 is a membrane-bound receptor activated by BAs, whereas FXR is a nuclear receptor involved in BA-mediated gene regulation.44 To investigate their roles in dendritic cell function, we examined the expression of these receptors in murine cDC1 cells. Notably, we found that TGR5 was expressed, whereas FXR was not. This finding aligns with data from the Human Protein Atlas, which shows similar results in human DC cells (Supplementary Fig. 13h). Mechanistically, TGR5 activation promotes adenylate cyclic AMP (cAMP) accumulation and protein kinase A (PKA) activation,44,45 which directly phosphorylates CREB and NF-κB to drive their transcriptional activity,46–48 thereby regulating downstream effector expression. (Supplementary Fig. 13i). In support of these findings, treatment of cDC1 cells with TCDCA for 24 h significantly elevated cAMP levels and increased the phosphorylation of PKA, CREB, and p65 (Supplementary Fig. 13j, k). To confirm the functional relevance of this pathway, we employed pharmacological inhibitors targeting key signaling components and validated the efficacy of these inhibitors (Supplementary Fig. 14a–e). The TGR5 inhibitor SBI-115 completely abolished TCDCA-induced increases in cAMP, PKA activity, and CREB/p65 activation (Fig. 7f–i) and abolished the TCDCA-mediated upregulation of MHC I in cDC1s (Fig. 7j, k). Additionally, a selective cAMP inhibitor (MDL12330A), a PKA inhibitor (H89), an NF-κB inhibitor (QNZ), and a CREB inhibitor (666-15) significantly reduced MHC I protein levels (Fig. 7l–o, Supplementary Fig. 14f). We further assessed OVA-FITC uptake and found that SBI-115, MDL12330A, H89, QNZ, and 666-15 significantly decreased OVA-FITC uptake (Supplementary Fig. 14g, h) and antigen presentation of SIINFEKLs in cDC1s (Supplementary Fig. 14i, j). To investigate whether TCDCA enhances the T-cell-priming capacity of cDC1s through TGR5 signaling, we pretreated cDC1s with 50 μM TCDCA in the presence or absence of various signaling inhibitors before they were cocultured with naive CD8+ T cells. As illustrated by the CFSE dilution assay (Supplementary Fig. 14k, l), cDC1s conditioned with TCDCA significantly potentiated the proliferation of CD8+ T cells. Crucially, this stimulatory effect was markedly abolished when cDC1s were pretreated with inhibitors targeting TGR5, cAMP, PKA, CREB, or NF-κB. Consistent with the proliferation data, TCDCA-primed cDC1s substantially increased the expression of effector molecules in CD8+ T cells, including IFN-γ, GzmB, and TNFα (Supplementary Fig. 14m–o). However, the pharmacological blockade of TGR5 and its downstream signaling pathway in cDC1s effectively neutralized their ability to enhance CD8+ T effector function. was effectively reversed by these inhibitors. These results indicate that TCDCA acts directly on cDC1s to augment their immunostimulatory potential via a TGR5-mediated canonical signaling cascade.

Finally, we assessed the correlation between these observations and disease outcomes. In the TCGA datasets for skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM), we observed a highly significant positive correlation between MHC I molecules (HLA-A, HLA-B, HLA-C, HLA-E, HLA-F, and HLA-H) and TGR5 (Fig. 7p). Notably, higher expression of TGR5 genes in SKCM tumor samples was significantly associated with improved patient survival (Fig. 7q). Similar trends were observed in CESC, MESO, and SRAC tumor samples (Supplementary Fig. 15a–c), where a significant positive correlation between MHC I molecules and TGR5 was also identified. We observed a highly significant positive correlation between MHC I molecules (HLA-A, HLA-B, HLA-C, HLA-E, HLA-F, and HLA-H) and TGR5 in CESC, MESO, and SRAC tumor samples (Supplementary Fig. 15d–f). These results suggest that TCDCA-mediated regulation of MHC I expression relies primarily on TGR5 and its associated downstream signaling pathways.

CDCA shows promising preclinical potential for application in tumor therapy

Given the important role of dendritic cells (DCs) in activating T cells, numerous studies have focused on developing DC-based vaccines for cancer immunotherapy.49,50 Previous studies have demonstrated that cDC1s predominantly prime CD8+ T cells via antigen cross-presentation and that the extent of CD8+ T-cell activation is correlated with the number of MHC I/peptide complexes displayed on the cell surface.3,11,12 Therefore, we further investigated whether TCDCA could improve the therapeutic effect of cDC1 vaccines on tumors by modulating MHC I (H-2Kb) expression in cDC1s. First, we sorted cDC1s induced from bone marrow (Supplementary Fig. 16a) and validated the effectiveness of both IV injection and SC injection routes in delivering ovalbumen (OVA)-preloaded BM-cDC1s (cDC1 vaccine) (Supplementary Fig. 16b). Tetramer-SIINFEKL+CD8+ T cells were observed in the blood and tdLNs of the mice after IV or SC injection (Supplementary Fig. 16c, d). Next, we administered PBS- or TCDCA-treated cDC1 vaccines to mice bearing B16-OVA tumors (Fig. 8a). Tumor growth rates were significantly lower in the mice treated with the cDC1 vaccine than in those not receiving the vaccine, and the mice receiving the TCDCA-pretreated cDC1 vaccine exhibited slower tumor growth than did those receiving the PBS-pretreated cDC1 vaccine (Fig. 8b). Similarly, we assessed CD8+ T cells in tumors and found that the percentage of CD8+ T cells within tumors significantly increased following cDC1 vaccine injection. Compared with the PBS-pretreated cDC1 vaccine, the TCDCA-pretreated cDC1 vaccine significantly increased the number of CD8+ T cells, including tetramer-SIINFEKL+CD8+ T cells, as well as the production of cytotoxic cytokines by CD8+ T cells (Fig. 8c). These data suggested that TCDCA-treated cDC1 vaccines enhance the effectiveness of tumor treatment.

Additionally, to investigate the role of TGR5 in TCDCA-mediated antitumor immunity, we generated TGR5−/− mice (Supplementary Fig. 16e–g) and adoptively transferred cDC1s derived from either WT or TGR5−/− mice, following TCDCA treatment, into B16-OVA tumor-bearing mice (Fig. 8d). The results demonstrated that in the B16-OVA tumor model, the tumor-suppressive effect was significantly weaker in the TGR5−/−-cDC1 treatment group than in the WT-cDC1 group (Fig. 8e). Flow cytometry analysis revealed that the proportion of SIINFEKL-H-2Kb complex-specific CD8+ T cells in the TME was approximately lower in the TGR5−/−-cDC1 group than in the WT-cDC1 group, and the ratio of CD8+ CD44+ T cells also showed the same trend (Supplementary Fig. 16h‒j). These findings indicate that TGR5 is a crucial molecule for TCDCA-enhanced cDC1-dependent antigen presentation and tumor suppression.

Immature DCs cannot efficiently prime T cells, highlighting the need to promote the maturation of cDC1s. cDC1s uniquely express Toll-like receptor 3 (TLR3),51 which recognizes viral double-stranded RNA and its synthetic analog polyriboinosinic acid-polyribocytidylic acid (poly I:C). We hypothesized that combining CDCA with poly I:C would enhance cDC1 maturation and thereby potentiate tumor growth inhibition (Fig. 8f). As further evidence of target engagement, CETSAs demonstrated that both CDCA and TCDCA induce thermal stabilization of TGR5 in cDC1s (Supplementary Fig. 16k–n). These results strongly support the direct binding of both bile acids to TGR5 and suggest that, in vivo, CDCA may act as a prodrug that is converted to TCDCA to mediate antitumor effects. To evaluate this combination strategy, mice bearing subcutaneous B16-OVA tumors were treated with CDCA, poly I:C, or both. While either agent alone showed limited efficacy, the combination of CDCA and poly I:C resulted in significantly enhanced suppression of tumor growth (Fig. 8g). Consistent with this outcome, although monotherapy with CDCA or poly I:C induced only modest increases in CD8⁺ T-cell and cDC1 infiltration, their combination led to substantial accumulation of both cell types throughout the tumor tissue (Fig. 8h, i, Supplementary Fig. 17a, b).

Moreover, the PD-L1/PD-1 signaling pathway induces T-cell exhaustion, thereby inhibiting the activation, proliferation, and antitumor function of tumor antigen-specific CD8+ T cells, resulting in tumor-immune escape. Anti-PD-1 therapy disrupts the PD-L1/PD-1 inhibitory signaling pathway in T cells within the TME, thereby increasing T-cell recognition and the response to tumor cells.52,53 cDC1s play a crucial role in the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy, as tumors lacking cDC1s exhibit poor responses to anti-PD-1 therapy in mice.4,54 Therefore, we hypothesized that combination therapy with CDCA and anti-PD-1 could enhance tumor treatment (Fig. 8j). To test this hypothesis, the mice were pretreated with CDCA and vehicle, followed by B16-OVA tumor injection and anti-PD-1 therapy. As expected, CDCA notably enhanced the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy against B16-OVA tumors (Fig. 8k). The frequency of intertumoral CD8+ T cells was significantly greater in the mice that received combined CDCA and anti-PD-1 treatment than in those that received either treatment alone (Fig. 8l). The distribution of cDC1s in tumors followed a similar trend to that of CD8+ T cells (Fig. 8m, Supplementary Fig. 18a, b). These data collectively highlight the potential of combining CDCA with anti-PD-1 therapy to stimulate potent antitumor immunity.

In summary, our study elucidates the specific mechanisms by which CDCA regulates tumors and reveals its significant potential for clinical translation (Fig. 9).

Discussion

Bile acids and their microbiota-derived modifications are critical regulators of metabolism and systemic immune balance, significantly impacting the development of diseases such as liver and cardiovascular conditions, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), and rheumatoid arthritis (RA).21,32,55 Bile acid signaling pathways exert complex and paradoxical effects on tumor-immune responses. For example, deoxycholic acid (DCA) inhibits CD8+ T-cell-mediated antitumor immunity through the Ca2+ signaling pathway, thereby promoting colorectal cancer development.56 In contrast, ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) enhances antitumor immunity by inhibiting the differentiation and activation of Treg cells in tumor-bearing mice.57 Recent studies have shown that TCDCA can induce oxidative stress, promoting the apoptosis of CD8+ T cells in the liver cancer microenvironment and thereby accelerating tumor progression. However, notably, the concentrations of TCDCA used in the in vitro experiments in this study (250–500 μM) were well beyond the physiological concentration range. Our preliminary experiments confirmed that at high concentrations, TCDCA significantly impaired the proliferative capacity of dendritic cells (DCs). However, importantly, that study did not investigate the regulatory effects of TCDCA on immune cells at physiological concentrations (<100 μM).37 These findings underscore the immunomodulatory significance of bile acid metabolism and stress the need for further investigation into the unexplored mechanisms of bile acid metabolism in tumor immunity.

The results from our xenograft model revealed that while nude mice developed larger tumors, their survival was comparable to that of C57BL/6 mice. This apparent paradox is attributed to the observation that tumors in C57BL/6 mice consistently exhibited surface ulceration and bleeding at approximately 14–15 days postinoculation, regardless of sex. Consequently, these mice reached the predefined humane endpoints earlier, necessitating euthanasia despite their smaller tumor volumes. We reason that this phenomenon stems from the intact T-cell and myeloid immune responses in C57BL/6 mice, which provoke a potent local inflammatory reaction at the tumor site. In contrast, the lack of T cells in nude mice results in a weaker immune response. Although their tumors grow faster and larger, the absence of severe local tissue damage allows the tumors to expand “smoothly” to a much greater size without ulceration. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting death in mice bearing B16 tumors even when the tumor volume was less than 200 mm³.58 Although B16-F10 tumors can grow naturally to 1000 mm³ before they ulcerate in C57BL/6 mice, the daily gavage procedure used in our study imposed persistent stimulation on the mice, leading to significantly earlier ulceration than documented in the literature.59 This discrepancy does not compromise the authenticity of our experimental results or affect the reliability of the subsequent flow cytometry data.

The literature indicates that plasma bile acid levels, including corresponding bile acid levels, can increase after 3 h of fasting.29,60 No significant increase in CDCA was detected in the plasma after 24 h of oral CDCA administration, which may be due to the efficient absorption of excess peripheral bile acids through the enterohepatic circulation, maintaining stable plasma bile acid levels. However, the underlying cause may be abnormalities in the tumor vascular system and compromised lymphatic drainage, which results in the accumulation of fluid extravasated from blood vessels within the stroma.61,62 These findings may explain why no significant changes in plasma bile acid levels were detected 24 h after CDCA treatment, whereas altered TCDCA concentrations were observed in the TIF.

Following oral administration of CDCA-d4, LC–MS tracing demonstrated rapid first-pass taurine conjugation to TCDCA-d4 and subsequent hepatobiliary secretion; however, critically, the conjugate exhibited a distinct distribution pattern characterized by a delayed peak in TIF and sustained detectability through 24 h while remaining low and relatively stable in plasma. This kinetic dissociation, together with progressive fecal accumulation, is most consistent with repeated enterohepatic recirculation that repeatedly reseeds the tumor milieu and, owing to the impaired lymphatic clearance and dense extracellular matrix typical of solid tumors, favors retention of TCDCA-d4 within the interstitium. Sustained residency of TCDCA-d4 in the TIF provides a pharmacokinetic basis for local immunomodulation. Conjugated bile acids can engage bile acid–sensing pathways (e.g., GPCRs) expressed on myeloid and lymphoid cells,63 thereby influencing antigen presentation. Thus, the kinetics observed here support the concept that enterally derived TCDCA-d4 establishes a sustained paracrine milieu in tumors capable of remodeling the immune microenvironment.

Although prior studies have indicated that microbiota-mediated regulation of bile acids plays an important role in tumor progression,21,64–66 our data showed that prophylactic oral administration of CDCA increases the abundance of the probiotic Akkermansia, which has been reported to inhibit tumor progression67–69; however, the therapeutic effect of CDCA persists even after depletion of Akkermansia. Recent research has revealed that the role of Akkermansia is primarily manifested in enhancing the efficacy of PD-1 antibodies, thereby exerting a synergistic antitumor effect.67,70,71 This finding also explains why, although oral CDCA administration increases the abundance of Akkermansia, its elimination does not compromise the intrinsic antitumor effect of CDCA, suggesting that CDCA may act through an independent pathway that does not rely on Akkermansia. Moreover, the oral administration of CDCA or TCDCA after tumor inoculation effectively suppressed tumor growth. Together, these findings support the conclusion that CDCA has intrinsic antitumor efficacy independent of Akkermansia.

Gene set enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment of the IL-17 signaling pathway and the Th17 cell differentiation pathway. Although these findings prompted us to investigate whether CDCA/TCDCA influenced Th17 cell differentiation, our in vivo flow cytometry data demonstrated that CDCA and TCDCA treatment did not alter the proportions of T helper cell subsets. This result is consistent with previous reports in the literature72 indicating that CDCA/TCDCA does not drive Th17 differentiation. Consequently, although transcriptomic data suggest that TCDCA upregulates Bcl6 in cDC1 cells, a transcription factor with a known role in Th17 differentiation,73,74 this regulatory effect was insufficient to cause significant changes in the Th17 cell population in vivo. These findings suggest that the antitumor effects of CDCA/TCDCA are likely independent of the Th17 pathway and that the upstream molecular event may exert its function by influencing other activities of cDC1s.

In the cDC1-specific antitumor-immune process, MHC I plays a pivotal role in the cross-presentation of antigens. Tumors evade immune surveillance by downregulating MHC I expression, which is a significant mechanism for tumor-immune escape. Loss of MHC I expression is often linked to shorter overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) as well as higher metastatic rates.75,76 Moreover, MHC I can enhance responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors by increasing the frequency of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes.77–79 Our study revealed the direct promoting effect of the taurine conjugate TCDCA on the effector functions of cDC1s and demonstrated that this effect is fundamental to CDCA-mediated tumor suppression, thereby expanding our understanding of the multiple roles of bile acids in different immune cell types. TGR5 has been identified as a membrane receptor for bile acids, and its activation leads to increased cyclic AMP (cAMP) levels,45 potentially activating protein kinase A (PKA) and phosphorylating the transcription factors CREB and NF-κB47 To confirm the functional relevance of this pathway, we employed pharmacological inhibitors targeting key signaling components and validated the efficacy of these inhibitors, initiating the transcription of downstream target genes. Interestingly, KEGG pathway analysis of our RNA-seq data suggested overall negative enrichment of the NF-κB signaling pathway, whereas Western blotting revealed increased p65 protein levels in the CDCA-treated group. This apparent discrepancy is not unexpected: numerous studies have reported only moderate concordance between mRNA and protein abundances due to extensive posttranscriptional regulation, variable translation efficiency, differences in protein stability, and other layers of gene expression control.80 Moreover, NF-κB activity is predominantly regulated by posttranslational mechanisms (e.g., phosphorylation and nuclear translocation) rather than by changes in NF-κB mRNA levels.81 Consistent with this framework, we showed that TCDCA enhances MHC I expression on cDC1s in a TGR5-dependent manner, thereby augmenting the effector function of CD8⁺ T cells. The discovery that TCDCA modulates MHC I levels in cDC1 cells via TGR5 and subsequently reshapes immune infiltration within the tumor microenvironment represents novel mechanistic insight into how bile acid signaling can influence antitumor immunity.

Monocyte-derived DC (Mo-DC) vaccines have been utilized in clinical trials for various cancers, but their therapeutic efficacy remains limited.82,83 Despite the ability of Mo-DC vaccines to elicit cross-priming of T cells, their suboptimal vaccine efficacy may be attributed to limited migration to lymph nodes.7 Recently, cDC1s have been developed as vaccines for preclinical tumor models because of their unique ability to cross-present antigens and activate CD8+ T cells.84 TCDCA modulates MHC I levels on cDC1s through TGR5 signaling, influencing immune infiltration within tumors, which represents a novel mechanistic finding. We demonstrated that TCDCA enhances the efficacy of the cDC1 vaccine and strongly inhibits melanoma growth in vivo, underscoring its significant potential for clinical application. Injecting the TLR3 agonist poly I:C into mice upregulated the expression of the costimulatory molecules CD40, CD86, and MHC II on the surface of cDC1s.4 When combined with intertumoral resiquimod therapy, this synergistically activates tumor-associated macrophages, resulting in a more robust systemic antitumor-immune response.85 Furthermore, we combined poly I:C with CDCA to increase MHC I molecule expression, resulting in superior antitumor therapeutic outcomes. These findings demonstrate that CDCA, through a distinct mechanism, enhances antitumor efficacy when combined with poly I:C therapy. Additionally, previous studies have indicated a positive correlation between the number of activated T cells and the success of immune checkpoint blockade therapy.86,87 Increasing CDCA levels in vivo promote CD8+ T-cell infiltration within tumors, potentially sensitizing them to PD-1 immune checkpoint treatment. Our results indicate that combining anti-PD-1 antibodies with CDCA has potent tumor-suppressive effects, suggesting a strong rationale for future clinical trials in cancer patients. Given that CDCA is a safe clinical drug, its combination with anti-PD-1 therapy has emerged as a promising tumor immunotherapy method with minimal side effects.

Although both CDCA and TCDCA can inhibit tumor growth, the safety of CDCA has been extensively validated in clinical applications. Compared with TCDCA, CDCA may have fewer serious side effects and lower treatment costs. This could result in a broader range of indications, fewer contraindications, and a lower economic burden, making it a gentler adjuvant therapy option in the early stages of cancer.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, regarding the identification of cDC1 subsets, while CD8α+ cDC1s primarily reside in lymphoid tissues and CD103+ cDC1s are key mediators of antigen presentation and T-cell activation in the tumor microenvironment,3 our work did not thoroughly explore the potential contribution of CD8α+ cDC1s to the progression of our specific tumor model. Second, although metabolites such as pyruvate88 and sphingosine89 are known to participate in tumor regulation, the mechanistic roles of cortisol and xanthine within the tumor microenvironment remain poorly defined. Our study only theoretically proposed mechanisms by which CDCA might influence the production of these metabolites, and experimental validation of their specific contributions to the observed antitumor effects is lacking. Finally, while these limitations highlight avenues for future research, they do not undermine the validity or reliability of the study’s primary conclusions.

Materials and methods

Mice and cell lines

C57BL/6J mice, BALB/c nude mice, and BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks old) were obtained from Biohfk (Beijing, China). OT-I mice were obtained from Biocytogen (Beijing, China). CD45.1 mice were generated as previously described.90 Batf3−/− and TGR5−/− mice on a C57BL/6 background were purchased from the Nanjing Biomedical Research Institute of Nanjing University. A mouse direct PCR kit (Bimake) was used to extract genomic DNA from tail biopsies for PCR detection and genotyping via gene-specific primers (Appendix Table S2). All animals were housed in the specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility at West China Science and Technology Park, West China Clinical Medical College, Sichuan University. All experimental protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Sichuan University (Chengdu, Sichuan, China).

The B16-OVA, MC38, B16-F10, LLC, and 4T1 cell lines were preserved at the State Key Laboratory of Biotherapy. MC38, 4T1, and Lewis lung carcinoma cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific), while B16-F10 and B16-OVA cells were grown in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS (FSP500; Excell Bio). All the cell cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2.

Tumor growth experiments and in vivo treatments

Two weeks prior to tumor inoculation, the mice were administered 5 mg/kg CDCA (Sigma) or vehicle daily via oral gavage until the conclusion of the experiment. C57BL/6J mice were subcutaneously injected with 5 × 105 LLC cells, 2.5 × 105 B16-F10 cells, 2 × 106 MC38 cells, or 2.5 × 105 B16-OVA cells. BALB/c mice received subcutaneous injections of 5 × 105 4T1 cells. Tumor growth was monitored daily with a Vernier caliper, and tumor volume was calculated via the following formula: V = (length × width²) × 0.5. Lethality was defined as the time when the mice met the following predefined humane endpoint criteria: 1. Severe body weight loss (>20% from baseline); 2. Significant morbidity (e.g., impaired mobility, ulceration, or signs of distress); and 3. a tumor size reaching 1500 mm2. Mice that reached any of these endpoints were euthanized, and the data were recorded for survival analysis.

To inhibit lymphocyte egress during CDCA treatment, 1 mg/kg FTY720 was administered (intraperitoneal injection, i.p.) one day before tumor inoculation and continued every other day until the end of the study.

For the depletion of CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, and CD11c+ cells, B16-OVA tumor-bearing mice received 250 μg of anti-CD8α (BE0061, Bio X Cell) and anti-CD4 (BE0003-1, Bio X Cell) antibodies, as well as an anti-IgG2b isotype control (BE0086, Bio X Cell), via intraperitoneal injection twice weekly. Additionally, 50 μg of anti-CD11c (GTX74940, GTX) and an anti-IgG isotype control (GTX00619, GTX) were administered every other day throughout the experiment (intraperitoneal injection, i.p.).

MHC I (H-2Kb) and MHC I (H-2Kb) bound to the SIINFEKL peptide (OVA residues 257–264) were depleted from B16-OVA tumor-bearing mice by administering 250 μg of the appropriate antibodies (BE0121 and BE0207, Bio X Cell) along with anti-IgG2a (BE0085, Bio X Cell) and IgG1 isotype controls (BE0083, Bio X Cell) via intraperitoneal injection twice a week for the entirety of the experiment.

Flow cytometry

The cell surface markers were stained according to the manufacturer’s instructions with conjugated antibodies for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. For intracellular staining, the cells were stimulated with PMA (P1585, 50 ng/ml, Sigma‒Aldrich), ionomycin (I3909, 1 μg/ml, Sigma‒Aldrich), and brefeldin A (420601, BioLegend) for 4 h at 37 °C, followed by incubation with intracellular antibodies for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Intranuclear staining was performed via an intracellular staining kit (562574, eBioscience). The stained cells were analyzed via a NovoExpress flow cytometer and a Challenbio FongCyte™, and the data were processed with NovoExpress software (ACEA Biosciences).

Single-cell RNA sequencing

Single-cell RNA-sequencing analysis: CD45+ tumor-infiltrating leukocytes were isolated from day 14 B16-OVA tumors and enriched through positive selection for single-cell analysis. Before magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS), the B16-OVA tumor tissues were minced in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 2% fetal bovine serum, 2 mg/mL collagenase I (Cat# 17104019, Gibco), and 50 μg/mL DNase I (Cat# D8071, Solarbio) and then incubated at 37 °C with gentle agitation for 30 min. CD45+ leukocytes were subsequently enriched via positive selection with CD45 MicroBeads (130-052-301, Miltenyi Biotec). For each group, CD45+ leukocytes sorted from two independent tumor specimens were pooled in equal amounts and loaded onto the instrument for analysis.

The scRNA-seq data analysis was conducted by NovelBio Co., Ltd., via the NovelBrain Cloud Analysis Platform (www.novelbrain.com). Initial processing of the raw sequencing data was performed via fastp with default settings to filter out adapter sequences and remove low-quality reads, resulting in high-quality clean data. The platform follows a standardized analytical pipeline: quality control, alignment, and quantification of raw sequencing data via Cell Ranger to generate the gene expression matrix; normalization of data; selection of highly variable genes; PCA-based dimensionality reduction via Seurat; visualization of cellular subpopulations via UMAP; and automated cell type annotation with SingleR, supplemented by manual validation via canonical marker genes (e.g., CD3E for T cells and CD79A for B cells) to ensure accurate cell type identification. To analyze the preference of specific cell subsets between the vehicle- and CDCA-treated groups, the ratio of the observed index to the expected (Ro/e) index was calculated. The chi-square (χ²) test was employed to determine the statistical significance of shifts in the cell proportions across different clusters.91

Intestinal microbiota depletion tumor model

The mice were orally administered antibiotics for 7 days prior to CDCA feeding. Afterward, they were gavaged daily with 5 mg/kg CDCA, and antibiotics were continued every other day until the end of the experiment. The antibiotic mixture (ABX) included ampicillin (100 mg/kg, Maclin), vancomycin (50 mg/kg, Maclin), neomycin (100 mg/kg, Maclin), and metronidazole (100 mg/kg, Maclin). Prior to tumor inoculation, fecal samples were collected from the mice for 16S rRNA gene analysis and colony formation observation from the fecal suspension plates. Subsequently, 2.5 × 105 B16-OVA cells were inoculated.

16S rRNA gene microbiome analysis

Fecal samples were collected from the mice and stored at −80 °C. Sample preparation and sequencing analysis were performed by Meiji Biotechnology Company. The experimental procedures were as follows: bacterial genomic DNA was extracted from the fecal samples via the PowerSoil DNA Isolation Kit (MoBio, catalog number 12888-50). The V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was PCR-amplified via uniquely barcoded universal primers with 30 ng of genomic DNA as the template. All PCRs were carried out in triplicate, and the amplicons were purified via Agencourt AMPure XP beads. The purified PCR products were pooled in equimolar concentrations, quantified via an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, and subjected to high-throughput sequencing on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform. For data analysis, an open-reference operational taxonomic unit (OTU) picking strategy was employed, with OTUs clustered at 97% sequence similarity against the Greengenes 13_5 database. Taxonomic classification and rarefaction analysis were performed via QIIME 1.8.0. Subsequent statistical analyses, including principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and hierarchical clustering, were conducted in the R programming environment.

TIF and serum collection

TIFs were collected 12–15 days post-tumor injection via a previously described method.23,92 Briefly, tumors were rinsed in PBS, dried with filter paper, placed on a 70 μm cell strainer (VWR) above 50 mL conical tubes, and then centrifuged at 400 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting TIF was collected, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for subsequent analysis. Blood was collected via retroorbital hemorrhage into heparinized tubes (VWR), followed by centrifugation at 1500 × g for 20 min at 4 °C to isolate the plasma. The plasma samples were also frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for further analysis.

Nontargeted metabolite analysis

Untargeted metabolomics of polar metabolites in TIFs and serum was performed by BGI Co., Ltd. (Shenzhen, China). A high-resolution mass spectrometer (Q Exactive, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was utilized to collect data in both positive and negative ion modes, ensuring comprehensive metabolite coverage. LC‒MS/MS data were processed via Compound Discoverer 3.1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for peak extraction, alignment, and compound identification. The metaX metabolomics R software package facilitated data preprocessing, statistical analysis, metabolite classification, and functional annotation. Principal component analysis (PCA) was employed to reduce dimensionality and analyze grouping, trends, and outliers within the dataset. Differentially abundant metabolites were identified via VIP values from the first two principal components of the PLS-DA (partial least squares discriminant analysis) model combined with the fold change and Student’s t-test results from univariate analysis.

Sample preparation and LC–MS/MS analysis

Male mice were orally administered chenodeoxycholic acid-d4 (CDCA-d4) at a dose of 10 mg/kg body weight. Blood plasma, feces, liver tissue, tumor interstitial fluid, and bile samples were collected at 1, 12, and 24 h post-administration. All the samples were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Prior to analysis, the plasma and tissue samples were thawed on ice and extracted with methanol (3:1, v/v), followed by vortex mixing and centrifugation at 13,300 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were collected, evaporated under nitrogen, and reconstituted in 50% methanol for LC–MS/MS analysis.

The quantification of CDCA-d₄ and its metabolites was performed via an ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography system (Shimadzu Nexera 30A, Kyoto, Japan) coupled to a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (SCIEX Triple Quad™ 6500+ ) equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source operating in negative ion mode. The UHPLC system consisted of LC-30AD pumps, an LC-30AC autosampler maintained at 10 °C, and a CTO-20AC column oven set at 40 °C. Chromatographic separation was achieved on a Waters BEH C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 µm; Waters, Milford, MA, USA). The flow rate was maintained at 0.3 mL/min.

For the Shimadzu-based analyses, the mobile phases consisted of (A) water with 0.1% formic acid and (B) acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid. The gradient elution program was as follows: 0–0.5 min, 10% B; 0.5–2 min, 10–45% B; 2–5.5 min, 45–50% B; 5.5–8 min, 50–55% B; 8–11 min, 55–95% B; 11–13 min, 95% B; 13–13.5 min, 95–10% B; and 13.5–16 min, 10% B for re-equilibration.

The mass spectrometer was operated in multiple-reaction monitoring (MRM) mode with negative electrospray ionization. The optimized parameters were as follows: ion spray voltage, −4500 V; curtain gas (CUR), 35 psi; ion source gas 1 (GS1), 50 psi; ion source gas 2 (GS2), 40 psi; and source temperature, 500–550 °C. The collision gas (CAD) pressure was set at 8 psi. The MRM transition for CDCA-d4 was m/z 395.3 → 376.4, with corresponding transitions optimized for its metabolites.

Data acquisition was performed via Analyst 1.7.2 software (SCIEX), and quantification was carried out via MultiQuant™ 3.0.3 (SCIEX, Framingham, MA, USA). Calibration curves were generated by plotting the peak area ratios of analytes to the internal standard against nominal concentrations, followed by linear regression with a 1/x² weighting factor. The concentrations of CDCA-d4 and its metabolites in the biological samples were calculated on the basis of the calibration curves. All analytical standards, including chenodeoxycholic acid-d₄ (CDCA-d4) and its metabolites (TCDCA-d4, UDCA-d4, TUDCA-d4, LCA-d4, and TLCA-d4), were purchased from MedChemExpress, and α-MCA-d4 and Tα-MCA-d4 were purchased from GlpBio.

T-cell isolation and activation

Naive CD8+ T cells or OT-1 CD8+ T cells were isolated from splenocytes via a naive CD8 sorting kit (19858, Stemcell) and activated in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin in the presence of TCDCA (S875461, Macklin) and either OVA257–264 peptide (RP10611, GenScript) or CD3+ /CD28 (11452D, Gibco) for 72 h. Following activation, the cells were subjected to surface and intracellular staining as described below. For CFSE labeling, naive CD8+ T cells were incubated with 5 μM CFSE dye (422701, BioLegend) for 15 min at 37 °C prior to activation.

Generation of bone marrow-derived cDC1 cells

Bone marrow cDC1s (CD103+ DCs) were induced as previously reported.93 Briefly, tibiae and femurs were isolated from mice, and bone marrow cells were harvested and lysed from red blood cells. A total of 1 × 106 bone marrow cells were cultured in 10 cm dishes with RPMI-1640 (C22400500BT, Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS (FSP500, Excell), 1% antibiotic-antimycotic, 50 µM β-mercaptoethanol, 20 ng/mL recombinant murine GM-CSF (315–03, PeproTech), and 100 ng/mL recombinant murine Flt3L (250-31L, PeproTech) and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2. The culture medium was changed every other day until days 15 or 16, at which point the isolated cells were analyzed via flow cytometry with anti-mouse CD11c and CD103 antibodies.

Cell viability and cytotoxicity assays

cDC1 cells were plated in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well and treated with 0 μM, 10 μM, 50 μM, or 100 μM TCDCA for 6 or 24 h. Cell viability was assessed via the Cell Counting Kit-8 (C0038; Beyotime), and the supernatants were collected for cytotoxicity analysis via the Lactic Dehydrogenase Release Assay Kit (C0016; Beyotime).

cDC1 antigen uptake and peptide presentation assay

The collected cDC1 cells were treated with 0 μM, 10 μM, or 50 μM TCDCA for 6 or 24 h, followed by incubation with OVA-FITC (SF069, Solarbio) to assess antigen uptake and peptide presentation. Briefly, cDC1 cells were incubated with 20 μg/mL OVA-FITC at 37 °C for 3 h. Dead cells were labeled with 7-AAD (420404, BioLegend), and the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of OVA-FITC in live cells was measured via flow cytometry. H-2Kb bound to SIINFEKL (141605, BioLegend) was used to evaluate peptide presentation.

RNA-sequencing analysis

For RNA-sequencing experiments, cDC1 cells were treated with 0 μM or 50 μM TCDCA for 24 h, followed by total RNA extraction via TRIzol (15596018CN, Invitrogen). RNA sequencing was conducted by Majorbio Biopharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), where cDNA libraries were constructed and sequenced. In brief, 200 ng of RNA per group was used for library preparation with the TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina), followed by PCR amplification and purification via a certified low-range superagarose gel (Bio-Rad). Cluster generation was performed on an Illumina cBot via the TruSeq PE Cluster Kit v3-cBot-HS, followed by high-throughput sequencing on the Illumina MiSeq sequencer via the TruSeq SBS Kit v3-HS (200 cycles). The aligned reads were summarized via featureCount (v.2.0.0) on the basis of Gencode vM19 annotations. Differential expression analysis was conducted with the DESeq2 (v.1.30.0) R (v.4.0.3) package. KEGG pathway analysis was performed on differentially expressed genes with a fold change (FC) > 1.5 between the two groups, with Padjust < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Protein extraction and western blotting

The collected cDC1 cells were washed with 1× PBS and lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (P00138, Beyotime) containing a 1× protein inhibitor cocktail (P8340, Sigma‒Aldrich). The protein concentration was quantified via a BCA kit (P0012, Beyotime). Equal amounts of protein lysates were mixed with 6× SDS‒PAGE loading buffer, boiled at 100 °C for 5 min, and loaded onto 8‒15% gels (Bio-Rad). Proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes (1620177, Bio-Rad) and probed with primary antibodies followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (A27036, Invitrogen). The membranes were washed three times with 1× TBST and developed with an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) solution (Merck Millipore, WBKLS0500), and detection was performed via an iBright CL1000 System (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA). The band intensity in the image was quantified via ImageJ (National Institutes of Health), focusing solely on the intensity within the linear range.

Real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted via TRIzol reagent (9109, TaKaRa, Kusatsu, Shiga, Japan) and reverse transcribed into cDNA via a cDNA synthesis kit (RR047A, TaKaRa) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Real-time PCR was conducted with SYBR Green (RR420, TaKaRa). The primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Serum obtained from cDC1s treated with TCDCA was analyzed for cAMP production via a cAMP kit (SY-M01502, Bioshuangying, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Immunohistochemical

The tumor tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h, followed by dehydration in alcohol and xylene before being embedded in paraffin wax. Sections (6 μm) were dried, dewaxed, and hydrated. Antigen retrieval was performed using 3% H2O2, followed by thermal repair in sodium citrate buffer at 1000 W for 3 min. Blocking was accomplished by incubation with 5% normal goat serum in PBS for 30 min at 37 °C. CD8 staining was performed with anti-CD8 antibodies (ab217344, Abcam) overnight at 4 °C. The slides were subsequently rinsed and incubated with a biotin-conjugated secondary antibody for 30 min, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase-streptavidin (HRP-streptavidin) for an additional 30 min (ZSGB-BIO). The sections were developed via a 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate kit (ZSGB-BIO, ZLI-9017), and hematoxylin was used for counterstaining. Images were captured, and the number of CD8-positive cells was determined using the ImageJ software.

Immunofluorescence imaging

The tumor tissues were fixed as described above and subsequently dehydrated, embedded, and sectioned. Antigen repair was performed via the use of EDTA antigen repair buffer, followed by blocking with BSA for 30–45 min. Sections were stained with CD103 antibodies (ab238010, Abcam) and CD11c antibodies (NB110–97871, Novus) overnight at 4 °C. Fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies corresponding to the primary antibodies were added, and the samples were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 50 min. DAPI staining was performed at room temperature in the dark for 10 min. The sections were sealed with an anti-fluorescence quenching agent and stored at 4 °C in the dark. Images were captured via a fluorescence microscope, and the average fluorescence intensity of the cDC1 cells was analyzed via ImageJ software.

cDC1 vaccine preparation

Induced cDC1 cells were sorted via a CD11c sorting kit (18780, Stemcell), treated with 50 μM TCDCA or PBS for 24 h, and then incubated with 20 μg/mL OVA (A5503, Sigma) at 37 °C for 24 h. Following washing with PBS, the tumor-bearing mice received 1 × 106 cDC1 cells via subcutaneous injection twice weekly on days 6, 10, and 13.

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)

The experimental procedure was conducted as follows: Initially, differentiated cDC1s were lysed on ice for 30 min to extract total protein. Equal aliquots of protein samples (2 mg/mL, 1 mL) were then incubated with either CDCA, TCDCA (20 μmol/L), or DMSO (vehicle control) at room temperature for 50‒60 min. The protein mixtures from each treatment group were equally distributed into 10 PCR tubes and subjected to gradient temperature treatment (10 distinct temperature points). Following 3 min of heating at each designated temperature, the samples were immediately transferred to a thermal cycler (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Scientific) for 3 min of incubation at 4 °C. After centrifugation (20,000 × g, 10 min), the supernatants were mixed with 5× loading buffer and denatured at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by subsequent western blot analysis.

The combination of in vivo tumor growth

For combination with the PD-1 antibody, C57BL/6J mice were administered 5 mg/kg CDCA daily via oral gavage for two weeks prior to subcutaneous injection of 2.5 × 105 B16-OVA cells. Five days after inoculation, the mice were treated with 200 μg of anti-mouse PD-1 (BE0273, Bio X Cell) or anti-IgG2a isotype control (BE0085, Bio X Cell) via intraperitoneal injection twice weekly.

For the combination with poly I:C, C57BL/6J mice were administered 5 mg/kg CDCA daily via oral gavage for two weeks. The mice were treated with 100 μg of poly I:C (B5551, APEXBIO) or vehicle via intraperitoneal injection twice weekly before tumor resection, followed by subcutaneous injection of 2.5 × 105 B16-OVA cells.

Correlation analysis of HLA and TGR5 expression in melanoma

RNA-seq data for HLA and TGR5 were obtained from the TCGA database and analyzed via cBioPortal. Patient survival was analyzed through the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA) database and retrospective analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were performed via GraphPad Prism 8 software. An unpaired Student’s t-test was used to compare differences between two groups, whereas two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni correction was used for assessments of tumor volume and the cell growth rate. Pearson correlation and linear regression analyses were employed to determine correlations in clinical samples. A two-sided rank test was used to analyze survival data. The experimental results are expressed as the means ± standard errors or standard deviations. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, with “n.s.” indicating not significant. The significance levels are denoted as follows: *p ≤ 0.05, **p ≤ 0.01, ***p ≤ 0.001, and ****p ≤ 0.0001.
