Executive Summary

The primary objective of the Tulare Country Carbon Storage Project (TCCSP) is to establish the
technical, community, and economic foundation for a commercial-scale regional geologic storage
complex for CO2 captured from Calgren’s ethanol production and dairy digester plant in Pixley,
California as well as dairy farms and industrial facilities in the area. An integral part of this effort
is the assessment of the technical, legal, logistical, and financial risks to developing a commercial
CO2 storage complex hub in Tulare County. A Risk Assessment Workshop was conducted to
identify and address technical and non-technical challenges that may arise. Technical and local
subject matter experts (SME) were included in the Risk Assessment Workshop to evaluate project
specific risks and possible region-specific risks pertaining to CO2 storage infrastructure
development. A master TCCSP Risk Assessment Matrix (RAM) was generated prior to the
workshop and was used by the project team to conduct a quantitative assessment of key project
risks and identify appropriate mitigation strategies for developing a safe, technically sound, and
economically feasible carbon storage project.

Utilizing the results and feedback gathered during the Risk Assessment Workshop, the project
team developed a Risk Management Plan (RMP) which is outlined in the Risk Management Plan
section of this report. The RMP describes the key outcomes of the RAM that was populated during
the workshop. The RAM contains pre- and post-mitigation ratings, risk prevention/mitigation
strategies, and responsibilities for risk management and will be continually updated over the life
of the project.

A CO2 management strategy for TCCSP is outlined in this report and is based on assessing the
nature, quantity, and reliability of CO2 sourcing in addition to viability, safety and operability
analysis of a CO2 transmission pipeline connecting CO2 supply to the storage site. The CO2
management plan incorporated applicable inputs from the Risk Assessment Workshop as well as
key findings from the Techno-Economic Assessment (Task 8).
To assess leakage risk for TCCSP, a leakage assessment model was developed in Open
Integrated Assessment Model (IAM) using the outputs from dynamic modeling efforts for TCCSP
(Subtask 5.3). The developed IAM model was leveraged against the tools developed by the
National Risk Assessment Partnership (NRAP) in conjunction with the DRM to fill gaps in leakage
risk assessment.

Purpose and Background

Risk Assessment Background

For this Risk Assessment Plan, TCCSP has conducted a site-based risk assessment that
quantifies the risk of CO2 and/or brine leakage as a result of CO2 injection at TCCSP. The risk
assessment covers project planning, construction, injection, post-injection periods and closure of
TCCSP. Key technical and non-technical Project risks are identified as part of the Risk Workshop
and include a thorough evaluation of related scenarios. Mitigation strategies for the identified risk
scenarios are defined to develop a scientifically sound, and community acceptable CO2 storage
Project.

Project Background

TCCSP is in the process of designing an industrial-scale carbon capture and storage (CCS) hub
located in Tulare County, California. Regional and site-specific geological characterization
indicates that the subsurface geology of the southern San Joaquin Basin is comprised of high
quality storage reservoir and confining units that can achieve large-scale CO2 storage of at least
50 million metric tons (Mmt) CO2 over a 20-year injection period. TCCSP will capture CO2
emissions from the Calgren Renewable Fuels ethanol plant, owned and operated by TCCSP,
along with other sources, and inject a total of 50 Mmt of CO2 into saline reservoirs.

The Calgren Renewable Fuels ethanol plant produces ethanol, wet distillers’ grains with solubles
(WDGS), and corn oil. The CO2 transport infrastructure will consist of a main trunk line with lateral
lines constructed to transport the capture volumes from each additional CO2 source.

TCCSP has drilled a stratigraphic test well, TCCSP_OBS-1, for data gathering in the project area.
The land surface surrounding the project is comprised of cultivated crops, open water,
pasture/hay, grassland/herbaceous, and developed land as defined by the National Land Cover
Database (2024). Resources in the vicinity of TCCSP that may be affected due to an emergency
event within the project area include:

 • Shallowest USDW source
 • Primary water source for the region
 • The lowermost USDW
 • Creeks, streams, and other water bodies
 • Agricultural land

Infrastructure in the vicinity of TCCSP that may be affected if an emergency at the project site
were to occur include:

 • Project infrastructure – CO2 capture and compression systems, CO2 pipeline, CO2
 injection wells, monitoring wells, and distributed control system (DCS) equipment
 • Potable water wells
 • Residential properties
 • Commercial properties

 • Industrial properties
 • Recreational properties
 • Protected areas

Risk Assessment Workshop

Overview of the Risk Assessment Workshop

An initial risk assessment template was created for TCCSP that combined risk items that were
identified in prior risk assessments for CO2 storage projects. For TCCSP, new risk items and risk
categories were generated and combined with risk items from previous CCS projects to
incorporate relevant challenges and findings of the current project.

The Project Team, Project Advisors and other SMEs participated in a two-day hybrid (i.e. in-
person and virtual) Risk Assessment Workshop on July 22-23, 2025. Assessment of risk was
conducted based on the current state-of-the-art knowledge for the various aspects of the project’s
design and development as well as technical viability of monitoring and mitigating project risks.
These risks could impact commercial-scale development of the project and require possible
mitigation.

Attendees discussed and gave feedback on the technical project risks related to geologic
suitability, new and existing wellbores, drilling and completions, leakage, induced seismicity,
operations, and monitoring. Non-technical risks addressed included financing and lending
agreements, financial performance, landowner agreements for surface access and pore space
use, public opposition, site ownership and liability. The participants ranked the project risks
individually and the results were averaged to determine the final ranking for each. The attendees
also provided input towards the development of a mitigation plan for identified project risks.

The risk categories from the assessment included:
 • Legal/Logistical
 • Outreach/Public Relations
 • Wellbores
 • Geology
 • Monitoring
 • Operations – Capture/Transport
 • Operations – Injection/Storage
 • Well Construction

Site-Based Risk Assessment

Methodology

TCCSP adopted a semi-quantitative risk assessment to assess project risks. The ratings for
project risks were calculated as a product of the ‘likelihood of occurrence’ and the ‘severity of
consequence’. Table 1 and Table 2 describe the basis for likelihood and severity of project risks
used for the TCCSP risk assessment. Table 3 lists various risk classifications (low, medium, high)
and their corresponding risk rating.

Once a risk has been assigned a likelihood of occurrence and severity of consequence, the risk
rating is calculated as:
 Risk Rating = Likelihood of Occurrence x Severity of Consequence

After each risk scenario was rated, the participants’ risk results for severity and likelihood are
averaged. For this risk assessment averaged risks that are rated as 3, or greater, were discussed
to identify preventative measures and to define a risk mitigation strategy that will lower the
likelihood of the risk, severity of the risk or both, resulting in lowering the overall risk rating.
Following the post-mitigation risk ratings, the updated probability and severity are tallied and
averaged.

High Risks
Collectively, no risks received a High-Risk rating (between 6 and 9) and all risks that received a
high-risk rating by workshop attendees are covered in the Medium-Risk (between 3 - 6)
discussion. The TCCSP risk workshop participants individually identified fifty-seven (57) risk
scenarios with a risk rating of six (6) or nine (9), which include all risk categories. Eight (8) risks
received a rating of nine (9) by at least one workshop participant and are from the following
categories: Monitoring, Operations – Capture/Transport, Operations – Injection/Storage, and
Legal/Logistical. Several of these are discussed in this section and the remainder are covered in
the Medium-Risk section, as they were collectively rated as medium-risks.

Operational risks had three scenarios that received a rating of nine (9) by at least one workshop
participant. Of those, two belong in the Injection/Storage category and were related to scenarios
where an increase in subsurface pressure compromised injection operations. In the
Capture/Transport category, the risk of seismic activity affecting the mechanical integrity of the
injection wells also received a high risk of nine (9).

In the Monitoring category, the identification of significant and unexpected plume movement
through the results and analysis of the monitoring program received a high rating of nine (9).

 10

For the Legal/Logistical category, delays in construction or injection operations due to supply
chain issues received a high-risk of nine (9) by at least one participant. Supply delays could be a
significant issue and have a “down-stream” impact on other elements of the project, delaying the
project schedule milestones, causing construction or necessary repairs to be halted, thereby
potentially impacting the compliance of the permit(s).

Medium Risks
The TCCSP risk workshop identified 39 medium risk scenarios for every risk category. The risk
categories with the highest counts (five or more medium-risk scenarios) are in Legal/Logistical
(eight medium risk scenarios), Operations (Capture/Transport and Injection/Storage) (11 medium
risk scenarios), Well Construction (seven medium risk scenarios) and Monitoring (six medium risk
scenarios).

Legal/Logistical
Legal and logistical risk scenarios identified for TCCSP are various and include delays related to
permitting activities or approval, shifts in local or federal politics and claims that the project is not
in compliance with permit requirements. Permitting requirements for CCS projects are extensive,
and TCCSP is aware that timing is essential and has been proactive in pursuing and submitting
key federal and state required permits that must be approved and accepted for TCCSP to proceed
to the injection phase. These include the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) UIC Class VI
permit for the injection wells, the California Air Resources Board (CARB) application, and the
California Environmental Quality Act (CEQA) permit. TCCSP has and will continue to maintain
good communication and prompt responses to requests from permitting entities. Hiring
experienced contractors for elements of the permitting process as needed and flexibility in
scheduling as issues arise are also part of TCCSP’s approach to the permitting process.

Shifts in political view at the local or federal level could impact any of the regulatory entities’
approach to the oversight of project activities. Additionally, permit requirements and overall project
expectations could shift resulting in a decrease in project viability. TCCSP will engage with
legislators and will continue their engagement with regulators to stay informed on any shifts in
regulations that may have an impact on the project. TCCSP will also keep open lines of
communication with community stakeholders to address any communication issues that may
arise and to build positive community interactions and knowledge transfer around project
activities.

Risk scenarios related to false public claims of CO2 leakage, CO2 trespass outside of the AoR,
and induced seismicity were rated as medium-risk. An essential part of the monitoring program
that will be implemented at TCCSP includes the collection of baseline testing and monitoring data
to identify any significant changes that may occur over the life of the project related to the injectate
stream, wellbore integrity, properties of the storage complex and/or above-zone, and CO2 plume
and pressure front migration. TCCSP will continuously monitor an appropriate seismic network to
determine the presence or absence of any seismic activity associated with CO2 injection
operations to supplement site-specific data obtained from the TCCSP monitoring program.

Well Construction and Operation
Well operations or construction challenges could present risk to TCCSP as identified in various
risks presented and discussed at the Risk Workshop. TCCSP has identified a combination of
engineering and administrative safeguards to prevent the probability of the occurrence of these
risks as low as reasonably possible and will proactively work to reduce the severity of any risks
related to construction and operations. Careful planning on well construction and injection/storage
operations, such as characterization and modeling of pressure gradients and fractures, utilizing
local expertise in contractors, regular operator training and enacting Standard Operating
Procedures (SOPs), will reduce the risks associated with scenarios in those categories. TCCSP
will also employ preventative maintenance and design for operations related to Capture/Transport
and Injection/Storage.

To address construction challenges and any subsequent risks, TCCSP will implement well design
strategies and materials focused on preventing movement of fluids into or between USDWs or
into any unauthorized zones, permit the use of appropriate testing devices and workover tools
and permit continuous monitoring of the annulus space between the tubing and long string casing.
The construction of the injection wells within the TCCSP storage site will be performed using best
practices and will conform to all requirements set forth in local, state, and federal permits required
for the project.

The injection wells for TCCSP are designed to accommodate the maximum instantaneous mass
rate that is expected from the capture facility while considering critical characteristics of the CO2
storage reservoir. Well design principles and materials were selected and vetted to ensure
construction materials have sufficient structural strength to provide sustained mechanical integrity
throughout the life of the CCS project, in addition to permitting the use of appropriate testing
devices, workover tools, and continuous monitoring of the annulus space between the injection
tubing and long string casing. All well construction materials were selected to be compatible with
fluids of which they may be expected to come into contact (e.g., corrosion-resistant cement) and
meet or exceed industry standards. All injection wells will undergo regular Mechanical Integrity
Testing (MIT) and adequate corrosion monitoring to ensure the integrity of materials that are in
contact with the CO2 stream.

Monitoring
The findings from the risk assessment workshop outlined the need for a robust and redundant
monitoring program to ensure plume tracking and CO2 leakage detection monitoring is not
compromised due to potential equipment failure. With the multitude of systems to be monitored,
and the complexities of processing and interpreting the resulting data, the potential of
misinterpretation of data or conflicting monitoring results was classified as a medium risk. TCCSP
is aware of the challenges that can be encountered by a complex monitoring program and is
defining and implementing SOPs, quality assurance checks, and implementation of engineering
controls to minimize this risk.

Outreach/Public Relations
Risk related to outreach and public relations that received a medium-risk rating are scenarios
where community backlash occurs due to mistrust in CCS projects in general or a lack of clear

communication from TCCSP, LLC concerning the project. TCCSP is committed to engaging with

CO2 Supply
Other medium project risks entail potential changes in the quality of CO2 supplied to TCCSP which
may alter injection operations and/or reservoir quality. TCCSP has conservatively designed the
pipeline and injection well to address such compositional changes and abate the potential impact
on mechanical integrity of the pipeline and project wells. The Risks section of this report provides
further preventative measures that TCCSP will pursue to address any issues with CO2 quality.

Geology
Geologic risks that received a medium rating include leakage of CO2 out of the storage zone due
to the primary confining zone not providing an adequate seal and high permeability zones in the
storage reservoir acting as a thief zone resulting in an unexpected and significant expansion of
the CO2 plume and limited storage capacity. To address these risks TCCSP will pursue a robust
assessment of the subsurface to obtain the physical and chemical characteristics of the geologic
zones of interest. To date, TCCSP has conducted a thorough characterization of the geology at
the site and will continue to pursue a rigorous characterization of the subsurface as the project
evolves and additional data from the construction of project wells, becomes available. Additional
preventative measures that can be used to address potential geologic risks will include periodic
geologic and dynamic model updates, uncertainty modeling, 3D seismic surveys, and additional
characterization or testing.

The current status of the geologic assessment indicates that the project area provides a
geologically favorable setting for safe, long-term storage for CO2. The CO2 injection zones
comprise thick and porous sandstones with interbedded mudstone and the primary seal
immediately caps the storage zones and provides a thick and laterally extensive seal across the
area. Structurally, there is a lack of faulting in the area in and around the site and no existing
wellbores within the modeled plume area.

Pre-operational testing of the injection wells to be drilled at the site will establish the necessary
baseline data for comparison to future measurements and demonstrate compliance with the
injection well construction requirements set forth in the permits that will be obtained for TCCSP
through regulatory entities. This additional geologic characterization at the site will include well
logging and geophysical surveying, mechanical integrity testing, geologic coring, fluid sampling,
and hydrogeologic testing.

 13

These methods will generate datasets to aid in determining and/or verifying the depth, thickness,
porosity, permeability, mineralogy, and geochemical profiles of the primary confining zone and
storage reservoir, and shallower intervals to establish a baseline description of the geology,
geochemistry, and groundwater quality of the above-zone (i.e., formations above the confining
zone) which will later be compared against data obtained throughout the injection phase. Testing
and monitoring of the above-zone will aid in the protection of USDWs as the above-zone
monitoring wells allow for early detection of containment loss prior to reaching shallow
groundwater sources.

Seismic Event
Southwestern Tulare County, where the project is located, has a history of relatively low seismicity
indicating that seismic related hazards are unlikely. TCCSP will pursue a geotechnical
assessment of the surface facilities to identify potential issues in the infrastructure and design that
may be impacted by a seismic event. The geotechnical assessment results will be used to inform
SOPs developed for design and operations. Pressures and saturations within the storage system
will be monitored to avoid fluid leakage and induced seismicity. If a seismic event occurs, a site
assessment will be performed to evaluate infrastructure integrity and monitoring data will be
reviewed and analyzed to determine if there is any drift from baseline that may indicate that the
seismic event has compromised containment of the CO2.

The following administrative and engineering controls will be implemented to mitigate fluid
leakage or induced seismicity related concerns during injection operations:

 • Periodically monitor for local seismic activity.
 • Develop protocols to systematically check monitoring network after earthquake of a certain
 magnitude.
 • TCCSP will develop a master TCCSP health, safety, and environment (HSE) plan to cover
 safety of project personnel, local communities, local infrastructure, and natural resources
 during all site operations. TCCSP will incorporate recommendations and SOPs from
 subcontractors and their respective HSE plans into the master TCCSP HSE plan.
 • TCCSP will develop emergency shutdown procedures that will be implemented as
 appropriate and a plan will be drawn up that will outline, in detail, all emergency and
 remedial responses developed for the project.
 • TCCSP will conduct timely, prompt, and transparent public outreach to communicate any
 project findings or events that pose a threat to the safety of local communities, resources,
 and infrastructure.
 • TCCSP will use CO2 resistant well materials to mitigate formation of potential leakage
 pathways for CO2 up the wellbore.
 • TCCSP will use a combination of temperature and pulsed neutron capture data to monitor
 the CO2 plume and for fluid leaks in the vicinity of the injection well as well as 3D surface
 seismic surveys to monitor the CO2 plume.
 • The injection wells will be modeled to inject below 80% of the fracture pressure
 corresponding to the shallowest perforation within the well.

Post-Mitigation Ratings
TCCSP risk workshop identified appropriate post-mitigation strategies for all medium and high
risks based on current state-of-the-art knowledge of mitigation techniques and principles. All
scenarios originally classified as “medium” risk have post-mitigation ratings of four (4) or less,
placing them in the “medium” or “low” risk classification. TCCSP is aware that there is inherent
uncertainty associated with large-scale, long-term geologic projects and will continue to manage
risk over the life of the project to further reduce the currently identified risks, and any additional
risks identified in future. Wherever technically and administratively feasible, TCCSP will apply
mitigation strategies outlined in the RAM.

Leakage Risk Assessment
TCCSP evaluated the potential for CO2 and brine migration into USDWs at the San Joaquin Basin
site using the U.S. Department of Energy’s (DOE) National Risk Assessment Partnership open-
source integrated assessment model (NRAP-Open IAM). The analyses covered 20 years of
injection and 50 years of post-injection for the target storage formations with two planned injection
wells and one legacy plugged well that intersects the formations.
Across all formations, pressure build-up was insufficient to drive upward brine migration, resulting
in effectively negligible brine leakage. In fact, modeled leakage often indicated flowback of brine
downward into the reservoirs due to hydrostatic imbalance. The CO2 leakage into the USDW
stayed below 0.1% of total injected volume even under the most permeable scenario. Brine
leakage remained negligible across all scenarios. These outcomes indicate that the TCCSP site
is well suited for long term geological carbon storage with low leakage risk to USDWs under the
modeled conditions. Uncertainty persists regarding whether the existing plug in the legacy well
fully seals below the USDW base.
Further recommendations for corrective action involve re-plugging or reinforcing the legacy well
to address plug-depth uncertainty and enhance long-term wellbore integrity. Long term monitoring
should be done by implementing sustained monitoring to confirm model predictions, provide early
leakage detection, and maintain public confidence. The integrated modeling demonstrates
negligible brine leakage and <0.1 % CO2 leakage risk, confirming the suitability of the site for
secure storage under CarbonSAFE Phase II objectives, provided that legacy well corrective
actions and monitoring plans are executed.

in the pipeline and custody transfer of CO2 at the injection well is outlined with details on the

Physical Characteristics of the CO2 Stream

Calgren Renewable Fuels will initially be the primary source of CO2 for TCCSP, but additional CO2
sources throughout the life of the project will be added. The chemical composition of the CO2
stream will be monitored to ensure it meets minimum composition specifications; these
specifications will be refined when sources are finalized, and capture equipment is operational.
The CO2 stream coming into the storage site is expected to have a CO2 concentration of at least
99 vol% with other chemical constituents.

The injection wells will have corrosion-resistant materials present where CO2 will be present.
Upon injection into the reservoir formation, the CO2 will remain in supercritical phase which will
allow for minimal interaction with the formations.

Storage Site CO2 Supply Infrastructure

Each gas source will have its own blower skid, positioned as close as practical to its respective

Preliminary space requirements indicate that each blower will need a dedicated equipment plot,

The capture and compression equipment will be sited within the existing operating area. Each

CO2 Intake and Transport

The CO2 management plan involves an assessment of the custody transfer of the CO2 and
accounting for physical and chemical changes in the CO2 stream from cradle-to-grave beginning
with farthest point source capture to the storage site. To establish realistic boundaries on CO2
stream changes, possible transport scenarios were assessed to outline desired CO2 phase during
transportation from emitter locations to the injection site. Desired properties for on-site storage of
CO2 were assumed from literature (Suzuki et al., 2013).
The CO2 will be transported by pipeline from the Calgren facility to the injection site. Injection
operations will be monitored using a distributed control system (DCS). All critical system
parameters (e.g., pressure, temperature, and flow rate) will have continuous electronic monitoring
with signals transmitted back to TCCSP’s DCS.
The wellhead and Christmas tree materials for the injection wells are designed to be compatible
with the CO2 stream. Critical components that come into contact with the CO2 stream will be made
of a corrosion-resistant alloy such as stainless steel. Materials that are not expected to contact
the injection fluid will be carbon steel.
Surface and downhole pressure gauges will be used to continuously monitor pressure whereas a
Coriolis mass flow meter will be utilized to measure the flow rate, volume, and density of the
injectate stream on the surface. Measured injection mass flow rate will be used along with
injectate pressure and temperature measurements to determine fluid density and convert mass
values to volumetric measurements. Wellhead gauges will be used to ensure surface pressure
remain below the maximum allowable pressure.
TCCSP supervisors and operations personnel will have the capability to monitor and control all
operations remotely with this system. The system will automatically sound an alarm and shut
down operations should the specified control parameters exceed their respective maximum
allowable values at any time. In the event of loss of communication with the DCS, operations
personnel will manually monitor critical parameters such as pressure at each wellhead until DCS
communication is reestablished.
Potential changes in the quality of CO2 supplied to TCCSP may alter injection operations and/or
reservoir quality. The risks associated with potential changes in the quality of CO2 supplied to
TCCSP have been considered in the design of the CO2 transport and intake infrastructure.
TCCSP has conservatively designed the pipeline and injection wells to address such
compositional changes and abate the potential impact on mechanical integrity of the pipeline and
project wells. As previously mentioned, corrosion-resistant materials will be used where CO2 will
be present. A nominal pipeline grade CO2 specification is also presented with the understanding
that changes in this specification will require a rethink of the metallurgy of the pipeline as well as
injection well tubulars and components that interact with the injectate.

CO2 Transmission Pipeline

Design
A pipeline will transport the combined CO2 stream from the surface facilities and the loading
terminal to the injection well; the final alignment is under development. Final line size and

 20

operating setpoints will be confirmed by detailed hydraulics to accommodate initial operations and
future throughput increases within the project’s build-out plan.
The purpose of the CO2 injection facility is to boost the pressure of the CO2 delivered via pipeline
to the required injection pressure of the storage reservoir. The delivery pressure of the pipeline
and the injection pressure of the reservoir are key parameters in the design of the injection facility.
The design and operation of the CO2 injection equipment can accommodate a change in pipeline
delivery or formation injection pressure if this occurs after further evaluation.
The pipeline delivers the CO2 to the injection facility as a dense phase, supercritical fluid; this
allows pumps to boost the pressure of the CO2 for storage in one or more injection wells (design
point for surface injection pressure). Operation of the pipeline above the CO2 critical pressure
allows for efficient transport of the fluid; transport of CO2 as a supercritical fluid moves 4 to 7 times
more product versus a gas-phase pipeline with the same diameter. In addition, the density of the
supercritical fluid simplifies the equipment used at the injection facility. Centrifugal pumps and
mass flow meters are the primary equipment at the injection facility required to boost the pressure
and provide a measurement of the amount of CO2 injected into the reservoir, respectively.
The injection facility boosts the pressure of CO2 from the pipeline to the injection well. The primary
design parameters for the injection facility are the delivery pressure of the CO2 pipeline (inlet), the
surface pressure requirement of the CO2 at the injection well (outlet), and the flow rate of CO2 to
be injected. Pumps are used to boost the pressure of CO₂ in many process applications. CO2
pumps are common equipment in pipeline booster stations and at the wellhead to inject CO2 into
subsurface geologic reservoirs associated with sequestration projects and enhanced oil recovery
(EOR) projects. The common pump types used are horizontal multi-stage centrifugal pumps and
split-case centrifugal pumps; these pumps are familiar to operators for moving fluids in the oil and
gas industry.
The horizontal multi-stage centrifugal pump utilizes numerous face-to-back, stacked stages of
identical impellers and diffusers mounted inside of the pump barrel. The pump line-shaft and
stacked impeller assembly uses line-shaft bushings and impeller wear-rings/bushings to maintain
the rotational stability of the rotating element assembly during pump operation. Due to the
continuous, face-to-back, stacked impeller design, all the hydraulic forces resulting from the pump
differential head are applied as a thrust force in a single axial direction towards the pump suction.
To counter the high axial thrust forces, the pump design incorporates a large thrust bearing
mounted in a bearing frame (thrust chamber) located between the pump suction chamber /
mechanical seal assembly and the motor coupling section.
The split case centrifugal pump is also referenced as an API 610 (Type BB3), horizontally (axially)
split, multistage, between-bearing type of pump design. The impellers of the pumps are mounted
on a relatively large diameter, stiff shaft which is concentrically fixed by bearings located at each
end to provide a well-centered rotating element having uniformly close radial clearances. A center
bushing and throttle bushing is commonly used to further improve rotor axial balance and rotor
dynamic stability. Each impeller stage also utilizes impeller wear rings and bushings to prevent
recirculation between stages, like the horizontal centrifugal pump. The pump has a hydraulically
balanced rotor assembly with impeller stages mounted in two groups facing opposite directions.
This results in low axial thrust forces and reduced probability of thrust bearing failure. Due to the
between-bearing design, two mechanical seals are required for a Type BB3 pump. Seal chamber

 21

pressure for each of the seals is typically balanced to a pressure slightly above the pump suction
pressure.
Both style pumps have good operating experience in CO2 service. The split-case centrifugal pump
is more typical of pipeline booster stations because of the API design rating. The split-case
centrifugal pump can achieve higher flow rates, has slightly higher efficiency, but costs about two
times more for similar capacity, and delivery time will be more than double that of a horizontal
centrifugal pump. Although not an API rated pump, the reliability of a horizontal centrifugal pump
is high based on experience and the pumps are widely used in field and plant operations. Typical
pump efficiency is above 80%, and equipment delivery is less than 20 weeks.
To provide flexibility for a long-term injection program, two injection sites are included. CO2 from
the source is transported via pipeline to the injection facility and custody transfer meters located
at either end of the pipeline confirm the integrity of the system. From the pipeline, CO2 would flow
through a manifold and route to one of two injection sites. At each site, two pumps are positioned
in parallel; either pump can provide 100% flow when operating. The discharge CO2 flows through
a back pressure valve (not shown) before injection. This valve is used to maintain differential
pressure across the pumps during startup, but it is typically open during normal operation.
The design supports wide turndown for variable receipts, provides pump/line bypass for
maintenance, and includes launcher/receiver connections to enable routine pigging and internal
inspection. Emergency shutdown (ESD) logic provides automatic isolation and controlled
blowdown on high-high pressure, leak detection, or fire/gas alarm; leak detection is based on
mass-balance between custody meters, with alarmed thresholds and operator response
procedures.
Safety and Operability Analysis
An operability analysis of the CO2 pipeline system will be performed for TCCSP to identify and
mitigate potential hazards and risks related to operation of the CO2 pipeline.
The first step of this analysis will be source modeling. Source modeling will be completed to
estimate the source conditions required for input to the consequence model. Parameters that can
have an impact on the subsequent consequence modeling include the following:
 • Release rate
 • Liquid mass fraction
 • Source temperature
 • Source size
The source characterization will be completed with a transient release blowdown model. The
model solves the time varying mass, momentum, and energy equations with the consideration of
the behavior of “real” and compressible fluids. The fluid properties will be estimated using the
Peng-Robinson equation-of-state.
The estimate of the transient fluid and flow properties at node points along the pipeline permits
estimates of the pressure and flow rate time history, which in turn can be used to estimate the
behavior of the valves (if applicable). A range of release sizes will be considered (i.e., from a
guillotine rupture to a leak) for each release locations assumed along the pipeline.
Consequence modeling will be the second part of the operability analysis. The intrinsic hazard
associated with accidental releases from a CO2 pipeline is the toxicity of the released fluid and

 22

the associated inhalation hazard. Consequence modeling will be completed to estimate the
downwind extents of selected hazardous CO2 concentrations.
Consequence modeling will be conducted using the SLAB dispersion model. The SLAB
dispersion model was developed at the Lawrence Livermore Labs and contains algorithms that
can model the physics of dense gas releases including gravity slumping, reduced air
entrainment resulting from stable density gradients (i.e., density within the plume is larger than
that of the ambient air) and the thermodynamics of phase change within the plume. The SLAB
model is used to meet dispersion modeling requirements in the U.S. EPA RMP. For time varying
source conditions, the method of observers, as applied in the HGSYSTEMS (McFarlane et al.,
1990) and DEGADIS (Havens and Spicer, 1990) dispersion models, is used to transform steady
state SLAB runs, at each simulated time-step, into a single transient run.
The SLAB dispersion model assumes the plume centerline is terrain following which is an
appropriate assumption for a ground-based release of a dense gas when the plume centerline
is close to or at ground- level. Additionally, the SLAB formulation allows for a first order
estimate of the effects of a laterally constrained plume (e.g., plume constrained in a gully) and
for a plume flowing down a moderate uniform slope. For assessment of three-dimensional (3-
D) terrain effects a more refined modeling (e.g., Computational Fluid Dynamics, CFD) can be
used. The topography will be reviewed with consideration of the initial terrain following results
to determine if there are release sites where elevation changes and channeling may influence
the estimated hazard extents. The dispersion modeling will be conducted over a range of
weather conditions that capture different levels of atmospheric stability and wind speeds for
each rupture case to be modeled.

Injection Well Design

TCCSP seeks to drill and construct two new Class VI CO2 injection wells to support CO2 storage
operations. The well construction plan for the two wells has been designed in accordance with
EPA UIC Class VI permit 40 CFR §146.86, pursuant to 40 CFR §146.82. TCCSP has implemented
well design strategies and materials focused on (1) preventing movement of fluids into or between
USDWs or into any unauthorized zones; (2) permitting the use of appropriate testing devices and
workover tools and (3) permit continuous monitoring of the annulus space between the tubing and
long string casing. Any necessary changes to this well plan due to logistical or geological
conditions encountered within the field will be communicated to the U.S. EPA Region 09 UIC
Program Director prior to well construction. The injection wells will be constructed as shown in
accordance with the requirements of the EPA UIC Class VI rule (40 CFR § 146.86).
Proposed Stimulation Program
A well stimulation program will be performed in both injection wells during the drilling operations.
After perforation of all injection wells, an acid wash will take place at low pressures and will not
endanger the confining zone or create a leakage pathway while improving the injectivity of the
near wellbore and allow for injection of CO2 to take place at lower startup pressures. No other
stimulation is proposed
Construction Procedures
The injection well construction plan has been designed around a staged completion strategy to
utilize the verticality in reservoir quality while managing the lateral extent of the free phase CO2
and elevated pressure plume.

Well design principles and materials detailed in subsequent sections were selected and vetted to

The TCCSP injection wells will be constructed with casing and cement that will be compatible with
